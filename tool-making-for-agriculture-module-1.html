1. Overview of Agricultural Tools and Their Uses

Agricultural tools are the backbone of farming in Africa. From the simple hoe to the ox-drawn plough, each tool has a specific job. In this lesson, you will learn about common tools and their uses. The hoe is the most basic tool, used for digging, weeding, and making ridges. There are two main types: the short-handled hoe for close work and the long-handled hoe for heavier tasks. The machete (panga) is used for clearing bush, harvesting, and cutting. The axe is for felling trees and splitting firewood. The sickle is for harvesting grains like rice and wheat. The plough, often pulled by oxen or donkeys, turns soil to prepare seedbeds. The harrow breaks clods and levels the soil. The planter drops seeds at the right spacing. The wheelbarrow carries heavy loads. In Africa, many farmers still use hand tools because they are affordable and easy to maintain. But knowing the right tool for the job saves time and energy. For example, using a hoe to harvest rice is slow and damages the grain; a sickle is much better. Similarly, a machete is not for digging. You must match the tool to the task. Also, tools are made from different materials: iron for blades, wood for handles. Understanding these basics will help you when you start making or repairing tools. As you go through this module, keep in mind that a well-made tool can last for years if properly cared for. In the next lessons, you will learn safety, maintenance, and how to make your own tools.

2. Safety Practices in the Tool Making Workshop

Safety is not optional in a tool making workshop. Every year, many accidents happen because people ignore simple rules. In this lesson, you will learn how to protect yourself and others. First, always wear protective gear. Safety glasses or goggles protect your eyes from flying metal chips when grinding or chipping. Leather gloves protect your hands from sharp edges and hot metal. Closed-toe shoes or boots protect your feet from dropped tools or hot sparks. An apron or overalls protect your clothes and skin. Second, keep your workshop clean and organized. A cluttered floor causes trips and falls. Keep tools in their places. Sweep up metal shavings and wood dust regularly. Third, use tools correctly. A wrench is not a hammer. A screwdriver is not a chisel. Using the wrong tool can cause injury and damage the tool. When using power tools like grinders or drills, check the cords for damage. Never use a power tool in wet conditions. Fourth, handle hot metal with tongs or pliers, never with bare hands. Quench hot metal in water or oil safely, away from your body. Fifth, have a first aid kit nearby and know how to use it. For burns, cool with clean water. For cuts, apply pressure and cover. Sixth, work in a well-ventilated area. Fumes from welding, soldering, or painting can be harmful. If you are welding, use a welding helmet and fire-resistant clothing. Seventh, never work alone if you can avoid it. If you must, tell someone where you are and what you are doing. Finally, learn to recognize fatigue. Tired people make mistakes. Take breaks. By following these practices, you can work safely and productively. Remember, a safe workshop is a productive workshop.

3. Basic Hand Tools and Their Maintenance

Hand tools are the foundation of any workshop. In this lesson, you will learn about common hand tools and how to keep them in good condition. The most important tools for tool making are: hacksaw, files, hammers, chisels, punches, pliers, wrenches, screwdrivers, and measuring tools like rulers and squares. A hacksaw cuts metal. Use a blade with 14 to 18 teeth per inch for general work. Keep the blade tight and replace it when dull. Files shape and smooth metal. Use a single-cut file for sharpening and a double-cut for fast removal. Clean files with a file card to remove metal chips. Store files separately to avoid dulling. Hammers come in different weights. A 2-pound ball-peen hammer is good for general work. Check the handle for cracks and the head for looseness. If the head is loose, replace the handle or wedge it tight. Chisels cut metal. Keep the cutting edge sharp with a file or grinder. Never use a chisel with a mushroomed headâgrind it back to shape. Punches mark or drive pins. Use the right size for the job. Pliers grip, bend, and cut wire. Keep the pivot oiled. Wrenches tighten and loosen nuts and bolts. Use the correct size to avoid rounding the nut. Screwdrivers must match the screw head. A worn tip will slip and damage the screw. Measuring tools like steel rules and try squares must be kept clean and rust-free. For all tools, rust is the enemy. After use, wipe them with an oily rag. Store them in a dry place. If rust appears, remove it with steel wool and oil. Sharp tools are safe tools because they require less force. Dull tools slip and cause accidents. So, sharpen your tools regularly. A few minutes of maintenance each day will save you hours of work and money. In the next lesson, you will learn how to read tool designs.

4. Reading and Interpreting Tool Designs

Before you make a tool, you need to understand its design. A design is a drawing or plan that shows the shape, size, and parts of a tool. In this lesson, you will learn how to read simple tool designs. Designs can be sketches on paper or technical drawings with dimensions. The most common views are the front view, top view, and side view. These show the tool from different angles. Dimensions are given in millimetres (mm) or inches. For example, a hoe blade might be 200 mm long, 150 mm wide, and 3 mm thick. The handle might be 1200 mm long and 30 mm in diameter. You need to know how to read these numbers. A scale drawing shows the tool smaller or larger than real size. For example, a scale of 1:2 means the drawing is half the real size. You must multiply the drawing measurements by 2 to get the real size. Lines on a drawing have meaning. Solid lines show visible edges. Dashed lines show hidden edges. Centre lines show the middle of round parts. Arrowheads show dimensions. You also need to understand symbols for materials. For example, a pattern of diagonal lines might mean steel. A wavy pattern might mean wood. Sometimes, notes are written on the drawing, like 'weld here' or 'grind smooth'. In Africa, many tool designs are passed down by tradition, but modern designs can improve performance. For instance, a hoe blade can be made from an old car spring, which is tough and wears well. The design must consider the local materials available. You also need to think about the user: a handle should fit the hand comfortably. A blade should be balanced. When you can read a design, you can make a tool that works. You can also modify designs to suit your needs. Practice by copying simple designs and making them. Start with a simple tool like a garden trowel. Measure, draw, and then build. This skill will serve you well.

5. Selecting Materials for Agricultural Tools

The quality of a tool depends on the materials used. In this lesson, you will learn how to choose the right materials for agricultural tools. The main materials are metals, wood, and sometimes plastic or rubber. For blades and cutting edges, you need steel that can be hardened. High-carbon steel is best because it can be sharpened and holds an edge. Old car springs, files, and leaf springs are good sources of high-carbon steel. Mild steel is softer and not good for cutting edges, but it can be used for handles or brackets. To test if a piece of steel is high-carbon, touch it to a grinding wheel. If it gives off long, yellow sparks, it is high-carbon. If the sparks are short and red, it is mild steel. For handles, you need strong, straight-grained wood that does not split easily. Good choices include hickory, ash, and locally available hardwoods like mahogany or mvule. Avoid softwoods like pine; they break easily. The wood must be dry to prevent shrinking and loosening. You can also use metal or fibreglass handles, but wood is traditional and easy to replace. For fasteners, use bolts, rivets, or welding. Bolts allow you to replace parts. Rivets are permanent. Welding is strong but requires skill. For the connection between blade and handle, a tang that goes into the handle is common. It should be long enough to prevent wobbling. You can also use a socket or a ferrule. When selecting materials, consider the local climate. In wet areas, wood rots quickly, so treat it with oil or paint. In dry areas, wood cracks, so choose a species that resists cracking. Also, consider the cost. Recycled materials are cheap and good for the environment. For example, an old hoe blade can be reforged into a new tool. Always inspect materials for cracks, rust, or defects. A weak material will fail when you need it most. By choosing wisely, you make tools that last. In the next lesson, you will learn how to organize your workshop for efficient work.

6. Workshop Organization and Workflow

A well-organized workshop saves time and prevents accidents. In this lesson, you will learn how to set up your workshop and plan your work. First, choose a location. It should be dry, well-lit, and have good ventilation. If possible, have a separate area for dirty work like grinding and welding, and a clean area for measuring and assembly. The floor should be concrete or packed earth, not slippery. Second, arrange your tools and materials. Keep frequently used tools on a wall board or in a rack within easy reach. Store materials by type and size. Label shelves or bins. Keep a clear space for assembly. Have a sturdy workbench. A good height is 900 mm from the floor. The bench should have a vice mounted securely. Third, organize your workflow. When making a tool, follow a logical sequence: measure, mark, cut, shape, drill, assemble, and finish. Keep the work area clean as you go. Put tools back after use. This prevents losing them and keeps the area safe. Fourth, manage waste. Have separate containers for metal scraps, wood scraps, and rubbish. Metal scraps can be recycled or used for small parts. Fifth, maintain your equipment. Check power tools before use. Lubricate machines. Replace worn parts. Sixth, plan your day. Write a list of tasks. Prioritize. If you are making many tools, do similar tasks together, like cutting all the blades first, then shaping them. This is called batch production and it saves time. Seventh, think about safety in your layout. Keep fire extinguishers and first aid kits accessible. Ensure good lighting to avoid mistakes. Eighth, keep a small inventory of common materials so you don't run out. By organizing your workshop, you can work faster and safer. A clean, well-ordered workshop is a sign of a professional tool maker. Practice these habits daily.

â  SAFETY WARNING â HEAT TREATMENT AND FORGE HAZARDS

Quenching hot steel in oil or water produces violent steam and can cause flash fires. Always quench in a well-ventilated area away from combustible materials, wear a full-face shield and leather apron, and never use wet hands or tools around molten metal. In many African workshops, open fires and charcoal forges produce carbon monoxide â never work in an enclosed space without ventilation. Ensure fire extinguishers, sand buckets, and a first-aid kit are within 3 metres of the forge at all times. Hearing protection is mandatory when striking anvil work; sustained noise above 85 dB causes permanent hearing loss.

Workshop Layout and Workflow

An efficient workshop minimizes walking distance and material handling. Arrange your workstations in a linear flow: raw material storage â cutting â forging â grinding â heat treatment â finishing â assembly â finished goods storage. Keep the forge at least 3 metres from flammable materials and position the anvil so the smith has clear striking space on all sides. A simple lean-to structure with open sides works well in tropical climates, but ensure a solid floor (concrete or compacted earth) to reduce dust and improve footing.

For a two-person operation, a workshop of 4 m Ã 6 m is sufficient. Reserve one wall for tool storage (pegs and racks), one corner for the forge and quench tank, and one bench for finishing and assembly. Keep a dedicated area for customer orders and finished stock so that production and sales do not interfere with each other.

  • Forge temperature: Forge medium and high carbon steel at 800â1,000Â°C (cherry red to orange). Avoid overheating â yellow sparks indicate burning and carbon loss.
  • Heat treatment: Harden at 780â860Â°C, quench in oil (for high carbon) or water (for medium carbon), then temper immediately at 180â300Â°C to relieve stress.
  • Handle fitting: Use hardwood (e.g., Terminalia, Combretum, or Acacia species) dried to below 15% moisture. Fit the handle so the blade eye is fully seated and wedged.
  • Edge geometry: Maintain a 30â45Â° bevel for hoes and axes; 15â20Â° for machetes and sickles. Sharpen after heat treatment, not before.
  • Quality check: Inspect every tool for cracks, incomplete welds, loose handles, and uneven edges before it leaves the workshop.
  • Safety gear: Wear eye protection, leather apron, gloves, and hearing protection. Keep a fire extinguisher and first-aid kit within reach.
  • Cost tracking: Record steel weight, fuel, labour hours, and consumables for every batch. Review pricing monthly against market rates.
