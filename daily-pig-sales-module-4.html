1. Dividing the Herd Into Groups

To sell pigs every day, you need a steady flow of pigs ready for market. The batch farrowing system divides your breeding herd into seven groups, each group farrowing one week apart. This way, you have pigs of different ages at all times, and you can sell a batch every week. But first, you must divide your herd into seven equal groups. If you have 70 sows, that's 10 sows per group. If you have 35 sows, that's 5 per group. The number doesn't have to be perfect, but try to keep groups similar in size so each week's output is consistent.

Start by listing all your sows and gilts (young females that haven't farrowed yet). You need to know each sow's age, health status, and last farrowing date. Use a notebook or a simple record card for each sow. If you don't have records, start now. Ear tags or numbered ear notches help you identify each sow. If you don't have ear tags, use colored zip ties on the ear, but change them regularly to avoid infection.

Next, decide which sows go into which batch. Ideally, you want sows at different stages of their reproductive cycle. If you have sows that are already pregnant, put them in batches according to when they are due. If you have sows that are not pregnant, you'll need to breed them in a staggered way. The goal is to have one batch farrowing each week. So, if you have 7 groups, group 1 farrows in week 1, group 2 in week 2, and so on. After week 7, group 1 farrows again. This gives each sow about 16 weeks between farrowings (115 days gestation + 3-4 weeks lactation + a few days weaning to breeding). That's a good target.

When dividing, consider the sow's body condition. Thin sows or first-time mothers may need extra care. Don't put all your best sows in one group. Spread them out so each batch has a mix of experienced and new mothers. Also, keep a boar or two for breeding. If you use artificial insemination, you'll need to order semen regularly. Write down your plan on a calendar. Mark the breeding dates, expected farrowing dates, and weaning dates for each batch. This calendar becomes your daily guide. Without it, you'll lose track and the system will fail.

2. Batch 1: Bred This Week

Batch 1 is the group of sows that you will breed this week. In the seven-batch system, each batch is bred in a specific week, so that they farrow 16 weeks later. For example, if you breed Batch 1 in week 1, they will farrow in week 17 (since gestation is about 115 days, or 16 weeks and 3 days). This week, your job is to make sure these sows get bred successfully. If you have a boar, bring him to the sows. If you use artificial insemination (AI), you'll need to order semen and breed the sows when they are in heat.

Detecting heat is critical. A sow comes into heat every 21 days, and heat lasts 2-3 days. Signs of heat include a swollen vulva, mucus discharge, restlessness, and a willingness to stand still when you press on her back (the standing reflex). Check your sows twice a day, morning and evening. When you see a sow standing, that's the time to breed. For AI, breed 12 hours after standing heat is first detected, and again 12 hours later. For natural mating, let the boar mate the sow twice, with a 12-24 hour gap.

Keep good records. Write down the date, sow ID, and whether she was bred naturally or with AI. Also note the boar's ID or semen batch number. If a sow doesn't conceive, she'll come back into heat 21 days later. You'll need to rebreed her, which will shift her to a different batch. That's okay; just adjust your calendar.

Feeding during breeding is important. Don't let sows get too fat or too thin. A body condition score of 3 out of 5 is ideal. Feed them a balanced diet with enough energy and protein. If you have access to commercial sow feed, use it. If not, a mix of maize, soybean meal, and minerals can work. Provide plenty of clean water. Stress can lower conception rates, so handle sows gently and avoid moving them around too much during breeding week.

After breeding, mark your calendar. Batch 1 will farrow in 16 weeks. That gives you time to prepare the farrowing pens. In the meantime, these sows will be in the 'bred' group. You'll move them to the farrowing house about a week before they are due. For now, focus on getting them bred. If you have a boar, make sure he is healthy and not overused. One boar can service 10-15 sows, but if you have many sows, you may need more boars or use AI.

3. Batch 2: Farrowing This Week

Batch 2 is the group of sows that will give birth (farrow) this week. These sows were bred 16 weeks ago. Your main job this week is to assist with farrowing and ensure the piglets survive. Farrowing usually happens at night, so be prepared to check on them regularly. A sow about to farrow will show signs: restlessness, nesting behavior (if she has bedding), and milk may be present in her teats. When you see these signs, stay close.

Prepare the farrowing pen a week before. Clean and disinfect it. Provide clean bedding like straw or wood shavings. If you have a farrowing crate, use it to prevent the sow from crushing piglets. If not, create a 'creep area'a corner where piglets can escape the sow. Use a heat lamp or a simple wooden box with a light bulb to keep piglets warm (they need 30-32C in the first week).

During farrowing, be ready to help if needed. Most sows farrow without problems, but sometimes a piglet gets stuck. If the sow strains for more than 30 minutes without delivering, check for a piglet in the birth canal. Use clean, lubricated gloves and gentle traction. After each piglet is born, wipe its nose and mouth to clear mucus, then dry it with a clean cloth. Dip the umbilical cord in iodine to prevent infection. Make sure each piglet gets colostrum (first milk) within the first hour. Colostrum is rich in antibodies and is essential for survival.

Count the piglets and record the number born alive and dead. Also note any stillbirths or mummies. After farrowing, the sow will expel the placenta. Remove it immediately to prevent the sow from eating it (which can cause digestive issues). Provide the sow with plenty of water and a light feed. Keep the pen quiet and clean.

Watch for problems like mastitis (hot, painful udder) or metritis (smelly discharge). If you see these, call a vet. Also, watch for piglets that are not getting enough milk. You may need to supplement with a milk replacer or cross-foster to another sow. Cross-fostering works best if done within 24 hours of birth. Make sure the piglets are similar in size. If you have a sow that farrows more piglets than she can feed, move some to a sow with fewer piglets. Always keep records: sow ID, date farrowed, number born alive, number weaned. This data helps you track performance and make decisions.

4. Batch 3: Weaning This Week

Batch 3 is the group of sows whose piglets will be weaned this week. Weaning usually happens at 3-4 weeks of age. In the seven-batch system, you wean piglets when they are about 21-28 days old. This week, you will separate the piglets from the sow and move the sow back to the breeding area. The piglets will then go to the growing pens. Weaning is stressful for both sow and piglets, so handle them calmly.

Before weaning, make sure the piglets are eating solid feed. They should be on a creep feed (a highly digestible starter diet) for at least a week before weaning. If they are not eating well, delay weaning by a few days. On weaning day, remove the sow from the pen and leave the piglets in the same pen for a few days. This reduces stress because they are in a familiar environment. Provide clean water and feed. The piglets will cry for the sow for a day or two, but they will settle.

The sow will also be stressed. After weaning, she may have a swollen udder. Reduce her feed and water for 24 hours to help dry up milk. If her udder becomes hot and painful, she may have mastitis. Check her daily. If she is fine, she will come into heat about 4-7 days after weaning. That's when you breed her again. In the seven-batch system, she will be bred in the next batch cycle. For example, if Batch 3 is weaned in week 3, those sows will be bred in week 4 (as part of Batch 4's breeding group). So, they move to the breeding group.

Keep records: weaning date, number of piglets weaned, average weaning weight. Weigh a few piglets to get an idea. A good weaning weight is 6-8 kg. If piglets are lighter, they may need extra care. Move the weaned piglets to a clean, dry pen. Group them by size to avoid bullying. Provide a warm environment (28C for the first week after weaning). Feed them a starter diet for another 2-3 weeks, then gradually switch to a grower diet.

After weaning, clean and disinfect the farrowing pen. Let it dry before the next sow moves in. This breaks the disease cycle. Also, check the sow's body condition. If she is thin, give her extra feed to regain condition before breeding. If she is fat, reduce feed. Aim for a body condition score of 3. The weaning week is busy, but it sets up the next cycle. By weaning this batch, you free up space for the next farrowing batch, keeping the system flowing.

5. Batch 4: Growing This Week

Batch 4 is the group of pigs that are in the growing stage. These are the piglets weaned from Batch 3 about 4-5 weeks ago. They are now 7-9 weeks old and weigh around 15-25 kg. This week, your job is to make sure they grow well and stay healthy. They are no longer with the sow, so they depend entirely on you for feed, water, and care.

Feeding is the biggest cost in pig production, so get it right. Use a grower diet with about 16-18% protein. If you mix your own feed, a typical recipe is 60% maize, 25% soybean meal, 10% wheat bran, and 5% minerals and vitamins. You can also add 1% limestone and 0.5% salt. Mix well and store in a dry place. Feed them twice a day, and make sure they have access to clean water at all times. A growing pig will drink 2-3 liters of water per day. If water is dirty or scarce, they won't eat well and will grow slowly.

Space is important. Overcrowding causes stress, fighting, and disease. Provide at least 0.5 square meters per pig in the growing pen. If you have 20 pigs, that's 10 square meters. The pen should have good ventilation but no drafts. Keep the floor dry. If you use concrete, clean it daily. If you use deep litter, add fresh bedding regularly. In hot climates, provide shade and maybe a wallow for cooling. Pigs can't sweat, so they need help to stay cool.

Health checks are daily. Look for signs of illness: coughing, diarrhea, lameness, or pigs that are thin or not eating. If you see any, isolate them immediately. Common problems in growing pigs include respiratory diseases (like pneumonia) and intestinal worms. Deworm every 6-8 weeks with a dewormer like ivermectin or albendazole. Vaccinate against diseases common in your area, such as swine fever or erysipelas. Consult your local vet for a vaccination schedule.

Weigh a few pigs every two weeks to track growth. A healthy growing pig should gain 500-700 grams per day. If they are not gaining, check feed quality, water, and health. Record feed intake and any treatments. At the end of this stage, when pigs reach about 60-70 kg, they will move to the finishing stage. In the seven-batch system, Batch 4 will eventually become Batch 5 (finishing) and then Batch 6 (market). So, this week is about setting them up for success. Keep them healthy and growing, and they'll be ready for market on schedule.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Setting Up the Seven Batches

In a batch farrowing system, the single most important operational decision you will make is how to divide your sow herd into fixed groups. Get this right and everything else feed planning, labour rostering, pen allocation, weaning dates, and weekly pig sales falls into a predictable rhythm. Get it wrong and you will be firefighting every week: empty pens one week, overcrowded pens the next, and no consistent income stream.

This reference module assumes a seven-batch system with a three-week batch interval. That means each batch farrows once every 21 weeks, and every week of the year a different batch is passing through a different stage of production. The result is a continuous, weekly flow of pigs to market the foundation of reliable cash flow for an African piggery.

Why Seven Batches?

Seven batches at three-week intervals gives a 21-week cycle (7 3 = 21). Gestation is roughly 16 weeks (114115 days), lactation is typically 34 weeks, and the remaining time covers weaning-to-service and grow-out. Seven batches align neatly with this biology while smoothing labour and sales across every week of the calendar.

Dividing the Herd Into Groups

Start by counting your breeding sows and gilts that are currently productive. If you run 70 sows, seven batches means ten sows per batch. If you run 35 sows, you have five per batch still workable, but you must accept that weekly sales will be smaller and less consistent.

The key rule: batches must be fixed, not fluid. Once a sow is assigned to Batch 3, she stays in Batch 3 for life (unless culled). Do not move sows between batches to "fill gaps" that destroys the rhythm and creates empty farrowing crates.

Batch 1: Bred This Week

Batch 1 sows are the ones you serve (mated) this week. In a seven-batch system, this is the "entry point" the week a sow is confirmed bred, she is counted into Batch 1. Track service dates carefully; a missed heat costs you 21 days and disrupts the whole cycle.

Action: Record service date, boar used, and expected farrowing date (service + 114 days). Move to gestation housing. Feed a maintenance ration (2.02.5 kg/day for a 180 kg sow).

Batch 2: Farrowing This Week

Batch 2 sows are due to farrow. Move them into clean, disinfected farrowing crates 35 days before the due date. Watch for nesting behaviour, milk let-down, and restlessness signs of imminent farrowing.

Action: Attend farrowings, dry piglets, ensure colostrum intake within 6 hours, clip needle teeth if needed, and iron-inject piglets at 3 days. Keep the creep area warm (3032C for newborns).

Batch 3: Weaning This Week

Batch 3 piglets are being weaned at 2128 days. Remove the sow to a separate area (or move piglets to nursery pens). Weaning is the highest-stress event in a pig's life expect a growth check for 57 days.

Action: Provide creep feed before weaning, keep water clean and accessible, and add a weaning ration with high digestibility. Sows return to the breeding group for re-service in 47 days.

Batch 4: Growing This Week

Batch 4 pigs are in the grower phase (roughly 1016 weeks old, 3060 kg). This is where feed conversion and daily gain determine your profit. Monitor feed intake, water, and signs of respiratory or digestive disease.

Action: Weigh a sample of pigs weekly, adjust feed ration, and check ventilation. In African conditions, heat stress in the growing phase can cut daily gain by 1520% provide shade, wallows, or sprinklers.

  • One batch weaning (Batch 3)
  • One batch breeding (Batch 1)
  • One batch growing (Batch 4)
  • Three batches in gestation or finishing (Batches 5, 6, 7)
