1. Testing Compost Maturity: Simple Field Tests

Maturity means your compost is stable and won't harm plants. Immature compost continues to heat, robbing soil of oxygen and nitrogen. To test, first do the bag test: seal a handful of moist compost in a clear plastic bag. Leave it in a warm spot for 3 days. If it smells sour or turns slimy, it's not ready. If it smells earthy and looks the same, it's likely mature. Second, the jar test: fill a jar halfway with compost, top with water, shake, and let settle for 30 minutes. Good compost has a dark brown layer on top, a clear middle layer, and a small sediment layer. Cloudy water means immature. Third, the germination test: plant 10 radish or bean seeds in a tray of your compost, and 10 in regular soil as a control. Keep moist. After 7 days, count sprouts. If compost sprouts are less than 80% of the control, wait 2 more weeks. Fourth, the temperature test: fill a bucket with compost, cover, and check temperature daily for 3 days. If it stays above 40Â°C, it's still active. Mature compost stays below 35Â°C. Finally, the smell test: mature compost smells like forest floor, not like ammonia or rotten eggs. In Africa, where temperatures are high, compost can mature in 4-6 weeks if turned regularly. Always test before using on seedlings; mature compost is dark, crumbly, and cool. If unsure, let it cure for another 2 weeks. These simple tests save you from crop failure.

2. Avoiding Contaminants: Plastics, Chemicals, and Heavy Metals

Contaminants can ruin your compost and your soil. Plastics are the most common problem. Never add plastic bags, bottles, or wrappers. They don't decompose and release toxins when burned. Before composting, sort your materials. Use a fork to remove any plastic pieces. If you use municipal waste, screen it through a 1 cm mesh sieve. Chemicals like pesticides, herbicides, and bleach kill the microbes that break down compost. Don't add grass clippings from lawns treated with weed killer within 3 weeks. Don't add sawdust from treated wood, which contains arsenic or copper. Heavy metals like lead, cadmium, and mercury come from batteries, old paint, and industrial waste. These accumulate in soil and enter crops. Test your compost if you suspect contamination. Simple test kits are available from agricultural extension offices. In Africa, a common source is car batteries or electronic waste. Keep these out of your compost pile. Also avoid adding meat, dairy, and oily foods, which attract pests and create odors. If you use manure, ensure it's from animals not fed antibiotics or growth hormones. Compost contaminated with heavy metals can't be fixed; you must discard it. Prevention is key: know your inputs. Keep a separate bin for questionable materials. Train your family or workers to recognize and remove contaminants. A clean compost pile produces safe fertilizer for vegetables and fruits.

3. Managing Odors and Pests in Your Compost

Odors mean your compost is too wet, too compacted, or has too much nitrogen. A healthy pile smells earthy. If it smells like rotten eggs, it's anaerobic. Fix by turning the pile to add oxygen. If it smells like ammonia, add carbon-rich materials like dry leaves, straw, or sawdust. Aim for a carbon-to-nitrogen ratio of 25:1 to 30:1. For every bucket of green material (kitchen scraps, grass), add two buckets of brown material. Moisture should be like a wrung-out sponge. If you squeeze and water drips, it's too wet; add dry material. If it's dusty, add water. Pests like flies, rodents, and cockroaches are attracted to exposed food scraps. Bury food scraps 15 cm deep in the center of the pile. Use a closed bin with a lid. In Africa, ants can be a problem if the pile is too dry. Moisten it. For rodents, avoid adding meat, bones, or oily foods. If pests persist, sprinkle wood ash or diatomaceous earth around the pile. Turn the pile every 3-5 days to disrupt pest breeding. A well-managed pile heats up to 55-65Â°C, which kills fly eggs and pathogens. If you see maggots, add lime or turn more often. Keep the area clean; don't leave piles of fresh waste. A secure bin with a tight-fitting lid and a base of wire mesh prevents rodents from burrowing. Regularly check for signs of pests and act quickly. Odor and pest control are signs of a healthy, active compost process.

4. Moisture and Temperature Monitoring

Moisture and temperature are the two most important factors for fast, clean composting. Ideal moisture is 50-60%. The squeeze test: grab a handful of compost and squeeze. It should feel like a wrung-out spongeâno dripping, but damp. If it drips, add dry browns like straw or sawdust. If it crumbles, add water while turning. In dry African climates, you may need to water the pile every 2-3 days. Cover it with a tarp or banana leaves to reduce evaporation. Temperature indicates microbial activity. Use a compost thermometer with a 50 cm probe, or a long metal rod. Push it into the center of the pile. In the first week, temperature should reach 55-65Â°C. This kills weed seeds and pathogens. If it's below 40Â°C, the pile is too small or too dry. Add more material or water. If it's above 70Â°C, turn it to cool it down; extreme heat kills beneficial microbes. Turn the pile when temperature drops below 40Â°C, usually every 3-5 days. After 3-4 weeks, temperature will stabilize around 30-35Â°C, meaning it's maturing. In hot climates, compost can overheat; monitor daily. In cooler highlands, it may take longer. Keep a record of temperature and moisture each day. A simple notebook works. Adjust as needed. Proper monitoring gives you finished compost in 4-6 weeks instead of 3 months.

5. Sieving and Grading Compost for Different Uses

Sieving removes large chunks, stones, and contaminants, giving you uniform compost. Use a sieve with 1 cm mesh for general garden use. For finer compost for seedlings, use a 5 mm mesh. Build a simple sieve with a wooden frame and wire mesh. Wear gloves. Sieve when compost is dry to avoid clogging. Work on a tarp to catch fine material. Grade your compost into three sizes: coarse (above 1 cm), medium (5 mm to 1 cm), and fine (below 5 mm). Coarse material can be used as mulch around trees and shrubs. It suppresses weeds and retains moisture. Medium grade is perfect for mixing into soil for vegetables. Fine grade is ideal for seed starting and potting mixes. Mix fine compost with sand and soil in equal parts for seedlings. For potting, use 1 part fine compost, 1 part sand, 1 part garden soil. You can also make compost tea: soak a sack of fine compost in water for 24 hours, then use the liquid as foliar feed. Sieving also helps you catch plastic or glass. In Africa, many farmers use woven baskets as sieves. That works, but wire mesh is more durable. After sieving, store compost in sacks in a dry place. Label each grade. Use coarse for perennial crops, medium for annuals, fine for nurseries. Proper grading increases the value of your compost and ensures you use the right material for each crop.

6. Record Keeping for Quality Assurance

Record keeping helps you track what works and prove quality to customers. Start a compost logbook. For each batch, record the date you started, materials added (type and amount), and carbon-to-nitrogen ratio. Note turning dates and temperatures. Record moisture levels. When compost is ready, note the maturity test results. Also record any problems like odors or pests and how you fixed them. This data helps you repeat success and avoid mistakes. For selling, keep a batch number and date. Buyers want to know the compost is safe and mature. You can show your log as proof. Use a simple notebook or a spreadsheet on a phone. If you sell to organic certifiers, they require detailed records. Include source of materials, especially manure, to show no contaminants. Record sieving dates and grades produced. Track yields: how many sacks of fine, medium, and coarse compost from each batch. This helps you price and plan. Also record sales and customer feedback. If a customer reports poor results, check your records to see if that batch had issues. In Africa, where mobile phones are common, use apps like Google Sheets or even SMS to keep notes. Train workers to record daily. A well-kept record system builds trust and helps you improve. It turns composting from a guess into a science.

3. Managing Odors and Pests in Your Compost

Odour and pests are the two most common reasons neighbours complain and buyers walk away. Both are symptoms of an imbalanced pile, not inevitable. The root causes are almost always: too wet, too much nitrogen, too little oxygen, or uncovered food waste.

  • Ammonia smell = excess nitrogen or low carbon. Add dry carbon (sawdust, maize cobs, rice husk) at a 2:1 carbon-to-nitrogen ratio by volume.
  • Rotten / sour smell = anaerobic conditions. Turn the pile immediately and reduce moisture below 60%.
  • Flies and rodents = exposed food waste or meat/dairy. Bury food waste 20 cm deep in the pile centre, cover with 10 cm of mature compost or soil, and never compost meat, bones or dairy in open windrows.
  • Attracting livestock = keep the site fenced, maintain a 1 m clean perimeter, and store finished compost under cover.

In dense urban and peri-urban areas of Lagos, Nairobi, Accra and Kampala, odour control is a licence to operate. A well-managed thermophilic pile should smell earthy and sweet, not offensive.

4. Moisture and Temperature Monitoring

Moisture and temperature are the two variables you can control daily to guarantee quality. Buy a 50â60 cm compost thermometer (US$15â40) and a simple moisture squeeze test.

  • Moisture target: 50â60%. Squeeze test: compost should feel like a wrung-out sponge â a few drops, not a stream. Too dry (<40%) stops microbial activity; too wet (>65%) causes anaerobic conditions and odour.
  • Temperature target: 55â65Â°C for at least 3 consecutive days during the thermophilic phase to kill pathogens and weed seeds. Turn when temperature exceeds 65Â°C or drops below 45Â°C.
  • Monitoring frequency: temperature daily during active phase; moisture every 2â3 days; record all readings in your batch log.
  • In hot African climates: cover piles with shade net or tarpaulin to reduce evaporation; water in the early morning or evening to reduce losses.

5. Sieving and Grading Compost for Different Uses

Grading is where quality control becomes a pricing strategy. The same compost can be sold at three different price points after sieving.

  • Fine grade (<10 mm): premium price. Sold to nurseries, seedling producers, horticultural exporters and for potting mixes. Free of stones, plastic and large fibres.
  • Medium grade (10â25 mm): standard field grade. Sold to maize, vegetable and banana growers for soil incorporation.
  • Coarse grade (>25 mm): lower price or re-composted. Used as mulch, erosion control, or blended back into new piles.

Sieving also removes residual plastic and stones, raising the perceived quality of the whole batch. A simple rotary or vibrating sieve (locally fabricated, US$200â800) pays back within one or two seasons for a commercial operation.

6. Record Keeping for Quality Assurance

Records are your proof of quality and your defence if a buyer complains. A simple batch log â paper or phone-based â should capture:

  • Batch number and date started
  • Feedstock sources and quantities
  • Turning dates and temperature readings
  • Moisture readings
  • Maturity test results (bag, germination, temperature)
  • Contaminant screening notes
  • Sieving date and grade yield
  • Final nutrient analysis (if available) and buyer/quantity sold

For buyers requiring certification (organic, export, supermarket), records are mandatory. They also allow you to trace any problem batch back to its feedstock â essential for continuous improvement and for protecting your business reputation.
