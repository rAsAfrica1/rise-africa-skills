1. Identifying Common PVC Pipe Sizes Used in African Homes (20mm to 110mm)

In African homes, PVC pipes come in standard metric sizes measured by outside diameter (OD). The most common sizes you will see are 20mm, 25mm, 32mm, 40mm, 50mm, 63mm, 75mm, 90mm, and 110mm. These are used for different purposes: 20mm and 25mm are for small supply lines to taps and toilets; 32mm and 40mm are for sink and basin waste; 50mm and 63mm are for shower and bath drains; 75mm and 90mm are for main soil stacks; and 110mm is for underground sewer lines and toilet outlets. In many markets, pipes are sold in 4-meter or 6-meter lengths. You can identify the size by looking at the markings printed on the pipe: it will say something like 'PVC 110mm x 4m' or 'uPVC 32mm'. If the markings are worn off, use a measuring tape to measure the outside diameter. For example, a 110mm pipe will measure about 110mm across the outside. Be careful: some older pipes use imperial sizes like 1/2 inch or 2 inch. A 1/2 inch pipe is about 21mm, which is close to 20mm but not exactly the same. Always match the pipe size to the fittings you have. If you are replacing a section, take a small piece of the old pipe to the shop to compare. In rural areas, you might find pipes sold without labels; in that case, measure the inside diameter (ID) as well, because the wall thickness affects flow. For example, a 20mm pipe with thick walls might have an ID of only 15mm, reducing water pressure. Always buy pipes from a reputable supplier who stores them out of direct sunlight, as UV light makes PVC brittle. When cutting, use a hacksaw and then deburr the edges so fittings slide on easily. Remember: the size number on the pipe refers to the outside diameter, not the inside. So a 110mm pipe will not fit into a 110mm fitting if the fitting is meant for a different standard. Always test-fit before gluing.

2. Understanding the Difference Between PVC, uPVC, and CPVC for Hot and Cold Water

PVC, uPVC, and CPVC are all types of plastic pipes, but they are made differently and used for different jobs. PVC (polyvinyl chloride) is the general term. It often contains plasticizers that make it flexible. You will see grey or white PVC pipes used for drainage and cold water. uPVC (unplasticized PVC) has no plasticizers, so it is rigid and strong. It is the most common pipe for cold water supply and drainage in African homes. uPVC is usually white or grey and comes in sizes from 20mm to 110mm. It cannot handle hot water; above 60C it softens and can burst. CPVC (chlorinated PVC) is PVC that has been chlorinated, making it resistant to higher temperatures. CPVC can handle water up to 90C, so it is used for hot water lines from geysers or solar heaters. CPVC is usually cream or light yellow and is more expensive. In many African markets, CPVC is less common and may need to be ordered. For cold water, always use uPVC. For hot water, use CPVC or PPR (another plastic). Never use ordinary PVC for hot water. If you are unsure, check the pipe marking: it will say 'uPVC' or 'CPVC' and may show a temperature rating. Also, solvent cement for uPVC is different from CPVC cement. You must use the correct cement. For example, uPVC cement will not properly bond CPVC. When buying, ask the supplier for the right cement. In rural areas, you might find only uPVC. For hot water, you can use copper or PPR if available. But for most cold water plumbing, uPVC is your best choice. It is cheap, easy to cut, and lasts for decades if installed correctly. Remember: uPVC and PVC are often used interchangeably in shops, but uPVC is the rigid type you want for plumbing. CPVC is for hot water only. Always read the label.

3. Tools You Need to Start: Hacksaw, Measuring Tape, and Deburring Tool

To start working with PVC pipes, you need a few basic tools. The most important are a hacksaw, a measuring tape, and a deburring tool. A hacksaw with a fine-tooth blade (24 teeth per inch) is best for cutting PVC. A coarse blade will chip the plastic. You can buy a good hacksaw for about $5 to $10 in most African markets. Always use a mitre box or a steady hand to make straight cuts. A measuring tape, at least 3 meters long, is essential for measuring pipe lengths and marking cut lines. Use a pencil or a permanent marker to mark the pipe. A deburring tool (also called a reamer) removes the burrs inside and outside the pipe after cutting. If you don't have a deburring tool, you can use a pocket knife or sandpaper, but a proper tool is faster and safer. You will also need a solvent cement applicator (a small brush), safety gloves, and safety glasses. For cutting larger pipes (50mm and above), a pipe cutter or a mitre saw can be used, but a hacksaw works fine. When cutting, secure the pipe in a vice or hold it firmly. Cut slowly to avoid cracking. After cutting, deburr both inside and outside. This step is critical: burrs can cause turbulence and leaks. Also, you need a tape measure that locks, so you can measure alone. A small spirit level helps to ensure pipes are straight. For gluing, you need clean rags to wipe dirt. A bucket of water and soap for cleaning hands is also good. In rural areas, you might not find a deburring tool, so use a file or sandpaper. But invest in one if you can; it costs about $3. Always keep your tools clean and dry. A hacksaw blade will last longer if you don't force it. Replace blades when they become dull. With these tools, you can cut and fit PVC pipes for any home repair. Practice on scrap pieces first. Remember: a straight cut and a clean deburr are the secrets to leak-free joints.

4. How Water Flows: Gravity vs. Pressure Systems in Rural and Urban Settings

Water flows in two main ways: gravity and pressure. In rural areas, many homes use gravity systems. Water is stored in a tank on a roof or a hill, and it flows down through pipes to taps. The higher the tank, the stronger the flow. For every 1 meter of height, you get about 0.1 bar of pressure. So a tank 5 meters above a tap gives about 0.5 bar, which is enough for a slow flow. Gravity systems are simple and don't need pumps, but pressure is low. You must use larger pipes (e.g., 25mm or 32mm) to reduce friction loss. In urban areas, water comes from a mains supply under pressure, often 2 to 4 bar. This pressure pushes water through pipes to your home. You can use smaller pipes (20mm) for supply lines. But high pressure can cause water hammer and leaks, so you need pressure reducers if it's above 4 bar. In both systems, water always flows from high pressure to low pressure. In gravity systems, the height difference creates pressure. In pressure systems, a pump or the municipal supply creates pressure. When designing a system, you must consider flow rate (liters per minute) and pressure. For a gravity system, use a tank at least 3 meters above the highest tap. For pressure systems, ensure pipes are rated for the pressure (uPVC is usually rated for 6 to 10 bar). Also, avoid sharp bends and use elbows to reduce friction. In rural areas, if you have a borehole, you might need a pump to fill the tank. In urban areas, if the mains pressure is low, you might need a booster pump. Always install a stop valve at the tank and at the main entry. For gravity systems, use a float valve in the tank to prevent overflow. For pressure systems, install a pressure gauge to monitor. Remember: water flow is affected by pipe size, length, and fittings. A 20mm pipe can only carry so much water. If you need more flow, use a larger pipe. Test your system by opening a tap and measuring how long it takes to fill a 10-liter bucket. That gives you flow rate in liters per minute. Adjust as needed.

5. Safety First: Handling PVC Solvent Cement and Cutting Tools

PVC solvent cement is a dangerous chemical. It contains solvents like tetrahydrofuran (THF) and cyclohexanone, which are flammable and toxic. Always work in a well-ventilated area, preferably outdoors or with open windows and fans. Never use it near an open flame or a water heater. Wear safety glasses and chemical-resistant gloves (nitrile or neoprene). Avoid breathing the fumes; if you feel dizzy, move to fresh air immediately. Keep the cement container closed when not in use. Do not smoke while working. Solvent cement can burn your skin, so wash it off with soap and water if you get it on you. If it gets in your eyes, flush with water for 15 minutes and seek medical help. When cutting pipes with a hacksaw, wear safety glasses to protect your eyes from plastic chips. Secure the pipe in a vice or clamp so it doesn't slip. Cut away from your body. Keep your fingers clear of the blade. A dull blade can jump and cut you. After cutting, deburr carefully; the cut edge can be sharp. Use a deburring tool or a file, not your fingers. When using a knife to deburr, cut away from yourself. Store tools safely when not in use. Keep solvent cement out of reach of children. Do not store it in a hot vehicle. The cement has a shelf life; check the date. If it becomes thick or stringy, throw it away. When gluing, work in a well-ventilated space and avoid inhaling deeply. Use a small brush to apply cement, not your fingers. If you spill cement, wipe it up with a rag and dispose of it properly. Do not pour it down the drain. Always have a first aid kit nearby with eye wash and bandages. In rural areas, if you cannot get proper gloves, use thick plastic bags as a temporary barrier, but get proper gloves as soon as possible. Remember: safety is not optional. A few minutes of preparation prevents serious injury.

6. Local Suppliers: Where to Buy PVC Pipes and Fittings in Your Area

Finding reliable suppliers for PVC pipes and fittings is key to your plumbing work. In most African towns and cities, you can buy from hardware stores, plumbing supply shops, or building material markets. Start by asking local plumbers where they buy their materials. In larger cities, there are specialized plumbing shops that stock a wide range of sizes and fittings. For example, in Nairobi, you can go to Industrial Area; in Lagos, try Ladipo Market; in Accra, go to Kaneshie Market. In rural areas, you might find pipes at general hardware stores or agro-dealers. Always check the quality: look for the manufacturer's mark, the size, and the pressure rating. Avoid pipes that are stored in direct sunlight or that look faded or cracked. When buying, take a sample of the pipe or fitting you need to match. Ask for a discount when buying in bulk. Compare prices at two or three shops. For fittings (elbows, tees, couplings, sockets), ensure they are the same brand and size as the pipe. Mixing brands can cause leaks. If you need CPVC for hot water, you may have to order from a specialized supplier or a larger city. For solvent cement, buy from a reputable shop; cheap cement may not bond properly. Also, consider buying online from platforms like Jumia or Kilimall, but check seller ratings and return policy. For large projects, you can contact manufacturers directly, like Sika, DPI, or local brands. Some suppliers offer delivery for a fee. Build a relationship with your supplier; they can advise you on new products and give you credit. Keep receipts for warranty. In rural areas, you might need to travel to a nearby town to find a good supplier. Plan ahead and buy extra fittings, as they are cheap and you don't want to stop work for a missing elbow. Always inspect pipes before leaving the shop. Check for cracks, warping, or thin walls. A good supplier will let you exchange defective items. Remember: your reputation depends on the quality of your materials. Don't compromise.

<h3>2. PVC, uPVC, and CPVC: What Is the Difference?</h3>

<p>This is where many installers lose money. The three names sound similar but behave differently:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>PVC</strong> is the general family name. In plumbing shops it often means PVC-U (unplasticised) pipe for cold water and drainage.</li>
<li><strong>uPVC</strong> (unplasticised PVC) is rigid, strong, and the standard for cold water supply and drainage. It should <em>not</em> be used for hot water above about 40C.</li>
<li><strong>CPVC</strong> (chlorinated PVC) is PVC that has been chlorinated to raise its temperature tolerance. It handles hot water up to about 9095C and is used for hot water lines, solar systems, and some industrial work.</li>
</ul>

<h3>4. How Water Flows: Gravity vs Pressure</h3>
<p>Understanding flow decides the pipe size and pressure class you buy.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Gravity systems</strong> rely on height. A tank 5m above a tap gives roughly 0.5 bar. Use larger pipes (32mm50mm) to reduce friction loss. Common in rural homes and smallholder farms.</li>
<li><strong>Pressure systems</strong> use a pump (booster, submersible, or jet pump). They can push water long distances and uphill. Use pressure-rated pipe (PN10 or higher) and check joints carefully.</li>
<li><strong>Rule of thumb:</strong> every 10m of height gives about 1 bar; every 10m of pipe adds friction loss. Undersized pipe kills pressure.</li>
</ul>

<h3>5. Local Suppliers: Where to Buy</h3>
<p>Buying smart is a business skill. In most African cities you will find PVC pipe at:</p>
<ul>
<li>Hardware shops and building material yards in industrial areas.</li>
<li>Specialist plumbing merchants (often near main markets or industrial parks).</li>
<li>Manufacturer depots (e.g., Duraplast, Flo-Tek, SAPPI, Plasco, and regional brands).</li>
<li>Online marketplaces such as Jumia, Kilimall, or local construction apps for small orders.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Buying tips:</strong> Always check the pressure class printed on the pipe (PN6, PN10, PN16). Ask for the manufacturer's certificate. Buy fittings from the same brand where possible sizes vary slightly between makers. Negotiate bulk prices; a 10% discount on a 100-pipe order is real profit.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Measure the run and choose the correct pipe size (20mm for taps, 110mm for soil).</li>
<li>Confirm cold (uPVC) or hot (CPVC) service before buying.</li>
<li>Check the pressure class (PN6 minimum; PN10 for pressure systems).</li>
<li>Cut square with a fine-tooth hacksaw and deburr both ends.</li>
<li>Dry-fit all joints before applying solvent cement.</li>
<li>Apply cement evenly, twist a quarter turn, and hold for 30 seconds.</li>
<li>Allow 24 hours curing before pressure testing.</li>
<li>Store cement and tools safely, away from children and flames.</li>
</ol>
