1. Identifying Bumpers, Fenders, and Doors for Common Models

When you work on cars in Africa, you see many models: Toyota Corolla, Nissan Sunny, Honda Civic, and pickups like Toyota Hilux. Each has different body parts. Start by learning the three main parts: bumpers (front and rear), fenders (the panels over the wheels), and doors. For a Toyota Corolla 2005, the front bumper is plastic, held by clips and bolts. The fender is metal, bolted to the frame. Doors have hinges and a latch. You can identify parts by year, make, and model. Look for a sticker inside the door jamb or under the bonnet. It shows the model code, like NZE121 for a Corolla. Use that code when buying parts. In Africa, many cars are used imports from Japan or Europe. So a Corolla might be a Japanese model. Check if the car is left-hand drive or right-hand drive. That affects mirror and door handle positions. For bumpers, note if they have fog light holes or not. For fenders, check if they have side marker holes. For doors, count the number of doors: 2-door, 4-door. Also, some doors have manual windows, others electric. When you go to a parts shop, tell them the model code and year. If you don't have the code, take a photo of the part. Many suppliers use WhatsApp to confirm. At the market, you'll see parts stacked. Learn to spot differences: a fender for a Hilux 2010 is wider than for a Corolla. Practice by visiting a local garage or parts market. Ask to see different models. Touch the parts, feel the weight. Metal fenders are heavier than plastic ones. Bumpers are usually plastic, but some older cars have metal bumpers. Doors are heavy, so bring a friend when buying. Also, note that some parts are interchangeable between models. For example, a bumper from a Toyota Vitz may fit a Yaris of the same year. But always confirm with the seller. Keep a notebook with sketches and model codes. This will save you time and money.

2. Removing and Replacing a Front Bumper

To remove a front bumper, you need basic tools: a 10mm socket, 12mm socket, Phillips screwdriver, and a trim removal tool. Start by parking the car on a flat surface. Turn off the engine. Open the bonnet. Disconnect the negative battery terminal if you are working near electrical parts. Look under the front of the car. You will see bolts and screws holding the bumper to the fender and the radiator support. Usually, there are 4 to 6 bolts along the bottom edge. Remove them with your socket. Then, look inside the wheel arch. There are screws or clips holding the bumper to the fender liner. Remove those on both sides. Next, open the bonnet and remove the bolts on top of the bumper, near the headlights. There are usually 2 to 4 bolts. Now, gently pull the sides of the bumper away from the fender. Be careful not to break clips. If the bumper has fog lights, disconnect the wiring harness. Also, disconnect any parking sensors. Once all bolts and clips are removed, pull the bumper straight off. Put it aside. To replace, reverse the process. First, connect any wiring. Then, align the bumper with the mounting points. Start with the top bolts near the headlights. Tighten them by hand first. Then, install the side screws and bottom bolts. Make sure the bumper sits evenly. Check the gap between the bumper and the fender. It should be uniform, about 5mm. Tighten all bolts snugly, but don't overtighten plastic. Test the lights and sensors. In Africa, many bumpers are aftermarket or used. Ensure the replacement bumper matches your model. If you buy a used bumper, check for cracks. You can repair small cracks with plastic welding or epoxy. Always keep spare clips. They break easily. A good tip: take photos before removal. That helps you remember where everything goes. Also, work in a shaded area to avoid sun damage to plastic. With practice, you can remove and replace a bumper in 30 minutes.

3. Aligning and Fitting a Bonnet

A bonnet, also called a hood, must align properly to close smoothly and keep water out. Start by opening the bonnet and supporting it with the prop rod. Check the hinges. They connect the bonnet to the body. If the bonnet is misaligned, first check the hinge bolts. They are usually 12mm or 14mm. Loosen them slightly, but do not remove. Then, adjust the bonnet forward, backward, or sideways. You want even gaps on both sides, about 4-6mm. Also, the front edge should be flush with the fenders. Use a helper to hold the bonnet while you tighten the bolts. If the bonnet does not close, check the latch. The latch is at the front center. It has a striker that catches the bonnet. Adjust the striker up or down by loosening its bolts. Also, check the rubber bumpers on the corners. They can be turned to raise or lower the bonnet. If the bonnet is twisted, you may need to twist it back. Two people can do this: one holds the front, the other the back, and twist gently. For a replacement bonnet, first remove the old one. Mark the hinge positions with a marker before removal. Then, bolt the new bonnet to the hinges loosely. Close it slowly to check alignment. Adjust as needed. In Africa, many bonnets are from used cars. Check for rust and dents. A rusty bonnet will not last. If you buy a new aftermarket bonnet, it may need slight bending to fit. Use a rubber mallet for gentle adjustments. Always ensure the bonnet latches securely. Test by lifting it slightly; it should not open. Also, check the safety catch. It should engage. If the bonnet flies open while driving, it can smash the windscreen. So take your time. After alignment, tighten all bolts. Then, check the gap again. A well-aligned bonnet adds value to the car and reduces wind noise.

4. Repairing Minor Dents with Body Filler

Minor dents are common on African roads due to stones and small accidents. You can repair them with body filler, also called Bondo. First, clean the area with soap and water. Remove any paint or rust with sandpaper. Start with 80-grit, then 180-grit. You want bare metal. If the dent is deep, you may need to push it out from behind. Use a hammer and dolly. A dolly is a small metal block. Tap gently from the inside. Then, clean again with a degreaser. Mix the body filler according to the instructions. Usually, you mix the filler with a hardener (cream) in a 50:1 ratio. Use a plastic spreader. Mix on a clean board for 30 seconds. Apply the filler to the dent with a spreader. Press it in, then smooth it. You want a slight bulge because it will shrink when dry. Let it dry for 20-30 minutes. It will be hard and warm. Then, sand it with 80-grit to shape. Use a sanding block. Keep sanding until the filler is level with the surrounding metal. Then, switch to 180-grit, then 320-grit for smoothness. You can use a guide coat: spray a light mist of black paint. Sand until the black is gone. That shows low spots. If you find low spots, apply more filler. After sanding, clean with a tack cloth. Then, apply primer. Use a filler primer to fill small scratches. Sand primer with 600-grit. Then, paint. In Africa, you can buy body filler at most auto parts shops. A 1kg tin costs about $10. Hardener is included. Always wear a mask when sanding and mixing. The dust is harmful. Also, work in a well-ventilated area. For small dents, you can also use a heat gun and compressed air to pop them out, but that works only on plastic bumpers. For metal, body filler is best. Practice on an old panel first. With patience, you can make a dent disappear.

5. Installing Side Mirrors and Door Handles

Side mirrors and door handles are easy to replace. Start with the side mirror. Most mirrors are held by 3 screws or bolts inside the door panel. First, remove the interior door panel. Use a trim tool to pop the clips. Then, disconnect the mirror wiring if it is electric. There is a connector behind the panel. Remove the screws holding the mirror. Pull the mirror out. For a manual mirror, there is a cable or rod. Disconnect it. To install, connect the wiring or cable first. Then, position the new mirror and tighten the screws. Test the adjustment. For electric mirrors, check that the switch works. In Africa, many used mirrors are available. Ensure the mirror glass is not cracked. Also, check the color. You can paint it to match. For door handles, both interior and exterior. Exterior door handle: remove the door panel. You will see a rod connecting the handle to the latch. Unclip the rod. Remove the two bolts holding the handle. Pull it out. Install the new handle in reverse. Make sure the rod is adjusted so the door opens smoothly. Interior door handle: it is held by screws or clips. Remove the panel, then unscrew the handle. Some handles have a cable. Reconnect it. Test the door from inside and outside. If the door does not open, adjust the rod. In Africa, door handles break often because of heat and use. Plastic handles become brittle. You can buy metal aftermarket handles for some models. They last longer. Also, keep spare clips. When removing the door panel, the clips often break. You can buy a bag of 20 clips for $2. Work carefully. Use a screwdriver to pry, but wrap it with tape to avoid scratching. Also, note that some cars have manual windows. The window crank must be removed first. It has a clip behind it. Use a rag to pull the clip. Then, the panel comes off. With practice, you can replace a mirror or handle in 20 minutes. Always test all functions before putting the panel back.

6. Sourcing Body Panels from Local Suppliers

Sourcing body panels in Africa can be challenging, but with the right approach, you can find good parts. Start by knowing your local market. In many cities, there are designated auto parts markets, like in Lagos, Nairobi, or Johannesburg. Visit these markets regularly. Build relationships with suppliers. Tell them your needs. Many suppliers have WhatsApp groups where they post new stock. Join those groups. When looking for a panel, specify the make, model, year, and model code. For example, a front fender for a Toyota Corolla 2007. Ask for both new and used options. Used panels are cheaper but may have rust or dents. Inspect carefully. Check for rust along edges, especially the bottom. Tap the panel; it should sound solid. Look for signs of previous repairs, like welding or filler. New aftermarket panels are often made in China or Taiwan. They are cheaper but may not fit perfectly. You may need to adjust them. Original panels from the dealer are expensive and often not available for older cars. So, used is common. Also, consider local fabricators. Some workshops can make panels from sheet metal. They can copy your old panel. This is good for rare models. But it takes time and skill. When buying, negotiate the price. Prices vary widely. A used fender for a Corolla might cost $30-$50. A new one might be $80-$120. Always ask for a warranty, even if it's just a few days. Test fit the panel before painting. If it doesn't fit, return it. Also, check that the panel includes mounting holes. Some aftermarket panels lack holes, so you must drill them. That is extra work. Shipping from abroad is possible but expensive and slow. So, local sourcing is best. Keep a list of reliable suppliers. Share contacts with other mechanics. Also, consider buying a whole parts car if you need many panels. That can be cost-effective. Finally, be patient. Sometimes you must wait for the right part. Don't rush and buy a poor-quality panel. It will cost you more in the long run.

<h1>Practical Reference: Body Parts and Panel Assembly</h1>
<p><strong>Module:</strong> Body Parts and Panel Assembly &nbsp;|&nbsp; <strong>Course:</strong> Auto Parts &amp; Assembly &nbsp;|&nbsp; <strong>Programme:</strong> Rise Africa Skills</p>

<p>This practical reference is written for the African workshop owner, technician, and apprentice who needs to identify, remove, replace, align, and source body panels for the most common vehicle models on African roads Toyota Corolla, Toyota Hilux, Nissan NP200, Isuzu KB/D-Max, and Toyota Hiace. It is business-first: every procedure is framed around doing the job faster, avoiding comebacks, and protecting margin. Body work is one of the highest-margin services in an African workshop because labour is skill-intensive, parts are locally available, and insurance and fleet work is growing. Get panel assembly right and you build a reputation that keeps taxis, pickups, and company fleets coming back.</p>

<h2>1. Why Panel Assembly Matters for Your Business</h2>
<p>In most African markets, collision repair is a recurring revenue stream. Taxis (matatus, danfos, keke), boda-boda-adjacent light commercial vehicles, and contractor pickups are involved in frequent low-speed collisions. Bumpers, fenders, doors, and bonnets are the first parts damaged. A workshop that can source these panels reliably and fit them correctly can turn a 2-day job into a same-day job, charge a fair labour rate, and still make a healthy margin on parts. The opposite misaligned panels, rattling doors, or a bonnet that will not latch destroys trust and generates warranty comebacks that eat profit.</p>

<h2>2. Identifying Bumpers, Fenders, and Doors for Common Models</h2>
<p>Identification is the first commercial skill. Ordering the wrong panel wastes transport money and delays the customer. Use three identifiers: <strong>chassis/VIN prefix</strong>, <strong>model year generation</strong>, and <strong>physical features</strong> such as fog lamp cut-outs, side indicator position, and panel material (steel vs. plastic).</p>

<h2>4. Removing and Replacing a Front Bumper</h2>
<p>This is the highest-frequency body job in most workshops. Follow a fixed sequence to avoid breaking clips and scratching paint.</p>
<ol>
<li>Park on level ground, apply handbrake, disconnect battery if working near fog lamp wiring.</li>
<li>Open the bonnet and remove the top radiator cover or grille if it blocks bumper bolts.</li>
<li>Locate and remove all bolts: typically 24 on top, 2 in each wheel arch, and 24 underneath.</li>
<li>Disconnect fog lamp and sensor connectors before pulling the bumper away.</li>
<li>With a helper, pull the bumper straight forward to release side clips. Never lever against painted panels.</li>
<li>Transfer reusable clips, fog lamps, and grille inserts to the new bumper.</li>
<li>Refit in reverse order. Hand-tighten all bolts first, align gaps, then torque to spec.</li>
<li>Reconnect electricals and test fog lamps, indicators, and parking sensors before closing the job.</li>
</ol>

<h2>5. Aligning and Fitting a Bonnet</h2>
<p>Bonnet alignment is a customer-visible quality signal. A bonnet that sits unevenly or does not latch smoothly will bring the vehicle back. Always fit the bonnet with the hinges loosely bolted, close it gently, adjust the hinges and latch striker until the gap is even on both sides, then torque. Check the bonnet release cable and secondary latch both must work. On Hilux and D-Max, the bonnet stay and gas struts should be checked for wear before refitting.</p>

<h2>6. Repairing Minor Dents with Body Filler</h2>
<p>Body filler is a profitable skill when done correctly. The key is preparation: clean, sand, and feather the damaged area; hammer out large dents first; apply filler in thin layers, not one thick coat. Sand progressively from 80 to 240 grit, then primer. In humid African climates, allow extra curing time and avoid applying filler in direct sun or rain. Never use filler to bridge rust cut out and weld first, or the repair will fail within months.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #d9534f; background:#fdf2f2; padding:16px; margin:20px 0; border-radius:6px;">
<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0; font-weight:bold; color:#a94442; font-size:16px;"> SAFETY WARNING Body Filler and Paint</p>
<p style="margin:0; color:#7a2e2e;">Body filler and primer contain isocyanates and solvents that are harmful to the lungs, eyes, and skin. Always work in a well-ventilated area or use a spray booth with extraction. Wear a NIOSH-approved respirator (not a simple dust mask), nitrile gloves, and safety glasses. Never sand or spray in an enclosed room without extraction. Keep filler and hardener away from children, and store in a cool, dry place away from direct sunlight. In hot African workshops, fumes build up quickly open both doors and use a fan to push air outward, not across your face.</p>
</div>

<h2>7. Installing Side Mirrors and Door Handles</h2>
<p>These are quick jobs that build customer trust. For side mirrors: remove the interior door trim panel, disconnect the wiring connector (if electric), unbolt the 3 mounting bolts, and fit the new mirror in reverse. Test adjustment and heating (if fitted). For door handles: remove the trim panel, disconnect the rod or cable, unbolt the handle, and refit. Always check the door lock and central locking function after refitting. A door handle that sticks or a mirror that vibrates at speed will bring the customer back.</p>

<h2>8. Sourcing Body Panels from Local Suppliers</h2>
<p>Your margin depends on sourcing. Build relationships with at least three suppliers: one for aftermarket new panels, one for used imports (Japan/UAE), and one for OEM or genuine parts for insurance jobs. Always inspect panels before payment check for rust, previous repairs, and correct model fitment. Negotiate bulk pricing on high-turnover items like Corolla and Hilux bumpers. Keep a simple stock register and reorder before you run out. In many African markets, the difference between a profitable and unprofitable body shop is not skill alone it is reliable, fairly priced parts supply.</p>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>
<ul>
<li>Confirm VIN/chassis and model year before ordering any panel.</li>
<li>Photograph the damaged area and the new panel before fitting protects you in disputes.</li>
<li>Disconnect battery before working near wiring (fog lamps, mirrors, sensors).</li>
<li>Replace all broken clips and rusty bolts never reuse damaged fixings.</li>
<li>Hand-tighten, align gaps, then torque never torque first.</li>
<li>Test all electrical functions (lights, sensors, mirrors, locks) before handover.</li>
<li>Record parts cost, labour hours, and supplier on every job for margin tracking.</li>
<li>Keep a minimum stock of fast-moving panels (Corolla, Hilux, Hiace bumpers and fenders).</li>
</ul>
