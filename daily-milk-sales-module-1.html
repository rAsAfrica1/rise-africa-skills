Lessons

1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Milk is money that walks on four legs. Unlike a goat you sell once at the market, a dairy cow or goat gives you cash every single day. In many parts of Africa, a good dairy cow can produce 10 to 20 litres of milk per day. If you sell that milk at 50 shillings per litre, that is 500 to 1,000 shillings daily. Over a month, that is 15,000 to 30,000 shillings. That is school fees, medicine, and food on the table.

But why is milk called 'daily cash'? Because it does not wait for harvest season. Every morning and evening, you milk your animals and you have a product that people need every day. Tea shops, hotels, and families buy milk daily. You do not need a big farm to start. One improved dairy cow or three dairy goats can be enough to begin. The key is to keep the animal healthy and milking steadily.

Many farmers fail because they treat dairy as a side activity. They milk when they remember, feed poor-quality grass, and sell to anyone who passes by. That is not a system. A dairy herd system means you plan: you feed, you breed, you milk, you sell, and you save. You keep records of how much milk each animal gives, how much feed you use, and how much money comes in. That way you know if you are making a profit or just working hard for nothing.

In this module, you will learn the basic tools, the step-by-step method of daily milk sales, common mistakes, safety rules, and you will do a practical exercise. By the end, you will understand why milk is the fastest way to turn grass into cash. You will be ready to start or improve your own daily milk business. Remember: the cow does not know it is a business. You must be the one who makes it one.

2. Tools and Materials

To sell milk daily, you need a few basic tools and materials. You do not need expensive equipment to start. But you must have clean, food-grade items. A food-grade bucket or milking can is the first tool. It should be stainless steel or plastic that is approved for milk. Never use a bucket that held chemicals or petrol. You need a clean cloth or filter to strain the milk after milking. A simple cotton cloth, boiled and dried, works. You also need a chiller or refrigerator to keep milk cold. If you have no electricity, you can use a charcoal cooler or a deep freezer with ice packs. In many African markets, you can sell milk immediately, but if you want to keep it for hours, cold is your friend.

For feeding and health, you need a water trough, a mineral block, and a feed scoop. A dairy cow drinks 60 to 100 litres of water per day. Without water, milk production drops fast. You also need a record book and a pen. Write down the date, animal name, amount of milk in litres, and amount of money from sales. This record is your business brain. You also need a clean milking parlour or a simple stall where the animal stands still. A rope or halter keeps the cow calm.

For selling, you need clean containers for customers. One-litre or half-litre plastic bottles or jerrycans with tight lids. If you sell to a milk collection centre, they may provide their own cans. You also need a weighing scale or a measuring jug to measure litres accurately. Customers trust you when you measure correctly. Finally, you need a simple first-aid kit for the animal: iodine for the teat after milking, and a wound spray. Keep these tools clean every day. Wash buckets with warm water and soap, then rinse with boiling water. Dirty tools spoil milk and make customers sick. Your tools are your reputation.

3. Step by Step Method

Step 1: Prepare the milking area. Tie your cow or goat in a clean, quiet place. Wash your hands with soap and water. Wash the animal's udder with clean warm water and dry it with a clean cloth. This removes dirt and helps milk let down.

Step 2: Milk gently but quickly. Use your whole hand, not just fingers. Squeeze and pull down in a rhythm. The first few squirts should go into a separate cup, not the main bucket, because they may have bacteria. Milk each teat until it is empty. Emptying the udder fully tells the cow to produce more next time. A good cow takes 5 to 10 minutes to milk.

Step 3: Strain the milk immediately. Pour it through a clean cloth into a clean container. This removes dust, hair, and insects. Then cool it quickly. If you have a fridge, put it in the fridge. If not, put the container in a basin of cold water or a charcoal cooler.

Step 4: Measure and record. Use a measuring jug to see how many litres you got. Write it in your record book. For example, 'Cow A: 12 litres morning.' Do this every milking, morning and evening.

Step 5: Sell your milk. You can sell to neighbours, tea shops, hotels, or a milk collection centre. Set a fair price. In many areas, fresh milk sells for 40 to 60 shillings per litre. If you sell 10 litres, that is 400 to 600 shillings. Sell quickly while it is fresh. If you have extra, you can make yoghurt or cheese to avoid waste.

Step 6: Clean everything. Wash buckets, cloths, and containers with hot water and soap. Rinse well. Leave them to dry in the sun. Clean tools mean safe milk and happy customers.

Step 7: Feed and water your animal after milking. Give her good hay, silage, or fresh grass, plus dairy meal. A cow giving 15 litres needs about 1 kg of dairy meal for every 2.5 litres of milk. Always provide clean water. Happy, well-fed animals give more milk tomorrow. Repeat this every day, without fail. That is the daily cash system.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Not milking at the same time every day. Cows are creatures of habit. If you milk at 6 a.m. one day and 9 a.m. the next, production drops. Fix: Set a fixed schedule, morning and evening, 12 hours apart. Write it on your wall.

Mistake 2: Leaving milk in a warm place. Warm milk spoils in 2 hours. Fix: Cool it immediately after straining. Use a fridge, or a charcoal cooler with wet sand and a wet cloth on top. In hot areas, sell within 1 hour if you cannot cool.

Mistake 3: Using dirty containers. Milk absorbs smells and bacteria. A bucket that held soap will ruin the taste. Fix: Use only food-grade buckets. Wash with hot water and no strong soap. Rinse with boiling water before use.

Mistake 4: Not feeding enough. A cow cannot give milk from nothing. If you give only dry grass, she will give 2 litres instead of 10. Fix: Give balanced feed: green fodder, hay, silage, and dairy meal. Add mineral salt. Water is the cheapest feed. Always have clean water available.

Mistake 5: Selling to anyone without measuring. If you guess litres, you lose money. Fix: Use a measuring jug or scale. Record every sale. Know your daily average.

Mistake 6: Ignoring animal health. A sick cow gives less milk. Mastitis (udder infection) is common. Fix: Wash udder before and after milking. Dry teats with iodine. If you see lumps or blood, call a vet. Treat quickly.

Mistake 7: Not keeping records. You cannot know profit without records. Fix: Keep a simple notebook. Write milk litres, feed cost, and sales. At the end of the week, subtract costs from sales. That is your profit. If it is low, find the problem.

Mistake 8: Giving up too soon. Dairy takes 2 to 3 months to stabilise. Fix: Be patient. Follow the system. Improve one thing at a time. Soon you will have daily cash.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety first for you and your customers. Milk can carry diseases like tuberculosis and brucellosis. Always boil milk before drinking or selling to customers who will not boil it. If you sell raw milk, tell customers to boil it. Wash your hands with soap before and after milking. Keep your fingernails short and clean. Do not milk if you have open cuts on your hands unless you cover them with a waterproof plaster and glove.

Keep the milking area clean. Remove manure daily. Do not let animals lie in mud. A clean shed reduces flies and bacteria. Use safe cleaning chemicals. Never mix bleach with acid. Store chemicals away from milk containers. Use food-grade sanitisers if available. Rinse everything with clean water after washing.

Handle animals calmly. Do not hit or shout at cows. Stress reduces milk let-down and can cause kicking. Use a halter and tie the cow short so she cannot step in the bucket. If she kicks, use a leg rope gently. Never kneel behind a cow. Stand to the side.

Best practice for milk quality: cool milk to 4C within 2 hours. Keep it cold until sale. Do not mix warm milk with cold milk. Use a thermometer if you can. If you sell to a collection centre, follow their quality rules. They may test for water, antibiotics, and bacteria. Never add water to milk. It is cheating and can make people sick. Never sell milk from a cow treated with antibiotics until the withdrawal period is over (usually 3 to 5 days). Ask your vet.

Keep records for traceability. If a customer gets sick, you can check your records. Also, keep your animals vaccinated. Common vaccines: anthrax, blackquarter, and foot-and-mouth. Deworm every 3 months. These steps protect your business and your community. Safe milk is profitable milk.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it is time to practise. Do this exercise over one week. You will need: a notebook, a pen, a measuring jug, a clean bucket, and access to a dairy animal (yours or a neighbour's). If you do not have an animal, visit a local dairy farm and ask to observe and help for one day.

Day 1: Set up your record book. Draw a table with columns: Date, Animal ID, Morning milk (litres), Evening milk (litres), Total litres, Price per litre, Total money. Also add a column for feed given (type and amount).

Day 2: Observe milking. If you have your own animal, milk it yourself following the steps from Lesson 3. If not, watch a farmer milk. Note how they clean the udder, how they milk, and how they strain. Write down three things they do well and one thing they could improve.

Day 3: Measure and record. Milk your animal (or help) and measure exactly how many litres you get in the morning and evening. Write it in your table. Do the same for the next three days. At the end of Day 5, add up all litres for the week.

Day 4: Calculate your daily cash. Multiply total litres by your selling price. For example, if you got 70 litres in a week and sold at 50 shillings per litre, your income is 3,500 shillings. Now subtract feed costs. If you bought 10 kg of dairy meal at 60 shillings per kg, that is 600 shillings. Your profit is 2,900 shillings for the week. Write this down.

Day 5: Identify one problem. Look at your records. Did milk drop on any day? Why? Was it feed, water, or health? Write a simple action plan: 'Next week, I will give 2 kg more dairy meal and check water twice a day.'

Day 6: Talk to a customer. Find one person who buys milk daily (neighbour, tea shop). Ask what they look for: freshness, price, quantity, or delivery time. Write their answer. This helps you sell better.

Day 7: Review and set a goal. Read your records. What was your best day? Your worst? Set a goal for next week: increase daily milk by 1 litre, or find one new customer. Write it down. Bring your notebook to the next lesson. This exercise turns knowledge into daily cash.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Daily Milk Sales in a Smallholder Dairy Herd System

Dairy is one of the few farm enterprises in Africa that generates cash every single day. A single crossbred cow producing 1220 litres per day can return more cash to a household than an entire hectare of maize and it does so 365 days a year instead of once or twice a season. But that daily cash only materialises if the herd system is managed as a business: feed, water, health, hygiene, breeding and record-keeping all feed directly into the litres in the can and the money in the hand.

This practical reference consolidates the key numbers, tools and routines you need to run a daily milk sales operation profitably. Use it alongside the other lessons in this module as a field-side checklist and benchmarking guide.

SAFETY WARNING: Raw milk can carry Brucella, Salmonella, E. coli and Listeria. Always boil milk or pasteurise before consumption. When handling animals, wear gloves during calving and abortion events brucellosis is a serious zoonosis in many African dairy systems. Never sell milk from a cow treated with antibiotics until the withdrawal period has passed (typically 35 days, check the label). Failure to observe withdrawal periods creates antibiotic residues and can cost you your market.

Quick Reference Checklist Daily Milk Sales Routine

  1. Milking hygiene: Wash hands and udder with clean water; use a separate clean cloth per cow; discard the first 23 squirts into a strip cup to check for mastitis.
  2. Feed first, milk second: Give concentrate before milking to encourage let-down; provide fresh water after milking to avoid tainted milk.
  3. Record litres per cow per milking: Use a simple notebook or app; daily records reveal yield drops that signal disease or heat stress within 2448 hours.
  4. Cool the milk fast: Filter through a clean cloth or strainer into a food-grade can; cool to below 10C within 2 hours where possible (use a water bath or shade).
  5. Test before selling: Alcohol test (70% ethanol) for acidity; lactometer for density. Reject milk that fails one bad batch can lose you a buyer permanently.
  6. Deliver on time: Cooperatives and processors have fixed collection windows; late milk is rejected or downgraded.
  7. Bank the cash daily: Separate milk money from household money; ring-fence at least 30% for feed, AI and veterinary costs.
  8. Clean equipment immediately: Wash cans and milking utensils with hot water and detergent; sun-dry upside down. Dirty equipment is the #1 cause of rejected milk.

Business-first takeaway: Milk is daily cash only if you treat every litre as a product with a customer. The cow converts feed and water into money; your job is to keep that conversion efficient, hygienic and reliable. Track litres, track costs, track cash and reinvest a fixed share every day. That is how a two-cow herd becomes a ten-cow dairy business.
