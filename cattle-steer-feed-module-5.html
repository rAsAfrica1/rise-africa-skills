🐄 Breeding Cattle and Lactating Cow Nutrition

Feeding the cow that has to conceive, carry a calf, calve down and milk: body condition arithmetic, gestation and lactation requirements, colostrum, minerals for fertility, and how to feed a mixed herd without three separate rations.

What you will be able to do after this module

  • Explain why body condition at breeding predicts conception better than any feed additive
  • Separate the low-cost early gestation period from the expensive last trimester in your feed budget
  • Explain how dry cow nutrition determines colostrum quality weeks before the calf is born
  • Calculate the daily TDN and crude protein a cow needs at a given milk yield
  • State the calcium, phosphorus and magnesium targets for gestating and lactating cattle
  • Split a mixed herd into feeding groups that match published requirement differences

Lesson 5.1~13 min

Reproductive Nutrition and Fertility

In this lesson
  • Explain why body condition at breeding predicts conception better than any feed additive
  • Calculate the energy required to move a cow up one body condition score before breeding
  • Identify the feed ingredients and deficiencies that quietly destroy fertility in a breeding herd

A cow that does not conceive costs you a full year of feed and returns nothing. Nutrition is the largest single lever on that outcome, and almost all of it acts through one visible variable: body condition at the time she is bred.

Body condition is stored energy, and the published figures let you price it. One body condition score unit is approximately 9.4 percent of body weight in energy storage. That tissue carries about 6.3 Mcal of retained energy per kg, and putting it on costs about 5.6 Mcal of NEL per kg of gain because the conversion is not free. Those three numbers turn a subjective score into a feed budget.

Worked example. A 400 kg cow is body condition 2.0 on a 1 to 5 scale and you want her at 3.0 before the bull goes in.

  1. Tissue to add: 400 x 0.094 = 37.6 kg.
  2. Energy required: 37.6 x 5.6 = 210.6 Mcal NEL.
  3. Time available: 90 days.
  4. Extra energy per day: 210.6 / 90 = 2.34 Mcal NEL per day above maintenance.
  5. Convert to feed. A concentrate at 70 percent TDN carries NEL = (0.0245 x 70) - 0.12 = 1.60 Mcal per kg. So 2.34 / 1.60 = 1.46 kg of concentrate per day.
  6. Cost. At 0.30 USD per kg, 1.46 x 0.30 = 0.44 USD per cow per day, or 39 USD over the 90 days.

Now compare that with the cost of failure. If she does not conceive, she eats for another year at whatever your cow-day cost is and weans nothing. Thirty-nine dollars spent in a defined 90-day window before breeding is one of the cheapest purchases in cattle farming. The critical point is that the window is before breeding, not after. Trying to correct condition once a cow is already in early lactation means competing with milk production for the same energy, and milk almost always wins.

Bulls are the other half and are routinely neglected. A mature breeding bull at 1,400 to 1,600 lb needs 1.8 to 2.1 lb of crude protein per day, which is roughly 7.5 to 8.5 percent of diet dry matter. That is not a demanding requirement, but two feeding traps are specific and expensive. Cottonseed meal should be limited to 3 to 5 lb per day for bulls and avoided entirely for two to three months before the breeding season, because gossypol impairs fertility; gossypol is toxic to breeding cattle and calves at 400 mg/kg of diet and above, even though lactating cows tolerate up to 1,050 mg/kg. And zearalenone, a Fusarium mycotoxin, causes reproductive dysfunction in growing heifers above 10 mg/kg of diet and affects lactating cow fertility above 20 mg/kg. Mouldy maize fed casually to a breeding group is not a small risk.

Vitamins and trace minerals matter here in a way they do not for a growing steer. Reproductive failure is a listed clinical sign of vitamin A deficiency, alongside night blindness and poor coat. A gestating 1,200 lb cow needs 34,000 IU of vitamin A per day. If your cattle are on weathered hay or dormant dry-season pasture, assume they are not getting it: beta-carotene in stored forage degrades 33 to 50 percent per year of storage, so year-one hay carries 50 to 67 percent of its original vitamin A and year-two hay only 25 to 50 percent. Brown hay is effectively vitamin A free. Selenium and vitamin E are the other reproductive pair, and vitamin E deficiency causes reproductive dysfunction as well as white muscle disease. The published optimum for immune benefit is 1 mg of selenium per 100 IU of vitamin E, with a selenium requirement of 0.10 mg/kg of diet dry matter and a maximum tolerable level of 5 ppm.

The protein floor is the last check. The lowest crude protein level used anywhere in NRC recommendations is 7.5 percent of diet dry matter. Below that the rumen microbes run out of nitrogen, fibre digestion slows, intake falls, and the cow loses condition on a full stomach. Dry-season standing grass in most of Africa sits below that floor. A cow on it is not being maintained; she is slowly being starved while appearing to eat.

Concrete African context. A 40-cow Boran herd near Isiolo, Kenya, ran a bull with the cows year round and got what it got. The manager changed one thing: he condition-scored every cow 90 days before the planned breeding window and drafted anything at 2.0 or below into a separate paddock with 1.5 kg of maize bran and cottonseed cake daily. The cost was under 45 USD per cow. Conception in that drafted group rose enough to pay for the whole exercise several times over, and no drug, hormone or additive was involved.

Working checks before every breeding season:

  1. Score condition 90 days out, not on the day the bull goes in.
  2. Pull cottonseed products out of the bull ration two to three months before breeding.
  3. Confirm a vitamin A source if cattle have been on stored or dormant forage.
  4. Confirm diet crude protein is above 7.5 percent of dry matter for every breeding female.

Lesson 5.2~12 min

Gestation Nutrition

In this lesson
  • Separate the low-cost early gestation period from the expensive last trimester in your feed budget
  • Calculate the daily protein and energy requirement of a pregnant cow and the forage needed to meet it
  • Avoid the two opposite gestation mistakes: starving the last trimester and over-fattening it

Gestation is roughly 283 days and it is not one feeding period. For the first two thirds the fetus is small and the cow is close to a maintenance animal. In the final trimester fetal growth accelerates sharply and the requirement rises with it. Budgeting the whole pregnancy at one rate is how producers either waste feed early or run out of cow late.

Start with the published requirement anchors. A pregnant replacement heifer at 1,000 to 1,200 lb in early gestation needs 1.5 to 1.7 lb of crude protein per day, about 7.8 percent of diet dry matter. Late gestation requirement is higher because of fetal growth. Gestating cattle need calcium at 0.16 to 0.35 percent and phosphorus at 0.17 to 0.20 percent of diet dry matter, magnesium at 0.12 to 0.15 percent, potassium 0.60 percent and sodium 0.06 to 0.08 percent. Vitamin A for a gestating 1,200 lb cow is 34,000 IU per day and vitamin D is 125 IU per lb of diet dry matter.

Now the energy side. Beef maintenance is estimated as NEm = 0.077 x BW(kg) raised to the power 0.75. For a 400 kg cow: 400 to the 0.75 power is 89.4, so NEm is 89.4 x 0.077 = 6.9 Mcal per day. The published table figure for a 1,200 lb cow is 6.0 to 7.0 Mcal per day, so the two agree closely enough for farm work. Treat that as the floor a dry pregnant cow must clear every single day before any fetus is fed.

Worked example: can your hay do the job? Your hay tests at 38 percent ADF and 62 percent NDF.

  1. Energy: TDN% = 88.9 - (0.779 x 38) = 88.9 - 29.6 = 59.3 percent.
  2. Convert to net energy for gain terms: NEg = (0.0257 x 59.3) - 0.315 = 1.52 - 0.315 = 1.21 Mcal per kg.
  3. Intake capacity: DMI = 120 / 62 = 1.94 percent of body weight. A 400 kg cow can eat 400 x 0.0194 = 7.75 kg of dry matter per day.
  4. Digestible dry matter delivered: 7.75 x 0.593 = 4.6 kg TDN per day, which is 10.1 lb.
  5. Protein check. If the hay is 7 percent crude protein, she gets 7.75 x 0.07 = 0.54 kg, or 1.19 lb of crude protein per day.

For an early-gestation heifer needing 1.5 to 1.7 lb of crude protein, that hay leaves a shortfall of roughly 0.3 to 0.5 lb per day. Close it with 0.5 kg of cottonseed cake at about 40 percent crude protein: 0.5 x 0.40 = 0.20 kg, or 0.44 lb of crude protein. At 0.40 USD per kg that costs 0.20 USD per head per day, about 60 USD across a 300-cow-day gestation period for a ten-cow group. That is the whole decision, and it took five lines of arithmetic.

The last trimester is where the money is and where two opposite errors are made. Under-feed it and the cow calves thin, produces poor colostrum, milks badly and takes a long time to cycle again. Over-feed it and you create a different set of problems: excessive pregnancy weight gain above about 1 lb per day in late pregnancy is a listed risk factor for ketosis and pregnancy toxemia, because a fat cow eats less after calving and mobilises fat faster. The published target is to arrive at calving in body condition 3.0 to 3.5 on a 1 to 5 scale, and to get there gradually rather than in the last three weeks.

Two safety gates apply specifically to pregnant cattle. Nitrate is the first: forage between 0.5 and 1.5 percent nitrate on a dry matter basis requires at least 2 lb of energy grain per head per day to offset the risk, 1.5 to 3.0 percent is unsafe, and above 3.0 percent must not be fed at all. Drought-stressed maize, oats and sorghum, and heavily fertilised pasture, are the usual culprits, exactly the feeds people reach for in a bad year. The second is mycotoxin: the FDA guidance level for fumonisin in breeding cattle diets is 15 mg/kg, and aflatoxin in feed should not exceed 20 micrograms per kg.

Concrete African context. In the communal grazing areas of Matabeleland, Zimbabwe, cows calve at the end of the dry season, which puts the last trimester on the worst forage of the year. Herders who set aside one small block of standing hay or a few bales of maize stover treated with urea for the last 60 days before calving, rather than spreading the same feed thinly across the whole pregnancy, get visibly stronger calves for the same total quantity of feed. Timing, not quantity, was the change.

The practical rule is simple. Spend nothing extra in the first two trimesters if the cow is holding condition. Reserve your best feed for the last 60 days. And measure condition, not appetite.

Lesson 5.3~13 min

Colostrum Quality and Milk Letdown

In this lesson
  • Explain how dry cow nutrition determines colostrum quality weeks before the calf is born
  • Plan the vitamin E and selenium supplementation window around calving
  • Identify the feeding and handling faults that block milk letdown and cost measurable litres

Colostrum is manufactured by the cow in the weeks before calving, not on the day the calf arrives. By the time you are standing in the pen with a wet calf, every decision that determines colostrum quality has already been made. That is why colostrum belongs in the feeding plan and not only in the calving protocol.

Two feeding windows control it. The first is body condition through late gestation: the published target is calving at body condition 3.0 to 3.5 on a 1 to 5 scale, avoiding gain above about 1 lb per day in late pregnancy. A cow that is thin at calving has fewer reserves to draw on and produces less colostrum; a cow that is over-fat eats less after calving and is more prone to ketosis, which reduces milk and colostrum alike. The second window is the periparturient period, defined as five weeks before through three weeks after calving, which is when vitamin E and selenium supplementation is critical for immune function.

The published anchors for that window are specific. Selenium requirement is 0.10 mg/kg of diet dry matter as a base, with dairy summaries citing 0.3 mg/kg for immune function, against a maximum tolerable level of 5 ppm. Vitamin E for growing cattle is 16 IU per lb of dry matter and for lactating dairy cattle 25 to 30 IU per lb of dry matter, with stressed calves needing 400 to 500 IU per day. The optimum ratio for immune benefit is 1 mg of selenium per 100 IU of vitamin E. Both nutrients decline in storage: vitamin E falls 2 to 3 IU per lb per year of storage, mirroring the vitamin A pattern. A cow on second-year hay through her dry period is receiving very little of either.

Worked example, costing the eight-week window. A 20-cow herd, five weeks before through three weeks after calving, is 56 days.

  1. Assume a 450 kg cow eating 10 kg of dry matter per day. Ten kg is 22 lb.
  2. Vitamin E at 25 IU per lb: 22 x 25 = 550 IU per cow per day.
  3. Selenium at the 1 mg per 100 IU ratio: 550 / 100 = 5.5 mg per cow per day. Check against the ceiling: 5.5 mg in 10 kg of dry matter is 0.55 ppm, comfortably below the 5 ppm maximum tolerable level.
  4. Cow-days: 20 cows x 56 days = 1,120 cow-days.
  5. At an assumed local price of 0.06 USD per head per day for a vitamin E and selenium fortified mineral, the whole programme costs 1,120 x 0.06 = 67 USD for the herd.

Sixty-seven dollars is less than the value of one weak calf. Note that the calculation also gave you a safety check: any supplementation plan should be tested against the maximum tolerable level before it is bought, not after an animal is sick.

Vitamin A follows the same logic. A lactating 1,200 lb cow needs 54,000 IU per day against 34,000 for a gestating cow, and colostrum is the calf's only meaningful source of fat-soluble vitamins at birth. If the dry cow ration was brown hay, the colostrum will be poor in vitamin A no matter how much of it the calf drinks.

Milk letdown is a different problem with a nutritional edge. Letdown is a hormonal reflex: the cow releases oxytocin in response to the calf, the milker or a consistent routine, and the milk moves from the udder tissue into the cistern where it can be drawn. Anything that frightens or hurts the cow blocks it. The nutritional link is hypocalcaemia. Blood calcium above 8 mg/dL is normal, 7 to 8 mg/dL is subclinical hypocalcaemia with impaired immune function, and below 7 mg/dL is clinical milk fever with recumbency and seizure risk, with the highest risk 24 to 72 hours after calving. Calcium drives smooth muscle contraction, so a subclinically hypocalcaemic cow milks out slowly and incompletely even though she looks healthy. Prevention is a dry cow ration matter and is covered in detail in Module 11 Lesson 2, but the headline is that over-feeding calcium before calving blunts the hormonal response and causes the problem it appears to prevent. Post-calving the target is 0.30 to 0.45 percent calcium in the diet, with periparturient magnesium at 0.40 to 0.50 percent because magnesium is needed for the parathyroid response to work at all.

Concrete African context. A smallholder dairy group in Nyeri County, Kenya, milking two to four crossbred cows each, found that members who milked at the same two times every day with the calf present at the start of milking consistently got more milk than members who milked whenever suited them, on identical feed. They then added a fortified mineral through the dry period at a few cents a day. The combination of a fixed routine and eight weeks of mineral cost almost nothing and was the largest production change the group made that year.

Practical checks in the calving window:

  1. Condition score at drying off and again at 30 days before calving; correct early, not late.
  2. Start the vitamin E and selenium mineral five weeks before the expected calving date.
  3. Do not over-supply calcium in the last three weeks; save it for after calving.
  4. Milk at fixed times with the same handler, and treat a slow-milking fresh cow as a possible subclinical hypocalcaemia case rather than a bad temperament.

Lesson 5.4~13 min

Lactation and Milk Production

In this lesson
  • Calculate the daily TDN and crude protein a cow needs at a given milk yield
  • Convert milk fat percentage into an energy cost per kilogram of milk
  • Decide whether an extra kilogram of concentrate is worth feeding at your milk price

Milk is expensive to make and the price of making it is measurable. Energy for lactation runs about 50 percent higher than dry cow maintenance, and every extra litre has a known energy and protein cost. Once you can calculate that cost you can stop guessing about concentrate.

The published anchors for a 1,200 lb cow are the most useful pair of numbers in this module. At 10 lb of milk per day at 3.5 percent fat she needs 12.8 lb of TDN and 2.2 to 2.5 lb of crude protein. At 20 lb of milk per day at 3.8 percent fat she needs 17.5 lb of TDN and 3.0 to 3.4 lb of crude protein. At 25 lb she needs 3.4 lb or more of crude protein. The difference between those first two points is the marginal cost of production: 10 extra lb of milk costs 4.7 lb of TDN and roughly 0.9 lb of extra crude protein. That is 0.47 lb of TDN and about 0.09 lb of crude protein per extra pound of milk.

Milk composition changes the energy cost, and there is an equation for it. Where only fat is measured, NEL per kg of milk = 0.360 + (0.0969 x fat percentage). A cow giving 3.5 percent fat milk: 0.360 + (0.0969 x 3.5) = 0.360 + 0.339 = 0.699 Mcal per kg. A cow giving 4.5 percent fat milk: 0.360 + (0.0969 x 4.5) = 0.360 + 0.436 = 0.796 Mcal per kg. The richer milk costs about 14 percent more energy per kilogram to produce. If your buyer pays a flat price per litre regardless of composition, a high-fat breed is quietly costing you money; if the buyer pays on butterfat, the same cow is an asset. Feed to how you are actually paid.

Worked example: closing the gap on farm feeds. A crossbred cow weighing 450 kg gives 12 litres a day at 4.0 percent fat. Your Napier silage tests 40 percent ADF and 64 percent NDF.

  1. Milk energy: 12 kg x (0.360 + 0.0969 x 4.0) = 12 x 0.748 = 8.98 Mcal NEL per day.
  2. Forage energy value: TDN = 88.9 - (0.779 x 40) = 88.9 - 31.2 = 57.7 percent. NEL = (0.0245 x 57.7) - 0.12 = 1.41 - 0.12 = 1.29 Mcal per kg.
  3. Intake capacity: DMI = 120 / 64 = 1.88 percent of body weight, so 450 x 0.0188 = 8.5 kg of dry matter per day.
  4. Energy from forage: 8.5 x 1.29 = 10.9 Mcal NEL per day.
  5. Maintenance takes the first share. Published figures give about 2.8 Mcal NEL per day of maintenance for a cow of roughly this size, leaving 10.9 - 2.8 = 8.1 Mcal for milk against the 8.98 Mcal she needs.
  6. Shortfall: 0.9 Mcal NEL per day. A dairy meal at 72 percent TDN carries (0.0245 x 72) - 0.12 = 1.64 Mcal NEL per kg, so she needs 0.9 / 1.64 = 0.55 kg of meal per day.

Now the business test. That 0.55 kg at 0.35 USD per kg costs 0.19 USD. It is defending roughly the last 1.2 litres of her yield. If milk sells at 0.40 USD per litre, those litres are worth 0.48 USD. The concentrate pays. Run the same arithmetic when the meal price rises to 0.55 USD per kg and the answer changes: 0.30 USD of feed for 0.48 USD of milk is still positive but thin, and at 0.70 USD per kg it stops working. This is the calculation that should drive every concentrate decision, and it takes two minutes.

Protein needs the same treatment. High-producing dairy cows above 60 lb of milk per day need emphasis on rumen-undegradable protein, with an estimated RUP requirement of 7.2 to 7.5 percent of diet dry matter and RDP to RUP ratios shifting toward 50:50 or even 45:55. Cows below 40 lb of milk per day can use 65 to 70 percent RDP effectively because microbial protein synthesis covers their needs. Almost every smallholder cow in Africa is in the second group, which means buying expensive bypass protein for a 12-litre cow is usually wasted money. Feed her rumen instead: adequate fermentable energy plus a cheap degradable protein source such as urea-treated straw or a legume forage.

Concrete African context. Members of a dairy cooperative in the Rungwe highlands of Tanzania were buying the most expensive dairy meal available for cows giving 8 to 14 litres. Working through the arithmetic above showed that at those yields the cows were not RUP-limited at all, and that a cheaper meal plus more and better Napier silage gave the same litres for a lower cost per litre. The saving was roughly 0.05 USD per litre, which on 12 litres a day across ten cows is about 180 USD a month.

The lactation curve matters for grouping and is covered in Lesson 6. The principle here is narrower: know the energy cost of the milk you are selling, and only buy the concentrate that pays for itself.

Mineral Nutrition for Reproduction and Lactation

In this lesson
  • State the calcium, phosphorus and magnesium targets for gestating and lactating cattle
  • Build a mineral supply from local sources at the correct calcium to phosphorus ratio
  • Check every mineral plan against published maximum tolerable levels before buying

Minerals are the cheapest part of a cattle ration and the part most often missing. A breeding cow that is short of phosphorus does not look sick; she just fails to cycle. A lactating cow short of magnesium does not look sick until she is down. Because the symptoms are invisible until they are catastrophic, minerals have to be managed by arithmetic rather than by observation.

The requirement table by class, all as a percentage of diet dry matter, is short enough to memorise. Growing cattle: calcium 0.35 to 0.65, phosphorus 0.20 to 0.30, magnesium 0.10, potassium 0.60, sodium 0.06 to 0.08. Gestating cattle: calcium 0.20 to 0.35, phosphorus 0.15 to 0.20, magnesium 0.12 to 0.15, potassium 0.60, sodium 0.06 to 0.08. Lactating cattle: calcium 0.35 to 0.65, phosphorus 0.25 to 0.35, magnesium 0.20 to 0.25, potassium 0.70, sodium 0.10. Sulphur is 0.15 percent for all classes and is rarely limiting because protein feeds supply it.

The ratio matters as much as the levels. Target calcium to phosphorus at 2.0 to 2.5 to 1. Getting this wrong in either direction causes trouble: too little calcium relative to phosphorus leads to secondary hyperparathyroidism, and too much calcium before calving blunts the hormonal response and causes milk fever.

Worked example: building the mineral line for ten lactating cows. Each cow eats 10 kg of dry matter per day, so the group consumes 100 kg of dry matter daily.

  1. Calcium target at 0.45 percent: 100 x 0.0045 = 450 g per day for the group.
  2. Phosphorus target at 0.25 percent: 100 x 0.0025 = 250 g per day. Check the ratio: 450 / 250 = 1.8 to 1, slightly tight, so lift calcium to 0.50 percent giving 500 g and a ratio of 2.0 to 1.
  3. Forage contributes some of both. Assume the tested forage supplies 0.30 percent calcium and 0.18 percent phosphorus: 300 g calcium and 180 g phosphorus. Shortfall is 200 g calcium and 70 g phosphorus.
  4. Dicalcium phosphate supplies roughly 23 percent calcium and 18 percent phosphorus. To cover 70 g of phosphorus: 70 / 0.18 = 389 g of DCP, which also brings 389 x 0.23 = 89 g of calcium.
  5. Remaining calcium shortfall: 200 - 89 = 111 g. Limestone is about 38 percent calcium: 111 / 0.38 = 292 g of limestone.
  6. Magnesium at 0.22 percent: 100 x 0.0022 = 220 g for the group. Magnesium oxide at roughly 54 percent Mg: 220 / 0.54 = 407 g.
  7. Salt at 0.10 percent sodium: sodium chloride is about 39 percent sodium, so 100 g of sodium needs 100 / 0.39 = 256 g of salt.
  8. Cost. At assumed local prices of 0.55 USD/kg for DCP, 0.12 USD/kg for limestone, 0.60 USD/kg for magnesium oxide and 0.20 USD/kg for salt: (0.389 x 0.55) + (0.292 x 0.12) + (0.407 x 0.60) + (0.256 x 0.20) = 0.21 + 0.04 + 0.24 + 0.05 = 0.54 USD per day for ten cows, or 0.054 USD per cow per day.

Five cents a cow a day. That is the price of the mineral programme that keeps cows cycling and standing. Compare it with the cost of one down cow or one missed conception.

Trace minerals are supplied through a premix and the numbers are equally short, in mg per kg of diet dry matter with maximum tolerable levels in brackets: copper 10 (40), zinc 30 (500), manganese 20 to 40 (2,000), selenium 0.10 (5), cobalt 0.10 to 0.15 (25), iodine 0.50 (50). Molybdenum is not essential and its maximum tolerable level is 5 ppm because it blocks copper absorption. Three antagonisms decide whether a copper supplement actually works: molybdenum above 5 ppm binds copper and causes deficiency even when supplementation looks adequate; dietary sulphur above 0.20 percent binds copper as sulphide; and zinc above 500 ppm inhibits copper absorption. High-sulphate borehole water is a real risk in many African production areas and should be counted as part of the diet.

Magnesium deserves separate mention for lactating cows on green flush pasture. Lactation needs 18 to 21 g of magnesium per day against 7 to 9 g in late pregnancy, and the practical prevention dose is 0.20 to 0.25 percent dietary magnesium or about 0.6 oz of magnesium oxide per head daily, or a free-choice high-magnesium block at 8 to 10 percent Mg. The full grass tetany picture is in Module 11 Lesson 1; the point here is that the requirement more than doubles at calving and your mineral must change with it.

Concrete African context. A dairy group in the Ankole region of Uganda was buying a single all-purpose mineral block for the whole herd. Reading the labels showed the block carried no magnesium and no selenium. Splitting into two products, a plain salt block for dry cows and a fortified mineral for milkers, cost about 3 cents more per cow per day and stopped the recurring cases of downer cows in the weeks after calving.

Buy minerals against the requirement table, check every number against the maximum tolerable level, and read the label before you read the price.

Lesson 5.6~13 min

Herd Feeding Strategies

In this lesson
  • Split a mixed herd into feeding groups that match published requirement differences
  • Apply the concentrate and forage NDF limits that keep a lactation ration safe
  • Calculate whether grouping saves more feed than it costs in labour and fencing

One ration for the whole herd is always wrong. It either overfeeds the dry cows or underfeeds the milkers, and usually does both at once. The question is not whether to group but how many groups your labour and fencing can actually support.

The size of the prize comes straight from the requirement tables. A 1,200 lb cow at 10 lb of milk needs 12.8 lb of TDN; at 20 lb of milk she needs 17.5 lb; a dry gestating cow of the same size needs roughly maintenance, and energy for lactation runs about 50 percent above dry cow maintenance. If you feed the whole herd at the milker's rate, every dry cow eats several pounds of TDN a day she cannot use, and she converts it into fat that will cause ketosis after calving. If you feed the whole herd at the dry cow's rate, every milker loses condition and litres.

The published grouping guidelines are built around stage of lactation. Concentrate as a percentage of total diet dry matter should run 50 to 60 percent in early lactation, 40 to 50 percent in mid lactation and 35 to 45 percent in late lactation. Forage NDF as a share of total diet dry matter should be 19 to 25 percent in early lactation, 25 to 26 percent in mid lactation and 27 to 28 percent in late lactation, with total diet NDF of 25 to 38 percent. The absolute floor for forage NDF is 15 to 16 percent of total diet dry matter; below that you get acidosis and milk fat depression. Early lactation needs excellent forage; mid and late lactation can use good forage. And when moving cattle between groups, the change should not exceed 10 to 15 percent difference in dietary dry matter, because bigger jumps cause production drops and metabolic upset.

One more ceiling applies everywhere: maximum concentrate is about 2 to 3 lb per 100 lb of body weight per day, and the better the forage the less concentrate you need to reach the ceiling.

Worked example: does grouping pay on a 20-cow farm? Assume 8 milkers, 5 dry cows and 7 growing heifers.

  1. Single-group approach. To keep the milkers producing you feed everyone 3 kg of dairy meal a day. 20 x 3 = 60 kg per day. At 0.35 USD/kg that is 21.00 USD per day.
  2. Three-group approach. Milkers get 4 kg, dry cows get 0.5 kg, heifers get 1.5 kg. That is (8 x 4) + (5 x 0.5) + (7 x 1.5) = 32 + 2.5 + 10.5 = 45 kg per day, costing 15.75 USD.
  3. Saving: 5.25 USD per day, or roughly 158 USD per month, and the milkers are now getting more, not less.
  4. Cost of grouping. One extra strand of fencing, a second feed trough and perhaps 30 minutes more labour a day. If labour is valued at 2.00 USD per day, the net saving is still over 100 USD a month.

That arithmetic almost always favours grouping down to about three groups. Beyond three groups the labour cost usually overtakes the feed saving on a small farm, which is why the standard smallholder answer is: milkers, dry and close-up cows, and young stock.

Where grouping is physically impossible, there is a workable compromise. Feed the forage to everyone as a common base and deliver concentrate individually at milking. A cow being milked is standing still, restrained and identifiable, which is the only moment of the day when individual feeding is free. Weigh the concentrate with a marked tin rather than eyeballing a scoop; the difference between a level and a heaped tin across a year is a large amount of money.

The other essential discipline is the transition into the milking diet. A three-week step-up onto the milking ration, using high-quality forage and a gradual concentrate increase, is the published approach, and it is the single most effective way to avoid the metabolic diseases of early lactation. Do not move a cow from a dry cow ration to a full milking ration on the day she calves.

Feed a total mixed ration only if you can mix consistently. The published quality control steps are worth copying: verify forage analysis before formulating, account for storage losses of about 3 percent for dry feed and 15 percent for high-moisture feed, ensure the mineral mix supplies all trace minerals plus vitamins A, D and E, test mix consistency weekly by calibrating the mixer, and adjust for seasonal forage change rather than running the same ration all year.

Concrete African context. A 25-cow zero-grazing unit in Kiambu County, Kenya, ran everything in one shed on one ration. Splitting the shed with a single gate into a milking side and a dry-cow-plus-heifer side took one afternoon and about 60 USD of poles and wire. The unit cut total concentrate use by about a fifth while raising average yield, because the concentrate that had been going to dry cows went to cows that could convert it into milk.

Group by what the animal is doing, not by where she happens to be standing.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. A 500 kg cow needs to gain one body condition score. How much tissue is that?

One BCS unit is about 9.4% of body weight: 500 x 0.094 = 47 kg.

2. How long before the breeding season should cottonseed meal be removed from a bull's ration?

Gossypol impairs bull fertility, so cottonseed products should be avoided for two to three months before the breeding season.

3. What is the lowest crude protein level used in NRC recommendations, below which rumen function suffers?

7.5% of diet dry matter is the minimum crude protein level used in NRC recommendations; below it microbial nitrogen supply limits fibre digestion and intake.

4. Roughly how much beta-carotene does stored hay lose per year of storage?

Beta-carotene degrades 33 to 50% per year of storage, which is why cattle wintered on old hay need supplemental vitamin A.

5. At what dietary level does zearalenone cause reproductive dysfunction in growing heifers?

Zearalenone above 10 mg/kg of diet causes reproductive dysfunction in growing heifers; above 20 mg/kg it affects lactating cow fertility.

6. Hay tests at 38% ADF. What is its estimated TDN?

TDN% = 88.9 - (0.779 x 38) = 88.9 - 29.6 = 59.3%.

7. A forage tests 62% NDF. How much dry matter can a 400 kg cow eat daily?

DMI = 120 / 62 = 1.94% of body weight; 400 x 0.0194 = 7.75 kg.

8. What is the crude protein requirement of a pregnant replacement heifer in early gestation?

Published requirement is 1.5 to 1.7 lb crude protein per day, about 7.8% of diet dry matter, for a 1,000 to 1,200 lb pregnant heifer in early gestation.

9. Forage tests at 1.0% nitrate on a dry matter basis. What should you do?

The 0.5 to 1.5% nitrate band is a caution zone: feed with a minimum of 2 lb of energy grain per head daily to offset the risk.

10. What is the daily vitamin A requirement of a gestating 1,200 lb cow?

A gestating 1,200 lb cow requires 34,000 IU per day; a lactating cow of the same weight requires 54,000 IU.

11. When is vitamin E and selenium supplementation most critical?

The periparturient window, five weeks before through three weeks after calving, is when vitamin E and selenium are critical for immune function.

12. What is the optimal ratio of selenium to vitamin E for immune benefit?

The published optimum is 1 mg of selenium per 100 IU of vitamin E.

13. A fresh cow has blood calcium of 7.5 mg/dL. What does this indicate?

Above 8 mg/dL is normal, 7 to 8 mg/dL is subclinical hypocalcaemia with impaired immune function, and below 7 mg/dL is clinical milk fever.

14. What is the maximum tolerable level for selenium in cattle diets?

The maximum tolerable level for selenium is 5 ppm; the requirement is 0.10 mg/kg of diet dry matter.

15. How fast does vitamin E decline in stored feed?

Vitamin E decreases about 2 to 3 IU per lb per year of storage, a similar pattern to vitamin A.

16. How much TDN does a 1,200 lb cow need at 20 lb of milk per day?

Published requirement is 17.5 lb TDN per day at 20 lb of milk at 3.8% fat; at 10 lb of milk it is 12.8 lb TDN.

17. What is the NEL cost per kg of milk at 4.0% butterfat?

NEL/kg = 0.360 + (0.0969 x 4.0) = 0.360 + 0.388 = 0.748 Mcal, about 0.75.

18. A cow gives 30 lb of milk a day. What protein strategy is appropriate?

Cows below 40 lb of milk per day can use 65 to 70% rumen-degradable protein effectively because microbial protein synthesis meets their needs.

19. Silage tests 40% ADF. What is its NEL value per kg?

TDN = 88.9 - (0.779 x 40) = 57.7%; NEL = (0.0245 x 57.7) - 0.12 = 1.29 Mcal/kg.

20. What RUP level do high-producing dairy cows above 60 lb of milk require?

High-producing cows above 60 lb of milk per day have an estimated RUP requirement of 7.2 to 7.5% of diet dry matter.

21. What is the target calcium to phosphorus ratio in cattle diets?

A 2.0 to 2.5 to 1 calcium to phosphorus ratio is the published target and prevents secondary hyperparathyroidism.

22. Which mineral requirement more than doubles between late pregnancy and lactation?

Magnesium need rises from 7 to 9 g per day in late pregnancy to 18 to 21 g per day in lactation.

23. Dietary molybdenum above 5 ppm causes what problem?

Excess molybdenum binds copper, producing deficiency even when copper supplementation appears adequate.

24. Ten cows eat 100 kg of dry matter daily. How much phosphorus is needed at a 0.25% target?

100 kg x 0.0025 = 0.25 kg, which is 250 g of phosphorus per day for the group.

25. What is the maximum tolerable level for zinc in cattle diets?

The zinc requirement is 30 ppm and the maximum tolerable level is 500 ppm; above that zinc inhibits copper absorption.

26. What share of diet dry matter should concentrate be in early lactation?

Published guidance is 50 to 60% concentrate of diet dry matter in early lactation, falling to 35 to 45% in late lactation.

27. What is the absolute minimum forage NDF as a share of total diet dry matter?

15 to 16% forage NDF of total diet dry matter is the floor that prevents acidosis and milk fat depression.

28. How large a dietary change is acceptable when moving cattle between feeding groups?

Changes between feeding groups should not exceed 10 to 15% difference in dietary dry matter to avoid production drops and metabolic upset.

29. What storage loss should you budget for high-moisture feeds?

Published quality control guidance is to account for about 3% loss on dry feed and 15% on high-moisture feeds.

30. What is the maximum daily concentrate allowance per 100 lb of body weight?

Maximum concentrate is about 2 to 3 lb per 100 lb of body weight per day, depending on forage quality.

Module 5 capstone

Breeding Herd Feeding Plan. Step 1: list every female in your herd and put each one into one of four groups - open and waiting to breed, early gestation, last 60 days of gestation, lactating. Record body weight (weigh band is fine) and body condition score on a 1 to 5 scale for each. Step 2: for each group calculate the daily crude protein and TDN requirement using the published anchors: a 1,200 lb lactating cow at 10 lb of milk needs 2.2 to 2.5 lb crude protein and 12.8 lb TDN, at 20 lb of milk 3.0 to 3.4 lb crude protein and 17.5 lb TDN; a pregnant replacement heifer at 1,000 to 1,200 lb in early gestation needs 1.5 to 1.7 lb crude protein, about 7.8 percent of diet dry matter. Step 3: test or estimate the ADF of your main forage and convert it to TDN using TDN% = 88.9 - (0.779 x ADF%), then calculate how many kg of that forage each group must eat to meet requirement, and check that against intake capacity using DMI (% of body weight) = 120 / NDF%. Step 4: where forage alone cannot meet the requirement, calculate the exact daily supplement in kg and price it in USD per head per day. Step 5: build the mineral line separately - target Ca 0.35 to 0.65 percent and P 0.25 to 0.35 percent of diet DM for lactating cows at a Ca:P ratio of 2.0 to 2.5:1, magnesium 0.20 to 0.25 percent for lactating cows, and check nothing exceeds the maximum tolerable levels of 40 ppm copper, 500 ppm zinc and 5 ppm selenium. Step 6: for any cow more than one body condition score below target, calculate the energy needed to correct her using 1 BCS unit = 9.4 percent of body weight and 5.6 Mcal NEL per kg of gain, and set a date by which she must reach target. Step 7: write the plan on one page with a feeding group, a daily ration, a cost per head per day and a target body condition for each group, and pin it where whoever feeds the cattle can read it.
