1. Rotating Wheat and Barley with Legumes

Rotating your wheat and barley with legumes is one of the cheapest ways to improve your soil and increase yields. Legumes like faba beans, field peas, chickpeas, and groundnuts add nitrogen to the soil. For example, faba beans can fix 100-200 kg of nitrogen per hectare. That nitrogen becomes available to your next wheat or barley crop. In Ethiopia, farmers who rotate wheat with faba beans get 20-30% higher wheat yields compared to continuous wheat.

Plan a simple rotation: Year 1: wheat, Year 2: legumes (faba beans or field peas), Year 3: barley, Year 4: legumes again. This breaks disease cycles, especially for take-all and root rots. In Kenya, rotating wheat with chickpeas reduced take-all disease by 40%. Legumes also improve soil structure with deep roots. After harvesting legumes, leave the residues on the field. They add organic matter and nutrients.

If you have livestock, you can graze the legume stubble after harvest, but don't overgraze. In South Africa, research shows that a wheat-legume rotation increased soil organic carbon by 0.2% over five years. That may not sound like much, but it improves water holding capacity and reduces erosion.

Start small: rotate one field this season. Choose a legume that grows well in your area. For example, in the highlands of Ethiopia, faba beans work well. In drier areas, chickpeas or field peas are better. You can also intercropping? No, keep it simple: rotate entire fields.

Remember, legumes need proper inoculation with rhizobia bacteria if the soil lacks them. You can buy inoculant from agro-dealers. Inoculation can increase nitrogen fixation by 50%. Also, apply phosphorus fertilizer (like DAP) at planting because legumes need phosphorus for good root growth. With rotation, you reduce fertilizer costs for the next cereal crop. This is a long-term investment in your farm's health.

2. Conservation Agriculture: Minimum Tillage and Cover Crops

Conservation agriculture (CA) saves you money, time, and soil. It has three principles: minimum soil disturbance (no-till or minimum tillage), permanent soil cover (crop residues or cover crops), and crop rotation. In Africa, CA is practiced in Zambia, Zimbabwe, and South Africa with great success. For wheat and barley, you can plant directly into previous crop residues using a no-till planter. This reduces fuel costs by up to 70% compared to conventional ploughing.

Minimum tillage means you don't plough. You only open a narrow slot for seed and fertilizer. This keeps soil structure intact, reduces erosion, and conserves moisture. In the Ethiopian highlands, farmers using minimum tillage for wheat got 15-20% higher yields in dry years because the soil held more water. You need a no-till planter or a direct seeder. If you don't have one, you can use a hoe to make planting holes, but that's labor-intensive.

Cover crops are plants grown between main crops to cover the soil. For wheat and barley rotations, you can plant cover crops like vetch, clover, or radish after harvest. These cover crops suppress weeds, add organic matter, and fix nitrogen (if legumes). In South Africa, planting grazing vetch after wheat increased the following wheat yield by 25%. Cover crops also reduce soil temperature and evaporation.

You must manage residues. After harvesting wheat or barley, leave at least 30% of the residues on the field. Don't burn them. If you have livestock, you can graze the residues lightly, but leave enough to cover the soil. In Zambia, farmers who left 2-3 tonnes of residues per hectare saw better yields.

CA requires a mindset shift. In the first two years, you might see slightly lower yields as soil adjusts. But by year three, yields improve. You also save on labor and fuel. Start with one field. Use a jab planter or direct seeder. If you can't find one, contact your local agricultural extension officer for help. CA is a long-term investment in your farm's productivity.

3. Saving Seed for Next Season

Saving your own seed can save you money and preserve adapted varieties. But you must do it correctly to maintain seed quality. For wheat and barley, you can save seed from your best plants. Choose plants that are healthy, tall, and have large heads. Avoid plants with disease or pest damage. In Ethiopia, farmers who saved seed from the best 10% of their crop maintained yields for years.

Harvest seed from the center of the field, not edges, because edges may be pollinated by other varieties. For wheat, you can save seed from the main crop. But if you grow modern varieties, they may be hybrids, and saving seed from hybrids gives poor results. Check with your seed supplier. Open-pollinated varieties like many landraces can be saved.

After harvest, dry the seed thoroughly. Moisture content should be below 12% for safe storage. You can test by biting a seed: it should crack, not be soft. Dry on a tarpaulin or concrete floor, not on bare ground. Turn the seed regularly.

Clean the seed to remove chaff, weed seeds, and broken grains. You can use a sieve or winnowing. Store in a cool, dry place. Use airtight containers like metal silos, plastic drums, or hermetic bags. Add a desiccant like dried ash or silica gel to absorb moisture. In Kenya, farmers using hermetic bags (like PICS bags) stored wheat seed for 12 months without insect damage.

Treat seed before storage. You can mix with wood ash or diatomaceous earth to repel insects. Avoid chemical pesticides if you plan to use the seed for food. Label the container with the variety name, date, and any notes.

Before planting, test germination. Take 100 seeds, plant in a pot or wet cloth, and count how many sprout. Good seed should have over 85% germination. If lower, increase seeding rate. Saving seed is a skill. Start with a small amount and learn. It gives you control and reduces costs.

4. Cooperatives and Farmer Groups for Better Prices

Joining or forming a cooperative can transform your farming business. When you sell alone, you are a price taker. But as a group, you can negotiate better prices, buy inputs in bulk, and access markets. In Ethiopia, wheat farmers in cooperatives get 10-15% higher prices than those selling individually. In Kenya, barley farmers in groups sell directly to malting companies like Kenya Breweries, cutting out middlemen.

A cooperative is a business owned by its members. You pool resources to buy seed, fertilizer, and pesticides at wholesale prices. For example, a group of 50 farmers can buy a tonne of fertilizer at 20% less than retail. You also share equipment like tractors or threshers. In Tanzania, a wheat cooperative bought a combine harvester, reducing harvest costs by 30%.

To form a cooperative, start with at least 10 farmers. Register with the government (usually the Ministry of Agriculture or Cooperatives). Write a constitution and elect leaders. Keep records of all transactions. Open a bank account.

Selling together gives you bargaining power. A group can sign contracts with buyers for a set price and quantity. In South Africa, grain cooperatives like Senwes help farmers store and market grain. You can also add value: clean, grade, and bag your grain. Graded wheat fetches higher prices.

But cooperatives need good management. Avoid conflicts by having clear rules. Hold regular meetings. Train members on financial literacy. In Zambia, the Zambia National Farmers Union supports cooperatives with training.

If you can't form a cooperative, join an existing one. Or create a farmer group for a specific purpose, like collective marketing. Even a small group of 5 farmers can share transport costs to market. The key is to work together. You gain access to information, credit, and new technologies. Start small, build trust, and grow.

5. Using Data to Improve Yields Year After Year

Data is not just for scientists. As a farmer, you can use simple records to improve your yields. Start by recording key information for each field: planting date, seed variety, fertilizer type and amount, rainfall, pests, and yield. Use a notebook or a simple spreadsheet on a phone. In Kenya, farmers who kept records increased yields by 20% over three years because they identified what worked.

Measure your yield accurately. Weigh the grain from a small plot (e.g., 10 square meters) and multiply to get per-hectare yield. For example, if you harvest 2 kg from 10 square meters, that's 2 tonnes per hectare. Record this for each field. Compare across seasons.

Look for patterns. Did you get more yield when you planted early? Did fertilizer increase yield enough to cover its cost? In Ethiopia, trials showed that applying 100 kg/ha of DAP and 50 kg/ha of urea gave the best economic return for wheat. But your soil may differ. Test your soil every 3 years.

Use a rainfall gauge. Record daily rainfall. Correlate with yield. If you see that 400 mm of rainfall gives 3 tonnes/ha but 300 mm gives 2 tonnes/ha, you can plan better.

Share data with other farmers. In farmer field schools, groups compare results. In Tanzania, farmers learned that planting in rows (instead of broadcasting) increased wheat yields by 30%.

You can also use mobile apps like FarmDrive or Kilimo Salama for record-keeping. But pen and paper work too. The key is consistency. At the end of each season, review your records. Ask: What went well? What can I change? Set a goal for next season, like increasing yield by 10%.

Data helps you make decisions. It turns farming from guesswork into a business. Start with one field. Record everything. After a few seasons, you'll see your yields climb.

6. Planning Your Next Season: Calendar and Goals

A good season starts with a good plan. Sit down before planting and create a calendar. Work backwards from your expected harvest date. For wheat and barley, the growing period is 120-150 days, depending on variety. If you want to harvest in July, you need to plant in February or March. In Ethiopia, the main wheat season is June to October (Meher), but in some areas, you can plant in February for irrigated wheat.

List all activities: land preparation, seed purchase, planting, fertilizer application, weeding, pest control, harvest, and storage. Assign dates. For example:
- 4 weeks before planting: buy certified seed and fertilizer.
- 2 weeks before: prepare land (if minimum tillage, spray weeds).
- Week 0: plant.
- Week 3: apply first fertilizer (DAP at planting, urea top-dress at tillering).
- Week 6: weed.
- Week 10: scout for pests.
- Week 16: harvest.

Set specific goals. Instead of "increase yield," say "achieve 4 tonnes per hectare on field 1." Break goals into steps. If you want 4 tonnes, you need good seed, fertilizer, and timely weeding.

Budget for inputs and labor. Estimate costs: seed (50 kg/ha), fertilizer (100 kg DAP + 50 kg urea), and labor. In Kenya, wheat production costs about $500 per hectare. Plan how you will finance it: savings, loan, or cooperative credit.

Prepare storage before harvest. Clean your granary or buy hermetic bags.

Review last season's records. What worked? What didn't? Adjust your plan.

Share your plan with your family or group. They can help you stay on track.

Finally, be flexible. Weather can change. Have a backup plan for drought or excess rain. But with a calendar and clear goals, you are more likely to succeed. Farming is a business. Plan it like one.

Practical Reference: Scaling Up and Sustainable Wheat & Barley Farming in Africa



This Practical Reference consolidates the operational knowledge from the "Scaling Up and Sustainable Farming" module into a single, field-ready guide. It is written for African smallholder and emerging commercial farmers who grow wheat and barley from the Ethiopian highlands and Kenyan Rift Valley to the North African coastal plains and South African winter rainfall regions. The focus is business-first: every practice here is tied to yield, cost, price, or risk. Use it alongside your farm records, your cooperative, and your extension officer.



1. Rotation, Conservation Agriculture, and Seed Saving



Rotating wheat and barley with legumes is the single most cost-effective way to rebuild soil nitrogen and break disease cycles. A well-planned rotation of cereal legume cereal can cut nitrogen fertilizer needs by 3060 kg N/ha in the following cereal, depending on the legume and biomass retained. In Ethiopia, faba bean and field pea are common rotational partners; in Kenya and Tanzania, common bean, cowpea, and lablab work well; in North Africa, chickpea and lentil are widely used.



Conservation Agriculture (CA) rests on three principles: minimum soil disturbance, permanent soil cover, and species diversification. For African wheat and barley systems, minimum tillage means ripping or direct seeding instead of repeated ploughing this conserves moisture, reduces fuel and labour costs, and protects soil structure. Cover crops such as lablab, cowpea, or vetch planted after harvest protect the soil through the dry season and can be grazed or incorporated. Seed saving reduces input costs but must be managed carefully: only save seed from certified or disease-free crops, and never from fields with smut, bunt, or loose smut.



2. Cooperatives, Data, and Season Planning



Cooperatives and farmer groups are the fastest route to better prices for African grain growers. By aggregating volume, groups can negotiate directly with millers, bakers, and brewers, bypassing exploitative middlemen. In Ethiopia, cooperatives affiliated with the Ethiopian Agricultural Transformation Institute (ATI) and in Kenya those linked to the Kenya Cereals Enhancement Programme have secured premium contracts for quality wheat. The business case is simple: a group selling 50 tonnes can often command 1025% more per tonne than an individual selling 2 tonnes.



Using data to improve yields year after year means keeping simple records: variety, planting date, seed rate, fertilizer, rainfall, yield, and price. Over three to five seasons, these records reveal which varieties and practices pay off on your farm. Finally, planning your next season with a calendar and clear goals hectares, target yield, input budget, labour schedule turns farming from a gamble into a business.

SAFETY WARNING Seed Treatment and Storage

Never store treated (chemically dressed) seed with food or animal feed. Treated wheat and barley seed is poisonous if eaten. Always wear gloves and a mask when handling treated seed, wash hands after use, and keep treated seed in clearly labelled, locked containers away from children and livestock. In humid African storage conditions, do not use damp containers fungal growth on stored seed can produce dangerous mycotoxins.




5. Quick Reference Checklist



  • Plan rotation at least one season ahead: cereal legume cereal, with a cover crop in the dry season.

  • Apply minimum tillage: rip or direct seed; avoid repeated ploughing that burns fuel and moisture.

  • Keep soil covered year-round with crop residues or cover crops (lablab, cowpea, vetch).

  • Save seed only from clean, certified, disease-free fields; test germination before planting.

  • Join or form a cooperative to aggregate volume and negotiate better prices with millers and brewers.

  • Record every season: variety, planting date, seed rate, fertilizer, rainfall, yield, and price.

  • Set clear goals for next season: hectares, target yield, input budget, labour calendar.

  • Review your data each year and change one or two practices do not change everything at once.
