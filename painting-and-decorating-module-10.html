1. Surface Preparation in African Climates

Welcome to Module 10, Lesson 1. In this lesson we focus on the single most important step in painting and decorating: surface preparation. In Africa, surfaces behave differently from those in temperate regions. High humidity, intense sun, dust, and temperature swings mean that poor preparation will destroy even the most expensive paint. Your reputation and profit depend on doing this correctly. Start with a proper assessment. Walk the site at different times of day. Check for damp patches, salt bloom, mould, cracks, and loose material. In coastal cities like Mombasa, Lagos, or Durban, salt air attacks walls and metal. Inland in places like Nairobi, Kampala, or Harare, the sun is brutal and surfaces dry fast. In West African forest zones, humidity stays high and paint dries slowly. You must adjust your method to the location. For new cement plaster, wait at least four weeks before painting. This is called curing. If you paint too early, the alkaline moisture will burn through the paint and cause peeling. Test the surface by pressing a damp cloth against it. If the cloth picks up white powder, the wall is not ready. You can speed up curing by washing the wall with clean water and allowing it to dry, but never use acid unless you are trained and have proper protective equipment. For old painted surfaces, remove all loose and flaking paint with a scraper, wire brush, or sandpaper. In dusty areas, dampen the surface lightly before sanding to keep dust down. Fill cracks and holes with a good quality filler. In many African markets you can buy local filler powder and mix it with water. Add a little cement for exterior cracks. Let it dry fully, then sand smooth. For metal surfaces, remove rust with a wire brush or sandpaper until you see bare metal. Treat with a rust converter or red oxide primer. In humid coastal areas, use a zinc-rich primer for extra protection. For wood, sand along the grain, fill nail holes, and seal knots with knotting solution or shellac. In hot dry areas, wood can crack if not sealed quickly. Apply a primer within hours of sanding. Dust is your enemy. In dry, dusty regions like the Sahel, cover floors and furniture with plastic sheets. Use a damp cloth to wipe surfaces before painting. In humid areas, mould is common. Wash mouldy surfaces with a solution of one part bleach to three parts water. Rinse well and let dry. Never paint over mould; it will return. For previously painted surfaces that are still sound, wash with sugar soap or a mild detergent to remove grease and dirt. Rinse and allow to dry. Sand lightly to create a key for the new paint. In Africa, many walls are painted with distemper or cheap chalk-based paint. These must be removed completely because new paint will not stick. Test by rubbing with a wet cloth. If the paint comes off, remove it with a scraper and water. For exterior walls, check for rising damp. This shows as a dark tide mark near the ground. You must treat the cause, not just paint over it. Install a damp-proof course or improve drainage. In some cases, you can use a breathable masonry paint after treating the wall. Always work with the sun. In hot climates, paint early morning or late afternoon. The surface should be dry but not hot. If the wall is too hot, paint will blister. If it is too wet, paint will not adhere. Use a moisture meter if you can afford one. Otherwise, tape a piece of plastic sheet to the wall overnight. If condensation forms underneath, the wall is too damp. Safety first. Wear dust masks when sanding, especially in areas with silica dust. Wear gloves when using bleach or solvents. In Africa, many painters work without safety gear. Do not be one of them. Your health is your business. Finally, document your preparation. Take photos before, during, and after. This protects you if a client complains later. A well-prepared surface is your best advertisement. In the next lesson, we will look at choosing the right paint for African conditions. Remember: preparation is not a cost, it is an investment. It takes time, but it saves you money on callbacks and repairs. Do it right the first time.

2. Choosing Paints and Materials for African Markets

Welcome to Lesson 2. Now that your surface is prepared, you must choose the right paint and materials. Africa is not one market. What works in Cairo may fail in Kinshasa. You need to understand the science and the local supply chain. Start with the binder. Paint is made of binder, pigment, solvent, and additives. The binder determines how the paint performs. In Africa, you will find acrylic, alkyd, epoxy, polyurethane, and cheap chalk-based paints. Acrylic is water-based and good for interiors and exteriors in most climates. It is flexible, breathable, and resistant to UV. In hot sunny areas, use 100 percent acrylic for exteriors. Vinyl acrylic is cheaper but less durable. Alkyd is oil-based and gives a hard finish. It is good for wood and metal but can become brittle in intense sun. In coastal areas, alkyd paints may chalk and fade quickly. Epoxy is two-part and very hard. It is good for floors and high-traffic areas. It is expensive and difficult to apply. Polyurethane is similar but more UV resistant. Use it for exterior metal in sunny areas. In many African markets, you will find locally manufactured paints. Some are excellent. Others are diluted or use poor quality pigments. Ask for the technical data sheet. Check the solids content. Higher solids mean better coverage. Check the VOC content. Low VOC is better for health. Check the recommended spread rate. If a paint claims 10 square metres per litre but you only get 5, it is likely diluted. Buy from reputable dealers. In some countries, counterfeit paint is a big problem. Look for security seals and batch numbers. If the price is too good to be true, it probably is. For primers, use the right one. For masonry, use an alkali-resistant primer. For wood, use an wood primer or knotting solution. For metal, use a zinc phosphate or red oxide primer. In humid areas, use a fungicidal primer to prevent mould. For topcoats, consider the sheen. Matt is good for ceilings and low-traffic walls. It hides imperfections. Silk or satin is good for high-traffic areas because it is washable. Gloss is durable and easy to clean but shows imperfections. In Africa, many clients want gloss because it looks shiny and expensive. But in hot climates, gloss can blister if applied too thick. Use two thin coats rather than one thick coat. For exteriors, use a breathable masonry paint. This allows moisture to escape. In areas with heavy rain, use a water-repellent additive. In dusty areas, use a paint that resists dirt pick-up. Some paints have silicone additives that make them self-cleaning. For roofs, use a reflective paint. In hot climates, a white or light-coloured roof paint can reduce indoor temperature by several degrees. This saves on air conditioning and is a selling point for your business. For wood, use a wood stain or varnish with UV inhibitors. In Africa, termites are a problem. Use treated wood and apply a borate-based preservative before painting. For metal, use a direct-to-metal paint if you cannot prime. But priming is always better. For floors, use a two-part epoxy or polyurethane. In dusty areas, add a non-slip aggregate. For walls in hospitals or food preparation areas, use an antibacterial paint. These are more expensive but necessary. Now, think about the local supply chain. Can you get the same paint next year? If not, you may have colour matching problems. Buy enough for the whole job plus 10 percent for touch-ups. Store paint correctly. In hot climates, store in a cool, shaded place. Do not let paint freeze, but that is rarely a problem in Africa. Stir paint thoroughly before use. Do not shake water-based paint; it creates bubbles. For oil-based, you can shake. Use the right tools. In Africa, many painters use cheap brushes that shed bristles. Invest in good quality brushes and rollers. For textured walls, use a long-pile roller. For smooth walls, use a short-pile roller. For cutting in, use a angled sash brush. For large areas, use a sprayer. Airless sprayers are fast but require masking and cleaning. In dusty areas, sprayers can clog. Always have a backup brush. Finally, consider the client budget. Offer three options: good, better, best. Explain the difference in lifespan. A cheap paint may last two years. A good paint may last five. A premium paint may last ten. Show the client the long-term cost. This builds trust and increases your profit. In the next lesson, we will cover application techniques for African conditions.

3. Application Techniques for Hot, Humid, and Dusty Environments

Welcome to Lesson 3. You have prepared the surface and chosen the paint. Now you must apply it correctly. In Africa, the environment fights you. Heat makes paint dry too fast. Humidity makes it dry too slow. Dust ruins the finish. Wind blows debris onto wet paint. You must adapt. Start with the weather. Check the forecast. Do not paint in direct sunlight. In hot climates, the surface temperature can be 10 to 15 degrees higher than the air. Paint applied to a hot surface will blister, flake, or fail to bond. Work on the shaded side of the building. Follow the sun. In the morning, paint the east side. In the afternoon, paint the west side. If you must paint in the sun, use a retarder additive to slow drying. But retarders can affect the final finish. Test first. For humidity, avoid painting when relative humidity is above 85 percent. In coastal areas, this may mean you can only paint between 10 am and 3 pm. In forest zones, you may need to wait for a dry spell. Use a moisture meter on the surface. If the surface is damp, do not paint. For dust, work in small sections. Clean the surface immediately before painting. Use a tack cloth or a damp rag. In very dusty areas, create a temporary screen with plastic sheeting. Keep the area wet with a fine mist to settle dust. But do not wet the surface you are about to paint. For application methods, you have brush, roller, and spray. Brush is best for cutting in and small areas. Roller is best for large flat areas. Spray is best for speed and smooth finish. In Africa, many painters use a combination. Start with cutting in. Use a good quality angled brush. Load the brush about one-third of the bristle length. Do not overload. Apply paint in a smooth stroke. Do not go back over wet paint. If you miss a spot, wait until it dries and touch up. For rollers, use the correct nap. For smooth walls, use a 5 to 10 mm nap. For rough walls, use a 15 to 20 mm nap. Pour paint into a roller tray. Roll the roller in the tray to load it evenly. Apply in a W pattern. Then fill in without lifting the roller. Do not press too hard. Let the roller do the work. For sprayers, use the correct tip size. Keep the gun perpendicular to the surface. Move at a steady speed. Overlap each pass by 50 percent. In dusty areas, use a sprayer with a remote pot to keep the paint clean. Always test spray on a piece of cardboard. For two-part paints like epoxy, mix only what you can use in the pot life. In hot climates, pot life is shorter. Mix small batches. Keep the container in a cool place. For water-based paints, do not add too much water. It reduces durability. For oil-based paints, use the correct thinner. In Africa, you may find locally available thinners. Test them on a small area first. For ceilings, use a roller with an extension pole. Work in sections. Do not let the edge dry before you roll the next section. For walls, work from top to bottom. For floors, work from the far corner to the exit. For exteriors, work from the top down. This prevents drips on finished areas. For textured surfaces, use a thicker paint or a texture additive. Apply with a roller or a texture sprayer. For smooth finishes, sand between coats. Use a fine sandpaper. Wipe off dust. Apply the next coat. In Africa, many painters skip sanding between coats. Do not do this. Sanding gives a better bond and a smoother finish. For metallic paints, apply in one direction. Do not over-brush. For clear finishes, apply thin coats. Build up the finish. Do not apply thick coats; they will wrinkle. For exterior masonry, use a two-coat system. First coat thinned slightly to penetrate. Second coat full strength. For metal, apply primer, then undercoat, then topcoat. For wood, apply primer, then undercoat, then two topcoats. Always allow proper drying time. In hot climates, water-based paint may dry in one hour. In humid climates, it may take four hours. Do not rush. If you apply the next coat too soon, you will get blistering or peeling. Use a fan or dehumidifier if available. But in most African settings, you rely on natural ventilation. Open windows and doors. Use a fan to move air. But do not blow dust onto wet paint. Finally, clean your tools immediately after use. Water-based paint cleans with water. Oil-based paint cleans with solvent. Do not let paint dry on brushes. In Africa, good brushes are expensive. Take care of them. In the next lesson, we will cover decorative finishes and special effects that can increase your profit.

4. Decorative Finishes and Special Effects for African Interiors

Welcome to Lesson 4. Basic painting pays the bills. Decorative finishes build your reputation and your profit margin. In Africa, there is a growing demand for unique interiors. Hotels, lodges, restaurants, and upmarket homes want something special. You can charge three to five times more for decorative work. But you must learn the techniques and practice them. Start with colour washing. This is a simple technique that creates a soft, mottled look. Use a base coat of satin or eggshell. Then mix a glaze with a slightly different colour. Apply the glaze in random strokes with a brush or rag. Work in small sections. Blend the edges. In hot climates, the glaze dries fast. Add a retarder. Colour washing is good for bedrooms and lounges. Next, sponging. This creates a textured look. Use a natural sea sponge. Dip in glaze. Dab on the wall. Use a light touch. Build up layers. Sponging is good for feature walls. It hides imperfections. In dusty areas, sponging can look dirty. Use light colours. Rag rolling. This creates a soft, striped effect. Roll a rag into a cylinder. Dip in glaze. Roll on the wall. Use a random pattern. Rag rolling is good for large walls. It is fast and forgiving. Stippling. Use a stippling brush. Dab the wall with the tips of the bristles. This creates a fine, uniform texture. Stippling is good for ceilings and small areas. It is time-consuming. Venetian plaster. This is a high-end finish. It looks like polished marble. You need special plaster and a trowel. Apply thin coats. Burnish with a trowel. In Africa, you can find local marble dust and lime. Mix your own Venetian plaster to save money. But test first. Venetian plaster is good for feature walls, columns, and reception areas. It is expensive and requires skill. Metallic finishes. Use metallic paint or metallic glaze. Apply over a dark base coat. Use a brush or roller. For a hammered look, use a sponge. Metallic finishes are good for bars, clubs, and boutique hotels. They are popular in Lagos, Nairobi, and Johannesburg. Stencilling. Cut a pattern from plastic sheet. Tape to the wall. Apply paint with a stencil brush. Use a dry brush technique. Stencilling is good for borders and feature walls. In Africa, you can create African-inspired patterns. Use geometric shapes, animal motifs, or tribal designs. This adds local flavour. Faux wood grain. Use a base coat of light brown. Then use a darker glaze. Drag a wood grain tool through the glaze. This creates a wood effect on metal, concrete, or cheap wood. Faux wood is good for doors, furniture, and beams. Faux stone. Use a sponge or rag to create a stone texture. Use grey, beige, and brown. Add highlights and shadows. Faux stone is good for exterior walls and columns. Textured finishes. Use a trowel or roller to apply texture. You can use sand, cement, or commercial texture. Texture hides cracks and uneven surfaces. It is good for exterior walls in harsh climates. In Africa, textured finishes are common. They are durable and low maintenance. Murals. This is a painted picture on a wall. You need artistic skill. Murals are good for schools, hospitals, and community centres. They can tell a story or promote a message. In Africa, murals are used for health education and branding. If you can paint murals, you can charge high fees. Now, business advice. Always create a sample board before starting. Let the client approve the sample. This prevents disputes. Charge for the sample. It is your time and materials. Price decorative work by the square metre or by the day. Do not charge by the hour. In Africa, clients may negotiate hard. Stand your ground. Explain the skill and the materials. Offer a warranty. If the finish fails, you will fix it. But only if you did the preparation correctly. Use quality materials. Cheap glaze will yellow or peel. In hot climates, use UV-resistant glazes. In humid areas, use fungicidal additives. Practice on your own walls or a friend's house. Take photos. Build a portfolio. Show it to clients. 5. Pricing, Quoting, and Running a Profitable Painting Business in Africa

Welcome to Lesson 5. You have the skills. Now you need the business sense. Many skilled painters in Africa fail because they do not price correctly. They undercharge, then run out of money for materials. They do not quote properly, then argue with clients. They do not manage cash flow, then cannot pay their workers. This lesson will help you build a profitable business. Start with your costs. You have direct costs: paint, primer, filler, sandpaper, brushes, rollers, tape, plastic sheeting, solvent, and cleaning rags. You have labour costs: your time and your workers' wages. You have indirect costs: transport, phone, internet, tools, vehicle maintenance, insurance, and taxes. You have overhead: rent, storage, and marketing. Add all these up. Then add your profit margin. In Africa, a common mistake is to only charge for paint and labour. You must charge for everything. For example, if a job needs 20 litres of paint at 10 dollars per litre, that is 200 dollars. Labour for two workers for three days at 20 dollars per day is 120 dollars. Transport is 30 dollars. Sandpaper and filler are 20 dollars. Overhead is 10 percent, or 37 dollars. Total cost is 407 dollars. Add 30 percent profit, which is 122 dollars. Your quote should be 529 dollars. Do not round down to 500 dollars. You are running a business, not a charity. Now, how to quote. Always visit the site. Measure the area. Check the condition. Ask about the client's expectations. Write a detailed quote. Include surface preparation, materials, labour, and timeline. State what is not included. For example, furniture moving, repairs, or scaffolding. State the payment terms. In Africa, a common term is 50 percent deposit, 40 percent on completion, and 10 percent retention for 30 days. This protects you if the client complains. But some clients will resist. Be firm. You need the deposit to buy materials. If the client will not pay a deposit, walk away. They are not serious. For large jobs, break the quote into stages. Get paid for each stage. This keeps cash flow positive. For government or corporate jobs, you may need to wait 30 to 90 days for payment. Factor this into your price. Add a financing cost. Or refuse the job if you cannot afford to wait. Now, managing workers. In Africa, many painters work as casual labour. You must train them. Teach them your methods. Pay them fairly. Pay on time. Provide safety gear. If you do not, they will leave or steal. Treat them with respect. They are your team. For subcontractors, get a written agreement. Agree on the scope, price, and timeline. Do not pay everything upfront. Pay in stages. Keep records. Use a simple notebook or a phone app. Record every expense. Record every payment. Record every hour. This helps you quote better next time. It also helps if there is a dispute. Marketing. In Africa, word of mouth is powerful. Do a good job. Ask for referrals. Give a small discount to clients who refer others. Use social media. Take before and after photos. Post on Facebook, Instagram, and WhatsApp. Join local business groups. Attend trade shows. Partner with architects, interior designers, and real estate agents. They can bring you work. Offer a guarantee. If you stand behind your work, clients will trust you. But do not guarantee against client abuse or natural disasters. Be specific. For example, guarantee against peeling and flaking for 12 months if the surface was prepared correctly and the paint was applied as specified. Now, legal and tax. In most African countries, you must register your business. You must pay tax. You must have insurance. This may seem like a burden, but it protects you. If a worker is injured, insurance pays. If a client sues, insurance helps. If you are not registered, you cannot bid on government or corporate jobs. So register. It is worth it. Finally, think about growth. Can you expand to other towns? Can you offer other services like tiling, plastering, or wallpaper? Can you train other painters and take a commission? Can you import paint directly and sell it? The opportunities are endless. But start small. Master your craft. Master your numbers. Then grow. Remember, painting and decorating is not just a job. It is a business. Treat it like one. You will succeed. This concludes Module 10. You now have the knowledge to prepare surfaces, choose materials, apply paint, create decorative finishes, and run a profitable business in Africa. Go out there and paint your future.

6. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Paint Product Data, Surface Compatibility & Site Safety in African Markets

This module closes the technical gap between knowing how to paint and knowing what you are actually buying. In most African markets, a painter-decorator is also a specifier, a purchaser and a quality controller. You will be handed unlabelled 20 L drums, locally repackaged tinting concentrates, and "imported" product with no batch number. Your margin, your reputation and your client's warranty all depend on reading a technical data sheet (TDS) correctly, matching the coating system to the substrate, and controlling the health and fire risks that come with solvent-based products in hot, poorly ventilated rooms.

Treat this lesson as a bench reference. Print it, laminate it, and keep it in the van next to your colour fan deck.

1. Reading a Technical Data Sheet Like a Buyer

A TDS is a legal and commercial document. If a supplier cannot produce one, you cannot warrant the work. The five fields that decide your quote are: volume solids, spreading rate, dry film thickness (DFT), recoat window, and VOC content. Volume solids tell you how much of the wet litre remains as dry film. A paint at 40% volume solids applied at 8 m/L wet gives you roughly 3.2 m/L of dry film that is the number that governs your material cost per square metre, not the drum price.

In African markets, spreading rate is frequently quoted as "one coat covers 10 m/L" without stating DFT or substrate. Always recalculate using the formula in the table below, then add 1015% wastage for rough plaster, roller texture and wind-driven spray loss on scaffolding.

2. Substrate Compatibility Matrix

Most coating failures in African buildings are adhesion failures, not paint failures. The cause is almost always a mismatch between substrate chemistry and the primer selected. New cement plaster is strongly alkaline (pH 1013) for weeks after curing and will saponify oil-based and some alkyd primers. Gypsum-based skim coats are hygroscopic and will blister under solvent-borne systems on coastlines. Timber must be sealed on all faces or it will move and crack the film.

SAFETY WARNING SOLVENTS, LEAD AND ISOCYANATES

Solvent-borne paints, thinners, two-pack epoxies and polyurethane varnishes release volatile organic compounds (VOCs) that cause acute headaches, narcosis, liver and kidney damage, and are flammable. In hot African conditions evaporation is faster and vapour concentration in enclosed rooms rises quickly.

  • Never spray or brush solvent-based products in a closed room. Force cross-ventilation with two openings minimum; use a fan extracting to outside air.
  • Wear a fitted organic-vapour respirator (A2/P2 or better) a dust mask gives zero protection against solvent vapour.
  • No smoking, no open flame, no angle grinders within 10 m of solvent work. Store thinners in a locked metal cabinet away from sunlight.
  • Two-pack isocyanate hardeners can sensitise the lungs permanently. Use nitrile gloves, eye protection and supplied-air or A2P3 respiratory protection.
  • Do not burn empty paint drums or solvent rags VOC and heavy-metal fumes are toxic. Dispose through a licensed waste handler.
  • Lead-based pigments remain in circulation in some legacy and repackaged stock. If the label does not state "lead-free", treat the product as lead-containing and control dust when sanding.

Reference: WHO Exposure to Lead: A Major Public Health Concern; WHO Indoor Air Quality Guidelines: Selected Pollutants; ILO Safety and Health in the Use of Chemicals at Work.

4. Job-Site Quality Control

Before any topcoat goes on, run four checks and record them on your job sheet: (1) moisture meter reading against Table 1; (2) pH of plaster using a surface pH pen or indicator strip; (3) adhesion test cross-hatch or pull-off on a 100 mm patch; (4) dust and contamination check by wiping with a dark cloth. If any check fails, you stop and you write it in the client file. This single habit is what separates a decorator who gets repeat commercial contracts from one who repaints for free.

Ambient conditions matter more in Africa than in temperate markets. Above 35 C, water-based paints skin over before they coalesce, giving a weak film. Below 10 C at highland sites, film formation stops. Above 85% relative humidity normal on the Gulf of Guinea coast and during the kaskazi season in East Africa water-based topcoats may not cure and will remain soft for days. Schedule exterior work accordingly and never paint in direct sun on a hot wall; follow the shade.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Demand the TDS. No data sheet, no warranty, no purchase. Photograph the batch number and drum label.
  2. Convert to dry coverage. Volume solids wet spreading rate = dry m/L. Quote from that figure, never from drum size.
  3. Test the substrate. Moisture, pH, adhesion and dust record all four before priming.
  4. Match primer to substrate. Alkali sealer on new plaster, etch primer on galvanised steel, aluminium primer on tannin-rich hardwood.
  5. Respect the recoat window. Too soon causes solvent entrapment; too late requires abrasion before the next coat.
  6. Control ventilation and PPE. Two openings minimum for solvent work; A2/P2 respirator, nitrile gloves, eye protection.
  7. Add 1015% wastage for rough plaster, roller texture and spray loss on scaffolding.
  8. Photograph and file everything before, during, after, plus the moisture and adhesion readings. This is your defence and your marketing asset.
