Lessons

1. Developing an Irrigation Schedule

An irrigation schedule tells you when to irrigate and how much water to apply. Without one, you risk overwatering (wasting water, leaching nutrients) or underwatering (stressing crops, reducing yields). Start by knowing your soil type. Sandy soils hold little water and need frequent, small applicationsmaybe every 2-3 days. Clay soils hold more but drain slowly; irrigate every 5-7 days. Loam is ideal: every 3-4 days. Next, know your crop's root depth and critical growth stages. For example, maize needs more water during tasseling and silking. Tomatoes peak during flowering and fruit fill. A simple schedule: check soil moisture by hand (dig 10 cm, squeeze a handfulif it crumbles, it's dry; if it forms a ball, it's moist). If dry, irrigate until the top 30 cm is wet. For a typical 1-hectare vegetable plot in Kenya, you might apply 20-30 mm per irrigation (20-30 litres per square metre) every 3 days. Use a rain gauge or a simple tin can to measure how much water you apply. If you have a 200-litre drum and a watering can, you can calculate: 1 mm of water = 1 litre per square metre. So 20 mm = 20 litres per square metre. For a 10 m x 10 m plot (100 m), you need 2000 litres. That's 10 drums. Adjust based on rainfall: if it rains 10 mm, skip that day's irrigation. Write your schedule on a calendar or phone reminder. Review weeklycrops change, weather changes. A good schedule saves water, time, and money.

2. Using Evapotranspiration Data for Scheduling

Evapotranspiration (ET) is the combined water lost from soil evaporation and plant transpiration. Knowing ET helps you replace exactly what the crop uses. In Africa, many weather stations and online tools provide ET data. For example, the FAO's AquaCrop or the online tool 'CropWat' (free from FAO) can estimate ET for your location. But you don't need a computer. A simple pan evaporimetera shallow pan of water exposed to suncan give you a rough ET estimate. Place a pan (like a frying pan) in an open area, fill with water, and measure daily loss. Pan evaporation is usually 1.2 to 1.5 times higher than crop ET. So if the pan loses 8 mm, your crop might use about 6 mm. For a maize field at flowering, daily ET might be 5-7 mm in hot, dry conditions. That means you need to apply 5-7 litres per square metre per day. If you irrigate every 3 days, apply 15-21 mm. For a 100 m plot, that's 1500-2100 litres. But ET varies with crop stage: young seedlings use 1-2 mm/day, while full canopy uses 5-8 mm/day. Use a simple table: for leafy vegetables, ET is about 3-5 mm/day; for tomatoes, 4-6 mm/day; for maize, 5-7 mm/day. Check local weather forecastsif a heatwave is coming, increase water. If cloudy and cool, reduce. Many African farmers use the 'crop coefficient' method: ETc = ETo x Kc. ETo is reference ET (from a weather station or pan), and Kc is crop coefficient (e.g., 0.4 for seedling, 1.1 for mid-season maize). You can find Kc values in FAO-56. Start with these numbers, then adjust by watching your plantsif leaves wilt in the afternoon, you're behind. Keep a simple notebook: date, pan reading, crop stage, water applied. After a season, you'll know your farm's pattern.

3. Simple Tools for Scheduling: Tensiometers and Apps

You don't need expensive equipment to schedule irrigation. Two practical tools are tensiometers and smartphone apps. A tensiometer is a sealed tube with a ceramic tip and a vacuum gauge. You bury the tip at root depth (e.g., 20 cm for vegetables). When soil is wet, the gauge reads low (0-10 centibars). As soil dries, the reading rises. For most vegetables, irrigate when the gauge reads 30-40 centibars; for maize, 50-60 centibars. Tensiometers cost about $50-100 and last several seasons. In Africa, you can find them at agricultural supply stores in Nairobi, Lagos, or Accra. If you can't afford one, use a simple 'gypsum block' or even a 'feel method': dig 10 cm, squeeze soilif it holds together but breaks when poked, it's time to irrigate. For apps, 'FAO WaPOR' provides free satellite ET data for Africa. 'CropWat' (offline) helps calculate water needs. 'IrriSAT' (Australia) but works globally if you input coordinates. 'AgriApp' or 'FarmBeats' are used in East Africa. Many apps use your phone's GPS to get weather and ET. For example, 'Rain Bird' app helps schedule based on crop and soil. But beware: apps need internet and data. A simpler tool: a 'watermark' sensor (granular matrix) that you read with a handheld metercosts about $30. Or a 'tensiometer' with a dial. For a smallholder, a $20 'soil moisture meter' (capacitance type) can be stuck in the soil and gives a reading 1-10. Calibrate it: when soil is dry, reading is low; when wet, high. Use it daily at 20 cm depth. Combine tools: a tensiometer at 20 cm and a simple rain gauge. Record readings. After a few weeks, you'll see patterns. Remember, tools are guidesyour eyes and hands are still the best. If plants look healthy and soil is moist, you're doing well.

4. Deficit Irrigation Strategies for Water Scarcity

When water is scarce, you can't keep soil at full moisture all the time. Deficit irrigation means deliberately applying less water than the crop's full requirement, accepting some yield reduction but saving water. The key is to target drought-tolerant stages. For many crops, flowering and grain filling are criticaldon't stress then. But vegetative stages can tolerate some stress. For example, maize can tolerate 50% water deficit during vegetative growth (before tasseling) with little yield loss. Tomatoes can tolerate deficit during early vegetative stage, but not during flowering. A common strategy: 'regulated deficit irrigation' (RDI). For fruit trees like mango, apply 50-70% of full ET during fruit set, then full water during fruit sizing. For vegetables, use 'partial root-zone drying' (PRD): water only one side of the plant's root zone, alternating sides every 10-14 days. This saves 30-50% water while maintaining yield. In Africa, PRD works well for tomatoes, peppers, and maize. Another method: 'skip irrigation'if you normally irrigate every 3 days, skip one day when soil is still moist. Use a tensiometer: allow readings to reach 60-70 centibars before irrigating, instead of 30-40. But watch for wilting. If leaves roll or droop in early morning, you've gone too far. Mulching helps: a 5 cm layer of dry grass or crop residues reduces evaporation by 30-50%, so deficit irrigation is less risky. Also, water at dawn or dusk to reduce evaporation. For a 1-hectare plot, deficit irrigation might save 1000-2000 cubic metres per season. But don't deficit irrigate young seedlings or during floweringthat can kill the crop. Start with a small trial: divide your field into two, apply full water on one half, deficit on the other, and compare yields. Many African farmers have found that 70% of full water gives 90% of yielda good trade when water is short.

5. Monitoring and Adjusting for Weather Variability

Weather in Africa is unpredictable. A week of hot, dry winds can dry soil fast; a sudden downpour can waterlog. You must monitor and adjust. Start with a simple rain gaugea straight-sided tin can (like a baked bean can) placed in an open area. Measure rainfall daily at the same time. If you get 10 mm of rain, that's 10 litres per square metre. Subtract that from your irrigation requirement. For example, if your crop needs 20 mm and it rained 10 mm, irrigate only 10 mm. Also, watch temperature and wind. On hot days (above 35C), ET increases by 20-30%. On windy days, more water evaporates. Use a simple thermometer and a windsock (a piece of cloth on a stick) to gauge. If it's very hot and windy, irrigate more frequentlymaybe every 2 days instead of 3. If cloudy and cool, stretch to 4-5 days. Keep a daily log: date, rainfall, max temp, wind, irrigation applied. After a season, you'll see patterns. For example, in the Sahel, the rainy season may bring 5 mm one day, then none for a week. In East Africa, short rains can be intense. Adjust by checking soil moisture: dig 10 cm, squeeze. If it's dry, irrigate even if you irrigated yesterday. If it's wet, wait. Also, watch for signs of waterlogging: yellowing leaves, wilting despite wet soil. That means too much waterstop irrigating and improve drainage. Use a simple 'checkbook' method: keep a water balance. Start with soil at field capacity (full). Each day, subtract ET (e.g., 5 mm) and add rainfall. When the balance drops to 50% of available water, irrigate to refill. For a loam soil, available water might be 150 mm per metre depth. So for 30 cm root zone, 45 mm available. Irrigate when 22 mm remains. This method works with just a rain gauge and a thermometer. Adjust weekly based on forecastif rain is predicted, skip irrigation. If a heatwave is coming, pre-irrigate. Being flexible is key.

6. Record Keeping for Efficient Water Use

Keeping records is the simplest way to improve water efficiency. Without records, you guess. With records, you learn. Start a notebook or a simple spreadsheet on your phone. Record: date, crop, growth stage, irrigation method (drip, sprinkler, furrow), amount of water applied (in mm or litres), duration, rainfall (mm), and any observations (wilting, pests, etc.). Also record soil moisture readings if you use a tensiometer or meter. For example: '15 May 2025, tomatoes, flowering, drip, 20 mm, 2 hours, rain 0 mm, tensiometer 35 cb.' After a few weeks, you'll see how much water you actually use. Calculate water productivity: kilograms of yield per cubic metre of water. For a 1-hectare tomato field yielding 20 tonnes with 6000 m water, that's 3.3 kg/m. If you can get 25 tonnes with 5000 m, that's 5 kg/mbetter. Use records to compare seasons. Did you overwater in a wet year? Did you underwater in a dry year? Adjust next season. Also, record costs: pump fuel, labour, maintenance. This helps you see if deficit irrigation saves money. For smallholders, a simple 'water diary' works. Use a calendar: mark each irrigation day with a tick and amount. At the end of the month, count ticks. If you irrigated 10 times with 20 mm each, that's 200 mm. Compare to crop requirement (e.g., 500 mm for maize). If you're below, you might be deficit irrigatingcheck yields. If above, you're wasting water. Share records with neighboursthey may have insights. In Kenya, farmer groups use 'record books' to track water use and share tips. You can also use a mobile app like 'FarmLogs' or 'AgriTracker' to log. But paper is fine. The key is consistency. Even a simple tally on a wall chart helps. After one season, you'll know your farm's water needs precisely. That knowledge is powerit saves water, money, and increases resilience to drought.

<h2>Practical Reference: Water Management and Scheduling for Smallholder Irrigation in Africa</h2>

<p>Water is the single largest recurring cost in most smallholder irrigation enterprises, and it is also the input most commonly wasted. A farmer who pumps for six hours when the crop needs three is not just burning diesel they are leaching nutrients below the root zone, waterlogging heavy soils, and creating conditions for fungal disease. Conversely, a farmer who under-irrigates during flowering can lose 4060% of a tomato or maize yield in a single week.</p>

<p>This Practical Reference consolidates the six lessons of the Water Management and Scheduling module into a working document you can print, laminate, and keep in the pump shed. It is written for the commercial smallholder the farmer who sells into a market, a cooperative, or a supermarket programme and who therefore needs predictable, repeatable yields rather than survival-level production.</p>

<h3>Why Scheduling Is a Business Decision, Not Just an Agronomic One</h3>

<p>Irrigation scheduling answers two questions: <strong>when</strong> to irrigate and <strong>how much</strong> to apply. Getting these right converts directly into money. Consider a 1-hectare tomato plot in the Arusha region of Tanzania:</p>

<ul>
<li>Fuel for a petrol pump: roughly 1.5 litres per hour at USD 1.40/litre.</li>
<li>A poorly scheduled farmer pumps 5 hours/day, 5 days/week = USD 52.50/week.</li>
<li>A well-scheduled farmer pumps 3 hours/day, 4 days/week = USD 25.20/week.</li>
<li>Season saving (16 weeks): <strong>USD 437</strong> often more than the net margin on the plot itself.</li>
</ul>

<p>Add avoided yield loss from over- or under-watering (typically 1530% in unscheduled systems), and the return on learning scheduling tools is the highest of any intervention available to a smallholder irrigator.</p>

<h3>Core Concepts at a Glance</h3>

<p><strong>Evapotranspiration (ET)</strong> is the combined water lost from soil evaporation and crop transpiration. Reference ET (ET) is the weather-driven demand for a standard grass crop; crop ET (ETc) adjusts ET by a crop coefficient (Kc) that changes with growth stage. Most irrigation decisions reduce to: replace the water the crop has used since the last irrigation, minus any rain.</p>

<p><strong>Readily Available Water (RAW)</strong> is the portion of soil moisture a crop can extract before stress begins typically 5060% of total available water for most vegetables and maize. Irrigating before RAW is exhausted keeps the crop in the "no-stress" zone; deliberately allowing depletion beyond RAW is the basis of deficit irrigation.</p>

<h3>Applying the Six Lessons in Practice</h3>

<p><strong>1. Developing an Irrigation Schedule.</strong> Start with a simple water balance. Record ET from a local weather station or a free app, multiply by the crop Kc, subtract effective rainfall, and irrigate when cumulative depletion reaches RAW. For a 1-ha tomato plot on loam in mid-season: ETc = 5.5 mm/day 1.15 = 6.3 mm/day. RAW = 90 mm/m 0.5 m root zone 45 mm. So you irrigate roughly every 7 days, applying 45 mm (450 m/ha) but split into two or three smaller applications if using drip to avoid runoff.</p>

<p><strong>2. Using Evapotranspiration Data.</strong> Free sources include the FAO WaPOR portal (Africa coverage), national meteorological services (e.g. Kenya Met, Tanzania Met Agency), and the <em>IRI/FEWS NET</em> agro-climate bulletins. If no data is available, use a Class A pan with a pan coefficient of 0.70.8, or a simple atmometer. A 10% error in ET estimate translates to roughly 10% over- or under-irrigation usually tolerable if you check soil moisture weekly.</p>

<p><strong>3. Simple Tools.</strong> A tensiometer (USD 3080) placed at 20 cm and 40 cm depth gives you a direct read of soil suction. Irrigate vegetables when the shallow tensiometer reads 30 to 40 kPa on sandy soil, 50 to 60 kPa on loam. Free apps such as <em>FAO WaPOR</em>, <em>IrriSAT</em>, and <em>AgriApp</em> (East Africa) provide ET and scheduling prompts. A cheap gypsum block or a hand-squeeze test ("ball test") works when nothing else is available.</p>

<p><strong>4. Deficit Irrigation.</strong> When water or fuel is short, deliberately stress the crop at less sensitive stages. For tomato, reduce water by 3040% during vegetative growth, then return to full irrigation at flowering and fruit set. For maize, the critical period is tasselling to grain fill never deficit there. Regulated deficit irrigation can save 2030% of water with less than 10% yield loss when applied correctly.</p>

<p><strong>5. Monitoring Weather Variability.</strong> African smallholders face high inter-annual and intra-seasonal variability. Keep a simple rain gauge (USD 515) and record daily rainfall. After any rain event above 10 mm, skip the next scheduled irrigation. During heatwaves (ET > 7 mm/day), shorten intervals by 2030%. During cool cloudy spells, extend them.</p>

<p><strong>6. Record Keeping.</strong> The most profitable habit in this module is a simple logbook. Record date, crop stage, ET or pan reading, rainfall, irrigation duration, fuel used, and any crop stress observed. After one season you will have a farm-specific schedule that beats any generic table. Use a notebook, a wall chart, or a free spreadsheet template but write it down.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ol>
<li><strong>Know your soil:</strong> do a jar test or hand-texture test to classify sandy, loam, or clay this sets your RAW and interval.</li>
<li><strong>Know your crop stage:</strong> use Table 1 Kc values and adjust weekly.</li>
<li><strong>Check ET daily</strong> from a free app, weather station, or pan; multiply by Kc to get ETc.</li>
<li><strong>Measure, don't guess:</strong> use a tensiometer or moisture probe at two depths before irrigating.</li>
<li><strong>Subtract rainfall:</strong> skip irrigation after 10 mm rain; record every event.</li>
<li><strong>Apply the right depth:</strong> replace depletion to field capacity, not beyond runoff and leaching waste money.</li>
<li><strong>Log everything:</strong> date, ET, rain, hours pumped, fuel used, crop observations.</li>
<li><strong>Review weekly:</strong> adjust intervals for heatwaves, cloudy spells, and crop stage changes.</li>
</ol>
