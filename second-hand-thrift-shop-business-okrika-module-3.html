1. Sorting Clothes by Quality: First Grade, Second Grade, and Rags

When your bale of okrika arrives, the first thing you must do is sort everything into three piles: First Grade, Second Grade, and Rags. This is the foundation of your profit. First Grade items are like newno stains, no holes, no fading, all buttons and zips working. These are your money-makers. Second Grade has minor flaws: a small stain that might wash out, a loose thread, slight fading, but still wearable. Rags are torn, heavily stained, or missing partsthese you can sell to tailors or rag pickers for a small amount.

Start by spreading everything on a clean tarpaulin or mat in a well-lit area. Work in natural daylight if possible, because artificial light can hide stains. Pick up each item, check the front, back, inside, seams, underarms, and crotch area. For trousers, check the knees and pockets. For dresses, check the hem and zips. A simple rule: if you would not wear it to a wedding or an interview, it is not First Grade.

Use a simple grading system: A for First Grade, B for Second Grade, C for Rags. You can mark this with a small tag or sticker, or just separate them into different bags. Keep a count as you gothis helps with record-keeping later. For example, from a 50kg bale costing 150,000 Naira, you might get 30% First Grade, 50% Second Grade, and 20% Rags. That means 15kg of First Grade, 25kg of Second Grade, and 10kg of Rags. Knowing these percentages helps you price your bale correctly.

Be ruthless with your sorting. It is better to under-grade than over-grade. A customer who buys a Second Grade item thinking it is First Grade will not return. Your reputation is built on honesty. Also, remember that some items can be upgraded: a Second Grade shirt with a missing button becomes First Grade after you sew on a matching button. Keep a small sewing kit handyneedle, thread, scissors, and spare buttons. A 30-minute repair can double the price of an item.

Finally, store your sorted piles separately. Use clean sacks or boxes labeled A, B, and C. Keep them dry and away from direct sunlight to prevent damage. This simple sorting process will save you hours later and ensure you get the best price for every item.

2. Identifying Branded and Vintage Items for Higher Profit

Not all okrika is equal. Some items are worth 10 times more than others because of the brand name or vintage appeal. Your job is to spot these gems before you price them. Branded items include known labels like Nike, Adidas, Zara, H&M, Levi's, Gucci, and local favorites like Ankara or Dashiki from reputable designers. Vintage items are older pieces, often from the 70s, 80s, or 90s, that have a unique style and quality.

How do you identify a brand? Check the label inside the collar or waistband. Look for the brand name, logo, and sometimes a size and country of origin. Be careful: some fakes exist. A real Nike will have a consistent logo, good stitching, and a quality feel. A fake will have crooked stitching, misspelled words, or a cheap label. For vintage, look for details like metal zips, single-stitch seams, and old-fashioned sizing (e.g., size 42 instead of L). Also, check for union labels or old care tags.

Once you find a branded or vintage item, set it aside in a special 'Premium' pile. These items should not be mixed with the general stock. For example, a vintage Levi's 501 jeans in good condition can sell for 15,000 to 25,000 Naira, while a regular jeans might sell for 3,000 Naira. A Nike hoodie can fetch 8,000 Naira, while a no-name hoodie goes for 2,500 Naira. The difference is huge.

To learn brands, spend time online. Follow thrift and vintage sellers on Instagram and TikTok. Use Google Lens to search for a logo you don't recognize. Join Facebook groups for okrika sellers where people share tips. Also, visit high-end thrift stores in your city and see what they price high. Ask questions. The more you know, the more money you make.

When you find a premium item, clean it carefully. Hand wash or dry clean if needed. Present it welluse a mannequin or hang it prominently in your shop. Take clear photos for online sales. Mention the brand and vintage status in your description. This attracts buyers who are willing to pay more. Remember, one premium item can pay for a whole bale, so train your eye to spot them.

3. Setting Prices Based on Condition, Brand, and Demand

Pricing is both an art and a science. You must consider three main factors: condition, brand, and demand. Condition is the most important. A First Grade item can be priced at 3 to 5 times your cost per item, while Second Grade should be 1.5 to 2 times cost, and Rags sold in bulk. For example, if your average cost per item from a bale is 500 Naira, First Grade could sell for 2,000 to 2,500 Naira, Second Grade for 800 to 1,000 Naira, and Rags for 100 Naira per kg.

Brand adds value. A branded item in First Grade can be priced 20-50% higher than a similar non-branded item. For instance, a plain shirt might sell for 1,500 Naira, but a Zara shirt in the same condition can sell for 2,500 Naira. Vintage items can be priced even higher, often 3-5 times the base price. Do your research: check online marketplaces like Jumia, Konga, or Facebook Marketplace to see what similar items are selling for.

Demand is about what your customers want. In many African markets, certain items are always in demand: jeans, t-shirts, dresses, and children's clothes. Seasonal demand also matters: raincoats during rainy season, sweaters during harmattan. If you have an item that is in high demand, you can price it slightly higher. But be careful not to overpricecustomers will walk away. A good strategy is to start with a price 10-20% above your target, so you have room to negotiate.

Also consider your location. Prices in a busy market like Balogun in Lagos will be different from a small town in Ghana. Talk to other sellers, observe what customers are buying, and adjust. Keep a notebook or use a simple app like Excel or Google Sheets to track your prices and sales. After a month, you will see which prices work and which don't.

Finally, don't be afraid to change prices. If an item is not selling after two weeks, reduce the price by 10-20%. If it sells quickly, you might have underpriced itraise the price for similar items next time. Pricing is a skill you build with experience.

4. Pricing Strategies for Quick Turnover vs. High Margins

You have two main pricing strategies: quick turnover (sell fast, lower profit per item) and high margins (sell slow, higher profit per item). Your choice depends on your cash flow needs, storage space, and customer base. Quick turnover is good if you need cash quickly to buy more bales or if you have limited storage. High margins work if you have a loyal customer base that values quality and you can afford to wait.

For quick turnover, price your items 20-30% lower than competitors. For example, if a shirt normally sells for 2,000 Naira, price it at 1,500 Naira. This attracts bargain hunters and moves stock fast. You might sell 50 items a day at 1,500 Naira, making 75,000 Naira, but your profit per item is small. This strategy works well in busy markets with high foot traffic. You can also offer 'buy 2 get 1 free' or '3 for 5,000 Naira' to encourage bulk buying.

For high margins, focus on quality and presentation. Price your First Grade and branded items higher. For instance, a vintage dress might be priced at 10,000 Naira instead of 5,000 Naira. You might sell only 5 items a day, but each sale gives you a big profit. This works if you have a nichelike vintage clothing or plus-size fashionand you market to the right audience. Use social media to showcase your premium items. Create a loyal following by offering excellent customer service and unique pieces.

You can also mix both strategies. Have a 'bargain bin' for quick turnover of Second Grade and Rags, and a 'premium rack' for high-margin items. This way, you attract all types of customers. For example, a customer comes for a cheap t-shirt, sees a beautiful vintage jacket, and buys both. The cheap item brings them in, the premium item makes your profit.

Track your sales weekly. If you find that you are selling out of certain items too fast, you are underpricing. If items sit for months, you are overpricing. Adjust accordingly. Remember, cash flow is king. Even if you make high margins, if you don't sell, you can't pay rent or buy new stock. Find the balance that works for your business.

5. Creating Bundle Deals and Discounts to Move Slow Items

Every okrika seller has slow-moving itemsthose that have been on the rack for weeks or months. Instead of letting them gather dust, use bundle deals and discounts to move them. Bundling means grouping several items together and selling them at a lower combined price. For example, if you have 10 shirts that are not selling at 1,000 Naira each, offer a '5 shirts for 4,000 Naira' deal. That's 800 Naira per shirt, but you sell 5 at once. Customers love a bargain, and you clear stock.

Another bundle idea: create themed packages. For children's clothes, offer '10 pieces for 3,000 Naira'. For ladies' tops, '3 for 2,500 Naira'. You can also bundle by size: 'All size M shirts, 4 for 3,000 Naira'. This makes it easy for customers to buy multiple items. Make sure the bundle price is lower than the individual total, but still gives you a profit. Calculate your cost per item and ensure the bundle price covers it plus a small margin.

Discounts are another tool. Offer a percentage off for bulk buys: 'Buy 3, get 10% off' or 'Buy 5, get 20% off'. You can also have a 'Happy Hour' discountsay, every Wednesday from 2-4 PM, everything is 20% off. This creates urgency and brings in customers during slow hours. For online sales, use promo codes like 'OKRIKA10' for 10% off. Announce these deals on WhatsApp, Instagram, and Facebook.

For very slow items, consider a 'clearance sale' at the end of the month. Price everything in a specific bin at 500 Naira or 1,000 Naira, regardless of original price. This generates excitement and brings people to your shop. They might buy other items too. Even if you break even or make a small loss on those items, you free up space for new stock that will sell better.

Always communicate the value. Tell customers why you are discounting'End of season sale' or 'Making room for new arrivals'. This makes them feel they are getting a deal, not buying rejected goods. Also, use bundles to upsell: when a customer buys one item, suggest a bundle. 'This shirt is 1,500 Naira, but if you add two more, you get all three for 4,000 Naira.' Many will take the offer. This increases your average sale and moves stock faster.

6. Record-Keeping for Stock and Sales

If you don't keep records, you are running a hobby, not a business. Record-keeping helps you know what you have, what you sold, and what you earned. Start simple: get a notebook and pen, or use a free app like Excel, Google Sheets, or a simple inventory app on your phone. The key is to record every item you buy and every item you sell.

For stock, create a list with columns: Date, Item Description, Grade (A, B, C), Cost Price, Selling Price, and Quantity. When you buy a bale, record the total cost and the number of items you sorted into each grade. For example, on 1st January, you bought a bale for 150,000 Naira and got 100 First Grade, 150 Second Grade, and 50 Rags. This gives you an average cost per item: 150,000 / 300 = 500 Naira per item. But you can allocate costs differently: First Grade might carry more of the cost because they sell higher. A simple method is to assign 70% of the bale cost to First Grade, 25% to Second Grade, and 5% to Rags. So First Grade cost per item = (0.7 * 150,000) / 100 = 1,050 Naira. Second Grade = (0.25 * 150,000) / 150 = 250 Naira. Rags = (0.05 * 150,000) / 50 = 150 Naira per item. This helps you set prices accurately.

For sales, record every sale: Date, Item, Quantity, Selling Price, Total. At the end of each day, total your sales. At the end of the week, calculate your profit: Total Sales - Total Cost of Items Sold. Also track expenses like rent, transport, and your own time. This shows if you are truly making a profit.

Use a simple stock card for each item or a spreadsheet. If you have many items, use a numbering system: write a number on a tag and attach it to the item. In your record book, write that number with the details. When sold, mark it as sold. This prevents theft and helps you know what is missing. For online sellers, keep a record of orders, customer names, and delivery status.

Finally, review your records monthly. Identify your best-selling items, your most profitable grades, and your slow movers. This data guides your future purchases. For example, if you see that First Grade jeans sell out every month, buy more bales with jeans. If Rags are not selling, find a new buyer or donate them. Good records turn a small okrika business into a growing enterprise.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Sorting, Grading, and Pricing Your Items

In the African second-hand clothing trade, the money is not made when you sell it is made when you sort, grade, and price. A bale of okrika arriving from Europe or North America is a mixed bag: premium branded pieces, wearable mid-grade clothing, and unsellable rags all compressed together. The trader who opens that bale and grades it quickly and accurately converts the same raw material into two to three times the revenue of a trader who simply dumps everything on a table at one price.

This practical reference covers the full workflow: sorting by quality, identifying branded and vintage items, pricing by condition and demand, choosing between quick-turnover and high-margin strategies, building bundles, and keeping records that let you repeat what works. Treat this as your operating manual, not theory.

1. Sorting Clothes by Quality: First Grade, Second Grade, and Rags

Open the bale in a well-lit, dry space. Sort fast speed matters because labour time is your biggest hidden cost. Use three piles, then subdivide later.

  • First Grade: No holes, no stains, no fading, all buttons and zips working, seams intact. These go straight to the premium rack.
  • Second Grade: Minor flaws a loose thread, slight fade, a missing button you can replace. These go to the bundle table or get repaired and upgraded.
  • Rags: Torn, heavily stained, stretched, or mouldy. Sell in bulk to recyclers, car-wash operators, or rag traders. Do not let rags sit they cost you space.

A useful rule: if you would not wear it to a church service or a job interview, it is not first grade. Be ruthless. A clean, well-organised stall with only first-grade items on the front rack signals quality and lets you charge more.

2. Identifying Branded and Vintage Items for Higher Profit

Branded and vintage items are where margin lives. Learn to spot them in seconds:

  • Labels and tags: Check the neck label and side seam tag. Names like Zara, H&M, Levi's, Nike, Adidas, Ralph Lauren, Tommy Hilfiger, Carhartt, and Patagonia command premium prices in most African cities.
  • Stitching and hardware: Heavy zips (YKK), reinforced seams, and metal buttons suggest quality.
  • Vintage markers: Old-school logos, "Made in USA" or "Made in England" labels, 100% cotton or wool, and cut styles from the 80s and 90s. Denim jackets, leather, and military surplus are strong vintage categories.
  • Fabric feel: Heavy cotton, real wool, and silk feel different from polyester blends. Learn by touching.

Set these aside immediately. Wash and press them, then display them on a separate "premium" rail with clear pricing. In Nairobi, Lagos, Accra, and Kampala, a good Levi's jacket can sell for 510 times the price of a generic shirt from the same bale.

3. Setting Prices Based on Condition, Brand, and Demand

Price is not guesswork. Use a simple formula: Base Cost + Condition Premium + Brand Premium + Demand Adjustment. Your base cost is the bale price divided by the number of sellable items. If a bale costs KES 15,000 and yields 150 sellable pieces, your base cost per item is KES 100. Everything above that is your margin.

4. Pricing Strategies for Quick Turnover vs. High Margins

You need both strategies running at the same time. Quick turnover keeps cash flowing; high margins build profit.

  • Quick turnover: Price second-grade and common items low 1020% above your base cost and move volume. This clears space and generates daily cash for restocking.
  • High margin: Hold first-grade branded and vintage items at premium prices. Do not discount too early. These are your profit engines.
  • The 70/30 rule: Aim for 70% of stock priced for quick turnover and 30% priced for margin. This balances cash flow and profitability.

5. Creating Bundle Deals and Discounts to Move Slow Items

Slow items are dead capital. Move them with bundles:

  • 3-for-2 bundles: Three second-grade shirts for the price of two. Increases basket size.
  • Family packs: Bundle children's clothing in packs of five at a flat price.
  • End-of-week discount: Every Saturday afternoon, mark down slow items by 2030%. Better to recover cost than store dead stock.
  • Loyalty: Give a small discount to repeat customers. In African markets, relationships drive repeat business.

6. Record-Keeping for Stock and Sales

You cannot grow what you do not measure. Keep a simple daily record a notebook works, but a spreadsheet is better. Track:

  • Bale cost, date opened, and number of sellable items.
  • Daily sales by grade (first, second, rags).
  • Stock remaining at the end of each week.
  • Best-selling brands and sizes.

SAFETY WARNING Health and Hygiene

Second-hand clothing can carry mould, dust mites, skin infections, and in rare cases chemical residues from fumigation. Always:

  • Wear gloves and a dust mask when opening and sorting bales.
  • Wash all clothing in hot water with detergent before displaying or selling.
  • Dry items in direct sunlight UV light kills many pathogens.
  • Never sell items with visible mould, blood stains, or chemical odours.
  • Keep children away from the sorting area.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Open bales in a dry, well-lit, ventilated space with gloves and mask.
  2. Sort into three piles fast: first grade, second grade, rags.
  3. Pull out branded and vintage items immediately these are your profit core.
  4. Calculate base cost per item before setting any price.
  5. Price 70% of stock for quick turnover, 30% for high margin.
  6. Bundle slow items (3-for-2, family packs) to clear dead stock.
  7. Record every sale and closing stock daily no exceptions.
  8. Wash, dry, and press all clothing before it reaches the customer.
