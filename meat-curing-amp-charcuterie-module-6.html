1. Dry-Cured Bacon: From Pork Belly to Slice

Dry-cured bacon is made by rubbing a cure mixture directly onto pork belly. For a 2 kg belly, mix 50 g fine salt, 25 g sugar, and 5 g Prague Powder #1 (also called pink curing salt). Rub this all over the belly, place it in a zip-lock bag or non-reactive container, and refrigerate at 2â4Â°C. Turn the belly every day for 7 days. You will see liquid drawn outâthis is normal. After 7 days, rinse the belly under cold water, pat dry, and let it rest uncovered in the fridge for 12â24 hours to form a tacky surface (the pellicle).

If you have a smoker, cold smoke at 20â30Â°C for 6â8 hours using hardwood like oak or hickory. If you don't have a smoker, you can hot smoke at 80â90Â°C until the internal temperature reaches 65Â°C, about 2â3 hours. Alternatively, you can bake it in a low oven (90Â°C) for 2 hours. After smoking or baking, cool completely, then slice thinly. Store wrapped in the fridge for up to 3 weeks or freeze for 3 months.

In Africa, pork belly is often available from local butchers or markets. If you cannot find Prague Powder #1, you can use a traditional salt-only cure, but the bacon will be gray rather than pink and must be cooked thoroughly. For a spicier version, add 10 g cracked black pepper, 5 g paprika, and 2 crushed bay leaves to the cure. Always weigh your ingredientsâvolume measures are unreliable. Use a digital kitchen scale. A simple plastic container and a fridge are all you need. This method works with any size belly; just scale the cure: 2.5% salt, 1.25% sugar, and 0.25% Prague Powder #1 by weight of the meat.

2. Wet-Cured Ham: Brining and Boiling

Wet-cured ham, also called brine-cured or pickled ham, is made by soaking a fresh pork leg in a salt solution. For a 5 kg leg, prepare a brine: 1.5 kg salt, 500 g sugar, 50 g Prague Powder #1, and 10 litres water. Dissolve everything in cold water. Submerge the leg in a food-grade bucket or cooler, ensuring it is fully covered. Weight it down with a clean plate. Refrigerate at 2â4Â°C for 5â7 days. The brine must stay cold; if your fridge is small, use a cooler with ice packs and change them twice daily.

After brining, remove the ham, rinse well, and soak in fresh water for 2 hours to reduce surface salt. Then place in a large pot, cover with water, and add aromatics: 2 onions, 4 bay leaves, 10 peppercorns, and 4 cloves garlic. Bring to a boil, then simmer gently for 2â3 hours, until the internal temperature reaches 68Â°C. A 5 kg ham typically takes 2.5 hours. Let it cool in the liquid for 1 hour, then remove and refrigerate. Slice cold.

In Africa, fresh pork legs are often sold as 'green ham' or simply as pork leg. If you cannot get Prague Powder #1, you can omit it, but the ham will be brownish and must be eaten within a week. For a smoked version, after brining and rinsing, hot smoke at 80â90Â°C until internal temp is 68Â°C. You can also add local spices like 20 g ground ginger or 10 g chili flakes to the brine. Always keep the brine cold and use a non-reactive container (plastic, glass, or stainless steel). Never use aluminium or cast iron. Test the brine strength by floating an egg: it should float with a coin-sized area above the surface. If it sinks, add more salt.

3. Pastrami: Curing and Smoking Beef Brisket

Pastrami is made from beef brisket that is brined, rubbed with spices, and smoked. Start with a 4 kg brisket. Prepare a brine: 1 kg salt, 500 g sugar, 40 g Prague Powder #1, 20 g black peppercorns, 10 g coriander seeds, 6 bay leaves, and 8 litres water. Bring to a boil, then cool completely. Submerge the brisket in the brine and refrigerate at 2â4Â°C for 10â14 days. Turn the meat every 2 days. After brining, rinse and soak in fresh water for 2 hours to remove excess salt.

Next, make the rub: 100 g coarse black pepper, 50 g ground coriander, 20 g paprika, 10 g garlic powder, and 10 g mustard powder. Coat the brisket thickly. Smoke at 110â120Â°C for 6â8 hours, until the internal temperature reaches 95Â°C. If you don't have a smoker, you can bake in a low oven (120Â°C) for the same time, then finish on a grill for 10 minutes for smoke flavour. Alternatively, use a charcoal grill with wood chips and indirect heat.

After smoking, wrap in foil and rest for 1 hour. Slice thinly against the grain. Pastrami is best steamed before serving: place slices in a steamer for 5 minutes. In Africa, brisket is often available from butcheries. If you cannot find Prague Powder #1, use 30 g saltpetre (potassium nitrate) instead, but it must be food-grade. For a local twist, add 20 g ground dried chili to the rub. Always weigh your cure. Store pastrami wrapped in the fridge for up to 2 weeks, or freeze for 3 months. The brine can be reused once if boiled and re-cooled.

4. Coppa and Lonza: Italian-Style Cured Pork

Coppa is made from pork neck (also called pork collar or coppa), and lonza is made from pork loin. Both are dry-cured whole muscles. For a 2 kg coppa, mix 60 g salt, 20 g sugar, 5 g Prague Powder #1, 10 g black pepper, 5 g coriander, and 2 g nutmeg. Rub all over the meat, place in a vacuum bag or zip-lock bag, and refrigerate at 2â4Â°C for 14 days. Turn daily. After curing, rinse, pat dry, and tie with butcher's twine. Hang in a cool, humid place (12â15Â°C, 70â80% humidity) for 4â8 weeks, until it loses 30â35% of its original weight. If you don't have a curing chamber, use a fridge with a bowl of salt water to maintain humidity, and open the door daily for air exchange.

For lonza, use the same cure but reduce salt to 50 g for a 2 kg loin. Cure for 10 days, then hang for 3â6 weeks. The meat should be firm and dry on the outside, with a deep red colour inside. Slice thinly and serve at room temperature.

In Africa, pork neck and loin are available from most butchers. If you cannot find Prague Powder #1, you can use a salt-only cure, but the meat will be brown and must be cooked before eating. For a local flavour, add 10 g smoked paprika or 5 g ground chili to the cure. Always use a digital scale. A simple wine fridge can be modified into a curing chamber with a small fan and a humidity controller. Alternatively, hang in a clean, well-ventilated room during cool dry season. Check for unwanted mouldâwhite mould is fine, but green or black mould means discard. The final product should be firm, not squishy. Store wrapped in the fridge for up to 2 months.

5. Using Goat and Lamb for Cured Delicacies

Goat and lamb can be cured into delicious hams, bacon, and pastrami-style meats. For a goat leg (about 2 kg), use a wet cure: 300 g salt, 100 g sugar, 10 g Prague Powder #1, 2 litres water. Brine for 4â5 days at 2â4Â°C. Then rinse and simmer in water with 2 onions, 4 bay leaves, and 10 peppercorns for 2 hours, until internal temp reaches 70Â°C. Cool and slice. This makes a goat ham similar to mutton ham.

For lamb breast (bacon), dry-cure: for 1.5 kg lamb breast, mix 40 g salt, 15 g sugar, 3 g Prague Powder #1, 5 g black pepper, and 5 g rosemary. Rub and refrigerate for 5 days, turning daily. Rinse, dry, and cold smoke at 25Â°C for 4 hours, or hot smoke at 80Â°C for 2 hours. Slice and fry.

For lamb pastrami, use a lamb shoulder (2 kg). Brine: 500 g salt, 200 g sugar, 20 g Prague Powder #1, 4 litres water. Brine for 7 days. Rub with 50 g black pepper, 20 g coriander, 10 g paprika. Smoke at 110Â°C for 4â5 hours until internal 95Â°C. Steam and slice.

In Africa, goat and lamb are common and often cheaper than beef. Use fresh, never frozen, for best results. If you cannot find Prague Powder #1, use salt-only cure and cook thoroughly. For local spices, add 10 g ground ginger, 5 g cumin, or 5 g chili. Always keep meat cold during curing. A simple cooler with ice works if you lack refrigeration. Test doneness by taste: it should be salty but not overpowering. Adjust cure time based on thickness: thin cuts cure faster. Store cured goat or lamb in the fridge for up to 2 weeks or freeze for 3 months. These meats are great for sandwiches or platters.

6. Smoking Techniques: Hot Smoking vs. Cold Smoking

Smoking is a key step in many cured meats. There are two main methods: hot smoking and cold smoking. Hot smoking cooks the meat while smoking it, using temperatures between 80Â°C and 120Â°C. This is ideal for bacon, ham, and pastrami that you want ready-to-eat. Cold smoking adds flavour without cooking, using temperatures below 30Â°C. It is used for bacon, coppa, and lonza that will be cooked later or eaten raw after curing.

To hot smoke, you need a smoker or a charcoal grill with a lid. Use hardwood chips like oak, hickory, apple, or cherry. Soak chips in water for 30 minutes, then drain. For a charcoal grill, set up indirect heat: coals on one side, meat on the other. Maintain temperature by adjusting vents. For a 2 kg bacon, smoke at 90Â°C for 2â3 hours until internal temp is 65Â°C. For ham, smoke at 80â90Â°C until internal 68Â°C. For pastrami, smoke at 110Â°C until internal 95Â°C.

Cold smoking requires a separate smoke generator or a maze smoker. You can build a simple cold smoker by connecting a firebox to a chamber with a pipe. Keep the smoke cool by passing it through a length of pipe. Smoke at 20â25Â°C for 6â12 hours. In hot climates, cold smoking is difficult; do it at night or in a cool room. If you cannot cold smoke, you can use a 'liquid smoke' alternative, but it's not the same.

In Africa, wood is abundant. Use indigenous hardwoods like acacia, mango, or guava. Avoid resinous woods like pine. Always cure meat before smoking. For safety, use a digital thermometer. Hot smoked meats can be stored in the fridge for 1â2 weeks; cold smoked meats must be cured and dried properly and stored in the fridge for up to 3 weeks. If you have no smoker, you can use a wok with wood chips and a rack, covered with foil, on a stoveâbut this is hot smoking. For cold smoking, a cardboard box with a small smouldering fire in a tin can works, but keep the temperature low.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Curing Whole Muscles in African Meat Enterprises

This Practical Reference module consolidates the working knowledge needed to move from raw whole muscles â pork belly, pork leg, beef brisket, goat shoulder, lamb leg â to saleable cured and smoked products. It is written for small and medium African meat processors, butcheries, and agripreneurs who need repeatable, food-safe processes that translate into margin. The emphasis is on bacon, ham, pastrami, coppa, lonza, and the adaptation of these techniques to goat and lamb, which are widely available and culturally accepted across the continent.

Whole-muscle curing differs from comminuted (ground) charcuterie in one critical way: the interior of the muscle is not sterile. Curing salts, time, temperature, and water activity do the preservation work. Getting these variables right is the difference between a premium product and a public health incident. In African markets where cold-chain reliability varies, the processor must design for resilience: shorter curing windows where refrigeration is uncertain, higher salt concentrations, and rigorous temperature logging.

Never use sodium nitrite (cure #1) and sodium nitrate (cure #2) interchangeably. Cure #1 is for cooked or short-cured products (ham, pastrami, bacon that will be cooked). Cure #2 is for long-cured, air-dried products (coppa, lonza, dry salami) where nitrate slowly converts to nitrite over weeks. Using the wrong cure, or exceeding 200 ppm nitrite in the finished product, can cause methemoglobinemia â a life-threatening blood disorder. In hot African climates, nitrite depletion is faster; do not extend cure times without refrigeration below 5Â°C. Always weigh cure salts on a 0.01 g precision scale â never by volume. Keep cure salts locked away from regular salt to prevent accidental poisoning. If you cannot maintain 2â5Â°C during curing, choose a wet-cure ham or pastrami process with a shorter window rather than a long dry-cure.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Weigh everything. Salt, cure, sugar, and meat must be weighed, not estimated. A 0.5% salt error changes shelf life and safety.
  2. Verify your scale. Use a 0.01 g precision scale for cure salts and a 1 g scale for meat and salt. Calibrate weekly.
  3. Log temperature. Record fridge and smokehouse temperatures at least twice daily. In power-cut zones, use a backup cooler or reduce batch size.
  4. Respect cure type. Cure #1 for cooked products; Cure #2 for air-dried products. Label containers clearly and store separately.
  5. Calculate cure time by thickness. One day per 500 g plus two days rest is a safe rule for dry-cured whole muscles.
  6. Target water activity. For shelf-stable coppa and lonza, aim for 30â40% weight loss. Use a moisture meter or track weight daily.
  7. Cook to temperature. Ham and pastrami must reach 68â72Â°C internal. Use a probe thermometer, not time alone.
  8. Package and label. Vacuum-seal, label with production date, cure type, and use-by date. Cold store at 0â4Â°C or freeze for longer shelf life.

Business Notes for African Processors

Whole-muscle curing is a margin business, not a volume business. A kilogram of pork belly bought at farmgate can become 700â800 g of premium bacon that retails at 2.5â4Ã the raw cost in urban delis, hotels, and expatriate markets. Pastrami from beef brisket commands similar multiples in South Africa, Kenya, and Nigeria. Goat and lamb coppa open niche markets: halal-certified cured meats for Middle East export, and tourism-linked deli sales in Ethiopia, Tanzania, and Morocco.

The constraints are cold chain, cure salt access, and regulatory compliance. Cure #1 and #2 are imported in most African countries; source from reputable food-ingredient suppliers and keep certificates of analysis. Where import is difficult, collaborate with a licensed butcher or food processor who already holds permits. National bureaus of standards â KEBS in Kenya, SON in Nigeria, SABS in South Africa, and equivalent bodies elsewhere â enforce Codex-aligned meat hygiene rules. Build your HACCP plan around the cure, smoke, and chill steps; these are your critical control points.

For start-ups, begin with one product: wet-cured ham or pastrami. Both are forgiving, cook to a safe internal temperature, and sell quickly. Add dry-cured bacon once you have reliable refrigeration and a vacuum sealer. Add coppa and lonza only when you can control humidity and temperature for weeks. Every additional product multiplies your food-safety risk and your working capital tied up in inventory.
