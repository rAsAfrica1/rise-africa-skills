1. Tracking Income and Expenses

As a reseller, every naira, shilling, or cedi that comes in and goes out must be recorded. Without tracking, you are guessing. Start with a simple notebook or a free spreadsheet like Google Sheets. Create columns for date, description, income (money received), and expenses (money spent). For example, on 1st March you buy 20 phone cases at 500 each from a supplier in Lagos, spending 10,000. That is an expense. On 5th March you sell 15 cases at 1,200 each, earning 18,000. That is income. Record every single transaction, even small ones like transport to the market or airtime for calls to customers. If you use a smartphone, apps like Wave or Kippa are designed for small businesses in Africa and can send reminders. At the end of each week, total your income and expenses. This habit shows you exactly where your money goes. Many resellers fail because they mix personal and business cash. Open a separate bank account or mobile money wallet for your business. Use a dedicated SIM card for business calls. When you track properly, you can answer simple questions: Did I make a profit last month? Which product sells best? Where can I cut costs? Without tracking, you might feel busy but be losing money. Set a fixed time each day, say 8 PM, to update your records. It takes 10 minutes. Do it consistently. After one month, you will have real data to guide your decisions. Remember, tracking is not for tax alone; it is for your own survival and growth. Start today with a notebook and pen if you have nothing else. The goal is to know your numbers.

2. Calculating Profit and Loss

Profit is what remains after you subtract all costs from your sales. Loss is when costs exceed sales. To calculate profit, you need two numbers: total revenue (all money from sales) and total expenses (all money spent to buy and sell goods). For example, you sell 50 bags at 2,000 each, revenue = 100,000. Your expenses: buying bags at 1,200 each = 60,000, transport = 5,000, packaging = 2,000, airtime and data = 3,000. Total expenses = 70,000. Profit = 100,000 - 70,000 = 30,000. That is your gross profit. But you also have overheads like rent for storage, electricity, or wages for a helper. Subtract those to get net profit. If net profit is negative, you have a loss. Many resellers forget to include their own time or transport. For instance, if you spend 2,000 on bus fare to meet a supplier, that is an expense. Track every cost. Use a simple formula: Profit = Sales - (Cost of Goods Sold + Operating Expenses). Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) is what you paid for the items you sold. If you bought 100 bags but only sold 50, COGS is for 50 bags only, not 100. The remaining 50 are inventory, not an expense yet. This is a common mistake. Also, calculate profit per item: selling price minus buying price. If you buy at 1,200 and sell at 2,000, profit per bag is 800. Then multiply by number sold. This helps you decide which products to focus on. If a product gives low profit, drop it. Review your profit and loss weekly or monthly. Write it down. Compare months to see if you are improving. If you are making a loss, find out why: Are prices too low? Are expenses too high? Adjust quickly. Profit is not optional; it is the reason you are in business.

3. Managing Cash Flow

Cash flow is the movement of money in and out of your business. You can be profitable on paper but still run out of cash to pay suppliers or transport. This happens when customers owe you money or when you spend too much on stock that sits unsold. For example, you have 50,000 in sales but 40,000 is on credit from customers who will pay next month. Meanwhile, you need 30,000 to buy new stock today. You have no cash. To manage cash flow, follow these rules: First, collect payments immediately. If you sell on credit, set clear terms: payment within 7 days. Follow up politely but firmly. Second, don't overstock. Buy only what you can sell in 2-3 weeks. Use the 50/30/20 rule: 50% of cash for restocking, 30% for expenses, 20% as reserve. Third, negotiate credit from your suppliers. Ask for 14 days to pay after delivery. This gives you time to sell and collect cash before paying. Fourth, keep a cash flow forecast. On a sheet, list expected cash in (from sales) and cash out (for purchases, transport, rent) for the next 4 weeks. If you see a shortfall, arrange a small loan from a microfinance bank or a family member early. Fifth, separate personal and business cash. Never use business cash for personal needs like school fees. Pay yourself a small salary. Tools like Kippa or Wave can help track cash. Also, mobile money statements show your balance. Review daily. If you have surplus cash, save it for slow months. Cash flow problems kill more reselling businesses than lack of profit. Stay on top of it.

4. Tax Deductions for Resellers

Tax deductions reduce the amount of income tax you pay. As a reseller, you can deduct ordinary and necessary expenses used to run your business. In many African countries, small businesses with turnover below a threshold (e.g., 25 million Naira in Nigeria) may pay a simplified tax or be exempt, but you still need records. Common deductions include: cost of goods sold (what you paid for items you resold), transport to buy or deliver goods, packaging materials, airtime and data for business calls, rent for storage or shop, salaries for helpers, and fees for accounting software. For example, if your revenue is 500,000 and you spent 300,000 on goods, 20,000 on transport, 10,000 on packaging, your taxable profit is 170,000. Without deductions, you would pay tax on 500,000. Keep receipts for every expense. A receipt can be a handwritten note from a supplier, a mobile money transfer screenshot, or a store invoice. If you buy from an open market without receipts, write the details in your record book and ask the seller to sign. In some countries, you can deduct a portion of your home rent if you use part of your home as an office. Also, depreciation on equipment like a laptop or scale can be deducted over time. To claim deductions, you must file your tax returns. Many revenue authorities now allow online filing. If you are unsure, visit a local tax office or ask a business association. Do not ignore tax; penalties can be high. But do not overpay by missing deductions. A simple spreadsheet can track deductible expenses. At the end of the year, total them and subtract from your income. This lowers your tax bill legally. Always keep records for at least 5 years.

5. Using Accounting Software

Accounting software makes tracking, profit calculation, and tax preparation easier. You don't need to be an accountant to use it. Start with free or low-cost tools designed for African small businesses. Wave (waveapps.com) is free for basic accounting and works on a smartphone. Kippa is a Nigerian app that helps you record sales, expenses, and debts, and sends reminders. For more features, try QuickBooks Online or Zoho Books; they have monthly plans around $10-20. If you sell on Jumia or Konga, their seller dashboards show sales and fees. To get started, download the app, create a business profile, and set up your chart of accounts: categories like Sales, Cost of Goods Sold, Transport, Rent, etc. Then, every time you make a sale or spend money, enter it. The software automatically calculates profit and loss. You can also attach photos of receipts. For cash flow, many apps show a dashboard with money in and out. Some integrate with mobile money or bank feeds, so transactions import automatically. If you have no smartphone, use a simple Excel template. Microsoft Excel or Google Sheets has free templates for income and expense tracking. The key is consistency: enter data daily. At month-end, generate reports: profit and loss, cash flow, and expense breakdown. These reports help you make decisions: Which product is most profitable? Where can you cut costs? They also make tax filing simple because you have all numbers ready. If you have multiple staff, you can give them limited access. Training: watch YouTube tutorials for your chosen app. Many are free. Start with one tool and master it. Do not jump between apps. A good software saves time and reduces errors. It also gives you confidence when applying for a loan, as you can show clean records.

6. Budgeting for Growth

A budget is a plan for how you will spend and save money to grow your reselling business. Without a budget, you may spend on impulse and never expand. Start by reviewing your last 3 months of income and expenses. Identify your average monthly profit. For example, if you make 50,000 profit per month, decide how much to reinvest. A common rule: reinvest 50%, save 30%, and use 20% for personal needs. Your growth budget should include: increasing stock of best-selling items, marketing (e.g., printing flyers, boosting posts on Facebook or Instagram), buying equipment (e.g., a weighing scale, a smartphone for better product photos), and training (e.g., a course on customer service). Set specific goals: In 6 months, I will double my sales by adding two new products and saving 100,000 for bulk purchases. Then break it down monthly. For instance, save 15,000 per month for bulk buying, spend 10,000 on ads, and 5,000 on packaging. Track your actual spending against the budget each month. If you overspend on transport, adjust next month. Use a simple envelope system: put cash for each category in separate envelopes. For digital money, use mobile money wallets or a spreadsheet. Also, build an emergency fund: aim for 3 months of expenses. This protects you from shocks like price hikes or delayed payments. When you stick to a budget, you can afford to take calculated risks, like buying a larger quantity at a discount. Review your budget quarterly. As your business grows, increase your reinvestment. But always pay yourself a small salary to stay motivated. Budgeting is not about restricting; it is about directing your money to where it works best. With a plan, you move from survival to growth.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Financial Management for Resellers

In the sourcing and reselling business, profit is not what you receive from a customer. Profit is what remains after you have paid for goods, transport, packaging, data, airtime, fees, and taxes. Across African markets from Balogun Market in Lagos to Kariakoo in Dar es Salaam, from Kamukunji in Nairobi to Oxford Street in Accra resellers often handle cash daily and reconcile records weekly or monthly. This lesson gives you the practical reference tools to track income and expenses, calculate profit and loss, manage cash flow, claim legitimate tax deductions, use accounting software, and budget for growth.

Use this module as your operating manual. The tables, checklist, and diagram are designed to be copied into your notebook, spreadsheet, or accounting app. The goal is simple: know your numbers so you can make faster, more profitable decisions.

1. Tracking Income and Expenses

Track every sale and every cost at the moment it happens. In African markets, where mobile money and cash coexist, a simple daily log prevents end-of-month confusion. Record date, description, category, amount, and payment method. Separate business money from personal money use a dedicated mobile money wallet or bank account for business.

2. Calculating Profit and Loss

Profit and loss is the scorecard of your reselling business. Use this formula:

Gross Profit = Sales Revenue Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)

Net Profit = Gross Profit Operating Expenses Taxes

If net profit is negative, you have a loss. Review pricing, sourcing costs, and expenses immediately.

3. Managing Cash Flow

Cash flow is the movement of money in and out of your business. A profitable business can still fail if cash is tied up in unsold stock or delayed customer payments. In many African markets, customers may pay on delivery, on credit, or via mobile money with delays. Manage this by:

  • Keeping a cash reserve for at least 24 weeks of expenses.
  • Collecting deposits before ordering stock.
  • Avoiding overstocking slow-moving items.
  • Reconciling mobile money and bank accounts weekly.
  • Separating personal and business cash.

4. Tax Deductions for Resellers

Tax rules vary by country, but many African tax authorities allow deductions for legitimate business expenses. Common deductible expenses for resellers include:

  • Cost of goods purchased for resale.
  • Transport and delivery costs.
  • Packaging materials.
  • Marketing and advertising costs.
  • Data and airtime used for business.
  • Rent for business premises or storage.
  • Mobile money and bank transaction fees.
  • Accounting software subscriptions.

Keep receipts, invoices, and mobile money statements. In countries like Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana, and South Africa, small businesses may qualify for simplified tax regimes. Always confirm with your local tax authority or a licensed tax professional.

SAFETY WARNING: Tax and Financial Compliance

Do not ignore tax obligations. Penalties, interest, and back taxes can destroy your business. Do not claim personal expenses as business deductions this is tax fraud. Do not mix personal and business bank accounts. Keep all receipts for at least 57 years. If you are unsure about your tax status, consult your national tax authority or a licensed accountant. In many African countries, failure to file returns can lead to fines, account freezes, or business closure.

5. Using Accounting Software

You do not need expensive software to start. Free and low-cost tools can help you track income, expenses, and profit. Options relevant to African resellers include:

  • Wave Accounting free invoicing and accounting for small businesses.
  • Zoho Books affordable plans with mobile app support.
  • QuickBooks Online widely used, supports multiple currencies.
  • Kippa Nigerian bookkeeping app for small businesses.
  • Moniepoint / OPay business tools transaction records and basic reporting.
  • Google Sheets / Microsoft Excel free templates for income and expense tracking.

Choose one tool and use it consistently. The best software is the one you will actually update daily.

6. Budgeting for Growth

A budget is a plan for how you will spend and save money. For resellers, budgeting helps you prepare for restocking, expand into new product lines, and build a cash buffer. Use the 50/30/20 rule as a starting point:

  • 50% of profit reinvest into stock and business operations.
  • 30% personal salary or owners draw.
  • 20% savings, emergency fund, and growth fund.

Review your budget monthly. Adjust based on sales trends, seasonality, and market conditions.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Record every sale and expense daily no exceptions.
  2. Separate business and personal money using different accounts or wallets.
  3. Calculate gross profit and net profit at least once a month.
  4. Keep a cash reserve covering 24 weeks of business expenses.
  5. Collect deposits before ordering stock from suppliers.
  6. Save all receipts, invoices, and mobile money statements for tax purposes.
  7. Use one accounting tool consistently spreadsheet or software.
  8. Review your budget monthly and adjust for growth or slow periods.
