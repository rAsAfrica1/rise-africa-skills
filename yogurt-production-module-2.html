1. Chemical Composition of Milk: Proteins, Fats, and Carbohydrates

Milk is mostly water (about 87%), but the solids in that water determine how your yogurt will turn out. The main solids are proteins, fats, and carbohydrates (lactose). In cow's milk, you'll find roughly 3.3% protein, 3.5% fat, and 4.8% lactose. Goat milk has similar protein but smaller fat globules, which makes it easier to digest but can give a thinner yogurt. Sheep milk is richer: about 5.5% protein and 7% fat, so it sets very thick. If you're working with local breeds, expect variation. For example, Zebu cattle in East Africa often produce milk with 4-5% fat, while European crosses may be lower.

Proteins are the building blocks of yogurt texture. Casein makes up about 80% of milk protein and forms the gel network when acidified by bacteria. Whey proteins (20%) stay liquid unless you heat the milk first. Heating to 85Â°C for 5 minutes denatures whey proteins, allowing them to bind with casein and trap water, giving a firmer yogurt. That's why boiled or pasteurized milk makes thicker yogurt than raw milk.

Fat adds creaminess and flavor. It also carries vitamins A, D, E, and K. If you skim the milk, your yogurt will be thinner and less rich. Lactose is the sugar that yogurt bacteria (Lactobacillus bulgaricus and Streptococcus thermophilus) ferment into lactic acid. This acid lowers pH from about 6.7 to 4.5, causing casein to coagulate. People who are lactose intolerant can often eat yogurt because the bacteria reduce lactose by 20-30%.

To measure these components, you don't need a lab. A simple lactometer (density meter) tells you if milk is watered down. A cream separator or even a clear jar left standing for 12 hours shows the fat layer. For proteins, you can use a formal titration kit, but for most small dairies, observing curd firmness is enough. Remember: more solids mean thicker yogurt. If your milk is low in fat or protein, add skim milk powder (1-2 tablespoons per liter) to boost texture.

2. Milk Sourcing and Quality Standards

Where you get your milk matters as much as how you process it. In many African countries, milk comes from smallholder farms, cooperatives, or informal vendors. Each source has different quality risks. For yogurt, you need clean, fresh milk with no antibiotics and low bacterial counts. A good standard is: total bacteria under 100,000 CFU/ml, somatic cell count under 400,000 cells/ml, and no visible dirt or blood. If you're buying from a cooperative, ask for a bulk tank sample. If from a vendor, smell the milkâit should be slightly sweet, not sour or manure-like. Look at the color: white to creamy yellow, not blue or watery.

In East Africa, the Kenya Dairy Board sets grades: Grade A (under 200,000 CFU/ml), Grade B (200,000-1,000,000), Grade C (over 1,000,000). For yogurt, aim for Grade A or B. In Nigeria, NAFDAC requires milk to be pasteurized and tested for aflatoxins if from local feed. Always ask for a certificate of analysis if buying in bulk. If you're collecting from multiple farmers, use a strip cup (a black cup with a screen) to check for clotsâthese indicate mastitis. Also test with a alcohol test: mix equal parts milk and 68% ethanol. If it curdles, the milk is acidic or unstable and won't make good yogurt.

Temperature at reception is critical. Milk should arrive at 4Â°C or below. If it's warm (above 10Â°C), bacteria double every 20 minutes. Use a thermometerâdon't guess. If you can't chill immediately, pasteurize within 2 hours. For small-scale producers, a simple chest freezer with ice packs works. Record everything: supplier name, date, time, temperature, smell, appearance, and any test results. This traceability helps you spot problems and build trust with buyers. Remember, good yogurt starts with good milk. No amount of processing fixes bad raw milk.

3. Testing Milk for Antibiotics and Adulterants

Antibiotics in milk kill your yogurt bacteria. If a cow was treated for mastitis and you use her milk too soon, the starter culture won't growâyou'll get sweet, runny milk instead of tangy yogurt. Withdrawal periods vary: penicillin needs 72 hours, tetracycline 96 hours. But farmers often sell milk before that. You need to test. The easiest field test is the yogurt culture test: take 10 ml of milk, add a pinch of your previous yogurt as starter, incubate at 43Â°C for 4 hours. If it doesn't set or tastes sweet, antibiotics are likely present. For a more precise result, use antibiotic test kits like Delvotest or BetaStar. These cost about $2-5 per test and give results in 2-3 hours. In Kenya, the Kenya Dairy Board offers testing at regional labs for a small fee.

Adulterants are another problem. Water is the most commonâit dilutes solids and makes thin yogurt. Test with a lactometer: pure cow milk reads 1.026-1.032 g/ml at 20Â°C. If lower, water was added. Starch or flour is added to thicken milk, but it ferments poorly and can cause off-flavors. Test with iodine: add a few drops of iodine solution to 5 ml milk. If it turns blue-black, starch is present. Detergents (to make milk look foamy) can be detected by shakingâexcess foam that lasts more than 30 seconds is suspicious. Also, formalin (a preservative) is dangerous; test with a formaldehyde kit or send to a lab.

For small dairies, build a simple testing station: a lactometer, iodine, alcohol, and a thermometer. Train your milk collector to do these tests at every delivery. Reject any milk that fails. If you suspect antibiotics, don't mix it with good milkâone contaminated batch can ruin 100 liters. Keep a record of test results and supplier names. If a supplier repeatedly fails, find another. Your yogurt's success depends on clean milk.

4. Raw Milk Handling and Storage

Raw milk is a living product. It contains enzymes, beneficial bacteria, and also harmful pathogens like E. coli, Salmonella, and Listeria. For yogurt, you will pasteurize, but how you handle raw milk before pasteurization affects final quality. The golden rule: chill immediately after milking. If you're a farmer, move milk to a clean, sanitized bucket and place it in a cold water bath or refrigerator within 30 minutes. Ideally, cool to 4Â°C. In rural areas without electricity, use a charcoal cooler or evaporative cooling: place milk cans in a wet sack and hang in a breezy shade. This can keep milk below 10Â°C for 4-6 hours.

Sanitation is everything. Wash your hands, use stainless steel or food-grade plastic containers (never aluminum or copperâthey react with acid). Clean all surfaces with hot water and detergent, then sanitize with chlorine solution (1 tablespoon bleach per 4 liters water). Rinse with clean water. Use a separate clean cloth for drying. Never store milk in a container that held chemicals or manure. Keep cows cleanâwipe udders with a damp cloth before milking. Discard the first few streams of milk (they have high bacteria).

Storage time: raw milk at 4Â°C can be kept for 24-48 hours before pasteurization, but the sooner you process, the better. At 10Â°C, use within 12 hours. At 20Â°C, use within 4 hours. Never leave milk at room temperature overnight. If you must transport, use insulated containers with ice packs. For long distances, add a frozen water bottle inside the milk can (sealed) to keep it cold. Label each batch with date and time. Use the oldest first (FIFO). If milk smells sour or looks clumpy, discard itâdon't try to pasteurize and use it. Toxins from bacteria may remain even after heating. Remember: pasteurization kills bacteria but does not remove their toxins. So start with clean, cold milk.

5. Role of Milk Solids in Yogurt Texture

Yogurt textureâthick, creamy, spoonableâcomes from milk solids, mainly protein and fat. If your yogurt is runny, the problem is usually low solids. Standard cow milk has about 12-13% total solids. For a firm set yogurt, you want 14-16% solids. How do you get there? Add skim milk powder (SMP) or evaporate some water. SMP is the easiest: add 1-2 tablespoons per liter of milk (about 10-20 grams). Mix well and let hydrate for 20 minutes before heating. This increases protein and lactose, giving the bacteria more food and the gel more structure. In Africa, SMP is available in supermarkets or dairy supply shops. If not, you can boil milk gently to evaporate 10-15% of waterâbut this also caramelizes lactose and changes flavor.

Fat globules act like tiny balloons that disrupt the protein network, making yogurt smoother but less firm. So whole milk yogurt is creamier but may be softer than skim milk yogurt with added SMP. A good compromise: use whole milk plus 1% SMP. For Greek-style yogurt, you strain the whey after fermentation, concentrating solids to 18-20%. That's why Greek yogurt is so thick. You can do this at home with a muslin cloth or a clean cotton bag: hang the yogurt for 4-6 hours in a refrigerator. The liquid that drips out is wheyâuse it in smoothies or as a protein drink.

Other solids like minerals (calcium, phosphorus) also help gelation. Calcium bridges between casein proteins. If you add too much acid (over-fermentation), calcium leaks out and the gel becomes grainy. So control fermentation time: 4-6 hours at 43Â°C. Test by tilting the containerâif it moves as one mass, it's set. If you want even thicker yogurt without additives, heat milk to 90Â°C for 10 minutes. This denatures whey proteins and increases water-binding. Then cool to 43Â°C and add starter. You'll see a noticeable difference. Remember: more solids = thicker yogurt. Measure your milk's solids with a lactometer or just observeâif it's watery, add SMP.

6. Seasonal Variations in Milk Composition

Milk is not the same year-round. In Africa, seasons affect what cows eat, and that changes milk composition. During the rainy season, lush green grass is abundant. Cows produce more milk, but it may be lower in fat and protein because of high water content in feed. For example, in Kenya's Rift Valley, milk fat can drop from 4.5% in dry season to 3.2% in peak rains. In the dry season, cows eat hay, crop residues, or conserved fodder. Milk volume drops, but fat and protein percentages riseâsometimes fat hits 5-6%. This means your yogurt will naturally be thicker in dry season and thinner in rainy season. You need to adjust.

Temperature also plays a role. Heat stress in hot months (over 30Â°C) reduces feed intake, lowering milk yield and sometimes protein. Cold stress (rare in most of Africa, but in highlands like Ethiopia) can increase fat. Also, forage type matters: cows grazing on legumes (alfalfa, clover) produce milk with more protein; those on maize silage produce more fat but less protein. If you buy from different regions, expect differences. For consistent yogurt, you must standardize. Test your milk's fat and protein monthly using a simple lactometer and maybe a fat test (Gerber method). If fat is low, add cream or SMP. If protein is low, add SMP.

How to manage seasonality? First, keep records: date, milk source, fat %, protein %, and yogurt firmness. After one year, you'll see patterns. Second, adjust your recipe: in rainy season, add 2 tablespoons SMP per liter; in dry season, maybe only 1. Third, blend milk from different suppliers to average out variations. Fourth, consider using a starter culture that produces exopolysaccharides (EPS)âthese are natural thickeners made by some bacteria (like some Lactobacillus delbrueckii strains). They make yogurt thicker even with low solids. Finally, communicate with your suppliers: ask them to feed cows consistently, especially during transitions. A sudden change from dry to rainy season can shock your process. Plan ahead. Your yogurt quality depends on understanding these cycles.

Practical Reference: Milk Composition & Quality for Yogurt in Africa

This practical reference is built for African yogurt makers who buy raw milk from smallholders, cooperatives, or peri-urban farms. Your margins live or die on two things: milk solids (what you actually sell as yogurt) and milk safety (what protects your brand and your licence). Every number below is something you can act on at intake, in the vat, and at the point of sale.

1. What You Are Actually Buying

Bovine milk is roughly 87% water and 13% total solids. Of those solids, the commercially decisive fractions for yogurt are:

  • Milk fat â carries flavour, mouthfeel, and much of your retail price. African raw milk typically ranges 3.0â5.5% depending on breed, feed, and season.
  • Protein (casein + whey) â casein forms the gel network. Low protein = thin, syneresis-prone yogurt.

4. Testing for Antibiotics and Adulterants

Antibiotic residues are the single most common cause of total fermentation failure in African yogurt plants. A cow treated with penicillin or tetracycline can shed residues for 3â7 days. Those residues kill your starter culture and you lose the entire batch â plus your reputation with the retailer.

Field-usable tests:

  • Delvotest / BetaStar / Charm â rapid kits, 3â10 minutes, detect beta-lactams and tetracyclines. Cost per test is small compared to a lost 200 L batch.
  • Alcohol test â 2 mL milk + 2 mL 75% ethanol. Any clotting = reject. Cheap, fast, catches acid milk and mastitic milk.
  • Specific gravity (lactometer) â detects added water. Below 1.028 = suspicious.
  • Starch/iodine test â a drop of iodine turns blue-black if starch thickener has been added.
  • Detergent test â shake equal volumes of milk and water; persistent lather suggests detergent.
  • Hydrogen peroxide strip â detects illegal preservative addition.

6. Seasonal Variation â Plan for It

In East and Southern Africa, milk composition swings sharply between dry and rainy seasons. In the dry season, cows on poor pasture produce less milk with lower solids and higher somatic cell counts. In the rainy season, volume rises but fat and protein can dilute.

Practical response: standardise every batch with a lactometer and, where affordable, a rapid protein/fat analyser. Adjust SMP addition seasonally. Lock in supply contracts that specify minimum total solids, not just litres. In the dry season, budget for higher SMP costs or lower yield; in the rainy season, budget for higher volume and tighter quality control.
