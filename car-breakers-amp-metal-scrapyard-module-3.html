Lessons

1. How to quickly assess a vehicle's reusable parts and scrap value

When a car arrives at your yard, you have about 15 minutes to decide its fate. Start with a quick walk-around. Check the tires: if they hold air and have more than 3mm tread, they're worth reselling. A good used tire sells for $10$30 in most African markets. Next, look at the body panels. Doors, bonnets, and boots that are straight and rust-free can fetch $20$50 each. Note any dents or rust holesthose parts are scrap metal.

Open the bonnet. The engine block, cylinder head, and gearbox are high-value if they turn over. Use a breaker bar to turn the crankshaft pulley. If it moves freely, the engine might be rebuildable. A used engine can sell for $200$800 depending on the model. Check the alternator, starter, and radiatorthese are quick sellers. The catalytic converter is worth $50$200 because of the precious metals inside. Remove it carefully.

Inside the car, test the seats, dashboard, and electronics. A working radio, speedometer cluster, or air conditioning compressor can be sold online or to local mechanics. Even switches and relays have value. Look for a spare tire and toolsthose are easy money.

For scrap value, weigh the shell after removing reusable parts. A typical sedan shell weighs about 8001,200 kg. Mixed steel scrap sells for $0.10$0.25 per kg in many African countries. So a stripped shell might bring $80$300. But if you leave valuable parts on it, you lose money. Always remove the battery (worth $5$15 as a core), the alternator (test it first), and the wiring harness (copper content).

Keep a notebook or phone app to record what you find. After 10 cars, you'll know which models have the best parts. For example, Toyota Corollas and Nissan Sunnys have high demand for used parts. Peugeots and Renaults are common in West Africa, so their parts sell fast. Focus on what moves in your local market.

2. Draining and safely storing fluids: fuel, oil, coolant, and brake fluid

Before you remove any part, drain all fluids. This prevents spills, protects you from burns, and lets you sell or reuse the fluids. You need: a drain pan (at least 10 litres), a funnel, clean containers with lids, and gloves. Work outdoors or in a well-ventilated area. Never smoke near fuel.

Start with fuel. Petrol is highly flammable. Use a hand pump or siphon hose to transfer fuel into a approved container. A typical car tank holds 4060 litres. If the fuel is clean, you can sell it or use it in your own vehicle. If it's old or contaminated, store it separately for disposal. Never mix petrol with diesel. Label every container clearly.

Next, engine oil. Warm the engine for 2 minutes to thin the oil, then remove the sump plug. Let it drain into a pan. A car holds 35 litres of oil. Used oil can be sold to recyclers who refine it into base oil or burn it in industrial furnaces. In many African cities, used oil fetches $0.20$0.50 per litre. Store it in sealed drums away from water.

Coolant (antifreeze) is toxic. Drain it from the radiator and engine block. A car holds 48 litres. Coolant often contains ethylene glycol, which is deadly to humans and animals. Never pour it on the ground. Store it in a labelled container. Some recyclers will take it, or you can dispose of it at a hazardous waste facility if available.

Brake fluid is also toxic and corrosive. Drain it from the master cylinder and brake lines. A car holds about 0.51 litre. Brake fluid absorbs water and becomes useless, so it cannot be reused. Store it separately and dispose of it properly. Never mix brake fluid with other fluids.

After draining, keep all containers closed and stored in a locked area. Spills should be cleaned with sand or sawdust, not washed into drains. Proper fluid handling protects your health, your community, and your business reputation.

3. Removing and disposing of batteries, tires, and catalytic converters

Batteries, tires, and catalytic converters are the three most valuable and hazardous items to remove. Handle them with care.

Batteries: A lead-acid car battery contains sulphuric acid and lead. Wear gloves and eye protection. Disconnect the negative terminal first, then the positive. Use a battery carrier or a strong strapbatteries weigh 1525 kg. Never tilt a battery; acid can leak. Store batteries upright on a plastic tray in a covered area. A used battery is worth $5$15 as a core, or you can sell it to a recycler who pays by weight (about $0.50$1.00 per kg). Never break open a battery to drain acidthat's illegal and dangerous.

Tires: Remove the valve core to deflate the tire, then use a tire iron or bead breaker to separate the tire from the rim. A used tire with good tread can be sold for $10$30. Worn tires can be sold to a recycler who shreds them for rubber crumbs, or you can sell them as fuel to cement kilns (worth $1$3 each). Never burn tires openlythe smoke is toxic. Store tires flat, out of the sun, to prevent cracking.

Catalytic converters: These contain platinum, palladium, and rhodium. A used converter can be worth $50$200 depending on the model and condition. Use a reciprocating saw or angle grinder to cut the exhaust pipe on both sides of the converter. Wear a dust maskthe ceramic honeycomb inside can release harmful fibres. Do not break the honeycomb to extract metals yourself; sell the whole converter to a reputable recycler. Some buyers will test it with a scanner. Keep a record of the serial number if visible. Store converters in a dry place.

After removal, dispose of any remaining hazardous waste properly. Check with local authorities for licensed recyclers. In many African countries, informal buyers exist, but always prioritise safe handling and legal disposal.

4. Handling refrigerants and other hazardous materials responsibly

Air conditioning systems contain refrigerants like R-134a or R-12. These gases damage the ozone layer and contribute to global warming. Never vent refrigerant into the airit's illegal in many countries and harmful. You need a refrigerant recovery machine. These cost $300$800, but you can share one with other yards or hire a technician. Recovery captures the gas into a cylinder. Then you can sell the refrigerant to a licensed recycler or reuse it. A typical car holds 0.51 kg of refrigerant. If you can't recover it, take the vehicle to a licensed AC shop before dismantling.

Other hazardous materials include: brake fluid (already covered), transmission fluid, power steering fluid, and differential oil. These are similar to engine oildrain and store them separately. Transmission fluid can sometimes be reused if clean, but usually it's recycled. Power steering fluid is often flammable; keep it away from sparks.

Batteries contain lead and acidsee previous lesson. Never drain acid into drains. Tires are not hazardous but burning them releases toxic fumes. Catalytic converters contain precious metals but also ceramic fibreswear a mask when cutting.

Airbags are explosive. Never cut or puncture an airbag. If you must remove them, disconnect the battery and wait 30 minutes, then follow the manufacturer's procedure. Better yet, leave airbags to a professional. In many African countries, airbags are not reused due to safety concerns, but they can be sold to licensed demilitarisation companies.

Oil filters contain about 0.2 litres of used oil. Crush them and drain the oil into your used oil container. Fuel filters may contain petroldrain them too.

Always wear personal protective equipment: gloves, goggles, and a dust mask. Work in a ventilated area. Keep a fire extinguisher nearby. Store all hazardous waste in labelled, sealed containers. Check with your local environmental agency for disposal rules. Responsible handling protects your health, your community, and your business from fines.

5. Using a checklist to ensure no valuable part is missed

A checklist prevents mistakes and maximises profit. Print or write this list and use it for every vehicle. Start with exterior: check all lights (headlights, taillights, indicators) sell working bulbs and lenses. Mirrors, door handles, and grilles are quick sellers. Wipers and motors. Check the radiator grille and bumper for chrome or plastic trim. Wheels and tires: remove alloy wheels separately they sell for $20$50 each. Steel wheels are scrap.

Under the bonnet: alternator, starter, radiator, fan, condenser, compressor, power steering pump, water pump, ignition coil, distributor, ECU (engine control unit), sensors, and relays. The ECU can be worth $50$200. Remove the battery. Check the wiring harness copper wire sells for $2$5 per kg. Remove the catalytic converter. Drain all fluids.

Interior: seats (if clean, $20$100 each), dashboard, instrument cluster, radio, speakers, air vents, switches, seat belts, and carpet (if good). Check for a spare tire and tools. Remove the gear knob and handbrake lever. Glovebox and door panels. Even the rear-view mirror can sell.

Mechanical: engine and gearbox (if rebuildable), driveshaft, differential, axles, suspension struts, brake calipers, and discs. These are heavy but valuable. A used gearbox can fetch $150$500.

After removing all reusable parts, weigh the shell. Then sort scrap metal: steel, aluminium (from engine blocks and wheels), copper (wiring), and brass (radiator). Aluminium sells for $1$2 per kg, copper for $4$8 per kg. Separate them for higher prices.

Keep a tally of parts sold and their prices. After 20 cars, you'll know which parts are worth your time. Update your checklist as you learn. For example, if you find that Toyota Hilux parts sell fast, add specific items like injectors and turbochargers. A good checklist turns a $200 scrap car into $800$1,500 in parts and metal.

6. Preparing the vehicle for dismantling: lifting, supporting, and stabilizing

Before you start removing heavy parts, you must lift and support the vehicle safely. Never work under a car held only by a jack. Use jack stands rated for at least 2 tonnes each. A typical car weighs 1,2001,800 kg, so you need four stands. Place them under the chassis rails or subframe, not under the floor pan or plastic panels. For a unibody car, use the pinch welds or reinforced areas. If in doubt, consult the owner's manual for jacking points.

Use a hydraulic trolley jack (23 tonne capacity) to lift one end at a time. Lift the front first, place two stands, then lift the rear. Lower the jack slowly until the car rests on the stands. Shake the car gently to test stability. If it moves, reposition the stands. Chock the wheels that are still on the ground.

For extra safety, place large wooden blocks or spare tires under the car as a backup. Never use bricks or concrete blocksthey can crumble. If you're removing the engine, support it with an engine crane or a strong beam before unbolting the mounts. Never let the engine hang by a single chain.

When removing suspension parts, the springs are under tension. Use a spring compressor or loosen the bolts slowly while the car is on the ground. Never cut a coil spring with a torchit can explode.

Work on a flat, hard surface like concrete. Dirt or gravel can shift. If you're outside, be aware of soft ground after rain. Use plywood under the jack stands to spread the load.

Always wear steel-toe boots and gloves. Keep the work area clear of tools and debris. Have a fire extinguisher nearby. If you're working alone, tell someone. A safe dismantling job is a profitable onean injury costs you time and money. After the car is stable, you can start removing parts from the top down: battery, fluids, then engine, then suspension. Always follow your checklist.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Vehicle Assessment and Depollution

In the African car-breaking and metal scrapyard business, the difference between profit and loss is decided before the first spanner turns. A vehicle that arrives on your yard is not just scrap metal it is a bundle of recoverable parts, recyclable metals, hazardous fluids, and compliance obligations. This practical reference gives you a working method to assess any vehicle quickly, extract maximum value safely, and keep your yard compliant with environmental and safety expectations that increasingly matter to buyers, regulators, and neighbours.

Whether you operate a roadside dismantling bay in Nairobi, a scrapyard in Lagos, or a breaker's yard in Johannesburg, the same discipline applies: assess fast, drain safely, remove valuables, handle hazards responsibly, and never miss a part. Time is money, but a spill, a fire, or a seized battery can wipe out a week's profit.

1. Quick Assessment: Reusable Parts and Scrap Value

In the African market, the resale value of used parts often exceeds the scrap metal value of the shell. A 10-minute assessment should identify high-demand components before you commit to dismantling. Priority parts by demand across African markets include:

  • Alternators, starters, and ECUs high demand, easy to test, high margin.
  • Transmissions and differentials heavy but valuable, especially for popular models (Toyota, Nissan, Isuzu, Mercedes).
  • Doors, bonnets, boots, and mirrors body panels for common models sell fast.
  • Lights, grilles, and bumpers low weight, high turnover.
  • Wheels and alloy rims aluminium value plus resale.
  • Catalytic converters platinum-group metal content makes these the single most valuable scrap component.
  • Batteries lead-acid scrap value plus core exchange.

Use a simple scoring approach: for each part, note condition (A/B/C), demand (high/medium/low), and removal time. Parts with high demand and short removal time get pulled first. This protects cash flow.

2. Draining and Safely Storing Fluids

Fluids are the number one cause of environmental fines and soil contamination at African scrapyards. Drain in this order: fuel, engine oil, coolant, brake fluid, transmission fluid, power steering fluid, and refrigerant (covered separately). Use dedicated, labelled containers. Never mix fluids mixed waste has no resale value and is expensive to dispose of.

  • Fuel: Siphon or drain into approved metal containers. Petrol is a fire risk; drain outdoors, away from sparks, with a fire extinguisher within reach. Recovered fuel can be used in yard vehicles or sold where legally permitted.
  • Engine oil: Drain into a clean pan. Used oil has resale value to recyclers who re-refine it. Store in sealed drums.
  • Coolant: Ethylene glycol is toxic to humans and animals. Collect separately; never pour on the ground. Some recyclers accept it for re-refining.
  • Brake fluid: Hygroscopic and corrosive to paint. Collect in a separate container; it can be recycled or sent to a licensed hazardous waste handler.
SAFETY WARNING FIRE AND BURNS

Petrol and other fuels produce explosive vapours. Never drain fuel near open flames, welding, grinding, or running engines. Static electricity can ignite fuel vapour bond and ground containers before transferring. Coolant and brake fluid are toxic if ingested and can cause burns to skin and eyes; wear nitrile gloves and safety goggles. Used oil can contain heavy metals and carcinogens avoid prolonged skin contact. Always keep a dry-powder or foam fire extinguisher within 5 metres of any draining operation. In hot African climates, fuel vapour pressure is higher work in shade and in the cool part of the day.

3. Removing and Disposing of Batteries, Tires, and Catalytic Converters

These three items carry the highest environmental and financial risk, and the highest recovery value. Handle them first, and handle them properly.

  • Batteries: Disconnect negative terminal first, then positive. Remove and store upright on a non-conductive surface. Lead-acid batteries contain sulphuric acid and lead both hazardous. Sell to licensed lead recyclers; never break casings on site.
  • Tires: Remove and sort by size and tread depth. Reusable tires have strong resale value across Africa. Scrap tires can be sold for retreading, crumb rubber, or as fuel in licensed cement kilns. Never burn tires it releases toxic fumes and is illegal in most jurisdictions.
  • Catalytic converters: Cut or unbolt carefully the internal ceramic honeycomb contains platinum, palladium, and rhodium. Store in a secure area; theft is common. Sell only to licensed PGM recyclers who provide documentation.

4. Handling Refrigerants and Other Hazardous Materials

Air-conditioning refrigerant must never be vented to atmosphere. R134a and R1234yf are potent greenhouse gases; R12 depletes the ozone layer. In many African countries, refrigerant recovery is still rare, but demand for compliant yards is rising among corporate buyers and export partners. Use a certified recovery machine to extract refrigerant into a recovery cylinder, then send it to a licensed reclaimer. If you cannot recover on site, do not puncture the system leave it sealed and send the vehicle to a facility with recovery equipment.

Other hazardous materials to check: airbag modules (do not cut deploy or sell to licensed handlers), lead wheel weights, mercury switches in older vehicles, and contaminated fuel filters. Each has a specific route; document what you remove and where it goes.

5. Using a Checklist to Ensure No Valuable Part Is Missed

A checklist is not bureaucracy it is profit protection. A missed alternator or catalytic converter is money left in the shell when it goes to the baler. Print the checklist below, laminate it, and attach it to each vehicle as it enters the bay. Tick as you go; sign off before the shell leaves the yard.

6. Preparing the Vehicle for Dismantling: Lifting, Supporting, and Stabilizing

Once fluids are drained and hazardous items removed, the vehicle must be stable before you cut, lift, or drop components. On uneven African yard surfaces, stability is critical.

  • Use rated jack stands on solid ground never rely on a hydraulic jack alone.
  • Chock wheels if the vehicle remains on the ground.
  • For shells being lifted, use a certified two-post or four-post lift, or a forklift with proper slings.
  • Never work under a vehicle supported only by bricks, logs, or rims.
  • Keep the work area clear of fluids, tools, and scrap to prevent slips.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Record make, model, year, VIN, and engine code.
  2. Photograph the vehicle before dismantling for records and resale listings.
  3. Remove battery first; store upright and off the ground.
  4. Drain fuel, oil, coolant, brake fluid, and transmission fluid into labelled containers.
  5. Recover refrigerant with certified equipment; never vent.
  6. Remove catalytic converter, tires, and any airbag modules; store securely.
  7. Pull high-demand parts (alternator, starter, ECU, lights, panels) and tag them.
  8. Stabilise the shell on jack stands or lift before any cutting or heavy removal.
  9. Sign off the checklist and record where each hazardous item was sent.
