1. Designing a Drip Irrigation System

A drip system saves water and labour, but you must design it for your plot. Start by measuring your field: 1 hectare is 100 m by 100 m. For vegetables, use drip lines spaced 3050 cm apart, with emitters every 2030 cm. If you grow tomatoes on 1 m beds, you need about 10,000 m of drip line per hectare. Calculate water needs: each plant needs 24 litres per day. With 20,000 plants, that's 40,00080,000 litres daily. A pump delivering 10,000 litres per hour can run for 48 hours. Choose a pump size: for a 1-hectare field, a 1.52 HP pump with a flow of 1020 m/h is typical. You also need a filter (screen or sand) to prevent clogging, a pressure regulator (11.5 bar), and a mainline (5063 mm PVC) and sub-mainlines (3240 mm). Lay drip lines along the beds. Use connectors and end caps. In Africa, many farmers use gravity-fed systems from a tank on a 23 m stand; that gives about 0.20.3 bar, enough for short runs. For larger fields, use a solar pump. Cost: a basic kit for 1 hectare costs $800$1,500. You can start with a smaller area and expand. Always install a valve for each section so you can water different crops separately. Test the system for even emission. Remember: drip irrigation reduces water use by 3050% compared to furrow. It also reduces weeds and diseases. But you must maintain it. Design for easy flushing: put flush valves at the end of each drip line. If your water is dirty, use a settling tank before the filter. Plan your layout on paper first. Mark the position of the pump, tank, mainlines, and beds. This saves time and money.

2. Scheduling Irrigation Based on Crop Needs

Crops need different amounts of water at different stages. For tomatoes, water 24 litres per plant per day during fruiting, but only 12 litres during early growth. For leafy greens like kale, 23 litres per plant per day. For onions, 12 litres. The key is to check soil moisture before watering. Stick your finger 10 cm into the soil. If it feels dry, irrigate. If it's moist, wait. A simple tool is a tensiometer, but you can also use a shovel to dig a small hole and feel the soil. In sandy soil, water drains quickly, so you may need to irrigate daily. In clay soil, water stays longer, so every 23 days. A good rule: irrigate when the soil moisture drops to 50% of field capacity. For drip, run the system for 12 hours per day, depending on flow rate. If each emitter gives 2 litres per hour, and you have 20,000 emitters, you deliver 40,000 litres in 1 hour. That's enough for 1 hectare of tomatoes. But in hot weather, increase to 1.52 hours. In cool weather, reduce. Use a simple schedule: early morning (68 am) is best because less evaporation. Avoid evening watering to prevent fungal diseases. Also, check the weather forecast: if rain is expected, skip irrigation. You can use a rain gauge or just a tin can. Keep a record of when you irrigate and how much. After a week, adjust. For example, if plants look wilted in the afternoon, they need more water. If leaves turn yellow and soil is soggy, you are overwatering. A cheap moisture meter costs $10$20. It saves water and improves yields. Remember: overwatering leaches nutrients and causes root rot. Underwatering stresses plants and reduces yield. So schedule based on crop, soil, and weather.

3. Rainwater Harvesting and Storage

In many parts of Africa, rain is seasonal. You can harvest rainwater from roofs or from the ground. For a 1-hectare farm, you need storage for dry spells. A simple method: dig a pond lined with clay or plastic. A pond of 10 m x 10 m x 2 m deep holds 200,000 litres. That can irrigate 1 hectare for 23 days. But you can also use tanks. A 5,000-litre tank costs $500$800. To fill it, you need a roof area of about 100 m with 50 mm of rain. That's common in a single storm. So harvest from your house, shed, or a small shelter. Use gutters and a first-flush diverter to keep out leaves and dirt. For field-scale, build a check dam or a small reservoir. In dry areas, use a sand dam: build a concrete wall across a seasonal riverbed, and sand accumulates behind it. Water is stored in the sand and can be extracted with a well. Another method: contour ridges and tied ridges to capture runoff in the field. For storage, a lined pond with a plastic liner (0.5 mm thick) costs about $2$3 per m. A 200 m pond costs $400$600. Cover the pond to reduce evaporation: use a shade net or floating balls. Evaporation can lose 510 mm per day. That's 1,0002,000 litres per day from a 200 m pond. So cover it. Also, keep the water clean: use a filter before drip irrigation. You can also harvest rainwater from the drip system's runoff? No, drip doesn't produce runoff. But you can direct roof water to a tank, then use a pump to the drip system. A solar pump (200 W) can lift water from a tank to a 2 m high tank for gravity feed. Cost: $200$400. Start small: a 1,000-litre tank for a nursery. Then scale up. Rainwater harvesting reduces your water bill and gives you control.

4. Using Mulch to Conserve Moisture

Mulch is any material you spread on the soil surface to reduce evaporation. In Africa, you can use dry grass, straw, maize stover, banana leaves, or plastic mulch. Organic mulch: spread a layer 510 cm thick around plants. It keeps soil moist, suppresses weeds, and adds organic matter. For example, in a tomato field, apply mulch after transplanting. You need about 23 tonnes of dry grass per hectare. That's a lot, but you can collect from nearby fields. Plastic mulch: use black or silver plastic film. It warms soil, prevents weeds, and saves water. A roll of 1.2 m wide plastic covers about 100 m of bed. Cost: $30$50 per roll. You need about 100 rolls for 1 hectare if beds are 1 m apart. That's $3,000$5,000, which is expensive. But it lasts 23 seasons if you handle it carefully. Organic mulch is cheaper but breaks down. You can also use living mulch: plant cover crops like cowpea or sweet potato between rows. They shade the soil and reduce evaporation. But they compete for water, so only use in rainy season. The best mulch for water conservation is a combination: organic mulch under the plants, and plastic on the paths? No, keep it simple. For a 1-hectare vegetable farm, start with organic mulch on half the field and compare. You will see that mulched soil stays moist 23 days longer. That means you can irrigate every 3 days instead of every day. You save 3050% water. Also, mulch keeps soil temperature stable, which helps roots. Apply mulch after rain or irrigation, when soil is wet. Don't put mulch right against the stem; leave a small gap to prevent rot. Reapply as it decomposes. In dry areas, mulch is essential. It is one of the cheapest ways to improve water use. Use what you have: grass, leaves, crop residues. Avoid mulching with fresh manure or hay that has seeds. Always test a small area first.

5. Monitoring Soil Moisture with Sensors

You don't need expensive tools to monitor soil moisture. But sensors give you accurate data. The cheapest is a tensiometer: a tube with a ceramic tip and a gauge. It measures how hard plants must work to extract water. Cost: $30$80. Place tensiometers at 20 cm and 40 cm depth. When the reading is above 50 centibars (cb), it's time to irrigate. For sandy soil, irrigate at 30 cb; for clay, at 60 cb. Another tool: a capacitance probe or a handheld moisture meter. These cost $50$200. They give a percentage reading. You can also use a simple gypsum block, but it degrades. For a 1-hectare farm, you need at least 35 sensors at different points. If you can't afford sensors, use the feel method: squeeze soil in your hand. If it forms a ball and leaves a slight moisture on your palm, it's fine. If it crumbles, it's dry. If it drips, it's too wet. You can also use a shovel to check. But sensors save water and time. For drip irrigation, you can automate with a controller that reads sensors. A basic controller costs $100$200. It turns the pump on when soil moisture drops below a set point. That saves water and labour. In Africa, you can use a smartphone app like 'Soil Moisture' or 'Irrigation Manager' to log readings. But you need a sensor that connects via Bluetooth. Some farmers use a simple tensiometer and check it daily. That's enough. Keep a record: date, reading, and action. After a season, you will know your soil's behaviour. Remember: sensors must be installed correctly. Insert them in the root zone, not on the surface. Avoid areas that are too wet or dry. Calibrate them according to the manufacturer. If you use a tensiometer, refill it with distilled water and remove air bubbles. A well-monitored system uses 2030% less water. So the investment pays back in one season.

6. Maintaining Irrigation Lines and Emitters

Drip irrigation clogs easily. You must maintain it weekly. First, flush the lines. Open the end caps of each drip line and let water run for 23 minutes. Do this every week. This removes sediment and roots. Second, clean the filter. If you have a screen filter, remove the screen and rinse it. If it's a sand filter, backwash it. Do this daily if water is dirty. Third, check emitters. Walk along the lines and look for dry spots or gushers. A clogged emitter shows no water; a broken one shows a jet. Replace clogged emitters with a goof plug or a new emitter. You can also use a needle to clean the emitter, but be careful not to damage it. Fourth, use acid treatment. Once a month, inject a small amount of hydrochloric acid (0.5%) or vinegar to dissolve mineral deposits. But be careful: wear gloves and goggles. For organic matter, use chlorine (12 ppm) once a month. Fifth, check pressure. Use a pressure gauge at the end of the mainline. If pressure drops, there is a leak or clog. Fix leaks with tape or new connectors. Sixth, store drip lines properly during off-season. Remove them, clean them, and hang them in a shed. Don't leave them in the sun. UV light damages plastic. Seventh, protect against rodents. Mice and rats chew drip lines. Use traps or keep the field clean. Eighth, train your workers. Show them how to flush and check emitters. A simple maintenance schedule: daily: check filter and pressure. Weekly: flush lines and check emitters. Monthly: acid or chlorine treatment. Yearly: replace damaged lines. If you maintain well, a drip system lasts 510 years. If not, it clogs in one season. So spend 30 minutes per day on maintenance. It saves water, money, and crops.

<p>Water is the single largest controllable input in a one-hectare vegetable enterprise and the one most often wasted. On a well-managed hectare of drip-irrigated tomatoes, onions, or kale, water costs can run 815% of total variable costs. On a poorly managed hectare using flood or overhead irrigation, that figure can double, and yields can fall 3050% through waterlogging, disease, and leaching of the fertiliser you paid for.</p>

<p>This module gives you the practical numbers, design rules, and maintenance routines to run a profitable one-hectare vegetable block. It is written for the African smallholder and emerging commercial grower: limited capital, unreliable grid power, seasonal rainfall, and a market that pays for quality and consistency. Every recommendation here is chosen because it pays back within one or two seasons.</p>

<h3>Designing a Drip Irrigation System for One Hectare</h3>

<p>Design starts with the crop, not the pipe. A hectare of tomatoes at 1.0 m 0.4 m spacing has roughly 25,000 plants. At peak demand, each plant needs 24 litres per day. That is 50,000100,000 litres (50100 m) per day the number that determines your pump, tank, and pipe sizes.</p>

<p>Rules of thumb for a one-hectare drip block in Africa:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Emitter flow:</strong> 2 L/h for sandy soils (wets a wider bulb faster), 4 L/h for heavy clay loams.</li>
<li><strong>Emitter spacing:</strong> 30 cm for vegetables, 20 cm for lettuce and herbs.</li>
<li><strong>Lateral spacing:</strong> match row spacing 0.91.2 m for most vegetables.</li>
<li><strong>Lateral length:</strong> keep below 60 m on flat ground to maintain pressure uniformity above 90%.</li>
<li><strong>Filtration:</strong> 120-mesh screen filter minimum; 200-mesh if using surface water or fertigation.</li>
<li><strong>Pump sizing:</strong> assume 2 bar operating pressure at the filter outlet plus friction losses.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Scheduling Irrigation Based on Crop Needs</h3>

<p>Do not irrigate on a fixed calendar. Irrigate when the crop needs it. The practical method for a one-hectare block is the <strong>checkbook method</strong>: track daily crop water use (ETc) and rainfall, and refill the soil when the deficit reaches your allowable depletion.</p>

<p>ETc = ETo Kc, where ETo is reference evapotranspiration (available free from FAO's CROPWAT or national meteorological services) and Kc is the crop coefficient. For tomatoes at mid-season, Kc 1.15. If ETo = 5 mm/day, ETc = 5.75 mm/day. One hectare needs 57.5 m of water per day matching the table above.</p>

<p>Run drip for the number of hours needed to replace that deficit. A 1-hectare block with 25,000 emitters at 2 L/h delivers 50,000 L/h = 50 m/h. To apply 57.5 m, run the system for about 1 hour 10 minutes. Split into two sessions (early morning and late afternoon) on very hot days to reduce evaporation and heat stress.</p>

<h3>Rainwater Harvesting and Storage</h3>

<p>In most of sub-Saharan Africa, a 1-hectare vegetable block receives 6001,200 mm of rain per year but in 23 heavy events. Capturing even 10% of that on a 1,000 m roof or runoff catchment yields 60120 m per year: enough to cover 1525% of your dry-season irrigation. A 10,000-litre tank (US$400800 installed) pays back in one season at a water cost of US$0.30/m.</p>

<p>Practical rules: line earth dams and ponds with 1.0 mm HDPE geomembrane; cover tanks to reduce evaporation and mosquito breeding; install a first-flush diverter to keep silt and bird droppings out of the tank.</p>

<h3>Using Mulch to Conserve Moisture</h3>

<p>Organic mulch (crop residue, grass, straw) 510 cm thick cuts evaporation from the soil surface by 3050% and suppresses weeds. On a one-hectare tomato block, mulching can save 8001,200 m of water per season worth US$240360 in water cost alone, before counting labour savings on weeding. Black plastic mulch is more expensive (US$300500/ha) but warms soil, reduces disease splash, and is common in commercial tomato and pepper production. Biodegradable mulch films now cost within 15% of conventional plastic and avoid disposal problems.</p>

<h3>Monitoring Soil Moisture with Sensors</h3>

<p>You do not need expensive equipment. A US$1540 tensiometer or a US$20 capacitance probe at 20 cm and 40 cm depth tells you when to irrigate and when to stop. Read them every morning at the same time. Target range for vegetables: 2040 kPa (tensiometer) in sandy soils, 3050 kPa in loams. If you cannot afford sensors, use the <strong>feel and appearance method</strong>: squeeze a handful of soil from 20 cm depth. If it forms a ball that crumbles easily, irrigate soon. If it drips, stop.</p>

<h3>Maintaining Irrigation Lines and Emitters</h3>

<p>Clogged emitters are the number one cause of drip system failure in Africa. Chlorine, iron, and calcium in borehole water, plus algae in surface water, block emitters within weeks if not managed. Maintenance schedule:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Weekly:</strong> flush lateral ends for 2 minutes; clean the screen filter.</li>
<li><strong>Monthly:</strong> inject chlorine (12 ppm free chlorine) for 30 minutes; check emitter flow in 5 random spots.</li>
<li><strong>Seasonally:</strong> acid-treat with 1% hydrochloric or citric acid to dissolve calcium; replace damaged laterals.</li>
<li><strong>Annually:</strong> replace filter cartridges, check pump seals, and test pressure at the farthest emitter.</li>
</ul>
