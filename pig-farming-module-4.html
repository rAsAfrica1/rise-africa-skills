1. Nutrient Requirements for Different Production Stages

Pigs need different nutrients at different stages of life. A piglet from birth to weaning (0-4 weeks) depends on sow's milk, which provides high energy, protein, and antibodies. If you wean early, use a creep feed with 20-22% crude protein (CP) and 14-16 MJ/kg digestible energy (DE). From 4-8 weeks (weaner), feed 18-20% CP and 13.5-14.5 MJ/kg DE. From 8-12 weeks (grower), drop to 16-18% CP and 13-14 MJ/kg DE. From 12 weeks to market (finisher), use 14-16% CP and 12.5-13.5 MJ/kg DE. For pregnant sows, feed 12-14% CP and 12-13 MJ/kg DE, but limit feed to 2-2.5 kg per day to prevent fatness. Lactating sows need 16-18% CP and 13-14 MJ/kg DE, and should eat 4-6 kg per day to produce milk. Boars need 14-16% CP and 12.5-13.5 MJ/kg DE. In Africa, many smallholders use local ingredients like maize, cassava, and soybean meal. For example, a typical piglet creep feed might be 60% maize, 25% soybean meal, 10% fishmeal, and 5% minerals/vitamins. Always provide clean water and a mineral lick. If you see poor growth, check protein and energy first. Remember, overfeeding pregnant sows wastes money and causes farrowing problems. Underfeeding lactating sows reduces milk and piglet survival. Adjust feed based on body condition: thin sows get more, fat sows get less. Use a weighing scale or a plastic bucket marked in kilograms to measure feed accurately. In Africa, where feed costs are high, matching nutrients to stage is the cheapest way to get good growth.

2. Common Feed Ingredients and Formulation

In Africa, pig feed is usually made from energy sources, protein sources, and supplements. Energy sources include maize (corn), cassava, sorghum, and millet. Maize is the best, with about 8-9% CP and high energy. Cassava is cheaper but low in protein (2-3% CP) and must be dried to avoid cyanide poisoning. Protein sources include soybean meal (44-48% CP), groundnut cake (40-45% CP), sunflower cake (28-30% CP), and fishmeal (55-65% CP). Fishmeal is excellent but expensive; use 5-10% in piglet diets. Animal by-products like blood meal (80% CP) and meat-and-bone meal (45-50% CP) can be used but check quality. Minerals and vitamins are needed: limestone (calcium), bone meal (calcium and phosphorus), and salt. A simple formulation for a grower pig (50 kg) might be: 65% maize, 20% soybean meal, 10% groundnut cake, 3% fishmeal, 1% limestone, 0.5% salt, and 0.5% vitamin-mineral premix. This gives about 16% CP. For a finisher, reduce soybean meal to 15% and increase maize to 70%. Always mix ingredients thoroughly on a clean concrete floor or in a mixer. Use a weighing scale, not cups, for accuracy. In Africa, many farmers use a hammer mill to grind maize and other grains. Store ingredients in dry, raised bags to prevent mold. Aflatoxin in maize and groundnut cake is a major risk; never use moldy feed. If you cannot afford soybean meal, use cowpeas or pigeon peas, but they need cooking to destroy anti-nutritional factors. Test your feed by weighing pigs monthly; if they gain less than 500 g per day, adjust the formula. Formulating on-farm saves 30-50% compared to buying commercial feed.

3. Feed Processing and Storage Best Practices

Processing feed properly improves digestion and reduces waste. First, grind all grains to a medium texture (like coarse sand). Fine powder causes dust and respiratory problems; too coarse reduces digestion. Use a hammer mill or a mortar and pestle for small scale. After grinding, mix ingredients thoroughly for at least 5 minutes. For piglets, you may need to pellet the feed to prevent sorting. Pelleting requires a pellet machine, but you can also moisten the feed slightly and press it. However, wet feed spoils quickly, so only mix what pigs eat in one day. For storage, keep feed in a cool, dry, dark place. Use raised pallets or wooden planks to keep bags off the floor. Never store feed directly on concrete, as moisture causes mold. Airtight plastic containers or metal silos are best; if using bags, tie them tightly. In humid African climates, add a mold inhibitor like propionic acid if available, or mix in 1% calcium propionate. Check for pests: rats and weevils. Use traps or cats, and avoid chemical poisons near feed. Rotate stock: use older feed first. Never store feed for more than 4 weeks in hot weather; vitamins degrade. If you see caking, discoloration, or a musty smell, discard it. Aflatoxin is invisible but deadly; test with a black light or send a sample to a lab. For silage or fermented feed, use airtight containers and keep pH below 4.5. In Africa, many farmers use plastic drums for fermentation of cassava or other by-products. Always clean storage areas monthly. Proper storage saves money by preventing spoilage and keeps pigs healthy.

4. Feeding Systems and Delivery Methods

How you feed pigs affects growth, health, and feed waste. The three main systems are dry feeding, wet feeding, and automatic feeding. Dry feeding is most common in Africa: you give dry feed in troughs. Use troughs with dividers to prevent bullying. Allow 30 cm of trough space per pig for grow-finish pigs. For piglets, use shallow troughs. Feed twice daily: morning and evening. Remove leftovers before the next feed to prevent mold. Wet feeding mixes feed with water (1:1 to 1:2 ratio) and is common in smallholder systems. It reduces dust and improves palatability, but wet feed spoils in 2-3 hours, especially in heat. Only mix what pigs eat in 30 minutes. Wet feeding can improve growth by 5-10% but requires more labour. Automatic feeders are used in large farms; they dispense dry feed on demand. For sows, use individual feeding stalls or crates to control intake. For piglets, use creep feeders with small holes. In Africa, many farmers use simple wooden or concrete troughs. Ensure troughs are smooth and easy to clean. Never feed on the ground; it causes parasite infection and waste. For lactating sows, feed 2-3 times daily. For boars, feed once daily. Weigh feed using a scale or a marked bucket. A common mistake is overfeeding finishers, leading to fat pigs and low prices. Use a feed chart: for example, a 50 kg pig eats 2 kg per day; a 90 kg pig eats 3 kg per day. Adjust based on body condition. Always provide water alongside feed. In group housing, use several feeding points to reduce aggression. Clean troughs daily with a brush and water. This prevents disease and keeps feed fresh.

5. Water Quality and Availability

Water is the most important nutrient for pigs. A pig can survive weeks without feed but only days without water. Pigs need 2-3 litres of water per kilogram of dry feed. A lactating sow may drink 20-30 litres per day. A grower pig (50 kg) needs 5-8 litres daily. In hot African climates, needs increase by 50%. Always provide clean, fresh water. Water quality matters: it should be clear, odourless, and free of harmful bacteria. Test your water source yearly for nitrates, salts, and bacteria. If using borehole or well water, check for iron and sulphur; high levels cause diarrhoea and poor growth. Rainwater is good but must be stored in clean tanks. Surface water from rivers or ponds can carry diseases; treat it by boiling, chlorinating (add 2-5 ml of bleach per 20 litres), or filtering. Never let pigs drink from stagnant pools. Provide water in clean troughs or nipple drinkers. Nipple drinkers save water and keep it clean, but pigs need training. For smallholders, use concrete or plastic troughs. Clean troughs daily; algae and dirt reduce intake. In hot weather, water can heat up; place troughs in shade. Check water flow rate: a nipple drinker should deliver 1-2 litres per minute for grow-finish pigs. For sows, 2-4 litres per minute. If pigs fight over water, add more drinkers. Dehydration signs: sunken eyes, dry skin, constipation. If you see these, check water supply immediately. In Africa, water scarcity is common; harvest rainwater and store in covered tanks. Add electrolytes (salt and sugar) during heat stress. Always have at least two water sources in case one fails. Clean water is cheaper than treating disease.

6. Managing Feed Costs and Efficiency

Feed is 60-80% of pig production costs. To make a profit, you must manage feed costs without sacrificing nutrition. First, buy ingredients in bulk when prices are low. Maize is cheapest after harvest; store it properly. Join a farmer cooperative to buy soybean meal and premixes at wholesale prices. Second, formulate feed on-farm using local ingredients. For example, replace some maize with cassava chips (up to 30% for finishers) or sweet potato. Use by-products like brewery waste, molasses, or rice bran. But balance nutrients: cassava is low in protein, so add more soybean meal. Third, reduce waste. Use good troughs, don't overfill, and store feed properly. A 5% waste reduction saves 5% of feed cost. Fourth, improve feed conversion ratio (FCR). FCR is feed eaten divided by weight gain. A good FCR is 3:1 for finishers (3 kg feed per 1 kg gain). If your FCR is 4:1, you are losing money. Improve by feeding the right nutrients, controlling disease, and providing clean water. Fifth, use phase feeding: give high-protein feed to young pigs and lower-protein to finishers. This matches nutrients to needs. Sixth, consider alternative feeds: insects (black soldier fly larvae) are 40-45% CP and can replace fishmeal. Grow them on waste. Also, ferment feed to improve digestibility. Seventh, track everything: weigh feed and pigs. Keep records. If a pig gains 700 g per day on 2.5 kg feed, FCR is 3.6. If you change feed and gain drops, adjust. Finally, sell pigs at the right weight. Going from 90 kg to 100 kg takes more feed per kg gain; often it's better to sell at 90 kg. In Africa, every kilogram of feed saved is money in your pocket.

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist: Daily &amp; Weekly Feed Management</h2>

<ol style="font-family:Arial,sans-serif; font-size:14px; line-height:1.7;">
<li><strong>Weigh feed, don't guess.</strong> Use a hanging scale or calibrated bucket. Overfeeding finishers by 10% wastes 1520 kg feed per pig pure lost profit.</li>
<li><strong>Check water flow daily.</strong> Nipple drinkers should deliver at least 1 litre/minute. Clean troughs every morning algae and biofilm reduce intake.</li>
<li><strong>Match feed to stage.</strong> Never feed finisher ration to weaners. The protein gap slows growth and extends days to market, increasing fixed costs.</li>
<li><strong>Smell and inspect every batch.</strong> Reject musty, clumped, or insect-infested feed. One bad batch can wipe out a month's margin.</li>
<li><strong>Record daily feed intake per pen.</strong> A sudden 10% drop is your earliest disease warning act before mortality starts.</li>
<li><strong>Calculate FCR monthly.</strong> Total feed consumed total weight gained. If FCR rises above 3.5 in finishers, investigate feed quality, health, or genetics.</li>
<li><strong>Buy ingredients when prices dip.</strong> Maize and soya prices follow seasonal harvest cycles forward-buy if you have dry storage.</li>
<li><strong>Mix only what you'll use in 710 days.</strong> Freshly mixed feed loses vitamin potency and risks mould growth in tropical humidity.</li>
</ol>
