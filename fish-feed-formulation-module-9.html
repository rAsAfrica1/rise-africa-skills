1. Feeding Rates and Frequency

Feeding rate is the amount of feed you give your fish daily, usually expressed as a percentage of their body weight. For tilapia, a common rate is 3-5% of body weight per day for fingerlings, 2-3% for juveniles, and 1-2% for adults. For catfish, rates are higher: 5-8% for fry, 3-5% for fingerlings, and 1-2% for table-size fish. You must sample your fish regularly to know their average weight. Use a kitchen scale to weigh 10-20 fish from different parts of the pond, then calculate the average. Multiply by the total number of fish to get total biomass. Then apply the percentage. For example, if you have 1,000 tilapia averaging 100g each, total biomass is 100kg. At 3% feeding rate, you need 3kg of feed per day. Feed frequency matters: small fish need more frequent meals. Fry should be fed 4-6 times daily, fingerlings 3-4 times, and adults 2-3 times. In Africa, where labor may be limited, you can feed twice dailyâmorning and late afternoonâbut never at midday when it's too hot. Adjust feeding rates based on water temperature: below 20Â°C, fish eat less; above 30Â°C, they may stop eating. Also, check for leftover feed: if feed remains after 30 minutes, reduce the next ration. Overfeeding wastes money and pollutes water; underfeeding stunts growth. Keep records of feed given and fish growth to fine-tune your rates. In rural areas, use locally available materials like a hanging scale or a simple balance to weigh feed. Always store feed in a cool, dry place to prevent mold. Remember, feeding rate is not fixedâit changes as fish grow and seasons change.

2. Feeding Methods and Equipment

How you feed your fish is as important as what you feed. There are three main methods: hand feeding, automatic feeders, and demand feeders. Hand feeding is the most common in Africa because it's cheap and allows you to observe fish behavior. Broadcast the feed evenly over the pond surface so all fish get a chance. For small ponds, use a plastic scoop or your hand. For larger ponds, use a bucket and walk around the perimeter. Automatic feeders are powered by batteries or electricity and release feed at set times. They save labor but are expensive and require maintenance. Demand feeders let fish trigger feed release by bumping a pendulum; they reduce waste but can be dominated by aggressive fish. In recirculating systems, use automatic feeders with timers. For catfish, which are bottom feeders, use sinking pellets; for tilapia, floating pellets are better because you can see when they stop eating. A simple tool is a feeding tray or ring: a floating ring that contains feed, allowing you to check consumption. In Africa, you can make a homemade demand feeder from a plastic bottle and a stick. Always feed at the same time and place daily to train fish. Avoid feeding during heavy rain or when water is turbid. Clean feeders regularly to prevent mold. For small-scale farmers, hand feeding is best because you can adjust based on weather and fish appetite. Remember, equipment should be affordable and locally repairable. Use a plastic bucket with holes for broadcasting, or a PVC pipe for sinking feed. Never use rusty metal containers. Proper feeding methods reduce waste and improve growth.

3. Feed Conversion Efficiency and Optimization

Feed Conversion Ratio (FCR) is the amount of feed needed to produce 1kg of fish. If you feed 1.5kg of feed and your fish gain 1kg, FCR is 1.5. Lower FCR means better efficiency and profit. For tilapia, a good FCR is 1.2-1.8; for catfish, 1.0-1.5. To calculate FCR, record total feed given and total fish weight gained. For example, if you stocked 100kg of fish and harvested 300kg, weight gain is 200kg. If you used 300kg of feed, FCR = 300/200 = 1.5. To optimize FCR, use high-quality feed with balanced protein (28-32% for tilapia, 35-40% for catfish). Avoid overfeeding: uneaten feed sinks and decays, lowering water quality and FCR. Feed at the right rate and frequency. Use floating pellets to monitor consumption. Maintain good water quality: low oxygen or high ammonia reduces appetite and growth. Keep stocking density appropriateâtoo many fish cause stress and poor FCR. Harvest at the right size: older fish convert feed less efficiently. In Africa, where feed costs are high, improving FCR by 0.2 can save significant money. For example, if feed costs $0.50 per kg, reducing FCR from 1.8 to 1.6 saves $0.10 per kg of fish. Use local ingredients like cassava, maize, and fishmeal to formulate cheap but effective feeds. Always weigh feed and fish samples accurately. Keep records. Test different feeds and feeding strategies to see what works best in your system. Remember, FCR is not just about feedâit's about management.

4. Feeding Behavior and Environmental Factors

Fish feeding behavior tells you a lot about their health and the environment. Healthy fish swim actively to the surface at feeding time. If they ignore feed, something is wrong. Check water temperature: tilapia feed best at 25-30Â°C, catfish at 25-30Â°C. Below 20Â°C, feeding drops; below 15Â°C, fish may stop eating. Above 32Â°C, appetite decreases. Dissolved oxygen is critical: if oxygen is below 3mg/L, fish gasp at the surface and won't eat. Use a simple oxygen test kit or observe behavior. Ammonia and nitrite should be near zero; high levels burn gills and reduce feeding. pH should be 6.5-8.5. Turbidity from mud or algae can block visibility for visual feeders like tilapia. In Africa, seasonal changes matter: during rainy season, water may become acidic and cold; adjust feeding. During dry season, water levels drop and temperatures rise; feed early morning or evening. Feed only what fish will eat in 15-20 minutes. If feed remains after 30 minutes, reduce the next ration. Observe fish: if they spit out feed, it may be too large or stale. For catfish, which are nocturnal, feed at dusk and dawn. For tilapia, feed during daylight. In recirculating systems, monitor water quality daily because waste accumulates quickly. Use a thermometer and pH kit (costing about $10-20) to track conditions. Keep a log of temperature, feeding response, and weather. Adjust feeding rates based on these factors. Never feed when fish are stressed from handling or transport. Good observation saves money and fish.

5. Feed Management in Recirculating Systems

Recirculating aquaculture systems (RAS) reuse water by filtering and pumping it back to the fish tanks. In RAS, feed management is critical because waste builds up quickly. You must feed a precise amount to avoid overloading the biofilter. Use high-quality, highly digestible feed to minimize waste. For tilapia in RAS, feed 1-2% of body weight daily, split into 3-4 meals. For catfish, 1-2% daily, split into 2-3 meals. Always use floating pellets so you can see if fish eat. Remove uneaten feed immediately with a net. In RAS, uneaten feed decays and produces ammonia, which is toxic. Test ammonia and nitrite daily; they should be 0 mg/L. Use a test kit (about $15). Monitor dissolved oxygen: it should be above 5mg/L. Use aerators or oxygen cones. Water temperature should be stable at 26-30Â°C for warm-water fish. In Africa, power outages are common; have a backup battery or generator for pumps and aerators. Feed only when the biofilter is mature (4-6 weeks after start-up). Start with small rations and increase gradually. Keep records of feed given, water quality, and fish growth. Use automatic feeders with timers to ensure consistent feeding. Clean feeders and tanks regularly to prevent bacterial growth. In RAS, feed conversion is often better (FCR 1.0-1.2) because you control the environment. However, feed cost is higher due to high-quality ingredients. To save money, you can formulate feed using local ingredients like soybean meal, maize, and fishmeal. Always store feed in sealed containers to prevent moisture. Remember, in RAS, every gram of feed mattersâwaste is your enemy.

6. Reducing Feed Waste and Environmental Impact

Feed waste is money lost and pollution gained. In Africa, where feed can be 60-70% of production costs, reducing waste is essential. First, feed the right amount: use feeding tables based on fish size and water temperature. For example, at 28Â°C, feed tilapia 2-3% of body weight; at 22Â°C, reduce to 1-2%. Second, use the right feed type: floating pellets for tilapia allow you to see consumption; sinking pellets for catfish should be fed slowly so they don't hit the bottom uneaten. Third, feed at the right time: early morning and late afternoon when oxygen is higher. Avoid feeding during rain or extreme heat. Fourth, use proper methods: broadcast feed evenly, not in one spot. Use a feeding ring to contain floating feed and check leftovers. Fifth, store feed properly: keep in cool, dry place, away from sunlight. Moldy feed is toxic and uneaten. Sixth, train fish to eat at a specific spot and time. Seventh, monitor water quality: poor quality reduces appetite and increases waste. Eighth, use high-quality feed with balanced nutrients; cheap feed often has poor digestibility, leading to more waste. Ninth, consider using biofloc or integrated systems where waste feeds other organisms. Tenth, keep records: track feed given and fish growth to adjust. For environmental impact, uneaten feed decays, consuming oxygen and releasing ammonia, which can kill fish and pollute waterways. In Africa, where water is scarce, reducing waste also saves water. You can compost feed waste or use it as fertilizer. Remember, every kilogram of feed saved is profit. Aim for FCR below 1.8 for tilapia and 1.5 for catfish. Small changesâlike using a feeding tray or adjusting ratesâcan cut waste by 20-30%.

FCR = feed fed Ã· fish weight gained. A farm buying 30% protein extruded floating pellets for tilapia should target FCR 1.4â1.7 in ponds and 1.1â1.4 in cages or RAS. Catfish (Clarias) typically run 0.9â1.2 on well-formulated sinking or floating diets.

Levers that move FCR in the right direction:

  • Water quality first. Low dissolved oxygen (<3 mg/L) suppresses appetite and wastes feed. Aerate at night if stocking is intensive.
  • Protein matching. Do not feed 35% protein to a 600 g tilapia â 28â30% is enough and cheaper per kg gain.
  • Pellet quality. Water-stable floating pellets reduce leaching. Test by soaking a handful for 10 minutes; good pellets hold shape.
  • Split rations. Three smaller meals beat one large meal for digestibility and water quality.
  • Record everything. A simple notebook of daily feed issued vs. fish sampled is the backbone of FCR control.

4. Feeding Behaviour and Environmental Factors

Fish eat less when dissolved oxygen is low, temperature drops below 24Â°C, ammonia exceeds 0.5 mg/L, or during heavy rain and sudden temperature shifts. In African ponds subject to Harmattan or cool highland nights, morning feeding may be delayed until water warms. Observe the surface: vigorous feeding response = healthy stock; sluggish or no response = investigate before adding more feed.

5. Feed Management in Recirculating Systems

RAS farms in Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa run at high stocking density, so feed is the dominant waste stream. Use high-digestibility diets, feed to apparent satiation in short bursts, and remove uneaten pellets promptly. Solids filters and biofilters must be sized to the feed load â roughly 25â30% of feed dry matter becomes solid waste and 30â40% becomes dissolved nitrogen. Underfeeding in RAS is safer than overfeeding; the biofilter will tell you within 24 hours via rising ammonia.

Never feed mouldy, caked, or musty-smelling pellets. Aflatoxin B1 from Aspergillus flavus in poorly dried maize, groundnut cake, or fishmeal causes liver damage in fish and can pass into fillets, creating a food-safety and export-rejection risk. Store feed off the floor on pallets in a dry, ventilated room below 25Â°C and under 12% moisture; use within 6â8 weeks in humid coastal climates. Discard any bag with visible mould â the cost of one bag is trivial against a rejected export consignment or a stunted crop.

6. Reducing Feed Waste and Environmental Impact

Feed not eaten is the single largest avoidable cost and the largest source of pond nutrient loading. Practical reductions:

  • Feed at fixed stations and remove leftovers after 30 minutes in tanks and cages.
  • Use feeding trays in ponds to check consumption before the next ration.
  • Match pellet size to mouth size â pellets too large are rejected and sink.
  • Introduce on-farm feed formulation using locally available ingredients (maize, soybean meal, fishmeal, cassava, moringa) to cut cost per kg while maintaining FCR.
  • Compost or digest settled sludge; it is nutrient-rich and can fertilise vegetable plots, closing the loop on-farm.
