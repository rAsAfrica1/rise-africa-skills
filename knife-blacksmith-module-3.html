1. Sourcing Steel and Materials in African Markets

Welcome to Module 3, Lesson 1. In this lesson we focus on the single most important input for any knife blacksmith: steel. Without reliable steel, your forge is just a fire. Across Africa, the steel supply chain is fragmented, informal, and full of opportunity for the smith who learns to navigate it. Your goal is not just to find steel but to build a repeatable sourcing system that protects your quality, your margins, and your reputation. Start by understanding the three main categories of steel available to you. First, recycled steel: car springs, leaf springs, ball bearings, files, rasps, machete blades, and old agricultural tools. Second, new stock: flat bar, round bar, and sheet from industrial suppliers, hardware stores, and scrap yards. Third, specialty steel: high-carbon tool steel, bearing steel, and spring steel that you may order through regional distributors or importers. Each has a different cost, consistency, and working character. Recycled steel is cheap and abundant but variable. New stock is consistent but costs more and may require travel. Specialty steel gives you the best performance but demands cash flow and planning. A practical African blacksmith learns to blend all three. Your first task is to map your local supply. Visit scrap yards, auto repair shops, agricultural cooperatives, construction sites, and market welders. Ask specific questions: Do you sell leaf springs by weight or by piece? Do you have old files? What is your price per kilogram? Build relationships with the people who handle metal daily. A mechanic who saves you broken springs is worth more than a distant wholesaler. Negotiate standing arrangements: you will buy all their worn-out files every month at a set price. This turns random scavenging into a supply line. Next, learn to identify steel by simple tests. The spark test is your most powerful field tool. Touch a sample to a grinding wheel in low light. High-carbon steel throws short, bright, branching sparks. Mild steel throws long, straight, yellow-orange sparks with few branches. Cast iron throws dull red sparks that are short and not branching. Spring steel and tool steel usually show dense, bushy sparks. Practice until you can sort a pile of scrap in minutes. The magnet test helps too: most knife steel is magnetic, but so is mild steel, so use it only to rule out non-ferrous metals like aluminum and brass. The file test tells you hardness: a sharp file bites into mild steel but skates over hardened high-carbon steel. The quench test is the final check: heat a small sample to non-magnetic, quench it in oil or water, then strike it with a hammer. If it snaps cleanly, it is high-carbon. If it bends, it is mild or low-carbon. These tests cost you nothing but time and save you from making knives that will not hold an edge. Pricing and cash flow matter as much as metallurgy. In many African markets, steel is sold by kilogram, by piece, or by the bundle. Always calculate your cost per blade. If a leaf spring costs 5000 shillings and yields four blades, your steel cost is 1250 shillings per blade before labor, fuel, abrasives, and handle material. If you sell a knife for 15000 shillings, your gross margin is thin unless you control waste. Cut patterns carefully. Use every offcut for smaller knives, guards, or fire strikers. Sell your scrap to other smiths or recyclers. Track your steel inventory in a notebook or phone app: date, supplier, type, weight, cost, and intended use. This record becomes your negotiating power. When a supplier sees you are organized, they treat you as a serious buyer. Consider group buying with other smiths in your area. A cooperative of five blacksmiths can buy a full sheet of high-carbon steel at a lower unit price and split it. You can also share transport costs from a regional city. In East Africa, for example, smiths near Nairobi can access industrial areas like Ruaraka and Athi River. In West Africa, Lagos and Accra have scrap markets and importers. In Southern Africa, Johannesburg and Harare are hubs. If you are far from these centers, build a monthly or quarterly buying trip. Combine it with deliveries of finished knives to customers. Never make a special trip for steel alone if you can avoid it. Quality control starts at sourcing. Reject steel with deep cracks, heavy rust that hides pits, or unknown coatings. Paint, chrome, and galvanizing must be ground off before forging because they release toxic fumes. Work outdoors or with strong ventilation. Keep a dedicated grinder for cleaning scrap. Store steel dry and oiled. Label every piece with a paint mark or tag so you know its origin and type. When you find a supplier who gives consistent quality, pay them fairly and promptly. In Africa, trust and reputation are currency. A supplier who trusts you will hold the best pieces for you and warn you when prices rise. Finally, think about alternatives. If high-carbon steel is scarce, you can make knives from mild steel and case-harden them, but this is a compromise. You can also forge-weld a high-carbon edge onto a mild steel body, a traditional technique that stretches expensive steel. Or you can trade with other smiths: your handle material for their steel. The blacksmith who masters sourcing never runs out of work. Your homework: list five local suppliers, test three steel samples with the spark test, and calculate your cost per blade for your most popular knife. Bring your notes to the next lesson. Remember, steel is not just metal. It is the foundation of your business. Source wisely, and your forge will never go cold.

2. Forge Design, Fuel Efficiency, and Heat Management

Welcome to Module 3, Lesson 2. Your forge is the heart of your workshop. In Africa, where fuel can be expensive or scarce, a well-designed forge saves money every day. This lesson covers forge types, fuel choices, airflow, and heat management for consistent knife production. We focus on practical builds using local materials. First, understand the three main forge types available to African blacksmiths. The ground forge, also called a trench forge, is the oldest and cheapest. You dig a shallow trench, line it with clay or mud, and run a pipe or tuyere from a bellows or blower into the side. Fuel sits in the trench. This forge works well with charcoal and is easy to build in rural areas. Its weakness is heat loss and poor control. The raised forge, often made from a car brake drum, a metal bucket, or a welded steel box, is the most common upgrade. You line it with clay, firebrick, or a mixture of clay and sand. A brake drum forge is cheap, portable, and durable. You can find drums at any auto repair shop. The side-blast forge directs air from the side rather than the bottom. This design is excellent for long blades because you can heat the middle without burning the tip. It also uses less fuel because the fire is concentrated. The gas forge uses propane or butane and is clean, fast, and easy to control, but fuel cost and canister availability are challenges in many African markets. For most small-scale knife makers, a charcoal or coal forge with a side blast or bottom blast is the best balance of cost and performance. Fuel choice is a business decision. Charcoal is widely available but burns quickly and produces less heat than coal. Hardwood charcoal is better than softwood. You can make your own charcoal from agricultural waste, wood offcuts, or coconut shells. A simple retort or pit kiln can turn waste into fuel. Coal burns hotter and longer but may be hard to find and smoky. In South Africa, coal is common. In East and West Africa, charcoal dominates. You can also use coke, which is coal with impurities burned off. It burns very hot and clean but is expensive. Some smiths use waste oil burners, but these require careful safety and produce smoke. Whatever fuel you choose, buy in bulk when prices are low. Store charcoal or coal in a dry shed. Wet fuel wastes heat and creates steam. Build a fuel budget for each knife. If you use two kilograms of charcoal per blade at 1000 shillings per kilogram, that is 2000 shillings per blade. If you can cut that to 1.5 kilograms through better forge design, you save 500 shillings per blade. Over 100 blades, that is 50000 shillings. Airflow is the secret to efficient forging. You need enough air to reach welding heat, but too much air blows heat away and burns fuel fast. A hand-cranked blower or bellows gives you control. A vacuum cleaner motor or hair dryer can work but often provides too much air. Use a gate valve or a sliding plate to regulate flow. For charcoal, a gentle steady blast is best. For coal, you can use more air to burn off volatiles. The tuyere, the pipe that delivers air, should be made of thick steel or cast iron because it sits in the fire. A car exhaust pipe or a piece of black iron pipe works. Angle the tuyere slightly downward so ash and clinker do not block it. Build a clinker breaker, a simple rod you can poke through the tuyere to clear blockages. Heat management is the skill that separates a professional from an amateur. Steel changes color as it heats. In daylight, you may not see the dull reds, so work in a shaded area or use a welding lens. The colors are: faint red around 500 degrees Celsius, dark cherry at 700, bright cherry at 800, orange at 900, yellow at 1000, and white at 1200. For most knife forging, you work between 800 and 1000 degrees. Forge welding requires 1200 to 1300 degrees. Never overheat steel. If it sparks like a fireworks display, you are burning carbon out of the steel. That blade will not harden properly. Soak time matters too. Let the steel heat evenly through its thickness. A thick leaf spring needs more time than a thin file. Rotate the blade and watch the color. If one section is brighter, move it away from the hot spot. Build a fire that is deep enough to surround the steel but not so deep that you waste fuel. A fire mound of 10 to 15 centimeters above the tuyere is usually right. Use a rake or poker to shape the fire. Keep a water bucket nearby for quenching and for safety. Quenching oil should be warm, around 50 to 60 degrees Celsius, for most carbon steels. You can warm it by placing a piece of hot steel in it before quenching. Never quench in cold water unless the steel is designed for it, because cold water can crack the blade. Forge safety in Africa includes respect for fire, fuel, and fumes. Work in a well-ventilated area. Charcoal produces carbon monoxide, which is deadly in closed rooms. Build your forge outside or under a roof with open sides. Wear leather gloves, safety glasses, and a leather apron. Keep a bucket of water and a bucket of sand nearby. Do not wear synthetic clothing near the forge. Tie back long hair. Keep children and animals away from the work area. Now let us talk about productivity. A forge that takes 30 minutes to reach working heat wastes time. Build a fire that is ready in 10 to 15 minutes. Keep your fuel dry and your air supply reliable. Have your tools organized: tongs, hammers, chisels, punches, and brushes. A good forge layout reduces movement and saves energy. If you produce knives in batches, you can heat multiple pieces and work while others soak. This is called batch forging. It increases output without increasing fuel use per blade. Consider building a second forge for heat treating. A small gas forge or a charcoal forge with a muffle pipe gives you precise temperature control for hardening and tempering. This improves quality and reduces guesswork. Finally, calculate your forge cost per hour. Add fuel, labor, and maintenance. If your forge costs 3000 shillings per hour to run and you make one knife in two hours, your forge cost is 6000 shillings. If you can make two knives in two hours, your forge cost per knife drops to 3000 shillings. Efficiency is profit. Your homework: measure your current forge fuel use for one knife, identify one improvement to reduce fuel use by 20 percent, and test a side-blast or brake drum design if you have not already. Bring your fuel cost per blade to the next lesson. A blacksmith who controls heat controls quality and cost.

3. Blade Geometry, Grinding, and Edge Performance

Welcome to Module 3, Lesson 3. A knife is a tool, and its value comes from how well it cuts. In this lesson we cover blade geometry, grinding techniques, and edge performance with an African market focus. Your customers use knives for farming, cooking, hunting, fishing, carving, and self-defense. Each use demands a different geometry. Your job is to match the blade to the job and to produce it efficiently. Start with the anatomy of a blade. The spine is the back, the edge is the cutting side, the tip is the point, the ricasso is the flat section before the handle, and the plunge line is where the grind meets the ricasso. The primary bevel is the main ground surface. The secondary bevel, or micro-bevel, is the final edge. The fuller is a groove that reduces weight. The choil is a notch before the edge. Understanding these terms helps you communicate with customers and other smiths. Blade geometry is a balance of three factors: sharpness, strength, and durability. A thin edge cuts well but chips easily. A thick edge is strong but cuts poorly. The ideal geometry depends on the steel, the heat treatment, and the intended use. For a kitchen knife, you want a thin edge, around 15 to 20 degrees total angle, because it slices soft food. For a machete or farm knife, you want a thicker edge, around 25 to 30 degrees, because it chops wood and bone. For a hunting knife, a compromise of 20 to 25 degrees works for skinning and cutting meat. For a combat or utility knife, 25 to 30 degrees gives durability. Write these angles on your workshop wall. They are your guide. Grinding is where geometry becomes reality. You can grind with a belt grinder, an angle grinder, a file, or a stone. In Africa, many smiths use a belt grinder made from a motor, a platen, and a contact wheel. If you do not have one, a good file and a water stone can produce excellent results, but they are slower. The belt grinder is the biggest productivity upgrade for most blacksmiths. Build or buy one with a 2 by 72 inch belt if possible. Use belts in grits: 36 for rough shaping, 80 for refining, 120 for smoothing, 220 for pre-polish, and 400 or higher for finishing. Do not skip grits. Skipping grits wastes time and produces scratches that show after heat treatment. When grinding, keep the blade cool. Dip it in water frequently. If the steel turns blue or purple, you have overheated it and softened the edge. That is called burning the edge. It ruins the heat treatment. Grind with light pressure and let the belt do the work. For a flat grind, hold the blade at a consistent angle against the platen. For a convex grind, use a slack belt. For a scandi grind, grind a single bevel with no secondary bevel. Each has advantages. The flat grind is easy to sharpen and good for slicing. The convex grind is strong and good for chopping. The scandi grind is excellent for wood carving. Choose the grind that matches your market. In many African markets, customers prefer a convex or flat grind because they are easy to maintain with a stone or file. Now let us talk about edge performance. A sharp edge is a wedge that separates material. The thinner the wedge, the easier it cuts. But thin edges are fragile. The solution is a micro-bevel. After grinding the primary bevel to a thin edge, sharpen at a slightly higher angle to create a strong micro-bevel. This gives you the best of both worlds: a thin cutting geometry with a durable edge. For example, grind a kitchen knife to 15 degrees, then sharpen at 20 degrees for the micro-bevel. The knife will slice well and hold up to cutting board contact. Edge retention depends on steel type, hardness, and geometry. High-carbon steel like 1080, 1095, or 5160 holds an edge well when hardened to 58 to 60 Rockwell C. Stainless steel like 440C or 12C27 is more corrosion-resistant but harder to sharpen. In Africa, carbon steel is often preferred because it is easy to sharpen with a file or stone and takes a keen edge. The trade-off is rust. Teach your customers to dry and oil their blades. You can also force a patina with vinegar or mustard to protect the steel. That patina also gives a unique look that customers value. Sharpening is a skill you must master and teach. A sharp knife sells more knives. Offer free sharpening for the first year or a discounted sharpening service. This brings customers back and builds loyalty. Learn to sharpen on water stones, oil stones, and diamond plates. Use a consistent angle. A simple sharpening jig can help you maintain angle. For field sharpening, a ceramic rod or a diamond rod works well. Sell a small sharpening stone with each knife as an upsell. It increases your average sale and helps the customer maintain the knife. Testing edge performance is part of quality control. After sharpening, test the knife on paper, rope, or wood. A sharp knife slices paper cleanly. A dull knife tears it. Cut a piece of rope or sisal. Count the cuts before the edge dulls. This gives you a benchmark. Test each batch and record results. If a batch fails, check your heat treatment and geometry. Do not sell a knife that fails your test. Your reputation is worth more than a quick sale. Consider the handle and balance as part of geometry. A blade-heavy knife is good for chopping. A handle-heavy knife is good for detail work. A balanced knife feels neutral. The handle should fit the hand. In Africa, handle materials include hardwood, bone, horn, leather, and recycled plastic. Shape the handle for comfort and control. A rough handle causes blisters and reduces control. Sand to at least 400 grit and finish with oil or wax. A comfortable handle makes the knife feel more valuable. Finally, think about product lines. You can offer three tiers: a basic farm knife with a simple handle and a 25-degree edge, a mid-range kitchen knife with a 20-degree edge and a polished handle, and a premium hunting knife with a 20-degree edge, a mirror polish, and a leather sheath. Each tier uses different amounts of labor and materials. Price them accordingly. Your homework: grind and sharpen three knives with different geometries, test each on rope and paper, and record the results. Bring your best and worst performer to the next lesson. Geometry is not guesswork. It is engineering. Master it, and your knives will sell themselves.

4. Heat Treatment, Hardening, and Tempering for African Steels

Welcome to Module 3, Lesson 4. Heat treatment is the difference between a knife that cuts and a knife that fails. In this lesson we cover hardening, tempering, and quality control using the steels most common in Africa. You will learn to build a simple heat-treating setup, read temperatures without expensive equipment, and produce consistent results. First, understand what heat treatment does. Steel is iron with carbon and other elements. When you heat steel above its critical temperature, around 750 to 800 degrees Celsius for most carbon steels, the carbon dissolves into the iron. If you cool it quickly, the carbon is trapped, and the steel becomes hard and brittle. This is hardening. If you then heat it to a lower temperature, around 150 to 250 degrees Celsius, you relieve some brittleness while keeping hardness. This is tempering. The combination of hardening and tempering gives you a tough, sharp edge. Without heat treatment, a knife is just shaped metal. The critical temperature is the point where steel becomes non-magnetic. This is your most reliable field test. Heat a piece of steel until a magnet no longer sticks. That is the critical temperature. For most simple carbon steels like 1080, 1095, 5160, and leaf spring, this is around 780 to 820 degrees Celsius. For files and rasps, it may be slightly higher. For stainless steels, you need more precise control, often with a kiln. In Africa, most blacksmiths work with carbon steels, so the magnet test is your best friend. Build a heat-treating forge. You can use your main forge, but a dedicated heat-treating forge gives better control. A simple charcoal forge with a muffle pipe works. A muffle pipe is a steel or cast iron pipe closed at one end and open at the other. You place the blade inside the pipe and heat the pipe from outside. This gives even heat and protects the blade from direct flame. A gas forge with a muffle is even better. If you have electricity, a small electric kiln is ideal, but it is expensive. Many African smiths use a charcoal forge with a pipe and a steady hand. The key is even heat. If one part of the blade is hotter than another, it will harden differently and may warp or crack. Quenching is the moment of truth. You need a quench tank deep enough to submerge the blade completely. A metal pipe or a bucket works. Use oil for most carbon steels. Canola oil, peanut oil, and motor oil are common. Warm the oil to about 50 to 60 degrees Celsius. Cold oil cools too fast and can crack the blade. Hot oil cools too slowly and may not harden. Test your oil with a sample piece. Heat a small piece of steel to non-magnetic, quench it, and break it. If it snaps cleanly with a fine grain, your oil is right. If it bends, the oil is too slow. If it shatters, the oil is too fast or the steel is overheated. For some steels, you can quench in water, but water is risky. It can crack thin blades. Use water only for simple steels like 1095 if oil does not harden them. Brine, salt water, is even faster and more risky. Avoid it unless you have no other option. When quenching, plunge the blade into the oil edge-first or point-first, depending on the blade shape. Move it slightly to break the vapor barrier. Do not stir violently. Hold it under the oil until it stops smoking, then pull it out. Check for warping. If it warps, you can straighten it while it is still warm, around 200 degrees Celsius, using a vise or a hammer. Do not straighten cold, hardened steel. It will snap. After quenching, the blade is hard but brittle. Test it with a file. A sharp file should skate over the edge without biting. If the file bites, the blade did not harden. You can re-harden it. Reheat to non-magnetic and quench again. If it still does not harden, the steel may be low-carbon or the quench is too slow. Now temper the blade. Temper immediately after quenching, within an hour if possible. Heat your kitchen oven to 200 degrees Celsius. Place the blade on a tray and leave it for one hour. Then let it cool. For a tougher edge, temper at 220 to 250 degrees Celsius. For a harder edge, temper at 150 to 180 degrees Celsius. The trade-off is toughness versus edge retention. For a kitchen knife, 180 to 200 degrees Celsius is good. For a machete, 220 to 250 degrees Celsius is better. You can also temper with a torch or a forge. Heat the spine of the blade until it turns blue, then let it cool. The edge should stay straw-colored or bronze. This is called differential tempering. It gives a hard edge and a tough spine. It is a traditional technique that works well in a simple workshop. If you do not have an oven, you can temper in a charcoal fire. Bury the blade in hot sand or ash at the right temperature. Use a thermometer if you have one. If not, use the color method. Clean the blade to bare metal. Heat it slowly. Watch the colors: pale yellow at 180 degrees, straw at 200, brown at 220, purple at 240, blue at 260. For most knives, stop at straw or brown. Quench in water to stop the tempering. This method is less precise but works with practice. Quality control is essential. After tempering, test the blade. Check for cracks with a magnifying glass or by tapping it. A cracked blade will ring dull. Check the edge by cutting rope or cardboard. A well-tempered blade should cut cleanly without chipping. If it chips, temper it again at a higher temperature. If it rolls, it is too soft. Re-harden and temper at a lower temperature. Record every batch: steel type, hardening temperature, quench medium, tempering temperature, and test results. This record helps you repeat success and avoid failure. Common problems and solutions: Warping happens when one side cools faster than the other. Prevent it by heating evenly, quenching straight, and straightening while warm. Cracking happens from overheating, cold quench oil, or too-fast quench. Prevent it by using the magnet test, warming your oil, and using oil instead of water. Soft spots happen from uneven heating or insufficient soak time. Prevent it by soaking the blade at temperature for one to two minutes per millimeter of thickness. Scale and decarburization happen when oxygen burns carbon from the surface. Prevent it by using a muffle pipe, coating the blade with clay, or wrapping it in foil if you have it. In Africa, foil may be scarce, so clay coating is a good alternative. Mix clay and water into a thin slurry, dip the blade, and let it dry. This also helps prevent warping. Safety during heat treatment: wear leather gloves, a face shield, and a leather apron. Keep a bucket of water and a fire extinguisher nearby. Do not quench in a plastic bucket because it can melt. Use metal. Work in a well-ventilated area. Oil quench produces smoke and fumes. Do not breathe them. Finally, think about consistency and business. A customer who buys a knife expects it to perform. If one knife is hard and another is soft, your reputation suffers. Heat treatment is not optional. It is the core of your craft. Invest in a good thermometer, a muffle pipe, and a dedicated quench tank. Practice on scrap steel until you can predict results. Your homework: heat treat three blades from different steels, record your process, and test each for hardness and toughness. Bring your results and your failures to the next lesson. Failure is data. Use it to improve.

5. Finishing, Handles, Sheaths, and Selling Your Knives

Welcome to Module 3, Lesson 5. You have sourced steel, built a forge, ground a blade, and heat treated it. Now you must finish the knife, fit a handle, make a sheath, and sell it. This lesson is about the final 20 percent that creates 80 percent of the customer's perception. In Africa, where craftsmanship and reputation matter, finishing is your competitive advantage. Start with blade finishing. After heat treatment, the blade has scale, oxidation, and grinding marks. Clean it with a wire brush, sandpaper, or a belt grinder. For a rustic look, leave some forge marks and a dark patina. For a polished look, sand progressively to 400, 800, and 1500 grit, then buff with a polishing compound. A mirror polish takes time but commands a higher price. For most African markets, a satin finish is practical and attractive. It hides scratches and is easy to maintain. Use a sharpening stone to create a clean edge bevel. Then etch the blade with vinegar or ferric chloride if you want a visible hamon or pattern. Ferric chloride may be hard to find, but vinegar works slowly. You can also use lemon juice or tamarind. Etching brings out the steel's character and makes the knife look handmade. Add your maker's mark. This is critical for branding. A simple stamp with your initials or a logo punched into the steel before heat treatment is best. If you do not have a stamp, engrave with a rotary tool or electro-etch with a car battery and salt water. A maker's mark tells customers who made the knife and builds trust. It also helps you track your work. Now handles. A handle must be comfortable, durable, and attractive. In Africa, you have access to wonderful materials: ebony, mahogany, olive wood, camel bone, cow horn, buffalo horn, leather, and recycled plastic. Choose dry, stable wood. Green wood shrinks and cracks. If you cut your own wood, dry it for at least six months. You can also stabilize wood by soaking it in resin or linseed oil, but this requires equipment. For most smiths, well-dried hardwood is enough. Shape the handle to fit the hand. Use a rasp, file, and sandpaper. The handle should be smooth but not slippery. A slight palm swell and a finger guard improve control. For a full tang knife, the handle scales are attached with epoxy and pins. For a hidden tang, the handle is a block with a hole for the tang. Both work. Full tang is stronger and easier to make with basic tools. Use strong epoxy. In Africa, you can find two-part epoxy at hardware stores. If not, use cutler's resin, a mixture of pine pitch, beeswax, and charcoal. It is traditional and works well. Pins can be brass, copper, or steel. Cut them slightly longer than the handle, peen them over, and sand flush. For a hidden tang, drill a hole, insert the tang, and fill with epoxy. Add a bolster or guard for strength and style. Brass, copper, or mild steel work. Shape it with files and sandpaper. A well-fitted guard prevents the hand from slipping onto the blade. Finish the handle with oil, wax, or varnish. Linseed oil, tung oil, and beeswax are good. They bring out the grain and protect the wood. For horn and bone, sand to 800 grit and polish with wax. Avoid over-polishing because it can look plastic. A natural finish is more valuable. Now sheaths. A knife without a sheath is unsafe and hard to sell. Leather is the best material. In Africa, you can find leather from local tanneries or from old belts and bags. Vegetable-tanned leather is ideal because it holds shape and can be tooled. Chrome-tanned leather is softer and less durable. Cut the leather to shape, wet it, and mold it around the knife. Stitch with a saddle stitch using waxed thread. A saddle stitch is stronger than a machine stitch. If you do not have leather, you can make sheaths from wood, plastic, or woven fiber. A wooden sheath lined with felt is elegant. A plastic sheath is cheap and waterproof. A woven sheath from sisal or palm leaves is traditional and lightweight. Whatever material you use, make sure the knife fits securely and cannot fall out. Add a belt loop or a lanyard hole. A good sheath protects the edge and the user. It also increases the perceived value of the knife. Now selling. Your first customers are your neighbors, farmers, hunters, cooks, and market traders. Show them your knives. Let them hold the knife. A knife that feels good in the hand sells itself. Explain the steel, the heat treatment, and the handle material. Teach them how to care for the knife. Offer a guarantee: if the knife fails under normal use, you will repair or replace it. This builds trust. Price your knives based on cost plus a fair profit. Calculate your material cost, labor cost, fuel cost, and overhead. Add 30 to 50 percent profit. Do not undercharge. Cheap knives attract customers who do not value your work. Fair prices attract customers who respect quality. Offer different tiers: a basic knife for everyday use, a mid-range knife for gifts, and a premium knife for collectors. Sell at markets, fairs, and online. In Africa, WhatsApp, Facebook, and Instagram are powerful sales tools. Take clear photos in natural light. Show the knife in use. Tell the story of the steel and the maker. A story adds value. You can also sell to shops, hotels, safari camps, and agricultural suppliers. Visit them with samples. Offer wholesale prices for bulk orders. Build relationships. A hotel that buys 20 kitchen knives is a repeat customer. Consider teaching workshops. People will pay to learn blacksmithing

<h3>3. Heat Treatment Reference for African Steels</h3>

<p>The heat-treatment lesson in this course covers theory. This reference gives you the working window for the steels you actually have. Use a magnet as your critical-temperature indicator: steel becomes non-magnetic at roughly 770 C, which is close to the hardening temperature for most medium-carbon and high-carbon steels. For a more precise reading, a <a href="https://www.msdmanuals.com/">thermocouple or infrared thermometer</a> is worth the investment once you are selling regularly.</p>

<p>Quenching media matter. Water is fast and can crack high-carbon steels. Oil is slower and safer for spring steel. Brine is the fastest and is used only for shallow-hardening steels such as W1 files. Always quench in a well-ventilated area and never quench into a plastic container hot steel will melt through it.</p>

<div style="border: 3px solid #c0392b; background: #fdecea; padding: 16px; margin: 24px 0; border-radius: 6px;">
<p style="margin: 0 0 8px 0; font-weight: bold; color: #922b21; font-size: 1.1em;"> SAFETY WARNING QUENCHING, SMOKE, AND METAL FUMES</p>
<p style="margin: 0 0 8px 0;">Quenching hot steel in oil produces a flash fire and dense smoke containing aldehydes and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs). Quenching in brine or water can produce explosive steam. Forge smoke from charcoal, coal, and waste oil contains carbon monoxide and fine particulate matter (PM2.5).</p>
<ul style="margin: 0 0 8px 0;">
<li>Never quench into a plastic bucket or any container that can melt or tip. Use a metal drum or heavy steel pot on stable, non-flammable ground.</li>
<li>Keep a lid or metal sheet within arm's reach to smother an oil flash fire. Never use water on an oil fire.</li>
<li>Work outdoors or with cross-ventilation. Position yourself upwind of the forge and quench tank.</li>
<li>Wear leather gloves, eye protection, and a cotton or leather apron. Synthetic clothing melts onto skin.</li>
<li>Do not quench galvanised, painted, or oil-coated steel without first grinding or burning off the coating outdoors zinc and paint fumes cause metal fume fever.</li>
<li>Keep children and customers at least 3 metres from the forge and quench area.</li>
</ul>
<p style="margin: 0;">Reference: <a href="https://www.who.int/">WHO</a> <a href="https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241548885">Guidelines for Indoor Air Quality: Household Fuel Combustion (2014)</a>; and <a href="https://www.msdmanuals.com/">MSD Manuals</a> on metal fume fever and carbon monoxide poisoning.</p>
</div>

<h3>4. Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Sort scrap before you light the forge.</strong> Separate known-hardenable steel (leaf spring, coil spring, bearing race, file) from unknown and non-hardenable stock. Label the bins.</li>
<li><strong>Spark-test every new batch.</strong> Bright yellow sparks with many bursts = high carbon. Few dull orange sparks = mild steel. Do not sell knives made from mild steel.</li>
<li><strong>Calculate fuel cost per blade before pricing.</strong> Fuel + labour + consumables + 25% margin = minimum price. Re-check every time fuel prices change.</li>
<li><strong>Magnet-test for critical temperature.</strong> Steel becomes non-magnetic at about 770 C. Quench within 2030 seconds of pulling from the fire for most spring steels.</li>
<li><strong>Match quench medium to steel.</strong> Oil for leaf and coil spring; water or brine for files and bearing races. Never use plastic containers.</li>
<li><strong>Temper immediately after hardening.</strong> Two cycles at 200230 C for one hour each, in a domestic oven or over a controlled charcoal bed, reduces brittleness and doubles edge life.</li>
<li><strong>Ventilate and separate.</strong> Forge and quench outdoors or with cross-ventilation; keep quench tank metal, stable, and at least 1 m from the fire; keep bystanders 3 m back.</li>
<li><strong>Record every batch.</strong> Write the steel source, hardening temperature, quench medium, and temper cycle in a notebook. Repeatable knives build repeat customers.</li>
</ol>
