Lessons

1. Making the initial cut without piercing intestines

After you have bled and scalded the pig, hang it on a gambrel or sturdy branch with the head down. Use a sharp 6-inch boning knife or a skinning knife. Stand slightly to the side so you are not directly in front of the belly. Make a shallow cut from the anus down to the sternum, but only through the skin and the thin layer of fat. Do not cut deep. The intestines lie just under the abdominal wall, so a cut deeper than 1 cm can puncture them. Use the tip of the knife, not the whole blade. Hold the knife like a pencil for control. Start at the anus: cut around it in a circle, about 2 cm away from the opening, to free it from the surrounding skin. Then slit downward, using two fingers of your free hand to guide the blade and keep the intestines away. If you feel resistance, stop and use your fingers to push the guts back. A common mistake is to cut too fast. Work slowly. If you accidentally nick the intestines, the smell will be terrible and the meat can be contaminated. If that happens, immediately wash the area with plenty of clean water and cut away any soiled meat. In rural areas, many people use a machete, but that is dangerous because it is too big. A small, sharp knife is safer. Keep a bucket of clean water nearby to rinse your hands and the knife. After the initial cut, you should see the white membrane (peritoneum) covering the organs. You will cut through that next, but first make sure the skin is fully open from anus to sternum. This first cut sets the stage for the whole evisceration. Take your time.

2. Removing the digestive tract and separating edible organs

Once the belly is open, you will see the intestines, stomach, liver, heart, and lungs. Start by pulling the intestines out gently. They are long and coiled. Use both hands to lift them out and place them in a clean bucket or on a clean tarp. Do not pull hard or you will tear them. Next, locate the stomach. It is a large, sac-like organ on the left side. Cut it free at the esophagus and at the duodenum (the first part of the small intestine). Put the stomach with the intestines for later processing. Now find the liver. It is a large, dark red organ on the right side, just behind the diaphragm. Cut the ligaments that hold it to the diaphragm and the vena cava. Be careful not to cut into the gallbladder, a small green sac attached to the liver. If it breaks, wash the liver immediately with clean water. Set the liver aside. The heart is in the chest cavity, enclosed in a sac called the pericardium. Cut the sac open and pull the heart out. Cut the major blood vessels close to the heart. The kidneys are embedded in fat on the back wall of the abdomen. They are bean-shaped and dark red. Use your fingers to scoop them out, leaving the fat. The lungs are light pink and spongy. They are not usually eaten in many African communities, but some people use them for dog food. Cut them out and set aside for disposal. Separate the edible organs (liver, heart, kidneys) into one clean container, and the stomach and intestines into another for tripe. Keep everything clean and cool. Work quickly, especially in hot weather.

3. Cleaning and preserving the liver, heart, and kidneys

After you have removed the liver, heart, and kidneys, rinse them with clean water. Do not soak them in water for a long time, as that makes them lose flavour and nutrients. For the liver, cut away any large blood vessels or bile ducts. If the gallbladder was broken, wash the liver with a weak solution of salt water (1 tablespoon of salt per litre) to remove bitterness. Pat dry with a clean cloth. For the heart, cut it open and remove any blood clots from the inside. Rinse well. For the kidneys, cut them in half lengthwise and remove the white core (the ureter and blood vessels). Soak them in salt water for 30 minutes to reduce the strong urine smell, then rinse again. To preserve these organs, you can refrigerate them at 4C for up to 3 days, or freeze them at -18C for up to 3 months. If you have no electricity, you can preserve them by salting: rub them generously with coarse salt and hang them in a cool, dry, well-ventilated place for 2-3 days. Then store in a cloth bag. Another method is to cook them immediately into a stew or soup. In many African markets, fresh liver and kidneys are sold quickly, so if you plan to sell, keep them in a cooler with ice. Always use clean containers and wash your hands before handling cooked or raw organs. Never leave them at room temperature for more than 2 hours. Proper cleaning and preservation prevent food poisoning and waste.

4. Handling the stomach and intestines for tripe

Tripe is a popular food in many African countries, but it requires thorough cleaning. Start with the stomach. Cut it open lengthwise and empty out any undigested food. Rinse with plenty of water. Then turn it inside out. The inner lining has a rough texture. You can scrape it with a blunt knife or a piece of broken tile to remove the slimy layer. Some people use wood ash or lime (calcium hydroxide) to help loosen the slime. Rub the ash or a small amount of lime on the stomach, then rinse. Be careful with lime; it can burn your skin, so wear gloves. For the intestines, you need to separate the small intestine from the large intestine. The small intestine is thinner and more coiled. Cut them into manageable lengths (about 50 cm). To clean, you can use a stick or a long, thin bamboo to push the contents out, or you can squeeze the contents out by hand. Then rinse with running water. Turn the intestines inside out by using a thin stick to push one end through. Wash again with salt and lemon juice or vinegar to remove odour. Some people soak them in salt water for 1 hour. After cleaning, boil the stomach and intestines in water with salt, onions, and spices for 1-2 hours until tender. In West Africa, tripe is often cooked in a spicy pepper soup. In East Africa, it is fried with onions and tomatoes. Always clean tripe thoroughly; if not, it can cause diarrhoea. Store cleaned tripe in the fridge for up to 2 days or freeze for later use.

5. Disposing of non-edible parts responsibly

Not all parts of the pig are edible or safe to eat. You must dispose of non-edible parts responsibly to avoid attracting flies, rodents, and dogs, and to prevent disease. Non-edible parts include the lungs, spleen, gallbladder, urinary bladder, and any organs that are damaged or diseased. Also, the contents of the intestines and stomach (manure) should be disposed of properly. Do not throw them into a river or stream, as that pollutes water sources. Instead, dig a pit at least 50 cm deep, away from wells and houses, and bury the waste. Cover it with soil and pack it down. Alternatively, you can compost the waste if you have a compost pit. But do not compost meat or organs unless you have a hot compost system, as it can attract pests. In some areas, you can sell non-edible parts to a rendering plant or use them for animal feed (after cooking). However, never feed raw pork waste to other pigs, as it can spread diseases like African swine fever. If you have a dog, you can cook the lungs and spleen and feed them, but only if they are from a healthy pig. Always wear gloves when handling waste. Wash your hands and tools with soap and water afterwards. Bury waste far from water sources and at least 30 metres from any home. This keeps your community healthy and prevents complaints. Proper disposal is part of being a responsible butcher.

6. Washing the carcass cavity with clean water

After removing all the internal organs, the carcass cavity will have blood, bone fragments, and bits of fat and tissue. You must wash it thoroughly with clean water. Use a clean bucket and a clean cloth or sponge. If you have running water, use a hose with low pressure. If not, use a jug to pour water. Start by rinsing the cavity from top to bottom. Use your hand or a soft brush to wipe down the inside of the rib cage, the spine, and the pelvic area. Pay attention to the diaphragm area and around the kidneys where fat may remain. You can add a little salt to the water (1 handful per 10 litres) to help remove blood and odours. Do not use soap or detergents, as they can leave harmful residues. After washing, tilt the carcass to let the water drain out. Repeat the rinsing two or three times until the water runs clear. Then dry the cavity with a clean cloth. If you are not going to cook or sell the meat immediately, you can rub the inside with a little vinegar or lemon juice to slow bacterial growth. Then hang the carcass in a cool, well-ventilated place. In hot climates, you should refrigerate within 2 hours. If you have no fridge, keep the carcass in the coolest part of the house and use it within 24 hours. Washing the cavity is important because leftover blood and tissue can spoil quickly and cause food poisoning. A clean carcass also looks better and fetches a higher price at market. Always use clean water; dirty water can introduce bacteria. After washing, clean your tools and workspace with soap and water.

<section>
<h2>Practical Reference: Evisceration Removing Internal Organs Safely</h2>

<p>In pig processing, evisceration is where money is won or lost. A clean, fast evisceration protects carcass value, preserves edible offal (liver, heart, kidneys, tripe) that can add 1020% to your revenue per pig, and prevents contamination that would force you to sell at a discount or discard the whole carcass. In African markets from Kampala and Nairobi to Lagos and Accra offal is not a by-product; it is a premium product. Tripe, liver and kidneys are sold at prices that rival or exceed prime cuts in many urban markets. This module gives you the practical sequence, the hygiene discipline, and the quality checkpoints that turn evisceration from a risky chore into a profitable, repeatable business process.</p>

<p>This Practical Reference lesson is designed for small and medium-scale abattoirs, butcheries, and farm-based processing units. It assumes you have a clean processing area, potable water, a sharp evisceration knife, a hook or gambrel, and a clean table or tray for offal. If you are processing more than 5 pigs per week, the workflow below should be standardised and timed so each carcass moves from bleeding to evisceration within 3045 minutes of slaughter to preserve meat quality.</p>

<h3>Why Evisceration Speed and Hygiene Drive Profit</h3>
<p>Every minute between slaughter and chilling increases bacterial growth. The warm carcass is an ideal environment for <em>Salmonella</em>, <em>E. coli</em>, and <em>Campylobacter</em>, which are the main food-safety risks in pork. A single intestinal rupture can contaminate the entire carcass cavity with gut contents, requiring trimming, washing, and in severe cases condemnation. In a competitive market, a carcass that smells or looks contaminated loses customer trust permanently. Conversely, a clean evisceration preserves the offal that customers pay premium prices for. In many African cities, a well-cleaned set of tripe sells for the equivalent of 24 kg of pork meat. That is direct margin you cannot afford to lose.</p>

<h3>Step-by-Step Evisceration Sequence</h3>

<h4>1. Making the Initial Cut Without Piercing Intestines</h4>
<p>With the carcass suspended on a gambrel or rail, head down or head up depending on your setup, make a shallow midline incision from the pelvic bone to the sternum. The key is depth control: insert only the tip of the knife (23 cm) and use two fingers as a guide under the skin and abdominal wall. Cut through the skin and the thin abdominal muscle layer, but stop before the peritoneum if you can. Then use your fingers or a gut hook to open the peritoneum. This technique is called the "fist and finger" method in many abattoirs: the non-knife hand lifts the abdominal wall away from the intestines while the knife hand cuts. Never cut toward the intestines. If you feel the blade touching a soft, slippery mass, stop immediately.</p>

<h4>2. Removing the Digestive Tract and Separating Edible Organs</h4>
<p>Once the abdomen is open, the intestines will begin to spill out. Let them fall onto a clean tray or into a clean bucket never onto the floor. Locate the rectum and tie it off with a string or clamp it with your hand to prevent faecal leakage. Cut around the anus from the outside, then pull the rectum through the pelvic canal. Next, separate the mesentery (the connective tissue holding the intestines) with your fingers or a knife, and pull the digestive tract free. The stomach, small intestine, and large intestine come out as one mass. The liver, heart, and kidneys are removed separately. The liver is attached to the diaphragm; cut the attachments carefully so you do not slice into the liver. The heart is inside the pericardial sac; open the sac and cut the major vessels. The kidneys are embedded in fat along the spine; use your fingers to scoop them out gently.</p>

<h4>3. Cleaning and Preserving the Liver, Heart, and Kidneys</h4>
<p>These three organs are high-value and highly perishable. Rinse them immediately in clean, potable water. Remove the gall bladder from the liver without breaking it bile is bitter and will ruin the liver if it spills. Trim any connective tissue and blood clots. For the heart, cut it open and rinse out the blood. For the kidneys, remove the outer membrane and rinse. After washing, place them in clean, food-grade containers and chill them to below 4C as quickly as possible. If you do not have refrigeration, sell or cook them within 23 hours. In many African markets, fresh liver and kidneys are sold the same day; do not hold them at ambient temperature.</p>

<h4>4. Handling the Stomach and Intestines for Tripe</h4>
<p>Tripe is the stomach lining; it is a major revenue source. The stomach must be emptied, washed, and scalded. First, cut the stomach open and empty the contents into a waste container. Rinse thoroughly. Then scald the stomach in water at 6570C for 23 minutes to loosen the inner lining. Scrape the lining with a knife or brush until it is white and clean. For the intestines, separate the small and large intestines, empty them, and rinse. Turn them inside out and wash with salt and lime or lemon juice a traditional and effective method across Africa. Rinse again in clean water. Tripe should be cooked or chilled immediately. If you are selling raw tripe, keep it below 4C and sell within 24 hours.</p>

<h4>5. Disposing of Non-Edible Parts Responsibly</h4>
<p>Non-edible parts include the gall bladder, spleen, lungs (in some markets), and any contaminated tissue. These must be disposed of in a way that does not create a public health or environmental hazard. Options include approved rendering, incineration, or deep burial at least 1 metre deep and 30 metres away from water sources. Never dump offal into rivers, open drains, or public land. In many African countries, environmental and public health regulations require abattoirs to have a waste management plan. Check with your local authority. Selling contaminated or spoiled offal is illegal and destroys your business reputation.</p>

<h4>6. Washing the Carcass Cavity with Clean Water</h4>
<p>After evisceration, wash the carcass cavity thoroughly with clean, potable water. Use a low-pressure hose or a clean cloth. Do not use dirty water or water from an unknown source. If you have access to a food-safe sanitiser (e.g., 50100 ppm chlorine solution) approved for meat, use it according to the manufacturer's instructions. Then rinse again with clean water. The carcass should be chilled to below 7C within 1 hour and below 4C within 24 hours. Proper washing removes blood, bone fragments, and bacteria, and it improves the appearance of the carcass for sale.</p>

<h3 style="color:#c0392b;margin-top:0;"> SAFETY WARNING: Never Pierce the Intestines or Gall Bladder</h3>
<p style="margin-bottom:0;">Piercing the intestines releases faecal material that can contaminate the entire carcass and cause severe foodborne illness (<em>Salmonella</em>, <em>E. coli</em>, <em>Campylobacter</em>). Piercing the gall bladder releases bile, which makes the liver bitter and unsellable. If a rupture occurs: stop work immediately, isolate the contaminated carcass, trim all visibly contaminated tissue, wash the area with potable water and an approved sanitiser, and change your knife and gloves before continuing. If contamination is extensive, the carcass may need to be condemned. This is a financial loss, but selling contaminated meat is illegal and dangerous. Always use a sharp knife, controlled depth, and the finger-guide method. Never rush evisceration.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist (68 Points)</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Prepare your station:</strong> Clean water, sharp knife, hook/gambrel, clean offal tray, waste container, gloves, and sanitiser ready before you start.</li>
<li><strong>Control cut depth:</strong> Use the finger-guide method; insert only the knife tip (23 cm) along the midline. Never cut toward the intestines.</li>
<li><strong>Tie off the rectum:</strong> Prevent faecal leakage before pulling the digestive tract free.</li>
<li><strong>Separate offal immediately:</strong> Liver, heart, and kidneys into clean containers; digestive tract onto a clean tray or bucket never the floor.</li>
<li><strong>Remove gall bladder intact:</strong> Do not let bile touch the liver. Rinse liver, heart, and kidneys in potable water and chill below 4C within 1 hour.</li>
<li><strong>Process tripe correctly:</strong> Empty, rinse, scald at 6570C for 23 minutes, scrape until white, wash with salt and lime/lemon, rinse again.</li>
<li><strong>Dispose of non-edible parts responsibly:</strong> Use approved rendering, incineration, or deep burial; never dump into water or public land.</li>
<li><strong>Wash and chill the carcass:</strong> Rinse the cavity with potable water (and approved sanitiser if available); chill to &lt;7C within 1 hour and &lt;4C within 24 hours.</li>
</ol>
