Module 10

Scaling, Risk and Growing the Business

One good batch is luck. A business is a system that survives bad batches. This module covers staggering batches for steady cash flow, working capital and the real danger of credit, price and feed cost risk, theft and security, drought shocks, when hiring actually pays, and a realistic three year growth path built on reinvestment rather than borrowing.

What you will be able to do after this module

  • You will be able to design a staggered batch calendar that produces regular sale income.
  • You will be able to calculate the working capital one batch cycle requires before you commit to it.
  • You will be able to judge whether a credit offer is affordable using the cost of gain and cycle length.
  • You will be able to name your main price and feed cost risks and put practical limits on each.
  • You will be able to put in place basic theft prevention and drought contingency measures.
  • You will be able to write a three year growth plan funded mainly from retained profit.

Batching and Staggering for Steady Cash Flow

In this lesson
  • Understand why one big batch is riskier than several small ones
  • Build a staggered intake calendar
  • Match batch size to pen, feed and cash capacity

The single batch model is how most fatteners start and how many of them fail. You put all your money into cattle once a year, feed for three or four months, sell everything on one day, and your entire year's result depends on the price available in that one week. If it is a bad week, you have no second chance and nothing coming in for months.

Staggering

Staggering means running several smaller batches that start and finish at different times. With a finishing period of about 90 to 120 days, three overlapping batches with intakes roughly six to eight weeks apart will give you a sale roughly every six to eight weeks all year.

AJanuaryApril to May
BMarchJune to July
CMayAugust to September
DJulyOctober to November

Why staggering is worth the extra work

  • Price risk drops. You sell into several different price weeks, so you get something near the average instead of gambling on one point in time.
  • Cash flow smooths. Sale income arrives regularly, which means you can pay wages and buy feed without borrowing.
  • Learning speeds up. Four smaller batches a year give four sets of records and four chances to improve. One batch gives one.
  • Mistakes get smaller. A ration error or a disease outbreak hits a portion of your business, not all of it.
  • Buyers stay interested. A butcher who can rely on animals every six weeks values you more than a once a year supplier.

The costs of staggering

It requires more pens, or at least separate feeding areas, because animals at different stages need different rations and must not compete at the same trough. It means more frequent handling, buying and record keeping. And it means you can never fully empty and rest the whole facility at once, so cleaning and disease break periods must be planned pen by pen.

Sizing a batch

Batch size is set by the tightest of four limits. Work all four out and take the smallest answer:

  1. Pen space. Overcrowding costs gain, causes injury and spreads disease. Get the recommended space per head for your pen type and climate from your local extension or veterinary service, and do not exceed it.
  2. Feed supply. Estimate total dry matter needed: number of animals times daily dry matter intake, roughly 2.0 to 2.5 percent of live weight, times days on feed. Can you secure and store that much, at a known price, for the whole cycle? If not, the batch is too big.
  3. Cash. Purchase cost plus feed plus running costs for the whole cycle, plus a reserve, must be available without borrowing against the sale.
  4. Labour and attention. How many animals can actually be fed, watered, observed and treated properly each day by the people you have.

Start smaller than you can afford

The commonest scaling mistake is jumping from four animals to forty because one batch went well. Double, do not multiply. Each step up exposes a weakness you did not know you had: water supply, mixing capacity, feed storage, labour, buyer capacity. Discover those on eight animals, not on eighty.

Plan intakes backwards from sale windows

Decide when you want the money, then count back the 90 to 120 days of finishing plus a few weeks for buying and settling. Put those purchase dates in a calendar on the wall. Buying opportunistically because cattle looked cheap, with no plan for when they will be sold or what will feed them, is how good farmers end up with animals they cannot afford to finish.

Lesson 10.216 min

Working Capital and the Danger of Credit

In this lesson
  • Calculate the working capital one batch cycle requires
  • Test whether a loan is affordable from cost of gain and margin
  • Recognise the credit arrangements that are most dangerous

Fattening is capital hungry. You pay for the animal and for four months of feed before a single cent comes back. Businesses in this trade fail from running out of cash far more often than from bad cattle. All figures below use CU, a placeholder currency unit; use your own.

Calculating the working capital requirement

For one batch, add:

  1. Purchase cost of the animals, plus buying transport and commission
  2. Total feed cost for the full cycle
  3. Health, water, power, repairs
  4. Labour for the cycle
  5. Selling transport and levies
  6. A contingency of at least 10 to 15 percent for feed price rises, a sick animal, a delayed sale

Worked example, ten steers, 100 day cycle:

ItemCU
Purchase, 10 head90,000
Buying transport and commission6,000
Feed, whole cycle48,000
Health and other running costs7,000
Labour9,000
Selling costs5,000
Subtotal165,000
Contingency at 12 percent19,800
Working capital needed184,800

Note that the money is not needed all at once. Purchase is day one; feed is spread over the cycle. Draw a simple month by month cash line so you know the largest amount you will ever be short by. That peak, not the total, is what you must be able to cover.

The problem with credit in this business

Credit is not automatically bad. Credit that costs more than the margin it creates is fatal. Test it directly.

Suppose a lender offers 100,000 CU for one cycle of 4 months at 5 percent per month, which is 20 percent over the cycle, or 20,000 CU of interest. Your planned margin on that batch, after all costs, is 25,000 CU. Interest eats 80 percent of the margin, and any one of a feed price rise, a slow sale or one dead animal turns the batch into a loss that you still have to repay. Now run the same test at 2 percent per month: 8,000 CU of interest against 25,000 CU of margin. That is survivable, though still not comfortable.

The affordability rules

  • Convert every credit offer to a total cost for the full cycle length, not a monthly or annual rate. Cattle do not care about annual rates; they care about the four months your money is tied up.
  • If interest over the cycle exceeds about a third of your planned margin, the batch has no room for a single thing going wrong.
  • Never borrow to cover a loss on an existing batch in the hope that the next one will fix it. This is the fastest known route out of the business.
  • Never borrow against a price you have not been offered in writing.
  • Never pledge your breeding stock, land or a house as security for a fattening batch. The batch can fail; the family should not.

The most dangerous arrangements

  • Feed on credit from a buyer who will also buy the cattle. The same person controls your input cost and your sale price, and you have no ability to walk away. If you use such a scheme, get both prices fixed in writing at the start.
  • Informal high interest lending. Monthly rates that sound small are enormous over a cycle.
  • Rolling short term debt. Borrowing again to repay the last loan while the cattle are still eating.
  • Verbal partnerships. Someone funds, someone feeds, nothing is written down about what happens if the batch loses money. Write down the split of both profit and loss, before any money moves.

The better route

Self funding is slower and safer. Start with the number of animals you can fully fund, keep a reserve equal to a couple of months of feed, and reinvest the margin. Growth funded by retained profit compounds quietly and cannot foreclose on you.

Lesson 10.315 min

Price Risk and the Buy Low Sell High Trap

In this lesson
  • Understand the buy sell margin and why it dominates results
  • Avoid buying cheap animals that are cheap for a reason
  • Apply practical ways to limit price risk

Fatteners talk about feed, but the difference between a good year and a bad year is usually the relationship between what you paid for the store animal and what you were paid for the finished one. That relationship is called the buy sell margin, and you control only half of it.

The structure of the problem

You buy at a price you know, on the day you buy. You sell three to four months later at a price you cannot know. Everything in between, however well you feed, sits inside that gap. This is why the purchase decision is a marketing decision, not a cattle decision.

Worked illustration in CU. A 280 kg store animal taken to 400 kg with a total cost of gain of 6,600 CU.

CaseBuy price per kg liveSell price per kg liveResult
Margin widens34 = 9,52044 = 17,600plus 1,480
Margin flat38 = 10,64040 = 16,000minus 1,240
Margin narrows40 = 11,20036 = 14,400minus 3,400

Same feeding performance in all three rows. Same animal. The entire outcome is the price relationship. Good feeding cannot rescue a bad buy sell margin; it can only make a good one better.

The buy low sell high trap

Everyone intends to buy low and sell high. The trap has three parts.

  1. Cheap animals are usually cheap for a reason. Old, already fat, sick, parasitised, poorly grown or of a type that converts badly. Old cows and very fat animals convert poorly and grade poorly, and the discount at purchase rarely covers what they cost you in feed and grade.
  2. Everyone buys at the same time. Store cattle are cheapest when everyone is destocking, usually late in the dry season, which is also when feed is dearest and grazing is finished. The cheap animal arrives at your gate at the moment it is most expensive to feed. Cost the whole cycle, not the purchase.
  3. Everyone sells at the same time. If cattle were cheap because a district destocked, that district's animals are finishing on roughly the same schedule as yours, and supply will be heavy when you want to sell.

Practical ways to limit price risk

  • Set a maximum buying price before you go. Work backwards: take a conservative sale price, subtract your expected cost of gain and your required margin, and that gives the most you can pay. Write it down and hold it. A discipline of walking away from a whole auction is normal and necessary.
  • Stagger, as in Lesson 1. Multiple sale dates give you the average instead of one bet.
  • Buy young, well grown, healthy animals with growth still ahead of them. Check dentition and body condition. Frame with condition still to gain converts feed into money; an already fat animal converts feed into waste.
  • Sell some forward if you can. An agreed price with a butcher or abattoir for delivery in three months, in writing, removes uncertainty from part of the batch. Never forward sell more than you are confident of delivering.
  • Keep records of prices paid and received every month. Your own price series is the only reliable guide to your own market's shape.

The rule that saves money

Do not buy an animal until you can state, on paper, three things: what you expect to pay per kilogram of live weight, what you expect it to cost you per kilogram of gain, and what price you must receive to make the margin worth the work. If you cannot fill in all three, you are not buying cattle, you are gambling with a feed bill attached.

Theft, Security and Loss Prevention

In this lesson
  • Identify where losses actually occur, including feed
  • Put in place layered, low cost security
  • Keep the identification and paperwork that makes recovery possible

A finished steer is the most valuable and most portable asset most smallholders will ever own, standing in a pen at the end of a road, at exactly the moment it is worth the most. Fattening operations are targeted precisely because the animals are concentrated, fat and easy to move. Treat security as a cost of the business, not an afterthought.

Where the losses actually happen

  • Stock theft from pens or grazing, most often at night and often near sale time, when everyone locally knows the animals are ready.
  • Feed theft. Underrated and constant. Bags disappearing, short deliveries, feed sold out the back gate. Feed is the dominant running cost, so a steady leak here quietly destroys the margin without a single animal going missing.
  • Short weighing and false weights at purchase, transport or sale.
  • Slaughter on site, where thieves kill in the field and remove only the meat.
  • Fraud at sale: animals released before payment clears, cheques or transfers that fail, buyers who cannot be traced afterwards.

Layered prevention

No single measure works. Layers do.

  1. Identification. Brand or tag every animal according to your national requirements, and photograph each one from both sides with any distinctive markings visible. Store photographs where they will survive a phone being lost. Identification requirements and their legal weight vary by country; confirm what is required and what proof your police and courts accept.
  2. Paperwork. Keep purchase receipts, movement permits, clearance certificates and stock registers complete and current. Recovery of stolen animals very often turns on whether you can prove ownership. Rules differ by country; confirm locally.
  3. Physical. A strong night pen or kraal close to the house, a single lockable gate, no vehicle access to the pen after dark, and lighting where power allows.
  4. Human. Dogs. A night watchman if the value justifies it. Neighbours who know your animals and vehicles, and a group message system that reports strange vehicles.
  5. Routine. Count every animal every morning and evening. Write the count down. A count that is only remembered is not a count. Discovering a theft at dawn instead of two days later is the difference between recovery and loss.
  6. Feed controls. Lock the feed store. One key holder. Count and sign for every delivery against the invoice, and reconcile feed bought against feed fed on the pen card every month. Investigate any gap immediately.

Around sale time

  • Do not announce that animals are ready or that a sale is coming.
  • Do not leave finished animals in a distant paddock in the final weeks.
  • Do not release animals before payment has actually cleared into your account. A promise is not payment, and a screenshot is not payment.
  • Do not carry large amounts of cash home from a sale, alone, on a predictable route.
  • Verify a first time buyer's identity, address and vehicle registration before loading.

    • Cost it, then decide

    Compare the annual cost of a measure against the value of one animal. If a night watchman, a strong gate or a padlock on the feed store costs less over a year than a single finished steer, it is not an expense, it is insurance you can actually collect on. Losing one animal from a ten head batch removes a large part of that batch's entire margin.

    Insurance

    Livestock insurance is available in some countries and not others, and terms vary widely. Ask what is offered locally, read what is actually covered, especially whether theft and disease are included, and check the conditions you must meet for a claim to be paid.

Lesson 10.515 min

Drought, Feed Price Shocks and Contingency

In this lesson
  • Recognise the sequence of a feed price shock
  • Build practical feed security before a shock arrives
  • Decide quickly whether to exit a batch early

Feed is the dominant running cost of fattening, so a feed price shock is a direct attack on your margin. In much of East and Southern Africa these shocks follow drought, and drought is not a rare event to be treated as a surprise. It is a recurring feature to be planned for.

How a shock unfolds

  1. Rains fail or are poor. Grazing and crop residues run short earlier than usual.
  2. Everyone destocks at once. Store and slaughter cattle flood the market, so cattle prices fall.
  3. Grain and by product prices rise, because the same failed harvest that killed the grazing also cut the maize crop.
  4. Your feed cost rises and your sale price falls, at the same time, which is the worst possible combination for a fattener.
  5. Water becomes scarce, and restricted water cuts intake and gain before anything else does.

Note the trap inside step 2. Cheap cattle look like an opportunity, and sometimes they are, but only if you can secure and afford feed for the whole cycle at a price you have already fixed. Buying cheap cattle into an expensive and rising feed market is the classic drought year loss.

Building feed security

  • Buy feed at harvest, not at the trough. Grain and by product prices are usually lowest just after harvest. Buying and storing then, with proper storage against moisture, insects and rodents, is the single most effective protection available to a smallholder fattener.
  • Store what you can safely store. Costed storage losses still usually beat buying at peak prices, but only if storage is genuinely dry and pest controlled.
  • Make and keep conserved forage. Hay, crop residues, silage where practical. Cut and store when material is available, not when you need it.
  • Know several by product suppliers. Brewers grains, molasses, cottonseed cake, bran, oilseed cakes and similar products vary in availability and price. Depending on one supplier for one ingredient is a risk.
  • Fix prices where you can. A written agreement to supply a quantity of feed at an agreed price over the cycle removes your biggest uncertainty. Offer to pay up front for part of it if that secures the price.
  • Treat water as feed. Storage, a backup source, and a plan for pump failure. Intake collapses without clean water, and gain collapses with it.

The decision rules when a shock hits

Run these at the next weighing, not in three months.

  1. Recalculate cost of gain at today's feed prices, not the prices you budgeted.
  2. Get a current realistic sale price for the animals as they stand, by phoning buyers.
  3. If cost of gain now exceeds the value of a kilogram of live weight, stop feeding for gain and sell, even unfinished, unless you have prepaid feed already in store.
  4. If cost of gain is still below the value of a kilogram but the margin has become thin, consider selling the poorest converters and feeding the best animals on with the feed you have. Concentrating scarce feed on the animals that convert best is better than spreading it thinly across all.
  5. Do not borrow to feed through a shock on the hope of a price recovery. That combines price risk, feed cost risk and debt in one position.

Contingency plan on one page

Write down now, before you need it: which buyers you would call for an emergency sale, which feed suppliers you would approach, what your minimum acceptable price would be, what your water backup is, and which animals you would sell first. A plan written calmly in a good month is worth far more than a decision made in panic in a bad one.

Lesson 10.616 min

Hiring Help and a Realistic Three Year Growth Path

In this lesson
  • Decide when hiring actually pays for itself
  • Set out a growth path funded by retained profit
  • Choose the few indicators to review each year

Growth in fattening is not about getting bigger fast. It is about repeating a profitable cycle more times, with the same discipline, until the numbers justify the next step.

When hiring pays

A hire pays when the extra margin it makes possible exceeds its full cost. Work it out in CU per year, using your own figures.

  • Full cost of a worker for a year: wages, food or housing if provided, statutory contributions where required, tools and protective clothing.
  • Extra margin made possible: the additional animals you can properly manage, times your average margin per animal, plus the value of losses avoided through better observation and feeding.

Worked example. A worker costs 36,000 CU per year all in. Your average margin is 2,000 CU per animal. The worker allows you to run 24 more animals a year, which is 48,000 CU of extra margin, and better daily observation reduces losses. This hire pays. If the same worker only allows 12 more animals, at 24,000 CU of margin, it does not, and you should either grow first or restructure the role.

Practical signals that you need help before the arithmetic is comfortable: animals not being observed individually every day, feed being mixed late or wrong, weighing being skipped, records falling behind, or you personally being unable to leave the farm for a day. Those are all early signs of losses you are not yet measuring.

Hire for the right job

Most first hires should be for daily feeding, water checking and observation, because those tasks are daily, non negotiable and directly affect gain. Train explicitly: how much of what to feed, in what order, how to spot a sick animal, when to call you. Written instructions with pictures, and a signed daily feed sheet, prevent most of the problems that get blamed on workers.

Employment law, minimum wages and contributions vary by country. Confirm the requirements with your local labour office and comply with them.

A realistic three year path

Year 1: prove the system. Run two to three small batches of the size you can fully self fund. Focus entirely on records, ADG, FCR, cost of gain and break-even. Sell to at least three different buyers to learn the market. Target: complete records for every batch, and a positive margin on at least two of three. Reinvest all margin.

Year 2: repeat and stagger. Move to a staggered calendar with three or four batches, roughly doubling annual throughput, funded from year one margin plus your own savings. Build feed security: storage, a harvest time feed purchase, one or two supplier relationships. Establish two reliable buyers who take animals regularly. Target: a sale every six to eight weeks, a cash reserve equal to about two months of feed cost, and your own recorded dressing percentage figure.

Year 3: consolidate, then step up. Roughly double again only if all three of these are true: your records show a positive margin in the great majority of batches, you have working capital for the larger scale without borrowing against unsold cattle, and your feed supply and pen space genuinely support the larger number. This is usually the year a first hire and a permanent improvement, such as a proper handling crush, a feed store, or a water tank, become justified.

The five year end indicators

  1. Average ADG across all batches
  2. Average FCR across all batches
  3. Average full cost per kilogram of gain
  4. Average margin per animal, and total margin for the year
  5. Number of batches with a negative margin, and the reason for each

Review those five once a year, on paper. If the trend is right and the reasons for losses are understood, grow. If not, fix before you scale, because scaling multiplies whatever the system already does, including its mistakes.

Knowledge check

Five questions. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. Why does staggering batches reduce risk compared with one large annual batch?

Staggering spreads sales across several price points and smooths cash flow, so no single bad week or single mistake decides the whole year.

2. A lender offers money for a 4 month cycle at 5 percent per month. Your planned margin on the batch is 25,000 CU and the interest would total 20,000 CU. What is the correct conclusion?

Interest of 20,000 against a planned margin of 25,000 leaves almost nothing for the things that routinely go wrong, and the debt must be repaid whether the batch works or not.

3. Two fatteners feed identically. One buys at 34 CU per kg live and sells at 44; the other buys at 40 and sells at 36. What does this show?

With identical feeding performance the entire difference comes from the price relationship between purchase and sale. Good feeding improves a sound buy sell margin but cannot rescue a bad one.

4. During a drought your feed cost has risen sharply. Which check comes first?

The stop feeding rule still applies but must be run with today's prices. If cost of gain exceeds the value of a kilogram of live weight, further feeding destroys money.

5. A worker will cost 36,000 CU a year all in. Your average margin is 2,000 CU per animal and the worker lets you properly manage 12 more animals a year. What does the arithmetic say?

12 animals times 2,000 CU is 24,000 CU of extra margin against a 36,000 CU cost, so this hire loses money at that scale. Grow first, or restructure the role.

Module 10 capstone

Final project: write a complete costed plan for one fattening batch from start to sale. Include the number and type of animals and your maximum buying price per kilogram, the ration and total dry matter required, expected ADG and FCR with the days on feed, full working capital with contingency and the month of peak cash need, cost per kilogram of gain, break-even prices on both a live weight and a carcass weight basis across a 50 to 58 percent dressing range, three named buyers with their price basis and deductions, the transport and shrink plan, the intended sale window and target price, and a one page contingency plan for theft, disease and a feed price shock.
