1. Nutritional Requirements for Hatchlings, Juveniles, and Adults

Hatchlings (0-6 months) need a diet high in protein and energy for rapid growth. In Africa, use minced fresh fish (e.g., tilapia, catfish) or day-old chicks, supplemented with a commercial crocodile starter crumble (40-45% protein). Feed them 5-8% of their body weight daily, split into two feeds. For example, a 100g hatchling gets 5-8g of feed per day. Calcium is critical: add 2% bone meal or oyster shell to prevent rickets. Juveniles (6-24 months) grow slower but still need 35-40% protein. Transition to chopped fish, poultry offal, and a grower pellet (32-36% protein). Feed 3-5% of body weight daily. A 5kg juvenile eats 150-250g per day. Adults (over 2 years) are maintained for meat or breeding. Protein needs drop to 28-32%. Use whole fish, meat scraps, and a maintenance pellet. Feed 1-2% of body weight every other day. A 50kg adult gets 500g-1kg per feed. Breeders need extra calcium and vitamin E for egg production. Always provide fresh water. In Africa, many farmers use locally available by-catch from Lake Victoria or small fish from rivers. Avoid feeding spoiled meat, which causes botulism. Adjust rations based on body condition: if the tail base looks thin, increase feed; if fat deposits appear, reduce. Record weekly weights to track growth. Hatchlings should gain 30-50g per week; juveniles 100-200g per week. Overfeeding leads to fatty liver and waste. Underfeeding stunts growth and increases aggression. Use a simple hanging scale and a plastic tub to weigh sample animals monthly. This data helps you fine-tune rations. Remember, crocodiles are ectothermic; they eat less in cold weather. In cooler months (below 25C), reduce feeding to once a week or stop if they are not active. In hot months, they may eat more. Always remove uneaten feed after 2 hours to prevent fouling.

2. Feed Formulation and Commercial Diets

You can mix your own feed to cut costs, but it must meet protein, fat, and mineral needs. A basic African formulation for growers: 50% minced fish (e.g., sardines, tilapia), 20% poultry offal (liver, heart, gizzard), 15% cooked maize or cassava, 10% soybean meal, and 5% vitamin-mineral premix. This gives about 35% protein. For adults, increase maize to 25% and reduce fish to 40%, yielding 28% protein. Mix in a cement mixer or by hand on a clean tarpaulin. Add 1% salt and 2% bone meal. Form into balls or use a meat mincer to make pellets. Store in airtight containers in a cool, dry place. Commercial crocodile diets are available from feed companies in South Africa, Zimbabwe, and Kenya. Brands like Croc Grow (32% protein) or Reptile Pellet (40% protein) are extruded and floating, which reduces waste. They cost more but save labour and ensure balanced nutrition. When switching from homemade to commercial, mix them for 5 days to avoid digestive upset. For hatchlings, use a starter crumble (45% protein) from a reputable supplier. If commercial feed is unavailable, you can use fish pellets meant for tilapia (28-32% protein) but supplement with minced fish and vitamins. Never use chicken layer mash (high calcium) for growersit can cause kidney damage. Test your formulation by sending a sample to a lab for proximate analysis; many African universities offer this for a small fee. Keep records of ingredients and costs. A good target is a feed conversion ratio (FCR) of 1.5-2.0 kg feed per kg gain. If FCR is above 2.5, your diet is inefficient. Adjust by increasing protein or reducing fibre. Always provide fresh water for drinking and soaking. In hot climates, feed early morning or late evening to reduce spoilage. Mix only enough for 2-3 days; mouldy feed kills crocodiles.

3. Feeding Schedules and Ration Sizes

Crocodiles are opportunistic feeders, but in farming you must control intake. Hatchlings (0-6 months) feed twice daily: at 8am and 4pm. Give 5-8% of body weight per day. For a 200g hatchling, that's 10-16g total, split into two 5-8g feeds. Juveniles (6-24 months) feed once daily, preferably in the morning. Give 3-5% of body weight. A 10kg juvenile gets 300-500g. Adults (over 2 years) feed every other day. Give 1-2% of body weight per feed. A 100kg adult gets 1-2kg every two days. Breeders (for egg production) feed 2-3% of body weight twice a week, with extra calcium. Use a feeding chart: weigh a sample of 10 animals monthly. If average weight gain is below 20g/week for hatchlings, increase ration by 10%. If above 50g/week, reduce to avoid obesity. In cold weather (below 25C), crocodiles become sluggish. Reduce feeding to once a week for juveniles and adults, and stop for hatchlings if they refuse food. In hot months (above 30C), they may eat more; offer slightly larger rations but remove leftovers after 2 hours. Use long-handled tongs to place feed on a clean concrete slab or feeding tray. Never throw feed into waterit sinks and rots. For large farms, use a feeding schedule board: Monday, Wednesday, Friday for adults; daily for juveniles. Record feed given and leftovers. Uneaten feed attracts flies and bacteria. If crocodiles don't eat, check water temperature, ammonia levels, and signs of disease. A sudden drop in appetite is the first sign of illness. Always feed at the same time and place to reduce stress. During transport or handling, skip one feed. After vaccination or deworming, wait 24 hours before feeding. Adjust rations based on body condition score: 1 (thin) to 5 (obese). Aim for 3 (ideal). This simple system prevents waste and keeps your crocodiles healthy.

4. Live Prey vs. Processed Feeds

In the wild, crocodiles eat live preyfish, birds, mammals. In captivity, you can use live prey to stimulate natural hunting, but it has risks. Live fish (e.g., tilapia) can introduce parasites like tapeworms. Live rodents may carry leptospirosis. Live poultry can spread avian flu. If you use live prey, source from disease-free farms and quarantine for 14 days. Live prey is expensive and labour-intensive. Processed feedsminced fish, offal, pelletsare safer, cheaper, and easier to store. They also allow precise nutrient control. For hatchlings, minced fish is best; they cannot handle live prey. For juveniles, you can offer live insects (crickets, locusts) as a treat to encourage exercise, but not as staple. For adults, whole dead fish or day-old chicks are ideal. Processed feeds should be fresh: mince fish within 24 hours of catch, or freeze at -20C for up to 3 months. Thaw in a refrigerator, not in the sun. Never feed spoiled meat. In Africa, many farmers use by-catch from fishing boatssmall fish not sold for human consumption. This is excellent if fresh. You can also use chicken slaughter waste (heads, feet, intestines) but must cook it to kill bacteria. Boil for 20 minutes, then mince. Commercial pellets are processed and balanced; they float, so you can see how much is eaten. They are more expensive but reduce waste and disease risk. A hybrid approach works well: use commercial pellets as the base (70% of diet) and supplement with fresh fish or offal (30%) for variety. This balances cost and nutrition. Never feed live prey that can bite or injure crocodiles, such as large crabs or turtles. Also avoid live prey that may escape and become invasive. For breeding adults, live prey can improve condition, but only if from a trusted source. Always remove uneaten live prey after 1 hour to prevent stress. In summary, processed feeds are the backbone of commercial farming; live prey is optional and only for enrichment or if you have a reliable, safe source.

5. Supplements and Vitamin Requirements

Crocodiles in captivity often lack calcium, vitamin D3, and vitamin E. Without them, hatchlings get soft bones (rickets), adults lay thin-shelled eggs, and breeders suffer infertility. Calcium: add 2% bone meal or oyster shell to homemade feed. For commercial pellets, check the labelmost already have calcium. If not, sprinkle 1 teaspoon of calcium carbonate per 1kg feed. Vitamin D3: crocodiles synthesise D3 from sunlight. If kept indoors or in shaded pens, supplement with a commercial reptile vitamin containing D3. Dose: 1ml per 10kg body weight, mixed into feed once a week. Vitamin E: important for reproduction. Give 100 IU per kg feed for breeders, or use wheat germ oil (1ml per 5kg feed) twice a week. Multivitamins: use a poultry or reptile multivitamin (e.g., Stress Pak) at 1g per 5kg feed weekly. In Africa, many farmers use cod liver oil (rich in A and D) at 5ml per kg feed, but avoid overdoseexcess vitamin A causes liver damage. For hatchlings, add a pinch of calcium powder to minced fish daily. For juveniles, supplement twice a week. For adults, once a week is enough unless breeding. Always provide a shallow water dish with clean water; crocodiles absorb some minerals through the skin. If your crocodiles are lethargic, have soft jaws, or show bent spines, they need immediate calcium and D3. Mix supplements thoroughly into feed; never top-dress because crocodiles may reject it. Store supplements in a cool, dark placeheat destroys vitamins. Check expiry dates. A simple rule: if you use fresh whole fish (with bones and organs), you need fewer supplements. If you use muscle meat only, you must supplement heavily. Keep a supplement log: date, product, dose, and animal group. This helps you track and adjust. Over-supplementation is as dangerous as deficiency. Stick to recommended doses. When in doubt, consult a veterinarian or extension officer. In hot climates, provide shade so crocodiles can regulate D3 synthesis. Never rely on supplements alone; a balanced diet is the foundation.

6. Feed Storage and Hygiene

Feed spoils quickly in Africa's heat. Store all feed in a cool, dry, dark room. Ideal temperature: below 25C. Humidity below 60%. Use airtight plastic drums or silos. Never store feed on the flooruse pallets. For fresh fish or offal, freeze at -20C. Use within 3 months. Thaw in a refrigerator, not in the sun. Once thawed, use within 24 hours. Never refreeze. Dry pellets: keep in original bags, sealed. Use within 6 weeks of opening. Write the date on the bag. If you mix your own feed, make only 2-3 days' worth. Store in a covered bucket in a cool place. Before feeding, inspect for mould, off-odours, or insects. Mouldy feed contains aflatoxinsdeadly to crocodiles. If you see green or black spots, discard the whole batch. Clean feeding trays and slabs daily. Remove uneaten feed after 2 hours. Scrub with soap and water, then rinse with disinfectant (e.g., 1% bleach solution). Let dry in the sun. This prevents bacterial growth. Keep feed preparation area separate from animal pens. Wash hands and tools after handling raw meat. Use dedicated knives and chopping boards. For large farms, have a cold room. For small farms, a deep freezer works. If power is unreliable, use a generator or buy ice. In rural areas, you can use evaporative cooling: place feed in a clay pot inside a wet sack. But this only works for short periods. Never feed spoiled or rancid feedit causes botulism, which kills within 24 hours. Symptoms: floppy neck, difficulty breathing. There is no cure. Prevention is key. Also, control rodents and insects. Use traps, not poisonpoison can contaminate feed. Store supplements separately in a locked cabinet. Keep records: date received, expiry, date opened. First in, first out. Train workers on hygiene. A clean farm is a profitable farm. Remember, crocodiles are resilient but not immune to bad feed. One mistake can wipe out your stock. So, invest in proper storage and hygiene from day one.

<h2>Practical Reference: Crocodile Nutrition and Feeding</h2>

<p>Crocodile farming is one of the highest-value agribusinesses in Africa. A single Nile crocodile (<em>Crocodylus niloticus</em>) can return USD 300900 as a belly skin plus premium meat, depending on size and grade, while a well-managed breeder pond can produce 4060 hatchlings per female per year. But those margins collapse fast when nutrition is wrong. Feed is the single largest operating cost in a crocodile operation typically 3555% of total production cost and poor nutrition shows up as slow growth, runting, skin defects, and mortality that wipe out profit. This module gives you the practical numbers, formulations, and hygiene protocols used by commercial farms in South Africa, Zimbabwe, Zambia, Kenya, and Nigeria.</p>

In South Africa, commercial extruded crocodile pellets (e.g., from specialist feed mills) are increasingly used because they standardise intake and reduce disease risk compared with raw offal.</p>

<h3>3. Feeding Schedules and Ration Sizes</h3>

<p>Feeding frequency drives both growth and feed conversion ratio (FCR). Commercial farms target an FCR of 1.62.2 kg feed per kg live weight gain. Overfeeding wastes money and fouls water; underfeeding stalls growth and causes runting a permanent, unprofitable condition.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Hatchlings:</strong> feed 57 times per week, 810% of body weight per day, chopped into 12 cm pieces.</li>
<li><strong>Juveniles:</strong> feed 35 times per week, 46% of body weight per day.</li>
<li><strong>Adults / breeders:</strong> feed 13 times per week, 24% of body weight per day; reduce to 1% in winter (MayAugust in Southern Africa).</li>
</ul>

<p>Water temperature is the master variable. Below 20 C, digestion slows dramatically and uneaten feed rots. In African operations, winter feeding should be suspended or heavily reduced unless ponds are heated or indoors.</p>

<h3>4. Live Prey vs. Processed Feeds</h3>

<p>Live prey (tilapia, frogs, insects) improves natural behaviour and reduces stress, but it is expensive per kilogram of gain and carries parasite risk. Processed feeds extruded pellets, minced offal, or sausage-style rations give you control over nutrient content, cost, and biosecurity. Most profitable African farms use processed feed as the base and live prey only as enrichment or for breeders during conditioning.</p>

<h3>5. Supplements and Vitamin Requirements</h3>

<p>The classic deficiency in farmed crocodiles is vitamin E / selenium myopathy, causing stiff, weak hatchlings with poor skin quality. Vitamin A deficiency causes eye problems and stunted growth. Vitamin D must be included in the premix because indoor pens block UV synthesis. Calcium and phosphorus must be balanced never feed raw meat alone, as its Ca:P ratio (~1:20) causes metabolic bone disease.</p>

<h3>6. Feed Storage and Hygiene</h3>

<p>Feed stored above 12% moisture grows mould and produces aflatoxins a serious risk in humid African climates. Store pelleted and dry feed in sealed bins on pallets, off the floor, in a cool, dark, ventilated room. Use FIFO (first-in, first-out) rotation. Fresh offal must be frozen at 18 C or used within 24 hours. Clean feeding troughs daily; remove uneaten feed within 2 hours to prevent water fouling and bacterial blooms.</p>
