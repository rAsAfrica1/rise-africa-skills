1. Selecting and Cutting Stock for a Hook

For your first hook, choose mild steel round bar, 10mm to 12mm diameter. This size is easy to heat and move by hand, yet strong enough for hanging pots, tools, or lanterns. In many African markets, you can buy offcuts from welding workshops or scrap yards. Look for clean, rust-free pieces. Avoid galvanized or painted metalâthe fumes are toxic when heated. A good length is 30cm to 40cm. That gives you enough to form the hook and a mounting plate or eye. If you only have a long bar, cut it with a hacksaw or a hot cut on the anvil. For a hot cut, heat the bar to bright orange (about 900Â°C), place it over the anvil's cutting edge, and strike with a chisel or the edge of your hammer. Remember to cut on the far side of the anvil so the cut piece doesn't fly. For a cold cut, clamp the bar in a vise and use a hacksaw with 18 to 24 teeth per inch. Add a few drops of oil to ease the cut. After cutting, smooth the ends with a file or grinder. If you don't have a vise, lay the bar on a wooden block and secure it with your foot. Always wear safety glasses and gloves. In rural areas, a machete or axe can score the metal, but a hacksaw is safer and more precise. Once cut, you're ready to forge.

2. Drawing Out the Hook Point

Drawing out means stretching the metal to make it longer and thinner. Heat one end of your 10mm bar to a bright yellow-orange (about 1000Â°C). Hold it on the anvil with tongs. Strike the hot end with the flat face of your hammer, aiming slightly downward to taper the tip. Work from the tip back toward the middle. Use a series of overlapping blowsâabout 20 to 30 strikesâturning the bar 90 degrees every few hits to keep it square or round. If the metal cools below cherry red (around 700Â°C), stop and reheat. Forging cold causes cracks. You want a point that is about 5mm thick at the tip and tapers over 8cm to 10cm. This point will become the sharp end of the hook. To make a neat taper, use the horn of the anvil for curves or the flat face for straight tapers. If you have a cross-peen hammer, use the peen to stretch faster, then smooth with the flat face. Check your work oftenâhold it up to the light to see if the taper is even. In many African smithies, a simple charcoal forge with a hand-cranked blower works well. Keep your fire clean and focused. After drawing out, you may need to straighten the bar by tapping it on the anvil. Don't rush; a good point makes the hook look professional.

3. Forming the Hook Curve

Now you'll bend the pointed end into a J-shaped curve. Heat the middle section of the barâabout 10cm from the pointâto a bright orange. Place the hot section over the horn of the anvil. The horn's rounded shape helps you form a smooth curve. Using your hammer, strike the bar gently, bending it around the horn. Work from the point backward, making a curve that is about 5cm in diameter. Don't hit too hard; you want a controlled bend, not a kink. If you don't have a horn, use a pipe or a thick metal rod clamped in a vise. For a tighter hook, bend around a smaller diameter. For a larger hook, use a bigger form. Once the basic curve is formed, check it by eye. The curve should be even and the point should face inward, parallel to the shank. If it's uneven, reheat and adjust. You can also use a bending forkâa tool that fits in the hardy holeâto fine-tune the curve. Remember to keep the metal hot; bending cold can cause cracks. After bending, you may want to flatten the curve slightly with a hammer to give it a forged look. This also strengthens the hook. In Africa, hooks are used for hanging pots over fires, so make sure the curve is deep enough to hold a handle securely. Test it with a stick or a pot handle. A good hook curve is the heart of your project.

4. Twisting the Hook for Decoration

A decorative twist adds beauty and shows off your skill. Heat the straight section of the barâbetween the hook curve and the opposite endâto a bright orange. You'll need a vise and a wrench or twisting bar. Clamp one end of the heated section in the vise. Grip the other end with a wrench or a pair of tongs with a twisting bar. Turn the wrench clockwise or counterclockwise to create a spiral. For a uniform twist, apply steady pressure and turn the same number of times. A good twist for a 10mm bar is about 3 to 4 full turns over 5cm. If you turn too much, the metal may crack. If you don't have a vise, you can twist by holding one end with tongs and the other with a hammer handle through the eyeâbut a vise is safer. After twisting, you may want to flatten the twist slightly with a hammer to create a faceted look. This also makes the twist more pronounced. Reheat as needed. Twisting is best done when the metal is at a consistent orange heat. If it cools, stop and reheat. Once twisted, straighten the bar if it bent during twisting. A decorative twist not only looks good but also adds strength. In many African cultures, twisted ironwork is traditional and adds value to your product. Practice on a scrap piece first to get the feel. Then apply the twist to your hook.

5. Finishing the Hook: Brushing and Waxing

Finishing makes your hook look professional and protects it from rust. Start by letting the hook cool to room temperature. Use a wire brushâhand brush or wire wheel on a drillâto remove scale and smooth the surface. Brush in one direction to create a nice grain. If you have a bench grinder with a wire wheel, that's faster. For a shiny finish, sand with 80-grit then 120-grit emery cloth. Next, heat the hook again to about 200Â°Câjust hot enough that a drop of water sizzles. You can do this in your forge or with a torch. Then apply a thin coat of beeswax or paraffin wax using a cloth or a brush. The heat melts the wax, which soaks into the metal and creates a protective black finish. Rub the wax in while the hook is hot. Let it cool, then buff with a clean cloth. For a darker finish, you can use linseed oil instead of wax. Heat the hook to about 300Â°C and apply oil with a rag; it will smoke and polymerize, leaving a tough coating. Repeat two or three times. In Africa, beeswax is often available from local beekeepers. Avoid using motor oilâit's toxic. After waxing, your hook should have a smooth, slightly glossy finish. Check for any sharp edges and file them smooth. A well-finished hook can be sold or used immediately. Hang it by the eye or mounting plate.

6. Evaluating Your Work and Identifying Flaws

After finishing, inspect your hook critically. Look for common flaws: cracks, uneven curves, cold shuts (folds where metal didn't weld), and hammer marks. Cracks often occur if you forged too cold or overheated the steel. If you see a crack, it's best to start overâcracks weaken the hook. Uneven curves can be fixed by reheating and bending, but if the hook is already waxed, you'll need to remove the finish first. Cold shuts happen when you strike with a hammer that has a sharp edge or when you fold metal without welding. They look like lines or overlaps. If a cold shut is deep, discard the hook. Hammer marks are normal but should be smooth. You can file them out. Check the twist: it should be even and not too tight. Check the point: it should be centered and sharp, not off to one side. Test the hook by hanging a weightâstart with 5kg, then 10kg. A good hook should hold at least 20kg without bending. If it bends, the metal may be too soft or the curve too shallow. Also check the eye or mounting holeâit should be smooth and not cut into the metal. Finally, compare your hook to a professional example. Ask yourself: Would I buy this? If not, note what to improve next time. Keep a log of your mistakes and successes. Practice makes perfect.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Forging Your First Project â A Basic Hook

This practical reference supports the RISE AFRICA Skills module Forging Your First Project: A Basic Hook. It is written for the working smith, the farm workshop, the vocational trainee, and the small enterprise that sells finished ironwork at local markets, hardware stores, or to construction clients. A basic hook is a low-cost, high-demand product. In much of Africa it is used for hanging tools, securing gates, lifting sacks, drying fish, and supporting tarpaulins. Mastery of the hook is therefore not only a training exercise; it is a route to cash flow. This reference gives you the measurements, stock choices, forming steps, finishing methods, and quality checks you need to produce hooks consistently and sell them profitably.

1. Selecting and Cutting Stock for a Hook

The hook is normally forged from mild steel round bar. For a general-purpose utility hook, 8 mm to 12 mm diameter is the most practical range. Smaller stock (6 mm) is suitable for light domestic hooks; larger stock (16 mm and above) is used for heavy lifting or structural hooks where the load may exceed 100 kg. In many African markets, stock is sold by length, by weight, or by the piece, and availability varies between urban steel merchants and rural scrap yards. Where new stock is unavailable, vehicle leaf springs, reinforcing bar offcuts, and old gate rods can be recycled, but these must be inspected for cracks and excessive corrosion before use.

Cutting should be done with a hacksaw for small diameters, a bolt cutter for up to 12 mm, or a hot cut with a cold chisel on the anvil for larger stock. Always allow an extra 15â20 mm on the cut length for the point and the finishing allowance. A common beginner error is cutting too short, which leaves no material for drawing out the point or forming the eye.

2. Drawing Out the Hook Point

Drawing out is the process of reducing the cross-section of the steel while increasing its length. For the hook point, heat the last 40â50 mm of the stock to a bright orange (approximately 900â1000Â°C) and hammer it on the horn or the edge of the anvil. Rotate the stock a quarter turn between blows so the taper remains square and symmetrical. The point should taper from the full stock diameter to about 3â4 mm over a length of 40â60 mm. Do not overheat the steel; if it sparks excessively, it is burning and will become brittle.

In a rural African workshop, charcoal or coal is the common fuel. Forge welding and drawing out require a clean, reducing fire. A simple side-blast forge made from a car brake drum and a hand-cranked blower is adequate. Keep the fire slightly green (excess fuel) to avoid oxidation. A useful rule for small enterprises: one person can draw out a hook point in 2â3 heats, which means a production rate of 15â25 hooks per day depending on stock size and skill.

3. Forming the Hook Curve

The curve is formed over the horn of the anvil or a bending fork. Heat the middle of the drawn section to a uniform orange and bend it progressively, not in one heavy blow. For a standard utility hook, the opening (the gap between the point and the shank) should be 40â60 mm, and the bend radius should be smooth, without flats or kinks. A common mistake is bending cold, which causes cracking, especially in recycled steel. If the steel is high-carbon or of unknown origin, normalize it after bending by heating to a dull red and allowing it to cool slowly in air or ash.

4. Twisting the Hook for Decoration

Decorative twisting adds market value without adding much material cost. Heat a 60â80 mm section of the shank to a uniform orange, clamp one end in a vice or hold it with tongs, and apply a steady twisting force with a wrench or a second pair of tongs. Count the turns; 2â4 full twists are usually enough for a decorative hook. Twisting too cold will crack the steel. Twisting too hot will produce a rough, scaly surface. After twisting, straighten the shank and reheat for the next operation. In tourist markets from Nairobi to Accra, twisted hooks can sell for 20â40% more than plain hooks because they are perceived as handcrafted.

5. Finishing the Hook: Brushing and Waxing

Finishing determines whether the hook looks professional and resists rust. First, brush the hot hook with a wire brush while it is still at a black heat (about 400â500Â°C). This removes scale and produces a smooth, dark surface. Then apply a thin coat of beeswax, paraffin wax, or a mixture of wax and linseed oil. The wax melts into the warm steel and forms a protective film. For outdoor hooks, a second coat of linseed oil or a locally available anti-rust paint is recommended. In humid coastal regions, zinc-rich paint or galvanizing is preferable, but waxing is the cheapest and most accessible method for small workshops.

â  SAFETY WARNING â HOT METAL AND FUMES

Always wear leather gloves, safety glasses, and a leather apron when forging. Never quench hot steel in water without eye protection; steam and spitting water can cause severe burns. Waxing produces smoke and fumes â work outdoors or in a well-ventilated area. Keep a bucket of sand, not water, near the forge for fire emergencies. In many African workshops, open fires and hand-cranked blowers are used; ensure the forge is on a non-combustible surface and that children and visitors are kept at a safe distance. Burns are the most common injury in blacksmithing, and most are preventable with basic PPE and discipline.

7. Business Notes for African Smiths

A basic hook is a fast-moving product. In many markets, a 150 mm hook made from 10 mm mild steel sells for the equivalent of US$1.50â4.00, depending on finish and location. The material cost is often less than US$0.50 if stock is bought in bulk or recycled. Labour time is 15â25 minutes for a trained smith. That means a single smith working a six-hour day can produce 15â20 hooks and generate a gross margin of US$15â60 per day, before fuel and overheads. The key business levers are:

  • Standardise sizes: Offer three sizes (small, medium, heavy) so customers can order repeatably.
  • Bundle for resale: Sell packs of 5 or 10 to hardware shops and construction sites.
  • Add value: Twisted or painted hooks command higher prices.
  • Reduce fuel cost: Use a covered forge, dry fuel, and batch production to keep the fire hot.
  • Record costs: Track steel, fuel, and time per hook to know your true profit.
