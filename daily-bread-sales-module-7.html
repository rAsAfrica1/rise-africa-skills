1. Introduction and Why It Matters

As a baker, you already know that bread is a daily need. But if you bake too little, you sell out and lose sales. If you bake too much, you are left with stale bread and wasted money. Daily production planning is the skill of deciding exactly how many loaves, rolls, and pastries to bake each day so that you meet customer demand without waste. This module will teach you how to plan your production based on real numbers from your own bakery, not guesswork.

In Africa, bakeries face unique challenges: power cuts, fluctuating flour prices, and customers who buy based on the day of the week or the weather. A plan that works in Europe may not work in your town. That is why you need to track your own sales and adjust your baking accordingly. Without a plan, you might bake 100 loaves on Monday and sell only 60, losing money on the 40 you throw away. With a plan, you bake 70, sell 68, and keep more profit.

Daily production planning also helps you manage your ingredients. If you know you need 50 loaves tomorrow, you can calculate how much flour, yeast, sugar, and salt to buy. You avoid running out of yeast in the middle of the night or having flour spoil because you bought too much. It also helps you schedule your work: when to start mixing, when to shape, when to bake, and when to open the shop. A good plan reduces stress and increases your income.

This module will guide you through the tools you need, the step-by-step method, common mistakes, safety practices, and a practical exercise. By the end, you will be able to create a daily production plan that fits your bakery, your customers, and your budget. Remember, every loaf you don't waste is money in your pocket. Let's get started.

2. Tools and Materials

To plan your daily production, you don't need expensive software. Simple, low-cost tools work best in most African bakeries. First, you need a sales record book. This can be a simple exercise book or a notebook where you write down every sale each day. Record the date, the item sold (e.g., white bread, brown bread, rolls), the quantity, and the time of sale. If you have a phone, you can use a free app like Google Sheets or a simple notes app, but paper is fine as long as you keep it dry and safe.

Second, you need a production chart. This is a large sheet of paper or a whiteboard where you write your planned production for the next day. It should have columns for each product, the quantity you plan to bake, the actual quantity baked, and the actual quantity sold. At the end of the day, you compare planned vs. actual. This helps you see where you overbaked or underbaked. You can buy a whiteboard from a local shop or use a large piece of cardboard.

Third, you need a calculator. A simple solar-powered calculator is cheap and reliable. You will use it to add up sales, calculate averages, and adjust your plan. If you have a smartphone, the calculator app works too. Fourth, you need a clock or watch to note the time of sales. This helps you understand when your peak hours are. For example, if most sales happen between 6 AM and 8 AM, you know you need bread ready by 5:30 AM.

Fifth, you need a scale to measure ingredients accurately. A digital kitchen scale that measures in grams is best, but a mechanical scale works if it's accurate. Finally, you need a calendar to track days of the week, holidays, and special events. In many African communities, sales are higher on weekends, market days, and during festivals. Mark these on your calendar so you can plan to bake more. All these tools are affordable and can be found in local markets or online. Keep them together in a dedicated 'planning corner' so you don't lose them.

3. Step by Step Method

Step 1: Record your sales for at least two weeks. Every time you sell bread, write it down. Include the date, day of the week, product, quantity, and time. If you sell to wholesalers, note that separately. After two weeks, you will have a clear picture of your daily sales pattern. For example, you might find that you sell 80 loaves on Monday, 90 on Tuesday, 120 on Friday, and 150 on Saturday.

Step 2: Calculate your average daily sales for each product. Add up the total sales for each product over the two weeks and divide by the number of days. For example, if you sold 700 loaves of white bread in 14 days, your average is 50 loaves per day. But don't stop therenote the high and low days. You need to plan for the high days to avoid running out, and reduce on low days to avoid waste.

Step 3: Adjust for the day of the week and special events. If Saturday sales are always 50% higher than the average, plan to bake 50% more on Saturday. If there is a funeral or wedding in town, you might need to bake extra. Check your calendar and talk to your customers to anticipate demand. Also consider the weather: in the rainy season, people may buy less if they can't go out, or more if they want comfort food.

Step 4: Set your production target for tomorrow. Based on your average, day-of-week adjustments, and any special events, decide how many of each product to bake. Write this on your production chart. For example: White bread: 60 loaves; Brown bread: 30 loaves; Rolls: 100 pieces. Be specific.

Step 5: Calculate your ingredient needs. Use your recipes to determine how much flour, yeast, sugar, salt, and other ingredients you need for your target. For example, if one loaf requires 500g of flour, then 60 loaves require 30kg of flour. Check your stock and make a shopping list for anything you are missing.

Step 6: Bake according to plan, but stay flexible. During the day, if you notice sales are higher than expected, you can bake an extra batch if you have time and ingredients. If sales are slow, you might reduce the next day's plan. Always record actual production and sales.

Step 7: Review at the end of the day. Compare planned vs. actual. Ask yourself: Did I sell everything? Did I have leftovers? If leftovers, why? Adjust tomorrow's plan accordingly. Repeat this daily. Over time, your planning will become more accurate, and your profits will grow.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Baking the same amount every day. Many bakers find a number that works once and stick to it forever. But demand changes. Fix: Use your sales records to adjust daily. If Monday is always slow, bake less on Monday. If Friday is busy, bake more.

Mistake 2: Ignoring the weather and seasons. In many parts of Africa, the rainy season can reduce foot traffic, while the dry season may increase it. Fix: Keep a simple weather log. Note how sales change with rain, heat, or cold. Adjust your plan accordingly. For example, on a rainy day, you might bake 20% less.

Mistake 3: Not accounting for holidays and events. A wedding, a funeral, or a national holiday can dramatically change demand. Fix: Use a calendar and ask around. If you know a big event is coming, bake extra. If a holiday means people travel, you might bake less.

Mistake 4: Overbaking to avoid selling out. It's tempting to bake extra so you never disappoint a customer. But unsold bread is wasted money. Fix: Aim to sell out at the end of the day, but not too early. If you sell out by 10 AM, you are losing afternoon sales. If you have leftovers at 6 PM, you baked too much. Find the balance.

Mistake 5: Not tracking sales accurately. If you guess your sales, your plan will be wrong. Fix: Write down every sale immediately. Use a notebook or a phone app. At the end of the day, total it up.

Mistake 6: Forgetting to plan for ingredient availability. If you plan to bake 100 loaves but only have enough flour for 80, you will fall short. Fix: Check your stock before finalizing your plan. Make a shopping list and buy ingredients in advance.

Mistake 7: Not involving your workers. If you have employees, they need to know the plan. Fix: Share the production target with them each evening. Write it on a board where everyone can see it. This reduces confusion and mistakes.

Mistake 8: Being too rigid. Sometimes unexpected things happena power cut, a machine breakdown, or a sudden order. Fix: Build a small buffer into your plan. For example, if you expect to sell 100 loaves, bake 105. That extra 5% covers surprises without creating huge waste.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety in production planning is not just about avoiding physical injuryit's also about food safety, financial safety, and personal well-being. First, food safety: When you plan production, ensure that you have enough time to cool bread properly before packaging. If you rush, you might pack bread while still hot, causing condensation and mold. Plan your baking schedule so that bread has at least 1 hour to cool on racks before bagging. Also, never mix old dough with new dough unless you follow safe fermentation practices. Always use clean utensils and surfaces.

Second, financial safety: Don't over-invest in ingredients for a plan that is too ambitious. If you are unsure, start with a conservative plan and increase gradually. Keep a cash reserve for emergencies like equipment repair or sudden price increases. Record all expenses and income so you know your true profit. A plan that sells out but costs more than it earns is not a good plan.

Third, personal safety: Baking is physically demanding. Plan your day to include breaks. Don't start baking at 2 AM and work until 6 PM without rest. If you have workers, schedule shifts. Use proper lifting techniques when handling heavy bags of flour. Wear closed shoes to protect your feet from hot spills. Keep a first-aid kit nearby.

Fourth, best practices for planning: Always plan the night before. This gives you time to check ingredients and prepare. Use a checklist to ensure you don't forget steps. Communicate the plan to everyone involved. Keep your records clean and organized. Review your plan weekly to spot trends. For example, you might notice that sales are increasing by 5% each week, so you can adjust your baseline.

Fifth, be consistent. A plan is only useful if you follow it and track results. Don't skip recording sales because you are tired. The more data you have, the better your decisions. Also, be willing to learn from mistakes. If you overbaked, don't just throw away the breadconsider turning it into breadcrumbs or croutons to sell, reducing waste. Finally, stay informed about local market conditions. If flour prices are rising, you may need to adjust your selling price or find a cheaper supplier. Planning is not just about numbers; it's about being a smart, safe, and sustainable baker.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it's time to put what you've learned into practice. Follow these steps to create your own daily production plan for tomorrow. You will need your sales records from the past two weeks, a calculator, a notebook, and your recipe book.

Step 1: Gather your sales data. If you don't have two weeks of records, start today. For this exercise, use whatever data you have. Write down the total sales for each product for each day. For example: Monday: 50 white loaves, 20 brown loaves, 80 rolls. Tuesday: 55 white, 25 brown, 90 rolls. And so on.

Step 2: Calculate averages. Add up the total for each product and divide by the number of days. For example, if you sold 700 white loaves in 14 days, the average is 50 per day. Do this for each product.

Step 3: Identify your high and low days. Look at your data. Which day had the highest sales? Which had the lowest? By what percentage do they differ from the average? For example, if Saturday sales are 150% of average, then you need to bake 50% more on Saturday.

Step 4: Check tomorrow's calendar. Is it a weekday or weekend? Any holidays or events? Note anything that might affect demand.

Step 5: Set your production target. Based on your average, day-of-week adjustment, and special events, decide how many of each product to bake. Write it down. For example: Tomorrow is Wednesday. Average white loaf sales are 50. Wednesday is usually average, so bake 50 white loaves. Brown: average 25, bake 25. Rolls: average 80, bake 80. But if you know a school order is coming, add 20 rolls.

Step 6: Calculate ingredients. Using your recipe, calculate how much flour, yeast, sugar, salt, and other ingredients you need. For example, if one white loaf needs 500g flour, 50 loaves need 25kg. Check your stock. Make a shopping list for missing items.

Step 7: Write your plan on a chart. Include columns for product, target quantity, actual baked, actual sold, and notes. Post it where you can see it.

Step 8: Execute and record. Bake according to plan. During the day, record actual sales. At the end of the day, fill in the actual baked and sold columns. Compare with your target.

Step 9: Review and adjust. Ask yourself: Did I meet demand? Did I have leftovers? If yes, why? Adjust tomorrow's plan. For example, if you had 10 leftover white loaves, reduce tomorrow's white target by 10. If you sold out by 10 AM, increase by 10%.

Repeat this exercise daily for one week. By the end, you will have a reliable production plan that reduces waste and increases profit. Good luck!

<strong style="color:#c0392b; font-size:1.1em;"> SAFETY WARNING: Oven and Dough Handling Hazards</strong>
<p style="margin-top:8px;">Bakery ovens operate at 180250C. In many African bakeries, ovens are wood-fired or gas-fired with limited ventilation. <strong>Never</strong> leave a firing oven unattended. Ensure proper chimney ventilation to prevent carbon monoxide buildup this is a silent killer in enclosed baking rooms. When loading or unloading ovens, always use heat-resistant gloves and a long-handled peel. Keep a fire extinguisher (dry powder or CO) within 3 metres of the oven at all times. For dough handling, be aware that prolonged contact with wet dough can cause dermatitis; wash hands thoroughly after handling. If using lye or caustic soda for pretzels or cleaning, store it in a locked cabinet away from food areas and wear eye protection. In case of burns, cool the affected area under running water for at least 20 minutes and seek medical attention immediately.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul style="line-height:1.8;">
<li><strong>Review yesterday's sales sheet</strong> before 6:00 AM note what sold out and what was left over.</li>
<li><strong>Check your flour stock and yeast viability</strong> old yeast means poor proofing and wasted dough.</li>
<li><strong>Calculate dough weight, not loaf count</strong> multiply forecast loaves by dough weight per loaf (e.g., 800g loaf = 800g dough).</li>
<li><strong>Plan bake batches to match oven capacity</strong> never overload a deck or drum oven; it ruins crust and wastes fuel.</li>
<li><strong>Set a production cut-off time</strong> for example, no new dough after 11:00 AM unless you have confirmed afternoon orders.</li>
<li><strong>Record actual production and sales hourly</strong> this is the single most important habit for reducing waste.</li>
<li><strong>Keep 510% of daily output as buffer</strong> for unexpected demand, but never more than 10% to avoid unsold stock.</li>
<li><strong>Review variance at end of day</strong> if you consistently over-bake by more than 5%, reduce tomorrow's forecast.</li>
</ul>
