1. Design and Construction of Ox-Drawn Ploughs

An ox-drawn plough is the backbone of animal-powered farming. You will need mild steel for the frame, a hardened steel share, and a mouldboard. Start with the beam: a straight piece of hardwood or steel pipe, 1.82.2 m long, 810 cm diameter. The beam connects the yoke to the plough body. Next, the handles: two wooden or steel shafts, 1.2 m long, set at a comfortable angle for the operator to walk behind. The body includes the frog (where share and mouldboard meet), the share (the cutting point), and the mouldboard (which turns the soil). For a 20 cm plough, use a share 25 cm long, 5 cm wide at the point, tapering to 10 cm at the base. Hardened steel from old truck leaf springs works well. Heat the share to cherry red (850900C) and quench in oil to harden. The mouldboard can be made from 3 mm mild steel sheet, curved to a radius of about 30 cm. Bolt all parts with 12 mm bolts for easy replacement. The hitch point should be adjustable to set depth: move it up for shallow ploughing, down for deeper. Test in loose soil first. Adjust the landside (a flat piece on the side) to keep the plough straight. A well-built plough should turn a furrow 1520 cm deep and 2530 cm wide with a single ox or a pair of donkeys. Remember to grease the hitch pin regularly.

2. Making Harrows and Cultivators

Harrows and cultivators prepare seedbeds and control weeds. A spike harrow is simplest: a rectangular frame of 5 cm angle iron, 1.5 m wide and 1 m long. Weld or bolt 1216 steel spikes (1 cm diameter, 15 cm long) in staggered rows. The spikes should angle back slightly to avoid clogging. For a disc harrow, use old car wheel hubs and discs (e.g., from a disc plough) mounted on a shaft. Space discs 20 cm apart, angled 1520 degrees to cut and turn soil. A cultivator (or tined cultivator) has curved tines made from 1.5 cm spring steel. Bend tines to a sweep shape: 30 cm long, curving forward. Mount 57 tines on a toolbar with clamps. For row crop cultivation, adjust tine spacing to match your crop rows (e.g., 75 cm for maize). Use a depth wheel to control how deep tines goset it to 1015 cm for weeding. Build a hitch that allows the implement to follow the oxen without swinging. All joints should be welded or bolted with 10 mm bolts. Paint with red oxide primer then enamel to prevent rust. Test on dry soil first: if tines bounce, add weight (a stone or metal block) on the frame. A good harrow leaves a level, fine seedbed; a cultivator uproots weeds without burying small crops.

3. Crafting Yokes and Harnesses

A yoke transfers the animal's pulling power to the implement. For oxen, use a neck yoke: a straight wooden bar 1.21.5 m long, 1012 cm diameter, made from tough wood like mukwa or mvule. Shape it with an axe and smooth with a spokeshave. The yoke sits across the necks of a pair of oxen. Carve two U-shaped bows (also wood) that go under the neck and attach to the yoke with wooden pegs or metal pins. The bows should be loose enough to allow the animal to breathe but tight enough not to slip. For donkeys, use a breast collar harness: a padded leather or canvas strap that goes across the chest, attached to traces (ropes or chains) that pull the implement. Make the collar from 5 cm wide leather, stuffed with straw or foam for comfort. The traces should be 34 m long, made from 8 mm wire rope or heavy chain. For a single animal, use a swingletree (a wooden bar) to balance the pull. All harness parts must be smooth to prevent chafing. Check daily for wear. For yokes, drill holes for pegs with a 12 mm bit. Treat wood with used engine oil to repel termites. A well-fitted yoke lets an ox pull 500800 kg; a poor one causes sores and reduces power by half.

4. Making Cart and Wagon Parts

Carts and wagons need strong wheels, axles, and a sturdy body. For a two-wheel cart, start with the axle: a 5 cm solid steel rod, 1.5 m long. Weld a hub (10 cm diameter pipe, 15 cm long) at each end. The wheels can be wooden spoked or steel disc. For wooden wheels, use 8 spokes of 4 cm hardwood, a felloe (rim) of 1.2 m diameter, and an iron tyre shrunk on. Heat the iron tyre to 300C and drop it over the rim; it shrinks tight as it cools. For steel wheels, cut two discs from 6 mm plate, 1 m diameter, and weld a 10 cm pipe hub in the centre. The cart body: a platform 2 m long, 1.2 m wide, made from 5 cm planks or steel sheet. Add sides 30 cm high for carrying loads. The shafts (for hitching to animals) should be 2.5 m long, 8 cm diameter, attached to the axle with U-bolts. Use a turntable for a four-wheel wagon: a circle of steel with a kingpin, allowing the front axle to pivot. Load capacity: a two-wheel cart with 1 m wheels can carry 500800 kg on flat ground. Grease wheel bearings every 50 km. Check all bolts weekly. Replace worn bushes in the hubs with bronze or nylon.

5. Repairing Animal-Drawn Equipment

Breakdowns happen. Learn to fix common problems fast. For ploughs: a dull share is the most common issue. Remove it, heat to 850C, and re-shape with a hammer on an anvil. Quench in oil. If the share is cracked, weld it with a 3.2 mm electrode, then grind smooth. For harrows: bent tines can be straightened cold with a heavy hammer, but if cracked, replace. Keep spare tines. For yokes: cracked wood can be repaired with a steel plate and bolts, but if the crack is deep, replace the yoke. For carts: loose wheel bearings cause wobble. Remove the wheel, knock out the old bearing, and press in a new one (use a socket and hammer). If the axle is bent, heat it red and straighten on a flat surface. For harnesses: replace worn leather straps with new ones; stitch with waxed thread and a saddler's needle. Always carry a repair kit: spare bolts (8, 10, 12 mm), wire, pliers, a file, and a hacksaw. Before each use, inspect for cracks, loose bolts, and worn parts. Fix small problems before they become big. A 30-minute repair in the evening saves a day of lost work. Teach yourself to weld if you canmany villages have a welder who can help.

6. Safety and Ergonomics in Animal-Drawn Tools

Animal-drawn tools can injure you or your animals if used carelessly. Always wear sturdy boots, long trousers, and gloves when handling implements. When hitching, stand to the side, never between the animal and the implement. Use a halter and lead rope to control the animal; never wrap ropes around your handif the animal bolts, you could lose a finger. Keep blades sharp: a dull share requires more force, tiring both you and the animal. For ergonomics, adjust handles to your height: when standing, your elbows should be slightly bent. The plough handles should be at hip level. If you must bend, you will get back pain. For the animal, ensure the yoke or collar fits wellcheck for sores daily. Pad collars with cloth if needed. Work at a steady pace; oxen walk at 11.5 m/s. Take breaks every hour. When lifting heavy parts (wheels, plough bodies), bend your knees, not your back. Use a cart or wheelbarrow to move heavy items. Keep your work area clear of tools and rocks. Store implements under cover to prevent rust and damage. Teach children to stay away from animals and moving equipment. A safe farmer is a productive farmer.

<h2>Practical Reference: Animal-Drawn Implement Making</h2>

<p>This Practical Reference is the working bench companion for the module <strong>Animal-Drawn Implement Making</strong>. It is written for artisans, small-scale manufacturers, and agripreneurs who build, sell, repair, and hire out ox-drawn equipment across Africa. The focus is business-first: every measurement, joint, and material choice below is framed around what makes a tool stronger, cheaper to produce, easier to repair, and more sellable to farmers. Whether you are welding ploughs in a roadside workshop in Mbeya or assembling harrows for a cooperative in Kaduna, the principles are the samebuild for the soil, the animal, and the customer's wallet.</p>

<p>Animal traction remains the backbone of smallholder cultivation across sub-Saharan Africa. According to FAO, animal-drawn implements are used on a significant share of cultivated land in countries such as Ethiopia, Tanzania, Kenya, Mali, and Zimbabwe, and demand for locally manufactured tools continues to grow as farmers seek affordable alternatives to tractors. For the artisan, this is a market opportunity: a well-made ox plough that can be repaired locally will outsell an imported unit that cannot.</p>

<h2>Core Design Principles for All Implements</h2>

<p>Before looking at individual tools, internalise these five principles. They apply to ploughs, harrows, cultivators, yokes, and carts alike.</p>

<p><strong>1. Match the tool to the soil and the animal.</strong> A light sandy soil needs a different plough body than heavy clay. A pair of oxen weighing 500700 kg can pull a plough with a 2025 cm furrow width; donkeys need lighter, narrower implements. Overloading animals reduces work output and shortens their working lifea direct cost to your customer.</p>

<p><strong>2. Build for local repair.</strong> Use standard bolts, flat bar, and angle iron that a village welder can replace. Avoid proprietary castings that must be imported. A tool that can be fixed in a day is worth more than one that sits idle for a month.</p>

<p><strong>3. Standardise your parts.</strong> If you produce ten ploughs a month, use the same beam length, the same share pattern, and the same yoke dimensions. Standardisation cuts production time, reduces waste, and lets you sell spare partsa high-margin side business.</p>

<p><strong>4. Control weight.</strong> Every kilogram of implement weight is a kilogram the animal must pull. Use the lightest section that gives the required strength. A typical ox plough beam is 1.21.5 m long and made from 50 50 mm hardwood or 40 40 mm steel box section.</p>

<p><strong>5. Design for the operator.</strong> Handles must be at a comfortable height (about 90110 cm for an adult), with grips that do not blister hands. Ergonomics is not a luxuryit determines whether a farmer can work a full day and whether they recommend your tool to neighbours.</p>

<h2>Design and Construction of Ox-Drawn Ploughs</h2>

<p>The ox plough is the highest-volume animal-drawn implement in most African markets. The standard mouldboard plough consists of a beam, a body (mouldboard and share), two handles, and a hitch point. Key dimensions for a medium ox plough:</p>

<ul>
<li>Beam length: 1.21.5 m, hardwood (e.g., <em>Pterocarpus</em> or <em>Brachystegia</em>) or steel box section</li>
<li>Share width: 2025 cm for oxen; 1218 cm for donkeys</li>
<li>Mouldboard: curved steel plate, 35 mm thick, radius matched to soil type</li>
<li>Handles: 90110 cm above ground, spaced 4050 cm apart</li>
<li>Hitch: adjustable vertical and horizontal clevis for depth and line of pull control</li>
</ul>

<p>The share is the wear partmake it replaceable. A bolt-on share that a farmer can swap in the field is a strong selling point. Harden the cutting edge by quenching in used engine oil, or source recycled spring steel from vehicle leaf springs, which is widely available and holds an edge well.</p>

<h2>Making Harrows and Cultivators</h2>

<p>Harrows break clods and level seedbeds; cultivators weed between rows. Both are lighter than ploughs and use tines or discs mounted on a frame. A typical ox-drawn spike-tooth harrow uses a 1.52.0 m hardwood or steel frame with 915 tines set at 1520 cm spacing. Cultivators use 35 sweeps or tines mounted on a toolbar, with adjustable spacing to match row width (commonly 6090 cm for maize).</p>

<p>Business tip: harrows and cultivators share the same frame and hitch system as your plough. If you standardise the hitch, a farmer can buy one yoke and hitch assembly and use it across three implements. That increases your average sale value per customer.</p>

<h2>Crafting Yokes and Harnesses</h2>

<p>The yoke transfers the animal's power to the implement. A poorly fitted yoke causes sores, reduces pulling efficiency, and loses you customers. The head yoke (neck yoke) is common for oxen in southern and eastern Africa; the withers yoke is used in West Africa. Key specification: the yoke must sit comfortably on the neck or withers without pinching, and the eye spacing must match the animal pair.</p>

<p>Use seasoned hardwood (e.g., <em>Acacia</em>, <em>Combretum</em>) or welded steel. Pad contact points with leather or canvas. Harness chains and traces should be rated well above the expected pulla pair of oxen can exert 1.52.5 kN of draught force, so use chain with a safe working load of at least 5 kN.</p>

<h2>Making Cart and Wagon Parts</h2>

<p>Ox carts are a high-value product for transport of harvest, water, and inputs. Key parts to manufacture locally:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Axle and bearings:</strong> use sealed roller bearings where affordable; otherwise hardwood bushes with grease nipples. A 1.52 tonne cart needs an axle of at least 40 mm solid steel.</li>
<li><strong>Wheels:</strong> steel rims with pneumatic tyres reduce draught on rough ground. Wooden spoked wheels are cheaper but need regular maintenance.</li>
<li><strong>Shafts and singletree:</strong> shafts 2.02.4 m long, angled so the cart balances with the load centred over the axle.</li>
<li><strong>Body:</strong> hardwood planks or welded steel frame with a wooden deck. Keep the centre of gravity low to prevent overturning.</li>
</ul>

<p>Carts are a strong business line because they can be sold or rented. A well-built cart that can carry 1.5 tonnes and be repaired locally will generate hire income for years.</p>

<h2>Repairing Animal-Drawn Equipment</h2>

<p>Repair is recurring revenue. Build a repair kit and a simple diagnostic routine:</p>

<ol>
<li>Inspect the share and mouldboard for wear; replace or re-edge.</li>
<li>Check beam for cracks, especially at the body joint; reinforce with steel plate if needed.</li>
<li>Check yoke and harness for cracks, worn padding, and stretched chain.</li>
<li>Check all bolts and pins; replace with galvanised or stainless where possible.</li>
<li>Check wheel bearings and axle play on carts; repack or replace bearings.</li>
<li>Test the tool in the field before returning it to the customer.</li>
</ol>

<p>Offer a service contract: annual inspection and share replacement for a fixed fee. This builds loyalty and smooths your cash flow.</p>

<h2>Safety and Ergonomics in Animal-Drawn Tools</h2>

<p>Animal traction injuries are common and preventable. The main risks are cuts from shares and discs, crushing from overturning carts, and back and shoulder strain from poor handle design. Train your customers on safe use, and build safety into the tool.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #c0392b; background:#fdecea; padding:14px 18px; margin:18px 0; border-radius:6px;">
<strong style="color:#c0392b;"> SAFETY WARNING Sharp Edges and Animal Handling</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#7b241c;">Shares, discs, and cultivator tines are sharp and can cause severe lacerations. Always fit transport guards or wrap blades during storage and transport. Never allow children to handle or hitch animals unattended. When hitching, keep hands clear of the yoke eye and traces; use a quick-release hitch so the animal can be freed if it panics. Wear closed shoes and eye protection when sharpening or welding. Ensure animals are properly trained and handled by an adult at all times. A panicked ox can overturn a cart and cause fatal crushing injuries.</p>
</div>

<p>Ergonomic improvements that cost little but add value: rubber or leather handle grips, adjustable handle height, balanced tool weight, and a hitch that lets the operator walk to the side rather than directly behind the animal. These reduce fatigue and injury, and they are selling points you can demonstrate to buyers.</p>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>

<ul>
<li> Confirm the target animal (ox, donkey, cow) and soil type before designing.</li>
<li> Use standard, locally available materials and bolt sizes for easy repair.</li>
<li> Make the share replaceable and harden the cutting edge.</li>
<li> Standardise beam, hitch, and yoke dimensions across your product range.</li>
<li> Fit padded, well-fitted yokes and quick-release hitches.</li>
<li> Add handle grips and check handle height for operator comfort.</li>
<li> Field-test every implement before delivery and train the buyer on safe use.</li>
<li> Offer a repair and spare-parts service to build recurring revenue.</li>
</ul>
