1. Selecting Breeding Stock

Selecting good breeding stock is the foundation of a profitable grasscutter farm. Start by choosing animals from proven lines. A good breeding female (doe) should be at least 6-8 months old and weigh 2.5-3.5 kg. Males (bucks) should be 8-10 months old and weigh 3.5-5 kg. Never breed animals that are too young or too old; females over 3 years often have smaller litters and more kindling problems.

Look for physical signs of health: bright eyes, clean nose, smooth fur, and no wounds or abscesses. Avoid animals that sneeze, have diarrhoea, or show lameness. Check the teeth; overgrown incisors can prevent eating. For females, examine the vulva: it should be clean and pink, not swollen or discharging. For males, ensure both testicles are descended and firm.

Temperament matters. Choose calm animals that do not bite or panic when handled. Aggressive grasscutters are hard to manage and may injure mates or kits. If possible, observe the parents and siblings. Fast growth, large litters (3-5 kits per kindling), and good mothering are inherited traits. Buy from a reputable breeder who keeps records.

In Africa, many farmers start with 1 male and 3-4 females. Select from different families to avoid inbreeding. If you cannot find unrelated stock, exchange males with a neighbour every 12-18 months. Always quarantine new animals for 2-3 weeks before mixing them with your herd. During quarantine, treat for external parasites with ivermectin (0.2 mg/kg body weight) and watch for signs of disease.

Finally, keep a simple record for each breeding animal: ID number, date of birth, weight, and parentage. This helps you track performance and make better culling decisions. Remember, good breeding stock is an investment; do not compromise on quality to save money.

2. Mating Systems and Ratios

Grasscutters can be bred using different mating systems. The most common for small-scale farmers is the harem system: one male with 3-5 females in a colony cage. This mimics their natural social structure and reduces fighting. The ratio of 1:4 is ideal; too many females per male leads to infertility, while too few causes the male to harass the female. For large farms, you can use a rotating system: move the male to a new group of females every 2-3 weeks. This prevents inbreeding and spreads his genetics.

Never keep two adult males together; they will fight, often to the death. If you must introduce a new male, do it gradually. Place cages side by side for a week, then swap bedding, and finally introduce them in a neutral cage with plenty of hiding spots. Monitor closely for 48 hours.

Mating can happen year-round in warm climates, but in areas with distinct dry and rainy seasons, grasscutters often breed more during the rains when food is abundant. Provide a diet rich in protein (16-18% crude protein) and energy to support reproduction. Supplements like palm kernel cake, soya bean meal, and fresh grasses help.

Detect mating by observing behaviour: the male will sniff the female's genital area, make a purring sound, and mount. After mating, a copulatory plug may be seen. For reliable results, keep a breeding calendar. If a female does not conceive after 2-3 months with a proven male, replace her.

In Africa, many farmers use the 'colony' system where several females share a large pen with one male. This works well if you provide enough space (at least 0.5 m per animal) and nest boxes. Overcrowding causes stress, abortions, and cannibalism. Always separate pregnant females into individual cages about 1 week before kindling. This prevents injuries to newborns and allows you to monitor the mother and kits closely.

3. Gestation and Kindling Management

The gestation period of a grasscutter is about 150-160 days (roughly 5 months). This is long compared to rabbits or guinea pigs, so accurate record keeping is essential. After mating, note the date. You can confirm pregnancy after 6-8 weeks by gentle abdominal palpation: feel for marble-sized lumps. Do this carefully to avoid injury. A pregnant female's belly will become round and she may eat more.

As kindling approaches, around day 140, prepare a nest box. Use a wooden box (30x30x30 cm) with a small entrance. Line it with dry, soft grass or wood shavings. The female will carry nesting material in her mouth to build a nest. Provide extra bedding. Reduce disturbances; stress can cause abortion or the mother to abandon her kits.

Kindling usually happens at night and lasts 1-2 hours. The female gives birth to 1-5 kits, average 3. Each kit weighs 80-120 g and is born with eyes open, fur, and able to move. The mother cleans them and they start nursing within an hour. Do not disturb the nest for the first 24 hours. If you must check, do it quietly and briefly. Remove any dead kits immediately.

After kindling, check the nest for kits that are cold or not nursing. If a kit is rejected, you can try to foster it to another lactating female. To do this, rub the kit with the foster mother's urine or bedding to mask the scent. If fostering fails, hand-feed with warm goat milk using a syringe every 2-3 hours.

Common problems include stillbirths, which can be caused by poor nutrition, stress, or disease. Ensure the pregnant female gets a balanced diet with plenty of fresh water. Keep the cage clean to prevent infections. If a female has difficulty kindling (dystocia), consult a vet; do not pull kits unless you know what you are doing. After kindling, give the mother a quiet space and plenty of food and water to support milk production.

4. Care of Pregnant and Lactating Females

Pregnant and lactating females have higher nutritional needs. During the last month of pregnancy, increase protein to 18-20% and provide extra energy. A good diet includes fresh grasses (elephant grass, guinea grass), legumes (moringa, leucaena), and concentrates like maize bran, palm kernel cake, and fish meal. Provide clean water at all times; a lactating female drinks up to 0.5 litres per day. Without enough water, milk production drops and kits may die.

Handle pregnant females gently. Avoid picking them up by the tail or belly. If you must move them, use a small box or net. Keep them in individual cages from the last week of pregnancy until kits are weaned. This prevents aggression from other adults and allows you to monitor feeding and health. The cage should be clean, dry, and free from draughts. Provide a nest box with soft bedding.

After kindling, the female needs peace and quiet. Do not clean the cage for the first 3 days unless absolutely necessary. The mother may eat the afterbirth, which is normal and provides nutrients. Check the kits daily without disturbing the nest. If the mother is nervous, she may cannibalise her young; this is often due to stress, hunger, or lack of water. Ensure she has enough food and water, and keep other animals away.

Lactation lasts 4-6 weeks. During this time, feed the mother a high-quality diet. You can give her extra greens and a handful of groundnut cake or soya bean meal. Some farmers give warm water with a little molasses to boost energy. Watch for signs of mastitis (swollen, hot, painful teats). If you see this, consult a vet; do not let kits nurse on infected teats.

After weaning, give the female a rest period of 2-4 weeks before re-mating. This allows her body to recover. If she is too thin, wait longer. A good body condition means you can feel her ribs but not see them. Keep records of each female's kindling dates, litter size, and weaning rate. This helps you identify poor mothers and cull them. With proper care, a healthy female can produce 2-3 litters per year.

5. Neonatal Care and Weaning

Newborn grasscutters (kits) are precocial: they are born with fur, open eyes, and can move around within hours. However, they still depend on mother's milk for the first few weeks. For the first 3 days, the kits stay in the nest, nursing frequently. The mother's colostrum (first milk) provides antibodies that protect against disease. Do not disturb the nest during this time.

By day 4-7, kits start nibbling on solid food. Provide soft, fresh greens like young elephant grass or sweet potato leaves. Also offer a shallow dish of water; kits may drown in deep containers. Use a small lid or a chick waterer. Keep the nest box clean; remove soiled bedding every few days, but leave some of the old bedding to maintain familiar scent.

Weaning usually occurs at 4-6 weeks, when kits weigh 400-600 g. You can wean earlier if the mother is thin or if kits are eating solid food well. To wean, simply separate the kits from the mother and place them in a grow-out cage. Do this gradually: remove the mother for short periods first, then permanently. Expect some stress; kits may cry for a day or two. Provide plenty of food and water, and keep the environment calm.

After weaning, feed kits a high-protein diet (16-18% CP) to support rapid growth. A mix of grasses, legumes, and concentrates works well. You can also give them a little milk replacer for the first week if they are not eating enough. Weigh kits weekly to monitor growth. Healthy kits gain 50-100 g per week. If a kit is not growing, check for parasites or disease.

Common problems in neonates include diarrhoea (often from dirty water or spoiled food), pneumonia (from damp, cold cages), and cannibalism by the mother. To prevent these, keep cages clean, dry, and well-ventilated. Avoid overcrowding. If a kit dies, remove it immediately and check the others. Record all births, deaths, and weaning weights. This data helps you improve management. Remember, healthy kits are the future of your farm; give them the best start possible.

6. Record Keeping for Breeding

Record keeping is essential for successful grasscutter breeding. Without records, you cannot know which females are productive, which males are infertile, or when to expect kindling. Use a simple notebook or a spreadsheet on your phone. For each animal, assign a unique ID number (e.g., F001 for female, M001 for male). Write it on an ear tag or use a marker on the cage.

Keep a breeding card for each female. Record: date of birth, date of mating, date of kindling, number of kits born (live and dead), number weaned, and any health issues. Also note the male's ID. This helps you calculate gestation length and kindling intervals. A good female should kindle every 5-6 months. If a female fails to conceive after 3 months with a proven male, cull her.

For males, record mating dates and which females they served. If a male is used for more than 2 years, his fertility may decline; replace him with a younger male. Keep a separate record for each cage: cleaning schedule, feeding, and treatments. This helps you spot problems early.

Use a calendar to mark important dates: expected kindling, weaning, and vaccination. For example, if a female mated on 1 January, expect kindling around 1 June (150 days later). Mark it. Check the nest box from day 145. This prevents surprises and allows you to prepare.

At the end of each year, review your records. Calculate average litter size, weaning rate, and mortality. Identify your best performing females and males. Keep their offspring as replacement stock. Cull poor performers. This selective breeding improves your herd over time. In Africa, many farmers use simple wall charts or chalkboards to track breeding. Whatever method you choose, be consistent. Accurate records turn a hobby into a profitable business.

<h2>Practical Reference: Breeding and Reproduction in Grasscutter (Greater Cane Rat) Farming</h2>

<p>Breeding is the profit engine of any grasscutter (<em>Thryonomys swinderianus</em>) enterprise. Unlike poultry or rabbits, grasscutters are slow breeders with small litters, long gestations, and high neonatal mortality when management is poor. In West and Central Africa, where demand for cane rat meat (often called "akrantie" in Ghana, "agouti" in francophone markets, or "grasscutter" across Nigeria) consistently outstrips supply, the farms that win are those that master reproduction economics: fewer lost litters, shorter inter-kindling intervals, and heavier weaners per female per year.</p>

<p>This Practical Reference consolidates the key operating numbers, timelines, and husbandry actions you need to run a breeding unit profitably. Use it alongside the other lessons in this module as a field-side decision tool.</p>

<h3>1. Selecting Breeding Stock</h3>
<p>Select from proven lines, not from the market cage. Look for does with: a straight back, deep belly, well-spaced teats (812), calm temperament, no history of litter loss, and a body weight of at least 2.53 kg at first mating. Males should be 34 kg, with both testicles descended, clean eyes, and a firm, alert posture. Avoid animals with visible lameness, chronic diarrhoea, or respiratory noise these are culls, not breeders.</p>

<p>In practice, buy foundation stock from a recognised breeder or a research station (e.g., CSIR-Animal Research Institute in Ghana, or university cane rat units in Nigeria and Benin). Record the dam and sire where possible this is your first data point for selection.</p>

<h3>2. Mating Systems and Ratios</h3>
<p>Grasscutters are not naturally pair-bonded in captivity, and group housing can trigger fighting. The two workable systems are:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Harem (colony) system:</strong> 1 male to 46 females in a well-structured pen with hiding places. Best for space efficiency and continuous breeding.</li>
<li><strong>Pair system:</strong> 1 male to 1 female. Lower risk of injury, easier record keeping, but higher housing cost per pup produced.</li>
</ul>
<p>Introduce animals at dusk, in a clean pen with fresh browse (e.g., <em>Panicum maximum</em>, sweet potato vines, sugarcane). Watch for 3045 minutes. If fighting occurs, separate and retry after 35 days. Never introduce a new male to a group with a resident male this is a common and costly mistake.</p>

<h3>3. Gestation and Kindling Management</h3>
<p>Gestation in grasscutters runs approximately 150155 days. Because pregnancy is hard to confirm visually in early stages, use body weight tracking and abdominal palpation from day 60. From day 120, move the doe to a quiet kindling pen bedded with dry grass or wood shavings. Provide a nest box or a covered corner.</p>

<p>Kindling is usually quick (3060 minutes) and typically happens at night. Do not disturb the doe for the first 2448 hours. Pups are precocial born with fur, eyes open, and able to move within hours. Litter size is small: 14 pups, with 23 being typical.</p>

<h3>4. Care of Pregnant and Lactating Females</h3>
<p>Nutrition is the single biggest lever on litter survival. Increase energy and protein from mid-gestation. Lactating does need roughly 1820% crude protein and continuous access to fresh green forage plus a concentrate supplement (e.g., maize bran, soybean meal, palm kernel cake, and a mineral premix).</p>

<p>Key actions:</p>
<ul>
<li>Provide clean water daily lactating does drink heavily.</li>
<li>Minimise handling and noise in the last 3 weeks of gestation.</li>
<li>Check the nest daily but do not touch pups unless necessary.</li>
<li>Watch for mastitis (hot, swollen mammary tissue) and metritis (bloody discharge) isolate and treat promptly.</li>
</ul>

<h3>5. Neonatal Care and Weaning</h3>
<p>Pups begin nibbling solid food from about day 1014 and are usually weaned between 5 and 7 weeks, or when they reach 500700 g. Weaning too early causes stunting; too late stresses the doe and delays the next mating. After weaning, separate males from females to prevent early, unplanned pregnancies.</p>

<p>Provide weaners with finely chopped forage and a starter mash. Keep group sizes small (46 per pen) to reduce stress and fighting.</p>

<h3>6. Record Keeping for Breeding</h3>
<p>Without records, you cannot select, cull, or price accurately. At minimum, keep a card per breeding animal and a pen-level log. The two tables below give the core data you must capture and the benchmark performance you should target.</p>

<strong style="color:#c0392b;"> SAFETY WARNING Handle breeding animals with care</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Grasscutters have powerful incisors and can inflict serious bites when cornered, handled roughly, or disturbed during kindling. Always:</p>
<ul style="margin:8px 0 0 18px;">
<li>Wear thick leather gloves when handling adults or moving animals between pens.</li>
<li>Never reach into a nest box with newborn pups while the doe is present she may attack.</li>
<li>Use a catch-net or a small carrying cage rather than bare hands.</li>
<li>Disinfect any bite wound immediately and seek medical attention rodent bites can transmit infections.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Select proven breeders:</strong> does 2.5 kg, males 3 kg, calm, no history of litter loss.</li>
<li><strong>Maintain correct ratio:</strong> 1 male to 46 females (harem) or 1:1 (pair).</li>
<li><strong>Track mating dates</strong> so you can predict kindling within a 5-day window.</li>
<li><strong>Move pregnant does to quiet kindling pens</strong> by day 120 of gestation.</li>
<li><strong>Do not disturb the nest</strong> for the first 2448 hours after kindling.</li>
<li><strong>Wean at 57 weeks</strong> or at 500700 g, and separate males from females immediately.</li>
<li><strong>Record every mating, kindling, litter size, and weaning weight</strong> no exceptions.</li>
<li><strong>Review benchmarks monthly</strong> and cull does that fall below target for two consecutive litters.</li>
</ol>
