1. Chapati: The Flaky Method

Chapati is the backbone of many African street food stalls. To get flaky layers, start with 500g all-purpose flour, 1 tsp salt, and 2 tbsp oil or ghee. Mix, then add warm water (about 250ml) gradually until a soft dough forms. Knead for 10 minutes until smooth and elastic. Cover and rest for 30 minutes. This resting step is crucialit relaxes the gluten so you can roll thin without tearing.

Divide dough into 8 balls. On a floured surface, roll each ball into a thin circle about 2mm thick. Brush lightly with oil, then dust with flour. Cut a slit from the center to the edge, then roll the circle into a cone. Press the cone down into a ball again. This creates layers. Roll out again to 2mm thick. Don't over-roll or layers will merge.

Heat a heavy pan or tawa over medium-high heat. Place chapati on dry pan. When bubbles appear (about 30 seconds), flip. Brush the top with oil, flip again, and press gently with a spatula. The chapati should puff up. Cook until golden brown spots appear on both sides. Each chapati takes 1-2 minutes.

For street sales, stack chapatis in a clean cloth-lined cooler to keep soft. Sell plain, or with fillings. Cost: 500g flour makes 8 chapatis. In Nairobi, flour costs about 60 KES, oil 20 KES, so each chapati costs roughly 10 KES. Sell at 20-30 KES each. You can also pre-cook and reheat on a griddle. Practice until you can roll and cook one in under 3 minutes. Consistency is keycustomers come back for the same flaky texture every time.

2. Layered and Stuffed Chapati

Layered chapati takes the flaky method further. After rolling your dough into a thin circle, brush generously with ghee or oil, then sprinkle with a mix of flour and a pinch of salt. Roll into a tight log, then coil the log into a spiral. Press down and roll gently to 1cm thick. This creates many layers that separate when cooked. Cook on medium heat, flipping and pressing, until golden. The layers should pull apart easily.

For stuffed chapati, you need a filling that holds together. Common fillings: mashed potatoes with peas and spices, minced meat cooked with onions and curry powder, or grated carrots and cabbage. Make sure filling is cool and drywet filling tears the dough. Roll a dough ball into a 10cm circle, place 2 tbsp filling in center, bring edges together and pinch to seal. Flatten gently, then roll to 5mm thick. Cook on hot pan with a little oil until both sides are golden and filling is hot.

In West Africa, 'chapati' may be called 'flatbread' but the technique is similar. In East Africa, stuffed chapati is often sold as 'chapati wrap' with a filling rolled inside. For street sales, keep fillings in separate containers and assemble on order. This prevents sogginess. You can also pre-make stuffed chapatis and reheat on a griddle.

Costing: For 8 stuffed chapatis, dough costs 80 KES. Filling: 200g minced meat (150 KES), 2 potatoes (30 KES), spices (10 KES). Total 270 KES. Sell at 60-80 KES each. Profit margin is good if you buy ingredients in bulk. Practice sealing so filling doesn't leak. A common mistake is overfillinguse only 2 tbsp per chapati. Also, don't roll too hard after stuffing or filling bursts out. Cook on medium heat, not high, so filling heats through without burning the crust.

3. Meat and Vegetable Samosas

Samosas are triangular pastries with a crispy shell and savory filling. For the dough: 250g all-purpose flour, 1/2 tsp salt, 2 tbsp oil, and about 100ml water. Mix to a stiff dough, knead 5 minutes, cover and rest 30 minutes. Roll into thin circles (1mm), cut each circle into 4 triangles. Keep covered to prevent drying.

For meat filling: 250g minced beef or chicken, 1 onion (chopped), 2 cloves garlic, 1 tsp ginger, 1 tsp curry powder, 1/2 tsp turmeric, salt, and 1/2 cup frozen peas. Cook meat with onions until browned, add spices and peas, cook 5 minutes. Cool completely. For vegetable filling: 2 potatoes (boiled and mashed), 1 cup cabbage (shredded), 1 carrot (grated), 1 tsp cumin, 1 tsp curry powder, salt. Mix well.

To fold: Take one triangle, brush edges with water. Place 1 tsp filling in center. Fold one corner over filling to opposite corner, then fold the remaining corner to seal into a triangle. Press edges with a fork. Heat oil to 170C. Fry samosas in batches for 3-4 minutes until golden brown. Drain on paper towels.

For street sales, make 50-100 samosas at a time. They can be frozen before fryingfreeze on a tray, then bag. Fry from frozen at 160C for 5-6 minutes. Cost: Dough for 20 samosas costs 30 KES. Meat filling for 20 costs 200 KES. Total 230 KES, or 11.5 KES each. Sell at 30-50 KES each. Vegetable samosas cost less: 150 KES for 20, or 7.5 KES each. Sell at 20-30 KES. Serve with chutney or hot sauce. Keep oil cleanstrain after each batch. Samosas are best sold hot and fresh. If you must hold them, keep in a warm oven (60C) for up to 1 hour.

4. Meat Pies and Sausage Rolls

Meat pies and sausage rolls use a flaky pastry similar to samosa dough but richer. For pastry: 500g flour, 250g cold butter or margarine, 1 tsp salt, 1 egg, and 100ml cold water. Cut butter into flour until pea-sized lumps. Add egg and water, mix gently until dough forms. Do not overwork. Chill 1 hour.

For meat pie filling: 500g minced beef, 1 onion, 2 potatoes (diced small), 1 carrot (diced), 2 tbsp curry powder, salt, pepper, 1 cup beef stock. Cook meat and onions, add vegetables and spices, then stock. Simmer until thick, about 15 minutes. Cool completely. For sausage rolls: 500g sausage meat or minced pork, 1 onion (grated), 1 tsp sage, salt, pepper. Mix well.

To assemble pies: Roll pastry to 3mm thick. Cut circles (10cm for small pies). Place 2 tbsp filling on one half, fold over, crimp edges with fork. Brush with egg wash. Bake at 200C for 20-25 minutes until golden. For sausage rolls: Roll pastry into rectangle, place sausage meat in a log, roll up, cut into 5cm pieces. Bake at 200C for 20 minutes.

For street sales, meat pies and sausage rolls are popular in West and Southern Africa. They can be baked in advance and sold at room temperature or warmed. Cost: Pastry for 12 pies costs 200 KES. Filling: 500g mince (300 KES), vegetables (50 KES), spices (20 KES). Total 570 KES, or 47.5 KES per pie. Sell at 100-150 KES. Sausage rolls: pastry 200 KES, sausage meat 300 KES, total 500 KES for 15 rolls, or 33 KES each. Sell at 80-100 KES. Package in paper bags or cling film. For parties, offer mini versions. Always label with date if selling later. Reheat in oven, not microwave, to keep pastry crisp.

5. Savoury Doughnuts and Mandazi Variants

Mandazi are East African doughnuts, slightly sweet and spiced. For basic mandazi: 500g flour, 1/2 cup sugar, 2 tsp baking powder, 1/2 tsp salt, 1 tsp ground cardamom, 1 egg, 1 cup coconut milk (or fresh milk), 2 tbsp melted butter. Mix dry ingredients, then add wet. Knead to a soft dough. Rest 30 minutes.

For savoury doughnuts, reduce sugar to 2 tbsp and add 1 tsp curry powder, 1/2 tsp turmeric, and 1 grated onion. This gives a savory version often sold with chutney. Roll dough to 1cm thick, cut into triangles or circles. Fry in oil at 170C until golden, about 2 minutes per side. Drain.

Variants: Add mashed banana for banana mandazi. Add grated coconut for coconut mandazi. For 'mahamri' (Swahili coconut doughnut), use coconut milk and cardamom. For 'puff-puff' (West African), use a wetter dough and drop by spoonfuls into oil. For 'vetkoek' (South African), make a plain dough and fry as a round ball, then split and fill with mince.

For street sales, mandazi are best sold warm. They cost very little: 500g flour (60 KES), sugar (20 KES), milk (30 KES), egg (15 KES), spices (10 KES). Total 135 KES for about 20 mandazi, or 7 KES each. Sell at 20-30 KES each. Savoury doughnuts cost similar. Package in paper bags. Sell with chutney or hot sauce. For events, make mini mandazi and sell in cups. Fry in clean oilstrain daily. You can also bake mandazi at 180C for 15 minutes for a healthier option, but frying gives the traditional taste. Experiment with fillings: jam, chocolate, or savory mince. Always test a small batch before selling to check taste and texture.

6. Sauces and Chutneys for Dipping

No street pastry is complete without a dipping sauce. Start with a basic tomato chutney: 4 ripe tomatoes (chopped), 1 onion (chopped), 2 cloves garlic, 1 tsp ginger, 2 tbsp oil, 1 tsp cumin, 1 tsp coriander, 1/2 tsp chili powder, salt, 1 tbsp sugar, 2 tbsp vinegar. Heat oil, fry onion, garlic, ginger. Add spices, then tomatoes. Simmer 20 minutes until thick. Blend smooth. Cool. This lasts 1 week in fridge.

For coconut chutney: 1 cup fresh grated coconut, 2 green chilies, 1 tsp ginger, 1 tbsp roasted peanuts, salt, 1 tbsp tamarind paste, 1/2 cup water. Blend all to a paste. Tempering: heat 1 tbsp oil, add 1 tsp mustard seeds, curry leaves. Pour over chutney. This is common in East Africa with mandazi.

For a spicy peri-peri sauce: 10 red chilies, 4 cloves garlic, 1 lemon (juice), 1/2 cup vinegar, 1 tsp salt, 1 tsp sugar. Blend and simmer 10 minutes. For a simple hot sauce: blend 5 chilies, 2 tomatoes, 1 onion, 1 tbsp vinegar, salt. No cooking needed.

For street sales, make sauces in bulk. Cost: Tomato chutney for 20 servings costs 100 KES (tomatoes 50, spices 20, onion 10, oil 20). Sell each small cup (50ml) for 10-20 KES. Coconut chutney costs 150 KES for 20 servings. Peri-peri costs 80 KES. Package in small plastic containers with lids. Label with spice level. Always keep sauces refrigerated. For hygiene, use a clean spoon per serving. Offer a variety: mild tomato, spicy chili, and tangy tamarind. You can also sell sauces separately in bottles. A good sauce increases salescustomers will buy more pastries to dip. Experiment with local ingredients: mango chutney, tamarind sauce, or groundnut sauce. Always taste before serving. Adjust salt, sugar, and chili to local preference. For longer shelf life, add more vinegar or lemon juice. Never leave sauces at room temperature for more than 2 hours.

<h2>Practical Reference: Chapati, Samosas and Savoury Pastries</h2>

<p>This practical reference is the working document for your street-food or pastry business. It is written for the African operator who is cooking at scale, selling from a stall, kiosk, or small kitchen, and who needs to control cost, quality, and safety on every batch. The lessons in this module cover the flaky chapati method, layered and stuffed chapati, meat and vegetable samosas, meat pies and sausage rolls, savoury doughnuts and mandazi variants, and the sauces and chutneys that lift a plate from ordinary to repeat-purchase. Here we pull those threads together into measurable standards you can train staff against and price against.</p>

<p>Across East and Southern Africa, chapati is a high-volume, high-margin product because the core ingredients (wheat flour, water, oil, salt, sugar) are cheap and widely available. Samosas and pies add value through filling and labour, while mandazi and savoury doughnuts capture the breakfast and snack trade. The commercial logic is simple: master a small number of doughs and fillings, standardise them, and rotate them across dayparts. A chapati at breakfast, a samosa at mid-morning, a meat pie at lunch, and mandazi with tea in the afternoon can all come from one prep kitchen if your systems are tight.</p>

<h3>Process Standards</h3>

<p><strong>Chapati flaky method.</strong> Use warm water, rest the dough at least 30 minutes, roll thin, oil, coil, and re-roll. The coil is what creates the layers. Cook on a dry griddle first, then add oil for the final colour. Stack under a clean cloth to keep soft. For layered and stuffed chapati, add a second filling layer (mince, peas, or greens) before the final roll. Keep the filling cold and well drained so the dough does not tear.</p>

<p><strong>Samosas.</strong> The wrapper should be thin and stiff enough to hold shape. Seal with a flour-water paste, not just water. Fry at 170180C; too low and they absorb oil, too high and they blister before the filling is hot. Meat fillings must be fully cooked before wrapping and cooled quickly.</p>

<p><strong>Pies and sausage rolls.</strong> Keep pastry cold, work quickly, and avoid over-handling. Bake on a preheated tray. Sausage rolls use the same pastry but a coarser filling; they sell well as a lower-price add-on. Egg-wash for shine and colour.</p>

<p><strong>Mandazi and savoury doughnuts.</strong> Mandazi uses coconut milk and cardamom for the coastal profile; savoury doughnuts use less sugar and a savoury dip or filling. Prove until doubled, then fry at 165175C. Drain on a rack, not paper alone, so the base does not steam.</p>

<p><strong>Sauces and chutneys.</strong> Keep a standard trio: tomato-chilli, tamarind, and coconut-coriander. Make in bulk, chill fast, and label with date. Never re-serve sauce that has been on the counter all day.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #b00020;background:#fdecec;padding:14px;border-radius:6px;margin:18px 0;">
<strong style="color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING HIGH-RISK PRACTICES TO STOP NOW</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#3b2f1e;">Never hold cooked meat fillings, pies, or samosas at room temperature for more than two hours. Bacteria multiply fastest between 5C and 60C. Keep hot food above 60C and cold food below 5C. Reheat leftovers only once, to steaming hot throughout. Do not reuse frying oil that is dark, foaming, or smelling of burnt food discard it and record the change. Raw meat and cooked food must never share a board, knife, or cloth. Wash hands with soap after handling raw meat, money, or waste. If you cannot control temperature, do not sell meat products sell vegetable versions instead. This is a legal and reputational risk, not just a quality one.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li>Weigh ingredients do not eyeball flour, salt, or yeast at commercial scale.</li>
<li>Rest chapati dough at least 30 minutes; rest pastry in the fridge before shaping.</li>
<li>Cook meat fillings fully, then cool within 90 minutes before wrapping.</li>
<li>Fry samosas and mandazi at 165180C; check with a thermometer, not guesswork.</li>
<li>Hold hot food above 60C, cold food below 5C; label and date all sauces.</li>
<li>Record every batch: yield, waste, cost per unit, and selling price.</li>
<li>Train one person per shift on temperature checks and cleaning logs.</li>
<li>Price for at least 40% gross margin; review monthly against input costs.</li>
</ul>
