3. Moulding, Curing and Firing

Raw materials become saleable blocks in this stage. Whether you hand-mould or use a press, curing properly is what separates blocks that last 50 years from blocks that crumble in the rain.

Module 3 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~50 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 3.1 — Manual moulding

Two methods dominate at the start-up level.

Slop-moulding (traditional, cheapest): Wet mix poured into a wooden mould on the ground. Mould removed immediately. Output 100–200 blocks/day/worker. Moderate strength.

Hand-press (better quality): Semi-dry mix compressed in a metal mould with a lever. Output 300–600/day/worker. High strength. Equipment $150–$400.

MethodStartup costOutput/dayStrength
Slop-moulding$30100–200Moderate
Hand-press (lever)$150–$400300–600High
Hydraulic press$800–$3,000800–2,000Very high

For most start-ups, a hand-press is the right choice. Quality blocks at low cost. You can scale by adding workers before buying machines.

Practical exercise 3.1: Price a hand-press from 3 suppliers. Watch one in operation for 30 minutes if possible. Decide: slop-moulding or hand-press?

Lesson 3.2 — Machine moulding

MachineOutput/hourPriceWhen to buy

Economics: at ~$0.30 per block saved on labour, a machine producing 1,000 blocks/day saves $300/day = $6,000/month. A $1,500 semi-automatic pays back in about 8 working days.

But do not buy too soon. Machines need reliable electricity, a covered production area, a trained operator, and a steady order book.

The machine rule: Buy the machine when hand production is 20% over capacity and you are turning orders away. Not before. Idle machines lose money.
Practical exercise 3.2: Research 3 machine suppliers. Get demonstration videos. Save this info for month 4 when your order book justifies the jump.

Lesson 3.3 — Drying — the critical 7-day protocol

Most beginner mistakes happen in drying. Get this wrong and blocks crack, warp, or crumble.

DayActionWhy
Day 0Mould on level ground, partial shadeInitial setting, no direct sun
Day 1Shade, cover with plastic or grassPrevent rapid drying, cracking
Day 2Shade, water lightly in hot weatherSlow curing, avoid surface cracks
Day 3Move to partial sun 1–2 hoursControlled drying begins
Day 4–5Increasing sun, water once dailyCure throughout the block
Day 6–7Full sun, no water neededComplete surface drying

Worst mistakes:

  • Full sun on day 1. Surface dries faster than interior → cracks that weaken the block forever.
  • Total shade for 7 days. Block stays soft and never reaches full strength.
  • No watering at all. Cement needs moisture to cure. Blocks that dry out stay weak.
  • Stacking before day 7. Always fatal to quality.
Do not skip the shade period. A block that dries too fast is a weak block, no matter how good the mix was.
Practical exercise 3.3: Mould 20 blocks and follow the 7-day protocol exactly. Compare to 20 blocks left in full sun from day 0. The difference will be obvious.

Lesson 3.4 — Curing — water and time

Curing is the chemical process by which cement hardens. It needs moisture. Blocks that dry out too fast stay weak.

Curing schedule:

  • Days 1–3: Water lightly twice daily (morning and late afternoon). Not soaking — just a light spray or sprinkle.
  • Days 4–7: Water once daily. In very hot weather, twice.
  • Days 8–14: Optional extended curing. Blocks continue gaining strength. Water every 2–3 days.
  • Day 28: Full strength achieved. Blocks can be used structurally.

Best curing conditions: warm (20–35°C), moist, shaded. Worst: hot dry sun, no water.

Curing methodEffectivenessCost
Sprinkling with watering canGoodVery low
Drip irrigation line over blocksExcellentLow (after setup)
Covering with wet burlap/plasticExcellentVery low
Watering pond (submerging blocks)Not recommendedHigh water use
The curing rule: Blocks need 7 days of moisture and shade, then 21 more days of slow drying. If you sell on day 8, warn the customer not to build with them until day 28.
Practical exercise 3.4: Set up a drip line or a sprinkler over your curing area. Time yourself doing manual watering for 20 blocks — this scales up badly. Automate before you scale.

Lesson 3.5 — Firing (for clay bricks)

If you produce fired clay bricks, firing is the defining stage. If you produce cement blocks, skip this lesson for now and return if you expand.

Firing stages:

  1. Preheating (0–500°C). Removes remaining moisture slowly. Too fast causes steam explosions that crack the bricks.
  2. Firing (500–900°C). Clay minerals fuse. This is where strength is created.
  3. Soaking (900°C hold). Time at peak temperature determines hardness. 2–6 hours depending on clay type.
  4. Cooling. Slow cooling avoids thermal shock. Rapid cooling causes cracks.

Kiln types:

KilnCapacityFuelCost to build
Clamp kiln (traditional)5,000–20,000 bricksWood, coal, dung$100–$500
Hoffman kiln50,000–200,000 bricksCoal, wood, gas$5,000–$20,000
Tunnel kiln500,000+ bricksGas, coal$100,000+

For most African producers, a clamp kiln is the practical choice at scale. Hoffman kilns are for commercial operations.

Practical exercise 3.5: If you plan to fire clay bricks, visit a working clamp kiln. Watch a full firing cycle. Ask: fuel cost per 1,000 bricks, firing time, waste rate. That is your real budget.

Lesson 3.6 — Quality control at the yard

Test your blocks before you sell them. A simple QC routine at the yard catches most problems.

TestHowWhat it tells you
VisualLook for cracks, chips, warpingBasic quality of surface
Drop testDrop from 1 m onto hard surfaceImpact strength
Compression testLocal lab or university ($5–$20 per sample)Load-bearing capacity (MPa)
Water absorptionWeigh dry, soak 24h, weigh againPorosity — high absorption means lower durability
Dimension checkMeasure 20 blocks with tapeConsistency of size

Target standards for cement blocks:

  • Compressive strength: 3.5–7 MPa for standard walls
  • Water absorption: under 12% by weight
  • Dimension tolerance: ±2 mm
  • No visible cracks or chips on load-bearing surfaces
Sell only tested blocks. Pick 5 blocks from every 500. Visual, drop, and dimension check. If any fail, quarantine the batch and identify what went wrong (mix, curing, weather).
Practical exercise 3.6: Set up your QC table next to your curing area. Run it on your first 100 blocks. Note any failures and their causes. Fix the cause before scaling production.
