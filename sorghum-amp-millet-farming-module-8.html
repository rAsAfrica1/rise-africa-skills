Lessons

1. Recognizing maturity signs for sorghum and millet

Knowing when to harvest is critical. If you harvest too early, grain is moist and shrivels; too late, grain shatters and birds eat it. For sorghum, maturity is 90-120 days after planting, depending on variety. Check the hard dough stage: grain is firm, not doughy, and a thumbnail pressed into it leaves a slight dent. The plant leaves turn yellow and dry, but the grain itself should still be slightly soft. For millet, maturity is 75-90 days. The seed head turns from green to brown or grey. Grain at the bottom of the panicle matures first, so test by pressing a few grainsthey should be hard and not milky. Another sign: when you shake the panicle, mature grain rattles. In Africa, many farmers use bird activity as a sign: when birds start flocking to the field, it's time to harvest. But don't wait too long. For sorghum, if you wait until the whole plant is brown, the grain may shatter. For millet, if the panicle is fully brown and dry, grain can fall off. A simple test: bite a grain. If it cracks between your teeth and is not soft, it's ready. Also, check moisture: grain should be below 20% moisture for harvest. You can use a moisture meter, but if you don't have one, squeeze a handful of grainif it sticks together, it's too wet. Harvest when 90% of the grains on the panicle are mature. In dry areas, harvest in the morning when dew is gone to avoid shattering. In wet areas, harvest quickly before rains cause mold.

2. Harvesting methods: hand cutting, sickles, and panicle collection

Harvesting sorghum and millet can be done by hand, with a sickle, or by collecting panicles. Each method suits different scales and conditions. Hand cutting: for small plots, you can snap the sorghum stalk by hand or twist the panicle. This is slow but avoids tools. For millet, you can pull the panicle by hand. But hand cutting can damage the plant and leave grain in the field. A sickle is better: a curved blade with a wooden handle. Cut the sorghum stalk about 30 cm below the panicle, or cut the whole plant at ground level if you want stover for livestock. For millet, cut the panicle with 10-15 cm of stalk. Work in a team: one person cuts, another gathers. A sharp sickle can harvest 0.5-1 hectare per day per person. Panicle collection: for sorghum, you can break off just the panicle by hand or with a small knife. This is faster for grain-only harvest and leaves stalks for fodder. But be careful not to shake the panicle, or grain will fall. For millet, you can collect panicles by hand or use a small sickle. In West Africa, farmers often use a knife to cut the panicle. After cutting, place panicles in baskets or sacks. Don't pile them too highbruising causes spoilage. If you cut whole plants, tie them in bundles and stand them in shocks to dry. In East Africa, farmers often harvest sorghum by cutting the whole plant and stacking it. For millet, harvest panicles individually. Always harvest in dry weather. If it rains, wait until plants dry. Wear gloves to avoid cuts. Keep harvested grain off the ground to prevent contamination.

3. Drying and threshing sorghum and millet efficiently

After harvest, grain must be dried to below 13% moisture for safe storage. If too wet, it molds; if too dry, it cracks. For sorghum and millet, drying is done on a clean tarpaulin, concrete floor, or raised platform. Spread grain in a thin layer (5-10 cm) and turn it every 2 hours. Sun drying takes 2-4 days. At night, cover with a tarpaulin to prevent dew. You can test dryness by biting a grainit should crack, not squash. Or use a moisture meter. For threshing, sorghum and millet need different methods. Sorghum: you can thresh by beating panicles with sticks on a clean surface, or use a mechanical thresher. For small amounts, rub panicles between your hands or use a wooden paddle. Be gentle to avoid breaking grain. Millet: threshing is easier because grain is small. You can rub panicles between your palms or use a mortar and pestle lightly. Or spread panicles and walk on them with clean shoes. For both, thresh on a tarpaulin to catch grain. After threshing, separate grain from chaff by winnowing. If you have many panicles, use a threshing machinebut rent one if you can't afford to buy. In Africa, many farmers thresh by beating with sticks. But this can crack grain. A better method: use a wooden threshing board with ridges. Or use a tarpaulin and beat with a stick. After threshing, clean the grain. Don't thresh on bare groundsoil and stones mix in. Also, thresh when grain is dry; if it's too dry, it breaks; if too wet, it smears. Aim for 12-14% moisture for threshing. Store threshed grain in clean sacks.

4. Cleaning and winnowing grain for market quality

Clean grain fetches a higher price. Buyers reject grain with stones, chaff, or broken kernels. After threshing, you must clean and winnow. Winnowing uses wind to separate lighter chaff from heavier grain. On a windy day, pour grain slowly from a height (1-1.5 meters) onto a tarpaulin. The wind blows chaff away, and grain falls straight down. If there's no wind, use a fan or a winnowing basket. In Africa, farmers use a flat basket (like a shallow bowl) to toss grain into the air. Practice to get the right angle. For sorghum, winnowing is easier because grain is heavier. For millet, grain is small, so winnow gently. After winnowing, remove stones and dirt by hand or with a sieve. Use a sieve with holes slightly smaller than grainthis lets dirt fall through but keeps grain. For sorghum, a 4-5 mm sieve works; for millet, 2-3 mm. You can also use a gravity separator if available, but it's expensive. For market quality, grain should be uniform in color and size. Remove broken kernels and foreign matter. If you have a lot of grain, use a cleaning machine. But manual cleaning is fine for small scale. After cleaning, bag grain in clean, dry sacks. Don't use sacks that held fertilizer or chemicals. Label bags with date and variety. For premium price, grade grain: remove discolored grains. In some markets, grain is graded as Grade 1 (clean, uniform) and Grade 2 (some foreign matter). Aim for Grade 1. Also, store cleaned grain in a dry place. If you sell, take a sample to the buyer to check quality. Clean grain can increase price by 10-20%.

5. Using hermetic storage bags (PICS) to prevent weevils

Weevils and other storage pests can destroy your grain in months. Hermetic storage bags, like PICS (Purdue Improved Crop Storage) bags, kill pests without chemicals. PICS bags have three layers: an outer woven polypropylene bag, and two inner high-density polyethylene liners. When you seal grain inside, insects use up oxygen and produce carbon dioxide, which kills them. To use PICS bags: first, dry grain to below 13% moisture. Then put grain in the inner liner, twist the top and tie it tightly with a string or rubber band. Do the same for the second liner. Finally, put the sealed liners inside the outer woven bag and stitch it closed. Store bags on a pallet, not on the floor. Keep away from walls. For sorghum and millet, PICS bags can store grain for 12-24 months without weevils. A 100 kg PICS bag costs about $2-3, but you can reuse it for 2-3 years. If you don't have PICS, you can use metal silos or plastic drums with tight lids. But PICS are affordable and effective. To check if bags are working, look for inflated linersinsects produce gas. If liners are flat, there may be a leak. Also, never open bags frequently; each opening lets in oxygen. For seed grain, PICS bags preserve germination. In Africa, PICS bags are sold in many agrodealer shops. If you can't find them, you can make a similar system with two plastic bags inside a woven sack. But ensure the inner bags are airtight. Hermetic storage is a game-changer for smallholders.

6. Building a simple improved granary for long-term storage

For long-term storage, a granary protects grain from pests, moisture, and theft. A simple improved granary can be built with local materials. The design: a raised platform on stones or concrete blocks, with a mud or brick wall, and a thatched or metal roof. The floor should be 50-100 cm above ground to prevent moisture and rodents. The walls can be made of mud bricks, burnt bricks, or woven sticks plastered with mud. The roof should overhang to keep rain out. Inside, you can divide into compartments for different grains. For sorghum and millet, grain should be stored in bags or bulk. If bulk, line the walls with plastic sheeting to keep moisture out. A good granary holds 1-5 tonnes. To build: dig holes for posts, set wooden posts or stones, build a platform of wood or concrete, then walls. Leave a small door with a lock. Ventilation: small holes covered with mesh allow air but keep insects out. For pest control, you can add ash or neem leaves between layers of grain. But hermetic bags inside the granary are better. Also, raise the granary on stones to prevent termites. In West Africa, farmers use mud granaries with thatched roofs. In East Africa, they use elevated wooden granaries. Make sure the granary is dry and clean before storing. Never store grain directly on the floor. Check monthly for leaks or pests. A well-built granary can store grain for 6-12 months. If you can't build a granary, use airtight drums or PICS bags in a dry room. But a granary gives more space and protection. Community granaries can be shared, but ensure good management.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Harvesting and Post-Harvest Handling of Sorghum & Millet

Sorghum and millet are Africa's drought-resilient cereals, but their market value is won or lost after the crop leaves the field. A farmer who harvests at the right moisture, dries and threshes efficiently, cleans to premium grade, and stores hermetically can cut losses from 2030% to under 5% and earn a premium from processors, millers, and institutional buyers. This module is business-first: every practice below is framed around grain quality, weight retention, and price.

Sorghum and millet are particularly vulnerable to post-harvest loss because their small grains are easily attacked by weevils (Sitophilus spp.) and larger grain borer, and because panicles dry unevenly. The good news: the interventions are low-cost, locally sourced, and proven across the Sahel, East Africa, and Southern Africa.

1. Recognizing Maturity

Harvest timing determines both yield and grain quality. For sorghum, the crop is physiologically mature when the black layer (abscission layer) forms at the point where the grain attaches to the panicle usually 3040 days after flowering. Grain should be in the hard-dough stage: a thumbnail pressed into the grain leaves no dent. For pearl millet, maturity is reached when the panicle turns from green to straw-yellow or brown, and the grain resists crushing.

Do not wait for the whole field to be uniformly dry. Millet matures in 7590 days and sorghum in 90120 days, but panicles within a field ripen over 12 weeks. Harvest in two or three passes to capture the best grain. Delaying harvest beyond physiological maturity exposes grain to bird damage, shattering, and pre-harvest sprouting after rain.

2. Harvesting Methods

Three methods dominate across Africa:

  • Hand cutting of whole stalks: Common for sorghum in West Africa. Stalks are cut at ground level and stacked in the field to dry. Advantage: stalks provide fodder and fuel. Disadvantage: slow, and grain is exposed to weather and pests.
  • Sickle harvesting of panicles: The most efficient manual method. A serrated sickle cuts the panicle 1015 cm below the neck. This reduces bulk, speeds drying, and limits shattering. Recommended for both sorghum and millet.
  • Panicle collection: For millet, panicles are twisted or snapped off by hand. Fast for small plots but risks leaving immature grain if done too early.

Business tip: harvest in the morning when dew reduces shattering. Use clean tarpaulins or woven mats never bare ground, which introduces soil, stones, and aflatoxin risk.

3. Drying and Threshing

Drying is the single most important post-harvest step. Sorghum and millet must be dried to 1213% moisture content (MC) for safe storage. On-farm testing: a grain sample in a sealed jar with salt should not clump; or use a cheap moisture meter (US$2040) a worthwhile investment for any farmer selling more than 2 tonnes.

Sun-dry panicles on a raised platform or tarpaulin for 35 days, turning twice daily. For threshing, the traditional method is beating with sticks or trampling by animals. This causes grain breakage and contamination. Improved options:

  • Mechanical threshers: Available from local fabricators in Kenya, Tanzania, and Nigeria for US$200600. Throughput 5001,000 kg/hour. Reduces losses by 510% and improves grain quality.
  • Improved manual threshing: Use a clean, hard surface and rubber-tipped flails. Separate grain from chaff immediately to prevent moisture re-absorption.

SAFETY WARNING Aflatoxin and Dust

Sorghum and millet stored above 13% moisture can develop aflatoxin, a carcinogen that is invisible, tasteless, and heat-stable. Never eat, sell, or feed mouldy grain even if only part of the bag is affected. Aflatoxin cannot be removed by cooking. During threshing and cleaning, wear a dust mask or cloth over the nose and mouth: grain dust causes chronic respiratory disease. Store grain off the floor on pallets, never against walls, and keep storage rooms ventilated.

4. Cleaning and Winnowing for Market Quality

Market grade is defined by cleanliness: foreign matter (stones, chaff, soil) must be below 2%, broken grains below 5%, and moisture below 13%. Cleaning is therefore a direct price lever. Winnowing tossing grain in the wind or using a fan separates light chaff. For higher volumes, use a mechanical winnower or a simple wooden box with a fan.

After winnowing, sieve to remove stones and undersized grain. For premium markets (millers, breweries, food processors), consider destoning and grading into uniform lots. A 1% improvement in grade can lift farmgate price by 510%.

5. Hermetic Storage with PICS Bags

Purdue Improved Crop Storage (PICS) bags are a triple-layer hermetic system: two inner high-density polyethylene liners and one outer woven polypropylene bag. When sealed, insects consume oxygen and die; grain remains chemical-free and safe for 1224 months. PICS bags cost US$23 each and hold 50100 kg. They are reusable for 23 seasons if handled carefully.

Best practice: dry grain to 1213% MC, clean thoroughly, fill the bag, twist and tie each liner tightly, then seal the outer bag. Store on a pallet in a cool, dry room. Do not open the bag unless necessary each opening resets the hermetic environment.

6. Building a Simple Improved Granary

For long-term storage of larger volumes (500 kg5 tonnes), an improved granary is more economical than PICS bags. Key design features:

  • Raised floor: 3050 cm above ground on stones or concrete, to prevent moisture and rodent entry.
  • Mud or cement plaster: Sealed walls reduce insect entry. A cement floor is best.
  • Metal or mesh lid: Fitted with a rubber gasket to keep out rodents and moisture.
  • Ventilation gap: Small screened vents allow air movement without admitting pests.
  • Roof overhang: 60 cm minimum to keep rain off the walls.

In the Sahel, the traditional secco granary and the improved "metal silo" (promoted by FAO and CIMMYT) are proven designs. A 1-tonne metal silo costs US$150300 and can pay for itself in one season through reduced losses.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Harvest at physiological maturity: black layer for sorghum, straw-yellow panicle for millet.
  2. Cut panicles with a sickle; harvest in the morning to reduce shattering.
  3. Dry on tarpaulins or raised platforms never on bare ground.
  4. Test moisture: target 1213% MC before threshing and storage.
  5. Thresh on a clean, hard surface; use mechanical threshers if available.
  6. Winnow and sieve to remove chaff, stones, and broken grain.
  7. Seal in PICS bags or store in an improved granary on a raised floor.
  8. Never sell or consume mouldy grain aflatoxin is a health and market risk.
