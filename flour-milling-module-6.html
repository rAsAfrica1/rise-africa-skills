1. Understanding Flour Extraction Rates

Flour extraction rate is the percentage of clean wheat that becomes flour. If you mill 100 kg of wheat and get 75 kg of flour, your extraction rate is 75%. In Africa, most small mills aim for 7080% extraction for white flour, but you can go up to 8590% for whole wheat. The rest becomes bran, germ, and shorts (by-products).

To calculate: Weigh your clean wheat before milling (e.g., 50 kg). After milling, weigh all flour streams combined (e.g., 37.5 kg). Divide flour weight by wheat weight, then multiply by 100: (37.5 50) 100 = 75%. Always use a calibrated scalea 5 kg error on a 50 kg batch changes your rate by 10%.

Higher extraction means more flour but darker color and shorter shelf life because germ oil goes rancid. Lower extraction (e.g., 65%) gives whiter, finer flour but more waste. Your choice depends on market: bakeries often want 7275% for bread; health-conscious buyers may pay more for 85% whole wheat. In many African countries, extraction rate is regulatedcheck local standards. For example, some governments require 80% extraction to keep flour affordable and nutritious.

To adjust extraction, change the gap between mill rolls or add/remove sieves. A tighter gap between break rolls produces more break flour and less bran contamination. But too tight, and you smear germ oil into flour. Test by milling a 5 kg sample at different settings, then weigh and compare. Record your results in a notebookthis becomes your mill's standard operating procedure.

Remember: extraction rate affects your profit. If you sell bran and germ as animal feed, higher extraction gives more by-products to sell. But if you only sell flour, lower extraction means less flour per tonne of wheat. Know your market and your costs before deciding.

2. Break Flour, Reduction Flour, and Straight Grade

During milling, wheat goes through a series of roller mills. The first few rolls are 'break rolls'they crack open the wheat kernel and scrape the endosperm away from bran. Flour produced at this stage is called break flour. It is usually coarser and may contain some bran specks. Break flour typically makes up 2030% of total flour.

After break rolls, you have semolina (chunks of endosperm) and bran. The semolina goes to 'reduction rolls'smooth rolls that grind it into fine flour. This is reduction flour, which is finer, whiter, and makes up 5060% of total flour. The rest comes from sizing and tail-end streams.

Straight grade flour is simply all the flour streams combinedbreak, reduction, and any otherswithout blending or separating. It is the total flour you get after milling, minus by-products. Straight grade is what most small mills sell as 'white flour' or 'bread flour'. Its extraction rate is usually 7275%.

Why separate streams? Because each stream has different qualities. Break flour may have more ash (mineral content) and darker color. Reduction flour is brighter and better for cakes and pastries. By blending streams, you can create specific flours: patent flour (only the best streams, 6070% extraction), clear flour (lower grade, darker), or straight grade. In Africa, many mills sell straight grade because it's simple and meets most baking needs.

To identify streams in your mill: take a small sample from each outlet (break, reduction, etc.). Rub between fingersbreak flour feels gritty; reduction flour feels like talcum powder. Put a spoonful in water: bran floats, endosperm sinks. Use a simple ash test: burn a sample in a furnace; higher ash means more bran contamination. With practice, you can adjust roll gaps and sieves to improve stream quality.

3. Streams Analysis and Blending

Streams analysis means checking each flour stream for qualitycolor, ash, protein, and moisture. You don't need a lab. Start with simple tests: color (compare against a white tile), feel (rub between fingers), and water test (mix 10 g flour with 50 ml water; bran floats, good flour sinks). For ash, send samples to a local lab or use a small furnace if available. Ash content tells you how much bran is in the flour: white flour should be under 0.5% ash; whole wheat is around 1.5%.

Blending is mixing streams to get a desired final flour. For example, if your break flour has 0.8% ash and reduction flour has 0.4%, blending 30% break with 70% reduction gives about 0.52% ashstill acceptable for many breads. Use a simple formula: (ash1 proportion1) + (ash2 proportion2) = final ash. Weigh streams on a scale, then mix in a ribbon blender or even a clean cement mixer for small batches.

In Africa, blending is often done to meet customer demands. A bakery may want flour with 11% protein for bread; you can blend a high-protein stream (like reduction from hard wheat) with a lower-protein stream. Or blend to adjust color: add more reduction flour for whiter flour. Always keep records: date, stream proportions, and test results. This helps you repeat a good blend.

Practical tip: Start with a 10 kg test blend. Mix thoroughly, then bake a small loaf. If it rises well and has good crumb, scale up. If not, adjust proportions. Remember, blending is not just about numbersyour customers' feedback matters. Many African millers sell different grades: 'Grade 1' (whiter, lower extraction) for hotels, 'Grade 2' (straight grade) for local bakeries. Know your market and blend accordingly.

4. Impact of Extraction on Flour Quality

Extraction rate directly affects flour quality. Higher extraction (8090%) means more bran and germ particles in the flour. This makes flour darker, gives a nuttier flavor, and increases fiber, vitamins, and minerals. But it also shortens shelf life because germ oil goes rancid within weeks. Lower extraction (6572%) gives whiter flour, longer shelf life, and better baking volume for cakes and pastries, but loses nutrients.

For bread, protein quality matters more than extraction. Hard wheat at 75% extraction gives strong gluten. If you push extraction to 85%, bran particles cut gluten strands, making dough weaker and loaf smaller. In Africa, many traditional breads use 7580% extraction because it balances nutrition and baking performance. For chapati or flatbreads, higher extraction (85%) is fine because gluten strength is less critical.

Moisture also changes with extraction. Bran holds water, so high-extraction flour absorbs more water in dough. You may need to add 510% more water when using 85% extraction versus 72%. Test by mixing a small dough: if it feels dry, add water until smooth. Also, high-extraction flour ferments faster because bran carries wild yeast and bacteria. Reduce fermentation time or use cooler water.

Storage: High-extraction flour should be used within 23 months. Store in cool, dry place, away from sunlight. In humid African climates, use airtight containers and add a desiccant sachet if possible. Low-extraction flour can last 612 months. Always label your flour with extraction rate and milling date.

Finally, extraction affects cost. Higher extraction means more flour per tonne of wheat, so lower production cost per kg. But if you must sell quickly due to short shelf life, you may lose money on spoilage. Find the sweet spot for your marketoften 7580% for general purpose flour in African small mills.

5. By-products: Bran, Germ, and Shorts

When you mill wheat, you get flour plus three main by-products: bran, germ, and shorts. Bran is the outer skincoarse, brown, high in fiber. Germ is the tiny embryorich in oil, vitamins, and protein. Shorts are a mix of fine bran, germ, and some flour; they are finer than bran but coarser than flour. Together, these by-products make up 2030% of the wheat kernel, depending on extraction rate.

In Africa, by-products are valuable income. Bran sells as animal feed for cattle, goats, chickens, and rabbits. A 50 kg bag of bran may sell for 1020% of the price of flour, but it adds up. Germ is often separated and sold as a health food or oil sourcewheat germ oil is expensive. Shorts are used in feed mixes or sometimes in low-cost human foods (e.g., porridge).

To separate by-products, your mill uses sieves and purifiers. After break rolls, bran flakes are sifted out. Germ is removed using a gravity separator or by sieving because it's flatter than endosperm. Shorts are collected from various sieves. In small mills, you may only get a mixed by-product called 'mill feed' or 'pollard'. That's okaysell it as feed.

Storage of by-products: Bran and shorts can be stored dry for months, but germ goes rancid quickly because of its oil. If you separate germ, sell it within a few weeks or refrigerate. In humid climates, dry bran to under 12% moisture before bagging to prevent mould. Use woven polypropylene bags with a plastic liner.

Marketing tip: Don't give away by-products. Many millers let farmers take bran for free, but you can sell it. Contact local poultry farms, dairy cooperatives, or feed stores. Even small amounts add profit. Also, consider pelleting bran for easier transporta simple pellet mill can increase its value. Remember, by-products are not waste; they are co-products that can double your mill's income if managed well.

6. Hands-on Stream Sampling and Analysis

To manage extraction and streams, you must sample and test regularly. Start by identifying all flour outlets on your mill: break flour, reduction flour, and any others. Label each with a number or name. Every day, take a 100 g sample from each outlet using a clean scoop. Put samples in separate, labelled paper bags or containers. Record date, time, and mill settings (roll gaps, sieve sizes).

Simple tests you can do on the spot: Colorspread a spoonful on white paper, compare to a standard photo or sample. Feelrub between fingers; break flour feels gritty, reduction flour smooth. Water testput 10 g in a glass of water; bran floats, good flour sinks. For ash, send a 50 g sample to a lab (costs a few dollars) or use a simple furnace if available. Moisture: use a moisture meter or oven-dry method (weigh 10 g, dry at 130C for 1 hour, reweigh; moisture % = (initial weight - final weight) / initial weight 100).

Once you have data, calculate stream proportions. Weigh total flour from each stream over a day. For example, if you mill 1000 kg wheat and get 250 kg break flour, 500 kg reduction flour, and 150 kg shorts, your total flour is 750 kg (75% extraction). Break is 33% of flour, reduction 67%. If break ash is high, adjust break roll gap wider to reduce bran contamination.

Blending test: Mix small batches (e.g., 1 kg) of different streams in various ratios. Bake a simple bread or chapati. Ask others to taste and judge color, texture, flavor. Keep the best blend recipe. This is how you develop your own signature flour.

Safety: Wear a dust mask when sampling, especially if flour is fine. Clean equipment between samples to avoid cross-contamination. Keep a notebookthis is your mill's memory. Over time, you'll know exactly how to adjust to get the flour your customers want.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Flour Extraction and Streams

For African millers, whether running a 20-tonne-per-day village mill in Tanzania or a 150-tonne-per-day roller mill in Nigeria, the difference between profit and loss is often measured in a few percentage points of extraction. This practical reference consolidates the key numbers, stream behaviours, and sampling routines you need on the mill floor. It is written for the plant manager, the shift miller, and the quality controller who must make commercial decisions daily what wheat to buy, how to set the break rolls, which streams to blend, and how to sell the by-products.

Extraction rate is the single most important economic lever in a flour mill. It determines how much saleable flour you get from every tonne of wheat, and therefore your gross margin. But extraction is not just a number it is the outcome of how you manage each milling stream: break, reduction, sizing, tailings, and the by-product streams of bran, germ, and shorts. This lesson gives you the reference data and field procedures to control those streams and defend your margin.

Understanding Extraction Rates in an African Context

Extraction rate is the mass of flour produced as a percentage of clean wheat milled. In commercial roller mills, straight-grade extraction typically ranges from 72% to 78%. In small plate or hammer mills common across East and West Africa, extraction can reach 8595% because bran is ground into the flour but that flour has higher ash, darker colour, and shorter shelf life. The commercial question is not "what is the highest extraction?" but "what extraction gives the best revenue per tonne of wheat?"

Consider a Nigerian mill buying wheat at US$320/tonne. Flour sells at US$520/tonne; bran sells at US$180/tonne. At 75% extraction, revenue per tonne of wheat = 0.75 520 + 0.25 180 = 390 + 45 = US$435. At 78% extraction, if bran yield drops to 22% and flour quality holds, revenue = 0.78 520 + 0.22 180 = 405.6 + 39.6 = US$445.2. That is roughly US$10 per tonne of wheat on a 100-tonne-per-day mill running 300 days, that is US$300,000 per year. Extraction rate is a business decision, not just a technical one.

Break Flour, Reduction Flour, and Straight Grade

Break flour comes from the break system the first corrugated rolls that open the wheat kernel and scrape endosperm from bran. Break flour is typically finer, lower in starch damage, and often blended into the final straight-grade flour. Reduction flour comes from the smooth reduction rolls that reduce semolina and middlings to flour size; it usually has higher starch damage and is the backbone of straight-grade flour.

Straight grade is the blend of all flour streams (break, reduction, sizing, tailings) in the proportions the miller chooses to meet a specification. In many African markets, straight grade is what is sold as "baker's flour" or "all-purpose flour." A miller can also produce patent flour (a lower-extraction, higher-quality fraction) and clear flour (the remaining stream) for different customers.

Streams Analysis and Blending

Each mill stream has a distinct composition. Break flour from the first break (B1) is relatively clean; later break streams (B3, B4) carry more bran contamination. Reduction streams (C1, C2) are cleaner than tailings streams (C5C8). Blending is the art of combining streams to hit a target ash, protein, colour, and moisture at the lowest cost.

Impact of Extraction on Flour Quality

As extraction rises, flour ash rises, colour darkens, and baking performance changes. Higher extraction includes more bran particles, which carry ash and enzymes (notably alpha-amylase and lipase). Lipase activity shortens shelf life critical in hot, humid African markets where flour may sit in a warehouse for weeks. A miller targeting the West African bread market should usually stay at 7276% extraction to protect colour and shelf life; a miller supplying local porridge or "semovita"-type products may push higher.

SAFETY WARNING Dust and Roller Mill Hazards

Flour dust is combustible. In enclosed spaces such as sifter floors, bin rooms, and bucket elevator pits, a dust cloud can ignite and cause a secondary explosion that levels a mill. Never use compressed air to clean flour dust; use approved vacuum systems or wet methods. Ensure all electrical equipment in dust zones is ATEX-rated or equivalent. Roller mills also present nip-point and entanglement hazards never reach into a running mill, and always lock out/tag out before clearing a choke. In many African mills, dust control and machine guarding are the two most commonly neglected safety controls. Treat them as non-negotiable.

By-products: Bran, Germ, and Shorts

By-products are not waste they are a revenue stream. In East Africa, bran is sold to dairy and poultry farmers; in West Africa, it moves into poultry and fish feed. Germ, if separated, can be sold for oil extraction or as a high-protein feed. Shorts (a mix of fine bran, germ, and some flour) are a valuable feed ingredient. A miller who treats by-products as an afterthought leaves money on the table. The practical rule: know your by-product yields, test them monthly, and negotiate contracts with feed millers and farmers directly rather than selling through a single trader.

Hands-on Stream Sampling and Analysis

Stream sampling is the miller's diagnostic tool. Sample each stream at least weekly, and after any roll change or wheat change. Use a clean scoop, sample from the spout or sifter outlet, and label with date, time, stream, and shift. Test for moisture (oven or moisture meter), ash (muffle furnace or near-infrared if available), and colour (visual or colorimeter). Record results in a simple logbook or spreadsheet. Trends matter more than single readings: a rising ash in C2 tells you a roll is worn or a sifter screen is torn.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Record wheat intake weight and moisture before milling extraction is meaningless without a clean wheat baseline.
  2. Check break release daily: B1 should release 2535% of the wheat, B2 4555% of the remainder.
  3. Sample every flour stream weekly and log ash, moisture, and colour.
  4. Verify sifter screen condition monthly torn screens contaminate flour and raise ash.
  5. Weigh and record bran, germ, and shorts yields per shift; investigate deviations above 2%.
  6. Test final straight-grade flour for ash, moisture, and falling number before dispatch.
  7. Maintain a by-product buyer list and negotiate prices monthly, not annually.
  8. Audit dust control and machine guarding every quarter; document corrective actions.

Bottom line: Extraction rate and stream management are the two levers that decide whether your mill makes money. Measure them, control them, and treat by-products as products. In African markets where wheat is imported and expensive, every percentage point of extraction is worth real money and every safety control you skip is a risk to your people and your plant.
