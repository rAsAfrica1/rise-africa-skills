Lessons

1. Recognizing Mild vs. Severe Choking

Choking happens when an object blocks the airway. In Africa, common causes include bones in fish or meat, groundnut pieces, and hard candies. The first step is to tell the difference between mild and severe choking. A person with mild choking can still cough forcefully, speak, or breathe. They may wheeze or make a whistling sound. Encourage them to keep coughingcoughing is the most effective way to clear a mild blockage. Do not slap their back or give abdominal thrusts if they are coughing effectively. Stay with them and watch closely.

Severe choking is a life-threatening emergency. The person cannot cough, speak, cry, or breathe. They may clutch their throat with one or both handsthis is the universal sign of choking. Their face may turn red, then blue (cyanosis). They may make a high-pitched noise or no sound at all. If the blockage is not relieved, they will become unconscious within minutes. In severe choking, you must act immediately.

In a business or community setting, ask loudly: Are you choking? Can you speak? If they nod yes and can cough, it's mild. If they shake their head no, cannot speak, or are silent, it's severe. For infants, look for weak cry, inability to breathe, or a silent cough. For children, they may hold their neck or panic. Always stay calm and act fast. Remember: mild choking = encourage coughing; severe choking = back blows and abdominal thrusts (or chest thrusts for infants).

2. Adult Choking: Back Blows and Abdominal Thrusts

When an adult is severely choking and cannot breathe, speak, or cough, start with back blows. Stand behind the person and slightly to one side. Support their chest with one hand and lean them forward so their head is lower than their chest. Give up to 5 sharp back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand. Check after each blow to see if the object is expelled. If not, move to abdominal thrusts (also called the Heimlich maneuver).

For abdominal thrusts, stand behind the person and wrap your arms around their waist. Make a fist with one hand and place the thumb side against the person's abdomen, just above the navel but well below the breastbone. Grasp your fist with your other hand and give up to 5 quick, inward and upward thrusts. The thrusts should be firm and distinct. Continue alternating 5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts until the object is forced out or the person becomes unconscious.

If the person becomes unconscious, lower them to the ground gently, call for emergency help (or ask someone to call), and begin CPR. In Africa, where ambulance services may be delayed, send someone to call for help while you continue. If you are alone, call for help on your mobile phone on speaker mode while starting CPR. Remember: for pregnant women or very obese adults, use chest thrusts instead of abdominal thrusts. Place your hands on the lower half of the breastbone and give backward thrusts. Always seek medical attention after any choking incident, even if the object is cleared, because internal injuries can occur.

3. Child Choking: Back Blows and Abdominal Thrusts

Children over 1 year old who are choking severely need back blows and abdominal thrusts, similar to adults but with less force. First, kneel or stand behind the child so you are at their level. Lean the child forward, supporting their chest with one hand. Give up to 5 back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand. Use less force than you would for an adultenough to dislodge the object but not injure the child. Check after each blow.

If back blows don't work, give abdominal thrusts. Kneel behind the child and wrap your arms around their waist. Make a fist with one hand and place it just above the child's navel. Grasp your fist with your other hand and give up to 5 quick, inward and upward thrusts. Be gentle but firm. Continue alternating 5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts until the object comes out or the child becomes unconscious.

If the child becomes unconscious, lay them on a flat surface, call for emergency help, and start CPR. For children, use one or two hands for chest compressions, depending on the child's size. In community settings, children often choke on groundnuts, maize kernels, or small toys. Prevention is key: cut food into small pieces, discourage running or playing while eating, and keep small objects out of reach. After any choking episode, take the child to a clinic or hospital for a check-up, even if they seem fine. Internal injuries from thrusts are possible.

4. Infant Choking: Back Blows and Chest Thrusts

Infants (under 1 year) have smaller airways and are more likely to choke on breast milk, formula, or small objects. If an infant is choking severelycannot breathe, cry, or coughact immediately. Do not use abdominal thrusts on an infant; instead, use back blows and chest thrusts. First, sit down and lay the infant face-down along your forearm, supporting their head and jaw with your hand. Keep the infant's head lower than their chest. Give up to 5 back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand. Use gentle but firm force.

If the object is not expelled, turn the infant face-up along your other forearm, still supporting the head. Place two fingers on the center of the infant's chest, just below the nipple line. Give up to 5 quick chest thrusts, pushing down about 1.5 inches (4 cm). Continue alternating 5 back blows and 5 chest thrusts until the object comes out or the infant becomes unconscious.

If the infant becomes unconscious, place them on a firm, flat surface and begin CPR. For infants, use two fingers for chest compressions, pushing down about 1.5 inches at a rate of 100-120 compressions per minute. Call for emergency help as soon as possible. In African homes, common choking hazards for infants include groundnuts, beans, and small stones. Never give whole nuts or hard foods to infants. After any choking incident, have the infant examined by a health worker, even if they appear normal. Chest thrusts can cause injury, so medical evaluation is important.

5. Unconscious Choking Victim: CPR Transition

If a choking victim becomes unconscious, you must transition to CPR immediately. First, gently lower the person to the ground, flat on their back. Call for emergency help or ask someone else to call. If you are alone, call on your mobile phone on speaker mode while starting CPR. For an adult, begin with 30 chest compressions. Place the heel of one hand on the center of the chest, and your other hand on top. Push hard and fast at a rate of 100-120 compressions per minute, pressing down at least 2 inches (5 cm).

After 30 compressions, open the airway using the head-tilt, chin-lift method. Look inside the mouth for any visible object. If you see it, remove it with your fingers. Do not do a blind finger sweepit can push the object deeper. Give 2 rescue breaths. Each breath should last about 1 second and make the chest rise. If the chest does not rise, reposition the head and try again. Continue cycles of 30 compressions and 2 breaths until the person starts breathing or emergency help arrives.

For children, use one or two hands for compressions, pressing down about 2 inches. For infants, use two fingers, pressing down about 1.5 inches. In all cases, the ratio is 30 compressions to 2 breaths. If you are unwilling or unable to give rescue breaths, perform compression-only CPR. In Africa, where training may be limited, compression-only CPR is better than nothing. Remember: after the object is cleared and the person recovers, they still need medical attention. Choking can cause internal damage or leave objects in the airway.

6. Preventing Choking in the Workplace and Community

Prevention is the best way to avoid choking emergencies. In the workplace, especially in food processing, restaurants, or markets, encourage staff to eat slowly and chew food thoroughly. Avoid talking or laughing with food in the mouth. Cut food into small pieces, especially meat, fish, and hard fruits. Keep small objects like pins, nails, or coins out of areas where food is prepared. For community gatherings, be aware of choking risks: groundnuts, hard candies, and bones are common culprits. Supervise children while they eat and discourage running or playing with food.

In businesses, post choking first aid posters in local languages. Train at least two staff members in first aid and CPR. Keep a first aid kit stocked with gloves and a CPR mask if available. In homes, store small items like buttons, beads, and batteries out of reach of children. Cut grapes, tomatoes, and other round foods into quarters. Avoid giving whole nuts or hard foods to children under 4 years old. For infants, mash or puree foods until they are safe.

In public spaces, ensure that food vendors are aware of choking risks and know basic first aid. Schools should teach children not to put small objects in their mouths. If someone has a known swallowing problem, they should eat slowly and avoid dry, crumbly foods. After any choking incident, review what happened and adjust practices to prevent it from happening again. Remember: a few simple changes can save lives. Share this knowledge with your family, colleagues, and community.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Choking and Airway Management

Choking is one of the fastest-moving emergencies any workplace or community can face. In an African context where staff canteens, market stalls, food-processing units, school feeding programmes and family gatherings all involve eating in groups a blocked airway can turn a routine lunch break into a life-or-death situation within three to four minutes. For a business, that means lost productivity, legal exposure, reputational damage and, worst of all, a preventable death. This Practical Reference lesson consolidates the six core lessons of this module into one usable field guide. It is written business-first: what a manager, supervisor, safety officer or trained first-aider must know, do and document. It assumes no advanced medical training, only the willingness to act correctly and quickly.

Choking happens when a foreign object most commonly food, but also small hardware, bottle caps, groundnut shells, fish bones or plastic wrapping partially or completely blocks the airway. The single most important decision you will make is distinguishing a mild obstruction (the person can still cough, speak or breathe) from a severe obstruction (the person cannot breathe, cough effectively or speak, and may clutch their throat). Everything else back blows, abdominal thrusts, chest thrusts, CPR flows from that decision. Get the recognition right and the response follows naturally.

Recognizing Mild vs. Severe Choking

A person with a mild obstruction is still moving air. They can cough forcefully, make sounds, speak, and usually bring the object up on their own. Your job is to encourage coughing and stay with them do not slap their back or thrust their abdomen, because a forceful cough is more effective than anything you can do, and unnecessary thrusts can convert a mild blockage into a severe one. A person with a severe obstruction cannot breathe, cough effectively or speak. They may make a silent or high-pitched gasping sound, clutch their neck (the universal choking sign), turn blue or grey around the lips, and become panicked or limp. This is a time-critical emergency: act immediately.

Adult Choking: Back Blows and Abdominal Thrusts

For a conscious adult with severe choking, stand behind and slightly to the side of the person. Deliver five firm back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand, supporting the persons chest with your other hand so they do not fall forward. If the object is not cleared, perform five abdominal thrusts (the Heimlich manoeuvre): stand behind the person, wrap your arms around their waist, make a fist with one hand and place it just above the navel, grasp the fist with your other hand, and pull sharply inward and upward. Repeat the cycle of five back blows and five abdominal thrusts until the object clears or the person becomes unconscious. If they become unconscious, lower them gently to the floor and begin CPR.

Two important cautions for the African workplace. First, abdominal thrusts are not appropriate for pregnant women, for very obese people, or for anyone whose abdomen you cannot safely access use chest thrusts instead (see infant section for the technique, adapted to an adults chest). Second, in many community settings there may be no ambulance available for hours. This makes accurate first aid and a clear handover to the nearest clinic or health centre even more important.

Child Choking: Back Blows and Abdominal Thrusts

Children over one year of age are treated like adults but with less force and more care. Kneel or crouch behind the child so you are at their level. Give five back blows between the shoulder blades, then five abdominal thrusts just above the navel, using one hand for smaller children and two hands for larger ones. Alternate the cycle until the object clears or the child becomes unconscious, at which point you start CPR. Children choke most often on nuts, sweets, maize kernels, groundnuts, grapes and small toy parts all common in African homes, schools and marketplaces. A child who is coughing forcefully should be encouraged to cough, not slapped on the back.

Infant Choking: Back Blows and Chest Thrusts

Infants under one year are different. Never perform abdominal thrusts on an infant their liver is large and unprotected, and abdominal thrusts can cause fatal internal injury. Instead, sit down and lay the infant face-down along your forearm, supporting the head and jaw with your hand, with the head lower than the chest. Deliver five back blows between the shoulder blades. Then turn the infant face-up along your forearm, keeping the head lower than the body, and give five chest thrusts using two fingers on the centre of the chest, just below the nipple line. Repeat the cycle until the object clears or the infant becomes unconscious, then begin infant CPR.

Unconscious Choking Victim: CPR Transition

If a choking victim becomes unresponsive, stop thrusts, lower them carefully to a firm flat surface, and call for help. Open the airway and check the mouth if you can clearly see the object, remove it with a finger sweep; if you cannot see it, do not blind-sweep, because you may push it deeper. Begin CPR: 30 chest compressions followed by 2 rescue breaths, at a rate of 100120 compressions per minute. Each time you open the airway for breaths, look inside the mouth for the object. Continue CPR until the person recovers, another trained responder takes over, or emergency help arrives. In settings without ambulance coverage, arrange transport to the nearest health facility as soon as another person is available.

RED SAFETY WARNING Do Not Do These Things

Never perform abdominal thrusts on an infant under one year. Never give back blows to a person who is coughing forcefully you may convert a mild obstruction into a severe one. Never put your fingers blindly into an unconscious persons mouth. Never give water or food to someone who is still choking. Never delay calling for emergency help because you are worried about cost or transport in most African countries, the nearest health centre, clinic or community health worker is your first link in the chain of survival. If you are unsure, act a correct first-aid attempt is almost always better than standing by.

Preventing Choking in the Workplace and Community

Prevention is cheaper than any emergency response. In food businesses, canteens and market stalls, enforce a no eating while walking, talking or laughing rule during busy periods. Cut food into small pieces, especially for children and older adults. Keep small hardware, bottle caps, plastic wrapping and groundnut shells out of food preparation and packaging areas. Train at least two staff members per shift in basic first aid and keep a choking poster visible in the canteen. In schools and community centres, supervise young children during meals, discourage running while eating, and keep small objects out of reach of infants. Document every near-miss and incident this protects your business legally and helps you fix the root cause.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Recognize: Can the person cough, speak or breathe? Yes = mild, encourage coughing. No = severe, act now.
  2. Call for help: Shout for a trained first-aider and call the nearest emergency number or health facility immediately.
  3. Adult / child severe: Give 5 back blows, then 5 abdominal thrusts. Repeat the 5-and-5 cycle.
  4. Infant under 1: 5 back blows with head down, then 5 chest thrusts with two fingers. Never abdominal thrusts.
  5. Pregnant or obese: Replace abdominal thrusts with chest thrusts.
  6. If unconscious: Lower to a firm flat surface, open the airway, remove only visible objects, then start CPR (30:2).
  7. Document: Record the incident, the time, the action taken and the outcome for your business safety log.
  8. Prevent: Cut food small, supervise children at meals, keep small objects away from food areas, and train at least two staff per shift.
