1. Twisting Techniques: Round, Square, and Rope Twists

Twisting turns a plain bar into something the eye wants to follow. Work hot: mild steel twists best between 950Â°C and 1150Â°C, a bright orange to light yellow. Below that it tears; above it burns. You need a vise bolted hard to a stump or wall, a heavy adjustable wrench or twisting fork, and a forge that heats at least 200 mm of bar evenly. In many African workshops a charcoal or coal forge with a hand blower does this well â keep a steady blast and rotate the bar every 20 seconds so heat spreads along the length.

Round twist: heat 150â200 mm of 10 mm round bar. Clamp one end in the vise, grip the other with a wrench, and turn steadily in one direction. For a tight twist, turn 1 full rotation per 25 mm of length. For an open twist, 1 rotation per 50 mm. Count your turns and write them down â repeatability is what sells a set of gate bars. Keep the twist axis straight: if the bar bends, straighten it on the anvil face while still hot.

Square twist: 12 mm square bar gives sharp, crisp edges that catch light. Heat a 200 mm section, clamp, and twist 3 to 4 full turns. Because square corners resist, use a longer wrench and steady pressure rather than jerking. The corners spiral into a diamond pattern. This is the classic look for window grilles and church doors.

Rope twist: first twist two or three 6â8 mm rods together lightly, or twist a single bar, then twist it back the opposite way. The reverse turn opens the strands and makes them look like laid rope. Do the first twist clockwise at 1 turn per 30 mm, then counter-twist at 1 turn per 40 mm. Work fast; if the bar cools, reheat rather than forcing it.

After twisting, brush off scale with a wire brush while the metal is still warm, then let it cool slowly in ash or sand. Never quench a twist in water â it hardens and can crack. Practise on 300 mm offcuts until your turn counts match every time.

2. Creating Leaves and Scrolls

Leaves and scrolls are the bread and butter of decorative ironwork, and they are made from flat bar, not round. Start with 25 x 5 mm or 30 x 6 mm mild steel flat. For a leaf, cut a piece 120â150 mm long. Heat the last 50 mm to bright orange and draw it to a taper on the anvil horn, hammering from the body toward the tip. You want the tip about 3 mm thick and the body 5 mm.

Shape the leaf on the anvil face with a cross-pein hammer. Strike along the centre line to spread the leaf wider and thinner, then use the pein to cut a shallow centre vein. With a hot chisel or a blunt cold chisel, notch the edges lightly to suggest serrations. Leaf shapes that sell in African markets are the acacia leaf, the baobab leaf, and a simple pointed lance leaf. Keep the leaf 25â35 mm wide at its widest point.

Curl the leaf by hammering the tip over the horn or by gripping the tip in a vise and pulling the stem. A slight upward curve looks alive; a flat leaf looks dead. Set the leaf aside and make the stem from the remaining bar, drawing it down to 6â8 mm round.

Scrolls are spirals made from flat or round bar. Heat 250â300 mm of 10 mm round or 20 x 5 mm flat to orange. Grip one end in the vise with about 40 mm sticking out. Bend that 40 mm to 90 degrees over the horn, then progressively feed more bar out of the vise and curl it around, each pass tighter than the last. Use scrolling tongs or a bending fork. Aim for 2.5 to 3 full turns. Keep the scroll in one plane â if it cones, flatten it on the anvil face.

For a matching pair, make the first scroll, then compare every bend of the second against it while both are hot. Scrolls are joined to frames by forge welding, riveting, or collaring with 3 mm flat strips wrapped and hammered tight. Finish leaves and scrolls by wire brushing at black heat and waxing.

3. Punching and Chasing Decorative Patterns

Punching makes holes; chasing makes lines and texture. Both are done hot, and both need the right tools. For punching you need a hot punch â a tapered square or round bar of 12â16 mm tool steel, hardened and tempered to a light straw colour, with a handle forged on one end. For chasing you need a blunt chisel, a round-nose punch, and a small ball punch.

Punching a hole: heat the work to bright orange, place it flat on the anvil or over a bolster plate (a plate with holes), and drive the punch halfway through with a 1â2 kg hammer. Turn the work over and punch from the other side until the holes meet. This keeps the hole clean and avoids a ragged exit. For a 10 mm hole, start with an 8 mm punch and drift it open. Space decorative holes 15â25 mm apart along a bar for a pierced grille effect.

Chasing lines: with the work at orange heat, hold the blunt chisel at about 60 degrees to the surface and strike with light, even blows. Move the chisel 3â5 mm between blows to make a continuous groove. For a border, mark two parallel lines 8 mm apart with a scriber while cold, then chase along them. A chased line catches wax and darkens, giving contrast.

Dots and dimples: use the ball punch with a single firm blow to leave a round dimple. Rows of dimples along a scroll stem look like beads. For a leaf, chase a centre vein and 3â4 side veins per side. For a gate panel, combine a chased border with punched holes and a central leaf.

Never punch or chase cold mild steel â it will crack and ruin the tool. Reheat every 3â4 blows. Support the work over a bolster so the punch does not hit the anvil. After chasing, dress the back of the work flat on the anvil face. Keep your punches cool by dipping the tip in water every few blows, but never quench the hot work itself.

4. Using a Swage Block for Shaping

A swage block is a thick cast-iron block with a range of half-round, square, and V-shaped grooves and holes. It is the cheapest way to make repeated curved shapes without a power hammer. In many African workshops a swage block is set on a wooden stump or a steel stand at waist height so you can work from both sides.

Half-round grooves: these come in sizes from about 10 mm to 50 mm. To make a decorative collar or a curved bracket, heat a bar to orange and lay it across the groove. Strike it with a round-faced hammer or a top swage of matching size. The bar sinks into the groove and takes the curve. For a 20 mm groove and a 12 mm bar, three or four blows along the length produce a smooth arc. Use the same groove every time to keep a set of parts identical.

V-grooves: these are for bending sharp angles and for making square-cornered scrolls. Heat the bar, lay it in the V, and strike with a flat hammer. The V supports the bar so the corner stays crisp. For a 90-degree bend, bend slightly past 90 degrees because the metal springs back about 2â3 degrees.

Square holes: these are for drifting and for holding a punch or a heading tool. A square hole 20â25 mm across is useful for making a decorative washer or a square collar. Heat a flat plate, place it over the hole, and drive a square drift through.

Round holes: use these with a bolster to punch clean holes in flat work. The hole supports the work and lets the punch exit cleanly.

Keep the swage block clean. Scale and rust make the grooves rough, and rough grooves mark your work. Brush it after every use and rub a little oil on it. Never strike the block directly with a hammer â always work hot metal in the groove. If a groove is worn, dress it lightly with a file or a grinder. A swage block will outlast you if you look after it.

5. Finishing Techniques: Wire Brushing, Wax, and Paint

Finishing decides whether your work looks professional or like scrap. The three main finishes for decorative ironwork are wire brushing, waxing, and painting. Each has a place, and each needs the metal at the right temperature.

Wire brushing: do this at black heat, about 400â500Â°C, when the metal is dark red in a dim light. Use a stiff steel wire brush â a hand brush for small work, a bench grinder with a wire wheel for larger pieces. Brush along the length of the bar, not across it, to pull scale off and leave a clean grey surface. If the metal is too hot, the brush burns; too cold, the scale will not move. Brush, then let the piece cool slowly in ash or sand. This is the traditional finish for hand-forged work and it needs no paint.

Wax: after wire brushing and cooling to about 150â200Â°C (you can touch it for a second), apply beeswax or a mixture of beeswax and linseed oil with a rag. The wax melts, soaks into the surface, and leaves a soft satin sheen. Reapply once a year. In humid coastal areas, use a wax with a little turpentine to stop mould. Wax is food-safe and is the best finish for kitchen hooks, bottle openers, and door handles.

Paint: for outdoor work, wire brush first, then apply a red oxide or zinc-rich primer while the metal is warm but not hot. Two coats of primer, then two coats of gloss enamel or a hammered-finish paint. Black, dark green, and red are the most popular colours in African markets. Paint hides hammer marks, so if you want to show your forge work, use wax instead.

For a quick rust-resistant finish, heat the piece to about 300Â°C and rub it with old engine oil. The oil burns on and leaves a black protective coat. Do this outdoors â the smoke is heavy. Whichever finish you choose, do it the same way every time so your customers know what to expect.

6. Designing a Decorative Piece

Design is not decoration added at the end; it is decided before you light the forge. Start with a purpose: a gate panel, a window grille, a wall hook, a candle holder, a garden gate. Write down the size, the material, and the finish. For a gate panel, a common African size is 600 x 900 mm, made from 12 mm square bar for the frame and 10 mm round for the scrolls.

Draw the piece full size on a wooden board or a sheet of plywood. Use a pencil and a pair of dividers. Mark the frame, then mark the centre line, then place the main scrolls. Keep the design symmetrical unless you have a good reason not to. Odd numbers of elements look better than even numbers â three leaves, five scrolls, seven bars. Leave at least 40 mm between elements so the eye can rest.

Make a full-size paper template for every repeated part. For a scroll, draw the spiral on paper, cut it out, and bend your hot metal to match the paper. For a leaf, draw the outline and use it to check the shape as you forge. This is how you make ten identical leaves without measuring each one.

Plan the joints before you cut metal. Forge welds are strong but slow; rivets are fast and decorative; collars of 3 mm flat bar wrapped around two bars look traditional. Decide the order of assembly: make all the parts first, then lay them on the board, then join. Never weld a piece while it is lying on the template â the heat will burn the board.

Think about the finish from the start. If the piece will be painted, hammer marks do not matter. If it will be waxed, every hammer blow shows, so keep your hammer faces polished and your blows even. Think about how the piece will be fixed: two 10 mm holes in the frame for coach bolts, or three legs for a freestanding piece.

Finally, make a small sample first â a 200 mm section of the full design. Show it to a customer or a fellow smith. If the sample works, build the full piece. If it does not, change the design before you waste 3 metres of steel.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Decorative Blacksmithing for African Markets

Decorative blacksmithing is where the money is. In most African markets, a plain hoe or door hinge earns a thin margin. A twisted, leafed, scroll-work gate panel, balcony railing, or decorative grille can earn two to five times the material cost because the customer is buying identity, status, and craftsmanship, not just metal. This Practical Reference lesson consolidates the six preceding lessons into a working field guide. It is written for the smith who wants to move from job work to commissioned decorative work â the kind of work that pays school fees, funds a second forge, and builds a brand.

Across the continent, decorative ironwork has deep roots: Hausa and Nupe brass-and-iron traditions in Nigeria, Ethiopian Coptic cross and window grille work, Swahili carved-and-forged door fittings along the East African coast, and North African moucharabieh-inspired screens. Modern demand is driven by urban construction: estate gates in Lagos and Nairobi, boutique hotel railings in Kigali and Cape Town, restaurant signage in Accra, and mosque and church decorative screens from Dakar to Mombasa. The smith who can execute clean twists, crisp scrolls, and consistent repeat patterns wins these contracts.

Twisting Techniques: Round, Square, and Rope Twists

Twisting is the highest-value-per-minute operation in decorative work. A clean twist takes 2â4 minutes of forge time but adds visible value a client can see from the gate. Heat the section to a uniform bright orange, clamp one end in the vise, and turn the other with a twisting wrench or pipe wrench. Uniform heat equals uniform pitch. For a round twist, keep the bar rotating as you twist; for a square twist, lock the bar so the corners spiral; for a rope twist, twist two bars together in opposite directions, then weld the ends. Practice on 12 mm square bar â the standard stock in most African hardware markets.

Creating Leaves and Scrolls

Leaves and scrolls are the vocabulary of decorative ironwork. A leaf begins as a tapered flat bar: draw out the stem, spread the body with a cross-peen hammer, then chase the central vein with a hot chisel. Scrolls are formed by bending hot stock around a scroll fork or the horn of the anvil, working from the tightest curl outward. In African markets, repeated scroll modules sell best because they allow jigs and repeat production: a gate with 20 identical scrolls quotes faster and prices higher than a one-off design.

Punching and Chasing Decorative Patterns

Punching and chasing add surface narrative â dots, lines, geometric borders, and motifs drawn from local textile and architectural traditions. Use a hot punch on bright orange steel, backing the work on a lead or hardwood block. Chasing (moving metal without cutting) is done with a blunt chisel or chasing tool. This is where regional identity becomes marketable: Adinkra-inspired symbols, Ndebele geometric bands, or Swahili coastal motifs transform a generic railing into a commissioned piece.

Using a Swage Block for Shaping

A swage block is a multi-hole, multi-groove cast iron block used to form curves, half-rounds, and decorative bosses. In small African workshops, a swage block is often the single most underused tool. It allows repeatable shaping of collars, half-round rails, and curved brackets without a power hammer. A locally cast or imported 30â50 kg swage block pays for itself within one gate commission.

Finishing Techniques: Wire Brushing, Wax, and Paint

Finish determines whether the piece survives the first rainy season. Wire brush at black heat (approx. 200â300 Â°C) to remove scale, then apply beeswax or linseed oil for an interior, heritage look, or a zinc-rich primer plus two topcoats for exterior work. In humid coastal markets (Lagos, Mombasa, Dar es Salaam), paint systems must be specified at quotation stage â this is a business decision, not an afterthought.

â  SAFETY WARNING â HOT METAL, FUMES, AND EYES

Decorative work involves repeated reheating, punching, and finishing. Always wear leather apron, gauntlets, and impact-rated eye protection. Never quench galvanised or painted scrap â zinc and paint fumes cause metal fume fever and are potentially fatal in enclosed workshops. Ensure cross-ventilation or forced extraction; many African workshops are semi-enclosed, so position the forge upwind of the anvil. Keep a dry-powder extinguisher within 3 m. Never leave hot work unattended near children or apprentices under 16. Wire brushing at black heat throws scale â wear a face shield, not just goggles.

Designing a Decorative Piece

Design is a commercial act. Before striking metal, sketch the piece, estimate bar length, list operations, and price the job. A simple design brief for an African market commission includes: (1) function (gate, railing, sign bracket), (2) dimensions and fixing points, (3) motif and cultural reference, (4) finish and environment, (5) delivery date, (6) price and deposit. Charge a 50% deposit on materials. Photograph every finished piece for a portfolio â this is your marketing asset for the next commission.
