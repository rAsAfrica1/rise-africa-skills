1. Lesson 1: Selecting Greenhouse Crops for African Markets

Welcome to Module 9, Lesson 1. In this lesson we focus on one of the most important business decisions you will make as a greenhouse vegetable farmer: choosing which crops to grow. A greenhouse is a significant investment. Whether you build a low-cost wooden frame with UV-treated polythene or a steel structure with insect netting, you need to recover that investment and make a profit. The crop you choose determines your income, your labour needs, your water use, and your risk. In Africa, the most common greenhouse vegetables are tomatoes, sweet peppers, cucumbers, leafy greens such as spinach and kale, and herbs such as basil and coriander. Each has different market demands, growing cycles, and price points. Start with the market, not the seed. Before you plant anything, visit your local markets, supermarkets, hotels, restaurants, and schools. Ask what vegetables they buy, in what quantities, at what prices, and how often. Talk to traders at busy urban markets. In many African cities, demand for clean, uniform, pest-free tomatoes and peppers is high, especially from hotels and supermarkets. Leafy greens have steady demand but lower prices and shorter shelf life. Herbs serve a niche market with high prices but small volumes. Choose crops that match your market access. If you are far from a city, focus on crops that travel well, such as tomatoes, peppers, and cucumbers. If you are near a city or have a contract with a hotel, you can grow leafy greens and herbs. Consider your climate and greenhouse type. In hot lowland areas, tomatoes and peppers can struggle with fruit set when temperatures exceed 32 degrees Celsius. Shade netting, ventilation, and misting can help. Cucumbers tolerate heat better but need trellising. Leafy greens prefer cooler conditions and may need shade in hot seasons. In cooler highland areas, tomatoes and peppers thrive, but growth is slower. Know your local temperatures, rainfall, and humidity. Choose crops that suit your environment rather than fighting nature. Think about crop duration and cash flow. Tomatoes can take 3 to 4 months from transplanting to first harvest and continue producing for several months. Peppers take similar time but can produce for a year or more in a greenhouse. Cucumbers start producing in 6 to 8 weeks and continue for 2 to 3 months. Leafy greens can be ready in 4 to 6 weeks. Herbs in 6 to 8 weeks. If you need quick income, start with leafy greens or cucumbers while your tomatoes or peppers are growing. Many successful farmers grow a mix of quick and long-term crops. Consider labour and skills. Tomatoes and peppers require pruning, staking, and pest management. Cucumbers need trellising and frequent harvesting. Leafy greens need careful washing and packaging. Herbs need gentle handling. If you have limited labour, choose crops that are less demanding. If you have family labour, you can manage more intensive crops. Train yourself and your workers on the specific needs of each crop. Water and nutrition are critical. Greenhouse crops need consistent watering. Drip irrigation is best because it saves water and reduces disease. You also need a fertiliser programme. Tomatoes and peppers are heavy feeders. Leafy greens need nitrogen for leaf growth. Herbs need balanced nutrients. Test your soil or use a hydroponic system if soil is poor. In many parts of Africa, soil fertility is low, so composting and manure are important. Plan your planting schedule. Do not plant everything at once. Stagger planting so you harvest continuously and avoid flooding the market. For example, plant tomatoes every 4 weeks so you have a steady supply. This also spreads your risk if pests or diseases attack one batch. Keep records. Record planting dates, harvest dates, yields, costs, and prices. This helps you know which crops are profitable and which are not. Many farmers grow crops that look good but lose money. Records show the truth. Start small and expand. Do not plant your entire greenhouse with one crop in your first season. Try small plots of several crops. See what grows well and what sells well. Then expand the winners. This reduces risk and builds knowledge. Consider contracts. A contract with a supermarket, hotel, or school can guarantee a market. But contracts often require consistent quality and quantity. Make sure you can deliver before you sign. Some contracts specify varieties, sizes, and packaging. Read carefully. Think about post-harvest handling. Greenhouse vegetables are delicate. Harvest in the cool of the morning. Use clean crates. Keep produce in shade. For leafy greens, wash and pack in clean bags. For tomatoes and peppers, grade by size and colour. Good handling reduces losses and increases prices. Finally, think about competition. If many farmers in your area grow tomatoes, prices may be low during harvest season. Greenhouses allow you to grow out of season when prices are higher. This is a major advantage. Use it. In summary, choose crops based on market demand, climate, labour, water, and cash flow. Start small, keep records, and adjust. Your greenhouse is a business, not a garden. Every crop choice should move you toward profit.

2. Lesson 2: Greenhouse Climate Control in African Conditions

Welcome to Lesson 2 of Module 9. In this lesson we cover climate control inside your greenhouse. A greenhouse creates a protected environment, but it also traps heat and humidity. In Africa, where temperatures can be very high, managing climate is essential for healthy crops and good yields. Without proper control, your greenhouse can become an oven, killing plants and wasting your investment. The main climate factors you must manage are temperature, humidity, ventilation, light, and air circulation. Temperature is the biggest challenge. On a sunny day, the temperature inside a closed greenhouse can be 10 to 20 degrees Celsius higher than outside. If outside is 30 degrees, inside can reach 45 to 50 degrees. Most vegetables stop growing above 35 degrees, and flowers may drop without setting fruit. To control temperature, use ventilation, shade, and cooling. Ventilation is the first tool. Greenhouses need openings for hot air to escape and cool air to enter. Roof vents, side vents, and roll-up sides are common. In simple greenhouses, you can open the sides during the day and close them at night. Insect netting on openings prevents pests from entering. Make sure openings are large enough. A common rule is that vent area should be at least 15 to 20 percent of the floor area. Shade is the second tool. Shade netting reduces sunlight and heat. Use 30 to 50 percent shade netting in hot lowland areas. You can place shade netting on top of the greenhouse or inside. Whitewash or shade paint on the polythene also works. In cooler highland areas, you may not need shade, or only during the hottest months. Cooling is the third tool. Misting or fogging systems spray fine water droplets into the air. As water evaporates, it cools the air. This works well in dry heat but less in humid conditions. You can also wet the floor or use wet pads on the windward side. In very hot areas, a fan and pad system is effective but expensive and needs reliable electricity. Humidity is the second factor. High humidity encourages fungal diseases such as powdery mildew, downy mildew, and botrytis. Low humidity causes plants to lose water and wilt. Most vegetables prefer 60 to 80 percent relative humidity. To reduce humidity, increase ventilation, avoid overwatering, and use drip irrigation instead of overhead watering. Water early in the day so leaves dry before night. To increase humidity, mist the air or wet the floor. In hot dry areas, humidity control is important for pollination. Tomatoes and peppers need humidity for pollen to stick. If humidity is too low, flowers drop. Ventilation is the third factor. Good ventilation removes hot air, brings in carbon dioxide, and reduces humidity. It also helps pollination because air movement shakes flowers. In greenhouses with insect netting, natural ventilation may be limited. You may need fans. Solar-powered fans are useful in areas with unreliable electricity. Light is the fourth factor. Vegetables need 6 to 8 hours of direct sunlight for good growth. In Africa, sunlight is usually abundant. But too much light and heat can damage plants. Shade netting reduces light. In cloudy highland areas, you may need to remove shade to maximise light. Clean polythene regularly because dust reduces light transmission. Air circulation is the fifth factor. Even with vents, air inside a greenhouse can be still. Stagnant air encourages disease. Small fans or open vents help. Pruning and spacing plants also improve air movement. Do not overcrowd. Climate control also depends on greenhouse design. A greenhouse with a high roof has more air volume and stays cooler. A roof with a ridge vent allows hot air to escape at the highest point. Roll-up sides are cheap and effective. In hot areas, a shade house with insect netting instead of polythene may be better than a closed greenhouse. It protects from pests and heavy rain but allows more airflow. In cooler areas, a closed greenhouse with double-layer polythene retains heat. You can also use thermal mass, such as water barrels or stone walls, to store heat during the day and release it at night. This is useful in highland areas where nights are cold. Monitoring is essential. Use a thermometer and hygrometer to measure temperature and humidity. Check them at least twice a day, morning and afternoon. Record the readings. If temperature is too high, open vents, turn on fans, or add shade. If humidity is too high, increase ventilation. If plants show stress, such as wilting or leaf curling, act quickly. Water management is linked to climate. In hot conditions, plants need more water. Drip irrigation delivers water directly to roots and reduces evaporation. Mulching with dry grass or plastic reduces evaporation from the soil. Water in the early morning or late evening to reduce heat stress. Avoid watering in the middle of the day. Seasonal changes matter. In the rainy season, humidity is high and disease pressure is greater. Increase ventilation and use preventive sprays such as neem or baking soda solutions. In the dry season, heat and dust are problems. Use shade and misting. In the cool season, close vents at night to retain heat. Pests and climate interact. Whiteflies and aphids thrive in hot dry conditions. Fungal diseases thrive in humid conditions. Monitor crops daily. Use sticky traps for flying insects. Remove diseased leaves immediately. Healthy climate control reduces pest and disease problems. Energy costs can be high if you use fans and pumps. Solar power is a good option in Africa. Solar panels can run fans, pumps, and lights. They reduce dependence on unreliable grid electricity. The initial cost is high, but savings are long-term. Finally, train your workers. Everyone who works in the greenhouse should understand how to open and close vents, when to water, and how to spot climate stress. A simple checklist on the wall helps. In summary, climate control is about managing temperature, humidity, ventilation, light, and air circulation. Use ventilation, shade, misting, and good design. Monitor daily. Adjust as seasons change. A well-controlled greenhouse produces healthy crops and higher profits.

3. Lesson 3: Integrated Pest and Disease Management in Greenhouses

Welcome to Lesson 3 of Module 9. In this lesson we cover integrated pest and disease management, often called IPM. Greenhouses protect crops from many pests and diseases, but they also create conditions where some problems can become severe. Because the environment is enclosed, pests and diseases can spread quickly. Your goal is not to eliminate all pests, but to keep them below levels that cause economic damage. IPM combines prevention, monitoring, biological control, and careful use of chemicals. Prevention is the foundation. Start with clean soil or clean growing media. If you use soil, solarise it by covering with clear plastic for several weeks to kill pests and pathogens. Or use compost that has been properly heated. If you use hydroponics, clean all equipment between crops. Use certified disease-free seeds and seedlings. Many diseases, such as bacterial wilt and viruses, come from infected seed or seedlings. Buy from reputable suppliers. Practice crop rotation. Do not grow the same crop family in the same soil season after season. Tomatoes, peppers, and eggplants are in the same family and share pests and diseases. Rotate with leafy greens, cucumbers, or herbs. Sanitation is critical. Remove weeds because they host pests. Remove and destroy diseased plants immediately. Do not compost diseased plants unless your compost reaches high temperatures. Clean tools with soap or bleach solution. Wear clean clothes and shoes in the greenhouse. Limit visitors. Keep the greenhouse free of debris and old plant material. Use insect netting on all openings. This is one of the best investments. Netting with a mesh size of 0.4 to 0.6 millimetres excludes whiteflies, aphids, and thrips. Install double doors or a vestibule to prevent pests entering when you enter. Monitor regularly. Walk through your greenhouse at least twice a week. Look at the underside of leaves, growing tips, and flowers. Use yellow sticky traps to catch whiteflies and aphids. Use blue sticky traps for thrips. Check traps weekly. Learn to identify common pests and diseases. Common pests include whiteflies, aphids, thrips, spider mites, leafminers, and caterpillars. Common diseases include powdery mildew, downy mildew, early blight, late blight, bacterial wilt, and mosaic viruses. Keep a notebook. Record what you see, where, and when. This helps you decide when to act. Biological control uses living organisms to control pests. This is safe and effective in greenhouses. For whiteflies, you can release Encarsia formosa, a tiny parasitic wasp. For aphids, use ladybirds or Aphidius wasps. For spider mites, use Phytoseiulus persimilis, a predatory mite. For thrips, use Amblyseius cucumeris. These beneficial insects are available from suppliers in some African countries, and you can also encourage natural enemies by planting flowers such as marigolds and sunflowers near the greenhouse. Biological control works best when pest numbers are low. Do not use broad-spectrum insecticides because they kill beneficial insects. Botanical pesticides are plant-based and safer. Neem oil controls aphids, whiteflies, and mites. Garlic and chilli sprays repel pests. Soap sprays kill soft-bodied insects. Baking soda and potassium bicarbonate control powdery mildew. Milk spray, one part milk to nine parts water, also controls powdery mildew. These remedies are cheap and available locally. Use them carefully. Test on a few plants first. Spray in the evening to avoid leaf burn. Chemical pesticides should be a last resort. If you must use them, choose selective pesticides that target the pest and do not harm beneficial insects. Always read the label. Wear protective clothing. Follow the recommended dose. Do not spray near harvest time. Observe the pre-harvest interval. Never mix different chemicals unless the label says it is safe. Store chemicals safely away from children and food. Remember that pests develop resistance to chemicals if you use them often. Rotate chemical groups. Use chemicals only when monitoring shows pest numbers are above the economic threshold. That threshold is the level of pest damage that costs more than the cost of control. For example, if you see a few whiteflies, you may not need to spray. If you see many, act. Diseases are often harder to control than pests. Once a plant is infected with a virus or bacterial wilt, there is no cure. Remove and destroy the plant. Prevent diseases by using resistant varieties. Many tomato varieties are resistant to Fusarium wilt, Verticillium wilt, and nematodes. Ask your seed supplier. Avoid overhead watering. Use drip irrigation. Keep leaves dry. Space plants for good airflow. Prune lower leaves to reduce splash from soil. Control weeds. Manage humidity with ventilation. If you see powdery mildew, remove affected leaves and spray with baking soda or milk. If you see downy mildew, improve ventilation and use copper-based sprays if allowed. For bacterial wilt, remove the plant and solarise the soil. Do not replant tomatoes or peppers in that spot for at least two seasons. Nematodes are tiny worms that attack roots. They cause stunting and yellowing. Solarise soil, use resistant varieties, and rotate with non-host crops such as maize or onions. Marigolds also repel nematodes. Record keeping is part of IPM. Keep a log of pest sightings, treatments, and results. This helps you learn what works. It also helps you plan the next season. Train your workers to identify pests and diseases. Show them pictures. Teach them to report problems early. A pest problem caught early is much easier and cheaper to control. Finally, think about the economics. IPM saves money because you use fewer chemicals and lose fewer crops. It also produces safer vegetables for consumers. Many supermarkets and hotels prefer vegetables grown with minimal chemicals. You can use IPM as a marketing advantage. In summary, IPM combines prevention, monitoring, biological control, botanical sprays, and careful chemical use. Keep your greenhouse clean. Monitor often. Act early. Use the least toxic method first. This protects your crops, your health, and your profits.

4. Lesson 4: Irrigation and Fertigation for Greenhouse Vegetables

Welcome to Lesson 4 of Module 9. In this lesson we cover irrigation and fertigation. Water and nutrients are the fuel for your greenhouse crops. Without proper irrigation and fertilisation, plants will not reach their potential. In Africa, water can be scarce or expensive, so efficiency is critical. Fertigation is the practice of applying fertiliser through the irrigation system. It saves labour and delivers nutrients directly to the root zone. Irrigation is the first topic. Greenhouse crops need consistent moisture. Too little water causes wilting, flower drop, and small fruit. Too much water causes root diseases, nutrient leaching, and poor growth. The best irrigation method for greenhouses is drip irrigation. Drip lines or drip tape deliver water slowly and directly to each plant. This saves water, reduces weeds, and keeps leaves dry. Drip irrigation can reduce water use by 30 to 50 percent compared to overhead watering. It also reduces disease because leaves stay dry. To set up drip irrigation, you need a water source, a pump or gravity tank, a filter, a main line, and drip lines. A filter is essential because drip emitters clog easily. Clean the filter regularly. A gravity system works if your water tank is at least 2 metres above the ground. This is common in smallholder greenhouses. A pump is needed if you have a large greenhouse or low water pressure. Drip lines are placed along each row. For tomatoes and peppers, one drip line per row is enough. For cucumbers, you may need two lines. For leafy greens, you can use drip tape with emitters every 20 to 30 centimetres. Watering schedule depends on crop, stage, soil, and weather. In hot dry weather, you may water twice a day. In cool weather, once every two or three days. Check the soil by digging with your hand. If it is dry 5 centimetres below the surface, water. If it is wet, wait. Do not water on a fixed schedule without checking. Overwatering is a common mistake. It wastes water, leaches nutrients, and causes root rot. Mulching helps conserve moisture. Use dry grass, straw, or plastic mulch. Mulch also reduces weeds and keeps soil temperature stable. Fertigation is the second topic. Plants need nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium, and micronutrients. In a greenhouse, plants grow fast and produce heavy yields, so they need more nutrients than in open fields. You can apply fertiliser as a solid before planting and then as a liquid through the drip system. Common fertilisers include NPK blends, calcium nitrate, potassium nitrate, and magnesium sulphate. For leafy greens, use more nitrogen. For fruiting crops like tomatoes and peppers, use more potassium and calcium during fruiting. Calcium prevents blossom end rot, a common problem in tomatoes and peppers. Magnesium prevents yellowing between leaf veins. Micronutrients such as iron, zinc, and boron are needed in small amounts. If you use soil, test it first. Soil tests are available from agricultural extension offices and private labs. The test tells you which nutrients are lacking and what to add. If you use hydroponics, you must mix a complete nutrient solution. This is more technical. Start with a pre-mixed hydroponic fertiliser. Follow the instructions. Measure the electrical conductivity, or EC, of the solution. EC measures the total salt concentration. Most vegetables grow well at EC 1.5 to 2.5 millisiemens per centimetre. Measure pH as well. Most vegetables prefer pH 5.8 to 6.5. Adjust with pH up or pH down products. Fertigation scheduling depends on crop stage. Seedlings need weak solutions. Vegetative growth needs more nitrogen. Flowering and fruiting need more potassium and calcium. Reduce nitrogen near harvest for leafy greens to avoid excessive nitrate accumulation. Do not apply fertiliser to dry soil. Water first, then apply fertiliser, then water again to flush the lines. This prevents salt burn. Flush drip lines weekly to prevent clogging. Collect drainage water if possible and check its EC. If drainage EC is much higher than the input EC, salts are building up. Leach the soil with clean water. In hydroponics, replace the nutrient solution regularly. Water quality matters. Test your water for salinity, pH, and contaminants. If water is salty, choose salt-tolerant crops such as cucumbers or beets. If water is alkaline, adjust pH. Rainwater harvesting is a good option. Collect roof runoff in tanks. This reduces dependence on boreholes and municipal water. In many African countries, water is a limiting factor. Drip irrigation and mulching are essential. Solar-powered pumps are increasingly affordable. They reduce fuel costs and are reliable. For small greenhouses, a simple gravity system with a 200-litre drum works well. You can mix fertiliser in the drum and let it flow through the drip lines. This is called a gravity-fed fertigation system. It is cheap and effective. Record keeping is important. Record how much water you use, how much fertiliser you apply, and the EC and pH readings. Also record crop performance. This helps you fine-tune your programme. Over time, you will learn what works best for your conditions. Train your workers on irrigation and fertigation. They should know how to check the system for leaks, clean filters, and measure EC and pH. A simple checklist helps. Finally, think about the cost. Drip irrigation and fertigation have upfront costs, but they save water, fertiliser, and labour. They also increase yields and quality. The return on investment is usually high. In summary, use drip irrigation, mulch, and regular soil checks. Apply fertiliser through the drip system based on crop stage. Monitor EC and pH. Keep records. Efficient water and nutrient management is the backbone of a profitable greenhouse.

5. Lesson 5: Harvesting, Post-Harvest Handling, and Marketing Greenhouse Vegetables

Welcome to Lesson 5 of Module 9. In this lesson we cover harvesting, post-harvest handling, and marketing. You have invested in your greenhouse, managed climate, controlled pests, and irrigated carefully. Now you must harvest at the right time, handle produce gently, and sell it profitably. In Africa, post-harvest losses can be 30 to 50 percent for vegetables. Reducing these losses is one of the fastest ways to increase income. Harvesting at the right time is the first step. Each crop has its own maturity signs. Tomatoes are harvested when they are mature green, pink, or red, depending on the market. For local markets, red ripe tomatoes are preferred. For distant markets, harvest at mature green or breaker stage so they can travel without damage. Peppers are harvested when they reach full size and colour. Green peppers are harvested before they turn red or yellow. Cucumbers are harvested when they are young and tender, usually 15 to 20 centimetres long. Do not let them grow too large or they become bitter and seedy. Leafy greens are harvested when leaves are young and tender, before they become tough. Herbs are harvested before flowering for best flavour. Harvest in the cool of the morning. This reduces heat stress on the produce. Use clean, sharp tools. For tomatoes and peppers, use scissors or pruners to cut the stem, leaving a short stalk. For cucumbers, cut the stem. For leafy greens, cut the whole plant or pick outer leaves. Do not pull plants roughly. Handle produce gently. Bruises and cuts reduce shelf life and quality. Use clean crates or baskets. Do not pile produce too high. Do not throw produce into containers. Post-harvest handling is the second step. After harvest, move produce to a cool, shaded area immediately. Do not leave it in the sun. Heat causes rapid deterioration. If possible, use a cool room or evaporative cooler. An evaporative cooler is a simple structure with wet pads and a fan. It can reduce temperature by 5 to 10 degrees Celsius. This is very useful in hot African climates. Wash produce if needed. For leafy greens, wash in clean water. You can add a small amount of vinegar or food-grade bleach to the water to reduce microbes. Dry gently. For tomatoes and peppers, do not wash unless necessary. Washing can remove the natural waxy coating and increase spoilage. Grade produce by size, colour, and quality. Grading allows you to sell premium produce at higher prices and lower grades at lower prices. It also builds trust with buyers. Use clean packaging. For supermarkets and hotels, use plastic punnets, bags, or boxes with labels. For local markets, use reusable crates or baskets. Packaging protects produce and makes it easier to handle. Store produce properly. Different crops have different storage needs. Tomatoes store best at 12 to 15 degrees Celsius. Peppers at 7 to 10 degrees. Cucumbers at 10 to 12 degrees. Leafy greens at 0 to 4 degrees with high humidity. Herbs at 0 to 4 degrees. If you do not have a cold room, keep produce in the coolest place available and sell quickly. Do not store tomatoes with leafy greens because tomatoes produce ethylene, which speeds up yellowing of greens. Marketing is the third step. Know your market. There are several options: local markets, supermarkets, hotels, restaurants, schools, hospitals, and direct sales to consumers. Each has different requirements. Local markets are easy to enter but prices are low and competition is high. Supermarkets pay better but require consistent quality, quantity, and packaging. Hotels and restaurants pay well but need regular delivery and specific varieties. Schools and hospitals buy in bulk but may have delayed payments. Direct sales, such as vegetable boxes or online orders, can be profitable but require marketing effort. Choose markets that match your production capacity. If you have a small greenhouse, focus on high-value markets such as hotels, restaurants, and direct consumers. If you have a large greenhouse, you can supply supermarkets and wholesalers. Build relationships. Visit buyers in person. Bring samples. Be reliable. Deliver on time. Communicate openly about challenges. A good relationship can lead to contracts and repeat business. Pricing is important. Know your cost of production. Add a profit margin. Check market prices regularly. Do not underprice your produce. It hurts you and other farmers. Do not overprice either. Find a fair price that covers costs and gives a reasonable profit. Value addition can increase income. For example, you can wash and pack leafy greens in ready-to-use bags. You can make tomato sauce or dried tomatoes. You can bundle herbs. Value addition requires extra labour and packaging, but it can raise prices. Branding helps. Give your farm a name. Use a simple logo. Label your produce. This builds recognition and trust. Many consumers prefer local, fresh, and safe vegetables. Use that in your marketing. Record keeping is essential. Record harvest dates, quantities, grades, prices, and buyers. This helps you plan production and negotiate better prices. It also helps you see which crops and markets are most profitable. Reduce losses by planning harvests carefully. Do not harvest more than you can sell or store. Harvest in batches. Keep records of losses and learn from them. Train your workers on harvesting and handling. A bruised tomato is worth less than a perfect one. A wilted leafy green may not sell at all. Teach them to handle produce like eggs. Finally, think about transport. Use clean, covered vehicles. Avoid overcrowding. Transport in the cool of the day or at night. If you deliver to a city, plan your route to avoid traffic and heat. In summary, harvest at the right time, handle gently, cool quickly, grade, pack, and market strategically. Good post-harvest handling and marketing turn a good crop into good money. Your greenhouse is a business. Treat every step from seed to sale as part of your profit.

<h3>Why This Reference Matters for African Growers</h3>

<p>Greenhouse vegetable production in Africa is expanding rapidly around peri-urban corridors Nairobi, Accra, Lagos, Addis Ababa, Kigali, and Lusaka driven by urban demand for tomatoes, capsicum, cucumbers, leafy greens, and herbs. Protected cultivation can deliver two to four times the yield of open-field production while using 4060% less water. However, profitability depends on disciplined management of climate, pests, nutrition, and post-harvest handling. The benchmarks below are drawn from FAO, ILRI, and university extension sources and are intended for practical decision-making.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #b00020;background:#fff0f0;padding:14px;margin:18px 0;font-family:Arial;font-size:14px;">
<strong style="color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING PESTICIDE AND FOOD SAFETY</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Pesticides used in greenhouses are concentrated in enclosed spaces, increasing inhalation and dermal exposure risk. Always wear the full PPE specified on the label (gloves, respirator, goggles, coveralls). Never enter a treated greenhouse before the stated re-entry interval (REI) commonly 1224 hours for many products. Observe pre-harvest intervals (PHI) strictly; residue violations can result in rejection at supermarkets and export markets, and pose serious health risks to consumers. Store all chemicals in a locked, ventilated cabinet away from food, feed, and children. In case of poisoning, contact your nearest poison control centre or health facility immediately and bring the product label. Follow WHO (2020) recommendations on safe use of pesticides in agriculture.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ol style="font-family:Arial;font-size:14px;line-height:1.7;">
<li><strong>Climate:</strong> Maintain daytime temperature 2228C and relative humidity 6075%. Open ridge vents by 9:00 a.m.; close by 5:00 p.m. to trap warmth.</li>
<li><strong>Irrigation:</strong> Check drip emitters weekly for clogging. Apply 46 L/m/day for fruiting crops in hot weather; reduce by 30% in cool, cloudy periods.</li>
<li><strong>Fertigation:</strong> Use EC 1.82.5 mS/cm and pH 5.86.2 for tomatoes and peppers. Flush lines weekly to prevent salt accumulation.</li>
<li><strong>Scouting:</strong> Inspect 20 plants per 500 m twice weekly. Record pest counts and act when thresholds are reached (e.g., 5 whiteflies per leaf).</li>
<li><strong>Sanitation:</strong> Remove and destroy diseased plant material immediately. Disinfect tools and boots between rows. Keep a footbath at the entrance.</li>
<li><strong>Harvest:</strong> Harvest in early morning. Use clean, ventilated crates. Pre-cool to 1012C for leafy greens and 1215C for tomatoes within 2 hours.</li>
<li><strong>Records:</strong> Log every input (seed, fertilizer, chemical, water), date, rate, and operator. Traceability is required by most supermarket buyers.</li>
<li><strong>Financials:</strong> Track cost per kg produced monthly. Target a cost of production below 55% of farm-gate price to remain profitable.</li>
</ol>
