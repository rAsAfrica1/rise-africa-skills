Staff, Training & Pay

8 lessons · 30 quiz questions · Business

1 Key roles in a food business

As you grow, roles become clear. Typical small food business:

  • Production workers — make the product (peel, cook, fill, seal)
  • Packer/labeller — package and label
  • Cleaner — maintains hygiene standards
  • Salesperson — finds new customers
  • Driver — deliveries
  • Bookkeeper — records and payments
  • Manager — coordinates everyone

You (owner) start by doing everything. Hire one role at a time as volume demands.

💡 First hire should be a cleaner/production worker — frees you to sell and manage.

2 Hiring food handlers

Food handlers have special requirements. Hire carefully.

Look for:

  • Reliability — shows up on time, every day
  • Attention to detail — notices when something's wrong
  • Cleanliness — personal hygiene comes naturally
  • Honesty — food handling is a trusted position
  • Team player — works well with others

How to hire:

  1. Ask for referrals from friends, family, community
  2. Interview in person — ask about previous work and habits
  3. Ask for references and check them
  4. Start with a 2-week trial period
  5. Sign a written contract (job, hours, pay, conduct)

Ask about medical history — some countries require a food handler's medical certificate.

3 Hygiene and process training

Every new hire gets trained on day one. No exceptions.

Training covers:

  • Personal hygiene rules (see Module 44)
  • Handwashing technique — demonstrate and check
  • Cleaning and sanitising procedures
  • Handling raw vs cooked food
  • Temperature control
  • Waste handling
  • Emergency procedures (fire, injury, spill)
  • The specific process for their role

Document training. Have the worker sign that they understood. Retrain monthly on one key topic.

4 Pay structure and incentives

Fair pay attracts and keeps good staff. But fair pay alone isn't enough.

Pay types:

  • Hourly — best for variable work (production varies)
  • Daily — common in Africa; simple but no incentive to work efficiently
  • Monthly salary — for office staff and managers
  • Piece rate — pay per unit produced (e.g. $0.05 per jar filled)
  • Commission — for sales staff (% of revenue)

Incentives that work:

  • Bonus for hitting production targets
  • Bonus for zero safety incidents per month
  • Bonus for zero waste over a week
  • End-of-year bonus if profitable
  • Free product to take home (small cost, big loyalty)
💡 Pay above minimum wage. It attracts better workers and reduces turnover. Replacing staff costs more than paying them well.

5 Shift planning

Plan shifts so production runs smoothly without burnout.

Single shift: 8 hours, one team. Best for under 500 units/day.

Two shifts: Morning and afternoon/evening. Doubles capacity without new equipment.

Three shifts: Round-the-clock. Only for large operations.

Rest days: At least one full rest day per week. Rotate so team isn't exhausted.

Breaks: 15 minutes every 4 hours, 30–60 minutes for a meal. Enforce breaks — tired workers make mistakes and get injured.

6 Discipline and dismissal

Handle problems early. Small issues become big ones if ignored.

Progressive discipline:

  1. Verbal warning — talk privately, document
  2. Written warning — formal, in writing, signed
  3. Final warning — last chance, clearly stated
  4. Dismissal — end employment

Instant dismissal (serious offences): theft, violence, food tampering, working drunk/high, gross negligence.

Document everything. Keep a file for each employee. Follow local labour laws — wrongful dismissal can lead to legal claims.

Pay final wages and any accrued leave when someone leaves.

7 Retention and motivation

Replacing workers costs time and money. Retain your best people.

What keeps good workers:

  • Fair pay, paid on time
  • Respect — greet by name, listen to ideas
  • Safe working conditions
  • Clear expectations and feedback
  • Training opportunities
  • Recognition for good work (public praise)
  • Small perks — tea break, celebration on birthdays

Ask staff for input — they see problems you don't. Simple ideas often save the most money.

💡 Invest in your best people. A good worker is worth 3 average ones. Loyalty is built, not bought.

8 When to promote or hire a manager

As business grows, you can't manage everything.

Promote from within when:

  • Someone consistently performs well
  • They show leadership (help others, take initiative)
  • They understand your standards
  • You trust them with responsibility

Hire externally when:

  • You need specialist skills (accounting, food tech)
  • Nobody internal is ready
  • The role is too different from production

Signs you need a manager:

  • You work 60+ hours a week
  • Decisions pile up while you're away
  • You can't leave for even one day
  • Quality drops when you're not watching

Hire or promote a manager before you burn out. A good manager lets you focus on growth.
