🧠 Brain Opener

A buyer in Europe will not buy your organic seed without a certificate. But you cannot afford 15,000 dollars for third-party certification. There is a second road, and it is legal, cheap, and works.

  • What is the difference between third-party certification and PGS?
  • What does the 2 to 3 year conversion period mean?
  • What does certification actually cost?
🎁 Reveal a strong answer

Third-party certification is done by an international company - it costs 8,000 to 15,000 dollars in year one. PGS (Participatory Guarantee System) is done by a local group of farmers inspecting each other - it costs 100 to 500 dollars per year. The conversion period is when you farm organically but cannot yet sell as organic. It is 2 to 3 years for land that has used chemicals.

📖 Chipo Chooses the PGS Road

Chipo had a choice to make. She had 1.2 hectares of good land, three years of chemical-free history, and a buyer in Germany who wanted organic tomato seed.

The buyer sent her the certification requirements. She needed either EU organic certification or an accepted equivalent. The cheapest third-party quote she found was 9,000 dollars.

Chipo did not have 9,000 dollars. She did not have 900.

She almost gave up. Then she heard about PGS from a neighbour.

PGS stands for Participatory Guarantee System. It is a local certification system where farmers inspect each other. A regional PGS council reviews and approves. The cost is shared across the group.

Chipo found a PGS group in her district - eight farmers who had started three years before. They met once a month. They inspected each other. They had a small regional fund for administration.

She joined. The annual fee was 180 dollars, split between her and three other new members.

The first inspection was thorough. Two neighbouring farmers walked her whole plot. They checked her compost pile, her inputs record, her seed storage. They took photos. They asked her to explain her pest management.

She passed on the first try. Her certificate arrived from the PGS council in six weeks.

She sent a copy to the German buyer. He reviewed it. He accepted it.

Three months later, Chipo delivered 18 kilograms of PGS-certified organic tomato seed at 90 dollars per kilogram. Total: 1,620 dollars.

Her certification cost for the year: 180 dollars.

She is now the PGS coordinator for her district. The group has grown to 22 farmers.

💡 The Big Idea

You do not need the expensive road to be certified

Most African organic producers start with PGS, not third-party certification. PGS is legal, recognised by many buyers, and costs 20 times less. It builds a local community of farmers who hold each other to a standard. Once you have export contracts, you can upgrade to third-party if the buyer requires it.

📚 Three Deep Lessons

1

The two certification roads

Third-party certification. Companies like Ecocert, Ceres, Control Union, or Soil Association. Independent inspectors. International recognition. Needed for EU, US, and Japanese markets. Cost: 8,000 to 15,000 dollars for year one, then 5,000 to 8,000 per year. Time to complete: 3 to 6 months.

Participatory Guarantee System (PGS). A group of farmers in the same area. They inspect each other. They share costs. They maintain standards together. Recognised by many national organic markets and by some international buyers. Cost: 100 to 500 dollars per year per member. Time to complete: 2 to 4 months.

2

The 2 to 3 year conversion period

You cannot claim organic on day one. The land must have been free of banned chemicals for 2 to 3 years. This is called the conversion period. During conversion, you farm organically but cannot label your produce "organic."

You can label it "in conversion." Some buyers pay a small premium for it. But most do not. The conversion period is a financial sacrifice you must plan for.

The clock starts the day you stop using chemicals. Write that date in a permanent record. It is the beginning of your organic business.

For land that has never used chemicals, the conversion period can be reduced or waived. For land that used chemicals recently, the full 3 years apply.

3

What certification covers and what it does not

Certification covers: how you farm (no banned inputs), how you record (records of every input and process), how you store and process (no contamination), how you label (correct claims), and how you handle complaints.

Certification does not cover: quality of your seed (that is a separate test), market access (you still have to find buyers), or price (you still have to negotiate). Certification is a doorway, not a destination.

The most common mistake: farmers get certified but do not have a buyer. Certification is worth nothing without a market. Find the buyer first, then get the certificate.

🛠 Hands-On - Write Your Certification Plan

You need

  • A notebook
  • A pen
  • A phone or computer with internet
  1. Write down the last 3 years of chemical use on your intended land. When did you last use banned chemicals?
  2. Calculate the conversion period remaining (months until 3 years from that date).
  3. Find the nearest PGS group in your district. Call or visit.
  4. Find the cost of the group annual fee.
  5. Find one third-party certifier that operates in your country. Ask for a quote.
  6. Compare the two options. Write the pros and cons.
  7. Make your decision: PGS first, or third-party first.
Why it works: The certification decision shapes your marketing. Plan it before you commit to a path.

🌍 Real Stories from Africa

Kenya PGS network

Kenya has over 50 PGS groups covering 8,000 farmers. The Kenya Organic Agriculture Network (KOAN) coordinates them. Most PGS-certified farmers sell to local and regional markets. Some export to diaspora markets in Europe.

India PGS exports

India has over 1 million PGS-certified farmers. Indian PGS cotton, spices, and vegetables are sold to European buyers who accept PGS as an equivalent to third-party certification. The PGS model is now being studied for adoption across Africa.

🏆 Project Challenge

Join or Start a PGS

Find a PGS group or start one with neighbours.

  1. Contact your national organic agriculture network (NOAM) for PGS groups in your area.
  2. If a group exists, attend one meeting. Ask the cost, the inspection process, and the buyer list.
  3. If no group exists, find three other organic farmers who want one.
  4. Draft a simple PGS charter together: standards, inspection process, dispute resolution.
  5. Register the group with the national organic network.
  6. Complete your first group inspection.
  7. Apply for your first certificate.
