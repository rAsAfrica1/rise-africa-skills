1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Sand casting is the oldest and most widely used metal casting method in the world. In Africa, it is the backbone of small-scale foundries from Nigeria to South Africa. The process involves pouring molten metal into a cavity shaped in sand, letting it cool, and breaking the sand away to reveal a solid metal part. It works for aluminium, bronze, brass, and cast iron. Why does it matter for you? Because it is cheap, flexible, and uses materials you can find almost anywhere. You can cast pots, machine parts, ploughshares, door hinges, and even engine components. A basic sand casting setup can be built for under $200 using a metal bucket as a furnace, charcoal or coke as fuel, and locally dug clay sand. Unlike welding or machining, sand casting lets you produce complex shapes in one pour. It also allows you to recycle scrap metal, turning old car parts and broken tools into new products. In many African communities, this skill creates immediate income: a single cast aluminium cooking pot sells for $5$15, and a dozen can be made in a day. The key advantage is low capital. You do not need electricity or expensive machines. You need sand, a pattern, a crucible, and heat. This module will teach you every step, from preparing sand to finishing your casting. By the end, you will be able to set up a small foundry in your backyard and start producing items for sale or personal use. The demand for locally made metal goods is high, especially in rural areas where imported items are expensive. Sand casting is not just a trade; it is a pathway to self-employment and community development. Lets get started.

2. Tools and Materials

To start sand casting, you need a few essential tools and materials. First, the flask: a two-part frame (cope and drag) made of wood or metal, typically 30 cm x 40 cm. You can make one from scrap wood. Second, a pattern: a replica of the part you want to cast, usually made of wood, metal, or plastic. It must be slightly larger than the final part to allow for shrinkage (about 1% for aluminium, 2% for brass). Third, sand: you need green sand, a mixture of silica sand (7085%), bentonite clay (1015%), and water (35%). You can mix it yourself: for a 20-litre bucket, use 14 litres of fine river sand, 3 litres of bentonite clay powder, and 1.5 litres of water. Test by squeezing a handful; it should hold shape but not drip water. Fourth, a crucible: a clay-graphite or steel pot to melt metal. A used steel pot works for aluminium. Fifth, a furnace: a simple charcoal or coke furnace made from a metal drum with a hole for air. A hairdryer or hand blower provides air. Sixth, tools: a rammer (wooden or metal), a strike-off bar, a sprue pin, a vent wire, a trowel, and a mallet. For safety, you need leather gloves, a face shield, and a leather apron. You also need a moulding board (flat surface), a sieve for sand, and a metal ladle for pouring. All these can be sourced locally: sand from a riverbank, clay from termite mounds (which is high in bentonite), and scrap metal from mechanics. Total cost: under $150 if you make your own flask and furnace. Keep everything dry and clean. Store sand in a covered bin to maintain moisture. With these tools, you are ready to cast.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps to make a simple sand casting. Step 1: Prepare the pattern. Clean it and apply a thin coat of oil or talcum powder to prevent sand sticking. Place the drag (bottom half of flask) on a flat board. Step 2: Fill the drag. Put the pattern in the drag, face down. Sprinkle facing sand (fine, sieved sand) over the pattern, then fill the drag with green sand. Ram it firmly with a rammer, adding sand in layers. Strike off excess sand with a strike bar. Step 3: Flip the drag. Turn the drag over, place the cope (top half) on it. Insert a sprue pin (a tapered pin) at one end to form the pouring hole. Step 4: Fill the cope. Sprinkle facing sand, then fill with green sand and ram. Remove the sprue pin. Step 5: Vent the mould. Use a vent wire to poke small holes through the sand to the pattern to let gases escape. Step 6: Separate the flask. Carefully lift the cope off the drag. Remove the pattern from the drag. If the pattern is in two parts, remove the bottom part first. Step 7: Cut the runner and gate. Use a trowel to cut a channel from the sprue hole to the mould cavity. Step 8: Close the mould. Put the cope back on the drag, aligning them with pins. Step 9: Melt the metal. In your furnace, melt scrap aluminium or brass in a crucible. Use a blower to reach 700800C for aluminium. Skim off slag with a metal rod. Step 10: Pour. Wearing safety gear, pour the molten metal into the sprue hole quickly and steadily. Fill until the metal appears at the top. Step 11: Cool. Let it cool for at least 30 minutes for small parts. Step 12: Shake out. Break the sand away and remove the casting. Cut off the sprue and gate with a hacksaw. Clean with a wire brush. You now have a finished part.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Even experienced casters make mistakes. Here are the most common and how to fix them. 1. Sand too dry or too wet: If sand crumbles, add water (a little at a time). If it sticks to your hand, add dry sand. Test by squeezing; it should hold shape without leaving wet marks. 2. Weak mould: If the mould collapses when you pour, you did not ram enough. Ram harder, especially around the pattern. Use a mallet to tap the sand down. 3. Rough surface: If the casting has a rough, sandy surface, your sand is too coarse or you did not use facing sand. Sieve sand finer and apply a thin layer of facing sand against the pattern. 4. Misrun (incomplete casting): Metal cools too fast before filling the mould. Fix: preheat the mould slightly, pour faster, or increase metal temperature. Also, make sure the sprue and runner are wide enough. 5. Shrinkage cavities: Metal shrinks as it cools, leaving holes. Fix: add a riser (a reservoir of molten metal) above the mould to feed shrinkage. For aluminium, allow 1% shrinkage in pattern size. 6. Gas porosity (bubbles): Trapped gases cause holes. Fix: vent the mould with a vent wire, and make sure sand is not too wet. Also, melt metal properly and skim slag. 7. Sand inclusions: Loose sand falls into the mould. Fix: clean the mould cavity with a soft brush before closing, and handle the cope carefully. 8. Poor parting line: The two halves of the mould don't align. Fix: use alignment pins on the flask, and check before pouring. 9. Cold shuts: Two streams of metal meet but don't fuse. Fix: pour continuously and quickly, and increase metal temperature. 10. Pattern stuck: If the pattern won't come out, you didn't apply enough parting dust. Use talcum powder or oil. Always practice on scrap before casting a valuable part. Keep a notebook of what works.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety is not optional in foundry work. Molten metal at 700800C can cause severe burns. Follow these rules. 1. Wear protective gear: Leather gloves, a face shield (not just safety glasses), a leather apron, and closed leather boots. Never wear synthetic clothing; it melts. 2. Work in a well-ventilated area: Melting metal releases toxic fumes, especially from painted or oily scrap. Set up your furnace outdoors or in a shed with open doors. 3. Keep water away: Molten metal explodes when it touches water. Dry all tools, crucibles, and moulds before pouring. Never pour on wet ground. 4. Handle crucible carefully: Use tongs designed for crucibles. Preheat the crucible before use to remove moisture. Do not overfill; leave 2 cm of space. 5. Pour steadily: Stand to the side, not directly in front of the mould. Pour in one continuous motion. If metal splashes, step back. 6. Have a fire extinguisher or sand bucket: For metal fires, use dry sand or a Class D extinguisher. Never use water. 7. Keep children and animals away: Set up a barrier. 8. First aid: For burns, cool with clean water for 10 minutes, cover with a sterile cloth, and seek medical help. 9. Best practices: Keep your work area clean and organized. Store sand covered. Label scrap metal. Maintain your furnace and tools. 10. Learn from every pour: Record temperatures, sand mix, and results. Join a local foundry group or online forum for advice. In Africa, many successful foundries started in a backyard with strict safety habits. Your health and life are more important than any casting. Always think before you act. If unsure, don't pour.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it's time to practice. This exercise will guide you through casting a simple aluminium keychain or small plate. You will need: green sand (mixed as in Lesson 2), a two-part flask (or a wooden box), a pattern (a small flat piece of wood or metal, like a 5 cm x 8 cm rectangle), a crucible, a furnace, scrap aluminium, and safety gear. Step 1: Prepare the sand. Mix 14 litres of river sand, 3 litres of bentonite clay, and 1.5 litres of water. Test by squeezing. Step 2: Set up the drag. Place the pattern face down in the drag. Sprinkle facing sand, then fill with green sand. Ram firmly in layers. Strike off excess. Step 3: Flip and set the cope. Place the cope on the drag. Insert a sprue pin 5 cm from the pattern. Fill the cope with sand and ram. Remove the sprue pin. Step 4: Vent the mould. Poke 68 vent holes through the cope sand to the pattern. Step 5: Open and remove pattern. Lift the cope. Remove the pattern. Cut a runner from the sprue hole to the mould cavity. Step 6: Close the mould. Replace the cope and align. Step 7: Melt aluminium. In your furnace, melt about 500 g of scrap aluminium. Use a blower to reach 750C. Skim slag. Step 8: Pour. Wearing gear, pour the molten aluminium into the sprue hole until full. Step 9: Cool for 20 minutes. Step 10: Shake out. Break the sand and remove your casting. Cut off the sprue and gate. File smooth. You now have a small aluminium plate. Inspect it for defects. If it has bubbles or rough spots, note why and try again. Repeat the exercise three times. Each time, aim to improve surface finish and reduce defects. When you can consistently produce a clean casting, try a more complex shape, like a small gear or a nameplate. This is your foundation for a foundry business.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Sand Casting for African Foundry Businesses

This practical reference consolidates the working knowledge a small or medium foundry enterprise needs to run sand casting profitably. It is written for artisans, TVET graduates, and workshop owners across Africa who are moving from theory to production. Sand casting remains the most accessible metal-forming route for jobbing foundries because the moulding material silica sand, clay, and water is locally available in most countries, and the process tolerates a wide range of metals, from aluminium scrap to cast iron and bronze. For a business, the decisive questions are not only "how do I pour metal?" but "how do I achieve repeatable dimensions, low scrap rates, and a cost per kilogram that lets me quote competitively?"

Use this lesson alongside the earlier modules on tools, step-by-step method, and safety. Here the emphasis is on reference data, tolerances, defect diagnosis, and the numbers you need to price work.

SAFETY WARNING:

Molten metal at 7001450 C causes catastrophic burns. Never pour over wet ground or near water entrapped moisture flashes to steam and can explode molten metal into the air. Always wear a face shield, leather apron, leggings, and heat-resistant gloves. Keep a dry sand bucket and a CO or dry-powder extinguisher within reach. In many African workshops, improvised crucibles and open charcoal furnaces are common; these increase the risk of metal splash and carbon monoxide exposure. Work outdoors or with forced ventilation, and never leave a melt unattended.

Costing and Business Notes

To price castings, build a simple cost model: metal cost per kg, melting fuel per kg, sand and binder consumption, labour hours per mould, and a scrap allowance. A realistic scrap rate for a new small foundry is 815 %, falling to 35 % once gating and sand control are stable. Quote with a margin that covers rework. Keep a daily log of metal charged, castings poured, and rejects; this data is your most valuable business asset.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Sand tested for moisture and strength before each moulding run.
  • Patterns checked for draft, shrinkage allowance, and surface finish.
  • Gating and riser system sized for the alloy and section thickness.
  • Moulds vented and cores secured against flotation.
  • Pouring temperature verified with a pyrometer or optical check.
  • Moulds poured on dry, level ground away from moisture.
  • Castings logged with defect type and corrective action taken.
  • Scrap rate reviewed weekly and fed back into pricing.
