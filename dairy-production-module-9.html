1. Milk composition and properties

Milk is about 87% water, 3.5% fat, 3.4% protein, 4.8% lactose (milk sugar), and 0.7% minerals like calcium. These percentages vary by animal: cow milk averages 3.5% fat, goat milk 3.8%, and sheep milk 7%. In Africa, many farmers keep indigenous cattle like Zebu, which produce milk with higher fat (4-5%) but lower volume. Knowing composition helps you decide what products to make. High-fat milk is better for butter and cheese; high-protein milk gives better yogurt.

Milk is a living fluid. It contains beneficial bacteria (lactic acid bacteria) and possibly harmful ones (E. coli, Salmonella). Fresh milk from a healthy cow is nearly sterile, but contamination happens quickly from dirty hands, utensils, or udders. The natural enzymes in milk, like alkaline phosphatase, indicate pasteurization success. When milk is heated to 72Â°C for 15 seconds, this enzyme is destroyed. A simple test using a phosphatase test kit (available in most African agro-vet shops) confirms pasteurization.

Milk also has physical properties. It freezes at -0.5Â°C and boils at 100.2Â°C. Its acidity (pH) is normally 6.6 to 6.8. When bacteria grow, they produce acid, dropping pH. At pH 4.6, milk curdlesâthis is how yogurt and cheese form. You can measure pH with litmus paper or a digital pH meter. Fresh milk should be slightly sweet. If it tastes sour, bacteria have already acted.

In hot African climates, milk spoils within 2 hours if left at 30Â°C. So immediate cooling to 4Â°C or processing is critical. A simple charcoal cooler or a clay pot wrapped in wet cloth can keep milk below 10Â°C for a few hours. Always use clean, dry containers. Never mix morning and evening milk without cooling. Understanding these basics prevents waste and ensures safe dairy products.

2. Basic milk processing: pasteurization and homogenization

Pasteurization kills harmful bacteria and extends shelf life. The two main methods are low-temperature long-time (LTLT) and high-temperature short-time (HTST). For small-scale African dairies, LTLT is easiest: heat milk to 63Â°C for 30 minutes in a double boiler or a stainless steel pot, stirring constantly. Use a dairy thermometer (costs about $5). Never boil milk for pasteurizationâboiling changes taste and destroys vitamins. After heating, cool quickly by placing the pot in cold water or a refrigerator. HTST requires 72Â°C for 15 seconds, then rapid cooling. This needs a plate heat exchanger, which is expensive for smallholders. Many cooperatives in Kenya and Uganda use HTST shared equipment.

After pasteurization, you can homogenize milk to prevent cream from rising. Homogenization forces milk through tiny holes at high pressure (2000-2500 psi), breaking fat globules into small droplets that stay mixed. For small-scale, a hand homogenizer or a blender on high speed for 2-3 minutes can approximate it. Homogenized milk looks whiter and smoother, but it's not essential for safety. Many African consumers prefer non-homogenized milk with a cream layer, so you can skip this step if selling locally.

Always test pasteurization efficiency. The phosphatase test is quick: add a substrate to a milk sample; if it turns blue, pasteurization failed. Also, check for post-pasteurization contamination by keeping milk at 4Â°C and testing after 24 hours. If it spoils early, your hygiene is poor. Clean all equipment with hot water and food-grade sanitizer (e.g., 1 tablespoon bleach per 4 liters water). Rinse well.

Pasteurized milk lasts 2-3 days refrigerated, or 6 months if ultra-high temperature (UHT) treated at 135Â°C for 2 seconds. UHT requires aseptic packaging, common in Tetra Pak. For rural Africa, solar pasteurization using a solar cooker can reach 65Â°C but is unreliable. Stick to firewood or gas for consistent results. Remember: pasteurization does not sterilize; it only reduces pathogens. Always keep milk cold after processing.

3. Production of cheese, yogurt, and butter

Cheese making starts with pasteurized milk heated to 32Â°C. Add rennet (or lemon juice/vinegar for fresh cheese) to curdle. For 10 liters of milk, use 1 teaspoon rennet dissolved in 50 ml cool water. Let sit 30-60 minutes until a clean breakâwhen you insert a knife, the curd splits cleanly. Cut curds into 1 cm cubes, then heat slowly to 39Â°C for 30 minutes, stirring. Drain whey through cheesecloth. For a simple African cheese like 'Wara' (West Africa), press the curd in a mold for 2 hours, then brine in 10% salt water for 24 hours. Store in cool place. For harder cheese, press longer and age 2-4 weeks.

Yogurt is easier. Heat 1 liter of milk to 85Â°C for 10 minutes (this denatures proteins for thicker yogurt). Cool to 45Â°C. Add 2 tablespoons of live yogurt culture (or a sachet of freeze-dried culture). Mix well. Pour into clean jars, cover, and incubate at 40-45Â°C for 4-6 hours. In Africa, use a warm place: a cooler with a bottle of hot water, or a thermos. After incubation, refrigerate. Yogurt lasts 2 weeks. For flavored yogurt, add mashed fruit or honey before chilling.

Butter: Let fresh milk stand for 12 hours at 10Â°C to allow cream to rise. Skim off cream (about 1 liter cream from 10 liters milk). Let cream sit at 15Â°C for 2 hours. Churn in a clean jar by shaking vigorously for 20-30 minutes. When butter granules form, drain buttermilk. Wash butter with cold water until water runs clear. Add salt (2% of butter weight) for preservation. Work salt in with a wooden spoon. Store in a cool place or refrigerator. One liter of cream yields about 500g butter.

All these products require strict hygiene. Use stainless steel or food-grade plastic. Avoid aluminum. Always sterilize utensils by boiling. In hot climates, work quickly and keep everything cool. Start small: 5 liters of milk can make 500g cheese, 1 liter yogurt, or 250g butter. Sell fresh for best income.

4. Quality control in dairy processing

Quality control ensures safe, consistent products. Start with raw milk testing. Use a lactometer to check milk densityâpure milk reads 1.026 to 1.032 g/ml. Watered milk reads lower. A simple alcohol test: mix equal parts milk and 68% ethanol. If it curdles, milk is acidic or has mastitis. Do this daily. Also, smell and taste: fresh milk is sweet, no off-odors. A clot-on-boiling test: boil 5 ml milk in a test tube; if it curdles, it's unstable.

During processing, monitor temperatures with a calibrated thermometer. For pasteurization, check that milk reaches 63Â°C for 30 minutes. After pasteurization, do a phosphatase test weekly. For yogurt, measure pH: should be 4.2-4.6 after incubation. Use pH strips (cost $2 for 100). For cheese, check curd firmness and whey acidity. Keep records: date, batch number, temperature, results. This helps trace problems.

Hygiene is critical. Clean all equipment with hot water (60Â°C) and detergent, then sanitize with 200 ppm chlorine solution (1 tablespoon bleach per 4 liters water). Air-dry on a rack. Workers must wash hands, wear clean aprons, and cover hair. No smoking or eating in processing area. Pests: use screens, traps, and keep garbage away.

Final product testing: For yogurt, check taste, texture, and no separation. For cheese, check rind, no cracks, and smell. For butter, check color (pale yellow), no rancid smell. If any batch fails, discard. Never sell spoiled products. In many African countries, milk processors must test for antibiotic residues (from treated cows). Use a simple test kit (e.g., Delvotest) if selling to formal markets. Record-keeping also helps with regulations. Remember: quality control saves money by reducing waste and builds customer trust.

5. Packaging and storage of dairy products

Packaging protects products from contamination, light, and air. For liquid milk, use clean glass bottles, food-grade plastic bottles, or pouches. In East Africa, many small dairies use plastic pouches sealed with a hot iron or a simple impulse sealer. For yogurt, use plastic tubs with lids or glass jars. For cheese, wrap in wax paper or vacuum-seal. For butter, use greaseproof paper or plastic wrap. Always label with product name, date, and batch number. Avoid reusing containers that held chemicals.

Storage temperatures: Pasteurized milk must be kept at 4Â°C or below. In areas without electricity, use a charcoal cooler: a double-walled clay pot with wet sand between walls. This keeps milk at 10-15Â°C for 12 hours. A more advanced cool chain uses solar-powered refrigerators (e.g., Sure Chill) or biogas-powered fridges. For yogurt, store at 4Â°C; it lasts 2 weeks. Cheese: fresh cheese lasts 1 week refrigerated; hard cheese 1-3 months if kept at 10-12Â°C and high humidity. Butter: refrigerate for 1 month, or freeze for 6 months. In rural Africa, many sell butter within days, so no refrigeration needed if kept cool.

Transport: Use insulated boxes with ice packs. For short trips, wrap products in wet cloth. Never leave milk in direct sun. For long-distance, use refrigerated trucks if available. Many cooperatives in Kenya use milk cans (stainless steel) for collection, then cool at a central depot.

Shelf life without refrigeration: UHT milk lasts 6 months at room temperature if unopened. Once opened, refrigerate and use within 3 days. Dried milk powder lasts 12 months in airtight containers. For yogurt, if no fridge, make it fresh daily. For cheese, salt and pressing increase shelf life. Always rotate stock: first in, first out. Check for spoilage: off-smell, mold, gas. Never sell expired products. Good packaging and storage reduce losses and increase income.

6. Regulations and food safety standards

Every country has food safety laws. In Kenya, the Kenya Bureau of Standards (KEBS) sets dairy standards. In Nigeria, NAFDAC regulates. In South Africa, it's the Department of Agriculture, Land Reform and Rural Development. For small-scale processors, you must register with local health authorities and obtain a food handling certificate. Many African countries require milk to be pasteurized before sale. Raw milk sales are banned in some urban areas. Check with your local public health office.

Key regulations: Pasteurized milk must have less than 10,000 colony-forming units (CFU) per ml of total bacteria, and no coliforms. For yogurt, live cultures must be present (at least 10^6 CFU/g). For cheese, moisture and salt levels are specified. For butter, fat content must be at least 80%. Labels must show product name, ingredients, net weight, production date, expiry date, and manufacturer address. In East Africa, the East African Community (EAC) harmonized standards for dairy products. For export, you need HACCP (Hazard Analysis Critical Control Point) certification.

Food safety practices: Use potable water. Never use raw milk for cheese unless you pasteurize it first. Keep animals away from processing area. Vaccinate workers against typhoid. Train staff on hygiene. Maintain cold chain. Document everything. In case of a recall, you must trace back to the batch.

Penalties for non-compliance include fines, closure, or imprisonment. But compliance opens markets: supermarkets, hotels, and exporters require certified products. Many African governments offer free training through extension officers. Join a dairy cooperative to share costs of testing and certification. For small-scale, start by meeting basic hygiene: clean premises, safe water, and correct pasteurization. Then work towards formal certification. Remember: safe dairy products protect your customers and your business.

3. Quality Control in Dairy Processing

Quality control is where African processors win or lose contracts with supermarkets, hotels, and institutional buyers. The two most common failure points are antibiotic residues (from treated cows not withheld properly) and high bacterial counts caused by poor chilling or dirty equipment. Both can be managed with simple, low-cost testing: the alcohol test, clot-on-boiling test, and rapid resazurin or methylene blue reduction tests. For compliance, processors should maintain a batch log recording temperature, pH, and test results.

Never process or sell milk from cows fed mouldy feed or silage. Aflatoxin M1, a carcinogenic mycotoxin, passes directly into milk and is not destroyed by pasteurisation, boiling, or fermentation. In tropical African conditions, poor feed storage (maize silage, cottonseed, groundnut cake) is a major risk. Test feed and milk where possible, reject suspect deliveries, and never blend contaminated milk with clean batches. This is a food safety and legal liability issue, not just a quality one.

4. Packaging, Storage, and Regulatory Compliance

Packaging choices must balance cost, shelf life, and market expectations. In many African markets, sachet packaging (HDPE or laminated film) dominates the low-income segment, while PET bottles and Tetra Pak-style cartons serve premium urban buyers. Cold chain integrity is the single biggest determinant of shelf lifeâevery hour above 8Â°C shortens it. Regulatory compliance varies by country but is converging around the East African Community standards (EAS) and national bureaus such as KEBS (Kenya), TBS (Tanzania), UNBS (Uganda), and RSB (Rwanda). Registration, milk grading, and hygiene certificates are mandatory for formal retail supply.
