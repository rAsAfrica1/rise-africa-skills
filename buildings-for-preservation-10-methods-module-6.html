Lessons

1. When to Use Cement vs. Traditional Materials

When repairing an old building in Africa, you must decide whether to use modern cement or traditional materials like mud, lime, or cow dung. Traditional materials are breathable and flexible, but they are weak against water and heavy loads. Cement is strong, water-resistant, and quick to set, but it can crack old walls if used incorrectly. Use cement when the damage is structural: cracked foundation, broken floor slab, or a wall carrying a heavy roof. For example, if a mud wall has a deep hole from water erosion, cement mortar (1 part cement to 4 parts sand) can fill it, but first remove all loose mud and dampen the area. Traditional materials work better for small patches on heritage buildings where you want to match the original look. In a busy market, cement is preferred for floors because it handles heavy foot traffic and spills. However, never use cement on soft, historic mud walls without a lime underlayit will trap moisture and rot the wall. A good rule: if the repair must bear weight or resist water, use cement. If the repair is cosmetic and the building is made of earth, use mud or lime. Always test a small area first. In Africa, cement is widely available and cheap, but traditional materials are often free. Choose based on strength, not habit. For a 1-square-meter patch, you need about 10 kg of cement and 40 kg of sand. Mix only what you can use in 30 minutes. Remember: cement sets hard and does not forgive mistakes. Traditional materials can be reworked. So for critical structural repairs, cement is your friend. For delicate heritage surfaces, stick with what was there before.

2. Mixing Concrete for Small Repairs

Mixing concrete for small repairs is not the same as mixing for a foundation. You need a strong, workable mix that sticks to old concrete. The standard mix for repairs is 1 part cement to 2 parts sand to 3 parts gravel (by volume). For a small patch, use a wheelbarrow or a clean bucket. First, measure 1 bucket of cement, 2 buckets of clean river sand (no clay), and 3 buckets of gravel (size 10-20 mm). Mix dry ingredients on a clean surface or in a wheelbarrow until the color is uniform. Then add water slowlyabout 1.5 to 2 buckets for the whole mixuntil it feels like thick peanut butter. Too much water weakens the concrete; too little makes it crumble. In hot African climates, work in the shade and mix small batches. A 50-kg bag of cement makes about 0.35 cubic meters of concrete, enough for a patch 1 meter by 1 meter by 10 cm deep. For small repairs, you can use a 1:3 cement-sand mortar without gravel for thin patches (less than 5 cm). Always add a bonding agent or a cement slurry (cement mixed with water to paint consistency) on the old surface before applying new concrete. This helps the new concrete stick. Use a trowel or a float to press the mix into the hole. Do not over-trowelit brings water to the surface and weakens the repair. Let it set for 24 hours before walking on it. In rural areas, if gravel is scarce, use crushed stone or even broken bricks. But never use dirty sandwash it if needed. Mix only what you need; concrete that sits for over an hour becomes useless. For a small repair, 10 kg of cement, 20 kg of sand, and 30 kg of gravel is enough. Always wear gloves and a maskcement dust is harmful.

3. Patching Concrete Floors and Walls

Patching concrete floors and walls is a common repair in African buildings. Start by cleaning the area. Remove all loose concrete, dust, and oil with a wire brush and water. For floors, chip the edges of the hole with a hammer and chisel to make a square or rectangular shapethis gives the patch a key. Undercut the edges slightly so the new concrete locks in. For walls, do the same, but be careful not to break through. Then dampen the old concrete with waterit should be wet but not dripping. Paint a bonding slurry (cement + water + a little sand) on the surface. For floors, mix a stiff concrete: 1 cement : 2 sand : 3 gravel. For walls, use a richer mortar: 1 cement : 2 sand. Press the mix into the hole with a trowel, building up in layers no thicker than 5 cm. Compact each layer with a wooden float or a piece of wood. For deep holes, fill halfway, let it set for 2 hours, then fill the rest. Finish the surface with a steel trowel for a smooth floor, or a wood float for a rough wall. For floors in a busy market, add a hardener or use a 1:1.5:2.5 mix for extra strength. After patching, cover with a plastic sheet or wet cloth for 3 days to cure. Do not let it dry too fastcracks will form. In hot sun, wet the patch twice a day. For a 1-square-meter floor patch 5 cm deep, you need about 25 kg of cement, 50 kg of sand, and 75 kg of gravel. For a wall patch, 10 kg of cement and 20 kg of sand is enough. Always check that the patch is level with the surrounding surface. If not, grind it down after 24 hours. Remember: a good patch lasts for years; a rushed patch fails in weeks.

4. Reinforcing Concrete with Steel Bars

Concrete is strong in compression but weak in tension. Steel bars (rebar) take the tension, so you must reinforce any repair that will bear heavy loads or resist cracking. For small repairs like a floor patch, you may not need rebar unless the hole is larger than 30 cm or near a vehicle path. But for structural repairscracked beams, columns, or foundationrebar is essential. Use bars of 8 mm or 10 mm diameter for most repairs. Clean the old concrete and expose any existing rebar. Remove rust with a wire brush and paint with a rust converter or cement slurry. Cut new bars to fit, leaving 5 cm of cover (concrete over the steel) on all sides. Tie bars together with thin wire (16-gauge) at intersections. For a floor patch, place a grid of bars 15 cm apart, supported on small stones or plastic chairs so they sit in the middle of the concrete. For a wall patch, drill holes into the old concrete and insert bars, then epoxy or cement them in place. Overlap bars by at least 40 times the bar diameter (e.g., 40 cm for a 10 mm bar). In Africa, rebar is available in most towns; if not, use galvanized wire mesh for light repairs. Never use bamboo or wood as reinforcementthey rot. After placing rebar, mix and pour concrete as usual. Vibrate or compact the concrete to remove air pockets around the bars. For a 1-square-meter patch with rebar, you need about 5 meters of 10 mm bar and 1 kg of tying wire. Always wear gloves when handling rebarit can cut. If the repair is near salt water or sewage, use stainless steel or epoxy-coated bars. For most market floors, ordinary mild steel is fine. Remember: rebar must be clean, tied, and covered by at least 2.5 cm of concrete. Otherwise, it will rust and crack the repair.

5. Curing and Finishing Concrete Surfaces

Curing is the most important step in concrete repair, yet many people skip it. Curing means keeping the new concrete wet for several days so it gains strength. If concrete dries too fast, it shrinks and cracks, and the repair fails. After finishing the surface, cover it with a plastic sheet, wet burlap, or a layer of wet sand. In hot African climates, start curing as soon as the concrete is hard enough not to be damagedusually 2 to 4 hours after pouring. Keep it wet for at least 7 days for floors and 3 days for small patches. If you cannot cover it, wet the surface with a hose or watering can twice a day. Do not let it dry out. For finishing, use a wood float for a rough, non-slip surface on walls or outdoor floors. Use a steel trowel for a smooth floor inside a shop. For a busy market floor, a broom finish gives good grip. Finish edges with an edging tool. After 24 hours, you can walk on it lightly, but wait 7 days before heavy loads. If the weather is very hot, cure at night when it is cooler. You can also use curing compounds (a liquid that seals in moisture) if water is scarce. In rural areas, wet sacks or banana leaves work well. Test the repair after 7 days: tap it with a hammera dull sound means it is solid. A hollow sound means it did not bond. If it fails, remove and redo. For a 1-square-meter patch, you need about 20 liters of water per day for curing. Never use seawater for curingsalt weakens concrete. Always keep the surface damp, not flooded. Curing is not optional; it is the difference between a repair that lasts 2 years and one that lasts 20.

6. Case Study: Repairing a Concrete Building in a Busy Market

In Kumasi, Ghana, a 40-year-old concrete market building had a cracked floor slab and spalling columns. The market was busy, so repairs had to be done at night. The team started by assessing the damage: a 3-meter by 2-meter floor area was broken, and two columns had exposed rebar. They decided to use modern cement repairs. First, they cleaned the area: removed loose concrete with chisels and wire brushes, and washed with water. For the columns, they chipped away all cracked concrete, treated the rusted rebar with a wire brush and cement slurry, and added new 10 mm bars tied to the old ones with wire. They built wooden formwork around the columns. For the floor, they cut the edges square and undercut them. They mixed concrete: 1 part cement, 2 parts sand, 3 parts gravel. For the columns, they used a richer mix: 1:1.5:3 with a plasticizer to make it flow. They applied a bonding slurry to all old surfaces. They poured the columns first, compacting with a steel rod to remove air bubbles. Then they patched the floor in 5 cm layers, compacting each with a wooden float. After finishing, they covered everything with plastic sheets and wet burlap. They cured for 7 days, wetting twice a day. The market was closed from 10 PM to 5 AM, so they worked in shifts. Total materials: 15 bags of cement, 2 cubic meters of sand, 3 cubic meters of gravel, 20 meters of rebar, and 5 kg of tying wire. Cost: about $500 USD. After 7 days, they removed the formwork and checked: the columns were solid, the floor was level. They applied a curing compound to the floor for extra protection. Six months later, the repair was still strong, with no cracks. The key lessons: work in cool hours, use bonding agents, compact well, and never skip curing. The market vendors were happy because the repair did not disrupt their business. This method can be used in any African market with similar problems.

<h2>Practical Reference: Modern Cement and Concrete Repairs</h2>

<p>In African market towns and fast-growing cities, concrete is the backbone of shops, warehouses, stalls, and homes. When concrete cracks, spalls, or crumbles, the repair decision is rarely just technical it is commercial. A landlord in Kumasi, a hardware supplier in Nairobi, or a contractor in Lagos all face the same question: patch it now with modern cement, or rebuild with traditional materials? This reference gives you the practical numbers, mixes, and methods to make that call profitably.</p>

<h3>Why Modern Cement Beats Traditional Materials in Most Repairs</h3>

<p>Traditional materials mud, lime, dung, and plant-fibre mortars remain useful for heritage buildings and low-cost rural housing. But for load-bearing concrete in commercial buildings, modern Portland cement (CEM I or CEM II) offers three business advantages: faster strength gain, predictable quality, and compatibility with steel reinforcement. In a busy market, downtime is money. A concrete floor patched with modern cement can reopen to traders in 2448 hours; a lime-based repair may need weeks.</p>

<h3>Mixing Concrete for Small Repairs The Numbers That Matter</h3>

<p>Most repair failures in African markets come from bad mixing, not bad cement. The golden rule: keep the water-to-cement (w/c) ratio below 0.50. Extra water makes the mix easier to spread but cuts strength by up to 40%. For small patches, use a bucket or wheelbarrow, not a shovel guess.</p>

<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#333;">Wet cement is highly alkaline (pH ~13) and can cause severe skin burns within minutes. Dry cement dust contains crystalline silica, which causes silicosis and lung cancer. In hot African conditions, sweat increases skin contact and risk.</p>
<ul style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#333;">
<li>Always wear waterproof gloves, long sleeves, and safety goggles.</li>
<li>Use a P2/N95 dust mask when mixing dry cement or cutting concrete.</li>
<li>Wash any cement contact with clean water immediately do not use vinegar or lemon.</li>
<li>Keep children and traders away from mixing areas in busy markets.</li>
<li>Store cement off the ground and away from moisture to prevent lumps and waste.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Patching Floors and Walls Step by Step</h3>

<p><strong>Step 1 Prepare the substrate.</strong> Break out all loose concrete until you reach sound material. Cut the edges square (minimum 10 mm deep) feathered edges fail. Chip the surface to a rough profile (about 35 mm deep) so the repair mortar can key in. Remove dust with a stiff brush and clean water. Do not use oil or diesel as a release agent.</p>

<p><strong>Step 2 Saturate, then dry.</strong> Soak the prepared area with clean water until it stops absorbing, then remove standing water. The substrate should be damp but not shiny. This prevents the repair mortar from drying out too fast a common cause of cracking in hot climates.</p>

<p><strong>Step 3 Apply bonding slurry.</strong> Mix pure cement with water to a paint-like consistency. Brush it into the prepared area. Apply the repair mortar while the slurry is still wet if it dries, reapply.</p>

<p><strong>Step 4 Place the mortar.</strong> Mix in small batches (1020 kg) so you can place it within 30 minutes. Compact firmly with a trowel or wooden float, working from the centre outward. For deep patches (over 40 mm), add a steel rebar grid see the diagram above. For floors, finish with a steel float for a hard, smooth surface. For walls, use a wooden float to leave a slightly rough texture that takes paint or plaster.</p>

<p><strong>Step 5 Cure properly.</strong> This is where most African repairs fail. In 35C heat, concrete can lose strength if it dries too fast. Cover the patch with a plastic sheet, wet burlap, or damp sand for at least 7 days. Keep it moist do not let it dry out. For floors in busy markets, use a curing compound if you cannot keep the area wet.</p>

<h3>Reinforcing with Steel Bars When and How</h3>

<p>If the repair depth exceeds 40 mm, or if the original concrete contained steel, you must replace the reinforcement. Use clean, rust-free rebar (812 mm is typical for small repairs). Cut it to fit the patch, leaving at least 20 mm of concrete cover on all sides. Tie bars with galvanised wire at 200 mm centres. For columns, overlap the new bars with the existing steel by at least 40 times the bar diameter (e.g., 400 mm for a 10 mm bar).</p>

<p>In coastal African cities like Mombasa, Dar es Salaam, or Lagos, use galvanised or epoxy-coated rebar if the repair is within 1 km of the sea. Salt air corrodes ordinary steel quickly, and a repair that fails in 2 years is a business loss.</p>

<h3>Case Study: Repairing a Concrete Building in a Busy Market</h3>

<p>A two-storey concrete building in Kariakoo Market, Dar es Salaam, had spalled columns and a cracked ground-floor slab. The landlord needed the ground floor back in operation within 3 days. The contractor used the following approach:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Day 1:</strong> Break out loose concrete, cut square edges, clean, and saturate. Apply bonding slurry and place 1:1.5:3 repair mortar with 10 mm rebar dowels into the columns. Cure with wet burlap.</li>
<li><strong>Day 2:</strong> Patch the floor slab with 1:2:3 mortar, steel-float finish. Cover with plastic sheeting.</li>
<li><strong>Day 3:</strong> Remove sheeting, apply curing compound, and allow traders to walk on the floor after 24 hours. Full load (trolleys, sacks) allowed after 7 days.</li>
</ul>

<p>Total material cost: approximately USD 180 for 0.5 m of repair mortar, 12 m of rebar, and bonding agent. The landlord reopened the stall in 3 days instead of the 3 weeks a full rebuild would have taken. The repair has now lasted 6 years with no significant cracking. The key was proper substrate preparation, low w/c ratio, and 7-day moist curing.</p>
