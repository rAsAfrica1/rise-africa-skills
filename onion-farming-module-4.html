1. Direct Seeding vs. Transplanting

When planting onions, you have two main choices: direct seeding or transplanting. Direct seeding means sowing seeds straight into the field where the onions will grow. Transplanting means starting seeds in a nursery bed or seedbed, then moving the young seedlings to the field later. Each method has pros and cons, and your choice depends on your land, labour, and water supply.

Direct seeding is faster and less labour-intensive. You sow seeds directly into prepared soil, spacing them about 1 cm apart in rows 30 cm apart. But onions from direct seeding need very fine, weed-free soil and consistent moisture. Seeds are small and weak; they can be washed away by heavy rain or choked by weeds. Also, you need more seedabout 4-5 kg per hectarebecause not all will germinate. Direct seeding works best in large fields with good irrigation and where labour is scarce.

Transplanting gives you more control. You raise seedlings in a nursery bed for 4-6 weeks, then move them to the field. This lets you start early, protect seedlings from harsh weather, and select only strong plants. You use less seedabout 2-3 kg per hectareand you can prepare the main field while seedlings grow. Transplanting also allows you to space plants precisely, which leads to uniform bulbs. However, it requires more labour: preparing nursery beds, watering, pulling seedlings, and replanting. The shock of transplanting can slow growth if not done carefully.

In Africa, many smallholder farmers prefer transplanting because it gives better control over water and weeds, especially in areas with erratic rainfall. But if you have a large farm, good irrigation, and limited labour, direct seeding can save time and money. Whichever you choose, ensure your soil is loose, fertile, and well-drained. Onions hate compacted soil and waterlogging. Test your choice on a small plot first to see what works best on your farm.

2. Starting Onions from Seed Indoors

Starting onions indoors gives you a head start, especially if your rainy season is short or your field is not ready. You can use seed trays, wooden boxes, or even old plastic containers. The goal is to produce strong, healthy seedlings that are ready to transplant in 4-6 weeks.

First, prepare your seed-starting mix. Use a light, well-draining medium: mix equal parts of river sand, compost, and garden soil. Sterilize the soil if possible by pouring boiling water over it and letting it coolthis kills weed seeds and diseases. Fill your trays or boxes with this mix, leaving about 2 cm at the top. Water the mix thoroughly before sowing.

Sow seeds thinlyabout 1 cm apartand cover with a thin layer of sand or fine soil, no more than 0.5 cm deep. Onion seeds need light to germinate, so don't bury them deep. Water gently with a fine spray to avoid washing seeds away. Cover the trays with a plastic sheet or a light cloth to keep moisture in. Keep the trays in a warm, sunny spot, but protect them from heavy rain. Ideal temperature for germination is 20-25C. Seeds should sprout in 7-14 days.

Once seedlings emerge, remove the cover and place them where they get at least 6 hours of sunlight daily. Water regularly but not excessivelykeep the soil moist, not soggy. If seedlings are too crowded, thin them to 2-3 cm apart to prevent weak, spindly growth. You can feed them with a weak liquid fertilizer (like diluted chicken manure tea) every two weeks. Avoid high-nitrogen fertilizers, which encourage leafy growth instead of strong roots.

Hardening off is crucial before transplanting. About a week before you move them to the field, reduce watering and expose seedlings to more sun and wind gradually. This toughens them up. By 4-6 weeks, seedlings should be pencil-thick and 10-15 cm tall. Then they are ready for the field. Starting indoors gives you healthier plants and a earlier harvest.

3. Transplanting Seedlings to the Field

Transplanting is a critical stepif done poorly, seedlings can die or grow slowly. The best time to transplant is late afternoon or on a cloudy day, so the sun doesn't scorch the young plants. Water the nursery bed thoroughly a few hours before lifting seedlings. This makes it easier to pull them without damaging roots.

Prepare your field at least two weeks before transplanting. Dig the soil to a depth of 20-30 cm, remove weeds and stones, and mix in well-rotted manure or compost (about 2-3 wheelbarrows per 100 square meters). Make raised beds 1 meter wide and 20 cm high if your area gets heavy rain; this improves drainage. Onions hate waterlogged roots. If your soil is acidic, add a little wood ash or lime.

When lifting seedlings, use a small fork or stick to loosen the soil around them. Hold the seedling by the leaf, not the stem, and gently pull. Try to keep a small ball of soil around the roots. If roots are long, trim them to about 2-3 cm with a sharp knifethis encourages new root growth. Sort seedlings: discard any with damaged roots, thick necks, or disease. Only plant the best.

Make holes in the field with a dibber (a pointed stick) or your finger, about 2-3 cm deep. Space holes 10-15 cm apart in rows 20-30 cm apart. Place one seedling per hole, with the roots spread out. Firm the soil around the roots gently but firmlyno air pockets. After planting, water each seedling immediately. Keep the soil moist for the first week. If the sun is strong, provide temporary shade using palm fronds or shade net for 2-3 days.

Transplanting shock can set plants back. To reduce it, avoid planting in hot midday sun, keep roots moist, and water daily until new growth appears. Within a week, you should see new leaves. Then you can reduce watering to twice a week. Proper transplanting gives you uniform stands and better bulb development.

4. Using Sets and Bulbs for Planting

Onions can also be grown from sets (small bulbs) or from mature bulbs. This method is faster than seed, but it has trade-offs. Sets are small onion bulbs, about 1-2 cm in diameter, grown from seed the previous year. They are planted directly into the field and produce mature bulbs in 60-90 days, much quicker than seed (which takes 100-120 days).

To plant sets, prepare your field as for transplanting: loose, fertile, well-drained soil. Plant sets 2-3 cm deep, pointed end up, spaced 10-15 cm apart in rows 20-30 cm apart. Cover with soil and firm gently. Water immediately. Sets are easy to handle and rarely fail, but they are more expensive than seed. Also, if sets are not stored properly, they can bolt (produce flower stalks) instead of forming bulbs. To avoid bolting, choose sets smaller than 2 cm and plant them in cool weather, not during extreme heat.

Planting mature bulbs is less common but possible. You can plant small bulbs from the previous harvest. However, large bulbs tend to bolt and produce seeds rather than a good bulb. If you use bulbs, choose ones that are 2-3 cm across and plant them the same way as sets. They will produce green onions (spring onions) if harvested early, or bulbs if left longer. But bulb-to-bulb planting is not recommended for large-scale production because it's costly and yields are lower.

In Africa, sets are popular in urban gardens and for off-season production because they are quick and reliable. But for commercial farming, seed is more economical. If you use sets, buy certified disease-free sets from a reputable supplier. Never plant sets that are soft, mouldy, or sprouting. Rotate crops: don't plant onions after onions or after garlic, as pests and diseases build up in the soil. Sets and bulbs can be a useful tool in your onion farming, but use them wisely.

5. Spacing and Plant Density

Spacing determines how much space each onion plant has to grow. If plants are too close, they compete for water, nutrients, and sunlight, resulting in small bulbs. If too far apart, you waste land and get lower yields per hectare. The ideal spacing for onions depends on the variety, soil fertility, and market preference.

For bulb onions, the recommended spacing is 10-15 cm between plants and 20-30 cm between rows. This gives a plant density of about 30-40 plants per square meter. In Africa, many farmers use 10 cm x 20 cm, which is about 50 plants per square meter. But on poor soil, wider spacing (15 cm x 30 cm) is better because each plant needs more area to find nutrients. On fertile soil, closer spacing can be used. The goal is to maximize yield without sacrificing bulb size.

For green onions (spring onions), you can plant closer: 5-7 cm between plants and 15-20 cm between rows. This gives higher plant density and more stems per area. For sets, spacing is similar to transplants: 10-15 cm x 20-30 cm. If you are growing for pickling onions (small bulbs), you can plant at 5 cm x 15 cm to get many small bulbs.

To achieve correct spacing, use a measuring stick or a marked rope. Make furrows or holes at the right distance. Don't guessmeasure. After planting, you can mulch between rows to suppress weeds and conserve moisture. Remember that onions have shallow roots, so they don't compete well with weeds. Proper spacing makes weeding easier.

Plant density also affects airflow, which influences disease. Too dense, and fungal diseases like downy mildew spread quickly. Too sparse, and you lose yield. Aim for a uniform stand. If gaps occur, replant with spare seedlings. Consistent spacing leads to uniform bulb size, which fetches better prices at market. So take time to space correctly.

6. Timing Planting for Optimal Bulbing

Onions are day-length sensitive. Bulbingthe process where the plant stops making leaves and starts swelling the bulbis triggered by the length of daylight. Each variety has a critical day length: short-day varieties bulb when days are 10-12 hours, long-day varieties need 14-16 hours. In Africa, most regions near the equator have 12 hours of daylight year-round, so short-day varieties are best. But if you plant at the wrong time, you may get lots of leaves and no bulbs.

Timing planting is crucial. For short-day onions in tropical Africa, plant at the start of the cool dry season, when temperatures are moderate and days are shortening or stable. For example, in Kenya, plant in March-April for harvest in August-September. In Nigeria, plant in October-November for harvest in February-March. Avoid planting in the hottest months, as high temperatures (above 30C) can cause bolting or poor bulb formation. Also, avoid planting too late: if bulbs start forming when days are still long, they may not size up properly.

In areas with distinct rainy and dry seasons, plant onions at the beginning of the dry season, using irrigation. If you plant during heavy rains, you risk root rot and fungal diseases. Also, onions need about 100-120 days from seed to harvest (or 60-90 days from sets). Count backwards from your target harvest date to determine planting time. For example, if you want to harvest in December, plant seeds in August-September.

Soil temperature also matters. Onion seeds germinate best when soil is 15-25C. If soil is too hot, germination is poor. You can start seeds in a nursery and transplant when conditions are better. For sets, plant when soil is at least 10C. In cooler highland areas, plant in early spring to take advantage of longer days. Always check with local extension officers for the best planting calendar for your area. Proper timing ensures good bulbing and high yields.

<h2>Practical Reference: Planting and Transplanting Onions</h2>

<p>Onion (<em>Allium cepa</em>) is one of Africa's most traded horticultural crops, moving through fresh markets, cross-border trade, and processing. For a commercial grower, the planting decision is not merely agronomic it determines your labour bill, your input cost per hectare, your harvest window, and ultimately your ability to hit a market price peak. This lesson is a working reference for the establishment stage: how to choose between direct seeding and transplanting, how to raise or buy seedlings, how to handle sets and bulbs, how to space for the bulb size your buyer wants, and how to time planting so bulbing coincides with the conditions that give you a sellable crop.</p>

<h3>1. Direct Seeding vs. Transplanting</h3>

<p>Direct seeding sows seed straight into the field at final spacing. It suits large, flat, well-prepared land and growers with precision seeders or trained labour for careful sowing. Its advantages are lower labour per hectare and no transplant shock. Its risks are real: onion seed is slow to emerge (1014 days under good conditions), so weeds compete hard; seedbed moisture must be maintained; and thinning is labour-intensive. Thin stands directly reduce marketable yield because onion bulb size is driven by plant density and early competition.</p>

<p>Transplanting raises seedlings in a nursery bed, tray, or seedbed and moves them to the field at 48 weeks. It gives you control over early moisture, weeds, and pests; it concentrates labour into a short, planable window; and it lets you select uniform, vigorous seedlings which matters when your buyer wants a consistent grade. The cost is nursery management, seedling transport, and a transplanting crew. In practice, most African commercial onion growers transplant because it stabilises establishment and makes planting dates predictable. Direct seeding is usually reserved for large mechanised operations or where labour is scarce at transplanting time.</p>

<h3>2. Starting Onions from Seed Indoors or in a Nursery</h3>

<p>Onion seed is short-lived. Buy certified seed with a recent germination test; if you must use your own seed, test germination on a sample before committing field area. Sow in a fine, level, well-drained bed or in trays with a light, fertile medium. Keep the bed moist but not waterlogged onion seedlings are prone to damping-off. Target 46 kg of seed per hectare of transplanted field, though this varies with germination percentage and seedling survival. Seedlings are ready when they are pencil-thick at the base and 1520 cm tall, usually 58 weeks in warm conditions.</p>

<p>Harden seedlings by reducing water and exposing them to full light for 57 days before transplanting. A hardened seedling suffers less transplant shock and resumes growth faster, which shortens the time to bulbing. If you buy seedlings, inspect for uniform height, strong root systems, and no yellowing or pest damage a cheap tray of weak seedlings is expensive in lost yield.</p>

<h3>3. Transplanting Seedlings to the Field</h3>

<p>Transplant in the late afternoon or on a cloudy day to reduce moisture stress. Trim the tops and roots lightly if seedlings are leggy this reduces transpiration and makes handling easier. Set seedlings upright, 23 cm deep; planting too deep can bury the growing point and delay bulbing, while too shallow leaves roots exposed. Firm the soil around each seedling and irrigate immediately. A starter irrigation is not optional: it settles the roots and can make the difference between a 90% and a 60% stand.</p>

<h3>4. Using Sets and Bulbs for Planting</h3>

<p>Sets are small bulbs grown from seed the previous season and planted out to produce a mature bulb. They are faster to establish than transplants and are useful where the growing window is short. However, sets are more expensive per hectare, are prone to bolting if stored or handled poorly, and carry a higher risk of disease transfer. In Africa, sets are most common in peri-urban production and in regions with a reliable cold-storage or dry-storage chain. Bulbs planted for seed production are a different enterprise; for bulb-onion production, sets and transplants are the practical options. If you use sets, buy from a reputable supplier and inspect for softness, sprouting, or mould.</p>

<h3>5. Spacing and Plant Density</h3>

<p>Spacing is your main lever on bulb size and market grade. Tighter spacing gives more bulbs per hectare but smaller bulbs; wider spacing gives larger bulbs but lower total count. Your buyer's specification should drive the decision: a supermarket or export buyer may want uniform medium bulbs, while a local fresh market may pay well for large bulbs. The table below gives practical starting points for African commercial production.</p>

<h3>6. Timing Planting for Optimal Bulbing</h3>

<p>Onion bulbing is triggered by day length and temperature, and the response is variety-specific. Short-day varieties bulb at 1012 hours of daylight and suit lower latitudes; intermediate and long-day varieties need 1214 hours and 14+ hours respectively. If you plant too late, bulbs may not size before the season ends; if you plant too early in a short-day region, plants may bolt or produce thick necks. The practical rule is to work backwards from your target harvest date: count the variety's days to maturity, add nursery time if transplanting, and plant so that bulbing occurs during your most reliable growing conditions.</p>
