1. Conducting a Hive Inspection

Inspecting a hive is like checking on a valuable but delicate friend. You want to know they are healthy and happy, but you don't want to disturb them too much. The best time to open a hive is on a warm, sunny day between 10am and 4pm, when most of the worker bees are out foraging. Avoid opening on windy, rainy, or very cold days. Wear protective clothing: a veil, gloves, and light-colored overalls. Use a smoker to calm the bees. Before you start, have your hive tool and a notebook ready. Stand to the side of the hive, not in front of the entrance, to avoid blocking the flight path. Give a few puffs of smoke at the entrance, wait 30 seconds, then lift the lid gently. Use your hive tool to pry it open. Work from the outside frames inward. Lift each frame slowly, keeping it over the hive to avoid dropping the queen. Look for signs of a healthy colony: eggs, larvae, capped brood, stored honey and pollen, and the queen. Check for pests, diseases, and swarm cells. Limit each inspection to 15-20 minutes to avoid stressing the bees. Close the hive carefully, ensuring no gaps. Record what you saw: date, time, weather, number of frames with brood, honey stores, queen seen (yes/no), and any problems. In Africa, many beekeepers use top-bar hives or Kenya Top Bar Hives (KTBH). The inspection method is similar, but you work with bars instead of frames. Always approach calmly and move slowly. If the bees become very aggressive, close the hive and try again another day. Regular inspections, every 7-10 days during the active season, help you catch problems early and keep your colony strong.

2. Reading Frames and Recognizing Queen Status

Reading frames is the skill of understanding what the bees are doing by looking at the comb. Each frame tells a story. You need to recognize the difference between eggs, larvae, and capped brood. Eggs are tiny, white, and stand upright at the bottom of cells. Larvae are white, curled, and lie in a pool of royal jelly. Capped brood is flat, biscuit-colored, and covers the cell. A healthy queen lays eggs in a tight pattern, often in the center of the frame. If you see eggs, you know the queen was there within the last three days. If you see larvae, she was there within the last nine days. If you see only capped brood, the queen may have stopped laying or is missing. The queen herself is longer than worker bees, with a pointed abdomen and shorter wings. She is usually surrounded by a circle of attendant bees. In Africa, local bee races like the African honey bee (Apis mellifera scutellata) are smaller and more defensive. Marking the queen with a small dot of paint (white, yellow, red, green, or blue) helps you find her quickly. If you cannot find the queen but see eggs, she is likely present. If you see no eggs and no larvae, the colony may be queenless. In that case, check for queen cells: peanut-shaped cells hanging from the comb. If you find queen cells, the colony is either swarming or replacing the queen. A laying worker can also occur in queenless colonies, but they only lay drone eggs, which are raised in larger cells. Knowing these signs helps you decide whether to requeen, add a frame of eggs from another hive, or leave the colony alone. Practice reading frames every inspection to build your confidence.

3. Detecting Pests and Diseases

Pests and diseases can weaken or kill your colony. Common pests in Africa include ants, termites, wax moths, small hive beetles, and varroa mites. Ants and termites can be controlled by placing the hive on stands with grease or oil barriers. Wax moths attack weak colonies, especially in stored combs. Keep your hive strong and remove unused combs. Small hive beetles are black, about 5mm long, and hide in corners. They can be trapped with oil traps or diatomaceous earth. Varroa mites are tiny, reddish-brown parasites that feed on bees. They are less common in Africa but can be a problem. Check for mites by using a sticky board or sugar shake method. Diseases include American foulbrood (AFB), European foulbrood (EFB), chalkbrood, and nosema. AFB causes sunken, perforated cappings and a foul smell. EFB causes twisted larvae that die before capping. Chalkbrood turns larvae into white, chalky mummies. Nosema causes dysentery and crawling bees. To detect diseases, inspect brood frames carefully. Look for discolored larvae, sunken cappings, or a bad smell. If you suspect AFB, burn the infected frames and sterilize your tools. For EFB, requeen and improve ventilation. Chalkbrood often clears up with better airflow and a stronger colony. Nosema can be treated with fumagillin, but prevention is better: keep hives dry and well-ventilated. Always maintain good hygiene: clean your hive tool between hives, and never exchange combs between sick and healthy colonies. Regular inspections help you spot problems early. If you see something unusual, take a photo and ask an experienced beekeeper or extension officer for advice. Early action saves colonies.

4. Managing Space: Adding Supers

Bees need space to store honey and raise brood. When a colony grows, it fills the brood chamber and may become crowded. This triggers swarming. To prevent swarming and increase honey production, you add supers. A super is a box placed on top of the brood chamber. It contains frames for honey storage. In Africa, many beekeepers use Langstroth hives with supers, or top-bar hives where you add more bars. The rule of thumb: when the brood chamber is 70-80% full, add a super. Check by looking at the frames: if 7 out of 10 frames are drawn and covered with bees, it's time. Add the super with frames that have foundation or starter strips. If you use a queen excluder, place it between the brood chamber and the super to keep the queen from laying in the honey super. However, some beekeepers avoid excluders because they can reduce honey yield. In Africa, African bees may be more reluctant to pass through excluders. Monitor the super: when the frames are 80% capped, add another super. You can add multiple supers, stacking them. Ensure the hive is level and stable. During a strong honey flow, bees can fill a super in 2-3 weeks. If you don't add space in time, the colony will swarm, and you lose half your bees. Conversely, adding too much space too early can stress the colony, as they must heat and defend a larger area. So add supers gradually. In top-bar hives, simply add more bars with guides. Always inspect the super every 7-10 days during the flow. Harvest honey only when frames are fully capped. Leave enough honey for the bees, especially before the dry season. Proper space management keeps your colony productive and healthy.

5. Dealing with Aggressive Colonies

Aggressive colonies are a common challenge, especially with African bee races. Aggressiveness can be genetic, environmental, or due to poor handling. Signs include bees following you, stinging without provocation, and boiling out of the hive when opened. First, check your own behavior: move slowly, wear clean, light-colored clothing, and avoid crushing bees. Use smoke gently. If a colony remains aggressive, consider requeening. Order a gentle queen from a reputable breeder. To requeen, find and kill the old queen, then introduce the new queen in a cage. Leave her caged for 2-3 days, then release her. Alternatively, you can split the aggressive colony and let them raise a new queen from eggs from a gentle colony. Another method is to move the aggressive hive to a new location, at least 3 km away, and place a gentle colony in its old spot. The foragers will join the gentle colony. If you cannot requeen, manage the colony defensively: work in the late afternoon when bees are calmer, use plenty of smoke, and keep inspections short. Wear extra protection: a thick suit, gloves, and boots. Never work an aggressive colony near people or animals. If the colony is too dangerous, consider destroying it by pouring soapy water into the hive. This is a last resort. In Africa, many beekeepers use local bees that are naturally aggressive. Some choose to work with them because they are productive and resistant to pests. But if you have neighbours or livestock, a gentle colony is safer. Always inform your neighbours before inspecting an aggressive hive. With patience and proper management, you can reduce aggression and make beekeeping enjoyable.

6. Seasonal Maintenance Tasks

Beekeeping follows the seasons. In Africa, seasons vary: wet and dry, or hot and cool. Your maintenance tasks should match the bees' needs. During the rainy season, ensure hives are dry and well-ventilated. Check for leaks and repair them. Raise hives on stands to prevent flooding and ant attacks. In the dry season, bees may need water. Provide a shallow water source with stones or sticks so bees don't drown. Also, watch for absconding due to lack of forage. If nectar is scarce, feed sugar syrup (1:1 sugar to water) to keep the colony alive. In hot regions, provide shade for the hive. In cool regions, reduce the entrance to keep warmth in. During the honey flow, add supers as needed and inspect weekly. After harvest, leave enough honey for the bees. In areas with a cold season, insulate the hive with grass or leaves, and reduce the entrance. In all seasons, keep the apiary clean: remove tall grass, and control pests. Check hives monthly during low activity. Replace old, dark combs with fresh foundation to reduce disease. Repair or replace damaged hive parts. In Africa, many beekeepers use locally available materials: mud, thatch, or metal sheets for roofs. Ensure the hive is predator-proof: use wire mesh to keep out honey badgers and monkeys. Record everything: date, tasks done, observations. This helps you plan for the next season. Remember, bees are resilient but need your help to thrive. By following a seasonal calendar, you keep your colonies strong and productive year-round.

<h2>Practical Reference: Hive Inspection and Maintenance</h2>

<p>In African beekeeping, hive inspection is not a hobby ritual it is a business control point. Every time you open a hive you spend bee labour, disturb colony temperature, and risk robbing or absconding. A well-planned inspection tells you whether the colony is making honey you can sell, whether it is preparing to swarm, whether pests are eating your margin, and whether you need to invest in another super or a new queen. This reference module gives you the field standards used by commercial beekeepers from the Miombo woodlands to the Kenyan highlands, so you can inspect faster, intervene earlier, and harvest more.</p>

<p>Work with the African bee (<em>Apis mellifera scutellata</em> and related subspecies). It is productive, but more defensive than European bees, quicker to abscond when disturbed or short of forage, and highly responsive to nectar flows. That means your inspection schedule and handling technique are commercial decisions, not personal preferences.</p>

<h3>1. Conducting a Hive Inspection</h3>

<p>Inspect during a nectar flow, mid-morning to early afternoon, when most foragers are out and the colony is calm. Avoid cold, windy, rainy days and the hottest part of the afternoon. Wear light-coloured protective clothing, use a smoker correctly (cool white smoke, not hot blasts), and work from the side or rear of the hive never stand in the flight path.</p>

<p>Open the hive, remove the first frame carefully, and inspect the remaining frames in order. Keep the brood nest together and return frames in the same position. Record what you see: queen presence, brood pattern, food stores, queen cells, pests, and available space. A full inspection should take 1015 minutes per hive; longer openings stress the colony and invite robbing.</p>

<h3>2. Reading Frames and Recognising Queen Status</h3>

<p>Learn to read a frame in seconds. A healthy brood pattern is compact, with few gaps, and larvae at different stages of development. The queen is usually found on a frame with open brood and a ring of pollen and honey around it. If you cannot find the queen, look for eggs newly laid eggs are the clearest proof that a queen was present within the last three days.</p>

<h3>3. Detecting Pests and Diseases</h3>

<p>African colonies face pests that directly reduce honey yield and hive survival. The most damaging are the small hive beetle (<em>Aethina tumida</em>), wax moths (<em>Galleria mellonella</em> and <em>Achroia grisella</em>), ants, and varroa mites in some regions. American and European foulbrood also occur. Early detection protects your investment; late detection can mean losing a whole apiary.</p>

<h3>4. Managing Space: Adding Supers</h3>

<p>Space management is the single most profitable inspection skill. When the brood box is about 7080% full of bees and brood, and the outer frames carry honey and pollen, add a super. In a strong nectar flow, colonies can fill a super in two to three weeks, so inspect weekly during flow. Add supers early in the morning or evening to reduce disturbance. Never add an empty super to a weak colony it invites wax moth and small hive beetle. For top-bar hives, add bars or a second box when bees occupy the last bar.</p>

<h3>5. Dealing with Aggressive Colonies</h3>

<p>Aggression is a management signal, not just a nuisance. A colony that stings repeatedly may be queenless, diseased, short of food, exposed to predators, or genetically defensive. Inspect at the best time of day, use smoke gently, and keep movements slow. If aggression persists after requeening from calmer local stock, relocate the hive away from footpaths and homesteads. For commercial apiaries, requeen aggressive colonies rather than tolerate them safe handling lowers labour cost and keeps neighbours supportive.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #c0392b; background:#fdecea; padding:14px 16px; margin:18px 0; border-radius:6px;">
<strong style="color:#c0392b;">SAFETY WARNING:</strong> African bee colonies can be highly defensive. Always wear a full bee suit, gloves, and closed footwear. Do not open hives alone in remote areas, near livestock, or close to homes and schools. If a colony becomes highly aggressive, close the hive and move away slowly. Never use petrol, kerosene, or harsh chemicals to calm bees they contaminate honey and kill the colony. Keep a charged smoker and a clean water source nearby. If you are allergic to bee stings, do not inspect hives without medical advice and a companion.
</div>

<h3>6. Seasonal Maintenance Tasks</h3>

<p>African seasons vary, but the beekeeping calendar follows rainfall and nectar flow. In the dry season, bees may need shade, water, and protection from ants and honey badgers. In the rainy season, ensure hives are raised, roofs are watertight, and vegetation is cleared. Before the main flow, clean hives, repair frames, and prepare supers. After harvest, leave enough honey for the colony and reduce entrance size to help guard bees.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li>Inspect during a nectar flow, mid-morning to early afternoon, on a calm day.</li>
<li>Wear full protective gear and use cool smoke; work slowly from the side or rear.</li>
<li>Check for eggs, larvae, sealed brood, queen cells, and food stores on every visit.</li>
<li>Look for small hive beetle, wax moth, ants, varroa, and foulbrood signs.</li>
<li>Add a super when the brood box is 7080% full and bees cover most frames.</li>
<li>Requeen aggressive or poorly performing colonies using calm local stock.</li>
<li>Record inspection date, colony strength, actions taken, and next visit.</li>
<li>Keep hives shaded, raised, watertight, and clear of vegetation and ant trails.</li>
</ul>
