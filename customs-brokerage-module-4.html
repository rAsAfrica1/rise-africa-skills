1. Customs Classification and the Harmonized System in African Trade

Welcome to Module 4, Lesson 1. This lesson focuses on customs classification, the foundation of every brokerage transaction. In Africa, where tariffs, trade agreements, and regulatory controls vary widely across 54 markets, correct classification is not a paperwork formality. It determines duty rates, eligibility for preferential treatment under agreements such as the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA), the Southern African Development Community (SADC), the Common Market for Eastern and Southern Africa (COMESA), and the Economic Community of West African States (ECOWAS), and whether goods require special permits. A broker who misclassifies a shipment can trigger penalties, delays, seizure, or loss of client trust. The World Customs Organization Harmonized System, or HS, provides the universal six-digit language. Countries then extend it to eight, ten, or more digits for national tariff lines. Your job is to move from a commercial description to a legally defensible code. Start with the General Rules for the Interpretation of the Harmonized System, known as the GIRs. Rule 1 says classification is determined by the terms of the headings and any relative section or chapter notes. That means the legal text, not the product marketing, controls. Rule 2 covers incomplete or unfinished articles and mixtures. Rule 3 handles goods that appear to fall under more than one heading, using specificity, essential character, and last-in-numerical-order. Rule 4 covers goods that cannot be classified otherwise, using the heading for goods most akin. Rules 5 and 6 deal with packing and subheading classification. In practice, African brokers often face ambiguous descriptions. A client may say they are importing solar panels. That sounds like heading 8541 for photosensitive semiconductor devices, but if the panels are assembled with a charge controller and battery into a solar home system, you may need to consider whether it is a set under GIR 3(b) or whether the essential character is the panel. Another common case is used clothing. Many African countries have specific tariff lines and import restrictions for used clothing, and misclassifying new clothing as used, or the reverse, can create serious compliance problems. Agricultural products are equally sensitive. A shipment of roasted groundnuts is not the same as raw groundnuts in the shell. Chapter 12 covers oil seeds and oleaginous fruits, while Chapter 20 covers preparations of vegetables, fruit, and nuts. The processing method changes the chapter. Textiles are another area where African customs authorities pay close attention. A broker must know the difference between woven and knitted fabrics, between garments and made-up articles, and between cotton and synthetic blends. The African Growth and Opportunity Act, or AGOA, gives preferential access to the United States market for eligible African countries, but only if the goods meet rules of origin and are classified correctly. Under AfCFTA, tariff concessions are tied to tariff lines, so a one-digit error can mean the difference between zero duty and a substantial payment. To classify accurately, build a disciplined process. First, obtain a complete product description, including composition, function, packaging, and intended use. Second, review the section and chapter notes. Third, apply the GIRs in order. Fourth, consult the national tariff schedule and any explanatory notes. Fifth, check for binding rulings or advance rulings offered by customs authorities. Many African customs administrations, including those in Kenya, Nigeria, South Africa, Ghana, and Rwanda, offer advance classification rulings. Use them for high-value or recurring shipments. Sixth, document your reasoning. If customs challenges your code, a written classification rationale with photos, technical specifications, and GIR references is your best defense. Technology is changing classification. Some customs authorities now use artificial intelligence and risk engines to flag unusual codes. The Kenya Revenue Authority, the Nigeria Customs Service, the South African Revenue Service, and others have invested in digital systems such as ICMS, NICIS, and SARS eFiling. As a broker, you should keep digital records of every classification decision. Also consider that classification affects more than duty. It affects value-added tax, excise duty, import permits, standards compliance, and eligibility for duty exemptions. In many African countries, goods classified under certain headings require a certificate of conformity, such as a PVoC certificate in Kenya or a SONCAP certificate in Nigeria. A wrong code can mean a shipment arrives without the right permit and is stuck at the port. Port delays in Africa are expensive. Demurrage, storage, and demurrage-related costs can exceed the value of the goods in extreme cases. Finally, remember that classification is a professional skill that improves with practice. Build a personal library of tariff schedules, explanatory notes, and rulings. Join professional associations such as the African Union of Customs Brokers or national broker associations. Attend customs workshops. When in doubt, ask for a binding ruling rather than guessing. Your reputation as a broker depends on getting classification right the first time. In the next lesson, we will examine customs valuation, the second pillar of customs compliance.

2. Customs Valuation and Transfer Pricing in African Markets

Welcome to Module 4, Lesson 2. This lesson covers customs valuation, the process of determining the customs value of imported goods. Customs value is the base on which ad valorem duties, VAT, and other charges are calculated. If classification is the language of customs, valuation is the number that determines how much money changes hands. In Africa, valuation disputes are among the most common causes of delays, audits, and penalties. The World Trade Organization Valuation Agreement, also known as the Customs Valuation Agreement, sets the international rules. Most African countries are WTO members and have incorporated these rules into national law. The primary method is transaction value, which is the price actually paid or payable for the goods when sold for export to the importing country. Transaction value is adjusted by adding certain costs, such as commissions and brokerage, cost of containers, packing, and assists, and by deducting certain costs if they are included in the price, such as post-importation transport. If transaction value cannot be used, customs moves through five alternative methods in sequence: transaction value of identical goods, transaction value of similar goods, deductive value, computed value, and fallback method. A broker must understand when transaction value is acceptable and when it is not. Related-party transactions are a major issue. If the buyer and seller are related, customs may examine whether the relationship influenced the price. In Africa, many importers buy from related suppliers in China, India, the United Arab Emirates, or Europe. A low invoice price between related parties can trigger customs scrutiny. The broker should be ready to provide evidence that the price is arm's length, such as comparable prices for identical goods, transfer pricing studies, or documentation showing that the relationship did not affect the price. Transfer pricing is the broader corporate practice of setting prices between related entities. African tax authorities are increasingly focused on transfer pricing, and customs authorities are following. In Kenya, Nigeria, South Africa, Ghana, and other markets, there is growing coordination between tax and customs departments. A company that sets a low transfer price to reduce customs duty may face a transfer pricing adjustment from the tax authority, and vice versa. Brokers should advise clients to align their customs valuation with their transfer pricing documentation. The invoice is not the only document that matters. Customs may look at the contract of sale, purchase orders, payment records, bank transfers, insurance certificates, freight invoices, and even email correspondence. In many African ports, customs uses transaction value databases and risk engines to compare declared values with historical data. If your declared value is significantly lower than the database average, your shipment may be flagged for verification. This does not mean you should inflate values. It means you should be prepared to justify the declared value with commercial evidence. Currency conversion is another practical issue. Invoices may be in US dollars, euros, Chinese yuan, or other currencies. Customs will convert to local currency using a published exchange rate, often the rate on the date of registration of the customs declaration. Brokers must use the correct rate and document it. Exchange rate fluctuations can create significant differences in duty payable, especially for large shipments. Freight and insurance are also important. Under the WTO Valuation Agreement, the cost of transport to the place of importation and insurance are added to the customs value if they are not already included in the price. This is known as a CIF basis. Some countries use FOB basis, but most African countries use CIF. The broker must know the national rule and ensure that freight and insurance are correctly apportioned. For container shipments with multiple products, apportionment can be complex. Assists are another area that trips up brokers. An assist is something supplied by the buyer free of charge or at a reduced cost for use in the production of the imported goods. Examples include tools, dies, molds, materials, components, and engineering work. If a buyer in Africa sends designs or molds to a manufacturer in Asia, the value of those assists must be added to the customs value. Many importers forget this, and customs audits often catch it. Royalties and license fees are also added if they are related to the imported goods and are a condition of sale. In Africa, this is common in the pharmaceutical, technology, and consumer goods sectors. A broker should ask the client whether any royalties are paid and whether they are included in the invoice price. If not, they may need to be added. Undervaluation is a serious problem in many African countries. It deprives governments of revenue and distorts competition. Customs authorities in Kenya, Nigeria, Tanzania, Uganda, and elsewhere have introduced minimum values, reference values, and valuation databases to combat it. These tools are controversial because they can conflict with WTO rules, but they are a reality. A broker must know how they work and how to challenge them through proper procedures. If you believe a customs valuation decision is wrong, you can request a review, appeal to a tribunal, or seek a court remedy, depending on national law. Documentation is your best protection. Keep invoices, contracts, proof of payment, freight and insurance documents, and any valuation rulings. For related-party transactions, keep transfer pricing studies and comparables. For assists, keep records of the value and the apportionment. For royalties, keep license agreements. A well-documented file can resolve a valuation dispute quickly. Finally, remember that valuation is not just a customs issue. It affects VAT, excise, and other taxes. In many African countries, customs collects VAT on imports on behalf of the tax authority. An error in valuation can lead to a VAT assessment later. Brokers should work closely with clients' finance and tax teams. In the next lesson, we will look at rules of origin and preferential trade agreements, which determine whether goods qualify for reduced or zero duty.

3. Rules of Origin and Preferential Trade Agreements in Africa

Welcome to Module 4, Lesson 3. This lesson covers rules of origin and preferential trade agreements, the legal framework that determines whether goods qualify for reduced or zero customs duty. In Africa, preferential trade is expanding rapidly. The African Continental Free Trade Area, or AfCFTA, is the largest free trade area in the world by number of participating countries. Regional economic communities such as SADC, COMESA, ECOWAS, the East African Community, or EAC, and the Economic and Monetary Community of Central Africa, or CEMAC, also offer preferential treatment. In addition, African countries benefit from unilateral preferences such as AGOA with the United States, the Everything But Arms arrangement with the European Union, and various bilateral agreements. For a customs broker, rules of origin are both an opportunity and a risk. The opportunity is that correct origin claims can save clients substantial duty. The risk is that incorrect claims can lead to penalties, retroactive duty recovery, and loss of preferential access. Origin is not the same as the country of shipment. Goods may be shipped from Dubai or Singapore but originate in Kenya or Nigeria. Origin is determined by where the goods were wholly obtained or underwent substantial transformation. For wholly obtained goods, the rule is straightforward. Agricultural products, minerals, and live animals born and raised in a country are wholly obtained there. For manufactured goods, the rule is more complex. Substantial transformation can be demonstrated through a change in tariff classification, a regional value content threshold, or specific processing operations. Each trade agreement has its own rules. Under AfCFTA, the rules of origin are still being finalized, but the general approach includes a change in tariff heading and a regional value content requirement, often around 40 percent. Under SADC, many products qualify with a change in tariff heading or a regional value content of 35 percent. Under COMESA, the rule is often a change in tariff heading or a value-added threshold. Under AGOA, the rule for apparel is particularly important. Apparel must be assembled in an eligible African country from fabric that is made in Africa or the United States, or from fabric that is made in a third country if the country has a visa for the third-country fabric provision. This provision has been critical for countries such as Kenya, Ethiopia, Lesotho, and Madagascar. A broker handling apparel shipments to the United States must know the AGOA visa requirements and the specific rules for each product. The EAC Customs Union has its own rules. Goods originating in one EAC partner state, such as Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, Rwanda, and Burundi, qualify for preferential treatment when imported into another partner state. The EAC has a common external tariff, but there are still disputes over origin, especially for goods that are repackaged or blended in one country and then exported to another. To claim preference, the importer must have a valid proof of origin. This can be a certificate of origin issued by an authorized body, such as a chamber of commerce or a customs authority, or an origin declaration made by the exporter on the invoice. The proof of origin must be accurate and must be kept for the required period, often five years. Customs authorities conduct post-clearance audits and can verify origin claims with the exporting country. If the claim is invalid, the importer must pay the full duty plus penalties. A broker should never assume that a supplier's certificate of origin is correct. Verify the details. Check that the goods are described accurately, that the tariff classification matches, that the origin criteria are met, and that the certificate is signed and stamped by the right authority. For AfCFTA, the rules of origin are being implemented through national laws and regulations. The AfCFTA Secretariat has published protocols and annexes, but implementation varies by country. Some countries have started trading under AfCFTA, while others are still building systems. A broker should monitor the status of AfCFTA in each country where they operate. The AfCFTA also has a dispute settlement mechanism, but in practice, most origin disputes are resolved at the national level. Another important issue is cumulation. Cumulation allows materials from one participating country to be treated as originating in another participating country when used in the production of a final good. This is essential for regional value chains. For example, cotton grown in Burkina Faso, spun into yarn in Ghana, and woven into fabric in Nigeria could qualify as originating in the region if cumulation rules are met. Brokers should understand how cumulation works in each agreement. Direct transport is also a common requirement. To qualify for preference, goods must usually be transported directly from the exporting country to the importing country, or through a third country without entering into commerce there. If goods are transshipped through a third country, the broker must provide evidence that they remained under customs control and did not undergo any operation other than unloading, reloading, or preservation. Documentation is critical. For each preferential shipment, keep the certificate of origin, commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading or airway bill, and any supporting documents such as mill certificates, production records, or supplier declarations. For AGOA, keep the visa number and the exporter's declaration. For AfCFTA, keep the origin declaration and any cumulation evidence. If customs requests verification, you will need to provide these documents quickly. Penalties for false origin claims can be severe. In some African countries, the penalty is a multiple of the duty evaded, and the importer may lose the right to use preferential treatment in the future. In serious cases, customs can seize the goods. A broker's professional indemnity insurance may not cover deliberate false claims, so never sign a certificate or declaration that you know is false. Finally, remember that rules of origin are not static. Trade agreements are renegotiated, tariff schedules change, and new protocols are adopted. A broker should subscribe to official gazettes, customs notices, and trade ministry updates. Attend AfCFTA and regional workshop sessions. Build relationships with customs origin units. The more you know, the better you can serve your clients. In the next lesson, we will cover customs procedures, documentation, and digital systems in African ports.

4. Customs Procedures, Documentation, and Digital Systems in African Ports

Welcome to Module 4, Lesson 4. This lesson covers customs procedures, documentation, and digital systems. In Africa, customs clearance is a complex process that involves multiple agencies, documents, and systems. A broker who masters procedures can move goods quickly and avoid demurrage, storage, and penalties. A broker who does not will struggle. The first step in any import is registration. The importer must be registered with the customs authority and have a taxpayer identification number, a business registration certificate, and any required import licenses. In many African countries, registration is now online. For example, Kenya has the iTax system and the Customs Simba system. Nigeria has the NICIS II system. South Africa has SARS eFiling and the Customs and Excise Act. Ghana has the ICUMS system. Rwanda has the RRA e-Tax and the Rwanda Electronic Single Window. These systems allow importers and brokers to submit declarations electronically, pay duties online, and track shipments. The broker must be registered as a customs agent or clearing agent. Requirements vary by country. In Kenya, clearing agents must be licensed by the Kenya Revenue Authority and must pass an examination. In Nigeria, agents must be licensed by the Nigeria Customs Service. In South Africa, customs brokers must be registered with SARS. In Ghana, agents must be licensed by the Ghana Revenue Authority. The broker should also be a member of a professional association, such as the Kenya International Freight and Warehousing Association, the Nigerian Association of Licensed Customs Agents, or the South African Association of Freight Forwarders. The core document in customs clearance is the customs declaration. In many countries, this is the Single Administrative Document, or SAD, or an electronic equivalent. The declaration includes the importer's details, the exporter's details, the country of origin, the country of export, the tariff classification, the customs value, the duty and tax amounts, the mode of transport, and the port of entry. The broker must ensure that all fields are accurate. Errors can lead to delays, penalties, or audits. Supporting documents typically include the commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading or airway bill, certificate of origin, insurance certificate, import permit or license, certificate of conformity, and any product-specific certificates such as phytosanitary certificates for plants, health certificates for food, and radiation certificates for certain goods. In many African countries, the certificate of conformity is mandatory for regulated products. Kenya requires a PVoC certificate from an authorized agency such as SGS, Intertek, or Bureau Veritas. Nigeria requires SONCAP. Tanzania requires PVoC. Uganda requires PVoC. These certificates must be obtained before shipment, not after arrival. A broker should advise clients to plan ahead. The clearance process usually follows these steps. First, the vessel or aircraft arrives and the carrier submits a cargo manifest to customs. Second, the broker lodges the customs declaration. Third, customs assesses the declaration using a risk management system. Fourth, if the declaration is selected for inspection, the broker arranges for the goods to be presented to customs. Fifth, customs examines the goods and verifies the documents. Sixth, customs issues a release order. Seventh, the broker pays the assessed duties and taxes. Eighth, the terminal releases the goods. Ninth, the broker arranges transport to the importer's premises. Each step can encounter delays. The most common causes are incomplete documents, incorrect classification, undervaluation, missing permits, and physical inspection. In many African ports, congestion is a major problem. The Port of Mombasa, the Port of Dar es Salaam, the Port of Lagos, the Port of Tema, the Port of Durban, and the Port of Abidjan are among the busiest in Africa. Delays can be costly. Demurrage is charged by the shipping line when containers stay at the port beyond the free time. Storage is charged by the terminal. In some cases, demurrage can exceed the value of the goods. A broker should monitor vessel schedules, prepare documents in advance, and use pre-clearance where allowed. Digital systems are transforming customs in Africa. The single window system allows traders to submit documents to multiple agencies through one portal. Kenya's single window, the Kenya TradeNet System, links customs, port authorities, standards bodies, and other agencies. Ghana's single window, the Ghana National Single Window, does the same. Nigeria is implementing the National Single Window. These systems reduce paperwork and speed up clearance. However, they also require brokers to be digitally literate. You must be able to upload documents, respond to queries, and track status online. Another important development is the use of scanners and non-intrusive inspection. Many African ports now use X-ray scanners to inspect containers without opening them. If a scanner image is unclear, customs may still require a physical inspection. Brokers should ensure that packing is orderly and that the manifest matches the cargo. Risk management systems are also becoming more sophisticated. Customs uses data analytics to identify high-risk shipments. Factors include the importer's history, the country of origin, the type of goods, the declared value, and the route. A broker with a good compliance record is more likely to receive fast-track treatment. A broker with a history of errors or fraud will face more inspections. Finally, remember that customs procedures are governed by national law and international standards. The World Customs Organization's Revised Kyoto Convention provides a blueprint for modern customs procedures. Many African countries are aligning their laws with the Kyoto Convention and the WTO Trade Facilitation Agreement. The Trade Facilitation Agreement requires countries to publish customs rules, allow advance rulings, use risk management, and provide appeal procedures. A broker should know these rights and use them. In the next lesson, we will cover compliance, ethics, and risk management for customs brokers in Africa.

5. Compliance, Ethics, and Risk Management for Customs Brokers in Africa

Welcome to Module 4, Lesson 5. This lesson covers compliance, ethics, and risk management. As a customs broker in Africa, you occupy a position of trust. You act on behalf of importers and exporters, but you also have a legal duty to customs authorities. Your work affects government revenue, public safety, and fair competition. A broker who cuts corners may win short-term business, but will eventually face penalties, loss of license, or criminal prosecution. Compliance starts with knowledge. You must know the customs laws, regulations, and procedures of every country where you operate. You must also know the international rules, including the WTO Valuation Agreement, the Harmonized System, and the Revised Kyoto Convention. Ignorance is not a defense. If you sign a declaration, you are responsible for its accuracy. Ethics is about doing the right thing even when no one is watching. Common ethical dilemmas in customs brokerage include undervaluation, misclassification, false origin claims, and facilitation payments. Undervaluation occurs when an importer declares a lower value than the true transaction value to reduce duty. Misclassification occurs when goods are declared under a code with a lower duty rate. False origin claims occur when goods are declared as originating in a country with preferential access when they do not. Facilitation payments are small payments made to customs officials to speed up processing. All of these are illegal or unethical. A professional broker refuses to participate. The consequences of non-compliance are serious. Customs authorities can impose fines, seize goods, suspend or revoke licenses, and refer cases for criminal prosecution. In some African countries, customs offenses carry prison sentences. The broker's reputation is also at risk. Word travels fast in the trade community. A broker known for shady practices will lose good clients and may be blacklisted by customs. Risk management is the process of identifying, assessing, and mitigating risks. For a customs broker, the main risks are financial, legal, operational, and reputational. Financial risks include penalties, demurrage, storage, and unpaid client invoices. Legal risks include lawsuits, license suspension, and criminal charges. Operational risks include system failures, port congestion, and document errors. Reputational risks include loss of trust and negative word of mouth. To manage these risks, build a compliance program. First, establish clear policies and procedures for classification, valuation, origin, and documentation. Second, train your staff regularly. Third, use checklists and peer review for high-risk shipments. Fourth, keep accurate records for the required period. Fifth, conduct internal audits. Sixth, cooperate with customs audits and investigations. Seventh, purchase professional indemnity insurance. Eighth, use written contracts with clients that clearly define responsibilities and limit liability where possible. Client due diligence is also essential. Before accepting a new client, verify their identity, business registration, tax status, and import history. Be cautious of clients who ask you to undervalue goods, use false documents, or avoid permits. These are red flags. If a client insists on illegal behavior, decline the engagement and document your decision. You may also have a legal obligation to report suspicious activity under anti-money laundering laws. Many African countries have financial intelligence units and require reporting of suspicious transactions. Customs brokers are often considered gatekeepers and may have reporting duties. Technology can help with compliance. Use customs software that validates declarations, calculates duties, and flags errors. Use document management systems to store records securely. Use communication tools to keep clients informed. But technology is not a substitute for judgment. You must still review every declaration and question anything that seems wrong. Professional development is a lifelong obligation. Attend training sessions offered by customs authorities, trade associations, and international organizations. The World Customs Organization, the World Trade Organization, and the African Union provide resources. The AfCFTA Secretariat publishes updates. National customs authorities publish notices and guides. Read them. Join a professional association and participate in its activities. Network with other brokers and share best practices. Mentorship is also valuable. If you are experienced, mentor younger brokers. If you are new, find a mentor. Finally, remember that compliance is good for business. Clients value brokers who keep them out of trouble. Customs authorities value brokers who are reliable. Banks and insurers value brokers who are low-risk. A reputation for integrity is your most valuable asset. In this module, we have covered classification, valuation, rules of origin, procedures, and compliance. These are the core pillars of customs brokerage. Master them, and you will be able to serve clients across Africa and beyond. Thank you for completing Module 4.

<h2>Practical Reference: Customs Classification, Valuation &amp; Origin A Working Desk Guide for African Brokers</h2>

<p>This lesson is a hands-on desk reference for the day-to-day work of a customs broker operating in African markets. It consolidates the practical mechanics of tariff classification, customs valuation, rules of origin, and port documentation into one working tool. The goal is commercial: clear, defensible declarations that clear goods faster, avoid penalties, and protect your client's margin. Use it alongside the earlier modules in this course, not instead of them.</p>

<p>Three realities shape brokerage practice across the continent. First, most African states apply the HS nomenclature at 6-digit international level, then extend it nationally to 8, 10, or even 12 digits so a code that works in Mombasa may not exist in Accra. Second, valuation disputes, not classification disputes, are the single largest source of post-clearance audits and duty reassessments. Third, preferential origin (AfCFTA, COMESA, SADC, EAC, ECOWAS) is where the largest legitimate duty savings sit and where documentation failures are most common.</p>

<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0;"><strong style="color:#b71c1c;"> SAFETY AND REGULATORY WARNING FOOD, PHARMACEUTICAL AND CHEMICAL IMPORTS</strong></p>
<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0;">Mis-classification or under-valuation of food, pharmaceutical, agrochemical, or industrial chemical consignments is not merely a revenue issue it is a <strong>public health and safety offence</strong> in most African jurisdictions. Specific risks:</p>
<ul style="margin:0 0 8px 18px;">
<li>Substandard or falsified medicines (HS 3004) entering through mis-declared consignments a documented problem across West, Central and East Africa.</li>
<li>Unregistered pesticides and veterinary drugs (HS 3808, 3002) bypassing national registration (e.g. PCPB in Kenya, NAFDAC in Nigeria, Ghana EPA).</li>
<li>Food consignments (HS 1622) lacking phytosanitary or sanitary certificates, or with expired shelf life, cleared under incorrect codes.</li>
<li>Hazardous chemicals (HS 2829, 38) requiring MSDS and prior informed consent under the Rotterdam Convention.</li>
</ul>
<p style="margin:0;">Brokers who facilitate such declarations risk <strong>personal criminal liability, licence revocation, and imprisonment</strong> under national customs and public health acts, in addition to client penalties. When in doubt, stop the clearance and escalate to the relevant regulator.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Verify the HS code at national level</strong> confirm the 8/10/12-digit national extension, not just the 6-digit international code. Check the current national tariff book and any recent gazette amendments.</li>
<li><strong>Apply the General Interpretative Rules in order</strong> GIR 1 (headings and section notes) before GIR 3 (essential character), and never skip Section/Chapter Notes.</li>
<li><strong>Build the valuation file</strong> transaction value, plus assists, royalties, commissions, packing, and freight/insurance to the place of importation. Keep the invoice, contract, and payment proof together.</li>
<li><strong>Test related-party transactions</strong> if buyer and seller are related, document that the relationship did not influence the price, using comparables or transfer pricing documentation.</li>
<li><strong>Confirm origin evidence before claiming preference</strong> valid Certificate of Origin, supplier declarations, and production records that support the PSR. No proof, no preference.</li>
<li><strong>Reconcile the manifest, entry, and invoice</strong> weights, quantities, descriptions and values must match across all three. Discrepancies trigger inspection and delay.</li>
<li><strong>Retain records for the statutory period</strong> typically 5 years in Kenya, Nigeria and Ghana; 5 years in South Africa. Store classification rulings, valuation workings, and origin proofs.</li>
<li><strong>Escalate red flags</strong> restricted goods, dual-use items, health products without registration, or suspiciously low values. Document your escalation and the regulator's response.</li>
</ol>
