1. Supplier Identification and Market Research in African Markets

Welcome to Module 9, Lesson 1. In this lesson we focus on finding, verifying and understanding suppliers across African markets. Purchasing and procurement professionals in Africa face a unique combination of opportunity and risk. Opportunities include a young, growing workforce, regional trade blocs such as the African Continental Free Trade Area, and expanding digital marketplaces. Risks include fragmented supplier information, informal operators, currency volatility, and infrastructure gaps. Your job is to build a supplier base that is reliable, competitive and compliant. Start with a clear specification. Before you search for suppliers, write a procurement brief that states what you need, the required quality standard, delivery timelines, volumes, packaging, payment terms and any regulatory requirements. A vague brief leads to vague quotes and poor comparisons. For example, if you are buying maize flour for a school feeding program in Kenya, specify moisture content, aflatoxin limits, packaging size, delivery frequency and whether you need KEBS certification. Once the brief is ready, map your market. Use a mix of sources: industry associations, chambers of commerce, trade directories, government supplier databases, online B2B platforms, trade fairs and referrals from trusted peers. In Nigeria, the Lagos Chamber of Commerce and Industry and the Corporate Affairs Commission database are useful starting points. In South Africa, the Central Supplier Database is a strong public sector tool. In East Africa, the East African Business Council and national manufacturers associations can help. For agricultural inputs, cooperatives and commodity exchanges such as the Ethiopia Commodity Exchange or the Zambia Agricultural Commodity Exchange can connect you to verified aggregators. Digital sourcing platforms are growing. Platforms like Twiga Foods, TradeDepot, Sabi, and various e-procurement portals can reduce search costs, but you must still verify the supplier behind the platform. Always conduct a three-step verification. First, legal verification: confirm business registration, tax compliance, and sector-specific licenses. Ask for a certificate of incorporation, tax clearance, VAT registration and any import or export permits. Second, financial verification: request bank references, audited accounts or management accounts, and check for insolvency or litigation records where available. Third, operational verification: visit the production site or warehouse, inspect equipment, talk to workers, and review quality control processes. A site visit is non-negotiable for high-value or high-risk categories. When visiting, look for signs of real capacity: raw material stocks, maintenance records, safety practices, and order books. Do not rely only on glossy brochures. Assess supplier risk using a simple matrix. Score suppliers on financial stability, capacity, quality systems, delivery reliability, ethical practices and geographic concentration. A supplier with a single factory in a flood-prone area or a single port of entry carries higher risk. Consider currency risk if you are buying in a foreign currency while your revenue is in local currency. Consider political risk if the supplier operates in a region with upcoming elections or civil unrest. Build a diversified supplier list. For critical items, have at least two qualified suppliers and one backup. For routine items, three to five suppliers give you competitive tension without overburdening your team. Document your findings in a supplier profile that includes contact details, ownership, key personnel, product range, capacity, lead times, payment terms, certifications, references and risk rating. Update these profiles at least annually. Market research also means understanding price drivers. In Africa, prices are influenced by fuel costs, exchange rates, import duties, seasonal harvests, transport corridors and border delays. Track these factors so you can time purchases and negotiate better. For instance, buying fertilizer after the planting season peak can reduce costs. Buying maize after harvest when supply is high can lower prices, but you need storage capacity. Use historical price data and talk to multiple suppliers to understand the fair market range. Finally, practice ethical sourcing. Avoid suppliers linked to child labor, forced labor, environmental damage or corruption. Ask for codes of conduct, audit reports and traceability documentation. In some markets, informal suppliers are unavoidable, but you can still require basic labor and safety standards. Remember that good supplier identification is not a one-time event. It is an ongoing process of scanning, verifying, engaging and reviewing. In the next lesson, we will look at how to run a competitive tender and evaluate bids fairly. Before moving on, complete this action: take one category you buy regularly and build a supplier map with at least five potential suppliers, their verification status and their risk rating. This practical step will prepare you for the rest of the module.

2. Running Competitive Tenders and Evaluating Bids

Welcome to Lesson 2 of Module 9. Competitive tendering is one of the most powerful tools in procurement, but it must be run fairly, transparently and efficiently. In many African contexts, tendering is also a compliance requirement for public bodies and large corporates. This lesson gives you a practical process for running a tender from preparation to award. First, decide whether a tender is the right approach. Use competitive tendering for high-value purchases, technically complex items, or when you need to demonstrate fairness. For low-value, routine purchases, a simple request for quotation from three suppliers may be enough. A good rule is to set thresholds in your procurement policy. For example, purchases below five thousand US dollars may use three quotes, purchases between five thousand and fifty thousand may use a formal request for proposal, and purchases above fifty thousand may require an open tender. Adjust these figures to your organization and country. Next, prepare the tender documents. These usually include an invitation to tender, instructions to bidders, terms of reference or technical specifications, a price schedule, a draft contract, evaluation criteria and required forms. Be precise about submission deadlines, format, and contact person. State clearly whether you will accept electronic bids, physical bids, or both. In many African countries, electronic submission is growing but physical delivery to a tender box is still common. Always allow enough time. A minimum of two to four weeks is reasonable for local suppliers; six weeks or more for international or complex tenders. Publish the tender widely. Use your website, national procurement portals, newspapers, industry associations and direct invitations to known suppliers. In public procurement, follow the legal advertising requirements of your country. For example, in Kenya, the Public Procurement and Asset Disposal Act requires advertisement in at least two newspapers of national circulation. In South Africa, the Preferential Procurement Policy Framework Act sets out specific rules. Even in the private sector, wide advertising improves competition and reduces suspicion of favoritism. Now, manage the bid period. Hold a pre-bid meeting or site visit if the requirement is complex. Answer all questions in writing and share the answers with all bidders to ensure equal information. Do not give one bidder an advantage. Keep a log of all communications. When bids arrive, safeguard them. Use a locked tender box with at least two people present at opening. Record the time, date, bidder name and bid amount. In public tenders, the opening may be public. In private tenders, keep the opening committee small and independent. Next comes evaluation. This is where many organizations fail. Establish an evaluation committee before the tender closes. The committee should include technical, financial and procurement representatives. For large tenders, include an independent observer. Evaluate in two stages: first, compliance and technical, then financial. For compliance, check that the bidder submitted all required documents, meets eligibility criteria, and accepts the tender terms. Disqualify non-compliant bids early and record the reasons. For technical evaluation, use a scoring matrix with clear criteria and weights. For example, technical approach thirty percent, experience twenty percent, key personnel twenty percent, equipment and facilities fifteen percent, quality assurance ten percent, and local content five percent. Score each bid independently, then meet to reconcile differences. Do not change criteria after bids are opened. For financial evaluation, calculate the total cost of ownership, not just the purchase price. Include transport, insurance, duties, installation, training, spare parts, maintenance and disposal. A cheap bid can become expensive if it fails often or requires imported spare parts with long lead times. Once you have technical and financial scores, you can combine them. A common method is a weighted score where technical is seventy percent and financial is thirty percent. The lowest evaluated bidder should win, but only if technically compliant. Avoid the temptation to pick the cheapest bid if it cannot deliver. Also avoid the opposite mistake of over-specifying so that only one supplier can qualify. After evaluation, prepare an evaluation report. It should list all bids received, compliance results, technical scores, financial scores, combined scores, recommended winner, and reasons for rejection of others. The report should be signed by all committee members. If any member disagrees, record the dissenting opinion. Then seek approval from the appropriate authority, such as the head of procurement, finance director or board tender committee. Notify the winner and the unsuccessful bidders. Provide debriefs to unsuccessful bidders on request. This improves future competition and reduces complaints. In public procurement, there may be a standstill period or a formal appeals process. Follow it strictly. Finally, sign the contract. Ensure the contract reflects the tender terms, the winning bid, and any clarifications. Include delivery schedules, payment terms, penalties for late delivery, quality standards, dispute resolution and termination clauses. In African cross-border transactions, specify the governing law, arbitration venue and currency. A well-run tender gives you better prices, better quality and a defensible audit trail. It also builds trust with suppliers. In the next lesson, we will cover negotiation strategies tailored to African market conditions. For now, review your organization's tender thresholds and evaluation criteria. Are they clear, fair and practical? If not, propose improvements.

3. Negotiation Strategies for African Procurement

Welcome to Lesson 3. Negotiation is not about winning at the supplier's expense. It is about reaching a deal that both sides can sustain. In African procurement, negotiation must account for relationship culture, currency risk, logistics challenges and power dynamics. This lesson gives you practical strategies. First, prepare thoroughly. Know your best alternative to a negotiated agreement, often called BATNA. If you cannot agree with this supplier, what will you do? Do you have another supplier, a different specification, or the option to delay? A strong BATNA gives you confidence. Also know your target price, your walk-away price, and your must-have terms. Research the supplier's costs. For a local manufacturer, consider raw material costs, labor, electricity, transport and financing. For an importer, consider freight, duties, clearing agents and exchange rates. Understanding their cost structure helps you propose realistic trade-offs. Second, understand the cultural context. In many African business settings, relationships and trust matter as much as price. Take time to greet, share a meal, and show respect. Do not rush to the numbers. In some cultures, direct confrontation is avoided; read indirect signals. In others, bargaining is expected. Ask open questions and listen more than you talk. Use local languages where possible, or work with a trusted interpreter. Be aware of hierarchy. In some organizations, the senior person present may not be the technical expert, but they make the final decision. Address both the technical team and the decision maker. Third, choose your negotiation style. Distributive negotiation is zero-sum: one side wins, the other loses. Integrative negotiation seeks mutual gains. Most procurement negotiations should be integrative. You can trade volume for price, longer payment terms for a discount, or a longer contract for better service. For example, if a supplier in Egypt needs cash flow, you might offer faster payment in exchange for a lower price. If a supplier in South Africa has excess capacity, you might offer a guaranteed minimum order in exchange for a lower unit rate. Fourth, manage currency risk. Many African currencies are volatile. If you are buying in US dollars but earning in local currency, you carry exchange rate risk. Negotiate in your revenue currency where possible. If not, agree on a currency adjustment clause that shares the risk. For example, if the exchange rate moves more than five percent, both parties share the difference. Alternatively, use forward contracts or local currency financing. Be clear about who pays bank charges and transfer fees. Fifth, negotiate payment terms carefully. Suppliers often ask for advance payment because they lack working capital. This is a major risk for you. Avoid large advances. Offer a letter of credit, escrow, or milestone payments instead. If an advance is unavoidable, require a bank guarantee. For local suppliers, consider paying for raw materials directly to their suppliers, or using a tripartite agreement with a bank. Also negotiate retention: hold back five to ten percent until delivery and acceptance. Sixth, negotiate quality and delivery terms. Specify inspection rights, testing methods, and rejection procedures. Agree on packaging, labeling and documentation. For cross-border shipments, clarify Incoterms. For example, FOB means the supplier delivers to the port; CIF means they pay freight and insurance to your port. DAP means delivered at place. Choose the Incoterm that matches your capacity. If you have strong customs expertise, FOB may be cheaper. If not, DAP reduces your hassle. Seventh, negotiate penalties and incentives. Late delivery penalties should be meaningful but not so harsh that they destroy the relationship. A common approach is zero point five percent of order value per week of delay, capped at ten percent. For quality failures, require replacement or credit. For early delivery, offer a small bonus if it helps you. Eighth, use a structured negotiation process. Start with an opening offer that is ambitious but justifiable. Explain your rationale. Ask the supplier to explain their price build-up. Identify areas of common interest. Make conditional concessions: if you do this, we can do that. Summarize agreements frequently. Take notes. Do not agree to anything you do not understand. If you need time, ask for a break or a follow-up meeting. Ninth, manage power imbalances. If you are a small buyer facing a large supplier, you may have less leverage. Consider joining a buying consortium or cooperative to increase volume. If you are a large buyer facing a small supplier, do not abuse your power. Fair treatment builds loyalty and better service. In public procurement, negotiation is often restricted. You may only negotiate with the lowest evaluated bidder, and only on specific issues. Know the rules. Tenth, document the negotiation. Keep a record of offers, counteroffers, and agreements. Have both parties sign a minutes of negotiation or a memorandum of understanding before the contract is drafted. This prevents disputes later. Finally, remember that negotiation does not end with the contract. Review performance regularly. If the supplier performs well, offer more business. If not, use the contract remedies. In the next lesson, we will look at contract management and supplier relationship management. For now, practice a negotiation for a common purchase. Write down your BATNA, target, walk-away, and three trade variables. This preparation will make you a stronger negotiator.

4. Contract Management and Supplier Relationship Management

Welcome to Lesson 4. Once the contract is signed, the real work begins. Contract management ensures that the supplier delivers what was promised, on time, at the agreed quality and price. Supplier relationship management goes further: it builds a long-term partnership that improves performance and reduces risk. In African procurement, both are essential because supply chains are often fragile and communication can be challenging. Start with a contract management plan. This is a simple document that states who is responsible for what, key dates, performance indicators, reporting requirements, and escalation paths. For a high-value contract, appoint a contract manager. For smaller contracts, the procurement officer may handle it alongside other duties. The plan should include a delivery schedule, quality inspection plan, payment schedule, and risk register. Next, mobilize the contract. Hold a kick-off meeting with the supplier. Review the scope, deliverables, timelines, roles, and communication channels. Agree on how often you will meet, what reports you need, and who to contact in an emergency. For cross-border contracts, clarify time zones, languages, and document formats. Set up a shared folder or portal for documents. Now, monitor performance. Use key performance indicators such as on-time delivery, quality rejection rate, order accuracy, responsiveness, and cost savings. Collect data regularly. For goods, inspect on arrival. For services, review progress against milestones. For construction or installation, use site inspections and progress reports. Do not wait until the end to discover problems. If performance slips, act early. Send a written notice, request a corrective action plan, and set a deadline. If the supplier fails to improve, escalate according to the contract. Manage changes carefully. In Africa, changes are common due to currency shifts, new regulations, or logistics disruptions. Any change to scope, price, or timeline should be documented in a contract amendment signed by both parties. Verbal changes are dangerous. They lead to disputes and audit findings. Also manage payments. Pay on time. Late payment damages trust and can halt deliveries. In some countries, late payment by public bodies is a serious problem. If you are the buyer, ensure your finance team has the invoice, delivery note, and acceptance certificate. If you are the supplier, follow up professionally. Consider using a payment tracker. Now, let us turn to supplier relationship management. Not all suppliers need the same attention. Use a segmentation model. Strategic suppliers are critical to your operations and have high spend. Manage them with senior-level relationships, joint improvement projects, and long-term contracts. Preferred suppliers are important but replaceable. Manage them with regular performance reviews and competitive benchmarking. Transactional suppliers provide routine items. Manage them with automated processes and minimal interaction. For strategic suppliers, hold quarterly business reviews. Discuss performance, market trends, innovation, and risks. Share your forecasts so they can plan. Invite them to suggest cost savings or quality improvements. Recognize good performance publicly. This motivates them and strengthens the relationship. For all suppliers, maintain clear communication. Respond to emails and calls promptly. Be honest about problems. If you cannot pay on time, tell them early and agree on a plan. If you need to change an order, give as much notice as possible. Respect their staff. In many African contexts, personal relationships matter. A phone call can resolve issues faster than a formal letter. But keep records. Use email or written minutes to confirm verbal agreements. Manage risks continuously. Monitor supplier financial health, political developments, weather events, and currency movements. Have contingency plans. For critical items, keep safety stock or qualify a backup supplier. For imported items, consider alternative ports or routes. For example, if a supplier in Sudan faces conflict, can you source from Uganda or Kenya? If a port in Nigeria is congested, can you use a different port? Also manage ethical and compliance risks. Ensure suppliers comply with labor laws, environmental regulations, and anti-corruption rules. Conduct audits where necessary. If you find violations, require corrective action or terminate the contract. Protect your reputation. Finally, close the contract properly. When the contract ends, conduct a final performance evaluation. Document lessons learned. Return any assets or documents. Settle final payments. Provide a completion certificate. If the supplier performed well, consider them for future work. If not, record the reasons and remove them from your approved list. Good contract management and supplier relationship management reduce total cost of ownership, improve quality, and build resilience. They also make your procurement function more strategic. In the next lesson, we will cover procurement ethics, fraud prevention and compliance in African contexts. For now, review one of your existing contracts. Does it have a contract management plan? Are performance indicators being tracked? If not, create a simple plan this week.

5. Procurement Ethics, Fraud Prevention and Compliance in Africa

Welcome to Lesson 5, the final lesson of Module 9. Ethics, fraud prevention and compliance are not optional extras. They are core to effective procurement. In Africa, procurement is often a high-risk area for corruption, collusion, and fraud. Your job is to protect your organization's money, reputation and legal standing. This lesson gives you practical tools. First, understand the common risks. Bribery and kickbacks occur when suppliers pay staff to win contracts or approve invoices. Collusion happens when bidders secretly agree on prices or rotate winning bids. Bid rigging includes cover pricing, bid suppression, and market allocation. Fraud includes fake invoices, duplicate payments, phantom suppliers, and inflated quantities. Conflict of interest occurs when staff have personal relationships with suppliers. These risks are present everywhere, but they are heightened in environments with weak oversight, low pay, and strong informal networks. Second, build a strong ethical framework. Adopt a code of conduct for procurement staff and suppliers. The code should cover gifts, hospitality, conflicts of interest, confidentiality, and fair treatment. Set a clear gift policy. For example, staff may accept modest promotional items but must declare anything above a low threshold. They must never accept cash, travel, or expensive gifts. Require suppliers to sign the code. Include anti-bribery and anti-corruption clauses in contracts. Third, implement segregation of duties. No single person should be able to initiate a purchase, approve it, receive the goods, and pay the invoice. Split these functions across different people or teams. For small organizations, this may be difficult, but you can at least have a second person review. Use a procurement committee for high-value purchases. Rotate committee members periodically. Fourth, use transparency tools. Publish tender opportunities and awards where possible. Use open competitive bidding. Keep a bid opening register. Record all bids and reasons for rejection. Use a supplier database with verified information. Conduct due diligence on new suppliers, including beneficial ownership checks. In some countries, you can access company registries online. In others, you may need to visit the registry. Check for links to politically exposed persons. Fifth, control payments. Match invoices to purchase orders and delivery notes. Use a three-way match: purchase order, goods receipt note, and invoice. Investigate any mismatch. Pay by bank transfer, not cash, so there is a trace. Verify bank account details directly with the supplier using a known contact, not the details on the invoice. This prevents invoice redirection fraud. Reconcile supplier statements regularly. Sixth, detect fraud early. Watch for warning signs: a supplier who always wins, a bidder who withdraws unexpectedly, prices that are higher than market, repeated emergency purchases, missing documents, or staff who refuse to take leave. Encourage whistleblowing. Set up a confidential reporting channel, such as a phone line or email. Protect whistleblowers from retaliation. Investigate all reports promptly and fairly. Seventh, comply with laws and regulations. Each African country has its own procurement laws. In Kenya, the Public Procurement and Asset Disposal Act and the Ethics and Anti-Corruption Commission Act apply. In Nigeria, the Public Procurement Act and the Corrupt Practices and Other Related Offences Act apply. In South Africa, the Public Finance Management Act and the Prevention and Combating of Corrupt Activities Act apply. In Ghana, the Public Procurement Act applies. Know the rules for your country and sector. Also comply with international standards if you work with donors or multinationals. For example, the World Bank has strict procurement guidelines and sanctions firms for fraud. The OECD Anti-Bribery Convention applies to many cross-border transactions. Eighth, train your staff. Ethics training should be practical, not just a lecture. Use case studies from African contexts. Role-play difficult situations. Discuss what to do if a supplier offers a gift, or if a manager pressures you to bypass a tender. Make training mandatory and repeat it annually. Ninth, manage conflicts of interest. Require staff to declare any personal, family, or financial relationship with a supplier. Keep a register of declarations. Recuse affected staff from related decisions. In some cultures, family obligations are strong. A clear policy helps staff say no. Tenth, build a culture of integrity. Leaders must model ethical behavior. If senior managers accept gifts or bypass rules, staff will follow. Celebrate ethical decisions. Recognize staff who report wrongdoing. Make ethics part of performance reviews. Finally, review and improve. Conduct periodic audits of procurement processes. Use internal audit or an external firm. Track key risk indicators, such as the percentage of single-source awards, average number of bids per tender, and payment errors. Act on findings. Update your policies as laws and risks change. Remember, ethical procurement is not just about avoiding punishment. It delivers better value, fair competition, and public trust. It also protects you personally. In many countries, procurement officers can be personally liable for fraud or corruption. By following the rules and documenting your decisions, you protect yourself and your organization. This concludes Module 9. You have learned how to identify suppliers, run tenders, negotiate, manage contracts, and uphold ethics. Use these lessons in your daily work. Start with one improvement this week. Build a supplier map, review a tender process, prepare a negotiation, create a contract management plan, or strengthen an ethics control. Small steps lead to big results. Thank you for your participation.

Procuring animal-health products, vaccines, medicated feed or agrochemicals carries legal and biological risk. Always verify: (1) the supplier holds a valid national registration (e.g. KEPHIS, NAFDAC, DAFF, or the national veterinary authority); (2) the batch number and expiry date are printed on the primary label, not just the carton; (3) the cold chain has been maintained â request the temperature logger download for vaccines; (4) the product is registered for use in your country, not just in the country of origin. Counterfeit and sub-potent veterinary products are widely documented across African markets and can cause treatment failure, drug residues in milk, meat or eggs, and antimicrobial resistance. Never accept repackaged, unlabelled or hand-decanted product, and quarantine all incoming batches until QC release.

Core Procurement Document Set

Every purchase above a small threshold should be traceable through a fixed document chain. Missing a single document is the most common cause of delayed clearing, failed audits and unrecoverable losses.

  • Requisition â internal, states quantity, specification, delivery date and budget line.
  • Request for Quotation / Tender â issued to at least three suppliers where feasible.
  • Purchase Order (PO) â the legal offer; must state Incoterm, currency, payment terms and inspection rights.
  • Proforma Invoice â supplier's confirmation; basis for LC or advance payment.
  • Commercial Invoice & Packing List â must match PO line by line.
  • Bill of Lading / Airway Bill / Road Consignment Note â title document; do not release original to a third party.
  • Certificate of Origin â determines duty under COMESA, EAC, SADC or AfCFTA preferential tariffs.
  • Phytosanitary / Health Certificate â mandatory for seeds, plants, animal products and veterinary inputs.
  • Goods Received Note (GRN) â signed at the gate after quantity and condition check; the trigger for payment.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Is the specification written in measurable terms (protein %, moisture %, CFU count, expiry window) rather than brand names?
  2. Have I obtained at least three written quotes and compared them on landed cost per usable unit, not invoice price?
  3. Does the PO state the Incoterm, currency, payment terms, inspection rights and delivery window in writing?
  4. Have I confirmed the supplier's registration, tax compliance certificate and, for health inputs, product registration?
  5. Is the Certificate of Origin present so I can claim AfCFTA, COMESA, EAC or SADC preferential duty?
  6. Have I budgeted for FX spread, clearing agent fees, demurrage risk and 1â4% shrinkage?
  7. Is the cold chain or storage condition verified on arrival, with a temperature log or seal number recorded on the GRN?
  8. Have I filed the full document chain (PO â invoice â BoL â CoO â GRN) in one auditable folder before releasing payment?
