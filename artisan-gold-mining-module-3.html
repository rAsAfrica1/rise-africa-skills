1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Prospecting is the first step in finding gold. It means looking for signs that gold exists in an area. Sampling is testing the ground to see if there is enough gold to make mining worthwhile. Without proper prospecting and sampling, you could waste weeks digging in a place with no gold. In Africa, many artisanal miners work old colonial mines or alluvial deposits along rivers. But gold can also be in quartz veins in hard rock. You need to know where to look and how to test. This module teaches you how to find gold and test it without expensive equipment. You will learn to read the land, use simple tools, and take samples correctly. Remember: gold is heavy and does not travel far from its source. So if you find gold in a river, it came from somewhere upstream. Follow the trail. Also, gold is often found with other minerals like quartz, ironstone, and pyrite. These are indicators. Learning to spot them saves time. Sampling means taking a small amount of soil or rock and panning it to see if there is gold. You must take samples from different spots and depths. A single sample is not enough. You need to test many points to find the richest area. This module will show you how. By the end, you will be able to prospect and sample like a professional, increasing your chances of striking gold. This knowledge is power. It turns guessing into a science. So pay attention and practice. Your success depends on it.

2. Tools and Materials

To prospect and sample, you need a few basic tools. Most can be found locally or made yourself. First, a gold pan. You can buy a plastic or metal pan, or make one from a car wheel rim or a large cooking pot. A pan about 30-40 cm wide works well. Second, a shovel and a pickaxe for digging. A hoe can also work in soft soil. Third, a bucket or two for carrying samples and water. Fourth, a sieve or classifier to remove large rocks. You can make one with a wooden frame and wire mesh (about 5 mm holes). Fifth, a magnifying glass or hand lens to examine gold particles. Sixth, a notebook and pencil to record where you took samples and what you found. Seventh, a small bottle or vial to store gold flakes. Eighth, a snuffer bottle (a plastic bottle with a tube) to suck up fine gold. Ninth, a trowel for scraping. Tenth, a measuring tape to mark distances. For hard rock, you need a hammer and chisel, or a crowbar. Safety gear: gloves, boots, and eye protection. In Africa, many miners use a âbanjoâ or âsluice boxâ for larger samples. You can build one with wood and a piece of carpet or mat. Water is essential for panning. If you are far from water, carry it in jerrycans. A small plastic basin is useful for panning when water is scarce. Always keep your tools clean to avoid contamination. A dirty pan can give false results. Also, a GPS or smartphone with GPS helps record locations. If you donât have one, use landmarks. Finally, a first aid kit for injuries. With these tools, you are ready to start prospecting.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps to prospect and sample effectively. Step 1: Research the area. Talk to local miners, look at geological maps if available, and check for old mine workings. Gold is often found near faults, quartz veins, and river bends. Step 2: Walk the land. Look for indicators: quartz rocks, ironstone, and black sand in streams. Check riverbanks and gravel bars. Gold is heavy, so it settles in inside bends and behind large rocks. Step 3: Choose sampling points. Take samples along a line across the stream or across a suspected vein. Space them 5-10 meters apart. Also sample at different depths: surface, 30 cm, and 60 cm. Step 4: Dig a hole about 30 cm wide and 30-60 cm deep. Put all material into a bucket. For stream samples, dig where the water is slow and gravel is deposited. Step 5: Sieve the material to remove large rocks. Keep the fine material. Step 6: Pan the sample. Fill your pan half with material and add water. Swirl and shake to wash away light material. Gold, being heavy, stays at the bottom. Use a gentle circular motion. Tilt the pan and let the lighter stuff wash off. Repeat until only black sand and maybe gold remain. Step 7: Look for gold. Use a magnifying glass. Gold is bright yellow and shiny. It does not tarnish. If you see gold, note how many flakes or pieces. Step 8: Record your findings. Write down the location, depth, and amount of gold. Step 9: Take multiple samples. One sample is not enough. Take at least 10 samples per area. Step 10: If you find good gold, take a larger sample (10-20 kg) for a more accurate test. Pan it down and weigh the gold. This tells you if the area is worth mining. Always clean your pan between samples to avoid contamination. Follow these steps and you will sample like a pro.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Many miners make mistakes that lead to poor results. Here are common ones and how to fix them. Mistake 1: Sampling only the surface. Gold often settles deeper. Fix: Dig at least 30-60 cm deep. Mistake 2: Taking only one sample. Gold is not evenly distributed. Fix: Take at least 10 samples from different spots. Mistake 3: Using a dirty pan. Leftover gold from a previous sample can mislead you. Fix: Wash your pan thoroughly between samples. Mistake 4: Panning too fast. You will lose fine gold. Fix: Use a slow, gentle circular motion. Let the water do the work. Mistake 5: Not using a sieve. Large rocks hide gold and make panning hard. Fix: Always sieve first. Mistake 6: Ignoring black sand. Black sand is heavy and often contains gold. Fix: Pan until only black sand remains, then look carefully. Mistake 7: Not recording locations. You will forget where you found gold. Fix: Keep a notebook with GPS coordinates or landmarks. Mistake 8: Sampling in the wrong place. Gold is not everywhere. Fix: Look for indicators like quartz veins, river bends, and old workings. Mistake 9: Using too much water. It washes away gold. Fix: Use just enough water to cover the material. Mistake 10: Giving up too soon. Prospecting takes time. Fix: Be patient and systematic. Mistake 11: Not checking for fine gold. Fine gold is hard to see. Fix: Use a magnifying glass and look for tiny specks. Mistake 12: Contaminating samples with gold from your hands or tools. Fix: Wash your hands and tools before sampling. Mistake 13: Sampling only in dry season. Water levels change. Fix: Sample in both dry and wet seasons if possible. Mistake 14: Not testing the bedrock. Gold can be trapped in cracks. Fix: Sample bedrock crevices. Mistake 15: Using wrong equipment. A shovel is not enough for hard rock. Fix: Use a pickaxe or hammer. Avoid these mistakes and your sampling will be accurate.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Prospecting and sampling can be dangerous if you ignore safety. Follow these rules to stay safe. Always work with a partner. Never go alone into remote areas. Tell someone where you are going and when you will return. Wear protective clothing: long sleeves, long trousers, boots, and gloves. Use eye protection when breaking rocks. In hot climates, carry plenty of water and avoid working during the hottest part of the day. Watch out for snakes, scorpions, and other animals. Shake out your boots before putting them on. Be careful near water. Rivers can rise quickly. Never enter deep water alone. If you are digging, make sure the hole is stable. Avoid digging under overhangs or in loose soil that can collapse. Use a ladder or rope to get out of deep holes. When panning, sit or stand on stable ground. Avoid slippery rocks. If you use mercury to recover fine gold, be extremely careful. Mercury is toxic. Never touch it with bare hands. Never heat it indoors. Use it only in a well-ventilated area and wear gloves and a mask. Better yet, avoid mercury and use panning or a sluice box. Respect the environment. Do not cut down trees unnecessarily. Fill in holes after sampling to prevent accidents and erosion. Do not pollute streams with oil or chemicals. Follow local laws. In many African countries, you need a permit to prospect or mine. Check with local authorities. Do not trespass on private land. Respect community rights. If you find gold, do not boast. It can attract thieves. Keep your gold secure. Best practice: keep detailed records. Note the date, location, depth, and amount of gold. Take photos. This helps you plan your mining. Also, keep your tools clean and in good condition. A broken shovel can cause injury. Finally, learn from experienced miners. Ask questions. Join a local mining association. Safety and good practice protect you and your community.

6. Practical Exercise

Now itâs time to practice what you learned. This exercise will guide you through a real sampling session. You will need: a gold pan, shovel, bucket, sieve, magnifying glass, notebook, and water. Choose a stream or river near you that is known to have gold, or a dry riverbed with gravel. If you are in a gold-bearing area, even better. Step 1: Select a 50-meter stretch of the stream. Walk along it and look for inside bends, gravel bars, and areas with black sand. Step 2: Mark 5 sampling points along the stretch, spaced about 10 meters apart. At each point, dig a hole 30 cm deep and 30 cm wide. Put the material into a bucket. Step 3: At each point, also dig a second hole 60 cm deep. Keep the material separate. Step 4: Sieve each sample to remove rocks larger than 5 mm. Step 5: Pan each sample separately. Use a slow, gentle motion. Pan until only black sand and maybe gold remain. Step 6: Examine the pan with a magnifying glass. Count the number of gold flakes or pieces. Record the count for each sample, noting the location and depth. Step 7: After panning all samples, compare your results. Which point had the most gold? Was the gold at 30 cm or 60 cm? Step 8: If you found good gold, take a larger sample (10 kg) from the best point. Pan it down and weigh the gold. If you have a scale, weigh it in grams. If not, use a small bottle and estimate. Step 9: Write a short report. Include a sketch of the stream, your sampling points, and your findings. Step 10: Clean all tools and store them properly. Repeat this exercise in different areas to build your skills. Remember, practice makes perfect. The more you sample, the better you will become at finding gold. Always follow safety rules. Good luck!

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Prospecting and Sampling for Artisan Gold Mining

This practical reference consolidates the field knowledge an artisanal gold miner in Africa needs to move from informal digging to a structured, business-minded prospecting and sampling operation. It is written for the working miner, the site supervisor, and the small-scale mining entrepreneur who must decide where to dig, how much material to process, and whether a deposit can sustain a profitable operation. In artisanal and small-scale gold mining (ASGM), the difference between a profitable season and a loss is usually made before the first shovel hits the ground â during prospecting and sampling. Poor sampling leads to over-investment in barren ground; disciplined sampling directs labour, fuel, and capital to the richest, most accessible ore.

1. Why Sampling Is a Business Decision, Not a Formality

Every sample you take is a small investment of time, fuel, and labour. The return on that investment is information: an estimate of grade (grams of gold per tonne of ore) and the continuity of the mineralised zone. In African ASGM, where most operators work on thin margins and rely on cash from each processing cycle, sampling is the cheapest way to avoid the most expensive mistake â sinking a shaft or buying a plot that cannot repay the cost of extraction. A disciplined sampling programme answers three commercial questions: Is there enough gold to cover my costs? How much material must I move per gram recovered? Where should I concentrate my workforce?

3. Step-by-Step Field Method

  1. Reconnaissance. Walk the ground. Note old workings, quartz float, ironstone, stream gravels, and vegetation changes. Mark boundaries with GPS or local landmarks.
  2. Set the grid. Use Table 2 to choose spacing. Peg corners with durable stakes and record coordinates.
  3. Dig test pits or auger holes. Reach bedrock or the target layer. Log depth of each layer.
  4. Collect a consistent sample. Take the full thickness of the target layer, not just the richest-looking pocket. Quarter or riffle to the required mass.
  5. Label and record. Unique sample ID, coordinates, depth, mass, date, and collector. This record is your business data.
  6. Composite where appropriate. Combine 4â5 points into a bulk sample to reduce assay cost while preserving spatial signal.
  7. Process or assay. Pan, sluice, or send to a laboratory. Panning is fast but only semi-quantitative; laboratory assay is required for investment decisions.
  8. Map results. Plot grade on the grid. Identify anomalies and decide the next round of sampling or a pilot pit.

Never enter or dig an unshored pit deeper than 1.5 m. Collapse is the leading cause of death in African ASGM. Test pit walls for loose material, keep a second person at surface, and never work alone. Mercury must never be used in sampling or processing without closed retort and PPE â it is highly toxic and illegal in many African jurisdictions. Abandoned old workings can contain bad air; test with a flame or gas meter before entry. Follow national mining regulations and obtain the required licences before sampling on any ground.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

  • Sampling only the richest spot. Fix: sample the full layer thickness and use a grid.
  • Too few samples. Fix: minimum 15â20 points per prospect area before any investment decision.
  • Contamination. Fix: clean tools between samples; use separate bags; avoid rusty or oily equipment.
  • Confusing pan colour with grade. Fix: panning is a guide only â confirm with assay.
  • No records. Fix: a notebook or phone log with sample ID, location, depth, and mass is non-negotiable.
