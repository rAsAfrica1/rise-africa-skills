Fly Ash Block

8 lessons · 30 quiz questions · Medium difficulty

1 What is fly ash

Fly ash is the fine, powdery ash carried out of a coal-fired furnace in the flue gases. It's captured by electrostatic precipitators or bag filters before the gases exit the chimney.

Two types matter for block-making:

  • Class F fly ash — from burning anthracite or bituminous coal. Low calcium. Pozzolanic — reacts with lime and water to form cement-like compounds, but only slowly.
  • Class C fly ash — from burning lignite or sub-bituminous coal. Higher calcium. Cementitious on its own — reacts with water directly.

Class F is more common in Africa. It works with lime + cement to make blocks. Class C is used in the US and parts of India.

💡 Fly ash is a huge industrial waste stream. Power stations pay to dispose of it. In many places you can get it free or for a nominal fee.

2 Sourcing from power stations

How to get fly ash in Africa:

  1. Contact the power station — ask for the "ash manager" or "byproduct officer".
  2. Ask for a sample — 25 kg is enough for testing.
  3. Test the ash — send to a lab for fineness, carbon content, pozzolanic activity.
  4. Negotiate a supply agreement — free if you collect, or $5–$20 per tonne delivered.
  5. Arrange transport — fly ash is dusty; use covered trucks or bulk tankers.

Sources: Hwange (Zimbabwe), Medupi, Kusile, Lethabo (South Africa), Kipevu (Kenya), Morupule (Botswana), Kriel (South Africa).

Some stations give away fly ash for free to encourage recycling. Others charge. Always negotiate in writing.

3 Fly ash reactivity basics

Class F fly ash is pozzolanic — it reacts with lime (Ca(OH)₂) and water to form calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H), the same compound that gives concrete its strength.

Reaction requires:

  • Lime — from cement hydration or added hydrated lime
  • Water — enough to keep the reaction going
  • Time — fly ash blocks gain strength slower than cement-only blocks

Strength development:

  • 7 days: 40–60% of final strength
  • 28 days: 70–80%
  • 90 days: 100% (much slower than dense blocks)

This is why fly ash blocks must be cured longer. Don't sell them at 7 days.

4 Cement-lime-fly ash ratios

Standard fly ash block mix (by weight):

  • Cement (OPC): 10–15%
  • Hydrated lime: 5–10%
  • Fly ash: 30–40%
  • Sharp sand: 35–50%
  • Water: earth-damp consistency

Example for a 100 kg dry batch:

  • Cement: 12 kg
  • Lime: 8 kg
  • Fly ash: 35 kg
  • Sand: 45 kg
  • Water: about 12–15 kg

Higher fly ash = lower cost but slower strength gain. Higher cement = faster cure and higher early strength.

For block machine production (vibrated, pressed), the mix is drier than hand-moulded. Water content must be right — too dry = crumbly, too wet = slumping.

5 Mixing for consistency

Fly ash is dustier than sand and clumps more easily. Mixing order matters:

  1. Mix cement + lime dry for 30 seconds
  2. Add fly ash and mix dry for 1 minute (breaks up clumps)
  3. Add sand and mix dry for 1 minute
  4. Add water slowly — 1 minute of adding while mixing
  5. Mix for 2–3 more minutes total

Total mixing time: 5–7 minutes. Longer than dense concrete.

Watch out for:

  • Dry clumps that don't mix — sieve fly ash before use if you see clumps
  • Dust clouds — wear masks (fly ash is a respiratory hazard, contains silica)
  • Over-mixing — fly ash mixes can stiffen after 8+ minutes; add small water splashes to keep workable

6 Compacting in moulds

Fly ash block production follows the same process as dense blocks:

  1. Fill mould in two layers
  2. Compact first layer
  3. Fill second layer
  4. Vibrate 5–7 seconds or tamp hard by hand
  5. Level top with a straight edge
  6. Demould straight up

Because fly ash is lighter than sand, the mix has less "weight" pushing down. Extra compaction is needed in the corners of the mould. Watch for honeycomb.

Fly ash blocks can also be made by static press (higher pressure) if you have a machine designed for it — gives stronger blocks with less cement.

7 Accelerated curing methods

Fly ash blocks gain strength slowly. Several methods speed this up:

  • Warm water (40°C): Warmer water in the mix accelerates the lime reaction. Only works for small batches.
  • Steam curing at atmospheric pressure: Steam the blocks in a covered chamber at 60–80°C for 12–24 hours. Cuts curing time in half.
  • Wet blanket curing: Keep blocks wrapped in damp cloth for 14 days instead of 7.
  • Higher cement: Add 2–3% more cement to the mix. Faster strength gain, more expensive.
  • Chemical accelerators: Calcium chloride (CaCl₂) at 1–2% of cement weight. Cheap and effective but not for reinforced applications.

The simplest approach: cure for 14 days instead of 7. Longer but free.

8 Selling fly ash as eco-friendly

Fly ash blocks have a strong environmental story:

  • Uses industrial waste — every tonne of fly ash used saves a tonne from landfill
  • Reduces cement use by 40–50% — cement production is 8% of global CO₂ emissions
  • Lower embodied carbon — an average fly ash block has 30% less carbon than a dense concrete block
  • Durable — fly ash blocks gain strength for years, not just months

Who to sell to:

  • Green building certifications (LEED, EDGE, Green Star) — fly ash contributes points
  • Environmental-conscious clients — NGO buildings, eco-lodges
  • Government projects — increasingly require recycled content
  • Cost-sensitive markets — where ash is cheap, fly ash blocks undercut competitors

Price: $0.50–$0.90 per block typical in Africa (cheaper than dense concrete when ash is cheap).

💰 Fly ash blocks are a smart play where you have free fly ash and want a green-selling story. The blocks are durable, cheaper, and get you into eco-friendly markets.
