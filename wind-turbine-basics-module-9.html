Lessons

1. Preventive Maintenance Schedules

Preventive maintenance (PM) means servicing a wind turbine before something breaks. In Africa, where turbines often run in dusty, hot, or humid conditions, sticking to a schedule is critical. Start by checking your turbine manual. It will list tasks like greasing bearings every 3 months, checking bolt torque every 6 months, and inspecting blades every 12 months. But you must adapt to your site. If your turbine is near a dusty road or in a coastal area with salt spray, shorten those intervals. For example, in the Sahel, dust can clog filters in weeks, so check air filters monthly.

Create a simple calendar. Use a notebook or a whiteboard in your control room. List each turbine, the task, and the due date. For each task, write the tools needed: torque wrench (set to 200 Nm for M20 bolts), grease gun with lithium-based grease, and a multimeter for electrical checks. Assign a technician. In many African workshops, one technician can handle 5 small turbines per day if tasks are grouped by location.

When you do a PM, record everything. Note the date, the technician's name, and any abnormalities. For example, if you find a loose bolt, tighten it and note the torque value. If you see rust on a tower section, note it for the next repair. This log becomes your history. After 6 months, review it. Are you doing too much or too little? Adjust intervals based on failures. If a gearbox oil analysis shows high iron particles at 6 months, change oil every 4 months instead.

Finally, train your team. A PM schedule only works if everyone follows it. Hold a 15-minute meeting each Monday to review the week's tasks. Use local languages if needed. Make sure spare parts and tools are ready before the task. In remote areas, plan for transport delays. Order grease and filters at least 2 months ahead. With discipline, PM cuts downtime by up to 50% and extends turbine life by years.

2. Gearbox and Bearing Lubrication

Lubrication is the lifeblood of a wind turbine's gearbox and bearings. Without it, metal grinds against metal, creating heat and failure. In Africa, high temperatures and dust make lubrication even more important. You need the right grease or oil, the right amount, and the right method.

For gearboxes, most small to medium turbines use synthetic gear oil, like ISO VG 320. Check your manual. You will need to drain old oil, flush if contaminated, and refill to the correct level. A typical 50 kW turbine gearbox holds about 10 liters. Use a hand pump or a funnel with a filter. Never mix different oil brands. In dusty areas, change oil every 6 months instead of 12. Send a sample to a lab for analysis if possible; if not, look for dark color or metal particles on a magnet.

For bearings, use a grease gun with a lithium complex grease, NLGI grade 2. Main shaft bearings and generator bearings need greasing every 3 months. Pump slowly until you see fresh grease come out of the seal. Wipe off excess; too much grease can overheat. In hot climates, use a grease with a high dropping point (above 200C). For yaw bearings, grease every 6 months.

Always clean the grease fitting before attaching the gun. Dust pushed into a bearing will destroy it. Use a rag and a brush. If a fitting is broken, replace it immediately. Keep a log: date, bearing location, grease type, and amount. For example, 'Main bearing: 30 grams, lithium complex, 15 Jan 2025.'

If you hear squealing or feel vibration, stop the turbine and check lubrication. Lack of grease can cause bearing failure in days. In remote African sites, keep a spare grease gun and 2 kg of grease per turbine. Train one person per site as the lubrication specialist. With proper lubrication, gearboxes and bearings can last 20 years instead of 5.

3. Blade Inspection and Repair

Wind turbine blades are constantly hit by dust, sand, rain, and sometimes birds. In Africa, UV radiation is strong, and sandstorms can erode the leading edge. Regular inspection catches small damage before it becomes a major repair.

Inspect blades every 6 months, or after a storm. Use binoculars from the ground first. Look for cracks, chips, holes, or discoloration. If you see damage, climb the tower or use a drone if available. For a closer look, use a rope access kit or a cherry picker. Check the leading edge, trailing edge, and tip. Pay attention to lightning receptors and any bond lines.

Common repairs: for small chips (less than 5 cm), clean the area with acetone, then fill with epoxy putty. Sand smooth. For cracks, drill a small hole at each end to stop propagation, then fill with epoxy and cover with fiberglass cloth. For leading edge erosion, apply a protective tape or paint. Use a two-part epoxy that cures at room temperature. In hot climates, work early morning to avoid rapid curing.

Always wear safety glasses, gloves, and a dust mask. Work with a partner. Use a torque wrench to tighten blade bolts to the specified value, often 300 Nm for M24 bolts. Check bolt torque every 12 months.

After repair, record the damage location, size, and materials used. Take photos. If damage is severe (crack longer than 30 cm or structural), call a professional. In many African countries, local fiberglass repair shops can help. Keep a repair kit on site: epoxy, fiberglass cloth, acetone, sandpaper, and brushes. With quick action, blades can last 15-20 years. Neglect leads to blade failure, which can destroy the turbine and injure people.

4. Vibration Analysis and Fault Detection

Vibration analysis is like a doctor listening to a heartbeat. It helps you find problems in bearings, gears, and shafts before they fail. In Africa, where spare parts can take weeks to arrive, early detection saves money and downtime.

You need a vibration meter or a simple accelerometer. A basic handheld meter costs around $200. Mount it on the gearbox, generator, and main bearing housing. Measure velocity in mm/s (RMS) and acceleration in g. Take readings monthly, or weekly if you suspect a problem. Record the values. A healthy turbine typically has vibration below 2.8 mm/s. If it rises above 4.5 mm/s, investigate.

Common faults: unbalance (high 1x RPM), misalignment (high 2x RPM), bearing wear (high frequency spikes), and gear mesh problems (high frequency at gear mesh frequency). You don't need to be an expert. Just look for trends. If vibration increases by 50% in a month, something is wrong.

Use a stethoscope or a screwdriver to your ear as a backup. Listen for grinding, clicking, or rumbling. For a more detailed look, use a smartphone app with an accelerometer. Many free apps can show a spectrum.

When you find high vibration, check the obvious: loose bolts, worn belts, or lack of lubrication. Tighten bolts to the correct torque. If vibration persists, plan to replace the bearing or gearbox. In remote areas, keep a spare bearing.

Train one technician per site to take readings and interpret simple trends. Write a one-page guide with acceptable limits. For example, 'Main bearing: alert if > 3.5 mm/s, stop if > 7 mm/s.' This simple system can prevent catastrophic failures. In Africa, where turbines often run unattended, vibration monitoring is your best early warning.

5. Spare Parts Management

Spare parts management is about having the right part, in the right place, at the right time. In Africa, supply chains can be slow, so you must plan ahead. Without spares, a small fault can stop a turbine for weeks.

Start by listing critical parts. These are items that cause downtime if they fail: gearbox oil, grease, bearings (main, generator, yaw), bolts (M20, M24), fuses, contactors, and brake pads. For each, decide the minimum stock. For example, keep 2 main bearings, 10 liters of gear oil, and 5 kg of grease per turbine. Also keep consumables: rags, acetone, epoxy, and sandpaper.

Use a simple bin system. Label each bin with the part name and minimum quantity. When you take a part, write it on a clipboard or a whiteboard. Once a week, check stock and order what is low. In areas with unreliable internet, use a paper log. For expensive parts like a gearbox, keep one spare for every 10 turbines.

Store parts properly. Keep bearings in their original packaging, away from moisture. Grease and oil should be in a cool, dry place. Bolts should be sorted by size. In dusty areas, cover bins with lids.

Build relationships with local suppliers. In Kenya, Nigeria, or South Africa, there are suppliers for bearings and electrical components. Get their phone numbers and delivery times. For specialized parts, order from overseas but allow 3 months. Use a courier that tracks shipments.

Review your spares usage every 6 months. If you never use a part, reduce stock. If you run out often, increase. Train one person as the storekeeper. A good spare parts system reduces downtime by 70%. It is not about having everything, but having the right things.

6. Work Order and CMMS Systems

A work order is a written instruction to do a maintenance task. A CMMS (Computerized Maintenance Management System) is software that tracks work orders, spare parts, and history. In Africa, you can start with paper, then move to a simple CMMS on a phone or computer.

For paper work orders, use a standard form. It should have: work order number, date, turbine ID, task description, tools needed, spare parts used, technician name, and completion time. For example, 'WO-101: Grease main bearing on Turbine 3. Tools: grease gun, lithium grease. Completed by John, 2 hours.' Keep these forms in a binder.

When you get a fault, create a work order. If it is an emergency (turbine stopped), mark it 'Priority 1' and act immediately. For routine tasks, schedule for the next week. Assign to a technician. After the work, the technician signs off and notes any issues.

A simple CMMS can be a spreadsheet. List all work orders, their status (open, in progress, closed), and due dates. Or use free apps like UpKeep or Fiix. They work offline and sync when you have internet. On a smartphone, you can take photos and attach them to the work order.

Benefits: you know what was done, when, and by whom. You can track recurring problems. For example, if Turbine 2 has a bearing failure every 6 months, you can investigate root cause. You can also plan spares based on work order history.

Train your team to use the system. Keep it simple. In remote areas, use paper and then enter into the CMMS weekly. Hold a 10-minute meeting each morning to review open work orders. With a good work order system, you reduce confusion, improve accountability, and extend turbine life. Start today with a notebook and a pen.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Maintenance and Reliability for Wind Turbines in Africa

Wind turbines are long-life assets 20 to 25 years but their economics are decided in the first three to five years of operation. In African markets where grid tariffs, diesel back-up costs and hard-currency spare parts are all volatile, reliability is not an engineering nicety. It is a cash-flow instrument. Every unplanned stop on a 2 MW turbine at a 35% capacity factor costs roughly 700 MWh of lost generation per day. At a typical commercial tariff of USD 0.10/kWh, that is USD 70,000 per day of downtime before you count the crane, the imported gearbox and the expatriate technician.

This practical reference consolidates the seven lessons in this module into a single working document. It is written for the site manager, the O&M supervisor and the asset owner who must defend a maintenance budget to a lender or an offtaker. Use it as a bench reference, not a textbook.

Why reliability pays in African wind portfolios

Most African wind capacity sits in South Africa, Egypt, Morocco, Kenya, Ethiopia and Tunisia, with newer projects in Senegal, Mauritania, Tanzania and Djibouti. Common operating realities:

  • Long spare-parts lead times. A main bearing or gearbox from a European OEM can take 1224 weeks to reach a site in Kenya or Senegal once customs clearance is included.
  • Skills concentration. Certified blade technicians and vibration analysts are scarce outside a few hubs (Cape Town, Casablanca, Nairobi, Cairo).
  • Dust and salt. Saharan dust accelerates blade leading-edge erosion; coastal sites in Morocco and Senegal drive corrosion on tower and nacelle hardware.
  • Grid instability. Frequent curtailment and voltage dips stress converters and yaw systems, which shows up later as drivetrain faults.
  • Financing covenants. Lenders and DFIs (IFC, AfDB, DFC) increasingly tie debt service to availability guarantees of 9597%.

The practical implication: shift from calendar-based maintenance to condition-based and risk-based maintenance, and make sure the CMMS can survive a two-week connectivity outage.

Preventive Maintenance Schedules the backbone

A good PM schedule is a contract with the asset. It defines what is done, by whom, with what tools, at what interval. For African sites, three adjustments matter:

  • Compress intervals during the first 12 months. Infant mortality on gearboxes, pitch bearings and yaw drives is real. A 6-month oil sample instead of 12 months costs little and catches early wear.
  • Align with weather windows. In the Western Cape, August winds make climbing unsafe. In Egypt's Gulf of Suez, summer heat limits rope-access blade work to early morning.
  • Bundle tasks. Every turbine visit has a fixed cost (crane, crew, permit). Combine lubrication, torque checks, blade inspection and vibration route into one mobilisation.

Gearbox and Bearing Lubrication

Lubrication is the cheapest reliability intervention available. Gearbox oil analysis detects iron, copper and water ingress long before a bearing fails. Establish a baseline at commissioning, then sample every 6 months. Watch three parameters: particle count (ISO 4406), water content (ppm) and additive depletion (viscosity, TAN).

Grease-lubricated pitch and yaw bearings need a different discipline. Over-greasing blows seals and pushes grease into the environment; under-greasing causes brinelling. Follow the OEM's gram-per-point specification and log every point on the CMMS.

Blade Inspection and Repair

Blades cause more lost production than any other subsystem because damage is progressive and often invisible from the ground. Leading-edge erosion from dust and rain, lightning damage, and trailing-edge cracks are the three most common findings. In Africa, add sand-blast erosion on coastal sites and UV degradation on high-altitude sites in Ethiopia and Kenya.

Inspect at least annually with rope access or drone, and after every major storm or lightning event. Repair within 30 days for leading-edge erosion and immediately for structural cracks. Keep a repair log with photos it is your evidence in an OEM warranty dispute.

Vibration Analysis and Fault Detection

Vibration monitoring is the highest-return condition-monitoring tool on a wind turbine. It detects gearbox bearing faults, generator bearing wear, main bearing damage and imbalance months before failure. Use ISO 10816 velocity limits as a starting point, then build site-specific baselines.

Spare Parts Management

Spare parts strategy is a capital allocation decision. Classify parts by criticality and lead time:

  • Class A (critical, long lead): gearbox, main bearing, generator, transformer. Keep one shared spare per fleet or a formal OEM service agreement.
  • Class B (critical, short lead): pitch motors, yaw drives, contactors, sensors. Stock on site.
  • Class C (consumables): filters, grease, bolts, seals. Stock with 3-month buffer.

For African sites, add a customs buffer: assume 48 weeks of clearance time on top of the supplier's quoted lead time. Use a consignment or bonded-warehouse arrangement with the OEM where possible.

SAFETY WARNING

Never release a work order for nacelle, hub or blade work without a valid permit-to-work, verified LOTO (lock-out/tag-out) on all energy sources, and a rescue plan for the specific access method. Wind turbine fatalities in emerging markets are disproportionately linked to skipped LOTO and inadequate rescue planning. Confirm the crew's working-at-height certification and rescue-at-height training before mobilisation this is a legal requirement under most national OSH acts and a condition of most project insurance policies.

Work Order and CMMS Systems

A CMMS is only as good as the data going into it. For African sites, choose a system that works offline, syncs when connectivity returns, and supports mobile data capture. Minimum fields per work order: asset ID, fault code, priority, parts consumed, labour hours, downtime start/end, and root-cause note.

Use the CMMS to feed three business reports monthly: (1) availability by turbine, (2) MTBF on critical components, (3) maintenance cost per MWh. These are the numbers your lender, your board and your O&M contractor will argue about.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Confirm PM schedule is loaded in the CMMS and aligned with local weather windows.
  2. Verify oil samples for all gearboxes are taken every 6 months and trended against baseline.
  3. Check all grease points have been serviced to OEM gram specification and logged.
  4. Complete annual blade inspection (rope access or drone) and log every defect with photo evidence.
  5. Review vibration data monthly against ISO 10816 zones; escalate Zone C or D immediately.
  6. Reconcile Class A and B spare parts against minimum stock levels and lead times.
  7. Audit work orders for LOTO, permit-to-work and rescue plan before any nacelle or blade task.
  8. Produce the monthly availability, MTBF and cost-per-MWh report for management and lenders.
