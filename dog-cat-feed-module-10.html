Module 10

🐕🐈 Feeding for Competition and Performance Dogs

Calculating and building rations for working, hunting, herding and sport dogs, where energy demand can be four to eight times resting and every extra kilogram of feed has to be carried, paid for and digested.

What you will be able to do after this module

  • Calculate a working dog's daily energy requirement using RER and the correct work multiplier
  • Express protein requirements on an energy basis and explain why that matters for high-intake dogs
  • Calculate how added fat changes the energy density of a ration using the modified Atwater route
  • Explain how dogs lose heat and why water access, not electrolyte products, is the main intervention
  • Set a defensible omega-3 level for a hard-working dog and check it against the safe upper limit
  • Match an energy multiplier and ration density to the demands of a specific canine job

The six lessons

1. Energy and Calorie Requirements for Active Dogs 2. Protein for Muscle Maintenance and Recovery 3. Fat as Endurance Fuel 4. Hydration and Electrolyte Management 5. Recovery Nutrition and Anti-Inflammatory Nutrition 6. Sport-Specific Nutrition
Lesson 10.1~12 min

Energy and Calorie Requirements for Active Dogs

In this lesson
  • Calculate a working dog's daily energy requirement using RER and the correct work multiplier
  • Choose between the RER-multiplier system and the FEDIAF allometric equations without mixing them
  • Convert an energy requirement into grams per day and into a yearly feed cost

Everything in performance feeding begins with an honest energy number. Get it wrong upward and the dog gets fat and slow; get it wrong downward and the dog burns muscle, loses condition and stops working. Both errors cost the handler money.

The base is resting energy requirement: RER in kcal per day = 70 x body weight in kilograms to the power 0.75. This form is valid at any weight. Between 2 and 45 kg you may use the linear approximation (30 x kg) + 70.

Maintenance energy requirement is RER multiplied by a life-stage or activity factor. The published canine factors are: adult intact 1.8, adult neutered 1.6, inactive or obesity-prone 1.2 to 1.4, light work 2.0, moderate work 3.0, and heavy work 4.0 to 8.0. Note how wide the heavy work band is. It runs from four times resting to eight times resting, which for a 30 kg dog is a difference of over 3500 kcal a day. That band is not sloppiness; it reflects that a sled dog in deep snow and a security dog on a long patrol are both heavy work and are not remotely the same job. You must narrow it with observation, not with confidence.

Worked example. A 30 kg herding dog, intact male, working most of the day on a livestock farm.

  1. RER = 70 x 30^0.75 = 897 kcal per day.
  2. If he were a pet, adult intact at 1.8 gives 1615 kcal per day.
  3. At light work, 2.0 x 897 = 1794 kcal per day.
  4. At moderate work, 3.0 x 897 = 2691 kcal per day.
  5. At the bottom of heavy work, 4.0 x 897 = 3588 kcal per day.

Now convert to food. On a mid-range dry food at 3800 kcal per kilogram, moderate work at 2691 kcal means 2691 divided by 3800 = 0.708 kg, that is 708 g of food per day. On a low-energy budget food at 3000 kcal per kilogram, the same dog needs 2691 divided by 3000 = 897 g per day. The dog must eat almost 200 g more food every day to get the same energy, and there is a limit to what a dog will eat and comfortably digest at one sitting. This is the central argument for energy density in a working ration: not a marketing claim, an intake-capacity constraint.

Cost. At USD 1.40 per kilogram for the dense food, 0.708 kg per day is USD 0.99 a day, USD 362 a year. At USD 1.10 per kilogram for the cheaper, less dense food, 0.897 kg per day is USD 0.99 a day, USD 360 a year. The cheaper bag is not cheaper per working dog. Always compare cost per 1000 kcal, not cost per kilogram: 1.40 divided by 3.8 = USD 0.368 per 1000 kcal against 1.10 divided by 3.0 = USD 0.367. Essentially identical. That single calculation will change how you sell.

A warning about mixing systems. There is a second, equally legitimate approach: the FEDIAF allometric equations, which give dog maintenance as 95 or 110 kcal x kg^0.75 depending on activity and neuter assumptions. For an adult dog, 110 kcal per kg^0.75 works out at about RER x 1.57 and 95 at about RER x 1.36. These will not agree with the RER-multiplier table, and neither is wrong. Pick one system, state which one you used on the feeding guide, and stay with it. A feeding table that silently blends the two is how a producer ends up with two different recommendations for the same dog.

African context. A security company in Nairobi feeds twelve adult German Shepherd-type dogs averaging 32 kg, working night patrols. RER for 32 kg is 70 x 32^0.75 = 940 kcal. The handlers describe long patrol hours at low intensity, which fits light to moderate work, so take 2.5 x 940 = 2350 kcal per day. At 3800 kcal per kilogram that is 618 g per dog per day, so 12 dogs eat 7.4 kg a day, 2705 kg a year. At USD 1.40 per kilogram the annual feed bill is USD 3787. If a local producer supplies at USD 1.20 with the same energy density, the company saves USD 541 a year, which is a real, checkable pitch. Notice that this claim survives scrutiny only because the energy density is stated. If your bag has no kcal per kilogram figure, you cannot make the argument at all.

Finally, energy needs are not constant across the year. A hunting dog worked hard for a three-month season and rested for nine must have its ration moved down after the season, or it becomes an obese dog by the following one.

Lesson 10.2~12 min

Protein for Muscle Maintenance and Recovery

In this lesson
  • Express protein requirements on an energy basis and explain why that matters for high-intake dogs
  • Check a working ration against the binding amino acid minima rather than crude protein alone
  • Calculate the actual grams of protein a working dog receives per day at a given intake

Working dogs eat more food, so they automatically eat more protein. That simple fact is the reason many performance protein claims are unnecessary, and it is also the reason the ones that matter are easy to miss. The way to see clearly is to stop thinking in percentages of the bag and start thinking in grams per day.

The verified minima. On a dry-matter basis, an adult dog at maintenance needs at least 18.0 percent crude protein and a dog at growth or reproduction at least 22.5 percent. The same minima expressed on an energy basis are 45 g of protein per 1000 kcal ME for adult maintenance and 56.3 g per 1000 kcal for growth and reproduction. The energy basis is the honest one for a working dog, because it accounts for the fact that the dog is eating far more calories than a pet of the same weight.

Worked example. Our 30 kg herding dog at moderate work eats 2691 kcal per day. On a food that just meets the adult minimum at 45 g protein per 1000 kcal, he receives 2.691 x 45 = 121 g of protein per day. A 30 kg pet dog neutered at maintenance eats 1436 kcal and receives 1.436 x 45 = 65 g. So the working dog on a plain adult food is already receiving 86 percent more protein per day than the pet, without any special performance formula. That is the argument you should be able to make against a supplier trying to sell an expensive high-protein product on percentages alone.

Where the real risk sits is not total protein but two things: amino acid quality and the effect of high fat. The binding amino acid minima for adult dogs are lysine 0.63 percent and methionine plus cystine 0.65 percent of dry matter, with threonine 0.48 and tryptophan 0.16. Rendered meals vary enormously in how much of their lysine survives processing, and crude protein measured by the standard nitrogen method cannot tell you the difference between intact lysine and heat-damaged lysine. That is why buying protein meals on crude protein alone is risky, and why the trial evidence matters: bone protein meal was digestible even at 24 percent inclusion, giving 82.4 percent apparent crude protein digestibility against 78.0 percent for the control, but faecal quality set a practical ceiling of 12 percent, with feather meal at 5 percent. Feather meal is the classic case of a very high crude protein number attached to a poor amino acid profile.

The second point about fat. Performance rations are usually high in fat because fat is the dense energy source. As fat rises, protein per 1000 kcal falls unless you deliberately raise protein inclusion, because fat contributes 9.4 kcal per gram against protein at 5.7 in the gross energy calculation. So the dog eats fewer grams of a denser food, and protein intake can fall even though the percentage on the bag looks unchanged. Always recheck grams of protein per day after you raise fat.

Worked check. Suppose you raise a food from 3800 to 4300 kcal per kilogram by adding fat, leaving crude protein at 24 percent. Our dog now eats 2691 divided by 4300 = 0.626 kg per day instead of 0.708 kg. Protein intake falls from 0.708 x 0.24 = 170 g to 0.626 x 0.24 = 150 g, a loss of 20 g a day, purely from the density change. To hold 170 g you need 170 divided by 626 = 27.2 percent crude protein in the denser food. That is the whole reasoning behind why performance formulas carry higher protein percentages, and it is far more defensible than a slogan about muscle.

On timing, be careful what you claim. Our verified reference set contains no data on protein timing windows after exercise in dogs. The widely repeated advice to feed protein within thirty minutes of work is not supported here, so do not print it as fact. What is sound and uncontroversial: do not feed a large meal immediately before hard work, give free access to water, and let the dog settle before and after feeding, particularly in deep-chested breeds where the risk of gastric dilatation is a well-known handler concern.

African context. A cooperative of six herding households in Nyeri County, Kenya was buying a 20 percent protein maize-heavy dog food at USD 0.95 per kilogram. Moving to a 26 percent protein food at USD 1.30 raised cost per dog from 0.708 x 0.95 = USD 0.67 to USD 0.92 a day, that is USD 91 more per dog per year. Whether that is worth it depends entirely on whether the first food was actually below the amino acid minima, which needs a laboratory analysis, not an argument. Pay for one analysis first.

Lesson 10.3~12 min

Fat as Endurance Fuel

In this lesson
  • Calculate how added fat changes the energy density of a ration using the modified Atwater route
  • Set a fat inclusion that meets energy needs without breaching omega-3 and vitamin D limits
  • Plan a staged fat transition and price the change per kilogram

Fat is the tool that makes a working ration possible, because it is the densest energy source you can add. In the predictive energy calculation, fat contributes 9.4 kcal per gram of gross energy against 5.7 for protein and 4.1 for carbohydrate plus fibre. That ratio is why a 5 percentage point increase in fat moves an entire ration.

The minima first. Adult dogs require at least 5.5 percent crude fat on a dry-matter basis and dogs at growth or reproduction 8.5 percent. Cats require 9.0 percent at both life stages, and note that unlike dogs the cat minimum does not fall for adult maintenance. Working rations sit far above these minima, but the minima are not what limits you; the ceilings and the practical consequences are.

How to calculate what fat does to your ration. The modified Atwater route is:

  1. Gross energy in kcal per 100 g = (5.7 x protein g) + (9.4 x fat g) + (4.1 x [nitrogen-free extract + crude fibre] g).
  2. Energy digestibility for dogs, as a percentage = 91.2 minus [(1.43 x crude fibre percent x 100) divided by (100 minus percent moisture)].
  3. Digestible energy = gross energy x digestibility.
  4. Metabolisable energy for dogs = digestible energy minus (1.04 x protein g), in kcal per 100 g.

Worked example. Take a ration per 100 g as fed containing 26 g protein, 12 g fat, 45 g nitrogen-free extract, 3 g crude fibre and 9 percent moisture. Gross energy = (5.7 x 26) + (9.4 x 12) + (4.1 x 48) = 148.2 + 112.8 + 196.8 = 457.8 kcal per 100 g. Digestibility = 91.2 minus [(1.43 x 3 x 100) divided by 91] = 91.2 minus 4.71 = 86.5 percent. Digestible energy = 457.8 x 0.865 = 396.0. Metabolisable energy = 396.0 minus (1.04 x 26) = 396.0 minus 27.0 = 369.0 kcal per 100 g, that is 3690 kcal per kilogram. Now raise fat from 12 to 18 g and drop nitrogen-free extract from 45 to 39 g. Gross energy = 148.2 + 169.2 + (4.1 x 42) = 148.2 + 169.2 + 172.2 = 489.6. Digestibility is unchanged at 86.5 percent, so digestible energy = 423.5 and metabolisable energy = 423.5 minus 27.0 = 396.5 kcal per 100 g, that is 3965 kcal per kilogram. Six percentage points of fat bought 275 kcal per kilogram, about 7.5 percent more energy. Typical dry foods run 2800 to 4800 kcal per kilogram, so both of these sit within the normal band.

What that buys the handler. Our 30 kg dog at 2691 kcal per day eats 2691 divided by 3690 = 729 g of the first food and 2691 divided by 3965 = 679 g of the second. Fifty grams less food carried and digested every day, 18 kg less over a year.

The limits you must respect. First, omega-3 stacking. The safe upper limit for combined EPA and DHA in dogs is 280 mg per 100 kcal, equivalently 370 mg per kg BW^0.75. For our dog at 2691 kcal that ceiling is 26.91 x 280 = 7535 mg a day. That sounds generous, but a high-fish-oil performance food plus a joint supplement can approach it. Second, vitamin D. High-fat fish carry vitamin D, and the AAFCO dog ceiling is 3000 IU per kilogram dry matter, with the EU legal limit lower at 2270 IU per kilogram dry matter. The 2016 reduction specifically lowered how much high-fat fish could be used in recipes. Third, oxidation. High polyunsaturated fat needs more vitamin E; the precise ratio is not stated in our verified sources, so agree it with your premix supplier. The dog vitamin E minimum is 50 IU per kilogram, which is a floor, not a target for a high-fat diet.

Transition. Move to a high-fat ration over two to four weeks rather than overnight, increasing the new food by about a quarter of the ration every four to five days. A sudden fat increase produces loose stool and blames your product for a handling error.

African context and cost. A producer in Ibadan, Nigeria raising fat from 12 to 18 percent using locally refined palm-based fat at USD 1.25 per kilogram adds 6 kg of fat per 100 kg batch, that is USD 7.50, while removing 6 kg of maize at USD 0.42, saving USD 2.52. Net cost increase is USD 4.98 per 100 kg, or USD 0.05 per kilogram, about 3.6 percent on a USD 1.40 product. The handler saves 50 g of food a day. At USD 1.45 per kilogram the new daily cost is 0.679 x 1.45 = USD 0.98 against 0.729 x 1.40 = USD 1.02. The denser food is cheaper to feed, and you can prove it on paper.

Lesson 10.4~12 min

Hydration and Electrolyte Management

In this lesson
  • Explain how dogs lose heat and why water access, not electrolyte products, is the main intervention
  • Identify which electrolyte figures your verified sources do and do not support
  • Estimate the water intake associated with a working dog's food intake and plan supply for a working day

This is the lesson where the honest answer is smaller than the market would like. Hydration matters enormously to a working dog. Electrolyte supplementation, as sold, is largely a human sports analogy that does not transfer cleanly, and our verified reference set does not give sodium, potassium or chloride requirements for dogs at all. Sodium, potassium and chloride were among the nutrients not captured from the AAFCO dog table in this pass. So this lesson gives you what is defensible and marks clearly what is not.

Start with the physiology that is not in dispute. Dogs do not cool themselves primarily by sweating across the skin the way humans do; they lose heat mainly by panting, with some evaporative loss from the paw pads. Panting loses water as vapour, and it loses very little salt. That is the core reason human sports drink logic does not carry across: the dominant loss in a working dog is water, not electrolyte. A working dog in a hot African climate that has free access to clean, cool water and shade is far better protected than one given a flavoured electrolyte product and limited water.

The practical rules that follow, and that you can state without overreach:

  1. Water available at all times, including at the work site, not only at the kennel.
  2. Offer water before, during breaks in, and after work, in small amounts frequently rather than one large drink.
  3. Work in the cooler parts of the day where the job allows.
  4. Watch for the dog that stops working, seeks shade, pants without settling, or becomes unsteady, and stop immediately. Heat stroke is a veterinary emergency and no feeding change treats it.

What you must not do is invent numbers. You will see confident claims that working dog diets should carry a particular sodium percentage. Our verified sources do not contain a dog sodium requirement or maximum, so you cannot support such a figure. If you want to formulate to a specific sodium level, obtain the current AAFCO Official Publication, which carries the full profile including the nutrients not captured here, and formulate against the published value. This is not an evasion; it is exactly the discipline that keeps a small producer out of trouble.

What you can calculate is water demand associated with food. A dry food carries about 8 to 10 percent moisture, so essentially all the animal's water must be drunk. A wet food at typical energy densities of 700 to 1300 kcal per kilogram is mostly water, and an animal on wet food drinks noticeably less. Compare our 30 kg working dog at 2691 kcal per day. On a dry food at 3800 kcal per kilogram he eats 708 g and takes in only about 64 g of water with it. On a wet food at 1000 kcal per kilogram he would eat 2691 g of food, most of it water. In hot conditions on dry food, all of that water has to come from the bucket, and it has to be there.

A practical planning figure for supply, stated as an estimate rather than a standard: plan at least one litre of clean water per 10 kg of body weight per working day in hot conditions, and carry more than you think you need. For twelve 32 kg patrol dogs that is at least 38 litres a day. Mark it clearly as a planning estimate for logistics, not a physiological requirement, because our sources do not publish one.

One genuine formulation link. Feeding a large meal and then working a deep-chested dog hard is a recognised handler concern, and water gulping around a large meal is part of the same caution. Split the ration into two or three meals around the work, rather than one large meal before it.

African context and cost. A hunting group in northern Ghana works six dogs in dry-season heat. They previously carried a single 10 litre jerrycan for the pack. Working the estimate above for six dogs averaging 22 kg gives at least 13 litres a day, so they were short before the day started. Two extra 10 litre jerrycans cost about USD 6 each, USD 12 in total, and a collapsible bowl about USD 4. Sixteen dollars, once, against the cost of a heat-stroke case or a dead dog worth USD 200 or more. That is the whole business case, and it needs no product.

Lesson 10.5~12 min

Recovery Nutrition and Anti-Inflammatory Nutrition

In this lesson
  • Set a defensible omega-3 level for a hard-working dog and check it against the safe upper limit
  • Distinguish antioxidant claims you can support from those you cannot
  • Design an eight-week monitoring record that shows whether a recovery ration is working

Recovery is where the performance market makes its boldest claims and where a careful producer earns long-term trust by making fewer of them. Work through what is supportable, then build the monitoring that lets the handler judge for themselves.

Start with the omega-3 arithmetic, because it is sourced. The safe upper limit for combined EPA and DHA in dogs is 280 mg per 100 kcal, equivalently 370 mg per kg BW^0.75, roughly 175 mg per kg body weight per day. The therapeutic doses used in dogs are, from the same source, osteoarthritis 310 mg per kg^0.75, kidney disease 140, and cardiovascular 115. There is no published performance-recovery dose in our verified sources, so the defensible position for a working ration is to sit clearly below the osteoarthritis dose and well below the ceiling.

Worked example. A 30 kg working dog: 30^0.75 = 12.82. The safe upper limit is 12.82 x 370 = 4743 mg EPA plus DHA per day. The osteoarthritis dose would be 12.82 x 310 = 3974 mg. A conservative working-dog level at, say, 115 to 140 mg per kg^0.75, the same band as the cardiovascular and kidney doses, gives 1474 to 1795 mg per day, using 31 to 38 percent of the ceiling and leaving generous room for whatever else the handler adds. Express it on the specification per 100 kcal as well: at 2691 kcal per day, 1795 mg is 1795 divided by 26.91 = 67 mg per 100 kcal, comfortably under the 280 mg per 100 kcal limit.

Be clear about why the room matters. Excess long-chain omega-3 is associated with supplement-drug interactions, altered platelet function, gastrointestinal disturbance and detrimental effects on wound healing, and omega-3 combined with NSAIDs has been linked to gastric ulceration and blood loss in affected dogs. A working dog with an injury is exactly the animal likely to be on an NSAID, and impaired wound healing is exactly the wrong side effect for a dog with a cut pad.

Antioxidants next. Vitamin E has a stated AAFCO minimum of 50 IU per kilogram dry matter for dogs and 40 for cats, and raising vitamin E alongside polyunsaturated fat is standard practice, though our verified sources do not give the ratio, so agree it with your premix supplier. On vitamin C, note that dogs and cats synthesise it and it does not appear as a required nutrient in the AAFCO profiles. It may be included, but a claim that it reduces exercise-induced oxidative stress in dogs is not supported in our reference set and should not be printed.

What about vitamin A and D as recovery ingredients? Leave them alone. These are the nutrients with real ceilings: dog vitamin A maximum 250,000 IU per kilogram and vitamin D maximum 3000 IU per kilogram dry matter, with the EU limit at 2270. Real-world toxicity cases come from premix and formulation error rather than raw ingredients, and vitamin D toxicity has produced repeated commercial recalls. Recovery is not a reason to push fat-soluble vitamins.

Now the part that actually distinguishes a serious producer: monitoring. Give every performance customer a simple eight-week record sheet with five columns and a weekly row.

  1. Body weight on the same scale, same day of the week.
  2. Body condition score out of 9, assessed by hand over the ribs.
  3. Stool score out of 4 on your standard card.
  4. Work performed that week, in hours and intensity.
  5. Anything unusual: lameness, refusal to eat, loose stool, injuries.

Steady weight with steady work is the target. Falling weight at constant work means the ration is under-supplying energy, and the fix is calories, not a supplement. Rising weight at constant work means the opposite. This record is also your product evidence: eight weeks of handler-recorded data across ten dogs is worth more to a veterinary clinic than any claim on a bag.

African context and cost. A sport and security dog trainer near Kampala runs fourteen dogs and agreed to keep these sheets for a local producer in exchange for a 10 percent discount. On a feed bill of about USD 0.95 per dog per day, fourteen dogs cost USD 13.30 a day, so the discount costs the producer USD 1.33 a day, USD 485 a year. In return the producer received fourteen dogs' worth of eight-week records, which became the basis of a supply agreement with two veterinary clinics. That is a cheaper and more honest route to credibility than any advertising budget of the same size.

Lesson 10.6~12 min

Sport-Specific Nutrition

In this lesson
  • Match an energy multiplier and ration density to the demands of a specific canine job
  • Adjust a ration across a working season and an off season without losing condition
  • Build a small product range that serves several sports without multiplying your stock-keeping units

Different canine jobs place genuinely different demands on a ration, but a small producer cannot make a different food for every sport. The skill is to read the job, place it correctly on the energy multiplier table, and cover several jobs with a small number of well-specified products.

Read the job on three axes. First, total daily energy output, which sets the multiplier: light work 2.0 x RER, moderate work 3.0, heavy work 4.0 to 8.0. Second, whether the work is sustained or in short bursts, which affects meal timing and how much bulk the dog can comfortably carry. Third, whether the dog must stay light, which constrains how much food you can put into it regardless of demand.

Apply that to the jobs you will actually meet in African markets.

  1. Livestock herding dog. Long hours, moderate intensity, most days. Place at moderate work, 3.0 x RER. For a 25 kg dog, RER = 70 x 25^0.75 = 783 kcal, so 2348 kcal a day. Needs a dense ration and fed twice daily.
  2. Security and patrol dog. Long shifts, mostly low intensity with unpredictable bursts. Light to moderate, 2.0 to 2.5 x RER. For a 32 kg dog, RER = 940, so 1880 to 2350 kcal a day. Predictable and easy to supply, which makes it good contract business.
  3. Hunting dog. Intense but seasonal. Moderate to heavy in season, 3.0 to 4.0 x RER; back to adult maintenance at 1.6 to 1.8 out of season. For a 22 kg dog, RER = 70 x 22^0.75 = 711 kcal, so 2133 to 2844 kcal in season and 1138 to 1280 out of season. The out-of-season drop is more than half.
  4. Agility and sport dog. Short, explosive, and the dog must stay lean. Often light work at 2.0 x RER despite looking athletic, because total energy output is small. Overfeeding is the commonest error here.
  5. Cart, sled or draught work where it exists. Heavy work, 4.0 to 8.0 x RER, and the only category where extreme energy density genuinely earns its cost.

Notice that the security dog and the agility dog can sit at the same multiplier while looking completely different, and that the hunting dog needs two rations a year rather than one. That is the practical insight to sell.

Season management, worked. Our 22 kg hunting dog eats 2844 kcal a day in season on a 3900 kcal per kilogram ration, that is 729 g a day. Out of season at 1280 kcal on the same ration he needs 328 g a day. If the handler keeps feeding the in-season amount for the nine-month off season, the dog receives an excess of about 1564 kcal every day. Even a rough conversion makes the direction obvious: this is a dog that will be substantially overweight by the next season, and obesity is exactly what shortens a working career. The producer's job is to put the two feeding rates on the same bag, clearly labelled in season and out of season, with the switch date written down.

Product range design. Three products can cover this whole market:

  1. An active maintenance food at roughly 3700 to 3800 kcal per kilogram, covering security dogs, agility dogs and off-season hunting dogs.
  2. A high-density working food at roughly 4000 to 4300 kcal per kilogram, covering herding dogs, in-season hunting dogs and draught dogs.
  3. A weight-management food for retired and off-season dogs, built on the weight-loss arithmetic in Module 9.

That is three stock-keeping units, three specifications and one premix relationship, rather than seven half-considered products.

African context and the numbers that close a sale. A livestock cooperative in Botswana runs 40 herding dogs averaging 25 kg at 2348 kcal a day. On a 3400 kcal per kilogram food they eat 690 g each, so 27.6 kg a day for the pack, 10,074 kg a year, at USD 1.15 per kilogram that is USD 11,585 a year. On a 4100 kcal per kilogram food they eat 573 g each, 22.9 kg a day, 8,359 kg a year, at USD 1.35 per kilogram that is USD 11,285 a year. Almost identical money, but 1,715 kg less feed to transport to a remote area every year, which for a cooperative paying for transport on rough roads is the actual saving. Work out the transport line separately and put it in the quotation, because that is the number that wins the contract.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. What is the daily energy requirement of a 30 kg dog at moderate work?

RER = 70 x 30^0.75 = 897 kcal, and the moderate work factor is 3.0, giving 2691 kcal. 3588 kcal is the bottom of the heavy work band at x4.

2. Why should working-dog rations be energy dense?

At 3000 kcal/kg the same 30 kg dog needs 897 g a day instead of 708 g. Intake capacity, not price, is the binding constraint.

3. A food at 3800 kcal/kg costs USD 1.40/kg. What is the cost per 1000 kcal?

1.40 divided by 3.8 thousand kcal per kilogram gives USD 0.368 per 1000 kcal. Comparing per 1000 kcal rather than per kilogram is how you compare working rations honestly.

4. What is wrong with using both the RER-multiplier table and the FEDIAF allometric equations in one feeding guide?

FEDIAF adult dog at 110 kcal x kg^0.75 is about RER x 1.57 and at 95 about RER x 1.36. Both systems are legitimate; mixing them is not.

5. A hunting dog works hard for three months and rests for nine. What should happen to its ration?

Energy requirement follows the work. Leaving a heavy-work ration in place through nine months of rest is one of the commonest causes of obesity in working dogs.

6. A 30 kg working dog eats 2691 kcal/day on a food supplying 45 g protein per 1000 kcal. How much protein per day?

2.691 x 45 = 121 g per day. A pet of the same weight eating 1436 kcal receives only 65 g from the same food.

7. Why can crude protein on the label mislead you about a rendered meal?

Crude protein is a nitrogen measurement. Heat damage during rendering destroys available lysine while leaving the nitrogen, and therefore the crude protein figure, unchanged.

8. You raise a food from 3800 to 4300 kcal/kg with added fat and keep protein at 24 percent. What happens to the dog's daily protein intake?

The dog eats to its energy need, so intake falls from 708 g to 626 g and protein falls from 170 g to 150 g. You would need 27.2 percent protein in the denser food to hold intake.

9. What is the tested faecal-quality ceiling for feather meal in dog food?

Feather meal up to 5 percent and bone protein meal up to 12 percent kept faecal scores acceptable. Digestibility remained acceptable higher, but stool quality did not.

10. What should you say about feeding protein within 30 minutes after exercise?

The claim is widely repeated but not supported in the verified reference set. Sound advice that is supportable concerns meal size around work, water access and settling time.

11. In the modified Atwater calculation, how many kcal per gram does fat contribute to gross energy?

Fat contributes 9.4 kcal per gram against 5.7 for protein and 4.1 for nitrogen-free extract plus crude fibre.

12. After calculating digestible energy, how is metabolisable energy obtained for dogs?

ME for dogs is DE minus 1.04 times the protein in grams per 100 g. The 0.77 factor is the cat equation.

13. Raising fat from 12 to 18 g per 100 g while dropping NFE by 6 g raised ME from 3690 to about what?

Gross energy rose from 457.8 to 489.6 kcal/100 g, digestibility was unchanged at 86.5 percent, and ME came to 396.5 kcal/100 g, that is 3965 kcal/kg.

14. Why does high fish oil inclusion have a second, non-omega-3 ceiling?

The 2016 safe upper limit reduction specifically lowered the amount of high-fat fish that could be used in recipes, because those fish carry vitamin D.

15. What is the correct way to move a dog onto a high-fat ration?

A sudden fat increase causes loose stools that get blamed on the product. A staged transition over two to four weeks avoids the digestive upset.

16. Why does human sports-drink logic transfer poorly to working dogs?

The dominant loss in a panting dog is water, not electrolyte, so free access to clean cool water is the main intervention rather than salt replacement.

17. What should you do if asked for the correct sodium percentage in a working dog diet?

Sodium, potassium and chloride were not captured from the dog table in the source used here. Inventing or borrowing a figure is exactly the error that gets a small producer into trouble.

18. A 30 kg dog eats 708 g of dry food at 9 percent moisture. Roughly how much water comes with the food?

708 g x 0.09 is about 64 g. Essentially all the rest of the dog's water intake has to be drunk, which is why water at the work site matters.

19. A working dog stops, seeks shade, pants without settling and becomes unsteady. What is the correct action?

These are signs of heat stress, which is a veterinary emergency. No feeding or supplement change treats it, and continuing work can be fatal.

20. Why split a working dog's ration into two or three meals around work?

Large meals immediately before hard work, and gulping water around them, are long-standing handler cautions, particularly in deep-chested breeds.

21. What is the EPA+DHA safe upper limit for a 30 kg dog?

30^0.75 = 12.82 and 12.82 x 370 = 4743 mg per day. 3974 mg would be the osteoarthritis dose for the same dog.

22. Why is impaired wound healing a particularly awkward side effect in working dogs?

Excess omega-3 is associated with detrimental effects on wound healing and, with NSAIDs, with gastric ulceration and blood loss. Working dogs are the group most likely to be in both categories.

23. What is the correct position on vitamin C for exercise-induced oxidative stress in dogs?

Vitamin C is not a required nutrient in the AAFCO dog profile and the verified reference set contains no data supporting an exercise-recovery claim in dogs.

24. A working dog loses weight over four weeks at constant workload. What is the first correction?

Falling weight at constant work is an energy shortfall. The fix is calories, delivered either as more food or a denser ration, not a supplement.

25. Why is a handler-kept eight-week record valuable to a small producer?

Recorded outcomes across a group of dogs are far more persuasive to professional buyers than label claims, and they cost only the discount you trade for them.

26. An agility dog looks highly athletic but competes in short bursts. Where does it usually sit?

Total daily energy output is small despite the intensity of each run, so overfeeding is the commonest error in this group.

27. A 22 kg hunting dog needs 2844 kcal in season and 1280 out of season. What is the daily excess if the in-season ration continues?

2844 minus 1280 = 1564 kcal a day, sustained across a nine-month off season, which is how working dogs become obese before the next season.

28. A 25 kg dog at moderate work needs how many kcal per day?

RER = 70 x 25^0.75 = 783 kcal, and the moderate work factor of 3.0 gives 2348 kcal per day.

29. Why does a denser ration matter to a remote cooperative even when cost per year is unchanged?

In the worked example the annual feed cost was nearly identical but 1715 kg less feed had to be moved. Transport is often the deciding line in a remote supply quotation.

30. What is the advantage of covering the performance market with three products rather than seven?

A small producer's quality control and cash both suffer from too many stock-keeping units. Matching several jobs to the same correctly specified ration is the practical answer.

Module 10 capstone

Produce a costed performance feeding plan for one real working dog in your area. Step 1: find a working dog you can access, a herding dog, a security dog, a hunting dog or a sport dog, and record its species, breed type, age, sex, neuter status and body weight measured on a scale. Step 2: interview the handler and write down what the dog actually does on a working day and a rest day, in hours and intensity. Step 3: calculate RER as 70 x kg^0.75, then produce three daily energy figures using the light work factor of 2.0, moderate work 3.0 and the bottom of the heavy work band 4.0, and state which one you believe fits and why. Step 4: get the guaranteed analysis and, where stated, the energy density of the food the dog eats now, and calculate grams per day for your chosen factor. Step 5: collect real local prices and calculate the cost per working day and per year at that intake. Step 6: build one alternative ration on paper with a higher energy density, calculate its cost per day, and show the difference in kilograms of food carried and dollars spent per year. Step 7: write half a page on what you would monitor over eight weeks, including body weight, body condition and stool score, and what would make you change the plan.
