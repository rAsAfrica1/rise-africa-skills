Lessons

1. Understanding Steel: Carbon Content and Alloys

Steel is iron mixed with a small amount of carbon. The carbon content decides how hard the steel can become when heat treated. Low-carbon steel (mild steel) has less than 0.3% carbon. It is easy to forge and weld but will not harden much when quenched. You find it in rebar, car parts, and cheap angle iron. Medium-carbon steel (0.30.6% carbon) is a good all-rounder. It hardens enough for many tools but stays tough. Old leaf springs from trucks and cars are often 5160 steel, a medium-carbon alloy with chromium. High-carbon steel (0.61.5% carbon) is what you want for knives, chisels, punches, and axes. It can get very hard but also brittle if not tempered. Files, old saw blades, and coil springs are good sources. Alloy steels add other elements: chromium for wear resistance, vanadium for toughness, molybdenum for heat resistance. In Africa, you often work with recycled steel. Learn to identify it: test with a grinder high-carbon steel throws bright yellow sparks that burst into many small forks; mild steel gives few dull orange sparks. A file test: a new file will skate over hardened high-carbon steel but bite into mild steel. When you buy steel from a scrap yard, ask for old agricultural equipment, truck axles, or plow discs these are often medium to high carbon. Keep a few known samples in your shop for comparison. Never assume; always test. The carbon content also affects forging temperature: high-carbon steel burns at a lower temperature than mild steel, so watch your fire. With practice, you will learn to judge by feel and spark.

2. Critical Temperatures and Color Indicators

In blacksmithing, you judge temperature by the color of the steel in dim light. The critical temperature is where steel changes its internal structure and becomes ready for hardening. For most high-carbon steels, this is between 760C and 870C. In color terms, that is a bright cherry red to orange-red. A common mistake is to quench too cold. If the steel is only dull red (around 600C), it will not harden. If it is bright yellow or white (over 1000C), you are burning the carbon and the steel will be weak. Learn to work in a shaded area or at dusk. The colors: faint red (550C) too cold; dark cherry (650C) still too cold; cherry red (750C) starting point for hardening some steels; bright cherry (800C) good for most; orange (900C) getting hot; yellow (1000C) too hot for hardening, but okay for forging some alloys; white (1200C) burning, sparks fly. A magnet is a useful tool: steel becomes non-magnetic at its critical temperature (around 770C for simple carbon steels). Heat a piece and touch it with a magnet. When it stops sticking, you are at the right temperature for quenching. This is a reliable trick in any light. For African smiths using charcoal or coal forges, the fire may be uneven. Rotate the steel to heat evenly. Use a pipe or a baffle to reduce oxygen if you are burning the steel. Always normalize after forging: heat to critical and let it cool in air. This relieves stress and refines the grain. Then you can harden. Practice with a piece of known steel until you can judge the color without a magnet. Your eyes will become your best thermometer.

3. Quenching: Oil, Water, and Air

Quenching is the rapid cooling of hot steel to make it hard. The speed of cooling matters: faster cooling makes harder steel but also more brittle. Water is the fastest, then oil, then air. For most simple high-carbon steels (like 1080 or 5160), oil is the best choice. Warm oil (around 5060C) cools more evenly than cold oil. You can use used motor oil, canola oil, or transmission fluid. Never use water on high-carbon steel unless you know it is a water-hardening grade (like W1). Water can crack the steel. For medium-carbon steels, oil is also good. Air-hardening steels (like A2) are rare in basic smithing; they harden by cooling in still air. But for most African workshops, oil is your friend. Set up a quench tank: a metal pipe or bucket filled with at least 4 liters of oil. The oil should be deep enough to fully submerge the tool. Heat the steel to critical (non-magnetic), then quickly plunge it into the oil. Move it around in a figure-eight motion to break the vapor barrier. Hold it under for 1015 seconds, then pull it out. It should be cool enough to touch. If you see flames, do not panic just cover the tank with a lid to smother the fire. Always quench point-first or edge-first for knives. For a chisel, quench the cutting end only, leaving the shank soft. This gives you a hard edge and a tough body. After quenching, test with a file. If the file skates, it is hard. If it bites, you need to reheat and quench again. Remember: safety first. Wear leather gloves, a face shield, and long sleeves. Oil can splatter and ignite. Keep a fire extinguisher or sand bucket nearby. Never quench in a plastic container it will melt. With practice, you will learn the right speed for each steel.

4. Tempering for Toughness and Hardness

After quenching, steel is very hard but also brittle it can crack or shatter like glass. Tempering removes some of that brittleness while keeping most of the hardness. You do this by heating the steel to a lower temperature (150300C) and holding it there for a while, then letting it cool slowly. The temperature you choose depends on the tool. For a knife, you want a temper around 200C this gives a good balance of hardness and toughness. For a chisel or punch, 220250C is better because it needs to withstand hammer blows. For a spring, 300C makes it tough and flexible. How do you judge temperature without a thermometer? Use the oxide colors that form on clean steel as it heats. First, the steel turns pale yellow (200C), then straw (220C), then brown (240C), then purple (280C), then blue (300C). These colors are your guide. Clean the steel with sandpaper or a grinder before tempering so you can see the colors clearly. Heat the steel slowly and evenly. You can use a kitchen oven, a toaster oven, or a small forge with a gentle fire. For a knife, you can also use a hot plate or even a bed of hot sand. Hold the steel at the desired color for about one hour (or two cycles of 30 minutes). Then let it cool in air. If you over-temper (go past blue), the steel becomes too soft you will need to re-harden and re-temper. In Africa, many smiths use a charcoal fire and watch the colors carefully. It works. For a simple tool, you can temper just the cutting edge by heating the back of the tool with a torch until the edge turns the right color. This leaves the back soft and tough. Always temper after quenching. Never use a quenched tool without tempering it will break.

5. Testing Hardness with a File

The file test is the simplest way to check if your steel is hard. A new, sharp file is harder than most knife steels. When you draw the file across the hardened edge, it should skate meaning it does not bite or cut. If the file bites and removes metal, the steel is too soft. This test is not precise, but it is good enough for most blacksmithing. To do it properly: first, clean the steel with a grinder or sandpaper to remove any scale or decarburized layer. Then take a sharp file (a new one is best) and push it across the edge at a slight angle. Use light pressure. On a properly hardened and tempered knife, the file will slide with a scratching sound but not cut. On a soft piece, it will dig in and leave a shiny mark. You can also test the tip and the spine often the spine is softer because it was not quenched or was tempered more. For a chisel, test the cutting edge. If it skates, it is hard. If it bites, re-harden and quench again. Remember that a file will also skate on work-hardened steel or on a piece that is not fully hardened but has a hard surface. So always test after grinding away a little metal. In Africa, files are common and cheap. Keep one file just for testing mark it with tape so you do not use it for sharpening. You can also use a set of hardness testing files (like those from a machinist supply) that are calibrated to different Rockwell C scales, but they are expensive. For most smiths, a single new file is enough. Practice on known samples: a piece of mild steel will always be soft; a piece of hardened 1080 will skate. Once you know the feel, you can judge your own work. If the file skates but the edge chips, your steel is too hard temper it more. If the file bites, it is too soft re-harden. The file test is your daily quality control.

6. Heat Treating Simple Tools

Now you will heat treat three common tools: a cold chisel, a center punch, and a simple knife. For each, the process is similar but the details differ. Start with a cold chisel made from 5160 or 1080 steel. Forge the cutting edge to a 60-degree angle. Normalize: heat to cherry red and let it cool in air. Then harden: heat just the first 5 cm of the cutting end to non-magnetic (bright cherry red). Quench only that end in warm oil, moving it in a figure-eight. Hold for 10 seconds, then check with a file. It should skate. Now temper: polish the cutting end with sandpaper, then heat the shank with a torch or in the forge until the cutting edge turns straw yellow (220C). Remove and cool. The edge is now hard and tough. For a center punch, use high-carbon steel. Forge a point. Harden the point by heating the tip to non-magnetic and quenching in oil. Temper to purple (280C) this makes it tough enough to take hammer blows without breaking. For a simple knife, use an old file or leaf spring. Forge the blade, then normalize three times to refine the grain. Harden the whole blade: heat to non-magnetic, quench in warm oil, edge-first. Then temper in an oven at 200C for one hour. Check with a file it should skate. If the blade warps during quenching, you can straighten it while it is still hot (around 200C) using a vise or a hammer. In Africa, many smiths use a charcoal fire and a pipe to direct heat. For tempering, a charcoal fire with a bed of hot coals works just watch the colors. Always test your tools before use. A chisel that is too hard will chip; one that is too soft will mushroom. Adjust by re-tempering or re-hardening. With practice, you will heat treat tools that last for years.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Heat Treating and Metallurgy Basics

Heat treating is where a blacksmith turns ordinary steel into a tool that earns money. In an African context whether you are serving farmers in the Rift Valley, construction crews in Accra, or motorcycle repair shops in Kampala the difference between a hoe that lasts one season and one that lasts five is metallurgy, not muscle. This practical reference gives you the numbers, colours, quenchants, and tests you need to run a repeatable heat-treating process, reduce customer returns, and build a reputation that supports premium pricing.

Treat this module as your workshop standard operating procedure. The lessons on carbon content, critical temperatures, quenching, tempering, file testing, and simple tools all converge here. Use it as a bench reference; print it, laminate it, and hang it near the forge.

1. Understanding Steel: Carbon Content and Alloys

Steel is iron plus carbon, and the carbon percentage determines what you can harden. Low-carbon steel (under 0.3% C) cannot be hardened by quenching it will only case-harden or remain tough and soft. Medium-carbon steel (0.30.6% C) is the blacksmith's workhorse for hammers, chisels, and agricultural tools. High-carbon steel (0.61.0% C) takes a keen edge and is used for knives, axes, and cutting edges. Above 1.0% C, steel becomes brittle and difficult to forge without cracking.

Alloying elements change the rules. Manganese improves hardenability. Chromium adds wear resistance and corrosion resistance. Vanadium refines grain and improves toughness. Molybdenum allows slower quenching. In Africa, common recycled steel sources include vehicle leaf springs (5160 / 9260 equivalent, excellent for tools), coil springs, files (W1 or 1095 equivalent), and bearing races (52100). Always test unknown steel before committing to a heat-treat recipe.

2. Critical Temperatures and Colour Indicators

Critical temperature is the point at which steel's crystal structure changes to austenite the state required for hardening. For most medium- and high-carbon steels, this occurs between 760 C and 870 C (14001600 F). In a charcoal or coal forge without a pyrometer, you read this by colour. The transition from cherry red to bright orange is your working window. Above bright yellow, you risk burning the steel; below cherry red, the steel will not harden.

3. Quenching: Oil, Water, and Air

Quenching is the rapid cooling that locks in hardness. The quenchant matters as much as the steel. Water cools fastest and is used for simple high-carbon steels (W1, 1095) but can crack complex or alloyed parts. Oil cools more slowly and is the safest choice for leaf spring, coil spring, and bearing steel. Air cooling is used for air-hardening steels (A2, D2) not commonly found in African workshops but occasionally recovered from industrial scrap.

Quench oil should be warm (5065 C) for best results warm oil cools faster than cold oil and reduces distortion. Used motor oil works but produces toxic smoke; canola or sunflower oil is safer and readily available across Africa. Always quench vertically, edge-first for blades, and move the part in a figure-eight to break the vapour barrier.

SAFETY WARNING Quenching Hazards

Never quench into used motor oil indoors or without ventilation. Used oil contains heavy metals and produces carcinogenic fumes. Quench outdoors or under a fume hood. Keep a dry-powder fire extinguisher within 3 metres of the quench tank. Never use water on an oil fire. Wear leather gloves, a face shield, and a leather apron. Molten salt or brine splash can cause blindness. Ensure the quench tank is metal, not plastic, and tall enough to fully submerge the workpiece without overflow. In shared workshops, mark the quench area clearly and keep children and untrained apprentices away.

4. Tempering for Toughness and Hardness

As-quenched steel is too brittle for real work. Tempering reheats the steel to a lower temperature (150300 C) to relieve stress and trade some hardness for toughness. The tempering colour is your guide: pale straw (200 C) for knives and cutting tools, brown (240 C) for axes and chisels, purple (280 C) for springs and hammers, and blue (300 C) for tools needing maximum shock resistance.

In an African workshop without a tempering oven, use a charcoal fire, a gas torch, or a bed of hot sand. Clean the steel to bright metal first so colours are visible. Temper for at least one hour per 25 mm of thickness, then cool in still air. For critical tools, temper twice with a cooling cycle between.

5. Testing Hardness with a File

The file test is the blacksmith's field hardness test. A new, sharp file (Nicholson or similar) has a hardness around 6065 HRC. If the file skates across the steel without biting, the steel is harder than the file good for a cutting edge. If the file bites and removes metal, the steel is softer than the file either under-hardened or over-tempered.

Test at the edge, mid-blade, and spine. A properly hardened and tempered machete should skate at the edge, bite slightly at mid-blade, and bite at the spine. This gradient is intentional; it gives a hard edge with a tough back. Record your results per batch so you can trace customer complaints back to a specific heat-treat run.

6. Heat Treating Simple Tools

Apply the process to the products that pay your bills: hoes, machetes, axes, chisels, and hammers. For a hoe blade from leaf spring: forge to shape, normalise three times (heat to non-magnetic, cool in air), austenitise to bright cherry red (800 C), quench vertically in warm canola oil, then temper immediately at 240 C for one hour to a brown colour. For a machete from 5160: same normalising, quench in warm oil, temper at 200 C for pale straw. For a cold chisel from a file: quench in water, temper at 220 C, then test with a file before delivery.

Every tool you sell should carry a batch number and a heat-treat record. This is the foundation of a business that can scale beyond walk-in customers and supply hardware shops, agricultural cooperatives, and export buyers.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Identify the steel spark test or file test before choosing a heat-treat recipe.
  2. Normalise three times heat to non-magnetic, cool in still air, to refine grain.
  3. Austenitise correctly bright cherry red (760870 C) for medium/high carbon steel.
  4. Quench in the right medium warm oil for springs, water for simple high-carbon, never cold water for alloy steel.
  5. Temper immediately within minutes of quenching to avoid cracking.
  6. Read the temper colour straw for knives, brown for hoes and chisels, purple/blue for hammers.
  7. File test every batch skate at the edge, bite at the spine.
  8. Record and label batch number, steel source, quenchant, temper temperature, date.
