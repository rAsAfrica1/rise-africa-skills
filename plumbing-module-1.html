1. Introduction to Plumbing in the African Context

Welcome to Module 1 of your plumbing training. This first lesson sets the foundation for everything that follows. Plumbing is not just about pipes and fittings; it is a business, a trade, and a vital service that keeps communities healthy and economies moving. Across Africa, the demand for skilled plumbers is enormous and growing. Rapid urbanisation, population growth, and expanding construction projects mean that every new home, school, hospital, and office block needs reliable water supply and sanitation. Yet the supply of qualified plumbers has not kept pace. This gap is your opportunity. In this lesson we will explore what plumbing really involves, why it matters so much in Africa, the basic principles of water flow, the main materials you will work with, and how to think like a professional from day one. By the end, you should understand the scope of the trade and the mindset required to succeed. Let us begin with a simple definition. Plumbing is the system of pipes, tanks, fittings, and fixtures installed in a building for the supply of water and the removal of waterborne wastes. It includes everything from the main water line entering a property to the taps, toilets, showers, sinks, and the drains that carry waste away. A plumber installs, maintains, and repairs these systems. But in the African context, plumbing often goes beyond the building. It connects to boreholes, rainwater harvesting tanks, solar water heaters, septic tanks, and municipal or community water schemes. You may work in a city high-rise, a rural clinic, a school, or a private home. Each setting brings its own challenges and rewards. Why is plumbing so important in Africa? First, health. The World Health Organization estimates that poor water and sanitation contribute to a large burden of disease. Safe plumbing prevents contamination, reduces diarrhoeal diseases, and protects children especially. Second, economic development. When water flows reliably, businesses operate, farms irrigate, and households save time that would otherwise be spent fetching water. Third, property value. A well-plumbed building is worth more and lasts longer. Fourth, employment. Plumbing is a skill that can earn you a good income, whether you work for a company, start your own business, or take on contracts. The demand is not going away. Now let us look at the basic science. Water flows from high pressure to low pressure. In a building, pressure usually comes from a municipal main, a elevated tank, or a pump. Gravity also plays a role: water stored in a tank on a roof will flow down to taps below. The key principle is that water seeks its own level and will always find the easiest path. This means that leaks, blockages, and airlocks can disrupt the whole system. You must understand pressure, flow rate, and pipe sizing. Pressure is measured in bars or metres head. Flow rate is measured in litres per minute. Pipe size affects both: a narrow pipe restricts flow, while a wider pipe allows more water but costs more. You will learn to calculate these in later lessons. For now, remember that every plumbing decision affects performance. Materials matter too. In Africa, you will encounter many types of pipes. Common ones include PVC (polyvinyl chloride) for drainage and cold water, PPR (polypropylene random copolymer) for hot and cold water supply, copper for hot water and gas, galvanised steel for older installations, and HDPE (high-density polyethylene) for underground water mains. Each has strengths and weaknesses. PVC is cheap, easy to cut, and resistant to corrosion, but it cannot handle hot water. PPR is heat-resistant and joined by heat fusion, making strong leak-proof joints. Copper is durable and handles high temperatures but is expensive and can be stolen for scrap. Galvanised steel rusts over time and restricts flow. HDPE is flexible, tough, and ideal for buried lines. You must know which material to use for which application. Fittings are equally important: elbows, tees, couplings, reducers, valves, and adaptors. Always use the correct fitting for the pipe material and size. Mixing materials incorrectly can cause leaks or failures. Tools of the trade include pipe cutters, wrenches, pliers, screwdrivers, hammers, levels, measuring tapes, and specialty tools like PPR welding machines, pipe threaders, and drain snakes. You do not need everything at once, but you should build your toolkit steadily. Safety is non-negotiable. Plumbing can be dangerous: hot water, sharp tools, heavy pipes, and confined spaces. Always wear personal protective equipment: gloves, safety boots, eye protection, and dust masks when cutting. When working on water systems, shut off the supply and drain the lines. When working on drainage, be aware of harmful gases and use ventilation. Never take shortcuts with electrical connections near water. In Africa, many plumbers work informally. That is changing. Formal training, certification, and business skills set you apart. Clients want someone reliable, clean, and honest. They want a plumber who arrives on time, explains the problem clearly, gives a written quote, and cleans up after the job. These soft skills are as important as technical ability. Think about your reputation. In a community, word of mouth is powerful. One satisfied customer can bring ten more. One bad job can ruin you. So treat every job, big or small, with the same professionalism. This module will cover five lessons. Lesson 1 is this introduction. Lesson 2 will focus on water supply systems: sources, storage, pumps, and distribution. Lesson 3 will cover drainage and sanitation: waste pipes, traps, vents, septic tanks, and sewers. Lesson 4 will dive into fixtures and appliances: taps, toilets, showers, sinks, water heaters, and washing machines. Lesson 5 will bring it all together with maintenance, troubleshooting, and business practices. Each lesson builds on the previous one. By the end of Module 1, you will have a solid overview of the plumbing trade and be ready for more advanced modules. Before we finish, let us consider the African context more deeply. Africa is not one place. Plumbing practices differ from Lagos to Nairobi to Accra to Johannesburg to Cairo. In some cities, municipal water is intermittent, so plumbers install storage tanks and booster pumps. In rural areas, boreholes and hand pumps are common, and you may need to connect them to gravity-fed systems. In informal settlements, shared sanitation blocks and communal taps require different skills. Climate matters too: in dry regions, water conservation is critical, so you will install low-flow fixtures and rainwater harvesting. In flood-prone areas, drainage must handle heavy rains. You must adapt. Another key issue is counterfeit materials. Fake pipes, fittings, and valves flood many markets. They may look real but fail quickly, causing leaks, floods, and angry customers. Always buy from reputable suppliers. Check for brand marks, thickness, and certifications. If a price seems too good to be true, it probably is. Your reputation depends on the quality of what you install. Finally, think about your career path. You can start as an apprentice or helper, learn on the job, and take formal courses like this one. You can specialise: residential, commercial, industrial, or maintenance. You can become a supervisor, a contractor, or a business owner. You can also train others. Plumbing is a lifelong learning journey. New technologies like smart leak detectors, greywater recycling, and solar water heating are creating new opportunities. Embrace them. In summary, plumbing is a skilled trade with huge demand in Africa. It combines science, practical skills, and business sense. You must understand water flow, materials, tools, safety, and customer service. This module will give you the foundation. Stay curious, practice often, and treat every job as a chance to learn. Welcome to the trade. Let us get to work.

2. Water Supply Systems: Sources, Storage, Pumps, and Distribution

In this lesson we focus on water supply systems. Every plumbing system starts with a source of water. In Africa, sources vary widely. Municipal mains supply treated water to many urban areas, but supply can be unreliable. Boreholes tap underground aquifers and are common in rural and peri-urban areas. Wells, springs, rivers, and lakes are also used, though they often require treatment. Rainwater harvesting collects roof runoff into tanks. Each source has its own characteristics and requires different plumbing approaches. Your job is to get water from the source to the point of use safely, reliably, and efficiently. Let us start with municipal supply. A municipal main is a pressurised pipe, usually buried under the street or sidewalk. It delivers water to buildings through a service line. The service line typically includes a meter, a stop valve, and a connection to the building. Pressure varies: it may be high during off-peak hours and low during peak demand. In many African cities, pressure is insufficient to reach upper floors, so plumbers install storage tanks and booster pumps. You must know how to tap into a main legally and safely. Always get permission from the utility. Never tamper with the meter. Use proper fittings and a backflow prevention device to protect the mains from contamination. Boreholes are another major source. A borehole is a narrow shaft drilled into the ground to reach an aquifer. It usually has a submersible pump at the bottom, a riser pipe, and a control box at the surface. The pump pushes water up to a storage tank or directly into the system. Borehole water may contain iron, manganese, or bacteria, so testing and treatment are important. You may need to install filters, softeners, or chlorinators. When connecting a borehole to a building, use a pressure tank and a pressure switch to maintain steady flow. The pressure tank stores water under pressure and reduces pump cycling. This saves energy and extends pump life. Storage tanks are a key component in most African plumbing systems. They come in many types: plastic (polyethylene), fibreglass, concrete, and steel. Plastic tanks are popular because they are affordable, lightweight, and resistant to corrosion. They come in various sizes, from 200 litres to 10,000 litres or more. Tanks can be placed on the ground, on a raised platform, or on a roof. The height determines the gravity pressure. A tank 10 metres above the tap gives roughly 1 bar of pressure. That is enough for most household fixtures. If more pressure is needed, install a booster pump. When installing a tank, consider these factors: a solid base, access for cleaning, an overflow pipe, a vent, and a drain. The inlet should have a float valve to shut off water when the tank is full. The outlet should be slightly above the bottom to avoid sediment. A first-flush diverter is useful for rainwater tanks. Also, protect tanks from sunlight to prevent algae growth. In hot climates, dark tanks absorb heat; light-coloured tanks reflect it. Insulate if necessary. Pumps are the heart of many systems. There are several types: centrifugal pumps, submersible pumps, jet pumps, and booster pumps. Centrifugal pumps are common for surface water and boosting pressure. Submersible pumps sit inside a borehole or tank. Jet pumps are used for shallow wells. Booster pumps increase pressure in a line. When selecting a pump, you need to know the flow rate required and the total head (the height water must be lifted plus friction losses). Pump sizing is critical: too small and it will not meet demand; too big and it wastes energy and causes water hammer. Always install a check valve to prevent backflow, and a pressure switch or float switch to control operation. Protect pumps from power surges with a surge protector or voltage stabiliser, especially in areas with unstable electricity. Solar pumps are increasingly popular in Africa because they reduce dependence on grid power. They use photovoltaic panels to drive a DC pump. They are ideal for remote areas and for irrigation. The initial cost is higher, but running costs are low. If you work in rural areas, learn about solar pumping systems. Distribution is the network of pipes that carries water from the source or tank to fixtures. In a building, this is called the plumbing system. It includes the main supply line, branches, risers, and fixtures. Pipe sizing is crucial. If pipes are too small, pressure drops and flow is poor. If too large, you waste money and water sits stagnant. Use the correct size based on fixture demand. A typical house may use a 20mm or 25mm main line, with 15mm branches to individual fixtures. For larger buildings, you may need 32mm, 40mm, or 50mm mains. Always calculate demand using fixture units. A fixture unit is a measure of the water demand of a fixture. For example, a toilet may be 3 fixture units, a sink 1.5, a shower 2. Add them up and consult a sizing table. You will learn more about this in advanced modules. Materials for distribution include PPR, copper, PEX, and galvanised steel. PPR is popular in Africa because it is affordable, heat-resistant, and easy to join with a heat fusion tool. Copper is excellent but expensive. PEX is flexible and easy to install but may not be readily available everywhere. Galvanised steel is common in older buildings but corrodes. When joining dissimilar metals, use dielectric unions to prevent galvanic corrosion. Always support pipes properly with clips or brackets. Allow for expansion and contraction, especially with hot water pipes. Install isolation valves on each branch so you can shut off one area without draining the whole system. This saves time and water during repairs. Pressure management is essential. Too much pressure causes leaks, noise, and water hammer. Too little pressure means poor flow. Install a pressure reducing valve if the incoming pressure is above 4 bar. Water hammer is a shock wave caused by sudden valve closure. It can burst pipes. Prevent it with water hammer arrestors or by installing slow-closing valves. Air in the system can also cause problems. Air vents and automatic air release valves help. Water quality is another critical aspect. In many African cities, municipal water is treated, but it can still contain sediment or chlorine. Borehole water may have iron, manganese, or hardness. Hard water leaves scale in pipes and appliances. Install a water softener if hardness is high. Sediment filters remove particles. Carbon filters remove chlorine and odours. UV filters kill bacteria. Always test water before designing a treatment system. A simple test kit can measure pH, hardness, iron, and bacteria. If you are not sure, send a sample to a laboratory. Now let us consider layout. A typical gravity-fed system has a tank on a roof or stand. Water flows down to fixtures. A direct-pressurised system uses the municipal main or a pump to push water directly to fixtures. A hybrid system uses both. In Africa, hybrid systems are common because municipal supply is unreliable. You might have a tank that fills when municipal water is available, and a booster pump that pressurises the house. When municipal water fails, the tank provides water. You need a check valve to prevent tank water from flowing back into the municipal main. You also need a float valve to control tank filling. For multi-storey buildings, you may need a break tank and booster pump on each floor or a pressurised riser system. Firefighting requirements may also apply. Always check local building codes. Maintenance is key. Tanks should be cleaned at least twice a year. Pumps should be serviced annually. Filters should be replaced as recommended. Valves should be exercised regularly to prevent seizing. Leaks should be fixed immediately. A small leak wastes thousands of litres per year and can damage the building. Teach your clients how to shut off the main valve in an emergency. Label valves clearly. Keep a maintenance log. In summary, water supply systems involve sources, storage, pumps, and distribution. You must understand pressure, flow, pipe sizing, materials, and water quality. In Africa, you will often need to design hybrid systems that combine municipal supply, boreholes, tanks, and pumps. Always prioritise safety, reliability, and efficiency. With practice, you will be able to design and install systems that serve communities well.

3. Drainage and Sanitation: Waste Pipes, Traps, Vents, Septic Tanks, and Sewers

Drainage and sanitation are the other half of plumbing. While water supply brings clean water in, drainage takes dirty water out. If supply is done poorly, you get inconvenience. If drainage is done poorly, you get health hazards, bad smells, and structural damage. In Africa, sanitation is a critical issue. Many communities lack safe disposal systems. As a plumber, you can make a real difference by installing and maintaining proper drainage. This lesson covers the principles and practices of drainage and sanitation. Let us start with the basics. Drainage systems carry two types of waste: greywater and blackwater. Greywater comes from sinks, showers, baths, and washing machines. It contains soap, dirt, and small amounts of organic matter. Blackwater comes from toilets and urinals. It contains human waste and pathogens. Blackwater is much more hazardous. In some systems, they are kept separate; in others, they combine. You must know the difference because it affects pipe sizing, treatment, and disposal. The main components of a drainage system are waste pipes, traps, vents, and the disposal system. Waste pipes carry waste from fixtures to the disposal point. They must be sized correctly and sloped properly. The general rule is that waste pipes should slope downwards at a gradient of 1 in 40 to 1 in 100, depending on pipe size. A 40mm pipe should slope at least 1 in 40. A 100mm pipe can slope at 1 in 100. The slope allows gravity to carry waste away. If the slope is too shallow, solids settle and cause blockages. If too steep, water runs away too fast, leaving solids behind. So use the correct gradient. Pipe materials for drainage include PVC, uPVC, cast iron, and HDPE. PVC is the most common in Africa because it is cheap, lightweight, and easy to work with. It comes in sizes from 32mm to 200mm or more. Use solvent cement to join PVC pipes. Make sure the joints are clean and dry. Apply cement to both surfaces, push together, and hold for a few seconds. Allow to cure before testing. Cast iron is strong and quiet but heavy and expensive. HDPE is flexible and used for underground lines. Traps are essential. A trap is a U-shaped section of pipe that holds a small amount of water. This water seal prevents sewer gases from entering the building. Every fixture except a toilet has a trap. Toilets have an integrated trap. Common traps include P-traps, S-traps, and bottle traps. S-traps are prone to siphoning, so P-traps are preferred. The water seal should be at least 50mm deep. If a trap dries out, gases enter. This happens in unused fixtures. Pour water into unused traps regularly. Also, install trap primers if available. Vents are equally important. A vent pipe allows air to enter the drainage system, preventing vacuum conditions that would suck the water out of traps. Without vents, traps gurgle and lose their seal. Vents also allow sewer gases to escape safely above the roof. The vent pipe should extend at least 300mm above the roof and be fitted with a mesh screen to keep out birds and debris. There are different venting systems: individual vents, common vents, wet vents, and stack vents. In a typical house, the main stack serves as both waste and vent. Each fixture connects to the stack via a branch waste pipe and a vent. The stack goes up through the roof. For long horizontal runs, you may need additional vents. Always follow local plumbing codes. Now let us look at disposal systems. The two main types are sewers and septic tanks. A sewer is a network of underground pipes that carries waste to a treatment plant. In cities, sewers are common. To connect to a sewer, you need a building drain, a building sewer, and a connection to the public sewer. The building drain is the pipe inside the building. The building sewer is the pipe outside, from the building to the public sewer. It must be laid on a stable bed, with proper slope and joints. Use inspection chambers or manholes at changes of direction. Never connect stormwater to the sanitary sewer unless allowed. Stormwater should go to a separate drainage system. In many African cities, sewers are overloaded or non-existent. So septic tanks are widely used. A septic tank is a watertight chamber that receives blackwater and greywater. It separates solids, liquids, and scum. Solids settle at the bottom and are digested by bacteria. Liquids flow out to a drain field or soakaway. Scum floats on top. The tank should be sized based on the number of users and the volume of waste. A typical household tank is 2,000 to 5,000 litres. It should have two compartments for better separation. The outlet should have a T-pipe or baffle to prevent scum from escaping. The tank must be pumped out every 2 to 5 years, depending on use. Never use chemical cleaners that kill the bacteria. They stop the digestion process. The drain field is a network of perforated pipes buried in gravel. It allows liquid to soak into the ground. The soil must be permeable. If the soil is clay, you need a raised bed or a mound system. Keep the drain field away from wells and water sources. At least 30 metres is recommended. In dense urban areas, soakaways may not be feasible. Then you need a conservancy tank that is emptied regularly. For areas without waterborne sanitation, there are alternatives: pit latrines, ventilated improved pit latrines, composting toilets, and bio-digesters. As a plumber, you may be asked to install or maintain these. Learn about them. They are often more appropriate and affordable for rural and peri-urban areas. Now let us discuss installation best practices. Always plan the drainage layout before cutting pipes. Use a level and a string line to set the slope. Support pipes with brackets every 1 to 2 metres. Use solvent cement properly. Do not use too much; it can block the pipe. Do not use too little; it will leak. Test the system with water before closing walls. Fill each fixture and check for leaks. Also, check for proper venting. If you hear gurgling, the vent is inadequate. If you smell sewer gas, a trap is dry or a vent is blocked. Fix it immediately. Maintenance is critical. Blockages are the most common problem. They are caused by grease, hair, soap, food, and foreign objects. Teach your clients not to pour grease down the sink. Use strainers in sinks and showers. For blockages, use a plunger, a drain snake, or a high-pressure jetter. Chemical drain cleaners are a last resort because they damage pipes and the environment. For septic tanks, inspect the tank annually. Check the inlet and outlet baffles. Pump out when the sludge level reaches one-third of the tank depth. Keep records. For sewers, inspect manholes for blockages. Remove roots and debris. If you notice a bad smell, investigate. It could be a broken pipe or a blocked vent. Safety is paramount. When working on drainage, wear gloves, boots, and eye protection. Use a mask if there are gases. Never enter a septic tank or manhole without proper training and equipment. Hydrogen sulphide and methane can kill. Ventilate the area. Use a gas detector. Work in pairs. In Africa, many drainage systems are informal. You may need to improvise. But never compromise on health. Always aim for proper slopes, traps, and vents. A cheap job that fails is more expensive in the long run. Also, consider the environment. Untreated waste pollutes rivers, lakes, and groundwater. As a plumber, you are a guardian of public health. Do your part. In summary, drainage and sanitation involve waste pipes, traps, vents, septic tanks, and sewers. You must understand the principles of gravity flow, venting, and treatment. In Africa, you will encounter a mix of systems. Learn them all. Install them correctly. Maintain them well. Your work saves lives.

4. Fixtures and Appliances: Taps, Toilets, Showers, Sinks, Water Heaters, and Washing Machines

In this lesson we focus on fixtures and appliances. These are the visible parts of the plumbing system that people use every day. A fixture is a receptacle that receives water and discharges waste. Examples include sinks, toilets, showers, baths, and urinals. An appliance is a machine that uses water, such as a water heater, washing machine, or dishwasher. Your ability to install, repair, and maintain these will determine your reputation as a plumber. Clients notice when a tap drips, a toilet runs, or a shower has low pressure. Let us start with taps. A tap, or faucet, controls the flow of water. There are many types: compression, ball, cartridge, and ceramic disc. Compression taps have a rubber washer that presses against a seat. They are common in older buildings and are cheap but prone to dripping. Ball taps use a ball valve and are found in kitchens. Cartridge taps have a removable cartridge that controls flow and temperature. Ceramic disc taps use two ceramic discs and are durable and precise. They are becoming more popular in Africa. When installing a tap, ensure the correct inlet size, usually 15mm or 20mm. Use PTFE tape or sealing compound on the threads. Do not overtighten; it can crack the fitting. For mixer taps, connect hot and cold supplies correctly. The hot should be on the left. Test for leaks. If a tap drips, replace the washer or cartridge. For ceramic taps, replace the cartridge. Always turn off the water supply first. Toilets are another essential fixture. There are two main types: close-coupled and low-level. A close-coupled toilet has the cistern sitting directly on the pan. A low-level toilet has the cistern mounted on the wall with a flush pipe. There are also squat toilets, common in many parts of Africa. A toilet has several parts: the pan, the cistern, the flush mechanism, the fill valve, the flush valve, and the seat. Common problems include running toilets, weak flush, and leaks. A running toilet is usually caused by a faulty fill valve or flush valve. Replace the washer or the whole valve. A weak flush may be due to a blocked rim or a faulty flush valve. Clean the rim holes with a wire brush. Adjust the water level in the cistern. For squat toilets, ensure the trap is properly connected and vented. Showers and baths are also important. A shower mixes hot and cold water. It can be a simple mixer or a thermostatic valve. Thermostatic valves maintain a constant temperature, which is safer. Install a shower with a proper waterproof seal. Use silicone sealant, not grout, at joints. For baths, ensure the overflow and waste are connected. A common problem is a leaking waste. Replace the washer. For showers, low pressure is common. Check for blockages in the showerhead. Clean with vinegar. If pressure is low throughout the house, check the pipe sizing and the pressure tank. Sinks are found in kitchens and bathrooms. Kitchen sinks often have a garbage disposal unit, though these are less common in Africa. They have a trap and a waste pipe. Bathroom sinks are similar. When installing a sink, ensure the trap is aligned with the waste pipe. Use a proper trap adapter. Check for leaks at the trap and the supply lines. For kitchen sinks, install a grease trap if required by local codes. Water heaters are a major appliance. There are several types: electric storage, gas storage, instantaneous, and solar. Electric storage heaters have a tank and a heating element. Gas heaters use a burner. Instantaneous heaters heat water on demand. Solar heaters use the sun. In Africa, solar water heaters are increasingly popular because they save electricity. When installing a water heater, follow the manufacturer instructions. Ensure proper ventilation for gas heaters. Install a pressure relief valve and a temperature and pressure relief valve. Connect the hot water outlet to the fixtures. Use copper or PPR pipes for hot water. Insulate the pipes to save energy. For electric heaters, ensure the circuit is properly grounded and protected by a circuit breaker. For gas heaters, check for leaks with soapy water. Never use a flame. Washing machines and dishwashers are appliances that need water supply and drainage. They usually connect to a cold water tap and a standpipe or sink trap. Use a washing machine valve with a hose connection. Ensure the drain hose is looped high to prevent backflow. Check the inlet filters regularly. If the machine does not fill, check the valve and the filter. If it does not drain, check the pump and the hose. Now let us discuss installation tips. Always read the manufacturer instructions. Use the correct tools. Do not force fittings. Use PTFE tape on threaded connections. For compression fittings, tighten by hand then a quarter turn with a wrench. For push-fit fittings, ensure the pipe is cut square and inserted fully. Mark the insertion depth. Test all connections with water before closing walls. For fixtures, ensure they are level and secure. Use proper anchors. Seal around fixtures with silicone. For toilets, use a wax ring or a rubber seal. Tighten the bolts evenly. Do not rock the toilet. For taps, use a basin wrench for tight spaces. For showers, use a level to ensure the valve is straight. Maintenance is key. Teach your clients how to care for their fixtures. Clean taps with a soft cloth, not abrasive cleaners. Do not use chemical drain cleaners. Fix leaks immediately. Replace washers and cartridges as needed. For water heaters, flush the tank annually to remove sediment. Check the anode rod every two years. For washing machines, clean the filter monthly. For toilets, check the flapper and fill valve yearly. In Africa, water quality can affect fixtures. Hard water causes scale. Install a water softener if needed. Sediment can clog aerators. Clean them regularly. Low pressure can be improved with a booster pump. High pressure can damage fixtures. Install a pressure reducing valve. Also, consider water conservation. Install low-flow showerheads, dual-flush toilets, and aerators. These save water and money. Clients appreciate the savings. When choosing fixtures, consider durability and availability of spare parts. Cheap fixtures may fail quickly and be hard to repair. Recommend reputable brands. But also respect the client budget. Offer options. For rural areas, choose fixtures that are simple and robust. Avoid complex electronic taps that need batteries or power. For public buildings, choose vandal-resistant fixtures. Finally, think about your business. Fixture installation and repair is a common source of income. Many plumbers specialise in this. You can offer a maintenance contract to hotels, schools, and offices. You can also sell fixtures and spare parts. Keep a stock of common washers, cartridges, and valves. This saves time and increases profit. In summary, fixtures and appliances are the interface between the plumbing system and the user. You must know how to install, repair, and maintain them. Understand taps, toilets, showers, sinks, water heaters, and washing machines. Use the right materials and tools. Test for leaks. Teach your clients about care. Your skill with fixtures will keep customers calling.

5. Maintenance, Troubleshooting, and Business Practices

In this final lesson of Module 1, we bring everything together. You have learned about water supply, drainage, and fixtures. Now you need to know how to maintain systems, troubleshoot problems, and run a successful plumbing business. This lesson is practical and business-first. It will help you turn your skills into income and build a reputation that lasts. Let us start with maintenance. Preventive maintenance is better than emergency repairs. It saves money, time, and stress. As a plumber, you can offer maintenance contracts to homeowners, landlords, hotels, schools, hospitals, and businesses. A maintenance contract means you visit regularly, inspect the system, and fix small issues before they become big ones. This gives you steady income and builds loyalty. What does a maintenance visit include? First, check for leaks. Look at all visible pipes, joints, taps, and fixtures. A small drip can waste thousands of litres per year. Second, check water pressure. Use a pressure gauge. If pressure is too high, install a pressure reducing valve. If too low, investigate the cause. Third, check the water heater. Flush the tank to remove sediment. Test the pressure relief valve. Check the anode rod. Fourth, check the drainage system. Pour water into unused traps. Check for slow drains. Inspect the septic tank if applicable. Fifth, check the pumps. Listen for unusual noises. Check the pressure switch and the check valve. Clean the filters. Sixth, check the tank. Clean it if needed. Check the float valve and the overflow. Seventh, check the fixtures. Replace worn washers. Clean aerators. Adjust flush valves. Eighth, test for water quality. If there is a filter, replace the cartridge. Ninth, document everything. Keep a log of what you did and what needs attention. Give a copy to the client. This professionalism sets you apart. Cross-connections between potable water lines and wastewater, manure, or chemical lines can cause immediate and severe health emergencies â including cholera, typhoid, and E. coli outbreaks â among workers, families, and downstream communities. Quick Reference Checklist: Farm Plumbing System Health

  1. Source protection: Borehole head sealed, no surface water ingress, apron sloped away from well, and no livestock within 30 metres.
  2. Storage integrity: Tank lid fitted and insect-proof, tank cleaned and chlorinated every 6 months, and overflow screened.
  3. Pump performance: Flow rate and pressure checked monthly against design specification; solar panels cleaned weekly; backup power or gravity bypass available.
  4. Pipe condition: No visible leaks, no exposed PVC in sunlight, all joints pressure-tested after any repair, and buried lines marked on a site map.
  5. Cross-connection control: Potable and non-potable lines physically separated, air gaps installed at all chemical mixing points, and backflow preventers on hose bibs.
  6. Wastewater management: Septic tank or biogas digester desludged per schedule, soakaway not waterlogged, and no untreated effluent entering watercourses.
  7. Water quality: Free chlorine residual 0.2â0.5 mg/L at point of use; microbial test every 3 months; records kept for buyers and regulators.
  8. Documentation: As-built drawings, maintenance log, chemical dosing records, and water test certificates filed and accessible for audits.

Every plumbing failure has a cost. A burst pipe in a poultry house can kill birds through heat stress within hours. The business case for preventive plumbing maintenance is therefore not about avoiding inconvenience â it is about protecting revenue, meeting buyer standards (especially for export horticulture and dairy), and maintaining your licence to operate.
