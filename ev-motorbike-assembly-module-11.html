1. Pre-Power Safety Checks

Before you connect any battery or turn any key, you must do a full safety check. This protects you and the bike. Start with the workshop: make sure the floor is dry and clear of tools. You need rubber-soled shoes, safety glasses, and insulated gloves. Never wear metal jewellery like rings or watchesâthey can short a battery and burn you badly.

Check the bike itself. Look at the battery pack. The casing must be free of cracks, dents, or leaks. If you see any white powder or liquid, stop and call your supervisor. That is electrolyte, and it is dangerous. Check all high-voltage cables (usually orange). They must not be cut, pinched, or touching sharp metal edges. Look at every connectorâpull gently to make sure they are clicked in. A loose connector can arc and start a fire.

Next, check the low-voltage system. The 12V accessory battery should read above 12.4V on a multimeter. If it is below 12V, charge it first. Inspect the fuse box: all fuses must be the correct rating (usually 10A for lights, 15A for controller). Never use a bigger fuseâit will not blow and will melt wires instead. Test the kill switch and the main contactor by handâthey should click smoothly.

Finally, set up your testing area. Put a fire extinguisher (CO2 or dry powder) within arm's reach. Have a wooden stick or insulated hook to pull apart a connector if something goes wrong. Make sure no one else is within 2 metres of the bike. Only then can you move to the next lesson. If anything fails these checks, tag the bike 'DO NOT POWER' and report it. Safety is not optionalâit is the first test every bike must pass.

2. Low-Voltage System Test

The low-voltage system runs the lights, horn, display, and controller logic. It uses a 12V battery, separate from the main high-voltage pack. To test it, first disconnect the high-voltage pack completely. Then connect your multimeter (set to DC volts, 20V range) across the 12V battery terminals. A healthy battery reads 12.6V to 12.8V. If it reads below 12.2V, charge it with a 12V smart charger at 2A until full.

Now turn the key to 'ON' but do not twist the throttle. The display should light up. If it does not, check the main fuse (usually 10A) near the battery. Use your multimeter to test the fuse: touch both ends with the probes. A good fuse shows 0V drop (continuity). A blown fuse shows battery voltage across itâreplace it with the same rating. Next, test the kill switch. Flip it to 'OFF'âthe display should turn off. If not, the switch is faulty; replace it.

Test each light one by one. Turn on the headlight: measure voltage at the headlight connector. You should read within 0.5V of battery voltage (e.g., 12.1V if battery is 12.6V). If you read 0V, the wire is broken or the switch is bad. Do the same for brake light, indicators, and horn. For the horn, press the buttonâit should sound loudly. If it clicks but does not sound, the horn is stuck; tap it gently or replace it.

Finally, check the controller's low-voltage input. With the key on, measure voltage between the controller's thin red wire and ground. It should match battery voltage. If it reads 0V, trace the wire back to the key switchâlook for a broken wire or corrosion. Clean all terminals with contact cleaner and a small brush. Reconnect the high-voltage pack only after all low-voltage tests pass. Write down every voltage you measuredâthis is your baseline for future troubleshooting.

3. High-Voltage System Activation

High-voltage activation is the most dangerous step. You will work with 48V, 60V, or 72V DCâenough to kill you. Never do this alone. Have a second person standing by with a wooden stick to pull you away if needed. Wear class 0 insulated gloves (rated 1000V) and safety glasses. Remove all metal objects from your body.

First, verify the high-voltage pack voltage. Use a CAT III 1000V multimeter. Set it to DC volts, 200V range. Touch the probes to the main positive and negative terminals of the battery pack. You should read the nominal voltage (e.g., 48V pack reads 50.4V when full, 42V when empty). If you read 0V, the pack's internal fuse or BMS may have trippedâdo not open the pack. If you read a voltage much lower than expected, the pack is faulty. Tag it and report.

Now connect the high-voltage cables to the controller. The main contactor (a big relay) should be open. Connect the negative cable first, then the positive. You may see a small spark when connecting the positiveâthis is normal for capacitors charging. But if you see a large arc or smoke, stop immediately. After connecting, measure voltage at the controller's input terminals. It should match pack voltage. If not, check the contactor: it may not be closing. Listen for a click when you turn the key on.

Turn the key on. The contactor should click, and the controller should power up (you may hear a beep or see a light). Measure voltage at the controller's output to the motor (three thick phase wires). With the throttle closed, you should read 0V between any two phase wires. If you read pack voltage, the controller is shortedâreplace it. Now slowly twist the throttle. The voltage between phase wires should change smoothly from 0V up to pack voltage. If it jumps or stays at 0V, the throttle or controller is faulty. Once you confirm smooth voltage change, you can move to the motor spin test. Always disconnect the high-voltage pack before leaving the bike.

4. Motor Spin and Throttle Response Test

With the high-voltage system activated, you are ready to spin the motor. But first, secure the bike. Put it on a centre stand so the rear wheel is off the ground. Never test with the wheel on the groundâthe bike could shoot forward. Clear everyone away from the front and rear. Keep your hands off the wheel.

Turn the key on. The controller should be powered. Slowly twist the throttle about 1/4 turn. The motor should spin the rear wheel smoothly and quietly. You should hear a soft whine, not a grinding or clicking noise. If the wheel does not spin, check the three phase wiresâthey must be tight. Swap any two phase wires and try again. If it spins now, your original wiring order was wrong. If it still does not spin, check the hall sensor connector (a thin 5-wire plug from the motor). A loose hall sensor will stop the motor.

Now test throttle response. Twist the throttle slowly from 0 to full. The wheel should speed up smoothly and proportionally. If it jumps to full speed instantly, the throttle is faultyâreplace it. If it hesitates or stutters, the controller may be in a wrong mode. Check the controller's learning wires (usually two thin wires that you connect once to learn the motor). Also, test the brake cut-off: squeeze the brake lever while the throttle is open. The motor must stop immediately. If it does not, the brake switch is badâfix it before riding.

Test reverse if your bike has it. Flip the reverse switch and twist the throttle. The wheel should spin backwards slowly. Finally, check the motor temperature after 2 minutes of running. Touch the motor casingâit should be warm, not hot. If it is too hot to touch, the motor is drawing too much current. Check for a dragging brake or a shorted phase wire. Write down the maximum no-load speed (e.g., 45 km/h on the display). This is your baseline. If the motor passes all these tests, you can move to brake and light tests.

5. Brake and Light Function Test

Brakes and lights are your life insurance on the road. Test them with the bike on the ground, but with the motor off. First, check the brake levers. Squeeze each leverâit should feel firm, not spongy. If it feels spongy, there is air in the hydraulic line. Bleed the brakes using DOT 3 or DOT 4 fluid (check your bike's manual). For cable brakes, adjust the barrel adjuster until the lever stops 2 cm from the handlebar. The brake pads should have at least 2 mm of friction material left. If less, replace them.

Spin the wheel by hand. It should turn freely but stop when you squeeze the brake. The brake should lock the wheelâyou should not be able to turn it by hand when the brake is fully squeezed. If the wheel still turns, the brake is not working. Check for oil or grease on the padsâclean with brake cleaner. Also check the brake disc for warping: spin the wheel and watch the disc. It should not wobble more than 0.5 mm. If it wobbles, replace the disc.

Now test the lights. Turn the key on. Check the headlight: it should have both low and high beam. Switch between themâthe high beam should be brighter and aim slightly higher. Check the tail light: it should be on when the key is on. Press the brake leverâthe brake light should get brighter. If not, check the brake light switch (usually a small plunger near the lever). Adjust it or replace it. Test the indicators: turn on the left indicatorâfront and rear left should blink at about 1 blink per second. If they blink fast, one bulb is burnt out. If they do not blink, check the flasher relay.

Test the horn: press the buttonâit should be loud (at least 105 dB). If it is weak, check the horn's ground wire. Finally, check the instrument cluster: speedometer, battery gauge, and odometer should all work. If the speedometer does not move when you spin the wheel, the speed sensor is misalignedâadjust it to 1-2 mm from the magnet. Write down any faults and fix them before the road test.

6. Road Test Simulation and Data Logging

Before you ride on public roads, do a road test simulation in a safe areaâa closed yard or empty parking lot. You need a helmet, gloves, and closed shoes. Check tyre pressure: 28 psi front, 32 psi rear for most EV motorbikes. Adjust according to your weight and load. Check that both brakes work and all lights function. Then sit on the bike, turn the key on, and check the display for any error codes. Common codes: E-01 (throttle fault), E-02 (brake fault), E-03 (controller overheat). If you see a code, do not rideâfix it first.

Start slowly. Twist the throttle gently and ride in a straight line at 10 km/h. Listen for unusual noises: grinding, clicking, or rubbing. Feel for vibrations in the handlebars or footpegs. If you feel a strong vibration, stop and check the motor mounts and wheel bearings. Test the brakes at low speed: squeeze the front brake first, then the rear. The bike should stop smoothly without skidding. If the front wheel locks and skids, you are squeezing too hardâpractice progressive braking.

Now do a figure-of-eight at 15 km/h. This tests steering and balance. The bike should lean smoothly. If it feels heavy or resists turning, check the steering head bearingsâthey may be too tight. Next, ride over a small bump (like a speed bump) at 20 km/h. The suspension should absorb it without bottoming out. If you hear a loud clunk, the suspension is too soft or the spring is broken.

Data logging is critical. Use a simple smartphone app (like 'EV Dashboard' or 'Torque') connected via Bluetooth to your controller. Log these values for 10 minutes of riding: battery voltage, current (amps), motor temperature, and speed. A healthy 48V system at 30 km/h draws about 15-20A. If you see 40A or more, the motor is overloaded or the controller is misconfigured. After the ride, check the log: battery voltage should not drop below 42V (for 48V pack). If it does, the battery is weak. Motor temperature should stay below 80Â°C. If it exceeds 100Â°C, stop and let it coolâyou may need a bigger controller or better airflow. Save the log file and compare it to your next test. This is how you catch problems before they become breakdowns.

Practical Reference: Testing and Quality Control for EV Motorbike Assembly

In African e-mobility assembly, quality control is not a formality â it is the difference between a customer who returns with referrals and one who returns with a warranty claim. This practical reference walks through the seven-stage testing protocol used by leading assemblers in Kenya, Rwanda, Nigeria, and South Africa. Each stage builds on the previous one. Skipping steps does not save time; it transfers cost from the workshop to the roadside.

For a small assembly business, the goal is simple: every bike that leaves your floor must start, run, stop, and charge reliably. The protocol below is designed for a two-person team with basic tools â a multimeter, a torque wrench, a test stand or rollers, and a laptop or smartphone for data logging.

Stage 1: Pre-Power Safety Checks

Before any voltage is applied, the bike must be mechanically and electrically safe. This stage catches 60â70% of assembly defects. Check every fastener on the battery box, motor mounts, and frame. Torque values matter: an under-torqued battery terminal becomes a resistance heater under load; an over-torqued one cracks the terminal.

Use a 500V DC insulation tester (megger) between the HV positive rail and chassis ground. The reading must exceed 20 MÎ©. Below that, you have a leakage path â often from pinched cable insulation or a stray strand at a connector. Also verify polarity at every connector with a multimeter before plugging in the battery. Reverse polarity on a controller is a 100% failure event.

Stage 2: Low-Voltage System Test

Power up the 12V or 24V DC-DC converter and verify the auxiliary rail. The controller should wake and communicate. Check the CAN bus or UART link between the BMS, controller, and display. In many African assembly shops, the DC-DC converter is the most common early-life failure â often because it is mounted where vibration or water ingress kills it. Test it under load: turn on the headlight, horn, and indicators simultaneously and confirm the rail stays within Â±10% of nominal.

Stage 3: High-Voltage System Activation

This is the first time the full pack voltage reaches the controller. The pre-charge circuit limits inrush current. If the pre-charge resistor is undersized or the contactor sticks, you will hear a pop and smell ozone. Verify the sequence: key on, pre-charge relay closes, bus voltage rises to pack voltage within 2 seconds, main contactor closes, pre-charge relay opens. The BMS must report cell voltages and temperatures over CAN within 500ms of wake-up. Any cell group more than 50mV out of balance at rest should be flagged.

A 72V nominal pack charges to over 84V DC. This is lethal. Never work on an energized HV system with rings, watches, or metal tools. Use one hand only (keep the other in your pocket) when probing live circuits. Disconnect the service plug and wait 5 minutes for capacitor discharge before touching controller terminals. In African workshops where ambient temperatures exceed 35Â°C, battery thermal runaway risk rises â never charge or test a pack that is hot to the touch or has been in direct sun. Keep a Class D fire extinguisher within 3 meters of the test bay.

Stage 4: Motor Spin and Throttle Response Test

With the rear wheel off the ground, apply throttle gradually. The motor should spin smoothly from zero. Listen for grinding (bearing or gear mesh) or cogging (Hall sensor or phase wiring). Measure phase current on all three motor cables with a clamp meter. They should be balanced within 10%. If one phase draws significantly more, check the Hall sensor connector and phase wire continuity.

Throttle response should be immediate â under 200ms from twist to torque. A laggy throttle usually means a misconfigured controller parameter or a worn throttle potentiometer. Test full throttle and full regen (if equipped) for smooth transitions. On a test stand, run the motor at 50% throttle for 5 minutes and monitor controller and motor temperatures with an infrared thermometer. Controller case temperature should stay below 70Â°C.

Stage 5: Brake and Light Function Test

Every brake lever must cut motor power. Test front and rear independently. The brake switch should trigger regen cut-off and, if the controller supports it, regen braking. Verify that the brake light illuminates before the pads contact the disc â this is a legal requirement in Kenya, Rwanda, and South Africa. Check headlight aim, tail light, indicators, and hazard function. Horn should be audible at 10 meters.

Stage 6: Road Test Simulation and Data Logging

If you have a dynamometer or roller test stand, run a simulated road load. If not, a 2km outdoor test ride on a known route is acceptable. Log the following: pack voltage sag under acceleration, max controller temperature, motor temperature, and any BMS fault codes. A healthy pack should not sag more than 15% under full acceleration from a 50% state of charge.

Stage 7: Data Logging and Sign-Off

Record every test result in a batch log. Tag the bike with a unique serial number and the date of test. Any bike that fails a stage goes back to the relevant assembly station with a red tag describing the fault. Do not "fix it later" â a red-tagged bike should never reach the sales floor. In high-volume shops, a simple spreadsheet or a free tool like Google Sheets works. For larger operations, a barcode scanner and a simple database reduce errors.
