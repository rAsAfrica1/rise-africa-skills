Lessons

1. CPR and AED Skills Practice

When someone collapses and is not breathing, every second counts. In this lesson, you will practice CPR and using an AED on a manikin. Work on a firm, flat surface like a table or the ground. Kneel beside the manikin. Place the heel of one hand on the center of the chest, lower half of the breastbone. Put your other hand on top and interlock your fingers. Keep your arms straight and your shoulders directly above your hands. Push down hard and fast: at least 5 cm (2 inches) deep, at a rate of 100 to 120 compressions per minute. That's about 2 compressions per second. Count out loud: "One and two and three..." After 30 compressions, give 2 rescue breaths if you are trained and have a barrier device. Tilt the head back, lift the chin, pinch the nose, and blow for 1 second until the chest rises. If you are not trained or uncomfortable, do hands-only CPR: continuous chest compressions. Do not stop until help arrives or the person starts breathing.

An AED (Automated External Defibrillator) can restart the heart. Turn it on and follow the voice prompts. Expose the chest and dry it if wet. Attach the pads as shown: one below the right collarbone, one on the left side below the armpit. Make sure no one touches the person during analysis or shock. If a shock is advised, press the flashing button. Then immediately resume CPR for 2 minutes before the AED reanalyzes. In Africa, AEDs are found in airports, large malls, and some offices. If you don't have one, continue CPR until the ambulance arrives. Practice switching compressors every 2 minutes to avoid fatigue. Use a metronome app or a song like "Stayin' Alive" to keep the right rhythm. Remember: for children, use one hand and push about 5 cm deep; for infants, use two fingers and push 4 cm deep. Always check for breathing and pulse for no more than 10 seconds before starting.

2. Choking Relief Skills Practice

Choking happens when an object blocks the airway. The person may clutch their throat, cough weakly, or make a high-pitched noise. If they can cough forcefully, encourage them to keep coughing. If they cannot breathe, speak, or cough, act immediately. For a conscious adult or child, stand behind them and give 5 back blows. Use the heel of your hand to strike between the shoulder blades. Then give 5 abdominal thrusts (Heimlich maneuver). Stand behind, wrap your arms around their waist, make a fist with one hand, place it just above the navel, and grab it with your other hand. Pull inward and upward sharply. Repeat 5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts until the object comes out or they become unconscious. If they become unconscious, lower them to the ground and start CPR.

For a choking infant (under 1 year), sit and place the infant face-down along your forearm, supporting the head. Give 5 back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand. Then turn the infant face-up and give 5 chest thrusts using two fingers on the center of the chest. Repeat until the object is expelled. Do not do abdominal thrusts on infants.

For pregnant women or very large people, use chest thrusts instead of abdominal thrusts. Stand behind, place your arms under their armpits, and give thrusts to the center of the chest. If you are alone and choking, you can give yourself abdominal thrusts with your own fist, or press your upper abdomen against a chair back or table edge. Practice on a manikin or a partner (with caution) to build muscle memory. In many African communities, people may try to give water or oil to a choking personnever do that; it can make things worse. Also, avoid blind finger sweeps in the mouth; you might push the object deeper. Only remove the object if you can see it. After successful choking relief, the person should still be checked by a health worker because internal injuries can occur.

3. Bleeding Control and Bandaging Practice

Severe bleeding can kill in minutes. Your first step is to ensure your own safety: wear gloves if available, or use a plastic bag as a barrier. For heavy bleeding, apply direct pressure with a clean cloth, gauze, or even a shirt. Press hard and do not lift to check. If blood soaks through, add more layers on topdo not remove the first layer. Maintain pressure for at least 5 minutes. If bleeding is on an arm or leg and does not stop, you can apply a tourniquet. Use a commercial tourniquet if available, or a wide strip of cloth (at least 5 cm wide) and a stick to tighten. Place it 5-7 cm above the wound, not on a joint. Tighten until bleeding stops. Note the time. Do not loosen it; that's for medical staff.

For smaller cuts, clean the wound with clean water and mild soap. Apply antibiotic ointment if available. Cover with a sterile bandage or clean cloth. For bandaging, use a roller bandage. Start below the wound and wrap upward, overlapping each turn by half. Do not wrap too tightyou should be able to slip a finger under the bandage. Check for circulation: press a nail bed; it should turn pink within 2 seconds. If fingers or toes are cold, blue, or numb, loosen the bandage. For a wound on the head, use a triangular bandage as a cap. For a wound on the palm, place a roll of gauze in the hand and wrap the fingers around it. For a wound on the elbow or knee, bandage in a bent position. Practice on a partner or a manikin. In rural Africa, you may not have sterile supplies. Use the cleanest cloth you can find and boil it if possible. After bandaging, always wash your hands. If the wound is deep, dirty, or from an animal bite, seek medical care for tetanus shot and possible rabies prevention.

4. Splinting and Immobilization Practice

A fracture is a broken bone. You may see deformity, swelling, or hear a snap. Do not try to push the bone back. Your goal is to immobilize the area to prevent further injury. For a broken arm, use a splint that extends from above the elbow to beyond the wrist. You can use a rolled newspaper, a piece of wood, or a sturdy stick. Pad the splint with cloth to avoid pressure sores. Place the splint on the injured side and secure it with bandages or strips of cloth at three points: above the break, below the break, and at the joint. Then use a sling to support the arm. For a sling, use a triangular bandage or a large cloth. Place the arm across the chest, tie the ends behind the neck, and knot at the elbow.

For a broken leg, do not move the person unless absolutely necessary. Immobilize the leg by tying it to the other leg (the unaffected one) using bandages or cloths at the ankles, knees, and thighs. If you have a splint, place it on the outside of the leg from the armpit to the foot. Secure it at the chest, waist, knee, and ankle. For a broken collarbone, use a sling and a swathe (a wide bandage wrapped around the chest and arm) to keep the arm from moving. For a broken finger, tape it to the next finger with a small piece of cloth or tape, but not too tight. For a broken rib, do not wrap the chest; instead, have the person hold a pillow against the ribs for support. Always check circulation after splinting: press the nail bed and see if it turns pink quickly. If not, loosen the splint. In Africa, traditional bonesetters are common, but improper setting can cause permanent disability. Always encourage the person to go to a clinic or hospital for an X-ray. Practice splinting on a partner using everyday materials. Remember: when in doubt, immobilize and seek help.

5. Scenario-Based Emergency Simulations

In this lesson, you will practice responding to realistic emergencies. Work in groups of 3-4: one person is the victim, one is the rescuer, and others are observers who give feedback. Use a timer and a manikin or a willing volunteer. Scenario 1: A man collapses in a shop. He is unresponsive and not breathing. You check the scene for safety, tap his shoulders, and shout, "Are you okay?" No response. You call for help and ask someone to call an ambulance and get an AED. You start CPR with 30 compressions and 2 breaths. After 2 minutes, the AED arrives. You apply it and follow prompts. Continue until help arrives. Debrief: Did you check for breathing? Did you compress deep enough?

Scenario 2: A child chokes on a groundnut. She is coughing weakly and turning blue. You give 5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts. The groundnut pops out. She starts crying. You comfort her and advise her parents to take her to a clinic. Scenario 3: A farmer cuts his leg with a machete. Blood is spurting. You put on gloves, apply direct pressure with a clean cloth, and elevate the leg. Bleeding slows. You bandage the wound and check circulation. You note the time and advise him to go to a health center for stitches.

Scenario 4: A woman falls from a bicycle and has a deformed forearm. You do not move her. You check for other injuries. You splint the arm with a rolled magazine and a sling. You monitor her breathing and keep her warm. You call for transport. After each scenario, discuss what went well and what could be improved. Use a checklist: Did you ensure scene safety? Did you call for help? Did you use correct technique? Did you show empathy? Practice until you can perform each skill without hesitation. In real life, you may be the only person who knows first aid. These simulations build confidence and speed. Do them monthly with your colleagues or community group. Remember: in Africa, help may take time to arrive, so your actions in the first 10 minutes are critical.

6. Final Skills Assessment and Certification

To earn your Basic First Aid certification, you must pass a skills assessment. This is a hands-on test where an instructor watches you perform key skills. You will be evaluated on CPR, choking relief, bleeding control, and splinting. For CPR, you must show correct hand placement, depth (at least 5 cm), rate (100-120 per minute), and use of an AED. You will do 2 minutes of CPR on a manikin. For choking, you will demonstrate back blows and abdominal thrusts on a manikin. For bleeding, you will apply direct pressure and a bandage. For splinting, you will immobilize a broken arm with a splint and sling. You must also answer 10 oral questions about when to call for help, signs of shock, and how to prevent infection.

The assessment takes about 30 minutes. You need to score at least 80% to pass. If you don't pass, you can retake it after one week of practice. Upon passing, you will receive a certificate valid for 2 years. Keep it in your business or community health post. To maintain your skills, practice monthly and consider taking a refresher course every year. In many African countries, first aid certification is recognized by the Red Cross and St. John Ambulance. You can also join a local first aid club. After certification, you can train others in your community. Start by teaching simple skills like handwashing and bleeding control. Remember: certification is just the beginning. Real confidence comes from regular practice. Set up a monthly drill with your coworkers. Use the same scenarios from Lesson 5. Keep your first aid kit stocked and check expiration dates. Your ability to act calmly and correctly can save a lifemaybe even someone you love.

<h2>Practical Reference: Hands-On Practice and Certification Preparation</h2>

<p>In African business settings from a maize milling plant in Nakuru to a market stall in Kumasi, a construction site in Lusaka, or a community cooperative in rural Senegal the difference between a trained responder and an untrained bystander is measured in minutes. This Practical Reference consolidates the hands-on skills you practised in the six preceding lessons: CPR and AED use, choking relief, bleeding control and bandaging, splinting and immobilisation, and scenario-based emergency simulation. It is designed as a workplace and community reference tool: print it, laminate it, and post it where staff and community members can reach it. It also prepares you for the Final Skills Assessment and Certification by summarising the performance standards assessors will apply.</p>

<p><strong>Business-first framing:</strong> every skill below reduces downtime, protects your workforce, and lowers the human and financial cost of an emergency. A single prevented fatality or permanent disability can cost a small enterprise more than a year of revenue in lost productivity, compensation, and reputational damage. First aid competence is therefore not a welfare extra it is operational risk management.</p>

<p style="margin:0; font-weight:bold; color:#c0392b; font-size:1.05em;"> SAFETY WARNING Do Not Practise on a Live Person Without Supervision</p>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Chest compressions and abdominal thrusts delivered with full force can cause rib fractures, internal organ injury, or death if applied to a healthy, breathing person. Practise only on approved manikins (CPR manikins, choking vests) under the supervision of a certified instructor. Never use a tourniquet for practice on a human limb. In a real emergency, if you are unsure whether a person is breathing normally, treat them as cardiac arrest and begin CPR the risk of doing nothing is far greater than the risk of doing CPR on someone who does not need it. In African settings where ambulance response may be delayed, early CPR and bleeding control are the single most effective life-saving interventions available to bystanders.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<p>Use this checklist before, during, and after any emergency response. It is designed to be read aloud or posted on a wall in a staff room, workshop, or community hall.</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Check safety first.</strong> Is the scene safe for you, the casualty, and bystanders? (Traffic, fire, electricity, violence, animals.)</li>
<li><strong>Check responsiveness and breathing.</strong> Shout and shake gently. If unresponsive and not breathing normally, call for help and start CPR.</li>
<li><strong>Activate emergency response.</strong> Call your local emergency number or designated first aider. In many African contexts, send a specific person do not shout someone call an ambulance.</li>
<li><strong>Control severe bleeding immediately.</strong> Direct pressure, pack the wound, and apply a tourniquet only for life-threatening limb bleeding. Record the time.</li>
<li><strong>Use the AED as soon as it arrives.</strong> Power on, attach pads, clear the patient, deliver shock if advised, resume compressions immediately.</li>
<li><strong>For choking, act fast.</strong> 5 back blows, then 5 abdominal thrusts (chest thrusts for pregnant or obese casualties). Repeat until relieved or the person collapses.</li>
<li><strong>Immobilise suspected fractures.</strong> Splint above and below the injury. Check distal pulse, sensation, and movement before and after.</li>
<li><strong>Handover and document.</strong> Give a clear handover (what happened, what you did, time, casualty response) and record the incident in your workplace or community log.</li>
</ol>
