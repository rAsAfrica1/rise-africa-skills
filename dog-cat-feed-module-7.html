Lesson 7.1~12 min

Ingredient Database and Nutrient Composition

In this lesson
  • Build a working ingredient table with price, moisture and nutrient columns
  • Convert any nutrient value between an as-fed and a dry matter basis
  • Decide which ingredient figures must be analysed rather than taken from a book

Nothing else in this module works without a table. Before you can formulate you need, for every ingredient you can actually buy, its price, its moisture and at least its crude protein, crude fat, crude fibre, calcium and phosphorus. Build the table once, keep it in a notebook or a spreadsheet, and update the price column every month.

Here is a starting table using typical values for ingredients available across much of Africa. Treat every nutrient figure here as approximate. They are book values for planning, and they must be replaced by your own supplier's analysis before you make a completeness claim. Prices are examples in USD per kilogram and will differ in your town.

  1. Maize meal: 88 percent dry matter, 9.0 percent crude protein, 4.0 percent fat, 2.5 percent fibre, 0.03 percent calcium, 0.28 percent phosphorus, USD 0.41.
  2. Cassava flour: 88 percent DM, 2.5 percent protein, 0.5 percent fat, 3.0 percent fibre, USD 0.28.
  3. Brewer's rice: 89 percent DM, 8.0 percent protein, 1.5 percent fat, USD 0.38.
  4. Sorghum: 88 percent DM, 10.0 percent protein, 3.0 percent fat, USD 0.36.
  5. Wheat bran: 88 percent DM, 15.0 percent protein, 4.0 percent fat, 11 percent fibre, USD 0.24.
  6. Soybean meal: 89 percent DM, 44 percent protein, 1.5 percent fat, 7 percent fibre, 0.30 percent calcium, 0.65 percent phosphorus, USD 0.62.
  7. Sunflower cake: 90 percent DM, 28 percent protein, 8 percent fat, 22 percent fibre, USD 0.34.

Now the conversion that trips up more small producers than any other. Nutrient standards are written on a dry matter basis. Your ingredients and your finished food are measured as fed, with water in them. To go from as fed to dry matter, divide by the dry matter fraction. A food testing 24.9 percent crude protein at 10 percent moisture has a dry matter fraction of 0.90, so on a dry matter basis it is 24.9 divided by 0.90 = 27.7 percent. To go the other way, multiply. A dog adult minimum of 18.0 percent on a dry matter basis becomes 18.0 x 0.90 = 16.2 percent as fed for a food at 10 percent moisture.

Get this wrong in the wrong direction and you fail the profile while believing you passed. A wet food at 78 percent moisture testing 9 percent protein as fed is 9 divided by 0.22 = 40.9 percent on a dry matter basis, which is a very high protein cat food, not a low one. Never compare a wet and a dry product on the label figures.

Which figures must be analysed rather than assumed? Use this rule. Anything that varies a lot between batches and matters a lot to the animal. In practice that means crude protein and moisture on every protein meal, calcium and phosphorus on any bone-containing meal, and fat on any rendered meal. Cereals are comparatively stable, so a book value is usually acceptable for maize or cassava starch. Meat and bone meal digestibility and inclusion figures are not established in the verified reference at all, and rendered meals vary with rendering temperature, so a supplier certificate matters most exactly where the book is weakest.

An example from Eldoret, Kenya. A producer bought two consignments of poultry by-product meal from different renderers at the same price. The first analysed at 58 percent protein and 3.4 percent calcium, the second at 49 percent protein and 7.1 percent calcium, because the second contained far more bone. Formulating both at 34 percent inclusion, the second consignment gave a food 3 percentage points short on protein and pushed dietary calcium from 1.32 to 2.53 percent, over the 1.8 percent ceiling that applies to any food that might be fed to a large-breed puppy. One USD 55 analysis would have caught it. She now buys nothing without a certificate and re-tests one sack in five.

Do this today: write down ten ingredients you can buy within a day's travel, telephone for today's price on at least three of them, and start the table with columns for supplier, price, moisture, protein, fat, fibre, calcium and phosphorus. Mark every cell you had to guess.

Pearson's Square Method of Poultry Feed Formulation

Feathers Pro

Lesson 7.2~12 min

Step-by-Step Formulation Process

In this lesson
  • Formulate a complete adult dog food using Pearson's Square and fixed ingredient reservation
  • Verify a finished formulation against protein, fat, calcium, phosphorus and ratio targets
  • Calculate metabolisable energy from the finished analysis

This is the lesson to copy. Work it once with the numbers given, then work it again with your own prices. The order of operations matters: reserve the fixed ingredients, use Pearson's Square for protein, correct fat with oil, correct minerals, then check everything on a dry matter basis.

Target: an adult dog dry food at about 25 percent crude protein and 12 percent fat as fed, at 10 percent moisture. On a dry matter basis that is 27.8 percent protein and 13.3 percent fat, well above the adult minima of 18.0 and 5.5 percent.

Step 1, reserve the fixed ingredients. Per 100 kilograms: vitamin and mineral premix 0.5 kg, salt 0.5 kg, dicalcium phosphate 0.5 kg, DL-methionine 0.1 kg, choline chloride 0.1 kg, vegetable oil 5.5 kg. That is 7.2 kg fixed, leaving 92.8 kg for the protein meal and the cereal.

Step 2, work out what the remaining 92.8 kg must contain. All 25 kg of crude protein per 100 kg has to come from those 92.8 kg, because oil and minerals contribute none. So the blend must test 25 divided by 0.928 = 26.9 percent crude protein.

Step 3, Pearson's Square. Use poultry by-product meal at 58 percent and maize meal at 9 percent. Write 26.9 in the middle, 58 top left, 9 bottom left. Subtract diagonally, smaller from larger. 26.9 minus 9 = 17.9 parts of poultry meal. 58 minus 26.9 = 31.1 parts of maize. Total 49.0 parts. Poultry meal is 17.9 divided by 49.0 = 36.5 percent of the blend, and maize 63.5 percent. Applied to 92.8 kg: poultry meal 0.365 x 92.8 = 33.9 kg and maize 0.635 x 92.8 = 58.9 kg.

Step 4, write the sheet per 100 kg. Poultry by-product meal 33.9, maize meal 58.9, vegetable oil 5.5, premix 0.5, salt 0.5, dicalcium phosphate 0.5, DL-methionine 0.1, choline chloride 0.1. Total 100.0 kg.

Step 5, check protein. 33.9 x 0.58 = 19.66 and 58.9 x 0.09 = 5.30. Total 24.96 percent as fed, which is 24.96 divided by 0.90 = 27.7 percent on a dry matter basis. Above the 18.0 minimum.

Step 6, check fat. 33.9 x 0.12 = 4.07, 58.9 x 0.04 = 2.36, oil 5.5 x 1.00 = 5.50. Total 11.93 percent as fed, 13.3 percent on a dry matter basis. Above the 5.5 minimum.

Step 7, check calcium and phosphorus. Calcium: 33.9 x 0.035 = 1.187, dicalcium phosphate 0.5 x 0.23 = 0.115, maize 58.9 x 0.0003 = 0.018. Total 1.320 percent as fed, 1.47 percent dry matter. Phosphorus: 33.9 x 0.018 = 0.610, dicalcium phosphate 0.5 x 0.18 = 0.090, maize 58.9 x 0.0028 = 0.165. Total 0.865 percent as fed, 0.96 percent dry matter, below the 1.6 percent maximum. Ratio 1.320 divided by 0.865 = 1.53 to 1, inside the 1:1 to 2:1 window and close to the quoted optimum of 1.2 to 1.4 to 1.

Step 8, energy. Assume the finished food is 2.5 percent crude fibre, 7 percent ash and 10 percent moisture. Nitrogen-free extract is 100 minus 24.96 minus 11.93 minus 2.5 minus 7 minus 10 = 43.61 percent. Gross energy per 100 g is 5.7 x 24.96 = 142.3, plus 9.4 x 11.93 = 112.1, plus 4.1 x (43.61 + 2.5) = 189.1, total 443.5 kcal. Dog energy digestibility is 91.2 minus 1.43 x 2.5 x 100 divided by 90, that is 91.2 minus 3.97 = 87.2 percent. Digestible energy is 443.5 x 0.872 = 386.7. Metabolisable energy for dogs is digestible energy minus 1.04 times protein, that is 386.7 minus 25.96 = 360.7 kcal per 100 g, or 3607 kcal per kilogram. That sits inside the normal dry food range of 2800 to 4800 kcal per kilogram.

Step 9, cost it. Poultry meal 33.9 x 1.10 = 37.29, maize 58.9 x 0.41 = 24.15, oil 5.5 x 1.30 = 7.15, premix 0.5 x 4.50 = 2.25, salt 0.5 x 0.25 = 0.13, dicalcium phosphate 0.5 x 0.75 = 0.38, methionine 0.1 x 4.80 = 0.48, choline 0.1 x 2.20 = 0.22. Total USD 72.05 per 100 kg, that is USD 0.72 per kilogram.

Step 10, the feeding guide. A 20 kilogram neutered adult dog needs 70 x 20^0.75 x 1.6 = 662 x 1.6 = 1059 kcal a day, which is 1059 divided by 3607 = 0.294 kg, so 294 grams a day.

An example from Jinja, Uganda. A producer ran exactly this sheet, then sent the food for analysis. Protein came back at 23.4 percent rather than 24.96, because his poultry meal was 54 percent, not 58. He raised inclusion to 36.4 kg and re-tested. The calculation was not wrong; the ingredient figure was.

Vitamin and Mineral Premix Preparation

In this lesson
  • Calculate the quantity of a mineral source needed to meet a dietary requirement
  • Build a premix specification at a stated inclusion rate with a suitable carrier
  • Use three-stage dilution and a weighing discipline that prevents vitamin D overdose

The premix is the smallest part of the formula and the part most likely to hurt an animal. Vitamin D is the nutrient you are most likely to poison an animal with by accident, and real-world cases come from premix and formulation error rather than from raw ingredients. Multiple dry food recalls followed vitamin D errors from one contract manufacturer in 2018, canned recalls followed in January, March and May 2019, and warning letters were issued in 2019. Those were companies with laboratories and quality managers.

Start with the arithmetic of a single mineral. Suppose you must meet the dog adult zinc minimum of 80 mg per kilogram of dry matter, and you use zinc oxide at 72 percent zinc. The quantity needed is 80 divided by 0.72 = 111 mg of zinc oxide per kilogram of diet, which is 111 grams per tonne. If your premix is included at 0.5 percent, that is 5 kg of premix per tonne, so zinc oxide is 111 g divided by 5000 g = 2.22 percent of the premix by weight.

Repeat for each mineral. Dog adult minima are iron 40, copper 7.3, manganese 5.0, zinc 80, iodine 1.0 and selenium 0.35 mg per kilogram of dry matter. Cat adult minima are iron 80, copper 5 for extruded food, manganese 7.6, zinc 75, iodine 0.6 and selenium 0.3. Note that the cat needs twice the iron of the dog and the dog needs more zinc than the cat, so a single premix for both species is a compromise you should make deliberately, not by accident. Growth figures are higher again: dog growth zinc is 100 and copper 12.4 mg per kilogram.

Now the dangerous one. Say your target is 1000 IU of vitamin D per kilogram of diet, safely between the dog minimum of 500 and the maximum of 3000 IU per kilogram of dry matter, and comfortably below the European legal ceiling of 2270 IU per kilogram of dry matter. Using a vitamin D3 product at 500,000 IU per gram, you need 1000 divided by 500,000 = 0.002 g per kilogram of diet, which is 2 grams per tonne. Two grams. In a tonne. Now see the failure: if an operator weighs 20 grams instead of 2, the diet contains 10,000 IU per kilogram, more than three times the maximum, and the signs described by regulators are vomiting, little appetite, increased drinking and urination, excessive drooling and weight loss, appearing within hours to days. The cat maximum is much higher at 30,080 IU per kilogram, but a dog food made on the same line is not protected by that.

The defence is three-stage dilution and never weighing a micro-ingredient straight into a batch.

  1. Stage one, the micro premix. Weigh vitamin D3, selenium and iodine on a balance reading to 0.01 g, and blend into 1 kilogram of a fine carrier such as rice polishings or maize meal. Mix five minutes.
  2. Stage two, the full premix. Blend that 1 kilogram into the remaining minerals, vitamins and carrier to make the total premix, at your chosen inclusion rate. Mix ten minutes.
  3. Stage three, the batch. Add the premix to the mixer with part of the cereal on top and below it, never as the first or last thing in, and mix the full batch for the time your mixer needs.

Two people, two signatures. One weighs, one checks and both sign the batch record before the material goes in. This costs nothing and it is the single most valuable control in a small mill.

Buying against making. A commercial dog premix at USD 4.50 a kilogram used at 0.5 percent costs 0.005 x 4.50 = USD 0.0225 per kilogram of feed, about 3 percent of a USD 0.72 ingredient cost. Buying your own straights to build the same premix might save a third of that, USD 0.0075 per kilogram, or USD 15 per tonne. Against that, you carry the weighing risk, you must buy selenium and vitamin D in quantities far larger than you need, and you need a balance reading to 0.01 g costing USD 120 to 300. For most producers under about 20 tonnes a month, buy a premix from a reputable supplier, insist on a specification sheet and an expiry date, and store it sealed, cool and dark.

An example from Kumasi, Ghana. A producer switched premix supplier to save USD 0.9 a kilogram and received a product with no specification sheet and no expiry date. Her next batch of cat food analysed short on vitamin A, which for a cat must be preformed at 3332 IU per kilogram for adult maintenance because beta-carotene cannot be converted. She went back to the original supplier. The saving on 2 tonnes a month had been USD 9. The recall of 400 kilograms cost her USD 840.

Least-Cost Formulation Algorithms

In this lesson
  • Calculate the cost of one percentage point of protein from each candidate ingredient
  • Perform a costed substitution that holds protein and fat constant
  • Set the constraints that stop a least-cost solution from producing a bad food

Least-cost formulation sounds like software. It is really one idea: for every constraint you must meet, find the cheapest way to meet it, subject to every other constraint. You can do most of it with a calculator, and you should, because doing it by hand is what teaches you where the money actually is.

The key figure is the cost of one percentage point of a nutrient, measured as a substitution against your base cereal. Take the worked formulation from Lesson 2, which used poultry by-product meal at USD 1.10 and maize at USD 0.41 per kilogram.

Swap 1 kilogram of maize for 1 kilogram of poultry meal in a 100 kilogram batch. Protein changes by 58 minus 9 = 49 g per 100 kg, that is 0.49 percentage points. Cost changes by 1.10 minus 0.41 = USD 0.69. So a percentage point of protein via poultry meal costs 0.69 divided by 0.49 = USD 1.41 per 100 kilograms.

Now soybean meal at USD 0.62 and 44 percent. Protein change is 44 minus 9 = 0.35 percentage points, cost change 0.62 minus 0.41 = USD 0.21. Cost per point is 0.21 divided by 0.35 = USD 0.60 per 100 kilograms. Soy delivers protein at less than half the cost of poultry meal.

Fish meal at USD 1.55 and 60 percent: change 0.51 points, cost USD 1.14, so USD 2.24 per point. Sunflower cake at USD 0.34 and 28 percent: change 0.19 points, cost minus USD 0.07, so it is cheaper than maize and adds protein, which looks like free money until you remember it carries 22 percent crude fibre.

Now do a real substitution and keep the food honest. Replace 10 kg of poultry meal with soybean meal, holding protein constant. Removing 10 kg of poultry meal removes 5.80 points of protein. Adding 14 kg of soybean meal adds 6.16 points. Removing 4 kg of maize removes 0.36 points. Net change is 6.16 minus 5.80 minus 0.36 = 0.00. Protein is unchanged.

Cost: minus 10 x 1.10 = minus USD 11.00; plus 14 x 0.62 = plus USD 8.68; minus 4 x 0.41 = minus USD 1.64. Net minus USD 3.96 per 100 kilograms.

But check fat. Poultry meal removed 10 x 0.12 = 1.20 points of fat; soy added 14 x 0.015 = 0.21; maize removed 4 x 0.04 = 0.16. Net fat is down 1.15 points. Restore it with 1.2 kg of oil at USD 1.30, costing USD 1.56, and take 1.2 kg out of the maize, recovering USD 0.49. Net saving is 3.96 minus 1.56 plus 0.49 = USD 2.89 per 100 kilograms, that is USD 0.029 per kilogram, about 4 percent of the USD 0.72 ingredient cost. On 2000 kilograms a month that is USD 58.

Now the constraints that stop the calculator making a bad food, because a least-cost solution without constraints will always drive to the cheapest nutrients and the worst diet.

  1. Nutrient minima and maxima: protein, fat, calcium, phosphorus, the calcium to phosphorus window of 1:1 to 2:1, and the phosphorus maximum of 1.6 percent for dogs.
  2. Amino acid minima, because crude protein is not protein quality. For dogs, lysine 0.63 and methionine plus cystine 0.65 percent of dry matter. Soy is short of methionine, which is why the substitution above needs its DL-methionine kept in.
  3. Ingredient inclusion ceilings from the evidence: bone protein meal to 12 percent and feather meal to 5 percent for faecal quality, and a cautious approach to soy given that it reduced dry matter and gross energy digestibility in most trials reviewed.
  4. A fibre ceiling. Sunflower cake looks free but at 22 percent fibre it will dilute energy and depress digestibility.
  5. A species rule. Cat foods must also hold taurine at 0.10 percent, arachidonic acid at 0.02 percent, preformed vitamin A at 3332 IU per kilogram and niacin at 60 mg per kilogram, none of which cheap plant ingredients supply.

When you move to a spreadsheet, the structure is one row per ingredient with a changing cell for its inclusion, one column per nutrient, a cost cell that sums price times inclusion, and a set of constraint rows. Set the total inclusion to equal 100, add the minima and maxima as constraints, and minimise cost. The free solver tools in common spreadsheet software will do this. The arithmetic above is what tells you whether the answer it gives is sensible.

An example from Ndola, Zambia. A producer ran a solver and it returned 31 percent soybean meal, because soy was the cheapest protein point. Stool scores in six customer dogs moved from a mean of 3.0 to 4.1 within two weeks. He added a soy ceiling of 15 percent to the model, cost rose by USD 0.021 per kilogram, and the stools recovered. The constraint was worth more than the saving.

Quality Assurance and Testing

In this lesson
  • Design a sampling and retained-sample routine for every production batch
  • Test mixer uniformity and interpret the result
  • Choose which laboratory tests to buy on a limited budget

Quality assurance in a small mill is not a laboratory. It is a set of routines that cost almost nothing and that make a problem visible before a customer finds it.

Start with sampling, because a bad sample makes an expensive analysis worthless. Take small portions from at least ten points in a batch, from the top, middle and bottom of the bin or from every fifth bag as it fills. Combine them in a clean bucket, mix, then quarter down: spread the pile, divide into four, discard two opposite quarters, remix, and repeat until you have about 500 grams. Split that into two 250 gram bags, both labelled with the product, the batch code and the date. One goes to the laboratory if needed; one is the retained sample.

Keep every retained sample for at least the full shelf life of the product plus one month, in a sealed bag, in a cool dark place, in date order. This costs you a shelf and a box of bags. It is the only thing that lets you answer a complaint with evidence instead of an apology.

Next, incoming goods. For every delivery, record the supplier, the date, the quantity, the lot number and your own observations. Smell it: rancid fat is obvious and disqualifying. Look at it: mould, insect damage, foreign material, caking. Check moisture if you can. Maize and groundnut cake in humid climates carry a mould toxin risk, and the legal limits and testing requirements differ by country, so ask your national standards body what applies to pet food where you are and write down the answer with the date and the officer's name. Do not accept visibly mouldy cereal at any price.

Mixer uniformity is the test most small producers have never run and should. Add a tracer that is easy to measure, most simply salt, at a known rate. Mix for your normal time. Then take ten samples from different points in the mixer, and have each analysed for salt, or use a simple chloride test kit. Calculate the mean and the standard deviation, then the coefficient of variation as the standard deviation divided by the mean times 100. The feed industry rule of thumb is that a coefficient of variation under 10 percent is acceptable, and this is a working convention rather than a figure from the verified nutrient standards. If yours is worse, extend the mixing time and repeat until it improves, then write the time down and never mix for less. This matters most for the premix: an unevenly mixed vitamin D is exactly how animals get hurt.

Now laboratory tests, in priority order for a producer with limited money.

  1. Proximate analysis, meaning moisture, crude protein, crude fat, crude fibre and ash. Typically USD 60 to 140 in a commercial African laboratory. Run it on every new formulation and at least quarterly on the routine ones.
  2. Calcium and phosphorus. Essential for any diet using bone-containing meals, and the way you check the 1:1 to 2:1 ratio and the 1.6 percent phosphorus maximum for dogs.
  3. Taurine, for any cat food, against the 0.10 percent minimum for extruded products and 0.20 percent for canned.
  4. Microbiology, meaning total plate count and Salmonella, on any product containing raw or lightly processed animal material and on any wet product.
  5. Amino acids and vitamins. Expensive, and worth buying only when you change protein source or premix supplier.

Budget it as a percentage. If you produce 2000 kilograms a month with an ingredient cost of USD 0.72 a kilogram, that is USD 1440 of ingredients. Spending USD 120 a month on testing is 8 percent of ingredient cost in the first year and can fall to 3 or 4 percent once your formulations are stable. Write it into your price.

Finally, the batch record, which is one sheet per batch and which you must be able to produce two years later. It carries the date, the batch code, the formulation version, every ingredient with its quantity and its supplier lot number, the mixing time, the operator's name, the checker's name, the number of bags produced, the bag codes, and where they were sold. The reason to keep it is recall. Large manufacturers with quality systems have had to recall product over vitamin D errors; what made recall possible at all was traceability.

An example from Dar es Salaam, Tanzania. A retailer complained that one 10 kilogram bag had a strong rancid smell. The producer looked up the batch code, found the record, saw that the batch had used oil from a lot delivered three weeks earlier, opened the retained sample and confirmed the smell. She withdrew the eleven remaining bags from that batch, replaced them, and changed oil supplier. Total cost USD 96 and a Saturday. Without the code and the retained sample she would have had to withdraw everything or deny everything.

Shelf Life and Storage Management

In this lesson
  • Identify the three processes that end a pet food's shelf life and control each one
  • Run a simple shelf-life trial and set a defensible best-before date
  • Calculate the cost of stock losses and design a first-in first-out store

Shelf life is not a number you copy from a competitor's bag. It is a claim about your product, in your climate, in your packaging, and you should be able to show how you arrived at it.

Three processes end a pet food's life, and they need different controls.

First, moisture and mould. A dry food must come out of the drier or extruder low enough that it does not support mould growth in a humid store. The ingredient definition for dried black soldier fly larvae requires drying to no more than 10 percent moisture, which is a reasonable working target for a dry pet food generally. Measure it. A cheap way is oven drying: weigh a 100 gram sample, dry it at 105 degrees Celsius until the weight stops changing, and the weight lost as a percentage is your moisture. If you cannot hold under about 10 percent, you do not have a shelf-stable dry product, whatever the recipe says.

Second, fat oxidation. Fat goes rancid with heat, light, air and time, and the more unsaturated the fat, the faster it goes. This is a particular problem exactly where you have added value: a food with fish oil for joint support oxidises faster than a plain one. Rancid fat is unpalatable, destroys fat-soluble vitamins, and is the commonest reason a returned bag smells wrong. Controls are an antioxidant in the oil, minimum head space, a light-barrier bag, cool storage, and not making more than you can sell.

Third, vitamin loss. Water-soluble vitamins degrade over time and with heat and moisture, which is why a premix carries an expiry date and why formulating exactly at the minimum is bad practice. If a cat food is formulated at exactly 60 mg per kilogram of niacin, any loss puts it below the requirement before it is eaten. Formulate above the minimum, and remember that cats cannot make up niacin from tryptophan, so a shortfall is a real shortfall.

Now run a shelf-life trial, which any producer can do. Make one batch. Set aside twelve sealed retail packs. Store six in your normal warehouse and six in the hottest place you can find, such as a roof space or a metal container. At 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6 months open one pack from each set and record: smell, colour, insect activity, mould, moisture if you can measure it, and a palatability check with two animals. Where budget allows, send the 0 and 6 month packs for proximate analysis and compare fat. Set your best-before date at the last point where the warm-stored packs were still acceptable, then subtract a safety margin. If the warm packs failed at four months, do not print six.

Storage design costs almost nothing. Pallets or timber off the floor, at least 30 centimetres from walls so air moves and so you can inspect for rodents, no direct sun on stacks, and stacks no higher than they are stable. Run first in, first out strictly: new stock goes behind, old stock in front, and every bag carries a date code. A simple stock card on each pallet with date in, quantity in, quantity out and balance is enough.

Cost the losses, because this is where a small producer quietly bleeds. Suppose you make 2000 kilograms a month at an ingredient cost of USD 0.72 and sell at USD 1.80. If 3 percent goes out of date, is spoiled or is returned, that is 60 kilograms, USD 43 of ingredients and USD 108 of lost revenue every month, USD 1296 a year. Cutting waste to 1 percent releases about USD 860 a year, which is more than the cost of a proper store.

A related discipline is batch sizing. Do not make more than roughly four to six weeks of expected sales of any one product. A producer with three products and a 200 kilogram mixer is better off running three smaller batches a month than one large one per product per quarter, even though the large batch is cheaper per kilogram to make. Fresh stock sells, old stock does not, and the difference in mixing labour is small.

An example from Blantyre, Malawi. A producer printed a twelve month best-before because a competitor's imported bag said twelve months. In the hot season her 5 kilogram bags stored in a shop with a tin roof were being returned at four to five months with a rancid smell. She ran the trial above, found her warm-stored packs failed between four and five months, reprinted the bag with a six month best-before and added an antioxidant to the oil at a cost of about USD 0.004 per kilogram of feed. Returns stopped. A shorter honest date sold more than a long false one, because the shops stopped losing money on her product.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. A food tests 24.9 percent crude protein at 10 percent moisture. What is it on a dry matter basis?

Divide by the dry matter fraction: 24.9 divided by 0.90 is 27.7 percent.

2. The dog adult protein minimum is 18.0 percent DM. What is that as fed at 10 percent moisture?

Multiply by the dry matter fraction: 18.0 x 0.90 = 16.2 percent as fed.

3. A wet cat food shows 9 percent protein as fed at 78 percent moisture. What does that mean?

9 divided by 0.22 is 40.9 percent on a dry matter basis, comfortably above the 26.0 percent cat adult minimum.

4. Which ingredient figure most needs a supplier certificate?

Bone content varies enormously between renderers, and calcium excess can breach the 1.8 percent ceiling for foods that may be fed to large-breed puppies.

5. Two consignments of poultry by-product meal analyse at 58 and 49 percent protein. What is the practical consequence at fixed inclusion?

At the same inclusion the lower-protein, higher-bone meal delivers less protein and much more calcium, which can push the diet past the calcium ceiling.

6. After reserving 7.2 kg of fixed ingredients per 100 kg, what protein must the remaining blend contain to give 25 percent in the finished food?

Oil and minerals contribute no protein, so 25 divided by 0.928 is 26.9 percent for the remaining 92.8 kg.

7. In the square, 26.9 in the middle with 58 and 9 on the left gives how many parts of poultry meal?

26.9 minus 9 is 17.9 parts of poultry meal, and 58 minus 26.9 is 31.1 parts of maize, giving 49.0 total parts.

8. What is the calcium to phosphorus ratio of the finished formulation?

1.320 divided by 0.865 is 1.53 to 1, inside the permitted 1:1 to 2:1 window.

9. What is the metabolisable energy of the worked formulation?

Gross energy 443.5 kcal per 100 g, times 87.2 percent digestibility gives 386.7, minus 1.04 times protein gives 360.7 kcal per 100 g, or 3607 per kilogram.

10. The laboratory reports 23.4 percent protein against a calculated 24.96. What is the most likely cause?

A formulation is only as accurate as its ingredient figures. A poultry meal at 54 rather than 58 percent explains the whole gap.

11. How much zinc oxide at 72 percent zinc is needed per tonne to supply 80 mg of zinc per kilogram of diet?

80 divided by 0.72 is 111 mg per kilogram of diet, which is 111 grams per tonne.

12. At a 1000 IU per kilogram target, how much vitamin D3 at 500,000 IU per gram goes into a tonne?

1000 divided by 500,000 is 0.002 g per kilogram of diet, which is 2 grams per tonne. This is why micro-ingredients are never weighed straight into a batch.

13. Why is three-stage dilution used?

A few grams cannot be distributed through a tonne directly. Diluting into a carrier in stages gives even distribution and an inspection point at each stage.

14. Where do real-world vitamin D poisonings in pet food come from?

Recalls in 2018 and 2019 arose from formulation and premix errors at manufacturers with laboratories and quality systems, not from raw ingredients.

15. For most producers under about 20 tonnes a month, what is the sensible premix decision?

The saving of roughly USD 15 a tonne does not justify carrying the weighing risk, the minimum purchase quantities and the need for a balance reading to 0.01 g.

16. Swapping 1 kg of maize at 9 percent protein for 1 kg of soybean meal at 44 percent in 100 kg changes protein by how much?

The difference is 44 minus 9 = 35 g per 100 kg, which is 0.35 percentage points.

17. Which ingredient delivers protein most cheaply in the worked example?

0.21 of extra cost divided by 0.35 of extra protein is USD 0.60 per percentage point per 100 kilograms.

18. After swapping poultry meal for soy at constant protein, what else must be corrected?

Poultry meal carries 12 percent fat and soy only 1.5 percent, so fat must be restored with oil, which eats into the saving.

19. Why does an unconstrained least-cost solution produce a bad food?

The model only respects the constraints you write. Minima, maxima, amino acids, inclusion ceilings and species rules must all be entered.

20. A solver returned 31 percent soybean meal and stool scores worsened. What is the correct fix?

Inclusion ceilings encode evidence the price column does not contain. Here the constraint was worth more than the saving it cost.

21. How should a batch sample be taken?

A single point sample does not represent the batch. Ten points combined and quartered down gives a representative 500 g, split into a laboratory sample and a retained sample.

22. What does a mixer uniformity test measure?

Ten samples are analysed for the tracer and the standard deviation divided by the mean gives the coefficient of variation, with under 10 percent taken as acceptable by industry convention.

23. Which laboratory test comes first on a limited budget?

Moisture, protein, fat, fibre and ash tell you whether the food is what you say it is, for USD 60 to 140. Amino acids and vitamins are bought only when a protein source or premix changes.

24. Why keep a retained sample of every batch?

The retained sample plus the batch record is what lets you confirm or refute a complaint and, if necessary, withdraw only the affected batch.

25. What made recall possible for the large manufacturers that had vitamin D errors?

Those firms had laboratories and quality managers and still got it wrong. Traceability is what limited the damage, and a batch code plus a record sheet gives a small producer the same protection.

26. Which three processes end a pet food's shelf life?

Each needs a different control: drying and packaging for moisture, antioxidants and light barriers for fat, and formulating above the minimum for vitamins.

27. How do you measure moisture without laboratory equipment?

Oven drying to constant weight is a simple and reliable method that any producer with a scale and an oven can run.

28. Why should a fish-oil enriched food have a shorter shelf life than a plain one?

The more unsaturated the fat, the faster it goes rancid, which is why exactly the products you add value to need tighter storage and dating.

29. How should a best-before date be set?

Shelf life is a claim about your product in your climate and packaging. The accelerated warm-storage arm of the trial is what makes the date defensible.

30. At 2000 kg a month, ingredient cost USD 0.72 and selling price USD 1.80, what does 3 percent waste cost in lost revenue a year?

3 percent of 2000 kg is 60 kg a month, which at USD 1.80 is USD 108 a month, or USD 1296 a year.
