1. Module 9 Lesson 1: Foundations of Business Translation for African Markets

Welcome to Module 9 of the Business Chinese, Arabic and Russian Translation course. This module is designed for translators, trade officers, export managers, import agents, logistics coordinators and entrepreneurs who work between African markets and Chinese, Arabic speaking and Russian speaking business partners. The focus is practical. You will learn how to translate commercial documents, negotiate meaning across cultures and avoid costly errors in contracts, invoices, shipping papers and correspondence. Africa is not a single market. It is a collection of more than fifty economies with different legal systems, currencies, languages and business customs. When you translate for a Nigerian importer dealing with a Guangzhou supplier, a Kenyan logistics firm handling Russian wheat shipments, or a Moroccan distributor negotiating with a Dubai trading house, you must understand both the source language and the local business reality. Business translation is not literary translation. It prioritizes accuracy, clarity, consistency and legal safety. A single mistranslated payment term can delay a container for weeks. A wrong Incoterm can shift insurance liability and cost thousands of dollars. A poorly rendered company name can cause customs rejection. In this lesson we build the foundation. First, understand the three target languages in an African context. Chinese is dominated by Mandarin in business, with significant written conventions in Simplified Chinese. Arabic spans Modern Standard Arabic for documents and many regional dialects for spoken negotiation. Russian is used across Russia, Central Asia and parts of Eastern Europe, and it remains important for fertilizer, wheat, energy, machinery and arms related trade with Africa. Second, understand the document types you will translate. These include proforma invoices, commercial invoices, packing lists, bills of lading, certificates of origin, letters of credit, sales contracts, purchase orders, agency agreements, joint venture memoranda, product labels, safety data sheets, tender documents, bank guarantees and email correspondence. Each has its own register, formatting rules and terminology. Third, understand the workflow. A professional translator receives the source file, confirms the purpose and audience, checks terminology, translates, edits, proofreads, formats and delivers. For business documents, you should also verify numbers, dates, currencies, units of measure, names, addresses and registration numbers. In Africa, common challenges include inconsistent address formats, multiple official languages, handwritten documents, poor scans and mixed English, French, Portuguese and Arabic content. You must be comfortable asking clarifying questions. Never guess a figure. Never invent a company name. Never change a legal term without flagging it. Fourth, build a terminology base. Start a glossary for each client and each product line. Include the source term, the target term, the part of speech, the context, the approved translation and the date. For Chinese, pay attention to measure words, compound nouns and official titles. For Arabic, pay attention to gender, number, case endings in formal documents and the difference between written and spoken forms. For Russian, pay attention to case government, aspect of verbs and the six case system. Fifth, learn the business cultures. Chinese business communication often values relationship building, indirect disagreement and respect for hierarchy. Arabic business communication often values personal trust, hospitality and relationship before transaction. Russian business communication often values directness, technical detail and formal written confirmation. None of these are absolute, but they shape how you translate tone. A polite Chinese refusal may need to become a clear but diplomatic English or French refusal for an African client. A Russian technical specification may need to be simplified for a West African procurement officer. An Arabic greeting may need to be preserved in correspondence but condensed in a formal invoice. Sixth, practice with real African trade scenarios. Scenario one: a Ghanaian cocoa exporter receives a Chinese purchase contract. Translate the quality clause, payment terms and arbitration clause. Scenario two: an Egyptian importer buys Russian sunflower oil. Translate the certificate of analysis and the shipping instructions. Scenario three: a South African machinery dealer negotiates with a Saudi distributor. Translate the agency agreement and the commission schedule. In each case, identify the parties, the goods, the quantity, the price, the currency, the delivery term, the payment method, the inspection regime, the dispute resolution forum and the governing law. These are the backbone of every commercial contract. Finally, adopt professional habits. Use translation memory tools where possible. Keep version control. Protect confidential data. Disclose your limitations. Charge for your expertise, not just your words. In Africa, the demand for Chinese, Arabic and Russian business translation is growing because trade is growing. Your value lies in reducing risk and enabling deals. In the next lesson we will go deeper into contract and legal terminology. Prepare by reviewing the Incoterms 2020, the UCP 600 for letters of credit and the standard United Nations Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods. Bring a real document from your own work if you have one. Remember: in business translation, precision is profit.

2. Module 9 Lesson 2: Translating Contracts and Legal Documents for African Trade

This lesson focuses on contracts and legal documents, the highest risk area in business translation. In African trade with Chinese, Arabic speaking and Russian speaking partners, contracts are the main tool for allocating risk. A translation error in a contract can invalidate a clause, trigger a dispute or cause a court to interpret the agreement differently from what the parties intended. Your job is to produce a target text that is legally equivalent, not just linguistically equivalent. Start with contract structure. Most international sales contracts contain a preamble, definitions, goods description, quantity, quality, price, delivery terms, payment terms, packaging, marking, inspection, force majeure, termination, confidentiality, intellectual property, governing law, dispute resolution, notices and signatures. Some contracts add warranties, indemnities, limitation of liability, assignment, entire agreement and counterparts clauses. When you translate, preserve the numbering, cross references and defined terms. If the source defines the Buyer in capital letters, your target must also mark the defined term consistently. In Chinese contracts, defined terms may appear as quoted phrases or as repeated nouns. In Arabic contracts, defined terms may be introduced with a specific article and repeated with the definite article. In Russian contracts, defined terms are often capitalized or placed in quotes. Consistency is more important than elegance. Next, master key legal terminology. Force majeure becomes bu ke kang li in Chinese, al quwwa al qahira in Arabic and fors mazhor in Russian. Governing law becomes guan xian fa, al qanun al hukm and primenimoe pravo. Arbitration becomes zhong cai, tahkim and arbitrazh. Without prejudice becomes wu sun yu quan li, ... and bez ushcherba dlya prav. Letter of credit becomes xin yong zheng, a'timad mustanadi and akkreditiv. Bill of lading becomes ti dan, sanad shahn and konosament. These are not optional. They are standard terms that courts and banks expect. Build a bilingual or trilingual glossary for each contract type. Then study the legal systems. Chinese law is civil law with a socialist legal tradition. Arabic legal systems vary. Some countries apply civil law codes, some apply Islamic law in personal and commercial matters, and some have mixed systems. Russian law is civil law with a strong statutory tradition. African legal systems are equally varied. Common law in Ghana, Nigeria, Kenya and South Africa. Civil law in Senegal, Cote d'Ivoire, Morocco and Egypt. Mixed systems in Cameroon, Mauritius and Seychelles. When you translate a governing law clause, do not translate the name of the law. Keep the official name and add the jurisdiction. For example, the laws of the People's Republic of China, the laws of the Russian Federation, the laws of the Arab Republic of Egypt, the laws of the Republic of Kenya. If the contract says the laws of England and Wales, keep that exact phrase. Now consider payment terms. Letters of credit are common in African trade because they reduce risk. Translate the L/C terms with extreme care. Sight payment, deferred payment, acceptance, negotiation, transferable, irrevocable, confirmed, unconfirmed, revolving, standby. Each has a precise meaning. The Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits, UCP 600, uses specific terms. If the source says latest shipment date, do not translate it as delivery date. If the source says partial shipment allowed, do not translate it as partial delivery permitted. Banks reject documents for small discrepancies. Your translation may be attached to the banking file, so accuracy is critical. Next, handle numbers, dates and currencies. In Chinese, numbers may be written in Arabic numerals or Chinese characters. In formal contracts, both may appear. In Arabic, numerals may be Eastern Arabic or Western Arabic depending on the country. In Russian, numbers use a comma as the decimal separator and a space as the thousands separator. Dates may be written as day month year, month day year or year month day. Always convert to the format used in the target jurisdiction and add the month name in words if there is any risk of confusion. Currencies must be identified by ISO code. USD, EUR, CNY, RUB, AED, SAR, ZAR, NGN, KES, EGP, MAD. Do not translate currency names in a way that creates ambiguity. Then work on dispute resolution. Arbitration is preferred in cross border trade. Common forums include CIETAC in China, the Hong Kong International Arbitration Centre, the Dubai International Arbitration Centre, the Cairo Regional Centre for International Commercial Arbitration, the Russian Arbitration Center and the Arbitration Foundation of Southern Africa. When translating an arbitration clause, preserve the seat, the language, the number of arbitrators, the rules and the appointing authority. A change in any of these can change the outcome. Also translate notices clauses carefully. Addresses, emails, courier details and deemed receipt rules must be exact. In Africa, address formats vary widely. A street address in Lagos may include landmarks. A postal address in Nairobi may use a box number. A rural address in Ethiopia may use a kebele. Do not normalize these. Preserve them and add a note if needed. Finally, practice with a real contract. Take a sales contract between a Chinese supplier and a Nigerian buyer. Translate the quality clause, the inspection clause, the payment clause and the arbitration clause. Then take a Russian language supply contract for fertilizer to Kenya and translate the delivery and force majeure clauses. Then take an Arabic language agency agreement from the UAE to Morocco and translate the commission and termination clauses. Compare your work with a partner. Check every number, every defined term and every cross reference. Remember: in legal translation, a small error can become a large liability. Your precision protects your client, your reputation and the deal.

3. Module 9 Lesson 3: Commercial Correspondence and Negotiation Across Chinese, Arabic and Russian

This lesson covers commercial correspondence and negotiation. In African trade, most deals begin with email, WhatsApp, WeChat, Telegram or a phone call. Your ability to translate and interpret messages accurately and diplomatically can determine whether a deal closes. Business correspondence is not just words. It is tone, timing, format and relationship. Start with email structure. Chinese business emails often begin with a polite greeting, refer to the company or the previous contact, state the purpose, provide details and end with a courteous close. Arabic business emails often begin with a formal greeting, sometimes religious, then a personal inquiry about health or business, then the purpose. Russian business emails often begin with a formal salutation using the recipient's name and patronymic, then a direct statement of purpose, then details and a formal close. When you translate, preserve the level of formality but adapt to the target reader. An African importer may prefer a direct English or French email. A Chinese supplier may expect a formal Chinese email. An Arab distributor may expect a warm Arabic email. A Russian manufacturer may expect a precise Russian email. Next, master common phrases. In Chinese, xie xie for thank you, qing for please, guan yu for regarding, fu jian for attachment, qing hui fu for please reply, he tong for contract, bao jia for quotation, fa piao for invoice, zhuang yun for shipment, bao xian for insurance, fu kuan for payment. In Arabic, shukran for thank you, min fadlik for please, bi sha'n for regarding, al murafaq for attachment, arju al rad for please reply, al aqd for contract, al ardh for quotation, al fatura for invoice, al shahn for shipment, al ta'min for insurance, al daf' for payment. In Russian, spasibo for thank you, pozhaluysta for please, kasatelno for regarding, prilozhenie for attachment, proshu otvetit for please reply, dogovor for contract, kommercheskoe predlozhenie for quotation, schet faktura for invoice, otgruzka for shipment, strakhovanie for insurance, platezh for payment. Use these in context. Do not translate word for word if the result sounds unnatural. Translate meaning and intent. Then learn negotiation moves. Opening, anchoring, concession, condition, deadline, walk away, agreement. In Chinese negotiation, relationships and face matter. A direct no may be softened. A maybe may mean no. Silence may be a tactic. In Arabic negotiation, personal trust and hospitality matter. Price may be discussed after relationship. A written offer may be followed by verbal adjustment. In Russian negotiation, technical detail and formal authority matter. Decisions may require approval from Moscow or a regional office. Written confirmation is essential. When you interpret or translate in a negotiation, do not add your own opinion. Do not soften a demand unless asked. Do not harden a concession. Your role is to convey meaning accurately and to alert your client to cultural signals. If you are also a business advisor, separate the two roles clearly. Next, handle specific message types. Inquiries, quotations, counter offers, order confirmations, shipping notices, payment reminders, complaints, claims and apologies. Each has a standard structure. An inquiry states the product, quantity, specifications, destination and requested terms. A quotation states the price, currency, validity, Incoterm, payment method, lead time and minimum order. A counter offer states the proposed change and the reason. An order confirmation repeats the agreed terms and asks for deposit or L/C. A shipping notice states the vessel, container number, bill of lading number, departure date, arrival date and documents sent. A payment reminder states the invoice number, amount, due date and requested action. A complaint states the problem, the evidence, the impact and the requested remedy. A claim states the loss, the legal basis and the amount claimed. An apology acknowledges the problem, explains the cause, offers a remedy and states preventive action. Translate these with precision and courtesy. Then consider channels. Email is formal and creates a record. WhatsApp is fast and informal but can be used for business in Africa, the Middle East and parts of Asia. WeChat is essential for Chinese business. Telegram is common in Russia, Central Asia and some African markets. Voice notes are common in Africa and the Middle East. When you translate a voice note, summarize the key points and confirm the action items. When you translate a chat, preserve the sequence and the speaker labels. Do not edit out repetition if it carries emphasis. Finally, practice with role plays. Scenario one: a Kenyan tea exporter negotiates with a Chinese buyer on price and packaging. Scenario two: a Nigerian construction company negotiates with a Russian steel supplier on delivery and payment. Scenario three: a Moroccan cosmetics importer negotiates with an Emirati distributor on exclusivity and marketing. In each scenario, write the inquiry, the quotation, the counter offer and the confirmation in the target language. Then exchange with a partner and translate back. Check for tone, clarity and completeness. Remember: in commercial correspondence, the goal is not to impress with vocabulary. The goal is to be understood, to build trust and to move the deal forward. Clear, polite and precise messages win business. Ambiguous, aggressive or careless messages lose it.

4. Module 9 Lesson 4: Technical, Logistics and Financial Translation for African Supply Chains

This lesson covers technical, logistics and financial translation. These are the operational documents that keep goods moving from factory to port to warehouse to customer. In African supply chains, delays and disputes often come from documentation errors. Your translation must be accurate, consistent and complete. Start with technical documents. Product specifications, technical data sheets, safety data sheets, operating manuals, maintenance schedules, quality certificates, test reports and standards. Chinese technical documents often use compact noun phrases and may omit subjects. Arabic technical documents may use long sentences and passive constructions. Russian technical documents often use standardized formats, abbreviations and GOST or TU references. When you translate, preserve the standard numbers, units, tolerances and test methods. Do not convert units unless the client asks. If you must convert, show the original in parentheses. Pay attention to chemical names, electrical ratings, pressure ratings, temperature ranges and material grades. A wrong grade can cause equipment failure or a safety incident. Then move to logistics documents. Proforma invoice, commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading, sea waybill, air waybill, road consignment note, rail consignment note, certificate of origin, phytosanitary certificate, fumigation certificate, insurance certificate, inspection certificate, delivery note, goods receipt note and customs declaration. Each has a specific function. The proforma invoice is used for quotation and import permit. The commercial invoice is used for customs valuation and payment. The packing list is used for cargo handling and inspection. The bill of lading is a document of title and a contract of carriage. The certificate of origin determines tariff treatment. The phytosanitary certificate confirms plant health. The fumigation certificate confirms pest control. The insurance certificate confirms cover. The inspection certificate confirms quality or quantity. The customs declaration triggers duty and tax. Translate these documents with exact matching of names, addresses, numbers, weights, volumes, package counts and marks. In Africa, customs authorities are strict. A mismatch between the invoice and the bill of lading can cause seizure, fines or demurrage. Then study Incoterms. EXW, FCA, FAS, FOB, CFR, CIF, CPT, CIP, DAP, DPU, DDP. Each defines the point of delivery, the transfer of risk and the allocation of costs. Translate the Incoterm and the named place exactly. Do not translate FOB Shanghai as free on board Shanghai port if the source says FOB Shanghai. Do not translate CIF Mombasa as cost insurance freight Mombasa if the source says CIF Mombasa. The three letter code plus the place is the standard. Add an explanation only if the client asks. Next, financial documents. Letters of credit, bank guarantees, documentary collections, bills of exchange, promissory notes, payment receipts, bank statements, SWIFT messages, remittance advices and foreign exchange permits. These documents use formal, repetitive language. Translate them with the same repetition. Do not vary terms for style. In letters of credit, every word matters. The applicant, beneficiary, issuing bank, advising bank, negotiating bank, amount, currency, expiry date, latest shipment date, presentation period, description of goods, documents required, additional conditions and charges. A single wrong word can cause a discrepancy. In African trade, common L/C issues include late shipment, late presentation, inconsistent description, missing documents and incorrect amounts. Your translation should help the client see these risks before the bank does. Then consider financial terminology. In Chinese, xin yong zheng for letter of credit, bao han for bank guarantee, hui piao for bill of exchange, tuo shou for documentary collection, jie hui for settlement, hui lv for exchange rate, shou yi ren for beneficiary, kai zheng hang for issuing bank, tong zhi hang for advising bank. In Arabic, a'timad mustanadi for letter of credit, khatab dhaman for bank guarantee, kambiyala for bill of exchange, tahsil mustanadi for documentary collection, tasfiya for settlement, si'r al sarf for exchange rate, al mustafid for beneficiary, al bank al musdir for issuing bank, al bank al mukhbir for advising bank. In Russian, akkreditiv for letter of credit, bankovskaya garantiya for bank guarantee, perevodnoy veksel for bill of exchange, dokumentarnoe inkasso for documentary collection, raschety for settlement, obmennyy kurs for exchange rate, beneficiary for beneficiary, bank emitter for issuing bank, avizuyushchiy bank for advising bank. Build these into your glossary and use them consistently. Then practice with a full document set. Take a shipment of solar panels from China to Senegal. Translate the proforma invoice, the commercial invoice, the packing list, the bill of lading and the certificate of origin. Check the names, addresses, HS codes, quantities, weights, values and Incoterm. Then take a shipment of wheat from Russia to Egypt. Translate the phytosanitary certificate, the quality certificate, the bill of lading and the insurance certificate. Check the moisture, protein, test weight, foreign matter and radiation levels. Then take a shipment of dates from Saudi Arabia to Nigeria. Translate the halal certificate, the health certificate, the commercial invoice and the packing list. Check the production date, expiry date, batch number and storage conditions. Finally, adopt quality control habits. Use checklists. Verify numbers twice. Cross check names against the passport or registration certificate. Keep a copy of every source and target document. Date and initial your work. If you find an error in the source, report it. If you find an error in your own work, correct it immediately and inform the client. In supply chain translation, speed matters but accuracy matters more. A fast wrong document is worse than a slow right one. Remember: your translation is part of the cargo. If it is wrong, the cargo stops. If it is right, the cargo moves. Your precision keeps African trade flowing.

5. Module 9 Lesson 5: Professional Practice, Quality Assurance and Business Development

This final lesson in Module 9 focuses on professional practice, quality assurance and business development. You have learned the foundations, contracts, correspondence, technical and financial translation. Now you must build a sustainable practice. That means setting standards, managing clients, pricing your work, protecting yourself and growing your expertise. Start with professional standards. A good business translator is accurate, consistent, confidential, punctual and transparent. Accuracy means the target text conveys the same meaning as the source. Consistency means the same term is translated the same way throughout a project and across projects for the same client. Confidentiality means you do not share contracts, prices, customer lists or trade secrets. Punctuality means you meet deadlines or communicate early if you cannot. Transparency means you explain your choices, your limitations and your fees. These standards are your reputation. In African markets, reputation travels fast. A single bad job can close many doors. A single excellent job can open many. Next, build a quality assurance process. Step one: preparation. Read the source document. Identify the purpose, audience, register, domain and deadline. Ask clarifying questions. Gather reference materials. Step two: terminology. Build or update the glossary. Check official translations of company names, product names, laws and standards. Step three: translation. Translate in segments. Keep the formatting. Mark uncertain terms. Step four: self review. Compare source and target. Check numbers, dates, names, units and cross references. Step five: peer review. Ask a colleague to review. Use a second pair of eyes for contracts, financial documents and technical specifications. Step six: proofreading. Check spelling, grammar, punctuation and style. Step seven: formatting. Match the original layout. Check tables, stamps, signatures and seals. Step eight: delivery. Send the final file with a note on any issues. Keep a copy. This process reduces errors and increases client confidence. Then consider tools. Translation memory, terminology management, spell check, grammar check, PDF conversion, OCR and secure file transfer. For Chinese, Arabic and Russian, you need fonts and keyboard layouts. You need to handle right to left text for Arabic. You need to handle Cyrillic for Russian. You need to handle Simplified and Traditional Chinese. Test your tools before a deadline. Do not rely on machine translation for legal, financial or technical documents without full human review. Machine translation can help with gist and speed but it can also produce dangerous errors. Use it as a draft, not as a final product. Next, pricing. Business translation is priced per word, per page, per hour or per project. In African markets, rates vary widely. Chinese, Arabic and Russian are often priced higher than European languages because there are fewer qualified translators. Do not compete on price alone. Compete on quality, speed, reliability and specialization. Charge extra for urgent work, certified translation, notarization, interpretation, desktop publishing and project management. Ask for a deposit for new clients. Invoice clearly. State the scope, the deadline, the revision policy and the payment terms. Follow up on late payments professionally. Then think about specialization. General translators are easy to find. Specialized translators are valuable. Choose one or two areas. Mining and minerals. Oil and gas. Agriculture and agro processing. Construction and infrastructure. Pharmaceuticals and healthcare. Information technology and telecommunications. Automotive and machinery. Textiles and apparel. Logistics and shipping. Finance and banking. Energy and renewables. Education and training. Government and public procurement. Within each area, learn the products, the processes, the regulations and the key players. Read trade publications. Attend trade fairs. Join professional associations. Follow the African Continental Free Trade Area and its implications for documentation, rules of origin and tariff schedules. Then build your business development plan. Define your services. Define your target clients. Define your value proposition. Create a professional profile in English, French, Chinese, Arabic and Russian if possible. List your education, experience, languages, specializations, software and certifications. Collect references and testimonials. Network with chambers of commerce, trade promotion agencies, embassies, banks, freight forwarders, customs brokers, law firms and business associations. Offer a free sample or a short consultation to new clients. Ask for feedback. Ask for referrals. Keep in touch with past clients. Share useful information, such as changes in customs rules, new Incoterms or currency regulations. Position yourself as a partner, not just a vendor. Finally, plan for growth. You can grow by adding languages, adding specializations, adding team members, adding services such as interpretation, localization, subtitling, transcreation and cultural consulting. You can also grow by training others. Africa needs more qualified business translators in Chinese, Arabic and Russian. You can mentor junior translators, teach short courses or develop glossaries for your industry. Remember: this module is not the end. It is a foundation. Practice every day. Read contracts, invoices, shipping documents and correspondence. Compare translations. Learn from mistakes. Ask questions. Keep learning. The demand for Chinese, Arabic and Russian business translation in Africa will continue to grow as trade, investment and cooperation grow. Your skills can help African businesses access new markets, negotiate better terms and reduce risk. Your work matters. Do it with precision, integrity and pride. Thank you for completing Module 9. In the next module we will apply these skills to a full simulated trade transaction from inquiry to delivery.

<h3>Translation Workflow for Live Deals</h3>

<p>Follow this sequence for every cross-border agribusiness transaction. It reduces the risk of a mistranslated clause becoming a payment dispute.</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Pre-negotiation glossary lock.</strong> Agree on the commercial terms and their translations before the first call. Circulate a bilingual glossary to both sides.</li>
<li><strong>Dual-language contract.</strong> Insist on a contract in both languages, with a governing-language clause stating which version prevails in dispute.</li>
<li><strong>Certified translation of certificates.</strong> Phytosanitary, health, and halal certificates must be translated by an accredited translator and, where required, notarised.</li>
<li><strong>Back-translation check.</strong> For high-value clauses (payment, penalty, force majeure), have a second translator render the translation back into English and compare.</li>
<li><strong>Customs description alignment.</strong> Ensure the commercial invoice description matches the HS code description in the destination language exactly.</li>
<li><strong>Archive everything.</strong> Keep signed PDFs, translator credentials, and revision history for at least five years.</li>
</ol>

<div style="border:2px solid #c62828; background:#ffebee; padding:16px; border-radius:8px; margin:20px 0;">
<p style="margin:0; color:#b71c1c; font-weight:bold;"> SAFETY WARNING Food, Feed and Chemical Documentation</p>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#333;">A single mistranslated word on a phytosanitary certificate, veterinary health certificate, or pesticide label can cause rejection of an entire shipment, quarantine of a consignment, or a public health incident. Never rely on machine translation for:</p>
<ul style="margin:8px 0 0 18px; color:#333;">
<li>Active ingredient names and concentrations on pesticide or veterinary product labels.</li>
<li>Withdrawal periods for veterinary drugs in livestock destined for halal or export markets.</li>
<li>Maximum Residue Limit (MRL) statements and laboratory test result units (ppm, ppb, mg/kg).</li>
<li>Halal slaughter and certification wording errors can invalidate religious compliance.</li>
<li>Allergen declarations and expiry dates on processed food.</li>
</ul>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#333;">Always engage a certified translator with domain expertise and have the final document verified by the importing country's competent authority (e.g. GCC Standardization Organization, Rosselkhoznadzor, GACC).</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul>
<li><strong>Governing language clause signed?</strong> Confirm which language version prevails in a dispute before signing.</li>
<li><strong>Glossary locked and circulated?</strong> Both parties must agree on key terms (FOB, CIF, LC, force majeure) in all three languages.</li>
<li><strong>Certificates certified?</strong> Phytosanitary, health, and halal documents translated by an accredited translator and notarised where required.</li>
<li><strong>Units and weights verified?</strong> Check tonne vs. metric tonne vs. long tonne, and confirm currency and payment terms.</li>
<li><strong>HS code description matched?</strong> Commercial invoice description must align with the destination-language customs description.</li>
<li><strong>Back-translation done for high-value clauses?</strong> Payment, penalty, and force majeure clauses double-checked by a second translator.</li>
<li><strong>Safety-critical terms human-verified?</strong> No machine translation for pesticides, veterinary drugs, MRLs, allergens, or halal wording.</li>
<li><strong>Archive complete?</strong> Signed PDFs, translator credentials, and revision history retained for at least five years.</li>
</ul>
