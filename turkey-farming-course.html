Turkey Farming

A complete 6-month program — brooding, growth-phase feeding, disease prevention (including the diseases that hit turkeys harder than chickens), and finishing birds for the festive-season market where turkey commands its highest price of the year.

Course Overview

Why turkey? Turkey is a slower, more capital-intensive bird than a broiler chicken — a full grow-out takes 5–6 months versus 5–8 weeks — but it commands a strong price premium, especially around festive/holiday seasons when demand spikes sharply above the rest of the year. A farmer who times production to land finished birds at that peak captures a price broiler chicken never reaches.

Structure: 6 monthly modules → lessons → practical assignments → a 10-question quiz each module → final business project.

How to study (hybrid): Online — modules on your phone, sessions in the Study Diary, questions to the rAs AI assistant. Offline — save this page (Print → Save as PDF); the practicals happen at the brooder and the feed trough.

⚠️ Read first: the three rules that protect your investment

  • Poult (baby turkey) mortality in the first 2 weeks is the single biggest risk in turkey farming. Poults are more fragile than chicks at the same age — correct brooder temperature and prompt feed/water access in the first 48 hours make the difference between a strong flock and heavy early losses.
  • Blackhead disease (histomoniasis) is far more dangerous to turkeys than to chickens. Never house or range turkeys and chickens together, or on ground previously used by chickens, without a long rest period — this single mixing mistake causes more turkey flock losses worldwide than almost any other single cause.
  • Turkeys are more prone to leg and joint problems than broilers if grown too fast on too rich a diet without adequate exercise space. Match feed phase to age correctly and don't overcrowd — a bird that goes lame close to market weight is a total loss on a high-value animal.
MONTH 1 — Breeds, Housing & Brooding ▾

Lesson 1.1 — Turkey breeds and production types

TypeNotes
Broad Breasted White/BronzeCommercial fast-growing types bred for meat yield; cannot mate naturally at full size in some strains and rely on artificial insemination in intensive systems — a consideration for anyone planning to breed rather than just grow out purchased poults
Heritage/traditional breeds (e.g. Bourbon Red, Narragansett types and local African turkey stock)Slower growing, hardier, able to mate naturally and forage more actively — often the practical smallholder choice where poult supply for commercial hybrids is unreliable

Lesson 1.2 — Housing needs

  • Turkeys need considerably more floor space per bird than broiler chickens as they mature — overcrowding is a direct cause of leg problems, feather pecking and stress-related disease susceptibility in a bird that's kept far longer than a broiler.
  • Provide good ventilation without draughts, and dry litter (wood shavings or similar) kept topped up and turned — damp litter is a major driver of respiratory disease and coccidiosis in turkeys.
  • Perches, if used, should be low and sturdy for heavier commercial-type birds — falls from high perches can cause leg injury in birds already carrying significant body weight.

Lesson 1.3 — Brooding poults (0–2 weeks)

  • Start brooder temperature at around 35–37°C at poult level in week 1, reducing by about 2.5–3°C per week thereafter until reaching ambient temperature by around week 6 — turkey poults are notably more sensitive to chilling in the first days than chicken chicks.
  • Ensure every poult finds feed and water within the first 24–48 hours — placing bright objects (marbles, coloured stones) in the water and using a shallow, wide feeder helps poults, which can be slower than chicks to learn to eat and drink independently.
  • Watch for piling (poults crowding together in a corner) — a classic sign the brooder is too cold; spread heat sources or adjust brooder guard placement immediately if seen.

Lesson 1.4 — Never mix turkeys with chickens

  • Chickens commonly carry Heterakis gallinarum (a caecal worm) that itself carries the blackhead disease parasite (Histomonas meleagridis) — chickens usually tolerate this combination with few symptoms, but it is frequently fatal in turkeys.
  • Never house turkeys on ground where chickens have recently ranged, and never mix species in the same pen or run — this single practice prevents the majority of blackhead disease outbreaks in smallholder turkey flocks.
  • If land must be shared over time, allow a long rest period (commonly a full season or more, and tillage/cultivation helps break the parasite's egg survival in soil) between chicken and turkey use.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 1): (1) Choose your breed/type based on your poult supply access and breeding plans. (2) Design housing with adequate floor space per bird and confirm ventilation and litter plan. (3) Set up and pre-warm a brooder before poult arrival, with wide shallow feeders/waterers.

MONTH 2 — Growth-Phase Feeding ▾

Lesson 2.1 — Why turkeys need higher protein than chickens

  • Turkeys are larger-framed birds with a longer growth period and greater lean muscle deposition potential than chickens — their protein requirement at every growth phase runs meaningfully higher than the equivalent chicken feed phase.
  • Feeding chicken feed to turkeys (a common smallholder shortcut where turkey-specific feed isn't stocked locally) under-supplies protein and slows growth — turkey feed can be formulated from the same base ingredients as poultry feed but with a higher protein inclusion.
  • Amino acid balance (lysine and methionine particularly) matters as much as raw CP% for turkeys' rapid frame and muscle development, just as it does for broilers.

Lesson 2.2 — Phase feeding by age

PhaseAgeTarget CP%
Starter0–4 weeks26–28%
Grower 14–8 weeks22–24%
Grower 28–16 weeks18–20%
Finisher16 weeks to slaughter14–16%

Protein need steadily drops while energy density rises through the phases, as the bird shifts from frame-building toward muscle and fat finishing in the final weeks before market.

Lesson 2.3 — Feeding for the finishing period

  • The finishing phase (typically the final 4–6 weeks before slaughter, timed to land on the target market date) uses a higher-energy, lower-protein ration to build the finished weight and fat cover buyers expect in a market bird.
  • Transition gradually between phases (blend old and new feed over several days) rather than switching abruptly — an abrupt change at any stage risks digestive upset in a bird whose gut is handling large daily feed volumes by this point.
  • Monitor feed conversion (feed consumed per kg of weight gained) through the finishing period — it naturally worsens as the bird approaches mature size, and tracking it tells you when further feeding gives diminishing returns relative to market timing.

Lesson 2.4 — Water and feeding equipment for growing turkeys

  • Provide ample, clean water at all times — turkeys drink proportionally more than chickens relative to body weight, especially in hot weather, and water shortage quickly slows growth.
  • Scale feeder and waterer height and capacity up as birds grow — equipment sized for poults becomes inadequate within weeks given how fast turkeys gain weight, and undersized equipment causes feed wastage and uneven access.
  • Keep feed fresh and free from mould — turkeys, like most poultry, are highly susceptible to aflatoxin from spoiled grain, with growth suppression and liver damage as consequences.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 2): (1) Formulate or source a starter ration hitting the 26–28% CP target. (2) Build a phase-feeding calendar for your flock from starter through finisher, timed to a target market date. (3) Track feed conversion for 4 weeks and record the trend.

MONTH 3 — Health & Disease Prevention ▾

Lesson 3.1 — Blackhead disease in depth

  • Signs include listlessness, drooping wings, sulphur-yellow diarrhoea, and in advanced cases a darkened head (giving the disease its name) — mortality in an unprotected turkey flock can be severe once established.
  • Prevention rests almost entirely on management: strict separation from chickens and chicken-used ground, good litter hygiene, and controlling earthworms (which can carry the Heterakis worm eggs and are eaten by free-ranging turkeys) where free-range systems are used.
  • Once an outbreak occurs, affected birds have poor recovery odds — this is a disease where prevention is overwhelmingly the effective strategy, not treatment after the fact.

Lesson 3.2 — Respiratory and other common diseases

  • Turkeys are susceptible to many of the same respiratory diseases as chickens (infectious bronchitis-type illnesses, mycoplasma infections) — good ventilation, dry litter and avoiding overcrowding reduce risk substantially.
  • Coccidiosis (an intestinal parasite disease) thrives in damp, dirty litter conditions — a clean, dry litter management routine is the primary prevention tool, alongside coccidiostat inclusion in starter feed where used.
  • Follow a vaccination schedule appropriate to your region's disease pressure, coordinated with a local veterinary/extension service — required vaccines vary significantly by country and local disease history.

Lesson 3.3 — Leg and joint problems

  • Rapid growth on a rich diet combined with inadequate space or exercise is the leading cause of leg weakness and lameness in growing turkeys — a bird that goes down close to market weight represents a near-total loss of the investment made in it.
  • Ensure adequate floor space, encourage natural movement where the system allows, and avoid pushing growth rate beyond what the phase-appropriate ration and the bird's frame can support.
  • Monitor gait regularly during routine flock checks — early, mild lameness caught and addressed (adjusting stocking density, checking for injury) is far more manageable than lameness discovered only once a bird is already down.

Lesson 3.4 — Biosecurity basics

  • Limit visitor access, disinfect footwear moving between different bird groups or species, and quarantine any new stock before introduction to the existing flock.
  • Control wild bird access to feed and water — wild birds can introduce disease, and open feed/water is a direct route into a flock's health.
  • Remove dead birds promptly and investigate cause where a pattern emerges — a single unexplained death is a data point; several in a short period is an active health event requiring prompt attention.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 3): (1) Confirm your turkey housing/ranging area has had no recent chicken contact, and document your separation plan. (2) Set up a litter cleaning schedule and follow it for 3 weeks. (3) Do a weekly gait/health check on your flock and log any concerns.

MONTH 4 — Breeding & Hatching (Optional Track) ▾

Lesson 4.1 — Natural breeding vs purchased poults

  • Heritage/traditional breeds can mate and reproduce naturally on-farm, letting a farmer build a self-sustaining flock rather than repeatedly purchasing poults — a meaningful cost and reliability advantage where poult supply chains are inconsistent.
  • Commercial Broad Breasted types often cannot mate naturally at full commercial size, meaning most smallholders growing these types will continue purchasing poults or day-old stock rather than breeding their own.
  • Decide your breeding strategy early, since it affects which breed/type you select in Month 1 — this module is most relevant to those raising heritage or traditional stock.

Lesson 4.2 — Selecting breeders

  • Select breeding stock for good body conformation, healthy weight for age, and freedom from any history of leg problems or illness — traits with a heritable component are worth selecting for across generations.
  • Toms (males) are typically kept at a ratio of about 1 tom to 8–10 hens for natural mating — overcrowding toms together causes fighting and reduces mating efficiency.
  • Maintain breeding stock on a moderate-energy ration rather than a growth/finisher ration — excess body fat reduces fertility in both sexes.

Lesson 4.3 — Egg collection and incubation

  • Collect eggs at least twice daily, store pointed-end down at cool (not cold) temperature, and set within about 7–10 days for best hatchability, exactly as with other poultry species.
  • Turkey eggs incubate for approximately 28 days (longer than the 21 days for chicken eggs) at around 37.5°C with correctly managed humidity, raised in the final days before hatch.
  • Turn eggs regularly until 2–3 days before expected hatch, then stop to allow poults to position correctly.

Lesson 4.4 — From hatch to brooder

  • Leave newly hatched poults in the incubator until fully dry and fluffed before moving to a pre-warmed brooder — exactly as covered for other poultry species in Month 1.
  • Poults from natural incubation under a broody turkey hen generally show strong early feeding/drinking behaviour learned from the mother — a genuine advantage of natural incubation where it's a practical option for the scale of operation.
  • Keep detailed hatch records (set date, hatch date, hatch rate) to identify your most productive breeding stock for future seasons.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 4): (1) Decide and document your breeding strategy (natural breeding vs purchased poults) based on your breed choice. (2) If breeding, select breeder stock and set up the correct tom-to-hen ratio. (3) If hatching eggs, set a batch and record hatch rate at 28 days.

MONTH 5 — Timing Production for Market ▾

Lesson 5.1 — Understanding the seasonal demand spike

  • Turkey demand rises sharply around major festive periods in most markets (Christmas and other year-end celebrations, and other locally significant holidays) — prices during these windows are commonly well above the rest-of-year baseline.
  • Missing the peak window by even a few weeks (birds ready too early or too late) can mean selling into the regular, lower-priced market instead of the seasonal peak — timing is as important to profitability as the feeding and health work covered in earlier modules.
  • Work backward from your target sale date: subtract the grow-out period (5–6 months from hatch/poult purchase) to determine your poult acquisition or hatch date.

Lesson 5.2 — Planning the production calendar

  • Build a written production calendar for the season: poult acquisition date, phase-feeding transition dates, expected finishing weight range, and target sale date — this turns a 6-month commitment into a set of trackable milestones rather than a vague plan.
  • Stagger acquisition in batches if supplying multiple sale points or spreading risk — a single batch failure (disease, feed problem) is less catastrophic if it isn't your entire season's production.
  • Communicate with likely buyers (butchers, restaurants, direct household customers) well ahead of the season to gauge demand and, where possible, secure orders or deposits before the peak selling window arrives.

Lesson 5.3 — Finishing weight targets and pre-sale management

  • Target market weight varies by buyer preference and local market norms — smaller birds may suit household buyers while larger birds suit institutional or restaurant buyers; know your buyer's preference before finalizing your finishing plan.
  • In the final 1–2 weeks before sale, avoid introducing any new stress (housing changes, new birds, vaccination unless essential) — stress in this window can affect meat quality and bird condition right before the highest-value sale point of the entire cycle.
  • Plan slaughter/processing logistics (your own capacity or an abattoir/processor relationship) well ahead of the peak season, since processing capacity is often itself under seasonal pressure at the exact time you need it.

Lesson 4.4 — Handling and transport to sale

  • Handle birds calmly and minimise stress during catching and transport — rough handling close to slaughter can cause bruising that downgrades carcass value.
  • Withdraw feed (but not water) for a period before transport/slaughter as locally standard practice — this reduces gut content and mess during processing without harming the bird.
  • Confirm any required local health/movement documentation ahead of the sale date — last-minute regulatory surprises at the peak season are avoidable with early preparation.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 5): (1) Work backward from a target festive-season sale date to set your poult acquisition/hatch date. (2) Build a written production calendar with all key milestone dates. (3) Contact at least 2 potential buyers ahead of the season to gauge demand.

MODULE 6 — Marketing & the Turkey Business ▾

Lesson 6.1 — Costing turkey production

  • Cost per bird = poult/hatching cost + total feed cost across all phases (turkey feed is a larger total cost than a broiler's given the much longer grow-out) + labour + housing/equipment share + health costs.
  • Feed is the dominant cost given the 5–6 month cycle — small improvements in feed conversion or avoided losses from disease/lameness have an outsized effect on final margin compared with a short-cycle broiler enterprise.
  • Compare cost per bird against expected peak-season price, not average annual price — the whole point of timing production is capturing that premium, and costing should reflect the price you're actually planning to sell at.

Lesson 6.2 — Market channels

  • Direct sale to households (live bird or dressed, sold directly, often around festive season) typically gives the best price per bird for a smallholder-scale operation.
  • Restaurants, hotels and institutional caterers are a further channel, particularly for larger birds — building a relationship ahead of season with a repeat institutional buyer smooths demand across multiple production cycles.
  • Live sale versus dressed (processed) sale each have different buyer bases and price points — dressed birds command more but require processing capacity, food safety practice and often more regulatory attention.

Lesson 6.3 — Building repeat, seasonal demand

  • Turkey is a seasonal-peak product for most buyers — building a list of repeat customers who order every season, ideally with early deposits, converts an uncertain sales scramble at peak season into predictable, planned demand.
  • Word of mouth from satisfied customers (bird quality, size, freshness) is the primary driver of repeat seasonal business — consistent quality across seasons builds the reputation that supports advance orders.
  • Consider a small value-added offering (fresh vs frozen, different weight classes) to serve different segments of the same seasonal customer base without requiring separate production systems.

Lesson 6.4 — Final project: your bankable business plan

  • Assemble: breed/type choice and breeding or poult-sourcing plan, housing design, phase-feeding calendar timed to a target sale date, disease prevention protocol (with explicit chicken-separation policy), a full cost-per-bird breakdown at your target market weight, target market channels and expected price, and a repeat/seasonal customer development plan.
  • Submit with your growth, health and hatch (if applicable) records for your rAs certificate — turkey business plans that show correct seasonal timing and disease prevention are exactly what lenders and larger buyers look for in this higher-value, longer-cycle enterprise.
