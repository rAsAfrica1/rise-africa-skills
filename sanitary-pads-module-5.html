1. Fabric Selection: Cotton, Bamboo, and Microfiber

Choosing the right fabrics is the first step to making a reusable pad that is comfortable, absorbent, and long-lasting. The top layer that touches the skin should be soft and breathable. Cotton is the best choice: it is cool, allows air to flow, and does not irritate the skin. Look for 100% cotton, preferably unbleached or naturally dyed. Flannel cotton is also good because it is soft and absorbent. Bamboo is another option; it is very soft, naturally antibacterial, and more absorbent than cotton. However, bamboo can be more expensive and harder to find in local markets. Microfiber is a synthetic fabric that absorbs liquid quickly and holds it well, but it should not be used directly against the skin because it can cause irritation and does not breathe. Instead, use microfiber as an inner absorbent layer.

For the core absorbent layers, you need fabrics that soak up liquid fast and hold it without leaking. Cotton fleece, bamboo fleece, or microfiber terry are excellent. You can also use old towels or flannel sheets, but make sure they are clean and not worn thin. For the waterproof backing, you need a fabric that stops liquid from passing through. Polyurethane laminate (PUL) is the best choice: it is thin, waterproof, and breathable. Alternatively, you can use a waterproof nylon or polyester fabric, but PUL is more comfortable. Avoid plastic sheets as they do not breathe and can cause rashes.

When buying fabrics, always wash them first to shrink them and remove any chemicals. Pre-washing also makes the fabric softer. In Africa, you can find suitable fabrics at local markets, fabric shops, or online suppliers. Cotton and bamboo are often sold as meters or yards. For a single reusable pad, you need about 20 cm by 30 cm of each fabric type. A typical pad has three layers: a soft top layer, one or two absorbent layers, and a waterproof backing. You can add more absorbent layers for heavy flow. Always test a small piece of fabric by dropping water on it to see how quickly it absorbs and whether it leaks through. This simple test saves you from making pads that fail.

2. Pattern Making and Sizing

A pattern is a template you use to cut fabric into the shape of a pad. You can make patterns from cardboard, old X-ray film, or thick plastic. Start by drawing the shape of a pad on paper. A standard reusable pad is about 24 cm long and 8 cm wide at the center, with wings that extend 4 cm on each side. For heavy flow, make it longer (28 cm) and wider (10 cm). For light flow or panty liners, use 18 cm by 6 cm. You can also make different sizes for day and night. Remember that the pad should fit comfortably in the underwear without bunching.

To make a pattern, draw half the pad shape on a folded piece of paper, then cut it out and unfold it to get a symmetrical shape. Use a ruler to keep lines straight. For the wings, draw curves that will wrap around the underwear. You can add a snap or button later. Once you have a paper pattern, trace it onto your chosen pattern material. Cut it out carefully. Label the pattern with the size and the type of fabric (e.g., 'Top layer', 'Absorbent', 'Backing').

When cutting fabric, place the pattern on the fabric and pin it down. Use sharp scissors or a rotary cutter. Cut two pieces for the top layer (if you want a folded edge) and two for the backing, plus one or two absorbent layers. Always cut on the grain of the fabric to prevent stretching. If you are making many pads, you can stack fabric layers and cut multiple pieces at once. But be careful to keep the pattern aligned.

Sizing is important for comfort and leak protection. Measure the underwear you will wear with the pad. The pad should be long enough to cover the crotch area and extend a little at the front and back. The wings should be long enough to wrap around the gusset and snap together. Test the pattern by making a sample pad with cheap fabric first. Wear it for a few hours to see if it fits well. Adjust the pattern as needed. In Africa, many women prefer pads that are not too bulky, so keep the absorbent layers thin but effective. You can also make patterns for different flow levels and offer them as a set.

3. Sewing and Assembly Techniques

Sewing a reusable pad requires a basic sewing machine or a needle and thread if you are hand-sewing. A sewing machine makes the work faster and stronger. Use a straight stitch for most seams and a zigzag stitch for edges to prevent fraying. If you have a serger, it is ideal for finishing edges. Start by sewing the absorbent layers together. Stack one or two absorbent layers (e.g., cotton fleece) and sew them along the edges with a zigzag stitch. This keeps them from shifting. If you want extra absorbency, you can quilt the layers by sewing lines across them.

Next, attach the absorbent layer to the waterproof backing. Place the backing with the shiny side (waterproof side) down, and the absorbent layer on top. Pin them together. Sew around the edges with a straight stitch, leaving a small opening (about 5 cm) on one side so you can turn the pad inside out. Trim the corners to reduce bulk. Then turn the pad right side out through the opening. Use a blunt tool like a chopstick to push out the corners. Press the pad flat with an iron on low heat (be careful with PUL; do not iron directly).

Now attach the top layer. The top layer should be slightly larger than the pad so you can fold it over the edges. Place the top layer over the absorbent side, right sides together. Sew around the entire pad, leaving a gap for turning. Turn it right side out and sew the opening closed with a slip stitch or topstitch. For a neat finish, topstitch around the entire pad 3 mm from the edge. This also secures all layers.

Finally, add the wings. Cut wing pieces from the top fabric and backing. Sew them to the sides of the pad, reinforcing with a zigzag stitch. You can also add a snap or Velcro to the wings for fastening. Test the pad by pulling gently on the seams to ensure they are strong. Remember to backstitch at the beginning and end of every seam. If you are hand-sewing, use a backstitch for strength. Always use polyester thread because it is strong and durable. Cotton thread can break easily. Keep your stitches small and even for a professional look.

4. Insertion of Absorbent Layers

The absorbent layers are the heart of a reusable pad. They determine how much liquid the pad can hold and how dry the top layer feels. You can use natural fabrics like cotton, bamboo, or hemp, or synthetic microfiber. For a typical pad, you need 2 to 4 layers of absorbent fabric, depending on the flow. For light flow, use 2 layers; for heavy flow, use 4 layers. Each layer should be cut to the same shape as the pad body, but slightly smaller so they fit inside the pad without sticking out.

When inserting absorbent layers, you can either sew them directly into the pad or make them removable. Sewn-in layers are simpler and less likely to shift. To sew them in, stack the layers on top of the waterproof backing, then cover with the top layer and sew all around. Removable layers are inserted into a pocket, which makes drying faster and allows you to adjust absorbency. To make a pocket, sew the top layer and backing together leaving one end open, then insert the absorbent layers. You can also make separate absorbent inserts that you place inside the pocket. This is useful for heavy flow days when you need extra protection.

If you use microfiber, place it in the middle, not against the skin. Microfiber absorbs quickly but can cause irritation if it touches the skin. Also, microfiber can be bulky, so use it sparingly. Natural fibers like cotton and bamboo are more breathable and comfortable against the skin. You can combine fabrics: a layer of microfiber for quick absorption, then a layer of cotton for holding liquid. This gives you the best of both.

Before sewing, test the absorbency of your layers by pouring a small amount of water (about 30 ml) onto them. See how quickly it absorbs and whether it leaks through. If it leaks, add more layers or use a more absorbent fabric. Also, consider the drying time. More layers take longer to dry, especially in humid climates. In Africa, where sun drying is common, thinner layers dry faster and are less likely to develop mold. Always wash and dry absorbent layers thoroughly before use. You can also pre-shrink them by washing in hot water and drying on high heat. This prevents the pad from shrinking after sewing.

5. Finishing and Fastening Methods

Finishing touches make your reusable pad look professional and function well. After sewing all layers, you need to secure the edges and add fastening. The most common fastening is a snap button, but you can also use Velcro (hook and loop tape) or even buttons and buttonholes. Snaps are durable, easy to use, and do not snag. You can buy snap pliers and snap sets from craft shops or online. To attach a snap, you need to make a small hole in the wing, then press the snap parts together with the pliers. Alternatively, you can sew on snaps by hand. Velcro is cheaper and easier to find, but it can wear out over time and may catch on other fabrics in the wash. If you use Velcro, sew the hook side on one wing and the loop side on the other.

Another finishing method is to add a layer of topstitching around the entire pad. This reinforces the edges and gives a neat look. Use a contrasting thread color if you like. You can also add a decorative stitch, but keep it functional. For the wings, you can shape them into a curve that wraps around the underwear. Make sure the wings are long enough to overlap by at least 2 cm when fastened. Test the fastening by putting the pad on underwear and adjusting the fit. The pad should stay in place without shifting.

If you are making pads for sale, consider adding a label with care instructions. You can sew a small tag on the back with washing symbols. Also, consider the color and pattern of the top fabric. Dark colors hide stains better, but light colors show if the pad is clean. Many women prefer dark or patterned fabrics for the top layer. For the backing, you can use a solid color. You can also add a layer of fleece or flannel on the back for extra softness against the underwear.

Finally, trim any loose threads and check all seams. A well-finished pad should have no raw edges that can fray. If you do not have a serger, use a zigzag stitch or fold the edges over and stitch. For a truly professional finish, you can bind the edges with bias tape. This is a strip of fabric that you sew around the edge. It adds durability and a nice look. Practice on scrap fabric until you are confident. Remember, the goal is a pad that is comfortable, leak-proof, and easy to wash.

6. Quality Checks for Reusable Pads

Quality checks ensure that every reusable pad you make is safe, effective, and durable. Start by inspecting the fabric before cutting. Look for holes, stains, or weak spots. Do not use damaged fabric. After cutting, check that all pieces are the correct size and shape. A simple ruler check saves time later. When sewing, check the seams as you go. Pull gently on the seam to see if it holds. If it opens, reinforce with a second stitch. Also, check for skipped stitches or loose threads. A good seam is tight and even.

After assembly, test the pad for absorbency. Pour 30 ml of water (about two tablespoons) onto the center of the pad. It should absorb within 30 seconds without leaking through the backing. If it pools on the surface, the absorbent layers are not working. You may need to add more layers or change the fabric. Also, check the waterproof backing by placing a tissue under the pad and pressing. If the tissue gets wet, the backing is not waterproof. In that case, use a different backing or add another layer of PUL.

Check the fastening. Snap or Velcro should hold securely but be easy to open. Test by fastening and unfastening five times. If it loosens, replace it. Check the wings: they should be long enough to wrap around the underwear and overlap. The pad should not shift when you walk or sit. You can test by wearing the pad for an hour with a light flow or with water. Also, check for comfort: no scratchy edges, no bulky areas. The pad should feel soft against the skin.

Finally, wash the pad according to instructions and check for shrinkage or damage. A good pad should survive at least 50 washes. After washing, check that the layers have not separated and that the waterproof backing is still intact. If you are selling pads, establish a quality control checklist. For example: 1) All seams are secure. 2) Pad absorbs 30 ml in 30 seconds. 3) No leaks through backing. 4) Fasteners work. 5) No loose threads. 6) Correct size. 7) Comfortable to wear. Keep records of your checks. This helps you maintain consistent quality and build trust with customers. Remember, a high-quality pad protects the user and the environment.
