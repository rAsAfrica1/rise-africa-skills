1. Standard mix ratios for common block types

When making blocks, the mix ratio is the amount of cement compared to the amount of sand and aggregate. In Africa, we usually measure by volume using buckets or wheelbarrows. The most common mix for solid load-bearing blocks is 1:5: meaning one part cement to five parts sand. For hollow blocks that are not load-bearing, you can use 1:6 or even 1:7. The ratio changes based on the strength you need. For a 9-inch solid block that will carry heavy walls, use 1:5. For a 6-inch hollow block for a fence, 1:6 is fine. Never go weaker than 1:7 for any block that will be exposed to weather. A common mistake is to add more sand to save cement. That makes weak blocks that crack and crumble. If you are making blocks for a foundation, use 1:4 or 1:5. For a single-storey house wall, 1:5 or 1:6. For a garden wall, 1:6 or 1:7. Remember, the ratio is by volume, not weight. So if you use one bucket of cement, you need five buckets of sand. But sand must be clean and free of clay or roots. If the sand is dirty, wash it or use more cement. In many parts of Africa, people use a wheelbarrow as the measure. One wheelbarrow of cement to five wheelbarrows of sand is too much cement; that is 1:5 but a wheelbarrow is large. Better to use smaller containers like buckets for accuracy. Always mix on a clean concrete floor or metal sheet. Never mix on bare ground because soil will contaminate the mix. Start by measuring sand first, then add cement on top. Mix dry until the colour is uniform. Then add water slowly. The mix should be stiff, not runny. A good test: squeeze a handful; it should hold shape without dripping water. If it drips, too wet. If it crumbles, too dry. Adjust with small amounts. These ratios work for most common blocks in Africa, but always test a few blocks first and let them cure for 7 days before using them in a wall.

2. Measuring materials without a weighing scale: using buckets and wheelbarrows

Most block makers in Africa don't have a weighing scale. That's fine. You can measure accurately with buckets, wheelbarrows, or even tins. The key is to use the same container for all measurements. A standard 20-litre bucket is common. For a 1:5 mix, you need 1 bucket of cement to 5 buckets of sand. But cement is often sold in 50kg bags. One 50kg bag of cement is about 33 litres by volume. So if you use a 20-litre bucket, one bag fills about 1.5 buckets. That means for a 1:5 mix, you would need 1.5 buckets of cement to 7.5 buckets of sand. But that's awkward. Better to use a smaller container, like a 10-litre bucket. Then one 50kg bag is about 3.3 buckets of cement. For 1:5, you need 3.3 buckets cement to 16.5 buckets sand. Still not round. Many block makers use a wheelbarrow as the main measure. A standard wheelbarrow holds about 60 litres. One bag of cement is about half a wheelbarrow. So for 1:5, you need half a wheelbarrow of cement to 2.5 wheelbarrows of sand. That's easy: 1 bag cement to 2.5 wheelbarrows sand. But wheelbarrows vary in size. So always check your own wheelbarrow. Fill it with water using a bucket to know its volume. Then you can calculate. Another method: use a wooden box. Make a box 30cm x 30cm x 30cm. That is 27 litres. One bag of cement is about 1.2 boxes. For 1:5, use 1.2 boxes cement to 6 boxes sand. The most important rule: never guess. Always use the same container for cement and sand. If you use a bucket for cement, use the same bucket for sand. Level off each bucket with a straight stick. Don't heap it. A heaped bucket has more material. Also, remember that sand is often wet. Wet sand takes more volume. So if sand is wet, use slightly less sand or add a little more cement. In the rainy season, sand can be soaked. Then you might need to adjust. But for dry sand, the bucket method works well. Practice with a small batch first. Make 10 blocks with your measurements. If they are strong after 7 days, your measurements are good. If they crack, add more cement. This is how you learn without a scale.

3. Adding water: how much is too much or too little

Water is the most common mistake in block making. Too much water makes weak, porous blocks that crack and shrink. Too little water makes a crumbly mix that won't hold shape. The right amount of water is about 5 to 7 litres per 50kg bag of cement for a 1:5 mix. But that depends on how wet your sand is. If your sand is dry, you might need 7 litres. If it's already damp, maybe 4 litres. Never just pour water from a hose. Use a bucket or watering can so you can control it. Add water slowly while mixing. The mix should be stiff, like thick porridge, not runny like soup. A good test: pick up a handful and squeeze. It should form a ball that holds together without dripping water. When you open your hand, the ball should stay intact. If water drips out, it's too wet. If it crumbles, it's too dry. Another test: drop the ball from knee height. It should flatten slightly but not splash. For block making, you want a mix that is drier than for concrete slabs. Blocks need to hold their shape immediately after pressing. If the mix is too wet, the block will sag and lose shape. If it's too dry, the block will have holes and weak edges. In hot African climates, water evaporates fast. So mix only as much as you can use in 30 minutes. If you leave the mix sitting, it will dry out. You can add a little water and remix, but never add water to a mix that has already started to set. That is dangerous. Also, use clean water. Dirty water with oil, salt, or organic matter will weaken the cement. In many rural areas, people use water from rivers or wells. That's fine if it's clear. But if it's muddy, let it settle and use the clear part. Never use seawater for reinforced blocks because salt rusts steel, but for plain blocks, seawater can be used in small amounts, though it may cause efflorescence (white stains). Best to use fresh water. Remember, the goal is a mix that is workable but stiff. Practice with small batches. If you can't shape a block that stands on its own without slumping, it's too wet. If the block crumbles when you touch it, it's too dry. Adjust by adding small amounts of water or dry cement and sand.

4. Using admixtures and waterproofers in rainy seasons

In the rainy season, block making becomes difficult. Too much moisture in the air and in the sand can ruin your blocks. Admixtures are chemicals you add to the mix to improve properties. Waterproofers are a type of admixture that makes blocks repel water. In Africa, you can buy liquid waterproofers at hardware stores. Common brands include Sika, Fosroc, or local equivalents. They are added at about 0.5 to 1 litre per 50kg bag of cement. For rainy season, a waterproofer helps blocks resist water penetration, reducing dampness and efflorescence. Another useful admixture is a plasticiser, which makes the mix more workable with less water. That means you can use a stiffer mix without adding too much water. This is great for block making because stiff mixes produce stronger blocks. A plasticiser is added at about 0.2 to 0.5 litres per bag. If you can't afford admixtures, you can use alternative methods. For example, add a small amount of lime (about 5% of cement weight) to improve workability and water resistance. Or use wood ash, but that reduces strength. In the rainy season, your sand will be wet. That means you must reduce the water you add. Test the sand first: squeeze a handful. If it drips, it's too wet. Spread it out to dry for a few hours before mixing. Also, cover your blocks with plastic sheets or tarpaulins after casting. Rain can erode fresh blocks. Cure them under cover for at least 7 days. If you must make blocks in the rain, work under a shed. Use a waterproofer to help the blocks shed water. But remember, admixtures are not a substitute for good mix ratios. You still need the right cement-to-sand ratio. Also, never mix admixtures with bare hands; use gloves. Store them away from children. In the dry season, you may not need waterproofers. But in the rainy season, they are worth the extra cost. They can reduce block breakage and improve durability. If you are making blocks for a foundation in a wet area, always use a waterproofer. It will save you money in the long run.

5. Mixing small batches by hand on a clean surface

For small-scale block making, you don't need a mixer. You can mix by hand on a clean, flat surface. This is how most blocks are made in Africa. First, choose a clean concrete floor or a metal sheet. Never mix on bare ground because soil will get into the mix and weaken it. If you must mix on ground, first lay a tarpaulin or plastic sheet. The surface should be hard and smooth. Start by measuring your sand. For a 1:5 mix, measure 5 buckets of sand. Spread it in a ring or a flat layer. Then measure 1 bucket of cement. Pour the cement on top of the sand. Now, using a shovel or hoe, mix the dry materials. Stand on one side and pull the sand and cement towards you, turning it over. Keep turning until the colour is uniform. There should be no streaks of brown sand or grey cement. This takes about 3 to 5 minutes. Next, make a crater in the middle of the dry mix. Pour water into the crater. Add water slowly. Start with about 4 litres for a 1:5 mix using one bag of cement. Then use your shovel to fold the dry mix into the water. Keep turning and folding. Add more water if needed, but a little at a time. The mix should become stiff but uniform. If you add too much water, you cannot remove it. So be careful. Once the mix is ready, use it within 30 minutes. If it starts to dry, you can add a tiny bit of water and remix, but never add water to a mix that has begun to set. After mixing, clean your surface immediately. Dried cement is hard to remove. For small batches, you can mix with a hoe in a wheelbarrow. That is even easier. Put sand and cement in the wheelbarrow, mix dry, then add water and mix with a hoe. This is good for making 10 to 20 blocks at a time. Always wear gloves and a dust mask when handling cement. Cement dust is harmful to your lungs. Also, work in a shaded area if possible. The sun can dry the mix too fast. With practice, you can mix a batch in 10 minutes. Remember, consistency is key. Use the same method every time so your blocks are uniform.

6. Calculating how many blocks one bag of cement can make

One of the most common questions is: how many blocks can I make with one bag of cement? The answer depends on the block size, mix ratio, and how much sand you use. Let's take a standard 9-inch solid block (450mm x 225mm x 225mm). Its volume is about 0.0228 cubic metres. For a 1:5 mix, the total volume of cement and sand needed for one block is about 0.0228 cubic metres. But cement is only 1/6 of that volume (since 1 part cement to 5 parts sand = 6 parts total). So cement volume per block = 0.0228 / 6 = 0.0038 cubic metres. One 50kg bag of cement has a volume of about 0.033 cubic metres (33 litres). So number of blocks per bag = 0.033 / 0.0038 = about 8.7 blocks. So you get about 8 to 9 solid 9-inch blocks per bag of cement. For a 6-inch hollow block (400mm x 200mm x 150mm), the volume is about 0.012 cubic metres. But hollow blocks have voids, so the actual material volume is less. Typically, a 6-inch hollow block uses about 0.008 cubic metres of mix. For 1:6 mix, cement volume per block = 0.008 / 7 = 0.00114 cubic metres. Blocks per bag = 0.033 / 0.00114 = about 29 blocks. So one bag can make about 25 to 30 hollow 6-inch blocks. For a 4-inch solid block (400mm x 200mm x 100mm), volume = 0.008 cubic metres. For 1:5 mix, cement volume = 0.008 / 6 = 0.00133 cubic metres. Blocks per bag = 0.033 / 0.00133 = about 25 blocks. So one bag makes about 25 solid 4-inch blocks. These numbers are estimates. Your actual yield depends on how well you measure and how much waste you have. Also, if you use a richer mix (1:4), you get fewer blocks per bag. If you use a leaner mix (1:7), you get more blocks but they are weaker. Always test a small batch to find your actual yield. For example, make 10 blocks and see how much cement you used. Then calculate. Keep a record. In Africa, many block makers sell blocks by the bag of cement. They know that one bag makes about 8 solid 9-inch blocks. So they price accordingly. If you are making blocks for a project, calculate the total number of blocks needed, then divide by the yield per bag to know how many bags to buy. Always add 5% extra for waste. Also, remember that the sand quality affects yield. Dirty sand needs more cement. So always use clean sand.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Block Making Materials and Mix Ratios

In block making, the mix ratio is the single biggest driver of your cost per block, your strength, and whether your blocks survive handling, curing and the rainy season. Get the ratio right and you can quote confidently, deliver on time, and avoid the heartbreak of a stack of blocks that crumble in a customer's hands. Get it wrong and you lose cement, sand, labour and reputation â all at once.

This Practical Reference is written for the small to medium producer working in an African context: a yard, a mixer (or a mixing floor), a few moulds, casual labour, and materials bought by the bag, the bucket, the wheelbarrow and the tipper load. The aim is to help you measure without a weighing scale, control water, calculate what one bag of cement actually produces, and price your blocks so the business makes money.

1. Standard mix ratios for common block types

Block strength is governed by the cement-to-aggregate ratio and the water-cement ratio, not by how "fat" the mix looks. A common African practice is to express the mix by volume: one part cement to so many parts sand (and stone, for solid or load-bearing blocks). The ratios below are typical for hand-mixed, vibrated or hand-tamped blocks using clean, well-graded sand and 19 mm (3/4") stone where used.

2. Measuring materials without a weighing scale

Most small yards do not have a scale, and that is acceptable if you measure consistently. The key is to fix one measuring container and never change it mid-project. A standard 20-litre bucket or a builder's wheelbarrow (typically 65â100 litres) works well. Always measure cement by the full bag (usually 50 kg) and sand by the same bucket or barrow every time.

If you use a wheelbarrow, first calibrate it: fill a 20-litre bucket and count how many buckets fill your barrow. A typical builder's barrow is 65â100 litres, so 3â5 buckets. Once calibrated, you can batch by the barrow and keep the ratio consistent. Write the calibration on the wall of your store â it stops arguments and protects your mix.

3. Adding water: how much is too much or too little

Water is the cheapest ingredient and the most common cause of weak blocks. Too little water means the mix will not compact, leaving voids and honeycombing. Too much water increases shrinkage, reduces strength, and produces blocks that crack during curing or handling. The practical target is a mix that holds its shape when squeezed in the hand and does not slump or bleed water when tamped.

Cement is highly alkaline and can cause severe skin burns, eye damage and silicosis from dust. Always wear gloves, eye protection and a dust mask when handling cement and dry sand. Never kneel in wet cement or allow it to remain on skin. Keep cement bags dry and off the ground in a covered store; damp cement loses strength and can set in the bag. Ensure good ventilation when mixing and never use bare hands to mix or test the mix.

4. Using admixtures and waterproofers in rainy seasons

In many African markets, the rainy season brings two problems: wet aggregates that throw off your water ratio, and blocks that are slow to cure or are damaged by rain before they gain strength. A waterproofing admixture (integral waterproofer) or a plasticiser can help, but it is not a substitute for a correct mix.

Integral waterproofers are dosed by volume or weight per bag of cement â typically 0.5â1.0 litre per 50 kg bag, but always follow the manufacturer's instructions. They reduce permeability and improve resistance to damp, which is useful for foundation blocks and external walls. Plasticisers improve workability so you can reduce water without losing compaction. Accelerators can help in cool, wet weather, but they must be used carefully and never mixed with other admixtures without advice.

Practical rainy-season controls: cover sand and stone stockpiles; measure the moisture of sand by the squeeze test and reduce added water; cure blocks under cover or plastic sheeting; never let rain fall directly on fresh blocks for the first 24â48 hours. If you are producing for a customer, agree in writing on the admixture and the protection method â it protects both your price and your reputation.

5. Mixing small batches by hand on a clean surface

For small batches, hand mixing on a clean, hard, level surface is entirely practical if done properly. The sequence matters:

  1. Prepare the surface: a concrete slab or strong tarpaulin, free of soil, stones and old set concrete.
  2. Measure dry materials: sand first, spread in a ring; add cement in the centre; add stone if used.
  3. Dry mix: turn the pile at least three times until the colour is uniform and no streaks of cement remain.
  4. Add water: form a crater, add water gradually, and turn the mix inward. Add water in small amounts â it is easier to add than to remove.
  5. Mix to consistency: turn the whole pile at least three more times until the mix is uniform and workable.
  6. Use promptly: in hot climates, place and compact within 30â45 minutes of mixing. Do not add water to a mix that has started to set.

Keep the batch size manageable: 2â4 bags of cement per hand batch is usually the practical limit for two workers. Larger batches are better done in a mixer to maintain consistency and output.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Fix one measuring container (bucket or calibrated barrow) and use it for every batch.
  • Write your mix ratio and barrow calibration on the store wall.
  • Measure cement by the full bag; never guess a "scoop".
  • Do the hand-squeeze test before moulding: firm ball, no free water.
  • In the rainy season, cover stockpiles and reduce added water.
  • Use waterproofing admixture only at the manufacturer's dose per bag.
  • Run a trial batch and count actual blocks per bag before quoting.
  • Wear gloves, eye protection and a dust mask at all times.
