1. Fiber Goat Breeds: Angora and Cashmere

In Africa, two main goat breeds produce valuable fiber: Angora and Cashmere. Angora goats are raised for mohair, a long, lustrous fiber. They thrive in dry, temperate regions like the Karoo in South Africa, Lesotho, and parts of Kenya. Angoras need good grazing and shelter from rain. Their fiber grows about 2 cm per month, and they are shorn twice a year. A healthy Angora produces 2â4 kg of mohair per year. Cashmere goats, on the other hand, produce cashmere, a soft, fine undercoat. They are found in colder, high-altitude areas like the Himalayas, but in Africa, they can be raised in the Atlas Mountains of Morocco and the Ethiopian Highlands. Cashmere is combed out during molting season, yielding 100â300 g per goat per year. Crossbreeds with local goats can adapt better to African conditions. For smallholders, starting with a few Angoras or Cashmeres can supplement income. However, these breeds require more care than meat goats: they need balanced feed, mineral supplements, and protection from predators. In South Africa, Angora studs are registered, and farmers can join the Mohair Growersâ Association for support. In East Africa, cashmere production is still emerging, but trials show promise. Before buying, check for healthy skin, bright eyes, and no signs of disease. Quarantine new animals for 30 days. With proper management, fiber goats can be a profitable enterprise, especially where there is access to shearing services and fiber buyers.

2. Shearing and Harvesting Fiber

Shearing Angora goats is done twice a year, typically in spring and autumn. Use sharp electric or manual shears. Before shearing, clean the fleece by removing vegetable matter and dust. Restrain the goat on a shearing board or a clean tarp. Start at the belly and work upwards, keeping the fleece in one piece. A skilled shearer can shear a goat in 3â5 minutes. For cashmere, harvesting is different: during molting season (late spring), use a fine-toothed comb to gently comb out the undercoat. Do this every few days, collecting the soft fiber. Do not pull forcefully. In Africa, many farmers use a metal comb with teeth spaced 2â3 mm apart. After harvesting, store fiber in breathable bags, away from moisture and pests. Avoid mixing different grades. For Angora, skirt the fleece: remove dirty, short, or coarse parts. A good mohair fleece weighs 1.5â2.5 kg. For cashmere, a single combing yields 30â50 g, and total annual yield is 100â300 g. Shearing should be done by a trained person to avoid cuts. If you are new, watch videos or attend a training. In South Africa, mobile shearing teams serve small farms. In Kenya, some cooperatives have shared shears. After shearing, keep goats in a warm, dry place for 24 hours to prevent chilling. Proper harvesting ensures high fiber prices.

3. Fiber Processing and Cleaning

After harvesting, fiber must be cleaned before spinning. Mohair and cashmere contain grease, dirt, and vegetable matter. Start by sorting: remove any stained, matted, or coarse fibers. Then wash the fiber in warm water (about 50Â°C) with a mild detergent. Do not use hot water or harsh chemicals, as this can damage the fiber. Use a ratio of 10 liters of water to 100 g of detergent for 1 kg of fiber. Soak for 15â20 minutes, then rinse in clean water of the same temperature. Repeat if needed. Squeeze gentlyâdo not wring or twist. Dry in the shade on a clean net or rack. For cashmere, dehairing is crucial to separate the fine undercoat from the coarse guard hair. This can be done by hand or with a dehairing machine. In Africa, small-scale processors use hand cards or drum carders to open and align fibers. Carding removes remaining debris and makes a fluffy batt. For mohair, you may also need to comb it to align the long fibers. After processing, store fiber in a dry, dark place. Clean fiber sells for higher prices. In Lesotho, some cooperatives process mohair locally, adding value. If you plan to sell, keep records of weight before and after cleaning: typically, 1 kg of raw mohair yields 700â800 g of clean fiber. For cashmere, 1 kg of raw yields only 200â300 g of clean down. Proper processing is labor-intensive but increases income.

4. Spinning and Weaving Basics

Spinning turns fiber into yarn. For beginners, a drop spindle is cheap and portable. You can make one from a wooden dowel and a CD. To spin, draft a small amount of fiber, twist it, and wind onto the spindle. For mohair, a worsted spin gives a smooth, lustrous yarn; for cashmere, a woolen spin gives a soft, fluffy yarn. In Africa, many women spin in groups, sharing techniques. A spinning wheel speeds up production but costs more. You can also use a supported spindle for fine cashmere. After spinning, ply two or more strands together for strength. Weaving is done on a loom. A simple frame loom can be made from wood. You can weave scarves, shawls, or blankets. For mohair, a plain weave shows the fiberâs sheen. For cashmere, a twill weave gives drape. In South Africa, the Karoo weavers use mohair to make luxury textiles. In Kenya, some groups weave cashmere blends. Dyeing can be done with natural dyes from plants like indigo or onion skins. Use alum as a mordant. Always test dye on a small sample. Selling finished products like scarves yields much more than selling raw fiber. A handspun, handwoven mohair scarf can sell for $30â$50 in tourist markets. Start small, practice daily, and join a cooperative for marketing. Remember, quality yarn comes from clean, well-carded fiber.

5. Grading and Selling Fiber

Grading is sorting fiber by quality to get the best price. For mohair, grade by fiber diameter (micron), length, and cleanliness. The best mohair is fine (under 30 microns), long (over 10 cm), and free of debris. In South Africa, the Mohair Growersâ Association provides official grades: Kid, Young Goat, Adult, and Fine. For cashmere, grade by color (white is most valuable), length (over 3 cm), and fineness (under 19 microns). Use a micrometer to measure diameter, or send samples to a lab. In Africa, many buyers at local markets do not grade, but you can get better prices by selling to cooperatives or export agents. Weigh your fiber accurately and pack in clean, labeled bags. Store in a dry place. Selling options: local markets, fiber cooperatives, online platforms (e.g., Etsy), or direct to spinners. Prices vary: in 2023, South African mohair averaged $25â$35 per kg for adult, $50â$70 for kid. Cashmere can fetch $80â$150 per kg for clean down. To get top prices, build a reputation for consistent quality. Join a cooperative to access better markets. In Lesotho, the Lesotho Mohair Company buys from smallholders. In Kenya, the Kenya Cashmere Initiative links farmers to buyers. Always get a receipt and keep records. Consider adding value by spinning or weaving before selling. A finished product can earn 5â10 times more than raw fiber.

6. Managing Fiber Goats for Quality

Quality fiber starts with healthy goats. Feed a balanced diet: good pasture, hay, and a supplement of 16% protein pellets. Provide loose minerals, especially sulfur, zinc, and copper, which improve fiber strength. Angora goats need more protein than meat goats. Rotate pastures to prevent parasites. Keep goats in clean, dry sheltersâwet fleeces can mold. Shear or comb regularly to prevent matting. For Angora, shear every 6 months; for cashmere, comb during molting. Keep records of each goatâs fiber yield and quality. Select breeding stock with fine, long fiber. In South Africa, Angora rams are tested for fiber diameter. In Kenya, cashmere goats are selected for down yield. Avoid inbreeding; bring in new genetics every few years. Health care: vaccinate against enterotoxemia and tetanus. Deworm strategically, not routinely. Check for external parasites like lice and mites, which damage fiber. Treat with pour-on insecticides. Provide clean water. Stress reduces fiber quality, so handle goats gently. In hot climates, provide shade. In cold, provide bedding. Keep goats away from thorny bushes that can contaminate fleece. Record everything: birth dates, shearing dates, weights, and fiber grades. Join a breed society for advice. With good management, a small herd of 20 Angoras can produce 60 kg of mohair per year, earning $1,500â$2,000. Cashmere goats yield less but higher value per gram. Consistent quality builds buyer trust.

Practical Reference: Fiber Production with Goats in Africa

Fiber goats â primarily Angora (mohair) and Cashmere â represent one of the highest-value small-stock enterprises available to African farmers. A single kilogram of quality mohair can fetch USD 20â40 on international markets, while cashmere can reach USD 60â120 per kilogram depending on grade and fiber length. For smallholders in Lesotho, South Africa, and the highlands of East Africa, fiber goats convert marginal land into premium export commodities. This lesson is a business-first reference: how to produce, harvest, process, grade, and sell fiber profitably.

Unlike meat or dairy goats, fiber goats are not primarily managed for weight gain. Your product is the fleece, and every management decision â nutrition, shelter, breeding, shearing timing â must optimize fiber yield, length, fineness, and cleanliness. Buyers pay premiums for consistency. A farmer who can deliver 200 kg of uniform, well-graded mohair annually has a repeatable export business. A farmer who delivers 200 kg of mixed, contaminated fleece has a one-time transaction.

Why Fiber Goats Make Business Sense in Africa

Africa produces roughly 60% of the world's mohair, with South Africa alone accounting for approximately 50% of global production. Lesotho, Namibia, and Tanzania also have established fiber goat sectors. The market is export-driven, with major buyers in Europe, China, and India. Domestic processing is growing but still limited, meaning most raw fiber is exported â creating both opportunity (access to hard currency) and risk (dependence on global prices).

The key business advantages:

  • High value per kilogram: Fiber goats generate 5â20x the revenue per kg of live weight compared to meat goats.
  • Low capital intensity: Fiber goats thrive on browse and low-quality forage unsuitable for cattle.
  • Repeatable harvest: Angora goats are shorn twice yearly; cashmere is combed once annually.
  • Export market access: Established grading and auction systems in South Africa and Lesotho.
  • Climate resilience: Fiber goats tolerate arid and semi-arid conditions better than most livestock.

Understanding the Two Main Fiber Types

Mohair (Angora goats): Long, lustrous, strong fiber with excellent dye affinity. Average fiber diameter ranges from 22â38 microns depending on age. Young goats (kids) produce the finest, most valuable fiber (22â27 microns). Mohair is used in upholstery, suits, blankets, and wigs. South African mohair is considered the global benchmark for quality.

Cashmere (Cashmere goats): The fine undercoat (down) beneath coarse guard hair. Average diameter is 14â19 microns â significantly finer and softer than mohair. Yield per goat is low (150â250 g per year), which explains the high price. Cashmere is used in luxury garments. Africa's cashmere production is small but growing, particularly in Lesotho and South Africa.

Shearing and Harvesting: Timing Is Money

Angora goats are shorn twice per year â typically in spring (SeptemberâOctober) and autumn (MarchâApril) in southern Africa. Shearing too early in winter risks cold stress and pneumonia; shearing too late reduces fiber length and value. Cashmere goats are not shorn; the down is combed out during the natural molt (late winter to early spring) using a specialized comb. Timing the comb correctly is critical â too early and the fiber is not fully released; too late and the down is lost to shedding.

Fiber Processing and Cleaning

Raw fiber is not saleable as-is. The value chain requires skirting (removing dirty, short, and contaminated edges), classing (sorting by color, length, and fineness), and baling. Contamination is the single biggest cause of price penalties. Vegetable matter (grass, thorns), manure, and synthetic fibers (from plastic twine or feed sacks) can render an entire bale unsellable.

Processing steps:

  1. Skirting: Remove belly, leg, and crutch wool, along with any stained or kempy (coarse) fiber.
  2. Classing: Sort by age and body area â kids, young goats, and adults produce different grades.
  3. Scouring: Industrial washing to remove grease (lanolin) and dirt. Smallholders usually sell unscoured (greasy) fiber to a buyer who scours centrally.
  4. Carding and combing: Aligning fibers for spinning; removes remaining vegetable matter and short fibers (noils).
  5. Spinning: Converting fiber into yarn. Hand-spinning is viable for value-added local products; machine spinning is for export.

Shearing involves sharp mechanical cutters and powerful electric motors. Never allow untrained workers to operate shearing equipment. Ensure: (1) blades are disinfected between animals to prevent spread of skin diseases such as dermatophilosis; (2) shearers wear protective footwear and use proper restraint cradles to avoid back injury; (3) animals are not shorn when wet â wet fleece clogs blades and increases cut risk; (4) electrical equipment is earthed and used with RCD protection, especially in rural areas with unstable power. In cashmere combing, use combs with rounded teeth to avoid tearing the skin. Always have a first-aid kit and a veterinarian contact on hand for accidental cuts.

Grading and Selling Fiber

Grading determines price. The main grading criteria are:

  • Fineness (micron): The single most important factor. Measured in microns; lower is more valuable.
  • Staple length: Longer fibers spin better and command higher prices.
  • Color: White is preferred and dyes well; colored fiber sells at a discount unless marketed as natural-colored niche.
  • Cleanliness: Low vegetable matter and no contamination.
  • Style and character: Crimp, lustre, and uniformity.

In South Africa, mohair is sold through the Cape Mohair & Wool auction system in Port Elizabeth (Gqeberha), which sets global benchmark prices. Smallholders typically sell to local brokers or cooperatives, which aggregate and class fiber before auction. In Lesotho, the Lesotho Mohair Project supports smallholder aggregation and direct market access.

Managing Fiber Goats for Quality

Quality is determined before shearing. Nutrition directly affects fiber diameter and strength. Overfeeding concentrates (grain) can cause fiber to thicken and lose value; underfeeding causes breaks and tender fleece. The ideal ration for fiber goats is browse-based with supplementary minerals â especially sulfur, which is essential for keratin (fiber protein) synthesis.

Key management practices:

  • Breeding: Select for fineness, staple length, and uniformity. Record micron test results for breeding stock.
  • Health: Control internal parasites (especially in wet seasons), vaccinate against pulpy kidney and pasteurellosis, and manage foot rot.
  • Shelter: Angora goats are susceptible to cold and wet; provide dry shelter, especially after shearing.
  • Record-keeping: Track fleece weight, micron, and price per animal to identify top performers.
  • Contamination control: Never use plastic twine in goat areas; keep feed sacks and synthetic fibers away from fleece.
