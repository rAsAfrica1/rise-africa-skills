1. Grinding and Sanding for Smooth Finishes

Start with safety: wear goggles, a dust mask, and earplugs. Grinding throws sparks and metal dust. For a 4.5-inch angle grinder, use a 40-grit flap disc to knock down weld spatter and rough edges. Keep the grinder moving at a 15â20 degree angle to the work. Don't press hard; let the disc do the cutting. In Africa, you can find these discs in most hardware stores in cities like Nairobi, Lagos, or Johannesburg. For smaller areas, a rotary tool with a carbide burr works well.

After grinding, switch to sanding. For flat surfaces, use a random orbital sander with 80-grit paper to remove grinder marks. Then step up: 120, 180, 220, and 320 grit. Each step should remove the scratches from the previous grit. If you skip grits, you'll waste time and sandpaper. For curved or detailed areas, sand by hand using a sanding block or a flexible pad. Use water as a lubricant when sanding non-ferrous metals like aluminium or brass to prevent clogging. For ferrous metals, dry sanding is fine.

In many African workshops, you can make your own sanding blocks from hardwood offcuts and wrap sandpaper around them. This saves money and gives you control. For deep scratches, start with a file, then 40-grit, then progress. Check your progress by wiping the surface with a rag and looking at it under good light. A raking light (a light held at a low angle) reveals scratches you might miss.

Finish sanding with 400-grit for a satin look, or go to 800-grit if you plan to polish. Remember, sanding is not just about smoothness; it's about preparing the surface for the next stepsâbrushing, polishing, or patination. A well-sanded piece takes patina more evenly. Clean the metal with acetone or denatured alcohol before moving on. In rural areas, you can use locally made charcoal mixed with water as a mild abrasive for final smoothing, but rinse well.

2. Brushing and Polishing Techniques

Brushing creates a textured, satin finish. Use a wire brush attachment on a drill or angle grinder. For stainless steel, use a stainless steel wire brush to avoid contamination. For carbon steel, a brass or mild steel brush works. Brush in one direction onlyâback and forth or circularâto create a uniform pattern. Keep the tool moving at a consistent speed. Too fast and you'll overheat the metal, causing discoloration. For a finer brushed finish, use a nylon abrasive brush with silicon carbide grit. These are available in most industrial supply shops.

Polishing is a multi-step process. Start with a stitched cotton buffing wheel charged with black emery compound for coarse cutting. Then move to a loose cotton wheel with white rouge for medium polishing, and finally a soft flannel wheel with red rouge for high shine. Mount these on a bench grinder or a dedicated polisher. Speed should be around 2000â3000 RPM. Apply compound sparinglyâtoo much creates a greasy mess. Hold the metal firmly and press lightly. Move the piece against the wheel's rotation to avoid it being snatched.

In Africa, you can improvise a polisher using a washing machine motor fitted with a spindle and buffing wheels. Many artisans in Ghana and Nigeria use this setup. For small pieces, a hand drill with a buffing pad works. Always wear a dust mask and safety glasses; polishing compounds can be toxic. After polishing, wipe off excess compound with a soft cloth. If you want a mirror finish, repeat the steps with finer compounds. For a matte finish, stop after the first polishing step.

Remember, brushing and polishing are not just for looksâthey also affect how patina adheres. A highly polished surface resists patina, while a brushed surface takes it more readily. So decide your final look before you start. Clean the metal with mineral spirits or alcohol after polishing to remove all compound residue. Store buffing wheels in a dust-free container to avoid cross-contamination.

3. Chemical Patinas for Ferrous Metals

Ferrous metals include mild steel, wrought iron, and cast iron. The most common patina is rust, but you can control it. For a traditional brown-black patina, use a mixture of 1 part hydrogen peroxide (3%), 1 part white vinegar, and 1/2 part table salt. Spray or brush this onto clean, bare steel. Let it sit for 10â30 minutes, then rinse with water and dry. Repeat until you get the depth you want. This works well in humid African climates, but you may need to speed up drying with a heat gun.

For a deep black patina, use a commercial product like Birchwood Casey Perma Blue or a homemade mix of 1 part selenium dioxide, 10 parts water, and a few drops of phosphoric acid. Apply with a rag, let it sit for 2â5 minutes, then rinse and oil. Selenium dioxide is toxicâwear gloves and a respirator. In many African countries, you can find selenium dioxide at chemical supply stores or online. Alternatively, use a strong tea solution (tannic acid) to create a dark brown patina on steel. Boil 100g of black tea in 1 litre of water, cool, and apply. Tannic acid reacts with rust to form a stable black compound.

For a rusted, aged look, mix 1 part muriatic acid (hydrochloric acid) with 3 parts water. Always add acid to water, never the other way. Apply to clean steel, let it fume for 5â10 minutes, then neutralize with baking soda solution (1 cup per litre of water). Rinse and dry. This gives a rough, orange-brown rust. For a more controlled rust, use a saltwater spray and let it sit in humid air. In coastal areas like Mombasa or Durban, the natural salt air speeds up rusting.

After patination, always neutralize the metal with a baking soda solution to stop the chemical reaction. Then rinse with distilled water and dry thoroughly. Apply a clear coat or wax immediately to protect the patina. Without protection, the patina will continue to change. Test your patina on scrap metal firstâresults vary with steel composition and temperature. Keep notes on your recipes and timings so you can repeat them.

4. Chemical Patinas for Non-Ferrous Metals

Non-ferrous metals include copper, brass, bronze, and aluminium. For copper and brass, the classic green patina (verdigris) is made by mixing 1 part ammonium chloride (sal ammoniac) and 4 parts water. Brush it on clean metal and let it sit in a warm, humid place for 24â48 hours. You can also use vinegar and salt: mix 1 part salt, 1 part vinegar, and 2 parts water. Spray and let it dry. For a deeper blue-green, add a few drops of ammonia. In Africa, ammonium chloride is available at some pharmacies or chemical shops. Always work outdoors or in a well-ventilated area.

For a brown patina on brass or bronze, use a solution of 1 part ferric nitrate and 4 parts water. Apply with a brush, let it dry, then repeat. This gives a rich antique brown. Another option is to use liver of sulphur (potassium sulphide). Mix 1 teaspoon in 1 litre of warm water. Dip the metal for a few seconds to a minute. It will turn dark brown to black. Rinse immediately and stop the reaction with cold water. Liver of sulphur is available from jewellery supply stores. It smells like rotten eggs, so use outdoors.

For aluminium, patination is tricky because it forms a natural oxide layer. To get a dark patina, first clean with a lye solution (sodium hydroxide) to remove the oxide, then apply a solution of 1 part copper sulphate and 10 parts water. This deposits copper onto the aluminium, which then patinas. Alternatively, use a commercial aluminium blackening solution. For a white-grey patina, use a mixture of 1 part nitric acid and 10 parts water, but this is dangerousâonly for experienced artisans. Safer: use a saltwater and vinegar mix and let it corrode naturally.

After patination, rinse with distilled water and dry. For a stable finish, apply a clear lacquer or wax. For copper and brass, a coat of Renaissance wax or a clear acrylic spray works well. In humid African climates, you need good protection to prevent uncontrolled corrosion. Always test on scrap first. Keep a notebook with your recipes, metal type, and timing. Patinas are affected by temperature, humidity, and metal alloyâso adjust as needed.

5. Clear Coating and Waxing for Protection

After patination, you must protect the surface. Without protection, patinas will continue to develop, and polished surfaces will tarnish. The two main options are clear coats (lacquers, varnishes, or acrylics) and waxes. For a durable, glossy finish, use a clear acrylic spray like Krylon or Rust-Oleum. Apply in thin coatsâ2â3 light passesârather than one thick coat. Hold the can 20â30 cm away and spray in a steady back-and-forth motion. Let each coat dry for 15â20 minutes. In hot, humid African conditions, spray early in the morning when humidity is lower. Avoid spraying in direct sunlight.

For a more natural, matte finish, use wax. Renaissance wax is a microcrystalline wax that is used by museums. It's expensive but goes a long way. Apply a thin layer with a soft cloth, let it dry for 10 minutes, then buff with a clean cloth. For a cheaper option, use carnauba wax or beeswax. Mix 1 part beeswax with 2 parts turpentine (or mineral spirits) to make a paste wax. Warm the metal slightly (not hot) to help the wax penetrate. Apply, let dry, and buff. Reapply every 6â12 months, or more often in coastal areas.

For outdoor sculptures, use a clear polyurethane varnish. Apply with a brush in thin coats, sanding lightly between coats with 400-grit paper. Two to three coats are enough. Polyurethane is tough but can yellow over time. For a non-yellowing option, use a clear acrylic lacquer. In Africa, you can find these at hardware stores in major cities. If you can't find commercial products, you can make a traditional wax from beeswax and paraffin: melt 1 part beeswax and 1 part paraffin, then add 2 parts turpentine. Stir well and let cool.

Before coating, ensure the metal is completely clean and dry. Any oil or dust will cause the coating to flake. Use acetone or denatured alcohol for a final wipe. For wax, the metal can be slightly warm, but for lacquer, it must be cool and dry. Test your coating on a small area first. Some patinas are sensitive to solvents in spraysâif the patina dissolves, use wax instead. Always work in a well-ventilated area and wear a respirator when spraying. Store your coated sculpture in a dust-free place for 24 hours to cure.

6. Project: Patinated Sculpture

In this project, you will create a small metal sculpture and apply a patina and protective finish. Choose a simple designâan abstract form, a stylised animal, or a geometric shape. Use mild steel, copper, or brass. You'll need: metal stock (at least 3 mm thick), an angle grinder, sandpaper (80â800 grit), a wire brush, patina chemicals (choose one recipe from the previous lessons), clear coat or wax, and safety gear. Work in a well-ventilated area.

Step 1: Cut and shape your metal. Use a hacksaw or angle grinder. Weld or braze pieces together if needed. Grind all welds and sharp edges smooth. Step 2: Sand the entire surface. Start with 80-grit to remove scratches, then progress to 220 and 400. For a polished look, go to 800. Clean with acetone. Step 3: Apply your chosen patina. For steel, try the vinegar-salt-peroxide mix for a brown rust. For copper, use the ammonium chloride recipe for green. Apply with a brush or spray, let it react, then rinse and neutralize with baking soda solution. Let dry completely.

Step 4: Protect the patina. If using wax, warm the metal slightly and apply a thin coat of Renaissance wax or beeswax mixture. Buff with a soft cloth. If using clear coat, spray 2â3 thin coats of clear acrylic, letting each dry. Step 5: Mount your sculpture on a base if desired. A wooden or stone base adds stability and presentation. Use a two-part epoxy or screws to attach. Step 6: Evaluate your work. Check for uneven patina, missed spots, or coating defects. If needed, touch up with more patina or wax.

Document your process: take photos, note the patina recipe, timing, and weather conditions. This helps you repeat successes. Present your sculpture to your class or community. In Africa, patinated metal art sells well to tourists and local buyers. A small piece (20â30 cm) can fetch $20â$100 depending on quality. With practice, you can scale up to larger commissions. Remember, the key to a good patina is patience and testing. Don't rush the chemical reactions. Always prioritize safetyâwear gloves, goggles, and a respirator when working with chemicals.

Practical Reference: Surface Finishing and Patination for Metal Sculpture

This practical reference is the working document for the module Surface Finishing and Patination in the RISE Africa Skills course Metal Sculpture. It is written for practising and aspiring metal sculptors, workshop owners, and artisanal businesses across Africa who need repeatable, market-ready finishes â not laboratory theory. Every procedure below is designed to be executed with equipment commonly available in African urban and peri-urban workshops: angle grinders, hand files, abrasive papers, brass brushes, blowtorches, and locally sourced or imported chemicals.

Why finishing is a business decision, not just a craft step. In the African metal art market â from the tourist corridors of Cape Town, Nairobi, Accra, Dakar, and Marrakech to gallery commissions and architectural installations â the finish is what the buyer sees, photographs, and prices. A well-patinated sculpture can command two to four times the price of an identical form left with mill scale and grinder marks. Finishing also determines durability in humid coastal markets, in dusty Sahelian conditions, and in outdoor installations exposed to harmattan dust, salt spray, and intense UV. Treat every finishing stage as a costed production step with a quality checkpoint, not as an afterthought.

â  SAFETY WARNING â CHEMICAL PATINATION

Many patina chemicals are corrosive, toxic, or both. Ammonia fumes, potassium sulphide, ferric chloride, and anodising acids can cause serious burns, respiratory damage, and permanent eye injury. Always work outdoors or in a forced-ventilation area, wear nitrile gloves, chemical splash goggles, and a P2/P3 respirator. Never mix chemicals unless the recipe explicitly states it. Store chemicals in clearly labelled, locked containers away from food, water sources, and children. In many African markets, some of these chemicals are sold without proper labelling â insist on supplier safety data sheets (SDS) and treat unlabelled containers as hazardous. Neutralise acid residues with baking soda solution and dispose of waste according to local environmental regulations.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Surface preparation first. No patina will hide poor grinding. Remove all mill scale, weld spatter, and deep scratches before any chemical work.
  2. Degrease before patination. Wash with detergent or wipe with acetone/isopropyl alcohol. Oils and fingerprints cause patchy, uneven colour.
  3. Test on scrap. Every batch of chemical behaves differently. Run a test coupon of the same alloy before committing the sculpture.
  4. Control the environment. Temperature, humidity, and airflow dramatically affect patina speed and colour. Record conditions for repeatability.
  5. Build colour in layers. Apply patina in thin, repeated passes rather than one heavy application. This gives depth and control.
  6. Neutralise and rinse. Stop the chemical reaction with baking soda solution or clean water, then dry thoroughly before sealing.
  7. Seal within 24 hours. Unsealed patinas continue to react and can be damaged by handling. Wax or lacquer immediately after full drying.
  8. Photograph and document. Record the recipe, timing, and result for every piece. This is your business's intellectual property and your quality control system.
