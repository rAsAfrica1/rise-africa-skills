1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Scrollwork and twists are the decorative heart of ornamental ironwork. In Africa, from the wrought-iron gates of Marrakech to the balconies of Cape Town, these elements turn plain metal into art that sells. As a self-taught blacksmith, you can charge 30-50% more for a gate with scrolls than a plain one. A simple twist on a burglar bar can make it a feature rather than a cage.

Why does it matter for your business? Because customers want identity. A scroll pattern can mimic traditional motifslike the Adinkra symbols of Ghana or Ndebele geometric patternsmaking your work culturally relevant. Twists add strength and visual rhythm. A 12mm square bar twisted 3 times per 300mm is both structural and beautiful.

You don't need expensive machines. A forge, an anvil, a vice, a hammer, and a scroll jig (which you can make from scrap) are enough. Start with mild steelit's cheap and forgiving. Practice on 8mm round bar: heat to cherry red (about 900C), bend around a jig, and quench in oil to set. One hour of practice per day for a week and you'll have your first sellable scroll.

In this module, you'll learn to make consistent scrolls and twists, avoid common breaks, and work safely. By the end, you'll produce a small decorative panel that you can show to clients. Remember: in Africa's growing construction market, decorative ironwork is in demand for homes, lodges, and offices. Master these skills and you control your income.

2. Tools and Materials

To do scrollwork and twists, you need the right tools. Start with a forgea simple charcoal or gas forge works. Charcoal is cheap in most African markets; a bag costs about $5 and lasts a week. For gas, a 9kg cylinder and a burner kit will set you back $150 but gives cleaner heat. You also need an anvil (any heavy steel block works; a 50kg piece of railway line is perfect), a cross-peen hammer (1-2kg), a flat hammer, and a wire brush.

For scrolls, make or buy a scroll jig. You can weld three pieces of 20mm round bar onto a base plate: one center pin and two outer pins to guide the curve. For twists, a sturdy vice and a twisting wrench (a long pipe wrench or a custom-made fork) are essential. You'll also need a hacksaw, angle grinder with cutting and grinding discs, and a set of files.

Materials: mild steel is your best friend. Use 8mm, 10mm, or 12mm round or square bar. Square bar twists more crisply; round bar scrolls smoother. Buy from local scrap yards or steel merchantsprices vary, but expect $1-2 per meter for 10mm round. Avoid high-carbon steel (like old files) for scrolls; it cracks when bent cold. For twists, mild steel is fine.

Safety gear: leather gloves, safety glasses, earplugs, and a leather apron. In Africa's heat, wear cotton clothing, not synthetic. Keep a bucket of water and a first-aid kit nearby. A fire extinguisher is a must if using gas. With these tools, you're ready to start.

3. Step by Step Method

Step 1: Design your scroll. Draw it on paper or directly on the floor with chalk. A classic scroll is a spiral that starts tight and opens up. For a 200mm scroll, start with a 300mm length of 10mm round bar. Mark the center.

Step 2: Heat the bar. Place it in the forge until it's cherry red (about 900C). Don't let it sparklethat's too hot and burns the steel. Hold it with tongs.

Step 3: Form the eye. With the hot bar, hammer a small 180 bend at one end around a 20mm pin on your jig. This is the 'eye' of the scroll. Work quickly; you have about 30 seconds before it cools.

Step 4: Scroll the spiral. Place the eye over the center pin of your jig. Pull the bar around the pin, using the outer pins as guides. Apply steady pressuredon't jerk. If it cools, reheat. For a tight scroll, use a smaller center pin (15mm); for an open scroll, use 25mm.

Step 5: Twist a bar. For a twist, heat a 300mm section of 10mm square bar to cherry red. Clamp one end in a vice. Attach your twisting wrench to the other end. Turn it clockwise 3 full turns for a tight twist, or 1.5 turns for a loose twist. Keep the heat even; if one part is cooler, it won't twist evenly.

Step 6: Assemble. Weld or forge-weld scrolls to a frame. For a simple panel, make a rectangular frame from 20mm square bar, then weld scrolls inside. Clean welds with a grinder, then brush and oil while warm for a black finish.

Practice each step separately before combining. With 2 hours of practice, you'll make a basic scroll. With 10 hours, you'll be consistent.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Overheating the steel. If you heat until it sparks, the steel burns and becomes weak. Fix: Heat only to cherry red (900C). In daylight, watch for a dull red; in dark, a bright red. Use a magnetwhen it stops sticking, it's at the right temperature.

Mistake 2: Twisting cold. Cold steel cracks when twisted. Fix: Always heat to cherry red before twisting. If you hear a crack, stop and reheat.

Mistake 3: Uneven scrolls. Your scrolls look different each time. Fix: Use a jig with fixed pins. Mark your starting point on the bar with a soapstone. Count your hammer blowsconsistency comes from repetition.

Mistake 4: Weak welds. Welds break under load. Fix: Clean the metal to bare shine before welding. Use a 2.5mm electrode at 80-100 amps for 10mm bar. Forge-weld for stronger joints: heat both ends to welding heat (bright yellow, almost white), sprinkle borax, and hammer together.

Mistake 5: Rust. Your finished piece rusts in a month. Fix: Brush off scale with a wire brush while hot, then apply linseed oil or a metal primer. In humid African coastal areas, use a zinc-rich primer.

Mistake 6: Bending the wrong way. You twist counter-clockwise and the pattern looks off. Fix: Decide direction before you start. For a right-hand twist, turn clockwise when viewed from the end.

Mistake 7: Using the wrong steel. You grab a piece of rebar and it cracks. Fix: Rebar is often high-carbon or brittle. Stick to mild steeltest by bending a cold piece; if it bends without cracking, it's good.

Each mistake teaches you. Keep a notebook of what works. Soon you'll avoid these entirely.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety first. In Africa, many workshops are informal, but that's no excuse for accidents. Always wear safety glasseshot scale flies when you hammer. Leather gloves protect from burns and cuts. Wear earplugs when grinding or hammering; hearing loss is permanent. A leather apron and closed shoes (not sandals) are a must.

Fire safety: Keep a bucket of water and a fire extinguisher near the forge. Never leave a fire unattended. If using gas, check hoses for leaks with soapy water. Store gas cylinders upright, outside the workshop. Charcoal forges produce carbon monoxidework in a well-ventilated area, ideally outdoors or with a chimney.

Tool safety: Before use, check hammers for loose heads. Grinders should have guards. Never use a grinder without the handle. When twisting, ensure the vice is securea flying bar can kill. Use a twisting wrench with a long handle to keep your hands away from the hot metal.

Best practices: Plan your work. Draw full-size templates. Keep your work area cleantripping over scrap causes falls. Quench hot metal in oil, not water, for mild steel to avoid cracking. For a professional finish, brush off scale and apply oil while the metal is warm (about 200C).

In Africa's heat, take breaks and drink water. Heat exhaustion is real. Work in the morning or evening. If you feel dizzy, stop. Teach apprentices safety from day one. A safe workshop is a productive workshop. Your reputation depends on quality and safety.

6. Practical Exercise

Now put it all together. Your task: make a decorative panel 300mm x 300mm with four scrolls and two twisted bars. This is a sellable itemyou can mount it on a gate or sell as a wall piece.

Materials: 2 meters of 10mm round bar (for scrolls), 1 meter of 10mm square bar (for twists), 1.2 meters of 20mm square bar (for frame). Tools: forge, anvil, hammer, scroll jig, vice, twisting wrench, welder, angle grinder, wire brush.

Step 1: Cut the frame. Cut two 300mm pieces and two 260mm pieces of 20mm square bar. Weld into a 300x300mm square. Grind welds smooth.

Step 2: Make four scrolls. Cut four 250mm pieces of 10mm round bar. Heat and form a scroll on your jig, each with a 20mm eye. Make them as identical as possible. Quench in oil.

Step 3: Make two twisted bars. Cut two 260mm pieces of 10mm square bar. Heat the middle 200mm and twist 3 full turns clockwise. Do both the same.

Step 4: Assemble. Arrange scrolls in the corners of the frame, pointing inward. Weld them to the frame. Place the twisted bars horizontally across the center, 50mm apart. Weld.

Step 5: Finish. Heat the whole panel with a torch or in the forge to about 200C (just too hot to touch). Brush with a wire brush to remove scale. Apply linseed oil or black paint. Let cool.

Step 6: Evaluate. Check that scrolls are even, twists are tight, and welds are strong. Show to a friend or potential customer. Ask for feedback. If you can make this in 3 hours, you're ready for client work. Repeat the exercise until you can do it in 2 hours. Then try a more complex design with 8 scrolls. Practice makes profit.

<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0; font-weight:bold; color:#8c2a20;"> SAFETY WARNING HEAT, PINCH AND EYE HAZARD</p>
<p style="margin:0;">Scroll and twist work involves heating steel to forging temperature (bright orange, roughly 9001100 C) and applying high leverage through wrenches, jigs, and bending forks. Serious injuries in this trade come from three sources: <strong>(1) burns</strong> from grabbing hot stock that looks cold in daylight always quench-test or mark hot ends; <strong>(2) pinch and crush injuries</strong> when a loaded twisting wrench slips or a jig pin shears under load; <strong>(3) eye injuries</strong> from scale, sparks, and metal fragments during hammering and cutting. Wear leather gauntlets, safety glasses or a full face shield, and steel-toe boots. Never stand in line with a loaded wrench or bar under tension. Keep a bucket of clean water and a first-aid burn kit within reach. In many African jurisdictions, workplace safety obligations fall under national occupational health and safety acts ignorance is not a defence if an apprentice is injured in your workshop.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Confirm the design and price before you cut steel.</strong> A sketch signed by the client prevents disputes over "that is not what I meant".</li>
<li><strong>Check material sizes against the quote.</strong> If the client's budget assumed 20 6 mm flat bar, do not substitute 25 8 mm without re-quoting.</li>
<li><strong>Set up the jig and test one scroll or twist.</strong> Verify radius and pitch against the drawing before batch production.</li>
<li><strong>Work to a consistent pitch and radius.</strong> Irregularity is the most common quality complaint on decorative ironwork.</li>
<li><strong>Quench and inspect for cracks.</strong> Sharp bends in thick sections can crack; reject and re-make rather than paint over a defect.</li>
<li><strong>Clean all scale and weld spatter before priming.</strong> Paint fails on dirty steel this is where repeat business is won or lost.</li>
<li><strong>Prime within 4 hours of cleaning.</strong> Flash rust starts quickly in humid African climates.</li>
<li><strong>Photograph the finished work and log the hours.</strong> Your next quote is only as good as your last job record.</li>
</ol>
