1. Preparing for Poults

Before your poults arrive, you must prepare a clean, warm, and safe brooder area. A good brooder can be a simple wooden box, a corner of a room, or a dedicated pen. The key is to keep poults warm, dry, and protected from drafts and predators. Start by cleaning and disinfecting the area at least one week before arrival. Use a disinfectant like Virocid or a bleach solution (1 part bleach to 10 parts water). Rinse well and let it dry completely.

Next, set up the brooder with bedding. Wood shavings are best, but you can also use chopped straw or rice husks. Avoid sawdust as poults may eat it and get sick. Spread bedding 5-8 cm thick. For the first week, cover the bedding with newspaper or burlap for the first 2-3 days to prevent poults from eating bedding, then remove it so they can scratch.

Heating is critical. For 1-50 poults, use a 100-watt infra-red bulb or a charcoal stove (with proper ventilation). Hang the bulb 45 cm above the bedding. You will need a thermometer at poult level to check temperature. Also provide a brooder guarda circle of cardboard or metal about 40 cm highto keep poults near heat and prevent drafts. The guard should be about 1.5-2 meters in diameter for 50 poults.

Provide feeders and drinkers. Use shallow, clean containers. For the first week, use jar drinkers or small troughs. Place them on the edge of the brooder area, not directly under the heat. Also, have turkey starter feed (28% protein) ready. You can buy commercial feed or mix your own: 10 kg maize meal, 5 kg soybean meal, 2 kg fish meal, and 1 kg mineral/vitamin premix. Store feed in a dry, rat-proof place.

Finally, ensure biosecurity. Limit visitors, wear clean boots and clothes, and keep other birds away. If you have other poultry, keep them separate. Check for holes in walls or nets. Prepare a first-aid kit with vitamins, electrolytes, and antibiotics (consult a vet). With these steps, your poults will have a strong start.

2. Brooding Temperature and Humidity

Temperature is the most critical factor for poult survival. Turkey poults need more heat than chicks. For the first week, maintain 35-38C (95-100F) at poult level (5 cm above bedding). Reduce by 3C each week until you reach 21C (70F) at week 5. Use a thermometer, not guesswork. If poults huddle under the heat source and chirp loudly, they are cold. If they spread out away from heat and pant, they are too hot. When comfortable, they will spread evenly around the brooder.

Humidity should be 50-60% for the first week, then 40-50% afterward. High humidity makes bedding damp and causes respiratory problems. Low humidity causes dehydration and dry eyes. To raise humidity, hang wet burlap sacks or place shallow pans of water near the heat source. To lower it, increase ventilation without causing drafts. In humid African climates, you may need a fan or more ventilation. In dry areas, use a humidifier or wet sacks.

Ventilation is important but must not create drafts. Poults are very sensitive to cold air. Use small vents near the ceiling or open a window slightly on the opposite side of the brooder. Ammonia smell means you need more ventilation. Never let the brooder become stuffy.

Monitor temperature at least three times a day: early morning, midday, and evening. Adjust the height of the heat bulb or the amount of charcoal. If using a charcoal stove, keep it outside the brooder and pipe heat in, or use a properly vented stove to avoid carbon monoxide. A simple thermometer costs little and saves lives.

As poults grow, they need less heat. By week 4, if nights are warm (above 20C), you can turn off heat during the day. By week 6, they can be fully off heat if weather is warm. Always provide a warm corner if nights are cold. Remember, poults that are chilled will pile up and suffocate. Check them often, especially at night.

3. Early Feeding and Watering

Poults can survive 24-48 hours without food, but they need water sooner. Start by giving them water with vitamins and electrolytes (like Vitamix or Stress Pack) for the first 2-3 days. Use clean, shallow drinkers. Dip each poult's beak into the water gently to teach them to drink. Add a few clean stones to the drinker to prevent drowning. Change water at least twice a day, or more if dirty.

After 2-3 hours of drinking, offer feed. Use a turkey starter feed with 28% protein. If you cannot buy commercial feed, mix your own: 10 kg maize meal, 5 kg soybean meal, 2 kg fish meal, 1 kg groundnut cake, and 0.5 kg mineral/vitamin premix. You can also add 0.5 kg of dried moringa leaves for extra vitamins. Mix well and store in a dry place. Feed poults free-choice (always available) for the first 4 weeks. Use shallow feeders or egg trays for the first week to make it easy for them to eat.

Poults are curious and may eat bedding. For the first 3 days, cover bedding with newspaper or burlap, and sprinkle feed on it. This teaches them to eat. After 3 days, remove the paper and use regular feeders. Clean feeders daily to prevent mold. Never let feed get wet.

A simple rule: 100 poults will eat about 1 kg of feed per day in the first week, increasing to 3 kg by week 4. Provide feed in small amounts several times a day to avoid waste. Watch for poults that are not eatingthey may be sick or weak. Separate them and give extra care.

Water is more critical than feed. A poult can die from dehydration in hours. Always ensure drinkers are full and clean. In hot weather, add ice cubes or place drinkers in shade. You can also add a little sugar (1 tablespoon per liter) to the first water to give energy. But do not use sugar for more than 24 hours as it can cause bacterial growth. Always provide fresh water.

4. Brooder Management

Daily management of the brooder keeps poults healthy. Start each day by checking temperature, water, and feed. Remove any wet bedding and add fresh. Wet bedding causes disease and chilling. Clean drinkers and feeders with soap and water, then rinse well. Once a week, disinfect them with a mild bleach solution.

Lighting helps poults find feed and water. For the first week, provide 23 hours of light and 1 hour of darkness. This helps them eat and grow. After week 1, reduce to 20 hours of light. Use a low-wattage bulb (40-60 watts) for light, not heat. Too much light can cause stress and feather pecking.

Stocking density: For the first 4 weeks, allow 10-12 poults per square meter. Overcrowding causes stress, disease, and poor growth. If you have 50 poults, you need at least 4-5 square meters. As they grow, reduce density to 5-6 per square meter by week 8. Provide enough space for all to eat and drink at once.

Biosecurity is crucial. Keep a footbath with disinfectant at the entrance. Wear dedicated boots and clothes. Limit visitors. Keep rodents and wild birds out. If you have other poultry, care for them after turkeys, not before. Wash hands before and after handling poults. Vaccinate according to local vet advicecommon vaccines include Newcastle and Fowl Pox.

Observe poults daily for signs of illness: drooping wings, watery droppings, sneezing, or huddling. Isolate sick poults immediately. Keep records of feed and water consumption, temperature, and deaths. This helps you spot problems early. A simple notebook is enough. Also, check for predators like snakes, rats, and cats. Secure the brooder with wire mesh.

Finally, handle poults gently. Never chase them. Move slowly and speak softly. Stress can cause them to pile up and suffocate. A calm environment promotes growth.

5. Transition to Grow-Out

At 4-6 weeks, poults are ready to move to grow-out pens. But transition must be gradual to avoid stress. Start by reducing brooder heat. If you use a heat lamp, raise it higher or turn it off during the day. At night, if temperatures are above 20C, you can turn off heat completely. If nights are cold, provide heat until 6-8 weeks. Before moving, make sure the grow-out pen is clean, dry, and draft-free. Disinfect it and put fresh bedding.

Move poults on a warm, sunny morning. Never move in the evening or during rain. Catch poults gently by their bodies, not wings or legs. Use a crate with ventilation. Place them in the new pen and give them water with vitamins. Keep them confined to a smaller area within the pen for the first few days so they can find feed and water. Use a brooder guard or fence.

Feed changes: At 4 weeks, switch from starter to grower feed (24-26% protein). Mix the two feeds for 3-4 days to avoid digestive upset. You can make grower feed by mixing 10 kg maize, 4 kg soybean meal, 2 kg fish meal, 1 kg wheat bran, and 0.5 kg mineral/vitamin premix. Provide grit (small stones) to help digestion. From 8 weeks, you can switch to finisher feed (18-20% protein).

Space: In grow-out, allow 1-2 square meters per bird by 12 weeks. Provide perches (wooden bars 5 cm wide) at 50 cm height. Turkeys like to roost. Also provide outdoor access if possible, but protect from predators with fencing. Range areas should have shade and clean water.

Monitor for stress: signs include panting, huddling, or decreased eating. Give electrolytes in water for 2-3 days after moving. Keep records of weight gain. A healthy turkey should weigh about 1.5 kg at 8 weeks and 4-5 kg at 16 weeks. If growth is slow, check feed quality and parasites. Deworm at 8 weeks with a poultry dewormer like Levamisole. Consult your vet for dosage.

6. Common Brooding Issues

Even with good care, problems can arise. Here are common brooding issues and how to fix them.

1. Chilling: Poults huddle and chirp loudly. They may pile up and suffocate. Fix: Increase heat immediately. Check thermometer. Ensure no drafts. If using charcoal, add more fuel. Never use open fire inside brooder.

2. Overheating: Poults pant, spread out away from heat, and drink a lot. Fix: Reduce heat. Raise bulb or reduce charcoal. Increase ventilation. Provide cool water.

3. Starvation: Poults may not find feed. They look weak and have empty crops. Fix: Dip beaks in water, sprinkle feed on paper. Use bright light. Check feeders are not too deep.

4. Pasting (sticky bottom): Droppings stick to vent, blocking waste. Fix: Gently wash with warm water and dry. Add vitamins to water. Check feed qualitytoo much protein or mold can cause it. Provide grit.

5. Splayed legs: Legs spread outward. Caused by slippery bedding or vitamin deficiency. Fix: Use rough bedding. Add vitamin B2 (riboflavin) to water. For mild cases, use a hobble (tape legs together) for a few days.

6. Cannibalism: Poults peck each other, often due to overcrowding, bright light, or lack of feed. Fix: Reduce density, dim lights, provide more feeders and drinkers. Add green feed like chopped grass. If severe, separate injured birds.

7. Respiratory issues: Sneezing, coughing, or rattling. Caused by ammonia, dust, or disease. Fix: Improve ventilation, clean bedding, reduce dust. If bacterial, use antibiotics (consult vet). Vaccinate against Newcastle.

8. Predators: Snakes, rats, cats, and birds of prey. Fix: Secure brooder with wire mesh. Use traps for rats. Keep a dog if possible. Check for holes daily.

9. Drowning: Poults can drown in deep drinkers. Fix: Use shallow drinkers or add stones. Never use open buckets.

10. Yolk sac infection: Poults hatch with unabsorbed yolk that gets infected. Signs: swollen abdomen, lethargy. Fix: Prevent by clean incubator and navel dipping in iodine. Treat with antibiotics if caught early.

Always act fast. Keep a first-aid kit with vitamins, electrolytes, antibiotics, and antiseptic. If many poults die, consult a vet or extension officer. Learn from each batch and keep records.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Turkey Brooding and Rearing

Turkey poults are among the most fragile young poultry in African smallholder and commercial systems. Unlike chickens, poults have a higher surface-area-to-body-weight ratio, slower early growth relative to feed intake, and a strong behavioural need for warmth and dry bedding. In Uganda, Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana, and South Africa, turkey farming is growing because of festive demand (Christmas, Eid, weddings) and the premium price of turkey meat compared with chicken. But the profit margin is decided in the first 28 days. A brooding loss of 10% can wipe out the entire gross margin of a 100-poult batch.

This Practical Reference module is written for the African agribusiness operator: the farmer, extension officer, or youth agripreneur who needs to make money from turkeys, not just raise them. It covers brooding temperature and humidity, early feeding and watering, brooder management, transition to grow-out, and common brooding issues. Two data tables and one diagram give you benchmarks. A red safety box highlights a lethal but common mistake. The checklist at the end is designed for daily use on the farm.

Why Brooding Determines Turkey Profitability in Africa

Poults cannot regulate body temperature until roughly 1014 days of age. In many African production zones, night temperatures fall below 20C, and in highland areas such as Kenya's Rift Valley or Ethiopia's highlands, they can drop below 12C. Without artificial heat, poults huddle, refuse feed, and die from chilling or starve. The brooding period is therefore not a husbandry detail; it is the financial control point.

Key business metrics for a 100-poult batch:

  • Target brooding mortality: under 5% by day 28 (commercial), under 8% (smallholder).
  • Feed conversion during brooding: 1.21.5 kg feed per kg gain is acceptable; above 1.8 signals waste, disease, or chill.
  • Labour: one person can manage 300500 poults with proper brooder design.
  • Energy cost: charcoal, gas, or electricity can be 1525% of brooding cost. Insulation and hover design reduce this directly.

Humidity matters because poults have immature respiratory systems. Too low humidity (below 50%) causes dehydration and pasty vents; too high (above 75%) causes wet litter, ammonia build-up, and respiratory disease. In humid coastal West Africa, ventilation is as important as heating. In dry Sahelian zones, water evaporation can drop humidity dangerously low; placing shallow water pans near the heat source helps.

Early Feeding and Watering

Poults should be placed on feed and water within 24 hours of arrival. Delayed access increases starve-out mortality. Use shallow, clean drinkers with marbles or stones to prevent drowning, and feed on flat trays or chick paper for the first 35 days.

Starter feed should be 28% crude protein for the first 4 weeks, with a fine crumb or mash texture. In many African markets, turkey starter is not always available; a practical alternative is to mix quality chick starter with boiled, mashed egg or fine fish meal, but this must be done under extension advice. Clean water must be available at all times; add glucose or electrolytes for the first 2448 hours after transport.

Brooder Management and Transition to Grow-Out

Brooder management is a daily discipline. Check poults at least three times per day: early morning, midday, and late evening. Look at their distribution, droppings, and noise. Quiet poults are comfortable; loud, distressed poults are cold, hungry, or sick.

Litter must remain dry. In humid zones, replace damp litter every 23 days. Ammonia smell indicates poor ventilation and will damage poult lungs. Brooder guards (cardboard or metal rings) should be expanded as poults grow, and removed by day 1014. By day 2128, begin transition to grow-out: reduce heat gradually, increase floor space, and introduce grower feed. A sudden move without heat reduction causes stress and mortality.

SAFETY WARNING Carbon Monoxide and Fire Risk

Charcoal stoves, kerosene lamps, and gas brooders produce carbon monoxide (CO), an odourless gas that kills poults and farmers. Never place a charcoal stove inside a closed brooder room without ventilation. CO poisoning causes poults to become drowsy, then die in huddles; humans can also be affected. Always install a vent or chimney, keep a window slightly open, and never leave a flame unattended overnight. Fire risk from dry bedding and open flames is high keep a bucket of sand or water near the brooder at all times. In South Africa and Kenya, fire-related brooder losses are a leading cause of poult mortality on small farms.

Common Brooding Issues and Practical Fixes

  • Piling or huddling: poults are cold. Increase heat or reduce draughts.
  • Panting and spreading away from heat: too hot. Lower heat or increase ventilation.
  • Pasty vent: often from cold, poor feed, or dehydration. Check temperature and water access.
  • Starve-out: poults fail to find feed or water. Use bright trays, marbles in drinkers, and dip beaks.
  • Wet litter: leaking drinkers or poor ventilation. Fix immediately; wet litter causes coccidiosis and ammonia burns.
  • Leg weakness: can indicate vitamin or mineral deficiency, or slippery bedding. Use rough-textured bedding and balanced starter feed.

Quick Reference Checklist Daily Brooder Routine

  1. Check poult distribution and noise at least 3 times daily; adjust heat if huddling or panting.
  2. Record minimum and maximum temperature and humidity every morning and evening.
  3. Ensure feed and clean water are available at all times; wash drinkers daily.
  4. Remove wet or soiled litter and top up with dry bedding.
  5. Check for draughts at floor level and seal gaps without blocking ventilation.
  6. Remove dead poults immediately and record mortality in a notebook.
  7. Expand brooder guard space as poults grow; remove guards by day 1014.
  8. Plan transition to grow-out from day 21; reduce heat gradually and prepare grower feed.

Business takeaway: Turkey brooding is a controlled investment. Every degree of temperature, every gram of feed, and every hour of labour should be tracked against poult survival and weight gain. In African markets where turkey meat sells at a premium, the farmer who masters brooding controls the margin. Start small, record everything, and scale only when mortality is consistently below 5%.
