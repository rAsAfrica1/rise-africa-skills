1. When and How to Hire Your First Employee

You know it's time to hire when you are consistently turning down orders, working more than 12 hours a day, or making mistakes because you're rushing. For example, if you produce 50 jars a day alone but demand is 80, you're losing 30 sales daily. Calculate your lost profit: if each jar gives 2,000 shillings profit, that's 60,000 shillings lost daily. Hiring one worker at 10,000 shillings per day still leaves you 50,000 shillings ahead. That's when you hire.

Start by hiring for the task you dislike or that takes the most time. In tomato paste production, that's usually stirring, filling jars, or washing tomatoes. Look for someone with basic hygiene knowledge, strength for lifting, and reliability. In many African communities, you can find workers through local markets, church groups, or vocational training centers. Pay a fair daily wage (e.g., 8,000â15,000 shillings depending on country) and provide one meal. Avoid hiring family unless they meet the same standards.

Train your first employee on your exact recipe and safety steps. Write down the steps in simple language (e.g., 'Wash tomatoes three times, remove all green parts, boil for 30 minutes'). Show them once, then watch them do it. Correct mistakes immediately. For the first week, pay daily and check quality. If they consistently follow steps, offer a monthly contract with a small bonus for good attendance.

Legal basics: Even for one employee, you need a written agreement stating hours, pay, and duties. In Kenya, for example, register with NHIF and NSSF if full-time. In Nigeria, get a tax ID. Keep records of payments. As you grow, consider hiring a second person for packaging while the first focuses on cooking. Never hire more than you can superviseâone bad hire can ruin your reputation.

2. Buying or Upgrading Equipment for Higher Output

To scale from 50 to 200 jars per day, you need better equipment. Start with a larger cooking potâa 100-liter stainless steel pot costs around 150,000â250,000 shillings in local markets (e.g., in Kampala or Nairobi). Avoid aluminum; it reacts with tomatoes. Next, a heavy-duty blender or a small pulping machine. A manual pulper (made in China) costs 300,000 shillings and can process 100 kg of tomatoes per hour. If electricity is reliable, a 2-horsepower electric pulper (500,000 shillings) saves hours.

For filling, a manual piston filler (50,000 shillings) gives consistent 500g portions. For sealing, a simple crown capping machine (80,000 shillings) works for glass jars. You'll also need a large stainless steel table for sorting and a gas burner (three-ring) for even heating. Total upgrade cost: about 1 million shillings. That's cheaper than a used car and pays back in 3â6 months if you sell 200 jars daily at 2,000 shillings profit each.

Buy equipment from reputable suppliers in industrial areas (e.g., Industrial Area in Nairobi, or Alaba International in Lagos). Test before paying. Ask for a demonstration with your own tomatoes. Check for rust, loose wires, or missing parts. Negotiateâprices are often inflated for first-time buyers. Consider buying second-hand from a food processing company that is closing. Inspect thoroughly.

Maintenance: Clean all stainless steel parts after each use with hot water and vinegar. Oil moving parts weekly. Keep a spare blade for the pulper. If you can't afford a machine, rent one from a local miller or cooperative for 20,000 shillings per day. That's better than turning down orders. As you grow, add a steam jacketed kettle (2 million shillings) for even cooking and a semi-automatic filling line (5 million shillings). But only upgrade when your current equipment is the bottleneck.

3. Organising a Small Production Line

A production line means arranging tasks in a sequence so that tomatoes move smoothly from raw to sealed jar. For a small operation with 3â5 workers, use a U-shaped layout in a clean room. Start at one end: receiving and washing. Then move to sorting (remove spoiled tomatoes), then chopping/pulping, then cooking, then filling, then sealing, then labeling, then packing. Each station should have a table, a bucket, and a waste bin. Keep raw and cooked areas separate to avoid contamination.

Assign one person per station. For 200 jars per day, you need: 1 washer/sorter, 1 pulper/cooker, 1 filler/sealer, 1 labeler/packer, and you as supervisor and quality checker. Pay them 10,000â15,000 shillings each per day. Total labor cost: 50,000 shillings. If each jar sells for 5,000 shillings and costs 2,000 to make, profit is 3,000 per jar. 200 jars give 600,000 shillings revenue, minus 50,000 labor and 400,000 materials = 150,000 shillings daily profit. That's scale.

Use color-coded buckets: red for waste, blue for raw tomatoes, green for cooked paste. This prevents mix-ups. Write simple signs in local language. Keep a whiteboard with daily targets: 'Today: 200 jars. Washed: 300 kg. Cooked: 180 kg.' Update hourly. Hold a 5-minute stand-up meeting each morning to assign roles and review yesterday's mistakes.

Safety: Provide aprons, hairnets, and gloves. Have a first-aid kit. Ensure floors are not slippery. Train everyone on handwashing for 20 seconds. Keep a logbook for cleaning. At the end of the day, 15 minutes of cleaning prevents bacteria growth. If you have 6+ workers, add a dedicated quality controller who checks every 10th jar for seal and taste. This line can produce 500 jars per day with 8 workers, but only after you master 200.

4. Managing Inventory of Raw Materials and Packaging

Inventory is money sitting on your shelf. Too much tomatoes and they rot; too few and you stop production. For fresh tomatoes, buy only what you can process in 2 days. A mature tomato lasts 3â5 days at room temperature. If you buy 500 kg on Monday, process 250 kg Monday and 250 kg Tuesday. Keep them in a cool, dark room on plastic crates, not stacked more than 3 high. Never store tomatoes in direct sun.

For jars and lids, buy in bulk to save 20â30%. A 500g glass jar costs 80 shillings each when bought in 1,000 units, but 120 shillings in small shops. Order 2,000 jars at a time (160,000 shillings). Store them in a dry place, upside down to keep dust out. Lids must be newâreused lids cause spoilage. Keep a minimum stock of 500 jars as buffer. Use a simple inventory card: date, quantity in, quantity out, balance. Count every Friday.

For tomato paste concentrate (if you use it), buy from a trusted supplier. A 70g sachet of concentrate costs 50 shillings and makes 1 kg of paste. Keep 10,000 sachets in stock (500,000 shillings). Rotate stock: first in, first out. Write dates on boxes. For spices (salt, sugar, citric acid), buy monthly. Salt in 25kg bags (20,000 shillings) is cheaper. Store in airtight containers.

Use a simple formula: Reorder level = (Average daily usage Ã Lead time) + Safety stock. If you use 100 jars per day and supplier takes 3 days, and you want 2 days safety, reorder at 500 jars. Keep a notebook or use a free app like 'Inventory Simple' on your phone. Never tie up more than 30% of your cash in inventory. If you have 1 million shillings working capital, keep 300,000 in stock, rest for labor and emergencies.

5. Finding Reliable Suppliers for Tomatoes and Jars

Your business depends on two things: good tomatoes and good jars. For tomatoes, go to the sourceâfarms around irrigation schemes (e.g., Mwea in Kenya, Zaria in Nigeria, or Awash in Ethiopia). Talk to the farm manager or cooperative. Ask for a sample. Test for firmness, redness, and no bruises. A reliable supplier delivers on time, every time. Start with a small order (100 kg) and pay cash. If quality is good, negotiate a weekly contract. Price should be 10â20% below market because you buy in bulk. For example, if market price is 50 shillings per kg, aim for 40â45 shillings. Agree on delivery to your door or you pick up.

For jars, find a local glass manufacturer or a large importer. In Kenya, Kaluworks or Central Glass; in Nigeria, Beta Glass. They sell in pallets of 1,000. Ask for a price list and minimum order. A reliable jar supplier has consistent quality: no cracks, correct neck size for your lids. Test 10 jars from each batch. If a supplier gives you 5% breakage, that's too high. Also buy lids from the same supplier to ensure fit. Consider plastic jars if glass is too expensive or breakage is highâthey cost 50 shillings each but are lighter and safer.

Build relationships: Visit your suppliers in person. Pay on time. Give small gifts during holidays. If a tomato farmer has a bad season, help them by buying smaller quantities but still buying. They will remember and prioritize you later. Always have a backup supplier for both tomatoes and jars. If your main tomato supplier fails, you can call the backup and get delivery within 2 days. Keep a list of 3 tomato farmers and 2 jar suppliers. Check their reliability by ordering small test batches. Never rely on one supplierâdrought, strikes, or price hikes can stop your production.

6. Planning for Seasonal Tomato Gluts and Price Fluctuations

Tomato prices swing wildly. In West Africa, a basket (50 kg) can cost 10,000 shillings in dry season and 2,000 shillings during harvest (DecemberâFebruary). In East Africa, gluts come in January and June. You must plan for both. During glut, buy extra tomatoes and process them into paste immediately. You can also sun-dry or freeze tomatoes for later. But the best strategy is to produce more paste and store it in jarsâpaste lasts 12 months if sealed properly. So when tomatoes are cheap, make 500 jars instead of 200. Rent extra storage.

During scarcity, prices triple. Don't buy fresh tomatoes at high prices unless you have a guaranteed buyer. Instead, sell from your stored paste. If you didn't store enough, reduce production and focus on high-margin products like spiced sauce. You can also switch to tomato concentrate (sachets) which has stable prices. Keep a price chart: record weekly tomato prices for 6 months. You'll see patterns. For example, in Nigeria, prices rise from March to May. So in February, stock up.

Financial planning: Set aside 20% of your profits during glut months as a 'tomato fund' for scarce months. If you make 150,000 shillings daily profit in glut, save 30,000 daily. After 30 days, you have 900,000 shillings to buy tomatoes when prices are high or to cover overheads. Also, consider contract farming: agree with a farmer to buy their entire crop at a fixed price (e.g., 30 shillings per kg) regardless of market. This gives them security and you stable supply.

Diversify products: During glut, make plain paste. During scarcity, make ready-to-use sauce with onions and spices, which sells for 7,000 shillings per jar. Also, package in smaller sizes (250g) for low-income customers. Never let your production stop completely. If tomatoes are too expensive, make pepper sauce or fruit jam using the same equipment. Keep your workers busy. Plan 3 months ahead using a simple calendar: mark harvest months, plant months, and school holidays (high demand).

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Scaling Up Production

This module gives you a practical, business-first reference for moving from a kitchen-scale tomato paste and sauce operation to a small commercial production unit. The focus is on decisions that affect cash flow, product quality, food safety, and your ability to serve retailers, hotels, and distributors consistently. Use this as a working document â print it, annotate it, and revisit it as your volumes grow.

1. When and How to Hire Your First Employee

Hire when your bottleneck is labour, not when you simply feel busy. Common triggers: you are turning down orders, working more than 60 hours per week on production alone, or making repeated errors in record-keeping and hygiene. In many African markets, your first hire is often a production assistant who also handles cleaning, packaging, and basic record-keeping. Pay fairly, provide written terms even if informal, and train on hygiene before any production task. Consider part-time or casual labour during peak tomato season, then convert to permanent once volumes stabilise.

2. Buying or Upgrading Equipment for Higher Output

Prioritise equipment that removes the hardest manual steps: pulping, sieving, and filling. A small pulper or sieve can double output with the same labour. A semi-automatic filler improves consistency and reduces spillage. Before buying, calculate payback: if a machine costs 3,000,000 TZS and saves 500,000 TZS per month in labour and waste, payback is six months. Buy locally where possible for spare parts and service. Always confirm voltage, fuel type, and water availability before purchase.

3. Organising a Small Production Line

Arrange the line so that raw tomatoes enter at one end and finished jars exit at the other, with no backtracking. Separate "dirty" areas (receiving, washing, waste) from "clean" areas (cooking, filling, sealing). Use colour-coded buckets and utensils. Keep a one-way flow to reduce cross-contamination. Post simple SOPs at each station in the local language. A well-organised line of 4â6 workers can produce 200â400 jars of 400g per day depending on equipment and tomato quality.

4. Managing Inventory of Raw Materials and Packaging

Tomatoes are perishable; jars, lids, and labels are not. Track both with a simple stock card or phone spreadsheet. Use FIFO (first in, first out). Keep minimum stock levels: enough jars and lids for two weeks of average sales, and enough tomatoes for no more than 2â3 days unless you have cold storage. Record every purchase and every use. This protects you from stockouts during peak demand and from spoilage losses.

5. Finding Reliable Suppliers for Tomatoes and Jars

Do not rely on one supplier. For tomatoes, work with 3â5 farmers or farmer groups within 50 km. Agree on variety, ripeness, and delivery schedule. For jars and lids, identify at least two suppliers â local glass manufacturers or importers â and test lid compatibility before bulk purchase. Negotiate written terms: price, quality grade, delivery, and payment. In many African markets, building a relationship with a supplier cooperative or aggregator can secure better prices and consistent volumes.

6. Planning for Seasonal Tomato Gluts and Price Fluctuations

Tomato prices in most African markets can fall by 50â70% during peak harvest and rise sharply in the dry season. Plan for this by:

  • Buying and processing intensively during glut periods to build finished-goods stock.
  • Investing in cold storage or solar drying for excess tomatoes if paste production capacity is limited.
  • Setting a target cost per jar and reviewing it monthly against market prices.
  • Holding 4â8 weeks of finished product stock to sell at stable prices during shortages.
  • Using contracts or advance agreements with farmers to smooth supply.

Tomato paste and sauce are low-acid or acidified products that can support dangerous bacteria if not properly processed. Never sell product from swollen, leaking, or damaged jars. Always use a tested recipe with correct acidity (pH 4.6 or below for acidified sauces) and adequate cooking time. Boil or sterilise jars and lids before filling. Keep raw and cooked product separate. Equipment such as pulpers, cookers, and sealers can cause severe burns or cuts â train all staff, use guards, and keep a first-aid kit on site. In case of suspected foodborne illness, stop sales immediately and contact your local public health authority.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Hire only when labour is the bottleneck; train on hygiene before production.
  • Upgrade equipment that removes manual pulping, sieving, or filling first.
  • Arrange production in a one-way flow: dirty to clean, raw to finished.
  • Track tomato, jar, lid, and label stock weekly; use FIFO and set reorder levels.
  • Work with at least three tomato suppliers and two jar/lid suppliers.
  • Buy and process heavily during seasonal gluts; hold 4â8 weeks of finished stock.
  • Never sell from damaged or swollen jars; test pH and cooking time.
  • Review cost per jar monthly against market prices and adjust pricing.
