1. Common Foods and Drinks

Start by learning the names of 20 everyday foods and drinks in Mandarin. For staples, say (mf n) for cooked rice, (mi ntio) for noodles, (mi nbo) for bread, and (jd n) for eggs. In many African markets, you can find rice, beans, and maize. The Mandarin for beans is (duzi), and maize is (ym). If you eat (msh) cassava you can ask for it by name. For drinks, (shu) is water, (ch) is tea, (kfi) is coffee, and (guzh) is juice. A bottle of water is (y png shu). When you buy food from a local vendor, you can say (w y o zhge) meaning 'I want this one.' Point and use that phrase. For quantities, use (y g) for one piece, (ling g) for two pieces. Prices are in (ku i) for yuan, but in Africa you might use local currency. Practice saying ? (dushao qin?) 'How much money?' That works everywhere. To say you are hungry, say (w le). Thirsty is (w k le). If you want to ask if a food is spicy, say ? (l b l ?). In many African countries, spicy food is common, so this is useful. Also learn (hoch) for 'delicious' and (b hoch) for 'not delicious.' When you visit a Chinese restaurant in Nairobi or Lagos, you can order (chof n) fried rice or (chomi n) fried noodles. For breakfast, (bozi) are steamed buns, and (zhu) is porridge. If you want to buy fruit, (pnggu) is apple, (xingjio) is banana, (chngzi) is orange. In markets, say (pinyi ydin) to ask for a lower price. Practice these words daily with a language partner. Write them on flashcards and stick them on your kitchen wall. In one week, you will recognize these foods when you hear them.

2. Ordering at a Restaurant

When you enter a Chinese restaurant in Africa, the staff may greet you with (hunyng gungln) 'Welcome.' You respond with (xixie). To ask for a table for two, say (ling g rn). The waiter will show you to a table. Then they may ask ? (n y o ch shnme?) 'What do you want to eat?' You can say (w y o zhge) and point to the menu. If you want a menu in English, ask ? (yu Yngwn c idn ma?). Many Chinese restaurants in Africa have Chinese menus, so this helps. To order a dish, say ... (w y o y g...) plus the dish name. For example, (w y o y g chof n) 'I want one fried rice.' If you want two, say (ling g). For drinks, say (w y o y png shu) 'I want one bottle of water.' To ask for no spicy, say (b y o l ). If you want it spicy, say (y o l ). If you need utensils, ask ? (yu ku izi ma?) 'Are there chopsticks?' Or ? (yu chzi ma?) 'Are there forks?' In many African settings, forks are common, so don't be shy. If you need more rice, say (z i li y wn mf n) 'Another bowl of rice, please.' To call the waiter, say (fwyun) 'Waiter.' That is polite. If you are with friends, you can say (wmen yq ch) 'We eat together.' In Chinese culture, dishes are shared. So order 2-3 dishes for two people. A common mistake is to order one dish per person; instead, order family style. If you don't understand, say (w tng b dng) 'I don't understand.' Then ask (qng z i shu y bi n) 'Please say it again.' Practice these phrases before you go. Role-play with a friend: one is waiter, one is customer. Do it three times. You will feel confident.

3. Expressing Likes and Dislikes with /

To say you like something, use (xhuan) before the food or activity. For example, (w xhuan ch mf n) 'I like to eat rice.' To say you don't like, add (b) before : (w b xhuan ch kgu) 'I don't like to eat bitter melon.' Note that changes tone to b before a fourth tone, but before (xhuan) it is b xhuan. If you want to ask someone what they like, say ? (n xhuan ch shnme?) 'What do you like to eat?' To answer, (w xhuan ch jru) 'I like to eat chicken.' In Africa, you might say (w xhuan ch y) 'I like to eat fish.' If you love something, you can say (w hn xhuan) 'I really like it.' If you hate it, say (w toy n) 'I hate it.' But is softer and more polite. When you are a guest at someone's home, if you don't like a food, say (w b t i xhuan) 'I don't quite like it.' That is polite. To say you prefer something, use (gng xhuan) 'like more.' For example, (w gng xhuan ch) 'I prefer tea.' If you want to ask if someone likes a food, say ? (n xhuan l de ma?) 'Do you like spicy food?' Answer: (xhuan) or (b xhuan). Practice with a list of 10 foods: rice, noodles, chicken, fish, beans, maize, cassava, tea, coffee, juice. For each, say or . Then ask a friend. In African markets, you can use these phrases when buying food. If a vendor offers you something you don't like, say (w b xhuan zhge) 'I don't like this.' Then point to what you want. This is direct but not rude. Remember that is a verb, so you can also say (w xhuan n) 'I like you' but that is for people, not food. Keep practicing daily.

4. Measure Words for Food and Portions

In Mandarin, you cannot say 'one rice' or 'two water.' You must use a measure word between the number and the noun. The most common measure word is (g). For example, (y g bozi) 'one steamed bun.' But many foods have specific measure words. For a bowl of rice or noodles, use (wn): (y wn mf n) 'one bowl of rice.' For a plate of food, use (pn): (y pn chomi n) 'one plate of fried noodles.' For a bottle of water or juice, use (png): (y png shu) 'one bottle of water.' For a cup of tea or coffee, use (bi): (y bi ch) 'one cup of tea.' For a piece of meat or fruit, use (ku i): (y ku i jru) 'one piece of chicken.' For a slice of bread, use (pi n): (y pi n mi nbo) 'one slice of bread.' For a bag of something, use (d i): (y d i m) 'one bag of rice.' For a dozen, use (d): (y d jd n) 'one dozen eggs.' For a kilogram, use (gngjn): (y gngjn ru) 'one kilogram of meat.' In African markets, you often buy by the heap or by the piece. You can say (sn g xingjio) 'three bananas.' Or (w g chngzi) 'five oranges.' To ask for a portion, say (w y o y wn) 'I want one bowl.' If you want two, say (ling wn). Note: (ling) is used instead of (r) before measure words. So 'two bowls' is , not . Practice with real objects: a cup, a bottle, a plate. Say the measure word with a number. For example, , , . When you order at a restaurant, use these: (y wn niru mi n) 'one bowl of beef noodles.' (ling png kl) 'two bottles of cola.' (sn wn mf n) 'three bowls of rice.' Write these on cards and practice daily.

5. Paying the Bill and Tipping Culture

When you finish eating, you need to pay. In Mandarin, ask (midn) 'the bill, please.' Or say (jizh ng) 'settle the bill.' The waiter will bring a bill or tell you the total. To ask how much, say ? (dushao qin?). If the total is 50 yuan, they say (wsh ku i). In Africa, prices are in local currency, but you can still use the phrase. If you want to pay by cash, say (w f xi njn). If by card, say (w shu k). In many African restaurants, cash is preferred. If you are splitting the bill, say (wmen fnki f) 'We pay separately.' Or (w qngk) 'It's my treat.' That means you pay for everyone. In Chinese culture, it is common to fight over the bill, but in Africa, splitting is fine. Tipping is not mandatory in China, but in Africa, tipping is appreciated. In Chinese restaurants in Africa, a 10% tip is polite if service was good. You can leave 10% on the table or give it directly. To say 'keep the change,' say (b yng zho le). That means 'no need for change.' If you want to tip, say (zh sh xiofi) 'This is a tip.' In some African countries, tipping 5-10% is standard. In others, rounding up is enough. Ask a local friend what is normal. When you pay, say (xixie) to the waiter. If you need a receipt, ask ? (yu fpi o ma?) 'Is there a receipt?' In business settings, you may need one. Practice a dialogue: You: Waiter: You: Waiter: You: Do this three times. Also learn (t i gu le) 'Too expensive.' But use it politely. If the bill seems wrong, say (zhge b du) 'This is not right.' Then ask (qng z i su n y bi n) 'Please calculate again.' Always check your bill before paying.

6. Role-Play: At a Noodle Shop

In this lesson, you will practice a full conversation at a noodle shop. Imagine you enter a small shop in Nairobi or Lagos that serves Chinese noodles. The waiter says (hunyng gungln). You say (xixie). Waiter: ? (j g rn?) 'How many people?' You: (y g rn) 'One person.' Waiter: (qng zu) 'Please sit.' You sit down. Waiter: ? (n y o ch shnme?) 'What do you want to eat?' You: (w y o y wn niru mi n) 'I want one bowl of beef noodles.' Waiter: ? (y o b y o l ?) 'Do you want spicy?' You: (y o l ) 'Yes, spicy.' Waiter: ? (h shnme?) 'What to drink?' You: (y png shu) 'One bottle of water.' Waiter: (ho de, qng dng) 'Okay, please wait.' After eating, you say (midn). Waiter: (ygng snsh ku i) 'Total 30 yuan.' You: (w f xi njn) 'I pay cash.' You give 50. Waiter gives change. You: (b yng zho le) 'Keep the change.' Waiter: (xixie). You: (z iji n) 'Goodbye.' Now practice this role-play with a partner. One plays waiter, one plays customer. Swap roles. Do it three times. Pay attention to tones: (y wn), (niru), (mi n). If you make a mistake, say (dubuq) 'Sorry.' And (qng z i shu y bi n) 'Please say it again.' Use real props: a bowl, a bottle, a menu. Write the dialogue on a card. Practice until you can say it without looking. Then try variations: order (ling wn mi n) for two people. Or ask ? (yu jiozi ma?) 'Are there dumplings?' If they say (miyu) 'No' then order something else. This role-play builds confidence for real situations. In many African cities, Chinese noodle shops are common. Go to one and try your Mandarin. The staff will appreciate your effort.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Food and Dining in Mandarin for African Agribusiness Professionals

This Practical Reference lesson consolidates the six core lessons of the Food and Dining module into a single, desk-ready resource. It is written for African agribusiness professionals exporters, procurement officers, trade facilitators, and entrepreneurs who need to function confidently in Mandarin-speaking business dining environments across China, Singapore, Taiwan, and Chinese-owned enterprises operating in Africa. The focus is on commercial outcomes: negotiating over a meal, reading a menu for procurement-relevant ingredients, ordering without dietary missteps, and handling the bill in a way that respects both Chinese and African hospitality norms.

Food is never "just food" in Chinese business culture. A shared meal is a trust-building instrument, and your ability to navigate the table signals commercial seriousness. For African exporters of coffee, cocoa, sesame, cashew, hibiscus, and specialty foods, the dining table is often where the first real relationship and the first real order is formed.

SAFETY WARNING Food Allergies & Dietary Restrictions

If you have a serious food allergy (peanut, shellfish, gluten) or strict religious dietary requirements (halal, kosher), do not rely on casual phrasebook Mandarin alone. Cross-contamination is common in shared-plate Chinese dining, and "" (b y o hushng no peanuts) may not be understood as an allergy warning. Carry a printed card in Chinese stating your allergy, inform your host in advance through a trusted bilingual colleague, and carry prescribed medication (e.g., epinephrine auto-injector). For halal requirements, confirm the restaurant is (qngzhn) in advance many business districts have certified halal venues, but mixed banquets often do not.

  1. Learn five dishes and five drinks by heart , , , , , . These cover 80% of banquet menus.
  2. Master the measure word (fn) for portions, and (bi) for cups "" (y bi ch) and "" (y fn jiozi).
  3. Always order one vegetable dish if you are hosting it signals balance and care for the guest's health.
  4. Learn "" before any trip to Sichuan, Hunan, or Chongqing.
  5. Expect the bill fight. The host pays. If you are the guest, offer once, then accept gracefully. If you are the host, insist firmly but politely.
  6. Carry WeChat Pay or Alipay. Cash and foreign cards are increasingly refused in mainland China. Set up before you travel.
  7. Always request (fpi o) if the meal is a business expense this is the only legally valid receipt.
  8. Never discuss price or contract terms before the meal is ordered. Relationship first, business second.

Africa Context: Why This Matters for Your Business

China is Africa's largest trading partner, and food is the primary social lubricant in Chinese commercial culture. Whether you are a Kenyan coffee exporter meeting a Guangzhou roaster, a Nigerian sesame trader visiting a Shandong oil mill, or a Ghanaian cocoa cooperative negotiating with a Shanghai confectioner, the dining table is where trust is built before contracts are signed. Knowing that (ch) is served before business, that the host orders for the table, and that refusing a toast requires a polite formula these are not cultural trivia; they are commercial competencies.

Conversely, African hospitality has its own grammar. When you host Chinese buyers in Nairobi, Accra, or Addis Ababa, you can reciprocate by explaining your own dishes ugali, jollof, injera using the same Mandarin phrases you learned here. This mutual exchange deepens the relationship and positions you as a partner, not merely a supplier.
