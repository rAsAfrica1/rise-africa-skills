Lessons

1. Separating business money from personal money

When you mix business money with personal money, you never know if your block business is making a profit or losing money. You might spend business cash on school fees or food, then fail to buy cement for a customer's order. The first step is to open a separate bank account or mobile money wallet just for the business. In many African countries, you can open a basic business account with a small deposit. If you cannot open a bank account, use a separate mobile money wallet (like M-Pesa, Airtel Money, or MTN Mobile Money) only for business. Never use that wallet for personal needs.

Next, pay yourself a fixed salary from the business. For example, if your block business earns 500,000 shillings per month, you might pay yourself 150,000 shillings as salary. The rest stays in the business account for buying cement, sand, and paying workers. This way, you know exactly how much you can spend personally without touching business capital. Write down your salary amount and stick to it.

Keep all business receipts and records in one place. Use a notebook or a simple app like a spreadsheet on your phone. Every time you spend business money, write it down. Every time you receive money from a sale, write it down. At the end of the week, compare your business wallet balance with your records. If they don't match, find out why. This habit stops you from accidentally using business money for personal things.

Finally, treat your business like a separate person. If you need to borrow from the business, write it down as a loan and pay it back. If you take stock (like cement) for personal use, record it as a sale to yourself. This discipline builds trust with suppliers and customers, and it helps your business grow. Many small block makers fail because they eat their capital. Don't be one of them.

2. Recording every sale, purchase, and expense daily

You cannot manage what you do not measure. Every day, you must write down three things: sales (money coming in), purchases (materials you buy), and expenses (other costs like transport, labour, or airtime). Use a simple exercise book or a notebook. Draw four columns: Date, Description, Money In, Money Out. At the end of each day, sit down for five minutes and fill in every transaction. Even small sales of 5,000 shillings must be recorded. If you sold 50 blocks at 500 shillings each, write '50 blocks x 500 = 25,000' under Money In. If you bought 2 bags of cement at 30,000 each, write '2 bags cement = 60,000' under Money Out.

For purchases, always ask for a receipt if possible. If not, write the seller's name and phone number. For expenses like paying a worker, write the worker's name and what you paid for. For example: 'John - labour for mixing - 10,000'. This helps you remember and also prevents arguments later. If you use mobile money, your phone records every transaction, but still write it in your book. That way you have a backup if your phone is lost.

At the end of the week, add up all Money In and all Money Out. Subtract Money Out from Money In to get your weekly profit or loss. If you have a loss, look at your records to see where you spent too much. Maybe you bought sand at a higher price, or you forgot to record a sale. Daily recording helps you catch mistakes early.

You can also use a simple app like 'Cash Book' or 'Money Manager' on your phone. But a notebook works fine. The key is consistency. Record every single day, even if you are tired. After one month, you will see patterns: which days are busiest, which expenses are highest, and how much profit you really make. This knowledge is power.

3. Calculating monthly profit and loss

At the end of every month, you must calculate your profit or loss. Profit is what remains after you subtract all your business expenses from your total sales. Loss is when your expenses are more than your sales. To do this, you need your daily records. Total up all the money that came in from selling blocks, cement, or any other product. That is your gross income. Then total up all the money you spent on cement, sand, labour, transport, rent, airtime, and any other business cost. That is your total expenses. Subtract expenses from income. If the answer is positive, you made a profit. If negative, you made a loss.

For example, in one month you sold 1,000 blocks at 500 shillings each, earning 500,000 shillings. You also sold 10 bags of cement at 35,000 each, earning 350,000. Total income = 850,000. Your expenses: cement 300,000, sand 100,000, labour 150,000, transport 50,000, rent 50,000, airtime 10,000. Total expenses = 660,000. Profit = 850,000 - 660,000 = 190,000 shillings. That is your monthly profit.

But be careful: some expenses are not paid every month. For example, you might buy a mould once a year. Divide that cost by 12 and include it each month as a 'depreciation' or 'equipment' expense. Also, if you took a loan, include the interest as an expense. If you paid yourself a salary, include that too. Only then will you know your true profit.

If you made a loss, do not panic. Look at your records and find out why. Maybe you sold fewer blocks because of rain. Maybe you increased worker wages. Maybe you forgot to record some sales. Adjust your plan for next month. If you made a profit, decide how much to save, how much to reinvest, and how much to take as salary. Calculating monthly profit and loss is like checking your health. Do it every month without fail.

4. Budgeting for cement, sand, and labour in advance

A budget is a plan for how you will spend your money before you earn it. For a block-making business, your main costs are cement, sand, and labour. To budget, you need to know how much of each material you need to produce a certain number of blocks. For example, one bag of cement can make about 30 to 40 blocks, depending on the mix. One trip of sand (usually 3 tonnes) can make about 300 blocks. Labour is usually paid per block or per day.

Suppose you want to produce 1,000 blocks next month. You will need about 25 bags of cement (if 40 blocks per bag) and about 3.5 trips of sand. If cement is 30,000 per bag, that is 750,000. If sand is 50,000 per trip, that is 175,000. Labour at 100 shillings per block is 100,000. Total budget = 1,025,000 shillings. You also need to add transport, water, and maybe rent. So your total budget might be 1,200,000 shillings.

Now, how will you get that money? You can use savings from last month's profit, or you can take orders with a deposit. Many customers will pay 50% upfront. If you get orders for 1,000 blocks at 500 each, you can collect 250,000 as deposit, which helps buy cement. But you must plan carefully so you don't run out of money before finishing the job. Write your budget on a piece of paper and stick it on your wall. Every time you buy something, check if it fits the budget.

Also budget for unexpected costs. Prices of cement and sand can go up. Labour may demand more. So add 10% extra to your budget for emergencies. If you stick to your budget, you will not be caught off guard. And if you finish a job under budget, that extra money is your bonus profit. Budgeting is not about restricting yourself; it is about making sure you always have what you need to complete the work and make a profit.

5. Saving for equipment and rainy-day emergencies

In the block-making business, equipment like moulds, wheelbarrows, shovels, and a vibrating table can break or wear out. And emergencies happen: a family illness, a sudden price increase, or a customer who doesn't pay. If you don't save, you will be forced to borrow at high interest or use your business capital, which can kill your business. The solution is to save a small amount every week or every month. Even 5,000 shillings per week adds up to 260,000 shillings in a year. That can buy a new mould or cover an emergency.

Open a separate savings account or mobile money savings wallet. Many mobile money services have a 'savings' option where you can lock money for a certain period. Or you can join a village savings group (VSLA) where you contribute weekly and can borrow from the group. But be careful: only save what you can afford to keep aside. A good rule is to save at least 10% of your monthly profit. If you made 200,000 shillings profit, save 20,000. Put it away immediately before you spend it.

Set specific savings goals. For example, 'I want to buy a new vibrating table for 500,000 shillings in 6 months.' Then calculate how much you need to save each month: 500,000 divided by 6 = about 83,333. If that is too much, extend the time or buy a cheaper table. For emergencies, aim to save at least one month's worth of business expenses. If your monthly expenses are 600,000, try to save 600,000 as an emergency fund. This may take time, but start small.

Keep your savings separate from your daily business money. Do not touch it unless it is a real emergency or you have reached your goal. Tell your family that this money is for the business, so they don't pressure you to spend it. Saving is a habit. The more you save, the more secure your business becomes. And when you have savings, you can take advantage of opportunities, like buying cement in bulk at a discount.

6. Using mobile money and bank accounts for business

Mobile money and bank accounts are powerful tools for managing your block business. They keep your money safe, help you track transactions, and make it easy for customers to pay you. In most African countries, mobile money is faster and cheaper than banks for small businesses. You can open a mobile money account with your national ID and phone number. Use a separate SIM card or wallet for business if possible. When a customer pays, they send money to your business number. You get an SMS confirmation, which is a record. You can also pay suppliers and workers directly from your phone.

A bank account is better for larger sums and for saving. You can open a business account with a small deposit. Banks offer cheque books, debit cards, and sometimes loans. But banks may have higher fees and require more paperwork. If you are just starting, mobile money is enough. As you grow, open a bank account for savings and big payments. Always keep your PIN secret and never share it. If you lose your phone, report immediately to your mobile money provider so they can block the account.

Use mobile money to separate business and personal money. For example, have one wallet for business and another for personal. When you make a sale, the money goes into the business wallet. When you need to pay yourself a salary, transfer a fixed amount to your personal wallet. This makes it easy to see how much the business has. Also, many mobile money apps have a 'statement' feature. You can request a statement for the month and use it to calculate profit and loss. This saves you from writing everything down manually, though you should still keep a notebook as backup.

Be aware of transaction fees. Sending money can cost a small percentage. Factor these fees into your prices. For example, if you sell a block for 500 shillings and the mobile money fee is 1%, you actually receive 495. So you might charge 505 to cover the fee. Also, avoid keeping large amounts in mobile money for long because of fraud risks. Transfer to a bank or savings account. With good use of mobile money and bank accounts, your business will be more organised, secure, and ready to grow.

<h2>Practical Reference: Financial Management and Record Keeping for Cement &amp; Blocks Businesses</h2>

<p>In the cement and blocks trade, money moves fast. A customer pays a deposit for 500 blocks, you buy 20 bags of cement, pay two casual labourers, fuel the mixer, and settle a sand supplier all before lunch. Without a simple system, cash leaks. This practical reference gives you the exact tools, formats, and habits used by successful block yards across Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana, Tanzania, and Uganda. It is written for the owner-operator who sells blocks by the piece, runs a small mixer, and wants to grow from a hand-to-mouth operation into a registered, bankable business.</p>

<p>Financial management in this trade is not accounting theory. It is the difference between having money for cement on Monday and losing a contract because you cannot buy raw materials. The core discipline is simple: separate your money, record every transaction daily, know your monthly profit, budget ahead, save for equipment and emergencies, and use mobile money and bank accounts properly.</p>

<h3>1. Separating Business Money from Personal Money</h3>

<p>The single biggest killer of small block businesses is mixed money. When school fees, rent, and block sales sit in the same pocket or mobile wallet, you cannot tell whether the business is profitable. Worse, you spend working capital on personal needs and then cannot buy cement for a confirmed order.</p>

<p>Open a separate business account even a simple mobile money account registered in the business name, or a bank account if you have registration documents. All block sales, customer deposits, and supplier payments go through this account. Pay yourself a fixed monthly salary or drawing, transferred to your personal account. This is not bureaucracy; it is how you build a credit history for equipment loans and prove income to suppliers who might offer you cement on credit.</p>

<p>In markets like Kano, Kumasi, or Nakuru, many block makers operate informally. You can still separate money using a dedicated mobile money wallet (for example, an M-Pesa or MTN MoMo business wallet) and a physical cash box with a lock for small daily transactions. The rule is: one wallet or box for business, one for personal. Never mix.</p>

<h3>2. Recording Every Sale, Purchase, and Expense Daily</h3>

<p>Record keeping is not for the taxman alone. It tells you which block sizes sell best, which customers pay late, and whether your price covers your true cost. Use a simple exercise book or a free spreadsheet on your phone. Record every transaction the same day not at the end of the week.</p>

<p>At minimum, capture: date, description, quantity, unit price, total amount, payment method (cash, mobile money, bank, credit), and customer or supplier name. For credit sales, note the balance owed and the agreed payment date. For purchases, note the supplier and whether it was cash or credit.</p>

<h3>3. Calculating Monthly Profit and Loss</h3>

<p>Profit is not the cash in your pocket at month-end. It is what remains after all costs. For a block yard, the formula is:</p>

<p><strong>Profit = Total Sales Cost of Materials Operating Expenses</strong></p>

<p>Cost of materials includes cement, sand, stone dust, water, and any additives. Operating expenses include labour, fuel or electricity, transport, rent, phone and mobile money charges, repairs, and depreciation on your mixer and moulds. Depreciation matters: if your mixer cost 150,000 and lasts five years, set aside 2,500 per month as a replacement cost.</p>

<h3>4. Budgeting for Cement, Sand, and Labour in Advance</h3>

<p>A budget is a plan for money you have not yet received. Before the month starts, estimate how many blocks you will sell, then work backwards to calculate how much cement, sand, and labour you need. This prevents the classic mistake of accepting a large order and then discovering you have no cash for cement.</p>

<p>Use a simple production budget. If one 50 kg bag of cement produces approximately 30 six-inch blocks (depending on the mix ratio and block size), and you plan to sell 4,000 blocks, you need at least 134 bags. Add 10% wastage say 148 bags. Multiply by your local cement price to get your cement budget. Do the same for sand, stone dust, and labour.</p>

<h3>5. Saving for Equipment and Rainy-Day Emergencies</h3>

<p>Block making is equipment-intensive. Mixers break, moulds wear out, and generators fail. If you have no savings, a single breakdown can shut you down for weeks. Set aside a fixed percentage of every sale even 5% into two separate savings pots: one for equipment replacement and one for emergencies.</p>

<p>Use a mobile money savings wallet or a bank savings account that is not linked to your daily transaction wallet. In Kenya, M-Shwari and similar products allow you to lock funds. In Nigeria, many microfinance banks offer target savings. In Ghana, susu collectors and rural banks provide similar services. The point is to make the money slightly difficult to access so you are not tempted to spend it on non-essentials.</p>

<h3>6. Using Mobile Money and Bank Accounts for Business</h3>

<p>Mobile money is the backbone of small business finance in Africa. It is fast, traceable, and increasingly accepted by suppliers. Use it deliberately:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Receive payments:</strong> Give customers your business mobile money number, not your personal one. This creates a transaction record you can reconcile monthly.</li>
<li><strong>Pay suppliers:</strong> Paying cement and sand suppliers via mobile money gives you a digital receipt and reduces the risk of disputes.</li>
<li><strong>Pay labour:</strong> Where possible, pay casual workers via mobile money. It is safer than carrying cash and creates a record for your wage budget.</li>
<li><strong>Build credit history:</strong> A bank account with regular deposits and withdrawals is evidence of business activity. This helps when applying for an equipment loan or a supplier credit line.</li>
<li><strong>Reconcile daily:</strong> Compare your mobile money statement with your record book every evening. Discrepancies are early warnings of theft or error.</li>
</ul>

<p>In countries like Tanzania, Uganda, and Rwanda, mobile money interoperability now allows transfers between networks, making it easier to pay suppliers on different platforms. Use this to your advantage, but always factor transaction charges into your pricing.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul>
<li> Business money and personal money are in separate accounts or wallets.</li>
<li> Every sale, purchase, and expense is recorded the same day.</li>
<li> Monthly profit and loss is calculated before you pay yourself.</li>
<li> Cement, sand, and labour are budgeted at least one month ahead.</li>
<li> At least 5% of every sale goes into equipment and emergency savings.</li>
<li> Daily takings are deposited into mobile money or bank the same day.</li>
<li> Mobile money and bank statements are reconciled with your record book weekly.</li>
<li> You know your cost per block and your break-even selling price.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Bottom line:</strong> In the cement and blocks business, the operators who survive and grow are not the ones who mix the best concrete. They are the ones who know their numbers, separate their money, and plan ahead. Start with one habit this week: record every transaction daily. Add the others one by one. Within three months, you will see your profit clearly and you will be able to prove it to a bank, a supplier, or a partner.</p>
