1. Where to Find Lemongrass in Local Markets and Farms

Lemongrass (Cymbopogon citratus) grows wild in many parts of Africa, but for oil production you need a reliable source. Start by visiting local markets that sell fresh herbs and traditional medicine. In countries like Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana, and South Africa, lemongrass is often sold in bunches by women who harvest from their gardens. Ask vendors where they get it they may connect you to farmers. A single mature lemongrass clump can yield 200-300 leaves per harvest, and you can harvest 3-4 times per year. Look for thick, pale green stalks with a strong lemon smell when crushed. Avoid stalks that are yellowing or dry. If you want to grow your own, lemongrass propagates easily from stalks: cut the bottom 5 cm of a fresh stalk, place it in water for 1-2 weeks until roots appear, then plant in well-drained soil. It needs 6-8 hours of sunlight daily and 800-1500 mm of rainfall per year. In drier areas, irrigate weekly. You can also find lemongrass at large farms that supply supermarkets or herbal processors. Contact agricultural extension officers or farmer cooperatives in your district they often know who grows it commercially. In West Africa, check with ministries of agriculture for lists of registered herb farmers. When buying from farms, ask for the harvest date. Freshly harvested lemongrass (within 2-3 days) gives the highest oil yield. If you must store it, keep it in a cool, shaded area and use within 5 days. For oil extraction, you need about 100 kg of fresh leaves to produce 0.5-1 kg of oil, depending on the variety and distillation method. So secure a steady supply before investing in a still.

2. Identifying Eucalyptus Trees in Your Area and Harvesting Leaves

Eucalyptus trees are common in many African countries, especially in South Africa, Kenya, Ethiopia, and Zimbabwe. They are tall (often 20-40 meters), with smooth, peeling bark and long, narrow leaves that hang downwards. The leaves are leathery, grey-green, and have a strong medicinal smell when crushed. To confirm you have eucalyptus, look for the distinctive operculum (a cap) on the flower buds, and the woody seed capsules that look like small cups. The most common species for oil production are Eucalyptus globulus (blue gum) and Eucalyptus citriodora (lemon-scented gum). E. globulus has a camphor-like smell, while E. citriodora smells like lemon. For oil extraction, harvest mature leaves from the lower branches these have higher oil content than young leaves. The best time to harvest is during the dry season when oil concentration is highest. Use a sharp pair of pruning shears or a sickle to cut branches, then strip the leaves by hand. Wear gloves because the oil can irritate skin. A single mature tree can yield 20-50 kg of leaves per harvest, and you can harvest every 6-12 months without killing the tree. Never cut the main trunk only side branches. After harvesting, spread leaves in a thin layer in a shaded, well-ventilated area to dry for 2-3 days. This reduces moisture and prevents mould. You need about 50-100 kg of fresh leaves to produce 1 kg of eucalyptus oil. If you dont have trees on your land, ask local forestry departments or tea estates they often have eucalyptus plantations and may sell leaves. In Kenya, for example, the Kenya Forestry Research Institute (KEFRI) can advise on sustainable harvesting. Always get permission before harvesting from public or private land.

3. Tea Tree: How to Recognize and Source It in Africa

Tea tree (Melaleuca alternifolia) is native to Australia, but it is now grown in several African countries, including South Africa, Kenya, Zimbabwe, and Zambia. It is a small tree or shrub (up to 6 meters) with narrow, needle-like leaves and papery, layered bark. The leaves are soft, aromatic, and have a strong medicinal, camphor-like smell. Tea tree grows best in warm, humid areas with 800-1200 mm annual rainfall and well-drained, acidic soil. It cannot tolerate frost. If you are in a high-altitude region, you may need to grow it in a greenhouse or under shade cloth. To source tea tree, contact commercial essential oil farms in South Africa (e.g., in Mpumalanga or KwaZulu-Natal) or Kenya (e.g., around Mount Kenya). Some nurseries sell tea tree seedlings. You can also propagate from cuttings: take 10-15 cm semi-hardwood cuttings, dip in rooting hormone, and plant in a mist bed. Roots form in 4-6 weeks. For oil production, harvest the leafy tips (top 30 cm of branches) when the plant is 12-18 months old. Harvest in the morning after dew has dried. You need about 100-200 kg of fresh leaves to produce 1 kg of tea tree oil. After harvest, distill immediately or dry in a shaded, well-ventilated area for 2-3 days. Tea tree oil is prone to oxidation, so use fresh material. If you cannot find tea tree locally, you can substitute with other Melaleuca species that grow in Africa, such as Melaleuca quinquenervia (broad-leaved paperbark), but the oil composition differs. Always test a small batch before large-scale production. For sourcing, join farmer groups on Facebook or WhatsApp many African essential oil producers share leads on where to buy seedlings and leaves.

4. Buying Dried Herbs vs. Fresh for Oil Extraction

When extracting essential oils, you can use either fresh or dried plant material, but each has trade-offs. Fresh herbs generally give higher oil yield and better aroma because volatile compounds havent evaporated. For lemongrass, fresh leaves yield about 0.5-1% oil by weight, while dried leaves yield 0.3-0.7%. For eucalyptus, fresh leaves yield 1-2% oil, dried leaves 0.5-1%. For tea tree, fresh leaves yield 1-2% oil, dried leaves 0.5-1.5%. However, fresh material is bulky and perishable. You must distill within 2-3 days of harvest, or the leaves will ferment and lose oil. Dried herbs are easier to store and transport. You can dry them in a shaded, well-ventilated area for 3-7 days until they are brittle but still green. Never dry in direct sunlight that destroys oils. A simple test: bend a leaf; if it snaps, its dry enough. Dried herbs can be stored in burlap sacks in a cool, dark room for up to 6 months. But beware: dried herbs absorb moisture and can grow mould. Check for musty smell or black spots. For small-scale producers, fresh is better if you have a still nearby. For larger operations, drying allows you to collect material over time and run the still continuously. Cost-wise, fresh herbs are cheaper per kg because they contain water, but you need more weight. For example, to get 1 kg of lemongrass oil, you need about 100-200 kg fresh leaves or 150-300 kg dried leaves. Calculate your transport and storage costs. In Africa, many farmers sell fresh herbs at markets. If you buy dried, ensure it was dried properly ask the seller how. Avoid herbs that are brown, dusty, or smell weak. Always buy from a trusted source and test a small batch before committing to a large order.

5. Working with Local Farmers to Secure Steady Supply

To run a successful essential oil business, you need a reliable supply of raw material. Working with local farmers is the best way to ensure this. Start by identifying farmers who already grow lemongrass, eucalyptus, or tea tree in your area. Visit their farms, see their practices, and discuss your needs. Be clear about quantity, quality, and timing. For example, if you need 500 kg of fresh lemongrass per week, tell them. Offer a fair price check local market rates and add a small premium for consistent quality. A written contract, even a simple one, protects both sides. Include: crop type, quantity, harvest date, price per kg, payment terms, and quality standards (e.g., no mould, no pesticides). Train farmers on proper harvesting and post-harvest handling. Show them how to cut lemongrass at the base, how to strip eucalyptus leaves without damaging trees, and how to dry herbs in shade. Provide tools if needed pruning shears, sacks, or drying racks. Many farmers will appreciate this because it reduces waste and increases their income. Consider forming a cooperative or farmer group. This allows you to aggregate supply from many smallholders and negotiate better transport rates. In Kenya, for example, the Tea Tree Growers Association helps members access markets. You can also offer advance payments or loans for seeds and seedlings. This builds loyalty and ensures they prioritize your order. Plan for seasonality: lemongrass grows year-round in tropical areas but slows in dry seasons. Eucalyptus leaves are best in dry season. Tea tree harvests peak in summer. So diversify your sources have at least three farmers for each crop. Keep records of each farmers performance: yield, quality, timeliness. Pay on time, every time. Trust is your most valuable asset. With a steady supply, you can focus on distillation and marketing.

6. Checking Quality: How to Tell Good Raw Material from Bad

Quality raw material is the foundation of good essential oil. Whether you buy fresh or dried herbs, you must inspect them before distillation. Heres how. For lemongrass: look for bright green, firm stalks. The leaves should be long and slender, with a strong lemon scent when crushed. Avoid stalks that are yellow, brown, or slimy. Check for mould a grey or white fuzz. Smell the cut end: it should be intensely lemony, not musty. For eucalyptus: leaves should be grey-green, leathery, and unbroken. Crush a leaf it should release a strong camphor or lemon smell. Avoid leaves with black spots, holes (insect damage), or a dull colour. For tea tree: leaves should be dark green, needle-like, and aromatic. Avoid leaves that are dry and brittle (unless you are buying dried) or have a weak smell. Moisture content matters. Fresh herbs should be turgid (not wilted). Dried herbs should be brittle but not powder-dry. A simple test: take a handful and squeeze. If it feels damp or sticks together, its too wet and will mould. If it crumbles to dust, its too dry and has lost oil. Aim for 10-15% moisture for dried herbs. You can buy a cheap moisture meter (around $20) or use the salt test: mix a sample with dry salt; if salt clumps, moisture is high. Also check for contaminants: soil, stones, insects, or other plants. A good supplier will sort and clean. Ask for a sample before buying in bulk. Distill a small batch (e.g., 1 kg) and measure oil yield. If yield is below expected (e.g., less than 0.3% for lemongrass), reject the lot. Finally, trust your nose. Good raw material smells clean, fresh, and true to the plant. Bad material smells mouldy, fermented, or chemical. Never distill bad material it wastes time and fuel, and the oil will be poor quality.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Sourcing and Identifying Lemongrass, Eucalyptus, and Tea Tree in Africa

This practical reference is the working toolbox for the module. It consolidates the identification markers, sourcing channels, quality tests, and farmer-engagement practices covered across the six lessons, so you can move from a market stall or a farm gate to a drum of distillable raw material without guesswork. Every decision here has a commercial consequence: the wrong species, the wrong harvest stage, or the wrong drying method will show up as a lower oil yield, a rejected batch, or a buyer who does not come back. Treat this page as the checklist you carry to the field, not as background reading.

Across Africa, the three crops occupy very different supply positions. Lemongrass (Cymbopogon citratus and C. flexuosus) is widely grown and traded as a fresh culinary and medicinal herb, which means raw material is abundant but often fragmented and inconsistently handled. Eucalyptus is a plantation and woodlot tree across Southern and Eastern Africa, so leaf material is a by-product of forestry and timber operations rather than a dedicated crop. Tea tree (Melaleuca alternifolia) is the outlier: it is native to Australia and is grown commercially at scale in only a few African locations, so sourcing it reliably requires contracted production rather than spot-market buying. Your sourcing strategy should reflect these differences rather than treating the three as interchangeable.

Fresh versus dried: what the buyer and the still require

Fresh and dried raw material are not interchangeable. Fresh material gives higher yields of lighter, top-note-rich oil in some species but spoils within days and requires distillation close to the field. Dried material is storable and transportable, which matters if your still is far from the farm, but over-drying or sun-drying can drive off volatile compounds and raise the risk of mould. The decision is therefore commercial as much as technical: match the form to your logistics, your still capacity, and your buyer's specification.

SAFETY WARNING Read before handling or distilling

Essential oils are concentrated and can cause skin burns, respiratory irritation, and poisoning if ingested. Never apply undiluted oil to skin, never ingest tea tree or eucalyptus oil, and keep all oils away from children and open flames. Eucalyptus and tea tree oils are highly flammable; store in sealed, labelled containers away from heat and direct sun. During distillation, ensure the still area is ventilated, keep a fire extinguisher rated for flammable liquids within reach, and never distil alone. If oil contacts eyes or skin, flush with clean water for 15 minutes and seek medical attention. If swallowed, do not induce vomiting contact a poison centre or clinic immediately. Pregnant women, asthmatics, and people with epilepsy should avoid prolonged exposure to camphoraceous vapours.

Source: World Health Organization, WHO Guidelines on Good Agricultural and Collection Practices (GACP) for Medicinal Plants (WHO, 2003); and International Programme on Chemical Safety, ICSC entries for Eucalyptus oil and Melaleuca oil (WHO/IPCS).

Working with local farmers to secure steady supply

Spot-market buying is cheap until it fails. A distillery running below capacity because a trader could not deliver is losing money every hour. The alternative is a contracted farmer network, which in the African context usually means smallholders organised through a cooperative or an outgrower scheme. The commercial logic is straightforward: you provide a guaranteed price and, where possible, inputs or training; the farmer provides volume, consistency, and traceability. Pay on delivery, not on promise, and record every batch against the farmer's name traceability is what allows you to prove origin to an export buyer and to fix a quality problem at its source.

Practical terms to agree in writing: species and variety, minimum quantity per delivery, moisture and cleanliness standard, delivery schedule, price and price-review mechanism, and who bears transport. For tea tree, where African supply is thin, you will likely need to co-invest in planting material and wait two to three years for first commercial harvest, so treat it as a medium-term contract rather than a seasonal purchase. For lemongrass and eucalyptus, you can often start with existing plantings and tighten standards over one or two seasons.

Quality checks: telling good raw material from bad

Run these checks before you pay or before the material enters the still. A simple field kit a clean cloth, a moisture meter or a salt-and-jar test, and your nose will catch most problems.

  • Colour: Lemongrass should be pale green to light brown when dried, not black or grey. Eucalyptus leaves should be dull green to olive, not brown and brittle. Tea tree needles should retain a green-grey tone.
  • Aroma: Crush a small sample in your hand. The aroma should match the species profile in Table 1. A camphor or musty note in lemongrass, or a weak aroma in eucalyptus, signals poor handling or the wrong species.
  • Moisture: Dried material should snap, not bend. A handful squeezed and released should not clump. Target 1012% moisture; above 14% risks mould in storage.
  • Foreign matter: Check for soil, stones, twigs, other species, and insect damage. Contamination lowers yield and can clog the still.
  • Mould and pests: Reject any batch with visible mould, musty smell, or live insects. Mould is a health risk and a buyer-rejection trigger.
  • Consistency: Compare the batch against your last accepted batch. Drift in colour or aroma across deliveries usually means a change in species, harvest stage, or drying method.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Confirm species using at least three markers from Table 1 before agreeing a price.
  2. Confirm the harvested part mature eucalyptus leaves, lemongrass blades, tea tree terminal branchlets.
  3. Check moisture dried material must snap, not bend; target 1012%.
  4. Run the aroma test on a crushed sample and compare to the expected profile.
  5. Inspect for mould, pests, soil, and foreign species; reject contaminated batches on the spot.
  6. Record every batch farmer name, date, weight, moisture, and quality result.
  7. Agree terms in writing with each farmer: species, quantity, standard, schedule, price, transport.
  8. Store correctly dry, dark, ventilated, off the floor, away from fuel and chemicals.

Bottom line: the producer who wins in African essential oils is the one who controls raw material quality at the source. Identify correctly, buy on written terms, test before you pay, and record every batch. That discipline is what turns a seasonal market purchase into a repeatable, export-ready supply chain.
