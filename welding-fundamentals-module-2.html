Lessons

1. Hand Tools for Welding and Cutting

As a welder in Africa, you will often work without electricity, so hand tools are your lifeline. Start with a good quality chipping hammerthe kind with a spring handle reduces shock. Use it to remove slag after each weld pass. A wire brush (stainless steel for stainless steel, carbon steel for mild steel) cleans the weld before and after. For cutting, you need a hacksaw with 18 or 24 teeth per inch blade for metal. In many workshops, you'll also find a cold chisel and hammer for cutting rivets or bolts. Always wear safety glasses when using these tools.

For oxy-fuel cutting, you need a cutting torch, oxygen and acetylene cylinders, regulators, hoses, and a spark lighter. In Africa, many welders use propane or LPG instead of acetylene because it's cheaper and more available. But LPG burns cooler, so you need a different torch tip. Always check hoses for cracksleaking gas is deadly. Use soapy water to test for leaks; never use a flame.

A bench vise is essential for holding work while you cut or grind. Mount it securely on a heavy table. Files are used for deburring and smoothing edges. A half-round file is most versatile. For scraping slag from tight corners, a slag hammer with a pointed tip works well. Keep your tools clean and oiled to prevent rusthumidity in coastal areas like Mombasa or Lagos will rust tools quickly.

When buying hand tools, look for forged steel, not cast iron. Brands like Stanley, Irwin, and local equivalents are fine. A good chipping hammer costs about $5-10. A hacksaw frame with blade costs $10-15. Invest in a file card to clean files. Store tools in a dry box with a little oil. Remember: a sharp tool is a safe tool. Dull blades require more force and slip easily, causing injuries.

2. Power Tools: Grinders, Drills, and Saws

Power tools speed up your work, but in Africa, power supply can be unreliable. A 4.5-inch angle grinder is the most important power tool for a welder. Use it with a grinding wheel to smooth welds, a cutting wheel to cut metal, and a wire cup brush to clean rust. Always unplug the grinder before changing wheels. Wear a face shield and gloves. In many African markets, you can buy a decent 4.5-inch grinder for $30-50. Brands like Bosch, Makita, or Chinese equivalents like Total or Ingco work well.

For drilling, a 1/2-inch electric drill with variable speed is useful. Use high-speed steel (HSS) drill bits for metal. Apply cutting oil or even used engine oil to keep the bit cool. Drill at slow speed for large holes. If you don't have electricity, a hand drill or a brace and bit works, but it's slow. A portable band saw or a chop saw is great for cutting metal accurately. A chop saw with an abrasive wheel cuts angle iron and pipe quickly. Always clamp your work securely.

In areas with frequent power cuts, consider a generator or inverter. A 2-3 kVA generator can run a grinder and small welder. But remember: power tools draw a lot of current. Use an extension cord with the correct wire gauge (at least 2.5 mm for 20 meters). Never use a damaged cord. Check the plug and cable before each use.

Maintenance: blow out dust from the motor vents weekly. Replace carbon brushes when they wear down. Keep grinding wheels dry and free from cracks. Store power tools in a dry place. If you work in a dusty environment, clean the tool after each use. A grinder that overheats will burn out quickly. Let it cool down. Always use the guard on a grinderit protects you from flying debris. Safety first: wear goggles, gloves, and hearing protection.

3. Measuring and Layout Tools

Accurate measurement is the difference between a strong weld and a failed joint. You need a steel measuring tape (5 meters is standard), a steel rule (150 mm or 300 mm), and a try square. A try square checks 90-degree angles. In Africa, many welders use a combination square because it can also mark 45 degrees. A scribe or a sharp pencil marks metal. For layout, use a soapstone or a permanent markersoapstone is better because it doesn't burn away when you weld.

A center punch marks drilling points. A dividers or compass draws circles. For pipe work, a wrap-around or a pipe tape measures circumference. A spirit level ensures your work is horizontal or vertical. A plumb bob checks vertical alignment. These tools are cheap but essential. A good tape measure costs $5-10. A try square costs $5-15. Buy stainless steel tools to resist rust.

When measuring, always measure twice and cut once. Use the same tape for all measurements on a project to avoid errors. For welding, you often need to allow for shrinkage. Steel expands when heated and contracts when cooled. For a 1-meter weld, allow about 1-2 mm shrinkage. Use a welding gauge to check fillet weld sizes and reinforcement. A fillet weld gauge costs about $10-20.

Mark clearly with soapstone. If you need precision, use a scriber and a hammer to make a small mark. For layout on curved surfaces, use a flexible curve or a piece of wire. In the field, you can use a chalk line for long straight lines. Always keep your measuring tools clean and dry. Store them in a case. A bent tape or a damaged square will give wrong readings. Check your square regularly against a known straight edge.

4. Clamps, Jigs, and Fixtures

Clamps hold your work steady so you can weld accurately. The most common are C-clamps, F-clamps, and bar clamps. In Africa, you can buy cheap C-clamps from local markets. A 4-inch C-clamp costs about $3-5. Use at least two clamps for a butt joint. For pipe welding, use a pipe clamp or a chain clamp. Magnetic clamps are very useful for holding steel plates at 90 degrees. A magnetic welding clamp costs $10-20 and can hold up to 50 kg. But remember: magnets lose strength when heated, so don't weld too close.

Jigs and fixtures are custom-made tools that hold parts in the correct position for welding. They save time and ensure repeatability. For example, if you are making 100 identical gates, a jig will hold the frame square while you weld. You can make a simple jig from angle iron and clamps. Use a steel table as a fixtureweld a few tabs to hold your work. But be careful: welding directly to a table can distort it. Use spacers or copper backing.

Tack welds are your friend. Before fully welding, put small tack welds (about 10-15 mm long) at the corners and every 150-200 mm along the joint. Check squareness and alignment. If something is off, you can break the tacks with a chipping hammer and re-clamp. Always use a square to check 90-degree angles. For round parts, use a pipe clamp or a vise.

When clamping, don't over-tightenyou can distort thin metal. Use a piece of wood or copper as a pad. For aluminum, use aluminum clamps to avoid contamination. Keep clamps clean and oil the threads. A rusty clamp will seize. Store clamps hanging on a rack. In the field, you can use a chain and a turnbuckle as a clamp. Be creative, but always ensure the work is secure before welding. A loose part can cause a dangerous kickback.

5. Welding Cables, Connectors, and Grounding

Welding cables carry high current from the machine to the electrode holder and work clamp. In Africa, you'll find cables with different insulation. Use rubber-insulated cable (like SOOW or H07RN-F) because it's flexible and oil-resistant. The cable size depends on amperage. For up to 200 amps, use 35 mm cable. For 300 amps, use 50 mm. Never use undersized cableit will overheat and cause a fire. A 10-meter length of 35 mm cable costs about $30-50.

Connectors must be tight. The electrode holder (stinger) should be rated for your amperage. A 300-amp holder costs $10-20. The work clamp (ground clamp) must make good contact. Clean the metal before clamping. A poor ground causes erratic arc and shocks. Use a brass or copper clamp. Never clamp to painted or rusty metal. In the field, you can clamp to a steel table or a piece of scrap.

Grounding is critical for safety. The work clamp should be connected to the workpiece, not to the ground. The welder's case must be grounded to earth. Use a ground rod if required. In many African workshops, grounding is ignored, but it can save your life. If you feel a tingle, stop immediately. Check cables for cuts, exposed wires, and damaged insulation. Replace damaged cables. Use cable connectors (quick connectors) to join cables. They must be tight and insulated.

Inspect cables daily. Look for burns, cracks, and exposed copper. Repair with heat shrink or electrical tapebut tape is temporary. Better to replace. Keep cables coiled loosely when not in use. Don't drag cables over sharp edges. Store in a dry place. For long runs, use a cable cover or ramp to prevent tripping. Remember: a good ground is 50% of a good weld.

6. Equipment Maintenance and Inspection

Maintenance keeps your tools safe and working. Start with a daily check: inspect cables, holders, clamps, and power tools. Look for damage, loose connections, and wear. A 5-minute check can prevent accidents. For welding machines, check the cooling fan and vents. Blow out dust with compressed air. Check the electrode holder for loose parts. Replace worn tips. For engine-driven welders, check oil and fuel daily. Change oil every 100 hours. Clean the air filter every 50 hours.

Grinders need regular care. Replace carbon brushes every 100-200 hours. Check the cord for cuts. Clean the vents. Replace grinding wheels when they are worn down to 2/3 of original diameter. Never use a cracked wheel. Store wheels flat. For drills, check the chuck and replace if worn. Lubricate the chuck. For saws, check blades for missing teeth. Replace dull bladesthey cause kickback.

In Africa, dust and humidity are enemies. After work, wipe tools with an oily rag. Store in a dry box with silica gel. For welding machines, keep them covered when not in use. Use a voltage stabilizer if power fluctuates. Check the ground connection monthly. Test the insulation resistance with a megohmmeter if available. A simple test: use a multimeter to check for continuity between the case and the ground pin.

Create a maintenance log. Record dates of inspection, repairs, and replacements. This helps you track when to service. For example, if you use your grinder daily, plan to replace brushes every 3 months. Keep spare parts: fuses, brushes, tips, and wheels. In remote areas, order spares before you run out. Train yourself to notice unusual sounds, smells, or vibrations. A bearing whine means it's failing. Fix small problems before they become big. A well-maintained tool lasts years and keeps you safe.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Welding Tools and Equipment for African Agribusiness Workshops

In African agribusiness, a welding workshop is not a hobby room it is a revenue centre. Whether you are fabricating maize shellers, repairing tractor implements, building poultry cages, or maintaining borehole pump brackets, the profitability of your operation depends on having the right tools, keeping them in service, and knowing when a worn component is costing you more in downtime than a replacement would cost in cash. This practical reference consolidates the essential knowledge from the seven lessons in this module into one field-ready document. It is written for the workshop owner, the farm mechanic, and the young artisan building a welding business in a peri-urban or rural enterprise zone.

Across Sub-Saharan Africa, the welding trade is dominated by small and medium enterprises. The informal metal fabrication sector employs hundreds of thousands of people, and demand is rising with agricultural mechanisation, construction, and the growth of agro-processing. The businesses that survive are those that treat tools and equipment as capital assets with a maintenance schedule, not as consumables to be abused until they fail. This reference gives you the numbers, the checks, and the sources to do exactly that.

1. Hand Tools for Welding and Cutting

Hand tools do the work that no machine can reach. In an African farm workshop, the core kit is modest but must be complete: a chipping hammer for slag removal, a wire brush (stainless for stainless work, carbon steel for mild steel), a hacksaw with 1824 TPI blades for cutting, a cold chisel, a ball-peen hammer, a punch, files (flat and half-round), and a set of spanners and adjustable wrenches. For oxy-fuel cutting, you need a cutting torch, spark lighter, and tip cleaners. The most common failure point is not the tool itself but the operator skipping the slag removal step, which leads to slag inclusions and failed welds. A chipping hammer costs the equivalent of a few US dollars and prevents rework that can cost a full day of production.

2. Power Tools: Grinders, Drills, and Saws

The angle grinder is the workhorse of any fabrication shop. A 115 mm or 125 mm grinder with a 9001100 W motor handles most farm implement work. For heavier fabrication, a 230 mm grinder with 20002400 W is standard. Always match the disc to the task: grinding discs for metal removal, cutting discs for severing, flap discs for finishing, and wire cup brushes for scale removal. Never use a cutting disc for grinding it is a leading cause of disc burst injuries. Drills should be variable-speed with a hammer function for masonry, and saws range from portable band saws to abrasive chop saws. In off-grid workshops, a petrol-driven generator of at least 5 kVA is required to run a 200 A inverter welder and a grinder simultaneously.

3. Measuring and Layout Tools

Accuracy in layout determines whether a gate hangs square or a trailer tracks straight. Essential tools: steel rule or tape measure (5 m minimum), combination square, try square, engineer's square, dividers, scribe, centre punch, and a chalk line for large layouts. For fabrication at scale, a spirit level and plumb bob are indispensable. In agribusiness, the most expensive layout error is misaligned implement hitch points a few millimetres of error translates into uneven ploughing and crop losses across an entire field.

4. Clamps, Jigs, and Fixtures

Clamps hold work while you weld, and jigs hold it in the right position repeatedly. A basic clamp set includes C-clamps, F-clamps, bar clamps, and magnetic welding squares. For repetitive production such as making poultry feeders or maize crib frames a simple jig made from angle iron and toggle clamps can cut assembly time by 6080% and improve consistency. In African workshops, jigs are often the difference between a one-man operation and a small factory employing five people.

Connectors must match cable size and be crimped or soldered, never twisted and taped. The work clamp (earth clamp) must make clean metal-to-metal contact; paint, rust, and grease are common causes of a weak arc and porosity in the weld. In many African workshops, a poor earth connection is the single most common cause of "the welder is not working" complaints before calling a technician, clean the contact point.

SAFETY WARNING ELECTRIC SHOCK AND FIRE RISK

Welding cables, connectors, and clamps carry open-circuit voltages that can be lethal, especially in damp conditions common during African rainy seasons. Never weld in wet clothing or standing water. Inspect cables daily for cuts, exposed conductors, and damaged insulation replace immediately, never tape over damage. Ensure the work return clamp is securely attached to clean metal. Isolate the power source before changing electrodes, cables, or connectors. In workshops without reliable earthing, install a residual current device (RCD) and a proper earth rod. Fire risk is high where welding is done near dry thatch, crop residues, or fuel stores keep a dry powder extinguisher within 5 metres of the welding station and clear combustibles from a 10 m radius.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Inspect before you weld: Check cables, clamps, holder insulation, and grinder discs for damage every morning before the first arc.
  2. Match the cable to the job: Use the correct copper cable size for your welding current and length undersized cable wastes power and overheats.
  3. Clean the earth point: Grind paint, rust, and dirt from the workpiece where the work clamp attaches; a poor earth causes porosity and weak arcs.
  4. Use the right disc: Never grind with a cutting disc or cut with a grinding disc match the disc to the task and respect the RPM rating.
  5. Keep a jig for repeat work: If you make more than ten identical parts, build a simple jig it pays for itself in a week.
  6. Maintain a logbook: Record every maintenance action, cable replacement, and equipment fault it protects your business and supports warranty claims.
  7. Control fire risk: Clear a 10 m radius of combustibles, keep a dry powder extinguisher within reach, and never weld near fuel or thatch.
  8. Budget for replacement: Set aside 510% of monthly revenue for tool and cable replacement downtime costs more than the tools.

For the African agribusiness welder, the message is simple: your tools are your business. A well-maintained 200 A inverter welder, a complete hand tool kit, correctly sized cables, and a disciplined maintenance routine will out-earn a larger workshop with neglected equipment every season. Treat the checklist above as a daily business routine, not an optional extra and the workshop will keep producing the gates, trailers, shellers, and repairs that keep farms and agribusinesses moving.
