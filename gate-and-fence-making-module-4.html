1. Selecting and Cutting Wood Posts and Rails

Choosing the right wood for your fence posts and rails is the first step to a fence that lasts. In Africa, the best woods for posts are those naturally resistant to termites and rot: eucalyptus, cedar, or treated pine. Avoid softwoods like cypress unless they are pressure-treated. For posts, you need round or square timber with a diameter of at least 100mm (4 inches). For rails, use 50mm x 100mm (2x4) or 50mm x 150mm (2x6) boards. Buy from a reputable timber yard, and check for straightness, cracks, and knots. Green wood is cheaper but will shrink and warp; air-dried or kiln-dried wood is better.

Before cutting, measure your fence line and mark post positions every 2.4 meters (8 feet). This spacing works for most fences and reduces waste. Use a tape measure, string line, and stakes to mark the spots. For corners and gates, add extra posts for strength. Cut posts to a uniform length: 2.4 meters is standard, but if you have soft soil, go 2.7 meters so you can set them deeper. For rails, cut them to fit between posts, allowing for a 50mm overlap on each end for nailing.

Use a circular saw or hand saw to cut wood. Always wear safety glasses and gloves. When cutting treated wood, wear a dust mask. Cut on a sawhorse or workbench, not on the ground. For accurate cuts, use a speed square to mark a straight line. If using a chainsaw, be extra careful and keep both hands on the saw. After cutting, treat the cut ends with a wood preservative, especially for posts that will be in the ground. This prevents rot and extends the life of your fence.

Store your cut wood off the ground on blocks, and cover it with a tarp if rain is expected. Stack it so air can circulate. This prevents warping and rot before installation. Remember, good preparation saves time and money later.

2. Installing Wood Posts and Rails

Installing posts properly is critical for a sturdy fence. Start by digging holes with a post-hole digger or auger. For a 2.4m post, dig a hole 60cm deep and 30cm wide. In sandy soil, go deeperâup to 90cm. If you hit rock, you may need to use a breaker bar or choose a different spot. Place the post in the hole and check it is plumb (vertical) using a spirit level. Temporarily brace it with stakes and string.

Mix concrete for setting posts. A standard mix is 1 part cement, 2 parts sand, 3 parts gravel. Add water until it's like thick oatmeal. Pour concrete around the post, filling the hole halfway. Tamp it down with a stick to remove air pockets. Check plumb again, then fill the rest with soil or more concrete. Slope the concrete away from the post so water runs off. Let it cure for at least 24 hours before attaching rails. In hot climates, keep the concrete moist by covering it with plastic or wet burlap for the first day.

For rails, attach them to the posts using galvanized nails or screws. Use 3 nails per connection, driven at slight angles for strength. If using screws, pre-drill to avoid splitting. Rails should be level and spaced evenly. For a typical fence, put rails at the top, middle, and bottom. For extra strength, add diagonal bracing at corners and gates. Use a string line to keep rails straight. If the ground is uneven, you may need to step the fence: each section is level, but the overall fence follows the slope.

After installing, check all posts are secure and rails are tight. A wobble means troubleâfix it now. Backfill around posts with soil and tamp firmly. Water the soil to settle it. For added stability, you can add a concrete collar at the base. This is especially important in areas with strong winds or soft soil.

3. Attaching Pickets and Boards

Pickets and boards give your fence its look and privacy. Start by deciding on the style: vertical pickets, horizontal boards, or lattice top. For vertical pickets, attach them to the rails with 2 nails per railâone near the top, one near the bottom. Use galvanized nails to prevent rust. Space pickets evenly: a common gap is 50mm (2 inches), but for privacy, use 10mm or less. Use a spacer block cut to the gap size to keep spacing consistent. Start at one end and work across, checking with a level every few pickets.

For horizontal boards, attach them to the posts or rails with screws or nails. Overlap boards by 25mm to allow for expansion and contraction. If using dog-eared boards, alternate the direction for a neat look. For a board-on-board fence, attach a board on one side, then another on the opposite side overlapping the gap. This gives total privacy. Pre-drill holes near the ends to prevent splitting, especially with hardwoods.

If you want a decorative top, cut pickets at an angle or add a lattice strip. Use a jigsaw or hand saw. Sand rough edges. For a professional finish, run a string line along the top of the pickets and trim them all to the same height with a circular saw. This is easier than cutting each individually. Alternatively, use a template.

When attaching near the ground, leave a 50mm gap between the bottom of the picket and the soil. This prevents rot and allows air circulation. For gates, leave space for the gate to swing. After attaching, check for loose pickets and tighten as needed. Use a level to ensure the tops are even. If using nails, drive them at a slight downward angle to shed water. For a stronger hold, use ring-shank nails or screws. Finally, step back and inspect your workâadjust any misaligned pickets before moving on.

4. Building Wooden Gates: Frame and Bracing

A wooden gate must be strong but not too heavy. Start with a frame: use 50mm x 100mm timber for the top and bottom rails, and 50mm x 75mm for the sides. Cut the pieces to fit your opening, allowing a 20mm gap on each side for clearance. Assemble the frame on a flat surface. Use galvanized screws or nails, and add corner braces (diagonal pieces) to prevent sagging. A Z-brace (diagonal from bottom hinge side to top latch side) is best. Cut the brace at 45-degree angles and attach with screws.

For the gate infill, use vertical pickets or boards. Attach them to the frame with 2 nails per rail. If using boards, you can overlap them for privacy. Make sure the gate is square: measure diagonalsâthey should be equal. If not, adjust the brace. For a stronger gate, use a double frame (two layers of timber) or add a metal brace. In areas with termites, use treated wood or add a physical barrier like a metal strip at the bottom.

Hinges are critical. Use heavy-duty galvanized hingesâT-hinges or strap hinges. For a gate up to 1.2m wide, use two hinges; for wider, use three. Attach hinges to the gate frame first, then to the post. Use bolts, not screws, for security. The gate should swing freely without dragging. If it does, adjust the hinges or plane the edge. For the latch, use a thumb latch or a slide bolt. Ensure it aligns with the post. Add a gate stop to prevent swinging both ways.

Finally, test the gate. It should open and close smoothly, and latch securely. If it sags, tighten the hinges or add a diagonal brace. For heavy gates, consider adding a wheel for support. Remember, a well-built gate adds security and curb appeal.

5. Hanging and Adjusting Wood Gates

Hanging a gate correctly ensures it works smoothly for years. Start by attaching the hinges to the gate frame. Position the top hinge about 150mm from the top and the bottom hinge 150mm from the bottom. For a wide gate, add a middle hinge. Use a drill to make pilot holes, then bolt the hinges in place. For T-hinges, the long strap goes on the gate, and the short strap on the post. For strap hinges, the pivot point should be on the post.

With help, lift the gate into position. Place a block under the gate to hold it at the correct heightâabout 20mm above the ground. Attach the hinge to the post using bolts. Check that the gate is level and plumb. If not, adjust the hinges or add shims. For adjustable hinges, you can fine-tune later. Once the hinges are secure, test the swing. The gate should open and close without binding. If it rubs, plane the edge or adjust the hinge position.

For latches, install the catch on the post and the latch on the gate. Ensure they align. A common type is the thumb latch, which can be operated from both sides. For security, add a padlock or a heavy-duty slide bolt. If the gate is in a windy area, add a spring or automatic closer. Adjust the latch so it engages smoothly. If the gate sags over time, tighten the hinges or add a diagonal brace. For wooden gates, you can also use a turnbuckle to adjust tension.

Regular maintenance is key. Check hinges and latches every few months. Lubricate hinges with oil or grease. Tighten bolts. If the gate swells in the rainy season, you may need to plane the edges. In dry season, gaps may appearâadd a weatherstrip if needed. For a gate that must be child-safe, install a self-latching mechanism. Always ensure the gate cannot fall off its hinges: use a safety chain or a top pivot. With proper hanging and adjustment, your gate will function well for years.

6. Finishing and Sealing Wood Fences

Finishing protects your fence from sun, rain, and insects. Start by sanding rough spots and removing splinters. Use a medium-grit sandpaper (80-120 grit) on a sanding block or orbital sander. Wipe off dust with a cloth. If the wood is treated, you can skip sanding, but it's still good to smooth edges. For a natural look, apply a clear wood sealer or varnish. For color, use a stain or paint. In Africa, choose products with UV protection and fungicide. Oil-based stains penetrate better; water-based are easier to clean up.

Apply the finish with a brush, roller, or sprayer. For large fences, a sprayer is fastest, but you'll need to back-brush to work it into the wood. Brush with the grain. Start at the top and work down to avoid drips. Two thin coats are better than one thick coat. Allow drying time between coatsâcheck the label. For posts, apply extra sealer at the ground line and cut ends. For gates, finish all sides, including the bottom edge, to prevent rot. If using paint, prime first with a wood primer.

Sealing is especially important in areas with high rainfall or termites. A good sealer repels water and prevents warping. Reapply every 2-3 years, or annually in harsh conditions. Before reapplying, clean the fence with a mild detergent and water, then rinse and let dry. For mold or mildew, use a bleach solution (1 part bleach to 3 parts water) and scrub. Rinse well. For termite protection, use a borate-based treatment. This soaks into the wood and kills termites. Apply before sealing.

Finally, inspect your fence regularly. Check for loose nails, cracked boards, and rot. Replace damaged pieces promptly. Touch up finish as needed. A well-maintained fence can last 15-20 years. Remember, the finish is not just cosmeticâit's your fence's armor against the elements.

Practical Reference: Wood Fence Construction for African Agribusinesses

Wood fencing remains one of the most accessible and cost-effective boundary solutions for smallholder farms, livestock keepers, and peri-urban agribusinesses across Africa. Whether you are enclosing a poultry run in Kiambu, a goat paddock in Dodoma, or a homestead perimeter in Mbarara, a well-built wood fence protects assets, reduces theft, and increases the value of your land. This Practical Reference consolidates the technical essentials from the six lessons in this module into a working document you can carry to the field.

Treat fencing as a business investment, not a chore. A fence that lasts eight to ten years costs far less per year than one that fails after two seasons. The difference is almost always in post selection, bracing, and finishing â the three areas where African builders most often cut corners.

1. Selecting and Cutting Wood Posts and Rails

Choose species that are locally abundant, durable in contact with soil, and affordable. In East and Southern Africa, Eucalyptus grandis and E. camaldulensis are widely used; in West Africa, Anogeissus leiocarpus (axlewood) and Prosopis africana offer excellent natural durability. Avoid untreated softwoods for in-ground posts â they rot within 18â24 months in humid tropical soils.

Cut posts at least 2.4 m long for line posts (1.8 m above ground, 0.6 m buried) and 2.7â3.0 m for corner and gate posts. Rails should be 3.0â3.6 m long, 75â100 mm diameter, and straight. Harvest during the dry season when sap content is lowest, then air-dry under shade for 4â6 weeks before treatment.

2. Installing Wood Posts and Rails

Set corner and gate posts first â they carry the tension of the entire fence. Dig holes 0.6 m deep and 200 mm wide, place 50 mm of gravel at the bottom for drainage, then set the post and backfill with alternating layers of soil and crushed stone, tamping every 150 mm. In sandy soils, brace with concrete (1:3:5 mix) or pack with quarry dust.

Use a string line between corner posts to align intermediate posts. Rails are nailed to the field side of posts so livestock pressure pushes the rail onto the post rather than pulling nails out. Use two 75 mm galvanised nails per rail end, driven at a slight downward angle.

3. Attaching Pickets and Boards

Pickets add privacy, windbreak, and small-stock containment. Space pickets at 100â150 mm for poultry, 75â100 mm for goats, and 50 mm or less for ornamental or security fencing. Attach with galvanised nails or, where corrosion is severe (coastal Kenya, Tanzania, Mozambique), use stainless steel or hot-dip galvanised screws.

Boards (wider flat timber) are preferred for windbreak fencing in the highlands and for boundary walls in peri-urban plots. Leave a 5â8 mm gap between boards to allow expansion during the rains and reduce wind loading.

4. Building Wooden Gates: Frame and Bracing

A gate is a structural frame, not just a door. Build a rectangular frame from 75 Ã 75 mm timber, then add a diagonal brace running from the bottom hinge corner to the top latch corner. This triangulation prevents sagging â the single most common gate failure in African farms.

Gate width should match the widest vehicle or animal that will pass: 1.2 m for foot traffic, 2.4 m for livestock, 3.0â3.6 m for tractors and pickup trucks. Use mortise-and-tenon or half-lap joints reinforced with galvanised bolts (M10 minimum), not nails alone.

5. Hanging and Adjusting Wood Gates

Hang gates on posts set in concrete, never on posts backfilled with soil alone. The gate post must be at least 150 mm diameter and buried 0.9 m deep. Position hinges so the gate swings away from the livestock pressure side. Leave 25â40 mm clearance between the gate bottom and the ground to allow for swelling in the wet season.

Adjust sagging gates by tightening the diagonal brace (add a turnbuckle or steel rod with nuts at both ends) or by re-setting the top hinge. Check gates monthly during the rainy season â wood movement is greatest then.

Creosote, pentachlorophenol (PCP), and CCA (chromated copper arsenate) treated timber are hazardous. In several African countries, CCA is restricted or banned for use near food crops, water sources, or livestock housing. Never burn treated wood offcuts â toxic fumes cause serious respiratory damage. Always wear gloves, dust mask, and eye protection when cutting treated timber, and wash hands before eating. Store treatment chemicals away from children, animals, and wells. Where possible, use boron-based or naturally durable species instead.

6. Finishing and Sealing Wood Fences

Finishing is what turns a fence into a durable asset. Apply a wood preservative (borate-based for interior or above-ground use, copper naphthenate or creosote for ground contact) within 48 hours of cutting. Seal end-grain â the most absorbent part of any post or rail â with two coats. For visible fences, add a UV-resistant exterior stain or paint every 3â4 years.

In high-rainfall zones (e.g., western Uganda, southern Nigeria, Cameroon), apply preservative annually for the first three years. In semi-arid zones (e.g., northern Kenya, Botswana), every 4â5 years is sufficient.
