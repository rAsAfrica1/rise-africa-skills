Lessons

1. Planning and Designing a Complex Project

Before you strike a single blow, you must plan. A complex project like a fire poker with twists or a pair of tongs needs a clear drawing. Use graph paper and a pencil. Decide on dimensions: for a fire poker, total length 60 cm, handle 15 cm, shaft 40 cm, point 5 cm. For tongs, jaw length 10 cm, handle 25 cm, pivot boss 3 cm. Draw full size or to scale. Mark where twists, bends, and joints will be. Calculate material: mild steel round bar 12 mm diameter for poker, 10 mm for tongs. Remember shrinkage: steel expands when hot, contracts when cool, so forge slightly oversize and finish to size.

Next, make a step-by-step plan. List operations in order: cutting, forging, bending, twisting, welding (if needed), finishing. For a poker with twists, you might: cut 60 cm of 12 mm bar, forge taper at one end, forge a decorative scroll at the other, then twist a section. For tongs, you need two identical pieces: forge the jaws, punch or drill the pivot hole, then rivet together. Write each step with the tools needed: forge, anvil, hammer, tongs, chisel, punch, drill, file.

Consider the environment. In many African workshops, you work outdoors or in a simple shed. Plan for ventilation, shade, and a safe distance from flammable materials. If you use charcoal, you need a constant supply; if using coal, manage the fire. Have a quench trough nearby. Plan your work around the heat: do heavy forging in the cool morning, finishing work in the afternoon.

Finally, make a jig or template for repetitive tasks. For tongs, a simple wooden template can help shape the jaws consistently. For twists, a twist wrench or a vise can ensure even twists. Always test your design on a scrap piece first. A good plan saves time, material, and frustration. It also makes the project safer because you know each step before you start.

2. Forging a Fire Poker with Twists

Start with a 60 cm length of 12 mm mild steel round bar. Heat one end to bright orange (about 900Â°C) in your forge. Using a hammer and anvil, forge a taper to a point: draw out the last 5 cm to a sharp point, working on the far edge of the anvil. Keep the taper even. Once pointed, forge a small scroll at the opposite end for a handle: heat 10 cm, then bend it around the horn of the anvil or use a scroll wrench. This scroll gives a good grip and a decorative finish.

Now for the twists. Decide where you want them: often two twists, each 10 cm long, separated by a straight section. Heat the first section to a uniform orange. Clamp one end in a vise (or hold with tongs) and use a twist wrench or a large adjustable wrench on the other end. Turn clockwise evenly. Count the turns: 5 to 7 full turns for a tight twist. Keep the heat even; if it cools, reheat. Do the second twist in the same direction for a consistent look.

After twisting, you may want to add a decorative knot or a basket twist. A simple knot: heat the middle, bend it into a loop, then twist the loop. For a basket twist, you need to split the bar, but thatâs advanced. For now, keep it simple. Once all forging is done, let the poker cool slowly in air (normalize) to relieve stresses. Then wire-brush off scale while still warm.

Finish with a wire brush and apply a coat of beeswax or linseed oil while the metal is warm (about 200Â°C). This prevents rust and gives a nice black finish. In Africa, where humidity can be high, this protection is important. Test the poker: it should be straight, balanced, and comfortable to hold. If itâs too long, cut and re-forge the point. Remember, practice makes perfect. Make several pokers to master the twist and taper.

3. Creating a Pair of Blacksmith Tongs

Tongs are essential tools. To make a pair, start with two pieces of 10 mm mild steel round bar, each 40 cm long. Forge one end of each into a jaw: heat 10 cm, then hammer it flat and shape it into a curved jaw that can grip round or square stock. The jaws should meet evenly when closed. Use a hot cut or hacksaw to trim if needed. Next, forge the handle end: taper it slightly for comfort and bend a small scroll for a good grip.

Now you need a pivot. The traditional method is to punch a hole. Heat the middle of each bar (about 15 cm from the jaw) to bright orange. Use a punch and hammer to drive a hole through the metal. Work from both sides to avoid distortion. The hole should be about 8 mm diameter to fit a rivet. Alternatively, drill the hole when cool. If you punch, reheat and use a drift to size the hole. Make sure the holes align when the two pieces are put together.

Rivet the two pieces together. Use a piece of 8 mm round bar as rivet stock. Heat the rivet, insert through both holes, then peen the ends with a hammer to form heads. The joint should be tight but allow the tongs to open and close smoothly. If too tight, heat the joint and work it. If too loose, tighten by hammering the rivet head.

Finally, adjust the jaws: heat them and bend to fit the stock you will hold. For general work, a jaw opening of 2-3 cm is good. Quench the jaws in oil or water to harden them slightly, but do not quench the handlesâthey should stay soft and tough. Test the tongs by gripping a piece of hot metal. They should hold securely without slipping. With practice, you can make a pair in about two hours. Tongs are a mark of a skilled blacksmith, so take pride in making them well.

4. Making a Door Handle with Backplate

A door handle with backplate is a practical project that combines forging and fitting. Start with a backplate: use a piece of 3 mm thick mild steel plate, about 10 cm x 15 cm. Cut it to shape with a hacksaw or cold chisel. Forge the edges to give a chamfered or decorative look. Drill or punch holes for screws (usually 4 mm diameter) at the corners. If you want a decorative texture, use a chisel to cut lines or a punch to make dimples.

For the handle, use 10 mm round bar, 20 cm long. Forge one end into a decorative scroll or leaf shape. The other end will be bent at 90 degrees to fit through the backplate and into the door. Heat the middle and bend a right angle, leaving about 5 cm to go through the door. The handle should be long enough to grip comfortably. You can also add a twist in the middle for decoration.

Now assemble: the handle passes through a hole in the backplate (drill a hole slightly larger than the bar). Then it goes through the door. On the inside, you need a mechanism to secure it. Simplest: use a wedge or a cotter pin through a hole drilled in the handle end inside the door. Alternatively, forge a decorative rosette to cover the hole on the inside. For a more secure fit, you can thread the end and use a nut, but that requires taps and dies.

Finish the backplate and handle with a wire brush and beeswax or oil. Install the backplate on the door with screws. Insert the handle, and secure it from the inside. Test the movement: the handle should turn smoothly if it's a lever type, or simply pull if it's a fixed handle. In Africa, where doors may be made of wood or metal, ensure the backplate sits flush. This project teaches you to combine forging with basic fitting, a valuable skill for making hardware.

5. Efficient Workshop Layout and Workflow

A good workshop layout saves time and energy. In a typical African blacksmith shop, space may be limited, so arrange your work triangle: forge, anvil, and vise. The forge should be centrally located but with its back to the wind if outdoors. The anvil should be within two steps of the forge, so you can move hot metal quickly. The vise should be near the anvil for holding work. Keep your quenching trough near the forge and anvil. Store your hammers and tongs on a rack within arm's reach of the anvil.

Organize your raw material by size and type. Keep short offcuts in a bin for small projects. Long stock can be stored horizontally on racks or vertically in a corner. Label or separate by metal type: mild steel, high carbon, etc. Have a designated area for finished work to avoid clutter. A clean floor is safer: sweep up scale and metal scraps daily. Use a shovel and broom.

Workflow is about sequence. For a complex project, stage your tools and materials before you start. Heat only what you need. Use the forge efficiently: bank the fire when not in use, and use a cover to control air. If you're doing multiple pieces, batch similar operations: cut all stock, then forge all tapers, then twist all. This reduces rehandling and saves fuel. Keep your hammer hand free: use tongs to hold work, not your hands. Have a plan for each heat: do as much as possible before the metal cools.

Safety and comfort: ensure good ventilation to avoid smoke inhalation. Use a chimney or hood if possible. Have a first aid kit and fire extinguisher. Wear safety glasses, leather apron, and gloves. In hot climates, take breaks and drink water. A well-organized shop is a productive shop. It also impresses customers and keeps you motivated. Spend 15 minutes at the end of each day to tidy up; you'll thank yourself next morning.

6. Safety Review and Continuing Education Resources

Safety first. Always wear safety glasses when hammering, especially when chiseling or punching. Hot metal can send sparks and scale flying. Wear leather gloves when handling hot metal, but be aware that gloves can get hot and burn you; use tongs whenever possible. Wear a leather apron to protect from burns and cuts. Tie back long hair and avoid loose clothing. Keep a fire extinguisher and a bucket of water or sand nearby. Never quench hot metal in water without warning othersâsteam can scald.

Forge safety: ensure your forge is stable and on a non-flammable surface. If using coal or charcoal, manage the fire carefully. Never leave a fire unattended. Use a spark screen if needed. Check your hammer: the head must be tight on the handle. A loose hammer head can fly off. Inspect your anvil for cracks. Use a wire brush to remove scale from hot metal before hammering to avoid flying flakes. When cutting with a hot cut, position the metal so the cut piece falls away from you. When punching, use a punch with a handle or hold with tongs.

Continuing education: blacksmithing is a lifelong learning. Seek out local blacksmiths and ask to apprentice or observe. Join online forums like I Forge Iron or the Blacksmithing subreddit. Watch YouTube channels such as Black Bear Forge, Christ Centered Ironworks, and TorbjÃ¶rn Ãhman. Read books: 'The Complete Modern Blacksmith' by Alexander Weygers, 'The Backyard Blacksmith' by Lorelei Sims. For African context, look for local artisans and training centers. Many universities and technical colleges offer short courses. Practice regularly: make simple items like hooks, nails, and leaves to build skills. Attend workshops and demonstrations. Consider joining a guild or association. Teach others what you learnâit reinforces your knowledge. Remember, safety and skill go hand in hand. Keep learning, keep forging, and stay safe.

Practical Reference: Advanced Projects and Workshop Practice

This reference sheet is the working companion for the Basic Blacksmithing â Advanced Projects and Workshop Practice module. It is written for the small African forge that must earn money from every heat, every hour, and every kilogram of charcoal or coal. Whether you are forging a fire poker with twists, a pair of tongs, a door handle with backplate, or planning a complex commission, the rule is the same: design for the market, forge for the price, and keep the workshop safe and productive.

Use this sheet alongside the six lessons in the module. It summarises the practical decisions that separate a hobby forge from a business forge: project planning, repeatable forging sequences, tool-making, efficient layout, safety, and continuing education.

1. Planning and Designing a Complex Project

Before you light the fire, answer four business questions:

  1. Who is the customer and what will they pay? A lodge in Livingstone or Naivasha may pay a premium for hand-forged door handles, fire pokers, and towel rails. A local hardware shop may only buy in bulk at a low margin.
  2. What material is available and at what cost? Use scrap spring steel, old leaf springs, rebar (where permitted), or mild steel from local suppliers. In many African markets, spring steel and leaf spring are the cheapest high-carbon sources for tongs and tools.
  3. How many heats and how much fuel? A fire poker with twists may take 3â5 heats. A pair of tongs may take 6â10 heats. Estimate fuel and labour before quoting.
  4. What is the finish and delivery time? Brushed, waxed, oiled, or painted. A simple beeswax or linseed oil finish is cheap, attractive, and protects against rust in humid climates.

Draw a full-size or scale sketch. Mark all dimensions, twists, and bends. A full-size chalk drawing on the workshop floor or a steel plate is the cheapest jig you will ever make. It reduces errors and speeds up production when making repeat items such as a set of six door handles.

2. Forging a Fire Poker with Twists

A fire poker is a high-turnover product for lodges, guesthouses, and homes. It is simple to make but must be straight, balanced, and comfortable to hold.

  • Material: 10â12 mm mild steel round bar, or 8â10 mm spring steel for a longer-lasting point.
  • Length: 600â800 mm depending on fireplace size.
  • Sequence: forge the point first, then draw out the handle, then twist the middle section, then bend the handle hook.
  • Twists: heat a 100â150 mm section evenly to a bright orange. Clamp one end in the vice and use a twisting wrench or pipe wrench on the other. Count the turns so repeat items match.
  • Finish: brush off scale, apply beeswax or linseed oil while warm, and buff.

Business tip: make pokers in batches of 5â10. The fire is already hot, the jigs are set, and the unit cost drops. Sell as a set with a matching stand or door handle.

3. Creating a Pair of Blacksmith Tongs

Tongs are the most important tool you will make. They save fuel, improve safety, and allow you to hold work securely. A good pair lasts years.

  • Material: 12â16 mm mild steel or spring steel. Rebar can work but may split; test before committing to a customer order.
  • Sequence: forge the jaws first, then the boss (pivot), then the reins (handles).
  • Rivet: use a 6â8 mm mild steel rivet. Heat the rivet, insert, and peen both ends. The joint must move freely but not wobble.
  • Fit: the jaws should meet evenly along their length. Test with a piece of hot steel before finishing.
  • Finish: leave the reins slightly rough for grip; oil the joint.

Business tip: tongs are a saleable product for other smiths and for training centres. A well-made pair of flat-jaw tongs can be sold to apprentices and hobby smiths. Standardise your sizes: 10 mm, 12 mm, 16 mm.

4. Making a Door Handle with Backplate

Door handles and backplates are high-value decorative items for lodges, hotels, and upmarket homes. They combine forging, twisting, punching, and finishing.

  • Material: 8â10 mm round or square bar for the handle; 3â5 mm flat bar for the backplate.
  • Backplate: cut, forge to shape, punch or drill screw holes, and texture with a hammer or chisel.
  • Handle: forge a decorative end, twist the shank, and bend to a comfortable grip. The handle must clear the door surface.
  • Assembly: rivet or weld the handle to the backplate. If welding is not available, use a riveted or bolted connection.
  • Finish: beeswax, linseed oil, or a clear lacquer for humid coastal areas.

Business tip: sell door handles as a set with matching hinges, nails, and a fire poker. Offer a âlodge packageâ to safari camps and guesthouses. Price per set, not per item.

6. Safety Review and Continuing Education Resources

Blacksmithing is safe when the risks are controlled. The main risks are burns, eye injuries, fire, and respiratory damage from dust and fumes. In many African forges, charcoal and coal fires produce carbon monoxide and fine dust. Good ventilation is not optional.

Never burn charcoal or coal in an enclosed or poorly ventilated space. Carbon monoxide is colourless, odourless, and can kill. Forge outdoors, under an open-sided shelter, or with forced ventilation and a chimney. Keep a fire extinguisher, sand bucket, or water trough within reach. Wear eye protection at all times when hammering, chipping, or brushing. Wear leather gloves when handling hot metal, but remove them when using a power hammer or drill. Tie back long hair and avoid loose clothing. Keep children and visitors at a safe distance, and mark the âhot zoneâ clearly.

Continuing education is a business investment. A smith who learns new techniques can charge more, reduce waste, and access new markets. Free and low-cost resources are available from development organisations, associations, and online platforms.
