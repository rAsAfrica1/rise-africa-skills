Lessons

1. Determining Harvest Maturity Indicators

Knowing when to harvest yams is critical. If you dig too early, tubers are small and store poorly. If you wait too long, tubers become fibrous and may rot in the ground. Most white yam (Dioscorea rotundata) matures 8 to 10 months after planting, while water yam (D. alata) takes 10 to 12 months. In West Africa, harvest typically starts from late November when vines begin to dry. Look for these signs: leaves turn yellow and dry, vines wither, and the stem base near the soil cracks. Another reliable indicator is when the tuber skin hardens and does not peel easily with your thumbnail. You can also dig a test plant: if the tuber is firm and the skin is set, the crop is ready. In drier savanna zones, harvest may start earlier, around October. For seed yams, harvest at 6 to 7 months when tubers are smaller but mature. Avoid harvesting during heavy rains because wet soil increases damage and rot. If you must harvest in wet conditions, let tubers dry in a shaded, ventilated area for a day before curing. In regions with two rainy seasons, like parts of Uganda and Nigeria, you may have two harvests: a main harvest and a second from volunteer plants. Always mark your calendar with planting date and check fields weekly from the expected month. A simple maturity test: cut a tuber; if the cut surface is white and dry, it's mature. If it's watery, wait. Harvesting at the right time ensures better taste, longer storage, and higher market price.

2. Harvesting Tools and Techniques

Harvesting yams requires the right tools to avoid damage. The most common tool is a strong, sharpened wooden or metal digging stick (called 'oko' in Yoruba) about 1.5 to 2 meters long. A machete or cutlass is used to cut vines and roots. For large-scale farms, a spade or fork can be used, but be careful not to pierce tubers. Before digging, cut the vines at ground level, leaving a 10-15 cm stub to use as a handle. Then, using the digging stick, loosen the soil around the tuber, starting about 30 cm away from the plant. Push the stick vertically into the soil and lever it gently to lift the tuber. Never pull the vine to uproot the yam; this breaks the tuber and reduces storage life. For heavy soils, water the area lightly a day before to soften the ground. In sandy soils, you can use your hands after loosening. Harvest in the cool of the morning or late afternoon to reduce heat stress. Work in teams: one person digs, another collects and removes soil. Place tubers gently on a tarpaulin or dry grass, never throw them. If a tuber is cut or bruised, set it aside for immediate consumption or sale, not storage. After digging, remove excess soil with your hands or a soft brush; do not wash. In parts of Ghana and Cte d'Ivoire, farmers use a 'yam fork' with blunt tines to lift tubers. Always keep tools clean and sharp; a blunt stick requires more force and causes more damage. Replace wooden sticks when they splinter. Proper technique reduces losses by up to 30%.

3. Proper Lifting and Handling to Avoid Damage

Yam tubers bruise easily, and bruises lead to rot. Always lift tubers with both hands, supporting the middle and ends. Never lift by the vine or the stub; the tuber can snap. When carrying, hold the tuber close to your body and avoid dropping it. Use a basket or crate lined with dry grass or banana leaves to cushion tubers during collection. Do not pile more than three layers high in a basket; heavy weight causes pressure bruises. In the field, place tubers in single layers on a tarpaulin or on dry, raised platforms. Avoid stepping on tubers. If you must move tubers over a distance, use a wheelbarrow or a cart with a padded base. For large operations, use a tractor with a modified potato harvester, but set the chain speed low to avoid skinning. After lifting, trim the roots and the vine stub to about 2 cm, but do not cut into the tuber. Remove any soil clumps by hand; do not beat tubers against each other. In Uganda, farmers use a 'yam cradle' a sling made from sisal rope to carry tubers on their backs. For storage, handle tubers at least twice: once at harvest, once at curing. Each handling increases damage risk, so minimize it. Train workers to be gentle. A simple rule: treat yams like eggs. In Nigeria, a common saying is 'Ibi ti a ba ti fi owo kan isu, ni isu yoo ti baj' where you touch yam with force, it spoils. After handling, inspect tubers for cuts or soft spots. Separate damaged ones immediately. Proper handling can reduce post-harvest losses from 20-30% to under 10%.

4. Field Sorting and Grading of Yams

Sorting and grading right after harvest ensures you get the best price and reduces storage losses. Start by removing damaged, diseased, or cut tubers. These should be sold or eaten within a week. Then grade tubers by size, shape, and health. Common grades in West African markets: Grade A (export or premium): tubers 2-3 kg, straight, no blemishes. Grade B (local market): 1-2 kg, slight curves allowed. Grade C (processing): any size, but no rot. In Nigeria, yam grades are often 'big size' (above 2.5 kg), 'medium' (1.5-2.5 kg), and 'small' (below 1.5 kg). Use a simple wooden gauge or a weighing scale. For seed yams, grade by weight: 200-500 g for seed. Sort by variety too; white yam, water yam, and yellow yam have different market preferences. Remove any tuber with signs of nematode damage (cracks, galls) or rot (soft, smelly). Do not mix grades in one pile; buyers pay less for mixed lots. After sorting, count and record each grade. In Ghana, farmers use a 'sorting table' a raised platform with slats to make sorting easier. Work in the shade to prevent sunburn. Once sorted, move each grade to its own curing area. For seed yams, select only healthy, medium-sized tubers; small or oversized seed yams produce poor yields. Reject tubers with deep cuts, even if they look fine, because they will rot in storage. A good practice is to sort within 24 hours of harvest. This quick action prevents disease spread and ensures you meet buyer specifications. Remember: grading adds value with little cost.

5. Curing and Storage Preparation

Curing is the process of healing minor wounds and thickening the skin so yams store longer. After harvest and sorting, spread tubers in a single layer in a shaded, well-ventilated area for 4 to 7 days. The temperature should be 25-30C and humidity 60-70%. In humid areas, use a raised platform with slats to allow air circulation. Do not cure in full sun; sunburn causes rot. During curing, tubers lose about 5-10% of weight, but the skin hardens and cuts heal. After curing, prepare storage. Traditional yam barns are common in West Africa: build a rectangular structure with wooden poles, roofed with thatch or palm fronds. Inside, tie tubers individually to vertical poles using strong twine, leaving 5-10 cm between tubers. This allows air flow and easy inspection. In drier regions, you can store in pits: dig a pit 1 m deep, line with dry grass, place tubers in layers, cover with grass and soil, leaving a vent. But pits risk flooding; use only in well-drained soil. Modern storage: use ventilated crates stacked in a cool room. Keep temperature between 15-20C and humidity 70-80%. Never store yams in plastic bags; they sweat and rot. Inspect stored yams every two weeks; remove any sprouting or rotting tubers. Sprouting can be reduced by storing in darkness. For seed yams, store separately and handle carefully. Before storage, dust tubers with wood ash or a recommended fungicide to prevent rot. In Nigeria, farmers often use 'barn storage' where yams last 4-6 months. Proper curing and storage can reduce losses from 30% to 10% or less.

6. Packaging for Transport and Market

Packaging protects yams during transport and makes them attractive to buyers. For local markets, use strong woven baskets or wooden crates lined with dry grass or newspaper. Do not use plastic sacks; they trap moisture and cause rot. For long-distance transport, use ventilated plastic crates (60 cm x 40 cm x 30 cm) that stack without crushing. Each crate should hold no more than 20-25 kg of yams, placed in a single layer or two layers with padding between. In West Africa, yams are often transported in open trucks, so secure the load with ropes and cover with a tarpaulin to protect from sun and rain, but leave sides open for air. For export, use cardboard boxes with holes, each tuber wrapped in newspaper or tissue paper. Label boxes with grade, variety, and weight. For seed yams, pack in well-ventilated boxes with dry sawdust or rice husk. Never mix grades in one package. Before packing, ensure tubers are clean, cured, and free of defects. For market display, arrange tubers neatly in baskets or on mats; a clean, well-sorted display attracts higher prices. In Nigeria, a common packaging is the 'basket' (about 50 kg) for local trade, while 'jute bags' are used for transport but cause bruising; avoid them. For transport, load and unload carefully; do not throw baskets. Use a cushion of dry grass at the bottom of the truck. If you are selling to processors, pack in bulk bins. Always keep records of packages and weights. Good packaging reduces breakage and spoilage, and can increase your profit by 20-30% because buyers pay more for well-presented yams.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Harvesting and Post-Harvest Handling of Yams

Yam (Dioscorea spp.) is one of West Africa's most commercially significant tuber crops, with Nigeria alone producing roughly two-thirds of global output. For agribusiness operators, the margin between profit and loss is often decided not in the field but in the 72 hours after lifting. Bruised tubers lose market grade, poorly cured yams rot in transit, and inadequate packaging can wipe out 2030% of a consignment's value before it reaches the buyer. This reference consolidates the practical decision points across the harvest-to-market chain.

1. Determining Harvest Maturity Indicators

Harvesting too early reduces starch content and storability; harvesting too late exposes tubers to soil pests and rot as rains end. For Dioscorea rotundata (white yam), the commercial mainstay of Nigeria and Ghana, the standard window is 810 months after planting, coinciding with vine senescence. Indicators include yellowing and drying of vines, firm tuber skin, and the onset of the dry season. For seed yam production, a shorter 67 month cycle is used to reduce tuber size and cost.

2. Harvesting Tools and Techniques

Use a narrow-bladed spade or a yam hoe (locally forged) to avoid slicing tubers. Begin digging 3040 cm from the plant base, working downward and outward. The first cut should reveal the tuber head, after which the operator lifts using the handle as a lever. Never pull vines to extract tubersthis snaps the tuber at the neck and destroys market value.

3. Proper Lifting and Handling to Avoid Damage

Bruising is the single largest cause of post-harvest loss in yam. Once lifted, tubers should be placednot droppedinto a shaded holding area. A 2019 study by the International Institute of Tropical Agriculture (IITA) found that mechanical damage from rough handling increased rot incidence by up to 40% during storage.

SAFETY WARNING: Yam harvesting involves heavy manual lifting and sharp tools. Always wear closed boots and gloves. Do not lift tubers heavier than 25 kg aloneuse a two-person lift or a wheelbarrow. Fresh yam sap can cause skin irritation; wash exposed skin with clean water immediately. Never harvest during a thunderstorm or on waterlogged soil, where slips and tool injuries are common.

4. Field Sorting and Grading of Yams

Field sorting happens at the point of lifting. Separate tubers into: (a) premium ware yam (whole, uniform, no cuts), (b) marketable but damaged, and (c) seed yam or reject. This reduces transport weight and protects premium lots from contamination.

5. Curing and Storage Preparation

Curing is the process of allowing freshly harvested tubers to heal minor skin wounds in a shaded, well-ventilated space for 47 days. This forms a protective cork layer that resists rot. After curing, yams are stored in traditional barns (raised wooden platforms) or improved ventilated rooms. Storage losses in traditional barns range from 1020% over 36 months; improved storage can reduce this to under 8%.

6. Packaging for Transport and Market

For long-haul transport to Lagos, Accra, or Abidjan, yams should be packed in ventilated wooden crates or plastic crates lined with dry straw, newspaper, or dried plantain leaves. Stack no more than three to four layers to prevent crushing. Avoid polythene sacks for ware yamthey trap moisture and accelerate rot. For export, tubers are washed, dried, and individually wrapped in tissue before crating; compliance with destination phytosanitary rules (e.g., EU MRLs for pesticides) is mandatory.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Confirm maturity: vines yellowing, 810 months after planting, dry season onset.
  • Use a narrow spade or yam hoe; dig 3040 cm from the plant base.
  • Lift tubersnever pull vines; place, don't drop, into shade.
  • Sort in the field into Grade A, B, C, and reject before transport.
  • Cure 47 days in a shaded, ventilated area before storage or sale.
  • Pack in ventilated crates with dry lining; maximum 34 layers.
  • Label each crate with variety, weight, farm, and harvest date.
  • Keep records of losses at each stage to identify weak links in your chain.
