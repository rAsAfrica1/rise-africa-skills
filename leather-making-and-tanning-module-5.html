Lessons

1. Buffing and Snuffing

Buffing and snuffing are mechanical operations that prepare the leather surface for finishing. Buffing removes minor defects, raises a nap on the grain side, or cleans the flesh side. Snuffing, on the other hand, creates a velvety nap on the flesh side (suede) or corrects the grain side to make it more absorbent. In Africa, many small tanneries use drum or wheel buffers. A common setup is a 1.5 kW motor turning a 30 cm diameter buffing wheel at 1,200 rpm. The wheel is made of compressed cotton or felt. For buffing grain, use a fine abrasive wheel (240-320 grit) and light pressure. Move the leather back and forth to avoid burn marks. For snuffing flesh side, use a coarser wheel (80-120 grit) to raise a uniform nap. Always work with dry leather (moisture below 14%). If the leather is too damp, the wheel will clog and tear the surface. Safety first: wear a dust mask, goggles, and gloves. Buffing creates fine dust that can irritate lungs. In many African workshops, buffing is done manually with a hand-held sanding block for small items like belts and wallets. For larger hides, a buffing machine saves hours. After buffing, wipe the surface with a dry cloth to remove dust. If you are making suede, snuff the flesh side after tanning and drying. Test on a scrap piece first. Adjust wheel speed and pressure until you get an even, soft nap. Remember, over-buffing can thin the leather and weaken it. Practice on scrap until you can produce a consistent finish. In rural areas, you can improvise a buffing wheel using a bicycle wheel fitted with a leather pad, but ensure it is securely mounted and guarded.

2. Coating and Spraying

Coating and spraying apply a protective and decorative film to leather. This improves appearance, water resistance, and durability. Common coatings include acrylic, polyurethane, and nitrocellulose lacquers. In Africa, many artisans use locally available acrylic floor finishes or shoe polish as a base. For a professional finish, mix a topcoat: 1 part acrylic binder, 1 part water, and a few drops of defoamer. Apply with a spray gun (gravity feed, 1.4 mm nozzle) at 2-3 bar pressure. Hold the gun 20-25 cm from the leather and move in overlapping passes. For small items, use an airbrush or even a sponge. Always spray in a well-ventilated area or outdoors. Wear a respirator with organic vapor cartridges. Before spraying, clean the leather with a damp cloth and let it dry. If the leather is absorbent, apply a thin base coat of diluted binder (1:3 with water) and let it dry. Then apply 2-3 thin coats of topcoat, drying 15-20 minutes between coats. Too thick a coat will crack. For a matte finish, add 2% matting agent. For gloss, add 1% gloss enhancer. In humid African climates, add 1% crosslinker to improve adhesion and water resistance. After the final coat, let it cure for 24 hours before use. If you don't have a spray gun, you can pad on the coating with a lint-free cloth. This gives a more rustic look but works for belts, bags, and sandals. Test on scrap first. Store coatings sealed and away from direct sun. Clean your gun immediately after use with water or solvent.

3. Embossing and Printing

Embossing and printing create raised or recessed designs on leather. Embossing uses heat and pressure to stamp a pattern. Printing uses ink or dye to transfer a design. In Africa, traditional techniques like Nigerian leather stamping and Moroccan tooling are common. For embossing, you need a heated embossing plate (brass or steel) and a press. A simple hand press with a 2-tonne jack works for small items. Heat the plate to 80-100Â°C for grain leather, 60-80Â°C for suede. Place the leather on a hard rubber pad, position the plate, and press for 5-10 seconds. Too much heat burns the leather; too little gives a faint impression. For deep embossing, dampen the leather slightly (12-14% moisture). For printing, use a rubber stamp or screen with leather dye or pigment. Apply the dye evenly, press the stamp, and hold for 3-5 seconds. For multi-color prints, use separate stamps and let each color dry. In many African workshops, artisans carve wooden blocks for printing. You can also use a pyrography tool to burn designs. Always test on scrap. For embossing, practice with different temperatures and pressures. A common mistake is overheating, which makes the leather brittle. After embossing, apply a light coat of wax to protect the design. For printing, seal with a clear acrylic topcoat. If you are embossing a logo, make a metal plate from a local welder. Embossing and printing add value to plain leather, turning it into unique cultural products. Keep your plates clean and store them dry to prevent rust.

4. Glazing and Plating

Glazing and plating give leather a smooth, glossy surface. Glazing is done with a glass cylinder or agate stone rubbed vigorously over the leather. Plating uses a heated metal plate under pressure. In Africa, glazing is often done by hand with a glass bottle or a smooth stone. For glazing, first case the leather (dampen slightly) and apply a thin coat of gum tragacanth or egg white. Then rub the glass cylinder back and forth with firm pressure. The friction heat polishes the surface. Work in one direction until you see a shine. For plating, use a heated platen press. Set the plate to 60-80Â°C and 100-200 bar pressure for 2-3 seconds. This flattens the grain and gives a mirror finish. For small items, you can use a hand iron (like a clothes iron) set to low heat, but keep it moving to avoid scorching. Always test on scrap. Glazing and plating are often used on upper-grade leather for shoes, belts, and bags. In many African tanneries, plating is done after dyeing and before final coating. The key is to have a smooth, clean surface. Any dust or grit will scratch the leather. After glazing, apply a light wax and buff with a soft cloth. For plating, ensure the leather is dry (moisture below 12%). Too much moisture causes steam and blisters. If you don't have a press, you can achieve a similar effect by ironing with a Teflon sheet between the iron and leather. Practice on different leathers. Vegetable-tanned leather responds best to glazing. Chrome-tanned leather may need a light sanding before plating. Store glazed leather flat to avoid creases.

5. Polishing and Ironing

Polishing and ironing are final steps to give leather a clean, professional look. Polishing removes minor scratches and adds shine. Ironing flattens wrinkles and sets the finish. In Africa, many artisans use a simple hand iron or a heated stone. For polishing, apply a wax-based polish (beeswax mixed with carnauba wax) with a soft cloth. Rub in circles, then buff with a clean cloth or brush. For a high shine, use a polishing wheel (cotton) at 1,500 rpm. Hold the leather firmly and move it against the wheel. Don't press too hard or you'll burn the surface. For ironing, set an iron to 60-70Â°C (low setting). Place a thin cotton cloth or Teflon sheet between the iron and leather. Iron with slow, even strokes. This removes creases and gives a flat finish. For embossed or printed leather, iron on the reverse side or use a lower temperature. Always test on scrap. In humid climates, ironing also helps remove moisture and set the shape. After ironing, let the leather cool before handling. For a final polish, apply a small amount of leather conditioner and buff. In many African workshops, polishing is done with a piece of soft leather or a banana leaf. The goal is a uniform, attractive surface. Avoid over-ironing, which can make leather stiff and brittle. If you are working with suede, do not iron; instead, brush with a suede brush. For smooth leather, ironing is safe if you use a protective layer. Keep your iron clean; any dirt will transfer to the leather. Store finished leather in a dry, cool place.

6. Quality Control in Finishing

Quality control (QC) ensures every finished leather meets standards. In Africa, small tanneries often skip QC, leading to rejects. A simple QC routine can save money. Start with visual inspection: check for uneven color, scratches, spots, or missed areas. Use a bright light and a clean table. For coated leather, check adhesion by scratching with a fingernail. If the coating peels, it's a reject. For embossed leather, check depth and clarity. For glazed leather, check gloss uniformity. Measure thickness with a gauge; variation should be within Â±0.2 mm. Test flexibility by bending the leather over a 2 cm rod. It should not crack. Test water resistance by placing a drop of water on the surface; it should bead for at least 5 minutes. For suede, check nap uniformity and color fastness by rubbing with a white cloth. Record all results in a log. Common defects include: orange peel (rough coating), fish eyes (craters), and bleeding (dye migration). To fix minor defects, re-buff and re-coat. For major defects, downgrade the leather. In many African markets, buyers reject leather with visible flaws. Train your workers to check at each stage. Use a simple checklist: color, gloss, feel, thickness, and defects. Keep samples of good and bad finishes for reference. QC is not about perfection; it's about consistency. Even a small workshop can implement a 5-minute inspection per hide. This builds trust with buyers and reduces waste. Always document your QC results and review them weekly to improve your process.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Finishing and Surface Treatment of Leather

Finishing is where leather stops being a commodity and becomes a product. For African tanneries and leather workshops â from the tanneries of Fez and Marrakech to the small-scale units in Ethiopia, Nigeria, Kenya, South Africa and Egypt â the finishing line is the single biggest determinant of selling price, order size and repeat business. A correctly finished hide can sell for two to five times the value of a crust (unfinished) hide of the same origin. This lesson is a business-first practical reference: what each operation does, what it costs, what can go wrong, and how to control quality so that your leather leaves the yard ready for export or for high-value local markets such as footwear, upholstery, automotive trim, bags and belts.

The six finishing lessons in this module â Buffing and Snuffing, Coating and Spraying, Embossing and Printing, Glazing and Plating, Polishing and Ironing, and Quality Control â are not independent steps. They form a sequence, and a defect at one stage usually shows up at the next. Treat the whole line as one production system with one quality standard.

Buffing and Snuffing

Buffing raises a fine nap on the grain or flesh side using an abrasive cylinder; snuffing is the same operation applied lightly to correct small grain defects or to prepare the surface for coating. For African tanneries producing suede, nubuck, nappa and "velour" splits, this is a value-adding step: a plain split can be converted into a suede that sells into the footwear and garment trade. The main business risk is over-buffing â cutting through the grain and creating a "bald" patch that cannot be coated over. Dust extraction is essential for both worker health and product cleanliness.

Coating and Spraying

Coating builds the film that gives colour, protection and handle. It is applied by spray gun, curtain coater or roller coater. In small African workshops, airless spray is common; in larger tanneries, curtain coating gives better transfer efficiency and less overspray waste. Formulations are usually water-based acrylic or polyurethane base coats, followed by a top coat. The key commercial variable is solids content: higher solids mean fewer passes, less solvent, lower energy cost and better coverage per litre. Coating is also where most factory rejections originate â orange peel, pin-holes, runs, colour mismatch and poor adhesion.

Embossing and Printing

Embossing presses a heated plate or roller into the coated surface to create grain patterns, logos or textures. Printing applies pattern via roller or transfer. For African producers, embossing is a route to competing with imported "printed" leathers at lower cost, and a way to add brand identity for local footwear and bag makers. The critical controls are plate temperature, pressure, dwell time and moisture content of the leather. Too hot or too wet produces a shiny, plastic look; too cold produces a weak, shallow pattern that fades in wear.

Glazing and Plating

Glazing uses a heated glass or steel cylinder under pressure to produce a high-gloss, "patent" or "box calf" appearance. Plating is the same principle using a heated plate. This is a high-margin finish for dress shoes, belts and luxury accessories. The risk is heat damage: if the leather is too dry or the cylinder too hot, the finish cracks or the grain shrinks. Glazing is also a strong differentiator for African tanneries targeting premium local and regional markets.

Polishing and Ironing

Polishing uses a soft wheel or felt roller with wax or polish to bring up lustre and smoothness. Ironing uses a heated platen to flatten the leather and set the finish. Both are low-cost operations with high visual impact. They are usually the last mechanical steps before final inspection. Over-ironing can make leather stiff and brittle â a common complaint from footwear manufacturers who need flexibility.

Quality Control in Finishing

QC is not a final inspection; it is a set of gates. The standard tests for finished leather are colour matching against an approved standard under defined lighting, adhesion (tape or flex test), dry and wet rub-fastness, flex resistance, thickness and substance measurement, and visual grading for grain defects, scars and coating faults. For export orders, buyers increasingly require test reports from recognised laboratories. African tanneries that build a simple in-house QC record â batch number, operator, test result, pass/fail â win more repeat orders than those that rely on the owner's eye alone.

â  SAFETY WARNING â CHEMICAL AND THERMAL HAZARDS

Finishing uses solvent-based and water-based coatings, cross-linkers, formaldehyde-releasing agents, ammonia and hot plates/cylinders. In poorly ventilated African workshops this causes respiratory and skin disease. Always: (1) install local exhaust ventilation at spray booths and buffing machines; (2) wear nitrile gloves, goggles and a fitted respirator (not a dust mask) when spraying; (3) store solvents away from heat and never smoke near the line; (4) guard all heated cylinders and plates â contact burns are the most common finishing injury; (5) keep a written COSHH-style chemical register and train every operator on the safety data sheet before first use.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Check the crust before you finish it. Reject or downgrade hides with deep scars, holes or grain damage â finishing cannot hide them profitably.
  2. Control moisture. Target 12â14% moisture before coating and embossing; too wet causes weak patterns and poor adhesion.
  3. Record every batch. Batch number, operator, chemical lot, spray settings and test results â this is your evidence when a buyer complains.
  4. Test adhesion and rub-fastness on every batch, not just the first. Use a simple tape test and a wet rub test with a white cloth.
  5. Match colour under standard lighting (D65 or equivalent), never under mixed workshop light.
  6. Keep spray booth filters and ventilation clean. Blocked filters cause orange peel and expose workers to solvent vapour.
  7. Calibrate heat: embossing plates, glazing cylinders and irons must be checked with a surface thermometer each shift.
  8. Grade before packing, with a written standard. Separate first, second and reject piles and price them accordingly.
