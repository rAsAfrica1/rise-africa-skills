1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Stainless steel is everywhere in Africa: food processing plants in Kenya, dairy equipment in South Africa, chemical tanks in Nigeria, and architectural railings in Egypt. Unlike mild steel, stainless steel contains at least 10.5% chromium, which forms a protective oxide layer that resists rust. But that same chromium makes welding tricky. If you weld it wrong, you get 'sensitization' chromium carbide precipitates at grain boundaries, leaving the steel vulnerable to corrosion. In Africa's humid coastal cities like Mombasa or Lagos, a bad stainless weld can rust through in months.

You need to care because stainless steel jobs pay better. A skilled stainless welder in Nairobi can earn 2-3 times more than a mild steel welder. Industries like breweries (e.g., East African Breweries), dairies (Brookside), and hospitals need sanitary stainless piping and tanks. The demand is high, but the supply of trained welders is low. This module will teach you the specific techniques TIG welding with DCEN, back purging, controlled heat input that make stainless welds last.

We'll focus on 304 and 316 stainless, the most common grades in Africa. 304 is for general use; 316 has molybdenum for chloride resistance, essential for coastal or chemical environments. You'll learn to set your machine, choose filler rods (308L for 304, 316L for 316), and avoid the mistakes that cause cracking and corrosion. By the end, you'll be able to weld a stainless steel water tank that passes a pressure test and a visual inspection.

This isn't theory. Every lesson is practical, using tools you can find in any African welding shop: an inverter TIG machine, argon gas, ceriated tungsten, and stainless filler rods. You'll practice on scrap before working on real jobs. Remember: stainless steel is expensive, so measure twice, weld once. Let's get started.

2. Tools and Materials

To weld stainless steel properly, you need a TIG (Tungsten Inert Gas) welder with DCEN (Direct Current Electrode Negative). A 200-amp inverter TIG machine is ideal; brands like Miller, Lincoln, or local Chinese imports (e.g., Riland) work. You'll also need a foot pedal or torch switch to control amperage. For stainless, amperage ranges from 40-80 amps for 1.6mm sheet, up to 120-150 amps for 6mm plate. Always use pure argon gas (99.99% purity) at 8-12 litres per minute flow rate. In Africa, gas suppliers like BOC or Afrox can refill your cylinder.

Tungsten electrodes: use 2% ceriated (grey tip) or 1.5% lanthanated (gold tip). They handle DC better than pure tungsten. Grind the tungsten to a sharp point, lengthwise, on a dedicated grinding wheel (never use the same wheel for steel, to avoid contamination). For filler rods, match the base metal: ER308L for 304 stainless, ER316L for 316. Rod diameters: 1.6mm for thin sheet, 2.4mm for thicker sections. You'll also need a stainless steel wire brush (never use a carbon steel brush) and acetone or denatured alcohol for cleaning.

Other essentials: a gas lens kit for better argon coverage, a #7 or #8 ceramic cup, and a back purge setup if welding pipes or tanks. Back purging uses argon inside the pipe to prevent oxidation on the backside of the weld. You can make a simple purge dam with aluminum foil and tape. For cutting stainless, use a plasma cutter or an angle grinder with a stainless-specific cutting disc (not the same disc you use for mild steel).

Personal protective equipment: auto-darkening welding helmet (shade 9-11 for TIG), leather gloves, and a respirator. Stainless welding produces hexavalent chromium fumes, which are carcinogenic. In a closed shop in Johannesburg or Cairo, you must have fume extraction or good ventilation. Finally, a stainless steel scratch brush and pickling paste (or a citric acid gel) for post-weld cleaning. These tools are available from welding supply shops in most African cities; if not, order online from suppliers like Weldability or Amazon.

3. Step by Step Method

Step 1: Clean the metal. Stainless must be spotless. Wipe the joint and 25mm around it with acetone. Remove any oil, grease, or marker ink. Use a new stainless wire brush to scrub the surface. Do not use a brush that has touched carbon steel it will embed iron particles and cause rust.

Step 2: Set up your machine. For 2mm stainless sheet, set the TIG machine to DCEN, 60-80 amps. Set argon flow to 10 L/min. Grind your ceriated tungsten to a sharp point. Install a #7 gas lens cup. If welding pipe or a tank, set up back purging: seal one end, flow argon at 5 L/min into the other end for 2 minutes before welding. Use a oxygen meter to check that oxygen is below 1% inside.

Step 3: Tack weld. Clamp the pieces together. Use the same settings. Make small tacks every 50mm. For thin sheet, use a heat sink (aluminum block) behind the joint to prevent warping. Let each tack cool before the next. Check alignment with a square.

Step 4: Weld the joint. For butt welds on 2mm sheet, use a keyhole technique: start at one end, create a small keyhole (a hole that opens up), then move forward, adding filler rod as you go. Dip the rod into the leading edge of the puddle. Keep the torch angle at 70-80 degrees, pushing forward. For lap joints, direct the arc at the edge of the top plate to melt both. For T-joints, angle the torch 45 degrees and add filler to the corner. Maintain a tight arc (tungsten 2-3mm from the work). Move steadily; if you go too slow, you'll burn through; too fast, you'll get incomplete fusion.

Step 5: Post-weld cleaning. After welding, the weld and heat-affected zone will have a rainbow tint (oxidation). Brush with a stainless wire brush. For critical applications, apply pickling paste (contains nitric and hydrofluoric acid wear gloves and goggles) or use an electrochemical cleaning machine. Rinse with water after 10-20 minutes. For sanitary welds, you may need to grind and polish the weld to a smooth finish.

Step 6: Inspect. Look for undercut, porosity, or cracks. A good stainless weld is shiny and has a smooth, consistent bead. If you see black spots, you had too much heat or poor gas coverage. Practice on scrap until you can produce a consistent bead.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Using the wrong gas. Some welders try to use CO2 or a CO2/argon mix (like MIG gas) for TIG. That will cause carbon pickup and corrosion. Fix: Always use pure argon (99.99%). If you only have a MIG machine, you can use it with stainless wire and a tri-mix gas (90% helium, 7.5% argon, 2.5% CO2), but TIG is preferred.

Mistake 2: Too much heat. Stainless has low thermal conductivity, so heat stays in the weld zone. If you use the same amperage as mild steel, you'll burn through or cause distortion. Fix: Reduce amperage by 20-30% compared to mild steel. Use a foot pedal to ramp down at the end. For thin sheet, use a heat sink or pulse mode.

Mistake 3: No back purge. When welding pipes or tanks, the backside of the weld oxidizes (sugaring) if not protected. That sugar is a corrosion site. Fix: Always back purge with argon. If you can't purge, use a flux-coated rod (like E308L-16) for stick welding, but that's only for non-critical work.

Mistake 4: Contaminated tungsten. If you dip the tungsten into the puddle, it gets contaminated and the arc wanders. Fix: Stop, remove the tungsten, break off the contaminated tip, regrind, and restart. Keep a dedicated grinder for tungsten.

Mistake 5: Using carbon steel brushes. That embeds iron into the stainless surface, causing rust spots. Fix: Use only stainless steel brushes, and keep them separate.

Mistake 6: Welding without cleaning. Oil, paint, or marker ink causes porosity and cracks. Fix: Clean with acetone and a stainless brush before welding.

Mistake 7: Fast cooling. Quenching a hot stainless weld with water can cause cracking. Fix: Let it air cool, or use a heat sink to draw heat away slowly.

Mistake 8: Wrong filler rod. Using mild steel filler on stainless will rust and crack. Fix: Match the filler to the base metal: 308L for 304, 316L for 316.

If you make a mistake, don't panic. Grind out the bad weld and start again. Stainless is forgiving if you follow the rules.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Welding stainless steel produces hexavalent chromium and nickel fumes, which can cause cancer, asthma, and skin irritation. In Africa, many workshops are poorly ventilated. You must protect yourself. Always weld in a well-ventilated area or use a fume extractor. A simple fan blowing fumes away from your breathing zone helps, but it can disrupt shielding gas. Better: use a respirator with P100 filters. 3M or Honeywell respirators are available in most African cities. If you smell a sweet, metallic taste, you're breathing fumes stop and ventilate.

Arc rays from TIG welding are intense. Use an auto-darkening helmet with a shade 9-11 for stainless. Never look at the arc without a helmet. Wear leather gloves, a leather apron, and closed shoes. TIG welding produces less spatter than stick, but the arc can still burn you. Keep a fire extinguisher nearby. In a shop with wood or fabric, clear the area of flammable materials.

Gas safety: Argon is an asphyxiant. It's heavier than air, so it can pool in low areas (pits, tanks). If you're welding inside a tank, you must have forced ventilation and a safety watch outside. Never use argon to 'cool' yourself it can cause immediate suffocation. Check cylinders for leaks with soapy water. Secure cylinders upright with a chain.

Electrical safety: TIG machines use high frequency for arc starting. Keep your gloves dry. Ground the work piece properly. Don't weld in wet conditions. In countries with unstable power (e.g., Nigeria), use a voltage stabilizer to protect your machine.

Best practices: Plan your welds to minimize distortion. Use the least amount of heat possible. Clean as you go. Label your stainless brushes and tools so they don't get contaminated. After welding, clean the weld with a stainless brush and pickling paste. For food-grade work, passivate the surface with citric acid. Document your settings for each job amperage, gas flow, filler rod so you can repeat good results. Finally, practice on scrap before welding a customer's expensive stainless part. A small mistake can cost thousands of naira or shillings.

6. Practical Exercise

Exercise: Weld a 304 stainless steel water tank. You will need two pieces of 2mm thick 304 stainless sheet, each 300mm x 300mm. You will form a 90-degree corner and weld the seam. This simulates a real tank corner. Tools: TIG welder (200A), pure argon cylinder, 2% ceriated tungsten (2.4mm), #7 gas lens cup, ER308L filler rod (1.6mm), stainless wire brush, acetone, clamps, and a heat sink (aluminum block).

Step 1: Clean both sheets with acetone and a stainless brush. Mark a 10mm bend line on each sheet. Using a sheet metal brake or a hammer and angle iron, bend a 90-degree flange on each sheet. The flanges should be 10mm wide. Clamp the two flanges together to form a corner. Check for a tight fit gaps should be less than 0.5mm.

Step 2: Set up your TIG machine. DCEN, 70 amps. Argon flow 10 L/min. Grind tungsten to a sharp point. If you have a foot pedal, set it to control amperage. Place an aluminum heat sink behind the joint to prevent warping. Tack weld at both ends and in the middle. Let each tack cool.

Step 3: Weld the seam. Start at one end. Use a keyhole technique: create a small keyhole at the start, then move forward, adding filler rod with a dip-and-move rhythm. Keep the arc tight. The weld should be a smooth, concave bead. If you burn through, stop, let it cool, and reduce amperage to 60. If you get a convex bead, you're adding too much filler; use less.

Step 4: After welding, inspect the weld. It should have a light gold or straw color, not black or grey. If black, you had too much heat or poor gas coverage. Brush the weld with a stainless brush. Apply pickling paste if available, or use a citric acid gel. Let it sit for 15 minutes, then rinse.

Step 5: Test for leaks. Fill the tank with water and check for leaks. If you find a leak, mark it, grind it out, and re-weld. For a food-grade tank, you may need to polish the weld. Repeat this exercise with 316 stainless and ER316L filler. Practice until you can produce a consistent, leak-free weld. This skill is in high demand for dairy and brewery tanks across Africa.

<h3>Key Practical Rules for the Bench</h3>

<ul>
<li><strong>Cleanliness is everything.</strong> Stainless steel must be free of oil, grease, paint, and carbon steel contamination. Use dedicated stainless-only wire brushes, grinding discs, and gloves. A carbon steel brush will embed iron and cause rust.</li>
<li><strong>Control heat input.</strong> Use the lowest amperage that gives full fusion. Interpass temperature should stay below 150 C. For 316L, this reduces sensitisation.</li>
<li><strong>Purge the back of the weld.</strong> For pipe and hygienic work, back-purge with argon to prevent oxidation (sugaring) on the inside surface. This is non-negotiable for food and dairy clients.</li>
<li><strong>Use the right filler.</strong> Match filler to base metal: ER308L for 304, ER316L for 316. Never use carbon steel filler on stainless.</li>
<li><strong>Post-weld cleaning.</strong> Remove heat tint by pickling, passivation, or mechanical methods. Heat tint destroys the passive chromium oxide layer and is the starting point for corrosion.</li>
<li><strong>Distortion control.</strong> Use tack welds, balanced sequences, and heat sinks. Stainless moves more than mild steel.</li>
</ul>

<p style="margin:0;color:#c1121f;font-weight:bold;font-size:16px"> SAFETY WARNING Hexavalent Chromium and Welding Fumes</p>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#333">Welding stainless steel produces fumes containing <strong>hexavalent chromium (Cr(VI))</strong> and nickel, which are carcinogenic and can cause asthma and skin sensitisation. In enclosed African workshop conditions common where ventilation is poor this risk is high. <strong>Always:</strong> weld with local exhaust ventilation or fume extraction at the arc; wear a powered air-purifying respirator (PAPR) or at minimum a P100/FFP3 respirator; keep your head out of the fume plume; and never weld in a confined space without forced ventilation and a spotter. Also protect against arc radiation (UV) with proper helmet shade (minimum shade 1013 for TIG), and cover exposed skin. <em>Source: <a href="https://www.osha.gov/hexavalent-chromium" target="_blank" rel="noopener">US OSHA, "Hexavalent Chromium"</a>; <a href="https://www.hse.gov.uk/welding/faqs.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener">UK HSE, "Welding FAQs"</a>.</em></p>

<h3>Business-First Notes for African Workshops</h3>

<p>Stainless steel work is a margin opportunity. Food and beverage processors, dairies, breweries, pharmaceutical plants, and water utilities across Africa need hygienic stainless fabrication and repair. They pay for reliability and documentation. To win and keep this work:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Quote with a procedure.</strong> Present a simple welding procedure specification (WPS) and, where required, a welder qualification record. Clients in regulated sectors (food, pharma) increasingly ask for this.</li>
<li><strong>Invest in gas supply.</strong> A reliable argon cylinder or bulk supply is the single biggest enabler of quality stainless work. Partner with a local gas supplier such as <a href="https://www.afrox.com" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Afrox</a> (South Africa) or regional distributors.</li>
<li><strong>Train on scrap before production.</strong> Run test coupons, cut and etch them, and check penetration. This reduces rework and protects your reputation.</li>
<li><strong>Price for post-weld treatment.</strong> Pickling paste, passivation chemicals, and labour are real costs. Include them.</li>
<li><strong>Keep records.</strong> Material certificates, filler batch numbers, and gas purity records protect you if a client disputes corrosion failure.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ol>
<li>Confirm base metal grade (304, 316L, etc.) and match filler (ER308L / ER316L).</li>
<li>Clean joint and surrounding 25 mm with stainless-only tools; remove all oil, paint, and carbon steel contamination.</li>
<li>Set amperage from Table 2 and verify on a test coupon before production.</li>
<li>Set argon flow (815 L/min) and check for leaks; use a gas lens for better coverage.</li>
<li>Back-purge pipe and hygienic joints with argon to prevent sugaring.</li>
<li>Control interpass temperature (below 150 C) and use balanced weld sequence to limit distortion.</li>
<li>Inspect for heat tint, undercut, and lack of fusion; remove tint by pickling or mechanical cleaning.</li>
<li>Passivate the weld and HAZ to restore corrosion resistance before handover.</li>
</ol>
