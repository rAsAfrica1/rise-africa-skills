1. Beeswax: Processing and Uses

Beeswax is the second most valuable hive product after honey. A strong colony produces 1"2 kg of wax per year, but you can harvest more by melting down old combs, cappings, and burr comb. To process wax, first rinse the comb in clean water to remove honey and debris. Then break it into small pieces and melt it in a double boiler or a solar wax melter. Never use direct fire"wax is flammable and scorches easily. For a simple solar melter, line a wooden box with black plastic, place a metal tray on a slight slope, and cover with glass. The sun melts the wax, which runs through a cloth filter into a container. For larger batches, use a pot of water: bring water to a boil, add wax pieces, stir gently, then let cool. The wax floats and hardens on top. Remove it, scrape off any dirt, and remelt if needed. Store wax in clean, dry blocks.

Uses of beeswax are many. In cosmetics, it thickens lotions, lip balms, and soaps. In candle making, it burns cleanly and slowly. It waterproofs leather and fabric. It is used in batik and tie-dye as a resist. It also makes furniture polish and food wraps. In Africa, beeswax is sold to cosmetics companies, candle makers, and export markets. A kilogram of clean beeswax can sell for $5"$15, depending on quality and location. To get top price, your wax must be free of honey, propolis, and debris. Filter it through a fine cloth or nylon stocking. Avoid overheating"wax darkens and loses value above 85C. Always label your wax with the date and source. With practice, you can turn waste comb into a steady income stream.

2. Propolis: Collection and Benefits

Propolis is a sticky, resinous substance that bees make from tree buds, sap, and other plant sources. They use it to seal cracks, strengthen combs, and protect the hive from bacteria and fungi. For beekeepers, propolis is a valuable product with medicinal and commercial uses. To collect propolis, use a propolis trap"a plastic or wooden grid with narrow slots that you place on top of the hive under the lid. Bees fill the slots with propolis. After a few weeks, remove the trap and put it in the freezer for a few hours. The propolis becomes brittle and you can pop it out easily. Alternatively, scrape propolis from frames, hive walls, and entrance reducers with a hive tool. Wear gloves"propolis is sticky and stains. Collect in small amounts and store in an airtight container in a cool, dark place. A strong hive can produce 100"300 grams of propolis per year.

Propolis has many benefits. It is antibacterial, antiviral, and anti-inflammatory. People use it for sore throats, wounds, and skin infections. It is sold as tinctures, capsules, creams, and throat sprays. In Africa, propolis is gaining popularity in natural medicine and cosmetics. To make a simple tincture, soak 10 grams of crushed propolis in 100 ml of 70% alcohol for two weeks, shaking daily. Strain and bottle. Sell in dark dropper bottles. Always label with ingredients and date. For market, emphasize its natural origin and health benefits. You can sell raw propolis to processors or make your own value-added products. Quality matters: clean propolis with less wax and debris fetches higher prices. With minimal effort, propolis adds another income stream to your bee farm.

3. Royal Jelly and Bee Venom

Royal jelly is a milky-white secretion from nurse bees that feeds queen larvae. It is rich in proteins, vitamins, and fatty acids. Beekeepers harvest it by grafting young larvae into queen cups and placing them in a strong hive. After three days, the bees deposit royal jelly in the cups. You then remove the larvae and scoop out the jelly with a small spatula. This is delicate work. You need a grafting tool, queen cups, and a cool, clean workspace. A single queen cell yields about 200"300 mg of royal jelly. A strong hive can produce 30"50 grams per harvest if managed well. Royal jelly must be stored immediately in a refrigerator or freezer. It spoils quickly at room temperature. It is sold fresh, freeze-dried, or in capsules. It is expensive"up to $100 per kilogram. Handle with care and keep everything sterile.

Bee venom is another high-value product. Bees produce venom in their sting. To collect it, use a venom collector"a glass plate with wires that deliver a mild electric shock. When bees land on the wires, they sting and deposit venom on the glass. The venom dries into crystals. Scrape it off with a razor blade and store in a dark, airtight vial. One hive can produce 1"2 grams of venom per collection, and you can collect every two weeks without harming the colony. Bee venom is used in apitherapy for arthritis and pain relief. It sells for $30"$100 per gram. Both royal jelly and bee venom require extra training and equipment. Start with honey and wax first, then add these specialty products when you have experience and a reliable market.

4. Pollen: Collection and Storage

Pollen is the male seed of flowers, collected by bees as a protein source. It is packed with vitamins, minerals, and antioxidants. Beekeepers collect pollen using a pollen trap"a device with small holes that bees must squeeze through to enter the hive. As they pass, some pollen pellets fall off their legs into a tray. Use a trap for only a few days at a time, and never during dearth periods, to avoid stressing the colony. A strong hive can yield 50"100 grams of pollen per day when flowers are abundant. Collect the pollen daily and clean it by removing debris, bees, and wax. Fresh pollen has high moisture and can ferment. Dry it immediately in a food dehydrator at 40C for 2"4 hours, or in a warm, ventilated room away from direct sunlight. Once dry, store in airtight jars in a cool, dark place. It keeps for up to one year.

Pollen is sold as a health food, often mixed with honey or taken as a supplement. It is also used in cosmetics and as a protein boost for animal feed. In Africa, pollen is less known but demand is growing in urban health markets. You can sell it raw, granulated, or in capsules. A kilogram of dried pollen can fetch $20"$50. To add value, mix pollen with honey (1 part pollen to 3 parts honey) and sell as a spread. Always label with harvest date and source. Avoid overheating pollen"it destroys nutrients. For best quality, collect from diverse flowers and keep your trap clean. Pollen collection is a simple way to increase hive income with minimal investment.

5. Making Value-Added Products

Value-added products turn raw hive goods into higher-priced items. Start with honey. You can make creamed honey by stirring crystallized honey until smooth, then letting it set. Flavored honey"with ginger, lemon, or chili"sells well in local markets. Infuse clean, dry herbs or spices in honey for two weeks, then strain. For beeswax, make candles, lip balms, and soaps. A simple lip balm: melt 30g beeswax, 30g shea butter, and 30ml coconut oil. Pour into small tins. For soap, mix 500g melted beeswax, 500g coconut oil, and 500g olive oil with 200g lye dissolved in 500ml water (careful"lye is caustic). Stir until trace, pour into molds, cure for 4 weeks. Propolis tincture: soak 10g propolis in 100ml 70% alcohol for 2 weeks. Royal jelly: sell fresh or freeze-dried in capsules. Pollen: mix with honey as a spread.

Packaging matters. Use clean glass jars for honey, dark bottles for tinctures, and airtight containers for wax. Label with product name, weight, ingredients, and your contact. A simple logo and story""from the hills of [your region]""adds appeal. Sell at local markets, health shops, and online. Price your products by calculating raw material cost, packaging, and your time, then add 30"50% profit. For example, if 1 kg honey costs $5 and you make 10 lip balms from $2 wax, each balm costs $0.20 in materials; sell at $2"$3. Start small, test the market, and scale up. Value-added products increase your income and reduce dependence on bulk honey sales.

6. Packaging and Marketing Hive Products

Packaging protects your products and attracts buyers. For honey, use food-grade glass or plastic jars with tight lids. Glass is best for premium markets. Choose sizes: 250g, 500g, 1kg. Label with product name, weight, your farm name, and contact. Include a batch number and harvest date. For beeswax, wrap in paper or sell in blocks in cloth bags. Propolis tincture goes in dark glass dropper bottles. Royal jelly in small opaque jars. Pollen in airtight jars. Always use clean, dry containers. Sterilize jars with hot water and let them dry completely. Avoid reusing containers that held chemicals. A simple, attractive label can be printed at a local print shop. Include a QR code linking to your social media or WhatsApp.

Marketing starts with your story. Tell customers where your hive is, how you care for bees, and why your products are pure. Sell at farmers' markets, health food stores, and directly to consumers. Use social media"WhatsApp, Facebook, Instagram"to show photos and videos of your hives and products. Offer free samples. Build relationships with local shops, pharmacies, and cosmetics makers. For export, you need certification and consistent quality. Start local, then expand. Price competitively: research what others charge. Offer discounts for bulk buys. Attend trade fairs and beekeeping association meetings. Network with other beekeepers to share tips and find buyers. Remember, repeat customers come from trust and quality. Always deliver what you promise. With good packaging and smart marketing, your hive products can reach far beyond your village.

7. Practical Reference


Practical Reference: Hive Products and By-Products



Hive products beyond honey " beeswax, propolis, royal jelly, bee venom, pollen, and value-added goods " are where many African beekeepers find their real margin. Honey is bulky, seasonal, and price-sensitive. Wax, propolis, and cosmetics-grade bee products are light, storable, and can be sold in small, high-value units to local and export buyers. This module gives you the practical reference you need to harvest, process, and sell these products profitably in an African context.





Lesson 1: Beeswax " Processing and Uses


Beeswax is the easiest hive by-product to process and the most reliable cash earner after honey. In Kenya, Tanzania, and Ethiopia, clean wax sells for 2"4- the price per kilogram of honey. The key is rendering clean, light-coloured wax free of honey, propolis, and debris.


Processing steps: (1) Soak cappings and old comb in clean water for 24 hours to remove honey. (2) Melt in a solar wax melter or a double boiler " never over open flame. (3) Strain through a fine cloth or muslin into a mould. (4) Cool slowly to avoid cracks. Repeat filtering for export-grade wax.

Lesson 2: Propolis " Collection and Benefits


Propolis is the resinous "bee glue" bees make from plant saps. It has antibacterial, antifungal, and anti-inflammatory properties, making it valuable in cosmetics, oral care, and nutraceuticals. African propolis is often rich in flavonoids from acacia, eucalyptus, and miombo woodland species.


Collection: Use propolis traps (plastic grids with small gaps) placed on top of the hive. Bees fill the gaps; you freeze the trap for 2 hours, then flex it to release the propolis. A strong colony can yield 100"300 g per year. Store in airtight dark jars away from heat.



Lesson 3: Royal Jelly and Bee Venom


Royal jelly and bee venom are high-value but require skill and safety. Royal jelly is secreted by nurse bees and is sold fresh (refrigerated) or freeze-dried. Bee venom is collected using electric venom collectors at the hive entrance " a specialist activity that requires protective gear and training.


Market reality: Royal jelly sells for USD 80"200/kg and bee venom for USD 30"100/g in international markets. However, African producers face certification, cold-chain, and buyer-trust barriers. Start with beeswax and propolis, then graduate to these products once you have a confirmed buyer.




SAFETY WARNING " Bee Venom and Royal Jelly Handling

Bee venom collection involves live bees and electrical devices. Never attempt venom collection without a full bee suit, gloves, and a trained supervisor. Venom can trigger severe allergic reactions (anaphylaxis) in handlers and bystanders. Royal jelly must be kept below 4C at all times; contaminated or warm product can cause serious food-safety incidents. Keep children and visitors away from venom collection areas. In case of a sting reaction with breathing difficulty, seek emergency medical help immediately.




Lesson 4: Pollen " Collection and Storage


Bee pollen is collected using pollen traps fitted at the hive entrance. Traps should be used for no more than 2"3 days per week to avoid stressing the colony. Fresh pollen has 20"30% moisture and spoils quickly.


Drying and storage: Dry pollen at 40"45C (solar dryer or food dehydrator) to below 8% moisture. Store in airtight, dark containers; refrigerate for long-term storage. Pollen sells locally to health shops and is increasingly exported as a superfood.



Lesson 5: Making Value-Added Products


The fastest way to increase income is to convert raw hive products into finished goods. High-margin examples for African producers:



  • Beeswax candles " sell for 3"5- the wax price.

  • Propolis tincture " 30% propolis in food-grade alcohol; sold in 30 ml dropper bottles.

  • Lip balms and ointments " beeswax + shea butter + essential oils.

  • Honey-pollen energy bars " combine honey, pollen, nuts, and dried fruit.

  • Gift hampers " honey, candles, soap, and balm in a branded box for tourism and corporate markets.

Lesson 6: Packaging and Marketing Hive Products


Packaging is not decoration " it is the difference between selling at a roadside stall and supplying a supermarket or export buyer. Use food-grade, airtight containers. Label with product name, weight, batch number, producer contact, and country of origin. For export, comply with EU, UK, or Gulf market labelling rules.


Marketing channels in Africa: local health shops, pharmacies, supermarkets, hotels and lodges, farmers' markets, WhatsApp/Instagram direct sales, and export via fair-trade or organic-certified aggregators. Certification (organic, fair trade, HACCP) opens premium markets but requires record-keeping from day one.



Quick Reference Checklist



  1. Harvest only clean, food-grade wax; never mix with honey or propolis.

  2. Store all hive products in airtight, dark, cool containers.

  3. Use pollen traps no more than 2"3 days per week.

  4. Keep royal jelly below 4C at all times.

  5. Never collect bee venom without a full suit and trained supervisor.

  6. Label every product with weight, batch, and producer contact.

  7. Keep a daily record of harvest, processing, and sales.

  8. Secure at least one confirmed buyer before scaling a new product.
