1. Collecting Crop Residues: Maize Stalks, Rice Straw, and More

After harvest, your field is full of free compost material. Maize stalks, rice straw, sorghum stover, millet stalks, and groundnut haulms are all valuable. The key is to collect them before they are burned or eaten by livestock. For maize, cut stalks at ground level with a sharp panga or sickle. Chop them into 5-10 cm piecesâsmaller pieces decompose faster. A cubic meter of chopped maize stalks weighs about 150-200 kg. You need roughly 1 cubic meter of crop residues to make 0.5 cubic meters of finished compost. Rice straw is high in silica and decomposes slowly, so mix it with nitrogen-rich materials like animal manure or fresh grass. Never compost diseased plants (e.g., maize with smut) or seeds of aggressive weeds like striga. Store residues in a dry, covered pile or under a tarp to keep them from getting waterlogged. If you have livestock, you can also use crop residues as bedding, then compost the bedding. Collect after each harvest and keep a dedicated pile. In dry areas, residues break down slowly, so add water and turn the pile every two weeks. A good mix by volume: 3 parts crop residues to 1 part manure or fresh green matter. This gives the right carbon-to-nitrogen ratio. Start collecting todayâevery stalk is money in your soil.

2. Using Animal Manure: Cattle, Goats, Poultry, and Rabbits

Animal manure is the richest free fertilizer you can get. But not all manures are equal. Poultry manure (chicken, duck) is the strongest: it contains about 3-5% nitrogen, 2-3% phosphorus, and 1-2% potassium. Rabbit manure is also excellentâ2-3% nitrogenâand can be used fresh without burning plants. Goat and sheep manure are similar, around 2% nitrogen. Cattle manure is weaker (0.5-1% nitrogen) but adds lots of organic matter. Collect manure daily or weekly. For poultry, scoop it from under roosts; for cattle, from kraals or stalls. Mix manure with crop residues or bedding to prevent nitrogen loss. Never use fresh manure directly on vegetablesâit can burn roots and spread pathogens. Compost it first: pile manure with straw or leaves, keep it as moist as a wrung-out sponge, and turn every 3-4 days for 3-4 weeks. A hot pile (55-65Â°C) kills weed seeds and parasites. If you have rabbits, place their cages over a compost pileâurine and droppings fall straight in. For cattle, collect manure mixed with urine-soaked bedding. One cow produces about 10-15 kg of manure per day. A goat gives 1-2 kg. A chicken gives 0.1-0.2 kg. Even a small flock of 10 chickens gives you 1-2 kg of high-nitrogen manure daily. Store manure under cover to prevent rain washing away nutrients. Use it within 3 months for best results.

3. Kitchen and Market Waste: Fruit Peels, Vegetable Trimmings

Your kitchen and local market are goldmines for compost. Fruit peels (banana, mango, orange, pineapple), vegetable trimmings (tomato, onion, cabbage, carrot), coffee grounds, tea leaves, and eggshells are all excellent. These materials are high in nitrogen and decompose quickly. Collect them in a bucket with a lid to avoid flies and smells. At the market, ask vendors for spoiled produceâthey often throw away large quantities. A single market stall can generate 5-10 kg of waste per day. Mix kitchen waste with dry materials like sawdust, dry leaves, or shredded paper to absorb moisture and prevent odor. The ideal mix is 1 part kitchen waste to 2 parts dry material by volume. Avoid meat, fish, dairy, and oily foodsâthey attract rats and dogs and smell terrible. Also avoid citrus peels in large amounts; they can slow decomposition and repel worms. But small amounts are fine. If you have a worm bin, fruit and vegetable scraps are perfect worm food. Chop large pieces to speed up composting. In a hot climate, a pile with kitchen waste can heat up within 24 hours. Turn it every 2-3 days. If you run a restaurant or hotel, you can collect 20-50 kg per day. That is enough to make compost for a small farm. Always cover fresh waste with a layer of dry material or soil to keep flies away. Use within a week to avoid rotting and attracting pests.

4. Weeds, Grass Clippings, and Leaves: What to Use and Avoid

Weeds and grass clippings are free and abundant. But you must be careful. Use weeds before they set seed. If you pull weeds with seeds, do not compost them unless your pile gets hot enough (55-65Â°C) to kill seeds. Otherwise, seeds will spread when you use the compost. Safe weeds: amaranth, blackjack, purslane, and most broadleaf weeds before flowering. Avoid aggressive weeds like striga, couch grass, and nutgrassâtheir roots and seeds survive composting. Grass clippings are high in nitrogen. But do not use clippings from lawns treated with herbicides or pesticidesâthey can harm your compost and plants. Also avoid grass that has been sprayed with weed killer. Leaves are high in carbon. Dry leaves decompose slowly, so chop or shred them. Oak and eucalyptus leaves are acidic and slow to break down; use them in small amounts. Banana leaves, mango leaves, and most tropical leaves are fine. Collect leaves in sacks after they fall. A large tree can give you 50-100 kg of dry leaves per season. Mix leaves with grass clippings or manure to balance carbon and nitrogen. Never use leaves from diseased trees. Also avoid thorny branches unless you chop them finely. If you have a lawn, collect clippings after mowing. Let them dry for a day before adding to the pile to prevent matting. A good ratio: 2 parts dry leaves to 1 part fresh grass clippings. Turn the pile regularly. This mix heats up fast and gives rich compost in 4-6 weeks.

5. Sourcing Wood Ash, Charcoal Dust, and Bone Meal

Wood ash, charcoal dust, and bone meal are mineral-rich amendments. Wood ash from untreated wood contains potassium (5-10%) and calcium, plus trace minerals. It raises soil pH, so use it on acidic soils. Do not use ash from charcoal briquettes (they contain chemicals) or from treated or painted wood. Collect ash from your cooking fire or local bakeries. Store it dryâmoisture makes it lose potency. Add small amounts to compost: 1 cup per 10 kg of compost. Too much ash can make compost alkaline. Charcoal dust (from charcoal production or crushing charcoal) is pure carbon. It does not add nutrients but improves soil structure and holds nutrients. Mix it into compost at 5-10% by volume. It also absorbs odors. Bone meal is made from crushed, steamed animal bones. It is high in phosphorus (15-20%) and calcium. You can make it at home: boil bones to remove meat, dry them in the sun, then crush with a hammer or grind. It is slow-release, so add it to compost or directly to planting holes. A handful per plant is enough. If you cannot make bone meal, ask butcheries for bones. Wood ash and bone meal are especially good for root crops like carrots, onions, and potatoes. Store all three in dry containers. Use ash within 6 months; bone meal lasts a year if kept dry. These materials turn waste into valuable fertilizer.

6. Making a Materials Collection Plan for Your Business

To run a compost business, you need a steady supply of materials. Start by mapping your sources. Walk around your neighborhood, market, farms, and livestock keepers. Make a list: who has crop residues? Who has manure? Who has kitchen waste? Talk to them. Offer to collect for free or pay a small fee. For example, a market vendor might pay to have waste removed; you can charge a small collection fee and get free material. A cattle keeper might give you manure if you clean the kraal. Set a weekly schedule. For a small business producing 1 ton of compost per month, you need about 3 tons of raw materials. That is roughly: 1.5 tons crop residues, 0.5 tons manure, 0.5 tons kitchen waste, 0.5 tons leaves/grass. Use a notebook to record collection dates, quantities, and sources. Invest in tools: a wheelbarrow, sacks, a rake, a fork, and a tarpaulin. Keep a dedicated collection area with separate piles for carbon-rich (dry) and nitrogen-rich (wet) materials. Build your compost pile in layers: 15 cm dry, 10 cm wet, sprinkle ash or bone meal, repeat. Water and turn. Train a helper if needed. Also, consider making arrangements with restaurants, schools, and hotels for regular pickup. A signed agreement ensures steady supply. Track your costs: transport, labor, sacks. Even if materials are free, collection costs money. Price your compost to cover costs and give profit. A 50 kg bag of good compost can sell for $10-20 in many African cities. With a good plan, you turn waste into wealth.

3. What to Use and What to Avoid

Use freely: cereal stalks and cobs, rice straw, legume haulms, cattle/goat/rabbit manure, poultry litter (in moderation), market and kitchen vegetable waste, grass clippings, tree leaves (except those listed below), wood ash, charcoal dust, and properly processed bone meal.

Avoid or pre-treat: meat, dairy, and oily kitchen waste (attracts rodents and flies); diseased plant material; herbicide-treated grass (residues can persist for months); glossy or coloured paper; plastic, glass, and metal; human sewage unless fully treated and permitted; and manure from animals recently treated with persistent veterinary antibiotics.

Handle with care: eucalyptus, neem, and some acacia leaves contain allelopathic compounds that slow decomposition â compost them separately or use in small proportions. Pine needles are extremely acidic and slow to break down.

â  SAFETY WARNING â Bone Meal and Manure Handling

Raw bone meal and fresh manure can carry anthrax spores, Salmonella, E. coli, and Brucella. In regions with known anthrax outbreaks (parts of East and Southern Africa), never handle raw bones or carcass material without official veterinary clearance. Boil bones for at least 30 minutes, dry them fully in the sun, and grind only while wearing a P2/N95 respirator, gloves, and eye protection. Always wear gloves and closed shoes when turning manure or compost, and wash hands and tools with soap after every session. Keep children and livestock away from the compost pad.

4. Making a Materials Collection Plan for Your Business

A collection plan converts "free" materials into a forecastable input. Work through these five steps each season:

  1. Map your sources within a 20 km radius. List every farm, market, abattoir, poultry house, and mill. Note what they produce, how much, and when.
  2. Estimate volumes and timing. Maize stalks arrive in one burst after harvest; market waste arrives daily. Match your collection schedule to both.
  3. Negotiate access early. A simple written agreement â even a signed note â prevents a competitor from taking your straw after you have invested in baling.
  4. Design the logistics. Decide vehicle, route, frequency, and labour. Batch collections to fill a truck rather than making half-load trips.
  5. Record everything. Keep a simple log: date, source, material, weight, cost, moisture. This data lets you calculate true cost per tonne and prove traceability to buyers.

Your target mix by volume should be roughly 2â3 parts carbon-rich material (stalks, straw, leaves, charcoal dust) to 1 part nitrogen-rich material (manure, vegetable waste, grass clippings), aiming for a C:N ratio of 25â30:1 and moisture of 50â60%. Test by squeezing a handful: it should feel like a wrung-out sponge.
