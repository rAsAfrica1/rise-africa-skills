Lesson 1.1~12 min

Rumen Microbiota and Fermentation

In this lesson
  • Describe in plain terms what the rumen does to the feed you buy
  • Explain why feed changes must be made slowly rather than overnight
  • Recognise from intake and body condition when the rumen is being underfed rather than the animal

The most useful idea in cattle feeding is also the simplest: you are not feeding a cow, you are feeding the population of microbes inside her. Those microbes digest the grass, and the cow then digests the microbes. Once you accept that, a lot of feeding decisions become obvious that were confusing before.

The rumen is a large fermentation chamber sitting on the left side of the animal, held at close to 39 degrees C, filled with a soup of bacteria, protozoa and fungi. Mammals cannot break the cellulose bonds in grass stems on their own. The microbes can. In return for shelter and a steady supply of feed, they hand the cow two things: energy compounds released by fermentation, and their own microbial bodies, which pass out of the rumen and are digested downstream as high-quality protein. On a forage diet, microbial protein is the main protein the cow actually absorbs. The exact chemistry of the acids produced, and the methane-forming microbes that ride along with them, are covered in detail in Module 9; here we only need the working picture.

The microbes come in two broad working groups. The fibre digesters attack stems, leaves and crop residues. They are slow, they are the reason a cow can survive on maize stover, and they are fussy about acidity: they work best when rumen pH sits roughly between 6.2 and 6.8. The starch and sugar fermenters attack maize grain, molasses and cassava. They are fast, they produce acid quickly, and they tolerate more acid conditions than the fibre group. Tip in a large amount of grain at once and the fast group multiplies, pH falls, and the fibre group stops working. You then digest less of the forage you already paid for.

The microbes need three things from you: fermentable energy, a nitrogen supply, and a stable environment. Nitrogen is where most small herds fail. Rumen bacteria build their bodies from rumen-degradable protein and ammonia. Below roughly 7.5 percent crude protein in diet dry matter, which is the lowest level used in the NRC 2016 beef recommendations, the population is nitrogen-starved. What you see is not a protein problem, it is an intake problem: undigested fibre sits in the rumen and physically stops the animal taking another mouthful.

Worked example. A 300 kg crossbred steer is given free-choice maize stover testing 4 percent crude protein and 70 percent NDF. Intake capacity from NDF is 120 / 70 = 1.71 percent of body weight, so 300 x 0.0171 = 5.1 kg dry matter per day at best. That supplies 5.1 x 0.04 = 0.20 kg crude protein. Now add 0.8 kg of sunflower cake at 30 percent crude protein: 0.8 x 0.30 = 0.24 kg protein. Total is 0.44 kg protein in 5.9 kg dry matter, which is 0.44 / 5.9 = 7.5 percent. You have just crossed the floor. At a cake price of 0.35 USD/kg, the supplement costs 0.8 x 0.35 = 0.28 USD per head per day, and it does not merely add protein - it restarts fibre digestion so the animal eats more of the stover you already own.

A named African case. In Kiambu County, Kenya, smallholder dairy farmers feeding mature Napier grass cut at over 1.5 m routinely see cows standing at a full trough while losing condition. Mature Napier is bulky and low in nitrogen. Extension advice there is not to cut more Napier but to add a small nitrogen source - lucerne, desmodium, cottonseed cake or a urea-molasses block - so the rumen population can work at all.

Four rules follow directly:

  1. Change diets slowly. Give the microbes two to three weeks, and keep steps between feeding groups under about 10 to 15 percent of diet dry matter.
  2. Feed at the same times every day, and split concentrate into two or three meals rather than one large pulse.
  3. Keep clean water available at all times; the rumen is a water bath and restricted water cuts intake immediately.
  4. Never let forage NDF fall below 15 to 16 percent of total diet dry matter. That is the published floor before acidosis and milk fat depression start.

Lesson 1.2~12 min

Fiber Quality and Digestibility

In this lesson
  • Calculate digestible dry matter and intake capacity from ADF and NDF values
  • Explain why cutting date matters more to forage value than fertiliser or species choice
  • Compare two forages on a cost per unit of digestible dry matter basis in USD

Fibre is not one thing. Two numbers describe it, they do different jobs, and if you learn only two equations in this whole course these should be the ones.

Acid detergent fibre (ADF) is cellulose plus lignin plus insoluble ash. It is the main predictor of how much of the forage the animal can actually digest. The prediction equation is DDM% = 88.9 - (0.779 x ADF%), where DDM is digestible dry matter. Neutral detergent fibre (NDF) is total plant fibre - cellulose, hemicellulose and lignin together. It is the main predictor of how much the animal will voluntarily eat, because bulky fibre fills the rumen. The prediction is DMI as a percent of body weight = 120 / NDF%. Between them, ADF tells you the quality of each mouthful and NDF tells you how many mouthfuls there will be. How laboratories generate these figures, how to read a full forage report and when to distrust it are handled in Module 10; here we simply use the numbers.

Worked comparison. You are offered two hays. Hay A is early-cut Rhodes grass at 32 percent ADF and 58 percent NDF, priced at 0.16 USD/kg. Hay B is late-cut veld hay at 42 percent ADF and 72 percent NDF, priced at 0.09 USD/kg. Run both through the equations.

Hay A: DDM = 88.9 - (0.779 x 32) = 88.9 - 24.9 = 64.0 percent. Intake = 120 / 58 = 2.07 percent of body weight. Hay B: DDM = 88.9 - (0.779 x 42) = 88.9 - 32.7 = 56.2 percent. Intake = 120 / 72 = 1.67 percent of body weight.

Now price the part that actually feeds the animal. Hay A delivers 0.640 kg of digestible dry matter per kg, so 0.16 / 0.640 = 0.25 USD per kg digestible DM. Hay B delivers 0.562 kg, so 0.09 / 0.562 = 0.16 USD per kg digestible DM. On price alone Hay B still wins - but look at intake. A 350 kg steer on Hay A can eat 350 x 0.0207 = 7.2 kg DM per day, giving 7.2 x 0.64 = 4.6 kg digestible DM. On Hay B he can eat 350 x 0.0167 = 5.8 kg DM, giving 5.8 x 0.562 = 3.3 kg digestible DM. The cheap hay physically cannot supply enough. It will hold a dry cow; it will not grow a steer without a supplement, and once you add that supplement the cost gap closes.

The single biggest lever on ADF and NDF is cutting date. As a grass matures its stem thickens and lignifies, and published work puts the rise in ADF at roughly 2 to 4 percentage units per week during the growth cycle, though the exact timing varies a great deal by region, species and weather. Two weeks of delay can move a forage from 32 to about 38 percent ADF, dropping DDM from 64.0 to 88.9 - (0.779 x 38) = 59.3 percent. Lignin is the reason: each 1 percent increase in ADF-bound lignin cuts digestibility by roughly 3 to 4 percentage units, and forages above about 12 percent lignin as a share of ADF are poor feed whatever the label says.

A useful summary index is relative feed value, RFV = DDM% x DMI% x 0.775. For Hay A that is 64.0 x 2.07 x 0.775 = 103. For Hay B, 56.2 x 1.67 x 0.775 = 73. Laboratory in-vitro digestibility (IVDMD) runs from about 45 percent for poor hay to 75 percent or more for excellent fresh forage, which gives you a sense of the range you are working inside.

A named African case. Commercial and smallholder producers in Zimbabwe's Mashonaland provinces cut veld hay after the rains have stopped, when the grass has already seeded. The hay is cheap and abundant but the ADF is high and the animals cannot eat enough of it to gain. Producers who shifted their cutting a few weeks earlier, even accepting a smaller bulk yield, reported cattle holding condition through the dry season on the same hectares. The tonnage went down and the feeding value went up.

Practical points to carry away:

  1. Buy hay on ADF and NDF where you can get them, not on colour or smell alone.
  2. Always convert price to cost per kg of digestible dry matter before comparing two feeds.
  3. Check whether the animal can physically eat enough before you decide a cheap forage is good value.
  4. Cut earlier. Yield per hectare falls; feeding value per kilogram rises, and the animal decides which one matters.

Lesson 1.3~12 min

Protein Metabolism in Ruminants

In this lesson
  • Distinguish rumen-degradable from rumen-undegradable protein and name feeds in each group
  • Calculate the supplement needed to lift a forage diet above the crude protein floor
  • Select a protein source on cost per kg of crude protein delivered in USD

Protein for a cow is really two products sold under one name. Part of what you buy is broken down in the rumen and rebuilt into microbial bodies. Part escapes the rumen intact and is digested further down the gut. Both matter, but they are not interchangeable, and knowing which one you are buying stops you wasting money.

Rumen-degradable protein (RDP) is the fraction the microbes break down. It becomes ammonia and then microbial protein. Fresh green forage, legumes and soybean meal are high in RDP. Rumen-undegradable protein (RUP), also called bypass protein, escapes fermentation and is absorbed directly as amino acids in the small intestine. Heat-treated meals, blood meal and fish meal are high in RUP - published figures put blood meal, fish meal and soy-plus products at 70 to 90 percent RUP of crude protein, soybean meal at 35 to 40 percent, and alfalfa haylage at 40 to 45 percent.

How much of each? For growing and finishing beef cattle the published target is 60 to 70 percent of total crude protein as RDP and 30 to 40 percent as RUP. Most African smallholder diets, built on grass and a cake, land inside that range without special effort. Dairy is different. Low-producing dairy cows under about 40 lb (18 kg) of milk a day can use 65 to 70 percent RDP effectively, because microbial protein alone covers their needs. High-producing cows above about 60 lb (27 kg) need bypass protein, with RDP:RUP shifting toward 50:50 or even 45:55 at peak lactation, and an estimated RUP requirement of 7.2 to 7.5 percent of diet dry matter. If your best cow is giving 12 litres, do not pay a premium for bypass protein. If she is giving 27 litres, you probably should.

The absolute floor is 7.5 percent crude protein in diet dry matter, the lowest level used in NRC 2016 beef recommendations. Requirements rise steeply above it with production. A 400 kg medium-frame steer needs 0.88 kg... in the published units, 0.88 lb per day of crude protein at 1.0 lb daily gain (10.2 percent of diet DM), 1.33 lb at 1.5 lb gain (10.5 percent) and 1.60 lb at 2.0 lb gain (10.8 percent). A 1,200 lb lactating cow giving 10 lb of milk needs 2.2 to 2.5 lb of crude protein a day; at 20 lb of milk she needs 3.0 to 3.4 lb. Post-calving crude protein requirement is close to double that of a dry cow.

Worked example. A 400 kg steer eats 8 kg of dry matter a day, made up of Rhodes hay at 7 percent crude protein. That supplies 8 x 0.07 = 0.56 kg protein, which is 7.0 percent of the diet - under the floor, so he will not gain. Target 10.5 percent for a moderate gain: 8 x 0.105 = 0.84 kg protein needed, a shortfall of 0.28 kg. Compare three supplements. Cottonseed cake at 36 percent crude protein and 0.32 USD/kg: you need 0.28 / 0.36 = 0.78 kg, costing 0.25 USD/day, or 0.32 / 0.36 = 0.89 USD per kg of crude protein. Sunflower cake at 30 percent and 0.28 USD/kg: 0.93 kg needed, 0.26 USD/day, 0.93 USD per kg protein. Groundnut cake at 45 percent and 0.48 USD/kg: 0.62 kg needed, 0.30 USD/day, 1.07 USD per kg protein. Cottonseed cake wins here, but only on today's prices - redo the sum every time prices move.

Watch the cautions on cottonseed. Gossypol is toxic to calves and breeding cattle at 400 mg/kg of diet dry matter or above, cottonseed meal should be limited to 3 to 5 lb per day for bulls and avoided for two to three months before breeding, and whole cottonseed should not exceed 25 percent of diet dry matter or 0.5 percent of body weight daily.

A named African case. In and around Kano State, Nigeria, cottonseed cake from local ginneries is the standard dry-season protein for fattening bulls, and the practice works well for that class of animal. The same cake fed carelessly to young calves or to bulls just before the breeding season is a known cause of poor growth and reduced fertility. Same feed, same farm, different animal - different answer.

Carbohydrate Types and Starch Digestion

In this lesson
  • Separate structural from non-structural carbohydrate and match each to a feed you can buy
  • Set a safe maximum daily concentrate allowance from body weight
  • Design a step-up schedule that raises grain without wrecking fibre digestion

Carbohydrate is where most of the energy in a cattle diet comes from, and it arrives in two very different forms. Structural carbohydrate is the cellulose and hemicellulose locked in plant cell walls - the fibre measured as NDF. Non-structural carbohydrate is starch and sugar: maize grain, sorghum, cassava chips, molasses, brewers by-products. The rumen handles them at completely different speeds, and the whole art of grain feeding is managing that speed difference.

Fibre ferments slowly over many hours and produces acid slowly enough that saliva and rumen buffering keep pace. Starch ferments fast. A large starch meal produces acid faster than the animal can buffer it, pH falls, the fibre digesters shut down, intake drops, and in bad cases you get acidosis, laminitis and in dairy cows milk fat depression. This is not a reason to avoid grain. Grain is usually the cheapest energy available and high-starch diets even reduce methane losses because fermentation shifts toward propionate. It is a reason to feed grain in a disciplined way.

Three published limits keep you safe. First, forage NDF must stay at or above 15 to 16 percent of total diet dry matter. That is the absolute floor. Second, maximum concentrate should sit around 2 to 3 lb per 100 lb of body weight per day, with better forage quality allowing less concentrate to do the same job. Third, changes between feeding levels should not exceed 10 to 15 percent of diet dry matter at a step, or you get production drops and metabolic upset.

There is also a quieter effect worth knowing. Published digestibility adjustments show that starch digestibility falls by about 1.0 percentage unit for every unit that DMI as a percent of body weight rises above 3.5 percent, and NDF digestibility falls by about 1.1 units on the same basis plus 0.59 units per percentage unit of dietary starch. In plain terms: the more you push intake and the more starch you add, the less of each additional kilogram you actually capture. Pushing harder gives you less back per unit than the first kilograms did.

Worked example. A 300 kg steer (660 lb) is being brought onto a fattening ration. Maximum concentrate at 2.5 lb per 100 lb body weight is 660 / 100 x 2.5 = 16.5 lb, about 7.5 kg as-fed. That is the ceiling, not the starting point. Suppose total dry matter intake is 8 kg. Starting at 1.5 kg concentrate DM, that is 1.5 / 8 = 19 percent of the diet. A 10 to 15 percent step means adding roughly 0.8 to 1.2 kg concentrate DM at each move, with about a week between steps. So: week 1 at 1.5 kg, week 2 at 2.5 kg, week 3 at 3.5 kg, week 4 at 4.5 kg, and so on. Check the fibre floor as you go: at 4.5 kg concentrate out of 8 kg total, forage supplies 3.5 kg DM. If that forage is 60 percent NDF, forage NDF is 3.5 x 0.60 = 2.1 kg, which is 2.1 / 8 = 26 percent of diet DM - comfortably above the 15 to 16 percent floor.

By-product energy feeds have their own ceilings. Published maximum inclusions are 50 percent of diet dry matter for distillers dried grains (above that, sulphur toxicity, and the high phosphorus to calcium ratio needs correcting), 25 to 30 percent for wet corn gluten feed, 30 to 40 percent for wet brewers grains with storage losses around 15 percent and palatability problems above 30 percent, 15 to 20 percent for soybean hulls, and 15 to 20 percent for beet pulp. As a general principle, by-products should not exceed 50 percent of the concentrate fraction.

A named African case. In central Uganda, dried cassava chips are a cheap, widely traded starch source and are increasingly used in dairy and fattening rations. Cassava starch ferments very rapidly. Farmers who replaced maize bran with cassava chips overnight at the same weight reported sudden drops in milk fat and off-feed animals. Those who stepped it in over three to four weeks, kept a long-fibre forage in front of the animals at all times, and split the ration into two feeds a day got the cost saving without the crash.

The rule to remember: forage first, grain second, and never change either faster than the microbes can follow.

Lesson 1.5~12 min

Essential Minerals in Ruminant Diets

In this lesson
  • State the calcium, phosphorus and magnesium targets for growing, gestating and lactating cattle
  • Check a mineral product against requirements and maximum tolerable levels before buying
  • Identify the interactions that cause deficiency even when the mineral is present in the diet

Minerals are the cheapest part of a ration and the easiest to get badly wrong. They cost a few cents a head a day and they decide whether the rest of your feed bill produces anything.

Start with the macrominerals, expressed as a percent of diet dry matter. Calcium runs 0.35 to 0.65 percent for growing cattle around 400 kg and for lactating cows, falling to 0.16 to 0.35 percent for heavier growing cattle and gestating cows. Phosphorus runs 0.20 to 0.30 percent for growing 400 kg cattle, 0.14 to 0.20 percent for 600 kg growing cattle, 0.17 to 0.20 percent for gestating cows and 0.25 to 0.35 percent for lactating cows. The ratio between them matters as much as the levels: target a calcium to phosphorus ratio of 2.0 to 2.5 to 1. Too much phosphorus relative to calcium drives secondary hyperparathyroidism, which is bone weakness caused by diet, not by injury.

Magnesium runs 0.10 to 0.12 percent for growing cattle, 0.12 to 0.15 percent for gestating cattle and 0.20 to 0.25 percent for lactating cattle. That last figure is the grass tetany number. On lush young pasture high in potassium and nitrogen, magnesium absorption is blocked and lactating cows can go down. Published prevention is to supply 0.20 to 0.25 percent dietary magnesium during high-risk grazing, or about 0.6 oz of magnesium oxide per head daily, equivalent to roughly 7 to 9 g of magnesium a day in late pregnancy and 18 to 21 g a day in lactation. Potassium requirement is 0.60 percent for growing and gestating cattle and 0.70 percent for lactating cattle, but excess potassium is exactly what blocks magnesium, so heavily manured or fertilised paddocks raise tetany risk. Sodium sits at 0.06 to 0.08 percent for growing and gestating stock and 0.10 percent for lactating cows; a free-choice salt block plus salt in the mineral mix covers it. Sulphur is 0.15 percent of diet dry matter and is rarely limiting where protein feeds are used.

The trace minerals are where money is lost. Published requirements in mg/kg of diet dry matter, with maximum tolerable levels in brackets, are: copper 10 (40), zinc 30 (500), manganese 20 to 40 for beef and 40 for dairy (2,000), selenium 0.10 (5), cobalt 0.10 for beef and 0.15 for dairy (25), and iodine 0.50 (50). Molybdenum is not a required nutrient at all, with a maximum tolerable level of 5 mg/kg.

Interactions explain most field failures. Excess molybdenum above 5 mg/kg binds copper and produces copper deficiency even when copper supplementation looks adequate on paper. High dietary sulphur above 0.20 percent of dry matter binds copper as sulphide and reduces its availability. Excess zinc above 500 mg/kg inhibits copper absorption in turn. Copper deficiency shows as coat depigmentation - the classic rusty, faded black animal - and poor hoof quality. Zinc deficiency shows as poor hoof, skin and coat. Iodine deficiency causes goitre. Selenium deficiency causes white muscle disease in young stock. Bioavailability also varies by source: oxide, sulphate and proteinate forms are not equal, and published requirement tables assume inorganic sources.

Worked example. You buy a mineral premix and feed it at 100 g per head per day to a 350 kg steer eating 8 kg dry matter. That premix is 8 / 8.1 of the diet by weight, so anything in the premix is diluted about 81-fold in the total diet. To deliver the 10 mg/kg copper requirement across 8.1 kg of diet you need 8.1 x 10 = 81 mg of copper per day, which means the premix must contain 81 mg per 100 g, that is 810 mg/kg or 0.081 percent copper. Check the label. If the bag says 400 mg/kg copper, you are delivering half the requirement and the animals will fade in colour before they fade in growth.

A named African case. Across the Zambian Copperbelt and parts of southern Africa, forage grown on high-molybdenum soils causes secondary copper deficiency in cattle that are already receiving a copper-containing lick. Producers who simply doubled the lick without testing sometimes pushed toward the 40 mg/kg tolerable ceiling without fixing the problem, because the molybdenum was still binding the copper. The workable route was a forage and water mineral analysis first, then a premix matched to the actual soil situation.

Water is part of the mineral picture too: high-sulphate water can undo a good copper programme on its own, which is where the next lesson starts.

Lesson 1.6~12 min

Water and Drinking Behavior

In this lesson
  • Explain how restricted or dirty water limits feed intake and therefore growth
  • Test and interpret water for nitrate and sulphate against published limits
  • Plan water point number, placement and flow so no animal is excluded

Water is the nutrient nobody budgets for and the one that quietly caps everything else. The rumen is a water bath: fermentation happens in liquid, and restricting water cuts feed intake immediately. An animal that cannot drink will not eat, and an animal that will not eat cannot grow no matter how good the ration on paper is.

Published requirement tables for cattle water intake vary widely with body weight, temperature, dry matter intake, diet dry matter content and milk yield, and the reference standards behind this course do not give a single fixed figure you can safely quote. Treat any daily litres-per-head number you see as approximate and situation-specific. What is not approximate is the direction of the effect: hotter conditions, drier feed, higher production and higher salt intake all raise water need, and any of them can turn a marginal water supply into a limiting one. In practice, plan for supply to be generous rather than exact, and judge adequacy by watching the animals rather than by a table.

Behaviour is the part most producers underuse. Cattle are herd drinkers and prefer to drink together, often after grazing bouts and in the hours around midday and evening. That means peak demand is far higher than average demand. A trough sized on daily average will run dry at the moment the whole group arrives. Dominant animals drink first; young stock and thin cows get pushed off and are the ones already least able to afford the loss. Signs you have a water problem include animals bunching and pushing at the trough, cattle walking long distances back and forth, dung that is unusually dry and firm, and quiet drops in intake or milk yield that do not track any feed change.

Water quality carries real toxic risk. Nitrate is the main one. Plants accumulate nitrate under drought, low light and heavy nitrogen fertilisation, and it also enters water. Rumen bacteria convert nitrate to nitrite, which binds haemoglobin and blocks oxygen transport. The published FDA water standard is 10 mg/L of nitrate-nitrogen, equivalent to 44 mg/L of nitrate. In feed the thresholds are: below 0.5 percent of dry matter is safe, 0.5 to 1.5 percent needs caution and at least 2 lb per head daily of energy grain to help the rumen handle it, 1.5 to 3.0 percent is unsafe for grazing, and above 3.0 percent must not be fed. Clinically, 30 to 40 percent methaemoglobin produces lethargy, brown mucous membranes and muscle tremors; 80 to 90 percent is fatal. Remember that a ponded, heavily fertilised paddock concentrates the risk in both the forage and the water sitting on it.

Sulphate is the second concern. Water mineral content should always be counted as part of the total diet, and high-sulphate water in particular matters because dietary sulphur above 0.20 percent of dry matter binds copper as sulphide and reduces its availability. A herd fading in coat colour on an apparently correct mineral lick may simply be drinking its copper away. The sulphur requirement itself is only 0.15 percent of diet dry matter and is normally met by protein feeds.

Worked example on cost. Suppose a borehole pump and 5,000 litre tank with two 400 litre troughs costs 2,400 USD installed and serves 40 head. That is 60 USD per head of capital. Spread over five years, 12 USD per head per year, or about 0.03 USD per head per day. Now compare that with what water restriction costs. If restricted water cuts dry matter intake by even 10 percent on a growing steer eating 8 kg DM, you have thrown away 0.8 kg of dry matter of feeding value a day - at a modest ration cost of 0.15 USD/kg DM, that is 0.12 USD a day of feed bought and not converted, four times the cost of the water infrastructure, before counting the lost gain.

A named African case. Pastoral and agro-pastoral herds in the Sahel around Maradi in Niger routinely walk long distances between grazing and the nearest functioning borehole during the dry season. The energy spent walking, plus the restricted drinking that comes with a queue at a single point, shows up as weight loss even where forage is not the binding constraint. Programmes that added a second water point closer to grazing areas - not more feed - recorded better condition scores through the same dry season on the same pasture.

Practical rules: keep water clean and available at all times, size troughs for peak group demand not daily average, place at least two watering points where the herd is large or the paddock long, test borehole water for nitrate and sulphate before you build around it, and check every trough daily with your own eyes rather than trusting the float valve.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. On a forage diet, where does most of the protein the cow absorbs actually come from?

Rumen microbes build their own protein from degradable protein and ammonia, then flow out of the rumen and are digested as the animal's main protein supply.

2. A steer on free-choice stover at 4% crude protein loses weight with a full trough in front of him. The best explanation is:

Below about 7.5% crude protein in diet DM, microbial growth stalls, undigested fibre stays in the rumen and physically limits further intake.

3. Which pH range suits the fibre-digesting microbes best?

Cellulolytic activity depends on pH staying near 6.2-6.8; heavy grain feeding drives it lower and shuts fibre digestion down.

4. What is the published floor for forage NDF as a share of total diet dry matter?

15-16% forage NDF of total diet DM is the absolute minimum published to prevent acidosis and milk fat depression.

5. A forage tests 70% NDF. What daily dry matter intake can a 300 kg steer be expected to manage from it?

DMI% of body weight = 120 / 70 = 1.71%, and 300 x 0.0171 = 5.1 kg dry matter per day.

6. A hay tests 35% ADF. What is its digestible dry matter?

DDM = 88.9 - (0.779 x 35) = 88.9 - 27.3 = 61.6%.

7. NDF is the main predictor of:

NDF measures total bulk fibre, which fills the rumen and limits intake. ADF predicts digestibility.

8. A forage tests 60% NDF. Intake capacity is about:

120 / 60 = 2.0% of body weight in dry matter.

9. Each 1 percentage point increase in ADF-bound lignin reduces digestibility by roughly:

Lignin is the primary negative driver of digestibility, cutting roughly 3-4 percentage units per 1% increase.

10. Why can a cheaper, higher-ADF hay still be the worse buy?

Price per kg ignores intake. Once NDF caps how much the animal can eat, the cheap hay may not supply enough digestible dry matter to meet requirements at all.

11. Bypass protein is another name for:

RUP escapes rumen fermentation and is digested in the small intestine, so it 'bypasses' the rumen.

12. For growing and finishing beef cattle, the published RDP share of total crude protein is:

NRC 2016 puts growing and finishing beef at 60-70% RDP and 30-40% RUP.

13. A 400 kg steer eating 8 kg DM of 7% crude protein hay receives how much crude protein daily?

8 x 0.07 = 0.56 kg, which is only 7.0% of the diet - below the 7.5% floor.

14. Which group of feeds is highest in RUP?

Blood meal, fish meal and soy-plus products are published at 70-90% RUP of crude protein.

15. When should cottonseed meal be avoided?

Gossypol affects fertility, so published guidance is to avoid cottonseed meal for bulls two to three months before the breeding season and to limit it for young stock.

16. Why is a large single grain meal riskier than the same grain split into two feeds?

Starch ferments fast. Pulse feeding swings rumen pH far harder than splitting the same quantity across the day.

17. A 660 lb steer at a 2.5 lb per 100 lb ceiling can receive at most:

660 / 100 x 2.5 = 16.5 lb per day, about 7.5 kg as-fed.

18. The published maximum inclusion for distillers dried grains is:

Above 50% of diet DM, sulphur toxicity becomes a risk and the phosphorus to calcium ratio needs correcting.

19. High-starch diets affect methane losses how?

Faster fermentation and propionate dominance on high-starch diets reduce methane relative to high-NDF forage diets.

20. Wet brewers grains become a palatability problem above roughly:

Published guidance allows 30-40% of diet DM but flags palatability issues above 30%, with storage losses near 15%.

21. The target calcium to phosphorus ratio in cattle diets is:

A 2.0-2.5:1 Ca:P ratio prevents secondary hyperparathyroidism.

22. The maximum tolerable level for copper in cattle diet dry matter is:

Requirement is 10 mg/kg; the published maximum tolerable level is 40 mg/kg, above which copper accumulates in the liver.

23. A herd on a copper lick still shows faded coats. The most likely cause is:

Molybdenum above 5 mg/kg and sulphur above 0.20% of DM both bind copper, causing deficiency despite adequate supplementation.

24. Lactating cattle on lush spring pasture need dietary magnesium of about:

0.20-0.25% dietary magnesium is the published grass tetany prevention level for lactating cattle.

25. Which mineral is not a required nutrient but has a tolerable ceiling because of its interference?

Molybdenum has no requirement figure; it is listed only with a 5 mg/kg maximum tolerable level because it blocks copper absorption.

26. The published FDA water standard for nitrate-nitrogen is:

10 mg/L nitrate-nitrogen, equivalent to 44 mg/L of nitrate, is the published water standard.

27. Why does restricted water reduce growth?

Fermentation happens in liquid. Restricted water cuts dry matter intake straight away, and nothing else in the ration can compensate.

28. Forage testing at 2.0% nitrate on a dry matter basis is:

The 1.5-3.0% band is classed as unsafe for grazing; the cautionary band with energy grain is 0.5-1.5%.

29. High-sulphate water is a problem mainly because it:

Dietary sulphur above 0.20% of DM binds copper, so sulphate-rich water can undermine an otherwise adequate mineral programme.

30. Troughs should be sized for:

Cattle are herd drinkers with pronounced peaks. A trough sized on daily average runs dry exactly when the whole group arrives, and the weakest animals lose out.

Feed Inventory and Ration Baseline for your own herd. Step 2: convert each price to USD per kg of dry matter using your best DM estimate (fresh green forage 20-25% DM, wilted 30%, dry hay or straw 88%, cakes and brans 88%). Step 3: for your two main forages, find or estimate ADF and NDF, then calculate digestible dry matter with DDM% = 88.9 - (0.779 x ADF%) and intake capacity with DMI% of body weight = 120 / NDF%. Step 5: calculate the crude protein percentage of that forage-only diet and compare it against the 7.5% of diet DM floor. Step 6: if you are below the floor, cost out three different protein supplements at the amount needed to cross it, and name the cheapest per kg of crude protein delivered. Step 7: write a half-page mineral and water plan naming the mineral product you will buy, its Ca:P ratio, and the number and location of the water points your animals will use.
