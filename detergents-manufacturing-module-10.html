Lessons

1. Sampling techniques and statistical process control

In detergent manufacturing, you cannot test every single gram of powder or litre of liquid. You take samples. A sample must represent the whole batch. For a 500 kg powder batch, take at least 10 random scoops from different depths using a clean stainless steel scoop. Mix them in a clean bucket, then take a 500 g composite sample. For liquid detergent in a 200 L drum, stir first, then take three samples from top, middle, and bottom using a sampling thief. Put samples in clean, dry, labelled plastic bottles. Label with batch number, date, time, and your name.

Statistical process control (SPC) helps you know if your process is stable. You do not need a computer. Use a control chart. For example, check the pH of your liquid detergent every hour. Record 20 readings. Calculate the average (mean) and the range. Set upper and lower control limits at 3 standard deviations. If a reading falls outside these limits, stop and check your raw materials or mixing. In a small African workshop, you can plot points on graph paper. A simple run chart works too: if 7 points in a row go up, something is changing.

Sample size matters. For a batch of 1,000 bottles, test 10 bottles. For 10,000 bottles, test 50. Use the square root rule: sample size = (batch size). So for 400 bottles, test 20. This gives you 95% confidence. Always sample after mixing is complete, never from the top only. For powders, watch for segregation: fine and coarse particles separate. Take samples from different parts of the blender.

Keep a sampling log. Write down who took the sample, when, and where. In Africa, humidity and heat can affect samples. Store samples in a cool, dry place. If you send samples to a lab in the city, pack them well to avoid leakage. Good sampling saves money: you catch problems before you fill 5,000 bottles with bad detergent.

2. Testing methods: titration, spectroscopy, chromatography

Titration is the cheapest and most common test in a small detergent lab. You need a burette, a conical flask, a pipette, and a standard solution. For example, to test the active matter (surfactant) in your liquid detergent, you can do a two-phase titration. But simpler: test alkalinity. Take 10 mL of your detergent, add 3 drops of phenolphthalein indicator. Fill a burette with 0.1 M hydrochloric acid. Slowly add acid until the pink colour disappears. The volume of acid used tells you the alkalinity. If you use 5 mL, that means your detergent has 0.5% alkali. For a laundry liquid, you want 0.30.8%. Record every titration in a notebook.

Spectroscopy is more advanced but useful. A simple colorimeter measures colour intensity. You can use it to test the concentration of a dye in your detergent. First, make a set of standard solutions with known dye concentrations (0.1%, 0.2%, 0.5%). Measure their colour. Then measure your sample. Compare. In Africa, many university labs or water testing centres have colorimeters. You can also use a smartphone with a colour meter app, but calibrate it with standards. For UV-Vis spectroscopy, you need a lab. It can measure surfactant concentration by reacting with a dye and measuring absorbance at 620 nm.

Chromatography separates mixtures. Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) is cheap. You need a TLC plate, a capillary tube, and a developing tank. Spot your detergent sample and a known standard. Let the solvent run. Different ingredients move different distances. You can check if your detergent contains the right surfactant or if there is a banned substance. Gas chromatography (GC) is for volatile compounds like perfumes. High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) is for non-volatile ingredients. These machines are expensive. In Africa, send samples to a certified lab in Nairobi, Lagos, or Johannesburg. Expect to pay $50$200 per sample.

Always calibrate your instruments. For titration, standardise your acid against a known base. For spectroscopy, run a blank. For chromatography, run a standard. Without calibration, your numbers are meaningless. Keep a log of all tests, including raw data. If you cannot afford a machine, use titration and performance tests. They catch most problems.

3. Performance testing: foam height, detergency, soil removal

Performance testing tells you if your detergent actually cleans. Start with foam height. Use a 1,000 mL graduated cylinder. Add 500 mL of water at 30C (typical hand-wash temperature in Africa). Add 1 g of your detergent. Shake 10 times. Measure the foam height immediately. For hand-wash powder, you want 150250 mm. For machine-wash, you want less foam: 50100 mm. If foam is too low, increase surfactant. If too high, add antifoam. Record foam height after 5 minutes: it should not drop more than 20%.

Detergency testing uses standard soiled swatches. You can make your own: cut 10 cm x 10 cm squares of white cotton. Soak them in a mixture of 10 g used engine oil, 5 g charcoal powder, and 100 mL water. Dry them. Then wash: use 2 g of your detergent in 1 L of water at 30C. Stir for 10 minutes. Rinse twice. Dry. Compare the swatch to a control washed with a commercial detergent. Use a grey scale (15) to rate cleaning. A good detergent gives a 4 or 5. In Africa, you can use red palm oil as a soil: it is common and shows stains clearly.

Soil removal can be measured by weight. Weigh the clean swatch before soiling. After soiling, weigh again. After washing and drying, weigh again. The percentage of soil removed = (weight of soil before wash weight of soil after wash) / weight of soil before wash 100. Aim for at least 70% removal for a general-purpose detergent. For protein stains (blood, milk), use a different test: soak in cold water first, then wash. For oily stains, use warm water (4050C).

Always test against a standard. Buy a cheap commercial detergent from the market and run the same test. Your product should match or beat it. Keep records: batch number, date, temperature, water hardness. Water hardness matters. In many African cities, water is hard (high calcium). Use 0.5 g of sodium carbonate per litre to soften hard water. Test your detergent in both soft and hard water. If performance drops in hard water, add a builder like zeolite or citric acid. Performance testing is the most important QC you can do because it directly reflects what your customer experiences.

4. Stability and shelf-life testing

Your detergent must still work after months on a shelf in a hot African shop. Stability testing checks if it separates, changes colour, or loses strength. For liquid detergent, fill three clear glass bottles. Store one at room temperature (2530C), one in a hot place (40C, like a metal box in the sun), and one in a cool place (15C, like a fridge). Check every week for 12 weeks. Look for separation (layers), cloudiness, or sediment. If separation occurs, your emulsifier is weak. Add 0.5% xanthan gum or 1% sodium chloride to thicken and stabilise. For powder detergent, store in sealed plastic bags. Check for caking. If powder becomes a hard block, your moisture is too high. Dry it more or add 1% sodium silicate as an anticaking agent.

Shelf-life testing predicts how long your product lasts. Use accelerated testing: store at 45C for 4 weeks. This is roughly equivalent to 6 months at 25C. Every week, test pH, foam height, and detergency. If pH drops by more than 0.5 units, your product is degrading. For liquid detergent, check viscosity. If it becomes watery, the thickener is breaking down. For powder, check flowability: pour 100 g through a funnel. It should flow in less than 10 seconds. If it takes longer, it is caking.

In Africa, humidity is a big enemy. Powder detergent absorbs moisture from the air. Use moisture-proof packaging: laminated foil or thick polyethylene bags. Add a desiccant sachet (silica gel) inside the bag. For liquid detergent, UV light from the sun can fade dyes and degrade perfume. Use opaque bottles or add UV absorber (0.1% benzophenone). Also, preservatives matter. If your liquid detergent contains water, bacteria can grow. Add 0.1% formaldehyde or 0.5% sodium benzoate. Test for microbial growth by leaving a sample at room temperature for 4 weeks and checking for smell or cloudiness.

Keep a stability log. Record date, temperature, observations, and test results. After 12 weeks, if the product at 40C still passes all tests, you can claim a 12-month shelf life. If it fails, reformulate. Always test at least three batches before launching. In Africa, many small producers skip this step and get returns. Do not be one of them. A simple stability test costs almost nothing but saves your reputation.

5. Documentation and batch records

If you cannot prove what you made, you cannot sell it safely or consistently. Documentation starts with a batch record. For every batch, write down: batch number, date, time, product name, and who made it. List every raw material: name, supplier, lot number, and amount weighed. For example: 10 kg LABSA, 5 kg caustic soda, 2 kg perfume, 100 L water. Record the order of mixing, temperatures, and mixing times. If you heated to 60C for 30 minutes, write it. If you added perfume at 40C, write it. This record lets you repeat the batch exactly.

During production, record in-process checks. For liquid detergent: pH after mixing, viscosity after cooling, foam height. For powder: moisture content, bulk density, particle size. Write the actual values, not just 'OK'. If pH was 8.2, write 8.2. If you had a problem, like a lump, write it and what you did. At the end, record the yield: how many bottles or kg you got. If you started with 100 kg raw materials and got 95 kg finished product, your yield is 95%. If yield drops below 90%, investigate.

After production, take a retain sample. Keep at least 200 g or 200 mL for 6 months. Label it with the batch number. Store it in a cool, dry place. If a customer complains, you can test the retain sample. Also, keep a certificate of analysis (CoA) for each batch. The CoA lists the test results: pH, active matter, foam height, detergency. Give a copy to your distributor. In Africa, many buyers, especially supermarkets and NGOs, ask for a CoA. Without it, they will not buy.

Use a simple notebook or a spreadsheet. Write in ink, not pencil. Do not erase mistakes; cross out with a single line and initial. Keep records for at least 2 years. If you have a computer, use a template. If not, a hardcover notebook works. Number every page. At the end of each month, review your records. Look for patterns: did batches fail more on humid days? Did a new supplier cause problems? Documentation is not paperwork for its own sake. It is your memory and your legal protection. If a customer gets sick, you can trace the batch and fix the problem.

6. ISO and GMP standards

ISO and GMP are international rules for making safe, consistent products. GMP stands for Good Manufacturing Practices. It is a set of basic rules: keep your factory clean, train your workers, control your raw materials, and write everything down. ISO 9001 is a quality management system. ISO 22716 is specifically for cosmetics and detergents. You do not need to be certified to follow these rules. But following them helps you sell to bigger buyers.

Start with GMP. Your building should have smooth, washable floors and walls. Separate areas for raw materials, mixing, filling, and storage. No eating or drinking in production. Workers must wash hands, wear clean uniforms, and cover hair. If someone is sick, they should not handle product. Clean equipment after every batch. Use food-grade or detergent-grade cleaning agents. Store raw materials off the floor on pallets. Keep pesticides and other chemicals far away. Have a pest control plan: seal cracks, use traps, never use poison near product.

For ISO 22716, you need documented procedures. Write a simple manual. Include: how to clean the mixer, how to calibrate the pH meter, how to handle customer complaints. Keep records of training. Every worker should know their job and the safety rules. For example, caustic soda is dangerous. Train workers to wear goggles and gloves. If a spill happens, know how to neutralise it with citric acid. Also, have a recall procedure. If a batch is bad, you must be able to pull it from the market. Write down who to call and how to inform customers.

In Africa, certification can be expensive. But you can implement the basics for free. Many local standards bodies, like KEBS in Kenya or SON in Nigeria, offer training. Start with a self-audit. Use a checklist: Are raw materials labelled? Are batch records complete? Is the floor clean? Are workers trained? Fix one thing at a time. After 6 months, you will have a strong system. Then consider ISO 9001 certification if you want to export. It costs $2,000$5,000, but it opens doors to supermarkets and international buyers. Remember: ISO and GMP are not about paperwork. They are about making the same safe product every time. That is what builds trust and profits.

7. Practical Reference

<section>
<h2>Practical Reference: Quality Control &amp; Assurance for Detergent Manufacturing in Africa</h2>

<p>In African detergent manufacturing from Lagos blending plants to Nairobi sachet-filling lines and Cairo spray towers quality control is not a laboratory vanity exercise. It is the difference between a brand that earns repeat purchase and one that dies on the shelf. Buyers in Kano, Kumasi, or Kinshasa judge detergent by three things: does it foam, does it clean, and does it smell and feel the same every time they buy it. Your QC system exists to guarantee that consistency at the lowest feasible cost, while keeping your factory compliant with regulators and your staff safe.</p>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the sampling, testing, stability, documentation, and standards knowledge you need to run a credible QC lab in an African context often with limited equipment, unreliable power, and imported raw materials whose quality varies from container to container.</p>

<h3>1. Sampling Techniques and Statistical Process Control (SPC)</h3>

<p>You cannot test quality into a product; you can only verify it. Sampling must be representative. For powdered detergent from a ribbon blender, take at least 10 incremental samples from different depths and positions, combine them, and quarter down to a laboratory sample of 500 g. For liquids, sample from the top, middle, and bottom of the batch tank after agitation. Never sample from the surface alone surfactant stratification is common in locally blended liquids.</p>

<p>Apply SPC with control charts for the critical parameters: active matter (%), pH, moisture (%), bulk density (g/L), and foam height. Plot the mean and 3 limits. A point outside the limits, or seven consecutive points on one side of the mean, signals an assignable cause a raw material lot change, a dosing pump drift, or a temperature excursion. In many African plants, the biggest SPC win is simply tracking batch-to-batch variation in active matter; reducing it by 1% often saves more in raw material cost than any purchasing negotiation.</p>

<h3>2. Testing Methods: Titration, Spectroscopy, Chromatography</h3>

<p><strong>Titration</strong> remains the workhorse of African QC labs because it is cheap, robust, and requires only a burette, glassware, and stable reagents. Anionic surfactant content is measured by two-phase titration (Epton or hyamine method). Alkalinity and free caustic are measured by acid-base titration. For a plant producing 10 tonnes/day, a well-run titration bench can verify every batch at negligible cost.</p>

<p><strong>Spectroscopy</strong> UV-Vis for color and anionic dye content, or FTIR for raw material identity adds speed and specificity. Handheld NIR devices are increasingly affordable and can screen incoming LABSA, soda ash, and STPP for adulteration within seconds, which matters when containers arrive from multiple suppliers.</p>

<p><strong>Chromatography</strong> (HPLC or GC) is used for fatty acid distribution in soap noodles, preservative assay, and detection of banned substances. Most small African manufacturers outsource chromatography to university or SGS-type labs; building in-house capability is justified only above roughly 50 tonnes/day.</p>

<h3>3. Performance Testing: Foam Height, Detergency, Soil Removal</h3>

<p>Consumers in Africa often equate foam with cleaning power, so foam height is a commercial parameter, not just technical. Measure it by the Ross-Miles method or a simplified shake test: 0.1% solution at 25 C, shake 10 times, read height in mm after 30 seconds and 5 minutes. Record both initial and persistence.</p>

<p>Detergency is measured by washing standardized soiled swatches (olive oil/carbon black on cotton) in a Terg-O-Tometer or, in low-budget labs, a Launder-Ometer or even a standardized hand-wash protocol with reflectance measured before and after using a portable reflectometer. Soil removal is expressed as % detergency = (Rw Rs)/(R0 Rs) 100. Always run a reference detergent alongside test samples to control for water hardness critical in Africa where hardness ranges from 20 mg/L in parts of Uganda to over 400 mg/L in northern Nigeria.</p>

<h3>4. Stability and Shelf-Life Testing</h3>

<p>African supply chains are long and hot. A detergent that performs in the lab in Mombasa may fail after three months in a warehouse in Juba at 40 C. Stability testing must therefore be aggressive. Standard practice: store samples at 25 C, 40 C, and 45 C at 75% relative humidity, and test at 0, 1, 2, 3, and 6 months. Accelerated testing at 45 C for 3 months approximates 24 months at ambient in many tropical conditions (Arrhenius-based estimate).</p>

<p>Watch for: caking and lumping in powders, phase separation in liquids, perfume degradation (olfactory panel), color drift (spectrophotometer), and loss of active matter (titration). For sachet products, also test the film seal integrity and ink rub resistance a common failure in low-cost packaging.</p>

<strong style="color:#c00;"> SAFETY WARNING Chemical Handling in Detergent QC Labs</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">LABSA, caustic soda (NaOH), and hypochlorite are corrosive and can cause severe burns and permanent eye damage. In African plants where air conditioning and fume hoods are often absent, the risk is higher. Always wear splash goggles, a face shield, nitrile gloves, and a rubber apron when handling concentrated acids or alkalis. Never add water to concentrated caustic or acid always add the chemical to water, slowly, with stirring. Ensure eyewash stations and a running-water shower are within 10 seconds' reach of every titration bench. Store LABSA away from caustic; accidental mixing releases intense heat. Train all lab staff on SDS (Safety Data Sheets) and re-train every 6 months. Where possible, substitute less hazardous reagents or use pre-standardized titration kits.</p>
</div>

<h3>5. Documentation and Batch Records</h3>

<p>A batch record is your legal and commercial memory. Every batch should have a unique number (e.g., DET-2024-11-023) linking raw material lots, process parameters, QC results, and the person who approved release. In practice, African manufacturers who win supermarket and export contracts are those with clean, traceable records. Regulators such as NAFDAC (Nigeria), KEBS (Kenya), and the Ghana Standards Authority will ask for them during inspections.</p>

<p>Minimum batch record contents: raw material lot numbers and COAs, weighment records, mixing times and temperatures, in-process checks, finished product test results, deviations and corrective actions, and QA release signature with date. Keep records for at least 3 years (5 years for export markets). Digital tools even a shared spreadsheet with locked formulas are acceptable and often more reliable than paper in humid environments.</p>

<h3>6. ISO and GMP Standards</h3>

<p>ISO 9001 provides the quality management framework; ISO 22716 (cosmetics GMP) is increasingly applied to detergents; and the IFS HPC standard governs many European retail supply chains. For African manufacturers targeting regional exports (ECOWAS, EAC, SADC), alignment with ISO 9001 plus local GMP requirements from the national regulator is the pragmatic path.</p>

<p>Key GMP elements: segregated raw material and finished goods storage, pest control, personnel hygiene, validated cleaning between product changeovers, calibrated equipment, and documented training. Calibration is often the weakest link a pH meter uncalibrated for two weeks can pass or fail an entire production week incorrectly. Calibrate daily with fresh buffers.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li> Sample each batch from multiple points; composite and quarter to a 500 g lab sample.</li>
<li> Plot active matter, pH, moisture, and foam height on control charts; act on out-of-control signals within 24 hours.</li>
<li> Calibrate pH meter and balance daily; record calibration in a logbook.</li>
<li> Run a reference detergent alongside every detergency test to control for water hardness.</li>
<li> Start stability samples at 25 C, 40 C, and 45 C on every new formulation or supplier change.</li>
<li> Complete batch records before release; no signature, no shipment.</li>
<li> Verify raw material COAs against incoming containers; reject non-conforming lots.</li>
<li> Review SDS and conduct safety refresher training every 6 months.</li>
</ul>
