1. Forming the Past Tense

In Russian, the past tense is formed by taking the infinitive (the dictionary form of the verb) and adding a suffix that agrees with the subject in gender and number. For most verbs, you remove the - ending and add - for masculine singular, - for feminine singular, - for neuter singular, and - for plural. For example, (to read) becomes (he read), (she read), (it read), (they read). This pattern works for the majority of verbs, including many common ones like (to work) , (to speak) , and (to watch) .

To use the past tense in real conversations, you need to match the verb to the subject. If you are a man, you say (I worked). If you are a woman, you say . For plural subjects like (we), (you all), or (they), you use -: (We worked). This is different from English, where the verb doesn't change with gender. So pay attention: in Russian, the speaker's gender is shown in the past tense.

Let's practice with a typical African business scenario. Imagine you are a market vendor in Lagos or Nairobi. You might say: (Yesterday I sold goods at the market) if you are male, or if you are female. If you and your partner worked together, you say: (We sold goods all day). Notice how the verb changes.

Common mistakes: Forgetting to change the ending for gender. Many learners say even when they are female. Also, don't confuse the past tense with the present tense. The present tense uses different endings (e.g., ). The past tense is simpler because it doesn't change for person (I, you, he, she) except for gender and number. So, learn the four endings: -, -, -, -. Practice by describing what you did yesterday: (I got up early male), (female), (we). With regular practice, you'll master this quickly.

3. Talking About Yesterday and Last Week

To talk about past events, you need time expressions that set the context. The most common are (yesterday), (the day before yesterday), (last week), (last month), and (last year). These phrases usually come at the beginning or end of a sentence. For example: (Yesterday I worked on the farm). (Last week we went to the city).

When you use these time expressions, the verb must be in the past tense. So, you combine the time phrase with the correct past form. For instance, if you want to say 'I sold tomatoes yesterday', you say: (male) or (female). If you want to say 'We sold tomatoes last week', you say: . Notice that the verb is plural.

4. Sequencing Events with Time Expressions

When narrating a story, you need to show the order of events. In Russian, you can use time expressions like (first), (then), (then, next), (after that), and (finally). These words help your listener follow your story. For example: , , , , . (First I had breakfast, then I went to work, then I worked until lunch, after that I had lunch, and finally I returned home.)

Notice that all verbs are in the past tense. The sequence words themselves don't change the verb form. So you just need to make sure each verb agrees with the subject in gender and number. If you are female, you say: , , , , .

In African contexts, you might tell a story about your day as a trader: 7 . . . . 6 . (First I opened the shop at 7 am. Then the first customers came. Then I sold many goods. After that I took a tea break. Finally I closed the shop at 6 pm.) This structure makes your story clear.

Other useful sequencing words include (earlier), (later), (and), (and/but), (but). For example: , . (I worked, and then I rested.) But note that and are not time expressions; they just connect clauses.

To practice, take a simple routine and narrate it in the past tense using these words. For example, describe your last Sunday: , , , , . (First I got up late, then I made breakfast, then I went to church, after that I visited friends, and finally I returned home.) Repeat with female forms. This will build your storytelling skills.

5. Aspects in the Past: Perfective vs. Imperfective

Russian verbs come in pairs: imperfective and perfective. The imperfective aspect describes ongoing, repeated, or habitual actions in the past. The perfective aspect describes a single, completed action with a result. For example, (imperfective) means 'to read' in general, while (perfective) means 'to read and finish'. In the past tense, you choose the aspect based on what you want to say.

Compare: . (Yesterday I was reading a book imperfective, focusing on the process.) . (Yesterday I read the book perfective, focusing on completion.) The first sentence doesn't say if you finished; the second says you finished. Similarly, (to write, imperfective) vs. (to write, perfective): . (I was writing a letter.) . (I wrote the letter and finished it.)

In African business contexts, this distinction is crucial. If you say , it means you were selling goods (maybe not all sold). If you say , it means you sold the goods (all sold, completed). So, if you want to report success, use perfective: 50 . (Yesterday I sold 50 kg of tomatoes.) If you just want to describe the activity, use imperfective: . (Yesterday I was selling tomatoes all day.)

Forming the perfective past is often regular: add the same gender/number endings to the perfective infinitive. For example, , , , . But note that some perfective verbs are irregular, like (to say, perfective) , , , .

When narrating a sequence, you often use perfective for each completed step. For example: , , . (I got up, washed, had breakfast, and went to work.) All these are perfective because they are completed actions. But if you describe a background action, use imperfective: , . (When I was walking to work, I met a friend.) Here is imperfective (ongoing), and is perfective (completed). Practice by telling your daily routine using both aspects appropriately.

6. Writing a Short Story About Your Day

Now it's time to put everything together and write a short story about your day in Russian. Use the past tense, time expressions, sequencing words, and both aspects. Start with a simple structure: morning, afternoon, evening. For example, a story from a market vendor in Kampala:

6 . . . , . . . . . . . 10 .

(Translation: Yesterday I got up at 6 am. First I washed and had breakfast. Then I went to the market. I walked because the bus didn't come. At the market I was selling vegetables. I sold many tomatoes and onions. At lunch I took a break and ate. After that I continued selling. In the evening I closed the shop and went home. At home I cooked dinner and rested. Finally I went to bed at 10 pm.)

Notice how the story uses both aspects: (imperfective, ongoing) and (perfective, completed). It also uses sequencing words: , , , . And time expressions: , , .

To write your own story, follow these steps: 1) Choose a typical day. 2) List the main events in order. 3) For each event, decide if it's a completed action (perfective) or an ongoing/habitual action (imperfective). 4) Write simple sentences in the past tense, matching gender and number. 5) Add time expressions and sequencing words. 6) Read it aloud to check flow.

For example, a tailor in Dakar might write: 7 . . . . 3 . . . . .

Practice by writing your own story every day for a week. Use a notebook and underline perfective verbs in one color and imperfective in another. This will help you master the past tense and narrate events confidently in Russian.

<h2>Practical Reference: Past Tense and Narrating Events in Russian</h2>

<p>For African agribusiness professionals trading with Russian-speaking partners, narrating what happenedshipments delayed, contracts signed, harvests completedis a daily necessity. This practical reference consolidates the core patterns of the Russian past tense, irregular verbs, time expressions, and aspect choice into a single working document. Use it alongside the six lessons in this module as a desk reference during negotiations, email drafting, and report writing.</p>

<p>Russian has no auxiliary verb for the past tense. Instead, the past tense is formed from the infinitive stem by adding gender and number endings. This means every past-tense sentence carries information about whether the subject was masculine, feminine, neuter, or plurala feature that matters when you refer to a company (feminine: ), a director (masculine or feminine depending on the person), or goods (plural). Getting this right signals professionalism and attention to detail in business correspondence.</p>

<h3>Aspect in the Past: Perfective vs. Imperfective</h3>

<p>Aspect is the single most important concept for narrating events accurately in Russian. Imperfective past describes ongoing, repeated, or background actions. Perfective past describes completed, single, result-oriented actions. In agribusiness, this distinction often determines whether you are saying "we were shipping" versus "we shipped and completed the shipment."</p>

<p>Consider the difference between <strong></strong> (imperfective: we were sending, repeatedly or over time) and <strong></strong> (perfective: we sent and completed it). If you tell a Russian partner " ," they may ask when and whether it actually left. If you say " ," the action is complete and the result is clear.</p>

<p>Time expressions often signal which aspect to use. <strong></strong> (yesterday), <strong> </strong> (last week), and <strong> </strong> (last month) can pair with either aspect, but <strong></strong> (often), <strong></strong> (sometimes), and <strong> </strong> (every day) almost always require imperfective. <strong></strong> (already), <strong></strong> (finally), and <strong></strong> (suddenly) typically signal perfective.</p>
