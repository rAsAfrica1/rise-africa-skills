1. Photographic Documentation Techniques

Photographic documentation is the fastest and cheapest way to record a historic building. You do not need a professional camera; a smartphone with at least 12 megapixels works well. Start by taking a wide shot of each elevation (north, south, east, west) in good daylight, preferably early morning or late afternoon when shadows are soft. Stand square to the wall and fill the frame. Then move closer for details: cracks, roof junctions, window frames, and any decorative elements. For each detail, take one photo with a scalea retractable tape measure or a ruler held against the feature. This tells future readers the size.

Always shoot in the highest resolution and save as JPEG or RAW. Avoid using digital zoom; instead, walk closer. For interiors, use a tripod or brace your phone against a wall. Turn on the grid lines to keep vertical lines straight. If the building is dark, use a portable LED work light (about 5000K) placed at a 45-degree angle to reveal texture. Take overlapping photos every two steps when recording a long wall; later you can stitch them into a panorama using free software like Microsoft Image Composite Editor.

Organize your photos immediately. Create folders named by date and building, e.g., '2025-03-15_MombasaOldTown'. Rename files with a location code and sequence: 'MOT_NorthElev_001.jpg'. Back up to two places: an external hard drive and a cloud service like Google Drive. For archival quality, print key photos on acid-free paper and store in a cool, dry place. In humid coastal areas like Lagos or Dar es Salaam, add silica gel packets to storage boxes to prevent mould.

Finally, record metadata. For each photo, note the date, time, GPS coordinates (use a free app like GPS Map Camera), direction faced, and a brief description. This turns a snapshot into a preservation record. A well-documented photo set can guide repairs, insurance claims, and heritage applications without needing to return to the site.

2. Measured Drawings and CAD Modeling

Measured drawings are scale plans, sections, and elevations that show a buildings exact dimensions. They are essential for planning repairs, getting permits, and ordering materials. You can create them with basic tools: a 30-metre tape measure, a plumb bob, a spirit level, and a clipboard. Start by sketching the floor plan freehand, then measure every wall, door, and window. Work in millimetres for accuracy. For a typical 3-bedroom historic house, expect to take 200300 measurements. Always measure twice and record in a table.

For elevations, use a laser distance meter (available from hardware stores for about $50) to measure heights safely. If the building is tall, measure from a fixed point like a window sill and add up. Take diagonal measurements to check for squareness. Record everything in a field notebook with a pencil, not penink runs in rain. Photograph your sketch before leaving the site.

Back in the office, transfer your measurements to CAD software. Free options include LibreCAD and SketchUp Free. Draw at 1:50 or 1:100 scale. Start with a grid, then draw walls as double lines. Add dimensions, labels, and a north arrow. For historic buildings, show irregularitiesdont correct them. Save files as DWG or PDF. If you lack computer access, draw neatly on graph paper at 1:50 scale; a 10-metre wall becomes 20 cm on paper.

CAD models let you test repairs virtually. You can add layers for electrical, plumbing, and structural elements. Share the file with engineers or heritage authorities. In Africa, where many old buildings are undocumented, a simple measured drawing set can be the first-ever record. Keep a printed copy in a waterproof folder on site. Update the drawing after any work, noting the date and changes. This living document becomes a maintenance tool for decades.

3. 3D Laser Scanning and Photogrammetry

3D laser scanning and photogrammetry capture a buildings shape in three dimensions. Laser scanners, like the Leica BLK360 or Faro Focus, fire millions of laser points per second to create a point clouda digital replica accurate to 26 mm. They cost $15,000$50,000, so for small projects, hire a survey firm. In Africa, companies in Nairobi, Cape Town, and Accra offer scanning services. A single scan covers about 30 metres; you need multiple scans to cover a whole building. Each scan takes 310 minutes. Plan for 2050 scans for a medium house.

Photogrammetry is cheaper: you take 50200 overlapping photos with any camera, then use software like Agisoft Metashape or free Meshroom to build a 3D model. Photos should overlap by 7080%. Walk around the building in a grid, keeping the same distance. For tall walls, use a drone (e.g., DJI Mini 3) to capture upper sections. Avoid shiny surfaces and shadows; overcast days are best. Process on a computer with at least 16GB RAM. The result is a textured mesh you can measure and rotate.

Both methods produce files that can be viewed in free software like CloudCompare or Blender. You can slice sections, measure cracks, and detect deformation. For preservation, scan before and after repairs to monitor movement. Point clouds can be archived as .E57 or .LAS files. Store on a hard drive and cloud; a single project may be 10100 GB. In humid areas, keep drives in a dry box.

Use these tools when a building is complex, at risk, or historically significant. They reduce time on site and improve safety. For a simple mud-brick house, photogrammetry with a smartphone may be enough. Always ground-truth: check a few dimensions with a tape measure. Share models with stakeholders via PDF reports or online viewers like Sketchfab. This technology is no longer a luxuryit is a practical way to preserve Africas built heritage.

4. Written and Archival Research

Written and archival research uncovers a buildings history, original materials, and past changes. Start with the building itself: look for date stones, inscriptions, old paint layers, and reused timbers. Photograph and transcribe any text. Then visit local archivesnational museums, university libraries, and municipal records. In Kenya, the National Museums of Kenya in Nairobi holds colonial-era building plans. In Ghana, the Public Records and Archives Administration Department (PRAAD) in Accra has similar collections. Call ahead; many archives require an appointment and a small fee (often $5$20).

Search for keywords: the buildings name, street, original owner, and architect. Old maps (e.g., 1:2,500 town plans) show changes in boundaries and roads. Title deeds give ownership history. Photographs from the 1950s or earlier may show lost features. If you cannot visit, write to the archive with a specific request; they may scan documents for a fee. Interview elderly residentsthey remember events, materials, and craftsmen. Record with permission, and note the date and name.

Organize your findings in a timeline. For each event, cite the source: author, title, date, and archive reference. Use a free tool like Zotero to manage references. Write a summary report of 25 pages, including copies of key documents. Note gapswhat you could not find. This honesty helps future researchers.

In Africa, oral history is as valid as written records. A masons descendant may know the exact sand source or lime mix. Always cross-check. If you find conflicting dates, present both. Archive your own research: donate copies to the local museum or heritage group. This builds a shared knowledge base. For a typical project, budget 1020 hours for archival work. It often reveals that a simple building was once a shop, school, or meeting hallcontext that changes how you preserve it.

5. Creating a Preservation Record

A preservation record is a single, organized file that tells the full story of a building and guides its future care. It combines photos, drawings, scans, research notes, and condition assessments. Start with a cover page: building name, address, GPS coordinates, date, and your name. Then include a table of contents. The record should be both digital and physical. Digital: a folder on a hard drive and a cloud backup (e.g., Google Drive, Dropbox). Physical: a ring binder with acid-free plastic sleeves for prints and original documents.

The core of the record is the condition survey. Walk the building room by room. For each elementwalls, roof, floors, windows, doors, servicesnote its condition as Good, Fair, Poor, or Critical. Use a standard form. For example: North wall: 3 vertical cracks, max width 5 mm, at 1.5 m from ground. Cause: foundation settlement. Priority: High. Add a photo with a scale. Rate urgency: Immediate (fix within 1 month), High (3 months), Medium (1 year), Low (monitor).

Include a maintenance log. After any repair, record the date, worker, materials used, and cost. This prevents repeated mistakes and helps future owners. Add a contact list: mason, carpenter, roofer, electrician, and heritage officer. In many African towns, skilled tradespeople for historic materials (lime, mud, thatch) are scarcelist them.

Store the record in a fireproof safe or a cool, dry cupboard. Make two copies: one on site, one off site. Update it annually. Share a summary with the local heritage authority; in some countries, this is required for listed buildings. A good preservation record turns a neglected structure into a managed asset. It also supports grant applications and insurance. For a small house, the record might be 50 pages; for a church, 200. Start simple and add over time. The act of recording itself often reveals hidden problems and solutions.

6. Using Documentation for Future Maintenance

Documentation is not an end in itselfit is a tool for maintenance. A well-made record lets you spot changes before they become disasters. Set a routine: every 6 months, compare the current building against your baseline photos and drawings. Check cracks with a crack monitor (a simple plastic gauge, about $10) or a feeler gauge. If a crack has widened by 2 mm, investigate. In coastal areas, check for salt crystallisation on walls; in savannah, check for termite mud tubes. Record findings in your maintenance log.

Use your measured drawings to plan work. For example, if you need to replace a roof sheet, the drawing tells you the exact length and pitch. Your photogrammetry model can show where water pools. Your archival research tells you the original materialperhaps corrugated iron from the 1920s, not modern plastic. Order materials in advance; in many African countries, special profiles take 46 weeks. Schedule work in the dry season.

Train a local caretaker. Give them a simplified one-page checklist with photos: Look for: broken gutters, loose hinges, cracked plaster, insect holes. Show them how to take a photo with a phone and send it to you. This early warning system costs almost nothing. For larger buildings, create a maintenance calendar: clean gutters in April before rains, repaint timber in June, check roof after storms in September.

Documentation also helps in emergencies. If a wall collapses, your record shows what was there, guiding reconstruction. For insurance, your photos and condition survey prove pre-disaster state. For heritage listing, your record demonstrates significance. Finally, share your documentation with the community. A simple poster or a digital file in the local library educates others. When people see the buildings story, they value it more. Maintenance becomes a collective effort, not a burden. In this way, documentation ensures that preservation is not a one-time project but a continuing practice.

<h2>Practical Reference: Documentation and Recording for African Built Heritage</h2>

<p>Documentation is not a bureaucratic afterthought it is the single highest-leverage investment in any preservation project. In the African context, where heritage buildings, colonial-era warehouses, mission stations, traditional earth structures, and post-independence civic architecture often lack complete original drawings, a disciplined documentation system protects both the asset value and the business case for restoration. Investors, insurers, heritage authorities, and future maintenance teams all depend on the same record. This lesson gives you practical, field-ready methods for capturing, organising, and using that record.</p>

<h3>Photographic Documentation Techniques</h3>

<p>Photography is the cheapest and most immediately useful documentation method. For African projects, the practical rules are: shoot in RAW, include a scale bar and colour chart in every frame, record GPS coordinates, and maintain a shot list keyed to a floor plan. Overcast days are preferable to harsh sun; in tropical conditions, shoot early morning or late afternoon to avoid heat haze and harsh shadows.</p>

<p>Metadata matters as much as the image. A photograph without a date, location, and description is nearly useless in a maintenance context. Use a simple file-naming convention: <code>SiteID_Building_Element_Date_Sequence</code> (e.g., <code>ACC_Warehouse_RoofTruss_2025-03-14_001</code>).</p>

<h3>Measured Drawings and CAD Modeling</h3>

<p>Measured drawings remain the backbone of preservation documentation in Africa, where many historic buildings predate digital records. Use a laser distance meter (USD 80300) rather than a tape for accuracy over 3 m. Record all measurements in a field book, then transfer to CAD (AutoCAD, LibreCAD, or Blender). For permit submissions, most African heritage authorities require drawings at 1:50 or 1:100 scale with north arrow, scale bar, and datum line.</p>

<h3>3D Laser Scanning and Photogrammetry</h3>

<p>Laser scanning is the gold standard for complex geometry vaulted ceilings, ornate facades, irregular earth structures. In Africa, equipment rental and skilled operators are concentrated in major cities (Nairobi, Cape Town, Accra, Lagos, Cairo). Photogrammetry (Structure-from-Motion) is the pragmatic alternative: a smartphone or DSLR, free software (Meshroom, COLMAP, or RealityCapture), and a laptop can produce a usable 3D model for under USD 500. Accuracy is lower but sufficient for condition mapping and community engagement.</p>

<h3>Written and Archival Research</h3>

<p>Archival research fills gaps that physical documentation cannot. Sources in the African context include national archives (often colonial-era records), mission society archives, municipal building control files, oral histories from long-term residents, and newspaper archives. Budget 25 days of archival work per building. The output is a significance statement and provenance chain essential for funding applications and heritage impact assessments.</p>

<h3>Creating a Preservation Record</h3>

<p>The preservation record is the integrated dossier. It should contain: (1) a significance statement, (2) measured drawings and CAD files, (3) photographic record with metadata, (4) 3D data where available, (5) condition survey with defect mapping, (6) archival research summary, (7) maintenance history, and (8) a digital index. Store in at least two locations: a cloud repository and a physical copy held by the building owner or heritage authority. In areas with unreliable internet, use encrypted external drives and schedule quarterly syncs.</p>

<h3>Using Documentation for Future Maintenance</h3>

<p>Documentation only creates value when it feeds maintenance. Practical uses include: condition trending (compare current photos to baseline), defect tracking (locate and monitor cracks, damp, erosion), planned maintenance scheduling (roof, gutters, drainage), insurance claims (evidence of pre-loss condition), and funding applications (demonstrating stewardship). Assign a documentation custodian a named person responsible for updates. Review the record annually and after any significant event (storm, flood, vandalism, renovation).</p>

<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0; font-weight:bold; color:#b30000;"> SAFETY WARNING Field Documentation Hazards</p>
<p style="margin:0; color:#3a0000;">Documentation fieldwork in African heritage buildings carries real risks: unstable structures (collapsing roofs, cracked walls, rotten timber), confined spaces (basements, roof voids), biological hazards (bat guano, mould, snake and insect bites), and electrical hazards from outdated wiring. <strong>Never enter a building without a structural assessment and a buddy system.</strong> Wear PPE (hard hat, boots, gloves, dust mask), carry a first-aid kit and charged phone, and inform a colleague of your location and expected return time. In malaria-endemic areas, use repellent and appropriate clothing. For laser scanning, ensure safe laser class (Class 1 or 2 only for public areas) and never point scanners at people's eyes. In politically sensitive or remote areas, obtain local permissions and security advice before mobilising.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Define scope and purpose</strong> what decision will this documentation support (permit, funding, maintenance)?</li>
<li><strong>Assemble the team</strong> photographer, surveyor, historian, and a named documentation custodian.</li>
<li><strong>Set the file-naming and metadata standard</strong> before fieldwork begins.</li>
<li><strong>Capture baseline data</strong> photos, measurements, GPS, condition notes, and archival references.</li>
<li><strong>Process and quality-check</strong> verify CAD accuracy, photo metadata, and 3D registration.</li>
<li><strong>Compile the integrated record</strong> significance statement, drawings, images, 3D data, condition survey, archive summary.</li>
<li><strong>Store in two locations</strong> cloud plus encrypted physical copy; lodge a copy with the relevant heritage authority.</li>
<li><strong>Assign a custodian and review schedule</strong> annual review plus post-event updates.</li>
