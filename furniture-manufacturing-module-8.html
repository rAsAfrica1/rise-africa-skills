1. Types of Hardware: Hinges, Slides, Locks, and Handles

Hardware is the skeleton and joints of your furniture. In Africa, where humidity and heat vary, choose hardware that resists rust. For hinges, the most common are butt hinges (for cabinet doors) and concealed hinges (for European-style cabinets). Butt hinges are simple: two plates joined by a pin. Use 3-inch (75mm) butt hinges for wardrobe doors. Concealed hinges, like the 35mm cup hinge, are adjustable and hidden. You need a Forstner bit to drill the cup hole. For drawers, slides come in two main types: roller slides (cheap, noisy) and ball-bearing slides (smooth, load-bearing). Ball-bearing slides come in lengths from 250mm to 600mm. For heavy drawers, use 45kg-rated slides. Locks include cam locks for cabinets and drawer locks. Cam locks require a 19mm hole. Handles: bar handles, knobs, and recessed pulls. In dusty environments, avoid intricate designs that trap dirt. Always buy hardware with a warranty; in many African markets, imported hardware from Turkey or China is common, but check for rust-proof coating like nickel or zinc. When selecting, match screw holes to your wood thickness: for 18mm particle board, use 4mm diameter screws. Test a sample before buying in bulk. Local hardware shops in Nairobi, Lagos, or Accra often sell Chinese-made hinges at low cost, but they may fail after a year. Invest in branded hinges like Blum or Hettich if budget allows. For rural areas, simple butt hinges and wooden knobs are repairable and available. Remember: hardware determines how long your furniture lasts. A well-built cabinet with poor hinges will sag. So spend 15-20% of your material budget on quality hardware.

2. Installation of Drawer Slides and Runners

Drawer slides must be installed parallel and level, or the drawer will jam. First, measure the drawer box depth. For a 500mm deep drawer, use 450mm slides (leave 50mm clearance). Mark the center line of the slide on the cabinet side. Use a combination square to draw a line at the desired height. For bottom-mount slides, the slide sits under the drawer bottom. For side-mount, the slide attaches to the drawer side. Most common in Africa are side-mount ball-bearing slides. Position the cabinet member of the slide 2mm back from the front edge. Pre-drill 2.5mm pilot holes to avoid splitting particle board. Use 4mm x 16mm screws. Attach the drawer member to the drawer side, ensuring the front of the slide is flush with the drawer front. Then, slide the drawer into the cabinet member. Test: pull out fully, push back. If it sticks, adjust the rear bracket. For roller slides, the wheel must sit in the track. If the drawer is too wide, plane the sides. For heavy-duty slides, use 45kg capacity and screw into solid wood or metal brackets. In humid conditions, lubricate with silicone spray, not oil, as oil attracts dust. A common mistake: installing slides at different heights. Use a spacer block to keep both sides equal. For undermount slides (concealed), you need a special jig. These are expensive but give a clean look. In small workshops, make a simple jig from scrap wood. Always install slides after finishing the cabinet. Test with a loaded drawer: put 10kg of tools inside and cycle 20 times. If it wobbles, tighten screws or add a rear support. In Africa, where power tools may be scarce, a hand drill and screwdriver work fine. Just take your time. A well-installed slide lasts 50,000 cycles.

3. Door Hinges and Cabinet Hardware Installation

Installing door hinges correctly ensures doors open smoothly and stay aligned. For butt hinges, first mark the hinge position on the door edge: typically 100mm from top and bottom. Use a marking gauge to score the hinge leaf outline. Chisel a mortise 2mm deep. For a 75mm hinge, the mortise is 75mm long, 25mm wide, and 2mm deep. Test fit the hinge; it should sit flush. Screw the hinge to the door with 3mm x 12mm screws. Then, hold the door in place and mark the hinge positions on the cabinet frame. Chisel matching mortises. Attach the frame leaf. For concealed hinges, use a 35mm Forstner bit to drill the cup hole in the door. The hole center is usually 22.5mm from the door edge. Drill depth: 12mm. Press the hinge cup in. Then, screw the mounting plate to the cabinet side. The plate has slots for adjustment. Use a screwdriver to adjust the door up/down, left/right, and in/out. For cabinet doors, use two hinges for doors up to 900mm tall, three for taller. For locks, drill a 19mm hole for cam locks. Insert the lock, then the cam. The cam should turn to lock the door. For handles, drill holes 5mm larger than the screw diameter. Use a drill guide to keep holes straight. In Africa, where wood may be termite-prone, use stainless steel screws. If the wood is soft, add a drop of wood glue in the screw hole for grip. Always check that doors close without rubbing. Adjust hinges until the gap is even, about 2mm. For wardrobes, use three hinges per door. After installation, open and close 10 times. If the door sags, tighten the hinge screws or move the hinge plate. Remember: a door that sticks is a customer complaint. Take 10 minutes to adjust properly.

4. Adjustable Shelving and Support Systems

Adjustable shelves let users change storage height. The most common system is the 5mm shelf pin and shelf support. Drill 5mm holes in the cabinet sides at 32mm intervals. This is the standard system used worldwide. Use a shelf pin jig or a simple template made from hardboard. Mark the first hole 100mm from the bottom, then every 32mm. For a 1800mm tall cabinet, you get about 50 holes. Use a brad-point drill bit for clean holes. Insert plastic or metal shelf supports. Metal supports are stronger; plastic ones are cheaper but can break. For heavy loads, use a shelf support with a locking clip. Another system is the wooden dowel: 8mm dowels in 8mm holes. This is common in traditional African furniture. But dowels can swell in humidity. For adjustable shelves, use a shelf with a thickness of at least 18mm. A 600mm wide shelf will sag under 20kg load if thinner. Add a stiffener or use a 25mm shelf. For corner cabinets, use a corner shelf support. In small workshops, you can make a drilling jig from a piece of plywood with holes at 32mm. Clamp it to the cabinet side and drill through. Always drill perpendicular. If holes are angled, the shelf will wobble. Test by placing a level on the shelf. For metal shelf standards (the vertical tracks), screw them into the cabinet sides with 4mm screws every 150mm. These are great for adjustable shelves in shops. In Africa, where particle board is common, seal the holes with paint or glue to prevent moisture swelling. For a professional look, use shelf pins with rubber sleeves. They grip better and don't fall out. When installing, ensure all holes are at the same height on both sides. Use a story stick: a long piece of wood with marks for each hole. This speeds up work. A well-installed adjustable shelf system can hold 50kg per shelf if done right.

5. Fasteners: Screws, Bolts, Nuts, and Specialty Fasteners

Fasteners hold furniture together. For wood, use wood screws with coarse threads. In Africa, common sizes are 4mm x 40mm for hinges, 5mm x 50mm for frames. Always pre-drill a pilot hole that is 75% of the screw diameter. For hardwoods like mahogany, use a larger pilot hole. For particle board, use confirmat screws (also called Euro screws). They need a 5mm pilot hole and a 7mm countersink. Confirmat screws are strong and don't strip. For bolts, use carriage bolts for outdoor furniture. A 6mm x 75mm carriage bolt with a washer and nut works well. For knockdown furniture, use cam locks and dowels. The cam lock requires a 15mm hole in the panel and a 8mm hole in the edge. Insert the cam, then the bolt. Turn with a screwdriver. For metal-to-wood, use machine screws with T-nuts. T-nuts have prongs that bite into wood. For specialty fasteners, consider: pocket screws (for face frames), which require a pocket hole jig. Use 32mm pocket screws for 18mm stock. For hinges, use hinge screws that are 3.5mm x 16mm. For drawer slides, use 4mm x 16mm. Never use drywall screws for furniture; they are brittle and snap. In humid climates, use stainless steel or coated screws to prevent rust. In many African markets, you can buy screws by weight. Check for smooth threads and sharp points. For outdoor furniture, use galvanized bolts. When assembling, use a screwdriver that fits the screw head. A Phillips #2 is most common. Don't over-tighten; you can strip the wood. For MDF, use screws with a coarse thread and a small pilot hole. If a screw hole is stripped, insert a wooden matchstick and glue, then re-screw. For bolts, use a wrench and tighten until snug. Always use washers under nuts. For heavy loads, use bolts with lock washers. A good rule: for every 10kg of load, use one 5mm screw. So a shelf holding 30kg needs three screws per support. Keep a variety of fasteners in your workshop: 3mm, 4mm, 5mm, 6mm screws, bolts, nuts, and washers.

6. Quality Checks for Hardware Functionality

After installing hardware, test every function. For drawers, open and close 10 times. The drawer should glide smoothly with no binding. Check that the drawer front is aligned with the cabinet face. If it's off by more than 2mm, adjust the slides. For doors, open and close 10 times. The door should not rub. Check the gap around the door: it should be even, about 2mm. If the door sags, tighten the hinge screws. For locks, insert the key and turn. The lock should engage and disengage without force. Test the lock 5 times. For handles, pull firmly. They should not wiggle. If they do, tighten the screws. For adjustable shelves, place a 10kg weight on the shelf. It should not sag more than 3mm. Check that shelf pins are fully inserted. For fasteners, check that all screws are tight. Use a screwdriver to test. A loose screw will cause squeaks. For bolts, check that nuts are snug. In Africa, where humidity changes, wood expands and contracts. So leave a 2mm gap between doors and frames. After a week, re-check all hardware. Tighten any loose screws. Lubricate slides and hinges with silicone spray. For a professional finish, test the furniture as if you were a customer: open every drawer, close every door, lock every lock. If something fails, fix it before delivery. A common quality check is the 'shake test': gently shake the furniture. If it wobbles, the hardware is loose or the joints are weak. For cabinets, apply a 20kg load on a shelf for 24 hours. If it holds, it's good. For drawer slides, test with 15kg load. For hinges, test with a 5kg door. Always keep a checklist: hinges adjusted, slides smooth, locks work, handles tight, shelves stable. Sign off after all checks. This reduces returns and builds your reputation. In many African markets, word-of-mouth is key. A single failure can ruin your name. So take quality checks seriously.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Hardware and Fittings for Furniture Manufacturing

In African furniture workshops from Kumasi's Sokoban Wood Village to Nairobi's Gikomba market, Lagos's Oshodi, and Johannesburg's Wynberg margins are thin and reputations are built on how a drawer feels after twelve months of daily use. Hardware is typically 815% of a cabinet's material cost but drives 60% or more of customer complaints. This practical reference gives you the numbers, torque values, tolerances, and sourcing logic you need to specify, install, and verify hardware so that your workshop competes on durability, not just price.

Treat this module as a bench-side document. Print it, laminate it, and pin it above the assembly table.

1. Choosing Hardware for the African Market Reality

Three environmental factors dominate hardware failure in African markets: high humidity (coastal West Africa, the Niger Delta, Mombasa, Dar es Salaam), airborne dust (Sahel, Northern Nigeria, semi-arid East Africa), and voltage-supply inconsistency where electric tools are used. Choose hardware accordingly:

  • Coastal and humid zones: Specify stainless steel (AISI 304) hinges and slides, or at minimum zinc-plated steel with a minimum 8 m coating and salt-spray rating of 120+ hours. Standard nickel-plated steel will pit within 1824 months near the coast.
  • Dusty inland zones: Use enclosed or fully concealed drawer runners with sealed ball-bearing cages. Open roller slides jam with fine dust.
  • High-load commercial use (hotels, offices, schools): Upgrade from 35 kg to 45 kg dynamic load slides and from 100 to 110165 hinges.
  • Import substitution: Local assembly of hinges and handles exists in Egypt, South Africa, Nigeria, and Kenya. Where a local supplier can match a 50,000-cycle test certificate, local sourcing cuts lead time from 812 weeks to 12 weeks and reduces forex exposure.

3. Installation Sequence for Drawer Slides (Bench Standard)

The single most common cause of a "sticky drawer" complaint in African workshops is a 12 mm misalignment between the two slide members. Follow this sequence without shortcuts:

  1. Measure the drawer box. The drawer width must be cabinet opening minus 25.4 mm (1 inch) for standard side-mount slides, or minus 10 mm for each side for under-mount slides.
  2. Mark the vertical centre line of the slide on the cabinet side and on the drawer side. Use a marking gauge, not a pencil by eye.
  3. Drill pilot holes per Table 2. Do not skip this step particleboard splits and the slide loses 3050% of its holding strength.
  4. Mount the cabinet member first. Fix with two screws at the front and rear, then check level with a spirit level.
  5. Insert the drawer member into the cabinet member, then pull the drawer out to engage the inner member. Fix the drawer side with two screws.
  6. Test the cycle. Open and close 20 times. Any binding indicates misalignment loosen, adjust, retighten.
  7. Apply a front-adjustment screw if the slide has 2D or 3D adjustment. Set the drawer front flush with the cabinet face.
SAFETY WARNING Personal Protective Equipment and Tool Safety

Hardware installation involves high-speed drilling, sharp metal edges, and fine airborne dust. Before any drilling or cutting:

  • Wear impact-rated safety glasses (EN 166 / ANSI Z87.1). Metal shavings from hinge cups and slides cause permanent eye injury.
  • Wear a P2/N95 dust mask when drilling MDF or particleboard. Formaldehyde and fine dust exposure is a documented occupational hazard in furniture workshops.
  • Use hearing protection above 85 dB(A). A router or hinge-boring machine exceeds this routinely.
  • Never wear gloves near a drill press or router entanglement causes degloving injuries. Use a push stick or jig instead.
  • Isolate power before changing drill bits or servicing machines. In workshops with unreliable earthing, use a residual-current device (RCD) on every portable tool.
  • Store gas cylinders, solvents, and adhesives away from welding and grinding areas. Many African workshops mix woodworking and metalwork in one bay this is a fire and explosion risk.

4. Quality Checks Before Dispatch

Every piece leaving your workshop should pass these checks. A returned cabinet costs 35 times the profit of the original sale once transport and reputation loss are counted.

  • Cycle test: Open and close each drawer and door 20 times. Listen for scraping or clicking.
  • Load test: Place 1.5 the rated load in the drawer for 10 minutes. Check for sag at the front.
  • Alignment: Door gaps should be uniform within 1 mm top, bottom, and sides.
  • Fastener check: Every screw seated flush, no stripped heads, no protruding tips inside the cabinet.
  • Lock function: Key turns smoothly through full 90; cam engages the strike plate without force.
  • Handle torque: Apply 5 Nm by hand handle must not rotate or loosen.
  • Shelf support: Load each shelf with 25 kg for 5 minutes; no visible deflection beyond 3 mm.
  • Finish protection: Hardware masked before spraying; no overspray on slides or hinges.

5. Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Hardware specification sheet signed off before cutting panels.
  2. Pilot holes drilled per Table 2 never drive screws into raw particleboard.
  3. Slides mounted level, parallel, and centred within 1 mm.
  4. Hinges set to 35 mm cup depth, door overlay calculated from hinge arm offset.
  5. All fasteners torqued to specification; no over-tightening (strips threads and crushes board).
  6. 20-cycle function test completed on every moving part.
  7. Load test at 1.5 rated capacity passed.
  8. Customer-facing warranty card lists hardware brand, load rating, and cycle life.
