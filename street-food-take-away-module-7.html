Lessons

1. Skewers and Satay: Meat and Vegetable Options

Skewers are one of the easiest and most profitable street foods. You need bamboo skewers (soak in water 30 minutes to prevent burning), a grill or charcoal stove, and a good marinade. For meat, use beef, chicken, or goat cut into 2-3 cm cubes. A simple African marinade: 1 cup oil, 1/2 cup soy sauce, 1/4 cup vinegar, 4 cloves garlic (minced), 1 tbsp ginger (grated), 1 tsp chili flakes, salt and pepper. Marinate meat for at least 2 hours, preferably overnight in a fridge. For vegetables, use onions, bell peppers, tomatoes, mushrooms, and zucchini cut into chunks. Thread meat and vegetables alternately onto skewers, leaving a small gap between pieces so they cook evenly. Grill over medium-high heat (charcoal should be grey with ash) for 8-12 minutes, turning every 2 minutes. For chicken, ensure internal temperature reaches 75C. For satay, make a peanut sauce: 1 cup roasted peanuts (ground), 1 cup coconut milk, 2 tbsp soy sauce, 1 tbsp palm sugar, 1 tsp chili paste, 1 tbsp lime juice. Simmer all for 5 minutes. Serve skewers with the sauce. In Africa, you can use local spices like suya mix (ground peanuts, ginger, paprika, cayenne) for a Nigerian-style skewer. For vegetables, brush with oil and grill 5-7 minutes until charred. Sell skewers in sets of 3-5 on a paper plate with a side of sauce. Cost per skewer: about 50-70% profit margin if you buy meat in bulk. Keep raw and cooked separate to avoid contamination. Always wash hands and tools after handling raw meat.

2. Fried Chicken: Korean, Southern, and Japanese Styles

Fried chicken is a global favourite. You need a deep pot, oil (vegetable or groundnut), a thermometer, and a wire rack. For all styles, start with chicken pieces (drumsticks, wings, thighs) seasoned with salt and pepper. For Southern style: make a buttermilk brine (2 cups buttermilk + 1 tbsp hot sauce) and soak chicken 4 hours. Dredge in flour mixed with 1 tbsp paprika, 1 tsp garlic powder, 1 tsp onion powder, 1 tsp cayenne, salt and pepper. Fry at 165C for 12-15 minutes until golden and internal temp 75C. For Korean style: use a thin batter of 1 cup flour, 1 cup cornstarch, 1 tsp baking powder, 1 cup cold water, 2 tbsp soy sauce. Fry once at 160C for 8 minutes, then double-fry at 190C for 3 minutes for extra crunch. Toss in a sauce: 2 tbsp gochujang, 2 tbsp honey, 1 tbsp soy sauce, 1 tbsp rice vinegar, 1 tbsp sesame oil, minced garlic. For Japanese karaage: marinate chicken in 2 tbsp soy sauce, 1 tbsp sake (or dry white wine), 1 tbsp ginger juice, 1 tsp sugar for 30 minutes. Coat in potato starch or cornstarch. Fry at 170C for 4-5 minutes until light golden. In Africa, you can use cassava flour for a gluten-free crunch. Always fry in batches to keep oil temperature stable. Drain on a wire rack, not paper, to keep crispy. Serve with lemon wedges, chili sauce, or mayo. Cost: use local chicken, buy whole and cut yourself to save money. Oil can be reused 3-4 times if strained and stored cool.

3. French Fries and Loaded Fries

French fries are a top seller. Use starchy potatoes like Russet or local Irish potatoes. Wash, peel, and cut into 1 cm thick sticks. Soak in cold water for 30 minutes to remove excess starch. Dry completely with a clean cloth. Heat oil to 150C (low-medium). Fry potatoes for 5-6 minutes until soft but not brown. Remove and let cool for 10 minutes. Then heat oil to 190C (high). Fry again for 2-3 minutes until golden and crispy. This double-fry method gives the best texture. Salt immediately after frying. For loaded fries: top hot fries with melted cheese (cheddar or local cheese), cooked minced meat, chopped onions, jalapeos, and a drizzle of mayo and ketchup. For a African twist, use suya spice, grilled chicken bits, and a spicy pepper sauce. You can also make masala fries: sprinkle with curry powder, chili powder, and salt. To keep fries hot for sale, hold under a heat lamp or in a warmer, but never cover tightly or they steam and get soggy. For large batches, pre-fry the first stage, cool, and store in fridge for up to 24 hours. Then fry to order. Oil temperature is critical: too low and fries absorb oil; too high and they burn outside while raw inside. Use a thermometer. Change oil when it darkens or smokes. Serve in paper cones or boxes with a fork. Profit margin is high: a 50 kg bag of potatoes makes many servings. Buy potatoes in bulk from local markets. Add seasoning like rosemary salt or peri-peri for variety.

4. Onion Rings and Tempura

Onion rings and tempura are crispy, light, and cheap to make. For onion rings: slice large onions into 1 cm rings, separate. For the batter: 1 cup flour, 1 tsp baking powder, 1 tsp salt, 1 tsp paprika, 1 cup cold water or beer. Mix until just combined (lumps are fine). Dip rings in batter, then fry at 180C for 2-3 minutes until golden. Drain on a rack. For extra crunch, coat in breadcrumbs after batter. For tempura: use a light batter of 1 cup flour, 1 cup ice-cold water, 1 egg yolk. Mix lightly; do not overmix. Dip vegetables (sweet potato slices, green beans, cauliflower, pumpkin) or shrimp. Fry at 175C for 2-3 minutes until light and crispy. Tempura should be pale, not brown. Serve with tentsuyu sauce: 1/4 cup soy sauce, 1/4 cup mirin (or honey), 1/4 cup dashi (or water), grated ginger. In Africa, you can use local vegetables like plantain, okra, and yam. For onion rings, a spicy mayo dip: 1/2 cup mayo, 2 tbsp ketchup, 1 tsp chili sauce, 1 tsp paprika. Keep oil clean: strain after each use. For volume sales, pre-batter onion rings and freeze on a tray, then fry from frozen at 180C for 3-4 minutes. Tempura batter must be used immediately; discard after 30 minutes. Serve in paper bags with a dip. Cost per serving is low, making these high-profit items. Experiment with local flours like cassava or maize for gluten-free versions.

5. Grilled Corn and Elote

Grilled corn is a simple, high-margin street food. Use fresh sweet corn or maize. Remove husks and silk, then grill over medium-high charcoal (grey ash) for 10-12 minutes, turning every 2 minutes until kernels are charred in spots. Brush with butter or oil during grilling. For elote (Mexican street corn): grill corn, then slather with a mixture of 1/2 cup mayo, 1/2 cup sour cream (or yoghurt), 1 cup crumbled cotija cheese (or feta), 1 tsp chili powder, 1 tsp garlic powder, juice of 1 lime, salt. Roll corn in the mixture, then sprinkle with more cheese and chili powder. Serve with a lime wedge. For a African twist, use a spicy peanut sauce: 1/2 cup ground roasted peanuts, 1 tbsp chili paste, 1 tbsp honey, 1 tbsp soy sauce, 2 tbsp water. Or a peri-peri butter: 1/2 cup butter, 2 tbsp peri-peri sauce, 1 tsp garlic. Grill corn, brush with peri-peri butter. You can also boil corn first for 5 minutes to speed up grilling. For large events, pre-grill corn and hold in a warmer. Sell corn on the cob or cut kernels into cups. For cups: cut kernels off grilled corn, top with butter, cheese, chili, and lime. Cost per cob is low; selling price can be 3-4 times cost. Use a sharp knife to cut kernels. Always wear gloves when handling hot corn. Provide toothpicks or forks. Keep corn warm in a cooler with a hot water bottle or in a chafing dish. Experiment with different seasonings: curry powder, smoked paprika, or suya spice.

6. Dipping Sauces and Seasonings

Great dipping sauces turn simple fried and grilled foods into memorable meals. You need a few base recipes and then vary them. For a universal spicy mayo: 1 cup mayonnaise, 2 tbsp ketchup, 1 tbsp hot sauce (like Tabasco), 1 tsp paprika, 1 tsp garlic powder, 1 tsp lime juice. Mix well. For a garlic aioli: 1 cup mayo, 4 cloves garlic (minced), 1 tbsp lemon juice, salt and pepper. For a sweet chili sauce: 1/2 cup sugar, 1/2 cup water, 1/4 cup rice vinegar, 2 tbsp chili flakes, 2 cloves garlic (minced), 1 tbsp cornstarch mixed with 2 tbsp water. Simmer sugar, water, vinegar, chili, and garlic for 5 minutes, then add cornstarch slurry and cook until thickened. For a peanut sauce: 1 cup peanut butter, 1/2 cup coconut milk, 2 tbsp soy sauce, 1 tbsp honey, 1 tbsp lime juice, 1 tsp chili paste. Whisk over low heat until smooth. For a yoghurt mint sauce: 1 cup yoghurt, 2 tbsp chopped mint, 1 tbsp lemon juice, 1 clove garlic (minced), salt. For dry seasonings: suya spice (ground peanuts, ginger, paprika, cayenne, salt), peri-peri (chili, garlic, lemon, oil), or a simple salt and pepper mix with paprika and garlic powder. Store sauces in squeeze bottles or small containers. Label and date. Keep cold sauces refrigerated; discard after 3 days. For fried foods, offer at least two dips: one creamy, one spicy. For grilled meats, offer a chimichurri: 1 cup parsley, 1/2 cup oil, 1/4 cup vinegar, 4 cloves garlic, 1 tsp chili flakes, salt. In Africa, use local ingredients like tamarind, groundnut, and scotch bonnet. Test your sauces with a small group before selling. Portion control: 30-50 ml per serving. Charge extra for premium dips. Always maintain hygiene: use clean spoons, never double-dip.

<h3 style="color:#b71c1c; margin-top:0;"> SAFETY WARNING: Hot Oil and Open Flames</h3>
<p style="margin-bottom:8px;"><strong>Never leave hot oil unattended.</strong> Oil at 170200C can cause severe burns and can ignite if overheated. In many African street food settings, fryers are operated near gas cylinders, charcoal stoves, and crowded walkways. Follow these rules:</p>
<ul style="margin-top:0;">
<li>Keep a fire blanket or lid nearby to smother oil fires. <strong>Never use water on an oil fire.</strong></li>
<li>Do not overfill the fryer oil should not exceed one-third of the pot depth.</li>
<li>Dry food before frying; wet food causes violent oil spitting.</li>
<li>Keep children and customers at least 1 metre from the fryer.</li>
<li>Turn off gas or remove charcoal before leaving the station, even briefly.</li>
<li>Wear closed shoes and an apron; avoid loose clothing near open flames.</li>
</ul>
<p style="margin-bottom:0; font-size:0.9em;">Source: Adapted from U.S. Fire Administration (USFA) kitchen fire safety guidance and WHO burn prevention resources.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Calibrate your thermometer weekly.</strong> Ice water should read 0C; boiling water 100C (adjust for altitude). A faulty thermometer means undercooked chicken or burnt fries.</li>
<li><strong>Weigh your portions.</strong> Use a digital scale for meat, fries, and batter. Consistent portion sizes protect your margin and build customer trust.</li>
<li><strong>Filter and monitor frying oil daily.</strong> Dark, smoky, or foaming oil must be replaced. Rancid oil ruins flavour and creates health risks.</li>
<li><strong>Marinate under refrigeration.</strong> Never leave raw meat marinade at ambient temperature for more than 30 minutes in hot climates.</li>
<li><strong>Use separate boards and knives for raw meat and vegetables.</strong> Colour-code if possible: red for meat, green for produce.</li>
<li><strong>Pre-portion dipping sauces into single-serve sachets or squeeze bottles.</strong> This reduces waste, improves hygiene, and speeds up service.</li>
<li><strong>Track your cost per portion weekly.</strong> Update your prices when input costs rise do not absorb losses silently.</li>
<li><strong>Train every helper on handwashing and fire safety.</strong> Your brand is only as safe as your least-trained staff member.</li>
</ol>
