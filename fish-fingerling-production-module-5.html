1. Transition from Endogenous to Exogenous Feeding

When fish larvae hatch, they survive on their yolk sac for the first few days. This is the endogenous feeding stage. For most African catfish (Clarias gariepinus), this lasts about 2-3 days at 27-28Â°C. You will know the yolk is almost gone when the larvae become darker and start swimming horizontally instead of hanging vertically. At this point, they must start eating external foodâthis is the exogenous feeding stage. If they don't eat within 24-48 hours after yolk absorption, they will die. The critical window is called 'point of no return' (PNR). For catfish, PNR is about 4-5 days after hatching. To check, look at the belly under a light: if it's flat and clear, the yolk is gone. Start feeding immediately.

First food must be small enough for the tiny mouth. For catfish, newly hatched Artemia nauplii (0.4-0.5 mm) work well. You can also use rotifers if you have them. In Africa, many farmers use decapsulated Artemia cysts or micro-particulate feed (0-50 microns). A simple trick: feed a few drops of egg yolk emulsion (boiled egg yolk mixed with water) as a temporary starter, but this fouls water quickly, so only use for 1-2 days.

Transition stress is real. Larvae that don't eat within 24 hours become weak and cannibalistic. To reduce stress, keep water temperature stable at 27-28Â°C, maintain gentle aeration, and keep light dim. Feed 6-8 times daily, small amounts each time. Observe under a torch at night: if larvae have pink or orange bellies, they are eating Artemia. If bellies are empty, increase feeding frequency.

In Africa, many hatcheries use the 'green water' technique: add a little algae (Chlorella) to the larval tank. This provides oxygen, reduces stress, and feeds rotifers that the larvae can eat. But don't let algae bloom too thickâit can cause oxygen crashes at night. A simple rule: keep water slightly green, not pea soup. After 3-4 days of exogenous feeding, larvae grow fast. You can then switch to larger feed like chopped tubifex or formulated crumbles. Always monitor survival daily: if you see many dead larvae at the bottom, something is wrongâcheck water quality, feed size, and density.

2. Live Feed Production: Rotifers, Artemia, and Copepods

Live feed is the backbone of larval rearing. In Africa, three types are most practical: rotifers, Artemia, and copepods. Rotifers (Brachionus) are tiny (100-200 microns) and perfect for first-feeding larvae of species like tilapia and catfish. To culture rotifers, you need a starter culture, green algae (Chlorella or Spirulina powder), and clean water. A simple method: fill a 20-litre bucket with dechlorinated water, add 1 teaspoon of Spirulina powder, and introduce rotifers. Keep at 25-28Â°C, aerate gently, and harvest daily by filtering through a 50-micron sieve. Feed them every 8 hours with a pinch of Spirulina. A healthy culture produces 50-100 rotifers per ml.

Artemia (brine shrimp) is the most common live feed in African hatcheries because cysts are easy to store and hatch. You can buy Artemia cysts from aquaculture suppliers in Nigeria, Kenya, or South Africa. To hatch: use a cone-shaped tank, add 1 litre of clean water, 1 tablespoon of sea salt (or rock salt), and 1 gram of cysts. Aerate strongly for 24 hours at 28Â°C. After hatching, turn off aeration, shine a light at the bottom, and siphon out the orange nauplii. Rinse them before feeding. Never feed unhatched cystsâthey can block larval guts. One gram of cysts yields about 200,000 nauplii.

Copepods are the best natural feed for many marine and some freshwater larvae because they are rich in omega-3. In Africa, you can culture copepods in outdoor ponds or tanks using green water and a starter culture. They are harder to grow than Artemia, but a simple method: fill a 100-litre tank with filtered pond water, add 10 litres of green water, and introduce copepods. Feed with rice bran or soybean meal. Harvest with a 100-micron net. Copepods reproduce slowly, so you need a large volume.

For small-scale farmers, the easiest combination is: rotifers for the first 3 days, then Artemia nauplii from day 4 onward. If you cannot get Artemia, use sieved termites or mosquito larvae (but risk of disease). Always rinse live feed in clean water before feeding to avoid introducing pathogens. Store Artemia cysts in a cool, dry placeâthey last for years. Rotifer cultures crash easily, so keep backup cultures. In Africa, many farmers use locally available materials: plastic buckets, mosquito nets as sieves, and solar-powered air pumps. Start small and scale up as you learn.

3. Feeding Strategies for Larval Fish

Feeding larvae is not like feeding adult fish. Their stomachs are tiny, and they need food constantly. The rule is: little and often. For the first week after yolk absorption, feed 8-10 times a day. This means every 2-3 hours, including at night if possible. In practice, many African farmers feed at 6am, 9am, 12pm, 3pm, 6pm, and 9pmâsix times. But for best survival, aim for more. Use a plastic dropper or a small cup to spread feed evenly across the water surface. Never dump food in one spotâit will rot and kill larvae.

Feed size must match mouth size. For catfish larvae, start with rotifers or Artemia nauplii (0.4-0.5 mm). After 5 days, they can eat larger Artemia (1 mm). After 10 days, switch to chopped tubifex, moina, or formulated crumbles (0.5-1 mm). A simple test: put a few larvae under a magnifying glass and look at their gut. If you see orange (Artemia) or green (algae), they are eating. If guts are empty, food is too big or too little. For tilapia, first feed is often finely ground rice bran or micro-particulate feed (0-50 microns).

Feed density matters. In a 100-litre tank with 10,000 larvae, you need about 5-10 Artemia nauplii per ml. That means 500,000 to 1,000,000 nauplii per feeding. If you feed too little, larvae starve. If too much, water fouls. A good practice: feed until you see a slight cloudiness in the water, then stop. After 30 minutes, check if larvae have full bellies. If not, feed again. Overfeeding is a common mistakeâit causes ammonia spikes and bacterial blooms.

In Africa, many farmers use the 'green water' technique to supplement feeding. The algae provide oxygen and feed rotifers, which larvae eat. But you still need to add Artemia. Another strategy is co-feeding: give both live feed and formulated feed from day 5. This helps larvae adapt to dry feed early. Start with 10% formulated, 90% live, then gradually increase. Wean by day 15-20. Always remove uneaten food after 1 hour using a siphon. Keep a feeding log: date, time, feed type, amount, and larval behavior. This helps you adjust. Remember, larvae are fragileâconsistency is key.

4. Water Quality Management in Larval Tanks

Larval fish are very sensitive to water quality. A small change can kill thousands in hours. The most important parameters are temperature, dissolved oxygen, ammonia, nitrite, and pH. For most African catfish and tilapia larvae, keep temperature at 27-28Â°C. Use a thermometer and adjust with a heater or by adding warm water. Avoid sudden changesâmore than 1Â°C per hour stresses larvae. Dissolved oxygen should be above 5 mg/L. Use a simple aeration pump with an air stone. Keep aeration gentleâstrong bubbles can damage tiny larvae. If you see larvae gasping at the surface, increase aeration.

Ammonia and nitrite are deadly. In new tanks, ammonia can rise quickly from waste and uneaten feed. Do not let ammonia go above 0.1 mg/L. You can test with a simple API test kit (available in most African cities). If ammonia is high, do a 50% water change with clean, dechlorinated water. Never use chlorinated tap water directlyâlet it sit for 24 hours or use a dechlorinator. For nitrite, keep below 0.5 mg/L. The best way to control both is to have a biofilter, but for larval tanks, daily water changes of 20-30% are common. Use a siphon to remove waste from the bottom without sucking up larvae. Cover the siphon with a fine mesh.

pH should be between 6.5 and 8.0. In Africa, well water can be acidic or alkaline. Test before use. If pH is too low, add a little baking soda (1 teaspoon per 100 litres). If too high, add a few drops of vinegar (but carefully). Always add to a bucket of water first, then pour into tank. Salinity for marine larvae: 28-32 ppt. For freshwater, 0-2 ppt. Use a refractometer or hydrometer.

Light and turbidity also matter. Keep light dim and diffuseâdirect sunlight causes algae blooms and temperature swings. Use a shade net over outdoor tanks. Green water (algae) is good but not too dense. If water turns dark green, reduce light or add fresh water. Always keep a spare tank with clean water for emergencies. Test water every morning and evening. Record results. In Africa, many farmers use a simple checklist: temperature, pH, ammonia, and observe larval behavior. If larvae are swimming actively and have full bellies, water is likely good. If they hang at the surface or bottom, test immediately. Prevention is better than cure.

5. Grading and Size Sorting of Larvae

As larvae grow, they do not all grow at the same rate. Some become bigger and stronger, and they will start eating the smaller ones. This is cannibalism, and it can wipe out 30-50% of your stock. Grading (size sorting) is the solution. You should grade larvae every 3-5 days starting from day 7 after hatching. The goal is to separate large, medium, and small larvae into different tanks. This reduces competition and cannibalism, and gives small larvae a chance to catch up.

How to grade? You need two or three sieves with different mesh sizes. For catfish larvae, use sieves of 1.0 mm, 1.5 mm, and 2.0 mm. Gently scoop larvae from the tank using a fine net or a plastic cup. Pour them into the top sieve and let water drain. The smallest larvae pass through the first sieve, medium through the second, and largest stay on top. Work quicklyâlarvae can suffocate out of water. Keep them in water at all times. A simple method: use two buckets with clean water. Put the sieve in the first bucket, gently rinse larvae through. Then move to the next sieve. Always handle larvae with wet hands or a soft net. Never use rough netsâthey damage the delicate skin.

After grading, put each size group into separate tanks. Stocking density: for small larvae, 50-100 per litre; for medium, 30-50 per litre; for large, 20-30 per litre. Always keep water quality high. Feed the small group more frequently (8-10 times a day) and give them smaller feed. The large group can eat larger feed. If you don't have sieves, you can use a simple method: use a plastic colander with different hole sizes, or even a mosquito net with different mesh. In Africa, many farmers make their own graders using PVC pipes and mesh. It's cheap and effective.

Grading is stressful for larvae. Do it early in the morning when temperatures are cool. Add a little salt (1-2 g/L) to the water to reduce stress. After grading, do not feed for 2 hours. Observe for any signs of shock (larvae lying on bottom). If many die, you were too rough. Practice on a small batch first. Grading also helps you estimate survival and growth. Record the number and average size of each group. This data helps you plan feed and tank space. Over time, you will learn to grade quickly and gently. Remember, a well-graded population grows faster and more uniformly, which means more fingerlings and more profit.

6. Common Diseases and Health Management in Larvae

Larval fish are very vulnerable to disease because their immune system is not fully developed. The most common problems in African hatcheries are bacterial infections, fungal infections, and parasites. Poor water quality is the number one cause. If you keep water clean, you prevent most diseases. But you still need to know the signs and what to do.

Bacterial infections: look for larvae that are lethargic, have red spots, or swollen bellies. They may hang at the surface or bottom. The bacteria are usually Aeromonas or Pseudomonas. These come from dirty water, overcrowding, or handling stress. Treatment: improve water quality immediatelyâdo a 50% water change. Add salt at 2-3 g/L (non-iodized) for 3 days. If severe, use oxytetracycline at 50 mg/L for 5 days (but check local regulations). In Africa, many farmers use neem leaf extract (soak 100 g of dried neem leaves in 10 litres of water for 24 hours, then add to tank at 10 ml/L). This has antibacterial properties.

Fungal infections: look for white cotton-like growth on the body, especially on dead eggs or injured larvae. This is usually Saprolegnia. It appears when water is dirty or temperatures fluctuate. Treatment: remove dead larvae immediatelyâthey are the source. Add salt at 3 g/L. For severe cases, use methylene blue at 2 mg/L (but it stains tanks). Prevent by keeping water clean and avoiding overcrowding.

Parasites: common ones are Trichodina (looks like a spinning top under microscope) and Ichthyophthirius (white spots). These cause scratching, flashing, and weight loss. Treatment: for Trichodina, use formalin at 25 ml/L for 1 hour (as a dip). For white spot, raise temperature to 30Â°C for 3 days (but check species tolerance). In Africa, many farmers use salt baths (5 g/L for 30 minutes) as a general treatment. Always test on a few larvae first.

Prevention is better than cure. Here are key practices: 1) Disinfect all tanks and equipment with chlorine (then rinse well) before use. 2) Quarantine new live feed (Artemia, rotifers) by rinsing in clean water. 3) Do not overfeedâuneaten food breeds bacteria. 4) Keep stocking density low. 5) Maintain stable temperature and pH. 6) Remove dead larvae daily. 7) Keep a separate sick tank. 8) Wash hands before handling. In Africa, many hatcheries lose 50% of larvae to disease. With good management, you can reduce that to less than 10%. If you see disease, act fast. Isolate affected larvae, treat, and record what worked. Share with other farmers.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Larval Rearing and Nursery Management for African Fish Fingerling Production

This practical reference consolidates the core operational knowledge required to rear fish larvae through the critical nursery phase in African hatchery systems. Whether you are producing Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus), African catfish (Clarias gariepinus), or emerging species such as Heterotis niloticus, the margin between profit and loss is measured in survival rates. A hatchery that achieves 60% larval survival versus one that achieves 20% operates at radically different unit economics. This module provides the technical benchmarks, feeding protocols, water quality thresholds, and health management practices that determine whether your fingerling enterprise scales or stalls.

Across sub-Saharan Africa, demand for quality fingerlings consistently outstrips supply. The African catfish sector in Nigeria alone requires an estimated 2 billion fingerlings annually, yet domestic hatcheries supply less than a third of that figure. Tilapia cage farming on Lake Volta, Lake Victoria, and the Zambezi system faces similar shortfalls. The bottleneck is rarely broodstock availability â it is larval and nursery survival. Mastering the six lessons in this module directly addresses that bottleneck and positions your hatchery to capture premium pricing for uniform, healthy fingerlings.

Feeding Strategies for Larval Fish

Feeding strategy determines growth rate, uniformity, and survival. The transition from endogenous to exogenous feeding is the most vulnerable period â typically occurring between day 3 and day 7 post-hatch for African catfish, and day 5 to day 10 for Nile tilapia. During this window, larvae must encounter prey at the right density, size, and nutritional profile.

Key principles for African hatchery operators:

  • Feed frequency: 6â12 times daily for first-feeding larvae. Continuous feeding via automatic feeders is ideal but manual feeding every 2â3 hours is acceptable for small-scale operations.
  • Prey density: Maintain 5â15 rotifers/mL or 2â5 Artemia nauplii/mL in the larval tank. Below 1 prey/mL, larvae expend more energy searching than they gain from feeding.
  • Weaning: Begin co-feeding formulated microdiets (200â400 Âµm) from day 7â10 while gradually reducing live feed over 5â7 days.
  • Green water technique: Adding cultured microalgae (Chlorella, Nannochloropsis) to larval tanks improves water quality, provides nutritional boost to live prey, and enhances larval feeding response through visual contrast.

Grading and Size Sorting of Larvae

Cannibalism is the leading cause of mortality in African catfish nurseries, and size heterogeneity is its primary driver. Larvae that are 2â3 mm larger than their siblings will actively prey on smaller tank-mates. Grading â the mechanical separation of larvae by size â must begin as early as day 7â10 post-hatch and be repeated every 3â5 days until the fingerlings reach 3â5 g.

Methods used in African hatcheries include:

  • Grading boxes with adjustable bar spacing: Low-cost, locally fabricated units with stainless steel or plastic bars set at 1 mm increments.
  • Mesh screens: Nylon or stainless mesh of varying aperture sizes (1 mm, 2 mm, 3 mm) fitted to rectangular frames.
  • Grading graders (commercial): Rotating drum or belt graders for larger operations processing >50,000 larvae per batch.

After grading, transfer the largest and smallest groups to separate tanks. The middle group can remain together. Feed the graded groups according to their size â smaller larvae require smaller feed particles and more frequent feeding. Re-grade every 3â5 days because growth rates diverge rapidly even within a single cohort.

Common Diseases and Health Management in Larvae

Larval fish are immunologically naive at hatching. Their adaptive immune system is not fully functional until several weeks post-hatch, making them entirely dependent on innate immunity and the hatchery environment for protection. The most common disease challenges in African larval rearing systems are:

  • Bacterial infections: Aeromonas hydrophila, Flavobacterium columnare, and Vibrio species cause fin rot, tail rot, and systemic infections. These emerge when organic load is high and water exchange is inadequate.
  • Fungal infections: Saprolegnia spp. attack damaged tissue, particularly after handling or grading. Eggs and weak larvae are most susceptible.
  • Parasitic infestations: Trichodina, Ichthyophthirius multifiliis (white spot), and gill flukes (Dactylogyrus) are common in recirculating systems with poor biosecurity.
  • Nutritional deficiencies: Vitamin C and essential fatty acid deficiencies manifest as spinal deformities, erratic swimming, and high mortality during metamorphosis.

Health management in larval tanks relies on prevention rather than treatment. Prophylactic antibiotic use is strongly discouraged â it drives antimicrobial resistance and is increasingly restricted in export-oriented value chains. Instead, focus on:

  • Strict biosecurity: dedicated equipment per tank, foot baths, hand sanitisation, no sharing of nets between tanks.
  • Water quality stability: daily monitoring of DO, pH, ammonia, and temperature.
  • Nutritional enrichment: ensure live feed is enriched with HUFA (highly unsaturated fatty acids) and vitamins before feeding.
  • Prompt removal of dead larvae: dead larvae decompose rapidly and fuel bacterial blooms.
  • Quarantine of new broodstock: 14â21 days before introduction to the hatchery.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Monitor water quality daily: Record DO, temperature, pH, ammonia, and nitrite every morning before feeding. Act immediately if any parameter drifts outside the ranges in Table 2.
  2. Feed small and often: First-feeding larvae need 6â12 feedings per day. Never let prey density drop below 1 organism/mL in the larval tank.
  3. Enrich live feed: Boost rotifers and Artemia with HUFA and vitamin supplements for at least 6â12 hours before feeding to larvae.
  4. Grade early and often: Begin size sorting at day 7â10 and repeat every 3â5 days. Separate the largest 20â30% to prevent cannibalism.
  5. Maintain biosecurity: Dedicated nets, foot baths, hand washing, and no sharing of equipment between tanks or between hatchery and grow-out ponds.
  6. Remove dead larvae immediately: Twice-daily checks. Dead larvae are a bacterial reservoir and a warning sign of water quality problems.
  7. Wean gradually: Transition from live feed to formulated microdiets over 5â7 days. Abrupt weaning causes starvation and stunting.
  8. Keep records: Log daily survival, feed amounts, water quality, and grading results. Data drives profitability â you cannot improve what you do not measure.
