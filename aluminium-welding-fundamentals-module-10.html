1. Porosity and How to Stop It

Porosity is those little holes you see in a weld, like bubbles frozen in the metal. In aluminium, it happens when hydrogen gas gets trapped as the weld cools. The gas comes from moisture, oil, grease, paint, or even the air. Aluminium is very sensitive to this because it melts at around 660Â°C and absorbs hydrogen quickly when hot, then releases it as it freezes, forming bubbles.

To stop porosity, you must clean the metal properly. Use a stainless steel wire brush dedicated only to aluminium. Brush the joint and 5 cm around it until it shines. Then wipe with acetone or denatured alcohol to remove oil. Do this immediately before welding. Also, keep your filler wire clean and dry. Store it in a sealed plastic bag with silica gel. If you use a gas cylinder, make sure the gas is pure argon (99.99% or better). Flow rate should be 10-15 litres per minute for a #6 or #7 cup. Too little flow lets air in; too much causes turbulence that also pulls in air.

Your torch angle and travel speed matter. Keep the torch tilted about 10-15 degrees back (pushing angle) and move steadily. If you go too slow, the weld pool overheats and absorbs more hydrogen. If you go too fast, you might not get enough penetration, but that's a different defect. For a butt weld on 3 mm aluminium, aim for a travel speed around 20-30 cm per minute. Listen to the sound: a steady sizzling like bacon is good. A popping or hissing sound means trouble.

If you still get porosity, check your gas lines for leaks. A tiny leak will pull in moist air. Use soapy water on connections. Also, preheat thick sections to 100-150Â°C to drive off moisture. But don't overheat. For thin sheets, no preheat needed. If you're welding outside, set up a windbreak. Even a gentle breeze can blow away your shielding gas. A simple cardboard or metal sheet works.

Remember, prevention is better than grinding out porosity. Once you have it, you must cut or grind out the porous area and re-weld. That wastes time and filler. So take the extra five minutes to clean and set up right. Your welds will be stronger and look better.

2. Cracks: Hot and Cold

Cracks in aluminium welds are serious. They can cause parts to fail suddenly. There are two main types: hot cracks and cold cracks. Hot cracks happen while the weld is still hot, just after it solidifies. Cold cracks happen later, sometimes hours or days after welding. Both are preventable if you know the causes.

Hot cracks usually occur in the weld metal itself. They happen when the weld metal is weak during cooling and shrinks. Aluminium alloys like 6061 are prone to hot cracking if you use the wrong filler. For 6061, use 4043 or 5356 filler. 4043 is more crack-resistant but less strong. 5356 is stronger but can crack if the weld is highly restrained. The best way to avoid hot cracks is to use the right filler and reduce restraint. Preheat to 150-200Â°C for thick sections to slow cooling and let the weld metal gain strength before shrinking. Also, use a backstep welding technique: weld short segments, then step back and weld over the start of the previous segment. This spreads out the heat and reduces stress.

Cold cracks, also called delayed cracks, happen in the heat-affected zone (HAZ) or at the weld toe. They are often caused by hydrogen embrittlement, similar to porosity but worse. Moisture or oil on the metal can introduce hydrogen. The crack may not appear until hours later. To prevent cold cracks, clean the metal thoroughly, use dry filler, and preheat to drive off moisture. Also, avoid welding on very thick sections without preheat, as fast cooling traps hydrogen. If you must weld a highly restrained joint, use a lower-strength filler like 4043, which is more ductile.

If you see a crack, you must remove it completely. Grind out the entire crack plus 2-3 cm beyond each end. Use a carbide burr or grinding wheel. Then re-weld with the correct filler and technique. Never try to just weld over a crack; it will come back. Inspect after welding with a magnifying glass or dye penetrant to ensure no cracks remain. For critical parts, like trailer frames or boat hulls, always use dye penetrant or X-ray after welding. Cracks can be hidden, so don't trust your eyes alone.

3. Lack of Fusion and Penetration

Lack of fusion (LOF) means the weld metal didn't bond to the base metal or to the previous weld pass. Lack of penetration (LOP) means the weld didn't go deep enough into the joint root. Both are weak points that can cause failures. They often happen because of wrong technique, insufficient heat, or dirty metal.

To prevent LOF, you need enough heat and the right torch angle. For TIG welding, set your amperage based on thickness: about 1 amp per 0.025 mm of thickness. For 3 mm aluminium, use 120-140 amps. For MIG, use 1 amp per 0.025 mm as a starting point, but adjust for travel speed. Keep a short arc length (for TIG, 2-3 mm; for MIG, 10-15 mm). Push the torch slightly into the joint so the arc digs in. If you move too fast, the edges won't melt. If you move too slow, you might get a wide, shallow weld that still lacks fusion at the sides. Use a weaving motion only if needed; for thin material, a straight drag is better.

LOP happens when the root doesn't melt. For butt welds, make sure you have a proper gap (1-2 mm for 3 mm thick aluminium) and use a backing plate if possible. For TIG, you can use a keyhole technique: melt through until you see a keyhole shape, then move forward. That ensures full penetration. For MIG, use a root gap and higher amperage. If you're welding from one side only, you might need to bevel the edges to get access. A 60-degree included bevel is common. Also, clean the joint faces; oxide and oil prevent fusion.

If you suspect LOF or LOP, you can check with a fillet weld break test: weld a T-joint, then break it in a vise. The weld should tear the base metal, not pull away cleanly. If it pulls away, you have LOF. For LOP, cut a cross-section and polish it to see the penetration depth. In the field, use a flashlight and mirror to look at the root. If you see a line where the weld meets the base metal without blending, that's LOF. Grind it out and re-weld. Prevention is easier: use enough heat, clean metal, and correct technique.

4. Distortion and Its Correction

Distortion is when your welded part bends, twists, or warps because of heat. Aluminium expands and contracts a lot when heated, about twice as much as steel. So distortion is a big problem. It can make your part unusable. But you can control it with good planning and technique.

First, minimize heat input. Use the lowest amperage that gives good fusion. Weld fast, but not so fast that you lose penetration. Use a smaller weld bead. For thin sheets, consider skip welding: weld a short segment, then move to another area, letting the first cool. This spreads the heat. Also, use a heat sink. A piece of copper or aluminium clamped next to the weld will draw heat away. You can also use a wet rag to cool the weld immediately after, but be careful not to quench too fast, as that can cause cracks.

Second, balance the stresses. If you're welding a T-joint, weld on both sides alternately. If you're welding a long seam, use backstep welding: start at the end, weld toward the start, then step back and weld over the start. This balances the shrinkage. For butt welds, tack the ends first, then weld from the center out. That way, the shrinkage pulls evenly. Also, pre-setting the parts in the opposite direction of expected distortion helps. For example, if you know the part will bend up, clamp it down with a slight downward bend before welding.

If distortion still happens, you can correct it. For thin sheets, you can hammer the weld area with a planishing hammer while it's still warm. That stretches the metal and flattens it. For thicker parts, you can use localized heating and quenching. Heat a small area with a torch until it's dull red (about 500Â°C), then quench with water. That shrinks the metal and pulls it back. But be careful: too much heat can weaken the metal. Another method is to use a hydraulic press or clamps to force it straight while heating. Always check with a straightedge or square after correction.

Prevention is better than correction. Plan your welds, use jigs and clamps, and don't rush. A little extra time setting up saves a lot of time fixing.

5. Visual Inspection

Visual inspection is the first and most important check you do on a weld. It's free, fast, and can catch most defects. But you need good lighting, a clean surface, and a keen eye. Do it before, during, and after welding.

Before welding, inspect the joint preparation. Check that the edges are clean, free of oil, grease, paint, and oxide. Look for cracks, nicks, or bends. Make sure the gap is correct: for 3 mm aluminium, a 1-2 mm gap is good for butt welds. Check the fit-up; misalignment should be less than 10% of thickness. Use a tape measure or feeler gauge. Also, check your equipment: gas flow, filler wire cleanliness, and torch condition.

During welding, watch the weld pool. It should be shiny and fluid. If it looks dull or has a scum, that's oxide or contamination. Listen to the sound: steady sizzling is good; popping means trouble. Check that the arc is stable and the filler is melting smoothly. If you see the pool getting too wide or too narrow, adjust your travel speed or amperage. Also, watch for undercut: a groove melted into the base metal along the weld toe. That happens if you move too fast or use too much heat. Stop and fix it before continuing.

After welding, let the part cool, then clean it. Use a stainless steel brush to remove smut and oxide. Then inspect with a bright light and a magnifying glass (10x). Look for porosity (small holes), cracks (especially at the ends), lack of fusion (a line where the weld doesn't blend), undercut, overlap, and spatter. Check the weld size: for a fillet weld, the leg should be at least the thickness of the thinner part. Use a weld gauge if you have one. Measure the length and check for uniformity. If you see a defect, mark it with a paint marker and grind it out. Don't try to cover it up.

For critical welds, like on a trailer or a boat, you might need to do a dye penetrant test after visual inspection. But visual is always first. Train your eye to spot problems early. A good welder is also a good inspector.

6. When to Use Dye Penetrant and X-Ray

Visual inspection catches surface defects, but some defects are hidden below the surface. That's when you need dye penetrant testing (PT) or X-ray (RT). PT finds surface-breaking defects like cracks, porosity, and lack of fusion that open to the surface. X-ray finds internal defects like porosity, inclusions, and lack of penetration. Both are used in critical applications, but they cost time and money, so you don't use them on every weld.

Dye penetrant testing is simple and cheap. You clean the weld, apply a red dye, let it soak for 10-15 minutes, wipe it off, then spray a white developer. Any dye that seeped into a defect will bleed out and show as a red line or spot. It works on non-porous materials like aluminium. Use it when you suspect surface cracks, especially after welding high-strength alloys like 6061 or 7075. It's also good for checking welds that will be machined or painted. PT kits cost about $50 and are available at welding supply shops. But PT only works on clean, dry surfaces. Don't use it on hot metal. And it only finds defects that reach the surface.

X-ray testing is more expensive and requires special equipment and training. A portable X-ray machine costs thousands of dollars, and you need radiation safety gear. But it gives a picture of the inside of the weld. You use it for critical welds: pressure vessels, structural beams, aircraft parts, and high-value projects. In Africa, you might find X-ray services in major cities like Nairobi, Lagos, or Johannesburg. For a small shop, it's often outsourced. X-ray can find tiny porosity (0.5 mm) and lack of penetration. But it has limits: it's hard to detect cracks that are not aligned with the beam, and it's expensive. Typically, you X-ray a percentage of welds, like 10% or 20%, depending on the code.

When to use which? Start with visual. If you see a defect, fix it. If you suspect hidden defects, use PT for surface cracks. For internal defects, use X-ray. But remember, the best inspection is prevention: clean metal, correct technique, and proper filler. That reduces the need for expensive testing. In a small workshop, you might only use PT on critical parts and skip X-ray unless required by a client. Always follow the project specifications.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Defects and Inspection in Aluminium Welding

This lesson is a working reference for African fabricators, workshop owners and welding supervisors who need to make fast, defensible decisions about weld quality. Aluminium is not steel. It conducts heat roughly five times faster, it has no colour change as it approaches melting, and its oxide layer melts at around 2,072 Â°C â more than double the melting point of the metal beneath it. Those three facts explain most of the defects you will ever see, and they explain why inspection in an African workshop must be systematic rather than hopeful.

For a business, the economics are blunt. A rejected weld on a market-stall frame costs you a few thousand shillings. A rejected weld on a fuel tanker, a cold-room panel, a borehole pump housing or an export-bound aluminium window frame can cost you the contract, the certification and the customer. Inspection is not overhead. It is the cheapest insurance your workshop will ever buy.

Defect 1: Porosity â and how to stop it

Porosity appears as small rounded cavities, either scattered, clustered or aligned along the weld axis. In aluminium it is caused almost entirely by hydrogen. Sources of hydrogen in a typical African workshop include:

  • Moisture in the shielding gas line, regulator or torch â very common during the rainy season.
  • Hydrocarbon contamination: oil, grease, cutting fluid, diesel film, or marker pen residue.
  • Oxide and hydrated oxide layers on the parent metal or filler wire.
  • Draughts and fans blowing shielding gas away from the pool.
  • Excessively long arc length, which draws in atmospheric contamination.
  • Wet filler wire stored in an open shed, or wire handled with bare, oily hands.

The fix is procedural, not heroic. Store filler wire in a sealed container with desiccant. Wipe the joint and the wire with a clean, lint-free cloth and acetone immediately before welding â not an hour before. Stainless-steel wire brushes must be dedicated to aluminium only; a brush that has touched carbon steel will embed iron and cause both porosity and corrosion. Purge the gas line before the shift. Weld indoors or build a simple windbreak from corrugated sheet. Set the correct gas flow â typically 12â18 litres per minute for GMAW, higher for thick sections â and check for leaks at every connection with soapy water.

Porosity acceptance limits are governed by the applicable standard. For structural work, ISO 10042 and AWS D1.2 both set maximum pore diameters and area percentages by quality level. A common shop rule for non-critical fabrication is that isolated pores up to 1.5 mm are acceptable if total porosity does not exceed 2â3% of the weld cross-section. Critical pressure and structural work requires zero visible porosity and radiographic confirmation.

Defect 2: Cracks â hot and cold

Hot cracks (solidification cracking) form while the weld is still partly liquid, usually along the centreline of the bead. They are promoted by high restraint, deep narrow beads, high sulphur or impurity content in the filler, and by the wide freezing range of certain 6xxx and 7xxx alloys. The cure is metallurgical and procedural: use the correct filler â 4043 or 5356 for most 6xxx work â reduce restraint, use a wider bead profile, and control heat input.

Cold cracks (hydrogen or delayed cracking) form after the weld has cooled, sometimes hours later. In aluminium they are less common than in steel but appear in high-magnesium 5xxx alloys and in heat-affected zones of 7xxx material. They are aggravated by residual stress, poor fit-up and hydrogen already in the joint.

Cracks are never acceptable in a load-bearing weld. If you find one, the repair is complete removal â grind out to sound metal, re-weld, and re-inspect. Never chase a crack with a cosmetic pass; it will return.

Defect 3: Lack of fusion and lack of penetration

Lack of fusion (LOF) is a cold lap where the weld metal fails to bond to the sidewall or the previous pass. Lack of penetration (LOP) is incomplete joint penetration at the root. Both are caused by insufficient heat input, wrong travel angle, too-fast travel speed, contamination, or a joint design the process cannot reach.

Aluminium makes LOF especially dangerous because the oxide layer must be broken mechanically or by cathodic cleaning action. If the arc does not have enough energy to disrupt the oxide, the filler simply sits on top. In service, LOF acts as a crack initiator under fatigue â a serious risk on truck bodies, trailers and any structure exposed to vibration on African road conditions.

Prevention: correct joint preparation and bevel angles, adequate root gap, sufficient current, and a slight back-and-forth manipulation to wash the sidewalls. For thick sections, preheat to 80â120 Â°C to compensate for aluminium's rapid heat loss.

Defect 4: Distortion and its correction

Aluminium's high thermal expansion â roughly twice that of steel â makes distortion the most visible and most commercially damaging defect in African fabrication. A warped window frame or a bowed cold-room panel is scrap.

Controls, in order of effectiveness:

  • Design: use balanced welding, symmetrical joints, and minimise weld volume.
  • Fixturing: clamp rigidly to a strongback or jig. Tacking sequence matters â back-step tacking from the centre outward.
  • Technique: use the lowest heat input that achieves fusion, weld in short passes with cooling between, and use back-step or skip welding.
  • Correction: controlled local heating with a heat gun or oxy-fuel flame, followed by mechanical straightening while warm. Never quench aluminium with water â it locks in stress and can induce cracking.

Visual inspection: your first and cheapest tool

Visual inspection (VT) catches the majority of defects at zero cost. Before welding, inspect joint preparation, cleanliness, fit-up, gap and tack quality. After welding, inspect bead profile, toe blending, spatter, undercut, crater treatment and surface porosity. Use a good light, a magnifier and a weld gauge. Record what you see. In a workshop supplying export customers or certified structural work, a written VT record is often the difference between passing and failing an audit.

Volumetric inspection: dye penetrant vs radiography

Dye penetrant inspection (PT) is a surface method. It detects cracks, laps and porosity that break the surface. It is cheap, fast, and works well in a workshop with no electricity. It will not find internal defects. Radiography (RT) and ultrasonic testing (UT) are volumetric methods â they see inside the weld.

Industrial radiography uses ionising radiation. In many African jurisdictions, only licensed operators with a calibrated survey meter, dosimeter and a demarcated exclusion zone may perform RT. Never allow unauthorised persons within the controlled area. Separately, aluminium welding produces ozone and metal fume; weld in a ventilated area or wear supplied-air respiratory protection. Do not weld on containers that have held fuel or solvents until they have been purged and gas-tested â the explosion risk is real and has killed welders across the continent.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Clean before you weld: acetone wipe on joint and filler wire; dedicated stainless brush for aluminium only.
  2. Check the gas: no leaks, correct flow (12â18 L/min), windbreak in place, line purged at shift start.
  3. Confirm the filler: 4043 or 5356 for 6xxx; match the filler to the alloy and the service condition.
  4. Control heat input: short arc, correct current, interpass temperature below 120 Â°C on heat-treatable alloys.
  5. Tack and fixture: back-step tacks from the centre; rigid jig to control distortion.
  6. Inspect visually every weld: bead profile, toe blending, undercut, crater, visible porosity.
  7. Use PT on critical surface welds: tanks, frames, repairs â before painting or dispatch.
  8. Use RT/UT on code or pressure work: engage a licensed NDT provider and keep the reports.
  9. Record and review: keep a defect log â pattern analysis is how a workshop improves and wins bigger contracts.
