Lessons

1. Month 1-2: Research and Supplier Vetting

In the first two months, your job is to find a product that sells and a supplier you can trust. Start by researching demand. Use Google Trends and Amazon Best Sellers to see what small machines (e.g., sewing machines, water pumps, generators) or pharmaceuticals (e.g., antimalarials, antibiotics, vitamins) are in demand in your country. Talk to local pharmacies, clinics, and hardware stores. Ask what they run out of and what they wish they could stock. For example, a pharmacy in Kenya might say they always need amoxicillin. A hardware store in Nigeria might need small generators. Write down 5-10 product ideas.

Next, find suppliers. Go to Alibaba.com, Made-in-China.com, and Global Sources. Search for your product plus 'Africa' or your country. Look for suppliers with 3+ years on the platform, a gold or verified badge, and transaction history. Read reviews carefully. Contact at least 10 suppliers. Ask for: product specifications, certifications (e.g., ISO, WHO-GMP for pharma), minimum order quantity (MOQ), price per unit, lead time, and shipping cost to your port. Compare quotes. A good supplier responds within 24 hours and answers all questions clearly.

Vet suppliers by requesting samples. Order a sample of the product and a sample of the packaging. Test the product yourself. For pharmaceuticals, check the expiry date and batch number. For machines, test if it works. Also check if the supplier can provide a Certificate of Analysis (CoA) for pharma or a CE certificate for machines. If they hesitate, drop them.

Finally, check regulations. For pharmaceuticals, you need to register with your country's drug regulatory authority (e.g., NAFDAC in Nigeria, PPB in Kenya). For machines, check import duties and standards. Visit your customs office or go online to find the HS code for your product. This tells you the import duty. Budget for these costs. By end of month 2, you should have a shortlist of 2-3 suppliers and a clear product choice.

2. Month 3-4: First Order and Certification

Months 3 and 4 are about placing your first order and getting the paperwork right. Start with a small orderdon't spend all your capital. For example, if your MOQ is 100 units, order 100. Negotiate with your supplier: ask for a 30% deposit and 70% on delivery, or use a trade assurance payment method like Alibaba Trade Assurance. Pay via bank transfer or letter of credit (LC) if the amount is large. Avoid Western Union or cash. Keep records of every payment.

While the order is being produced, work on certifications. For pharmaceuticals, you need import permits and product registration. In Nigeria, NAFDAC requires: application form, CoA, GMP certificate, free sale certificate, and label design. It can take 2-3 months and cost $500-$2000 per product. Start early. For machines, you may need a SONCAP certificate in Nigeria or a PVoC in Kenya. These ensure the product meets local standards. Contact a local inspection agency like SGS or Bureau Veritas. They will inspect the goods at the supplier's factory or at the port. Budget $300-$800 per shipment.

Also, prepare your import documents: proforma invoice, commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading, and certificate of origin. Your supplier should provide these. Check that the HS code on the invoice matches your product. A wrong HS code can lead to higher duties or delays. If you're importing pharmaceuticals, ensure the labels are in English and include dosage, batch number, expiry date, and manufacturer details. For machines, include a user manual in English.

By the end of month 4, your order should be ready and your certifications in progress. Confirm the shipping date with the supplier. Book a freight forwarderask for quotes from 3 forwarders. A 20-foot container from China to Lagos might cost $2000-$4000. For smaller orders, use air freight or groupage. Get a clear quote that includes all charges: freight, insurance, and terminal handling.

3. Month 5-6: Shipping and Customs Clearance

Months 5 and 6 focus on shipping and clearing customs. Once your supplier ships, they will send you a bill of lading (B/L) and other documents. Check the B/L carefully: your name and address must be correct. If you see errors, ask for a corrected copy immediately. For sea freight, transit takes 25-40 days from China to West Africa, 20-30 days to East Africa. Air freight takes 5-10 days but costs 5-10 times more. Choose based on urgency and budget.

Before the ship arrives, start customs clearance. In most African countries, you need a customs broker (clearing agent). Ask other importers for recommendations. A good broker charges a fee (e.g., $200-$500) plus government duties. Provide your broker with: B/L, invoice, packing list, certificate of origin, and any permits (e.g., NAFDAC, SONCAP). Your broker will assess duties. Import duty for pharmaceuticals is often 0-5% in many African countries, while machines may be 5-20%. Also budget for VAT (e.g., 7.5% in Nigeria, 16% in Kenya), port charges, and terminal fees. Total clearance costs can be 30-50% of the goods' value.

When the container arrives, your broker will clear it. You may need to pay duties before release. Inspect the goods at the port or warehouse. Count the units and check for damage. If there are shortages or defects, file a claim with the supplier and shipping line immediately. Take photos and videos as evidence. For pharmaceuticals, check the temperature log if it's temperature-sensitive. If the goods are damaged, you may be able to claim insurance.

After clearance, transport the goods to your storage. For pharmaceuticals, you need a dry, cool, secure room. For machines, a locked warehouse. If you don't have storage, rent a small space. Now your goods are ready for sale. By end of month 6, you should have your first stock in hand.

4. Month 7-9: Sales and Distribution

Months 7 to 9 are about selling. Your goal is to turn stock into cash quickly. Start by pricing your product. Calculate your landed cost: product cost + shipping + duties + clearing + storage + your time. Then add a profit margin. For pharmaceuticals, margins are often 20-40%. For machines, 30-50%. Check competitor prices. If you're too expensive, you won't sell. If too cheap, you lose money. For example, if your landed cost for a sewing machine is $80, sell at $120-$140.

Next, find buyers. For pharmaceuticals, visit pharmacies, clinics, and hospitals. Bring samples and your registration certificate. Offer a trade discount for bulk orders (e.g., 10% off for 10+ units). For machines, visit hardware stores, market stalls, and online marketplaces like Jumia or Konga. Also use social media: WhatsApp Business, Facebook Marketplace, and Instagram. Post clear photos and videos of the product in use. Offer delivery within your city.

Consider distribution. You can sell directly or use distributors. A distributor buys in bulk at a lower price and sells to retailers. This moves stock faster but reduces your margin. For example, you might sell to a distributor at $100 per unit instead of $140 retail. That's okay if you need cash flow. Alternatively, consignment: you leave goods with a shop and they pay you after selling. This is risky if they don't pay. Start with direct sales to learn the market.

Track your sales daily. Use a simple notebook or Excel sheet. Record: date, buyer, quantity, price, and payment status. Follow up on unpaid invoices. Reinvest profits into more stock. By end of month 9, you should have sold at least 70% of your first order. If not, adjust your pricing or marketing. Don't waitunsold stock ties up cash.

5. Month 10-12: Scaling and Reinvestment

Months 10 to 12 are about scaling. You've sold your first order and learned what works. Now increase your order size. If you ordered 100 units, order 200-300. This lowers your per-unit cost because suppliers give volume discounts. For example, a supplier might charge $5 per unit for 100 units but $4 for 500 units. Also negotiate better shipping rates. A full container (20ft) costs less per unit than groupage. Ask your freight forwarder for a contract rate.

Expand your product line. If you sold antimalarials, add vitamins or painkillers. If you sold sewing machines, add generators or water pumps. But don't diversify too fast. Add one new product at a time. Test it with a small order. Use the same supplier vetting process. Also consider new markets: if you sold in Lagos, try Ibadan or Abuja. Use social media ads targeted to those cities. Hire a salesperson on commission if you can't cover the area yourself.

Reinvest profits. A good rule: 50% back into stock, 30% for operating expenses, 20% as your salary. Keep a cash reserve for emergenciescustoms delays, price drops, or damaged goods. Build relationships with your bank. Open a business account if you haven't. After 6 months of transactions, you may qualify for a small business loan or invoice financing. This can help you scale faster.

Also, formalize your business. Register with your country's corporate affairs commission (e.g., CAC in Nigeria, eCitizen in Kenya). Get a tax identification number (TIN). This allows you to import in your business name and claim VAT refunds. It also builds trust with suppliers and buyers. By month 12, you should have a repeatable system: research, order, clear, sell, reinvest. Your goal is to double your monthly revenue every 6 months. With discipline, you can build a sustainable import business.

6. Building a Sustainable Import Business

A sustainable import business is not just about buying and selling. It's about building systems that work without you. First, document everything. Create a supplier list with contact details, prices, and lead times. Keep a customer database with names, phone numbers, and purchase history. Use a simple CRM like Google Contacts or a notebook. When you're sick or traveling, someone else can step in.

Second, diversify suppliers. Don't rely on one supplier. If they raise prices or run out of stock, you're stuck. Have at least two suppliers per product. Test a small order with a new supplier every 3 months. Also, consider local manufacturing or assembly. For example, you can import machine parts and assemble them locally, reducing import duties and creating jobs. For pharmaceuticals, you can import raw materials and package locally if you meet regulations.

Third, manage cash flow. Importing ties up cash for 3-6 months. You need enough working capital to cover the next order while the current one sells. A common mistake is to spend all profits on personal items. Instead, keep a separate business account. Pay yourself a fixed salary. Reinvest the rest. Use a simple cash flow forecast: list expected sales, expenses, and loan payments for the next 6 months. If you see a shortfall, arrange credit early.

Fourth, stay compliant. Regulations change. Join an importers association or online group (e.g., LinkedIn groups, WhatsApp groups for importers in your country). Attend trade fairs like the Canton Fair or local expos. Read customs notices. Renew your licenses before they expire. For pharmaceuticals, keep batch records and adverse event reports. For machines, keep warranty and service records.

Finally, think long-term. A sustainable business builds trust with customers, suppliers, and regulators. Offer after-sales service. For machines, provide spare parts and repairs. For pharmaceuticals, provide information leaflets. Happy customers come back. After 2-3 years, you can expand to other countries or start your own brand. The 12-month plan is just the beginning. With discipline and adaptability, you can grow a profitable import business that supports your family and community.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: The 12-Month Import Business Plan

Importing small machines and pharmaceuticals into Africa is not a get-rich-quick scheme. It is a working-capital business with regulatory gates, currency exposure, and logistics friction. The difference between importers who survive year one and those who fail is rarely the product idea. It is the plan. This practical reference gives you a month-by-month operating calendar, the documents you need, the numbers you must track, and the traps that quietly kill margins.

Use this as a working document. Print it. Put the dates in your calendar. Assign an owner to every task. If you are a solo operator, you are the owner of everything, which is exactly why the plan must be written down.

Month 12: Research and Supplier Vetting

Your first job is not to find a supplier. It is to prove demand at a price that still leaves margin after duty, freight, and financing. For small machines, talk to repair shops, hardware retailers, and cooperatives. For pharmaceuticals, talk to licensed wholesalers and clinic procurement officers, but never sell before you are registered.

Vet at least three suppliers per product. Ask for business licence, ISO or GMP certificate, export history, and references from African buyers. Use a paid verification service or your bank's trade desk. A supplier who refuses a video factory tour or third-party inspection is a red flag.

Month 34: First Order and Certification

Place a small, controlled first order. Do not fill a container on your first attempt. Use a letter of credit (LC) or a 30% deposit and 70% against bill of lading. Get the product tested or registered early. In Nigeria, NAFDAC registration can take months. In Kenya, the Pharmacy and Poisons Board and KEBS both have a say. For machinery, PVoC (Pre-Export Verification of Conformity) is required for many markets.

Month 56: Shipping and Customs Clearance

Choose Incoterms deliberately. FOB gives you control of freight. CIF is easier but hides cost. Budget for demurrage, storage, and clearing agent fees. Clearance delays of 721 days are normal at many African ports. Build that into your cash cycle, not your hope.

Month 79: Sales and Distribution

This is where the plan meets reality. Sell to wholesalers, not one-off retail customers, if you want repeat volume. Offer 1430 day credit only to buyers you have verified. Track receivables weekly. A sale on credit is not cash.

Month 1012: Scaling and Reinvestment

If the first cycle worked, reinvest 4060% of profit. Add a second product line or a second supplier. Do not scale by borrowing heavily before you have a repeatable cash cycle. Build a three-month cash buffer first.

Building a Sustainable Import Business

Sustainability means documented systems: supplier files, compliance certificates, customs records, and a simple profit-and-loss per shipment. Know your landed cost per unit. If you do not, you are guessing. And guessing is how importers lose money quietly for a year before they notice.

Safety and Legal Warning

Never import or distribute pharmaceuticals without the required national registration and licence. Unregistered medicines are illegal, dangerous, and can carry criminal liability. Counterfeit or substandard drugs harm patients and destroy your business permanently. Always verify product authenticity, batch numbers, and expiry dates. For machines, ensure electrical standards and safety certification meet your country's requirements before sale.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Prove demand and landed cost before you pay any supplier.
  • Vet at least three suppliers; verify licences and certificates.
  • Start with a small first order, not a full container.
  • Confirm import licence, product registration, and PVoC needs early.
  • Choose Incoterms deliberately; know who pays freight and duty.
  • Budget for demurrage, storage, and clearance delays.
  • Sell to verified wholesalers; track receivables weekly.
  • Reinvest 4060% of profit and build a three-month cash buffer.
