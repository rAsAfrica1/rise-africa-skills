1. Purpose and Methods of Degumming

Crude oil straight from your press contains gumsphosphatides, waxes, and other sticky substances. These gums cause problems: they make the oil cloudy, they burn and stick to your frying pan, and they shorten shelf life. Degumming removes them. In Africa, where many small producers sell to local markets and restaurants, degummed oil looks cleaner and lasts longer, which means better prices and less waste.

There are three main methods. Water degumming is the simplest: you add 23% hot water (about 70C) to oil heated to 6070C, stir gently for 3060 minutes, then let it settle. The gums absorb water, swell, and sink or can be centrifuged out. This works well for oils like sunflower and groundnut. Acid degumming uses food-grade phosphoric acid (0.050.2% of oil weight) or citric acid. The acid converts non-hydratable gums into hydratable ones, so they can be removed with water. This is better for oils like soy and palm that have more stubborn gums. Enzymatic degumming uses enzymes (phospholipases) at 5060C for 24 hours; its expensive and rarely used by small producers in Africa.

For a small-scale operation in Kenya, Nigeria, or Ghana, water degumming is the most practical. You need a stainless steel tank with a heating source, a stirrer, and a way to separate the gum layereither a settling tank with a bottom drain or a simple centrifuge. After degumming, the oil is heated to 90100C to evaporate residual water. Test for gums by heating a sample in a pan: if it browns and sticks, gums remain.

Degumming also protects downstream equipment. If you skip it, the neutralization step will use more chemicals and produce more soapstock. So always degum first. Store degummed oil in clean, dry containers to avoid rehydration of any leftover gums.

2. Water Degumming and Acid Degumming

Water degumming is the go-to method for small and medium producers in Africa. Start with crude oil at 6070C. Add 23% clean water (by weight of oil) heated to about 70C. Stir gently at 6080 rpm for 3060 minutes. Youll see the oil turn cloudy as gums hydrate. Stop stirring and let the tank settle for 24 hours. The gums drop to the bottom as a thick, watery layer. Drain them off. Then heat the oil to 90100C for 30 minutes to drive off moisture. If you have a centrifuge, use it after stirring to speed separationspin at 30004000 rpm.

For oils with high non-hydratable gums (like soy, palm, or cottonseed), water alone wont work. Use acid degumming. First, heat oil to 6070C. Add food-grade phosphoric acid (0.050.2% of oil weight) or citric acid (0.10.3%) diluted in a little water. Mix for 1520 minutes. Then add 23% hot water and stir for another 3060 minutes. The acid breaks down metal-phosphatide complexes so the gums can absorb water. Let settle and drain. Finally, heat to 90100C to remove moisture.

In practice, many African processors combine methods: a small acid dose followed by water. This gives clearer oil and less gum residue. Always use stainless steel or enameled tanksiron or copper will react with the acid and darken the oil. Test your degumming by heating a small sample in a frying pan. If it foams, spatters, or leaves a brown film, you need more acid or longer settling. Store degummed oil in clean, dry drums. Proper degumming reduces refining losses by 12% and improves the final oils color and stability.

3. Chemical Neutralization of Free Fatty Acids

Free fatty acids (FFAs) are the enemy of good oil. They cause rancidity, off-flavors, and smoke at low temperatures. In Africa, where storage conditions can be hot and humid, FFAs rise quickly. Neutralization removes them by reacting with an alkaliusually caustic soda (sodium hydroxide, NaOH). The reaction forms soap, which can be separated.

First, measure the FFA content. A simple titration kit is cheap and available from lab suppliers. For most edible oils, you want FFA below 0.1% after refining. If your crude oil has 2% FFA, you need about 0.14% NaOH (as a percentage of oil weight) for every 1% FFA, plus a small excess (0.050.1%) to drive the reaction. So for 100 kg oil with 2% FFA, use about 0.280.38 kg NaOH. Dissolve the NaOH in water to make a 1020% solution. Heat the oil to 6070C. Add the caustic slowly with gentle stirring (60 rpm) for 1520 minutes. Youll see soapstock form as a curdy layer.

Stop stirring and let settle for 48 hours, or use a centrifuge. Drain the soapstock from the bottom. Then wash the oil with hot water (1020% of oil volume) at 7080C to remove residual soap. Settle and drain again. Repeat washing if needed until the wash water is clear. Finally, dry the oil by heating to 90100C under vacuum or with gentle stirring until no bubbles form.

Safety: NaOH is corrosive. Wear gloves, goggles, and an apron. Always add NaOH to water, never water to NaOH. In Africa, many small refineries use this method successfully. It requires careful measurement and patience, but it turns poor-quality crude oil into clean, saleable cooking oil.

4. Soapstock Treatment and Utilization

Soapstock is the thick, dark, soapy paste that settles out during neutralization. For every 100 kg of crude oil, you might get 515 kg of soapstock, depending on FFA content. Dont throw it awayit has value. In Africa, soapstock can be a source of income or used on the farm.

The first step is to recover entrained oil. Soapstock contains 2040% neutral oil. Heat the soapstock to 8090C with stirring, add 510% hot water, and let it settle. The oil floats and can be skimmed off. You can also add a little salt (12%) to help break the emulsion. This recovered oil can be added back to the next batch of crude oil or sold as technical oil for soap making.

After oil recovery, the remaining soapstock is rich in fatty acids and can be acidulated. Add sulfuric acid (1020% solution) slowly until the pH drops to 23. This converts soaps back to free fatty acids, which float as a dark layer. This acid oil can be sold to soap factories, used as animal feed additive, or burned as fuel. In many African countries, theres a market for acid oil among local soap makers.

If you cant sell it, compost it. Soapstock is biodegradable, but its alkaline, so mix it with acidic materials like fruit waste or cow dung. It adds nutrients to the soil. Some farmers use it as a pest repellent when diluted with water. Never dump soapstock into rivers or streamsit harms fish and plants. Store it in covered drums to prevent rain from washing it away. With a little effort, soapstock becomes a resource, not a waste.

5. Physical Refining vs. Chemical Refining

After degumming, you have two main paths to remove free fatty acids: chemical refining (neutralization with caustic soda) or physical refining (steam distillation under vacuum). Each has pros and cons for African processors.

Chemical refining is what we covered in Lesson 3. It uses NaOH to neutralize FFAs, then washing and drying. It works well for oils with low to medium FFA (up to 5%) and is forgiving if you lack vacuum equipment. But it produces soapstock, uses water, and loses some neutral oil. Its the standard for small-scale operations in Africa because the equipment is simple: tanks, stirrers, and a heat source.

Physical refining uses high temperature (240260C) and vacuum (25 mbar) to steam-strip FFAs. It requires a vacuum pump, a deodorizer, and precise temperature control. Its more energy-intensive but produces less waste and higher yield. Its best for oils with high FFA (like palm oil, which can have 510% FFA) and for large-scale plants. In Africa, physical refining is used in big palm oil mills in Nigeria, Ghana, and Cte dIvoire, but its rare in small workshops because of the cost.

Which should you choose? If you process less than 1 ton per day, chemical refining is more practical. If you have access to reliable electricity and can afford the equipment, physical refining saves money on chemicals and reduces waste. Many medium-scale African processors use a hybrid: degum, then chemical neutralization for high-FFA oils, and physical refining for low-FFA oils like sunflower. Always calculate your FFA first. For FFA above 2%, physical refining may be cheaper in the long run. For FFA below 1%, chemical refining is fine. Consider your local market for soapstockif you can sell it, chemical refining has an extra income stream.

6. Monitoring and Quality Control in Degumming and Neutralization

Quality control (QC) is what separates a hobby from a business. In degumming and neutralization, you must test at every step. Without QC, you waste chemicals, lose oil, and produce inconsistent batches that customers wont trust.

Start with the crude oil. Measure FFA by titration. A simple kit costs less than $50 and gives results in minutes. Also check moisture (using a moisture meter or by heating a sample and weighing) and impurities (filter a sample and weigh the residue). For degumming, test for gums by the cold test: put a sample in an ice bath for 5 hours; if it clouds, gums remain. Or do a hot pan test: heat a tablespoon of oil in a pan; if it foams and spatters, degumming is incomplete.

During neutralization, monitor pH of the soapstock and the oil. After washing, the wash water should be neutral (pH 7). If its still soapy, wash again. After drying, check FFA againit should be below 0.1% for good oil. Also measure color using a simple Lovibond comparator or a colorimeter. A good refined oil is light yellow to golden. If its dark, you may have used too much caustic or overheated.

Keep records: date, batch number, oil type, FFA before and after, chemicals used, temperatures, and yields. In Africa, where electricity can be unreliable, use manual thermometers and hydrometers. Train your workers to follow the same steps every time. A small notebook is your best QC tool. Finally, store samples from each batch for at least 3 months. If a customer complains, you can trace the problem. Good QC builds reputationand in the cooking oil business, reputation is everything.

<h2>Practical Reference: Oil Refining Degumming and Neutralization</h2>

<p>This practical reference is written for small and medium-sized African edible oil processors from a 2 t/day batch plant in Uganda to a 50 t/day continuous refinery in Nigeria. It consolidates the six lessons of this module into shop-floor guidance: what to do, what to measure, what it costs, and where the money is made or lost. In crude vegetable oil, gums and free fatty acids (FFA) are not just quality problems they are yield losses, energy costs, and the difference between selling at a discount or at a premium to a buyer, hotel, or supermarket chain.</p>

<h3>1. Why Degumming and Neutralization Decide Your Margin</h3>

<p>Crude oil from a screw press or expeller typically contains 0.53% phospholipids (gums) and 0.55% FFA depending on seed quality, storage, and pressing temperature. If you skip degumming, gums hydrate during storage and form a sludge that plugs filters, fouls the deodorizer, and causes the oil to darken and foam when heated a fatal defect for frying oil sold to street-food vendors or restaurants. If you skip neutralization, high FFA oil smokes, tastes harsh, and oxidizes quickly, shortening shelf life and destroying repeat business.</p>

<p>Every 1% of FFA removed as soapstock is roughly 1% of your oil volume leaving as a by-product. Recovering and selling soapstock (or acid oil) is therefore a direct revenue stream, not just waste disposal. In Kenya, Nigeria, and Ghana, acid oil and soapstock are bought by soap makers, animal feed blenders, and oleochemical processors. Treating soapstock as a product rather than a cost is one of the fastest margin improvements available to an African refiner.</p>

<h3>2. Degumming: Water vs. Acid Choosing by Feedstock</h3>

<p>Water degumming is the cheapest route and works well for oils where the phospholipids are hydratable typical of good-quality groundnut, sunflower, and sesame oil. Add 23% hot water (7080C) to oil at 6070C, mix gently for 2030 minutes, then separate by settling or centrifuge. The gums drop out and can be dried and sold as lecithin or feed ingredient.</p>

<p>Acid degumming (using food-grade phosphoric or citric acid) is needed when the oil contains non-hydratable phospholipids common in cottonseed, soybean, and oils from damaged or poorly stored seed. Acid converts these gums into hydratable forms, allowing removal. For a small African processor, the practical decision rule is: if the oil comes from well-stored, freshly pressed seed and is destined for local frying oil, water degumming is usually sufficient. If the oil is for export, long shelf life, or comes from cottonseed/soybean, use acid degumming.</p>

<h3>3. Chemical Neutralization: The Core Yield Decision</h3>

<p>Neutralization uses caustic soda (NaOH) or caustic potash (KOH) to convert FFA into soap, which is then separated. The amount of caustic needed depends on FFA content, the oil type, and the desired excess. A common rule of thumb for batch refining is:</p>

<p style="font-family:monospace;background:#f5f5f5;padding:10px;border-left:4px solid #1565c0;">
NaOH required (kg per tonne oil) [0.142 FFA%] + excess (0.050.15%)
</p>

<p>For example, an oil with 3% FFA needs roughly 0.43 kg NaOH per 100 kg oil plus a small excess. Over-dosing wastes caustic and increases neutral oil loss through saponification; under-dosing leaves FFA behind. The soapstock yield is roughly proportional to FFA removed, but typically includes 2050% neutral oil entrained this is where money is lost. Good temperature control (6080C), gentle agitation, and prompt separation minimize entrainment.</p>

<h3>4. Soapstock: Turn a Waste Cost into a Revenue Line</h3>

<p>Soapstock from neutralization typically contains 3060% water, 2050% soap, and 520% entrained neutral oil. Options for African processors:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Sell wet soapstock</strong> to local soap makers simplest, lowest capital, but low price and heavy transport cost.</li>
<li><strong>Acidulate to acid oil</strong> using sulfuric acid recovers fatty acids and some neutral oil; acid oil sells to soap, feed, and oleochemical buyers at a higher price than wet soapstock.</li>
<li><strong>Recover entrained neutral oil</strong> by heating and settling or centrifuging directly increases edible oil yield.</li>
<li><strong>Use as animal feed ingredient</strong> only after proper treatment and with veterinary/feed-safety approval; raw soapstock is high in salt and moisture.</li>
</ul>

<p>Effluent from washing and acidulation must be treated high pH, oil, and COD will fail environmental inspections and can contaminate local water. Simple settling ponds, pH neutralization, and oil traps are minimum requirements in most African regulatory contexts.</p>

<h3>5. Physical vs. Chemical Refining Which Fits Your Business?</h3>

<p>Physical refining (steam stripping under vacuum) removes FFA without caustic, producing less soapstock and higher yield, but requires reliable vacuum, high-temperature steam, and consistent degumming to very low phosphorus (&lt;10 ppm). It suits larger, well-capitalized plants processing palm, coconut, or well-degummed sunflower. Chemical refining is more forgiving, works at smaller scale, and is the default for most African SMEs especially where feedstock quality varies and capital is limited. The business rule: choose physical refining only if you can consistently degum to &lt;10 ppm phosphorus and maintain vacuum/steam systems; otherwise chemical refining will give more stable quality and fewer rejected batches.</p>

<h3>6. Monitoring and Quality Control</h3>

<p>You cannot manage what you do not measure. Minimum QC for a small refinery:</p>
<ul>
<li>FFA titration on every incoming crude batch and every neutralized batch.</li>
<li>Phosphorus or simple gum test (hot water test) after degumming.</li>
<li>Soap content in washed oil (hot water + phenolphthalein).</li>
<li>Moisture and volatile matter after drying.</li>
<li>Color (Lovibond) and peroxide value for finished oil.</li>
<li>Record caustic used, oil in, oil out, soapstock out this is your yield and margin dashboard.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Test FFA and gum content of every crude oil batch before deciding degumming method.</li>
<li>Use water degumming for good-quality groundnut/sunflower/sesame; acid degumming for cottonseed, soybean, or damaged seed.</li>
<li>Calculate caustic dose from FFA% (0.142 FFA% + 0.050.15% excess) never guess.</li>
<li>Hold neutralization at 6080C with gentle mixing; separate soapstock promptly to reduce oil loss.</li>
<li>Wash neutralized oil until soap content is negligible; dry before bleaching/deodorizing.</li>
<li>Recover or sell soapstock/acid oil treat it as a product, not waste.</li>
<li>Wear full PPE for caustic and hot oil; keep eyewash and clean water at the workstation.</li>
<li>Record oil in, oil out, chemical used, and by-product sold for every batch review weekly against yield targets.</li>
</ol>
