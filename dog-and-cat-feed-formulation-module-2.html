Lessons

1. Water: The Most Critical Nutrient

Water is the nutrient your animal needs in the largest amount and the one it can survive without for the shortest time. A dog can lose almost all its fat and half its protein and still live, but losing just 10 percent of its body water causes serious illness, and 15 percent loss usually kills. A healthy dog drinks roughly 50 to 60 ml of water per kilogram of body weight each day. A 20 kg dog therefore needs about 1 to 1.2 litres daily. Cats are desert-adapted animals and drink less by nature, only about 45 to 50 ml per kg per day, so a 4 kg cat needs roughly 180 to 200 ml. Cats evolved to get most of their water from prey, so a cat on dry kibble is often chronically under-hydrated. This is a major cause of urinary and kidney problems in African homes where dry food is the cheap, easy option.

Water quality matters as much as quantity. In many parts of Africa, borehole, well and river water carries high levels of salts, iron, nitrates or bacteria. Never assume your animal can drink what you drink without checking. If your own water is salty or smells of iron, filter it, boil it and let it cool, or buy treated water for your animals. Change drinking water at least twice a day in hot weather. Bowls must be scrubbed daily because biofilm slime grows fast in warm climates and causes diarrhoea.

You can boost water intake cheaply without buying anything. Add warm water to kibble at a ratio of one part water to two parts kibble and let it soak for ten minutes. Feed wet food, or mix in cooked pumpkin, carrot or bone broth with no salt and no onion. Cats in particular respond to running water, so a small solar-powered pet fountain or a dripping tap in a clean basin encourages drinking.

Watch for dehydration signs: skin that stays tented when you pinch the neck, dry tacky gums, sunken eyes, and concentrated dark yellow urine. A quick test is to lift the skin between the shoulder blades; it should snap back in under two seconds. If it stays up, get water into the animal immediately and see a vet. In hot northern Nigerian, Sahelian or Kalahari conditions, increase water allowance by 20 to 30 percent and always provide shade. Clean, cool, fresh water is the cheapest health intervention you will ever give your dog or cat.

2. Protein and Amino Acid Requirements

Protein is built from about 20 amino acids, and your animal's body cannot make 10 of them, so these must come from food. For dogs the essential ones include arginine, histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan and valine. Cats are stricter: they also cannot make taurine, arachidonic acid or enough arginine, so a cat fed dog food will go blind and develop heart failure from taurine deficiency within months. Never feed cat food to dogs as a substitute for dog food, and never feed dog food to cats at all.

Numbers you can work with. A healthy adult dog needs a diet containing at least 18 percent crude protein on a dry matter basis, and 22 to 25 percent for growth, pregnancy or heavy work. A cat needs at least 26 percent crude protein for maintenance and 30 percent for growth and reproduction, though most quality cat foods sit at 32 to 40 percent. These are minimums, not targets. Protein quality matters more than quantity. Egg, fish, poultry, milk and meat are complete proteins with all essential amino acids. Maize, cassava, rice and sorghum are incomplete, low in lysine and tryptophan, so they must be combined with animal or legume protein.

Local and affordable protein sources in Africa include fishmeal from Lake Victoria or the Niger Delta, dried sardines and kapenta, poultry offal, blood meal, soybean meal, groundnut cake, cottonseed meal, and insect meal such as black soldier fly larvae. Black soldier fly larvae contain 40 to 45 percent protein and can be farmed on food waste in a plastic drum, which cuts feed cost dramatically for small producers. Cook all animal proteins thoroughly. Raw poultry carries Salmonella and Campylobacter, and raw freshwater fish in parts of Africa carries parasites. Boiling for 20 minutes is enough.

Signs of protein deficiency include poor growth in puppies and kittens, dull coat, muscle wasting along the spine, slow wound healing, and a swollen belly from low blood albumin. Excess protein is not harmful to healthy adult animals but is wasteful and expensive, and it burdens kidneys already damaged by disease. A practical rule for a small producer: aim for 25 percent protein in dog food and 35 percent in cat food, and always include at least two protein sources so that amino acid gaps in one are filled by the other. Weigh ingredients with a kitchen scale in grams, not by eye, because a 5 percent error in protein can mean the difference between a healthy litter and stunted pups.

3. Fats and Essential Fatty Acids

Fats are the most concentrated energy source in feed, giving about 8.5 kilocalories per gram compared with 3.5 for protein and carbohydrate. A dog needs a minimum of 5.5 percent fat in the diet, and 8 to 15 percent is normal for active animals. Cats need at least 9 percent fat, and 15 to 20 percent is common in good cat food. Fat also carries the fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K, makes food palatable, and supplies the essential fatty acids your animal cannot make: linoleic acid for dogs and cats, plus arachidonic acid for cats, and alpha-linolenic acid which converts to EPA and DHA.

Good fat sources available across Africa include poultry fat, beef tallow, lard, sunflower oil, groundnut oil, palm oil in moderation, and fish oil. Fish oil from sardines, anchovies or mackerel is the best source of EPA and DHA. For a 20 kg dog, half a teaspoon to one teaspoon of fish oil daily supports skin, coat and joints. For a 4 kg cat, a quarter teaspoon is enough. Never exceed this because too much fish oil thins the blood and can cause vitamin E deficiency. Red palm oil is rich in vitamin A and is widely available, but use no more than 2 percent of the diet because excess saturated fat causes pancreatitis.

Essential fatty acid deficiency shows as dry flaky skin, dull brittle coat, excessive shedding, slow wound healing, and in cats poor reproductive performance. These signs are common in African dogs fed mostly maize porridge and scraps. Adding one tablespoon of sunflower oil per kilogram of cooked food for dogs, or a few drops for cats, corrects mild deficiency within three to four weeks.

Rancid fat is dangerous. In hot, humid climates fats oxidise quickly and produce peroxides that destroy vitamins and cause diarrhoea and liver damage. Store oils in dark bottles, keep them out of the sun, and smell them before use; a bitter or paint-like smell means throw it away. Never use oil that has been used for frying because it contains free radicals and burnt particles. When mixing your own feed, add fat last after the food has cooled to below 40 degrees Celsius, since heat destroys essential fatty acids. Weigh fat accurately: it is easy to over-pour. A simple tool is a 5 ml medicine spoon or a small kitchen scale. Balanced fat gives glossy coats, healthy skin, strong puppies and good milk in nursing mothers, all of which raise the market value of your animals.

4. Carbohydrates and Fiber: Roles and Sources

Carbohydrates are not strictly essential for dogs and cats because the body can make glucose from protein and fat, but they are the cheapest energy source and they make feed affordable. Cooked starch from maize, rice, sorghum, millet, cassava, sweet potato and potato supplies 3.5 kilocalories per gram. Dogs digest cooked starch well, up to 90 percent, but cats have low amylase enzyme and digest starch less efficiently, so cat food should stay below 30 percent carbohydrate and preferably under 20 percent. Raw starch is poorly digested by both species and causes gas and loose stool, so always cook grains thoroughly, ideally by boiling or pressure cooking until soft.

A practical African ration for a 20 kg dog might contain 50 percent cooked maize or rice, 25 percent animal protein, 10 percent legumes, 8 percent fat and 7 percent vegetables and minerals. For a cat, reduce carbohydrate to 15 to 20 percent and raise animal protein to 40 to 50 percent. Cassava must be properly processed because raw cassava contains cyanogenic glycosides that are toxic. Peel, soak for 24 hours, and boil thoroughly or ferment into gari before use. Never feed mouldy maize or groundnuts; aflatoxin causes liver cancer and is common in poorly stored African grain.

Fiber is the carbohydrate your animal cannot digest, and it has three jobs: it feeds good gut bacteria, it regulates stool consistency, and it slows sugar absorption. Soluble fiber such as pumpkin, sweet potato, oats, psyllium and beet pulp ferments in the colon and supports gut health. Insoluble fiber such as wheat bran, rice husk and cellulose adds bulk and speeds transit. Dogs do well with 2.5 to 4.5 percent crude fiber, and cats with 1 to 3 percent. For diarrhoea, add one tablespoon of cooked pumpkin per 10 kg body weight twice daily. For constipation, add one teaspoon of psyllium husk or wheat bran per 10 kg body weight mixed into wet food with extra water.

Too much fiber is as bad as too little. Excess fiber binds minerals like calcium, zinc and iron so they pass out in the stool, and it reduces calorie intake, which is dangerous for growing puppies and kittens. Signs of excess fiber are large soft stools, frequent defecation and weight loss despite eating. Weigh fiber sources with a scale. A useful local trick for small producers is to keep a record book of every batch: date, ingredients, weights, and how the animals responded. This turns ordinary cooking into real formulation and lets you repeat what works.

5. Vitamins: Fat-Soluble and Water-Soluble

Vitamins are needed in tiny amounts, measured in milligrams or micrograms, but missing one can cripple or kill your animal. They split into two groups. Fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K are stored in the liver and body fat, so they can build up to toxic levels if overfed, especially A and D. Water-soluble vitamins, the B group and vitamin C, are not stored. Excess passes out in urine, so they must be supplied daily and deficiency appears faster.

Vitamin A is essential for vision, immunity and skin. Dogs and cats cannot convert carotene from plants efficiently, so they need preformed vitamin A from liver, egg yolk, fish oil or fortified premix. A cat needs about 900 micrograms and a dog about 1,500 micrograms per 1,000 kilocalories. Liver is the richest source but feed it only once or twice a week because too much causes bone pain and deformities. Vitamin D controls calcium and phosphorus absorption. Dogs and cats do not make vitamin D from sunlight the way humans do, so it must come from fish liver oil, egg yolk or premix. Deficiency causes rickets in puppies and kittens; excess causes kidney calcification.

Vitamin E protects cell membranes and works with selenium. Sunflower oil, wheat germ and green leafy vegetables supply it. Deficiency causes muscle weakness and in cats a painful condition called steatitis from too much fish oil without enough E. Vitamin K is made by gut bacteria and is rarely deficient unless the animal eats warfarin-type rat poison or has prolonged diarrhoea. B vitamins include thiamine, riboflavin, niacin, pyridoxine, B12, folate, biotin and pantothenic acid. They drive energy metabolism and nerve function. Cats need far more niacin and B6 than dogs because they cannot convert tryptophan efficiently. Thiamine deficiency, common when fish is fed raw and unrefrigerated because an enzyme destroys thiamine, causes head tilt, circling and seizures. Cooking the fish destroys the enzyme and protects the vitamin.

In practice, most African home-made diets are short on vitamins A, D, E and several B vitamins, especially when based on maize and scraps. The simplest fix is a commercial vitamin-mineral premix for dogs or cats, dosed exactly per the label, usually 1 to 2 grams per kilogram of finished feed. For a small producer, buy premix in sealed foil packs, keep it in a cool dark place, and use it within three months of opening because vitamins degrade. Never guess doses of vitamin A or D. Use a gram scale or the measuring scoop supplied. A balanced premix plus fresh liver once a week, egg yolk twice a week, and a variety of vegetables will keep most animals healthy without expensive supplements.

6. Minerals: Macro and Trace Elements

Minerals are divided into macro minerals needed in grams per day and trace minerals needed in milligrams. The macro group is calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, sodium, potassium, chloride and sulphur. The trace group includes iron, zinc, copper, manganese, iodine, selenium, cobalt and molybdenum. The most important balance in any dog or cat diet is calcium to phosphorus. For dogs the ideal ratio is between 1.2 to 1 and 1.4 to 1. For cats it is 1.1 to 1 to 1.3 to 1. Puppies and kittens are very sensitive: a wrong ratio causes bone deformities that cannot be reversed. Meat and offal are high in phosphorus and low in calcium, so a diet of only meat and rice will cause rubber jaw and fractures in growing animals.

Calcium sources available in Africa include bone meal, limestone flour, oyster shell, eggshell powder and dairy. To make eggshell powder, wash shells, boil them, dry them in the sun, and grind to a fine powder with a pestle. One teaspoon of eggshell powder contains about 2,000 mg of calcium. Phosphorus comes from meat, fish, bone and cereal bran. Sodium and chloride come from salt; a dog needs about 0.3 percent salt in the diet and a cat about 0.2 percent, but excess salt causes kidney damage, so weigh it. Potassium is plentiful in meat, potato and banana. Magnesium is in bone, cereal and green vegetables and is rarely deficient.

Trace minerals matter in tiny amounts. Iron from liver, blood meal, red meat and dark green vegetables prevents anaemia. Zinc from meat, egg and shellfish supports skin and immunity; deficiency causes hair loss and poor wound healing. Copper from liver and shellfish works with iron and zinc; deficiency causes anaemia and coat colour change, while excess causes liver damage, especially in Bedlington terriers. Manganese from cereal and bone supports joints. Iodine from iodised salt or seaweed prevents goitre and stillbirths. Selenium from fish, meat and eggs works with vitamin E; deficiency causes muscle weakness and in some African regions where soil selenium is low, white muscle disease in pups. Cobalt is needed only as part of vitamin B12.

Practical formulation rules: always add a calcium source when feeding meat and rice, use a commercial trace mineral premix rather than guessing, and never add extra of any single trace mineral without veterinary advice because the safe range is narrow. Keep a simple record of the mineral mix used per batch. If animals show lameness, bone pain, poor coat or anaemia despite adequate protein, suspect mineral imbalance and correct the calcium to phosphorus ratio first. Weigh minerals on a jeweller's scale accurate to 0.01 grams, because a 1 gram error in a trace mineral can poison a small cat.

<h2>Practical Reference: Essential Nutrients for Dogs and Cats An African Formulator's Field Guide</h2>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the six nutrient lessons in this module into a form you can use at the mixing bench, in a client consultation, or when costing a batch of dog or cat feed for the Kenyan, Nigerian, South African, or wider African market. The objective is commercial: produce a nutritionally complete, safe, and affordable feed that keeps animals healthy, reduces customer complaints, and protects your brand. Every formulation decision from the water content of a wet mix to the inclusion rate of bone meal has a cost and a biological consequence. This guide links the two.</p>

<h3>Why the Nutrient Hierarchy Matters in Business Terms</h3>
<p>Nutrient requirements are not equal in urgency. Water deficiency kills in hours or days. Energy and protein deficiency stunts growth and wastes feed. Vitamin and mineral deficiencies may take weeks to appear but destroy repeat business once a customer's dog develops skin lesions, lameness, or poor coat condition. A formulator who understands this hierarchy can prioritise ingredient spend: secure water and energy first, then protein quality, then fatty acids, then micronutrients. In many African markets, the most expensive mistakes are made by over-spending on imported vitamin premixes while under-spending on digestible protein and clean water.</p>

<h3>Red Safety Warning</h3>
<div style="border:2px solid #b71c1c;background:#ffebee;padding:14px;border-radius:6px;margin:16px 0;">
<strong style="color:#b71c1c;"> SAFETY WARNING DO NOT FORMULATE BY GUESSWORK</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#7f0000;">Certain nutrients are toxic in excess. Vitamin A toxicity from liver-heavy diets causes bone and liver damage. Excess calcium in growing puppies causes osteochondrosis and lameness. High vitamin D causes kidney calcification. Aflatoxin-contaminated maize or groundnut cake common in humid African storage conditions can kill dogs and cats and expose you to legal liability. Never use mouldy or discoloured raw materials. Always weigh micronutrients with a calibrated scale, never by volume. If you cannot verify a premix's composition, do not use it. When in doubt, consult a qualified animal nutritionist or veterinarian before selling the batch.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Water first:</strong> Confirm clean, cool, uncontaminated water is available at every point of production and to every customer. Cats especially need moisture-rich feed or rehydration guidance.</li>
<li><strong>Protein quality over quantity:</strong> Check digestibility and amino acid profile, not just the crude protein percentage on the label. Cats need taurine, arginine, and arachidonic acid.</li>
<li><strong>Balance fats:</strong> Include both omega-6 and omega-3 sources. Add vitamin E as an antioxidant, especially in hot, humid African conditions where fats oxidise quickly.</li>
<li><strong>Fibre with purpose:</strong> Use fibre for gut health and stool quality, not as cheap filler. Cats tolerate less carbohydrate than dogs.</li>
<li><strong>Use a complete vitamin premix:</strong> Do not rely on liver, vegetables, or guesswork to meet vitamin needs. Store premixes cool, dry, and away from sunlight.</li>
<li><strong>Calculate Ca:P:</strong> Target 1.21.4:1 for most life stages. Never exceed 1.5:1 without veterinary guidance, especially for puppies.</li>
<li><strong>Test raw materials:</strong> Check maize and groundnut cake for aflatoxin. Check fish meal for ash, salt, and rancidity. Reject anything suspect.</li>
<li><strong>Label honestly and keep records:</strong> Record batch numbers, ingredient sources, and dates. This protects your business if a customer raises a complaint and supports regulatory compliance.</li>
</ol>

<h3>African Market Business Notes</h3>
<p>In many African cities, pet food is sold through agro-veterinary shops, supermarkets, and increasingly through WhatsApp and social media. Customers are price-sensitive but also increasingly quality-conscious, particularly in urban middle-class segments in Nairobi, Lagos, Accra, Johannesburg, and Kigali. The formulator who can demonstrate nutritional completeness using recognised standards such as AAFCO or FEDIAF gains a competitive edge over informal mixers who sell by volume without analysis. Consider offering both a maintenance dry diet and a wet or rehydrated option, since cats and older dogs often benefit from higher moisture. Where imported premixes are expensive or subject to currency fluctuations, explore regional suppliers and always verify certificates of analysis. Finally, invest in simple staff training on hygiene, weighing, and storage; most feed failures in small and medium enterprises come from process errors, not from formulation theory.</p>
