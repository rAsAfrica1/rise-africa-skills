1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Gold is not just a shiny metalit is money, security, and a way to feed your family. In Africa, gold has been mined for centuries, from the Ashanti kingdom in Ghana to the gold reefs of South Africa and Zimbabwe. Today, artisan miners produce about 20% of the world's gold, and in many rural areas, it is the main source of cash income. But finding gold is not about luck. It is about understanding where gold comes from and how to read the land.

Gold is formed deep underground in rocks called reefs, usually in quartz veins. Over millions of years, weathering and erosion break these rocks down. Rain washes the gold particles into rivers, where they settle in sand and gravel. That is why you often find gold in riverbeds and old river terraces. In Africa, some of the richest areas are in the greenstone belts of Tanzania, the Birimian formations of West Africa, and the ancient riverbeds of the Congo. Knowing this helps you target the right spots instead of digging randomly.

For the artisan miner, this matters because gold is heavyabout 19 times heavier than water. That means it sinks and stays in places where the water slows down: behind big rocks, on the inside of river bends, and in cracks in the bedrock. If you learn to think like a gold particle, you will find more gold with less effort. This module will teach you the basics of where gold comes from, the tools you need, how to work safely, and how to test your ground. It is practical knowledge that you can use today.

Remember, gold mining is not just about digging. It is about reading the land, knowing the signs, and working smart. By the end of this module, you will be able to identify likely gold-bearing spots and extract gold using simple, effective methods. That is the difference between a hobby and a livelihood.

2. Tools and Materials

To find and extract gold, you need the right tools. You do not need expensive machinery. Start with basics: a pickaxe, a shovel, a bucket, a pan, and a sluice box. In Africa, many miners make their own tools. For example, a pan can be a large plastic basin or a metal dish. A sluice box can be built from wood and a piece of carpet or burlap. The key is to have tools that can move and wash gravel efficiently.

For digging, a sturdy pickaxe and shovel are essential. In hard ground, a crowbar helps. You also need a sieve or classifier to separate big rocks from fine gravel. A 5-gallon bucket is perfect for carrying material. For panning, a plastic pan about 30-40 cm wide works well. If you can afford it, a metal pan with riffles is better because it catches fine gold. For sluicing, you need a sluice box with riffles and a water source. Many miners use a small pump or just a stream.

Safety gear is not optional. You need gloves, boots, and eye protection. A dust mask is important when digging dry soil, as silica dust can damage your lungs. In some areas, you may need a headlamp if working underground. Always carry drinking water and a basic first aid kit. If you are using mercury to amalgamate gold, you must have proper ventilation and never use it near water sources. Mercury is dangerous and illegal in many countries.

Other useful items: a magnifying glass to inspect gold particles, a small vial to store gold, and a notebook to record where you found gold. A GPS or smartphone can help you mark spots. In Africa, many miners use a simple ground sluice or a blanket sluice to catch fine gold. These are cheap and effective. Remember, the goal is to process as much gravel as possible with the least effort. So invest in tools that save time and are durable. With these tools, you are ready to start.

3. Step by Step Method

Finding gold is a step-by-step process. First, you need to locate a likely spot. Look for areas where gold has been found before, or where the geology suggests gold. In Africa, check riverbeds, especially where the river bends or where there are large rocks. Also, look for quartz veins in hillsides. If you see quartz, there might be gold. Ask local miners or eldersthey often know where gold has been found.

Once you have a spot, test it. Dig a small hole, about 1 meter deep, and take a sample of gravel from the bottom. Pan it. If you see fine gold, you are in the right place. If not, move on. Do not waste time on ground that shows no gold. Next, if you find gold, dig a larger pit. Remove the topsoil and set it aside. Then dig out the gravel layer, which is usually where gold is concentrated. In riverbeds, gold is often found in the bedrock cracks. So dig down to the bedrock and clean out the cracks.

Now, process the gravel. If you have a sluice box, set it up in a stream with a good flow. Shovel gravel into the sluice, and let the water wash it. The riffles will catch the heavy gold. If you are panning, fill your pan about half full with gravel, add water, and swirl. Remove big rocks by hand. Then tilt the pan and let the lighter material wash out. Keep swirling until only black sand and gold remain. Use a magnet to remove black sand if it is magnetic. Finally, pick out the gold with tweezers or a small brush.

For fine gold, you can use a blanket sluice. Lay a blanket in a stream, pour gravel over it, and let the water wash it. Gold particles get trapped in the blanket fibers. Then wash the blanket in a bucket and pan the concentrate. This method is cheap and works well in Africa. Remember to always clean your equipment after use, and store your gold in a secure place. With practice, you will get faster and find more gold.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Many beginner miners make mistakes that cost them gold. One common mistake is digging in the wrong place. Gold is where you find it, but it is not everywhere. If you dig random holes without testing, you waste time. Fix: Always test the ground first. Pan a sample before you dig a big pit. Look for signs like black sand, quartz, or old workings.

Another mistake is not digging deep enough. Gold is heavy, so it sinks. In riverbeds, it often sits on bedrock or in cracks. If you only dig the top gravel, you miss the gold. Fix: Dig down to bedrock. If the bedrock is deep, at least dig until you hit a clay layer or hardpan, which can trap gold. In some places, gold is found in old river channels that are now dry. You may need to dig deeper there.

Overloading your pan or sluice is another error. If you put too much gravel in a pan, you cannot wash it properly, and gold washes out. Fix: Work with small amounts. For a pan, fill it no more than half full. For a sluice, feed gravel slowly and evenly. Let the water do the work.

Using too much water flow in a sluice can also wash gold out. The flow should be fast enough to move gravel but not so fast that it flushes gold. Fix: Adjust the slope of your sluice. A gentle slope with moderate flow is best. Test with a known weight to see if it catches.

Not cleaning equipment between sites can contaminate samples. If you move to a new spot, clean your pan and sluice to avoid false readings. Fix: Rinse everything with clean water. Also, do not use mercury without proper training. It is dangerous and illegal in many African countries. Fix: Use gravity methods instead, like panning and sluicing. They are safer and effective.

Finally, giving up too soon. Gold mining requires patience. Fix: Keep testing and learning. Talk to experienced miners. Join a local mining association. With time, you will improve.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety is not just a ruleit is what keeps you alive and able to mine another day. In artisan gold mining, the biggest risks are collapsing pits, falling rocks, drowning, and exposure to dust and chemicals. In Africa, many accidents happen because miners dig unsafe holes without support. Always shore up your pit with timber or rocks if it is deeper than 1.5 meters. Never work alone. Have a partner who can help if something goes wrong.

When digging, wear a hard hat, gloves, and steel-toe boots. If you are working in a river, be aware of flash floods. In many parts of Africa, rains come suddenly. Never work in a riverbed when it is raining upstream. Also, avoid working under overhanging banks. They can collapse without warning.

Dust is a silent killer. When you dig dry soil, you breathe in silica particles that scar your lungs. Always wear a dust mask, especially when breaking rock or digging dry gravel. If you use mercury, you must know that it is highly toxic. It can damage your brain and kidneys. Never use mercury near water sources, and never burn it in the open. Many countries have banned mercury. Use a retort if you must, but better to avoid it entirely. Gravity methods are safer.

Good practice also means respecting the environment. Do not cut down trees unnecessarily. Fill your pits after mining to prevent accidents and erosion. Do not dump waste into rivers. Use settling ponds for muddy water. In some African countries, you need a permit to mine. Check with local authorities. Also, work with your community. Form a group and share knowledge. This reduces conflict and improves safety.

Finally, take care of your health. Drink clean water, eat well, and rest. Mining is hard work. Stretch before you start. If you feel dizzy or short of breath, stop and get fresh air. Keep a first aid kit with bandages, antiseptic, and painkillers. Know where the nearest clinic is. By following these practices, you protect yourself and your future.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it is time to put your knowledge into practice. This exercise will guide you through finding and panning gold in a local stream. You will need: a pan, a shovel, a bucket, a sieve, and a notebook. Choose a stream where gold has been found before, or ask local miners for a good spot. Always get permission from the landowner or local authorities.

Step 1: Walk along the stream and look for signs of gold. Check the inside of bends, behind large rocks, and where the stream slows down. Look for black sand, which often accompanies gold. Also, look for quartz pebbles. If you see these, test that spot.

Step 2: Dig a test hole about 50 cm deep in the stream bank or bed. Take a bucket of gravel from the bottom. If you hit bedrock, scrape the cracks and collect that material. Pour the gravel into your sieve to remove big rocks. Then put the fine material into your pan.

Step 3: Pan the material. Fill the pan about half full with gravel. Add water and swirl gently to mix. Then tilt the pan slightly and let the lighter material wash out. Use your fingers to break up clay. Keep swirling until only black sand and maybe gold remain. Look carefully for goldit is bright yellow and heavy. Even tiny specks are worth keeping.

Step 4: If you find gold, mark the spot with a stick or GPS. Dig a larger pit and process more gravel. If you find no gold, move 10 meters and try again. Record your findings in your notebook: location, depth, amount of gold. This helps you see patterns.

Step 5: Clean up. Fill your test holes to prevent accidents. Take all your equipment and trash with you. Store your gold in a small vial. If you found nothing, do not be discouraged. Gold mining takes patience. Try different spots and different depths. With practice, you will learn to read the land. Remember, safety first: wear gloves and boots, and never work alone. Good luck!

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Artisan Gold Mining Where Gold Comes From

This practical reference is the working companion to the six lessons in Where Gold Comes From. It is written for the small-scale operator who treats mining as a business: someone who needs to know where gold actually occurs, how to read ground, what equipment pays for itself, and how to stay alive and legal while doing it. Across Africa, artisanal and small-scale gold mining (ASGM) directly supports an estimated 1020 million people, with millions more dependent on the income it generates. In Ghana alone, ASGM is thought to employ over one million people and account for a large share of national gold output. If you are running a claim, a licensed buying operation, or a small processing centre, the geology and practice below are your competitive edge.

Gold is not randomly scattered. It is concentrated by specific geological processes, and understanding those processes lets you choose ground with a real chance of payback rather than chasing rumours. This module gives you the reference tables, a schematic of how a placer deposit forms, safety rules, and a checklist you can carry to site.

How Gold Deposits Form The Two Families You Will Work

Nearly all economically worked gold in Africa falls into two broad categories:

  • Primary (lode or reef) deposits: Gold is still locked in the host rock where it was originally deposited by hydrothermal fluids. Examples include the Birimian greenstone belts of Ghana, Burkina Faso, Mali and Cte d'Ivoire, the greenstone belts of Tanzania and Zimbabwe, and the quartz veins of the Congo Craton. You mine hard rock, crush it, and recover fine gold usually with gravity and/or chemical methods.
  • Secondary (placer or alluvial) deposits: Gold has been eroded from a primary source, transported by rivers, and concentrated in gravels, sands and soils. Examples include the alluvial workings along the Pra, Ankobra and Offin rivers in Ghana, the Niger and Senegal river basins, and the extensive alluvial fields of eastern Cameroon and the DRC. Placer gold is usually coarser, easier to liberate, and cheaper to process which is why alluvial ground is often the entry point for new operators.

Knowing which family you are working determines your tools, your recovery method, your capital needs and your licensing pathway. A lode operation needs crushers, mills and often a cyanidation or gravity circuit; a placer operation may need only a sluice, a pump and a wash plant.

Reading the Ground: Where to Look First

Before you spend money on a pump or a licence, walk the ground. Gold is heavy (specific gravity ~19.3), so it behaves predictably:

  • Inside bends of rivers current slows, heavy particles drop out.
  • Behind large boulders and bedrock ledges natural traps.
  • At the base of gravel bars gold sinks to the bottom of the bar.
  • In old river channels (palaeochannels) often buried under later sediments.
  • Downslope of a quartz reef or gossan eroded gold accumulates in soils and streams.
  • In laterite and saprolite over greenstone belts weathered primary mineralisation.

In the Birimian belts of West Africa, the most productive alluvial ground is often within a few kilometres of a known lode or reef. If you can trace gold float (coarse gold in soil) uphill to a quartz outcrop, you may have both a placer and a lode target on the same licence a strong business case.

Step by Step Method From Sampling to Sale

The following sequence is the practical workflow for a small placer operation. Lode operations follow the same logic but add crushing and milling before concentration.

  1. Desk study and licensing. Confirm the ground is covered by a valid small-scale mining licence and, where required, an environmental permit. In Ghana, the Minerals Commission issues these; in Tanzania, the Mining Commission. Never work unlicensed ground it destroys your ability to sell legally and exposes you to arrest.
  2. Reconnaissance and panning. Take pan samples every 50100 m along the target stream or gravel bar. Record GPS coordinates, depth and gold colour (fine vs coarse). Coarse gold means you are close to source.
  3. Test pits and bulk samples. Dig test pits to bedrock. Sample the bottom 2030 cm of gravel that is where gold concentrates. A 50100 kg bulk sample through a sluice gives a realistic grade estimate.
  4. Grade calculation. Weigh recovered gold accurately (0.01 g scale). Calculate grade in grams per cubic metre (g/m) or grams per tonne (g/t). Compare against your cost per cubic metre to decide if the ground is payable.
  5. Site setup. Establish a stable water supply, a wash pad, and a secure storage area. Build settling ponds to prevent silt discharge into rivers this is both a legal requirement and good neighbour practice.
  6. Extraction and washing. Strip overburden, stockpile gravel, feed the sluice at a controlled rate. Do not overload the sluice gold will wash through. Classify material before sluicing where possible.
  7. Concentration and cleanup. Run concentrates across a pan or shaking table. Clean up the sluice regularly (every 24 hours in rich ground).
  8. Smelting and sale. Smelt to dor bars or sell concentrate to a licensed buyer. Keep records of every sale: date, weight, price, buyer. This is your business intelligence and your legal protection.

SAFETY WARNING MERCURY AND UNSTABLE GROUND

Mercury is still used in many African ASGM operations to amalgamate fine gold. It is highly toxic: inhalation of vapour causes permanent brain and kidney damage, and it contaminates rivers and soils for generations. Do not use mercury without proper training, closed retorts, and full personal protective equipment. Where possible, use gravity concentration (shaking tables, centrifuges) instead. If you must use mercury, work outdoors, never heat amalgam in an open pan, and use a retort to capture vapour. Mercury use is restricted or banned in several African countries check your national regulations before proceeding.

Also: never enter an unsupported pit or tunnel. Alluvial gravels and weathered rock collapse without warning. Bench pit walls, support tunnels, and never work alone underground.

Common Mistakes and Fixes

  • Mistake: Sampling only the surface. Fix: Gold sinks sample to bedrock, especially the bottom 2030 cm.
  • Mistake: Overloading the sluice. Fix: Feed at a controlled rate; classify material first.
  • Mistake: Using mercury as a first resort. Fix: Maximise gravity recovery first; mercury should be a last, controlled step.
  • Mistake: Ignoring licensing and environmental rules. Fix: Get the licence and permit before you dig. It protects your market access.
  • Mistake: No record-keeping. Fix: Log every sample, every cleanup, every sale. Data is your business.
  • Mistake: Working unstable ground. Fix: Bench, support, and never work alone.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Valid small-scale mining licence and environmental permit in hand before any digging.
  2. Ground geologically assessed: lode or placer identified, gold float traced, GPS points recorded.
  3. Bulk samples taken to bedrock and grade calculated in g/m or g/t.
  4. Sluice or gravity circuit set up and calibrated; feed rate controlled.
  5. Mercury avoided or used only with retort and full PPE; national rules checked.
  6. Pit walls benched, tunnels supported, no lone working underground.
  7. Water managed: settling ponds in place, no silt discharge to rivers.
  8. Every sale logged: date, weight, price, licensed buyer records kept for at least 3 years.
