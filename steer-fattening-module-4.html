Module 4

Feeds and Rations for Fattening

This module teaches you to think about feed the way a nutritionist does: in dry matter, in energy and protein, and in cost per unit of nutrient rather than cost per bag. You will learn the roughages and concentrates available across the region, how to judge them, how to read a feed label, and how to balance a workable finishing ration by hand. It closes with the safe use of urea and a clear list of what must never enter a cattle ration.

What you will be able to do after this module

  • You will be able to convert any feed between as fed weight and dry matter and explain why it matters.
  • You will be able to estimate dry matter intake for a pen from average body weight.
  • You will be able to judge roughage quality by look, feel, smell and shake, and set a safe minimum roughage level.
  • You will be able to compare protein feeds on cost per kg of crude protein using your own quoted prices.
  • You will be able to read a commercial feed label and identify NPN, mineral balance and withdrawal periods.
  • You will be able to balance a simple two feed ration with the Pearson square and convert it into daily scoops.

Lesson 4.114 min

Energy and Protein: What a Fattening Steer Actually Needs

In this lesson
  • Explain why energy, not protein, drives finishing
  • Read a ration in terms of dry matter
  • Estimate dry matter intake for a given steer

Every ration decision you make in this course comes back to three questions: how much dry feed will this steer eat, how much energy is in that feed, and is there enough protein for the rumen bugs to work. Get those three right and the rest is detail.

Dry matter is the language of rationing

Feeds carry water. Fresh green forage may be 70 to 80 percent water. Maize silage is commonly around 30 to 35 percent dry matter. Air dry feeds such as stover, hay, grain and bran usually sit near 85 to 90 percent dry matter. If you compare a bag of silage with a bag of maize grain as fed, you are comparing mostly water with mostly feed. So we always convert to dry matter (DM) first.

The conversion is simple arithmetic:

  • Dry matter fed = as fed weight x DM percent.
  • As fed weight needed = dry matter wanted divided by DM percent.

Example: you want 4 kg DM of silage at 32 percent DM. As fed = 4 / 0.32 = 12.5 kg of silage in the bunk. Feed 4 kg of silage as fed and the animal has eaten only about 1.3 kg DM. This single mistake is behind a large share of disappointing feedlot results.

How much will he eat?

Dry matter intake in finishing cattle commonly runs around 2 to 3 percent of body weight per day. Younger, lighter, higher roughage cattle sit at the top of that band; heavy, near finished cattle on high concentrate rations sit at the bottom. A 300 kg steer at 2.5 percent eats about 7.5 kg DM per day. A 400 kg steer at 2.3 percent eats about 9.2 kg DM. Use the band as a planning figure, then correct it with what the bunk actually tells you (Module 5, Lesson 6).

Heat cuts intake. In hot, humid weather cattle eat less during the day and more at night. Plan your feeding times around that rather than fighting it.

Energy is the driver

Fattening is the deposition of fat and muscle, and that takes energy above what the animal needs for maintenance. Maintenance comes first: the steer pays for body temperature, walking, breathing and repair before a single gram goes onto his back. This is why a thin ration produces almost no gain while a slightly richer one produces good gain. The extra energy above maintenance is what you are selling.

Energy in ruminant feeds is usually described as metabolisable energy (ME) in megajoules per kg DM, or as total digestible nutrients (TDN) as a percent. Rough working values: mature crop residues such as maize stover sit low, good grass hay is moderate, maize silage is moderate to good, and cereal grains are high. Your ration builds up from a roughage base and adds concentrate energy on top. Feed conversion on a well built finishing ration commonly falls in the range of roughly 6 to 10 kg DM per kg of gain, and average daily gain commonly runs 1.0 to 1.5 kg per day. Poorer conversion than that usually means the energy density is too low, the cattle are sick, or feed is being wasted.

Protein feeds the rumen first

The microbes in the rumen need nitrogen to grow. Those microbes then digest fibre and are themselves digested downstream, so most of the protein reaching a steer is microbial protein the animal built himself. That is why crude protein requirements for growing and finishing cattle are moderate, and why pouring in expensive cake beyond the requirement mostly produces expensive urine. Younger, lighter cattle need a higher protein percentage than heavy finishing cattle because they are laying down more muscle relative to fat.

Decision rule

Before you buy any feed, write down three columns for it: DM percent, energy class (low, medium, high), and crude protein percent. If you cannot fill those three columns, you cannot ration with it. Get the figures from the supplier label, a published local feed table, or a laboratory analysis.

Lesson 4.215 min

Roughages: Stover, Hay, Silage and Sugarcane Tops

In this lesson
  • Rank the roughages available to you by feeding value
  • Judge roughage quality by eye, hand and nose
  • Set a minimum roughage level that protects the rumen

Roughage is not filler. It is the part of the ration that keeps the rumen mat floating, keeps the animal chewing the cud, and keeps saliva flowing. Saliva is the animal's own buffer against acid. Roughage must never drop to zero in a fattening ration, no matter how cheap grain becomes.

Effective fibre versus fibre on paper

A laboratory can tell you a feed is high in fibre, but finely ground fibre does not do the job. What matters is effective fibre: particle length long enough to be chewed and to form a mat. As a practical test, a good share of your roughage particles should be roughly the length of your thumb, or long enough that a handful springs back when you squeeze it. Milling hay to dust converts a roughage into a slow energy source and removes its protection.

The common regional roughages

  • Maize and sorghum stover. Abundant after harvest, cheap, and low in both protein and energy. Crude protein is typically very low, so stover on its own will not maintain a steer, let alone fatten one. It works well as the fibre base under a concentrate, especially if chopped to 3 to 5 cm and offered with a protein source. Leaf and husk are far better than stalk; cattle sort for leaf and leave stalk, which you will see in the bunk.
  • Grass hay. Quality swings enormously with cutting stage. Hay cut before seed head emergence is leafy, green, soft and much higher in protein and digestibility than hay cut after seeding, which is stemmy, pale and coarse. Buy hay by looking at leaf to stem ratio, colour and smell, not by bale size.
  • Legume hay such as lucerne or forage groundnut. Higher protein than grass hay and useful for cutting the amount of oilseed cake you must buy. More expensive per bale but often cheaper per unit of protein. Do the sum from Lesson 5 before deciding.
  • Maize or sorghum silage. The strongest roughage option for a feedlot because it carries grain and fibre together, and it is moderate to good in energy. It must be well made: chopped fine enough to pack, compacted hard, sealed airtight, and left to ferment. Good silage smells sharply sour and slightly sweet, is olive to yellow brown, and is not slimy. Silage that smells of rotting meat, ammonia or manure, or is black and slimy, has spoiled and should not be fed.
  • Sugarcane tops. Common in cane growing districts and cheap at harvest. Palatable, high in sugars, low in protein, and they must be chopped. They spoil quickly in heat, so feed within a day or two of cutting.

Judging any roughage in the yard

  1. Look. Green to olive is good. Bleached, grey or black is poor or spoiled.
  2. Feel. Soft and leafy is good. Woody, sharp stalks mean low digestibility.
  3. Smell. Sweet and grassy, or sharply sour for silage, is good. Mould smell, mustiness or ammonia is a rejection.
  4. Shake. A cloud of dust or visible white mould means reject it. Mouldy feed causes abortions, gut upsets and mycotoxin losses.
  5. Break open a bale. Sellers put the good side out. Always open a bale from the middle of the stack before paying.

Decision rules on roughage level

Start new arrivals on roughage as the large majority of the diet and step concentrate up gradually over about 14 to 21 days, as covered in Module 5. In the finishing phase, keep a meaningful roughage share in the ration at all times so that cattle are visibly chewing the cud. A practical yard check: stand quietly at the pen at midday and count how many resting animals are ruminating. If far fewer than half of the resting animals are chewing, your effective fibre is too low or your cattle are off feed. Investigate before you push concentrate any higher.

Lesson 4.315 min

Concentrates: Grain, Bran, Oilseed Cake, Molasses and Brewers Grains

In this lesson
  • Classify each concentrate as an energy or protein feed
  • Set safe upper limits for the risky ingredients
  • Choose concentrates on cost per unit of nutrient

Concentrates are the feeds that carry the energy and protein density a fattening ration needs. Split them in your head into two piles: energy feeds and protein feeds. Buying without knowing which pile a feed sits in is how people end up paying protein prices for energy.

Energy concentrates

  • Maize grain. The regional standard. High in starch, high in energy, low in protein and low in calcium. Feed it cracked or coarsely rolled, not finely ground: fine grinding speeds fermentation and raises acidosis risk. Whole maize is also fed successfully in some systems but is less consistent when the roughage level is high.
  • Sorghum grain. Similar energy to maize but harder, so processing matters more. Some varieties carry tannins which cut digestibility and palatability.
  • Maize bran and wheat bran. Cheap milling by-products, moderate energy, moderate protein, bulky and high in phosphorus. Useful as a diluent to slow a hot ration, but bran alone will not push high gains. Bran goes rancid and mouldy quickly in heat and humidity, so buy fresh and store dry.
  • Hominy chop and maize germ meal. Milling fractions that vary a great deal between mills. Ask for the label and expect variation between deliveries.
  • Molasses. Fast fermenting sugar, very palatable, useful as a carrier for urea and minerals and to reduce dust and sorting. It is an energy feed with almost no protein. Because it ferments fast, keep it a modest part of the ration rather than a main energy source, and never introduce a large amount suddenly.

Protein concentrates

  • Cottonseed cake or meal. Widely available and a strong protein source. The caution is gossypol, a natural compound in cotton seed. Mature ruminants tolerate it far better than young calves or monogastrics, but it is still a reason to keep cottonseed to a moderate share of the ration and to avoid feeding it to very young stock. Ask whether the product is expeller or solvent extracted, since that changes both oil and gossypol.
  • Sunflower cake. Good protein, higher fibre than cottonseed, palatable, and often the cheapest protein per unit in sunflower growing districts. Expeller cake retains oil, which adds energy but also shortens shelf life.
  • Soyabean meal. The highest quality of the common plant proteins, and usually the most expensive. Raw soyabeans must be heat treated before feeding; untreated raw beans carry anti nutritional factors.
  • Brewers grains. The spent grain from beer making. Good protein and moderate energy, very palatable, and often available cheaply near a brewery. The problem is water: wet brewers grains are commonly only around 20 to 30 percent dry matter, so a large as fed weight is a small dry matter contribution. They also spoil within days in heat. Feed wet grains fresh, or ensile or sun dry them if you must hold stock. Never feed a load that smells putrid or has grown visible fungal mats.

Choosing on cost, not on habit

Compare feeds on cost per kg of dry matter and, for protein feeds, cost per kg of crude protein. The method is:

  1. Phone three local suppliers and record the delivered price per bag or per tonne, including transport.
  2. Convert to price per kg as fed.
  3. Divide by the DM percent to get price per kg DM.
  4. For protein feeds, divide the price per kg DM by the crude protein percent to get price per kg of protein.

Worked example with clearly fake placeholder prices. Suppose cake A costs P 300 per 50 kg bag, is 90 percent DM and 36 percent crude protein: that is P 6.00 per kg as fed, P 6.67 per kg DM, and P 18.5 per kg protein. Cake B at P 240 per 50 kg, 90 percent DM and 26 percent protein is P 4.80 per kg, P 5.33 per kg DM, and P 20.5 per kg protein. The cheaper bag is the dearer protein. Do this arithmetic with your own quoted prices before every purchase.

Lesson 4.413 min

Minerals, Salt, Water and How to Read a Feed Label

In this lesson
  • Explain why calcium and phosphorus balance matters on a grain ration
  • Plan water supply for hot weather and dry feed
  • Read a commercial feed label and challenge the seller

Minerals are a small part of the ration by weight and a large part of whether it works. Water is not usually called a feed at all, and it is the one input that will destroy your results fastest if it fails.

Calcium and phosphorus

Cereal grains are low in calcium and reasonably high in phosphorus. A ration heavy in maize and bran therefore drifts to a low calcium, high phosphorus balance. In male cattle this imbalance is a recognised risk factor for urinary stones, which block the urethra and cause a painful, often fatal, blocked bladder. The standard preventives are to keep calcium clearly above phosphorus in the total ration, to include salt to encourage water intake, and in some systems to use a urine acidifier under veterinary advice. Feed grade limestone is the usual cheap calcium source. If you are feeding a lot of bran, assume you need added calcium.

Warn every worker: a steer straining to urinate, dribbling, kicking at his belly or standing with an arched back is an emergency. Call a vet the same day.

Salt and trace minerals

Common salt drives water intake and carries trace elements when bought as a trace mineralised block or premix. Regional soils vary widely in trace element status, and the deficiencies that matter for cattle differ by district. Rather than guess, ask your district veterinary or extension office which trace elements are known to be deficient in your area and buy a premix that matches. Offer a mineral block in the pen as insurance, but do not rely on block licking to correct a real deficiency because intake per animal is uncontrolled.

Water

Water requirement rises sharply with heat and with dry feed. Cattle eating dry stover and grain in hot weather drink far more than the same cattle grazing green pasture in cool weather. Practical rules:

  • Water must be available at all times. A fattening steer that cannot drink stops eating within hours, and gain stops with it.
  • Trough space and refill rate matter as much as tank volume. Cattle drink in bunches after feeding. If the trough empties and refills slowly, the timid animals lose out.
  • Clean troughs at least weekly. Algae, manure and dead birds reduce intake.
  • Site troughs in shade where you can, and check them twice daily in hot weather.
  • Test borehole water for salinity if cattle drink poorly or scour without another explanation.

Reading a feed label

Commercial concentrates and premixes carry a label. Learn to read it rather than trusting the bag picture.

  1. Product class and species. Is it a beef finisher, a dairy meal, a calf starter, a poultry feed? Never feed a poultry or pig ration to cattle; some contain ionophores or copper levels unsuitable for other species, and some contain animal protein.
  2. Crude protein. Usually a minimum percentage. Note whether it includes non protein nitrogen.
  3. NPN or urea declaration. If the label declares equivalent protein from NPN, part of the protein figure is urea, not true protein. This matters for young cattle and for unadapted cattle.
  4. Fibre and fat. Usually a maximum for fibre and a minimum for fat.
  5. Calcium, phosphorus and salt. Check calcium is above phosphorus for a grain based finisher.
  6. Additives. Any ionophore, antibiotic or growth promoter must be declared with its withdrawal period. Write that withdrawal period in your diary against your intended slaughter date.
  7. Feeding directions and inclusion rate. A concentrate meant to be fed at a set rate alongside roughage is dangerous if fed free choice.
  8. Batch number and manufacture date. Reject stock with no date, or old stock, especially vitamin containing premixes.

If a seller cannot show you a label, treat the price as irrelevant and walk away. Unlabelled mixed feed is the commonest way small fatteners buy sand, sweepings and mouldy screenings.

Lesson 4.516 min

Balancing a Simple Ration by Hand

In this lesson
  • Set an intake target from body weight
  • Use the Pearson square to hit a protein target
  • Convert a dry matter ration into scoops and bags

You do not need software to build a workable fattening ration. You need the dry matter figures from Lesson 1, the protein figures from your labels, a pencil, and a discipline about checking your answer.

Step 1: set the intake target

Take the average body weight of the pen. Say 320 kg. Dry matter intake in finishing cattle commonly runs around 2 to 3 percent of body weight. Take 2.5 percent as a starting planning figure: 320 x 0.025 = 8 kg DM per head per day. Write that number at the top of the page. Everything below must add up to it.

Step 2: set the protein target

Lighter, younger cattle need a higher crude protein percentage in the diet than heavy finishing cattle. Take your target from a published requirement table for the weight and target gain you are feeding, or from your feed company's recommendation for that class. Write it down as a single number, for example a percentage of the total dry matter.

Step 3: fix the roughage first

Decide the roughage share that keeps the rumen safe, and hold it. Suppose you decide roughage will be 35 percent of the ration dry matter. That is 8 x 0.35 = 2.8 kg DM of roughage, and 5.2 kg DM of concentrate mix.

Step 4: use the Pearson square for the concentrate

The Pearson square blends two feeds to hit a protein target. Suppose your roughage and energy base contributes an average of 8 percent crude protein, your cake is 36 percent crude protein, and you need the concentrate mix to average 14 percent.

  1. Draw a square. Put the target, 14, in the middle.
  2. Put the base, 8, at the top left, and the cake, 36, at the bottom left.
  3. Subtract diagonally, ignoring the sign. Top left to middle: 36 minus 14 = 22 parts of base. Bottom left to middle: 14 minus 8 = 6 parts of cake.
  4. Total parts = 22 + 6 = 28. Base share = 22 / 28 = 78.6 percent. Cake share = 6 / 28 = 21.4 percent.

So of the 5.2 kg DM of concentrate, about 4.1 kg DM is base and about 1.1 kg DM is cake. Always sanity check by multiplying back: (4.1 x 8) + (1.1 x 36) = 32.8 + 39.6 = 72.4, divided by 5.2 = 13.9 percent. Close enough to the 14 percent target.

Step 5: add minerals and salt

Include your limestone, salt and trace mineral premix at the inclusion rate printed on the premix label. These are small weights but they must be weighed, not guessed, and they must be mixed evenly.

Step 6: convert dry matter back to as fed

This is where rations fail. Divide each dry matter figure by that feed's DM percent.

If you swap the stover for wet brewers grains at 25 percent DM, that same 2.8 kg DM becomes 11.2 kg as fed. Fail to make that conversion and you will underfeed by a wide margin while believing the ration is correct.

Step 7: turn it into a scoop

Workers cannot weigh every ingredient every day. Fill your scoop or bucket with each ingredient, weigh it on a hanging scale, and write the weight on the side of the scoop in paint. Re-weigh monthly and whenever a delivery changes, because bulk density changes with moisture and grind.

Checks before you feed it

  • Do the dry matter figures add to your intake target?
  • Is the roughage share above your safe minimum?
  • Does the protein back-calculation match the target?
  • Is calcium above phosphorus?
  • Is every change from last week's ration a gradual one?

Recalculate the ration every time cattle gain roughly 40 to 50 kg, because intake and requirements both move with weight.

Lesson 4.614 min

Urea, NPN and What Never to Feed

In this lesson
  • State the conditions under which urea can be used safely
  • Recognise urea poisoning and act
  • List the feeds that must never enter a cattle ration

Urea is non protein nitrogen. Rumen microbes can convert it into microbial protein, which makes it a cheap substitute for part of the true protein in a ration. It is also the single most dangerous thing a smallholder can put in a feed mixer. This lesson is about earning the benefit without the funeral.

How urea kills

Urea is broken down in the rumen to ammonia. If ammonia is released faster than the microbes can use it, it is absorbed into the blood and poisons the animal. Death can follow within an hour or two of eating a bad mouthful. Three things cause that overload: too much urea at once, uneven mixing so one animal gets a hot pocket, and unadapted cattle whose rumen microbe population has not built up the capacity to use it.

Rules for safe use

  1. Never exceed a small share of the ration. Urea should only ever be a small fraction of total ration dry matter and a limited share of total dietary nitrogen. Use the inclusion rate on the product label or your nutritionist's figure, and never round it up.
  2. Never feed urea to unadapted cattle. Introduce it gradually over the adaptation period alongside the concentrate step-up in Module 5.
  3. Never feed urea without readily available energy. Microbes need starch or sugar to build protein from ammonia. Urea in a straw only diet is both useless and more dangerous.
  4. Mix it properly or do not use it. Dry urea is a hard crystal that does not spread evenly by shovel. Dissolve it in water or molasses and spray it on, or use a properly compounded commercial urea containing concentrate or lick block. Hand mixing dry urea into a heap with a spade is how cattle die.
  5. Never let cattle eat urea fertiliser or lick a spill. Store urea locked away from the feed store and away from animals. Fertiliser grade urea has killed many cattle that broke into a shed.
  6. Never dissolve urea in drinking water. An animal can drink a lethal dose in minutes.
  7. Do not feed urea to young calves whose rumen is not yet fully functional.

Recognising urea poisoning

Signs come on fast: restlessness and twitching around the muzzle and ears, drooling, grinding teeth, bloating, staggering, laboured breathing, then collapse and convulsions. This is a veterinary emergency and survival depends on speed. Call your vet immediately and describe what was fed. The classic field measure while waiting is drenching with a large volume of cold water and a weak acid such as vinegar to slow ammonia release, but this must be done under veterinary instruction and carries its own risk of drenching pneumonia if done badly. Remove all remaining feed from the bunk and stop the whole pen eating it.

What never to feed cattle

  • Mammalian meat and bone meal, or any ruminant derived protein. Banned in most countries because of BSE risk. Do not use, whatever the price.
  • Poultry, pig or dog feed. May contain ionophores at rates unsafe for cattle, high copper, or animal proteins. Species specific feeds are formulated for that species only.
  • Mouldy feed of any kind. Mycotoxins cut intake and gain, damage the liver, and cause abortion. Mould that you can see or smell is a rejection, not a discount.
  • Untreated raw soyabeans. Must be heat treated first.
  • Wilted or frosted sorghum, sudan grass and johnson grass regrowth. Can carry dangerous prussic acid. Do not graze or cut young or stressed regrowth.
  • Large sudden loads of sugar or grain such as spilt molasses, bakery waste or a burst grain bag. This is the classic route to fatal acidosis.
  • Poisonous plants and garden clippings, including oleander, and any plant material you cannot identify.
  • Potato, avocado and other kitchen wastes unless you have confirmed advice on that specific material.
  • Feeds still inside a drug withdrawal period before slaughter.

The safest position for a small fattener is simple. If you are not confident you can mix urea evenly every single day, buy a commercially compounded concentrate that already contains it, and let the mill carry the mixing risk.

Knowledge check

Five questions. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. You want to feed 4 kg of dry matter as maize silage that is 32 percent dry matter. How much silage as fed do you put in the bunk?

As fed = dry matter divided by DM percent, so 4 / 0.32 = 12.5 kg. Feeding 4 kg as fed would supply only about 1.3 kg of dry matter.

2. A 400 kg steer on a finishing ration will most likely eat about how much dry matter per day?

Dry matter intake in finishing cattle commonly runs around 2 to 3 percent of body weight, which for a 400 kg animal is roughly 8 to 12 kg per day.

3. Cake A costs the equivalent of P 6.67 per kg dry matter at 36 percent crude protein. Cake B costs P 5.33 per kg dry matter at 26 percent crude protein. Which is the cheaper protein?

Divide price per kg DM by protein percent. Cake A is about P 18.5 per kg protein, Cake B about P 20.5. The cheaper bag is the dearer protein. Prices here are placeholders only.

4. Which practice makes urea use most dangerous?

Uneven mixing creates hot pockets. One animal can eat a lethal dose while the rest of the pen eats none. Dissolve and spray, or buy a compounded product.

5. Why must roughage never drop to zero in a finishing ration?

Effective fibre keeps cattle ruminating and salivating. Saliva is the animal's own buffer. Without it, acidosis risk rises sharply.
