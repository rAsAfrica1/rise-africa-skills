1. Total Mixed Ration (TMR) Basics

A Total Mixed Ration (TMR) is a feeding system where all feed ingredientsroughages, grains, protein supplements, minerals, and vitaminsare weighed and mixed together into a single feed. This prevents steers from sorting and ensures every bite delivers a balanced diet. On small African farms, you can mix TMR by hand on a concrete floor using a shovel, or use a small mixer wagon if available. For a 300 kg steer gaining 1 kg per day, a typical TMR might include 40% maize silage, 30% chopped hay, 20% maize meal, 8% cottonseed cake, and 2% mineral premix. Weigh ingredients using a hanging scale or bathroom scale. Mix in layers: first roughages, then grains, then supplements. Mix for at least 5 minutes until uniform. Feed twice daily at 8 AM and 4 PM. Provide fresh water always. TMR reduces feed waste by 10-15% compared to separate feeding. Store mixed feed in a cool, dry place and use within 24 hours to prevent mould. In Kenya, farmers using TMR with locally available maize stover and molasses have seen daily gains increase from 0.6 kg to 1.1 kg. Start with a simple two-ingredient mix if you are new, then add more ingredients as you gain confidence. Always introduce TMR gradually over 7-10 days to avoid digestive upset. A well-mixed TMR is the foundation of efficient steer fattening.

2. Phase Feeding and Step-Up Programs

Phase feeding means changing the ration as the steer grows and its nutritional needs change. A typical fattening period is divided into three phases: starter (first 30 days), grower (next 60 days), and finisher (last 30-60 days). In the starter phase, the steer is adapting to a high-grain diet. Feed 50% roughage and 50% concentrate. For example, 5 kg of maize silage plus 5 kg of a mix of maize meal and cottonseed cake. In the grower phase, increase concentrate to 65% and reduce roughage to 35%. A 350 kg steer might get 4 kg silage and 7 kg concentrate. In the finisher phase, push concentrate to 75-80% for maximum weight gain. Use 3 kg silage and 10 kg concentrate. Step-up programs gradually increase grain over 2-3 weeks to prevent acidosis. Start with 20% grain in the diet, then add 10% every 3 days until you reach the target. For example, day 1-3: 20% maize meal; day 4-6: 30%; day 7-9: 40%; and so on. Always provide long-stem hay or straw to stimulate chewing. Monitor manure: loose manure means too much grain; firm manure means the ration is balanced. Weigh steers every 30 days to adjust feed amounts. In South Africa, phase feeding with local maize and soybean meal improved feed conversion by 15%. Adjust phases based on body condition, not just calendar days. If a steer is thin, extend the grower phase. If it is too fat, move to finisher earlier. Phase feeding saves money by matching nutrients to needs.

3. Feed Bunk Management and Delivery

Feed bunk management is about how you deliver feed to steers to maximise intake and minimise waste. First, ensure the bunk is clean and empty before each feeding. Remove old, spoiled feed. A clean bunk prevents mould and disease. Feed at the same times every daysteers are creatures of habit. Twice daily feeding is best: early morning and late afternoon. Deliver only what the steers will clean up in 20-30 minutes. If feed remains after 30 minutes, you are overfeeding; if they clean up in 10 minutes and look for more, you are underfeeding. A good rule: feed 2-3% of body weight per day on a dry matter basis. For a 300 kg steer, that is 6-9 kg of dry matter. Adjust based on leftovers. Keep a record of feed offered and refused. Aim for 2-5% refusal (orts) to ensure all steers get enough. Use bunk dividers to give each steer 60-75 cm of space. Overcrowding reduces intake for shy feeders. In hot weather, feed more in the evening when it is cooler. In cold weather, increase energy by 10%. Water is critical: a steer drinks 30-50 litres per day. Clean water troughs daily. In Ethiopia, farmers who fed at consistent times and cleaned bunks daily reduced feed waste by 20%. Use a feed cart or wheelbarrow to deliver feed evenly along the bunk. Do not let feed sit in the sun. If using TMR, mix just before feeding. Never feed mouldy or musty feed. Observe steers during feeding: sluggish eating may mean illness or poor feed quality. Adjust delivery based on weather, health, and growth stage.

4. Ration Balancing Using Software

Ration balancing software helps you mix feeds to meet nutrient requirements precisely. You do not need a computer; many apps work on smartphones. Popular tools include Feedsoft, RationPro, and the free BR-CORTE for beef cattle. First, gather feed composition data. For local feeds, use a feed composition table from your extension officer or the FAO database. For example, maize meal has 9% crude protein, 80% TDN; cottonseed cake has 40% protein, 70% TDN. Next, input the steer's weight, target daily gain, and feed ingredients. The software calculates the cheapest mix that meets protein, energy, mineral, and vitamin needs. For a 250 kg steer gaining 1 kg/day, you need 12% crude protein and 65% TDN. A simple mix: 2 kg maize meal, 1 kg cottonseed cake, 5 kg maize silage, and 50 g mineral premix. Software will adjust quantities. If you lack software, use the Pearson Square method for two ingredients. For example, to make a 14% protein mix from maize meal (9% protein) and cottonseed cake (40% protein): draw a square, put 14 in the centre, 9 on top left, 40 on bottom left. Subtract diagonally: 40-14=26 parts maize, 14-9=5 parts cottonseed. So 26 kg maize and 5 kg cottonseed make 31 kg of 14% mix. Always check calcium and phosphorus: a 2:1 ratio is ideal. Add limestone or bone meal if needed. In Nigeria, farmers using a free app reduced feed costs by 18% by substituting expensive ingredients. Update rations when feed prices change or when steers reach a new weight. Software is a guideobserve your steers and adjust.

5. Adapting Rations to Feed Costs and Availability

Feed costs are 60-70% of steer fattening expenses. Adapting rations to local availability and price is key to profit. Start by listing all feedstuffs available in your area and their prices per kg. Compare cost per unit of energy (TDN) and protein. For example, if maize is expensive, replace part with cassava peels, sweet potato vines, or brewers' grains. In Uganda, farmers use banana peels and molasses to cut costs. Use the cheapest energy source as the base. Then add the cheapest protein source: cottonseed cake, sunflower cake, or fish meal. If soybean meal is too costly, use urea-treated straw for protein. Urea treatment: dissolve 4 kg urea in 100 litres water, sprinkle on 100 kg chopped straw, cover for 21 days. This raises protein from 4% to 10%. By-products like rice bran, wheat bran, and molasses are often cheap. Molasses can replace up to 10% of the ration but causes loose manure if overfed. Always check for toxins: cottonseed cake has gossypol, limit to 20% of concentrate. Adapt to seasons: in dry season, use more crop residues; in wet season, use fresh forage. Buy in bulk when prices are low. Store properly to avoid spoilage. Formulate rations that meet nutrient needs at lowest cost. A simple rule: if a feed costs more per kg than maize meal, use it only for specific nutrients. In Tanzania, farmers who substituted 30% of maize with cassava chips reduced feed cost by 25% without affecting gain. Keep records of feed prices and adjust monthly. Join a farmer group to buy feed collectively and negotiate lower prices. Never sacrifice nutrient balance for costpoor rations lead to slow growth and disease.

6. Common Feeding Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

Mistake 1: Sudden diet changes. Steers need 7-10 days to adapt to new feed. Always transition gradually by mixing old and new feed. Mistake 2: Overfeeding grain. Too much grain causes acidosis, bloating, and death. Limit grain to 1% of body weight per feeding. Provide long hay. Mistake 3: Not providing minerals. A lack of salt, calcium, or phosphorus reduces growth. Offer a free-choice mineral block or add 50 g of premix per steer per day. Mistake 4: Dirty water. Steers drink 30-50 litres daily. Clean troughs daily; dirty water spreads disease. Mistake 5: Feeding mouldy feed. Mould causes liver damage and poor gain. Store feed in dry, ventilated place. Mistake 6: Inconsistent feeding times. Steers thrive on routine. Feed at the same times daily. Mistake 7: Not weighing feed. Guessing leads to over- or underfeeding. Use a scale. Mistake 8: Ignoring body condition. Adjust feed based on condition score, not just weight. Mistake 9: Overstocking. Each steer needs 60-75 cm of bunk space and 20-30 square metres of pen space. Mistake 10: Not keeping records. Record feed intake, weight gain, and costs. This helps you spot problems early. In Zimbabwe, farmers who corrected these mistakes increased daily gain from 0.5 kg to 1.2 kg. To avoid mistakes, make a daily checklist: clean water, correct ration, clean bunk, observe steers. Train workers on proper feeding. If you see loose manure, reduce grain and add hay. If steers are restless, check for empty bunk or spoiled feed. Always consult an extension officer when unsure. Good feeding is about consistency and attention to detail.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Feeding Systems and Ration Formulation for Steer Fattening in Africa

Feed is the single largest cost in a steer fattening enterprisetypically 6075% of total operating expenses. For African feedlot operators, the difference between profit and loss often comes down to how well rations are formulated, how consistently they are delivered, and how quickly the system adapts to fluctuating feed prices and availability. This practical reference consolidates the essential knowledge from the seven lessons in this module into a working guide you can use at the feed bunk, in the office, and when negotiating with feed suppliers.

1. Total Mixed Ration (TMR) Basics

A Total Mixed Ration is a blend of all feed ingredientsforages, grains, protein supplements, minerals, and additivesmixed together to a consistent, homogenous feed. TMR prevents selective eating, reduces sorting, and ensures every animal receives the intended balance of nutrients in every bite. In African contexts where ingredient quality varies widely, TMR is particularly valuable because it buffers against variability in individual ingredients.

Key principles for TMR success:

  • Particle size matters: Aim for 1020% of particles above 19 mm to maintain rumen function, with the majority in the 419 mm range.
  • Moisture content: Target 3550% dry matter. Too dry causes sorting; too wet causes spoilage and reduced intake.
  • Mixing sequence: Add forages first, then grains, then protein/mineral premixes. Mix for 46 minutes after the last ingredient is added.
  • Consistency: Weigh ingredients accurately. A 5% error in grain inclusion can shift the energy-to-protein ratio enough to affect daily gain.

2. Phase Feeding and Step-Up Programs

Steers entering the feedlot must be adapted gradually to high-concentrate diets to avoid acidosis and other digestive disorders. Phase feeding divides the feeding period into distinct stages, each with a specific ration formulation. A typical 120-day fattening cycle in Africa uses three phases:

Step-up protocol: Within the starter phase, increase concentrate by 510% every 23 days. Monitor manure consistency dailyloose manure indicates too-rapid adaptation. If acidosis signs appear (reduced intake, diarrhea, lethargy), drop back one step and hold for 34 days before advancing again.

3. Feed Bunk Management and Delivery

Consistent delivery and bunk management directly influence intake, gain, and feed efficiency. In African feedlots where labour and equipment may be limited, simple protocols yield significant returns.

  • Feed at the same times daily: Twice-daily feeding (morning and late afternoon) is ideal. Cattle are creatures of habit; irregular feeding reduces intake by 515%.
  • Target 25% refusals (orts): A clean bunk at the next feeding means you underfed; more than 5% refusals means you overfed. Adjust the next batch by 510% accordingly.
  • Bunk reading: Score bunks 04 (0 = empty, 4 = full of old feed). Aim for score 12 at feeding time. This simple practice can improve feed efficiency by 812%.
  • Water availability: Steers consume 3050 litres of water daily. Ensure clean water is available at all timeswater restriction reduces intake immediately.
  • Feed push-up: Push feed back within reach 34 times daily between feedings to encourage intake.

4. Ration Balancing Using Software

Manual ration balancing (Pearson Square, simultaneous equations) works for simple two- or three-ingredient mixes but becomes impractical with multiple ingredients and nutrient constraints. Several free or low-cost software tools are available:

  • FeedSoft / RationPro: Free basic versions available; widely used in East and Southern Africa.
  • BR-CORTE (Brazilian): Free Excel-based tool for beef cattle; adaptable to African conditions with local feed composition data.
  • CNCPS (Cornell Net Carbohydrate and Protein System): More advanced; requires training but available through universities.
  • Spreadsheet-based tools: ILRI and national research institutions often provide Excel templates with local feed databases.

When using software, always verify the feed composition values against local laboratory analyses or published tables (e.g., FAO feed tables for tropical feeds). Software is only as good as the data you input.

5. Adapting Rations to Feed Costs and Availability

Feed prices in Africa fluctuate seasonally and are affected by drought, import restrictions, and currency volatility. A profitable feeding system must be flexible. Key strategies:

  • Least-cost formulation: Use software to identify the cheapest combination of ingredients that meets nutrient requirements. Small changes in ingredient inclusion can reduce costs by 1020%.
  • By-product utilisation: Agro-industrial by-products (maize bran, molasses, cottonseed cake, brewers' grains, citrus pulp) are often 3050% cheaper than conventional feeds. They require careful inclusion rate management.
  • On-farm feed mixing: Mixing your own TMR using locally sourced ingredients typically reduces feed cost by 1530% compared to purchasing complete feeds.
  • Strategic supplementation: When forage quality is low (dry season), supplement with protein and energy concentrates rather than feeding more low-quality forage.
  • Contract growing: Negotiate forward contracts with feed suppliers to lock in prices during harvest season when grains are cheapest.

SAFETY WARNING: Acidosis and Feed Toxicity

Sudden increases in concentrate level, excessive fine particles, or mouldy feed can cause acute rumen acidosis, which can kill steers within 2448 hours. Never increase concentrate by more than 10% per step, and always ensure adequate long fibre (10% particles >19 mm). Never feed mouldy maize, cottonseed, or silagemycotoxins cause liver damage, reduced fertility, and death. If you observe rapid breathing, staggering, or cattle going down after feeding, contact a veterinarian immediately and remove the suspect feed. Always provide free-choice clean water and a source of buffering (e.g., sodium bicarbonate at 0.51% of DM) during high-concentrate feeding.

6. Common Feeding Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

  1. Inconsistent mixing: Poorly mixed TMR leads to sorting and nutrient imbalances. Solution: Calibrate your mixer wagon monthly; mix for the full recommended time.
  2. Overfeeding concentrates too quickly: The most common cause of acidosis. Solution: Follow a strict step-up schedule; never skip adaptation steps.
  3. Ignoring feed quality variation: A batch of maize bran with 8% CP instead of 10% changes the entire ration. Solution: Sample and analyse ingredients regularly; adjust formulations accordingly.
  4. Inaccurate weighing: Guessing ingredient amounts leads to inconsistent rations. Solution: Use calibrated scales; weigh every ingredient, every batch.
  5. Poor bunk management: Letting bunks go empty or allowing feed to accumulate. Solution: Feed at fixed times; read bunks daily; adjust amounts based on intake.
  6. Neglecting water: Water is the most important nutrient. Solution: Clean troughs daily; ensure adequate flow rate and trough space (at least 75 cm per steer).
  7. Feeding mouldy or spoiled feed: Mycotoxins reduce performance and can cause death. Solution: Inspect all feed before use; store feed properly (dry, ventilated, off the ground).
  8. Not adjusting for weather: Heat stress reduces intake by 1030%. Solution: Increase nutrient density during hot periods; feed during cooler hours.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Weigh all ingredients accurately use calibrated scales, not estimates.
  • Mix TMR for 46 minutes after adding the last ingredient; check particle size monthly.
  • Follow the step-up schedule never increase concentrate by more than 10% per step.
  • Read bunks daily target 25% refusals; adjust next batch accordingly.
  • Feed at the same times every day twice daily is ideal.
  • Ensure clean water is always available check troughs and flow rates daily.
  • Sample and analyse feed ingredients at least every 3 months or when suppliers change.
  • Monitor manure consistency and cattle behaviour early signs of acidosis require immediate action.
