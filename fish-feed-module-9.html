Lesson 9.1~11 min

Choosing Between Earthen Ponds, Tanks and Recirculating Systems

In this lesson
  • Compare earthen pond, static tank, raceway and recirculating systems by stocking density, feed waste and expected FCR
  • Match a production system to your available water supply, land and capital
  • Estimate the annual fish output your chosen system can carry before water quality becomes the limit

Your production system decides almost everything else on the farm: how many fish you can hold, how much of your feed reaches a fish mouth, and how hard you must work to keep water safe. Choose it against your water supply and your capital, not against what a neighbour is doing.

There are four systems a small or medium African farm realistically chooses between.

  1. Extensive earthen pond. Stocking 1-5 kg/ha, fed mostly by natural pond productivity that you encourage with manure or fertiliser. Feed waste runs 15-25 percent and FCR is poor at 2.5-3.5. Cheap to start, low output.
  2. Semi-intensive earthen pond. Stocking 5-50 kg/ha, hand-fed two to three times a day to satiation. Feed waste 10-15 percent, FCR 1.8-2.2. This is where most smallholders sit and where most improvement is available.
  3. Intensive earthen pond. Stocking 50-150 kg/ha, fed three to four times daily, aeration required, water quality monitored daily. Feed waste falls to 5-10 percent and FCR improves to 1.5-1.8.
  4. Tanks, raceways and recirculating systems (RAS). Static tanks carry 100-200 kg/m3, raceways 50-150 kg/m3, RAS 200-500 kg/m3 or more. Feed waste drops to 2-8 percent and FCR to 1.2-1.6, but you now depend on pumps, power and a biofilter that dies if you overfeed.

Notice the pattern. Every step up buys you better feed conversion and more fish per unit of land, and every step charges you for it in equipment, electricity and management attention. Feed is 50-70 percent of production cost, so FCR improvements are real money, but only if the system does not fail.

Worked example. A farmer near Kisumu, Kenya has a 0.2 ha earthen pond (2,000 m2) fed from a stream, with no electricity at the pond. He plans to stock 2,000 Nile tilapia fingerlings and sell them at 300 g. His standing biomass at harvest would be 2,000 x 0.3 kg = 600 kg, which on 0.2 ha is 3,000 kg/ha. Compare that with the reference bands: semi-intensive ponds are described at 5-50 kg/ha and intensive ponds at 50-150 kg/ha. His plan is far above both. Many African farmers do carry loads well above these published bands, but only by adding aeration, frequent water exchange and daily monitoring. Without power at the pond he has three honest options: stock fewer fish, harvest at a smaller size, or accept that he is running an intensive system on extensive infrastructure and will pay for it in mortality. Cutting to 600 fingerlings gives 180 kg standing biomass, which is 900 kg/ha, still demanding but manageable with good feeding discipline and a stream inflow he can open. The lesson is that stocking is decided backwards from harvest biomass and water supply, not forwards from the price of fingerlings.

Second worked example, on cost. Assume tilapia grow-out feed at USD 0.70/kg (a mill-gate to retail price typical of sub-Saharan Africa, where pellet is USD 0.35-0.55/kg at mill gate and USD 0.60-0.80/kg retail). To grow 180 kg of fish at FCR 2.0 you need 360 kg of feed, costing 360 x 0.70 = USD 252. The same 180 kg at FCR 1.6 needs 288 kg, costing USD 201.60. You saved USD 50.40 on one small pond cycle purely by managing water and feeding better. Repeat that across two cycles a year and four ponds and it is USD 403 a year, which is close to the cost of a small pellet mill.

Before you commit, answer three questions honestly. Can I supply and exchange water in the dry season? Do I have reliable power if the design needs it? Can I be at the pond at dawn, every day, when oxygen is lowest? If any answer is no, choose the simpler system and get its FCR right first.

Stocking Density: Matching Fish Load to Your Pond's Carrying Capacity

In this lesson
  • Calculate standing biomass in kg/ha or kg/m3 from fingerling number and target harvest weight
  • Identify the growth and FCR penalty that follows overstocking
  • Set a stocking number that your water exchange and aeration can actually support

Overstocking is the most common and most expensive mistake in small-scale aquaculture. Fingerlings are cheap and hope is free, so farmers put in more fish than the pond can carry, then blame the feed when growth stalls.

Start with the arithmetic. Standing biomass is what matters, not fish number:

Standing biomass (kg) = number of fish x survival rate x target harvest weight (kg)

Worked example, Nigeria. A farmer in Ibadan has a 1,000 m2 (0.1 ha) earthen pond with a small 0.75 kW aerator he runs at night. He stocks 1,500 African catfish fingerlings and expects 85 percent survival to a 400 g market fish.

1,500 x 0.85 = 1,275 fish at harvest 1,275 x 0.4 kg = 510 kg standing biomass 510 kg / 0.1 ha = 5,100 kg/ha

That is many times the 50-150 kg/ha band that defines an intensive pond. African catfish is exceptionally tolerant, being an air-breather that survives down to about 1.0 mg/L dissolved oxygen, so farmers do push these numbers with heavy exchange. But a farmer running that load with one small aerator and no daily water exchange is running an experiment, not a farm. Either the stocking comes down, or the water exchange and aeration go up, or the fish are harvested earlier at a smaller size.

The cost of ignoring this is measurable. High density in tilapia, above roughly 100 fish per cubic metre, worsens FCR by 10-15 percent through stress and competition at the feeding point. Add the water quality consequences and it compounds: ammonia above 0.1 mg/L costs another 5-10 percent of FCR, and dissolved oxygen below 4 mg/L costs 15-30 percent.

Stack those. A pond that is overstocked, ammonia-loaded and oxygen-short can run 30-50 percent worse FCR than the same fish, the same feed and the same farmer at a sensible density. If your baseline FCR is 1.8 and you push it to 2.5 through overstocking, then on 500 kg of fish you have bought 1,250 kg of feed instead of 900 kg. At USD 0.70/kg that is USD 875 instead of USD 630, or USD 245 wasted, and you still harvested the same fish.

Practical rules to set your number:

  1. Decide the harvest weight the market pays best for, then work backwards.
  2. Apply a realistic survival rate. Use your own last cycle's figure. If you have never counted, assume 80-85 percent for catfish and tilapia in a managed pond and correct it next cycle.
  3. Check standing biomass against your system band: 5-50 kg/ha semi-intensive without aeration, 50-150 kg/ha intensive with aeration and 25-40 percent water exchange two to three times a week.
  4. If the number exceeds your band, reduce fish or increase exchange and aeration. Do not simply hope.
  5. Grade and split. Tilapia and catfish both spread widely in size. Moving the largest 20 percent to a second pond reduces competition and lets you feed two correct pellet sizes instead of one wrong one.

One more consideration for tilapia specifically: uncontrolled breeding in mixed-sex ponds silently raises density. Hundreds of unplanned fry eat feed, compete for oxygen and never reach market size. Mono-sex male fingerlings, or stocking a small number of predatory African catfish alongside tilapia, are the two field-practical answers.

Do this today: count or estimate the fish in one pond, multiply by your expected survival and your target harvest weight, divide by pond area in hectares, and write the kg/ha figure on a card nailed to the pond bank. If it is above 150 kg/ha and you have no aerator, plan a partial harvest.

Dissolved Oxygen: The Dawn Crash That Kills Fish

In this lesson
  • State the critical and optimal dissolved oxygen levels for tilapia, African catfish, channel catfish and carp
  • Explain why pond oxygen falls to its minimum just before sunrise
  • Adjust feeding times and aeration to avoid the daily oxygen low

Fish do not usually die at midday. They die between 04:00 and 07:00, when the pond has spent the whole night respiring and no sunlight has yet restarted photosynthesis. If you only ever check your pond after breakfast, you have never seen your real oxygen minimum.

Know your thresholds. These are the levels below which growth stops or fish die:

  • Nile tilapia, grow-out: critical 2.0 mg/L, optimal above 5 mg/L
  • Nile tilapia, fry: critical 1.5 mg/L, optimal above 6 mg/L
  • Channel catfish, grow-out: critical 3.0 mg/L, optimal above 5 mg/L
  • Channel catfish, fry: critical 2.5 mg/L, optimal above 6 mg/L
  • African catfish: critical about 1.0 mg/L thanks to air-breathing, but preferred above 4 mg/L
  • Common carp: critical 2.5 mg/L, optimal above 5 mg/L

"Critical" is where survival is threatened, not where the fish is comfortable. Long before death, oxygen shortage costs you money: below 5 mg/L, tilapia reduce feed intake and FCR worsens by 15-30 percent.

Feeding drives oxygen demand. Fish fed to satiation consume 30-40 percent more oxygen while digesting, and high-protein feeds above 40 percent crude protein can raise post-feeding oxygen demand by 50-100 percent. So the meal you give at 18:00 is still burning oxygen at 02:00, exactly when the pond has the least to give.

The practical rule for low-aeration systems, meaning less than about 1 kW of aeration per 100 m3 of pond, is to reduce feeding by 20-30 percent at dawn and after sunset, and put the bulk of the ration into the mid-morning and early afternoon feeds when photosynthesis is adding oxygen.

Worked example, Uganda. A farmer at Wakiso feeds 1.75 kg per day to a tilapia pond holding 50 kg of fish, at 3.5 percent of body weight, split into five meals of 0.35 kg each at 06:00, 10:00, 14:00, 17:00 and 18:00. Two of those five meals sit in the danger window. Rebalancing to 0.25 kg at 06:00, 0.45 kg at 10:00, 0.45 kg at 14:00, 0.35 kg at 17:00 and 0.25 kg at 18:00 keeps the same 1.75 kg total but moves 0.20 kg of feed out of the low-oxygen periods. No cost, no equipment, measurable reduction in night-time oxygen demand.

What raises oxygen, in order of cost:

  1. Free: feed earlier in the day, avoid overfeeding, remove uneaten feed and dead vegetation, keep stocking sane.
  2. Cheap: splash water on entry, position the inlet so it falls rather than flows, stir the pond surface with a paddle at dawn during hot spells.
  3. Moderate: a paddlewheel or aspirator aerator run overnight. A small 0.75-1 kW unit is the usual first purchase on an intensifying farm.
  4. Structural: reduce standing biomass, increase water exchange, deepen the pond so the water column holds more oxygen and heats less.

Watch for the warning signs before you own a meter: fish gathered at the inlet or gulping at the surface at dawn, sudden stopping of feeding, a heavy green bloom that has turned brown or grey overnight (a crashed algal bloom is a classic oxygen killer), and a strong smell from the pond bottom. If you see fish piping at the surface at first light, stop feeding that morning entirely and get water moving.

Write down whether fish are at the surface, whether the water colour changed overnight, and what you fed the previous evening. That one observation tells you more than a week of afternoon checks.

Ammonia and Nitrite: Managing the Nitrogen Your Feed Creates

In this lesson
  • Trace the path from feed protein to ammonia, nitrite and nitrate in your water
  • Apply the safe ammonia and nitrite thresholds for your species
  • Use salt, water exchange and protein adjustment to reduce nitrogen load

Every bag of feed you throw in the pond is also a bag of nitrogen. Fish retain only 28-34 percent of the protein they eat as body tissue. The other 66-72 percent leaves as ammonia across the gills or as faecal nitrogen that bacteria break down into ammonia in the mud. High-protein feeding and high stocking are, in nitrogen terms, the same problem.

How much? A tilapia pond holding 100 kg of fish produces roughly 50-80 g of nitrogen a day. Channel catfish and carp at grow-out protein levels produce 40-60 g per 100 kg per day. That nitrogen has to go somewhere: into algae, into a biofilter, or out with exchanged water.

Safe thresholds for un-ionised ammonia (NH3, the toxic form):

  • Nile tilapia: below 0.05 mg/L
  • African catfish: below 0.05 mg/L
  • Channel catfish: below 0.1 mg/L
  • Common carp: below 0.1 mg/L

What the numbers do to fish: at 0.02 mg/L you get subtle immune depression and 2-5 percent slower growth. At 0.05 mg/L, gill irritation and 10-15 percent growth reduction. At 0.1 mg/L, severe effects, 25-30 percent growth loss in Nile tilapia, reduced feed intake and fin erosion. Above 0.2 mg/L is acutely toxic within days.

Nitrite is the next step. Bacteria convert ammonia to nitrite in about 2-7 days, then nitrite to harmless nitrate over another 3-7 days. During that gap nitrite accumulates and blocks oxygen transport in the blood. Lethal levels (LC50 at 96 hours) are 7.1 mg/L for channel catfish, 8-10 mg/L for tilapia and 6-8 mg/L for carp, but you should operate far below: under 1 mg/L for channel catfish and under 0.5 mg/L for tilapia and carp.

The cheapest nitrite insurance in the world is salt. Freshwater is naturally low in chloride, and chloride competes with nitrite at the gill. Adding sodium chloride at 1-2 g per litre cuts nitrite toxicity by about 50 percent with no harm to the fish.

Worked example, Ghana. A farmer in the Volta Region runs a 200 m3 concrete tank system holding 400 kg of tilapia. Nitrite has climbed to 0.9 mg/L after he increased feeding. He needs 1 g/L of salt: 200 m3 = 200,000 litres, so 200,000 x 1 g = 200,000 g = 200 kg of salt. At a local coarse salt price of about USD 0.15/kg (check your own supplier; this is an assumed local price), that is USD 30 to buy back a wide safety margin. He should still fix the underlying cause.

Five ways to cut nitrogen load, roughly in order of cost:

  1. Stop overfeeding. Uneaten pellets decompose straight into ammonia. Feed to observed appetite, not to the scoop.
  2. Reduce dietary protein where the fish stage allows. Every 5 percent cut in dietary protein produces 15-20 percent less ammonia. Tilapia grow-out needs only 25-32 percent crude protein, not the 40 percent some farmers buy out of habit.
  3. Improve protein quality rather than quantity. A feed with balanced amino acids wastes less protein to deamination, which is where the ammonia comes from.
  4. Raise digestibility. Above 80 percent ingredient digestibility means less faecal nitrogen and less sediment ammonia.
  5. Exchange water and aerate. Aeration speeds nitrification; exchange simply removes the nitrogen. Semi-intensive ponds do 10-20 percent weekly, intensive ponds 25-40 percent two to three times a week.

A biofilter or an established green-water pond needs 2-3 weeks for nitrifying bacteria to build up. Never stock a new tank at full density and full feeding on day one.

Feeding Rules When Water Quality Turns Bad

In this lesson
  • Set feeding rates by species, temperature and stocking density
  • Apply specific ration reductions when oxygen, ammonia or temperature move out of range
  • Split a daily ration into meals that fit the pond's daily oxygen cycle

Feeding is not a fixed routine. It is a decision you make each morning based on what the water is doing. A farmer who feeds the same amount every day regardless of conditions will waste feed on bad days and underfeed on good ones.

Start with the baseline rate, as a percentage of standing body weight per day:

  • Tilapia at 27-30 C: 2-3 percent at low density, 3-4 percent at medium, 4-5 percent at high density
  • Tilapia at 20-25 C: 1-2 percent low, 2-3 percent medium, 2-3.5 percent high
  • Channel catfish at 25-28 C: 1.5-2.5 percent low, 2.5-3.5 percent medium, 3-4 percent high
  • Channel catfish at 15-20 C: 1-1.5 percent low, 1.5-2 percent medium, 2-2.5 percent high
  • Carp at 20-25 C: 1.5-2.5 percent low, 2.5-3.5 percent medium, 3-4 percent high

Note that the high-density figures look generous but come with a warning: at high density you should reduce the satiation level by about 20 percent because crowding stress reduces appetite and increases waste.

Worked example, Tanzania. A farmer at Morogoro has 1,000 tilapia averaging 50 g, so 50 kg standing biomass, at 30 C in a medium-density pond. Target 3.5 percent of body weight: 50 x 0.035 = 1.75 kg of feed per day. Split into five meals that is 0.35 kg each. At a feed price of USD 0.70/kg, that pond consumes USD 1.23 of feed a day, or USD 36.75 in a 30-day month.

Now apply the adjustment rules. These are the ones that keep money in your pocket:

  1. If ammonia is above 0.1 mg/L or dissolved oxygen is below 5 mg/L, reduce the ration by 20-30 percent.
  2. If water temperature drops below 20 C, reduce by 50 percent. Tilapia FCR collapses to 3.0-5.0 below 20 C and channel catfish to 3.5-6.0 below 20 C; feed given in cold water is largely wasted.
  3. If fish show disease signs, reduce by 30-50 percent. Sick fish do not digest well and uneaten feed makes the water worse.
  4. In low-aeration systems, cut the dawn and post-sunset meals by 20-30 percent.

Apply rule 1 to the Morogoro pond: at DO 4.2 mg/L one morning, the farmer feeds 1.75 x 0.75 = 1.31 kg instead of 1.75 kg. He saves 0.44 kg of feed worth USD 0.31 that day and, more importantly, avoids adding oxygen demand and ammonia to a pond that is already struggling.

Observe, do not just measure. Broadcast a small handful and watch. Vigorous, immediate feeding means you can give the full ration. Slow, half-hearted feeding means stop at that point and record it. Feed that is still floating after 15-20 minutes is money on its way to becoming ammonia.

Pellet choice matters here too. Floating pellets waste 15-25 percent of feed but let you see exactly when the fish stop eating, which is worth a great deal to a farmer learning their pond. Sinking pellets waste only 5-10 percent but you are feeding blind. For tilapia in ponds, many farmers use floating pellets while they learn their ration, then move to a mix.

Finally, keep a feeding record. Date, weight fed, water temperature, dawn observation, and any ammonia or DO reading. After one cycle you will have a curve that tells you what your pond does in each season, which is worth more than any general advice.

Lesson 9.6~11 min

Building a Low-Cost Water Quality Monitoring Routine

In this lesson
  • Assemble a minimum viable water testing kit and know what each test costs
  • Set a weekly monitoring schedule matched to your stocking intensity
  • Interpret readings against species thresholds and act on them the same day

You cannot manage what you do not measure, but you also cannot afford a laboratory. The goal is a routine that costs little, takes fifteen minutes a day, and catches the three things that actually kill fish: low oxygen, high ammonia and high nitrite.

The minimum kit. Prices below are indicative and you should confirm them locally; the reference data does not fix retail kit prices, so treat these as approximate planning figures rather than quotes.

  1. A thermometer. Cheap, and temperature drives everything: feeding rate, oxygen solubility and the fraction of total ammonia that is in the toxic un-ionised form.
  2. A pH test, strips or a simple liquid kit. pH determines how much of your total ammonia is the toxic NH3 form. The same total ammonia reading is far more dangerous at pH 9 than at pH 7.
  3. An ammonia test kit, liquid reagent type. This is the one test that most directly reflects your feeding decisions.
  4. A nitrite test kit, especially important for tank and recirculating systems in their first two months.
  5. A Secchi disc, which you can make yourself from a painted tin lid and a marked string. It reads algal density: too clear means low natural productivity, too dark green means a bloom that may crash and take your oxygen with it.

A dissolved oxygen meter is the most useful and most expensive item. If you cannot buy one, substitute discipline: a dawn walk every day, watching for surface piping, is a free oxygen test that catches most emergencies.

Schedule by intensity:

  • Semi-intensive pond, 5-50 kg/ha: ammonia weekly, pH weekly, temperature daily, dawn observation daily, water exchange 10-20 percent weekly.
  • Intensive pond, 50-150 kg/ha: oxygen daily, ammonia twice weekly, nitrite weekly, water exchange 25-40 percent two to three times a week.
  • Tanks and RAS: oxygen and ammonia daily during the first two months, nitrite daily until the biofilter is established, then settle into a daily oxygen and twice-weekly chemistry routine.

Worked example, Zambia. A farmer near Kafue runs three 800 m2 ponds at intensive density. She buys an ammonia kit and a nitrite kit at an assumed USD 25 each and makes her own Secchi disc for nothing, so about USD 50 outlay. Her ponds carry roughly 300 kg of fish each. If a monitoring routine prevents just one partial mortality event costing 15 percent of one pond, that is 45 kg of fish. At an assumed farm-gate price of USD 2.00/kg (verify your own market price) that is USD 90 saved, so the kit pays for itself the first time it works.

What to write down every day, in one exercise book, one line per pond:

Date | time | water temperature | dawn observation | feed given (kg) | any test result | action taken

That record is what turns a hunch into a decision. When growth stalls, you go back and look: was ammonia climbing? Did feeding rise just before it? Did the bloom crash?

One bloom warning worth memorising. Over-fertilising a pond pushes nitrogen and phosphorus out of balance and encourages cyanobacteria, which can produce microcystin and anatoxin. Keep pond fertilisation at an N:P ratio of roughly 3:1 to 5:1, aerate, and if you see a heavy scum-forming bloom, isolate the pond, drain and clean before restocking rather than trying to feed through it.

Finally, cross-check your monitoring against your fish. A pond with good numbers but fish that are dark, listless, thin or with eroded fins is telling you something the test kit missed. Trust both.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. Which system typically gives the best feed conversion ratio?

RAS typically runs FCR 1.2-1.5 with only 2-5 percent feed waste, because feeding is controlled and feed is not lost to a large water body.

2. Typical feed waste in an extensive earthen pond is about:

Extensive ponds broadcast feed over a large area with little control, so 15-25 percent is lost, and FCR sits at 2.5-3.5.

3. Feed accounts for roughly what share of aquaculture production cost?

Feed is 50-70 percent of production cost, which is why FCR and feed waste dominate farm profitability.

4. A farmer with no electricity and an unreliable dry-season water supply should most sensibly run:

Raceways and RAS depend on continuous water flow and power. Without both, a semi-intensive pond managed well is the lower-risk choice.

5. Growing 180 kg of fish at FCR 2.0 instead of 1.6, with feed at USD 0.70/kg, costs you roughly how much extra?

360 kg minus 288 kg is 72 kg of extra feed; 72 x 0.70 = USD 50.40.

6. Standing biomass is best calculated as:

Standing biomass is the weight of live fish the water must carry, so it depends on how many survive and how big they get.

7. Which species tolerates the lowest dissolved oxygen because it can breathe air?

African catfish has an air-breathing adaptation and survives at around 1.0 mg/L, though it still prefers above 4 mg/L for good growth.

8. Combined overstocking, high ammonia and low oxygen can worsen FCR by roughly:

The penalties stack: 10-15 percent from density, 5-10 percent from ammonia and 15-30 percent from low oxygen.

9. An unaerated semi-intensive pond should generally stay below:

Semi-intensive ponds run 5-50 kg/ha; going above that band requires aeration and regular water exchange.

10. Uncontrolled tilapia breeding in a grow-out pond is a problem mainly because:

Recruits consume feed and oxygen while adding no marketable weight, quietly pushing the pond past its carrying capacity.

11. Pond dissolved oxygen is normally at its lowest:

All night the pond respires without photosynthesis, so oxygen bottoms out just before first light.

12. Below what dissolved oxygen does tilapia FCR worsen by 15-30 percent?

Optimal for tilapia grow-out is above 5 mg/L; below that, feed intake falls and FCR deteriorates 15-30 percent.

13. Feeding fish to satiation increases their oxygen consumption by about:

Digestion and protein catabolism raise oxygen demand 30-40 percent, and more still with very high-protein feeds.

14. In a pond with little aeration, the safest change to the feeding schedule is:

Mid-day feeding coincides with photosynthetic oxygen production, and keeps digestion demand away from the pre-dawn low.

15. Fish gulping at the surface at first light most likely indicates:

Surface piping is the classic sign of oxygen shortage; stop feeding and get water moving immediately.

16. Roughly what proportion of dietary protein do fish retain as body tissue?

Only 28-34 percent is retained; the rest is excreted as ammonia or faecal nitrogen.

17. The safe un-ionised ammonia level for Nile tilapia is:

Tilapia and African catfish need NH3 below 0.05 mg/L; channel catfish and carp tolerate up to 0.1 mg/L.

18. Adding 1-2 g of salt per litre of pond water mainly:

Chloride from the salt competes with nitrite at the gill, cutting toxicity by around 50 percent.

19. Cutting dietary protein by 5 percentage points reduces ammonia output by about:

Less surplus protein means less deamination, giving a 15-20 percent reduction in ammonia output.

20. A brand new tank system should not be stocked at full density immediately because:

Without an established nitrifying population, ammonia and then nitrite spike dangerously.

21. A pond holds 50 kg of tilapia at 30 C, medium density. At 3.5 percent of body weight the daily ration is:

50 kg x 0.035 = 1.75 kg per day, usually split across four or five meals.

22. If water temperature drops below 20 C, you should:

Below 20 C tilapia FCR worsens to 3.0-5.0, so most of the feed is wasted; halve the ration.

23. Dissolved oxygen at 4.2 mg/L in a tilapia pond means you should:

Below 5 mg/L, cut the ration 20-30 percent to reduce oxygen demand and ammonia loading.

24. The main advantage of floating pellets for a farmer learning their pond is:

Visible feeding response lets you set the ration accurately, although floating feed wastes 15-25 percent.

25. Feed still floating 15-20 minutes after broadcasting is best understood as:

Uneaten feed decomposes into ammonia, so it costs you twice: once in feed and once in water quality.

26. Why does pH matter when interpreting an ammonia reading?

The same total ammonia is far more toxic at high pH because a greater share exists as un-ionised NH3.

27. A Secchi disc measures:

It reads how deep you can see, which reflects the algal bloom; very dark green warns of a bloom that may crash.

28. For an intensive pond above 50 kg/ha, recommended water exchange is:

Intensive stocking generates nitrogen faster than a pond can process, so frequent partial exchange is required.

29. The recommended nitrogen to phosphorus ratio for pond fertilisation is roughly:

Excess nitrogen relative to phosphorus promotes cyanobacteria, which can release microcystin and anatoxin.

30. A farmer with no dissolved oxygen meter should substitute:

Watching for surface piping at first light is a free check that catches most oxygen emergencies.

Module 9 capstone

Over four weeks, profile your own pond or tank as a production system. Measure and record surface area or volume, average depth, water source and how much water you can exchange in a day. Weigh a sample of at least 30 fish to estimate average weight, then estimate total standing biomass and express it as kg/ha (ponds) or kg/m3 (tanks). Take dissolved oxygen at dawn and mid-afternoon on at least six days, and ammonia and pH at least twice a week, writing every reading in a notebook with the date, time, water temperature and how much feed you gave that day. At the end, produce one page that states: your system type and carrying capacity, your measured DO minimum, your measured ammonia maximum, the two days water quality was worst and what you had fed the day before, and three specific changes you will make (a feeding-time change, a stocking or exchange change, and an aeration or fertilisation change). Cost each change in USD using real local prices you collect from a supplier.
