1. Pad Shapes and Sizes for Different Flows

Sanitary pads come in different shapes and sizes to handle light, medium, and heavy menstrual flows. For light flow days, use a panty liner or a thin pad that is 12 mm thick and 1518 cm long. For medium flow, a regular pad of 34 mm thickness and 2225 cm length works well. Heavy flow or overnight needs a maxi pad that is 58 mm thick and 2835 cm long, often with wider ends for coverage. In Africa, many women prefer longer pads because they provide more security during long days of work or travel.

To make your own pads, you can cut patterns from old newspapers or cardboard. A simple pattern is a rectangle with rounded ends. For a heavy flow pad, add extra width at the back (about 1012 cm wide) to prevent leaks when sitting or lying down. You can also add wings that fold around the panty these are especially useful for active women who walk long distances.

When choosing pad size, consider the absorbency needs. A pad that is too small will leak, while one that is too large may be uncomfortable. Test different sizes with a small amount of water (25 ml) to see how much they hold. A good rule: 1 ml of blood is roughly equal to 1 gram of weight. So if a pad gains 10 grams after use, it absorbed about 10 ml. Most women lose 3040 ml per period, but heavy bleeders can lose 80 ml or more.

In rural areas, you can use locally available materials like cotton fabric, flannel, or even soft old t-shirts for reusable pads. For disposable pads, you need absorbent core materials like wood pulp or SAP (super absorbent polymer). The shape should follow the curve of the underwear a slight hourglass shape helps the pad stay in place. Always test your prototype by wearing it for a few hours to see if it shifts or leaks. Adjust the length and width until you find what works for your body and flow.

2. Layers and Their Functions

A sanitary pad is made of several layers, each with a specific job. The top layer (coverstock) touches the skin. It must be soft, dry, and allow liquid to pass through quickly. Common materials are nonwoven polypropylene or cotton. For reusable pads, use soft cotton or bamboo fleece. The coverstock should be at least 1015 cm wide and run the full length of the pad.

The second layer is the acquisition/distribution layer (ADL). This layer spreads liquid evenly so the core can absorb it faster. It is often made of polyester or a blend. Without an ADL, liquid pools in one spot and leaks. A simple ADL can be a piece of thin polyester batting or even a layer of cotton gauze.

The core is the most important layer. It absorbs and holds the liquid. For disposable pads, the core is made of fluff pulp (wood pulp) mixed with super absorbent polymer (SAP). SAP can absorb up to 300 times its weight in water. For a heavy flow pad, use 23 grams of SAP and 58 grams of fluff pulp. For reusable pads, the core is multiple layers of cotton, flannel, or microfiber. You need 46 layers of cotton for medium flow. Test absorbency by pouring 10 ml of water on the core it should absorb within 10 seconds.

The back layer is the barrier. It stops liquid from soaking through to the underwear. For disposable pads, use a thin polyethylene film (about 0.02 mm thick). For reusable pads, use PUL (polyurethane laminate) or a layer of nylon. The barrier must cover the entire back of the pad, including wings. If you are making reusable pads, you can use a piece of waterproof fabric from an old raincoat or umbrella.

Finally, the adhesive or attachment system. Disposable pads use pressure-sensitive adhesive strips that stick to the panty. Reusable pads use snaps, Velcro, or ties. For wings, add adhesive or snaps to hold the pad in place. Always test the layers together: pour 10 ml of water on the top and check that the bottom stays dry. If the barrier leaks, add another layer or change the material.

3. Leakage Prevention: Barriers and Fit

Leakage is the number one complaint about sanitary pads. To prevent leaks, you need three things: a good barrier, proper fit, and side guards. The barrier is the waterproof back layer. For disposable pads, this is a thin plastic film. For reusable pads, use PUL or a double layer of nylon. The barrier must extend beyond the absorbent core by at least 1 cm on all sides. If the barrier is too small, liquid will wick around the edges.

Fit is critical. A pad that is too narrow will shift and leak. Measure your underwear: the crotch width is usually 68 cm. Your pad should be at least 2 cm wider than the crotch on each side. For heavy flow, add wings that fold around the panty these wings should be at least 3 cm long and have adhesive or snaps. If you have heavy flow at night, use a longer pad (3035 cm) that extends to the back. You can also add a second pad behind the first for extra protection.

Side guards (also called barriers or cuffs) are raised edges that stop liquid from running off the sides. You can make them by folding the edges of the top layer and stitching them to create a standing cuff. For disposable pads, some brands have elasticized cuffs. For reusable pads, you can sew a strip of elastic along the side edges. The cuffs should be 12 cm high. Test them by pouring 5 ml of water on the pad while it is on a panty if water runs off the sides, your cuffs are too low.

Another key is the adhesive or attachment. If the pad moves, it leaks. For disposable pads, use strong adhesive that sticks to cotton underwear. For reusable pads, use snaps that are tight but not uncomfortable. You can also add a strip of silicone grip or velcro to the back of the pad. In Africa, many women use safety pins or ties made from strips of cloth. Whatever method you choose, test it by walking, sitting, and squatting for 10 minutes. If the pad shifts, adjust the fit.

Finally, change your pad every 46 hours, or sooner if flow is heavy. Even the best pad will leak if it is saturated. For heavy flow, use a pad with a higher absorbency and change every 34 hours.

4. Comfort and Breathability Considerations

Comfort is essential for a woman to wear a pad all day. A comfortable pad is soft, thin, and breathable. Breathability means air can pass through, which reduces heat, moisture, and the risk of rash or infection. The top layer should be made of soft, skin-friendly material. For disposable pads, look for nonwoven polypropylene that is treated to be hydrophilic (water-loving). For reusable pads, use cotton, bamboo, or hemp these are naturally breathable and soft.

Thickness affects comfort. A pad that is too thick (over 8 mm) can feel bulky and cause chafing. For light flow, aim for 12 mm. For medium, 34 mm. For heavy, 57 mm. You can reduce thickness by using a thinner core with more SAP. For reusable pads, use fewer layers of cotton but add a layer of microfiber for absorbency. Test by wearing the pad for 30 minutes if you feel heat or sweat, it is not breathable enough.

The back barrier should also be breathable if possible. Traditional polyethylene film is not breathable, but you can use a breathable film (like those used in baby diapers) that allows air but not liquid. For reusable pads, PUL is breathable but waterproof. If you cannot find breathable materials, make the pad with a cotton back and change it more often to avoid moisture buildup.

Wings and edges can cause irritation. Make sure all edges are smooth and not scratchy. For reusable pads, sew with a soft thread and avoid rough seams. You can also add a layer of soft fleece on the wings. For disposable pads, the adhesive should not touch the skin place it only on the back of the wings.

Odor is also a comfort issue. A breathable pad allows air to circulate, which reduces odor-causing bacteria. Avoid perfumes or deodorants in the pad they can irritate sensitive skin. Instead, use natural materials like charcoal or baking soda in the core to absorb odors. For reusable pads, wash with mild soap and dry in direct sunlight to kill bacteria. Always test a new pad for comfort by wearing it for a full day. Adjust the materials until you find a combination that feels dry, soft, and cool.

5. Odor Control Technologies

Menstrual odor comes from bacteria breaking down blood and sweat. To control odor, you need to reduce moisture, inhibit bacterial growth, and neutralize smells. The simplest method is to change your pad frequently every 34 hours for heavy flow, every 6 hours for light flow. But good design can also help.

One technology is the use of super absorbent polymers (SAP) that lock liquid inside the core. When blood is absorbed, it is held away from the air, so bacteria have less to feed on. SAP also keeps the top layer dry, which reduces odor. For reusable pads, use a core of microfiber or bamboo charcoal. Bamboo charcoal is a natural deodorizer it absorbs odors and moisture. You can buy bamboo charcoal fabric or make your own by burning bamboo and mixing the ash with cotton. Add a thin layer (12 mm) of charcoal fabric between the core and the barrier.

Another method is to add an odor-neutralizing layer. For disposable pads, some brands use zeolites or baking soda in the core. You can make your own by sprinkling 1 teaspoon of baking soda between the absorbent layers. For reusable pads, you can sew a small pouch of baking soda or activated charcoal into the pad. Activated charcoal is very effective it can absorb many times its weight in odor molecules. You can make activated charcoal by heating charcoal (from wood or coconut shell) in a closed container at high heat, but it is easier to buy it.

Antibacterial agents can also help. Some pads use silver nanoparticles or chitosan (from shrimp shells) to kill bacteria. For a natural option, add a few drops of tea tree oil or lavender oil to the core these have antibacterial properties. But be careful: essential oils can irritate skin, so use only 12 drops and never directly on the top layer.

Finally, breathability is key. A breathable pad allows air to flow, which reduces the warm, moist environment that bacteria love. Use breathable materials like cotton or breathable film. Avoid plastic back sheets that trap moisture. For reusable pads, wash them with hot water and vinegar to kill bacteria and remove odors. Dry them in direct sunlight UV light is a natural disinfectant. Always test your odor control method by wearing the pad for 4 hours and checking for smell. If odor persists, add more charcoal or change the pad more often.

6. Innovations in Pad Design

Innovations in sanitary pad design are making pads more affordable, sustainable, and effective. One major innovation is the reusable pad made from locally available materials. In Africa, many women use cotton fabric, flannel, or old clothes to make pads that can be washed and reused for 12 years. These pads cost about $0.50 to $1.00 each to make, compared to $0.10$0.30 per disposable pad. Over a year, a woman can save over $50. Reusable pads also reduce waste a disposable pad takes 500800 years to decompose, while a reusable pad lasts 12 years.

Another innovation is the use of super absorbent polymers (SAP) in disposable pads. SAP can absorb 300 times its weight in water, so a thin pad can hold a lot of liquid. This makes pads thinner and more comfortable. However, SAP is not biodegradable. Some companies are now using biodegradable SAP made from corn starch or cassava. You can also make your own SAP from cassava starch it absorbs less (about 50 times its weight) but is cheap and local.

Biodegradable pads are a growing trend. These pads use bamboo, banana fiber, or water hyacinth for the absorbent core. Banana fiber is strong and absorbent it can hold 510 times its weight in water. Water hyacinth, an invasive plant in many African lakes, can be harvested and processed into pulp for pads. This not only makes pads but also helps clean the environment. A biodegradable pad can decompose in 612 months.

Smart pads are also being developed. Some have a sensor that detects when the pad is full and sends a signal to a phone app. This is useful for women with heavy flow or who cannot change frequently. However, these are expensive and not yet widely available in Africa. A simpler innovation is the pad with wings that have extra adhesive or snaps for better fit. Another is the pad with a built-in disposal bag you can wrap the used pad in the bag and throw it away without touching it.

Finally, there are innovations in production. Small-scale pad-making machines are now available for $500$2,000. These machines can produce 5001,000 pads per day. In Africa, women's groups are setting up small factories to make pads locally. This creates jobs and reduces the cost of pads. For example, in Kenya, a group called ZanaAfrica makes pads from local materials and sells them for $0.05 each. You can also make pads by hand with a sewing machine and simple tools. The key is to test your design with local women and get feedback. Innovation is not just about technology it is about solving real problems with what you have.

<h3>Leakage prevention: barriers and fit</h3>
<p>Leakage is the number one complaint in every market, and it is almost never about absorbency alone. It is about fit, barrier geometry, and how the pad behaves when the wearer sits, walks, or sleeps. Three mechanisms matter:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Side barriers and wings.</strong> Wings that wrap the gusset stop lateral movement. If wings are too narrow for the underwear sold in your market, they tear or fail to anchor.</li>
<li><strong>Rear extension.</strong> Overnight leakage usually happens at the back. A longer, wider rear core not just a longer pad is the fix.</li>
<li><strong>Compression and rewet.</strong> When a wearer sits, pressure squeezes fluid back out of a saturated core. A well-designed ADL and a SAP with good gel strength under load prevent this.</li>
</ul>

<p style="margin:0;">Do not use industrial-grade or recycled fluff pulp, unwashed textile offcuts, or non-medical adhesives in pads intended for skin contact. Contaminated or irritant materials can cause contact dermatitis, vulvovaginal irritation, and where sterility is claimed for post-partum or post-surgical pads serious infection risk. All skin-contact materials must be non-toxic, dermatologically acceptable, and free of loose fibres. Production areas must control dust, humidity, and pest access. Any pad marketed as sterile must be validated through a documented sterilisation process. Where a product makes a health claim, verify it against your national standards body (for example KEBS in Kenya, SON in Nigeria, SABS in South Africa) before sale.</p>
</div>

<h3>Comfort and breathability</h3>
<p>Comfort drives repeat purchase more than absorbency numbers. In hot, humid African climates, breathability is not a luxury it is a retention feature. A non-breathable polyethylene backsheet traps heat and moisture, which causes sweating, itching, and bacterial growth. Microporous breathable films allow vapour to escape while blocking liquid. The trade-off is cost: breathable film typically adds 1530% to backsheet cost, so many producers offer it only on premium SKUs.</p>
<p>Other comfort levers: softness of the topsheet (measured by hand-feel panels, not just spec sheets), pad thickness (thinner is not always better a very thin pad can feel "unsafe" to users), wing softness, and edge sealing that does not scratch. Test with real users in your market, not with imported assumptions.</p>

<h3>Odour control technologies</h3>
<p>Odour comes from bacterial breakdown of blood and from ammonia. Control works at three levels:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Source control:</strong> fast fluid acquisition keeps fluid away from the skin and reduces bacterial exposure time.</li>
<li><strong>Chemical control:</strong> pH buffers, citric acid, or zeolite-based odour absorbers in the core neutralise ammonia and volatile compounds.</li>
<li><strong>Physical control:</strong> breathable backsheets reduce trapped moisture and the warm, damp environment bacteria prefer.</li>
</ul>
<p>Odour claims are regulated in many markets. If you print "odour control" on the pack, be ready to show test data.</p>

<h3>Innovations in pad design</h3>
<p>Several directions are relevant to African producers:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Reusable cloth pads.</strong> Lower lifetime cost, locally sewable, and increasingly accepted. Design focus: leak-proof PUL backing, stay-dry lining, and snap or button wings.</li>
<li><strong>SAP-free and plant-based cores.</strong> Banana fibre, bamboo, and cotton cores reduce import dependence on SAP.</li>
<li><strong>Biodegradable backsheets.</strong> PLA and starch-based films are emerging but still cost more and perform differently in humidity.</li>
<li><strong>Modular and low-cost formats.</strong> Shorter pads with higher SAP load can reduce material use without cutting absorbency.</li>
<li><strong>Localised fit.</strong> Patterns developed from measurements of locally worn underwear outperform imported patterns.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Define your SKU range by flow (liner, regular, long/overnight, maternity) before designing any single pad.</li>
<li>Verify wing width against the underwear actually sold in your target market.</li>
<li>Specify each layer's function in writing topsheet, ADL, core, backsheet, adhesive and test them as a stack, not individually.</li>
<li>Run a rewet test under load (simulated sitting pressure), not just a capacity test.</li>
<li>Confirm all skin-contact materials are non-toxic and free of loose fibres; keep material certificates on file.</li>
<li>Offer a breathable backsheet option on at least one SKU for hot-climate comfort.</li>
<li>If you claim odour control, hold test data before printing the claim.</li>
<li>Check every health or sterility claim against your national standards body before launch.</li>
</ol>
