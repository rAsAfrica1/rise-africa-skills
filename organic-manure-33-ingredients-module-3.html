1. Module 3 Lesson 1: Sourcing and Preparing the 33 Ingredients Locally

Welcome to Module 3 of the Organic Manure with 33 Ingredients training. In this first lesson we focus on the heart of the entire process: sourcing and preparing the thirty three ingredients using materials that are available in your own community across Africa. Many farmers believe that making a powerful organic manure requires buying expensive inputs from agro dealers. That is not true. The thirty three ingredient formula was designed so that every component can be found within a few kilometres of your farm, whether you are in the highlands of Ethiopia, the savannah of Ghana, the Rift Valley of Kenya, or the drylands of Niger. Your task is to become a collector, not a customer. The first category of ingredients is animal manures. You will need at least eight different animal manures if possible. Cattle dung, goat droppings, sheep droppings, poultry litter, rabbit droppings, donkey dung, pig manure, and droppings from wild birds or bats where available. Each animal manure brings a different balance of nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and micronutrients. Poultry litter is rich in nitrogen and calcium. Goat and rabbit droppings are rich in potash and are considered cold manures that do not burn plants. Cattle and donkey dung provide fibre and organic matter that improves soil structure. Collect these manures from kraals, poultry houses, and resting places. Avoid manure that has been mixed with plastic, stones, or chemical residues. If you keep animals, start separating their manure by type so you can measure accurately. The second category is crop residues and plant biomass. You need at least ten plant based ingredients. Common examples include maize stover, sorghum stalks, millet stalks, rice straw, wheat straw, bean haulms, groundnut haulms, banana pseudostems, sugarcane bagasse, and coffee husks. These materials provide carbon, fibre, and a habitat for beneficial microbes. Chop them into pieces of about five to ten centimetres so they decompose faster. Do not burn your crop residues. Burning destroys nitrogen and organic carbon that your manure needs. The third category is green leafy materials. You need at least six green ingredients. Examples are tithonia diversifolia, also called Mexican sunflower, moringa leaves, neem leaves, pawpaw leaves, comfrey, and legume leaves such as cowpea, lablab, or pigeon pea. These green materials are rich in nitrogen and speed up decomposition. Tithonia is especially powerful and grows along many roadsides and field boundaries in East and Central Africa. Neem leaves add pest and disease suppression. Moringa adds growth hormones and micronutrients. Harvest these leaves when they are still green and fresh. The fourth category is mineral and ash based ingredients. You need at least five mineral ingredients. Wood ash from cooking fires, charcoal dust, rock phosphate where available, limestone or dolomite powder, and gypsum. Wood ash provides potassium, calcium, and magnesium. Charcoal dust improves soil microbial habitat and water retention. Rock phosphate is a slow release phosphorus source. Limestone corrects soil acidity. Gypsum adds calcium and sulphur. If you cannot find rock phosphate, you can use bone meal made from crushed and burned animal bones. The fifth category is microbial and activator ingredients. You need at least four activators. These include topsoil from a healthy forest or undisturbed bush, compost from a mature pile, fresh cow dung slurry, and urine from cattle or goats diluted with water. Forest topsoil contains billions of beneficial microorganisms that will multiply in your manure. Urine is rich in nitrogen and acts as a microbial stimulant. These activators are the engine of the whole process. Without them, decomposition will be slow and incomplete. Now let us talk about preparation. Before mixing, every ingredient must be prepared properly. Animal manures should be air dried under shade for two to three days to reduce moisture and kill some pathogens. Crop residues must be chopped. Green leaves should be wilted for one day so they do not create too much heat at once. Wood ash must be sieved to remove charcoal chunks and stones. Bones must be burned and crushed into powder. Forest topsoil should be collected from under trees, not from open ground, and kept moist. Urine should be diluted at one part urine to five parts water. Once all thirty three ingredients are prepared, you will have a diverse pile that no single chemical fertilizer can match. The key principle is diversity. Each ingredient plays a role. Some provide energy for microbes, some provide nitrogen, some provide phosphorus, some provide potassium, some provide calcium, some provide trace elements, some suppress pests, and some improve soil structure. When you combine all thirty three, you create a complete food for your soil and your crops. In the next lesson we will cover the exact mixing ratios and the layering method. For now, your assignment is to make a list of every ingredient available in your area and mark the ones you already have. Then walk your farm and community and collect the missing ones. Remember, the best manure is the one you make yourself from local materials. That is the African way. It is cheap, it is sustainable, and it works.

2. Module 3 Lesson 2: Mixing Ratios and Layering Method for 33 Ingredients

In this second lesson of Module 3 we move from sourcing to the actual construction of the thirty three ingredient manure pile. The mixing ratio and the layering method determine whether your manure becomes a rich, dark, sweet smelling humus or a slimy, smelly, fly infested waste. Follow these steps carefully and you will succeed even if this is your first time. The first rule is the carbon to nitrogen ratio. Your pile should have about thirty parts carbon to one part nitrogen. In practice this means you need more dry, brown, fibrous materials than fresh, green, wet materials. A simple way to remember is three buckets of dry material to one bucket of green material. If your pile is too dry, decomposition stops. If it is too wet, it becomes anaerobic and smells like rotten eggs. Aim for a moisture level like a wrung out sponge. When you squeeze a handful, a few drops should appear but not a stream of water. The second rule is particle size. All ingredients should be chopped or crushed to between two and ten centimetres. Smaller particles decompose faster because microbes have more surface area to work on. If you have a chaff cutter, use it. If not, use a machete or a hoe. This is labour intensive but it is the most important step for fast results. The third rule is the layering sequence. Do not simply dump all thirty three ingredients into one heap. Build the pile in thin layers, each no more than ten centimetres thick. Start with a base layer of coarse crop residues such as maize stover or sorghum stalks. This allows air to enter from the bottom. The base should be about twenty centimetres thick. Next add a layer of animal manure. Then a layer of green leaves. Then a thin layer of wood ash and charcoal dust. Then a layer of forest topsoil. Then a layer of chopped crop residues. Repeat this sequence until all ingredients are used. The final pile should be about one and a half metres wide, one and a half metres long, and one to one and a half metres high. If the pile is too small it will not heat up. If it is too large it will not get enough air. The fourth rule is the activator solution. Before you start layering, prepare a microbial starter. Mix ten litres of water, two litres of fresh cow dung slurry, one litre of diluted urine, and one kilogram of mature compost or forest topsoil. Stir well and let it sit for two days. As you build each layer, sprinkle this solution over the layer using a watering can. This introduces billions of microbes directly into the pile. The fifth rule is the mineral sprinkling. Rock phosphate, limestone, gypsum, and bone meal should be sprinkled in very thin layers, no more than one centimetre thick, between the organic layers. These minerals are concentrated and can burn plant roots if applied directly, but in a compost pile they are slowly broken down and chelated by microbial action. The sixth rule is turning. After the pile is built, cover it with banana leaves, palm fronds, or a tarpaulin to retain moisture and heat. Check the temperature after three days. Put a metal rod into the centre and pull it out. If it is too hot to hold, the pile is working. Turn the pile every seven days for the first three weeks. Turning means moving the outside of the pile to the inside and the inside to the outside. This ensures even decomposition. After three weeks, turn every fourteen days. The pile is ready when it is dark brown or black, crumbly, smells like sweet earth, and the original ingredients are no longer recognisable. This usually takes six to ten weeks depending on climate. In hot dry areas it may take eight weeks. In cool highland areas it may take twelve weeks. The seventh rule is protection from rain and sun. Too much rain leaches nutrients. Too much sun dries the pile. Build your pile under a tree or a simple shade structure. If you have no shade, cover it with a tarpaulin during heavy rains and remove the cover during light rains. The eighth rule is record keeping. Write down the date you built the pile, the ingredients you used, the quantities, the dates you turned it, and the date it was ready. This record will help you improve your recipe next season. Many farmers fail because they do not keep records and cannot repeat their success. Now let us talk about quantities for a standard pile. For a pile of one and a half cubic metres you will need approximately one hundred kilograms of mixed animal manures, one hundred kilograms of chopped dry crop residues, fifty kilograms of green leaves, ten kilograms of wood ash, five kilograms of charcoal dust, five kilograms of rock phosphate or bone meal, five kilograms of limestone, two kilograms of gypsum, twenty kilograms of forest topsoil, twenty litres of activator solution, and small quantities of any other ingredients you have. This is not a rigid formula. If you have more of one ingredient and less of another, adjust. The goal is diversity, not exact measurement. The ninth rule is safety. Wear gloves and a nose mask when handling poultry litter, pig manure, and urine. Wash your hands with soap after working. Do not let children play in the pile. Keep animals away from the pile. The tenth rule is patience. Do not use the manure before it is fully mature. Immature manure can burn your crops and introduce pests and diseases. When in doubt, wait another two weeks. In the next lesson we will discuss how to test manure quality and how to apply it to different crops. For now, your assignment is to build one pile using the layering method and record every step. Share your results with your neighbour. Together we can restore the soil of Africa.

3. Module 3 Lesson 3: Testing Manure Quality and Troubleshooting Common Problems

In this third lesson of Module 3 we focus on quality control. Making manure is not enough. You must be able to tell whether your manure is good, mediocre, or harmful. You must also know how to fix problems when they arise. A farmer who cannot diagnose a bad pile will waste months of work and may damage the farm. Let us start with the signs of good manure. First, smell. Good mature manure smells like sweet forest soil, like the ground under a big tree after rain. It does not smell like ammonia, rotten eggs, or sewage. Second, colour. Good manure is dark brown to black. If it is still light brown or green, it is not ready. Third, texture. Good manure is crumbly and loose. It breaks apart in your hand. It is not sticky, not slimy, and not powdery dry. Fourth, temperature. Good mature manure is cool to the touch. If it is still warm, it is still decomposing. Fifth, life. Good manure is full of earthworms, millipedes, and tiny white fungi. If nothing is living in it, something is wrong. Sixth, the jar test. Put a handful of manure in a glass jar, add water, shake, and let it settle. Good manure will have a dark brown liquid on top, a clear middle layer, and a small amount of grit at the bottom. If the water is cloudy and grey, the manure is immature. If it smells like rot, it is anaerobic. Now let us look at common problems and their solutions. Problem one: the pile smells like rotten eggs or sewage. This means the pile is too wet and has no air. Solution: turn the pile immediately and add dry materials such as chopped maize stover, rice husks, or sawdust. Do not add water for the next two weeks. Problem two: the pile smells like ammonia. This means too much nitrogen and not enough carbon. Solution: add dry brown materials and turn the pile. Problem three: the pile is not heating up. This means it is too dry or too small or lacks nitrogen. Solution: add water and green leaves, and make the pile bigger. Problem four: the pile is too hot, above sixty five degrees Celsius. This means too much nitrogen and the pile is burning. Solution: turn the pile and add dry materials. Problem five: the pile is full of flies and maggots. This means fresh manure or kitchen waste was added without covering. Solution: cover the pile with a layer of soil or mature compost. Do not add fresh meat, fish, or oily food. Problem six: the pile is full of ants. This means the pile is too dry. Solution: add water and turn. Problem seven: the pile has white mould on the surface. This is usually harmless and means fungal decomposition is active. Turn it into the pile. Problem eight: the pile has green or blue mould. This can be harmful. Remove the affected part and do not use it on food crops. Problem nine: the pile is attracting rodents. This means food waste was added. Remove it and set traps. Problem ten: the pile is not breaking down after three months. This means the ingredients were too large, too dry, or lacked microbes. Solution: chop everything smaller, add activator solution, and turn weekly. Now let us talk about laboratory testing. Most smallholder farmers in Africa cannot afford laboratory tests. But you can do simple field tests. The first is the germination test. Take a handful of your manure and mix it with soil in a small pot. Plant ten bean or maize seeds. Do the same with plain soil as a control. If the manure treated seeds germinate faster and grow greener, your manure is good. If they germinate slower or die, your manure is immature or toxic. The second is the earthworm test. Put ten earthworms in a bucket with your manure and a bucket with plain soil. If the worms stay alive and active in the manure for three days, it is safe. If they die or try to escape, the manure is not ready. The third is the water test. Mix one part manure with five parts water. Let it sit for one day. Use the liquid to water a small patch of grass. If the grass turns yellow or brown, the manure is too strong. Dilute it more before use. The fourth is the smell test after drying. Dry a sample in the shade for two days. If it still smells bad, it is not ready. Now let us talk about storage. Once your manure is ready, store it under shade in a dry place. Do not store it in direct sun because ultraviolet light kills microbes. Do not store it in a place where rain can leach nutrients. Use sacks, bins, or a covered heap. Good manure can be stored for six to twelve months without losing much quality. If you store it properly, you can make manure in the dry season and use it in the rainy season. Finally, let us talk about record keeping and quality control charts. Keep a notebook with the following columns: date, pile number, ingredients used, quantity of each ingredient, date turned, temperature at turning, smell, colour, texture, date ready, and final quality score. After three or four piles you will see patterns. You will learn which ingredients work best in your area. You will learn how weather affects decomposition. You will learn how to adjust. This is the difference between a farmer who makes manure once and a farmer who becomes a master manure maker. In the next lesson we will cover application rates for different crops and soil types. For now, your assignment is to test one pile using the germination test and the earthworm test. Write down your results and compare with your neighbour. If your manure fails, do not throw it away. Mix it with fresh materials and rebuild the pile. Every failure is a lesson.

4. Module 3 Lesson 4: Application Rates and Methods for Different African Crops

In this fourth lesson of Module 3 we move from production to use. You have made your thirty three ingredient organic manure. Now you must apply it correctly. The wrong rate or the wrong method will waste your manure and may reduce your yield. The right rate and method will double or triple your harvest. Let us start with the general principle. Organic manure is not a chemical fertilizer. It does not dissolve instantly. It feeds the soil first, and the soil feeds the plant. Therefore you must apply it before planting, not after the plant is already hungry. The best time is two to four weeks before planting. This gives the manure time to integrate with the soil and for microbes to start cycling nutrients. The second principle is placement. Do not broadcast manure on the surface. Sun and rain will destroy it. Place it in the planting hole, in the planting furrow, or in a band beside the row. This puts the nutrients where the roots can reach them. The third principle is quantity. More is not always better. Too much manure can burn young seedlings, especially if the manure is fresh. Too little will not feed the crop. The right quantity depends on the crop, the soil, and the quality of your manure. Let us go crop by crop. Maize is the most important staple in many parts of Africa. For maize, apply two to three handfuls of mature manure per planting station. A planting station is the spot where you will put two or three seeds. Mix the manure with the soil in the hole before dropping the seeds. If you are planting in rows, apply five to ten tonnes per hectare. That is about five to ten kilograms per square metre. If your manure is very rich, use the lower rate. If your soil is very poor, use the higher rate. For sorghum and millet in the drylands, apply one to two handfuls per station. These crops are more drought tolerant and need less nitrogen. Too much manure can make them grow tall and leafy but produce little grain. For rice, apply manure in the paddy before flooding. Use two to three tonnes per hectare. Mix it into the mud. Rice responds well to manure because the flooded soil preserves organic matter. For beans, cowpeas, groundnuts, and other legumes, apply one handful per station. Legumes fix their own nitrogen, so they need less. But they still need phosphorus, potassium, and micronutrients from the manure. For cassava, yams, and sweet potatoes, apply two to three handfuls per planting mound. These root crops need loose soil and plenty of potassium. Your manure with wood ash and banana pseudostems is perfect for them. For vegetables such as tomatoes, peppers, onions, cabbage, and kale, apply a thick layer of manure in the nursery bed and in the transplant hole. Use one to two handfuls per plant. For leafy vegetables, you can also make a manure tea. Put one kilogram of mature manure in a sack, dip it in twenty litres of water for three days, and use the liquid to water your vegetables every two weeks. This is a powerful foliar feed. For bananas, apply ten to twenty kilograms of manure per mat per year. Spread it around the base but not touching the stem. Cover with mulch. For coffee and tea, apply two to five kilograms per bush per year. Spread it in a ring around the drip line. For fruit trees such as mango, avocado, and citrus, apply five to twenty kilograms per tree per year, depending on age. Spread it under the canopy and cover with mulch. Now let us talk about soil types. Sandy soils drain quickly and lose nutrients. They need more manure, applied more often. Clay soils hold nutrients but can become waterlogged. They need manure mixed with sand or rice husks to improve drainage. Loamy soils are ideal. They need moderate amounts. Acidic soils need manure with limestone or wood ash. Saline soils need manure with gypsum and plenty of organic matter. Now let us talk about application methods. The first method is the planting hole method. This is best for maize, sorghum, millet, and vegetables. Dig the hole, put in the manure, mix with soil, plant the seed. The second method is the furrow method. This is best for row crops. Open a furrow, put manure in the bottom, cover with a thin layer of soil, plant the seeds. The third method is the broadcasting method. This is best for pastures and cover crops. Spread manure evenly and incorporate it with a hoe or rake. The fourth method is the ring method. This is best for trees and shrubs. Dig a ring around the plant, put manure in the ring, cover with soil. The fifth method is the mulch method. This is best for perennial crops and vegetables. Spread manure on the surface as a mulch. It will slowly release nutrients and suppress weeds. The sixth method is manure tea. This is best for quick boosts during the growing season. The seventh method is the seed coating method. This is best for small seeds like teff and finger millet. Mix seeds with fine, dry, sieved manure before planting. This gives the seedlings a head start. Now let us talk about timing. For rain fed crops, apply manure at land preparation, two to four weeks before the first rains. For irrigated crops, apply at any time but avoid waterlogging. For perennial crops, apply at the beginning of the rainy season. For vegetables, apply before transplanting and then every four to six weeks as a side dressing. Now let us talk about combinations. Organic manure works best when combined with other good practices. Combine it with crop rotation. Combine it with mulching. Combine it with agroforestry. Combine it with water harvesting. Combine it with pest and disease management. Do not combine it with chemical fertilizers in the same hole at the same time. The chemicals can kill the microbes in your manure. If you must use chemicals, apply them at least two weeks apart. Finally, let us talk about economics. One tonne of good organic manure can replace two to three bags of chemical fertilizer. In many African countries a bag of chemical fertilizer costs the equivalent of twenty to forty US dollars. A tonne of manure costs only your labour and local materials. If you make ten tonnes of manure, you save two hundred to four hundred dollars. That is money you can use for school fees, medicine, or improving your farm. In the next lesson we will cover scaling up, marketing, and training others. For now, your assignment is to apply your manure to one crop using the correct rate and method. Measure the yield and compare it with a plot where you did not apply manure. Write down the difference. Share your results with your community.

5. Module 3 Lesson 5: Scaling Up, Quality Standards, and Training Others

In this fifth and final lesson of Module 3 we move from the individual farmer to the community and the enterprise. You have learned to source, mix, test, and apply the thirty three ingredient organic manure. Now you must learn to scale up, maintain quality standards, and train others. This is how we transform agriculture across Africa. Let us start with scaling up. The first step is to calculate your needs. How much manure do you need for your whole farm? If you have one hectare of maize, you need five to ten tonnes. If you have one hectare of vegetables, you need ten to twenty tonnes. If you have one hectare of bananas, you need twenty to thirty tonnes. Write down your total requirement. The second step is to plan your production. One pile of one and a half cubic metres produces about five hundred kilograms of mature manure. To produce ten tonnes, you need twenty piles. You cannot build twenty piles at once by hand. You must mechanise or cooperate. The third step is to organise labour. Manure making is labour intensive. You need people to collect, chop, mix, turn, and transport. You can do this with family labour, hired labour, or a farmer group. A farmer group is best because you can share tools, transport, and knowledge. The fourth step is to invest in simple tools. A chaff cutter, a wheelbarrow, a watering can, a fork, a shovel, a sieve, and a tarpaulin. These tools cost money but they save time and improve quality. The fifth step is to find a permanent site. Your manure site should be near your fields, near water, under shade, and accessible by wheelbarrow or truck. Do not make manure in the middle of your garden because you will compact the soil. The sixth step is to schedule production. In most of Africa you can make manure year round if you have water. But the best time is the dry season, so the manure is ready for the rainy season. Make a calendar. Build piles every two weeks. Turn them on schedule. Harvest them on schedule. The seventh step is to store and transport. Store mature manure under shade in sacks or in a covered heap. Transport it to the field with a wheelbarrow, donkey cart, or truck. Do not transport it in the hot sun. The eighth step is to keep records. You must know how many piles you built, how much manure you produced, how much you used, and how much you sold. Now let us talk about quality standards. If you want to sell manure, you must meet standards. The first standard is maturity. The manure must be fully decomposed. It must not smell bad. It must not contain recognisable ingredients. The second standard is moisture. The manure should be dry enough to store but not dusty. About twenty to thirty percent moisture. The third standard is purity. The manure must not contain plastic, glass, stones, or metal. Sieve it before selling. The fourth standard is nutrient content. You can send a sample to a laboratory if one is available. If not, use the germination test and the earthworm test. The fifth standard is packaging. Use strong sacks of fifty kilograms. Label them with your name, the date, the ingredients, and the application rate. The sixth standard is consistency. Every sack must be the same quality. Do not mix good manure with bad manure. The seventh standard is customer service. Give your customers clear instructions. Tell them how to apply it. Tell them what to expect. Tell them to call you if they have problems. Now let us talk about marketing. Who will buy your manure? Vegetable growers, flower growers, tea and coffee estates, nurseries, landscapers, schools, churches, and municipalities. How will you reach them? Through farmer groups, radio, mobile phone, market days, agricultural shows, and demonstration plots. What price will you charge? Calculate your cost of production. Add a fair profit. Compare with chemical fertilizer. In many African countries, a fifty kilogram sack of organic manure sells for five to fifteen US dollars. A tonne sells for one hundred to three hundred US dollars. If you produce ten tonnes, you can earn one thousand to three thousand US dollars. That is a real business. Now let us talk about training others. You have a duty to pass on this knowledge. The first way is to train your family. Teach your children and your spouse. Make it a family activity. The second way is to train your neighbours. Invite them to your manure site. Show them the pile. Let them smell it, touch it, and test it. Give them a small sample to try. The third way is to train a farmer group. Organise a workshop. Use this manual. Use practical demonstrations. Let every participant build a small pile. The fourth way is to train trainers. Select the best farmers in your group. Teach them how to teach others. Give them a certificate. Help them start their own training. The fifth way is to use media. Write a simple leaflet. Record a voice message. Post on social media. Call in to a radio program. The sixth way is to work with extension officers. Invite them to your farm. Show them your results. Ask them to include organic manure in their programs. The seventh way is to work with schools. Teach children how to make manure. Start a school garden. The eighth way is to work with churches and mosques. Use their land. Use their networks. The ninth way is to work with local government. Ask for land, tools, or transport. The tenth way is to document everything. Take photos. Write notes. Keep records. Share your story. Now let us talk about challenges and solutions. Challenge one: lack of water. Solution: harvest rainwater, use greywater, make manure during the rainy season. Challenge two: lack of labour. Solution: use a farmer group, use oxen or donkeys, use simple machines. Challenge three: lack of transport. Solution: make manure near the field, use a wheelbarrow, share a truck with neighbours. Challenge four: lack of market. Solution: create demand by demonstrating results, work with extension officers, sell in small quantities first. Challenge five: pests and diseases. Solution: keep the pile covered, do not add meat or oil, turn regularly. Challenge six: theft. Solution: build the pile near your house, use a fence, involve the community. Challenge seven: discouragement. Solution: start small, celebrate small wins, join a group, keep learning. Finally, let us talk about the future. Organic manure with thirty three ingredients is not just a technology. It is a movement. It is a way to restore the soil, improve nutrition, reduce poverty, and fight climate change. Every tonne of manure you make keeps carbon in the soil and reduces greenhouse gases. Every hectare you improve feeds more people. Every farmer you train multiplies the impact. You are not just a farmer. You are a soil doctor, a community leader, and a climate hero. Your assignment for this final lesson is to train at least three other farmers in your community. Help them build their first pile. Follow up with them after one month. Report your results to your farmer group. Together we will build a green, prosperous, and food secure Africa. Thank you for completing Module 3. Go and make manure. Go and teach others. Go and transform your community.

<h2>Module 3 Practical Reference: Field Guide to the 33-Ingredient Organic Manure</h2>

<p>This Practical Reference lesson is designed as a stand-alone field companion for trainers, lead farmers, and small agribusiness operators who have completed Lessons 15 of Module 3. It consolidates the most important numbers, ratios, safety rules, and diagnostic steps into a single reference you can laminate, pin to the shed wall, or share as a printed handout. The focus is commercial: every figure below is chosen because it affects yield, cost per hectare, or market access. Use it when you are sourcing ingredients, mixing, testing, applying, or training others.</p>

<p>Organic manure with 33 ingredients is not a single recipe it is a platform. The exact ingredient list varies by agro-ecology (savannah, highland, humid forest, coastal), by livestock available, and by the crop you are targeting. What stays constant is the <strong>principle of balanced carbon, nitrogen, minerals, and microbial inoculum</strong>. Get that balance right and you produce a manure that rivals and in some trials exceeds the yield response of moderate NPK applications, at a fraction of the cash cost and with better long-term soil health.</p>

<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0; font-weight:bold; color:#a61c00; font-size:16px;"> SAFETY WARNING HEALTH, FOOD &amp; CHEMICAL HAZARDS</p>
<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0; font-size:13px; color:#333;">Fresh manure and urine carry <strong>E. coli, Salmonella, Cryptosporidium, and helminth eggs</strong> that can cause serious human illness. Never apply fresh manure to vegetables eaten raw (lettuce, tomato, onion, herbs). Always compost to maturity the 5565 C thermophilic phase for at least 3 consecutive days is what kills pathogens (WHO, 2006).</p>
<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0; font-size:13px; color:#333;">Wear <strong>gloves, boots, and a dust mask</strong> when handling poultry litter, ash, and bone meal. Wood ash is strongly alkaline (pH 1012) and causes chemical burns to skin and eyes never mix with ammonium fertilizers or fresh urine in a closed container, as ammonia gas can be released.</p>
<p style="margin:0; font-size:13px; color:#333;">Keep manure piles <strong>at least 30 m from wells, boreholes, streams, and household water sources</strong>. Wash hands with soap after handling. Do not allow children or pregnant women to turn compost. If you sell manure commercially, label it with batch date and maturity status.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist Print and Pin in Your Store</h3>

<ol style="font-family:Arial, sans-serif; font-size:14px; line-height:1.7;">
<li><strong>Source 33 ingredients before the rains:</strong> Collect dung, crop residues, ash, bone meal, urine, and legume biomass in the dry season so materials are ready when planting starts.</li>
<li><strong>Chop and dry:</strong> Reduce residues to 510 cm and dry poultry litter to below 40% moisture before layering this prevents anaerobic rot and foul odour.</li>
<li><strong>Layer correctly:</strong> Alternate 15 cm carbon layers with 5 cm nitrogen/mineral layers; target overall C:N of 2530:1 at the start.</li>
<li><strong>Moisture squeeze test:</strong> A handful should feel like a wrung-out sponge 5060% moisture. Too dry stops microbial activity; too wet causes anaerobic conditions.</li>
<li><strong>Turn on schedule:</strong> Turn every 35 days for 2130 days. Temperature should reach 5565 C in the first week, then fall as the pile matures.</li>
<li><strong>Test before selling:</strong> Cool, earthy smell, no ammonia, crumbly texture, C:N 1215:1, and a germination test above 80% confirm maturity.</li>
<li><strong>Apply by band or spot:</strong> Place manure near the root zone, not broadcast. Incorporate within 24 hours to reduce nitrogen loss.</li>
<li><strong>Record every batch:</strong> Log input cost, labour hours, maturation days, yield response, and customer feedback. This is your business intelligence for pricing and scaling.</li>
</ol>
