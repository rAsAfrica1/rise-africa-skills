1. What is Home-Based Catering?

Home-based catering means preparing food in your own kitchen and delivering it to clients for small events like birthdays, weddings, or corporate lunches. You are not running a restaurant; you cook in bulk at home, then transport the food to the event venue or deliver it ready to serve. In Africa, this model works well because you avoid high rent for a commercial space. You can start with one gas cooker, a few large pots, and a reliable motorbike or car for delivery. For example, in Nairobi, a home caterer might prepare 50 plates of pilau and kachumbari for a graduation party, charging KES 500 per plate. Your profit comes from your cooking skill, your ability to buy ingredients in bulk, and your reputation for punctuality. You must be organized: take orders at least 3 days in advance, shop the day before, and cook early on the event day. Unlike a restaurant, you don't need a dining area, but you do need a clean kitchen, safe food handling, and a way to keep food hot or cold during transport. Start small: offer a set menu for 20"30 people. As you gain trust, you can expand to 100 plates or more. Remember, your home is your production site, so keep it clean, pest-free, and separate from family living areas as much as possible. This business is low-cost to start but demands discipline and consistency.

2. Legal Requirements and Permits

Before you sell your first plate, you need to know the legal rules for home-based food businesses in your country. In most African countries, you must register your business with the local government or companies office. For example, in Kenya, you register with the Business Registration Service and get a business permit from your county. In Nigeria, you register with the Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC) and obtain a food handler's certificate from the local government. In South Africa, you need a Certificate of Acceptability from your municipality. These permits ensure your kitchen meets basic hygiene standards. You will also need a food handler's medical certificate, which requires a stool test and typhoid vaccination. This costs about $20"$50 and must be renewed yearly. If you deliver food, you may need a food transport permit. For home-based catering, many countries allow you to operate from home if you have a separate kitchen area and proper waste disposal. You must also pay taxes on your income. Keep records of all sales and expenses. Some local councils require an inspection of your kitchen before issuing a permit. The inspector will check for clean water, handwashing facilities, pest control, and proper refrigeration. Do not skip these steps: operating illegally can lead to fines or closure. Start by visiting your local health department or business office to ask for a checklist. It may take 2"4 weeks and cost $100"$300 total, but it protects your business and builds client trust.

3. Assessing Your Home Kitchen

Your home kitchen must be able to handle bulk cooking without compromising safety. First, measure your workspace: you need at least 2 meters of clear counter space for chopping and plating. Check your water source: you need running water, preferably treated or boiled, for washing hands and utensils. If you only have a shared tap, plan to store water in clean jerrycans. Next, look at your cooking capacity. A single-burner gas cooker can handle one large pot, but for 50 plates you need at least two burners or a large charcoal stove. Count your pots: you need at least one 20-liter pot for rice or stew, one 10-liter pot for vegetables, and a large frying pan. Refrigeration is critical: you need a fridge that can hold 20"30 kg of meat or vegetables at 4C or below. If you don't have a fridge, you must buy fresh ingredients daily and cook immediately. Check your storage: you need airtight containers for dry goods like rice, flour, and spices. Your kitchen must have a handwashing station with soap and a towel. Pest control is non-negotiable: seal cracks, use screens on windows, and store food off the floor. Also, consider your family: if you share the kitchen, schedule your cooking times so you don't cross-contaminate. Finally, think about ventilation: a window or exhaust fan to remove smoke and heat. If your kitchen fails any of these, fix it before you take orders. A simple upgrade like adding a second burner or a plastic table for prep can make a big difference.

4. Essential Equipment for Small Events

To cater for 20"50 people, you need specific tools that can handle volume. Start with cooking equipment: two large gas cookers (or one gas and one charcoal stove), at least three 20-liter pots, two 10-liter pots, and a large frying pan. You also need a heavy-duty knife set, a chopping board (separate ones for meat and vegetables), and a large wooden spoon or spatula. For measuring, get a kitchen scale (up to 5 kg) and measuring cups. For food safety, you need a thermometer to check meat doneness (75C for chicken) and a fridge thermometer. For transport, invest in insulated food carriers or coolers. In Africa, you can buy large plastic coolers (like the 50-liter Igloo) for about $40. You also need chafing dishes or hot boxes to keep food warm. A simple alternative is a large pot wrapped in a blanket inside a cardboard box. For serving, get disposable plates, cutlery, and napkins in bulk. You'll need serving spoons, tongs, and gloves. For cleaning, have plenty of dish soap, sponges, and bleach. Don't forget a first aid kit and fire extinguisher. If you deliver, you need a motorbike or car with a clean, covered carrier. A hand truck helps move heavy pots. Buy equipment gradually: start with the basics (pots, knives, cooler) and add as you earn. Many items can be found second-hand in local markets. Keep a list and prioritize safety and capacity over brand names.

5. Setting Up a Workflow

A good workflow prevents chaos and keeps food safe. Start by planning your menu and shopping list 3 days before the event. The day before, shop for all ingredients, then wash and chop vegetables, marinate meat, and cook any sauces that can be reheated. Store everything in the fridge in labeled containers. On event day, wake up early and follow a timed schedule. For example: 6:00 AM start cooking rice; 7:00 AM cook stew; 8:00 AM fry plantains; 9:00 AM pack food into insulated carriers. Work in zones: a prep zone (chopping), a cooking zone (stoves), and a packing zone (coolers). Keep raw and cooked food separate. Use color-coded chopping boards (red for meat, green for vegetables). Clean as you go: wash dishes immediately to avoid pests. Assign tasks if you have help: one person chops, one cooks, one packs. For delivery, load food last, keep hot food above 60C and cold food below 5C. At the venue, set up a serving station with gloves, tongs, and trash bags. After the event, collect leftovers, clean equipment, and record what worked and what didn't. A written checklist for each event helps you improve. Time yourself: if you promise food at noon, start cooking at 6 AM. Always add a 30-minute buffer for transport delays. With practice, you can cater for 50 people in 4 hours. Without a workflow, you risk late food, cold food, or food poisoning.

6. Basic Food Safety Certification

Food safety certification proves you know how to handle food without making people sick. In most African countries, you need a Food Handler's Certificate from your local health authority. The training covers four key areas: personal hygiene, temperature control, cross-contamination, and cleaning. For personal hygiene, you must wash hands for 20 seconds with soap before cooking, after using the toilet, and after handling raw meat. Wear clean aprons and tie back hair. If you are sick, do not cook. For temperature control, keep hot food above 60C and cold food below 5C. Cook chicken to 75C, beef to 63C, and reheat leftovers to 74C. Use a thermometer, not guesswork. For cross-contamination, use separate cutting boards and knives for raw meat and vegetables. Never put cooked food on a plate that held raw meat. Store raw meat on the bottom shelf of the fridge. For cleaning, sanitize surfaces with a bleach solution (1 tablespoon bleach per 4 liters water). Wash dishes in hot soapy water, then air dry. The certification course usually takes 1 day and costs $10"$30. You will take a short written test. After passing, you get a card valid for 1"2 years. Some countries require renewals with a new stool test. Display your certificate at your kitchen or include it in your client proposals. It shows you are professional and reduces your legal risk. Even if not required, take the course: it prevents foodborne illness, which can destroy your reputation.

<h2>Practical Reference: Costing, Pricing and Portion Control for Home-Based Catering</h2>

<p>In home-based catering across Africa, the difference between a hobby and a business is rarely the quality of the food. It is the quality of the numbers. A caterer in Kumasi, Kampala or Nairobi can cook exceptionally well and still close down within a year because the price of a plate of jollof, pilau or nyama choma was guessed rather than calculated. This Practical Reference lesson gives you the working tools: how to build a true cost per portion, how to set a price that protects your margin, and how to control portions so that every event leaves money in your pocket.</p>

<h3>Why Costing Comes Before Cooking</h3>

<p>Most home caterers in Africa price by comparing with the woman down the road. That is a race to the bottom. The correct anchor is your own cost structure. When you know your true cost per portion, you can decide whether to compete on price, on quality, or on reliability and you can say no to an event that will lose money.</p>

<p>The costing model has five layers: raw ingredients, overhead allocation, labour, waste allowance, and target margin. Skipping any one of them understates your cost and overstates your profit. The waste allowance is especially important in African home kitchens where power interruptions, market spoilage and transport losses are common. FAO estimates that globally around 14 percent of food produced is lost between harvest and retail, with significant losses in sub-Saharan Africa's perishable supply chains (FAO, 2019). Your waste allowance is not pessimism; it is realism.</p>

<strong style="color:#b71c1c;font-size:16px;"> SAFETY WARNING FOOD SAFETY IS NON-NEGOTIABLE</strong>
<p style="color:#b71c1c;margin:8px 0 0 0;">Home-based catering carries legal and health risk. You are handling food for people outside your household, including vulnerable guests (children, pregnant women, the elderly, immunocompromised). Follow these rules without exception:</p>
<ul style="color:#b71c1c;margin:8px 0 0 18px;">
<li><strong>Temperature danger zone:</strong> Keep hot food above 60C and cold food below 5C. Bacteria multiply fastest between 5C and 60C. Do not leave cooked food at room temperature for more than 2 hours.</li>
<li><strong>Cook thoroughly:</strong> Poultry and minced meat must reach 74C internal temperature; whole cuts of beef, pork and fish at least 63C. Use a food thermometer.</li>
<li><strong>Separate raw and cooked:</strong> Use separate chopping boards, knives and containers for raw meat and ready-to-eat food. Wash hands with soap for at least 20 seconds after handling raw meat.</li>
<li><strong>Water:</strong> Use safe, potable water for cooking, washing produce and cleaning. If water safety is uncertain, boil or treat it.</li>
<li><strong>Chemicals:</strong> Store cleaning chemicals, pesticides and fuel far from food, in clearly labelled containers. Never decant chemicals into drink bottles. Wash hands after handling.</li>
<li><strong>If in doubt, throw it out.</strong> Do not taste food to check if it is safe. Do not re-serve food that has been out of temperature control. One food-poisoning incident can end your business and harm your community.</li>
</ul>
<p style="color:#b71c1c;margin:8px 0 0 0;">Reference: WHO <em>Five Keys to Safer Food</em> (WHO, 2006) and the FAO/WHO Codex Alimentarius General Principles of Food Hygiene (CXC 1-1969, latest revision).</p>

<h3>How to Set Your Price Without Guessing</h3>

<p>Use the formula in Figure 1. First, calculate total cost per portion. Second, decide your target margin. In African home catering, a margin of 30 to 50 percent is realistic for small events, but it must cover your time, your risk and your reinvestment. A 30 percent margin on a 2.73 cost gives a price of 3.90; a 50 percent margin gives 5.46. The market will tell you which is achievable but never let the market push you below your break-even floor.</p>

<p>When a client says your price is too high, do not immediately cut. Instead, offer a lower-cost menu option: swap beef for beans, reduce the protein portion slightly, or remove a side dish. That protects your margin and gives the client a real choice. This is how professional caterers in Nairobi, Lagos and Accra stay in business.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ol style="font-family:Arial,Helvetica,sans-serif;line-height:1.7;">
<li><strong>Weigh, do not guess.</strong> Use a kitchen scale for staples and proteins. Eyeballing costs you money on every plate.</li>
<li><strong>Cost every dish before you quote.</strong> Build the five-layer cost: ingredients, overhead, labour, waste, margin.</li>
<li><strong>Pay yourself a labour line.</strong> If your time is free, your business is not real. Include it in every quote.</li>
<li><strong>Add a 515% waste allowance.</strong> Power cuts, spoilage and transport losses are real. Budget for them.</li>
<li><strong>Pre-portion proteins.</strong> Count chicken pieces, fish and meat portions before service. Do not let servers decide.</li>
<li><strong>Use standard ladles and scoops.</strong> A known-volume ladle for sauce and a known-weight scoop for rice keeps portions consistent.</li>
<li><strong>Track actual vs planned.</strong> After each event, compare what you bought with what you served. Adjust next time.</li>
<li><strong>Keep a price floor.</strong> Write down your break-even price per plate and never quote below it, no matter the pressure.</li>
</ol>
