7. Packaging, Selling and Distributing to Farmers

Producing spawn is only half the business. The other half is getting it to growers in good condition, charging the right price, and building a customer base that buys from you every single month. This module is about the commercial side — packaging, sales, distribution, and the recurring revenue that turns spawn into a real business.

Module 7 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~50 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 7.1 — Packaging — bags, jars, and the breathable filter

Spawn must be packaged in a way that keeps it alive and uncontaminated during transport and short-term storage. The packaging must:

  • Allow gas exchange (mycelium breathes)
  • Block contaminants (no holes, no dust entry)
  • Survive handling and transport
  • Be affordable
  • Present professionally to the customer
FormatCost per unitBest for
Glass jar (500 ml – 1 L)$0.30–$1.00Local sales, small-scale
Polypropylene bag with filter patch (1–2 kg)$0.30–$0.50Bulk sales to commercial growers
Plastic bag with tape-covered hole$0.10Emergency or very cheap option
Vacuum-sealed bag$0.60–$1.50Long-distance shipping

For startup, keep your spawn in glass jars — the same jars you grew it in. Sell them as-is with a filter lid. The grower returns the jar, or keeps it (jars are cheap). Move to polypropylene bags when you are shipping larger volumes.

Label requirements:

  • Species name (e.g. Pleurotus ostreatus)
  • Common name (e.g. Oyster mushroom)
  • Strain code (e.g. OST-A1)
  • Date of inoculation
  • Best before date (usually 3–4 weeks after full colonisation)
  • Weight (net weight of grain)
  • Your business name and contact
  • Storage instructions (cool, dark, use within X weeks)
Labeling is trust. A jar with a proper label sells for 30% more than the same jar with no label. Do not skip this. Even a handwritten label in permanent marker is better than nothing.
Practical exercise 7.1: Print 100 labels for your first batch. Include all eight fields. If you cannot afford printing, write them by hand with a permanent marker. Consistency of format matters more than looks.

Lesson 7.2 — Labelling — strain, date, expiry, usage instructions

Every jar of spawn must carry certain information. Here is the standard.

1 — Species and strain. "Pleurotus ostreatus (Oyster), strain OST-A1".

2 — Batch number. For traceability: OSP-20260915-A.

3 — Inoculation date. When the batch was started.

4 — Best before date. Usually 3–4 weeks from full colonisation. If you inoculated 15 Sept and it colonised by 27 Sept, best before is around 24 October.

5 — Net weight. The weight of grain spawn, excluding jar.

6 — Storage instructions. "Store at 4–8°C or in a cool dark place. Use within X weeks. Do not freeze."

7 — Usage summary. "Break up and mix into sterile substrate at 5–10% by weight. Keep at 22–28°C. Full colonisation in 14–21 days."

8 — Contact. Business name, phone, WhatsApp, email.

Add a QR code if you can. It links to your WhatsApp or a page with detailed usage instructions. This costs almost nothing and looks very professional.

Never change the strain code without changing the label. If you have a different strain, use a different code. Mixing strains up destroys the customer's harvest planning and your reputation.
Practical exercise 7.2: Design your label in a simple text document. Include all eight fields. Print or write 100 labels. Apply to your first batch before sale.

Lesson 7.3 — Cold storage and transport without refrigeration

This is one of the biggest practical challenges in African spawn production. Spawn needs cool conditions to stay fresh, but most customers do not have refrigeration and shipping distances can be long.

Best practices for no-fridge distribution:

  • Sell local. If you can deliver within 24–48 hours, spawn survives fine at ambient temperatures (up to 28°C) as long as it is fresh.
  • Sell small batches frequently. Do not produce a month's worth of spawn at once. Produce weekly, sell weekly. Fresh beats preserved.
  • Ship in insulated boxes. A simple polystyrene box (used for vaccines/ice cream) with a small frozen water bottle will hold 4–8°C for 24–36 hours. Reusable, cheap, effective.
  • Ship overnight if possible. A courier or bus service that delivers same-day or overnight is worth the cost for distant customers.
  • Advise customers to plant within 5 days. Spawn is not a long-term product for a grower without refrigeration. They should use it as soon as it arrives.

For producers with a fridge, spawn keeps 3–4 weeks at 4–8°C. Longer than that, it starts to fruit inside the jar or lose vigour.

The freshness rule: Spawn sold within 7 days of full colonisation is at peak vigour. Every week after that is a 10% decline in vigour. Fresh spawn is your competitive advantage against imported spawn that arrives 4 weeks old.
Practical exercise 7.3: Buy a small polystyrene cooler box and two ice packs. Test: can you ship a jar 200 km and have it arrive cool? If yes, you can serve distant customers without a full cold chain.

Lesson 7.4 — Pricing for different customer types

Spawn buyers are not one market — they are three, and each pays differently.

Customer typeVolumePrice sensitivityTypical price
Smallholder grower1–2 kg per monthHigh — price mattersRetail: $6–$10 per kg or per jar
Commercial grower20–100 kg per monthMedium — wants quality and reliabilityWholesale: $5–$8 per kg
Institutional / NGO / training centre10–200 kg per orderLow — wants documentation and reliabilityBulk: $4–$7 per kg with contract

How to price for each:

Smallholder (retail). Higher per-unit price, sold in small jars. Simple instructions. Personal delivery or local pickup. You are selling convenience and support as much as spawn.

Commercial. Bulk pricing, 20–40% lower per kg than retail, but the volume and repeat business are worth it. Provide training, ongoing support, replacement if a batch fails. Charge a deposit on reusable containers if you use them.

Institutional. Contracts, documentation, invoices, delivery schedules. Higher administrative burden — charge accordingly. But the revenue is predictable and the volumes are large.

Segment your customers. Do not sell to everyone at the same price. Smallholders pay retail, commercial buyers get wholesale, institutions sign contracts. Cross-selling destroys your margins.
Practical exercise 7.4: Set your three prices — retail per jar, wholesale per kg, contract per kg. Test them on real customers. Adjust based on what sells and what does not.

Lesson 7.5 — Marketing to mushroom growers — where they gather

Where do you find mushroom growers to sell to?

1 — Facebook groups. Every country in Africa has mushroom grower groups on Facebook. Join them. Participate (do not just spam). Share photos of your spawn lab, your batches, your results. Post when you have fresh spawn ready.

2 — WhatsApp groups. The same communities exist on WhatsApp. Ask a grower you know to add you to the local grower group. Be a helpful participant, not a spammer.

3 — Ministry of Agriculture and extension officers. The horticulture department at the ministry knows who is growing mushrooms. Contact the extension officer for your area. They are usually happy to connect a new supplier with growers.

4 — Farmer training centres and agricultural colleges. These schools teach mushroom farming. Their students become growers. Get on the school's list of recommended spawn suppliers.

5 — Mushroom buyer networks. The people who buy mushrooms from growers also know growers. Talk to hotel purchasing managers, market traders, and restaurant chefs.

6 — Local fairs and farmer field days. Attend. Set up a small stall. Show your spawn. Collect contacts. These events are where serious growers network.

7 — Direct outreach. Find a list of growers, message them personally. "Hi, I produce locally-made oyster spawn in [city]. I am offering X% off to the first 10 growers in your area. Can I send you a sample?"

Practical exercise 7.5: Join 3 online mushroom grower groups this week. Introduce yourself honestly: "I am starting local spawn production in [area]. I want to learn from experienced growers." Do not sell yet. Build trust first. Sales follow trust.

Lesson 7.6 — Contracts, repeat customers, and building a reputation

The value of a spawn customer is not one sale — it is 12 sales a year, for years. Here is how to build that.

1 — Deliver on time, every time. Growers plan their substrate preparation around your delivery. If you are late, their entire cycle is disrupted. Deliver when you promised.

2 — Replace failed batches without argument. If a grower buys spawn from you and their substrate fails, do not immediately blame their technique. Replace the spawn. If it was your fault, apologize and replace. If it was theirs, still replace — and then teach them the correct method. Losing one batch to keep a customer is a bargain.

3 — Offer a simple written agreement for commercial customers. Volume, price, delivery schedule, payment terms, replacement policy. Simple one-pager. Signed. This protects both parties.

4 — Become a teacher, not just a supplier. Your commercial customers want to grow more mushrooms. Teach them. Host workshops. Answer WhatsApp questions. The suppliers who are seen as experts get 80% of the market.

5 — Track every customer. Name, contact, purchase history, contact frequency. Follow up once a month to ask how the last batch performed. This simple practice puts you ahead of 95% of competitors.

6 — Build a referral engine. Offer existing customers a discount on their next order for every new grower they introduce. Word-of-mouth is the dominant marketing channel in this industry.

The lifetime-value rule: A commercial grower who buys 30 kg per month for 3 years is a $2,000+ customer. Treat them that way. Never sacrifice a long-term customer for a short-term pricing win.
Practical exercise 7.6 — Capstone: Write a one-page business plan for your spawn enterprise. Include: target species, monthly production target, pricing, first 10 customers, marketing plan, and 12-month revenue goal. This is your roadmap. Post it where you will see it daily.
