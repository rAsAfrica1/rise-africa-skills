Lessons

1. Calculating the Cost of Production per Kilogram

To know if your dried fruit business is making money, you must first know exactly how much it costs to produce one kilogram of dried product. This is your cost of production per kilogram. Start by listing all your expenses for a batch. For example, you buy 10 kg of fresh mangoes at 1,000 shillings per kg, so raw materials cost 10,000 shillings. You also pay for electricity or firewood to run the dryer, say 2,000 shillings. Labour for peeling and slicing might be 3,000 shillings. Packaging materials like airtight bags cost 1,500 shillings. Add any transport to buy inputs or deliver to market, say 1,000 shillings. Total cost = 10,000 + 2,000 + 3,000 + 1,500 + 1,000 = 17,500 shillings.

Next, find out how many kilograms of dried mango you get. Fresh mangoes lose about 80% of their weight when dried. So 10 kg fresh gives about 2 kg dried. Now divide total cost by dried weight: 17,500 2 = 8,750 shillings per kg. That is your cost of production per kilogram. If you sell below this, you make a loss. If you sell at 10,000 shillings per kg, your profit is 1,250 shillings per kg.

Always include all costs, even small ones like salt for pretreatment or cleaning supplies. If you use your own labour, put a fair wage for your time. Keep a notebook for each batch. Write date, fresh weight, dried weight, and every expense. After a few batches, you will know your average cost. This helps you set prices and spot when costs rise, like when mangoes are out of season. Remember, accurate costing is the foundation of a profitable business.

2. Setting Profitable Prices for Your Market

Once you know your cost per kilogram, you can set a price that covers costs and gives you a profit. But you must also consider what customers are willing to pay. In many African markets, dried fruit is sold in small packets, like 100g or 250g. If your cost is 8,750 shillings per kg, then 100g costs 875 shillings. You might sell a 100g packet for 1,500 shillings. That gives a profit of 625 shillings per packet, or 6,250 shillings per kg. Check prices at local supermarkets, markets, and online. If others sell 100g for 1,200 shillings, you may need to lower your price or reduce costs. But never sell below cost.

Consider your target market. Health-conscious urban buyers may pay more for organic or unsulphured dried fruit. Tourists might buy fancy packaging. For bulk buyers like hotels or schools, you can offer a discount per kg, but ensure it still covers costs. A simple pricing formula: Price = Cost per kg + Desired profit margin. If you want a 30% margin, price = 8,750 + (0.30 8,750) = 11,375 shillings per kg. Round to 11,500 shillings.

Test your prices. Start with a small batch and see how fast it sells. If it sells out in a day, your price may be too low. If it sits for weeks, it may be too high. Adjust accordingly. Also, factor in seasonality: during mango season, costs are lower, so you can price competitively. Off-season, costs rise, so you may need to increase prices. Always explain your value: no additives, natural, locally made. That justifies a fair price. Keep records of what you charge and what customers say. Pricing is not permanent; review it every few months.

3. Keeping Simple Records of Sales and Expenses

Record keeping is not paperwork for its own sakeit is how you know if you are making a profit. Use a simple notebook or a free phone app like Excel or Google Sheets. Divide it into two main sections: sales and expenses. For every sale, write the date, quantity sold (e.g., 5 packets of 100g), unit price, and total amount received. For every expense, write the date, item (e.g., mangoes, electricity, packaging), and amount paid. Also note if it was cash or mobile money.

At the end of each week, add up total sales and total expenses. Subtract expenses from sales to get your profit or loss. For example, if you sold 50 packets at 1,500 shillings each, sales = 75,000 shillings. Expenses might be 40,000 shillings for mangoes, 5,000 for electricity, 7,500 for packaging, and 10,000 for labour. Total expenses = 62,500 shillings. Profit = 12,500 shillings. That is your weekly profit. Do this every week. You will quickly see patterns: which products sell best, which months are slow, and where you can cut costs.

Keep receipts for large purchases. If you use a phone app, take photos of receipts and attach them. Back up your data. If you use a notebook, keep it in a safe, dry place. At the end of each month, calculate your total profit. Compare it to previous months. If profit is dropping, check if expenses are rising or sales are falling. Records also help you get loans or grantslenders want to see your numbers. Start simple, but be consistent. Even a few lines a day will transform your business decisions.

4. Managing Cash Flow for a Small Drying Business

Cash flow is the movement of money in and out of your business. Even if you are profitable on paper, you can run out of cash if you spend too much before sales come in. For a drying business, cash often goes out first: you buy fresh produce, pay for drying, packaging, and transport. Then you sell and get cash later. If you dry mangoes in December and sell in January, you need enough cash to cover December expenses. Plan ahead. Set aside money from previous sales to cover future production. For example, if you know you need 50,000 shillings for a batch, save 10,000 shillings each week for five weeks.

Keep a cash flow record. In your notebook, list all cash coming in (sales, loans) and going out (expenses, loan repayments). At the start of each week, note how much cash you have. After each transaction, update the balance. If you see the balance getting low, delay non-essential purchases or push for faster sales. Offer discounts for bulk buyers who pay immediately. Avoid borrowing for daily expenses; borrow only for investments that increase sales or reduce costs, like a better dryer.

Separate business and personal money. Use a separate mobile money account or cash box for the business. Pay yourself a fixed salary, say 20,000 shillings per month, instead of taking money randomly. That way, you always know your business cash position. During low seasons, you might need to reduce production or find alternative products like dried tomatoes or herbs. A simple cash flow forecast: for the next month, estimate sales and expenses. If you expect more expenses than sales, find ways to increase cash, like selling older stock at a discount. Good cash flow management keeps your business alive.

5. Understanding Profit Margins and Break-Even Point

Profit margin is the percentage of each sale that is profit. It tells you how efficient your business is. To calculate profit margin, use this formula: Profit Margin = (Profit Sales) 100. For example, if you sell 1 kg of dried mangoes for 11,500 shillings and your cost is 8,750 shillings, profit = 2,750 shillings. Profit margin = (2,750 11,500) 100 = 23.9%. That means for every 100 shillings in sales, you keep about 24 shillings as profit. A healthy margin for small food businesses is often 20-30%. If your margin is below 10%, you may need to raise prices or cut costs.

Break-even point is the number of units you must sell to cover all your costsno profit, no loss. To find it, first calculate your total fixed costs (rent, equipment, salaries) per month. Suppose fixed costs are 50,000 shillings. Then calculate your contribution margin per unit: selling price minus variable cost per unit. If you sell 100g packets for 1,500 shillings and variable cost (mangoes, packaging, electricity) is 1,000 shillings, contribution margin = 500 shillings. Break-even point = Fixed Costs Contribution Margin = 50,000 500 = 100 packets. So you must sell 100 packets per month just to cover costs. Every packet after that gives profit.

Knowing your break-even point helps you set sales targets. If you sell 150 packets, profit = (150 - 100) 500 = 25,000 shillings. If you sell only 80 packets, you make a loss of 10,000 shillings. Use this to motivate yourself and your team. Also, if you reduce variable costs by buying mangoes in bulk, your contribution margin increases, lowering your break-even point. Review these numbers monthly. They are your business compass.

6. Using a Notebook or Phone App for Bookkeeping

You do not need expensive software to keep good books. A simple notebook and pen work perfectly, as long as you are consistent. Choose a notebook with columns for date, description, money in, money out, and balance. At the top of each page, write the month and year. Every time you receive money, write it in the 'money in' column. Every time you spend, write it in 'money out'. After each entry, calculate the new balance. For example: start with 0. On 1st June, buy mangoes for 10,000 shillingsbalance becomes -10,000. On 5th June, sell 20 packets for 30,000 shillingsbalance becomes 20,000. This running balance shows your cash position at a glance.

If you have a smartphone, free apps like 'Money Manager' or 'Expense Manager' can make it easier. They allow you to take photos of receipts, set categories, and generate reports. Google Sheets is also free and works offline. Create a simple spreadsheet with columns: Date, Description, Category, Money In, Money Out, Balance. Use formulas to auto-calculate balance. You can also create charts to see trends. Whichever method you choose, back up your data. For notebooks, keep a photo of each page on your phone. For apps, enable cloud backup.

At the end of each month, summarize your records. Total money in, total money out, and final balance. Compare with previous months. This summary is your profit and loss statement. Use it to make decisions: if packaging costs are rising, find a cheaper supplier. If sales are growing, consider hiring help. Good bookkeeping also helps you when applying for a loan or grant. Lenders want to see clean records. Start todayeven if you only write one sale and one expense. Consistency matters more than complexity. Your notebook or phone is your business's memory.

<h2>Practical Reference: Costing, Pricing &amp; Record Keeping for Dried Fruit &amp; Vegetable Businesses</h2>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the six lessons in this module into one working document you can keep beside your drying trays, notebook, or phone. In dried fruit and vegetable processing across Africa, margins are thin and losses are common not because the drying itself fails, but because producers do not know their true costs, price by guesswork, and keep no records. A 2021 study of small and medium agro-processors in Kenya, Nigeria, and Tanzania found that fewer than one in three maintained written cost records, while those that did were significantly more likely to report stable profits (Alliance for a Green Revolution in Africa, 2021). This reference gives you the numbers, formulas, tables, and habits to change that.</p>

<h3>1. Calculating the Cost of Production per Kilogram</h3>

<p>Your cost per kilogram of finished dried product is the single most important number in your business. Without it, every price you set is a guess. The formula is:</p>

<p><strong>Cost per kg = (Total raw material cost + Total processing cost + Total packaging cost + Total overheads) Total kg of finished product</strong></p>

<p>The critical trap is the <em>fresh-to-dried conversion ratio</em>. Most fruits and vegetables lose 7090% of their weight during drying. One kilogram of finished dried mango may require 710 kg of fresh mango. If you cost your dried product using the fresh weight price, you will underprice by a factor of seven or more and lose money on every sale.</p>

<h3>2. Setting Profitable Prices for Your Market</h3>

<p>Once you know your cost per kilogram, pricing becomes a decision, not a gamble. Use this sequence:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Cost floor:</strong> Your cost per kg is the absolute minimum. Selling below it means you are paying customers to take your product.</li>
<li><strong>Target margin:</strong> Add 3050% for direct sales, 2030% for wholesale to shops or exporters.</li>
<li><strong>Market check:</strong> Compare with what similar products sell for in your area. If your cost-plus price is far above the market, your problem is cost, not pricing.</li>
<li><strong>Value addition:</strong> Packaging, labelling, and consistent quality justify a premium. Loose dried fruit in an open basin sells for less than the same product in a sealed, labelled pouch.</li>
</ol>

<p>Record keeping does not require accounting software. It requires consistency. At minimum, record every sale and every expense on the same day it happens. A single exercise book with two sections one for money in, one for money out is enough to start. The discipline matters more than the tool.</p>

<h3>4. Managing Cash Flow for a Small Drying Business</h3>

<p>Profit and cash are not the same thing. You can be profitable on paper and still run out of money to buy raw materials. Drying businesses face a specific cash-flow challenge: you spend money on fresh produce and fuel today, but you sell the dried product days or weeks later.</p>

<p>Three practical rules:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Keep a cash reserve</strong> equal to at least one production cycle (typically 12 weeks of raw material and fuel costs).</li>
<li><strong>Match payment terms to your cycle.</strong> If you sell to a shop on 30-day credit, you need 30 days of working capital. If you cannot afford that, sell for cash at a lower margin.</li>
<li><strong>Separate business and household money.</strong> Mixing the two is the most common cause of business failure in small-scale processing.</li>
</ul>

<h3>5. Understanding Profit Margins and Break-Even Point</h3>

<p><strong>Gross profit margin</strong> = (Selling price Cost per kg) Selling price 100.</p>
<p><strong>Break-even point</strong> = Total fixed costs (Selling price per kg Variable cost per kg).</p>

<p>Example: If your monthly fixed costs (rent, equipment depreciation, licence) are $120, your selling price is $6.75/kg, and your variable cost is $4.50/kg, your break-even is 120 (6.75 4.50) = 53.3 kg per month. Below that, you lose money. Above it, every kilogram adds profit.</p>

<strong style="color:#c0392b;"> SAFETY WARNING Dried Fruit &amp; Vegetable Processing</strong><br>
Improper drying and storage can cause <strong>aflatoxin contamination</strong> (from moulds) and <strong>foodborne illness</strong>. Never sell or consume dried products with visible mould, off-smells, or discolouration. Dry to a moisture content below 1215% (depending on product), store in airtight containers away from moisture, and keep records of drying dates and batch numbers. Aflatoxin is invisible, heat-stable, and toxic it cannot be removed by cooking or re-drying. If you suspect contamination, discard the entire batch. (Source: WHO/FAO, 2018, <em>Code of Practice for the Prevention of Mycotoxins in Dried Fruits</em>.)
</div>

<h3>6. Using a Notebook or Phone App for Bookkeeping</h3>

<p>A notebook costs almost nothing and works everywhere. A phone app adds searchability, automatic totals, and backup. For most small drying businesses, the best approach is a hybrid: record daily in a notebook, then transfer weekly totals to a phone app or spreadsheet. Free apps suitable for African small businesses include <strong>Wave</strong> (invoicing and accounting), <strong>Kobo Collect</strong> (data capture), and simple Google Sheets templates. The key is not the tool it is the daily habit.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Know your conversion ratio.</strong> Weigh fresh input and dried output for every batch. Record it.</li>
<li><strong>Calculate cost per kg weekly.</strong> Include raw materials, fuel, labour, packaging, and a share of overheads.</li>
<li><strong>Never price below cost.</strong> Set a floor and stick to it, even when buyers push for discounts.</li>
<li><strong>Record every sale and expense the same day.</strong> No exceptions, no "I'll remember."</li>
<li><strong>Keep business and household cash separate.</strong> Use separate tins, envelopes, or mobile money wallets.</li>
<li><strong>Calculate your break-even point monthly.</strong> Know how many kg you must sell to cover fixed costs.</li>
<li><strong>Maintain a cash reserve</strong> of at least one production cycle.</li>
<li><strong>Review your records every Sunday.</strong> One hour of review prevents weeks of loss.</li>
</ol>
