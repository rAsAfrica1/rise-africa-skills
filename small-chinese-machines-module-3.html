1. How to Use Alibaba, Made-in-China, and 1688

Alibaba is the world's largest B2B marketplace for sourcing from China. Go to alibaba.com and search for your machine, e.g., 'brick making machine'. Use filters: 'Supplier Country: China', 'Verified Supplier', and 'Ready to Ship' for faster delivery. Alibaba shows prices in USD and supports English. Made-in-China.com is similar but often has more industrial suppliers. 1688.com is Alibaba's domestic Chinese platform with lower prices but no English and requires a Chinese agent to buy. For beginners, start with Alibaba and Made-in-China. Example: A block-making machine on Alibaba might cost $2,500 FOB, while the same on 1688 could be $1,800 but you need an agent who charges 5-10% commission. Use Alibaba's 'Chat Now' to message suppliers directly. Ask for a catalog, price list, and shipping cost to your port. Always compare at least 5 suppliers. Check their transaction level and years in business. A supplier with 5+ years and $100,000+ in transactions is safer. For 1688, you can use agents like Yoybuy or Superbuy, but verify their fees. Remember: Alibaba is for international buyers, 1688 is for locals. If you want small quantities, Alibaba is better because 1688 sellers often refuse to export directly. Use Google Translate for Chinese pages. Save screenshots of product specs to avoid disputes. Never pay outside the platform until you trust the supplier. For African buyers, Alibaba has a dedicated Africa site (alibaba.com/africa) with local support. Start by watching YouTube tutorials on 'how to buy from Alibaba' and '1688 sourcing agent'. Practice by searching for a simple item like a welding machine. Within a week, you'll understand the basics.

2. Vetting Suppliers: Verified, Gold, and Trade Assurance Badges

On Alibaba, badges are your first filter. 'Verified Supplier' means a third-party (like SGS) inspected the factory. It costs the supplier money, so it's a good sign. 'Gold Supplier' is a paid membership, not a quality guaranteeâmany good suppliers are Gold, but so are some bad ones. 'Trade Assurance' is the most important: it protects your payment if the supplier doesn't ship on time or product doesn't match the contract. Always choose suppliers with Trade Assurance. Check the supplier's 'Transaction Level'âlevels range from 1 to 6, with 6 being the highest. Aim for level 3 or above. Look at 'Response Rate' and 'Response Time'âabove 80% and under 12 hours is good. Read 'Company Profile': years in business, factory size, main markets. A supplier with 10 years and 500+ workers is likely reliable. For Made-in-China, look for 'Audited Supplier' badges. For 1688, check 'å®ååå®¶' (Strength Merchant) badge. Use third-party tools: ImportYeti (free) shows U.S. import records, so you can see if a supplier has shipped to America. Panjiva (paid) gives more data. Ask for business license and export license. Verify the license number on China's National Enterprise Credit Information Publicity System (gsxt.gov.cn). If a supplier refuses to give license or Trade Assurance, walk away. Example: A supplier with Gold badge but no Trade Assurance and 1-year membership is risky. A supplier with Verified, Trade Assurance, 5 years, and level 4 is safe. Always check reviews on Alibabaâlook for negative feedback about quality or delays. For African buyers, also check if the supplier has experience shipping to your country. Ask for references from other African buyers. Don't trust badges alone; combine with direct communication and sample testing.

3. Requesting Samples and Conducting Inspections

Never order a full container without a sample. Request a sample of the machine or its critical parts. Expect to pay for the sample plus shipping (e.g., $50-$200 for a small machine part via DHL). Some suppliers refund sample cost on bulk order. Ask for a 'sample order' with clear specs. When the sample arrives, test it thoroughly. For a brick machine, check motor power, steel thickness, and output. Record video of it working. If it fails, you have evidence. For inspections, hire a third-party inspector like SGS, Bureau Veritas, or Intertek. Cost: $300-$500 per man-day. They visit the factory, check production line, and issue a report. For small orders, you can use a local agent in China (e.g., from Freelancer.com) for $100-$200. They can take photos and videos. Before inspection, send a detailed checklist: dimensions, materials, voltage, color, packaging. During production, ask for weekly photo updates. After production, do a pre-shipment inspection (PSI). If you can't afford a third-party, use a video call with the supplier showing the machine running. Record the call. For African buyers, some inspection companies have offices in Lagos, Nairobi, or Johannesburg. Example: A Kenyan buyer ordered a $5,000 maize milling machine. He paid $400 for SGS inspection in China, which found a wrong motor. Supplier fixed it. Without inspection, he would have lost money. Always inspect before paying the balance. If supplier refuses inspection, cancel the order. For 1688, your sourcing agent can inspect. Remember: sample first, then small trial order (e.g., 5 units), then bulk. This reduces risk.

4. Understanding MOQs, FOB, and CIF Pricing

MOQ stands for Minimum Order Quantity. A supplier might say MOQ: 1 set for a machine, or MOQ: 100 pieces for parts. If you need less, ask if they accept smaller ordersâoften they do for a higher price. For African startups, start with 1-5 units. FOB (Free On Board) means the supplier delivers goods to a Chinese port (e.g., Shanghai, Ningbo). You pay for shipping from that port to your country. FOB price is lower, but you handle freight and insurance. CIF (Cost, Insurance, Freight) means supplier arranges and pays for shipping to your port. CIF is easier for beginners but often 10-20% more expensive. Example: A machine FOB Shanghai is $2,000. CIF Mombasa is $2,500. You save $500 by using FOB and a freight forwarder. For FOB, get quotes from freight forwarders like DHL Global Forwarding, Kuehne+Nagel, or local African forwarders. For CIF, ask supplier for a breakdown: ocean freight, insurance, port charges. Always clarify: does CIF include unloading at destination? Usually not. Also check Incoterms: EXW (Ex Works) means you pick up from factory; FOB is port; CIF is destination port. For small machines, air freight might be cheaper than sea for under 100kg. Sea freight takes 30-45 days to Africa, air 5-10 days. Calculate total landed cost: FOB + freight + insurance + import duty + VAT + clearing. In Kenya, duty on machinery is often 0-10%, VAT 16%. In Nigeria, duty 5-20%, VAT 7.5%. Use an online duty calculator. Always get a proforma invoice with FOB or CIF terms clearly stated. Don't accept vague 'shipping included' without details.

5. Payment Methods: T/T, Letters of Credit, and Escrow

T/T (Telegraphic Transfer) is a bank wire. Most Chinese suppliers prefer 30% deposit, 70% before shipment. For small orders under $5,000, T/T is common. Always pay to the company bank account, not a personal account. Verify the account name matches the business license. For larger orders, use a Letter of Credit (L/C). An L/C is a bank guarantee: your bank pays the supplier only when shipping documents are presented. It protects both parties. Cost: 0.5-2% of order value. L/C is complex but safer for orders above $20,000. Escrow services like Alibaba's Trade Assurance act as a middleman: you pay Alibaba, they hold funds until you confirm receipt. This is the safest for beginners. For 1688, use an agent's escrow or Alipay. PayPal is rarely used for B2B machines due to fees. Western Union is unsafeânever use it for business. Example: A Ghanaian buyer paid $3,000 via T/T to a supplier's personal account and lost money. Always use Trade Assurance or L/C. For T/T, split payments: 30% deposit, 30% after production photos, 40% after inspection. Never pay 100% upfront. Ask for a proforma invoice with bank details. Double-check SWIFT code. Use your bank's online portal, not email links. For L/C, work with your bank's trade finance department. They will review documents. For escrow, Alibaba charges 2-3% fee but it's worth it. For orders above $50,000, consider a standby L/C. Always keep records of all payments. If supplier pressures you to pay outside platform, refuse. For African buyers, some banks like Ecobank, Standard Bank have trade finance desks. Ask for help. Never use cryptocurrency for sourcingâno protection.

6. Avoiding Scams and Rogue Traders

Common scams: 1) Fake supplier: They copy a real company's photos and name. Check the business license number on gsxt.gov.cn. 2) Price too low: If a $5,000 machine is offered for $1,500, it's a scam. 3) Payment to personal account: Always pay to company account. 4) Fake Trade Assurance: They send a fake link. Only pay through Alibaba's official website. 5) Sample switch: They send a good sample, then ship inferior goods. Use inspection. 6) Shipping scam: They ask for extra fees after you pay. Agree on all costs in writing. 7) Phishing emails: They hack supplier email and change bank details. Always confirm by phone or video. 8) Rogue trader: A middleman with no factory. Ask for factory video tour. 9) MOQ trap: They accept small order then demand more money. Get everything in proforma invoice. 10) Fake tracking: They give a fake tracking number. Use a freight forwarder you trust. To protect yourself: Use Trade Assurance or L/C. Never pay by Western Union, MoneyGram, or crypto. Get a video call with the factory. Ask for references from other African buyers. Check Alibaba's blacklist forum. For 1688, use a reputable agent with escrow. If a deal seems too good, walk away. Example: A Nigerian buyer lost $10,000 to a fake supplier on Alibaba who had a Gold badge but no Trade Assurance. The supplier disappeared. Always trust your gut. If they rush you, it's a red flag. Use a contract with clear penalties. For small orders, use a credit card via PayPal (if accepted) for chargeback. For larger, use L/C. Report scams to Alibaba and your local police. Remember: due diligence takes time but saves money. Never skip vetting.

3. Requesting Samples and Conducting Inspections

Always request a sample before bulk order. For small machines, a full unit sample is preferable; for parts, a component sample is acceptable. Budget for sample cost plus air freight â typically USD 80â300 for a small maize mill or oil press, depending on weight.

For orders above USD 5,000, commission a pre-shipment inspection through SGS, Bureau Veritas, Intertek, or a local Chinese QC agent. Inspection costs USD 250â400 per man-day but routinely prevents losses of thousands of dollars from wrong voltage, missing spares, or substandard motors.

â  SAFETY WARNING â Electrical and Mechanical Hazards

Small Chinese machines are frequently supplied with 220V/50Hz motors that may not match local grid stability. In many African markets, voltage fluctuates between 180V and 260V. Always:

  • Confirm motor voltage and frequency in writing before payment.
  • Install a voltage stabiliser or AVR for every machine (USD 40â120).
  • Verify earthing and use RCD/earth-leakage protection.
  • Ensure guards are fitted on belts, pulleys, and feed openings before operation.
  • Train operators on lockout/tagout and never bypass safety switches.

Non-compliant machines have caused electrocution and amputation incidents in agro-processing workshops across East and West Africa. Treat electrical safety as non-negotiable.

4. Understanding MOQs, FOB, and CIF Pricing

MOQ (Minimum Order Quantity) is the smallest order a supplier will accept. For small machines, MOQs are often 1â5 units. Negotiate down by offering repeat business or combining orders with other buyers in your region.

FOB (Free On Board) means the supplier delivers the goods to the Chinese port; you pay freight, insurance, and destination charges. CIF (Cost, Insurance, Freight) means the supplier covers freight and insurance to your destination port. CIF is simpler for first-time buyers but usually 10â20% more expensive than arranging your own freight.

For African importers, FOB plus a trusted freight forwarder is usually cheaper. Typical freight for a 20ft container from Shanghai to Mombasa is USD 1,800â2,800; to Lagos USD 2,200â3,200; to Durban USD 2,000â3,000 (rates fluctuate with fuel and season).

5. Payment Methods: T/T, Letters of Credit, and Escrow

T/T (Telegraphic Transfer) is the most common method. Standard terms are 30% deposit, 70% before shipment. Never pay 100% upfront to a new supplier.

L/C (Letter of Credit) is a bank guarantee â the supplier is paid only when compliant documents are presented. L/Cs are safer for orders above USD 20,000 but cost USD 150â400 in bank fees and require precise documentation. Many African banks now support L/Cs for Chinese trade.

Escrow (Trade Assurance) is the best option for orders under USD 20,000. Funds are held by the platform and released only on your confirmation. Use it whenever the supplier accepts it.

6. Avoiding Scams and Rogue Traders

Common scams targeting African buyers include: fake supplier websites cloned from real ones; bait pricing far below market; requests to pay to a personal bank account rather than the company account; and "customs fee" scams where a fake clearing agent demands extra payment. Protect yourself by: verifying the bank account name matches the company name; using Trade Assurance or L/C; never paying to a personal account; and using a licensed clearing agent registered with your national customs authority.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Confirm the supplier's business licence matches the bank account name.

  5. Commission a pre-shipment inspection for orders above USD 5,000.
  6. Compare FOB and CIF quotes; use a licensed freight forwarder.
  7. Verify import permits (KEBS, SONCAP, PVoC) and duty rates before shipping.
