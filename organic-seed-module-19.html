Quality Control Systems - Preventing Failures Before They Happen

~14 min read20 min gameAdvanced
Shared phone

🧠 Brain Opener

One rejected batch can wipe out a season. A quality control system stops rejections before they happen. It is not paperwork. It is a checklist that saves your business.

  • What is quality control?
  • What are the five checkpoints of a seed business?
  • What is a corrective action?
🎁 Reveal a strong answer

Quality control is a set of checks at every stage to catch problems early. The five checkpoints are: seed source, growing, harvest, processing, and storage. A corrective action is what you do when a check fails - you fix the problem and record it.

📖 Nyari Builds a QC System

Nyari had been producing organic tomato seed for four years. She had a good reputation. Then one batch went wrong.

A buyer in Bulawayo ordered 10 kilograms. He tested on arrival: 71 percent germination. He had paid 800 dollars. He asked for a refund.

Nyari did not know what had gone wrong. She tested her stored sample. Also 71 percent. She tested a sample from the previous batch. 91 percent. The problem was in the batch itself, not the storage.

She went through her records. No records. She had not written anything down.

She could not tell whether the seed had failed at planting, at flowering, at harvest, at drying, or at some other step.

She decided never to be in that position again. She built a quality control system.

Five checkpoints. At each one, a check and a record.

Checkpoint 1 - seed source. Every new foundation seed packet: batch number, supplier, date, weight, visual quality.

Checkpoint 2 - growing. Weekly walk of every plot: pest signs, disease signs, off-types rogued, count of rogued plants.

Checkpoint 3 - harvest. Date, weather, moisture at harvest, batch number assigned.

Checkpoint 4 - processing. Drying days, moisture after drying, sieving sizes, final weight, germination test at 14 days.

Checkpoint 5 - storage. Temperature weekly, moisture monthly, germination test at 3 months.

The next season, her harvest was clean. 92 percent germination. But the real value came six months later.

A buyer in Kenya asked for 20 kilograms. His terms were strict: 90 percent minimum germination. Nyari pulled her stored sample from the same batch. 89.7 percent.

She tested again with a fresh sample. 91 percent. She sent the batch.

The buyer tested. 90.2 percent. He paid in full, and ordered 40 kilograms the next season.

Nyari had built a system that catches failure before it reaches the buyer.

💡 The Big Idea

Quality control is a habit, not a document

A quality control system is five checkpoints, each with one check and one record. It takes 20 minutes a week. It catches 90 percent of problems before they become rejections. Without it, you are gambling every season with your reputation.

📚 Three Deep Lessons

1

The five checkpoints

Checkpoint 1 - Input check. Every bag of foundation seed, every input, every compost batch. Check: variety, supplier, batch, weight, appearance. Record in log.

Checkpoint 2 - Growing check. Weekly walk of each plot. Check: pest signs, disease signs, off-types. Record roguing counts.

Checkpoint 3 - Harvest check. Before, during, and after harvest. Check: moisture, weather, batch number. Record in log.

Checkpoint 4 - Processing check. Drying, threshing, cleaning, grading. Check: moisture, sieving sizes, final weight, germination. Record in log.

Checkpoint 5 - Storage check. Weekly temperature, monthly moisture, quarterly germination. Record in log.

2

The testing schedule

Every batch: germination test at 14 days. This is the minimum.

Every 3 months: germination test on stored batches. Germination falls with time.

Every 6 months: purity test on stored batches. Storage pests can introduce foreign matter.

Before every sale: confirm the batch has a fresh germination test (within 30 days), correct moisture (8 to 12 percent), and clean appearance.

The testing schedule sounds heavy. It is not. A germination test takes 10 minutes to set up and 5 minutes to count. That is 15 minutes per batch per test.

3

What to do when something fails

Step 1: Isolate the failing batch. Do not mix it with good batches.

Step 2: Find the cause. Check records for the batch. When was it harvested? What was the moisture? What was the storage temperature?

Step 3: Take corrective action. If the batch is under 80 percent germination, do not sell. If it is 80 to 85 percent, sell at a discount with disclosure. Above 85 percent, sell normally.

Step 4: Record everything. What failed, why, what you did about it. This record prevents the same problem next season.

Step 5: Tell the buyer. If a buyer reports a problem, tell them what you found. Honesty is more valuable than the batch.

🛠 Hands-On - Build Your QC Checklist

You need

  • A notebook
  • A pen
  • A clipboard
  1. Write your five checkpoints on one page.
  2. For each checkpoint, write down 3 checks you will perform.
  3. For each check, write down: when it happens, who does it, what record is kept.
  4. Print the checklist and pin it up in your work area.
  5. Use it for one week. Note which checks are hard to do.
  6. Adjust the checklist to make it practical.
  7. Use it for one full season.
  8. Review and improve it at the end of the season.
Why it works: A checklist is only useful if you actually use it. Build the habit first.

🏆 Project Challenge

Run Your First QC Season

  2. For each, write 3 specific checks and a record format.
  3. Print the checklist and pin it up.
  4. Use it every day for the season.
