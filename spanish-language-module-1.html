1. Greetings and Introductions for Customer Welcome

In any African market, a warm greeting opens the door to a sale. In Spanish, start with "Buenos das!" (good morning) before noon, "Buenas tardes!" (good afternoon) from noon until sunset, and "Buenas noches!" (good evening/night) after dark. These are universal and respectful. For a more casual welcome, you can say "Hola!" (hello) to anyone, but adding the time of day shows you care. When a customer approaches your stall, smile and say, "Bienvenido!" (welcome to a man) or "Bienvenida!" (to a woman). If you're welcoming a group, use "Bienvenidos!" (mixed or all male) or "Bienvenidas!" (all female).

Introduce yourself simply: "Me llamo [your name]" (My name is...). Ask their name: "Cmo se llama?" (What is your name? formal) or "Cmo te llamas?" (informal). Respond with "Mucho gusto" (nice to meet you). If you want to know how they are, ask "Cmo est?" (formal) or "Cmo ests?" (informal), meaning "How are you?" Answer with "Bien, gracias" (fine, thanks) or "Muy bien" (very good).

In many African markets, customers appreciate when you try their language. If you're in Equatorial Guinea (the only Spanish-speaking African country), these greetings are standard. But across Africa, you'll meet Spanish-speaking tourists, businesspeople, and migrants from Latin America. Practice with a partner: one plays vendor, one plays customer. Use real items from your stalltomatoes, phone credit, fabric. Greet, introduce yourself, ask their name and how they are. Repeat until it feels natural. Write the phrases on a card and keep it at your stall. Within a week, you'll greet confidently and make customers feel at home.

2. Numbers 1-20 for Pricing and Quantities

Numbers are the backbone of market trade. In Spanish, learn 1-20 by heart: uno, dos, tres, cuatro, cinco, seis, siete, ocho, nueve, diez, once, doce, trece, catorce, quince, diecisis, diecisiete, dieciocho, diecinueve, veinte. Practice counting your goods: "una naranja" (one orange), "dos naranjas" (two oranges), "tres naranjas" (three oranges). Note that "one" changes to "una" before feminine nouns like naranja, and "un" before masculine nouns like mango. For prices, say "cuesta" (it costs) or "son" (they are). For example: "Cuesta cinco dlares" (It costs five dollars). "Son diez euros" (They are ten euros). If you sell in local currency, just substitute: "Cuesta cien chelines" (It costs one hundred shillings).

When a customer asks "Cunto cuesta?" (How much does it cost?), you reply with the number and currency. To ask how many they want, say "Cuntos?" (for masculine items) or "Cuntas?" (for feminine items). For example, "Cuntos mangos?" (How many mangoes?) or "Cuntas camisetas?" (How many t-shirts?). If they want a specific quantity, they'll say "Quiero dos" (I want two) or "Deme tres" (Give me three).

Bargaining often involves numbers. Start with "Son diez" (They are ten). Customer: "Ocho?" (Eight?). You: "Nueve" (Nine). Customer: "De acuerdo" (Agreed). Practice with real prices from your stall. Write numbers 1-20 on a cardboard and hang it. Use your fingers to show numbers as you say themthis helps memory. In Equatorial Guinea, the currency is the Central African CFA franc, so you'd say "cuesta mil francos" (it costs one thousand francs). But the numbers 1-20 remain the same. Drill daily: count your stock, price items aloud, and role-play with a friend. Soon, numbers will flow without thinking.

3. Days, Weeks, and Months for Market Schedules

Knowing days, weeks, and months helps you plan market days and talk about schedules with customers and suppliers. The days of the week in Spanish are: lunes (Monday), martes (Tuesday), mircoles (Wednesday), jueves (Thursday), viernes (Friday), sbado (Saturday), domingo (Sunday). Note they are not capitalized. To say "on Monday," use "el lunes." For "every Monday," say "todos los lunes." If your market is held on Wednesdays and Saturdays, you can tell a customer: "El mercado es los mircoles y sbados" (The market is on Wednesdays and Saturdays).

Weeks are "semanas." "This week" is "esta semana," "next week" is "la prxima semana," and "last week" is "la semana pasada." Months are: enero (January), febrero (February), marzo (March), abril (April), mayo (May), junio (June), julio (July), agosto (August), septiembre (September), octubre (October), noviembre (November), diciembre (December). Also not capitalized. To say "in January," use "en enero." For "the first of May," say "el primero de mayo." For other dates, use "el dos de mayo" (the second of May), etc.

In many African markets, harvest seasons and holidays affect trade. For example, you might say: "En diciembre, hay muchos clientes" (In December, there are many customers). Or "La cosecha de mango es en marzo" (The mango harvest is in March). Practice by writing your market schedule in Spanish: "Lunes: tomates. Martes: cebollas. Mircoles: descanso" (Monday: tomatoes. Tuesday: onions. Wednesday: rest). Use a calendar and label it in Spanish. Ask a Spanish-speaking friend to quiz you: "Qu da es hoy?" (What day is today?). Answer: "Hoy es jueves" (Today is Thursday). With daily practice, you'll discuss schedules confidently and never miss a market day.

4. Essential Polite Phrases for Negotiation

Negotiation in African markets is an art, and politeness opens doors. Start with "Por favor" (please) and "Gracias" (thank you). When you want to make an offer, say "Le puedo ofrecer..." (I can offer you...). For example: "Le puedo ofrecer diez dlares" (I can offer you ten dollars). If the customer says "Es muy caro" (It's too expensive), respond politely: "Entiendo, pero es de buena calidad" (I understand, but it's good quality). You can also say "Cunto quiere pagar?" (How much do you want to pay?).

To ask for a discount, say "Me puede hacer un descuento?" (Can you give me a discount?). If you're the seller and want to accept, say "De acuerdo" (Agreed) or "Est bien" (It's fine). If you need to decline, say "Lo siento, no puedo" (I'm sorry, I can't). Other useful phrases: "Puede ser un poco menos?" (Can it be a little less?), "Es mi ltima oferta" (It's my final offer), "Es un buen precio" (It's a good price). When you reach an agreement, say "Trato hecho" (Deal done).

In many African cultures, negotiation is friendly and relationship-based. Use "amigo" (friend) or "hermano" (brother) to build rapport, but be respectful. If you're in Equatorial Guinea, you might hear "Cunto es lo menos?" (What's the lowest price?). Practice with a partner: one sells a basket, the other bargains. Use real amounts. Record yourself and listen for politeness. Write these phrases on a card and keep it at your stall. Remember, a smile and a polite tone are as important as the words. With practice, you'll negotiate confidently and fairly.

5. Saying Goodbye and Thanking Customers

Ending a transaction well ensures customers return. After a sale, always say "Gracias" (Thank you). For more emphasis, say "Muchas gracias" (Thank you very much). If the customer bought something, you can add "Que lo disfrute" (Enjoy it) or "Que tenga un buen da" (Have a good day). When saying goodbye, use "Adis" (Goodbye) or "Hasta luego" (See you later). For a more formal goodbye, say "Hasta pronto" (See you soon) or "Nos vemos" (We'll see each other). If you want to invite them back, say "Vuelva pronto" (Come back soon) or "Le espero" (I'll wait for you).

If the customer didn't buy, still thank them: "Gracias por venir" (Thank you for coming). You can add "Vuelva cuando quiera" (Come back whenever you want). This leaves a positive impression. In many African markets, building a loyal customer base is key. Use the customer's name if you know it: "Gracias, seora" (Thank you, ma'am) or "Gracias, joven" (Thank you, young man). For a group, say "Gracias a todos" (Thank you everyone).

Practice these phrases with a friend. Role-play: one is the vendor, one is the customer. After the transaction, the vendor says goodbye and thanks. Then switch roles. Write the phrases in a notebook and review them daily. At your stall, use them consistently. You'll notice customers smiling and returning. In Equatorial Guinea, you might hear "Que Dios le bendiga" (God bless you) as a warm goodbye. You can use it too if it fits your context. Remember, a genuine thank you and a warm goodbye cost nothing but build trust and repeat business.

6. Practice: Role-play a Market Stall Interaction

Now put everything together in a role-play. Imagine you are a vendor at a busy market in Equatorial Guinea or a market in your country where Spanish is spoken. You sell mangoes, phone credit, and fabric. A customer approaches. You greet: "Buenos das! Bienvenido!" The customer replies: "Buenos das. Cmo est?" You answer: "Muy bien, gracias. En qu puedo ayudarle?" (Very well, thanks. How can I help you?). The customer asks: "Cunto cuestan los mangos?" (How much do the mangoes cost?). You say: "Cuesta un dlar cada uno" (It costs one dollar each). Customer: "Es muy caro. Me puede hacer un descuento?" You: "Si lleva cinco, le doy un descuento. Son cuatro dlares" (If you take five, I'll give you a discount. They are four dollars). Customer: "De acuerdo. Quiero cinco" (Agreed. I want five). You: "Aqu tiene. Son cuatro dlares" (Here you go. That's four dollars). Customer pays. You: "Gracias. Que tenga un buen da" (Thank you. Have a good day). Customer: "Gracias. Hasta luego" (Thanks. See you later).

Practice this script with a partner. Swap roles. Use real items and real money (or pretend). Time yourself: a good interaction takes 1-2 minutes. Repeat until you can do it without looking at notes. Then change the items and prices. For example, sell fabric: "Cunto cuesta el metro?" (How much per meter?). Or phone credit: "Quiero diez dlares de crdito" (I want ten dollars of credit).

Record yourself on your phone and listen. Check pronunciation and politeness. Ask a Spanish speaker to give feedback. If you don't have a partner, practice in front of a mirror. The goal is to feel confident and natural. After this module, you should be able to welcome customers, discuss prices, negotiate politely, and say goodbye warmly. Keep practicing daily at your stall. Within a month, you'll handle basic Spanish interactions with ease and grow your customer base.

<h2>3. Days, Weeks, and Months for Market Schedules</h2>
<p>Customers often ask when fresh stock arrives, when the market opens, or when you will return. Use these phrases to confirm schedules and avoid missed sales.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Days:</strong> lunes, martes, mircoles, jueves, viernes, sbado, domingo.</li>
<li><strong>Weeks:</strong> esta semana (this week), la prxima semana (next week), la semana pasada (last week).</li>
<li><strong>Months:</strong> enero, febrero, marzo, abril, mayo, junio, julio, agosto, septiembre, octubre, noviembre, diciembre.</li>
<li><strong>Useful phrases:</strong> El mercado abre el lunes. / La prxima entrega es en marzo. / Vuelvo el viernes.</li>
</ul>
<p>In African cross-border markets, it is common to trade in multiple currencies and follow both local and international calendars. Always confirm the date and time in writing or by phone if the order is large.</p>

<h2>4. Essential Polite Phrases for Negotiation</h2>
<p>Negotiation should protect your margin while keeping the customer respectful. Polite phrases reduce tension and help you say no without losing the sale.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Por favor</strong> please.</li>
<li><strong>Gracias</strong> thank you.</li>
<li><strong>Lo siento</strong> I am sorry.</li>
<li><strong>Puede repetir, por favor?</strong> Can you repeat, please?</li>
<li><strong>Puede hablar ms despacio?</strong> Can you speak more slowly?</li>
<li><strong>Es mi mejor precio.</strong> It is my best price.</li>
<li><strong>Si compra dos, le doy un descuento.</strong> If you buy two, I will give you a discount.</li>
<li><strong>No puedo bajar ms.</strong> I cannot go lower.</li>
<li><strong>Est de acuerdo?</strong> Do you agree?</li>
</ul>

<h2>5. Saying Goodbye and Thanking Customers</h2>
<p>Closing well creates repeat business. Thank the customer, confirm the sale, and invite them back.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Muchas gracias por su compra.</strong> Thank you very much for your purchase.</li>
<li><strong>Que tenga un buen da!</strong> Have a good day!</li>
<li><strong>Adis! Hasta luego.</strong> Goodbye! See you later.</li>
<li><strong>Vuelva pronto.</strong> Come back soon.</li>
<li><strong>Aqu estaremos maana.</strong> We will be here tomorrow.</li>
</ul>

<p>Use this script to practice with a partner. One person is the trader, the other is the customer. Repeat until the phrases feel natural.</p>

<li><strong>Trader:</strong> Buenos das! Bienvenido! En qu puedo ayudarle?</li>
<li><strong>Customer:</strong> Buenos das. Cunto cuesta el kilo de arroz?</li>
<li><strong>Trader:</strong> Cuesta dos dlares el kilo. Es arroz de buena calidad.</li>
<li><strong>Customer:</strong> Y si compro cinco kilos?</li>
<li><strong>Trader:</strong> Si compra cinco kilos, le doy un descuento. Son nueve dlares.</li>
<li><strong>Customer:</strong> Est bien. Puede entregar el lunes?</li>
<li><strong>Trader:</strong> S, el lunes por la maana. Est de acuerdo?</li>
<li><strong>Customer:</strong> S, de acuerdo. Gracias.</li>
<li><strong>Trader:</strong> Muchas gracias por su compra. Que tenga un buen da! Vuelva pronto.</li>
</ol>

<h3 style="color: #b00020; margin-top: 0;"> Safety Warning: Currency, Weight, and Contract Clarity</h3>
<p>In cross-border and multilingual markets, misunderstandings about currency, weight, and payment terms can cause financial loss or conflict. Always confirm the currency (dlares, euros, francos, nairas, cedis, etc.), the unit of measure (kilo, litro, saco, caja), and the total price before the customer pays. Write the agreed price and quantity on paper or a phone screen. For large orders, use a simple written receipt with date, quantity, unit price, total, and delivery date. Do not rely only on spoken Spanish if numbers are large or if the customer speaks a different first language. If you feel unsafe or pressured, pause the negotiation and ask a trusted colleague or market official to help.</p>
</div>
