1. Varroa Mites: Monitoring and Control

Varroa mites are tiny reddish-brown parasites that attach to bees and suck their fat body, weakening them and spreading viruses. In Africa, Varroa destructor is common in many regions, though some areas still remain free. You must monitor regularly. Use a varroa screen (a white tray with a mesh top) placed under the brood box. Coat the tray with cooking oil or petroleum jelly. After 24 hours, count the mites that fall through. If you find more than 10 mites per day, treatment is needed. Another method is the sugar shake: put 200 bees in a jar with 2 tablespoons of powdered sugar, shake for 2 minutes, then count mites on the jar bottom. More than 5 mites per 100 bees means action.

Control options for African beekeepers include cultural and chemical methods. Cultural: use screened bottom boards to let mites fall out, and remove drone brood (mites prefer drone cells) every 24 days. Chemical: in many African countries, amitraz-based strips (e.g., Apivar) are available. Always follow label instructions. For organic options, oxalic acid dribble (3.2% solution) can be used in broodless periods. Formic acid pads also work but are temperature-sensitive. Never use chemicals during honey flow; remove honey supers first.

Integrated approach: combine monitoring, cultural controls, and minimal chemical use. Breed for hygienic behavior: bees that uncap and remove infected brood. In Africa, local bees like Apis mellifera scutellata are often more resistant, but still need help. Requeen with resistant stock if available. Keep records of mite counts and treatments. Treat when thresholds are exceeded, not on a calendar. Always wear protective gear when applying treatments.

Remember, Varroa is a serious threat. Without control, colonies can die within 1-2 years. But with regular monitoring and timely action, you can keep your bees healthy and productive.

2. Nosema and Other Diseases

Nosema is a fungal disease caused by Nosema apis or Nosema ceranae. It infects the bee gut, causing dysentery (diarrhea), poor brood rearing, and slow colony decline. You may see brown streaks of feces on the hive entrance and combs. Bees become sluggish and die early. To diagnose, you need a microscope. Collect 30 forager bees, remove their guts, and examine under 400x magnification. Nosema spores appear as oval rice grains. If more than 1 million spores per bee, it's a severe infection.

Other diseases include American Foulbrood (AFB) and European Foulbrood (EFB). AFB is caused by a spore-forming bacterium; infected larvae turn coffee-brown and stringy when probed with a stick. The smell is foul. EFB is less severe; larvae turn yellow and twisted. Chalkbrood is a fungal disease where larvae become white and chalky. Sacbrood virus makes larvae look like a sac of fluid. In Africa, these diseases are present but often less common than in temperate zones due to continuous brood rearing and genetic resistance.

Prevention is key. Keep strong colonies: weak bees get sick. Ensure good ventilation to reduce moisture. Provide clean water and avoid feeding honey from unknown sources, which can spread spores. Sterilize tools between hives with a flame or 70% alcohol. Replace old combs every 3-4 years. For Nosema, improve nutrition and reduce stress. Some beekeepers use fumagillin, but it's banned in many countries. In Africa, focus on management: move hives to sunny, dry locations. For AFB, burn infected frames and bees if allowed; otherwise, shake bees onto new foundation with a new queen. Antibiotics like oxytetracycline can suppress EFB but not AFB.

Monitor regularly. If you see unusual larvae or dysentery, act fast. Quarantine sick hives. Report notifiable diseases to local authorities. Healthy bees are your best defense.

3. Small Hive Beetles and Wax Moths

Small hive beetles (SHB) are black or dark brown beetles about 5-7 mm long. They invade weak hives, lay eggs in cracks, and larvae (white, spiky) tunnel through combs, fermenting honey and causing slime. In Africa, Aethina tumida is native and widespread. Strong colonies usually keep them in check, but weak ones can be overwhelmed. Monitor with beetle traps: use a small plastic container with a lid and holes, filled with apple cider vinegar and a drop of dish soap. Place it on the bottom board. Check weekly. If you find more than 20 beetles per hive, take action.

Control: maintain strong colonies. Reduce hive entrance size to 2-3 cm to help bees guard. Remove burr comb and propolis that create hiding spots. Use diatomaceous earth under the hive if legal. For severe infestations, remove frames and freeze them for 24 hours to kill eggs and larvae. You can also use beneficial nematodes (Steinernema carpocapsae) in soil around the hive to kill pupating beetles. Chemical treatments like CheckMite+ are available in some countries but follow regulations.

Wax moths (Galleria mellonella and Achroia grisella) are more of a storage pest. Adult moths lay eggs in dark, warm places. Larvae (white caterpillars) spin silk tunnels and eat wax, leaving webbing and frass. They rarely kill strong colonies but destroy stored combs. Prevent by storing combs in a dry, well-lit, ventilated place. Freeze combs for 48 hours before storage. Use airtight containers with paramoth (paradichlorobenzene) crystals, but never on honey supers. In Africa, wax moths are common in neglected hives. Keep colonies strong and remove unused combs. Inspect stored equipment monthly.

Integrated management: combine strong hives, traps, and sanitation. Avoid leaving old combs lying around. Harvest honey promptly. If you see slime or webbing, act immediately.

4. Bears, Skunks, and Other Predators

In Africa, predators vary by region. Honey badgers (ratels) are the most notorious. They are strong, intelligent, and love honey and brood. They can destroy a hive in minutes. To protect against honey badgers, use elevated hive stands at least 1.5 meters high with smooth metal poles that they cannot climb. Or use electric fencing (if available) with 2-3 strands at 15 cm, 30 cm, and 45 cm heights. Keep grass short around hives to remove cover. Some beekeepers use reinforced hive boxes with metal straps. In areas with honey badgers, never leave honey or brood exposed.

Skunks and other small mammals (e.g., genets, mongooses) may scratch at hive entrances to eat bees. They leave grass blades or dirt at the entrance. Use a raised hive stand and place a carpet of thorny branches (e.g., acacia) around the base. For skunks, a simple fence of chicken wire (1 meter high) with the bottom 30 cm bent outward prevents digging. In Africa, ants (especially safari ants) can raid hives. Place hive legs in containers of used engine oil or water to create a moat. Refresh oil monthly.

Birds like bee-eaters and shrikes can eat bees, but usually not a major threat unless many birds. Use reflective tape or CDs hanging around the hive to scare them. For lizards and frogs, keep grass short and remove debris. In some areas, monkeys and baboons raid hives. Use strong wire mesh around the apiary and secure lids with straps. Human thieves are also a concern: brand your hives and keep them near your home.

Integrated approach: combine physical barriers, sanitation, and monitoring. Inspect hives weekly for signs of disturbance. If you find a predator, act quickly. For honey badgers, electric fencing is most effective. In all cases, keep hives strong and healthy; weak colonies attract predators. Work with neighbors to control predators collectively.

5. Integrated Pest Management

Integrated Pest Management (IPM) is a smart, low-cost approach that combines multiple methods to keep pests and diseases below damaging levels. It avoids relying on one solution, especially chemicals. The steps are: monitor, identify, decide, act, and evaluate. First, monitor your hives every 7-10 days. Keep a notebook. Record pest counts (e.g., varroa mites per day, beetle numbers), disease signs, and predator damage. Use sticky boards, sugar shakes, and visual inspections. Know your enemy: identify the pest correctly. A wax moth larva is different from a small hive beetle larva.

Set action thresholds. For varroa, treat when you find >10 mites per day. For small hive beetles, >20 beetles per hive. For Nosema, >1 million spores per bee. These are guidelines; adjust based on your local conditions. When thresholds are exceeded, choose the least toxic method first. Cultural controls: keep strong colonies, requeen with resistant stock, remove drone brood, maintain good ventilation, and sanitize tools. Mechanical controls: traps for beetles, screens for varroa, electric fences for bears. Biological controls: beneficial nematodes for SHB, and encourage natural enemies like ants that eat wax moth eggs (but not bee-eating ants).

Chemical controls are a last resort. If needed, use soft chemicals like oxalic acid, formic acid, or thymol. Always follow label instructions, wear protective gear, and never treat during honey flow. Rotate chemicals to prevent resistance. In Africa, many synthetic pesticides are banned or unavailable; focus on cultural and mechanical methods. Also, consider local remedies: some beekeepers use tobacco leaves or neem oil, but these are not scientifically proven and can harm bees. Test on a small scale first.

Evaluate your results. Did the pest numbers drop? Did the colony recover? Adjust your plan. IPM saves money, protects bees, and keeps honey safe. It requires regular attention but pays off with healthier hives and better harvests.

6. Using Treatments Safely

Any treatment you apply to a hive can harm bees, contaminate honey, or harm you. Safety must come first. Before using any chemical, read the label carefully. Understand the active ingredient, dosage, and withdrawal period. In Africa, many products are sold informally; only use approved treatments from reputable suppliers. Wear protective clothing: gloves (nitrile or rubber), goggles, long sleeves, long pants, and a respirator if dust or fumes are present. Never eat, drink, or smoke while handling treatments. Wash hands and clothes after use. Store chemicals in original containers, locked away from children and animals.

For varroa treatments: oxalic acid dribble is common. Mix 3.2% oxalic acid in sugar syrup (1:1). Apply 5 ml per bee space between frames. Do this when no brood is present (e.g., after swarming or in dearth period). Wear gloves and goggles; oxalic acid is toxic if ingested. Formic acid pads: use only when temperatures are between 10-30Â°C. Higher temperatures release fumes that can kill bees. Place pad on top bars, not near honey supers. Amitraz strips: hang between brood frames, remove after 6 weeks. Do not use during honey flow. Withdrawal period is usually 2-4 weeks.

For Nosema: fumagillin is banned in many countries. Instead, use good management. For AFB: burn infected hives if required by law. Antibiotics like oxytetracycline can be used for EFB, but follow withdrawal (usually 4 weeks). Never use antibiotics preventively. For small hive beetles: CheckMite+ strips contain coumaphos; use only as directed, and remove before honey supers. For wax moths: paramoth crystals in storage, never on live hives.

Always have a first aid kit nearby. If you spill chemicals on skin, wash with soap and water. If in eyes, rinse for 15 minutes. If swallowed, seek medical help immediately. Keep records of all treatments: date, product, dose, hive number. This helps you track effectiveness and withdrawal times. Remember, honey sold with chemical residues can be rejected. Your health and your bees' health depend on safe practices.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Pests, Diseases, and Predators in African Bee Farming

Honey is money. Every colony lost to pests, disease, or predators is lost revenue, lost pollination services, and lost credibility with buyers. This practical reference gives you the field-ready facts you need to protect your hives, your harvest, and your bottom line. It is written for African conditions: smallholder apiaries, limited access to veterinary inputs, variable climates, and markets that reward clean, residue-free honey.

Use this lesson as a bench-top guide. Print it, laminate it, and keep it in your honey house. The numbers, thresholds, and treatment cautions here are drawn from international apicultural research and adapted to African realities.

Varroa Mites: Monitoring and Control

Varroa destructor is the single most economically damaging parasite of honey bees worldwide. In Africa, Varroa is present in most regions, though African honey bee subspecies (Apis mellifera scutellata and others) often tolerate higher loads than European bees. Tolerance is not immunity. Untreated high loads reduce brood, shorten worker lifespan, and spread viruses such as Deformed Wing Virus (DWV).

Monitoring. Use a sugar roll or alcohol wash on 300 adult bees from the brood nest. Sample monthly during the active season. In Africa, treat when mite counts exceed 3â5 mites per 100 bees, or when you see >5% of brood cells infested. Sticky-board counts are less reliable but useful for trend monitoring.

Control options. Cultural methods include screened bottom boards, drone brood removal, and maintaining strong colonies. Chemical options include oxalic acid (dribble or vaporization), formic acid, and thymol-based products. In many African countries, registered varroacides are scarce or expensive. Oxalic acid is often the most accessible and residue-safe option when used correctly. Always follow label directions and observe withdrawal periods.

Nosema and Other Diseases

Nosema apis and Nosema ceranae are microsporidian gut parasites. N. ceranae is now dominant in many African regions and is linked to colony depopulation, reduced honey yield, and increased winter mortality. Diagnosis requires microscopy; field signs include dysentery (brown streaks on combs and hive entrance), sluggish bees, and dwindling colonies.

Other diseases to watch: American Foulbrood (AFB), European Foulbrood (EFB), chalkbrood, and sacbrood virus. AFB is a notifiable disease in many countries. Burn infected equipment where legally required. Never feed honey from unknown sources.

Management. Replace old combs, ensure good ventilation, avoid overcrowding, and maintain strong colonies. For Nosema, some beekeepers use fumagillin where legal, but it is banned in many markets. The safest business approach is prevention: clean water, good nutrition, and comb rotation.

Small Hive Beetles and Wax Moths

Aethina tumida (small hive beetle, SHB) is native to sub-Saharan Africa and has spread globally. Adult beetles lay eggs in combs; larvae tunnel through honey and pollen, causing fermentation and slime. SHB is a major post-harvest pest in honey houses. Wax moths (Galleria mellonella and Achroia grisella) attack weak colonies and stored combs, leaving webbing and frass.

Control. Keep colonies strong. Reduce hive entrance size. Use beetle traps (oil or diatomaceous earth). Harvest honey promptly and store combs in dry, well-lit, ventilated rooms. Freeze stored combs for 24â48 hours to kill eggs and larvae. Never stack wet supers.

Bears, Skunks, and Other Predators

In Africa, honey badgers (Mellivora capensis) are the most destructive vertebrate predator. They tear hives apart for brood and honey. Other threats include baboons, monkeys, ants (especially safari ants), and toads. In some regions, bears are absent, but in North Africa and parts of East Africa, human-wildlife conflict is rising.

Deterrence. Elevate hives on stands at least 1 m high. Use metal sheeting around stand legs to stop ants and small predators. Electric fencing is effective where legal and affordable. Guard dogs, night watches, and community-based deterrents work in high-risk areas. For ants, use ash, oil, or grease bandsânever toxic pesticides near hives.

Integrated Pest Management (IPM)

IPM is a business strategy, not just a farming method. It reduces input costs, protects honey quality, and builds resilience. The IPM pyramid for African beekeeping:

  1. Prevention: strong colonies, good nutrition, clean equipment, apiary hygiene.
  2. Monitoring: regular inspections, mite counts, disease checks, record-keeping.
  3. Mechanical control: traps, screens, comb removal, physical barriers.
  4. Biological control: encourage natural enemies where safe; use resistant bee stock.
  5. Chemical control: last resort, targeted, legal, and residue-aware.

Using Treatments Safely

Every treatment is a business decision. Residues can disqualify your honey from premium markets. Always check national registration and export requirements. Never treat during honey flow. Observe withdrawal periods strictly. Wear protective equipment. Store chemicals away from food and children.
