Lessons

1. Reading Assembly Drawings

Assembly drawings show how individual sheet metal parts fit together to make a finished product. Unlike detail drawings that show one part, assembly drawings include multiple parts, often with numbers called balloon references. Each balloon points to a part, and a parts list on the drawing tells you the part number, description, quantity, and material. For example, a drawing for a metal cabinet might have balloons 1 to 10, with part 1 being the back panel (1.2 mm galvanised sheet), part 2 the side panels (2 off), and so on. You must learn to read three views: front, top, and side. In Africa, many workshops use A3 or A4 printed drawings, but you can also work from a PDF on a phone. Always check the scale often 1:10 or 1:5 and use a ruler to verify dimensions if the drawing is printed to scale. Look for hidden lines (dashed) that show edges you cannot see, and centre lines (long-short-long) for holes and symmetry. Before you start cutting, identify the datum the reference point from which all measurements are taken, often a corner or a hole. Then list the tools you need: measuring tape, try square, scriber, and centre punch. A common mistake is to assume all dimensions are in millimetres; in Africa, most sheet metal drawings use mm, but some older ones use inches. If in doubt, ask. Also, check for notes like 'break sharp edges' or 'weld all around'. These notes are as important as the dimensions. Practice by taking a simple assembly drawing of a box and naming each part. Soon you will read drawings as easily as a newspaper.

2. Jigs and Fixtures for Assembly

A jig holds the parts and guides the tool, while a fixture only holds the parts. In sheet metal assembly, jigs and fixtures save time, ensure repeatability, and improve quality. For example, when drilling holes in a series of identical panels, a drill jig with hardened steel bushings lets you drill accurately without marking each hole. For welding a frame, a fixture made from angle iron and clamps keeps the parts square and flat. In African workshops, you can make simple jigs from wood, steel offcuts, or even concrete blocks. Start with a flat base a steel plate at least 6 mm thick works well. Weld or bolt stops and clamps to locate parts. Use toggle clamps for quick loading and unloading. For small production runs, a wooden jig with hardwood blocks is cheap and effective. When making a jig, consider: locating points (pins or edges), clamping method, and clearance for tools. For example, to assemble 50 identical metal boxes, build a fixture that holds the base and two sides at 90 degrees. Use a magnetic square or a welded corner block to set the angle. Then tack weld. The fixture ensures every box is identical. Remember that jigs and fixtures must be robust sheet metal can spring back, so clamp firmly. Also, design for easy loading: if it takes longer to clamp than to weld, you lose time. Maintain your jigs: keep them clean, replace worn pins, and store them properly. A good jig can last for thousands of parts. In Africa, where labour is often manual, a simple jig can triple your output. Start with one fixture for your most common product, then expand.

3. Duplex and Pittsburgh Seams

Duplex and Pittsburgh seams are interlocking joints used to join two sheet metal edges without welding or rivets. They are common in ductwork, roofing, and containers. The Pittsburgh seam is a single lock seam where one edge is folded over the other and then flattened. The duplex seam is a double lock seam stronger and more airtight. To make a Pittsburgh seam, you need a folding bar, a mallet, and a seam set. First, bend a 6 mm flange on one edge (the male part) and a 10 mm flange on the other (the female part). Then hook the male into the female and use a hammer and seam set to close the lock. For a duplex seam, you fold the edge over twice before locking. In Africa, you can make these seams by hand with a mallet and a straight edge, but a hand-cranked roll former speeds up the process. The key is to keep the folds uniform use a marking gauge to scribe the fold lines. For 0.8 mm galvanised sheet, a 6 mm fold is typical. Practice on scrap pieces until your seams are tight and flat. These seams are not waterproof unless you add solder or sealant. For roofing, apply a bead of bitumen or silicone before closing the seam. Duplex seams are used on gutters and downpipes. Pittsburgh seams are common on rectangular ductwork. When assembling, work from one end to the other, closing the seam gradually with a hammer. Avoid hitting too hard you can stretch the metal. A good seam should have no gaps and lie flat. With practice, you can make a 1-metre seam in under two minutes.

4. Riveted and Bolted Assemblies

Rivets and bolts are removable or permanent fasteners for sheet metal. Rivets are permanent; bolts can be removed. For riveting, you need a drill, rivet gun (or hammer and bucking bar), and rivets. The most common in African workshops are blind rivets (pop rivets) because they only need access from one side. Use a drill bit the same diameter as the rivet typically 3.2 mm, 4.0 mm, or 4.8 mm. Drill holes in both parts, align them, insert the rivet, and squeeze the gun. For solid rivets, you need a bucking bar on the back side and a rivet set on the front. Hammer the rivet tail to form a second head. Bolts are used where disassembly is needed. Use M6 or M8 bolts with nuts and washers. For sheet metal, use large washers to spread the load. Always drill holes slightly larger than the bolt 0.5 mm clearance. For a strong joint, use at least two bolts per edge. In Africa, galvanised bolts and nuts are common for outdoor use. When bolting, tighten evenly use a spanner, not pliers. For rivets, space them at 4 to 6 times the diameter. For example, 4 mm rivets should be about 20 mm apart. Edge distance should be at least twice the diameter. Always deburr holes with a countersink or larger drill bit. Riveted joints are strong in shear but weak in tension. Bolted joints can be tightened later. For a watertight joint, add a gasket or sealant. Practice on scrap: drill, rivet, and test. A good riveted joint will fail only when the metal tears, not the rivet.

5. Applying Gaskets and Seals

Gaskets and seals prevent leaks of air, water, or gas in sheet metal assemblies. Common materials are rubber, cork, felt, silicone, and foam tape. For ducts, use closed-cell foam tape or neoprene gaskets. For water tanks, use EPDM rubber or silicone. Before applying a gasket, clean the surfaces with a rag and solvent (acetone or alcohol). Remove all oil, dust, and rust. Cut the gasket to size with scissors or a sharp knife. For bolt holes, use a hollow punch or drill the gasket while clamped to the metal. Apply contact adhesive to both surfaces if the gasket is not self-adhesive. Wait until tacky, then press together. For flanged joints, place the gasket between the flanges, align the bolt holes, and tighten bolts evenly in a star pattern. Do not overtighten you can split the gasket. For seams, use a bead of silicone sealant. Apply a 3 mm bead along the seam, then close and rivet or bolt. Wipe excess immediately. In Africa, silicone sealants are widely available. For high-temperature applications, use red silicone. For potable water, use food-grade silicone. Gaskets must be compressed by about 30% to seal properly. For example, a 3 mm thick rubber gasket should compress to about 2 mm when bolted. Check for gaps by shining a light from behind. If you see light, add more sealant or tighten. Store gaskets flat, away from sunlight and heat. Replace gaskets whenever you open a joint they are not reusable. A good seal saves energy and prevents corrosion.

6. Quality Control in Assembly

Quality control (QC) means checking your work at every stage to ensure it meets the drawing and the customer's needs. In sheet metal assembly, QC starts with incoming materials: check sheet thickness with a micrometer (e.g., 1.2 mm 0.05 mm), check for rust or dents. Then check each part before assembly: dimensions, squareness, hole positions. Use a tape measure, try square, and calipers. During assembly, check alignment, gaps, and tightness. After assembly, inspect the finished product: overall dimensions, flatness, seam tightness, and function. For a metal box, check that the lid fits, hinges operate, and there are no sharp edges. Use a checklist. For example: 1) All parts present? 2) All holes drilled? 3) Rivets tight? 4) Seams closed? 5) Gaskets in place? 6) No distortion? 7) Clean and deburred? Record results. If a defect is found, fix it or scrap the part. Do not pass defects downstream. In Africa, where customers often pay cash, reputation is everything. A simple QC system: use a go/no-go gauge for critical dimensions. For example, a 10 mm pin should fit into a 10.2 mm hole but not a 9.8 mm hole. For angles, use a protractor or a fixed template. Train one person as QC inspector. Keep a logbook of defects and fixes. Common defects: misaligned holes, warped panels, loose rivets, leaky seams. Root causes: dull drill bits, wrong clamp pressure, dirty surfaces. Fix the cause, not just the symptom. A good QC program reduces rework and increases profit. Even a one-person workshop can do QC by checking each job against a sample.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Assembly and Fabrication for Sheet Metal Work

In African sheet metal workshopsfrom Nairobi's Industrial Area to Lagos's Ladipo market, from Kigali's Special Economic Zone to Johannesburg's Germistonassembly is where raw cut metal becomes sellable product. A duct run for a hospital, a grain silo for a cooperative, a cold-room panel for a fish trader, a bus body for a transport SACCO: all depend on how well joints are made, how accurately fixtures hold parts, and how consistently seals keep out water, dust, and pests. This reference lesson gives you the practical numbers, methods, and checks that determine whether your fabrication business wins repeat contracts or loses money on rework.

Assembly and fabrication in sheet metal work is not just "joining pieces." It is a business capability. Clients pay for fit, finish, and function. If your seams leak, your rivets loosen, or your gaskets fail in the first rainy season, you will not get the next order. The lessons in this moduleReading Assembly Drawings, Jigs and Fixtures for Assembly, Duplex and Pittsburgh Seams, Riveted and Bolted Assemblies, Applying Gaskets and Seals, and Quality Control in Assemblycome together here as a single practical discipline.

Why Assembly Discipline Matters in African Markets

African sheet metal businesses often compete on price, but price competition destroys margins when rework is high. A duct fabricator in Kampala who delivers a leaking system loses the maintenance contract. A water tank maker in Tamale whose seams split after the first harmattan loses the village order. A cold-room installer in Mombasa whose gaskets fail loses the hotel client to a competitor. Assembly quality is the difference between a one-off job and a repeat business.

Assembly also affects labour cost. If you need highly skilled workers for every joint, your wage bill rises. Jigs and fixtures reduce skill dependence and increase output per worker. That is a direct business gain: more units per day, lower unit cost, faster delivery, happier clients.

Reading Assembly Drawings

Assembly drawings show how parts fit together, not just what each part looks like. In African workshops, drawings are often photocopied, faded, or redrawn by hand. You must read them accurately. Key elements: bill of materials (BOM), part numbers, section views, detail views, dimensions, tolerances, and notes on fasteners, seals, and finishes. Always check the revision numberbuilding to an old revision is a common and expensive mistake.

Practical tip: before cutting, lay out all parts on the floor or a clean table and match them to the BOM. If a part is missing or wrong, stop. Do not "make it work" on the fly. That is where rework begins.

Jigs and Fixtures for Assembly

A jig holds the tool; a fixture holds the work. In sheet metal assembly, fixtures position parts for welding, riveting, or bolting. They are essential for repeatability. A simple angle-iron frame with toggle clamps can hold duct sections square while you rivet. A wooden or steel template can position bolt holes consistently.

In low-resource settings, fixtures can be made from scrap steel, hardwood, or even concrete blocks. The principle is the same: locate, clamp, and hold. If you make 50 identical panels, a fixture pays for itself in the first day.

Duplex and Pittsburgh Seams

These are the two workhorse seams in sheet metal assembly. The Pittsburgh lock seam is a folded, interlocked joint used in HVAC ductwork. It is strong, relatively airtight, and can be machine-formed. The duplex seam is a double-folded joint used where weather-tightness matters: roofing, flashing, water tanks, and outdoor enclosures. It is more labour-intensive but more resistant to water penetration.

For African conditions, duplex seams are preferred for anything exposed to rain or dust. Pittsburgh seams are fine for indoor ductwork but should be sealed with mastic or tape if used outdoors.

Riveted and Bolted Assemblies

Rivets are fast, permanent, and require only a hammer or rivet gun. Bolts are removable and stronger in tension. In African workshops, pop rivets are common for ductwork and light panels; bolts are used for structural frames, tank flanges, and machine guards.

Applying Gaskets and Seals

Gaskets and seals keep out water, dust, insects, and air. In African markets, failure of seals is a top complaint in cold rooms, water tanks, and grain storage. Common gasket materials: neoprene, EPDM, silicone, cork, and felt. Selection depends on temperature, chemicals, and UV exposure.

SAFETY WARNING: Sheet metal edges are razor-sharp. Always wear cut-resistant gloves when handling, fitting, or assembling sheet metal. When riveting or bolting, wear safety glassesrivet stems and metal chips can fly. When using sealants and adhesives, work in a ventilated area and avoid skin contact. In hot African climates, dehydration and heat stress increase accident risk: drink water, take breaks, and never rush assembly work. A cut or eye injury can end a worker's livelihood and cost your business far more than the time saved by skipping PPE.

Quality Control in Assembly

Quality control is not a final inspection; it is a process. Check at every stage: incoming material, cutting, forming, assembly, and final finish. Use simple go/no-go gauges, tape measures, squares, and visual checks. Record defects and fix root causes, not just symptoms.

For African workshops, a simple QC log book is enough. Record: date, job number, part, defect found, action taken, and who checked. Over time, this data shows which processes need improvement. It also proves to clients that you take quality seriouslya strong selling point.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Read the drawing before cutting. Confirm revision number, BOM, and tolerances.
  2. Lay out all parts first. Match to BOM; stop if anything is missing or wrong.
  3. Use a fixture for repeat work. Locate, clamp, holdthen assemble.
  4. Choose the right seam. Pittsburgh for indoor ducts; duplex for weather-exposed work.
  5. Match rivet or bolt to thickness. Use Table 1 as a guide; do not oversize or undersize.
  6. Select gasket for the application. Use Table 2; check temperature and chemical resistance.
  7. Wear PPE every time. Gloves, glasses, and ventilation are non-negotiable.
  8. Record QC checks. Keep a log; fix root causes, not just symptoms.
