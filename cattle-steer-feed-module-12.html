🐄 Beef Production Economics and Systems

Lesson 12.1~12 min

Feedlot Economics and Feed Efficiency

In this lesson
  • Calculate cost of gain per kilogram from feed conversion ratio and feed price
  • Judge whether an extra day on feed adds or destroys margin
  • Value a feed additive or implant against its published performance response

A feedlot is not a cattle business. It is a feed-conversion business that happens to use cattle as the machine. The only question that matters is whether the cost of putting on one more kilogram of live weight is less than what that kilogram sells for. Everything else is detail.

Start with the two published performance anchors. Grain-finished cattle on a high-concentrate diet convert at a feed-to-gain ratio of 5.5 to 6.5 to 1 on a dry matter basis, gain 3.0 to 4.0 lb per day in the terminal phase, and take 150 to 180 days to move from 600 lb to 1,200 lb. Forage requirement is minimal, roughly 5 to 10 percent forage NDF, purely to keep the rumen functioning. Those numbers are your yardstick. If your own pen is converting at 9:1, you are not running a feedlot, you are running an expensive holding pen.

Worked example, using assumed local prices you should replace with your own. Buy a 250 kg feeder steer at 2.20 USD/kg live weight: 250 x 2.20 = 550 USD. Target 400 kg out, so 150 kg of gain.

  1. Days on feed. At the middle of the published range, 3.0 lb/day is 1.36 kg/day. 150 / 1.36 = 110 days.
  2. Feed used. At a 6.0:1 conversion, 150 x 6.0 = 900 kg of dry matter.
  3. Feed cost. At 0.26 USD per kg of ration dry matter, 900 x 0.26 = 234 USD.
  4. Non-feed cost. Labour, water, health, mortality allowance and interest at 0.35 USD per head per day: 110 x 0.35 = 38.50 USD.
  5. Cost of gain. (234 + 38.50) / 150 = 1.82 USD per kg of gain.
  6. Sale. 400 kg at 2.60 USD/kg = 1,040 USD. Total cost 550 + 234 + 38.50 = 822.50 USD. Margin = 217.50 USD per head.

Now test the sensitivity, because this is where feedlots die. If conversion slips from 6.0 to 6.5, feed rises to 975 kg, costing 253.50 USD, and cost of gain rises to 1.95 USD/kg. That single half-point of conversion took 19.50 USD off every animal. On 60 head that is 1,170 USD. If the feeder purchase price rises from 2.20 to 2.45 USD/kg, you have lost 62.50 USD per head before a single day on feed. Feeder price and conversion, in that order, are the two levers.

Energy density drives conversion. A high-concentrate diet at 80 to 90 percent concentrate carries 1.45 to 1.65 Mcal NEg per lb, a moderate 60 to 80 percent concentrate diet 1.30 to 1.45, and a 40 to 60 percent concentrate diet only 1.15 to 1.30. Higher density means faster gain and fewer maintenance days, but the ingredient is dearer. The correct choice depends on the ratio of feed price to beef price, not on habit. Frame size matters too: large-frame cattle can reach an acceptable finish on lower energy density, while small-frame cattle need higher density or they simply lay down fat.

Additives are pure arithmetic. Monensin, used in an estimated 90 to 97 percent of US feedlots at typical inclusion rates of 20 to 30 g per ton of diet, improves feed efficiency by 6.4 percent and average daily gain by 2.5 percent while reducing dry matter intake 1 to 2 percent. Applied to the example, 6.4 percent off 900 kg of feed saves 58 kg, worth 15 USD per head, and the additive itself costs a fraction of that. Growth implants raise ADG 10 to 15 percent and feed efficiency 6 to 8 percent. Beta-agonists such as ractopamine, fed for the last 28 to 42 days, raise ADG 15 to 25 percent without raising intake. All of these are regulated products with country-specific approvals, and implants and ractopamine are restricted or banned in the EU, so check your market and use veterinary guidance before you budget the response.

Concrete African context. A 60-head commercial fattening unit outside Nakuru, Kenya, buying Boran crossbred steers from pastoral areas and selling into Nairobi butcheries, keeps a single laminated card on the office wall with three numbers updated weekly: feeder price per kg, ration cost per kg of dry matter, and finished price per kg. When feeder price rises faster than finished price, they stop buying. That discipline, not the ration, is what keeps them solvent.

Lesson 12.2~12 min

Cost Analysis: Pasture vs Feedlot

In this lesson
  • Compare cost of gain between pasture and feedlot on the same 100 kg of live weight
  • Account for time, land and dry-season weight loss in a pasture budget
  • Design a hybrid grow-then-finish system suited to a seasonal forage supply

Farmers argue about pasture versus feedlot as if it were a philosophy. It is an arithmetic problem with three variables: cost of gain, time to market, and the risk carried in between.

The published performance gap is large. Feedlot cattle convert at 5.5 to 6.5 kg of feed per kg of gain and put on 3.0 to 4.0 lb per day, finishing in 150 to 180 days. Pasture-finished cattle convert at 8:1 to 12:1, roughly 50 percent worse, gain 1.0 to 2.0 lb per day depending on pasture quality, and take 24 to 36 months from birth to finish. Land requirement differs by orders of magnitude: a feedlot needs 150 to 500 sq ft per head, pasture needs 2 to 5 acres per head depending on forage productivity.

But worse conversion does not mean worse economics, because the feed is nearly free. Work the same 150 kg of gain both ways, using assumed local prices.

Feedlot route, from the previous lesson: 110 days, 900 kg of ration dry matter at 0.26 USD/kg = 234 USD, plus 110 days of non-feed cost at 0.35 USD = 38.50 USD. Cost of gain = 272.50 / 150 = 1.82 USD per kg.

Pasture route: at 1.5 lb per day, which is 0.68 kg/day, 150 kg takes 220 days. Costs per head per day: land or grazing fee 0.12, mineral supplement 0.04, herding labour share 0.10, health 0.02. That is 0.28 USD per day. 220 x 0.28 = 61.60 USD. Cost of gain = 61.60 / 150 = 0.41 USD per kg, less than a quarter of the feedlot figure.

So why does anyone build a feedlot? Three reasons the simple sum hides.

  1. Time and capital. The pasture animal ties up its purchase value for 220 days instead of 110. On a 550 USD animal at 18 percent annual interest, the extra 110 days costs about 550 x 0.18 x 110/365 = 29.80 USD. That eats half the apparent advantage.
  2. Dry-season loss. Pasture gain is not linear. In a five-month dry season a steer on standing dead grass, where ADF has climbed and digestibility has collapsed, may hold or lose weight. If he loses 20 kg and has to regain it, that is 20 kg of gain you paid for twice, and the 220-day figure becomes 300 or more.
  3. Carcass outcome. Marbling develops on 6 to 8 weeks of high-concentrate feeding, and external fat cover is laid down in the final 30 to 40 days. Pasture-finished carcasses are leaner, with lower trim loss and higher lean percentage, but often grade lower where the market pays on marbling. If your buyer pays a flat live-weight price, that difference is worth nothing to you. If your buyer pays on grade, it is worth a great deal.

The answer for most African producers is neither pure system but a hybrid: grow cheaply on forage through the wet season, then finish on concentrate for a defined short period. Take a steer from 200 kg to 320 kg on pasture over the rains at 0.41 USD/kg of gain, costing about 49 USD, then finish 320 kg to 400 kg on an 80 day feedlot phase at 1.82 USD/kg, costing about 146 USD. Total 195 USD for 200 kg of gain, an average of 0.98 USD/kg, and the finishing phase lands in the dry season when pasture would have delivered nothing anyway. That is the structure to aim for.

Concrete African context. Ranchers in the Ghanzi district of Botswana have run this split for decades, growing weaners out on Kalahari rangeland and moving them to feedlots near Lobatse for a short finishing phase before slaughter for export. The rangeland does the cheap kilograms; the feedlot does the expensive ones that the market pays a premium for. Applying the same logic to a ten-head smallholder herd changes only the scale, not the arithmetic.

The discipline is to cost your own grazing honestly. Land you own is not free: it has a rental value, and if you would not rent it out for less than 0.12 USD per head per day, that is what it costs you to graze on it.

Lesson 12.3~12 min

Dairy Economics and Milk Price Impact

In this lesson
  • Calculate income over feed cost per cow per day
  • Work out the break-even milk response for one extra kilogram of concentrate
  • Adjust a feeding decision when the milk price or the payment basis changes

A dairy cow's profitability is not measured in litres. It is measured in income over feed cost, which is milk revenue minus feed cost, per cow per day. A cow giving 18 litres on expensive feed can easily earn less than a cow giving 12 litres on cheap forage, and the herd that chases litres without doing this sum usually loses money quietly for years.

Start with income over feed cost, or IOFC. Assumed local prices, which you must replace with yours: milk 0.42 USD per litre, hay at 0.15 USD per kg of dry matter, dairy meal at 0.38 USD per kg.

A cow at 12 litres per day. Revenue = 12 x 0.42 = 5.04 USD. Feed = 8 kg hay DM x 0.15 = 1.20 USD, plus 4 kg dairy meal x 0.38 = 1.52 USD, total 2.72 USD. IOFC = 5.04 - 2.72 = 2.32 USD per cow per day, or about 70 USD per cow per month. That is the number to track weekly.

Now the marginal decision, which is where the money is. Should you feed one more kilogram of dairy meal? Use the energy arithmetic from earlier modules. Energy in milk is given by NEL per kg of milk = 0.360 + (0.0969 x fat percent). At 4.0 percent fat, that is 0.360 + 0.388 = 0.748 Mcal NEL per kg of milk. A dairy meal at 70 percent TDN supplies NEL = (0.0245 x 70) - 0.12 = 1.595 Mcal NEL per kg.

So one extra kilogram of meal carries enough energy for 1.595 / 0.748 = 2.1 kg of milk, if every calorie went to the udder. In practice it does not: some concentrate displaces forage, some goes to body condition, and the response falls as the cow approaches her genetic ceiling. A realistic field response on a smallholder cow is often 1.0 to 1.5 kg of milk per extra kg of meal, and that number is worth measuring on your own cows rather than assuming.

Break-even is simple: feed cost divided by milk price. 0.38 / 0.42 = 0.90. You need at least 0.90 kg of extra milk per extra kg of meal to break even. If you get 1.3 kg, you make 1.3 x 0.42 - 0.38 = 0.17 USD per kg of meal fed. Feed it.

Now drop the milk price to 0.28 USD, as happens in the wet-season flush when everyone's cows peak at once. Break-even becomes 0.38 / 0.28 = 1.36 kg of milk per kg of meal. Your 1.3 kg response now loses 0.02 USD per kg fed. The correct response to a milk price collapse is to cut concentrate and lean harder on forage, and the correct response to a dry-season price spike is the reverse. Most farmers do neither, because they never calculated the break-even.

The payment basis changes the answer too. Milk fat costs energy: at 3.2 percent fat, NEL per kg of milk is 0.360 + (0.0969 x 3.2) = 0.670 Mcal, but at 4.5 percent it is 0.796 Mcal, nearly 19 percent more energy in every litre. If your buyer pays a flat price per litre regardless of composition, high butterfat is a cost you carry and are not paid for. If your buyer pays on solids, it is revenue. Find out which before you change a ration to shift milk composition.

One more line that belongs in the budget: the feed a cow eats when she is not milking. Energy for lactation runs about 50 percent above dry cow maintenance, so a long dry period is cheap feeding but zero revenue, and a long calving interval spreads a whole year's cow cost over less milk.

Concrete African context. Smallholder members of a cooperative cooling plant in Nyeri County, Kenya, typically deliver 8 to 15 litres per cow per day and receive a price that varies substantially between the flush and the dry season. The members who make money keep a small notebook with three columns: litres delivered, kilograms of meal fed, and the price received. Three columns, and it tells them exactly when to feed harder and when to stop.

Lesson 12.4~12 min

Breeding Cow Economics

In this lesson
  • Build an annual cow cost and convert it into a cost per weaned calf
  • Quantify the cost of a long calving interval in USD per day
  • Decide when culling a cow beats feeding her another year

The breeding cow is the least glamorous and most misunderstood enterprise on a cattle farm. She produces one saleable item a year at best, so every cost she incurs over 365 days has to be recovered from that single calf. The arithmetic is unforgiving and almost nobody does it.

Build the annual cow cost first. Assumed local figures, replace with yours.

  1. Dry period feed. Roughly 200 days at 0.25 USD per day of grazing, minerals and hay: 50.00 USD.
  2. Lactating period feed. Roughly 165 days at 0.55 USD per day, because energy for lactation runs about 50 percent above dry cow maintenance: 90.75 USD.
  3. Year-round mineral supplement at 0.05 USD per day: 18.25 USD.
  4. Health, breeding, vaccination, dipping: 15.00 USD.
  5. Share of bull cost, labour, fencing and land overhead: 40.00 USD.

Total: 214.00 USD per cow per year.

Now convert to cost per weaned calf, because that is what you actually sell. At a 65 percent weaning rate, which is common on unmanaged African herds, 214 / 0.65 = 329 USD per weaned calf. At an 80 percent weaning rate, 214 / 0.80 = 268 USD. At 90 percent, 238 USD. If a weaner sells for 250 USD, the 65 percent herd is losing 79 USD on every calf it sells and the 90 percent herd is making 12 USD. Same cows, same feed bill, opposite businesses. Weaning rate, not calf price, is the lever.

Calving interval is the same lever expressed differently. A 365-day interval gives one calf per cow per year. A 450-day interval gives 365/450 = 0.81 calves per year, which raises the cost per calf from 214 to 264 USD before anything else changes. Each extra open day costs roughly the annual cow cost divided by 365, or 214 / 365 = 0.59 USD, plus the compounding delay to every future calf. Eighty-five extra days is 50 USD from one cow, and if you have thirty cows averaging that, it is 1,500 USD a year evaporating with no visible event to blame.

What actually closes the interval is body condition at calving and adequate nutrition through early lactation, which is why the feeding programme is the reproduction programme. Published anchors help you check yours. A 1,200 lb lactating cow producing 10 lb of milk daily needs 2.2 to 2.5 lb of crude protein and 12.8 lb of TDN per day, with calcium 0.35 to 0.45 percent and phosphorus 0.25 to 0.30 percent of diet dry matter, magnesium at least 0.20 percent, and 34,000 IU of vitamin A. A pregnant replacement heifer in early gestation at 1,000 to 1,200 lb needs 1.5 to 1.7 lb of crude protein daily, about 7.8 percent of diet dry matter. A gestating cow needs 34,000 IU of vitamin A per day and a lactating one 54,000 IU, which matters enormously if you winter cattle on weathered hay, since beta-carotene degrades 33 to 50 percent per year of storage.

The bull is a cost centre with a reproduction job. A mature breeding bull at 1,400 to 1,600 lb needs 1.8 to 2.1 lb of crude protein daily, roughly 7.5 to 8.5 percent of diet dry matter. Watch one specific trap: cottonseed meal should be limited to 3 to 5 lb per day for bulls and avoided entirely for 2 to 3 months before the breeding season, because gossypol damages fertility. A cheap protein source that costs you a breeding season is not cheap.

Culling. A cow that fails to wean a calf costs you the full 214 USD with zero revenue. Two consecutive failures is 428 USD, which is usually more than her salvage value. The rule is simple: a cow gets one miss, then she goes, unless you can name the specific and fixed reason she missed.

Concrete African context. Beef herds in Namwala district, Zambia, run on floodplain grazing where wet-season forage is abundant and dry-season forage is poor, and calving interval commonly stretches well beyond 365 days. Producers who moved their calving season so that peak lactation lands on green grass, rather than calving whenever the bull happened to work, lifted weaning rates without buying a single extra bag of feed.

Lesson 12.5~12 min

Vertical Integration and Value-Added Production

In this lesson
  • Cost a local byproduct feed per unit of TDN against a bought compound feed
  • Identify the inclusion limit and safety gate for each byproduct you can access
  • Evaluate whether processing your own milk or meat captures more margin than it costs

Vertical integration means capturing a margin that someone else is currently taking. There are two directions: backwards into your own feed supply, and forwards into processing and selling your own product. Both work, both have a specific trap, and the arithmetic decides which one is worth your capital.

Backwards integration first, because it is cheaper and safer. Every town with a brewery, a maize mill, an oil press or a sugar factory produces byproducts that are feed. The published inclusion limits are your guardrails: distillers dried grain up to 50 percent of diet dry matter, above which sulphur becomes a problem and the phosphorus to calcium ratio needs correcting; wet corn gluten feed 25 to 30 percent; soybean hulls 15 to 20 percent; whole cottonseed 25 percent of diet DM and never above 0.5 percent of body weight daily because of gossypol; wet brewers grains 30 to 40 percent, with storage losses around 15 percent and palatability problems above 30 percent; beet pulp 15 to 20 percent. As a general rule byproducts should not exceed 50 percent of the concentrate fraction, and you keep forage quality up to buffer the variability in byproduct nutrient content.

Worked example. Wet brewers grains offered at 0.04 USD per kg as delivered, at 22 percent dry matter. Dry matter cost = 0.04 / 0.22 = 0.18 USD per kg of dry matter. Now apply the published 15 percent storage loss for high-moisture feeds: 0.18 / 0.85 = 0.21 USD per kg of dry matter actually eaten. Compare with a dairy meal at 0.38 USD per kg. On dry matter alone the byproduct is 45 percent cheaper.

But compare on nutrients, not on bulk. If the brewers grains run about 55 percent TDN and the dairy meal 70 percent, then TDN costs 0.21 / 0.55 = 0.38 USD per kg of TDN from brewers grains against 0.38 / 0.70 = 0.54 USD per kg of TDN from the meal. Still clearly cheaper, and brewers grains also bring useful protein. Substituting 4 kg of dry matter per cow per day saves roughly 4 x (0.38 - 0.21) = 0.68 USD per cow per day, which on ten cows over 200 days is 1,360 USD. That figure justifies a transport arrangement and a covered storage slab.

The traps are real and they are mostly about moisture and mould. High-moisture byproducts spoil fast in heat, so you need either rapid turnover or proper ensiling, and you must keep feed dry at 12 to 14 percent moisture where it is stored dry. Aflatoxin is the hard limit: 20 micrograms per kg in feed for both dairy and beef cattle, because 2.0 to 6.2 percent of what a dairy cow eats transfers into her milk, and the milk safety limit is 0.5 micrograms per kg specifically to protect human infants. A contaminated cheap feed does not just cost you a cow, it can cost you your buyer.

Forwards integration next. Selling raw milk at 0.42 USD per litre while a yoghurt sells at 1.20 USD per litre equivalent looks like an obvious 0.78 USD margin. It is not. Subtract culture and sugar, packaging, energy, cold chain, spoilage, licensing and the labour of selling: if that comes to 0.55 USD per litre, your true added margin is 0.23 USD per litre, and it only exists if you actually sell everything you make. On 100 litres a day that is 23 USD a day, which is real money, but it demands volume, refrigeration and a market that does not disappear. Test it with 20 litres a day for a month before you buy equipment.

The same logic applies to beef. Selling a finished steer live at 2.60 USD per kg versus slaughtering and selling cuts directly captures the butcher's margin, but you take on the cold chain, the licence, the offal disposal, and the risk of unsold cuts on a hot afternoon.

Concrete African context. Dairy groups around Njombe, Tanzania, have built their feeding programmes around locally available byproducts and their income around collective bulking and chilling rather than each farmer processing alone. The lesson is that backwards integration usually pays at any scale, while forwards integration usually needs a group to reach the volume that makes cold chain and packaging affordable.

Lesson 12.6~12 min

Sustainability and Long-Term Profitability

In this lesson
  • Explain why methane loss and poor forage quality are the same economic problem
  • Calculate the feed saved by improving grazing management on your own pasture
  • Choose sustainability measures that pay for themselves without a carbon payment

Sustainability is usually presented to farmers as an obligation. Treat it instead as an efficiency audit, because almost everything on the list is energy or nutrients you are currently paying for and losing.

Start with methane, which is the clearest case. Beef cattle lose roughly 6 to 8 percent of gross energy intake as methane and dairy cattle 5 to 7 percent. That is not an emissions statistic, it is a feed bill walking out of the animal. The factors that raise it are the same factors that make a diet expensive: higher NDF means more methane because fermentation takes longer, and lower digestibility means more methane because fermentation is incomplete. High-starch diets ferment faster, shift toward propionate, and produce less methane. Feedlot cattle sit near 6 percent of gross energy while pasture cattle sit at 7 to 8 percent, although pasture systems often produce less methane per kilogram of gain than the crude percentage suggests once slower growth is accounted for.

The practical mitigation tools, in order of what an African smallholder can actually use:

  1. Dietary management. Raising concentrate proportion and using higher-quality forage cuts methane 10 to 20 percent through a fermentation shift, and it usually improves feed efficiency at the same time. This is free in the sense that you were going to buy feed anyway.
  2. Ionophores. Monensin is used for its 6.4 percent feed efficiency gain, with a secondary methane reduction of about 3 to 5 percent.
  3. 3-nitrooxypropanol, sold as Bovaer. Meta-analysis puts mean reduction at 20 to 40 percent, and 39.0 plus or minus 5.4 percent at an optimised dose of 123 mg per kg of diet dry matter, with the best results above 100 mg/kg. It works better on high-starch diets, around 40 percent, than on high-NDF forage diets, around 20 to 25 percent, and dairy cattle respond more strongly than beef, which sit at 20 to 30 percent. It does not depress intake, digestibility or milk yield. Cost-effectiveness depends on carbon credit prices, and availability outside the EU and North America is limited.
  4. Seaweed such as Asparagopsis shows 50 to 80 percent reductions in research settings, but palatability is variable and commercial availability is limited.

Now the measure that pays regardless of any carbon market: forage quality. Work it with the standard equations. A pasture grazed late at 42 percent ADF and 68 percent NDF gives TDN = 88.9 - (0.779 x 42) = 56.2 percent, and intake capacity = 120 / 68 = 1.76 percent of body weight. For a 300 kg steer, that is 5.29 kg of dry matter delivering 5.29 x 0.562 = 2.97 kg of TDN per day.

The same pasture grazed at the right stage, at 34 percent ADF and 58 percent NDF, gives TDN = 88.9 - (0.779 x 34) = 62.4 percent and intake = 120 / 58 = 2.07 percent, so 6.21 kg of dry matter delivering 6.21 x 0.624 = 3.88 kg of TDN. That is 0.91 kg more TDN per head per day from the same field, obtained by moving cattle on time.

Cost that out. Replacing 0.91 kg of TDN with maize bran at 70 percent TDN and 0.22 USD per kg requires 0.91 / 0.70 = 1.30 kg, costing 0.29 USD per head per day. Over a 180-day grazing season with 40 head, that is 0.29 x 180 x 40 = 2,088 USD of feed you did not have to buy. Rotational grazing also builds soil carbon over time, which is the one genuine sequestration opportunity in a cattle system, but note that the 2,088 USD arrives whether or not anyone ever pays you for the carbon.

The rest of long-term profitability is unglamorous. Land use runs 2 to 5 acres per head on pasture against 150 to 500 sq ft in a feedlot, so land is the binding constraint on extensive systems and manure management is the binding constraint on intensive ones. Pasture systems are rainfall-dependent and drought is the recurring shock; a dry-season feed reserve is a sustainability measure and an insurance policy at the same time. Cow longevity reduces replacement rearing cost. And market access increasingly depends on production practice: growth implants are generally banned in the EU and ractopamine is banned in the EU and Canada, so a producer aiming at export markets is constrained by what buyers will accept, not only by what performs.

Concrete African context. Namibian rangeland producers supplying EU export markets through the national abattoir system operate under exactly these constraints: hormone-free production, traceability, and stocking rates matched to a fragile Kalahari rangeland. Their profitability comes from grazing management and market access rather than from feed additives, which is the model most African producers should study before they copy a US feedlot.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. What is the published feed-to-gain ratio for grain-finished feedlot cattle?

High-concentrate feedlot diets convert at 5.5-6.5:1 on a dry matter basis. 8:1 to 12:1 is the figure for forage-only pasture finishing.

2. A steer needs 150 kg of gain at a 6.0:1 conversion and feed costs 0.26 USD/kg DM. What is the feed cost of that gain?

150 x 6.0 = 900 kg of dry matter; 900 x 0.26 = 234 USD.

3. Which diet carries the highest energy density for gain?

An 80-90% concentrate diet supplies 1.45-1.65 Mcal NEg per lb, against 1.30-1.45 for 60-80% and 1.15-1.30 for 40-60%.

4. Monensin improves feed efficiency by how much?

The published response is a 6.4% improvement in feed efficiency and 2.5% in average daily gain, with dry matter intake down 1-2%.

5. Which two variables most quickly destroy feedlot margin?

Feeder price is the largest single cost item and conversion determines the cost of every kilogram added; a half-point slip in conversion cost 19.50 USD per head in the worked example.

6. What is the published feed conversion for forage-only pasture finishing?

Pasture-finished cattle convert at 8:1 to 12:1, approximately 50% worse than the 5.5-6.5:1 of grain-finished feedlot cattle.

7. How much land does a pasture system typically require per head?

Pasture needs 2-5 acres per head depending on forage productivity; a feedlot needs only 150-500 sq ft per head.

8. Marbling in grain-finished cattle develops over what period on a high-concentrate diet?

Intramuscular fat develops over 6-8 weeks of high-concentrate feeding, with external fat cover laid down in the final 30-40 days.

9. Why is a low pasture cost of gain sometimes misleading?

The longer pasture period adds interest on the animal's value and risks dry-season loss, so kilograms already paid for must be regained.

10. What is the recommended structure for a seasonal African forage supply?

The hybrid captures cheap forage gain when grass is growing and places the expensive concentrate phase when pasture would deliver nothing anyway.

11. Income over feed cost is calculated as:

IOFC is milk revenue minus feed cost per cow per day, and it is the single most useful daily profitability figure on a dairy.

12. How much NEL is in a kilogram of milk at 4.0% fat?

NEL = 0.360 + (0.0969 x 4.0) = 0.360 + 0.388 = 0.748 Mcal per kg of milk.

13. Dairy meal at 70% TDN supplies how much NEL per kilogram?

NEL = (0.0245 x 70) - 0.12 = 1.715 - 0.12 = 1.595 Mcal per kg.

14. Milk price falls from 0.42 to 0.28 USD per litre while meal stays at 0.38 USD per kg. What happens to the break-even response?

Break-even is feed cost divided by milk price: 0.38 / 0.28 = 1.36 kg, so a response that paid at the higher price may now lose money.

15. Why does the milk payment basis matter before changing a ration to raise butterfat?

NEL per kg of milk rises from 0.670 at 3.2% fat to 0.796 at 4.5% fat, so raising fat costs energy that only pays if the buyer pays on composition.

16. Annual cow cost is 214 USD and the weaning rate is 65%. What is the cost per weaned calf?

214 / 0.65 = 329 USD. Every unweaned calf loads its dam's full annual cost onto the calves that did survive.

17. A 1,200 lb lactating beef cow giving 10 lb of milk per day requires how much TDN?

The published requirement is 12.8 lb TDN daily, with 2.2-2.5 lb of crude protein.

18. How much vitamin A does a lactating 1,200 lb cow require daily?

A lactating cow needs 54,000 IU per day against 34,000 IU when gestating, which is why hay-wintered cows need supplementation.

19. Why should cottonseed meal be withdrawn from bulls before the breeding season?

Gossypol in cottonseed products affects fertility; the recommendation is to avoid cottonseed meal for bulls 2-3 months before the breeding season.

20. If annual cow cost is 214 USD, roughly what does each extra open day cost?

214 / 365 = 0.59 USD per day, before counting the compounding delay to every subsequent calving.

21. Wet brewers grains cost 0.04 USD/kg at 22% dry matter. What is the cost per kg of dry matter before storage losses?

0.04 / 0.22 = 0.18 USD per kg of dry matter. Always convert as-fed prices to a dry matter basis before comparing feeds.

22. What is the maximum recommended inclusion of distillers dried grain in the diet?

Up to 50% of diet dry matter; beyond that sulphur toxicity becomes a risk and the phosphorus to calcium ratio needs correction.

23. What is the aflatoxin limit in milk?

The milk safety limit is 0.5 ug/kg, set to protect human infants; feed is limited to 20 ug/kg because 2.0-6.2% of intake transfers to milk.

24. Whole cottonseed should not exceed what proportion of body weight daily?

Maximum 0.5% of body weight daily, and 25% of diet dry matter, because of gossypol.

25. Why is the apparent margin on processing milk into yoghurt usually overstated?

The gross price gap is not margin. Once processing, packaging, cold chain, spoilage and licensing costs are subtracted, the true added margin is much smaller and only exists if everything made is sold.

26. Roughly what share of gross energy intake do beef cattle lose as methane?

Beef cattle lose about 6-8% of gross energy intake as methane, dairy cattle 5-7%. It is a direct loss of feed energy you paid for.

27. What methane reduction does 3-NOP achieve at an optimised dose of 123 mg/kg diet DM?

Meta-analysis reports 39.0 plus or minus 5.4% at that dose, within an overall range of 20-40%, with better results above 100 mg/kg.

28. A pasture at 34% ADF has what TDN by the standard equation?

TDN = 88.9 - (0.779 x 34) = 88.9 - 26.5 = 62.4%.

29. Which mitigation option is most immediately available to a smallholder without any carbon payment?

Better forage quality and timely grazing cut methane 10-20% through a fermentation shift and simultaneously deliver more TDN per head per day, paying for themselves in feed saved.

30. Why does export market access constrain feedlot practice?

Implants are generally banned in the EU and ractopamine is banned in the EU and Canada, so producers targeting those markets cannot use the additives that raise gain in domestic feedlots.

Module 12 capstone

Whole-Enterprise Profitability Audit using your own herd and your own local market prices. Step 1: choose the enterprise that carries most of your income, either a beef fattening group, a breeding cow herd, or a dairy herd, and list every animal in it by class and current live weight, measured on a scale or a weigh-band. Step 2: collect real local prices this week, not remembered ones: feeder or weaner price per kg live weight, finished price per kg, milk price per litre and how it is calculated, and the delivered price and dry matter percentage of every feed and byproduct available to you within reasonable transport distance. Convert each feed to cost per kg of dry matter and, using TDN from the ADF equation TDN = 88.9 - (0.779 x ADF), to cost per kg of TDN. Step 3: measure your actual performance rather than assuming it. Weigh a group at the start and end of 21 days to get real average daily gain, weigh the feed that group ate over the same period, and calculate your true feed-to-gain ratio. Compare it with 5.5-6.5:1 for a feedlot diet or 8:1 to 12:1 for pasture. Step 4: build the budget. For fattening, calculate cost of gain per kg as (feed cost + non-feed cost) divided by kg gained, and compare it against the finished price per kg. For a breeding herd, build an annual cow cost from dry period feed, lactation feed, minerals, health, breeding and overhead, then divide by your actual weaning percentage to get cost per weaned calf. For a dairy, calculate income over feed cost per cow per day and the break-even milk response, which is concentrate cost per kg divided by milk price per litre. Step 5: run three scenarios on the same spreadsheet or page: feeder or feed price 20 percent higher, output price 20 percent lower, and a dry season two months longer than normal. Write down at what point each scenario turns your margin negative. Step 6: identify your three cheapest improvements from this module. Candidates include closing calving interval, shifting the calving season so peak lactation lands on green grass, moving cattle onto paddocks before forage goes stemmy, substituting a local byproduct within its published inclusion limit, and cutting concentrate when the milk price falls below break-even. Cost each one and put a USD figure on the annual gain. Deliver a two-page document: page one is the current budget with your real numbers, page two is the costed action list with dates and the person responsible.
