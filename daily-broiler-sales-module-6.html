1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Feed is the single largest cost in broiler production, often accounting for 70% or more of your total expenses. If you are selling broilers daily, you must manage feed carefully or you will eat your profits. A broiler eats about 3.5 to 4 kg of feed to reach 2 kg live weight. At a feed price of 500 shillings per kg, that is 1,750 to 2,000 shillings per bird just for feed. If you sell a 2 kg bird for 2,500 shillings, you are left with only 500 to 750 shillings to cover chicks, medication, labour, and profit. That is why a feed budget is not optionalit is the backbone of your business.

In a batch production system, you raise birds in groups (batches) and sell them daily as they reach market weight. This means you must plan feed purchases for each batch from day one to day sale. Without a budget, you may run out of feed, forcing you to buy expensive small quantities, or you may overbuy and tie up cash that could be used elsewhere. A feed budget helps you know exactly how much feed you need, when you need it, and how much money to set aside.

For example, if you plan to raise 100 broilers, you will need about 350 to 400 kg of feed from day-old to 2 kg. If you buy in 50 kg bags, that is 7 to 8 bags. At 25,000 shillings per bag, you need 175,000 to 200,000 shillings for feed alone. Breaking this down weekly helps you avoid cash flow problems. In week one, chicks eat littleabout 10 kg total. By week four, they eat about 100 kg. So you can stagger your purchases.

This module will teach you how to calculate feed needs, choose the right feed, store it properly, and stick to a budget. You will learn to avoid common mistakes like overfeeding, underfeeding, and wasting feed. With a good feed budget, you can predict your costs, set your selling price, and ensure you make a profit every day. Remember: the bird that eats well pays well, but only if you control the cost.

2. Tools and Materials

To manage feed and your feed budget effectively, you need a few simple tools and materials. You do not need expensive equipment, but you must be organised. First, get a weighing scale. A hanging scale that can weigh up to 50 kg is ideal. You will use it to weigh feed bags, feed portions, and even birds to check growth. A small kitchen scale (0-5 kg) is useful for weighing feed for young chicks. Without a scale, you are guessing, and guessing leads to waste or underfeeding.

Second, you need feed storage containers. Feed must be kept dry and safe from pests. Use plastic drums with tight lids, or metal bins. If you buy feed in bags, keep them on a wooden pallet off the floor to prevent moisture. Never store feed directly on the ground. Also, get a scoop or container that you use only for feed. A 1-litre plastic cup or a tin can works well. Know how much it holdsfor example, a 1-litre cup holds about 0.5 kg of feed. This helps you measure without weighing every time.

Third, you need a record book and a pen. This is your feed budget tool. Record every feed purchase: date, quantity, cost, and supplier. Also record daily feed consumption per batch. A simple notebook is fine. You can draw a table with columns for date, batch number, feed given (kg), and notes. At the end of the week, add up the feed used and compare with your budget. This record will show you if you are on track or overspending.

Fourth, you need a calculator or a phone with a calculator. You will do simple maths: multiply feed price by quantity, divide total feed by number of birds, etc. If you have a smartphone, you can use a notes app or a simple spreadsheet. But a paper record is more reliable in areas with no electricity.

Finally, consider a feed mixer if you plan to mix your own feed. This can save money, but only if you can source ingredients cheaply and mix correctly. For beginners, buying complete feed from a reputable supplier is safer. You will also need a wheelbarrow or bucket to carry feed to the pens. Keep all tools clean and in one place. A well-organised feed corner saves time and prevents mistakes.

3. Step by Step Method

Step 1: Calculate total feed needed for your batch. Decide how many birds you will raise and to what weight. For broilers, a good target is 2 kg live weight at 6 weeks. On average, a broiler eats 3.8 kg of feed to reach 2 kg. So for 100 birds, you need 100 x 3.8 = 380 kg of feed. Round up to 400 kg to be safe. If you buy 50 kg bags, that is 8 bags.

Step 2: Break down feed by week. Broilers eat differently each week. Here is a typical weekly feed intake per bird: Week 1: 0.15 kg, Week 2: 0.4 kg, Week 3: 0.7 kg, Week 4: 1.0 kg, Week 5: 1.2 kg, Week 6: 1.35 kg. Total = 4.8 kg? That is too high. Actually, these are cumulative? Let me correct: For 100 birds, weekly total feed: Week 1: 15 kg, Week 2: 40 kg, Week 3: 70 kg, Week 4: 100 kg, Week 5: 120 kg, Week 6: 135 kg. Total = 480 kg. That is for 2.5 kg? I need to be accurate. Common figures: A broiler eats about 1 kg feed per 0.5 kg gain? No, feed conversion ratio (FCR) is about 1.8. So for 2 kg gain, feed = 3.6 kg. For 100 birds, 360 kg. Weekly: Week 1: 10 kg, Week 2: 30 kg, Week 3: 60 kg, Week 4: 90 kg, Week 5: 100 kg, Week 6: 70 kg. Sum = 360 kg. Let's use that. So plan to buy 7 bags (350 kg) and adjust.

Step 3: Cost the feed. Find the current price per bag. Suppose a 50 kg bag costs 25,000 shillings. Total feed cost = 7 x 25,000 = 175,000 shillings. Add 10% for price changes and waste = 192,500 shillings. This is your feed budget for the batch.

Step 4: Schedule purchases. Do not buy all feed at once. Buy week 1 and 2 feed (40 kg) at the start. Then buy week 3 and 4 feed (150 kg) at the end of week 2. Buy week 5 and 6 feed (170 kg) at the end of week 4. This spreads your cash outlay.

Step 5: Measure and feed daily. Use your scoop to give the right amount. For 100 birds in week 1, each bird eats 0.15 kg? Actually, 10 kg total for 100 birds = 0.1 kg per bird per day? No, 10 kg for the week = 1.4 kg per day for 100 birds = 14 g per bird per day. That is too low. Let's recalc: Week 1 total 10 kg for 100 birds = 0.1 kg per bird for the week = 14 g per day. That seems low. Typical day-old chicks eat about 10-15 g per day in first week. So 100 birds eat 1-1.5 kg per day. Over 7 days, 7-10 kg. So 10 kg is fine. So daily feed: Week 1: 1.5 kg/day, Week 2: 4 kg/day, Week 3: 8.5 kg/day, Week 4: 13 kg/day, Week 5: 14 kg/day, Week 6: 10 kg/day. Adjust based on consumption.

Step 6: Record and review. Every day, note how much feed you gave. At the end of the week, compare with your plan. If you used more, find out why. Adjust next week. Keep your budget updated.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Not keeping feed records. Many farmers feed by guesswork and never write down how much they use. This leads to running out of feed or overspending. Fix: Keep a daily feed log. Write the date, batch, and amount given. At the end of the week, add it up. Compare with your budget. If you are using more than planned, investigate.

Mistake 2: Buying feed in small quantities. When you run out and buy a few kilograms from a local shop, you pay a higher price per kg. Fix: Plan your purchases. Buy in 50 kg bags from a supplier. If cash is tight, join with other farmers to buy in bulk and share the discount.

Mistake 3: Overfeeding. Some farmers think more feed means faster growth. But broilers have limited capacity. Overfeeding wastes feed and can cause health problems like ascites. Fix: Follow the feeding guide. Use a scale to weigh feed for the first two weeks. After that, use a scoop and stick to the recommended daily amount. If birds leave feed in the trough, reduce the amount.

Mistake 4: Underfeeding. This is worse because birds will not reach market weight on time, and you will sell at a loss. Fix: Monitor bird weight weekly. Weigh a few birds (5-10) each week. If they are below target, increase feed slightly. But do not overdo it.

Mistake 5: Poor storage. Feed that gets wet or is attacked by rats is money lost. Fix: Store feed in a dry, raised place. Use airtight containers. Keep only one week's feed in the poultry house. The rest stays in the store. Rotate stock: use older feed first.

Mistake 6: Not adjusting for feed price changes. Feed prices fluctuate. If you budgeted at 25,000 per bag and the price rises to 28,000, your budget is off. Fix: Check prices weekly. Keep a buffer of 10% in your budget. If prices rise, adjust your selling price or find a cheaper supplier.

Mistake 7: Feeding the wrong feed. Broilers need different feed at different stages: starter (0-4 weeks), grower (4-6 weeks), and finisher (6 weeks to market). Using the wrong feed wastes money. Fix: Buy the correct feed for each stage. Label your storage clearly.

Mistake 8: Not accounting for mortality. If birds die, you need less feed. But many farmers keep feeding the same amount. Fix: Adjust feed after any mortality. If you lose 5 birds out of 100, reduce feed by 5%.

By avoiding these mistakes, you will keep your feed budget on track and your profits healthy.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety in feed management is about protecting your health, your birds, and your money. First, personal safety. Feed dust can cause breathing problems. Always wear a dust mask when handling feed, especially when mixing or scooping. Wash your hands after handling feed, especially before eating. Keep feed away from children and pets. Store feed in a locked area if possible.

Second, feed safety. Feed can carry bacteria like Salmonella. Never use mouldy or spoiled feed. Mould produces toxins that can kill your birds. If feed smells sour or has clumps, do not use it. Keep feed dry. Moisture leads to mould and spoilage. Store feed in a cool, dry place. Use pallets to keep bags off the floor. Close bags tightly after opening. Use opened bags within one week.

Third, bird safety. Feed must be fresh and free of contaminants. Do not use feed that has been spilled on the floor and swept upit may contain droppings or dirt. Use clean feeders. Clean feeders daily to prevent feed buildup. Remove any wet or caked feed. Provide enough feeder space so birds do not crowd. A good rule: 5 cm of feeder space per bird. For 100 birds, you need 5 metres of feeder length. If feeders are too small, some birds will not get enough feed.

Fourth, best practices for feed budgeting. Keep a separate bank account or cash box for feed money. Do not mix feed money with personal money. Buy feed from reputable suppliers who provide quality feed. Ask for a receipt. Check the manufacturing date on the bag. Use feed within three months of manufacturing. Weigh feed for the first two weeks to calibrate your scoop. Then you can rely on the scoop. But re-calibrate if you change feed type or supplier.

Fifth, environmental safety. Store feed away from chemicals like pesticides or fuel. These can contaminate feed and poison your birds. Keep feed storage clean and free of rodents. Use traps, not poison, near feed. If you must use poison, place it far from feed.

Sixth, financial safety. Your feed budget should include a contingency of 10% for price increases or unexpected needs. Review your budget weekly. If you are overspending, find out why and fix it. Do not borrow money for feed unless you are sure of your selling price. Remember, feed is your biggest costprotect it like your life.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it is time to put what you have learned into practice. This exercise will help you create a feed budget for a batch of 100 broilers. Follow these steps and write your answers in your record book.

Step 1: Calculate total feed needed. Assume you will raise 100 broilers to 2 kg live weight. Using the average feed conversion ratio of 1.8, calculate total feed: 100 birds x 2 kg x 1.8 = 360 kg. Round up to 400 kg to be safe. How many 50 kg bags is that? 400 / 50 = 8 bags.

Step 2: Find the current feed price. Call your local feed supplier or visit the market. Ask for the price of broiler starter, grower, and finisher. For this exercise, assume starter costs 26,000 per 50 kg bag, grower costs 25,000, and finisher costs 24,000. You will need: Starter for weeks 1-4: about 200 kg (4 bags). Grower for weeks 4-6: about 120 kg (2.4 bags, so 3 bags). Finisher for week 6 to market: about 80 kg (1.6 bags, so 2 bags). Total bags: 4+3+2 = 9 bags. But you only need 8 bags total. Adjust: use 3 bags starter, 3 bags grower, 2 bags finisher = 8 bags. Calculate total cost: 3 x 26,000 = 78,000; 3 x 25,000 = 75,000; 2 x 24,000 = 48,000. Total = 201,000 shillings.

Step 3: Add a 10% contingency. 201,000 x 0.10 = 20,100. Total budget = 221,100 shillings.

Step 4: Schedule purchases. Week 1: buy 1 bag starter (26,000). Week 2: buy 1 bag starter (26,000). Week 3: buy 1 bag starter and 1 bag grower (51,000). Week 4: buy 1 bag grower (25,000). Week 5: buy 1 bag grower and 1 bag finisher (49,000). Week 6: buy 1 bag finisher (24,000). Total = 201,000. This spreads your cash outlay.

Step 5: Daily feeding plan. For 100 birds, daily feed: Week 1: 1.5 kg/day. Week 2: 4 kg/day. Week 3: 8.5 kg/day. Week 4: 13 kg/day. Week 5: 14 kg/day. Week 6: 10 kg/day. Use a scale to measure feed for the first week. Then use a scoop that you have calibrated. For example, if 1 scoop = 0.5 kg, then week 1 you give 3 scoops per day.

Step 6: Record and review. For one week, record daily feed given. At the end of the week, compare with the plan. If you used more, check for spillage or overfeeding. If you used less, check if birds are underweight. Adjust as needed.

Step 7: Calculate your break-even selling price. If total feed cost is 221,100, and you have other costs (chicks, medication, labour) of 100,000, total cost = 321,100. If you expect 95 birds to survive, cost per bird = 321,100 / 95 = 3,380 shillings. To make a 20% profit, selling price per bird = 3,380 x 1.20 = 4,056 shillings. So you must sell each bird for at least 4,100 shillings to be safe.

Write all these calculations in your record book. This is your feed budget. Keep it safe and refer to it daily.

<h2>Practical Reference: Feed and the Feed Budget for Daily Broiler Sales</h2>

<p>In a daily broiler sales operation, feed is not a cost line it is the engine of your batch calendar. Every day you delay a sale, every gram of feed wasted, and every bird that dies before market weight directly reduces the money that lands in your pocket. This Practical Reference lesson gives you the numbers, tools, and habits you need to build a feed budget that protects your margin from day-old chick to final sale.</p>

<p>Broiler production in Africa is dominated by small and medium-scale producers who sell live birds daily to local markets, restaurants, and households. Feed typically represents <strong>6575% of total variable production cost</strong>. If you cannot forecast feed need and cost per bird, you cannot price your birds correctly, and you cannot know whether a batch is profitable until it is too late. This module is about turning feed from a guess into a controlled, budgeted input.</p>

<h2>Why the Feed Budget Is a Business Tool, Not a Farm Chore</h2>

<p>A feed budget answers four questions every day:</p>
<ol>
<li>How much feed do my birds need today, this week, and until sale?</li>
<li>How much will that feed cost at current market prices?</li>
<li>What is my feed cost per bird and per kilogram of live weight?</li>
<li>When will I run out of feed, and what is my reorder point?</li>
</ol>

<p>In practice, many farmers buy feed reactively when the bag is empty. This creates two problems: emergency purchases at higher prices, and feed outages that stall growth. A feed budget converts these emergencies into planned purchases, which often means better prices, fewer transport trips, and a batch that reaches market weight on schedule.</p>

<h2>Feed Conversion Ratio: The Number That Decides Profit</h2>

<p>FCR is calculated as:</p>
<p><strong>FCR = Total feed consumed (kg) Total live weight gained (kg)</strong></p>

<p>If a bird eats 4.8 kg and reaches 2.0 kg, FCR = 2.4. If it eats 4.4 kg and reaches 2.2 kg, FCR = 2.0. The difference is 0.4 kg of feed per bird on a 200-bird batch, that is 80 kg of feed, which at USD 0.50/kg is USD 40. That is real money. Improving FCR is the fastest way to improve margin without raising prices.</p>

<h2>Feed Storage and Loss Control</h2>

<p>Feed that is stored badly is feed you paid for but never fed. In humid African climates, feed can spoil within weeks. Key practices:</p>
<ul>
<li>Store feed on pallets, never directly on the floor.</li>
<li>Keep bags at least 10 cm from walls to allow air circulation.</li>
<li>Use the oldest feed first (FIFO First In, First Out).</li>
<li>Keep storage dry and ventilated; mouldy feed causes disease and rejection.</li>
<li>Protect against rodents and birds, which consume and contaminate feed.</li>
</ul>

<p>Feed loss of 5% on a 960 kg batch is 48 kg nearly USD 25. Over a year of batches, this is a significant and preventable loss.</p>

<h2>Feed Budget and Daily Sales: The Connection</h2>

<p>In a daily broiler sales model, you are not selling all birds at once. You sell a few birds each day as they reach market weight. This means your feed budget must be dynamic: birds remaining in the house still need feed, while sold birds stop consuming. The practical approach is to recalculate feed need every week based on the number of birds remaining and their average weight.</p>

<p>A simple weekly feed budget:</p>
<ul>
<li>Count remaining birds.</li>
<li>Estimate average weight (weigh a sample of 10 birds).</li>
<li>Use the benchmark intake table to estimate feed for the coming week.</li>
<li>Compare with feed in store; order the difference.</li>
</ul>

<p>This prevents both overstocking feed (which ties up cash and risks spoilage) and understocking (which stalls growth and delays sales).</p>

<h3 style="color: #c0392b; margin-top: 0;"> Safety Warning: Feed and Chemical Hazards</h3>
<p><strong>Never store feed in the same room as pesticides, herbicides, or veterinary drugs.</strong> Feed absorbs chemical vapours and can become toxic to birds and, through meat, to consumers. Always store feed in a dedicated, dry, ventilated space. When using medicated feed or adding drugs to feed, wear gloves and a mask, follow withdrawal periods strictly, and record all treatments. Selling birds before the withdrawal period ends can leave drug residues in meat and is illegal in most countries.</p>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Know your FCR.</strong> Measure feed in and weight gained. Target 2.02.3 for broilers to 2.02.2 kg.</li>
<li><strong>Calculate feed cost per bird</strong> before you set your selling price. If feed cost per bird is USD 2.50 and you sell at USD 2.60, you are losing money.</li>
<li><strong>Update the feed budget weekly</strong> as birds are sold and feed prices change.</li>
<li><strong>Set a reorder point.</strong> Never let feed drop below one week's requirement.</li>
<li><strong>Store feed correctly.</strong> Pallets, ventilation, FIFO, no chemicals nearby.</li>
<li><strong>Weigh a sample of birds weekly</strong> to track growth and detect problems early.</li>
<li><strong>Record every bag in and every bag fed.</strong> If the numbers do not match, investigate.</li>
<li><strong>Review mortality and culls.</strong> Dead birds still consumed feed they are a direct loss.</li>
