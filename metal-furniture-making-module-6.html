1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Frames and structures are the skeleton of any metal furniture. Whether you are making a chair, a table, a bed, or a gate, the frame carries the load and keeps everything square and stable. In Africa, where furniture often has to withstand heavy use, rough surfaces, and sometimes outdoor conditions, a strong frame is not optionalit is the difference between a product that lasts for years and one that collapses in months. Think about the metal beds common in many homes: if the frame is weak, the bed sags and squeaks. A well-built frame, on the other hand, can support a family and be passed down.

Why does this matter for your business? Customers talk. If you sell a wobbly table, word spreads fast. But if your furniture is rock-solid, you get repeat orders and referrals. Good frames also allow you to use less materialif you design smart, you can make a strong frame with thinner tubing, saving money on steel. That is profit in your pocket.

In this module, you will learn the basics of frame construction: how to measure, cut, join, and brace metal to create structures that do not twist or collapse. We focus on practical methods using common tools like angle grinders, welding machines, and measuring tapes. You will learn to work with square and rectangular tubing, round pipes, and angle ironmaterials easily found in local markets or scrap yards.

By the end of this module, you will be able to build a simple stool or small table frame from start to finish. You will know how to check for square, how to weld joints that hold, and how to avoid common mistakes that lead to weak furniture. This skill is the foundation for every other metal furniture project you will ever do. So lets get startedyour ability to build strong frames will set you apart as a craftsman.

2. Tools and Materials

To build metal frames, you need a basic set of tools and the right materials. You do not need a fully equipped workshop to startmany successful metalworkers begin with just a few key tools. Here is what you need: a measuring tape (5m is fine), a carpenters square or engineers square, a scriber or marker, an angle grinder with cutting and grinding discs, a welding machine (arc welder is common and affordable), welding rods (2.5mm or 3.2mm), a hammer, a wire brush, and safety gear (goggles, gloves, apron, and boots). A magnetic welding square is also very helpful for holding joints at 90 degrees. If you dont have a welder yet, you can start with bolted joints, but welding is stronger and faster.

For materials, the most common are mild steel tubes: square tubing (e.g., 25mm x 25mm x 1.5mm wall thickness), rectangular tubing (e.g., 40mm x 20mm), and round pipe (e.g., 25mm diameter). Angle iron (e.g., 25mm x 25mm x 3mm) is also useful for legs and supports. You can buy new steel from hardware stores or steel merchants, but many African craftsmen save money by using recycled steel from scrap yardsold bed frames, car parts, and gates can be cut and reused. Just make sure the metal is clean and not badly rusted. For outdoor furniture, consider galvanized steel or apply anti-rust paint.

You will also need consumables: cutting discs, grinding discs, welding rods, and paint or oil to protect the finished frame. A good workbench or sturdy table is essentialyou can make one from wood or metal. A vice is useful for holding pieces while cutting or welding. Finally, a fire extinguisher or bucket of sand should be nearby when welding, as sparks can start fires.

Start with the basics: a grinder, a welder, a square, and a tape. As you earn, add more tools like a chop saw (for accurate cuts) or a magnetic drill. Remember, quality tools save time and produce better work. Buy the best you can afford, and maintain themclean your welder, change discs when worn, and keep your square accurate.

3. Step by Step Method

Building a metal frame follows a clear sequence. We will use a simple stool frame as an example: a square top (350mm x 350mm) with four legs (450mm high). First, plan and measure. Draw a simple sketch with dimensions. For the top, cut four pieces of 25mm square tubing to 350mm each. For the legs, cut four pieces to 450mm. Use an angle grinder with a cutting disc. Make sure cuts are squarecheck with a square. Deburr the ends with a grinding disc.

Second, lay out the top frame on a flat surface. Arrange the four pieces into a square, with the ends meeting at 90 degrees. Use a carpenters square to check corners. Clamp or tack weld the corners. Tack welding means making small welds at each joint to hold everything in place. Check for square by measuring diagonalsthey must be equal. If not, adjust and re-tack. Once square, weld all around each joint. Grind the welds smooth if desired.

Third, attach the legs. Turn the top frame upside down. Place a leg at each corner, ensuring it is flush with the outside edges. Use a square to make sure the leg is perpendicular to the top. Tack weld each leg in two places (opposite sides). Check that all legs are vertical and the frame does not wobble. Measure from the bottom of one leg to the bottom of the opposite leg diagonallyboth diagonals should be equal. If not, adjust. Then weld all around each leg joint.

Fourth, add braces for strength. For a stool, you can add a brace between legs about 100mm from the bottom. Cut two pieces of tubing to fit between the legs (front-back and side-side). Weld them in place. This prevents the legs from splaying out. Finally, clean the frame: grind welds, brush off slag, and wipe with a cloth. Apply a coat of red oxide primer, then paint your chosen colour. Let it dry. Your stool frame is complete and ready for a seat (wood, metal, or woven).

Remember: measure twice, cut once. Tack weld, check, then fully weld. Always work on a flat surface. With practice, you can build a frame like this in under two hours.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Even experienced welders make mistakes. Here are common problems when building frames and how to fix them. Mistake 1: Frame is not square. This happens when you dont check diagonals or weld too quickly. Fix: Before fully welding, measure diagonals. If unequal, use a clamp or hammer to adjust until equal, then re-tack. Always tack weld first, check, then weld fully. Mistake 2: Legs are not perpendicular. If legs lean, the furniture will wobble. Fix: Use a square against the top and leg. Tack weld, check with a plumb line or square, adjust, then weld. Mistake 3: Weak welds. This occurs if the metal is dirty, the current is too low, or you move too fast. Fix: Clean metal with a grinder before welding. Set welder to correct amperage (e.g., 90-120 amps for 2.5mm rods on 3mm steel). Practice on scrap. Make sure you penetrate the joint. Mistake 4: Distortion. Welding heats metal, causing it to bend. Fix: Weld small sections at a time, alternating sides. Clamp pieces to a flat surface. Allow to cool between welds. Mistake 5: Porous welds (holes). This is from moisture or rust. Fix: Use dry rods, clean metal, and proper technique. Mistake 6: Sharp edges. Grind all cut ends and welds smooth to avoid injuries and improve appearance. Mistake 7: No bracing. Without braces, frames twist. Fix: Add diagonal or horizontal braces. For a table, add a brace between legs. Mistake 8: Using wrong material thickness. Too thin and it bends; too thick and its heavy and expensive. Fix: For furniture, 1.5mm to 2mm wall thickness is good for most applications. Mistake 9: Not checking level. If the floor is uneven, your frame might rock. Fix: After building, place on a flat surface and adjust legs by grinding or adding shims. Mistake 10: Skipping safety. Always wear PPE. Fix: Put on goggles, gloves, and apron before starting. These mistakes are easy to avoid with patience and careful checking. Remember: a few extra minutes checking saves hours of rework.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety is not optional in metalwork. Every year, craftsmen are injured because they skip basic precautions. You must protect your eyes, lungs, skin, and ears. Always wear safety goggles when cutting or grindingsparks and metal shards can blind you. Wear a welding helmet or face shield when welding. Use earplugs or earmuffs when grinding, as the noise can damage hearing. Wear leather gloves to protect hands from cuts and burns. A leather apron or heavy cotton overalls protect your body from sparks and hot metal. Steel-toe boots protect your feet from falling metal. Never wear loose clothing or open shoes.

Ventilation is critical. Welding fumes are toxic. Work outdoors or in a well-ventilated area. If indoors, use a fan to blow fumes away, but not directly on the weld (it can disrupt shielding gas if using MIG). Keep a fire extinguisher or bucket of sand nearby. Never weld near flammable materials like paint, petrol, or wood. Check for fire hazards after weldingsparks can smoulder for hours. Use a fire blanket or wet cloth to cool hot metal before touching.

Best practices for quality and efficiency: Plan your work. Measure twice, cut once. Keep your work area clean and organized. Store tools safely. Maintain your equipment: change grinder discs when worn, check welding cables for damage, and keep your welder dry. Use jigs or fixtures for repeated partsthis ensures consistency. When welding, use the correct amperage and rod size. For 3mm steel, use 2.5mm rod at 90-110 amps. For thinner metal, use lower amps. Practice on scrap to dial in settings. Always tack weld before full welding. Check square and level at every stage. Grind welds for a clean finish. Apply anti-rust primer and paint to protect your work. Finally, take breaks. Fatigue leads to mistakes and injuries. Drink water. Stretch. A fresh mind works better. By following these safety rules and best practices, you will build strong, safe furniture and build a reputation as a reliable craftsman.

6. Practical Exercise

Now its time to put your skills to work. In this exercise, you will build a simple metal stool frame. This project uses the techniques from this module: measuring, cutting, squaring, tack welding, and bracing. You will need: 25mm square tubing (about 2.5 meters total), angle grinder, welding machine, 2.5mm welding rods, square, measuring tape, scriber, hammer, wire brush, and safety gear. Optional: magnetic square, clamps.

Step 1: Cut the pieces. You need four pieces of 350mm for the top frame, four pieces of 450mm for the legs, and two pieces of 250mm for braces (these will be cut to fit later). Use your grinder to cut. Deburr all ends.

Step 2: Build the top frame. Lay the four 350mm pieces on a flat surface to form a square. Check corners with a square. Tack weld each corner. Measure diagonalsthey must be equal. Adjust if needed. Then weld all around each corner. Grind smooth.

Step 3: Attach legs. Turn the top frame upside down. Place a leg at each corner, flush with the outside. Use a square to ensure each leg is perpendicular. Tack weld each leg in two opposite spots. Check that all legs are vertical and the frame sits flat. Measure diagonals from leg bottomsthey must be equal. Adjust if needed. Weld all around each leg joint. Grind smooth.

Step 4: Add braces. Measure the distance between the front legs at about 100mm from the bottom. Cut a piece of tubing to fit. Weld it between the legs. Repeat for the side braces. These braces prevent the legs from spreading. Ensure they are level.

Step 5: Clean and finish. Grind all welds smooth. Brush off slag. Wipe with a cloth. Apply a coat of red oxide primer. Let dry. Then paint with your chosen colour (e.g., black, red, or green). Let dry completely.

Step 6: Test. Place the stool on a flat floor. It should not wobble. Sit on it (carefully) to test strength. If it holds, you have succeeded. If not, check for weak welds or missing braces. Take a photo of your finished framethis is your portfolio piece. With this stool, you have mastered the basics of frame construction. Now you can scale up to tables, beds, and more. Practice makes perfectbuild at least three stools to perfect your technique.

<h3>Practical Method: Building a Basic Chair Frame</h3>

<p>The following sequence is the standard approach used in productive workshops. It assumes a simple four-leg chair with a seat frame and back frame using 25 25 mm square tube.</p>

<p><strong>Step 1 Measure and cut.</strong> Cut four legs to equal length (typically 450 mm for a standard chair). Cut two seat side rails, two seat cross rails, two back uprights, and two back cross rails. Use a mitre saw or angle grinder with a cutting disc. Deburr every cut end with a file or grinder sharp burrs cause injuries and poor welds.</p>

<p><strong>Step 2 Lay out on a flat jig.</strong> Build or use a flat welding table. Clamp the seat frame pieces square using a carpenter's square or a pre-built jig. Tack weld the four corners. Check diagonals they must be equal. If not, adjust and re-tack.</p>

<p><strong>Step 3 Attach legs.</strong> Position legs at the corners, checking that all four feet sit flat on the ground. Tack weld, re-check, then complete the welds. Add cross braces between front and rear legs at the lower level to prevent racking.</p>

<p><strong>Step 4 Build and attach the back frame.</strong> Weld the back frame as a separate rectangle, then attach it to the rear legs at the correct angle (typically 510 degrees back from vertical for comfort).</p>

<p><strong>Step 5 Clean and finish.</strong> Grind welds smooth, wire-brush the entire frame, apply anti-rust primer, then topcoat. For outdoor furniture, use epoxy or polyurethane paint. For indoor, enamel is acceptable and cheaper.</p>

<p><strong>Step 6 Quality check.</strong> Place the chair on a flat floor. All four feet must touch. Sit on it (or apply a 100 kg test load). Listen for creaks they indicate a weak joint. Fix before selling.</p>

<h3>Business Notes for African Workshops</h3>

<p>Frames are where your profit is made or lost. Three practical business points:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Standardise your designs.</strong> If every chair uses the same tube sizes and cut lengths, you can batch-cut and batch-weld, cutting production time by 3050%. This is the single biggest profitability lever for small metal furniture businesses.</li>
<li><strong>Buy steel in bulk where possible.</strong> In most African markets, buying a full 6 m length is 1525% cheaper per metre than buying cut pieces. Coordinate with neighbouring workshops if your capital is limited.</li>
<li><strong>Price for the frame, not the whole piece.</strong> Know your cost per frame (materials + labour + consumables + overhead). Add 3050% margin. This prevents the common mistake of undercharging on frames and losing money on the finished piece.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ol>
<li>Steel cut to length, ends deburred and square.</li>
<li>Welding area ventilated; helmet, gloves, and apron worn.</li>
<li>Frame laid out on a flat surface and clamped before tacking.</li>
<li>Diagonals checked and equal before final welding.</li>
<li>All four feet sit flat on the floor no rocking.</li>
<li>Cross braces fitted to prevent racking and twisting.</li>
<li>Welds ground smooth and frame wire-brushed before painting.</li>
<li>Load test completed (100 kg static) before the piece leaves the workshop.</li>
