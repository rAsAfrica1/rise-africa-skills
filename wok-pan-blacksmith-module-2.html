1. Sourcing and Preparing Local Scrap Steel in African Markets

Welcome to Module 2, Lesson 1 of the Wok and Pan Blacksmith training. In this lesson we focus on the lifeblood of any blacksmith business: raw material. In many African towns and cities, the best steel is not bought new from a factory. It is recovered from scrap yards, mechanic workshops, old vehicles, broken farm tools, and construction sites. Your ability to identify, buy, and prepare good scrap steel will determine the quality of your Wok and Pan products and your profit margin. Start by mapping your local scrap ecosystem. Visit mechanics who replace leaf springs, axles, drive shafts, and coil springs. Visit construction sites for rebar offcuts and steel plates. Visit market areas where old hoes, machetes, and pots are discarded. Build relationships with these suppliers. Offer to collect scrap regularly so they do not have to transport it. A reliable supply chain is more valuable than a single big purchase. Learn to identify steel by spark testing and by simple bend tests. High carbon steel, often from old files, springs, and bearings, throws short yellow-orange sparks when touched to a grinding wheel. Mild steel, common in rebar and plates, throws longer orange sparks with fewer bursts. For Wok components that need toughness, such as the main beam or pan rim, medium carbon steel from leaf springs is excellent. For cutting edges, use high carbon steel from old files or bearing races. For decorative work, mild steel is fine. Never assume a piece of metal is what it looks like. Test it before you invest time and fuel. Pricing is critical. In many African markets, scrap is sold by weight or by piece. Weigh your options. A whole leaf spring may cost more than a pile of mixed offcuts, but it gives you predictable quality. Negotiate politely and consistently. Pay a fair price but not a foolish one. Keep a small notebook or phone record of what you paid per kilogram and what you produced from it. This data will tell you which suppliers and which types of scrap give the best return. Preparation begins before the forge. Cut long pieces to manageable lengths with a hacksaw or angle grinder. Remove dirt, paint, rubber, and grease. Burning rubber and paint releases toxic fumes, so never do it in a closed workshop. Use a wire brush and a bucket of water or sand to clean surfaces. For heavily rusted steel, soak it in vinegar or a mild acid solution if available, then scrub and rinse. Rust is not always a deal breaker, but it wastes fuel and creates scale. Sort your steel into three piles: high carbon, medium carbon, and mild steel. Label them with paint or tags. This saves time and prevents mixing. Storage matters. In humid coastal areas, steel rusts fast. Keep your stock under a roof, off the ground, on wooden pallets or dry concrete. Oil or grease your high carbon pieces lightly to slow corrosion. Organize by size and type so you can find what you need without digging. For Wok and Pan production, you will need consistent cross sections. If you rely on random scrap, your jigs and fixtures will be useless. Standardize where possible. For example, decide that all Wok beams will be made from 50 mm by 10 mm leaf spring steel. Then collect and cut to that specification. This allows you to train helpers, speed up production, and guarantee fit. Finally, think about legal and ethical sourcing. Do not buy stolen manhole covers, railway clips, or public infrastructure. It is illegal and it damages your reputation. Work with legitimate scrap dealers and mechanics. If you can, get a receipt or at least a witness for large purchases. In some countries, scrap dealing requires a license. Check your local rules. Your business depends on trust. A blacksmith who buys stolen metal will soon lose customers and may face arrest. In summary, treat scrap sourcing as a professional activity. Build a network, test your steel, negotiate fairly, prepare and sort your material, store it well, and standardize your stock. Do this and your Wok and Pan products will be stronger, your fuel costs lower, and your profits higher. In the next lesson we will cover forge design and fuel efficiency for African conditions.

2. Forge Design and Fuel Efficiency for African Blacksmiths

Welcome to Module 2, Lesson 2. A forge is the heart of your workshop. In Africa, you have choices: traditional charcoal forges, improved clay forges, gas forges, and even solar concentrators. Each has trade-offs in cost, performance, and availability. This lesson helps you choose, build, and run a forge that suits your Wok and Pan production. Start with fuel. Charcoal is widely available but expensive and often linked to deforestation. If you use charcoal, make it from agricultural waste or buy from sustainable producers. Better options include coal, coke, or biomass briquettes made from sawdust, rice husks, or maize cobs. In many countries, coke is imported and costly, but it burns hot and clean. Gas forges are clean and fast, but gas cylinders and regulators can be expensive and unreliable in rural areas. A good compromise is a multi-fuel forge that can burn charcoal, coke, or biomass. Design your forge around three zones: the firepot, the tuyere, and the air supply. The firepot should be a shallow bowl, about 20 to 30 cm across and 10 to 15 cm deep. It can be made from a car brake drum, a thick steel plate, or firebricks. Line it with clay or refractory cement to insulate and protect the metal. The tuyere is the pipe that brings air into the fire. It should enter from the side, slightly above the bottom, and be made of steel pipe that can withstand heat. Use a cast iron or steel elbow so it does not burn through quickly. The air supply can be a hand crank blower, a vacuum cleaner motor, a car heater blower, or a bellows. For consistent heat, a small electric blower with a speed control is best. If you have no electricity, a double-chamber bellows made from leather or rubber works well. Build a table around the forge so you can rest tools and work comfortably. The table should be at waist height, about 90 cm for most adults. Cover it with sheet metal to protect from hot sparks. Add a hood and chimney to carry smoke away. In a small workshop, smoke is a health hazard. A simple hood made from a metal drum cut in half, connected to a vertical pipe, will save your lungs. Forge efficiency means getting the most heat from the least fuel. Key practices: keep the fire concentrated, not spread out. Use a deep firepot so heat reflects back onto the work. Do not blast too much air; it cools the fire and wastes fuel. Learn to read the fire. A bright yellow-white fire is too hot for most work and burns steel. A cherry red to orange fire is right for forging. A dull red fire is too cold. Use a lid or cover when heating to reduce heat loss. Preheat large pieces slowly to avoid cracking. Group your work. Instead of heating one piece at a time, heat several pieces together if they need the same temperature. This saves fuel and time. Use the forge's residual heat for annealing or tempering. When you finish forging, bury your high carbon pieces in ash or lime to cool slowly. This softens them for filing and drilling. For Wok and Pan production, you will often need to bend, punch, and weld. A forge that reaches welding heat, around 1200 to 1300 degrees Celsius, is essential. To get there, use coke or charcoal with a good air blast. Keep the fire clean. Remove clinker and ash regularly. A dirty fire creates cold spots and wastes fuel. If you use gas, choose a forge with a forced air burner or a venturi burner. Forge welding with gas is possible but requires practice. In Africa, many blacksmiths use a simple open forge with a side blast. This works well for long pieces like Wok beams. You can build a trench forge in the ground, lined with clay, with a tuyere at one end. This allows you to heat the middle of a long beam without moving it. For pan production, you need a forge that can heat a flat disk evenly. A round firepot with a wide rim works well. You can also use a separate heating hearth for large pans. Safety first. Always wear leather gloves, safety glasses, and a leather apron. Keep water or sand nearby for burns. Never use a forge in a closed room without ventilation. Carbon monoxide kills. If you use gas, check for leaks with soapy water. Store fuel away from the forge. In summary, choose a forge design that matches your fuel supply and production needs. Build it well, insulate it, and run it efficiently. A good forge will cut your fuel costs by half and double your output. 3. Hammer Techniques and Tool Making for Wok and Pan Production

Welcome to Module 2, Lesson 3. A blacksmith's hammer is not just a tool; it is an extension of the hand. In this lesson you will learn the core hammer techniques for making Wok and Pan products, and you will learn to make your own tools from local scrap. Start with the hammer itself. For heavy forging, use a 2 to 4 kg sledgehammer. For general work, a 1 to 2 kg cross-peen or straight-peen hammer is best. The peen is the wedge-shaped end. It moves metal quickly. The face is for flattening and finishing. Dress your hammer face. A slightly domed face prevents sharp edges from cutting into the steel. Remove any chips or cracks with a grinder. The handle should be hardwood, like hickory or local hard wood, fitted tightly and wedged. Do not use a loose handle. It is dangerous and wastes energy. Your anvil is the next tool. A good anvil is heavy, at least 50 kg, with a hard face and a horn. In Africa, many blacksmiths use a piece of railway line, a large forklift tine, or a heavy block of steel. Mount it securely on a stump or a steel stand. The height should be about knuckle height when you stand straight. This allows you to swing efficiently. Now the techniques. First, drawing out. This is how you make a piece longer and thinner. Heat the steel to orange. Hold it on the anvil with tongs. Strike with the peen or the face at a slight angle. Work from the center outward. Turn the piece 90 degrees and repeat. This keeps the cross section square or rectangular. For Wok beams, you will draw out the ends to fit into sockets or to form tenons. Second, upsetting. This is how you make a piece shorter and thicker. Heat the end to orange. Hold the piece vertically on the anvil. Strike the top end with a hammer. The metal will thicken. This is useful for making bolt heads or for thickening a pan rim. Third, bending. Heat the area to orange. Use the horn of the anvil or a bending fork. For smooth curves, bend over the horn. For sharp corners, use the edge of the anvil. For Wok frames, you will bend angles and curves. Make a jig from steel bars welded to a plate. This ensures every bend is identical. Fourth, punching. Heat the steel to bright orange. Use a punch made from high carbon steel, like a old file or a car axle. Drive it through the metal with a hammer. Turn the piece over and punch from the other side to meet in the middle. This makes a clean hole. For Wok and Pan, you need holes for bolts, rivets, and handles. Fifth, cutting. Use a hot chisel or a cold chisel. A hot chisel is thin and long. A cold chisel is thicker and shorter. For cutting hot steel, place the chisel on the cut line and strike with a hammer. For cold steel, use a hacksaw or angle grinder. Sixth, forge welding. This is the most difficult skill. Heat two pieces to welding temperature, which is a bright yellow-white. Sprinkle borax or fine sand on the joint to prevent oxidation. Bring them together and strike quick, light blows. Then increase the force. For Wok and Pan, you will weld handles, rims, and reinforcements. Practice on scrap until you can do it reliably. Now tool making. You need tongs, punches, chisels, drifts, and fullers. Make them from high carbon steel. For tongs, use 12 mm to 16 mm round or square bar. Forge the jaws to fit the stock you work with. Make several sizes. For punches, use 10 mm to 20 mm bar. Forge a tapered point and a flat head. For chisels, use 15 mm to 25 mm bar. Forge a cutting edge and harden it. For drifts, use round bar to enlarge holes. For fullers, use round bar to make grooves. Harden and temper your tools. Heat to non-magnetic, about 780 degrees Celsius, then quench in oil or water. For oil, use old engine oil. For water, use clean water. Then temper by heating to a straw or blue color and cooling. This gives a tough edge. In Africa, you can find excellent tool steel in old files, leaf springs, and bearing races. Do not throw away broken tools. Reforge them. Make a tool rack near your anvil. Keep your hammers, tongs, and punches within reach. Time spent looking for tools is time not forging. Practice your hammer technique daily. Use a piece of mild steel and practice drawing out, upsetting, and bending. Count your blows. Aim for accuracy, not speed. A controlled hammer blow moves metal where you want it. A wild blow wastes energy and may damage the work. For Wok and Pan production, consistency is key. Make a set of jigs and fixtures. For example, a jig for bending Wok corners, a jig for drilling pan holes, and a jig for welding handles. This allows you to train helpers and increase output. In summary, master your hammer, make your own tools, and use jigs for repeatability. Your skill with the hammer will determine the quality of your Wok and Pan products. In the next lesson we will cover heat treatment and finishing for durability and market appeal.

4. Heat Treatment and Finishing for Durability and Market Appeal

Welcome to Module 2, Lesson 4. Heat treatment is what turns a piece of shaped steel into a reliable tool or product. Without it, your Wok and Pan may bend, crack, or wear out quickly. With it, your products will last for years and earn you a reputation for quality. In this lesson you will learn the practical steps for annealing, normalizing, hardening, and tempering, plus finishing techniques that make your work sell. Start with annealing. Annealing softens steel so you can file, drill, or bend it. Heat the steel to its critical temperature, which is when it becomes non-magnetic. For most carbon steels, this is around 780 to 850 degrees Celsius. Hold it at that temperature for a few minutes, then bury it in dry ash, lime, or sand. Let it cool slowly overnight. The slower the cooling, the softer the steel. Use annealing for high carbon parts that need shaping after forging. Next, normalizing. This refines the grain structure and relieves stress. Heat to critical temperature, then let it cool in still air. Do not quench. Normalizing is good for medium carbon steel parts like Wok beams. It makes them tougher and less likely to crack. Then hardening. This makes the steel hard and wear-resistant. Heat to critical temperature, then quench quickly in oil or water. Oil is slower and safer for high carbon steel. Water is faster and can cause cracks. For Wok and Pan cutting edges, use oil quenching. For simple tools, water is fine. Move the piece in the quench to avoid soft spots. After quenching, the steel is hard but brittle. It will crack if used. So you must temper it. Tempering reduces brittleness and relieves stress. Clean the steel to bare metal. Heat it gently. Watch the oxide colors. Straw yellow, about 220 degrees Celsius, is for cutting tools like chisels and punches. Brown, about 250 degrees, is for hammers and axes. Purple, about 280 degrees, is for springs and Wok beams. Blue, about 300 degrees, is for pan rims and other parts that need toughness. When you see the right color, quench in oil or water to stop the process. If you have a temperature-controlled oven, use it. If not, use a bed of hot sand or a charcoal fire and watch the colors carefully. For Wok and Pan, heat treatment varies by part. The Wok beam should be normalized and then tempered to a spring temper, purple-blue. This gives it flexibility and strength. The pan body should be normalized and then tempered to a blue color. This prevents cracking when the pan is dropped. The cutting edge of a hoe or machete should be hardened and tempered to straw or brown. The handle should be left soft or normalized. Now finishing. A good finish protects against rust and makes the product attractive. Start by removing scale and forging marks. Use a wire brush, a file, or a belt grinder. For a smooth finish, sand with progressively finer grits, from 80 to 400. Then polish with a buffing wheel and compound if available. For a traditional look, leave some hammer marks but remove sharp edges. Next, apply a protective coating. Options include oil, wax, paint, or powder coating. For Wok and Pan used outdoors, use a good quality enamel paint or a baked-on oil finish. To bake on oil, heat the piece to about 200 degrees Celsius and rub with linseed oil or old engine oil. Repeat three times. This creates a hard, black, rust-resistant finish. For a shiny finish, use a clear lacquer or wax. In Africa, customers often prefer a black or dark finish. You can achieve this by heating the steel and dipping it in oil, or by using a chemical blackening agent. If you use paint, choose a brand that resists chipping and UV light. Apply two thin coats rather than one thick coat. Let it dry fully before handling. For pans, make sure the finish is food-safe. Do not use lead-based paints or toxic oils. Use food-grade mineral oil or a baked-on vegetable oil finish. Season the pan like a cast iron skillet. This protects it and makes it non-stick. For Wok, the finish should be durable and weather-resistant. Use a primer and a topcoat. If the Wok will be used in wet conditions, consider galvanizing or using a zinc-rich paint. But be careful: welding galvanized steel releases toxic fumes. Always work in a well-ventilated area. Quality control is part of finishing. Check every product for cracks, sharp edges, loose rivets, and proper fit. Test moving parts. For a Wok, make sure the beam is straight and the holes align. For a pan, make sure it sits flat and the handle is secure. A simple checklist will save you returns and complaints. Packaging and presentation matter. In local markets, a clean, well-finished product sells faster and at a higher price. Wrap your products in paper or cloth. Attach a small tag with your name and a care instruction. If you sell in bulk, use a sturdy box or crate. In summary, heat treatment and finishing are not optional. They are what separate a rough forging from a professional product. Learn the colors, practice the quench, and take pride in your finish. In the next lesson we will cover pricing, marketing, and selling your Wok and Pan products in African markets.

5. Pricing, Marketing, and Selling Wok and Pan Products in African Markets

Welcome to Module 2, Lesson 5. You have learned to source steel, build a forge, hammer, and finish. Now you must sell. This lesson is about the business side: pricing, marketing, and distribution for Wok and Pan products in African markets. Your goal is not just to make a sale, but to build a brand that customers trust and return to. Start with cost calculation. You must know your true cost per unit. List every input: steel, fuel, labor, consumables like grinding discs and paint, transport, and overhead like rent and electricity. Add a percentage for wear and tear on tools. Then add your profit margin. A common mistake is to price based on what competitors charge without knowing your own costs. If you do that, you may be losing money on every sale. Use a simple formula: total cost plus desired profit equals selling price. For example, if a Wok costs 5000 naira in materials and labor, and you want a 30 percent profit, sell it for 6500 naira. But be careful: in many African markets, customers bargain. So set your initial price higher than your minimum. Know your walk-away price. Never sell below cost. If a customer cannot pay, offer a smaller size or a different product. Do not devalue your work. Next, understand your market. Who buys Wok and Pan? Farmers, builders, market women, households, and institutions like schools and hotels. Each group has different needs. A farmer wants a durable Wok for plowing. A market woman wants a pan that is light and easy to clean. A hotel wants a large pan that heats evenly. Talk to your customers. Visit farms, markets, and construction sites. Ask what they like and dislike about current products. Then design your products to meet those needs. For example, if farmers complain that Wok handles break, make a stronger handle. If market women say pans are too heavy, use thinner steel or a different design. Marketing in Africa is often word of mouth. But you can do more. Create a simple sign for your workshop. Use bright colors and clear text. Include your phone number. Make business cards or flyers. Give them to customers and suppliers. Use social media. WhatsApp, Facebook, and Instagram are powerful. Post photos of your products and your workshop. Show the process. People love to see how things are made. Ask satisfied customers to give testimonials. Offer a small discount for referrals. Attend local markets and trade fairs. Set up a table with your products. Demonstrate your skills. Let people hold the Wok and Pan. A hands-on experience sells. Partner with hardware stores, agricultural supply shops, and online marketplaces. Offer them a wholesale price. Make sure they display your products well. Provide a small commission for sales. Build relationships with local government and NGOs. They often buy tools for community projects. Register your business if possible. A formal business can bid on contracts and open a bank account. Keep records. Use a notebook or a simple accounting app. Track sales, expenses, and debts. This helps you know what is profitable and what is not. Customer service is key. Deliver on time. If you promise a product on Friday, have it ready. If there is a delay, communicate. Offer a guarantee. For example, if a Wok breaks within three months due to a manufacturing defect, repair or replace it. This builds trust. Handle complaints calmly. A dissatisfied customer tells many people. A satisfied customer also tells many people. Make it easy for them to say good things. Pricing strategies for different markets: In rural areas, price lower but sell in volume. In cities, price higher and emphasize quality and finish. For export or tourist markets, use traditional designs and premium finishes. You can also offer customization. A farmer may want a Wok with a specific length. A hotel may want a pan with a logo. Charge extra for customization. Consider offering a product line: basic, standard, and premium. The basic model uses mild steel and a simple finish. The standard uses medium carbon steel and a good finish. The premium uses high carbon steel, a polished finish, and a warranty. This allows you to serve different customers with the same workshop. Payment methods: cash is common, but mobile money is growing. In Kenya, M-Pesa. In Ghana, MTN Mobile Money. In Nigeria, bank transfer and USSD. Accept multiple payment methods. This makes it easy for customers to pay. For large orders, ask for a deposit. This covers your material costs and reduces risk. Finally, think about growth. As your sales increase, hire and train apprentices. Teach them your methods. Pay them fairly. Use their skills to increase production. Invest in better tools and a larger workshop. But do not grow too fast. Keep your quality high. A good reputation is your most valuable asset. In summary, price for profit, market with stories and demonstrations, sell through multiple channels, and serve your customers well. Your Wok and Pan business can thrive in Africa if you combine blacksmith skills with business sense. This concludes Module 2. 6. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Workshop Math, Material Yields, and Client-Ready Specifications for Wok and Pan Blacksmiths

This practical reference lesson is built for working blacksmiths, apprentices, and small-scale metal fabricators who already know how to source scrap, design a forge, hammer steel, and heat-treat a finished Wok or pan. The gap this lesson closes is commercial, not technical: most African blacksmiths can make a good product, but many lose money because they cannot quote confidently, cannot estimate how much steel a job will consume, and cannot describe their work in the standard terms that institutional buyers, agro-dealers, and NGO procurement officers expect. This module gives you the numbers, formulas, and reference tables to price, plan, and present your work like a business.

Throughout, "Wok" refers to the woke or hoe blade assembly common in West, Central, and East African farming systems, and "pan" refers to the broad-bladed pan or shovel used for land preparation, harvesting, and construction. The exact form varies by region, but the business logic is identical: you are converting scrap steel, fuel, labour, and skill into a finished tool that a farmer or contractor will pay for. Your profit lives in the difference between what you charge and what the job actually costs you.

Forging, grinding, and finishing steel produces metal fume, silica dust, and sparks. WHO and ILO occupational health guidance classifies welding and metal fume as a respiratory hazard associated with metal fume fever and long-term lung damage. Always forge and grind in a well-ventilated area or outdoors with the wind at your back, wear eye protection when grinding, use leather gloves and apron when handling hot stock, and never quench or grind galvanised or painted scrap without respiratory protection â zinc and paint fumes are acutely toxic. Keep a bucket of clean water and a dry sand bucket within arm's reach of the forge. Do not forge in enclosed rooms with charcoal or coal; carbon monoxide kills silently.

Two rules of thumb that keep African blacksmiths profitable:

  • Price the job, not the hour. Institutional buyers (NGOs, cooperatives, agro-dealers) buy units, not hours. Quote per finished tool, with a clear specification sheet.

Specification Sheet: What Institutional Buyers Expect

When you sell to an NGO, cooperative, or agro-dealer, they will ask for a written specification. Prepare a one-page sheet for each product with these fields:

  • Product name and code (e.g., "Medium Pan MP-2")
  • Dimensions: blade length, blade width, thickness at edge and spine, handle length, total weight
  • Steel type and source: e.g., "recycled leaf spring, medium-carbon"
  • Heat treatment: quench medium, temper colour or temperature
  • Finish: ground edge angle, handle fitting, surface treatment
  • Durability claim: e.g., "edge retains sharpness for 40â60 hours of typical field use"
  • Unit price, minimum order, lead time
  • Warranty or replacement policy (even a simple 30-day replacement builds trust)

This sheet alone can move you from selling one tool at a time at a roadside market to filling a 200-unit order for a cooperative. It costs you nothing but an hour to write and it signals that you are a business, not a hobbyist.
