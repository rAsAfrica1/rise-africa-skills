Lessons

1. Testing Starch Purity and Whiteness

Purity and whiteness determine the price and use of your cassava starch. Buyers reject starch that is grey, yellow, or gritty. To test purity, you need to separate starch from fibre, sand, and protein. A simple method is the sedimentation test. Take 10 grams of dry starch, mix with 100 ml of clean water in a clear glass jar. Stir well and let it stand for 2 hours. Pure starch settles to the bottom as a dense white layer. Fibre and impurities float or form a thin brown layer on top. Carefully pour off the water and top layer. Dry the settled starch and weigh it. If you started with 10 g and get 9 g or more, purity is above 90%. For whiteness, use a simple whiteness scale. You can buy a cheap whiteness meter (like a portable rice whiteness tester) for around $50. Or use a visual comparison: place a small pile of your starch next to a pure white tile or a commercial starch sample. In Africa, many buyers accept starch with a whiteness of 85% or higher. To improve whiteness, wash the starch slurry more times. After the first settling, add fresh water, stir, and settle again. Repeat 3 times. Also, avoid iron tools during processing because iron causes grey spots. Use stainless steel or plastic. Dry starch in a clean, shaded area away from dust. If you see black specks, those are sand or dirt. Sieve the dry starch through a 100-mesh sieve to remove them. Record your results in a notebook for each batch. This helps you track quality and adjust your process. Remember, consistent purity and whiteness build trust with buyers and allow you to sell at a premium price.

2. Moisture Content Determination

Moisture content is the amount of water left in your dried starch. Too much moisture causes mould, caking, and short shelf life. Too little wastes energy and reduces yield. The standard for cassava starch is 12-14% moisture. To measure moisture without a lab, use the oven-drying method. You need a small digital scale accurate to 0.1 g (cost about $20) and a heat source. An oven or a solar dryer works. Weigh a clean, dry metal plate or aluminium foil. Record its weight. Add 10 g of starch sample and spread it thin. Weigh the plate plus starch. Put it in an oven at 105C for 3 hours. Remove and cool in a dry place (not open air, as it absorbs moisture). Weigh again. The difference in weight is the water lost. For example, if you started with 10 g and after drying you have 8.7 g, moisture is 1.3 g, which is 13%. That is acceptable. If you have no oven, use a frying pan on low heat. Stir constantly to avoid burning. When the starch stops steaming and feels bone dry, it is done. But this method is less accurate. For a quick field test, squeeze a handful of starch. If it holds together and feels damp, moisture is above 15%. If it crumbles and feels dry, it is below 12%. Always test each batch before packaging. Store dried starch in airtight bags or containers in a dry room. In humid African climates, moisture can rise during storage. So test again before selling. Keep records of moisture for every batch. If moisture is too high, spread the starch in the sun for a few hours or run it through a dryer again. Never pack hot starch; let it cool first. Consistent moisture control prevents losses and keeps customers happy.

3. Viscosity and Gelatinization Tests

Viscosity is how thick your starch becomes when cooked. Gelatinization is the point when starch granules swell and thicken in hot water. High-quality cassava starch should have high viscosity and a clear gel. To test viscosity simply, make a paste. Mix 20 g of starch with 100 ml of cold water in a pot. Stir until smooth. Heat on a stove, stirring constantly. As it heats, the mixture will thicken. Note the temperature when it starts to thicken. For cassava starch, this is usually around 60-70C. Continue heating to 80C and hold for 5 minutes. You now have a gel. Let it cool. A good gel is thick, smooth, and slightly clear. If it is watery, your starch has low viscosity, possibly due to too much moisture or poor extraction. If it is lumpy, you did not stir well or the starch had impurities. To measure viscosity more objectively, use a simple funnel test. Fill a funnel with the hot gel and time how long it takes to flow out. Pure cassava starch gel takes about 30-60 seconds for 100 ml. If it takes longer, it is too thick (maybe high amylose, which is rare in cassava). If it flows too fast (under 20 seconds), viscosity is low. Another test is the finger test: dip a clean finger in the cool gel. It should coat the finger and drip slowly. For gelatinization, you can also check clarity. A clear gel indicates high purity. A cloudy gel means leftover fibre or protein. To improve viscosity, ensure your starch is fully dried and stored properly. Avoid overheating during drying, as it can break down starch. Also, use fresh roots; old or rotten roots give low viscosity. Record your results: temperature of thickening, flow time, and gel appearance. This helps you compare batches and maintain quality for buyers who use starch in food, paper, or textiles.

4. Microbiological Testing

Microbiological testing checks for harmful bacteria, mould, and yeast in your starch. High levels can cause food poisoning or spoilage. In Africa, many small producers skip this test, but buyers, especially exporters, require it. You can do simple tests without a full lab. First, look for visible mould. Green, black, or white fuzzy spots on starch mean mould growth. Discard such batches. For bacteria, use a total plate count (TPC) test. You can buy ready-made petri dishes with nutrient agar from a lab supply store (about $2 per plate). Or make your own: boil 10 g of agar powder in 500 ml of water, pour into clean jars, and sterilise by steaming for 30 minutes. Let cool but not set. Open a petri dish and sprinkle 1 g of starch sample on the agar. Close and tape. Keep in a warm place (25-30C) for 48 hours. Count the colonies (spots). For good quality starch, TPC should be less than 10,000 colonies per gram. If you see more than 100 colonies on your plate, that indicates poor hygiene. For mould and yeast, use potato dextrose agar (PDA). Incubate for 3-5 days. Mould colonies are fuzzy; yeast are smooth. Acceptable levels: less than 100 colonies per gram for mould, less than 1000 for yeast. To prevent microbial growth, dry starch to below 14% moisture. Store in clean, dry, airtight containers. Wash all equipment with hot water and soap before use. Use clean water for processing. Avoid touching starch with bare hands; use gloves or clean scoops. If you cannot test, follow strict hygiene: wash roots, peel, and grate quickly. Ferment or settle starch in clean water, and change water often. Sun-dry on clean mats, not on the ground. These steps reduce microbes. For export, send samples to a certified lab. Regular testing builds confidence and opens bigger markets.

5. Detection of Adulterants

Adulterants are cheap substances mixed into starch to increase weight or profit. Common adulterants in cassava starch include talc, chalk, gypsum, wheat flour, and even sand. These lower quality and can harm consumers. You can detect them with simple tests. First, visual inspection: pure cassava starch is bright white and smooth. If you see grey or off-white powder, it may contain chalk or talc. Rub a small amount between your fingers. Pure starch feels smooth and squeaky. Adulterants feel gritty or powdery. Second, water test: mix 10 g of starch in 100 ml of water. Pure starch settles slowly and forms a dense layer. Sand and grit settle immediately at the bottom. Chalk makes the water cloudy and milky. Talc floats as a fine film. Third, iodine test: add a few drops of iodine solution (available at pharmacies) to a starch sample. Pure starch turns dark blue-black. If it turns red or orange, it may contain wheat flour or other starches. If it stays yellow-brown, it may be chalk or talc. Fourth, acid test: add a few drops of vinegar to a sample. If it fizzes, it contains chalk or limestone. Fifth, burn test: burn a small amount of starch in a spoon. Pure starch burns with a sweet smell and leaves little ash. Adulterants like talc or sand leave a lot of ash or a hard residue. For sand, dry a sample and weigh. Then mix with water, stir, and let settle. Pour off the starch and water. The sand remains at the bottom. Dry and weigh the sand. If it is more than 0.5% of the original weight, the starch is adulterated. To avoid adulteration, buy from trusted suppliers, or produce your own. If you suspect a batch, do these tests before selling. Never mix adulterants yourself; it ruins your reputation and can be illegal. Keep records of tests and reject any batch that fails.

6. Standard Specifications for Cassava Starch

To sell cassava starch, you must meet standard specifications. These are the rules that buyers, especially exporters, use to judge quality. The main standards are from the Codex Alimentarius (international) and local African standards like NIS (Nigeria), KS (Kenya), or SANS (South Africa). Here are the key specifications for food-grade cassava starch. Moisture: maximum 14% (ideally 12-13%). Purity: minimum 96% starch content. Whiteness: minimum 85% (some buyers want 90%+). Viscosity: high, with a gel that is clear and thick. pH: between 4.5 and 7.0 (slightly acidic to neutral). Ash: maximum 0.5% (ash indicates mineral content; high ash means sand or dirt). Fibre: maximum 0.5%. Protein: maximum 0.5%. Sulphur dioxide: maximum 50 mg/kg (used in some processing, but too much is harmful). Heavy metals: lead max 0.5 mg/kg, arsenic max 0.5 mg/kg. Microbiological: total plate count max 10,000 cfu/g; mould max 100 cfu/g; yeast max 1000 cfu/g; E. coli and Salmonella absent. For industrial grade (e.g., for glue or paper), specifications are less strict on whiteness and microbiology but still require low moisture and high viscosity. To meet these, you need good practices: use clean water, stainless steel equipment, proper drying, and hygienic storage. Test each batch for moisture, purity, and whiteness at minimum. For export, send samples to a certified lab for full analysis. Keep a certificate of analysis for each lot. Label your bags with batch number, production date, and moisture content. This traceability builds trust. If you cannot meet all specs, sell to local markets that accept lower grades. But aim for the standard. Many African countries have agencies that help small producers with testing and certification, such as the Standards Organisation of Nigeria (SON) or Kenya Bureau of Standards (KEBS). Contact them for guidance. Meeting standards opens doors to higher prices and bigger buyers.

<h2>Practical Reference: Quality Control and Analysis for Cassava Starch Extraction</h2>

<p>In African cassava starch enterprises from smallholder cooperatives in Nigeria's Ogun State to mid-sized processors in Ghana's Ashanti Region, Tanzania's Coast Region, and Uganda's Busoga sub-region quality control is not a laboratory luxury. It is the difference between selling starch at commodity prices for local adhesive and food use, and commanding premium prices from food, pharmaceutical, textile, and paper buyers. This practical reference consolidates the core tests you need to run, the specifications buyers expect, and the business decisions each test informs.</p>

<p>Cassava (<em>Manihot esculenta</em> Crantz) starch is valued for its neutral taste, high paste clarity, and low gelatinization temperature. But these virtues are easily lost through poor extraction water quality, delayed drying, or contamination. Every test below maps to a commercial risk: rejected consignments, price discounts, or loss of a buyer contract.</p>

<h3>1. Testing Starch Purity and Whiteness</h3>
<p>Purity and whiteness are the first signals a buyer reads. Residual fibre, sand, and protein lower both. In African processing, the biggest purity failures come from inadequate washing of roots, worn raspers, and open-air drying on bare ground. Whiteness is measured either visually against a standard or with a reflectance colorimeter (L* value). A practical field method uses a simple whiteness scale or a calibrated colour chart.</p>

<h3>2. Moisture Content Determination</h3>
<p>Moisture is the single most commercially critical parameter in African starch trade. High moisture (above 14%) invites mould, caking, and rapid quality loss during transport often by open truck over long distances. Low moisture (below 10%) increases dust and handling losses. The standard method is oven drying at 105C to constant weight. A moisture meter calibrated for starch can be used for rapid in-process checks, but must be verified against the oven method weekly.</p>

<h3>3. Viscosity and Gelatinization Tests</h3>
<p>Viscosity determines end-use. A starch that thickens poorly will fail in food, textile sizing, and adhesive applications. Cassava starch typically gelatinizes between 58C and 70C, lower than maize or potato starch an advantage in low-energy processing. The Brabender Viscoamylograph or a rapid visco analyser (RVA) gives a full pasting curve, but a simple Brookfield viscometer reading on a cooked 5% slurry is a practical proxy in African labs.</p>
<p>Key indicators: peak viscosity (thickening power), breakdown (shear stability), and setback (retrogradation on cooling). High setback indicates the starch will gel on cooling desirable for some adhesives but not for clear food sauces.</p>

<h3>4. Microbiological Testing</h3>
<p>Microbiological quality matters most for food and pharmaceutical buyers. Common tests include total plate count (TPC), yeast and mould, <em>E. coli</em>, and <em>Salmonella</em>. In many African processing settings, contamination arises from unclean water, unwashed hands, and open drying. A TPC above 10 CFU/g is a red flag for food-grade starch.</p>
<p>Practical controls: chlorinated wash water (50100 ppm free chlorine), covered drying, and clean packaging. For small processors without a lab, partner with a national food laboratory or a university (e.g., University of Ibadan, Makerere University, University of Ghana) for periodic testing.</p>

<h3>5. Detection of Adulterants</h3>
<p>Adulteration mixing cassava starch with cheaper flours such as maize, wheat, or rice is a real risk in informal markets. Buyers use iodine tests (cassava starch gives a blue-black colour, but so do other starches), microscopic examination of granule shape (cassava granules are round to oval with a characteristic hilum), and, for serious buyers, near-infrared spectroscopy or HPLC. A simple field check: cassava starch produces a clearer, less opaque paste than maize starch at the same concentration.</p>

<h3>6. Standard Specifications for Cassava Starch</h3>
<p>African processors should align with the Codex Standard for Cassava Starch (CODEX STAN 176-1989) and, where applicable, East African Community (EAC) standards, Nigerian (SON), Ghanaian (GS), and Kenyan (KEBS) national standards. Typical specification ranges are summarised in Table 1. Buyer contracts often add tighter limits on whiteness, moisture, and microbiological counts.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #b30000;background:#ffe6e6;padding:12px;margin:18px 0;border-radius:6px;">
<strong style="color:#b30000;"> SAFETY WARNING Hydrocyanic Acid (HCN) and Aflatoxin Risk</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Fresh cassava roots contain linamarin, which releases hydrogen cyanide (HCN) during processing. Inadequate washing, soaking, or drying can leave dangerous HCN residues in starch. Additionally, poorly dried or stored starch can develop aflatoxins from mould. Never sell or consume starch that has not been tested for HCN (target &lt; 10 mg/kg) and aflatoxin (target &lt; 10 g/kg for total aflatoxins). Wear gloves and eye protection when handling processing water and chemicals. Ensure drying areas are covered and away from livestock.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li>Sample each batch at three points: after rasping, after dewatering, and after drying.</li>
<li>Check moisture with a calibrated meter daily; verify weekly against oven method.</li>
<li>Measure whiteness (L*) against a standard; record batch number and date.</li>
<li>Run a viscosity check on cooked 5% slurry for every export batch.</li>
<li>Test TPC, yeast, and mould at least monthly; more often for food-grade contracts.</li>
<li>Screen for adulterants using iodine and microscopic granule shape checks.</li>
<li>Confirm HCN and aflatoxin levels before releasing any batch for food use.</li>
<li>Keep a batch record book: date, raw material source, test results, decision, buyer.</li>
</ul>
