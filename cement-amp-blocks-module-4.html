Lessons

1. Setting up your block moulds: wooden vs. metal moulds

Before you make blocks, you need a mould. You can buy one or make it yourself. The two main types are wooden and metal. Wooden moulds are cheaper and easier to make if you have basic carpentry skills. Use hardwood like mahogany or iroko, not softwood like pine, because softwood warps when wet. A standard wooden mould for a 6-inch block (15 cm x 20 cm x 40 cm) costs about $20"$30 in materials. But wooden moulds wear out faster"after 200"300 blocks, the edges round off and blocks become uneven. Metal moulds, usually made from 2"3 mm steel sheet, cost $50"$100 but last for thousands of blocks. They keep their shape better, so your blocks are more uniform. If you plan to sell blocks, invest in metal. For a small backyard project, wood is fine. When setting up, make sure the mould is square: measure diagonals"they must be equal. Bolt or screw all joints tightly. Any gap larger than 1 mm will let mortar leak and create weak edges. For mass production, build a mould that makes 2 or 3 blocks at once. A 3-block metal mould can cost $150 but triples your output. Set the mould on a flat, level surface"a wooden pallet or concrete slab. If the ground is uneven, blocks will be different thicknesses. Also, decide if you want a bottom plate or if you will cast on the ground. A bottom plate makes moving easier but adds cost. In Africa, many artisans use a simple wooden mould with a removable base. Whatever you choose, keep the mould clean and oiled (next lesson). Check for wear weekly. Replace or repair any bent sides. A good mould is the backbone of block making.

2. Preparing the mould: oiling and cleaning for easy release

If you skip oiling, your blocks will stick, and you will break corners trying to remove them. Oil creates a thin barrier so the concrete does not bond to the mould. Use any light oil: used engine oil, palm oil, or commercial release agent. Used engine oil is cheap and works well, but it stains the blocks dark. If you want clean-looking blocks, use vegetable oil or a proper mould oil. Apply oil with a rag or brush. Coat all inside surfaces, especially corners and edges. Do not pour oil"just a thin film. Too much oil can cause surface bubbles. Clean the mould after every use. Scrape off stuck concrete with a wooden scraper or wire brush. Do not use a metal hammer"it dents the mould. Rinse with water and dry. Then oil again before the next block. If you are making many blocks, oil the mould every 5"10 blocks, or whenever you see the mix starting to stick. In hot, dry weather, oil evaporates faster, so oil more often. For wooden moulds, oil also protects the wood from water damage. But do not soak the wood"too much oil can soften it. A good routine: after demoulding, clean the mould immediately while concrete is still green (soft). Then oil it and set it aside for the next batch. If you are using a manual machine, follow the manufacturer's instructions"some machines need grease on moving parts, not oil on the mould faces. Keep a small bucket of oil and a rag near your work area. A dirty, rusty mould will ruin your blocks. In Africa, where dust and sand are common, cover your moulds when not in use to keep them clean. Proper preparation takes 2 minutes per mould but saves hours of frustration.

3. Filling and compacting the mix for solid blocks

Filling and compacting is where strength is made or lost. Your mix should be stiff"like damp sand that holds its shape when squeezed. A typical mix for solid blocks is 1 part cement to 6 parts sand (by volume) for a 6-inch block. For stronger blocks, use 1:5. Add water gradually; too much water weakens the block. A good test: squeeze a handful"it should form a ball that does not crumble or drip water. Now fill the mould. Use a shovel or trowel to place the mix in layers. For a 6-inch block, fill halfway (about 10 cm deep), then compact. Compaction removes air pockets. Use a wooden tamper or a steel rod. Tamp evenly across the surface"about 20"25 strokes per layer. Do not just poke; lift the tamper and drop it. For the second layer, fill to the top of the mould and tamp again. Overfill slightly (1"2 cm) because the mix will settle. Then strike off the excess with a straight edge (a piece of wood or steel). Move the straight edge in a sawing motion across the top. Do not press down hard"just level it. For hollow blocks, you will use a core (a metal or wooden insert) to create voids. But for solid blocks, you want a dense, uniform fill. If you see voids or honeycomb after demoulding, you did not compact enough. In Africa, many makers use a manual vibration method: tap the sides of the mould with a hammer or lift the mould 2"3 cm and drop it on the ground 10 times. This settles the mix. But be careful"too much dropping can cause segregation. After filling and compacting, let the block sit for 10"15 minutes before moving. This allows initial set. Then demould (next lesson). Remember: a well-compacted block is 30% stronger than a poorly compacted one. Take your time.

4. Using a manual block-making machine vs. hand tamping

You have two main options for compacting: a manual block-making machine or hand tamping. A manual machine (like a CINVA-Ram or a local copy) uses a lever to compress the mix. It costs $200"$500 but produces denser, stronger blocks and is faster. With a machine, you fill the mould, pull the lever, and the block is compressed with high pressure. You can make 200"400 blocks per day with a team. Hand tamping uses a wooden or metal tamper to compact the mix manually. It is cheap (a tamper costs $5) but slower"maybe 50"100 blocks per day. Hand tamping also gives less consistent density; some blocks will be stronger than others. If you are building a house for yourself, hand tamping is fine. If you are selling blocks, a machine pays for itself in a few months. But machines need maintenance: grease the moving parts, check the lever, and replace worn parts. In rural Africa, where spare parts are hard to find, a simple wooden tamper is more reliable. You can also make a simple vibrating table from a small motor and a metal plate. That costs $100"$200 and works well for small production. The key difference is pressure. A manual machine applies 2"5 MPa (megapascals) of pressure; hand tamping gives maybe 0.5"1 MPa. Higher pressure means you can use less cement (1:7 mix instead of 1:6) and still get strong blocks. But for beginners, start with hand tamping to learn the feel of the mix. Then upgrade to a machine when you have consistent sales. Whichever you choose, the goal is a dense block with no voids. Test your blocks: drop one from waist height onto a hard surface. If it shatters, your compaction or mix is wrong. If it stays intact, you are doing well.

5. Removing blocks from moulds without breaking corners

Removing blocks too early or roughly is the number one cause of broken corners. Wait until the block is firm enough to hold its shape but still soft enough to release easily. This is usually 10"30 minutes after compaction, depending on weather. In hot, dry weather, it might be 10 minutes; in cool, humid weather, 30 minutes. Test by pressing the top of the block with your thumb. If it leaves a slight dent but does not crumble, it is ready. To remove, first loosen the mould. For a wooden mould, tap the sides gently with a rubber mallet or a block of wood. Do not use a metal hammer"it will crack the wood. For a metal mould, you may have a release mechanism (a lever or a screw). If not, tap the sides. Then lift the mould straight up. Do not twist or tilt"that breaks corners. If the block sticks, do not force it. Wait 5 more minutes and try again. If it still sticks, your oiling was insufficient. Next time, oil more. For a bottomless mould (casting on the ground), you lift the mould off the block. For a mould with a bottom plate, you flip the whole thing over onto a pallet or board, then lift the mould. Always lift the mould, not the block. After demoulding, leave the block on a flat pallet in the shade for 24 hours. Do not move it. Then cure it by watering it twice a day for 7 days. Broken corners mean wasted cement and time. A good demoulder can remove 200 blocks per day with less than 5% breakage. In Africa, many use a simple wooden mould with a removable base. Practice on a few blocks first. If you break one, remix it"do not throw it away. With practice, you will get a feel for the right time.

6. Making special blocks: hollow, solid, and interlocking types

Once you master solid blocks, you can make special types that save cement, reduce weight, or speed up construction. Hollow blocks have two or three vertical voids. They use 30"40% less cement and are lighter, so they are easier to carry. To make them, you need a mould with cores"metal or wooden inserts that create the holes. Fill the mould around the cores, compact, and remove the cores after demoulding. A standard hollow block (6-inch) has two voids, each about 10 cm x 15 cm. The wall thickness should be at least 3 cm for strength. Hollow blocks are popular in Africa because they save money. Interlocking blocks have a tongue-and-groove shape so they fit together without mortar. They are faster to build and stronger in earthquake zones. But they require a precise mould, usually metal, and a strong mix (1:5 cement to sand). You can buy interlocking moulds for $100"$300. They are common in East Africa (e.g., the "Interlocking Stabilised Soil Block" or ISSB). You can also make solid blocks with a decorative face"just add a metal plate with a pattern to the mould. For all special blocks, the mix must be consistent. Use a measuring box (a 20-litre bucket) for cement and sand. Do not guess. Curing is critical: hollow and interlocking blocks need 14 days of curing because they have less mass. Water them twice a day. Test each batch: break one block after 7 days. If it breaks easily, increase cement. In Africa, many artisans make hollow blocks with a simple wooden core. Start with one type, perfect it, then expand. Remember: special blocks sell for 20"50% more than solid blocks, so they are worth learning.

<h2>Practical Reference: Block Curing, Testing and Costing for African Markets</h2>

<p>Curing is the step most small block yards in Africa get wrong, and it is the step that decides whether your blocks earn money or become rubble. A block that looks perfect on the pallet can lose 4060% of its design strength if it dries too fast in the sun, is stacked too early, or is made with dirty water. This lesson gives you the numbers, the tests, and the costing model you need to run a block yard as a business not a hobby.</p>

<p>The practical rule for a commercial yard: <strong>cure 7 days minimum, sell at 14 days, and never sell a block you have not broken in a test.</strong> Everything below supports that rule with field-ready figures.</p>

<h3>Why curing is a business decision, not a technical detail</h3>

<p>In most African markets Nairobi, Kampala, Lusaka, Accra, Kigali a 6-inch solid block sells for between USD 0.35 and USD 0.90 depending on cement content and finish. The cement is 5575% of your variable cost. If your curing is bad, you do not lose the cement cost you lose the whole block, and you lose the customer who bought it. A single failed wall in a school or church project can cost a yard its reputation for years.</p>

<p>Two numbers drive your margin: <strong>cement per block</strong> and <strong>breakage rate</strong>. A well-run yard keeps breakage under 3%. A poorly run yard runs 1525% during demoulding and transport. That difference is the entire profit margin on a small yard.</p>

<strong style="color:#a33; font-size:15px;"> SAFETY WARNING CEMENT AND DUST</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; font-size:13px; color:#333;">Cement is strongly alkaline (pH ~1213). Direct skin contact causes chemical burns, and wet cement inside boots or gloves is the most common cause of serious injury on small block yards. Dry cement dust irritates eyes and lungs and can cause silicosis with long-term exposure. Always wear waterproof gloves, boots, eye protection, and a dust mask when mixing. Never kneel in wet mortar. Wash any cement-contaminated skin immediately with clean water. Keep cement bags away from children and store them dry and off the ground. If cement enters the eye, rinse with clean water for at least 15 minutes and seek medical help.</p>

<h3>Field tests you can do without a lab</h3>

<p>You cannot afford a compression testing machine on a small yard. But you can use three field tests that catch 90% of bad batches before they reach a customer:</p>

<p><strong>1. The drop test.</strong> Take a cured block (14 days), lift it to chest height, and drop it flat onto a hard surface. A good load-bearing block cracks but does not shatter. If it breaks into three or more pieces, your mix is too lean or your curing was too fast.</p>

<p><strong>2. The scratch test.</strong> Scratch the block face with a nail. If it powders easily, the cement has not hydrated properly usually too little water during curing, or curing was skipped. If it resists and leaves a shallow mark, it is sound.</p>

<p><strong>3. The water drop test.</strong> Drop a small amount of water on the block face. If it is absorbed in under 5 seconds, the block is porous and will need plastering. If it beads slightly and absorbs over 1020 seconds, the surface is dense enough for exposed work.</p>

<p>Do these tests on 1 in every 500 blocks, or at least once per production day. Record the results in a notebook. When a customer complains, you have data and data protects your reputation.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul style="font-size:14px; line-height:1.7;">
<li><strong>Clean water only.</strong> No saline, no muddy, no recycled wash water on finished blocks.</li>
<li><strong>Shade for the first 48 hours.</strong> Direct sun cracks blocks before they gain strength.</li>
<li><strong>Water 2 daily for days 17</strong>, then 1 daily until day 14. Keep burlap or grass cover damp.</li>
<li><strong>Do not stack higher than 4 blocks</strong> in the first week. Stack higher only after day 8.</li>
<li><strong>Never sell before day 14.</strong> Sell at day 1421 for best strength and lowest breakage.</li>
<li><strong>Test 1 in 500 blocks</strong> with the drop, scratch, and water tests. Record results.</li>
<li><strong>Keep 5% breakage allowance</strong> in your pricing. If breakage exceeds 10%, fix the mix or curing.</li>
<li><strong>Wear gloves, boots, eye protection, and a dust mask</strong> every time you handle cement or mix dry materials.</li>
</ul>
