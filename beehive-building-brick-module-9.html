Module 9 — Hive Stands, Bases and Apiary Layout

Plinths, bases and permanent siting — laying out an apiary that will still work in ten years.

Learning outcomes

  • Design a plinth or foundation that keeps a masonry hive dry, level and permanent.
  • Detail the base — the interface between concrete and the bee space.
  • Lay out a permanent apiary that respects hive weight, access and drainage.
  • Control termites, ants and moisture through masonry design.
  • Plan for future expansion without rebuilding.

1. A brick hive needs a plinth, not a stand

Wooden hives sit on stands. Brick hives sit on plinths. The difference is weight, permanence and the fact that a masonry hive has nowhere to move once built.

Design the plinth before the first brick is laid. It must:

  1. Lift the hive off the ground by 300–500 mm — enough to work comfortably and to keep water and crawl-in pests out.
  2. Carry the full hive weight — a masonry hive plus full honey supers can weigh 400–600 kg.
  3. Keep the hive level side to side and tilted 5–10 mm toward the entrance.
  4. Break the termite and ant path — a metal or concrete barrier, or a termite-resistant plinth.
  5. Drain away from the hive — the plinth should sit on ground that falls away on all sides.

How much weight is on the plinth?

Brood box + 2 supers + roof, loaded:
Walls and roof: ~180 kg
Comb, honey and bees: ~140 kg
Total ≈ 320 kg on a footprint of 0.55 × 0.55 m = 0.30 m².
Bearing pressure = 320 × 9.81 / 0.30 = 10 500 Pa = 10.5 kPa.

Typical firm soil bears 100–200 kPa and stiff clay 150–300 kPa. The bearing pressure is trivial, so the design driver is not soil strength — it is differential settlement and termites. Footing depth and pest barrier matter more than width.

2. Base details for a masonry hive

The base is the only part of a brick hive that should follow the same logic as a wooden hive: a flat, level, screened floor with a controlled entrance.

Options

Base typeConstructionBest for
Solid masonry baseConcrete slab with a timber or metal entrance stripCool climates; simple construction
Screened masonry baseSlab with a central mesh panel and a slider tray belowHot, humid climates; varroa monitoring
Timber base on masonry plinthWooden base board set on the plinth, hive walls built around itWhere you may want to change bases later
Removable base moduleA separate base unit that can be lifted out for cleaningHigh hygiene requirement; disease recovery

Construction notes

  • Mesh: galvanised #8 (3 mm aperture), stretched flat, fixed to a removable frame.
  • Slider: galvanised or HDPE sheet, removable from the back, with a 20 mm lip.
  • Entrance: full width × 20 mm, with a reducer. In a brick hive the entrance is often a slot cast into the base — make it clean and square.
  • Landing: a 75 mm concrete or stone apron, sloping down and out, front of the entrance.
  • Bee space at the join: keep 6–9 mm between the base and the first frame. A wrong gap here is where burr comb and propolis build up.

The base is the hardest part to change

Once the walls are up, changing the base means dismantling the hive. Get it right at build stage. If you are unsure between solid and screened, build screened with a slider — you can always close it, but you cannot easily open a solid slab.

3. Permanent apiary layout

A brick apiary is a permanent installation. Layout decisions are effectively forever.

Spacing for brick hives

ElementMinimumPreferredReason
Plinth to plinth, same row1.5 m2.0 mBrick hives are wider than wooden; working space
Row to row3.0 m4.0 mFlight path and access for trolley
Plinth to boundary5 m10 mNeighbour relations
Plinth to access track2 m3 mSpace to manoeuvre and to stack boxes
Plinth to windbreak3 m5 mLift-off and landing space; less drift into hedge

Leave room to expand

When you lay out a permanent apiary, mark plinth positions for twice the number of hives you plan now. Adding a plinth later is easy if the space is there and hard if it is not. Keep the extra positions clear of trees and of the water line.

4. Termites, ants and moisture

ThreatHow it attacks a brick hiveDesign control
TermitesBuild mud tubes up the plinth and into the base or cavityPhysical barrier at plinth base (stainless mesh or graded stone), plus chemical treatment; inspect quarterly
AntsClimb the plinth and enter at the base join or through cracksKeep plinth face smooth and free of vegetation; a 50 mm overhang lip at the top of the plinth makes climbing harder; oil or sticky barrier around the base join
Rising dampMoves from soil into the masonry and then into the brood cavityDamp-proof course; base slab; ventilated base if screened
Rain splashWets the plinth and the base joinEntrance above the splash zone; ground falls away
CondensationForms on the inside of the base slab in cool weatherSlope to a screened drain hole; ventilate the base

5. Hive numbering and permanent records

A brick hive is fixed. Its number must be fixed too — cast into the plinth, painted on the wall, or on a stainless tag bolted to the plinth face.

6. Access and working space

  • Track wide enough for a small vehicle or trolley: 2.5 m minimum.
  • Firm, all-weather surface — gravel, compacted earth or concrete strip.
  • Turning space at the end of the apiary.
  • A small covered area for tools, smoker fuel and spare boxes, out of the sun and rain.
  • Space to stack supers during inspection — a 1 m clear area beside each plinth, or a central stacking point.

7. Water supply

A permanent apiary should have a permanent water source within 100 m — a shallow trough with landing stones, shaded, and refilled regularly. Bees will find water, but if the only source is a neighbour's pool or livestock trough, you will hear about it.

8. Biosecurity for a permanent site

  • Because hives cannot be moved easily, a disease outbreak on a permanent site is harder to control. Strong biosecurity is not optional.
  • Dedicated tools per apiary where possible. If not, clean thoroughly between sites.
  • Never bring frames, boxes or bees from an unknown source onto the site.
  • Inspect for termites and for damp at the same time as you inspect the bees — quarterly, minimum.
  • Keep a record of every hive movement or queen introduction, even on a fixed site.

Assignment 9

  1. Draw your plinth cross-section: footing depth, plinth height, damp-proof course, termite barrier, base slab and slope.
  2. Calculate the total loaded weight on your plinth and the bearing pressure in kPa. State whether your soil can carry it.
  3. Choose your base type and specify the mesh aperture, slider arrangement, entrance height and reducer settings.
  4. Draw your apiary plan with all plinth positions marked — including the expansion positions.
  5. State the spacing you will use for plinth-to-plinth, row-to-row, and to the windbreak. Justify each against the table.
  6. List the termite and ant controls you will use and when you will inspect them.
  7. Design your hive numbering scheme and write the first entry for a new hive on the site.
