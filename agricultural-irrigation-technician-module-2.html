1. Water Sources and Intake Assessment for Irrigation Systems

Every irrigation job in Africa begins with a hard look at the water source, because the source determines the pump, the pipe, the cost, and whether the farm survives a dry spell. As a technician, you are not just installing hardware; you are protecting a farmer's investment and a family's food supply. This lesson teaches you how to assess surface water, groundwater, and stored water in the field, using practical methods that work where laboratory tests and reliable grid power are not always available. Start with the source type. Surface sources include rivers, streams, dams, ponds, and canals. They are cheap to access but vary hugely between wet and dry seasons. A river that delivers 40 litres per second in March may drop to 5 litres per second in September. Your job is to measure the worst-case flow, not the best-case flow. Use the float method for a rough estimate: choose a straight stretch of stream about 10 metres long, measure the width and average depth, drop a stick or orange peel and time it over the 10 metres, then multiply cross-sectional area by velocity and a correction factor of about 0.8 for rough beds. For small streams, a bucket and stopwatch is more accurate: divert the flow into a container for a known time and calculate litres per second. Record everything in a field notebook with date, time, and recent rainfall. Groundwater sources include boreholes, wells, and springs. Boreholes are expensive but reliable if the aquifer is good. You need the borehole log if available, the static water level, the pumping water level, and the safe yield. If there is no log, ask the driller or the previous owner. A simple test is to pump at a known rate for one hour and measure drawdown. If the water level drops more than 2 to 3 metres and does not recover within 30 minutes, the borehole may not support continuous irrigation. Springs are excellent because they flow without pumping, but you must measure dry-season flow and protect the eye from animals and erosion. Stored water includes tanks, reservoirs, and lined ponds. The key questions are volume, refill rate, and evaporation. In hot areas such as the Sahel or northern Kenya, open water can lose 5 to 8 millimetres per day to evaporation. A 100 cubic metre pond can lose half a metre per month, which is 50 cubic metres, enough to irrigate a small plot for a week. Recommend covers, shade, or plastic liners where possible. Water quality matters as much as quantity. You do not need a full lab test for every job, but you should check salinity, sediment, and biological risk. Use a cheap electrical conductivity meter. Readings above 2 deciSiemens per metre are risky for many vegetables; above 4 deciSiemens per metre, only salt-tolerant crops such as date palm, barley, or cotton may survive. Sediment from rivers and canals destroys pumps and blocks drip lines. A simple settling pond or a sand filter can save thousands in repairs. Biological risk includes algae, bacteria, and snails that carry disease. If the water is used for drip irrigation on vegetables, filtration and chlorination may be needed, but never overdose chlorine because it harms plants and soil life. Now think business. A farmer does not buy water; they buy yield and time. Your assessment should produce three numbers: available flow in litres per second or cubic metres per day, total dynamic head in metres, and water quality risk level. From these, you can size a pump and estimate running cost. For example, a farmer with a 5 litre per second stream, 20 metres of head, and 200 metres of pipe can use a pump curve to find a pump that delivers 5 litres per second at 25 metres total head. If the pump is 60 percent efficient and driven by a diesel engine using 0.3 litres of fuel per kilowatt-hour, you can calculate fuel cost per hour and compare it with the value of the crop. Always ask the farmer about their water rights, community rules, and dry-season conflicts. In many African countries, water is managed by catchment committees or local authorities. Installing a pump without permission can lead to fines, confiscation, or conflict. Your reputation depends on doing the paperwork and respecting local agreements. Finally, document everything with photos, GPS coordinates, and simple sketches. A hand-drawn map of the source, pump position, tank, and field layout is more useful than a perfect digital drawing that never gets updated. Train the farmer or a family member to record daily water levels and pump hours. This data turns a one-time installation into a managed system. When you leave, the farmer should know how to check the source, clean the intake, and call you before the pump runs dry. That is the difference between a technician and a business partner.

2. Pump Selection, Solar Options, and Power Management

Pumps are the heart of an irrigation system, and in Africa the wrong pump choice is the most common reason a project fails. This lesson gives you a practical method for selecting pumps, matching them to water sources, and choosing between diesel, petrol, electric, and solar power. You will learn to read pump curves, calculate total dynamic head, and design a power system that a farmer can afford to run and maintain. Start with total dynamic head, or TDH. TDH is the total height the pump must push water, plus friction losses in pipes and fittings. Measure static lift from the water surface to the highest point of discharge. Add drawdown, which is how far the water level drops when pumping. Add pressure required at the outlet, for example 10 metres for drip irrigation or 20 metres for sprinklers. Then add friction loss. For a 32 millimetre polyethylene pipe, friction loss is roughly 0.1 to 0.2 metres per 10 metres of pipe at 1 litre per second, but it rises quickly with flow. Use a friction chart or a simple rule: keep pipe velocity below 1.5 metres per second to avoid high losses and water hammer. Once you have TDH and required flow, open the pump curve. A pump curve shows flow on the horizontal axis and head on the vertical axis. The duty point is where your system curve crosses the pump curve. Choose a pump whose best efficiency point is close to your duty point. If the duty point is far to the right, the pump will cavitate and wear out. If it is far to the left, you waste energy and may overheat the motor. For solar pumps, the curve changes with sunlight. A solar pump rated at 1000 watts may deliver 10 cubic metres per day in full sun but only 4 cubic metres per day under cloud. Always size solar pumps for the worst month, not the best. In many parts of Africa, the dry season is also the sunniest, which helps, but dust and heat reduce panel output by 10 to 20 percent. Use a safety factor of 1.3 on both flow and head. Now compare power options. Diesel and petrol pumps are cheap to buy and can run day or night, but fuel is expensive and often unreliable in remote areas. A 5 kilowatt diesel pump may use 1.5 litres per hour, which at 1.50 US dollars per litre costs 2.25 US dollars per hour. If it runs 6 hours per day, that is 13.50 US dollars per day, or over 400 US dollars per month. Solar pumps have high upfront cost, often 1500 to 5000 US dollars for a complete system, but near-zero running cost and low maintenance. The payback period compared with diesel is often 1 to 3 years. Electric pumps are best where grid power is stable and affordable, but many rural areas suffer outages and voltage fluctuations. Use a surge protector and a voltage stabiliser. For grid-connected systems, consider a hybrid inverter with battery backup for critical hours. For solar, design the array and pump together. Calculate daily water requirement in cubic metres. Multiply by TDH in metres by 2.725 to get watt-hours, then divide by pump efficiency and motor efficiency. For example, 20 cubic metres per day at 30 metres TDH needs about 20 x 30 x 2.725 = 1635 watt-hours. With 50 percent overall efficiency, you need about 3270 watt-hours. In a location with 5 peak sun hours, that is 654 watts of panels. Add 30 percent for losses, giving about 850 watts. Use a pump controller with dry-run protection, and set the low-level cutoff to protect the pump when the source runs dry. Mount panels facing the equator, tilted at latitude plus 10 degrees for year-round performance. Keep panels clean; dust can cut output by 20 percent in a week. Protect cables from animals and UV damage with conduit. Use proper earthing and lightning protection, because a single strike can destroy an entire solar array. Maintenance is where most systems fail. Diesel pumps need oil changes every 100 to 200 hours, air filter cleaning, and fuel filter replacement. Solar pumps need panel cleaning, controller checks, and occasional pump inspection. Train the farmer to keep a logbook of pump hours, water levels, and any unusual noises. A pump that runs dry for even a few minutes can burn out the motor or melt the impeller. Install a float switch or level probe. For surface pumps, use a foot valve and a strainer. For submersible pumps, ensure the pump is set at least 1 metre below the lowest expected water level and 1 metre above the bottom of the borehole to avoid silt. Finally, think about the business case. A farmer who spends 400 US dollars per month on diesel may be able to lease a solar pump or join a community water-sharing scheme. Some technicians offer a service contract that includes quarterly maintenance for a fixed fee. This creates recurring income and keeps systems running. Always provide a simple payback calculation in local currency, and show the farmer how many kilograms of maize, tomatoes, or milk the saved fuel money can buy. That is the language of business, and it wins trust.

3. Designing Drip, Sprinkler, and Surface Irrigation Layouts

The layout of an irrigation system determines how evenly water reaches crops, how much labour is needed, and how much money the farmer makes. This lesson covers the practical design of drip, sprinkler, and surface irrigation for small and medium farms in Africa. You will learn to calculate crop water needs, choose emitters and sprinklers, plan zones, and avoid the mistakes that cause waterlogging, erosion, and uneven growth. Begin with crop water requirement. Use the FAO method or a simple local estimate. In hot dry areas, a mature maize crop may need 5 to 7 millimetres per day, which is 50 to 70 cubic metres per hectare per day. Vegetables such as tomatoes may need 4 to 6 millimetres per day. Multiply by the area to get daily volume. For a 0.5 hectare tomato plot, 5 millimetres per day is 25 cubic metres per day. That is your design flow. If the source can only supply 10 cubic metres per day, you must either reduce the area, store water, or irrigate every other day with a larger application. Never design for peak demand without checking the source. For drip irrigation, the goal is to wet the root zone without wetting the leaves. Choose emitters based on soil type. Sandy soils need higher flow emitters, 2 to 4 litres per hour, spaced 20 to 30 centimetres apart, because water moves down quickly and sideways slowly. Clay soils need lower flow, 1 to 2 litres per hour, spaced 30 to 50 centimetres apart, because water spreads sideways. Loam soils are in between. Drip lines should run along the crop row, with emitters near the plant base. For row crops, one line per row is common. For orchards, use one or two emitters per tree, placed at the drip line, not against the trunk. Always install a filter. Sand filters are best for river or canal water; screen filters are enough for clean borehole water. Add a pressure regulator to keep the system at the recommended pressure, usually 1 to 2 bar for drip. Use a manifold and valves to divide the field into zones. A zone is the area irrigated at one time. Size zones so that the pump operates at its best efficiency point and the total flow does not exceed the source. For example, if the pump delivers 10 cubic metres per hour and each hectare of drip needs 5 cubic metres per hour, you can run two hectares at a time. Use a simple chart to record zone run times. For sprinkler irrigation, choose sprinklers based on crop, soil, and wind. Impact sprinklers are robust and common in Africa, but they need higher pressure, often 2 to 4 bar. Rotating sprinklers are more efficient and uniform, but they are more sensitive to dirty water. Spacing depends on nozzle size and pressure. A common layout is 12 by 12 metres or 12 by 18 metres. Always overlap sprinkler patterns by 50 percent to get uniform coverage. Avoid irrigating in strong wind, especially midday, because up to 30 percent of water can be lost to evaporation and drift. For vegetables, use low-angle sprinklers or micro-sprinklers to reduce leaf wetness and disease. For fodder and cereals, high-angle sprinklers are fine. Surface irrigation includes furrow, basin, and border methods. It is cheap but labour-intensive and can cause erosion and waterlogging. Use it only on level land with suitable soil. For furrow irrigation, make furrows 10 to 15 centimetres deep and 75 to 100 centimetres apart. Control the flow so water reaches the end of the furrow without overtopping. For basins, level the ground and build bunds 20 to 30 centimetres high. Basin irrigation is good for rice and trees. For borders, use long strips with a gentle slope of 0.1 to 0.5 percent. Always add a tail drain to carry excess water away. Now plan the layout on paper. Draw the field to scale. Mark the water source, pump, tank, mainline, sub-mainlines, and zones. Use different colours for different pipe sizes. Calculate pipe sizes to keep velocity below 1.5 metres per second. For a 10 cubic metre per hour flow, a 50 millimetre pipe gives about 1.4 metres per second, which is acceptable. A 40 millimetre pipe gives about 2.2 metres per second, which is too high. Use larger pipes for mainlines and smaller pipes for laterals. Include valves, air release valves at high points, and drain valves at low points. In Africa, theft and animal damage are real risks. Bury mainlines 30 to 50 centimetres deep where possible. Use lockable valve boxes. Fence the pump and tank area. For drip lines, consider subsurface drip in high-theft areas, but it is more expensive and harder to repair. Always leave a spare roll of drip tape and a few sprinklers with the farmer. Train the farmer to flush drip lines every two weeks. Open the end caps and let water run for a few minutes. This removes sediment and prevents clogging. Check emitters monthly by measuring flow from a sample of emitters. If flow drops by more than 20 percent, clean or replace them. For sprinklers, check nozzles for wear and replace them every season or two. Finally, calculate the cost per hectare. Drip systems in Africa often cost 1500 to 3000 US dollars per hectare for materials, plus installation. Sprinkler systems cost 800 to 2000 US dollars per hectare. Surface irrigation costs 200 to 500 US dollars per hectare but needs more labour. Show the farmer the yield difference. Drip on tomatoes can increase yield by 30 to 50 percent and save 40 to 60 percent of water compared with furrow. That is the business case. Your design should be simple, repairable, and expandable. Use standard pipe sizes and fittings that are available in local markets. Do not specify exotic parts that take months to import. The best system is the one that keeps working after you leave.

4. Installation, Testing, and Commissioning of Irrigation Systems

Installation is where a good design meets real-world soil, stones, and surprises. This lesson covers the step-by-step process of installing pumps, pipes, filters, and emitters, then testing and commissioning the system so the farmer can operate it with confidence. You will learn practical field skills, safety rules, and documentation that protects your work and your reputation. Before you start, walk the site with the farmer. Confirm the design, mark the pipeline route with stakes or lime, and check for buried cables, rocks, and roots. Measure and cut pipes carefully. For polyethylene pipe, use a pipe cutter or a fine-tooth saw, then chamfer the end with a knife to avoid damaging the O-ring. For PVC pipe, use a hacksaw and deburr the inside. Use solvent cement only on PVC and follow the instructions; too much cement weakens the joint, too little causes leaks. For compression fittings, tighten by hand plus a quarter turn with a wrench. Do not overtighten, because it cracks the plastic. Install the pump on a stable base, preferably concrete, and align it with the suction and discharge pipes. For surface pumps, keep the suction pipe as short and straight as possible. Use a foot valve with a strainer and ensure it is at least 30 centimetres above the bottom of the source to avoid silt. For submersible pumps, lower the pump slowly with a safety rope. Never let it drop. Tape the cable to the riser pipe every 2 to 3 metres. Use heat-shrink connectors for cable joints and keep them above water level. Install a non-return valve on the discharge line to prevent backflow. Install a pressure gauge before and after the filter. Install a sand filter or screen filter according to the water quality. Backwash sand filters regularly; a dirty filter causes pressure loss and poor irrigation. For drip systems, install the filter, pressure regulator, and manifold. Flush the mainlines before connecting the drip tape. This removes construction debris that would clog emitters. Connect drip tape to the manifold with rubber grommets or barbed fittings. Do not stretch the tape; leave a little slack for expansion. Close the ends with end caps or figure-eight knots. For sprinklers, install risers at the correct height so the sprinkler is above the crop. Use a spirit level to keep them vertical. Install a valve at each zone and label it clearly. Now test the system. Start with the pump. Check the direction of rotation for three-phase motors. Run the pump with the discharge valve partly closed to avoid overload, then open slowly. Watch the pressure gauge. If pressure is too high, open a valve or check for a blockage. If pressure is too low, check for air leaks, a clogged filter, or a worn impeller. Listen for cavitation, which sounds like gravel in the pump. Cavitation means the pump is starving or the suction lift is too high. Stop and fix it immediately. Check the water level in the source. If it drops faster than expected, reduce the flow or install a larger source. For drip, check the pressure at the end of the lateral. It should be within 10 percent of the design pressure. If not, adjust the pressure regulator or divide the zone. Measure the flow from at least 10 emitters per zone. Calculate the average and compare it with the design. If the average is more than 10 percent low, flush the lines and check the filter. If some emitters are dry, check for kinks, blockages, or air locks. For sprinklers, measure the throw and coverage. Place small cans or cups in a grid and run the system for 15 minutes. Measure the depth in each can. The difference between the highest and lowest should be less than 20 percent. If it is greater, adjust the spacing or pressure. Commissioning is more than a test. It is a training session. Walk the farmer through the system. Show them how to start and stop the pump, how to open and close valves, how to backwash the filter, and how to flush the drip lines. Explain the signs of trouble: low pressure, dry emitters, unusual noise, and water where it should not be. Give them a simple logbook with columns for date, pump hours, water level, filter cleaning, and observations. Leave a laminated one-page operating manual in the local language if possible. Include a contact number for emergencies. Safety is non-negotiable. Wear gloves, boots, and eye protection. Never work on electrical connections while the power is on. Use a qualified electrician for solar and grid connections. Keep children and animals away from open trenches and pump areas. Use proper lifting techniques for pumps and pipes. Have a basic first aid kit on site. After installation, clean the site. Remove packaging, cut pipe, and tools. Leave the field tidy. Take photos of the completed installation, including the pump, filter, valves, and emitters. Record GPS coordinates and serial numbers. Give the farmer a signed handover document listing what was installed, the warranty terms, and the maintenance schedule. Finally, schedule a follow-up visit in two to four weeks. Check the system under real operating conditions. Look for leaks, wear, and operator errors. Adjust as needed. A follow-up visit is your best marketing tool. A farmer who sees you return without being called will recommend you to neighbours. That is how you build a business in agricultural irrigation. Do not rush the commissioning. A system that is tested and explained properly will last for years. A system that is rushed will fail in weeks, and the farmer will blame you, not the design.

5. Maintenance, Troubleshooting, and Business Growth for Irrigation Technicians

A successful irrigation technician does not just install systems; they keep them running and grow a service business. This lesson covers preventive maintenance, troubleshooting common faults, spare parts management, and the business skills needed to earn a steady income in Africa. You will learn how to build a maintenance schedule, diagnose problems quickly, and turn one-time installations into long-term relationships. Start with preventive maintenance. Create a schedule for each client based on the system type and season. For diesel and petrol pumps, change engine oil every 100 to 200 hours, clean the air filter every 50 hours, and replace the fuel filter every 200 hours. Check the cooling system and belts weekly. For solar pumps, clean panels weekly during the dry season, check the controller for error codes monthly, and inspect the pump and cables every three months. For drip systems, flush laterals every two weeks, clean filters weekly, and check emitters monthly. For sprinklers, inspect nozzles monthly and replace worn ones every season. For surface irrigation, check bunds and furrows after every irrigation and repair erosion immediately. Teach the farmer to do daily checks: look at the pressure gauge, listen to the pump, and walk the field to spot dry patches or leaks. A daily five-minute check prevents most major failures. Now troubleshooting. When a system fails, work from the source to the emitter. First, check the water source. Is there enough water? Is the intake blocked? Is the foot valve stuck? Second, check the pump. Is it running? Is it making noise? Is the voltage correct? For solar, check the panels for shade or dust. Third, check the filter. A clogged filter is the most common cause of low pressure. Backwash or clean it. Fourth, check the pipes and valves. Look for leaks, closed valves, and air locks. Fifth, check the emitters or sprinklers. If only one zone is affected, the problem is in that zone. If the whole system is affected, the problem is at the source, pump, or filter. Learn to use a pressure gauge and a flow meter. These two tools solve most problems. For example, if the pressure is high and flow is low, there is a blockage. If pressure is low and flow is high, there is a leak or a worn pump. If pressure and flow are both low, the source or pump is the problem. Keep a small toolkit with pipe cutters, wrenches, Teflon tape, spare fittings, a multimeter, and a pressure gauge. Carry common spare parts: emitters, drip connectors, end caps, filters, and a spare pump seal. In remote areas, waiting for parts can take weeks, so stock what you need. Now business growth. Treat every installation as the start of a service relationship. Offer a maintenance contract. For a small fee, visit monthly or quarterly to check the system, clean filters, and train the farmer. This gives you predictable income and reduces emergency call-outs. Use a simple invoice book and record every job. Track your costs: fuel, parts, labour, and transport. Know your break-even point. If a call-out costs you 20 US dollars in transport and takes 4 hours, charge at least 50 US dollars. Do not compete on price alone. Compete on reliability and knowledge. Farmers talk to each other. A technician who answers the phone and shows up on time will get more work than one who is cheap but unreliable. Diversify your services. Offer pump testing, water quality testing, system design, and training. Sell spare parts and filters. Partner with local agro-dealers and solar companies. Attend farmer field days and demonstrate a small solar pump or drip kit. Use a smartphone to take photos and videos of your work. Show before-and-after results. A farmer who sees a 40 percent water saving will pay for a better system. Manage your money. Save a percentage of every job for tools and emergencies. Keep records of warranties and serial numbers. Pay taxes and licence fees if required. Build a team. Train one or two assistants to do installations and routine maintenance while you focus on design and sales. Pay them fairly and train them well. Your reputation is your biggest asset. Never oversell a system. If a farmer cannot afford drip, recommend a cheaper sprinkler or surface system that works. If the water source is too weak, say so. Honesty builds trust. Finally, think about the future. Climate change is making rainfall less predictable across Africa. Irrigation is becoming essential, not optional. Farmers who invest in irrigation will survive and prosper. As a technician, you are part of that transformation. Keep learning. Read pump manuals, attend training, and share knowledge with other technicians. Join a local or national irrigation association if one exists. The demand for skilled irrigation technicians is growing. By combining practical skills, honest business practices, and a commitment to maintenance, you can build a career that supports your family and helps farmers feed their communities. Start today by reviewing your last installation. Did you leave a logbook? Did you schedule a follow-up? Did you explain the maintenance schedule? If not, go back and do it. That is how you turn a job into a business.

  2. Calculate total dynamic head (TDH). TDH = static lift + friction loss + discharge pressure + fittings allowance (typically 10â15%). Quote this number in writing.
  3. Test water quality. EC, pH, iron and (if wastewater or surface water) faecal coliforms. Use the limits in Table 2 to decide drip vs sprinkler vs surface.
  4. Size the solar array with a 1.3â1.5 oversizing factor for cloudy days and pump derating. Never design for perfect sun.
  5. Install filtration in the right order: sand/media filter first, then screen or disc filter (120 mesh for drip), then the mainline. Flush filters weekly.
  6. Pressure-test before backfilling. Run the system at 1.5Ã working pressure for at least 1 hour and check every joint, valve and emitter.
  7. Commission with the farmer present. Show them the flush valves, the filter cleaning routine, and the daily pressure gauge reading. Hand over a one-page maintenance sheet.
  8. Price your work as a service, not a one-off. Offer a quarterly maintenance contract (filter change, emitter check, pump inspection, solar panel cleaning) â recurring revenue is what makes an irrigation business survive the dry season.

Business Note for African Technicians

Irrigation is one of the few agricultural services with year-round demand in Africa, but the money is not in the install â it is in the maintenance contract and the data you collect. A technician who keeps a simple logbook of every system's flow rate, pressure, EC and filter change interval can sell that data back to the farmer as an irrigation scheduling service, and to input suppliers as field validation. In Kenya, the Ahero and Mwea irrigation schemes, and in Nigeria the Kano River and Bakolori schemes, have demonstrated that well-maintained systems raise yields by 30â60% over rain-fed â but only when maintenance is systematic.
