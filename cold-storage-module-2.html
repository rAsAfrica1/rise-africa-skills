1. Thermodynamics Basics for Cold Storage

Thermodynamics is the study of heat and how it moves. In cold storage, we don't create coldâwe remove heat. Heat always flows from a hotter place to a colder place. Your job is to move heat from inside the cold room to outside. The unit of heat is the joule (J) or kilojoule (kJ). One kilojoule is about the heat from a small candle burning for a minute. In refrigeration, we often use kilocalories (kcal): 1 kcal = 4.184 kJ. A typical cold room for vegetables might need to remove 10,000 kJ per hour (about 2,400 kcal/h).

Temperature is not the same as heat. A cup of water at 80Â°C has less heat than a bathtub at 40Â°C because the bathtub has more mass. Specific heat capacity tells you how much heat a substance holds. Water has a high specific heat: 4.18 kJ/kgÂ°C. That means to cool 1 kg of water by 1Â°C, you must remove 4.18 kJ. For tomatoes, specific heat is about 3.9 kJ/kgÂ°C. So to cool 100 kg of tomatoes from 30Â°C to 4Â°C, you remove 100 Ã 3.9 Ã 26 = 10,140 kJ. That's the 'sensible heat'âheat that changes temperature.

There's also 'latent heat'âheat that changes state without changing temperature. When water freezes, it releases 334 kJ per kg. When it melts, it absorbs the same. This is why ice is so good for cooling: 1 kg of ice melting absorbs 334 kJ, equivalent to cooling 1 kg of water by 80Â°C. In cold storage, condensation and freezing of water in the air add extra load. For example, if 2 kg of water vapour condenses on the evaporator per hour, that's 2 Ã 2,260 kJ = 4,520 kJ/h of latent heat (latent heat of vaporisation is about 2,260 kJ/kg).

Finally, the first law of thermodynamics says energy is conserved. The heat removed from the cold room plus the electrical energy used by the compressor equals the heat rejected to the outside. So a cold room removing 10,000 kJ/h with a compressor using 3,000 kJ/h will reject 13,000 kJ/h to the condenser. Understanding these basics helps you size equipment and troubleshoot. Always measure temperatures and weights with real tools: a digital thermometer (like a Testo 108) and a kitchen scale. In Africa, where ambient temperatures can exceed 40Â°C, these calculations are critical for efficient cold storage.

2. Heat Transfer Mechanisms

Heat moves in three ways: conduction, convection, and radiation. In cold storage, all three matter. Conduction is heat moving through solid materials. For example, heat travels through the walls of your cold room. The rate depends on the material's thermal conductivity (k). Insulation like polyurethane foam has k = 0.02 W/mÂ°C, while brick has k = 0.6 W/mÂ°C. So a 100 mm foam panel conducts 30 times less heat than 100 mm of brick. In Africa, many cold rooms use foam panels with k = 0.022 W/mÂ°C. That's small, but over 24 hours it's 3,800 kJâenough to melt 11 kg of ice.

Convection is heat moving with fluids (air or liquid). Inside the cold room, fans circulate air to transfer heat from produce to the evaporator. Natural convection (no fan) is slow; forced convection with fans is faster. A typical evaporator fan moves 1,000 mÂ³/h. The heat transfer coefficient for forced air is about 20 W/mÂ²Â°C, while still air is only 5 W/mÂ²Â°C. So fans quadruple heat transfer. Outside, the condenser uses convection to reject heat to the air. In hot African climates, you need larger condensers or higher fan speeds. Keep condenser coils cleanâdust reduces convection.

Radiation is heat transfer by infrared waves, no medium needed. The sun radiates about 1,000 W/mÂ² at noon. A cold room roof exposed to sun absorbs heat. Painting the roof white reflects radiation; a shiny aluminium surface reflects even more. Also, cold surfaces inside the room can radiate heat to warmer surfaces. But radiation is usually minor in well-insulated rooms. However, if you load warm produce, it radiates heat to the evaporator. For example, a pallet of tomatoes at 30Â°C radiates about 50 W/mÂ² to a 0Â°C evaporatorâsmall but adds up.

To reduce heat transfer, insulate walls, seal doors, use curtains, and keep doors closed. In many African markets, cold rooms lose heat through open doors and poor seals. A simple test: on a hot day, feel for cold air escaping around door edges. Fix with rubber gaskets. Also, use thermal breaksâwood or plastic between metal parts that conduct heat. Remember, every kilojoule you keep out saves money on electricity. With diesel generators common in rural areas, efficiency is crucial.

3. Refrigerants and Their Properties

A refrigerant is a fluid that absorbs heat at low temperature and pressure and releases it at high temperature and pressure. Common refrigerants in African cold storage include R134a, R404A, R407C, R22 (being phased out), and ammonia (R717). Each has properties that affect performance. The most important are boiling point, latent heat, pressure, and safety. R134a boils at -26Â°C at atmospheric pressure. It's used in medium-temperature cold rooms (0 to 10Â°C). R404A boils at -46Â°C, good for freezers (-20Â°C). Ammonia boils at -33Â°C but has high latent heat (1,370 kJ/kg) and is cheap, but toxicâneeds expert handling.

Latent heat of vaporisation is the heat absorbed when 1 kg of liquid refrigerant turns to gas. Higher latent heat means less refrigerant needed. Ammonia has 1,370 kJ/kg, R134a has 217 kJ/kg, R404A has 160 kJ/kg. So ammonia circulates about 6 times less mass for the same cooling. But ammonia is toxic and flammable in high concentrations. In Africa, many small cold rooms use R134a because it's safe and available. However, R134a has a high global warming potential (GWP 1430). New alternatives like R290 (propane, GWP 3) are emerging but flammable. Always check local regulations.

Pressure matters. At -10Â°C, R134a evaporates at 2.0 bar (absolute), while R404A at -10Â°C evaporates at 4.7 bar. Higher pressure means thicker pipes and stronger compressors. The condensing pressure at 40Â°C ambient: R134a is 10.2 bar, R404A is 18.2 bar. In hot African climates, condensing pressures can exceed 20 bar for R404A, stressing the system. That's why many technicians set condensing temperature at 45Â°C or use larger condensers. Always use a manifold gauge set (like a Yellow Jacket) to check pressures.

Refrigerant oils are also critical. Mineral oil works with R22 and ammonia; synthetic oils (POE) are needed for R134a and R404A. Mixing oils can cause compressor failure. When charging, use a digital scale to weigh refrigerantânever guess. A typical 5 kW cold room might need 2-3 kg of R134a. Leaks are common; use a leak detector (electronic or soap bubbles). In Africa, refrigerant smuggling and counterfeit gas are problemsâbuy from reputable suppliers. Always recover refrigerant, don't vent it. Proper handling protects your health and the environment.

4. The Vapor Compression Cycle

The vapor compression cycle is the heart of most cold storage systems. It has four main components: compressor, condenser, expansion valve, and evaporator. The cycle moves heat from the cold room to the outside. Start at the evaporator: low-pressure liquid refrigerant absorbs heat from the cold room and boils into a low-pressure gas. For example, R134a enters the evaporator at -10Â°C and 2 bar, leaves as gas at -5Â°C and 2 bar. The heat absorbed is the latent heat plus any superheat. A typical evaporator might remove 5 kW (5 kJ/s) from the room.

Next, the compressor sucks this low-pressure gas and compresses it to high pressure. This raises the temperatureâhot gas leaves the compressor at 70-80Â°C and 10-12 bar for R134a. The compressor uses electricity; a 5 kW cooling capacity might need a 1.5 kW compressor. The compression process adds heat, so the gas now contains the heat from the room plus the compressor work. In Africa, where power can be unreliable, a good compressor with overload protection is essential.

Then the hot high-pressure gas enters the condenser. Here, it rejects heat to the outside air (or water) and condenses into a high-pressure liquid. The condenser fan blows ambient air (say 35Â°C) across the coils. The refrigerant cools from 80Â°C to 40Â°C and condenses at 40Â°C and 10 bar. The heat rejected equals the heat absorbed in the evaporator plus compressor work: 5 kW + 1.5 kW = 6.5 kW. So the condenser must reject 6.5 kW. If the condenser is dirty or undersized, pressure rises, and cooling capacity drops.

Finally, the high-pressure liquid goes through an expansion valve (or capillary tube). This restricts flow, dropping pressure from 10 bar to 2 bar. Some liquid flashes into gas, cooling the mixture to -10Â°C. The mixture enters the evaporator, and the cycle repeats. The expansion valve also controls superheatâthe amount of gas leaving the evaporator above its boiling point. Too little superheat can flood the compressor; too much reduces efficiency. Set superheat to 5-8Â°C. Use a gauge manifold and thermometer to measure. In the field, listen for the compressor, check frost pattern on the evaporator, and feel the suction lineâit should be cold and sweating. This cycle is the same for a small fridge or a large cold room. Understanding it helps you diagnose problems: if the evaporator ices up, check the expansion valve; if the condenser is hot, check airflow.

5. Pressure-Enthalpy Diagrams

A pressure-enthalpy (P-H) diagram is a map of refrigerant properties. It shows pressure on the vertical axis and enthalpy (heat content per kg) on the horizontal axis. You use it to calculate cooling capacity, compressor work, and efficiency. Every refrigerant has its own diagram. For R134a, you can find free diagrams online or in apps like 'Refrigerant Slider'. Let's walk through a simple example. Suppose your cold room uses R134a, evaporating at -10Â°C (2.0 bar) and condensing at 40Â°C (10.2 bar). Locate the evaporating pressure line on the diagram. The point where it meets the saturated vapour line is the compressor suction. The enthalpy there (h1) is about 395 kJ/kg. The point where the condensing pressure line meets the saturated liquid line is after the condenser (h3), about 250 kJ/kg. The difference h1 - h3 = 145 kJ/kg is the refrigeration effectâheat absorbed per kg of refrigerant in the evaporator.

Now, the compressor adds work. The compression line goes from h1 to h2 (discharge). For an ideal cycle, h2 is about 435 kJ/kg. So compressor work = h2 - h1 = 40 kJ/kg. The coefficient of performance (COP) is refrigeration effect divided by work: 145 / 40 = 3.6. That means for every 1 kJ of electricity, you remove 3.6 kJ of heat. Real systems have lower COP (2.5-3.5) due to losses. If you know the mass flow rate, you can calculate capacity. For a 5 kW system, mass flow = 5 kW / 145 kJ/kg = 0.0345 kg/s. Multiply by 3600 to get 124 kg/h.

P-H diagrams also show superheat and subcooling. Superheat is the temperature above boiling at the evaporator outlet. On the diagram, it's the horizontal distance from the saturated vapour line to your suction point. Subcooling is cooling the liquid below condensing temperature before the expansion valve; it increases refrigeration effect. For example, 5Â°C subcooling adds about 5 kJ/kg to the effect. In hot African climates, subcooling is hard to achieve because the condenser is already hot. But you can add a subcooler or use a larger condenser.

To use the diagram, you need pressure and temperature readings. A manifold gauge gives pressures; a clamp thermometer gives temperatures. Plot the points, read enthalpies, and calculate. This helps you diagnose: if the actual COP is much lower than the diagram, you might have a restriction, wrong refrigerant, or compressor wear. Practice with different refrigerants. Apps like 'Danfoss Ref Tools' have built-in P-H diagrams. In the field, you won't always have a diagram, but understanding the principles helps you make quick judgments. For example, if suction pressure is low and superheat is high, you're low on refrigerant or have a clogged expansion valve.

Practical Reference: Refrigeration Principles for Cold Storage in Africa

This practical reference consolidates the core engineering knowledge a cold storage operator, technician, or agribusiness manager needs to run a reliable facility in African conditions. It is written business-first: every principle here connects to product loss, energy cost, equipment life, or compliance. Use it as a bench reference when sizing systems, diagnosing faults, or negotiating with equipment suppliers.

1. Thermodynamics Basics for Cold Storage

Refrigeration does not "create cold." It moves heat from a cold space to a warmer environment using work input. The First Law (energy conservation) means every kilowatt-hour of electricity you buy is ultimately rejected as heat at the condenser. The Second Law means heat only flows from hot to cold naturally, so removing heat from a cold room always costs energy. In practice, the lower the target temperature and the hotter the ambient, the more energy per kilogram of product you must spend.

For a typical cold room, the Coefficient of Performance (COP) is the ratio of heat removed to electrical energy consumed. A well-maintained system at 0Â°C with 32Â°C ambient might achieve COP 2.5â3.5. If the condenser is fouled or the ambient rises to 40Â°C, COP can drop below 2.0 â meaning your electricity bill per tonne of chilled produce can rise by 40% or more. This is the single most important business metric in cold storage.

2. Heat Transfer Mechanisms

Heat enters your cold room through three routes:

  • Conduction â through walls, roof, and floor. Insulation thickness and panel condition dominate this load.
  • Convection â warm air moving across surfaces, including infiltration through doors and gaps.
  • Radiation â solar gain on roofs and walls, significant in African climates with high solar irradiance.

Infiltration is often the largest uncontrolled load. A door left open for 30 seconds can introduce more heat than an hour of wall conduction. Air curtains, strip curtains, and disciplined door management are low-cost, high-return interventions.

4. The Vapor Compression Cycle

The vapor compression cycle is the workhorse of cold storage. Four stages:

  1. Compression â the compressor raises refrigerant pressure and temperature.
  2. Condensation â heat is rejected to ambient air or water; refrigerant becomes liquid.
  3. Expansion â the expansion valve drops pressure and temperature.
  4. Evaporation â refrigerant absorbs heat from the cold space and returns to gas.

Each stage has losses. Superheat and subcooling settings, compressor efficiency, and condenser condition determine real-world performance. A 1Â°C reduction in condensing temperature can cut compressor power by 2â4% â a direct saving on your electricity bill.

5. Pressure-Enthalpy Diagrams

The P-h diagram is the technician's map. It plots refrigerant pressure against enthalpy and shows the four cycle stages as a loop. Key readings:

  • Evaporating pressure â set by the cold room temperature and refrigerant.
  • Condensing pressure â set by ambient temperature and condenser condition.
  • Superheat â temperature above saturation at evaporator outlet; typically 5â8 K.
  • Subcooling â temperature below saturation at condenser outlet; typically 5â10 K.

If your P-h readings drift from design, you have a fault: undercharge, overcharge, fouled condenser, or a failing compressor. Regular P-h logging is the cheapest predictive maintenance you can do.

  1. Transmission load â through walls, roof, floor.

  3. Internal load â lights, motors, people, defrost.
