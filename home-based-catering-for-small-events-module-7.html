1. Initial Client Consultation

The first meeting with a client sets the tone for the entire job. Whether they come to your home or you visit them, be prepared. Have a notebook, pen, and a simple quotation template ready. Start by asking open questions: What is the occasion? How many guests? What date and time? Where will it be held? What is your budget? Listen more than you talk. Write down everything. If they mention allergies, note them immediately. If they say 'we want something nice,' ask them to describe what 'nice' means to them. Show them photos of past events on your phone or a small album. This helps them visualize and builds trust.

After the meeting, send a summary via WhatsApp or email within 24 hours. This confirms you heard them correctly and gives them a chance to correct any mistakes. For example: 'Thank you for meeting today. You need catering for 30 guests on Saturday, 15th June, at your home in Karen. Menu: pilau, kachumbari, grilled chicken, and chapati. Budget: KES 25,000. Please confirm or correct.' This simple step prevents big problems later.

Always ask about dietary restrictions and food preferences. In many African contexts, this includes religious halal or kosher needs, vegetarianism, and allergies like peanuts or shellfish. Also ask about spice levelssome clients love hot food, others cannot eat it. Note any cultural traditions, such as serving certain dishes at weddings or funerals. If the client is from a different region, ask about their local favourites. This shows respect and helps you plan.

Finally, discuss payment terms. A common practice is a 50% deposit to secure the date, with the balance due on the event day. Be clear about what the deposit covers: ingredients, transport, and your time. If the client hesitates, explain that the deposit protects both of you. Do not start shopping or cooking without a deposit. This protects your small business from losses. End the meeting by thanking them and confirming the next step, such as sending a formal proposal within two days.

2. Creating Proposals and Contracts

A proposal is your offer; a contract is your agreement. Both protect you and your client. After the initial consultation, prepare a written proposal. You can type it on your phone using Google Docs or Microsoft Word, then save as a PDF and send via WhatsApp or email. If you don't have a smartphone, write it neatly by hand and take a photo to send. The proposal should include: your business name and contact, client name and event date, number of guests, full menu with descriptions, price per person or total price, deposit amount, payment deadline, and any extra services like serving staff, cutlery, or setup.

Be specific about what is included and what is not. For example: 'Includes: 30 plates of pilau, 30 pieces of grilled chicken, 30 chapatis, kachumbari, and disposable plates and cutlery. Does not include: drinks, dessert, or serving staff.' This prevents the client from assuming you will provide extra items for free. Also state your cancellation policy: if the client cancels within 7 days of the event, the deposit is non-refundable. If you cancel, you will return the deposit and offer a discount on a future event.

Once the client agrees to the proposal, turn it into a simple contract. The contract can be one page. It should repeat the key details and include a signature line for both parties. You can sign digitally using apps like Adobe Fill & Sign, or print, sign, and scan. If you cannot print, you can type 'I agree' in a WhatsApp message and keep that as proof. In many African countries, a written agreement, even informal, is enforceable if there is a clear offer, acceptance, and consideration (the deposit).

Always keep a copy of the signed contract for your records. If the client wants changeslike adding 10 more guestscreate a revised proposal and contract. Never rely on verbal promises. A written contract reduces disputes and helps you look professional. It also makes it easier to get paid on time because the client knows exactly what they agreed to.

3. Handling Dietary Restrictions

Dietary restrictions are not optionalthey are health and religious requirements. Ignoring them can make guests sick, offend beliefs, and ruin your reputation. Always ask during the consultation: 'Does anyone have allergies, religious dietary needs, or foods they cannot eat?' Write down every detail. Common restrictions in Africa include: no pork (for Muslim and some Christian clients), no shellfish, no peanuts, vegetarian, vegan, gluten-free, and low-sugar for diabetics. Also consider lactose intoleranceavoid heavy cream or milk in dishes if needed.

For allergies, treat them as life-threatening. If a client says a guest is allergic to peanuts, you must ensure no peanut oil, peanut flour, or cross-contamination. Use separate pots and utensils. Wash hands and surfaces thoroughly. If you cannot guarantee a peanut-free environment, tell the client honestly. It is better to decline than to risk anaphylaxis. For religious needs, such as halal, you may need to buy meat from a certified halal butcher. In many cities like Nairobi, Mombasa, Accra, or Lagos, halal butchers are common. Ask the client for recommendations if unsure.

When planning the menu, offer alternatives. For vegetarians, replace meat with beans, lentils, or mushrooms. For gluten-free, use rice, maize meal, or cassava instead of wheat. For low-sugar, reduce sugar in desserts and offer fruit. Always label dishes at the event if there are multiple options. For example, put a small sign: 'Vegetarian pilau' and 'Chicken pilau.' This helps guests serve themselves safely. Also keep a list of ingredients for each dish in case someone asks.

Finally, confirm dietary needs again 3 days before the event. People's situations change, or new guests may be added. Send a WhatsApp message: 'Just confirming: 2 vegetarian meals, 1 gluten-free, no peanuts. Correct?' This final check prevents last-minute panic. If you make a mistake, apologize immediately and offer a replacement dish or refund for that portion. Handling restrictions well builds trust and can lead to repeat business from health-conscious or religious clients.

4. Managing Expectations

Unmet expectations are the number one reason clients complain. Managing expectations means being clear about what you can and cannot do, and then delivering exactly that. Start by being honest about your capacity. If you can only cook for 50 people, do not accept a job for 100. If you cannot provide waiters, say so. If your transport cannot reach a remote area, explain. Clients appreciate honesty more than broken promises.

Set clear timelines. Tell the client when you will arrive, when food will be ready, and when you will leave. For a lunch event at 1 PM, you might arrive at 8 AM to set up, start cooking by 9 AM, and have food ready by 12:30 PM. Share this schedule in writing. If you are running late due to traffic or a supplier delay, inform the client immediately via call or WhatsApp. A simple message like 'Traffic on Mombasa Road, I will arrive 30 minutes late' shows respect.

Be clear about portion sizes. In many African cultures, generous portions are expected. If you serve small portions, guests may feel cheated. Agree on a standard: for example, 250g of rice per person, 200g of meat, and 2 chapatis. Use a kitchen scale for the first few events to train your eye. If the client wants more, they must pay more. Do not overpromise on quantity to win the job.

Also manage expectations about quality. Show photos of your actual food, not stock images. If you use disposable plates, say so. If you cannot do elaborate decorations, say so. Under-promise and over-deliver: if you tell them the food will be good and it is excellent, they will be happy. If you promise five-star plating and deliver home-style, they will be disappointed. Finally, discuss what happens if something goes wronglike a power outage or a missing ingredient. Have a backup plan, such as a gas cooker or a substitute dish. Communicate the plan to the client so they feel secure.

5. Confirming Event Details

Three days before the event, send a confirmation message to the client. This is not a repeat of the contract; it is a final checklist to catch any changes. Use WhatsApp or a phone call. Say: 'Hello [Client], just confirming your event on Saturday, 15th June. 30 guests, lunch at 1 PM, at your home in Karen. Menu: pilau, grilled chicken, chapati, kachumbari. 2 vegetarian meals, no peanuts. Deposit received: KES 12,500. Balance due on event day: KES 12,500. Please confirm all is correct.' This gives the client a chance to say 'Actually, we now have 35 guests' or 'The event is at 2 PM, not 1 PM.'

After the client confirms, send a final summary message: 'Confirmed. I will arrive at 8 AM with all ingredients and equipment. I will need access to your kitchen and a power point. I will bring my own gas cooker and serving dishes. I will leave after cleaning up. Please have the balance ready in cash or mobile money.' This sets clear logistics.

On the day before the event, do a final shopping check. Buy all non-perishable items early. For perishables like meat and vegetables, buy fresh on the morning of the event or the evening before. Confirm with any helpers (if you have them) what time they will arrive. Charge your phone and have a power bank. Print or write out the menu and guest count. Pack your equipment: pots, pans, knives, serving spoons, plates, cups, napkins, and cleaning supplies.

On the event day, arrive on time. Before you start cooking, do a quick walk-through with the client: 'Here is where I will set up. Here is the menu. Here is the timeline.' If anything is different from what was agreed, address it immediately. For example, if the kitchen is smaller than expected, ask if you can use an outdoor area. If the client added 5 guests without telling you, you may need to adjust portions or ask for extra payment. Stay calm and solution-focused. After the event, ask the client to sign a simple completion note: 'Event completed on [date]. Food served as agreed. Balance paid.' This protects you if there is a later dispute.

6. Post-Event Follow-Up

The event is over, but your job is not done. A good follow-up turns a one-time client into a repeat customer and a source of referrals. Within 24 hours, send a thank-you message. For example: 'Thank you for choosing [Your Business] for your event yesterday. I hope everyone enjoyed the food. It was a pleasure to serve you. If you have any feedback, please let me know.' This shows professionalism and opens the door for honest comments.

If the client is happy, ask for a testimonial. You can say: 'If you were satisfied, would you mind writing a short review I can share with future clients? A sentence or two is fine.' You can also ask for a photo of the event if they took any. Use these testimonials on your WhatsApp status, Facebook page, or a simple flyer. In Africa, word-of-mouth is powerful. A happy client will tell friends, family, and colleagues.

If the client had complaints, listen without arguing. Apologize for any shortcomings and ask how you can make it right. Maybe you can offer a discount on their next event or a free dessert. Do not get defensive. A complaint handled well can turn a dissatisfied client into a loyal one. For example, if the food was late, you might say: 'I am sorry the food was 20 minutes late. I will offer you a 10% discount on your next order.' This shows you care about their experience.

Also, follow up on payment if there is any balance outstanding. Send a polite reminder: 'Just checking if you were able to send the remaining KES 5,000. You can send to M-Pesa number [your number]. Thank you.' If they have already paid, confirm receipt: 'Thank you, I have received the full payment. I appreciate your business.'

Finally, keep a simple client record. Write down their name, contact, event date, menu, and any preferences. In a notebook or a spreadsheet on your phone. Then, three months later, send a friendly message: 'Hello [Client], I hope you are well. I am now taking bookings for the holiday season. If you have any events coming up, I would love to serve you again.' This keeps your business in their mind. Repeat clients cost less to acquire and often spend more. A good follow-up system is the backbone of a sustainable home catering business.

<h2>Practical Reference: Client Management and Communication for Home-Based Caterers</h2>

<p>In home-based catering across Africa, the difference between a one-off job and a repeat client rarely comes down to the food alone. It comes down to how professionally you manage the relationship from the first phone call to the follow-up message three days after the event. This practical reference module gives you the templates, benchmarks and checklists to run client communication like a business, whether you are catering a 20-guest baby shower in Kumasi, a 60-person corporate lunch in Nairobi, or a 100-plate wedding reception in Lagos.</p>

<p>Small-event catering is relationship-driven. Most of your bookings will come from referrals, WhatsApp forwards and repeat clients. That means every interaction is marketing. The systems below are designed to be low-cost, phone-friendly and usable without expensive software a notebook, a smartphone and a consistent process are enough to start.</p>

If you cannot guarantee separation, decline the booking rather than risk a medical emergency a reaction can be fatal and will end your business.</p>

<h3>4. Managing Expectations: What to Put in Writing</h3>

<p>Expectation gaps are the number one cause of client disputes in small-event catering. The fix is not better food it is clearer writing. Every proposal should state, in plain language:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Portion size:</strong> "One plate = 250g rice + 120g protein + 2 sides." Avoid vague words like "generous."</li>
<li><strong>Service style:</strong> buffet, plated, family-style or drop-off. Each has a different labour cost.</li>
<li><strong>Staffing:</strong> how many servers, for how many hours, and whether transport is included.</li>
<li><strong>Setup and breakdown:</strong> who provides chafing dishes, plates, cutlery, and who clears after.</li>
<li><strong>Timing:</strong> arrival time, serving time window, and what happens if the event runs late.</li>
<li><strong>Payment terms:</strong> deposit amount, balance due date (typically 48 hours before the event), accepted payment methods.</li>
<li><strong>Cancellation policy:</strong> deposit refundable up to 7 days before; non-refundable within 72 hours.</li>
<li><strong>What is NOT included:</strong> drinks, cake, decorations, extra guests beyond the confirmed headcount.</li>
</ul>

<h3>5. Confirming Event Details: The 72-Hour Rule</h3>

<p>Three days before the event, send a written confirmation message (WhatsApp is fine, but save a copy). It should repeat the date, time, venue, headcount, menu, dietary notes, balance due and your arrival time. Ask the client to reply "CONFIRMED" in writing. This single message prevents most day-of confusion and gives you a written record if the client later disputes what was agreed.</p>

<h3>6. Post-Event Follow-Up: Turning One Job Into Three</h3>

<p>Within 48 hours of the event, send a short thank-you message. Ask two specific questions: "What did your guests enjoy most?" and "Was there anything we could improve?" Then, if the feedback is positive, ask for a referral: "If you know anyone planning an event in the next three months, I would be grateful for an introduction." Keep a simple client log name, event type, date, menu, feedback so you can send a personalised message on the anniversary or on major holidays. Repeat clients cost nothing to acquire and typically spend 2030% more than first-time clients.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul>
<li> Consultation notes captured: date, venue, guest count, budget, dietary needs.</li>
<li> Written proposal sent within 48 hours of consultation.</li>
<li> Deposit received and receipt issued before any shopping begins.</li>
<li> Dietary restrictions confirmed in writing and cross-contamination plan documented.</li>
<li> 72-hour confirmation message sent and acknowledged with "CONFIRMED".</li>
<li> Balance payment collected before or on event day.</li>
<li> Post-event thank-you and feedback request sent within 48 hours.</li>
<li> Client logged for future follow-up and referral request.</li>
</ul>
