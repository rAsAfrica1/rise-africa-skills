Lessons

1. Estimating and Costing

Estimating and costing is how you figure out what to charge for a steel fabrication job so you make a profit and don't go broke. Start by measuring the job. For a simple gate, measure width, height, and count bars. If you use 20mm square tubing, each meter weighs about 1.2 kg. Steel costs around $1.20 per kg in many African cities. So a 5-meter gate frame using 12 meters of tubing needs 14.4 kg, costing about $17. Add hinges, paint, and consumables like welding rods and grinding discs. A 5kg box of rods costs $8 and you might use half. Paint: 1 liter covers 5 square meters and costs $6. A gate has about 3 square meters of surface, so $4 of paint. Now labor: if you pay a helper $10 per day and the job takes 2 days, that's $20. Add your own time at $15 per day for 2 days = $30. Overhead: electricity for welder, rent, wear on tools. Estimate 10% of material and labor. Total material: $17 + $4 + $4 = $25. Labor: $50. Overhead: $7.50. Total cost: $82.50. Now add profit. A 30% markup gives $107.25. Round to $110. Always add 10% contingency for mistakes or price changes. Keep a notebook of all costs per job. After the job, compare actual to estimate. If you spent more on grinding discs, adjust next time. In Africa, steel prices change often due to imports. Call your supplier for current prices before quoting. Never quote from memory. Use a simple calculator and write everything down. For bigger jobs like staircases, break into sections: stringers, treads, handrails. Estimate each separately. Remember transport: hiring a truck to deliver the gate might cost $20. Include that. If you forget, you lose profit. Also, charge for design time if you draw plans. A good estimate wins the job and keeps you in business.

2. Production Planning and Scheduling

Production planning means deciding what work to do, in what order, and when, so you meet deadlines without chaos. Start with a job list. Write each project, its deadline, and how many hours it needs. For example, you have three jobs: a window frame (4 hours), a door (8 hours), and a trailer repair (12 hours). You have two workers and one welder. You work 8 hours a day, 5 days a week. Total hours needed: 24. With two workers, you have 16 hours per day. So you can finish in 2 days if no delays. But you must schedule tasks that need the welder first, because only one person can weld at a time. Make a simple chart: Monday morning: cut all steel for all jobs. Monday afternoon: weld window frame. Tuesday morning: weld door. Tuesday afternoon: start trailer. Wednesday: finish trailer and paint everything. This is a Gantt chart in your notebook. Always plan for 20% extra time for delays: power cuts, material shortages, sick workers. In Africa, power cuts are common. If you have a generator, schedule welding during likely power cuts. If not, do cutting and grinding when power is out. Keep a whiteboard in the shop with each job and its status: not started, in progress, done. Update it daily. Order materials at least 3 days before you need them. Steel suppliers may take time. For a big job like a roof truss, break into stages: cutting, welding, drilling, painting, delivery. Assign each stage a day. Tell the customer a realistic completion date, not an optimistic one. If you promise Friday and deliver Monday, you lose trust. Use a calendar and mark deadlines in red. Check progress every morning for 5 minutes with your team. Ask: what did you finish yesterday? What will you finish today? What do you need? This keeps everyone focused. If a job runs late, decide whether to work overtime or move another job. Never sacrifice safety for speed. A planned shop is a profitable shop.

3. Inventory and Material Management

Inventory and material management is about knowing what steel, tools, and consumables you have, where they are, and when to reorder. Without this, you waste money buying duplicates or lose jobs because you ran out of welding rods. Start by organizing your shop into zones: steel rack, tool cabinet, consumables shelf, and scrap bin. Label everything. Use a notebook or a simple spreadsheet on your phone. For each item, record: description, size, quantity, minimum stock level, and supplier. For example: 20mm square tubing, 6-meter lengths, 10 pieces, minimum 4 pieces. When you take a piece, write it down. When you buy, add it. Do a physical count every Friday afternoon. This takes 30 minutes but saves hours. Set reorder points. If you use 2 pieces of tubing per week, and delivery takes 1 week, keep at least 4 pieces as minimum. When stock hits 4, order 10 more. For consumables like grinding discs, welding rods, and paint, keep a minimum of one week's usage. A 5kg box of 3.2mm rods lasts about 20 hours of welding. If you weld 4 hours a day, that's 5 days. So keep 2 boxes. Store steel off the ground on wooden pallets to prevent rust. Cover with a tarp in rainy season. Keep paint and thinners in a locked metal cabinet away from welding sparks. Tools: keep grinders, drills, and clamps in a cabinet. Assign one person to be tool keeper. They sign tools out and in. If a tool breaks, tag it and tell the owner. Never leave tools on the floor. Scrap: keep all offcuts longer than 30cm in a bin. You will use them for small jobs like brackets. Sell truly unusable scrap to a recycler. In Africa, theft can be a problem. Lock your shop at night. Keep a record of who has keys. A well-managed shop never stops work because of missing materials.

4. Shop Safety and OSHA Regulations

Shop safety is not optional. In steel fabrication, you face burns, cuts, eye injuries, and electric shock. OSHA (Occupational Safety and Health Administration) sets rules that many countries adopt. Even if your country doesn't enforce them, follow them to stay alive and productive. First, personal protective equipment (PPE). Always wear safety glasses when grinding, cutting, or chipping. A grinding disc can shatter and blind you. Wear a welding helmet with at least shade 10 for arc welding. Wear leather gloves when handling steel. Wear steel-toe boots. Wear ear plugs when grinding for long periods. Wear a respirator when painting or welding galvanized steel (which releases toxic zinc fumes). Second, fire safety. Keep a fire extinguisher (dry chemical, ABC type) within 10 meters of welding. Never weld near paint, thinners, or gas cylinders. Keep a bucket of sand or water for small fires. Third, electrical safety. Inspect welder cables for cracks. Never weld in wet conditions. Use a ground fault circuit interrupter (GFCI) if possible. Turn off welder when changing electrodes. Fourth, machine safety. Use guards on grinders. Never remove the guard. Clamp workpieces securely. Keep hands away from the blade. Fifth, housekeeping. Keep floors dry and free of scrap. Trip hazards cause falls. Store gas cylinders upright and chained. Sixth, ventilation. Weld in a well-ventilated area or use a fume extractor. Manganese fumes cause Parkinson's-like symptoms. Seventh, training. Every worker must know where the first aid kit is and how to use it. Have a first aid kit with burn gel, bandages, and eye wash. Eighth, emergency plan. Know the nearest clinic and have a phone number. In Africa, many shops lack formal OSHA enforcement, but you can self-regulate. Post safety rules on the wall. Hold a 5-minute safety talk every Monday. Lead by example: wear your PPE. If you see unsafe act, stop it. A safe shop has fewer accidents, less downtime, and better morale.

5. Team Leadership and Communication

Team leadership and communication is how you get your workers to do quality work on time without fighting. In a small African shop, you might have 2 to 10 workers. You are the boss, but you also work alongside them. Respect is key. Start each day with a 10-minute meeting. Stand in a circle. Ask each person what they will do today. Listen. Then assign tasks clearly: 'John, you cut all the 40mm tubing for the gate. Mary, you weld the frame. I will grind and paint.' Give deadlines: 'Finish cutting by 11am.' Check progress at lunch. If someone is slow, ask why. Maybe the blade is dull. Fix the problem, don't just shout. Communicate in the language everyone understands. If you have workers from different tribes, use the common language (English, French, Swahili, etc.). Avoid sarcasm. Give feedback privately if it's negative. Praise publicly. When a worker does good work, say 'Thank you, that weld is clean.' A simple thank you costs nothing and builds loyalty. Pay wages on time. If you delay pay, workers steal or leave. If you can't pay, tell them early and agree on a plan. Train your team. Show a new worker how to hold a grinder safely. Let them practice on scrap. Don't just throw them into a job. Cross-train: teach everyone to weld, cut, and paint. Then if one person is sick, work continues. Handle conflicts quickly. If two workers argue, separate them and talk to each one alone. Find a solution. Never take sides without facts. Keep a notebook of worker skills and attendance. Reward good attendance with a small bonus or lunch. In Africa, many workers support extended families. A small bonus means a lot. Lead by example: be on time, wear PPE, clean up. If you work hard, they will too. A good team makes your shop profitable and your life easier.

6. Capstone Fabrication Project

The capstone project brings together everything you learned in this module. You will plan, estimate, schedule, manage materials, work safely, and lead a team to build a real steel product. Choose a project that is useful and sellable. A good choice is a double gate for a compound, a staircase for a two-story house, or a set of window frames for a small shop. For this example, we build a 3-meter wide double gate. Step 1: Estimate. Measure opening: 3m wide, 1.8m high. Use 40mm square tubing for frame, 20mm for bars. Calculate steel: frame needs 2 verticals (1.8m each) + 3 horizontals (1.5m each) = 3.6 + 4.5 = 8.1m of 40mm. Bars: 10 vertical bars per leaf, 1.5m each, 2 leaves = 30m of 20mm. Weight: 40mm is 2.4kg/m, so 19.4kg. 20mm is 1.2kg/m, so 36kg. Total 55.4kg. At $1.20/kg = $66.50. Hinges, latch, paint: $20. Labor: 2 workers x 3 days x $10 = $60. Overhead 10% = $14.65. Total cost = $161.15. Add 30% profit = $209.50. Quote $210. Step 2: Plan. Day 1: cut all steel. Day 2: weld frames. Day 3: weld bars, grind, paint. Day 4: install. Step 3: Inventory. Check you have 20kg of 40mm, 40kg of 20mm, 2 boxes of rods, 5 grinding discs, 1 liter paint, 2 hinges, 1 latch. Order missing items. Step 4: Safety. Wear PPE. Set up fire extinguisher. Ensure ventilation. Step 5: Team. Assign tasks: Worker A cuts, Worker B welds, you grind and paint. Hold morning meetings. Step 6: Build. Follow your drawing. Measure twice, cut once. Weld clean. Grind smooth. Paint two coats. Step 7: Install. Dig post holes, set posts in concrete, hang gates. Check swing. Step 8: Review. Compare actual cost to estimate. Note what went wrong. This project proves you can run a fabrication job from start to finish.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Project Management and Shop Operations for Steel Fabrication

This practical reference consolidates the core operating knowledge a steel fabrication shop needs to win work, deliver on time, and stay profitable. It is written for the African context where power reliability, material lead times, and skilled-labour availability often differ sharply from European or North American assumptions and it is written business-first: every recommendation here should be traceable to cost, schedule, quality, or safety outcomes that a shop owner or project manager can defend to a client or a bank.

Steel fabrication in Africa spans a wide spectrum: small jobbing shops welding gates and trusses, mid-sized workshops serving mining and construction, and larger firms executing structural steel packages for bridges, plants, and warehouses. Across all sizes, the same six disciplines determine whether a shop is profitable: estimating and costing, production planning and scheduling, inventory and material management, safety compliance, team leadership, and disciplined project execution. This module gives you a working reference for each.

1. Estimating and Costing

Estimating is where profit is made or lost. A steel fabrication estimate must capture material, labour, consumables, machine time, subcontracting, overhead, contingency, and margin not just "steel plus welding." In African markets, three cost drivers are frequently underestimated: (a) transport and clearing of imported sections (HS code 7216 profiles often attract duty and port delays), (b) generator or inverter power for welding where grid supply is unstable, and (c) rework caused by dimensional errors that only surface at erection.

Use a bottom-up estimate for anything above a small jobbing threshold. Price welding by deposited-metal volume and electrode consumption, not by "per joint" guesswork. Track actual versus estimated hours on every job this is the single most valuable management habit a shop can build.

2. Production Planning and Scheduling

Planning converts an estimate into a sequence of shop activities. The critical path in most fabrication jobs runs through: material receipt marking and cutting fit-up welding NDT/inspection surface treatment delivery. Bottlenecks usually appear at welding bays and at the paint/blast stage, not at cutting.

For African shops, schedule with explicit buffers for: power interruptions, delayed imported steel, and public holidays that reduce effective working days. A realistic schedule that is 10% longer than the client's wish is worth more than an optimistic one that fails at week three. Use a simple bar chart (Gantt) for jobs under 50 tonnes and a network diagram for larger or multi-phase work.

3. Inventory and Material Management

Steel is money sitting on the floor. Manage it accordingly. Every section, plate, and consumable should be received against a delivery note, checked for mill certificates (EN 10204 3.1 or equivalent), and stored off the ground on dunnage to prevent corrosion. In humid coastal or tropical locations, indoor or covered storage is not optional it protects both the steel and your margin.

Maintain a simple bin-card or spreadsheet system with minimum stock levels for high-turnover consumables (electrodes, cutting discs, grinding wheels, primer). Offcuts above 500 mm should be tagged and returned to stock in many African shops, offcut wastage alone accounts for 36% of material cost. Reconcile physical stock to records monthly.

4. Shop Safety and OSHA Regulations

Safety is a legal, moral, and commercial issue. In many African jurisdictions, occupational safety and health law is modelled on or directly references OSHA-style frameworks for example, Kenya's Occupational Safety and Health Act (OSHA, 2007), Nigeria's Factories Act, and South Africa's Occupational Health and Safety Act (Act 85 of 1993) with its General Safety Regulations. Compliance is increasingly a precondition for tendering on mining, oil and gas, and public infrastructure work.

SAFETY WARNING WELDING FUME AND CONFINED SPACE HAZARDS

Welding fumes contain manganese, hexavalent chromium, and other metal oxides that cause irreversible neurological and respiratory damage. In confined spaces (tanks, columns, hoppers), fume accumulation can be lethal within minutes. Never weld in a confined space without mechanical forced ventilation, atmospheric testing, a written permit, a trained standby person, and a rescue plan. Ensure local exhaust ventilation at the arc, and issue fit-tested respiratory protection (minimum P2/P95 particulate filter; supplied air for stainless or galvanised steel). This applies equally to small jobbing shops and large yards the hazard does not scale with company size.

Minimum shop controls: fire extinguishers within 10 m of every hot-work station; flashback arrestors on all oxy-fuel sets; welding screens to protect adjacent workers; eye protection at all times in the shop; and a documented incident register. Hold a five-minute toolbox talk every morning it is the cheapest safety intervention available and the most consistently effective.

5. Team Leadership and Communication

Fabrication is a team trade. A boilermaker, a welder, a rigger, and an inspector must share the same drawing revision and the same tolerance expectations. In multilingual African shops, brief in the working language of the crew and confirm understanding by asking them to repeat the instruction back not by asking "do you understand?"

Practical leadership behaviours that pay: assign a named lead per work package; hold a 15-minute daily stand-up at the bay; display the current drawing revision and weld map at the workstation; and give feedback on quality within the shift, not at month-end. Recognise good work publicly retention of skilled welders is a chronic constraint across the continent, and turnover destroys both margin and schedule.

6. Capstone Fabrication Project

The capstone integrates all five disciplines. A typical capstone is a small structural package for example, a 6 m 4 m mezzanine platform, a roof truss set, or a skid-mounted equipment frame delivered against a client-style specification. You will produce: a priced estimate with assumptions, a Gantt schedule with buffers, a material take-off and procurement plan, a job safety analysis, a weld map and inspection plan, and a handover dossier with as-built drawings and mill certificates.

Assessment is business-first: did the job make a defensible margin, was it delivered on the promised date, did it pass inspection, and was anyone injured? A technically beautiful fabrication delivered late and over budget is a failed project.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Estimate bottom-up price material, labour, consumables, power, transport, overhead, contingency, and margin separately; never quote a single lump figure without a build-up.
  • Track actual vs estimated hours on every job; review at completion and feed the variance back into the next estimate.
  • Buffer the schedule for power interruptions, import delays, and public holidays typically 1015% on the critical path.
  • Verify mill certificates on receipt; store steel off the ground and under cover in humid climates.
  • Tag and return offcuts above 500 mm to stock; reconcile physical inventory monthly.
  • Enforce confined-space and fume controls without exception permit, ventilation, atmospheric test, standby person, rescue plan.
  • Hold a daily 15-minute stand-up at the bay; confirm instructions by having crew repeat them back.
  • Close every job with a handover dossier as-built drawings, weld maps, NDT reports, mill certificates, and a lessons-learned note.
