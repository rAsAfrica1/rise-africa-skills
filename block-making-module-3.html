Aerated Concrete Block (AAC)

8 lessons · 30 quiz questions · Hard difficulty

1 What makes concrete "aerated"

AAC (Autoclaved Aerated Concrete) is concrete with millions of tiny air bubbles inside it. The result: a block 4× lighter than normal concrete, but still strong enough for walls.

How it works:

The mix contains a small amount of aluminium powder (about 0.1% of the mix). In the wet, alkaline concrete, the aluminium reacts with lime to produce hydrogen gas. The gas bubbles up through the mix, expanding it like bread dough. The concrete rises to 2–3× its original volume.

After the mix rises, it's left to set. The bubbles stay in place, creating the aerated structure. Then it's cut into blocks and autoclaved (steam-cured under high pressure) to lock in the strength.

⚠ The autoclave is essential. Without it, AAC is weak and won't meet standards. This is why AAC is factory-scale only.

2 Aluminium powder reaction basics

The chemistry is simple but sensitive.

The reaction: 2 Al + 6 H₂O → 2 Al(OH)₃ + 3 H₂↑ (hydrogen gas).

Three things control the reaction:

  • Temperature — the mix must be 35–40°C when aluminium is added. Cooler = slow reaction, poor aeration.
  • Aluminium particle size — fine powder (under 100 microns). Coarse aluminium gives big bubbles and weak blocks.
  • Alkali content — the cement-lime mix must be alkaline enough. Standard AAC mixes use 15–25% lime.

Timing: aluminium is added last, and the mix must be poured into moulds within 2–3 minutes. After that, it starts to rise. Rising takes 20–40 minutes. Setting takes another 1–2 hours.

3 Autoclave (steam chamber) design

The autoclave is a large pressure vessel — essentially a giant pressure cooker. It's the single biggest cost of an AAC factory.

Key specifications:

  • Diameter: 2–3 m
  • Length: 20–40 m
  • Pressure: 10–12 bar (about 10× atmospheric)
  • Temperature: 180–200°C
  • Cycle time: 8–12 hours per batch

Steam-cured blocks gain 5–10× the strength of air-cured blocks. The high pressure turns the calcium-silicate reaction into a fast, complete process.

Autoclaves cost $80,000–$300,000 depending on size. They require certified pressure-vessel operators, safety systems, and annual inspections. This is not a small-yard piece of equipment.

⚠ Never attempt to build an autoclave yourself. Pressure vessel failure is lethal. Buy from certified manufacturers only.

4 Mix proportions for AAC

Standard AAC mix (by weight):

  • Sand (fine, 0–1 mm): 55–65%
  • Cement (OPC): 15–20%
  • Lime (quicklime or hydrated): 15–20%
  • Gypsum: 3–5% (regulates setting)
  • Aluminium powder: 0.05–0.10%
  • Water: to reach a pourable slurry (water:solids ratio 0.6–0.8)

The mix is a thin slurry, not a stiff concrete. It's poured into large steel moulds (typically 4×1.2×0.6 m), rises, sets, and is then cut.

Fine sand (0–1 mm) is essential. Coarse sand kills the aeration. Many African AAC plants use fly ash instead of sand — a cheaper alternative where available.

5 Casting large AAC cake

The "cake" is the large block of AAC that comes out of the mould before cutting.

  1. Heat the mould to 35–40°C (steam jacket around the sides)
  2. Mix the slurry in the order: sand + water first, then cement + lime + gypsum, then aluminium last
  3. Pour into the mould within 2–3 minutes
  4. Move the mould to the rising bay (warm, still air, 30–40°C)
  5. The mix rises for 20–40 minutes to 2–3× volume
  6. Leave to set for 1–3 hours until firm enough to cut
  7. Remove the side plates of the mould

Temperature control during rising is critical. Cool air or drafts stop the rise. Hot spots make uneven texture.

A typical mould produces 3–4 m³ of AAC cake, which becomes 40–50 blocks.

6 Wire-cutting the cake

The soft cake is cut into precise blocks by wire cutters.

Steps:

  1. The cake is moved onto a cutting trolley
  2. Vertical wires cut the cake into strips
  3. Horizontal wires (or a second vertical pass) cut strips into blocks
  4. A profile-cutting wire can create tongue-and-groove or other shapes
  5. The cut blocks go onto the autoclave trolley

Wire diameters are typically 0.5–1 mm. They must be tensioned tight and replaced regularly — worn wires give wavy cuts.

The cut waste from the top and sides (called "returns") is recycled back into the slurry — usually 5–15% of production.

💡 Wire accuracy determines block tolerance. AAC is used for thin-bed mortar (1–3 mm) — blocks must be precise.

7 Steam-curing schedule

Autoclave curing is a controlled heating and cooling cycle. Rushing it cracks the blocks.

Typical 12-hour cycle:

  • 0–2 hours: Purge air, raise steam slowly to 100°C
  • 2–4 hours: Raise pressure to 10–12 bar (180–200°C)
  • 4–10 hours: Hold at pressure (the curing phase)
  • 10–12 hours: Release pressure slowly, cool down

Total cycle: 10–14 hours. Some modern plants cycle faster with higher pressure.

After autoclaving, blocks are ready. They're stored 24 hours, then shipped or warehoused.

After curing, AAC blocks have a moisture content of 20–30%. They dry naturally over 2–4 weeks to reach the target density.

8 Why AAC needs a factory

AAC cannot be made in a small yard. The barriers are:

  • Autoclave: $80K–$300K, plus infrastructure, safety systems, certified operators
  • Moulds: $30K–$100K for a full set (large, heated, precision)
  • Cutting line: $50K–$200K
  • Building: 500–2,000 m², high roof, steam lines
  • Power: 500 kW+ for steam generation

Total investment: $250,000–$1,000,000 for the smallest AAC plant.

Smallest economic scale: 50,000 m³ per year (about 5 million blocks). That requires major construction demand to sell.

Recommended path: Start with dense and hollow concrete blocks (Modules 1–2). Build the business. Once you have steady customers and cash, consider AAC — but only with a partner or investor.

💰 AAC is a factory business, not a yard business. Skip it until you're running a profitable concrete block yard already.
