Lessons

1. Setting up a simple cash book and inventory log

To run a profitable scrapyard, you must know where every shilling goes. Start with a simple cash booka notebook with two columns: money in and money out. Every time you sell a part or scrap metal, write the date, description, and amount in the 'money in' column. Every time you buy fuel, pay a worker, or buy tools, write it in 'money out'. At the end of each day, total both columns and calculate your balance. Use a separate page for each month. For inventory, keep a log of all parts and scrap you have. List each item: e.g., 'Toyota Corolla alternator 2 units', 'copper wire 5 kg'. Update it when you sell or acquire items. This helps you know what's in stock and what sells fast. Use a ruler to draw linesneatness matters. If you have a smartphone, you can use a free app like 'Cash Book' or 'Money Manager', but a paper book works fine. Keep your cash book and inventory log in a safe, dry place. At the end of each week, review your records to see if you're making a profit. If money out is more than money in, you're losing moneyadjust your prices or cut costs. This simple habit will transform your business.

2. Tracking expenses: fuel, labor, tools, and transport

Every expense must be recorded, no matter how small. Fuel is a major costfor your generator, cutting torches, or truck. Keep a small notebook in your pocket and write down every litre you buy. For example, if you buy 5 litres of petrol for 1,000 KES, note it. Labor: pay your workers daily or weekly and record the amount and their name. If you pay a casual worker 500 KES to help dismantle a car, write it down. Tools: when you buy a new hammer, chisel, or grinder, record the cost. Also note when tools break and need replacement. Transport: if you hire a pickup to deliver scrap to the buyer, record the fare. At the end of the month, add up all these expenses. Compare with your income. If expenses are more than 70% of income, you need to reduce costs. For example, negotiate better fuel prices, or do maintenance on tools to make them last longer. Use a simple table in your cash book: Date, Item, Amount. This helps you see where money leaks. You might discover that transport is eating your profitthen consider buying a used wheelbarrow to move scrap short distances. Tracking expenses gives you power to make smart decisions.

3. Calculating profit margins on parts and scrap metal

Profit margin is the difference between what you sell an item for and what it cost you to get it ready for sale. For a part, your cost includes the price you paid for the scrap car, plus labor to remove the part, plus any cleaning or testing. For example, you buy a scrap car for 20,000 KES. You pay a worker 2,000 KES to remove the alternator. You sell the alternator for 8,000 KES. Your cost for that alternator is a share of the car price plus labor. If the car has 10 main parts, each part's share of the car price is 2,000 KES. Add labor 200 KES (if worker spent 1 hour on it at 200 KES/hour). Total cost = 2,200 KES. Profit = 8,000 - 2,200 = 5,800 KES. Profit margin = (5,800 / 8,000) x 100 = 72.5%. For scrap metal, say you buy 100 kg of mixed metal for 5,000 KES. You sort it: 20 kg copper (sell at 800 KES/kg = 16,000 KES), 30 kg aluminium (sell at 300 KES/kg = 9,000 KES), 50 kg steel (sell at 50 KES/kg = 2,500 KES). Total income = 27,500 KES. Profit = 27,500 - 5,000 = 22,500 KES. Margin = 81.8%. Always calculate margins per item. If a part has low margin, consider raising its price or not buying that type of car again. Keep a record of margins to know your best sellers.

4. Managing employee roles and responsibilities in a small yard

Even with two or three workers, you need clear roles. Assign one person as the 'dismantler'they remove parts from cars using tools. Another as 'sorter'they separate metals (copper, aluminium, steel) and clean parts. A third as 'salesperson'they deal with customers and record sales. If you only have one helper, they can do all tasks but you must tell them what to do each morning. Write down each person's duties on a board or paper. For example: 'John: dismantle engines, remove batteries. Mary: sort copper and brass, keep inventory log. You: buy scrap, pay wages, keep cash book.' Pay fairly and on time. If you promise 500 KES per day, pay it. This builds trust. Also, train them on safety: wear gloves and goggles when cutting. Hold a short meeting every Monday to review the week's goals. For example, 'This week we need to dismantle 3 cars and sell 50 kg of copper.' Give feedback: if someone does well, say so. If they make mistakes, correct gently. Never let workers handle cash without recording it. If a worker sells a part, they must write it in the cash book immediately. This prevents theft and confusion. A well-managed team works faster and makes more profit.

5. Scheduling daily operations: sourcing, dismantling, and sales

A daily schedule keeps your yard productive. Start early: 7 AM to 8 AM, source scrap. Visit local garages, car repair shops, or individuals selling old cars. Negotiate prices. For example, offer 15,000 KES for a non-running Toyota. By 8 AM, return to the yard. From 8 AM to 12 PM, dismantle. Focus on high-value parts first: alternators, starters, batteries, copper wiring. Use a checklist: remove battery, drain fluids, take out engine parts. From 12 PM to 1 PM, lunch break. From 1 PM to 3 PM, sort and clean parts. Separate metals into bins: copper, aluminium, brass, steel. Clean parts with a wire brush. From 3 PM to 5 PM, sales. Call regular buyers or wait for customers. Record every sale in your cash book. From 5 PM to 6 PM, tidy up and plan next day. Write a to-do list: 'Buy 2 cars tomorrow, pay John, order new grinder blade.' If you have workers, assign tasks each morning. For example, 'John, dismantle the blue car. Mary, sort the copper pile.' Stick to the schedule. If a car is not fully dismantled by noon, continue after lunch. But don't let one task eat all your time. Use a clock or phone alarm to stay on track. A consistent schedule means you never miss a sourcing opportunity or a sale.

6. Using a mobile phone for basic bookkeeping and communication

Your mobile phone is a powerful business tool. Use it for bookkeeping: download a free app like 'Cash Book' or 'Expense Manager'. Enter every transaction as it happens. For example, when you sell a part for 5,000 KES, open the app, select 'Income', type 'Alternator sale', and save. For expenses, do the same. At the end of the day, the app shows your balance. If you don't have a smartphone, use SMS to send yourself notes: 'Sold alternator 5000' or 'Bought fuel 1000'. Then transfer to your paper cash book. For communication, use WhatsApp to connect with buyers and suppliers. Create a broadcast list of regular buyers. Send a message: 'New stock: Toyota Corolla parts, call 0712...'. Use the camera to take photos of parts and send to buyers. For sourcing, join local Facebook groups or WhatsApp groups where people sell scrap cars. Post: 'Looking for scrap cars, call 0712...'. Also use the phone to set reminders: 'Pay John salary on Friday' or 'Call copper buyer at 3 PM'. Keep your phone charged and have a backup power bank. If you have a feature phone, use the calendar and alarm. A phone helps you stay organized, reach more customers, and keep accurate records without a computer.

<h2>Practical Reference: Business Operations and Record Keeping for Car Breakers & Metal Scrapyards</h2>

<p>A car breaking and metal scrapyard business in Africa lives or dies on two things: knowing what comes in, and knowing what goes out. Whether you are operating in Nairobi's Industrial Area, Kumasi's Suame Magazine, or a roadside yard in Gweru, the difference between a profitable yard and a struggling one is rarely the quality of the scrap it is the quality of the records. This practical reference gives you the tools to run a tight operation using nothing more than a cash book, a mobile phone, and disciplined daily habits.</p>

<h3>1. Setting Up a Simple Cash Book and Inventory Log</h3>

<p>Your cash book is the single most important document in the business. It does not need to be complicated. A hardcover exercise book with ruled columns works perfectly, though a mobile spreadsheet is even better because it calculates totals automatically and can be backed up.</p>

<p>Divide each page into two halves: <strong>Money In</strong> (sales of parts, scrap metal sold to a mill, deposits from customers) and <strong>Money Out</strong> (fuel, labour, tools, transport, rent, licenses, airtime). Record every transaction on the day it happens not at the end of the week. A record kept from memory is a guess, not a record.</p>

<p>Your <strong>inventory log</strong> tracks what you actually have on the yard. Each vehicle that arrives should be logged with: date received, registration number, make and model, purchase price, and source (dealer, individual, auction). Then track what is removed from it engine, gearbox, alternator, catalytic converter, battery, tyres, body panels and the date each item was either sold or scrapped. This is how you catch theft, identify which vehicle models yield the best margins, and prove stock value to a lender or buyer.</p>

<h3>2. Tracking Expenses: Fuel, Labour, Tools, and Transport</h3>

<p>Most yard owners undercount expenses because they only track the big purchases. The small daily costs a litre of petrol for the generator, a spanner that broke, a boda-boda ride to collect a spare part are what quietly eat your margin. Every expense goes into the Money Out column with a date, description, and amount.</p>

<h3>3. Calculating Profit Margins on Parts and Scrap Metal</h3>

<p>You must know your margin on every item, not just the total for the month. The formula is simple:</p>
<p style="text-align:center;font-weight:bold;background:#e9ecef;padding:10px;border-radius:6px;">Profit Margin = (Selling Price Total Cost) Selling Price 100</p>

<p><strong>Total Cost</strong> includes the vehicle purchase price, the labour to strip it, fuel used, transport to collect it, and a share of your overheads (rent, tools, airtime). If you bought a Toyota Corolla for $800, spent $60 on labour and $40 on transport, your total cost is $900. If you sell the parts and scrap for $1,350, your profit is $450 and your margin is 33%. That is healthy. If you sell for $1,000, your margin is 10% thin, and one unexpected expense wipes it out.</p>

<p>Scrap metal margins work differently. You buy by weight and sell by weight, so your main variables are the buying price per kilogram, the selling price per kilogram, and the weight loss during sorting. Always weigh on a calibrated scale eyeballing weight is how you lose money on every transaction.</p>

<h3>4. Managing Employee Roles and Responsibilities</h3>

<p>Even a yard with three workers needs clear roles. Ambiguity breeds theft, laziness, and conflict. Define who does what, and write it on a board where everyone can see it. Typical roles in a small yard:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Yard Supervisor:</strong> Opens and closes the yard, assigns daily tasks, checks inventory log, approves parts removal.</li>
<li><strong>Dismantler / Mechanic:</strong> Strips vehicles, tags parts, sorts scrap by metal type, reports tools needed.</li>
<li><strong>Driver / Collector:</strong> Collects vehicles and delivers parts, records fuel and transport costs, maintains vehicle log.</li>
<li><strong>Sales / Front Desk:</strong> Handles customer enquiries, records sales in cash book, issues receipts, manages mobile money payments.</li>
<li><strong>Security / Watchman:</strong> Guards the yard at night, logs any after-hours activity, reports incidents.</li>
</ul>
<p>In smaller yards, one person may hold two roles but never let the same person both handle cash and keep the inventory log without oversight. That is how internal theft happens. Rotate cash-handling duties or have the owner review the cash book daily.</p>

<h3>5. Scheduling Daily Operations: Sourcing, Dismantling, and Sales</h3>

<p>A predictable schedule reduces chaos and increases output. Here is a workable daily rhythm:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>7:008:00 AM:</strong> Open yard. Review yesterday's cash book. Assign tasks. Check tools.</li>
<li><strong>8:0011:00 AM:</strong> Dismantling and sorting. Focus on the highest-value vehicle first.</li>
<li><strong>11:00 AM1:00 PM:</strong> Sourcing visit dealers, auctions, or respond to callers selling vehicles.</li>
<li><strong>1:002:00 PM:</strong> Lunch break. Yard locked or supervised.</li>
<li><strong>2:004:00 PM:</strong> Sales and customer service. Process parts orders. Arrange deliveries.</li>
<li><strong>4:005:00 PM:</strong> Update inventory log. Record all expenses. Reconcile cash and mobile money.</li>
<li><strong>5:006:00 PM:</strong> Clean yard. Secure tools. Lock up. Send daily summary via WhatsApp to owner if off-site.</li>
</ul>

<div style="background:#ffe0e0;border-left:6px solid #d00000;padding:14px 18px;margin:20px 0;border-radius:4px;">
<strong style="color:#d00000;"> SAFETY WARNING:</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0;color:#333;">Car breaking and metal scrapping is one of the most dangerous informal occupations in Africa. Before any dismantling begins, ensure: (1) fuel tanks are drained and stored safely never cut a tank that has not been drained; (2) batteries are removed first to eliminate spark risk; (3) catalytic converters are removed with proper ventilation; (4) all workers wear steel-toe boots, gloves, and eye protection; (5) a fire extinguisher and first-aid kit are within 10 metres of any cutting or welding activity. In Kenya, the Occupational Safety and Health Act (OSHA, 2007) requires employers to provide a safe working environment this applies to informal yards too. One accident can destroy your business and your family's livelihood.</p>
</div>

<h3>6. Using a Mobile Phone for Basic Bookkeeping and Communication</h3>

<p>Your phone is a business tool, not just a communication device. Here is how to use it:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Spreadsheet apps:</strong> Google Sheets (free) or Microsoft Excel mobile. Create three tabs: Cash Book, Inventory, Expenses. Share with a trusted family member for backup.</li>
<li><strong>Mobile money records:</strong> M-Pesa, MTN MoMo, and Airtel Money all provide transaction histories. Screenshot or export monthly statements and reconcile against your cash book.</li>
<li><strong>WhatsApp Business:</strong> Use catalogue features to list parts with prices. Use broadcast lists to notify regular buyers of new stock. Save all customer conversations as records of orders.</li>
<li><strong>Camera:</strong> Photograph every vehicle on arrival (shows condition and registration), every part before sale (proof of quality), and every receipt. Store in a dedicated album.</li>
<li><strong>Voice notes:</strong> If typing is slow, record voice notes for daily summaries and transcribe them later. This is faster and more accurate than memory.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li> Record every sale and expense in the cash book on the same day no exceptions.</li>
<li> Log every vehicle on arrival: registration, make, purchase price, source, date.</li>
<li> Tag and photograph every part removed before it leaves the yard.</li>
<li> Weigh all scrap metal on a calibrated scale never estimate weight.</li>
<li> Reconcile cash, mobile money, and cash book every evening before locking up.</li>
<li> Review profit margin per vehicle weekly; stop buying models that consistently underperform.</li>
<li> Never let one person control both cash and inventory without oversight.</li>
<li> Back up your mobile spreadsheet weekly to Google Drive or email.</li>
</ul>
