Fishing & Fish Farming

A complete 6-month program following one tilapia/catfish production cycle — from choosing your site to harvesting and selling your first 200+ kg of fish.

⏱️ 6 Months (8–12 hrs/week)📊 Beginner → Business-ready🌍 Hybrid: Online + Offline🎓 Certificate on completion

Course Overview

Why 6 months? Because that is one tilapia production cycle: a 5-gram fingerling stocked in Month 2 reaches 300–500 g table size in about 6 months of good management. You study each month while your own pond (or your plan) moves through exactly that stage — by graduation you have harvested your first fish or hold a complete, costed plan.

Structure: 6 monthly modules → lessons → practical assignments → a 10-question quiz each month → final business project.

How to study (hybrid): Online — modules on your phone, sessions logged in the Study Diary, questions to the rAs AI assistant. Offline — save this page (Print → Save as PDF); the practicals happen at the water, not the screen.

⚠️ Read first: the three rules that protect your investment

  • Oxygen kills faster than hunger. Most mass fish deaths are dawn oxygen crashes in over-fed, over-green ponds. Month 4 teaches you to read your water daily.
  • Never buy fingerlings blind. Mixed-sex, stunted or mislabelled fingerlings doom a cycle before it starts — Month 3 gives you the buying checklist.
  • Water is the law's business too: dam construction, stream diversion and some species (e.g. catfish in some countries) need permits. One visit to your fisheries office in Month 1 prevents a demolition order in Month 6.
MONTH 1 — Aquaculture Science, Species & Systems ▾

Lesson 1.1 — The opportunity

  • Aquaculture is the world's fastest-growing food sector; Africa imports huge volumes of frozen fish while local demand for fresh fish goes unmet — a pond near a town sells itself.
  • Fish are the most efficient farmed animal: good tilapia convert 1.5–2.0 kg of feed into 1 kg of fish (FCR), against ~3 for pigs and ~6+ for cattle.
  • Fish don't spend energy standing or staying warm — that's why their FCR beats land animals.

Lesson 1.2 — Choose your species

Lesson 1.3 — Production systems compared

  • Earthen ponds: cheapest per m², natural food (plankton) supplements feed — the default choice and this course's main track.
  • Concrete/lined tanks: where soil won't hold water; higher cost, full feed dependence, easier harvest.
  • Cages in dams/lakes: low land cost, high stocking — but needs permits, security and open water access.
  • Recirculating systems (RAS): high-tech, electricity-dependent — not a beginner route in load-shedding regions.

Lesson 1.5 — Legal groundwork

  • Visit your fisheries/agriculture office: pond permits, water rights, protected species rules, and whether catfish or non-native tilapia strains are restricted in your area.
  • Ask the same office for hatchery lists and extension support — they know the honest fingerling suppliers.

MONTH 2 — Pond Siting, Design & Construction ▾

Lesson 2.1 — Site selection

  • Water first: a reliable source (stream, borehole, roof/dam runoff) able to fill the pond and replace losses of roughly 1–2 cm depth per day to seepage and evaporation.
  • Soil test: squeeze a moist ball of subsoil — if it holds shape and ribbons between fingers there's enough clay to hold water; sandy soil means lining or tanks.
  • Gentle slope (1–2%) lets you fill and drain by gravity — pumps are cost and breakdowns.
  • Security and access: close enough to watch, reachable by the buyer's vehicle.

Lesson 2.2 — Design of a starter pond

  • Starter size: 200–300 m² (e.g. 10 m × 20–30 m) — big enough for real income, small enough to manage and afford.
  • Depth: 0.8–1 m at the shallow end, 1.2–1.5 m at the drain end; deeper is NOT better (dark, cold, unproductive bottom water).
  • Dike (wall) slopes of about 2:1 (2 m horizontal per 1 m height) resist erosion; top width 1 m minimum for walking.
  • Essential fittings: inlet with screen (keep wild fish OUT), outlet/overflow with screen (keep your fish IN), and a drain (standpipe or siphon) for full harvest.

Lesson 2.3 — Construction step by step

  • Mark the pond, strip and SAVE the topsoil (it goes back on the dikes to grow protective grass).
  • Dig from the centre outward, building dikes in 15–20 cm compacted layers — compaction is what holds water; loose-tipped soil leaks.
  • Cut-and-fill balance: soil dug from the pond basin should equal soil needed for dikes — good design means no soil bought or carted away.
  • A 300 m² hand-dug pond is typically 25–60 person-days of labour; hiring a small excavator for the basin can pay for itself in time saved.
  • Plant creeping grass on dikes immediately; bare dikes wash away in the first rains.

Lesson 2.4 — Preparing the pond for life

  • Lime the dry pond bottom (agricultural lime ~150–250 kg per 300 m², more on acid soils): corrects pH and disinfects.
  • Fertilise to start the plankton bloom: well-rotted manure ~50–100 kg per 300 m² per week initially, or a small dose of DAP/urea — green water is free fish food.
  • Fill through the screened inlet and let the pond "brew" 1–2 weeks until light green before any fish arrive.
✅ Iron-Clad Price Check: price digging labour per m³, lime, piping and screens at 3 suppliers each. Labour quotes for pond digging vary more than any other item in aquaculture.

Inlet screens keep wild fish and eggs out; outlet screens keep your stock in.

Corrects soil/water pH and disinfects the bottom (~150–250 kg lime per 300 m²).
Q6. Why fertilise the pond before stocking?
Show answer
Manure/fertiliser grows a plankton bloom — green water is free natural feed for the fish.
Q7. What dike slope resists erosion?

About 2:1 (2 horizontal to 1 vertical), grassed as soon as built.
Q8. Why is a gravity drain so valuable?
Show answer
It allows complete, cheap harvest and full drying between cycles without pumping costs.
Q9. Daily water loss to plan for?

Roughly 1–2 cm of depth per day to seepage plus evaporation.

MONTH 3 — Fingerlings, Stocking & Early Care ▾

Lesson 3.1 — Buying fingerlings like a professional

  • Buy ONLY from a registered hatchery — ask for their fisheries registration and other farmers' references.
  • For tilapia insist on all-male (sex-reversed or hand-sexed) fingerlings: males grow ~40% faster and, with no females, no pond-stunting baby boom.
  • Good fingerlings: uniform size (3–10 g), active swimming, no white spots/frayed fins, from parents replaced regularly (avoid inbred stock).
  • Order 5–10% extra to cover normal early losses.

Lesson 3.2 — Stocking rates

SystemStockingNotes
Tilapia, fertilised pond + feed2–3 fish/m²The beginner sweet spot: 300 m² pond = 600–900 fish
Tilapia, feed-only intensive4–6/m²Needs aeration awareness and strict water checks
Catfish5–10/m²Air-breathing allows density; grade sizes to limit cannibalism
Polyculture2–3 tilapia/m² + 10% catfishStock catfish SMALLER than the tilapia

Lesson 3.3 — Transport and release (where cycles are won)

  • Transport early morning in oxygen-filled bags or aerated containers; heat + crowding in transit kills more fingerlings than any disease.
  • Temperature acclimation: float the sealed bags on the pond 15–30 minutes, then mix pond water into the bag gradually before release — dumping fish into water of a different temperature shocks and kills them.
  • Release in the shallow end, in the cool of the day; do not feed on day one.

Lesson 3.4 — The first month in the pond

  • Start feeding day 2: a high-protein starter crumble (30–35% CP) at ~5% of estimated body weight daily, split morning and afternoon.
  • Feed at the same spot and time — fish learn the dinner bell, and you get a daily health parade.
  • Watch the feed: if it's finished in 15 minutes, slightly increase; floating leftovers after 30 minutes mean cut back (leftover feed becomes ammonia).
  • Predator defence from day one: herons and kingfishers take fingerlings daily — lines/netting over shallow areas, monitor lizards and otters excluded by tidy dikes and fencing where needed.

(2) Stock your pond using the full acclimation procedure — photograph each step. (3) Set the feeding station and start the daily feed log: date, grams fed, minutes to finish, dead fish count.

MONTH 4 — Feeding, Water Management & Pond Health ▾

Lesson 4.1 — The feeding program

Fish sizeFeed typeProteinDaily rate (% body weight)
5–20 gStarter crumble30–35%5–8%
20–100 gGrower pellet (2 mm)28–30%3–5%
100–300 gGrower pellet (3–4 mm)25–28%2–3%
300 g+Finisher pellet25%1.5–2%
  • Recalculate feed monthly from sampling (Lesson 4.4): daily feed = average weight × fish count × rate%.
  • Floating pellets cost more but show you exactly what's eaten — for beginners they pay for themselves in avoided waste.
  • Feed cost cutters that work: pond fertilisation (green water), duckweed and azolla grown in side ditches, kitchen-safe vegetable supplements for tilapia. They supplement — the pellet remains the growth engine.

Lesson 4.2 — Managing the oxygen budget

  • Oxygen enters from air and daytime plankton photosynthesis; plankton and fish CONSUME it all night — dawn is always the lowest point.
  • Danger builds when: water is pea-soup green, weather is hot and windless, cloudy days follow each other, or you overfeed.
  • Dawn patrol: at first light, gasping fish at the surface = act NOW — flush in fresh water, splash/beat the water surface, stop feeding for the day.
  • Prevention beats rescue: keep the arm-visible-to-30 cm green, never feed more than fish clear in 30 minutes, and partial-exchange water in the hottest months.

Lesson 4.3 — Routine water care

  • Top up losses weekly through the screened inlet; after heavy rain check pH and overflow screens.
  • Monthly: repeat a small manure/fertiliser dose only if the green is fading — fertilising a dark-green pond invites a crash.
  • Remove marginal weeds before they carpet the surface (they shade out plankton and shelter predators).

Lesson 4.4 — Sampling: weighing the invisible herd

  • Every 2–4 weeks, seine or cast-net 20–30 fish, weigh together, divide for average — this drives your feed maths and tells you growth rate.
  • Healthy tilapia growth: roughly 1.5–3 g/day mid-cycle; flat growth means feed, oxygen or stunting problems.
  • Handle fish with wet hands/nets, sample in the cool morning, and return them within minutes.

Lesson 4.5 — Disease: read the pond, not just the fish

  • Most "disease" in ponds is water quality in disguise: fix oxygen, ammonia and crowding first.
  • Warning signs: fish rubbing/flashing (parasites), white spots or cotton patches (treat early, improve water), sunken eyes, ragged fins, deaths climbing over days.
  • First response protocol: stop feeding, exchange 20–30% of water, count and remove dead fish daily (buried with lime, never thrown in the bush), and call fisheries extension if deaths continue 3+ days.
  • Never pour antibiotics or chemicals into a food-fish pond without extension/vet guidance — illegal residues destroy your market.

QUIZ 4 — Feeding & water

Q1. When is pond oxygen lowest and why?
Show answer
At dawn — plankton and fish consume oxygen all night with no photosynthesis to replace it.

Show answer
0.1 kg × 800 × 3% = 2.4 kg per day.

Show answer
Over-dark green water, hot windless/cloudy spells, and overfeeding (any three).
Q4. Emergency steps for gasping fish?
Show answer
Flush in fresh water, agitate/splash the surface, stop feeding that day.
Q5. What growth rate should mid-cycle tilapia show?
Show answer
Roughly 1.5–3 g/day; flat growth signals feed, oxygen or stunting problems.
Q6. Why are floating pellets beginner-friendly?
Show answer
You can SEE consumption, so overfeeding (the ammonia machine) is avoided.
Q7. What does fish 'flashing' (rubbing) suggest?
Show answer
External parasites irritating the skin — improve water and treat early with guidance.
Q8. How are dead fish disposed of?
Show answer
Counted, removed daily and buried with lime — never left or thrown nearby.
Q9. Why not fertilise a dark-green pond?
Show answer
The bloom is already at crash density; more nutrients invite a die-off and dawn oxygen collapse.

MONTH 5 — Growth to Market Size, Records & Problem-Solving ▾

Lesson 5.1 — Pushing to market size

  • Target: tilapia 300–500 g (plate size) at month 6; catfish 800 g–1.2 kg. Ask YOUR buyers first — some markets pay best for 250 g "braai size", others only buy 400 g+.
  • Grade if growth spreads: move the biggest 20% ("shooters") to a holding hapa/net or sell them early — they hog feed and, in catfish, eat their siblings.
  • Last month feed discipline: keep protein up; cheap finishing on maize bran alone gives soft, fatty fish and slow growth.

Lesson 5.2 — The records that run the farm

  • Feed register (bags in, kg fed daily), sampling log (dates, average weights), mortality log, water/weather notes, money book.
  • Compute your live FCR monthly: total feed used ÷ total weight gained (from sampling) — it is THE profitability dial.
  • Survival benchmark: 80–90% from stocking to harvest is good; below 70% demands a cause-hunt in your logs.

Lesson 5.3 — Troubleshooting table

Lesson 5.4 — Planning the harvest before the harvest

  • Pre-sell: 2–4 weeks out, confirm buyers, sizes, prices and dates — fish out of water lose value by the hour, so the market is arranged BEFORE the net goes in.
  • Partial harvesting (netting the biggest every 2 weeks) smooths cash flow and reduces pond load; full drain-harvest ends the cycle.
  • Line up harvest gear: seine net sized to your pond, baskets/crates, clean water for holding, scale, ice or wet sacks for transport.

MONTH 6 — Harvest, Selling, Economics & Your Business Plan ▾

Lesson 6.1 — Harvest day, professionally run

  • Stop feeding 24 hours before harvest (cleaner fish, cleaner water in transport).
  • Harvest at dawn while water and air are cool; seine first, then drain for the remainder.
  • Sort by size at the pond into holding containers of clean water; weigh every sale on a scale in front of the buyer.
  • Sell live or on ice within hours — ice at 1:1 with fish weight keeps quality for a full market day.

Lesson 6.2 — Where fish money is made

  • Farm-gate live sales: zero transport, customers pay premium for "swimming fresh".
  • Market/vendor women networks: volume movers — reliability wins them, not price alone.
  • Braai stands, lodges and restaurants: pay top prices for graded, consistent sizes on a schedule.
  • Value addition next steps: gutted-on-ice, smoked fish (see the rAs Smoking Fish & Meat course), and fingerling production once you master grow-out.

Lesson 6.4 — Restart and scale

  • Between cycles: drain fully, sun-dry the bottom 1–2 weeks, repair dikes, re-lime — clean restart prevents disease carry-over.
  • Scaling ladder: 1 pond → 2–3 staggered ponds (harvest every 2 months) → your own fingerling hapas → smoked-fish line.
  • Staggered ponds are the unlock for restaurant/lodge contracts: they buy schedules, not gluts.

Lesson 6.5 — Final project: your bankable business plan

  • Assemble from your six months of real records: site and system description, cycle calendar, full costings with YOUR numbers, two buyer commitments, risk plan (oxygen crash, drought, theft), and a 3-year scaling ladder.
  • Submit with photos and your logs summary for your rAs certificate — the same document is your loan or partner application.

📖 Glossary

  • Fingerling — young fish of stocking size (3–10 g).
  • All-male tilapia — sex-reversed or hand-sexed males; faster growth, no pond breeding.
  • FCR — feed conversion ratio: kg feed per kg fish gained.
  • DO — dissolved oxygen; keep above 5 mg/L.
  • Bloom — the green plankton population that feeds fish and makes daytime oxygen.
  • Secchi/arm test — visibility check; arm seen to ~30 cm = healthy green.
  • Hapa — a net enclosure inside a pond for grading or fingerlings.
  • Shooter — an oversized fast-grower that dominates or cannibalises.
  • Seine — the long harvest net pulled through the pond.
  • Stunting — a pond of small fish caused by overbreeding/overcrowding.
