Lessons

1. Restraining the pig safely with ropes or a crate

Before you slaughter, you must restrain the pig so it cannot injure itself, you, or helpers. A panicked pig can weigh 60100 kg and thrash violently. The goal is to limit movement without causing pain or stress, which affects meat quality. For small pigs under 50 kg, a simple rope restraint works. Use a 1015 mm thick soft rope, 34 meters long. Make a loop around the snout (a pigs snout is sensitive, so do not tighten too much), then cross the rope behind the ears and tie to a sturdy post or tree. The pig will pull backward, but the loop tightens only slightly. Alternatively, use a pig crate a wooden or metal box 1.2 m long, 0.6 m wide, 0.9 m high, with a sliding door. The crate should have ventilation holes and a floor that prevents slipping. Lead the pig in with feed, then close the door. This method is less stressful because the pig feels secure. For larger pigs (over 80 kg), a crate is safer. Never lift a pig by the ears or tail. Always approach calmly and quietly. Work in a shaded area away from other pigs to reduce panic. If using ropes, tie the pigs hind legs together with a 1.5 m rope, then secure the front legs separately. Leave about 30 cm of slack so the pig can stand but not run. Check that ropes are not cutting into skin. Restraining should take less than 2 minutes. If the pig fights for more than 5 minutes, release and try again later. Proper restraint reduces stress hormones like cortisol, which cause pale, soft, exudative (PSE) meat. A calm pig bleeds better and yields tastier meat.

2. Stunning methods approved for small-scale operations

Stunning makes the pig unconscious before sticking, so it feels no pain. For small-scale African operations, three methods are approved: captive bolt, electrical stunning, and CO2 stunning. Captive bolt is most common. Use a penetrating captive bolt gun (e.g., .22 caliber) placed on the pigs forehead, 23 cm above the eyes, aiming slightly upward toward the brain. The bolt must be cleaned and oiled before each use. After firing, the pig should collapse immediately with legs rigid, then relax. If the pig vocalizes or tries to stand, the stun failed do not stick; re-stun. Electrical stunning: use a stunner with 220240 volts, 1.52.5 amps for 510 seconds. Place electrodes on both sides of the head (behind the ears) or on the head and chest. The pig will become rigid, then convulse. This method requires a power source or a generator. CO2 stunning is rarely used in small setups because it needs a gas chamber and is expensive. For traditional or religious slaughter without stunning, check local laws some countries allow it, but it causes more stress. After stunning, the pig must be stuck within 30 seconds. If you wait longer, the pig may regain consciousness. Signs of effective stunning: no blink reflex, no rhythmic breathing, drooping tongue, and no response to a nose prick. Always have a backup captive bolt gun. Never use a hammer or axe it causes pain and incomplete stunning. Train at least two people: one to restrain, one to stun. Keep the stun gun clean with 70% alcohol between pigs. Proper stunning reduces suffering and prevents blood spots in meat.

3. Proper sticking technique to ensure complete bleeding

Sticking means cutting the major blood vessels in the neck to drain blood quickly. Do it within 30 seconds after stunning. Lay the pig on its side or back on a clean surface. Use a sharp, sterile knife with a 1520 cm blade (e.g., a sticking knife or boning knife). The best spot is the jugular furrow a groove on the lower neck, just in front of the chest. Insert the knife 58 cm deep, pointing toward the opposite shoulder. Cut upward and outward to sever the carotid artery and jugular vein. Do not stab the heart that causes blood to pool in the chest and ruins the lungs. For complete bleeding, the pig should hang head-down or be on a slope so blood drains away. If hanging, use a gambrel or rope through the Achilles tendons, hoist to 1.52 m height. Bleeding takes 35 minutes. You should see a steady stream, then a trickle. Collect blood in a clean bucket if you want it for blood sausage add 1 teaspoon of salt per liter of blood to prevent clotting, and stir every 2 minutes. If blood stops too early, the knife may have missed the artery make a second cut slightly lower. After bleeding, check for signs of incomplete bleed: dark red meat, engorged organs, or blood in the chest cavity. Incomplete bleeding causes meat to spoil faster and taste gamey. Never stick a conscious pig it will thrash and cause bruises. Always wear gloves and an apron. Wash the knife with hot water and soap after each pig. Proper sticking yields 34 liters of blood from a 100 kg pig, which is enough for 2 kg of blood sausage.

4. Collecting blood for local dishes like blood sausage

Blood is a valuable food in many African communities used for blood sausage (e.g., mutura in Kenya, morcilla in Spanish-speaking areas, or black pudding). To collect blood safely, start with a clean 1015 liter bucket. Add 1 tablespoon of salt per liter of blood (about 10 g/L) and stir well. Salt prevents clotting so the blood stays liquid. You can also add 1 teaspoon of vinegar per liter to preserve color. As soon as you stick the pig, hold the bucket under the stream. Avoid getting hair, dirt, or stomach contents in the blood. If the pig is hanging, place the bucket on the ground below the head. Stir gently every 2 minutes with a clean stick or spoon. After collection, strain the blood through a fine sieve or cheesecloth to remove any clots or debris. Use within 2 hours, or refrigerate at 4C for up to 24 hours. For blood sausage, mix blood with cooked rice, onions, spices (salt, pepper, cumin), and fat (e.g., pork fat) in a 2:1 ratio of blood to filler. Stuff into cleaned pig intestines (casings) that have been soaked in salt water for 30 minutes. Tie ends and boil gently for 45 minutes. Alternatively, make blood pudding by baking the mixture in a covered pot at 180C for 1 hour. Always cook blood thoroughly to kill bacteria internal temperature must reach 71C. Never consume raw blood. If you do not want to use blood, dispose of it in a pit 50 cm deep, away from water sources. In many cultures, sharing blood dishes builds community and reduces waste. Proper collection also keeps the slaughter area clean and prevents flies.

5. Checking for signs of stress and its effect on meat quality

Stress before and during slaughter ruins meat. When a pig is scared, its body releases adrenaline and cortisol. These hormones use up energy and cause the meat to become pale, soft, and watery (PSE) or dark, firm, and dry (DFD). PSE meat loses water quickly, so it shrinks when cooked and tastes bland. DFD meat spoils faster and has a sticky texture. To check for stress, watch the pig before stunning. Signs of stress: wide eyes, rapid breathing (over 40 breaths per minute), high-pitched squealing, attempts to escape, and defecating or urinating. If you see these, stop and calm the pig. Move slowly, speak softly, and avoid sudden movements. Provide shade and water. A calm pig has relaxed ears, normal breathing (1525 breaths per minute), and no vocalization. After stunning, check the carcass for stress signs: blood spots in the muscle, broken bones from thrashing, or a dark red color from incomplete bleeding. To prevent stress, handle pigs gently in the days before slaughter. Do not mix unfamiliar pigs they fight. Do not transport pigs for more than 2 hours without rest. At the slaughter site, let the pig rest for 30 minutes before stunning. Use the crate or rope restraint calmly. Stun properly a bad stun causes pain and stress. After sticking, bleed completely. Meat from a calm, well-bled pig is bright red, firm, and holds water well. It tastes sweeter and stays fresh longer. You can test meat quality by pressing it good meat springs back. If it stays dented, it may be DFD. Always keep records of which pigs had stress signs so you can improve handling.

6. Cleaning and sanitizing tools after bleeding

After slaughter, clean all tools immediately. Blood and fat dry quickly and can harbor bacteria like Salmonella and E. coli. You need: hot water (at least 60C), dish soap, a scrub brush, and a sanitizer (e.g., 1 tablespoon of bleach per 4 liters of water, or 70% alcohol). First, rinse tools with cold water to remove blood. Then wash with hot soapy water, scrubbing all surfaces knives, stun gun, ropes, buckets, and the crate. Pay attention to knife handles and the bolt of the captive bolt gun. Use a bottle brush for holes. Rinse with clean hot water. Then sanitize by soaking in bleach solution for 2 minutes or wiping with alcohol. Do not mix bleach with other chemicals. Air-dry tools on a clean rack do not use a towel, which can spread bacteria. For ropes, wash with soap and hot water, then soak in bleach solution for 10 minutes, rinse, and hang to dry in sunlight. Sunlight kills many germs. The crate should be scrubbed, then sprayed with bleach solution, and left to dry. Knives should be sharpened after cleaning a dull knife is dangerous. Store all tools in a clean, dry place. Never leave blood or fat on tools overnight. Also clean your hands and apron with soap and water. If you used a stun gun, remove the bolt and clean the barrel with a patch and oil. For electrical stunners, wipe with a damp cloth never immerse in water. Proper cleaning prevents cross-contamination between pigs and keeps your operation healthy. Make a cleaning schedule: after every pig, and a deep clean weekly. Good sanitation also keeps flies away and reduces smell. Remember, clean tools mean safer meat for your family and customers.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Humane Slaughter and Bleeding for Quality Meat

For African smallholder pig enterprises, the slaughter stage is where the largest share of carcass value is either protected or lost. A poorly stunned, stressed pig produces pale, soft, exudative (PSE) or dark, firm, dry (DFD) meat that bleeds poorly, discolours quickly, and loses weight at retail. In markets where customers judge pork by colour, smell, and shelf life and where blood is sold separately for blood sausage (mutura, black pudding, and similar products) the difference between a clean kill and a rushed one is directly measurable in cash. This Practical Reference covers restraining, stunning, sticking, blood collection, stress assessment, and tool hygiene for small-scale operations in Africa.

Lesson 1: Restraining the Pig Safely

Restraint is the first point where meat quality is decided. Pigs are intelligent, strong, and vocal; mishandling triggers adrenaline and cortisol, which deplete muscle glycogen and push the carcass toward DFD meat. Use one of two approved methods:

  • Restraint crate: A simple metal or hardwood crate with a head gate and open side for access. Size it so the pig can stand but not turn. This is the preferred method for pigs above 60 kg.
  • Rope restraint: A nose snare (rope loop over the upper jaw behind the canine teeth) controls the head without choking. For smaller pigs, a leg hobble and a wall board work. Never lift a pig by the ears, tail, or single leg.

Keep the area quiet, dim, and free of other animals. Move pigs in small groups using boards, not sticks. A stressed pig that has been chased for 10 minutes will bleed poorly and produce darker meat.

Lesson 2: Stunning Methods for Small-Scale Operations

Stunning renders the pig unconscious and insensible to pain before sticking. Two methods are practical and approved for small-scale African operations:

After stunning, check for unconsciousness: no corneal reflex, no rhythmic breathing, floppy jaw, and no response to a nose pinch. If any sign of consciousness returns, re-stun immediately. Do not stick a conscious pig this is both inhumane and illegal in most jurisdictions, and it ruins bleeding.

Lesson 3: Proper Sticking Technique

Sticking severs the major blood vessels at the base of the heart so the pig bleeds out fast and fully. The correct technique is the chest stick:

  1. Place the stunned pig on its back or side on a clean, hard surface.
  2. Locate the jugular notch the soft depression at the base of the neck, just above the breastbone.
  3. Insert a sharp, clean knife (1215 cm blade) at a 45 angle, pointing toward the heart.
  4. Sever the anterior vena cava and carotid arteries in one clean motion. Do not stab repeatedly.
  5. Hold the pig with the head slightly lower than the body so blood drains freely.

Bleeding should take 46 minutes. A pig that bleeds out in under 3 minutes was likely over-stressed; one that takes over 8 minutes was poorly stuck or has a clotting disorder. Complete bleeding removes about 34% of live weight and is essential for shelf life.

SAFETY WARNING: Never stick a pig before confirming unconsciousness. A conscious pig will struggle, the knife can slip, and you risk cutting yourself or a helper. Always have a second person present, wear cut-resistant gloves, and keep a first-aid kit with sterile dressings within reach. Blood-borne zoonoses (e.g., Streptococcus suis, brucellosis) can enter through cuts cover all open wounds before slaughter.

Lesson 4: Collecting Blood for Local Dishes

Blood sausage, mutura, and black pudding are high-margin products in many African markets. Clean collection adds value to the same slaughter event. Use a clean, food-grade container (stainless steel or food-grade plastic) placed directly under the stick wound. Add an anticoagulant immediately 5 g of salt per litre of blood, or 1 g of sodium citrate per litre and stir gently with a clean wooden paddle to prevent clotting. Strain through a fine sieve within 30 minutes and refrigerate or cook within 2 hours.

Lesson 5: Checking for Signs of Stress

Stress before and during slaughter is the single largest cause of quality loss in smallholder pig meat. Watch for these signs:

  • Pre-slaughter: Panting, squealing, muscle tremors, attempts to escape, defecation all indicate acute stress.
  • During bleeding: Slow or intermittent blood flow, dark blood, or incomplete exsanguination.
  • Post-slaughter: Meat that is pale, soft, and watery (PSE) or dark, firm, and dry (DFD). PSE comes from acute stress; DFD from prolonged stress.

Both conditions reduce shelf life and customer appeal. DFD meat has a high pH (>6.0), spoils faster, and is often rejected at premium markets. The fix is simple: calm handling, proper stunning, and fast sticking.

Lesson 6: Cleaning and Sanitising Tools After Bleeding

Blood is a rich medium for bacteria. Clean tools immediately after use:

  1. Rinse all blood off knives, bolts, and containers with cold water (hot water coagulates blood and makes it stick).
  2. Wash with warm water and detergent, scrubbing all surfaces.
  3. Sanitise with 1:10 bleach solution (or 70% alcohol for knives) for at least 2 minutes.
  4. Air-dry on a clean rack never wipe with a shared cloth.
  5. Store knives in a clean sheath or on a magnetic strip, not in a drawer with other tools.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Restrain the pig calmly in a crate or with a nose snare no chasing, no sticks.
  • Stun correctly (captive bolt or head-only electrical) and verify unconsciousness.
  • Stick within 30 seconds of stunning, at the jugular notch, aimed at the heart.
  • Allow 46 minutes of complete bleeding; position the pig head-low.
  • Collect blood in a clean container with anticoagulant; strain and chill within 30 minutes.
  • Watch for stress signs (panting, tremors, PSE/DFD meat) and correct handling next time.
  • Rinse tools in cold water, wash with detergent, sanitise, and air-dry.
  • Record live weight, blood volume, and meat colour for every slaughter to track quality.
