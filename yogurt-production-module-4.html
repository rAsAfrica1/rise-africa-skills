1. Standardization of Milk Fat and Solids

Milk straight from the cow or goat varies in fat and solids. For yogurt, you need consistent fat and solids to get the same texture and taste every batch. Standardization means adjusting fat and solids to a target level. In Africa, many small dairies use fresh cow milk with 3.5-4% fat. For set yogurt, aim for 3-3.5% fat; for stirred yogurt, 1.5-2% is common. If your milk is too rich, skim some cream. If too thin, add skim milk powder (SMP) or cream.

To measure fat, use a butyrometer (Gerber method). Take 10 ml sulfuric acid in a butyrometer, add 11 ml milk, 1 ml amyl alcohol, centrifuge for 5 minutes, then read the fat percentage. For solids, use a lactometer or a refractometer. A simple method: weigh 10 g milk, dry in an oven at 105Â°C for 3 hours, and weigh the residue. That gives total solids. Target total solids for yogurt: 12-14%. If your milk has 11% solids, add 1-3% SMP.

Calculate how much cream or SMP to add. Example: You have 100 L milk with 4% fat. You want 2% fat. You need to remove cream. Using Pearson's square: (4-2) = 2 parts milk, (2-0) = 2 parts cream? Actually, if cream is 40% fat, mix 95 L milk with 5 L cream? No, to reduce fat, you remove cream. For 100 L at 4% fat, you have 4 L fat. To get 2% fat, you need 200 L total? That's wrong. Better: Use formula: Volume of milk to skim = (Initial fat - Target fat) / (Initial fat - Fat in skim) * total volume. If skim has 0.1% fat, then (4-2)/(4-0.1)*100 = 51.3 L. So you skim 51.3 L, leaving 48.7 L whole milk? That gives 48.7 L at 4% + 51.3 L at 0.1% = total fat 1.95+0.05=2.0% in 100 L. So you separate 51.3 L, remove cream, and mix back the skim. That's standardization.

For solids, add SMP at 1-3% of milk weight. For 100 L milk (about 103 kg), add 1-3 kg SMP. Mix well with a whisk or blender. Let it hydrate for 20 minutes. Always standardize before pasteurization and homogenization.

2. Homogenization Principles and Equipment

Homogenization breaks fat globules into tiny ones so they don't rise as cream. In yogurt, it prevents a cream layer and makes texture smoother. Without homogenization, fat separates and the yogurt looks uneven. The principle: force hot milk through a narrow valve at high pressure. The fat globules shatter into smaller ones (1-2 microns) and get coated with proteins, so they stay suspended.

Equipment: A homogenizer is a high-pressure pump with a valve. Small-scale: manual homogenizer or a blender. For 100-500 L batches, use a two-stage homogenizer. First stage: 150-200 bar (15-20 MPa). Second stage: 30-50 bar (3-5 MPa). The second stage breaks clusters that form after the first stage. In Africa, many small dairies use a single-stage homogenizer at 200 bar, but two-stage gives better stability. If you don't have a homogenizer, a high-speed blender (like a kitchen blender) can work for small batches: blend for 2-3 minutes at high speed. But it's not as consistent.

Temperature matters: Homogenize at 60-70Â°C. Cold milk is too thick and may clog. Hot milk (above 75Â°C) can cause protein denaturation. So pasteurize first (63Â°C for 30 min or 72Â°C for 15 sec), then homogenize while still hot. If you homogenize before pasteurization, do it at 60Â°C, then pasteurize.

Pressure: Too low (below 100 bar) leaves large globules. Too high (above 250 bar) can damage proteins and make yogurt too thin. For yogurt, 150-200 bar is ideal. For set yogurt, 100-150 bar. For drinking yogurt, 200-250 bar. Always check pressure gauge. After homogenization, cool milk to 42-45Â°C for inoculation. Clean the homogenizer immediately after use with warm water and detergent, then sanitize. In rural areas, a hand-cranked homogenizer exists but is slow. A simple alternative: use a manual cream separator to remove cream, then re-mix with skim milk and blend. That's not true homogenization but helps.

3. Effects of Homogenization on Yogurt Texture

Homogenization changes yogurt texture in three main ways: it reduces cream separation, increases viscosity, and improves water-holding capacity. Without homogenization, fat rises and forms a cream layer on top. That looks unappetizing and makes the yogurt inconsistent. Homogenized yogurt has a uniform, creamy look.

Second, homogenization increases viscosity. Smaller fat globules have more surface area, so they interact with proteins (casein) and form a stronger gel. This means thicker yogurt. For set yogurt, homogenization at 150 bar gives a firm gel. For stirred yogurt, 200 bar gives a smooth, thick body. But too much homogenization (above 250 bar) can break the gel and make it runny. So find the right pressure for your product.

Third, water-holding capacity improves. Homogenized yogurt holds water better, so less whey separates during storage. This is important in Africa where cold storage is limited. If you don't homogenize, whey leaks out and the yogurt looks spoiled. Homogenization also makes the yogurt taste creamier because fat is evenly distributed.

However, homogenization can make yogurt more sensitive to heat. If you pasteurize after homogenization, you might get a weaker gel. So always homogenize after pasteurization. Also, homogenization can increase the risk of oxidation, causing off-flavors. Use fresh milk and avoid excessive pressure. In practice, for a small dairy in Kenya or Nigeria, a two-stage homogenizer at 180/40 bar gives excellent yogurt. If you use a blender, blend for 2 minutes, but don't overheat. Test your yogurt: after 24 hours, check for cream layer and whey. If cream layer is thick, increase homogenization pressure. If whey is high, increase solids or homogenization.

4. Deaeration and Vacuum Treatment

Deaeration removes air from milk before fermentation. Air in milk comes from pumping, stirring, or milking. Air causes problems: it oxidizes fats and vitamins, creates foam, and can make yogurt texture uneven. In yogurt, trapped air can cause pinholes and weak gel. Vacuum treatment uses a vacuum chamber to pull air out. Milk is heated to 60-70Â°C, then sprayed into a vacuum chamber at low pressure (0.5-0.8 bar absolute). The air bubbles expand and escape. The milk is then cooled and processed.

In Africa, deaeration is often skipped because equipment is expensive. But you can do simple deaeration: let milk stand in a tall container for 30 minutes, then skim off foam. Or use a vacuum pump with a sealed pot. For small-scale, a manual vacuum pump (like for wine) can work. Heat milk to 65Â°C, put in a sealed container, apply vacuum for 10 minutes. You'll see foam rise; remove it. This reduces air by 50-70%.

Why do it? Deaeration improves homogenization efficiency because air interferes with fat globule breakup. It also reduces oxidation, so yogurt tastes fresher and lasts longer. It reduces foaming during pasteurization, which can cause fouling in heat exchangers. For set yogurt, deaeration helps get a smooth surface without bubbles. For drinking yogurt, it prevents foamy texture.

If you don't have a vacuum, you can use a simple method: after pasteurization, let milk cool to 60Â°C, then pour it from a height into another container. This releases some air. Or use a hand blender on low speed for 1 minute to release bubbles, then let stand. But be careful not to introduce more air. In large dairies, deaeration is standard before homogenization. In small dairies, focus on minimizing air by gentle pumping and avoiding splashing. Even simple deaeration can improve yogurt quality. Always deaerate before homogenization for best results.

5. Adding Fortifiers: Skim Milk Powder and Whey Proteins

Fortifiers increase solids and protein, making yogurt thicker and creamier. The two common ones are skim milk powder (SMP) and whey protein concentrate (WPC). SMP is dried skim milk, usually 34-36% protein, 50% lactose, 1% fat. WPC is 35-80% protein, depending on grade. In Africa, SMP is widely available and cheap. WPC is more expensive but gives a smoother texture and higher protein.

How much to add? For yogurt, target 12-14% total solids. If your milk has 11% solids, add 1-3% SMP. For 100 L milk (about 103 kg), add 1-3 kg SMP. For WPC, add 0.5-2% because it's more concentrated. For example, to boost protein by 1%, add 2-3% SMP or 1-1.5% WPC. Always add slowly while stirring to avoid lumps. Use a whisk or blender. Let it hydrate for 20-30 minutes before pasteurization. Hydration allows proteins to absorb water and improves texture.

SMP affects flavor: it adds a cooked or powdery taste if too much. Keep below 4% of milk weight. WPC has a clean flavor. For set yogurt, SMP at 2% gives a firm gel. For drinking yogurt, 1% SMP plus 0.5% WPC gives a smooth mouthfeel. In Africa, some producers use local alternatives: powdered milk from local dairies, or even soy milk powder, but that changes flavor. Stick to dairy powders for authentic yogurt.

Always check the label: SMP should be low-heat or medium-heat, not high-heat, because high-heat powder has denatured proteins that won't gel well. Store SMP in a cool, dry place. Add fortifiers before homogenization so they are evenly mixed. If you add after homogenization, you might get lumps. Also, adding SMP increases viscosity, so you might need to adjust homogenization pressure (lower pressure for thicker mix). Test your batch: after fermentation, check texture. If too thin, add more SMP next time. If too powdery, reduce SMP.

6. Calculating Formulation Ratios

Formulation ratios help you mix milk, cream, SMP, and WPC to hit target fat, solids, and protein. You need simple math. Let's use a 100 L batch. Target: 3% fat, 12% solids, 4% protein. Your base milk: 4% fat, 11% solids, 3.2% protein. You have cream (40% fat), SMP (35% protein, 96% solids), and WPC (70% protein, 95% solids).

Step 1: Adjust fat. You want 3% fat from 4%. Use Pearson's square: (4-3)/(4-0.1) * 100 = 25.6 L of milk to skim? Actually, you remove cream. For 100 L at 4% fat, you have 4 L fat. To get 3% fat in 100 L, you need 3 L fat. So remove 1 L fat. Cream is 40% fat, so remove 1/0.4 = 2.5 L cream. That leaves 97.5 L milk with 3 L fat? Wait, removing 2.5 L cream removes 1 L fat and 1.5 L milk solids. So new volume = 97.5 L, fat = 3 L, fat% = 3/97.5 = 3.08%. Close enough. To be exact, use formula: Volume of cream to remove = (Initial fat - Target fat) * Total volume / (Cream fat - Target fat). So (4-3)*100/(40-3) = 100/37 = 2.7 L. Remove 2.7 L cream. Remaining volume = 97.3 L, fat = 4 - 2.7*0.4 = 2.92 L, fat% = 3.0%. Good.

Step 2: Adjust solids. After cream removal, solids: initial 11% of 100 L = 11 kg solids. Cream removal removes 2.7 L * 0.4 fat? Actually cream has 40% fat, 5% solids non-fat? Assume cream has 45% solids total. So 2.7 L cream removes 2.7*0.45 = 1.215 kg solids. Remaining solids = 11 - 1.215 = 9.785 kg in 97.3 L. Solids% = 10.06%. Target 12%, so need 12% of 97.3 = 11.676 kg solids. Add 11.676 - 9.785 = 1.891 kg SMP. SMP is 96% solids, so add 1.891/0.96 = 1.97 kg SMP. That also adds protein: SMP is 35% protein, so 1.97*0.35 = 0.69 kg protein. Initial protein: 3.2% of 100 L = 3.2 kg. Cream removal removes 2.7*0.05? Assume cream has 2% protein? Actually cream has little protein. So protein remains ~3.2 kg in 97.3 L = 3.29%. Add 0.69 kg protein gives 3.89 kg in 97.3+1.97=99.27 L? Volume change small. Protein% = 3.89/99.27*100 = 3.92%. Target 4%, so add 0.08 kg protein. Use WPC: 0.08/0.70 = 0.114 kg WPC. That adds solids: 0.114*0.95 = 0.108 kg solids. Total solids now 11.676+0.108=11.784 kg in 99.38 L = 11.86%, close to 12%. Adjust if needed.

Step 3: Mix all. Add SMP and WPC to milk at 45Â°C, stir well, hydrate 20 min. Then pasteurize, homogenize, cool, inoculate. Always weigh ingredients accurately. Use a scale. Keep records. This ensures consistent yogurt.
