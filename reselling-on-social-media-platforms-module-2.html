Lessons

1. Finding Wholesale and Liquidation Suppliers

To make money reselling, you need a reliable source of cheap products. Wholesale suppliers sell in bulk at lower prices, while liquidation suppliers sell off excess stock, customer returns, or bankrupt inventory at even deeper discounts. In Africa, start by visiting local wholesale markets like Balogun Market in Lagos, Kantamanto in Accra, or Toi Market in Nairobi. Walk the aisles, ask traders where they get their goods, and note prices. Many will source from importers who bring in containers from China, Dubai, or Turkey. Ask if you can buy a mixed pallet or a bale of assorted items. For example, a bale of 50 mixed-brand t-shirts might cost 500 Ghana cedis and resell for 20-30 cedis each, giving you 500-1000 cedis profit. Online, try Alibaba, but beware of scams; use Trade Assurance. Also check local Facebook groups like 'Lagos Wholesale and Liquidation' where sellers post clearance lots. For liquidation, look for stores closing down, or contact companies that sell off returned electronics. Always ask for a sample before committing to a large order. Build relationships: a good supplier will give you credit after a few cash purchases. Start small with 200-500 cedis, test what sells, then scale. Remember, your profit margin depends on buying low. A 30% margin is common; aim for 50% or more on trending items.

2. Thrift Store and Garage Sale Sourcing Strategies

Thrift stores (called 'bend-down' or 'second-hand' markets in many African countries) and garage sales are goldmines for resellers. In Kenya, Gikomba Market is famous for second-hand clothes; in Nigeria, Yaba and Oshodi have thrift stalls. Go early in the morning when new bales are opened. The best items are grabbed within the first hour. Bring cash and a large bag. Focus on quality brands: Zara, H&M, Nike, and Levi's sell fast on social media. Check for stains, tears, or missing buttons; minor flaws can be fixed and still profitable. A typical bale of 100 mixed clothes might cost 300-500 Kenyan shillings per piece, but you can sell individual pieces for 500-1500 shillings. For garage sales, scan local Facebook Marketplace, WhatsApp groups, and community notice boards. Estate sales often have household items, books, and electronics at low prices. Negotiate: offer to buy multiple items for a discount. For example, if a seller wants 2000 naira for a used blender, offer 1500 naira cash. Also, attend church jumble sales and school fairs. Always test electronics before buying. Clean and repair items before reselling; a little polish can double the price. Keep a notebook of what you paid and what you sold it for, so you know which thrift sources are most profitable.

3. Using Online Marketplaces for Sourcing

Online marketplaces let you source products without leaving your home. In Africa, popular platforms include Jumia (Nigeria, Kenya, Egypt), Kilimall (Kenya), and Takealot (South Africa). But for resellers, the best deals often come from Facebook Marketplace, Facebook groups, and WhatsApp Business catalogs. Join groups like 'Wholesale Suppliers Nigeria' or 'Nairobi Buy and Sell'. Post a 'wanted' ad: 'Looking for bulk phone cases, 100 pieces, budget 20,000 naira.' You'll get responses from suppliers. For international sourcing, use Alibaba, AliExpress, or DHgate. Always check seller ratings and reviews. Start with a small trial order of 10-20 units to test quality and shipping time. Shipping to Africa can take 2-6 weeks and incur customs duties; factor that into your cost. For example, a phone accessory that costs $1 on AliExpress might land at $2 after shipping and duty, and you can sell it for $5 locally. Also, use local classifieds like OLX (now Jiji in Nigeria) to find clearance lots. When buying online, use secure payment methods like PayPal or escrow. Never send money via Western Union to a stranger. Meet local sellers in person if possible, and inspect goods before paying. Keep records of all transactions for tax and profit tracking.

4. Negotiating Bulk Purchases

Negotiation is a skill that directly increases your profit. Before you negotiate, know the market price. Research online and visit multiple suppliers. If you're buying 50 units, ask for a 20-30% discount from the single-unit price. For example, if a supplier quotes 1000 naira per unit, offer 700 naira per unit for 50 units. Be polite but firm. Use phrases like 'I'm a regular buyer' or 'I can pay cash today.' Cash is king; many suppliers will lower the price for immediate payment. If they won't budge on price, ask for free delivery or extra items. Another tactic: buy in larger quantities. A supplier might give 40% off if you buy 200 units instead of 50. But don't overbuy if you can't sell that much. Start with a small order to build trust, then negotiate better terms on the next order. Also, consider group buying: team up with other resellers to pool money and buy a whole pallet at a lower price. Always get the agreement in writing via WhatsApp or email, including the price, quantity, and delivery date. If a supplier refuses to negotiate, walk away; there are many others. Remember, your goal is to get the lowest cost per unit without sacrificing quality.

5. Evaluating Product Quality and Profit Potential

Not every cheap product is a good deal. Before you buy in bulk, evaluate quality and profit potential. For quality, check materials, stitching, and functionality. For clothes, look at fabric thickness, seams, and zippers. For electronics, test charging, buttons, and screen. Ask for a sample if buying online. A common mistake is buying a large lot of low-quality items that won't sell. Instead, test a small batch first. For profit potential, calculate your total cost: purchase price + shipping + customs + packaging + social media ads. Then research the selling price on Facebook, Instagram, or Jumia. A good rule: your selling price should be at least double your total cost. For example, if a handbag costs 2000 naira landed, sell for 4000-5000 naira. Check demand: search hashtags like #handbagnigeria to see what's popular. Also consider seasonality: umbrellas sell in rainy season, Christmas decorations in December. Use a simple spreadsheet to track cost, price, and profit margin for each product. If an item doesn't sell within 2 weeks, discount it to recover cash. Focus on products with high turnover and repeat customers, like phone accessories, hair, or skincare. Avoid fads that die quickly.

6. Managing Inventory and Storage

Once you start buying in bulk, you need to manage inventory and storage to avoid losses. First, keep a record of every item: date bought, cost, quantity, and location. Use a notebook or a free app like Google Sheets or Zoho Inventory. Label boxes with purchase date and product name. Store items in a dry, clean space away from sunlight and pests. For clothes, use plastic bins or hang them to avoid wrinkles. For electronics, keep them in anti-static bags. If you don't have a spare room, use a locked trunk or rent a small storage unit (in Lagos, a 10x10 ft unit might cost 20,000 naira per month). But for most beginners, a corner of your bedroom works. Rotate stock: sell older items first (FIFO). Set a reorder point: when stock drops to 10 units, reorder. Track sales daily to know what's moving. If an item hasn't sold in 30 days, bundle it with a popular item or run a flash sale. Also, insure high-value inventory if possible. Keep your storage organized so you can find items quickly when an order comes in. A messy inventory leads to lost sales and wasted money.

<p>Social commerce across Africa is growing fast. According to GSMA's <em>Mobile Economy Sub-Saharan Africa 2024</em>, smartphone adoption in Sub-Saharan Africa reached 51% in 2023 and is projected to rise to 87% by 2030, which means your customer base is expanding. But competition is also rising. The resellers who win are those who source reliably, price intelligently, and manage stock without tying up cash unnecessarily.</p>

<h3>1. Finding Wholesale and Liquidation Suppliers</h3>

<p>Wholesale suppliers sell in bulk at reduced unit prices. Liquidation suppliers sell surplus, overstock, or returned goods, often at deep discounts but with less consistency. In Africa, key wholesale hubs include Balogun Market (Lagos), Kantamanto Market (Accra), and Gikomba Market (Nairobi). For liquidation, platforms like Liquidation.com and B-Stock serve international buyers, while regional options are emerging through Jumia and Kilimall business accounts.</p>

<p>When evaluating a wholesale supplier, ask for their minimum order quantity (MOQ), lead time, and whether they offer credit terms. A supplier who accepts 50% deposit and 50% on delivery is often more valuable to a cash-constrained reseller than one offering a 5% lower price but demanding full upfront payment.</p>

<h3>2. Thrift Store and Garage Sale Sourcing Strategies</h3>

<p>Second-hand clothing, books, and household items remain high-demand categories across African social commerce. Thrift sourcing works best when you specialize: pick one category (e.g., children's clothing, denim, or kitchenware) and become known for it. Visit markets early in the morning when the best bales are opened, and build relationships with the sorters who set aside quality items.</p>

<p>Garage sales and estate sales are less common in many African cities but are growing in expatriate and middle-class neighborhoods. Facebook Marketplace and local WhatsApp groups are increasingly used to announce these sales. Always inspect for stains, tears, missing buttons, and odors before buying. A quick test: if you would not gift it to a friend, do not resell it.</p>

<h3>3. Using Online Marketplaces for Sourcing</h3>

<p>Online marketplaces let you source without traveling. Alibaba, AliExpress, and DHgate are widely used by African resellers for electronics accessories, fashion, and beauty products. For local sourcing, Jumia, Kilimall, and Facebook Marketplace connect you with regional suppliers. When buying from overseas, always calculate the full landed cost: product price + shipping + customs duty + clearing fees + local transport.</p>

<p>Use supplier ratings, transaction history, and verified badges as first filters. Request product samples before placing large orders. A sample that costs $20 can save you $2,000 in unsellable stock.</p>

<h3>4. Negotiating Bulk Purchases</h3>

<p>Negotiation is a skill, not a confrontation. Before you negotiate, know your target price, your walk-away price, and your alternative suppliers. Volume is your main lever: if you can commit to a larger order, ask for a tiered discount. Payment terms are your second lever: ask for partial payment on delivery or a 7-day credit window.</p>

<p>In many African markets, relationship matters as much as price. Repeat business, referrals, and consistent payment history earn you better terms over time. Always confirm the agreed price, quantity, quality grade, and delivery date in writing, even if only via WhatsApp message.</p>

<h3>5. Evaluating Product Quality and Profit Potential</h3>

<p>Quality evaluation should be systematic. For each product, check: material durability, functionality (does it work?), packaging condition, and compliance with local standards. For electronics, test charging, connectivity, and battery life. For clothing, check stitching, fabric weight, and colorfastness.</p>

<p>Profit potential is not just selling price minus buying price. Use this formula: <strong>Net Profit = Selling Price (Buying Price + Shipping + Packaging + Platform Fees + Returns Allowance)</strong>. A product with a 40% gross margin can easily become a 10% net margin after returns and fees.</p>

<h3>6. Managing Inventory and Storage</h3>

<p>Dead stock kills resale businesses. Use a simple inventory system: track product name, cost price, selling price, quantity in stock, date received, and date last sold. A notebook works, but a free spreadsheet (Google Sheets) or an app like Zoho Inventory or Sortly is better once you exceed 50 SKUs.</p>

<p>Set a reorder point for your best sellers: when stock drops to a two-week supply, reorder. For slow movers, run a clearance promotion after 60 days. Store goods in a dry, secure place away from direct sunlight. In humid coastal cities like Lagos, Mombasa, and Dar es Salaam, use silica gel packets and raised shelving to prevent mold and rust.</p>

Never source electrical items, cosmetics, or children's products without verifying certification. If a deal looks too good to be true, it usually is. Protect your brand and your customers.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Calculate landed cost before buying.</strong> Include shipping, customs, packaging, and fees not just the unit price.</li>
<li><strong>Request samples for any new supplier or product category.</strong> Test quality before committing to bulk.</li>
<li><strong>Negotiate on volume and payment terms, not just price.</strong> A deposit-and-balance arrangement preserves cash flow.</li>
<li><strong>Confirm every order in writing.</strong> WhatsApp messages count as written records in most African jurisdictions.</li>
<li><strong>Set a reorder point for best sellers and a clearance date for slow movers.</strong> Do not let stock age beyond 6090 days.</li>
<li><strong>Verify compliance and authenticity for regulated categories.</strong> Check SONCAP, KEBS, GSA, or equivalent certification.</li>
<li><strong>Track net profit per SKU, not just revenue.</strong> A high-selling product with low margin can drain your business.</li>
<li><strong>Build supplier relationships for the long term.</strong> Consistent, reliable buyers get better prices and priority stock.</li>
</ol>
