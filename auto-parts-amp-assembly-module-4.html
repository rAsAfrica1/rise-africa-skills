1. Identifying Brake Pads, Shoes, and Discs for Popular Models

When a customer drives in with a Toyota Corolla, Nissan Hardbody, or Honda Civic, you need to know exactly which brake parts fit. Start by noting the vehicle's year, make, model, and engine size. For example, a 2014 Toyota Corolla uses front brake pads with part number 04465-02220, while a 2010 Nissan Hardbody uses 41060-2S625. Always verify by checking the VIN or the sticker on the driver's door jamb. In Africa, many vehicles are imported used, so parts often cross-reference between similar models. For instance, brake pads for a 2005-2010 Toyota Hilux also fit some Fortuner models. Keep a notebook with these numbersit saves time and avoids returns.

Brake pads are for disc brakes, found on front wheels of most modern cars and rear wheels of higher-end models. They consist of a metal backing plate with friction material. Discs (rotors) are the shiny metal discs the pads squeeze. Shoes are for drum brakes, typically on rear wheels of older or cheaper vehicles like the Toyota Corolla up to 2003. Shoes are curved metal pieces with friction lining. To identify visually: pads are flat and rectangular; shoes are curved and semi-circular. Discs are thick metal discs; drums are hollow metal cylinders that house the shoes.

Measure wear with a ruler or caliper. New brake pads are 10-12mm thick. Replace when friction material is below 3mm. New discs are 20-25mm thick; minimum thickness is stamped on the disc edge (e.g., 22mm). If below, replace. For shoes, new lining is 4-5mm; replace at 1.5mm. In dusty African conditions, wear is faster. Always check both sides of the vehicleuneven wear indicates a stuck caliper or wheel cylinder.

Sourcing parts: Local auto parts shops in Nairobi, Lagos, or Johannesburg often stock common models. Ask for OEM (original equipment manufacturer) or good aftermarket brands like Bosch, TRW, or Ferodo. Avoid cheap unknown brandsthey wear fast and can damage discs. If unsure, bring the old part to match. For rare models, order online from RockAuto or Amazon, but check shipping costs and customs. Always compare prices from at least three suppliers. Remember: correct identification prevents comebacks and keeps customers safe.

2. Replacing Brake Pads on a Toyota Corolla

Replacing front brake pads on a 2014 Toyota Corolla takes about 45 minutes with basic tools. You'll need: a jack, jack stands, lug wrench, 14mm and 17mm sockets, C-clamp or brake caliper piston tool, wire brush, brake cleaner, and new pads (part number 04465-02220). Park on flat ground, engage handbrake, and loosen front wheel lug nuts slightly before lifting. Jack up the car, secure with jack stands, and remove the wheel. Turn the steering wheel outward to access the caliper.

Remove the two 14mm bolts holding the caliper to the bracket. They are usually at the back of the caliper. Once removed, lift the caliper off the rotor and hang it with a wire or bungee corddo not let it hang by the brake hose. Remove the old pads from the bracket. They may be held by clips; note their orientation. Use a wire brush to clean the bracket and caliper of rust and dust. Spray brake cleaner on the rotor and wipe with a clean rag.

Now compress the caliper piston. Use a C-clamp or a dedicated tool, turning clockwise while pushing. Open the brake fluid reservoir cap firstfluid will rise. Compress until the piston is flush with the caliper body. Apply a thin layer of brake grease to the pad backing plates and the caliper bracket contact points. Install new pads with shims and clips. Reinstall the caliper and tighten the 14mm bolts to 25-30 Nm (18-22 ft-lb).

Pump the brake pedal several times until it feels firm. This seats the pads against the rotor. Check brake fluid level and top up with DOT 3 or DOT 4. Reinstall the wheel, lower the car, and tighten lug nuts to 103 Nm (76 ft-lb) in a star pattern. Test drive slowly, braking gently for the first 200 km to bed in the pads. In Africa, dusty roads mean you should clean and lubricate caliper slides every 10,000 km. If the brake pedal is spongy, bleed the brakes. Always dispose of old brake fluid properlyit's toxic.

3. Bleeding Brake Lines to Remove Air

Air in brake lines makes the pedal feel spongy and reduces braking power. Bleeding removes air and old fluid. You need: fresh brake fluid (DOT 3 or DOT 4), a clear plastic tube, a small bottle, an 8mm or 10mm wrench, and a helper. Start by checking the reservoirtop it up with fresh fluid. Never let it run dry, or you'll introduce more air. The bleeding sequence is: right rear, left rear, right front, left front (farthest to closest to master cylinder).

Jack up the car and remove all four wheels, or at least the one you're working on. Locate the bleeder screw on each brake caliper or wheel cylinderit's a small nipple behind the brake line. Clean it with a wire brush. Attach the clear tube to the bleeder screw and put the other end in a bottle with a little brake fluid to prevent air from being sucked back. Have your helper sit in the driver's seat.

Instruct your helper to pump the brake pedal three times and hold it down firmly. While they hold, open the bleeder screw with the wrench about half a turn. You'll see fluid and air bubbles flow through the tube. Close the screw before your helper releases the pedal. Repeat this processpump, hold, open, close, releaseuntil no air bubbles appear in the tube, only clear fluid. Check the reservoir every 5-6 bleeds and top up. Each wheel takes about 10-15 minutes.

After bleeding all four wheels, top up the reservoir to the MAX line. Pump the pedalit should feel firm and high. If still spongy, there may be air trapped in the master cylinder. You can bench bleed it or use a pressure bleeder. In Africa, where humidity is high, brake fluid absorbs moisture quickly. Replace fluid every two years or 40,000 km. Old fluid is dark and watery. Always use fresh fluid from a sealed container. Dispose of old fluid at a garage or recycling centernever pour it down the drain. Test drive carefully and recheck for leaks.

4. Inspecting and Replacing Brake Drums

Brake drums are used on rear wheels of many African workhorses like the Toyota Hilux, Nissan Hardbody, and older Corollas. They work with brake shoes that press outward against the drum's inner surface. To inspect, jack up the rear, remove the wheel, and remove the drum. On some models, you must first remove the axle nut or a retaining screw. If the drum is stuck, tap it with a rubber mallet or use a puller. Never pry it off with a screwdriveryou can damage the backing plate.

Once the drum is off, inspect the inside surface. It should be smooth and shiny. Deep grooves, scoring, or hot spots (blue spots) mean the drum needs resurfacing or replacement. Measure the inside diameter with a caliper. The maximum diameter is stamped on the drum (e.g., 230mm). If it exceeds this, replace it. New drums cost $30-$60 each. Also check the brake shoes: new lining is 4-5mm thick. Replace if below 1.5mm. Look for uneven wear, cracks, or oil contamination. If one shoe is more worn than the other, the wheel cylinder or adjuster may be faulty.

To replace shoes, first remove the return springs, hold-down springs, and adjuster. Note how they are assembledtake a photo. Use a brake spring tool to avoid injury. Clean the backing plate with brake cleaner and apply a thin layer of brake grease to the contact points. Install new shoes, springs, and adjuster. Adjust the shoes so the drum fits snugly but turns freely. On many African vehicles, you can adjust via a star wheel through a slot in the backing plate. Turn the star wheel until the drum locks, then back off 5-7 clicks.

Reinstall the drum and wheel. Pump the brake pedal to seat the shoes. Test drive and make several reverse stops to activate the self-adjuster. In dusty conditions, inspect drums every 20,000 km. If you hear grinding, replace immediately. Always use quality shoescheap ones wear fast and can crack. For parts, check local shops for Nissan or Toyota genuine or brands like Bosch. If drums are beyond resurfacing, replace in pairs.

5. Understanding ABS Sensors and Common Faults

ABS (Anti-lock Braking System) prevents wheel lock-up during hard braking. It uses wheel speed sensors at each wheel, an ABS control module, and a hydraulic pump. The sensors are usually magnetic or Hall-effect, mounted near the wheel hub or axle. They read a toothed ring (reluctor ring) that spins with the wheel. When a wheel stops turning while others still roll, the module releases brake pressure to that wheel to prevent skidding. In Africa, ABS faults are common due to dust, mud, and poor roads.

Common symptoms of a faulty ABS sensor: ABS warning light on dashboard, pulsating brake pedal, or brake pedal that feels normal but ABS doesn't engage. Sometimes the speedometer stops working if the sensor also feeds it. To diagnose, use an OBD-II scanner to read fault codes. Codes like C0035 (left front wheel speed sensor) point to the problem. If no scanner, you can test the sensor with a multimeter. Set to AC volts, spin the wheel by hand, and check for a small voltage (0.5-1.5V). No voltage means a bad sensor. Also inspect the sensor tip for metal shavings or dirtclean it with a rag.

Common faults: 1) Dirty sensorclean with brake cleaner. 2) Damaged reluctor ringrust or broken teeth; replace the ring or axle. 3) Broken wiringcheck for cuts or corrosion near the connector. 4) Failed sensorreplace with OEM or quality aftermarket. Sensors cost $20-$80. When replacing, ensure the new sensor is fully seated and the bolt tightened to 8-10 Nm. Do not hammer it in. After replacement, clear the codes and test drive. The ABS light should go off after a few seconds if the system is working.

In many African vehicles, ABS is often disabled because of sensor faults. Don't ignore itABS reduces stopping distance on wet or gravel roads. For older cars without ABS, you can retrofit a used system from a scrapyard, but it's complex. Better to maintain the existing system. Check sensors every 20,000 km, especially after driving through mud or water. Keep the connectors clean and dry. If the ABS light stays on, get it scanned. A faulty ABS can also affect traction control and hill assist. Always use the correct sensor for your modelToyota, Nissan, and Honda have different part numbers.

6. Sourcing Affordable Brake Fluid and Parts

Brake fluid is the lifeblood of your braking system. It must be fresh, clean, and the correct type. Most African vehicles use DOT 3 or DOT 4. DOT 5 is silicone-based and not compatible with ABS. Check the reservoir cap or owner's manual. Never mix DOT 3 and DOT 4? Actually, DOT 4 is backward compatible with DOT 3, but DOT 5 is not. For top-ups, use the same type. A 500ml bottle of DOT 4 costs $5-$10 at local shops. Buy from a reputable brand like Castrol, Bosch, or Total. Avoid unbranded fluid in unsealed containersit absorbs moisture and can boil, causing brake failure.

Brake fluid absorbs moisture from the air, especially in humid African climates. Replace every two years or 40,000 km. Test with a brake fluid tester (pen-type) that measures water content. If over 3%, replace. When bleeding, use only fresh fluid from a sealed container. Once opened, use within a few days or discard. Old fluid looks dark and watery. Store in a cool, dry place. In dusty areas, keep the reservoir cap tightly closed.

Sourcing brake parts affordably: Start with local auto parts shops in major cities like Nairobi's Kirinyaga Road, Lagos's Ladipo Market, or Johannesburg's Kempton Park. They often stock used (second-hand) parts from Japan or Europe. For brake pads and shoes, aftermarket brands like Bosch, TRW, and Ferodo offer good quality at lower prices than OEM. For discs and drums, consider resurfacing if thickness allowsit costs $10-$15 per disc versus $40-$80 new. For ABS sensors, scrapyards are a goldminetest before buying. Online, sites like Amazon, eBay, and RockAuto ship to Africa, but check shipping and customs. Compare prices: a set of front pads for a Corolla ranges from $20 (cheap) to $60 (premium).

Always negotiate. Buy in bulk if you run a garage. Join local mechanic WhatsApp groups for leads on suppliers. Inspect parts before paying: check for cracks, rust, or missing hardware. For brake fluid, buy from a shop that sells a lotstock rotates. Never use engine oil or transmission fluid as brake fluid. It will destroy seals. Keep a record of part numbers and suppliers. Train your customers to replace fluid every two years. Affordable doesn't mean cheapyour reputation depends on safety. A good brake job brings repeat customers.

<h2>Practical Reference: Brake System Parts and Assembly</h2>

<p>In the African aftermarket, brakes are the single most reliable revenue line in any auto workshop. Every vehicle on the road from a Nairobi matatu to a Kigali motorcycle taxi to a Lagos ride-hailing Corolla consumes brake pads, shoes, discs, drums, and fluid on a predictable cycle. For a Rise AFRICA learner running or joining a workshop, the brake bay is where you build cash flow: consumables sell every month, labour is repeatable, and customers understand the safety argument without being sold to. This reference lesson consolidates the six core lessons in this module into one working document you can print, laminate, and pin to the workshop wall.</p>

<p>Two market realities shape everything below. First, genuine OEM brake parts are often 24 the price of quality aftermarket equivalents, and for most African fleet and private customers the aftermarket tier is the correct commercial answer provided you buy from a supplier who can name the manufacturer and show a homologation or E-mark. Second, counterfeit and re-boxed pads are widespread in open-air markets; a workshop that installs them once will lose the customer permanently when the pads squeal, fade, or crack. Your reputation is the asset. Protect it with sourcing discipline.</p>

<h3>Lesson 1: Identifying Brake Pads, Shoes, and Discs for Popular Models</h3>
<p>Africa's vehicle parc is dominated by a small number of platforms: Toyota Corolla, Toyota Hilux, Nissan NP200, Toyota Hiace, Mazda BT-50, Isuzu D-Max, plus the ubiquitous Toyota Probox and Indian/Chinese light trucks. Learn the pad and disc part numbers for the top ten platforms in your city and you can serve 70% of walk-in traffic without a catalogue. Always identify by VIN, not by model year alone facelifts change caliper brackets. Measure disc thickness with a micrometer against the minimum thickness stamped on the disc hat; if you are within 1 mm of minimum, quote a replacement in the same job.</p>

<h3>Lesson 2: Replacing Brake Pads on a Toyota Corolla</h3>
<p>The Corolla is the single most common brake job in East and West Africa. Standard sequence: loosen wheel nuts, jack and support on stands, remove wheel, unbolt the caliper guide pins (usually 1214 mm), swing the caliper up, extract the old pads and anti-rattle shims, compress the piston with a C-clamp or piston tool, clean and grease the guide pins with high-temperature silicone grease, fit new pads with shims, refit caliper, torque guide pins to spec (typically 3040 Nm), pump the pedal before moving the vehicle, and road-test. Budget 4560 minutes per axle for a trained technician. Charge labour separately from parts and quote both African customers increasingly compare labour rates across workshops, so transparency wins repeat business.</p>

<h3>Lesson 3: Bleeding Brake Lines to Remove Air</h3>
<p>Air in the hydraulic circuit is the most common cause of a spongy pedal after a pad change or fluid top-up. Bleed in the correct order: start at the wheel furthest from the master cylinder (usually rear left), then rear right, front left, front right check your vehicle's manual, as ABS-equipped cars sometimes specify a different sequence. Use the two-person method (one pumps, one opens the nipple) or a vacuum/pressure bleeder if you have one. Never reuse fluid from the catch bottle. Keep the master cylinder reservoir topped up throughout letting it run dry pulls air back into the ABS unit and turns a 20-minute job into a dealer-level diagnostic session.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #c0392b;background:#fdecea;padding:14px 18px;border-radius:6px;margin:18px 0">
<strong style="color:#c0392b"> SAFETY WARNING BRAKE FLUID AND HYDRAULIC PRESSURE</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0">Brake fluid (DOT 3, DOT 4, DOT 5.1) is hygroscopic, corrosive to paint, and toxic. Wear nitrile gloves and eye protection. Never siphon brake fluid by mouth. Do not mix DOT 5 (silicone) with DOT 3/4/5.1 the result is seal failure and total brake loss. Never open a bleed nipple while the system is under pressure from a running engine or a pressurised bleeder without eye protection. After any hydraulic work, pump the pedal to full firmness and check for leaks at every joint before the vehicle moves. A vehicle that leaves your bay with a spongy pedal is a fatality waiting to happen and a criminal liability for the workshop owner.</p>
</div>

<h3>Lesson 4: Inspecting and Replacing Brake Drums</h3>
<p>Drum brakes remain common on the rear of pickups, minibuses, and older sedans. Inspect for: shoe lining thickness (replace below 1.5 mm above rivet or bonded minimum), drum scoring, out-of-round (measure inside diameter at three points with a drum gauge), wheel cylinder leaks, and seized adjusters. Drums can often be machined if within the maximum rebore stamped on the drum; beyond that, replace. In dusty African conditions, seized adjusters and glazed shoes are more common than worn-out linings clean, lubricate the adjuster, and deglaze the shoes with emery cloth before condemning the assembly.</p>

<h3>Lesson 5: Understanding ABS Sensors and Common Faults</h3>
<p>ABS faults are the most misdiagnosed brake jobs in African workshops. The four common causes are: (1) a dirty or damaged wheel speed sensor or reluctor ring (dust, mud, or a mechanic's hammer); (2) a broken sensor harness at the wheel arch extremely common on Nairobi and Lagos roads; (3) a failing ABS pump motor or module; (4) low brake fluid or a blown ABS fuse. A basic OBD-II scanner with ABS capability (many sub-$150 units now read ABS codes) pays for itself in a month. Never guess-replace the ABS module it is the most expensive part in the system and the least likely to be the fault.</p>

<h3>Lesson 6: Sourcing Affordable Brake Fluid and Parts</h3>
<p>Brake fluid is a margin product: buy DOT 4 in 500 ml and 1 L sealed bottles from a supplier who can show batch dates, store it sealed and away from sunlight, and never sell a bottle that has been open for more than a few days. For pads and discs, build relationships with two or three importers who stock recognised aftermarket brands (e.g. Ferodo, TRW, Bosch, Blue Print, ATE) and can supply consistently. Ask for the manufacturer's name, country of origin, and any E-mark or equivalent certification. Avoid unbranded pads sold by the sack the friction material is the one place where cheap genuinely means dangerous. For fleet customers, negotiate a quarterly consumables contract: fixed price per axle set, guaranteed stock, monthly invoicing. That contract is the difference between a workshop and a business.</p>
