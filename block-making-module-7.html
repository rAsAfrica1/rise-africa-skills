Breeze Block (Clinker/Ash)

8 lessons · 30 quiz questions · Easy difficulty

1 History of breeze blocks

"Breeze block" is a British term from the 1920s. Originally, they were made from breeze — the ash and clinker left over from burning coal in power stations or domestic fires.

After World War II, millions of British homes were built from breeze blocks. They were cheap, light, and used a waste product that would otherwise go to landfill. Many are still standing today.

In Africa, breeze blocks are common in older buildings (Zimbabwe, South Africa, Ghana) and are still made where there's a source of furnace ash or clinker — power stations, industrial kilns, large boilers.

💡 Breeze blocks are a sustainability product: they take industrial waste and turn it into construction material.

2 Where to source furnace ash

Furnace ash sources in Africa:

  • Coal-fired power stations — biggest source. Hwange (Zimbabwe), Medupi/Kusile (South Africa), Kipevu (Kenya).
  • Industrial boilers — factories burning coal for steam. Common in sugar, paper, textile, cement industries.
  • Brick kilns — clinker from fired clay brick plants.
  • Rail yards — historic clinker beds from old steam locomotives.

Two types of ash:

  • Bottom ash / clinker — heavier, sinks to bottom of furnace. What breeze blocks traditionally used.
  • Fly ash — fine powder carried up with flue gases. Used in Module 8 (fly ash block).

Get written permission from the source. Many power stations give away or sell cheaply for a small handling fee. Test for heavy metals (lead, cadmium, mercury) before use.

3 Ash screening and prep

Raw ash and clinker need preparation before mixing.

  1. Dry the ash — spread in sun for 2–3 days if wet.
  2. Break up large clinker — clinker comes in chunks up to 100 mm. Crush to under 20 mm.
  3. Screen out large pieces — a 20 mm sieve removes oversize.
  4. Screen out very fine dust — some ash is powdery and reduces block strength. A 1 mm sieve below the coarse screen separates the useful grain.
  5. Test ash for unburnt carbon — high carbon content (black color) weakens the block. Look for grey-brown ash.

Ash prep is labour-intensive. Budget 1 worker per 100 blocks produced.

4 Mix ratios with cement

Traditional breeze blocks used lime. Modern ones use cement. Standard mix by volume:

  • Cement: 1 part
  • Sharp sand: 2 parts
  • Screened ash/clinker: 4 parts

This gives a breeze block density of 900–1,300 kg/m³, similar to lightweight aggregate blocks.

Optional: replace 20% of the cement with hydrated lime. Slow-setting but gives a smoother finish.

Cement use: 1 bag (50 kg) makes 30–40 breeze blocks of 440 × 215 × 100 mm.

⚠ Testing is essential. Different ash sources give different strengths. Make 10 trial blocks from each source before committing.

5 Mould filling with ash mix

Breeze block mix handles similar to lightweight aggregate mix. Same rules:

  1. Fill in two layers.
  2. Compact the first layer — ash compacts less than sand.
  3. Vibrate or tamp — 4–6 seconds on a machine, or 20 firm taps by hand.
  4. Level the top with a straight edge.

Watch for:

  • Honeycomb in corners — ash has lower flow than sand. Vibrate longer.
  • Cracking during demoulding — mix too dry. Add water slowly.
  • Block slumping — mix too wet. Add ash or sand.

6 Curing breeze blocks

Breeze blocks cure like other concrete blocks: 7 days damp, 28 days full strength.

Special considerations:

  • Slower initial set. Ash absorbs water and can delay setting. Leave blocks 36–48 hours before moving.
  • Colour tells the story. Grey blocks are setting well. Very dark blocks are drying too fast — increase spray.
  • Cold weather danger. Ash blocks are more frost-sensitive than dense blocks. Don't make below 5°C.
  • Rain protection. Ash absorbs water — keep covered.

Strength test: same drop test. Breeze blocks are typically 3–5 N/mm² at 28 days.

7 Limitations of breeze blocks

Breeze blocks are not for every use. Avoid them for:

  • Below-ground foundations — ash absorbs water and softens with constant moisture
  • High-load bearing walls — maximum 3–5 N/mm², lower than dense block (7+ N/mm²)
  • Coastal areas — salt air attacks ash blocks faster than dense concrete
  • Wet rooms — bathrooms, kitchens, laundry — unless rendered and sealed
  • Severe climates — heavy frost causes spalling

Where breeze blocks excel:

  • Interior partition walls
  • Non-structural external walls (rendered)
  • Farming buildings (not animal housing where urine is present)
  • Garden walls and landscaping
  • Insulation infill on framed buildings

8 Traditional market niches

Breeze blocks still have a market in Africa:

  • Historic restoration — rebuilding old breeze-block walls needs matching blocks
  • Eco-construction — using industrial waste appeals to green builders
  • Cost-sensitive markets — where ash is free or cheap, breeze blocks are cheaper than dense
  • Rural construction — near power stations and industrial kilns, breeze blocks undercut everything else
  • Insulation walls — ash block has better insulation than dense concrete

Sales pitch: recycled content + insulation + lower cost. Target builders looking for affordable, insulated walls in areas near an ash source.

💰 Breeze blocks are a niche product. Best when you have free or cheap ash nearby. Otherwise dense or hollow concrete is more competitive.
