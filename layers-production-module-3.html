1. Why Pullet Rearing Determines Your Layer Business Profit

Every egg you sell for the next 72 to 90 weeks is decided in the first 18 weeks of a pullet's life. This is not a slogan; it is the hard economic reality of layer farming in Africa. A pullet that enters the laying house underweight, with a poor frame, weak immunity or an underdeveloped digestive tract will never reach her genetic potential. She will lay smaller eggs, fewer eggs, and she will die earlier. In a business where feed alone is 65 to 75 percent of total cost, a flock that underperforms by even 5 percent can wipe out your entire profit margin. So Module 3 is not just about keeping birds alive. It is about building a productive asset. Think of pullet rearing as manufacturing. You are manufacturing a laying machine. The raw materials are day-old chicks, feed, water, vaccines, labour and housing. The finished product is a uniform flock of 18-week-old pullets weighing between 1.35 and 1.55 kilograms depending on breed, with a well-developed skeleton, a strong immune system, and a digestive tract ready for the stress of transfer and the onset of lay. If your manufacturing process is sloppy, the finished product is defective, and no amount of good management in the layer house can fully repair it. In Africa, pullet rearing carries specific challenges that European or American manuals often ignore. Day-old chicks may travel long distances in unreliable vehicles, arriving dehydrated and chilled. Feed ingredients vary in quality from one mill to another, and maize or soybean meal may be contaminated with aflatoxins. Power supply is unstable, so brooding that depends on electricity can fail. Heat stress is a constant threat in lowland and coastal regions. Disease pressure is high, especially Newcastle disease, Gumboro, fowl pox and coccidiosis. And market prices for eggs fluctuate, so your cost of producing a pullet must be controlled tightly. The good news is that African farmers who follow basic principles consistently can achieve 95 percent livability and uniform body weights. The principles are simple: start with quality chicks, provide correct brooding temperature, give clean water and balanced feed, follow a strict vaccination programme, control coccidiosis, monitor body weight weekly, and manage the transition to the laying house carefully. Each of these is covered in the five lessons of this module. Lesson 1 covers brooding from day one to week four. Lesson 2 covers weeks five to eight, the rapid growth phase. Lesson 3 covers weeks nine to twelve, when the skeleton and frame develop. Lesson 4 covers weeks thirteen to eighteen, when the reproductive system matures and the bird prepares for lay. Lesson 5 covers health, vaccination and biosecurity across the whole 18 weeks. Before we go into details, let us set the business targets. By 18 weeks, you want at least 95 percent of the chicks you started with still alive. You want a flock uniformity of at least 80 percent, meaning 80 percent of birds fall within plus or minus 10 percent of the average body weight. You want an average body weight that matches the breed standard, usually 1.4 to 1.5 kilograms for brown layers and 1.3 to 1.4 kilograms for white layers. You want a feed conversion ratio of about 5 to 6 kilograms of feed per kilogram of body weight gain, though this varies. You want the pullet to have received all required vaccinations and to be free of internal and external parasites. You want the pullet to be trained to drink from nipple lines or troughs and to be comfortable with human handling. And you want the cost per pullet at 18 weeks to be known to the nearest cent, because that cost becomes part of your egg production cost. Many farmers fail because they do not weigh birds. They look at the flock and say the birds look fine. But looks deceive. A bird that is 100 grams underweight at 18 weeks will lay eggs that are 3 to 5 grams lighter for the first 10 weeks of lay, and she will peak later and lower. In a flock of 1,000 birds, that can mean 50 to 100 fewer eggs per day at peak, which at a farm gate price of 0.15 US dollars per egg is 7.50 to 15 US dollars lost every day. Over a 10-week period, that is 525 to 1,050 US dollars. That is why weighing scales and record sheets are not optional. They are your profit protection tools. Another common failure is poor brooding. Chicks that are chilled in the first week develop weak hearts, poor immunity and uneven growth. Chicks that are overheated become dehydrated, eat less and develop respiratory problems. In Africa, many farmers brood with charcoal stoves or kerosene lamps because electricity is unreliable. This works if managed well, but it requires constant attention. You must check the behaviour of the chicks, not just the thermometer. If chicks are huddled and chirping loudly, they are cold. If they are spread out and panting, they are hot. If they are evenly spread and quiet, they are comfortable. This behavioural observation is more reliable than any gadget. Feed quality is another critical point. Pullet feed must be fresh, free of mould, and correctly formulated. In many African markets, commercial pullet mash is available, but it may be expensive or inconsistent. If you mix your own feed, you must use a proven formula and test your ingredients for aflatoxin. Aflatoxin B1 above 20 parts per billion will damage the liver, reduce growth and suppress immunity. Maize and groundnut cake are the usual culprits. Store feed in a dry, ventilated place on pallets, never on the floor. Use feed within four weeks of milling, especially in humid conditions. Water is the most important nutrient. A pullet drinks about 1.5 to 2 times her feed intake by weight. If water is restricted, feed intake drops immediately. Clean water means no visible dirt, no smell, and a bacterial count below 100 colony forming units per millilitre. In many farms, water tanks are never cleaned. Algae and biofilm build up, and birds get diarrhoea and poor growth. Clean and disinfect water tanks every two weeks. Use chlorine at 2 to 3 parts per million if water quality is doubtful. Vaccination is non-negotiable. Newcastle disease and Gumboro can wipe out an entire flock in days. Your vaccination programme must be designed for your local disease pressure and must be followed exactly. Do not skip boosters. Do not use expired vaccines. Do not vaccinate sick birds. Keep a cold chain from the supplier to the farm. In hot African conditions, a vaccine that is not kept cold is useless. Finally, remember that pullet rearing is a team effort. Train your workers to observe birds, record data, and report problems early. A worker who notices that feed intake dropped by 10 percent today can save you thousands of dollars by triggering an immediate investigation. A worker who ignores it may find dead birds tomorrow. This module will give you the knowledge to set up systems that make success repeatable. Let us begin with Lesson 1 on brooding.

2. Brooding and Early Care: Weeks 0 to 4

The first four weeks of a pullet's life are the most fragile and the most important. During this period, the chick transitions from relying on the yolk sac for nutrition to eating and drinking on its own, its body temperature regulation system matures, its immune system begins to respond to vaccines, and its skeleton and digestive organs grow rapidly. A mistake in week one can reduce the flock's performance for the next 80 weeks. So brooding is not just about keeping chicks warm. It is about creating an environment where chicks can thrive with minimal stress. Let us start with preparation before the chicks arrive. The brooder house must be cleaned, washed and disinfected at least three days before arrival. Remove all old litter, dust, cobwebs and feed residues. Wash walls, floor and ceiling with detergent, then apply a disinfectant such as glutaraldehyde or a strong chlorine solution. Allow to dry completely. Then place fresh, dry, dust-free litter such as wood shavings, rice husks or chopped straw to a depth of 5 to 8 centimetres. Do not use sawdust from treated wood or mouldy straw. Set up the brooder. If you use a charcoal stove, place it in a metal container and surround it with a wire mesh guard so chicks cannot burn themselves. If you use a gas brooder or electric bulb, check that it works and that you have a backup. In Africa, power cuts are common, so always have a charcoal or kerosene backup. Create a brooding ring using cardboard or metal sheets about 40 to 50 centimetres high, forming a circle of 3 to 4 metres diameter for 250 chicks. This prevents chicks from wandering away from heat and feed. Place feed in flat trays or chick feeders and water in shallow drinkers. Add vitamins and electrolytes to the water for the first three days to help chicks recover from transport stress. Also add glucose at 5 percent for the first 24 hours if chicks appear weak. Now the chicks arrive. The ideal day-old chick is active, has bright eyes, a dry fluffy down, a small yolk sac that is fully absorbed, and no deformities. Count the chicks as you place them. Record the number. Place them gently under the brooder. The temperature under the brooder should be 32 to 35 degrees Celsius in week one, then reduce by 2 to 3 degrees each week until it reaches 20 to 22 degrees Celsius by week four or five. But do not rely on the thermometer alone. Watch the chicks. If they are huddled directly under the heat source and chirping loudly, they are cold. Increase heat. If they are spread far from the heat source and panting with wings out, they are hot. Reduce heat. If they are evenly distributed around the brooder, eating and drinking quietly, the temperature is correct. This behavioural check is the most reliable guide. Humidity should be 60 to 70 percent in week one, then 50 to 60 percent later. In dry African conditions, you may need to hang wet sacks or use a humidifier. In humid coastal areas, you may need to increase ventilation to prevent damp litter. Ventilation is critical but must not create drafts. Chicks need fresh air to prevent respiratory disease and to remove ammonia. Ammonia should never exceed 20 parts per million. If you smell ammonia strongly, you need more ventilation or to remove wet litter. Light is important. In week one, provide 22 to 23 hours of light so chicks can find feed and water. Use a dim light, about 20 to 30 lux, which is enough to see but not so bright that it causes stress. From week two, reduce to 20 hours, then gradually reduce to natural daylight by week four or five, depending on your breed and production system. If you are in a dark house, you may need to provide artificial light later to control sexual maturity, but that is covered in Lesson 4. Feed and water management in the first four weeks is about encouraging intake. Chicks have small crops and eat little and often. Use shallow feeders and drinkers. Provide feed in crumb or mash form. A good quality chick starter should contain 20 to 22 percent crude protein, 2,800 to 2,900 kilocalories per kilogram metabolisable energy, and adequate vitamins and minerals. In Africa, many farmers use commercial chick mash, which is fine if fresh. If you mix your own, use a formula such as 55 percent maize, 30 percent soybean meal, 10 percent fishmeal, 3 percent limestone, 1 percent bone meal, 0.5 percent salt, and 0.5 percent premix. But always test your ingredients for aflatoxin. Never feed mouldy maize. Feed intake should be about 10 to 12 grams per chick per day in week one, rising to 25 to 30 grams by week four. Water intake is about 1.5 to 2 times feed intake. Clean drinkers twice daily. Provide one drinker per 50 chicks and one feeder per 40 chicks, and increase as birds grow. Vaccination starts early. In most African countries, the first Newcastle disease vaccine is given at day 7 to 10, often as a spray or eye drop. Gumboro vaccine is given at day 10 to 14, depending on the local challenge. Follow your local veterinary advice. Always use a cold chain. Do not vaccinate sick birds. Record the vaccine name, batch number, date and method. Health problems in the first four weeks include omphalitis or yolk sac infection, which causes weak chicks and death in the first week. This comes from dirty hatchery or brooder conditions. Prevent it by good hygiene. Coccidiosis usually appears from week three onwards, causing bloody droppings and poor growth. Use a coccidiostat in feed or water as directed. Do not use the same coccidiostat continuously; rotate to prevent resistance. Respiratory disease can occur if ventilation is poor or ammonia is high. Watch for sneezing, coughing and nasal discharge. Improve ventilation and consult a vet. Starve-out is a condition where chicks do not find feed and water. It is common when chicks are placed on slippery paper or when drinkers are too deep. Use rough paper or trays for the first three days, and use shallow drinkers with marbles or stones to prevent drowning. By the end of week four, your pullets should weigh about 250 to 300 grams, depending on breed. They should be uniform, active, and have a good appetite. You should have a mortality rate below 2 percent. If mortality is higher, investigate immediately. Common causes are poor brooding temperature, dirty water, overcrowding, or disease. Overcrowding is a major issue. Provide at least 10 chicks per square metre in the brooder, and reduce density as they grow. By week four, you should have no more than 15 to 20 birds per square metre. In Africa, many farmers try to raise too many birds in a small space to save on housing. This is false economy. Overcrowding causes stress, poor growth, feather pecking, and disease. It also makes it harder to reach feed and water. Always give birds space. Another key point is record keeping. Keep a daily record of temperature, humidity, mortality, feed intake, water intake, and any medications or vaccines. This data helps you spot problems early and make decisions. For example, if feed intake drops by 10 percent for two days, you know something is wrong. You can then check water quality, temperature, or disease. Without records, you are guessing. Finally, train your workers. They should know how to check chick behaviour, clean drinkers, adjust brooder temperature, and report any abnormality. A well-trained worker is your best insurance. In summary, weeks 0 to 4 are about creating a comfortable, clean, well-ventilated environment with easy access to feed and water, and starting the vaccination programme. Get this right, and you have a strong foundation for the next 14 weeks.

3. Weeks 5 to 8: Rapid Growth and Frame Development

From week five to week eight, your pullets enter a phase of rapid growth. They will roughly double their body weight during this period. The skeleton lengthens and strengthens, the digestive tract expands, and the immune system continues to mature. This is also the time when you transition from chick starter to grower feed, and when you must begin to manage body weight and uniformity seriously. If you get this phase right, your pullets will have the frame to support high egg production later. If you get it wrong, you will have small, weak birds that never catch up. Let us start with housing and space. By week five, your birds no longer need supplemental heat in most African climates, unless you are in a highland area with cold nights. They can be moved to grower houses or given more space in the brooder house. The recommended space allowance is 10 to 12 birds per square metre from week five to eight, and then 8 to 10 birds per square metre from week nine onwards. In hot climates, give more space to reduce heat stress. Ensure the house has good ventilation but no drafts. If you are using deep litter, keep it dry and friable. Wet litter causes ammonia, which damages the respiratory tract and predisposes birds to disease. Stir the litter regularly and add fresh shavings if needed. If you use cages or slatted floors, ensure the manure does not accumulate under the birds. Feeders and drinkers must be adjusted as birds grow. Raise them so the feed level is at the height of the birds' backs. This reduces spillage and contamination. Provide one feeder per 25 birds and one drinker per 50 birds. In hot weather, birds drink more, so provide extra drinkers. Water quality is still critical. Clean drinkers daily. If you use nipple lines, check that all nipples work and that water pressure is correct. Now feed. From week five, transition from chick starter to grower feed. Do this gradually over three to four days by mixing starter and grower. A good grower feed should contain 16 to 18 percent crude protein and 2,700 to 2,800 kilocalories per kilogram metabolisable energy. It should have adequate calcium and phosphorus for bone development, usually 0.9 to 1.0 percent calcium and 0.45 to 0.5 percent available phosphorus. In Africa, commercial grower mash is widely available. If you mix your own, use a formula such as 60 percent maize, 25 percent soybean meal, 8 percent wheat bran, 4 percent fishmeal, 2 percent limestone, 0.5 percent bone meal, 0.3 percent salt, and 0.2 percent premix. But again, test for aflatoxin. Feed intake will increase from about 30 grams per bird per day at week five to 50 to 60 grams by week eight. Do not restrict feed during this phase unless your breed guide specifically says so. The goal is to build frame and muscle. However, you must monitor body weight. Weigh a sample of birds every week. Use a digital scale and weigh at least 5 percent of the flock, or a minimum of 20 birds. Weigh them at the same time each week, preferably before feeding. Record the average weight and compare it to the breed standard. If your birds are underweight, increase feed quantity or improve feed quality. If they are overweight, do not reduce feed drastically; instead, increase fibre slightly or reduce energy density. But in most African smallholder farms, underweight is more common than overweight. Uniformity is the percentage of birds within plus or minus 10 percent of the average weight. You want at least 80 percent uniformity. If uniformity is low, it means some birds are getting more feed than others. This can happen if feeder space is inadequate, if feeders are not evenly distributed, or if there are sick birds. Sort out the cause. You can also grade birds into light, medium and heavy groups and feed them separately for a few weeks. This is extra work but pays off. Vaccination continues. In weeks five to eight, you may need boosters for Newcastle disease and Gumboro, depending on your programme. Fowl pox vaccine is often given at week six to eight in areas where the disease is common. Use the correct method: wing web stab for fowl pox, eye drop or drinking water for Newcastle, and eye drop or drinking water for Gumboro. Always follow the manufacturer's instructions. Do not vaccinate birds that are sick or stressed. If you are using drinking water vaccination, withhold water for one to two hours before vaccination so birds are thirsty, then mix the vaccine in the correct volume of water and ensure all birds drink within one to two hours. Use skim milk powder or a vaccine stabiliser to protect the virus. Health management in weeks five to eight focuses on coccidiosis and worms. Coccidiosis is a major threat in deep litter systems. It causes bloody droppings, ruffled feathers, and poor growth. Use a coccidiostat in feed or water. Rotate between different coccidiostats every few months to prevent resistance. If you see clinical signs, treat immediately with amprolium or toltrazuril. Internal parasites such as roundworms and tapeworms can also affect growth. Deworm at week six to eight if your vet recommends it. Use a broad-spectrum dewormer such as levamisole or albendazole. Follow the withdrawal period before slaughter or egg production. External parasites like mites and lice can cause irritation and anaemia. Check birds regularly, especially under the wings and around the vent. Treat with approved acaricides or dusting powder. Keep the house clean and avoid overcrowding. Heat stress is a real concern in many African regions. From week five, birds are more feathered and less able to lose heat. Provide shade, increase ventilation, and ensure cool water. You can add electrolytes to water during heat waves. Avoid handling birds during the hottest part of the day. If you are in a hot climate, consider raising birds in open-sided houses with wire mesh walls and a solid roof. In summary, weeks 5 to 8 are about growth and frame development. Provide good feed, clean water, adequate space, and a strict vaccination and parasite control programme. Weigh birds weekly and manage uniformity. By the end of week eight, your pullets should weigh about 600 to 700 grams, depending on breed. They should be active, with good feather cover and bright eyes. Mortality should be below 3 percent cumulative. If you achieve these targets, you are on track for a successful laying flock.

4. Weeks 9 to 12: Skeleton, Uniformity and Preparing for Puberty

Weeks nine to twelve are a transition period. The pullet is no longer a chick, but she is not yet a young layer. Her growth rate slows slightly compared to weeks five to eight, but her skeleton continues to develop, and her reproductive system begins to prepare for puberty. This is the time when you must focus on body weight, uniformity, and the correct balance of nutrients to support both frame and future egg production. Mistakes here can lead to delayed sexual maturity, small eggs, or prolapse later. Let us start with housing and environment. By week nine, your pullets should be in their final grower house or in the laying house if you use a single-age system. Space allowance should be 8 to 10 birds per square metre in deep litter, or 6 to 8 birds per square metre in hot climates. If you use cages, follow the manufacturer's recommendations, usually 3 to 4 birds per cage depending on cage size. Ventilation is critical. Ammonia levels should be below 20 parts per million. If you smell ammonia, increase ventilation or remove wet litter. In hot climates, use fans or open-sided houses. In cold highland areas, avoid drafts but maintain air exchange. Light management becomes important in this phase. If you are raising pullets for commercial egg production, you need to control light to delay sexual maturity until the bird has sufficient body weight. In open-sided houses, natural daylight is usually sufficient, but if you are in a controlled-environment house, you should provide 10 to 12 hours of light per day during weeks 9 to 12. Do not increase light before week 16, as this can cause early lay and small eggs. If you are in an area with long natural daylight, you may need to use dark curtains or blackout shades to keep light hours constant. Feed management is the core of this phase. From week nine, transition from grower feed to developer or pullet feed. A good developer feed should contain 15 to 16 percent crude protein and 2,700 to 2,800 kilocalories per kilogram metabolisable energy. Calcium should be around 1.0 to 1.2 percent, and available phosphorus 0.4 to 0.45 percent. In Africa, some farmers continue with grower feed until week 12, then switch to developer. Either way, the key is to match feed intake to body weight goals. Feed intake will be about 60 to 70 grams per bird per day by week 12. Do not restrict feed unless your breed guide says so. However, you must monitor body weight weekly. Weigh at least 5 percent of the flock. Compare to the breed standard. If birds are underweight, increase feed quantity or improve feed quality. If they are overweight, you can slightly reduce energy or increase fibre, but never starve them. Uniformity is still critical. You want at least 80 percent of birds within plus or minus 10 percent of the average weight. If uniformity is poor, grade birds into light and heavy groups and feed them separately. This is extra work but it pays off in better egg production later. Skeleton development is a key focus. The pullet's skeleton is still growing, and she needs adequate calcium and phosphorus. However, too much calcium before week 16 can damage the kidneys and cause urolithiasis. So do not feed layer feed before week 16 or 17. The developer feed should have enough calcium for bone growth but not for eggshell formation. If you mix your own feed, use a formula such as 65 percent maize, 20 percent soybean meal, 10 percent wheat bran, 3 percent fishmeal, 1 percent limestone, 0.5 percent bone meal, 0.3 percent salt, and 0.2 percent premix. Adjust according to your ingredient analysis. Vaccination continues. In weeks 9 to 12, you may need a Newcastle disease booster, especially if you are in a high-risk area. Some programmes also give a second fowl pox vaccine if the first was given early. Follow your local veterinary advice. Always use a cold chain and record all vaccinations. Health management focuses on parasites and coccidiosis. Continue coccidiostat in feed if you are in a high-risk area. Deworm again if needed, especially if birds are on deep litter. Check for external parasites. Keep litter dry. Respiratory diseases can occur if ventilation is poor. Watch for coughing, sneezing, and nasal discharge. If you see these signs, improve ventilation and consult a vet. Do not use antibiotics indiscriminately. Use only under veterinary supervision and respect withdrawal periods. Heat stress is a major issue in weeks 9 to 12, especially in hot African regions. Birds have more feathers and are less able to lose heat. Provide shade, increase ventilation, and ensure cool water. Add electrolytes to water during heat waves. Avoid handling birds during the hottest part of the day. If you are in a very hot area, consider feeding during the cooler hours of the day, such as early morning and late evening. This improves feed intake. Body weight targets for weeks 9 to 12 vary by breed. For brown layers, aim for about 800 to 900 grams at week 9, 950 to 1,050 grams at week 10, 1,050 to 1,150 grams at week 11, and 1,150 to 1,250 grams at week 12. For white layers, aim for slightly lower weights. Always check your breed's specific guide. If your birds are below target, increase feed. If they are above target, do not reduce feed drastically; instead, maintain feed and monitor. Overweight pullets can have prolapse and other problems, but underweight pullets are more common and more costly. Record keeping is essential. Keep weekly records of body weight, feed intake, water intake, mortality, and any treatments. This data helps you make decisions. For example, if feed intake drops, you can check for disease, heat stress, or poor feed quality. If body weight is not increasing, you can check feed quality or parasite load. In summary, weeks 9 to 12 are about building a strong skeleton and maintaining uniformity. Provide a balanced developer feed, control light to prevent early lay, monitor body weight weekly, and continue vaccination and parasite control. By the end of week 12, your pullets should weigh about 1.15 to 1.25 kilograms, with good uniformity and a strong frame. They should be ready for the final phase of rearing, weeks 13 to 18, where they will prepare for lay.

5. Weeks 13 to 18: Preparing for Lay and Transfer

Weeks thirteen to eighteen are the final and most critical phase of pullet rearing. During this period, the pullet's reproductive system matures, her skeleton finishes growing, and she prepares to start laying eggs. Your management decisions now will determine how quickly she comes into lay, how large her eggs are, and how well she persists in production. This is also the time when you must transition from developer feed to pre-layer or layer feed, increase light stimulation, and prepare the laying house. Let us start with the timeline. Most commercial layer breeds start laying between 18 and 20 weeks of age, depending on breed, body weight, and lighting programme. Your goal is to have pullets at the correct body weight and with a well-developed frame by week 18, so they can start lay smoothly. If pullets are underweight at 18 weeks, they will start lay later, produce smaller eggs, and may have higher mortality. If they are overweight, they may have prolapse or egg peritonitis. So body weight management is still the top priority. Housing and space. By week 13, pullets should be in their final laying house or in a grower house that meets laying house specifications. Space allowance should be 6 to 8 birds per square metre in deep litter, or 4 to 6 birds per square metre in hot climates. If you use cages, follow the manufacturer's recommendations. Ensure the house is clean, disinfected, and free of drafts. Provide adequate feeders and drinkers. In the laying house, you may use chain feeders, pan feeders, or trough feeders. Provide one feeder per 20 to 25 birds and one drinker per 50 to 60 birds. If you use nipple drinkers, provide one nipple per 8 to 10 birds. Feed management is the most important factor in this phase. From week 13 to 16, continue with developer feed. From week 16 to 18, transition to pre-layer feed, which is a feed with higher calcium (2.0 to 2.5 percent) and similar protein to developer. Pre-layer feed helps the bird's medullary bone develop, which is a reserve of calcium for eggshell formation. Do not feed layer feed (3.5 to 4.0 percent calcium) before week 17 or 18, as too much calcium too early can damage the kidneys. However, if your pullets are underweight, delay the transition to pre-layer until they reach target weight. Feed intake will increase from about 70 grams per bird per day at week 13 to 90 to 100 grams by week 18. Do not restrict feed unless your breed guide says so. In fact, many breeds need a slight increase in feed during weeks 16 to 18 to support the growing reproductive system. Monitor body weight weekly. Weigh at least 5 percent of the flock. Compare to the breed standard. For brown layers, target weights are approximately 1.25 to 1.35 kilograms at week 13, 1.35 to 1.45 kilograms at week 14, 1.45 to 1.55 kilograms at week 15, 1.55 to 1.65 kilograms at week 16, 1.65 to 1.75 kilograms at week 17, and 1.75 to 1.85 kilograms at week 18. For white layers, target weights are slightly lower. Always check your breed's specific guide. If birds are underweight, increase feed quantity or improve feed quality. If they are overweight, do not reduce feed drastically; instead, maintain feed and monitor. Uniformity should still be at least 80 percent. If uniformity is poor, grade birds and feed separately. Light management is critical in weeks 13 to 18. Light stimulates the pituitary gland to release hormones that trigger egg production. In open-sided houses, natural daylight is usually sufficient, but if you are in a controlled-environment house, you should provide 12 hours of light per day from week 13 to 16, then increase by 30 minutes per week from week 17 until you reach 16 hours per day. Do not increase light before week 16, as this can cause early lay and small eggs. If you are in an area with long natural daylight, you may need to use dark curtains to keep light hours constant. Vaccination continues. In weeks 13 to 18, you may need a final Newcastle disease booster, often given at week 16 to 18. Some programmes also give a vaccine for infectious bronchitis or egg drop syndrome. Follow your local veterinary advice. Always use a cold chain and record all vaccinations. <h2>Practical Reference: Pullet Rearing Benchmarks, Records and Decision Tools for African Layer Farms</h2>

<p>Pullet rearing (weeks 018) is the single most consequential investment phase in a layer enterprise. In most African markets, a point-of-lay (POL) pullet represents 2035% of the total cost of producing the first egg, and rearing mortality, feed waste and poor uniformity are the three silent profit leaks that show up later as delayed onset of lay, small egg size, high feed conversion ratio (FCR) and elevated mortality in the laying house. This Practical Reference lesson gives you the numbers, records and decision rules you need to run pullet rearing as a business not as guesswork.</p>

<p>Business-first principle: every naira, shilling, cedi or birr spent on pullets must be tracked against a measurable outcome. If you cannot state your pullet cost per bird, your 18-week body weight and your uniformity percentage, you cannot price your eggs correctly or know whether your flock will be profitable.</p>

<h3>Reading the Numbers: What They Mean for Your Business</h3>
<p>Each 1% of mortality in rearing costs you roughly 10 pullets per 1,000 chicks and each lost pullet is a future laying hen that would have produced 300+ eggs. At USD 0.10 per egg, that is USD 30 per lost bird over the laying cycle. A 15% mortality rate versus a 4% rate is a swing of about USD 715 in this illustrative model, before accounting for the higher feed cost of the surviving birds and the delayed start of lay.</p>
<p>Uniformity is equally financial. A flock with 80% uniformity reaches peak production (9095%) by week 2426. A flock with 60% uniformity may not peak until week 2830, and peak may only reach 8085%. That 24 week delay and 510% lower peak is the difference between a profitable and a marginal flock.</p>

<div style="border:3px solid #b00020; background:#fdecec; padding:14px; margin:20px 0; border-radius:8px;">
<strong style="color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING Vaccines, Drugs and Chemicals</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Pullet rearing involves live vaccines, antibiotics and disinfectants. Always:</p>
<ul>
<li>Store vaccines at 28 C (never freeze live vaccines); use a cold chain from supplier to farm.</li>
<li>Never mix vaccines or administer expired products this causes vaccine failure and disease outbreaks.</li>
<li>Use antibiotics only on veterinary prescription; observe withdrawal periods before eggs or meat enter the food chain (WHO/FAO antimicrobial resistance guidance).</li>
<li>Wear gloves and a mask when handling disinfectants (formalin, glutaraldehyde, chlorine). Formalin is a carcinogen ventilate and never enter a fumigated room.</li>
<li>Dispose of dead birds and used vaccine vials by deep burial or incineration, away from water sources.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist: Weekly Pullet Rearing Actions</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Weigh 510% of the flock weekly</strong> (or at least at weeks 4, 8, 12, 16) and record average body weight against the breed standard.</li>
<li><strong>Calculate uniformity</strong> at weeks 8, 12 and 16: percentage of birds within 10% of the flock average. Target 80%.</li>
<li><strong>Record daily mortality and culls</strong>; investigate any spike above 0.5% per week. Post-mortem or vet visit if unexplained.</li>
<li><strong>Track feed consumed per bird per week</strong>; sudden drops signal disease, heat stress or poor feed quality.</li>
<li><strong>Grade and split flocks</strong> if uniformity falls below 80% feed small birds separately to catch up.</li>
<li><strong>Follow the vaccination programme</strong> (Marek's, Newcastle, IBD, fowl pox, etc.) and record batch numbers and dates.</li>
<li><strong>Maintain a cold chain and biosecurity log</strong>: footbaths, visitor log, rodent control, all-in/all-out where possible.</li>
<li><strong>Calculate cost per pullet</strong> at 18 weeks (chick + feed + labour + health + housing share) and compare to market POL prices before deciding to rear or buy.</li>
</ol>

<h3>Decision Tool: Rear or Buy Pullets?</h3>
<p>Use this simple rule: if your calculated cost per pullet at 18 weeks is more than 85% of the local market price for a POL pullet of the same breed and quality, buy instead of rear unless you have spare labour, housing and a reliable feed supply. If your cost is below 70% of market price, rearing is a clear business advantage. Between 70% and 85%, decide based on biosecurity risk and your management capacity.</p>
