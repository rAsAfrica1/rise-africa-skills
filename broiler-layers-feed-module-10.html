Lesson 10.1~12 min

Buying Grain You Can Trust: Practical Intake Checks Without a Laboratory

In this lesson
  • Perform a five-minute sensory and physical inspection on any grain delivery
  • Estimate grain moisture using simple field methods
  • Calculate the true cost per unit of nutrient rather than cost per bag

Feed is typically 60-75% of the cost of producing a chicken. The single most profitable hour of your week is the hour you spend inspecting grain before you pay for it. Once a bad load is in your store, it is your problem.

Sample properly first. One handful from the top of one bag tells you nothing, because sellers put the good grain where you will look. Take a small scoop from at least five different bags in a delivery, and from the top, middle and bottom of any bag you can open. Tip all the scoops onto a clean sheet or a flat surface, mix them, and spread them out. That is your working sample.

Run through six checks.

  1. Smell. Good maize smells faintly sweet and dusty. Musty, sour, fermented or paint-like smells mean mould activity or storage rot. Reject on smell alone.
  2. Moisture. Grain must be dry enough to store. The classic field test: bite a kernel. It should crack sharply and hurt your teeth, not dent. Another is the salt test - put a small handful of grain and a spoon of dry table salt in a clean dry glass jar, seal, shake for a minute and leave for fifteen. If the salt cakes on the jar wall the grain is too wet. Neither method replaces a moisture meter, but both are better than nothing, and a basic grain moisture meter at roughly $80-$150 is one of the best small investments a growing farm can make.
  3. Count damage. Take out exactly 100 kernels and sort them into sound, broken, shrivelled, insect-holed and discoloured. That gives you a percentage figure you can record and compare between suppliers. Broken and dust-heavy grain absorbs moisture and moulds faster.
  4. Look for mould. Chalky white, green, blue or pink growth, particularly on the germ end of maize, means fungal growth and possible toxin. Bright greenish-yellow fluorescence under a UV torch is a rough indicator worth checking, though it is not proof of aflatoxin.
  5. Check for foreign matter. Stones, soil, cob fragments and weed seeds are weight you paid for that has no feeding value. If a load is 4% foreign matter, you paid for 40 kg of dirt in every tonne.
  6. Feel the temperature. Push your hand deep into a bag. Warm grain is respiring, which means it is either too wet or already infested.

Now do the arithmetic that most buyers skip. Cost per bag is the wrong number. Cost per unit of nutrient is the right one. Suppose supplier A offers maize at $0.32/kg and supplier B at $0.29/kg but supplier B's load is visibly dirtier. If B carries 4% foreign matter and 2% more moisture than A, then a kilogram of B contains roughly 0.94 kg of usable dry maize against A's 0.99 kg. Effective cost for A is 0.32 / 0.99 = $0.323 per usable kg. For B it is 0.29 / 0.94 = $0.309. B still wins on price, but the gap has narrowed from 9% to 4%, and that is before you count higher mould risk and extra screening labour. If B were 6% foreign matter and 3% wetter, effective cost becomes 0.29 / 0.91 = $0.319, and the saving nearly disappears.

Do the same for protein sources. Soybean meal at 46% crude protein for $0.62/kg costs $1.35 per kg of protein. Sunflower cake at 30% crude protein for $0.42/kg costs $1.40 per kg of protein - and carries far more fibre. The cheaper bag is the more expensive protein.

A Malawian example: a producer near Lilongwe bought maize each month from whoever came to the gate. After starting a simple notebook recording supplier name, price, moisture bite test result and broken-grain count out of 100, patterns emerged within three months. One trader consistently delivered 12-15% broken grain while another delivered 4-6% at a price only 3% higher. Switching to the better supplier improved flock uniformity and cut visible mould in the store to nearly zero.

Write everything down. A supplier who knows you inspect and record will bring you their better grain.

Mycotoxins in African Maize and Groundnut: The Limits That Matter

In this lesson
  • State the regulatory limits for aflatoxin, ochratoxin, fumonisin and T-2 by bird class
  • Recognise the production symptoms caused by each major mycotoxin
  • Choose practical mitigation steps within a smallholder budget

Mycotoxins are poisons produced by moulds growing on crops in the field and in storage. They are a serious problem across Africa because maize and groundnut are staples, humidity is high, and grain is often dried on bare ground. You cannot see, smell or taste most mycotoxins at harmful levels, and heat during pelleting does not destroy them.

Learn the four that matter and their published limits.

Aflatoxin, produced mainly by Aspergillus flavus on maize and groundnut, is a known carcinogen. The maximum allowable level is 0.02 ppm (20 ppb) in both the EU and US. But sensitivity varies by bird class: chicks aged 0-4 weeks have the highest sensitivity with a limit of 0.005 ppm (5 ppb), while broilers, layers and breeders are at 0.02 ppm. Below those thresholds you still get reduced growth rate and feed efficiency, enlarged liver, immunosuppression through reduced antibody response, and increased disease susceptibility. That last point matters enormously: a flock that vaccinates poorly and keeps getting sick may be suffering aflatoxin immunosuppression rather than a vaccine failure.

Ochratoxin A limits are 0.05 ppm for chicks, 0.10 ppm for broilers, layers and turkeys, and just 0.01 ppm for breeder flocks, which are highly sensitive. Effects include immunosuppression in all bird types, decreased egg production and shell quality in layers - with a 50-75% production decline reported at 0.5 ppm - reduced hatchability and teratogenic effects on progeny in breeders, and growth depression with higher condemnation rates in broilers.

T-2 and HT-2 trichothecenes have a threshold of 0.25 ppm for all poultry. Broilers show significantly reduced body weight at dietary levels as low as 1 ppm over six weeks. Laying hens develop oral lesions - necrotic stomatitis - along with production depression. Lymphoid tissue atrophy and immunosuppression follow. If you see mouth ulcers and birds that approach the feeder then back away, think T-2.

Fumonisin limits are 10 ppm for chicks, 20 ppm for broilers and turkeys, and 30 ppm for layers and breeders. The critical finding is that at 18.6 ppm - below the nominal safe 20 ppm - fumonisins altered intestinal microflora and predisposed broilers to necrotic enteritis. The mechanism is disruption of sphingolipid metabolism causing epithelial barrier dysfunction.

A vital practical point: chronic low-level exposure to several mycotoxins at once is normal in commercial operations, and interactions may be additive, synergistic or antagonistic. Testing for one toxin is therefore insufficient; a comprehensive panel is recommended where you can access one.

What can a smallholder actually do?

  1. Buy from suppliers who dry on tarpaulins or concrete, never bare soil.
  2. Reject visibly mouldy, insect-bored or discoloured grain rather than negotiating a discount. A discount on toxic grain is not a bargain.
  3. Hand-sort where volumes allow. Sorting out broken, shrivelled and discoloured kernels from maize and groundnut removes a disproportionate share of the aflatoxin, because damaged kernels carry most of it.
  4. Store dry, cool and ventilated - the subject of the next lesson.
  5. Never feed the worst grain to chicks. If you must use a marginal batch, feed it to older birds where tolerance is higher, and never to breeders or day-old chicks.
  6. Use a binder where risk is real. Clay-based binders included at roughly 1-2 kg per tonne bind aflatoxin effectively.

Cost check: a binder at $1.60/kg included at 1.5 kg per tonne costs $2.40 per tonne, or $9.12 on 3,800 kg for 1,000 broilers. A 5% mortality event plus a 0.1 FCR penalty from a mycotoxin-contaminated batch would cost roughly 50 birds x $4.00 = $200 plus 950 x 2.0 x 0.1 = 190 kg of extra feed at $0.55 = $105, so around $305.

A Nigerian example: a Kano groundnut-cake buyer switched to sourcing cake only from a processor who stored raw nuts on raised slatted platforms under a roof. Cake price rose about 6%, but the pale, poorly feathered chicks and the persistent liver enlargement seen at post-mortem disappeared within two cycles.

Building a Dry, Rat-Proof Feed Store on a Small Budget

In this lesson
  • Lay out a feed store that keeps moisture, rodents and old stock under control
  • Apply first-in-first-out stock rotation to prevent spoilage losses
  • Calculate the payback period on simple storage improvements

A good store is not an expensive building. It is a dry floor, a sound roof, air movement, and discipline. Most spoilage on small farms comes from four failures that cost almost nothing to fix.

Failure one is bags on the floor. Concrete and beaten earth both wick moisture upward. The bottom two layers of any stack sitting directly on the floor will gain moisture and mould, and you often will not see it until you open the bag. Wooden pallets are the standard fix. If you cannot buy pallets, lay timber battens or old poles across the floor and stack on those. Even 10 cm of air underneath makes a large difference.

Failure two is stacks against walls. A wall exposed to sun heats up and drives moisture into the outer bags, and a wall in shade condenses. Leave at least 45 cm between the stack and every wall. That gap also lets you walk around the stack to inspect it and denies rodents the covered runway they prefer.

Failure three is no ventilation. Grain and feed respire and give off moisture and heat. A sealed store traps both. Ventilation openings high on opposite walls, covered with fine wire mesh against birds and rodents, let warm damp air escape. Mesh matters - an open vent is an invitation to wild birds, which are a major disease vector.

Failure four is no rotation. Feed bought in March gets buried behind feed bought in June and is still there in September. Vitamin activity degrades over time, especially in heat, and fat in feed goes rancid. Practise first-in-first-out: mark every bag or batch with the date received in large marker on the bag itself, stack so the oldest is reachable, and always take from the oldest. Aim to use compound feed within about four to six weeks in hot humid conditions, and premix within its stated shelf life kept sealed and out of sunlight.

Rodents deserve their own plan. A single rat eats roughly 25-30 grams of feed a day and contaminates far more with urine and droppings, which spread Salmonella. Twenty rats eating 25 g each for a year consume about 180 kg of feed, worth roughly $99 at $0.55/kg - and that ignores contamination and the bags they destroy. Control means removing harbourage (clear vegetation and rubbish for at least a metre around the store), sealing gaps larger than about 1 cm, keeping spilled feed swept up daily, and using bait stations placed along walls where rats run. Cats help but do not solve a real infestation.

Work the payback. Say your store holds 4 tonnes of feed at a time and you currently lose an estimated 3% to moisture, mould and rodents. That is 120 kg per turnover, worth $66 at $0.55/kg. If you turn stock over eight times a year, the annual loss is $528. Ten wooden pallets at about $9 each cost $90; wire mesh for four vents about $20; a rodent bait station programme about $40 a year. First-year cost is roughly $150. If the improvements cut losses from 3% to 1%, you save $352 a year. Payback is under six months, and the pallets last a decade.

Also store ingredients apart from finished feed, and premix apart from everything. Premix contains concentrated vitamins and trace minerals; sunlight, heat and moisture destroy vitamin activity fastest. Keep it in a sealed drum or lidded bucket in the coolest, darkest corner, and never leave the bag open between mixes.

A Kenyan example: a producer in Nakuru stored feed in a corner of the chicken house itself because it was convenient. Dust, humidity from the birds, ammonia and rodents all attacked the stock, and premix sat in an open sack. Moving feed to a separate small block store with pallets, mesh vents and dated bags cost about $180 in materials plus family labour. Reported mould losses dropped to almost nothing and feed conversion improved noticeably in the first full cycle.

Anti-Nutritional Factors: Cottonseed, Rapeseed, Phytate and Safe Inclusion Limits

In this lesson
  • Set safe maximum inclusion rates for cottonseed and rapeseed meals
  • Explain how phytic acid reduces the value of a plant-based diet
  • Decide when a cheap local ingredient is genuinely cheap

Local by-products are often the difference between profit and loss on an African poultry farm. Cottonseed cake, rapeseed and sunflower meal, palm kernel cake and brewers' grains can all be cheap. But every one carries something that limits how much you can safely use. Knowing the ceiling lets you capture the saving without paying for it in dead birds.

Gossypol in cottonseed. Gossypol is a natural polyphenol in cottonseed. The maximum recommended level is below 0.1% gossypol in the finished broiler diet, which in practice means limiting cottonseed meal to 5-10% of the diet depending on the gossypol content of the particular meal. Effects of exceeding it include reproductive toxicity in both sexes, liver enzyme induction and enlarged liver, immune suppression and growth depression. Broilers are more sensitive than layers, and chicks more sensitive than older birds. Two practical consequences: never use cottonseed cake in breeder diets, and keep it out of starter feed entirely. In layers gossypol can also cause olive-green yolk discolouration, which will destroy your market.

Glucosinolates in rapeseed and canola. Modern low-glucosinolate canola meal can be included at 5-15% of the diet. Traditional high-glucosinolate rapeseed meal must be limited to 2-5% because of goitrogenic effects. High inclusion causes thyroid enlargement, reduced feed intake through poor palatability, growth depression and immune suppression. Energy value is also modest - canola meal AMEn is around 1,900-2,000 kcal/kg against maize at 3,350 - so you are not just limited by toxicity but by the energy dilution.

If you cannot tell whether a local rapeseed meal is a modern low-glucosinolate variety, assume the worst and stay near the 2-5% band.

Phytic acid is present in all plant seeds and is highest in grains and legumes. Approximate contents are maize 0.7-1.0%, soybean meal 1.0-1.3%, barley 0.8-1.2%, while fish meal is below 0.1%. Phytate chelates calcium, phosphorus, zinc, iron, copper and manganese, cutting phosphorus bioavailability by 20-50%, zinc by 30-60% and calcium, iron and copper variably by 15-40%. It may also form complexes that reduce protein digestibility.

Mitigation is straightforward. Phytase at 500-1,500 FTU/kg degrades phytic acid, and roughly 1,000 FTU/kg is worth about 0.15-0.25% available phosphorus. The response is most pronounced in young broilers at days 2-14. Moderate heat processing reduces phytic acid by about 20-30%, but excessive heat damages amino acids, so there is a real trade-off in home-toasting. Ingredient selection also matters: diets carrying fish meal or animal by-products have lower phytate load and need less phytase.

Now decide whether a cheap ingredient is truly cheap. Suppose cottonseed cake at 36% crude protein costs $0.40/kg while soybean meal at 46% costs $0.62/kg. Protein cost is $1.11/kg for cottonseed against $1.35/kg for soya - an apparent 18% saving. But you can only use cottonseed to about 8% of the diet. In a tonne of feed that is 80 kg, contributing 80 x 0.36 = 28.8 kg of crude protein. If your diet needs 200 kg of crude protein per tonne, cottonseed can cover only 14% of the requirement. The saving is 80 kg x ($0.62 x (0.36/0.46) - $0.40)... simpler: replacing the protein equivalent, 28.8 kg of protein from soya would need 28.8 / 0.46 = 62.6 kg of soya costing $38.81, versus 80 kg of cottonseed at $32.00. Saving is $6.81 per tonne, about 1% of a typical feed cost. Real, but modest - and it disappears entirely if you push inclusion too high and lose birds.

A Zimbabwean example: a Chegutu grower fed 20% cottonseed cake to layers during a soya price spike. Within six weeks egg production fell, several yolks came out olive-green, and his shop customers stopped buying. Pulling inclusion back to 6% restored yolk colour over the following month, but the customer list took a year to rebuild.

Mixing, Milling and Batch Records That Prevent Costly Mistakes

In this lesson
  • Sequence ingredients and set mixing times for a uniform batch
  • Use a premix pre-blend so micro-ingredients distribute evenly
  • Keep batch records that let you trace a problem back to its cause

A perfectly formulated diet is worthless if it is badly mixed. On small farms the most common cause of unexplained mortality, uneven growth and leg problems is not the formula but the mixing. Micro-ingredients present at a few hundred grams per tonne - premix, salt, an amino acid, a coccidiostat - must be spread evenly through 1,000 kg of grain. If they are not, some birds eat five times the intended dose and others eat none.

Start with particle size. Grinding too fine produces dusty feed that birds refuse and that causes gizzard problems. Grinding too coarse means birds selectively eat the large particles and leave the fines, which is where the premix sits. For mash fed to broilers, a medium grind that still shows visible grain fragments generally works best, and a hammer mill screen around 4-6 mm is a reasonable starting point for maize. Layers benefit from some coarse material - and coarse limestone particles of about 2-4 mm are deliberately retained in the gizzard for slow calcium release, so never grind your limestone fine for layers.

Use a pre-blend for micro-ingredients. Never tip 2 kg of premix straight into a tonne of maize. Instead take a bucket, put in about 10 kg of ground maize, add the premix, salt and any amino acid, and mix that thoroughly by hand or shovel. Then add that pre-blend to the main batch. This step-up dilution is standard practice in commercial mills and it costs you three minutes.

Sequence the main batch by volume. Put in the largest ingredient first to form a bed, then the next largest, adding the pre-blend into the middle of the batch rather than on top. Add oil or fat last and slowly, because fat added early coats particles and prevents the dry ingredients from moving past each other.

Mixing time has an optimum. Too short and it is uneven; too long and a horizontal mixer can actually de-mix, with fine and heavy particles separating again. For a small horizontal or vertical mixer, 4-8 minutes after the last dry ingredient is in is a common working range, and you should test your own machine rather than guess. The simple field test: after a mix, take five samples from different points in the batch, spread each on paper, and count coloured premix particles or added marker grains in each. If the counts differ wildly, extend mixing time and retest.

Batch records are the difference between fixing a problem and repeating it. A one-page card per batch should record: date and time, batch number, every ingredient and its actual weighed amount, the supplier and lot of each major ingredient, the premix batch number and expiry, the mixing time, the operator's name, the total output weight, and which house or age group received it. Keep the cards for at least a year.

Why it pays: suppose birds in house 2 develop leg weakness at week three. Without records you guess. With records you can see that batch 47, which went to house 2, used limestone from a new supplier and weighed 18 kg instead of the standard 12 kg because a bucket was miscounted. You fix it in one afternoon and you never repeat it. Records also let you calculate real feed cost per tonne rather than an estimate, and prove to a buyer or lender that you run a controlled operation.

Weigh, do not scoop. A bucket of maize and a bucket of soybean meal have different densities, so volume measures drift. A used platform scale costs roughly $60-$120 and pays for itself the first time it prevents a mis-mixed batch. Check the scale monthly against a known weight, such as a sealed 25 kg bag.

Cost of getting it wrong: one badly mixed tonne fed to 250 growing birds that causes 5% extra mortality and a week of lost growth costs perhaps 12 birds at $4.00 plus reduced sale weight - easily $70-$100, against three minutes of pre-blending.

A Rwandan example: a cooperative near Musanze shared one mixer between six members. Nobody recorded batches, and when a coccidiostat overdose caused sudden deaths there was no way to know whose batch or which bag was at fault. Introducing a numbered carbon-copy batch card - one copy to the member, one kept at the mixer - meant the next problem was traced within a day to a premix bag stored open in the sun.

Farm Biosecurity a Smallholder Can Actually Afford

In this lesson
  • Separate your farm into clean and dirty zones with one crossing point
  • Rank biosecurity measures by cost and by risk reduction
  • Justify all-in all-out management with a cost calculation

Biosecurity has a reputation for meaning expensive fences and shower-in facilities. On a small African farm the highest-value measures are nearly free, and the money is better spent on the few that genuinely cost something. The organising idea is simple: disease arrives on things that move. Birds, people, vehicles, equipment, feed, water, wild birds, rodents and insects. Control the movement and you control most of the risk.

Start by drawing a line. On paper, mark your poultry houses and the ground immediately around them as the clean area, and everything else - the road, the house, the market, the neighbour's farm - as the dirty area. Then decide on exactly one crossing point. At that point you need three things: a pair of boots that never leave the clean side, a basin or footbath, and a hand-washing point. That is the core of the whole system, and it can be built for under $20.

Footbaths only work if the disinfectant is fresh and the boots are clean first. A footbath full of mud and old solution is decoration. Change it at least every two days, more in rain, and scrape boots before stepping in.

Rank your interventions by cost against risk reduction.

Free or nearly free:

  1. No visitors in the houses. Buyers, neighbours and other farmers are the highest-risk visitors of all because they have been in other flocks. Sell birds at the gate, never from inside the house.
  2. Clean boots and dedicated clothing for the poultry area.
  3. Work from youngest birds to oldest, never the reverse.
  4. Keep your own poultry away from live-bird markets. If you go to a market, change clothes and boots before entering your houses.
  5. Sweep spilled feed daily and keep grass cut around houses.
  6. Never bring back unsold live birds and mix them with the flock.

Modest cost:

  1. Wire mesh on all house openings to exclude wild birds, which are a major vector.
  2. A lockable feed store as covered in Lesson 3.
  3. Rodent bait stations along walls.
  4. Proper dead-bird disposal - a covered pit or burial well away from houses and water, never thrown in the bush where scavengers spread it back.

Higher cost but high value: 11. All-in all-out management. This means one age group on the site at a time, then a full clean-out and rest period before the next batch.

All-in all-out deserves the arithmetic because many farmers resist it. Suppose you keep 500 broilers continuously, restocking in staggered batches so you always have birds to sell. The disease cycle never breaks, and you carry a chronic 10% mortality. Moving to all-in all-out with a 14-day empty period means about 26 days a year with no birds, costing you roughly half a batch of throughput. But if mortality falls from 10% to 5%, you save 25 birds per 500-bird batch. Over six batches a year at $4.00 a bird that is 6 x 25 x $4.00 = $600, plus the feed those birds ate before dying. Against that, half a batch of lost gross margin - if margin is about $1.20 a bird, 250 birds is $300. You are ahead by roughly $300 a year, before counting reduced medicine costs and better feed conversion in a healthier flock.

Cleaning between batches: dry clean first - remove all litter, dust and cobwebs, because disinfectant does not work through organic matter. Then wash with detergent and water. Then disinfect. Then rest the house dry. Skipping the dry clean is the most common mistake and it wastes the disinfectant you paid for.

A Tanzanian example: a producer outside Arusha ran three overlapping batches in one house and treated respiratory disease almost continuously. Restructuring to single-age batches with a two-week break, plus mesh on the windows and a single gated entry with a footbath, cut his veterinary spend by more than half within a year. The total capital outlay was mesh, a gate latch, two basins and disinfectant.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. How should you sample a grain delivery?

Sellers place good grain where buyers look. Taking scoops from at least five bags, and from different depths, gives a representative working sample.

2. Maize A costs $0.32/kg with 1% waste; maize B costs $0.29/kg with 6% waste and moisture. What is B's effective cost per usable kg?

Only 0.91 of each kilogram is usable, so 0.29 / 0.91 = $0.319 - almost identical to A's effective cost of 0.32 / 0.99 = $0.323, so the apparent saving largely disappears.

3. Soybean meal at 46% CP costs $0.62/kg. What is the cost per kg of protein?

$0.62 divided by 0.46 = $1.35 per kilogram of crude protein. Comparing protein sources on this basis, not on price per bag, is what reveals real value.

4. What does warm grain deep inside a bag indicate?

Warmth deep in a stack means biological activity - the grain is respiring due to excess moisture, or insects are active. Both lead to mould and loss.

5. In the salt-jar moisture test, what indicates grain that is too wet?

Moisture released by wet grain makes the dry salt clump and stick to the glass. It is a rough field check, not a replacement for a moisture meter, but it is free.

6. What is the aflatoxin limit for chicks aged 0-4 weeks?

Chicks are the most sensitive class at 0.005 ppm (5 ppb), four times stricter than the 0.02 ppm applied to broilers, layers and breeders.

7. Which mycotoxin causes oral lesions and necrotic stomatitis in laying hens?

T-2 and HT-2 trichothecenes cause necrotic stomatitis, oral lesions and production depression in laying hens, along with lymphoid atrophy and immunosuppression.

8. Ochratoxin A at 0.5 ppm in layers causes what?

A 50-75% production decline has been reported in layers at 0.5 ppm ochratoxin A, alongside decreased shell quality and immunosuppression.

9. Why is testing for a single mycotoxin insufficient?

Commercial feeds commonly carry several toxins at low levels. Interactions may be additive, synergistic or antagonistic, so a comprehensive panel is recommended rather than a single-toxin test.

10. Which grain should never be fed to day-old chicks or breeders?

Chicks and breeders are the most mycotoxin-sensitive classes, with limits several times stricter than broilers. A marginal batch may be tolerable for older birds but never for these groups.

11. Why should feed bags never sit directly on a concrete floor?

Both concrete and earth floors wick moisture upward. The bottom layers gain moisture and mould, often invisibly, until the bag is opened. Pallets or timber battens create an air gap.

12. What is the minimum recommended gap between a feed stack and a wall?

At least 45 cm prevents moisture transfer from sun-heated or condensing walls, allows inspection access, and denies rodents a covered runway.

13. A 4-tonne store loses 3% of stock, turned over 8 times a year, with feed at $0.55/kg. What is the annual loss?

4,000 kg x 3% = 120 kg per turnover x $0.55 = $66. Multiplied by 8 turnovers gives $528 per year.

14. How should premix be stored?

Premix carries concentrated vitamins that heat, light and moisture destroy fastest. A sealed drum or lidded bucket in the coolest dark corner protects the most valuable part of your feed.

15. What does first-in-first-out mean in practice?

FIFO means marking the date received on every bag and stacking so the oldest is accessible and always used first, so no stock sits long enough to lose vitamin activity or go rancid.

16. What is the maximum recommended gossypol level in a finished broiler diet?

Gossypol must stay below 0.1% in the finished diet, which in practice means limiting cottonseed meal to 5-10% depending on the gossypol content of that particular meal.

17. Traditional high-glucosinolate rapeseed meal should be limited to what share of the diet?

High-glucosinolate rapeseed is limited to 2-5% because of goitrogenic effects. Only modern low-glucosinolate canola varieties can be used at 5-15%.

18. Which ingredient has the lowest phytic acid content?

Fish meal contains below 0.1% phytic acid, against maize at 0.7-1.0%, barley 0.8-1.2% and soybean meal 1.0-1.3%. Diets with animal protein need less phytase.

19. What is the trade-off in heat-treating ingredients to reduce phytic acid?

Moderate heat processing reduces phytic acid by about 20-30%, but over-processing damages amino acids, so the benefit reverses if you cook too hard or too long.

20. Why can a cheaper protein source deliver only a small feed cost saving?

Cottonseed cake may be cheaper per kilogram of protein, but an 8% inclusion ceiling means it can only cover a small share of the diet's protein need, so the saving on a full tonne is modest.

21. Why should premix be pre-blended with about 10 kg of maize before being added to a tonne batch?

Two kilograms of premix cannot distribute evenly through 1,000 kg directly. Pre-blending into a bucket of ground maize first is standard commercial practice and takes three minutes.

22. What happens if a horizontal mixer runs far too long?

Beyond the optimum, mixers can segregate rather than blend, with fine and dense particles separating out. That is why you should test your own machine rather than assume more time is better.

23. Why should limestone for layers not be ground fine?

Coarse calcium particles of 2-4 mm sit in the gizzard and dissolve slowly, supplying calcium through the night when the shell gland is working. Fine particles are absorbed quickly and used for bone.

24. When should oil or fat be added to a batch?

Fat added early coats dry particles and prevents them moving past each other, ruining mixing uniformity. It goes in last and slowly, after the dry ingredients are blended.

25. What is the main practical value of a batch record card?

When a health or growth problem appears, records turn guesswork into diagnosis - you can identify which batch went to which house and what was different about it, then fix it permanently.

26. Who are the highest-risk visitors to a poultry house?

Other farmers and buyers have recently been in contact with other flocks, so they carry the greatest pathogen load. Selling at the gate rather than inside the house removes most of this risk at zero cost.

27. Why must you dry clean a house before disinfecting?

Disinfectants are neutralised by organic material. Removing litter, dust and cobwebs first is what allows the chemical to reach and kill pathogens on the surfaces.

28. In what order should you work through houses of different ages?

Older birds shed more pathogens. Working from the youngest and most vulnerable birds toward the oldest avoids carrying infection backward on your boots and hands.

29. Moving from continuous stocking to all-in all-out cuts mortality from 10% to 5% on 500-bird batches, six batches a year at $4.00 per bird. What is the annual saving on mortality alone?

5% of 500 is 25 birds saved per batch. 25 x 6 batches x $4.00 = $600 per year, before counting the feed those birds would have eaten and reduced medicine costs.

30. What makes a footbath useless?

Mud and organic matter inactivate disinfectant. A footbath with old solution and dirty boots stepping through it provides no protection and creates false confidence.

Module 10 capstone

Build a written feed quality and biosecurity system for your own farm and prove it works for one full batch. Step 1: visit two or three local grain suppliers, take a 500 g sample from each, and complete a written intake check on every sample covering moisture feel, smell, broken and shrivelled grain percentage from a 100-grain count, visible mould, insect damage and foreign matter, then rank the suppliers and record their prices per kg. Step 2: measure your feed store - floor area, roof condition, ventilation openings, distance of stacks from walls - and draw a corrected layout with pallets, aisle space and a first-in-first-out flow, costing any materials in USD. Step 3: design and use a one-page batch record card for every mix for four weeks, capturing date, ingredient weights, premix batch number, mixing time and the house it went to. Step 4: map your farm on paper marking the clean area, the dirty area and the crossing point, then list the three cheapest biosecurity improvements you can make and implement one. Present the supplier ranking, the store layout, four weeks of completed batch cards and the biosecurity map together.
