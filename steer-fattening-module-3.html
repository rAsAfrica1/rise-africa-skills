Lessons

1. Nutrient Requirements for Different Stages of Fattening

Fattening steers goes through three stages: starter (first 30 days), grower (next 60 days), and finisher (last 30-60 days). Each stage needs different amounts of energy, protein, and minerals. In Africa, most steers start at 200-250 kg and finish at 400-450 kg. During the starter stage, give 1.5-2 kg of concentrate per day plus free-choice hay. The concentrate should have 14-16% crude protein (CP) and 70-75% total digestible nutrients (TDN). This helps the rumen adjust to grain and builds appetite.

In the grower stage, increase concentrate to 3-4 kg per day. The diet should have 12-14% CP and 72-76% TDN. This is when the steer builds muscle and frame. Use locally available energy feeds like maize, sorghum, or millet. Mix with protein sources like cottonseed cake or groundnut cake. Always provide clean water and mineral licks.

During the finisher stage, push energy to 80-85% TDN and drop protein to 10-12% CP. Give 5-7 kg of concentrate per day. This stage adds fat and marbling. Too much protein at this stage is wasteful and expensive. Watch for acidosis: if the steer goes off feed, reduce grain and add roughage.

A simple rule: a 300 kg steer eats about 2.5-3% of its body weight in dry matter daily. So a 300 kg steer needs 7.5-9 kg of feed dry matter. Adjust based on body condition. In hot African climates, feed early morning and evening when it's cool. Always provide shade and water. Weigh your steers every 30 days to track gain. Aim for 0.8-1.2 kg gain per day. If gain is below 0.5 kg, check feed quality and health.

2. Energy Feeds: Grains and By-Products

Energy feeds are the fuel for fattening. In Africa, the most common energy feeds are maize, sorghum, millet, and their by-products. Maize is king: it has about 85% TDN and 8-9% CP. Crack or grind maize to improve digestion. Whole kernels often pass through undigested. For a 300 kg steer, 3-4 kg of cracked maize per day is a good starting point. But maize is also human food, so use by-products where possible.

Maize bran (also called maize offal or pollard) is a great energy feed. It has 70-75% TDN and 8-10% CP. It is cheaper than whole maize and widely available from local mills. Rice bran is another option: 65-70% TDN and 10-12% CP. But rice bran can go rancid quickly, so store it dry and use within two weeks. Wheat bran has 65-70% TDN and 14-16% CP, making it both energy and protein. It is excellent for fattening.

Sorghum and millet are drought-tolerant and grow well in dry areas. They have similar energy to maize (80-85% TDN) but slightly lower protein (7-9% CP). Use them when maize is expensive. Molasses is a liquid energy feed with 50-60% TDN. It is cheap but can cause scours if overfed. Limit molasses to 1-1.5 kg per day for a 300 kg steer. Mix it with dry feed to prevent sticking.

Cassava peels and chips are energy feeds in West and Central Africa. They have 60-70% TDN but low protein (2-3% CP). Never feed raw cassavait contains cyanide. Dry or ensile it first. Always introduce any new energy feed slowly over 7-10 days to avoid digestive upset. Store grains in dry, raised cribs to prevent aflatoxin from mold.

3. Protein Sources: Oilseed Meals and Legumes

Protein builds muscle and is critical in the starter and grower stages. The best oilseed meals in Africa are cottonseed cake, groundnut cake, sunflower cake, and soybean meal. Cottonseed cake has 30-35% CP. It is widely available in West and East Africa. But it contains gossypol, which can be toxic. Limit cottonseed cake to 1-1.5 kg per day for a 300 kg steer. Groundnut cake has 40-45% CP and is very palatable. It is excellent but can be expensive. Sunflower cake has 28-32% CP and is a good mid-range option.

Soybean meal is the gold standard with 44-48% CP. It is often imported or expensive, so use it sparingly0.5-1 kg per day is enough to balance a ration. Always heat-treat raw soybeans to destroy trypsin inhibitors. Legume forages are another protein source. Lucerne (alfalfa) hay has 15-18% CP. Lablab, cowpea, and groundnut haulms are common in Africa. Groundnut haulms have 10-12% CP and are very digestible. Use them as part of the roughage.

For a 300 kg steer in the grower stage, aim for 12-14% CP in the total diet. If you feed 3 kg of maize (9% CP) and 4 kg of hay (8% CP), you need about 0.5 kg of cottonseed cake (32% CP) to reach 13% CP. Mix well. Protein is expensive, so do not overfeed. Excess protein is excreted as urea, wasting money and polluting the environment. In the finisher stage, drop protein to 10-12% CP. Too much protein can make the meat dark and reduce fat deposition. Store oilseed cakes in dry, cool places to prevent mold and rancidity.

4. Forages: Hay, Silage, and Pasture

Forages are the backbone of ruminant nutrition. They provide fiber, which keeps the rumen healthy. In Africa, common forages include natural pasture, crop residues (maize stover, sorghum stover, rice straw), and planted forages (elephant grass, Rhodes grass, lucerne). Hay is dried forage. Good hay is green, leafy, and smells sweet. It should have 8-12% CP and 50-60% TDN. Make hay when grass is young and before flowering. Dry it in the sun for 2-3 days, then store under cover. Poor hay is brown, stemmy, and dustyit has little feed value.

Silage is fermented forage. It is made by chopping green forage, packing it tightly in a pit or silo, and covering it to exclude air. Maize silage is the best, with 65-70% TDN and 8-10% CP. Sorghum silage is also good. To make silage, chop maize at dough stage (when kernels are soft but not dry). Add molasses if the crop is too dry. Pack in layers, press out air, and seal with plastic. After 3-4 weeks, it is ready. Good silage smells like vinegar, not manure. Feed 5-10 kg per steer per day.

Pasture is the cheapest forage. Rotational grazing improves yield and prevents parasites. Allow grass to grow to 20-30 cm before grazing, and graze down to 10 cm. Do not overgraze. In dry seasons, pasture quality drops. Supplement with hay, silage, or concentrate. Crop residues are abundant after harvest. Maize stover has 4-6% CP and 45-50% TDN. Treat with urea to improve digestibility: mix 4 kg urea in 100 liters water, sprinkle on 100 kg stover, cover for 2 weeks. This raises CP to 8-10%. Always provide forages free-choice to steers. They need at least 1.5-2% of body weight in forage dry matter daily.

5. Minerals, Vitamins, and Feed Additives

Minerals and vitamins are needed in small amounts but are critical for health and growth. The most important minerals for fattening steers are calcium (Ca), phosphorus (P), salt (NaCl), and trace minerals like zinc, copper, and selenium. In Africa, soils are often deficient in phosphorus and selenium. Provide a mineral lick free-choice. A good lick should have 8-10% Ca, 6-8% P, and 20-25% salt. If you cannot buy a commercial lick, mix your own: 40% limestone, 30% bone meal, 20% salt, 10% trace mineral premix. Offer it in a wooden box or trough.

Vitamins A, D, and E are the most important. Green forage provides vitamin A precursors (carotene). If steers are on dry feed for more than 2 months, inject vitamin A or add a supplement. Vitamin D is made by sunlight, so steers in sunny Africa rarely need extra. Vitamin E is in green feed and grains. Feed additives are non-nutrient substances that improve growth or health. Common additives include ionophores (like monensin), which improve feed efficiency by 5-10%. They also reduce bloat and acidosis. Use according to label directionsusually 20-30 g per ton of feed.

Probiotics (live yeast) can stabilize the rumen and improve fiber digestion. Add 5-10 g per steer per day. Buffers like sodium bicarbonate (baking soda) help prevent acidosis when feeding high grain. Add 0.5-1% of the concentrate. Urea can be used as a non-protein nitrogen source for ruminants, but never feed more than 1% of the diet or 0.3 g per kg body weight. Too much urea is toxic. Mix urea thoroughly with feed. Never feed urea to non-ruminants. Always provide clean watersteers drink 30-50 liters per day. Store minerals and additives in dry, labeled containers away from children and animals.

6. Feed Analysis and Quality Assessment

You cannot manage what you do not measure. Feed analysis tells you the nutrient content of your feeds. The most important measures are dry matter (DM), crude protein (CP), total digestible nutrients (TDN), and fiber (NDF). You can send samples to a laboratory, but simple on-farm tests can help. For dry matter, weigh a sample, dry it in an oven at 60C for 24 hours, and weigh again. The difference is moisture. Most feeds are 85-90% DM when air-dry. For protein, you need a lab. But you can estimate: oilseed cakes are high (30-45% CP), cereals are medium (8-12% CP), and straws are low (3-6% CP).

Physical assessment is quick and free. Good hay is green, leafy, and smells fresh. Bad hay is brown, moldy, or dusty. Good silage smells like vinegar and is not slimy. Good grain is dry, clean, and free of mold. Moldy grain may contain aflatoxin, which is poisonous. Test for aflatoxin with a simple kit if available. For concentrates, look for uniform particle size and no clumping. Smell for rancidity in oilseed cakes. Taste a small amount of mineral mixit should be salty but not bitter.

A simple tool is the Penn State Forage Particle Separator. It has three screens that separate feed into long, medium, and fine particles. For fattening steers, you want 10-20% long particles, 30-50% medium, and 40-60% fine. Too many fines cause acidosis; too many long particles reduce intake. You can make a homemade version with three boxes with different mesh sizes. Also, weigh your steers monthly. If average daily gain is below 0.8 kg, your feed quality is likely poor. Adjust the ration. Keep records of feed purchases, weights, and gains. This helps you identify which feeds give the best return. Always buy feed from trusted suppliers and store it properly.

<h2>Practical Reference: Nutritional Requirements and Feedstuffs for Steer Fattening in Africa</h2>

<p>This practical reference distils the core decisions a fattening operation must make daily: what to feed, how much, at what cost, and with what risk. In African beef systems, where feed typically represents 6075% of total production cost, the difference between profit and loss is usually a feed-management difference, not a breed difference. The lessons in this module nutrient requirements by stage, energy feeds, protein sources, forages, minerals and additives, and feed analysis converge here into an operational toolkit.</p>

<p>Fattening is a business of margins. A 450 kg steer gaining 1.2 kg/day consumes roughly 1012 kg dry matter (DM) daily and requires about 1213% crude protein (CP) and 6570% total digestible nutrients (TDN) in the diet. Small errors in ration formulation compound over a 90120 day finishing cycle, so the reference values below should be treated as working targets, adjusted using local feed analysis and observed animal performance.</p>

<h3>Minerals, Vitamins, and Additives</h3>
<p>Mineral deficiencies are widespread in African rangelands, particularly phosphorus, copper, zinc, and selenium. A free-choice mineral lick containing 68% phosphorus, plus trace minerals, should be available at all times. Vitamin A supplementation is critical for cattle on dry-season roughage, as carotene degrades rapidly in stored forage. Ionophores such as monensin improve feed efficiency by 510% and reduce bloat risk when included at 2530 g/tonne of feed. Yeast cultures support rumen stability during ration transitions.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #b00020; background:#fdecea; padding:14px 18px; margin:18px 0; border-radius:6px; font-family:Arial, sans-serif;">
<strong style="color:#b00020; font-size:15px;"> SAFETY WARNING Aflatoxin and Gossypol</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; font-size:13px; color:#3a0a0a;">
Groundnut and cottonseed by-products are among the most economical protein sources in Africa, but they carry serious risks. <strong>Aflatoxin</strong> from poorly stored groundnut cake causes liver damage, reduced feed intake, and residue in milk and meat reject any batch with visible mould, musty smell, or discolouration, and test where possible (safe limit: below 20 ppb for beef cattle). <strong>Gossypol</strong> from cottonseed cake is toxic at high inclusion never exceed 15% of the ration for growing steers, and never feed to breeding bulls. When in doubt, source from a certified feed mill or laboratory-tested supplier. Cheap feed that poisons your herd is the most expensive feed you will ever buy.
</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol style="font-family:Arial, sans-serif; font-size:14px; line-height:1.7;">
<li><strong>Weigh your animals monthly.</strong> If average daily gain falls below 0.8 kg during Phase 2, review the ration before blaming the breed.</li>
<li><strong>Test your feedstuffs.</strong> A 23% variation in CP or TDN between batches can shift cost per kg gain by 1015%. Use a NIR or wet-chemistry lab where available; otherwise, visual and smell checks are the minimum.</li>
<li><strong>Transition rations over 57 days.</strong> Sudden increases in grain cause sub-acute acidosis, reducing intake and gains for a week or more.</li>
<li><strong>Keep a free-choice mineral lick available at all times.</strong> Phosphorus and trace mineral deficiencies silently cap growth.</li>
<li><strong>Monitor trough refusals daily.</strong> 510% refusals indicate correct intake; 0% means you are underfeeding; over 15% means you are wasting money.</li>
<li><strong>Store feed dry and elevated.</strong> Moisture above 12% invites mould and aflatoxin. Use pallets and cover with tarpaulin.</li>
<li><strong>Calculate cost per kg gain, not cost per bag.</strong> The cheapest feed per tonne is rarely the cheapest per kilogram of beef produced.</li>
<li><strong>Keep records.</strong> Feed in, weight out, cost per phase. Without records, every decision is a guess.</li>
</ol>
