1. Creating a simple timeline for the event day

A simple timeline is your event's backbone. Start by listing every key activity: vendor arrivals, setup, guest arrival, program items, meals, and pack-down. Use a notebook or a free app like Google Sheets on your phone. Write times in one column and tasks in another. For a typical African wedding reception starting at 2 PM, your timeline might look like: 6 AM â setup crew arrives; 8 AM â decor team starts; 10 AM â caterer arrives; 12 PM â sound check; 1 PM â ushers in position; 2 PM â guests arrive; 3 PM â introductions; 4 PM â lunch; 5 PM â speeches; 6 PM â cake cutting; 7 PM â dancing; 9 PM â pack-down. Always add a 30-minute buffer before guest arrival. Share the timeline with your team and key vendors via WhatsApp. Print two copies: one for you, one for your assistant. During the event, check off tasks as they happen. If something runs late, adjust the next items but keep the end time fixed. For example, if speeches run 20 minutes over, cut 10 minutes from open dancing. This keeps the event from dragging. Remember, African events often start later than planned, so build in flexibility for 'African time' but gently encourage punctuality for critical vendors. Review the timeline the night before with your team to ensure everyone knows their cues. A good timeline reduces stress and keeps you in control.

2. Assigning roles to helpers: ushers, servers, and setup crew

Clear roles prevent confusion. For a 100-guest event, you need at least 4 ushers, 6 servers, and 4 setup crew. Ushers greet guests, check invitations, and guide them to seats. Servers handle food and drinks. Setup crew arranges tables, chairs, and decor. Assign one team leader per group. Give each person a written card with their duties and reporting time. For example, ushers arrive 2 hours before guests, servers 1 hour before food service, setup crew 4 hours before. Pay them fairly: in Kenya, ushers might earn KES 1,000-1,500 per event, servers KES 800-1,200, setup crew KES 1,000-2,000. Use WhatsApp to create groups for each team. Send a voice note explaining tasks for those who prefer listening. During the event, rotate breaks: servers eat after guests are served. Have a backup person for each role in case someone doesn't show. Train ushers to handle guest questions politely and direct special requests to you. Servers should know the menu and dietary options. Setup crew must know the floor plan. At the end, assign pack-down tasks to the same teams. Recognize good work with a small bonus or public thank you. This builds loyalty and ensures your next event has reliable helpers.

3. Communicating with vendors using phone calls and WhatsApp groups

Vendors can make or break your event. Start communication a week before with a phone call to confirm arrival times, items, and payment. Then create a WhatsApp group for all vendors: caterer, decorator, DJ, photographer, and rentals. In the group, share the timeline and your contact. Use voice notes for complex instructions, as many vendors prefer them. For example, call the caterer to confirm the number of guests and serving time, then send a WhatsApp message summarizing: 'Caterer: 100 guests, lunch at 4 PM, please arrive by 2 PM.' For rentals, call to confirm delivery of 50 chairs, 10 tables, and tent by 8 AM. If a vendor is late, call immediately. Don't just text. If they don't answer, call again. Keep a backup vendor list. In the group, post updates: 'DJ has arrived', 'Caterer delayed by 30 minutes'. This keeps everyone informed. After the event, send a thank-you message and pay promptly. For future events, maintain a vendor database with ratings. Use WhatsApp's 'broadcast' feature to send updates to multiple vendors without a group. Always confirm payments and receipts via WhatsApp for record-keeping. Good communication builds trust and ensures vendors prioritize your event.

4. Solving common problems: late deliveries, missing items, and guest issues

Problems will happen. Stay calm and act fast. For late deliveries, call the vendor every 15 minutes. If they are over an hour late, activate your backup: rent from a nearby shop or borrow from a fellow planner. Keep a list of emergency suppliers. For missing items, like a missing cake or microphone, check with the vendor first. If unavailable, improvise: use a speaker for music, or serve dessert instead. For guest issues, like seating conflicts or dietary needs, have ushers handle politely. If a guest is drunk and disruptive, quietly ask them to step outside with a security person. For power outages, have a generator or backup battery for music. For rain, have a tent or move indoors. Always carry a small toolkit with tape, scissors, and extra extension cords. Keep a first aid kit. If a guest faints, call for help and clear the area. Document incidents for future planning. After the event, review what went wrong and update your checklist. For example, if the caterer was late, find a more reliable one. In Africa, traffic and weather can cause delays, so always add buffer time. Train your team to report problems to you immediately, not to argue with guests. Your job is to solve, not to panic.

5. Keeping the event on schedule without rushing guests

Guests should feel relaxed, not rushed. To keep on schedule, use gentle cues. For example, have the MC say, 'In 10 minutes, we'll move to the next activity.' Use background music changes to signal transitions. If dinner is running late, keep guests entertained with a performance or games. Never cut short a guest's speech or dance. Instead, adjust your own tasks. For example, if speeches run over, shorten the cake cutting or skip the bouquet toss. Have a timekeeper (your assistant) who signals you when 10 minutes remain for each segment. Use a silent signal like a raised hand. For African events, 'African time' is common, so build in 30 minutes of flex time. But for key moments like cake cutting, stick to the schedule. If guests are still arriving, delay the start by 15 minutes max. Communicate delays to vendors so they adjust. For example, tell the caterer to hold food. Keep the energy high with music. If a segment is dragging, have the MC wrap it up politely. After the event, thank guests for their patience if there were delays. The goal is a smooth flow, not a military drill. Your calmness sets the tone.

6. Packing down and returning rented items efficiently

Pack-down starts before the event ends. Assign a pack-down leader. As soon as guests leave, your team moves. First, collect all rented items: chairs, tables, linens, cutlery, and decor. Count them against your rental list. Use a checklist on your phone. Have boxes or bags ready for items. For example, stack chairs in tens and tie them. Fold linens and place in a laundry bag. For breakages, note them and inform the rental company immediately. If you rented a tent, the rental company usually dismantles it, but confirm. For items you own, pack them in labeled boxes for next time. Return rentals the next morning to avoid extra charges. In Nairobi, late returns can cost 10% extra per day. Clean items before returning: wipe chairs, shake out linens. If you hired a truck, load efficiently: heavy items first. Pay your helpers immediately after pack-down to keep them motivated. Do a final walk-through to ensure nothing is left behind. Check for personal items of guests and keep them safe. After returning rentals, get a receipt. Update your inventory list. Review what went well and what didn't. Store your decor in a dry place. Efficient pack-down saves money and keeps your reputation clean.

Practical Reference: Event Day Coordination and Team Management

In the African event services business, the difference between a profitable event and a loss-making one is rarely the dÃ©cor or the menu. It is coordination on the day. A wedding in Nairobi with 300 guests, a corporate launch in Lagos, or a traditional ceremony in Accra all depend on one thing: a team that knows what to do, when to do it, and who to call when something goes wrong. This Practical Reference gives you the operational tools to run event day like a business â protecting your reputation, your equipment, and your margin.

Event day is where your planning meets reality. Vendors arrive late, helpers get confused, guests change plans, and the schedule slips. The businesses that survive and grow are those with simple systems: a written timeline, assigned roles, clear communication channels, and a fast problem-solving routine. Below you will find the practical structures used by working event companies across Africa, adapted for teams of 3 to 30 people.

1. Creating a Simple Timeline for the Event Day

Your timeline is the backbone of event day. It does not need to be complicated. A one-page document with times, tasks, and responsible persons is enough. The key is that everyone â your team, your vendors, and the client â works from the same version.

Build your timeline backwards from the event start time. If the ceremony begins at 2:00 PM, then decoration must be complete by 12:00 PM, catering set up by 12:30 PM, sound check by 1:00 PM, and ushers briefed by 1:15 PM. Add a 30-minute buffer before the guest arrival window. In African cities where traffic can double travel time, buffers are not optional â they are insurance.

2. Assigning Roles to Helpers

Helpers without clear roles become spectators. Every person on your team â whether paid staff or casual labour â must know three things: their station, their task, and who to report to. In the African event context, where teams are often assembled per-event, this is even more critical.

Use a simple role card system. Each helper gets a card (or a WhatsApp message) with their name, role, reporting time, station, and the name of their supervisor. Colour-code the cards: green for ushers, blue for servers, orange for setup crew, red for supervisors.

3. Communicating with Vendors Using Phone Calls and WhatsApp Groups

In Africa, WhatsApp is the operational backbone of event coordination. But WhatsApp groups without rules become noise. Set up two groups: one for your internal team (coordination) and one for vendors (logistics). Keep the vendor group strictly for arrival times, location pins, and payment confirmations. Move detailed discussions to phone calls.

Before event day, call every vendor. Confirm three things: arrival time, contact person on the day, and what they are bringing. Send a written summary on WhatsApp after the call. On event day, use voice notes for complex instructions and text for simple confirmations. If a vendor is late, call â do not wait for a WhatsApp reply.

For payments, use mobile money (M-Pesa, MTN MoMo, Airtel Money) for deposits and balances. Confirm receipt on WhatsApp with a screenshot. This creates a paper trail that protects both parties.

Never send large cash amounts with a helper who is not a trusted supervisor. In many African cities, event-day cash theft and robbery are real risks. Use mobile money or bank transfers for all vendor payments above a small float. Keep the float (for emergency purchases like extra ice, tape, or fuel) in a locked bag with a single accountable person. Do not announce on WhatsApp how much cash you are carrying or where it is kept. If you must carry cash, vary your route and avoid sharing arrival times publicly.

4. Solving Common Problems

Late deliveries: Call the vendor immediately. Ask for a realistic ETA. If the delay threatens the timeline, activate your backup: borrow from another vendor, use what you have, or adjust the schedule. Communicate the change to the client before they notice.

Missing items: Keep a master inventory list. Assign one person to check items against the list at setup and again at pack-down. If something is missing, check the truck, the storage area, and with the setup crew before assuming theft. For rented items, missing pieces mean deductions from your deposit â so count carefully.

Guest issues: Common problems include seating conflicts, dietary needs, and intoxicated guests. Ushers should never argue with a guest. Escalate to a supervisor immediately. The supervisor's job is to solve the problem quietly and keep the guest comfortable. Never discuss guest issues on the vendor WhatsApp group.

5. Keeping the Event on Schedule Without Rushing Guests

The schedule serves the guest experience, not the other way around. If guests are enjoying themselves, a 15-minute delay to the cake cutting is not a crisis. What matters is that your team knows the sequence and can adapt. Use a "pacekeeper" â one supervisor whose only job is to watch the clock and signal the MC or coordinator when a segment is running long.

In African events, speeches and cultural performances often run longer than planned. Build this into your timeline. If you know speeches average 45 minutes instead of 30, schedule 45. The goal is not a rigid schedule â it is a realistic one that keeps the event flowing without making guests feel pushed.

6. Packing Down and Returning Rented Items Efficiently

Pack-down is where businesses lose money. Damaged items, missing rentals, and overtime labour costs eat into your margin. Create a pack-down sequence: first, clear guests and secure the venue; second, collect all rented items and count them against the inventory list; third, pack in reverse order of setup (last in, first out); fourth, load and secure the truck; fifth, return rentals the same day or first thing the next morning to avoid extra charges.

Assign one person as the pack-down lead. They hold the inventory list and sign off on every item. Take photos of returned items with the rental company's representative â this protects you from false damage claims.
