1. Understanding Pigment Volume Concentration (PVC)

Pigment Volume Concentration (PVC) is the ratio of pigment volume to the total volume of pigment plus binder in a paint formula. It is expressed as a percentage. For example, if you have 30 litres of pigment and 70 litres of binder, the PVC is 30%. This number controls critical paint properties: gloss, durability, scrub resistance, and hiding power. In Africa, where raw materials like titanium dioxide (TiO2) are expensive, understanding PVC helps you reduce cost without ruining quality. A common mistake is to add too much cheap filler (like calcium carbonate) and too little binder, pushing PVC above the critical point. The critical PVC (CPVC) is the point where binder just barely fills the gaps between pigment particles. Above CPVC, the paint becomes porous, chalky, and fails quickly in harsh sun and rain.

To calculate PVC, you need the density and volume of each ingredient. Use a simple bucket and scale: measure the volume of pigment (e.g., 10 litres of TiO2 and 20 litres of talc) and the volume of binder (e.g., 70 litres of acrylic emulsion). Total pigment volume = 30 L, total volume = 100 L, so PVC = 30%. For a typical exterior wall paint in Africa, aim for PVC between 45% and 55% for matt finishes, and 20% to 30% for gloss. Interior emulsion often uses 50-60% PVC. If you are making a low-cost ceiling paint, PVC can go up to 70%, but expect shorter life. Always test a small batch: brush it on a concrete block, let it dry in the sun for 3 days, then rub with a wet cloth. If pigment rubs off, your PVC is too high.

Practical tip: Keep a record book. For each 20-litre bucket of paint, write down the volume of pigment, binder, and water. After a few batches, you will see how PVC affects coverage. In Nigeria, many small factories use local clay as extender; clay has low oil absorption, so it allows higher PVC without cracking. In Kenya, use diatomaceous earth for matt finishes. Remember: PVC is not just a numberâit is the balance between cost and performance. Start with a proven recipe, then adjust PVC by 5% up or down and observe the change. That is how you learn.

2. Calculating Binder Demand and Formulation Ratios

Binder demand is the amount of binder (resin or emulsion) needed to coat all pigment particles and fill the voids between them. If you use too little binder, the paint is weak and powdery. Too much binder, and the paint is glossy but expensive and may sag. In Africa, where acrylic and alkyd resins are imported and costly, calculating binder demand precisely saves money. The formula is: Binder demand (litres) = (Pigment surface area Ã Oil absorption) / Binder efficiency. But for a small producer, a simpler method is the 'binder-to-pigment ratio' by weight. For exterior emulsion, use 1 part binder to 2 parts pigment (1:2). For interior matt, 1:3. For gloss enamel, 1:1.5. These ratios work for most local pigments.

To calculate for a 100 kg batch: if you want a 1:2 ratio, you need 33 kg binder and 67 kg pigment. But pigments differ. Titanium dioxide (TiO2) has high oil absorption (about 20 g oil per 100 g pigment), while calcium carbonate has low (about 15 g). So if you replace TiO2 with calcium carbonate, you need less binder. Weigh your pigments, then use a simple test: put 100 g pigment in a cup, add linseed oil drop by drop until it forms a paste. The millilitres of oil used is the oil absorption. Multiply by the pigment weight to get total oil demand. Then divide by the binder's solid content (e.g., 50% for acrylic emulsion) to get the wet binder needed. For example, 100 kg pigment with oil absorption 20 mL/100g needs 20 L oil equivalent. If binder is 50% solids, you need 40 L wet binder.

In practice, many African formulators use a 'let-down' method: make a pigment paste with part of the binder, then add the rest. This ensures good dispersion. Keep a notebook with ratios for each product: wall paint, wood varnish, metal primer. Test each batch by applying on a metal plate and bending itâif it cracks, increase binder by 10%. In Ghana, cassava starch is sometimes used as a binder extender, but it reduces water resistance. Always use the minimum binder that gives a flexible, adherent film. That is your true binder demand.

3. Solvent Balance and Evaporation Rates

Solvents are volatile liquids that dissolve the binder and control drying. In water-based paints, the solvent is water, but co-solvents like glycols are added to slow evaporation and improve film formation. In solvent-based paints, you use mineral turpentine, xylene, or acetone. The key is to balance fast, medium, and slow evaporating solvents. If all solvent evaporates too fast, the paint dries before it levels, leaving brush marks. If too slow, the paint sags and dust sticks. In hot African climates, evaporation is rapid, so you need more slow solvent. A typical enamel formula uses 50% fast (acetone), 30% medium (xylene), and 20% slow (butyl glycol). For water-based emulsion, add 2-5% propylene glycol to prevent skinning in the bucket and improve open time.

Evaporation rate is measured relative to butyl acetate (rate = 1.0). Acetone has rate 5.6 (very fast), xylene 0.6, butyl glycol 0.1 (slow). To calculate the blend, use the formula: Blend rate = (fraction A Ã rate A) + (fraction B Ã rate B) + ... For a 100 L batch, if you want a blend rate of 1.0, mix 20 L acetone (0.2Ã5.6=1.12), 50 L xylene (0.5Ã0.6=0.3), and 30 L butyl glycol (0.3Ã0.1=0.03). Total = 1.45, too fast. Adjust by adding more slow solvent. In practice, test on a glass plate: draw a line with a brush and watch how long it takes to become touch-dry. In Lagos, at 35Â°C, a paint that dries in 30 minutes may dry in 10 minutes if you use too much acetone. Add 5% butyl glycol to extend to 20 minutes.

For water-based paints, water evaporates slowly, but humidity matters. In coastal regions like Mombasa, high humidity slows drying; add 2% methanol as a fast co-solvent. In dry Sahel, add 3% glycol to prevent too-fast drying. Always store solvents in sealed drums away from sun. Use a hydrometer to check specific gravity, which indicates solvent purity. Never mix solvents without calculating the blend rate. A simple rule: for brush application, aim for a blend rate between 0.8 and 1.2. For spray, 1.5 to 2.0. Keep a chart of evaporation rates for your local solvents. That is your compass.

4. Additive Dosage and Compatibility

Additives are small amounts of chemicals that fix problems: thickeners, dispersants, defoamers, biocides, and driers. Dosage is usually 0.1% to 2% of total weight. Too little does nothing; too much ruins the paint. In Africa, where water quality varies, additives are critical. For example, a dispersant like sodium polyacrylate at 0.5% helps pigment particles separate and prevents settling. Without it, your paint becomes a hard cake in the bucket. A thickener like hydroxyethyl cellulose (HEC) at 0.3-0.8% controls viscosity. If you add too much, the paint gels and cannot be brushed. Add slowly while stirringânever dump.

Compatibility is the biggest headache. Some additives react with each other. For instance, anionic dispersants (like polyacrylates) are incompatible with cationic biocides (like quaternary ammonium). They form a precipitate that looks like curdled milk. Always check the ionic charge: most water-based paints use anionic systems. Use non-ionic or anionic additives. For solvent-based paints, dispersants like lecithin work well with alkyds. Defoamers (silicone or mineral oil) must be added at 0.1-0.3%; more causes fish eyes. Biocides: in hot, humid Africa, use 0.2% isothiazolinone to prevent bacteria and mold. But if you add too much, it can irritate skin.

Practical method: Make a small 1-litre batch. Add half the recommended dose, mix for 10 minutes, then check. If foam persists, add another 0.1% defoamer. If paint is too thin, add 0.2% thickener. Test compatibility by mixing a few drops of each additive in a glass of waterâif it turns cloudy or forms lumps, do not use together. In Kenya, many formulators use local cassava starch as a thickener, but it ferments; add 0.1% formaldehyde as a preservative (handle with care). Always keep a log: additive name, supplier, dose, result. After 10 batches, you will know your system. Never trust a new additive without a jar test. That is how you avoid a 200-litre batch turning to waste.

5. Formulating for Specific Substrates

Different surfaces need different paint formulas. In Africa, common substrates are concrete, mud brick, wood, metal, and previously painted surfaces. For new concrete, the high alkalinity (pH 12-13) attacks alkyd resins. So use acrylic or vinyl emulsion. A typical concrete wall paint: 30% acrylic binder, 40% pigment (TiO2 + calcium carbonate), 25% water, 5% additives. Add 1% alkali-resistant primer first. For mud brick, which is porous and dusty, use a penetrating primer (diluted acrylic 1:3 with water) then a matt emulsion with high PVC (60%) to allow breathing. If you use gloss on mud, moisture trapped inside will blister the paint.

Wood swells and shrinks with humidity. Use flexible alkyd or polyurethane. A wood varnish: 50% alkyd resin, 30% solvent (xylene), 15% pigment (optional), 5% additives (drier, UV absorber). For exterior wood in rainy regions, add 2% iron oxide for UV protection. Metal needs rust prevention. First, a primer with zinc phosphate (5% by weight) and alkyd binder. Then a topcoat with 40% alkyd, 30% TiO2, 20% solvent, 10% additives. For galvanised metal, use a wash primer with 10% phosphoric acid to etch the surface. In coastal areas, add 2% anti-corrosive pigment.

Previously painted surfaces: if old paint is peeling, remove it. If it is sound, sand and apply a bonding primer. For glossy surfaces, add 5% sanding sealer or use a high-solvent primer to bite in. In Nigeria, many walls are painted with distemper (chalk and glue). You cannot paint emulsion over distemperâit will flake. First, wash off distemper with water, then apply a stabilising primer. Always test a small area: paint a 1m x 1m patch, wait 7 days, then scratch with a coin. If it peels, reformulate. For roofs (metal sheets), use a reflective paint with 20% TiO2 and 10% ceramic microspheres to reduce heat. That is how you formulate for Africa's sun and rain.

6. Computer-Aided Formulation Software

Computer-aided formulation (CAF) software helps you calculate recipes, predict properties, and reduce trial-and-error. For small African paint producers, free or low-cost tools exist. Start with a spreadsheet like Microsoft Excel or Google Sheets. You can build a simple formulator: columns for ingredient name, density, cost per kg, volume, PVC, and binder demand. Use formulas to calculate total PVC, cost per litre, and VOC. For example, if you change TiO2 from 20 kg to 15 kg, the spreadsheet instantly shows the new PVC and cost. This is faster than hand calculations and avoids mistakes.

More advanced software includes 'Paint Formulator' (free trial) and 'Formulator' by Chempute. These allow you to input pigment oil absorption, binder solids, and solvent evaporation rates, then optimise for target gloss or scrub resistance. They also flag incompatibilities. For a small factory in Africa, a laptop and a generator are enough. You do not need internet. Download the software at a cybercafÃ© and install it. Use it to create a database of your local raw materials: titanium dioxide from China, calcium carbonate from Tanzania, acrylic emulsion from South Africa. Enter their real densities and oil absorption values.

Practical steps: Start with a known recipe. Enter all ingredients. Run a 'what-if' analysis: reduce binder by 5%, see how PVC changes. The software will warn if PVC exceeds CPVC. Use the 'cost optimisation' feature to find the cheapest formula that still meets your quality target. For example, replace 10% TiO2 with 10% kaolin clayâsoftware shows the hiding power drop. Then test in the lab. Keep digital records of every batch. After 6 months, you will have a library of successful formulas. In Kenya, some training centres teach CAF using open-source software like GNU Octave. Even a simple spreadsheet can save you 20% on raw materials. That is the power of computer-aided formulation: it turns guesswork into science.

Practical Reference: Paint Formulation Principles for African Paint Manufacturers

This practical reference consolidates the core formulation principles covered in this module into a working desk guide for production managers, formulators, and technical sales staff in African paint plants. It is written for the realities of our market: variable raw material supply, high pigment costs, humidity extremes, and customers who judge paint by coverage, durability, and price per square metre â not by laboratory elegance.

Whether you are running a 500-litre batch mixer in Kano, a 5,000-litre plant in Nairobi, or importing base concentrates in Abidjan, the same six levers determine whether your paint performs and whether you make money: PVC, binder demand, solvent balance, additive dosage, substrate matching, and formulation software discipline.

1. Understanding PVC in Practice

PVC is the volume of pigment divided by the total volume of pigment plus binder, expressed as a percentage. It is the single most powerful cost lever in your formulation. In African markets, titanium dioxide (TiOâ) and binder resin are typically the two most expensive inputs â often 40â60% of raw material cost. Moving PVC from 45% to 60% can cut binder cost by 20â30% while sacrificing gloss and exterior durability.

Every formulator must know the Critical Pigment Volume Concentration (CPVC) of their system. Below CPVC, pigment particles are fully wetted by binder and the film is continuous. Above CPVC, binder is insufficient, voids form, and the film becomes chalky, porous, and prone to blistering. For African exterior walls exposed to intense UV and monsoon or harmattan conditions, never exceed CPVC minus 5% for exterior grades.

In Africa, where many formulators substitute expensive TiOâ with local extenders like calcium carbonate (from Nigeria, Egypt, Kenya), kaolin, talc, and silica, this calculation is decisive.

3. Solvent Balance and Evaporation Rates

For solvent-borne paints â still dominant in wood finishes, metal primers, and automotive refinish across Africa â solvent selection controls open time, sag resistance, and film formation. In hot, dry climates (Sahel, Kalahari, North Africa), fast evaporating solvents cause dry spray and poor flow. In humid coastal markets (Lagos, Mombasa, Durban), slow solvents trap moisture and cause blushing.

Use the evaporation rate scale (n-butyl acetate = 1.0): acetone 6.3, ethyl acetate 4.2, MEK 3.8, toluene 2.0, xylene 0.6, butyl glycol 0.08. Blend fast, medium, and slow solvents so that the last 20% of solvent evaporates over 30â60 minutes. A practical African exterior alkyd uses xylene:white spirit at 60:40 with 2% butyl glycol as a flow agent.

4. Additive Dosage and Compatibility

Additives are 1â3% of formulation cost but cause 80% of production failures. Key rules:

  • Dispersants: 0.3â1.0% on pigment weight for TiOâ; 0.5â2.0% for extenders. Overdosing causes foam and water sensitivity.
  • Defoamers: 0.1â0.5%. Add in two stages â during grind and let-down.
  • Biocides: 0.1â0.3%. Non-negotiable in tropical climates; without them, emulsions fail within weeks.
  • Rheology modifiers (HEC/HEUR): 0.2â0.8% for sag and spatter control.
  • Driers (for alkyds): 0.02â0.06% metal on binder solids; cobalt/zirconium/calcium synergy.

Always run a compatibility jar test before scaling: mix additive with your actual binder and pigment paste at production ratios, observe for 24 hours for seeding, gelling, or phase separation.

Solvent vapours (xylene, toluene, MEK) are flammable and neurotoxic. Biocides (isothiazolinones, formaldehyde donors) cause severe skin sensitisation. Pigment dusts â especially crystalline silica and cobalt driers â are respiratory hazards. In African plants with limited ventilation, this is a daily risk.

  • Install explosion-proof extraction and earthing on all solvent handling. No open flames within 15 m.
  • Provide nitrile gloves, splash goggles, and organic vapour respirators (A2/P2).
  • Store biocides in locked, labelled cabinets; never decant into unlabelled bottles.
  • Fit dust extraction on grinding and powder weighing stations.
  • Train all staff on GHS labels and keep safety data sheets (SDS) accessible in English, French, or local language.

5. Formulating for Specific Substrates

African substrates are unforgiving. Formulation must match the surface, climate, and application method.

  • Cement / plaster: Alkaline (pH 10â13) and porous. Use alkali-resistant acrylic or styrene-acrylic binders; avoid alkyds (saponification). Prime with a diluted alkali-resistant primer.
  • Steel: Rust kills paint from below. Use zinc phosphate or zinc-rich primers at 40â50% PVC, topcoat with alkyd or acrylic at 25â35% PVC.
  • Wood: Dimensional movement demands flexible binders (alkyd or acrylic). Moisture content must be below 15% before coating.
  • Concrete floors: Use epoxy or polyurethane at 20â30% PVC; check moisture with calcium chloride test before application.
  • Previously painted surfaces: Test compatibility with a small patch; alkyd over emulsion will fail.
