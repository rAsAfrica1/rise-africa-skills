1. Setting up an attractive market stall

A good stall catches the eye from 20 metres away. Start with a clean, level spot. If you sell tomatoes, you need at least 2 square metres of ground. Use a wooden table or a sturdy box covered with a clean cloth. A bright clothâyellow, blue, or greenâmakes your produce pop. In many African markets, a simple wooden frame with a tarpaulin roof costs about 5,000 to 15,000 shillings (or equivalent) and lasts for months. Build the roof high enough to shade your produce but low enough to block the midday sun. Shade keeps leafy greens from wilting and tomatoes from splitting.

Arrange your stall in zones. Put heavy items like sacks of maize or beans at the back. Put your best-looking produce at the front, at waist height. That is where buyers look first. Use baskets or trays to lift produce off the groundâthis stops mud and dust. If you sell multiple items, group them: all fruits together, all vegetables together. Never mix onions with ripe mangoes because the smell transfers. A small mirror placed behind your stall lets you see customers approaching from behind while you serve someone in front.

Lighting matters. If you sell in the evening, a solar lamp or a rechargeable torch hung above your stall makes your produce visible. A stall that is dark after 6 pm loses sales. Also, keep a small stool for yourself. Standing all day tires you, and tired sellers make mistakes with money. Finally, have a small bin or bag for rubbish. A clean stall tells customers you care about quality. Sweep your area before you leave each day. The next morning, you will set up faster because your spot is already tidy.

2. Displaying produce to attract buyers

Display is not decorationâit is sales. A pyramid of oranges sells twice as fast as a flat pile. Why? Because a pyramid shows abundance and lets customers see every fruit. Build pyramids on a tray or a low box. Start with a base of 4 by 4 fruits, then 3 by 3, then 2 by 2, then one on top. For tomatoes, use a shallow basket and arrange them in a single layer, stem-side up. Never pile tomatoes more than two deepâthe bottom ones crush and rot. For leafy greens like sukuma wiki or spinach, tie them in small bundles of 5 to 7 leaves using banana fibre or rubber bands. Stand the bundles upright in a container with 2 cm of water. They stay fresh for hours.

Colour contrast sells. Place green peppers next to red tomatoes. Put yellow bananas beside purple eggplant. The human eye notices contrast. If you sell only one colour, add a coloured cloth underneath. A white cloth under green produce makes the green look brighter. Change your display every 2 hours. Move the front items to the back and bring fresh ones forward. This keeps the display looking full and avoids bruising. Remove any bruised or rotting item immediatelyâone bad fruit makes customers think the whole stall is old.

Use height. A small wooden box under a tray lifts produce to chest level. Customers do not like bending down. If you sell from the ground, you lose sales to taller stalls. Also, use signs. Write prices on a piece of cardboard with a marker. For example: 'Tomatoes 3 for 100 KES' or 'Onions 1 kg = 80 KES'. Clear prices reduce haggling and build trust. Finally, keep a small spray bottle of water for leafy greens. A light mist every hour makes them look fresh. But do not spray tomatoes or onionsâmoisture makes them rot faster.

3. Handling cash and giving change safely

Cash handling is where many market sellers lose money. Start with a cash boxâa small metal box with a lock, or even a thick plastic container with a lid. Keep it under your table, not on top. Never keep all your money in one pocket. Divide your cash: a small amount for giving change (float) in your apron, and the rest in the locked box. At the start of the day, count your float. For example, if you sell tomatoes at 50 KES each, you need at least 500 KES in coins and small notes (10s, 20s, 50s) to give change. Count it out loud: '10, 20, 30...' so you remember.

When a customer pays, say the amount out loud. 'You gave me 200 shillings.' Then count the change back into their hand, not on the table. Count it aloud: '50, 100, 150, 200.' This prevents arguments. If you are busy, ask the customer to wait while you finish the current sale. Never rush. Rushing leads to giving too much change. If a customer gives you a large note, check it against a known genuine note. In many countries, fake 1000-shilling notes are common. Feel the paper, look at the watermark, and tilt it. If you are unsure, ask another seller or refuse politely: 'Sorry, I cannot accept this note. Do you have another?'

At the end of the day, reconcile your sales. Count all the money in your box and apron. Subtract your starting float. The difference is your sales. Write it down in a notebook. If the amount is less than you expected, check your change-giving. A common mistake is giving change for 100 when the customer gave 50. To avoid this, keep small notes separate from large ones. Also, never leave your cash box unattended. If you must step away, ask a trusted neighbour to watch your stall, or pack up and take your money with you. Safety first.

4. Dealing with price haggling politely

Haggling is normal in African markets. Do not fear itâmanage it. First, set a fair price. Know your cost: seeds, transport, market fee, your time. Add a reasonable profit. For example, if a bucket of tomatoes costs you 800 KES and you sell 20 tomatoes per bucket, your cost per tomato is 40 KES. Add 20 KES profit, so your asking price is 60 KES. But your minimum price (the lowest you will go) is 50 KES. Never go below 50. Write your minimum price on a small card and keep it in your pocket. When a customer offers 30 KES, you smile and say, 'Sorry, my friend, 60 is the price. But if you buy 3, I can do 160.' That is a bundle discount, not a price cut.

Use polite phrases. In Swahili, 'Bei ni 60, lakini kwa wewe nitakupa 55' (The price is 60, but for you I will give 55). This makes the customer feel special. If they still push, offer a small extra: 'I will add one extra tomato.' That costs you less than cutting the price. Never argue or insult. If a customer is rude, stay calm. Say, 'I understand, but this is my best price.' If they walk away, let them. Another customer will come. Do not chase themâit looks desperate.

Know when to hold firm. If you sell a unique productâlike fresh strawberries out of seasonâyou can keep your price. If you sell the same tomatoes as 10 other stalls, you must be competitive. Check nearby prices each morning. If everyone sells at 50 KES, you cannot ask 80. But you can compete on quality: bigger tomatoes, cleaner stall, friendlier service. Also, use a 'take-it-or-leave-it' price for small items. For a bunch of greens, say '10 KES each, no discount.' That saves time. Finally, always thank the customer, even if they do not buy. 'Thank you, come again.' They might return tomorrow.

5. Building relationships with regular customers

A regular customer is worth 10 one-time buyers. They come back weekly, they bring friends, and they do not haggle as hard. To build regulars, start by remembering names and faces. If you cannot remember names, remember what they buy. 'Ah, you are the lady who always buys 2 kg of onions.' That personal touch makes them feel valued. Keep a small notebook and write down regular customers' names and usual purchases. For example: 'Mama Aisha â 1 kg tomatoes, 2 bunches sukuma, every Tuesday.' Next time she comes, greet her by name and have her order ready. That saves her time and builds loyalty.

Offer small rewards. After 10 purchases, give a free bunch of greens or a discount of 10 KES. You can use a simple card with 10 boxes; punch a hole each time they buy. When all boxes are punched, they get a reward. This costs you very little but keeps them coming back. Also, give credit carefully. If a trusted regular is short of cash, you can say, 'Pay me next time.' But only for people you know well. Write the amount in your notebook. Never give credit to strangers.

Be consistent. Open your stall at the same time every day. If you are always there by 7 am, customers will plan around you. If you close early, they will find another seller. Also, keep your prices stable for regulars. If you raise prices for everyone, tell your regulars first: 'My friend, tomatoes are expensive this week, so I have to ask 65 instead of 60.' They will understand if you explain. Finally, ask for feedback. 'Is the produce fresh enough? Would you like me to stock something else?' This shows you care. Happy regulars will defend your stall if someone tries to cheat you. They become your best marketing.

6. Keeping records of daily sales

If you do not write it down, you will not know if you are making a profit. Use a simple exercise book or a notebook. At the top of each page, write the date. Then make 4 columns: Item, Quantity, Price per unit, Total. For example: 'Tomatoes, 10 kg, 80 KES/kg, 800 KES.' At the end of the day, add up the Total column. That is your gross sales. Also, write down your expenses: transport, market fee, food, and any money you took out for personal use. Subtract expenses from gross sales to get your net profit. If your gross is 2,500 KES and expenses are 1,200 KES, your profit is 1,300 KES. Do this every day.

At the end of the week, review your records. Which item sold best? Which day had the highest sales? For example, you might find that you sell more tomatoes on Saturday and more greens on Wednesday. Then you can buy more of what sells on those days. Also, track your prices. If you sold tomatoes at 80 KES last week and 70 KES this week, write down why: 'More sellers today' or 'Rainy day, fewer customers.' This helps you set prices next week.

Keep your records safe. A plastic bag or a tin box protects the notebook from rain and dust. Do not leave it on the stall where it can be stolen. At the end of the month, add up all your profits. If you are not making a profit, check your costs. Maybe you are buying too much and throwing away spoiled produce. Or maybe your prices are too low. Use your records to make decisions. For example, if you see that you always throw away 2 kg of tomatoes, buy 2 kg less next time. Finally, show your records to a trusted friend or family member. They might spot a mistake or give advice. Good records turn a market stall into a real business.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Selling at Local Markets and Roadside Stalls

Selling at a local market or roadside stall is one of the most accessible routes to market for smallholder farmers and small-scale agro-processors across Africa. But access alone does not guarantee profit. The difference between a stall that moves stock quickly at a fair margin and one that sits half-empty by midday usually comes down to six practical disciplines: stall presentation, produce display, cash handling, price negotiation, customer relationships, and record keeping. This reference lesson consolidates those disciplines into a single working guide you can apply from your next market day.

Treat your stall as a small retail business, not a table of surplus. Buyers â whether a mother buying tomatoes for the evening meal, a restaurant cook sourcing peppers, or a trader bulking for resale â make split-second judgements about freshness, cleanliness, price fairness, and trustworthiness. Every element below is designed to improve those judgements in your favour and to convert one-off buyers into repeat customers.

1. Setting Up an Attractive Market Stall

Location and visibility matter more than decoration. Arrive early to claim a position with good foot traffic, shade where possible, and a clear sightline from the main path. Use a raised table or crates so produce is not on the ground â this reduces spoilage, keeps produce clean, and lifts goods closer to the buyer's eye. A simple shade net or umbrella protects leafy greens from wilting and protects you from heat fatigue, which directly affects how well you negotiate and count change.

Group your stall into clear zones: a "hero" display at the front for your best-looking, highest-margin item; a mid-table zone for staples; and a back zone for bulk or backstock. Keep a small waste bag out of sight and a clean cloth for wiping surfaces. In many markets, a tidy stall signals that you handle money and food hygienically â a decisive advantage when institutional buyers such as school kitchens or small hotels are scouting suppliers.

2. Displaying Produce to Attract Buyers

Display is free advertising. Build height and colour contrast: stack tomatoes in a pyramid, fan out leafy greens, and place contrasting colours side by side (green peppers next to red tomatoes). Face the "best side" of every item toward the buyer. Never hide bruised produce at the bottom of a pile â it destroys trust when discovered and damages repeat business.

Use clean containers and avoid overfilling to the point where produce is crushed. For grains and legumes, open one sample bowl so buyers can inspect quality, and keep the bulk sack tied. For perishables, sell in small, uniform units (small bowls, tied bunches, or pre-weighed bags) so buyers can compare value instantly and you can price consistently without a scale for every transaction.

3. Handling Cash and Giving Change Safely

Cash handling is where small traders lose the most money â through errors, theft, and "mystery shortages" at the end of the day. Follow three rules. First, keep a fixed change float (a starting amount of small notes and coins) and never spend from it mid-day. Second, separate denominations: use a cash box or a divided pouch so you are not searching through a bundle while a queue builds. Third, count change aloud back to the customer before handing it over â this prevents disputes and deters short-change tricks.

Where mobile money is available, offer it. M-Pesa, MTN MoMo, Airtel Money and similar services reduce cash on your person, create a transaction record, and appeal to younger urban buyers. Display your till or merchant number clearly. Where cash is unavoidable, avoid displaying large notes, bank at the end of the day rather than carrying cash home, and never count money openly in a crowded area.

â  SAFETY WARNING â Cash and Personal Security

Do not carry large sums of cash home from the market. Robbery and theft at markets and on routes home are common risks. Bank or deposit earnings the same day where possible, use mobile money to reduce cash on hand, vary your route and timing, and avoid counting money in public. Never leave your cash box unattended, even briefly. If you trade with others, agree a witness system for large cash transactions.

4. Dealing with Price Haggling Politely

Haggling is normal and is not an insult â it is part of the market culture. The mistake is to react emotionally or to drop price too quickly, which trains buyers to expect discounts and erodes your margin. Set a firm "floor price" before you arrive (the lowest price at which the sale is still profitable) and a "target price" you open with. When a buyer pushes, respond with value, not just price: "This price is for the best grade â I can offer you the second grade at a lower price."

Useful phrases: "That is my best price for one, but for three I can do X." "I can meet you at X because you are a regular." Bundle offers (buy 3, get a small discount) protect unit price while giving the buyer a win. Stay calm, smile, and never argue in front of other customers â it drives away the queue.

5. Building Relationships with Regular Customers

In African markets, trust and reciprocity drive repeat business more than price alone. Learn names, remember preferences ("you always take the small bunch"), and reserve good stock for known buyers. Offer a small loyalty gesture â an extra tomato, priority on fresh arrivals, or a heads-up when a preferred item is in season. For institutional buyers (restaurants, schools, small hotels), reliability beats price: consistent quality, consistent quantity, and consistent timing will win contracts that a cheaper but erratic supplier will lose.

Keep a simple customer list with contact numbers so you can notify buyers of new stock or take pre-orders. Pre-orders reduce waste and improve cash flow because you buy or harvest to confirmed demand.

6. Keeping Records of Daily Sales

Records turn a market stall into a business. You cannot price well, restock intelligently, or prove creditworthiness without them. At minimum, record for each market day: opening stock, quantities sold, unit prices, total sales, expenses (transport, stall fee, ice, packaging), cash on hand at close, and any credit given. A small notebook is enough; a phone spreadsheet is better if you have the skills.

Review weekly. Which items sell out fastest? Which have the best margin? Which days are strongest? Use the answers to change what you buy, how much you buy, and what you charge.
