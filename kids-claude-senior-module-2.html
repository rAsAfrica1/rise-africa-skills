Multi-Step Reasoning - Break Big Jobs into Small Ones

~14 min read20 min gameIntermediate

🧠 Brain Opener

Ask Claude to plan a whole school event and you get general advice. Ask Claude to plan the first hour of the event and you get something usable. The bigger the request, the worse the output. The answer is to break it up.

  • Why does a big request produce a generic answer?
  • What is the smallest useful unit of a big job?
  • How do you decide what order to do the steps in?
🎁 Reveal a good answer

A big request has too many unknowns for the AI to focus on. Small requests have specific context, so the AI can be specific. The smallest useful unit is one decision - one choice that leads to the next step. You order the steps by dependency: what must happen before what else can begin.

📖 Rudo Plans the School Event

Rudo, fourteen, was put in charge of the school end-of-year party. Thirty students, two teachers, a budget of eighty dollars, and four weeks of preparation time.

She opened Claude and typed: "Plan a school end-of-year party."

Claude gave her a two-page response. Balloons. Snacks. Music. Games. Invitations. A schedule. It looked complete. It was useless.

"Snacks" for thirty people could be two hundred dollars or ten. "Music" could be a phone speaker or a live DJ. The response was a list of categories, not a plan.

Rudo tried again. This time she broke it down.

"I have thirty students aged 13 to 16. The budget is eighty dollars. The event is four weeks from today. What are the eight biggest decisions I must make, in order?"

Claude gave her: (1) venue, (2) date and time, (3) food plan, (4) drinks, (5) music, (6) activities, (7) invitations, (8) cleanup and budget check.

Then Rudo asked one question per decision. "What are three venue options for thirty students that are free?" "What is a realistic snack list for thirty people under twenty dollars?" "What is one game that works for thirty teenagers?"

Each answer was specific. Each answer was useful. By the end of one hour, Rudo had a real plan.

She wrote it on a single piece of paper and pinned it to her bedroom wall. Thirty students. Eighty dollars. Four weeks. One plan.

On the day of the party, everything ran. Two things went wrong. Both were on her plan as risks she had already thought about.

💡 The Big Idea

One decision at a time

A big job is a bundle of small decisions. The mistake is asking for the whole bundle at once. The skill is asking for one decision at a time, in order, and then combining the answers yourself. This is how professionals use AI on real work: they steer the loop, one decision at a time.

📚 Three Deep Lessons

1

Why big requests fail

When you ask a big question, the AI has to guess what you actually want. It does not know your budget, your age, your audience, your time. So it averages: it gives you the answer most people would want, which is the answer nobody specifically wants.

The fix is not to write longer prompts. It is to write narrower ones. One decision, full context, then move to the next decision. Small prompts with real context always beat large prompts with vague context.

2

The dependency chain

Steps are not independent. You cannot plan food before you know the venue, because the venue decides whether you can cook. You cannot plan music before you know the time, because daytime events and evening events need different music. Before you start, draw the dependency chain: which decisions must be made before which others.

The AI can help you draw this chain. Ask it: "What are the eight biggest decisions for this project, in order, and which ones block which others?" Use its answer as a starting point. Fix it where it does not fit your reality. Now you have a map.

3

One question at a time

For each decision, ask one specific question with full context. Not "what should I do for food" but "I have thirty 13-to-16-year-olds, a budget of twenty dollars for snacks, no kitchen access, and two hours. What are three specific snack options?" The answer will be specific because the question was specific.

Do this eight times and you have eight specific answers. Combine them and you have a plan. The AI never had to guess, because you never let it. This is the whole method.

🛠 Hands-On - Plan Something Real in Eight Questions

You need

  • A phone or laptop with Claude open
  • A notebook
  • A pen
  • One real project you actually need to plan
  1. Write down your real project - a party, a study plan, a fundraiser, a trip.
  2. Ask Claude for the eight biggest decisions, in order, with dependencies.
  3. Write the eight decisions in your notebook as a list.
  4. For each decision, ask Claude one specific question with full context.
  5. Write the best answer next to the decision in your notebook.
  6. Combine all eight into a one-page plan. Pin it up.
Why it works: Specific questions get specific answers. The eight-question method forces you to break the project down before you ask for help.

🎮 Game - Break the Big Job

2 to 8 players - 20 min

The teacher reads out a big job: "Plan a three-day trip for four people on a fifty-dollar budget." Every player has five minutes to write as many small decisions as they can. Then compare lists. The player with the most specific, correctly-ordered decisions wins. Vague decisions (food, transport) score zero. Specific ones (which route, which hostel, which meal) score one.

🎯 Cross-Skill Links

♞
Chess

Grandmasters do not think about "the game." They think about the next three moves and their dependencies. Every move is a small decision that assumes the previous ones. Chess is multi-step reasoning with a board.

🎹
Music and Piano

A symphony is a bundle of small phrases. Learn the phrases. Assemble them. A pianist who tries to play the whole piece at once will fail on every note.

🏴
Chinese

Yibu yibu means step by step. Yi means one, bu means step. One step, then the next. Every Chinese artisan learns this first.

🤖 AI-Era Skill

Multi-step reasoning is one of Claude's strongest abilities - if you use it correctly. Ask for one step at a time and you get depth. Ask for the whole thing and you get width.

Try this prompt: "I want to plan a three-day study schedule for four subjects, one hour per day, over ten days. What are the eight biggest scheduling decisions I need to make, in order, and which decisions depend on which others?"

Check the order of the decisions. Scheduling decisions usually start with: total days, subjects per day, rest days, review sessions, exam proximity. If the AI reversed the order, it got the dependencies wrong. Fix it.

🌍 Real Stories from Africa

M-KOPA solar in Kenya

M-KOPA sells solar home systems on a pay-as-you-go plan. To make it work, they had to break one big problem (how does a poor family afford solar) into dozens of small decisions: payment plan, credit check, shut-off rules, repair network, agent training. Each decision took months. Together, they serve over a million homes.

Addis Ababa light rail

Building the Addis Ababa light rail took years of decisions: routes, tunnels, power, stations, ticketing, safety. The city did not build it in one big move. They broke it into hundreds of small ones and did them in order.
