1. Sourcing and Preparing Local Scrap Steel in African Markets

Welcome to Module 4, Lesson 1 of the Yok and Pan Blacksmith training. In this lesson we focus on the single most important input for any blacksmith business: your raw material. In many African markets, new steel is expensive and often imported. The profitable blacksmith learns to see treasure in scrap. Your ability to source, sort, and prepare scrap steel determines your cost per finished piece, your product quality, and your reputation. We will cover where to find scrap, how to identify good steel, how to negotiate prices, and how to prepare it for the forge. First, know your local scrap streams. In most African towns and cities, scrap comes from several predictable sources. Auto repair garages and mechanic villages produce leaf springs, coil springs, axles, tie rods, and brake drums. These are often high-carbon or spring steel, excellent for tools, knives, and machetes. Construction sites produce rebar, angle iron, flat bar, and pipe offcuts. Rebar is low-carbon and good for decorative work, gates, and grills, but it will not hold a sharp edge. Market areas and dumpsites produce discarded furniture frames, bed frames, metal doors, roofing sheets, and appliance parts. Bed frames and angle iron are usually mild steel, good for structural and decorative pieces. Old files, rasps, and saw blades are high-carbon and make excellent knives, chisels, and punches. Vehicle suspension parts are among the best free steel in Africa. A single leaf spring can yield several knives or two to three hand tools. Coil springs from cars and trucks are great for punches, chisels, and small anvils. Axles are tough and can be used for hammers and anvil stakes. When you visit a mechanic village, build relationships. Talk to the mechanics, bring a small gift, and ask them to set aside springs and axles. Agree on a fair price per kilogram or per piece. In many places, scrap is sold by the kilogram at a fraction of new steel cost. Always carry a small magnet and a file. The magnet tells you if the metal is ferrous, which is what you want. The file tells you about hardness. If a file skips or slides over the metal, it is hard, likely high-carbon. If the file bites and cuts easily, it is mild steel. This simple test saves you from buying the wrong material. Sorting matters. Keep three piles: high-carbon for tools and knives, mild steel for structural and decorative work, and unknown or mixed steel for practice and non-critical items. Label or mark your piles. In a busy workshop, confusion costs time and money. Next, preparation. Scrap steel is often dirty, painted, rusted, or coated. Before forging, clean it. Remove paint and oil with a wire brush, sandpaper, or by burning it off in a fire. Do not breathe the fumes from burning paint, especially old paint that may contain lead. Work outdoors or in a well-ventilated area. Cut long pieces to manageable lengths using a hacksaw, angle grinder, or chisel. A piece that is too long is dangerous to handle at the forge. Forge welding and bending are easier when your stock is clean and sized. For high-carbon steel, you may need to anneal it first. Annealing means heating it to a dull red or orange and then letting it cool slowly, ideally in ash, sand, or vermiculite. This softens the steel so you can cut, file, and shape it. Without annealing, old files and springs can be too hard and brittle to work efficiently. Think about your product line before you source. If you make agricultural tools like hoes, machetes, and axes, focus on spring steel and old files. If you make household items like grills, stands, and door frames, focus on rebar, angle iron, and pipe. If you make decorative items like gates, railings, and furniture, focus on mild steel and flat bar. Knowing what you sell helps you buy the right scrap at the right price. Pricing is a business skill. In many African markets, scrap prices fluctuate. Ask several suppliers before you commit. Build a network of at least three reliable sources. Pay promptly and fairly. A mechanic who trusts you will save the best springs for you. Consider buying in bulk when prices are low and storing material in a dry place. Rust is not fatal, but it adds cleaning time. A simple rack or shelf keeps your stock organized and off the ground. Safety is non-negotiable. Wear gloves, eye protection, and sturdy boots when handling scrap. Watch for sharp edges, protruding nails, and hidden glass or plastic. Use a magnet to check for non-ferrous metals like aluminum and brass, which are not suitable for most blacksmith work. Keep a first aid kit nearby. Tetanus shots are recommended for anyone working with scrap metal. Finally, calculate your material cost per finished item. If a leaf spring costs you 5000 shillings and yields three knives, your material cost per knife is about 1667 shillings. Add labor, fuel, and consumables. This simple math tells you your minimum selling price and your profit margin. Many blacksmiths fail because they do not track material cost. Do not be one of them. In our next lesson, we will look at forge setup and fuel efficiency using local materials. For now, your assignment is to visit two scrap sources, collect at least five kilograms of suitable steel, sort it, and record what you paid. Bring your notes to the next session.

2. Forge Setup and Fuel Efficiency with Local Materials

Welcome to Module 4, Lesson 2. A blacksmith without a good forge is like a farmer without land. In this lesson we build a practical, low-cost forge using materials available in most African communities. We also focus on fuel efficiency, because fuel is one of your biggest ongoing costs. Whether you use charcoal, coal, or a gas forge, the principles are the same: concentrate heat, control air, and reduce waste. Let us start with the traditional charcoal forge. You need a firepot or hearth, a tuyere or air pipe, a blower or bellows, and a table or stand. The firepot can be made from a car brake drum, a section of thick pipe, or a clay-lined hole in the ground. A brake drum is excellent because it is shallow, wide, and heat-resistant. Set it on a metal stand or stones. Line the bottom with clay or fire cement to protect the metal and insulate the heat. The tuyere is a pipe that brings air into the fire. It can be made from black iron pipe, a car exhaust pipe, or even a length of galvanized pipe, though galvanized pipe releases toxic fumes when heated, so use it only for the air line, not the firepot. The tuyere should enter the firepot from the side, slightly above the bottom, angled downward. This keeps the air blast from blowing ash and coal out of the fire. For air supply, you have several options. A hand-cranked blower from an old grinding machine or a bicycle pump modified with a pipe can work. A vacuum cleaner in reverse, also called a blower mode, is common but noisy and uses electricity. A simple bellows made from leather or rubber and wood is quiet and requires no electricity. In rural areas, a double-chamber bellows made from two clay pots and a goatskin is traditional and effective. Whatever you choose, you must be able to control the air flow. Too much air wastes fuel and oxidizes the steel. Too little air gives you a cold fire. A simple gate valve or a piece of metal to block the pipe gives you control. Fuel choice matters. Charcoal is widely available in Africa and burns cleanly, but it is expensive in many cities and contributes to deforestation. If you use charcoal, buy from sustainable sources or make your own from agricultural waste. Coal is hotter and lasts longer, but it produces smoke and sulfur. Bituminous coal is best for forging. Anthracite is harder to light but burns clean. In many African countries, coal is imported and costly. Consider making charcoal from maize cobs, coconut shells, or rice husks. These burn well and are often free. A simple retort made from an oil drum can convert agricultural waste into charcoal. This reduces cost and environmental impact. For a gas forge, you need a burner, a forge body, and a propane or butane tank. Gas forges are clean, fast, and easy to control, but gas can be expensive and unavailable in some areas. A single-burner forge made from a freon tank or a length of pipe lined with ceramic fiber or firebrick works well. The burner can be a simple atmospheric burner made from black iron pipe and a mig tip. Gas forges are ideal for knife making and small tools. They are less suited for large structural work. Whichever forge you build, location is important. Set up outdoors or in a well-ventilated shed. Never forge in a closed room. Carbon monoxide from charcoal and coal is deadly. Keep a bucket of water and a fire extinguisher nearby. Clear the area of dry grass, paper, and flammable liquids. A forge fire can throw sparks several meters. Now, fuel efficiency. The biggest waste in African blacksmithies is poor fire management. Here are practical tips. First, keep your fire small and concentrated. A big fire wastes fuel and heats the air, not the steel. Second, only heat the part of the metal you are working. If you are making a knife tip, do not put the whole blade in the fire. Third, use a fire rake or poker to keep the fire open and the air passages clear. Fourth, quench and cool your work in a separate container, not in the forge water, to avoid steam and ash. Fifth, bank your fire when not in use. Cover the coals with ash or a metal lid to slow burning. Sixth, recycle heat. After forging, put your hot metal on a firebrick or in a sand box to anneal slowly. This saves fuel on the next heating cycle. Seventh, maintain your blower. A worn blower leaks air and wastes fuel. Oil it regularly and replace worn bearings. Eighth, consider a side-blast forge. In a side-blast forge, air enters from the side, and the fire sits in a trench. This design uses less fuel because the fire is deeper and hotter. It is common in West Africa and works well with charcoal. Ninth, use a hood or chimney to draw smoke away. This improves air quality and can also preheat incoming air if you build a simple heat exchanger. Tenth, track your fuel use. Write down how much charcoal or coal you use per day and per product. If you use one bag of charcoal to make ten knives, your fuel cost per knife is one-tenth of the bag price. This number helps you price your work. Let us talk about tools around the forge. You need tongs, hammers, a chisel, a punch, a hot cut, and an anvil or anvil substitute. A section of railway track makes an excellent anvil. A large hammer head or a piece of heavy steel plate also works. Mount your anvil at a comfortable height, about knuckle height when you stand. Your forge should be at a similar height so you can move metal easily. Keep your tools within reach. A simple tool rack on the wall saves time and prevents accidents. Water for quenching should be in a metal bucket or drum. Oil for quenching high-carbon steel should be in a separate container with a lid. Never use water on high-carbon steel if you want a hard edge, unless you are doing a specific interrupted quench. Oil is safer and gives better results for knives and tools. Finally, think about your business. A good forge lets you work faster and with less fuel. That means lower costs and higher profits. If you currently use an open fire, build a simple brake drum forge this week. If you already have a forge, measure your fuel use for one week. Then try one improvement, such as a smaller fire or a better blower, and measure again. Small savings add up. In the next lesson, we will cover hammer techniques and forging exercises that improve your speed and accuracy.

3. Hammer Technique, Forging Exercises, and Production Speed

Welcome to Module 4, Lesson 3. In this lesson we focus on the hammer, your most important tool. Good hammer technique saves fuel, reduces injury, and improves the quality of your work. We will cover stance, grip, types of blows, forging exercises, and how to increase production speed without sacrificing quality. First, stance. Stand close to the anvil with your feet shoulder-width apart. Your anvil should be at knuckle height. If it is too high, you will tire quickly. If it is too low, you will lose control. Keep your knees slightly bent and your back straight. Do not lock your elbows. Your body should be relaxed but ready. Your hammer hand is your dominant hand. Your other hand holds the tongs or the work. For heavy blows, you can use both hands on the hammer, but for most blacksmith work, one hand is enough. Grip the hammer near the end of the handle. A tight grip causes fatigue. A loose grip causes the hammer to slip. Find a grip that is firm but relaxed. Your thumb should wrap around the handle, not rest on top. The hammer should be an extension of your arm. Next, types of blows. The first is the forging blow. This is a heavy, deliberate blow that moves metal. You raise the hammer and let it fall, using gravity and your shoulder. Do not muscle the hammer down. Let the hammer do the work. The second is the drawing blow. This is a slightly angled blow that stretches metal. You strike with the face of the hammer at a slight angle, moving the metal away from you. The third is the planishing blow. This is a light, quick blow that smooths the surface. You use the flat face of the hammer and strike lightly and rapidly. The fourth is the upsetting blow. This is a vertical blow on the end of a bar to thicken it. You stand the bar on the anvil and strike the top. Upsetting is useful for making hammer heads, chisels, and punches. The fifth is the shouldering blow. This is a blow that creates a shoulder or step in the metal. You use the edge of the hammer or a set tool. Practice each blow slowly until it becomes natural. Speed comes from accuracy, not from rushing. Now, forging exercises. These are simple projects that build skill. Exercise one: draw out a taper. Take a piece of mild steel round bar, about 12 millimeters thick. Heat one end to orange. Place it on the anvil and strike with the hammer, turning the bar a quarter turn after each blow. Work from the tip back to the body. Your goal is a smooth, even taper. This exercise teaches you heat management, hammer control, and turning. Exercise two: forge a square point. Heat a square bar and forge it into a point. This is similar to the taper but with flat sides. Exercise three: upset a bar. Heat the end of a bar and strike it vertically to thicken it. Then forge it back to shape. This teaches you how to move metal in the opposite direction. Exercise four: make a scroll. Heat a flat bar and bend it into a spiral using the horn of the anvil and a scrolling wrench. This teaches you bending and shaping. Exercise five: make a simple hook. Draw out a taper, bend it into a hook, and forge a decorative end. This is a sellable product. Exercise six: make a chisel from a coil spring. Anneal the spring, cut a length, forge the tip, harden and temper it. This teaches you high-carbon steel work. Exercise seven: make a pair of tongs. This is a rite of passage for many blacksmiths. You need to forge the jaws, the boss, and the reins. It takes practice, but once you can make tongs, you can make almost anything. Exercise eight: make a knife from a leaf spring. This combines drawing, shaping, grinding, hardening, and tempering. It is a high-value product in many African markets. Do these exercises regularly. Set aside one hour each day for practice. Keep a notebook and record what you made, what went wrong, and what you will improve next time. Production speed is a business issue. If you take three hours to make a simple hook, you cannot compete. Here are ways to speed up without losing quality. First, batch your work. Make ten hooks at once. Heat several pieces at the same time if your forge allows. Use a production line: one person draws tapers, another bends, another finishes. Second, use jigs and fixtures. A simple bending jig made from steel pins on a plate lets you bend identical scrolls quickly. A hot cut hardy tool in your anvil speeds up cutting. Third, keep your tools sharp and organized. A dull chisel takes more blows. A missing hammer costs time. Fourth, reduce trips to the forge. Plan your sequence so you do all the forging on one heat. For example, draw the taper, bend the hook, and punch the hole before you return to the fire. Fifth, use the right steel. Mild steel moves faster than high-carbon steel. Use mild steel for decorative work and save high-carbon for tools. Sixth, improve your fire. A hotter fire heats metal faster. A clean fire with good air flow is more efficient. Seventh, consider a power hammer or a treadle hammer if you do heavy work. A treadle hammer is a simple machine that uses a foot pedal to raise and drop a hammer. It can be built locally and saves your arm. Eighth, train an apprentice. In many African workshops, apprentices do the basic work while the master does the finishing. This increases output and passes on skills. Ninth, standardize your products. If you make the same gate ten times, you will get faster and better. Custom work is profitable but slow. Balance custom and standard work. Tenth, track your time. Write down how long each product takes. If a product takes too long, either raise the price or find a faster way. Do not work for free. Safety during forging. Wear eye protection. Hot metal can throw scale. Wear leather gloves when handling hot metal, but not when hammering, because gloves can slip. Wear ear protection if you use a power hammer. Keep your work area clear. Do not leave hot metal where someone can step on it. Use tongs that fit the work. If the tongs slip, stop and adjust. A dropped piece of hot metal can cause a serious burn. Finally, remember that hammer technique is a lifelong journey. Even master blacksmiths learn new things. Watch other blacksmiths. Share ideas. Practice deliberately. In the next lesson, we will cover heat treatment, hardening, and tempering for tools and knives.

4. Heat Treatment, Hardening, and Tempering for Tools and Knives

Welcome to Module 4, Lesson 4. Heat treatment is what separates a piece of shaped metal from a useful tool. In this lesson we cover the science and practice of hardening and tempering, with a focus on African conditions and locally available materials. You will learn how to identify critical temperatures by color, how to quench in oil and water, how to temper for different tools, and how to avoid common mistakes. First, the basics. Steel is an alloy of iron and carbon. The amount of carbon determines how hard the steel can become. Mild steel has low carbon and cannot be hardened. High-carbon steel, such as old files, leaf springs, and coil springs, can be hardened. To harden steel, you heat it to a specific temperature, called the critical temperature, and then cool it quickly. The critical temperature is different for each steel, but for most simple high-carbon steels, it is around 750 to 850 degrees Celsius. You can judge this by color. In a dimly lit workshop, steel at 750 degrees Celsius glows a cherry red. At 800 degrees, it is a bright cherry red. At 850 degrees, it is orange. Do not rely on bright sunlight. If you can, shade your forge or work in the evening. A magnet is a useful tool. Steel becomes non-magnetic at its critical temperature. As you heat the steel, touch it with a magnet. When the magnet no longer sticks, you are at or slightly above the critical temperature. This is a reliable test for many simple steels. Once the steel is at critical temperature, you must cool it quickly. This is called quenching. The choice of quenchant depends on the steel and the tool. Water is the fastest quench and gives the hardest result, but it can crack the steel. Oil is slower and safer. Brine, which is salt water, is faster than water but can cause uneven hardening. For most African blacksmiths, used motor oil is the best choice. It is cheap, available, and gives good results for knives and tools. Do not use new oil if you can avoid it. Used oil has additives that can be harmful when heated, so work outdoors. For quenching, you need a container deep enough to submerge the whole blade or tool. A metal pipe with a cap on one end works well. Heat the oil to about 50 to 60 degrees Celsius. Warm oil quenches faster than cold oil. You can test the temperature with a piece of steel or a thermometer. Do not overheat the oil. If it smokes heavily, it is too hot. When quenching, plunge the steel into the oil quickly and move it around. Do not hold it still. Movement breaks the vapor barrier that forms around the steel and ensures even cooling. For a knife, plunge the blade edge-first, then move it side to side. For a chisel, plunge the tip first. Keep the steel in the oil until it is cool enough to touch, usually about ten to twenty seconds. Then remove it and check for hardness. A file should skate across the surface without biting. If the file bites, the steel did not harden. You may need to reheat and quench again. After hardening, the steel is very hard but also brittle. It can crack or chip. To make it useful, you must temper it. Tempering means reheating the steel to a lower temperature, usually between 150 and 300 degrees Celsius, and holding it there for a period, then cooling slowly. This reduces hardness slightly and increases toughness. The temperature you choose depends on the tool. For a knife, you want a tempering temperature of about 200 degrees Celsius. This gives a hard edge that can still take impact. For a chisel or punch, use about 220 to 250 degrees Celsius. For a spring or a machete, use about 300 degrees Celsius. You can judge tempering temperature by color. Clean the steel with sandpaper or a grinder so you can see the oxide colors. Heat the steel slowly and evenly. As it heats, the surface will change color. The colors appear in this order: pale yellow, straw yellow, brown, purple, dark blue, light blue, and gray. For a knife, you want a straw or brown color. For a chisel, a purple color. For a spring, a dark blue. These colors are a guide. If you have a thermometer, use it. If not, practice and record your results. A simple tempering method is to use a kitchen oven. Set the oven to the desired temperature and leave the steel in for one hour. This is slow but accurate. Another method is to use a bed of hot sand or ash. Heat the sand to the desired temperature and bury the steel in it. A third method is to use a torch or the forge itself. Heat the steel slowly and watch the colors run. This is fast but requires skill. For a knife, you can also temper by heating the spine with a torch while the edge stays cool. The spine turns blue while the edge stays straw. This gives a tough spine and a hard edge. This is called differential tempering. It is common in Japanese knife making and works well with African leaf spring knives. Now, common mistakes. First, overheating the steel before quenching. If the steel is too hot, the grain grows and the steel becomes brittle. Use the magnet test and watch the color. Second, quenching in the wrong medium. Water can crack high-carbon steel. Oil is safer. Third, not moving the steel during the quench. This causes soft spots. Fourth, tempering at too high a temperature. This makes the tool too soft. Fifth, tempering at too low a temperature. This leaves the tool brittle. Sixth, not cleaning the steel before tempering. You cannot see the colors if the surface is dirty. Seventh, using the wrong steel. Mild steel will not harden no matter what you do. Test your steel with a file and a magnet before you start. Let us talk about specific tools. For a knife, the process is: forge the blade, normalize it three times, grind the bevels, heat to critical, quench in warm oil, temper at 200 degrees Celsius for one hour, then finish and sharpen. Normalizing means heating to critical and letting it cool in air. This relieves stress and refines the grain. For a chisel, the process is: forge the tip, normalize, heat to critical, quench in oil, temper at 230 degrees Celsius, then grind the bevel. For a machete, use a spring steel, normalize, quench in oil, temper at 300 degrees Celsius. For a hammer, use a medium-carbon steel, quench the face in water, temper at 250 degrees Celsius. Always test your tools before you sell them. A knife should cut wood without chipping. A chisel should cut metal without rolling. A machete should chop without cracking. If a tool fails, learn from it. Write down what you did and change one thing next time. Heat treatment is a skill that takes practice. Do not expect perfect results on your first try. Make test pieces. Cut them, break them, and look at the grain. Fine grain is good. Coarse grain is bad. In the next lesson, we will cover finishing, marketing, and selling your products in African markets.

5. Finishing, Marketing, and Selling Blacksmith Products in Africa

Welcome to Module 4, Lesson 5. You have learned to source steel, build a forge, hammer efficiently, and heat treat tools. Now we focus on the business side: finishing, pricing, marketing, and selling. A blacksmith who cannot sell will not survive. This lesson is practical and Africa-focused. First, finishing. A well-finished product sells for more. Finishing includes grinding, sanding, polishing, and protecting the surface. For knives, start with a coarse grinding wheel or belt sander. Remove the scale and smooth the bevels. Then use a finer grit, such as 120, then 240, then 400. For a mirror finish, go up to 800 or 1000. In many African markets, a satin finish is enough. It looks good and takes less time. For tools, remove sharp edges and smooth the handles. A wooden handle should be sanded and oiled. Use linseed oil, palm oil, or any drying oil. This protects the wood and makes it comfortable. For decorative ironwork, remove scale with a wire brush and apply a coat of oil or paint. Black paint is traditional for gates and railings. For a natural finish, heat the metal to a blue color and apply beeswax or linseed oil. This gives a rich, dark finish that resists rust. Rust is the enemy of ironwork. In humid African climates, unprotected steel rusts quickly. Always apply a protective coating. Options include oil, paint, wax, and galvanizing. Galvanizing is expensive and usually done in a factory. For most blacksmiths, oil and paint are enough. Reapply oil every few months. Now, pricing. Many blacksmiths undercharge. They forget to include labor, fuel, consumables, and profit. Use this formula: Price equals material cost plus labor cost plus fuel cost plus overhead plus profit. Material cost is what you paid for the steel. Labor cost is your hourly rate times the hours worked. Fuel cost is the charcoal, coal, or gas used. Overhead includes rent, tools, electricity, and transport. Profit is what you keep to grow your business. A common mistake is to price only by comparing to other blacksmiths. If your competitor sells a hoe for 5000 shillings, you might think you must sell for 5000. But if your hoe is better finished, uses better steel, and lasts longer, you can charge 7000. Do not compete on price alone. Compete on quality, service, and reliability. Know your break-even point. If it costs you 3000 shillings to make a hoe, you must sell it for more than 3000 to survive. If you sell for 3500, your profit is 500. If you sell for 5000, your profit is 2000. Small differences matter. Track your sales. Write down every sale: date, product, price, and customer. At the end of the month, add up your revenue and your costs. Are you making a profit? If not, change something. Marketing. In Africa, word of mouth is powerful. A satisfied customer tells others. A dissatisfied customer tells more. So quality is your best marketing. But you can also do more. First, make a sign. Paint your workshop name and what you make. Include your phone number. Second, use social media. WhatsApp, Facebook, and Instagram are free. Take photos of your products. Post them in local buy-and-sell groups. Ask customers to send photos of your products in use. Third, attend local markets and trade fairs. Set up a table with your best products. Demonstrate your work if possible. People love to watch a blacksmith. Fourth, build relationships with hardware stores, agricultural supply shops, and construction companies. They can sell your products or recommend you. Fifth, offer a guarantee. If a tool breaks within a reasonable time, repair or replace it. This builds trust. Sixth, create a brand. A simple stamp or mark on your products tells customers who made it. It also makes your work look professional. Seventh, tell your story. Customers like to know where their products come from. If you use recycled steel, say so. If you support local farmers, say so. Authenticity sells. Selling. There are several channels. Direct sales from your workshop. This is best because you meet the customer and can explain your work. Market sales. This reaches many people but requires transport and time. Wholesale to shops. This gives you bulk orders but lower prices. Online sales. This works for high-value items like knives and custom tools. Export. This is possible for high-quality knives and art pieces, but it requires paperwork and shipping. Start with direct and market sales. As you grow, add wholesale and online. When selling, know your product. Be able to explain what steel you used, how you hardened it, and how to care for it. A customer who understands the value will pay more. Offer after-sales service. Sharpening, repair, and advice are cheap to provide and build loyalty. Handle complaints well. If a customer is unhappy, listen, apologize, and fix the problem. A complaint handled well creates a loyal customer. A complaint ignored creates an enemy. Now, let us talk about specific products for African markets. Agricultural tools: hoes, machetes, axes, sickles, and plough parts. These are always in demand. Focus on durability and sharpness. Household items: knives, cooking stands, grills, door hinges, and window frames. These sell in every community. Construction items: gates, railings, window grills, and roof trusses. These are high-value but require precision. Decorative items: wall art, sculptures, and furniture. These sell to tourists, hotels, and wealthy customers. Custom orders: this is where the money is. A customer who wants a specific knife or gate will pay a premium. Ask questions, make a drawing, and agree on a price before you start. Finally, think about growth. Reinvest your profits. Buy better tools. Train apprentices. Build a bigger workshop. Save for the dry season when sales may be slow. Keep learning. Attend workshops. Watch videos. Read books. The blacksmith trade is ancient, but it is not static. The best blacksmiths are always improving. Your assignment for this module is to finish three products to a high standard, price them using the formula, and sell at least one. Record the sale and the customer feedback. Bring your notes to the next module. Congratulations on completing Module 4. You now have the skills to source, forge, heat treat, finish, and sell. The rest is practice and persistence. Keep your fire hot and your hammer moving.

<h2>Practical Reference: Workshop Records, Costing, and Safe Handling for African Blacksmiths</h2>

<p>Blacksmithing in Africa is rarely only a craft it is a small manufacturing business. Whether you forge hoes in Mbale, pangas in Tabora, or kitchen knives in Kumasi, your margin is decided long before the customer arrives: by how you buy steel, how much charcoal or gas you burn, how many hours you lose to rework, and how safely you handle the chemicals used in finishing and heat treatment. This module gives you a practical reference system record sheets, costing tables, and handling rules that turns a workshop into a predictable enterprise. It is written to sit alongside Module 3 (hammer technique and production speed) and Module 5 (heat treatment and finishing), and assumes you already know how to source scrap and run a forge.</p>

<h3>Why records decide profit, not skill</h3>

<p>Two blacksmiths can have identical hammer skills and very different incomes. The difference is almost always information: the first knows what a panga costs to make, the second guesses. In African markets, scrap steel prices swing with construction activity, fuel prices move with transport costs, and buyers negotiate hard. Without records you cannot tell whether a price is profitable or whether a batch of knives lost money. The tables below are designed to be copied into a notebook or a simple spreadsheet on a phone. They use figures that are realistic for East and West African markets in 20232024 and are sourced so you can update them yourself.</p>

<ul style="margin:0;color:#5a2a22;line-height:1.6;">
<li><strong>Quenching oils and salts:</strong> Used motor oil and brine/salt quenchants can cause dermatitis, burns, and toxic fumes. Never quench in oil indoors. Keep a lid on the quench tank. Store all chemicals in labelled containers, away from children and food. Do not use old pesticide or fuel containers.</li>

Work outdoors or with extraction, and wear a P2/N95 respirator when grinding or welding. Never forge galvanised, painted, or plastic-coated scrap without stripping it first.</li>

<h3>How to run the system without paperwork slowing you down</h3>

<p>Record-keeping fails when it is complicated. Use a single exercise book with one page per day. Write four lines before you start: steel bought, fuel bought, hours planned, units planned. Write four lines at the end: units completed, rejects, cash received, notes. That is 60 seconds of work. At the end of the month, copy the totals into Table 2. If you have a smartphone, a simple spreadsheet or a notes app works equally well. The goal is not accounting for a bank it is knowing your own business.</p>

<p>Pair the records with a pricing rule. A simple and defensible rule for African rural markets is: <strong>selling price = direct cost 2</strong> for standard items, and <strong>direct cost 2.5</strong> for items with high finishing labour (knives, decorated tools). This covers fuel, steel, labour, tool wear, and a small profit. Adjust upward if you have transport costs to market or pay market fees. Adjust downward only if you are clearing slow stock and record it as a discount, not as your normal price.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul>
<li><strong>Weigh every scrap purchase</strong> and write the price per kilogram the same day. Never buy by "a heap" without a weight estimate.</li>
<li><strong>Sort steel before forging.</strong> Test with a magnet and a spark test; keep spring steel, mild steel, and high-carbon steel in separate labelled piles.</li>
<li><strong>Record fuel per batch.</strong> If fuel per unit rises for two months, fix the forge before buying more steel.</li>
<li><strong>Log every heat-treatment batch</strong> with colour, quench medium, and temper colour. This is how you repeat a good knife.</li>
<li><strong>Count rejects weekly.</strong> Above 5% means a process problem, not bad luck.</li>
<li><strong>Price from cost, not from the neighbour's price.</strong> Use the 2 rule and record your actual margin.</li>
<li><strong>Keep chemicals labelled, locked, and away from food and children.</strong> Never quench in oil indoors.</li>
<li><strong>Review the dashboard monthly</strong> and change one thing at a time fuel, price, or process.</li>
</ul>
