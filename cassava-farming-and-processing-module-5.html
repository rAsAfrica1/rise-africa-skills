1. Nutrient Requirements of Cassava

Cassava is a heavy feeder. A crop of 30 tonnes of fresh roots per hectare removes about 120 kg nitrogen (N), 30 kg phosphorus (P2O5), and 150 kg potassium (K2O) from the soil. That is more potassium than most cereals. If you harvest roots and remove stems, you are mining the soil. Without replacement, yields drop every season.

Cassava needs nitrogen for leaf and stem growth. Early in the season, N drives canopy formation. A shortage shows as pale, small leaves and thin stems. Phosphorus is critical for root development. It helps the plant build a strong root system and improves starch content. Potassium is the most important nutrient for root bulking and starch formation. It also helps the plant resist diseases. Cassava also needs calcium, magnesium, sulphur, zinc, and boron, but in smaller amounts.

In Africa, most smallholder soils are sandy or degraded. They are low in organic matter, nitrogen, and phosphorus. Potassium is often low in sandy soils but can be adequate in clay soils. Continuous cassava cropping without fertilizer will exhaust even good soil. A farmer who plants cassava after cassava for three years without inputs may see yields fall from 20 tonnes per hectare to 8 tonnes or less.

To meet these needs, you must combine organic and inorganic sources. Farmyard manure, poultry litter, and compost supply multiple nutrients and build soil organic matter. Mineral fertilizers give a quick boost. The exact amount depends on your soil test, but a general recommendation for cassava in Africa is 60-90 kg N, 30-45 kg P2O5, and 60-90 kg K2O per hectare. That translates to about 4-6 bags of NPK 15-15-15 per hectare, plus additional potassium if needed.

Remember: cassava yields best when nutrients are available steadily. Split applications and place fertilizer correctly. Do not guess. Test your soil if possible. If not, use the general rates and adjust based on leaf colour and root size.

2. Organic Manure Application Techniques

Organic manure is the backbone of cassava fertility in Africa. It adds nutrients slowly, improves soil structure, and feeds soil life. Common types are farmyard manure (cattle, goat, sheep), poultry litter, compost, and green manure from legume cover crops like mucuna or cowpea.

Fresh manure can burn plants and carry weeds. Compost or age manure for at least 4-6 weeks before use. Poultry litter is rich: one tonne contains about 20 kg N, 15 kg P2O5, and 10 kg K2O. Cattle manure is weaker: one tonne has about 5 kg N, 2 kg P2O5, and 5 kg K2O. So you need more cattle manure to get the same effect.

Apply manure 2-4 weeks before planting. Broadcast it evenly over the plot, then incorporate it into the top 15-20 cm of soil with a hoe or plough. For cassava, a good rate is 5-10 tonnes per hectare of cattle manure, or 2-4 tonnes per hectare of poultry litter. If you have little manure, concentrate it in the planting holes. Dig a hole 30 cm wide and 20 cm deep, mix a handful (about 200-300 g) of well-rotted manure with the soil, then plant the cutting. This 'micro-dosing' gives each plant a good start.

You can also make compost from crop residues, kitchen waste, and animal dung. Build a heap 1.5 m wide and 1 m high. Turn it every 2 weeks. Keep it moist but not soaked. In 6-8 weeks, it becomes dark, crumbly compost. Apply 2-3 tonnes per hectare.

Green manure: plant mucuna or cowpea in the off-season. When they flower, cut them down and incorporate into the soil. This adds 40-60 kg N per hectare. Do not burn residues; that loses nitrogen. Organic manure works best when combined with small amounts of mineral fertilizer. Do not rely on manure alone if your soil is very poor.

3. Inorganic Fertilizer Types and Application Rates

Inorganic fertilizers are concentrated and fast-acting. They are sold in bags marked with three numbers, like 15-15-15. These numbers are the percentage of nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P2O5), and potassium (K2O). A 50 kg bag of 15-15-15 contains 7.5 kg N, 7.5 kg P2O5, and 7.5 kg K2O.

For cassava, common fertilizers are NPK 15-15-15, NPK 20-10-10, urea (46% N), and muriate of potash (60% K2O). Cassava needs more potassium than many crops. A general recommendation for a 20-30 tonne per hectare crop is 60-90 kg N, 30-45 kg P2O5, and 60-90 kg K2O per hectare. That means about 4-6 bags of NPK 15-15-15 per hectare, plus 1-2 bags of muriate of potash if your soil is low in K.

Apply fertilizer in a ring or band 10-15 cm from the plant base, not touching the stem. For a single plant, use about 10-15 g of NPK 15-15-15 at planting, and another 10-15 g at 6-8 weeks after planting. If you use urea, apply 50 kg per hectare (about 1 bag) split into two doses: half at 4 weeks, half at 8 weeks. Urea must be covered with soil to avoid losing nitrogen to the air.

Do not over-apply. Too much nitrogen gives big leaves but small roots. Too much fertilizer can burn young plants. Always read the label. Store fertilizer in a dry place, off the floor. Wear gloves and wash hands after use. If you cannot afford full rates, use half rates and combine with manure. A soil test will tell you exactly what you need. Without a test, start with 4 bags of NPK 15-15-15 per hectare and adjust based on plant growth.

4. Fertilizer Timing and Placement Methods

When and how you apply fertilizer matters as much as what you apply. Cassava has a long growing season (8-12 months). It needs nutrients early for root establishment, and again during root bulking (3-5 months after planting).

At planting: Apply a starter dose of phosphorus and potassium. Mix 5-10 g of NPK 15-15-15 per planting hole with the soil before placing the cutting. This gives the young roots immediate access to nutrients. Do not let fertilizer touch the cutting directly; it can burn the buds.

First top-dressing: 4-6 weeks after planting, when shoots are 15-20 cm tall. Apply 10-15 g of NPK 15-15-15 per plant, or 50 kg of urea per hectare. Place fertilizer in a ring 10-15 cm from the stem, then cover lightly with soil. This is the most critical application for canopy development.

Second top-dressing: 8-12 weeks after planting, just before the roots start bulking. Apply 10-15 g of NPK 15-15-15 per plant, or 50 kg of muriate of potash per hectare. Potassium at this stage increases root size and starch content. If you use urea, split it: half at 4 weeks, half at 8 weeks.

Placement methods: Broadcasting is easy but less efficient; you need higher rates. Banding or ring placement puts fertilizer near the roots, so you can use less. For ring placement, make a shallow trench 10-15 cm from the plant, spread fertilizer, and cover with soil. Do not place fertilizer in a heap; it can damage roots. On slopes, place fertilizer on the uphill side to reduce runoff.

If you use manure, apply it 2-4 weeks before planting and incorporate it. Then supplement with mineral fertilizer at the times above. Always apply when soil is moist, not dry. If it is dry, wait for rain or water the soil lightly after application.

5. Integrated Soil Fertility Management

Integrated Soil Fertility Management (ISFM) means combining organic and inorganic nutrient sources with good agronomic practices. It is the best way to keep cassava yields high without destroying the soil. ISFM has three pillars: use organic matter, use mineral fertilizer wisely, and improve crop management.

First, organic matter. Apply 5-10 tonnes of cattle manure or 2-4 tonnes of poultry litter per hectare. Use compost from crop residues. Plant legume cover crops like mucuna or cowpea in rotation or intercropped. These add nitrogen and improve soil structure. Keep crop residues on the field; do not burn them.

Second, mineral fertilizer. Use the right type and rate based on soil test or general recommendations. For cassava, apply 4-6 bags of NPK 15-15-15 per hectare, split into planting and top-dressing. Add muriate of potash if potassium is low. Do not apply fertilizer without organic matter; the combination works better than either alone.

Third, good agronomic practices. Use improved varieties that respond to fertilizer. Plant on time at the start of the rains. Control weeds, especially in the first 3 months. Use proper spacing (1 m x 1 m or 1 m x 0.8 m). Practice crop rotation with cereals or legumes to break pest cycles and improve soil. Avoid continuous cassava on the same plot for more than 3 years without heavy organic inputs.

ISFM also includes soil conservation: contour ploughing, mulching, and cover crops to reduce erosion. On sandy soils, add clay or organic matter to increase water and nutrient holding capacity. Test your soil every 2-3 years if possible. If not, observe your plants: pale leaves mean nitrogen shortage; purple leaves mean phosphorus shortage; yellow leaf edges mean potassium shortage.

A simple ISFM plan for one hectare: apply 5 tonnes of manure before planting. At planting, apply 2 bags of NPK 15-15-15. At 6 weeks, apply 1 bag of urea. At 10 weeks, apply 1 bag of NPK 15-15-15 plus 1 bag of muriate of potash. This gives about 60 kg N, 30 kg P2O5, and 60 kg K2O, plus organic matter. Yields can reach 25-30 tonnes per hectare.

6. Diagnosing Nutrient Deficiencies in Cassava

Cassava plants tell you what they need. Learn to read the leaves. Nutrient deficiencies show specific symptoms. Early diagnosis saves your harvest.

Nitrogen deficiency: Older leaves turn pale green, then yellow. The whole plant looks stunted. Stems are thin. Leaves may drop early. This happens in sandy soils with low organic matter, or after heavy rains that leach nitrogen. To fix: apply urea or NPK 15-15-15 at 50 kg per hectare. Split applications.

Phosphorus deficiency: Leaves become dark green with a purple or reddish tint, especially on older leaves. Stems may be thin and short. Roots are small. This is common in acidic soils. To fix: apply NPK with phosphorus, or bone meal. Lime acidic soils to pH 5.5-6.5.

Potassium deficiency: Leaf edges turn yellow, then brown and dry (scorch). Older leaves are affected first. Roots are small and have low starch. This is common in sandy soils and after continuous cropping. To fix: apply muriate of potash at 50-100 kg per hectare, or wood ash.

Magnesium deficiency: Older leaves have yellow patches between green veins (interveinal chlorosis). Leaf edges may turn reddish-purple. To fix: apply magnesium sulphate (Epsom salt) at 50 kg per hectare, or dolomitic lime.

Zinc deficiency: Young leaves are small and narrow, with yellowing between veins. Internodes are short. To fix: spray zinc sulphate at 5 g per litre of water.

Boron deficiency: Growing points die. Leaves are thick and brittle. Roots are cracked. To fix: apply borax at 10 kg per hectare.

How to tell deficiency from disease? Nutrient deficiencies appear on many plants uniformly, often on older or newer leaves in a pattern. Diseases show spots, wilting, or rot. If you are unsure, test your soil. A simple soil test costs little and saves money on wrong fertilizers. Keep a notebook: record symptoms, what you applied, and the result. Over time, you will know your farm's needs.

<h2>Practical Reference: Soil Fertility &amp; Nutrient Management for Cassava</h2>

<p>Cassava is often called a "poor man's crop" because it tolerates acid soils, low phosphorus, and erratic rainfall. But tolerance is not the same as profitability. A cassava field that yields 810 t/ha of fresh roots is surviving; a field that yields 2535 t/ha is a business. The difference, in most African production systems, is nutrient management not variety alone, not rainfall alone.</p>

<p>This Practical Reference consolidates the six lessons in this module into field-ready guidance for commercial and semi-commercial cassava growers, processors, and outgrower scheme managers. It assumes you are managing soil fertility as an input cost line, not as a development activity. Every tonne of fresh roots removes nutrients from your land. Your job is to replace them at the lowest cost per tonne of starch produced.</p>

<h3>1. What Cassava Actually Removes From Your Soil</h3>

<p>Cassava produces more dry matter per unit of nutrient than most staple crops, but it also extracts heavily especially potassium, which drives root bulking and starch accumulation. A crop yielding 30 t/ha of fresh roots removes roughly 150180 kg KO, 4060 kg N, and 1525 kg PO per hectare, depending on variety, age at harvest, and whether residues are returned to the field.</p>

<p>This is the single most important commercial fact in cassava agronomy: <strong>potassium is your yield-limiting nutrient, not nitrogen.</strong> Nitrogen drives leaf and stem growth. Potassium drives root fill and starch content. Applying urea alone to a cassava field produces tall, leafy plants and disappointing root tonnage. Processors buying by weight and starch content will penalise you twice.</p>

A single soil test soil pH, organic carbon, available P, exchangeable K, Ca, Mg, and micronutrient status costs less than one bag of NPK and prevents you from misallocating an entire season's input budget.</p>

<h3>3. Organic Manure: The Cheapest Base of Your Programme</h3>

<p>Organic matter is not a soft option it is the foundation of a cost-efficient fertility programme on African cassava land. It improves water-holding capacity, buffers acid soils, feeds the microbial community that mineralises nutrients, and supplies K and micronutrients that inorganic blends often miss.</p>

<p><strong>Application rates that pay:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Poultry litter:</strong> 35 t/ha, applied 24 weeks before planting, incorporated into the top 15 cm. Approximate nutrient content: 2.5% N, 2.5% PO, 2.0% KO.</li>
<li><strong>Cattle/goat manure (well-rotted):</strong> 812 t/ha. Lower nutrient density but strong soil-conditioning effect.</li>
<li><strong>Composted cassava peel:</strong> 58 t/ha. A circular-economy input for processors your waste stream becomes your fertiliser line item.</li>
<li><strong>Green manure / legume intercrop:</strong> Mucuna, cowpea, or pigeon pea relay-cropped in the first 34 months. Fixes 4080 kg N/ha and adds biomass.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Critical rule:</strong> fresh, unrotted manure burns young cassava cuttings and carries weed seeds. Compost or stack for at least 68 weeks, turning twice, until internal temperature drops and material is dark and crumbly.</p>

<h3>5. Timing and Placement: Where Most Yield Is Won or Lost</h3>

<p>Cassava's nutrient uptake peaks between 3 and 5 months after planting the period of rapid root bulking. Fertiliser applied at month 7 is largely wasted. The commercial rule is simple:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>All phosphorus at planting.</strong> P is immobile; band it near the cutting.</li>
<li><strong>Nitrogen split:</strong> 50% basal, 50% at 810 weeks.</li>
<li><strong>Potassium split:</strong> 50% basal, 50% at 1012 weeks. On sandy soils, split into three applications to reduce leaching.</li>
<li><strong>Placement:</strong> band 58 cm from the cutting, 5 cm deep. Broadcasting on the surface without incorporation loses 2040% of N to volatilisation and is a direct hit to your margin.</li>
</ul>

<h3>6. Integrated Soil Fertility Management (ISFM) for Cassava Businesses</h3>

<p>ISFM is not an academic framework it is a cost-management strategy. The principle: combine organic inputs, mineral fertiliser, improved varieties, and good agronomy so that each input raises the efficiency of the others. On a commercial cassava farm, ISFM looks like this:</p>

<ol>
<li>Soil test every 23 years, or every season on high-value blocks.</li>
<li>Apply compost or manure as the base it raises fertiliser use efficiency by 2040%.</li>
<li>Use the right NPK blend at the right rate, split by crop stage.</li>
<li>Return crop residues (tops, stems, peel) to the field wherever possible.</li>
<li>Rotate or intercrop with legumes to break pest cycles and fix nitrogen.</li>
<li>Control erosion with contour planting and cover crops eroded topsoil is lost profit.</li>
<li>Keep records: input cost per hectare, yield per hectare, and cost per tonne of roots.</li>
</ol>

<h3>7. Diagnosing Nutrient Deficiencies in the Field</h3>

<p>Visual diagnosis is a screening tool, not a substitute for soil or leaf analysis. But it helps you act fast.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Nitrogen:</strong> uniform pale-green to yellow older leaves; stunted growth; thin stems.</li>
<li><strong>Phosphorus:</strong> dark green leaves with purple/reddish petioles and leaf margins; poor root development.</li>
<li><strong>Potassium:</strong> yellowing and necrosis at leaf margins of older leaves; small roots; low starch.</li>
<li><strong>Magnesium:</strong> interveinal chlorosis on older leaves, margins remain green.</li>
<li><strong>Zinc:</strong> small, narrow leaves clustered at the growing tip; shortened internodes.</li>
<li><strong>Boron:</strong> brittle, distorted young leaves; cracked or hollow stems.</li>
</ul>

<p>Confirm with leaf sampling: take the youngest fully expanded leaf (YFEL) from 2030 plants at 34 months after planting, and send to a certified laboratory.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ol>
<li>Soil test done within the last 23 years pH, organic carbon, P, K, Ca, Mg, Zn recorded.</li>
<li>Lime applied and incorporated at least 3 months before planting if pH &lt; 5.2.</li>
<li>Organic manure composted for 68 weeks and applied at 310 t/ha before planting.</li>
<li>Basal NPK banded 58 cm from cutting, 5 cm deep never broadcast and left.</li>
<li>Nitrogen and potassium split across two or three applications timed to root bulking.</li>
<li>Crop residues returned to the field, not burned or exported.</li>
<li>Fertiliser stored dry, off the ground, away from water sources and children.</li>
<li>Input cost per hectare and yield per hectare recorded for every block, every season.</li>
</ol>
