1. Common Sheep Diseases

Sheep in Africa face several diseases that can wipe out your flock if you don't act fast. The most common are Peste des Petits Ruminants (PPR), foot-and-mouth disease (FMD), bluetongue, sheep pox, and internal parasites like Haemonchus contortus (barber's pole worm). PPR is viral, spreads quickly, and kills up to 90% of infected sheep. Signs include high fever (40-41Â°C), mouth ulcers, nasal discharge, and diarrhea. There is no cure, so vaccination is your only defense. FMD causes blisters on the mouth and hooves, making sheep lame and unable to eat. It spreads through saliva, urine, and contaminated feed. Bluetongue, spread by midges, causes swelling of the face, tongue, and lips, and can kill within a week. Sheep pox causes skin nodules and fever; it spreads through direct contact and contaminated equipment. Internal parasites are the silent killer. Barber's pole worm sucks blood, causing anemia (pale gums) and sudden death. You can check for anemia using the FAMACHA eye color chart: pale white eyes mean severe infection. Other diseases include pneumonia (caused by stress, cold, or dusty environments), enterotoxemia (overeating disease from sudden grain intake), and caseous lymphadenitis (abscesses in lymph nodes). To identify diseases early, walk through your flock daily. Look for sheep that are separated from the group, have droopy ears, don't come to feed, or have abnormal droppings. Take note of any sudden deaths. For any suspected outbreak, isolate sick animals immediately and call a vet. In many African countries, government vets provide free or low-cost vaccination against PPR and FMD. Always report unusual deaths to your local veterinary office. Remember, prevention is cheaper than treatment. A single dose of PPR vaccine costs less than 1 USD and protects for 3 years. Treating a sick sheep can cost 10 times more and often fails. Keep records of all diseases you see, and share with other farmers to track outbreaks.

2. Vaccination Protocols

Vaccination is the cheapest insurance for your sheep. In Africa, the core vaccines are PPR, FMD, sheep pox, and anthrax (in endemic areas). Follow a strict schedule: lambs born to vaccinated ewes get their first PPR vaccine at 3 months old, then a booster 3-4 weeks later. If the ewe was not vaccinated, vaccinate the lamb at 1 month, then booster at 2 months. For FMD, vaccinate every 6 months because immunity is short-lived. Sheep pox vaccine is given once a year, preferably before the rainy season when outbreaks peak. Anthrax vaccine is given annually in areas with a history of the disease. Always buy vaccines from a reputable agro-vet store and check the expiry date. Keep vaccines in a cold chain: use a cooler box with ice packs during transport, and store at 2-8Â°C in a refrigerator. Never freeze PPR or FMD vaccines; freezing kills them. Use a new needle for each animal to prevent spreading diseases like caseous lymphadenitis. For small flocks, a 2 ml syringe with a 20-gauge needle works well. Inject subcutaneously (under the skin) in the neck, not intramuscularly, unless the label says otherwise. Restrain the sheep properly: hold it between your legs or use a halter. Clean the injection site with cotton wool and alcohol. After vaccination, watch for allergic reactions like swelling, drooling, or difficulty breathing. Keep epinephrine (adrenaline) on hand; dose is 0.5-1 ml per 50 kg body weight. Record every vaccination: date, vaccine name, batch number, and animal ID. If you have a large flock, organize a vaccination day with neighbors to share costs and labor. In many countries, the government runs annual vaccination campaigns against PPR and FMD. Take advantage of these free or subsidized programs. Do not vaccinate sick or stressed sheep; wait until they recover. Also, do not mix vaccines unless the manufacturer says it's safe. After vaccination, immunity takes 2-3 weeks to develop, so keep animals away from possible sources of infection during that time. A well-vaccinated flock has fewer deaths and higher profits.

3. Parasite Control

Parasites are the number one cause of poor growth and death in African sheep. They come in two types: internal (worms) and external (ticks, mites, lice, fleas). Internal parasites like Haemonchus contortus (barber's pole worm), Ostertagia, and Trichostrongylus live in the stomach and intestines. They cause diarrhea, weight loss, anemia, and bottle jaw (swelling under the jaw). To control them, use a combination of deworming, grazing management, and selective treatment. Deworm every 3-4 months, but rotate between different drug classes to prevent resistance. Common dewormers: albendazole (white drench), ivermectin, and levamisole. Dose correctly by weight; underdosing creates resistant worms. Weigh your sheep using a scale or a weigh tape. For example, a 40 kg sheep gets 1 ml of ivermectin 1% injection. After deworming, move sheep to a clean pasture that hasn't been grazed for at least 3 months. The FAMACHA system helps you treat only anemic sheep, reducing drug use. Check eye color: 1 (red) = healthy, 5 (white) = severe anemia, treat immediately. External parasites: ticks transmit heartwater and other diseases. Use tick grease (e.g., amitraz) applied to the ears, tail base, and under the tail every 2 weeks. For mites (scabies), inject ivermectin or use pour-on products. Lice cause itching and hair loss; dust with insecticidal powder or use a pour-on. Keep bedding clean and dry. Rotate pastures and avoid overstocking. If you see diarrhea, isolate the sheep and collect a fecal sample for a vet to check worm eggs. A simple fecal egg count (FEC) tells you the worm burden. In Africa, many farmers use local plants like neem (Azadirachta indica) and aloe vera as dewormers, but these are less effective than modern drugs. Use them only as supplements. Always store dewormers in a cool, dark place and check expiry dates. After handling dewormers, wash your hands. Never slaughter sheep within the withdrawal period (meat: 14-30 days depending on drug). Parasite control is a year-round job, but it pays off with healthier, faster-growing sheep.

4. Hoof Care and Lameness

Lameness is a major welfare and economic problem in sheep. It causes weight loss, reduced fertility, and early culling. The main causes are foot rot, foot abscess, and foot scald. Foot rot is a bacterial infection (Dichelobacter nodosus) that thrives in wet, muddy conditions. Signs: foul smell, blackening of the hoof, separation of the hoof wall, and severe lameness. Foot scald is milder, with red, inflamed skin between the toes. Foot abscess is a pocket of pus, often from a thorn or stone. To prevent lameness, keep pastures dry and well-drained. Avoid muddy areas near water troughs. Provide a dry, clean bedding area. Trim hooves every 6-8 months, or more often in wet seasons. Use sharp hoof shears or a hoof knife. Restrain the sheep by sitting it on its rump (shearing position) or use a tilt table. Clean the hoof with a wire brush, then trim the overgrown wall and sole. Be careful not to cut too deep; stop when you see pink tissue. For treatment of foot rot, trim away all dead tissue, then soak the foot in a 10% zinc sulfate solution for 30 minutes. Or use a copper sulfate footbath (5% solution) for 10 minutes. Repeat every 5-7 days for 3 weeks. For severe cases, inject long-acting oxytetracycline (20 mg/kg body weight) once, and apply a topical antibiotic spray. Isolate lame sheep to a dry pen until they recover. Foot abscesses need to be lanced and drained by a vet. After treatment, apply a bandage with iodine. To prevent spread, cull chronically lame sheep. In Africa, many farmers use a football (footbath) made from a plastic drum cut in half. Place it at the entrance of the pen so sheep walk through it daily. Add 5 kg of copper sulfate to 100 liters of water. Change the solution every 50 sheep. Also, avoid grazing on wet, marshy land. If you have rocky terrain, clear sharp stones. Regular hoof trimming and footbaths reduce lameness by up to 80%. Healthy hooves mean sheep can walk to feed and water, which keeps them productive.

5. Recognizing Signs of Illness

Sheep hide illness well because they are prey animals. By the time you see obvious signs, the disease may be advanced. Your best tool is daily observation. Spend 10 minutes each morning walking among your flock. Look for the 'sick sheep' signs: separation from the group, droopy ears, head down, not eating, lagging behind, or lying down more than usual. Check the eyes: pale or white gums and eyelids indicate anemia (worms). Yellow eyes and gums mean jaundice (liver fluke or leptospirosis). Normal temperature for a sheep is 38.5-40Â°C. Take it rectally with a digital thermometer. Fever above 40.5Â°C suggests infection. A low temperature (below 37.5Â°C) means shock or hypothermia. Check breathing: normal is 12-20 breaths per minute. Rapid, shallow breathing or coughing suggests pneumonia. Look at droppings: normal are pelleted. Diarrhea (scours) can be caused by worms, coccidia, or bacteria. Bloody diarrhea is an emergency. Check the nose: clear discharge is normal; thick, yellow or green discharge means infection. Check the feet: swelling, heat, or foul smell means foot rot. Check the mouth: ulcers, blisters, or drooling could be PPR or FMD. Check the body condition: run your hand over the ribs and spine. If you can feel bones easily, the sheep is thin (score 2 or less on a 1-5 scale). Also, watch for sudden deaths. If more than one sheep dies in a week, call a vet immediately. Keep a simple notebook: record date, animal ID, signs, and action taken. Take photos on your phone to show the vet. Isolate sick sheep in a quiet, shaded pen with easy access to water and hay. Provide supportive care: fresh water, electrolytes, and soft feed. Do not give antibiotics without a vet's advice; wrong drugs waste money and create resistance. Learn to do a basic physical exam: check gums, eyes, temperature, and breathing. If you suspect a notifiable disease like PPR or FMD, report it to the authorities. Early detection saves lives and money.

6. Biosecurity Measures

Biosecurity means keeping diseases out of your farm. It is the cheapest and most effective health strategy. Start with a closed flock: buy new sheep only from known, healthy sources. Quarantine all new animals for at least 30 days in a separate pen, far from your main flock. During quarantine, check for signs of illness, deworm, and vaccinate. Use separate boots, clothing, and equipment for the quarantine area. If you visit another farm or market, change clothes and shower before entering your sheep pen. Do not let visitors enter your pens unless they wear clean boots or disposable shoe covers. Provide a footbath with 5% copper sulfate or 10% bleach at the entrance. Keep vehicles and dogs away from feed and bedding. Control rodents and wild birds; they carry diseases. Store feed in sealed containers. Use clean water from a well or tap, not stagnant pools. Keep pastures free of garbage and sharp objects. Isolate sick sheep immediately. If a sheep dies, bury it deep (at least 2 meters) or burn it. Never sell or eat a dead sheep. Disinfect pens and equipment regularly. Use a 1:10 bleach solution or a commercial disinfectant like Virkon. Remove manure daily and compost it; composting kills many pathogens. Keep a record of all animals and their health history. Limit the number of people who handle your sheep. Train your workers on hygiene. If you share a ram or grazing land, be extra careful. In Africa, many farmers share communal grazing, which increases disease spread. To reduce risk, graze your sheep on your own land or rotate pastures. Avoid mixing with other flocks, especially at watering points. If you attend a livestock market, do not bring your sheep; send a healthy-looking animal as a sample, or better, sell through a middleman. After any contact with outside sheep, change clothes and disinfect. Vaccinate against common diseases. A good biosecurity plan includes: quarantine, vaccination, parasite control, and hygiene. Write it down and follow it every day. One outbreak of PPR can wipe out your entire flock. Biosecurity is not expensive; it is just a habit. Start today.

3. Parasite Control â Internal and External

Parasites are the silent tax on African sheep production. Internal parasites (especially Haemonchus contortus) cause anaemia, weight loss, and lamb mortality. External parasites (ticks, mites, lice) transmit heartwater, babesiosis, and cause hide damage that reduces pelt value.

Integrated parasite management (IPM) principles:

  • Use the FAMACHAÂ© eye-colour chart to treat only anaemic animals, slowing resistance.
  • Rotate dewormer classes annually (benzimidazoles, macrocyclic lactones, imidazothiazoles).
  • Rotate pastures and avoid grazing the same camp for more than 10â14 days in wet seasons.
  • Dip or spray for ticks every 1â2 weeks in high-pressure areas; use acaricide rotation.
  • Quarantine all incoming animals and treat before introduction.

4. Hoof Care and Lameness

Lameness is the most under-estimated profit leak on African sheep farms. A lame ewe cannot graze properly, loses condition, and produces weaker lambs. In feedlots, lame animals are downgraded at sale. Footrot thrives in wet, muddy kraals and is highly contagious.

Practical hoof care routine:

  • Inspect hooves every 6â8 weeks; trim overgrown or cracked hooves with sharp shears.
  • Use a 10% zinc sulphate or 5% formalin footbath for flocks with recurring footrot.
  • Isolate and treat lame animals immediately; do not let them contaminate the kraal floor.
  • Keep bedding dry and avoid standing water in pens.
  • Select breeding stock from animals with sound feet and legs â this is a heritable trait.

5. Recognizing Signs of Illness â The Daily Walk-Through

Early detection is the difference between a cheap treatment and a dead ewe. Train every herder to scan the flock daily using the "5 S" check:

  • Separation: An animal standing apart from the flock is usually sick.
  • Stance: Drooped head, arched back, or reluctance to move.
  • Silence: Reduced bleating, no response to feed calls.
  • Stomach: Sunken flanks, bloating, or diarrhoea.
  • Surface: Dull coat, pale or yellow eye membranes, discharge from nose or eyes.

6. Biosecurity Measures â Keeping Disease Out

Biosecurity is not a luxury for large farms; it is the foundation of any profitable flock. The core rules are simple and cheap:

  • Quarantine all new animals for 21â30 days before mixing with the flock.
  • Limit visitor access; provide footbaths and clean boots at entry points.
  • Never share rams, buckets, or shearing equipment without disinfection.
  • Control ticks, rodents, and wild birds around feed stores.
  • Dispose of dead animals by deep burial or burning â never leave carcasses accessible.
  • Keep records of every treatment, vaccination, and death; records are your early-warning system.
