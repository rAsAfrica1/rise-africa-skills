1. Costing and Pricing Your Products

To price your ceramics profitably, you must first know your costs. Start by listing every expense: clay, glaze, kiln fuel (firewood, gas, or electricity), packaging, and labour. For example, if you buy clay at 5000 Naira per 25kg bag and use 2kg per mug, clay cost per mug is 400 Naira. Glaze might cost 100 Naira per mug. Firing a kiln load of 50 mugs uses 2000 Naira of gas, so 40 Naira per mug. Add packaging at 50 Naira. Your materials cost is 590 Naira. Now add labour: if you pay yourself 2000 Naira per hour and make 10 mugs per hour, that's 200 Naira per mug. Total cost is 790 Naira. Add overheads like rent, tools, and transportsay 10%so 79 Naira. Total cost = 869 Naira. To make a profit, mark up by at least 50%: selling price = 869 x 1.5 = 1303 Naira. Round to 1300 Naira. This is your break-even plus profit. For export or wholesale, you may need to lower margin but increase volume. Always track actual costs; keep receipts. Use a simple notebook or Excel sheet. Review prices every 3 months because fuel and material costs change. Don't guesscalculate. Also consider perceived value: a beautifully glazed mug can sell for 2000 Naira in a city gift shop. Test prices at markets and adjust. Remember, undercharging kills your business faster than overcharging. Start with cost-plus pricing, then experiment.

2. Finding Local and International Markets

Local markets are your first target. In your town, visit hotels, restaurants, gift shops, and offices. For example, a hotel in Kampala may need 200 custom coffee mugs with their logo. Offer a sample and a price list. Attend local craft fairs and trade showsrent a table for 50,000 Ugandan Shillings and display your best work. Network with interior designers and architects who specify tiles for buildings. For tiles, approach hardware stores and construction companies. In Nigeria, go to building material markets like Alaba International. For international markets, start online. Platforms like Etsy, Amazon Handmade, and NOVICA accept African artisans. Etsy charges a listing fee of $0.20 and 6.5% transaction fee. You'll need good photos and clear shipping costs. Also consider export agents who buy in bulk. For example, a buyer in Europe may order 5000 tiles for a hotel project. To find them, attend international trade fairs like the Ambiente in Germany or use platforms like Alibaba. But before exporting, ensure your products meet quality standards and you can handle shipping. Start small: sell to diaspora communities in the US or UK through social media. Join Facebook groups like 'African Artisans Network'. Always calculate shipping and customs duties. For air freight from Kenya to UK, 10kg costs about 8000 KES. Include that in your price. Build relationshipstrust is key. Follow up with buyers and deliver on time.

3. Marketing Online: Social Media and E-commerce

Social media is free advertising. Create a business page on Facebook, Instagram, and TikTok. Post photos of your ceramics in natural light. Show the making processpeople love videos of throwing on a wheel or glazing. Use hashtags like #AfricanCeramics #HandmadeTiles #PotteryAfrica. Post 3 times a week. Engage with followers: reply to comments and messages within 24 hours. For e-commerce, set up a simple shop on WhatsApp Businessit's free and widely used in Africa. List products with prices and delivery options. For wider reach, use Jumia (Nigeria, Kenya, Egypt) or Takealot (South Africa). They charge commission but handle payments and delivery. Alternatively, create a free website with Wix or Shopify (Shopify costs $29/month). Link it to your social media. Use high-quality photos: borrow a smartphone, use natural light, and a plain background. Write clear descriptions: size, material, care instructions. Offer secure payment: mobile money (M-Pesa, MTN MoMo), bank transfer, or PayPal for international. For shipping, partner with local couriers like G4S or DHL. Promote posts for as little as $5 to reach more people. Run a giveaway: 'Win a free mug' to get followers. Collaborate with influencerssend them a free product for a review. Track what works: use Facebook Insights to see which posts get most engagement. Double down on that. Also, list on Google My Business so local customers find you. Online marketing takes time, but consistent effort builds a brand.

4. Managing Inventory and Production Scheduling

Inventory management prevents waste and ensures you can fulfil orders. For ceramics, raw materials like clay and glaze have shelf life. Keep a simple stock card for each item: record date, quantity in, quantity out, and balance. For example, if you have 10 bags of clay and use 2 per week, reorder when you reach 3 bags. Use a notebook or a free app like 'Inventory Simple'. For finished goods, store them safely to avoid breakage. Count your stock weekly. Production scheduling means planning what to make and when. If you have a big order for 100 tiles due in 4 weeks, work backwards. Suppose you can make 25 tiles per day, but you only have 3 days a week for production. That's 75 tiles per week. You need 2 weeks to make 100 tiles, leaving 2 weeks for drying, glazing, and firing. Create a calendar: Week 1: make 100 tiles, Week 2: dry and bisque fire, Week 3: glaze and glaze fire, Week 4: pack and deliver. Always add buffer time for kiln breakdowns or power cuts. Keep a 'to-do' list for each day. Prioritise orders by deadline. If you have multiple orders, use a simple kanban board: 'To make', 'In progress', 'Done'. Don't overproduceunsold stock ties up cash. Make in batches of popular items like mugs and plates. For custom orders, schedule them after standard production. Train an assistant to help with repetitive tasks. Review your schedule every Monday.

5. Customer Service and Custom Orders

Good customer service turns one-time buyers into repeat clients. Always greet customers warmly and listen to their needs. If someone asks for a custom tile design, ask questions: What size? What colour? What quantity? When do you need it? Write everything down and repeat it back to confirm. For example, a customer wants 50 dinner plates with a blue rim. You agree on a price of 2500 Naira per plate, total 125,000 Naira. Ask for a 50% deposit (62,500 Naira) before starting. This protects you if they cancel. Give a clear timeline: 'I will have them ready in 3 weeks.' Send progress photos via WhatsApp. If there's a delay, inform them immediately. When delivering, pack items carefullyuse bubble wrap or newspaper. Ask for feedback: 'Are you happy with the plates?' If there's a defect, offer a replacement or refund. For custom orders, always make a sample first and get approval. This avoids costly mistakes. Keep a record of each customer: name, contact, order details, and preferences. Send a thank-you message after delivery. Offer a loyalty discount: 'Get 10% off your next order.' Respond to complaints calmlylisten, apologise, and fix the problem. A satisfied customer tells others; an unhappy one tells everyone. Also, be professional: answer calls during business hours, have a business card, and use a polite tone. Word-of-mouth is powerful in Africayour reputation is your best marketing.

6. Record Keeping and Basic Bookkeeping

Record keeping is not optionalit's how you know if you're making a profit. Use a simple cash book: a notebook with columns for date, description, money in, money out, and balance. Every time you buy clay, record it. Every sale, record it. At the end of the week, total your income and expenses. For example, if you earned 200,000 Naira and spent 120,000 Naira, your profit is 80,000 Naira. Keep all receipts in a folder. For larger businesses, use a spreadsheet like Excel or Google Sheets. Create tabs for Sales, Expenses, Inventory, and Customers. For taxes, many African countries require small businesses to register and pay tax. In Kenya, turnover tax is 3% for businesses with sales under 5 million KES. In Nigeria, you may need to register with FIRS. Keep records for at least 5 years. Separate business and personal moneyopen a business bank account or mobile money wallet. Pay yourself a fixed salary. Track debts: who owes you and whom you owe. Send invoices for unpaid orders. Use a simple invoice template: your business name, customer name, date, items, total, and payment terms. At the end of each month, review your records. Are you spending too much on fuel? Can you negotiate better clay prices? This data helps you make decisions. Also, record your production: how many items made, sold, and broken. This helps you price better and reduce waste. Good records also help you get loans or grants. Start todayeven a simple notebook works.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Business Skills for Ceramic Entrepreneurs

This practical reference supports the RISE AFRICA Skills module Tiles & Ceramics, lesson cluster Business Skills for Ceramic Entrepreneurs. It is written for working ceramicists, tile makers, and small studio owners who need clear tools for costing, pricing, market entry, online selling, inventory control, customer service, and record keeping. The focus is on decisions that improve cash flow, reduce waste, and build repeat customers in African markets and beyond.

Ceramics is a capital and energy-intensive trade. Clay, glaze, fuel, transport, breakage, and kiln time all consume money before a single item is sold. Many talented makers fail not because their work is poor, but because they price below true cost, lose track of stock, or cannot document sales for a loan or wholesale buyer. This reference gives you the numbers, formats, and checklists to run the business side with the same care you give the wheel and kiln.

Costing and Pricing Your Products

Price from cost, not from what a neighbour charges. A simple unit costing sheet for one mug, tile, or bowl should include: clay consumed, glaze and slip, packaging, direct labour hours multiplied by a fair wage, kiln fuel or electricity per firing divided by the number of saleable pieces, studio rent and tools share, transport, and a breakage allowance. Add a target margin that covers market risk, credit sales, and reinvestment. Keep wholesale and retail prices separate.

Finding Local and International Markets

Start with local demand you can reach without credit risk: hotels, restaurants, lodges, interior designers, hardware stores, construction contractors, gift shops, and weekly markets. For tiles, target builders and renovators. For tableware, target hospitality and retail. International buyers usually require consistent quality, lead times, export documentation, and volume. Use trade fairs, chambers of commerce, export promotion agencies, and online B2B platforms to find them.

Marketing Online: Social Media and E-commerce

Use a smartphone, natural light, and a clean background. Post process videos, finished products, prices, and delivery options. Sell through WhatsApp Business, Instagram, Facebook Marketplace, Jumia, Etsy, or your own simple website. Always show dimensions, care instructions, and delivery time. Use mobile money and bank transfer for prepaid orders. Keep a written order log with customer name, item, price, deposit, and delivery date.

Managing Inventory and Production Scheduling

Separate raw materials (clay, glaze, fuel, packaging) from work in progress (greenware, bisque) and finished goods. Record every firing: date, items loaded, items saleable, breakage, fuel used. Schedule production around drying and firing cycles, not around orders alone. Keep a minimum stock level for fast-moving items and a maximum for slow-moving ones. Review weekly.

Customer Service and Custom Orders

For custom orders, always confirm in writing: design, size, colour, quantity, price, deposit, and delivery date. Take a 50% deposit before starting. Show a sample or drawing for approval. Build in a realistic lead time that includes drying and firing. Communicate delays early. After delivery, ask for a review and offer a small repeat-order discount.

Record Keeping and Basic Bookkeeping

Keep four simple books: cash book (money in and out), sales book (invoice number, customer, items, amount, paid/unpaid), purchase book (supplier, items, cost), and stock book (in, out, balance). File receipts in date order. Reconcile mobile money and bank statements monthly. These records are required for loans, grants, and wholesale contracts.

SAFETY WARNING LEAD GLAZE AND FOOD-SAFE CERAMICS

Do not sell or export food-contact ceramics glazed with lead or cadmium. Lead glazes are still used in some informal production and cause serious health harm, especially to children. Use lead-free, food-safe glazes and verify supplier certificates. If you export to the EU, US, or UK, your products must meet strict lead and cadmium release limits. Keep Material Safety Data Sheets (MSDS) for every glaze and raw material on file. Work in a ventilated area, wear a dust mask when mixing dry materials, and never eat or drink in the glaze room.

Reference: WHO Lead in Paint and Ceramics guidance; EU Directive 84/500/EEC and amendments; US FDA Ceramic Ware Compliance Policy Guide.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Calculate full unit cost including materials, labour, energy, overhead, and breakage before setting any price.
  2. Keep separate wholesale and retail prices; never quote from memory.
  3. Confirm every custom order in writing with a 50% deposit and a realistic lead time.
  4. Record every firing: items loaded, saleable, breakage, and fuel used.
  5. Keep four books: cash, sales, purchases, and stock; reconcile monthly.
  6. Photograph products in natural light and post with price, size, and delivery time.
  7. Verify lead-free, food-safe glaze certificates and keep MSDS on file.
  8. Review slow-moving stock weekly and adjust production before cash is tied up.
