1. Determining Maturity: Signs and Timing

Sweet potatoes are ready to harvest when the roots reach a good size and the leaves start to yellow and dry. In most African varieties, this happens 3 to 5 months after planting. For example, local varieties like 'Tanzania' or 'Kenya' mature in 3-4 months, while some improved varieties take up to 5 months. The best sign is when the vines turn yellow and begin to die back. If you dig up a test plant and the roots are 2-3 inches in diameter and have a firm skin, they are ready. Don't wait too long, because over-mature roots become woody and crack.

Timing also depends on your goal. For fresh market, harvest when roots are medium size (150-300 grams each). For processing, you can let them grow larger. In dry areas, harvest before the soil dries completely, as dry soil makes digging difficult and damages roots. In wet areas, harvest before heavy rains to avoid rot. A simple tool: a moisture meter or just feel the soil. If it's too dry, irrigate lightly a day before harvest to soften the soil.

Another sign is the 'cracking' of the soil around the plant base. As roots swell, they push the soil up, creating cracks. This is a clear indicator that roots are mature. Also, check the root's skin: mature roots have a tough skin that doesn't rub off easily with your thumb. Immature roots have thin, fragile skin. If you harvest too early, yields are low and roots don't store well. If too late, roots become fibrous and may rot in the ground.

In practice, farmers often harvest piecemeal: they dig up a few plants to check, then harvest the whole field within a week. This is common in Nigeria and Ghana. For maximum storage life, harvest when the soil is dry but not bone-dry. Early morning or late afternoon is best to avoid heat stress. Remember, sweet potatoes continue to grow until the vines die, so timing is a balance between size and quality.

To sum up: watch for yellowing vines, cracked soil, and firm skin. Test-dig a plant. Harvest at 3-5 months depending on variety. Adjust for weather. This ensures good yield and better storage.

2. Harvesting Tools and Techniques

Harvesting sweet potatoes requires the right tools to avoid damage. The most common tool in Africa is a hand hoe, but a fork or spade is better because it lifts the roots without cutting them. A digging fork with rounded tines is ideal. You can also use a machete to cut vines first, then a fork to lift roots. For large fields, a plough or tractor-drawn lifter can be used, but small-scale farmers often use manual tools. Always keep tools sharp and clean to prevent disease spread.

Technique matters: start by cutting the vines about 15-20 cm above the ground using a machete or sickle. This makes digging easier and reduces tangling. Then, insert your fork about 20-30 cm away from the plant base and push it into the soil at an angle. Lift the soil and roots gently. Don't stab directly at the roots. If using a hoe, use it to loosen soil around the plant, then pull the roots by hand. For sandy soil, you can often pull the roots by hand after loosening. In clay soil, you need more force but be careful not to bruise.

After lifting, shake off excess soil gently. Don't bang roots together to remove soil; that causes bruises. Use your hands to rub off soil. Leave roots on the surface for 30 minutes to 1 hour to dry slightly, but not too long in direct sun. In hot areas, cover with leaves or a shade cloth to prevent sunscald. Collect roots in baskets or crates lined with soft material like grass or paper. Never throw roots into a pile; place them gently.

For large-scale harvesting, you can use a potato digger or a modified plough. But even then, manual sorting follows. In many African countries, farmers hire laborers who use forks. Pay attention to soil moisture: if too dry, irrigate lightly a day before. If too wet, wait. Harvest when soil is moist but not muddy. This reduces skin damage.

A good technique is to work in rows, harvesting one row at a time. This keeps the field organized and reduces trampling. After harvesting, remove vines and debris from the field to prevent pest buildup. Remember, a careful harvest means less loss and better prices. A bruised root rots quickly, so treat each root like an egg.

3. Minimizing Mechanical Damage During Harvest

Mechanical damage includes cuts, bruises, and skinning. These open the door to rot and reduce market value. To minimize damage, handle roots gently at every step. First, ensure tools are not too sharp that they cut roots, but sharp enough to cut soil. A fork with blunt tines is better than a sharp spade. When digging, always lift from below the roots, not through them. If you feel resistance, stop and reposition. Never pull roots by the stem; that can break the root from the plant, leaving it in the soil.

Second, avoid dropping roots. When you lift a root, place it in a basket, don't toss it. Baskets should be shallow and lined with soft material like dry grass or banana leaves. Plastic crates are good but must have smooth edges. Don't overfill baskets; roots at the bottom get crushed. A common mistake is to pile roots high in a wheelbarrow; the weight causes bruising. Instead, use several small containers.

Third, minimize skinning. Skinning happens when the skin rubs off, exposing the flesh. This occurs when roots rub against each other or against rough surfaces. So, after harvest, don't wash roots immediately; let them dry in shade for a few hours. Then, gently brush off dry soil with your hands or a soft brush. Never use water to wash before curing, as moisture promotes rot. Also, avoid harvesting when soil is too wet, as wet soil sticks and requires more rubbing.

Fourth, control temperature. Heat causes roots to respire and lose moisture, making them more prone to damage. After harvest, move roots to a cool, shaded area within 1-2 hours. In hot African afternoons, use a tree shade or a simple shed. Don't leave roots in direct sun for more than 30 minutes. If you must transport, do it in the early morning or evening.

Finally, train your workers. Show them how to dig, lift, and place roots. A quick demonstration reduces damage significantly. In Kenya, studies show that proper handling can reduce post-harvest losses from 30% to 10%. So, invest time in training. Remember, every bruise is a future rotten spot. Gentle handling pays off.

4. Curing Sweet Potatoes for Storage

Curing is a process that heals wounds and thickens the skin, making sweet potatoes store longer. It involves keeping roots in warm, humid conditions for a few days. In Africa, you can cure in a simple shed or under a tree. Ideal conditions: temperature 25-30Â°C and humidity 85-90%. This promotes wound healing (suberization) and reduces rot. Curing takes 4-7 days. If you skip curing, roots may last only a few weeks; cured roots can last 2-3 months or more.

How to cure: After harvest, gently remove excess soil (don't wash). Place roots in a single layer in a shaded, well-ventilated area. You can use a curing chamber: a small room with a humidifier, but for small-scale, a simple structure with wet sacks on the floor works. In dry areas, sprinkle water on the floor to raise humidity. In humid areas, ensure good airflow to prevent mold. Cover roots with a light cloth or grass to retain humidity, but don't seal them. Check daily for any rotten roots and remove them.

After curing, roots should be stored in a cool, dark, well-ventilated place. Ideal storage temperature is 13-15Â°C with 85-90% humidity. In many African homes, a pit or a raised platform works. Pits are dug 1-2 feet deep, lined with dry grass, and roots are layered with grass between layers. The pit is covered with soil and a roof to keep rain out. This keeps them cool and humid. Alternatively, store in baskets in a dark room. Never store in plastic bags; they trap moisture and cause rot.

Curing also improves taste by converting starches to sugars. So, cured roots are sweeter. For market, cured roots fetch higher prices because they look better and last longer. If you plan to sell within a week, curing is less critical, but still beneficial. For long-term storage, curing is a must.

Monitor stored roots regularly. Remove any that show mold or soft spots. Rodents are a problem; use traps or cats. With proper curing and storage, you can sell when prices are high, not just at harvest. This is a key way to increase income. Start with a small batch to learn the process.

5. Grading and Sorting for Market

Grading and sorting mean separating sweet potatoes by size, shape, and quality. This adds value and makes buyers happy. After curing, spread roots on a clean surface. Sort into three grades: Grade A (large, uniform, no blemishes), Grade B (medium, minor defects), Grade C (small, misshapen, or damaged). In many African markets, Grade A goes to supermarkets or export, Grade B to local markets, and Grade C to processing or animal feed. Remove any rotten, cut, or bruised roots immediately; they will spoil others.

Size matters: For local markets, roots of 150-300 grams are preferred. For chips or flour, any size works. Use a simple sizing gauge: a wooden board with holes of different diameters. Or just use your hands: small (fist size), medium (two fists), large (bigger). Uniformity is key for packaging. Buyers pay more for consistent sizes. For example, in Uganda, a crate of uniform Grade A sweet potatoes can fetch 20% more than mixed grades.

Sorting also involves removing foreign matter: stones, soil clods, and vines. Clean roots with a dry cloth or soft brush. Don't wash if you plan to store; washing shortens shelf life. For market, some washing is okay if you sell immediately. But for transport, dry cleaning is better. Also, check for insect damage: small holes indicate weevils. Discard those roots.

After sorting, pack in clean crates or baskets. Label with grade and weight if possible. For export, follow buyer specifications. In Kenya, exporters require roots of 200-400 grams, smooth skin, and no cracks. So, grading is not just for looks; it meets market standards.

Finally, keep records: how many crates of each grade. This helps you plan production. If you have too many Grade C, consider processing into flour or chips. Grading takes time but pays off. Train workers to grade consistently. A simple visual guide posted on the wall helps. Remember, a well-graded product builds your reputation and brings repeat buyers.

6. Packaging and Transportation Best Practices

Packaging protects sweet potatoes from damage during transport and storage. Use clean, ventilated containers. Plastic crates with holes are best because they stack well and allow air circulation. Wooden boxes or baskets also work but must be lined with paper or grass to prevent bruising. Never use sacks that are too tight; they cause rubbing. For local transport, baskets are common, but they must be handled gently. For long-distance, use crates. Each crate should hold 20-25 kg, not more, to avoid crushing.

Before packing, ensure roots are cured and graded. Place roots in the container in layers, not thrown. Fill to the top but don't overfill. If using baskets, line with banana leaves or dry grass. For crates, you can use newspaper to separate layers. Don't mix grades in one container. Label each container with grade, weight, and date. This helps in marketing and traceability.

Transportation: Load containers carefully. Don't drop them. Stack crates only as high as recommended (usually 4-5 layers). Use a truck with a covered bed to protect from sun and rain. If open, use a tarpaulin. Avoid transport during the hottest part of the day. Early morning or evening is better. For short distances, a bicycle or motorbike with a carrier works, but secure the load. In many African countries, poor roads cause jolting; so, pack tightly to reduce movement.

Upon arrival, unload gently. Don't throw crates. Store in a cool, shaded area. If selling immediately, display in a way that doesn't bruise. For storage, keep in a well-ventilated room. Never leave in direct sun or rain. Also, avoid stacking heavy items on top of sweet potatoes.

Finally, consider packaging for value addition: small nets or paper bags for retail. For example, 1 kg packs for supermarkets. This can increase price. But ensure packaging is food-grade. In Kenya, some farmers use branded paper bags. This builds customer loyalty. Always keep records of transport losses. If you see bruising, adjust your methods. Good packaging and transport reduce losses by up to 20%. So, invest in crates and train your team.
