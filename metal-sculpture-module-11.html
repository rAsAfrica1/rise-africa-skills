1. Scaling Up: From Maquette to Monument

A maquette is a small model, usually 1:10 or 1:20 scale. To scale up, you need a grid system. Build a wooden frame around your maquette and stretch string across it every 5 cm horizontally and vertically. This creates a coordinate grid. For the full-size sculpture, build a similar frame but with string every 50 cm (if 1:10 scale). Transfer each point where the sculpture touches a grid line. Use a plumb bob to check vertical alignment. For complex curves, take cross-sections: slice the maquette virtually at intervals, measure the profile, and cut cardboard templates. These templates guide bending and welding. In Africa, use locally available materials: mild steel rods (6mm, 8mm, 12mm) from hardware stores in Nairobi or Lagos. A common mistake is to scale only the height; you must scale all three dimensions. If your maquette is 30 cm tall and you want a 3 m sculpture, multiply every measurement by 10. Keep a scaling factor chart. For very large works, use a pantograph or a digital projector to cast the maquette's shadow onto a wall, then trace. But remember, shadows distort; better to use the grid. Start by building a sturdy workbench at least 2 m x 1 m. Clamp your maquette securely. Use calipers to measure small details. For the full-size, you will need a MIG welder (180-250 amps) and an angle grinder. Work in sections: scale up the legs first, then the torso, then the head. Weld temporary braces to keep shapes from warping. Check against the maquette every hour. If you are in a rural area without electricity, use a generator (5-7 kVA) or oxy-acetylene welding. Always wear a welding helmet, gloves, and leather apron. This process takes patience: a 3 m sculpture might take 2-3 weeks of full-time work. But with a good grid, you will achieve accuracy within 1-2 cm.

2. Armatures and Internal Support Structures

An armature is the skeleton inside a sculpture. For large metal works, it must support the weight of the metal skin, resist wind (in open air), and allow for expansion. Use mild steel for the core: 12mm or 16mm round bars for main supports, 8mm for cross-bracing. For a 4 m tall figure, a central spine of 20mm pipe (schedule 40) works well. Weld a base plate (10mm thick, 300x300mm) to the bottom of the spine. This plate will bolt to a concrete foundation. Add horizontal ribs every 50 cm: these are 8mm bars bent to follow the cross-section shape. Weld them to the spine. Then add diagonal bracing (6mm bars) in a zigzag pattern to prevent twisting. For a human figure, the armature should have separate supports for arms and legs: use 12mm bars that extend from the spine outward. At joints (shoulders, hips), reinforce with gusset plates (5mm thick). If the sculpture will be outdoors, drill 10mm drainage holes at the lowest points of hollow sections to let rainwater out. For coastal areas (like Mombasa or Dakar), use galvanized or stainless steel for critical parts, or coat with zinc-rich primer. A common failure is insufficient anchoring: the armature must be strong enough that a person can hang from any point without bending. Test by applying a 50 kg load at the extremities. For abstract works, use a truss system: two parallel pipes connected by zigzag bars. This is light and strong. Remember, the armature will be hidden, so focus on strength, not beauty. But keep it accessible for future maintenance: leave a small hatch or removable panel. In Africa, recycled materials work: old bed frames, car axles, and scaffolding pipes are excellent. Clean them with a wire brush and grinder before welding. Always weld in a well-ventilated area; galvanized steel produces toxic fumes. If using recycled steel, test for weldability with a small bead first.

3. Transportation and Installation Logistics

Moving a large sculpture requires planning. First, calculate weight: steel weighs 7.85 kg per meter for 1 cm cross-section. A 4 m sculpture with 12mm bars and 3mm sheet might weigh 300-500 kg. For transport, you need a truck with a flatbed or a low-bed trailer. In many African cities, hire a 7-ton truck (e.g., Isuzu FRR) for local moves. For inter-city, use a 10-ton truck. Secure the sculpture with ratchet straps (5-ton capacity) at four points. Protect delicate parts with blankets or cardboard. If the sculpture is taller than 4 m, you may need a police escort and a route survey to avoid low bridges and power lines. For installation, you need a mobile crane or a truck-mounted crane. A 5-ton crane can lift 500 kg at 3 m radius. Book the crane for at least 4 hours. Prepare the foundation in advance: a concrete pad (minimum 1 m x 1 m x 1 m for a 500 kg sculpture) with anchor bolts (M20) set in place. Use a template to ensure bolts match the base plate. On installation day, lift the sculpture slowly. Use tag lines (ropes) to control rotation. Lower onto the bolts, then secure with nuts and washers. Check verticality with a spirit level. If the sculpture is in a public space, you may need permits from the city council (e.g., Nairobi City County) and possibly the roads authority. Allow 2-4 weeks for permits. For remote sites, consider assembling on-site: weld sections together after transport. This reduces transport size. Always have a backup plan: if the crane fails, can you use a forklift or a block and tackle? For very heavy works, hire a rigging company. Document everything with photos and videos for your portfolio. Insurance is wise: transit insurance and public liability. Typical costs: truck hire $200-$500, crane $300-$800 per day, permits $50-$200. Budget 10% extra for contingencies.

4. Public Art: Proposal Writing and Permitting

A public art proposal must convince a committee. Start with a one-page summary: title, concept, dimensions, materials, budget, timeline. Then a detailed section: concept statement (why this sculpture for this site), design sketches (3-5 views), maquette photos, and a site plan showing placement. Include a budget breakdown: materials (steel, paint, concrete), labour (your time and assistants), equipment (crane, welder), transport, permits, insurance, and a 15% contingency. For a 3 m sculpture, typical budget in Africa: materials $800-$1500, labour $1000-$2000, equipment $500-$1000, permits $100-$300, total $2400-$4800. Add your artist fee (20-30% of costs). Write a maintenance plan: how to clean, repaint, and inspect. Include a timeline: design (2 weeks), fabrication (4-6 weeks), installation (1 week). For permits, contact the local council's arts and culture department. In Kenya, you need a permit from the county government and possibly the National Museums of Kenya if the site is heritage. In Nigeria, state arts councils. In South Africa, local municipalities. Provide a site map, proof of landowner permission (e.g., letter from the mall or park), and your ID. Fees vary: $50-$200. For private commissions (hotels, malls), the process is simpler: just a contract. Always get a written agreement specifying payment schedule (e.g., 30% deposit, 40% on approval of maquette, 30% on installation). Include a clause for changes: extra work billed at hourly rate. For public art, you may need to present to a community board. Prepare a 5-minute pitch and answer questions. Show you understand the site: sun direction, wind, pedestrian flow. Propose a sculpture that is safe: no sharp edges, stable, and resistant to climbing. If children can reach it, ensure no gaps that trap heads. Finally, submit a digital portfolio with previous work. Even if you have none, show your maquette and drawings. Persistence pays: it may take 3-5 proposals before you win a commission.

5. Collaboration with Engineers and Fabricators

You are the artist, but for large-scale work you need engineers and specialist fabricators. A structural engineer can calculate wind loads and foundation sizes. For a 5 m sculpture in a windy area, wind load can be 1.5 kN/m. The engineer will tell you the required base plate thickness and anchor bolt size. In Africa, find engineers through local universities (e.g., Makerere, University of Nairobi) or professional bodies (IEK, ECSA). Expect to pay $200-$500 for a stamped drawing. For fabrication, you may need a workshop with a 5-ton overhead crane, a plasma cutter, and a large MIG welder. In cities like Johannesburg, Lagos, or Nairobi, there are metal fabrication shops that can cut, bend, and weld to your specifications. Provide them with detailed shop drawings: every part dimensioned, with weld symbols. Use a CAD program like AutoCAD or free alternatives like LibreCAD. If you can't draw, make a full-size cardboard template and trace onto steel. When working with a fabricator, agree on tolerances: 2 mm for critical joints. Visit the shop daily to check progress. For specialised tasks like casting bronze, find a foundry. In West Africa, the Igun Street foundries in Benin City, Nigeria, are famous for lost-wax casting. For stainless steel, look for workshops that do kitchen equipment. Always get a written quote and timeline. Pay 50% deposit, 50% on delivery. Keep a communication log: emails, WhatsApp messages. If the fabricator makes a mistake, be calm but firm: show the drawing and ask for correction. For complex curves, consider using a CNC plasma table; many shops have them. If not, use a template and hand-cut with a plasma torch. Remember, engineers and fabricators are partners, not servants. Respect their expertise. They may suggest cheaper or stronger methods. For example, they might recommend using a truss instead of a solid plate. Listen, but ensure the artistic vision is preserved. Build a team you can rely on for future projects.

6. Project: Proposal for a Public Sculpture

For this project, you will create a complete proposal for a public sculpture of your design. Choose a real site in your community: a roundabout, park, school, or market square. The sculpture must be at least 2 m tall and made of metal. Follow these steps: 1) Site analysis: visit the site, take photos, note sun direction, wind, pedestrian traffic, and existing structures. Measure available space. 2) Concept: write a 200-word statement explaining what the sculpture represents and why it suits the site. 3) Maquette: build a 1:10 scale model using wire, cardboard, or clay. Photograph it from multiple angles. 4) Drawings: produce a dimensioned sketch of the front, side, and top views. Include a site plan showing placement. 5) Budget: list materials (steel bars, sheet, paint, concrete), labour (your hours at a fair rate, e.g., $10/hour), equipment (welder, grinder, crane), transport, permits, and a 15% contingency. For a 2 m sculpture, aim for a total of $1500-$3000. 6) Timeline: a Gantt chart showing weeks for design, fabrication, and installation. 7) Permitting: identify the office you need to contact (e.g., county arts department) and draft a cover letter. 8) Maintenance: describe how to clean and inspect annually. 9) Presentation: compile everything into a PDF (max 10 pages). If you don't have a computer, make a physical folder with printed pages and drawings. 10) Submit to your instructor for feedback. This project simulates a real commission. You can also present it to your local council for actual consideration. Many public sculptures in Africa started as student projects. Be bold: your proposal could become a landmark. Remember, the best proposals are clear, visual, and realistic. Avoid overpromising. Show you can deliver. Include a photo of yourself with your maquette to personalise it. This project is your portfolio piece. It proves you can think like a professional artist.

<h2>Practical Reference: Large-Scale and Public Art in Metal Sculpture</h2>
<p><strong>Module:</strong> Large-Scale and Public Art <strong>Lesson:</strong> Practical Reference <strong>Audience:</strong> African metal sculptors, fabricators and studio managers moving from gallery-scale work into public commissions and monument-scale production.</p>

<p>This reference lesson is the working companion to the five lessons in this module. It is written for the studio owner or lead fabricator who must quote, build, move, install and defend a large metal sculpture often in a city where cranes, rigging crews and engineering sign-off are not automatically available. The commercial reality in most African markets is that public art is won through relationships with municipalities, corporates, developers, universities and cultural institutions, and delivered under fixed-price contracts where underestimating mass, transport or permitting can wipe out the entire margin. Treat every number below as a planning baseline, then verify locally.</p>

<h3>1. Scaling up: from maquette to monument</h3>
<p>A maquette is a sales tool and a structural preview, not a construction drawing. The two rules that govern scale-up are <em>dimensional scaling</em> (every linear dimension multiplies by the scale factor) and <em>mass scaling</em> (volume, and therefore mass, multiplies by the scale factor cubed). A 0.4 m maquette scaled to 8 m is a factor of 20 but the steel mass is 20 = 8,000 times heavier. This is why armature design, lifting points and foundation loads must be re-engineered at full scale rather than extrapolated from the model.</p>
<p>For African studios, the practical bottleneck is usually section size and transport envelope, not artistic ambition. Design the monument so it can be fabricated in modules that fit the largest truck or low-bed trailer realistically available, and so that bolted or welded field joints fall on structurally sensible lines. Keep a digital model (even a simple 3D CAD file) so engineers, fabricators and crane operators are all working from the same geometry.</p>

<h3>2. Armatures and internal support structures</h3>
<p>The armature carries wind load, self-weight and handling loads. For outdoor work in high-wind or coastal African cities (Lagos, Accra, Dar es Salaam, Cape Town), design for a minimum wind pressure consistent with the local code commonly 0.61.2 kN/m depending on terrain and height. Use mild steel sections (SHS/RHS, angle, flat bar) for hidden armatures and stainless or hot-dip galvanised steel where moisture and salt are present. All welded joints on primary structure should be full-penetration where tension governs, and every base plate should be drilled for anchor bolts before galvanising.</p>
<p>Plan corrosion protection as a line item, not an afterthought: blast or wire-brush to Sa 2 where possible, apply zinc-rich primer, intermediate coat and a UV-stable topcoat. In coastal or high-rainfall markets, budget for hot-dip galvanising plus a two-coat system, and specify stainless fixings. This is a direct commercial issue a monument that rusts within three years destroys the reference that wins the next commission.</p>

<h3>3. Transportation and installation logistics</h3>
<p>Most African public sculpture projects fail commercially at the transport and installation stage, not in the studio. Before quoting, confirm: maximum legal load dimensions, bridge and overhead clearances on the route, crane capacity and reach at the site, ground bearing capacity for the crane outriggers, and whether a road closure or police escort is required. A crane lift plan, rigging schedule and method statement should be written and signed before mobilisation.</p>

<p style="margin:0">Never lift a sculpture over people, occupied buildings or live traffic. A dropped 500 kg section is lethal. Before any lift: (1) confirm crane capacity at the actual radius, not the chart maximum; (2) check ground bearing under outriggers and use spreader mats; (3) use certified slings, shackles and lifting eyes with a documented inspection; (4) establish an exclusion zone with barriers and a banksman; (5) stop work if wind exceeds the crane manufacturers limit (commonly 10 m/s for large lifts); (6) brief the crew in a written method statement. In many African jurisdictions there is no statutory lift supervisor the studio owner carries the legal and moral duty. Insist on hard hats, high-visibility vests, safety boots and gloves, and keep a first-aid kit and emergency numbers on site.</p>

<h3>4. Public art: proposal writing and permitting</h3>
<p>A winning public art proposal is a business document. It answers: what will be built, where, why it belongs there, what it costs, who maintains it, and what the client gets in return (footfall, identity, tourism, corporate social investment visibility). Include a one-page concept, a maquette or render, a budget with contingency (1525% is realistic on first monuments), a programme, a maintenance plan, and a risk register. Permits typically involve the local municipality (land use, building/planning consent), roads authority (transport and closure), and sometimes heritage or environmental authorities. Build 612 weeks into the programme for approvals in some cities it takes longer.</p>

<h3>5. Collaboration with engineers and fabricators</h3>
<p>Engage a registered structural engineer early before the maquette is finalised. In many African markets the engineer-of-record must sign off foundation and connection design for municipal approval. Agree deliverables: stamped drawings, connection details, foundation design, lift plan and a certificate of structural adequacy. For fabrication, use a workshop with documented welding procedures and, where possible, certified welders (e.g. to ISO 9606 or AWS D1.1). Keep a single point of contact for the client and a written change-order process scope creep on public projects is the main cause of loss.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li>Confirm scale factor and recalculate mass using the cube rule (F) before quoting.</li>
<li>Design modules to fit the real transport envelope and the crane available at site.</li>
<li>Have a registered engineer sign off armature, connections and foundation.</li>
<li>Specify corrosion protection appropriate to coastal, high-rainfall or urban pollution conditions.</li>
<li>Write a lift plan and method statement; use certified gear and an exclusion zone.</li>
<li>Budget 1525% contingency and 612 weeks for permits and approvals.</li>
<li>Price transport, rigging and installation from three written quotes, not estimates.</li>
<li>Offer a maintenance contract recurring revenue and a reference for the next commission.</li>
</ul>
