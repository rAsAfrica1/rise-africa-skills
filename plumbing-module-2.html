1. Module 2 Lesson 1: Understanding Water Supply Systems in African Homes

Welcome to Module 2 of your plumbing training. In this first lesson we focus on water supply systems, which are the foundation of every plumbing installation you will encounter. Across Africa, water supply varies greatly from urban piped networks to rural boreholes, hand-dug wells, and rainwater harvesting. Your job as a plumber is to understand how water gets from its source to the tap, and how to install, maintain, and repair the components that make this possible. We begin with the main types of water supply. The first is the direct mains supply, common in cities like Nairobi, Lagos, Accra, and Johannesburg. Here water arrives under pressure from the municipal network and flows directly to fixtures. The second is the indirect supply, where water is stored in tanks, usually on rooftops or on raised stands, and then gravity-fed to the building. This is very common in areas with intermittent supply or low pressure. The third is the pumped supply, where a booster pump moves water from a ground tank or borehole to an elevated tank or directly into the building. Understanding which system you are working with determines the pipe sizes, fittings, and valves you will use. Next, let us look at the components of a typical supply system. The service pipe connects the main or tank to the building. It should be buried below the frost line, though frost is rare in most of Africa, and protected from damage. The water meter measures consumption and is usually located at the property boundary. After the meter you will find the main shutoff valve, which every plumber must know how to operate. Inside the building, the distribution pipes branch to different fixtures. These pipes can be made of galvanized steel, copper, PPR, PEX, or PVC. In many African countries, PPR and PEX are becoming popular because they resist corrosion and are easy to join. Galvanized steel is still found in older buildings but tends to rust and scale, reducing water flow. Copper is durable but expensive and can be stolen for scrap, so it is less common in some areas. PVC is mainly used for drainage and cold water, not for hot water. When installing supply pipes, always follow local plumbing codes. In Kenya, for example, the Kenya Bureau of Standards provides guidelines. In Nigeria, the Nigerian Building Code applies. These codes specify pipe sizes, support spacing, and testing procedures. A common rule is that the pipe diameter must be large enough to deliver the required flow at the available pressure. Too small a pipe causes low pressure and noise. Too large a pipe wastes material and money. For a typical house, a 20 mm or 25 mm service pipe is often sufficient, with 15 mm branches to individual fixtures. Pressure is another critical factor. Most fixtures need a minimum pressure of about 1 bar to work well. If pressure is too low, you may need a booster pump or an elevated tank. If pressure is too high, you must install a pressure-reducing valve to protect fixtures and pipes from bursting. In many African cities, pressure fluctuates throughout the day, so a storage tank with a float valve is a practical solution. The float valve shuts off the water when the tank is full, preventing overflow and waste. Water quality also matters. Sediment, rust, and bacteria can damage valves and harm users. Install a strainer or filter before the water heater and consider a whole-house filter if the source is a borehole or well. For drinking water, point-of-use filters or boiling are recommended. As a plumber, you should always advise customers on simple water treatment options. Now let us discuss testing and commissioning. After installing a supply system, you must pressure-test it. Close all outlets, connect a test pump to the system, and pressurize to at least 1.5 times the working pressure, but not less than 10 bar for plastic pipes or as specified by the manufacturer. Hold the pressure for at least one hour. If it drops, there is a leak. Find and repair it before proceeding. Also flush the system to remove debris. Open all taps and let water run until it is clear. Check for leaks at every joint. Only then should you hand over the system to the customer. Safety is paramount. Always wear eye protection and gloves when working with pipes and chemicals. Use proper tools, including pipe cutters, deburring tools, and correct wrenches. When working on live systems, shut off the water and drain the pipes. Be aware of electrical hazards if pumps or heaters are involved. In rural areas, you may encounter hand pumps and windmills. Learn how to service these too, as they are lifelines for many communities. Finally, remember that in Africa, water is precious. Your work directly affects health, sanitation, and daily life. A well-installed supply system saves time, money, and water. A poorly installed one wastes all three. Practice your skills, ask questions, and keep learning. In the next lesson we will cover drainage systems, which are equally important for safe sanitation. Before you move on, complete this practical task: sketch a simple indirect water supply system for a two-bedroom house with a rooftop tank. Label the service pipe, float valve, distribution pipes, and fixtures. Then list the tools and materials you would need. This will prepare you for hands-on work in the field.

2. Module 2 Lesson 2: Drainage and Sanitation Systems for African Buildings

In this lesson we turn to drainage and sanitation, the other half of every plumbing system. While supply brings clean water in, drainage takes wastewater and sewage out. If supply is done poorly, you get low pressure. If drainage is done poorly, you get blockages, bad smells, and disease. In Africa, where many communities face sanitation challenges, your skills as a plumber can save lives. Let us start with the types of drainage systems. The first is the combined system, where stormwater and sewage flow in the same pipe. This is common in older cities but is not recommended because heavy rain can overwhelm the system and cause sewage to back up into homes. The second is the separate system, where stormwater and sewage use different pipes. This is the modern standard and is required in most new construction. The third is the on-site system, where sewage is treated in a septic tank or pit latrine, and stormwater is drained to the ground or a soakaway. This is common in rural areas and peri-urban settlements. Next, let us examine the components of a drainage system. The soil pipe carries sewage from toilets. It must be at least 100 mm in diameter and made of PVC or cast iron. The waste pipe carries wastewater from sinks, showers, and basins. It is usually 40 mm to 50 mm in diameter. The vent pipe allows air into the system so that water flows smoothly and sewer gases escape above the roof. Without proper venting, traps will siphon dry and smells will enter the building. The trap is a U-shaped fitting that holds water to block gases. Every fixture must have a trap. The drain pipe slopes downward to carry waste to the sewer or septic tank. The slope, or fall, is critical. Too little slope and solids settle, causing blockages. Too much slope and water runs away too fast, leaving solids behind. The recommended fall for a 100 mm pipe is about 1 in 40 to 1 in 60, which means 2.5 cm to 1.7 cm per meter. For smaller pipes, use 1 in 20 to 1 in 30. Always use a spirit level to check your slope. Now let us discuss septic tanks and soakaways. A septic tank is a watertight chamber that separates solids, liquids, and scum. It should be at least 2 meters from the building and 15 meters from any water source. The size depends on the number of users. For a family of six, a 2,000-liter tank is usually adequate. The tank has an inlet, an outlet, and a manhole for emptying. The outlet leads to a soakaway, which is a pit filled with stones or rubble where liquid seeps into the ground. Soakaways must be at least 1 meter above the water table and not in waterlogged soil. In many African cities, septic tanks are emptied by vacuum trucks, but in rural areas, manual emptying is sometimes done. As a plumber, you should design systems that are easy to empty and maintain. Pit latrines are another common sanitation option. A simple pit latrine is a hole in the ground with a slab and a shelter. A ventilated improved pit latrine, or VIP, has a vent pipe that reduces flies and smells. A pour-flush latrine uses a small amount of water to flush waste into a pit. When installing or repairing latrines, ensure the pit is at least 30 meters from any well or borehole, and preferably downhill from it. Line the pit with bricks or concrete rings to prevent collapse. The slab must be strong and fit tightly to keep out flies and rain. Now let us cover pipe materials and installation. For drainage, PVC is the most common material in Africa because it is cheap, durable, and easy to work with. Use solvent cement to join PVC pipes. Apply cement to both the pipe and the fitting, push together with a quarter turn, and hold for a few seconds. For larger pipes, you may use rubber ring joints. Cast iron is used in older buildings and where fire resistance is required. It is heavy and expensive but quiet. Asbestos cement pipes are found in some older systems and are hazardous if disturbed. Never cut or sand asbestos pipes without proper protective equipment. If you encounter them, advise the customer to have them professionally removed. When installing drainage, always support the pipes properly. Use brackets or clips every 1 meter for vertical pipes and every 1.5 meters for horizontal pipes. Provide access points, such as rodding eyes and inspection chambers, so that blockages can be cleared easily. An inspection chamber is a small box with a removable cover, placed at changes of direction or where pipes meet. It should be at least 300 mm by 300 mm and have a channel at the bottom to guide flow. Testing drainage systems is different from testing supply systems. You cannot pressure-test with water because the pipes are open to the atmosphere. Instead, you do a water test or an air test. For a water test, plug the lower end of the system and fill it with water to the level of the highest fixture. Let it stand for at least 15 minutes. If the water level drops, there is a leak. For an air test, plug all openings and pump air into the system to a pressure of about 38 mm of water gauge. If the pressure holds for 5 minutes, the system is sound. Always test before you bury pipes or close walls. Safety in drainage work is serious. Sewage contains bacteria, viruses, and parasites. Always wear gloves, boots, and eye protection. Use a mask when working in confined spaces or near open sewage. Wash your hands thoroughly with soap and water after work. Never smoke or eat while working on drainage. If you must enter a septic tank or pit, follow confined space procedures: test the air for toxic gases, use a harness and tripod, and have a standby person outside. Many plumbers have died from hydrogen sulfide gas in septic tanks. Do not become a statistic. Finally, consider the African context. In many areas, water is scarce, so use low-flush toilets and waterless urinals where possible. In flood-prone areas, install backflow valves to prevent sewage from entering homes during heavy rains. In informal settlements, shared sanitation blocks require careful design to handle high usage. Your work must be practical, affordable, and culturally acceptable. For your practical task, design a drainage system for a three-bedroom house with two bathrooms, a kitchen, and a laundry. Show the soil pipes, waste pipes, vent pipes, traps, and the connection to a septic tank and soakaway. Calculate the slope for each horizontal run. List the fittings you would need. This will prepare you for the next lesson on fixtures and appliances.

3. Module 2 Lesson 3: Installing and Repairing Plumbing Fixtures and Appliances

In this lesson we focus on the fixtures and appliances that people use every day: toilets, taps, showers, sinks, water heaters, and washing machines. As a plumber in Africa, you will encounter a wide range of fixtures, from modern imported models to locally made and older units. Your ability to install, maintain, and repair them will determine your reputation and income. We start with toilets. The most common types are the close-coupled toilet, the low-level toilet, and the squat toilet. The close-coupled toilet has the cistern sitting directly on the pan. The low-level toilet has the cistern mounted on the wall above the pan, connected by a flush pipe. The squat toilet is set into the floor and is common in many African countries for cultural and practical reasons. When installing a toilet, first check the floor drain or soil pipe position. The pan must sit level and be sealed to the floor with a wax ring or silicone sealant. The cistern must be connected to the water supply with a flexible hose and an isolation valve. The flush mechanism can be a siphon, a flap valve, or a dual-flush valve. Dual-flush toilets save water, which is important in dry regions. Common problems include a running cistern, a weak flush, and a leaking pan. A running cistern is usually caused by a worn flapper or a faulty float valve. Replace the flapper or adjust the float. A weak flush may be due to a blocked rim or a low water level. Clean the rim holes and adjust the float. A leaking pan is often due to a worn wax ring or loose bolts. Tighten the bolts or replace the ring. Next, taps and mixers. Taps come in many types: pillar taps, bib taps, pillar mixers, and single-lever mixers. They are made of brass, chrome-plated brass, or plastic. In Africa, plastic taps are common in low-cost housing, but they crack easily. Brass taps last longer but are more expensive. When installing a tap, use PTFE tape or thread sealant on the threads. Do not overtighten, as this can crack the fitting. For mixers, ensure the hot and cold inlets are connected correctly. In most countries, hot is on the left and cold on the right. Test for leaks after installation. Common tap problems include dripping, low flow, and stiff handles. A dripping tap is usually caused by a worn washer or ceramic cartridge. Turn off the water, dismantle the tap, and replace the washer or cartridge. Low flow may be due to a blocked aerator or a clogged supply line. Clean the aerator and flush the line. A stiff handle may need lubrication or a new cartridge. Showers are another important fixture. They can be simple hand showers or fixed rain showers. The shower mixer must be connected to both hot and cold supplies. The showerhead should be at least 2 meters above the floor. In many African homes, showers are installed over a bathtub or in a tiled cubicle. Waterproofing is essential. Use cement board or waterproof membrane behind the tiles. The drain must have a proper trap and slope. Common problems include low pressure, leaking joints, and clogged showerheads. Descale the showerhead by soaking it in vinegar or a commercial descaler. Check the flexible hose for cracks. Sinks and basins are similar. They require a trap, a waste pipe, and a tap. The trap must be vented or self-venting to prevent gurgling. The waste pipe must slope to the drain. In kitchens, install a grease trap if the sink is used heavily. Grease traps prevent blockages in the main drainage line. Clean the grease trap regularly. Water heaters are critical appliances. In Africa, you will find electric storage heaters, instant electric heaters, solar water heaters, and gas heaters. Solar heaters are popular because they save electricity, but they require a backup element for cloudy days. Electric storage heaters have a tank and a heating element. They must be connected to a safety valve and an expansion tank. The safety valve releases pressure if the thermostat fails. Never block or remove it. Gas heaters must be installed by a licensed gas fitter. They require proper ventilation to prevent carbon monoxide poisoning. Common water heater problems include no hot water, insufficient hot water, and leaks. No hot water may be due to a tripped breaker, a faulty element, or a broken thermostat. Test the element with a multimeter and replace if necessary. Insufficient hot water may be due to a undersized tank or a dip tube failure. Leaks may come from the tank, the fittings, or the safety valve. If the tank is leaking, replace the heater. Washing machines and dishwashers are increasingly common in African cities. They require a water supply, a drain, and often a dedicated electrical circuit. The supply should have a shutoff valve and a hammer arrestor to prevent water hammer. The drain hose must be connected to a standpipe or a sink trap with an air gap. Never connect the drain hose directly to a soil pipe without an air gap, as this can cause sewage to back up into the machine. Common problems include leaks, poor drainage, and error codes. Check the inlet hoses and filters. Clean the drain pump filter. If the machine does not fill, check the water supply and the inlet valve. When installing any fixture or appliance, follow the manufacturer instructions. Use the correct tools and materials. Do not mix metals that corrode, such as copper and galvanized steel, without a dielectric union. Support heavy fixtures properly. Use isolation valves on every supply line so that future repairs are easy. Label the valves if there are many. In Africa, water pressure can be unreliable, so consider installing a small pressure tank or a booster pump for appliances that need consistent pressure. Finally, always test your work. Turn on the water, check for leaks, and operate the fixture through a full cycle. For water heaters, check the temperature and the safety valve. For toilets, check the flush and the refill. For taps, check both hot and cold. Leave the work area clean and explain the basic maintenance to the customer. A good plumber is not just a repairer but a teacher. For your practical task, choose three fixtures: a toilet, a tap, and a water heater. For each, write a step-by-step installation guide and a troubleshooting checklist for three common problems. Then practice installing or repairing them in your workshop. This will build your confidence and speed.

4. Module 2 Lesson 4: Pipe Materials, Fittings, and Joining Techniques in Africa

In this lesson we dive deep into pipe materials, fittings, and joining techniques. This is the hands-on core of plumbing. In Africa, you will work with many materials, and each has its own rules. Knowing them well will make your work faster, stronger, and leak-free. We start with the main pipe materials. The first is galvanized steel. It is strong and can handle hot water, but it corrodes from the inside, especially in areas with acidic water. It is joined by threaded fittings. You need a pipe wrench, a die set, and cutting oil. The threads must be clean and tight. Use PTFE tape or pipe dope on the male threads. Galvanized pipes are still common in older buildings and for outdoor use, but they are being replaced by plastics. The second is copper. Copper is durable, handles hot water, and resists corrosion. It is joined by soldering or compression fittings. Soldering requires a torch, flux, and solder. The pipe must be clean and dry. Compression fittings are easier and do not require a torch, but they can loosen over time. Copper is expensive and is often stolen for scrap, so it is less common in some African countries. The third is PVC, or polyvinyl chloride. PVC is cheap, easy to cut, and joined with solvent cement. It is used for cold water and drainage. It is not suitable for hot water because it softens and deforms. CPVC is a variant that can handle hot water, but it is more expensive and less available in Africa. The fourth is PPR, or polypropylene random copolymer. PPR is popular in East Africa and is gaining ground elsewhere. It is joined by heat fusion, using a welding machine with a heating element and dies. The pipe and fitting are heated and then pushed together. The joint is stronger than the pipe itself. PPR handles hot and cold water and is resistant to corrosion. It is more expensive than PVC but cheaper than copper. The fifth is PEX, or cross-linked polyethylene. PEX is flexible, easy to install, and handles hot and cold water. It is joined with crimp rings, clamp rings, or push-fit fittings. PEX is great for retrofitting because it can bend around corners, reducing the number of fittings. However, it is not widely available in all African countries, and it can be damaged by UV light, so it must be protected. The sixth is HDPE, or high-density polyethylene. HDPE is used for underground water mains and borehole pipes. It is joined by butt fusion or electrofusion. It is tough, flexible, and resistant to chemicals. It is not used inside buildings because it is not rated for hot water and looks unattractive. Now let us discuss fittings. Fittings connect pipes, change direction, reduce size, or branch. Common fittings include elbows, tees, couplings, reducers, caps, and unions. Unions are important because they allow you to disconnect pipes for repair. Always install a union near valves and fixtures. For threaded fittings, use PTFE tape on all threads except the first two. Wrap the tape clockwise, in the direction of the threads. For solvent-welded PVC, apply cement to both surfaces, push together with a quarter turn, and hold for 30 seconds. Do not touch the joint for at least 15 minutes. For PPR, heat the pipe and fitting for the specified time, usually 5 to 10 seconds for 20 mm pipe, then push together and hold for 30 seconds. Do not twist. For PEX, use the correct crimp tool and rings. Check the crimp with a go gauge. For compression fittings, tighten the nut by hand and then one quarter turn with a wrench. Do not overtighten, as this can crush the ferrule. Now let us talk about pipe sizing. The size of the pipe depends on the flow rate and the pressure. For a single tap, a 15 mm pipe is usually enough. For a bathroom group, use 20 mm. For a whole house, use 25 mm or 32 mm. For a main line from the meter, use 32 mm or 40 mm. Always check the local code. In many African countries, the minimum size for a service pipe is 20 mm. For drainage, the minimum size for a toilet is 100 mm, for a sink is 40 mm, and for a shower is 50 mm. Use the correct size to avoid noise, pressure loss, and blockages. Support and protection are also important. Pipes must be supported to prevent sagging and vibration. Use plastic clips or metal brackets. Space them every 1 meter for horizontal runs and every 1.2 meters for vertical runs. For hot water pipes, use clips that allow expansion. Insulate hot water pipes to save energy and prevent burns. Insulate cold water pipes in hot climates to prevent condensation. Bury underground pipes below the frost line, though frost is rare in most of Africa. In areas with heavy traffic, use a concrete slab or a sleeve to protect the pipe. Now let us cover common mistakes. One is mixing materials without a dielectric union. When copper and galvanized steel touch, they corrode quickly. Use a brass or plastic union between them. Another mistake is using too many fittings. Every fitting adds friction and a potential leak. Use bends instead of elbows where possible. Another mistake is not deburring cut pipes. Burrs create turbulence and can damage seals. Always deburr with a reamer or a knife. Another mistake is not pressure-testing. Always test before you close walls or bury pipes. Another mistake is using the wrong cement for PVC. Use PVC cement for PVC, not ABS cement. Another mistake is not allowing for expansion. PPR and PEX expand and contract with temperature changes. Use expansion loops or offsets. Finally, let us discuss tools. You need a pipe cutter for plastic and copper, a hacksaw for steel, a deburring tool, a crimp tool for PEX, a heat fusion tool for PPR, a threading machine or die set for steel, a torch for soldering, a pressure test pump, a spirit level, a tape measure, and a set of wrenches. Keep your tools clean and sharp. A dull cutter crushes the pipe. A dirty torch gives a bad flame. In Africa, you may not have access to every tool, so learn to improvise safely. For example, you can use a hacksaw to cut PPR, but you must deburr carefully. You can use a propane torch for soldering copper, but be careful with open flames near thatch or flammable materials. For your practical task, collect samples of at least four pipe materials and their fittings. Practice joining each type. Make a small test rig with a pressure gauge and test each joint to 10 bar. Record which joints leak and why. This will teach you more than any book. In the next lesson we will cover plumbing codes, safety, and customer service.

5. Module 2 Lesson 5: Plumbing Codes, Safety, and Customer Service in African Contexts

In this final lesson of Module 2, we cover the professional side of plumbing: codes, safety, and customer service. These topics may seem less exciting than pipes and fittings, but they are what separate a handyman from a professional plumber. In Africa, where regulation varies widely, your integrity and knowledge will set you apart. Let us start with plumbing codes. A code is a set of rules that ensures plumbing systems are safe, sanitary, and efficient. In Africa, codes are set at national, regional, or municipal levels. For example, Kenya has the Kenya Bureau of Standards and the National Construction Authority. Nigeria has the Nigerian Building Code and the Standards Organisation of Nigeria. South Africa has the South African National Standards, including SANS 10252 for water supply and drainage. Ghana has the Ghana Building Code. Tanzania has the Tanzania Bureau of Standards. These codes cover pipe sizes, materials, slopes, venting, backflow prevention, and water quality. As a plumber, you must know the code for your area. If you do not, you risk failed inspections, legal liability, and unsafe systems. Codes are not just bureaucracy. They are based on decades of experience and research. For example, the requirement for a vent pipe prevents traps from siphoning and keeps sewer gases out of homes. The requirement for a backflow preventer keeps contaminated water from entering the clean supply. The requirement for a minimum slope ensures that drains do not block. When you follow the code, you protect your customers and yourself. If a customer asks you to cut corners, explain why the code matters. If they insist, walk away. Your reputation is worth more than one job. Now let us discuss safety. Plumbing can be dangerous. You face electrical hazards, chemical hazards, biological hazards, and physical hazards. Let us go through each. Electrical hazards: water and electricity do not mix. When working near water heaters, pumps, or washing machines, turn off the power at the breaker. Use a voltage tester to confirm. Never work with wet hands. Use ground fault circuit interrupters on temporary power. If you must work on a live circuit, use insulated tools and wear rubber gloves. Chemical hazards: you work with solvents, cement, flux, and drain cleaners. These can burn your skin, damage your eyes, and produce toxic fumes. Always wear gloves, eye protection, and a respirator when needed. Work in a well-ventilated area. Store chemicals in original containers, away from children and animals. Never mix chemicals, especially bleach and ammonia, which produce deadly gas. Biological hazards: sewage contains bacteria, viruses, and parasites. Wear gloves, boots, and eye protection. Cover cuts with waterproof bandages. Wash your hands with soap and water after work. Do not eat, drink, or smoke while working on drainage. If you get sewage in your eyes or mouth, rinse immediately and seek medical help. Physical hazards: you lift heavy pipes, work in awkward positions, and use sharp tools. Lift with your legs, not your back. Use a trolley or a helper for heavy items. Keep your work area clean to prevent trips and falls. Use a ladder safely: three points of contact, no overreaching, and never stand on the top rung. When working in trenches, shore them up to prevent collapse. When working on roofs, use a harness and a safety line. In Africa, you may also face hazards from wildlife, such as snakes and insects, and from extreme weather, such as heat and rain. Plan your work accordingly. Now let us cover customer service. As a plumber, you are a service provider. Your technical skills matter, but so do your people skills. Here are some key principles. First, be punctual. If you say you will arrive at 8 AM, be there at 8 AM. If you are delayed, call the customer. Time is money for everyone. Second, be clean and tidy. Wear clean work clothes. Use a mat to protect floors. Clean up after yourself. Leave the work area better than you found it. Third, communicate clearly. Explain what you are going to do, how long it will take, and how much it will cost. Get approval before starting. If you find additional problems, explain them and get approval before fixing them. Do not surprise the customer with a big bill. Fourth, be honest. Do not overcharge. Do not claim to have done work you did not do. Do not use cheap materials and charge for expensive ones. In Africa, word of mouth is powerful. A honest plumber gets more work than a dishonest one. Fifth, respect the customer. Respect their home, their culture, and their budget. Remove your shoes if that is the custom. Do not comment on their belongings or lifestyle. If they cannot afford a full repair, offer a temporary solution and explain the risks. Sixth, provide a warranty. If you install a fixture or repair a pipe, guarantee your work for a reasonable period, such as 30 days. If something fails due to your workmanship, fix it for free. This builds trust. Seventh, keep records. Write down what you did, the materials used, and the date. Give the customer a copy. This helps with future repairs and protects you if there is a dispute. Eighth, continue learning. Plumbing technology changes. New materials, new fixtures, and new codes emerge. Attend training, read manuals, and talk to other plumbers. Join a professional association if one exists in your country. In Africa, there are associations like the Plumbers Association of Kenya, the Nigerian Association of Plumbers, and the Institute of Plumbing South Africa. These groups offer training, networking, and advocacy. Finally, think about your business. Many plumbers in Africa work informally. To grow, consider registering your business, getting a tax identification number, and opening a bank account. This allows you to take on larger contracts and work with government and NGOs. Keep track of your income and expenses. Save for tools and training. Hire apprentices and train them well. Your business can create jobs and improve sanitation in your community. For your practical task, write a one-page code of conduct for yourself as a plumber. Include rules for safety, honesty, punctuality, and customer respect. Then visit a local plumbing supplier and ask about the codes and standards for your area. Finally, practice explaining a plumbing repair to a friend or family member in simple, non-technical language. This will prepare you for the real world. Congratulations on completing Module 2. You now have a solid foundation in water supply, drainage, fixtures, pipe materials, and professionalism. In Module 3, we will cover advanced topics such as pump installation, solar water heating, and commercial plumbing systems. Keep practicing, stay safe, and serve your community well.

<h3>Why sizing and layout decide your profit</h3>
<p>Most plumbing failures in African homes are not caused by poor workmanship alone. They are caused by under-sized storage, wrong pipe diameter for the available head, or drainage falls that are too shallow for the solids load. Each of these leads to a callback, and callbacks destroy margin. If you size correctly once, you sell the job, get paid, and get referred. This lesson gives you the numbers to do that.</p>

<div style="border:3px solid #b00020;background:#fdecea;padding:14px;margin:18px 0;font-family:Arial;">
<strong style="color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING WATER QUALITY AND CHEMICALS</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Never connect a storage tank directly to a sewer or septic line, and never allow cross-connection between drinking water pipes and drainage. Backflow can introduce <em>E. coli</em>, <em>Salmonella</em>, and <em>Vibrio cholerae</em> into the household supply. When chlorinating a tank, use only approved chlorine products at the dose stated on the label (typically 25 mg/L free chlorine residual). Wear gloves and eye protection, never mix chlorine with acid or ammonia-based cleaners, and ventilate the area. If a client reports diarrhoea or cholera in the household, stop work, advise them to boil or treat water, and refer them to the local health authority. Plumbing work that touches drinking water is a public health activity treat it as such.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Confirm the client's daily demand (persons L/person/day) before quoting tank size.</li>
<li>Measure the actual head (tank outlet to highest fixture) do not assume 2 m.</li>
<li>Select pipe diameter from Table 1, then go one size up if head is under 1 m.</li>
<li>Check drain fall with a spirit level: minimum 1:60, target 1:40.</li>
<li>Pressure-test the supply line at 1.5 working pressure for at least 30 minutes before covering.</li>
<li>Confirm no cross-connection between supply and drainage; install a backflow preventer where required.</li>
<li>Size septic tank and soakaway from Table 2, and keep 30 m from any well or borehole.</li>
<li>Hand over with a written maintenance schedule: tank cleaning every 6 months, drain rodding every 12 months.</li>
</ol>

<h3>Business-first application</h3>
<p>In African markets, the plumber who can show a client a sizing table and a pressure-test record wins the job over the plumber who guesses. Use these tables in your quotation. Add a line item for "system sizing and pressure test" it is legitimate, it protects you, and it justifies a 1020% premium. For landlords with multiple units, offer an annual maintenance contract covering tank cleaning, drain rodding, and leak inspection. That recurring revenue is what turns a plumbing trade into a plumbing business.</p>
