1. Nutritional Requirements of Farmed Fish

Fish need six main nutrients: protein, carbohydrates, fats, vitamins, minerals, and water. Protein is the most critical for growth. For tilapia, a common farmed fish in Africa, fingerlings need 35-40% protein in their feed, while adults need 28-32%. Catfish require even more: 40-45% for fry, 35-40% for adults. If you feed too little protein, fish grow slowly and you waste money on feed. Too much protein is also wasteful because fish cannot store it and excrete it as ammonia, which pollutes water.

Carbohydrates are cheaper energy sources. Tilapia can use up to 40% carbohydrates in their diet, but catfish handle only 20-25%. Fats provide concentrated energy and essential fatty acids. A good feed has 5-10% fat. Vitamins and minerals are needed in tiny amounts but are vital for health and bone formation. In Africa, many farmers use agricultural by-products like rice bran, maize bran, and cassava peels. These are good energy sources but low in protein. To meet protein needs, you must add protein-rich ingredients like fishmeal, soybean meal, or cottonseed meal.

Water quality affects nutrition too. If water is too cold (below 20C), fish eat less and digest slowly. In warm African climates (25-30C), fish eat more and grow faster. So feed according to temperature and fish size. A practical rule: check your fish weekly. If they are not growing, adjust protein or feeding rate. Local feed mills often sell pellets with guaranteed analysis on the bag. Read the label: it tells you protein, fat, and fibre percentages. Choose feed that matches your fish species and age. Remember, underfeeding causes stunted growth, but overfeeding wastes money and fouls water.

2. Types of Fish Feed: Commercial and Farm-Made

Commercial fish feed comes in three forms: sinking pellets, floating pellets, and crumbles. Floating pellets are best for tilapia because you can see if they eat. Sinking pellets suit catfish that feed at the bottom. Crumbles are for fry and fingerlings. In Africa, brands like Aller Aqua, Skretting, and local mills like Raanan Fish Feed (Nigeria) or Kenya's Unga Farm Care sell feeds. A 20 kg bag of 30% protein tilapia feed costs about $25-40, depending on country. Commercial feed is convenient and balanced, but expensive. Many small-scale farmers cannot afford it alone.

Farm-made feed is cheaper but requires careful mixing. You can make feed using locally available ingredients: fishmeal (from local fish processing waste), soybean meal, maize bran, rice bran, cassava flour, and vitamin premix. A simple recipe for tilapia grow-out: 30% fishmeal, 30% soybean meal, 35% maize bran, 5% vitamin/mineral premix. Mix dry ingredients, then add water to make a dough. Pass through a mincer or meat grinder to form pellets. Sun-dry for 1-2 days until moisture is below 10%. Store in sacks away from damp.

Farm-made feed has risks: nutrient levels vary, and mixing may be inconsistent. Always use a scale, not cups. A 20 kg batch should have exactly 6 kg fishmeal, 6 kg soybean meal, etc. If you cannot get fishmeal, use more soybean meal but add 1% methionine (an amino acid) because soybean lacks it. For catfish, increase fishmeal to 40%. Also, consider using black soldier fly larvae (BSF) as a protein source. You can raise BSF on kitchen waste; dried larvae contain 40-45% protein. Many African farmers now use BSF to cut feed costs by 30%. Whether commercial or farm-made, the key is consistency and meeting the fish's protein needs.

3. Feed Formulation and Ingredient Selection

Feed formulation means mixing ingredients to meet specific nutrient targets. Start with a Pearson Square method for simplicity. Suppose you want a 30% protein feed using fishmeal (60% protein) and maize bran (10% protein). Draw a square, put 60 on left top, 10 on left bottom, and 30 in the centre. Subtract diagonally: 60-30=30, 30-10=20. So you need 20 parts fishmeal and 30 parts maize bran. That gives 40% fishmeal and 60% maize bran. But that's too much fishmeal; you can substitute part with soybean meal (44% protein). Use trial and error with a calculator or free app like Feed Formulation (available on Google Play).

Ingredient selection depends on cost and availability. In Africa, common ingredients: fishmeal (60-65% protein), soybean meal (44-48%), groundnut cake (40-45%), cottonseed meal (32-38%, but toxic gossypol must be limited to 10% of diet), sunflower cake (28-30%), maize (8-9%), maize bran (8-12%), rice bran (10-12%), cassava peels (2-3%), and palm kernel cake (15-18%). Also, use vitamin premix (1-2%) and mineral premix (1-2%). For catfish, replace some plant protein with fishmeal because catfish need more essential amino acids. A typical catfish diet: 40% fishmeal, 20% soybean meal, 30% maize bran, 5% vitamin premix, 5% fish oil.

Always test your feed. Mix a small batch, feed to a few fish in a hapa (net cage) for two weeks. Compare growth with commercial feed. If fish grow slower, adjust protein or add lysine and methionine. Also, check for anti-nutritional factors: raw soybean has trypsin inhibitor, so roast or cook soybeans before use. Cottonseed meal must be limited. Cassava peels should be sun-dried to remove cyanide. Use a hammer mill to grind ingredients to fine powder (less than 1 mm) for better digestion. Store ingredients in dry, ventilated place. Buy in bulk to reduce cost. Join a farmer cooperative to share purchases and formulation costs.

4. Feeding Strategies and Ration Calculation

Feeding strategy determines how much, how often, and when you feed. Overfeeding wastes money and pollutes water; underfeeding stunts growth. The daily ration is a percentage of fish body weight. For tilapia: fry (0.5-5 g) eat 10-15% of body weight per day; fingerlings (5-20 g) eat 6-8%; adults (over 50 g) eat 2-3%. For catfish: fry eat 8-10%, adults 1-2%. To calculate: weigh a sample of 10 fish, get average weight, multiply by number of fish to get total biomass. Then multiply by feeding rate. Example: 1000 tilapia averaging 100 g = 100 kg biomass. At 3% feeding rate, feed 3 kg per day. Split into two meals: 1.5 kg morning and 1.5 kg evening.

Feed at fixed times daily. Tilapia feed best in morning (8-10 am) and afternoon (3-5 pm). Catfish feed more at dusk and night. Use floating pellets for tilapia so you can see when they stop eating. Stop feeding when they no longer come to surface. For catfish, use sinking pellets and check after 30 minutes; remove uneaten feed to avoid fouling. Adjust feeding based on water temperature: if below 20C, reduce by half; if above 32C, also reduce because oxygen is low. In Africa, many farms have rainy and dry seasons. During heavy rain, fish eat less. During dry season, watch for low water levels and high ammonia.

Keep records. Write down daily feed amount, fish behaviour, and water temperature. Every two weeks, sample weigh 10 fish. If average weight gain is less than expected (e.g., tilapia should gain 2-3 g per day in optimal conditions), increase feed slightly or check for disease. A simple tool: a hanging scale (5 kg capacity) and a bucket. For small ponds, use a feeding tray to check consumption. Never feed mouldy or expired feed. If feed smells sour, discard. Also, train fish to eat at a specific spot by tapping the water before feeding. This reduces waste. In integrated systems (e.g., with chickens), chicken manure can fertilise pond water to grow natural fish food, reducing feed needs by 20-30%.

5. Feed Storage and Quality Control

Feed storage is critical because poor storage kills nutrients and grows mould. Mould produces aflatoxin, which can poison fish and humans who eat the fish. Store feed in a cool, dry, dark place. Ideal temperature: below 25C. Humidity should be below 12%. In many African villages, this means a raised wooden platform inside a ventilated room, not on the floor. Use pallets to keep bags off the ground. Keep bags away from walls to allow air circulation. Never store feed near chemicals like fuel, pesticides, or fertiliser because fish feed absorbs odours and toxins.

Use airtight containers for farm-made feed. Plastic drums with lids or metal silos work well. For commercial feed, keep in original bags and seal tightly after opening. A 50 kg bag opened should be used within 2-4 weeks. If you buy in bulk, use first-in, first-out. Label each bag with date of purchase or production. Check feed before use: smell it (should smell fresh, not musty), look for clumps or mould (white or green patches), and feel for dampness. If feed is damp, dry it in sun for a few hours but not too long because sunlight destroys vitamins. Better to prevent dampness.

Quality control also means testing feed. You can do a simple water stability test: put a handful of pellets in a glass of water. Good pellets hold shape for at least 30 minutes. If they disintegrate quickly, they will foul water. For protein content, you cannot test at home, but you can send a sample to a laboratory (e.g., at a university or agricultural research station). In Kenya, KALRO offers feed analysis for about $20 per sample. For farm-made feed, ensure ingredients are fresh. Never use mouldy maize or rancid fishmeal. Store fishmeal in freezer if possible. Add mould inhibitor like propionic acid (0.5%) to farm-made feed if storing more than one month. Also, keep rodents and insects out. Use traps or cats. A well-stored feed retains nutrients for 3-6 months. If you see weevils, discard the feed. Remember: cheap feed that is spoiled is more expensive than good feed.

6. Reducing Feed Waste and Cost

Feed is 60-70% of fish farming costs. Reducing waste directly increases profit. First, use the right feed size. Fry need crumbles (0.5-1 mm), fingerlings need 1-2 mm pellets, and adults need 3-5 mm pellets. If pellets are too big, fish cannot eat them; too small, they dissolve before eating. Second, feed only what fish can consume in 20-30 minutes. For tilapia, feed until they stop coming to surface. For catfish, feed 80% of what they would eat in 30 minutes. Use a feeding tray or a fixed feeding spot to monitor. Third, avoid overfeeding. Many farmers feed 5% body weight when 3% is enough. Weigh feed with a kitchen scale, not by hand.

Use alternative protein sources. Black soldier fly (BSF) larvae can replace 30-50% of fishmeal. You can raise BSF in a box with kitchen waste. A 1 m BSF bin produces 1-2 kg of larvae per week. Dry them and grind into meal. Also, use agricultural by-products: brewers' spent grain (20-25% protein), palm kernel cake, and cassava peels. But balance with protein concentrates. Another way: fertilise your pond. Add chicken manure (500 kg per hectare per week) or cow dung (1 ton per hectare per week) to grow natural plankton. This reduces commercial feed by 20-30%. For tilapia, plankton is excellent food.

Improve feed conversion ratio (FCR). FCR = feed given / fish weight gain. Good FCR for tilapia is 1.5-2.0; for catfish 1.2-1.5. If your FCR is above 2.5, you are wasting feed. To improve FCR: maintain good water quality (oxygen above 5 mg/L, ammonia below 0.5 mg/L), keep stocking density moderate (3-5 fish per m for tilapia), and harvest at optimal size. Also, store feed properly to avoid spoilage. Buy feed in bulk with other farmers to get discounts. Make your own feed using a hammer mill and mixer (cost: $500-1000, but pays back in one season). Finally, keep records: track feed used and fish harvested. If you see waste, adjust. A simple rule: every kilogram of feed saved is profit.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Feed Management for Profitable Fish Farming in Africa

Feed represents 6070% of the total operating cost in most African fish farming enterprises. Whether you are running a small pond in Kisumu, a cage farm on Lake Volta, or a recirculating system outside Lagos, the difference between profit and loss is usually decided at the feed store, not at the market. This practical reference consolidates the key numbers, rules of thumb, and decision points that matter for tilapia, catfish, and other commonly farmed species on the continent.

Use this module alongside the six core lessons in this course. Here we focus on the operational detail that turns nutritional theory into cash flow: how to read a feed label, how to calculate a ration without software, how to store feed so it does not lose value, and how to cut waste without cutting growth.

1. Reading a Feed Label Like a Business Owner

In most African markets, feed is sold in 15 kg, 20 kg, or 50 kg bags. The label should state protein, fat, fibre, moisture, and ash content, plus the manufacturing date. If any of these are missing, treat the feed as unknown risk. The two numbers that most affect your cost per kilogram of fish are crude protein (CP) and feed conversion ratio (FCR). A 28% CP feed at a lower price is not cheaper if it produces an FCR of 2.2 instead of 1.6.

2. Feed Formulation Without a Laboratory

Farm-made feed is common across Africa because commercial pellets are often 3050% more expensive per kilogram and may be unavailable in remote areas. The Pearson Square method allows you to blend two ingredients to hit a target protein level. For example, to make a 28% CP grow-out feed from maize (9% CP) and fishmeal (60% CP), the ratio is approximately 64 parts maize to 36 parts fishmeal. In practice, you would also add a vitamin-mineral premix (12%), a binder such as cassava starch (35%), and a lipid source like palm oil (36%).

Ingredient selection is a business decision, not just a nutritional one. Local availability, seasonal price swings, and transport cost often matter more than the theoretical nutrient profile. Inland farms in Zambia or Uganda may find sunflower cake cheaper than fishmeal, but sunflower cake has lower lysine and methionine, so it must be supplemented or blended with animal protein for catfish.

SAFETY WARNING AFLATOXIN AND FEED SPOILAGE

Never feed mouldy maize, groundnut cake, or cottonseed cake to fish. Aflatoxin B1 produced by Aspergillus flavus causes liver damage in fish, reduces growth by 2040%, and can accumulate in fish tissue, creating a food safety risk for consumers. In humid African conditions, feed stored for more than 68 weeks without proper ventilation and moisture control is high risk. Discard any feed with visible mould, a musty smell, or clumping. Do not attempt to "dry it out" and use it the toxin is heat-stable and survives cooking or pelleting.

3. Feeding Strategies and Ration Calculation

The single most common profit leak on African fish farms is overfeeding. Uneaten feed sinks, decomposes, consumes oxygen, and produces ammonia. The fish then eat less, grow slower, and the farmer buys more feed to compensate a downward spiral.

A practical daily ration is calculated as a percentage of body weight, adjusted for water temperature and fish size. The formula is:

Daily feed (kg) = (Number of fish Average weight in kg) Feeding rate (%)

For tilapia at 28C, feeding rates are roughly 57% of body weight for fingerlings, 34% for 50150 g fish, and 1.52.5% for fish above 250 g. For catfish, rates are higher: 810% for fry, 46% for juveniles, and 23% for grow-out. Feed 23 times per day for small fish, and once or twice daily for larger fish. In cool highland areas (below 22C), reduce the ration by 3050% because metabolism slows.

Use feeding trays or observation points to check whether feed is being consumed within 1520 minutes. If pellets remain after 30 minutes, cut the ration by 20% at the next feeding. This simple discipline can improve FCR by 0.20.4 points, which on a 5-tonne tilapia harvest translates to roughly 12 tonnes of feed saved.

4. Feed Storage and Quality Control

Feed begins to lose quality the moment it leaves the extruder. In tropical African conditions, humidity and heat accelerate oxidation of lipids and growth of mould. Store feed on wooden pallets, never directly on concrete or earth floors. Keep bags at least 30 cm away from walls and stack no more than 810 bags high to avoid compression and heat build-up. The store should be dry, shaded, and ventilated, with a target relative humidity below 65% and temperature below 25C where possible.

Use the FIFO rule first in, first out. Record the manufacturing date and arrival date on every bag. Do not buy more than 46 weeks of feed at a time unless you have proper cold or dry storage. For farm-made feed, produce only what you will use within 710 days, and dry pellets to below 10% moisture before storage.

5. Reducing Feed Waste and Cost

Five practical levers reduce feed cost without sacrificing growth:

1. Match particle size to fish size. Feed that is too fine leaches nutrients before the fish can eat it. Feed that is too large is rejected. Use crumble for fry, 2 mm pellets for fingerlings, and 46 mm pellets for grow-out.

2. Use pond natural productivity. Fertilise ponds with manure or compost to grow plankton. In semi-intensive tilapia ponds, natural food can supply 2030% of nutritional needs, reducing the feed bill by a similar margin.

3. Split feeding into smaller, more frequent meals. Two or three small feeds produce better FCR than one large feed, especially for tilapia.

4. Buy ingredients in bulk as a cooperative. Smallholder clusters in Nigeria, Ghana, and Kenya have reduced feed cost by 1525% by pooling orders for soybean meal, fishmeal, and premix.

5. Monitor FCR monthly. If FCR rises above your target, investigate before buying more feed. The problem is usually water quality, disease, or overfeeding not underfeeding.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Check feed label for CP, fat, moisture, and manufacturing date before purchase.
  • Calculate daily ration by body weight and adjust for water temperature.
  • Feed 23 times daily for small fish; observe consumption within 20 minutes.
  • Store feed on pallets, below 25C, under 65% humidity, FIFO rotation.
  • Never use mouldy or musty feed aflatoxin risk is real and cumulative.
  • Match pellet size to fish size to reduce leaching and rejection.
  • Record FCR monthly; investigate any rise above target immediately.
  • Pool ingredient purchases with neighbouring farms to cut unit cost.
