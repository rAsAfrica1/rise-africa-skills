1. Team Formation and Role Assignment

A solar installation is never a one-man job. For a typical 1kW home system, you need at least three people: one team leader, one installer, and one safety watcher. The team leader reads the plan, checks measurements, and makes decisions. The installer does the physical work on the roof or ground. The safety watcher keeps an eye on ladders, tools, and electrical hazards. If you have four people, add a materials handler who keeps track of bolts, cables, and tools.

Before you start, gather your team and assign roles clearly. Write the roles on a piece of paper or a small whiteboard. For example: âAmina â team leader, Kofi â installer, Zainab â safety watcher.â Each person must know what they are responsible for. The team leader should also check that everyone has the right personal protective equipment (PPE): hard hats, safety glasses, gloves, and non-slip shoes. In many African communities, you can buy these at local hardware stores or markets for about 50â100 Ghanaian cedis or 500â1000 Nigerian naira per set.

Communication is key. Agree on simple hand signals for âstop,â âokay,â and âlower.â If you are working on a roof, the safety watcher should stay on the ground and shout warnings if someone approaches the ladder or if a tool falls. Use a rope to haul tools up instead of carrying them in your hands. During the practicum, practice switching roles so everyone learns each job. This builds flexibility and helps if someone is absent on installation day.

Finally, set a schedule. For a 1kW system, the mounting structure takes about 2 hours, panel installation 1 hour, wiring 1.5 hours, and testing 30 minutes. Add breaks for water and rest, especially in hot sun. A well-organised team finishes faster and safer than a disorganised one. Remember: a good team is like a solar panelâeach cell works together to produce power.

2. Mock Installation of Mounting Structure

The mounting structure is the skeleton of your solar array. For a small 1kW system, you can use aluminium or galvanised iron rails. Aluminium is lighter and rust-resistant but costs more; galvanised iron is cheaper but heavier. In many African towns, you can buy 4-meter rails from metal fabricators for about 200â300 cedis each. You will also need L-brackets, bolts (M8 size), nuts, washers, and concrete anchors if mounting on a roof.

Start by measuring and marking the roof or ground. For a ground mount, dig four holes 50 cm deep and 30 cm wide. Mix concrete (1 part cement, 2 parts sand, 3 parts gravel) and pour it into the holes. Insert the base plates or posts and check they are level using a spirit level. Let the concrete cure for at least 24 hours. For a roof mount, locate the rafters using a stud finder or by tapping. Drill pilot holes and bolt the L-brackets into the rafters. Never bolt only into roofing sheetsâthey will pull out in wind.

Assemble the rails on the ground first. Lay two rails parallel, 1 meter apart for a single panel, or wider for multiple panels. Attach the rails to the brackets with M8 bolts. Tighten with a spanner, but do not over-tighten as you may strip the threads. Use a torque wrench if available; otherwise, tighten until snug plus a quarter turn. Check that the rails are straight by running a string line along them.

Lift the assembled structure into place. This is where your team works together: two people lift, one person guides, and the safety watcher spots. Bolt the structure to the base plates or roof brackets. Check for level in both directions. A tilt angle of 10â15 degrees is good for most of Africa to let rain run off and maximise sun exposure. Finally, shake the structure firmlyâit should not wobble. If it does, tighten bolts or add diagonal bracing.

3. Panel Installation Practice

Solar panels are fragile but tough. A typical 250W panel weighs about 18 kg and measures 1.6 m by 1 m. You need at least two people to lift it safely. Before lifting, check the panel for cracks or scratches. A cracked panel will not work and must be replaced. Wear gloves to avoid cuts from sharp edges.

Place the panel on the mounting rails. Most panels have mounting holes on the frame. Use M8 bolts with washers to secure the panel to the rails. Tighten the bolts evenlyâfirst finger-tight, then snug with a spanner. Do not overtighten; you can crack the frame. Leave a small gap (about 1 cm) between panels for air circulation. If you are installing multiple panels, connect them in series or parallel according to your system design. For a 1kW system, you might use four 250W panels. In series, you add voltages; in parallel, you add currents. Check your charge controllerâs maximum input voltage before wiring.

Lift the panel with two people: one at each end. Walk slowly and keep the panel flat to avoid bending. Place it on the rails and hold it while a third person inserts the bolts. Never stand on a panelâit will break. If you must work on a roof, use roof hooks or a harness. In many African communities, roof work is common, but safety is often ignored. A fall from 3 meters can kill. So always use a ladder that extends 1 meter above the roof edge, and have someone hold the ladder.

After securing all panels, check that they are aligned and evenly spaced. Use a tape measure to confirm the gaps. Tilt the panels to the correct angleâfor example, 10 degrees in Nairobi, 15 degrees in Accra. Finally, cover the panels with an opaque cloth until you are ready to connect wiring. This prevents electric shock and damage to the charge controller.

4. Wiring and Connection Exercise

Wiring is the most dangerous part of solar installation. You will work with DC voltages that can be 50â150V and currents of 10â30A. Always wear insulated gloves and use tools with rubber handles. Work with the panels covered and the battery disconnected. Use correct cable sizes: for a 1kW system with 12V battery, use 6 mmÂ² cable for panel-to-controller and 10 mmÂ² for controller-to-battery. Undersized cables cause voltage drop and heat, which can start a fire.

Start by connecting the panels. If you have four 250W panels, connect them in series: positive of panel 1 to negative of panel 2, and so on. This gives you 4 x 30V = 120V and 8A. Use MC4 connectorsâthey are waterproof and click securely. Strip the cable ends 1 cm, insert into the connector, and crimp with a crimping tool. Pull gently to test. Run the cables through UV-resistant conduit or conduit clips to protect from sun and rats. In many African homes, rats chew cables, so conduit is essential.

Next, connect the charge controller. Connect the battery first, then the solar panels. The controller will show a light or display. Set the battery type (e.g., lead-acid or lithium) according to the manual. Then connect the inverter to the battery. Use a fuse or circuit breaker on the positive cable near the battery. A 30A fuse is good for a 1kW system. Connect the inverterâs AC output to your loadâa light bulb or phone charger for testing.

Check all connections with a multimeter. Measure voltage at the panel output (should be around 120V DC), at the battery (12â14V), and at the inverter output (220â240V AC). If any reading is zero or wrong, trace the circuit. Common mistakes: reversed polarity, loose connections, or blown fuses. After testing, cover all exposed terminals with electrical tape or junction boxes. Label cables for future maintenance.

5. System Testing and Commissioning Drill

Commissioning is the final check before you hand over the system to the client. Start by inspecting everything: are all bolts tight? Are cables secured and not touching sharp edges? Is the controller and inverter in a dry, ventilated place? Use a checklist. For a 1kW system, you should have: 4 panels, 1 charge controller (30A), 1 inverter (1000W), 1 battery (100Ah), cables, fuses, and mounting structure.

Turn on the system in the correct order: first the battery to controller, then panels to controller, then controller to battery (if separate), then inverter to battery. Watch the controller display. It should show battery voltage (e.g., 12.6V) and charging current (e.g., 5A). If it shows zero, check fuses and connections. Measure the panel open-circuit voltage with a multimeterâit should be around 120V DC. Then measure the battery voltageâit should rise slowly as it charges. If the battery is full, the controller will stop charging.

Test the inverter by plugging in a small load, like a 10W LED bulb. It should light up. Measure the AC voltage at the socketâit should be 220â240V. If it is lower, check battery voltage; a low battery will cause the inverter to shut down. Test with a larger load, like a 100W TV, for 10 minutes. The inverter should not overheat. Check the battery voltage after 10 minutesâit should not drop below 11.5V. If it does, the battery may be too small or old.

Record all readings in a logbook: date, time, panel voltage, battery voltage, charging current, AC output. This helps diagnose problems later. Finally, train the client on basic operation: how to turn the system on and off, how to clean panels (with water and soft cloth, not soap), and what to do if a fuse blows. Give them a simple manual with your phone number. A well-commissioned system can last 20 years with proper care.

6. Peer Review and Feedback Session

After the practicum, gather your team for a peer review. This is not about blaming anyoneâit is about learning. Sit in a circle and take turns answering three questions: What went well? What went wrong? What would you do differently next time? Use a notebook to record key points. For example, one team might say: âWe installed the mounting structure in 2 hours, but we forgot to check the tilt angle. Next time we will use a protractor.â Another might say: âWe dropped a bolt and lost it. We should use a tool belt.â

Give specific feedback. Instead of âYou did a bad job,â say âWhen you tightened the bolts, you missed one on the left rail. Please check all bolts again.â Feedback should be about actions, not people. Also, praise good work: âAminaâs safety watch prevented a fall when the ladder slipped.â This builds confidence and teamwork. Each person should receive at least one compliment and one suggestion for improvement.

Use a simple scoring sheet. Rate each other on: safety, teamwork, technical skill, and time management. Use a scale of 1 to 5. For example, 5 = excellent, 1 = needs practice. Add up the scores and discuss. If someone scores low in safety, plan a short refresher on ladder use. If the team scored low in wiring, practice crimping MC4 connectors again.

Finally, write an action plan. List three things the team will improve before the next installation. For example: 1) Always use a torque wrench on panel bolts. 2) Keep a first aid kit on site. 3) Test the system with a multimeter before connecting the inverter. Share the plan with your instructor. Peer review turns mistakes into skills. In the solar business, your reputation depends on quality work. A team that reviews and improves will win more contracts and serve their community better.

Practical Reference: Hands-On Installation Practicum

This practical reference consolidates the core field skills for the module Hands-On Installation Practicum within the course Solar Installation in the Community. It is written for technicians, apprentices, and small enterprise teams working in off-grid and weak-grid African contexts, where every connection, mount, and test has a direct impact on customer trust, system uptime, and repeat business. The lessons covered hereâTeam Formation and Role Assignment, Mock Installation of Mounting Structure, Panel Installation Practice, Wiring and Connection Exercise, System Testing and Commissioning Drill, and Peer Review and Feedback Sessionâare sequenced to mirror a real job: plan, build, wire, test, and review. Treat this document as a field companion, not a substitute for the manufacturer's installation manual or local electrical codes.

Business-first principle: A solar installation is not just a technical task; it is a commercial promise. A well-mounted array that survives a Harmattan dust storm or a coastal salt-laden breeze protects your reputation and reduces costly callbacks. A correctly torqued connection prevents a fire that could destroy a client's home and your business. Every step below is framed to help you deliver a system that is safe, durable, and profitable to maintain.

Before any physical work begins, the team must be formed and roles assigned. In a typical African community solar projectâwhether a 1 kWp home system or a 10 kWp school arrayâthe minimum viable team is four people: a Team Leader (safety, client communication, final sign-off), a Mounting Technician (structure, roof penetration, tilt), a Wiring Technician (DC/AC cabling, terminations, labelling), and a Testing & Commissioning Officer (multimeter, irradiance, documentation). On smaller jobs, one person may hold two roles, but never combine the Wiring Technician and Testing Officer on the same system without a second pair of eyesâthis is a basic internal control that prevents "self-certified" mistakes.

Role assignment should be written on a simple job card and signed by each team member. This is not bureaucracy; it is risk management. When a client later reports a loose connection, the job card tells you who wired it, who tested it, and when. In many African markets, word-of-mouth is the primary marketing channel, and a documented quality process is a competitive advantage.

Mock Installation of Mounting Structure

The mounting structure is the foundation of the entire system. In the practicum, teams should first build a mock structure on the ground before touching a roof. Use the same rails, clamps, and bolts specified for the actual job. The goal is to practice alignment, torque, and tilt without the risk of a fall or roof damage.

Key field rules for mounting in African conditions:

  • Tilt angle: For most of sub-Saharan Africa (0â20Â° latitude), a tilt of 10â15Â° is sufficient for self-cleaning during rains, while avoiding excessive wind loading. In dusty Sahelian zones, a 15Â° tilt helps dust slide off during the first rains.
  • Roof penetration: Use EPDM or neoprene washers and sealant on every penetration. In corrugated iron roofsâcommon across the continentâpre-drill and use self-sealing screws. Never rely on silicone alone.
  • Corrosion: Coastal installations (e.g., Mombasa, Lagos, Dakar) require hot-dip galvanised or aluminium rails. Stainless steel bolts are non-negotiable near the ocean.
  • Wind loading: In open areas, add diagonal bracing. A 4-module array can generate significant lift in a storm; the structure must be anchored to the roof trusses, not just the roofing sheet.

Panel Installation Practice

Panels are the most visible and expensive single component. In the practicum, teams should handle at least two different module types (e.g., 330 W polycrystalline and 450 W monocrystalline) to appreciate weight, frame thickness, and clamp placement. Always lift panels by the frame, never by the junction box or cables. On a roof, use a panel-lifting harness or two-person carry; in many African installations, falls from height are a leading cause of injury.

Clamp placement matters: follow the manufacturer's clamping zones, typically 200â400 mm from the module edge. Clamping outside this zone can void the warranty and crack the glass under wind load. Leave a 20 mm gap between modules for thermal expansion and water drainage. Torque clamp bolts to the manufacturer's specificationâusually 15â20 Nmâusing a calibrated torque wrench. A loose clamp is a future hotspot; an over-torqued clamp is a cracked frame.

Wiring and Connection Exercise

This is where most field failures occur. The practicum should include a full DC string wiring exercise on a mock combiner box, followed by AC wiring to the inverter and distribution board. Use correct cable sizes: for a 1 kWp system at 12 V, 6 mmÂ² DC cable is typical; at 24 V or 48 V, 4 mmÂ² or 2.5 mmÂ² may suffice. Undersized cable causes voltage drop, heat, and fire risk.

MC4 connectors must be crimped with the correct tool, not pliers. After crimping, pull-test each connection. Polarity must be checked with a multimeter before plugging into the combiner. In the practicum, deliberately reverse one string and have the team identify the error using a clamp meterâthis builds the habit of verification.

PV modules generate voltage whenever exposed to light. Cover panels with an opaque sheet during wiring. Never work on live DC circuits alone. Use insulated tools, remove metal jewellery, and confirm zero voltage with a multimeter before touching any conductor. In African field conditions, heat, dust, and worn insulation increase risk. A DC arc can sustain and cause severe burns or fire. If in doubt, isolate and test again.

System Testing and Commissioning Drill

Commissioning is the formal handover. The practicum should simulate a full commissioning sequence: visual inspection, open-circuit voltage (Voc) per string, short-circuit current (Isc), insulation resistance, earth continuity, charge controller settings, inverter output, and load test. Record every reading on a commissioning sheet. In many African markets, a signed commissioning sheet is your proof of quality and your defence if a client disputes the installation.

Test sequence for a typical off-grid system:

  1. Visual: no loose clamps, no exposed copper, all labels present.
  2. Voc per string: compare to expected (module Voc Ã number of modules in series).
  3. Isc per string: use a clamp meter; compare between strings (should be within 5%).
  4. Insulation resistance: minimum 1 MÎ© between conductors and earth.
  5. Earth continuity: <1 Î© from array frame to earth electrode.
  6. Controller and inverter settings: battery type, charge voltage, low-voltage disconnect.
  7. Load test: run a known load (e.g., 500 W) for 15 minutes and record voltage drop.

Peer Review and Feedback Session

The final lesson is not optional. Teams present their installation to another team, using the commissioning sheet and photos. The reviewing team checks torque marks, cable routing, labelling, and documentation. Feedback must be specific and actionable: "The DC cable is not UV-rated; replace with PV1-F cable" is useful; "good job" is not. This peer review builds a culture of quality that is essential for scaling a solar business in Africa, where certification and reputation drive access to finance and larger contracts.
