1. Frame Anatomy: Top Bar, Bottom Bar, Side Bars

A bee frame is a rectangular wooden structure that holds honeycomb. It has four parts: top bar, bottom bar, and two side bars. The top bar is the longest piece, usually 48.3 cm (19 inches) for a standard Langstroth hive. It has a thicker profile to support the weight of honey and to hang on the hive body's frame rest. The bottom bar is the same length but thinner, often 1.9 cm (3/4 inch) wide and 1 cm (3/8 inch) thick. The side bars are 23.2 cm (9 1/8 inches) tall, connecting the top and bottom bars. They have a notch at the top to fit under the top bar and a hole or slot at the bottom for the bottom bar. In Africa, many beekeepers use local timber like cypress, pine, or eucalyptus. Avoid termite-prone woods like soft mango. Cut all pieces accurately; a frame that is 2 mm off will not sit properly in the hive. Use a tape measure, pencil, and a mitre saw or hand saw. Sand rough edges to prevent bee injuries. The top bar often has a groove on the underside to hold foundation. The side bars may have holes for wiring. Understanding these parts helps you build strong, interchangeable frames. A well-built frame lasts many years and makes hive inspections easy.

2. Cutting and Assembling Wooden Frames

To cut frames, start with dry, straight timber. Use a table saw or circular saw for speed, but a hand saw works fine. Set a stop block to cut multiple pieces to the same length. For a standard Langstroth frame, cut top bars to 48.3 cm, bottom bars to 48.3 cm, and side bars to 23.2 cm. You need two side bars per frame. Cut a notch at the top of each side bar to fit the top bar. The notch is usually 1.5 cm deep and 2 cm wide. At the bottom, cut a slot for the bottom bar. Sand all pieces with 80-grit sandpaper. Assembly: apply waterproof wood glue (like Titebond III) to all joints. Use a frame jig to hold pieces square. Nail with 4 cm (1.5 inch) galvanised nails: two nails through each side bar into the top bar, and two into the bottom bar. Pre-drill holes to avoid splitting. Check for square by measuring diagonals; they must be equal. Let glue dry for 24 hours. In Africa, use nails that won't rust in humid conditions. If using local hardwood, pre-drill all holes. A good frame is rigid and does not twist. Build at least 10 frames per hive box. Stack them flat to dry. Poor assembly leads to burr comb and broken frames.

3. Wiring Frames for Strength

Wiring a frame reinforces the comb so it doesn't break during extraction or hot weather. You need frame wire (26 or 28 gauge, stainless steel or galvanised), a wire embedder (spur or electric), and wire cutters. First, drill four small holes in the side bars: two near the top and two near the bottom. They should be about 1 cm from the edges. Thread one end of the wire through a hole and secure it with a small nail or a figure-eight knot. Pull the wire across to the opposite hole, then back, creating a zigzag pattern: two horizontal wires across the frame. Keep the wire tight; it should twang when plucked. Use a wire tensioner or a nail to twist the wire at the end. For embedding, place a sheet of wax foundation on the frame. Run a spur embedder (a tool with a small wheel) along the wires, pressing them into the wax. Alternatively, use an electric embedder that heats the wire. In Africa, many beekeepers use a 12V car battery and a length of resistance wire to heat and embed. Do not overheat; wax melts at 63C. Let the wax cool and set. Check that wires are embedded but not cutting through. Properly wired frames last many seasons and produce straight comb.

4. Installing Wax Foundation: Crimp-Wire and Pin

Wax foundation is a thin sheet of beeswax with a hexagon pattern. It guides bees to build straight comb. You can install it using crimp-wire or pins. Crimp-wire method: after wiring the frame, place the foundation on the wires. Use a crimping tool (or a small pliers) to press the wire into the wax. Start at one end and work along each wire. The wire should sit just below the surface. Then, use a spur embedder to press the wire deeper. For extra security, use a hot wire embedder. Pin method: use foundation pins (small metal pins with a flat head) or a stapler. Place the foundation on the frame and push pins through the wax into the top bar and side bars. Space pins every 5 cm. In Africa, many beekeepers make their own foundation using a silicone mould and melted beeswax. When installing, do it in a warm room (25-30C) so the wax is pliable. Do not install in direct sun; it will melt. If foundation is brittle, warm it slightly. Ensure the foundation is centred and flat. Bees will draw comb from it. A well-installed foundation saves the colony energy and reduces drone comb. Replace foundation every 2-3 years to avoid disease buildup.

5. Building Foundationless Frames with Starter Strips

Foundationless frames have no full wax sheet, only a starter strip at the top. This allows bees to build natural comb, which many African beekeepers prefer because it saves money and wax. To make a starter strip, cut a piece of beeswax foundation 2-3 cm tall and as wide as the top bar. Alternatively, use a wooden strip or a plastic strip. Attach it to the underside of the top bar using melted beeswax or small nails. You can also run a bead of melted wax along the top bar and press the strip into it. The strip acts as a guide. For strength, wire the frame as usual, but leave the wires exposed. Bees will build comb around the wires. Another method is to cut a V-shaped groove in the top bar and pour melted wax into it. The bees will follow the wax line. In Africa, use local beeswax from your own hives. Make sure the starter strip is straight and centred. When placing the frame in the hive, ensure it is level. Bees build comb downward. Check frames every week; if comb is crooked, straighten it early. Foundationless frames require more frequent inspections but produce high-quality wax. They are ideal for top-bar hives and for beekeepers who want to avoid buying foundation.

6. Assembling Plastic Frames and Foundations

Plastic frames and foundations are one-piece units, often made of food-grade plastic. They are durable, resistant to wax moths, and easy to clean. To assemble, you simply snap the plastic foundation into the plastic frame. No wiring or embedding is needed. Some plastic frames come pre-assembled; others require you to fit the foundation into the frame's grooves. Use your thumbs to press the foundation into the frame until it clicks. Make sure it is seated evenly. Plastic frames are heavier than wood but last many years. They are ideal for African beekeepers who lack woodworking tools or who want to reduce labour. However, bees may be slow to accept plastic, especially if it is smooth. To encourage acceptance, coat the foundation with a thin layer of melted beeswax. Use a paintbrush or dip the foundation in warm wax. Let it dry before placing in the hive. Plastic frames can be cleaned with a hive tool and warm water. Do not use boiling water; it will warp. In hot African climates, plastic can become brittle; store them out of direct sun. They are also less forgiving if dropped. Use plastic frames in all hive boxes for uniformity. They are a good choice for beginners and for areas with high humidity.

<h2>Practical Reference: Constructing Frames and Foundations</h2>

<p>In African beekeeping, the frame is the single most handled piece of equipment in the hive. A well-built frame survives seasons of inspection, honey extraction, and transport. A poorly built one collapses under the weight of capped honey, killing brood and costing you a harvest. This lesson is a business-first reference: every choice here affects your cost per frame, your extraction efficiency, and your ability to scale from 5 hives to 50. Whether you are building Langstroth frames in Kenya's Rift Valley or top-bar frames in Tanzania's miombo woodland, the principles are the same. Get the frame right and the colony does the rest.</p>

<p>Before you cut a single piece of timber, decide your business model. Foundation frames (with wax sheets and wire) cost more per unit but produce straight, extractable combs that survive a centrifugal extractor. Foundationless frames with starter strips cost roughly 4060% less in materials and labour, but combs are fragile and can only be harvested by crushing and straining which destroys the comb and forces the colony to rebuild. For commercial honey production targeting supermarkets and export, wire-and-foundation is the only viable route. For low-capital startups, top-bar hives and foundationless frames in Langstroth boxes are a legitimate entry point. Match the frame type to your market, not to your workshop's convenience.</p>

<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0; font-weight:bold; color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING WIRING AND WAX HANDLING</p>
<p style="margin:0; color:#3a1010;">Wiring frames involves a heated embedding tool and tensioned wire that can snap and whip toward the eyes. Always wear safety glasses or a face shield when tensioning wire and when using an electric embedding tool. Never embed wax over an open flame in a confined room beeswax vapour is flammable and can flash. Work in a ventilated space, keep a fire blanket or dry sand bucket within arm's reach, and never leave a heated embedder unattended. In areas with unreliable mains power, use a 12V battery-powered embedder rather than improvised mains wiring. If you feel dizzy or smell hot wax burning, stop and ventilate immediately.</p>
</div>

<h3>Step-by-Step: Cutting and Assembling Wooden Frames</h3>
<p>Cut all components to specification before assembly. A simple jig a wooden block with stops lets one person cut 50 top bars in the time it takes to cut 10 freehand. Pre-drill side bars to prevent splitting; African hardwoods like mvule split easily when nailed cold. Assemble the frame square: the top bar and bottom bar must be parallel, and the side bars perpendicular. A frame out of square by more than 2 mm will not sit flush in the hive body and will trigger burr comb and propolis, which slows every future inspection. Use a framing jig or a simple 90 corner block. Nail two nails per joint, offset slightly, and add a dab of wood glue on end grain for longevity. Let assembled frames cure for 24 hours before wiring.</p>

<h3>Wiring Frames for Strength</h3>
<p>Wiring is what turns a wooden rectangle into a load-bearing structure. Fit brass eyelets into the pre-drilled side bars four per frame for a standard Langstroth. Thread 2628 gauge wire through the eyelets in a continuous zigzag or parallel pattern, tensioning each run to a firm but not guitar-string tautness. Over-tensioned wire cuts through wax foundation; under-tensioned wire sags and lets comb bulge. Embed the wire into the wax foundation using a heated embedder or a spur embedder wheel, working gently so the wax does not tear. In coastal Kenya and Tanzania, stainless wire is worth the premium galvanised wire rusts within two seasons in humid zones and stains both wax and honey.</p>

<h3>Installing Wax Foundation: Crimp-Wire and Pin</h3>
<p>The crimp-wire and pin method is the most common in African workshops because it requires no electricity. Lay the foundation sheet on the wired frame, align it with the top bar groove, and press the wedge or top bar strip down to lock the sheet in place. Then, using a crimp-wire tool or a hand-crimped pin, press the wire into the wax at each intersection. Work from the centre outward to avoid trapping air pockets. The foundation should sit flat, with no waves or buckles. If the sheet cracks, do not patch it with candle wax the colony will build drone comb around the crack. Replace the sheet.</p>

<h3>Building Foundationless Frames with Starter Strips</h3>
<p>A starter strip is a 1520 mm strip of wax or a thin wooden guide fixed into the top bar groove. The bees draw comb down from it, guided by gravity and the strip's edge. This method is cheap, fast, and ideal for top-bar hives and low-capital Langstroth operations. The trade-off is fragility: natural comb has no wire reinforcement, so it cannot be centrifuged. Harvest by cutting the comb from the frame and crushing it. For comb honey producers, this is not a drawback it is the product. For honey bulkers, it is a bottleneck.</p>

<h3>Assembling Plastic Frames and Foundations</h3>
<p>Plastic frames snap together in minutes and resist wax moth and small hive beetle better than wood. They are increasingly popular in Uganda and Nigeria, where timber prices have risen sharply. The main drawback is cost and the need for a wax coating to encourage acceptance. Some plastic foundations come pre-coated; if not, rub a thin layer of beeswax over the surface before installing. Plastic frames are not immune to breakage UV exposure makes them brittle, so store them out of direct sunlight. For high-turnover commercial operations, the labour saving often justifies the higher unit price.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Cut to spec:</strong> Verify top bar 483 mm, side bars 232 mm, bottom bar 448 mm before assembly.</li>
<li><strong>Square every frame:</strong> Check diagonals; reject any frame out of square by more than 2 mm.</li>
<li><strong>Pre-drill and glue:</strong> Pre-drill side bars and apply wood glue to end grain joints.</li>
<li><strong>Fit eyelets:</strong> Four brass or stainless eyelets per frame; never skip this step.</li>
<li><strong>Tension wire correctly:</strong> Firm, not guitar-string tight; test by plucking.</li>
<li><strong>Embed wax gently:</strong> Work centre-outward; replace cracked sheets rather than patching.</li>
<li><strong>Store dry and flat:</strong> Stack frames flat in a dry, shaded store; never lean them against a wall.</li>
<li><strong>Cost the frame:</strong> Record material and labour cost per frame; review quarterly against supplier prices.</li>
</ol>

<p><strong>Business takeaway:</strong> The frame is not a consumable it is a capital asset. A frame built to spec, wired correctly, and stored properly will serve 58 extraction cycles. A frame built in a hurry will fail in its first season and take a colony's productivity with it. Standardise your frame build, train one person to do it well, and track your cost per frame. That discipline is what separates a hobby from an agribusiness.</p>
