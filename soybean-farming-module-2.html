1. Determinate vs. Indeterminate Growth Habits

Soybean plants grow in two main ways: determinate and indeterminate. Determinate varieties stop growing taller once they start flowering. They put all their energy into filling pods. These are often shorter, bushy plants, maturing in a shorter window. Indeterminate varieties keep growing while they flower and set pods. They get taller, produce more leaves, and can yield more over a longer season. In Africa, most commercial varieties are indeterminate because they adapt to varying rainfall. For example, in Nigeria, the variety 'TGX 1448-2E' is indeterminate and yields about 2.5 tonnes per hectare with good management. Determinate types like 'Doko' are common in shorter-season areas of northern Ghana. The choice affects your planting date and spacing. Indeterminate types need wider spacing (75 cm between rows) to allow air movement and reduce disease. Determinate types can be planted closer (50 cm). Also, indeterminate soybeans are more prone to lodging if you apply too much nitrogen. Since soybean fixes its own nitrogen, avoid nitrogen fertilizer; instead, inoculate seed with rhizobia. For a smallholder, indeterminate varieties are often better because they can recover from early stress like drought. But if your rainy season is short (under 100 days), choose determinate. Always check with your local extension officer for the best growth habit for your area. Knowing this helps you plan harvest and avoid losses.

2. Maturity Groups and Selection Criteria

Soybean varieties are grouped by how many days they take to mature, called maturity groups (MG). Groups range from 000 (very early, 75-85 days) to X (very late, 160+ days). In Africa, most recommended varieties fall in MG III to MG VII. For example, in Zambia, 'Magoye' is MG IV (110-120 days). In Uganda, 'Maksoy 3N' is MG V (120-130 days). Choosing the right maturity group is critical. If you plant a late variety in a short rainy season, pods won't fill before dry weather. If you plant an early variety in a long season, you lose yield potential. Start by finding your local rainfall pattern: how many days of reliable rain do you get? Then pick a variety that matures 10-15 days before the rains normally end. For a 120-day season, choose MG IV (110 days). For 150 days, MG V or VI. Also consider day length: near the equator, day length is stable, so maturity is mostly driven by temperature. In southern Africa, longer days in summer can delay flowering. Tools like the 'Soybean Maturity Group Map' from IITA help. Ask your seed dealer for the maturity group number. If they don't know, avoid that variety. Also look at yield potential: a good variety yields 2-3 tonnes per hectare with proper spacing and inoculant. But don't chase highest yield if it matures too late. A slightly lower-yielding early variety that finishes before drought is better than a high-yielding late one that fails. Always test a new variety on a small plot before planting your whole farm.

3. GMO, Non-GMO, and Organic Varieties

Soybean varieties come in three main types: GMO (genetically modified), non-GMO (conventional), and organic. GMO soybeans have genes inserted for traits like herbicide tolerance (e.g., Roundup Ready) or insect resistance. In Africa, GMO soybeans are grown commercially only in South Africa. Most other African countries do not allow GMO soybean planting, though some import GMO soy for animal feed. Non-GMO varieties are bred through traditional crossing. They are the standard in Nigeria, Zambia, Ghana, and Kenya. Organic soybeans are non-GMO and grown without synthetic pesticides or fertilizers. They fetch premium prices in export markets, sometimes 20-30% more. But organic yields are often lower (1-1.5 tonnes per hectare) because weeds and pests are harder to control. For a smallholder, non-GMO is usually best: seed is cheaper, you can save seed (though not recommended for hybrids), and local markets accept it. If you want to sell to organic buyers, you need certification, which costs money and takes 2-3 years. GMO seed is expensive and you cannot save it legally. Also, some countries like Kenya require labeling of GMO foods. Before planting, check your country's biosafety laws. In most of Africa, planting GMO soybean without approval is illegal. So stick to non-GMO unless you have a contract with a buyer who wants GMO and you are in a country where it's legal. For organic, start with a small plot, use compost and manual weeding, and find a certified buyer first. Never mix GMO and non-GMO seed in the same field if you want to sell as non-GMO.

4. Seed Quality and Certification

Good seed is the foundation of a good soybean crop. Poor seed can cut yields by 50% or more. Quality seed has four key attributes: high germination (at least 85%), genetic purity (true to variety), physical purity (no weed seeds or stones), and freedom from disease. In Africa, always buy certified seed from a registered dealer. Look for the certification tag from your national seed authority, like NASC in Nigeria, SCCI in Zambia, or KEPHIS in Kenya. The tag shows the variety name, lot number, germination percentage, and test date. If a seller cannot show a tag, walk away. You can also do a simple germination test at home: count 100 seeds, plant them in a tray of soil, keep moist, and count how many sprout after 7 days. If less than 85 sprout, don't use that seed for planting. Certified seed costs more (about $1-2 per kg) but is worth it. Saved seed from your previous crop often has lower germination and may carry diseases like soybean mosaic virus. If you must save seed, select from healthy plants, dry it well to 12% moisture, and store in a cool, dry place with ash or neem leaves to deter insects. Never use seed from a field with disease. Also, check seed size: larger seeds have more food reserves and emerge stronger. But don't buy based on size alone. Always ask for the variety name and maturity group. For organic production, look for organic-certified seed, which is harder to find but available from some cooperatives. Remember: cheap seed is expensive in the long run.

5. Regional Adaptation and Yield Potential

Soybean varieties perform differently across Africa's diverse climates. A variety that yields 3 tonnes per hectare in the highlands of Ethiopia may yield only 1 tonne in the lowlands of Mozambique. Regional adaptation means matching a variety to your local rainfall, temperature, soil, and day length. In West Africa, IITA has released many varieties like 'TGX 1835-10E' (yields 2.5 t/ha, matures in 100 days) for the savannas. In East Africa, 'Maksoy' series from Uganda yields 2-2.5 t/ha in 110-120 days. In southern Africa, 'Magoye' is popular in Zambia and Zimbabwe for its drought tolerance, yielding 1.5-2 t/ha. Yield potential is the maximum yield a variety can achieve under ideal conditions. But your actual yield depends on management: planting date, spacing, weeding, and pest control. For example, planting at 60 cm x 10 cm with 2 seeds per hole gives about 333,000 plants per hectare. That density can produce 2.5 t/ha if you control weeds early. If you plant late or skip weeding, yield drops to 0.5 t/ha. Also, soybean needs phosphorus and potassium. Apply 100 kg/ha of NPK 20-10-10 at planting if your soil is poor. But don't apply nitrogen; soybean fixes its own. Inoculate seed with rhizobia (e.g., Biofix) for best results. Check with your local agricultural research station for the best varieties for your district. They often have trial data. Never assume a variety from another region will work for you. Test it first on a small plot. With good management, even a 1.5 t/ha variety can be profitable.

6. Choosing the Right Variety for Your Farm

Choosing the right soybean variety is a decision that affects your entire season. Start by answering four questions: (1) How long is my rainy season? (2) What is my market? (3) What are my pests and diseases? (4) What seed can I afford? For rainfall, count the reliable rainy days. If you have 100-120 days, choose an early maturity group (MG III or IV) like 'Doko' or 'TGX 1448-2E'. If you have 150+ days, choose MG V or VI like 'Maksoy 3N'. For market, if you sell to a processor for oil or feed, any non-GMO variety works. If you sell to organic exporters, you need organic-certified seed. If you sell to a GMO-accepting buyer in South Africa, you can plant GMO. For pests, if you have high soybean rust, choose resistant varieties like 'TGX 1835-10E'. For nematodes, choose resistant varieties like 'UG 5'. For seed cost, certified seed is best but if money is tight, buy a small amount and multiply it: plant 5 kg on 0.2 ha, harvest 100 kg, and plant that next season. But never save seed from hybrid varieties. Also consider your labor: indeterminate varieties need more spacing and may need staking if they lodge. Determinate types are easier to manage. Finally, talk to other farmers in your area. What did they plant last year? What yielded well? Visit a demonstration plot at your extension office. Try at least two varieties on small plots side by side. Measure yield from each. Then scale up the winner. That is the surest way to choose the right variety for your farm.

Practical Reference: Choosing Soybean Varieties for African Farms

In soybean farming, variety choice is the single most consequential decision a farmer makes before planting. It determines yield potential, maturity timing, disease resistance, market eligibility, and ultimately profit per hectare. For African agribusinesses â whether you are a 2-hectare smallholder or a 500-hectare commercial operation supplying processors and feed mills â the right variety is the one that matches your agro-ecological zone, your target market, and your management capacity.

This practical reference consolidates the key technical criteria into decision-ready tools. Use it alongside the other lessons in this module when selecting seed for the coming season.

1. Growth Habit and Canopy Architecture

Soybean varieties fall into two broad growth habits: determinate and indeterminate. Determinate varieties stop vegetative growth when flowering begins, producing a compact, bushy plant â typically 40â80 cm tall. Indeterminate varieties continue growing while flowering, producing a taller, more sprawling plant with extended flowering and pod-fill windows.

In African production systems, determinate varieties are generally preferred in short-season environments and high-density plantings because they concentrate pod set and mature more uniformly. Indeterminate varieties perform better where the growing season is long and rainfall is distributed, but they demand more careful management of lodging and canopy closure.

2. Maturity Groups: The Calendar That Drives Your Rotation

Maturity groups (MGs) range from 000 (very early, short season) to X (very late, long season). In Africa, the most widely grown varieties fall between MG III and MG VII, depending on latitude and rainfall pattern. Choosing the wrong maturity group is one of the most common and costly mistakes: a late-maturing variety planted late in a short-season zone will flower into drought; an early variety in a long-season zone will finish before the rains end, leaving yield potential unused.

3. GMO, Non-GMO, and Organic Market Segments

Africa's soybean market is segmented by consumer and regulatory preference. Genetically modified (GM) soybean â primarily herbicide-tolerant (Roundup Ready) and insect-resistant (Bt) traits â is widely grown in South Africa and increasingly in parts of West Africa, but is restricted or banned in many other African countries. Non-GMO soybean commands a premium in European and Asian export markets, while organic soybean attracts the highest premium but requires certified organic land, inputs, and traceability.

For agribusinesses, the decision is market-driven: identify your buyer before selecting seed. A processor supplying local poultry feed may accept GM at a lower price; an exporter supplying EU feed mills will require non-GMO certification with documented segregation.

Never plant untreated seed that has been stored with pesticides, and never eat or feed treated seed to livestock. Some soybean seed treatments (e.g., fungicide/insecticide coatings) are toxic to humans and animals. Always wear gloves and a mask when handling treated seed, wash hands after handling, and store treated seed separately from food and feed. In areas where aflatoxin contamination is a risk, do not use mouldy or discoloured seed for planting or consumption â aflatoxin is a serious health hazard and can render your crop unsellable.

4. Seed Quality and Certification

Seed quality determines whether your genetic potential is realised. Certified seed guarantees varietal purity, germination percentage (typically â¥85%), and freedom from seed-borne diseases. In many African countries, certified seed is produced and marketed through national seed authorities (e.g., Kenya's KEPHIS, Nigeria's NASC, Zambia's SCCI) and regional bodies such as the West Africa Seed Committee (WASC/ CORAF).

For agribusinesses, the economics are clear: certified seed costs more per kilogram but typically delivers 15â30% higher yields than saved seed, and it opens access to formal markets that require traceability. If you save seed, do so only from non-hybrid, non-GM varieties and only for one generation, and always test germination before planting.

5. Regional Adaptation and Yield Potential

Soybean is photoperiod-sensitive: varieties bred for one latitude may flower too early or too late when moved to another. Always select varieties tested in your agro-ecological zone. In West Africa, IITA-bred varieties such as TGx 1448-2E and TGx 1835-10E are widely adapted and resistant to rust and bacterial pustule. In Southern Africa, varieties from Seed Co, Pannar, and MRI are widely used. In East Africa, varieties from KALRO, NARO, and CIAT are common.

Yield potential under good management in Africa ranges from 1.5â2.5 t/ha for smallholders to 3.0â4.5 t/ha for commercial operations. The gap between potential and actual yield is usually explained by variety mismatch, late planting, poor weed control, and low plant populations â not by genetics alone.

6. Quick Reference Checklist

  • Identify your market first: GM, non-GMO, organic, or seed production â the buyer determines the variety class.
  • Match maturity group to your rainfall window: count backward from the end of rains to determine the latest safe maturity group.
  • Confirm agro-ecological adaptation: choose varieties tested in your zone, not just your country.
  • Check disease resistance profile: prioritise resistance to rust, bacterial pustule, frogeye leaf spot, and soybean mosaic virus where prevalent.
  • Buy certified seed: verify germination %, purity, and certification label from a recognised authority.
  • Calculate seed rate by target plant population: aim for 350,000â450,000 plants/ha for determinate, 250,000â350,000 for indeterminate.
  • Plan for segregation: if growing non-GMO or organic, separate storage, equipment, and transport from GM or conventional lots.
  • Keep records: variety name, lot number, planting date, and yield per field â this data drives next season's decision.
