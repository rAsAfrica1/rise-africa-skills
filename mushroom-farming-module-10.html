Module 10

🍄 Harvesting, Handling and Shelf Life

Mushrooms are 85 to 95 percent water, and everything you earn from a crop can be lost between the shelf and the customer. This module covers when to pick, how to pick without bruising, why cooling immediately is the single biggest lever you have over shelf life, how to pack, and how to measure your own real fresh window under your own transport conditions. It ends with the ordinary food-safety hygiene that protects both your customer and your reputation.

What you will be able to do after this module

  • Recognise the visual cap cues that say a mushroom is ready
  • Demonstrate the twist-and-pull method and say why it beats cutting
  • State the sourced cooling targets and the time window for reaching them
  • Explain the two opposite risks packaging has to balance
  • Plan a sales calendar around a harvest that is front-loaded and perishable
  • Apply the routine food-safety practices that protect a fresh food buyer

Lesson 10.1~12 min

Knowing When to Pick

In this lesson
  • Recognise the visual cap cues that say a mushroom is ready
  • Explain the three costs of harvesting an over-mature mushroom
  • State honestly that no sourced maturity index exists for African oyster mushroom

Picking at the right moment is a skill of the eye, and it is worth more than most growers expect. Pick too early and you throw away weight you could have sold. Pick too late and you damage quality, shorten shelf life, and spray spores through your own growing room. The window is not long, so this lesson is about learning to see it.

Start with the honest gap. No single sourced hour-by-hour or day-by-day maturity index specific to African-grown oyster mushroom was retrieved for this course. There is no figure to give you saying "harvest at 68 hours after pinning" or "harvest on day four". If you meet such a number, ask where it was measured. What this course can teach you, because it is properly sourced, is the visual cue - and honestly, the visual cue is what an experienced grower actually uses anyway.

The rule is this: harvest before the cap fully flattens, and before the veil under the cap, where the species has one, breaks and releases spores.

What that looks like on an oyster mushroom. A young cap curls under at its edge - the rim turns down toward the substrate. As the mushroom matures the cap flattens out, and then the edge begins to lift and curl upward instead. The moment to pick is while the edge is still curled slightly under, before it goes flat. Once the edge starts turning up, you are past it. Learn that shape on your own crop, on your own strain, in your own room, and you will not need a clock at all.

Remember the speed you are working against. The Botswana guide describes the pinhead-to-mature-mushroom stage as taking three to four days, with the mushroom roughly doubling in size daily. Doubling daily means that a bag which looks a day away from harvest in the morning can be ready by evening, and can be past its best by the following day. That is why bags in fruiting need looking at every day - not every other day, and certainly not once a week. In no other part of the production cycle does a single missed day cost you so much.

Now the three costs of leaving a mushroom too long.

First, moisture. An over-mature mushroom loses moisture faster once picked. Given that a fresh mushroom is 85 to 95 percent water, losing water is losing both saleable weight and visible quality. It shrivels sooner in the buyer's hands, which is exactly the complaint that loses you a repeat customer.

Second, spores. This is the cost growers most often overlook. A mature mushroom left too long releases spores continuously. Those spores land on the other bags in your room, on your shelves, on your stored produce and in your air. Remember that a mushroom's spores are its escape mechanism, not part of your crop. Letting a crop over-mature on the shelf is choosing to fill your own growing house with fungal spores, which is both a facility-contamination concern and a food-safety one for the produce sitting nearby.

Third, the buyer. An over-mature mushroom is simply less attractive to a buyer. A flat, upturned, dry-edged cluster does not look like the same product as a tight, curled, firm one, and any buyer with a choice will pay less for it or take the other grower's.

So how do you build the judgement, given that there is no number to memorise? Two habits.

  1. Walk the fruiting room every single day once pins appear, and look at cap edges, not at overall size. Size tells you how much you have. Edge shape tells you when to take it.
  2. Photograph or sketch a cluster you were happy with on the day you picked it, and note the day number from pinning. Do that for a few batches and you will build your own maturity guide - the very thing the sources could not give you - for your own strain and your own conditions.

One practical point about harvesting a cluster. Oyster mushrooms fruit in clusters, and the individual mushrooms in a cluster do not all reach the ideal stage at exactly the same moment. The usual practice is to take the whole cluster when most of it is at the right stage rather than picking individual mushrooms out of it and leaving damaged tissue behind. Damaged and dying tissue on a warm, humid bag is a feeding station for exactly the bacteria and flies the previous module spent six lessons teaching you to keep out.

And one last framing. Every judgement in this lesson is about the mushroom, not the calendar. The calendar tells you roughly when to start looking. Your eyes tell you when to pick.

Lesson 10.2~12 min

How to Pick Without Damaging the Crop

In this lesson
  • Demonstrate the twist-and-pull method and say why it beats cutting
  • Explain how bruising shortens shelf life before the product ever leaves the farm
  • Prepare clean hands, containers and surfaces before a harvest begins

How you pick decides two things: whether the bag you are picking from stays clean enough to give you another flush, and whether the mushrooms in your hand are still worth full price in two days. Both are decided in the few seconds of the pick itself.

The method, as the sources give it. Twist and pull the whole cluster gently at its base rather than cutting it, where that is practical. The reason is simple and it is about what you leave behind. Cutting leaves a stem stub embedded in the substrate. That stub then dies and rots on a warm, humid, richly fed surface - which is a perfect feeding station for bacteria and for the fly larvae you spent the last module excluding. Twisting the whole cluster off at the base takes the stub with it.

Where a stub is unavoidable, remove it promptly. Some clusters break rather than release, and some strains hold on more stubbornly than others. Do not leave the remnant sitting there until the next visit. Clear it, along with any dead or aborted tissue on the bag surface, as part of the same job as the harvest itself. Clearing the bag surface immediately after a flush is also the correct preparation for the next flush - the mycelium underneath needs to rebuild, and it does that better without rotting tissue sitting on top of it.

Now bruising, which is where most avoidable quality loss happens. Mushrooms bruise, and bruised tissue browns. That browning is not just cosmetic: bruised tissue breaks down faster, so a bruised mushroom has a shorter shelf life than an unbruised one from the same flush on the same day. You can destroy two days of shelf life in ten seconds of rough handling and never know you did it, because the damage does not become visible until the mushrooms are already with the buyer.

Remember why this crop bruises so easily. It is 85 to 95 percent water. It is a soft, water-filled structure with very little in the way of protective skin. Any bruising, packing pressure or delay in cooling has an outsized effect on visible quality within hours - the sources say so explicitly. Handle mushrooms the way you would handle ripe tomatoes, not the way you would handle maize cobs.

What gentle handling actually means, in practice:

  • Support the cluster in your palm as you twist, rather than gripping the caps with your fingertips. Fingertip pressure is exactly what produces those brown thumb-shaped marks.
  • Place mushrooms into the container. Do not drop them, and never throw them.
  • Do not pile deeply. The weight of mushrooms on top of mushrooms is packing pressure, and packing pressure bruises. Shallow layers in a wider container beat a deep pile in a narrow one.
  • Do not tip a full container into another container. Every transfer is another chance to bruise, and most of them are avoidable.
  • Move the filled container out of the growing room and into the coolest place available immediately, not at the end of the picking session. The next lesson explains why this matters more than anything else in the module.

Now the hygiene side, which is a food-safety requirement and not merely good practice. Wash your hands before harvest and during it. Use clean harvesting containers. This is not fussiness: mushrooms are often eaten raw or only lightly cooked, unlike most of the food a small farm produces, so anything your hands or your container carry onto the product may reach the customer without a cooking step in between to make it safe.

Think about the containers themselves for a moment. A crate that has been sitting outside, or that carried spent substrate last week, or that was used for something else on the farm, is not a harvest container. Keep dedicated containers for harvest, wash them, and dry them. A cheap crate washed properly is a better food-safety asset than an expensive one that is never cleaned.

One useful habit to close on: harvest into the container you intend to cool the product in. Every extra handling step between the bag and the cool store is a chance to bruise, a chance to warm the product up, and a chance to contaminate it. The fewer times a mushroom is touched between the substrate and the buyer, the better it will look when it gets there and the longer it will last afterwards.

Cooling, the Biggest Lever You Have

In this lesson
  • State the sourced cooling targets and the time window for reaching them
  • Explain why cooling speed matters more than any other post-harvest decision
  • Design a cooling step that works without a refrigerated vehicle

If you take one thing from this module, take this: cooling immediately and consistently is the single biggest lever you have over how much of your harvest reaches your customer in sellable condition. Not packaging. Not variety. Not the buyer you choose. Cooling.

The sourced figures. The MushWorld handbook states that harvested mushrooms should be cooled to 0 to 2 degrees C within five hours of picking for best results. It gives short-term refrigerated storage at 1 to 4 degrees C as extending usable shelf life to somewhere between one day and two weeks, depending on how quickly the cooling happens and how the product is packed. And it gives a second, more achievable figure for growers without full refrigeration: 8 to 10 degrees C, packed in a container wrapped in plastic film, as a practical optimal fresh-storage condition where cooling to near zero is not available.

Look hard at the shelf-life range in the middle of that: one day to two weeks. That is not vagueness. That is the size of the difference that cooling and packing make. The same mushroom, picked on the same morning from the same bag, can be finished in a day or last a fortnight depending on what happens to it in the hours after it leaves the bag. Almost nothing else in this course offers you a fourteenfold difference in outcome for a decision you make on the day.

Why does cooling matter so much? Because a harvested mushroom is still alive. It goes on respiring, using up its own substance, losing water and breaking down its own tissue. All of those processes run faster when it is warm. Cooling does not stop them; it slows them down. And because the mushroom is 85 to 95 percent water with almost no protective skin, the losses show up as visible quality change within hours rather than days.

Now the honest gap, and it is a serious one for anyone selling into a city. No African-specific cold-chain, transport-time, or market shelf-life study for oyster mushroom was retrieved for this course. That matters because most small-scale African growers will not have a refrigerated vehicle, and the sourced figures above come from a handbook written for a different context. So the specific shelf life you will achieve under your own conditions is a figure this course cannot give you. What it can do is tell you the principle, and tell you to measure your own - which is exactly what the capstone of this module asks you to do.

What can you actually do without a fridge? Work down this list in order, and do the ones you can.

  1. Get the product out of the growing room the moment it is picked. Fruiting rooms are held at 20 to 26 degrees C and near-saturated humidity. That is a good room for growing mushrooms and a bad room for storing them.
  2. Harvest early in the morning, when both the crop and the air are at their coolest. This is free and it buys you hours.
  3. Have the coolest available place identified before you pick, not after. A shaded, ventilated store on the cool side of a building. A cellar or dug store. A room with thick walls.
  4. Use evaporative cooling if you have no electricity. A wet cloth over a ventilated container, or a shaded rack with airflow across it, will hold product below ambient - and any degrees below ambient are worth having.
  5. If you have any refrigeration at all, remember the 8 to 10 degrees C figure. You do not need to reach 0 to 2 to gain most of the benefit, and chasing near-zero cooling can be an excuse for doing nothing at all.
  6. Time the journey. If your market is two hours away, that is two hours the product is warming unless you plan for it. Move in the cool of the morning, insulate the load, cover it from direct sun.

One warning about a common mistake. Do not cool by wetting the mushrooms. It is tempting, because water is cooling and the crop looks fresher wet. But caps that stay wet for four to six hours or longer are the precise precondition for bacterial blotch, and you will arrive at market with slimy, blotched product that you cooled properly and ruined anyway. Cool the air around the mushrooms, not the mushrooms themselves.

Finally, record what you do. Write the picking time, the time the product reached your cool place, and the temperature if you have a gauge. Without those three entries you cannot connect a bad delivery to its cause.

Packing for the Journey

In this lesson
  • Explain the two opposite risks packaging has to balance
  • Describe the sourced packing approach and its evidence limits
  • Trial local packaging materials against your own measured shelf life

Packaging looks like a small decision and it is not. It is the last thing you do to the product before it stops being yours, and it decides how the mushroom looks in the hand of the person deciding whether to buy from you again.

Start with what packaging has to achieve, because it is balancing two opposite risks at once.

The first risk is moisture loss. A mushroom is 85 to 95 percent water, and in dry air it keeps losing that water. The result is shrivelling: a cluster that was plump and bright in the morning looks tired and leathery by afternoon. Sealed, humid packaging prevents this.

The second risk is the exact opposite. Moisture accumulation. Water condensing inside a sealed pack, sitting on the caps, accelerates spoilage - and specifically it recreates the bacterial blotch condition you met in the last module, where caps staying wet for four to six hours or longer lets Pseudomonas do its work. Ventilated packaging prevents this.

So good packaging is neither fully sealed nor fully open. It has to hold humidity around the product while allowing enough gas exchange to prevent free water forming on the caps. That is the whole principle, and it explains the sourced practice.

What the sources actually say. Fresh mushrooms are commonly packed in a perforated plastic film or wrap, which allows some gas exchange while retaining humidity around the product. That is the general postharvest handling described in the MushWorld handbook. The word perforated is doing all the work in that sentence. Plain sealed film traps condensation; perforated film holds humidity while letting the pack breathe.

Now the gap, which is significant if you are choosing what to buy. No African-specific packaging-material or packaging-format trial was retrieved for this course - nothing comparing punnets against plastic bags against open crates under real African market and transport conditions. So this course cannot tell you which format performs best where you sell. It can tell you the principle, and it can tell you how to find out for yourself, which is the same method as for shelf life: trial the materials actually available to you against your own observed results.

How to run that trial without spending anything much. Pick a single flush on a single day. Split it into three or four equal lots. Pack each lot in a different way with what you can actually get locally - perforated film over a punnet, a loosely tied bag, a punnet with no wrap, an open crate. Put them all through the same journey and the same storage. Then write down how each looked at 24 and 48 hours. Repeat it once to be sure the result was not the weather that day. That is a real trial, it costs one flush of observation, and it answers the question for your market rather than someone else's.

Some practical points that apply whatever material wins.

  • Pack shallow. Depth of product is packing pressure, and packing pressure bruises. Two shallow packs beat one deep one.
  • Pack cool. Packing warm mushrooms into a closed pack traps warmth and drives condensation as it cools unevenly. Cool first where you can, then pack.
  • Keep the pack out of direct sun at every stage, including the walk from the vehicle to the stall. Sun through clear film heats a pack fast.
  • Do not overfill so that the lid presses on the caps. Pressure marks look exactly like careless handling to a buyer, because they are.
  • Keep packaging materials clean and stored covered. A punnet that has been sitting in dust is a food-safety problem, not just a presentation one.

One commercial point to close on. Packaging is also where a small grower can look more professional than their size suggests. A consistent pack size, clean material, a simple label with your name and the pack date, and product that arrives unbruised will distinguish you from a competitor selling from an open sack - and the buyers named in the sources for this course, hotels and restaurants, supermarkets and greengrocers, and direct household buyers, all notice presentation. Labelling itself is covered properly in the next module, alongside dried product, because what you must put on a label is a question for your national authority and not for a course.

Lesson 10.5~12 min

The Short Fresh Window and How to Sell Inside It

In this lesson
  • Plan a sales calendar around a harvest that is front-loaded and perishable
  • Match buyer types to the fresh window they can absorb
  • Decide what to do with product you cannot sell fresh in time

You now know the biology of the fresh window. This lesson is about the business of it, because a short shelf life is not only a technical problem - it is the thing that decides who you can sell to and how you must organise your week.

Start with the two shapes you are working with at once.

The first shape is the flush pattern. Most growers take three flushes from a batch, with roughly a week to ten days between them, and each flush is smaller than the one before until the substrate is exhausted. Your production is front-loaded and tapering, not even.

The second shape is the fresh window. Refrigerated storage gives a usable shelf life anywhere between one day and two weeks depending on cooling and packing - and without any cooling at all, at the warm end of that, you are working in hours. So your crop arrives in lumps and must move quickly.

Put those together and you get the central scheduling problem of a fresh mushroom business: a big flush is only an asset if you have somewhere for it to go within days. A grower who plans production without planning sales ends up throwing away their best week of the cycle.

The first fix is one you already met: stagger your spawning. Splitting your bags across two spawning dates a week apart, in the same room, at no capital cost, converts one large peak into two smaller ones. Two peaks you can sell beat one peak you cannot.

The second fix is to know your buyers by the window they can absorb. The sources for this course name three consistent buyer categories worth treating as a targeting checklist: hotels and restaurants, supermarkets and greengrocers, and direct household buyers at farmers' markets or through delivery. Think about each in terms of time.

  • Hotels and restaurants take a regular quantity on a regular day and use it quickly. They are excellent for a predictable, staggered supply and demanding about reliability - if you cannot deliver every week they will find someone who can.
  • Supermarkets and greengrocers hold product on a shelf, so your fresh window has to survive not just your journey but their display time. Cooling and packing quality matter most for this buyer.
  • Direct household buyers at a market or by delivery give you the shortest chain and often the best price per kilogram, but the smallest and least predictable volume per customer.

Most small growers should build a mix, and the reason is the flush pattern. A restaurant contract absorbs a steady base. Market and household sales absorb the peak of a big first flush. Neither alone matches the shape of your production.

The third fix is honest pre-selling. Because you can see pins forming three to four days before harvest, and because mushrooms roughly double in size daily in that window, you have several days of warning about roughly how much is coming. Use it. Tell your buyers what is coming and when. A buyer who has been told on Monday that there will be product on Thursday can plan for it; the same buyer surprised on Thursday often cannot.

Now, what about product you genuinely cannot sell fresh in time? There are only three honest answers, and one dishonest one.

The honest answers are: sell it fast at a lower price to a buyer who can use it immediately; dry it, which is the whole subject of the next module and turns a highly perishable crop into a shelf-stable one that reaches markets fresh product cannot; or accept the loss and record it, so you can size the problem and fix the scheduling that caused it.

The dishonest answer is selling product you know is past its best without saying so. It is tempting because the loss feels unfair. Do not do it. A buyer who receives one slimy delivery remembers it far longer than they remember five good ones, and a small grower's whole business runs on being the person a buyer can rely on.

One last discipline. Record every sale: date, quantity, price and buyer. Also record every loss: date, quantity and reason. This course could not give you a verified African price for mushrooms - no peer-reviewed, dated, multi-country African price survey for fresh oyster or button mushroom was retrieved, and the prices circulating on blogs could not be corroborated - so your own sales records are the only price data you will ever have that you can trust. They are also what lets you say, at the end of a cycle, exactly how much of your crop reached a paying customer. That percentage, tracked over several cycles, is the clearest single measure of whether your post-harvest handling is improving.

Lesson 10.6~12 min

Food Safety at Harvest and Handover

In this lesson
  • Apply the routine food-safety practices that protect a fresh food buyer
  • State the water-quality principle and name the kind of authority that sets the standard
  • Refuse to sell any batch showing contamination, and explain why to a customer

This lesson is the unglamorous one, and it is the one that protects both your customer and your business. It is about ordinary food-safety practice: the things that apply to any fresh food crop, mushrooms included, and that a buyer will never see you doing but will certainly notice if you stop.

Start with water. Clean water for any misting, washing or processing step is a precondition, not an optional upgrade. Water carrying bacterial or chemical contamination becomes part of the product - it is applied to a food that will often be eaten raw or barely cooked, and nothing later in the chain removes it.

Now the honest position on the standard. No African-specific water-quality standard for mushroom production was retrieved for this course, and this is territory where a national authority sets the rule: a food-safety or water-quality authority, depending on the country. Your extension office can point you to it. What is safe and necessary to teach regardless of any specific numeric standard is the underlying principle: do not use water you would not be willing to drink or cook with. If you would not give it to your own child in a cup, it does not belong on a crop you are selling to feed someone else's.

Next, hands and containers. Wash hands before and during harvest. Use clean harvesting containers. This is stated as routine practice in the sources for a specific reason: mushrooms, unlike most farm produce, are often eaten raw or only lightly cooked, which removes the cooking step that would otherwise make a handling mistake harmless. There is no second line of defence behind your hands.

Third, and this is the rule with the sharpest commercial edge: never sell a batch that shows any contamination mixed in with clean product, even if only a fraction of the harvest looks affected.

This rule is hard to follow because it costs money in the moment and looks over-cautious. It is not. The customer cannot see the mould that was growing on the bag next door, or the mould that was cut away from an adjacent mushroom in the same cluster. They are not in a position to make the judgement you are making on their behalf. And the arithmetic runs against you badly: a single reported illness, or one visibly mouldy punnet on a supermarket shelf with your name on it, damages a small grower's reputation far more than the value of the product you would have thrown away. You are not protecting a batch. You are protecting the business that will sell a hundred more batches.

If a buyer asks why a delivery is short, tell them the truth: you found contamination in part of the batch and would not send it. Growers expect that to sound like failure. It usually reads to a professional buyer as exactly the opposite, because the thing a hotel or a supermarket fears most from a small supplier is not a short delivery - it is an unreliable one that sends bad product without warning.

Fourth, chemical residue discipline, which connects this lesson straight back to the pest and disease module. Any chemical used anywhere in your growing cycle - for pasteurisation, for pest control, for cleaning surfaces, for washing containers - that was not approved for use on a food crop by your own national regulator is a food-safety risk to your buyer, not merely a compliance risk to you. This is decided by a national authority, and no approved active ingredient, dose or pre-harvest interval for a food-crop mushroom in any named African country appears in this course's sources. The principle to carry is simple: if it can reach the product, ask before you use it, and ask the authority rather than the shopkeeper who sells it.

Finally, a short practical routine you can actually keep. Before every harvest: wash hands, check that containers are clean and dry, check that the water you will use is water you would drink. During harvest: handle gently, keep containers off the floor, remove anything that does not look right into a separate container rather than into the crop. After harvest: get the product to your cooling point, wash the containers you used, and write down what you picked, what you rejected and why.

That last entry - what you rejected and why - is worth more than it looks. Over a few months it tells you which bags, which rooms and which batches are producing the losses, and it feeds directly into the contamination records the previous module asked you to keep. Food safety and pest control are the same discipline seen from two ends: one protects the crop from the farm, the other protects the customer from the crop.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. What is the sourced visual cue for harvest time?

Pick while the cap edge is still curled slightly under. Once it flattens and the edge lifts, you are past the best moment for quality and shelf life.

2. Why must fruiting bags be checked every single day?

At that rate a bag a day away from harvest in the morning can be ready by evening and past its best the next day. No other stage punishes a missed day so heavily.

3. What is the cost most growers overlook when a mushroom over-matures on the shelf?

Spores are the fungus's escape mechanism, not part of your crop. Letting a crop over-mature is choosing to fill your own growing house with fungal spores.

4. What maturity timing figure does this course give for African-grown oyster mushroom?

The gap is real and the course will not fill it with an invented number. Photographing clusters you were happy with, with the day number from pinning, builds your own guide for your own strain.

5. Why take the whole cluster rather than picking individual mushrooms out of it?

The usual practice is to take the whole cluster when most of it is at the right stage, so no torn tissue is left behind as a feeding station.

6. Why is twisting and pulling preferred over cutting?

The stub is the problem. A dying stub on a warm humid bag surface is exactly the feeding station the pest and disease module teaches you to remove.

7. What does bruising actually do to a mushroom?

You can destroy two days of shelf life in ten seconds of rough handling, and never see it, because the damage shows up after the product has left you.

8. Which packing habit reduces bruising?

The weight of mushrooms on mushrooms is packing pressure, and packing pressure bruises. Every transfer between containers is another avoidable chance to damage the crop.

9. Why does harvest hygiene matter more for mushrooms than for many farm products?

Anything your hands or containers carry onto the product may reach the customer directly. Washed hands and dedicated, clean harvest containers are a food-safety requirement.

10. What should you do with a filled harvest container?

Cooling speed is the single biggest lever on shelf life, as the next lesson shows. Waiting until the end of the session costs you hours of the window the crop has.

11. What is the single biggest lever a grower has over post-harvest losses?

The sourced shelf-life range for refrigerated storage runs from one day to two weeks depending on cooling speed and packing. Almost nothing else in the course offers that size of difference.

12. What is the sourced practical target where full refrigeration is not available?

You do not need near-zero cooling to gain most of the benefit, and waiting until you can afford a proper cold room is an excuse for doing nothing.

13. Why should you not cool mushrooms by wetting them?

Cool the air around the mushrooms, not the mushrooms themselves, or you will arrive at market with slimy blotched product that you cooled correctly and ruined anyway.

14. Why does a harvested mushroom deteriorate so fast in a warm room?

Cooling does not stop those processes, it slows them. With 85 to 95 percent water and almost no protective skin, the losses show as visible quality change within hours.

15. What does this course give as your expected shelf life under African transport conditions?

The gap is logged honestly. The capstone of this module is a three-group test that gives you your own real fresh window under your own real conditions.

16. Why is perforated film used rather than sealed film?

Sealed film traps condensation, which sits on caps and recreates the bacterial blotch condition. Fully open packaging lets the product shrivel instead.

17. What are the two opposite risks packaging must balance?

That is why good packaging is neither fully sealed nor fully open, and why the principle matters more than any specific material this course could name.

18. What packaging comparison data exists for African market conditions?

Splitting one flush into three or four lots packed differently, put through the same journey and inspected at 24 and 48 hours, answers the question for your market at almost no cost.

19. Why should you cool before packing rather than after?

Condensation inside the pack is exactly the wet-cap condition that causes blotch, so a warm pack can undo good cooling work later in the journey.

20. Where does this course say labelling requirements come from?

What must appear on a food label is set by a national authority and differs by country, so a course cannot state it as a rule. The next module covers the principle and who to ask.

21. What is the central scheduling problem of a fresh mushroom business?

A grower who plans production without planning sales ends up throwing away the best week of the cycle. Staggered spawning turns one unsellable peak into two sellable ones.

22. Which buyer type puts the most pressure on your cooling and packing quality?

A restaurant uses product quickly. A shelf holds it for days, so whatever window you have left on delivery is what the retailer has to work with.

23. What advance warning does the crop give you for pre-selling?

Mushrooms roughly double in size daily in that window, so you can see roughly how much is coming and tell buyers before it arrives. A buyer warned on Monday can plan for Thursday.

24. Which is NOT an honest response to product you cannot sell fresh in time?

One slimy delivery is remembered far longer than five good ones, and a small grower's business runs on reliability more than on any single sale.

25. Why are your own sales records the only price data you can trust?

The blog figures checked during compilation could not be corroborated and in one case were internally inconsistent. Your recorded sales are real, local and current.

26. What is the teachable principle on water quality, given that no African standard was retrieved?

Water carrying bacterial or chemical contamination becomes part of a product often eaten raw. The specific standard is set by a national authority, not by a course.

27. Why is harvest hygiene more critical for mushrooms than for most farm produce?

There is no second line of defence behind your hands and your containers, which is why washing both is stated as routine practice rather than an optional extra.

28. Part of a batch shows contamination. What do you sell?

The customer cannot see the mould that was on the neighbouring bag or cut from an adjacent mushroom. One reported illness or one mouldy punnet costs far more than the discarded product.

29. A buyer asks why your delivery is short because you rejected part of a batch. What should you tell them?

What a professional buyer fears most from a small supplier is not a short delivery but an unreliable one that sends bad product without warning. Honesty reads as reliability.

30. Which chemicals fall under residue discipline?

It is a food-safety risk to your buyer, not merely a compliance risk to you, and the approval question goes to your national authority rather than to the shop that sells the product.

Module 10 capstone

Measure your own true shelf life under your own real conditions, because no African cold-chain or shelf-life study exists to tell you what it is. Step 1: at your next harvest, pick twelve mushrooms of similar size and quality at the same moment, and note the exact time of picking. Step 2: divide them into three groups of four and label the groups A, B and C. Step 3: leave group A in ordinary room conditions where you normally hold harvested product. Put group B into the coolest place you can reach quickly - a cool room, a shaded store, an evaporative cooler, a fridge if you have one - and write down how long after picking it got there. Put group C through your actual sales journey: pack it as you would pack it for a buyer and carry it the distance and by the method you really use. Step 4: write down the time each group was set up and describe the appearance of each group in writing at 6 hours, 24 hours, 48 hours and 72 hours - colour, firmness, smell, any sliminess or browning. Step 5: for each group, record the hour at which you would no longer be willing to sell it to a paying customer. Step 6: write those three figures on one page headed with the date and the weather. Those are your real fresh windows. Step 7: name the one change to cooling, packing or transport you will make first, and repeat the test after you make it.
