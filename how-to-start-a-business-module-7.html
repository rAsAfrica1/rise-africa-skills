7. Step 7 — Grow, Diversify, or Exit

After 12 months of operating, you will know whether the business is growing, stable, or stuck. This module is about what to do next — how to decide whether to reinvest, hire, add a second product, open a second location, sell the business, or walk away cleanly.

Module 7 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~50 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 7.1 — Reinvest vs take profit — how to decide

Every month, you have profit. What you do with it in the first two years determines the size of the business you will have in year five.

Three options for profit:

  • Reinvest — put money back into stock, equipment, staff, or marketing
  • Take out — use it for personal expenses, savings, or investments
  • Split — some of each

Most successful businesses in their first two years take out 30–50% and reinvest 50–70%. The exact split depends on three factors:

FactorLean toward reinvestLean toward take out
Business stageUnder 2 years oldOver 3 years old, stable
OpportunitiesClear growth available (new product, more stock, hire)Market is flat, no obvious growth
Personal situationYou have other income or savingsThis is your family's only income

Never take out 100% of profit for personal use. A business with no reinvestment stalls in year two and loses ground to competitors who kept investing. Never reinvest 100% either — the owner burns out and the business loses its reason for existing.

The reinvestment rule: In year one, aim to reinvest 70%. In year two, 50%. In year three and beyond, 30% — unless you are actively opening new locations or lines, in which case bump it back up.
Practical exercise 7.1: Look at your last 3 months of profit. Decide the split for the next 6 months. Write down exactly what the reinvested portion will buy (more stock, equipment, staff, marketing). Commit for 6 months.

Lesson 7.2 — Hiring your first helper

Hiring is one of the biggest leaps in a small business. Done right, it triples your capacity. Done wrong, it eats your margin and creates a legal headache.

Hire when one of these is true:

  • You are turning away customers because you cannot serve them fast enough
  • You are working 12+ hours a day, 6 days a week, and still behind
  • There is a specific task (delivery, packaging, accounts) that someone else could do at 60% of your pay

Hire carefully:

  • Start with a 1-month trial. Paid, clear expectations, review at 30 days. Not a "permanent job" until they have proven themselves.
  • Write down the job. What are the 5 things this person is responsible for? What is the schedule? What are they NOT allowed to do without asking?
  • Pay on time, every time. Nothing destroys trust faster than late wages. If you cannot afford to pay on time, you cannot afford to hire yet.
  • Legal basics. Register them under PAYE and social security. Get a simple written contract. Even for a family member. Especially for a family member.
  • Train your standard. Not just what to do — how you do it. Consistency is the whole point of hiring.
Never hire family on the basis of relationship alone. If they cannot do the job, they will not do the job — and firing family is much harder than firing a stranger. Same rules apply. Trial period, written job description, review.
Practical exercise 7.2: Write the job description for your first hire. Five responsibilities, schedule, pay range, review period. Even if you are not hiring yet, having this ready speeds up the process when you are.

Lesson 7.3 — Adding a second product line or location

Growth through new products or new locations is tempting — and risky. The failure rate for second product lines and second locations in small business is high, because owners underestimate the extra complexity.

Do a new product line when:

  • Existing customers are asking for it (real demand, not your idea)
  • It uses the same equipment, staff, and suppliers (low incremental cost)
  • You have a proven 3-month track record in the first product

Do a new location when:

  • The first location is stable for 6+ months, with profit
  • You have a trained manager ready to run it
  • You can fund 6 months of losses at the new location without touching the first location's profit
Expansion typeRiskTime to breakeven
Related product line (uses same setup)Low2–3 months
Unrelated product lineHigh6–12 months
Second location, same areaMedium3–6 months
Second location, new areaHigh6–12 months
Franchise your modelVery high complexity1–2 years

Expand one thing at a time. Never add a product and a location in the same quarter. You will not be able to tell which one is failing if something goes wrong.

Practical exercise 7.3: List 3 possible expansions for your business. Rank them by risk (low/medium/high). Pick the lowest-risk one to try first. Plan the 90-day test period with clear success criteria.

Lesson 7.4 — Franchising, licensing, or selling the business

At some point, you may want to scale without personally operating every unit — or exit entirely. Three paths:

Franchising — you license your brand, system, and processes to others in exchange for a fee and/or a share of revenue. Complex to set up (needs a franchise agreement, training manual, brand assets) but scales fast. Best for businesses that are extremely standardised and replicable.

Licensing — you allow someone to use your brand or produce your product in a specific territory for a fee. Simpler than franchising. Common in food, cosmetics, and manufacturing. Best for businesses with a strong brand or formula.

Selling the business — you sell 100% of the business to a buyer. Valuation is typically 2–4× annual profit for a small business in Africa. Sometimes higher if the business has strong brand, contracts, or equipment. Takes 3–12 months to complete properly.

Exit typeWhat you getWhat you give upTime to set up
FranchisingFranchise fees + royaltiesSome control, brand standards6–12 months
LicensingLicense feesSome control over brand use3–6 months
SellingLump sum paymentEverything3–12 months

All three require clean records. A buyer or franchise partner will ask for 12–24 months of bank statements, tax filings, and profit statements. If you have not been keeping records, you will not be able to prove the business is worth anything.

The exit rule: Build every business as if you will sell it someday. Clean records, standardised processes, trained staff, documented systems. Even if you never sell, this discipline makes the business more valuable and easier to run.
Practical exercise 7.4: Which exit path interests you most? Write down what you would need to do in the next 12 months to prepare for it. Even if you never do it, that preparation makes your business stronger.

Lesson 7.5 — Scaling profitably — the art of saying no

Growth is not always good. Every business has an optimal size — beyond which extra volume reduces profit, quality, or your own sanity. Knowing when to stop growing is as important as knowing when to grow.

Signs you have hit your optimal size:

  • Adding more customers or orders no longer increases net profit
  • Quality is dropping because you are stretched thin
  • You are working more hours for the same money as 6 months ago
  • Your health, relationships, or mental state are suffering

Scaling further means changing something fundamental. If you want a bigger business than your current model supports, you need to change the model. Hire more staff. Add a second location. Standardise and license. Raise prices. Any of these can scale a business beyond its current ceiling — but each requires change.

What "saying no" looks like in practice:

  • Turning down orders you cannot deliver well
  • Firing customers who are unprofitable or difficult
  • Refusing to add products just because competitors have them
  • Stopping work at a set time each day, not working until everything is done
The right-size rule: A business that earns a comfortable profit in 40 hours a week is worth more than a bigger business that earns slightly more in 80 hours a week. Know what size you actually want.
Practical exercise 7.5: Write down the maximum size you want your business to be in 3 years — in revenue, staff, locations, and hours you personally work. Design your growth toward that specific target, not "as big as possible".

Lesson 7.6 — The 3-year vision

All the previous lessons have been about the first 90 days, the first year, the next expansion. This final lesson zooms out to 3 years.

Write your 3-year vision on one page. It has five parts:

  1. Business size. What is annual revenue? How many staff? How many locations? How many customers?
  2. Your role. Are you still the operator, or a manager, or an owner-away?
  3. Income. How much do you take out personally each month?
  4. Impact. What does the business do beyond profit — jobs created, products sold, communities served?
  5. Exit or continue. Are you building to sell, to franchise, to hand to family, or to run forever?

Then work backwards. If in 3 years you want $50,000 annual revenue and 3 staff, what does that mean for year 2? Year 1? This month?

Most small businesses never plan 3 years ahead. They react to whatever is happening this week. The ones that grow have a plan, revisit it every 90 days, and adjust — but never lose sight of where they are heading.

Written vision beats mental vision 10 to 1. A plan on paper gets executed. A plan in your head drifts with the mood of the day. Write it down. Read it quarterly. Adjust it yearly.
Practical exercise 7.6 — Capstone: Write your one-page 3-year vision. All five parts. Date it. Put it somewhere you will see every week. In 90 days, review it — has anything changed? This is the document that keeps your business pointed in a direction.
