1. Common Citrus Pests (Aphids, Mites, Scales)

Aphids are small, soft-bodied insects, usually green, black, or brown, that cluster on new shoots and the undersides of young leaves. They suck sap and excrete honeydew, a sticky substance that attracts ants and grows sooty mould, a black fungus that blocks sunlight. In Africa, the brown citrus aphid is common and can transmit tristeza virus. Check for aphids weekly by inspecting 10 random new shoots per tree. If you see more than 5 aphids per shoot, act. You can spray a strong jet of water to knock them off, or use insecticidal soap (5 ml per litre of water) in the evening. Ladybirds and lacewings eat aphids, so avoid broad-spectrum pesticides.

Mites are tiny, eight-legged pests, often red or rust-coloured, that cause stippling, yellowing, and leaf drop. The citrus red mite and rust mite are major in dry, dusty conditions. Use a hand lens (10x) to check leaves. If you see more than 2 mites per leaf, treat. Sulphur dust (20 kg per hectare) or abamectin (0.5 ml per litre) works. But sulphur can burn leaves in hot weather, so apply in the evening. Keep trees well-watered; drought-stressed trees suffer more.

Scales are small, immobile insects covered by a waxy shell, looking like tiny bumps on leaves, twigs, and fruit. They suck sap and can kill branches. The soft brown scale and California red scale are common. Check for scales by looking for sticky honeydew and black sooty mould. Prune out heavily infested branches. Spray horticultural oil (20 ml per litre) in the dormant season to smother scales. In summer, use oil at 10 ml per litre. Repeat after 2 weeks. Encourage parasitic wasps like Aphytis melinus, which lay eggs inside scales. Avoid ant nests by banding tree trunks with sticky glue.

Always monitor your orchard weekly. Keep records of pest numbers and treatments. Healthy trees tolerate some pests, so don't spray unless thresholds are reached. Rotate chemicals to prevent resistance. For African smallholders, handpicking and water sprays are cheap first steps.

2. Major Diseases (Citrus Canker, Greening, Tristeza)

Citrus canker is a bacterial disease causing raised, corky, brown lesions on leaves, stems, and fruit. It spreads by wind-driven rain, contaminated tools, and infected nursery stock. In Africa, it is a quarantine disease in many countries. Symptoms appear 7-14 days after infection. Lesions have a yellow halo. To manage, prune infected branches 30 cm below the lesion and burn them. Disinfect tools with 10% bleach solution between trees. Copper-based sprays (e.g., copper hydroxide at 2 g per litre) every 21 days during wet season reduce spread. Do not move fruit or plants from infected areas. Report to agricultural authorities.

Citrus greening, or huanglongbing (HLB), is the most devastating citrus disease worldwide. It is caused by bacteria spread by the Asian citrus psyllid, a small, mottled brown insect. Symptoms include blotchy mottled leaves, yellow shoots, small lopsided fruit with bitter juice, and tree decline. There is no cure. In Africa, it is present in some countries. Control psyllids with imidacloprid (0.5 ml per litre) soil drench every 3 months, or spray fenpropathrin (1 ml per litre) when psyllid numbers are high. Remove and burn infected trees to prevent spread. Use certified disease-free nursery trees.

Tristeza is a viral disease spread by aphids, especially the brown citrus aphid. It causes quick decline (sudden wilting and death) in trees grafted on sour orange rootstock, and stem pitting in others. In Africa, use tristeza-tolerant rootstocks like rough lemon, Volkameriana, or Cleopatra mandarin. There is no cure for infected trees; remove and replant with tolerant rootstock. Control aphids with insecticidal soap or imidacloprid. Avoid moving budwood from infected areas.

For all three diseases, prevention is key: buy certified disease-free trees, maintain orchard hygiene, control insect vectors, and report unusual symptoms to extension officers. Keep records of disease incidence. In Africa, collective action among neighbouring farmers is essential to prevent regional spread.

3. Integrated Pest Management (IPM) Strategies

Integrated Pest Management (IPM) is a common-sense way to control pests and diseases using a combination of methods, with chemicals as a last resort. It saves money, protects your health, and keeps the environment safe. The first step is monitoring: walk your orchard at least once a week. Check 10 trees randomly. Look at new shoots, leaves, and fruit. Write down what you see. Use a hand lens for mites and scales. Set action thresholds: for aphids, treat when 20% of new shoots have 5 or more aphids; for mites, when 10% of leaves have 2 or more mites. This prevents unnecessary spraying.

Cultural controls are the cheapest. Plant certified disease-free trees. Space trees 5-6 metres apart for good airflow. Prune dead wood and water sprouts to reduce hiding places. Remove and burn heavily infested branches. Keep the orchard floor clean of fallen fruit and leaves, which harbour pests. Water and fertilise properly; healthy trees resist pests better. Use cover crops like cowpeas between rows to attract beneficial insects.

Biological control means using natural enemies. Ladybirds, lacewings, and predatory mites eat aphids and mites. Parasitic wasps attack scales and psyllids. You can buy these from suppliers or encourage them by planting flowers like sunflowers and marigolds. Avoid broad-spectrum pesticides like cypermethrin, which kill beneficial insects. If you must spray, use selective products like insecticidal soap or horticultural oil.

Chemical control is the last option. Choose the right product for the pest. Rotate chemicals with different modes of action to prevent resistance. Spray in the evening when bees are not active. Follow label instructions exactly. Wear protective clothing. Keep records of all sprays.

IPM is a cycle: monitor, decide, act, and evaluate. Share your experiences with other farmers. In Africa, IPM has helped smallholders reduce pesticide costs by 30-50% while getting better fruit quality.

4. Biological Control Agents

Biological control uses living organisms to control pests. It is safe, cheap, and sustainable. In Africa, many natural enemies already exist in your orchard. Your job is to protect and increase them. The main groups are predators, parasitoids, and pathogens.

Predators eat pests directly. Ladybirds (ladybugs) are common. Both adults and larvae eat aphids, scales, and mites. One ladybird can eat 50 aphids a day. Lacewing larvae, called aphid lions, eat 200 aphids or mites in their development. Predatory mites (e.g., Phytoseiulus persimilis) eat spider mites. You can buy them from commercial suppliers, but they are expensive. Instead, encourage wild ones by planting nectar-rich flowers like dill, fennel, and marigolds. Avoid dusty conditions; mites thrive in dust, but predatory mites do not. Hose down dusty roads.

Parasitoids are insects that lay their eggs inside or on a pest, killing it. The most important for citrus are tiny wasps. Aphytis melinus parasitises California red scale. Encarsia formosa parasitises whiteflies. Trichogramma wasps parasitise moth eggs. You can buy these wasps from insectaries, but they are sensitive to pesticides. Release them when pest numbers are low. Provide sugar water or honey as food for adult wasps.

Pathogens are disease-causing organisms. Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) is a bacterium that kills caterpillar larvae when eaten. It is safe for humans and beneficial insects. Beauveria bassiana is a fungus that infects aphids, whiteflies, and thrips. Mix 5 g per litre of water and spray in the evening. Metarhizium anisopliae controls termites and beetles. These biopesticides are available in many African agro-dealer shops.

To use biological control successfully: identify your pest correctly, reduce broad-spectrum pesticide use, provide habitat for natural enemies, and release commercially available agents only when needed. Monitor regularly to see if beneficials are present. If you see mummies (parasitised aphids) or ladybird larvae, you have free pest control. Be patient; biological control takes time but gives long-term results.

5. Safe Pesticide Handling and Application

Pesticides are poisons. They can kill pests, but also harm you, your family, and the environment if misused. Always read the label before opening a container. The label tells you the active ingredient, target pest, dose, safety precautions, and pre-harvest interval (PHI) the number of days you must wait before harvesting. Never use a pesticide not registered for citrus. In Africa, buy from licensed agro-dealers and insist on a receipt.

Wear protective clothing: long-sleeved shirt, long trousers, rubber boots, gloves, goggles, and a face mask. Mix chemicals outdoors or in a well-ventilated area, never near food or water sources. Use clean water. Measure accurately using a measuring cup or syringe. Never use more than the recommended dose; it wastes money and harms beneficial insects. Mix only what you need for one day. Triple-rinse empty containers and puncture them so they cannot be reused. Never burn or bury containers near wells.

Apply pesticides in the early morning or late evening when bees are not flying and wind is low. Spray the undersides of leaves where pests hide. Use a calibrated knapsack sprayer. Calibrate by spraying water on a measured area (e.g., 10 m x 10 m) and measuring how much water you used. Then adjust your walking speed. Keep the nozzle 30-50 cm from the target. Do not spray if rain is expected within 6 hours. Avoid spraying near water bodies; pesticides kill fish.

After spraying, wash your hands and face with soap. Wash contaminated clothing separately. Shower and change clothes. Store pesticides in a locked cabinet away from children and animals. Keep records: date, product, dose, area sprayed, and weather. If you feel dizzy, nauseous, or have blurred vision, stop work and seek medical help immediately. Take the label with you. In case of skin contact, wash with plenty of water. For eye contact, rinse for 15 minutes.

Dispose of leftover spray mix by applying it to a non-crop area according to label instructions. Never pour it down a drain. Teach your workers about safe handling. A safe farmer is a productive farmer.

6. Quarantine and Regulatory Measures

Quarantine and regulatory measures are laws and rules that prevent the introduction and spread of dangerous pests and diseases. In Africa, every country has a plant protection organisation, often under the Ministry of Agriculture. They enforce quarantine at borders, airports, and within the country. As a citrus farmer, you must know and follow these rules. Breaking them can lead to fines, jail, or loss of your farm.

Key quarantine pests and diseases for citrus include citrus greening (HLB), citrus canker, citrus tristeza virus, and the Asian citrus psyllid. Many African countries have strict rules: you cannot bring citrus plants, budwood, or fruit from infected areas into clean areas. For example, South Africa has strict controls on citrus imports. If you travel, never bring back citrus seeds or plants. Buy only certified disease-free nursery stock from registered nurseries. Ask for a phytosanitary certificate.

Within a country, quarantine may be declared when a new pest is found. Movement of citrus fruit, plants, and equipment from the quarantine area is banned. You may be required to allow inspectors onto your farm. They may order you to remove and burn infected trees. You are entitled to compensation in some countries, but not all. Report unusual symptoms immediately to your extension officer. Early detection can save the whole region.

Good on-farm biosecurity is your responsibility. Disinfect tools, boots, and vehicle tyres when moving between orchards. Use footbaths with 10% bleach at orchard entrances. Do not share pruning equipment without disinfecting. Control ants, which can carry pests. Keep records of all visitors and equipment. If you sell fruit, follow grading and packing rules. Some markets require fruit to be free of certain pests. Inspect fruit before sale.

International trade: if you want to export, your country must have a recognised phytosanitary system. You will need to register your orchard and follow good agricultural practices (GAP). The African Union has a plant health strategy to harmonise rules. Support it by complying. Quarantine is not a burden; it protects your livelihood and your neighbours'.

<h2>Practical Reference: Pest and Disease Management for African Citrus Enterprises</h2>

<p>Citrus is one of Africa's most commercially valuable horticultural crops, with South Africa alone exporting over 160 million cartons annually and countries such as Egypt, Morocco, Ghana, Kenya, Tanzania and Zimbabwe supplying fresh fruit, juice and essential oils to regional and global markets. For a citrus enterprise, pests and diseases are not merely agronomic problems they are <strong>business risks</strong>. A single greening (HLB) outbreak can wipe out an orchard's productive life; citrus canker can close export markets overnight; and scale infestations can downgrade fruit from Class 1 export to processing, cutting farmgate revenue by 4060%.</p>

<p>This Practical Reference consolidates the six lessons in this module into an operational, business-first guide for African citrus growers, extension officers and agribusiness managers. It covers the major pests (aphids, mites, scales), the major diseases (citrus canker, greening/HLB, tristeza), Integrated Pest Management (IPM), biological control agents, safe pesticide handling, and quarantine and regulatory compliance.</p>

<h2>1. The Business Case for IPM</h2>
<p>African citrus growers face a dual pressure: <strong>export market compliance</strong> (maximum residue limits, phytosanitary certification) and <strong>input cost inflation</strong> (pesticide, fuel and labour costs). IPM is not an environmental luxury it is a cost-control and market-access strategy. Research from the University of Florida and replicated in South African orchards shows that well-implemented IPM programmes reduce pesticide applications by 3050% while maintaining or improving marketable yield.</p>

<h2>2. Major Pests and Economic Injury Levels</h2>
<p>The three pest groups covered in this module aphids, mites and scales share one trait: they are <em>small, fast-multiplying and often unnoticed until damage is economic</em>. Weekly scouting is therefore the cheapest control measure available.</p>

<h2>3. Major Diseases: Canker, Greening and Tristeza</h2>
<p>Diseases are the greatest existential threat to African citrus. <strong>Citrus greening (HLB)</strong>, caused by <em>Candidatus</em> Liberibacter and spread by the Asian citrus psyllid, has devastated orchards in Asia and the Americas and is now present in several African countries, including Ethiopia, Kenya, Tanzania and South Africa's neighbour states. <strong>Citrus canker</strong> (<em>Xanthomonas citri</em>) causes raised corky lesions and triggers export bans. <strong>Citrus tristeza virus (CTV)</strong> causes stem pitting and decline, especially on sour orange rootstock.</p>

<h2>4. Integrated Pest Management (IPM) Strategies</h2>
<p>IPM combines six practical steps (see Figure 1):</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Monitor and scout weekly</strong> walk a W-pattern through each block, inspect new flush, fruit and twigs. Record findings in a scouting log.</li>
<li><strong>Identify and apply thresholds</strong> do not spray on calendar schedules. Spray only when pest counts exceed the action threshold (Table 1).</li>
<li><strong>Cultural and mechanical control</strong> prune for airflow, remove infested shoots, manage weeds that host pests, use windbreaks to reduce canker spread, and maintain tree nutrition to boost tolerance.</li>
<li><strong>Biological control</strong> conserve and augment natural enemies (see section 5).</li>
<li><strong>Targeted chemical control</strong> use selective, registered products, rotate modes of action to prevent resistance, and respect PHI and MRLs.</li>
<li><strong>Record and review</strong> keep spray diaries, scouting records and residue test results. Use them to refine next season's programme and to satisfy buyer audits.</li>
</ol>

<h2>5. Biological Control Agents</h2>
<p>Africa has a strong history of classical biological control in citrus. The most successful example is the importation of <em>Aphytis melinus</em> and <em>Comperiella bifasciata</em> against California red scale, and <em>Rodolia cardinalis</em> (vedalia beetle) against cottony cushion scale. Conservation biocontrol reducing broad-spectrum insecticide use and planting flowering cover crops allows these agents to persist.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Predators:</strong> ladybirds, lacewings, hoverflies, predatory mites (<em>Euseius</em> spp.).</li>
<li><strong>Parasitoids:</strong> <em>Aphytis</em> wasps (scale), <em>Lysiphlebus</em> wasps (aphids).</li>
<li><strong>Pathogens:</strong> <em>Beauveria bassiana</em> and <em>Metarhizium</em> for psyllids and aphids.</li>
</ul>
<p>For African growers, biological control reduces input costs and helps meet the residue requirements of EU and UK retailers. However, it requires patience beneficial insects take 24 weeks to build up after a pest outbreak.</p>

<h2>6. Safe Pesticide Handling and Application</h2>
<p>Pesticides remain a necessary tool in IPM, but mishandling them is the fastest route to market rejection, worker injury and reputational damage. Key rules:</p>
<ul>
<li>Use only products <strong>registered in your country</strong> for the specific pest and crop.</li>
<li>Calibrate sprayers before every season and check nozzles for wear.</li>
<li>Apply in calm conditions (wind &lt;10 km/h), early morning or late afternoon.</li>
<li>Maintain a <strong>spray diary</strong>: date, block, product, active ingredient, rate, operator, PHI.</li>
<li>Train all operators annually and keep certificates on file for audits.</li>
<li>Test fruit for residues before export one failed test can close a market for the whole industry.</li>
</ul>

<h2>7. Quarantine and Regulatory Measures</h2>
<p>African citrus exporters must comply with phytosanitary requirements set by importing countries and regional bodies:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>IPPC / FAO</strong> International Standards for Phytosanitary Measures (ISPMs), especially ISPM 15 (wood packaging) and ISPM 28 (phytosanitary treatments).</li>
<li><strong>National Plant Protection Organisations (NPPOs)</strong> e.g. DALRRD (South Africa), KEPHIS (Kenya), PPECB (export certification).</li>
<li><strong>EU and UK market access</strong> citrus black spot, canker and false codling moth requirements.</li>
<li><strong>Regional harmonisation</strong> COMESA, SADC and AfCFTA protocols on seed and budwood movement.</li>
<li><strong>Certified budwood schemes</strong> mandatory in most African citrus industries to prevent CTV and HLB spread.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>
<ol>
<li>Scout each block weekly using a W-pattern; record pest counts and symptoms.</li>
<li>Spray only when action thresholds are exceeded never on a calendar schedule.</li>
<li>Use certified disease-free budwood and nursery stock from an approved scheme.</li>
<li>Conserve natural enemies by minimising broad-spectrum insecticide use.</li>
<li>Wear full PPE and observe pre-harvest intervals on every spray.</li>
<li>Keep a spray diary and residue test records for every export consignment.</li>
<li>Report suspected greening, canker or new pests to your NPPO immediately.</li>
<li>Train workers annually on IPM, safe handling and quarantine rules.</li>
</ol>
