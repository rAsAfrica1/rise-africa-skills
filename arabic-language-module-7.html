1. Past Tense Conjugation for Regular Verbs

In Arabic, the past tense () is formed by adding suffixes to the verb root. For regular verbs, you take the base form (third person masculine singular) and attach endings based on the subject. For example, the verb 'kataba' () means 'he wrote'. To say 'I wrote', add 'tu' 'katabtu' (). For 'you (masc.) wrote', add 'ta' 'katabta' (). For 'she wrote', add 'at' 'katabat' (). For 'we wrote', add 'naa' 'katabnaa' (). For 'they (masc.) wrote', add 'uu' 'katabuu' (). For 'they (fem.) wrote', add 'na' 'katabna' (). Practice with common verbs: 'darasa' () to study, 'fa'ala' () to do, 'dhahaba' () to go. Conjugate each with all pronouns. In African contexts, use these in daily sentences: 'Darastu al-lugha al-'arabiyya fi al-jaami'a' (I studied Arabic at the university). Write out a table with pronouns and endings. Repeat aloud 10 times each. This builds muscle memory. Avoid mixing up 'ta' for you (masc.) and 'ti' for you (fem.) listen to native speakers on YouTube. With 15 minutes daily, you'll master regular past tense in two weeks.

2. Using Past Tense to Describe Events

When describing past events in Arabic, you use the past tense verb plus time markers. Start with a simple sentence: 'Yesterday, I ate rice and stew' 'Ams, akaltu al-aruz wa al-maraq' ( ). Notice 'ams' (yesterday) sets the past. For events in a sequence, use conjunctions like 'thumma' () meaning 'then'. Example: 'I woke up, then I prayed, then I went to the market' 'Istaiqaztu, thumma sallaytu, thumma dhahabtu ila al-suq' ( ). In African markets, you might say: 'I sold three bags of maize' 'Bi'tu thalathat akyaas min al-dhura' ( ). Use numbers: 'thalaatha' (3), 'khamsa' (5), 'ashara' (10). Describe a typical day: 'I woke at 5 AM, prayed, drank tea, and went to farm' 'Istaiqaztu al-saa'a al-khamisa, sallaytu, sharibtu al-shai, wa dhahabtu ila al-mazra'a' ( ). Practice by narrating your own day in Arabic. Record yourself and compare with a native speaker. Focus on verb-subject agreement: 'I' takes 'tu', 'he' takes no suffix, 'she' takes 'at'. With 10 sentences daily, you'll speak about events confidently in one month.

3. Negation of Past Tense Verbs

To negate a past tense verb in Arabic, you simply add 'maa' () before the verb. For example, 'I wrote' is 'katabtu' (). 'I did not write' is 'maa katabtu' ( ). This works for all regular past tense verbs. 'He did not go' 'maa dhahaba' ( ). 'She did not eat' 'maa akalat' ( ). 'We did not study' 'maa darasnaa' ( ). In African daily life: 'I did not sell the maize' 'Maa bi'tu al-dhura' ( ). 'They did not come yesterday' 'Maa jaa'uu ams' ( ). Note that 'maa' is invariable it does not change with gender or number. Another negation particle is 'lam' () + jussive verb, but that is for present tense negation in the past. For pure past negation, 'maa' is simplest. Practice by taking affirmative sentences and adding 'maa'. Example: 'Sharibtu al-shai' ( ) 'Maa sharibtu al-shai' ( ). Listen to native speakers on YouTube for pronunciation. Write 20 negated sentences about your week. Use 'maa' with all pronouns. Avoid using 'laa' () for past negation that is for present/future. With consistent practice, you'll negate past actions naturally in two weeks.

4. Time Expressions with Past Tense

Time expressions in Arabic help anchor past tense verbs. Common ones: 'ams' () yesterday, 'qabla' () before, 'ba'da' () after, 'al-layla' () last night, 'al-usbu' al-maadi' ( ) last week, 'al-shahr al-maadi' ( ) last month, 'al-sana al-maadiya' ( ) last year. Use them at the beginning or end of sentences. Example: 'Last week, I traveled to Kampala' 'Al-usbu' al-maadi, safartu ila Kampala' ( ). 'I finished work before sunset' 'Antahaitu al-amal qabla al-ghurub' ( ). 'After two hours, I slept' 'Ba'da sa'atain, nimtu' ( ). In African contexts: 'Last month, we harvested 10 sacks of beans' 'Al-shahr al-maadi, hasadnaa 'asharat akyaas min al-fasulya' ( ). Practice by writing your schedule for yesterday: 'At 7 AM, I drank tea. At 8 AM, I went to work. At 2 PM, I ate lunch.' Use 'al-saa'a' () for clock time. Combine with past tense verbs. Listen to Arabic news for time expressions. With 10 sentences daily, you'll master time expressions in three weeks.

5. Sequencing Events in a Story

Sequencing events in Arabic uses connectors like 'thumma' () then, 'ba'da dhalika' ( ) after that, 'qabla dhalika' ( ) before that, 'wa' () and, 'fa' () so/then. Start with a clear opening: 'Fi yawmin min al-ayyam' ( ) One day. Then list events in order. Example story: 'One day, I woke up early. Then I prayed. After that, I ate breakfast. Then I went to the farm. I worked for five hours. After that, I returned home. Then I slept.' In Arabic: 'Fi yawmin min al-ayyam, istaiqaztu mubakkiran. Thumma sallaytu. Ba'da dhalika, akaltu al-futur. Thumma dhahabtu ila al-mazra'a. 'Amiltu khamsa sa'at. Ba'da dhalika, raja'tu ila al-bait. Thumma nimtu.' ( . . . . . . .) Notice the past tense verbs: istaiqaztu, sallaytu, akaltu, dhahabtu, 'amiltu, raja'tu, nimtu. Practice by telling a story about your last trip. Use at least 6 events. Record yourself. Use 'thumma' and 'ba'da dhalika' to link. In African storytelling, you might start with 'Kan ya ma kan' ( ) Once upon a time. With 5 stories, you'll sequence naturally.

6. Common Irregular Past Tense Verbs

Irregular past tense verbs in Arabic do not follow the regular suffix pattern. Common ones: 'kaana' () was, 'qaala' () said, 'jaa'a' () came, 'ra'aa' () saw, 'akala' () ate, 'shariba' () drank, 'dhahaba' () went, 'fa'ala' () did. These have irregular conjugations. For 'kaana': 'kuntu' () I was, 'kunta' () you were, 'kaana' () he was, 'kaanat' () she was, 'kunnaa' () we were, 'kaanuu' () they were. For 'qaala': 'qultu' () I said, 'qulta' () you said, 'qaala' () he said, 'qaalat' () she said, 'qulnaa' () we said, 'qaaluu' () they said. For 'jaa'a': 'ji'tu' () I came, 'ji'ta' () you came, 'jaa'a' () he came, 'jaa'at' () she came, 'ji'naa' () we came, 'jaa'uu' () they came. In African contexts: 'Kuntu fi al-suq ams' ( ) I was in the market yesterday. 'Qaala al-mu'allim' ( ) The teacher said. 'Ji'tu min al-madrasa' ( ) I came from school. Practice by conjugating each verb with all pronouns. Write 20 sentences. Listen to native speakers. With daily practice, you'll master these in one month.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Past Tense Verbs and Narratives for African Agribusiness Professionals

For agribusiness professionals operating across Arabic-speaking markets in North and East Africa from date exporters in Sudan to livestock traders in Somalia, and from cotton cooperatives in Egypt to sesame buyers in Ethiopia the Arabic past tense ( / al-m) is the working tense of record-keeping, negotiation follow-up, and field reporting. Contracts reference what was agreed. Buyers ask what was shipped. Cooperatives report what was harvested. This reference lesson consolidates the six lessons in this module into a field-ready tool: conjugation patterns, negation, time expressions, sequencing devices, and the irregular verbs that appear most often in trade correspondence.

Arabic verbs in the past tense are built from a root, usually of three consonants, and are conjugated by attaching suffixes that mark person, gender, and number. Unlike the present tense, the past tense does not use prefixes which makes it faster to read and write under commercial time pressure, but also means the ending carries the entire grammatical load. Getting the ending wrong in a shipping confirmation can change "I shipped" into "she shipped" a small error with real contractual consequences.

1. Regular Verbs: The Suffix Is the Contract

For sound () triliteral verbs those with no weak radical the past tense is the stem plus a suffix. The stem for Form I is typically CaCaC- (katab-, sharib-, aad-). The suffix tells you who acted. In agribusiness correspondence, the two most consequential pairs are:

  • (-tu) = I "" (shahantu) = I shipped
  • (-n) = we "" (shahan) = we shipped
  • (no suffix) = he "" (shahana) = he shipped
  • (-at) = she "" (shahanat) = she shipped

In a WhatsApp message to a Juba-based buyer, " " ("we shipped the product yesterday") is unambiguous only if the -n is present. Drop it and you have written "he shipped" a different party, a different liability. In Arabic, the suffix is the subject marker.

2. Negation: The Most Common Source of Disputes

Past tense negation in Modern Standard Arabic uses (m) or (lam). The choice matters commercially:

Negator Effect on verb Example (agribusiness) Register
(m) Verb stays in past tense "The shipment did not arrive" Neutral; common in speech and correspondence
(lam) Verb becomes jussive (apocopate) "The shipment has not arrived" Formal; standard in contracts and reports
(l) Normally present/future; with past = emphatic denial rare; emphatic "it absolutely did not arrive" Rhetorical; avoid in commercial writing

Sources: Ryding (2005), A Reference Grammar of Modern Standard Arabic, 12.6 (negation of the past); Badawi, Carter & Gully (2004), Modern Written Arabic: A Comprehensive Grammar, Routledge, 2.8.

SAFETY / COMMERCIAL WARNING Negation in Binding Documents

Do not mix m and lam in the same clause when drafting a contract, Letter of Credit, or insurance claim. Lam triggers the jussive mood, which can change the verb's final vowel and, in weak verbs, its entire shape (e.g., lam yashan vs. m shaana). A mis-vocalised negation in a Bill of Lading dispute or a demurrage claim has been used to argue that a delivery obligation was never triggered. Have any Arabic-language contract clause containing a negative past-tense verb reviewed by a qualified legal translator before signature.

4. Sequencing Events in a Story

Agribusiness narratives a crop failure, a successful export season, a dispute over quality are told in sequence. Arabic uses a small set of connectors to order past events. The most important are:

  • (thumma) "then," with a slight pause; implies a deliberate sequence.
  • (bada dhlika) "after that"; more formal, common in written reports.
  • (wa-badah) "and after it/them"; useful when the subject is a shipment or a batch.
  • (indam) "when"; introduces a past event that triggers another.
  • (lamm) "when" with a past-perfect nuance; "when we arrived, they had already loaded."

A model sequence for a field report: " ." ("We harvested the cotton in October, then we moved it to the warehouse, and after that we shipped it to Egypt.") Each verb is past tense; each connector marks a distinct stage. This is the pattern buyers expect in written Arabic.

6. Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Identify the doer before writing. Choose the suffix first: -tu (I), -n (we), -at (she), - (they m.). In trade Arabic, "we" and "I" carry different liability.
  2. Default to Form I sound verbs for everyday reporting: (harvested), (shipped), (sold), (bought).
  3. Negate with for neutral statements and + jussive for formal/contractual ones. Never mix them in one clause.
  4. Anchor every past-tense report with a time expression so the reader can place the event.
  5. Sequence with / / rather than stringing verbs with plain ; the reader will follow the chronology faster.
  6. Memorise the weak-verb 1st person forms ( ) these appear constantly in meeting notes and trade logs.
  7. Read your sentence aloud. If the final vowel is unclear, the person is unclear. In written Arabic, add the harakt when the meaning is commercially material.
  8. When in doubt, get a legal translator. For contracts, L/Cs, and claims, a mis-suffixed past-tense verb is not a grammar error it is a liability.
