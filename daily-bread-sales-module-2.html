1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Welcome to Module 2. If you want to sell bread daily and make a steady profit, you cannot guess. You need a formula. The Bakery Production Formula is a simple set of steps that tells you exactly how much flour, water, yeast, salt, and sugar to use for any number of loaves. Without it, you waste ingredients, your bread comes out different every day, and customers stop buying. With it, you produce the same good bread every single time, so people trust you and come back.

Why does this matter in Africa? Because most small bakeries here operate by 'feel' a cup of this, a pinch of that. That works for home cooking, but for business, it fails. If you use too much yeast, your bread rises too fast and collapses. Too little salt, and the bread tastes flat. Too much water, and the dough is sticky and hard to shape. The formula fixes all that. It lets you scale up or down. Want to bake 10 loaves? Use the formula. Want 50? Multiply. You control your costs and your quality.

Think about your profit. Flour is your biggest expense. If you use 10% more flour than needed because you didn't measure, you lose money on every loaf. The formula prevents that. It also helps you plan. You know that one bag of flour (50 kg) makes about 80 loaves of standard bread. So if you sell 80 loaves a day, you need one bag. That's how you order supplies and manage cash flow.

In this module, we will cover the tools you need, the step-by-step method, common mistakes, safety, and a practical exercise. By the end, you will be able to write your own production sheet for any size order. Remember: baking is chemistry, but business is math. The formula combines both. Let's get started.

2. Tools and Materials

To follow the bakery production formula, you need the right tools and materials. You don't need expensive equipment, but you must have accurate measuring tools. A digital kitchen scale that measures in grams is the most important tool. Cups and spoons are not accurate enough for business baking. A scale costs about 5,000 to 15,000 Naira (or equivalent) and will save you money in wasted ingredients. You also need a large mixing bowl or a kneading table. A wooden table is best because dough doesn't stick as much. If you don't have a table, a clean plastic basin works.

For measuring liquids, use a graduated jug. For yeast, use a small spoon and the scale. For salt and sugar, the scale again. You need a dough scraper (a flat metal or plastic tool) to cut and lift dough. A rolling pin is optional for some breads, but useful for shaping. You need baking pans or trays. In many African bakeries, loaves are baked on flat trays or in locally made metal pans. Make sure they are clean and greased. You also need a way to control temperature: a thermometer for the oven if possible, or at least a reliable oven with a dial.

Ingredients: flour (bread flour or all-purpose, but bread flour gives better rise), water (clean, preferably filtered), yeast (instant dry yeast is easiest), salt (iodized or non-iodized, but consistent), sugar (optional, but helps browning and yeast activity), and oil or fat (vegetable oil or butter). You may also use milk powder for softer bread. For African markets, you can find these in bulk at lower cost. Buy yeast in small packets to keep it fresh. Store flour in a dry, cool place, off the floor, in a sealed container to prevent weevils.

Other useful items: a timer or clock, a notebook to record your formulas and results, and a clean apron. A dough cutter (bench knife) helps divide dough evenly. If you plan to sell, you need packaging: bread bags, twist ties, and labels. Keep everything clean. A dirty workspace ruins bread. Soap, water, and a clean cloth are part of your tool kit. With these basics, you can produce consistent bread daily.

3. Step by Step Method

The bakery production formula is based on baker's percentage. This means flour is always 100%. Every other ingredient is a percentage of the flour weight. For a standard white bread, the formula is: Flour 100%, Water 60%, Yeast 1.5%, Salt 2%, Sugar 2%, Oil 2%. So if you use 1 kg of flour, you need 600 g water, 15 g yeast, 20 g salt, 20 g sugar, and 20 g oil. This makes about 1.6 kg of dough, which yields two 800g loaves or three 500g loaves. Always weigh, don't guess.

Step 1: Weigh all ingredients. Use your scale. Put flour in a large bowl. In a separate jug, mix water (lukewarm, about 30-35C if you don't have a thermometer, it should feel like baby bath water), yeast, sugar, and salt. Stir to dissolve. Let it sit 5 minutes until foamy. This proves the yeast is alive. If it doesn't foam, your yeast is dead get new yeast.

Step 2: Make a well in the flour. Pour the liquid mixture in. Add oil. Mix with your hands or a wooden spoon until a shaggy dough forms. Then knead on a clean table for 10-15 minutes. Kneading develops gluten, which makes bread chewy and helps it rise. The dough should become smooth and elastic. If it's too sticky, add a little flour (but not too much too much flour makes dry bread). If too dry, add water a spoon at a time.

Step 3: First rise (proof). Put dough in a lightly oiled bowl, cover with a clean cloth or plastic, and leave in a warm place (25-30C) for 1 hour, or until doubled in size. In hot African climates, it may take only 45 minutes. Don't let it over-rise; if it collapses, it's over-proofed.

Step 4: Punch down. Knock the air out. Divide into equal pieces using your scale. Shape into balls or loaves. Place in greased pans. Cover and let rise again for 30-45 minutes until nearly doubled.

Step 5: Bake. Preheat oven to 200C (400F). Bake for 30-35 minutes for 800g loaves, or 20-25 minutes for smaller rolls. The bread is done when golden brown and sounds hollow when tapped on the bottom. Remove from pans and cool on a wire rack. Don't slice hot bread it gets gummy. This method gives you consistent bread every time.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Even with the formula, things can go wrong. Here are the most common mistakes and how to fix them. Mistake 1: Not measuring accurately. If you use cups instead of a scale, your bread will vary. Fix: Buy a digital scale and use grams for everything. Write down your formula and stick to it.

Mistake 2: Water too hot. Hot water kills yeast. If the water is above 45C, yeast dies and dough won't rise. Fix: Use lukewarm water (30-35C). Test with your wrist it should feel neither hot nor cold. If you don't have a thermometer, err on the cooler side; yeast works slower but survives.

Mistake 3: Too much flour during kneading. Adding flour because dough is sticky seems right, but it makes dry, dense bread. Fix: Lightly oil your hands and table instead of flouring. Let the dough rest 10 minutes it absorbs water and becomes less sticky.

Mistake 4: Under-kneading or over-kneading. Under-kneaded dough doesn't develop gluten, so bread is crumbly. Over-kneaded dough is tough. Fix: Knead for 10-15 minutes by hand. The dough should be smooth and spring back when poked. If you have a mixer, 8-10 minutes on medium.

Mistake 5: Skipping the second rise. Many bakers shape and bake immediately. That gives small, dense loaves. Fix: Always let shaped dough rise again until nearly doubled, about 30-45 minutes. Cover it to prevent drying.

Mistake 6: Oven temperature wrong. If oven is too hot, bread browns outside but is raw inside. Too cool, and it doesn't rise well. Fix: Use an oven thermometer. If you don't have one, test with a piece of bread: it should toast in 1 minute at 200C. Preheat for at least 15 minutes.

Mistake 7: Not cooling properly. Cutting hot bread makes it gummy. Fix: Cool completely on a wire rack for at least 30 minutes. This also lets the crust set.

Mistake 8: Storing bread wrongly. Bread in plastic gets soggy; in paper it dries out. Fix: For daily sales, keep bread in paper bags or breathable cloth. For longer storage, freeze. Never store in direct sun. By avoiding these mistakes, your bread will be consistent and sellable.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety in the bakery is not just about avoiding burns it's about producing clean, safe food that won't make customers sick. Start with personal hygiene. Wash your hands with soap and water before touching any ingredients. Tie back hair, wear a clean apron, and avoid jewelry that can fall into dough. If you are sick, especially with stomach issues, don't bake for others. Your hands carry bacteria.

Workspace safety: Keep floors dry to prevent slips. Clean up spills immediately. Store knives and sharp tools safely. When using the oven, use thick dry cloths or oven mitts. Never use wet cloths steam burns. Open oven doors slowly to avoid a blast of hot air. Keep children away from the oven area.

Ingredient safety: Check expiration dates on yeast, flour, and oil. Old yeast won't rise. Rancid oil smells bad and ruins bread. Store ingredients in sealed containers, off the floor, away from chemicals. Never use the same container for soap and flour. If you have pests, use traps, not poison near food.

Food safety: Bread can grow mold if stored damp. For daily sales, bake fresh each day. If you have leftovers, sell them at a discount the same day or turn them into breadcrumbs. Do not sell moldy bread it's dangerous and destroys your reputation. Keep samples covered. Use clean packaging. If you handle money, wash hands before touching bread again.

Best practices for business: Keep a production log. Write down the date, flour weight, water temperature, rise times, and how the bread turned out. This helps you repeat success and fix problems. Weigh your dough before shaping so every loaf is the same size customers appreciate fairness. Price your bread to cover flour, yeast, salt, sugar, oil, fuel, and your time. A simple formula: cost of ingredients per loaf + 30% overhead + 20% profit = selling price. For example, if ingredients cost 200 Naira, add 60 for overhead and 40 for profit, so sell at 300 Naira. Adjust for your market. Finally, keep learning. Try small changes, record results, and improve. That is the best practice.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it's time to put the formula into practice. This exercise will help you master the bakery production formula by baking a small batch and recording everything. You will need: 1 kg bread flour, 600 g lukewarm water, 15 g instant dry yeast, 20 g salt, 20 g sugar, 20 g vegetable oil, a digital scale, a large bowl, a clean table, a baking tray or pan, and an oven. If you don't have a scale, borrow one or buy the cheapest digital scale you can find it is essential.

Step 1: Set up your workspace. Clean the table and all tools. Wash your hands. Weigh all ingredients separately. Write down the weights in a notebook. This is your production sheet.

Step 2: Activate yeast. In a jug, combine water, yeast, sugar, and salt. Stir and wait 5 minutes. It should foam. If not, stop and get new yeast.

Step 3: Mix and knead. Pour liquid into flour, add oil, and mix until a dough forms. Knead on a lightly oiled table for 12 minutes. Set a timer. Notice how the dough changes from sticky to smooth. Record the time.

Step 4: First rise. Place dough in an oiled bowl, cover, and leave in a warm spot. Check every 15 minutes. Record how long it takes to double in size. In a hot kitchen, it might be 45 minutes; in a cool room, 90 minutes. This teaches you to judge by sight, not just clock.

Step 5: Shape and second rise. Punch down dough. Divide into 3 equal pieces (weigh them to be exact each should be about 530g). Shape into balls or loaves. Place on greased tray. Cover and let rise 30-45 minutes until nearly doubled. Record time.

Step 6: Bake. Preheat oven to 200C. Bake for 25-30 minutes. Check at 20 minutes. The bread should be golden and sound hollow when tapped. Record baking time and how it looks.

Step 7: Cool and evaluate. Cool on a rack for 30 minutes. Then cut a slice. Taste it. Is it soft? Does it have a good crust? Write down your observations. Compare with your notes from step 1. Did you follow the formula exactly? If not, what will you change next time? Repeat this exercise at least three times. Each time, aim for the same result. Once you can produce consistent bread, you are ready to scale up for sales. Bring your notes to the next lesson.

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul style="font-family:Arial, sans-serif; font-size:14px; line-height:1.8;">
<li><strong>Weigh everything.</strong> Never estimate flour, water, yeast, or salt. Use a digital scale.</li>
<li><strong>Check water temperature.</strong> It should be 2428C. Too hot kills yeast; too cold slows fermentation.</li>
<li><strong>Mix until gluten develops.</strong> The dough should stretch without tearing (windowpane test).</li>
<li><strong>Control fermentation time.</strong> 6090 minutes for bulk. Do not rush it.</li>
<li><strong>Shape consistently.</strong> Same weight per loaf ensures even baking and customer trust.</li>
<li><strong>Proof until doubled.</strong> 3045 minutes. Poke test: dough springs back slowly.</li>
<li><strong>Bake at the right temperature.</strong> 180220C. Use an oven thermometer, not guesswork.</li>
<li><strong>Cool completely before packing.</strong> At least 1 hour on racks. Hot bread sweats and spoils faster.</li>
</ul>
