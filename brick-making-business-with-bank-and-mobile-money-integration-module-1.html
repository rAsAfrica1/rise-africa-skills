1. Overview of the Brick Making Industry

Brick making is a centuries-old trade that remains a cornerstone of Africa's construction boom. From Lagos to Nairobi to Accra, demand for bricks is rising due to rapid urbanization and infrastructure projects. You can start a brick making business with minimal capitalâa few wooden moulds, a shovel, and a source of clay or cement. In many African countries, small-scale brick makers supply local builders who prefer bricks over costly imported materials. For example, in Uganda, a simple manual press can produce 500 bricks per day, selling for about UGX 300 each. That's UGX 150,000 daily revenue. The industry has three main segments: traditional fired clay bricks, cement-stabilized bricks, and interlocking bricks. Each has different equipment and skill needs. Your choice depends on local soil, water access, and market demand. The key is to start small, test your product with nearby contractors, and reinvest profits. Banks and mobile money services are crucial here: they help you separate business and personal funds, save for equipment, and accept payments from customers who rarely carry cash. For instance, M-Pesa in Kenya allows you to receive payments instantly and track sales. Without financial integration, you risk mixing money, losing track of profits, and missing growth opportunities. This module will guide you through the financial side so your brick business can thrive.

2. Types of Bricks and Their Uses

Not all bricks are the same. Knowing the types helps you target the right customers and price your product correctly. The most common in Africa are: 1) Fired clay bricksâmade by moulding clay, drying, and firing in a kiln. They are strong, water-resistant, and used for load-bearing walls. A typical kiln in Nigeria can fire 10,000 bricks per cycle, selling at â¦50 each. 2) Cement-stabilized bricksâmade by mixing soil with 5-10% cement and compressing. They don't need firing, saving fuel costs. In Ghana, these bricks sell for GHS 2.50 and are popular for low-cost housing. 3) Interlocking bricksâdesigned to fit together like Lego, reducing mortar use. They are ideal for quick assembly and are gaining traction in South Africa. 4) Hollow blocksâlighter and used for non-load-bearing walls. Each type requires different equipment: manual presses for stabilized bricks, kilns for fired bricks, and moulds for hollow blocks. Your market research should identify what local builders prefer. For example, in Tanzania, interlocking bricks are in high demand for schools and clinics. Also consider production speed: a manual press makes 200 bricks per hour, while a motorized one makes 1,000. Start with one type, master it, then diversify. Remember to calculate your cost per brickâincluding labour, water, and cementâto set a profitable price. Banks can finance equipment upgrades, and mobile money lets you pay suppliers quickly.

3. The Role of Banks and Mobile Money in Small Businesses

In Africa, banks and mobile money are not just conveniencesâthey are lifelines for small businesses like brick making. Banks offer business accounts, loans, and savings products. For example, in Kenya, Equity Bank's 'Mama Biashara' account has no monthly fees and provides loans up to KES 500,000 for equipment. Mobile money, like M-Pesa, MTN Mobile Money, or Airtel Money, allows you to receive payments, pay workers, and buy materials instantly. A brick maker in Rwanda can receive payment from a contractor via MTN MoMo and immediately pay a supplier for cement. This speed reduces theft and errors. Banks also help you build a credit history. When you deposit regularly, you qualify for loans at lower interest rates. Mobile money platforms often offer savings accounts with interest, like M-Pesa's M-Shwari. Additionally, both provide records: bank statements and mobile money transaction histories are accepted as proof of income when applying for larger loans. For a brick business, you can use mobile money to pay casual labourers daily, avoiding cash shortages. You can also set up automatic savings for future equipment. The key is to separate business and personal finances from day one. Open a business bank account and register a mobile money till number. This builds trust with customers who prefer paying electronically. In short, banks and mobile money give you security, access to credit, and efficiency.

4. Identifying Financial Needs for a Brick Making Business

Before you approach a bank or mobile money agent, you must know exactly how much money you need and when. Start by listing your startup costs: land lease (if not owned), clay or soil, cement, water, moulds, a wheelbarrow, shovels, and labour. For a small manual operation in Zambia, startup can be as low as ZMW 5,000. If you buy a motorized brick press, it can cost ZMW 25,000. Next, estimate monthly operating costs: fuel for kiln (if firing), wages for 2-3 workers (ZMW 1,500 each), and transport. Then project your income: if you sell 3,000 bricks per month at ZMW 2 each, that's ZMW 6,000. Subtract costs to find profit. You also need working capital to cover expenses before sales come in. Many brick makers fail because they run out of cash during the drying period. Identify your financial gaps: do you need a loan for equipment? A savings plan for rainy days? Mobile money for daily transactions? For example, a brick maker in Senegal might need CFA 200,000 for a used truck to deliver bricks. Break your needs into short-term (weekly wages), medium-term (equipment), and long-term (land purchase). Write them down. Then match each need to a financial product: mobile money for daily wages, bank savings for equipment, and a microfinance loan for expansion. Being specific helps you get approved faster and avoid debt traps.

5. Setting Up a Business Bank Account

A business bank account is the foundation of financial discipline. It separates your brick money from personal funds, making it easier to track profit and pay taxes. In most African countries, you need: a business registration certificate (from the companies registry), a tax identification number (TIN), and identification documents (national ID or passport). For example, in Nigeria, you register with the Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC) to get a certificate, then open an account at a bank like GTBank or Access Bank. The process takes 1-2 weeks. Some banks offer zero-balance accounts for small businesses, like Zenith Bank's 'SME account'. When choosing a bank, compare monthly fees, transaction costs, and mobile banking features. You want a bank with a branch near your brick site and good mobile app. Once opened, deposit all business income into this account. Use it to pay suppliers, workers, and yourself a fixed salary. Never mix personal and business expenses. Also, register for mobile money services linked to your bank account, so you can transfer funds instantly. For example, in Ghana, you can link your Fidelity Bank account to MTN MoMo. Keep records of every transaction. Banks provide statements that help you apply for loans. After 6 months of regular deposits, you can request a small business loan. A business account also builds credibility with customers who prefer paying by cheque or transfer. Start simple: open a savings account first, then upgrade to a current account as you grow.

6. Registering for Mobile Money Services

Mobile money is the fastest way to handle payments in Africa. To register, visit an agent of a provider like M-Pesa, MTN Mobile Money, Airtel Money, or Orange Money. You need your national ID and a phone number. For a business account, you may also need your business registration certificate. In Kenya, registering for M-Pesa's 'Lipa na M-Pesa' till number is free; you just dial *334# and follow prompts. In Ghana, MTN MoMo business registration costs GHS 10 and requires a Ghana Card. Once registered, you get a merchant code or till number. Customers can pay you directly via their phones. You can also use mobile money to pay suppliers, buy airtime, and save. For brick making, this means you can receive payment for a delivery instantly, even if the buyer is far away. You can pay your workers their daily wages via mobile money, reducing the risk of theft. Set up automatic transfers to your bank account to save for equipment. For example, in Tanzania, a brick maker can receive TZS 500,000 via M-Pesa and transfer TZS 100,000 to a savings account. Keep your PIN secret and never share it. Also, be aware of transaction limits: daily limits may be TZS 1,000,000 for basic accounts. Upgrade to a business account for higher limits. Mobile money statements are accepted as proof of income. Finally, educate your customersâmany are already using mobile money for other purchases. Display your till number prominently at your brick site.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Setting Up Your Money Systems for a Brick Making Business

This lesson is a working reference. It does not explain brick making theory; it tells you exactly what to do, in what order, and what to bring, so that your brick business can receive money from customers, pay suppliers, and build a record that a bank or mobile money provider can trust. Keep it open on your phone or print it and keep it in your site office.

In most African markets, a small brick maker operates in a cash-heavy environment. Buyers â contractors, hardware shops, individual home builders â often prefer to pay cash on delivery. That is fine, but cash alone will limit your growth. Without a bank account and a registered mobile money wallet, you cannot easily receive large transfers, pay for raw materials like cement and sand at scale, qualify for a loan, or prove income when you want to expand. This lesson gives you the practical steps to fix that.

The diagram shows the core money loop. Money comes in from customers â some through mobile money, some through the bank, some in cash that you should deposit. Money goes out to suppliers, workers, and government. Your job is to make that loop traceable. A traceable loop is what turns a small brick yard into a business that banks and mobile money lenders will finance.

Step 1: Register for Mobile Money Services (Do This First)

Mobile money is the fastest financial tool to set up in most African countries. In Kenya, M-Pesa is operated by Safaricom; in Ghana, MTN Mobile Money and Vodafone Cash dominate; in Tanzania, M-Pesa, Tigo Pesa, and Airtel Money are widespread; in Nigeria, OPay, PalmPay, and Paga are common. The process is similar across markets.

What to bring: A national ID card or passport, a SIM card registered in your own name, and a phone that can receive SMS. In some countries you also need a second form of identification or a passport photo.

What to do: Visit an agent or the operator's service centre. Ask specifically for a business wallet or merchant account, not just a personal account. A business wallet usually has higher transaction limits, gives you a till number or paybill, and produces statements that lenders accept. Personal wallets are often capped and are not designed for business records.

Once registered, test it with a small transaction. Confirm that you can receive money, send money, and view a statement. Save the customer care number. Learn how to reverse a wrong transaction and how to dispute a failed one â these will happen.

Step 2: Set Up a Business Bank Account

A bank account does three things a mobile wallet cannot do well: it holds larger balances safely, it produces formal statements that lenders and tax authorities accept, and it allows you to pay by cheque or electronic transfer for big purchases like a brick-making machine or a lorry of sand.

What to bring (typical requirements across African markets):

  • Business registration certificate (from the companies registry or small business authority)
  • Tax identification number (TIN) or equivalent
  • Directors' or owner's national ID and passport photos
  • Proof of business address â a utility bill, tenancy agreement, or a letter from your local authority
  • Business permit or trading licence from the local council
  • Initial deposit (varies; some banks now offer zero-balance SME accounts)

What to ask for: Ask for an SME account or business current account, not a personal savings account. Ask about monthly maintenance fees, transaction charges, and minimum balance. Ask whether the bank has a mobile banking app and whether it links to mobile money. In Kenya, for example, M-Pesa is integrated with many bank accounts; in Ghana, MTN MoMo is linked to several banks. This integration lets you move money between wallet and bank cheaply.

Step 4: Separate Business and Personal Money

This is the single most important habit. If your business money and your personal money share one wallet or one account, you will never know whether the brick business is profitable, and no lender will trust your records. Open a dedicated business wallet and a dedicated business bank account. Pay yourself a fixed "salary" from the business on a set day each week or month. Everything else stays in the business.

â  SAFETY WARNING â Fraud and Mobile Money Risks

Mobile money fraud is common across Africa. Never share your PIN with anyone, including someone claiming to be from the mobile money company or the bank. No legitimate agent will ask for your PIN. Do not accept a transaction reversal from a stranger who claims they "sent money by mistake" â this is a common scam. Register your SIM in your own name; unregistered or borrowed SIMs are a major fraud risk and can be blocked without warning, freezing your business money. Keep your phone locked and your wallet PIN different from your phone unlock code. If you receive a suspicious message, call the official customer care number printed on your SIM pack or the operator's website â not a number sent to you by SMS.

  2. Get a till number or paybill so customers can pay you directly.
  3. Register your business name and get a TIN or tax number.
  4. Open a business bank account with the documents listed in Step 2.
  5. Link your mobile wallet to your bank account if your operator and bank allow it.
  6. Write down your weekly raw material, wage, and fuel costs so you know your cash needs.
  7. Separate business and personal money â pay yourself a fixed salary.
  8. Keep every mobile money message and bank statement; save them monthly for loan applications.
