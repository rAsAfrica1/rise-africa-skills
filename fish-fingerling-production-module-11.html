1. Harvesting Techniques for Fingerlings

Harvesting fingerlings is stressful for the fish, so do it quickly and gently. The best time is early morning when temperatures are cool. Use a seine net with a soft, knotless mesh (1-2 mm) to avoid injuring the fish. In a typical earthen pond of 200 m, two people can pull a 10 m net. Start at one end and pull slowly towards the other, keeping the net's bottom edge on the pond floor. When the net is close to the bank, lift it carefully and transfer fish into a holding tank or basin with pond water. For concrete tanks, you can lower the water level to about 20 cm, then use a scoop net to catch fingerlings. Never chase fish around the pond; it causes stress and mud. If you have many fish, harvest in batches. Sort immediately: any fish with signs of disease or injury should be culled. Work in the shade if possible. Have aeration ready in the holding tanka small air pump or battery aerator works. If you harvest more than 5,000 fingerlings, use two holding tanks to avoid overcrowding. After harvest, return any broodstock or other fish to the pond quickly. Clean the net and dry it to prevent disease transfer. Record the number and weight of fish harvested. Remember, happy fish are healthy fish; rough handling can cause up to 30% mortality within 24 hours. So be gentle, be fast, and keep the fish in water at all times.

2. Grading by Size and Quality

Grading means separating fingerlings into groups of similar size. This is crucial because bigger fish eat more and can outcompete smaller ones, leading to stunted growth. Use a set of graded mesh boxes or a simple grading table with bars spaced at 5 mm, 8 mm, and 12 mm. For tilapia, grade at 2-3 week intervals. For catfish, grade every 10 days. Start by placing all fish in a tank with clean water. Then, using a scoop net, pour fish onto the top screen. Small fish fall through, larger ones stay. Work quickly but gently. You can also grade by hand: pick up fish one by one and place them in separate buckets based on size. This is slower but less stressful for small batches. Quality grading means removing any fish with deformities, cloudy eyes, or damaged fins. These are likely sick and should be culled. Also remove any predators or wild fish that may have entered the pond. After grading, count each group and record the numbers. Then move each group to its own tank or pond. Do not mix sizes again. If you are selling, graded fish fetch a better price because buyers want uniform sizes. In Africa, many farmers grade into three classes: small (under 2 g), medium (2-5 g), and large (over 5 g). Always use clean, dechlorinated water in the grading area. Add a little salt (1-2 g per litre) to reduce stress. Grading takes practice, but it pays off with faster growth and higher survival.

3. Counting and Biomass Estimation

You need to know how many fingerlings you have and their total weight (biomass) to plan feeding, transport, and sales. Counting every fish is slow and stressful, so use sampling. For small numbers (under 500), you can count by hand. For larger numbers, use the volume method: take a small container (e.g., a 100 ml cup), fill it with water, and count the fish in it. Do this 5 times and take the average. Then multiply by the total volume of the tank. For example, if 100 ml holds 20 fish, and your tank holds 100 litres (100,000 ml), then you have 20 x 1000 = 20,000 fish. For biomass, weigh a sample of 50 fish on a kitchen scale (0.1 g precision). Multiply the average weight by the total number. If 50 fish weigh 25 g, average is 0.5 g, and 20,000 fish weigh 10,000 g (10 kg). Always use a representative sample: mix the tank gently before scooping. For catfish, which are uneven, take samples from different parts of the tank. Record your counts in a notebook. This helps you track growth and feed conversion. If you are selling, buyers often ask for number and average size. Accurate counting prevents under- or over-feeding. Overfeeding wastes money and fouls water; underfeeding stunts growth. Weigh your fish every week to monitor. Use a digital scale if possible; they are cheap and accurate. A simple hanging scale with a bucket works too. Remember, 1 litre of water weighs 1 kg, so you can also weigh the container with fish and subtract the container weight. But the sampling method is faster.

4. Preparation for Transport: Conditioning and Fasting

Before transporting fingerlings, you must condition them. This means starving them for 24 to 48 hours. Fasting empties their gut, reducing waste in the transport water. Waste produces ammonia, which is toxic. For tilapia, fast for 24 hours; for catfish, 36-48 hours. During fasting, keep water clean and aerated. Do not feed. On the day of transport, do not feed. Also, lower the water temperature gradually by 2-3C if possible. Cooler water holds more oxygen and slows metabolism. You can add ice in sealed bottles to the tank, but never directly. If you are moving fish in plastic bags, use clean, dechlorinated water. Add a small amount of salt (1 g per litre) to reduce stress. For every litre of water, you can pack 50-100 g of fingerlings for a 2-hour trip; for longer trips, reduce to 30-50 g per litre. Use oxygen gas to fill the bag: one part water, two parts oxygen. Seal the bag tightly with rubber bands. Place bags in insulated boxes or styrofoam coolers. For tank transport, use a tank with aeration and a lid. Fill only half full to allow oxygen exchange. Add a water conditioner like zeolite to absorb ammonia. If you are transporting in a vehicle, secure the tanks to prevent splashing. Avoid direct sunlight. Plan your route to minimize travel time. If you must stop, check fish every hour. If fish gasp at the surface, stop and add oxygen or change part of the water. Conditioning and fasting are not optional; they can mean the difference between 5% and 50% mortality. Always prepare the day before.

5. Transport Methods and Equipment

There are three main methods for transporting fingerlings: plastic bags, tanks, and baskets. For short distances (under 2 hours) and small numbers, plastic bags are best. Use 2-3 litre bags, fill one-third with water, add fish, then fill two-thirds with oxygen. Seal and place in a styrofoam box. You can fit 200-500 fingerlings per bag depending on size. For medium distances (2-6 hours), use 20-50 litre plastic jerrycans or drums. Fill half with water, add fish, and aerate with a battery-powered air pump. Keep the container covered to prevent splashing. For long distances (over 6 hours), use a transport tank (100-500 litres) with a built-in aeration system. You can also use a pickup truck with a tank and a small generator for aeration. Always use clean, dechlorinated water. Add 1 g of salt per litre. If you are using bags, do not stack more than three high. For tanks, secure them with ropes. In Africa, many farmers use baskets lined with plastic bags, but this is risky because oxygen depletes quickly. If using baskets, keep them shaded and change water every hour. Equipment you need: oxygen cylinder (for bags), battery aerator, thermometer, net, buckets, and a notebook. Test your equipment before loading. Never overload: a good rule is 1 kg of fish per 10 litres of water for a 6-hour trip. For every extra hour, reduce by 20%. If you are selling, transport early morning or late evening. Avoid midday heat. If you are moving fish between ponds on the same farm, you can use a wheelbarrow with a lined basin, but keep the distance short. Always have a backup plan: extra bags, oxygen, and water.

6. Acclimation and Release at Destination

When you arrive at your destination, do not dump the fish straight into the new pond. The water temperature and chemistry may be different, and sudden changes can kill fish. First, float the sealed bags in the pond for 15-20 minutes. This equalizes temperature. For tanks, slowly add pond water to the tank over 10-15 minutes until the tank is half pond water. Then, gently net the fish and release them into the pond. Do not pour the transport water into the pond; it may contain waste and diseases. If you are releasing into a tank, use a similar method. After release, monitor the fish for 30 minutes. Some may be stressed and swim erratically. That is normal. Do not feed for 24 hours after release. Let them settle. If you see many fish gasping at the surface, check water quality. Acclimation is especially important for catfish, which are sensitive to temperature changes. For tilapia, a difference of more than 3C can cause shock. So always take your time. If you are releasing into a pond with predators, release at multiple points to avoid crowding. Also, release in the morning or evening when temperatures are cool. After release, record the number of fish, date, and pond. Check for dead fish the next day and remove them. If mortality is high, review your transport and acclimation steps. With proper acclimation, you can expect over 95% survival. Remember, the goal is to get healthy fingerlings growing quickly. A little patience at release saves a lot of loss.

<h2>Practical Reference: Harvesting, Grading, and Transport of Fish Fingerlings</h2>

<p>For African hatcheries and grow-out farms, the harvest-to-stocking pipeline is where margin is won or lost. Fingerlings that are handled roughly, graded poorly, or transported without conditioning can suffer 1040% mortality within 72 hours of release a direct hit to revenue and reputation. This practical reference distils the operational standards used by commercial tilapia and catfish hatcheries in Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya, Uganda, and Zambia into a single working document. Treat it as your standard operating procedure (SOP) reference for the six lessons in this module.</p>

<h3>1. Harvesting Techniques for Fingerlings</h3>
<p>Harvest when water temperature is coolest early morning (05:0008:00) or late evening. Warm water holds less oxygen and raises metabolic stress. Use a soft-mesh seine net (24 mm mesh) with a knotless weave to reduce scale loss and abrasion. For pond harvests, draw down 3050% of the water first to concentrate fish, then seine slowly. Never chase fish; guide them. For tank and hapa systems, use a dip net and transfer fish directly into a holding container of the same water. Stop feeding 2448 hours before harvest (see Section 4).</p>

<h3>2. Grading by Size and Quality</h3>
<p>Uniform size at stocking reduces cannibalism (critical in catfish) and competition for feed. Grade into at least three classes using bar graders or adjustable box graders. Cull any fingerling with deformities, visible disease, or abnormal swimming behaviour these are a biosecurity and reputational risk. Grade quickly (under 10 minutes per batch) and keep fish in well-aerated water throughout.</p>

<h3>3. Counting and Biomass Estimation</h3>
<p>Accurate counting protects both buyer and seller. The volumetric method is fastest: count a representative sample (e.g. 100 fingerlings), weigh it, then weigh the full batch and extrapolate. For high-value consignments, use the batch-weight method with a calibrated scale (accuracy 1 g). Always record average body weight (ABW) and total biomass in your delivery note this is your commercial document.</p>

<h3>4. Preparation for Transport: Conditioning and Fasting</h3>
<p>Conditioning is the single highest-ROI step in the entire pipeline. Fast fingerlings for 2448 hours (catfish: 24 h; tilapia: 2436 h) before transport. Fasting empties the gut, reduces ammonia excretion, and lowers oxygen demand by up to 30%. During conditioning, hold fish in clean, well-aerated water at the transport temperature. Do not feed, but monitor dissolved oxygen (DO) twice daily keep above 5 mg/L. For catfish, a 24-hour fast is standard; for tilapia, 2436 hours is optimal. Never fast longer than 72 hours it weakens the fish and reduces post-stocking survival.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #b00020; background:#fdecec; padding:12px 16px; margin:18px 0; font-family:Arial;">
<strong style="color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING Ammonia and Oxygen Risk</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#333;">Never transport fingerlings in water that has not been tested for ammonia, nitrite, and dissolved oxygen. Ammonia above 0.05 mg/L and DO below 4 mg/L during transport can cause mass mortality within 24 hours. Always carry a backup oxygen cylinder and a spare battery aerator. In warm African conditions (2832 C), oxygen demand rises sharply reduce transport density by 2530% compared to temperate-zone guidelines. Never seal bags without checking oxygen fill; a collapsed bag is a dead consignment.</p>
</div>

<h3>5. Transport Methods and Equipment</h3>
<p>Two dominant methods are used across Africa: (a) sealed oxygen bags (polythene bags, 1/3 water, 2/3 oxygen) for air freight and short road trips; and (b) open tanks with mechanical aeration for road transport over 412 hours. For road transport, use insulated tanks (polystyrene or foam-lined) and keep the vehicle shaded. For air transport, use double-bagged, oxygen-filled bags packed in insulated boxes. Density guidelines are species- and temperature-dependent always test with a small batch first.</p>

<h3>6. Acclimation and Release at Destination</h3>
<p>Acclimation is non-negotiable. Float the sealed bags or containers on the destination pond/tank surface for 1530 minutes to equalise temperature. Then gradually exchange water: add small volumes of pond water to the bag every 5 minutes over 1520 minutes until the bag is mostly pond water. Release fish gently into the pond, avoiding splashing. Do not release transport water into the pond it carries ammonia and potential pathogens. Record survival at 24 h, 72 h, and 7 days. This data is your commercial proof of quality and your feedback loop to improve the next batch.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li>Harvest during cool hours (05:0008:00) with soft-mesh nets.</li>
<li>Grade into at least 3 size classes; cull deformed or diseased fish.</li>
<li>Count using batch-weight or sub-sample method; record ABW and biomass.</li>
<li>Fast fish 2448 hours before transport; never exceed 72 hours.</li>
<li>Test water for ammonia (&lt;0.05 mg/L) and DO (&gt;5 mg/L) before packing.</li>
<li>Use oxygen bags or aerated insulated tanks; reduce density in warm conditions.</li>
<li>Acclimate 1530 minutes; exchange water gradually; never release transport water.</li>
<li>Record survival at 24 h, 72 h, and 7 days; feed back to hatchery management.</li>
</ul>
