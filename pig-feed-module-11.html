Lesson 11.1~12 min

Where the Premium Actually Comes From: Eating Quality, Consistency and Story

In this lesson
  • Identify the three separate sources of price premium in specialty pork markets
  • Evaluate whether your farm can deliver consistency before chasing a premium
  • Calculate the revenue effect of a premium on your existing volume

Producers often assume a premium comes from doing something unusual. In practice buyers pay more for three distinct things, and only one of them is unusual.

The first is eating quality. That means fat that is firm rather than oily, meat with enough intramuscular fat to stay juicy, colour that is not pale and watery, and consistent tenderness. Fat quality is directly controlled by what you feed in the last six weeks, which lesson 3 covers in detail. Pale, soft, exudative pork is a well-documented quality defect, and diets heavy in unsaturated fat sources such as high inclusion of distillers grains or high-linoleic oils produce soft pork fat that degrades bacon quality. This part of the premium is earned in the feed store.

The second is consistency. A hotel chef who puts pork belly on the menu needs the same belly every week. A butcher building a customer following needs the same carcass weight and the same fat cover. Consistency is worth more than excellence to almost every commercial buyer, and it is where small producers most often fail. If you deliver a superb pig in March and nothing in April, you will not hold the account. Consistency comes from batch planning, which Module 12 covers, not from genetics or feed.

The third is story: breed, welfare system, feeding system, locality, antibiotic status. Story raises the ceiling on what a buyer can charge their own customer, which is what lets them pay you more. But story only works when the first two are already in place. A story attached to inconsistent, poor-quality pork is just a complaint waiting to happen.

Understand also that the premium is claimed by whoever can prove it. A verbal assurance that your pigs run outside is worth nothing. Photographs, dated records, an open-farm visit and a written specification are what convert a story into money.

Worked example. A producer in Lusaka, Zambia, sells 150 pigs a year at about 85 kg liveweight, roughly 61 kg of carcass at a 72 percent dressing yield, which is an approximate figure that varies with breed and condition. At a commodity price of 3.00 USD per kg carcass, that is 61 x 3.00 = 183 USD per pig and 27,450 USD a year. Two boutique butcheries offer 3.60 USD per kg carcass for pigs finished outdoors on a documented ration, but only want 3 pigs a week, which is 156 a year, just above his output. At 3.60 per kg he earns 61 x 3.60 = 219.60 per pig, a gain of 36.60 per pig. Across 150 pigs that is 5,490 USD more revenue on the same number of animals.

But he must also count the cost. Outdoor finishing means fencing, shade and higher feed conversion because pigs move more and lose heat. Suppose feed conversion worsens by 8 percent. If he uses roughly 230 kg of feed per pig at 0.45 USD, that is 103.50, and 8 percent more is 8.28 USD per pig. Fencing 2 hectares might cost 2,000 USD, depreciated over 8 years at 250 USD a year, which is 1.67 per pig. Documentation and delivery costs, say 3.00 per pig. Total added cost about 12.95 per pig against 36.60 of extra revenue. Net gain 23.65 per pig, or 3,547 USD a year. That is a real and defensible business decision. Note that the fencing and feed conversion figures here are illustrative estimates, not published standards; you must generate your own.

Before chasing any premium, apply three tests:

  1. Can I deliver the same specification every week for twelve months? If not, fix that first.
  2. Can I prove the claim with records a stranger would believe?
  3. Does the extra revenue exceed the extra cost with a margin big enough to survive a bad month?

If the answer to any of these is no, the correct decision is to keep selling commodity pork and improve your production efficiency, which raises profit without any market risk at all. Premium markets are a genuine opportunity, but they are the second step, not the first.

One last observation from producers who have made this work. The buyers who pay the most are usually the smallest and the most demanding. A single restaurant may pay a very high price for six kilograms a week and expect a phone call if you are late. Building a premium business often means managing five small accounts rather than one big one, and that is a different kind of work from farming.

Local and Heritage Breeds: Matching Genetics to Low-Input Systems

In this lesson
  • Compare the nutritional and management demands of improved versus local pig genetics
  • Select a crossbreeding strategy suited to your feed supply and market
  • Adjust dietary lysine specification to the lean growth potential of your pigs

There is a persistent belief that improved exotic genetics are always better. They are better under one condition: that you can feed them properly. A high lean-gain pig fed a low-specification diet grows no faster than a local pig and costs far more to buy, house and keep healthy. Matching genetics to your actual feed supply is one of the highest-value decisions on a small farm.

The technical basis is lean growth potential, and it drives the protein to energy ratio you must feed. The published grouping uses three categories: high lean gain above 0.80 lb per day, medium at 0.65 to 0.80, and low below 0.65. For a high lean-gain pig the early finisher protein to energy ratio should be 3.4 to 3.6 g SID lysine per Mcal of metabolisable energy, falling to 3.1 to 3.3 in mid finisher and 2.8 to 3.0 in late finisher. For a medium pig it is 3.2 to 3.4, then 2.9 to 3.1, then 2.6 to 2.8. For a low lean-gain pig it is 3.0 to 3.2, then 2.7 to 2.9, then 2.4 to 2.6.

Read that table backwards and the economics become clear. A low lean-gain pig needs meaningfully less lysine per unit of energy, which means less soybean meal, fishmeal or groundnut cake in the ration. Those are the most expensive components of almost any African pig diet. If your feed supply is cereal-heavy and protein-poor, low or medium lean-gain genetics will out-earn high lean-gain genetics because they will not waste the expensive amino acids you cannot afford to buy.

Local and indigenous pig populations across Africa typically show slower growth and fatter carcasses than modern Large White, Landrace or Pietrain crosses, but greater hardiness, better tolerance of fibrous and variable feed, better heat tolerance and stronger mothering under low-input conditions. Modern Pietrain and Large White crosses target the leanest viable growth and are correspondingly demanding. Note that specific breed performance figures vary so widely across systems that you should measure your own rather than trusting a published table from another country.

The usual practical answer is a crossbreeding programme rather than a purebred choice. A common and workable structure is to keep hardy local or local-cross sows, which survive the heat, tolerate variable feed and raise litters with less intervention, and mate them to an improved terminal boar to lift the growth rate and leanness of the market pigs. This gives you maternal hardiness and terminal performance at the same time. The boar is a single animal to feed well, which is far more affordable than feeding an entire herd of demanding sows correctly. Remember from Module 9 that a boar needs 0.62 percent SID lysine, about 13 to 14 percent crude protein, notably higher sulphur amino acid ratios at 70 percent methionine plus cysteine of lysine and 74 percent threonine, plus 0.30 to 0.50 mg selenium per kg and 44 IU vitamin E per kg. Underfeeding a boar has documented negative consequences for sperm production.

Worked example. A producer in the Northern Region of Ghana keeps 10 local-type sows and cannot reliably buy soybean meal. His grower diet realistically reaches about 0.90 percent SID lysine at 3,250 kcal ME per kg, giving a protein to energy ratio of 0.90 divided by 3.25, which is 0.277 percent per Mcal, or expressed in the usual units 2.77 g lysine per Mcal. That sits in the low lean-gain band for a mid finisher. Buying high lean-gain genetics that need 3.1 to 3.3 would mean paying for growth potential he cannot feed. He instead buys one improved terminal boar for roughly 400 USD, feeds that single animal a properly fortified 2.5 kg a day, and lifts the growth of every market pig he sells while his sow herd stays cheap to run. If the boar serves 10 sows producing 180 market pigs a year and adds even 3 kg of extra market weight per pig, at 3.00 USD per kg carcass equivalent that is 180 x 3 x 3.00 = 1,620 USD a year from a 400 USD animal.

For the premium market specifically, slower-growing and fatter local breeds are sometimes an advantage rather than a defect. Intramuscular fat is what chefs pay for. A carcass that a commodity abattoir would penalise for backfat may be exactly what a charcuterie buyer wants. Modern market targets sit at roughly 0.60 to 0.75 inches, or 15 to 19 mm, of tenth rib backfat for gilts and barrows, so anything fatter is discounted on the commodity grid. That discount is precisely the opportunity: buy the pig the commodity market does not want, and sell it to the market that does.

Feeding for Fat Quality: Controlling Softness in the Last Six Weeks

In this lesson
  • Explain how dietary fatty acids determine carcass fat firmness
  • Design a final-phase diet change that hardens fat before slaughter
  • Use the lysine to energy ratio deliberately to set backfat and lean deposition

Fat quality is the single most controllable determinant of premium pork value, and it is decided almost entirely in the last four to six weeks before slaughter. The rule is simple: pigs deposit the fat they eat. Feed unsaturated fat and you get soft, oily body fat that will not hold a slice, greases up during curing, goes rancid quickly and produces poor bacon. Feed saturated fat and the carcass fat is firm and white.

The main culprits in an African ration are high inclusion rates of distillers grains, which are documented as problematic above about 15 percent, and high-linoleic oils such as some canola, sunflower and cottonseed oil residues. Full-fat soybean and rice bran are also high in unsaturated fat and are common cheap energy sources. None of these are bad ingredients. They simply need to come out or come down in the final phase.

The practical management rule is to limit unsaturated fat sources in the final four to six weeks and shift the fat contribution towards saturated sources such as tallow or palm oil, or simply to reduce added fat and let cereal starch supply the energy. Palm oil is widely available across West and Central Africa and is a genuinely useful tool here.

The second lever is the lysine to energy ratio, and this one is deliberate carcass engineering. A higher SID lysine percentage relative to energy pushes the pig towards lean deposition and reduces backfat. A lower SID lysine percentage relative to energy pushes it towards fat deposition and thicker backfat. This means you can aim your carcass at your market. For a commodity grid that wants 15 to 19 mm of tenth rib backfat, keep lysine up. For a charcuterie or traditional cured product buyer who wants a fatter carcass with more intramuscular fat, deliberately reduce lysine relative to energy in the final phase and let the pig lay down fat.

Use the published phase table as your starting frame. Late finisher at 100 to 120 kg is 0.70 percent SID lysine at 3,150 kcal ME, and the final 120 to 130 kg phase is 0.65 percent at 3,100 kcal ME. Those are already reduced-lysine, fat-permissive settings. Cutting further should be done knowingly, and you should check the carcass result before repeating it.

Remember also that adding 1 percent of fat improves feed conversion by about 0.04 points. So removing fat in the final phase costs you a little efficiency, and you should price that against the carcass value you gain.

Worked example. A producer in Kisumu, Kenya, sells to a butchery that makes traditional cured pork belly and has complained that his bellies are floppy and hard to slice. His finisher diet includes 20 percent rice bran, which is high in unsaturated fat, and 3 percent sunflower oil. He makes one change for the last five weeks: rice bran drops from 20 to 8 percent, sunflower oil is removed, and 2 percent palm oil replaces part of the energy, with maize meal filling the rest. Suppose maize costs 0.35 USD per kg, rice bran 0.20 and palm oil 1.40. For a tonne of feed he removes 120 kg of rice bran at 0.20, saving 24.00 USD, removes 30 kg of sunflower oil at say 1.60, saving 48.00, adds 20 kg of palm oil at 1.40, costing 28.00, and adds 130 kg of maize at 0.35, costing 45.50. Net change is 28.00 + 45.50 minus 24.00 minus 48.00 = plus 1.50 USD per tonne. The diet is essentially cost-neutral. If each pig eats about 60 kg in that final five weeks, the change costs him 0.09 USD per pig and fixes the complaint that was threatening a 0.60 USD per kg premium on a 61 kg carcass, worth 36.60 per pig. All ingredient prices here are illustrative; use your own.

Two practical cautions. First, make the change gradually over about a week so intake does not dip. Second, do not attempt to fix fat quality in the last ten days. Body fat composition changes over weeks, not days, so a late panic change achieves nothing. Plan the final-phase diet into your batch schedule from the start.

Finally, keep records of what each batch was fed and ask your buyer for feedback on each batch. Fat quality is invisible on the hoof. The only way to learn is to link a written ration to a written carcass comment, batch after batch, until you know exactly which ration produces the product your buyer wants.

Pasture, Forage and Free-Range Systems That Still Balance Nutritionally

In this lesson
  • Estimate the realistic nutritional contribution of forage to a pig's diet by class
  • Formulate a supplement that complements rather than duplicates pasture intake
  • Manage the parasite, mineral and mycotoxin risks specific to outdoor pigs

Outdoor and pasture-based pork sells at a premium in most African cities, and the system genuinely reduces housing capital. What it does not do is feed the pigs. The most common and expensive mistake in free-range pig production is assuming forage replaces a meaningful share of the concentrate ration for growing pigs.

The pig is a monogastric with a simple stomach. It cannot ferment fibre the way a cow does. Published fibre guidance reflects this: young pigs under 15 kg have low apparent total tract digestibility of fibre because of immature enzyme systems, while pigs over 60 kg digest fibre far better, approaching adult ruminant efficiency for some sources. That is the key to the whole system. Forage contributes very little to a weaner and quite a lot to a mature sow.

So apply forage by class. Gestating sows tolerate 6 to 8 percent crude fibre and benefit from the satiety, which makes them the best candidates for pasture and forage. Grower and finisher diets tolerate 4 to 6 percent crude fibre, so pasture can make a real but partial contribution once pigs are over about 60 kg. Weaners should be held to 2 to 4 percent crude fibre maximum and should not be expected to get nutrition from pasture at all. Lactating sows need 3 to 4 percent crude fibre only, because bulk displaces the energy she desperately needs, so a lactating sow on pasture still needs full ad libitum concentrate.

What forage does supply reliably: some energy and protein for older pigs, carotene contributing to vitamin A status, occupation that reduces aggression and tail biting, and iron from soil, which matters enormously for suckling piglets. Outdoor piglets rooting in soil are far less likely to become anaemic than piglets on concrete, though you should still verify this rather than assume it.

Risks specific to outdoor systems need active management. Internal and external parasites increase with soil contact and need a worming programme worked out with a veterinarian. Sunburn is real, especially on white-skinned improved breeds, so shade and wallows are not optional. Heat stress reduces feed intake sharply, and lactating sows lose 15 to 30 percent of intake above about 29 degrees Celsius. Mycotoxin exposure can rise, because fallen and rain-wetted grain, spilled feed and mouldy pasture residues are all sources, and the limits from Module 9 apply just as strictly outdoors. Feeders should be covered and any spilled feed cleared daily.

A supplement for outdoor pigs must complement, not duplicate. The nutrients pasture cannot supply reliably are the ones your supplement must guarantee: amino acids, particularly lysine, methionine and threonine at the ratios for the class, calcium and phosphorus at the published levels, salt, and the full trace mineral and vitamin premix. Selenium and vitamin E matter especially because outdoor pigs face more oxidative and thermal stress, so hold selenium at 0.15 to 0.30 mg per kg for growers and 0.30 to 0.50 for boars, with vitamin E at 44 IU per kg or above for breeding stock and stressed pigs.

Worked example. A producer outside Harare, Zimbabwe, finishes 40 pigs on 1.5 hectares of mixed pasture with a supplement fed twice daily. He assumed forage would cut his concentrate use by 30 percent and budgeted accordingly. His pigs reached market weight three weeks late. He then measured properly: he weighed the supplement fed, weighed pigs fortnightly, and found actual concentrate saving was closer to 10 percent for pigs over 60 kg and effectively zero below that. Recalculating, 40 pigs at 230 kg of feed each is 9,200 kg at 0.45 USD, which is 4,140 USD. A 10 percent saving is 414 USD, not the 1,242 USD he had budgeted. Meanwhile three extra weeks on farm cost him housing, labour and cash flow. The honest conclusion was that pasture paid through the premium price and the lower building cost, not through the feed bill. That is the correct way to justify an outdoor system, and it is a perfectly good justification.

Measure your own forage contribution the same way: weigh what you feed, weigh the pigs, and compare against an indoor group if you can. Never budget a forage saving you have not measured on your own land.

Lesson 11.5~12 min

Adding Value On Farm: Curing, Smoking and Small-Batch Pork Products

In this lesson
  • Identify which value-added pork products are realistic without refrigerated capital
  • Calculate the value uplift from processing a carcass into products
  • Apply the food safety and record disciplines that value addition demands

The largest margin in the pork chain usually sits between the farm gate and the retail counter. Capturing part of it is attractive, but value addition is a different business from pig production and it fails for producers who underestimate it. This lesson is about doing it carefully and at small scale.

Start with what is realistic without heavy capital. Fresh cuts sold directly, meaning breaking a carcass into pork chops, belly, shoulder and leg and selling to households or restaurants, requires only a clean room, a good knife set, a scale and reliable same-day sale or ice. Smoked and cured products such as bacon, cured belly and smoked leg require salt, curing salt, a smoker that can be built from a drum, and time. Sausages require a mincer and a filler, which is more capital but still modest. Fully dry-cured hams require months of temperature and humidity control and are usually beyond a first venture.

The fat quality lesson matters more here than anywhere. Curing and smoking concentrate the fat characteristics of the carcass. Soft, oily fat from a diet high in unsaturated fat sources will not cure well, will not slice cleanly and will go rancid faster. If you intend to cure, the final four to six week diet change is not optional.

Worked example. A producer near Blantyre, Malawi, sells whole live pigs at about 85 kg liveweight for roughly 3.00 USD per kg carcass on a 61 kg carcass, so 183 USD per pig. She trials processing two pigs a week herself. Say she gets from each 61 kg carcass roughly 45 kg of saleable product after bone and trim losses, which is an illustrative assumption she needs to verify by actually weighing. Suppose she sells 15 kg as fresh chops and cuts at 5.00 USD per kg, 10 kg as cured belly and bacon at 8.00, 12 kg as sausage at 4.50, and 8 kg as lower-value cuts and offal at 2.50. That is 75.00 + 80.00 + 54.00 + 20.00 = 229.00 USD per carcass against 183.00 sold whole, a gross uplift of 46.00 per pig.

Now subtract the costs she must count honestly: slaughter and inspection fee, salt and curing ingredients, casings, firewood or charcoal for smoking, packaging, ice or cold storage, transport to customers, and her own labour, which is substantial. If those total 30.00 per pig, her net uplift is 16.00 per pig, or 32.00 a week on two pigs. That is real money, but it is earned through many hours of skilled work, and she must decide whether those hours are better spent on the pigs. All prices above are illustrative and must be replaced with your own market figures.

Food safety is not optional and is where small processors get shut down. The basic disciplines are: slaughter through a licensed facility where one exists and where the law requires it; keep raw meat cold, ideally at or below 4 degrees Celsius, and never break the cold chain; separate raw and finished product with different boards, knives and surfaces; use nitrite curing salts only at the label rate, because these are genuinely dangerous if over-dosed and must never be guessed; keep a written production record for every batch with date, pig identity, salt and cure quantities, temperatures and where the product went; and label finished product with the production date. That production record is also what lets you trace a problem back to a specific pig, which is exactly the traceability your premium buyers in Module 10 wanted.

Check your national and local regulations before you sell any processed meat. Requirements for licensed premises, meat inspection, labelling and business registration vary widely by country and even by municipality, and the penalty for guessing is losing the business.

One strategic point. Value addition suits producers who already have consistent supply and a customer base, which is why it appears in this module after the market lessons rather than before them. If you cannot yet deliver the same pig every week, adding a processing business on top will multiply your problems rather than your margin. Start with one product, do it very well, sell it to people who already buy from you, and expand only when you run out of that product every week.

Lesson 11.6~12 min

Finding and Keeping Premium Buyers: Chefs, Butchers and Direct Sales

In this lesson
  • Build a prospect list of premium buyers and approach them with a structured offer
  • Set a supply commitment you can actually meet every week of the year
  • Design a simple customer retention routine that protects a premium account

Finding one good premium buyer changes a small pig business more than any feed additive. Keeping that buyer is harder than finding them, and it is almost entirely about reliability rather than charm.

Build the prospect list first. In most African cities the realistic premium channels are: independent butcheries serving higher-income neighbourhoods; hotels and lodges, especially those serving international guests; restaurants with a defined cuisine, particularly those making their own charcuterie or serving a set menu; delicatessens and speciality grocers; supermarket chains with a local sourcing programme; and direct-to-consumer sales through a WhatsApp group, a weekly delivery round or a farm shop. Write down twenty names before you approach anyone. Most will say no, and that is normal.

Prepare before the first visit. Take four things: a one-page written description of your product with weight, specification, and how it is produced; a photograph of your farm and pigs; a sample if the product is fresh and you can keep it cold; and a clear statement of how many pigs or kilograms you can supply per week and from which date. That last item is what most producers cannot answer, and it is the one buyers care about most.

Be careful about the volume you commit to. Under-promise. If you can produce eight pigs a month and you commit to eight, one bad farrowing will break your contract. Commit to six and offer the other two when you have them. A supplier who always delivers what they promised and sometimes offers extra builds a reputation. A supplier who often falls short does not get a second contract.

Price the conversation properly. Ask what they currently pay and what they currently dislike about their supply. Very often the answer is not price at all but inconsistency, late delivery, variable carcass size or poor fat quality. If you can fix those you can charge more without ever arguing about price. State your premium on a clear basis, liveweight or carcass, and get the agreement in writing with product specification, price per kilogram, volume and delivery day.

Worked example. A producer near Kampala, Uganda, approached fourteen outlets over six weeks. Eleven said no. Two lodges and one butchery said yes, together taking five pigs a week at 3.50 USD per kg carcass against her previous 2.90 to a trader, on carcasses averaging 60 kg. Her extra revenue is 60 x 0.60 = 36.00 per pig, and 5 pigs a week is 260 a year, so 9,360 USD of additional annual revenue. Her added costs were a second-hand chest freezer, delivery fuel three days a week, and better record keeping. Suppose those total 3,200 USD in year one. Net gain about 6,160 USD. The eleven refusals were the price of the three acceptances, and that ratio is normal.

Retention is a routine, not a feeling. Four habits protect a premium account:

  1. Deliver on the agreed day at the agreed time, every time. If you will be late, telephone before you are late, not after.
  2. Ask for feedback on every delivery and write the answer down. Ask specifically about fat firmness, carcass size consistency and trim.
  3. Give notice of any change. If your batch is a week behind, tell them three weeks before, not the day before.
  4. Visit in person at least once a quarter without trying to sell anything.

And protect yourself commercially. Never let one buyer take more than a large share of your output without a written agreement, because a single buyer who leaves can end your business. Aim for three or four accounts. Agree payment terms in writing and enforce them politely but firmly from the first invoice, because payment discipline is set in the first month of a relationship and is very hard to change later.

Finally, keep a simple sales record: date, buyer, number of pigs, weight, price, and any complaint. Twelve months of that record is what lets you see which buyer is actually most profitable once you have counted delivery cost and payment delay. Producers are frequently surprised to find that their highest-priced buyer is not their most profitable one.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. Which source of premium do commercial buyers usually value most?

A chef or butcher building a product line needs the same carcass every week. Consistency is worth more than occasional excellence.

2. Which feeding practice most directly causes soft pork fat?

Unsaturated dietary fat is deposited as unsaturated body fat, producing soft fat and degrading bacon quality. Managing this is a feed-store decision.

3. A 61 kg carcass sold at a $0.60 per kg premium earns how much extra per pig?

61 multiplied by 0.60 gives 36.60 USD of extra revenue per pig, before subtracting the added cost of meeting the premium specification.

4. What should a producer do if they cannot yet deliver the same specification every week?

Inconsistent supply loses premium accounts and damages reputation. Improving production efficiency raises profit with no market risk while consistency is built.

5. What turns a production story into an actual price premium?

The premium is claimed by whoever can prove it. Evidence lets the buyer make the claim to their own customer with confidence.

6. Why can low lean-gain genetics be more profitable where protein meals are expensive?

Low lean-gain pigs need 3.0-3.2 g SID lysine per Mcal in early finisher versus 3.4-3.6 for high lean-gain pigs, reducing the most expensive part of the ration.

7. What is the usual practical crossbreeding structure for a low-input African unit?

This captures maternal hardiness and heat tolerance in the sow herd while lifting market pig growth through one well-fed terminal boar.

8. What SID lysine level does a mature breeding boar require?

The published figure is 0.62 percent SID lysine, with methionine plus cysteine at 70 percent and threonine at 74 percent of lysine, higher ratios than for sows.

9. What is the modern commodity market backfat target at the tenth rib?

Roughly 0.60-0.75 inches or 15-19 mm for gilt and barrow carcasses. Fatter carcasses attract a commodity discount, which can create a specialty opportunity.

10. A grower diet delivers 0.90 percent SID lysine at 3,250 kcal ME per kg. Which lean-gain band does this support in mid finisher?

0.90 divided by 3.25 Mcal gives about 2.77 g lysine per Mcal, which falls within the low lean-gain mid-finisher band of 2.7-2.9.

11. How long before slaughter must a fat quality diet change be made to have an effect?

Body fat composition shifts over weeks. The published management guidance is to limit unsaturated fat sources in the final four to six weeks.

12. To reduce backfat and increase lean deposition, how should the lysine to energy ratio be set?

Higher SID lysine relative to energy drives lean deposition and reduces backfat. Lowering it does the opposite, which is useful when a buyer wants a fatter carcass.

13. Which fat source would you add in the final phase to harden carcass fat?

Saturated fat sources such as tallow and palm oil produce firm white carcass fat. The others listed are high in unsaturated fatty acids.

14. Adding 1 percent fat to a diet changes feed conversion by roughly how much?

Each 1 percent of added fat improves feed conversion by about 0.04 points, which is the efficiency cost of removing fat for quality reasons.

15. Why should ration records be linked to buyer feedback on each batch?

Carcass fat quality is invisible before slaughter. Matching each written ration to the buyer's comment on that batch is how you learn which ration delivers the product.

16. Which class of pig gains the most nutritional value from pasture and forage?

Gestating sows tolerate 6-8 percent crude fibre and benefit from the satiety bulk provides while being restricted-fed.

17. Why do weaners get almost no nutrition from pasture?

Apparent total tract digestibility of fibre is low in young pigs because of immature enzyme systems. Weaner diets should stay at 2-4 percent crude fibre.

18. What is a genuine and reliable nutritional benefit of outdoor rearing for suckling piglets?

Piglets on concrete cannot obtain iron and are prone to anaemia. Rooting in soil supplies incidental iron, although the status should still be verified.

19. A producer budgeted a 30 percent concentrate saving from pasture but measured 10 percent for pigs over 60 kg. What is the correct conclusion?

Forage contribution to a monogastric is limited. The outdoor system is justified by market premium and reduced capital, which is a sound justification on its own.

20. Which mycotoxin risk is specific to outdoor systems?

Spilled feed that gets wet, fallen grain and mouldy residues all support mould growth. Covered feeders and daily clearing of spillage are basic outdoor hygiene.

21. Why does fat quality matter more for cured products than for fresh pork?

Soft, oily fat from an unsaturated-heavy diet performs badly through curing. The final four to six week diet change becomes essential if you intend to cure.

22. Which value-added product requires the least capital for a first venture?

Breaking a carcass into fresh cuts needs only a clean room, knives, a scale and reliable same-day sale or ice. Dry-cured ham needs months of controlled temperature and humidity.

23. What must never be estimated when curing pork?

Nitrite curing salts are dangerous if over-dosed. They must be weighed to the label rate every time, with the quantity written into the batch record.

24. A carcass sold whole earns $183 and processed earns $229 gross, with $30 of processing costs. What is the net uplift?

229 minus 183 is 46 gross, and subtracting 30 of processing cost leaves 16 USD net per pig, before valuing the producer's own labour.

25. What should a producer establish before selling any processed meat product?

Requirements vary widely by country and municipality, and operating without the right licence or inspection can end the business entirely.

26. What do premium buyers most often complain about with their existing supplier?

Buyers usually raise reliability and consistency rather than price. Fixing those lets you charge more without a price argument.

27. You can produce eight pigs a month. What volume should you commit to?

Committing below capacity means one bad farrowing does not break the contract, and delivering extra builds a reputation for reliability.

28. Why should a producer avoid depending on a single premium buyer?

Buyer concentration is a serious commercial risk. Three or four accounts protect the business if one relationship ends.

29. When should you tell a buyer that a batch will be late?

Early notice lets the buyer plan their menu or counter. Late notice damages trust more than the shortfall itself.

30. What does a twelve-month sales record most usefully reveal?

The highest-priced buyer is often not the most profitable once delivery costs and slow payment are accounted for. Only the record shows this.

Module 11 capstone

Build a Premium Pork Market Case for your own farm. Step 1: visit or telephone at least five potential premium outlets in your nearest city, such as a butchery, a hotel, a restaurant, a supermarket and a delicatessen, and record for each what pork products they buy, what they pay per kilogram, what volume they need per week, and what would make them pay more. Step 2: buy one kilogram of the highest-priced pork product you can find locally, weigh it, note its price per kilogram, and photograph its packaging and any claims it makes. Step 3: assess your own herd against that product: your breed or crossbreed, your finishing weight, your backfat, your feeding system and your story. Step 4: pick one specific premium product you could realistically deliver, whether that is pasture-raised pork, a local breed, a documented antibiotic-free line, or a cured product, and write out the production changes it needs. Step 5: calculate the cost of those changes per pig and the additional revenue per pig at the price your interviews found, and state your net gain or loss per pig. Step 6: draft a one-page offer document for the single most promising buyer, including product description, volume you can commit to each month, price, and the evidence you can provide. Deliver it to them and record their response.
