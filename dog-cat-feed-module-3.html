🐕🐈 Adult Dog Nutrition

Maintenance feeding for the working majority of your market: how to calculate what an adult dog actually needs, adjust for activity and body condition, and build a diet that holds its nutrient specification at a price local owners will pay.

  • Calculate maintenance energy by both the resting energy times factor route and the direct allometric route

  • Formulate a reduced-energy maintenance diet that still meets every adult minimum
  • Separate documented breed-linked nutritional issues from breed marketing
  • Use a Pearson's square to hit a protein target with two ingredients and then cost the result

Lesson 3.1~12 min

Maintenance Energy Requirement Calculations

In this lesson
  • Calculate maintenance energy by both the resting energy times factor route and the direct allometric route
  • Explain why the two systems disagree and choose one consistently for your feeding table
  • Turn an energy requirement into grams per day and a monthly cost at your own prices

Everything downstream of this calculation depends on getting it right: how much the owner feeds, how long a bag lasts, what your product costs to use, and whether the dog holds condition. Adult maintenance is also the biggest single segment of any pet food market, so this is the arithmetic you will use most.

The first route is resting energy multiplied by a life stage factor. Resting energy is 70 multiplied by body weight in kilograms raised to the power 0.75, and it is valid at any weight. Between 2 and 45 kilograms a linear approximation of 30 multiplied by body weight plus 70 is close enough for a feeding guide. The adult factors are 1.8 for an intact adult, 1.6 for a neutered adult, 1.2 to 1.4 for an inactive or obesity-prone dog, 1.0 for weight loss and 1.2 to 1.8 for weight gain.

The second route skips resting energy entirely and uses a direct allometric equation: maintenance energy equals 95 kcal multiplied by body weight to the power 0.75, or 110 kcal multiplied by the same term, depending on the activity and neutering assumptions built into the guideline.

The two systems do not agree, and you must know by how much. Work a 20 kilogram dog.

  1. Twenty to the power 0.75 is 9.457.
  2. Resting energy is 70 multiplied by 9.457 = 662 kcal per day.
  3. The linear approximation gives 30 multiplied by 20 plus 70 = 670 kcal, about one percent higher. Either is fine inside the two to forty-five kilogram range.
  4. Neutered adult by the factor route is 662 multiplied by 1.6 = 1059 kcal per day.
  5. The direct route gives 95 multiplied by 9.457 = 898 kcal at the lower assumption and 110 multiplied by 9.457 = 1040 kcal at the higher one.
  6. So for the same dog you can defensibly write anything from 898 to 1059 kcal per day, a spread of about 18 percent. Expressed as a multiple of resting energy, the lower equation is about 1.36 times and the higher about 1.57 times.

That spread is not a mistake in either system; it reflects genuine variation between individual dogs. What is a mistake is mixing the two, so that your feeding table is built one way and your marketing claim another. Pick one, write it on your specification sheet, and be consistent. For a producer selling into households with moderately active, mostly neutered dogs, the resting energy times 1.6 route is the easier one to explain and to adjust.

Turn energy into product. On a dry food at 3800 kcal per kilogram, our 20 kilogram neutered dog eats 1059 divided by 3800 = 0.279 kilograms, that is 279 grams per day. On a wet food at 1000 kcal per kilogram it would be 1059 grams. At USD 1.60 per kilogram of dry food the daily cost is 0.279 multiplied by 1.60 = USD 0.45, about USD 13.60 a month and USD 163 a year.

Build the whole table at once. At the same 1.6 factor and 3800 kcal per kilogram, a 5 kilogram dog needs 375 kcal and 99 grams, a 10 kilogram dog 630 kcal and 166 grams, a 30 kilogram dog 1436 kcal and 378 grams, and a 40 kilogram dog 1781 kcal and 469 grams. Notice that quadrupling body weight from 10 to 40 kilograms only multiplies intake by about 2.8, because energy scales with weight to the power 0.75 rather than in proportion. Owners of large dogs consistently over-estimate what their animal needs, and a table built on the exponent is your defence.

Every number in a feeding table is a starting point to be titrated against body condition. Print that sentence. An individual dog can sit well outside these figures for reasons of temperament, coat, housing and climate, and a dog that will not hold condition on a correctly calculated ration should be seen by a veterinarian rather than simply fed more.

A producer in Accra, Ghana selling a 15 kilogram bag at USD 24, that is USD 1.60 per kilogram, found the most effective thing on the pack was not the analysis but a line saying how many days the bag would last a 20 kilogram dog: 15 divided by 0.279 equals 54 days. Owners compare bags on price; that line let them compare on cost per day, which is the number they actually experience.

Dogs in heavy work, sport or competition are a different calculation, running from 2.0 times resting energy for light work up to 4.0 to 8.0 for heavy work, and that is the subject of module 10.

Lesson 3.2~12 min

Protein and Fat for Active vs Sedentary Dogs

In this lesson
  • Hold protein constant on an energy basis when energy density changes
  • Set fat inclusion to match activity without breaching vitamin D or omega-3 limits
  • Cost the difference per kilogram between a standard and a higher-energy maintenance diet

The mistake almost every small producer makes is to think of protein and fat as percentages of the bag. The dog does not eat percentages, it eats calories, and it eats fewer grams of a denser food. If you raise energy density and leave protein at the same percentage, you have quietly cut the dog's protein intake.

The floors first. Adult dog maintenance requires at least 18.0 percent crude protein and 5.5 percent crude fat on a dry matter basis, and at least 45 grams of protein per 1000 kcal of metabolisable energy. The energy-basis figure is the one that protects the dog when density changes, which is why it exists.

Work the trap in full. Suppose you produce a standard diet at 3400 kcal per kilogram with 22 percent crude protein as fed, and you want a more energy-dense version for active dogs.

  1. Protein per kilogram is 220 grams. Divided by 3.4 thousand kcal that is 64.7 grams per 1000 kcal, comfortably above 45.
  2. You add fat and lift energy density to 4000 kcal per kilogram, keeping crude protein at 22 percent.
  3. Protein per 1000 kcal is now 220 divided by 4.0 = 55 grams. Still above the minimum, but you have cut the dog's protein intake by 15 percent without changing a single label figure.
  4. To hold 64.7 grams per 1000 kcal at 4000 kcal per kilogram you need 0.0647 multiplied by 4000 = 259 grams per kilogram, that is 25.9 percent crude protein.
  5. That extra 3.9 percentage points of protein is what the higher-energy diet really costs, on top of the fat.

Fat is the lever that moves energy density, contributing 9.4 kcal per gram of gross energy against 5.7 for protein and 4.1 for carbohydrate and fibre. A working or highly active dog is easier to feed on a denser diet because it can physically eat enough. A sedentary or neutered house dog is the opposite problem: at a factor of 1.2 to 1.4 times resting energy it needs so little of a dense food that the portion looks insulting to the owner, who then adds more.

So the two products differ in more than fat. An active-dog diet is denser, higher in fat and correspondingly higher in protein to hold the energy basis. A sedentary-dog diet is less dense, which means more volume per meal, and usually higher in fibre, remembering from module 1 lesson 4 that each percentage point of crude fibre costs roughly 1.57 points of energy digestibility, and that beet pulp at 10 percent inclusion raises dry matter digestibility but softens stools.

Respect the ceilings when you add fat. If the fat comes from fish oil, the safe upper limit for combined EPA and DHA in dogs is 280 milligrams per 100 kcal, that is 2800 milligrams per 1000 kcal. For a dog eating 1059 kcal a day the ceiling is 10.59 multiplied by 280 = 2965 milligrams per day. High-fat fish also carry vitamin D, and the dog ceiling is 3000 IU per kilogram of dry matter, with the European legal limit at 2270. The 2016 reduction in the safe upper limit specifically lowered how much high-fat fish could be used in recipes. Polyunsaturated fat also needs more vitamin E to resist oxidation; the correct ratio is not stated in our verified sources, so agree it with your premix supplier rather than guessing.

Cost the difference. Adding 5 kilograms of fat per 100 kilograms at USD 1.25 per kilogram costs USD 6.25, while removing 5 kilograms of maize at USD 0.42 saves USD 2.10, a net USD 4.15 per 100 kilograms or USD 0.042 per kilogram. Lifting protein from 22 to 25.9 percent by adding about 7 kilograms of a 60 percent protein meal at USD 1.40 in place of maize adds roughly 7 multiplied by 0.98 = USD 6.86 per 100 kilograms, that is USD 0.069 per kilogram. Together the active diet costs about USD 0.11 per kilogram more to make, which on a USD 1.60 product is a seven percent increase you can price for.

A producer in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia supplying both household pets and a security company's kennels runs exactly these two diets from one extruder, changing only the fat coating and the protein meal inclusion. One machine, one premix, two specifications, two prices. Dogs in genuinely heavy work need more than this, and module 10 covers that territory.

Where a dog is losing muscle on a correctly calculated ration, that is not a formulation question. Muscle loss with adequate intake needs a veterinary examination.

Lesson 3.3~12 min

Dental Health Through Diet

In this lesson
  • State honestly what a producer can and cannot claim about diet and dental health
  • Specify kibble form and hardness as a product property rather than a health claim
  • Cost a die plate change and decide whether the marketing benefit justifies it

Dental disease is one of the commonest problems owners notice in adult dogs, and one of the most heavily marketed. It is also the topic in this course where our verified reference set is thinnest: it contains AAFCO nutrient profiles, digestibility trials, energy equations and toxicity thresholds, and it contains no efficacy data at all on kibble texture, dental additives or plaque reduction. That is not a reason to ignore the subject. It is a reason to be precise about what you say.

Here is the line. You may describe a physical property of your product: kibble diameter in millimetres, shape, hardness as measured by whatever test you can run, and the fact that a larger kibble requires more chewing before it is swallowed. You may state that you have not added sugars. You may recommend that owners have their dog's teeth examined by a veterinarian at least annually. You may not state or imply a percentage reduction in tartar, plaque or gum disease, and you may not describe your food as dental, tartar-control or oral-care unless you hold a substantiation dossier for that claim in the market where you sell it.

The honest mechanism argument is simple and worth explaining to customers without exaggerating it. A very small or very soft kibble is swallowed with little chewing. A larger, firmer kibble is broken by the teeth, and mechanical contact with tooth surfaces is the plausible basis of any texture effect. Whether that translates into measurable dental benefit in your product is a question only a properly designed trial can answer, and running one is beyond a small producer. Say so.

What you can do is get the kibble size right for the dog, which is a real product quality issue regardless of dental claims. A kibble sized for a 5 kilogram dog is swallowed whole by a 40 kilogram one. Most small producers run one die plate and one kibble size, which is a compromise across their whole customer base.

Cost a size option properly before you commit.

  1. A second die plate for your extruder is quoted at USD 180.
  2. Each changeover costs roughly 40 minutes of production time. At a machine running 300 kilograms an hour, that is 200 kilograms of lost output.
  3. At a gross margin of USD 0.35 per kilogram, the lost output costs 200 multiplied by 0.35 = USD 70 per changeover.
  4. If you change over twice a month, the annual cost is 24 multiplied by 70 = USD 1680, plus the one-off USD 180.
  5. To justify that you need the large-kibble product to add at least 1860 divided by 0.35 = about 5314 kilograms of additional annual sales, that is roughly 355 fifteen-kilogram bags. Ask yourself honestly whether a kibble size will sell 355 extra bags before you buy the plate.

Two things to avoid in the pursuit of dental marketing. First, do not add sugars or sticky palatants to improve acceptance; they work against the argument you are making and add nothing nutritionally. Second, do not recommend bones. Owners will ask. Recreational bone feeding carries fracture and obstruction risks, and any advice about it should come from the animal's veterinarian, not from the company selling the food.

Remember also that some human dental products are toxic to dogs. Xylitol causes hypoglycaemia in dogs at more than 100 milligrams per kilogram of body weight and hepatic injury above 500 milligrams per kilogram, and dogs are the only domestic species in which xylitol toxicosis has been reported. It appears in sugar-free chewing gum and some toothpastes. A dog owner using a human toothpaste on their animal is the practical risk, and warning them is a useful, honest service that costs you nothing.

A producer in Harare, Zimbabwe took a different route to the same customer. Instead of a dental claim, the pack carries a short printed insert on brushing a dog's teeth, written with a local veterinary practice and carrying that practice's name and telephone number. It cost USD 90 to design and print 2000 copies. It sells no false claim, builds a referral relationship with a clinic, and gives the customer something the imported bag beside it does not have.

Where a dog already has bad breath, bleeding gums or difficulty eating, the answer is a veterinary dental examination. Diet does not treat established periodontal disease, and any therapeutic dietary role after a diagnosis belongs in module 9.

Lesson 3.4~12 min

Weight Management Formulations

In this lesson
  • Formulate a reduced-energy maintenance diet that still meets every adult minimum
  • Calculate the energy dilution needed to move a dog from one activity factor to a lower one
  • Explain to an owner why portion control, not a special bag, is the main lever

Most overweight dogs are not eating a bad food, they are eating too much of a good one. Before you sell anyone a weight product, be honest that the first intervention is a weighed ration and a measured body condition. Your commercial opportunity is the everyday prevention diet for the neutered, house-kept, moderately inactive dog that makes up a large part of the pet population. Managing an animal already diagnosed as obese, with the veterinary supervision that requires, is covered in module 9 lesson 5.

The energy factors define the target. A neutered adult sits at 1.6 times resting energy, an inactive or obesity-prone dog at 1.2 to 1.4, and a dog in an active weight loss programme at 1.0. For cats the equivalents are 1.2 to 1.4 neutered, 1.0 obesity-prone and 0.8 for weight loss.

Work a 30 kilogram dog through the range.

  1. Thirty to the power 0.75 is 12.82, so resting energy is 897 kcal.
  2. At the intact adult factor of 1.8 the requirement is 1615 kcal per day.
  3. At the neutered factor of 1.6 it is 1436 kcal.
  4. At the obesity-prone factor of 1.3 it is 1167 kcal, that is 19 percent less than the neutered figure and 28 percent less than the intact one.
  5. On a 3800 kcal per kilogram food those three are 425, 378 and 307 grams a day. The difference between a dog fed as if it were intact and one fed for its real activity is 118 grams a day, roughly 43 kilograms of food a year.

That arithmetic explains why so many neutered house dogs gain weight: nothing about the food changed, only the multiplier, and nobody adjusted the scoop.

Now formulate for it. The goal is a diet the dog can eat a satisfying volume of while taking in fewer calories, without dropping below any minimum. Lower the energy density by reducing fat towards, but not below, the 5.5 percent dry matter minimum, and by raising fibre. Then check what that does to protein on an energy basis. A diet at 3200 kcal per kilogram with 20 percent crude protein delivers 200 divided by 3.2 = 62.5 grams per 1000 kcal, comfortably above the 45 gram minimum, and the dog eating less food still gets adequate protein because the density fell alongside the intake.

Fibre is the tool and it has a price. Each percentage point of crude fibre removes roughly 1.57 points of energy digestibility in the dog equation, which is exactly the effect you want here. But fibre choice changes stool. At 10 percent inclusion, beet pulp gave dry matter digestibility of 81.3 percent against 77.2 for cellulose and 78.2 for Miscanthus grass, and organic matter 86.1 against 80.8 and 82.1, while producing softer stools with a score of 3.15 against 3.68 and 3.64. If you want energy dilution with firm stools, a less fermentable fibre does the job; if you want digestibility maintained, beet pulp does, at the cost of stool consistency. Trial your choice on real dogs before you scale it.

Cost the reformulation. Replacing 6 kilograms of fat per 100 with 6 kilograms of a fibre source at USD 0.30 per kilogram against fat at USD 1.25 saves 6 multiplied by 0.95 = USD 5.70 per 100 kilograms, that is USD 0.057 per kilogram. A reduced-energy diet is genuinely cheaper to make, and pricing it above your standard product purely because it says light on the bag is a short-term trick that customers eventually notice.

A producer in Windhoek, Namibia sells its reduced-energy adult diet with a printed weight chart and a cheap tape measure, and asks the owner to record body weight every two weeks. Owners returning the completed chart get a discount on their fourth bag. It costs about USD 1.10 per customer in tape measures and printing, and it produces the one thing that makes a weight product work, which is a customer who is actually measuring.

Set expectations honestly. Weight loss should be gradual, monitored, and supervised by a veterinarian, particularly in cats, where rapid weight loss carries its own risks. If an owner reports fast or unexplained weight change in either direction, that is a reason to see a veterinarian rather than to change bag.

Lesson 3.5~12 min

Breed-Specific Health Considerations

In this lesson
  • Separate documented breed-linked nutritional issues from breed marketing
  • State the evidence position on grain-free diets and dilated cardiomyopathy accurately
  • Cost taurine supplementation as insurance in a high-pulse formulation

Adult breed marketing is a large industry built on a small evidence base. Two breed-linked issues in our verified reference set are genuinely actionable for a producer. Everything else is a claim you would have to substantiate yourself.

The first is body size, which continues to matter after growth. A dog that matured at 70 pounds or more is not subject to a special adult calcium ceiling, but the adult limits still apply: calcium at least 0.5 percent of dry matter, phosphorus at least 0.4 percent with a maximum of 1.6, and the calcium to phosphorus ratio between 1 to 1 and 2 to 1. Where large-breed nutrition really bites is during growth, covered in module 2 lesson 2.

The second is taurine-responsive dilated cardiomyopathy. This one requires careful language, because the surrounding public debate has been noisy and much of what circulates is wrong. What is established is that taurine deficiency causes dilated cardiomyopathy in cats and in specific dog breeds, notably the American Cocker Spaniel and the Golden Retriever, and that this has been known independently of any grain-free question. It is also established that taurine is not a dietary essential for dogs, that dogs synthesise it, and that there is no taurine minimum in the AAFCO dog profile.

What is not established is a causal link between grain-free diets and dilated cardiomyopathy. The regulator received 524 reports between January 2014 and 30 April 2019, of which 515 were canine and 9 feline, including 119 dog deaths, and named 16 brands by report frequency. But adverse event numbers can be a potential signal of an issue while by themselves not supplying sufficient data to establish a causal relationship, the agency has described it as a complex scientific issue that may involve multiple factors, and in December 2022 it stated it did not intend to release further public updates until there was meaningful new scientific information. The investigation has been paused for want of evidence, not closed with a finding. Many of the reported dogs were not taurine-deficient, which is precisely why the mechanism remains open. A subsequent eighteen-month prospective study in healthy adult dogs found that different carbohydrate sources supported overall health and cardiac function, which is evidence against a simple explanation but is one study.

So what do you actually do? Do not market grain-free as a health claim and do not imply that containing grain is protective. If you formulate with high pulse inclusion, take three defensible steps: run digestibility and taurine or amino acid analysis on the finished diet, ensure generous methionine plus cystine, which are the taurine precursors, against the adult dog minimum of 0.65 percent of dry matter, and consider adding taurine as cheap insurance even though there is no established canine requirement.

Cost that insurance.

  1. Adding taurine at the level the cat profile uses for dry food, 0.10 percent of dry matter, means 1 gram per kilogram of dry matter.
  2. For a tonne of food at 92 percent dry matter that is 920 grams, call it 0.92 kilograms.
  3. At USD 18 per kilogram for feed-grade taurine, the cost is 0.92 multiplied by 18 = USD 16.56 per tonne.
  4. That is USD 0.0166 per kilogram of finished food, about one percent of a USD 1.60 product.
  5. Note also from module 1 lesson 4 that the pulse diets, pea and lentil, had the lowest dry matter, organic matter and energy digestibility of the six carbohydrate sources tested. If you are using pulses, you are already paying a digestibility penalty; the taurine addition is the smaller of your two costs.

Be equally disciplined about everything else. Skin and coat, hip and joint predisposition, sensitive digestion and breed-specific kibble shapes are all marketed heavily and none of them appears as a breed-differentiated nutrient requirement in the published profiles. You may say your food meets the adult minima. You may not say it is formulated for a named breed's specific needs without evidence for what those needs are.

A producer in Durban, South Africa serving a market with many working and pedigree dogs decided the honest differentiator was a printed certificate of analysis with every batch, including taurine, rather than a range of breed-named bags. That decision cost roughly USD 140 per batch in laboratory fees and removed the need for four separate stock lines. Breeders and veterinarians could see a number instead of a slogan.

Where a dog of any breed shows exercise intolerance, coughing or collapse, that is an urgent veterinary matter and not a diet question. Say so immediately and without qualification.

Lesson 3.6~12 min

Ingredient Selection and Cost Optimization

In this lesson
  • Use a Pearson's square to hit a protein target with two ingredients and then cost the result
  • Apply published inclusion ceilings for by-product meals and choose a starch source on digestibility
  • Compare formulations on cost per 1000 kcal rather than cost per kilogram of ingredient

Formulation is where nutrition meets the bank balance. For a small producer with limited working capital the discipline is not to find the cheapest ingredients but to find the cheapest combination that still meets every minimum, and then to hold that specification when prices move.

Start with the simplest tool, the Pearson's square, which balances two ingredients to a target. Suppose you need 22 percent crude protein as fed from maize at a declared 8.5 percent and fish meal at a declared 60 percent.

  1. Subtract the target from the high ingredient: 60 minus 22 = 38 parts of maize.
  2. Subtract the low ingredient from the target: 22 minus 8.5 = 13.5 parts of fish meal.
  3. Total parts are 38 plus 13.5 = 51.5.
  4. Maize is 38 divided by 51.5 = 73.8 percent; fish meal is 13.5 divided by 51.5 = 26.2 percent.
  5. Check: 0.738 multiplied by 8.5 plus 0.262 multiplied by 60 = 6.27 plus 15.72 = 21.99 percent. Correct.
  6. Cost at maize USD 0.42 and fish meal USD 1.40 per kilogram: 0.738 multiplied by 0.42 plus 0.262 multiplied by 1.40 = 0.310 plus 0.367 = USD 0.677 per kilogram.

That two-ingredient mix is not a diet, it is the backbone. Now substitute to reduce cost while respecting real ceilings. Replace 10 kilograms per 100 of the fish meal with bone protein meal at USD 0.80 per kilogram. The Beagle trial data says bone protein meal may be included up to 12 percent and feather meal up to 5 percent without negative effects on faecal scores, so 10 percent is inside the evidence. If that meal declares 50 percent protein, protein falls by 10 multiplied by 0.10, that is one percentage point, which you correct elsewhere; the cost saving is 10 multiplied by 0.60 = USD 6.00 per 100 kilograms, that is USD 0.06 per kilogram.

Choose your starch on evidence rather than fashion. In the six-source comparison, brewer's rice and cassava flour gave the greatest digestibility of dry matter, organic matter and gross energy, followed by corn and sorghum, with pea and lentil lowest, and starch digestibility exceeded 98 percent across every diet. For most African producers that ranking is good news, because cassava and sorghum are locally available and maize is the regional staple. Buying imported pulses to satisfy a marketing trend costs you money and digestibility at the same time.

Soy needs a clear head. A systematic review of adult dog diets found soybean meal reduced dry matter digestibility in most studies, increased crude protein digestibility in several, decreased fat digestibility in multiple trials and reduced gross energy digestibility across most. No maximum inclusion consensus exists, and the review explicitly declined to set one. Soy is cheap protein with a digestibility cost, and the correct response is to test your own diet rather than to adopt a number someone quoted at you.

Now compare properly. Never compare two formulations on cost per kilogram alone.

  1. Formulation A costs USD 0.94 per kilogram of ingredients and calculates to 3686 kcal per kilogram, that is 0.94 divided by 3.686 = USD 0.255 per 1000 kcal.
  2. Formulation B costs USD 0.86 per kilogram and calculates to 3100 kcal per kilogram, that is 0.86 divided by 3.1 = USD 0.277 per 1000 kcal.
  3. The dog needing 1059 kcal a day costs USD 0.270 a day on A and USD 0.293 on B.
  4. Over a year that is USD 98.55 against USD 106.95. The apparently cheaper formulation costs the customer more.

Protect the specification when prices move. Write your formulation as a set of nutrient constraints, not as a fixed ingredient list, so that when maize doubles in price after a poor season you can substitute sorghum or cassava and recheck the constraints rather than starting from nothing. Keep at least two qualified suppliers for every critical ingredient, and never let the premix be the item you improvise on.

A producer in Ouagadougou, Burkina Faso runs a spreadsheet with four columns for each candidate ingredient: price per kilogram this week, declared protein, declared fat and declared calcium. Every Monday morning the prices are updated and the least-cost mix is recalculated against fixed nutrient constraints. The whole exercise takes twenty minutes and has repeatedly caught price movements that would have quietly eaten the month's margin.

One last discipline. Cheapness that costs you a customer is not cheapness. Faecal quality, palatability and bag-to-bag consistency are what bring the repeat purchase, and module 12 covers the business side of holding that consistency as you grow.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. What is the resting energy requirement of a 20 kilogram dog?

70 multiplied by 20 to the power 0.75, that is 70 times 9.457, equals 662 kcal per day.

2. The direct allometric equation at 110 kcal per kilogram to the power 0.75 is roughly what multiple of resting energy?

110 divided by 70 is about 1.57. The 95 kcal version corresponds to about 1.36 times resting energy.

3. Why does intake rise less than proportionally with body weight?

The exponent of 0.75 means quadrupling weight from 10 to 40 kilograms multiplies energy needs by about 2.8, which is why owners of big dogs routinely overfeed.

4. What is the correct discipline when two energy systems give different answers?

The systems embed different assumptions and cannot be mixed. Consistency is what makes your feeding table and your marketing defensible.

5. A 15 kilogram bag feeds a 20 kilogram neutered dog for roughly how long at 279 grams a day?

15 divided by 0.279 equals about 54 days. Cost per day is the comparison customers actually experience, unlike price per bag.

6. A food has 22 percent protein and 4000 kcal per kilogram. Protein per 1000 kcal?

220 grams per kilogram divided by 4.0 thousand kcal gives 55 grams per 1000 kcal. At 3400 kcal per kilogram the same percentage would give 64.7.

7. Why must protein percentage rise when energy density rises?

Requirements are met per calorie eaten. A denser food is fed in smaller amounts, so the same percentage delivers less protein per day.

8. Which contributes the most gross energy per gram?

Fat contributes 9.4 kcal per gram in the modified Atwater route, which is why it is the lever for energy density.

9. What limits how much high-fat fish you can put in a dog food?

The 2016 cut in the vitamin D safe upper limit specifically reduced how much high-fat fish could be used, and combined EPA plus DHA is capped at 280 milligrams per 100 kcal.

10. Why is a very energy-dense food awkward for a sedentary neutered dog?

At 1.2 to 1.4 times resting energy the calculated portion of a dense food looks too small, and owners compensate. A less dense, higher-fibre diet gives more volume for the same energy.

11. What may a producer state honestly about kibble and dental health?

Measurable physical properties are verifiable. Efficacy claims require a substantiation dossier that a small producer is unlikely to hold.

12. Why is a very small or soft kibble weak on the mechanical argument?

Any texture effect depends on the teeth actually breaking the kibble. A piece swallowed whole provides no mechanical contact.

13. A second die plate costs USD 180 and USD 70 per changeover, twice a month. What must it earn back annually at USD 0.35 per kilogram margin?

USD 1680 of changeover cost plus USD 180 is USD 1860, divided by USD 0.35 per kilogram gives about 5314 kilograms of extra sales.

14. Which human product poses a specific poisoning risk to dogs?

Xylitol causes hypoglycaemia in dogs above 100 milligrams per kilogram of body weight and hepatic injury above 500, and dogs are the only domestic species in which toxicosis has been reported.

15. A customer's dog has bleeding gums and difficulty eating. What is the right response?

Established periodontal disease is a clinical problem. Diet does not treat it, and any therapeutic dietary role follows a veterinary diagnosis.

16. What is the resting energy of a 30 kilogram dog?

70 multiplied by 30 to the power 0.75, that is 70 times 12.82, equals 897 kcal per day.

17. Which factor applies to an inactive or obesity-prone adult dog?

Inactive or obesity-prone dogs sit at 1.2 to 1.4 times resting energy. The 1.0 factor is active weight loss and 0.8 is the feline weight loss figure.

18. A diet at 3200 kcal per kilogram with 20 percent protein gives how many grams per 1000 kcal?

200 grams per kilogram divided by 3.2 thousand kcal gives 62.5 grams per 1000 kcal, well above the 45 gram adult minimum.

19. What happened to stool score with 10 percent beet pulp compared with cellulose?

Beet pulp raised dry matter and organic matter digestibility but produced softer stools, a trade-off you must test on real dogs before scaling.

20. What should you tell an owner is the first intervention for an overweight dog?

Most overweight dogs are overfed rather than fed the wrong food, and supervised gradual loss is safer, particularly in cats.

21. What is the accurate statement about grain-free diets and canine dilated cardiomyopathy?

Adverse event reports are a potential signal but do not by themselves establish causation, and the agency paused updates pending meaningful new scientific information.

22. Is taurine a dietary essential for dogs?

Dogs synthesise taurine and the AAFCO dog profile contains no taurine row, although deficiency-linked cardiomyopathy is documented in specific breeds.

23. Adding taurine at 1 gram per kilogram of dry matter costs about what per kilogram of finished food at USD 18 per kilogram?

0.92 kilograms per tonne at USD 18 is USD 16.56 per tonne, that is about USD 0.017 per kilogram.

24. Which carbohydrate sources had the lowest digestibility in the six-source trial?

Pea and lentil diets gave the lowest dry matter, organic matter and gross energy digestibility, which is a cost of high pulse inclusion independent of any cardiomyopathy question.

25. A customer's dog shows exercise intolerance and coughing. What do you say?

Those signs need urgent veterinary assessment. A producer suggesting a dietary fix in that situation is delaying care.

26. Using a Pearson's square for 22 percent protein from 8.5 percent maize and 60 percent fish meal, what proportion is maize?

The parts are 60 minus 22 equals 38 for maize and 22 minus 8.5 equals 13.5 for fish meal, so maize is 38 divided by 51.5, that is 73.8 percent.

27. What did the systematic review conclude about a maximum inclusion level for soybean meal?

The heterogeneity of methodologies prevented any definitive maximum, so the honest response is to test your own diet rather than to quote a figure.

28. Formulation A is USD 0.94 per kilogram at 3686 kcal/kg, B is USD 0.86 at 3100. Which is cheaper to feed?

A costs USD 0.255 per 1000 kcal against B at USD 0.277, so the apparently cheaper bag is the more expensive food.

29. Why write a formulation as nutrient constraints rather than a fixed ingredient list?

Constraints let you swap maize for sorghum or cassava after a bad season and recheck compliance, instead of reformulating from nothing under time pressure.

30. Replacing 10 kg per 100 of fish meal at USD 1.40 with bone protein meal at USD 0.80 saves how much per kilogram of food?

The saving is 10 kilograms multiplied by USD 0.60, that is USD 6.00 per 100 kilograms, which is USD 0.06 per kilogram of finished food.

Module 3 capstone

Produce a complete adult dog maintenance product file that you could hand to a regulator, a veterinarian or an investor. Step 1: define your target customer in one paragraph, naming the typical dog, its weight, its activity level and whether it is neutered. Step 2: calculate maintenance energy for that dog by both routes, resting energy times the life stage factor and the direct allometric equation at 95 and at 110 kcal per kilogram to the power 0.75, and state in writing which system your feeding table uses and why. Step 3: build a least-cost formulation using a Pearson's square for the main protein constraint, then refine it by hand against your local prices, respecting a bone protein meal ceiling of 12 percent and a feather meal ceiling of 5 percent. Step 4: check the finished diet on a dry matter basis against every adult dog minimum: protein 18.0 percent, fat 5.5 percent, lysine 0.63 percent, methionine plus cystine 0.65 percent, calcium 0.5 percent, phosphorus 0.4 percent with a ceiling of 1.6 percent, and the calcium to phosphorus ratio between 1 to 1 and 2 to 1. Step 5: calculate metabolisable energy and build a feeding table in grams per day for six body weights and three activity levels. Step 6: cost the recipe per kilogram, per 1000 kcal and per dog per day, and set a retail price with a stated gross margin. Step 7: write the label text, and write a second page listing every claim you have deliberately chosen not to make, including dental, breed and grain-free claims, with your reason for each. Have a veterinarian review the feeding table before you print.
