1. Choosing Handles, Locks, and Hinges for Local Conditions

In Africa, hardware must survive heat, dust, humidity, and salt air in coastal areas. Choose handles made from stainless steel 304 or powder-coated aluminum. Avoid cheap zinc alloy that rusts in months. For inland dry areas, nylon or UV-stabilized plastic handles can work but check they don't become brittle. Locks: use brass or stainless steel cylinders with at least 5 pins. In dusty regions, avoid exposed springs; choose enclosed mechanisms. Hinges: stainless steel 304 or 316 for coastal, with nylon washers to reduce friction. For heavy doors, use 3 hinges per door leaf, each rated for 40 kg. In hot climates, expansion gaps matter; choose hinges with adjustable height. Always test a sample: spray with salt water for 48 hours (coastal) or leave in sun for a week (inland). Local hardware shops often sell Chinese imports; ask for grade 304 stamp. For security, use multipoint locks with 3 locking points. In high-crime areas, add a secondary deadbolt. Budget: a good handle set costs $15-30, a lock cylinder $10-20, hinges $5-10 each. Don't mix metals that cause galvanic corrosion (e.g., aluminum and steel without barrier). Use nylon or Teflon tape between dissimilar metals. For sliding doors, choose rollers with sealed bearings and UV-resistant nylon wheels. In sandy areas, choose larger wheels (30mm) that ride over grit. Always keep spare parts; hardware fails first. Train yourself to identify quality: weight, smooth operation, no sharp edges, and a warranty of at least 2 years. Remember, cheap hardware costs more in replacements and customer complaints.

2. Installing Window Handles and Fasteners

Start by checking the window frame profile. Most aluminum windows have a Euro-groove or a specific channel. Measure the distance between the two mounting holes on your handle (common: 43mm or 55mm). If it doesn't match, you'll need an adapter plate or a different handle. Use a 3.5mm or 4mm drill bit for pilot holes in aluminum; never use wood bits. Drill at slow speed (800-1200 rpm) and use a few drops of kerosene or WD-40 as lubricant to prevent chips. For fasteners, use stainless steel self-tapping screws with a pan head. Length should be 10-12mm longer than the handle base thickness. For PVC or thermal break frames, use screws with a drill point. Insert screws by hand first to avoid cross-threading. Tighten firmly but not so tight that you crush the frame. For casement windows, the handle operates a multi-point lock; ensure the espagnolette bar moves freely. If stiff, adjust the striker plate on the frame. For sliding windows, handles are often surface-mounted with two screws; use a template to align. After installation, test open/close 10 times. If the handle feels loose, add a drop of threadlocker (blue) on screws. In dusty areas, apply silicone spray to the moving parts monthly. For security, choose handles with a key lock or a child safety catch. In coastal areas, apply a thin coat of petroleum jelly on screws to prevent rust. Common mistake: over-tightening screws, which strips the aluminum threads. If threads strip, use a larger screw or a rivet nut. Always wear safety glasses when drilling. Keep a vacuum or brush to clean aluminum shavings; they stain and cause corrosion. For a typical window, installation takes 15-20 minutes. Charge accordingly.

3. Installing Door Locks and Cylinders

Door locks for aluminum doors are usually mortise locks or narrow stile locks. First, determine the backset: the distance from the door edge to the center of the lock cylinder hole. Common backsets are 30mm, 35mm, and 40mm. Measure your door stile width; it must be at least 45mm for a standard mortise lock. Use a lock template or measure carefully. Drill the cylinder hole (usually 20mm) and the mortise pocket with a chisel or router. For narrow stile doors, use a lock with a 20mm faceplate. Cylinders: choose a euro profile cylinder with a length that matches door thickness plus 10mm. For example, a 45mm door needs a 55mm cylinder (45+10). If the cylinder sticks out, it's a security risk; use a security escutcheon. Install the cylinder from the outside, then the lock body from the inside. Secure with the long screw through the lock body into the cylinder. Test the key: it should turn smoothly without forcing. For double doors, use a flush bolt on the inactive leaf. In dusty areas, use a cylinder with a dust cover. For high security, choose a cylinder with drill resistance and at least 6 pins. Master keying is possible but keep it simple. After installation, adjust the strike plate so the latch enters freely. If the door sags, fix the hinges first. Lubricate the lock with graphite powder, not oil, which attracts dust. In coastal areas, use stainless steel or brass locks; avoid zinc. For emergency exit doors, use panic bars. Always keep a spare key outside the building. Cost: a good mortise lock set $25-50, cylinder $15-30. Installation time: 1-2 hours per door. Practice on a scrap door first.

4. Fitting Rollers and Tracks for Sliding Systems

Sliding aluminum doors and windows need smooth rollers and clean tracks. First, inspect the track: it should be straight, level, and free of debris. Use a vacuum and a wire brush to clean. For new installations, choose tracks with a raised centre rib to guide rollers. Rollers come in single, double, or tandem wheels. For heavy doors (over 40 kg), use tandem rollers with sealed bearings. Wheel diameter: 20-30mm for normal, 30-40mm for sandy areas. Material: nylon or polyurethane; avoid cheap plastic that flattens. Adjustable rollers are best; they have a screw to raise or lower the door by up to 5mm. Install rollers at the bottom of the door stile. Usually, there is a slot; slide the roller in and secure with a screw. For top-hung systems, rollers go on top. Ensure the roller is centered in the track. The door should slide with one finger. If it binds, check for debris, bent track, or misaligned rollers. For tracks, use stainless steel or anodized aluminum. In coastal areas, stainless steel 304 is best. Fix track with countersunk screws every 300mm. Use a spirit level to ensure it's flat. For sliding windows, the track often has a drainage hole; keep it clear. Lubricate tracks with silicone spray, never oil. For security, add anti-lift blocks to prevent the door being lifted out. In dusty regions, use brush seals on the track to keep out grit. Replace rollers when they develop flat spots or become noisy. A set of 4 rollers costs $10-20. Installation time: 30-60 minutes per door. Test by sliding 20 times. If the door jumps, adjust roller height. Always keep spare rollers.

5. Weather Stripping and Insect Screens

Weather stripping stops drafts, water, and dust. For aluminum windows and doors, use EPDM or silicone rubber seals. Avoid cheap foam that crumbles in heat. Common types: brush seals for sliding tracks, bulb seals for casement windows, and fin seals for doors. Measure the gap: for sliding windows, use a 5-7mm brush seal; for doors, a 10-12mm bulb seal. Clean the surface with alcohol before applying. Most seals have a self-adhesive backing; press firmly for 30 seconds. For better hold, use a small amount of superglue at the ends. In hot climates, choose UV-resistant silicone. Replace seals every 3-5 years. Insect screens: use aluminum or fiberglass mesh. Aluminum mesh is stronger but dents; fiberglass is cheaper but tears. For coastal areas, use stainless steel mesh to resist salt. Frame the mesh with aluminum U-channel and spline (rubber cord). Cut mesh 20mm larger than frame. Roll the spline into the groove with a screen roller tool. For windows, make screens removable for cleaning. For doors, use a hinged screen door with a closer. In areas with mosquitoes, use 18x16 mesh (18 holes per inch). For sandflies, use 20x20. Install screens on the outside of the frame. Keep screens tight; loose mesh lets insects in. Clean with a soft brush and soapy water. Repair small holes with clear nail polish or a patch kit. Cost: weather strip $1-2 per meter, screen mesh $5-10 per square meter. Installation time: 15-30 minutes per window. Always test for gaps with a light or smoke test.

6. Maintaining and Replacing Hardware

Hardware fails first. Set a maintenance schedule: every 3 months in dusty or coastal areas, every 6 months inland. Start by cleaning: use a soft cloth and mild soapy water. Never use abrasive cleaners or steel wool on aluminum. For locks, apply graphite powder or PTFE spray. For hinges, apply a drop of light machine oil (SAE 20) and wipe excess. For rollers, clean tracks and spray silicone. Check screws: tighten any loose ones. Look for rust: if you see white rust on aluminum, clean with a 50/50 vinegar water solution and apply a protective wax. For coastal areas, apply a corrosion inhibitor like Boeshield T-9 every 6 months. Replace hardware when: handles wobble, keys stick, rollers flatten, seals harden, or hinges squeak after lubrication. Keep a stock of common spares: handles, rollers, lock cylinders, and screws. When replacing, take the old part to the shop to match. For locks, if the key is lost, you can rekey a cylinder by replacing the pins, but it's easier to buy a new cylinder. For sliding doors, if the roller is not adjustable, replace with an adjustable type. In an emergency, you can use a screwdriver to pry a sliding door back onto track, but fix properly. Train customers: show them how to clean tracks and lubricate. Offer a 6-month service contract. Cost of maintenance: minimal, but saves expensive repairs. A well-maintained door lasts 20 years; neglected, 5. Always use the right tool: Allen keys, Phillips screwdrivers, and a small brush. Document what you did and when. This builds trust and repeat business.

<h2>Practical Reference: Hardware &amp; Accessories for Aluminium Windows and Doors</h2>

<p>In African aluminium fabrication, hardware is where profit is won or lost. A window frame that costs KES 4,500 in extrusion can be destroyed by a KES 300 handle that fails in the first Harmattan or coastal rainy season. This Practical Reference gives fabricators, installers and workshop managers a field-ready guide to selecting, installing and maintaining hardware that survives local conditions and keeps customers paying.</p>

<p>Across Sub-Saharan Africa, hardware failures cluster around three forces: <strong>UV and heat</strong> (accelerated polymer degradation), <strong>moisture and salt</strong> (coastal corrosion, monsoon humidity), and <strong>dust and grit</strong> (abrasion in rollers, tracks and lock cylinders). Choose hardware against these three forces first; aesthetics second.</p>

<p><strong>Hinges:</strong> For casement windows, use friction-stay hinges rated to the sash weight (typically 1225 kg for a 600900 mm sash). In dusty regions, dry-film lubricated stainless hinges outperform grease-packed units. Never mix hinge brands on a single sash alignment drift causes draughts and lock misalignment.</p>

<p><strong>Locks:</strong> Multi-point locking (MPL) is now the market default for entrance doors in Nairobi, Lagos and Johannesburg. For rental and commercial projects, specify a euro-profile cylinder with a 5-pin or 6-pin mechanism; for high-security, add an anti-snap, anti-bump cylinder. Keep a master-key suite if the client manages many units.</p>

<h3>2. Installing Window Handles and Fasteners</h3>

<p>The most common installation error is <strong>over-torquing self-tapping screws into thin aluminium</strong>, stripping the thread and causing the handle to loosen within weeks. Correct sequence:</p>
<ol>
<li>Mark handle position with the sash closed and locked in the frame never on a bench.</li>
<li>Drill pilot holes 0.2 mm smaller than the screw shank; use a depth stop.</li>
<li>Apply a bead of neutral-cure silicone around the base plate to stop water tracking.</li>
<li>Use stainless A2 or A4 screws; brass or zinc-plated screws will galvanically corrode against aluminium.</li>
<li>Tighten to hand-torque only; a cordless driver on low clutch setting avoids stripping.</li>
</ol>

<h3>3. Installing Door Locks and Cylinders</h3>

<p>Door lock installation is a three-part operation: mortise preparation, cylinder fit, and strike-plate alignment. In African workshops, the strike plate is where most rework happens. Set the door in the frame with packers, mark the strike with a sharp blade, and route the recess 1 mm deeper than the plate so it sits flush. For coastal projects, choose a stainless faceplate and a cylinder with a brushed finish polished chrome shows salt bloom within weeks.</p>

<h3>4. Fitting Rollers and Tracks for Sliding Systems</h3>

<p>Sliding systems carry the highest maintenance load in any African building. Grit in the bottom track acts as sandpaper on rollers. Best practice:</p>
<ul>
<li>Use <strong>tandem rollers</strong> (two wheels per carriage) for sashes over 25 kg.</li>
<li>Specify <strong>stainless-steel axles</strong> with UV-stabilised nylon wheels.</li>
<li>Fit an <strong>anti-jump block</strong> at the top track to prevent sash lift-out in high wind.</li>
<li>Set the bottom track with a 12 mm drainage slot every 600 mm to shed rainwater.</li>
<li>Adjust roller height with the sash in place; a 2 mm vertical error causes drag and premature wear.</li>
</ul>

<h3>5. Weather Stripping and Insect Screens</h3>

<p>Weather stripping is the cheapest energy-efficiency upgrade in African buildings. A 5 mm gap around a 1.2 m 1.5 m window can leak the equivalent of 812% of cooling load. Use <strong>EPDM or silicone</strong> seals not PVC, which hardens in UV within 18 months. In malaria-endemic regions, combine weather stripping with a <strong>stainless 1816 mesh insect screen</strong>; aluminium mesh corrodes on the coast and tears easily.</p>

<h3>6. Maintaining and Replacing Hardware</h3>

<p>Build a maintenance contract into every commercial quote. A quarterly service visit clean tracks, lubricate rollers with dry PTFE, check lock alignment, replace worn seals extends hardware life by 4060% and creates recurring revenue. Train the client's caretaker on the 3-minute monthly check: wipe tracks, test lock, listen for roller drag.</p>

<strong style="color:#c0392b"> SAFETY WARNING Working at Height</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0">Installing or servicing window hardware above ground floor in Africa is a leading cause of construction fatalities. Always use a certified scaffold, mobile elevating work platform (MEWP) or full-body harness anchored to a rated point. Never stand on window sills, buckets or unsecured ladders. In many jurisdictions (e.g. Kenya OSHA 2007, South Africa Construction Regulations 2014), working at height above 2 m without fall protection is a legal offence. Isolate the work area below before starting.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li> Confirm environment class (coastal / humid / hot-dry / dusty) before specifying hardware.</li>
<li> Verify stainless grade (A2 for inland, A4/316 for coastal).</li>
<li> Check roller load rating against actual sash weight never guess.</li>
<li> Drill pilot holes and use stainless fasteners; seal handle bases with neutral silicone.</li>
<li> Align strike plate flush with 1 mm recess; test lock 10 before handover.</li>
<li> Fit EPDM/silicone seals and stainless insect mesh where malaria risk applies.</li>
<li> Set drainage slots in bottom track every 600 mm.</li>
<li> Offer a quarterly maintenance contract it protects margin and builds repeat business.</li>
</ul>
