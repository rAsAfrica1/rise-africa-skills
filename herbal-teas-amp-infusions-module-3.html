1. Best Times and Methods to Harvest Herbs

Harvest herbs when they have the highest concentration of oils and flavor. For most leafy herbs like mint, basil, and lemon balm, the best time is in the morning after the dew has dried but before the sun gets hotusually between 7 and 10 a.m. At this time, the essential oils are at their peak. If you harvest later in the day, the heat can cause oils to evaporate, leaving you with weaker tea. For herbs grown for their flowers, such as chamomile or hibiscus, pick the flowers just as they open fully. For roots like ginger or turmeric, wait until the plant is 810 months old and the leaves start to yellow; dig them up in the dry season when the soil is not too wet.

Use clean, sharp scissors or pruning shears to cut stems. Avoid tearing the plant, as this can damage it and invite disease. For leafy herbs, cut the top 1015 cm of growth, just above a leaf node (where leaves join the stem). This encourages the plant to branch out and produce more leaves. Never harvest more than one-third of the plant at a time; leave enough foliage for it to continue growing. For flowers, pick them individually or cut whole flower heads. For roots, lift them carefully with a fork or spade, shake off excess soil, and wash them thoroughly.

The best season to harvest depends on your climate. In tropical Africa, many herbs grow year-round, but the dry season is often best because there is less moisture, reducing the risk of mold during drying. In areas with a rainy season, harvest after the rains have stopped. If you irrigate, you can harvest multiple times, but give plants a rest period between harvests. Always check the specific needs of each herb: some, like rosemary and thyme, are more aromatic when harvested just before flowering; others, like mint, are best before they flower because after flowering, the leaves become bitter.

After cutting, keep the herbs cool and shaded until you can process them. Do not pile them in a heap, as they will heat up and spoil. Spread them loosely in a basket or on a clean cloth. The sooner you start drying, the better the quality. Aim to harvest only what you can process within a few hours. With practice, you will learn to read your plants and know exactly when they are ready for the best cup of tea.

2. Cleaning and Preparing Herbs After Harvest

After harvesting, the first step is to remove any foreign matter: insects, dust, soil, and dead or diseased plant parts. Inspect each stem and leaf. Pick off any yellowed, spotted, or damaged leaves. If you see insects, gently shake the herbs or use a soft brush to remove them. Do not wash herbs unless they are very dirty, because excess moisture can lead to mold during drying. If you must wash, do it quickly in a basin of clean water, then spin or pat dry with a clean cloth. For roots, washing is essentialuse a stiff brush to scrub off soil under running water.

Next, decide what parts you will use. For tea, you typically use leaves, flowers, or roots. Strip the leaves from thick stems; stems can be composted or used for other purposes. For herbs like mint, you can leave small stems attachedthey also contain flavor. For flowers like chamomile, pluck the petals or use the whole flower head. For roots, slice them into thin pieces (23 mm thick) to speed up drying. The smaller the pieces, the faster they dry, but don't cut too small or you'll lose aroma.

Sort the herbs by size and type. This ensures even drying. Remove any thick stems that won't dry at the same rate as leaves. If you are drying different herbs, keep them separate to avoid mixing flavors. Label each batch with the herb name and date. Use clean baskets, trays, or screens for drying. Make sure all equipment is clean and dry. Avoid using newspaper for drying because the ink can transfer to the herbs; use clean cotton cloth, mesh screens, or bamboo trays.

Finally, prepare your drying area. It should be well-ventilated, out of direct sunlight (unless you are sun-drying), and free from dust and animals. If drying indoors, choose a room with good airflow. If drying outdoors, use a raised screen or hang herbs in a shaded, breezy spot. The goal is to remove moisture quickly and evenly. Proper cleaning and preparation set the stage for high-quality dried herbs that will make excellent tea.

3. Sun-Drying vs. Shade-Drying: Which Works Best in Your Climate

Sun-drying is the oldest and cheapest method. It works best in hot, dry climates with low humiditylike many parts of Sahel, East Africa, and Southern Africa during the dry season. To sun-dry, spread herbs in a single layer on a clean screen, tray, or mat. Place them in direct sunlight for 68 hours per day. Turn them once or twice a day for even drying. Sun-drying is fast for tough herbs like rosemary, thyme, and oregano. However, direct sun can fade color and evaporate delicate oils in herbs like mint, basil, and lemon balm. If you sun-dry these, they may lose flavor. Also, sun-drying is not suitable in humid or rainy seasons because herbs may mold before they dry.

Shade-drying is better for preserving color, flavor, and aroma. It is ideal for delicate herbs and in hot, humid climates where sun-drying is risky. To shade-dry, hang herbs in small bunches (510 stems) upside down in a dark, well-ventilated room or under a shade tree. You can also spread them on screens in a shaded, airy place. Shade-drying takes longeroften 12 weeksbut the result is higher quality. In humid areas, you can use a fan to improve air circulation. Shade-drying is also good for flowers like chamomile and hibiscus, which can lose color in direct sun.

Which method to choose? Consider your climate and the herb. If you live in a dry, hot area and are drying tough herbs, sun-drying is efficient. If you have delicate herbs or high humidity, shade-drying is safer. You can combine methods: start with a few hours of morning sun, then move to shade. In very humid conditions, consider using a solar dryer or a dehydrator. Always monitor the herbs daily. If you see mold, discard the affected parts. With practice, you'll know which method suits your situation. Remember, the goal is to dry herbs quickly but gently to preserve the essential oils that give tea its flavor and health benefits.

4. Using Solar Dryers and Other Low-Cost Drying Methods

Solar dryers are a step up from open sun-drying. They protect herbs from dust, insects, and rain while concentrating heat. A simple solar dryer can be made from a wooden box with a glass or plastic top, and vents for air circulation. The black interior absorbs heat, and the herbs dry faster and cleaner. You can build one using local materials: a wooden frame, a clear plastic sheet (UV-resistant), and mesh trays. Place the dryer in direct sun. Temperatures inside can reach 5070C, which dries herbs in 13 days. This is ideal for humid climates because the higher temperature prevents mold. Solar dryers are affordable and can be scaled up for commercial production.

Other low-cost methods include using a dehydrator (electric or solar-powered), which gives precise temperature control. Electric dehydrators are more expensive but work in any weather. Set the temperature to 3540C for delicate herbs and 5060C for roots and tough herbs. If you don't have electricity, you can use a makeshift dehydrator: a box with a light bulb (60100W) and a small fan. But be careful not to cook the herbskeep the temperature below 60C. Another method is oven-drying, but ovens often don't go low enough; if you use an oven, set it to the lowest setting and leave the door slightly open. This is not ideal for large quantities.

For small-scale drying, you can use hanging methods: tie herbs in bunches and hang them in a dry, airy room. This is shade-drying. You can also use paper bags: put herbs in a paper bag with holes punched for ventilation, and hang the bag. This protects from light and dust. For roots, slice and dry on screens in a solar dryer or a warm, dry room. In all methods, ensure good airflow. Turn herbs daily. Avoid overcrowding. The key is to remove moisture evenly. With a solar dryer, you can dry herbs even during light rain, as long as you can bring it inside. Investing in a solar dryer can pay off if you dry herbs regularly for sale. It improves quality and reduces losses.

5. Testing Herbs for Dryness and Quality

Properly dried herbs should be crisp, not brittle or leathery. To test for dryness, take a leaf or root piece and try to bend it. It should snap or crumble easily. If it bends without breaking, it still has moisture and needs more drying. For roots, they should be hard and snap when broken. Another test: put a small amount in a sealed jar for 24 hours. If condensation appears on the inside of the jar, the herbs are not dry enough. You can also weigh a sample before and after drying; herbs should lose about 7080% of their weight. For example, 1 kg of fresh mint yields about 200300 g of dried mint. If you get more, it's likely not fully dry.

Quality goes beyond dryness. Good dried herbs retain their color: green herbs should be green, not brown or yellow. They should smell strongly of the herbif the aroma is weak, they may have been dried at too high a temperature or stored too long. Check for mold: any white, grey, or black fuzz means the batch is spoiled and should be discarded. Also look for insects or dirt. For tea, you want clean, fragrant, and flavorful herbs. If you are selling, consistency matters. Sort out any discolored or damaged pieces. You can sift the dried herbs to remove dust and small particles.

Testing should be done throughout the drying process. Check daily after the first day. Once dry, cool the herbs completely before packaging. Warm herbs can create condensation in the container, leading to mold. Store in a cool, dark, dry place. Properly dried and stored herbs can last 12 years. But always check before use: if they have lost their smell or taste, they are past their prime. For commercial sales, you may want to test moisture content with a moisture meter; aim for less than 10% moisture. Without a meter, use the snap test and jar test. These simple methods are reliable and require no special equipment. Remember, the quality of your tea depends on the quality of your dried herbs.

6. Packaging Dried Herbs for Sale or Storage

Packaging protects dried herbs from moisture, light, air, and pests. For storage, use airtight containers: glass jars with rubber seals, metal tins, or high-quality plastic containers with tight lids. Avoid plastic bags that are not airtight; they allow moisture and odors to enter. For long-term storage, keep herbs whole and crush them just before use; whole leaves retain flavor longer. If you are selling, packaging is also about presentation and branding. Use clean, food-grade bags or jars. Clear bags show the product, but they let in light, so store them in a dark box. Better yet, use opaque bags or jars. Label with the herb name, weight, and harvest date. Include brewing instructions: 1 teaspoon per cup, steep 5 minutes.

For small-scale sales, you can use zip-lock bags, but invest in a heat sealer for a professional look. Paper bags are not airtight; use them only for short-term storage. For larger quantities, use food-grade buckets with airtight lids. Always package in a clean, dry environment. Humidity is the enemy. If you live in a humid area, add a small desiccant packet (silica gel) to each container. Do not use cotton bags for long-term storage; they breathe, which means moisture can get in. For tea bags, you can buy empty tea bags and fill them, but this is labor-intensive. Many small producers sell loose herbs in bulk.

Hygiene is critical. Wash your hands, wear gloves, and ensure all containers are sterile. Avoid packaging herbs when they are still warm. Label everything clearly. For sales, you need to know your market: health food stores, online, or local markets. Package sizes: 50g, 100g, 250g are common. Include contact information and a batch number for traceability. Store packaged herbs in a cool, dark place. If you are selling at a market, keep them out of direct sun. With good packaging, your herbs will stay fresh and fetch a better price. Remember, repeat customers come from consistent quality. So, package well and your tea business will grow.

<h2>Practical Reference: Harvesting and Drying Herbs for Tea</h2>

<p>This reference lesson consolidates the technical detail behind the six core lessons in this module. It is written for African herb growers, aggregators, and small tea processors who need repeatable, low-cost methods that protect quality, meet buyer expectations, and reduce post-harvest losses. In many African markets, 2040% of harvested aromatic material is lost to poor handling, over-drying, or contamination. Getting harvesting and drying right is the single fastest way to increase income per kilogram.</p>

<h3>1. Best Times and Methods to Harvest Herbs</h3>
<p>Essential oil content, flavour, and colour peak at specific growth stages. As a rule:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Leafy herbs</strong> (mint, lemon balm, basil, moringa): harvest just before flowering, in the cool of early morning after dew has dried.</li>
<li><strong>Flowering herbs</strong> (chamomile, hibiscus, roselle): harvest when flowers are fully open but before petals fade.</li>
<li><strong>Roots and barks</strong> (ginger, turmeric, cinnamon): harvest at the end of the growing season when active compounds are highest.</li>
</ul>
<p>Use clean, sharp secateurs or knives. Cut stems 510 cm above the soil for regrowth. Never pull plants up by the roots unless harvesting the root itself. Harvest in the early morning (69 a.m.) to capture peak volatile oils, and never harvest wet plants surface moisture encourages mould during drying.</p>

<h3>2. Cleaning and Preparing Herbs After Harvest</h3>
<p>Do not wash herbs unless heavily soiled. Washing removes essential oils and adds drying time. If washing is unavoidable, use clean, cool water, drain thoroughly, and pat dry with a clean cloth. Remove:</p>
<ul>
<li>Yellow, diseased, or insect-damaged leaves</li>
<li>Roots, thick stems, and foreign matter (stones, plastic, soil)</li>
<li>Any material showing mould, rot, or pesticide residue</li>
</ul>
<p>Spread herbs in a single layer in shallow, ventilated trays. Never pile fresh herbs in sacks or buckets heat builds up and destroys quality within hours.</p>

<h3>3. Sun-Drying vs. Shade-Drying: Which Works Best in Your Climate</h3>
<p>Sun-drying is fast and free but destroys volatile oils and colour in delicate herbs. Shade-drying is slower but preserves aroma, colour, and market value. The right choice depends on your climate and the herb.</p>

<h3>4. Using Solar Dryers and Other Low-Cost Drying Methods</h3>
<p>Solar dryers are the most cost-effective quality tool for African herb processors. A simple cabinet or tunnel dryer can cut drying time by 5070% and protect herbs from dust, rain, insects, and animals.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cabinet solar dryer:</strong> wooden or metal box with a glass/polycarbonate top, ventilation holes, and mesh trays. Cost: US$80300 locally built.</li>
<li><strong>Tunnel solar dryer:</strong> larger capacity, good for cooperatives. Cost: US$3001,500.</li>
<li><strong>Hybrid dryer:</strong> solar plus biomass or electric backup for cloudy days.</li>
</ul>
<p>Other low-cost methods include raised mesh drying racks under a shade roof, and ventilated attic drying. Avoid drying herbs directly on the ground, on bare metal roofs, or near smoke, fuel, or livestock.</p>

<h3>5. Testing Herbs for Dryness and Quality</h3>
<p>Over-drying wastes energy and crushes leaves; under-drying causes mould and rejection. Target moisture content for dried herbs is typically 812%.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Snap test:</strong> stems should snap cleanly, not bend.</li>
<li><strong>Crush test:</strong> leaves should crumble easily, not smear.</li>
<li><strong>Weight test:</strong> weigh a small sample before and after drying; aim for 7080% weight loss for leafy herbs.</li>
<li><strong>Moisture meter:</strong> most accurate; use a calibrated grain/herb moisture meter.</li>
</ul>

<strong style="color:#c0392b;"> SAFETY WARNING AFLATOXIN AND MOULD RISK</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#7b241c;">Never sell or consume herbs showing any sign of mould, musty smell, or black/green patches. Improper drying especially in humid conditions can produce <strong>aflatoxins</strong> and other mycotoxins that are invisible, heat-stable, and cause liver damage and cancer. Discard affected batches entirely; do not blend them with good stock. Store dried herbs below 12% moisture in airtight containers away from walls and floors. If you suspect contamination, contact your national bureau of standards or a food safety laboratory before selling.</p>
</div>

<h3>6. Packaging Dried Herbs for Sale or Storage</h3>
<p>Packaging protects aroma, colour, and moisture balance and it is a marketing tool. Choose based on your market:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Bulk buyers / processors:</strong> food-grade polypropylene or paper-lined sacks, 1025 kg, clearly labelled with herb, lot number, moisture, and harvest date.</li>
<li><strong>Retail / local markets:</strong> airtight glass jars, stand-up pouches with zip locks, or sealed kraft paper bags with a window.</li>
<li><strong>Export:</strong> vacuum-sealed or nitrogen-flushed food-grade bags inside cartons, with phytosanitary and traceability documentation.</li>
</ul>
<p>Always label with: herb name, botanical name, net weight, harvest date, best-before date, origin, and producer contact. Store in a cool, dark, dry room away from chemicals, fuel, and livestock. Rotate stock (first in, first out).</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Harvest in the early morning after dew dries; never harvest wet plants.</li>
<li>Use clean, sharp tools and harvest at peak oil/flavour stage.</li>
<li>Remove damaged leaves, thick stems, roots, and foreign matter before drying.</li>
<li>Match drying method to herb and climate shade for delicate leaves, solar dryer for humid zones.</li>
<li>Dry in single layers on mesh trays; never on the ground or bare metal.</li>
<li>Test for dryness (snap, crush, weight, or moisture meter) target 812% moisture.</li>
<li>Discard any mouldy or musty batch immediately aflatoxin risk.</li>
<li>Package in airtight, food-grade containers; label with harvest date and lot number.</li>
</ol>
