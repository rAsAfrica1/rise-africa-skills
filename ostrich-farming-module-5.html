1. Common Diseases: Avian Influenza, Newcastle, Enteritis

Ostriches in Africa face three major viral and bacterial diseases that can wipe out your flock if you don't act fast. Avian Influenza (bird flu) is caused by influenza A viruses. It spreads through wild birds, contaminated water, and equipment. Symptoms include sudden death, swollen heads, purple wattles, and respiratory distress. In Africa, outbreaks are common near wetlands where wild waterfowl gather. There is no treatment; cull infected birds and report to authorities immediately. Newcastle disease is a paramyxovirus that attacks the respiratory, nervous, and digestive systems. Look for gasping, twisted necks, paralysis, and greenish diarrhoea. Mortality can reach 90% in unvaccinated flocks. Vaccinate with live or killed vaccinesfollow the manufacturer's schedule. Enteritis is often bacterial (E. coli, Salmonella) or parasitic. It causes watery droppings, dehydration, and weight loss. Common triggers are dirty water, mouldy feed, and overcrowding. Treat with antibiotics like oxytetracycline (20 mg/kg body weight for 5 days) only under vet advice. Prevent by cleaning drinkers daily, using chlorinated water (2-3 ppm), and rotating pastures. In Africa, heat stress worsens these diseases. Provide shade and cool water. Isolate new birds for 30 days. Quarantine sick ones immediately. Keep records of all deaths and symptoms. Early detection saves moneya single outbreak can cost you thousands of dollars.

2. Vaccination Programs and Medications

Vaccination is your cheapest insurance against deadly ostrich diseases. In Africa, core vaccines are Newcastle, Avian Influenza (where permitted), and Fowl Pox. Start chicks at day 1 with Marek's vaccine if available. At 2 weeks, give Newcastle (live B1 strain) via eye drop or drinking water. Repeat at 6 weeks with La Sota strain. For layers and breeders, boost every 3 months. Avian Influenza vaccine is not allowed in many African countriescheck with your vet. Fowl Pox vaccine is given at 8 weeks by wing web stab. Always use clean equipment: boil syringes for 10 minutes, use one needle per 10 birds. For medications, keep a farm kit: oxytetracycline (broad-spectrum antibiotic), amprolium (for coccidiosis), ivermectin (for parasites), and electrolytes. Dosages: oxytetracycline 20 mg/kg for 5 days in water; amprolium 1 g per 2 litres of water for 5 days; ivermectin 0.2 mg/kg injected under skin. Never use antibiotics without vet adviceresistance and residues harm your business. Store vaccines at 2-8C in a fridge, not freezer. Protect from sunlight. Record every vaccination: date, vaccine name, batch number, and bird age. In remote areas, organise a vaccine day with neighbours to share costs. A well-vaccinated flock grows faster and lays more eggs. Budget about $0.10 per bird per vaccine dose. That's small compared to losing your whole flock.

3. Parasite Control: Internal and External

Ostriches suffer from both internal and external parasites, especially in warm, humid African climates. Internal parasites include roundworms (Ascaridia), tapeworms, and coccidia. Signs: poor growth, dull feathers, diarrhoea, and worms in droppings. Deworm every 3 months with fenbendazole (10 mg/kg orally) or ivermectin (0.2 mg/kg under skin). For coccidia, use amprolium (1 g per 2 L water for 5 days) or sulfa drugs. Rotate pastures to break the life cyclerest each paddock for 30 days. Keep feed and water off the ground. External parasites are ticks, mites, lice, and fleas. Ticks transmit diseases like avian spirochaetosis. Check under wings, around the vent, and on the neck weekly. Treat with permethrin spray (0.5% solution) or dust with diatomaceous earth. For severe infestations, use ivermectin injection (0.2 mg/kg) every 2 weeks for 3 treatments. Mites cause feather loss and anaemia. Clean and spray houses with 2% caustic soda or commercial acaricide. In Africa, tick control is criticaluse tick grease on ears and around the vent. Keep grass short around pens. Introduce guinea fowl or chickens? Nothey carry diseases. Instead, use rotational grazing with cattle? No, ostriches are separate. Quarantine new birds and treat them for parasites before mixing. Record all treatments: date, drug, dose, and withdrawal period (meat: 14 days for ivermectin; eggs: 7 days). Never underdoseit creates resistant parasites. A parasite-free flock gains 20% more weight.

4. Recognizing Signs of Illness and Injury

Ostriches hide illness until they are very sicka survival instinct. You must observe your flock daily, ideally at dawn and dusk. Healthy ostriches are alert, eat well, and move actively. Sick ones separate from the group, stand hunched, or lie down often. Key signs: drooping wings, closed eyes, pale comb/wattles, and laboured breathing. Check droppings: normal is firm, dark green with white urate. Watery, bloody, or yellow droppings mean trouble. Injuries are common from fighting, fences, or predators. Look for limping, swollen joints, cuts, or bleeding. A broken leg is obviousthe bird cannot stand. For wounds, clean with saline or diluted iodine (1:10). Apply antibiotic spray and bandage. For fractures, immobilise with splint and call a vet. Other signs: weight loss (weigh monthly), reduced egg production, soft-shelled eggs, and unusual behaviour like head shaking or circling. Take temperature: normal is 39-40C. Above 41C indicates fever. In Africa, heat stress causes panting, wing spreading, and collapse. Move birds to shade, spray water, and add electrolytes. Keep a notebook: record any abnormality, date, and action. Early isolation of sick birds prevents spread. If more than 3 birds show same signs in 24 hours, call a vet immediately. Remember: a ostrich that is down and cannot rise is an emergencyit may die within hours. Act fast.

5. First Aid and Basic Veterinary Procedures

First aid saves lives before the vet arrives. Keep a farm kit: sterile gloves, saline, iodine, antibiotic powder, bandages, scissors, tweezers, syringes (1ml, 5ml, 10ml), needles (18G, 21G), and a thermometer. For bleeding wounds, apply direct pressure with clean cloth for 5 minutes. Clean with saline, apply iodine, then antibiotic powder. Bandage looselytoo tight cuts circulation. For broken bones, splint with a stick and vet wrap. Do not try to set a fracture yourself; immobilise and call for help. For heat stress, move bird to shade, pour cool water on legs and neck, and give electrolytes (1 sachet per 5 L water) via crop tube if needed. For poisoning (e.g., from mouldy feed), give activated charcoal (1 g/kg body weight) mixed in water. For bloat (common after eating too much lucerne), pass a stomach tube to release gasonly if trained. Injections: intramuscular (IM) in breast muscle, subcutaneous (SC) under loose skin on neck. Use 18G needle for IM, 21G for SC. Always aspirate before injecting IMif blood appears, withdraw and reposition. For oral medications, use a crop tube or syringe without needle at the side of the beak. Restrain birds by placing a hood over the head or holding wings close to body. Never lift an ostrich by the neck. Record all treatments. If you are unsure, do not guesscall a vet. Basic first aid can reduce mortality by 30% on your farm.

6. Working with Veterinarians and Diagnostic Labs

A good veterinarian is your partner in ostrich farming. In Africa, find a vet with poultry or ratite experience. Contact your local veterinary office or agricultural extension officer for referrals. Establish a relationship before an emergencyinvite the vet for a farm visit once a year. Keep a file with your vet's phone number, the nearest diagnostic lab, and a map to your farm. When a disease outbreak occurs, call the vet immediately. Describe symptoms, number of affected birds, and recent changes (feed, weather, new birds). Collect samples for diagnosis: blood (2 ml in EDTA tube), swabs from throat/cloaca, and fresh droppings. For dead birds, submit the whole carcass in a sealed plastic bag on icenever freeze. Diagnostic labs test for Avian Influenza, Newcastle, Salmonella, and parasites. Results take 2-7 days. While waiting, isolate sick birds and disinfect equipment. In remote areas, use mobile labs or send samples via courier. Costs: basic blood test $10-20, PCR for viruses $30-50. It's worth itwrong treatment wastes money and kills birds. Work with the vet to create a health plan: vaccination schedule, deworming calendar, and biosecurity rules. Biosecurity: footbaths at entry, limit visitors, and quarantine new birds for 30 days. Keep records of all vet visits, prescriptions, and lab results. Share them with your vet for better advice. A strong vet-farmer relationship reduces losses and builds a sustainable ostrich business.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Ostrich Health & Disease Management for African Farms

Ostrich farming in Africa is a high-value, high-risk business. A single disease outbreak can wipe out an entire flock's profitability not just in mortality, but in lost sales, quarantine costs, and reputational damage with buyers. This practical reference brings together the six lessons in this module into one field-ready document you can print, laminate, and keep in the farm office or vet room. It is written for farm managers, not veterinarians, and focuses on decisions that protect cash flow.

Why business-first? Because in African ostrich production, the margins are tight. Feed, labour, and transport already consume most of the revenue per bird. A preventable Newcastle outbreak or a chronic parasite burden can push a farm from profit to loss within one season. The information below is designed to help you act early, document properly, and work efficiently with veterinary professionals and diagnostic labs.

1. Common Diseases at a Glance

Ostriches are susceptible to several diseases that also affect poultry, but their size, stress sensitivity, and handling requirements make outbreaks harder to control. The three most economically damaging in African conditions are Avian Influenza, Newcastle Disease, and Enteritis complex.

Avian Influenza (AI): Highly pathogenic strains (H5N1, H7N9) cause sudden death, respiratory distress, and drop in egg production. Ostriches are particularly susceptible to H5N1 and can die within 2448 hours. In Africa, wild waterfowl are the main reservoir. Any suspicion of AI must be reported immediately to national veterinary authorities it is a notifiable disease in every African country.

Newcastle Disease: A viral disease with rapid spread. Symptoms include twisting of the neck (torticollis), paralysis, greenish diarrhoea, and respiratory difficulty. Mortality can reach 80100% in unvaccinated flocks. It is also notifiable.

Enteritis complex: Caused by bacteria (E. coli, Salmonella, Clostridium), parasites (coccidia), or nutritional factors. Symptoms: watery or bloody droppings, dehydration, weight loss, and sudden death in chicks. Often secondary to stress, poor hygiene, or contaminated water.

Vaccination is the cheapest insurance in ostrich farming. A single Newcastle outbreak can cost more than 10 years of vaccine. 3. Parasite Control: Internal and External

Parasites are a silent profit killer. They reduce feed conversion, slow growth, and increase mortality. In African ostrich farms, the most common internal parasites are Libyostrongylus douglassii (wireworm), Houttuynia struthionis (tapeworm), and coccidia. External parasites include ticks, lice, and mites.

RED SAFETY WARNING Notifiable Diseases

Avian Influenza and Newcastle Disease are notifiable in all African countries. If you suspect either, you are legally required to report to your national veterinary authority immediately. Do NOT move birds, sell meat, or transport eggs. Failure to report can result in fines, farm closure, and criminal liability. Keep the national veterinary hotline number posted in your farm office.

4. Recognizing Signs of Illness and Injury

Ostriches mask illness until they are severely affected a survival instinct. Your best tool is daily observation. Walk the flock at the same times each day and look for deviations from normal behaviour.

  • Behaviour: Isolation from flock, reluctance to move, head down, drooping wings.
  • Appetite & water: Sudden drop in feed intake or excessive water consumption.
  • Droppings: Watery, bloody, greenish, or containing undigested feed.
  • Respiration: Open-mouth breathing, sneezing, nasal discharge, rattling sounds.
  • Physical: Swollen joints, limping, wounds, feather loss, eye discharge.
  • Egg production: Sudden drop or soft-shelled/misshapen eggs in breeders.

Any bird showing two or more of these signs should be isolated immediately in a quarantine pen and examined by your veterinarian. Record the date, pen number, and symptoms this data is gold for diagnostic labs.

5. First Aid and Basic Veterinary Procedures

You are not a vet, but you can save a bird's life and protect your flock with basic first aid:

  • Isolation pen: Keep a dedicated, easy-to-clean pen for sick or injured birds, away from the main flock.
  • Wound care: Clean minor wounds with saline or dilute iodine; apply antiseptic; cover if needed. Watch for fly strike (myiasis) in warm climates.
  • Heat stress: Move bird to shade, provide cool water, wet the legs and underbelly. Ostriches are highly susceptible to heat stress.
  • Fractures: Immobilise the limb and call the vet immediately. Do not attempt to set a fracture without training.
  • Sample collection: Learn to collect faecal samples for parasite counts and blood samples (if trained) for disease testing. Label clearly with date, bird ID, and farm name.
  • Record keeping: Every treatment, vaccine, and death must be logged. This protects your business and helps your vet diagnose patterns.

6. Working with Veterinarians and Diagnostic Labs

A good relationship with a vet and a diagnostic lab is an investment, not a cost. In many African countries, government veterinary services offer subsidised or free diagnostic testing for notifiable diseases. Private labs are faster but cost more.

  • Find your vet before you need one. Identify a vet with poultry or ratite experience in your area. Keep their number on the farm wall.
  • Build a diagnostic file: Keep a folder with farm history, vaccination records, and previous lab results. Vets work faster with context.
  • Use official labs: For AI and Newcastle, only national reference labs can confirm. Private labs can screen for parasites and bacteria.
  • Submit properly: Use correct sample containers, keep samples cool (not frozen unless instructed), and include a clear submission form.
  • Act on results: A lab report without action is wasted money. Review results with your vet and adjust your health plan.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Daily walk-through: observe behaviour, droppings, feed and water intake.
  2. Isolate any sick or injured bird immediately in a quarantine pen.
  3. Vaccinate on schedule Newcastle is non-negotiable.
  4. Deworm every 68 weeks; do faecal egg counts to confirm effectiveness.
  5. Control external parasites with approved acaricides/insecticides.
  6. Keep footbaths and visitor logs at every entry point.
  7. Report suspected AI or Newcastle to veterinary authorities immediately.
  8. Log every treatment, vaccine, and death review monthly with your vet.
