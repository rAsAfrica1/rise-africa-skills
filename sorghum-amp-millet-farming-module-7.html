1. Timing first and second weeding for sorghum and millet

Weeding at the right time is the cheapest way to double your sorghum or millet yield. The first weeding must be done when the crop is 2 to 3 weeks old, or when the weeds are still small and before they compete for water and nutrients. At this stage, the sorghum or millet seedlings are fragile, so use a hand hoe carefully, shallow weeding to avoid damaging roots. If you delay beyond 4 weeks, weeds like striga and grasses will already have stolen nutrients, and you will lose up to 50% of your harvest. In dry areas, the first weeding also breaks the soil surface, helping rainwater enter the soil.

The second weeding should be done 5 to 6 weeks after planting, just before the crop starts to shoot up and close the canopy. At this time, the plants are taller and stronger, so you can use an oxen weeder or a hand hoe with more speed. The goal is to remove any weeds that survived the first weeding and to stop new flushes of weeds from setting seed. If you miss the second weeding, weeds will compete during the critical grain-filling stage, and your heads will be small and light.

In areas with heavy rains, weeds grow faster, so you may need a third weeding around 8 weeks. But for most African smallholders, two well-timed weedings are enough if you also plant in rows and keep the field clean. Always weed when the soil is moist but not waterlogged; dry soil makes hoeing hard, and wet soil can cause compaction. Use a sharp hoe to cut weeds at the base, and shake soil off the roots so they don't re-grow. Never let weeds go to seedâone striga plant can produce 50,000 seeds that stay in the soil for 20 years.

Mark your calendar: first weeding at 2-3 weeks, second at 5-6 weeks. Train your family or hired labour to recognise the crop from the weeds. In sorghum, young plants have a reddish base and broader leaves; in millet, they are thin and grassy. If you intercrop with cowpea or groundnut, weed around the legume too, but avoid deep hoeing near their roots. Timely weeding is hard work, but it is the single most important thing you can do to protect your harvest.

2. Using hand hoes, oxen weeders, and selective herbicides safely

For most smallholder sorghum and millet farmers in Africa, the hand hoe is the main weeding tool. A sharp, well-balanced hoe (like the common 'jembe' in East Africa) lets you cut weeds just below the soil surface without pulling up crop roots. Keep your hoe sharp by filing the blade every few days. When weeding, stand with your feet apart, swing the hoe with short strokes, and aim to slice the weed stem at ground level. For young sorghum (2-3 weeks), weed only 2-3 cm deep to avoid cutting crop roots. For older plants, you can go deeper. Wear closed shoes to protect your feet from the blade.

Oxen weeders are faster and cheaper for larger fields (1 hectare or more). They are pulled by a pair of trained oxen and have a V-shaped blade that cuts weeds between the rows. You must plant your sorghum or millet in rows spaced 75-90 cm apart so the oxen can pass without stepping on the crop. Guide the oxen slowly and steadily; do not let the blade go too deep or it will uproot the crop. Oxen weeding works best when weeds are small and the soil is slightly moist. Train your oxen for a few days before the actual weeding. After weeding, check behind the oxen and remove any weeds that were missed, especially around the crop plants.

Selective herbicides can save labour, but they must be used correctly. For sorghum, you can use atrazine (pre-emergence) or 2,4-D (post-emergence) but only if the sorghum is at the right stageâ2,4-D should be applied when sorghum is 15-30 cm tall and before it flowers, otherwise it will damage the crop. For millet, atrazine is also used, but millet is more sensitive, so follow the label exactly. Always wear protective clothing: long sleeves, trousers, gloves, boots, and a face mask. Mix the herbicide in a bucket away from water sources. Use a calibrated knapsack sprayer and spray in the early morning or late evening when there is no wind. Never spray when it is raining or about to rain. Keep children and animals away from sprayed fields for at least 24 hours. Store herbicides in a locked box, away from food and water. If you feel dizzy or sick after spraying, stop immediately and seek medical help. Remember, herbicides are poisonâuse them only as a last resort after hand and oxen weeding.

3. Identifying and controlling striga weed in sorghum fields

Striga, also called witchweed, is the worst enemy of sorghum in Africa. It is a small, pretty plant with bright pink or purple flowers, but it is a parasite. Striga attaches its roots to the sorghum roots and sucks out water and nutrients. You will first notice stunted sorghum plants with yellow, wilted leaves, even when there is enough rain. Later, you see the striga plants themselvesâthey grow 15-30 cm tall, with narrow leaves and flowers. One striga plant can produce up to 50,000 tiny seeds that stay in the soil for up to 20 years. That is why striga is so hard to control once it is in your field.

The best control is prevention. Plant striga-resistant sorghum varieties, such as 'Gadam' or 'Serena' in East Africa, or 'Sariaso' in West Africa. These varieties produce less striga germination stimulant. Also, rotate your sorghum with non-host crops like cowpea, groundnut, or cotton for at least two seasons. These crops do not feed striga, so the striga seeds die out. Intercropping sorghum with cowpea or groundnut also reduces striga because the legumes shade the soil and release chemicals that suppress striga. Never plant sorghum after sorghum in a striga-infested field.

If you already have striga, hand-pull it before it flowers. Pull the entire plant, including the roots, and burn it or feed it to animals (but not pigs, as striga can pass through and spread). Do this every week during the growing season. Do not compost striga. You can also apply nitrogen fertiliser (like urea) at 50 kg per hectareâthis helps the sorghum grow stronger and tolerate striga. Some farmers use a trap crop like cotton or soybean, which makes striga seeds germinate but then die because they cannot attach. After the trap crop, you can plant sorghum. Another method is to apply a small amount of nitrogen and then cover the soil with mulch to reduce light for striga seedlings. Always clean your tools and boots when moving from a striga field to a clean field. Striga is a community problemâwork with your neighbours to control it on all farms, or it will come back.

4. Managing stem borers, armyworms, and millet head miner

Stem borers are moth larvae that bore into sorghum and millet stems. You will see small holes in the stem, and the central shoot may die, a condition called 'dead heart'. The larvae are creamy white or pinkish, 1-2 cm long. They feed inside the stem, so by the time you see damage, it is often too late. To control them, plant early and uniformly with your neighbours, so the moths do not have a continuous food supply. Remove and burn crop residues after harvest, because borers hide in them. You can also release natural enemies like Trichogramma wasps, which lay their eggs inside borer eggs. If you see egg masses on leaves, crush them by hand. For severe infestations, apply a recommended insecticide like carbaryl or a biopesticide like Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) into the leaf whorl. Always follow label instructions.

Armyworms are caterpillars that march in large numbers and can strip a field overnight. They are green, brown, or black, with stripes along the body, and they curl up when touched. Young armyworms (before they are 1.5 cm long) are easiest to kill. Scout your field twice a week, especially in the morning or evening. Look for chewed leaves and small holes. If you find 2-3 armyworms per plant, act immediately. Hand-pick them and drop them in soapy water. You can also spray with neem extract (see Lesson 6) or with a commercial insecticide like lambda-cyhalothrin. For small fields, dig a trench 30 cm deep around the field and fill it with water and a little soap; armyworms will fall in and drown. Encourage birds and ants, which eat armyworms.

Millet head miner is a small moth whose larvae attack millet heads. You will see small holes in the head and frass (sawdust-like waste). The larvae feed on the developing grains, reducing yield. To control it, plant early and harvest as soon as the crop is mature. Remove and burn infested heads. You can also use a botanical pesticide made from neem or tobacco. Some farmers use wood ash dusted on the heads to repel the moths. If you use chemical insecticides, apply them when the moths are flying, before they lay eggs. Always wear protective clothing. Rotate crops with legumes to break the pest cycle. Keep your field clean and remove wild grasses that can host these pests.

5. Controlling birds (quelea) with scaring techniques

Quelea birds are small, sparrow-like birds that gather in huge flocksâsometimes millionsâand can eat an entire sorghum or millet field in a few hours. They are especially bad in the Sahel and East Africa. You will see them swooping down in clouds, landing on the heads, and pecking out the grain. The best time to act is when the grain is in the milk or dough stage, as that is when quelea prefer it. Once the grain is hard, they are less interested, but they can still cause damage.

Scaring is the main control method for smallholders. Start early in the morning and continue until sunset. Use a combination of methods: shout, clap, and throw stones (but not at the birdsâjust to make noise). Tie bright cloths or plastic strips to long poles and wave them. Use reflective tape or old CDs hung on strings across the field; the flashing light scares the birds. You can also make a simple scarecrow with old clothes stuffed with straw, but move it every few days or the birds get used to it. A more effective method is to use a 'bird scarer' made from a tin can with small stones inside; shake it loudly. In some communities, farmers take turns to patrol the fields, making noise from different directions.

For larger fields, you can use a propane cannon or a recorded distress call of quelea played over a loudspeaker. These are more expensive but can be shared by a group. Some farmers use nets to catch quelea, but this is labour-intensive. Never use poison bait for queleaâit kills other birds and animals and is illegal in many countries. Also, do not kill quelea indiscriminately; they are part of the ecosystem. The most sustainable approach is to plant early so your crop matures before the quelea flocks arrive in large numbers. Also, plant a small 'sacrificial' plot of sorghum or millet away from your main field to attract the birds. Harvest as soon as the crop is readyâdo not leave it in the field. Work with your neighbours to scare birds together, because if you scare them from your field, they will just go to your neighbour's. Community action is key.

6. Organic pest control: neem extract, ash, and wood ash

Organic pest control uses materials you already have on your farm. It is cheap, safe for you and the environment, and does not leave harmful residues. The three most common methods for sorghum and millet are neem extract, ash, and wood ash.

Neem extract: Neem trees grow in many parts of Africa. To make a spray, collect 1 kg of neem leaves (or 500 g of neem seeds) and soak them in 10 litres of water for 24 hours. Strain the liquid through a cloth. Add a small amount of soap (about 1 tablespoon) to help the spray stick to the plants. Pour into a knapsack sprayer and spray your sorghum or millet every 7-10 days when you see pests like armyworms, stem borers, or aphids. Neem works as a repellent and growth regulatorâit stops insects from feeding and moulting. It is not a quick kill, so apply before pest populations get large. You can also dry neem leaves, crush them into powder, and dust them on the plants. Store the powder in a dry place.

Ash: Wood ash from cooking fires is a good insect repellent. Dust ash directly on the leaves and stems of your crop, especially where you see pests. The ash clogs the breathing pores of insects and also adds potassium to the soil. For armyworms, mix ash with a little sand and sprinkle it in the leaf whorls. For stem borers, apply ash into the whorl. You can also mix ash with water (1 cup ash per 5 litres of water), let it settle, and spray the clear liquid. This works well against soft-bodied insects like aphids and caterpillars. Do not use ash from charcoal that contains chemicals or from treated wood.

Wood ash and neem can be combined for stronger effect. For example, mix neem powder with wood ash (1 part neem to 3 parts ash) and dust on millet heads to control head miner. Another organic method is to plant marigolds or other repellent plants around your field. Always test any spray on a few plants first to make sure it does not burn the leaves. Apply in the early morning or late evening to avoid the hot sun. Remember, organic control works best when you scout regularly and act early. It is not a one-time solutionâyou must be consistent. But it protects your health, your soil, and your harvest.

Practical Reference: Weeding, Pest, and Disease Control in Sorghum & Millet

Weeds, insects, and birds can cut sorghum and millet yields by 30â70% if left uncontrolled. For African smallholders and emerging agribusinesses, the margin between profit and loss often comes down to how well you execute weeding and pest control in the first 45 days after planting. This practical reference gives you field-ready protocols, cost-conscious tool choices, and safety standards that protect both your crop and your workers. Treat this as your operational checklist for the season.

Why Timing Is Everything

Sorghum and millet are slow starters. During the first three to four weeks, seedlings are short and their root systems are shallow. Weeds â especially Striga, Echinochloa, and broadleaf species â emerge faster and compete aggressively for water, nitrogen, and sunlight. Research from ICRISAT shows that the critical period for weed competition in sorghum is the first 30â45 days after emergence. Miss that window and you lose yield potential that no later weeding can recover.

The first weeding should happen 10â15 days after emergence, when weeds are still small and easily uprooted. The second weeding follows 25â35 days after emergence, before the crop canopy closes. In millet, which is often planted on poorer soils, the same timing applies but weeds can be even more damaging because millet seedlings are thinner and less competitive.

Tool Selection: Hand Hoes, Oxen Weeders, and Herbicides

Your tool choice depends on farm size, labour availability, and cash flow. For 0.5â2 hectares, hand hoes remain the most cost-effective option, but labour costs are rising across Africa. For 2â10 hectares, oxen-drawn weeders (such as the Mandava or Bongolava types) can reduce weeding time by 60â70% compared to hand hoeing. For larger operations or where labour is scarce, selective herbicides offer a viable alternative â but only if you follow dosage and safety rules strictly.

Striga: The Silent Yield Killer

Striga hermonthica and Striga asiatica are parasitic weeds that attach to sorghum and millet roots, stealing water and nutrients. A heavy infestation can cause 40â80% yield loss. Striga thrives in poor, nitrogen-deficient soils and is spread through contaminated seed and farm equipment.

Control strategy: Combine tolerant varieties (e.g., Striga-resistant sorghum lines from ICRISAT), crop rotation with legumes (cowpea, groundnut), nitrogen application, and hand-pulling before Striga flowers. Never let Striga set seed â one plant can produce 50,000â500,000 seeds that remain viable in soil for 15â20 years. For heavy infestations, apply ammonium sulphate or urea at 50â100 kg/ha, which suppresses Striga germination. Organic options include intercropping with cowpea or applying neem cake at 200â400 kg/ha.

Bird Control: Quelea Quelea

The red-billed quelea is the world's most numerous wild bird and a devastating pest of millet and sorghum in the Sahel. A single flock can consume 10â20 tonnes of grain per day. Control methods include scaring (flags, reflective tape, noise), early harvesting, and â where legally permitted â avicides. For most smallholders, scaring techniques are the most practical: deploy 3â5 scare devices per hectare, rotate their positions every 2â3 days, and combine with community-wide scaring campaigns. Quelea roost in acacia thickets; community roost destruction (with government permission) can reduce populations significantly.

Organic Pest Control: Neem, Ash, and Wood Ash

Neem extract is a proven antifeedant and growth regulator. Crush 500 g of neem seed kernel, soak in 10 litres of water for 24 hours, strain, and spray every 7â10 days. Wood ash dusted on leaves deters armyworms and aphids. For soil pests, incorporate ash at 200â300 kg/ha. These methods are cheap, safe, and effective for low-to-moderate infestations â but they are not a substitute for integrated pest management when pressure is high.

Follow the label dosage exactly â overdosing kills your crop and contaminates water sources. Use them only as a last resort within an integrated pest management plan.
