1. How TIG Works on Aluminium

TIG welding uses a non-consumable tungsten electrode to create an arc that melts the aluminium. Unlike steel, aluminium conducts heat away fast, so you need higher amperage. A 3mm thick aluminium plate needs about 120-150 amps. You also need 100% argon gas, not CO2. The arc breaks the tough aluminium oxide layer, which melts at 2072C while the aluminium itself melts at 660C. Without the arc, the oxide would float on the molten pool and cause defects. You add filler rod manually with your other hand. In Africa, many workshops use inverter TIG machines like the ESAB or local brands. Set gas flow to 10-15 litres per minute. Use a 2% lanthanated tungsten electrode (grey tip) for AC welding. Keep a short arc lengthabout 2-3mm. If you hear a crackling sound, your amperage is too low. If the pool looks dirty and black, you need more cleaning action (more AC balance). Practice on scrap aluminium before welding a real job. Clean the metal with a stainless steel brush dedicated to aluminium, then wipe with acetone. Never use a brush that touched steel. TIG on aluminium is slower than MIG but gives better control for thin sheets and repairs. In many African countries, small fabrication shops use TIG for window frames, water tanks, and motorcycle parts. The key is to watch the molten pool, not the arc. The pool should look shiny and fluid. If it looks like mercury, you are too cold. If it boils and spits, you are too hot. Adjust your foot pedal or machine settings accordingly.

2. AC vs DC and Why It Matters

Aluminium needs alternating current (AC) for TIG welding. DC (direct current) is for steel and stainless. Why? Because aluminium forms a hard oxide layer that melts at 2072C, while the metal below melts at 660C. AC switches polarity 50 or 60 times per second. On the positive half-cycle, the arc blasts the oxide away (cleaning action). On the negative half-cycle, it penetrates and melts the base metal. If you use DC on aluminium, the oxide stays and your weld looks like a dirty mess. Most TIG machines have an AC/DC switch. Set it to AC. Then adjust the AC balance or cleaning width. On older machines, this is a knob from 0 to 10. On modern inverters, it's a percentage. Start at 30% cleaning (70% penetration). If the weld is black and sooty, increase cleaning to 40-50%. If the tungsten ball gets too big and the arc wanders, reduce cleaning to 20-25%. The frequency control (if available) sets how fast AC alternates. Higher frequency (100-200 Hz) gives a tighter arc for thin metal. Lower frequency (50-60 Hz) gives a wider arc for thick sections. For most African workshops, 60 Hz is fine. Note: you cannot weld aluminium with a DC-only machine. If your machine only has DC, you need a spool gun MIG or a different machine. Many cheap TIG machines sold in local markets are DC-only. Check before you buy. A good AC/DC TIG machine for a small shop costs around $800-$1500. Brands like Miller, Lincoln, or Chinese equivalents like Andeli or Riland work. Always use pure argon gas. Argon/helium mixes can help on thick aluminium (over 6mm) but are expensive and hard to find in many African countries. Stick to pure argon.

3. Choosing Tungsten Electrodes

For aluminium TIG, you need a tungsten electrode that handles AC without splitting. The best choice is 2% lanthanated (grey tip) or 2% ceriated (orange tip). Pure tungsten (green tip) also works but balls up faster and can't handle high amps. Thoriated tungsten (red tip) is for DC steel weldingdo not use it on aluminium because it spits and contaminates the weld. Choose the diameter based on amperage: 1.6mm for 15-80 amps, 2.4mm for 70-150 amps, 3.2mm for 140-250 amps. For most aluminium jobs in a small shop (3-6mm thick), use 2.4mm lanthanated. You need to ball the tip before welding. Set your machine to AC, set amperage to about 100-120, and strike an arc on a scrap piece of aluminium for 2-3 seconds. The tip will melt into a smooth ball. The ball should be about 1.5 times the electrode diameter. If the ball is too big, reduce cleaning action or lower amperage. If the ball is tiny, increase amperage. Keep the tungsten sharpened on a dedicated grinder. Use a fine grit (100-200) diamond wheel or a dedicated stone. Never use the same grinder for steel because contamination causes porosity. Sharpen lengthwise, not across. In Africa, you can buy lanthanated tungsten from welding supply shops in major cities like Lagos, Nairobi, Johannesburg, or Cairo. A pack of 10 electrodes costs $15-$30. Store them in a dry place. If the tip gets contaminated (dips into the pool), stop, break off the dirty end, re-ball, and continue. A contaminated tungsten will cause a wandering arc and dirty weld. Always keep spares.

4. Filler Rods for Aluminium

Aluminium filler rods come in different alloys. The most common for general welding is 4043 (5% silicon). It melts at a lower temperature (580-620C) and flows well, giving a shiny weld. It's good for casting repairs and most 6000-series aluminium. But 4043 is softer and not heat-treatable. For structural work or 5000-series aluminium (like 5052 or 5083), use 5356 (5% magnesium). It's stronger and more corrosion-resistant but harder to feed and can crack if used on high-silicon castings. For 7000-series, use 4043 or 5356 depending on the base. In Africa, 4043 is the easiest to find and cheapest. A 1kg pack of 2.4mm rods costs $10-$20. For most jobs, use a rod diameter that matches your base metal thickness. For 3mm aluminium, use 2.4mm rod. For 6mm, use 3.2mm rod. Feed the rod into the leading edge of the pool at a 15-20 angle. Don't dip it into the arcthat causes spatter and contamination. Push the rod in, then pull it out slightly. The rod should melt and flow into the pool. If the rod sticks, you're too cold. If it balls up and doesn't flow, you're too hot. Clean the rod with acetone before use. Store rods in a dry place. Never use filler rod for steel or stainless on aluminium. Keep separate. For thin aluminium (1-2mm), you can sometimes weld without filler (autogenous) if the fit-up is tight. But for most repairs, use filler. In many African workshops, welders use 4043 for everything. That works for non-critical jobs, but for structural parts like truck frames or boat hulls, use 5356. Always check the base metal grade if you can. If unknown, 4043 is a safe default.

5. Setting Up Your Machine

Set up your TIG machine correctly before you strike an arc. First, connect the ground clamp to a clean, bare metal part of your workpiece. For aluminium, grind off any paint or oxide where the clamp touches. Use a good quality clampcheap ones overheat. Next, connect the torch. For AC TIG, the torch cable goes to the negative terminal (on most machines) and the ground to positive. But check your manualsome inverters are different. Set the machine to AC. Set amperage based on thickness: 1.6mm = 60-80A, 3mm = 120-150A, 6mm = 200-250A. If you have a foot pedal, use it to control heat. If not, set amperage and use a switch. Set gas flow to 10-15 litres per minute (LPM). Use a flowmeter, not just a regulator. Too little gas gives porosity; too much causes turbulence and sucks in air. Set AC balance to 30% cleaning (or 70% penetration). If your machine has frequency, set to 60 Hz for thick, 100-150 Hz for thin. Install a 2.4mm lanthanated tungsten, ball it on scrap. Set the torch angle at 70-80 from the workpiece, pushing slightly forward. For filler, use 2.4mm 4043 rod. Wear a good helmet with shade 10-11 for aluminium (brighter than steel). Use clean glovesdirty gloves contaminate. In Africa, power can be unstable. Use a voltage stabiliser or a generator if needed. A 200A TIG machine draws about 5-6 kW. Make sure your wiring can handle it. Never bypass the safety ground. Practice on scrap before welding a real part. Adjust settings as you go. If the weld is too cold, increase amps or reduce cleaning. If too hot, do the opposite.

6. Your First Bead on Aluminium

Before you weld a real part, practice on a scrap piece of 3mm aluminium. Clean it with a stainless brush and acetone. Set your machine: AC, 120 amps, 30% cleaning, 12 LPM argon, 2.4mm lanthanated tungsten (balled), 2.4mm 4043 filler. Put on your helmet (shade 10), gloves, and long sleeves. Rest your torch hand on the table for stability. Hold the torch at 75 angle, pointing forward. Touch the tungsten to the metal to start the arc (or use high-frequency start if available). Once the arc starts, lift to 2-3mm arc length. Watch the pool form. It should look shiny and fluid. If it looks like a mirror, you're too coldincrease amps. If it boils and spits, you're too hotreduce amps or increase travel speed. Move the torch forward slowly, about 2-3mm per second. Dip the filler rod into the leading edge of the pool, then pull it out. Do this rhythmically: move, dip, move, dip. The bead should be about 4-6mm wide. Don't worry if it's ugly at first. After you finish, let it cool, then inspect. A good bead has even ripples, no holes, no black soot. If you see black soot, increase cleaning action or reduce amperage. If you see holes (porosity), increase gas flow or clean the metal better. Practice for at least an hour on scrap. Then try a butt joint on two pieces of 3mm aluminium. Tack both ends first. Weld from one end to the other. Use a backstep technique (weld 50mm, then start 50mm back) to control distortion. Aluminium warps easily, so clamp your work. In Africa, many welders learn by doing. Don't be afraid to make mistakes. Each bead teaches you something. Keep your tungsten sharp and your metal clean. That's 80% of the battle.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: TIG Welding Aluminium

In an African fabrication workshop, TIG welding aluminium is rarely an academic exercise. It is the difference between winning a contract to build aluminium canopies for a safari fleet, repairing a cracked irrigation pump housing, or fabricating cold-room panels for a food-processing SME and watching that work go to a competitor. The good news: aluminium TIG is learnable, repeatable, and highly profitable once you understand the fundamentals. This module gives you the practical reference you need to set up, weld, and troubleshoot with confidence.

Aluminium behaves differently from steel in ways that matter commercially. It conducts heat about five times faster, it forms an instant oxide layer that melts at roughly 2,072C (compared with aluminium's own melting point of about 660C), and it has no colour change as it approaches melting. That means you cannot rely on the visual cues you learned on mild steel. You must control the process deliberately and that starts with understanding the machine and the setup.

Why AC Is Non-Negotiable for Aluminium

Alternating current (AC) is the standard for TIG welding aluminium because it alternates between two essential functions. On the electrode-positive half-cycle, the arc bombards the workpiece with positive ions, breaking up the tenacious aluminium oxide layer this is called "cleaning action." On the electrode-negative half-cycle, the workpiece receives more heat and the tungsten electrode is cooled, which is called "penetration action." Direct current electrode negative (DCEN) gives deep penetration but no oxide cleaning; direct current electrode positive (DCEP) cleans well but overheats the tungsten and gives shallow penetration. AC balances both. Modern inverter machines also offer adjustable AC frequency and balance control, which lets you fine-tune the cleaning-to-penetration ratio for thin sheet or thick plate.

For a small workshop in Nairobi, Lagos, or Johannesburg, the practical implication is simple: if you are buying a TIG machine for aluminium, confirm it has AC output. A DC-only TIG set will weld steel and stainless beautifully but will frustrate you on aluminium and that frustration costs money.

Choosing Tungsten Electrodes

For AC TIG on aluminium, pure tungsten (green) was historically the standard because it forms a rounded ball tip that handles the alternating polarity well. However, modern inverter machines perform better with ceriated (grey, EWCe-2) or lanthanated (gold, EWLa-1.5) tungsten, which maintain a stable tip, arc more easily, and last longer. Thoriated tungsten (red) is generally avoided for AC aluminium because it is radioactive and does not ball as cleanly. In many African markets, ceriated and lanthanated electrodes are now widely available through welding supply distributors in major cities, though pure tungsten remains common in smaller towns.

Grind your tungsten longitudinally (not radially) with a dedicated grinding wheel reserved for tungsten only. Contamination from steel grinding dust is a common cause of arc wander and porosity. For AC, a slightly blunt tip or a small ball is acceptable; for thin sheet, a sharper tip gives better arc control.

Setting Up Your Machine: A Practical Sequence

Whether you are running a 200A inverter from a 5 kVA generator in a rural workshop or a 300A transformer set in an industrial park in Lagos, the setup sequence is the same. First, connect the work clamp directly to clean aluminium never to a painted or anodised surface. Second, set the machine to AC, select the appropriate amperage from Table 1, and set the AC balance to around 70% electrode negative (30% cleaning) as a starting point. Third, set argon flow to the recommended rate; too little causes porosity, too much causes turbulence and porosity. Fourth, prepare the tungsten and filler rod. Fifth, and most importantly, clean the aluminium. Wipe with acetone or a dedicated aluminium cleaner, then stainless-steel brush the joint area with a brush used only for aluminium. Do this immediately before welding oxide reforms within hours.

For businesses, standardising this setup across your team reduces rework. A simple laminated checklist posted at each welding bay pays for itself within weeks.

SAFETY WARNING Aluminium TIG Hazards

Aluminium TIG welding produces intense ultraviolet radiation and ozone. Never weld without a proper welding helmet with shade 1013, leather gloves, and flame-resistant clothing. Aluminium fumes and ozone can cause metal fume fever and respiratory irritation always weld in a well-ventilated area or use fume extraction. Argon is an asphyxiant: in confined spaces it displaces oxygen and can kill without warning. Never weld inside tanks or enclosed rooms without forced ventilation and a gas monitor. Ensure your electrical supply is properly earthed; generator-powered inverters must be bonded correctly. In African workshops where ventilation may be limited, open doors and use fans to move air but do not blow shielding gas away from the weld.

Your First Bead on Aluminium

Start on a scrap piece of 3 mm aluminium plate. Set the machine to about 120A, AC, 70% EN balance, and 10 L/min argon. Strike the arc on a copper or aluminium starting block, not directly on the workpiece, to avoid tungsten contamination. Once the arc is stable, move slowly and watch the weld pool it will look like a shiny, fluid silver puddle. Add filler rod by dipping the tip into the leading edge of the pool, then withdrawing. Do not pull the rod out of the gas shield while it is hot, or it will oxidise. Keep a consistent travel speed and a short arc length (about 23 mm). If the pool becomes too wide or the plate starts to sag, reduce amperage or increase travel speed.

For a business, the first bead is not about perfection it is about repeatability. Train your welders to produce consistent beads on scrap before they touch a customer's job. That discipline reduces scrap, rework, and warranty claims.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Machine set to AC DC will not clean aluminium oxide.
  • Clean the workpiece acetone wipe plus stainless brush immediately before welding.
  • Correct tungsten ceriated or lanthanated, ground longitudinally, dedicated wheel.
  • Right filler rod match to base alloy (Table 2); ER4043 for general work, ER5356 for marine/structural.
  • Argon flow set 815 L/min depending on thickness; check for leaks at the torch.
  • AC balance start at 70% EN / 30% cleaning; adjust for oxide thickness.
  • Work clamp on clean metal never on paint, anodising, or rust.
  • Ventilation and PPE helmet, gloves, fume extraction, and gas monitoring in confined spaces.
