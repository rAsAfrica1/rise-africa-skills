Soybean Farming

The protein contract crop — inoculation as the highest-return act in field farming, population stands, the rust watch, beating the shattering clock, and selling into mills, food trade and the maize–soybean system.

Course Overview

Soybean rode the poultry boom from curiosity to contract crop across Africa's maize belts: 40% protein, 20% oil, feed mills hungry for tonnage, and free nitrogen left behind for the maize that follows. It is also a crop of specific, learnable tricks — and this course teaches them in order of return. First, inoculation: the few-dollar sachet of specialist rhizobium that most African soils lack, worth 30–50%+ on new land, taught as a ten-minute technique with a pink-nodule test to prove it worked. Then the population stand this crop demands, phosphorus as the purchased key, the daylength rule behind released-variety discipline, the rust scouting habit, and the quality war against splits, greens and moulds. Harvest is a race the course names honestly — the shattering clock — and storage splits into grain stock and the fragile, premium-earning seed stock. The business module sells into contracts, traders, the town food trade (with the absolute cooked-soy heat rule) and your own livestock rations, and writes the final plan for the maize–soybean pair, because the system margin beats either crop alone.

What You Will Learn

  • Understand the specialist-rhizobium trap and master seed inoculation with the pink-nodule proof

Month 1: The Protein Crop, the Rhizobium Trap & the Markets
Start

Lesson 1.1 — The protein crop the feed mills are hunting

Soybean is the world's protein workhorse — ~40% protein and 20% oil in one seed — and Africa's fastest-growing field-crop market. The engine is the poultry boom: every broiler and layer ration is built on soybean meal, and most African feed mills import it at painful prices. Add oil pressing, human foods (soy milk, flour, textured 'chunks'), and the crop's nitrogen-fixing gift to the following maize, and soybean has moved from curiosity to contract crop across the continent's maize belts in a decade.

  • Season: 90–130 days by variety; a rains-season row crop for the same land and equipment as maize.
  • Yields: African smallholder average ~1 t/ha; good management with inoculated improved seed reaches 2–3.5 t/ha.
  • Rotation gold: a well-nodulated crop can leave the equivalent of a meaningful nitrogen dressing for next season's maize, plus a clean disease break.

Lesson 1.2 — The one lesson that towers over the rest

Soybean fixes nitrogen ONLY when its roots carry nodules of the right rhizobium bacteria — and here is the trap: the soybean rhizobium is a specialist that most African soils do not naturally contain (unlike cowpea's and groundnut's easy-going partners). An uninoculated soybean crop on new land sits pale, hungry and stunted while its owner blames the seed. The fixes, in order of importance:

  1. Inoculate the seed with soybean-specific rhizobium inoculant at planting — a sachet costing a few dollars that routinely adds 30–50%+ yield on first-time land (Module 2 teaches the technique).
  2. Or plant 'promiscuous' varieties — African-bred lines (many from IITA) that nodulate with native rhizobia; lower ceiling than inoculated specialist varieties, but forgiving where inoculant is unreachable.
  3. Once soybean has grown inoculated on a field for some seasons, the bacteria persist and the response fades — but on NEW land, inoculation is the single highest-return act in this course.

Month 2: Seed, Inoculation & the Population Stand
Locked

Lesson 2.1 — Seed: fragile, short-lived, handled like eggs

Soybean seed is the most delicate in this college: a thin coat over a living embryo that cracks when dropped, dies young in storage (viability falls sharply after ~8–12 months in ordinary stores), and drinks moisture from humid air. Rules: source fresh certified/QDS seed each season or save your own with real care (dry, cool, hermetic, never dropped in sacks); always germination-test (50 seeds, damp cloth, 5 days — below 70–75%, raise the rate or reject the lot); never plant last year's leftover bag on hope.

Lesson 2.2 — Inoculation technique: ten minutes that grow the crop

  1. Buy soybean-specific inoculant (check the expiry date and that it was kept cool — living bacteria die in a hot shop window).
  2. In shade, on planting day: moisten seed lightly with water (a sugar or gum-arabic solution sticks better), sprinkle the inoculant powder, mix gently until every seed is dusted grey-black.
  3. Plant within hours, into moist soil — sun and heat kill the bacteria on the seed; never leave inoculated seed baking in an open pan.
  4. Check success at 4–6 weeks: dig (don't pull) a few plants — pink-red insides in root nodules = working nitrogen factories; white/green nodules or none = the field will need rescue nitrogen and next season a fresh, better-kept inoculant.

Month 3: Weeds, Water, Stages & the Rotation Seat
Locked

Lesson 3.1 — Weeds: win the first six weeks

Soybean seedlings are slow and low; weeds in the first 4–6 weeks cost more yield than any other management failure after stand and nodulation. Programme: clean seedbed at planting; first weeding at 2–3 weeks, second at 5–6 weeks; after canopy closure (helped by your dense stand) the crop shades the rest out. Hoe shallow — soybean roots and nodules ride near the surface. Where herbicides are used locally, follow label and extension guidance exactly; the hand-hoe programme above delivers the same result with timeliness.

Lesson 3.2 — Water and the flowering clock

  • Critical windows: establishment, and flowering through pod fill (~weeks 6–12) — stress there aborts flowers and shrinks seed. Mid-vegetative dry spells are tolerated far better.
  • Waterlogging suffocates nodules within days — drainage or ridging on heavy land.
  • Soybean is daylength-sensitive: varieties are bred for latitude bands, and a variety moved far north/south flowers at the wrong size. Plant RELEASED-for-your-area varieties and respect recommended planting windows — the calendar is part of the genetics.

Lesson 3.4 — The rotation seat

Soybean's classic seat is between maize crops: it returns nitrogen, breaks cereal pest cycles, and its early canopy fits the machinery and labour calendar maize built. Never soybean after soybean repeatedly (rust, nematodes and soil diseases build); groundnut and beans share some diseases — space legumes apart in the cycle. The maize–soybean pair is the backbone of every serious feed-grain area on the continent for a reason: each crop sells to the same feed mill, and each makes the other cheaper to grow.

Month 4: Rust, Pod Pests & the Quality Enemies
Locked

Lesson 4.1 — Soybean rust: the airborne threat

Asian soybean rust is the crop's most explosive disease in Africa: tiny tan-brown pustules on lower-leaf undersides that, in warm wet spells, climb the plant and defoliate a field in 2–3 weeks during pod fill — the exact moment yield is being assembled. Management:

  • Scout from flowering onward: hand lens or sharp eyes on LOWER leaf undersides, weekly in humid weather.
  • Tolerant/resistant released varieties where available; early-maturing varieties often escape the worst late-season pressure.
  • Fungicide response where the crop justifies it: a triazole-type spray at first pustules (or preventively at R1–R3 in known rust areas), repeated per label — timing beats product; a spray after defoliation is money thrown at history.
  • Destroy volunteer soybean between seasons — rust's green bridge.

Lesson 4.3 — The quality enemies: splits, greens and moulds

Feed mills and food processors buy QUALITY, and soybean's quality is made at the margins of harvest. Three graders' enemies: splits (cracked seed from rough threshing and over-dry handling), green seed (harvested immature or drought-forced), and moulds/heating (stored damp — with the same mycotoxin stakes as every crop in this college). Module 5's timing and gentleness rules are written against exactly these three.

Month 5: The Shattering Clock, Threshing & Two Stocks
Locked

Lesson 5.1 — The shattering clock

Soybean announces harvest honestly — leaves drop, pods brown, seeds rattle at ~13–15% moisture — and then punishes delay like no other crop here: dry pods shatter, twisting open in afternoon heat and firing seed onto the ground. Field losses of 10–30% from late harvest are common and invisible until you look down. Rules:

  • Harvest within days of rattle stage — not weeks. Morning work (pods slightly damp, less shatter) for hand harvest.
  • Hand method: pull or cut plants, stack small heaps/bundles on tarpaulins, move to the threshing floor same day where possible.
  • Where small combines or hired threshers operate, book them AGAINST the rattle date, not after it.
  • Shattering-tolerant released varieties exist — one more column worth reading at seed choice.

Lesson 5.2 — Threshing gently, drying honestly

  1. Thresh on tarpaulins: beat bundles LIGHTLY with flexible sticks, or rub through by trampling boards — soybean splits under enthusiasm, and splits are the number-one grade cut. Motorised threshers: correct drum speed per the operator's soybean setting.
  2. Winnow clean; remove greens, stones and pods.
  3. Dry to 11–12% for sale, 10–11% for storage — thin layers on tarpaulins/racks, never bare soil, turned gently. The bite test: cracks cleanly, no dent. Over-dried seed (sun-baked days at 8%) splits at every later touch — dry enough, then STOP.

Lesson 5.3 — Storage and the two stocks

  • Grain stock (sale): clean sacks on pallets, dry ventilated store, monthly checks; soybean stores well at correct moisture but heats fast if damp — a warm sack is an alarm.
  • Seed stock (next season + the premium niche): the fragile-seed rules concentrated — hand-harvested from your best area, gently threshed, dried to ~10–11%, hermetic containers, cool and shaded, never dropped; germination-tested before the season. Because everyone's soybean seed dies young, the careful grower's tested fresh seed sells at a strong premium every planting time — the potato/yam seed business logic in its lightest form.

Lesson 5.4 — Home and town processing

Beyond the mill gate, soybean's food uses are a real cottage industry: soy milk (soak, grind, boil WELL, strain), soy flour for porridge blends (roast before milling — raw soybean contains anti-nutrition factors that heat destroys; never feed raw beans or raw flour to people, poultry or pigs), tofu from milk plus a coagulant, and roasted snack beans. The heat rule is absolute and is the food-safety centrepiece of this module: properly cooked/roasted soy is excellent food; raw soy is not.

Lesson 6.3 — The rotation bonus, counted

The honest soybean ledger adds a line most budgets forget: the following maize crop's saved nitrogen (often the equivalent of a bag or more of urea) and its measurable yield bump on soybean land. When the pair is planned together — same feed-mill buyer for both grains, machinery and labour shared, legume nitrogen banked — the SYSTEM margin beats either crop alone. This is why the course's business plan is written for the maize–soybean pair, not soybean solo.

Lesson 6.4 — Records and the growth path

Per plot per season: variety, seed source and germination result; inoculation details (product, expiry, storage) and the 4–6 week nodule score (pink/white/none); stand count at 3 weeks; weeding and any spray dates; rattle date vs harvest date (the shattering meter); yield, splits estimate and moisture at sale; price and channel; the following maize's yield on this land. Report card: nodule score, stand count, and the rattle-to-harvest gap. Growth path: year one — one inoculated hectare done exactly right; year two — the maize–soybean pair with a contract registration and hermetic seed stock; year three — group tonnage on contract, the seed-sales niche, and home-mixed rations feeding your own poultry or pigs.

📚 Glossary

Rhizobium (soybean-specific)
The specialist nitrogen-fixing bacterium most African soils lack — supplied on the seed via inoculant.
Inoculation
Coating moistened seed with live rhizobium powder in shade and planting within hours — the course's highest-return act.
Nodule score
The 4–6 week dig-and-cut check: pink-red inside = working; white/none = rescue N and diagnose.
Promiscuous varieties
African-bred soybeans that nodulate with native rhizobia — the no-inoculant fallback.
Population crop
A crop yielding on plant numbers: 300,000–400,000/ha, dense rows, pods set higher.
Daylength sensitivity
Flowering triggered by day length — why varieties are bred for latitude bands and planted in released windows.
V and R stages
The leaf (vegetative) and flowering-to-maturity (reproductive) stage ladder used to time every decision.
Asian soybean rust
The explosive defoliating disease scouted on lower-leaf undersides from flowering; fought with tolerance, earliness and timed triazoles.
Green bridge
Volunteer plants carrying disease between seasons — destroyed to make rotation real.
Shattering
Dry pods twisting open and firing seed to the ground — the reason harvest is booked against the rattle date.
Rattle date
The day seeds rattle in browned pods (~13–15% moisture): the harvest clock's start.
Splits
Cracked seed from rough threshing or over-drying — the number-one grade cut.
Anti-nutritional factors
Compounds in RAW soybean harmful to people and livestock — destroyed by proper roasting or boiling.
Hermetic seed stock
The fragile seed lot kept airtight, cool and untouched — next season's stand and the planting-week premium.
Outgrower scheme
A processor's contracted farmer network with specs, prices and collection points — soybean's anchor channel.
Rotation bonus
The nitrogen and yield bump soybean banks for the following maize — the pair's hidden margin line.
