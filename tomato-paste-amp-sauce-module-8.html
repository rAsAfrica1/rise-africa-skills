1. Calculating the Cost of Producing One Jar or Sachet

To know if you are making a profit, you must first know exactly how much it costs to produce one jar or sachet of tomato paste or sauce. This is called your unit cost. Start by listing every ingredient and material you use for one batch. For example, to make 50 jars of tomato paste, you might use 20 kg of fresh tomatoes at 500 CFA per kg, 2 kg of onions at 400 CFA per kg, 1 litre of oil at 800 CFA, 0.5 kg of salt at 300 CFA, 0.2 kg of spices at 1,000 CFA, and 50 jars with lids at 100 CFA each. Add the cost of fuel or electricity for cooking, and labour if you pay someone. Let's calculate: tomatoes = 10,000 CFA; onions = 800 CFA; oil = 800 CFA; salt = 150 CFA; spices = 200 CFA; jars = 5,000 CFA; fuel = 1,000 CFA; labour = 2,000 CFA. Total batch cost = 19,950 CFA. Divide by 50 jars to get cost per jar: 19,950 50 = 399 CFA per jar. Always round up to 400 CFA to be safe. If you also use labels, add that cost. For sachets, do the same but count sachets per batch. Remember to include a small amount for spoilage or wastage, say 5%. So add 5% of 400 = 20 CFA, making unit cost 420 CFA. You must recalculate whenever prices change. Keep a notebook with these numbers. This unit cost is the foundation for setting your price.

2. Setting a Selling Price that Covers Costs and Gives Profit

Once you know your unit cost, you can set a selling price that covers all costs and gives you a profit. The simplest method is cost-plus pricing. Decide on a profit margina percentage added to your unit cost. For example, if your unit cost is 420 CFA per jar, and you want a 30% profit margin, multiply 420 by 1.30 to get 546 CFA. You might round to 550 CFA. But you must also check what customers are willing to pay. Go to local markets and see the price of similar tomato paste. If competitors sell at 500 CFA, you may need to reduce your margin or find ways to lower your cost. Another method is to work backwards: if the market price is 500 CFA, and you want 20% profit, your maximum cost must be 500 1.20 = 416 CFA. If your cost is higher, you must reduce costs or accept lower profit. Always consider your target customers: in low-income areas, small sachets at 50 CFA may sell better than large jars. Calculate the price per gram or per serving to compare. Don't forget to include a little extra for transport to market and market fees. For example, if transport adds 10 CFA per jar, your total cost becomes 430 CFA, so at 550 CFA your profit is 120 CFA per jar. Write down your price and review it every few months. Never sell below your unit cost, even to friends. A simple rule: price = unit cost + desired profit + selling expenses.

3. Keeping Simple Records of Sales and Expenses

Keeping records is not optional if you want your food business to grow. You don't need a computer or expensive software. A simple exercise book and a pen are enough. Divide the book into two sections: one for sales and one for expenses. Every time you sell a jar or sachet, write the date, quantity sold, and amount received. For example: '5 Jan 2025, 10 jars, 5,500 CFA'. Every time you buy ingredients or pay for fuel, write it in the expenses section: '5 Jan 2025, tomatoes 20 kg, 10,000 CFA'. At the end of each day, total your sales and expenses. At the end of the week, add up all sales and all expenses. Subtract expenses from sales to get your profit or loss. Keep all receipts in a small envelope or plastic bag. If you sell on credit, write the customer's name and amount owed in a separate 'debts' list. Follow up weekly. Also record your inventory: how many jars you have left, how many tomatoes in stock. This helps you know when to buy more. At the end of the month, review your records to see which products sell best and which expenses are highest. You can then adjust. Many small businesses fail because they mix personal money with business money. Open a separate bank account or mobile money wallet for the business. Pay yourself a small salary from the business, but record it as an expense. Good records help you get loans from microfinance institutions, because they can see you are serious.

4. Managing Cash Flow for a Small Food Business

Cash flow is the movement of money in and out of your business. Even if you are making a profit on paper, you can run out of cash if you don't manage it well. For example, you might sell 100 jars on credit, meaning you have 'sales' but no cash to buy more tomatoes. To manage cash flow, follow these rules: First, encourage cash sales. Offer a small discount for immediate payment, like 5% off. Second, if you must sell on credit, set a clear payment date and follow up. Third, keep a cash reserve for emergencies and for buying raw materials when prices are low. Aim to have enough cash to cover at least one month's expenses. Fourth, buy ingredients in bulk when you have cash, but don't tie up all your cash in stock. Fifth, pay your suppliers on time to maintain good relationships, but negotiate for credit if possible. Sixth, separate your personal wallet from the business cash box. At the end of each day, count your cash and compare it to your records. If you are short, find out why. Use a simple cash flow forecast: list expected cash inflows (sales, loans) and outflows (ingredients, labour, transport) for the next month. This helps you see if you will have enough cash. For example, if you expect to sell 500 jars at 550 CFA each, that's 275,000 CFA. If your expenses are 200,000 CFA, you have a surplus of 75,000 CFA. But if half your sales are on credit, you only have 137,500 CFA cash, which may not cover expenses. So plan accordingly. Always keep a small buffer.

5. Understanding Break-Even Point

The break-even point is the number of jars or sachets you must sell to cover all your costsboth fixed and variablewithout making a profit or a loss. Fixed costs are expenses that don't change with production, like rent for your kitchen, a license, or a monthly loan payment. Variable costs change with each unit produced, like tomatoes, jars, and labour. To calculate break-even, first find your unit selling price and unit variable cost. Suppose you sell a jar for 550 CFA. Your variable cost per jar (ingredients, packaging, fuel) is 420 CFA. Your contribution margin per jar is 550 - 420 = 130 CFA. This 130 CFA goes towards covering your fixed costs. If your fixed costs per month are 50,000 CFA (rent, license, etc.), then break-even = fixed costs contribution margin = 50,000 130 = 385 jars. So you must sell at least 385 jars per month to break even. If you sell more, you make a profit; if less, you make a loss. You can also calculate break-even in sales revenue: fixed costs (contribution margin selling price) = 50,000 (130/550) = 50,000 0.236 = 211,538 CFA. So you need 211,538 CFA in sales to break even. Knowing your break-even helps you set sales targets. If you think you can only sell 300 jars, you might reduce fixed costs (e.g., work from home) or increase contribution margin (raise price or reduce variable costs). Review your break-even every month as costs change. Use a simple calculator or notebook. This number is your survival linenever go below it for long.

6. Avoiding Common Money Mistakes in Food Processing

Many small food processors make the same money mistakes that kill their businesses. Here are the most common and how to avoid them. First, not separating business and personal money. You buy tomatoes with the same wallet you use for school fees. Then you can't tell if you made a profit. Solution: open a separate mobile money account or bank account for the business. Pay yourself a fixed salary. Second, underpricing. You calculate cost as only ingredients, forgetting fuel, labour, and packaging. Then you sell at a price that doesn't cover everything. Always use the full unit cost method. Third, not keeping records. Without records, you don't know your best-selling product or biggest expense. Keep a daily sales and expense book. Fourth, buying too much stock. You see tomatoes on sale and buy 100 kg, but they spoil before you use them. Buy only what you need for a week or two. Fifth, selling on credit without follow-up. You give 50 jars to a shop on credit, but you never collect the money. Set clear terms and follow up weekly. Sixth, mixing loans with personal use. You borrow for the business but spend it on a family event. Use loans only for business expenses. Seventh, not saving for emergencies. When your cooker breaks, you have no money to repair it. Save at least 5% of sales each week. Eighth, ignoring taxes and licenses. You may face fines later. Check local regulations. Ninth, not tracking break-even. You might be busy but still losing money. Calculate break-even monthly. Tenth, spending all profit on personal items. Reinvest at least 30% of profit into the business to grow. Avoid these mistakes and your tomato paste business will be stronger.

<h2>Practical Reference: Costing, Pricing and Money Records for Tomato Paste &amp; Sauce</h2>

<p>This Practical Reference is your working desk tool. It pulls together the six lessons in this module calculating the cost of producing one jar or sachet, setting a selling price that covers costs and gives profit, keeping simple records of sales and expenses, managing cash flow, understanding break-even point, and avoiding common money mistakes in food processing into one place you can return to every week. It is written for small and growing African tomato-processing businesses: home kitchens, cottage operations, and small registered workshops supplying shops, schools, restaurants and market traders.</p>

<p>The principle throughout is simple: <strong>know your numbers, price with confidence, and keep records that let you make decisions.</strong> A business that does not know its cost per jar cannot know whether it is making money, no matter how busy it looks.</p>

<h3>1. Costing a Single Jar or Sachet</h3>

<p>Your cost per unit has three parts: <strong>direct materials, direct labour, and overheads</strong>. Direct materials are everything that physically goes into the product and its packaging tomatoes, onions, pepper, salt, sugar, vinegar, preservative (where permitted), jars or sachets, lids, and labels. Direct labour is the time paid specifically to produce that batch. Overheads are the shared costs of running the business: rent, electricity or gas, water, transport, market levies, phone/data, and equipment depreciation.</p>

<p>Do this calculation <em>per batch</em>, then divide by the number of units produced. If a batch uses 40 kg of tomatoes at a local price and yields 60 jars, the tomato cost per jar is that total divided by 60. Add every other input the same way. This is your <strong>unit cost of production</strong> the floor below which you must never sell.</p>

<h3>2. Setting a Selling Price</h3>

<p>Pricing has two methods, and you should use both. <strong>Cost-plus pricing</strong> starts with your unit cost and adds a profit margin. <strong>Market-based pricing</strong> checks what competitors and customers will actually pay. If your cost-plus price is far above the market price, you must reduce costs or improve quality and branding you cannot simply insist on a price the market rejects.</p>

<p>A practical formula:</p>
<p><strong>Selling price = Unit cost (1 desired profit margin)</strong></p>
<p>If your unit cost is 800 and you want a 30% margin, price = 800 0.70 = 1,143. Round to a clean price like 1,150 or 1,200. Always add a small buffer for spoilage, breakages, and price changes in raw materials.</p>

<h3>3. Simple Records of Sales and Expenses</h3>

<p>You do not need accounting software to start. You need three books or notebooks (or a simple spreadsheet on a phone):</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cash book</strong> every amount received and every amount paid, dated.</li>
<li><strong>Sales record</strong> date, product, quantity, unit price, total, customer/outlet.</li>
<li><strong>Expense record</strong> date, item, supplier, amount, category (raw material, packaging, transport, labour, utilities).</li>
</ul>
<p>Record the same day. A record written from memory at month-end is unreliable. Keep receipts and supplier slips in an envelope by month. At the end of each week, add up sales and expenses to see your <strong>gross position</strong>. At month-end, produce a simple profit statement: total sales total expenses = profit or loss.</p>

<h3>4. Managing Cash Flow</h3>

<p>Profit is not the same as cash. You can be profitable on paper and still run out of money to buy tomatoes next week. Cash flow management means timing: when money comes in versus when it must go out.</p>
<ul>
<li>Collect payment on delivery where possible; avoid long credit to new customers.</li>
<li>Buy raw materials when prices are low (harvest season) if you can store or process safely.</li>
<li>Keep a small cash reserve for emergencies and equipment repairs.</li>
<li>Separate business money from household money use separate tins, wallets, or mobile-money accounts.</li>
<li>Do not use tomorrow's production money to pay today's personal expenses.</li>
</ul>

<h3>5. Understanding Break-Even Point</h3>

<p>Break-even point (BEP) is the number of units you must sell to cover all your costs no profit, no loss. Above it, you profit; below it, you lose. The formula:</p>
<p><strong>BEP (units) = Fixed costs (Selling price Variable cost per unit)</strong></p>
<p>Example: fixed costs (rent, licences, equipment) = 30,000 per month. Selling price = 1,650. Variable cost per unit = 1,150. Contribution per unit = 500. BEP = 30,000 500 = <strong>60 jars per month</strong>. Everything above 60 jars adds profit.</p>
<p>Knowing your BEP tells you the minimum production target, whether a price cut is safe, and whether a new cost (like a delivery bike) is affordable.</p>

<strong style="color:#c0392b;"> SAFETY AND COMPLIANCE WARNING</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#7b241c;">Never reduce costs by cutting corners on food safety. Do not use unapproved or over-dosed preservatives, do not reuse dirty jars or damaged lids, and do not sell product made with spoiled or mouldy tomatoes. Adulterated or unsafe tomato paste and sauce can cause serious illness and will destroy your brand, attract regulatory fines, and may lead to criminal liability. Check your country's food safety authority (for example NAFDAC in Nigeria, KEBS in Kenya, FDA Ghana, or the national bureau of standards) for permitted additives, labelling and hygiene rules before you sell. Cutting safety costs is not a saving it is a business-ending risk.</p>

<h3>6. Avoiding Common Money Mistakes</h3>

<ul>
<li><strong>Mixing business and personal money.</strong> You lose track of true profit.</li>
<li><strong>Pricing by guesswork or copying competitors</strong> without knowing your own cost.</li>
<li><strong>Ignoring hidden costs</strong> transport, spoilage, market levies, phone data, depreciation.</li>
<li><strong>Selling on long credit</strong> to customers who pay late or not at all.</li>
<li><strong>No records</strong>, so you cannot prove profit, qualify for a loan, or plan growth.</li>
<li><strong>Over-buying raw materials</strong> that spoil before processing.</li>
<li><strong>Spending profit before covering next batch's costs.</strong></li>
<li><strong>Under-insuring or not replacing worn equipment</strong>, causing sudden production stops.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>I know my exact cost per jar or sachet (materials + labour + overheads).</li>
<li>My selling price is above unit cost and includes a clear profit margin.</li>
<li>I record every sale and every expense the same day.</li>
<li>I keep business cash separate from household cash.</li>
<li>I know my monthly break-even number of units.</li>
<li>I review my cash flow weekly and my profit monthly.</li>
<li>I keep receipts, supplier slips and batch records.</li>
<li>I comply with national food safety and labelling rules.</li>
</ol>
