1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Iron furniture is a growing business across Africa. From bustling markets in Lagos to tourist lodges in Nairobi, there is high demand for sturdy, stylish pieces made from metal. Unlike wood, iron furniture does not rot, is not eaten by termites, and can be left outdoors in the rain. This makes it perfect for garden chairs, tables, bed frames, and shelving. As a welder, adding furniture to your skills means you can earn more than just making gates and burglar bars. You can create products that sell quickly and have higher profit margins.

In many African cities, imported furniture is expensive. Locally made iron furniture can be sold at a competitive price because you use readily available materials like square tubes, round bars, and angle iron. You also save on transport and can customise sizes to fit small apartments or large verandas. For example, a simple metal stool might cost 5,000 Naira to make and sell for 15,000 Naira. A dining table set can bring even more profit. The key is to combine strength with good design.

This module will teach you how to make basic furniture items step by step. You will learn which tools and materials to use, how to measure and cut accurately, how to weld clean joints, and how to finish your pieces so they look professional. You will also learn common mistakes that ruin furniture and how to fix them. Safety is critical because you will be working with sharp metal, sparks, and heavy pieces. By the end, you will have the skills to start producing and selling your own iron furniture, creating jobs and income for yourself and your community.

2. Tools and Materials

To make iron furniture, you need a basic welding setup plus some specific tools. Your core equipment includes a welding machine (arc welder of 200-300 amps is fine), an angle grinder with cutting and grinding discs, a metal chop saw or hacksaw, a tape measure, a try square, a centre punch, a hammer, and clamps. For bending curves, a pipe bender or a simple jig made from pegs on a workbench works well. You also need a sturdy workbench and a fire extinguisher. Personal protective equipment is mandatory: welding helmet, gloves, apron, safety boots, and earplugs when grinding.

Materials for iron furniture are usually mild steel. Common shapes are square tubes (e.g., 25mm x 25mm x 2mm wall thickness), rectangular tubes (20mm x 40mm), round tubes (25mm diameter), angle iron (25mm x 25mm x 3mm), and flat bars (20mm x 3mm). For seats and tabletops, you can use expanded metal mesh, flat bars, or wooden slats. Buy from local steel merchants; in many African cities, you can find these in industrial areas or main markets. Always check for rust and straightness. A 6-metre length of 25mm square tube is a standard size and can be cut to make chair frames, table legs, and more.

You will also need finishing materials: wire brush, sandpaper (80-120 grit), red oxide primer, and topcoat paint (enamel or hammered finish). For outdoor furniture, use galvanised or stainless steel if you can afford it, or apply a good primer and two coats of paint. Consumables include welding electrodes (2.5mm or 3.2mm, E6013 for general work), cutting discs, grinding discs, and anti-spatter spray. Keep a notebook to record your material list for each project so you can calculate costs and set prices. A small furniture piece like a stool needs about 2 metres of square tube, some flat bar, and a small piece of mesh.

3. Step by Step Method

Start by choosing a simple project like a stool or a small table. Draw a full-size plan on paper or directly on the workshop floor using chalk. For a stool, decide on seat height (45cm is standard) and seat size (30cm x 30cm). Cut four legs from 25mm square tube at 45cm each. Cut four horizontal rails: two at 30cm for the front and back, and two at 25cm for the sides, to fit between the legs. Use a try square to ensure all cuts are 90 degrees. Deburr the cut ends with a grinder.

Clamp the legs and rails on a flat surface to form a frame. Tack weld the joints first, checking that the frame is square by measuring diagonals; they must be equal. Once square, weld all around each joint. For the seat, cut a piece of expanded metal mesh to 30cm x 30cm and weld it on top of the frame. Alternatively, cut five flat bars at 30cm and weld them across the seat with 5cm gaps. Grind the welds smooth, especially on the top and edges where people will touch.

For a table, follow similar steps but larger. A dining table might be 75cm high, 120cm long, and 70cm wide. Use 40mm x 40mm square tube for legs and 20mm x 40mm rectangular tube for the apron (the frame under the top). Cut legs to 75cm, and apron pieces to fit between legs. Weld the apron to the legs at 5cm below the top. For the tabletop, you can use a 3mm steel plate, wooden planks, or a glass top. If using steel plate, weld it to the apron. If using wood, drill holes and bolt it. Always check for levelness and squareness at every stage. After welding, grind all joints flush. Then clean the entire piece with a wire brush and apply primer and paint. Let it dry completely before use.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

One common mistake is poor measurement leading to wobbly furniture. If legs are not cut to exactly the same length, the piece will rock. Fix: cut all legs together using a stop block on your chop saw, or measure each one twice. If already cut wrong, you can weld a small plate under the short leg and grind it flat, but prevention is better.

Another mistake is weak welds that break under weight. This happens when the welder uses too low amperage or moves too fast. Fix: set your welder to the correct amperage for the metal thickness (e.g., 70-90 amps for 2mm tube with 2.5mm electrode). Practice on scrap pieces until you get good penetration. If a weld breaks, grind it out and re-weld with proper heat. Also, avoid welding over rust or paint; clean the metal to bare steel first.

A third mistake is warping or distortion. Welding heat causes metal to bend. Fix: clamp your frame to a flat surface before welding, and use tack welds first, alternating sides to balance heat. After welding, if the piece is twisted, you can heat the opposite side with a torch and bend it back, but this takes skill. Better to prevent by welding small sections at a time and letting the metal cool.

Finally, many beginners skip finishing, leaving sharp edges and bare metal that rusts quickly. Fix: always grind welds smooth, especially on seats and arms. Apply a rust-preventive primer and at least two coats of enamel paint. For outdoor furniture, use a topcoat with UV protection. If you see rust starting, sand it off and repaint immediately. Also, check that all legs sit flat on the ground; if not, grind the bottom slightly until stable. These small fixes make your furniture safe and long-lasting.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety must be your first priority when making iron furniture. Welding produces intense ultraviolet light that can burn your eyes and skin. Always wear a welding helmet with a proper filter lens (shade 10-12 for arc welding) and cover all exposed skin with leather gloves, apron, and long sleeves. Never look at the arc with naked eyes. Keep a fire extinguisher nearby and a bucket of sand or water. Work in a well-ventilated area to avoid breathing welding fumes, which can cause metal fume fever. If working indoors, use a fan or open windows and doors.

Grinding and cutting metal create sparks and flying debris. Wear safety goggles or a face shield in addition to your welding helmet when grinding. Secure your work with clamps; never hold a piece with your hand while cutting or grinding. Use the correct disc for the jobânever use a cutting disc for grinding or vice versa. Check that discs are not cracked and that the grinder guard is in place. Keep your work area clean and free of flammable materials like paint thinners, rags, or paper. After welding, hot metal stays hot for a long time; use pliers or tongs to move pieces, and mark them with chalk to warn others.

Best practices for quality and efficiency: plan your cuts to minimise waste. Keep a notebook with measurements and angles. Use jigs and fixtures for repetitive parts like chair legs. Maintain your tools: replace worn electrodes, clean the welder's earth clamp, and keep the grinder free of dust. When lifting heavy furniture, bend your knees and ask for help. For outdoor furniture, choose galvanised steel or apply extra coats of paint. Test every piece for stability and strength before selling. Finally, keep learning: observe furniture in shops, take photos, and try new designs. Your reputation depends on safe, strong, and beautiful work.

6. Practical Exercise

For this exercise, you will build a simple iron stool. This project uses the skills from all previous lessons and can be completed in 3-4 hours. Gather your materials: 2.5 metres of 25mm x 25mm x 2mm square tube, a 30cm x 30cm piece of expanded metal mesh (or five 30cm flat bars), welding electrodes (2.5mm), cutting and grinding discs, red oxide primer, and black enamel paint. Tools: welder, angle grinder, chop saw or hacksaw, tape measure, try square, clamps, hammer, wire brush, and PPE.

Step 1: Cut four legs at 45cm each. Cut four rails: two at 30cm (front and back) and two at 25cm (sides). Deburr all ends. Step 2: On a flat surface, arrange two legs and two 30cm rails to form a rectangle. Clamp and tack weld. Check diagonals for squareness. Weld all joints. Repeat for the other side. Step 3: Connect the two side frames with the 25cm rails, forming a box. Tack weld, check square, then weld fully. Step 4: Cut the mesh to 30cm x 30cm and weld it on top of the frame. If using flat bars, weld them across with 5cm gaps. Step 5: Grind all welds smooth, especially on the seat and edges. Step 6: Clean with a wire brush, apply primer, and let dry. Then apply two coats of enamel paint. Let dry completely.

Test your stool: it should not wobble, and the seat should support your weight (at least 100kg). If it wobbles, grind the bottom of the short leg until level. If a weld looks weak, re-weld it. After completing, calculate your costs: steel, electrodes, paint, and your time. A stool like this can sell for 3-5 times the material cost. Take a photo for your portfolio. Try making a second stool with a different design, such as a round seat using a pipe bender. This exercise builds your confidence and gives you a product to sell immediately.

Step-by-step production sequence

  1. Plan the batch. Decide units per run (e.g. 10 chairs). Batch cutting and jigging cuts cost per unit by 30â50% versus one-off builds.
  2. Cut to a cut-list. Mark every piece with a paint pen. Keep a master cut-list on the wall so apprentices cut correctly without you present.
  3. Build a jig. Weld a simple angle-iron jig on a flat steel table for the seat frame and leg positions. This is the single biggest quality and speed gain.
  4. Deburr and clean. Remove mill scale and burrs at weld zones. Poor fit-up causes weak, ugly joints.
  5. Tack, check square, then weld. Tack all four corners, measure diagonals (they must match within 2 mm), then complete welds.
  6. Grind and finish. Flush-grind visible welds, sand 80â120â240 grit, then apply primer and topcoat, or send for powder coating.
  7. Fit feet and accessories. Add nylon or rubber foot caps to protect floors â a small detail buyers notice and pay for.
  8. Inspect and price. Weigh the piece, record material and labour, and set price at 2.5â3Ã material cost as a floor for profitability.

Common mistakes and fixes

  • Warped frames after welding. Fix: tack evenly, alternate sides, allow cooling between passes, and clamp to a flat table.
  • Wobbly chair. Fix: build a leg jig; check diagonal measurements before welding; add adjustable feet if needed.
  • Burn-through on thin tube. Fix: reduce current, use a faster travel speed, or switch to MIG.
  • Rust returning within weeks. Fix: degrease, prime with zinc-rich primer, and topcoat both inside and outside of tubes.
  • Inconsistent finish. Fix: standardise your grinding and sanding grit sequence; batch all finishing together.
  • Underpricing. Fix: track material, consumables, labour, overhead and a 25â35% profit margin before quoting.
