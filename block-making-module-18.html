Fired Clay Brick

8 lessons · 30 quiz questions · Medium difficulty

1 Clay selection

Fired clay bricks are the traditional brick of Europe, Asia, and Africa. Raw clay is shaped, dried, and fired in a kiln at 900–1,100°C. The heat fuses the clay particles into a hard, durable, water-resistant brick.

Good brick clay:

  • 20–30% clay (for binding)
  • 50–60% silt and fine sand (for body)
  • Low in lime (lime causes popping in firing)
  • Low in organic matter
  • Plastic when wet (easy to shape)

Field tests: Roll a 3 mm thread between your palms. If it holds together and bends without breaking, the clay is plastic enough. If it breaks early, it needs more clay or aging.

⚠ Test your clay by firing a small batch first. Some clays crack, bloat, or turn brittle in the kiln.

2 Pugging and forming

Clay is dug, weathered for weeks (to break down and improve plasticity), then pugged (mixed with water) into a uniform, stiff paste.

Pugging methods:

  • Traditional: mixing in a pit by foot or with a bullock
  • Pug mill: mechanical mixer with rotating blades (small electric $2,000–$8,000)
  • Extruder: press clay through a die to form continuous brick-sized ribbons

Forming methods:

  • Moulding by hand: clay is pressed into wooden moulds — traditional, slow, low-cost
  • Extrusion: clay is pushed through a die and cut to length — fast, uniform, industrial
  • Press: dry-press clay under high pressure — modern, very uniform

For small-scale: hand-moulding with sanded wooden moulds is the standard.

3 Drying green bricks

Before firing, bricks must be completely dry. Water in the brick turns to steam in the kiln and shatters the brick.

Drying stages:

  1. Fresh-moulded bricks rest in the mould for 1–2 days.
  2. Remove and stack on edge under a shaded drying shed.
  3. Air-dry for 5–10 days (hot dry weather) or 3–4 weeks (humid weather).
  4. Turn every 2–3 days to dry evenly.
  5. Brick is dry when it rings when tapped and feels light.

Do not rush — a wet brick in the kiln wastes the entire firing cycle for that batch.

4 Kiln types (clamp, Hoffman)

Firing is what makes clay brick different from adobe. Two kiln types matter for small producers:

Clamp kiln (traditional):

  • Bricks are stacked in a large pile with fuel (wood, coal, dung) distributed through the stack
  • Fire channels run through the base
  • The pile is plastered with mud to hold heat
  • Fires are lit and burn for 3–7 days
  • Cool-down: 3–5 days

Cost: $0 to build (just labour). Fuel: 1 tonne wood per 5,000 bricks.

Hoffman kiln (modern):

  • Permanent brick structure with multiple chambers
  • Continuous firing — fires move from chamber to chamber as new bricks are added
  • Much more fuel-efficient (40% less fuel)
  • Continuous production 24/7

Cost: $50,000–$500,000 to build. Only viable at scale.

💡 Start with a clamp kiln. It costs almost nothing and teaches you the firing process.

5 Firing curve

Firing follows a specific temperature curve. Rushing through stages cracks or weakens the brick.

Typical 5-day firing:

  • Day 1: Raise temp slowly to 200°C. Remaining moisture escapes.
  • Day 2: 200–500°C. Organic matter burns off. Yellow smoke.
  • Day 3: 500–900°C. Chemical changes start. Clay becomes ceramic.
  • Day 4: 900–1,100°C. Full vitrification. Clay particles fuse.
  • Day 5: Hold temp and start cooling.

Cool-down is 3–5 days. Fast cooling = cracked bricks.

Colour indicates temperature: red-brown at 900°C, darker at 1,000°C, sometimes nearly black at 1,100°C.

6 Cooling cycle

After firing, the kiln cools for 3–5 days. Different cooling rates give different brick properties.

  • Slow cooling: Stronger, less likely to crack, better for structural bricks.
  • Fast cooling: More brittle, harder surface, better for decorative bricks.

In a clamp kiln, cooling happens naturally as the fire dies. Don't try to speed it up by opening vents — this causes the top layer of bricks to crack.

Do not open a clamp kiln before it is fully cool. The heat inside can be 400–500°C even 3 days after firing stops.

7 Quality grading

Not every brick comes out of the kiln equally. Grade into three categories:

First class: Uniform colour, sharp edges, no cracks. Used for facing walls (visible). Sold at premium price.

Second class: Slight colour variation or minor cracks. Used for internal walls. Sold at standard price.

Third class (overburnt or underfired): Dark, misshapen, or brittle. Used for foundations, fill, or drainage. Sold at low price.

Typical yield: 60–70% first class, 20–30% second, 5–15% waste.

Grading at the kiln side is critical — it sets your pricing and your waste rate.

8 Traditional brick markets

Fired clay brick has a specific market that differs from concrete blocks.

Who buys:

  • Traditional house builders wanting brick aesthetics
  • Chimney and fireplace builders (heat-resistant)
  • Restoration projects for historic buildings
  • High-end residential and commercial projects
  • Paving (clay pavers)

Pricing (Africa, 2026):

  • Standard brick (220 × 105 × 70 mm): $0.15–$0.40 each
  • Premium facing brick: $0.40–$0.80 each
  • Decorative/handmade: $0.80–$2.00 each

Cost to make: $0.08–$0.20 per brick (mostly fuel and labour). Margin: 30–50%.

Selling points vs concrete blocks:

  • Warmer look — traditional brick is prestigious
  • Better rain resistance (no plaster needed)
  • Better fire resistance
  • Smaller size = more design flexibility

Limitations:

  • Energy-intensive (kiln uses wood or coal)
  • Environmental concerns about wood use
  • Slower to lay than concrete blocks (more bricks per m²)
  • Higher labour cost per m² of wall
💰 Fired clay brick is a mid-scale business: more capital than adobe, less than AAC. Best when local clay and fuel are cheap.
