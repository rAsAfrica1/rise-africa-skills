1. Designing Duck Houses for Different Climates

Ducks need shelter that keeps them dry, cool in heat, and warm in cold. In hot, humid areas like coastal West Africa, build an open-sided house with a high roof (3â4 meters at the peak) and wide eaves. Use local materials: bamboo poles for framing, palm fronds or corrugated iron for roofing, and woven mats or wire mesh for walls. The floor should be raised 30â45 cm above ground to prevent flooding and allow air circulation. In Sahelian dry heat, add a thatched roof for insulation and orient the house east-west so the long side faces north-south, reducing direct sun. For cold highlands (e.g., Ethiopian highlands), use mud-brick or timber walls with a low ceiling (2â2.5 m) and insulate the roof with grass thatch. Provide a solid windbreak on the prevailing wind side. In all climates, the house must have a sloped floor (2â3% grade) to drain water, and a concrete or compacted earth floor covered with litter. Allow 0.3â0.5 square meters per duck inside, plus an outdoor run of at least 1 square meter per duck. For a flock of 50 ducks, a house of 3 m x 5 m works well. In flood-prone areas, build on stilts or a raised platform of stones and sand. Always include a secure door and windows with wire mesh (1 cm openings) to keep out predators and wild birds. Test your design: if you can stand inside and feel a breeze on a hot day, itâs good. If rain blows in, add a removable tarp on the windward side. Remember, ducks are hardy but poor at regulating heat; shade and airflow are more important than fancy materials.

2. Bedding Materials and Litter Management

Good bedding keeps ducks clean, dry, and healthy. Use materials that are absorbent, cheap, and locally available: rice husks, wood shavings (not sawdust, which ducks may eat), chopped maize stover, dry grass, or peanut shells. Avoid sand, which gets muddy, and straw, which molds quickly. Spread a layer 5â8 cm thick inside the house. For a 3 m x 5 m house, you need about 1.5 cubic meters of loose bedding. Replace wet or soiled litter daily, especially around drinkers. Every 2â3 weeks, remove all litter and replace it. Between changes, turn the litter with a rake to keep it dry and aerated. If it smells of ammonia, add fresh bedding or increase ventilation. Ducks produce a lot of wet droppings; litter that stays wet breeds bacteria and foot problems. In humid climates, mix bedding with agricultural lime (1 kg per 10 square meters) to reduce smell and moisture. Store extra bedding in a dry, raised shed. Compost used litter with other farm waste; after 6â8 weeks, it becomes excellent fertilizer for gardens. For ducks kept on wire floors, provide a small solid area with bedding for resting and nesting. Never use newspaper aloneâitâs slippery and not absorbent. In free-range systems, rotate the bedding area or move the house to fresh ground every few weeks. A simple test: pick up a handful of litter and squeeze. If water drips out, itâs too wet; add dry material. If it crumbles, itâs perfect. Keep a wheelbarrow, shovel, and rake near the house for daily chores. Clean litter means fewer flies, less disease, and better egg production.

3. Ventilation and Temperature Control

Ducks need fresh air but not drafts. Ventilation removes moisture, ammonia, and heat. In hot climates, aim for a complete air exchange every minute. Use open sides with wire mesh (1 cm holes) and adjustable curtains or tarps. Place vents high on walls and low near the floor to create a chimney effect: cool air enters low, hot air exits high. A ridge vent along the roof peak helps. For a 3 m x 5 m house, provide at least 1 square meter of permanent ventilation openings. In cold climates, reduce openings to 0.3 square meters but never seal the house completelyâhumidity causes frostbite and respiratory illness. Use a thermometer and hygrometer (cheap, from agro-vet shops) to monitor. Ideal temperature for adult ducks is 18â24Â°C. For ducklings, start at 32Â°C in week one, then reduce by 3Â°C each week until 18Â°C. Use a 100-watt bulb or hurricane lamp for brooding, hung 45 cm above the litter. In extreme heat (above 35Â°C), spray the roof with water, add shade trees, and give ducks a shallow pool to cool off. In cold, add extra dry bedding and block drafts with sacks or mud. Signs of poor ventilation: condensation on walls, strong ammonia smell, ducks panting or huddling. Fix immediately. In humid tropics, use ceiling fans or open gable ends. Never use air conditioningâtoo expensive. A simple trick: hang a strip of cloth near a vent; if it moves, air is flowing. Check ventilation at duck height, not just at your head. Adjust curtains morning and evening. Good airflow keeps ducks healthy and productive.

4. Predator-Proofing and Security

Predators can wipe out a flock in one night. Common threats in Africa: snakes, mongooses, wild cats, hawks, owls, jackals, and even rats (which eat ducklings). Build a secure house with a solid floor (concrete or packed earth with wire mesh underneath) and walls of wire mesh (1 cm openings) or mud brick. Cover all openings with wire mesh, including windows and vents. Use a strong latch and padlock on the doorânot just a hook. For outdoor runs, bury wire mesh 30 cm deep and bend it outward 30 cm to stop digging animals. Add an electric fence if lions or leopards are nearby (solar-powered units work well). For aerial predators, cover the run with wire mesh or shade netting. Keep grass short around the house to remove hiding places. At night, lock ducks inside. A guard dog or donkey can help deter predators. For snakes, spread ash or lime around the perimeter; keep feed in metal bins. For rats, use traps and keep feed spilled to a minimum. In areas with crocodiles, build houses far from water bodies or fence the pond. Inspect your house weekly for holes, loose wire, or weak spots. Repair immediately with pliers and extra wire. A simple alarm: string small bells on a wire across the door; if a predator pushes in, bells ring. For ducklings, keep them in a raised brooder with wire mesh floor and a solid lid. Never leave ducklings unattended outdoors. If you lose a duck, find the entry point and fix it. Prevention is cheaper than replacement. Share predator-proofing tips with neighboursâcommunity-wide security works best.

5. Water Access: Ponds, Pools, and Drinkers

Ducks need water for drinking, bathing, and mating. A clean drinker is essential. Use a 20-liter plastic container with a lid and a small trough, or a commercial bell drinker. Place drinkers on a wire platform over a drain so spilled water flows away. Clean and refill twice daily. For 50 ducks, provide at least 3 drinkers. For bathing, a small pool (2 m x 2 m, 30 cm deep) is enough for 20â30 ducks. Line it with a plastic sheet or use a concrete tank. Change water every 2â3 days, or when it becomes dirty. In hot weather, ducks need to submerge their heads to clean their eyes and nostrils. If you have a natural pond, fence it to prevent erosion and predator access. Do not let ducks drink from stagnant, algae-filled waterâit causes disease. Add a tap and hose for easy filling. In dry areas, recycle water from the pool to irrigate vegetables. For ducklings, use shallow trays (2 cm deep) with marbles or stones so they donât drown. Provide water 24/7. In cold weather, check for ice and break it. A simple drinker: cut a 5-liter jerrycan in half lengthwise, fill with water, and secure it in a wooden frame. For large flocks, use a float valve connected to a tank. Always place drinkers away from feed to keep feed dry. If ducks have access to a pond, they may get parasites; treat with approved dewormers every 3 months. Fresh, clean water is the cheapest health insurance.

6. Nesting Boxes and Egg Collection Areas

Ducks lay eggs early in the morning, often before 8 AM. Provide one nesting box for every 3â4 ducks. Boxes should be 40 cm x 40 cm x 40 cm, made of wood or plastic, with a low front (10 cm) to keep eggs in but allow entry. Place them on the floor in a quiet, dark corner of the house. Line with dry bedding (wood shavings or straw). Do not use slippery materials. Keep boxes clean; replace bedding weekly. For a flock of 50 ducks, provide 12â15 boxes. If ducks lay on the floor, collect eggs immediately and place a fake egg (wooden or plastic) in the box to encourage use. Collect eggs at least twice dailyâearly morning and late afternoon. Use a clean basket with soft lining. Wash hands before and after. Store eggs in a cool room (15â20Â°C) in clean trays, pointed end down. Never wash eggs unless dirty; if dirty, wipe with a dry cloth or use fine sandpaper. For large farms, build a roll-away nest box: floor sloped 10Â° so eggs roll into a collection tray. This keeps eggs clean and reduces breakage. In free-range systems, provide mobile nesting boxes (a wooden box with a lid) near the flockâs resting area. Check for hidden nests in bushesâducks often lay outside. A simple trick: keep ducks confined until 9 AM; most will lay in the boxes. Then let them out. Collect eggs before they get dirty or eaten by predators. Record daily egg numbers to track productivity. If eggs are dirty, check bedding and drinker placement. Clean eggs fetch better prices at market.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Duck Housing and Facilities

Duck farming in Africa is one of the fastest-turnaround livestock enterprises available to small and medium-scale agribusinesses. A well-designed house determines your mortality rate, feed conversion, egg output, and ultimately your profit margin. This practical reference consolidates the six core lessons of this module into one working document you can print, laminate, and keep on the farm. Every decision below is framed around cost per duck, return on investment, and risk reduction.

Why Housing Is a Business Decision, Not a Construction Project

In African markets, ducks (Muscovy, Pekin, Khaki Campbell, and local crosses) command premium prices because demand from hotels, urban consumers, and export processors outstrips supply. The bottleneck for most farmers is not demand but losses: predation, disease from wet litter, heat stress, and drowned ducklings. A house that costs 20% more to build but cuts mortality from 15% to 4% pays for itself within two production cycles. Treat housing as an investment with a measurable return, not an overhead.

Designing for Africa's Climates

Africa spans humid tropical coastlines, hot dry savannahs, highland cool zones, and semi-arid regions. One design will not serve all. The key variables are roof height, wall openness, floor type, and orientation.

  • Hot humid (West/Central Africa, coastal East Africa): Raise the house on a 30â45 cm platform or concrete plinth, use open-sided walls with wire mesh, and install a high-pitched roof (minimum 3 m at eaves) with wide overhangs. Airflow is your cooling system.
  • Hot dry (Sahel, Northern Nigeria, Sudan): Use thick mud-brick or compressed earth walls on the western side to block afternoon heat, orient the long axis eastâwest, and add a shaded run with trees or shade cloth.
  • Highland cool (Ethiopian highlands, Kenyan highlands, Southern Africa): Use closed walls with adjustable vents, deep litter bedding for warmth, and insulate the roof. Ducklings under two weeks need supplementary heat.
  • Semi-arid and flood-prone areas: Elevate floors 60 cm or more, ensure drainage channels around the structure, and avoid siting near seasonal watercourses that flood.

Bedding Materials and Litter Management

Wet litter is the single largest cause of respiratory disease, foot rot, and ammonia damage in duck houses. Ducks spill water constantly; your litter system must handle that reality. Deep litter of 8â12 cm is standard, topped up weekly and fully replaced every 6â8 weeks or between batches.

Predator-Proofing and Security

In most African contexts, predators are the number one cause of duckling loss. Snakes, monitor lizards, genets, mongooses, rats, hawks, and even domestic dogs will take birds. Your house must be a fortress at night and a monitored space by day.

  • Bury wire mesh 30 cm into the ground around the perimeter to stop digging predators.

  • Lock birds in at dusk; open at dawn. A simple padlock on a sliding door costs little and prevents theft.

  • Store feed in sealed metal drums, not sacks â rats and mould both destroy profit.

Water Access: Ponds, Pools, and Drinkers

Ducks need water for drinking, cleaning their nostrils, and mating. But standing water is also a disease reservoir. The business-smart approach is controlled water: clean drinkers changed at least twice daily, and if you provide a pool, drain and refill it daily.
