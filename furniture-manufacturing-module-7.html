Lessons

1. Introduction to Upholstery: Frames, Webbing, and Springs

Upholstery starts with a strong frame. In Africa, hardwoods like mahogany, iroko, or teak are best, but if too expensive, use well-seasoned local wood like eucalyptus or mvule. Avoid softwoods like pine for load-bearing parts. Build a frame with mortise-and-tenon joints, glued and screwed. For a standard armchair, use 50mm x 50mm hardwood for legs and rails. Ensure the frame is square by measuring diagonals they must be equal.

Next, add webbing. Use 50mm wide elastic webbing (or jute webbing for traditional style). Stretch webbing across the frame in a basketweave pattern: first side-to-side, then front-to-back, interlocking. Staple each strip with a heavy-duty staple gun using 10mm staples, pulling tight it should sound like a drum when tapped. Space strips 50-75mm apart. For a seat, use 5-6 strips each way. Fix with a webbing stretcher or a sturdy stick for leverage.

Springs come next. For a seat, use 8-10 coil springs (e.g., 100mm tall, 12-gauge wire). Place them evenly, then tie each spring to the webbing with strong twine (like butcher's twine) using a slip knot. Then tie springs to each other with a figure-eight knot across the top, compressing them slightly. This distributes weight. For backrests, use lighter springs or just foam. In hot, humid climates, rust can be an issue use galvanised springs or coat with oil.

Finally, cover the springs with a layer of burlap (hessian) stapled over the frame, then add a 25mm layer of foam or cotton felt to prevent springs from poking through. This foundation ensures your upholstery lasts for years, even with daily use in a family home.

2. Foam Selection, Cutting, and Shaping

Foam is the heart of comfortable upholstery. In Africa, high-density polyurethane foam is common. For seats, use density 30-35 kg/m (medium-firm) and thickness 100-150mm. For backrests, use 25-30 kg/m and 50-75mm. Avoid cheap low-density foam (under 20 kg/m) it flattens within months. If you can, buy foam blocks from a supplier and cut yourself to save money.

Cutting foam: Use an electric carving knife or a sharp bread knife. For straight cuts, mark with chalk, then run the knife along a metal ruler. For curves, use a template made from plywood. Keep the blade vertical a slight angle ruins the fit. For a 100mm thick foam, cut slowly to avoid tearing. If you have no electric knife, a hacksaw blade with a handle works, but sand the edges with 80-grit sandpaper after cutting to smooth.

Shaping: For a rounded cushion, use a foam shaper or an angle grinder with a sanding disc (careful it melts foam). Better: use a hot wire cutter made from a nichrome wire and a 12V battery charger. This gives clean, sealed edges. For a box cushion, cut foam 10mm larger than the cover to ensure a tight fit. For a T-cushion, cut the main shape, then add a 50mm thick topper.

In humid regions, foam can grow mould. Spray with a diluted bleach solution (1 part bleach to 10 parts water) and let dry in sun before use. Always wear a dust mask when cutting foam particles are harmful. Store foam flat, not compressed, to keep its shape. With proper selection and cutting, your cushions will be comfortable and durable for years.

3. Fabric Selection and Pattern Layout

Choosing fabric: For African homes, durability matters. Use heavy cotton canvas (250-350 gsm), linen, or upholstery-grade polyester. Avoid thin fabrics like chintz. For outdoor furniture, use solution-dyed acrylic (e.g., Sunbrella) or treated canvas. Check rub count aim for 15,000+ for domestic use. In markets, test by rubbing a sample with a coin if it pills, reject. Also consider colour: dark colours hide stains, but light colours reflect heat.

Pattern layout: Before cutting, study the fabric's pattern repeat. For a floral print, you need to match patterns across seams. Buy extra fabric add 20% to your measurements. For a plain fabric, you can nest pieces closely. Always lay fabric on a flat surface, grain straight. The grain (selvedge) should run vertically on chair backs and seats for strength. Use weights, not pins, to hold pattern pieces pins leave holes.

Marking and cutting: Use tailor's chalk or a washable marker. For dark fabrics, use white chalk. Cut with sharp dressmaking shears never use pinking shears on upholstery fabric. Cut on a single layer for patterned fabric, double layer for plain. For piping, cut bias strips 40mm wide. For a standard armchair, you need about 3-4 metres of 140cm wide fabric. Add 0.5m for cushion covers.

Pre-shrink fabric by washing and drying before cutting cotton can shrink 5-10%. Also, check for flaws: hold fabric to light. If you find a flaw, cut around it. Store fabric rolled, not folded, to avoid creases. In dusty environments, keep fabric covered until use.

4. Cutting, Sewing, and Stitching Techniques

Sewing upholstery requires a heavy-duty machine a walking foot or industrial machine like a Singer 211 or Juki DNU-1541. Use a size 16 or 18 needle for medium fabric, size 20 for leather. Thread: bonded nylon or polyester, size 69 or 92. For seams, use a lockstitch, 4-5 stitches per cm. Always test on a scrap.

Cutting: After marking, cut with sharp shears. For straight lines, use a rotary cutter and mat. Leave 15mm seam allowance. For curves, clip the seam allowance to 6mm before turning. For corners, trim diagonally to reduce bulk. For piping, cut bias strips, fold over cord, and stitch close to the cord with a zipper foot. For a box cushion, cut top and bottom panels, plus a side gusset. Add a zipper to the gusset for removable covers.

Stitching: Sew with right sides together. For a strong seam, backstitch at start and end. For cushions, use a French seam for a clean finish: stitch wrong sides together, trim, then stitch right sides together. For tufting, use a long needle and button twine. For a tight seat, sew a welt cord around the top edge. For a skirt, hem with a 50mm double fold.

In Africa, power cuts happen. Keep a hand-crank machine as backup. Also, use a seam ripper carefully unpicking is common. After sewing, press seams open with an iron (test heat on scrap). For vinyl, use a teflon foot or tissue paper to prevent sticking. Always sew a sample to check tension. With practice, you can sew a complete chair cover in 3-4 hours.

5. Applying Upholstery: Stapling, Tacking, and Finishing

Start by attaching the cover to the frame. Use a pneumatic staple gun with 10mm staples for hardwood, or 6mm for softwood. For manual, use a heavy-duty Arrow T50. Begin at the centre of each side, pull fabric tight, and staple. Work outward, spacing staples 50mm apart. For curves, make small pleats. For corners, fold fabric neatly and staple. Never staple through the front always on the underside or back.

For a professional finish, use tacking strips or cardboard strips to create a straight edge. For a seat, pull fabric over the foam, staple underneath. For a back, pull fabric over the front, staple on the back. For arms, wrap fabric around and staple inside. Use a staple remover if you make a mistake. For a tight pull, use a web stretcher or a pair of pliers.

Finishing: Trim excess fabric to 15mm. For a clean edge, glue on gimp braid or use a hot glue gun to attach decorative trim. For tufting, mark button positions, thread a long needle with button twine, push through foam, and secure with a button. For a skirt, measure from seat to floor, add 20mm hem, and staple to frame. For a dust cover, staple a piece of black cambric to the bottom.

Inspect your work: check for wrinkles, loose staples, and alignment. If a staple pops, replace it. For leather, use a mallet to tap down staples. For a final touch, steam the fabric to remove creases. In humid climates, apply a fabric protector spray. With careful stapling and finishing, your upholstery will look factory-made.

6. Leather and Vinyl Upholstery Methods

Leather and vinyl are popular in Africa for their durability and easy cleaning. Leather is expensive, so use it for high-wear areas like seats and armrests. Vinyl (PVC or PU) is cheaper and comes in many colours. For upholstery, use automotive-grade vinyl (0.8-1.2mm thick) or full-grain leather (1.2-1.5mm). Avoid bonded leather it peels. In hot climates, leather can crack; condition it with leather balm every 6 months.

Cutting: Leather has natural stretch, so cut with the stretch direction (usually along the backbone) running vertically. Use a rotary cutter or sharp shears. For vinyl, cut with scissors. Always cut single layer leather has flaws. For seams, use a leather needle (size 18) and nylon thread. For a flat-felled seam, glue edges with contact cement, then stitch. For piping, use leather welting.

Sewing: Use a walking foot to prevent slipping. For leather, use a teflon foot. Stitch length 3-4mm. For vinyl, use a roller foot. Always test on scrap. For curves, clip the seam allowance. For button tufting, use a leather punch to make holes, then thread buttons with waxed thread. For a diamond pattern, mark with chalk and stitch.

Stapling: Leather and vinyl are stapled like fabric, but use stainless steel staples to prevent rust. For leather, you can also use decorative nails (tacks) for a traditional look. For vinyl, avoid stretching too much it can tear. For a smooth finish, warm the vinyl with a heat gun (careful not to melt). For cleaning, use a damp cloth and mild soap. Never use alcohol on vinyl. With proper care, leather and vinyl upholstery can last 10+ years.

<h2>Practical Reference: Upholstery and Soft Furnishings for African Furniture Enterprises</h2>

<p>This practical reference module consolidates the core skills covered in the six lessons of the Upholstery and Soft Furnishings course. It is written for African furniture makers, workshop owners, and apprentices who want to produce durable, market-ready upholstered furniture from simple foam-cushion stools to full three-seater sofas using locally available materials and tools. The focus is on commercial viability: choosing the right frame, foam, fabric, and fixing method to match the price point your customers can pay while maintaining quality that builds repeat business.</p>

<h3>1. Frames, Webbing, and Springs</h3>
<p>The frame is the skeleton of any upholstered piece. In most African workshops, hardwood such as <em>Milicia excelsa</em> (Iroko), <em>Khaya</em> spp. (Mahogany), or locally sourced <em>Pterocarpus</em> (Padauk) is preferred because it resists termites and holds staples and tacks firmly. Softwoods like pine are cheaper but compress under load and loosen staples over time. Joints must be glued (PVA or polyurethane) and reinforced with dowels, screws, or corner blocks never rely on nails alone.</p>
<p>Webbing provides the initial suspension. Elastic rubber webbing (often imported from South Africa, Egypt, or China) is stretched at 1520% tension in an interlaced pattern. Jute or canvas webbing is cheaper but sags faster. Springs either coil (open or pocketed) or sinuous (zig-zag) are tied to the frame with jute twine or clipped with metal clips. For African markets where humidity and heat vary widely, sinuous springs are often more practical: they are lighter, cheaper, and less likely to rust than coil springs in coastal cities like Mombasa, Lagos, or Durban.</p>

<h3>2. Foam Selection, Cutting, and Shaping</h3>
<p>Foam density is the single most important quality indicator. For seat cushions in domestic furniture, use 2535 kg/m high-density polyurethane foam. For back cushions, 1822 kg/m is acceptable. In Africa, foam is often sold by the sheet or block; always ask for density, not just thickness. Cheap foam (15 kg/m or below) collapses within months, especially in hot climates where heat accelerates compression set.</p>
<p>Cutting: use an electric carving knife or a sharp bread knife with a serrated blade. For clean edges, freeze the foam slightly (if a freezer is available) or use a hot wire cutter. Shaping: foam can be sculpted with an angle grinder fitted with a flap disc, or by hand with a surform. For cushions that need a crown, glue two layers of different densities together using a spray adhesive a firm base and a softer top layer. This is a common technique in South African and Kenyan workshops producing hotel furniture.</p>

<h3>3. Fabric Selection and Pattern Layout</h3>
<p>Fabric choice drives both cost and customer satisfaction. In African markets, popular choices include:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cotton duck / canvas</strong> durable, affordable, locally woven in Nigeria, Ghana, and Tanzania.</li>
<li><strong>Ankara / Kitenge prints</strong> high visual appeal, but often lower abrasion resistance; best for accent chairs and headboards, not heavy-use sofas.</li>
<li><strong>Polyester blends</strong> stain-resistant, good for family homes and hospitality.</li>
<li><strong>Leather and vinyl</strong> premium and easy-clean options; see section 6.</li>
</ul>
<p>Pattern layout matters for both aesthetics and waste reduction. Always align the fabric grain vertically on the front of the piece. Match patterns at seams. For striped or plaid fabrics, centre the dominant stripe on the seat and back. Calculate fabric requirement using the formula: (seat width + 20 cm) (seat depth + 20 cm) per cushion, plus 15% waste allowance. For a standard three-seater sofa, expect 812 metres of 140 cm-wide fabric.</p>

<h3>4. Cutting, Sewing, and Stitching Techniques</h3>
<p>Cutting fabric requires sharp shears or a rotary cutter, a cutting mat, and pattern weights. Always cut on a flat surface. For piping (welting), cut bias strips 45 cm wide, fold over cord, and stitch with a zipper foot. Sewing: use a heavy-duty walking-foot machine (e.g., Singer Heavy Duty or Juki) with size 1618 needles and bonded nylon thread. Stitch length 34 mm. For seams that will bear stress, use a double-stitched or lock-stitch seam. Box cushions require a zipper or Velcro closure; place the zipper on the back or bottom panel.</p>
<p>In African workshops where power supply may be unreliable, treadle machines (like the Butterfly or Singer 15K) remain viable for light upholstery. For heavy fabrics and leather, a hand-crank or industrial machine is necessary. Always test stitch tension on a scrap of the actual fabric before sewing the final piece.</p>

<h3>5. Applying Upholstery: Stapling, Tacking, and Finishing</h3>
<p>Stapling is the primary fixing method for modern upholstery. Use a pneumatic staple gun with 812 mm staples for fabric, and longer staples (1416 mm) for webbing and leather. Staple at 35 cm intervals, pulling fabric taut as you go. Work from the centre outward to avoid wrinkles. For traditional or high-end pieces, tacking with decorative nails (gimp pins) is still used especially in North and West African markets where hand-finished details command a premium.</p>
<p>Finishing includes: trimming excess fabric, applying dust cover (black cambric) to the underside, attaching glides or felt pads to protect floors, and adding decorative piping or double-welting. A well-finished piece should have no visible raw edges, no loose threads, and symmetrical seams.</p>

<div style="border:3px solid #c0392b; background:#fdecea; padding:16px; margin:20px 0; border-radius:6px;">
<h4 style="color:#c0392b; margin-top:0;"> SAFETY WARNING Chemical and Fire Hazards in Upholstery</h4>
<p style="margin-bottom:0;">Polyurethane foam and many upholstery adhesives release toxic fumes (isocyanates, VOCs) when cut, sanded, or sprayed. Always work in a well-ventilated area, wear a P2/N95 dust mask and safety goggles, and never use spray adhesive near an open flame. Foam dust is highly flammable keep heat sources away and clean dust daily. In workshops using petrol-powered generators, store fuel separately and ensure fire extinguishers (CO or dry powder) are within reach. Leather dyes and vinyl cleaners may contain solvents; use nitrile gloves and avoid skin contact. In many African cities, informal workshop fires spread quickly enforce a no-smoking rule and maintain clear exits.</p>
</div>

<h3>6. Leather and Vinyl Upholstery Methods</h3>
<p>Leather and vinyl are premium options with distinct handling requirements. Leather stretches, so it must be cut with the stretch direction running across the frame, not along it. Use a rotary cutter and a self-healing mat. Staple leather with stainless steel staples to avoid rust stains. Vinyl (PVC or PU) is more affordable and easier to clean, making it popular for restaurants, clinics, and public transport seating across Africa. However, vinyl can crack in extreme heat specify UV-stabilised or marine-grade vinyl for outdoor or semi-outdoor use.</p>
<p>For leather, use a leather needle (size 1820) and nylon thread. Avoid pinning use clips or tape. For vinyl, a standard heavy-duty needle works, but reduce stitch length to 2.53 mm to avoid perforation tearing. Both materials benefit from a backing layer of 5 mm foam or wadding to improve comfort and reduce noise.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Frame:</strong> Hardwood, glued and screwed joints, corner blocks fitted, no nails alone.</li>
<li><strong>Suspension:</strong> Webbing interlaced at 1520% tension; springs tied or clipped securely and evenly spaced.</li>
<li><strong>Foam:</strong> Seat 2835 kg/m, back 1822 kg/m; cut clean, shaped, and glued if layered.</li>
<li><strong>Fabric:</strong> Grain aligned vertically, pattern matched at seams, 15% waste allowance calculated.</li>
<li><strong>Sewing:</strong> Heavy-duty needle (1618), bonded nylon thread, 34 mm stitch, test tension first.</li>
<li><strong>Fixing:</strong> Staples 35 cm apart, centre-out, fabric taut; decorative tacks if specified.</li>
<li><strong>Finishing:</strong> Dust cover fitted, raw edges trimmed, glides attached, symmetry checked.</li>
<li><strong>Safety:</strong> Ventilation, mask, goggles, gloves, fire extinguisher, no smoking, clean foam dust daily.</li>
</ol>

By mastering foam density, fabric selection, and clean finishing, you reduce returns and build a reputation that supports premium pricing. Start with a standard three-seater sofa or a set of dining chairs, document your material costs, and reinvest profits into better tools (pneumatic stapler, walking-foot machine) that cut production time per unit.</p>
