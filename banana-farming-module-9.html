1. Determining Harvest Maturity

Harvest bananas at the right stage to get good taste, long shelf life, and top market price. For local markets, harvest when fruits are mature but still green, with no yellow. For export, harvest even earlier, when fingers are fully formed but before any ripening starts. In Africa, most bananas are harvested by checking the age of the bunch. From flowering to harvest, typical times are: 8090 days for sweet bananas (like Sukari), 90100 days for cooking bananas (like Matoke), and 100120 days for plantains. Mark the flowering date on a stick or record it in a notebook. When that number of days is reached, start checking.

Look at the fruit. Mature green bananas have rounded fingers with no sharp angles. The tips of the fingers lose their dark colour and become lighter green. The bunch stalk (peduncle) starts to turn from green to light yellow-green. Cut a test finger from the middle of the bunch and slice it across. The seeds should be small and black, and the flesh should be firm, not watery. If you see white sap, it's still too early. For export, the fruit must be at 7580% maturity, meaning the fingers are full but not ripe. A simple tool is a maturity chart with colour photos, available from extension officers.

Do not harvest too early: fruits will shrivel and never ripen well. Do not harvest too late: fruits ripen on the tree, split, and attract insects. In hot areas, maturity comes faster; in cool highlands, slower. Adjust days by 510 days based on altitude. For best results, harvest in the cool morning hours. If you sell to a processor, ask them for their preferred maturity stage. Always test a few bunches before harvesting the whole field.

2. Harvesting Techniques and Tools

Harvest bananas carefully to avoid bruises that reduce price. Use the right tools: a sharp curved knife or machete for cutting the bunch, and a forked pole (made from a strong stick with a V-shape at the top) to support the bunch as you cut. For tall plants, a ladder or climbing harness helps. Never pull the bunch down by handit damages the crown and fingers.

First, clear the area around the plant. Cut the bunch stalk about 3045 cm above the first hand. With one person supporting the bunch with the forked pole, another cuts the stalk with a clean, sharp knife. Lower the bunch gently to the ground or onto a clean tarp or banana leaves. Do not drop it. For export bananas, cut the bunch with a long stalk (about 1520 cm) and leave the bunch whole until you reach the packing shed.

After cutting, immediately place the bunch in the shade. If you leave it in the sun, the fruit heats up and ripens too fast. Use a wheelbarrow or a small cart with padding to move bunches to the collection point. Do not stack more than two bunches high. For plantains and cooking bananas, you can harvest the whole bunch and hang it in a cool shed.

Clean your tools with soap and water or a 10% bleach solution between plants to prevent spreading diseases like banana bunchy top virus. Wear gloves to protect your hands from sap, which stains and irritates. In dry areas, harvest early in the morning or late evening. In wet areas, avoid harvesting during rain to reduce fungal growth. Always harvest with a partner for safety and efficiency.

3. Dehanding and Cluster Preparation

Dehanding means separating the bunch into individual hands or smaller clusters. This is done in a shaded area, preferably on a clean table or mat. For local markets, many farmers sell whole bunches, but for higher prices, dehand and sell hands or clusters. For export, dehanding is done in a packing shed with running water and clean knives.

To dehand, hold the bunch by the stalk. Use a sharp, clean knife to cut between the hands, leaving a small piece of the crown (the cushion) attached to each hand. Do not cut into the fingers. For clusters, cut hands into groups of 36 fingers. This is popular for supermarkets and hotels. For plantains, you can leave hands whole or cut into smaller pieces for cooking. Always cut on a plastic or stainless steel surface, not on bare ground, to avoid contamination.

After dehanding, trim the fingers: remove any damaged, bruised, or undersized fingers. Also trim the flower remnants (the black tip) with a small knife or scissors. Be careful not to cut the skin of neighbouring fingers. For export, leave the crown intact and trim the fingers to a uniform length. For local markets, you can leave the natural crown but remove any dried flower parts.

Work quickly and keep the fruit in the shade. Sap from the cut crown can stain the fruit; wipe it off with a clean cloth. Do not wash the fruit yet if you are not going to pack immediately. Place dehanded clusters in clean plastic crates lined with banana leaves or newspaper. Do not pile more than three layers high. Keep the crates in a cool, shaded area until packaging. This step adds value and reduces waste.

4. Cleaning, Grading, and Sorting

Cleaning removes dirt, sap, and insects. For local markets, wipe each finger with a dry or slightly damp clean cloth. For export, wash the fruit in clean water with a small amount of food-grade detergent (2 ml per litre) and then rinse with clean water. Do not soak the fruit; dip quickly and shake off excess water. Use a soft brush for stubborn dirt. After washing, dry the fruit with a clean cloth or air-dry in the shade. Never use dirty waterit spreads disease.

Grading means sorting fruit by size, shape, and colour. For export, bananas are graded by finger length and diameter. Common grades: Extra Class (perfect, no defects), Class I (minor defects), Class II (more defects but still good). For local markets, grade into: Grade A (large, clean, no spots), Grade B (small or slight spots), and Grade C (damaged, for processing). Use a sizing ring or a simple ruler to measure finger diameter. For export, fingers should be 1214 cm long and 3.23.8 cm in diameter for standard Cavendish. For local, any size is acceptable but uniform size sells better.

Sorting also removes any fruit with signs of disease, insect damage, or over-ripeness. These should be sold quickly or processed into flour, chips, or animal feed. Keep different grades separate in different crates. Label each crate with the grade and date. For export, follow the buyer's specific grade standards. In Africa, many cooperatives have grading tables with pictures. Ask your extension officer for a copy.

After grading, pack immediately or store in a cool room (1315C) for up to 24 hours. Do not store near ethylene-producing fruits like mangoes or tomatoes. Clean your hands and tools between grades to avoid mixing. Good grading gets you better prices and repeat customers.

5. Packaging for Local and Export Markets

Packaging protects bananas from bruising, dirt, and moisture loss. For local markets, use clean plastic crates (60x40x30 cm) lined with banana leaves or newspaper. Do not use sacksthey crush the fruit. For short transport, you can use baskets lined with cloth. For export, use standard corrugated cardboard boxes with ventilation holes. The box size is usually 50x30x25 cm and holds about 1820 kg of bananas. Line the box with a perforated polyethylene liner (0.04 mm thick) to keep humidity high. Place a paper towel or absorbent pad at the bottom to soak up sap.

Pack bananas in clusters or hands, not single fingers, for export. Arrange them in the box in a single layer, with crowns facing down or to the side. Do not overfill; leave 23 cm of space at the top. For local markets, you can pack hands or whole bunches in crates, but keep them loose. For plantains, pack in crates with leaves between layers. Always label the box with: product name, grade, count (number of fingers or hands), weight, origin, and date. For export, include the farm code and traceability number.

Use cushioning material like shredded paper or foam nets for premium markets. Avoid using newspaper with coloured ink directly touching the fruit. For organic markets, use compostable liners and boxes. In Africa, many exporters use reusable plastic crates (foldable) to reduce cost. After packing, stack boxes no more than 5 high for cardboard, 8 high for plastic crates. Keep out of direct sun and rain. For air freight, pack in smaller boxes (10 kg) to meet airline rules. Always check with your buyer for specific packaging requirements before harvest.

6. Transportation and Cold Chain Management

Bananas are sensitive to heat, cold, and vibration. After harvest, the goal is to cool them quickly and keep them cool until they reach the buyer. For local markets, transport in the early morning or evening when it's cool. Use a covered vehicle or cover the crates with a tarpaulin. Do not transport in open trucks under hot sun. For short distances (under 2 hours), you can use a bicycle or motorcycle with a padded box. For longer distances, use a truck with good suspension. Stack crates securely to prevent movement.

For export or long-distance transport, you need a cold chain. Pre-cool the bananas to 1315C within 24 hours of harvest. Use a forced-air cooler or a cold room. Then load into a refrigerated truck or container set at 1315C. Do not go below 12Cchilling injury causes black spots and failure to ripen. Keep relative humidity at 9095%. Use a data logger to record temperature during transit. If you don't have a cold room, use a well-insulated truck with ice packs for short trips, but this is only for local high-value markets.

For air freight, pack in insulated boxes with gel packs. Keep the boxes in a cool area at the airport. For sea freight, use a controlled atmosphere container with 25% oxygen and 25% carbon dioxide to slow ripening. This allows 2030 days of transport. Always pre-cool before loading. In Africa, many exporters use refrigerated containers at the port. Work with a freight forwarder who understands perishables.

At the destination, unload quickly into a cool area. Do not break the cold chain. For local markets, sell within 23 days. For export, the buyer will ripen the bananas in special rooms. Keep records of temperatures and times. Good cold chain management reduces losses from 30% to under 5%.

<h2>Practical Reference: Harvesting &amp; Post-Harvest Handling of Bananas in Africa</h2>

<p>In African banana and plantain value chains from Uganda's matooke highlands to Ghana's Cavendish export belts and Kenya's Meru County plantations <strong>post-harvest losses routinely reach 2040%</strong> of the harvested crop. For a commercial grower, that is not an agronomic statistic; it is lost revenue, wasted input costs, and defaulted buyer contracts. This practical reference consolidates the field-tested numbers, tools, and workflows that determine whether a banana harvest becomes profit or waste. Treat it as a bench-side manual for farm managers, packhouse supervisors, and outgrower scheme coordinators.</p>

<h3>1. Determining Harvest Maturity</h3>
<p>Harvest timing is the single highest-leverage decision in the banana value chain. Fruit harvested too early never develops full flavour and fails organoleptic tests; fruit harvested too late bruises in transit and collapses at the ripening centre. Commercial growers use <strong>degree of fullness (angularity)</strong> and <strong>days from flower emergence (bunch age)</strong> as the two primary maturity indices.</p>

<h3>3. Dehanding and Cluster Preparation</h3>
<p>Dehanding is the conversion of a field bunch into marketable hands and clusters. In export operations, hands are cut into <strong>47 finger clusters</strong>; for local markets, whole hands are often acceptable. The critical control points are:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Timing:</strong> Dehand within 24 hours of harvest to limit latex flow and field heat accumulation.</li>
<li><strong>Latex management:</strong> Allow latex to drain from the cut crown for 510 minutes before placing fruit on packing lines. Latex stains reduce grade.</li>
<li><strong>Crown cut:</strong> Cut crowns cleanly with a single stroke; ragged crowns invite fungal entry (Colletotrichum, Fusarium).</li>
<li><strong>Cluster sizing:</strong> Standard export cluster = 47 fingers, 100140 g per finger for Cavendish. Local market tolerance is wider.</li>
</ul>

<h3>4. Cleaning, Grading, and Sorting</h3>
<p>Grading converts a heterogeneous field harvest into homogeneous, buyer-spec lots. In African export operations, grading follows the <strong>UNECE FFV-14 standard</strong> or buyer-specific specifications (e.g., Chiquita, Del Monte, or European retail private labels). For regional markets (Nairobi, Kampala, Accra, Lagos), grading is simpler but still critical for price realisation.</p>

<h3>5. Packaging for Local and Export Markets</h3>
<p>Packaging is a cost centre and a quality protector simultaneously. The wrong package adds 1015% to fruit loss and can void a buyer contract.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Export:</strong> 18.5 kg telescopic corrugated cartons with 0.51.0% ventilation holes; polyliner bags (0.04 mm) with ethylene absorbent sachets for extended transit. Palletisation: 48 cartons per Euro pallet, 1.2 m high.</li>
<li><strong>Regional/urban:</strong> 1015 kg returnable plastic crates (stackable, washable) increasingly mandated in Kenya and Rwanda to replace single-use sacks.</li>
<li><strong>Local/rural:</strong> 2030 kg woven polypropylene sacks with internal lining; acceptable only for same-day sale.</li>
<li><strong>Labelling:</strong> Include farm ID, harvest date, grade, and traceability code. EU buyers require GlobalG.A.P. and often a QR-coded traceability record.</li>
</ul>

<h3>6. Transportation and Cold Chain Management</h3>
<p>Banana pulp temperature is the single most important variable in shelf life. Every 10C rise above 13C roughly doubles the respiration rate and halves remaining shelf life. The optimal transport window is <strong>1314C for export Cavendish</strong>; local cooking bananas tolerate 1518C for short hauls.</p>

<div style="background:#fdecea;border-left:6px solid #c0392b;padding:14px 18px;margin:18px 0;border-radius:4px;">
<strong style="color:#c0392b;"> SAFETY WARNING Cold Chain Breaks and Chilling Injury</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Never store or transport bananas below <strong>12C</strong>. Temperatures of 812C cause <em>chilling injury</em>: skin discolouration, failure to ripen, and pulp breakdown often invisible at arrival but manifesting 35 days later at the ripening centre or retail shelf. This results in full consignment rejection. Additionally, <strong>never mix bananas with ethylene-producing fruits</strong> (tomatoes, avocados, apples) or with strong-smelling cargo (fish, onions, diesel) in the same vehicle. Ethylene cross-contamination triggers premature ripening; odour contamination is unremovable. Ensure refrigerated trucks are pre-cooled to 13C <em>before</em> loading, and that pulp temperature not air temperature is monitored with a calibrated probe at 3 points per load.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Maturity confirmed:</strong> Fruit at Stage 3 (export) or Stage 4 (local) using angularity + bunch age (days from flowering).</li>
<li><strong>Harvest tools sharp and sanitised:</strong> Knives sharpened daily; dehanding blades dipped in 1% sodium hypochlorite between bunches.</li>
<li><strong>Field-to-packhouse time &lt; 4 hours:</strong> Shade bunches immediately; never leave fruit in direct sun.</li>
<li><strong>Latex drained and crowns cleanly cut:</strong> 510 minute drain; single-stroke crown cut; no ragged edges.</li>
<li><strong>Grading to spec:</strong> Grade I/II/III separated; rejects removed; grade recorded per lot.</li>
<li><strong>Packaging correct for destination:</strong> Ventilated export cartons (18.5 kg) or returnable crates (1015 kg); labels and traceability codes applied.</li>
<li><strong>Cold chain intact:</strong> Pulp temperature 1314C; pre-cooled truck; no ethylene or odour cross-contamination.</li>
<li><strong>Records complete:</strong> Harvest date, field block, grade, weight, buyer, and dispatch temperature logged for traceability and buyer audits.</li>
</ol>
