1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Feeding your layers correctly is the difference between a flock that gives you 10 eggs a day and one that gives you 25. In Africa, where feed costs can eat up 70% of your egg income, you cannot afford to guess. A good feeding programme ensures each hen gets the right nutrients at the right time, so she lays consistently for 72 weeks or more. Without it, you will see thin shells, small eggs, and hens that stop laying after a few months. The goal is simple: maximise eggs per bag of feed. For a flock of 100 hens, a proper programme can mean the difference between 60 and 90 eggs per day. That is real money. You also reduce waste, because hens fed correctly eat only what they need. In this module, you will learn exactly what to feed, when, and how much. We focus on practical steps using locally available ingredients like maize, soya, fishmeal, and limestone. You will also learn to mix your own feed to cut costs. Remember, feeding is not just about quantityit is about balance. Too much of one thing (like maize) makes hens fat and lazy; too little of another (like calcium) gives you cracked eggs. By the end of this module, you will be able to set up a feeding plan that keeps your hens healthy and your egg tray full every morning.

2. Tools and Materials

To run a proper layer feeding programme, you need more than just a bag of feed. Start with the right feeders. For 100 hens, use at least 10 linear feeders (each 1 metre long) or 20 round hanging feeders. Hanging feeders reduce waste because hens cannot scratch feed onto the floor. You also need a feed scoop (2 kg size) and a weighing scale (up to 50 kg) to measure ingredients if you mix your own feed. For mixing, a clean concrete floor or a large plastic sheet works well. A shovel or spade for turning the feed. For storage, use airtight plastic drums or metal silos to keep feed dry and away from rats. Moisture and pests destroy feed value quickly. You will also need a record book and pen to track daily feed consumption and egg production. A simple thermometer helps you monitor feed storage temperatureheat above 30C can cause mould. For ingredients, you need a reliable supplier of maize, soya bean meal, fishmeal (or sunflower cake), limestone or oyster shell, and a mineral-vitamin premix. A hammer mill or a local grinding service is necessary if you mix your own feed. Finally, a water source is criticallayers drink twice as much as they eat, and without clean water, feed is wasted. Keep a 20-litre bucket and a drinker for every 50 hens. These tools are affordable and can be found in most agro-vet shops or local markets. Investing in them now saves money later.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps to feed your layers correctly from day one of laying (around 18-20 weeks) until you sell them at 72-80 weeks. Step 1: Choose the right feed type. Use 'Layer Mash' or 'Layer Pellets' with 16-18% protein and 3.5-4.5% calcium. If you mix your own, use this formula for 100 kg: 60 kg maize, 20 kg soya meal, 8 kg fishmeal, 8 kg limestone, 3 kg premix, 1 kg salt. Step 2: Feed at the same times dailyearly morning (6-7 am) and late afternoon (4-5 pm). Hens are creatures of habit. Step 3: Give the correct amount. A hen needs about 120-130 grams of feed per day. For 100 hens, that is 12-13 kg per day, or roughly one 50 kg bag every 4 days. Adjust based on body weight: if hens look thin, increase by 5 grams per hen. Step 4: Provide clean water at all times. A layer drinks 200-300 ml per day. Step 5: Add extra calcium (oyster shell or limestone grit) in a separate feeder. This is especially important after 40 weeks when eggshell quality drops. Step 6: Monitor feed intake weekly. If consumption drops suddenly, check for disease or stress. Step 7: Keep feed freshnever use mouldy feed. Store in a cool, dry place. Step 8: Record daily feed used and eggs collected. This helps you spot problems early. For free-range systems, supplement with 50-70 grams of commercial feed per hen per day, as they will find some natural feed. Always provide grit for digestion.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Feeding too much maize. Maize is energy-rich but low in protein. Hens get fat and lay fewer eggs. Fix: Stick to a balanced rationnever more than 65% maize. Use the formula in Lesson 3. Mistake 2: Not providing enough calcium. Thin-shelled or soft eggs are a sign. Fix: Add 2-3 grams of limestone or oyster shell per hen per day, especially in the afternoon. Mistake 3: Using the same feed for chicks and layers. Chicks need 20% protein, layers need 16-18%. Fix: Buy separate feeds or mix according to age. Mistake 4: Letting feeders run empty for hours. Hens stop laying if stressed. Fix: Fill feeders twice daily and never let them go empty. Mistake 5: Storing feed on the floor. Moisture and rats ruin it. Fix: Use pallets and airtight containers. Mistake 6: Overfeeding or underfeeding. Weigh feed for a week to calibrate your scoop. Fix: Use a scale to check your scoop holds exactly 2 kg. Mistake 7: Ignoring water quality. Dirty water reduces feed intake. Fix: Clean drinkers daily and provide fresh water. Mistake 8: Sudden feed changes. This causes digestive upset and egg drop. Fix: Transition over 7 days by mixing old and new feed. Mistake 9: Not adding grit. Without grit, hens cannot digest whole grains. Fix: Provide a small amount of grit weekly. Mistake 10: Feeding mouldy or expired feed. This can kill your flock. Fix: Smell feed before useif it smells musty, throw it away. Each of these mistakes costs you eggs and money. Correct them early and your flock will reward you.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety in feeding is not just about the hensit is about you and your workers. Always wear a dust mask when mixing feed, especially if you use fishmeal or premix. Dust can cause breathing problems. Wash your hands after handling feed, and never eat or smoke in the feed storage area. Store feed away from chemicals like pesticides or fertilisersfumes can contaminate feed and kill your hens. Keep feed bags off the floor on wooden pallets to prevent moisture and mould. Maintain a temperature below 25C if possible; heat destroys vitamins. Use feed within 4 weeks of mixing or opening a bag. Rotate stockuse older feed first. Best practice: Weigh feed daily for the first month to ensure accuracy. Keep a record of feed intake per hen. A sudden drop of more than 10% means something is wrongcheck for disease, heat stress, or water shortage. Provide feed in clean feeders; remove any wet or caked feed immediately. Never feed kitchen scraps that contain meat or high saltthis can cause disease. For free-range, ensure hens have shade and access to clean water. In hot climates, feed early morning and late evening when it is cooler. During rainy season, protect feed from humidity. Finally, train anyone who helps you. A mistake by a worker can cost you a week's production. Use a simple checklist: feed type, amount, time, water, and record. Follow it daily. These habits keep your flock healthy and your business profitable.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it is time to put what you learned into practice. This exercise will help you set up a feeding programme for your own flock. You will need: a notebook, a pen, a weighing scale, a feed scoop, and your current feed. If you do not have a flock yet, use a neighbour's flock or imagine a flock of 100 hens. Step 1: Calculate daily feed needs. For 100 layers, multiply 100 hens by 125 grams = 12,500 grams = 12.5 kg per day. Step 2: Weigh your scoop. Fill your scoop with feed and weigh it. If it holds 2 kg, you need 6.25 scoops per day. Round to 6 scoops in the morning and 6 in the afternoon, then adjust. Step 3: Mix a small batch of feed. Using the formula from Lesson 3, mix 10 kg: 6 kg maize, 2 kg soya, 0.8 kg fishmeal, 0.8 kg limestone, 0.3 kg premix, 0.1 kg salt. Weigh each ingredient. Step 4: Feed your hens. Give half in the morning and half in the afternoon. Provide fresh water. Add a handful of oyster shell in a separate feeder. Step 5: Record for 7 days. Each day, write down: date, feed given (kg), eggs collected, and any problems (e.g., soft eggs, feed leftover). Step 6: Review after 7 days. Calculate feed per egg. If you used 87.5 kg feed and collected 560 eggs, that is 0.156 kg per egg. A good target is 0.15-0.17 kg per egg. If higher, check for waste or overfeeding. Step 7: Adjust. If hens are thin, add 5 grams per hen. If fat, reduce by 5 grams. Share your results with a fellow farmer. This exercise teaches you to manage feed for profit. Do it every season to stay sharp.

<h2>Practical Reference: Layer Feeding Programme</h2>

<p>Feeding is the single largest recurring cost in a layer enterprise typically 6575% of total production cost. For a smallholder or semi-commercial egg business in Africa, the difference between a well-run feeding programme and a poorly managed one is often the difference between profit and loss. This practical reference consolidates the essential numbers, tools, tables and checks you need to run a disciplined feeding programme for a layer flock under African conditions. It assumes you are running a daily egg sales operation and need consistent, predictable feed intake to protect both egg output and margin.</p>

<p>Before you read further, note the core business logic: a layer hen converts feed into eggs at a ratio of roughly 1.82.2 kg of feed per kg of egg, depending on breed, age and feed quality. If feed is wasted, under-supplied or badly formulated, your cost per egg rises and your daily sales margin collapses. The tables and checklist below are designed to be used at the feed store, in the poultry house, and at the record book.</p>

<h3>Feeding Method: Step-by-Step Discipline</h3>

<ol>
<li><strong>Weigh the feed daily.</strong> Use a hanging scale or calibrated scoop. Do not estimate. Over-feeding wastes money; under-feeding cuts egg numbers.</li>
<li><strong>Feed twice daily.</strong> Give roughly 60% in the early morning and 40% in the late afternoon. This matches natural intake patterns and reduces stress in heat.</li>
<li><strong>Use linear feeders.</strong> Allow 1012 cm of feeder space per bird. Crowding causes uneven intake some birds get fat, others stay thin.</li>
<li><strong>Keep clean water available at all times.</strong> Layers drink 1.52 times their feed weight in water. Water restriction cuts intake and egg size immediately.</li>
<li><strong>Provide grit and oyster shell separately.</strong> Free-choice calcium helps hens self-regulate shell formation, especially in late lay.</li>
<li><strong>Record daily intake.</strong> A sudden drop of 5 g/bird/day is an early warning of disease, heat stress or bad feed.</li>
<li><strong>Adjust for heat.</strong> In temperatures above 30C, shift more feed to early morning and consider a slightly higher nutrient density.</li>
</ol>

<h3>Common Mistakes and Their Business Cost</h3>

<ul>
<li><strong>Feeding by eye, not by weight.</strong> Leads to 515% waste on a 500-bird flock that is the margin on dozens of eggs per day.</li>
<li><strong>Using the same ration for chicks, growers and layers.</strong> Calcium and protein needs differ sharply. Wrong ration = poor shells and stunted birds.</li>
<li><strong>Buying the cheapest feed without checking protein.</strong> Cheap feed often has low protein and high fibre birds eat more and lay less.</li>
<li><strong>Storing feed on the floor.</strong> Moisture and rodents cause mould and loss. Always use pallets and sealed containers.</li>
<li><strong>Ignoring the feed intake record.</strong> Without records you cannot detect problems until egg numbers fall by then you have lost a week of margin.</li>
</ul>
