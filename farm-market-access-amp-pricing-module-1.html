1. Mapping local buyers: who buys what in your village or town

Start by walking through your village or town and writing down every place where food is bought and sold. This includes the main market, small shops (dukas), roadside stalls, schools, restaurants, and even individuals who buy in bulk to resell. Carry a notebook and pen. For each buyer, note what they buy, how much they buy per week, and what price they pay. For example, a school may buy 50 kg of maize and 20 kg of beans every week. A restaurant might buy 10 crates of tomatoes and 5 sacks of onions per week.

Next, talk to these buyers. Ask simple questions: What do you buy most? Where do you buy it from now? How much do you pay? How often do you need it? Write down their answers. Also note if they complain about anything, like late deliveries or poor quality. This tells you where you might fit in.

After mapping all buyers, group them by type: households, traders, institutions (schools, hospitals), and processors (millers, oil pressers). For each group, list the top 3 crops they buy. For example, households may buy maize, beans, and vegetables. Traders may buy maize, groundnuts, and soybeans. Institutions may buy maize, beans, and cooking oil. Processors may buy groundnuts, sunflower, and cassava.

Now create a simple map on paper. Draw your village in the center, and draw lines to each buyer. Write the crop and quantity on each line. This visual map helps you see who buys what and where the gaps are. For instance, you might notice that no one is supplying fresh vegetables to the school, or that traders travel 20 km to buy onions. These are opportunities.

Finally, keep this map updated. Buyers' needs change with seasons and prices. Revisit your map every few months. This simple exercise gives you a clear picture of your local market and helps you decide what to grow and who to sell to.

2. Identifying customer needs: what do households and traders want?

Households and traders have different needs. Households buy small quantities for daily meals. They want fresh food, good quality, and low prices. They often buy on credit or in small amounts (e.g., a cup of maize, a bunch of greens). Traders buy in bulk to resell. They want consistent supply, uniform quality, and a price that lets them make a profit. They may also want transport and packaging.

To identify needs, talk to at least 10 households and 5 traders. Ask households: What do you buy daily? What do you buy weekly? What quality do you look for? How much do you pay? What would make you buy from a farmer instead of a shop? For example, a household might say they buy 2 kg of tomatoes per week, prefer firm tomatoes, and pay 500 shillings per kg. They might buy from a farmer if the price is lower or if the farmer delivers.

For traders, ask: What crops do you trade? How much do you buy per week? What price do you pay? What quality do you require? What problems do you face with current suppliers? For instance, a trader might buy 100 kg of maize per week, pay 800 shillings per kg, and need dry, clean maize. They might complain that suppliers sometimes deliver wet maize.

Record all answers in a notebook. Then look for patterns. If many households want fresh vegetables daily, you could grow a small vegetable garden and sell directly. If traders want consistent maize, you could contract with them to supply a fixed amount each week.

Also consider what customers are willing to pay extra for. Some may pay more for organic produce, or for produce that is already sorted and cleaned. For example, a trader might pay 10% more for maize that is already graded and bagged. This adds value and increases your income.

Remember, needs change with seasons and income. During harvest, prices are low and households may buy in bulk. During lean seasons, they buy small amounts and prices are high. Traders may switch crops based on demand. Keep asking questions and adjust your production and sales to meet these changing needs.

3. Seasonal demand patterns for common African crops

Demand for crops changes with seasons. In many African countries, there is a dry season and a rainy season. During the rainy season, many crops are harvested, so supply is high and prices are low. During the dry season, supply is low and prices are high. For example, in East Africa, maize prices can drop to 500 shillings per kg at harvest in August, and rise to 1,200 shillings per kg in February. Tomatoes may sell for 300 shillings per kg in the rainy season and 1,000 shillings per kg in the dry season.

To take advantage of this, you need to know the seasonal calendar for your area. Talk to other farmers, extension officers, and traders. Ask: When is harvest for each crop? When do prices start to rise? When do they fall? Write this down for the top 5 crops you grow or plan to grow. For example, in Nigeria, yam is harvested from August to October, and prices are lowest then. From March to June, prices are highest.

Also consider demand from institutions. Schools buy more food during school terms. During holidays, demand drops. Hospitals and restaurants may have steady demand year-round. For example, a school may buy 200 kg of beans per week during term, but nothing during holidays. If you supply schools, you need to plan for these breaks.

Another pattern is festive seasons. During Christmas, Eid, or other holidays, demand for certain foods increases. For instance, demand for rice, chicken, and cooking oil goes up during Christmas. Prices may rise by 20-30%. You can plan to harvest or buy extra stock before these periods.

To use seasonal patterns, you can store crops when prices are low and sell when prices are high. But storage must be done properly to avoid losses. For grains, use airtight bags or silos. For perishables like tomatoes, you cannot store long, so you might process them into tomato paste or sauce. Alternatively, you can grow crops that mature in the off-season, like irrigated vegetables. This requires water and investment, but can give high returns. Keep a seasonal calendar and update it each year based on your observations.

4. How to talk to potential buyers: questions to ask

Talking to potential buyers is a skill. You need to be polite, clear, and listen more than you talk. Start by introducing yourself and your farm. Say what you grow and that you are looking for buyers. Then ask open-ended questions. Here is a list of questions you can ask:

1. What crops do you buy regularly? 2. How much do you buy per week or per month? 3. Where do you currently buy from? 4. What price do you pay? 5. What quality do you need? (e.g., dry, clean, sorted) 6. How do you prefer to receive the goods? (e.g., delivered to your shop, picked up from farm) 7. What problems do you have with your current suppliers? 8. Would you buy from a local farmer if the price and quality are good? 9. How do you pay? (cash, mobile money, credit) 10. What would make you switch suppliers?

Write down the answers. Do not interrupt. If they ask you questions, answer honestly. If you don't know something, say you will find out and get back to them. For example, if they ask if you can supply 500 kg of maize per week, and you are not sure, say you will check and call them back.

After the conversation, summarize what you learned. Note their name, phone number, and what they buy. If they are interested, agree on a next step. For example, you could say, "I will bring a sample of my maize next week for you to check." Or, "I can deliver 50 kg of tomatoes every Tuesday."

Practice these questions with a friend before you go. Role-play the conversation. This will make you more confident. Also, dress neatly and be on time. First impressions matter. If you are visiting a market, go early in the morning when traders are not too busy. If you are visiting a school, make an appointment with the bursar or head teacher.

Remember, building relationships takes time. Even if a buyer is not interested now, they may buy from you later. Always thank them for their time. Keep a record of all the people you talk to, and follow up after a few weeks. A simple phone call or visit can keep the relationship alive.

5. Recording customer information without a smartphone

You don't need a smartphone to keep good customer records. A simple notebook and pen work perfectly. Buy a hardcover notebook that will not tear easily. Write the date, customer name, phone number (if they have one), what they buy, quantity, price, and any notes. For example: "15/03/2025 - Mary's Restaurant - 10 kg tomatoes - 800 shillings/kg - prefers ripe tomatoes - pays on delivery."

Divide the notebook into sections. One section for households, one for traders, one for institutions. Or use one page per customer. At the top of each page, write the customer's details. Below, record each transaction or conversation. This makes it easy to find information when you need it.

If you have many customers, use a simple index at the front. Write the customer's name and the page number. This saves time. You can also use symbols or colors. For example, put a star next to customers who pay on time, or a circle for those who need delivery.

For illiterate farmers, you can use drawings or symbols. Draw a picture of the crop (e.g., a maize cob) and write the number of bags. Use tally marks for quantities. For prices, you can use colored stickers or tokens. But it's best to learn basic numbers and letters if possible. Ask a family member or friend to help you write if needed.

Keep the notebook in a safe, dry place. Protect it from rain and insects. At the end of each week, review your notes. See who bought what, and who owes you money. This helps you plan your next harvest and follow up on payments.

You can also use a calendar to record seasonal demand. Mark the months when each customer buys more. For example, a school buys more during term time. Write this on a wall calendar. This visual reminder helps you prepare.

Finally, back up your information. If you have a trusted friend or family member with a phone, ask them to take photos of the pages once a month. Or simply copy the most important details into a second notebook. Losing your records can cost you money and customers.

6. Spotting opportunities: gaps in the local market

A gap is something that customers want but cannot easily get. To spot gaps, look for complaints, long travel distances, and high prices. For example, if villagers travel 30 km to buy fresh fish, there is a gap for a local fish supplier. If restaurants complain about inconsistent tomato supply, there is a gap for a reliable tomato farmer.

Start by reviewing your buyer map and customer notes. Look for crops that are in high demand but few people supply. For instance, you might notice that many households buy onions, but only one trader sells them, and often runs out. That is a gap. You could grow onions and sell directly to households.

Also look at quality gaps. Maybe traders sell maize that is not properly dried, leading to mold. If you can produce clean, dry maize, you can charge a higher price. Or maybe customers want pre-packaged vegetables, but no one offers them. You could wash, sort, and bag vegetables for busy households.

Another gap is timing. If a crop is only available during harvest, but customers want it year-round, you can store or preserve it. For example, you could dry tomatoes and sell them in the off-season. Or you could grow crops under irrigation to supply during dry spells.

Talk to customers about what they wish they could buy. Ask: What do you need but cannot find? What would you buy more of if it was available? Their answers will point to gaps. For example, a school might say they want fresh fruit for students, but no local farmer supplies it. You could plant a small orchard or buy fruit from other farmers and resell.

Once you identify a gap, test it. Start small. Grow a small plot of the crop or offer a small quantity. See if customers buy. If they do, expand. If not, try another gap. Keep your risk low.

Also consider gaps in services. Maybe farmers need transport to take produce to market, but no one offers it. You could start a small transport business with a bicycle or motorbike. Or maybe traders need storage, and you have a spare room. Renting out storage is a service gap.

Finally, keep your eyes open. Opportunities change with seasons and trends. What is a gap today may be filled tomorrow. Regularly talk to customers and watch the market. The best opportunities are often found by simply asking and listening.

2. Customer needs: what households and traders actually want

Households and traders buy the same crop for different reasons, and they will judge your produce on different criteria. Households buy for today's meal: freshness, small quantity, low price, and the ability to buy on credit until payday. â  SAFETY WARNING â Cash, credit and personal data
  • Never carry large cash alone to or from a market. Split money between two pockets, use mobile money where available, and arrange for a trusted person to accompany you on high-value sale days.
  • Do not lend produce on credit without a written record. A notebook entry with the buyer's name, date, quantity, price and agreed repayment date is legally useful and protects both sides.
  • Protect customer information. Names, phone numbers and payment habits are personal data. Keep your notebook locked or with you; do not share a customer's details with other traders without their consent.
  • Be cautious of buyers offering prices far above the local market. Advance-fee scams and fake "export" buyers target smallholders. Verify through your cooperative or extension officer before delivering goods.

4. How to talk to potential buyers: the question script

Most farmers lose sales because they tell instead of ask. Use these questions in order â they take ten minutes and will tell you whether a buyer is worth pursuing.

  1. What do you buy most of in a normal week? (Establishes the real product, not the one you want to sell.)
  2. Where do you buy it now, and what do you pay? (Gives you a price benchmark.)
  3. What makes you reject a supplier? (Reveals quality thresholds â moisture, size, timing.)
  4. How much would you buy from me, and how often? (Tests volume, not just interest.)
  5. How do you prefer to pay â cash on delivery, mobile money, or after resale? (Payment risk is the most common cause of failed deals.)
  6. What would make you switch to me for the whole season? (Opens the door to a supply agreement.)
  7. Who else should I talk to? (Referrals are the cheapest route to new buyers.)

6. Spotting gaps in the local market

A market gap is not "a product nobody sells." It is a need that is currently met badly, expensively, or only seasonally. Look for these five signals:

  • Long queues or empty stalls at a particular time of day or week â a supply timing gap.
  • Buyers travelling far for a product you could grow â a distance gap.
  • Complaints about quality ("the ones from X are always wet") â a quality gap.
  • Processors running below capacity â a raw-material supply gap.
  • Products imported from another region that grow locally â a substitution gap.

Once you spot a gap, test it with a small batch before committing land or capital. Sell to ten buyers, record what happens, and only then scale.
