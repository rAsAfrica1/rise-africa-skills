1. Hiring and Training Local Youth for Moulding and Firing

When you start making bricks, you need reliable helpers. Look for young people aged 18 to 30 in your village or nearby town. They are strong, eager to learn, and often unemployed. Post a notice at the market, church, or mosque, or talk to the local chief or youth leader. Ask for people who are punctual and not afraid of hard work. You do not need formal education, but basic counting and measuring helps. Start by hiring 3 to 5 workers for a small operation. Pay a fair daily wage from the first daydo not ask for free trial work. In many African countries, a common starting wage is 5,000 to 10,000 shillings per day (about $2$5), depending on the region.

Training should be hands-on and short. Spend the first morning showing them how to mix clay and sand in the right ratiousually 3 parts clay to 1 part sand for solid bricks. Demonstrate how to fill the mould, strike off excess, and turn out the brick onto a drying rack. Let each worker try while you watch and correct. For firing, teach them how to stack bricks in the kiln with gaps for air, and how to control the fire by adding wood or coal slowly. Safety during firing is criticalshow them how to use long-handled tools and never stand directly in front of the kiln opening.

After the first week, assign one experienced worker as a team leader. Pay them a small extra allowance, like 1,000 shillings per day, to guide others. Hold a 10-minute meeting every morning to set goals: Today we mould 500 bricks or We load 1,000 bricks into the kiln. Check work daily and give feedback. If someone consistently makes poor bricks, retrain them instead of firing them immediately. Keep a simple attendance book and pay at the end of each week. This builds trust and reduces turnover. Remember, trained workers are your biggest assettreat them well and they will stay with you for years.

2. Setting Fair Wages and Working Hours

Fair pay keeps your team loyal and productive. Before you hire, find out the local daily wage for casual labour. In rural areas, it might be 3,000 to 5,000 shillings; in towns, 7,000 to 15,000 shillings. Pay at least the local rate, and if possible, a little more. You can also pay by piece rate: for example, 200 shillings for every 100 bricks moulded, or 500 shillings for every 1,000 bricks stacked in the kiln. Piece rate motivates fast work, but make sure workers still earn a fair daily minimum. Never pay less than the agreed rate. Pay on timeevery Friday or at the end of the month. If you delay, workers will leave or steal.

Working hours should be reasonable. In hot African climates, start early at 6:30 AM and finish by 2:00 PM, with a 30-minute break for tea or porridge. That is 7.5 hours of work. Avoid forcing overtime, but if you need extra bricks for a big order, offer overtime pay at 1.5 times the normal rate. For example, if the daily wage is 6,000 shillings for 8 hours, overtime is 1,125 shillings per extra hour. Provide clean drinking water and a shaded rest area. If workers must travel far, consider providing a simple lunch or a small transport allowance.

You must also handle rest days. Give one day off per week, usually Sunday, and allow workers to rest on public holidays. If they work on a rest day, pay double. Keep a written record of hours and wages for each worker. Use a notebook or a simple phone app. At the end of each month, show each worker their total pay and deductions, if any. Deductions should only be for things like advances you gave them. Never deduct for tools or mistakesthat is your cost of doing business. When workers feel paid fairly and treated with respect, they work harder and stay longer. That saves you money on training new people and keeps your brick quality high.

3. Providing Basic Safety Gear: Gloves, Boots, and Masks

Brick making is dirty and dangerous. Dust, sharp edges, and heat can hurt your workers. You must provide basic safety gear. Start with heavy-duty gloves. Leather or thick cotton gloves cost about 2,000 to 5,000 shillings per pair. They protect hands from cuts when handling rough bricks and from burns when touching hot kiln bricks. Buy one pair per worker and replace them when they get holes. Next, boots. Rubber or leather boots with steel toes are best. They cost 10,000 to 25,000 shillings per pair. They protect feet from falling bricks, sharp objects, and hot embers. Do not let workers wear sandals or flip-flops on site. If boots are too expensive, at least provide closed leather shoes.

Dust masks are also essential, especially during mixing and firing. Clay dust contains silica, which can damage lungs over time. Simple disposable masks cost 500 to 1,000 shillings each. Buy a box of 50 and give a new mask each day to workers who are mixing or near the kiln. For firing, use a mask with a filterthese cost 5,000 to 10,000 shillings but last a month. Also provide eye protection: cheap plastic goggles cost 2,000 shillings and prevent flying chips from entering eyes. For workers near the kiln, add a heavy apron or overall to protect against sparks.

You must enforce the use of this gear. Do not allow anyone to work without gloves and boots. Keep a spare set for visitors or new workers. Set a rule: no gear, no work. If a worker refuses to wear gear, send them home without pay for that day. It sounds harsh, but it prevents injuries that could cost you more in medical bills and lost work. Also, keep a first-aid box on site with bandages, antiseptic, and burn cream. Train one worker as a safety officer to check gear daily and refill supplies. The cost of safety gear is small compared to the cost of an accident. A safe site is a productive site.

4. Preventing Injuries from Heavy Lifting and Hot Kilns

Heavy lifting and hot kilns cause most injuries on brick sites. Teach your workers to lift correctly. They should bend their knees, keep their back straight, and hold the load close to their body. Never twist while lifting. For bricks, use a wheelbarrow or a hand cart instead of carrying stacks by hand. A wheelbarrow costs 30,000 to 50,000 shillings and can move 50 bricks at once. For very heavy loads, use two people or a simple pulley. Set a weight limit: no single worker should carry more than 20 kilograms alone. If a brick weighs 3 kilograms, that is about 7 bricks. Use a scale to check loads. Rotate tasks every two hours so no one does heavy lifting all day.

Hot kilns are extremely dangerous. The outside of a working kiln can reach 200C or more. Never let workers touch the kiln walls with bare hands. Use long-handled tongs (at least 1 metre long) to move hot bricks. These cost 10,000 to 20,000 shillings. Workers must wear leather gloves, heavy boots, and a face shield or goggles when opening the kiln. Open the kiln slowly to let heat escape gradually. Never stand directly in front of the openingstand to the side. Keep a bucket of water and a first-aid kit nearby. If someone gets burned, cool the burn with clean running water for 10 minutes, then cover with a clean cloth. Do not apply oil or mud.

Also prevent heat stress. Provide shade and plenty of drinking water. Workers should drink 1 cup of water every 20 minutes. Add a pinch of salt to the water to replace sweat. Work in shifts near the kiln: 30 minutes on, 30 minutes off. Watch for signs of heat exhaustion: dizziness, headache, nausea, or heavy sweating. If you see these, move the worker to a cool place, give water, and let them rest. If they faint, seek medical help. Finally, keep the site clean. Remove broken bricks and tools from walkways. Mark hot areas with warning signs. A little planning prevents painful accidents and keeps your team working.

5. Organising Work Teams for Efficient Production

To make bricks efficiently, divide your workers into teams with clear roles. For a small operation of 6 to 10 workers, create three teams: mixing, moulding, and firing. The mixing team (2 people) digs clay, adds sand and water, and mixes until smooth. They should produce enough mixture for 500 bricks per day. The moulding team (3 people) fills moulds, strikes off excess, and turns out bricks onto drying racks. They can make 150 to 200 bricks per person per day. The firing team (2 people) stacks dried bricks into the kiln and controls the fire. They work in shifts because firing takes 3 to 5 days. One person can be a floater to help where needed.

Assign a team leader for each group. The leader is responsible for quality and speed. For example, the moulding leader checks that each brick is the right size and shape. The firing leader monitors temperature using a simple pyrometer or by watching the colour of the firebright orange is good, white is too hot. Pay team leaders an extra 1,000 to 2,000 shillings per day. Hold a 15-minute meeting every morning to set daily targets. Write the target on a board: Today: 600 moulded, 400 stacked. At the end of the day, count what was done. If a team meets the target, give a small bonus, like 500 shillings per person. If they miss it, find out whymaybe the clay was too wet or the moulds were broken.

Organise the site so teams do not get in each others way. Keep clay piles near the mixing area, drying racks near the moulding area, and the kiln a safe distance away. Use a simple flow: clay mixing moulding drying firing storage. Make sure tools are shared and returned to a central point. Have a schedule for drying: bricks need 3 to 7 days in the sun before firing. Turn them once after 2 days. With good organisation, you can increase production from 500 to 1,500 bricks per day without hiring more people. Efficient teams also reduce accidents because everyone knows their job and stays out of danger zones.

6. Keeping Workers Motivated During Peak Seasons

Peak seasonslike before the rainy season when everyone wants bricksput pressure on your team. You need to keep workers motivated so they do not quit or slow down. Start by paying a peak-season bonus. For example, if a worker normally earns 6,000 shillings per day, add 1,000 to 2,000 shillings per day during the busiest month. Or offer a weekly bonus of 5,000 shillings if the team meets production targets. Announce the bonus at the start of the season so everyone knows what to aim for. Also provide extra food. A hot lunch of rice and beans costs 1,500 shillings per person but gives energy and shows you care. On very hot days, bring cold water or a local drink like maas or lemonade.

Recognise good work publicly. At the end of each week, thank the team and name the top worker. Give them a small gift: a new pair of gloves, a t-shirt, or 2,000 shillings. This costs little but boosts morale. Listen to complaints. If workers say the moulds are too heavy or the kiln is too hot, fix the problem quickly. Do not ignore them. Also, rotate jobs so no one gets bored or exhausted. A moulder can switch to stacking for a day. This keeps skills sharp and reduces injuries.

Plan rest breaks. During peak season, work longer hours but give a full day off after every 10 days. If you push too hard, workers get sick or make mistakes. Provide a shaded area with benches where they can rest. Keep a radio playing music if possibleit lifts spirits. Finally, be fair. Do not favour relatives or friends. Give everyone the same chance to earn bonuses. If you see a worker struggling, help them or pair them with a stronger worker. When the peak season ends, thank everyone and give a small end-of-season gift, like a bag of rice or 10,000 shillings. They will come back to work for you next season. Motivated workers produce more and better bricks, and your business grows.

<h2>Practical Reference: Managing Workers and Safety on Site</h2>

<p>In brick and paver making, your product is only as good as the team that moulds, dries, fires and stacks it. For most small and medium brick enterprises in Africa, labour is the single largest operating cost after fuel or biomass, and it is also the biggest source of quality variation, downtime and legal risk. This practical reference gives you a business-first framework for hiring, paying, protecting and organising workers so that your yard runs predictably through peak demand and remains compliant with occupational safety expectations.</p>

<p>Use this module alongside the other five lessons. Here you will find the numbers, ratios and checklists you need to make fast decisions on site: how many moulders per kiln cycle, what a fair daily wage looks like relative to output, which safety gear is non-negotiable, and how to keep a team productive when orders surge before the rainy season.</p>

<h3>Hiring and Training Local Youth for Moulding and Firing</h3>

<p>Youth labour is abundant in most peri-urban and rural African settings, but untrained labour destroys margins through broken moulds, cracked green bricks and inconsistent firing. Treat hiring as an investment, not a cost. A practical rule of thumb from enterprise surveys in East and West Africa is that a new moulder reaches 70% of target output in week one, 90% in week three, and full productivity by week six if paired with an experienced mentor.</p>

<p>Recruit in cohorts of 46 so that training costs are shared and peer accountability develops. Use a simple practical test: ask candidates to mould 20 bricks and assess consistency, speed and care with equipment. Pay a training stipend for the first two weeks, then move to piece-rate or daily rate once output is verified. Document attendance and output daily this becomes your payroll and performance record.</p>

<h3>Setting Fair Wages and Working Hours</h3>

<p>Fair does not mean generous; it means predictable, transparent and compliant with national labour law. In most African jurisdictions, the statutory minimum wage, maximum ordinary hours (typically 4548 per week) and overtime rules apply to brick yards. Piece rates are common and acceptable, but they must not fall below the hourly equivalent of the minimum wage for an average worker.</p>

Always issue a payslip or written record showing days worked, units produced and deductions. This protects you in a labour dispute and makes it easier to access formal finance or supply contracts that require proof of fair labour practices.</p>

<h3>Providing Basic Safety Gear: Gloves, Boots, and Masks</h3>

<p>Personal protective equipment (PPE) is cheap relative to the cost of an injury. Budget USD 2545 per worker per year for basic PPE: leather or rubber-coated gloves, steel-toe or reinforced boots, N95 or FFP2 dust masks, and eye protection for kiln work. Replace gloves every 48 weeks and masks weekly in dusty conditions.</p>

<h3>Preventing Injuries from Heavy Lifting and Hot Kilns</h3>

<p>Two injury categories dominate brick yards: musculoskeletal damage from repetitive lifting and burns from kilns. Both are preventable with simple engineering and work-rule controls.</p>

<p><strong>Lifting:</strong> Set a maximum single-person lift of 25 kg for adults; anything heavier requires a two-person team or a wheelbarrow, trolley or simple pulley. Train workers to lift with legs, keep loads close to the body and avoid twisting. Rotate tasks every two hours so no worker does continuous lifting for a full shift. Provide back-support belts for stacking crews.</p>

<p><strong>Kilns:</strong> Mark a 2-metre exclusion zone around firing kilns with painted lines or rope. Only trained firemen enter. Provide long-handled tools for stoking and unloading. Enforce a cooling protocol: no unloading until the kiln has been vented and surface temperature is below 50C. Keep a first-aid kit with burn gel, clean water and sterile dressings within 30 seconds of the kiln.</p>

<h3>Organising Work Teams for Efficient Production</h3>

<p>Production efficiency comes from clear team structure, not from pushing individuals harder. A proven model for a small yard producing 8,00015,000 bricks per week is:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Moulding team:</strong> 610 workers, organised in pairs (one fills, one strikes and carries).</li>
<li><strong>Drying and turning crew:</strong> 23 workers responsible for stacking green bricks and rotating them.</li>
<li><strong>Kiln team:</strong> 34 trained firemen and loaders working in shifts.</li>
<li><strong>Stacking and dispatch:</strong> 24 workers with trolleys, responsible for sorting and loading.</li>
<li><strong>Team leader:</strong> one experienced worker per 10 staff, responsible for daily output records, safety checks and first-line problem solving.</li>
</ul>

<p>Pay team leaders a 1015% premium and give them authority to stop work if a safety rule is breached. This single change reduces accidents and quality rejects more than any other management action.</p>

<h3>Keeping Workers Motivated During Peak Seasons</h3>

<p>Peak season typically the dry months before the rains can double your order book overnight. Motivation during peak is about fairness and stamina, not slogans.</p>

<ul>
<li>Pay a peak-season bonus of 1020% on units above the normal weekly target.</li>
<li>Provide one hot meal and clean drinking water on site during peak weeks.</li>
<li>Cap overtime at 12 hours per week and enforce one rest day in seven.</li>
<li>Recognise top performers publicly with a small cash award or airtime.</li>
<li>Communicate the order pipeline weekly so workers see why the pace is high and when it will ease.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Business takeaway:</strong> Safe, fairly paid and well-organised workers produce more consistent bricks, waste less material and stay with you through peak season. The cost of PPE, training and fair wages is typically less than 8% of total production cost far less than the cost of one serious injury, one kiln accident or one labour dispute.</p>
