1. Reading Bar Bending Schedules and Cutting Lists

Welcome to Module 2, Lesson 1. In this lesson we move from general site awareness into the paperwork that controls every kilogram of steel you touch. On a well run African construction site, the bar bending schedule, often called the BBS, is the single most important document for a steel fixer. It tells you the bar mark, the diameter, the shape, the number of bars, the cutting length and the bending dimensions. If you cannot read it, you are guessing, and guessing wastes money. A typical BBS is prepared by the structural engineer or detailer and issued to the main contractor. Your foreman will give you a copy. Your job is to translate that copy into cut and bent bars that match the drawings exactly. Start by learning the columns. Bar mark is a unique label such as B1, B2 or C12. Member identifies where the bar goes, for example footing F1, column C3, beam B2 or slab S1. Type and size gives the diameter in millimetres, so Y12 means high yield 12 mm bar. Number of members tells you how many identical elements exist. Number per member tells you how many bars go into each element. Total number is the multiplication of those two. Shape code is a standard reference, often from BS 8666 or a local adaptation, that shows the bent shape. A, B, C, D and E dimensions are the lengths of each leg or segment. Cutting length is the total length of straight bar needed before bending. Bending dimensions are the lengths after bending. The difference between cutting length and the sum of bending dimensions is the bend deduction, which accounts for the elongation of steel at each bend. In many African markets, steel is sold by weight or by length. A 12 m bar is standard, but you will also see 6 m and 9 m lengths. Knowing the cutting length helps you plan how many bars you can get from each stock length. For example, if a cutting length is 3.8 m and you buy 12 m bars, you can get three pieces per bar with 0.6 m leftover. That leftover is not waste if you plan it into another bar mark. This is where profit is made or lost. A steel fixing business that plans its cuts carefully can reduce waste from 15 percent to under 5 percent. On a recent project in Kenya, a contractor saved over 200,000 shillings on a single floor by nesting cuts properly. The BBS also tells you the lap lengths. Laps are where two bars overlap to transfer force. Lap length depends on diameter, concrete grade and whether the bar is in tension or compression. A common rule of thumb is 40 times the diameter for tension laps in grade 30 concrete, but always follow the engineer's specification. If the BBS says 50D, that means 50 times the diameter. For a 16 mm bar, that is 800 mm. Getting laps wrong is one of the most common and most expensive mistakes. Too short and the structure is unsafe. Too long and you waste steel and money. When you receive a BBS, check it against the structural drawings. Look for obvious errors such as a bar mark that appears in the schedule but not on the drawing, or a diameter that changes between the two. Raise queries immediately through your foreman. Do not assume the schedule is correct. Detailers are human and mistakes happen. A good steel fixer catches errors before they become costly rework. Now let us talk about the cutting list. The cutting list is your practical working document. It groups bars by diameter and shape so you can plan your cutting and bending. It should include the bar mark, diameter, shape code, cutting length, number required and the total weight. Weight is important because it tells you how much steel to order and how much to charge if you are a subcontractor. The unit weight of steel is approximately 0.006165 times the diameter squared, in kilograms per metre. So a 12 mm bar weighs 0.888 kg per metre, a 16 mm bar weighs 1.578 kg per metre, and a 20 mm bar weighs 2.466 kg per metre. Memorise these three and you can estimate most jobs quickly. When you prepare your own cutting list from the BBS, sort by diameter first. This reduces machine setup time and avoids mixing bars. Then sort by shape code. Then optimise your cuts against available stock lengths. Use a simple spreadsheet or even a notebook. Many successful fixers in Nigeria, Ghana and South Africa use handwritten cutting lists that are checked by a second person. That second check catches arithmetic errors that could cost thousands. Finally, keep your BBS and cutting list dry and protected. On site, paper gets wet, dirty and lost. Use a clear plastic sleeve or a clipboard with a cover. If you have a smartphone, take a photo of the approved BBS and keep it in a folder. But remember that only the latest revision is valid. Check the revision number and date. If a new revision is issued, stop work, collect the old copies and replace them. Working from an outdated schedule is a recipe for disaster. In summary, the BBS and cutting list are your maps. Read them carefully, check them against drawings, plan your cuts, calculate weights and protect your documents. Do this and you will save steel, save time and make more profit.

2. Cutting and Bending Rebar Accurately

Welcome to Lesson 2. Cutting and bending are the core hands-on skills of steel fixing. If you cut too short, the bar is scrap. If you cut too long, you waste steel and may not fit the formwork. If you bend in the wrong place or to the wrong angle, the bar will not sit correctly and may be rejected by the engineer. Accuracy is not optional. It is the difference between a professional and an amateur. Let us start with cutting. The most common tools on African sites are manual bolt cutters, angle grinders, hacksaws and electric bar cutting machines. For small diameters up to 12 mm, bolt cutters are fast and cheap. For 16 mm and above, an angle grinder with a cutting disc is common, but it is slow and produces sparks. A dedicated bar cutter is the best option for volume work. It gives clean, square cuts and can handle 32 mm bars. Whatever tool you use, measure twice and cut once. Use a tape measure and a piece of soapstone or a marker to mark the cut point. For repeated cuts, set up a stop block or a measuring jig. A simple piece of timber clamped to the bench at the correct distance will speed up your work and improve consistency. When cutting, support the bar on both sides so it does not fall and bend. If you are cutting many bars of the same length, stack them and cut together where possible, but only if your machine can handle the bundle safely. Never cut a bar that is under tension or being used as a temporary support. Always check that the bar is free and clear. Now bending. Bending can be done with a manual bending machine, a hydraulic bender or a bar bending machine. For site work, a manual bender with a long handle is common. It uses a former, also called a mandrel, to set the radius. The minimum bend diameter depends on the bar diameter and the grade. For high yield bars, a common rule is 4 times the diameter for stirrups and 7 times the diameter for main bars. So a 10 mm stirrup needs a mandrel of at least 40 mm. A 16 mm main bar needs at least 112 mm. Always check the specification. Bending cold is standard. Do not heat the bar to bend it unless the engineer approves, because heating changes the steel properties and can make it brittle. When bending, place the bar in the machine with the correct length on the correct side of the former. Use the measuring scale on the machine or mark the bar with soapstone. Pull the handle smoothly and steadily. Do not jerk. For a 90 degree bend, pull until the bar touches the stop. For a 135 degree bend, which is common for stirrup hooks, use the correct stop setting. After bending, check the angle with a set square or a template. Check the dimensions with a tape. If the bar is out of tolerance, it may be rejected. Typical tolerance is plus or minus 25 mm for cutting length and plus or minus 5 degrees for bends. For stirrups, the shape is critical. A rectangular stirrup must have four sides, a 135 degree hook at each end, and the hooks must be on alternate corners. The hook length is usually 10 times the diameter, but check the drawing. A 10 mm stirrup needs a 100 mm hook. When you bend stirrups, make a jig on a plywood board with nails or pegs at the correct spacing. This allows you to produce identical stirrups quickly. On a large project, you may need hundreds of stirrups. A jig can double your output and improve quality. For links and spacers, the same principle applies. Make a template, check the first one, then produce in batches. Always check every tenth bar against the template. If one is wrong, check the whole batch. In Africa, many sites do not have reliable power. Manual benders and cutters are essential. Keep them maintained. Oil the moving parts. Replace worn pins and formers. A worn former will produce inconsistent bends. Store your tools in a dry place. Rust on a bender will make it stiff and inaccurate. When working, wear gloves and eye protection. Cutting and bending steel produces sharp edges and flying particles. A simple pair of safety glasses and leather gloves can prevent a life changing injury. Also be aware of the weight of bars. A 12 m length of 32 mm bar weighs over 75 kg. Do not try to lift it alone. Use two people or a mechanical aid. Plan your work area. Keep the cutting and bending area clear of obstructions. Stack cut bars by bar mark and diameter. Use timber dunnage to keep them off the ground. This prevents rust and makes counting easy. Label each stack with a tag showing the bar mark, diameter, number and member. This saves time during fixing. Finally, record your work. Keep a simple log of how many bars you cut and bent each day. This helps you track productivity and estimate future jobs. A good steel fixer can cut and bend 500 kg to 1,000 kg per day depending on the complexity. With practice and good organisation, you can achieve the higher end. Remember, accuracy saves money. A bar that is cut wrong is a bar that must be replaced. A bar that is bent wrong is a bar that must be straightened or scrapped. Do it right the first time.

3. Lapping, Anchorage and Cover in African Conditions

Welcome to Lesson 3. Lapping, anchorage and cover are the three technical details that most often cause problems on site. Get them right and the structure is strong and durable. Get them wrong and you risk cracks, corrosion, and in the worst case, collapse. In this lesson we focus on practical rules and African site realities. Let us start with laps. A lap is where two bars overlap to transfer load. The lap length depends on the bar diameter, the concrete strength, the bar surface and the position of the bar. As a general guide, tension laps are 40 to 50 times the diameter. Compression laps are 30 to 40 times the diameter. For a 12 mm bar in tension, a 50D lap is 600 mm. For a 20 mm bar, it is 1,000 mm. Always check the engineer's drawing. Laps must be staggered. Do not put all laps at the same section. Stagger them by at least 1.3 times the lap length. This distributes the stress and prevents a weak plane. In columns, stagger laps by at least 500 mm or as specified. In beams, stagger laps in the top and bottom bars. In slabs, stagger laps in both directions. When you lap bars, tie them securely with binding wire. Use at least three ties per lap for bars up to 16 mm and four ties for larger bars. The ties should be tight and the bars should be in contact along the lap length. Do not allow a gap. A gap means the concrete cannot transfer force properly. Also, do not lap bars in high stress areas such as the middle of a beam span or the top of a column. Laps should be in low stress zones, typically near supports for bottom bars and near midspan for top bars. Now anchorage. Anchorage is how a bar is fixed into concrete at its end. It can be a straight length, a hook, a bend or a mechanical device. The anchorage length is similar to lap length, often 40D to 50D. For a hook, the standard is a 90 degree bend with a 12D extension, or a 135 degree bend with a 10D extension for stirrups. The hook must be bent around a mandrel of the correct diameter. Do not use a sharp bend that cracks the bar. In footings, column bars must be anchored into the footing with a 90 degree bend and a straight leg of at least 150 mm or as specified. In beams, top bars must be anchored into columns with a 90 degree bend and a leg of at least 12D. These details are critical for earthquake resistance and overall stability. Many African cities are in seismic zones, so anchorage is not optional. Cover is the distance from the outer surface of the concrete to the nearest steel bar. Cover protects the steel from corrosion and fire. It also ensures that the steel and concrete bond properly. Cover depends on the exposure condition. For internal columns and beams, cover is typically 25 mm to 40 mm. For external elements exposed to rain, cover is 40 mm to 50 mm. For foundations in contact with soil, cover is 50 mm to 75 mm. For marine or coastal areas, cover can be 75 mm or more. In many parts of Africa, coastal humidity and salt spray accelerate corrosion. If cover is too small, rust will appear within a few years. If cover is too large, the effective depth of the section is reduced and the structure may be weaker. Use cover blocks, also called spacers, to maintain cover. Cover blocks can be made of concrete, plastic or mortar. Concrete cover blocks are best because they have the same durability as the concrete. Make them with a mix of 1 part cement to 2 parts sand, and cure them properly. Do not use wooden blocks or stones because they rot or crush. Place cover blocks at intervals of 500 mm to 800 mm along bars. For slabs, place them under the bottom mat and between the top and bottom mats. For beams, place them on the sides and bottom. For columns, place them on all four sides. In African conditions, there are additional challenges. High temperatures can cause concrete to set quickly, so you must place and tie bars fast. Dust and sand can contaminate the bars and reduce bond. Keep bars clean. If bars are stored on the ground, they will rust. Rust reduces the effective diameter and weakens the bar. Store bars on timber dunnage at least 150 mm above the ground. Cover them with tarpaulin if possible. In areas with termites, do not use timber that will be eaten. Use steel or concrete supports. In areas with high rainfall, ensure that water does not pond around the foundation. Proper drainage protects the steel. Another issue is theft. Steel is valuable and can be stolen from site. Store it in a secure compound. Mark your bars with paint or a stamp. Keep records. If you are a subcontractor, you are responsible for the steel until it is cast into the concrete. Insurance is expensive, so prevention is better. Finally, always inspect before casting. Check that all bars are in place, laps are correct, cover blocks are installed, and the steel is clean. Take photos. If the engineer asks for a change, get it in writing. Do not rely on verbal instructions. A written record protects you and your business. In summary, laps, anchorage and cover are the details that make the difference between a structure that lasts 50 years and one that fails in 5. Follow the drawings, use the correct lengths, maintain cover, and keep your steel clean and dry. Your reputation depends on it.

4. Fixing Steel in Footings, Columns, Beams and Slabs

Welcome to Lesson 4. This lesson covers the practical sequence of fixing steel in the four main structural elements: footings, columns, beams and slabs. We will go step by step, with African site conditions in mind. Start with footings. Footings are the base of the structure. They can be pad footings, strip footings or raft foundations. For a pad footing, the bottom mat is usually a grid of bars in two directions. The bars are placed on cover blocks, typically 50 mm to 75 mm from the soil. The spacing is given on the drawing, for example Y16 at 150 mm centres both ways. Mark the spacing on the formwork or on a timber batten. Place the bars in one direction first, then the other. Tie every intersection with binding wire. For a strip footing, the main bars run longitudinally and the distribution bars run across. The main bars are usually at the bottom. For a raft, you may have top and bottom mats with chairs to maintain the spacing. Chairs are supports made of bent bars or plastic. They must be strong enough to hold the top mat during concreting. In footings, the column starter bars must be fixed. These are the bars that will later connect to the column. They must be positioned accurately. Use a template or a setting out board to hold them in the correct location. The starter bars must have the correct lap length above the footing. If they are wrong, the column will be in the wrong place or the lap will be insufficient. Next, columns. Column steel is usually a cage of vertical bars and horizontal links or stirrups. The vertical bars are lapped at the base with the starter bars. The links are spaced according to the drawing, often closer at the top and bottom of the column. The links must have 135 degree hooks that engage the vertical bars. The hooks should alternate corners. Tie the links to the vertical bars at every intersection or at least every other intersection. Use a plumb bob or a level to keep the column vertical. Use spacers to maintain cover. For a rectangular column, you need at least four spacers per metre. For a circular column, use a spiral or circular links. In African cities, columns are often cast in one lift of 3 to 4 metres. Make sure the cage is stable. Brace it with timber or steel props until the formwork is fixed. If the column is tall, provide a working platform. Safety first. Now beams. Beam steel consists of top bars, bottom bars and stirrups. The bottom bars are usually the main tension bars. The top bars may be continuous or curtailed. The stirrups are spaced closer near the supports. The beam cage is usually assembled on the ground or on the formwork. If assembled on the ground, lift it into the formwork with care. Use two people or a small crane. Do not drag it. Once in place, check the cover. Use cover blocks under the bottom bars and on the sides. Tie the beam cage to the column bars. The beam bars must be anchored into the columns with the correct bend and length. For a simple beam, the bottom bars go straight into the column and bend up 90 degrees with a 12D leg. The top bars go straight and bend down 90 degrees with a 12D leg. Check the drawing. For continuous beams, the top bars may run over the supports. Make sure the laps are staggered and in the correct zone. Finally, slabs. Slab steel is usually a bottom mat and a top mat. The bottom mat is placed first on cover blocks. The bars are tied at every intersection. The top mat is placed on chairs. The chairs must be strong enough to support the weight of the top mat and the workers during concreting. In a two way slab, the bars run in both directions. In a one way slab, the main bars run in the short direction and the distribution bars in the long direction. The top mat usually has bars over the supports to resist negative moments. These bars must be placed correctly. A common mistake is to place the top bars too low. They must be at the top of the slab with the correct cover. Use a bar chair or a spacer to hold them up. In African conditions, slabs are often cast in hot weather. The steel must be clean and free of dust. If the slab is large, you may need to cast in sections. Plan the construction joints. The steel must continue across the joint with the correct lap or dowel. Do not cut bars at a construction joint unless the engineer approves. Throughout all these elements, communication is key. Work with the carpenter to ensure the formwork is correct before you fix steel. Work with the electrician and plumber to avoid clashes. If a pipe or conduit passes through a beam, the steel must be detailed to accommodate it. Do not cut bars without approval. If you must cut a bar, get a written instruction and a repair detail. After fixing, inspect your work. Check the number of bars, diameter, spacing, laps, anchorage, cover and ties. Use a checklist. Take photos. Invite the engineer for inspection. If there are defects, fix them before casting. Once concrete is poured, it is too late. Remember, your work is the skeleton of the building. If the skeleton is weak, the building is weak. If the skeleton is strong, the building will last. Be proud of your work. Do it right.

5. Quality Control, Safety and Productivity on Site

Welcome to Lesson 5, the final lesson in Module 2. Here we bring together quality control, safety and productivity. These three are not separate. A safe site is a productive site. A productive site is a quality site. Let us start with quality control. Quality control means checking your work against the specification before, during and after fixing. Before you start, check that the drawings and bar bending schedule are the latest revision. Check that the steel delivered matches the specification. Look at the bar diameter, the surface condition and the mill certificate. A mill certificate shows the chemical and mechanical properties of the steel. Keep a copy. If the steel is rusty, it may still be acceptable if the rust is surface rust and the bar is not pitted. Wire brush it before fixing. If the rust is heavy, reject it. During fixing, check spacing, laps, anchorage and cover. Use a tape measure. Use a set square. Use a level. Do not rely on your eye. After fixing, do a final inspection. Count the bars. Check the ties. Check the cover blocks. Make sure the steel is clean and free of oil, paint or mud. If you find a problem, fix it immediately. Do not leave it for someone else. A good quality control system includes a checklist and a sign off. The foreman signs, the engineer signs, and you keep a copy. This protects you if there is a dispute later. Now safety. Steel fixing is a physically demanding job with many hazards. The main hazards are cuts, punctures, falls, back injuries and eye injuries. Wear the right personal protective equipment. That means gloves, safety boots, a hard hat, safety glasses and a high visibility vest. When cutting or grinding, wear a face shield. When working at height, wear a harness and clip on. When lifting bars, use your legs, not your back. Get help for heavy bars. Keep your work area tidy. Do not leave bars sticking up where someone can trip or fall onto them. Cap the ends of vertical bars with plastic caps or a piece of timber. This simple measure prevents impalement injuries. In Africa, heat is a major hazard. Drink water frequently. Take breaks in the shade. Start early in the morning and avoid the hottest part of the day if possible. Use sunscreen and wear a hat. Watch out for dehydration. If you feel dizzy, stop work and rest. Do not operate machines if you are tired. A tired worker is a dangerous worker. Also be aware of electrical hazards. Use residual current devices on all power tools. Check cables for damage. Do not use tools with wet hands. Keep a first aid kit on site. Know where it is and how to use it. Report all injuries, even small cuts. An infected cut can put you out of work for weeks. Now productivity. Productivity is about doing more with less. It is not about rushing and making mistakes. It is about planning, preparation and practice. Plan your day. Know what you will fix and what tools and materials you need. Prepare your cutting and bending area. Keep your tools sharp and maintained. Use jigs and templates for repetitive work. Work in a team. A team of three or four can fix more steel than the same number of individuals working alone. Assign roles. One person cuts, one bends, one fixes, one ties. Rotate roles to reduce fatigue. Track your progress. At the end of each day, record how many kilograms or how many bars you fixed. Compare with your target. If you are behind, find out why. Maybe the drawings are unclear. Maybe the steel is not delivered on time. Maybe the formwork is not ready. Solve the problem. Do not just work harder. Work smarter. As a steel fixing business, your profit depends on productivity. If you bid a job at 1,000 kg per day and you only achieve 500 kg per day, you will lose money. So train your team. Practice on small jobs. Invest in good tools. A good bar cutter and bender will pay for themselves in a few months. Keep your workers happy. Pay them fairly and on time. Provide safe working conditions. A happy worker is a productive worker. Finally, think about the future. Africa is building fast. There is a huge demand for skilled steel fixers. If you are good, you will always have work. If you are excellent, you can start your own company. Learn to read drawings. Learn to estimate. Learn to manage a team. Learn to communicate with engineers and clients. Every lesson in this module is a step on that path. Quality, safety and productivity are the three pillars of a successful steel fixing career. Master them and you will build not just structures, but a better future for yourself and your community. Thank you for completing Module 2. In Module 3 we will look at advanced topics such as post tensioning, prefabrication and site management. Until then, stay safe, stay accurate and keep building.

6. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Steel Fixing (Rebar) Module 2 Field Companion

This practical reference supports Module 2 of the RISE AFRICA Steel Fixing (Rebar) course. It is written for site supervisors, foremen, and small-to-medium contractors working on residential, commercial, and light industrial projects across Africa. The goal is simple: give you numbers, tables, and checklists you can use on site today to cut waste, avoid rework, and protect your margin. Steel can account for 2540% of the structural cost of a reinforced concrete frame, so small errors in cutting, bending, lapping, and fixing translate directly into lost profit. Use this lesson alongside the five core lessons in Module 2.

1. Why Practical Reference Matters on African Sites

On many African construction sites, bar bending schedules (BBS) are prepared in the office but interpreted differently in the yard. Common problems include:

  • Bars cut too short, leading to expensive laps or rejected pours.
  • Bars cut too long, wasting steel that is already the single largest material cost in the frame.
  • Incorrect bend diameters causing cracking at the bend or non-compliance with design.
  • Insufficient cover in aggressive ground or coastal environments, leading to corrosion within 510 years.
  • Poor lap detailing in columns and beams, reducing structural capacity.

Each of these has a direct financial cost. A single 12 m length of 16 mm rebar can cost the equivalent of USD 815 depending on country and market conditions. On a 200 m slab, a 5% cutting error can waste hundreds of dollars. Multiply that across a project and the loss is significant.

4. Lapping, Anchorage, and Cover in African Conditions

African conditions vary widely: coastal humidity, saline groundwater, high temperatures, and aggressive soils. These conditions increase the risk of corrosion and reduce the effective life of reinforcement if cover and lap detailing are poor. Key practical points:

  • Lap length: For tension bars, a common rule of thumb is 4050 times the bar diameter (40d50d) for grade 500 steel in normal conditions. In aggressive conditions, increase to 50d60d or follow the engineer's specification.
  • Anchorage: Ensure bars are anchored beyond the point of maximum stress. In footings, use a 90 bend with a minimum anchorage length of 12d15d unless otherwise specified.
  • Cover: Minimum cover for footings in contact with ground is typically 5075 mm. For columns and beams exposed to weather, 4050 mm is common. In coastal or saline environments, increase cover by 1020 mm and consider corrosion-resistant coatings or stainless steel where specified.
  • Spacers: Use concrete or plastic spacers at 1 m centres maximum to maintain cover. Do not use wooden blocks or scrap steel they rot, corrode, or create weak points.
  • Lap staggering: Stagger laps in columns and beams so that no more than 50% of bars are lapped at the same section. This reduces the risk of a weak plane.
SAFETY WARNING CHEMICAL AND PHYSICAL HAZARDS

Rebar cutting and bending involves physical hazards (cuts, crush injuries, eye injuries from flying debris) and chemical hazards (cutting fluids, anti-rust coatings, and dust). Always wear:

  • Cut-resistant gloves and safety glasses.
  • Hearing protection when using angle grinders or bar cutters.
  • Steel-toed boots and high-visibility clothing.
  • Respiratory protection when cutting coated or galvanised bars.

Store cutting fluids and coatings away from food and water sources. Wash hands before eating. If a chemical contacts the eye, flush with clean water for 15 minutes and seek medical attention. For more information, see WHO guidance on occupational health and safety.

5. Quick Reference Checklist for Site Supervisors

Use this checklist before, during, and after fixing steel on any element. It is designed to be printed and pinned in the site office or yard.

  1. Check the BBS against the latest drawing revision. Confirm bar marks, diameters, lengths, and quantities before cutting.
  2. Verify bar diameter and grade on delivery. Use a calliper or gauge. Check mill certificates or supplier tags.
  3. Confirm cut lengths and bend schedules with the foreman. Mark bars clearly with tags or paint.
  4. Inspect bend diameters and angles. Ensure mandrel sizes match the specification (typically 4d6d).
  5. Check lap lengths and staggering. Measure at least 10% of laps on each element.
  6. Verify cover with spacers. Check at 1 m intervals and at corners. Photograph before pouring.
  7. Ensure bars are tied securely. Use correct tie wire (typically 1.6 mm annealed) and tie at every intersection or as specified.
  8. Record and report non-conformances. Photograph, log, and correct before concrete is ordered.

6. Business-First Notes: Cost and Productivity

Steel fixing is a labour-intensive activity. Productivity varies widely across African sites depending on training, tools, and supervision. Typical output for a trained gang using a bar cutter and bender is:

  • Cutting and bending: 0.51.0 tonnes per person per day.
  • Fixing in footings: 0.30.6 tonnes per person per day.
  • Fixing in columns and beams: 0.20.5 tonnes per person per day.
  • Fixing in slabs: 0.40.8 tonnes per person per day.

Improving productivity by 20% through better planning, correct tools, and on-site training can reduce labour cost per tonne by 1525%. On a project using 50 tonnes of rebar, that can mean thousands of dollars in savings. Invest in a good bar cutter and bender, train your gang, and use the checklist above to reduce rework.

Waste reduction is equally important. A 3% reduction in steel waste on a 100-tonne project saves 3 tonnes of steel a direct cost saving. Use the BBS, cut in batches, and reuse offcuts for stirrups and spacers where allowed.
