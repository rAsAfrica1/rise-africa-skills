1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Permanent mould casting is a method where molten metal is poured into a reusable metal mould, usually made of cast iron or steel. Unlike sand casting, where the mould is destroyed after each pour, a permanent mould can be used hundreds or even thousands of times. This makes it ideal for producing many identical parts quickly and cheaply. In Africa, where small foundries often supply automotive, agricultural, and construction sectors, permanent mould casting can boost production rates and reduce waste. For example, a workshop in Kenya making aluminium cooking pots can switch from sand to permanent moulds and increase output from 20 pots per day to over 100, while using less sand and labour.

The process is best for metals with lower melting points, such as aluminium, zinc, and magnesium. It is not suitable for steel or cast iron because the high temperatures would quickly damage the mould. The mould is typically made in two halves that open and close, with a pouring basin and runners cut into the joint. Gravity or low pressure fills the cavity. Because the mould is metal, it cools the casting faster than sand, giving a finer grain structure and better surface finish. This means less machining is needed afterwards.

However, permanent mould casting requires more preparation and precision than sand casting. The mould must be preheated to around 200â300Â°C before pouring to prevent the metal from freezing too quickly. A refractory coating, such as graphite or zircon wash, is sprayed on the mould surface to protect it and help release the part. Without this coating, the casting may stick or the mould may crack. Also, the mould design must allow for easy removal of the part, often using ejector pins.

For a small business in Africa, investing in a permanent mould can pay off if you have a steady demand for a specific part. The initial cost of making the metal mould is higher than a sand pattern, but the savings in labour, sand, and time add up quickly. You also get more consistent parts, which is important when supplying formal markets. This module will teach you how to set up, operate, and maintain a permanent mould casting operation safely and efficiently.

2. Tools and Materials

To start permanent mould casting, you need the right tools and materials. The core item is the permanent mould itself, usually made from cast iron or H13 tool steel. For aluminium casting, a low-carbon steel mould works well and is cheaper. You can make the mould by machining two blocks of steel to fit together, with the cavity cut using a milling machine or, for simple shapes, a lathe. In many African workshops, local machinists can produce these moulds if you provide accurate drawings. Expect to pay between $200 and $800 per mould, depending on complexity.

You will also need a furnace capable of melting your chosen metal. For aluminium, a crucible furnace fired by diesel, waste oil, or gas works well. A 50 kg capacity furnace can melt enough metal for several small castings per hour. You need crucibles made of clay-graphite or silicon carbide. Tongs, skimmers, and a pouring ladle are essential. The ladle should be coated with refractory wash to prevent iron contamination.

For mould preparation, you need a preheating oven or a gas torch to heat the mould to 200â300Â°C. A temperature probe or infrared thermometer helps you monitor this. You also need a spray gun for applying the mould coating. Common coatings are graphite-based (for aluminium) or zircon-based (for higher temperatures). These are mixed with water and sprayed on, then dried. A good coating extends mould life and improves surface finish.

Safety gear is critical: a face shield, leather apron, heat-resistant gloves, and safety boots. Also have a fire extinguisher and a bucket of dry sand nearby. For finishing, you need a hacksaw, grinder, and files to remove gates and runners. A set of callipers helps check dimensions. Finally, keep a logbook to record mould temperatures, pouring temperatures, and any defects. This data helps you improve. With these tools, a small team of two or three people can run a permanent mould casting cell efficiently.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps to produce a casting using a permanent mould. First, preheat the mould to 250Â°C. Use a gas torch or put it in an oven. A cold mould will cause the metal to chill too fast, leading to misruns. Check the temperature with an infrared thermometer. While it heats, prepare the metal. For aluminium, melt scrap or ingots in a crucible furnace. Bring the temperature to 700â750Â°C. Use a skimmer to remove dross (impurities) from the surface. Do not overheat, as this absorbs hydrogen and causes porosity.

Next, apply the mould coating. Mix graphite powder with water to a thin paint consistency. Spray a light, even coat on the mould cavity and the pouring basin. Let it dry for a few minutes. The coating should be about 0.2â0.5 mm thick. Too thick and it flakes; too thin and the metal may stick. Then close the mould halves and clamp them tightly. Make sure the alignment pins are engaged so the two halves match perfectly. Any gap will cause flash (thin fins of metal).

Now pour the molten metal. Use a ladle that has been preheated and coated. Pour steadily into the pouring basin, keeping a full stream to avoid air bubbles. Fill the mould in 3â5 seconds for small parts. Do not overfill; let the metal rise to the top of the sprue. After pouring, wait for the metal to solidify. For a small aluminium part, this takes 30â60 seconds. You will see the glow fade. Do not open the mould too early or the part may warp.

Once solid, open the mould and remove the casting using tongs or ejector pins. Be carefulâit is still hot (around 400Â°C). Quench it in water if needed. Then cut off the gates and runners with a hacksaw or abrasive wheel. Grind the stubs flush. Inspect the part for defects like blowholes, cold shuts, or cracks. If defects are found, adjust your temperatures or coating. Clean the mould, re-spray if necessary, and it is ready for the next pour. With practice, you can complete a cycle in under 5 minutes.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Even experienced casters make mistakes. Here are common problems in permanent mould casting and how to fix them. First, cold shuts: these are seams where two streams of metal meet but do not fuse. They happen when the mould is too cold or the metal is too cold. Fix: preheat the mould to 250â300Â°C and increase metal temperature by 20â30Â°C. Also, pour faster to keep the metal fluid.

Second, porosity: small holes or gas pockets inside the casting. This is often caused by hydrogen gas from damp tools, wet coating, or overheated metal. Fix: preheat all tools, ensure the coating is fully dry, and avoid overheating aluminium above 750Â°C. Degas the melt with a degassing tablet if available.

Third, misruns: when the metal does not fill the whole cavity. This can be due to low pouring temperature, slow pouring, or a blocked runner. Fix: check that the runner and gates are clear, increase pouring temperature, and pour in one continuous motion. Also, make sure the mould is not too cold.

Fourth, sticking: the casting sticks to the mould. This is usually because the coating is too thin or worn out. Fix: apply a thicker coating (0.3â0.5 mm) and ensure it covers all surfaces. Re-coat every 5â10 pours.

Fifth, flash: thin fins of metal squeezed out between mould halves. This means the mould halves are not clamped tightly or the mating surfaces are damaged. Fix: clean the mating surfaces, replace worn alignment pins, and use stronger clamps.

Sixth, cracks: hot tears or cold cracks. Hot tears happen when the casting is restrained during cooling. Fix: ensure the mould design allows for shrinkage, and do not open the mould too early. Cold cracks can occur if you quench too quickly. Let the casting cool to below 200Â°C before quenching.

Finally, mould wear: permanent moulds degrade over time. Signs include rough surface, cracks, or warping. Fix: maintain the mould by re-machining the cavity if needed, and always use a good coating. Keep records of how many pours each mould has done; after 500â1000 pours, it may need refurbishment. By learning from these mistakes, you will save time and money.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety is non-negotiable in foundry work. Molten metal at 700Â°C can cause severe burns. Always wear a face shield, leather apron, heat-resistant gloves, and safety boots. Never wear synthetic clothing; it melts and sticks to skin. Keep the work area dryâwater on the floor or in tools can cause steam explosions. Have a fire extinguisher and a bucket of dry sand or a fire blanket within reach. Never use water on a metal fire.

Before starting, inspect your equipment. Check the furnace for leaks, the crucible for cracks, and the mould for damage. Preheat the mould in a well-ventilated area, away from flammable materials. When melting, charge clean, dry metal. Wet scrap can cause a steam explosion that throws molten metal. Use tongs to place metal in the crucible, not your hands. Skim dross gently; do not stir aggressively, as this introduces gas.

During pouring, stand to the side, not directly in front of the mould. Molten metal can splash. Pour steadily and avoid overfilling. After pouring, let the mould cool before opening. Use tongs or a lifting tool to remove the casting. Quench only when the casting is below 200Â°C to avoid steam bursts. Never quench in a closed container.

Best practices for quality and efficiency: Keep a logbook. Record mould temperature, metal temperature, pouring time, and any defects. This helps you identify patterns. Maintain your moulds: clean them after each use, re-coat regularly, and store them in a dry place. Use a temperature controller on your furnace for consistency. Train all workers on safety and standard procedures. In Africa, where foundries may operate in informal settings, good housekeeping is key. Keep the floor clear, tools organized, and waste metal recycled. By following these practices, you reduce accidents and produce better castings. Remember, a safe foundry is a productive foundry.

6. Practical Exercise

Now itâs time to practice. In this exercise, you will cast a simple aluminium partâa 10 cm x 5 cm x 1 cm flat plate with a 2 cm hole in the centreâusing a permanent mould. This mimics common parts like brackets or base plates. You will need: a two-part steel mould with the cavity machined, a crucible furnace, aluminium scrap, a ladle, graphite coating, and safety gear. Work in a team of two: one pours, one assists.

Step 1: Preheat the mould to 250Â°C. Use a gas torch or oven. Check with an infrared thermometer. Step 2: Melt 2 kg of clean aluminium in the furnace. Bring to 720Â°C. Skim off dross. Step 3: Spray the mould cavity with graphite coating. Let dry. Step 4: Close and clamp the mould. Ensure tight fit. Step 5: Pour the molten aluminium into the pouring basin in one steady stream. Fill in about 4 seconds. Step 6: Wait 60 seconds for solidification. Step 7: Open the mould and remove the plate with tongs. Step 8: Let it cool to room temperature, then cut off gates and grind smooth. Step 9: Inspect the plate for defects: check for cold shuts, porosity, or incomplete fill. Measure the dimensions with callipers.

If the plate has defects, discuss with your team what went wrong. Was the mould too cold? Was the pour too slow? Adjust and try again. Aim to produce three good plates in a row. Record your results: mould temperature, metal temperature, pouring time, and observations. After the exercise, clean the mould, re-coat it, and store it properly. This exercise builds muscle memory and teaches you to control variables. In a real business, you would repeat this cycle hundreds of times per day. With practice, you will increase your speed and quality. Share your results with your instructor and classmates to learn from each other.

â  SAFETY WARNING â MOLTEN METAL & MOULD HAZARD

Never pour into a wet, damp, or cold permanent mould. Trapped moisture flashes to steam and can eject molten metal violently (steam explosion). Always pre-heat moulds to the specified temperature, dry all coatings fully, and wear a face shield, aluminised apron, spats, and heat-resistant gloves. Keep a dry sand or Class D extinguisher within reach â never use water on a metal fire. In many African workshops, moulds are re-used across shifts; inspect for cracks before every pour, as a cracked die can burst under thermal stress.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Mould condition: Inspect for cracks, erosion, and worn coating; re-coat if needed.
  2. Pre-heat: Bring mould to specified temperature (see Table 1) â never pour cold.
  3. Metal temperature: Verify with a calibrated pyrometer before every pour.
  4. Coating: Apply and fully dry the release/insulating coating; check thickness.
  5. Gating & vents: Confirm runners, risers, and vents are clear and correctly sized.
  6. Pour technique: Steady, continuous stream; avoid turbulence and interruption.
  7. Ejection timing: Remove part at the correct temperature to avoid hot tearing.
  8. Record keeping: Log cycle number, temperatures, defects, and mould life for costing.
