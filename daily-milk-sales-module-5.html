Lessons

1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Milk is a perfect food for germs as well as for people. If you are careless, bacteria double every 20 minutes in warm milk. That means a clean pail of milk can turn sour in 3 hours. With good hygiene, you can keep milk fresh for 8 to 10 hours without a cooler. For a small dairy farmer in Africa selling to neighbours, hotels, or a milk bar, freshness is your reputation. One bad batch and customers go elsewhere.

Good milking routine also protects the cow. Dirty hands and cups can push bacteria into the teat canal, causing mastitis. A cow with mastitis gives less milk, sometimes with blood or pus, and you may need to buy antibiotics. That costs money and you lose milk sales during withdrawal. A clean routine prevents this.

In Africa, heat is your biggest enemy. In the lowlands, afternoon temperatures often reach 30â35Â°C. Milk left in the sun spoils fast. So hygiene must be combined with quick cooling. If you have no fridge, use a charcoal cooler or a bucket of cold water. But even with cooling, dirty milk will spoil faster than clean milk.

This module teaches you a simple, repeatable routine. You will learn what tools you need, how to use them, common mistakes, and how to stay safe. By the end, you will be able to milk your cows in a way that keeps milk sweet and customers happy. Remember: clean milk sells itself. Dirty milk destroys your business.

2. Tools and Materials

You do not need expensive equipment. But you need the right tools, kept clean. Start with a stainless steel or aluminium milking bucket. Avoid plastic buckets because they scratch and hold bacteria. A 10-litre bucket is enough for one or two cows. If you milk by hand, use a full-hand technique, not fingers only. For hand milking, you need a milking stool, a clean cloth, and a strip cup (a small cup with a black screen) to check for mastitis.

For cleaning, you need three things: clean water, detergent, and a disinfectant. In many African villages, you can use dishwashing liquid and then rinse with a bleach solution (1 tablespoon of bleach per 4 litres of water). Do not mix bleach with detergentâit produces dangerous gas. Instead, wash first, rinse, then disinfect. Use a bottle brush to clean inside the bucket and a teat dip cup for dipping teats after milking.

You also need a cooling method. A charcoal cooler is easy to build: a wooden frame, wet sand, and charcoal. Or use a 20-litre bucket filled with cold water and a smaller bucket inside for milk. If you have electricity, a refrigerator is best. But many farmers use a freezer with bottles of ice. Always keep milk below 4Â°C within 2 hours of milking.

Finally, personal items: clean overalls or apron, a cap or headscarf, and soap for washing hands. Keep a nail brush to clean under your nails. All these tools should be stored in a clean, dry place away from flies and dust. A simple plastic box with a lid works well. Never leave buckets on the ground where animals can lick them.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps every time you milk. First, prepare the cow. Tie her tail to her leg or use a kick bar if she kicks. Wash your hands with soap and water for 20 seconds. Put on clean clothes. Then wash the udder with clean warm water and dry with a clean cloth. Use a separate cloth for each cow to avoid spreading germs. If you have many cows, use paper towels or disposable wipes.

Next, check for mastitis. Squirt the first few streams of milk from each teat into a strip cup. Look for lumps, blood, or watery milk. If you see any, milk that cow last and treat her separately. Do not mix her milk with the rest. Then start milking. Use the full-hand method: grip the teat at the top with your thumb and index finger, then squeeze the other fingers in order. Do not pull. Milk in a steady rhythm, about 60 to 80 squeezes per minute. It should take 5 to 7 minutes per cow. If you use a machine, follow the manufacturer's instructions for attaching and removing cups.

After milking, dip each teat in a teat dip (iodine or chlorhexidine). This seals the teat canal and prevents mastitis. Then strain the milk through a clean cloth or filter into your storage container. Cool the milk immediately: place the container in cold water or a charcoal cooler. Stir gently to cool faster. Never leave milk in the sun. Within 2 hours, milk should be below 4Â°C. If you sell raw milk, keep it cold until delivery. If you pasteurise, heat to 63Â°C for 30 minutes, then cool quickly.

Finally, clean all equipment. Rinse buckets with cold water first to remove milk residue, then wash with hot water and detergent, then disinfect. Store upside down to dry. Repeat this routine twice a day, morning and evening, at the same times. Cows are creatures of habit and will let down milk easier.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Using dirty water to wash the udder. Many farmers use water from a pond or well without treatment. That water carries bacteria. Fix: Boil water or add a little bleach (1 teaspoon per 10 litres) and let it cool. Use a clean cloth. If water is scarce, use a dry wipe with a clean cloth and then dip teats before milking? Noâalways wash first.

Mistake 2: Not checking for mastitis. Skipping the strip cup test means you mix bad milk with good. Fix: Always use a strip cup. It takes 10 seconds per teat. If you find mastitis, milk that cow last, treat her, and discard her milk for the withdrawal period.

Mistake 3: Milking with wet hands or wet teats. Water drips into the bucket and introduces bacteria. Fix: Dry teats with a clean cloth before milking. Keep a dry cloth for drying and a wet one for washing.

Mistake 4: Leaving milk in a warm place. In 30Â°C heat, milk spoils in 2 hours. Fix: Cool immediately. Use a charcoal cooler or a bucket of cold water. If you have no ice, dig a hole in wet sand and place the milk container inside.

Mistake 5: Not cleaning buckets properly. Milk residue left in scratches breeds bacteria. Fix: Rinse with cold water right after milking, then scrub with hot soapy water and a brush, then disinfect. Do not use the same bucket for other purposes.

Mistake 6: Milking cows with dirty udders. Mud and manure fall into the bucket. Fix: Wash and dry the udder before milking. Clip long hair around the udder and tail.

Mistake 7: Using plastic buckets. They scratch and hold bacteria. Fix: Switch to stainless steel or aluminium. If you must use plastic, replace it every 6 months.

Mistake 8: Not washing hands between cows. Germs spread from one cow to another. Fix: Wash hands with soap after each cow, or use a hand sanitiser. Keep a bucket of clean water and soap nearby.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety first: you and your cows. Always tie the cow's tail or use a kick bar. Never stand directly behind a cow. Approach from the side. If a cow is nervous, talk softly and move slowly. Wear closed shoes to protect your feet from hooves and manure. Keep the milking area clean and free of sharp objects.

For your health, raw milk can carry diseases like brucellosis and tuberculosis. If you drink raw milk, you risk getting sick. Always pasteurise milk for home use: heat to 63Â°C for 30 minutes, then cool quickly. If you sell raw milk, tell customers to boil it. Wash your hands after handling cows. Keep your nails short and clean.

Best practice for milk quality: use a strip cup every milking. Dip teats after milking. Cool milk quickly. Keep records: date, time, cow ID, amount, and any signs of mastitis. This helps you track problems. If a cow gets mastitis, treat her and discard her milk for the withdrawal period (usually 3 to 5 days). Do not sell milk with antibiotics.

Clean your milking area daily. Remove manure and wet bedding. Use a fly trap or screen to keep flies away. Flies carry bacteria. If you have many cows, milk healthy cows first, then sick ones. Never milk a cow with a dirty udder. Always use a clean cloth per cow.

Finally, train anyone who helps you. A worker who does not follow the routine can ruin your milk. Show them the steps, watch them, and correct mistakes. Reward good hygiene. Remember: your reputation is built on every bucket of milk you sell. One bad batch can lose a customer forever. So be consistent, be clean, and be safe.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it is time to practice. Do this exercise over three days. You will need: a milking bucket, a strip cup, clean cloths, soap, a teat dip, and a cooling method (charcoal cooler or bucket of cold water). If you do not have a strip cup, use a dark-coloured cup or a piece of black cloth stretched over a cup.

Day 1: Set up your milking area. Wash your hands. Wash and dry the udder of one cow. Do the strip cup test on all four teats. Record what you see: any lumps, blood, or watery milk? Then milk the cow using the full-hand method. Time yourself. Aim for 5 to 7 minutes. After milking, dip each teat. Strain the milk into a clean container. Cool it immediately in your cooling method. Write down the time you finished milking and the time the milk reached cold (below 4Â°C). Clean all equipment.

Day 2: Repeat Day 1, but this time focus on speed and cleanliness. Do not splash water. Keep the bucket covered when not milking. After milking, check the milk for any dirt or hair. If you find any, note what went wrong. Then cool the milk. At the end of the day, smell the milk. Does it smell fresh? If it smells sour, your hygiene or cooling failed. Try again.

Day 3: Do the full routine with two cows. Milk the healthy cow first, then the one with any signs of mastitis (if you have one). Use separate cloths. After milking, clean and disinfect all tools. Store them upside down. Then review your notes. What went well? What needs improvement? Share your findings with a fellow farmer or your trainer.

By the end of this exercise, you should be able to milk hygienically, check for mastitis, cool milk quickly, and clean equipment properly. Keep practicing until it becomes a habit. Your milk will stay fresh longer, and your customers will notice the difference.

Antibiotic residue in milk causes processor rejection, can trigger allergic reactions, and contributes to antimicrobial resistance â a major health threat in Africa. Quick Reference Checklist

  • Before milking: Tie or restrain the cow calmly; wash your hands with soap and running water; assemble only clean, dry equipment.
  • Udder preparation: Wash udder with clean water, dry with a clean cloth, and fore-strip each quarter into a strip cup to check for mastitis.
  • Milking technique: Use full-hand grip (not pinching); milk quickly and gently; keep the pail covered to reduce dust and flies.
  • After milking: Dip each teat in disinfectant; rinse all equipment in cold water first, then hot water with detergent; sanitise and air-dry upside down.
  • Milk handling: Filter immediately, cool to â¤4Â°C within 2 hours, and keep covered until sale or delivery.
  • Record keeping: Note milk volume, cow health, treatments, and withdrawal dates daily â this protects your business and your buyers.
  • Personal hygiene: Wear clean clothes; keep hair covered; never milk when sick with a contagious illness.
  • Customer trust: Deliver on time, keep containers clean, and never dilute milk â your reputation is your best marketing.
