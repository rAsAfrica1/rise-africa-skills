1. Common Ostrich Breeds: Red Neck, Blue Neck, African Black

In Africa, three main ostrich breeds are farmed: Red Neck, Blue Neck, and African Black. Red Neck (also called Masai) is native to East Africa. It has a reddish neck and legs, grows fast, and is hardy in hot, dry conditions. A mature Red Neck male can weigh 120150 kg, and hens lay 4060 eggs per year. Blue Neck (Somali) is found in the Horn of Africa. It has a bluish-grey neck, is slightly smaller (100130 kg), and produces high-quality feathers. African Black (South African Black) is a composite breed developed in South Africa. It is docile, adapts well to confinement, and is the top choice for meat and leather. Males weigh 110140 kg, and hens lay 6080 eggs per year under good management.

Choosing a breed depends on your goal. For meat and leather, African Black is best because of its rapid growth and calm temperament. For extensive farming in arid areas, Red Neck survives on poor forage. Blue Neck is preferred for feather production, though feathers are a minor income stream today. Many farmers crossbreed to combine traits: Red Neck African Black gives fast growth and hardiness.

Before buying, visit a registered breeder. Ask for performance records: average daily gain (ADG), feed conversion ratio (FCR), and egg production. A good African Black chick should reach 100 kg live weight in 1012 months on 34 kg of feed per kg gain. Red Neck may take 1214 months. Always buy from a farm with a health certificate and vaccination records.

In Africa, ostrich farming is growing in South Africa, Namibia, Botswana, Kenya, and Nigeria. South Africa has the largest industry, with strict breed standards. If you are in West Africa, choose African Black for heat tolerance. In East Africa, Red Neck is common. Start with at least 10 hens and 3 males to avoid inbreeding. Remember: pure breeds cost more but give predictable results. Crossbreeds may be cheaper but vary in performance. Always check local regulationssome countries restrict live ostrich movement.

2. Characteristics for Meat, Leather, and Feather Production

Ostrich meat is red, low in fat (less than 3%), and high in protein (2022%). To maximize meat yield, select birds with a broad chest, thick thighs, and a fast growth rate. A good meat bird reaches 100 kg live weight by 1012 months, yielding 3540 kg of saleable meat. The best breeds for meat are African Black and Red Neck. Feed conversion is key: aim for 3.5 kg of feed per 1 kg of weight gain. Poor converters cost you money. Check the breast muscle: it should be firm and wide. Avoid birds with narrow chests or slow growth.

Leather comes from the hide. Ostrich leather is prized for its quill follicles, which create a unique pattern. High-quality leather requires undamaged skin. Select birds with smooth, clean hides and no scars from fighting or ticks. The best leather comes from birds slaughtered at 1214 months, when the hide is 1.21.5 m. African Black is preferred for leather because of its tight feather follicles. Red Neck hides are also good but may have more marks. To prevent damage, provide soft bedding, control ticks with acaricides, and avoid overcrowding.

Feathers are a by-product. The best feathers come from Blue Neck and Red Neck. Prime feathers are the white plumes from the wings and tail of males. A mature male produces 12 kg of feathers per year. Clean, unbroken feathers fetch higher prices. Select birds with dense, long feathers. However, feather income is small compared to meat and leather. Do not sacrifice meat quality for feathers.

In Africa, market demand varies. South Africa exports leather to Europe and Asia. Kenya and Nigeria have growing meat markets. Before selecting stock, study your local market. If you sell to a butcher, focus on meat. If you supply a tannery, focus on hide quality. Always keep records of each bird's performance: weight at 6, 12, and 18 months; feed intake; and any skin damage. This data helps you cull poor performers and breed from the best.

3. Selecting Breeding Stock: Health and Conformation

Selecting breeding stock is the most important decision on your farm. Start with health. A healthy ostrich is alert, stands upright, and has bright eyes. Check for signs of disease: runny nose, coughing, diarrhoea, or lameness. Look at the droppingsthey should be well-formed, not watery or bloody. Inspect the skin for ticks, lice, and wounds. Ask for a veterinary health certificate and vaccination history. Common vaccinations in Africa include Newcastle disease and avian influenza. A good breeder will have records.

Conformation matters. For breeding, choose birds with straight legs, strong feet, and a broad chest. Leg deformities (bowed legs, twisted toes) are hereditarydo not breed from affected birds. The beak should be straight and not overgrown. The neck should be long and flexible. In males, the penis should be clean and not prolapsed. In females, the vent should be clean and free of discharge. Age also matters: breed hens from 2 to 10 years, males from 3 to 10 years. Younger birds may have low fertility; older birds produce fewer eggs.

Body condition is critical. A breeding hen should weigh 100120 kg; a male 120150 kg. Use a livestock scale or weigh tape. Condition score: 1 (thin) to 5 (obese). Aim for 34. Thin birds have low fertility; fat birds have egg-laying problems. Feel the breastboneit should be felt but not sharp. Check the keel for sores.

In Africa, heat stress affects fertility. Select birds that tolerate high temperaturesRed Neck and African Black are good choices. Avoid breeding from birds that pant heavily or seek shade constantly. Also consider temperament: calm birds are easier to handle and less likely to injure themselves or handlers. Never breed from aggressive birds. Finally, buy from a reputable breeder who keeps performance records. Ask for the parents' egg production and growth data. This helps you predict the offspring's performance.

4. Genetic Improvement and Record Keeping

Genetic improvement means breeding better birds over time. You need three things: clear goals, accurate records, and selection pressure. Start by deciding what you want: more meat, better leather, or more eggs. For meat, select for fast growth and high feed efficiency. For leather, select for clean hides and large body size. For eggs, select for high egg production and fertility. Write down your goals and stick to them.

Record keeping is the backbone of genetic improvement. Use a notebook or a simple spreadsheet. For each bird, record: ID number, breed, hatch date, weight at 6, 12, and 18 months, feed intake, egg production (for hens), fertility, hatch rate, and health issues. Also record parentage. In Africa, many farmers use paper records, but a smartphone app like Farmbrite or a simple Excel sheet works well. Keep records for at least 5 years.

Selection means choosing the best birds to breed and culling the rest. For meat, keep the top 20% of males and top 50% of females based on growth rate. For eggs, keep hens that lay more than 60 eggs per year. Cull birds with poor growth, deformities, or low fertility. Never breed from a bird with a known genetic defect. Inbreeding is a major problemit causes small chicks, low fertility, and weak immunity. Avoid mating close relatives. Rotate males every 2 years. If you have a small flock, bring in a new male from a different farm every 3 years.

In Africa, organizations like the South African Ostrich Business Chamber provide breeding standards. Follow them. Also, participate in local farmer groups to exchange breeding stock and knowledge. Remember: genetic improvement is slow. It takes 35 generations to see major changes. But with consistent selection and good records, you can increase meat yield by 1015% in 5 years. Start small: select your best 10 hens and 3 males, and build from there.

5. Sourcing Chicks or Adult Ostriches

You can start ostrich farming by buying chicks, young birds, or adult breeders. Each has pros and cons. Chicks (day-old to 3 months) are cheaper but require intensive care. A day-old chick costs $30$50 in South Africa, $50$80 in Kenya, and $80$120 in Nigeria. They need a brooder with a heat lamp (3235C for the first week, reducing by 2C weekly). Feed them a starter mash with 20% protein. Mortality can be 2030% if not managed well. Buy chicks from a reputable hatchery with a health certificate. Ask for vaccination records.

Young birds (312 months) are hardier but more expensive. A 6-month-old bird costs $150$300. They are easier to raise and have lower mortality. However, they may be stressed from transport. Adults (over 12 months) are the most expensive: $500$1,500 per bird. They are ready to breed but may have bad habits or health issues. Only buy adults from a farm with full records. Avoid buying from auctions or unknown sellersdisease risk is high.

In Africa, source birds from established farms. South Africa has many registered breeders (e.g., in Oudtshoorn). Namibia and Botswana also have good stock. In East Africa, Kenya's Ostrich Farmers Association can connect you. In West Africa, Nigeria has a few large farms near Abuja and Lagos. Always visit the farm before buying. Check the birds' condition, ask about mortality rates, and see the parent stock. Never buy birds that look sick or thin.

Transport is critical. Ostriches are stressed easily. Use a well-ventilated truck with soft bedding. Do not transport during the hottest part of the day. For chicks, use a climate-controlled vehicle. For adults, cover their heads with a hood to reduce stress. Transport at night if possible. On arrival, isolate the birds in a quarantine pen for 30 days. Provide fresh water and feed. Watch for signs of disease. Only mix with your flock after quarantine. Always check local regulationssome countries require import permits and health certificates for live ostriches.

6. Quarantine and Acclimation Procedures

Quarantine is a mandatory step for any new ostrich. It protects your existing flock from diseases. Set up a quarantine pen at least 50 meters from your main flock. The pen should have a solid fence, shade, and clean water. Use separate boots, clothing, and tools for quarantine. Never let quarantine birds mix with others. The quarantine period is 30 days for ostriches. During this time, observe the birds daily for signs of illness: lethargy, loss of appetite, diarrhoea, coughing, or lameness. Take their temperature weekly (normal is 3940C). If any bird shows signs, call a vet immediately.

During quarantine, test for common diseases. In Africa, test for avian influenza, Newcastle disease, and salmonella. Also treat for internal parasites (deworm with fenbendazole at 10 mg/kg) and external parasites (spray with deltamethrin). Vaccinate if not already done. Keep records of all treatments. After 30 days, if all birds are healthy, you can move them to the main flock. But first, introduce them gradually: put them in a pen next to the main flock for 7 days so they can see and hear each other. Then mix them.

Acclimation means helping the birds adjust to their new home. Ostriches are creatures of habit. Changes in feed, water, and routine cause stress. For the first week, feed the same diet they were eating at the source farm. Gradually switch to your feed over 710 days. Provide plenty of clean water. Add electrolytes or vitamin C to the water to reduce stress. Keep noise and handling to a minimum. Spend time near the pen so they get used to you.

In hot African climates, acclimation includes heat management. Provide shade, misters, or sprinklers. Ostriches pant to cool down, but excessive panting means heat stress. Ensure they have access to cool water at all times. In cold areas, provide dry bedding and windbreaks. Monitor their behaviour: happy ostriches forage, preen, and rest. Stressed ostriches pace, pant, or hide. If you see stress, check for predators, noise, or overcrowding. Always keep a quarantine log: date, bird ID, observations, treatments, and vet visits. This helps you track health and prove compliance with regulations.

<h2>Practical Reference: Ostrich Breeds, Selection &amp; Sourcing for African Farms</h2>

<p>This practical reference is the working document for the <strong>Ostrich Breeds and Selection</strong> module. It is written for the African commercial producer who must decide, in order: <em>which breed or cross</em>, <em>which individual birds</em>, <em>where to buy them</em>, and <em>how to land them alive and productive</em>. Every decision here has a direct cost attached feed conversion, slaughter age, skin grade, feather yield, and mortality in the first 30 days. Treat the numbers below as planning benchmarks, not guarantees; your local market, climate, and abattoir contracts will shift them.</p>

<p>Ostrich farming in Africa spans two very different business models. The first is the <strong>intensive commercial model</strong> (South Africa, Namibia, Botswana, Zimbabwe, Kenya, Egypt, Nigeria, Zambia), built around slaughter birds for meat and leather, with feathers as a by-product. The second is the <strong>emerging smallholder and semi-intensive model</strong>, where birds are raised on pasture, often for live sales, breeding stock, tourism, or egg production. Breed choice and sourcing strategy differ sharply between the two.</p>

<h3>1. Breeds and Their Commercial Logic</h3>

<p>The domesticated ostrich (<em>Struthio camelus domesticus</em>) in Africa is largely derived from the South African Black, with infusions of Red Neck (Kenya/Tanzania/Zimbabwe origin) and Blue Neck (North African origin) genetics. The three names you will meet in the trade are:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>African Black (South African Black):</strong> the industry standard for slaughter. Compact frame, high skin value, calmer temperament, earlier sexual maturity (females from 1824 months), and the best documented feed conversion. This is the breed to build a meat-and-leather business around.</li>
<li><strong>Red Neck:</strong> larger, faster-growing, heavier frame, good for meat weight and crossbreeding vigour. More aggressive and flightier; needs better fencing and handling facilities. Common in East and Southern Africa.</li>
<li><strong>Blue Neck:</strong> hardy, tolerant of heat and poorer forage, good for semi-arid and extensive systems. Slower growth and lower skin grade than African Black; often used for hardiness and crossbreeding.</li>
</ul>

<p>In practice, most commercial African flocks are <strong>crosses</strong>. A common productive strategy is a Red Neck or Blue Neck sire over African Black hens, or a three-way cross, to capture hybrid vigour (heterosis) in growth while retaining skin quality. Selection is therefore not "which pure breed" but "which combination best fits my market and my environment."</p>

<h3>2. Characteristics for Meat, Leather and Feather Production</h3>

<p>Selection must be driven by the end product, because the three revenue streams reward different traits. Meat rewards growth rate, feed conversion, dressing percentage and breast muscle depth. Leather rewards skin size, freedom from scars, and follicle uniformity a single scar across the crown or leg can downgrade a skin by 3050%. Feathers reward volume, length, and colour consistency, though feather income is now a minor fraction of gross margin for most African producers.</p>

<h3>3. Selecting Breeding Stock: Health and Conformation</h3>

<p>Selection is where profit is made or lost, long before the first chick hatches. Buy breeding stock only from a breeder who can show you <strong>written records</strong>: hatch date, parentage, vaccination history, and previous reproductive performance. In Africa, the most common costly mistakes are buying birds with no traceable history, buying at auction without a health check, and buying "cheap" adults that turn out to be infertile or chronically lame.</p>

<p><strong>Conformation checklist for a breeding bird:</strong> straight, well-set legs with no swelling at the hock; broad, deep chest; wings that sit symmetrically and do not droop; clean eyes and nares; smooth, unblemished skin with no old scars or feather cysts; and a beak that meets evenly. In males, check the penis (phallus) for prolapse or injury this is a common cause of infertility and is easily missed. In females, check for a distended or soiled vent, which may indicate egg-binding or infection.</p>

<p><strong>Health checklist:</strong> birds should be alert, move freely, and have no diarrhoea. Ask for the last 12 months of mortality records and a recent veterinary certificate. Test or quarantine for the major risks: avian influenza, Newcastle disease, and internal parasites. In many African jurisdictions, live ostrich movement requires a veterinary import/health permit confirm this before you pay a deposit.</p>

<h3>4. Genetic Improvement and Record Keeping</h3>

<p>Genetic gain in ostrich is slow because generation interval is long (females first lay at 1824 months) and heritability of many traits is moderate. The practical levers are: (a) select for growth and skin quality simultaneously, not one at the expense of the other; (b) cull aggressively for poor fertility, lameness, and temperament; (c) avoid close inbreeding by rotating sires every two to three years; and (d) record everything.</p>

<p>Records that matter commercially: individual bird ID, hatch weight, monthly weights, feed consumed, egg production per hen, fertility and hatchability, mortality and cause, and skin grade at slaughter. Without these, you cannot calculate cost per kilogram of gain or identify your best-performing bloodlines. A simple paper or spreadsheet system is sufficient; consistency matters more than software.</p>

<h3>5. Sourcing Chicks or Adult Ostriches</h3>

<p>You have three sourcing routes. <strong>Day-old chicks</strong> are cheapest per bird and easiest to acclimatise, but carry the highest early mortality risk and require brooding infrastructure. <strong>Weaners (36 months)</strong> are the most popular commercial entry point: lower mortality than day-olds, faster to slaughter, and easier to transport. <strong>Adult breeding stock</strong> is the most expensive and carries the highest risk of introducing disease or infertile birds, but it is the only route to immediate egg production.</p>

<p>Whichever route you choose, buy from a breeder registered with your national poultry or ostrich association, inspect the farm yourself if possible, and insist on a written sales agreement covering health status, vaccination history, and a replacement or refund clause for birds that die of pre-existing conditions within a defined window (commonly 714 days).</p>

<h3>6. Quarantine and Acclimation Procedures</h3>

<p>Quarantine is not optional. Plan for <strong>30 days minimum</strong> (some authorities require 45) in a facility at least 100 metres from your main flock, with its own water, feed, and protective clothing. On arrival: inspect every bird, weigh and record, check for injuries and parasites, and isolate any bird showing lameness, diarrhoea, or respiratory signs. Provide shade, clean water, and a familiar feed type; transition to your own ration over 710 days to avoid digestive upset.</p>

<p>Acclimation is gradual. Introduce birds to your climate and terrain in short supervised periods, starting in the coolest part of the day. Watch for heat stress (panting, wings held out) and cold stress (huddling) in the first two weeks. Keep records of feed intake and weight gain during quarantine a bird that is not gaining weight in quarantine is a bird that will cost you money later.</p>

<ul>
<li>Define your market first: meat, leather, feathers, or breeding stock then choose breed and cross accordingly.</li>
<li>For intensive meat and leather, prioritise African Black genetics; use Red Neck or Blue Neck for hardiness and crossbreeding vigour.</li>
<li>Buy only from registered breeders with written health, vaccination and performance records.</li>
<li>Inspect conformation: legs, chest, wings, skin, eyes, and (in males) the phallus.</li>
<li>Weaners are usually the best risk-adjusted entry point for a new commercial farm.</li>
<li>Quarantine all new birds for at least 30 days in a separate facility before mixing.</li>
<li>Keep individual records from day one: ID, weight, feed, fertility, mortality, skin grade.</li>
