1. Sorting and Cleaning Raw Materials: Removing Stones, Plastic, and Metal

Before you can make good briquettes, you must start with clean raw materials. Whether you are using agricultural waste like maize cobs, rice husks, or sawdust, or collecting paper and cardboard, these materials often contain unwanted items that can damage your equipment or ruin your briquettes. Sorting and cleaning is the first and most important step.

Start by spreading your raw materials on a clean tarpaulin or concrete floor. Wear thick gloves to protect your hands. Pick out any stones, rocks, or pieces of metal. Stones will dull your crusher blades and can even break them. Metal pieces like nails, wire, or screws can jam your grinder and cause serious injury. Use a strong magnet to help remove small metal bitsyou can find magnets in old speakers or buy one at a hardware store.

Plastic is another common contaminant, especially if you collect waste from markets or dumpsites. Plastic melts during burning and releases toxic fumes. It also weakens the briquette. Remove all plastic bags, wrappers, and bottles. If you are using paper, remove any plastic tape or windows from envelopes.

For fine materials like rice husks or sawdust, use a sieve or mesh screen to sift out sand, dirt, and small stones. A simple wire mesh with 5-10 mm holes works well. You can build a wooden frame and staple the mesh to it. Shake the sieve over a wheelbarrow or container. The clean material falls through, while stones and debris stay on top.

After sorting, store your clean materials in a dry, covered area to prevent recontamination. Use sacks or a covered bin. Keep them off the ground on a pallet or stones. This simple step saves you time and money by protecting your machines and ensuring consistent, high-quality briquettes.

2. Drying Methods: Sun Drying, Solar Dryers, and Simple Kilns

Drying is critical because wet raw materials won't bind well and will produce smoky, crumbly briquettes. The goal is to reduce moisture content to below 15%, ideally around 10-12%. There are three main methods you can use in Africa: sun drying, solar dryers, and simple kilns.

Sun drying is the cheapest and most common method. Spread your raw materials in a thin layer (5-10 cm thick) on a black plastic sheet, concrete floor, or tarpaulin. Black absorbs heat better. Stir the material every 2-3 hours with a rake or shovel to expose fresh surfaces. In hot, dry weather (30-35C), thin materials like rice husks or sawdust can dry in 1-2 days. Thicker materials like maize cobs may take 3-5 days. Cover the materials at night or if it rains. A major drawback is that you need a large flat area and good weather.

Solar dryers are more efficient and protect materials from rain and dust. A simple solar dryer is a wooden box with a transparent polythene or glass top and ventilation holes at the bottom and top. The sun heats the air inside, which rises and escapes, pulling in fresh air. You can build one using local timber and clear plastic sheets. Inside, place your materials on trays made of mesh. A solar dryer can dry materials in 1-2 days even in moderate sunshine, and it keeps them clean.

Simple kilns are used for drying and also for carbonizing materials if you want to make charcoal briquettes. A kiln is a metal drum or brick structure where you light a small fire at the bottom and let the heat and smoke dry the material above. This is faster but requires more skill and fuel. For drying only, you can use a drum with a fire underneath and a tray on top. Be careful not to burn the material. Kilns are best for large-scale operations.

Whichever method you choose, test dryness by squeezing a handful. If it crumbles and feels dry, it's ready. If it holds together like dough, it needs more drying.

3. Size Reduction: Using Manual Crushers, Grinders, and Hammermills

Size reduction is the process of breaking raw materials into smaller pieces. For briquettes, you need particles that are small and uniformusually between 2 mm and 10 mm, depending on your briquette press. Smaller particles bind better and produce denser, stronger briquettes. There are three main tools: manual crushers, grinders, and hammermills.

Manual crushers are the simplest and cheapest. They include mortar and pestle, hand-cranked grain mills, and wooden mallets. Use a mortar and pestle to crush brittle materials like charcoal, dry leaves, or small cobs. For larger quantities, a hand-cranked grinder (like those used for maize) can be adjusted to produce coarse particles. These are labor-intensive but require no fuel or electricity. They are ideal for small-scale production or when you are just starting.

Grinders (also called hammer mills or burr mills) are motorized or engine-driven. A hammer mill has rotating hammers that beat the material against a screen until it is small enough to pass through. You can buy a small hammer mill (5-10 HP) for around $300-$800. It can process 100-500 kg per hour. For agricultural residues like maize cobs, rice husks, or groundnut shells, a hammer mill is very effective. Make sure the screen size matches your desired particle size. A 5 mm screen gives you finer material; a 10 mm screen gives coarser.

Hammermills are larger and more powerful, often used in commercial operations. They can handle tough materials like wood chips, palm kernel shells, and coconut shells. They come with different screen sizes and can be powered by diesel or electric motors. A typical hammermill for briquetting costs $1,000-$3,000 and can process 500-2000 kg per hour. If you are producing more than 500 kg of briquettes per day, a hammermill is a good investment.

Whichever tool you use, always wear safety glasses, a dust mask, and gloves. Feed material slowly and evenly to avoid jamming. Keep the machine clean and sharp. After crushing, store the material in a dry place. Uniform particle size is key to consistent briquette quality.

4. Mixing Different Materials for Better Briquettes

Mixing different raw materials is a smart way to improve briquette quality, reduce costs, and use what you have locally. Different materials have different properties: some burn hot, some bind well, some are cheap, and some are abundant. By blending them, you can create a briquette that is strong, burns long, and produces less smoke.

A good briquette mix usually has three components: a base material (for heat), a binder (to hold it together), and a filler (to add bulk and reduce cost). For example, charcoal dust (base) mixed with cassava starch (binder) and sawdust (filler) makes excellent charcoal briquettes. For non-carbonized briquettes, you can mix maize cobs, rice husks, and paper pulp. The ratio depends on your materials, but a common starting point is 50% base, 30% filler, and 20% binder (by dry weight).

When mixing, aim for a uniform blend. Use a shovel or hoe to turn the pile at least three times. For small batches, mix in a wheelbarrow or on a clean tarpaulin. For larger batches, a cement mixer or a simple drum mixer works well. Add water gradually while mixing until the mixture holds together when squeezed but doesn't drip. The ideal moisture for pressing is 15-25% depending on your press.

Test different ratios. Make a small batch (e.g., 5 kg) and press a few briquettes. Let them dry and then test how they burn. Adjust the mix: if they crumble, add more binder; if they burn too fast, add more dense material like charcoal fines; if they smoke too much, reduce the amount of green or wet materials. Keep records of what works. Local materials like cassava peels, banana peels, and coconut shells can also be dried and ground to add binding or heat value.

Remember, consistency is key. Once you find a mix that works, stick to it and measure your ingredients by weight or volume. This ensures every batch of briquettes performs the same. Mixing is not just about combining materialsit's about creating a product that meets your customers' needs.

5. Adding Binders: Cassava Starch, Clay, and Other Local Options

Binders are the glue that holds briquettes together. Without a good binder, your briquettes will crumble and fall apart. The binder helps particles stick together under pressure and keeps the briquette intact after drying. There are many local binder options in Africa, and the best one depends on your raw materials and what is available.

Cassava starch is one of the best binders. It is cheap, widely available, and works well. To make cassava starch binder, peel and wash fresh cassava tubers, then grate or blend them. Mix the grated cassava with water (about 1 part cassava to 3 parts water) and stir. You can also use cassava flour: mix 1 kg of flour with 10 liters of water and heat gently until it forms a thick paste. This paste is then mixed with your raw materials. Use about 5-10% binder by weight of dry materials. For example, for 10 kg of dry material, use 0.5-1 kg of cassava starch. Cassava binder gives strong briquettes but can attract insects if not dried properly.

Clay is another common binder. Use fine clay soil, not sandy soil. Dry the clay, crush it, and mix with water to form a slurry. Add the slurry to your raw materials. Clay is very cheap and improves the briquette's ability to hold together during burning. However, clay adds ash and reduces heat value. Use only 5-15% clay by weight. Too much clay makes the briquette burn poorly.

Other local binders include: wheat flour (works like cassava but more expensive), rice starch (from broken rice or rice water), okra mucilage (from boiled okra), and even cow dung (for non-food use, but it smells). You can also use waste paper pulp as a bindersoak waste paper in water for 24 hours, then blend it into a pulp. Paper pulp is excellent for binding and is free if you collect waste paper.

To use any binder, mix it thoroughly with your dry materials before adding water. The binder should be evenly distributed. Then add water until the mixture reaches the right consistency. Test by squeezing a handful: it should hold together without dripping. Let the briquettes dry completely before use. With the right binder, your briquettes will be strong, durable, and easy to handle.

6. Achieving the Right Moisture Level for Pressing

Moisture level is the most critical factor in briquette pressing. If the mixture is too dry, the briquettes will crumble and not hold together. If it's too wet, the briquettes will be soft, take too long to dry, and may grow mold. The ideal moisture content for pressing is usually between 15% and 25%, depending on your press type and raw materials. For manual presses, aim for 20-25%; for motorized presses, 15-20% is better.

How do you know if you have the right moisture? The squeeze test is the simplest method. Take a handful of the mixed material and squeeze it tightly in your fist. Open your hand. The material should form a ball that holds its shape but does not drip water. If it drips, it's too wetadd more dry material and mix. If it crumbles and falls apart, it's too dryadd a little water and mix again. Repeat until you get a firm ball that doesn't stick to your hand.

For more accuracy, you can use a moisture meter. These are available at agricultural supply stores or online for $20-$50. A simple moisture meter has two prongs that you stick into the material. It gives a reading in percentage. If you don't have a meter, you can do a rough test by weighing a sample before and after drying. Weigh 100 g of your mixture, dry it in an oven or under the sun until completely dry, then weigh again. The weight loss is the moisture. For example, if it weighs 80 g after drying, the moisture was 20%.

Adjusting moisture: If your mixture is too wet, spread it out on a tarpaulin in the sun for 1-2 hours, stirring occasionally. Or add more dry raw material. If it's too dry, add water gradually using a watering can or spray bottle, mixing continuously. Never dump water all at onceyou might overshoot. Always mix thoroughly after each addition.

Once you have the right moisture, press your briquettes immediately. Don't let the mixture sit for too long, as it can dry out or ferment. Pressed briquettes should be placed in a shaded, ventilated area to dry slowly. Rapid drying in direct sun can cause cracks. With practice, you will develop a feel for the right moisture, and your briquettes will be consistently strong and burn well.

7. Practical Reference

<section>
<h2>Practical Reference: Preparing Raw Materials for Briquetting</h2>

<p>In briquette and pellet production, raw material preparation accounts for up to <strong>60% of total production cost</strong> and determines whether your final product burns cleanly, holds together, and sells at a profit. Across Africa from cassava peels in Nigeria to rice husks in Tanzania, maize cobs in Kenya, and sawdust in Ghana the difference between a briquette business that thrives and one that fails often comes down to how well you sort, dry, grind, mix, bind, and moisture-balance your feedstock before pressing.</p>

<p>This practical reference distils the six preparation stages into a business-first workflow you can apply on any scale: from a 50 kg/day manual operation to a 2-tonne/day semi-industrial line.</p>

<h3>1. Sorting and Cleaning Raw Materials: Removing Stones, Plastic, and Metal</h3>

<p>Contamination is the number-one cause of equipment failure and product rejection. Stones and metal shatter hammers and dies; plastic melts into the binder matrix and produces toxic fumes when the briquette burns; sand and grit reduce calorific value and increase ash content.</p>

<p>Practical sorting sequence for African feedstock:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Visual pick:</strong> Remove plastic bags, nylon, cloth, and large stones by hand on a sorting table or tarpaulin.</li>
<li><strong>Magnetic separation:</strong> A simple permanent magnet (or magnetic pulley) over the conveyor catches nails, wire, and scrap metal.</li>
<li><strong>Screening:</strong> A 1020 mm mesh screen removes sand, soil, and fines before drying.</li>
<li><strong>Density separation:</strong> For mixed municipal or market waste, a simple water float-sink or air classifier separates heavy inerts.</li>
<li><strong>Quality gate:</strong> Reject any batch with visible plastic one contaminated tonne can ruin a week of production.</li>
</ul>

<h3>2. Drying Methods: Sun Drying, Solar Dryers, and Simple Kilns</h3>

<p>Moisture is the enemy of briquetting. Wet feedstock clogs hammermills, produces steam explosions in the press, and yields weak, mould-prone briquettes. Target feedstock moisture before grinding is typically <strong>1015%</strong>; before pressing, <strong>812%</strong> depending on binder and press type.</p>

<h3>4. Mixing Different Materials for Better Briquettes</h3>

<p>Single-feedstock briquettes are rarely optimal. Blending improves calorific value, combustion, and mechanical strength. Proven African blends:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Rice husk (60%) + sawdust (40%)</strong> high silica husk needs sawdust's lignin to bind.</li>
<li><strong>Maize cob (50%) + cassava peel (30%) + sawdust (20%)</strong> balanced density and burn time.</li>
<li><strong>Charcoal fines (70%) + clay (20%) + starch (10%)</strong> classic cookstove briquette.</li>
<li><strong>Groundnut shell (50%) + palm kernel shell (50%)</strong> high energy, good for industrial use.</li>
</ul>
<p>Mix in a horizontal mixer, concrete mixer, or even on a clean concrete floor with shovels for small batches. Aim for <strong>homogeneity within 5%</strong> visible colour streaks mean poor mixing.</p>

<h3>5. Adding Binders: Cassava Starch, Clay, and Other Local Options</h3>

<p>Binders hold particles together and determine briquette durability. Local, low-cost options dominate African production:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cassava starch (510% dry weight):</strong> Best all-round binder. Gelatinise by cooking in hot water before mixing. Cost: low where cassava is grown.</li>
<li><strong>Clay / bentonite (1020%):</strong> Cheap, widely available, but increases ash and reduces calorific value. Use sparingly.</li>
<li><strong>Molasses (515%):</strong> Excellent binding and adds energy; can attract insects if briquettes are stored damp.</li>
<li><strong>Waste paper pulp (510%):</strong> Free in urban areas; good for charcoal briquettes.</li>
<li><strong>Palm oil mill effluent (POME) sludge:</strong> Emerging binder in West Africa; needs drying.</li>
</ul>

<div style="border:3px solid #c62828; background:#ffebee; padding:16px; margin:20px 0; border-radius:6px;">
<strong style="color:#b71c1c; font-size:1.05em;"> SAFETY WARNING Binder and Dust Hazards</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#1b1b1b;">Biomass dust is <strong>explosive and carcinogenic</strong>. Always wear an N95 dust mask, safety goggles, and gloves when grinding, mixing, and handling dry feedstock. Never operate a hammermill in an enclosed space without forced ventilation dust explosions have killed operators across Africa. When cooking cassava starch binder, use heat-resistant gloves to avoid scalds. Store molasses and starch binders away from pesticides and fuel to prevent cross-contamination of the final briquette. Keep a dry-powder fire extinguisher within 5 metres of all grinding and mixing equipment.</p>
</div>

<h3>6. Achieving the Right Moisture Level for Pressing</h3>

<p>Moisture is the single most controllable variable in briquette quality. Too wet weak, mouldy briquettes, press clogging, steam explosions. Too dry crumbly briquettes that fail to bind.</p>

<p><strong>Target moisture before pressing:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Manual / low-pressure press: 1218%</li>
<li>Hydraulic press: 812%</li>
<li>Screw extruder: 610%</li>
<li>Pellet mill: 1015%</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Low-cost moisture test (the "squeeze test"):</strong> Take a handful of mixed feedstock, squeeze firmly. If it holds shape without dripping water and crumbles when poked, moisture is close to target. For accuracy, use a <strong>moisture meter (USD 30150)</strong> a worthwhile investment for any commercial operation.</p>

<p>If too wet: spread on drying bed for 26 hours, or add dry feedstock to the mix. If too dry: spray water evenly while mixing never dump water in one spot.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li> Sort feedstock on a clean tarpaulin remove all plastic, metal, and stones before any processing.</li>
<li> Run a magnet over the feedstock to catch nails and wire.</li>
<li> Screen out sand and soil with a 1020 mm mesh.</li>
<li> Dry feedstock to 1015% moisture before grinding; test with a moisture meter or squeeze test.</li>
<li> Grind to 36 mm for most press types; check by sieving a sample.</li>
<li> Blend 23 feedstocks for better density, burn time, and strength.</li>
<li> Add binder (510% cassava starch is the safest default) and mix until uniform.</li>
<li> Re-check moisture (812% for pressing) and adjust before feeding the press.</li>
</ol>
