1. Biosecurity measures on dairy farms

Biosecurity means keeping diseases out of your farm and stopping them from spreading if they get in. On an African dairy farm, you don't need expensive equipmentâjust consistent habits. First, control who enters your farm. Keep a visitor log and allow only essential people. Provide boots and overalls for visitors, or at least a footbath with disinfectant (e.g., 2% sodium hypochlorite) at the entrance. Vehicles should park away from animal areas.

Second, manage new animals carefully. Never mix a new cow with your herd immediately. Isolate her for at least 21 days in a separate pen, 50 meters away if possible. Test for common diseases like brucellosis and tuberculosis before introduction. If you buy from a market, assume the animal is infected until proven otherwise.

Third, practice good hygiene. Clean and disinfect calf pens, milking parlors, and water troughs regularly. Use separate boots and tools for sick animals. Remove manure daily to reduce flies and bacteria. Store feed in sealed containers to prevent rodents.

Fourth, control wildlife and pests. Rodents, birds, and stray dogs can carry diseases. Use traps and keep feed covered. Fence your farm to keep out neighboring livestock. If you have an outbreak, quarantine sick animals immediately and call a vet. Remember, biosecurity is cheaper than treatment. A simple footbath and isolation pen can save you thousands of shillings in lost milk and dead cows.

Finally, train your workers. Everyone should know the rules: no spitting, no sharing tools between sick and healthy pens, and always wash hands after handling animals. Review biosecurity monthly. In many African farms, diseases like foot-and-mouth spread through shared grazing or water points. By keeping your herd separate and clean, you protect your investment.

2. Common diseases: mastitis, lameness, and metabolic disorders

Mastitis is the most costly disease for dairy farmers. It is an infection of the udder, usually caused by bacteria entering through the teat canal. Signs include hot, swollen quarters, clots or blood in milk, and a drop in yield. To check, use the California Mastitis Test (CMT) on each quarter. Strip milk onto a paddle, add reagent, and look for gel formation. Treat mild cases with intramammary antibiotics (e.g., cloxacillin) for 3-5 days. For severe cases with fever, call a vet for injectable antibiotics. Prevent mastitis by dipping teats after milking with 0.5% iodine, keeping bedding dry, and milking healthy cows first.

Lameness makes cows reluctant to walk, eat, and mate. Common causes are foot rot, sole ulcers, and digital dermatitis. In Africa, wet, muddy floors and sharp stones are major culprits. Check hooves monthly. Trim overgrown hooves with a hoof knife and apply a footbath with 5% copper sulfate weekly. For foot rot, clean the hoof and give injectable oxytetracycline (20 mg/kg) for 3 days. Isolate lame cows on soft, dry bedding. Prevent by keeping walkways clean and dry, and avoiding sharp objects.

Metabolic disorders happen when the cow's body cannot adjust to high milk production. The most common are milk fever (hypocalcemia) and ketosis. Milk fever occurs around calving: the cow goes down, cannot stand, and has cold ears. Treat immediately with 500 ml of 23% calcium borogluconate injected slowly into the vein. Ketosis shows as weight loss, reduced appetite, and sweet-smelling breath. Treat with 500 ml of 50% dextrose IV or oral propylene glycol (300 ml daily for 3 days). Prevent both by feeding a balanced diet, avoiding overconditioning, and providing easy access to water and minerals. Always consult a vet for severe cases.

3. Vaccination programs and herd health protocols

Vaccination protects your herd from deadly diseases. In Africa, the core vaccines for dairy cattle are: anthrax, blackleg, foot-and-mouth disease (FMD), lumpy skin disease (LSD), and rabies (in some regions). Your local vet will know which are required by law. For example, in Kenya, FMD is endemic, so vaccinate every 6 months. Anthrax and blackleg are given annually before the rainy season.

Follow a strict schedule. Calves: vaccinate against blackleg at 3 months, then boost at 6 months. For FMD, start at 4 months and repeat every 6 months. Pregnant cows: avoid live vaccines; use killed ones. Always record the vaccine name, batch number, date, and animal ID. Store vaccines at 2-8Â°C in a fridgeânever freeze. Use a new needle for each animal to prevent abscesses.

A herd health protocol is a written plan for routine care. It includes: monthly hoof trimming, weekly footbathing, daily mastitis checks, and quarterly fecal egg counts for parasites. Set a calving protocol: move dry cows to a clean pen 3 weeks before calving, give calcium boluses after calving, and check for retained placenta. For calves, ensure colostrum within 2 hours of birth (2 liters).

Also plan for disease outbreaks. Keep a quarantine pen, disinfectant, and a list of emergency contacts. If you see sudden deaths, call a vet immediatelyâdo not wait. Many African farms lose animals to preventable diseases like anthrax because they skip vaccination. Work with your local veterinary officer to design a protocol that fits your farm and budget. Review it every 6 months.

4. Parasite control strategies

Parasites steal milk, cause weight loss, and even kill. The main types are internal (worms, flukes) and external (ticks, flies, lice). In Africa, ticks transmit deadly diseases like East Coast fever (ECF) and heartwater. Control them with regular dipping or spraying. Use acaricides such as amitraz (e.g., Taktic) every week during the rainy season, every two weeks in dry season. Rotate chemicals to prevent resistance. For zero-grazing units, hand-spray the ears, tail, and udder where ticks hide.

Internal parasites: roundworms cause diarrhea and poor growth. Do fecal egg counts every 3 months. If eggs per gram (EPG) exceed 200, deworm with albendazole (7.5 mg/kg) or ivermectin (0.2 mg/kg). Treat all calves at weaning and all cows at drying off. Liver flukes need a different drug: triclabendazole (12 mg/kg) once a year in wet areas. Rotate dewormers to avoid resistance. Do not deworm pregnant cows in the first trimester.

Flies are a nuisance and spread mastitis and pinkeye. Use fly traps, keep manure dry, and apply pour-on insecticides (e.g., cypermethrin) every 4 weeks. For lice, dust with coumaphos powder. Also control rodents and birdsâthey carry ticks and worms.

Integrated parasite management works best: combine chemical treatment with good hygiene, pasture rotation, and resistant breeds (e.g., Zebu crosses). In many African farms, overuse of drugs has led to resistant worms. So always dose accuratelyâweigh your animals or use a weigh tape. And never use expired drugs. If you see anemia (pale gums), bottle jaw, or chronic diarrhea, call a vet for a proper diagnosis. Remember, healthy animals need fewer drugs.

5. Recognizing signs of illness and when to call a veterinarian

Cows cannot talk, so you must learn their body language. A healthy cow is alert, eats well, chews cud, and has bright eyes and a moist nose. Sick cows show subtle signs first: standing apart from the herd, droopy ears, reduced appetite, and a drop in milk yield. Check your herd at least twice dailyâearly morning and evening. Look for these key indicators:

- Appetite: A sudden drop in feed intake is the first sign of many diseases.
- Temperature: Normal is 38.0-39.3Â°C. Take it rectally with a digital thermometer. Fever above 39.5Â°C means infection.
- Rumination: A healthy cow chews cud 6-8 hours daily. If she stops, her rumen is not working.
- Manure: Watery, bloody, or black manure indicates problems. Hard, dry manure means dehydration.
- Breathing: Rapid, labored breathing or coughing suggests pneumonia.
- Udder: Hot, swollen quarters or clots in milk mean mastitis.
- Hooves: Lameness, swelling, or reluctance to walk.

When to call a vet? Call immediately if: the cow cannot stand, has a temperature above 40Â°C, is bleeding, has a prolapsed uterus, or has severe diarrhea with blood. Also call if you see sudden death in more than one animalâthis could be anthrax, which is zoonotic (spreads to humans). For mild cases like a slight drop in milk or a small wound, you can treat yourself if you have training. But never guess with antibiotics. Overuse creates resistance. Keep a notebook with your vet's phone number. In many African villages, the vet may be farâso learn basic first aid: rehydration with oral fluids (e.g., 20 liters of water with electrolytes), wound cleaning with iodine, and isolation. But for anything serious, a vet's visit is cheaper than losing a cow.

6. Record keeping for health and treatment

Good records turn a hobby into a business. Without them, you repeat mistakes and lose money. For health, you need four types of records: individual animal treatments, herd health events, vaccination logs, and calving records. Use a simple notebook or a smartphone app like Farmbrite or a printed template from your vet. Each cow should have an ID (ear tag or name). Write down every treatment: date, animal ID, disease, drug used, dose, route (injection, oral), and withdrawal period.

Withdrawal period is critical. For example, if you treat a cow with oxytetracycline, milk must be discarded for 3-5 days (check the label). If you sell milk with drug residues, it can harm people and get you banned from the market. So record the date and time of last treatment. Also record vaccinations: vaccine name, batch number, date, and next due date. For calves, record birth date, colostrum intake, and any scours or pneumonia.

Why keep records? First, they help you spot patterns. If three cows get mastitis in one month, you know your milking hygiene needs work. Second, they prove your compliance to buyers and vets. Third, they save moneyâyou avoid buying the same drug twice or treating the wrong animal. Fourth, they help you calculate costs and profit per cow.

Use a simple system: a whiteboard in the milking parlor for daily events, a notebook for detailed treatments, and a folder for receipts. Review records weekly with your workers. In Africa, many farms lose money because they forget which cow was treated. So make it a habit: every treatment gets written down before you leave the pen. If you use a smartphone, take a photo of the drug label. At the end of the year, tally up: how many cases of mastitis? How many vaccinations? This data helps you plan and improve. Remember, the cow that gets treated but not recorded is a cow you will treat againâand that costs double.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Dairy Herd Health Management in African Dairy Enterprises

In African dairy systems â whether a 3-cow zero-grazing unit in Kiambu, a 40-head grazing herd in Zimbabwe's Mashonaland, or a 200-cow peri-urban farm supplying Nairobi or Addis Ababa â herd health is not a veterinary cost centre. It is a profit centre. Every case of clinical mastitis costs an East African farmer roughly USD 100â250 in lost milk, drugs, discarded milk, and replacement risk. Subclinical mastitis can quietly erase 10â20% of a herd's milk yield. Lameness removes cows from the milking string for weeks. Metabolic disorders around calving drive down fertility, which drives up calving interval, which drives down lifetime profitability.

This Practical Reference lesson is written for the farm manager, not the veterinarian. It gives you the operating protocols, decision thresholds, and record systems you need to run a health programme that protects margin. It assumes the constraints of African dairy: limited veterinary density (often 1 vet per 50,000+ livestock units in rural areas), cold-chain gaps, counterfeit or expired drugs in informal markets, and seasonal feed shortages that trigger metabolic disease.

1. Biosecurity: The Cheapest Medicine You Will Ever Buy

Biosecurity is the set of practices that stops disease entering and spreading. On African farms, the two biggest entry routes are purchased animals and visitors/vehicles. A newly bought heifer can bring brucellosis, bovine tuberculosis, or IBR into a clean herd. A milk tanker or AI technician's boot can carry mastitis pathogens between farms.

Minimum viable biosecurity for a commercial African dairy:

  • Quarantine every incoming animal for 30 days at least 50 m from the main herd, with separate equipment. Test for brucellosis and TB before purchase where labs are accessible.
  • Vehicle and foot dip at the gate. A 2% sodium hypochlorite or 1% Virkon footbath changed every 2â3 days (more in rain) costs almost nothing.
  • Visitor log and boot policy â no farm boots leave the farm, no street shoes enter the animal area.
  • All-in/all-out for calves where possible; if continuous calving, individual calf pens with clean bedding.
  • Rodent and tick control â rodents spread Salmonella and Leptospira; ticks spread ECF and heartwater.

2. The Three Profit-Killing Diseases: Mastitis, Lameness, Metabolic Disorders

Mastitis is the single most expensive disease in dairy. In African smallholder and commercial herds, Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus agalactiae, and environmental coliforms dominate. Control rests on five pillars: (1) post-milking teat dipping with an approved disinfectant; (2) pre-milking fore-stripping into a strip cup to detect flakes/clots; (3) dry cow therapy for every quarter at drying-off; (4) clean, dry bedding and no muddy lying areas; (5) milking order â healthy cows first, mastitis cows last, hospital cows never in the main parlour.

Lameness in African dairy is most often caused by foot rot (Fusobacterium necrophorum), digital dermatitis, white line disease from long standing on concrete, and laminitis from rumen acidosis. The economic hit is through reduced grazing time, lower milk yield, and poor fertility. A simple locomotion scoring system (1â5) done weekly by the herdsman catches 80% of cases before they become chronic.

Metabolic disorders â milk fever (hypocalcaemia), ketosis, and rumen acidosis â cluster around calving and early lactation, and are made worse by low-quality dry-season forage and sudden concentrate increases. Prevention is about the transition cow: controlled energy density, adequate calcium and magnesium, and gradual concentrate step-up over 2â3 weeks post-calving.

4. Parasite Control Strategies

Internal parasites (especially Haemonchus contortus â the barber's pole worm) and external parasites (ticks, tsetse, biting flies) are year-round threats. The strategic mistake on many African farms is calendar-based blanket drenching, which accelerates anthelmintic resistance. Move to targeted selective treatment: treat only animals with high FAMACHA scores (pale conjunctiva = anaemia), high faecal egg counts, or clinical signs. Rotate anthelmintic classes annually. For ticks, rotate acaricide classes every 6â12 months and dip/spray on a schedule matched to tick challenge, not habit.

5. Recognising Illness and When to Call the Vet

Train every stockman to check the four vital signs daily: appetite, rumination, temperature (normal 38.0â39.3Â°C), and milk yield versus the cow's own 7-day average. A drop of more than 10% in a cow's daily yield is a red flag worth investigating before clinical signs appear.

â  SAFETY WARNING â Zoonotic and Drug-Residue Risks

Several dairy diseases are zoonotic (transmissible to humans): brucellosis, bovine TB, anthrax, and rabies. Always wear gloves for calving assistance, handling aborted fetuses, and post-mortems. Aborted material must be buried or incinerated, never fed to dogs. Drink only pasteurised or boiled milk.

Drug residues: Never sell milk from a cow under antibiotic treatment, or during the labelled withdrawal period (typically 3â5 days for intramammary, longer for long-acting injectables). Residue-positive milk can cause human antibiotic resistance, fails processor tests, and can get your milk rejected â a direct financial loss. Record every treatment with date, drug, dose, and withdrawal end date.

6. Record Keeping for Health and Treatment

If it isn't written down, it didn't happen. A simple paper or phone-based herd health register should capture, per animal: date, sign observed, diagnosis, drug used (name, batch, expiry), dose, route, who treated, withdrawal end date, and outcome. Review the register monthly for three business metrics: mastitis incidence per 100 cows per month (target <5), lameness prevalence (target <10%), and calf mortality (target <5%). These three numbers tell you whether your health programme is working or bleeding money.
