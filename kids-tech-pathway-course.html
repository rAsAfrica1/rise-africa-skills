✅ Iron-Clad Fact Check

Every claim about Chinese schooling in this course is sourced and dated. Where we do not know something, we say so. Where the popular story is exaggerated, we correct it — including the exaggerations that would have made this course easier to sell.

Three things you were probably told that are wrong

"Chinese babies learn robotics from age two."
They do not. China's mandate begins at age six, with the start of primary school. Chinese kindergartens (ages 3–6) do play-based activities — sorting, sequencing, building — not coding or robotics. Any programme selling robotics to a two-year-old is selling to the parent, not teaching the child. This course starts at three, and what it asks a three-year-old to do is play a game with no screen in it.
"Every child in China is becoming an AI engineer."
The national requirement is at least eight hours per year — roughly one hour a month. Hangzhou requires ten. That is meaningful literacy, not engineer training. The children doing serious robotics are mostly in paid after-school programmes in wealthy cities. The gap between a Beijing school and a rural Chinese school is real, and Chinese education officials say so themselves.
"They give the kids ChatGPT and let them run."
The opposite. China's Ministry of Education prohibits primary-school pupils from independently using generative AI, and bars schools from setting homework that requires commercial AI tools. They also ruled out standardised AI testing until at least 2030. The approach is deliberately slow.
What is actually true, and verified:
  • From 1 September 2025, Beijing required every primary and secondary school to deliver at least 8 hours of AI instruction per year, starting at age six.
  • Hangzhou — home to DeepSeek and Unitree Robotics — set its own floor at 10 class hours per year.
  • By the 2026–27 academic year, all elementary and middle schools are required to offer introductory AI courses.
  • Teachers are being trained to match: computer science teachers must complete 120 hours of AI professional development by 2028.
  • Reported classroom progression in Beijing: third graders learn basics, fourth graders focus on data and coding, fifth graders move to algorithms and "intelligent agents".
Sources: Beijing Municipal Education Commission announcements (Mar 2025); South China Morning Post on Hangzhou (Aug 2025); NPR classroom reporting from Beijing (Jan 2026); Chinese Ministry of Education guidelines (May 2026). Verify current requirements yourself — policy in this area changes fast.

The real lesson for an African parent

The interesting thing about China's approach is not the equipment. It is the sequence: understand it, then control it, then build with it, then question it. That sequence costs nothing. A child who can explain why a face-unlock sometimes fails, and who can write a precise set of instructions for another person to follow, has more real AI literacy than a child who owns a robot kit and only presses the demo button.

So this course teaches the sequence first and the hardware last. Every module has a $0 version that works with a stick, some stones and a notebook, and an upgrade version for when there is money.

The six modules

MODULE 1  Ages 3–5: The robot game (no screens)$0

What the child will be able to do:

  • Give a clear instruction, one step at a time, and watch it be followed exactly
  • Notice when an instruction was wrong — the first taste of debugging
  • Sort objects by a rule they invented themselves (this is classification, which is what most AI actually does)
  • Repeat a pattern and predict what comes next
The core game: the adult becomes the robot. The child must get you across the room to pick up a cup, using only "step", "turn left", "turn right", "pick up". You follow exactly what they say, including the mistakes — if they forget a turn, you walk into the wall. Children find this hilarious, and it teaches precision faster than any app. That walk into the wall is the lesson.
Screen time at this age. The World Health Organization advises no screen time at all under two, and no more than one hour a day for ages two to four. This module has no screens in it on purpose. A child who plays the robot game at four will move faster at seven than a child who watched coding videos at four.

Show someone: a photograph of the chalk grid on the ground with the child's instruction cards laid beside it.

MODULE 2  Ages 6–8: Spot the AI (where China actually starts)$0 to phone

What the child will be able to do:

  • Point at five things in daily life that use AI and explain what each one is deciding
  • Understand that a machine "recognising" a face is comparing patterns, not seeing a person
  • Write an algorithm on paper for something they do every day — fetching water, making tea
  • Use ScratchJr on a shared phone to make a character follow their instructions
The AI hunt: walk to the shops. Count every AI the child can find — the phone that unlocks with a face, the keyboard that guesses the next word, the mobile-money app that flags a strange transaction, the music app that suggests a song. Chinese first-graders do exactly this: "everyday AI applications" is the official phrase for grades one to three. It costs nothing and it works.

Show someone: the child's hand-drawn "AI map" of their own street.

MODULE 3  Ages 9–11: Data, and why the machine gets it wrongPhone or shared PC

What the child will be able to do:

  • Build a working Scratch project with loops, conditions and variables
  • Train an actual image classifier in a browser and test where it fails
  • Explain in their own words why a model trained on few examples is confidently wrong
  • Collect and organise their own dataset — and see their choices show up in the result
The bias experiment: train a browser-based image classifier to tell maize from beans using twenty photos. Then test it on a photo taken in different light, or on a variety it has never seen. It fails, confidently. Ask the child why. This single exercise teaches more about AI's real limits than a term of theory — and it maps to what Beijing fourth-graders do under "data and coding".
Keep the generative AI away for now. China's own ministry restricts primary pupils from using generative AI independently. The reason is sound: a child who asks a chatbot for answers before learning to reason will not learn to reason. Let them build and break small models instead of consuming a large one.

Show someone: a screen recording of their classifier working, then failing, with the child explaining why.

MODULE 4  Ages 11–13: Making a real thing move$20–35 board

What the child will be able to do:

  • Write code that reads a sensor and acts on it — light, temperature, movement, a button
  • Move from blocks to typed code, usually Python, without it feeling like a cliff
  • Build one device that solves a problem in their own home
  • Debug hardware: check power, check connection, check code, in that order
Build something the house needs: a water-tank level alarm, a door sensor, a temperature logger for a chicken run, a load-shedding light that comes on by itself. A device that solves a real household problem gets used, and a child whose invention gets used keeps going. A demo gets photographed once and forgotten.
Battery safety. Lithium batteries burn fiercely and cannot be put out with water. Never charge unattended, never charge on a bed or sofa, never use a swollen or punctured cell, and never let a child improvise a charger from another device.

Show someone: the working device installed and doing its job, with the code printed beside it.

MODULE 5  Ages 12–14: Robots and drones that decide for themselves$80–200 or shared kit

What the child will be able to do:

  • Program a robot to follow a line, avoid an obstacle, and recover when it loses the line
  • Program a flight path rather than fly by hand — the actual skill
  • Understand the sense–decide–act loop that every autonomous machine runs
  • Enter a competition, or run one at their own school
Why competitions matter in the Chinese model: the serious skill in China is built in robotics competitions, not in the eight mandated hours. A deadline, a fixed challenge and other teams watching produce more learning than any syllabus. You do not need an international event — a line-following race between four schools in your district does the same job.
Drone law is real law. Rules differ by country and are enforced. In Zimbabwe, drones must be registered with the Civil Aviation Authority and flying without authorisation carries penalties. Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana and Uganda all have their own registration regimes. Check your national civil aviation authority before you buy, not after. Never fly near an airport, over a crowd, or over someone's property without permission.

Show someone: video of the robot completing the course, including one run where it recovers from a mistake.

MODULE 6  Ages 12–14: Asking the hard questions, and teaching someone younger$0

What the child will be able to do:

  • Argue both sides of a real AI question — whose data, whose job, whose mistake
  • Explain what a model cannot know, and why confident output is not the same as correct output
  • Recognise when a system's error falls hardest on people who look like them
  • Teach Module 1 or 2 to a younger child, which is the real test of understanding
The capstone: the learner runs a one-hour session for at least three younger children, using the robot game and the AI hunt. They plan it, run it, and write down what confused the little ones. Teaching exposes every gap in your own understanding — and it is how a village gets a second generation of learners without a second course fee.
An honest word on ethics teaching. China teaches AI ethics as part of this, but within a state-defined frame, alongside political instruction. We are not copying that part. The questions here are open ones, and a child is allowed to reach a conclusion you disagree with. That is the point of teaching thinking rather than answers.

Show someone: photographs of the session they ran, and their written notes on what to do differently next time.

What we do not promise

This course will not turn a child into an AI engineer, and neither will China's eight hours a year. What it does is remove the gap in starting point. A child in Chitungwiza with a chalk grid and an adult willing to play robot is not behind a child in Beijing at age six — they are doing the same thing. The gap opens later, at the equipment stage, and it opens most for families who never knew the first stage was free.

We also cannot promise your child access to a computer, reliable power, or a teacher who knows this material. Those are real constraints and we are not going to pretend a course fixes them.
