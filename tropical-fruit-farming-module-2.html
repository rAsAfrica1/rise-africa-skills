1. Growth Habits and Life Cycles of Tropical Fruit Trees

Tropical fruit trees grow in different ways. Some, like mango and avocado, grow tall and upright. Others, like guava and citrus, spread wide. Knowing a tree's natural shape helps you prune and space it correctly. For example, a mango tree can reach 15 meters if not pruned, but in an orchard you keep it at 4-5 meters for easy harvesting. Trees also have life cycles: juvenile (young, not fruiting), mature (fruiting), and senescent (old, declining). Most grafted trees start fruiting in 3-5 years, while seedling trees take 7-10 years. In Africa, many farmers grow seedlings, but grafted trees are better because they fruit earlier and are true to type.

Growth habits also determine how you train the tree. Central leader trees (like avocado) have one main trunk. Open center trees (like citrus) have a bowl shape. You prune to allow sunlight into the canopy, which improves fruit quality. For example, remove water sprouts (fast-growing vertical shoots) that block light. In the tropics, trees may grow year-round if water is available. But many have flowering triggered by dry spells. Mango, for instance, flowers after a dry period. If you irrigate too much, you get leaves instead of flowers.

Life cycles vary: some trees are evergreen (mango, citrus) and keep leaves all year. Others are deciduous (some annonas) and drop leaves before flowering. In Africa, erratic rainfall can confuse trees. You can manage this by withholding water for 2-3 months before the expected flowering season. Also, apply mulch to conserve moisture.

Knowing these habits helps you plan your orchard. Space trees according to their mature size. Prune to shape and remove dead wood. Feed with compost or manure to support growth. Remember, a healthy tree lives 30-50 years, so invest in proper care from the start.

2. Sexual Propagation: Seed Selection and Germination

Sexual propagation means growing trees from seeds. It is cheap and easy, but the offspring may not be exactly like the parent. For example, a mango seed may produce fruit that is different in taste or size. However, for some trees like papaya, seeds are the main method because they grow true to type. To select good seeds, choose from healthy, high-yielding mother trees. Pick fully ripe fruit. For mango, select seeds from fruit that is fully mature but not overripe. Remove the flesh, wash the seed, and dry it in the shade for 1-2 days. Do not dry in direct sun, as it kills the embryo.

Germination requires the right conditions: moisture, warmth, and air. Most tropical seeds germinate best at 25-30C. You can sow in a nursery bed or in polybags. Use a light soil mix: 1 part sand, 1 part compost, 1 part topsoil. Sow seeds 2-3 cm deep. Water daily but do not soak. For mango, the seed is polyembryonic (multiple seedlings from one seed), so you can get several plants. For avocado, the seed is large and germinates in 4-6 weeks. Keep the nursery shaded for the first few weeks, then gradually expose to sun.

Some seeds have hard coats and need scarification. For example, tamarind seeds can be soaked in warm water for 24 hours or nicked with a file. After germination, transplant seedlings when they have 4-6 leaves. Harden them off by reducing water and increasing sun. In Africa, many farmers use seeds because they are free, but remember: seedling trees take longer to fruit and may be thorny or have poor fruit quality. Use seeds for rootstocks or for trees like papaya that grow true. Otherwise, grafting is better.

3. Asexual Propagation: Grafting Techniques

Grafting joins a scion (the desired variety) to a rootstock (the base tree). This produces a tree that is identical to the parent and often fruits earlier. The best time to graft is when the rootstock is actively growing and the scion is dormant. In the tropics, this can be any time with adequate water, but avoid heavy rains. You need a sharp grafting knife, grafting tape, and clean cuts.

One common method is cleft grafting. Choose a rootstock with a trunk diameter of 1-2 cm. Cut it off horizontally. Make a vertical slit 3-4 cm deep in the center. Take a scion with 2-3 buds, cut the base into a wedge shape. Insert the wedge into the slit so the cambium layers (the green layer under the bark) match on at least one side. Wrap tightly with grafting tape. Cover with a plastic bag to keep humidity high. In 2-3 weeks, the scion should start growing. Remove the bag gradually.

Another method is side grafting, used when the rootstock is larger. Make a diagonal cut into the side of the rootstock. Cut the scion into a matching wedge and insert it. Wrap and wait. For mango, cleft grafting is common. For avocado, side grafting works well. Always use clean tools and sterilize with alcohol to prevent disease. After grafting, remove any shoots from the rootstock below the graft. This directs energy to the scion.

Grafting requires practice. Start with easy trees like citrus. In Africa, many nurseries sell grafted trees, but you can do it yourself. Keep records of what you grafted and when. With skill, you can produce hundreds of trees from one mother tree.

4. Asexual Propagation: Budding and Cutting Methods

Budding is a form of grafting where a single bud is inserted into the rootstock. It is faster and uses less scion wood. The most common is T-budding. Make a T-shaped cut in the rootstock bark, about 20-30 cm from the ground. Take a bud from a mature scion (a leaf axil bud). Cut a shield-shaped piece of bark with the bud. Insert it under the bark flaps of the T. Wrap with budding tape, leaving the bud exposed. In 2-3 weeks, if the bud is green, it has taken. Cut off the rootstock above the bud to force it to grow.

Another method is patch budding, used for thick-barked trees like mango. Remove a rectangular patch of bark from the rootstock and replace it with a matching patch containing a bud. Wrap tightly. This is slower but has high success. Budding is best done when the sap is flowing, so the bark slips easily. In the tropics, this is during the rainy season.

Cuttings are pieces of stem that root to form new trees. Some tropical fruits root easily from cuttings, like guava, fig, and mulberry. Take cuttings from healthy, mature wood, 20-30 cm long and 1-2 cm thick. Cut the bottom at an angle and the top straight. Remove leaves. Dip the bottom in rooting hormone (or honey) and plant in a well-drained mix of sand and compost. Keep in a humid, shaded place. Roots form in 4-8 weeks.

For difficult-to-root trees like mango, cuttings rarely work. Instead, use grafting or budding. Always label your cuttings with the variety and date. Water regularly but avoid waterlogging. Once rooted, transplant to a larger pot or field.

5. Rootstock Selection and Management

The rootstock is the foundation of a grafted tree. It provides anchorage, absorbs water and nutrients, and can influence the scion's size, fruit quality, and disease resistance. Choosing the right rootstock is crucial. For mango, common rootstocks are local mango seedlings that are vigorous and adapted to local soils. For avocado, use seedlings of Mexican or Guatemalan races, depending on climate. For citrus, use rough lemon or sour orange for vigor, or trifoliate orange for dwarfing.

In Africa, select rootstocks that tolerate drought, poor soil, and pests. For example, in sandy soils, use rootstocks with deep taproots. In areas with Phytophthora root rot, use resistant rootstocks like 'Cleopatra' mandarin for citrus. Collect seeds from healthy, disease-free mother trees. Grow them in a nursery for 6-12 months until they reach pencil thickness (about 1 cm).

Manage rootstocks by regular watering, weeding, and pest control. Apply mulch to conserve moisture. Before grafting, ensure the rootstock is free of pests and diseases. After grafting, remove any suckers that grow from the rootstock below the graft. These suckers will compete with the scion. Also, shape the rootstock by removing lower leaves to make grafting easier.

If you plant a grafted tree, ensure the graft union is 15-20 cm above the soil to prevent scion rooting. If the scion roots, the rootstock's benefits are lost. In the field, manage rootstocks by not damaging the base with tools. Healthy rootstocks lead to healthy trees. Remember, a good rootstock can make the difference between a dying orchard and a productive one.

6. Practical: Propagating a Tropical Fruit Tree

In this practical, you will propagate a tropical fruit tree using grafting. We will use mango as an example. You need: a healthy mango seedling (rootstock) that is 6-12 months old and pencil-thick, a scion from a desired mango variety (e.g., Apple mango), a sharp grafting knife, grafting tape, and a plastic bag.

Step 1: Prepare the rootstock. Water it well the day before. Cut the stem horizontally at 20-30 cm from the soil, using a clean cut. Make a vertical slit 3-4 cm deep in the center of the cut surface.

Step 2: Prepare the scion. Take a scion with 2-3 buds from a mature branch of the desired variety. Cut the base into a wedge shape, about 3-4 cm long, matching the slit. Ensure the wedge is smooth and clean.

Step 3: Insert the scion into the slit so the cambium layers match on at least one side. Wrap tightly with grafting tape, covering the union and the slit. Then cover the scion with a plastic bag to maintain humidity.

Step 4: Place the grafted plant in a shaded area. Water lightly. After 2-3 weeks, check for new growth. If the scion is green and sprouting, the graft has taken. Gradually remove the plastic bag over a week.

Step 5: After 2-3 months, the grafted plant can be transplanted to the field. Choose a sunny spot with well-drained soil. Dig a hole 30x30x30 cm, mix compost with soil, and plant the tree at the same depth as in the nursery. Water immediately.

Remember to label your plant with the variety and date. With practice, you can propagate many trees. This skill saves money and preserves good varieties.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Propagating a Tropical Fruit Tree

This practical reference sits at the end of the Tropical Fruit Tree Biology and Propagation module and is built for one purpose: to help you produce healthy, sellable planting material at the lowest possible cost. In most African markets, a grafted mango, avocado or citrus seedling sells for 310 times the price of a wild seedling, and demand from commercial orchards, NGOs, government programmes and smallholders is rising every season. Mastering propagation turns a nursery into a business.

Below you will find the core workflow, two decision tables, a safety warning, a quick reference checklist and free official resources. Treat this as a bench manual, not a textbook.

3. Rootstock Selection and Management

Rootstock is the engine of the tree. In Africa, the most common commercial rootstocks are polyembryonic mango (e.g. 'Sabre', 'Peach'), rough lemon and sour orange for citrus, and 'Duke 7' or local avocado races for avocado. The rootstock controls drought tolerance, salinity tolerance, disease resistance (especially Phytophthora and Fusarium), and even tree size which determines how densely a farmer can plant and therefore how much they earn per hectare.

SAFETY WARNING Sap, thorns and chemicals

Mango, cashew and some fig species produce caustic sap that can burn skin and eyes. Always wear gloves, long sleeves and eye protection when collecting scion wood or grafting. Cashew nut shell liquid (CNSL) is a severe skin irritant never handle fresh cashew material with bare hands. Never store grafting knives open in a pocket; cut away from your body. If you use fungicide or hormone powder (e.g. IBA), follow the label, keep it out of reach of children, and never eat, drink or smoke while handling it. In case of sap in the eye, rinse with clean water for 15 minutes and seek medical help.

4. Practical Steps in the Nursery

Step 1 Mother tree selection. Mark trees that have produced heavy, consistent crops for at least three seasons, with fruit that buyers pay a premium for. Record yield, taste, fruit size, disease history and harvest date. This is your scion bank.

Step 2 Seed and rootstock raising. Use fresh seed. For mango, sow polyembryonic seed within 35 days of extraction. For citrus, sow within 12 weeks. Fill nursery bags with a mix of topsoil, sand and well-decomposed manure (roughly 3:1:1). Raise the bags on benches or on polythene sheets never directly on bare soil, where nematodes and termites attack roots.

Step 3 Grafting or budding. Graft when the rootstock stem is pencil-thick (about 0.81.2 cm) and the bark slips easily. For cleft grafting, match cambium layers on at least one side. Wrap tightly with grafting tape or clean polythene strips; this prevents water loss and holds the union. Keep the grafted plants in a shade house with 5070% shade for 23 weeks, then gradually increase light.

Step 4 Aftercare. Remove rootstock shoots (suckers) weekly for the first two months they steal resources from the scion. Water in the morning, not at night, to reduce fungal disease. Watch for aphids, mealybugs and termites. Apply a balanced foliar feed once new leaves harden.

Step 5 Hardening and grading. Two weeks before sale, move plants to full sun and reduce watering slightly. Grade by height and stem diameter, label with variety and rootstock, and bundle in tens. Buyers pay more for uniform, labelled, healthy stock.

5. Quick Reference Checklist

  • Select mother trees with 3+ seasons of high yield and good fruit quality.
  • Collect scion wood early morning; wrap in moist cloth, use within 2448 hours.
  • Use fresh seed; sow mango within 5 days, citrus within 14 days.
  • Raise rootstock to pencil thickness (0.81.2 cm) before grafting or budding.
  • Match cambium layers, wrap tightly, and keep grafts in 5070% shade for 23 weeks.
  • Remove rootstock suckers weekly for the first two months.
  • Harden plants in full sun 2 weeks before sale; grade, label and bundle.
  • Keep records: variety, rootstock, graft date, success rate, cost per seedling, sale price.

6. Business-First Notes for African Nurseries

A well-run nursery bed of 1,000 grafted mango seedlings can gross USD 1,5004,000 depending on country and season, with seed, bags, labour and tape costing a fraction of that. The margin comes from three things: success rate, uniformity and trust. Success rate is a skill you build with practice. Uniformity comes from using the same rootstock and the same mother tree. Trust comes from labelling honestly and delivering what you promise. NGOs, government subsidy programmes and commercial orchards all prefer to buy from a nursery that can supply 500 identical, healthy, correctly labelled trees on a fixed date. Build that reputation and you will not need to chase buyers they will come to you.
