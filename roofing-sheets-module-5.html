1. Power Tools: Shears, Nibblers, and Saws

For cutting and shaping roofing sheets, you need a few essential hand tools. The most common are tin snips (also called aviation snips). In Africa, you'll find them in three types: straight-cut (yellow handles), left-cut (red handles), and right-cut (green handles). For straight cuts, use straight snips. For curves, use left or right snips depending on the direction. A good pair costs about 5,000 to 15,000 Naira (or equivalent) and will last years if kept dry. Always wear glovesâcut edges are razor-sharp.

For shaping, a hand seamer (or hand brake) is useful for bending edges. You can also use a mallet and a wooden block to bend sheets without stretching the metal. A claw hammer is too hard and will dent the sheet. For cutting long straight lines, a utility knife with a carbide blade can score and snap thin sheets (up to 0.5mm). But for thicker sheets (0.7mm and above), snips are better.

A hacksaw with a fine-tooth blade (24 or 32 teeth per inch) works for cutting small sections or pipes, but it's slow for large sheets. A nibbler is a hand tool that punches out small bitesâuseful for cutting curves in corrugated sheets. In many African markets, you'll find Chinese-made snips that are affordable but may need sharpening often. Learn to sharpen them with a file.

When cutting, always support the sheet on both sides of the cut to prevent bending. Mark your cut line with a marker or chalk. For corrugated sheets, cut along the flat pan, not across the ridges, to keep strength. After cutting, file the edges with a smooth file to remove burrs. Store snips with a drop of oil on the pivot. With practice, you can cut a 3-meter sheet in under a minute. Remember: never cut towards your body, and keep your free hand away from the blade path. A simple bench shear bolted to a table makes repetitive cuts faster and safer.

2. Fastening Tools and Techniques

Power tools speed up roofing work, but they need care and the right blade. The most common are electric shears (also called power snips). These look like a drill with a cutting head. They cut up to 1.5mm mild steel and leave a clean edge. A good brand like Makita or Bosch costs 50,000 to 150,000 Naira. Cheaper ones from China are 20,000 to 40,000 Naira but may overheat. Always use a residual current device (RCD) or earth leakage to prevent shocks, especially on wet roofs.

Nibblers are like a sewing machine for metalâthey punch out small crescent-shaped chips. They cut curves and straight lines without distorting the sheet. A nibbler is ideal for cutting holes for vents or pipes. They are slower than shears but more versatile. You can get pneumatic nibblers (run by an air compressor) or electric ones. For site work, electric is easier. Keep the punch and die lubricated with a few drops of oil every 10 minutes of use.

Circular saws with a metal-cutting blade (carbide-tipped or abrasive) can cut sheets fast, but they produce hot sparks and a rough edge. They are best for cutting many sheets at once on the ground. Always wear goggles and a dust maskâmetal dust is harmful. A jigsaw with a metal blade is good for curved cuts in thin sheets. For corrugated sheets, use a saw with a fine-tooth blade and cut slowly to avoid chipping the coating.

In Africa, power supply can be unreliable. Consider a cordless shear (18V) for small jobs. Always check the blade sharpnessâdull blades burn the metal and slow you down. Never force the tool; let the motor do the work. After use, blow out dust and wipe with an oily rag. Store in a dry box. Remember: power tools are dangerousâkeep both hands on the tool, never on the sheet near the blade. Use clamps to hold the sheet steady.

3. Safety Equipment and Fall Protection

Fastening roofing sheets means fixing them to purlins (wood or metal) so they don't leak or blow off. The main tools are: a screw gun (or drill with a screwdriver bit), a hammer, a rivet gun, and a socket set for bolts. For most metal roofs, you'll use self-drilling screws with rubber washers (also called roofing screws). These have a drill point that makes its own hole, then a thread that grips. You need a screw gun with adjustable torque so you don't overdrive and crush the washer. A good cordless screw gun costs 30,000 to 80,000 Naira.

For wooden purlins, use screws with a coarse thread. For steel purlins, use fine-thread screws. The screw length should be at least 50mm to go through the sheet and into the purlin by 25mm. Drive screws perpendicular to the sheetâif you angle them, the washer won't seal. The rubber washer should sit flat and slightly compressed, not squashed flat. A common mistake is over-tightening, which splits the washer and causes leaks. Practice on a scrap piece.

For corrugated sheets, fix at every second ridge (crest) along the purlin. Space screws 300mm apart along the sheet edge and 450mm in the middle. For flat sheets, use rivets or bolts. A hand rivet gun (pop rivet tool) costs 5,000 to 15,000 Naira and uses 4mm or 5mm aluminium rivets. Rivets are fast but not as strong as screws. For heavy-duty, use bolts with neoprene washers and nuts. You'll need a spanner or socket wrench.

In Africa, many roofers use a hammer and roofing nails for corrugated sheets. Nails are cheaper but can work loose and leak. If you use nails, use umbrella-head galvanised nails with a rubber washer. Drive them straight, not at an angle. For fastening sheets to each other (side laps), use stitch screws (short self-drilling screws) or rivets every 300mm. Always use a magnetic bit holder to keep screws from falling. After fastening, check for any screws that missed the purlinâremove and replace. A good fastening job will last 20 years.

4. Lifting and Handling Equipment

Roofing is one of the most dangerous jobs. Falls are the number one killer. In Africa, many roofers work without protection, but you don't have to be one of them. Basic safety equipment includes: a hard hat, safety glasses, gloves, non-slip boots, and a dust mask. For fall protection, you need a full-body harness, a lanyard, and an anchor point. A good harness costs 30,000 to 80,000 Naira. It's worth every cent. Anchor points must be strongâa purlin or a dedicated roof anchor. Never tie off to a pipe or a vent.

Before you climb, inspect your harness for cuts, fraying, or rust. Check the lanyard and carabiners. Use a shock-absorbing lanyard so if you fall, the force is reduced. For roofs with a slope greater than 30 degrees, you need a rope grab or a lifeline. On flat roofs, use a guardrail system if possible. In many African cities, you can hire harnesses from safety equipment shops. If you can't afford one, at least use a safety line tied to a strong point and a rope around your waistâbut this is not ideal.

Other safety gear: goggles for cutting (metal chips fly), earplugs for power tools (noise above 85dB), and a respirator when cutting fibreglass or asbestos sheets (asbestos is deadlyânever cut it dry). For hot work (soldering, grinding), have a fire extinguisher nearby. Keep the roof cleanâremove offcuts and tools that can trip you. Use a tool belt so your hands are free. Never work alone on a roof; have someone on the ground.

In hot African sun, wear light-coloured long sleeves and a hat to prevent heat stroke. Drink water every 20 minutes. If you feel dizzy, get down. For ladders, use a 4:1 ratio (base 1m out for every 4m up) and tie it off. Scaffolding should be on firm ground with base plates. If you use a roof ladder, hook it over the ridge. Remember: no roof is worth your life. Plan your fall protection before you start. Many companies now require a safety plan. Even for a small job, take 10 minutes to set up your harness and anchor.

5. Maintenance of Roofing Tools

Roofing sheets are long, heavy, and awkward. A 3-meter sheet of 0.5mm steel weighs about 12kg, but a bundle of 10 sheets weighs 120kg. Lifting them by hand can hurt your back. Use the right equipment. The simplest is a sheet lifterâa tool with a hook and a handle that lets you carry one sheet on your shoulder. It costs 5,000 to 10,000 Naira. For bundles, use a hand truck (trolley) with a strap. A good hand truck costs 20,000 to 50,000 Naira and saves your back.

For lifting sheets to the roof, you have several options. On a low roof (up to 3m), two people can pass sheets up by handâone on the ground, one on a ladder. Use gloves. For higher roofs, use a rope and a pulley. Tie a clove hitch around the sheet, then hoist. A simple block and tackle (chain hoist) costs 30,000 to 100,000 Naira and can lift 500kg. If you have a vehicle, use a roof rack to transport sheets. Never let sheets overhang more than 1 meterâthey can bend or hit people.

In Africa, many roofers use a 'sheet skate'âa piece of wood with a rope that slides sheets up a ladder. It works but is risky. Better: use a ladder hoist (also called a material lift). These are manual winches that clamp to a ladder and lift up to 100kg. They cost 50,000 to 150,000 Naira. For very long sheets, two people carry them on their shoulders, walking in step. Keep the sheet level. Watch for power lines.

When stacking sheets on the ground, place them on timber bearers (50mm x 100mm) to keep them off the ground and prevent bending. Stack no more than 20 sheets high. For corrugated sheets, stack them nested (one inside another) to save space. When moving sheets on the roof, slide them, don't lift. Use a sheet hook to pull them into place. Always wear glovesâedges are sharp. If a sheet is too heavy, get help. Never try to lift a bundle alone. Back injuries can end your career. Use mechanical aids whenever possible.

6. Maintenance of Roofing Tools

Good tools last longer and work better. After every job, clean your tools. For cutting tools (snips, shears, nibblers), wipe off metal chips and sap. Apply a few drops of machine oil to the pivot and blades. For power tools, blow out dust with compressed air or a brush. Check the power cord for cuts. Replace worn carbon brushes every 100 hours of use. A dull blade is dangerousâit slips and cuts you. Sharpen snips with a fine file at the original angle (usually 30 degrees). For power shear blades, replace them when they nick or dull. A new set costs 10,000 to 30,000 Naira.

For screw guns, check the bit holder. Worn bits strip screw heads. Replace bits every 500 screws. Clean the chuck and apply a little grease. For hammer and rivet guns, oil the moving parts. For hand tools like hammers, check the handle for cracks. A loose head can fly off. Replace wooden handles if splintered. For measuring tapes, wipe with a dry clothâmoisture causes rust. For levels, check the bubble is still accurate by placing on a known level surface.

Store tools in a dry place. In humid African climates, rust is a big problem. Use a toolbox with a lid. Put silica gel packets or a piece of chalk to absorb moisture. For long-term storage, coat metal parts with grease or oil. Hang snips and shears by their handlesâdon't throw them in a bucket. For power tools, store in their cases. Remove batteries from cordless tools if not used for a month. Charge batteries every 3 months.

Make a maintenance schedule: daily (clean and oil), weekly (check cords and bits), monthly (sharpen or replace blades), yearly (professional service for power tools). Keep a logbook. If a tool is broken, fix it or throw it awayâdon't use a damaged tool. A cracked hammer handle or a frayed cord can kill you. Teach your apprentice to maintain tools. In Africa, where tools are expensive, good maintenance means you buy less often. A well-maintained pair of snips can last 5 years; a neglected one, 6 months. Spend 10 minutes at the end of each day. Your tools will thank you.

Cutting and Shaping: Practical Notes

Hand snips remain the backbone of roofing work in Africa because they are cheap, repairable and need no power. Use left-cut snips for clockwise curves and right-cut snips for counter-clockwise curves; never force a straight-cut snip through a curve, as this crimps the sheet edge and creates a water trap. For corrugated or IBR profiles, cut across the crown with a nibbler rather than snips to avoid deforming the profile.

Power shears and nibblers dramatically raise output on large contracts. A two-person crew using a nibbler can cut a 30 mÂ² roof in roughly half the time of a snip-only crew. The trade-off is cost: a mid-range nibbler consumes 500â700 W and costs more to repair than a pair of snips. Budget for blade or punch replacement every 200â400 metres of cut depending on sheet gauge.

Fastening Tools and Techniques

Self-drilling screws with EPDM washers are the dominant fixing in African roofing. The critical tool is a variable-speed drill with an adjustable clutch. Set the clutch low enough that the screw seats firmly without crushing the washer â over-torque is the single most common cause of leaks on new roofs. Drive screws perpendicular to the purlin; angled driving deforms the washer and voids the seal.

For flashings and ridge caps, rivets or stitch screws are preferred. Hand rivet guns are adequate for low volume; lazy-tong riveters reduce hand fatigue on larger jobs. Always match rivet material to sheet material to avoid galvanic corrosion â aluminium rivets on aluminium sheets, galvanised or stainless on steel.

Safety Equipment and Fall Protection

Falls are the leading cause of death in roofing work globally, and African construction sites are no exception. A full-body harness with a shock-absorbing lanyard, anchored to a rated point, is non-negotiable for any work above 2 metres. Harnesses must be inspected before every use: check webbing for cuts, UV fading and chemical damage; check stitching and D-rings for deformation.

Never work on a roof without a full-body harness anchored to a rated point, and never anchor to a single purlin or a sheet fixing. Purlins and sheet fixings are not rated anchor points and will pull out under fall load. In many African jurisdictions, including Kenya (DOSHS), Nigeria (FME) and South Africa (OHS Act), the employer is legally liable for providing fall protection. A fall from 3 metres onto a hard surface is frequently fatal; a fall onto a sheet roof often results in the worker passing through the sheet. Inspect harnesses before every shift and retire any harness that has arrested a fall.

Lifting and Handling Equipment

Long sheets (6â12 m) are awkward and dangerous to handle manually. Two workers carrying a 6 m sheet on a windy roof is a serious risk. Use a sheet lifter, gin wheel or hoist to raise sheets to roof level, and use sheet hooks or carriers for horizontal movement. Never lift sheets over live electrical lines or in wind above 20 km/h.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Match the tool to the sheet gauge and profile â snips for light curves, nibbler for profiles, shears for volume.
  2. Inspect all cutting edges before each shift; replace or sharpen dull blades immediately.
  3. Set drill clutch to avoid over-torque; drive screws perpendicular to purlins.
  4. Match rivet material to sheet material to prevent galvanic corrosion.
  5. Wear PPE: gloves, eye protection, hearing protection for power tools, and non-slip footwear.
  6. Use a full-body harness anchored to a rated point for any work above 2 metres.
  7. Use mechanical lifting for sheets over 4 metres; never lift in wind above 20 km/h.
  8. Log all tool and harness inspections; retire any harness that has arrested a fall.
