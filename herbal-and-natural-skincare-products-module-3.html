1. Cold Infusions and Decoctions

Cold infusions and decoctions are the simplest ways to pull medicine from plants. Use cold infusion for delicate parts: flowers, leaves, fresh herbs. Use decoction for tough parts: roots, bark, seeds, dried berries. In Africa, you already know these methods from making tea or boiling roots for medicine. Now we measure for skincare.

Cold infusion: Take 20g dried or 40g fresh plant material. Put in a clean glass jar. Add 250ml clean water (filtered or boiled and cooled). Cover. Leave at room temperature for 48 hours, or overnight. Strain through muslin cloth. Use within 2 days in the fridge. Example: hibiscus petals, lavender flowers, mint leaves. For a facial toner, mix 50ml cold infusion with 50ml witch hazel.

Decoction: Take 30g dried tough plant material. Put in a pot. Add 500ml water. Bring to a boil, then reduce heat and simmer for 2030 minutes. Cover with a lid to trap steam. Let it cool. Strain. This makes a stronger liquid. Use within 3 days in the fridge. Example: ginger root, licorice root, neem bark, cinnamon bark. For a skin wash, use 100ml decoction in 1 litre warm water.

Tools you need: kitchen scale, measuring jug, stainless steel or enamel pot (not aluminium), glass jars, muslin cloth or clean cotton cloth, funnel, airtight bottles. Always label with plant name and date. If you see mould, throw it away. For longer storage, freeze in ice cube trays and use one cube per session. In hot climates, keep everything cool and dark. These water-based extracts are gentle but spoil fast. Make small batches weekly. You can combine cold infusion and decoction: for example, a toner with hibiscus cold infusion and a small amount of ginger decoction for stimulation.

2. Oil Infusions: Solar and Heat Methods

Oil infusions capture fat-soluble plant compounds. They are the base for salves, balms, massage oils, and facial serums. In Africa, you have abundant oils: coconut, palm kernel, shea nut, baobab, moringa, sesame, groundnut. Choose a stable oil like coconut or shea for long shelf life. For solar infusion, use dried plant material only. Fresh herbs contain water and will cause mould. Dry your herbs in shade for 37 days until brittle.

Solar method: Fill a clean glass jar halfway with dried herb. Cover completely with oil, leaving 2cm oil above the herb. Stir to remove air bubbles. Seal and place in a sunny window or outdoors for 24 weeks. Shake daily. Strain through muslin, squeeze well. Bottle in dark glass. Label. Shelf life: 612 months if kept cool and dark. Example: 100g dried calendula flowers in 500ml coconut oil. For a skin balm, use 50ml infused oil with 10g beeswax.

Heat method (faster): Use a double boiler or a glass bowl over a pot of water. Put herb and oil in the bowl. Heat water to 6070C (not boiling). Keep for 24 hours, stirring occasionally. Strain. This method is faster but can degrade delicate compounds. Use for roots, barks, and harder herbs. Example: 50g dried neem leaves in 250ml sesame oil. Heat for 3 hours. Strain. Use within 6 months.

Important: Never use direct high heat. Never use aluminium. For solar infusion in very hot climates, place jar in partial shade to avoid overheating. Test for rancidity: smell the oil. If it smells like crayons or old nuts, discard. For a richer extract, repeat the process with fresh herb in the same oil (double infusion). Always label with plant, oil, and date. These oils are excellent for African skin: shea and baobab are non-comedogenic and healing.

3. Tinctures and Glycerites

Tinctures use alcohol to extract a wide range of plant compounds. Glycerites use vegetable glycerin for a sweet, alcohol-free option. Both are concentrated and last 13 years. In Africa, you can buy strong alcohol like vodka (40% or higher) or use local spirits like waragi, kachasu, or ogogoro if they are at least 40% alcohol. For glycerites, use food-grade vegetable glycerin.

Tincture method: Use a 1:5 ratio for dried herbs (1 part herb to 5 parts alcohol by weight). For fresh herbs, use 1:2. Example: 50g dried hibiscus in 250ml vodka. Put in a glass jar. Seal. Shake daily for 46 weeks. Strain through muslin. Squeeze. Bottle in dark glass with dropper. Dosage for skincare: add 510 drops to 30ml carrier oil or cream. For a facial toner, mix 10ml tincture with 90ml water. Shelf life: 23 years.

Glycerite method: Use a 1:4 ratio for dried herbs (1 part herb to 4 parts glycerin). For fresh herbs, 1:3. Example: 25g dried lavender in 100ml glycerin. Add 25ml distilled water to thin it (optional). Heat gently in a double boiler at 60C for 23 hours. Or cold macerate for 46 weeks, shaking daily. Strain. Bottle. Shelf life: 12 years. Glycerites are less potent than tinctures but gentler. Use 12 teaspoons in 100ml water for a toner.

Safety: Alcohol tinctures are not for internal use unless you know the plant. For skincare, they are applied diluted. Glycerites can be used directly on skin but may be sticky. Always label with plant, solvent, ratio, and date. Store in cool, dark place. In hot climates, keep in fridge to extend life. You can combine tincture and glycerite for a custom extract. Test on a small skin patch before full use.

4. Steam Distillation for Hydrosols

Steam distillation produces hydrosols (floral waters) and essential oils. Hydrosols are gentle, water-based, and perfect for toners, face mists, and creams. You can build a simple still at home using a large pot, a steamer basket or colander, a lid inverted, and a bowl to collect. Or buy a copper or stainless steel still. In Africa, you can use a pressure cooker with a copper tube if you are careful.

Basic method: Place 200g fresh or 100g dried plant material in the pot. Add water to just below the plant. Bring to a boil. Steam carries the plant oils. The steam hits a cold surface (lid with ice on top) and condenses. It drips into a bowl. Collect 200500ml hydrosol. Example: 200g fresh rose petals gives about 300ml rose water. For lavender, use 150g dried flowers. Distill for 12 hours. Do not boil dry. Keep the heat steady.

Hydrosols are delicate. Store in sterilised dark glass bottles in the fridge. Shelf life: 612 months if refrigerated. Without fridge, 23 months. Add 1% preservative (like Geogard or potassium sorbate) for longer shelf life if selling. Use hydrosol directly as a toner or mix with glycerin (10ml glycerin per 100ml hydrosol) for a moisturising mist. For a face mask, mix 2 tablespoons clay with 1 tablespoon hydrosol.

Essential oil: If you want essential oil, you need a separator funnel. The oil floats on top. Collect it separately. Yield is very low: 200g lavender gives about 12ml oil. Use essential oils at 12% in skincare. Hydrosols are safer for direct use. Always label with plant and date. In Africa, good plants for distillation: rose, lavender, rosemary, eucalyptus, lemongrass, mint, chamomile. Avoid distilling poisonous plants. Clean your still thoroughly after each use.

5. Making Herbal Extracts with Alcohol

Alcohol extracts (tinctures) are the most efficient for many herbs. They pull out resins, alkaloids, and volatile oils. For skincare, alcohol tinctures are used as preservatives, astringents, and active ingredients. You need strong alcohol: minimum 40% ABV (80 proof). Vodka is ideal. In Africa, you can use cane spirit, waragi, or even pure ethanol diluted to 4060% with distilled water. Never use denatured alcohol or methylated spirits on skin.

Method: Measure dried herb. Use a 1:5 ratio (1 part herb to 5 parts alcohol by weight). For fresh herb, 1:2. Example: 20g dried rosemary in 100ml vodka. Chop herb coarsely. Place in a glass jar. Pour alcohol over. Seal. Shake well. Store in a dark cupboard for 46 weeks. Shake daily. Strain through muslin or coffee filter. Squeeze the herb to get all liquid. Bottle in dark glass with dropper. Label with plant, alcohol percentage, and date. Shelf life: 23 years.

For a stronger extract, use 6070% alcohol for resins and barks. For delicate flowers, use 4050%. You can also make a double extraction: first alcohol, then water decoction, and combine. This is good for mushrooms and tough roots. Example: 30g dried neem bark in 150ml 60% alcohol. Macerate 6 weeks. Strain. Use 510 drops in 30ml carrier oil for acne-prone skin.

Safety: Alcohol tinctures are for external use unless you are trained. They can sting on broken skin. Always dilute: 1020 drops per 30ml of carrier oil or cream. For a facial toner, mix 10ml tincture with 90ml water or hydrosol. Store away from heat and light. In hot climates, keep in a cool cupboard. Do not use plastic bottles for long storage; glass is best. Test on a small area before using widely. Label everything. Keep out of reach of children.

6. Preserving Extracts for Longevity

Preservation stops mould, bacteria, and rancidity. Water-based extracts (infusions, decoctions, hydrosols) spoil fastest: 27 days in the fridge. Oil-based extracts last 612 months. Alcohol and glycerin extracts last 13 years. The key is to prevent water from getting into oils and to keep everything clean.

For water extracts: Refrigerate immediately. Freeze in ice cube trays for up to 3 months. Add preservatives if selling: 1% Geogard 221 or 0.5% potassium sorbate plus 0.5% sodium benzoate. For hydrosols, add 1% preservative and store in dark glass in the fridge. For tinctures and glycerites, no preservative needed if alcohol or glycerin content is high enough. Alcohol above 20% prevents microbial growth. Glycerin above 50% also preserves. Always use sterilised bottles: boil glass for 10 minutes or wash with hot soapy water and rinse with alcohol.

For oil infusions: Add 1% vitamin E oil (tocopherol) to prevent rancidity. Store in dark glass bottles in a cool, dark place. Avoid sunlight. If you see cloudiness or smell off, discard. For balms and salves, the beeswax and oil mixture lasts 612 months. Add 0.5% rosemary oleoresin for extra protection. In hot African climates, keep oils in a cool box or fridge if possible. Never mix water into oil without an emulsifier and preservative; it will grow mould in days.

General rules: Label everything with plant name, extraction method, date, and expiry. Use clean utensils. Work with dry hands. Keep a notebook. Make small batches. If you sell, follow your country's regulations for cosmetics. Test preservative efficacy if possible. For home use, the fridge is your best friend. When in doubt, throw it out. Mould can cause skin infections. Proper preservation means your hard work lasts and your customers stay safe.

<h3>Method 1: Cold Infusions and Decoctions</h3>
<p>Cold infusion suits heat-sensitive botanicals hibiscus petals, moringa leaf, baobab fruit pulp, and aloe. Steep 1 part dried herb in 10 parts cool, clean water for 812 hours, then strain through muslin. Decoction is for hard materials ginger root, cinnamon bark, licorice root, and African potato. Simmer 1 part herb in 16 parts water for 1545 minutes. In African contexts, decoctions are the traditional backbone of herbal medicine, but for skincare you must adjust: decoctions are water-based and therefore require preservation. Use them within 48 hours or add a preservative system (e.g., 1% phenoxyethanol or 0.5% sodium benzoate) for retail products.</p>

<p><strong>Business note:</strong> Water-based extracts are the cheapest to produce but have the shortest shelf life and lowest concentration. Use them for fresh masks, toners, and clay blends sold within a week, or as the aqueous phase in emulsions where you add preservatives.</p>

<h3>Method 2: Oil Infusions Solar and Heat Methods</h3>
<p>Oil infusions are the workhorse of African skincare. Solar infusion packing dried herb in a jar with a carrier oil (baobab, marula, shea olein, moringa, or sunflower) and leaving it in direct sunlight for 26 weeks is traditional and low-cost. Heat infusion uses a double boiler at 4060C for 24 hours. Solar infusion preserves delicate actives but risks rancidity in hot, humid climates. Heat infusion is faster but can degrade heat-sensitive compounds.</p>

<p>Critical quality control: use dried herb, not fresh, to avoid water content that causes mold. Aim for a 1:5 herb-to-oil ratio by weight for potent herbs (e.g., calendula, chamomile) and 1:3 for mild ones. After infusion, strain through muslin and add 0.51% vitamin E (tocopherol) as an antioxidant. In West Africa, shea butter is often infused with moringa or neem for skin healing; in East Africa, marula and baobab oils are infused with rose geranium and lavender.</p>

<h3>Method 3: Tinctures and Glycerites</h3>
<p>Tinctures use ethanol (typically 6095%) to extract a broad spectrum of compounds alkaloids, glycosides, resins, and volatile oils. Glycerites use vegetable glycerin, often mixed with water (glycerin is a poor solvent for resins and alkaloids alone). The standard ratio is 1:5 for dried herb in 60% ethanol, or 1:4 for fresh herb in 95% ethanol. Glycerites are typically 1:4 or 1:5 in a 60:40 glycerin-to-water mix.</p>

<p><strong>African business angle:</strong> Tinctures are the highest-value extract per litre a 100 ml tincture can retail for USD 815 in Nairobi or Lagos, versus USD 35 for a comparable oil infusion. But ethanol is expensive and regulated in some countries. Glycerites are alcohol-free, appeal to Muslim and conservative Christian markets, and are gentler on skin ideal for baby products and sensitive-skin lines. However, glycerites are less potent and require higher concentrations in formulations.</p>

<h3>Method 4: Steam Distillation for Hydrosols</h3>
<p>Steam distillation produces hydrosols (floral waters) and essential oils. For African producers, hydrosols are the more accessible entry point: a 20-litre copper still can process 510 kg of fresh rose geranium, lavender, or chamomile, yielding 38 litres of hydrosol. Hydrosols are water-based, so they need preservation (0.51% preservative) and should be stored in dark glass, away from heat.</p>

<p><strong>Market insight:</strong> Hydrosols retail at USD 512 per 100 ml in urban African markets and are popular as toners, facial mists, and linen sprays. They are also a gateway product for export European and North American buyers pay premium prices for single-origin, organic-certified African hydrosols. Invest in a stainless steel or copper still (USD 3001,500 depending on size) and learn to monitor distillation temperature and time to avoid off-notes.</p>

<h3>Method 5: Making Herbal Extracts with Alcohol</h3>
<p>Alcohol extraction is the most efficient method for capturing the full spectrum of plant actives. Use food-grade ethanol (not denatured) at 6095% concentration. For dried herbs, use 1:5 ratio; for fresh, 1:4. Macerate for 24 weeks in a cool, dark place, shaking daily. Strain through muslin, then filter through coffee filter or fine mesh. Store in amber glass bottles with dropper caps.</p>

<p><strong>Regulatory warning:</strong> In many African countries, high-proof ethanol requires a licence. Check with your national bureau of standards (e.g., KEBS in Kenya, SON in Nigeria, GSA in Ghana). Some markets (e.g., Saudi Arabia, parts of North Africa) prohibit alcohol-based products entirely glycerites are your alternative.</p>

<h3>Method 6: Preserving Extracts for Longevity</h3>
<p>Preservation determines whether you can sell wholesale, export, or hold inventory. Key principles:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Water-based extracts:</strong> 48 hours refrigerated, or 36 months with preservative (phenoxyethanol, sodium benzoate, potassium sorbate).</li>
<li><strong>Oil infusions:</strong> 612 months with vitamin E; 1224 months if stored cool and dark.</li>
<li><strong>Tinctures:</strong> 25 years if ethanol 60% and stored properly.</li>
<li><strong>Glycerites:</strong> 1224 months with 0.5% preservative.</li>
<li><strong>Hydrosols:</strong> 612 months with preservative, refrigerated after opening.</li>
</ul>
