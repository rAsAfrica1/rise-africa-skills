1. Lesson 1: Mapping Water Storage Demand and Choosing the Right Tank Type for Your Site

Before you buy a single bag of cement or order a plastic tank, you must answer one question: what is this water for, and how much do you need? In the African context, water storage is not a single product but a family of solutions. A 1,000-litre plastic tank in a Kenyan school yard, a 10,000-litre ferrocement tank on a Nigerian farm, a 20,000-litre concrete reservoir at a Ghanaian clinic, and a 5,000-litre brick tank at a South African homestead all serve different jobs. Your first task as a builder or entrepreneur is to match the tank to the demand, the budget, and the local materials. Start with a simple demand audit. Count the users, the livestock, the crops, and the business activities. A household of six in Kisumu using 20 litres per person per day needs 120 litres daily. Add laundry, cleaning, and a small kitchen garden and you are at 200 litres. A dry season of 90 days means 18,000 litres of storage if you want full independence. That is a 20,000-litre tank. A primary school with 300 pupils at 5 litres per day for drinking and handwashing needs 1,500 litres daily. With a 5-day buffer, that is 7,500 litres, so a 10,000-litre tank is the practical minimum. A small poultry farm with 500 birds at 0.3 litres per bird per day needs 150 litres daily, or 13,500 litres over 90 days. These numbers are not academic. They determine whether you build a 2,000-litre plastic tank for KES 18,000 to 25,000 in Nairobi, or a 10,000-litre ferrocement tank for KES 80,000 to 120,000 in materials and labour. In Nigeria, a 1,000-litre plastic tank costs NGN 120,000 to 180,000 in Lagos, while a 10,000-litre concrete tank can be built for NGN 450,000 to 700,000 depending on sand and cement prices. In Ghana, a 5,000-litre plastic tank runs GHS 3,500 to 5,500 in Accra, while a brick tank of the same size costs GHS 4,000 to 6,000 in materials. In South Africa, a 5,000-litre polyethylene tank is ZAR 6,000 to 9,000, and a 10,000-litre ferrocement tank can be built for ZAR 15,000 to 25,000. These are real ranges, but they move with transport, exchange rates, and local supply. Now consider the four main tank types. Plastic tanks are fast, clean, and portable. They are rotationally moulded polyethylene, UV-stabilised, and food-grade. They come in 100, 250, 500, 1,000, 2,500, 5,000, 10,000, and 20,000 litres. They are ideal for drinking water, small households, and emergency response. Their weaknesses are cost per litre at large sizes, vulnerability to UV degradation after 10 to 15 years, and difficulty repairing cracks. Concrete tanks are strong, long-lasting, and can be built in any size. They are cast in place using a mix of 1 part cement to 2 parts sand to 3 parts aggregate, with a water-cement ratio of 0.5. A 10,000-litre concrete tank needs about 3 cubic metres of concrete, which is roughly 30 bags of cement, 3 tonnes of sand, and 4 tonnes of aggregate. In Kenya, that is about KES 45,000 to 60,000 in materials. Concrete tanks are excellent for underground storage, fire reserves, and large institutions. They are heavy, require skilled labour, and can crack if not cured properly. Ferrocement tanks are the African workhorse. They use a thin cement mortar over a wire mesh and steel frame. A 10,000-litre ferrocement tank needs about 15 to 20 bags of cement, 1.5 tonnes of sand, 30 to 40 square metres of chicken wire or welded mesh, and 20 to 30 kilograms of steel reinforcement. In Nigeria, that is NGN 250,000 to 400,000 in materials. Ferrocement is ideal for rural areas because it uses local labour and can be built in 7 to 14 days. It is strong, repairable, and resistant to impact. It requires careful plastering and curing. Brick tanks are built with burnt clay bricks or concrete blocks, rendered inside and out. A 5,000-litre brick tank needs about 1,200 bricks, 10 bags of cement, and 1 tonne of sand. In Ghana, that is GHS 2,500 to 4,000 in materials. Brick tanks are good for underground or partially buried storage, and they blend with traditional construction. They are slower to build and can leak if the render is poor. Your choice depends on five factors: water quality, budget, lifespan, site access, and labour. For drinking water, plastic or well-rendered concrete is best. For irrigation, ferrocement or brick is cost-effective. For a business selling water, a 20,000-litre plastic tank on a raised stand gives you gravity flow and easy cleaning. For a farm, a 50,000-litre ferrocement tank can be built for KES 300,000 to 450,000 and will last 30 years. Always calculate cost per litre per year. A plastic tank at KES 20,000 for 1,000 litres lasting 10 years costs KES 2 per litre per year. A ferrocement tank at KES 100,000 for 10,000 litres lasting 25 years costs KES 0.40 per litre per year. That is the business case. Safety note: never site a tank near a pit latrine, septic tank, or animal pen. Keep at least 30 metres from any toilet and 15 metres from livestock. Ensure the tank base is level and compacted. A 10,000-litre tank weighs 10 tonnes when full, so the ground must bear that load. Use a reinforced concrete ring beam or a compacted murram base. For plastic tanks, use a sand or concrete pad. For underground tanks, ensure the water table is at least 1 metre below the tank floor. Finally, plan for overflow, drainage, and access. Every tank needs a first-flush diverter, a mosquito-proof screen, and a tap or pump. A 10,000-litre tank needs a 50mm overflow pipe and a 25mm outlet. If you are building for a business, add a water meter and a lockable tap. This lesson is the foundation. In the next four lessons, we will build each tank type step by step, with costs, materials, and safety.

2. Lesson 2: Ferrocement Tanks - The Affordable, Durable African Standard

Ferrocement is not a compromise. It is a high-performance composite of cement mortar and steel mesh that has been used in Africa for over 50 years. It is the best value for rural and peri-urban water storage because it uses local materials, local labour, and simple tools. A well-built ferrocement tank can last 30 to 50 years, resist earthquakes, and be repaired with a trowel and a bag of cement. This lesson gives you the full construction process for a 10,000-litre cylindrical ferrocement tank, with costs in KES, NGN, GHS, and ZAR. First, design. A 10,000-litre tank holds 10 cubic metres. A cylinder 2.5 metres in diameter and 2.1 metres high holds 10.3 cubic metres. Add 0.3 metres freeboard and the wall height is 2.4 metres. The wall thickness is 25 to 40 millimetres. The base is 75 to 100 millimetres thick. The tank sits on a reinforced concrete ring beam 200 millimetres wide and 150 millimetres deep. The ring beam prevents settlement and holds the wall. The base slab is 100 millimetres thick with a 1:2:4 mix and a single layer of welded mesh. The wall is built in three stages: the armature, the first plaster, and the second plaster. The armature is the skeleton. Use 6 millimetre mild steel bars for vertical ribs every 300 millimetres around the circumference. For a 2.5 metre diameter tank, the circumference is 7.85 metres, so you need 26 vertical ribs. Each rib is 2.4 metres long. Use 6 millimetre bars for two horizontal rings at the top and bottom, and one at mid-height. Tie all intersections with 1.6 millimetre galvanised wire. Then wrap the outside with chicken wire, 25 millimetre mesh, in two layers, and the inside with one layer. Overlap the wire by 100 millimetres and tie it tightly. The wire must be clean and free of oil. The next step is the first plaster. Mix cement and sand at 1:2 by volume. Add water until the mortar is workable but not runny. A 50 kilogram bag of cement makes about 0.035 cubic metres of mortar. For a 10,000-litre tank, you need about 0.8 cubic metres of mortar for the walls, which is 23 bags of cement. Add 10 percent for waste, so 25 bags. Apply the first coat from the inside, pushing the mortar through the wire. The coat should be 10 to 15 millimetres thick. Cure it for 24 hours by covering with plastic or wet sacks. The second plaster is applied from the outside, 10 to 15 millimetres thick, and then a third coat from the inside to reach 25 to 40 millimetres total. The final coat is a rich mix of 1:1 cement and sand, with a waterproofing admixture if available. Trowel it smooth. Cure the tank for 7 days, keeping it wet. Then fill it with water and let it stand for 7 days. If the water level drops, find the leak and patch it with a 1:1 mortar. Now costs. In Kenya, a 10,000-litre ferrocement tank needs 25 bags of cement at KES 700 each, which is KES 17,500. Sand: 2 tonnes at KES 2,500 per tonne, KES 5,000. Ballast for the base: 1 tonne at KES 2,500. Steel bars: 60 kilograms at KES 120 per kilogram, KES 7,200. Chicken wire: 40 square metres at KES 200 per square metre, KES 8,000. Tie wire: 5 kilograms at KES 200 per kilogram, KES 1,000. Waterproofing admixture: KES 2,000. Labour: 2 masons for 10 days at KES 1,500 per day, KES 30,000. Total: KES 73,200. Add 15 percent contingency: KES 84,000. In Nigeria, the same tank costs NGN 25 bags of cement at NGN 8,000 each, NGN 200,000. Sand: 2 tonnes at NGN 25,000 per tonne, NGN 50,000. Ballast: NGN 25,000. Steel: 60 kilograms at NGN 1,200 per kilogram, NGN 72,000. Chicken wire: 40 square metres at NGN 1,500 per square metre, NGN 60,000. Tie wire: NGN 5,000. Admixture: NGN 15,000. Labour: 2 masons for 10 days at NGN 10,000 per day, NGN 200,000. Total: NGN 627,000. Add 15 percent: NGN 721,000. In Ghana, cement is GHS 60 per bag, so 25 bags is GHS 1,500. Sand: GHS 400 per tonne, so GHS 800. Ballast: GHS 400. Steel: GHS 12 per kilogram, so GHS 720. Chicken wire: GHS 20 per square metre, so GHS 800. Tie wire: GHS 50. Admixture: GHS 150. Labour: 2 masons for 10 days at GHS 150 per day, GHS 3,000. Total: GHS 7,420. Add 15 percent: GHS 8,533. In South Africa, cement is ZAR 120 per bag, so 25 bags is ZAR 3,000. Sand: ZAR 400 per tonne, so ZAR 800. Ballast: ZAR 400. Steel: ZAR 20 per kilogram, so ZAR 1,200. Chicken wire: ZAR 30 per square metre, so ZAR 1,200. Tie wire: ZAR 100. Admixture: ZAR 300. Labour: 2 masons for 10 days at ZAR 400 per day, ZAR 8,000. Total: ZAR 15,000. Add 15 percent: ZAR 17,250. These are real numbers for 2024. They show that ferrocement is the cheapest per litre for large tanks in most African countries. Now safety. Always wear gloves, dust masks, and eye protection when mixing cement. Cement burns skin. Keep children away from the site. Do not build a tank on a slope without a retaining wall. Do not use dirty water for mixing. Do not skip curing. A tank that is not cured will crack and leak. Test the tank with water before you connect it to the supply. If you are building for a business, add a roof to keep out leaves and mosquitoes. A simple corrugated iron roof on a timber frame costs KES 8,000 to 12,000. Add a first-flush diverter made from a 100 millimetre PVC pipe and a ball valve. This diverts the first 20 litres of rain, which carries dirt from the roof. Add a mosquito screen on the overflow and the inlet. For a farm, you can build a 50,000-litre ferrocement tank for about 5 times the materials and 3 times the labour. That is KES 350,000 to 450,000 in Kenya. It will store enough water for 2 acres of drip irrigation for 30 days. Ferrocement is not just a tank. It is a business. You can train a team of 4 workers and build 2 tanks per month. At a 30 percent profit margin, that is KES 50,000 per month in Kenya, NGN 400,000 in Nigeria, GHS 5,000 in Ghana, or ZAR 10,000 in South Africa. Start with your own tank. Then build for neighbours. Then build for schools and clinics. The demand is everywhere.

3. Lesson 3: Concrete Tanks - Underground and Above-Ground Reservoirs for Institutions and Businesses

Concrete tanks are the heavyweights of water storage. They are strong, fireproof, and can be built underground, partially buried, or above ground. They are the best choice for schools, clinics, hotels, factories, and large farms because they can be designed by an engineer and built to last 50 years or more. This lesson covers the design, materials, and construction of a 20,000-litre reinforced concrete tank, with costs in KES, NGN, GHS, and ZAR. First, decide the type. An underground tank saves space and keeps water cool, but it needs a high water table check and a pump. An above-ground tank needs a strong base and a roof. A partially buried tank is a compromise: half underground, half above, with an earth berm for insulation. For a 20,000-litre tank, the internal dimensions can be 3 metres long, 2.5 metres wide, and 2.7 metres high. That is 20.25 cubic metres. Add 0.3 metres freeboard, so the wall height is 3 metres. The walls are 200 millimetres thick, reinforced with 12 millimetre bars at 200 millimetres centre to centre, both horizontal and vertical. The base is 200 millimetres thick, reinforced with 12 millimetre bars at 200 millimetres in both directions. The roof is 150 millimetres thick, reinforced with 10 millimetre bars at 200 millimetres, with a 600 millimetre by 600 millimetre access hatch. The concrete mix is 1:2:3 with a water-cement ratio of 0.5. For 20 cubic metres of concrete, you need about 6 cubic metres of concrete for the walls, base, and roof. That is 60 bags of cement, 6 tonnes of sand, and 9 tonnes of aggregate. In Kenya, that is KES 42,000 for cement, KES 15,000 for sand, and KES 22,500 for aggregate. Steel: 500 kilograms at KES 120 per kilogram, KES 60,000. Formwork: timber and plywood, KES 20,000. Labour: 4 masons and 6 helpers for 21 days. Masons at KES 1,500 per day, helpers at KES 800 per day. Total labour: KES 226,800. Total: KES 386,300. Add 15 percent: KES 444,000. In Nigeria, cement at NGN 8,000 per bag, 60 bags is NGN 480,000. Sand: 6 tonnes at NGN 25,000, NGN 150,000. Aggregate: 9 tonnes at NGN 30,000, NGN 270,000. Steel: 500 kilograms at NGN 1,200, NGN 600,000. Formwork: NGN 150,000. Labour: 4 masons at NGN 10,000 per day and 6 helpers at NGN 5,000 per day for 21 days, NGN 1,470,000. Total: NGN 3,120,000. Add 15 percent: NGN 3,588,000. In Ghana, cement at GHS 60 per bag, 60 bags is GHS 3,600. Sand: 6 tonnes at GHS 400, GHS 2,400. Aggregate: 9 tonnes at GHS 500, GHS 4,500. Steel: 500 kilograms at GHS 12, GHS 6,000. Formwork: GHS 1,500. Labour: 4 masons at GHS 150 per day and 6 helpers at GHS 80 per day for 21 days, GHS 22,680. Total: GHS 40,680. Add 15 percent: GHS 46,782. In South Africa, cement at ZAR 120 per bag, 60 bags is ZAR 7,200. Sand: 6 tonnes at ZAR 400, ZAR 2,400. Aggregate: 9 tonnes at ZAR 500, ZAR 4,500. Steel: 500 kilograms at ZAR 20, ZAR 10,000. Formwork: ZAR 3,000. Labour: 4 masons at ZAR 400 per day and 6 helpers at ZAR 200 per day for 21 days, ZAR 58,800. Total: ZAR 85,900. Add 15 percent: ZAR 98,785. These costs are for a tank built by a contractor. If you are the contractor, your profit is in the labour and management. A 20,000-litre concrete tank can be sold for KES 550,000 to 650,000 in Kenya, NGN 4.5 million to 5.5 million in Nigeria, GHS 60,000 to 75,000 in Ghana, and ZAR 130,000 to 160,000 in South Africa. That is a 25 to 35 percent margin. Now construction steps. First, excavate. For an underground tank, excavate 3.5 metres by 3 metres by 3.5 metres deep. Keep the soil for backfilling. Compact the bottom with a plate compactor. Pour a 50 millimetre blinding layer of lean concrete. Then set the base reinforcement on 50 millimetre cover blocks. Pour the base in one continuous pour. Vibrate the concrete with a poker vibrator to remove air pockets. Cure for 7 days. Then build the wall formwork. Use steel or timber forms. Tie the reinforcement to the base starter bars. Pour the walls in 300 millimetre lifts, vibrating each lift. Do not pour more than 1 metre per day. Cure the walls for 7 days. Then build the roof formwork and reinforcement. Pour the roof. Cure for 14 days. Then apply a waterproof render inside: 1:2 cement-sand with a waterproofing admixture, 20 millimetres thick. Trowel it smooth. Then fill the tank with water and test for 7 days. If the water level drops, drain and patch. Safety is critical. Concrete work is heavy. Use gloves, boots, and hard hats. Do not stand under a suspended load. Use proper scaffolding, not makeshift planks. Vibrators are electrical: use a residual current device. Never enter a tank without ventilation. Concrete curing releases carbon dioxide, which can displace oxygen. Always have a second person outside. For underground tanks, check for gas before entry. Now the business case. A concrete tank is not just for storage. It is an asset. A school with a 20,000-litre tank can sell water to the community during dry season at KES 5 per 20 litres. That is KES 5,000 per day if they sell 20,000 litres. A hotel can use it for laundry and cleaning, saving KES 10,000 per month on water bills. A farm can use it for drip irrigation, growing tomatoes for KES 50,000 per season. The tank pays for itself in 2 to 3 years. For a contractor, the key is to standardise. Build a reusable steel formwork set for a 20,000-litre tank. That costs KES 150,000 but can be used 50 times. Then you can build a tank in 14 days with 4 workers. Your cost drops to KES 300,000, and you sell for KES 550,000. That is a KES 250,000 profit per tank. In Nigeria, the same formwork costs NGN 1.2 million and you sell for NGN 4.5 million. In Ghana, GHS 20,000 formwork and GHS 60,000 sale. In South Africa, ZAR 40,000 formwork and ZAR 130,000 sale. This is a real business. Start with one tank for a local school. Use it as a demonstration. Then bid for government tenders. Counties and municipalities in Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana, and South Africa all have budgets for water projects. A concrete tank is a visible, measurable, and durable investment. That is what they want.

4. Lesson 4: Plastic Tanks - Fast, Clean, and Profitable for Households and Small Businesses

Plastic tanks are the fastest way to store water. You can buy one today, install it tomorrow, and drink from it the same week. They are made from food-grade polyethylene, rotationally moulded, and UV-stabilised. They come in sizes from 100 litres to 20,000 litres. They are the best choice for households, small shops, poultry farms, and emergency water supply. This lesson covers selection, installation, maintenance, and the business of selling and installing plastic tanks in Africa. First, understand the product. A 1,000-litre plastic tank in Nairobi costs KES 18,000 to 25,000. A 2,500-litre tank costs KES 35,000 to 50,000. A 5,000-litre tank costs KES 60,000 to 85,000. A 10,000-litre tank costs KES 110,000 to 150,000. In Lagos, a 1,000-litre tank is NGN 120,000 to 180,000. A 5,000-litre tank is NGN 450,000 to 600,000. A 10,000-litre tank is NGN 800,000 to 1,100,000. In Accra, a 1,000-litre tank is GHS 1,200 to 1,800. A 5,000-litre tank is GHS 3,500 to 5,500. A 10,000-litre tank is GHS 6,500 to 9,000. In Johannesburg, a 1,000-litre tank is ZAR 2,500 to 3,500. A 5,000-litre tank is ZAR 6,000 to 9,000. A 10,000-litre tank is ZAR 12,000 to 18,000. These prices include the tank, a lid, and a 25 millimetre outlet. They do not include delivery, a base, or installation. Delivery can add 10 to 20 percent. A concrete or sand base adds KES 5,000 to 10,000. Installation with a tap and a first-flush diverter adds KES 3,000 to 5,000. Now selection. Look for three things: thickness, UV rating, and certification. A good tank has walls 5 to 8 millimetres thick. It has a UV8 or UV10 rating, which means it can stay in the sun for 10 years without becoming brittle. It has a food-grade certification, such as NSF or equivalent. In Africa, look for tanks made by Roto, Sintex, or local manufacturers like Kentainers in Kenya, GeePee in Nigeria, or JoJo in South Africa. These brands cost more but last longer. A cheap tank may last 3 years. A good tank lasts 15 years. The cost per year is what matters. A KES 20,000 tank lasting 10 years costs KES 2,000 per year. A KES 12,000 tank lasting 3 years costs KES 4,000 per year. Buy quality. Installation is simple but must be done right. First, prepare a level base. A 5,000-litre tank weighs 5 tonnes when full. The base must be compacted and level. Use a concrete slab 100 millimetres thick, or a sand pad 150 millimetres thick with a retaining ring of bricks. Do not put a plastic tank on bare ground. It will settle and crack. Second, place the tank on the base. Ensure the outlet is facing the direction you want. Third, connect the inlet. If you are harvesting rain, use a 100 millimetre PVC pipe from the gutter to the tank. Install a first-flush diverter. This is a 100 millimetre pipe with a ball valve that fills with the first 20 litres of rain and then diverts the rest to the tank. It costs KES 2,500 to 4,000. Fourth, install the overflow. Use a 50 millimetre pipe that leads to a drain or a garden. Cover it with a mosquito screen. Fifth, install the outlet. Use a 25 millimetre ball valve and a tap. For a business, add a water meter. Sixth, cover the tank. A lid is essential to keep out mosquitoes, leaves, and animals. If the tank is in the sun, build a simple shade with poles and a tarpaulin or iron sheets. This extends the life of the plastic. Maintenance is minimal. Check the tank every month for cracks, especially around the outlet. Clean the inside every 6 months with a soft brush and clean water. Do not use soap or chemicals. If you find a crack, you can repair it with a plastic welding kit or a patch kit. A patch kit costs KES 1,500 and includes a patch and adhesive. For a small crack, you can also use a self-tapping screw with a rubber washer. But prevention is better: do not over-tighten the outlet. Do not let the tank overflow and erode the base. Do not let children climb on the tank. Now the business. Selling and installing plastic tanks is a low-capital, high-turnover business. You can start with one tank as a demo. Buy a 1,000-litre tank for KES 20,000. Install it at your home or shop. Take photos. Then offer a package: tank, base, installation, first-flush diverter, and tap for KES 35,000. Your cost is KES 25,000. Your profit is KES 10,000 per installation. If you do 10 installations per month, that is KES 100,000 profit. In Nigeria, the same package sells for NGN 250,000 with a cost of NGN 180,000, profit NGN 70,000. In Ghana, GHS 2,500 sale, GHS 1,800 cost, GHS 700 profit. In South Africa, ZAR 5,000 sale, ZAR 3,500 cost, ZAR 1,500 profit. You can also rent tanks for events. A 1,000-litre tank rents for KES 3,000 per day. For a wedding or a construction site, that is easy money. You can also sell water. Fill a 10,000-litre tank with treated water and sell 20-litre jerrycans for KES 50. That is KES 25,000 per tank. Your cost is KES 5,000 for water and transport. Profit is KES 20,000. Safety note: plastic tanks are safe for drinking water if they are food-grade and clean. But you must clean them before first use. Fill with water and add 1 litre of bleach per 1,000 litres. Let it stand for 24 hours. Drain and rinse. Do not use a tank that has stored chemicals. Do not store water in a tank that has been in the sun for years without a UV rating. Do not drink water from a tank that has a bad smell or colour. Test the water at a local lab if you are selling it. A basic water test costs KES 3,000 to 5,000. It is worth it for your reputation. Finally, think about scale. If you are a distributor, you can buy 20 tanks at a time and get a 15 percent discount. You can then sell to hardware stores and contractors. You can also offer a layaway plan: customers pay 50 percent now and 50 percent on installation. This increases sales. In Africa, plastic tanks are the entry point for water storage. They are not the cheapest per litre for large volumes, but they are the fastest and cleanest for small volumes. Use them to build your customer base, then upsell to ferrocement or concrete for larger needs. That is how you grow a water business.

5. Lesson 5: Brick and Block Tanks - Traditional Strength for Underground and Courtyard Storage

Brick and block tanks are the traditional African solution for water storage. They are built from burnt clay bricks, concrete blocks, or stabilised soil blocks. They are rendered inside and out with cement mortar. They are strong, repairable, and can be built with local materials and local labour. They are ideal for underground storage, courtyard rainwater harvesting, and small-scale irrigation. This lesson covers the design, construction, and business case for a 5,000-litre brick tank, with costs in KES, NGN, GHS, and ZAR. First, design. A 5,000-litre tank holds 5 cubic metres. A rectangular tank 2 metres long, 1.5 metres wide, and 1.7 metres high holds 5.1 cubic metres. Add 0.3 metres freeboard, so the wall height is 2 metres. The walls are 230 millimetres thick, which is the width of a standard brick. The base is 150 millimetres thick concrete, reinforced with a single layer of 8 millimetre bars at 200 millimetres centre to centre. The walls are reinforced with 8 millimetre bars every 4 courses, both horizontal and vertical. The render is 20 millimetres thick inside and 15 millimetres outside, with a 1:3 cement-sand mix. The inside render is finished with a 1:1 cement-sand slurry with a waterproofing admixture. The tank can be underground, partially buried, or above ground. For underground, the walls must be designed to resist soil pressure. For above ground, the walls must resist water pressure. A 2 metre high wall of water exerts a pressure of 20 kilopascals at the base. That is why reinforcement is needed. Now materials. For a 5,000-litre brick tank, you need about 1,200 bricks.
