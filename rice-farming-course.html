Rice Farming

A complete 6-month program — levelled bunded fields, young shallow transplanting, water depth by growth stage and AWD, split nitrogen at panicle initiation, bird guarding, and the drying and milling discipline that decides your price.

⏱️ 6 Months (season + milling)📊 Beginner → Business-ready🌍 Hybrid: Online + Offline🎓 Certificate on completion

Course Overview

Why 6 months? A rice crop runs 100–150 days from nursery to harvest, and then milling and marketing decide what you actually earn. Six months covers a full season plus the post-harvest work — because in rice, the difference between a farmer who sells paddy at the farm gate and one who dries, mills and grades properly is often a doubling of income from the same field.

Structure: 6 monthly modules → lessons → practical assignments → a 10-question quiz each module → final business project.

How to study (hybrid): Online — modules on your phone, sessions in the Study Diary, questions to the rAs AI assistant. Offline — save this page (Print → Save as PDF); the practicals happen in the nursery, the bunded field and the drying yard.

⚠️ Read first: the three rules that protect your investment

  • Water control, not water quantity, is what makes rice work. A field you can flood but not drain will cost you yield at every stage. Bunds, an inlet and an OUTLET are the foundation of the whole enterprise.
  • Standing water breeds mosquitoes and bilharzia snails. Practise alternate wetting and drying where possible, keep canals clear, sleep under a net, and never wade in stagnant water with open wounds. Rice farming and malaria control belong in the same plan.
  • Birds can take an entire crop in the two weeks before harvest. Quelea and weaver flocks are not a nuisance — they are the single biggest late-season risk in African rice. Plan guarding before you plant.
MONTH 1 — Rice Systems, Varieties & Site Selection ▾

Lesson 1.1 — Why rice

  • Rice consumption is rising faster than any other staple in Africa, and most countries import heavily — meaning locally grown, well-milled rice has a guaranteed and growing market.
  • Yield gap: smallholder rainfed yields often sit at 1–2.5 t/ha, while irrigated well-managed systems reach 5–8 t/ha. The gap is water control, variety, transplanting method, weeding and fertility.
  • Milling matters enormously: badly dried, badly milled rice breaks into fragments worth far less than whole grains. In rice, post-harvest skill is worth as much as agronomy.

Lesson 1.2 — The four production systems

Wetland/inland valley (dambo/fadama)Natural seepage and flooding2–5 t/haHuge under-used potential across Africa

Lesson 1.3 — Choosing varieties

  • Match to system: lowland varieties for flooded fields, upland varieties for rain-only fields — planting the wrong type is a guaranteed failure.
  • NERICA varieties (New Rice for Africa) were bred for African conditions, combining African rice hardiness with Asian rice yield — widely available and worth asking for by name.
  • Select for: duration (short 90–110 days for short seasons; medium/long for reliable water), grain type your market wants (long slender aromatic grains fetch premiums; short bold grains suit some local markets), blast and RYMV (rice yellow mottle virus) resistance, and lodging resistance.
  • Use certified seed where possible — clean seed avoids seed-borne blast and weedy rice contamination. Otherwise select from your best field and clean it carefully.

Lesson 1.4 — Site and layout

  • Ideal: flat or gently sloping land with clay or clay-loam soil that holds water; a reliable water source; and — crucially — the ability to DRAIN.
  • Field layout: level basins surrounded by bunds 20–30 cm high and 30 cm wide, compacted and free of rat holes, with an inlet and outlet on opposite sides.
  • Levelling is the highest-return operation in rice: a field level within ±5 cm gives uniform water depth, meaning uniform weed suppression, uniform fertiliser response and uniform ripening. Unlevel fields waste water, grow weeds in the high spots and drown seedlings in the low.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 1): (1) Identify your system and confirm whether you can both flood AND drain the field. (2) Source variety recommendations (ask specifically about NERICA and about duration) with 3 seed quotes. (3) Survey your field's levelness using a water-filled hose or simple line level and map the high and low spots.

MONTH 2 — Nursery, Land Preparation & Transplanting ▾

Lesson 2.1 — Seed preparation

  • Clean the seed: pour seed into salt water (add salt until an egg floats) — good filled grains sink, empty and light grains float and are skimmed off. Rinse thoroughly in clean water afterwards, or the salt kills germination.
  • Soak cleaned seed 24 hours, drain, then incubate covered in a damp sack for 24–48 hours until sprouts just show — pre-germinated seed establishes far faster.
  • Seed rate: roughly 40–60 kg/ha for transplanting (nursery), or 80–100 kg/ha for direct seeding. Transplanting uses far less seed — one of its main advantages.

Lesson 2.2 — The nursery

  • Wet-bed nursery: puddled level bed 1–1.5 m wide, raised slightly, well fertilised with compost; broadcast pre-germinated seed evenly and keep saturated (not deeply flooded) for the first days.
  • Nursery area: about 500 m² of nursery supplies 1 hectare of transplanted field.
  • Protect from birds from day one — sprouting rice is bird candy.
  • Seedlings are ready at 18–25 days (3–4 leaves) for most varieties. Young seedlings transplant better: they suffer less shock and tiller far more than old, tall, stressed seedlings.

Lesson 2.3 — Land preparation and puddling

  • Flood the field, then plough/harrow the wet soil into a soft mud — "puddling". This kills weeds, seals the soil against water loss, and creates the soft bed transplanting needs.
  • Level immediately after puddling using a plank or leveller dragged across the mud — this is when the ±5 cm target is achieved.
  • Repair bunds, close rat holes, and check the inlet/outlet before flooding.
  • Basal fertiliser: incorporate compound (phosphorus and potassium with some nitrogen) into the mud before transplanting, roughly 100–200 kg/ha depending on soil fertility.

Lesson 2.4 — Transplanting

  • Spacing: 20 × 20 cm is the standard (25 plants/m²); 20 × 15 cm on less fertile soil for higher density.
  • 1–3 seedlings per hill, planted SHALLOW (2–3 cm) in straight rows. This is the biggest single change most smallholders can make: deep planting and large clumps of 6–8 seedlings suppress tillering and cut yields substantially, while shallow, sparse, straight-row planting lets each plant tiller into 15–25 productive stems.
  • Straight rows (using a marker or rope) make weeding, rotary weeders and fertiliser application dramatically faster.
  • Keep water shallow (2–3 cm) for the first week after transplanting so seedlings establish without drowning.

MONTH 3 — Water Management & Fertility ▾

Lesson 3.1 — Water depth by growth stage

StageWater depthReason
Transplant → establishment (wk 1)2–3 cmSeedlings must not drown
Tillering (wk 2–6)3–5 cmSuppresses weeds; too deep suppresses tillering
Panicle initiation → flowering5–10 cmThe most drought-sensitive period — never let the field dry now
Grain filling3–5 cmSteady moisture fills grains
2–3 weeks before harvestDrain completelyRipens evenly, hardens grain, allows harvesting machinery/people onto firm ground

Lesson 3.2 — Alternate wetting and drying (AWD)

  • AWD means letting the field water level drop to about 15 cm BELOW the soil surface (measured in a perforated tube sunk in the field) before re-flooding to 5 cm — repeatedly, except during the flowering period when water must be maintained.
  • Benefits: 15–30% water savings with no yield loss, stronger roots, less lodging, fewer mosquito breeding cycles, and reduced methane emissions.
  • It requires drainage control and is not for fields that cannot be re-flooded on demand.

Lesson 3.3 — Fertiliser: split nitrogen is everything

  • Rice takes up nitrogen in waves. Apply in splits: basal at transplanting, top dress 1 at active tillering (2–3 weeks), and top dress 2 at panicle initiation (roughly 5–7 weeks, when the young panicle is forming inside the stem) — this last split is the one that fills grains.
  • Total nitrogen guide: 60–120 kg N/ha depending on system and soil; phosphorus and potassium applied basally.
  • Apply nitrogen onto SHALLOW water or drained soil, then re-flood — broadcasting urea into deep standing water loses much of it.
  • The leaf colour chart (or simply comparing leaf greenness against healthy plants) is the practical way to decide top-dressing amounts — pale leaves mean apply, dark leaves mean wait.
  • Zinc deficiency is common in flooded rice: stunted plants with rusty brown spots on lower leaves shortly after transplanting — corrected with zinc sulphate where diagnosed.

Lesson 3.4 — Weeding

  • Weeds are the biggest yield thief in rice — losses of 30–70% in poorly weeded fields, worst in upland and direct-seeded systems.
  • Water is your best herbicide: 3–5 cm of standing water in a level field suppresses most weeds. Unlevel fields lose this advantage in the high spots.
  • Hand weeding at 2–3 weeks and 5–6 weeks; a rotary/cono weeder pushed between straight rows does the job several times faster and stirs the mud (which helps the crop).
  • Weedy rice ("red rice"): a wild relative that mimics the crop, shatters early and contaminates the harvest — control by using clean certified seed, roguing out off-type plants before flowering, and never saving seed from a contaminated field.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 3): (1) Install an AWD tube and manage water to the depth table; log levels weekly. (2) Apply the three fertiliser splits, using leaf colour to guide amounts. (3) Weed at 2–3 and 5–6 weeks and rogue any weedy rice before it flowers.

MONTH 4 — Pests, Diseases & Birds ▾

Lesson 4.1 — Diseases

  • Notice the pattern: excess nitrogen makes almost every rice disease worse. Split, measured nitrogen is disease control as well as nutrition.

Lesson 4.2 — Insect pests

  • Stem borers: "dead heart" (dead central shoot) early, or "white head" (empty white panicle that pulls out easily) later. Manage with resistant varieties, synchronised planting across the community, destroying stubble after harvest, and treating early where thresholds are met.
  • Rice bugs: suck developing grains at milk stage, causing empty and discoloured "pecky" grains. Scout at flowering/milk stage, use nets or early-morning collection on small plots, treat where severe.
  • African rice gall midge: shoots become tubular "onion leaf" galls; resistant varieties and early synchronised planting are the answers.
  • Termites (upland rice): lodging and dead plants — clean fields and timely harvest.
  • Natural enemies (spiders, dragonflies, frogs, wasps) do enormous work in a rice field; blanket insecticide spraying kills them and commonly makes pest problems worse.

Lesson 4.3 — Birds: the biggest late-season risk

  • Quelea and weaver flocks can strip a field in days at the milk-to-dough stage. Planning is required BEFORE the crop reaches that stage.
  • Practical defences: community synchronised planting (so birds are spread across many fields rather than concentrated on the only ripe one), active guarding from platforms with noise-makers and slings during daylight hours, reflective tapes and bird scarers moved regularly (birds habituate to anything static), and netting on small high-value plots.
  • Guarding is labour — budget it in the business plan honestly; many African rice farmers spend 2–3 weeks of daylight hours in the field at this stage.

Lesson 4.4 — Rodents and other vertebrates

  • Rats nest in bunds and canal banks — keep bunds clean and compact, remove weeds around the field, and use trapping campaigns synchronised with neighbours (individual efforts fail).
  • Where present, plan for hippos, bushpigs and cane rats with fencing/guarding — these are real crop losses in some African rice areas.

MODULE 5 — Harvest, Drying & Milling ▾

Lesson 5.1 — Harvest timing (the quality decision)

  • Harvest when 80–85% of grains on the panicle are straw-coloured and the grains in the lower third are firm and hard — roughly 30–35 days after flowering.
  • Too early: many immature, chalky grains and high breakage in milling. Too late: shattering losses in the field, bird damage, sun-cracked grains that shatter in the mill.
  • The field should have been drained 2–3 weeks earlier so the ground is firm and grain moisture is falling.

Lesson 5.2 — Cutting and threshing

  • Cut with sickles low on the stem; stack neatly with panicles inward and off the ground.
  • Thresh within 24 hours where possible — grain left in heaps heats and discolours quickly.
  • Thresh onto a tarpaulin or clean threshing floor, NEVER on bare soil — stones and soil in paddy destroy milling machines and downgrade the rice.
  • Pedal or motorised threshers reduce grain loss and labour massively compared with beating; even a shared community thresher pays for itself in saved grain.

Lesson 5.3 — Drying: where milling quality is decided

  • Target: dry paddy to 13–14% moisture for storage, and mill at that moisture for the highest whole-grain recovery.
  • Dry in thin layers (3–5 cm) on tarpaulins or drying floors, turning every 30–60 minutes so drying is even.
  • The critical rule: dry gradually and evenly. Fast drying on hot concrete at midday creates internal stress that cracks grains — and cracked grains SHATTER in the mill, turning premium whole rice into low-value brokens. Shade the crop during the hottest hours or start drying in the afternoon.
  • Never dry on bare ground; never leave paddy out overnight to absorb dew and re-wet (re-wetting cracks grain badly).
  • The bite test: a properly dried grain cracks cleanly and sharply between the teeth; a soft dent means it's still too wet.

Lesson 5.4 — Milling and grading

  • Milling removes the husk (making brown rice) and then the bran layer (making white rice). Head rice (whole grains) is what commands the price; brokens are worth much less.
  • Choose your miller carefully: a well-maintained mill with correct settings returns far more whole grains from the same paddy. Test different millers with the same batch and compare head-rice percentage — this single test can be worth more than a season of agronomy.
  • Parboiling (soaking, steaming and drying paddy before milling) is widespread in West Africa: it hardens the grain, sharply reduces breakage, improves nutrition and creates a distinct market product — worth learning if your market accepts it.
  • Grade and clean the milled rice: remove stones, discoloured and broken grains. Clean, uniform, stone-free rice in printed bags earns a serious premium over loose unbranded rice.
  • By-products are income: rice bran is valuable livestock feed (see rAs Animal Feed Making), and husks are used for fuel, poultry litter and building materials.

MODULE 6 — Storage, Marketing, Economics & Business Plan ▾

Lesson 6.1 — Storage

  • Store PADDY rather than milled rice where possible — the husk protects the grain, and milled rice deteriorates and attracts insects faster.
  • Store at 13–14% moisture in clean bags on pallets, off the floor, away from walls, in a dry ventilated store; hermetic bags where insect pressure is high.
  • Inspect monthly for insects, musty smell and rodent damage; re-dry if moisture has risen.
  • Mill on demand: fresh-milled rice tastes better and looks brighter, which is a genuine selling point over old stock.

Lesson 6.2 — Marketing

  • Channels: paddy to traders/millers at the farm gate (lowest price, immediate cash), milled rice to shops and households (much better), branded packed rice to supermarkets and institutions (best, but needs consistency, packaging and often registration).
  • The local-rice advantage: freshness and traceability. Many consumers prefer local rice when it is clean, stone-free and well milled — poor cleaning is what pushes them to imports.
  • Price curve: paddy is cheapest at harvest; storing and selling milled rice through the year captures a substantial premium.
  • Branding: a printed bag with your farm/group name, weight and milling date turns a commodity into a product.

📖 Glossary

  • Paddy — rice grain still in its husk.
  • Puddling — ploughing flooded soil into soft mud.
  • Tillering — the crop producing multiple stems from one plant.
  • Panicle initiation — when the young panicle forms inside the stem; the key nitrogen split.
  • AWD — alternate wetting and drying water management.
  • Head rice — whole milled grains; the value fraction.
  • Brokens — fragmented grains worth far less.
  • Parboiling — soaking/steaming/drying paddy before milling.
  • Neck blast — blast at the panicle base that empties the head.
  • Weedy rice — crop-mimicking wild rice that contaminates harvests.
