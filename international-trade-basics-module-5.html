1. Steps to Start Exporting

Starting to export from Africa might seem complex, but breaking it into steps makes it manageable. First, identify a product you can supply consistently. For example, Kenyan coffee, Ghanaian shea butter, or South African wine. Ensure you have a reliable source and can meet quality standards. Next, research target markets. Use tools like the International Trade Centre's Trade Map (trademap.org) to see demand, prices, and competitors. For instance, check which countries import shea butter and their tariffs. Then, register your business and obtain necessary licenses. In Nigeria, you need a CAC registration and an export license from the Nigerian Export Promotion Council (NEPC). In Kenya, register with the Kenya Revenue Authority and get an export permit. After that, find buyers. Attend trade fairs like the Intra-African Trade Fair or use online platforms like Alibaba and Africa Trade. Once you have a buyer, negotiate terms using Incoterms (e.g., FOB, CIF). Then, prepare documents: commercial invoice, packing list, certificate of origin, and possibly a phytosanitary certificate. For customs, you'll need a customs declaration. Finally, arrange shipping via a freight forwarder and ensure payment security, perhaps using a letter of credit. Start small: export a sample shipment to test the process. Many African export promotion agencies offer training and subsidies. For example, the Ghana Export Promotion Authority provides export readiness programs. Remember, exporting requires patience and attention to detail, but it opens doors to global markets.

2. Steps to Start Importing

Importing into Africa can be a great way to access goods not available locally or at lower prices. Start by identifying what you want to import. It could be electronics, machinery, or raw materials. Check local demand and regulations. For example, importing used cars into Kenya is restricted to vehicles older than 8 years. Next, find suppliers. Use platforms like Alibaba, Global Sources, or attend trade shows. Request samples and verify quality. Then, calculate total landed cost: product cost + shipping + insurance + customs duties + VAT + other fees. Use a landed cost calculator like the one on Freightos. In many African countries, you need an import license. In Nigeria, register with the Nigeria Customs Service and obtain a Form M. In South Africa, you need an importer code from SARS. You also need a customs broker to handle clearance. Choose a reputable freight forwarder. For shipping, decide between sea, air, or road. Sea is cheaper for bulky goods but slower. Air is faster but expensive. Once goods arrive, they must be cleared through customs. You'll need documents: bill of lading, commercial invoice, packing list, and certificate of origin. Pay applicable duties and taxes. For example, in Kenya, import duty on most goods is 25% plus 16% VAT. After clearance, arrange transport to your warehouse. Consider using a customs bonded warehouse if you need to defer duties. Start with small shipments to learn the process. Many African countries have online portals like the Kenya Trade Network Agency (KenTrade) to simplify procedures. Always comply with regulations to avoid penalties.

In international trade, documents are the backbone of every transaction. Missing or incorrect paperwork can delay shipments, cause penalties, or even lead to confiscation. 4. Customs Clearance Process

Customs clearance is the process of getting your goods through customs control. It involves declaring goods, paying duties and taxes, and obtaining release. In most African countries, this is done electronically. For example, in Kenya, you use the Kenya Trade Network Agency (KenTrade) system; in Nigeria, the Nigeria Customs Service uses the NICIS II. The process starts before goods arrive. You or your customs broker submit a customs declaration. This includes details like HS codes, value, quantity, and origin. HS codes determine duty rates. For instance, the HS code for raw shea butter is 1515.90. You then pay assessed duties. In many countries, you can pay online. After payment, customs may inspect the goods. Inspections can be physical or documentary. If everything is in order, customs releases the goods. You then arrange transport. Delays often happen due to missing documents, undervaluation, or incorrect HS codes. To speed up, ensure all documents are accurate and complete. Use a licensed customs broker who knows the local system. In some countries, you can use the Authorized Economic Operator (AEO) program for faster clearance if you have a good compliance record. For small importers, consider using a consolidated shipment to share costs. Always keep records for at least 5 years. African countries are increasingly digitizing customs to reduce clearance times. For example, the Single Window system in Ghana allows submission of all documents in one place. Be aware of additional fees like port charges, terminal handling, and storage if goods stay too long.

5. Incoterms 2020: Rules for Delivery

Incoterms 2020 are standard trade terms published by the International Chamber of Commerce (ICC). They define who is responsible for costs, risks, and tasks like transport, insurance, and customs clearance. There are 11 terms, but for African traders, the most common are FOB, CIF, EXW, and DDP. EXW (Ex Works) means the buyer picks up goods at the seller's premises. The buyer bears all costs and risks. This is simple for the seller but risky for the buyer. FOB (Free On Board) means the seller delivers goods on board the vessel at the port of shipment. The buyer pays for sea freight and insurance. This is common for African exports. CIF (Cost, Insurance, and Freight) means the seller pays for freight and insurance to the destination port. The buyer handles import clearance. This is common for imports into Africa. DDP (Delivered Duty Paid) means the seller delivers goods to the buyer's location, paying all duties and taxes. This is convenient for the buyer but risky for the seller. Other terms include FCA, CPT, CIP, DAP, DPU. When negotiating, choose terms that match your capacity. If you're new to exporting, FOB is safer because you don't have to arrange international transport. If you're importing, CIF might be easier. Always specify the Incoterm and the named place, e.g., FOB Mombasa. Incoterms do not cover payment terms or ownership transfer. Use them with a sales contract. Many African trade promotion agencies offer training on Incoterms. Understanding them helps avoid disputes and unexpected costs.

6. Working with Freight Forwarders and Customs Brokers

Freight forwarders and customs brokers are essential partners in international trade. A freight forwarder arranges the transport of goods from origin to destination. They handle booking cargo space, preparing documents, and coordinating with carriers. A customs broker specializes in clearing goods through customs. In many African countries, you must use a licensed customs broker to file declarations. For example, in Kenya, only licensed clearing agents can access the customs system. When choosing a forwarder or broker, check their license, experience, and reputation. Ask for references. Get quotes for your specific shipment. Fees vary: a freight forwarder might charge $200-$500 for a small shipment, while a customs broker charges a fee based on the value of goods or a flat rate (e.g., $150 per declaration). Good communication is key. Provide all documents early. Use a checklist: commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading, certificate of origin, etc. Many forwarders offer door-to-door service. For African trade, companies like Bollore Logistics, DHL Global Forwarding, and Maersk are active. Local forwarders can be more cost-effective. Always get a written contract and clarify responsibilities. For example, who pays port charges? Who handles inspections? In case of delays, your forwarder should keep you informed. Consider using a forwarder who is a member of FIATA or a local association like the Kenya International Freight and Warehousing Association (KIFWA). They can help with customs compliance and avoid costly mistakes. Building a good relationship can lead to smoother shipments and better rates.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Import and Export Procedures in African Trade

This module gives you the working tools you need to move goods across borders. Whether you are a smallholder cooperative in Ghana exporting shea butter, a Kenyan agri-processor importing cold-chain packaging, or a Nigerian trader sourcing machinery, the procedures are similar. What changes is the paperwork, the institutions, and the cost. This Practical Reference consolidates the six lessons in this module into a single operational guide you can keep on your desk.

African trade is governed by a patchwork of regional and national rules. The African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) is progressively reducing tariffs on intra-African trade, but customs procedures, documentation, and standards remain largely national. The World Trade Organization's Trade Facilitation Agreement (TFA) commits African governments to simplify and digitise border processes. Your job as a business is to know which rules apply to your specific product, origin, and destination.

Key Documentation at a Glance

Documentation is where most African small and medium enterprises lose time and money. A missing certificate of origin can cost you preferential tariff treatment under AfCFTA or a regional bloc like ECOWAS, SADC, or the EAC. Incoterms 2020: Choosing the Right Rule

Incoterms define who pays for what, where risk transfers, and who handles customs. Choosing the wrong term can leave you liable for costs you did not anticipate. For African agribusiness, FCA and CIF are the most common. The table below summarises the rules most relevant to African trade.

Table 2: Incoterms 2020 rules most used in African import/export
Incoterm Mode Risk Transfers Best For
EXW (Ex Works) Any At seller's premises Buyer with own logistics in-country
FCA (Free Carrier) Any When goods handed to carrier Containerised exports from landlocked countries
FOB (Free On Board) Sea When goods on vessel at port Bulk agricultural commodities
CIF (Cost, Insurance, Freight) Sea When goods on vessel at destination port Buyers wanting seller to arrange freight
CPT (Carriage Paid To) Any When goods handed to first carrier Air freight of perishables
DAP (Delivered At Place) Any At named destination, ready for unloading Regional trade within AfCFTA
DDP (Delivered Duty Paid) Any At destination, duties paid Seller with strong customs capability

Source: International Chamber of Commerce, Incoterms 2020 (ICC Publication No. 723E).

Customs Clearance: What Actually Happens

Customs clearance is not a single event. It is a sequence. First, you or your broker files a declaration, usually electronically through systems like Ghana's ICUMS, Kenya's iCMS, Nigeria's NICIS II, or South Africa's SARS eFiling. Second, customs assesses duty and VAT based on the HS code and declared value. Third, goods may be inspected physically or by scanner. Fourth, duties are paid. Fifth, goods are released.

For agricultural products, add phytosanitary inspection and standards conformity checks. For perishables, time is critical. A 24-hour delay at the port can destroy a shipment of fresh produce. This is why pre-clearance, pre-arrival processing, and authorised economic operator (AEO) status matter. The WTO TFA encourages African customs authorities to release goods before final duty payment for trusted traders.

Freight forwarders and customs brokers are not optional for most SMEs. A good broker knows the local HS code interpretation, the current inspection regime, and the informal fees that may be demanded. Choose a broker licensed by your national customs authority and, where possible, a member of a regional association like the Federation of West African Freight Forwarders or the East African Freight Forwarders Association.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Confirm HS code and tariff treatment before quoting a price. Use your national customs tariff and check AfCFTA or regional preferences.
  • Verify buyer/seller identity through your bank, embassy, or chamber of commerce. Never rely on email alone.
  • Agree Incoterms in writing and state the named place. "CIF" alone is not enough; specify the port.
  • Prepare documents before shipment: invoice, packing list, certificate of origin, phytosanitary certificate, and any conformity certificate.
  • Insure the cargo for at least 110% of CIF value, even if the Incoterm says the other party insures.
  • File declarations electronically and pre-clear where your customs authority allows it.
  • Budget for landed cost: duty, VAT, port charges, demurrage, broker fees, and inland transport.
  • Keep records for at least five years. Customs audits can happen years after clearance.
