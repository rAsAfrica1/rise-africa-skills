1. Keeping Daily Production and Sales Records in a Notebook

You don't need a computer or fancy software to keep good records. A simple exercise book and a pen will do. Every day, before you start work, write the date at the top of a new page. Then draw four columns: Production, Sales, Expenses, and Notes. Under Production, record how many bricks or pavers you made that day, and what type (e.g., solid, hollow, interlocking). For example: 'Made 250 solid bricks.' Under Sales, write how many you sold and the price per unit. If you sold 200 bricks at 150 shillings each, write '200 x 150 = 30,000 shillings.' Under Expenses, list everything you spent money on that day: clay, sand, cement, water, transport, fuel for the kiln, wages for helpers. Be specific: 'Paid 5,000 for 2 bags of cement.' Under Notes, write anything unusual: 'Kiln fired for 12 hours,' 'Rain stopped work at noon,' 'Customer complained about cracked bricks.'

At the end of each week, add up your total production, total sales, total expenses, and calculate your profit (sales minus expenses). This simple habit will show you if you are making money or losing it. Many small brick makers think they are profitable until they see the numbers. For instance, you might discover that you spend 40,000 shillings on materials and labour to make 500 bricks, but sell them for only 35,000. That means you are losing 5,000 shillings per batch. Without records, you would never know.

Keep your notebook in a safe, dry place. At the end of each month, copy the weekly totals into a summary page. This makes it easy to see trends: Are sales going up or down? Are expenses rising? Use this information to adjust your prices or find cheaper suppliers. You can also use your records to apply for a loan or grant later, because lenders want to see that you track your business. Remember, a notebook costs less than 2,000 shillings and can save you from big losses. Start today, even if you only make 50 bricks a day.

2. Separating Business Money from Personal Money

One of the biggest reasons small businesses fail is mixing business money with personal money. If you use the same pocket or bank account for both, you will never know if your brick business is actually making a profit. You might spend 20,000 shillings from sales on school fees, then later not have enough to buy cement for the next batch. To avoid this, open a separate bank account or mobile money wallet just for your brick business. If you don't have a bank account, use a separate mobile money number, or even a locked box with two compartments: one for business cash, one for personal cash.

Decide on a fixed salary for yourself. For example, if your business makes 300,000 shillings profit in a month, you might pay yourself 100,000 shillings as personal income, and keep 200,000 in the business for materials, labour, and savings. Write this salary in your record book. When you take money out, label it 'owner's salary' and note the date. Never take extra money without recording it as a loan from the business to yourself. That way, you can pay it back later.

Use your business account only for business expenses: buying clay, sand, cement, paying workers, repairing the kiln, and transport. When a customer pays you, deposit that money into the business account immediately. Don't carry it in your pocket where you might be tempted to spend it on personal things. If you must use cash, keep a small float for daily expenses, but record every coin you spend.

At the end of each month, compare your business account balance with your records. If the numbers don't match, find out why. This discipline will help you see if you can afford to buy a new mould or hire another worker. It also makes it easier to apply for a loan, because lenders will see that you run your business professionally. Start by opening a separate mobile money account todayâit costs nothing and takes five minutes.

3. Budgeting for Raw Materials, Labour, and Repairs

A budget is simply a plan for how you will spend your money before you spend it. For a brick and paver business, your main costs are raw materials (clay, sand, cement, water), labour (wages for helpers, kiln firing), and repairs (fixing moulds, kiln, wheelbarrows). To make a simple budget, take a notebook page and list all your expected expenses for the next month. Then estimate how much each will cost. For example: 10 tonnes of clay at 5,000 per tonne = 50,000; 5 bags of cement at 12,000 each = 60,000; 2 lorries of sand at 15,000 each = 30,000; water = 10,000; wages for 2 helpers at 8,000 per week each = 64,000; kiln fuel (firewood) = 40,000; repairs and maintenance = 20,000. Total = 274,000 shillings. Now estimate your production: if you make 5,000 bricks per month and sell them at 100 shillings each, your income is 500,000 shillings. Your budget shows a profit of 226,000 shillings before other costs.

But budgets must be realistic. If you know that the rainy season slows production, reduce your expected output. If cement prices are rising, add 10% to your estimate. Always keep a small bufferâsay 10% of total expensesâfor unexpected repairs. For example, if your kiln cracks, you might need 30,000 shillings for repairs. Without a buffer, you might have to borrow at high interest.

Review your budget every week. Compare what you actually spent with what you planned. If you overspent on labour, find out why. Maybe you hired extra help for a big order. If you underspent on materials, maybe you found a cheaper supplier. Adjust your budget for the next month based on these lessons. A good budget helps you avoid running out of money mid-month and keeps your business stable. Write your budget on a large paper and stick it on your workshop wall so you can see it daily.

4. Saving for Equipment Upgrades and Rainy Days

Saving is setting aside money today so you can use it tomorrow. For a brick maker, savings serve two purposes: to upgrade equipment and to cover emergencies or slow seasons. Start by opening a separate savings account or mobile money savings wallet. Every time you sell bricks, put a small percentageâsay 10%âinto savings before you spend anything else. If you sell 1,000 bricks at 100 shillings each, that's 100,000 shillings. Put 10,000 shillings into savings immediately. Over a year, that adds up to 120,000 shillings, enough to buy a new brick moulding machine or a used wheelbarrow.

Set specific savings goals. For example, 'I want to buy a diesel-powered block machine that costs 800,000 shillings.' Then calculate how much you need to save each month. If you save 20,000 shillings per month, you'll reach your goal in 40 months. That might be too long, so you could increase your savings rate or find a cheaper machine. Alternatively, save for smaller upgrades first: a new set of moulds (50,000 shillings), a better kiln grate (30,000 shillings), or a tarpaulin to cover bricks from rain (20,000 shillings).

Rainy days are both literal and figurative. In many parts of Africa, the rainy season stops brick making because clay becomes too wet and bricks cannot dry. During those months, you may have no income. If you have saved 3 months' worth of expenses (say 300,000 shillings), you can pay your workers and buy materials when the sun returns. Also, save for health emergencies, school fees, or funerals. A good rule: keep at least 20% of your monthly profit in a savings account that you can access quickly.

Don't keep savings in a tin under your bedâit can be stolen or eaten by rats. Use a bank, SACCO, or mobile money savings. Some mobile money services lock your savings for a fixed period and pay interest. Even 2% interest is better than nothing. Start small: save 1,000 shillings today, and add more each week.

5. Accessing Loans and Grants for Small-Scale Manufacturers

Many brick makers need extra money to buy a new machine, hire more workers, or buy raw materials in bulk at a discount. You can get this money from loans (which you must repay with interest) or grants (which you don't repay). To qualify, you need good records, a clear plan, and sometimes collateral (something valuable you own, like land or a vehicle).

Start with local sources. In many African countries, there are Savings and Credit Cooperative Organizations (SACCOs) where members save together and can borrow at lower interest rates than banks. For example, a SACCO might lend you 500,000 shillings at 12% annual interest, while a bank might charge 20%. To join, you usually need to save regularly for 3-6 months. Other options include microfinance institutions (MFIs) like BRAC, FINCA, or Opportunity International, which offer small business loans. They often require a business plan and may ask for a group guarantee (where other borrowers promise to repay if you default).

Government grants and NGO grants exist but are competitive. Check with your local government office for programs like the Youth Enterprise Fund, Women Enterprise Fund, or agricultural development funds. Some NGOs support environmentally friendly brick makingâfor example, those promoting energy-efficient kilns or reforestation. To apply, you'll need a proposal: describe your business, how much money you need, what you'll use it for, and how it will increase your income. Include your record book summaries and a budget. Be honestâdon't exaggerate.

Avoid loan sharks who charge 10% per week or take your tools as collateral. Before taking any loan, calculate if you can repay it. If you borrow 200,000 shillings at 15% annual interest for one year, you'll repay about 230,000 shillings. Will your extra production cover that? If not, wait and save more. Also, consider leasing equipment instead of buyingâsome companies rent brick machines for a monthly fee. Finally, keep your credit record clean by repaying on time. That way, you can borrow more in the future.

6. Planning for Environmental Sustainability: Reforestation and Waste Management

Brick making can harm the environment if you cut trees for firewood, dig clay without filling holes, and dump waste. But you can run a sustainable business that saves money and earns community respect. Start with reforestation: for every tree you cut for kiln fuel, plant at least two new ones. Choose fast-growing species like eucalyptus, grevillea, or acacia. You can raise seedlings in a small nursery using old containers. Plant them around your workshop, along boundaries, or on degraded land. In 3-5 years, you can harvest some for fuel, and the rest will provide shade and prevent soil erosion. Some NGOs give free seedlings or pay you to plant trees. For example, a project might pay 50 shillings per surviving tree after one year. If you plant 500 trees, that's 25,000 shillings extra income.

Waste management is another opportunity. Brick making produces broken bricks, ash, and unused clay. Don't throw them away. Broken bricks can be crushed and used as aggregate for foundations or pathways. Ash from the kiln can be mixed with soil to improve fertilityâmany farmers will buy it or exchange it for food. Unused clay can be used to make smaller bricks or to fill pot holes in your workshop yard. Also, recycle water: use a settling pond to collect muddy water from washing tools or mixing, so the clay settles and you can reuse the water for mixing again. This reduces your water bill.

Reduce air pollution by improving your kiln. Traditional open-air firing wastes fuel and produces thick smoke. A simple improved kiln with a chimney and controlled air flow uses 30-40% less firewood and burns cleaner. You can build one with local bricks and a metal grate. Also, avoid burning plastic or tyres in the kilnâthey release toxic fumes. Instead, use dry wood, sawdust, or agricultural waste like rice husks or maize cobs. Some entrepreneurs make briquettes from waste paper and sawdust as fuel.

Finally, manage your site. Fill in clay pits after digging, or turn them into fish ponds or water storage. Keep your workshop tidy to prevent accidents. Customers prefer to buy from a clean, green business. You can even advertise your bricks as 'eco-friendly' and charge a slightly higher price. Sustainability is not just good for the planetâit is good for your profits.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Record Keeping, Finance & Business Sustainability for Brick & Paver Makers

In brick and paver making, the difference between a business that survives and one that collapses is rarely the kiln or the moulds â it is the books. A maker who knows their true cost per brick, separates business cash from household cash, and plans for repairs and rainy days will still be trading in five years. One who guesses will not. This practical reference gives you the tools, numbers, and habits to run a small-scale brick or paver enterprise as a real business.

Why records are your most profitable tool

Your notebook is not paperwork â it is management information. Without daily records you cannot answer three questions that decide everything: What does one brick cost me to make? Where did last week's money go? Can I afford to replace the mixer when it breaks? Studies of micro and small enterprises across Sub-Saharan Africa consistently find that firms which keep formal records are significantly more likely to access credit and to survive past the five-year mark. The World Bank's Enterprise Surveys show that access to finance is the single biggest constraint reported by small manufacturers in Africa â and lenders and grant programmes almost always require records before they release funds.

Your record system needs only four elements: a daily production log, a daily sales log, a cash book (money in, money out), and a monthly cost summary. A 200-page hardcover notebook costs almost nothing and works when the power is off. If you have a smartphone, a simple spreadsheet or a free app such as a basic expense tracker is a useful backup â but never let the phone replace the paper book, and never keep only one copy.

Keeping daily production and sales records

Record every batch the same day it is made. A batch is your unit of truth: how many bricks or pavers were moulded, how many were fired or cured, how many broke, and how many are ready for sale. Then record every sale â quantity, price per unit, buyer, and whether it was cash, mobile money, or credit. Credit sales must be written down with the buyer's name and the agreed date of payment, or you will lose the money.

Separating business money from personal money

This single habit saves more small businesses than any loan. When business cash and household cash share one pocket or one mobile-money wallet, you can never know whether you are profitable, and you will spend capital on food without noticing. Open a separate bank account or mobile-money wallet for the business. Pay yourself a fixed, modest owner's salary on a set day each month, and treat that as a business expense. All sales income goes into the business account; all business purchases come out of it. If you must take extra money, record it as a loan from the business to yourself, and repay it.

Budgeting for raw materials, labour, and repairs

A budget is simply a plan for money you expect to earn and spend. Build it from your records, not from hope. Start with your known cost per unit (materials + labour + fuel + transport + a share of repairs), add your target profit margin, and set your selling price. Then forecast monthly sales conservatively â use your worst recent month, not your best.

Saving for equipment upgrades and rainy days

Every month, move a fixed percentage of profit into two separate savings pots before you spend anything else. A common rule for small manufacturers is 10% for equipment replacement and 5% for emergencies. Bricks and pavers sell slowly in the rainy season and fast in the dry season; your rainy-day fund is what pays wages and raw materials in the slow months. Keep these savings in a separate account or a locked mobile-money savings wallet so they are not easy to spend. When you need a new mixer or a better kiln, you buy it from the equipment fund rather than borrowing at high interest.

Accessing loans and grants for small-scale manufacturers

Lenders and grant programmes ask the same questions: Do you keep records? Is your business separate from your household? Can you show a repayment plan? Before you apply, prepare a one-page summary of your last six months of production, sales, and costs, plus a simple cash-flow projection for the next six months. In many African countries, sources include:

  • Commercial and microfinance banks â ask specifically about SME or manufacturing loan products.
  • Government enterprise funds â for example, Kenya's Uwezo Fund and Women Enterprise Fund, Nigeria's BOI SME loans, and similar national schemes.
  • Development finance and donor programmes â the African Development Bank and its partners run SME support facilities in several countries.
  • Cooperatives and SACCOs â often the most accessible for small makers with regular savings.
  • Grants â usually for equipment, training, or green technology; watch for application deadlines and never pay a fee to apply.

Borrow only for things that will earn money back â a better mixer, a delivery vehicle, a curing shed. Never borrow for routine household consumption; that is what your salary and rainy-day fund are for.

Brick and paver production involves serious hazards. Never allow children or untrained workers near an active kiln or firing pit. Kiln gases (carbon monoxide) are invisible and can kill â always fire in a well-ventilated area, never in an enclosed shed. Wear gloves, eye protection, and covered footwear when handling hot bricks, lime, or cement; cement burns skin and eyes on contact. Keep a bucket of clean water and a basic first-aid kit on site. Do not burn plastic, tyres, or treated wood in the kiln â the fumes are toxic and the ash contaminates your product. Store fuels and chemicals away from the firing area and away from children. If you feel dizzy or short of breath near the kiln, move to fresh air immediately and seek help.

Planning for environmental sustainability: reforestation and waste management

Brick making consumes wood, water, and clay, and produces ash, broken bricks, and kiln smoke. Sustainability is not only good citizenship â it protects your raw materials and your licence to operate. Practical steps:

  • Reforestation: for every tree you cut for firing, plant at least three. Work with your local forestry service or a farmers' group to plant fast-growing species such as eucalyptus, grevillea, or casuarina on a dedicated woodlot. This secures your future fuel supply and can earn carbon or agroforestry income.
  • Waste management: crush broken bricks and pavers and reuse them as aggregate or road fill. Collect kiln ash and use it as a soil amendment or in block mixes where appropriate. Never dump waste in rivers or on neighbours' land.
  • Energy: improve kiln insulation and use drier fuel to reduce smoke and wood use. Where available, explore cleaner firing technologies or stabilised (cement or lime) bricks that need less firing.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Record production and sales every day, in the same notebook, before you go home.
  2. Keep business money in a separate account or wallet from household money.
  3. Pay yourself a fixed monthly salary and record it as a business expense.
  4. Update your monthly budget and compare planned vs. actual spending.
  5. Move 10% of profit to equipment savings and 5% to a rainy-day fund each month.
  6. Write down every credit sale with the buyer's name and payment date.
  7. Keep a repairs fund â never let a breakdown catch you without cash.
  8. Plant trees and reuse or recycle waste every season, not just when it is convenient.
