1. Startup Costs Breakdown

Before you buy your first pig, you need to know exactly what you'll spend. In Africa, a small batch farrowing unit for 5 sows can start with about $3,000 to $5,000. The biggest costs are housing, equipment, and your first set of breeding stock. For housing, a simple open-sided shed with concrete floor and iron sheet roof costs around $1,500 for 5 sows and their piglets. You can reduce this by using local materials like bamboo and thatch, but concrete is better for cleaning and disease control. Equipment includes feeders, drinkers, and farrowing crates. A basic farrowing crate costs $80 to $120 each. You need at least 5 crates, so budget $500. Feeders and drinkers for 5 sows and 50 piglets cost about $300. A water tank and pipes: $200. Now breeding stock: a good young boar costs $200 to $400, and each sow costs $150 to $300. For 5 sows and 1 boar, that's roughly $1,000 to $1,900. Don't forget initial feed: you need to feed your pigs for at least 2 months before any sales. That's about 500 kg of feed at $0.50 per kg, so $250. Vet and vaccines for the first 3 months: $150. Labour: if you do it yourself, you save, but if you hire a caretaker, budget $100 per month. Transport for buying pigs and feed: $100. Miscellaneous (tools, buckets, disinfectants): $200. Total startup: about $4,000 to $5,000. To reduce costs, start small: 2 sows and 1 boar, use second-hand materials, and build slowly. But never compromise on concrete floors and proper drainagethey save you money on disease treatment later. Write every expense down in a notebook. This is your baseline. Many farmers fail because they underestimate startup costs. Be honest with yourself. If you have $2,000, start with 2 sows. You can expand later from your profits.

2. Monthly Operating Costs

Once your pig farm is running, you face monthly costs that must be paid even before you sell any pigs. For a batch farrowing system with 5 sows, your monthly operating costs will be around $800 to $1,200. The biggest chunk is feed, which we'll cover in the next lesson, but for now, know that feed alone can be $500 to $700 per month. Labour is next: if you hire one full-time worker, expect to pay $100 to $200 per month, depending on your country. In many African countries, you can find reliable help for $120 per month. If you do the work yourself, that's money saved, but your time has value. Utilities: water and electricity. If you have a borehole, water is cheap, but if you buy water, budget $50 per month. Electricity for lighting and maybe a feed grinder: $30 to $50. Vet and health: even without a disease outbreak, you need routine deworming, vaccinations, and vitamins. Budget $50 to $100 per month. Transport: to buy feed, take pigs to market, or visit the vet. If you have a motorcycle, fuel and maintenance cost $50 per month. If you hire transport, it can be $100 or more. Maintenance and repairs: things breakwater pipes, feeders, roofs. Set aside $50 per month. Miscellaneous: soap, disinfectants, tools, phone airtime for calling buyers. $30. Loan repayments: if you borrowed money to start, include that. For a $2,000 loan at 10% annual interest over 2 years, you pay about $92 per month. Total monthly operating costs: roughly $900 to $1,200. To manage these costs, keep a monthly budget book. Track every expense. At the end of each month, compare actual spending to your budget. If you overspend on feed, find out whymaybe you're wasting feed or your pigs are sick. Buy feed in bulk to get discounts. Share transport with neighbouring farmers. Do your own vaccinations if trained. And always pay yourself a small salary so you don't dip into operating money. Remember, you only get income when you sell pigs. In batch farrowing, sales come in cycles, so you must have enough cash to cover 2-3 months of expenses before your first batch is ready.

3. Feed: 65-75% of All Costs

Feed is the single biggest expense in pig farming. In Africa, feed alone takes 65 to 75 cents out of every dollar you spend. If your monthly costs are $1,000, then $650 to $750 goes to feed. That's why managing feed is the key to profit. A lactating sow with piglets eats about 5 kg of feed per day. A dry sow eats 2.5 kg. A growing pig eats 1.5 to 2 kg per day. For 5 sows and 50 piglets, you need roughly 500 kg of feed per week, or 2,000 kg per month. If commercial feed costs $0.50 per kg, that's $1,000 per month just for feed. But you can reduce this. First, mix your own feed. Buy maize, soya bean meal, fish meal, and premix in bulk. A typical pig feed recipe: 60% maize, 20% soya, 10% fish meal, 5% wheat bran, 5% premix and minerals. This can cut your cost to $0.35 per kg. That saves you $300 per month. Second, grow your own maize or cassava. If you have land, plant maize. One hectare can produce 2,000 kg of maize, worth $600. That's three months of feed for your pigs. Third, reduce waste. Use feeders that prevent spillage. Don't overfeed. A sow doesn't need unlimited feed; follow a feeding chart. Fourth, store feed properly to avoid mould and rats. Use airtight containers or raised pallets. Mouldy feed causes abortions and slow growth. Fifth, use alternative feeds like sweet potato vines, pumpkin, and kitchen waste, but only as supplementsthey don't replace balanced feed. Sixth, buy feed ingredients during harvest when prices are low. Maize is cheapest right after harvest. Store it dry. Seventh, consider fermenting feed. Fermented feed improves digestion and reduces cost by 10-15%. To ferment, mix feed with water in a sealed container for 2-3 days. It smells like silage. Pigs love it. Finally, monitor your feed conversion ratio (FCR). FCR is how many kg of feed to produce 1 kg of pig. A good FCR is 3:1. If your FCR is 4:1, you're wasting feed. Weigh your pigs monthly to track growth. Keep records of feed bought and used. If feed is 70% of costs, a 10% saving on feed is a 7% saving on total costs. That's real money.

4. Labour and Overheads

Labour and overheads are the second and third biggest costs after feed. In a small pig farm in Africa, you might do all the work yourself, but as you grow, you'll need help. Labour costs include salaries, food, and housing for workers. A full-time pig caretaker in Kenya or Nigeria earns about $100 to $150 per month. In South Africa, it's $200 to $300. If you hire part-time help for cleaning and feeding, you might pay $50 per month. But labour is not just salary. You must also provide gloves, boots, and overalls. That's $20 per month. If the worker lives on the farm, you provide housing and food, which can add $50 per month. Overheads are the ongoing costs that don't change with the number of pigs. These include rent or land rates, insurance, licences, and administration. If you rent land, that's $50 to $100 per month. If you own land, you still pay land ratesmaybe $10 per month. Insurance for pigs and buildings: $30 per month. Business licence and permits: $20 per month. Administration: phone, internet, stationery, bank charges. $30 per month. Security: a guard or fence maintenance. $40 per month. Water and electricity we covered under operating costs, but they are also overheads. Total labour and overheads for a 5-sow farm: about $300 to $500 per month. To reduce labour costs, train your workers to do multiple tasks. A good caretaker can also do minor vet work, record keeping, and feed mixing. Pay them well to avoid theft and negligence. Use family labour where possible, but be professional. For overheads, share costs with neighbours. Buy insurance as a group to get lower premiums. Use solar power to cut electricity bills. Harvest rainwater to cut water bills. Keep overheads low, but don't compromise on security or health. Remember, overheads are fixedyou pay them whether you sell pigs or not. So in months with no sales, you still need cash. That's why you must have a reserve fund. Aim to keep 3 months of overheads in a savings account. This buffer will save your farm during disease outbreaks or market slumps.

5. Vet, Transport and Maintenance

These three costs are often forgotten, but they can break your budget if you don't plan for them. Vet costs include vaccinations, dewormers, antibiotics, and professional fees. In Africa, you must vaccinate against swine fever, foot and mouth, and erysipelas. A vaccine dose costs $0.50 to $1 per pig. For 5 sows and 50 piglets, that's $30 per round. You vaccinate sows twice a year and piglets once. Deworming: every 3 months, at $0.20 per pig, so $10 per round. Antibiotics for sick pigs: $20 per month on average. Vet consultation: $20 per visit, maybe twice a year. Total vet costs: about $100 to $150 per month. To reduce, learn to do your own vaccinations and deworming. Attend a training course. Buy vaccines in bulk and share with other farmers. Keep your farm clean to prevent disease. Transport costs include moving feed, pigs, and supplies. If you buy feed from town, you need a pickup or motorcycle. Fuel and maintenance: $50 to $100 per month. If you hire transport, it's $0.10 per kg per km. For 2,000 kg of feed over 20 km, that's $40 per trip. You might do 4 trips per month, so $160. To reduce, buy feed in bulk once a month. Use a bicycle or wheelbarrow for short distances. Sell pigs at the farm gate to avoid transport to market. If you must transport pigs, use a well-ventilated truck and avoid overcrowding. Maintenance costs: your buildings, fences, water pipes, and feeders need repair. A leaking roof, a broken drinker, a rusty gatethese add up. Set aside $50 per month for maintenance. Keep a toolbox and spare parts. Fix small problems before they become big. For example, a $2 washer can save a $200 pump. Train your workers to report damage immediately. Keep a maintenance log. Total vet, transport, and maintenance: $200 to $300 per month. To manage these, include them in your monthly budget. Don't wait for an emergency. If you see a sick pig, act fastearly treatment is cheaper. If you see a broken fence, fix it before pigs escape. These costs are controllable if you are proactive. Remember, a healthy pig is a profitable pig. A well-maintained farm has fewer disease problems. So spend wisely hereit's an investment, not just an expense.

6. Cost Per Pig Formula

To know if you are making a profit, you must calculate your cost per pig. This is the total cost of producing one pig from birth to sale. The formula is: Cost per Pig = Total Costs Number of Pigs Sold. But total costs include all expenses: feed, labour, vet, transport, maintenance, overheads, and startup costs spread over time. Let's work through an example. Suppose you have 5 sows. Each sow produces 10 piglets per batch, and you have 2 batches per year. So you sell 100 pigs per year. Your annual costs: feed $9,000, labour $1,800, vet $1,200, transport $1,200, maintenance $600, overheads $2,400, startup $4,000 (spread over 5 years, so $800 per year). Total annual cost = $9,000 + $1,800 + $1,200 + $1,200 + $600 + $2,400 + $800 = $17,000. Cost per pig = $17,000 100 = $170. So each pig costs you $170 to produce. If you sell at $250, your profit per pig is $80. Total profit = $8,000 per year. But if you sell at $150, you lose $20 per pig. That's why you must know your cost per pig. To calculate your own, follow these steps: 1) Record all expenses for a year. 2) Count all pigs sold. 3) Divide total expenses by number of pigs. 4) Compare to your selling price. If your cost per pig is too high, find where to cut. Maybe feed is too expensivemix your own. Maybe labour is highdo more yourself. Maybe you have too many pig deathsimprove health. Also calculate cost per kg. If a pig weighs 80 kg and costs $170, then cost per kg is $2.13. If market price is $2.50 per kg, you profit $0.37 per kg. To reduce cost per pig, increase productivity. A sow that gives 12 piglets instead of 10 spreads your fixed costs over more pigs. Improve feed conversion. Reduce mortality. Sell at the right weightpigs gain weight cheaply up to 80 kg, then it gets expensive. Weigh your pigs monthly to track. Use a simple notebook or a smartphone app like Farmbrite. Update your costs weekly. Review your cost per pig every batch. This number is your compass. Without it, you are farming blind. With it, you can make smart decisions and grow your profit.

<h2>Practical Reference: Expenses at Every Stage</h2>

<p>In a batch farrowing system, every pig you sell carries the accumulated cost of every stage that came before it. A weaner that leaves your pen at 6 weeks, a porker at 22 weeks, or a sow at cull each one must repay the feed, labour, vet care, transport and overheads that were spent on it. This lesson gives you a practical reference for tracking those expenses in an African smallholder or commercial pig unit, and for turning them into a single number you can act on: <strong>cost per pig</strong>.</p>

<p>The business logic is simple. If you do not know your cost per pig, you cannot know whether a buyer's offer is profit or loss. If you do not know which stage consumes the most money, you cannot fix the leak. Batch farrowing makes this easier because animals move through stages together but it also means a single mistake in feed or health management is multiplied across the whole batch.</p>

<h3>Startup Costs: What You Pay Before the First Pig Sells</h3>

<p>Startup costs are one-time or near-one-time expenses. They do not repeat every batch, but they must be recovered over the life of the enterprise. In African smallholder systems, the biggest startup items are housing (pens, farrowing crates or individual pens, roofing, concrete floor), water supply (tank, trough, plumbing), and breeding stock (gilts or sows, plus a boar or AI access). Equipment includes feeders, drinkers, weighing scale, feed storage bins, and basic tools.</p>

<p>Do not treat startup as "free" because it was built with family labour or local materials. Assign a realistic replacement value. If your pen cost the equivalent of 800 USD to build and will last 10 years, that is 80 USD per year roughly 6.7 USD per month that must appear in your cost per pig.</p>

<h3>Monthly Operating Costs and the Feed Reality</h3>

<p>Operating costs repeat every month or every batch. They are the ones that decide whether you stay in business. In batch farrowing, the operating month includes feed for dry sows, gestating sows, lactating sows and piglets, plus labour, water, electricity or fuel, veterinary inputs, transport, and repairs.</p>

<p>Feed dominates. Across African pig systems, feed accounts for <strong>6575% of all production costs</strong>. This is not a small detail it is the single most important number in your business. If feed is 70% of costs and you waste 10% of feed, you have just lost 7% of your entire revenue potential. Weigh feed, store it properly, and mix rations to match the stage of production.</p>

<h3>Cost Per Pig Formula</h3>

<p>This is the number that turns your records into decisions. Use this formula every batch:</p>

<p style="font-family:monospace; background:#f1f8e9; padding:12px; border-left:4px solid #2e7d32; font-size:15px;">
Cost per pig = (Startup recovery for the batch + Operating costs for the batch) Number of pigs sold
</p>

<p>Example: A 10-sow batch produces 80 weaners sold. Startup recovery allocated to this batch = 100 USD. Operating costs for the batch = 1,500 USD. Cost per pig = (100 + 1,500) 80 = <strong>20 USD per weaner</strong>. If the market price is 25 USD, your margin is 5 USD per pig before tax and financing. If the market price is 18 USD, you are losing money and must either cut feed cost, improve litter size, or find a better market.</p>

<p>Run this formula for each stage: cost per weaner, cost per grower, cost per porker, cost per cull sow. Batch farrowing makes this possible because you know how many animals entered and left each stage.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li> Record every feed purchase, bag by bag, with date and weight.</li>
<li> Weigh a sample of pigs monthly to calculate feed conversion.</li>
<li> Allocate startup costs over 510 years, not into one batch.</li>
<li> Calculate cost per pig after every batch, not once a year.</li>
<li> Compare your feed cost percentage if above 75%, investigate waste or poor ration.</li>
<li> Keep vet and transport receipts; these are real costs, not incidentals.</li>
<li> Store feed dry and use within 6 weeks to avoid aflatoxin and spoilage.</li>
<li> Review market price against cost per pig before agreeing to sell.</li>
</ul>
