Agriculture · 6-Month Program

Cassava Farming

Africa's insurance crop, done properly — clean cuttings, the weed window, the virus defence triangle, the 48-hour harvest clock, and safe processing that turns roots into gari, flour and real margins.

Course Overview

Cassava feeds half a billion Africans precisely because it forgives drought, poor soil and neglect — but forgiveness is not profit. The gap between the continental average of 8–10 tonnes per hectare and the 20–35 tonnes good farmers achieve comes down to four learnable disciplines: clean cuttings of resistant varieties, winning the 3–4 month weed window, roguing out virus, and respecting the 48-hour clock that starts the moment a root is dug. This course teaches all four, then goes where the money is: safe processing into gari, fufu, flour and chips — including the cyanide safety knowledge that protects your family and your customers, taught bluntly because it matters.

  • Choose your business model — fresh, outgrower, processor or seed entrepreneur — and plan it

Month 1: Why Cassava, Varieties & Cyanide Safety
Start

Lesson 1.1 — Why cassava is Africa's insurance policy

Cassava feeds more than 500 million Africans. It grows where maize fails: it tolerates drought that would kill most crops, produces on tired sandy soils, and — uniquely — stores itself in the ground. Roots can be left unharvested for months and dug when you need food or cash. That is why farmers call it the famine reserve. But cassava is also a serious cash crop: gari, fufu, high-quality cassava flour (HQCF), industrial starch and livestock feed chips are all growth markets across the continent.

  • Maturity: most varieties 9–12 months; some improved types from 8 months; roots can wait in the soil to ~18 months (older = woodier).
  • Yields: African average is a sad 8–10 t/ha; well-managed fields with improved varieties routinely reach 20–35 t/ha. The gap is management, and this course is about closing it.
  • Everything above ground is useful too: tender leaves are a protein-rich vegetable in much of Central Africa (must be properly pounded and boiled — see Lesson 1.3), and stems are next season's planting material.

Lesson 1.2 — Sweet vs bitter: know what you are growing

All cassava contains cyanogenic compounds (mainly linamarin) that release hydrogen cyanide (HCN) when the root is damaged. Varieties differ enormously:

TypeCyanogen levelUse
Sweet varietiesLowCan be eaten boiled/roasted after normal peeling and cooking
Bitter varietiesHighMUST be processed (fermented, grated & pressed, or soaked and dried) before eating; often higher yielding and less stolen/browsed
Safety rule you never break: never eat bitter cassava — or feed it to children or animals — that has only been boiled. Peeling removes much of the cyanogen (it concentrates in the peel) but bitter varieties need full processing: fermentation, grating-and-pressing, or prolonged soaking plus thorough drying. Module 5 teaches the safe methods in detail. Know which type every plant in your field is, and label planting material accordingly.

Lesson 1.3 — Choosing varieties: three questions

  1. Is it disease-resistant? Cassava mosaic disease (CMD) and cassava brown streak disease (CBSD) are the two yield destroyers (Module 4). Modern released varieties — e.g. the TME 419 and TMS lines in West Africa, NASE and MM series in East Africa, and national releases elsewhere — carry resistance. Ask your extension office which released varieties are recommended for your district this year.
  2. What does your market want? Fresh-eating markets want sweet, early, good-tasting roots. Gari and fufu processors want high dry matter. Starch and HQCF factories specify dry matter and root size. Feed chip buyers mostly want cheap tonnage.
  3. Does it fit your season? Match maturity length to your rainfall pattern and your cash-flow needs.

Cassava leaves for the pot: use tender top leaves, pound them and boil thoroughly (15+ minutes, lid off) — the pounding and long boiling drive off the cyanide. Never eat raw or lightly cooked cassava leaves.

Why: Districts differ in which diseases and strains circulate. National programmes release varieties matched to local pressure — starting there beats guessing, and food-market roots tell you nothing about disease resistance.

Month 2: Cuttings, Land Preparation & Planting
Locked

Lesson 2.1 — Cassava is planted from stems, and that changes everything

Cassava has no true seed in normal farming — you plant stem cuttings from last season's plants. Every virus and pest in the mother plant rides into your new field inside the cutting. So, exactly as with banana, the health of your planting material is the single biggest yield decision you make:

  • Take cuttings only from healthy, vigorous plants 8–18 months old that showed no mosaic-patterned leaves or brown-streak symptoms all season.
  • Use the middle portion of the stem — the woody brown section. The green top is too soft and dries out; the oldest base is too woody and sprouts poorly.
  • Cut pieces 20–25 cm (8–10 in) long with 5–8 nodes, using a sharp clean blade (crushing the stem with a blunt machete kills buds).
  • Buy certified clean cuttings of resistant varieties where seed-system programmes exist — the premium repays itself in the first season.
Storing stems: if planting is weeks away, store whole 1–1.5 m stems (not cut pieces) upright under tree shade, bases pushed a few centimetres into moist soil. They stay viable for up to 2 months. Cut into planting pieces only on planting day.

Lesson 2.2 — Land preparation and planting geometry

DecisionStandard practiceWhy
TillagePlough/dig to 20–30 cm; make ridges or mounds on heavy or wet soils, flat on deep light soilsRoots need loose soil to bulk; ridges prevent waterlogging and make harvest far easier
Spacing1 m × 1 m = 10,000 plants/ha (~4,000/acre) for sole cropThe standard that balances root size and total tonnage; widen to 1 m × 1.5 m when intercropping
Cutting positionPlant at an angle (~45°) or vertically with 2/3 of the cutting buried, buds pointing up; flat and 5–10 cm deep works in moist loose soilAngled/vertical planting gives fast, uniform sprouting and roots clustered for easy harvest

Lesson 2.3 — Orientation matters

Look at a cutting: the buds sit above old leaf scars and all point one way — toward what was the top of the plant. Plant that end up. Upside-down cuttings sprout late and weak or not at all. When planting at speed with hired labour, this is the error to police.

Gap-fill at 3–4 weeks: walk the field, and wherever a cutting failed, plant a fresh one from your reserve bundle. A 95% stand at month one is the cheapest yield insurance there is.

Month 3: Weeds, Feeding & Intercropping
Locked

Lesson 3.1 — The 3–4 month weed window decides your harvest

Cassava starts life slowly. For its first 3–4 months it is a small plant losing every fight with weeds — and trials across Africa show uncontrolled weeds in this window cut final root yield by 50% or more. After canopy closure (month 4–5), cassava shades weeds out and largely defends itself.

  • Plan 2–3 thorough weedings: around weeks 3–4, weeks 8–9, and if needed week 12–14. Early planting into a clean seedbed makes each one lighter.
  • Shallow hoeing only near plants — deep hoeing wounds the young storage roots and every wound is a rot entry.
  • Re-ridge/earth-up during the second weeding: it buries in-row weeds and loosens soil for root bulking.

Lesson 3.2 — Feeding a crop famous for needing nothing

Cassava's reputation for growing on poor soil is true — and misleading. It survives poverty; it pays on fertility. A 25 t/ha root harvest removes large amounts of potassium especially. The honest feeding picture:

SituationRecommendation
Fresh land or after good fallowOften little fertiliser needed in year one; manure at planting still pays
Continuous croppingManure/compost in the planting hole or ridge + a K-strong NPK (e.g. ~200 kg/ha of a balanced compound, split at 1 and 3 months) lifts yields sharply
Any situationPotassium is cassava's number-one mineral — starch is built on K. Wood ash (a free K source) worked into ridges is valuable where fertiliser is unaffordable

Too much nitrogen alone grows a jungle of stems and leaves with disappointing roots — balance is the word.

Lesson 3.3 — Intercropping: cassava's best habit

That slow first three months is an opportunity: the space between young cassava rows grows a full short-season crop before the canopy needs it.

  • Classic partners: maize, beans, cowpea, groundnuts, melons/pumpkins — planted between widened cassava rows (1 m × 1.5 m).
  • The legumes fix nitrogen and their residues feed the cassava; the intercrop income pays for the weedings; and a covered soil surface is itself weed control.
  • Rule: the intercrop must be short-season and out of the field by month 3–4, before cassava needs full light and water.

Lesson 3.4 — What cassava cannot forgive

  • Waterlogging — even a few days of standing water rots roots. Ridges and drains, always, on suspect land.
  • Deep cultivation after month 4 — bulking roots spread wider than you think; late deep hoeing wounds them.
  • Continuous cassava on the same land without manure or rotation — yields slide and pests build. Rotate with legumes or cereals every 2–3 crops.

Why: Continuous cassava mines K, multiplies whitefly and soil pests, and lets disease inoculum accumulate. Rotating with legumes or cereals every 2–3 crops resets the field.

Month 4: Pests, Viruses & the Defence Triangle
Locked

Lesson 4.1 — The two viruses that decide cassava's fate

Two viral diseases, both spread by the whitefly (Bemisia tabaci) and — far more importantly — by infected cuttings, dominate cassava production in Africa:

Cassava Mosaic Disease (CMD)Cassava Brown Streak Disease (CBSD)
Leaf symptomsYellow-green mosaic patches, twisted, shrunken leavesFeathery yellowing along veins of lower leaves; brown streaks on green stems
The real damageStunted plants, yield losses up to total in sensitive varietiesDry brown corky rot inside the roots — plants can look almost normal until harvest reveals ruined roots
Region of greatest threatContinent-wideEast, Central and spreading — the most feared cassava disease in Africa today

Neither can be cured in the field. The defence triangle:

  1. Resistant released varieties — the foundation. This is why Module 1 sent you to the extension office.
  2. Clean cuttings — never take planting material from a field that showed symptoms, however healthy an individual plant looks. CBSD especially hides.
  3. Roguing — from month 1, walk the field monthly; uproot and destroy (burn or bury) any plant with mosaic or brown-streak leaves. One infected plant is a whitefly-served buffet infecting its neighbours all season.

Lesson 4.2 — Cassava mealybug and green mite: the biological-control success story

In the 1980s the cassava mealybug devastated the continent until scientists introduced a tiny parasitic wasp (Anagyrus lopezi) that now controls it almost everywhere — one of history's greatest biological control victories. The practical lessons for you:

  • Mealybug (white waxy clusters on shoot tips, 'bunchy top' distortion) and cassava green mite (speckled, mottled leaves from the underside, candle-stick bare tips in dry season) flare mainly in long dry seasons and on stressed plants.
  • Do not spray broad insecticides at the first sign — you will kill the wasps and predatory mites doing your pest control for free, and usually make the outbreak worse.
  • Real defences: healthy well-fed plants, early planting, clean cuttings (mealybug travels on stems), and patience — natural enemies usually catch up.

Lesson 4.3 — Other troubles worth recognising

  • Cassava bacterial blight: angular water-soaked leaf spots, blighted dying shoots, gum on stems — worst in wet seasons. Managed by clean cuttings, rotation, and removing infected debris.
  • Termites and rodents: attack cuttings and roots on dry sandy land; early planting into moist soil and field sanitation reduce losses.
  • Root rots: almost always a drainage message — ridge higher, drain better, rotate.
The one-sentence plant-health plan: resistant variety + certified or carefully selected clean cuttings + monthly roguing walks + no panic spraying. Farmers doing these four things routinely double the district average yield with no other change.

Month 5: Harvest, the 48-Hour Clock & Safe Processing
Locked

Lesson 5.1 — Harvest: the clock starts when the root leaves the ground

Here is cassava's great paradox: a root that stores itself perfectly in the soil becomes one of the world's most perishable commodities the moment you dig it. Postharvest physiological deterioration (PPD) begins within 24 hours: blue-black streaks spread through the flesh, and within 2–3 days fresh roots are unsaleable. Every cassava marketing plan is built around this 48-to-72-hour clock.

  • Piecemeal harvesting: for home use, dig only the roots needed today and leave the plant living — the rest keep perfectly.
  • Commercial harvesting: never dig until transport and the buyer are confirmed. Harvest in the cool morning, handle gently (wounds accelerate PPD), keep roots shaded and covered.
  • Ease of harvest is decided months earlier: ridged loose soil and angled planting = roots that lift with a stick; compacted flat land = broken roots and hard labour.
  • Save the best stems at harvest, bundle and shade-store them — that is next season's planting material, selected while you can still see which plants performed.

Lesson 5.2 — Processing: how cassava becomes money and becomes safe

Processing solves both cassava problems at once: it stops the spoilage clock and removes the cyanogens. The main product routes:

ProductProcess (simplified)Notes
GariPeel → wash → grate → ferment in sacks under press 2–4 days → sieve → roast ('garify') in a pan to dry crisp grainsWest Africa's everyday staple; shelf-stable for months; fermentation + roasting removes cyanogens
Fufu / fermented pastesPeel → soak roots in water 3–5 days to ferment soft → sieve/pound → cookFermentation detoxifies; sold wet or dried as flour
High-quality cassava flour (HQCF)Peel → wash → grate → press out water → dry the same day → mill fineUnfermented, white, neutral flour for bakeries and industry; demands speed and hygiene — the whole process ideally within 24 hours
Dried chipsPeel (for food grade) → chip → sun-dry hard on racks 2–4 daysLivestock feed and milling; simplest entry-level processing
StarchIndustrial extraction from fresh rootsFactory route — you supply roots on contract, dry matter specified
The konzo warning — read this twice. When drought or hunger pushes people to shortcut the processing of bitter cassava — soaking for hours instead of days, drying briefly, skipping fermentation — chronic cyanide exposure can cause konzo, a sudden, permanent paralysis of the legs, striking mostly children and young mothers. Outbreaks happen in exactly the seasons when the temptation to shortcut is strongest. The rules that prevent it: give fermentation and soaking their full time; dry thoroughly; where possible mix cassava flour with other foods; and prefer sweet varieties for any fresh eating. Processing time is not tradition — it is the safety mechanism.

Lesson 5.3 — Drying done properly

For chips and flours, drying is the quality step: dry on raised racks or clean sheets (never bare earth), in thin layers, turned several times daily, protected from rain and animals, down to hard, snap-dry pieces. Slow damp drying grows moulds — including aflatoxin producers — and undermines everything the processing achieved. (The Solar Drying course covers dryer construction that cuts drying time and dust.)

Cassava supports four distinct business models. Pick deliberately — they need different varieties, scales and skills:

ModelYou sellKey success factor
Fresh-root supplierRoots to urban food marketsSweet varieties, the 48-hour logistics chain, morning market relationships
Processor's outgrowerRoots on contract to gari/HQCF/starch factoriesTonnage per hectare, dry matter, delivery reliability — contracts smooth your price

Lesson 6.3 — Selling into strength

  • Gluts are self-inflicted: because roots wait in the ground, you choose your harvest week. Track your local price calendar for a year (your notebook) and dig into the high months, not with the crowd.
  • Contracts beat spot markets for outgrowers: a slightly lower fixed price with guaranteed offtake usually out-earns gambling on spot prices with perishable roots.
  • Processed products store — gari and dry chips let you hold stock for lean-season prices, exactly the hermetic-storage logic from the Beans & Legumes course. Store dry products in sealed containers off the floor.

Lesson 6.4 — Records and growth

Keep the five-line notebook: planting dates and variety per block; weeding dates and labour costs; roguing counts; harvest tonnage per block; sales (kg, form, buyer, price). Within two seasons you will know your cost per tonne, your best variety, and whether processing pays you better than volume — the three numbers on which every expansion decision rests. Grow by adding blocks in rotation with legumes, and by climbing one step down the value table per year, not three.

📚 Glossary

Cyanogens / linamarin
Compounds in cassava that release hydrogen cyanide (HCN) when tissue is damaged; low in sweet, high in bitter varieties.
Sweet / bitter cassava
Market terms for low- vs high-cyanogen varieties. Bitter types must be fully processed before eating.
Konzo
An irreversible paralysis caused by chronic cyanide exposure from inadequately processed bitter cassava, typically during drought/hunger seasons.
Stem cutting
A 20–25 cm piece of mature stem with 5–8 nodes — cassava's planting material, and the main carrier of its diseases.
CMD
Cassava mosaic disease — whitefly- and cutting-borne virus causing mosaic leaves and severe stunting.
CBSD
Cassava brown streak disease — virus causing corky brown root rot, often with mild leaf symptoms; Africa's most feared cassava disease.
Roguing
Uprooting and destroying symptomatic plants during regular field walks to remove virus sources.
Whitefly (Bemisia tabaci)
The tiny insect vector spreading CMD and CBSD between plants.
Weed window
The first 3–4 months, when uncontrolled weeds can halve final yield; closed by canopy closure.
Earthing up
Rebuilding ridges over the row during weeding — burying weeds and loosening soil for root bulking.
PPD
Postharvest physiological deterioration — the blue-black spoilage that ruins fresh roots within 2–3 days of digging.
Piecemeal harvest
Digging only what is needed while the plant lives on as in-ground storage.
Gari
Roasted fermented cassava grains — West Africa's shelf-stable staple; the classic value-addition product.
HQCF
High-quality cassava flour — unfermented white flour requiring same-day processing and fast, clean drying.
Dry matter
The non-water fraction of the root; what processors and starch factories actually pay for.
Outgrower
A farmer producing under contract to a processor at agreed volumes, quality and price.
