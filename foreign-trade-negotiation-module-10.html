1. Foundations of Foreign Trade Negotiation in Africa

Foreign trade negotiation is the structured process by which buyers, sellers, intermediaries, and governments reach agreement on the exchange of goods, services, or rights across national borders. In Africa, this process carries extra layers because you are often dealing with different currencies, legal systems, languages, logistics corridors, and regulatory regimes. A negotiation that works in Nairobi may fail in Lagos or Abidjan if you do not adapt. This lesson gives you the practical foundations you need before you sit at any table. The first principle is that trade negotiation is not only about price. Price matters, but it is one variable among many. Payment terms, delivery schedules, quality standards, packaging, documentation, dispute resolution, and currency risk often decide whether a deal is profitable. A supplier who gives you a low unit price but demands full payment upfront in a volatile currency may cost you more than a supplier with a slightly higher price who accepts letters of credit and shares freight risk. Start every negotiation by mapping your interests, not just your position. Your position is what you say you want, such as a 10 percent discount. Your interests are why you want it, such as protecting your margin against local inflation or matching a competitor price. When you understand interests, you can create trades. You might accept a smaller discount if the seller extends credit for 90 days or includes spare parts. The second foundation is preparation. In African cross-border trade, preparation means knowing the tariff line of your product under the applicable trade agreement, the rules of origin, the documentary requirements, and the informal costs along the corridor. If you are exporting processed cashew from Tanzania to Kenya, you need to know the East African Community tariff phase-down, the certificate of origin requirements, and the standards imposed by the Kenya Bureau of Standards. If you are importing machinery into Nigeria, you need to understand Form M, the destination inspection regime, and the applicable duty. Negotiation leverage often comes from this technical knowledge. A buyer who knows the exact duty and clearance cost can challenge an inflated landed-cost claim. A seller who knows the buyer has few alternative suppliers can hold firm on price. The third foundation is cultural and communication awareness. Africa is not one market. Negotiation styles differ across regions, sectors, and generations. In many West African trading relationships, trust and relationship-building precede detailed contracting. In parts of East and Southern Africa, a more direct, document-driven approach may be expected. In North Africa, language and personal networks often shape the pace. Do not stereotype, but do observe. Ask questions. Learn how your counterpart prefers to communicate. In some contexts, a written email is formal and binding in tone; in others, a WhatsApp voice note is the real channel. Adapt your method without abandoning your discipline. The fourth foundation is legal and institutional awareness. Cross-border contracts must specify governing law, jurisdiction, arbitration seat, and language. Many African trade disputes are resolved through arbitration because local courts can be slow or unfamiliar with international commercial practice. The Arbitration Foundation of Southern Africa, the Cairo Regional Centre for International Commercial Arbitration, and the Lagos Chamber of Commerce arbitration services are examples of institutions you may encounter. Your contract should also address force majeure, sanctions, anti-bribery compliance, and data protection where relevant. If you are trading under the African Continental Free Trade Area, you should understand the protocol on rules of origin, the dispute settlement mechanism, and the phased tariff schedules. The fifth foundation is financial risk management. African currencies can move sharply. Inflation can change cost structures mid-contract. Payment delays are common. Build these realities into your negotiation. Ask for advance payment or partial advance. Use confirmed irrevocable letters of credit from reputable banks. Consider export credit insurance or political risk insurance. Price in a currency that matches your costs where possible. If you must accept local currency, negotiate a price adjustment clause tied to a published exchange rate or inflation index. Do not treat these as hostile demands. Frame them as risk-sharing mechanisms that make the deal bankable. The sixth foundation is logistics and infrastructure reality. Port congestion, border delays, poor road conditions, and cold-chain gaps affect delivery. A negotiated delivery date that ignores these realities creates disputes. Build realistic lead times. Agree on Incoterms that match your capacity. If you are a small exporter, you may prefer FOB so the buyer controls freight. If you are a large buyer, you may prefer DDP so the seller carries the risk to your warehouse. Understand what each Incoterm means for cost, risk, and insurance. Finally, remember that negotiation is a repeat game. In African trade, reputation travels. A hard bargain that leaves the other side humiliated may win today and lose tomorrow. Aim for agreements that are profitable and sustainable. Document everything. Confirm verbal understandings in writing. Follow up. Pay on time when you are the buyer. Deliver as promised when you are the seller. These habits build the trust that reduces transaction costs over time. In the next lesson, we will apply these foundations to a structured preparation method.

2. Preparing Your Negotiation Strategy and Team

Preparation separates professional negotiators from hopeful ones. In foreign trade, preparation must cover commercial, technical, legal, and logistical dimensions. This lesson gives you a practical preparation framework you can use for any African cross-border deal. The framework has seven steps: define objectives, map stakeholders, gather market intelligence, analyze your leverage, set your reservation value, plan concessions, and build your team. Step one is defining objectives. Write down what success looks like in measurable terms. Do not write improve supplier relationship. Write secure a two-year supply of 500 metric tons per month of raw sesame at a landed cost below 620 US dollars per metric ton, with payment against documents and a quality certificate from an accredited laboratory. Specific objectives force clarity. They also reveal trade-offs. If the price is above your target, what else can you accept? Longer contract? Better payment terms? Technical support? Rank your objectives as must-have, important, and desirable. Step two is mapping stakeholders. In many African trade negotiations, the person across the table is not the only decision-maker. There may be a family owner, a government regulator, a customs broker, a shipping agent, or a community leader whose consent matters. Map who influences the outcome and what they care about. If you are negotiating a mining supply contract in Ghana, the local assembly, the Minerals Commission, and the chief may all have interests. If you are negotiating an agricultural export from Ethiopia, the cooperative union, the regional bureau of agriculture, and the national export authority may be involved. Ignoring a stakeholder can derail a signed contract. Step three is gathering market intelligence. You need to know prevailing prices, alternative suppliers, freight rates, duty structures, and competitor behavior. Use trade data from sources such as the International Trade Centre, UN Comtrade, national statistics offices, and industry associations. Talk to freight forwarders, customs brokers, and bankers. Visit the market if possible. In African trade, ground truth often differs from published data. A customs broker in Mombasa may know the real clearance time better than an official report. A banker in Accra may know which letters of credit are actually honored. Step four is analyzing your leverage. Leverage comes from alternatives, information, time pressure, and legitimacy. If you have three credible alternative suppliers, your leverage is stronger. If you know the seller has excess stock and a loan payment due, your leverage is stronger. If you are under time pressure and the seller knows it, your leverage is weaker. List your sources of leverage and your counterpart's sources. Then decide how to strengthen yours. You can develop alternatives, gather more information, or change the timeline. Step five is setting your reservation value. This is the worst acceptable outcome before you walk away or escalate. It must be based on your economics, not your emotions. Calculate your break-even landed cost, your minimum acceptable margin, and your maximum acceptable risk. Write it down before the negotiation. Once you are in the room, it is easy to drift. A written reservation value keeps you disciplined. Step six is planning concessions. Never give a concession without getting something in return. Plan a sequence. Start with issues that are cheap for you but valuable to them. If you are a buyer, you might offer a longer contract or a larger volume commitment in exchange for a lower price. If you are a seller, you might offer faster delivery or extended warranty in exchange for a higher price or advance payment. Use conditional language: if you can do X, then I can consider Y. This keeps the negotiation collaborative and prevents unilateral giveaways. Step seven is building your team. A trade negotiation team should include a commercial lead, a technical expert, a legal or contracts advisor, and a logistics or finance person. For smaller deals, one person may wear several hats, but the roles must still be covered. Decide who speaks, who observes, who takes notes, and who has authority to commit. In many African contexts, the senior person should not speak first on technical details. Let the expert present the data. The senior person can then endorse or adjust. This preserves flexibility and shows respect for expertise. Before the meeting, agree on your internal communication signals and your break-out procedure. If the other side makes an unexpected demand, you need a way to pause and consult. A simple phrase such as we need to review this internally is enough. Do not improvise on major terms. Also prepare your opening. A good opening frames the discussion around shared interests and the agenda. It should not be a long speech. It should confirm the purpose, the time available, and the process. For example: Thank you for meeting. Our goal today is to agree on price, volume, payment, and delivery for the next season. We have two hours. We suggest starting with volume and quality, then price and payment. Does that work? This kind of opening sets a professional tone and reduces surprises. Finally, prepare your BATNA, your best alternative to a negotiated agreement. If you are a buyer, your BATNA might be another supplier, a substitute product, or delaying the purchase. If you are a seller, your BATNA might be another buyer, processing the raw material locally, or holding stock. A strong BATNA gives you confidence. A weak BATNA tells you to invest in improving your alternatives before you negotiate. In African cross-border trade, BATNAs are often limited by logistics, financing, and market access. That is exactly why preparation matters. The more you know, the more options you can create. In the next lesson, we will move from preparation to the actual negotiation conversation.

3. Conducting the Negotiation: Tactics and Communication

The negotiation conversation is where preparation meets reality. In African foreign trade, the conversation may happen in a boardroom, a hotel lobby, a factory floor, a trade fair, or a video call. The setting matters less than the process. This lesson covers practical tactics and communication skills for conducting the negotiation. Start with the agenda. A clear agenda signals professionalism and keeps the discussion focused. Send it in advance if possible. At the table, confirm it verbally. If the other side wants to change it, agree on the change explicitly. Do not let the agenda drift into a series of unrelated complaints. If a new issue arises, note it and schedule it for later unless it is urgent. Next, manage the opening exchange. In many African business cultures, a period of relationship-building is expected before substantive discussion. This may involve greetings, tea, inquiries about family or travel, or a discussion of the broader business environment. Do not rush this. It builds trust. But also watch for signals that the other side is ready to move to business. A good rule is to follow their lead. When they shift to the agenda, you shift with them. Then move to information exchange. Ask open questions. Listen carefully. Take notes. Confirm your understanding by summarizing. For example: Let me make sure I understand. You can supply 300 metric tons per month from October, but only if we provide a 30 percent advance payment. Is that correct? This does two things. It prevents misunderstandings, and it shows you are serious. It also gives you time to think. Do not fill every silence. Silence is a tool. After you make an offer, stop talking. Let them respond. Many negotiators talk themselves out of a good position because they are uncomfortable with silence. When you present your position, anchor it with a rationale. Do not just say we need a 10 percent discount. Say our landed cost target requires a 10 percent discount because freight and duty have increased by 15 percent since last year. Here is the breakdown. A reasoned position is harder to reject than a bare demand. It also invites problem-solving. Use conditional concessions. If you can reduce the lead time to 30 days, we can increase the order by 20 percent. If you can accept payment in 60 days, we can accept your packaging specification. This keeps the exchange balanced. Never give a concession for free. If you do, you teach the other side that your concessions are cheap. Handle objections without becoming defensive. When the other side says your price is too high, do not immediately cut it. Ask: Too high compared to what? What is your target? What would make this work for you? This turns an objection into a problem to solve together. It also reveals their real constraints. If they say they have a cheaper offer, ask for the details. Is it the same quality? Same delivery terms? Same payment terms? Often the cheaper offer is not comparable. If it is, you may need to adjust or walk away. Be careful with aggressive tactics. Some negotiators use good cop bad cop, false deadlines, or take-it-or-leave-it ultimatums. In African trade, these tactics can damage relationships. You may encounter them. Do not react emotionally. Test the deadline. Ask what happens if we miss it. Test the ultimatum. Ask if there is any flexibility. If there is none, you have learned something important. If there is, you have called their bluff without confrontation. Manage the pace. If you feel rushed, slow down. Ask for a break. Review your notes. Consult your team. A common tactic is to create time pressure near the end of a meeting. Do not accept major terms under pressure. It is better to reconvene than to sign a bad deal. If the other side is genuinely under time pressure, you may gain leverage, but do not exploit it cruelly. Remember the repeat game. Communication style matters. Use clear, simple language. Avoid jargon unless you are sure the other side understands it. If you are negotiating in a second or third language for either party, confirm understanding frequently. Use written summaries after each session. Send a short email: Here is what we agreed today. Please confirm or correct. This creates a paper trail and prevents later disputes. Pay attention to non-verbal cues. In some cultures, direct eye contact is a sign of confidence. In others, it is disrespectful. A nod may mean I understand, not I agree. A smile may be politeness, not consent. Do not over-interpret, but do observe. When in doubt, ask a clarifying question. Also manage your own non-verbal signals. Sit upright. Keep an open posture. Speak at a moderate pace. Avoid showing frustration through sighs or eye-rolling. In high-stakes trade negotiations, emotional control is a competitive advantage. Finally, know when to close. A negotiation can drift if you do not recognize the moment of agreement. Summarize the terms. Confirm each point. Ask: Are we agreed on price, volume, payment, delivery, and quality? If yes, move to documentation. If no, identify the remaining gap and decide whether to continue or reconvene. Do not leave the room with vague understandings. In African cross-border trade, vague understandings are expensive. In the next lesson, we will cover the documentation and implementation phase, where many deals succeed or fail.

4. Documentation, Contracts, and Implementation

A negotiated deal is only as good as its documentation and implementation. In African foreign trade, poor documentation is one of the most common causes of delay, loss, and dispute. This lesson covers the practical steps from verbal agreement to successful delivery and payment. Start with the term sheet. After the negotiation, prepare a short term sheet that captures the key commercial terms: parties, product, quantity, quality, price, currency, payment method, delivery terms, delivery date, packaging, inspection, and dispute resolution. Send it to the other side for confirmation. The term sheet is not the final contract, but it prevents misunderstandings before the lawyers get involved. If there is a disagreement, it is better to find it now than after the contract is signed. Next, move to the formal contract. A cross-border sales contract should include several essential elements. First, the identification of the parties, including legal names, registration numbers, addresses, and authorized signatories. Second, the product description, including specifications, grade, and any applicable standards. Third, the quantity and tolerance. In commodity trade, a plus or minus 5 percent tolerance is common. Fourth, the price and currency. Specify whether the price is fixed or subject to adjustment. If adjustment is allowed, define the formula and the source of the index. Fifth, the payment terms. This is critical. Options include advance payment, open account, documentary collection, and letters of credit. Each has different risk levels. A confirmed irrevocable letter of credit from a reputable bank is one of the safest instruments for an African exporter, but it adds cost and paperwork. An open account is cheaper but riskier. Choose based on your relationship and your risk capacity. Sixth, the delivery terms. Use Incoterms 2020. Specify the named place. For example, FOB Mombasa, CIF Rotterdam, or DAP Kampala. Each term allocates cost, risk, and insurance differently. Do not use Incoterms vaguely. Seventh, the delivery schedule. Include a start date, a completion date, and any milestones. If delivery depends on a letter of credit or an import permit, state that clearly. Eighth, quality and inspection. Specify who inspects, where, when, and using what standard. Name an independent inspection company if needed, such as SGS, Bureau Veritas, or Intertek. Ninth, packaging and marking. This matters for handling, storage, and customs. Tenth, force majeure. Define what events qualify and what happens if they occur. Eleventh, dispute resolution. Specify the governing law, the arbitration seat, the language, and the number of arbitrators. Twelfth, termination and remedies. Define what happens if one party fails to perform. Once the contract is signed, implementation begins. The first step is usually the letter of credit or the advance payment. If you are the seller, check the letter of credit carefully. Does it match the contract? Are the required documents available? Can you meet the latest shipment date? A small discrepancy can delay payment for weeks. If you are the buyer, make sure the letter of credit is issued by a bank acceptable to the seller and confirmed if required. The second step is production or procurement. Monitor progress. Do not assume the seller will meet the schedule. Ask for regular updates. If you are the seller, communicate proactively. Send photos, inspection reports, and shipping updates. This builds trust and reduces the chance of a last-minute dispute. The third step is inspection. If an independent inspection is required, schedule it early. Make sure the inspector has access to the goods and the necessary documents. If the goods fail inspection, you need time to fix the problem. Do not wait until the ship is at the port. The fourth step is shipping and documentation. The seller must prepare the commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading or airway bill, certificate of origin, inspection certificate, insurance certificate, and any other required documents. Each document must be accurate and consistent. A misspelled name or a wrong date can cause customs delays. If you are the buyer, check the documents against the letter of credit before the bank releases payment. The fifth step is customs clearance. In many African countries, clearance can be the biggest source of delay. Work with a reputable customs broker. Ensure that the tariff classification is correct and that all duties and taxes are paid. If you are using a trade agreement, make sure the certificate of origin is valid and that the goods meet the rules of origin. The sixth step is payment. If you are the seller, follow up on payment. Do not assume the buyer will pay on time. If payment is late, send a polite reminder. If it remains late, escalate according to the contract. If you are the buyer, pay on time. Your reputation depends on it. The seventh step is post-delivery review. After the goods are delivered and payment is complete, review what went well and what went wrong. Document the lessons. If you plan to trade again, use the review to improve the contract and the process. In African cross-border trade, implementation is where relationships are tested. A seller who delivers on time and a buyer who pays on time become preferred partners. That preference is worth more than a small price advantage. In the next lesson, we will cover dispute resolution and long-term relationship management.

5. Dispute Resolution and Building Long-Term Trade Relationships

Even with good preparation and documentation, disputes happen. In African foreign trade, disputes may arise over quality, quantity, delivery time, payment, or interpretation of contract terms. How you handle a dispute determines whether you lose a deal, lose a customer, or strengthen a relationship. This lesson covers practical dispute resolution and long-term relationship management. The first principle is to prevent disputes before they occur. Most disputes come from unclear expectations. Be specific in your contract. Define quality standards precisely. Specify inspection methods. Agree on delivery windows, not just dates. State what happens if a shipment is late. State what happens if payment is late. If you are selling agricultural products, define moisture content, foreign matter, and defect tolerance. If you are selling manufactured goods, define performance specifications and warranty terms. If you are providing services, define deliverables and acceptance criteria. The second principle is to communicate early. If you see a problem developing, tell the other side immediately. Do not hide it. If your factory is behind schedule, inform the buyer as soon as you know. Propose a solution. If your payment is delayed, tell the seller. Explain the reason and give a new date. Early communication gives the other side time to adjust. It also builds trust. The third principle is to resolve at the lowest level possible. A dispute between a buyer and a seller should first be handled by the commercial teams. If they cannot resolve it, escalate to senior management. Only if that fails should you move to formal dispute resolution. Formal processes are slow and expensive. They also damage relationships. Use them as a last resort, not a first response. When you discuss a dispute, focus on interests, not blame. Ask: What outcome do we need? What would be fair? What can we do to prevent this from happening again? If the goods are defective, the buyer may need a price reduction, a replacement, or a repair. The seller may need time to investigate. Find a solution that addresses the underlying problem. If you cannot resolve the dispute directly, consider mediation. Mediation is a voluntary process where a neutral third party helps the parties reach an agreement. It is faster and cheaper than arbitration or litigation. Many African trade associations and chambers of commerce offer mediation services. The mediation agreement can be enforced as a contract if both parties sign it. If mediation fails, the next step is arbitration. Arbitration is a private process where one or more arbitrators hear the case and issue a binding decision. It is often preferred in international trade because it is more enforceable across borders than court judgments. The New York Convention on the Recognition and Enforcement of Foreign Arbitral Awards has been ratified by most African countries. This makes arbitration awards easier to enforce than court decisions. When drafting an arbitration clause, specify the seat, the rules, the language, and the number of arbitrators. A common clause might say: Any dispute arising out of or in connection with this contract shall be referred to arbitration in Nairobi under the rules of the Nairobi Centre for International Arbitration. The language of the arbitration shall be English. The number of arbitrators shall be one. Be careful with the seat. The seat determines the legal framework and the courts that can supervise the arbitration. Choose a seat that is neutral and has a good arbitration law. If arbitration is not suitable, you may need to go to court. But cross-border litigation is complex. You must consider jurisdiction, service of process, language, and enforcement. In many African countries, court cases can take years. Use litigation only when the amount is large and the legal position is clear. Now let us turn to long-term relationship management. In African trade, relationships are assets. A reliable buyer or seller is worth more than a one-time gain. To build long-term relationships, do the following. First, be reliable. Deliver what you promise. Pay what you owe. Meet your deadlines. Reliability is the foundation of trust. Second, be transparent. Share information about your capacity, your constraints, and your plans. If you cannot meet a request, say so early. Do not overpromise. Third, be fair. Do not exploit a temporary advantage. If your supplier is in difficulty, consider helping them. They will remember it. If your buyer is facing a cash flow problem, consider a short extension. This does not mean being weak. It means being strategic. Fourth, invest in the relationship. Visit your partners. Attend trade fairs together. Share market intelligence. Introduce them to useful contacts. Celebrate their successes. These gestures build goodwill. Fifth, review the relationship regularly. Once a year, sit down and discuss what is working and what is not. Discuss price, quality, delivery, and payment. Discuss future plans. This prevents small problems from becoming big ones. Sixth, diversify but do not abandon. It is wise to have more than one supplier or buyer. But do not use diversification as a threat. Use it as a risk management strategy. Keep your main partners informed. If you are expanding your supplier base, explain why. If you are entering a new market, ask for their advice. Finally, remember that African trade is increasingly regional and continental. The African Continental Free Trade Area is creating new opportunities. But it is also creating new competition. The negotiators who succeed will be those who combine technical knowledge, cultural awareness, and relationship skills. They will know how to read a tariff schedule and how to read a room. They will know when to push and when to pause. They will know that a contract is not just a legal document but a framework for a working relationship. As you apply the lessons in this module, remember that negotiation is a skill. It improves with practice, feedback, and reflection. After every negotiation, ask yourself: What did I do well? What could I do better? What did I learn about the other side? What did I learn about myself? Keep a negotiation journal. Review it before your next deal. Over time, you will build the judgment that separates a good negotiator from a great one. That judgment is your most valuable trade asset.

Practical Reference: Tools, Tariffs, and Trade Data for African Negotiators

Foreign trade negotiation is not won by charisma alone. It is won by the negotiator who arrives at the table with the right tariff line, the correct sanitary certificate, the current exchange rate, and a clear picture of what the counterparty needs. This Practical Reference lesson equips you with the working tools that African exporters, importers, and trade officers use daily: tariff schedules, rules-of-origin calculators, sanitary and phytosanitary (SPS) checklists, and live trade data platforms. The focus is on the practical, business-first application of these instruments in real African trade corridors.

SAFETY WARNING SPS AND FOOD SAFETY COMPLIANCE

Agricultural and food products traded across African borders are subject to mandatory sanitary and phytosanitary (SPS) controls. Non-compliance can result in border rejection, destruction of consignments, fines, or suspension of export privileges. Before negotiating any agricultural contract, verify:

  • Phytosanitary certificate from the national plant protection organisation (IPPC model).
  • Veterinary health certificate for animal products (WOAH/OIE standards).
  • Maximum residue limits (MRLs) for pesticides per Codex Alimentarius.
  • Aflatoxin limits for groundnuts, maize, and cottonseed often 410 ppb in EU markets.
  • Cold-chain integrity for perishables; temperature log evidence may be required at destination.
  • Fumigation and pest-free certification for stored commodities.

Sources: FAO/WHO Codex Alimentarius (2023); IPPC (2022); WOAH Terrestrial Animal Health Code (2023).

Using the Tools: A Business-First Workflow

Start with the market. Before you negotiate price, know the landed cost. Use ITC Trade Map or UN Comtrade to identify the current import volume, average unit value, and top suppliers for your product in the target market. If you are exporting Kenyan avocado to Egypt, for example, you need to know Egypt's current import tariff under the AfCFTA, the seasonal demand window, and the competing suppliers (Peru, Chile, South Africa). This data is free and publicly available.

Next, confirm the tariff line. A single digit error in the HS code can change your duty from 0% to 35%. Verify the HS code with your national customs authority and cross-check against the importing country's tariff schedule. Under the AfCFTA, tariff concessions are country-specific and product-specific. Do not assume that because a product is liberalised in one REC, it is liberalised across the continent.

Then, lock down SPS compliance. This is where most African agricultural exports fail. The cost of a rejected consignment is not just the lost sale it is the freight, the insurance, the storage, and the reputational damage with the buyer. Build SPS compliance into your production cost from the start, not as an afterthought at the port.

Finally, negotiate with data. When you can show a buyer that your product meets Codex MRLs, that your cold chain is certified, and that your tariff line is 0% under AfCFTA, you are no longer asking for a favour. You are offering a verified, compliant, cost-competitive supply. That is the position from which African negotiators win long-term contracts.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Verify the HS code confirm with national customs and the importing country's tariff schedule before quoting.
  2. Check AfCFTA tariff concessions confirm whether your product and destination country are covered by a phased or immediate concession.
  3. Confirm SPS requirements phytosanitary, veterinary, MRLs, aflatoxin limits, and cold-chain standards for the destination market.
  4. Calculate landed cost include freight, insurance, duties, port charges, and inland transport before agreeing a price.
  5. Validate the buyer check trade references, payment history, and whether the buyer is a registered importer in the destination country.
  6. Agree INCOTERMS specify FOB, CIF, or DAP clearly; ambiguity here causes most payment disputes.
  7. Secure payment terms use letters of credit, advance payment, or confirmed irrevocable LC for first-time buyers.
  8. Document everything certificates, inspection reports, temperature logs, and correspondence. If it is not written, it did not happen.
