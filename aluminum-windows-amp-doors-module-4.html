1. Using a Mitre Saw for Accurate Cuts

A mitre saw is your most important tool for cutting aluminum profiles at precise angles. In Africa, most workshops use a 10-inch or 12-inch compound mitre saw with a carbide-tipped blade specifically for non-ferrous metals. Before you start, check that the blade is sharp and has at least 60 teethdull blades will grab the aluminum and cause dangerous kickback. Set your saw on a sturdy workbench and clamp it down. Never cut aluminum on a saw that is not secured.

For window and door frames, you will mostly cut 45-degree angles for corners and 90-degree cuts for T-joints and sills. To set a 45-degree cut, loosen the mitre gauge lock, rotate the table until the pointer aligns exactly with the 45-degree mark, then tighten the lock. Always test on a scrap piece first. Measure the length of your cut from the longest point of the angle (the heel) to the opposite end. Use a tape measure and mark with a fine permanent marker or a scribe. A common mistake is measuring from the short point, which makes the frame too small.

When cutting, wear safety glasses and ear protection. Hold the profile firmly against the fence and the table. Start the saw, let it reach full speed, then lower the blade slowly and steadily. Do not force itlet the teeth do the work. For smooth cuts, use a few drops of cutting fluid or kerosene on the blade. After each cut, release the trigger and wait for the blade to stop completely before lifting the arm.

In many African workshops, power supply can be unstable. If you use a generator, ensure it delivers at least 2000 watts for a 10-inch saw. Also, keep a spare blade on hand. A sharp blade cuts faster, lasts longer, and gives you clean edges that need less deburring. Practice on scrap pieces until you can consistently get square, smooth cuts. That skill will save you time and material on every job.

2. Cutting Aluminum Profiles Safely

Aluminum may look soft, but it can be dangerous if you cut it carelessly. The biggest risks are kickback, flying chips, and cuts from sharp edges. Before you cut any profile, inspect it for bends or twists. A bent profile will bind in the saw and can kick back violently. Also, remove any screws or hardware.

Wear the right personal protective equipment (PPE): safety glasses, ear muffs or plugs, and gloves that fit snugly. Never wear loose clothing or jewelry that can get caught in the blade. Tie back long hair. Keep your hands at least 15 cm (6 inches) away from the blade path. Use a push stick for short pieces.

When cutting, support both ends of the profile. If the piece is long, use roller stands or have a helper hold the far endbut the helper must stand to the side, not in line with the blade. Never reach across the saw while it is running. Make sure the blade guard works freely and returns to cover the blade when you release the handle. If the guard sticks, fix it before cutting.

Aluminum chips are hot and sharp. They can fly into your eyes or skin. Always wear safety glasses, not just regular glasses. After cutting, use a brush or vacuum to clean the saw tablenever use your bare hands. Also, be aware of the noise. A mitre saw can exceed 100 decibels. Prolonged exposure without hearing protection can cause permanent hearing loss.

In Africa, many workshops have limited space. Keep your cutting area clear of other workers and flammable materials. Store aluminum profiles horizontally on a rack, not leaning against a wall where they can fall. If you use a circular saw instead of a mitre saw, clamp the profile securely and use both hands on the saw. Never cut aluminum with a wood bladeit will clog and overheat. Use a blade designed for non-ferrous metals. Finally, unplug the saw before changing blades or doing any maintenance. Safety habits protect you and your team.

3. Deburring and Cleaning Cut Ends

After cutting aluminum profiles, the cut ends will have sharp burrstiny rough edges that can cut your hands and prevent frames from fitting together tightly. Deburring is the process of removing these burrs. It takes only a minute per cut but makes a huge difference in the quality of your finished window or door.

The simplest tool for deburring is a deburring tool with a sharp blade, available at most hardware stores. You can also use a fine flat file (a 10-inch mill file works well) or a piece of emery cloth. For inside edges, a round file or a deburring tool with a curved blade is best. Hold the profile firmly in a vice or clamp it to your workbench. Run the deburring tool or file along the cut edge at a slight angle, applying light pressure. Do not gouge the aluminumjust remove the raised burr. Two or three passes are usually enough. For a professional finish, follow with a fine file to smooth any remaining roughness.

After deburring, clean the cut end. Use a brush or compressed air to remove aluminum chips. If you don't have compressed air, a clean paintbrush works. Then wipe the end with a rag dampened with mineral spirits or acetone to remove cutting fluid and dust. This step is important because any chips left inside the profile can rattle or block hardware later.

For corners that will be joined with cleats or corner connectors, check that the deburred ends are flat and square. You can test by placing a square against the cut. If light shows through, the cut is not squareyou may need to recut. Deburring also prepares the surface for welding or for applying silicone sealant. A clean, burr-free edge allows sealant to adhere properly and prevents leaks.

In many African workshops, deburring is skipped to save time, but it leads to callbacks and unhappy customers. Make it a habit: cut, deburr, clean. Keep your deburring tools sharp and within reach. A small investment in a good deburring tool will pay off in faster assembly and better-looking windows and doors.

4. Marking and Punching Holes for Hardware

Accurate marking and punching are critical for installing handles, hinges, locks, and other hardware. A hole that is even 2 mm off can make a window hard to open or a door that doesn't latch. Start by studying the hardware instructions. Every handle, hinge, and lock comes with a template or a specified hole size and spacing. For example, a typical window handle requires two M5 screws spaced 43 mm apart, with a 10 mm hole for the spindle. Write these numbers down.

Use a tape measure, a square, and a fine-point permanent marker or a scribe to mark the hole centers. Measure from a reference edgeusually the inside face of the profile. For hinges on doors, measure from the top and bottom of the door stile. Double-check your marks before punching. A good trick is to mark with a center punch: place the punch on the mark and tap it lightly with a hammer to create a small dimple. This keeps the drill bit from wandering.

For punching, you can use a drill press for precision, or a hand drill with a sharp HSS (high-speed steel) bit. For aluminum, use a bit with a 118-degree point and a slow speed (around 500-1000 RPM). Apply a few drops of cutting fluid or kerosene. Clamp the profile securelynever hold it with your hand. Start with a small pilot hole (3 mm) then step up to the final size. For large holes, like a 25 mm hole for a lock cylinder, use a hole saw or a step drill bit. Step drills are ideal because they cut clean, round holes in thin aluminum.

After drilling, deburr the hole on both sides. Use a deburring tool or a larger drill bit turned by hand. Then clean away all chips. Test-fit the hardware before assembling the frame. If a screw doesn't go in straight, the hole may be misalignedfix it now, not after the frame is welded. In Africa, where hardware may be imported and expensive, measure twice and cut once. Keep a notebook with the hole patterns for the hardware you use most often. That way, you can mark and punch quickly and consistently.

5. Fabricating Window Frames Step by Step

Fabricating a window frame is like building a puzzleeach piece must be cut and prepared correctly before assembly. Follow these steps for a standard casement or sliding window.

Step 1: Measure the opening. Measure the width and height at three points (top, middle, bottom for width; left, middle, right for height). Use the smallest measurement. Subtract 10 mm for clearance and expansion. This is your frame's outside dimension.

Step 2: Cut the profiles. For a fixed window, you need two vertical jambs and two horizontal head and sill pieces. Cut them at 45 degrees for a mitred corner, or 90 degrees if you use corner cleats. For sliding windows, you also need tracks and interlocks. Cut all pieces to length, allowing for the corner joint. For a 45-degree mitre, the length of each piece is the outside dimension minus twice the profile width. Always cut one piece and test-fit before cutting the rest.

Step 3: Deburr and clean all cut ends. Then mark and punch holes for corner connectors, handles, and any reinforcement. For strength, insert aluminum corner cleats or use a corner crimping machine. If you don't have a crimper, you can use L-shaped brackets screwed into the profiles.

Step 4: Assemble the frame on a flat surface. Start with the bottom sill and one jamb. Apply a small bead of silicone sealant in the joint, then push the pieces together. Check for square by measuring diagonalsthey must be equal. If not, adjust until they are. Then attach the other jamb and the head. Clamp the frame together.

Step 5: Secure the corners. If using screws, drill pilot holes and drive self-tapping screws through the corner. For a cleaner look, use a corner crimper to compress the cleats. Wipe away excess sealant.

Step 6: Install the glass and gaskets. Cut the glass 5 mm smaller than the frame opening. Place rubber gaskets or glazing tape around the glass, then snap the glass into the frame. For sliding windows, install the rollers and tracks.

Step 7: Check operation. Open and close the window several times. Adjust hinges or rollers if needed. Finally, clean the frame with a soft cloth and mild detergent. In Africa, where dust and heat are common, use UV-resistant gaskets and stainless steel screws to prevent corrosion. A well-made frame will last for decades.

6. Fabricating Door Frames Step by Step

Door frames are larger and heavier than window frames, so they need extra care in fabrication. Here is a step-by-step method for a standard aluminum door frame.

Step 1: Measure the door opening. Measure width and height at several points. Use the smallest measurement. Subtract 15 mm for clearance and expansion. This is your frame's outside dimension. For a single door, you need two vertical jambs, one head, and one sill (if it's an exterior door). For double doors, add a mullion in the center.

Step 2: Cut the profiles. Door profiles are usually thicker (e.g., 2 mm wall thickness) than window profiles. Use a mitre saw with a sharp blade. For 45-degree corners, cut each piece to the outside dimension minus twice the profile width. For 90-degree corners, cut to exact length. Always cut one piece and test-fit. Deburr and clean all cut ends.

Step 3: Mark and punch holes for hinges, locks, and handles. A typical door hinge requires three holes on the jamb and three on the door leaf. Use a template or measure carefully. For the lock, you need a 25 mm hole for the cylinder and a 50 mm hole for the handle. Punch and drill as described in Lesson 4.

Step 4: Assemble the frame on a flat surface. Start with the sill (if used) and one jamb. Apply silicone sealant in the joint. Insert corner cleats or brackets. Check square by measuring diagonals. Then add the head and the other jamb. Clamp the frame.

Step 5: Secure the corners. Use a corner crimper for a strong, clean joint. If you don't have one, use L-brackets and self-tapping screws. For extra strength, you can weld the corners, but this requires a TIG welder and skill. Most African workshops use mechanical fasteners.

Step 6: Install the door leaf. Hang the door on its hinges. Check that it swings freely and closes flush. Adjust hinge positions if needed. Install the lock and handle. Test the lock several times.

Step 7: Finish and seal. Apply weatherstripping around the frame to keep out dust and water. For exterior doors, apply a bead of silicone sealant between the frame and the wall. Clean the frame with a soft cloth. In hot African climates, use thermal breaks if possible to reduce heat transfer. Always use stainless steel screws and UV-resistant seals. A properly fabricated door frame will give years of service.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Cutting & Fabrication Basics for Aluminium Windows & Doors

In African aluminium fabrication workshops from Nairobi's Industrial Area to Lagos' Ikeja and Johannesburg's Selby the difference between a profitable window contract and a loss-making one is rarely the machine. It is the discipline applied to cutting, punching, and assembly. This Practical Reference lesson consolidates the six core lessons in this module into a single shop-floor guide you can pin above the mitre saw. It assumes you are running a small-to-medium fabrication business: 215 staff, one or two mitre saws, a punch press or drill station, and a client base of contractors, homeowners, and developers.

Everything here is written business-first. Every tolerance, every deburring pass, every punch mark has a cost or a revenue consequence. Read it once, then use it as a checking tool before every production run.

1. Setting Up the Mitre Saw for Repeatable Accuracy

Aluminium window and door profiles are cut at 45 for mitred corners or 90 for T-joints and transoms. The saw must be calibrated before every shift, not every week. A 0.5 error over a 1,200 mm profile produces a visible gap at the corner the single most common reason clients reject a finished frame.

Calibration sequence:

  1. Lock the saw head down and check the blade is square to the fence using an engineer's square.
  2. Cut two scrap pieces at 45, butt them together on a flat surface, and check with a square.
  3. If light passes through the joint, adjust the mitre detent screw by a quarter turn and repeat.
  4. Record the setting on the machine with a paint marker so the next operator knows the baseline.

For high-volume work (estate windows, school blocks), invest in a digital angle gauge (KES 4,0008,000 in Nairobi, similar in Accra and Lagos). It pays back on the first rejected batch it prevents.

2. Cutting Aluminium Profiles Safely

Aluminium cuts fast, throws fine chips, and produces a burr that will slice a hand. The safety rules are non-negotiable in a workshop where staff are paid by output.

SAFETY WARNING READ BEFORE EVERY CUT
  • Eye protection is mandatory. Aluminium chips cause permanent eye injury. Safety glasses alone are not enough wear a full face shield when cutting extruded profiles with a circular saw.
  • Never cut without clamping. Hand-held profiles can be thrown by the blade. Always clamp the profile against the fence before lowering the blade.
  • Use the correct blade. A negative-rake, carbide-tipped blade designed for non-ferrous metals. A wood blade will grab, overheat, and shatter.
  • Check the blade guard. A disabled or wedged guard is a fatality waiting to happen. If the guard does not return freely, stop and repair before cutting.
  • No loose clothing, no gloves near the blade. Gloves can catch and pull the hand in. Bare hands, clean and dry, on the profile only.
  • Hearing protection. Sustained exposure above 85 dB causes permanent loss. Mitre saws routinely exceed this.

Cutting sequence for a typical window frame (four sides): measure twice, mark once, cut the two longest profiles first, then the two shortest. Off-cuts longer than 300 mm go into a labelled rack by profile code they are revenue, not waste. In many African workshops, off-cut recovery alone saves 812% of monthly aluminium spend.

3. Deburring and Cleaning Cut Ends

Every cut end carries a burr. If you do not remove it, three things happen: the corner joint will not close, the punch die will misalign, and the powder-coat or anodised finish will chip at the joint. Deburring is a 20-second operation per cut end that protects the whole downstream process.

Method: use a deburring tool or a fine flat file at 3045 to the cut face, two or three light passes, then a nylon brush. Do not use a grinding wheel it removes too much material and changes the profile geometry. Wipe the end with a clean lint-free cloth to remove swarf before assembly.

4. Marking and Punching Holes for Hardware

Hardware positions handles, hinges, friction stays, locks are dictated by the system supplier's technical manual. Never guess. A 2 mm error in a hinge position becomes a 10 mm misalignment at the sash edge, and the window will not close.

Punching is faster and more repeatable than drilling for high-volume production. Set the punch press stop once, verify with a test piece, and lock it. For low-volume or custom work, a drill press with a depth stop is acceptable, but mark every hole with a centre punch first a wandering drill bit on anodised aluminium is expensive to hide.

5. Fabricating Window Frames Step by Step

  1. Cut: two verticals and two horizontals at 45, deburred.
  2. Punch: corner cleat holes at 45 mm from each end on all four profiles.
  3. Assemble: insert corner cleats, tap lightly with a rubber mallet, check square.
  4. Fix: crimp or screw the cleats per system specification.
  5. Seal: apply a bead of silicone sealant at the internal corner before final tightening.
  6. Glaze: fit gaskets, insert glass, secure with beads or clips.
  7. QC: check diagonal measurements (they must be equal within 1 mm), check opening clearance, check drainage slots.

Target cycle time for a 1,200 1,200 mm casement: 2535 minutes per unit with a two-person team. Anything above 45 minutes means a process problem, not a skill problem.

6. Fabricating Door Frames Step by Step

  1. Cut: two jambs and one head at 45 or 90 per design; deburr all ends.
  2. Punch: hinge positions on the jamb (typically 150 mm, centre, and 150 mm from bottom for a three-hinge door), lock keep, and threshold fixing holes.
  3. Assemble: head to jambs using corner cleats or brackets; check square.
  4. Reinforce: fit a galvanised steel or aluminium sub-frame at the threshold for heavy doors.
  5. Hang door: align hinges, check 35 mm perimeter clearance, check swing.
  6. Fit hardware: lock, handle, closer, and any restrictor stay.
  7. QC: check diagonal, check clearance, check lock engagement, check threshold seal.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Mitre saw calibrated with engineer's square and test cut before first production piece.
  • Blade correct for aluminium (negative rake, carbide tip), guard functioning, blade sharp.
  • Eye, ear, and face protection worn by every operator, every cut.
  • Profile clamped against fence never hand-held.
  • Every cut end deburred and wiped before assembly.
  • Hardware positions verified against system manual before punching.
  • Frame diagonals measured and equal within 1 mm before glazing or hanging.
  • Off-cuts over 300 mm labelled and racked by profile code.
  • Final QC sign-off recorded on job card with operator name and date.
