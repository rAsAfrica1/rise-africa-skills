1. Selecting improved sorghum varieties: SAMSORG, ICSV, and local landraces

When choosing sorghum, you have three main options: improved varieties from research stations, local landraces passed down through generations, and hybrids. SAMSORG varieties, developed in Nigeria, are popular in West Africa. For example, SAMSORG 17 is a short-season variety (75-80 days) with good yield (2.5-3 t/ha) and tolerance to Striga. ICSV varieties from ICRISAT, like ICSV 111, are medium-maturity (90-110 days) and high-yielding (3-4 t/ha) with good grain quality. Local landraces like 'Kaura' in Nigeria or 'Macia' in Mozambique are adapted to local conditions, taste preferred, and often fetch higher market prices, but yield lower (1-1.5 t/ha).

To select, first identify your goal: home consumption, market, or both. For market, improved varieties with white grain and high yield are better. For home use, landraces may be preferred for taste. Check with your local extension officer or research station for varieties recommended in your area. In West Africa, SAMSORG 14, 17, and 40 are common. In East Africa, ICSV 111, 400, and 705 are grown. In Southern Africa, Macia and ICSR 93034 are popular.

Always buy certified seed from a reputable source. Certified seed is tested for germination, purity, and disease. It may cost more (e.g., 2-3 USD per kg vs. 1 USD for saved seed), but yields are higher and more reliable. If you use landraces, select seed from healthy, high-yielding plants at harvest and store properly.

Consider disease resistance. In areas with Striga, choose Striga-resistant varieties like SAMSORG 40 or ICSV 1007. For drought-prone areas, choose drought-tolerant types like ICSV 111. For bird damage, choose varieties with tight panicles or awns.

Finally, test any new variety on a small plot before planting widely. Compare yield, taste, and market acceptance. Keep records of planting date, rainfall, and yield to make informed decisions next season.

2. Selecting improved millet varieties: SOSAT, ICMV, and pearl millet types

Millet, especially pearl millet, is a staple in dry areas of Africa. Improved varieties like SOSAT (from ICRISAT) and ICMV (from ICRISAT) offer higher yields and disease resistance. SOSAT varieties, such as SOSAT-C88, are early maturing (65-75 days), high-yielding (1.5-2.5 t/ha), and tolerant to downy mildew. ICMV varieties, like ICMV 221, are medium maturing (80-90 days) and yield 2-3 t/ha with good grain quality. Local pearl millet types, like 'Souna' in Senegal or 'Okashana' in Namibia, are well-adapted but yield less (0.8-1.2 t/ha).

When selecting, match maturity to your rainfall. In the Sahel, where rains last 60-90 days, choose early SOSAT types. In areas with 90-120 days of rain, ICMV types perform well. In Southern Africa, where rains are 90-120 days, varieties like Okashana 1 (early) and Kangara (medium) are recommended.

Check for resistance to downy mildew, a major disease. Improved varieties are usually resistant. Also consider grain color: white grain fetches higher market price, while gray or brown may be preferred for local dishes. For fodder, choose tall varieties like ICMV 221, which produce more stover.

Buy certified seed from agrodealers, research stations, or seed companies. In West Africa, SOSAT varieties are available from ICRISAT and national seed services. In East Africa, ICMV varieties are distributed by KALRO in Kenya and NARO in Uganda. In Southern Africa, seed companies like Seed Co sell pearl millet varieties.

If you save seed from improved varieties, they may not remain pure if cross-pollinated with other millets. Pearl millet is cross-pollinated, so to maintain purity, isolate your seed plot by at least 200 meters from other millet fields. For local landraces, select seed from healthy plants with large heads and store properly.

Always test germination before planting. Take 100 seeds, place between moist cloths, and count how many sprout in 5-7 days. Good seed should have at least 85% germination. If lower, increase seeding rate or buy new seed.

3. Matching variety maturity to your rainfall pattern (short, medium, long)

The most important decision in choosing a variety is matching its maturity to your rainfall. If you plant a long-maturing variety in a short-rainfall area, it will dry up before grain fill. If you plant a short-maturing variety in a long-rainfall area, you lose yield potential. So, know your rainfall pattern.

In West Africa, the Sahel has a short rainy season (60-90 days). Choose early varieties: sorghum like SAMSORG 17 (75-80 days) or millet like SOSAT-C88 (65-75 days). The Sudan savanna has 90-120 days: choose medium varieties like ICSV 111 (90-110 days) or ICMV 221 (80-90 days). The Guinea savanna has 120-180 days: choose long varieties like SAMSORG 40 (120-130 days) or local landraces.

In East Africa, the semi-arid areas of Kenya and Tanzania have 60-90 days of rain. Plant early sorghum like Gadam (65-70 days) or millet like Okashana 1 (75-80 days). In higher rainfall areas (90-120 days), plant medium varieties like ICSV 400 (100-110 days).

In Southern Africa, the rainy season is 90-120 days in most areas. Early varieties like Macia (85-95 days) are popular. In drier parts of Namibia and Botswana, early millet like Okashana 1 (75-80 days) is grown.

To determine your rainfall pattern, check with your local meteorological office or extension officer. They can tell you the average start and end of rains, and the length of the growing season. Also, look at when farmers in your area plant and harvest. If they plant in June and harvest in September, that's about 90 days.

If you have irrigation, you can grow longer-maturing varieties. But for rainfed farming, always choose a variety that matures 1-2 weeks before the rains normally end. This ensures grain fills before dry spells. Also, consider planting date: if you plant early, you can use a longer variety; if you plant late, switch to a shorter variety.

Always keep a few seeds of different maturities to hedge against unpredictable rains. In a year with late rains, a short variety may save your harvest.

4. Where to buy certified seed in West, East, and Southern Africa

Certified seed is your best guarantee of good germination, purity, and yield. It may cost more, but the returns are higher. Here is where to find certified sorghum and millet seed in different regions.

In West Africa, major sources include ICRISAT in Mali, Niger, and Nigeria, which distributes improved varieties through national agricultural research systems (NARS). In Nigeria, the National Agricultural Seeds Council (NASC) licenses seed companies like Premier Seeds, Value Seeds, and Da-Allgreen. In Mali, the Institut d'Economie Rurale (IER) and seed cooperatives sell certified seed. In Senegal, the Institut Sngalais de Recherches Agricoles (ISRA) and private companies like Seed Co West Africa. You can also buy from agrodealers affiliated with the West Africa Seed Alliance (WASA).

In East Africa, the Kenya Agricultural and Livestock Research Organization (KALRO) produces and sells certified seed of sorghum and millet. In Tanzania, the Tanzania Agricultural Research Institute (TARI) and seed companies like Seed Co Tanzania and Beula Seeds. In Uganda, the National Agricultural Research Organisation (NARO) and companies like Otis Seeds. In Ethiopia, the Ethiopian Seed Enterprise (ESE) and cooperatives. In Rwanda, the Rwanda Agriculture Board (RAB) and seed companies. You can also find certified seed at agrodealer shops in major towns.

In Southern Africa, Seed Co (operating in Zimbabwe, Zambia, Malawi, Mozambique) sells certified sorghum and millet seed. In South Africa, Pannar Seed and Agricol. In Namibia, the Namibia Seed Company and the Ministry of Agriculture. In Botswana, the Department of Agricultural Research and Seed Multiplication. In Mozambique, the Instituto de Investigao Agrria de Moambique (IIAM) and private companies. In Zimbabwe, the Seed Co and National Tested Seeds.

To find a local seller, ask your extension officer or visit the nearest research station. You can also search online for 'certified sorghum seed [your country]' or contact the national seed authority. Always buy seed in sealed, labeled bags with the variety name, lot number, germination percentage, and expiry date. Avoid buying seed from open markets or unknown sources, as it may be fake or poor quality.

When buying, check the label for the germination percentage (should be at least 85%) and the date of testing. If the seed is more than one year old, germination may be lower. Store seed in a cool, dry place until planting.

5. Testing seed germination at home before planting

Before you plant, test your seed to know if it will grow. This simple test saves you from planting dead seed and losing a season. You need: a sample of your seed, a clean cloth or paper towel, water, and a warm place.

Step 1: Take a random sample of 100 seeds from your seed lot. If you have less than 100, use 10 seeds and multiply the result by 10. Step 2: Moisten the cloth or paper towel with clean water. It should be damp, not dripping. Step 3: Spread the seeds evenly on the cloth, then fold the cloth over them. You can also place seeds between two layers of cloth. Step 4: Put the cloth in a plastic bag or container to keep it moist. Place it in a warm spot (20-30C), away from direct sunlight. Step 5: Check daily and add water if the cloth dries out. Step 6: After 5-7 days (for sorghum) or 3-5 days (for millet), count how many seeds have sprouted. A seed is sprouted when a small root or shoot appears.

Calculate germination percentage: (number of sprouted seeds / total seeds) x 100. For example, if 85 out of 100 seeds sprout, germination is 85%. Good seed should have at least 85% germination. If it is 70-85%, you can still plant but increase your seeding rate by 20-30%. If below 70%, buy new seed or plant a different crop.

For a more accurate test, do three samples of 100 seeds each and average the results. Also, test seed from different bags if you have many. If you find many abnormal seedlings (e.g., no root, twisted shoot), the seed may be diseased or damaged.

This test works for both sorghum and millet. For millet, seeds are smaller, so be careful when counting. You can also use a tray with soil: plant 100 seeds in rows, water, and count seedlings after 7-10 days. This is closer to field conditions but takes more space.

Always test before planting, especially if you saved seed from last season or bought from an unknown source. It takes only a week and can save you from a failed crop. If germination is low, you can still use the seed for fodder or compost, but not for grain production.

6. Storing seed safely for next season without chemicals

Storing seed without chemicals is possible if you control moisture, temperature, and pests. Good storage keeps germination high for 1-2 years. Here is how to do it.

First, dry your seed properly. After harvest, dry seed in the sun on a clean mat or tarpaulin for 3-5 days until moisture content is 10-12% for sorghum and 8-10% for millet. To test, bite a seed: it should crack, not dent. Or put seed in a bottle with salt: if salt sticks to the seed, it's too wet. Dry until salt falls off.

Second, clean the seed. Remove broken seeds, chaff, and insect-damaged seeds. These attract pests and reduce storage life. Winnow or use a sieve.

Third, choose a storage container. Use airtight containers like metal silos, plastic drums with tight lids, or clay pots. You can also use double-layered bags: put seed in a plastic bag, tie it, then put in a jute bag. For small quantities, use glass jars or plastic bottles with tight caps. Avoid paper or cloth bags, as pests can enter.

Fourth, add natural protectants. Mix seed with wood ash (1 part ash to 10 parts seed) or diatomaceous earth (1-2 kg per 100 kg seed). These dry out insects and are safe. You can also add dried neem leaves (1 kg per 100 kg seed) or dried chili powder. These repel insects. Do not use chemical pesticides if you want chemical-free storage.

Fifth, store in a cool, dry place. Keep containers off the floor on wooden pallets or stones. Keep away from walls. The ideal temperature is below 20C and relative humidity below 60%. In hot areas, store in the coolest part of the house, like a dark room or underground pit. You can also bury sealed clay pots in the ground for cool storage.

Sixth, check regularly. Every month, open containers and inspect for insects, mold, or moisture. If you see insects, remove them and sun-dry the seed again. If mold, discard the seed. If moisture, dry again.

Finally, label your seed with the variety name, date of storage, and germination percentage. Use different containers for different varieties to avoid mixing. With proper storage, you can keep seed for 2-3 years without chemicals. Always test germination before planting next season.

<h2>1. Improved Sorghum Varieties: SAMSORG, ICSV and Local Landraces</h2>

<p>SAMSORG is the sorghum series released by Nigeria's Institute for Agricultural Research (IAR) at Samaru, Zaria, in partnership with ICRISAT. SAMSORG lines are bred for the Sudan and Sahel savannahs and vary from short-season (SAMSORG 40, 41, 42) to medium and long-season types. ICSV lines are ICRISAT-bred tropically adapted sorghums; ICSV 111 and ICSV 400 are widely grown for their stay-green drought tolerance and grain quality. Local landraces red guinea, white kaura, mashila, durra types remain valuable for their taste, fodder and resilience, but usually yield 2040% less than improved lines under the same management.</p>

<p>From a business view, choose improved varieties for the market and landraces for niche or risk diversification. A trader buying for flour mills wants uniform grain colour and high extraction rate; a brewer wants tannin-free white sorghum. Confirm these traits before you plant, not after harvest.</p>

<h2>2. Improved Millet Varieties: SOSAT, ICMV and Pearl Millet Types</h2>

<p>SOSAT-C88 is a short-season, downy-mildew-resistant pearl millet released in Nigeria and now grown across the Sahel. ICMV lines (ICMV 155, ICMV 221, ICMV 167) are ICRISAT pearl millet varieties with early maturity and good grain yield under low rainfall. Pearl millet types range from early-maturing Sahel types (7590 days) to late-maturing types for higher-rainfall zones. Finger millet and fonio are also traded in East and West Africa but have separate seed systems.</p>

<p>For millet, the key commercial traits are grain colour (grey-green for some markets), panicle size for fodder value, and threshing ease. A dual-purpose SOSAT-C88 crop can sell grain and stover, raising gross margin per hectare.</p>

<h2>3. Matching Variety Maturity to Rainfall Pattern</h2>

<p>Rule of thumb: choose a variety whose days-to-maturity is at least 1520 days shorter than your average length of growing period. This buffer protects against a dry spell at flowering or grain fill. If your rains typically last 100 days, plant a 7585 day variety, not a 110-day one.</p>

<h2>4. Where to Buy Certified Seed</h2>

<p>Certified seed is your insurance policy. Buy from licensed agro-dealers, national seed councils, or IITA/ICRISAT-linked seed companies. Avoid grain from the market as seed it is usually a mixture, may be dead, and carries no germination guarantee.</p>

<h2>5. Testing Seed Germination at Home Before Planting</h2>

<p>You can test germination in 57 days with a cloth, a plate and clean water. Count 100 seeds at random, wrap them in a moist cloth, keep them warm and damp, and count how many sprout. If fewer than 80 germinate, buy fresh seed or increase your seeding rate. This simple test prevents the most expensive mistake in dryland farming: planting dead seed into a short rainy season.</p>

<h2>6. Storing Seed Safely for Next Season Without Chemicals</h2>

<p>Store only fully dry seed (moisture below 12%). Use airtight containers jerry cans, metal drums, or triple-layer hermetic bags. Add dried neem leaves, wood ash, or diatomaceous earth to deter storage insects. Keep the store cool, dark and off the floor. Label each container with variety name, lot number, germination percentage and date. Never mix varieties in one container; you lose traceability and market value.</p>

<p style="margin:0 0 6px 0;font-weight:bold;color:#8a1c16;font-size:15px"> SAFETY WARNING Seed treatment and storage chemicals</p>
<p style="margin:0;font-size:13px;color:#5a1a15">Never eat, sell or feed treated seed to livestock. Treated seed is often dyed red or blue and is poisonous. Store treated seed away from food, children and animals. If you use any chemical seed treatment, wear gloves, a mask and long sleeves, and follow the label exactly. Where possible, use the non-chemical storage methods above they are safer, cheaper and effective for one season.</p>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>
<ul style="font-family:Arial,Helvetica,sans-serif;font-size:14px;line-height:1.6">
<li>Know your average length of growing period before choosing a variety.</li>
<li>Pick a variety that matures 1520 days before your rains normally end.</li>
<li>Match improved varieties (SAMSORG, ICSV, SOSAT, ICMV) to your rainfall zone.</li>
<li>Keep at least one local landrace for risk diversification and niche markets.</li>
<li>Buy only certified seed with a valid label and lot number never market grain.</li>
<li>Run a 100-seed germination test 57 days before planting; reject below 80%.</li>
<li>Store seed dry, airtight, labelled and chemical-free where possible.</li>
<li>Record variety, source, date and germination rate for every lot you keep.</li>
</ul>
