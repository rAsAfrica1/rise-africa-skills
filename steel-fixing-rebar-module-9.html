1. Lesson 1: Introduction to Steel Fixing and Rebar in African Construction

Welcome to Module 9 of your Steel Fixing training. This module is designed to give you practical, hands-on knowledge that you can apply immediately on site, whether you are working in Lagos, Nairobi, Accra, Johannesburg, or any growing town across the continent. Steel fixing, also known as rebar installation, is the backbone of reinforced concrete construction. Without properly placed and tied reinforcement, concrete structures crack, collapse, or fail to carry the loads they were designed for. In Africa, where rapid urbanisation is driving a construction boom, skilled steel fixers are in high demand. This lesson introduces you to the trade, its importance, and the opportunities it creates. We will cover what rebar is, why it matters, the basic tools you need, and how to think like a professional steel fixer. By the end of this lesson, you will understand the role of a steel fixer and be ready to learn the technical skills in the following lessons. Let us begin with the basics. Rebar, short for reinforcing bar, is a steel rod or mesh used to strengthen concrete. Concrete is strong in compression but weak in tension. Steel is strong in both. When you combine them, you get reinforced concrete, a material that can resist bending, stretching, and heavy loads. In Africa, most buildings, bridges, roads, and water tanks use reinforced concrete. The steel fixer reads drawings, cuts and bends bars, places them in the formwork, and ties them together securely before concrete is poured. This is a critical job. A mistake in steel fixing can cost lives and money. The demand for steel fixers is growing because Africa is building fast. Governments are investing in infrastructure, private developers are putting up housing and commercial buildings, and communities are building schools and clinics. This means steady work for trained steel fixers. But competition exists. To stand out, you need skill, speed, accuracy, and safety awareness. You also need to understand the business side: how to price your work, manage a small team, and keep clients happy. This module will cover all of that. Let us look at the basic tools of the trade. You will need a tape measure, preferably a 5-metre or 8-metre steel tape. A chalk line or marking crayon for layout. A rebar cutter, which can be manual or electric. A rebar bender, also manual or electric. Pliers for tying wire. A tying hook, which is a simple tool that speeds up tying. A spirit level for checking alignment. A hammer for adjusting formwork and stakes. Safety gear: gloves, safety boots, hard hat, safety glasses, and high-visibility vest. In many African markets, you can buy these tools locally or online. Start with good quality manual tools and upgrade to electric ones as your business grows. Now, let us talk about the different types of rebar. The most common is mild steel rebar, which is smooth and used for light reinforcement. Then there is high-yield deformed rebar, which has ridges or ribs that help it bond with concrete. This is the standard for structural work. You will also find stainless steel rebar for special projects, and epoxy-coated rebar for areas with high corrosion risk, like coastal regions. In Africa, corrosion is a big issue because of humidity, salt, and poor drainage. Always check the rebar for rust, bends, or damage before using it. Rusty rebar can still be used if the rust is light and surface-level, but heavy rust or pitting weakens the bar. Clean it with a wire brush if needed. Next, understand the drawings. As a steel fixer, you must read bar bending schedules and structural drawings. These show the size, shape, length, and spacing of every bar. You will learn to read them in Lesson 2. For now, know that every project has a plan, and your job is to follow it exactly. If something is unclear, ask the engineer or foreman. Never guess. Guessing leads to rework, which wastes time and money. Safety is not optional. Steel fixing involves sharp edges, heavy loads, and working at heights. Always wear your PPE. Keep your work area tidy. Lift with your legs, not your back. Use gloves when handling rebar. Be careful with cutting and bending machines. In Africa, many sites lack proper safety enforcement, so you must protect yourself. A safe worker is a productive worker. Finally, think about your career path. You can start as a helper, then become a steel fixer, then a foreman, then a contractor. You can also specialise in post-tensioning, precast, or marine construction. The skills you learn here can take you across Africa and beyond. In the next lesson, we will dive into reading drawings and bar bending schedules. Make sure you have your notebook and tape measure ready. See you there.

2. Lesson 2: Reading Drawings and Bar Bending Schedules

In this lesson, you will learn how to read structural drawings and bar bending schedules. This is the most important skill for a steel fixer because it tells you exactly what to build. If you cannot read drawings, you cannot work independently. You will always need someone to explain, and that limits your income and opportunities. In Africa, many construction projects suffer from delays because workers cannot interpret drawings correctly. By mastering this skill, you become valuable and can command higher pay. Let us start with the basics. A structural drawing shows the layout of a building or structure. It includes plans, elevations, sections, and details. For steel fixing, you will mostly use the reinforcement plan, which shows where the rebar goes in slabs, beams, columns, and foundations. You will also use the bar bending schedule, often called the BBS. The BBS is a table that lists every bar in the project. It tells you the bar mark, diameter, shape, length, quantity, and bending dimensions. Think of the BBS as your shopping list and instruction manual combined. Before you start, gather all the drawings. Check the title block for the project name, drawing number, revision, and date. Always use the latest revision. Old drawings can cause serious errors. If you are unsure, ask the site engineer. Now, let us look at common symbols. Rebar is usually shown as a line with a number, like T12 or Y16. The letter indicates the type: T for high-yield, Y for mild steel, or sometimes R for ribbed. The number is the diameter in millimetres. So T12 means high-yield rebar, 12 mm diameter. You will also see spacing, like T12-200, which means 12 mm bars at 200 mm centres. Centres means the distance from the middle of one bar to the middle of the next. Sometimes you see T12-200 B1, which means bottom layer, first bar. Top bars are often marked T1 or T2. Stirrups and links are shown as closed shapes, often with a note like R10-150. That means 10 mm mild steel stirrups at 150 mm centres. Now, the bar bending schedule. Each row has a bar mark, like B1, C2, or S3. The mark corresponds to a shape on the drawing. The schedule gives the diameter, the shape code, the length of each leg, the total length, and the number required. Shape codes are standardised. For example, shape code 00 is a straight bar. Shape code 11 is an L-bar. Shape code 21 is a U-bar. Shape code 31 is a closed stirrup. You need to memorise the common shape codes. In Africa, many engineers use the British Standard or the South African standard. The shape codes are similar. Always check the project specification. When you read the BBS, pay attention to the bending dimensions. These are the lengths of the straight portions and the angles of the bends. The total length is the sum of all the legs minus the bend deductions. Bend deductions account for the elongation of steel when it is bent. For a 90-degree bend, the deduction is typically 2 times the diameter. For a 135-degree bend, it is 3 times the diameter. These deductions are important because if you cut the bar to the total length without deducting, the bar will be too long and will not fit. Let us practice with an example. Suppose you have a beam with a bar mark B1. The schedule says T16, shape code 11, leg lengths 500 mm and 3000 mm, total length 3450 mm, quantity 4. Shape code 11 is an L-bar. The total length is 500 + 3000 = 3500 mm. But because there is a 90-degree bend, you deduct 2 times the diameter, which is 2 x 16 = 32 mm. So the cut length is 3500 - 32 = 3468 mm. The schedule says 3450 mm, which is close enough. Always follow the schedule. If there is a discrepancy, ask. Now, let us talk about placing drawings. These show the actual layout of the rebar in the structure. You will see the bottom bars, top bars, distribution bars, and chairs. Chairs are small supports that hold the top bars at the correct height. You will also see laps. A lap is where two bars overlap to transfer force. Lap lengths depend on the bar diameter and the concrete grade. For example, a T12 bar in C25 concrete might need a lap of 40 times the diameter, which is 480 mm. Always check the drawing for lap lengths. In Africa, lap lengths are often specified in the general notes. Do not guess. Another important concept is cover. Cover is the distance from the outside of the concrete to the nearest rebar. Cover protects the steel from rust and fire. Typical cover is 25 mm for slabs, 40 mm for beams, and 50 mm for foundations. You use spacers or chairs to maintain cover. If cover is too small, the steel will corrode. If it is too large, the concrete may crack. Now, let us discuss how to use the drawings on site. First, study the drawings before you start. Make a list of all the bar marks and quantities. Cut and bend the bars according to the BBS. Label them clearly with tags or paint. Store them neatly. When you are ready to fix, lay out the bars according to the placing drawing. Start with the main bars, then the distribution bars. Tie them with tying wire. Check spacing and cover as you go. Do not wait until the end. In Africa, many sites have limited space, so organise your work area. Keep the drawings in a safe, dry place. Protect them from rain and mud. If you are working on a large project, you may need to make a bar list for each floor or section. This helps you track progress and avoid shortages. Finally, remember that drawings are a communication tool. If you see a mistake, report it immediately. Do not try to fix it yourself without approval. Engineers and architects have the final say. By reading drawings accurately, you save time, reduce waste, and build a reputation as a reliable steel fixer. In the next lesson, we will cover cutting, bending, and tying rebar. Practice reading a simple BBS today. Take a drawing from a small project and try to understand every bar. The more you practice, the faster you will become.

3. Lesson 3: Cutting, Bending, and Tying Rebar

Now that you can read drawings, it is time to get your hands dirty. In this lesson, you will learn how to cut, bend, and tie rebar correctly. These are the core skills of a steel fixer. In Africa, where labour is often manual, you must be efficient and accurate. A good steel fixer can cut and bend bars quickly without wasting material. Let us start with cutting. You need a rebar cutter. Manual cutters are common and affordable. They work like a large pair of pliers with a lever. You place the bar between the jaws and pull the handle. For larger bars, you may need a hydraulic cutter or an electric cut-off saw. Always wear safety glasses and gloves when cutting. Rebar can splinter and fly. Measure twice, cut once. Use your tape measure and mark the bar with a crayon or chalk. If you are cutting many bars of the same length, set up a stop block on your cutting bench. This ensures consistency. When cutting, support the bar on both sides to avoid bending. Cut perpendicular to the bar. After cutting, check the length. If it is wrong, do not use it for that location. Set it aside for another use. Waste is money. In Africa, rebar is expensive, so minimise offcuts. Plan your cuts to use the full length of the stock bar. Stock bars are usually 12 metres long. If you need 3-metre bars, you can get four from one stock bar with no waste. If you need 5-metre bars, you get two with 2 metres left over. Use that 2 metres for stirrups or small bars. Now, bending. You can bend rebar manually with a bending machine or a bending jig. A manual bender has a handle and a series of pins. You place the bar and pull the handle to bend it. For small bars, you can use a bending fork or even a strong piece of pipe. But for accurate work, use a proper bender. Electric benders are faster and easier, but they cost more. If you are starting out, buy a good manual bender. When bending, follow the shape codes from the BBS. Mark the bend points with chalk. For a 90-degree bend, align the mark with the bending pin. Pull the handle until the bar is at 90 degrees. Check with a square. For a 135-degree bend, which is common for stirrup hooks, bend slightly past 90 degrees and check with a protractor. Do not over-bend. Over-bending weakens the steel. If you bend a bar incorrectly, do not bend it back. Bending back and forth causes cracks and reduces strength. Cut it and use it for a shorter bar. Always bend bars cold. Do not heat them with a torch. Heating changes the steel properties and makes it brittle. In Africa, some workers heat bars to bend them easily. This is dangerous and unacceptable. Never do it. Now, tying. Tying is what holds the rebar cage together before and during the concrete pour. You use tying wire, which is usually 1.6 mm or 2 mm diameter black annealed wire. You also use a tying hook. The hook is a simple tool with a handle and a curved end. To tie, you cut a piece of wire about 150 to 200 mm long. You loop it around the intersection of two bars. You pull the ends tight with the hook and twist. The twist should be tight but not so tight that it breaks the wire. You can use a double loop for extra strength. There are different types of ties. The snap tie is quick. The figure-eight tie is strong. The saddle tie is good for heavy bars. Learn all of them. In Africa, many steel fixers use the snap tie because it is fast. But for critical structures, use a stronger tie. Tie every intersection? Not always. The drawing will specify. Usually, you tie every second or third intersection for slabs, and every intersection for columns and beams. But check the specification. If in doubt, tie more. A loose bar can move during pouring, causing honeycombing or exposure. When tying, keep the wire ends pointing away from the concrete surface. If they point outward, they can rust and stain the concrete. Bend them inward. Also, do not leave wire clippings on the formwork. They can rust and cause stains. Clean as you go. Now, let us talk about placing and fixing. You have cut, bent, and tied the bars into cages or mats. Now you need to place them in the formwork. For a slab, you lay the bottom mat first. Use chairs or spacers to maintain the correct cover. Then place the top mat. Use chairs to hold the top mat at the right height. For a beam, you assemble the cage on the ground or on a workbench, then lift it into the formwork. For a column, you assemble the cage and lower it into the formwork, or you build it in place. Always check the drawings for the correct orientation. Main bars go in the direction of the span. Distribution bars go perpendicular. Stirrups go around the main bars. In Africa, many projects use precast cages to save time. You can build cages at a central location and transport them to the site. This improves quality and speed. But you need a good lifting plan. Use a spreader beam to avoid bending the cage. Now, let us discuss quality control. After placing, check the following: bar diameter, spacing, cover, lap lengths, and tie tightness. Use a tape measure and a cover meter if available. If you find a mistake, fix it before pouring. Once concrete is poured, it is too late. Also, check that the rebar is clean. Remove mud, oil, and loose rust. Concrete does not bond well to dirty steel. If the rebar is wet, that is okay, but standing water should be removed. Finally, think about productivity. A skilled steel fixer can tie about 100 to 150 intersections per hour for simple work. With practice, you can increase speed. But do not sacrifice quality for speed. In Africa, word spreads fast. If you do good work, you get more contracts. If you do bad work, you lose clients. In the next lesson, we will cover safety and site management. For now, practice your cutting, bending, and tying. Get some scrap rebar and make a small cage. Time yourself. Improve your speed and accuracy. That is how you become a professional.

4. Lesson 4: Safety, Site Management, and Quality Control

Safety is not just a rule; it is a mindset. In this lesson, you will learn how to work safely, manage a site, and control quality. In Africa, construction sites can be hazardous. Many accidents happen because workers ignore safety or lack training. As a steel fixer, you must protect yourself and your team. Let us start with personal protective equipment, or PPE. You need a hard hat, safety boots, gloves, safety glasses, and a high-visibility vest. In some areas, you also need earplugs and dust masks. Wear your PPE every time you are on site. No exceptions. Your hard hat protects you from falling objects. Your boots protect your feet from sharp rebar and heavy loads. Your gloves protect your hands from cuts and wire punctures. Your glasses protect your eyes from flying chips. Your vest makes you visible to machine operators. If you are an employer, provide PPE for your workers. It is cheaper than an accident. Next, let us talk about common hazards. The biggest hazard is impalement. Rebar is sharp. If you fall on it, you can be seriously injured or killed. Always cap the ends of vertical bars with plastic caps or wooden blocks. Never leave exposed bars without protection. Another hazard is manual handling. Rebar is heavy. A 12-metre T16 bar weighs about 19 kilograms. A bundle can weigh hundreds of kilograms. Lift with your legs, not your back. Use two people for long bars. Use a trolley or a wheelbarrow for bundles. Do not drag bars across the ground. It damages the bars and can cause back injuries. Cutting and bending machines are also hazardous. Keep your hands away from the blades and pins. Use a push stick for small pieces. Never remove guards. If a machine is faulty, tag it out and report it. Do not use it. Working at heights is another risk. If you are fixing rebar on a scaffold or a high wall, use a safety harness. Make sure the scaffold is stable and has guardrails. Do not stand on the rebar itself. It can move or collapse. Now, let us discuss site management. A well-managed site is safe and productive. As a steel fixer, you should keep your work area clean and organised. Store rebar on timber bearers, not on the ground. This prevents rust and contamination. Sort bars by size and shape. Label them clearly. Keep your tools in a toolbox or a designated area. Do not leave tools lying around. They can be stolen or cause trips. Plan your work. Before you start, check the drawings, the formwork, and the rebar supply. Make sure you have enough tying wire and spacers. If you are working with a team, assign tasks clearly. One person can cut, another can bend, another can tie. This improves efficiency. Communicate with other trades. The carpenter needs to finish the formwork before you place rebar. The electrician may need to install conduits. Coordinate so you do not block each other. In Africa, many sites have limited supervision. You may be the most experienced person on site. Take charge. If you see an unsafe act, stop it. If you see a mistake, correct it. Do not wait for the engineer. Now, quality control. Quality is what keeps clients coming back. After you finish fixing, inspect your work. Check the following: bar diameter and type. Use a calliper or a tape measure. Check spacing. Use a tape measure. Check cover. Use a cover meter or a simple spacer check. Check laps. Make sure they are the correct length and are tied properly. Check ties. Make sure they are tight and the wire ends are bent inward. Check for missing bars. Compare with the drawing. Check for cleanliness. Remove any mud, oil, or loose rust. If you find a problem, fix it immediately. Do not leave it for someone else. Document your work. Take photos. Keep a record of the bars you used. This helps with billing and future projects. In Africa, many clients pay based on progress. Good records help you get paid faster. Also, think about the concrete pour. Before the pour, walk through the area with the engineer. Make sure everything is approved. During the pour, monitor the rebar. If a bar moves, fix it. If a tie breaks, retie it. Do not let workers walk on the rebar without protection. Use walkways or boards. After the pour, clean your tools and store them. Check the concrete for any exposed rebar. If you see exposed steel, report it. It needs to be treated or covered. Finally, let us talk about continuous improvement. After every project, review what went well and what went wrong. Did you waste material? Did you have an accident? Did the client complain? Learn from your mistakes. Invest in better tools. Train your team. Join a professional association if one exists in your country. Network with other steel fixers. Share knowledge. In Africa, the construction industry is growing. There is room for everyone who is skilled and reliable. By focusing on safety, management, and quality, you will build a strong reputation. In the next lesson, we will cover business skills: how to price your work, manage money, and grow your steel fixing business. For now, make safety your priority. A safe site is a profitable site.

5. Lesson 5: Business Skills for Steel Fixers in Africa

You have learned the technical skills. Now it is time to learn the business skills. Many steel fixers are excellent craftsmen but poor businesspeople. They struggle to find work, undercharge, or run out of money. This lesson will help you avoid those traps. We will cover pricing, estimating, managing cash flow, marketing, and growing your team. By the end, you will have a clear plan to turn your skill into a profitable business. Let us start with pricing. How much should you charge? There are two common methods: day rate and piece rate. Day rate means you charge per day. Piece rate means you charge per bar, per ton, or per square metre. In Africa, piece rate is common for steel fixing. For example, you might charge per kilogram of rebar fixed. Or per square metre of slab. To set your price, you need to know your costs. List all your costs: labour, tying wire, spacers, tools, transport, and overhead. Add a profit margin. A typical profit margin is 15 to 30 percent. But it depends on the market. Check what other steel fixers charge. Do not undercut too much. If you are too cheap, clients think you are desperate or unskilled. If you are too expensive, you lose work. Find a balance. Let us do an example. Suppose you are fixing a slab. The slab has 2 tons of rebar. You estimate it will take 4 workers 3 days to fix. Your labour cost is 4 workers x 3 days x daily wage. Say the daily wage is 20 USD. That is 240 USD. Tying wire and spacers cost 50 USD. Transport costs 30 USD. Overhead is 20 USD. Total cost is 340 USD. Add 25 percent profit, which is 85 USD. Your price is 425 USD. That is about 212 USD per ton. Is that reasonable? Check the local market. In many African cities, steel fixing rates range from 150 to 300 USD per ton. So 212 is competitive. Now, estimating. To win a job, you need to give a quote. A quote is a written price for the work. To prepare a quote, you need to read the drawings and calculate the quantities. Use the bar bending schedule. Calculate the total weight of rebar. The weight of a bar is calculated as: weight per metre = diameter squared divided by 162. For example, a 12 mm bar weighs 12 x 12 / 162 = 0.888 kg per metre. A 16 mm bar weighs 16 x 16 / 162 = 1.58 kg per metre. Multiply by the total length. Then add 5 to 10 percent for laps and waste. Then apply your rate. Always include a contingency. Things go wrong. Weather delays, rework, or missing bars. Add 10 percent to your time estimate. Also, state your terms. How long will the job take? When do you need payment? What is not included? For example, you may not include the cost of rebar itself. That is usually supplied by the client. Be clear. Now, cash flow. Cash flow is the money coming in and going out. Many steel fixers fail because they run out of cash before they get paid. To manage cash flow, ask for a deposit. A 30 to 50 percent deposit is normal. Use it to buy materials and pay your workers. Do not use it for personal expenses. Keep business and personal money separate. Open a bank account or a mobile money account for the business. Track every expense. Use a notebook or a simple app. At the end of each job, calculate your profit. If you are not making a profit, you are not in business. You are just busy. Now, marketing. How do clients find you? In Africa, word of mouth is powerful. Do good work. Be reliable. Finish on time. Treat clients with respect. Ask satisfied clients for referrals. Also, network with engineers, architects, and contractors. Attend construction events. Join WhatsApp groups for builders. Create a simple business card. Put your name, phone number, and what you do. If you have a smartphone, take photos of your work. Show them to potential clients. You can also advertise on local radio or online marketplaces. But the best marketing is a job well done. Now, growing your team. As you get more work, you will need helpers. Hire people who are willing to learn. Train them in safety and quality. Pay them fairly and on time. A happy team is productive. Do not hire too many people too fast. Start with one or two helpers. Teach them to cut, bend, and tie. As they improve, give them more responsibility. You can also subcontract to other steel fixers. But make sure they meet your quality standards. Finally, let us talk about contracts. Always have a written contract. It does not need to be complicated. It should state the scope of work, the price, the payment schedule, the start and end dates, and the responsibilities of both parties. Both parties should sign. Keep a copy. If there is a dispute, the contract protects you. In Africa, many deals are done verbally. That is risky. A simple contract prevents misunderstandings. Also, keep records of everything: quotes, invoices, receipts, and delivery notes. This helps with taxes and legal issues. Now, let us discuss challenges. You will face late payments. You will face price increases. You will face competition. How do you handle these? For late payments, follow up politely but firmly. Send reminders. If necessary, stop work until you are paid. For price increases, include a clause in your contract that allows you to adjust if material prices change significantly. For competition, focus on quality and speed. Do not compete on price alone. There will always be someone cheaper. But there are fewer who are reliable and skilled. Be the best. Finally, think about the future. You can grow from a sole trader to a registered company. You can buy your own bending and cutting machines. You can take on larger projects. You can train other steel fixers. You can even manufacture rebar cages for sale. The opportunities are endless. Africa is building. You are part of that. With the technical skills from this module and the business skills from this lesson, you are ready to succeed. Remember: safety first, quality always, and money matters. Go out there and build your business. Thank you for completing Module 9.

<div style="border:3px solid #c00000; background:#fff5f5; padding:16px; margin:20px 0; font-family:Arial, sans-serif;">
<strong style="color:#c00000; font-size:16px;"> SAFETY WARNING REBAR AND CONCRETE CONTACT</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#222;">Rebar is a hazardous material in three ways. First, cut ends are sharp and cause puncture wounds; tetanus risk is real on dusty sites with poor first aid. Keep a tetanus vaccination current and wear cut-resistant gloves. Second, fresh concrete is highly alkaline (pH 1213) and causes chemical burns and dermatitis; wash skin immediately with clean water and never kneel in wet concrete without waterproof trousers. Third, rebar rust and cement dust are respiratory irritants; wear a dust mask when cutting with power tools and work upwind of dust. Do not eat, drink, or smoke on the fixing deck without washing hands first. If concrete enters the eye, irrigate with clean water for 15 minutes and seek medical attention immediately. Report every cut, burn, or eye splash to your supervisor the same day.</p>
</div>

<h3>Business Workflow: From Enquiry to Final Payment</h3>

<ol>
<li><strong>Site visit and measurement.</strong> Take your own dimensions. Never quote from the client's sketch alone. Photograph the site, note access, power, water, and storage.</li>
<li><strong>Bill of quantities.</strong> Use the BBS to calculate total kilograms by bar size. Add 58% wastage for cutting losses.</li>
<li><strong>Pricing.</strong> Material + transport + labour + tools + overhead + profit margin (1525%). Present as a written quote with validity period (usually 14 days).</li>
<li><strong>Contract.</strong> One page is enough: scope, price, payment schedule, start and finish dates, who supplies materials, and who provides safety equipment.</li>
<li><strong>Mobilisation.</strong> Confirm steel delivery date before you move workers to site. Idle labour is your biggest hidden cost.</li>
<li><strong>Execution and records.</strong> Daily log: date, activity, kilograms fixed, workers on site, delays. Photograph each completed element before concrete pour.</li>
<li><strong>Inspection and handover.</strong> Walk the engineer through the work. Get sign-off in writing before the pour.</li>
<li><strong>Invoicing.</strong> Invoice against the agreed milestones. Follow up within 48 hours. Keep a simple ledger of payments received.</li>
</ol>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul>
<li> Bar bending schedule and latest drawing revision on site before cutting starts.</li>
<li> Steel delivered matches specification (bar diameter, grade, and mill certificate if required).</li>
<li> Cover blocks or spacers in place minimum 40 mm for columns and beams, 50 mm for footings in contact with ground.</li>
<li> Link spacing checked against drawing at every third link, not just the first.</li>
<li> Laps staggered and lap length verified (typically 4050 bar diameter for tension bars).</li>
<li> All ties tight, no protruding wire ends that could injure workers or puncture formwork.</li>
<li> Daily record of kilograms fixed, workers present, and any delay or variation.</li>
<li> Engineer sign-off obtained and photographed before concrete is ordered.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Managing Cash Flow on African Sites</h3>

<p>Most steel fixing businesses fail not because of bad work but because of bad cash flow. Contractors often pay 3060 days after invoicing. Your workers and your steel supplier want money now. The practical rules are:</p>

<ul>
<li>Ask for a 3050% mobilisation payment before you buy steel. This is normal practice and protects you from client default.</li>
<li>Never fund a job entirely from your own pocket. If the client refuses any advance, treat that as a warning sign.</li>
<li>Keep a separate business account or mobile money wallet. Do not mix job money with household money.</li>
<li>Build a 10% contingency into every quote for transport, breakage, and weather delays.</li>
<li>Keep receipts for every bag of tie wire, every blade, and every transport trip. These are tax-deductible and prove your costs if a client disputes a variation.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Scaling from Fixer to Sub-Contractor</h3>

<p>Once you can reliably fix 400600 kg per day with a small team, you can bid for whole-element packages: all columns on a floor, or all slab mesh on a house. This is where margins improve, because you are selling a completed scope, not a daily wage. To make that jump you need three things: a written method statement, a small set of your own tools (bar bender, cutter, spirit level, tape, tie hooks), and two or three reliable workers you have trained yourself. Register the business, open a business bank or mobile money account, and keep your tax compliance current. In most African markets, formal registration unlocks access to larger contractors, NGO construction projects, and government tenders.</p>
