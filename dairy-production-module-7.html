Lessons

1. Housing systems: freestall, tie-stall, and pasture-based

Choosing a housing system for your dairy cows depends on your climate, land, and budget. In Africa, three common systems are freestall, tie-stall, and pasture-based. Each has trade-offs in cost, cow comfort, and labour.

Freestall housing gives each cow a clean, dry lying area but allows her to move freely. A typical freestall is 1.2 m wide and 2.4 m long, with a curb to keep bedding in. You need 1 stall per cow. Alleyways behind the stalls should be 2.43 m wide for scraping. This system works well in hot climates if you add fans and misters. It costs more to build but reduces bedding use and keeps cows cleaner.

Tie-stall housing ties cows in individual stalls. Each stall is about 1.2 m wide and 1.8 m long. You need a neck rail and a manger. This system uses less space and is cheaper to build, but cows get less exercise and are more prone to lameness. It suits small farms with limited land, but you must clean stalls twice daily and provide daily exercise.

Pasture-based housing lets cows graze on grass. You need a rotational grazing plan with paddocks, a water trough in each paddock, and shade. In Africa, this is the cheapest system, but milk yield depends on grass quality. During dry seasons, you must supplement with hay or silage. A simple shade structure of 34 m per cow is essential. Pasture-based systems need less labour but more landabout 0.40.8 hectares per cow in good rainfall areas.

Many African farms combine systems: cows graze by day and are housed in freestalls at night. Choose based on your resources. If you have land, pasture is cheapest. If you have little land but can invest, freestall is best for cow comfort. Tie-stall is a low-cost option for a few cows, but it demands more daily labour. Always prioritise dry bedding, good ventilation, and easy access to feed and water.

2. Designing comfortable and efficient barns

A well-designed barn keeps cows cool, dry, and productive. Start with the site: build on high ground with good drainage, away from swamps and prevailing winds that carry odours. Orient the barn east-west to minimise direct sun on the long sides. In hot African climates, open-sided barns with high roofs (45 m at the ridge) allow hot air to rise and escape.

Space per cow is critical. For freestall barns, allow 810 m per cow including alleys. The feed alley should be 3.54 m wide for a tractor or feed cart. Water troughs need 6080 cm of linear space per 10 cows. Place them in alleys, not in stalls, and keep them clean. A cow drinks 60120 litres per day, so troughs must refill quickly.

Flooring: concrete is best for alleys because it's easy to scrape, but it's hard on hooves. Add grooves every 10 cm for traction. In stalls, use a 10 cm layer of sand, sawdust, or chopped straw. Rubber mats can reduce injuries but are expensive. Slopes: alleys should slope 12% toward a drain. Stalls should slope 2% toward the rear so urine drains away.

Ventilation is not optional. Without airflow, cows suffer heat stress and respiratory disease. In open barns, the roof overhang should be at least 1 m. Add ridge vents or cupolas. For closed barns, install fans every 810 m along the feed alley. A simple rule: if you can't feel a breeze on your face inside the barn, cows can't either.

Lighting: cows need 16 hours of light per day for milk production. Use LED bulbs or skylights. Provide 200 lux at cow level. Also, design a holding pen near the milking parlourit should hold 1.5 m per cow and have a sprinkler system for cooling. Keep the parlour clean and well-lit.

Finally, plan for expansion. Build a barn that can be extended. Use local materials like treated poles, clay bricks, or galvanised iron sheets. A simple, well-ventilated barn with good drainage will outperform an expensive, poorly designed one.

3. Manure management systems

A dairy cow produces 1520 tonnes of manure per year. If not managed, it causes flies, odours, and water pollution. But manure is also a valuable resourceit can fertilise crops, produce biogas, or be sold. Choose a system that fits your farm and climate.

Solid manure systems: In freestall or tie-stall barns, you scrape manure daily. Use a tractor with a scraper blade or a manual scraper. Push manure to a concrete storage pad. The pad should be 30 cm thick, with a 1% slope to a liquid collection pit. Store solid manure in a heap 1.52 m high. Turn it every two weeks to compost. Compost heats to 5565C, killing weed seeds and pathogens. After 23 months, it's ready to apply to fields.

Liquid manure systems: If you use flush water or have a slurry pit, you need a liquid storage tank. A covered tank prevents rain from diluting the slurry and reduces odours. Agitate before pumping. Use a slurry tanker or a hose reel to apply to fields. In Africa, many farms use a simple anaerobic digester to produce biogas for cooking and lighting. A fixed-dome digester for 10 cows costs about $1,500$3,000 and produces 23 m of gas per dayenough for a family.

Daily routine: Scrape alleys twice a day. Remove wet bedding from stalls daily. Add fresh bedding. Keep the manure pad away from wells and streamsat least 30 m. Flies breed in wet manure, so keep it dry. Use fly traps or parasitic wasps. For odour control, add lime or zeolite to the manure.

If you have limited land, consider selling manure to neighbouring crop farmers. A tonne of composted dairy manure can sell for $20$50. That turns a waste problem into income. Never dump manure into riversit kills fish and causes disease. A well-managed manure system keeps your farm clean, reduces disease, and improves soil fertility.

4. Feed storage and handling equipment

Feed is the biggest cost in dairy farmingup to 60% of production. Proper storage prevents spoilage, mould, and nutrient loss. You need three types of storage: for concentrates, for dry roughages (hay), and for silage.

Concentrates: Store in a dry, rodent-proof room. Use metal or plastic bins with tight lids. A 50 kg bag of dairy meal should be used within 4 weeks. Keep bags on pallets, 10 cm off the floor, and away from walls. Label each bin with the date of purchase. Never mix old and new feed. A simple feed bin made from a 200-litre drum with a lid works well. For larger farms, a bulk bin holds 12 tonnes and has an auger for dispensing.

Hay: Store in a dry, ventilated shed. Hay must be below 15% moisture before baling, or it will mould and catch fire. Stack bales on a pallet, leaving 30 cm between stacks and walls for air. A shed with a raised floor and open sides is ideal. In humid areas, add a roof vent. Hay stored properly can last 612 months. Check for heatingif the stack is warm, spread it out to dry.

Silage: This is fermented green forage. You need a siloa pit, bunker, or plastic tube. A bunker silo is cheapest: dig a trench 2 m deep, line with concrete or plastic, and pack chopped maize or grass. Cover with a plastic sheet and weigh it down with tyres. Anaerobic fermentation preserves the feed. After 34 weeks, it's ready. Feed out 1015 cm per day from the face to prevent spoilage. A tube silo (plastic bag) works for small farms50 tonnes capacity for about $300.

Handling equipment: You need a feed cart or wheelbarrow, a shovel, a feed scoop, and a scale. Weigh feed to avoid over- or under-feeding. A simple hanging scale costs $20. For silage, use a silage fork. For concentrates, a bucket. Keep equipment cleanmouldy feed left in a bucket contaminates fresh feed. Always store feed in a cool, dry place. Protect from rain and sun. Good storage saves money and keeps cows healthy.

5. Water systems and supply

Water is the most important nutrient for dairy cows. A lactating cow drinks 60120 litres per daymore in hot weather. She needs water available at all times. If she drinks less, milk yield drops immediately. Your water system must deliver clean, cool water to every cow, every day.

Sources: Boreholes, wells, rivers, dams, and municipal supply. Test water quality annually for bacteria, nitrates, and salts. Clean water has no odour and no visible particles. If using river water, filter and chlorinate. A simple sand filter removes sediment. For bacteria, add 25 mg of chlorine per litre. Let it sit for 30 minutes before use.

Storage: You need a tank that holds at least 2 days of water. For 20 cows, that's 4,000 litres. Use a black polyethylene tank to prevent algae growth. Place it on a raised platform 2 m high for gravity flow. Insulate or shade the tankwater above 25C reduces intake. A concrete tank is cheaper but must be covered.

Distribution: Use PVC pipes (2540 mm diameter) buried 30 cm deep to prevent damage and keep water cool. Each water trough should be 6080 cm long per 10 cows. Troughs must be easy to cleanuse a drain plug. Place troughs in the barn alley and in each paddock. For pasture, use portable troughs connected to a hose. Check troughs twice daily for dirt, algae, and leaks. Clean with a brush and chlorine solution weekly.

Flow rate: A cow can drink 1020 litres per minute. So a trough valve must deliver at least 10 litres per minute. Use a float valve to maintain level. If pressure is low, install a pressure pump. In hot climates, add a shade over the trough to keep water cool. Also, consider a water meter to track usagesudden increases may indicate a leak.

Backup: Always have a backup source. A hand pump or a reserve tank can save you if the main supply fails. During dry seasons, water scarcity is common. Harvest rainwater from roofs into tanks. A 100 m roof collects 1,000 litres from 10 mm of rain. Store it. Water is lifefor your cows and your farm.

6. Ventilation and cooling systems

Heat stress costs African dairy farmers millions in lost milk. When temperature rises above 25C, cows eat less and produce less. At 35C, milk yield can drop by 2030%. Ventilation and cooling are not luxuriesthey are essential.

Natural ventilation: In open barns, use the stack effect. Hot air rises and exits through a ridge vent or open ridge. Cool air enters through open sides. The roof should be 45 m high at the ridge, with a 1 m overhang. The building should be at least 15 m wide for good cross-ventilation. Remove any obstructions like hay bales or walls. In tie-stall barns, open the windows and doors. A simple test: hold a ribbon at cow level. If it hangs still, you need more ventilation.

Mechanical ventilation: For closed barns, install fans. Use 1.2 m diameter fans every 810 m along the feed alley, angled 15 downward. Each fan moves 20,00030,000 m/hour. Run them when temperature exceeds 25C. Use a thermostat to control. Fans cost $150$300 each. Solar-powered fans are available for remote farmsthey cost more but save on electricity.

Cooling systems: Sprinklers and misters. Sprinklers wet the cow's back and head; as water evaporates, it cools her. Use large droplets (not mist) to soak the skin. Run sprinklers for 2 minutes every 15 minutes when temperature is above 28C. Misters produce fine droplets that cool the air, but they can cause wet bedding and respiratory problems. Use misters only in open, well-ventilated areas. Combine fans with sprinklers for best resultsthe fan blows air over the wet cow, increasing evaporation.

Shade: In pasture, provide shade trees or shade cloth. A shade structure should provide 34 m per cow. Orient it east-west to block midday sun. White or reflective roofs reflect heat. In the holding pen, install a sprinkler system and fans. Cows wait there before milkingcool them down.

Water: Ensure cool drinking water. Cows drink more when water is below 20C. Add ice to troughs in extreme heat? Not practical, but shade the trough. Monitor cows for panting, drooling, and reduced feed intakethese are signs of heat stress. Act early. A comfortable cow gives more milk.

<h2>Practical Reference: Facilities and Equipment for Dairy Farms</h2>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the core technical and business decisions covered in this module. It is written for African dairy enterprisesfrom two-cow zero-grazing units to 200-cow commercial herdswhere every shilling spent on infrastructure must translate into milk yield, animal health, and labour efficiency. Use it alongside the six lessons as a field guide, a procurement checklist, and a design brief for your contractor or artisan.</p>

<h3>1. Housing Systems: Choosing What Fits Your Business Model</h3>

<p>Housing is the single largest capital item on a dairy farm after land and cattle. The right system depends on climate, land size, labour availability, and your production target. In Africa, the dominant systems are:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Freestall:</strong> Cows rest in individual cubicles, with alleyways for walking and feeding. Best for 30+ cows in high-rainfall or high-heat areas. Requires investment in cubicle design, bedding, and alley scraping equipment. Labour-efficient and scalable.</li>
<li><strong>Tie-stall:</strong> Cows are tethered in individual stalls. Common in Kenya's highlands and parts of Ethiopia. Low initial cost, but high labour demand, poor cow comfort, and difficult mechanisation. Suited to small herds (under 20 cows) with family labour.</li>
<li><strong>Pasture-based (including zero-grazing):</strong> Cows graze or are cut-and-carried. Zero-grazing units keep cows in a confined area with cut fodder. Low housing cost, but requires reliable forage production and careful manure management. Ideal for mixed crop-livestock farms.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Business-first rule:</strong> Choose the system that minimises your cost per litre of milk produced over 10 years, not the one with the lowest upfront cost. A poorly designed tie-stall that costs $200 per cow but reduces yield by 15% will cost more than a $1,000 per cow freestall over a decade.</p>

<h3>4. Feed Storage and Handling Equipment</h3>

<p>Feed costs represent 6070% of milk production costs in Africa. Poor storage causes 1030% losses to mould, rodents, and insects. Essential equipment includes:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Silage bunkers or pits:</strong> Concrete or earth-lined, with 23 m walls. Capacity: 1.52.0 m per cow per month for maize silage.</li>
<li><strong>Hay barns:</strong> Raised floor (30 cm above ground), ventilated walls, roof overhang of at least 1 m. Store bales at 1518% moisture.</li>
<li><strong>Concentrate feed bins:</strong> Metal or plastic, rodent-proof, with tight lids. Capacity: 710 days of feed per cow.</li>
<li><strong>Feed mixer wagon or manual mixing:</strong> For herds over 30 cows, a mixer wagon (tractor-drawn or stationary) ensures consistent TMR. For smaller herds, a concrete mixing pad and shovel suffice.</li>
<li><strong>Fodder chopper:</strong> Motorised or manual. Chopping reduces wastage by 2030% and improves digestibility.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Business tip:</strong> Buy feed storage capacity for 3 months of concentrate and 6 months of forage. This lets you buy at harvest-time low prices and protects against supply disruptions.</p>

<h3>5. Water Systems and Supply</h3>

<p>A lactating cow drinks 60120 litres of water per day, depending on yield and temperature. Water is the cheapest feed ingredient and the most neglected. A cow deprived of water for 4 hours can lose 510 litres of milk.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Source:</strong> Borehole, river, rainwater harvesting, or municipal supply. Always have a backup source.</li>
<li><strong>Storage:</strong> Minimum 3 days of storage for the whole herd. A 10,000-litre tank for 20 cows is a practical minimum.</li>
<li><strong>Distribution:</strong> Gravity-fed where possible (reduces pumping costs). Use 2532 mm HDPE pipes for mains, 20 mm for trough lines.</li>
<li><strong>Troughs:</strong> One trough per 1520 cows, with a float valve. Clean dailyalgae and manure reduce intake.</li>
<li><strong>Quality:</strong> Test water for salinity, nitrates, and bacterial contamination annually. In coastal areas, check for salt intrusion.</li>
</ul>

<h3>6. Ventilation and Cooling Systems</h3>

<p>Heat stress costs African dairy farmers millions of litres of milk annually. When the temperature-humidity index (THI) exceeds 72, cows begin to reduce feed intake and milk yield. At THI above 80, yield drops by 1025%.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Natural ventilation:</strong> Open ridge (1015% of floor area), open sidewalls, and 4.55.5 m ridge height. This is the cheapest and most reliable system in most of Africa.</li>
<li><strong>Fans:</strong> Horizontal circulation fans (9001,200 mm diameter) placed every 1012 m along the feed alley. Cost: $150$300 per fan. Running cost: $0.50$1.00 per day per fan.</li>
<li><strong>Sprinklers:</strong> Low-pressure sprinklers over the feed alley or holding area. Use 12 litres per cow per cycle, 510 cycles per day. Combine with fans for evaporative cooling.</li>
<li><strong>Shade:</strong> In pasture-based systems, provide 35 m of shade per cow. Trees (e.g. <em>Faidherbia albida</em>, <em>Grevillea robusta</em>) are the cheapest option.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Business case:</strong> A 20-cow farm in Uganda investing $2,000 in fans and sprinklers can recover the cost in 612 months through prevented milk losses during the hot season.</p>

<li><strong>Housing system matches your business model:</strong> Freestall for 30+ cows, tie-stall for under 20, pasture/zero-grazing for mixed farms.</li>
<li><strong>Cubicle dimensions are correct for your breed:</strong> Width 1.101.25 m, length 2.202.60 m, with a 12% floor slope.</li>
<li><strong>Ventilation is adequate:</strong> Open ridge (1015% of floor area), ridge height 4.55.5 m, and cross-ventilation from prevailing winds.</li>
<li><strong>Water supply is reliable:</strong> 3 days of storage, one trough per 1520 cows, and a backup source.</li>
<li><strong>Manure system is planned:</strong> Composting or biogas for small herds, slurry or separator for large herds. Never enter a pit.</li>
<li><strong>Feed storage prevents losses:</strong> 3 months concentrate, 6 months forage, rodent-proof and moisture-controlled.</li>
<li><strong>Heat stress measures are in place:</strong> Shade, fans, or sprinklers when THI exceeds 72.</li>
