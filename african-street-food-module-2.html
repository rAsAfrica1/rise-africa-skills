Lessons

1. Setting Up a Street Kitchen

Start with a spot that has three things: foot traffic, shade, and a water source within 50 metres. In most African cities, you need a permit from the local council. Visit the municipal offices and ask for the 'hawker's licence' or 'street trading permit'. In Nairobi, it costs about 2,000 KES per year; in Accra, around 100 GHS per quarter. Budget for it. Your stall must be stable. Use a wooden table at least 1 metre long and 70 cm high. If you fry, place your oil drum or gas cooker on a separate metal stand, never on the same table where you chop vegetables. Keep a 2-metre gap between the cooking fire and where customers stand. For storage, use two plastic crates with lids: one for dry goods (flour, salt, spices), one for tools (knives, spoons, tongs). Never store food on the ground. A simple three-sided windbreak made from corrugated iron sheets will protect your flame and keep dust off your food. For washing, bring two 20-litre jerrycans: one with clean water and soap, one for rinsing. A small basin and a bottle of liquid soap cost less than 500 KES. Set up a handwashing station at the front of your stall so customers see you wash. Finally, arrange your workspace in a line: raw ingredients on the left, cooking in the centre, finished food and serving on the right. This prevents cross-contamination and speeds up service. Test your setup at home before you go live. Time yourself: you should be able to serve your first customer within 15 minutes of arriving.

2. Charcoal, Gas and Wood: Choosing Your Heat

Charcoal is the most common street fuel in Africa because it is cheap, widely available, and gives high heat. A 50 kg sack of charcoal in Kampala costs about 80,000 UGX and lasts 5-7 days if you cook 6 hours a day. Charcoal burns at 600-800C, perfect for deep frying and grilling. But it produces carbon monoxide, so never use it in a closed room. Gas (LPG) is cleaner and faster to control. A 6 kg cylinder costs around 15,000 NGN in Lagos and lasts 10-12 days if you fry 4 hours daily. Gas burns at 1,000C and you can turn it off instantly, saving fuel. However, refilling can be unreliable in rural areas. Wood is free in many villages but burns slower and produces more smoke. Use dry hardwood, not softwood, to avoid creosote that flavours food badly. For a beginner, start with charcoal: it is forgiving and you can see the heat. Buy a 'jiko' (Kenya) or 'coal pot' (Ghana) for 500-1,500 KES. For gas, buy a single-burner stove with a hose and regulator. Always check the hose for cracks before lighting. For wood, build a three-stone fire and keep a bucket of sand nearby to control flames. Whichever you choose, never leave a fire unattended. Keep a fire extinguisher (dry powder, 1 kg) or a bucket of sand within arm's reach. Learn to judge heat by holding your palm 15 cm above the coals: if you can hold it for 3 seconds, it is medium; 1 second, high. This skill saves fuel and prevents burnt food.

3. Oil for Frying: Types, Temperature, Reuse

Use vegetable oils with a high smoke point: palm oil (smoke point 235C), groundnut oil (225C), sunflower oil (225C), or canola (205C). Avoid olive oil for deep fryingit is expensive and smokes at 190C. In West Africa, red palm oil gives colour and flavour to puff-puff and plantain, but it foams. Let it settle for 10 minutes after heating before frying. In East Africa, sunflower oil is common and cheap. Buy in 5-litre jerrycans to save money: 5 litres of sunflower oil in Dar es Salaam costs about 25,000 TZS. Heat oil to 170-180C for most doughnuts and fritters. If you don't have a thermometer, drop a 1 cm cube of bread: it should brown in 60 seconds. If it browns in 30 seconds, the oil is too hot (200C+); if it takes 90 seconds, too cool. Reuse oil only 3-4 times. Each time, strain it through a fine mesh or cloth to remove crumbs. Store in a dark, cool place. When oil turns dark, smells rancid, or foams excessively, throw it away. Never mix old oil with new. To dispose, let it cool, pour into a sealed container, and give to a soap maker or waste collectornever down the drain. For safety, keep a lid nearby to smother flames if oil catches fire. Never use water on an oil fire. A simple oil thermometer costs 5,000 KES and pays for itself in one week by preventing burnt batches.

4. The Dough Toolkit: Mixing, Resting, Shaping

You need five tools: a large bowl (plastic or stainless steel, 5-litre), a wooden spoon or your clean hands, a measuring cup, a clean cloth, and a flat surface for kneading. For most street doughsmandazi, puff-puff, doughnutsuse all-purpose flour. In Nigeria, you might use cassava flour for 'akara' but that's a batter, not a dough. Start with a basic ratio: 500 g flour, 1 teaspoon instant yeast, 1 teaspoon salt, 2 tablespoons sugar, 250 ml warm water (not hothot water kills yeast). Mix dry ingredients first. Add water slowly, stirring with a spoon until it forms a shaggy mass. Then knead on a floured surface for 8-10 minutes until smooth and elastic. If it sticks, add flour one tablespoon at a time. Resting is critical: cover with a damp cloth and leave in a warm place for 1-2 hours until doubled in size. In cold weather, put the bowl near your fire but not too close. After resting, punch down the dough to release air. Shape into balls (50 g each for mandazi) or rings. For puff-puff, use a spoon to drop small balls into oil. Always shape with clean, oiled hands to prevent sticking. If you want to save time, make the dough the night before and keep it in a cool box with ice packs. It will rise slowly and be ready by morning. Never skip restingit makes the dough light and digestible. Test a small piece first: fry one ball. If it floats and browns in 2 minutes, your oil is ready.

5. Food Safety on the Street

Street food must be safe or you will lose customers and get sick yourself. The biggest risks are bacteria from raw meat, dirty water, and dirty hands. Keep raw meat and vegetables separate. Use two cutting boards: red for meat, green for vegetables. If you can't afford two, cut vegetables first, then meat, then wash the board with soap and hot water. Cook meat to 75Cno pink inside. For fried foods, oil at 175C kills most bacteria in 2 minutes. Never leave cooked food at room temperature for more than 2 hours. If you prepare ahead, keep hot food above 60C and cold food below 5C. In hot African climates, bacteria double every 20 minutes. So serve immediately or reheat thoroughly. Cover all food with mesh lids or clean cloths to keep flies off. Flies carry typhoid and cholera. Buy a fly trap or use a fan to create a breeze. Wash your hands with soap for 20 seconds after using the toilet, handling raw meat, or touching money. Keep a bottle of hand sanitiser (70% alcohol) for quick clean-ups. Never smoke or chew while cooking. Wear a clean apron and tie back long hair. If you have diarrhoea or a cut on your hand, stay home or wear gloves. Clean your stall at the end of each day: scrape tables, sweep the ground, and wash utensils in hot soapy water. A clean stall attracts more customersthey can see you care.

6. Water, Cleanliness and Hand Hygiene

Water is the most common source of sickness in street food. Never use tap water directly unless you know it is treated. In most African cities, tap water is chlorinated but may still contain bacteria. Boil water for 1 minute, then cool it in a clean covered container. Or use a filter (ceramic or biosand) or add 2 drops of household bleach (5% sodium hypochlorite) per litre, wait 30 minutes. For washing hands, use running waternot a bowl where everyone dips. A simple 'tippy-tap' made from a 5-litre jerrycan with a hole and a string costs nothing and saves water. Fill it with clean water and hang it at your stall. Add soap. Wash hands for 20 seconds: palms, back of hands, between fingers, under nails. Rinse. Dry with a clean cloth or air dry. For washing utensils, use three basins: wash with soap and hot water, rinse with clean water, then sanitise with a bleach solution (1 tablespoon bleach per 4 litres water) for 1 minute. Air dry on a clean rackdo not wipe with a dirty cloth. For dishes, use a separate basin from handwashing. Change water every 2 hours or when it looks dirty. Store clean water in a covered container with a tap or ladlenever dip a cup into it. If you use a bucket, keep a dedicated scoop that only touches water. Finally, keep your fingernails short and clean. Dirt under nails carries bacteria. Wash your hands before you start cooking, after handling money, after using the toilet, and after touching your face or phone. A bar of soap and a jerrycan cost less than 500 KES but can save you from a week of sickness and lost sales.

<h2>Practical Reference: Fire, Oil and Dough The Street Kitchen Operator's Handbook</h2>

<p>This Practical Reference consolidates the six lessons in <strong>Foundations: Fire, Oil and Dough</strong> into one working document. It is written for the operator who is already selling or about to sell fried dough, <em>mandazi</em>, <em>puff-puff</em>, <em>vetkoek</em>, <em>kebab</em>, <em>chips</em>, <em>chapati</em> or <em>bhajia</em> from a roadside stand, a market bay, a school gate or a mobile cart. Every decision here is framed around three business questions: <strong>Does it reduce my cost per unit? Does it protect my customer? Does it keep me trading tomorrow?</strong></p>

<p>Street food in Africa is not a side hustle it is a major employer and a daily food source for millions. The World Bank estimates that the food system employs a large share of the continent's workforce, and informal food vending is one of the most accessible entry points for women and youth entrepreneurs. Your margin comes from three controllable inputs: <strong>heat, oil and dough</strong>. Master these and you control your business.</p>

<h3>2. Oil: Temperature, Reuse and Cost Control</h3>

<p>Oil is usually the single largest recurring cost in a fried-dough or chips business often 1525% of turnover. It is also the most common food safety failure point. Frying below <strong>160 C</strong> makes dough greasy and oil-logged; above <strong>190 C</strong> the oil breaks down quickly and forms acrylamide and other harmful compounds. The sweet spot for most African fried dough is <strong>170180 C</strong>.</p>

<h3>3. The Dough Toolkit: Consistency Is Profit</h3>

<p>Dough is where your brand lives. A <em>mandazi</em> that is soft today and hard tomorrow loses customers. The four-step toolkit <strong>mix, rest, shape, fry</strong> should be standardised so that any trained helper produces the same result. Weigh ingredients rather than using cups; a 5% variation in water changes texture noticeably. Rest the dough covered for at least 30 minutes at ambient temperature (longer in cool highland areas, shorter in hot coastal towns) so the gluten relaxes and the dough shapes without shrinking. Shape on a lightly floured board, never on a wet surface. Fry in small batches overcrowding drops the oil temperature below 160 C and produces greasy, pale dough.</p>

<h3>4. Food Safety on the Street: The Five Keys in Practice</h3>

<p>The WHO <em>Five Keys to Safer Food</em> are the global standard: keep clean; separate raw and cooked; cook thoroughly; keep food at safe temperatures; use safe water and raw materials. On a street stand these translate into concrete actions: a tippy-tap or jerrycan with a tap for handwashing (not a shared bowl), a covered display so flies cannot land on cooked food, a clean cloth for drying hands (changed daily), and a separate pair of tongs for raw and cooked dough. If you handle money, wash hands or use a tong-and-cash-box system cash is one of the dirtiest items on your stand.</p>

<h3>5. Water, Cleanliness and Hand Hygiene</h3>

<p>Water is both an ingredient and a cleaning input. Use potable water for dough and for rinsing utensils. Where piped water is unreliable, treat water by boiling (rolling boil for 1 minute) or by using an approved chlorine tablet. Hand hygiene is the single highest-return habit in street food: wash with soap for at least 20 seconds before starting, after handling raw dough, after using the toilet, after handling waste, and after touching money. Keep fingernails short, cover cuts with waterproof plasters, and wear a clean apron. The WHO estimates that handwashing with soap alone can reduce diarrhoeal disease by around 30% a direct saving on lost trading days.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul style="line-height:1.7;font-size:14px">
<li><strong>Heat:</strong> Set up burner on a stable, level surface away from customers and flammable materials; keep sand or extinguisher within reach.</li>
<li><strong>Oil:</strong> Check temperature with a thermometer before the first batch target 170180 C; never fry below 160 C.</li>
<li><strong>Oil reuse:</strong> Filter after each session, store in a covered tin away from sunlight, and discard when it darkens, foams or smells rancid.</li>
<li><strong>Dough:</strong> Weigh ingredients, rest covered for at least 30 minutes, and fry in small batches to protect oil temperature.</li>
<li><strong>Separation:</strong> Keep raw dough, cooked food and money physically apart separate tongs, separate surfaces.</li>
<li><strong>Hygiene:</strong> Tippy-tap with soap at the stand; wash hands for 20 seconds before, during and after handling food or cash.</li>
<li><strong>Water:</strong> Use potable water for dough and rinsing; treat stored water by boiling or approved chlorine.</li>
<li><strong>Display:</strong> Keep all cooked food covered; never display food on the ground or near a drain.</li>
</ul>
