1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Fluxes are chemicals added to gold ore or concentrate before smelting to help separate the gold from impurities. Without fluxes, the smelting process would be inefficient, and you would lose a lot of gold in the slag. In Africa, many small-scale miners smelt gold using simple furnaces and crucibles. The right flux mix can increase gold recovery from 70% to over 95%. That is a huge difference in income.

Fluxes work by lowering the melting point of impurities and making them flow easily. They also help remove oxides and other unwanted materials. Common fluxes include borax, soda ash, silica sand, and fluorspar. Each has a specific job. For example, borax helps dissolve metal oxides and prevents gold from sticking to the crucible. Soda ash helps reduce melting point and improves slag fluidity. Silica sand adds silica to balance the slag chemistry.

The type and amount of flux depend on the ore. If your ore is high in iron, you need more silica. If it is high in silica, you need more soda ash or limestone. In Africa, ores vary widely: from quartz veins in Ghana to alluvial deposits in Zimbabwe. So you must test your ore and adjust your flux recipe. A common mistake is using too much borax, which can make the slag too thin and cause gold to be lost in the slag. A good starting point for many African ores is: 30% borax, 30% soda ash, 20% silica sand, and 20% fluorspar by weight of ore. But this is just a guide.

Smelting is not just about melting gold; it is about creating two distinct layers: a metal layer (gold and other heavy metals) and a slag layer (light impurities). Fluxes make this separation clean. If you get the flux right, the gold button will be shiny and the slag will be glassy and easy to break away. If you get it wrong, you get a messy mix and lost gold. That is why this module matters: it can make or break your smelting business.

2. Tools and Materials

To do proper fluxing and smelting, you need the right tools and materials. In Africa, you can find most of these at mining supply stores or hardware shops in cities like Johannesburg, Accra, or Nairobi. Some you can make yourself.

First, you need a furnace. A simple clay furnace or a metal drum furnace works. It must reach temperatures of 1100C to 1200C. You can use charcoal or propane as fuel. Charcoal is cheaper and more available in rural areas. You also need a crucible. Clay-graphite crucibles are best because they withstand thermal shock. A #4 or #6 crucible is good for 5-10 kg of ore. Never use a crucible that is cracked or has been used for other chemicals.

For fluxes, you need: borax (sodium borate), soda ash (sodium carbonate), silica sand (clean, white sand), and fluorspar (calcium fluoride). You can buy these in powder form. Store them in dry containers. You also need a scale that measures in grams, a mixing bucket, and a respirator mask. Flux dust is harmful if inhaled. Wear safety glasses, gloves, and a leather apron.

Other tools: a mould for pouring gold (steel or cast iron), tongs long enough to handle hot crucibles, a hammer and chisel to break slag, and a fire extinguisher or sand bucket for safety. In many African workshops, you will also see a slag hammer and a crucible tong. Make sure your tongs are drywet tongs can cause steam explosion.

Finally, you need a good source of gold ore or concentrate. If you are buying ore, test it first. A simple assay can tell you how much gold is in it. Do not smelt blindly. The cost of fluxes and fuel adds up. With the right tools and materials, you can set up a small smelting operation in your backyard or at a mining site.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps to smelt gold with fluxes. First, prepare your ore. Crush it to a fine powder, about 100 mesh. You can use a hammer mill or a stamp mill. Then weigh your ore. For a small batch, use 5 kg of ore.

Next, mix your fluxes. A good starting recipe for many African ores: 1.5 kg borax, 1.5 kg soda ash, 1 kg silica sand, and 1 kg fluorspar. That is 30% borax, 30% soda ash, 20% silica, and 20% fluorspar by weight of ore. Mix the fluxes thoroughly with the ore in a clean bucket. Add a little water to make a paste if you want, but dry mixing is fine. Make sure there are no lumps.

Now, preheat your furnace to 1000C. Place the crucible in the furnace and let it heat up slowly. Then carefully load the ore-flux mixture into the crucible. Do not fill it more than two-thirds full, because it will bubble and expand. Close the furnace and increase the temperature to 1150C. Let it smelt for 45 to 60 minutes. You will see the mixture become liquid and bubble. That is the flux working.

After 45 minutes, check the melt. It should be fluid like water. If it is thick, add a little more soda ash. If it is too thin, add a little silica. When ready, pour the molten mixture into your mould. Let it cool. You will see two layers: a glassy slag on top and a gold button at the bottom. Break the slag with a hammer. The gold button may contain other metals like silver or copper. You can refine it further if needed.

Remember, every ore is different. You may need to adjust the flux amounts. Keep records of what you used and the result. That way, you can improve your recovery. In Africa, many smelters use this method and get good results. Practice with small batches first.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Even experienced smelters make mistakes. Here are the most common ones and how to fix them. First, using too much borax. Borax is good, but too much makes the slag very thin. Thin slag can trap tiny gold particles and carry them away. Fix: reduce borax to 20% and increase silica or soda ash. Second, not mixing fluxes well. If fluxes are not evenly mixed, some parts of the melt will be too thick and others too thin. Fix: mix for at least 5 minutes until the color is uniform.

Third, wet crucibles or wet tools. If you put a wet crucible in a hot furnace, it can crack or explode. Fix: always preheat crucibles slowly and keep tools dry. Fourth, wrong temperature. If the furnace is too cold, the slag will not flow. If too hot, you lose gold as fumes. Fix: use a pyrometer or observe the melt. It should be red-orange and fluid. Fifth, not enough flux. If you use too little flux, the slag will be thick and gold will not separate. Fix: use at least 10% flux by weight of ore. For difficult ores, use up to 30%.

Sixth, adding flux after the melt has started. This causes splashing and uneven mixing. Fix: mix all fluxes with ore before loading. Seventh, using dirty ore. If your ore has a lot of organic matter or sulfides, it can cause foaming. Fix: roast sulfide ores first to burn off sulfur. Eighth, pouring too fast. If you pour too fast, slag can mix with gold. Fix: pour slowly and steadily into a preheated mould.

Finally, not keeping records. If you don't record your flux recipe and results, you cannot improve. Fix: keep a notebook with ore type, flux amounts, temperature, and gold recovered. In Africa, many small-scale miners lose money because of these mistakes. With a little care, you can avoid them and increase your gold recovery.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety is not optional when smelting gold. Fluxes and molten metal can cause serious burns, and fumes can damage your lungs. Always wear personal protective equipment (PPE): a respirator mask (N95 or better), safety glasses, leather gloves, and a leather apron. Never wear synthetic clothing; it melts and sticks to skin. Work in a well-ventilated area. If you are indoors, use a fume hood or exhaust fan. In Africa, many smelters work outdoors, which is better, but still avoid breathing fumes.

Flux dust is harmful. Borax and silica can cause respiratory problems if inhaled. Always mix fluxes in a well-ventilated area, and wear your mask. Store fluxes in labeled, sealed containers away from children and animals. When heating the crucible, use long tongs. Never look directly into the furnace without eye protection. The glow can damage your eyes.

Molten metal is dangerous. It can cause deep burns. Never pour molten metal onto wet ground or into a wet mould. Steam explosion can spray molten metal. Always preheat your mould. Keep a bucket of dry sand and a fire extinguisher nearby. Do not use water on a metal fire; use sand or a Class D extinguisher.

Best practices: work with a partner. Never smelt alone. Have a first aid kit ready. Know the location of the nearest clinic. Keep your work area clean and free of clutter. Do not eat or drink in the smelting area. Wash your hands after handling fluxes. Dispose of slag properly; it can contain heavy metals. Do not dump it in rivers or farmland.

Finally, follow local laws. In many African countries, you need a permit to smelt gold. Also, be aware of mercury usethis module does not cover mercury, but if you use it, follow strict safety. Remember, a safe smelter is a productive smelter. Your health and life are more valuable than any gold.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it's time to practice. This exercise will guide you through a small-scale smelt using fluxes. You will need: 1 kg of crushed gold ore (100 mesh), 300 g borax, 300 g soda ash, 200 g silica sand, 200 g fluorspar, a clay-graphite crucible, a furnace, a mould, tongs, PPE, and a scale.

Step 1: Weigh your ore. Record the weight. Step 2: Weigh each flux according to the recipe above (30% borax, 30% soda ash, 20% silica, 20% fluorspar). Mix all fluxes with the ore in a bucket until uniform. Step 3: Preheat your furnace to 1000C. Place the crucible inside and let it heat for 10 minutes. Step 4: Carefully load the ore-flux mixture into the crucible. Do not fill more than two-thirds. Step 5: Increase temperature to 1150C. Close the furnace and wait 45 minutes. Step 6: Check the melt. It should be fluid. If not, add a little soda ash. Step 7: Pour into a preheated mould. Let it cool. Step 8: Break the slag and recover the gold button. Weigh the gold. Calculate your recovery: (gold weight / gold content in ore) x 100. If you don't know the gold content, you can estimate from previous assays.

After the exercise, answer these questions: What was your recovery? Did the slag flow easily? What would you change next time? Write down your observations. Try the exercise with different flux ratios to see how it affects recovery. For example, reduce borax to 20% and increase silica to 30%. Compare results. This hands-on practice is the best way to learn. In Africa, many successful smelters started with small tests like this. Repeat until you can consistently get over 90% recovery. Then you can scale up.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Fluxes and the Smelting Process

This practical reference consolidates the working knowledge you need to run a small-to-medium gold smelting operation in an African context. It is written for the operator who must convert gold-bearing concentrate, dor scraps, or amalgamation residue into a clean, marketable gold button while controlling cost, recovering maximum metal, and keeping the workshop safe. Every figure, table, and checklist below is designed to be printed, pinned to the workshop wall, and used during a live smelt.

In African artisanal and small-scale gold mining (ASGM), smelting is often the single highest-value step in the whole value chain. A single bad flux recipe, a wet crucible, or an over-oxidising melt can lock gold into slag or send it up the flue as fume. The difference between a 92% and a 98% recovery on a 500 g dor bar is roughly 30 g of gold at prevailing prices that is a material business loss. Treat flux selection and furnace control as profit levers, not as chores.

Understanding the Flux System

Fluxes are not a single ingredient; they are a designed chemical system. In gold smelting, the flux performs four jobs:

  1. Lower the melting point of the gangue (waste rock) so it flows as slag at a temperature the furnace can reach.
  2. Collect and separate base-metal oxides (iron, copper, lead, zinc) into the slag phase.
  3. Protect the gold from oxidation and from re-absorbing into the slag.
  4. Scavenge residual values a good flux captures fine gold that would otherwise be lost.

The classic flux triad is borax (flux, lowers melting point), soda ash / sodium carbonate (alkaline flux, thins slag), and silica sand (acidic flux, controls slag viscosity). To this base, African operators commonly add niter (potassium nitrate) as an oxidiser, fluorspar as a slag thinner, and charcoal or wheat flour as a reducing agent. The correct ratio depends on the ore's silica-to-base ratio, which is why a small assay or a trial smelt before a full batch is essential.

Step-by-Step Practical Method

  1. Weigh and record. Weigh concentrate, flux ingredients, and crucible. Record everything in a smelt log. This is your only defence against unexplained losses.
  2. Dry the charge. Wet concentrate causes steam explosions and fume. Dry at low heat (under 150 C) until constant weight.
  3. Mix flux. Combine borax, soda ash, silica, niter, fluorspar, and charcoal in the proportions from Table 1. Mix dry, thoroughly, in a clean bucket.
  4. Charge the crucible. Layer concentrate and flux, or mix them. Fill to no more than two-thirds. Pre-heat the crucible gradually to avoid thermal shock.
  5. Melt. Bring furnace to 11001200 C. Hold for 3060 minutes depending on charge mass. Stir with a graphite rod if the furnace allows.
  6. Settle. Stop stirring and hold for 1015 minutes so gold droplets sink through the slag.
  7. Pour. Pour into a pre-heated conical mould. Let slag solidify on top; quench the mould in water if safe, then break slag away.
  8. Recover and weigh. Retrieve the gold button, clean it, weigh it, and record the recovery percentage against the assayed head grade.

SAFETY WARNING READ BEFORE EVERY SMELT

Gold smelting produces toxic metal fumes (lead, arsenic, cadmium, mercury) and carbon monoxide. In ASGM communities across Africa, chronic lead and mercury exposure is a documented public-health crisis. Never smelt indoors without forced ventilation. Always wear a fitted respirator (P100 or equivalent), leather gloves, a face shield, and non-synthetic clothing. Keep a dry-powder fire extinguisher and a bucket of dry sand within arm's reach. Never add mercury to a smelt it volatilises and is inhaled by everyone in the room. If you feel dizzy, leave the area immediately and get fresh air.

Quick Reference Checklist (pin this to the wall)

  • Charge weighed and logged (concentrate, flux, crucible).
  • Concentrate fully dried no steam on heating.
  • Flux mixed in correct ratio for this ore batch.
  • Crucible pre-heated gradually; filled to no more than 2/3.
  • Furnace at 11001200 C; hold time respected.
  • Melt settled 1015 min before pouring.
  • PPE on: respirator, gloves, face shield, leather apron.
  • Ventilation running; fire extinguisher and sand bucket present.
  • Gold button weighed and recovery % calculated against assay.
  • Slag stored for re-processing, not discarded.

Business Notes for African Operators

Flux is cheap; gold is expensive. The economics of smelting are dominated not by the cost of borax or soda ash but by the recovery percentage. A workshop that consistently recovers 9798% of assayed gold can afford to pay more for concentrate and still profit. A workshop at 90% recovery is quietly losing money on every tonne. Track recovery per batch in a simple notebook or spreadsheet this single habit separates professional operators from hobbyists.

In many African jurisdictions, smelting is regulated under mining, environmental, and occupational-safety laws. In Kenya, the Mining Act and NEMA regulations apply; in Ghana, the Minerals Commission and EPA; in Tanzania, the Mining Act and NEMC. Keep your smelt log, flux purchase receipts, and PPE records. When you sell dor to a licensed buyer or refinery, documentation supports a better price and protects you in an inspection.

Finally, consider collective action. A shared smelting facility serving several licensed ASGM groups can afford better ventilation, a proper furnace, and a trained operator and can negotiate better flux prices and better dor prices. This is the model promoted by programmes such as the planetGOLD initiative and is increasingly common in Ghana, Kenya, and Tanzania.
