1. Routine Cleaning Schedules and Revenue for Tank Service Businesses

Every water tank in Africa is a revenue stream waiting to be tapped, but only if you treat cleaning as a business, not a chore. In Kenya, a standard 10,000-litre plastic tank cleaning job in Nairobi's Kasarani area fetches KES 4,500 to KES 7,000. In Lagos, a similar job for a 1,000-gallon GP tank runs NGN 25,000 to NGN 40,000. In Accra, GHS 400 to GHS 700. In Johannesburg, ZAR 800 to ZAR 1,500. These are not one-off payments. A well-run cleaning business signs annual contracts with schools, clinics, hotels, and residential estates. One school with four 5,000-litre tanks can pay you KES 60,000 per year for quarterly cleaning. Ten such clients and you have a six-figure business without buying a single tank. The key is a schedule. Domestic tanks need cleaning every 6 months if the water is from a borehole with high iron or manganese, every 12 months if from treated municipal supply. Schools and clinics need quarterly cleaning because of high usage and health risks. Hotels and restaurants need monthly cleaning of their kitchen tanks. Your pricing must reflect frequency and difficulty. A tank with a narrow manhole and heavy sludge takes twice as long as a clean tank. Charge accordingly. For a 10,000-litre tank, your cost breakdown: labour for two people for 3 hours at KES 500 each = KES 1,000. Transport and fuel = KES 800. Chemicals: 2 litres of sodium hypochlorite at KES 300 per litre = KES 600. Equipment depreciation: pump, hoses, brushes, PPE = KES 400. Total cost KES 2,800. Your price KES 6,000 gives you KES 3,200 gross profit. Do four jobs a day with two teams and you clear KES 25,600 per day. That is real money. Now the cleaning process itself. First, drain the tank. Use a submersible pump, not buckets, because buckets waste time and spill water. For a 10,000-litre tank, a 1 horsepower pump drains it in 20 minutes. While draining, scrub the walls with a stiff nylon brush and a solution of 1 part chlorine bleach to 10 parts water. For concrete and ferrocement tanks, use a wire brush on stubborn algae but avoid gouging the surface. For plastic tanks, use only soft brushes because abrasive tools scratch the polyethylene and create places for bacteria to hide. For brick tanks, check the mortar joints while scrubbing. If mortar crumbles, you have a repair job to sell. After scrubbing, rinse with clean water and pump out the rinse water. Then refill and add chlorine at 5 grams per 1,000 litres for drinking water storage. Let it sit for 30 minutes, then drain and refill. Safety is non-negotiable. Never enter a tank without a harness and a second person outside. Hydrogen sulphide and methane from decomposing sludge can kill you in seconds. In 2022, two workers died in a tank in Aba, Nigeria, because they entered without gas testing. Use a gas detector that costs NGN 80,000 to NGN 150,000. It pays for itself the first time it saves a life. Wear rubber boots, gloves, goggles, and a respirator. Have a rope tied to the entrant and a winch or strong helper outside. For maintenance contracts, you need a simple CRM. Use a notebook or a free app like Google Sheets. Record client name, phone, tank size, tank material, last cleaning date, next due date, and payment status. Send SMS reminders two weeks before due date. In Kenya, SMS costs KES 1 per message. In Nigeria, NGN 4. In South Africa, ZAR 0.30. This small investment keeps your schedule full. Upsell services: tank repair, gutter cleaning, water testing, and installation of first-flush diverters. A client who trusts you with cleaning will trust you with a KES 150,000 ferrocement tank build. That is the real business. Start with cleaning, build trust, then sell construction and repair. Your cleaning crew is your sales force. Train them to spot cracks, leaks, and corrosion and report back to you. Pay them a commission of 10 percent on any repair or new tank they refer. This aligns incentives and grows your revenue without extra marketing spend. In summary, cleaning is not a cost centre. It is a profit centre that opens doors to bigger contracts. Price for profit, schedule for consistency, and never compromise on safety. Your reputation is built on clean tanks and alive workers.

2. Repairing Cracks and Leaks in Concrete and Ferrocement Tanks

Concrete and ferrocement tanks are the workhorses of African water storage. They last 30 to 50 years if built well, but they crack. They leak. They fail at the joints. Your job as a repair specialist is to fix them permanently, not temporarily. A temporary fix is a callback. A permanent fix is a referral. Let us start with diagnosis. Before you touch a crack, you must know why it exists. Cracks in concrete tanks fall into three categories: structural, shrinkage, and settlement. Structural cracks are wide, often diagonal, and run through the wall. They happen when the tank is overloaded, when the foundation is weak, or when the reinforcement is insufficient. Shrinkage cracks are hairline, map-like, and appear on the surface. They are cosmetic but can leak if they go deep. Settlement cracks are usually vertical and appear near corners or where the tank meets the ground. They indicate foundation movement. Your repair method depends on the cause. For shrinkage cracks, clean the crack with a wire brush and water. Let it dry. Then apply a cement slurry mixed with a bonding agent. In Kenya, a 5-litre bottle of SikaLatex or a local equivalent costs KES 1,200 to KES 1,800. Mix 1 part bonding agent to 3 parts water, then add cement to make a paint-like slurry. Brush it into the crack and let it cure for 24 hours. Then apply a second coat of pure cement slurry. This seals hairline cracks for less than KES 500 per square metre. For structural cracks, you need to open the crack into a V-shape using a grinder or chisel. The V should be 20mm wide at the surface and 15mm deep. Clean it with water and a brush. Then fill it with a non-shrink grout. In Nigeria, a 25kg bag of SikaGrout or MasterFlow 928 costs NGN 18,000 to NGN 25,000. Mix according to instructions and pack it into the V with a trowel. Let it cure for 7 days. Then apply a waterproof coating over the entire wall. A 20kg bag of cement-based waterproof coating like Sika 107 or Dr. Fixit costs NGN 15,000 to NGN 22,000 and covers 10 to 15 square metres. For settlement cracks, you must first stabilise the foundation. Dig around the base of the tank and check for soil erosion. If the soil is soft, remove it and replace it with compacted hardcore and a 1:3:6 concrete mix. In Ghana, a cubic metre of this concrete costs GHS 1,200 to GHS 1,800. After the foundation is stable, repair the crack as a structural crack. Ferrocement tanks are different. They have a thin skin of mortar over wire mesh. Cracks often follow the mesh lines. The repair is to cut back the mortar around the crack until you expose the mesh. Clean the mesh with a wire brush and apply a rust converter if it is corroded. Then apply a new layer of mortar. The mortar mix for ferrocement repair is 1 part cement to 2 parts sand, with a water-cement ratio of 0.4. Add a bonding agent to the mix for better adhesion. Apply the mortar in two layers. The first layer is 10mm thick and should be scratched with a trowel to create a key. Let it cure for 24 hours. The second layer is 5mm thick and should be finished with a steel trowel. Cure the repair for 7 days by keeping it wet. In South Africa, a 50kg bag of cement costs ZAR 120 to ZAR 150. Sand costs ZAR 300 per cubic metre. A 5-litre bonding agent costs ZAR 350. Your material cost for a 1 square metre ferrocement repair is about ZAR 200. You can charge ZAR 800 to ZAR 1,200. That is a 4 to 6 times markup. Leaks at pipe penetrations are common. Where the inlet or outlet pipe enters the tank wall, the seal fails. The fix is to chip away the old sealant and mortar around the pipe. Clean the pipe with sandpaper and apply a PVC primer. Then pack the gap with a waterproof mortar mix. Use a mix of 1 part cement, 2 parts sand, and a waterproofing admixture like Xypex or Kryton. In Kenya, a 5kg pail of Xypex costs KES 8,000 to KES 12,000. It is expensive but it works. Alternatively, use a polyurethane sealant like Sikaflex. A 600ml sausage costs KES 1,500 to KES 2,000. Apply it with a caulking gun and smooth it with a wet finger. For plastic tanks, cracks are repaired with a plastic welding kit. A hot air welder costs NGN 120,000 to NGN 200,000 in Nigeria. You also need polyethylene welding rods that match the tank material. A 1kg pack costs NGN 5,000 to NGN 8,000. The process: clean the crack, drill a small hole at each end of the crack to stop it from spreading, then weld the crack using the hot air welder and welding rod. For a 1,000-litre tank with a 10cm crack, the repair takes 30 minutes and costs NGN 3,000 in materials. You can charge NGN 15,000. For brick tanks, leaks usually occur at the mortar joints. The repair is to rake out the old mortar to a depth of 15mm and repoint with a 1:3 cement-sand mix with a waterproofing additive. In Ghana, a 50kg bag of cement costs GHS 60 to GHS 80. A 20kg bag of waterproofing powder costs GHS 150 to GHS 250. A 10 square metre repointing job uses 2 bags of cement and 1 bag of waterproofing powder, costing GHS 200. You charge GHS 800. Always test your repair. Fill the tank with water and let it sit for 24 hours. Check for damp spots on the outside. If you find a leak, drain the tank and repair again. Do not charge the client until the tank holds water. Your guarantee should be 12 months on the repair. If it leaks within 12 months, you fix it for free. This builds trust and generates referrals. In Africa, word of mouth is the best marketing. A single satisfied client can bring you five more. So do the repair right the first time. Use quality materials. Cure properly. Test thoroughly. And always leave the site cleaner than you found it. That is how you build a repair business that lasts.

3. Desludging and Sludge Management for Commercial and Institutional Tanks

Sludge is the silent killer of water quality and tank capacity. In a 10,000-litre tank, 5 years of neglect can accumulate 1,500 litres of sludge. That is 15 percent of your storage capacity gone. For a school with 20,000 litres of storage, that is 3,000 litres of lost water. In Kenya, water from a borehole costs KES 50 per 1,000 litres if you buy from a vendor. Losing 3,000 litres per fill cycle means losing KES 150 every time you fill. Over a year, that is KES 54,000 wasted. Desludging is not a luxury. It is a financial necessity. The first step is to know when to desludge. For domestic tanks, every 2 to 3 years. For commercial tanks, every 12 to 18 months. For tanks receiving water from rivers or unprotected wells, every 6 months. Signs that a tank needs desludging: water tastes earthy or musty, water is cloudy, there is a visible layer of sediment at the bottom, or the tank has not been cleaned in over 2 years. Your desludging process must be efficient and safe. You need a sludge pump, not a water pump. A diaphragm sludge pump handles solids up to 50mm. In Nigeria, a 2-inch diaphragm pump costs NGN 250,000 to NGN 400,000. In Kenya, a similar pump costs KES 80,000 to KES 150,000. You also need 50 metres of 2-inch suction hose, which costs KES 15,000 to KES 25,000. A 5,000-litre sludge tanker or a 200-litre drum for smaller jobs. For a 10,000-litre tank with 1,500 litres of sludge, the job takes 2 hours with a pump. Without a pump, it takes 2 days with buckets. Time is money. The pump pays for itself in 10 jobs. Before you pump, you must test the sludge for gases. Use a multi-gas detector. If hydrogen sulphide is above 10 ppm, ventilate the tank for 30 minutes before entering. Never enter a tank with a gas reading above 20 ppm. If you must enter, use a full-face respirator with an acid gas cartridge. In South Africa, a 3M full-face respirator costs ZAR 2,500 to ZAR 4,000. Cartridges cost ZAR 300 each and last 8 hours. This is cheap insurance. The sludge you pump out is not waste. It is fertiliser. In Ghana, farmers pay GHS 50 to GHS 100 per 200-litre drum of sludge for their fields. In Kenya, a 1,000-litre tanker of sludge sells for KES 3,000 to KES 5,000 to flower farms in Naivasha or vegetable farms in Kiambu. In Nigeria, poultry farmers in Ogun State pay NGN 10,000 to NGN 15,000 per tanker. So your desludging job has two revenue streams: the client pays you to remove the sludge, and the farmer pays you for the sludge. For a 10,000-litre tank, you charge the client KES 15,000 for desludging and you sell the sludge for KES 4,000. Your cost: labour KES 2,000, fuel KES 1,500, pump depreciation KES 500, disposal or transport to farm KES 1,000. Total cost KES 5,000. Profit KES 14,000. That is a 280 percent return on cost. Now, sludge management for septic tanks and wastewater tanks is a different business. In many African cities, septic tank emptying is a licensed service. In Nairobi, you need a licence from NEMA and the county government. The licence costs KES 50,000 to KES 100,000 per year. In Lagos, you need a licence from LASEPA, costing NGN 200,000 to NGN 500,000. But the revenue is high. A single septic tank emptying job for a 10,000-litre tank costs KES 25,000 to KES 40,000. You can do 3 jobs per day with one tanker. That is KES 75,000 to KES 120,000 per day. The disposal site charges KES 2,000 to KES 5,000 per load. Your fuel and labour cost KES 10,000 per day. Your net profit is KES 60,000 to KES 100,000 per day. This is a serious business. But you must handle the sludge legally. Dumping sludge in a river or on open ground is illegal and dangerous. In Kenya, the penalty is KES 1 million or 2 years in prison. In Nigeria, NGN 500,000 or 6 months. In South Africa, ZAR 5 million or 10 years. Do not risk it. Use licensed disposal sites. For water tank desludging, you can often discharge the sludge water into a soak pit or a constructed wetland. A simple wetland with papyrus and reeds can treat 1,000 litres of sludge water per day. It costs KES 30,000 to KES 50,000 to build and requires no chemicals. This is a good upsell for schools and hotels. For the actual desludging, here is the step-by-step. First, isolate the tank from the water supply. Close the inlet valve. Second, drain the clear water on top. You can pump this into a storage tank for later use or into the garden. Third, pump the sludge from the bottom. Use a sludge pump with a wide hose. Fourth, once the sludge is removed, scrub the walls and floor with a chlorine solution. Fifth, rinse and pump out the rinse water. Sixth, refill and chlorinate. Seventh, dispose of the sludge properly. For pricing, use a tiered structure. Base price for a 5,000-litre tank: KES 10,000. For 10,000 litres: KES 15,000. For 20,000 litres: KES 25,000. Add KES 2,000 if the tank is underground or has a narrow manhole. Add KES 3,000 if the sludge is more than 30cm deep. Add KES 5,000 if you have to carry the sludge by bucket because a pump cannot reach. Always give a written quote before starting. In Africa, verbal agreements lead to disputes. A written quote with your terms and conditions protects you. Include a clause that says you are not responsible for pre-existing structural damage. Include a clause that says payment is due on completion. Include a clause that says you will dispose of the sludge legally and provide a receipt from the disposal site. This professionalism sets you apart from the informal sector. It allows you to charge premium prices. And it builds a business that can scale. You can buy more pumps, more tankers, and hire more staff. The demand is there. Every tank in Africa needs desludging. Your job is to show up on time, do the work safely, and charge a fair price. Do that and you will never lack work.

4. Preventive Maintenance Plans and Service Contracts for Tank Owners

Preventive maintenance is the difference between a tank that lasts 40 years and a tank that fails in 5. In Africa, most tank owners only call a repair person when the tank is already leaking or the water is already contaminated. That is reactive maintenance. It is expensive and stressful. Your business should sell preventive maintenance plans. These are recurring revenue contracts that keep tanks healthy and keep your cash flow predictable. A preventive maintenance plan includes quarterly or bi-annual inspections, cleaning, minor repairs, water quality testing, and a written report. For a domestic client with one 10,000-litre tank, you charge KES 25,000 per year for a quarterly plan. That is KES 6,250 per visit. Your cost per visit is KES 2,500. Your profit is KES 3,750 per visit or KES 15,000 per year per client. If you have 50 clients on this plan, your annual revenue is KES 1,250,000 and your profit is KES 750,000. That is a solid business. For commercial clients like schools, hospitals, and hotels, the plan is more comprehensive. A school with 4 tanks of 10,000 litres each pays KES 120,000 per year for monthly inspections and quarterly cleaning. Your cost per month is KES 5,000. Your profit is KES 60,000 per year per school. Ten schools and you are making KES 600,000 profit per year. In Nigeria, a similar plan for a hotel with 2 tanks of 5,000 litres each costs NGN 300,000 per year. Your cost is NGN 120,000. Profit is NGN 180,000. In Ghana, a clinic with 3 tanks of 5,000 litres pays GHS 6,000 per year. Your cost is GHS 2,500. Profit is GHS 3,500. In South Africa, a guesthouse with 2 tanks of 10,000 litres pays ZAR 18,000 per year. Your cost is ZAR 7,000. Profit is ZAR 11,000. The key to a successful maintenance plan is a checklist. Every visit, you must inspect the following: tank exterior for cracks, leaks, and corrosion. Tank interior for sludge, algae, and biofilm. Inlet and outlet pipes for blockages and leaks. Valves for proper operation. Overflow pipe for blockages. Roof and lid for damage. Foundation for settlement or erosion. Water quality for pH, turbidity, and chlorine residual. For concrete and ferrocement tanks, check for spalling concrete and exposed reinforcement. For plastic tanks, check for UV degradation and cracks. For brick tanks, check for mortar joint deterioration. For steel tanks, check for rust. Your report must be written and signed. Include photos. In Africa, a smartphone with a good camera is your best tool. Take before and after photos. Send the report to the client via WhatsApp or email. This documentation proves you did the work and justifies your fee. It also creates a record that protects you if the client claims you damaged something. Pricing your maintenance plan requires knowing your costs. For a quarterly plan, you visit 4 times per year. Each visit takes 2 hours for a 10,000-litre tank. Labour for 2 people at KES 500 each = KES 1,000. Transport = KES 800. Chemicals and consumables = KES 500. Equipment depreciation = KES 200. Total cost per visit = KES 2,500. Add 20 percent overhead for office, phone, and insurance = KES 500. Total cost = KES 3,000. Your price should be at least KES 5,000 per visit to make a 40 percent margin. For a quarterly plan, that is KES 20,000 per year. Charge KES 25,000 to include a water quality test and a minor repair allowance. The minor repair allowance is important. It covers small fixes like tightening a valve or patching a hairline crack. If you do not include it, you will end up doing free work. Set a limit: KES 5,000 per year in minor repairs. If the repair costs more, you quote separately. Your contract must be clear. It should state the scope of work, the frequency of visits, the response time for emergencies, the payment terms, and the termination clause. In Kenya, a simple contract can be written in English and Swahili. In Nigeria, English and Pidgin. In Ghana, English and Twi. In South Africa, English and Afrikaans or Zulu. The contract should be signed by both parties. Keep a copy for your records. Payment terms: 50 percent upfront, 50 percent on completion of the first visit. Or monthly installments via M-Pesa, bank transfer, or mobile money. In Kenya, M-Pesa is king. In Nigeria, bank transfer and USSD. In Ghana, MTN Mobile Money. In South Africa, EFT and SnapScan. Make it easy for clients to pay. The easier it is, the faster you get paid. Now, how do you sell these plans? Start with your existing cleaning clients. Offer them a discount if they sign up for a 12-month plan. For example, if a single cleaning costs KES 6,000, offer a quarterly plan for KES 25,000 per year. That is KES 6,250 per visit, but they get a written report, a water test, and priority response. Most clients will see the value. Next, approach property managers. In Nairobi, there are hundreds of property management companies managing thousands of apartments. A single property manager can give you 50 tanks. In Lagos, the same. In Accra, the same. In Johannesburg, the same. Prepare a one-page proposal. Include your services, your prices, your credentials, and three references. Dress professionally. Show up on time. Speak clearly. Follow up. The sale is in the follow-up. Most salespeople give up after one call. You should call, email, and visit until you get a yes or a definitive no. Finally, use social media. In Africa, WhatsApp is the most powerful business tool. Create a WhatsApp Business profile. Post before and after photos. Post testimonials. Post tips about tank maintenance. Join community groups and offer free advice. When someone asks for a recommendation, your name will come up. This is how you build a maintenance business that runs on recurring revenue. It is not glamorous. But it is profitable. And it is scalable. Start with 10 clients. Then 20. Then 50. Hire and train technicians. Systemise your operations. Use a simple app like Trello or Google Calendar to schedule visits. Pay your technicians well and they will stay. Treat your clients like gold and they will refer their friends. That is the business.

5. Water Quality Testing, Treatment and Emergency Response for Tanks

Water quality is the most important product you sell. If the water in a tank is contaminated, the tank is worthless. In Africa, waterborne diseases kill over 500,000 people every year. Most of these deaths are children under 5. Your job as a tank professional is to prevent these deaths. That is a serious responsibility. It is also a serious business opportunity. Water quality testing and treatment is a high-margin service. A basic test for pH, turbidity, and chlorine residual takes 10 minutes and costs you KES 200 in consumables. You can charge KES 2,000. A full laboratory test for bacteria, nitrates, and heavy metals costs KES 5,000 to KES 10,000 at a private lab in Nairobi. You can charge the client KES 15,000 and keep the difference. In Lagos, a full test costs NGN 30,000 to NGN 50,000. You charge NGN 80,000. In Accra, GHS 500 to GHS 800 cost, charge GHS 1,500. In Johannesburg, ZAR 1,500 to ZAR 2,500 cost, charge ZAR 4,000. The first step is to know what to test for. For drinking water, you must test for E. coli and total coliforms. These indicate faecal contamination. You also test for pH, which should be between 6.5 and 8.5. Turbidity should be below 5 NTU. Chlorine residual should be between 0.2 and 0.5 mg per litre at the point of use. For water from boreholes, test for iron, manganese, fluoride, and arsenic. In Kenya, high fluoride is a problem in the Rift Valley. In Nigeria, high iron is common in the Niger Delta. In Ghana, high manganese is found in the Ashanti Region. In South Africa, high nitrates are a problem in agricultural areas. You can buy a portable water testing kit for KES 30,000 to KES 50,000. It includes test tubes, reagents, a pH meter, and a turbidity meter. For bacterial testing, you need an incubator and petri dishes. A basic incubator costs KES 25,000 to KES 40,000. Petrifilm plates cost KES 150 each. You can also partner with a local laboratory. You collect the sample, take it to the lab, and they give you the results. You pay the lab KES 3,000 and charge the client KES 10,000. This is a simple way to start without buying equipment. Treatment is the next step. If the water is contaminated with bacteria, you chlorinate. The dose is 5 grams of chlorine per 1,000 litres of water. For a 10,000-litre tank, that is 50 grams. Use calcium hypochlorite granules, which are 65 to 70 percent chlorine. In Kenya, a 1kg jar costs KES 1,500. In Nigeria, NGN 8,000. In Ghana, GHS 80. In South Africa, ZAR 250. Mix the granules in a bucket of water first, then pour it into the tank. Let it sit for 30 minutes. Test the chlorine residual. If it is below 0.2 mg per litre, add more. If it is above 0.5 mg per litre, drain some water and refill. For turbidity, you need to filter the water. A sand filter or a multimedia filter removes particles. A 1,000-litre per hour sand filter costs KES 40,000 to KES 60,000. You can install it as an upsell. For iron and manganese, you need an oxidation filter. A greensand filter or a birm filter costs KES 80,000 to KES 150,000. For fluoride, you need activated alumina or bone char. A household bone char filter costs KES 5,000 to KES 10,000. For arsenic, you need an adsorptive media like granular ferric hydroxide. A 10-inch cartridge costs KES 15,000 to KES 25,000. Emergency response is where you can charge premium prices. When a tank is contaminated, the client is desperate. They will pay for fast service. Your emergency response kit should include: chlorine granules, test kits, a portable pump, hoses, brushes, PPE, and a generator. You should be able to mobilise within 2 hours. Your emergency call-out fee is KES 10,000 to KES 20,000. The treatment itself is extra. For a 10,000-litre tank that is contaminated with E. coli, the job takes 3 hours. You drain the tank, scrub it with chlorine, rinse, refill, and chlorinate. Your cost is KES 5,000. You charge KES 30,000. That is a KES 25,000 profit for 3 hours of work. In Nigeria, charge NGN 150,000. In Ghana, GHS 3,000. In South Africa, ZAR 8,000. But emergency response is not just about money. It is about saving lives. In 2021, a cholera outbreak in Nigeria was traced to a contaminated tank at a school. If that school had a maintenance contract, the outbreak could have been prevented. Your job is to prevent outbreaks. You do that by testing regularly and treating promptly. You also need to educate your clients. Teach them to keep the tank lid closed. Teach them to clean the gutters before the rainy season. Teach them to divert the first flush of rainwater away from the tank. Teach them to test the water every 6 months. Teach them to chlorinate after every cleaning. When you educate your clients, you build trust. When you build trust, you get referrals. When you get referrals, you grow your business. Your pricing for water quality services should be transparent. Publish a price list. Basic test: KES 2,000. Full lab test: KES 15,000. Chlorination of a 10,000-litre tank: KES 5,000. Emergency call-out: KES 20,000 plus treatment. Filter installation: from KES 50,000. Maintenance plan with quarterly testing: KES 30,000 per year. In Nigeria, multiply by 5. In Ghana, multiply by 0.8. In South Africa, multiply by 1.5. These are real numbers. Use them. Finally, document everything. Every test result. Every treatment. Every client. Keep a database. Use it to send reminders. Use it to track trends. If a client's water quality is getting worse, investigate. Maybe the tank is cracked. Maybe the source is contaminated. Maybe the treatment is not working. Your documentation is your proof. It is also your marketing. Show potential clients your reports. Show them that you are professional. Show them that you care. That is how you win in the water business. In Africa, water is life. But clean water is business. And business is good.

SAFETY WARNING - CONFINED SPACE ENTRY

Never enter a water tank alone. Confined spaces can accumulate hydrogen sulphide (H2S), methane (CH4) and carbon dioxide (CO2), causing rapid unconsciousness and death. Before entry: (1) ventilate for at least 30 minutes with a blower or open manhole; (2) test atmosphere for oxygen (must be 19.5-23.5%), H2S (< 10 ppm) and flammable gases (< 10% LEL); (3) use a full-body harness with a tripod and winch; (4) have a trained standby person outside with communication. In Kenya, the Directorate of Occupational Safety and Health Services (DOSHS) requires confined space entry permits under the Occupational Safety and Health Act (2007). In Nigeria, the Factory Act Cap F1 LFN 2004 applies. In South Africa, the Occupational Health and Safety Act 85 of 1993 and its Confined Spaces Regulations (2001) apply. In Ghana, the Labour Act 651 (2003) and LI 1833 apply. Failure to follow these rules has caused multiple fatalities in African water and sanitation work.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Before any tank work: Confirm confined space entry procedures, ventilate, test atmosphere, and set up harness and tripod.
  • Monthly: Visually inspect walls, roof and base for cracks, seepage, and vector entry points.
  • Weekly: Test free chlorine residual (target 0.2-0.5 mg/L at outlet).
  • Every 6-12 months: Drain, scrub, and remove all sediment and biofilm from walls and floor.
  • Annually: Desludge commercial and institutional tanks; check sludge depth is below 5 cm after cleaning.
  • Every 3-6 months: Send water sample for bacteriological testing (E. coli must be 0 CFU/100 mL).
  • Quarterly: Inspect inlet/outlet valves, pipes and screens for leaks and blockages.
  • After any repair: Disinfect tank with chlorine solution (50 mg/L for 24 hours or 200 mg/L for 1 hour), then flush and retest before use.
  • Document everything: Keep a logbook of cleaning dates, chlorine readings, repairs, and test results for service contract clients.
