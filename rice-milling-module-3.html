1. Pre-cleaning Equipment and Operations

Pre-cleaning removes straw, leaves, empty grains, and dirt before paddy enters the mill. In Africa, most small mills use a combination of a vibrating screen and a destoner. The vibrating screen has two or three layers with different hole sizes: top layer 10-12 mm for large trash, middle layer 5-6 mm for smaller trash, and bottom layer 2-3 mm for sand. A fan blows light materials away. For a 1-ton-per-hour mill, the screen area should be at least 1.5 square meters. The screen angle is usually 10-15 degrees, and the vibration speed is 300-400 rpm. Feed paddy evenly using a hopper with a gate; uneven feeding reduces efficiency. Check screens daily for holes or tears; replace worn screens immediately. Clean the machine after every 8 hours of operation. In rural areas, pre-cleaning can also be done manually with a sieve or winnowing, but this is slow and less effective. A simple motorized pre-cleaner costs about $500-$1,500 and pays for itself by reducing wear on downstream equipment. Always wear a dust mask when cleaning the machine. After pre-cleaning, paddy should have less than 1% foreign matter. Test by taking a 1 kg sample and hand-sorting. If trash exceeds 1%, adjust the screen or feed rate. Pre-cleaning also removes stones larger than 5 mm, but smaller stones need a destoner. Remember: pre-cleaning protects your husker and polisher from damage and improves rice quality. In West Africa, many mills skip pre-cleaning and suffer frequent breakdowns. Don't be that mill. Invest in a good pre-cleaner and maintain it daily.

2. De-stoning and Magnetic Separation

De-stoning removes stones and heavy particles that can damage the husker and end up in final rice. A destoner works by fluidizing a bed of paddy on a vibrating deck while air blows upward. Stones, being heavier, sink and move up the inclined deck, while paddy floats and moves down. The deck angle is typically 5-10 degrees, and vibration is 400-600 rpm. Air velocity must be adjusted so paddy just fluidizes; too much air blows paddy away, too little lets stones stay mixed. For a 1-ton-per-hour mill, the destoner deck should be about 1 meter wide and 1.5 meters long. Feed paddy at a constant rate; sudden surges reduce separation. Check the stone discharge outlet every 30 minutes to ensure stones are being removed. Magnetic separation uses magnets to remove iron particles like nails, wire, and screws. Install a permanent magnet or electromagnet in the chute before the husker. A good magnet can catch particles as small as 0.5 mm. Clean the magnet every 4 hours; iron buildup reduces its strength. In Africa, many mills use a simple bar magnet mounted at an angle. Test magnetic strength with a small nail; if it doesn't stick, replace the magnet. After de-stoning and magnetic separation, paddy should have less than 0.1% stones and zero iron particles. Take a 1 kg sample and check by hand. If stones remain, reduce feed rate or increase air. If iron remains, add a second magnet. These steps are critical because a single stone can break a husker rubber roll, costing $50-$200 to replace. A nail can spark and cause a fire. So always run de-stoning and magnetic separation before husking.

3. Paddy Husking and Separation

Husking removes the outer hull from paddy to produce brown rice. In Africa, most small mills use a rubber roll husker. Two rubber rolls spin in opposite directions at different speeds: one fast (about 1000 rpm) and one slow (about 800 rpm). The speed difference creates a shearing action that pulls the hull off. The gap between rolls is critical: too wide and paddy passes unhusked; too narrow and grains break. Set the gap to about 0.5-0.8 mm for long grain, 0.4-0.6 mm for short grain. Adjust while running: feed a handful of paddy and check the output. You want at least 90% husked with less than 5% broken. After husking, a separator separates brown rice from unhulled paddy and hulls. The mixture goes over a vibrating screen with holes sized to let brown rice through but retain unhulled paddy. Unhulled paddy is returned to the husker. Hulls are blown away by a fan and can be used for fuel or animal bedding. For a 1-ton-per-hour mill, the husker needs a 5-7.5 kW motor. Rubber rolls wear out after 80-120 hours of operation; replace them when the surface becomes smooth or cracked. In rural areas, some use a steel huller, but it breaks more grains. Rubber rolls are better for quality. After husking, brown rice should have less than 2% unhulled paddy. Check by taking a 100 g sample. If unhulled paddy is high, narrow the roll gap slightly. If broken grains are high, widen the gap. Always keep the husker clean and lubricated. A well-adjusted husker saves money and produces better rice.

4. Paddy Drying and Tempering

Paddy must be dried to 12-14% moisture before milling to prevent broken grains and mold. In Africa, sun drying is common: spread paddy in a thin layer (5-10 cm) on a concrete floor or tarpaulin. Stir every 30 minutes. On a sunny day, it takes 1-2 days to dry from 20% to 14%. Use a moisture meter to check; if you don't have one, bite a grain: it should be hard but not shatter. For larger quantities, use a mechanical dryer. A batch dryer with a 1-ton capacity costs about $2,000-$5,000. Set drying air temperature to 40-45C for seed paddy, 50-60C for milling paddy. Higher temperatures cause cracks. Drying too fast also causes cracks; aim for a drying rate of 0.5-1% moisture per hour. After drying, temper paddy by letting it rest in a bin for 12-24 hours. Tempering allows moisture inside the grain to equalize, reducing stress and breakage during milling. During tempering, keep paddy covered to prevent re-wetting. In humid regions, use a fan to circulate air. After tempering, check moisture again; it should be uniform. If you skip tempering, broken grains can increase by 10-20%. In West Africa, many farmers dry on roadsides, which contaminates paddy with sand and stones. Instead, use a clean tarp or concrete floor. Never dry on bare ground. Also, avoid drying on very hot surfaces like metal roofs; they can overheat the grain. For storage, paddy should be at 12-13% moisture. If it's higher, continue drying. Remember: proper drying and tempering are the most important steps for high milling yield. A 1% reduction in broken grains means 1% more head rice, which is money in your pocket.

5. Storage and Aeration of Paddy

After drying and tempering, store paddy in clean, dry, airtight containers. In Africa, use jute bags, plastic silos, or metal bins. Avoid storing in open piles; rats, insects, and moisture will ruin it. For a small mill, a 5-ton capacity metal silo costs about $1,000-$2,000. Before storing, clean the store and spray with insecticide like Actellic. Place bags on pallets 10 cm off the floor and 30 cm away from walls. Stack bags no more than 10 high to prevent crushing. For bulk storage, fill silos to 80% capacity to allow air circulation. Aeration uses fans to push air through the grain mass to control temperature and moisture. In hot climates, grain can heat up due to respiration. Install aeration ducts at the bottom of the silo and a fan on top. Run the fan when grain temperature exceeds 30C or when moisture is above 13%. Aeration for 1-2 hours per day is usually enough. Check temperature weekly using a probe. If temperature rises above 35C, aerate immediately; otherwise mold and insects thrive. Also check moisture monthly; if it increases, re-dry. In humid areas, store paddy at 12% moisture or less. Use hermetic bags like GrainPro for small quantities; they block oxygen and kill insects without chemicals. A 100 kg hermetic bag costs about $10. For long-term storage (6-12 months), keep paddy at 12% moisture and below 25C. Inspect monthly for insects, rodents, and leaks. Fumigate with phosphine tablets if insects are found. Always follow safety instructions. Proper storage preserves quality and reduces losses. In Africa, post-harvest losses can be 10-20%; good storage cuts that to less than 5%.

6. Quality Control at Pre-Milling Stage

Quality control at pre-milling ensures paddy is clean, dry, and free of defects before husking. Start with sampling: take 1 kg from every 100 kg lot. Test moisture with a meter; it should be 12-14%. If not, dry or temper again. Check foreign matter by hand-sorting: stones, sand, straw, and metal should be less than 1%. Use a sieve to separate smaller particles. Check for discolored or damaged grains: they should be less than 2%. Discolored grains indicate mold or heat damage; they lower rice quality. Also check for insect damage: look for holes or live insects. If found, fumigate or reject the lot. Test husking potential: take 100 g sample, husk with a small hand husker, and calculate husking ratio. A good lot gives 80-85% brown rice. If less, adjust husker or reject. Check for red rice or other varieties; they reduce market value. Red rice should be less than 0.5%. For seed paddy, test germination: at least 80% should sprout. Keep records of all tests: date, lot number, moisture, foreign matter, and results. This helps trace problems. In Africa, many mills skip quality control and suffer poor yields. A simple quality control kit costs about $200 and includes a moisture meter, sieves, and a hand husker. Train one worker as quality controller. Reject any lot that fails moisture or foreign matter. Remember: garbage in, garbage out. Good pre-milling quality control leads to higher head rice yield, better prices, and happy customers. Do it every day, every lot.

<section>
<h2>Practical Reference: Paddy Cleaning and Preparation for African Rice Mills</h2>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the operational knowledge a mill manager, shift supervisor, or quality officer needs to run the pre-milling section of a rice mill profitably. In Africa, where paddy arrives from smallholder fields with high moisture variability, sand, stones, and mixed varieties, the cleaning and preparation stage often determines whether a mill earns money or loses it. Poor preparation raises broken grain percentages, accelerates wear on rubber rolls, and creates reject loads that buyers discount. Good preparation, by contrast, lifts head rice recovery, reduces downtime, and protects the mill's reputation with traders and institutional buyers such as the World Food Programme.</p>

<p>The sequence below follows the standard flow used in commercial mills across Nigeria, Tanzania, Senegal, Ghana, and Uganda: pre-cleaning, de-stoning and magnetic separation, husking and separation, drying and tempering, storage with aeration, and quality control. Each stage has a business cost and a business benefit. The objective is not maximum cleanliness at any cost it is the lowest total cost per tonne of head rice produced.</p>

<h3>1. Pre-Cleaning Equipment and Operations</h3>
<p>Pre-cleaning removes the coarse material that arrives with paddy from threshing floors: straw, panicles, empty grains, sand, and stones. In most African mills, a combination of a vibrating or oscillating sieve and an aspirator does this work. The sieve separates by size; the aspirator removes light material by air. A well-set pre-cleaner should reduce foreign matter to below 1% before the paddy reaches the de-stoner.</p>
<p>For a mill buying from smallholders, the pre-cleaner is a business filter. If it is bypassed or poorly maintained, the de-stoner and husker do the heavy work, capacity drops, and the rubber rolls wear faster. In West Africa, where paddy is often sun-dried on tarpaulins or bare ground, sand content can be 25% at intake. That sand is abrasive and expensive to remove later.</p>

<h3>2. De-stoning and Magnetic Separation</h3>
<p>De-stoners use a vibrating, inclined deck with air fluidisation to separate stones from paddy by density. Magnetic separators usually plate magnets or drum magnets catch tramp iron such as nails, wire, and machine fragments. Both protect downstream equipment. A stone that reaches the husker can crack a rubber roll; a piece of metal can damage a screen or start a spark in a dusty environment.</p>
<p>Operationally, the de-stoner should be checked at every shift change: deck angle, air volume, and stone discharge rate. Magnetic separators should be cleaned daily and the collected metal logged. This is a low-cost routine that prevents high-cost failures.</p>

<h3>3. Paddy Husking and Separation</h3>
<p>Husking is done with rubber-roll hullers. The gap between rolls and the speed differential determine husking efficiency. If the gap is too wide, unhusked paddy passes through and recirculates; if too narrow, more grain is broken. After husking, a paddy separator (often a compartment-type or gravity separator) returns unhusked paddy to the husker while sending brown rice forward.</p>
<p>In African mills, a common mistake is to run the husker at maximum throughput to meet a delivery deadline. This raises broken grain and reduces head rice yield, which is where the margin lives. A disciplined mill runs the husker at the rate that maximises head rice, not the rate that empties the intake pit fastest.</p>

<h3>4. Paddy Drying and Tempering</h3>
<p>Drying brings paddy to a safe milling moisture, typically 1214%. Tempering is the rest period after drying that allows moisture to equalise within the grain, reducing stress cracks and broken grains during milling. In many African contexts, drying is done in the sun on concrete floors or tarpaulins, while larger mills use mechanical dryers (flat-bed, recirculating, or fluidised-bed).</p>
<p>Tempering is often skipped in small and medium mills, and that omission shows up as broken rice. A 1224 hour rest after drying is a cheap intervention with a measurable return. For a mill producing 2 tonnes of head rice per day, a 2% improvement in head rice recovery is roughly 40 kg per day a direct margin gain.</p>

<h3>5. Storage and Aeration of Paddy</h3>
<p>Paddy should be stored at or below 14% moisture and kept cool. Aeration with ambient or slightly cooled air prevents hotspots, mould, and insect activity. In humid tropical conditions, storage without aeration can lead to rapid quality loss, especially in the rainy season. Silos, warehouses with pallets, and hermetic bags are all used across Africa, with hermetic storage increasingly favoured for small lots because it controls insects without chemicals.</p>
<p>From a business perspective, storage is a working-capital decision. Holding paddy to sell or mill later can capture seasonal price gains, but only if quality is preserved. Aeration and moisture monitoring are the insurance policy on that trade.</p>

<h3>6. Quality Control at the Pre-Milling Stage</h3>
<p>Quality control at this stage covers moisture, foreign matter, purity, and test milling. Every incoming lot should be sampled and tested for moisture before it is accepted. Reject or discount loads that are too wet, too dirty, or mixed with other varieties. Keep a log of supplier, moisture, foreign matter, and price. Over time, this log tells you which suppliers deliver reliable paddy and which ones cost you money in downtime and broken grain.</p>

<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0;font-weight:bold;color:#b30000;font-size:1.05em;"> SAFETY WARNING Dust, Machinery, and Confined Spaces</p>
<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0;color:#5a0000;">Rice dust is combustible. Fine dust from pre-cleaning, de-stoning, and husking can form an explosive atmosphere inside enclosed equipment, ducts, and silos. Never weld, cut, or smoke near dust collection systems. Use dust extraction, bond and earth all metal equipment, and clean settled dust regularly. Do not enter a silo or bin without confined-space procedures: test the air, use a harness and a second person outside, and never enter alone. Keep hands, clothing, and tools away from rotating rolls, belts, and sieves lock out and tag out before maintenance. In many African mills, the biggest safety risks are unguarded drive belts, accumulated dust, and unauthorised entry into bins during cleaning.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li>Test moisture of every incoming paddy lot before acceptance; record supplier, moisture, and foreign matter.</li>
<li>Run pre-cleaner at the designed feed rate; check sieve condition and aspiration at the start of each shift.</li>
<li>Verify de-stoner deck angle and air setting; check stone discharge is steady and not carrying grain.</li>
<li>Clean magnetic separators daily and log collected metal; inspect for tramp metal at intake.</li>
<li>Confirm paddy moisture is 1214% before husking; do not mill wet paddy to meet a deadline.</li>
<li>Allow 1224 hours tempering after drying before milling.</li>
<li>Check storage moisture and temperature weekly; run aeration when temperature or humidity conditions require it.</li>
<li>Keep a supplier quality log and review it monthly to guide buying decisions.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Business takeaway:</strong> Pre-milling is where margin is made or lost. Clean paddy, correct moisture, and proper tempering are not technical niceties they are the difference between a mill that competes for contracts and one that discounts its output. For African mills serving regional traders, institutional buyers, and local markets, disciplined pre-milling is the cheapest route to higher head rice recovery and stronger customer trust.</p>
