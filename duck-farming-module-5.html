1. Selecting Breeding Stock

Selecting good breeding ducks is the foundation of a profitable flock. Start by choosing ducks that are healthy, active, and free from defects. For meat production, look for fast growth, broad breast, and strong legs. For egg production, choose ducks that lay early and often. In Africa, common breeds are Muscovy, Pekin, and Khaki Campbell. Muscovy is hardy and good for meat; Khaki Campbell is excellent for eggs.

Select birds that are at least 6-8 months old. They should have bright eyes, clean nostrils, and smooth feathers. Avoid birds with crooked bills, lameness, or pasty vent. Check the vent: in layers, it should be moist and wide; in males, the vent should be flexible. For breeding, choose ducks from families with high hatchability and low mortality.

Keep records: tag or mark your best birds. A good ratio is 1 drake to 4-6 ducks for Muscovy, or 1:5 for Pekin. Never keep more than one drake per pen unless you have many females, as they will fight and injure each other. Replace breeding stock every 2-3 years to avoid inbreeding. Introduce new bloodlines every few years.

In Africa, heat stress can reduce fertility. Select ducks that tolerate high temperatures. Provide shade and cool water. Feed breeders a diet with 18-20% protein, plus extra calcium for layers. Oyster shell or limestone grit should be available free-choice. Always select from your best performers, not from the cull pen. Healthy breeders produce healthy chicks, which means more money for you.

2. Mating Ratios and Breeding Pens

Getting the mating ratio right is critical for fertile eggs. Too many drakes stress the females and cause injuries; too few mean many eggs won't be fertile. For most breeds, use 1 drake for every 4 to 6 ducks. For heavy breeds like Muscovy, 1:4 is best; for lighter breeds like Khaki Campbell, 1:6 works. If you have multiple drakes, give each his own pen or at least 10 females per drake to reduce fighting.

Breeding pens should be spacious: at least 1 square meter per duck inside, plus an outdoor run of 2-3 square meters per duck. The pen must have a shelter for shade, rain, and predators. Use wire mesh buried 30 cm deep to stop digging predators. Provide clean bedding like wood shavings or straw, changed weekly. A small pond or trough for bathing is important for mating; ducks mate more successfully in water. If no pond, use a large tub with fresh water daily.

In Africa, many farmers use raised pens with slatted floors to ease cleaning and reduce disease. Ensure good ventilation but no drafts. Keep the pen dry. Introduce the drake to the females at least 2 weeks before you want fertile eggs. Watch for bullying: if a female is being over-mated, separate her. Collect eggs daily for hatching. A good breeding pen produces 80-90% fertile eggs. Keep records of matings and egg fertility to adjust ratios. Replace old drakes every 2 years. Always provide fresh water and feed in the pen, not outside where wild birds can contaminate it.

3. Egg Collection and Storage for Hatching

Collect eggs at least twice a day, early morning and late afternoon. Eggs left in the sun or mud will spoil. Use clean hands or a soft cloth to pick them up. Do not wash eggs unless heavily soiled; washing removes the protective bloom and lets bacteria in. If you must wash, use warm water (37Â°C) with a mild disinfectant, then dry gently. Store eggs in clean, open trays or cartons, pointed end down. This keeps the air cell stable.

For hatching, store eggs at 15-18Â°C (59-65Â°F) and 75-80% humidity. In hot African climates, a cool room or evaporative cooler works. Never store eggs in a refrigeratorâit's too cold and will kill the embryo. Store for no more than 7 days; after 7 days, hatchability drops. Turn eggs at least twice a day during storage to prevent the embryo sticking to the shell. Mark eggs with a pencil to track turning.

Select only clean, uncracked, normal-shaped eggs. Discard thin-shelled, double-yolked, or very large eggs. Handle eggs gently; a cracked shell lets in bacteria. Before setting, warm eggs to room temperature (25Â°C) for 4-6 hours. This reduces condensation when they go into the incubator. In areas with power cuts, plan to set eggs when you can monitor them. Keep records: date collected, number set, and source pen. Good storage means up to 90% of fertile eggs will hatch. Poor storage can drop that to 50% or less. Always collect eggs from healthy breeders and store them properly to maximise your hatch.

4. Incubation Techniques and Equipment

Duck eggs need 28 days to hatch (Muscovy may take 35 days). You can use a still-air incubator, forced-air incubator, or a broody duck. Forced-air is best for large batches. Set temperature at 37.5Â°C (99.5Â°F) for forced-air; for still-air, set 38.5Â°C (101Â°F) at the top of the egg. Humidity should be 55-60% for the first 25 days, then 65-75% for the last 3 days. Use a hygrometer to check humidity. If you don't have one, watch the air cell size when candling.

Turn eggs at least 4 times a day, but 6-8 times is better. If your incubator has an automatic turner, use it. Otherwise, mark eggs with an X and O and turn 180 degrees each time. Stop turning on day 25 (lockdown). Ventilation is important: open vents slightly to allow oxygen in and carbon dioxide out. In Africa, power cuts are common. Keep a backup battery or a kerosene lamp incubator. A simple still-air incubator can be made from a wooden box with a 40-watt bulb and a thermostat.

Before setting eggs, run the incubator for 24 hours to stabilise temperature. Place a thermometer at egg level. Set eggs pointed end down if using trays, or lay them flat if turning by hand. Never mix eggs of different ages in the same incubator. Keep a record of setting date and expected hatch date. Clean and disinfect the incubator between batches. For small-scale farmers, a broody Muscovy duck can hatch 15-20 eggs. Let her sit on a nest in a quiet, dark place. Provide food and water nearby. She will turn the eggs herself. Whichever method you choose, consistency is key. Check temperature and humidity daily, and adjust as needed.

5. Candling and Embryo Development

Candling means shining a bright light through the egg to see the embryo inside. Do it in a dark room. Use a candling lamp, a strong torch, or even a phone flashlight. Candling helps you remove infertile eggs and dead embryos, preventing them from rotting and exploding in the incubator. Candling is safe if done quickly and gently. Wash your hands first.

Candle on day 7 and day 14. On day 7, a fertile egg shows a spider-like network of blood vessels and a small dark spot (the embryo). Infertile eggs look clear, like a fresh egg. Dead embryos show a blood ring or a dark mass with no veins. Remove clear and dead eggs immediately. On day 14, the embryo is larger, and you can see movement. The air cell should be about one-third of the egg. By day 21, the egg is mostly dark, and the air cell is larger. You may see the duckling's bill in the air cell. Do not candle after day 25; it can harm the hatching duckling.

In Africa, many farmers skip candling, but it's worth the effort. It saves incubator space and prevents contamination. Keep records of how many fertile eggs you had. If fertility is low, check your mating ratio and breeder nutrition. Embryo development stages: day 1-2: blastoderm; day 3-4: blood vessels; day 5-7: eye pigment; day 8-10: feathers; day 11-14: beak; day 15-20: scales and claws; day 21-25: growth; day 26-28: internal pip, external pip, hatch. Understanding these stages helps you know if things are on track. If many eggs die at day 7, it could be due to poor storage or temperature spikes. Adjust and try again.

6. Hatch Management and Assisted Hatching

The last three days are critical. On day 25, stop turning eggs and increase humidity to 65-75%. This is called lockdown. Do not open the incubator unless absolutely necessary. The ducklings will internally pip (break into the air cell) around day 26, then externally pip (break the shell) around day 27. Hatching usually takes 24-48 hours. Do not help unless the duckling is stuck for more than 12 hours after external pip and the membrane is dry and brown. Assisted hatching is risky; if you help too early, the duckling may bleed to death. If you must help, use clean hands, warm water, and tweezers. Moisten the membrane with warm water, then carefully peel away small pieces of shell. Stop if you see blood.

After hatching, leave ducklings in the incubator until they are dry and fluffy (6-12 hours). Then move them to a brooder. The brooder should be warm: 30-32Â°C (86-90Â°F) for the first week, reducing by 3Â°C each week. Use a heat lamp or a charcoal stove with a guard. Provide clean water in shallow dishes with stones to prevent drowning. Feed starter crumbs with 20-22% protein. Dip beaks in water to teach them to drink. Keep bedding dry and clean. In Africa, watch for predators like snakes and rats. A wire mesh cover on the brooder helps. Hatch rates vary: good management gives 80-90% of fertile eggs hatching. If you have many unhatched eggs, check temperature, humidity, and turning. Keep records of hatch rate and sale of ducklings. Healthy ducklings grow fast and can be sold at 4-6 weeks for meat or kept for layers. Always clean and disinfect the incubator after each hatch.

Practical Reference: Duck Breeding and Incubation

It is written for small- and medium-scale commercial producers who sell day-old ducklings, table eggs, or breeding stock. Every figure here has a business consequence: hatchability below 70% destroys margins, and poor storage can wipe out an entire week of egg collection.
