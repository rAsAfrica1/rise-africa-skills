1. Why Thin Aluminium Is Hard

Thin aluminium, usually 1mm to 3mm thick, is tricky to weld because it conducts heat away very quickly and has a low melting point. Unlike steel, aluminium doesn't glow red when it gets hot, so you can't see when it's about to melt. By the time you notice, you've already burned a hole. Also, aluminium forms a tough oxide layer that melts at about 2072C, while the metal underneath melts at around 660C. This oxide layer must be removed or broken up during welding, or you'll get poor fusion and porosity.

In Africa, thin aluminium is common in roofing sheets, cooking pots, window frames, and repair work on vehicles and boats. Many workshops use basic AC stick welders or small MIG units without pulse. The challenge is that these machines often have limited heat control, making it easy to blow through thin sections. You need to be patient and use techniques that manage heat input carefully.

Another issue is that aluminium expands and contracts a lot when heated. This can cause warping and distortion, especially on long seams. Thin material is even more prone because it has less stiffness. You'll often see the metal buckle as you weld. To avoid this, you need to use tack welds, clamps, and sometimes chill blocks to hold the shape and pull heat away.

Finally, thin aluminium is often dirty or oxidised, especially if it's been outside. Dirt, oil, and moisture can cause porosity and weak welds. Proper cleaning with a stainless steel brush and acetone is critical. In many African settings, clean conditions are hard to maintain, so you must take extra care to prepare the metal just before welding.

All these factors mean that welding thin aluminium requires a different approach: lower heat, faster travel, and good preparation. This module will teach you how to set up your machine, control your travel speed, use backing bars, and test your work so you can make strong, leak-free welds on thin material.

2. Machine Setup for Thin Material

Setting up your welder correctly is half the battle when welding thin aluminium. If you're using MIG, you need a spool gun or a Teflon liner because aluminium wire is soft and will birdnest in a standard liner. For thin material, use a 0.8mm or 1.0mm aluminium wire. Set your voltage and wire speed according to the thickness. As a starting point, for 1.5mm aluminium, try 16-18 volts and wire speed around 3-4 meters per minute. But every machine is different, so practice on scrap.

If you're using TIG, you need AC current and a tungsten electrode. For thin aluminium, use a 1.6mm or 2.4mm tungsten, preferably lanthanated (gold) or ceriated (grey). Sharpen it to a point, but for AC, a slightly blunted tip works better. Set your amperage low: for 1.5mm, start at 40-60 amps. Use a foot pedal if you have one, so you can control heat instantly. If not, set your machine to the lowest stable amperage.

For stick welding (SMAW), it's very difficult on thin aluminium because electrodes are designed for thicker material. If you must, use a 2.5mm aluminium electrode and keep your arc very short. But I recommend MIG or TIG for thin work.

Gas flow is critical. Use pure argon for both MIG and TIG. Set flow rate to 10-15 litres per minute. Too little and you get porosity; too much and you get turbulence that draws in air. Check for leaks in your gas lines.

In Africa, power supply can be unstable. Use a voltage stabiliser or generator with clean output. Fluctuating voltage will ruin your welds. Also, keep your machine clean and dry; dust and humidity damage electronics. Before welding, always run a test bead on scrap of the same thickness to dial in your settings. Remember, thin aluminium needs lower heat and faster travel than thick material, so start conservative and adjust.

3. Travel Speed and Heat Input

Travel speed is your main tool for controlling heat input when welding thin aluminium. If you move too slowly, heat builds up and you burn through. If you move too fast, you don't get enough penetration and the weld sits on top. The sweet spot is a steady, consistent speed that melts the edges without melting through. For 1.5mm aluminium, a good starting speed is about 20-30 cm per minute with MIG, and slightly slower with TIG. But you must adjust based on what you see.

Watch the weld pool. It should be small, about 3-5mm wide, and shiny. If it gets too wide or starts to sink, you're too slow. Speed up or reduce amperage. If the pool looks like it's not wetting in, you're too fast. Slow down slightly. With TIG, you can also use the foot pedal to back off heat as you go. With MIG, you can't change heat on the fly easily, so you rely on travel speed and technique.

Another trick is to use a backstep technique: weld short segments, then skip ahead and weld back towards the previous bead. This lets each segment cool before you weld next to it. It's very effective on thin material. Also, use a stitching motion: move forward, pause, move forward. This gives the metal a moment to cool.

Heat input is also affected by your arc length. Keep your arc short, especially with MIG. A long arc spreads heat and causes more distortion. For TIG, keep the tungsten close to the work, about 2-3mm. And don't linger in one spot. Keep moving.

In hot African climates, the ambient temperature is already high, so the metal starts warm. That means you have even less margin before burn-through. Work in the shade if possible, and let the metal cool between passes. If you're welding a long seam, clamp a heat sink like a piece of copper or aluminium block next to the weld to draw heat away. Practice on scrap until you can maintain a consistent speed without burning through.

4. Backing Bars and Chill Blocks

Backing bars and chill blocks are simple but powerful tools for welding thin aluminium. A backing bar is a piece of metal you place behind the joint to support the molten pool and pull heat away. Chill blocks are similar but are usually clamped alongside the weld to absorb heat. Both help prevent burn-through and distortion. For thin aluminium, you can use copper, brass, or even thick aluminium as backing. Copper is best because it conducts heat away very fast, but it's expensive. In many African workshops, you can find scrap copper busbar or thick aluminium plate.

To use a backing bar, clean it and clamp it tightly behind the joint. The weld will fuse to the aluminium but not to the copper or steel backing, so you can remove it afterwards. If you're using aluminium backing, it might fuse to the workpiece, so coat it with a little chalk or use a release agent. For butt welds, a backing bar with a groove can help shape the back bead. For lap welds, a flat bar works fine.

Chill blocks are placed on the top surface, alongside the weld, to absorb heat. You can clamp them on both sides of the seam. They act as heat sinks, reducing the chance of burn-through and warping. Use them especially on long seams or when welding near edges. You can also use wet rags or compressed air to cool the weld between passes, but be careful not to quench too fast, as it can cause cracking.

In Africa, where thin aluminium is often used for roofing and repairs, backing bars are essential when welding overhead or vertical. They hold the molten metal in place. You can make your own backing bars from scrap metal. Just make sure they are clean and flat. Always clamp them securely so they don't move. After welding, remove the backing and clean any residue. With practice, you'll find that backing bars and chill blocks let you weld thinner material than you thought possible.

5. Avoiding Burn-Through

Burn-through is the most common problem when welding thin aluminium. It happens when the heat input is too high for the thickness, and the molten pool collapses, leaving a hole. To avoid it, you need to control heat at every step. First, make sure your material is clean. Dirt and oxide force you to use more heat to get fusion, increasing burn-through risk. Clean with a stainless steel brush and acetone right before welding.

Second, set your machine correctly. Use the lowest amperage that gives you a good weld. For 1mm aluminium, TIG amperage might be 30-50 amps; for MIG, voltage around 14-16 volts. Always test on scrap. If you're using a foot pedal, keep your heat low and only increase when needed. With MIG, you can't adjust heat on the fly, so you rely on travel speed and technique.

Third, use a fast travel speed. Move steadily and don't pause too long in one spot. If you see the pool getting too hot, speed up. With TIG, you can also add filler rod more frequently to cool the pool. The filler rod absorbs heat as it melts. For MIG, use a push technique (pushing the gun away from the weld) rather than drag, as it gives better visibility and control.

Fourth, use backing bars and chill blocks as described in the previous lesson. They are your best defence against burn-through. Also, consider using a pulse function if your machine has it. Pulse welding alternates high and low current, giving the pool time to cool. If you don't have pulse, you can simulate it by using a stitching motion: weld for a second, pause, weld again.

Finally, if you do burn through, don't panic. Stop, let it cool, and repair the hole. You can fill it with filler rod or a small piece of aluminium. Then grind smooth. With practice, you'll develop a feel for how much heat the metal can take. Remember, thin aluminium gives you very little warning before it melts, so always err on the side of less heat and faster travel.

6. Testing Your Work

After welding thin aluminium, you must test your work to ensure it's strong and leak-free. Visual inspection is first. Look for a smooth, consistent bead with even ripples. There should be no holes, cracks, or undercut. Undercut is when the edges of the weld melt away, leaving a groove. It weakens the joint. Also, check for porosity: small holes on the surface or inside. Porosity comes from dirt, moisture, or poor gas coverage. If you see black soot around the weld, that's a sign of poor shielding gas or too much heat.

For a more thorough test, you can do a bend test. Cut a sample across the weld, then bend it in a vice. If the weld is good, it will bend without cracking. If it cracks, the weld is weak. Another test is the dye penetrant test. Clean the weld, spray on dye, wait, then wipe and spray developer. Cracks and porosity will show up as red lines or spots. This is useful for critical parts like fuel tanks or boat hulls.

For leak testing, you can use water or air. For a tank, fill it with water and look for leaks. For air, pressurise to a low pressure (like 0.5 bar) and spray soapy water on the weld. Bubbles indicate a leak. In Africa, where many aluminium repairs are for cooking pots, water tanks, and roofing, leak testing is essential. A leaky weld will fail quickly.

Also, check for distortion. Thin aluminium warps easily. Place a straight edge along the weld. If there's a gap, it's warped. You can sometimes straighten it with heat and quenching, but prevention is better. Use tack welds and chill blocks.

Finally, record your settings and results. Keep a notebook of what worked for different thicknesses. Over time, you'll build a reference that helps you weld faster and better. Remember, testing isn't just about finding faults; it's about learning. Every weld you test teaches you something. So test regularly, even on scrap, to improve your skill.

<h2>Practical Reference: Welding Thin Aluminium in African Fabrication Shops</h2>

<p>Thin aluminium sheet typically 1.0 mm to 3.0 mm is the backbone of African light fabrication: aluminium roofing accessories, window and door frames, tank panels, cold-room linings, boat hulls on the lakes, and the growing market for solar mounting brackets and battery enclosures. The margins on these jobs are thin, and burn-through is the fastest way to turn a profitable order into scrap. This reference module gives you the numbers, settings, and shop-floor habits that keep thin aluminium work profitable and repeatable.</p>

<p>Treat this lesson as your bench manual. It assumes you have completed the fundamentals module, are using a TIG (GTAW) or MIG (GMAW) set with AC or pulsed capability, and are working in a shop where power supply may fluctuate. Where settings differ between TIG and MIG, both are given.</p>

<h3>Why Thin Aluminium Is Hard</h3>
<p>Aluminium conducts heat roughly five times faster than mild steel and has no colour change before it melts. By the time you see the puddle, you are already at the burn-through threshold. Its oxide layer melts at around 2,072 C while the base metal melts at about 660 C, so you must break the oxide without overheating the parent metal. Combined with low hot strength thin sheet sags under its own weight this makes fit-up, clamping, and heat control the real skills, not the torch angle.</p>

<h3>Machine Setup for Thin Material</h3>
<p>For TIG on 1.6 mm sheet, set AC frequency high (120200 Hz) to focus the arc and narrow the heat-affected zone. Use a pointed, lightly balled tungsten (2% lanthanated is the shop favourite in most African cities because it holds up on AC). For MIG, use a spool gun or push-pull torch with 0.8 mm 4043 or 5356 wire and short arc / pulsed transfer. Keep your gas flow at 1215 L/min of pure argon; drafts from open shop doors are a common cause of porosity.</p>

<h3>Travel Speed and Heat Input</h3>
<p>Heat input is the single most controllable variable in your shop. The formula is:</p>
<p><strong>Heat input (kJ/mm) = (Voltage Amperage 60) (Travel speed in mm/min 1000)</strong></p>
<p>For 1.6 mm aluminium, aim for 0.30.6 kJ/mm. That means moving faster than most beginners are comfortable with. If you slow down to "see the puddle better", you will burn through. Practice a steady, rhythmic travel with a slight forward push angle of 1015.</p>

<h3>Backing Bars and Chill Blocks</h3>
<p>In African shops, copper backing bars are often made from reclaimed busbar or old transformer windings an excellent low-cost solution. Stainless steel or aluminium backing bars also work but conduct heat less efficiently. The purpose is threefold: support the molten puddle, pull heat out of the weld zone, and shield the root from oxidation. For pipe and tank work, purge the backside with argon at 510 L/min if a backing bar is not possible.</p>

<strong style="color:#c00;"> SAFETY WARNING ALUMINIUM WELDING HAZARDS</strong>
<ul style="margin:8px 0 0 18px;">
<li><strong>Aluminium oxide fume and ozone</strong> are generated at high current. Weld only with local exhaust ventilation or a fitted respirator (P2/P3). Never weld in a closed room this is common in small African workshops and causes chronic lung damage.</li>
<li><strong>Arc eye and UV burn</strong>: use a minimum shade 1011 auto-darkening helmet. Thin aluminium means high frequency AC UV output is higher than DC steel welding.</li>
<li><strong>Fire risk</strong>: sparks travel far. Clear sawdust, paint thinners, and petrol containers from a 10 m radius. Keep a CO or dry powder extinguisher within reach.</li>
<li><strong>Electrical</strong>: unstable mains supply and damp floors are common. Use an RCD/earth leakage breaker and never work with wet gloves.</li>
<li><strong>Gas cylinder handling</strong>: secure argon cylinders upright; a falling cylinder can shear the valve and turn into a missile.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h3>Avoiding Burn-Through</h3>
<p>Burn-through is caused by too much heat in one spot, poor fit-up (gaps force you to fill and dwell), or a backing bar that is not clamped tight. Practical fixes:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Tack every 4060 mm</strong> before welding. Tacks cool the joint and hold gaps closed.</li>
<li><strong>Use pulse</strong>: 12 pulses per second at 50% background current gives you time to reposition without losing the puddle.</li>
<li><strong>Weld downhill on vertical joints</strong> where code allows gravity helps you move faster.</li>
<li><strong>If you burn through</strong>: stop, let it cool, clean the hole, and repair with a small patch tacked from the back. Do not try to fill a hole while the surrounding metal is still hot it will grow.</li>
<li><strong>Use a heat sink paste</strong> or clamp a scrap aluminium block alongside the weld line.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Testing Your Work</h3>
<p>For structural or pressure work, visual inspection is not enough. In a typical African job shop, use these low-cost checks:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Visual:</strong> uniform ripple, no undercut, no porosity, no black soot (soot = gas or cleaning issue).</li>
<li><strong>Dye penetrant</strong> on non-porous welds cheap and effective for crack detection.</li>
<li><strong>Bend test</strong> on a coupon: bend the weld over a 3 thickness radius. Cracks indicate lack of fusion.</li>
<li><strong>Water/soap pressure test</strong> for tanks: pressurise to 0.5 bar and look for bubbles.</li>
<li><strong>Macro etch</strong> for critical jobs: cut, polish, and etch with caustic soda to reveal penetration.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Material identified (5052, 6061, etc.) and cleaned with a stainless brush + acetone within 30 minutes of welding.</li>
<li>Machine set to AC (TIG) or pulsed (MIG), argon flow 1215 L/min, tungsten sharpened and correct diameter.</li>
<li>Joint fit-up gap 0.5 mm; tacks every 4060 mm.</li>
<li>Backing bar or chill block clamped tight under the joint.</li>
<li>Heat input calculated or settings taken from Table 1 and adjusted for ambient temperature.</li>
<li>PPE on: shade 1011 helmet, P2/P3 respirator, leather gloves, no exposed skin.</li>
<li>Fire watch: extinguisher within reach, combustibles cleared 10 m.</li>
<li>Post-weld: visual check, then dye pen or pressure test as required by the client.</li>
</ol>
