1. Selecting High-Quality Seeds for Your Region

Choosing the right tomato seed is the first step to a successful harvest. In Africa, you need varieties that tolerate heat, resist local diseases, and produce well in your specific climate. Start by identifying your agro-ecological zone: highlands (cool, wet), mid-altitude (moderate), or lowlands (hot, dry). For highlands, choose varieties like 'Moneymaker' or 'Roma' that set fruit at lower temperatures. In hot lowlands, pick heat-tolerant types such as 'Tengeru 97' or 'Pesa' from Tanzania. If you face bacterial wilt, look for grafted seeds or resistant varieties like 'MT 56'.

Always buy certified seed from a reputable agro-dealer or government research station. Avoid saving seeds from hybrid tomatoes because they won't grow true to type. Check the label for germination percentage (should be above 85%), purity (above 98%), and the production date (use within one year). For open-pollinated varieties, you can save seeds from your best plants, but ferment them first: scoop seeds into a jar, add water, let sit for 3 days, then rinse and dry.

Test germination before planting: count 10 seeds, place them on a wet paper towel, keep moist and warm (25-30C). After 7-10 days, count how many sprout. If fewer than 7 germinate, buy new seed. Store seeds in a cool, dark, dry placea sealed jar with a desiccant packet works well. Never store in a plastic bag in the sun.

Consider seed treatments. Coating seeds with fungicide (like Thiram) reduces damping-off. For organic growers, soak seeds in hot water (50C) for 25 minutes to kill seed-borne bacteria, then dry. Always wear gloves when handling treated seeds. Finally, calculate how much seed you need: about 100-150 grams per hectare for direct seeding, or 50 grams for transplanting. Buy enough for the season plus 10% extra.

2. Seed Starting Indoors: Timing and Materials

Starting tomato seeds indoors gives you a head start, especially in areas with short rainy seasons. Begin 4-6 weeks before you plan to transplant to the field. In East Africa, if the long rains start in March, sow seeds in early February. In West Africa, for dry-season irrigation, sow in October for a November transplant. Use a calendar and count back from your expected last frost or first reliable rain.

You need clean containers: seedling trays, plastic cups with holes punched in the bottom, or wooden boxes. Avoid using soil from your garden because it may carry diseases and compacts easily. Instead, make your own seed-starting mix: 1 part sterilized garden soil, 1 part river sand, and 1 part well-rotted manure or compost. Sterilize soil by baking it in an oven at 180C for 30 minutes or pouring boiling water over it and letting it cool. Fill containers to 1 cm from the top.

Sow seeds 0.5 cm deep, one seed per cell or 2-3 seeds in a cup, then thin to the strongest seedling. Water gently with a fine rose watering can or spray bottle until the mix is moist but not soggy. Cover trays with a plastic sheet or newspaper to keep humidity high. Keep them in a warm place (20-25C) away from direct sun. Germination takes 5-10 days.

As soon as seedlings emerge, remove the cover and move them to bright lighta sunny windowsill or a shade net structure that lets in 50% light. If light is poor, seedlings become tall and weak (leggy). Turn trays daily so stems grow straight. Water from the bottom by placing trays in a shallow pan of water for 10 minutes, then remove. This prevents damping-off. Label each variety with a waterproof marker and date. With proper timing and clean materials, you'll have strong transplants ready for the next stage.

3. Managing Temperature, Light, and Moisture for Germination

Tomato seeds germinate best at 20-25C. Below 15C, germination is slow and uneven; above 35C, seeds may die. In hot African lowlands, start seeds in the coolest part of the dayearly morning or eveningand place trays in a shaded nursery. In highlands, use a cold frame or greenhouse to trap warmth. You can also use a simple thermometer to monitor soil temperature. If it's too cold, place trays on a warm surface like a sunny rock or use a heated mat if available. For small-scale growers, a black plastic sheet over the soil before sowing can raise temperature by 2-3C.

Light is critical after germination. Once seedlings break the surface, they need 12-16 hours of bright light daily. If growing indoors, use fluorescent tubes 5 cm above the plants, moving them up as plants grow. Outdoors, use a shade net that allows 50-70% lighttoo much direct sun scorches young leaves, too little causes leggy stems. In areas with heavy rain, a simple polythene tunnel protects seedlings from downpours and keeps light adequate.

Moisture must be consistent. The seed-starting mix should feel like a wrung-out spongenever dry, never waterlogged. Water with a fine mist twice daily in hot weather, once daily in cool weather. Overwatering causes damping-off, a fungal disease that rots stems at the soil line. To prevent it, ensure good drainage, avoid overcrowding, and water in the morning so leaves dry by evening. If you see white mold on the soil surface, reduce watering and increase airflow.

You can make a self-watering system using a cotton wick: place one end in a water reservoir and the other in the tray. The wick pulls water as needed. For large nurseries, use drip irrigation with a timer. Check moisture by sticking your finger 2 cm into the mix. If it's dry, water; if wet, wait. Keep a record of temperature, watering, and germination dates to learn what works best in your area.

4. Transplanting Seedlings into Larger Containers

When seedlings have 2-3 true leaves (not the first two seed leaves), they need more space. Transplant them into larger containersindividual pots, plastic bags, or seedbedsso roots can grow without competition. This is called 'pricking out' or 'potting on'. Do it in the evening or on a cloudy day to reduce shock. Water the seedlings thoroughly a few hours before transplanting so the soil holds together.

Prepare your new containers: 8-10 cm diameter pots or 10x15 cm plastic bags with drainage holes. Use the same soil mix as before, but add a handful of well-rotted manure or a teaspoon of NPK 17:17:17 per pot. Moisten the mix. Using a small stick or pencil, make a hole in the center deep enough to bury the stem up to the first leaves. Tomatoes can be buried deeper than they were growingthis promotes new roots along the stem.

Gently lift a seedling by its leaf (never the stem) from the seed tray. If roots are tangled, separate them carefully. Place the seedling in the hole, fill with soil, and press gently to remove air pockets. Water immediately with a dilute starter solution (1 tablespoon of NPK 17:17:17 in 10 liters of water) to reduce transplant shock. Keep the transplanted seedlings in a shaded area for 2-3 days, then gradually expose them to more sun.

Space pots 10 cm apart to allow air circulation. Water daily, but avoid wetting leaves to prevent fungal diseases. After one week, fertilize weekly with a balanced liquid fertilizer. If you see yellow lower leaves, add a little nitrogen. Seedlings are ready for the field when they are 15-20 cm tall, have a thick stem, and 5-7 true leavesusually 4-6 weeks after sowing. Hardening off comes next.

5. Hardening Off Seedlings Before Field Planting

Hardening off is the process of gradually exposing seedlings to outdoor conditions so they can survive the shock of transplanting. Without it, seedlings grown indoors or in a sheltered nursery may wilt, sunburn, or die when moved to the field. Start hardening off 7-10 days before your planned field planting date. If your seedlings were already outdoors in a shade net, you still need to reduce water and expose them to more sun and wind.

On day 1, move seedlings outside to a protected spot with morning sun and no wind for 2 hours. Bring them back indoors or under shade. On day 2, leave them out for 4 hours. Day 3: 6 hours. Day 4: 8 hours, including some direct sun. By day 7, leave them out overnight if temperatures stay above 15C. If a cold spell or heavy rain is forecast, delay. In hot lowlands, avoid midday sun during hardening; use early morning and late afternoon sun instead.

Reduce watering during hardening. Let the soil dry slightly between wateringsthis encourages roots to grow deeper and toughens the plant. But don't let them wilt completely. If leaves start to yellow or drop, you're pushing too hard; back off for a day. A dilute seaweed extract or compost tea spray can help reduce stress.

If you don't have space to move trays in and out, use a shade net that you can open and close. Start with 50% shade, then remove it gradually. Another method: place seedlings under a tree that provides dappled light, then move them to full sun after a week. Hardened seedlings have thicker stems, darker green leaves, and a stronger root system. They will establish faster and produce earlier. Always transplant in the evening or on a cloudy day, and water immediately after planting.

6. Grafting Techniques for Disease Resistance

Grafting joins a tasty tomato variety (scion) onto a rootstock that resists soil-borne diseases like bacterial wilt, fusarium, and nematodes. This is especially valuable in Africa where these diseases devastate crops. You need rootstock seeds (e.g., 'Beaufort', 'Maxifort', or local resistant varieties like 'Hawaii 7996') and your preferred scion. Start rootstock seeds 5-7 days before scion seeds so stems are the same size.

Use the 'tube graft' or 'slant graft' method. When seedlings have 2-3 true leaves and stems are 2-3 mm thick, cut the rootstock stem at a 45 angle about 2 cm above the soil, leaving one cotyledon. Cut the scion stem at a matching angle, also 2 cm above the soil. Slide the two cut surfaces together so they interlock. Secure with a small grafting clip or a piece of aluminum foil wrapped gently. Keep the grafted plant in a humid, shaded healing chamber for 5-7 daysa plastic tent with 90% humidity and 25C works well. No direct light during this time.

After 5 days, open the chamber slightly each day to reduce humidity gradually. After 10 days, remove the clip if the graft has taken. You'll know it's successful if the scion stays green and starts growing. If it wilts, the graft failed; discard. Harden off grafted plants like normal seedlings before transplanting. Plant them with the graft union 2-3 cm above the soil line to prevent the scion from rooting and losing disease resistance.

Grafting costs more and takes skill, but it can double yields in infected fields. For smallholders, buy grafted seedlings from a reputable nursery if you can't graft yourself. Always sterilize your cutting tool with alcohol or bleach between plants to avoid spreading disease. With practice, you can produce your own grafted tomatoes and reduce losses significantly.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Seed Selection and Transplant Production for Tomato Farming in Africa

Tomato farming is one of the most profitable horticultural enterprises in Africa, but profitability begins long before the first fruit is harvested. It begins with the seed. Whether you are a smallholder producing for local markets or a commercial grower supplying supermarkets and processors, the quality of your seed and the health of your transplants determine your yield ceiling, your input costs, and your access to premium buyers. This practical reference consolidates the essential knowledge from this module into a single, business-focused guide for African tomato producers.

Across sub-Saharan Africa, tomato yields average 1015 tonnes per hectare, compared with 4060 tonnes per hectare in well-managed commercial systems. A significant portion of this gap is attributable to poor seed selection, weak seedling management, and inadequate hardening before transplanting. Each of these factors is within your control. This lesson provides the data, diagrams, and checklists you need to close that gap.

SAFETY WARNING: Seed Treatment and Chemical Handling

Many certified tomato seeds are sold pre-treated with fungicides (e.g., thiram, captan) or insecticides (e.g., imidacloprid). These treated seeds are toxic if ingested and can cause skin and eye irritation. Always wear gloves when handling treated seed. Never eat, drink, or smoke while handling. Store treated seed away from food, feed, and children. In Africa, where informal seed exchange is common, never assume seed is untreated check the label or ask the supplier. If you treat seed yourself with hot water (50C for 25 minutes) to control bacterial pathogens, use a calibrated thermometer and do not exceed the temperature, as this can kill the embryo. Dispose of treatment water safely; do not pour into streams or wells. For grafting, use clean, sterilised blades and wash hands with soap after handling plant sap, which can cause skin irritation in sensitive individuals.

Quick Reference Checklist: Seed Selection and Transplant Production

  • Select certified, region-adapted seed choose varieties with resistance to local diseases (bacterial wilt, TYLCV, Fusarium) and matching your market (fresh, processing, export).
  • Verify seed quality check germination rate (minimum 85%), purity, and expiry date. Buy from licensed agro-dealers, not open markets.
  • Start seeds indoors or in a protected nursery use sterile media, clean trays, and sow at the correct depth (0.51 cm).
  • Manage temperature, light, and moisture maintain 2025C for germination, 1416 hours of light, and consistent moisture without waterlogging.
  • Pot up at the 23 true leaf stage transplant into larger containers to prevent root binding and promote strong root systems.
  • Harden off for 710 days gradually reduce water and increase sun exposure before field planting to prevent transplant shock.
  • Consider grafting for bacterial wilt or Fusarium-prone soils, graft onto resistant rootstock and heal under high humidity and shade.
  • Transplant at the right age 2535 days, 57 true leaves, and always in the cool of the evening or on a cloudy day.
