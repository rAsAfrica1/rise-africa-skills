Module 5

Starting Cattle Safely: The Adaptation Period

The first three weeks decide most of the profit or loss in a fattening cycle. This module covers the arrival routine, quarantine and health processing, the 14 to 21 day step-up onto a high concentrate ration, and how to recognise and prevent acidosis and bloat. It ends with daily bunk management and an honest look at what a botched transition actually costs.

What you will be able to do after this module

  • You will be able to run a correct arrival routine of rest, water and hay before any concentrate is fed.
  • You will be able to set up quarantine and a health processing routine, and say when a vet is required.
  • You will be able to write and follow a 14 to 21 day step-up schedule with defined step diets.
  • You will be able to recognise acute and subacute acidosis and act correctly on each.
  • You will be able to tell frothy bloat from free gas bloat and follow the correct emergency sequence.
  • You will be able to score bunks daily and use the score to decide the next feed delivery.

Lesson 5.113 min

The First 24 Hours: Rest, Water and Hay

In this lesson
  • Run a correct arrival routine for newly bought cattle
  • Explain why concentrate is withheld on arrival
  • Spot the animals that need attention on day one

Cattle arrive at your yard dehydrated, hungry, bruised and frightened. They have been walked or trucked, mixed with strangers, and often held at a sale yard without feed or water for many hours. Shrink of several percent of body weight is normal. What you do in the first 24 hours determines whether they eat well in week one, and whether you spend the next month treating pneumonia.

Before they arrive

  1. Have the receiving pen clean, bedded if you bed, and empty of other cattle.
  2. Fill and clean the water trough. Check the flow rate, not just the level.
  3. Put good quality grass or legume hay in the bunk before the truck arrives. Not concentrate. Hay.
  4. Have shade available if the day is hot.
  5. Have your record book, weigh scale or weigh band, ear tags and a working crush ready.

Unloading

Unload quietly. No shouting, no dogs, no electric prods. Cattle that are chased on arrival lose more weight, take longer to find water, and are more likely to get sick. Move them at a walk into the receiving pen and then leave them alone.

Water first

Thirsty cattle drink before they eat. Make sure the trough is obvious: running water makes a noise animals follow, so let the trough overflow slightly at first. Watch that every animal finds it. Newly arrived cattle from a range or communal grazing system may never have drunk from a concrete trough or a ball valve, and will stand thirsty next to water they do not recognise. If a group is not drinking, run water into an open dish or an old drum they can see into.

Do not let a very thirsty animal gorge itself unchecked in extreme cases, but for practical yard purposes free access to clean water on arrival is correct and is what you should provide.

Hay, not concentrate

Long grass hay is the right first feed. It restores the rumen mat, encourages rumination and saliva flow, and carries no risk of acidosis. A hungry, empty animal put straight on a grain ration is the classic acidosis and bloat case. Hold the cattle on hay and water only for roughly the first day, and often longer if they arrived badly stressed. Some yards give free choice hay for two to three days before any concentrate at all. That decision costs you a little time and saves you deaths.

Rest before processing

Do not weigh, vaccinate, dehorn, castrate or dose cattle the moment they come off the truck if you can avoid it. Handling on top of transport stress is what tips animals into respiratory disease. Give them rest, water and hay, then process the next day when they have settled. The exception is an animal that is visibly ill or injured, which is dealt with immediately.

The day one walk through

Walk the pen quietly twice on the first day and look for:

  • Animals standing apart from the group, head down, ears drooping.
  • Nasal discharge, coughing, or fast, shallow breathing.
  • Sunken eyes and a tucked up flank, which suggest continuing dehydration.
  • Lameness, wounds, horn damage or transport bruising.
  • Scour, and whether it is watery, bloody or mucous.
  • Animals not drinking at all.

Mark anything you find in the record book with the tag number and the date. Call a vet for animals showing fever signs, laboured breathing, bloody scour, or any animal that is down and cannot rise. Respiratory disease in newly arrived cattle progresses fast and is much cheaper to treat early.

Write down the start weight

Once cattle have rested and drunk, take a start weight. That number is the base for every gain, feed conversion and margin calculation in the rest of the course. Weigh at the same time of day each time, ideally before feeding, so that gut fill does not distort your comparisons.

Lesson 5.214 min

Quarantine and the Arrival Health Routine

In this lesson
  • Set up a workable quarantine pen
  • Decide what to treat on arrival and what to leave to a vet
  • Keep records that let you trace a problem back

Every group of bought cattle is a disease import. They come from different herds with different parasite burdens, different vaccination histories and, sometimes, notifiable disease. Quarantine is the cheapest insurance in the business, and most small fatteners skip it.

Setting up quarantine

  • Use a pen physically separate from your existing cattle, ideally with no shared fence line and no shared water trough or drainage.
  • Keep new arrivals there for a set period. Two to four weeks is the common range in practice. Ask your district veterinary office what period and what tests or movement permits apply in your country, because these are legal requirements, not preferences.
  • Feed and handle quarantine cattle last each day, then wash boots and hands before returning to the main yard.
  • Keep separate equipment where you can: buckets, halters, dosing guns.
  • Never mix a new group into an existing pen just because the pen has space.

The arrival processing routine

Do this on the day after arrival, once the cattle have rested, watered and eaten hay.

  1. Identify. Tag every animal with a number you control. Record the seller, the date, the purchase weight and the price.
  2. Weigh. Scale if you have one, weigh band if you do not. Record it.
  3. Inspect individually in the crush. Body condition, teeth and age, eyes, feet, skin, lumps, ticks, wounds, and for bulls or steers check the sheath and that they can urinate freely.
  4. Sex and status check. Confirm what you actually bought. Buying a supposed steer that turns out to be an entire bull changes your management and your market. Check for pregnancy in any female you bought as a fattener, because feeding a pregnant heifer to slaughter weight is a different and often losing proposition.
  5. Parasite control. Internal and external parasites drain gain. What you use, at what dose, and what withdrawal period applies must follow the product label and local veterinary advice, because resistance patterns and registered products differ by country.
  6. Vaccination. The core diseases differ by region, and some are legally controlled. Discuss with your vet which vaccines apply where you are, and note that some need a second dose weeks later to work at all.
  7. Record everything. Product name, batch number, dose, date, and the withdrawal period. Write the earliest legal slaughter date in your diary.

Where a vet is required, not optional

Call a veterinarian for any of the following:

  • More than a small number of animals in a group off feed at the same time.
  • Fever, laboured breathing, deep coughing, or nasal discharge that is thick or bloody.
  • Bloody scour, or scour that does not stop within a day or two.
  • Nervous signs: staggering, circling, head pressing, convulsions.
  • Any sudden death, especially more than one. Do not open a suspected anthrax carcase yourself; it is dangerous to you and spreads spores. Call the vet.
  • Blisters or erosions on the muzzle, mouth or feet with lameness and drooling, which may indicate a notifiable disease. Report it.
  • Any animal you intend to treat with a prescription medicine.

Records that earn their keep

Keep one page per group and one line per animal. At minimum record tag number, arrival date, source, arrival weight, treatments with dates and withdrawal periods, any illness, and exit weight and date. This is what lets you answer the questions that decide whether you make money: which seller gives cattle that perform, which group had the disease problem, and whether the treatment cost was worth the gain it saved.

Poor records also carry legal risk. Selling an animal before the end of a drug withdrawal period puts residues in the food chain and can end your access to a formal abattoir.

Lesson 5.316 min

The Step-Up: Moving to a High Concentrate Ration Over 14 to 21 Days

In this lesson
  • Build a written step-up schedule with defined diets
  • Apply the rules for holding or backing up a step
  • Adapt the rumen without wrecking it

The rumen is a fermentation vat run by a microbe population. That population is matched to the diet the animal was eating last week, not the one you want to feed. Move too fast and the starch fermenting bugs outrun the acid users, pH crashes, and you get acidosis. Cattle must be adapted to a high concentrate diet gradually over about 14 to 21 days.

The shape of a step-up

The standard approach is a series of step diets, each fed for a set number of days, in which the concentrate share of the ration dry matter rises and the roughage share falls. A typical structure uses three or four steps over 14 to 21 days, for example starting with roughage as the clear majority of dry matter and ending at the finishing ration. What matters is not the exact percentages, which depend on your feeds, but four rules:

  1. Each step is a real step of similar size, not a random daily creep.
  2. Each step is held for enough days for intake to settle, commonly around three to five days.
  3. You only move up when the pen has cleaned up its bunk consistently and is behaving normally.
  4. Roughage never goes to zero.

A worked schedule

StepDaysRoughage share of DMConcentrate share of DM
Receiving1 to 3Hay only, free choiceNone
Step 14 or 5Roughly 70 percentRoughly 30 percent
Step 24 or 5Roughly 55 percentRoughly 45 percent
Step 34 or 5Roughly 45 percentRoughly 55 percent
FinisherRest of the feeding periodYour chosen finishing roughage level, above zeroBalance

Adjust the levels to your feeds and your final ration. If your finishing ration is modest in concentrate, you can reach it in fewer steps. If it is very high in grain, use more steps and more days, toward the 21 day end.

Rules for holding and backing up

  • Hold the step if intake fell yesterday, if there is more feed left over than usual, if manure has become looser across the pen, or if the weather has turned very hot or a storm has disturbed the cattle.
  • Back up one step if you see loose, bubbly or pale manure across several animals, undigested grain in the manure, or a group of animals standing off feed.
  • Never make up a missed feeding. If cattle were not fed on time, feed the normal amount, not double. Doubling after a fast is the single most reliable way to cause fatal acidosis.
  • Never jump a step because cattle look hungry. Hungry looking cattle at the bunk are the normal appearance of a correctly managed step-up.

Mixing and delivery during the step-up

The percentages only mean something if the mix is even and the delivery is consistent. Mix thoroughly. Feed at the same times every day, ideally twice daily, and keep the interval between feeds even. Cattle are creatures of routine and a disturbed routine shows up immediately in intake.

Introduce every new ingredient gradually too, not only the concentrate as a whole. A sudden switch of grain source, a new load of silage from a different pit, or a first delivery of molasses all deserve their own short transition of several days.

What good adaptation looks like

By the end of the step-up you should see cattle eating quickly and evenly at the bunk, manure that is firm enough to stack in a low pile but not hard, a clear majority of resting animals chewing the cud, and daily intake climbing steadily rather than swinging up and down. Intake that swings between very high and very low days is the signature of subacute acidosis, and it costs you gain even when no animal ever looks properly ill.

Write the schedule down

Put the schedule on the pen wall with a column for each day: the date, the step, the kilograms of each ingredient, the leftover score, and the initials of who fed. Adaptation fails most often not because the manager did not know the plan but because a relief worker on a Sunday did not.

Lesson 5.415 min

Acidosis: Recognising It, Stopping It, Preventing It

In this lesson
  • Distinguish acute from subacute acidosis
  • Act correctly in the first hour of an acute case
  • Prevent acidosis through ration and routine

Acidosis is the disease that defines feedlot management. It is caused by rapid fermentation of starch or sugar producing acid faster than the rumen can buffer or absorb it. Rumen pH falls, fibre digesting microbes die, the rumen wall is damaged, and in bad cases the animal dies. Most acidosis you will actually experience is not the dramatic dead animal. It is the quiet version that steals your gain.

Acute acidosis

This follows a large, sudden intake of grain, molasses or other rapidly fermentable feed. Typical causes are a broken gate into the feed store, a spilt bag, a double feed after a missed feeding, an unmixed hot pocket in a load, or an animal that gorges after being off feed.

Signs, usually within several hours to a day:

  • Depression, standing apart, not eating.
  • Distended, sloshing abdomen and no rumen contractions.
  • Profuse watery, foul smelling, often pale or grey diarrhoea, sometimes with grain visible.
  • Staggering, weakness, teeth grinding, and eventually going down.
  • Sunken eyes from severe dehydration.

This is a veterinary emergency. Call the vet immediately. Meanwhile, remove all concentrate from the pen, offer good long hay and clean water, and identify how much every animal in the pen may have eaten, because acute cases usually come in groups. Do not attempt aggressive home drenching or dosing of unknown quantities of household chemicals. Treatment of a severe case involves fluids and correction of body acid balance, and that requires a vet.

Animals that survive an acute episode often carry lasting damage: rumen wall scarring, liver abscesses, and laminitis that shows as sore feet, an odd gait and long, ridged hooves weeks later. Their gain is permanently reduced.

Subacute acidosis, the quiet thief

This is the common form and the expensive one. Rumen pH sits too low for hours a day without ever producing a spectacular case. What you see across the pen:

  • Intake that swings sharply day to day rather than climbing steadily.
  • Loose, bubbly, or foamy manure with a shiny look, sometimes with mucus casts.
  • Undigested grain in the manure.
  • Reduced rumination: fewer resting animals chewing the cud.
  • Cattle sorting the bunk, eating the fines and leaving the long roughage.
  • Sore footed cattle and unexplained poor performers.
  • Feed conversion drifting worse than the expected roughly 6 to 10 kg DM per kg gain.

Prevention, in order of importance

  1. Adapt properly. The 14 to 21 day step-up in Lesson 3 is the main defence.
  2. Keep effective fibre in the ration. Roughage never to zero, and long enough to be chewed.
  3. Feed the same amount at the same time every day. Consistency beats formulation.
  4. Never double feed after a missed feeding.
  5. Mix evenly and do not overprocess grain. Crack or coarsely roll, do not grind to flour.
  6. Stop cattle sorting. Add a little moisture or molasses to bind the mix, and chop roughage to a consistent length.
  7. Watch water. Cattle that lose water for a day and then drink and eat heavily are at high risk.
  8. Consider a buffer. Feed grade sodium bicarbonate is commonly used in high grain rations. Discuss inclusion with your feed supplier or vet.
  9. Secure the feed store. A locked door prevents more acidosis than any additive.

The manure decision rule

Score manure across the pen each morning. Firm enough to hold a low stacked pile is your target. If several animals show watery, bubbly manure with grain in it, hold the step. If it persists into a second day, back up one step and check your mixing and your roughage particle length before you try to advance again.

Lesson 5.514 min

Bloat and the Other Transition Disorders

In this lesson
  • Tell frothy bloat from free gas bloat
  • Act in the right order on a bloating animal
  • Recognise laminitis, liver abscess and urinary stones as consequences

Bloat is gas trapped in the rumen that the animal cannot belch out. Fermentation produces large volumes of gas continuously, and a healthy animal belches it away without you noticing. When belching is blocked, pressure builds, the left flank swells, breathing is compressed, and an animal can die within an hour.

Two kinds of bloat

  • Frothy bloat. The gas is trapped in a stable foam mixed through the rumen contents, so there is no free gas pocket to release. Caused by lush legume pasture and also by finely ground, high grain feedlot rations. This is the type you will most often meet in a fattening pen.
  • Free gas bloat. A single gas pocket that cannot escape because the oesophagus is blocked, the animal is lying badly, or the nerve control is affected. Caused by a lodged potato or root, a swollen gullet, or illness.

Recognising it

The left flank behind the last rib swells and becomes drum tight; tapping it sounds hollow. The animal is restless, stops eating, kicks at its belly, salivates, breathes with an open mouth and extended neck, and may stagger. As pressure rises it goes down and can die quickly.

What to do, in order

  1. Get the animal up and moving slowly. Walking helps some cases belch.
  2. Remove the pen from concentrate immediately, because bloat rarely arrives alone.
  3. Call the vet now, not after trying everything. A severe bloat is a minutes to hours problem.
  4. Passing a stomach tube relieves free gas bloat and is the correct first mechanical step. It must be done by someone trained; a tube in the windpipe kills the animal.
  5. An anti foaming agent given by mouth or through the tube is the treatment for frothy bloat, because a tube alone will not release foam. Use a registered product at the label dose, on veterinary advice.
  6. Emergency trocar or surgical puncture of the rumen is a last resort for an animal that is going down and about to die. It causes peritonitis risk and needs follow up treatment. This is a veterinary procedure. Never make it your routine response.

Preventing bloat in a fattening pen

  • Keep effective fibre in the ration and do not grind grain to flour.
  • Feed consistently so cattle do not gorge.
  • Introduce legume rich forage gradually and never turn hungry cattle onto lush legume pasture.
  • Stop sorting at the bunk, since animals eating only fines are the ones that bloat.
  • Where bloat is recurring, discuss an anti foaming feed additive with your vet or supplier.

The slower consequences of a bad transition

  • Laminitis. Inflammation in the hoof following acidosis. Cattle stand oddly, shift weight, walk short and stiff, and lie down more. Over weeks the hooves grow long and ridged. Lame cattle do not walk to the bunk, so they do not gain. There is no cheap cure; prevention is the ration.
  • Liver abscess. Damage to the rumen wall lets bacteria into the blood and they lodge in the liver. You never see it until the abattoir condemns the liver and, in bad cases, docks the carcase. It is a direct cash loss from poor adaptation.
  • Urinary stones. As covered in Module 4, a low calcium high phosphorus grain ration is a recognised risk in male cattle. A steer straining, dribbling urine, kicking at his belly or standing with an arched back needs a vet the same day. A fully blocked animal can rupture its bladder and die.
  • Respiratory disease. Not a transition disorder in itself, but the arrival period and the step-up overlap, so a coughing pen and a struggling step-up often appear together. Do not push the concentrate up in a pen that is fighting a respiratory outbreak.

The pattern behind all of these is the same. They are not bad luck. They are the bill for feeding too much concentrate too fast, mixing unevenly, or letting cattle eat irregularly.

Lesson 5.615 min

Bunk Management: Reading Leftover Feed

In this lesson
  • Score bunks consistently using a fixed scale
  • Decide the next feed delivery from the score
  • Detect a problem pen before the animals look sick

Bunk reading is the daily skill that separates a fattener who makes money from one who does not. The bunk tells you what the cattle actually ate, which is never exactly what the ration sheet says they should have eaten. Read it at the same time every morning, before the first feed, by the same person where possible.

A simple bunk scoring scale

ScoreWhat you seeMeaning
0Bunk completely empty, licked clean, and has been empty for some hoursCattle ran out too early; risk of gorging at next feed
0.5Empty but only recently, scattered crumbs remainClose to right, the target for many yards
1A thin scattering of feed across the bunk floorSlight surplus; safe and often the target during step-up
2A clear layer of feed, roughly a quarter of a feeding remainingCattle are backing off; investigate before adding more
3Half or more of a feeding remainingProblem. Do not increase. Look for a cause today

The decision rules

  • Score 0 two days running: increase the next delivery by a small increment, in the order of a few percent of the daily amount, not a large jump.
  • Score 0.5 to 1: hold the amount steady. This is where you want to live.
  • Score 2: hold the amount, and check water, weather, health and mix quality.
  • Score 3: reduce the next delivery, remove and discard stale feed, and inspect the pen animal by animal. Never feed on top of a large residue.
  • During the step-up deliberately run slightly on the generous side of empty, at a score of about 1, so that no animal is ever ravenous when the new higher concentrate diet arrives.

What the leftovers themselves tell you

Do not only measure the amount. Look at what is left.

  • Long roughage left, fines gone. Cattle are sorting. That means the animals that ate first got a much hotter ration than you intended, and the shy animals got sticks. Fix by chopping roughage shorter and adding moisture or molasses to bind the mix.
  • Fines and cake left, roughage gone. Palatability problem in the concentrate. Check for mould, rancid bran, or a new load that smells different.
  • Feed caked, wet or mouldy in the bunk. Rain, poor drainage or stale carryover. Clean bunks out properly, not just to one side.
  • Even leftovers across the bunk length but the same amount each day. You are simply feeding slightly too much. Trim it.

Other things that change intake tomorrow

Note them in the same book: a hot day, a cold wet night, a storm, a change in feeding time, a new load of any ingredient, a vaccination or handling day, or a broken water valve. Almost every unexplained intake drop has one of these behind it.

The cost of getting the transition wrong

Work the arithmetic on your own cattle rather than taking anyone's word for it. Take a pen of steers on a 100 day finish targeting the common range of 1.0 to 1.5 kg per day. If a botched step-up costs you two weeks of near zero gain, and then cattle return to normal gain, you have lost roughly the gain of those two weeks: at 1.2 kg per day that is around 17 kg of liveweight per head, plus two weeks of feed that produced nothing, plus the extra days of overheads if you hold the cattle longer to catch up. Add one dead animal in a pen of twenty and you have lost that animal's entire purchase price and every shilling of feed spent on it, spread across the survivors' margin.

Put your own placeholder numbers into that structure: lost kilograms times your expected sale price per kg, plus the wasted feed days times your daily feed cost, plus mortality. Never use a price from this course as if it were your local price. Phone three buyers and three feed suppliers and use their figures. What the arithmetic always shows, whatever the prices, is that the two or three weeks of careful adaptation is the cheapest part of the whole enterprise.

Knowledge check

Five questions. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. A truck of bought steers arrives at midday after a long journey. What do you put in front of them first?

Thirsty, empty cattle need water and long hay. Concentrate on arrival is a classic cause of acidosis and bloat. Hold on hay for roughly the first day, sometimes two to three.

2. The feeder was away and the pen missed its evening feed. What do you feed the next morning?

Never make up a missed feeding. Doubling after a fast is the most reliable way to cause fatal acidosis in a pen.

3. Which set of signs best fits subacute acidosis across a pen?

Subacute acidosis is the quiet form: unstable intake, loose bubbly manure, undigested grain, reduced rumination and drifting feed conversion, without dramatic individual cases.

4. A stomach tube passed into a bloating steer releases very little gas. What does that most likely mean?

In frothy bloat the gas is trapped in a stable foam, so there is no free gas pocket for a tube to release. An anti foaming agent is the treatment, on veterinary advice.

5. You find bunk score 3 in a pen, with half a feeding left over. What is the correct action?

A large residue means cattle have backed off. Never feed on top of it or advance a step. Clean the bunk, cut the delivery, and find the cause the same day.
