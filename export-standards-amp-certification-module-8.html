Lessons

1. How to find an accredited lab in your country

You need a lab that is accredited to ISO/IEC 17025 for the specific test you require. Accreditation means the lab has been checked by an independent body and found competent. In Africa, national accreditation bodies (NABs) are members of the African Accreditation Cooperation (AFRAC) and often sign mutual recognition arrangements with the International Laboratory Accreditation Cooperation (ILAC). Start by visiting the AFRAC website or the ILAC website to find your country's NAB. For example, in Kenya it's KENAS, in Nigeria it's NiNAS, in South Africa it's SANAS, in Ghana it's GAC, in Egypt it's EGAC. These NABs publish lists of accredited labs and their scopes. You must check that the lab's accreditation scope includes the exact test method you need, e.g., 'determination of aflatoxin in groundnuts' or 'heavy metals in fish'. If the lab is not accredited for that test, your certificate may be rejected by buyers. Also, confirm the lab is still accredited accreditation can be suspended. Ask for a copy of their accreditation certificate and check the expiry date. If you can't find a local accredited lab, look for one in a neighbouring country or use an international lab with a local branch. But be aware that samples may need to be shipped, which adds cost and time. For routine tests, many exporters use local private labs that are not accredited but follow ISO methods; however, for official export certificates, accredited labs are often required. Always verify with your buyer or the destination country's requirements. Finally, ask the lab about sample submission procedures, turnaround time, and cost. Typical costs range from $50 for a simple test to $500 for a full pesticide screen. Turnaround can be 3-10 working days. Plan ahead.

2. SGS, Bureau Veritas, Intertek: what they do and cost

SGS, Bureau Veritas (BV), and Intertek are the three largest inspection, verification, testing, and certification companies in the world, often called the 'Big Three'. They have offices in most African countries and provide services that governments and buyers trust. Their main services include: pre-shipment inspection (PSI) to check quantity and quality before export; testing of products in their labs; certification that your product meets a specific standard (e.g., ISO, CE, organic); and social audits (e.g., SMETA). Many African governments mandate PSI for imports, but for exports, buyers often require a certificate of inspection from one of these companies. For example, a cocoa buyer in Europe may ask for a SGS certificate of quality and weight. Costs vary widely. A basic pre-shipment inspection for a container of goods might cost $300$800. A laboratory test for aflatoxin could be $100$200. A full certification audit for ISO 22000 could be $2,000$5,000 depending on company size. These companies also offer training. To get a quote, contact their local office with details: product, destination, required standard, and volume. Be clear about what you need a one-off inspection or ongoing certification. They will send a proposal. Note that the Big Three are not always the cheapest, but their certificates are widely accepted. For small exporters, sometimes a national standards body or a local accredited lab is more affordable. However, if your buyer insists on SGS, BV, or Intertek, you have little choice. Always ask for a breakdown of costs: inspection fee, travel, lab tests, certificate issuance. Also, check if they have a local presence to avoid travel surcharges. In some countries, they have mobile labs. Plan at least 2-4 weeks for scheduling.

3. National standards bodies: services and fees

Every African country has a national standards body (NSB). Examples: KEBS in Kenya, SON in Nigeria, SABS in South Africa, GS in Ghana, EOS in Egypt, and so on. NSBs are government or semi-government agencies responsible for developing standards, testing products, certifying products and systems, and providing calibration services. For exporters, NSBs offer several key services: product testing (often at lower cost than private labs), certification of products to national or international standards (e.g., Kenya's KEBS diamond mark), and export certification. Many NSBs also offer training on standards and quality management. Fees for testing vary. For example, a basic microbiological test at KEBS might cost KES 5,00010,000 (about $40$80). A chemical test for aflatoxin might be KES 8,00015,000. Certification fees depend on the product and scheme; a product certification could cost $500$2,000 for initial assessment plus annual fees. NSBs often have laboratories accredited to ISO 17025 for specific tests. However, not all NSBs are equally equipped; some may lack certain tests and send samples abroad. It's important to check the scope of accreditation. Also, NSBs may be slower than private labs due to bureaucracy. But their certificates are often required for local market access and sometimes for export. For export, some buyers accept NSB test reports, but others require international accreditation. If your NSB is not accredited for a test, you may need to use a private lab. NSBs also provide calibration services for weighing scales, thermometers, etc., which are needed for quality control. Fees for calibration are usually modest. To use NSB services, visit their office, submit samples with a request form, and pay the fee. Turnaround can be 1-3 weeks. Always get a proforma invoice first. Some NSBs have regional offices, which can save travel.

4. Mutual recognition agreements and their limits

A mutual recognition agreement (MRA) is a formal arrangement between countries or accreditation bodies to accept each other's conformity assessment results tests, inspections, certifications. In Africa, the African Accreditation Cooperation (AFRAC) promotes MRAs among national accreditation bodies. Also, many African NABs are signatories to the ILAC Mutual Recognition Arrangement (MRA) and the International Accreditation Forum (IAF) MLA. This means a test report from an accredited lab in Kenya (KENAS) is accepted in Europe or Asia, provided the lab's accreditation scope covers the test. MRAs reduce the need for duplicate testing, saving time and money. However, MRAs have limits. First, they only cover accredited bodies. If a lab is not accredited, its results may not be accepted. Second, the MRA only covers the specific scope of accreditation; if the test method is not listed, the report may be rejected. Third, MRAs do not override national regulations. A country may still require its own testing for phytosanitary or food safety reasons, even if an accredited lab elsewhere has tested. For example, the EU requires that certain products be tested for pesticide residues by labs recognized by the EU, not just any accredited lab. Fourth, political and administrative barriers can exist; some customs officials may not understand MRAs and may insist on local testing. Fifth, MRAs are between accreditation bodies, not between countries directly, so they depend on the accreditation system functioning properly. To benefit, always ensure your test report comes from a lab accredited to ISO 17025 for the specific test, and that the accreditation body is a signatory to the relevant MRA. You can check the ILAC or AFRAC websites for a list of signatories. Keep copies of the lab's accreditation certificate and scope. If a buyer questions your report, provide this evidence. Despite MRAs, some buyers still prefer specific labs (like SGS) for commercial reasons. So MRAs help but are not a magic bullet.

5. Sampling and testing: what to expect

Sampling is the process of taking a small portion of your product that represents the whole lot. If sampling is done wrong, even the best lab will give a wrong result. For export, sampling must follow a standard method, often specified by the buyer or the destination country. Common sampling standards include ISO 5555 for animal and vegetable fats, ISO 707 for milk and milk products, and ISO 24333 for cereals. For aflatoxin in groundnuts, the standard is often ISO 16050 or the USDA method. The key is to take a representative sample: from different parts of the lot, using a clean tool, and mixing them. For a container of 20 tons of maize, you might take 10-20 incremental samples, combine them, and then quarter down to a final sample of 1-2 kg. The sample must be sealed, labelled with date, time, location, lot number, and signed by all parties. A chain of custody form must accompany the sample to the lab. Testing then follows the method. You should ask the lab which method they use and whether it is accredited. For example, aflatoxin testing might use HPLC (high-performance liquid chromatography) or ELISA. HPLC is more accurate but costs more. Pesticide residue testing often uses GC-MS or LC-MS/MS. Expect turnaround times: rapid tests like ELISA can be done in a day; confirmatory tests take 3-7 days. Costs: aflatoxin test $50-$150, pesticide screen $200-$500, heavy metals $100-$300. When you get the test report, check that it includes: lab name, accreditation number, sample details, method, result, limit of detection, and interpretation. If the result is 'not detected', it means below the detection limit. If it exceeds the maximum residue limit (MRL), your product may be rejected. Always keep a retain sample for your own records. If you disagree with the result, you can ask for a retest on the retain sample, but this may cost extra.

6. Appealing a failed test result

A failed test result can be devastating, but you have options. First, don't panic. Review the test report carefully. Check for obvious errors: wrong sample ID, wrong test method, calculation mistakes, or expired reagents. Sometimes labs make mistakes. If you suspect an error, contact the lab immediately and ask for clarification. They may re-check the raw data or re-run the test at no cost if it's their error. If you believe the result is correct but due to a sampling issue, you can request a retest on the retained sample. Most labs keep the sample for a certain period (e.g., 30 days). You will likely have to pay for the retest. If the retest confirms the failure, you need to investigate the cause: was it a batch problem, storage issue, or contamination? You can then decide to rework, reject, or sell to a less demanding market. If you disagree with the lab's result and believe the lab is at fault, you can file a formal appeal. The first step is to write to the lab manager, stating your case and providing evidence. If that fails, you can complain to the lab's accreditation body. For example, if the lab is accredited by SANAS, you can lodge a complaint with SANAS. The accreditation body will investigate whether the lab followed its procedures. This can take weeks. Another option is to use a different accredited lab for a second opinion. This is often the fastest route. Choose a lab that is accredited for the same test and send a portion of the retained sample. If the second result differs, you have grounds to challenge the first. However, be aware that different labs may use different methods, so results may not be directly comparable. In some cases, you can ask for a referee lab a third independent lab agreed by both parties. This is common in trade disputes. Finally, if the failure is due to a regulatory limit, you can appeal to the relevant authority (e.g., the national standards body or the destination country's food safety authority) with your evidence. Always keep all documents. Prevention is better: use accredited labs, follow sampling protocols, and test early.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Testing, Inspection, and Certification in African Agribusiness

For any African exporter, the difference between a signed purchase order and a rejected container often comes down to one thing: credible proof that your product meets the buyer's standard. That proof is produced by laboratories, inspection companies, and certification bodies. This lesson gives you a working map of who does what, what it costs, how mutual recognition actually works on the ground, and how to challenge a failed result without losing the shipment or the buyer.

Treat this as your desk reference. Keep it open when you are preparing a first export, switching buyers, or troubleshooting a rejection.

1. How to Find an Accredited Lab in Your Country

An "accredited" lab is not the same as a "registered" or "licensed" one. Accreditation means an independent body has assessed the lab against ISO/IEC 17025. In Africa, the reference regional body is SADCAS (Southern African Development Community Accreditation Service), which covers most SADC countries, while individual national accreditation bodies operate in South Africa (SANAS), Kenya (KENAS), Nigeria (NiNAS), Egypt (EGAC), Tunisia (TUNAC), and others.

Steps for a business:

  • Check the scope of accreditation, not just the certificate. A lab accredited for water testing may not be accredited for pesticide residues.
  • Confirm the specific test method (e.g., LC-MS/MS for MRLs, HPLC for aflatoxin).
  • Ask whether the lab is recognised by your target buyer or by the importing country's authority (e.g., EU, UK, US FDA).
  • Verify turnaround time and sample logistics courier delays kill shelf life.

2. SGS, Bureau Veritas, Intertek: What They Do and Cost

These three are the largest global inspection, verification, testing, and certification (TIC) companies. In Africa they operate through country offices and subcontract local labs. They are typically used when a buyer contractually requires a specific Certificate of Conformity (CoC), or when a destination country operates a pre-shipment verification of conformity (PVoC) programme.

4. Mutual Recognition Agreements and Their Limits

Mutual Recognition Agreements (MRAs) and Multilateral Recognition Arrangements (MLAs) allow test results and certificates issued in one country to be accepted in another. The ILAC MRA and the IAF MLA are the two global frameworks. In Africa, SADCAS, SANAS, KENAS, and others are signatories.

Limits you must plan around:

  • MRAs cover accreditation, not the commercial contract. A buyer can still demand a specific brand (SGS, BV, Intertek).
  • Scope matters a lab accredited for one method is not automatically recognised for another.
  • Destination authorities (e.g., EU DG SANTE, US FDA) may impose additional requirements such as residue monitoring plans.
  • Political and regulatory changes can suspend recognition overnight.

5. Sampling and Testing: What to Expect

Sampling is the weakest link in most African export rejections. If the sample does not represent the lot, the result is legally and commercially meaningless. Insist on:

  • ISO 2859 or Codex Alimentarius sampling plans for the specific commodity.
  • Sealed, tamper-evident samples with chain-of-custody documentation.
  • Retained samples held for the shelf-life period plus 30 days.
  • Testing against the correct standard EU MRLs, Codex, or buyer specification.

Common turnaround: 310 working days for routine parameters; 1021 days for pesticide residue panels. Budget for courier cost and possible re-test.

6. Appealing a Failed Test Result

A failed result is not the end. Most accredited labs have a formal appeals and complaints procedure under ISO/IEC 17025. Your appeal path:

  1. Request the full test report, raw data, and method used.
  2. Check chain of custody and sample integrity.
  3. Request re-test of the retained sample at the same lab.
  4. If unresolved, use a second accredited lab and compare.
  5. Escalate to the accreditation body (e.g., SADCAS, SANAS) if procedure was breached.
  6. Notify your buyer early silence is worse than a delay.
SAFETY WARNING:

Never accept a verbal result or an unsigned certificate. Falsified or "issued to order" certificates have caused container seizures, buyer blacklisting, and in some cases criminal prosecution in destination markets. Always verify certificate numbers directly with the issuing body's online portal before shipping. If a broker offers a "fast certificate" without sampling, walk away.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Confirm the buyer's required standard and the destination country's legal requirements before production.
  2. Verify lab accreditation scope (ISO/IEC 17025) and method for your specific commodity.
  3. Agree sampling plan (ISO 2859 or Codex) and chain-of-custody procedure in writing.
  4. Budget 0.450.55% of FOB value for PVoC/CoC and 60250 USD per test parameter.
  5. Retain sealed samples for shelf life + 30 days.
  6. Check MRA/MLA coverage but do not assume buyer acceptance.
  7. Keep a written appeals procedure and a second accredited lab on standby.
  8. Verify every certificate number on the issuing body's portal before shipment.
