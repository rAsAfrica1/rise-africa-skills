1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Broiler farming is one of the fastest ways to make money in African agriculture. A broiler chicken can be ready for market in just 42 days if you follow a strict cycle. This 42-day cycle is the backbone of a daily sales business. Instead of selling chickens once in a while, you produce batches every week so you always have birds to sell. Customers want fresh chicken every day, and if you can supply that, you build a loyal market. The 42-day cycle works because broilers grow quickly when given the right feed, water, and shelter. From day-old chicks to 2.5 kg live weight, it takes about six weeks. If you start a new batch every week, you will have a continuous harvest. For example, if you keep 100 birds per batch and start a batch every Monday, then after 42 days you will be selling 100 birds every week. That means daily sales of about 14 birds. At a profit of 500 naira per bird, that's 7,000 naira daily. This system turns a small space into a steady income. But it only works if you stick to the timeline. Delays in feeding, dirty water, or disease can push the cycle to 50 or 60 days, eating your profit. In Africa, many farmers fail because they treat broilers like local chickensâfree-ranging and irregular. The 42-day cycle demands discipline: exact feed amounts, clean water, vaccines on time, and proper ventilation. It also matters because feed is your biggest cost. A broiler eats about 4 kg of feed to reach 2.5 kg. If you waste feed or let it spoil, you lose money. The cycle helps you plan your cash flow. You know when to buy chicks, when to buy feed, and when to expect income. This module will teach you the exact steps to run a 42-day cycle, from preparing the brooder to selling the last bird. You will learn tools, methods, mistakes to avoid, safety, and a practical exercise. By the end, you will be ready to start your own batch and sell daily.

2. Tools and Materials

To run a successful 42-day broiler cycle, you need the right tools and materials. Without them, you will struggle with disease, waste, and slow growth. Here is a complete list for a batch of 100 birds. First, housing: a deep litter pen of 10 square meters (100 birds at 10 birds per square meter). The floor should be concrete or packed earth, covered with 5-10 cm of wood shavings or rice husks. Never use sawdust from treated wood. You need a brooder for the first 2-3 weeks: a charcoal stove, kerosene lamp, or electric bulb (100 watts for 100 chicks). A thermometer to monitor temperature (start at 35Â°C and reduce by 3Â°C each week). Feeders: 10 tube feeders (capacity 5 kg each) and 10 drinkers (capacity 3 liters each). For the first week, use chick feeders and drinkers (small trays). You will need feed: 1 bag of chick starter (25 kg), 3 bags of grower (75 kg), and 2 bags of finisher (50 kg) for 100 birds. That's about 150 kg total. Also buy vaccines: Newcastle disease (ND) and Infectious Bursal Disease (IBD). You need a vaccine carrier with ice packs. For biosecurity: a footbath with disinfectant, a sprayer for disinfecting, and overalls or boots. For record-keeping: a notebook and pen to track feed, water, mortality, and weight. For selling: a weighing scale (5 kg capacity), crates for transport, and a sharp knife for processing if you sell dressed chicken. Other useful items: a water hose, a broom, a shovel, and a bucket. In Africa, you can find most of these at local agro-vet shops or market. For example, in Nigeria, a bag of chick starter costs about 12,000 naira, grower 10,000 naira, finisher 9,000 naira. A thermometer costs 2,000 naira. You can improvise drinkers using cut plastic jerrycans, but clean them daily. Do not use rusty metal containers. Always have a backup source of heat in case of power cuts. With these tools, you are ready to start.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps exactly to complete the 42-day cycle. Step 1: Prepare the brooder 24 hours before chicks arrive. Clean and disinfect the pen. Spread litter 5 cm deep. Set up the brooder and heat it to 35Â°C. Put feed and water in chick trays. Step 2: Receive day-old chicks. Count them and place them gently near the heat source. Dip their beaks in water to teach them to drink. Keep the brooder temperature at 35Â°C for the first week. Step 3: Week 1 (Days 1-7). Feed chick starter mash free-choice. Give clean water with glucose or vitamins for the first 3 days. Vaccinate against Newcastle disease (ND) on day 7 via eye drops. Reduce temperature to 32Â°C. Step 4: Week 2 (Days 8-14). Continue starter feed. Vaccinate against IBD on day 14 in drinking water. Reduce temperature to 29Â°C. Expand the brooder space as chicks grow. Step 5: Week 3 (Days 15-21). Switch to grower feed. Reduce temperature to 26Â°C. If using charcoal, you can stop heating by day 21 if the weather is warm. Give ND booster on day 21. Step 6: Week 4 (Days 22-28). Continue grower feed. Increase feeder and drinker space. Weigh a few birds weekly to check growth. A broiler should weigh about 1 kg at day 28. Step 7: Week 5 (Days 29-35). Switch to finisher feed. Ensure plenty of ventilation. At day 35, average weight should be 1.8 kg. Step 8: Week 6 (Days 36-42). Continue finisher feed. Withdraw feed 8 hours before selling (to empty the crop). On day 42, weigh and sell. Average weight should be 2.5 kg. Throughout the cycle, give feed twice daily (morning and evening) and water always. Clean drinkers daily. Remove wet litter. Record daily mortality. If a bird dies, remove it immediately. By following these steps, you will achieve a 42-day cycle. Remember, any delay in feeding or vaccination will extend the cycle and reduce profit.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Many farmers fail to complete the 42-day cycle because of avoidable mistakes. Here are the most common ones and how to fix them. Mistake 1: Overcrowding. Putting more than 10 birds per square meter causes stress, poor growth, and disease. Fix: Stick to 10 birds per square meter. For 100 birds, use 10 square meters. Mistake 2: Poor brooding temperature. Chicks huddle if too cold or pant if too hot. Fix: Use a thermometer. Start at 35Â°C and reduce by 3Â°C weekly. Watch chick behavior: if they spread out evenly, temperature is right. Mistake 3: Dirty water. Water with droppings or feed will cause disease. Fix: Clean drinkers twice daily and refill with fresh water. Mistake 4: Skipping vaccines. Newcastle disease can kill 90% of your flock. Fix: Vaccinate on day 7, 14, and 21. Use a vaccine carrier with ice. Mistake 5: Using the wrong feed. Starter, grower, and finisher have different protein levels. Fix: Follow the feed schedule. Do not use layer feed. Mistake 6: Wet litter. Wet litter causes ammonia gas and respiratory disease. Fix: Stir litter daily and add fresh shavings. Fix leaks immediately. Mistake 7: Not recording. Without records, you cannot know your profit or losses. Fix: Keep a notebook. Write down feed used, deaths, and weights. Mistake 8: Selling too early or too late. Selling before 42 days gives low weight; after 42 days, feed conversion worsens. Fix: Weigh birds weekly. Sell at 2.5 kg or day 42. Mistake 9: No biosecurity. Visitors can bring disease. Fix: Use a footbath. Limit visitors. Wear boots. Mistake 10: Power failure for brooder. Chicks can chill and die. Fix: Have a backup charcoal stove or kerosene lamp. By avoiding these mistakes, you will achieve a successful 42-day cycle and steady profit.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety is not just about the birdsâit is also about you, your workers, and your customers. Follow these best practices to keep everyone safe and your business running. First, personal safety. When handling feed or cleaning pens, wear a nose mask and gloves. Dust from feed and litter can cause respiratory problems. Always wash your hands with soap after working with birds. When using disinfectants, read the label and dilute correctly. Never mix chemicals. Second, biosecurity. Keep a footbath with disinfectant at the entrance of your pen. Change it daily. Do not allow visitors into the pen unless necessary. If they must enter, give them boots and overalls. Keep wild birds outâthey carry diseases. Cover windows with wire mesh. Third, bird safety. Handle chicks gently. Never lift a chicken by its wings or neck. Support its body. When vaccinating, use clean equipment. Do not vaccinate sick birds. If you see a sick bird, isolate it immediately. Fourth, feed and water safety. Store feed in a dry, raised place. Do not use moldy feedâit contains aflatoxins that can kill birds and harm people who eat the meat. Clean drinkers daily with a brush. Do not let algae grow. Fifth, environment. Dispose of dead birds properly. Burn or bury them deep, away from the pen. Do not throw them in the bush. Compost litter away from water sources. Sixth, selling safety. If you sell live birds, handle them humanely. If you slaughter, use a clean knife and clean water. Wash your hands and the carcass. Sell fresh meat within 2 hours or refrigerate. Seventh, record keeping for safety. Note any disease outbreaks and treatments. This helps you avoid repeating mistakes. Finally, best practice: start with a small batch (50-100 birds) to learn. Keep a daily routine. Visit your birds at least three times a day. Observe their behavior. Healthy birds are active and eat well. By following these safety measures, you protect your investment and your health.

6. Practical Exercise

This practical exercise will help you apply what you have learned. You will plan and simulate a 42-day broiler cycle for a batch of 100 birds. Follow these steps. Step 1: Calculate your costs. Write down the cost of 100 day-old chicks (e.g., 800 naira each = 80,000 naira). Feed: 1 bag starter (12,000), 3 bags grower (30,000), 2 bags finisher (18,000). Vaccines and vitamins: 5,000. Litter and disinfectant: 3,000. Total cost: 148,000 naira. Step 2: Plan your timeline. On a calendar, mark day 1 as the day chicks arrive. Mark vaccination days: day 7 (ND), day 14 (IBD), day 21 (ND booster). Mark feed changes: day 1 starter, day 15 grower, day 29 finisher. Mark day 42 as selling day. Step 3: Create a daily routine. Write a checklist: morningâcheck temperature, give feed, clean drinkers, remove dead birds. Afternoonâstir litter, check water. Eveningâgive feed, record mortality and feed used. Step 4: Simulate growth. Assume average daily gain: week 1: 100g, week 2: 250g, week 3: 500g, week 4: 900g, week 5: 1400g, week 6: 2000g. At day 42, average weight 2.5 kg. If you sell at 2,500 naira per bird, revenue = 250,000 naira. Profit = 250,000 - 148,000 = 102,000 naira. Step 5: Identify risks. What if 10 birds die? You lose 25,000 naira. What if feed prices rise? Adjust your selling price. Step 6: Write a one-page plan. Include your batch size, start date, expected selling date, total cost, expected revenue, and profit. Step 7: Present your plan to a fellow learner or your trainer. Discuss what could go wrong and how you will fix it. This exercise gives you a real feel for the business. Do it on paper first, then try with a small batch of 50 birds. Keep records and compare with your plan. With practice, you will master the 42-day cycle and build a daily sales business.

Practical Reference: The 42-Day Broiler Cycle

This practical reference is the working document for small- and medium-scale broiler producers in Africa who sell birds daily rather than in single large batches. The 42-day cycle is the backbone of batch production: if you know exactly where each batch should be on any given day, you can plan feed purchases, labour, sales slots, and cash flow with far greater confidence. Treat this page as a bench reference â print it, pin it in the brooder house, and update the figures with your own farm records.

Broiler production in Africa is rarely a single-flock operation. Most successful producers run overlapping batches â for example, a new batch every 7 to 10 days â so that market-ready birds are available continuously. That is the essence of a daily broiler sales model: instead of one harvest every 6 weeks, you harvest a portion of birds every day or every few days. The 42-day cycle remains the planning unit, but it is repeated in staggered form.

â  SAFETY WARNING â Veterinary Drug Residues

Never sell or slaughter birds before the withdrawal period stated on any veterinary drug or feed additive label. Antibiotic residues in meat are a serious public health risk and can lead to rejection at markets, fines, and loss of customer trust. For most antibiotics used in broilers, the withdrawal period is 5â7 days; for coccidiostats, follow the label exactly. If in doubt, consult a licensed veterinarian or your local veterinary authority. In many African countries, selling meat with drug residues violates national food safety law.
