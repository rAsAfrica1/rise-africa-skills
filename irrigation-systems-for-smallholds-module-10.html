1. Routine Maintenance Checklist for Irrigation Systems

A good maintenance routine saves you money and water. Start with a daily walk-through. Every morning before you irrigate, check the pump, mainline, and valves. Look for leaks, listen for unusual noises, and check the pressure gauge. If pressure is below 1 bar (15 psi) for drip or 2 bar (30 psi) for sprinklers, stop and find the problem. Weekly, clean the filter. A dirty filter is the number one cause of poor irrigation. Also, check emitters and sprinklers for clogs. In sandy areas, you may need to clean filters every two days. Monthly, flush the mainline and sub-mainlines. Open the flush valves at the end of each line and let water run for 2-3 minutes. This removes sediment and roots. Inspect all pipes and fittings for cracks or loose connections. Tighten clamps and replace worn gaskets. Check the pump oil level if it has a dipstick, and lubricate moving parts. Every three months, test the water quality. High salt or iron can clog emitters. If you see white crust, your water is hard. You may need to acid-treat the system. Annually, before the rainy season, do a full system audit. Measure flow at the pump and at the farthest emitter. If flow drops by more than 10%, there is a blockage or leak. Replace any damaged parts. Keep a logbook. Write down dates of maintenance, problems found, and actions taken. This helps you spot patterns. For example, if filters clog every week, your water source has too much debris. You may need a settling pond or a sand filter. A simple checklist on a wooden board near the pump house works well. Include: pump oil, filter clean, leaks, pressure, emitters, flush lines, and valve operation. Tick each item. This takes 15 minutes but prevents big breakdowns. Remember, preventive maintenance is cheaper than repairs. A new pump can cost $200, but a bearing replacement is $20. So walk your system daily.

2. Cleaning Filters and Emitters

Filters and emitters are the heart of your drip system. If they clog, your crops suffer. Start with the filter. Most smallholder systems use a screen filter or a sand filter. For screen filters, turn off the pump. Open the drain valve at the bottom and let the water out. Unscrew the filter cap. Pull out the screen. Use a soft brush and clean water to remove debris. Never use a wire brush; it tears the mesh. If the screen is torn, replace it. A 120-mesh screen is common for drip. For sand filters, backwash them. Open the backwash valve and run water backwards for 3-5 minutes until the water runs clear. Do this weekly. If your water is very dirty, backwash every two days. Emitters clog from sediment, algae, and chemical deposits. First, check if the clog is at the emitter or the lateral line. If only one emitter is blocked, pull it off and clean it with a thin wire or a toothbrush. Soak it in vinegar overnight to dissolve calcium. If many emitters are clogged, flush the whole line. Open the end cap and let water run for 2 minutes. If that fails, use a chlorine treatment. Mix 1 liter of household bleach (5% chlorine) in 1000 liters of water. Run it through the system for 30 minutes, then flush with clean water. For iron clogs, use a commercial acid cleaner, but follow safety instructions. Always wear gloves and goggles. In Africa, many farmers use gravity-fed systems. Pressure is low, so emitters clog faster. Clean filters daily. Also, install a simple settling tank before the filter. Let water sit for a few hours so sand and silt settle. Then draw from the top. This reduces filter cleaning. For sprinklers, clean nozzles with a small brush. Check for worn nozzles; replace them if the spray pattern is uneven. Keep spare emitters and filters on hand. A pack of 50 emitters costs about $5. Train one person on your farm to do this weekly. They should record when they cleaned. If you see algae, cover your tank to block sunlight. This simple step reduces cleaning by half.

3. Repairing Leaks in Pipes and Hoses

Leaks waste water and money. A small leak can lose 20 liters per day. Find leaks by walking the line when the system is running. Look for wet spots, spraying water, or hissing sounds. In buried pipes, look for unusually green patches. Once you find a leak, turn off the pump and drain the line. For PVC pipes, cut out the damaged section with a hacksaw. Make clean, square cuts. Dry the ends. Apply PVC primer, then PVC cement. Slide a coupling over both ends and twist a quarter turn. Hold for 30 seconds. Let it cure for 1 hour before pressurizing. For poly pipe, use a barbed coupling and stainless steel clamps. Heat the pipe end with a lighter or hot water to make it flexible, then push the coupling in. Tighten clamps with a screwdriver. For hoses, if the leak is at a fitting, cut off the damaged end and reinsert the fitting. Use a hose clamp. If the hose is split, use a hose repair kit with a barbed connector. For a temporary fix, wrap self-fusing silicone tape around the leak. Stretch it tight and overlap. It bonds to itself in minutes. For large holes, use a rubber patch and a clamp. In Africa, many farmers use old inner tubes as patches. Cut a piece, place it over the leak, and secure with wire or a clamp. For buried pipes, dig around the leak carefully. Don't use a pickaxe; you may crack the pipe. Use a hand trowel. After repair, backfill with sand to protect the pipe. Then test. Run water and check for drips. If the leak persists, redo the repair. Always keep spare parts: couplings, clamps, tape, and a small saw. A repair kit costs about $15 and saves many trips to town. Also, check your pump connections. Vibration can loosen fittings. Tighten them monthly. If you have a metal pipe, rust can cause pinhole leaks. Replace rusted sections. For drip tape, leaks are common at the emitters. You can plug a leaking emitter with a goof plug. These cost $0.10 each. Inspect your system after every storm or heavy use. Quick action prevents small leaks from becoming big breaks.

4. Winterizing and Seasonal Startup

In many parts of Africa, you don't have freezing winters, but you do have dry seasons and rainy seasons. Winterizing means preparing your system for a period of non-use. If you have frost, water left in pipes can freeze and burst them. Even without frost, leaving water in pipes for months can cause algae and sediment buildup. So, at the end of the season, turn off the pump. Open all drain valves and end caps. Let the water drain out. If water remains in low spots, use a compressor to blow air through the lines. For small systems, you can disconnect the pipes and tip them. Remove filters and clean them. Store them dry. Remove the pump and store it indoors. If you can't remove it, drain it and add a little oil to the impeller. Cover the pump with a tarp. For drip tape, roll it up and store it in a cool, dark place. Rodents love to chew drip tape, so keep it off the ground. Before the next season, do a startup checklist. First, inspect all pipes for cracks or rodent damage. Replace damaged parts. Reconnect everything. Clean the filter and install a new screen if needed. Check the pump oil and fuel. Prime the pump. Open all valves slowly to avoid water hammer. Start the pump and check pressure. It should be within 10% of the original pressure. Flush the mainline and each lateral for 2 minutes. Check emitters for clogs. If you use a sand filter, backwash it before use. Test the water quality. If you see a lot of debris, consider a settling tank. In Africa, the rainy season can bring debris into open water sources. So, before startup, clean your intake. Use a screen or a gravel filter. Also, check your power source. If you use solar, clean the panels. If you use a generator, change the oil and spark plug. A good startup takes half a day but prevents failures. Keep a record of what you did. If you find a problem, fix it before planting. A system that starts clean will run all season with fewer headaches.

5. Diagnosing Pressure and Flow Problems

Pressure and flow problems are common. If your sprinklers don't reach, or drip emitters barely drip, you have a problem. Start with the pressure gauge. Install one at the pump and one at the end of the mainline. Normal pressure for drip is 1-1.5 bar (15-22 psi). For sprinklers, 2-3 bar (30-45 psi). If pressure is low at the pump, check the pump. Is it primed? Is the impeller worn? Is the suction line clogged? If pressure is high at the pump but low at the end, you have a blockage or a leak. Check filters first. A clogged filter drops pressure. Clean it. Then check for closed valves. A partially closed valve restricts flow. Open all valves fully. Next, check for leaks. A large leak will drop pressure. Walk the line. If pressure is low everywhere, your pump may be too small, or your pipe diameter is too small. For example, a 1-inch pipe can only carry about 30 liters per minute. If you need 50, you need a 1.5-inch pipe. If flow is low but pressure is normal, you have a clogged emitter or a broken lateral. Check each emitter. If only the first few emitters work, the line is clogged. Flush it. If pressure is high, you may have a closed valve or a clogged nozzle. High pressure can burst pipes. Install a pressure regulator. For gravity systems, pressure depends on tank height. Every 1 meter of height gives 0.1 bar. So a 10-meter tank gives 1 bar. If pressure is low, raise the tank. Also, check for air locks. Air in the line causes sputtering and low flow. Open the highest point and let air out. If you have a centrifugal pump, check the foot valve. A stuck foot valve causes no flow. Clean or replace it. Use a flow meter to measure actual flow. Compare to the design flow. If flow is 20% below design, find the cause. Keep a log of pressure and flow. This helps you spot trends. For example, if pressure drops slowly over a week, your filter is clogging. Clean it. If it drops suddenly, you have a leak or a broken pump.

6. Preventive Maintenance for Pumps and Valves

Pumps and valves are the most expensive parts of your system. Take care of them. For pumps, check the oil level daily if it has a dipstick. Use SAE 30 oil for most small pumps. Change the oil every 100 hours of operation. Clean the air filter every 50 hours. If you use a petrol pump, clean the spark plug every 100 hours. Replace it yearly. Check the impeller for wear. A worn impeller reduces flow. If you see cracks or heavy pitting, replace it. For solar pumps, clean the panels weekly. Dust reduces power by up to 30%. Check the wiring for corrosion. Tighten all connections. For valves, operate them monthly. A valve that sits closed for months can seize. Turn it open and closed a few times. Lubricate the stem with silicone grease. Check for leaks around the packing. If it leaks, tighten the packing nut a quarter turn. If that doesn't stop it, replace the packing. For gate valves, check the gate for debris. For ball valves, check the handle for smooth operation. In Africa, water can be gritty. Grit wears out valves quickly. Install a filter before the valves. Also, check the pressure relief valve. It should open at the set pressure. Test it by adjusting the pressure. If it doesn't open, replace it. For pump starters, check the contacts for burning. Clean them with fine sandpaper. If you have a foot valve, clean it monthly. A clogged foot valve causes the pump to lose prime. Keep spare parts: a spare impeller, a set of gaskets, a foot valve, and a pressure switch. A spare pump is ideal if you can afford it. But at least have a repair kit. Train one person to do daily checks. They should look for leaks, listen for noises, and check pressure. If they hear a grinding noise, stop the pump immediately. That means bearings are failing. Replace bearings before they damage the shaft. A bearing costs $10, but a new pump costs $200. So preventive maintenance pays. Keep a logbook. Write down oil changes, valve operations, and any repairs. This helps you predict when parts will fail. For example, if you change oil every 100 hours, you know when the next change is due. A simple calendar on the wall works. Mark the date of the last maintenance and the next due date. This simple habit doubles the life of your pump and valves.

A pump that runs 20% below rated flow can cut a farmer's irrigated area in half, turning a potentially profitable dry-season tomato crop into a loss. The reason is simple: a well-maintained system delivers uniform water, which means uniform germination, uniform fruit size, and higher marketable yield. A neglected system delivers uneven water, which means patchy crops, disease stress, and lower prices at market.

Think of maintenance as an insurance premium. Spending 2â3 hours per week and roughly 5% of your irrigation investment annually on preventive maintenance prevents the 30â40% yield losses that come from pump failure, clogged emitters, or burst pipes during peak season.

Never open a pump casing, filter housing, or pipe joint while the system is pressurised. Always switch off the pump at the mains and open a flush valve to release pressure before maintenance. When using chlorine or acid for line cleaning, wear gloves, eye protection, and work upwind. Never mix chlorine with acid â it produces toxic gas. In areas with unreliable earthing, use a residual current device (RCD) on all pump circuits. Store chemicals away from children and livestock, and never use irrigation chemical containers for drinking water.

Preventive Maintenance for Pumps and Valves

Pumps are the heart of your system and the most expensive component to replace. A 1.5 kW surface pump costs KES 25,000â40,000 in Kenya, and a submersible pump can exceed KES 80,000. Preventive maintenance costs less than 10% of replacement value per year.

  • Check oil levels in the pump crankcase monthly. Low oil causes overheating and bearing failure.
  • Inspect seals and gaskets every 3 months. A leaking seal lets air into the system and reduces pressure.
  • Clean the foot valve or suction strainer weekly during the irrigation season. Debris here is the number one cause of pump cavitation.
  • Test non-return valves quarterly by shutting off the pump and listening for water flowing back. A failed valve causes repeated priming and pump wear.
  • Lubricate gate and ball valves every 6 months. Stuck valves lead to over-pressurisation and pipe bursts.
  • Record pump running hours and schedule a professional service every 1,000 hours or annually, whichever comes first.

Winterizing and Seasonal Startup (Africa Context)

In most of sub-Saharan Africa, "winterizing" is less about freezing and more about dry-season shutdown and wet-season storage. When you stop irrigating at the end of the dry season:

  1. Flush all lines with clean water to remove sediment and salts.
  2. Drain the pump, mainline, and drip lines completely. Standing water breeds algae and mosquitoes.
  3. Remove and store filters, pressure gauges, and emitters in a dry, shaded place.
  4. Disconnect and store the pump indoors, covered, with a light oil coating on exposed metal.
  5. Roll up drip lines and store them off the ground, away from sunlight and rodents.

At seasonal startup, reverse the process: inspect for rodent damage, flush lines before installing emitters, check all seals, and run the pump for 10 minutes with the flush valves open before irrigating crops.
