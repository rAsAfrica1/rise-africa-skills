2. The Science of Corn, Soy and Milk

You are not just mixing three foods. You are assembling a complete protein, balancing fats and carbohydrates, and delivering the vitamins and minerals that a growing child's body cannot make on its own. This module explains why each ingredient is there.

Module 2 of 12 · 6 lessons · ~50 minutes reading · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 2.1 — Maize: energy, starch, and the missing amino acids lysine and tryptophan

Maize — called corn in many countries — is the single most important cereal crop in sub-Saharan Africa. About 40% of the calories eaten in the region come from maize. It is cheap, familiar, filling, and available almost everywhere. That is why we use it as the base of this porridge.

But maize alone cannot feed a child. It is missing the two amino acids that a growing body cannot make itself. Understanding this one fact explains why so many children in maize-eating communities are malnourished despite eating a full bowl of porridge three times a day.

What maize actually contains

Per 100 grams of dry white maize flour:

  • Energy: ~360 calories
  • Carbohydrates: ~75 g (mostly starch)
  • Protein: 8–9 g
  • Fat: 3–4 g
  • Fibre: 2–3 g
  • Iron: 1.5–2 mg (poorly absorbed — more on this later)
  • Zinc: 1.5–2 mg (also poorly absorbed)

Read that protein number again. 8 to 9 grams of protein per 100 grams of flour sounds decent. Bread is similar. So why is a maize-fed child malnourished?

The problem is not the amount of protein. It is the quality.

Protein is built from 20 different amino acids, like a wall built from 20 different kinds of bricks. Nine of those bricks the human body cannot make — they must come from food. These are called essential amino acids.

If even one essential brick is missing, the wall cannot be built. The other 19 bricks pile up unused. That is exactly what happens with maize.

Maize is missing two essential amino acids in adequate amounts:

  • Lysine — critical for growth, immune function, and collagen formation
  • Tryptophan — a precursor to serotonin, which regulates mood, sleep and appetite

Because these two are low, the other 18 amino acids in maize go to waste. The body cannot use them. It breaks them down, converts them to energy or fat, and passes them out. A child can eat 500 grams of plain maize porridge and still grow poorly because most of the protein cannot be used.

The lysine problem in one sentence: Maize has enough protein by weight, but it is incomplete protein — the body cannot build muscle, enzymes or immune cells because two essential building blocks are missing.

Why white vs yellow maize matters

In Africa, white maize is preferred for porridge and yellow maize is often seen as animal feed. That is a mistake for nutrition purposes.

  • White maize is higher in starch and lower in the yellow pigments called carotenoids.
  • Yellow maize contains beta-carotene — the plant form of vitamin A. Beta-carotene gives yellow maize its colour and provides the vitamin A that prevents night blindness, supports the immune system, and reduces child mortality from infections.

For a malnourished child, yellow maize is better. If only white maize is available, do not panic — you will add vitamin A in the micronutrient premix in a later module. But if you have a choice, choose yellow.

Whole grain vs refined maize meal

In southern and eastern Africa, maize meal comes in different grades:

TypeWhat it isNutrition
Whole grain (mugaiwa, chibage)The whole kernel ground, including germ and branHighest in fibre, fat, vitamins and minerals
Roller meal (mealie meal)Germ and bran mostly removedMiddle ground — most common for home use
Super refined / breakfast mealAlmost only the starchy endospermLowest in nutrients — mostly starch

The more refined the maize, the fewer nutrients remain. The germ and bran contain the oils, B vitamins and minerals. Removing them makes the flour whiter and longer-lasting, but removes exactly what a malnourished child needs.

Use whole-grain or roller meal, not super-refined breakfast meal. The slight difference in taste and texture is nothing compared to the nutritional loss. Whole grain also has more fat, which raises the energy density — a benefit we will discuss in lesson 2.5.
Practical exercise 2.1: Look at the maize meal in your kitchen. Which type is it? If it is super-refined, buy whole grain or roller meal on your next shopping trip. Taste them side by side — the difference matters.

Lesson 2.2 — Soybean: the highest-protein plant food available in most African markets

Soybean is the second ingredient and the most important single change that turns plain porridge into a lifesaving food. It is the highest-protein plant food available in most African markets, and its protein has a very different amino acid profile from maize.

What soybean actually contains

Per 100 grams of dry, whole soybean:

  • Energy: ~446 calories (much higher than maize)
  • Protein: 36–40 g (4 to 5 times more than maize)
  • Fat: 20 g (mostly healthy unsaturated fats)
  • Carbohydrates: 30 g
  • Fibre: 9 g
  • Iron: 15 mg (but poorly absorbed — more in a later module)
  • Calcium: 280 mg
  • Zinc: 5 mg

Compare 40 g of protein in soy to 8 g in maize. Soy is not just a protein source — it is the protein source available to families without access to animal foods. This is why WHO, UNICEF and WFP use soybean in almost every therapeutic food formula they distribute.

The amino acids that maize is missing, soy has

Remember lysine and tryptophan — the two essential amino acids maize is short on?

  • Soy is very high in lysine
  • Soy is adequate in tryptophan

This is the key insight of the whole course. Alone, maize has protein the body cannot use. Alone, soy is an excellent but expensive protein source. Together, they form a complete protein — the maize provides what soy lacks (methionine, for example) and the soy provides what maize lacks.

Full-fat vs defatted soy

You will find soybean sold in several forms:

FormWhat it isBest for
Whole dry soybeanThe intact bean, ~20% fatHome processing — you roast and grind yourself
Full-fat soy flourGround whole bean, ~20% fatDirect use, longer shelf life than whole bean
Defatted soy flourOil extracted, ~1% fat, higher protein %Industrial food production
Soy grits / chunksCoarse pieces of defatted soyMeat substitutes, not porridge
Textured soy protein (TSP)Extruded defatted soy, meat-like textureNot suitable for porridge

For fortified porridge, use full-fat soy flour or make your own from whole dry soybean. The fat in full-fat soy is energy a malnourished child needs. Defatted soy is higher in protein percentage but loses the calories — you would need to add oil separately.

Why you must process soy before eating it

This is the most important safety point about soybean, and it will come up again in module 6.

Raw soybean is toxic. It contains enzymes called trypsin inhibitors and a compound called lectin (phytohemagglutinin) that block protein digestion and can cause vomiting, diarrhoea, and gut damage within hours. A child who eats raw or undercooked soybean will get sicker, not stronger.

The fix is simple: heat. Cooking, roasting, or boiling destroys these anti-nutrients completely. But the heat must be sufficient — lightly warmed soy is not enough. Module 6 teaches the exact methods. For now, just remember: never give a child raw soybean in any form, ever.

Rule of thumb: If you can taste a bitter, beany, raw flavour in your soy flour, it has not been processed enough. Properly processed soy flour tastes slightly nutty and slightly sweet. When in doubt, roast or boil longer.

Cost and availability

Soybean prices vary widely by country and season:

  • In East Africa (Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania): $0.60–$1.00 per kg
  • In Southern Africa (Zambia, Zimbabwe, Malawi): $0.70–$1.10 per kg
  • In West Africa (Nigeria, Ghana): $0.80–$1.30 per kg
  • In retail shops (small quantity): up to $2.00 per kg

Buying whole dry soybean in bulk from farmers or agricultural suppliers is 40–60% cheaper than buying pre-ground soy flour from a shop. In a 25 kg bag, you can process enough flour for 100 kg of fortified porridge — which feeds one child for roughly four months.

Practical exercise 2.2: Call three suppliers today and ask: (1) do you sell whole dry soybean, (2) what is the price per kg, (3) is it current season or old stock? Old stock can be rancid and full of aflatoxin — always prefer fresh. Write the three prices down. That is your price baseline.

Lesson 2.3 — The complementary protein principle: maize + soy = complete protein

This is the theoretical heart of the course. If you understand this one idea, you understand why the porridge works when plain maize porridge fails.

The brick wall analogy, extended

Think of protein as a wall made of 20 different coloured bricks. The body can build the wall only if every colour is available in the right proportion. If it runs out of even one colour, construction stops — and the other bricks pile up unused.

Two walls, two different missing bricks:

FoodMissing or low inStrong in
MaizeLysine, tryptophanMethionine, cysteine
SoybeanMethionine (slightly low)Lysine, tryptophan

Bring them together and the gaps close. Maize provides what soy lacks. Soy provides what maize lacks. The result is a protein that the body can use as fully as it uses egg or milk protein.

Nutritionists call this protein complementation — a concept discovered in the early 20th century and used ever since in every humanitarian food formula. Beans and rice. Wheat and chickpeas. Maize and soy. Each pair closes the other's gap.

How complete is "complete"?

Nutritionists score proteins using a measure called PDCAAS (Protein Digestibility-Corrected Amino Acid Score). A score of 1.0 is a perfect, fully usable protein — like egg white or casein.

FoodPDCAAS score
Egg white1.00
Milk (casein)1.00
Soy protein isolate1.00
Whole soybean0.90
Maize alone0.42
Maize + soy (65:25 ratio)~0.85
Maize + soy + milk~0.92

Read those last two rows carefully. Maize alone gives the body less than half of the protein it should. Maize and soy together bring that up to 85%. Add milk and you approach 92% — nearly as good as eating eggs.

The 65:25 ratio is not arbitrary. It is the ratio that WHO and WFP have used for decades in fortified blended foods for emergency feeding. When you follow that ratio, you are producing a food that meets international standards for child nutrition. Deviation up or down means wasted ingredients or diluted nutrition.

Why you cannot just "add more soy"

A common reaction is: if soy is so good, why not use more of it? Why not 50% soy?

Three reasons:

  1. Taste. Above about 30% soy, the porridge becomes overwhelmingly beany. Children refuse it. A rejected food helps no one.
  2. Anti-nutrients. The more soy in the mix, the more pressure on your processing to eliminate trypsin inhibitors and phytates. At 25% soy, correct roasting is enough. At 50%, the tolerance for processing errors drops sharply.
  3. Economics. Soy costs more than maize. Every extra percentage of soy drives up the price of the final flour. For a family buying this themselves, cost matters.

The 65:25 ratio is the point where nutrition, taste and cost all balance. Stick to it.

Practical exercise 2.3: Weigh out a 1 kg batch using the ratio: 650 g maize flour, 250 g soy flour, 100 g milk powder. Look at the pile of each on a table. That visual will stay with you. When you buy ingredients at the market, you will know at a glance whether your quantities are right.

Lesson 2.4 — Milk powder: calcium, fat, and the vitamins that make the mix work

Milk powder is the third ingredient and the smallest by weight — but it plays three roles that nothing else quite fills.

Role 1 — Fills the last amino acid gap

Maize plus soy gives a strong complete protein, but milk protein is truly excellent — with a PDCAAS of 1.00. Adding milk powder at 10% closes whatever small gap remains and lifts the entire mix's protein quality from 85% to ~92% usable.

This is why the WHO and WFP formulas include milk powder even though it is expensive. The bump in protein quality is small but consistent and meaningful, especially in the first weeks when a severely wasted child is rebuilding tissue fast.

Role 2 — Calcium

Calcium is essential for bone growth, blood clotting, muscle function and nerve signalling. A child aged 1–3 needs about 500 mg of calcium a day. A child aged 4–8 needs 800 mg.

Per 100 grams of the fortified flour mix (using full-cream milk powder at 10%):

  • Calcium from milk powder: ~90 mg
  • Calcium from soy flour: ~70 mg
  • Calcium from maize: ~5 mg
  • Total: ~165 mg per 100 g flour

A child eating 150 g of dry flour per day gets about 250 mg of calcium from the porridge. That is half to one-third of what they need — the rest must come from other food or, if unavailable, from a supplement. But the porridge contributes a solid baseline that plain maize porridge gives almost none of.

Role 3 — Fat, vitamins A and D

Full-cream milk powder contains about 26% fat. That fat is critical for three reasons:

  • Energy density. Fat has 9 calories per gram, more than double carbohydrates. Fat makes the porridge actually fill a child up.
  • Fat-soluble vitamins. Vitamins A, D, E and K dissolve in fat, not water. Without fat in the porridge, these vitamins do not get absorbed — even if they are in the premix.
  • Brain development. A young child's brain is roughly 60% fat. Dietary fat directly supports brain growth in the first two years of life.

Full-cream vs skim milk powder

Full-cream is the better choice for malnourished children. Skim milk powder has had the fat removed — it saves money and shelf life, but loses the fat-soluble vitamins, the energy density, and part of the child's brain-building input.

TypeFatBest for
Full-cream (whole)~26%Malnourished children — the target of this course
Skim / low-fat~1%Adult weight loss, not child nutrition
Buttermilk powder~5%Baking, not fortification

Full-cream milk powder costs more and goes rancid faster. But for this specific application, it is the right choice. If cost forces you to use skim, add extra vegetable oil in the recipe (see lesson 2.5) to compensate.

Which brands to trust

In most African markets you will find:

  • Locally packaged full-cream milk powder — usually the cheapest, quality varies by supplier
  • Imported branded milk powder (Nido, Anchor, Lakeland, etc.) — more consistent, higher cost
  • Unbranded bulk powder from farms — cheapest, but check for smell, colour and clumping before buying

A simple test: open the bag. Good milk powder smells like fresh milk and pours freely. Bad milk powder smells sour, cheesy or stale, or has hard lumps. If the smell is off, do not buy it — the powder has absorbed moisture and is likely contaminated.

Never use infant formula in place of milk powder. Infant formula is balanced for bottle-feeding human infants and is not designed for cooking at high temperatures. It also has strict dilution instructions that are not met by porridge preparation. Use only full-cream milk powder sold as a food ingredient.
Practical exercise 2.4: Buy three small sachets of milk powder from three different sources. Open all three and smell them side by side. Note which smells freshest. Buy from that source when you scale up.

Lesson 2.5 — Sugar and oil: energy density — why plain porridge fails a malnourished child

You have now assembled a complete protein from maize and soy, added calcium and fat-soluble vitamins from milk. But the porridge is still thin. A child would have to eat two litres a day to hit their calorie target — impossible. This lesson fixes that.

The energy problem in plain porridge

Plain thin maize porridge, cooked traditionally, has roughly 30–40 calories per 100 millilitres. It is mostly water. A 10 kg malnourished child needs about 1,500–2,000 calories per day.

So the child would need to drink roughly 4,000 millilitres — four litres — of plain porridge every day. That is physically impossible for a child whose stomach is the size of two adult fists. They will fill up on water and stop. This is why mothers in maize-based communities so often say "the child eats well but does not grow" — the porridge fills the stomach without feeding the body.

The solution is not to feed them more porridge. It is to make each spoonful carry more calories.

Sugar — the quickest source of energy

Sugar (sucrose) provides 4 calories per gram. The recipe adds sugar to reach roughly 10% of the dry mix by weight.

Why not 20%? Because:

  • It is expensive relative to maize and soy
  • Above 10% the porridge becomes sickly sweet and children refuse it
  • Excessive sugar in a child's diet distorts their palate for the rest of life
  • The energy is empty — sugar gives calories but no nutrients

Sugar's role is to raise the calorie count and make the porridge palatable. It is not a nutrient.

Oil — the dense energy source

Oil provides 9 calories per gram, more than twice what sugar or starch gives. A teaspoon of vegetable oil (5 g) adds 45 calories. A tablespoon (15 g) adds 135. In a formula for malnourished children, this is transformative.

The recipe uses around 5% vegetable oil by weight — 50 g of oil in a 1 kg batch. That adds 450 calories to the dry mix. Compared to the same mix without oil (~3,500 calories), this is a significant jump.

Why vegetable oil, not palm oil or animal fat

  • Vegetable oil (sunflower, soybean, canola, groundnut): high in unsaturated fats, liquid at room temperature, mild taste, long shelf life. Best choice.
  • Palm oil: high in saturated fat, sold as red palm oil in West Africa. Red palm oil has some vitamin A, which is a bonus — but its strong taste changes the porridge significantly. Use it if it is what the family normally cooks with.
  • Animal fat (beef tallow, lard): solid at room temperature, difficult to mix evenly into dry flour, and can go rancid faster. Not suitable.

The energy-density calculation

Here is what a 1 kg dry mix actually delivers, with all five ingredients:

IngredientWeightCalories
Maize flour (whole grain)650 g2,340
Soy flour (full-fat)250 g1,115
Milk powder (full-cream)100 g500
Sugar50 g200
Vegetable oil50 g450
Total (approx)1,100 g4,605

Note: the total weight rises above 1,000 g because we add sugar and oil on top of the base ingredients. Adjust the ratio so the final dry mix is roughly 4,200–4,600 calories per kg.

Now cook 100 g of this dry mix in 500 ml water. You get a thick porridge that delivers:

  • ~420 calories
  • ~15 g complete protein
  • ~12 g fat
  • Calcium, iron, zinc, vitamin A from the natural ingredients

Four or five such servings per day give a 10 kg child everything they need. Two litres of plain porridge cannot.

The single biggest mistake home producers make is leaving out the oil because it seems expensive. Without oil, the porridge is a good protein drink but a poor energy source. Fat is what makes the difference between a child gaining weight and a child staying thin. Do not skip it.
Practical exercise 2.5: Cook two small batches of porridge — one plain, one with oil added. Note the difference in texture, mouthfeel and how long it keeps a child satisfied. The oil version will feel "heavier" and hold appetite for longer. That is the point.

Lesson 2.6 — Micronutrients: the vitamins and minerals you must add (and where to get them)

You now have a complete protein, good fats, and enough calories. What is still missing? The micronutrients — vitamins and minerals that the body needs in tiny amounts but cannot make at all.

The five critical micronutrients

For a malnourished child in a low-resource setting, five micronutrients matter most:

NutrientWhat it doesDeficiency signNatural source
Vitamin AVision, immunity, growthNight blindness, frequent infectionsYellow maize, red palm oil, liver, orange vegetables
IronOxygen transport in bloodPale palms, fatigue, poor concentrationDark leafy greens, liver, beans
ZincGrowth, immunity, appetitePoor appetite, slow growth, delayed wound healingBeans, nuts, animal foods
IodineThyroid, brain developmentGoitre, mental retardation in severe casesIodised salt, seafood
Folate / B12Cell division, red blood cellsAnaemia, fatigueLeafy greens, animal foods

The maize, soy and milk provide some of these naturally. But not enough — and crucially, they are poorly absorbed because of phytates and other anti-nutrients in maize and soy. The body takes up less than 10% of the iron in the porridge without help.

The micronutrient premix — your solution

The food industry solves this with a product called a micronutrient premix — a carefully balanced powder of vitamins and minerals designed to be added to fortified blended foods. It is used by WFP, UNICEF and every commercial fortified-food producer in the world.

A typical premix for child porridge contains:

  • Vitamin A (as retinyl palmitate)
  • Vitamin D3
  • Vitamin E
  • Vitamin K
  • Vitamin C
  • Thiamine (B1), Riboflavin (B2), Niacin (B3), B6, B12
  • Folic acid
  • Pantothenic acid
  • Iron (as ferrous fumarate — the most absorbable form for food fortification)
  • Zinc
  • Copper
  • Iodine (in iodised salt)
  • Selenium

Different premix formulations exist for different ages. For children 6–59 months, the standard is roughly:

  • Vitamin A: 400–800 micrograms per day
  • Iron: 10–12 mg per day
  • Zinc: 5–8 mg per day
  • And the others in proportion

In the recipe, we add premix at around 0.5% of the dry mix by weight — that is 5 grams per kilogram. A 500 g sachet of the final flour contains 2.5 g of premix, which delivers the correct daily dose across several servings.

Where to buy a premix

The premix is not sold in ordinary shops. It is a B2B ingredient. Suppliers include:

  • DSM (formerly Roche) — the largest global supplier of food premixes. Has offices in Kenya, South Africa, Nigeria, and sells in small quantities to approved processors.
  • BASF — also global, sells food premixes. European and African distribution.
  • Pioma / SternVitamin / Vitablend — smaller specialist premix suppliers, ship to Africa.
  • Local millers and food processors — often buy premix in bulk and will sell small quantities with a markup.
  • WFP / UNICEF procurement — if you are registered as an NGO, you can access their prequalified suppliers.

Small producers are usually best served by buying from a local miller who already imports premix. Ask around. Many fortified maize meal and cooking oil producers will sell you a kilogram or two at a small markup.

If premix is not available — your fallback

For a home cook who cannot get premix, the porridge still works without it. The natural vitamins and minerals in maize, soy and milk will do a lot. The premix closes the last 10–15% of the nutrition gap — important, but not the difference between life and death in most cases.

Fallback options that improve micronutrient content naturally:

  • Use yellow maize — more vitamin A
  • Add 1–2 tablespoons of red palm oil — more vitamin A
  • Use iodised salt — 1 pinch per serving — more iodine
  • Add a small amount of cooked mashed green leafy vegetables (amaranth, moringa, spinach) — more iron and folate
  • Add a few groundnuts or groundnut paste — more zinc and healthy fats
  • Include a small amount of egg or liver if available — the most bioavailable iron, zinc and vitamin A of any food

None of these fully replaces a professionally formulated premix. But if you cannot access premix, use what you have.

Do not attempt to make your own vitamin premix from individual supplement pills. Vitamin toxicity is real — especially vitamin A, which can cause liver damage and birth defects in excess. Children's daily vitamin A is measured in micrograms, not milligrams. If you use a premix, use it according to the recipe. If you do not have a premix, rely on food sources.

Phytates — the absorption problem

One final piece of the science. Maize and soy both contain phytic acid — a compound that binds to iron, zinc and calcium and prevents the body from absorbing them. Whole grains contain more phytate than refined grains. Soy contains a lot.

That means even though soy has 15 mg of iron per 100 g, the body may absorb less than 2 mg. This is why fortified porridges still fall short unless a premix provides extra — and why the WHO formulas include absorption enhancers.

Simple ways to reduce phytate impact:

  • Soak soy before roasting — reduces phytates by up to 30%
  • Add a small amount of vitamin C — an orange slice or lemon juice in the cooked porridge dramatically boosts iron absorption
  • Use fermented or germinated flour — both reduce phytates further, though this adds complexity

Module 6 (soy processing) will cover soaking and roasting in detail. For now, remember: the premix is designed knowing about phytates, so the doses are already adjusted for real-world absorption.

Practical exercise 2.6: Ask three local millers or fortified food producers this week: "Do you use a vitamin premix in your products? Can you sell me 1 kg?" Write down names and prices. If none sell it, ask who does. Within a month you should know where your premix will come from.
