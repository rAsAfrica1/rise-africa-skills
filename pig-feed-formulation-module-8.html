1. Grinding, Mixing, and Pelleting Processes

Grinding is the first step in feed processing. You need to reduce particle size so pigs can digest feed better. A hammer mill is common in Africa. It uses swinging hammers to crush grains like maize, sorghum, and soybean meal. The screen size determines particle size. For pigs, a screen of 3-4 mm is good for finishing pigs, but 2-3 mm for piglets. If you grind too fine, it can cause ulcers and dust; too coarse, pigs waste feed. A typical hammer mill in Kenya or Nigeria costs between $500 and $2,000. You can also use a roller mill, which produces more uniform particles but is more expensive.

After grinding, you must mix all ingredients evenly. A horizontal mixer or vertical mixer works well. For small farms, a cement mixer can be used, but it must be cleaned. Mix for at least 5-10 minutes to ensure uniformity. Add premixes and minerals after mixing the main grains. A common mistake is adding too much or too little of a premix, which can harm pigs. Use a scale accurate to 0.1 kg for small ingredients.

Pelleting is optional but improves feed efficiency. A pellet mill forces steam-conditioned feed through a die to form pellets. Pellets reduce waste, improve digestibility, and prevent pigs from sorting ingredients. However, pelleting requires capital (a small pellet mill costs $1,500-$5,000) and energy. In many African settings, pelleting is done by commercial mills. If you pellet, cool the pellets properly to prevent mold. For piglets, crumbled pellets are best. Always check pellet durability: good pellets should not break easily when dropped from 1 meter.

Remember, processing affects cost. Grinding and mixing on-farm can save money but requires labour. Pelleting adds cost but can improve feed conversion by 5-10%. Choose based on your scale and market.

2. Effects of Processing on Nutrient Digestibility

Processing changes how well pigs can digest nutrients. Grinding is the most important. When you grind maize to a fine particle size (around 500-700 microns), pigs digest starch and protein better. Studies show that reducing particle size from 1000 to 500 microns can improve feed efficiency by 5-8% in grower pigs. But too fine (below 400 microns) can cause stomach ulcers and increase dust. For soybean meal, grinding helps but also heat processing is needed to destroy anti-nutritional factors like trypsin inhibitor. In Africa, many farmers use full-fat soybeans; they must be roasted or extruded before feeding. Raw soybeans reduce protein digestibility by up to 50%.

Pelleting improves digestibility by breaking down starch and denaturing proteins through heat and pressure. Pelleting can increase digestibility of energy by 2-5% and protein by 1-3%. However, excessive heat (above 90Â°C) can destroy vitamins and enzymes. So, if you add enzymes or vitamins, add them after pelleting or use a post-pellet liquid application. For piglets, pelleted feed often leads to better growth because it reduces feed wastage and sorting.

Other processes: Extrusion is used for full-fat soybeans and can improve digestibility by 10-15% but requires expensive equipment. Steam flaking of grains (like maize) gelatinizes starch, improving digestibility by 5-10%. This is common in large mills but not small farms. Fermentation is a low-cost method: fermenting cassava or other by-products can reduce cyanide and improve protein availability. For example, fermented cassava peel can replace 20-30% of maize in pig diets after proper drying.

In summary, processing can either help or hurt. Over-processing wastes money and can destroy nutrients. Under-processing means pigs get less value from feed. Test your feed regularly with a simple digestibility trial: feed pigs a known amount and collect feces to see how much is undigested. Work with local extension officers to fine-tune your processing.

3. Feed Mill Equipment and Layout

Setting up a feed mill in Africa requires careful planning. For a small-to-medium pig farm (50-200 sows), you need a hammer mill, a mixer, a weighing scale, and a bag closer. A typical layout: receiving area for raw materials, grinding section, mixing section, and bagging area. Keep the flow in one direction to avoid cross-contamination. The mill should be on a concrete floor, with good ventilation and dust control. A 10 HP hammer mill can grind 500-1000 kg per hour. A horizontal mixer of 500 kg capacity takes 5-10 minutes per batch.

Essential equipment: 1) Hammer mill with screens of different sizes. 2) Mixer â horizontal ribbon or paddle mixer is better than vertical for uniformity. 3) Weighing scale â a platform scale for 50-100 kg bags, and a precision scale for premixes. 4) Bag closer â manual or electric. 5) Dust collector â to protect workers' lungs. 6) Magnetic separator â to remove metal pieces that can damage equipment or harm pigs. 7) Pellet mill if you pellet. For small farms, you can buy a combined grinder-mixer. Costs: hammer mill $800-$2000, mixer $1500-$4000, scale $200-$500. In Nigeria, local fabricators make affordable versions.

Layout tips: Place the hammer mill near the raw material storage to reduce carrying. The mixer should be close to the mill for easy transfer. Use augers or conveyors if possible, but manual transfer is common. Keep finished feed storage away from raw materials. Have a separate area for adding premixes and medications to avoid contamination. Ensure there is a water source for cleaning and fire extinguisher for safety.

Maintenance: Grease bearings weekly, check hammers for wear, and replace screens when worn. Keep a logbook for maintenance. Train workers on safety: wear masks, ear protection, and avoid loose clothing near moving parts. A well-laid-out mill saves time, reduces errors, and produces consistent feed.

4. Quality Control in Feed Manufacturing

Quality control (QC) ensures your feed meets the nutritional needs of pigs and is safe. Start with raw materials. When you receive maize, check for moisture (should be below 14%), mold, and insects. Use a moisture meter (cost $100-$300) or the salt-and-bottle test: mix salt with grain in a bottle, shake, and if salt sticks, moisture is too high. For soybean meal, check for a sweet smell and light color; dark or burnt smell means over-processing. Fishmeal should smell fishy, not rancid. Take samples and keep them for at least 3 months.

During processing, check particle size regularly. Use a set of sieves to test grind fineness. For pigs, aim for 700-800 microns. Check mixer uniformity: after mixing, take 10 samples from different parts of the mixer and test for salt or a tracer (like iron filings). If variation is more than 10%, mix longer. For pellets, check durability: drop 10 pellets from 1 meter onto a hard surface; less than 10% should break. Also check pellet temperature after cooling â should be within 5Â°C of room temperature.

Finished feed QC: Test for protein, moisture, and mycotoxins. Simple kits for protein (Kjeldahl or NIR) are expensive, but you can send samples to a lab. In many African countries, private labs charge $20-$50 per sample. For mycotoxins (aflatoxin), use rapid test kits ($5-$10 per test). Never feed moldy feed to pigs, especially breeding sows. Keep records of batch numbers, ingredients, and test results.

Implement a HACCP-like plan: identify critical points (e.g., receiving, mixing, storage) and set limits. Train workers to follow protocols. A simple QC checklist can prevent costly mistakes. Remember, consistent quality builds trust and improves pig performance.

5. Preventing Cross-Contamination and Carryover

Cross-contamination happens when residues from one batch of feed mix with another. This is dangerous when you make medicated feed for sick pigs and then non-medicated feed for healthy pigs. Carryover of drugs like antibiotics can cause residues in pork and promote antibiotic resistance. To prevent this, follow a strict sequence: produce non-medicated feed first, then medicated feed, and always flush the system with ground maize or a dedicated flush material between batches. The flush should be 5-10% of the mixer capacity. For example, if your mixer is 500 kg, use 25-50 kg of maize to flush. This flushed material should be labeled and used only for the same medicated feed or discarded according to regulations.

Equipment design helps. Use dedicated bins, augers, and mixers for medicated feed if possible. If not, clean thoroughly. After making medicated feed, dismantle and clean the mixer, augers, and bucket elevators. Use a vacuum or brush to remove dust. Never use compressed air, as it spreads dust. Wet cleaning is best but dry the equipment before use. For small farms, have a separate small mixer for medicated feed.

Also, prevent cross-contamination from raw materials. Store allergens (like soybean meal) away from other ingredients. Use separate scoops and buckets. Color-code them: red for medicated, green for non-medicated. Train workers to wash hands and change clothes between batches. Pests like rodents and birds can also spread contaminants; control them with traps and proper storage.

Finally, test for carryover. Send samples of non-medicated feed to a lab to check for drug residues. If positive, improve your cleaning and flushing. In Africa, many feed mills are small, so simple measures like a dedicated flush bin and written protocols can make a big difference. Always follow local regulations on medicated feed.

6. Storage and Handling of Finished Feed

Proper storage keeps feed fresh and safe. Finished feed should be stored in a cool, dry, well-ventilated place. Ideal temperature is below 25Â°C and humidity below 65%. In hot African climates, use shade or air conditioning if possible. Store feed on pallets, at least 10 cm off the floor and 30 cm away from walls to prevent moisture and pests. Stack bags no more than 10 high to avoid crushing. Use the first-in, first-out (FIFO) rule: always use older feed first. Label each bag with date of manufacture and batch number.

Moisture is the enemy. Feed with more than 12% moisture can grow mold and produce aflatoxins. If feed gets wet, dry it immediately or discard. Use moisture absorbers like silica gel in storage rooms? Not practical for large volumes. Instead, ensure the roof does not leak, and use plastic sheeting under bags if the floor is damp. For long storage (more than 2 months), add a mold inhibitor like propionic acid (0.2-0.5% of feed). This is common in commercial feed. For home-mixed feed, try to use within 4 weeks.

Pests: rodents, insects, and birds. Keep storage clean, remove spilled feed, and use rodent-proof containers. For insects, you can fumigate with phosphine tablets (follow safety rules) or use diatomaceous earth. Never store feed near chemicals like pesticides or fertilizers. Keep bags sealed; use a bag closer or tie tightly. If you see weevils, freeze the feed for 48 hours or spread it in the sun (but this can reduce vitamins).

Handling: When loading and unloading, avoid tearing bags. Use trolleys. Do not throw bags. For bulk feed, use clean bins with lids. Train workers to wash hands before handling feed. Record inventory: date in, date out, quantity. A simple notebook works. In Africa, many farmers lose 10-20% of feed to spoilage; good storage can cut this to under 5%.

Practical Reference: Feed Processing and Manufacturing for Pig Enterprises in Africa

Feed represents 60â75% of the total cost of pig production in most African systems. A well-run feed mill is therefore not a cost centre â it is the single largest profit lever on the farm. This practical reference consolidates the six core lessons of this module into a working document: grinding, mixing and pelleting; the effects of processing on nutrient digestibility; mill equipment and layout; quality control; cross-contamination prevention; and storage of finished feed. The emphasis throughout is on decisions that pay back in money, not just in theory.

1. Grinding: the cheapest performance gain you will ever make

Particle size determines how much of the feed the pig can actually use. A pig's stomach and small intestine cannot extract nutrients from a whole maize kernel efficiently; grinding increases the surface area exposed to digestive enzymes. Research consistently shows that reducing mean particle size from about 1,000 microns to 500â600 microns improves feed conversion in growing pigs by roughly 5â8%, and the effect is larger in young pigs. In a 100-sow unit, a 5% improvement in feed conversion on 250 tonnes of feed per year is worth more than most equipment upgrades on the farm.

But finer is not always better. Below about 400 microns, the risk of gastric ulcers rises sharply, dust becomes a respiratory hazard for workers, and hammer mill throughput falls while energy consumption rises. The practical target for most African pig operations is 500â700 microns for maize and 600â800 microns for protein meals, checked with a simple sieving test rather than guessed by eye or by the sound of the mill.

2. Mixing: where formulation accuracy is won or lost

A perfectly formulated diet is worthless if the mixer delivers a different diet in every bag. Mixing must achieve two things: homogeneity of the whole batch, and correct distribution of micro-ingredients (vitamins, minerals, amino acids, medication) that are present at grams per tonne. The industry benchmark is a coefficient of variation (CV) of less than 10% for a tracer such as salt or a coloured marker, and mixers should be validated at least annually and after any repair.

Practical mixing rules for African mills:

  • Overfilling a mixer above 80% of rated capacity destroys mixing quality â the most common and most expensive mistake.
  • Mixing time is not "the longer the better". Most horizontal ribbon mixers reach CV < 10% in 3â5 minutes of dry mixing; beyond 10 minutes, segregation can begin again, especially with dense minerals.
  • Add micro-ingredients through a premix or after a carrier (ground maize or bran) to avoid clumping and electrostatic loss.
  • Never mix medicated and non-medicated feed in the same mixer without a validated flush and clean-out procedure (see Section 5).

3. Pelleting: benefits, costs and when it is worth it

Pelleting improves feed conversion in pigs by 4â8% compared with mash, mainly by reducing feed wastage and, to a lesser extent, by heat-induced starch gelatinisation and protein denaturation during conditioning. However, pelleting adds capital cost, energy cost, and die wear, and it can destroy heat-sensitive vitamins and enzymes if conditioning temperatures are not controlled. In most African small and medium pig operations, mash feeding is the rational default; pelleting becomes attractive when feed is sold commercially, when labour for cleaning troughs is expensive, or when the farm has access to cheap electricity or biomass energy.

Where pelleting is used, the practical targets are:

  • Conditioning temperature: 80â85 Â°C for pig feed, with 20â40 seconds retention time.
  • Moisture addition in the conditioner: typically 3â5% steam, not water, to avoid mould.
  • Pellet durability index (PDI) above 90% for bagged feed, tested with a simple tumbling box.
  • Cool pellets to within 5 Â°C of ambient before bagging â warm bagged feed sweats and moulds.

5. Feed mill equipment and layout

Two layouts dominate: horizontal (single-floor) and vertical (multi-floor, gravity-fed). For small African mills, a horizontal layout is cheaper to build and easier to maintain, but requires more conveying equipment and labour. A vertical layout uses gravity, cutting energy and labour, but needs a taller building â often a constraint where construction costs are high.

Essential equipment for a pig feed mill of 1â5 tonnes per hour:

  • Hammer mill with interchangeable screens (3â6 mm) and a magnetic separator before the mill to protect it from metal.
  • Batch scale or hopper scale with 0.1% accuracy for macro-ingredients; a separate small scale (0.01 kg) for micro-ingredients.
  • Horizontal ribbon or paddle mixer, 500 kgâ2 t capacity, with a validated CV < 10%.
  • Bucket elevator and screw conveyors, sized to avoid segregation of dense minerals.
  • Bins for finished feed with a minimum of two compartments (medicated and non-medicated) where medication is used.
  • Dust extraction and personal protective equipment â respiratory disease from feed dust is a real occupational hazard.

7. Preventing cross-contamination and carryover

Cross-contamination is a food-safety and legal issue, not just a quality issue. Carryover of medicated feed into non-medicated feed can leave residues in pork, and carryover of one species' feed into pig feed can cause real harm (for example, ruminant feed containing animal protein, or poultry feed with high calcium). The main controls are:

  • Dedicated lines or dedicated mixers for medicated feed where volumes justify it.
  • Sequencing: produce non-medicated feed first, then medicated feed, then flush the system.
  • A documented flush procedure using ground maize or bran, with the flush material clearly labelled and used only in the medicated stream.
  • Physical separation of intake, storage and dispatch of medicated and non-medicated materials.
  • Colour-coded bags, labels and bins to prevent human error at the bagging point.
â  SAFETY WARNING â AFLATOXIN AND FEED DUST

Maize, groundnut cake and other African feed ingredients are frequently contaminated with aflatoxins. Aflatoxin B1 is a potent liver carcinogen and is carried into pork and milk. Never use mouldy, discoloured or musty grain, even if it is cheap â the cost of a condemned carcass or a sick herd is far higher than the saving. Test every maize delivery with a rapid aflatoxin kit; reject anything above 20 ppb. In addition, feed dust from grinding and mixing is a respiratory hazard: always use dust extraction, and wear a fitted N95 or equivalent respirator, not a cloth mask, when handling dusty ingredients or cleaning mills.

8. Storage and handling of finished feed

Finished feed is a living product. Its quality declines from the moment it leaves the mixer. The three enemies are moisture, heat and time.

  • Moisture: keep finished feed below 13% moisture; above 14%, mould growth is rapid in tropical conditions.
  • Heat: store in shaded, ventilated sheds. Bag stacks should be off the floor on pallets, with at least 30 cm clearance from walls and 1 m from the roof.
  • Time: use feed within 4â6 weeks of manufacture in hot, humid African climates; vitamins and fats oxidise and mould risk rises with time.
  • Pests: use rodent-proof stores, keep the perimeter clean, and rotate stock (first in, first out).
  • Traceability: label every bag with batch number, date of manufacture and medication status. This is required by most national feed regulations and is essential if a problem arises.
