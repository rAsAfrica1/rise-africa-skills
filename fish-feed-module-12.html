Lesson 12.1~11 min

Costing Your Production Cycle Per Kilogram of Fish

In this lesson
  • Build a complete cost per kilogram calculation from feed, seed, labour and fixed costs
  • Identify your break-even selling price and compare it with local market prices
  • Find which single cost line offers the most realistic reduction on your farm

You cannot improve a business you have not costed. Most small fish farmers know their feed price and their sale price and nothing in between, which is why they are surprised at the end of a cycle.

Build the sheet with six lines, for one pond, one cycle.

  1. Fingerlings: number stocked x cost each
  2. Feed: kg used x price per kg
  3. Labour: days x daily rate, including your own time
  4. Water, power and aeration: fuel or electricity for pumps and aerators
  5. Fixed cost allocation: nets, buckets, pond maintenance, spread across cycles
  6. Losses: mortality is not a separate cost, it appears as fewer kilograms to divide by

Worked example, Zambia. A farmer near Lusaka runs one 800 m2 pond of Nile tilapia.

  • Stocked 3,000 fingerlings at an assumed USD 0.05 each = USD 150
  • Survival 80 percent, so 2,400 fish harvested at 300 g = 720 kg
  • Feed at FCR 1.8 on roughly 720 kg of gain: 1,296 kg at USD 0.70/kg = USD 907.20
  • Labour, 150 days at an assumed USD 2.00/day allocated to this pond = USD 300
  • Pumping and aeration fuel, assumed USD 80
  • Fixed cost allocation, assumed USD 60

Total cost = 150 + 907.20 + 300 + 80 + 60 = USD 1,497.20 Cost per kg = 1,497.20 / 720 = USD 2.08/kg

That is his break-even price. If the local farm-gate price is USD 2.00/kg he is losing money on every fish, even though the pond looks busy and productive. The reference data puts small-scale pond break-even at USD 0.80-1.50/kg in efficient operations, so USD 2.08 says something on this farm is out of line.

Where is the fat? Feed is USD 907.20, or 61 percent of total cost, which matches the general rule that feed is 50-70 percent of production cost. So feed is where the money is, and there are only two ways to reduce it: pay less per kilogram, or use fewer kilograms per kilogram of fish.

  • Improving FCR from 1.8 to 1.5 cuts feed to 1,080 kg, saving USD 151.20 and dropping cost per kg to USD 1.87.
  • Buying at USD 0.55/kg instead of USD 0.70/kg through a bulk group cuts feed cost to USD 712.80, saving USD 194.40 and dropping cost per kg to USD 1.81.
  • Doing both gives feed at 1,080 x 0.55 = USD 594, total cost USD 1,184, and USD 1.64/kg. Now he has a margin.

Notice that improving survival also matters. At 90 percent survival he harvests 2,700 fish, or 810 kg, and the same total cost spreads over more fish: 1,497.20 / 810 = USD 1.85/kg without changing anything else. Mortality reduction is a cost reduction.

The discipline. Keep three records and everything else follows: feed bought and fed by weight, fish harvested by weight, and every payment made. From those three you can calculate FCR, cost per kilogram and margin at any time. Without them you are guessing.

One caution about prices. Every USD figure in this lesson that is marked as assumed must be replaced with your own. Fingerling prices, labour rates and farm-gate fish prices vary enormously between Kisumu, Kano and Kafue. The method transfers; the numbers do not.

Feed Conversion Ratio as Your Main Profit Lever

In this lesson
  • Calculate FCR correctly from feed used and weight gained
  • Rank the six main drivers of FCR variation by how much each costs you
  • Set an achievable FCR target for your species and system and track it each cycle

FCR is the number that connects everything else in this course to money. It is simple to calculate and brutal in what it reveals.

FCR = total feed used (kg) divided by weight gain (kg)

Example: 1,000 kg of feed producing 600 kg of fish gain is an FCR of 1.67. Note that FCR includes mortality losses. If survival is below 90 percent, your apparent FCR looks worse than the biological reality, because you fed fish that died. That is not a bookkeeping error, it is the true cost.

What you should be achieving, at good temperature:

  • African catfish, fingerling 10-50 g at 25-28 C: 1.2-1.4; grow-out 50-500 g: 1.4-1.7
  • Nile tilapia, fingerling 20-50 g at 27-30 C: 1.3-1.5; juvenile 50-200 g: 1.5-1.8; grow-out 200-500 g: 1.6-2.0
  • Channel catfish, fingerling at 27-30 C: 1.4-1.6; grow-out 200-500 g: 1.8-2.2
  • Common carp, fingerling at 20-25 C: 1.5-1.8; grow-out: 2.0-2.5

And what happens when conditions go wrong: tilapia below 20 C runs FCR 3.0-5.0, and channel catfish below 20 C runs 3.5-6.0. Cold-season feeding is close to pouring money in the water.

The six drivers of FCR variation, with their documented cost:

  1. Starch gelatinisation. Raw, unprocessed feed is 10-20 percent worse FCR because starch digestibility drops 20-40 percent. Steam-extruded maize is 75-85 percent digestible against 30-40 percent raw.
  2. Water temperature. Each 1 C below optimal costs about 3-5 percent of FCR efficiency.
  3. Stocking density. Above about 100 tilapia per cubic metre, 10-15 percent worse.
  4. Protein to energy balance. Imbalance costs 10-20 percent; the optimum is 8-10 kcal digestible energy per gram of protein, so a 30 percent protein feed at 3,000 kcal DE/kg gives exactly 10 kcal/g.
  5. Water quality. Ammonia above 0.1 mg/L costs 5-10 percent; dissolved oxygen below 4 mg/L costs 15-30 percent.
  6. Feed quality. A balanced feed with over 80 percent ingredient digestibility gives 1.3-1.5; a poor feed under 60 percent digestibility gives 2.0 or worse.

Worked example, Nigeria. A farmer in Delta State produces 5,000 kg of African catfish a year. At FCR 2.0 he uses 10,000 kg of feed; at USD 0.70/kg that is USD 7,000. His species should be achieving 1.4-1.7. Moving to 1.6 means 8,000 kg of feed, USD 5,600, a saving of USD 1,400 a year. Moving to 1.5 means 7,500 kg, USD 5,250, saving USD 1,750.

What gets him there, in order of cost:

  • Free: feed at the right times of day, stop when fish stop eating, remove dead fish, grade to reduce competition. Fixing water quality alone can recover 15-30 percent.
  • Cheap: add lysine and methionine at USD 0.02-0.05 per kg for an FCR gain of 0.05-0.10, and phytase at USD 0.03-0.08 per kg.
  • Moderate: buy or hire extrusion or at least cook the starch fraction of a home mix, recovering the 10-20 percent gelatinisation penalty.
  • Structural: aeration, lower stocking, better fingerlings.

A demand feeder is worth noting for larger operations. Field trials report FCR improving from 1.5 to 1.3, survival up 8-10 percent and the production cycle shortening by about four weeks. Equipment costs USD 500-2,000 per feeder and breaks even at roughly 5,000-20,000 kg of feed consumption, which is one to two years on a small pond. It is a strong investment for an intensive farm and marginal for an extensive one.

Track FCR every cycle. Write feed used and fish harvested on the same page. A farm that knows its FCR trend is a farm that can be improved.

On-Farm Feed Mixing and Milling: When the Equipment Pays Back

In this lesson
  • Sequence the five investment steps that reduce feed cost, from free to capital-intensive
  • Calculate payback period for a small pellet mill using your own feed volume
  • Recognise the quality risks of home-made feed and how to control them

Making your own feed can cut cost substantially, but only in the right order and only if you can hold quality. Farmers who buy a pellet mill first and learn formulation later usually make expensive, poorly conserved feed.

The documented cost-reduction roadmap, in order:

  1. Buy premixes and hand-mix feed on-farm. Investment: none beyond a mixing surface. Break-even: immediate. Feed cost reduction: 15-20 percent.
  2. Ferment plant ingredients locally. Investment: USD 50-100 for containers and starter. Break-even: 2-3 months. Additional reduction: 5-10 percent.
  3. Establish a black soldier fly larvae colony or an algae culture. Investment: USD 200-500. Break-even: 3-6 months. Additional reduction: 10-15 percent.
  4. Buy a small pellet mill of 2-3 kW. Investment: USD 500-1,500. Break-even: 6-12 months. Additional reduction: 5-10 percent from pelleting efficiency.
  5. Join or form a farmer cooperative for bulk ingredient buying. Investment: organisational, minimal cash. Break-even: immediate. Reduction: 10-15 percent.

Notice that the two steps with immediate payback and no capital, hand-mixing with bought premixes and cooperative buying, together deliver 25-35 percent. Do those first.

Worked example, Nigeria. A farmer in Kaduna uses 6,000 kg of feed a year. Commercial pellet at retail is USD 0.70/kg, so USD 4,200 a year. A do-it-yourself mix using rice bran at 5-10 percent, soy at 30-35 percent, groundnut cake at 15-20 percent, maize at 15-20 percent and 5-10 percent local fish by-product costs USD 0.25-0.40/kg. Take the middle at USD 0.33/kg: 6,000 x 0.33 = USD 1,980, a saving of USD 2,220 a year.

Against that, a 2-3 kW pellet mill at USD 1,000 pays back in 1,000 / 2,220 = 0.45 of a year, about five and a half months, and that is before counting the labour. The reference data warns that hand-mixing and small-scale pelleting add 20-30 percent in indirect labour cost, so apply that honestly: if labour adds USD 500 a year, the saving is USD 1,720 and the payback is about seven months. Still strong.

The quality risks you must manage, or the saving evaporates:

  • Starch gelatinisation. Raw mixes are 10-20 percent worse in FCR. Cook or steam the starch fraction; a mill that heats during extrusion does this for you.
  • Water stability. A simple fish meal and wheat flour mix holds together for only 2-4 hours and loses 20-30 percent of protein and energy to leaching within 2 hours. Adding gelatinised maize starch gives 4-8 hours for about 5 percent more cost, and a proper binder such as alginate, CMC or bentonite gives 8-24 hours with under 5 percent leaching for 5-10 percent more cost. In a hand-fed pond, poor stability plus uneaten feed can total 30-45 percent waste.
  • Vitamin and mineral adequacy. Buy a complete premix. Mineral premix costs USD 0.03-0.05 per kg of feed and vitamin premix USD 0.03-0.05. This is the mandatory tier and it is not optional.
  • Amino acid balance. Add DL-methionine at 0.2-0.3 percent and L-lysine at around 0.3-0.4 percent to any plant-heavy mix, at USD 0.02-0.05 per kg for a 2 to 5 times return.
  • Ingredient safety. Keep maize under 20 percent, reject mouldy material, use rice bran fresh within 2-3 weeks unless stabilised.

A reference formulation from the data, a channel catfish fingerling diet at 28 percent crude protein and 3,000 kcal DE per kg, comes out at roughly USD 0.80/kg using 15 percent fish meal, 40 percent soybean meal, 20 percent maize, 10 percent wheat, 8 percent soybean oil, 5 percent mineral premix, 1.3 percent vitamin premix, 0.3 percent DL-methionine and 0.4 percent L-lysine. That is a template to adapt, not a recipe to copy, because your local ingredient prices and availability differ.

Start small. Mix one batch, feed it to one pond alongside a control pond on commercial feed, and compare growth over four to six weeks before you convert the whole farm.

Buying Together: Cooperatives, Bulk Purchasing and Price Risk

In this lesson
  • Quantify the savings available from cooperative bulk ingredient purchasing
  • Assess how much feed price increase your farm can absorb before it becomes unviable
  • Design a simple group buying arrangement with clear rules on money and quality

Feed price volatility is the biggest external threat to a small fish farm, and buying alone is the most expensive way to buy.

The vulnerability is documented. Feed is 60-70 percent of revenue for small-scale ponds in places like Egypt, Uganda and Vietnam, which means those farms can only absorb a 10-15 percent feed price rise before margins go, and become marginal above 20 percent. When feed prices rose 30-40 percent in the 2008 crisis, Vietnamese catfish farmers could absorb only about 15 percent and Thai tilapia farmers about 31 percent. Many small producers left the industry and it consolidated. That is what price risk actually looks like.

Commercial raceway operations, where feed is 50-60 percent of revenue, could absorb 25-30 percent. Intensive tank and recirculating systems in high-income countries, at 40-50 percent, could absorb over 40 percent by shifting cost to premium markets. The lesson is uncomfortable but clear: the smaller and less efficient the farm, the more exposed it is.

Cooperative buying is the cheapest defence. The reference roadmap puts farmer cooperative bulk ingredient buying at a 10-15 percent cost reduction with immediate payback and essentially no cash investment, just organisation.

Worked example, Kenya. Eight farmers around Kisumu each use 3,000 kg of feed a year, so 24 tonnes between them. Buying individually at USD 0.70/kg costs each farmer USD 2,100 and the group USD 16,800. Negotiating a 12 percent bulk discount takes the price to USD 0.616/kg, so the group pays USD 14,784 and saves USD 2,016, which is USD 252 per farmer per year. If they go further and buy raw ingredients as a group to hand-mix, the roadmap suggests they can stack 15-20 percent from on-farm mixing on top of the 10-15 percent buying discount.

That group also unlocks other things: a shared pellet mill at USD 500-1,500 that no single member could justify, a shared vehicle for transport, a shared order of vitamin premix, and joint negotiation with buyers at harvest.

How to make a buying group work. The failures are almost always about money and trust, not about fish.

  1. Write down the rules before the first purchase. Who orders, who holds the money, who checks quality on delivery, how disputes are settled.
  2. Everyone pays before the order is placed. Group buying dies the first time one member takes feed on credit and does not pay.
  3. Appoint one quality checker who inspects every delivery for mould, caking, fines and manufacture date, with the authority to reject.
  4. Keep the group small enough to trust and large enough to matter. Six to twelve farmers is usually workable.
  5. Rotate the administrative role annually so no one person carries all the burden or all the power.
  6. Start with one product, usually the feed or the single biggest ingredient, and add more only after two or three successful orders.

Other price risk defences worth using alongside:

  • Diversify ingredients. If you can substitute canola or groundnut cake for part of your soybean meal, a soy price spike hurts less. Recall that soy is lysine-rich and methionine-poor while canola is the reverse, so a 60 percent soy, 30 percent canola, 10 percent fish meal blend gives a balanced amino acid profile at 20-25 percent lower cost than 50 percent soy with 50 percent fish meal.
  • Reduce dependence on purchased protein. A black soldier fly larvae colony at USD 200-500 investment reduces cost a further 10-15 percent.
  • Buy at harvest time when local grain and oilseed cake are cheapest, but only within your storage potency window of about eight weeks unless the feed is stabilised.
  • Improve FCR, because using less feed is the only defence that works regardless of price.

Harvest Planning, Market Timing and Cash Flow

In this lesson
  • Build a month-by-month cash flow for a production cycle
  • Plan staggered stocking and partial harvests to smooth income
  • Match harvest size and timing to what local buyers actually pay for

Fish farms rarely fail because the fish die. They fail because the money runs out three months before harvest. Feed is a continuous cost and fish revenue is a single event, and that mismatch is the whole problem.

Worked cash flow, Tanzania. A farmer at Iringa stocks 3,000 tilapia fingerlings in month 1 and harvests 720 kg in month 7. Feed use rises as the fish grow, roughly following body weight.

  • Month 1: fingerlings USD 150, feed about 40 kg = USD 28. Outflow USD 178
  • Month 2: feed about 90 kg = USD 63
  • Month 3: feed about 150 kg = USD 105
  • Month 4: feed about 220 kg = USD 154
  • Month 5: feed about 290 kg = USD 203
  • Month 6: feed about 330 kg = USD 231
  • Month 7: feed about 176 kg = USD 123, then harvest revenue of 720 kg at an assumed USD 2.00/kg = USD 1,440

Cumulative outflow before any income is 178 + 63 + 105 + 154 + 203 + 231 + 123 = USD 1,057. That is the working capital he must have or borrow, and the peak need is in months 5 and 6, exactly when a farmer with one pond has the least cash and the greatest temptation to underfeed. Underfeeding at that point is the worst possible economy, because it lengthens the cycle and worsens FCR.

Three ways to smooth this.

  1. Stagger stocking. With three ponds, stock them two months apart so a harvest arrives every two months rather than all at once. Income then overlaps with the peak feeding cost of the next pond.
  2. Partial harvests. Tilapia and catfish spread in size. Seining out the largest 20-30 percent at month 5 gives early cash, reduces standing biomass and therefore water quality pressure, and lets the remaining fish grow faster with less competition.
  3. Plan the borrowing before you need it. A loan arranged in month 1 at a known rate is far cheaper than a distress sale of undersized fish in month 5.

Market timing. Ask three buyers three questions: what size do you want, what do you pay, and when do you buy most? Then work backwards. If your best buyer pays a premium for 400 g fish in the month before a major festival, that is your harvest date and your stocking date is set by it. Tilapia reach 500 g in six to eight months above 25 C, so count back accordingly and allow for the cool season, when growth slows sharply and feeding must be cut by half below 20 C.

Avoid the two common traps. The first is holding fish past their optimal size hoping for a better price, while feeding them at grow-out FCR of 1.6-2.0, which means every extra kilogram costs you nearly two kilograms of feed. The second is harvesting everything into a small local market at once and depressing your own price. If you cannot place 720 kg in a week, either arrange buyers in advance or plan partial harvests.

Post-harvest matters as much as price. Fish lose value fast in heat. Ice, a cool box, or a same-morning sale are what keep the price you negotiated. If you have reliable buyers who collect, harvest to their schedule rather than yours.

Finally, record the actual price you received, not the price you were promised. A written record of realised prices by month, over two years, is the most valuable market information any small farmer can own, and almost nobody keeps it.

Writing a Scale-Up Plan and Managing the Risks

In this lesson
  • Structure a scale-up plan with investment, returns, payback and risk sections
  • Test a plan against the assumptions most likely to be wrong
  • Sequence expansion so that each step funds the next

A scale-up plan is not a dream. It is a document that states what you will spend, what you expect back, when, and what would have to go wrong for it to fail. Lenders, partners and grant programmes all want the same thing, and so should you.

The structure, on two pages.

  1. Where you are now. Ponds, area, species, last cycle's production in kilograms, your measured FCR, your cost per kilogram, your realised selling price, your survival rate. Real figures from your record book, not estimates.
  2. What you propose. One specific change, costed. Adding two ponds, buying a pellet mill, starting a hatchery, joining a buying group, buying aeration.
  3. What it costs. Capital, plus the extra working capital the expansion needs. This second number is what most plans forget.
  4. What it returns. Extra kilograms or reduced cost, in the same units as your current sheet.
  5. Payback period. Investment divided by annual net benefit.
  6. Risks and what you will do about each.
  7. Month-by-month cash flow for the first twelve months.

Worked example, Uganda. A farmer at Masaka currently runs two ponds producing 1,400 kg a year at FCR 1.9, feed at USD 0.70/kg, cost per kilogram USD 1.95, selling at an assumed USD 2.20/kg. Annual margin is 1,400 x 0.25 = USD 350. Thin.

He proposes three changes at once, sequenced.

Step 1, no capital. Join a five-farmer buying group for a 12 percent feed discount, taking feed to USD 0.616/kg, and fix feeding times and water quality to bring FCR from 1.9 to 1.6. Feed use falls from 2,660 kg to 2,240 kg and the price falls too: 2,240 x 0.616 = USD 1,379.84 against 2,660 x 0.70 = USD 1,862. Saving USD 482 a year, for nothing but organisation and discipline.

Step 2, USD 100. Set up fermentation of his soybean meal, adding a documented 5-10 percent further feed cost reduction. Say USD 100 a year saved on this volume. Payback under three months.

Step 3, USD 1,200. With USD 582 a year now released, add a third pond costing an assumed USD 1,200 to construct. The third pond adds 700 kg a year. At the improved cost structure his cost per kilogram is now roughly USD 1.60, so the extra 700 kg earns 700 x (2.20 - 1.60) = USD 420 a year. Payback on the pond is 1,200 / 420 = 2.9 years, plus he needs about USD 500 more working capital to feed it through its first cycle.

That sequence matters. Steps 1 and 2 cost almost nothing and fund step 3. A farmer who starts with step 3 borrows USD 1,700 against a margin of USD 350 a year and will struggle.

Now the risk section, which is what separates a serious plan from a hopeful one.

  • Feed price rise. His farm can absorb only 10-15 percent before margins go. Mitigation: the buying group, ingredient diversification into groundnut cake and canola, and continued FCR improvement.
  • Market price fall. Mitigation: three named buyers rather than one, partial harvests to test the market, and knowing his break-even price exactly so he never sells below it without deciding to.
  • Mortality event. A 20 percent loss on 2,100 kg is 420 kg, USD 924 of revenue. Mitigation: the water quality thresholds and feed quality controls from modules 9 and 10, and staggered stocking so one event cannot take the whole farm.
  • Water shortage in the dry season. Mitigation: know your minimum water depth, plan a smaller dry-season stocking, and never stock to a biomass your dry-season water cannot carry.
  • Cash flow. Mitigation: the twelve-month cash flow table, arranged borrowing before it is needed, and staggered harvests.

Test every plan by asking which single assumption, if wrong, changes the answer. Usually it is the selling price or the survival rate. Write down what you will do if that assumption fails, and you have a plan rather than a hope.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. A cycle costing USD 1,497.20 that produces 720 kg of fish has a cost per kilogram of:

1,497.20 divided by 720 is USD 2.08, which is the break-even selling price.

2. Efficient small-scale pond operations break even at roughly:

Small-scale pond systems in developing regions break even around USD 0.80-1.50/kg; intensive RAS in high-income countries is USD 4.00-8.00/kg.

3. Improving survival from 80 to 90 percent lowers cost per kilogram because:

Mortality reduction is a cost reduction: more saleable weight divides the same spend.

4. The two ways to reduce total feed cost are:

Feed cost is price times quantity, so the levers are purchase price and feed conversion ratio.

5. Which three records make every other calculation possible?

Those three give you FCR, cost per kilogram and margin at any point in the cycle.

6. A farm uses 1,000 kg of feed and gains 600 kg of fish. Its FCR is:

FCR is feed divided by gain, so 1,000 / 600 = 1.67.

7. Which condition causes the largest single FCR penalty?

Low oxygen costs 15-30 percent, more than ammonia at 5-10 percent or density at 10-15 percent.

8. The optimal protein to energy ratio for growing fish is:

Below 8 causes fat deposition and waste; above 10 means protein is burned for energy, producing ammonia.

9. Feeding raw, ungelatinised starch worsens FCR by about:

Starch digestibility drops 20-40 percent when raw, costing 10-20 percent of feed efficiency.

10. Reducing FCR from 2.0 to 1.6 on 5,000 kg of annual fish production at USD 0.70/kg feed saves:

10,000 kg minus 8,000 kg is 2,000 kg of feed, and 2,000 x 0.70 = USD 1,400 a year.

11. Which cost-reduction step requires no capital and pays back immediately?

Hand-mixing on-farm with purchased premixes cuts feed cost 15-20 percent with no equipment purchase.

12. A simple fish meal and wheat flour pellet holds together in water for about:

It loses 20-30 percent of protein and energy to leaching within two hours; binders extend stability to 8-24 hours.

13. A USD 1,000 pellet mill saving USD 2,220 a year in feed cost pays back in roughly:

1,000 divided by 2,220 is about 0.45 of a year, though labour costs push the true figure closer to seven months.

14. Home-mixed feed made from raw, uncooked grain suffers:

Ungelatinised starch is only 30-40 percent digestible, costing 10-20 percent of feed efficiency.

15. The mandatory tier of supplementation for any home-mixed feed is:

Mineral premix at USD 0.03-0.05, vitamin premix at USD 0.03-0.05 and salt are the non-negotiable base.

16. For small-scale pond farms, feed represents roughly what share of revenue?

At 60-70 percent of revenue, these farms can only absorb a 10-15 percent feed price increase before margins disappear.

17. Cooperative bulk ingredient buying typically reduces feed cost by:

It delivers 10-15 percent with immediate payback and requires organisation rather than capital.

18. Eight farmers using 3,000 kg each per year, negotiating a 12 percent discount off USD 0.70/kg, save the group about:

24,000 kg x USD 0.70 = USD 16,800; a 12 percent discount saves USD 2,016, about USD 252 each.

19. The most common reason a farmer buying group collapses is:

Groups fail on payment discipline, which is why everyone should pay before the order is placed.

20. Which defence against feed price rises works regardless of what the price does?

Reducing the quantity of feed per kilogram of fish lowers exposure to any price level.

21. The main cash flow problem in fish farming is that:

Months of continuous feed spending precede a single harvest payment, creating a working capital gap.

22. Staggered stocking across several ponds mainly:

Sequencing harvests every couple of months keeps cash coming in while the next cycle is at its most expensive.

23. A partial harvest of the largest 20-30 percent at month 5 delivers:

It brings income forward, reduces water quality pressure and reduces competition for the remaining fish.

24. Holding tilapia past market size is expensive because grow-out FCR is:

At 1.6-2.0, each extra kilogram of fish costs close to two kilograms of feed.

25. The most valuable market record a small farmer can keep is:

Realised prices by month reveal seasonal patterns and give real negotiating power, and almost nobody keeps them.

26. Which section do most scale-up plans forget?

Capital cost is usually stated but the additional working capital to feed the new production through its first cycle is often omitted.

27. Why should no-capital steps come before capital steps in a scale-up sequence?

Buying-group savings and FCR improvement cost nothing and build the margin that supports later investment.

28. Payback period is calculated as:

A USD 1,200 pond returning USD 420 a year pays back in about 2.9 years.

29. The best test of a scale-up plan is to ask:

Identifying the fragile assumption, usually price or survival, and planning for its failure is what makes the plan usable.

30. Staggered stocking is listed as a mitigation for which risk?

Spreading stocking across time means a single disease or oxygen event cannot destroy all the farm's production.

Module 12 capstone

Build a complete twelve-month business case for your own farm using only your own numbers. Collect and record: your actual feed price per kg from at least two suppliers, your actual fingerling cost, your last cycle's feed used and fish harvested so you can calculate your real FCR, your labour hours, and the farm-gate price at least three local buyers actually paid in the last month. From these, produce a one-page cost sheet showing your cost per kilogram of fish produced, your break-even selling price, and your margin at current market prices. Then model one specific scaling decision, such as adding a pond, buying a small pellet mill, joining or forming a bulk-buying group, or starting a hatchery. Show the investment, the annual saving or extra revenue, the payback period, and the two risks that would break the plan. Finish with a month-by-month cash flow, since the most common reason a technically sound fish farm fails is running out of cash before harvest.
