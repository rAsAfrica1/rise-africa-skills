Lesson 4.1

The welding machine

A welding machine is not an ordinary power supply. It delivers a controlled, low-voltage, high-current output, and it does so in a way that keeps the arc stable as conditions change.

What it actually does

Mains supply might be 220–240 volts at 15 amps. A welding machine takes that in and delivers something like 20–35 volts at 60–200 amps at the torch. Two terms you will meet:

  • Constant current (CC) — the machine holds current steady and lets voltage vary. This is what stick welding needs
  • Constant voltage (CV) — the machine holds voltage steady and lets current vary. This is what MIG needs

A stick machine is CC. A MIG machine is CV. TIG is also CC.

Transformer versus inverter

TransformerInverter
How it worksHeavy copper-wound transformer steps voltage down and current upElectronics convert to high-frequency AC, then rectify and smooth
WeightHeavy — often 40–80 kgLight — often 3–10 kg
DurabilityExtremely robust — survives dust, heat, rough handlingElectronic — dust and heat shorten its life

Which is right for Africa?

For a fixed workshop with mains power, a big transformer machine is often the better long-term buy — more repairable, more robust, parts more available locally.

For portable work, off-grid use, or running from a small generator, an inverter is far better — lighter and drawing less from a modest supply.

What matters most is who can fix it when it breaks. Ask your local technician which brand they can repair, and buy that brand.

Duty cycle — the spec nobody reads

The single most important number on a machine's plate. Duty cycle is the percentage of a ten-minute period the machine can weld at a given current before it has to cool. A 60% duty cycle at 150 amps means: weld for six minutes, rest for four, repeat.

  • The headline duty cycle is often quoted at a much lower current than you will use. A machine advertised as "200 amps, 60% duty cycle" may be 200 amps at 40% and only 150 amps at 60%
  • Duty cycle gets worse in heat. A machine rated at 60% in a 25°C workshop might be 40% in a 40°C workshop

What to look for on the plate

  • Input voltage and phases — 220V single phase, or 380V three phase
  • Output current range — e.g. 20–200 amps
  • Duty cycle table — usually three or four rows at different currents
  • Open circuit voltage (OCV) — above about 80V DC it becomes a shock hazard
  • Insulation class — A to H. H is highest temperature tolerance
  • IP rating — dust and water protection. IP21 is common minimum

Photograph the plate of any machine you consider. If it has no plate, walk away.

Lesson 4.2

Leads, holders and earth clamps

Welding leads

  • Current rating. Every lead has a size and a current it will carry without overheating
  • Rubber, not plastic. Rubber stays flexible when cold and survives heat, abrasion and spatter
  • Length. Long leads cause voltage drop. Keep them as short as practical

Inspect every session

WhatLooking for
Lead insulationCuts, abrasion, burns, exposed conductor, perished rubber
Connections at holderLoose, corroded or fraying
Earth clamp jawsClean, unburned, spring tension sound
Holder jawsGrip the electrode firmly

The earth clamp

  • Good clean contact on clean metal. Clamp to bare steel, not paint or rust
  • Close to the weld. The further the current travels through your work, the worse the arc
  • Not to a pipe, chain, bench or building. Current will find its own path
Lesson 4.3

Buying a stick welder

  1. What is my power supply? Single phase 220–240V common. Three-phase 380V better
  2. What is the thickest steel I will actually weld? 140 amp does 3–4mm. 180–200 amps for 5–6mm
  3. Where will it live? Fixed workshop = transformer. Off-grid = inverter
  4. Who will fix it? Ask the local technician before the salesman
  5. Can I test it? If not, walk away

What this course cannot tell you

Machine prices vary hugely between countries and towns. Ratings on unbranded imports are frequently overstated by two or three times.

Read the plate, test on scrap, and ask the local repair technician which brand they see least often in for repair. That last answer is worth more than any price comparison.

Lesson 4.4

Buying a MIG set

What a MIG setup actually includes

  • The machine (CV power supply + wire feed)
  • Gas cylinder — hired, bought or rented
  • Regulator — reduces cylinder pressure to 10–15 L/min
  • Torch — carries wire, gas and current
  • Consumables — contact tips, nozzles, liners
  • Wire in the correct diameter and type

The gas supply question, before you buy anything

MIG needs a continuous supply of shielding gas. Find out, before you buy the machine, whether anyone near you sells or refills cylinders — and what it costs.

If that is difficult, either buy a machine that runs flux-cored wire, or stay with stick.

Lesson 4.6

Setting up a workshop

  • Concrete floor. Not timber, not dirt with dry matter
  • Steel or non-combustible bench. Timber benches burn
  • Ventilation and extraction. Fume extractor with hood positioned near the arc
  • Screens on sides where people pass
  • Isolator switch findable and reachable
  • Cylinders secured upright, out of way of sparks and heat
  • Extinguisher and water to hand

Fire, again

Clear the area before starting, check below and behind, have extinguishing means to hand, and stay in the area after finishing. The dangerous fire is not the one you see.

Module 4 knowledge check

1. Why is duty cycle the most important figure on a machine's plate?

  1. It tells you the weight
  2. It tells you the weld/rest cycle at a given current — and the headline figure is often quoted at a much lower current than you will use
  3. It tells you the input voltage
  4. Only amperage matters
Show answer

b. Duty cycle defines the weld/rest cycle at a stated current. Read the full table, not the headline, and derate for a hot workshop.

2. Why is an inverter better than a transformer for off-grid or portable work?

  1. More powerful
  2. Much lighter and draws less from a modest supply
  3. Welds thicker steel
  4. Does not need earth clamp
Show answer

b. Inverters are far lighter and more efficient, which matters for portable work or running from a small generator.

3. How should the earth clamp be attached?

  1. To a nearby pipe or building frame
  2. To the work, on clean bare metal, close to the weld
  3. To the bench
  4. To a chain wrapped around the work
Show answer

b. Clamp to the work on bare metal close to the weld. Attaching elsewhere forces the current to find its own path.

4. Critical question before buying MIG?

  1. Machine colour
  2. Whether anyone near you sells or refills shielding gas, and at what cost
  3. Digital display
  4. Website availability
Show answer

b. MIG depends on continuous gas supply. If the nearest supplier is hours away, either buy flux-cored capable or stay with stick.

5. Why not weld in a workshop with a timber floor?

  1. Timber conducts electricity
  2. Spatter falls to the floor and starts fires
  3. Too slippery
  4. Legal requirement
Show answer

b. Spatter is molten metal falling under gravity. A timber floor can smoulder for a long time before becoming visible.
