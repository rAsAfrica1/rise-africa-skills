1. GMAW Equipment and Setup

Gas Metal Arc Welding (GMAW), often called MIG welding, uses a continuous wire electrode and a shielding gas to join metals. In this lesson, you will learn the basic equipment and how to set it up safely. You need a GMAW power source (welding machine), a wire feeder, a welding gun, a gas cylinder with regulator and flowmeter, and a return lead with clamp. In Africa, common brands include Miller, Lincoln, and local imports like Deca or Telwin. For most small workshops, a 200-amp machine running on 220V single-phase is sufficient for mild steel up to 6mm thick.

Start by placing the gas cylinder upright and secure it with a chain or strap to a trolley or wall. Fit the regulator and flowmeter, then connect the gas hose to the wire feeder. The wire feeder holds a spool of wire (usually 0.8mm or 1.0mm diameter for mild steel). Feed the wire through the drive rolls and into the gun liner. Adjust the drive roll tension so the wire does not slip but also does not crush. Set the wire speed on the machine to a mid-range setting as a starting point.

Connect the work clamp (earth clamp) to your metal workpiece or welding table. Ensure bare metal contactâclean off rust, paint, or oil. The gun tip (contact tip) should match the wire diameter; for 0.8mm wire, use a 0.8mm contact tip. The nozzle (shield cup) directs the shielding gas. Check that the gas flow rate is set to about 10-15 litres per minute (LPM) for indoor welding. Open the gas valve on the cylinder slowly and set the flowmeter.

Before welding, test the wire feed by triggering the gun for a secondâwire should come out smoothly and the gas should flow. Listen for a hiss. If the wire stubs or birds-nests, adjust the drive roll tension or replace the liner. Always wear a welding helmet with at least a shade 10 lens, leather gloves, and a flame-resistant apron. In many African settings, power supply can be unstable; use a voltage stabiliser if possible. Practice on scrap metal first to get comfortable with the controls.

2. Shielding Gases and Flow Rates

Shielding gas is critical in GMAW because it protects the molten weld pool from oxygen and nitrogen in the air, which cause porosity and weak welds. The most common gas for mild steel is a mixture of 75% argon and 25% carbon dioxide (often called C25). Pure CO2 is cheaper and gives deeper penetration but more spatter. In Africa, C25 may be expensive or hard to find, so many welders use pure CO2. For stainless steel, use a mixture of 98% argon and 2% oxygen, or tri-mix (helium, argon, CO2). For aluminium, use pure argon.

Flow rate is measured in litres per minute (LPM). Too low a flow rate (below 8 LPM) gives poor shielding, leading to porosity. Too high (above 20 LPM) causes turbulence that draws in air, also causing porosity. For indoor welding, set 10-15 LPM. Outdoors, if there is a breeze, increase to 15-20 LPM and use a windbreak. In dusty or windy African environments, always weld indoors or erect a simple screen from corrugated iron sheets.

To set the flow rate: open the gas cylinder valve slowly, then turn the regulator knob until the float in the flowmeter rises to the desired LPM. Trigger the gun to let gas flow, then adjust again while gas is flowing. After welding, close the cylinder valve and bleed the line. Check for leaks by brushing soapy water on connectionsâbubbles indicate a leak. Replace damaged hoses immediately.

Gas cylinder safety: always store upright, away from heat and sparks. Use a chain to secure. Never weld near a leaking cylinder. In many African workshops, cylinders are exchanged rather than refilled on-site; ensure you get the right gas mix. Keep a spare cylinder if possible. Remember, gas cost is a big part of GMAW, so minimise waste by turning off the gas when not welding for long periods. Use a flowmeter with a clear ball for easy reading.

3. Wire Electrode Selection and Feed

The wire electrode in GMAW is a consumable that melts to form the weld. For mild steel, the most common wire is ER70S-6, which has good deoxidizers for welding on slightly rusty or dirty steel. It comes in diameters: 0.6mm, 0.8mm, 1.0mm, and 1.2mm. For thin sheet (1-3mm), use 0.6mm or 0.8mm. For general work (3-6mm), use 0.8mm or 1.0mm. For heavy sections (6mm+), use 1.2mm. In Africa, 0.8mm and 1.0mm are easiest to find. For stainless steel, use ER308L or ER316L. For aluminium, use ER4043 or ER5356.

Wire feed speed (WFS) is measured in metres per minute (m/min) or inches per minute (IPM). It must match your voltage and travel speed. As a starting point for 0.8mm wire on 3mm steel, set WFS around 4-5 m/min (160-200 IPM) and voltage around 18-20V. For 1.0mm wire on 6mm steel, try 6-7 m/min (240-280 IPM) and 22-24V. Always check the wire manufacturer's recommended settings. The wire feeder drive rolls must match wire diameterâuse a groove for the wire size. Tension should be just enough to feed wire without slipping; too tight crushes the wire and causes feeding problems.

The gun liner also must match wire diameter. A worn liner causes erratic feeding. Replace if you see wire shavings or if the wire snags. The contact tip should be replaced when the hole wears ovalâusually after 10-15 kg of wire. Keep the tip clean; spatter can block it. Use anti-spatter spray on the nozzle and tip.

When loading a new spool, cut the wire end cleanly, feed it through the liner, and secure the spool so it doesn't unravel. Do not overtighten the spool hub; it should turn with slight resistance. If the wire birds-nests at the drive rolls, release tension, cut the tangled wire, and re-feed. Store wire in a dry place to prevent rust. In humid African climates, keep spools in a sealed container with silica gel.

4. Setting Voltage and Wire Speed

Voltage and wire feed speed (WFS) are the two main controls in GMAW. Voltage controls the arc length and weld bead shape. Wire speed controls the amount of wire fed into the weld, which affects penetration and deposition rate. They must be balanced. If voltage is too low, the wire stubs into the work and spatters; if too high, the arc becomes unstable and the wire may ball up. If wire speed is too low, the arc wanders; if too high, the wire pushes into the work and causes a cold lap.

Start with the machine's recommended settings for your material thickness and wire diameter. For example, for 3mm mild steel with 0.8mm wire, set voltage at 18V and WFS at 4.5 m/min. For 6mm steel with 1.0mm wire, set 22V and 6.5 m/min. These are starting points. Fine-tune by listening to the arc: a steady crackling sound like frying bacon means good settings. A popping or hissing sound means voltage too high or wire speed too low. A stubbing sound means voltage too low or wire speed too high.

To adjust: first set voltage, then adjust wire speed until the arc sounds right. Make test welds on scrap. Look at the bead: it should be smooth, with distinct ripples, and penetrate into the base metal. If the bead is too convex (tall and narrow), increase voltage or reduce wire speed. If too flat and wide, reduce voltage or increase wire speed. For out-of-position welding (vertical or overhead), use slightly lower voltage and wire speed to control the pool.

On many African machines, the voltage is set by a coarse switch (e.g., 1-10) and wire speed by a dial. Note the numbers for future jobs. Keep a notebook. If you change wire diameter or gas mix, re-adjust. Always practice on scrap before welding a critical joint. Remember, machine settings vary with input power; if voltage drops, your settings may need tweaking. Use a voltage stabiliser if possible.

5. Basic GMAW Beads and Joints

Now that you have set up your machine, it's time to run beads. A bead is a single pass of weld metal. Start with a flat plate. Clean it with a grinder to bare metal. Set your machine for 3mm steel: 0.8mm wire, 18V, 4.5 m/min WFS, gas at 12 LPM. Hold the gun at a 10-15 degree angle, pushing away from the weld (forehand) or pulling (backhand). For beginners, push angle gives better visibility and gas coverage. Keep the contact tip to work distance (CTWD) at 10-15mm. Too close, the tip overheats; too far, you lose gas shielding.

Trigger the gun and move steadily along the plate. Watch the molten pool. You want a pool about 6-8mm wide. Travel speed should be steadyâtoo fast gives a thin, narrow bead with poor penetration; too slow gives a wide, convex bead with excessive heat. For a 3mm plate, a travel speed of about 20-30 cm/min works. Practice straight beads until you can produce a consistent bead with even ripples. Then try weaving: a slight side-to-side motion to widen the bead. This is useful for filling gaps.

Next, learn joints. The butt joint: two plates edge to edge with a small gap (1-2mm). Tack weld at both ends, then weld from one end to the other. For a square butt on 3mm steel, you can do a single pass. For T-joints: one plate vertical on another. Weld the corner, aiming the wire at the root where the plates meet. Use a 45-degree gun angle. For lap joints: one plate overlapping another. Weld along the edge of the top plate. For corner joints: two plates at 90 degrees, weld the outside corner.

In Africa, common projects include gates, window frames, and vehicle repairs. Practice on scrap of similar thickness. Always tack weld first to prevent distortion. Use clamps or magnets to hold parts. After welding, chip off slag (if any) and inspect. A good bead should be uniform, with no undercut or holes. Clean your welds with a wire brush. Remember, GMAW does not produce slag like stick welding, but spatter can be removed with a chipping hammer or grinder.

6. GMAW Troubleshooting and Defects

Even with good setup, problems happen. Learn to recognise defects and fix them. Porosity: small holes in the weld, caused by gas coverage issues. Check for low gas flow, empty cylinder, leaks, or a clogged nozzle. Also, dirty base metal (oil, rust, paint) can cause porosity. Clean with a grinder. If welding outdoors, increase gas flow and use a windbreak. Wire feed problems: if the wire stubs or birds-nests, check drive roll tension, worn liner, or incorrect tip size. Replace worn parts. If the wire slips, increase tension slightly; if it crushes, decrease.

Spatter: excessive spatter can be caused by voltage too high, wire speed too low, or wrong gas mix (pure CO2 gives more spatter than C25). Adjust settings. Use anti-spatter spray. Undercut: a groove melted into the base metal at the weld toe. Caused by voltage too high, travel speed too fast, or gun angle incorrect. Reduce voltage, slow down, or adjust angle. Lack of penetration: the weld does not fuse into the root. Caused by voltage too low, wire speed too high, travel too fast, or joint design too narrow. Increase voltage, reduce wire speed, or open the gap.

Burn-through: holes melted through thin metal. Caused by too much heat (high voltage or slow travel). Use lower voltage, faster travel, or thicker metal. For thin sheet, use 0.6mm wire and lower settings. Distortion: warping due to heat. Use tack welds, weld in short segments, or alternate sides. Clamp parts to a heavy table. Cracks: can occur from poor fit-up, high stress, or wrong wire. Use correct wire for the base metal and ensure good penetration.

In African workshops, power fluctuations can cause inconsistent welds. Use a voltage stabiliser. Keep your machine clean; blow out dust with compressed air. Check cables for damage. Always wear PPE. If a defect appears, cut out the weld and re-weld. Practice on scrap to diagnose. Keep a log of settings that work for different jobs.

Setting Voltage and Wire Speed

On a constant-voltage (CV) GMAW machine, voltage controls arc length and wire feed speed (WFS) controls current. The two are linked: for a given wire diameter, increasing WFS without increasing voltage causes the wire to stub into the plate; increasing voltage without WFS causes a long, unstable arc with excess spatter.

Setting procedure for a new job:

  1. Set voltage from the table for your plate thickness.
  2. Set WFS to roughly the mid-range value.
  3. Run a test bead on scrap of the same thickness.
  4. Listen: a steady "frying bacon" sound means you are close. A harsh crackle means too little voltage; a hiss with spatter means too much.
  5. Adjust WFS up in small increments until the arc is stable, then fine-tune voltage Â±1 V.

Write your successful settings on a card and tape it to the machine. In a busy workshop with multiple operators, this single habit prevents hours of re-tuning and reduces wire and gas waste â both of which come straight out of your margin.
