Lessons

1. Table Saw Operations: Ripping, Crosscutting, and Dadoing

The table saw is the backbone of any furniture workshop. In Africa, you will often find a locally made table saw with a 10-inch blade and a 2 HP motor, costing around $300 to $500. Before any cut, check the blade height: it should be just 3-5 mm above the wood. For ripping, set the fence to the desired width, like 50 mm, and feed the wood with a push stick. Keep the wood tight against the fence and use a riving knife to prevent kickback. For crosscutting, use a crosscut sled or miter gauge. Set the miter gauge to 90 degrees for square cuts. For angled cuts, adjust to 45 degrees. Always support long pieces with an outfeed table. Dadoing creates grooves for shelves or joints. Use a stacked dado set, adjusting the width by adding or removing chippers. For a 19 mm dado, use two outer blades and three chippers. Make multiple passes for wider dados. Ensure the dado blade is sharp; a dull blade burns the wood. In many African workshops, dust collection is basic, so wear a mask. Also, check the saw's alignment: the blade should be parallel to the miter slot. A misaligned saw causes binding and kickback. Use a featherboard to keep the wood against the fence. For safety, never reach over the blade. Turn off the saw and wait for the blade to stop before removing offcuts. Regular maintenance includes waxing the table to reduce friction and checking belt tension. With practice, you can rip, crosscut, and dado accurately, which are essential for building beds, tables, and cabinets.

2. Band Saw and Scroll Saw Techniques for Curved Cuts

Curved cuts are common in furniture making, from chair backs to decorative legs. A band saw with a 1/2-inch blade can cut curves with a radius of about 50 mm. For tighter curves, use a 1/4-inch blade. In Africa, many workshops use a 14-inch band saw, which costs around $400. Adjust the blade guides to within 1 mm of the blade and set the upper guide to just above the wood. For cutting, mark your curve with a pencil or chalk. Feed the wood slowly, letting the blade do the work. Too fast and the blade will twist or break. For sharp corners, make relief cuts first. A scroll saw is better for intricate curves, like marquetry. It uses a thin blade that moves up and down. A basic scroll saw costs about $150. Use it for cuts with a radius as small as 5 mm. When cutting, keep the wood flat on the table and apply gentle pressure. For both saws, wear safety glasses. In many African settings, power supply can be unstable, so consider a foot pedal for the scroll saw. After cutting, smooth the curve with a spokeshave or rasp. For tight inside curves, use a file. Remember, band saw blades can drift, so check the tension and tracking. Also, use a fence for straight cuts on the band saw. For resawing, use a tall fence and a 3/4-inch blade. Practice on scrap wood to get a feel for the machine. Curved cuts add beauty and ergonomics to furniture, so master these techniques.

3. Jointer and Planer: Squaring and Dimensioning Lumber

Rough lumber from the sawmill is never flat or square. The jointer and planer turn it into accurate boards. A jointer has a flat bed and a rotating cutterhead. In Africa, a 6-inch jointer costs about $500. First, flatten one face: set the depth of cut to 1 mm and feed the board with the grain. Use push blocks to keep your hands safe. After several passes, the face is flat. Then, square one edge: hold the flat face against the fence and run the edge over the cutterhead. Now you have a flat face and a square edge. Next, use a thickness planer to make the opposite face parallel. A 13-inch planer costs around $400. Set the depth to 1 mm and feed the board flat face down. The planer will make the top face parallel to the bottom. Repeat until you reach the desired thickness, like 20 mm. Finally, rip the remaining edge on the table saw to width. This process is called dimensioning. In many African workshops, you might not have a jointer, so you can use a hand plane and a straight edge. But a jointer saves time. Always check for nails and grit before planing; they dull blades. Sharpen or replace blades regularly. For safety, never stand directly behind the board when planing; kickback can happen. Use a dust mask because wood dust is harmful. With properly dimensioned lumber, your joints will fit tightly, and your furniture will be strong. Practice on cheap wood like pine before working with hardwoods like mahogany or iroko.

4. Router and Shaper: Edge Profiling and Decorative Cuts

Routers and shapers add shape and decoration to edges. A handheld router with a 1/2-inch collet costs about $150. A router table is essential for safe and accurate work. For edge profiling, like a roundover, install a roundover bit. Set the depth so the bearing rides on the wood. For a 6 mm roundover, set the bit height accordingly. Feed the wood from right to left. For decorative cuts like ogee or Roman ogee, use the corresponding bit. In Africa, you can buy router bits locally or import them. Always make multiple passes for deep cuts; one pass can burn the wood or break the bit. For a shaper, which is a larger, stationary machine, use cutters with a 1-inch bore. A shaper costs around $800. It is more powerful and can handle raised panel doors. When using a shaper, use a power feeder for safety and consistency. For both tools, wear eye and ear protection. Dust collection is critical; routers produce a lot of chips. Use a shop vac or a dust collector. For decorative cuts on table legs, use a router with a template guide. Make a template from MDF and clamp it to the workpiece. For curved edges, use a flush trim bit. Remember, climb cutting (feeding in the same direction as the bit rotation) can be dangerous; only do it for a very light pass. After routing, sand the profile with sandpaper wrapped around a dowel. With practice, you can create beautiful edges that make your furniture stand out. Always unplug the router before changing bits.

5. Drill Press and Boring Operations

The drill press makes accurate holes at precise angles and depths. A floor-standing drill press with a 16 mm chuck costs about $250. For furniture, you need holes for dowels, screws, and hardware. First, mark the hole location with an awl. Use a brad-point bit for clean holes in wood. For a 10 mm dowel, use a 10 mm bit. Set the depth stop to avoid drilling through. For angled holes, tilt the table and use a clamp to hold the workpiece. For repetitive holes, use a fence and stop blocks. In Africa, many workshops use a hand drill, but a drill press is more accurate. For boring large holes, like for a lock, use a Forstner bit. It cuts flat-bottomed holes. Use a slow speed for large bits: 500 RPM for a 25 mm bit. For small bits, use 1500 RPM. Always clamp the wood to the table; never hold it by hand. Wear safety glasses. For deep holes, clear the chips by raising the bit often. For dowel joints, use a dowel jig for alignment. For pocket holes, use a pocket hole jig with a step bit. After drilling, countersink screw holes with a countersink bit. For hinges, use a hinge boring bit. Regular maintenance includes lubricating the quill and checking belt tension. In areas with unstable power, use a surge protector. With a drill press, you can drill straight and repeatable holes, which is key for assembly. Practice on scrap wood to get the speed and feed right.

6. CNC Machining Basics for Furniture Parts

CNC (Computer Numerical Control) machines automate cutting and shaping. In Africa, small CNC routers are becoming affordable, starting at around $3000. A basic CNC router has a bed size of 1.2 m by 2.4 m, enough for a sheet of plywood. You need to learn CAD (Computer-Aided Design) software like Fusion 360 or VCarve. Draw your part, then generate G-code. The machine follows the G-code to cut. For furniture, you can cut intricate shapes, drill holes, and carve designs. Start with simple projects like a stool seat. Clamp the workpiece to the spoilboard. Set the X, Y, and Z zero points. Use a 6 mm end mill for cutting. For cutting plywood, use a downcut bit to avoid tear-out. Set the feed rate and spindle speed according to the material. For MDF, use 2000 mm/min feed and 18000 RPM. For hardwood, reduce feed to 1000 mm/min. Always wear safety glasses and hearing protection. Dust collection is essential; CNC routers produce fine dust. In many African workshops, you can build a simple dust shoe. After cutting, sand the edges. CNC is not a replacement for hand skills; you still need to assemble and finish. But it saves time and increases accuracy. For production runs, CNC is ideal. Learn from online tutorials and practice on scrap. Also, consider the cost of electricity and maintenance. With CNC, you can offer custom designs to customers, which can grow your business.

<h2>Practical Reference: Machining and Shaping Operations for African Furniture Workshops</h2>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the essential machine settings, feeds, speeds, and safety practices for the six core machining operations in furniture manufacturing: table saw work (ripping, crosscutting, dadoing), band and scroll sawing, jointing and planing, routing and shaping, drill press boring, and CNC machining. It is written for workshop owners, production supervisors, and technician-trainers operating in African contexts where power supply can be unstable, imported tooling is expensive, and the margin between profit and loss is thin. Every setting here is chosen to reduce waste, extend tool life, and protect the people on the shop floor.</p>

<p>Use this module alongside the six lesson modules. Treat the tables as starting points, then calibrate to your own timber species, moisture content, and machine condition. In African workshops, the most common causes of poor finish and machine damage are dull tooling, incorrect feed rate, and timber that has not been properly dried. Fix those three variables first before blaming the machine.</p>

<h2>3. Business-First Notes for African Workshops</h2>

<p><strong>Power stability.</strong> Voltage fluctuations damage spindle bearings and CNC controllers. A 5 kVA automatic voltage stabiliser on the CNC and a 3 kVA unit on the shaper pay for themselves within a year. Where grid supply is intermittent, size your generator to at least 1.5 the total motor rating to handle start-up surge.</p>

<p><strong>Dust and chips.</strong> Fine dust from MDF and hardwood (especially iroko, mahogany, and teak) is a health hazard and a fire risk. A 2 HP extractor with 100 mm ducting to each machine is the minimum. Chips can be sold to poultry farms or briquette makers a small but real income stream.</p>

<p><strong>Timber species.</strong> African hardwoods such as iroko, mahogany, and padauk blunt tooling faster than European softwoods because of mineral inclusions and density. Budget 2030% more tooling cost when quoting hardwood jobs, and reduce feed rates accordingly.</p>

<p><strong>Training and apprentices.</strong> Most machine accidents happen in the first six months of operating a new machine. Pair every apprentice with a trained operator for at least 40 hours of supervised running before allowing independent work.</p>

<h3 style="margin-top:0;color:#b02020;"> SAFETY WARNING Table Saw Kickback and Blade Contact</h3>
<p style="margin-bottom:8px;">Kickback is the leading cause of serious injury on the table saw. It happens when the workpiece is lifted by the rear of the blade and thrown back at the operator at high speed. To prevent it:</p>
<ul style="margin:0 0 0 18px;padding:0;">
<li>Always fit and correctly align the riving knife it must sit within 3 mm of the blade and no higher than the blade top.</li>
<li>Never rip timber that is twisted, bowed, or has a loose knot in the path of the blade.</li>
<li>Stand to the left of the blade line, never directly behind the workpiece.</li>
<li>Use push sticks and a push block for any cut where the hand would pass within 150 mm of the blade.</li>
<li>Never reach over or behind a running blade to retrieve offcuts.</li>
<li>Isolate the machine and wait for the blade to stop before clearing jammed material.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2>4. Quick Reference Checklist</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Check timber moisture</strong> with a pin meter before machining reject anything above 15% MC for fine furniture.</li>
<li><strong>Inspect and tension tooling</strong> sharp blade, correct TPI, riving knife aligned, guards in place.</li>
<li><strong>Set blade or cutter height</strong> to the minimum that clears the workpiece (one tooth for table saw, 1 tool diameter for CNC).</li>
<li><strong>Confirm feed direction</strong> against cutter rotation never climb-cut on a manual router or shaper without a power feeder.</li>
<li><strong>Test on scrap</strong> of the same species and thickness before running production parts.</li>
<li><strong>Wear PPE</strong>: safety glasses, hearing protection above 85 dB, and a dust mask rated P2 or better.</li>
<li><strong>Record settings</strong> in a job card so the next operator can repeat the setup without trial cuts.</li>
<li><strong>Shut down and clean</strong> at end of shift chips and dust left overnight harden and shorten tool life.</li>
</ol>
