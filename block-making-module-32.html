Cement Sourcing and Bulk Buying

8 lessons · 30 quiz questions · Business

1 Cement brands and grades

Cement is 30–40% of your material cost. Getting the right grade at the right price matters.

Grades:

  • CEM I 52.5N: Pure Portland, highest strength. Most expensive. Overkill for blocks.
  • CEM II 42.5N: Standard for most block work. Best value. Most common.
  • CEM II 32.5N: Lower strength. Fine for non-load-bearing blocks and bricks.
  • CEM III / IV / V: Blends with slag or pozzolans. Varies by region.

For standard blocks: CEM II 42.5N is the sweet spot. CEM I only if your customer specifies high strength. Never use lower than 32.5N for load-bearing work.

💡 Match the grade to the product. Don't pay for 52.5N if 42.5N does the job.

2 Buying by the pallet vs bag

Three purchase options, each with different economics:

Bagged (50 kg bags):

  • Highest price per tonne (30–50% more than bulk)
  • Easy to handle, no equipment needed
  • Fine for <500 blocks/day
  • Cost: £6–£9 per 50 kg bag

Pallet (e.g. 40 × 50 kg = 2 tonnes):

  • 5–15% cheaper than single bags
  • Delivered by truck with tail-lift
  • Needs pallet truck and covered store
  • Cost: £220–£340 per 2-tonne pallet

Bulk tanker (10–30 tonnes):

  • Cheapest per tonne (20–40% less than bags)
  • Requires silo + pneumatic pump
  • Only viable above 500 blocks/day
  • Cost: $110–$180 per tonne

Move from bags to bulk when you use over 5 tonnes/day consistently.

3 Negotiating with suppliers

Every £1 per tonne off your cement cost saves thousands per year.

Negotiation tactics:

  • Volume commitment: Sign a 6–12 month agreement for a fixed price or discount.
  • Multiple quotes: Get 3 quotes and share them with each supplier.
  • Payment terms: 30-day terms are worth 3–5% discount equivalent (cost of capital).
  • Annual discount: Ask for a 5–10% end-of-year volume rebate.
  • Direct from manufacturer: Buying direct from cement plants saves the middleman margin.

Negotiate once a year — not every order. Suppliers respect serious buyers, not chisellers.

4 Storage and moisture control

Cement is hygroscopic — it absorbs moisture from air. Once wet, it's ruined.

Storage rules:

  • Dry, covered shed — no leaks, no humidity
  • Off the floor — pallets 100 mm above ground
  • Not against external walls — condensation forms on cold walls
  • Stacked no more than 10 bags high — higher compresses and sets the bottom bags
  • Use FIFO — first in, first out
  • Max storage time: 3 months in good conditions; 6 weeks in humid climates

Store capacity: for 5,000 blocks/day, you use about 12 tonnes/day. Stock 2 weeks = 170 tonnes = 30 m² of shelf space, 3 m high.

5 Checking for stale cement

Cement loses 10–20% strength after 6 weeks in humid conditions. Check before using old stock.

Signs of stale cement:

  • Lumps: Any hard lumps mean moisture has reacted with the cement
  • Colour change: Fresh grey vs dull grey-blue
  • Feel: Fresh cement is cool and powdery; stale is warm and gritty
  • Smell: Fresh cement has almost no smell; stale has a slight musty note

Test: Mix a small batch (1 part cement, 3 sand). Cure 7 days. Drop test. If it crumbles, the cement is weak — discount or return it.

⚠ Never use cement with hard lumps for structural blocks. Fine for non-critical fill or drainage.

6 Price tracking

Cement prices rise — sometimes 20–50% in a year. Track them.

What to track:

  • Price per 50 kg bag (or per tonne) weekly
  • Supplier by supplier — different prices
  • Any government levies or taxes
  • Seasonal patterns (prices often rise before rainy season when building peaks)

Tools: Simple spreadsheet. Update every order. Review monthly.

Actions from tracking:

  • If price rises 10%+ — adjust your block price accordingly
  • If price drops — buy extra stock (up to 3 months' worth)
  • If one supplier lags behind — switch or renegotiate

Never let a price rise surprise you. Track weekly.

7 Building a credit relationship

Credit from your cement supplier is worth more than a discount — it lets you build production before payment.

How to earn credit:

  1. Pay cash for the first 3 months — prove reliability
  2. Ask for 30-day terms after 6 months of good history
  3. Stick to terms strictly — never miss a payment
  4. After 12 months, ask for 60-day terms (some suppliers offer)

What credit is worth: If you buy £20,000 of cement per month and get 30-day terms, you free up £20,000 of working capital. That's £20,000 available for pallets, wages, or marketing.

Never abuse credit — a supplier who cuts you off is hard to replace.

8 When prices rise

Cement price rises are inevitable. Plan for them.

Immediate responses:

  • Adjust your block selling price by the same % (or more)
  • Notify customers 30 days in advance of a price change
  • Quote large jobs with a "price valid for 30 days" clause

Long-term protection:

  • Fix a supply agreement with a cement producer for 6–12 months
  • Buy bulk when prices dip (up to 3 months stock)
  • Improve mix efficiency — better vibration uses less cement per block
  • Add pozzolans (fly ash, slag) to reduce cement content

Raising prices is uncomfortable but necessary. Never absorb a rise indefinitely — you'll go bankrupt while looking "loyal".

💰 Cement is your biggest variable cost. Treat sourcing like a strategic function, not a routine purchase. 5% savings on cement is often 20% on net profit.
