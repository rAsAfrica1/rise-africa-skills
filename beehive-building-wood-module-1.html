Beehive Building — Wood / Module 1 of 12

Module 1

The Hive as a Machine for Bees

Before you cut a single board, understand what a hive actually is, what it must do for the colony, and why the bee space above all else decides whether your hive succeeds or fails.

Read time: 25 minutesThen: Module 2 — Timber Selection
By the end of this module you will be able to
  • Explain what a hive does for a bee colony, physically
  • State the three bee space measurements and what each one applies to
  • Distinguish the three main hive types — Langstroth, top-bar and traditional — by what they require from the beekeeper
  • Decide which hive type fits your climate, bee race and management style
  • Recognise what a hive does not need to do, so you do not waste money on features
  • Produce a hive type decision for your own apiary before buying timber

Lesson 1.1

What a hive actually does

A wild colony of honeybees lives in a hollow tree, a rock cavity, a wall, or under a roof. The cavity is the hive. Everything else — comb, brood, stores, workers, queen — is what the bees build inside it. Our job, as beekeepers, is not to invent a home for them. It is to imitate the cavity they would choose, with three additions: access for the beekeeper, removable structure so we can inspect without destroying the colony, and dimensional consistency so parts fit together across every hive in the apiary.

Everything a hive must do, physically:

  1. Provide a dry, dark cavity. Bees prefer darkness and a stable cavity. A hive that leaks rain will fail.
  2. Maintain a stable internal climate. The colony holds its brood nest at 34–35°C regardless of outside temperature. The hive must make that possible without forcing the bees to burn excessive honey.
  3. Allow ventilation without draught. Bees need airflow to remove moisture and CO₂, but they cannot tolerate a through-draught across the brood nest.
  4. Be defensible. A small entrance is easier to defend against robbers, wasps and other insects than a wide one.
  5. Allow the beekeeper to open, inspect and harvest without destroying the colony. This is the only part of a hive that is "modern". It is what separates a box hive from a log hive.
  6. Hold together under its own weight. A full Langstroth hive with honey can exceed 40 kg. It has to survive being lifted.

Notice what is not on the list: it does not have to be beautiful. It does not have to be painted any particular colour. It does not have to be made of any particular timber. Those are choices, not requirements.

The rule: a hive is a cavity with a removable frame. Everything else is a variation on that theme.

Lesson 1.2

Bee space — the one measurement that decides everything

In the 1850s an American clergyman, Lorenzo Langstroth, noticed that honeybees leave a consistent gap between adjacent combs and between a comb and the wall of their cavity. Too small, and they fill it with propolis (bee glue). Too large, and they build another comb in the gap. But between about 6 and 9 mm, they leave it empty. That gap is called bee space, and it is the physical basis of every modern hive in the world.

Its significance cannot be overstated. Bee space is what makes a hive removable. If you build to bee space, the frames lift out cleanly. If you build narrower, the frames are glued in with propolis and you must lever them out, killing bees and damaging comb. If you build wider, the bees build burr comb in the gap and you have created a mess that has to be scraped out at every inspection.

The three bee space measurements

MeasurementDistanceWhere it applies
Between combs (comb centre to comb centre)35 mmFrame spacing in a Langstroth hive, or top-bar spacing in a top-bar hive
Between comb face and any parallel surface6–9 mmGap between the frame side bars and the box wall; gap between the top of frames and the inner cover
Between the top of the frame and the underside of the lid6–9 mmThis is the gap the bees leave empty. Make it deeper than 10 mm and they build burr comb in it.

The units matter — and they are not metric by accident

Bee space was measured in the imperial system. Standard Langstroth hives use 3/8 inch (9.5 mm) between the frame and the box wall, and frame spacing of 35 mm (about 1 3/8 inch). If you are cutting timber in metric and trying to follow a metric pattern, you will be within a fraction of a millimetre of the imperial standard for most dimensions — fine. But if you invent dimensions freely, you will drift out of the bee space range and the hive will not work properly.

The rule: when in doubt, build to 35 mm frame spacing and 8 mm clearance. These two numbers are the entire foundation of a working modern hive.

What happens if you get it wrong

  • Too narrow (under 5 mm): bees fill the gap with propolis. Frames stick. Every inspection damages comb.
  • Just right (6–9 mm): bees leave the gap empty. Frames lift out cleanly. Inspections are quick and calm.
  • Too wide (over 11 mm): bees build a bridge of burr comb in the gap. Frames stick on the burr. You must scrape it out at every inspection, and it wastes bee effort.

An apiary built entirely to bee space runs smoothly. An apiary built with careless dimensions runs in a state of permanent argument with the bees. There is no middle ground.

Lesson 1.3

The three hive types, honestly compared

Every wooden hive in use today falls into one of three families. Each is a legitimate choice for a small African beekeeper, and each requires something different from the person running it.

Type 1 — Langstroth (frame hive)

Stackable rectangular boxes, each holding 8–10 removable frames. The modern industry standard. Invented in 1852 and essentially unchanged since.

StrengthsWeaknesses
Interchangeable parts — one hive's supers fit another'sRequires precise woodworking — dimensions have to be within a millimetre or two
Frames lift out for inspection, harvest and disease treatmentMore wood and more cost per hive than a top-bar
Honey is harvested by spinning frames, leaving comb intact for reuseRequires either a honey extractor or to harvest comb honey
Compatible with the international bee supply industryRequires more storage space and careful handling of boxes
Can be expanded upward by adding supers as the colony growsHeavy when full — a full super can exceed 25 kg

Best for: beekeepers who will build to precise dimensions, who want to scale, and who have access to a honey extractor or plan to sell comb honey in standard frames.

Type 2 — Top-bar hive (Kenyan and Tanzanian)

A single horizontal box, without frames. A row of top bars spans the top; the bees build natural comb down from each bar. You lift one bar at a time to inspect.

StrengthsWeaknesses
Simpler to build — no frames, no supers, fewer jointsComb is less supported and can break if handled hot
Cheaper — roughly half the material and labour of a LangstrothHoney harvested by crushing comb, which is destroyed
Very good for African bee races that build fast and swarm oftenNot standardised — one maker's bars do not fit another maker's hive
Easier to manage the colony for swarm control by adding barsHarder to move — awkward to transport when full
Better airflow in hot climates — long body helpsRequires a very specific bar width to maintain bee space

Best for: smallholders in hot, humid areas, beekeepers starting with limited capital, and those willing to accept comb honey and crushed-comb extraction.

Type 3 — Traditional (log, bark, pot, wall)

A hollowed log, a bark cylinder, a clay pot, or an existing cavity in a wall. The colony builds free comb. You harvest by cutting comb out, often killing the colony or driving it off.

StrengthsWeaknesses
Cheapest to make — uses local materialCannot inspect without destroying comb
Bees often accept it readily — it looks like a natural cavityCannot treat for disease without destroying the colony
Long tradition of use in many parts of AfricaLow yield per hive compared with a managed modern hive
Requires no woodworking skillDestructive harvest means losing the colony and starting over

Best for: not recommended for a commercial apiary. Traditional hives are useful for attracting wild swarms onto a site, and the swarm can then be transferred to a modern hive. They are not a business hive.

The honest recommendation for a small African beekeeper

For a start, build one or two Langstroth hives and one or two Kenyan top-bar hives. Learn from both. The Langstroth teaches you precise woodwork and how to manage frames. The top-bar teaches you how a colony builds naturally and how to manage colony growth. After one season, you will know which fits your climate, your bees and your management style. Then standardise and build twenty of that type.

Lesson 1.4

Choosing a hive for your bees

Bee races differ. So do climates. So do management styles. The right hive is the one that fits all three.

Bee race and hive type

Bee typeBehaviour notesSuitable hive
Apis mellifera scutellata (African honeybee)Fast build-up, strong swarming instinct, defensive, productiveTop-bar (easy to manage swarm impulse) or Langstroth (managed with swarm control)
Apis mellifera adansonii (West African)Similar to scutellata, smaller, fast build-upTop-bar in humid zones, Langstroth for managed honey production
Apis mellifera monticola (East African highland)Larger, calmer, better in cooler temperaturesLangstroth — reaches good size before swarming
Apis mellifera capensis (Cape)Quiet, tolerant, capable of laying workersLangstroth — easier to manage the laying-worker problem

Climate and hive type

  • Hot, humid, high rainfall. Ventilation matters. Top-bar hives have better natural airflow. Whatever you build, do not seal the hive airtight — bees will die of humidity stress.
  • Hot, dry, low rainfall. Shade matters. Build a stand with a roof over the hive, and orient so the entrance faces away from the afternoon sun.
  • Cool, high-altitude. Thermal mass matters. Langstroth hives are easier to insulate, and hives should face morning sun to warm them.
  • Coastal. Corrosion of any metal parts matters. Build entirely in wood and bamboo, or use stainless fixings. Avoid bare steel in any hive.

Management style and hive type

If you willChoose
Harvest comb honey and sell it in bulkTop-bar — comb harvest is what top-bar does well
Harvest extracted honey in quantityLangstroth — this is the standard for extracted honey
Move hives to track nectar flowsLangstroth — designed to be moved; top-bar hives are awkward to transport
Keep a few hives quietly in one placeEither; top-bar is more forgiving for a beginner
Sell hives and grow a hive-making businessLangstroth — high demand and standardised
Have minimal woodworking skillTop-bar — fewer, simpler cuts

Lesson 1.5

What a hive does NOT need

Suppliers and self-taught beekeepers add features that sound good and often make the hive worse. The following are not necessary and are sometimes harmful:

  • A painted interior. Bees propolise all internal surfaces anyway. Painting the inside is wasted effort and can introduce fumes into the colony.
  • A metal-screened floor. A screen bottom is not needed for a working African hive and it lets in robbers, ants and dampness. A wooden floor with a small entrance is standard.
  • Complicated inner covers. A simple wooden or cloth inner cover is enough. A plastic sheet with dozens of holes is more trouble than it is worth.
  • Queen excluders as a permanent fixture. Useful when you want honey frames kept clear of brood, but leaving one on year-round slows the colony and can fail the queen. Use only when needed.
  • Feeding trays inside the brood box. Bulk feeders outside the hive are safer — they do not disturb the colony and they do not encourage robbing.
  • Over-thick or over-thin walls for insulation. Bees manage their own climate. A well-ventilated 20 mm wall is fine in most of Africa. Special insulation is only worth adding in very cold highland areas or for a freezer-cold room.
The plain hive rule: simple, well-built, correct bee space, tight against rain, ventilated enough to breathe, small enough to defend. Everything beyond that is decoration.

Lesson 1.6

Deciding your hive type

Before buying a single board, write down your decision in one page:

  1. Bee race. What is dominant in your area — scutellata, adansonii, monticola or capensis? Ask a local beekeeper or the extension officer.
  2. Climate. Hot/humid, hot/dry, cool/highland, or coastal?
  3. Management style. Quiet hobby, small commercial honey, hive-making business, or swarm capture?
  4. Hive type. Langstroth, top-bar, or both? State your reason.
  5. Number of hives for year 1. Start with at least two (one colony alone is fragile; two let you compare and equalise).
  6. Timber availability. What will you actually cut this hive from? Local species, imported pine, or reclaimed timber?
  7. Tools available. A hand saw and a chisel can build a top-bar. A Langstroth needs a table saw or careful measuring and cutting with a circular saw.
  8. One risk. The biggest risk in your first year — swarming, robbing, ants, theft, drought dearth — and how you will reduce it.
The one-page rule: two hours of planning on one page is worth more than the first two boards you cut. Get the type and the timber right, and the rest of the hive is straightforward carpentry.

Knowledge check — Module 1

1. What is bee space, and what is its typical width?

Correct: 6–9 mm. Too narrow and bees propolise it shut; too wide and they build burr comb. In that range they leave it empty.

2. What is the standard distance between comb centres in a Langstroth hive?

Correct: 35 mm. Frame-to-frame centre distance. Spacing wider or narrower breaks bee space and makes management difficult.

3. Which hive type is easiest to transport between nectar flows?

Correct: Langstroth. Stackable boxes with rectangular outlines are designed to be moved. Top-bar hives are awkward to transport when full.

4. Why is a top-bar hive often recommended for African bee races?

Correct: easier swarm management and cheaper. Top-bar hives let you add bars as the colony grows and cost roughly half as much as a Langstroth.

5. Which of the following is NOT necessary in a working African hive?

Correct: painted interior. Bees propolise the interior anyway. It wastes effort and can introduce fumes. Everything else on the list is genuinely needed.

Practical assignment — Module 1

Produce a one-page hive type decision for your own apiary. This is a paper exercise. No timber is cut.

Submit:

  1. Your bee race — name it, and say how you established it (local beekeeper, extension officer, or observed on your site).
  2. Your climate classification — hot/humid, hot/dry, cool/highland, or coastal. One sentence justifying the choice.
  3. Your management intent — hobby, small commercial honey, hive-making business, or swarm capture. One sentence.
  4. Your hive type choice — Langstroth, top-bar, or both, with a reason of 3–5 sentences.
  5. Number of hives for year 1 — and where they will sit on the site.
  6. Timber source — species, quantity, and price from at least one supplier or from your own assessment of local availability.
  7. Tools — list the tools you have or will buy to build the hive type you chose. Be honest if you need a table saw for a Langstroth.
  8. One paragraph (150 words) naming the single biggest risk in your first year and how you will reduce it.

Marks are weighted toward points 4 and 8. The hive type decision has to be defensible, and the risk paragraph has to name something specific to your area.
