1. Understanding Egg Quality: What Buyers See and Pay For

Egg quality is not a vague idea. It is a set of measurable traits that determine whether a trader in Kampala, a hotel chef in Nairobi, a supermarket buyer in Accra or a roadside consumer in Lagos will pay premium, average or reject prices. For a layer farm in Africa, quality is the difference between selling 30 trays a day at a good margin and struggling to move stock at a discount. This lesson breaks quality into external and internal factors, explains how they are judged in local markets, and shows you how to build a quality-first routine on your farm. External quality includes shell colour, shell cleanliness, shell strength, egg size, shape and uniformity. Internal quality includes albumen thickness, yolk colour, yolk strength, absence of blood or meat spots, and absence of off-odours or off-flavours. In most African markets, the first judgement is visual. A trader opens a tray and looks at the eggs. Cracked shells, dirty shells, misshapen eggs and mixed sizes reduce the price immediately. In some markets, brown eggs attract a premium, while in others white eggs are preferred. The key is to know your specific market and produce consistently for it. Do not assume that what sells in one city sells in another. Visit your buyers, ask what they reject and why, and record their answers. Shell colour is genetic. If your buyers want brown eggs, you must stock brown-egg breeds. No amount of management will turn a white-egg breed into a brown-egg producer. Shell strength is influenced by genetics, age of the bird, nutrition especially calcium and phosphorus, heat stress, disease and handling. Weak shells break during collection, grading, transport and retail display. Every broken egg is a direct loss. In hot African conditions, heat stress is a major cause of thin shells. Provide shade, ventilation, cool drinking water and consider electrolytes during heat waves. Shell cleanliness depends on nest management, litter condition, bird health and collection frequency. Dirty eggs come from dirty nests, wet litter, diarrhoea, prolapse or eggs laid on the floor. Never wash dirty eggs with water unless you have a controlled washing and sanitising system, because washing pushes bacteria through the shell pores and shortens shelf life. Instead, use dry cleaning methods such as fine sandpaper or a dry cloth for light marks, and fix the source of dirt. Egg size is driven by breed, age and nutrition. Pullets start with small eggs and gradually increase size. Buyers often want uniform tray sizes. Mixing small and large eggs in one tray looks unprofessional and invites price cuts. Sort by size and sell each grade to the right buyer. Small eggs can go to bakeries, restaurants or local consumers who buy by number rather than weight. Large eggs can go to hotels and supermarkets that pay by weight or premium grade. Egg shape should be normal oval. Misshapen eggs, ridged shells, thin spots and rough shells indicate disease, stress, poor nutrition or age. These eggs should be diverted to processing or sold at a discount, not mixed with premium stock. Internal quality is harder for buyers to see, but it determines repeat purchases. Albumen thickness is measured by the Haugh unit. Fresh eggs have thick, firm albumen that stands up when the egg is broken. Old eggs have thin, watery albumen that spreads. Yolk colour is influenced by feed pigments such as maize, lucerne, marigold or synthetic carotenoids. In many African markets, a deep orange yolk is associated with healthy, free-range or quality eggs, and buyers pay more. However, yolk colour must be consistent. Sudden changes confuse buyers. Yolk strength matters for frying and boiling. Weak yolks break easily and are rejected by hotels and restaurants. Blood and meat spots are small inclusions that occur naturally in some eggs. They are not harmful but are unacceptable in premium markets. Good lighting during candling helps detect them. Off-odours and off-flavours come from storage near chemicals, manure, smoke or strong-smelling goods, or from spoiled feed ingredients such as fishmeal or rancid oil. Store eggs away from these sources. To build a quality-first routine, start with daily collection. Collect eggs at least three times a day in hot weather, and more often if possible. Use clean, dry, sanitised trays or baskets. Never stack eggs more than six layers high in a basket, because the bottom eggs crack. Move eggs to a cool, shaded grading area immediately. Grade eggs by size, shell condition and cleanliness. Remove cracked, dirty, misshapen and thin-shelled eggs. Sell or use these separately. Train your workers to handle eggs with clean, dry hands and to never throw or drop eggs into trays. A single drop can crack an egg that looks fine but leaks later. Keep records of daily collection, grades, rejections and sales. This data shows you where losses occur and helps you negotiate better prices. Finally, talk to your buyers. Ask them what they consider premium, what they reject and what price they pay for each grade. Then produce to that standard. Quality is not an expense. It is a marketing strategy that earns loyalty and higher margins. In the next lesson, we will look at handling from collection to point of sale.

2. Handling Eggs from Nest to Buyer: Reducing Breakage and Contamination

Handling is where many African layer farms lose money without realising it. Eggs are fragile. A single crack, a dirty shell or a delay in cooling can turn a premium egg into a discounted one. This lesson covers practical handling from the moment an egg is laid to the moment it reaches the buyer. The goal is simple: minimise breakage, minimise contamination and maintain quality. Start at the nest. A good nest is clean, dry, well-bedded and private. Provide one nest box for every five hens. Use soft, clean bedding such as wood shavings, rice husks or chopped straw. Replace wet or dirty bedding immediately. Collect eggs at least three times a day, and in hot weather, collect four or five times. The longer an egg stays in a hot, dirty nest, the more likely it will be dirty, cracked or spoiled. Use clean, dry hands or clean gloves. Never use the same hands that have handled manure, dead birds or chemicals. Wash hands before collection and after any dirty task. Use clean, dry trays or baskets. Plastic trays are easy to wash and sanitise. If you use cardboard trays, keep them dry and do not reuse them if they are soiled. Never overfill a tray. Eggs should sit in the cups without pressure. When carrying eggs, support the bottom of the tray with one hand and steady the top with the other. Do not stack trays too high on a trolley or head. A fall can crack dozens of eggs. Move slowly over rough ground. In many African farms, the path from house to grading room is uneven. Fix the path or use a trolley with good wheels. At the grading area, work on a clean, dry table. Sort eggs by size and shell condition. Remove cracked, dirty, misshapen and thin-shelled eggs. Do not try to sell cracked eggs as fresh. They leak, attract flies and spoil other eggs. Sell them immediately for processing or use them on the farm, following food safety rules. Dirty eggs should be dry-cleaned if the dirt is light. Use fine sandpaper or a dry cloth. If an egg is heavily soiled, do not wash it with water unless you have a controlled washing system with clean water, sanitiser and immediate drying. Washing without proper drying opens the shell pores and allows bacteria to enter. In most small and medium African farms, dry cleaning is safer. After grading, pack eggs into clean trays or cartons. Use egg cartons that protect the eggs and allow air circulation. Do not pack eggs too tightly. Label the trays with the date of collection and the grade. This helps with stock rotation. First in, first out. Sell older eggs first. Store eggs in a cool, shaded, well-ventilated room. The ideal temperature is 10 to 15 degrees Celsius with relative humidity of 70 to 80 percent. In many African conditions, this means using evaporative cooling, fans, insulation or a cool room. Do not store eggs in direct sunlight or near the roof. Do not store eggs near strong-smelling goods such as kerosene, soap, fish, onions or chemicals. Egg shells are porous and absorb odours. Do not store eggs with manure or dead birds. Keep the storage area clean and free of rodents and insects. If you do not have a cool room, use the coolest part of the farm, such as a shaded, ventilated room with a damp floor or a simple evaporative cooler. In dry heat, a wet sack or a fan over a water source can lower temperature significantly. In humid heat, use fans and shade. Never refrigerate eggs if you cannot maintain a consistent cold chain. Fluctuating temperatures cause condensation on the shell, which encourages mould and bacteria. If you do refrigerate, keep the temperature constant and do not move eggs in and out of the fridge. For transport, use a vehicle with a covered, shaded cargo area. Do not transport eggs in the open sun. Use crates or trays that fit tightly to prevent movement. Drive slowly on rough roads. Avoid overloading. If you deliver to hotels, supermarkets or distant markets, invest in a small cool box or insulated container for high-value orders. For local sales, deliver early in the morning or late in the evening when temperatures are lower. Train your delivery staff to handle eggs gently and to never leave eggs in a hot vehicle. At the point of sale, display eggs in a shaded, clean area. Do not display eggs in direct sun. Do not stack trays too high. Turn stock regularly. Sell older eggs first. Keep a record of daily sales and returns. If buyers complain about cracks or spoilage, investigate your handling chain. Where are the weak points? Is it collection, grading, storage or transport? Fix the weakest link first. A simple handling audit can save you money. Walk through your farm and follow an egg from nest to buyer. Note every place where eggs are dropped, squeezed, exposed to heat or contaminated. Then make a list of low-cost improvements. For example, buy more trays, fix the path, add a shade net, train workers, or change collection times. In Africa, labour is often available but training is not. Invest in training. Show workers how to hold an egg, how to place it in a tray and how to stack trays. Use simple demonstrations. Praise good handling and correct bad habits immediately. Consider incentives for low breakage rates. A small bonus for workers who keep breakage below a target can pay for itself many times over. Finally, remember that handling is not just about eggs. It is about your reputation. A buyer who receives clean, unbroken eggs on time will order again. A buyer who receives cracked, dirty eggs will look for another supplier. In the next lesson, we will look at storage and shelf life in more detail.

3. Storage and Shelf Life: Keeping Eggs Fresh in African Conditions

Eggs are perishable. In hot, humid African conditions, they lose quality faster than in temperate climates. A fresh egg can last several weeks if stored correctly, but a poorly stored egg can spoil in a few days. This lesson explains how to store eggs to maximise shelf life, reduce losses and maintain quality. The first rule is temperature. The ideal storage temperature for table eggs is 10 to 15 degrees Celsius. Below 10 degrees, eggs can be stored longer, but if you cannot maintain a consistent cold chain, do not refrigerate. Fluctuating temperatures cause condensation, which damages the shell and allows bacteria to enter. In most African farms without refrigeration, aim for the coolest, most stable temperature you can achieve. This might be a shaded, well-ventilated room, a cellar, an evaporative cooler or a cool box. The second rule is humidity. Relative humidity of 70 to 80 percent is ideal. If humidity is too low, eggs lose moisture and the air cell grows. If humidity is too high, mould can grow on the shell. In dry African heat, you can increase humidity by placing shallow pans of water in the storage room or by using a wet sack or evaporative cooler. In humid coastal areas, use fans and ventilation to reduce mould risk. The third rule is air circulation. Eggs need gentle air movement to prevent heat build-up and odour absorption. Do not stack trays against walls or in tight corners. Leave space between stacks. Use slatted shelves or crates that allow air to move. Do not store eggs in sealed plastic bags or airtight containers. The fourth rule is cleanliness. Store eggs in a clean, dry area. Do not store them with manure, dead birds, chemicals, fuel, soap, fish, onions or strong-smelling goods. Egg shells are porous and absorb odours. Keep the storage area free of rodents, insects and dust. Clean and disinfect the area regularly. The fifth rule is rotation. Use first in, first out. Mark trays with the collection date. Sell older eggs first. Do not mix old and new eggs in the same stack. If you have a market that buys older eggs for baking or processing, separate them clearly. The sixth rule is handling. Handle stored eggs gently. Do not drop or shake trays. Do not turn eggs upside down. Keep the pointed end down if possible, because this helps keep the yolk centred and reduces the risk of the yolk touching the shell. The seventh rule is monitoring. Check your storage area daily. Use a thermometer and hygrometer if possible. Record temperature and humidity. Look for signs of spoilage: cracked shells, leaks, off-odours, mould or flies. Remove spoiled eggs immediately. Do not sell spoiled eggs. They are a health risk and will damage your reputation. How long can eggs last? Under ideal storage at 10 to 15 degrees Celsius, fresh, clean, unwashed eggs can last three to five weeks. In a cool, shaded room in African conditions, expect two to three weeks. In hot conditions above 25 degrees Celsius, eggs may last only seven to ten days. In a refrigerator at 4 degrees Celsius, eggs can last five weeks or more, but only if the cold chain is consistent. These are general guides. Actual shelf life depends on shell quality, cleanliness, handling and humidity. To extend shelf life without refrigeration, use these practical methods. First, collect eggs frequently and store them cool quickly. Second, do not wash eggs unless you have a controlled system. Washing removes the natural bloom, a protective coating on the shell. Third, use clean, dry bedding and nests to keep shells clean. Fourth, sort out cracked and dirty eggs immediately. Fifth, use evaporative cooling. A simple evaporative cooler can be built with a porous pot, wet sand, a wet sack or a fan over a water source. In dry heat, this can lower temperature by 5 to 10 degrees Celsius. Sixth, store eggs in the coolest part of the building, away from the roof and direct sun. Seventh, use shade nets, trees or insulation to reduce heat. Eighth, consider a small solar-powered fan or cooler for high-value markets. If you sell to supermarkets, hotels or exporters, you may need a cold room. A cold room is a major investment, but it can pay for itself if you sell premium eggs at higher prices. Before investing, calculate your expected volume, price premium and energy costs. Consider sharing a cold room with other farmers or renting space in a nearby cold store. For many small and medium farms, a well-managed cool room or evaporative cooler is enough. What about egg coatings? Some producers use mineral oil or food-grade coatings to seal shell pores and extend shelf life. This can work, but it must be done correctly with approved products. In many African markets, coated eggs may not be accepted because buyers prefer natural shells. Check with your buyers before using coatings. What about washing? In some countries, washing eggs is common in large packing stations. They use warm water, sanitiser and immediate drying. If you wash eggs, you must do it properly. Use water warmer than the egg, add approved sanitiser, rinse, dry immediately and refrigerate. If you cannot do this, do not wash. Dry clean instead. What about broken eggs? Never sell cracked eggs as fresh. Use them immediately for baking or cooking, following food safety rules. If you cannot use them, dispose of them properly. Do not feed spoiled eggs to livestock without proper processing. What about old eggs? Test freshness using a simple float test. A fresh egg sinks in water. An older egg floats because the air cell has grown. A floating egg is not necessarily spoiled, but it is old. Break it and smell it. If it smells bad, discard it. For business, do not rely on float tests for premium sales. Use candling and date rotation. Finally, train your staff. Storage is only as good as the people who manage it. Teach them to check temperature, rotate stock, keep the area clean and report problems. Keep simple records. A notebook with daily temperature, humidity, stock in, stock out and losses is enough. Review records weekly. If losses are high, find the cause. Is it heat? Dirty nests? Rough handling? Old stock? Fix the cause, not just the symptom. In the next lesson, we will look at grading, packaging and presentation for different markets.

4. Grading, Packaging and Presentation for African Markets

Grading and packaging turn a commodity into a product. In many African markets, eggs are sold in bulk trays with little grading. But buyers are becoming more demanding. Supermarkets, hotels, restaurants, bakeries and export markets want consistent size, clean shells, proper packaging and clear labelling. If you can meet those standards, you can earn higher prices and build long-term contracts. This lesson covers practical grading, packaging and presentation. Start with grading. Grading is sorting eggs by size, weight, shell quality and cleanliness. The simplest grade is by size: small, medium, large and extra large. In many countries, egg sizes are defined by weight. For example, small may be under 53 grams, medium 53 to 63 grams, large 63 to 73 grams and extra large above 73 grams. Check your local standards or buyer requirements. You can grade by hand using a simple scale or by using a plastic egg gauge. For larger volumes, consider a manual or electric egg grader. Grading by weight is more accurate than grading by eye. Buyers who pay by weight will reward accuracy. Grading by shell quality means removing cracked, dirty, misshapen, thin-shelled and rough-shelled eggs. These eggs should be sold separately or used for processing. Do not mix them with premium eggs. Grading by cleanliness means removing dirty eggs. If the dirt is light, dry clean. If heavy, sell for processing or use on the farm. Do not wash unless you have a controlled system. After grading, pack eggs into clean trays or cartons. For local markets, plastic trays or cardboard trays are common. For supermarkets and hotels, use branded cartons or labels. Packaging should protect eggs from breakage, allow air circulation and present the product well. Do not overfill trays. Do not use dirty or broken trays. Do not use trays that have held chemicals or strong-smelling goods. For premium markets, consider these packaging improvements. First, use clean, new cartons with your farm name, logo, contact details, grade, date of packing and best-before date. Second, use cartons that hold six, ten, twelve or thirty eggs, depending on the buyer. Third, use labels that are clear and professional. Fourth, use a consistent colour scheme. Fifth, include storage instructions such as keep cool and dry. Sixth, include a batch number for traceability. Seventh, consider a small leaflet or sticker with recipes or cooking tips. These small touches build trust and brand recognition. For hotels and restaurants, packaging may need to be in larger trays or crates. Ask the chef or purchasing officer what they prefer. Some want eggs in trays of thirty. Others want eggs in cartons of twelve for display. Some want eggs delivered in reusable plastic crates. Match their needs. For supermarkets, packaging must meet their standards. They may require barcodes, expiry dates, nutritional information and specific carton sizes. They may also require a minimum volume and regular delivery. Before approaching a supermarket, visit the store and study how eggs are displayed. Talk to the buyer. Ask about their requirements, payment terms and delivery schedule. Be prepared to provide samples. For export markets, requirements are stricter. You may need certification for food safety, animal health and packaging. You may need a cold chain. You may need to meet specific size, weight and labelling standards. Export is not for every farm. Start with local premium markets first. Presentation also matters at the point of sale. If you sell directly to consumers, display eggs in a clean, shaded area. Use a clean table or stall. Do not display eggs in direct sun. Do not stack trays too high. Use signs that show your grade, price and farm name. Keep the display clean and tidy. If you sell in a market, arrive early and set up a neat display. Customers judge quality by appearance. A clean, well-organised display attracts more buyers. If you sell to traders, deliver on time with the agreed grade and quantity. Do not mix grades. Do not deliver dirty or cracked eggs. Build a reputation for consistency. Consistency is more valuable than a low price. A trader who can rely on your eggs will pay a fair price and order regularly. To build consistency, keep records. Record daily collection, grading results, stock levels, sales and losses. Use simple notebooks or a phone app. Review records weekly. Identify your best-selling grades and your most profitable buyers. Focus on them. If you have excess small eggs, find a bakery or a local market that buys them. If you have excess large eggs, find a hotel or supermarket. Do not try to sell everything to one buyer. Diversify your market. Also, consider value addition. If you have cracked or dirty eggs, you can process them into liquid egg, boiled eggs, pickled eggs or egg powder, depending on your market and regulations. Value addition can turn a loss into a profit, but it requires food safety knowledge, equipment and packaging. Start small and learn. For many farms, the simplest value addition is selling boiled eggs to local vendors or making egg sandwiches for nearby schools or offices. Finally, train your staff in grading and packaging. Show them how to use a scale, how to identify defects, how to pack trays and how to label cartons. Praise good work and correct mistakes. Consider a quality bonus for low rejection rates. A small incentive can improve performance significantly. In the next lesson, we will look at quality control, record keeping and troubleshooting common problems.

5. Quality Control, Record Keeping and Troubleshooting

Quality control is the system that keeps your eggs at the standard your buyers expect. Without it, quality drifts. Today the eggs are clean and large. Tomorrow they are dirty and mixed. Buyers notice. They reduce orders or demand discounts. This lesson shows you how to set up a simple quality control system, keep useful records and troubleshoot common problems. Start with a quality standard. Write down what your target market wants. For example: brown eggs, large size, clean shells, no cracks, delivered in new cartons of thirty, twice a week. Put this standard on the wall in your grading room. Make sure every worker knows it. Then set up checks at each stage. At collection, check for dirty, cracked and misshapen eggs. At grading, check size, weight and shell quality. At packing, check carton condition, labelling and count. At storage, check temperature, humidity and rotation. At dispatch, check the order against the buyer's requirements. At delivery, check for breakage and on-time arrival. Keep records for each check. A simple notebook or spreadsheet is enough. Record date, time, number of eggs collected, number rejected, reasons for rejection, number packed, number sold, price and buyer. Also record storage temperature and humidity if you have instruments. Review records weekly. Look for patterns. Are rejections higher on hot days? Are cracks higher after a new worker starts? Are dirty eggs higher when bedding is wet? Use the data to fix the cause. For example, if dirty eggs increase, check nest bedding, collect more often or treat for diarrhoea. If cracks increase, check handling, tray condition and transport. If off-odours appear, check storage near chemicals or spoiled feed. If yolk colour changes, check feed ingredients. If shell strength drops, check calcium, phosphorus, heat stress and disease. Troubleshooting common problems is a core skill. Here are some common issues and practical solutions. Problem: dirty eggs. Causes: wet litter, dirty nests, diarrhoea, eggs laid on floor, late collection. Solutions: replace wet bedding, add more nests, collect more often, treat sick birds, keep floor clean, provide perches. Problem: cracked eggs. Causes: rough handling, overcrowded trays, weak shells, poor transport, birds laying on floor. Solutions: train workers, use good trays, do not overfill, improve nutrition, reduce heat stress, provide nests. Problem: thin shells. Causes: age, heat stress, low calcium, low phosphorus, disease, poor lighting. Solutions: provide limestone or oyster shell, adjust feed, cool the house, treat disease, check lighting programme. Problem: small eggs. Causes: young pullets, poor nutrition, disease, stress. Solutions: wait for pullets to mature, improve feed, reduce stress, treat disease. Problem: mixed sizes. Causes: no grading, mixed age flocks, poor sorting. Solutions: grade by weight, keep age groups separate, train workers. Problem: off-odours. Causes: storage near chemicals, manure, smoke, spoiled feed, old eggs. Solutions: move storage, clean area, rotate stock, check feed. Problem: mould on shells. Causes: high humidity, wet trays, condensation, dirty storage. Solutions: reduce humidity, ventilate, use dry trays, clean storage. Problem: blood spots. Causes: genetics, stress, disease, sudden noise. Solutions: reduce stress, check lighting, cull affected birds if severe. Problem: low sales or rejected orders. Causes: inconsistent quality, late delivery, wrong grade, poor packaging. Solutions: talk to buyer, check standard, improve grading, deliver on time. Problem: high breakage in transport. Causes: rough roads, poor packing, overloading, no shade. Solutions: use crates, pack tightly, drive slowly, cover cargo. Problem: spoilage in storage. Causes: high temperature, old stock, dirty eggs, no rotation. Solutions: cool storage, rotate stock, dry clean, remove spoiled eggs. For each problem, write a simple action plan. Who will do what, by when, and how will you check? Review the plan weekly. Celebrate improvements. Quality control is not a one-time task. It is a daily habit. It requires leadership. As the farm owner or manager, you must set the standard, provide the tools, train the team and follow up. If you ignore quality, your workers will ignore it too. If you check quality daily, your workers will take it seriously. Consider a simple quality scorecard. Score each batch on cleanliness, size uniformity, shell strength, packing and delivery. A score of one to five. Track scores over time. Share results with your team. Reward high scores. Investigate low scores. Use the scorecard to compare suppliers, workers, houses and seasons. It will show you where to invest. Also, listen to your buyers. Ask them for feedback. What do they like? What do they reject? What would make them buy more? A short phone call or visit can reveal problems before they become lost sales. Build relationships. In Africa, business is personal. A buyer who trusts you will give you a second chance. A buyer who does not trust you will not. Finally, keep learning. Attend training, join farmer groups, visit other farms, read industry updates. Egg quality standards change. Markets change. What works today may not work tomorrow. Stay curious. Stay consistent. Stay profitable. This module has covered egg quality, handling, storage, grading, packaging and quality control. Use these lessons to build a system that works for your farm, your market and your climate. Start small. Improve one thing at a time. Measure the results. Then improve again. Your eggs are your product. Your reputation is your business. Protect both.

6. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Egg Quality Troubleshooting, Measurement and Record Keeping

This practical reference is written for the commercial layer farm manager, egg collection supervisor or agribusiness owner who sells eggs into African urban and peri-urban markets. Its purpose is simple: give you the numbers, thresholds and record-keeping habits that protect your price per tray. Egg quality is not a vague idea. It is a set of measurable traits â shell strength, shell cleanliness, albumen height, yolk colour, air cell size and internal freshness â and each one can be checked on-farm with low-cost tools. When you can measure quality, you can negotiate from evidence rather than from opinion, and you can trace a complaint back to the exact flock, feed batch or collection shift that caused it.

Across African markets, buyers judge eggs first by appearance and second by freshness. A trader in Accra, Nairobi or Lagos may reject a tray because of dirty shells, cracks, weak shells, off-colours or a stale smell â long before any laboratory test is done. That means your quality control must be practical, fast and repeatable. The methods below are designed for farms with limited cold chain and limited laboratory access, using equipment that costs less than a few trays of eggs.

1. Measuring Shell Quality

Shell quality is the single largest driver of breakage losses between nest and buyer. In African conditions, where eggs often travel on rough roads in open crates or baskets, a weak shell can turn a profitable tray into a loss. Shell strength is influenced by hen age, calcium and phosphorus nutrition, heat stress, disease (especially infectious bronchitis and egg drop syndrome) and the time eggs spend in the nest.

You do not need a laboratory. A simple crack-detection and shell-thickness check can be done weekly:

  • Visual and candling check: Candle 100 eggs per flock per week. Record cracks, hairline cracks and rough shells as a percentage.
  • Shell thickness: Use a micrometer on shell fragments from 20 eggs. Target 0.33â0.38 mm for commercial layers.
  • Specific gravity test: Float eggs in salt solutions of known density. Fresh, strong-shelled eggs sink in higher-density solutions. A common farm test uses solutions from 1.070 to 1.090 g/mL.
  • Breakage rate: Record the number of cracked or broken eggs per 1,000 collected. A well-managed flock should stay below 2â3%.

2. Measuring Internal Quality

Internal quality is what the buyer cannot see until the egg is opened â but it is what determines repeat purchase. The most practical farm measurement is the Haugh unit, derived from egg weight and thick albumen height. You can estimate it with a simple tripod-mounted micrometer or a commercial Haugh meter. Fresh eggs from healthy hens typically score above 72 Haugh units; eggs above 60 are still acceptable for most markets, but below 50 indicates ageing or poor storage.

Yolk colour is equally important in African markets, where deep orange yolks often command a premium. Colour comes from carotenoids in feed â maize, maize gluten, alfalfa, marigold or synthetic pigments. Measure it against a Roche yolk colour fan. A score of 8â12 is the commercial sweet spot; too pale suggests low pigment in feed, too deep may suggest excessive supplementation or adulteration.

Air cell size is a simple freshness indicator. In a fresh egg, the air cell is small. As the egg loses moisture and carbon dioxide through the shell, the air cell grows. Candle a sample weekly and record air cell depth. An air cell above 6 mm usually means the egg is old or has been stored poorly.

3. Record Keeping That Protects Your Price

Quality records are a business tool, not paperwork. They let you:

  • Prove to buyers that your eggs meet a stated standard.
  • Trace a complaint back to a specific flock, feed batch or collection shift.
  • Compare flocks and identify underperformers.
  • Support loan applications, contracts and certification.
  • Reduce rejections and renegotiate price with evidence.

Keep at least these records: daily collection and breakage log, weekly shell quality sample, weekly internal quality sample, feed batch and additive record, disease and medication record, storage temperature and humidity log, and dispatch record with buyer name and batch code. A simple exercise book or a spreadsheet on a phone is enough to start. The key is consistency and traceability.

Eggs are a high-risk food for Salmonella and other foodborne pathogens. Never sell cracked, dirty, leaking or off-odour eggs for human consumption, even at a discount. Do not wash eggs in dirty water or reuse egg trays without disinfection â washing with contaminated water pushes bacteria through the shell. If you use disinfectants, insecticides, rodenticides or feed additives, follow the label exactly, wear gloves and a mask, store chemicals away from eggs and feed, and respect withdrawal periods. 4. Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Candle 100 eggs per flock weekly and record cracks, dirt, air cell size and internal defects.
  2. Measure shell thickness on 20 eggs weekly; investigate if below 0.30 mm.
  3. Track breakage rate per 1,000 eggs collected; act if above 3% for two weeks.
  4. Check Haugh unit or albumen height monthly; keep fresh eggs above 72 HU.
  5. Score yolk colour against a Roche fan; target 8â12 for premium markets.
  6. Record storage temperature and humidity daily; keep eggs cool, shaded and ventilated.
  7. Label every dispatch batch with date, flock and collection shift for traceability.
  8. Review records monthly with your team and set one quality improvement target per month.
