Lessons

1. Designing the Governance Framework

A cooperative's governance framework is like the skeleton of a body: it gives shape and support. In Africa, where many agricultural cooperatives fail due to weak governance, a clear framework is vital. Start by drafting a constitution and bylaws. These documents state the cooperative's name, location, objectives, membership rules, and how decisions are made. For example, the Kilimanjaro Coffee Cooperative in Tanzania uses a constitution that requires a general assembly meeting at least once a year. Your bylaws should specify the number of board members (typically 5 to 9), their terms (usually 3 years, staggered), and the powers of the general assembly. Also, define the roles of key officers: chairperson, vice-chairperson, secretary, and treasurer. In many African cooperatives, the treasurer handles funds, so include a clause requiring two signatories on bank accounts. Next, establish a clear separation of powers: the general assembly is the supreme authority, the board sets policy, and the manager handles daily operations. This prevents conflict. Include a dispute resolution mechanism, such as mediation by a local council of elders or a cooperative federation. Finally, register the cooperative with the relevant government authority, like the Ministry of Agriculture or a cooperative commission. In Kenya, registration is done under the Cooperative Societies Act. Ensure the framework is written in simple language and translated into local languages if needed. Hold a meeting to explain it to all members. A good framework is not just paper; it must be understood and followed. Review it every three years to adapt to changes. With a solid governance framework, your cooperative can grow and serve its members effectively.

2. Board of Directors: Election and Duties

The board of directors is the engine that drives your cooperative. In Africa, where trust is key, electing the right people is crucial. Elections should be held at the annual general assembly. Use a secret ballot to avoid intimidation. Each member has one vote, regardless of shares. Nominate candidates who are active members, have a good reputation, and can read and write. For example, in the Nyakyusa Cooperative in Tanzania, board members must have been members for at least two years. The board usually has 5 to 9 directors, serving staggered terms of three years, so that not all change at once. After election, the board elects its own officers: chairperson, vice-chairperson, secretary, and treasurer. Duties of the board include: hiring and supervising the manager, approving budgets, setting policies (e.g., pricing of crops), and representing the cooperative to outside bodies. They must meet at least once a month. Keep minutes of every meeting. Board members should not interfere with daily management; that is the manager's job. They must also avoid conflicts of interest. For instance, if a board member wants to sell produce to the cooperative, they should declare it and step out of the decision. In many African cooperatives, board members are unpaid but receive a sitting allowance, like 10,000 shillings per meeting. Training is important: organize workshops on financial literacy and leadership. The board is accountable to the general assembly. If they fail, members can vote them out at the next assembly. A dedicated board can transform a cooperative from poverty to prosperity.

3. Role of the General Assembly

The general assembly is the supreme authority in a cooperative. It is all the members coming together to make big decisions. In Africa, where communities are tight-knit, the general assembly is a powerful tool for democracy. It must meet at least once a year for the annual general meeting (AGM). At the AGM, members review the previous year's performance, approve the budget, elect board members, and decide on major issues like merging with another cooperative or changing the bylaws. Each member has one vote, no matter how many shares they hold. Quorum is important: usually, at least 50% of members must be present for decisions to be valid. If not, reschedule. The assembly also has the power to remove board members or the manager if they are not performing. In some countries, like Ghana, the AGM must be held within three months after the end of the financial year. Notice of the meeting must be given at least 14 days in advance, with the agenda. Members can propose items for the agenda. During the meeting, the chairperson presents a report, the treasurer presents financial statements, and the manager gives an operational update. Members can ask questions and vote. Decisions are made by simple majority, except for constitutional changes which may require two-thirds. After the meeting, minutes must be recorded and shared. In many African cooperatives, the general assembly is also a social event, with food and music. But don't let that overshadow the business. Educate members on their rights and responsibilities. A strong general assembly keeps leaders accountable and ensures the cooperative serves its members.

4. Hiring and Managing a Cooperative Manager

A cooperative manager is the day-to-day leader. In Africa, where many cooperatives are small, the manager might be the only paid employee. Hiring the right person is critical. Start by writing a job description: duties include overseeing operations, managing staff, keeping records, and reporting to the board. Required qualifications: at least a diploma in agriculture, business, or cooperative management; 2 years of experience; and knowledge of local languages. Advertise widely: in local markets, radio, and notice boards. Shortlist candidates and interview them. The board should conduct the interview, not just the chairperson. Ask practical questions: 'How would you handle a member who refuses to follow quality standards?' Check references. Once hired, offer a contract with a probation period of 3 to 6 months. Salary should be competitive but within the cooperative's budget. In Kenya, a manager might earn 30,000 to 50,000 shillings per month. The manager reports to the board and attends board meetings but does not vote. The board should set clear performance targets: e.g., increase sales by 10% in one year. Conduct an annual performance review. If the manager underperforms, provide training or warnings, and dismiss if necessary. The manager must not be a board member to avoid conflict of interest. Also, ensure the manager is not related to board members. In many African cooperatives, nepotism is a problem. The manager should manage staff, but hiring of staff should be approved by the board. Provide the manager with tools: a computer, accounting software like QuickBooks, and a mobile phone for communication. A good manager can double a cooperative's income. Treat them well, and they will stay.

5. Establishing Committees for Specific Functions

Committees are small groups that handle specific tasks. In African agricultural cooperatives, committees make work easier and involve more members. Common committees include: finance, marketing, production, and social. The finance committee oversees budgets, audits, and financial reports. It should have 3 to 5 members, including the treasurer. The marketing committee finds buyers, negotiates prices, and organizes transport. For example, in the coffee cooperatives of Ethiopia, a marketing committee liaises with exporters. The production committee advises on farming practices, seeds, and fertilizers. The social committee organizes events, welfare, and conflict resolution. Each committee is formed by the board, but members can volunteer. Terms last one year. Committees meet monthly and report to the board. They have no decision-making power unless delegated. For instance, the finance committee can approve small expenses up to 100,000 shillings, but larger ones need board approval. Training is needed: teach committee members how to read budgets and write reports. In many African cooperatives, committees fail because members lack skills. So, hold a workshop. Also, rotate committee members to prevent burnout. Committees should keep minutes and share with the board. They help distribute leadership and build skills for future board members. For example, a young member who serves on the marketing committee may later become a board director. Avoid too many committees; start with three: finance, marketing, and production. As the cooperative grows, add more. Ensure gender balance: at least one woman on each committee. In some cultures, women may not speak up, so create a safe space. Committees are the training ground for cooperative democracy.

6. Leadership Development for Cooperative Members

Leadership development is about growing skills so members can lead effectively. In Africa, where many cooperatives are in rural areas, lack of leadership training is a major weakness. Start by identifying potential leaders: look for members who are active, honest, and willing to learn. Offer training in communication, conflict resolution, financial literacy, and cooperative principles. Use local trainers or partner with organizations like the Cooperative College in Kenya or the Moshi University College of Co-operative and Business Studies in Tanzania. Training can be in workshops, exchange visits, or online. For example, send a group of members to visit a successful cooperative in another region. They learn by seeing. Also, create a mentorship program: pair young members with experienced ones. Hold regular meetings where members practice public speaking and decision-making. In many African cooperatives, women and youth are underrepresented. So, target them: hold separate sessions for women to build confidence. Encourage them to take up committee roles. Leadership is not just for the board; every member should understand their rights and responsibilities. Use role-playing to simulate general assembly meetings. Teach how to read financial statements. Provide materials in local languages. Budget for leadership development: allocate at least 5% of the cooperative's annual budget to training. This is an investment. When members are skilled, they make better decisions, hold leaders accountable, and the cooperative thrives. For example, the Oromia Coffee Farmers Cooperative Union in Ethiopia invests heavily in member training, and it has become one of the largest cooperatives in Africa. Remember, leadership development is continuous. Hold short sessions at every general assembly. Celebrate graduates. A cooperative with strong leaders can overcome any challenge.

Practical Reference: Governance & Organizational Structure for African Agricultural Cooperatives

This practical reference consolidates the six lessons of the Governance and Organizational Structure module into an actionable toolkit for cooperative promoters, board members, and managers. It is written for the African context: mixed legal traditions (anglophone common law, francophone civil law, and customary practice), member bases that are often smallholder farmers with low literacy, and value chains ranging from cocoa in CÃ´te d'Ivoire to dairy in Kenya and maize in Zambia. The guiding principle throughout is that governance is not paperwork â it is the operating system that determines whether a cooperative can access finance, win offtake contracts, and retain member loyalty.

1. Designing the Governance Framework

Start with the legal vehicle. Most African jurisdictions now have a dedicated cooperative law (e.g., Kenya's Co-operative Societies Act, Cap 490; Ghana's Co-operative Societies Act, 1968 as amended; Nigeria's Co-operative Societies Act; South Africa's Co-operatives Act 14 of 2005). Registration typically requires a minimum of 10 (sometimes 20) members, a constitution or by-laws, and a registered address. The by-laws are where governance is actually designed â not in the statute. Key design decisions:

  • One member, one vote regardless of shareholding â the defining cooperative principle that distinguishes it from a company.
  • Director term limits (typically two or three consecutive terms of three years) to prevent entrenchment.
  • Board size: 5â9 directors is the practical sweet spot; larger boards become dysfunctional in rural settings.
  • Quorum rules for the General Assembly â usually one-third or 50 members, whichever is lower, to avoid paralysis.
  • Dispute resolution tier: internal committee â arbitration â registrar â courts.

Business-first implication: donors and lenders (e.g., the Agricultural Finance Corporation in Kenya, or Rabobank Foundation in East Africa) will read your by-laws before disbursing. Weak by-laws mean no credit.

2. Board of Directors: Election and Duties

The board is elected by the General Assembly, usually at the Annual General Meeting (AGM). In most African jurisdictions, directors serve 3-year terms, often staggered so that one-third retire each year. Duties fall into four buckets:

  • Fiduciary: safeguard assets, approve budgets, ensure statutory audits.
  • Strategic: set the business plan, approve offtake contracts and capital investments.
  • Representational: represent members to buyers, government, and financiers.
  • Compliance: file annual returns with the cooperative registrar, pay taxes, maintain the register of members.

In practice, the biggest governance failure in African cooperatives is the board drifting into management â signing cheques, hiring relatives, or bypassing the manager. The board governs; the manager manages. Write this into the by-laws and the manager's job description.

3. Role of the General Assembly

The General Assembly (GA) is the supreme authority. It meets at least annually (AGM) and may meet extraordinarily. Reserved powers typically include: electing and removing directors, approving audited accounts, amending by-laws, distributing surplus (patronage refunds), and dissolving the cooperative. Quorum must be realistic â if your by-laws require two-thirds attendance and you have 800 members, you will never achieve quorum. Set quorum at one-third or a fixed number (e.g., 50), whichever is lower.

4. Hiring and Managing a Cooperative Manager

The manager is the only senior staff position the board directly hires (other staff report to the manager). A structured process:

  1. Board writes a job description tied to the business plan (e.g., "grow milk collection from 2,000 to 5,000 litres/day within 24 months").
  2. Advertise locally and via the cooperative apex body (e.g., KUSCCO in Kenya, or the Ghana Co-operative Marketing Association).
  3. Shortlist and interview with at least three board members; check references with previous employers.
  4. Offer a fixed-term contract (2â3 years, renewable) with performance milestones.
  5. Appraise annually against milestones; the board â not individual directors â gives direction.

5. Establishing Committees for Specific Functions

Committees do the detailed work the full board cannot. Standard committees in African agricultural cooperatives:

  • Audit & Supervisory Committee â elected by the GA, not the board, to preserve independence. Reviews accounts and reports to members.
  • Credit Committee â screens member loan applications (critical where cooperatives run input credit schemes).
  • Marketing & Production Committee â plans aggregation, quality control, and offtake contracts.
  • Membership & Education Committee â recruits members, runs training, handles grievances.
  • Ad-hoc committees â e.g., a disciplinary panel or a tender evaluation committee.

6. Leadership Development for Cooperative Members

Governance collapses when members cannot read a balance sheet or understand a by-law. Invest in:

  • Board induction â a half-day session on fiduciary duties, conflict of interest, and reading financial statements.
  • Member education â annual sessions on cooperative principles, voting rights, and the business model.
  • Women and youth inclusion â set targets (e.g., at least 30% women on the board) and mentor emerging leaders. In many African cooperatives, women provide most labour but hold few board seats.
  • Exchange visits â send directors to a well-run cooperative in another district or country.

â  SAFETY & COMPLIANCE WARNING

Do not allow the board to handle cash, sign cheques, or approve loans to its own members without independent scrutiny. In several African jurisdictions, cooperative fraud has led to criminal prosecution of directors under anti-corruption and money-laundering laws (e.g., Kenya's Ethics and Anti-Corruption Commission, Nigeria's EFCC). Ensure the Audit & Supervisory Committee is elected by the General Assembly â not the board â and that all directors sign a conflict-of-interest declaration annually. Never co-mingle member savings with operating funds.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. By-laws registered with the national cooperative registrar, with quorum, term limits, and dispute resolution clearly stated.
  2. Board of 5â9 directors, elected by secret ballot at the AGM, with staggered terms.
  3. General Assembly meets at least annually; audited accounts presented and approved.
  4. Manager hired through a competitive process, on a fixed-term contract with performance milestones.
  5. Audit & Supervisory Committee elected by the General Assembly, not the board.
  6. At least four functional committees (Audit, Credit, Marketing, Membership) operational with written terms of reference.
  7. Annual board induction and member education sessions held and documented.
  8. Conflict-of-interest declarations signed by all directors and senior staff each year.
  9. Statutory annual returns and tax filings submitted on time.
