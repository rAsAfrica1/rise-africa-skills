1. Causes of Segregation in Blends

Segregation happens when particles in a blended fertilizer separate by size, shape, or density. In Africa, many blenders use simple drum mixers or shovel mixing, which can cause segregation if not controlled. The main causes are: differences in particle size, differences in density, and handling methods like pouring or dropping from height. For example, if you blend urea (prills, 2-4 mm) with rock phosphate (powder, <1 mm), the fine powder sifts through the gaps between prills, sinking to the bottom. This is called sifting segregation. Another cause is fluidization: when you pour a blend from a height, air resistance affects light particles more, blowing them away from heavy ones. In a typical African warehouse, a blend of 50 kg urea, 50 kg DAP, and 50 kg MOP dropped from a 2-meter height can segregate by up to 20% in nutrient content from top to bottom of the pile. Vibration during transport on rough roads also causes smaller particles to move downward. Moisture makes it worse: damp urea sticks together while dry MOP stays separate. To avoid segregation, you must match particle sizes (within 1 mm difference) and avoid free-fall drops. Always test your blend by sampling from top, middle, and bottom of a bag or pile to see if segregation is happening.

2. Particle Size Distribution and Its Impact

Particle size distribution (PSD) is the range of sizes of particles in your fertilizer materials. If all particles are the same size, they mix evenly and stay mixed. But in reality, each fertilizer has a different PSD. For example, urea prills are typically 2-4 mm, DAP granules are 2-4 mm, but MOP (muriate of potash) can be 1-3 mm, and some local rock phosphates are powder (<0.5 mm). The wider the PSD, the more segregation you get. A good rule: the largest particle should not be more than 1.5 times the smallest. So if your smallest is 1 mm, largest should be 1.5 mm. To measure PSD, use a set of sieves (e.g., 1 mm, 2 mm, 3 mm, 4 mm) and shake a 500 g sample for 5 minutes. Weigh what stays on each sieve. In Africa, many blenders skip this test. But a simple sieve set costs about $50 and saves money. If your PSD is too wide, you can screen out fines or crush oversize particles. You can also buy fertilizers with similar PSD. For example, blend urea (2-4 mm) with DAP (2-4 mm) and MOP (2-4 mm) all from the same supplier. Avoid blending powder with prills unless you first granulate the powder. Remember: uniform size means uniform blend.

3. Preventing Segregation During Handling

Even a perfect blend will segregate if you handle it badly. In Africa, common handling mistakes include pouring from height, using augers, and dropping bags. Here is how to prevent segregation: First, never drop blend from more than 1 meter. Use a chute or lower the bag slowly. Second, avoid conveyor belts that cause vibration instead use bucket elevators or gentle screw conveyors with full flights. Third, when loading into a truck, pour the blend in one spot and let it form a pile, then level it do not pour from the side. Fourth, minimize transfers: each transfer increases segregation. If you must transfer, use a closed system. Fifth, keep moisture low if blend is damp, particles stick and segregate less, but too damp causes caking. Aim for 1-2% moisture. Sixth, use a ribbon blender or paddle mixer instead of a drum mixer they cause less segregation. Seventh, after blending, bag immediately and store bags flat, not stacked too high (max 8 bags high). Eighth, when applying in the field, do not broadcast by hand from a height use a cyclone spreader or drop spreader at low height. Finally, train workers: a simple rule like 'no pouring from shoulder height' can reduce segregation by 30%. Test your handling by sampling from different parts of a truckload after transport.

4. Evaluating Blend Quality by Segregation Tests

To know if your blend is uniform, you must test it. The simplest test is the 'bag sampling test'. Take 10 bags from a production run. From each bag, take three samples: top, middle, and bottom (using a scoop or thief sampler). Mix each sample separately, then send to a lab for nutrient analysis (N, P, K). If the nutrient content varies by more than 10% between top and bottom of the same bag, you have segregation. A cheaper field test: use a 'segregation column'. Fill a 1-meter tall pipe (10 cm diameter) with blend, then vibrate it for 5 minutes on a vibrating table or by tapping. Then carefully remove the blend in 10 cm layers and weigh each layer. If the ratio of fine to coarse particles changes by more than 15% from top to bottom, segregation is high. Another test: the 'sieve test' take a 1 kg sample, sieve it into size fractions, and check if the proportion of each fertilizer matches the original recipe. For example, if your recipe is 50% urea, 30% DAP, 20% MOP, then each sieve fraction should have similar ratios. In Africa, you can do a simple 'salt test': mix 1 kg blend with 1 liter of water, stir, and let settle. The fertilizer that dissolves first (urea) will be in the top water layer, while sand-like particles (MOP) sink. This gives a rough idea. Record all results and adjust your blending if variation is above 10%.

5. Strategies for Improving Blend Homogeneity

To get a homogeneous blend, you need the right recipe, right equipment, and right method. First, match particle sizes: buy fertilizers with similar PSD. If not possible, screen out fines below 1 mm and oversize above 4 mm. Second, use a proper mixer: a horizontal ribbon blender or a paddle mixer runs at 20-30 rpm for 3-5 minutes. Do not overmix that causes segregation. Third, add liquids (like water or oil) at 1-2% to reduce dust and stick particles together. Fourth, use a 'master batch' approach: pre-blend small amounts of minor nutrients with a carrier (like sand) before adding to the main mix. Fifth, load the mixer correctly: add the largest particles first, then medium, then fine. Sixth, control moisture: keep blend at 1-2% moisture by storing in dry conditions. Seventh, use anti-segregation additives like clay or starch at 0.5-1% they coat particles and reduce separation. Eighth, after mixing, test uniformity by sampling from 5 points in the mixer. Ninth, bag immediately and avoid drops. Tenth, train workers on gentle handling. In Africa, a simple improvement is to use a cement mixer (rented for $20/day) instead of shovel mixing it gives 40% better uniformity. Another low-cost method: mix on a clean concrete floor by rolling a drum back and forth for 10 minutes. Always aim for a coefficient of variation (CV) below 10% for N, P, and K.

6. Case Studies of Segregation Issues

Case 1: In Kenya, a blender mixed 100 kg urea (2-4 mm) with 50 kg rock phosphate powder (<0.5 mm) in a drum mixer. After 5 minutes, they bagged and sold to farmers. Farmers reported yellow patches (urea) and white patches (rock phosphate) in the field. Sampling showed nitrogen varied from 18% to 32% between bags. Solution: they switched to granulated rock phosphate (2-4 mm) and used a ribbon blender. Variation dropped to 5%. Case 2: In Nigeria, a cooperative blended 200 kg NPK 20-10-10 using shovel mixing on a concrete floor. They poured from a height of 2 meters into bags. After transport on rough roads, the bottom of each bag had 30% more MOP (heavy) and the top had more urea (light). Farmers complained of poor growth. Solution: they built a simple chute to lower bags and used a cement mixer. Segregation reduced by 60%. Case 3: In South Africa, a large blender used a conveyor belt to move blend to a bagging hopper. Vibration caused fines to settle, so the first 10 bags had 25% more fines than the last 10. Solution: they replaced the belt with a bucket elevator and added a re-mixer before bagging. Uniformity improved to CV 8%. Case 4: In Tanzania, a farmer mixed DAP and urea by hand in a bucket and applied by broadcasting. He saw uneven crop growth. Solution: he now mixes in a closed drum for 5 minutes and applies with a drop spreader. Yield increased by 20%. These cases show that simple, low-cost changes matching particle sizes, gentle handling, and proper mixing solve most segregation problems.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Blend Uniformity and Segregation in African Fertilizer Blending

In fertilizer blending, a uniform blend is not just a quality target it is a commercial contract. When a farmer pays for a 20:10:10 NPK blend, they expect every bag to deliver the same nutrient ratio. When segregation occurs, some bags over-deliver nitrogen while others short-change phosphorus or potassium. The result is uneven crop performance, customer complaints, reputational damage, and lost repeat business. For African blenders operating in competitive markets from Kenya and Tanzania to Nigeria, Zambia, and South Africa mastering blend uniformity is a direct driver of profitability and customer retention.

This practical reference consolidates the key operational knowledge from the module into a single actionable guide. It covers the mechanisms of segregation, the critical role of particle size distribution (PSD), handling practices that prevent or accelerate segregation, testing methods, and proven strategies for improving homogeneity. Two data tables provide real operational benchmarks, and a safety warning highlights a frequently overlooked hazard.

Why Segregation Matters Commercially

Segregation is the tendency of particles in a blended fertilizer to separate by size, density, or shape during handling, transport, and storage. For African blenders, this translates directly to:

  • Customer complaints: Farmers report uneven crop response across a field or between bags from the same batch.
  • Regulatory risk: National fertilizer quality standards (e.g., KEBS in Kenya, SON in Nigeria, TBS in Tanzania) require guaranteed nutrient content per bag. Segregated blends may fail compliance testing.
  • Wasted raw materials: Over-blending to compensate for segregation increases input costs without guaranteeing quality.
  • Brand damage: In markets where trust is built through demonstration plots and word-of-mouth, a single bad batch can destroy years of relationship-building.

Causes of Segregation in Blends

Segregation occurs through four primary mechanisms, all driven by differences in particle properties:

  1. Sifting or percolation: Fine particles migrate downward through the voids between larger particles. This is the most common cause in fertilizer blends where urea prills (24 mm) are mixed with fine powdered phosphates or micronutrients.
  2. Vibration: During transport or conveyor movement, vibration causes smaller particles to settle to the bottom of the pile or bag.
  3. Impact and flow: When blend is poured onto a pile or into a hopper, larger particles roll to the edges while fines remain at the center. This creates a "core-and-periphery" segregation pattern.
  4. Air entrainment: During pneumatic conveying or high-drop filling, fine particles become airborne and settle at different rates than coarse particles.

Particle Size Distribution and Its Impact

Particle Size Distribution (PSD) is the single most important variable controlling segregation. The general rule: the closer the PSD of all ingredients, the lower the segregation risk. Industry best practice targets a PSD where 90% of particles fall within a 1.5:1 to 2:1 size ratio between the largest and smallest particles.

In African blending operations, PSD mismatch is common because raw materials are sourced from multiple suppliers with different granulation processes. For example, imported DAP (typically 24 mm) blended with locally sourced fine rock phosphate (0.10.5 mm) will segregate rapidly without corrective action.

Preventing Segregation During Handling

Prevention is cheaper than correction. Key operational controls include:

  • Minimize drop heights: Keep transfer drops below 1 meter where possible. Use telescopic chutes or cascade systems.
  • Avoid free-fall into piles: Use a spreader or rotating chute to distribute material evenly rather than allowing a central cone to form.
  • Limit conveyor transfers: Each transfer point is a segregation opportunity. Design layouts with minimum handling steps.
  • Control vibration: Use rubber-mounted conveyors and avoid excessive vibration during bagging and palletizing.
  • Use appropriate packaging: Fill bags from the bottom up (bottom-fill) rather than top-fill to reduce impact segregation.
  • Store correctly: Avoid dropping bags from height onto pallets. Stack bags flat and avoid excessive handling.

SAFETY WARNING: Dust Explosion and Respiratory Hazard

Fine fertilizer dust especially from phosphates, micronutrient powders, and ammonium nitrate-based materials poses two serious risks. First, airborne dust in enclosed blending areas can form explosive mixtures if ignited by a spark or hot surface. Second, prolonged inhalation of phosphate and micronutrient dust can cause respiratory irritation, silicosis-like conditions, or heavy metal toxicity. Always ensure adequate ventilation, use dust extraction systems at transfer points, and require workers to wear NIOSH-approved N95 or P2 respirators, safety goggles, and gloves. Never use compressed air to clean dust in enclosed spaces. In African operations where ventilation may be limited, prioritize natural cross-ventilation and regular air quality monitoring.

You cannot manage what you do not measure. Regular segregation testing is essential for quality assurance and customer confidence.

Strategies for Improving Blend Homogeneity

Beyond handling controls, blenders can implement formulation and process strategies:

  • Match PSD: Source raw materials with similar particle size ranges. If unavailable, consider grinding or granulating fine materials to match.
  • Use a binder or coating: Light coating with oil or a binder can reduce fines migration and improve adhesion between particles.
  • Optimize mixing time: Under-mixing leaves patches; over-mixing can cause segregation. Test to find the optimal mixing time for each formulation.
  • Use a ribbon or paddle blender: These are generally better for fertilizer blends than tumble blenders, which can cause segregation.
  • Compact or granulate the final blend: If segregation is severe, consider compacting the blend into uniform granules though this adds cost.
  • Quality control at every step: Test raw materials on arrival, monitor blending, and test finished bags before dispatch.

Case Studies of Segregation Issues

Case Study 1: Kenyan Blender Urea + SSP Segregation
A mid-sized blender in Nakuru produced a 20:10:10 blend using imported urea prills (24 mm) and locally sourced SSP (0.51.5 mm). Farmers reported yellowing (nitrogen deficiency) in some field patches. Bag analysis showed nitrogen CV of 12% well above the 5% threshold. Root cause: PSD mismatch and free-fall filling. Solution: switched to a bottom-fill bagging system, reduced drop heights, and sourced a coarser SSP. Nitrogen CV dropped to 3.8% within two batches.

Case Study 2: Nigerian Blender Micronutrient Segregation
A blender in Kano added zinc and boron powders to an NPK blend. After three months of storage, farmers reported micronutrient deficiency symptoms. Testing revealed that micronutrient fines had migrated to the bottom of bags during transport. Solution: pre-granulated micronutrients to match the PSD of the NPK granules and added a light oil coating. Segregation was reduced by over 80%.

Case Study 3: Zambian Cooperative Transport Vibration
A cooperative in Lusaka blended DAP, MOP, and urea, then transported bags 400 km on unpaved roads. Upon arrival, bags showed significant potassium segregation. Solution: used tighter bags, reduced pallet stacking height, and switched to a blend with more closely matched PSD. The cooperative also began testing bags after transport, not just at the factory gate.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Check PSD of all raw materials on arrival target a size ratio below 2:1 between largest and smallest particles.
  • Minimize drop heights at every transfer point keep below 1 meter and use cascade or telescopic chutes.
  • Use bottom-fill bagging rather than top-fill to reduce impact segregation.
  • Test blend uniformity per batch sample top, middle, and bottom of bags and calculate CV.
  • Calibrate your blender and establish optimal mixing time for each formulation.
  • Train staff on handling practices that prevent segregation no free-fall dumping, no excessive vibration.
  • Store bags flat and avoid dropping from height during palletizing and loading.
  • Test after transport segregation can occur between factory and farm gate.
