1. Module 9 Lesson 1: Irrigation System Components and Their Functions

Welcome to Module 9 of your Agricultural Irrigation Technician training. In this first lesson we lay the foundation by examining every major component of a modern irrigation system and explaining how each part works in the field. As an irrigation technician working in Africa, you will encounter systems that range from simple bucket and watering can operations to sophisticated drip and sprinkler networks powered by solar pumps. Your ability to identify, install, maintain and repair each component will determine whether a farmer harvests a healthy crop or loses everything to drought or waterlogging. Let us begin with the water source. In most African smallholder and commercial farms, the source may be a borehole, a river, a dam, a shallow well, a rainwater harvesting tank or even a municipal connection. The source determines the pump type, the filtration needs and the total system pressure. A borehole with a deep water table requires a submersible pump, while a river or dam often uses a surface centrifugal pump. Always measure the static water level and the drawdown before selecting a pump. Next comes the pump itself. Pumps convert energy into water flow and pressure. The three most common types in Africa are submersible pumps, centrifugal pumps and solar-powered pumps. Solar pumps are increasingly popular because they reduce fuel costs and work well in remote areas with good sunshine. When installing a pump, check the impeller for wear, ensure the suction line is airtight and confirm the direction of rotation for three-phase motors. The pump curve supplied by the manufacturer tells you the flow rate at different heads. Never guess; always match the pump to the system demand. After the pump, water travels through the mainline, which is usually a rigid PVC or HDPE pipe buried underground. The mainline carries water from the source to the field. Sub-mainlines branch off the mainline to serve different blocks. Lateral lines, also called laterals, are the smaller pipes that deliver water directly to the emitters or sprinklers. In drip systems, laterals are often made of flexible polyethylene and lie on the soil surface or are buried shallowly. In sprinkler systems, laterals may be aluminium or PVC and are moved manually or permanently installed. Valves control the flow of water. Gate valves isolate sections for repair. Ball valves provide quick shutoff. Check valves prevent backflow, which protects the pump and prevents contamination. Air release valves remove trapped air that can cause water hammer and pipe bursts. Pressure regulators keep the pressure steady so that emitters and sprinklers perform as designed. A pressure gauge on the mainline and on each zone helps you diagnose problems quickly. Filters are critical, especially for drip irrigation. Sand filters remove organic matter. Screen filters catch finer particles. Disk filters combine both functions. In Africa, where water often carries silt, algae and debris, a clogged filter is the number one cause of poor drip performance. Clean filters daily or as needed, and install them before the mainline enters the field. Emitters and sprinklers are the final delivery points. Drip emitters release water slowly at the root zone, saving up to 60 percent compared to flood irrigation. Sprinklers spray water over the canopy and are better for leafy crops and pastures. Micro-sprinklers and micro-jets are used for orchards and vegetables. Each emitter has a flow rate measured in litres per hour. Sprinklers have a precipitation rate measured in millimetres per hour. You must know these numbers to design a system that matches the crop water requirement. The control head, sometimes called the pump station, brings together the pump, filters, valves, pressure gauges and often a fertigation unit. Fertigation allows you to inject soluble fertiliser into the irrigation water. This saves labour and improves nutrient use efficiency, but it requires careful calibration and backflow prevention to protect the water source. Finally, consider the energy source. Diesel and petrol pumps are common but expensive to run. Electric pumps are cheaper per hour but need a reliable grid. Solar pumps have high upfront cost but low running cost and are ideal for remote areas. Hybrid systems combine solar with a generator or grid backup. As a technician, you must be able to calculate the total dynamic head, which is the sum of the static lift, friction losses in pipes and fittings, and the pressure required at the emitters. This calculation determines whether a pump will deliver enough water. You must also understand pipe sizing. Undersized pipes cause high friction loss and wasted energy. Oversized pipes waste money. Use friction loss charts or mobile apps to select the correct diameter. In the African context, you will often work with limited budgets and unreliable supply chains. Therefore, you must be creative. You may need to repair a cracked PVC pipe with a rubber sleeve and hose clamps. You may need to clean a sand filter with locally available materials. You may need to rewire a solar pump controller. Your practical skills will keep farmers irrigating when others give up. Remember that every component interacts with the others. A weak pump cannot push water through a dirty filter. A leaking valve reduces pressure to the whole zone. A single clogged emitter can cause uneven crop growth. Your job is to see the system as a whole, not just a collection of parts. In the next lesson we will look at how to assess a site and design an irrigation system that fits the farm. Before you move on, complete this activity: visit a nearby farm or your own workshop and list every component of an irrigation system you can find. Note the brand, size and condition. This inventory will become your reference for future troubleshooting. Keep a notebook and record flow rates, pressures and filter cleaning schedules. Practical record keeping is the mark of a professional technician. See you in Lesson 2.

2. Module 9 Lesson 2: Site Assessment and Irrigation System Design

In Lesson 1 you learned the components. Now we focus on the critical process of site assessment and system design. A poorly designed system wastes water, energy and money. A well designed system delivers the right amount of water to the right place at the right time. As an irrigation technician in Africa, you will often be called to a farm where the previous system failed. Your first task is to assess the site honestly and gather data before you recommend any changes. Start with the water source. Measure the flow rate using a bucket and stopwatch. For a borehole, record the static water level and the pumping water level after running the pump for at least thirty minutes. For a river or dam, measure the width, depth and velocity to estimate available flow. Never assume the source is unlimited. In many parts of Africa, water sources decline in the dry season. Design for the worst case, not the best case. Next, assess the soil. Soil type determines how fast water infiltrates and how much water the soil can hold. Sandy soils drain quickly and need more frequent, smaller applications. Clay soils hold water but drain slowly, so you must avoid applying water faster than it can infiltrate, or you will get runoff and waterlogging. Loamy soils are ideal. You can do a simple jar test or a double-ring infiltrometer test. Record the infiltration rate in millimetres per hour. This number will guide your emitter selection and irrigation scheduling. Then assess the topography. A slope of more than two percent affects pressure distribution. Water flows downhill, so emitters at the bottom of a slope receive higher pressure than those at the top. You may need pressure compensating emitters or multiple zones with separate pressure regulators. Use a dumpy level or a laser level to map elevations. Even a one metre difference over a hundred metres can change pressure significantly. Next, assess the crop. Different crops have different root depths, water requirements and sensitivity to salinity. Maize, for example, needs more water than millet. Tomatoes are sensitive to water stress during flowering. Leafy vegetables need frequent light irrigation. You must know the crop water requirement, often expressed as evapotranspiration or ET. In Africa, you can get ET data from local agricultural extension offices or from the FAO56 method. Calculate the peak daily water requirement in millimetres per day. Multiply by the area to get the total volume needed per day. Then assess the energy source. Is there grid electricity? How reliable is it? Is there diesel supply nearby? How many sunny days per year? Solar pumping is often the best choice for remote farms, but you must size the solar array correctly. A common mistake is to buy a solar pump that is too small for the dry season demand. Always add a safety margin of at least twenty percent. Now you have enough data to design the system. Start with the water source and pump. Calculate the total dynamic head as you learned in Lesson 1. Select a pump that delivers the required flow at that head. Then size the mainline and sub-mainlines. Use the Hazen-Williams equation or a friction loss chart. Keep velocity below 1.5 metres per second in PVC pipes to avoid water hammer. For laterals, choose a diameter that keeps pressure variation within ten percent across the zone. Next, select the emission method. Drip irrigation is best for row crops, orchards and vegetables where water savings and precise placement matter. Sprinklers are better for close-growing crops like wheat, barley and pasture. Micro-sprinklers work well for tree crops. Consider the cost. Drip tape is cheap but lasts one or two seasons. Drip line with built-in emitters lasts five to ten years. Sprinklers have higher pressure requirements but lower initial cost per hectare. Then design the zones. A zone is a group of laterals that operate together. Divide the field into zones based on crop type, soil type, elevation and the pump capacity. Each zone should have its own valve and pressure regulator. Never operate more zones than the pump can supply. Calculate the flow rate for each zone and compare it to the pump curve. If the total flow exceeds the pump capacity, you must reduce the zone size or upgrade the pump. Next, plan the filtration. For drip systems, install a sand filter followed by a screen or disk filter. For sprinkler systems, a screen filter is usually enough. Place the filter after the pump and before the mainline. Install pressure gauges before and after the filter so you can monitor clogging. A pressure difference of more than 0.5 bar means the filter needs cleaning. Then design the fertigation system. A simple venturi injector is cheap and works well for small farms. A dosing pump is more accurate for large farms. Always install a backflow prevention device to protect the water source. Never allow fertiliser to contaminate drinking water. Finally, create an irrigation schedule. This tells the farmer when to irrigate and for how long. Use the crop water requirement, the emitter flow rate and the number of emitters per plant to calculate the run time. For example, if a tomato plant needs four litres per day and has four emitters each delivering two litres per hour, you need to run the system for half an hour per day. But you must also account for rainfall and soil moisture. A simple tensiometer or a soil moisture probe can help. In Africa, many farmers over-irrigate or under-irrigate because they guess. Your schedule removes the guesswork. Document everything. Create a design report with a map of the field, the pipe layout, the zone layout, the pump specifications, the filter specifications and the irrigation schedule. Give a copy to the farmer and keep one for yourself. This report becomes your baseline for maintenance and troubleshooting. Remember that design is not a one-time event. As the farm grows or the crop changes, the system may need to be modified. A good technician revisits the design every season. In the next lesson we will cover installation best practices, including trenching, pipe joining, pump wiring and commissioning. Before you move on, practice a site assessment on a local farm. Measure the water source, test the soil, map the slope and calculate the peak water requirement. Then sketch a simple drip system for one hectare of tomatoes. Share your sketch with a fellow technician and ask for feedback. This practical exercise will build your confidence and your reputation. See you in Lesson 3.

3. Module 9 Lesson 3: Installation, Commissioning and Safety

Now that you have a design, you must install the system correctly. Poor installation causes more failures than poor design. In this lesson we cover trenching, pipe joining, pump installation, wiring, filter assembly, emitter placement and commissioning. We also cover safety, because irrigation work involves electricity, heavy pipes, open water and sometimes chemicals. Start with trenching. For buried mainlines, dig trenches at least forty centimetres deep for small pipes and sixty centimetres for large pipes. The trench bottom should be smooth and free of sharp rocks. In rocky soils, add a ten centimetre sand bedding. Slope the trench slightly so that water can drain and so that air can escape. Mark the trench with tape or stones to prevent damage from tractors or animals. In Africa, many farmers plough with oxen or tractors, so bury pipes deep enough to avoid the plough layer. For surface laterals, lay them along the crop rows without kinks or sharp bends. Use stakes or clips to hold them in place. Next, pipe joining. PVC pipes are joined with solvent cement. Cut the pipe square, deburr the edges, clean with primer, apply cement to both the pipe and the fitting, and push together with a quarter turn. Hold for thirty seconds. Do not twist after the cement sets. For HDPE pipes, use compression fittings or electrofusion. Compression fittings are easy but can leak if not tightened properly. Electrofusion requires a fusion machine and is more reliable for large diameters. For drip tape, use barbed connectors and figure eight end closures. Always flush the lines before installing emitters, because debris from construction can clog them. Pump installation is critical. For a submersible pump, lower it into the borehole using a strong rope or cable. Never use the electrical cable to support the pump. Install a non-return valve just above the pump to prevent backflow. For a surface pump, place it as close to the water source as possible to reduce suction lift. The suction pipe must be airtight and should have a foot valve with a strainer. Prime the pump before starting. For solar pumps, mount the panels facing the equator, tilted at an angle equal to your latitude. In Africa, that means facing north in the southern hemisphere and south in the northern hemisphere. Use a charge controller and a pump controller. Protect the panels from theft with a locked frame or fence. Wiring must be done by a qualified electrician. Use correct cable sizes to avoid voltage drop. For three-phase motors, check the rotation direction. For single-phase motors, check the capacitor. Install a circuit breaker and an earth leakage unit. Never work on live wires. Always switch off the power and lock the panel before maintenance. Filter assembly follows the pump. Install the sand filter first, then the screen or disk filter. The sand filter needs a backwash valve. Backwash weekly or when the pressure difference exceeds 0.5 bar. The screen filter needs a flush valve. Clean the screen daily in dirty water conditions. Install pressure gauges before and after each filter. Install air release valves at high points in the mainline. Install a pressure regulator at the head of each zone. Emitter placement depends on the crop. For row crops, place one emitter per plant or one emitter between two plants. For orchards, place two to four emitters per tree, depending on canopy size. For vegetables, use drip tape with emitters every twenty to thirty centimetres. Bury drip tape two to five centimetres deep to protect it from sunlight and animals. For sprinklers, space them according to the manufacturer recommendation, usually fifty to sixty percent of the wetted diameter. Ensure that sprinkler patterns overlap for uniform coverage. Commissioning is the final step. Before you start the pump, open all valves and flush the mainline and laterals. Then close the ends and pressurise the system slowly. Check for leaks. Check the pressure at the farthest emitter. It should be within ten percent of the design pressure. Check the flow rate at each zone. It should match the design flow. Adjust the pressure regulator if needed. Run the system for at least one hour and observe. Look for dry spots, puddles, clogged emitters and leaks. Fix all problems before you hand over to the farmer. Safety is not optional. Wear personal protective equipment: gloves, boots, eye protection and a dust mask when mixing cement or chemicals. When working with electricity, use insulated tools and rubber gloves. When working near water, be aware of drowning risk, especially in dams and rivers. When using fertigation, wear chemical resistant gloves and goggles. Never eat or drink while handling fertiliser. Store chemicals in a locked cabinet away from children and animals. In Africa, many accidents happen because technicians take shortcuts. Do not be one of them. Also consider security. Pumps, solar panels and copper cables are targets for theft. Install locks, fences and alarms. Bury cables in conduit. Mark equipment with the farmer name and phone number. Train the farmer and workers on basic operation and safety. Provide a simple manual with pictures. Show them how to clean filters, check pressure, flush laterals and shut down the system. Show them what to do in an emergency, such as a burst pipe or a pump failure. Keep spare parts on hand: emitters, connectors, filters, valves, fuses and a spare pump if possible. In remote areas, waiting for a spare part can cost a whole season. Finally, document the installation. Take photos of the pump, filters, valves and emitters. Record the pressure and flow readings. Write down the date of installation and the names of the installers. This documentation helps with warranty claims and future troubleshooting. In the next lesson we will cover maintenance and troubleshooting, which is where you will spend most of your time as a technician. Before you move on, practice installing a small drip system on a demonstration plot. Use a bucket, a small pump, a filter and ten metres of drip tape. Flush, pressurise and check for leaks. Then disassemble and reassemble. The more you practice, the faster and more confident you will become. See you in Lesson 4.

4. Module 9 Lesson 4: Maintenance, Troubleshooting and Repair

A well installed irrigation system still needs regular maintenance. In Africa, where dust, heat, insects and poor water quality are common, maintenance is the difference between a system that lasts ten years and one that fails in one season. This lesson covers routine maintenance, troubleshooting common problems and repairing components in the field. Start with a maintenance schedule. Daily tasks include checking the pump for unusual noise or vibration, checking the pressure gauges, cleaning the screen filter if the water is dirty, and inspecting the field for leaks or clogged emitters. Weekly tasks include backwashing the sand filter, flushing the laterals, checking the valves and inspecting the solar panels for dust or shade. Monthly tasks include checking the pump impeller for wear, testing the pressure regulator, checking the electrical connections and cleaning the tank if there is one. Seasonal tasks include replacing worn emitters, repairing leaks, testing the water quality and recalibrating the fertigation system. Keep a logbook. Record every maintenance activity, every repair and every observation. This logbook becomes your memory and your evidence of professional service. Now let us troubleshoot common problems. Problem one: low pressure at the emitters. Possible causes include a clogged filter, a leaking pipe, a worn pump impeller, a closed valve, or a pressure regulator set too low. Check the pressure gauge before and after the filter. If the difference is more than 0.5 bar, clean or backwash the filter. Check for leaks by walking the mainline and laterals. Listen for hissing sounds. Check the pump curve and compare with actual flow. If the pump is worn, replace the impeller or the pump. Problem two: uneven water distribution. Possible causes include pressure differences due to slope, clogged emitters, or different emitter flow rates. Check the pressure at the beginning and end of the lateral. If the difference is more than ten percent, install a pressure compensating emitter or divide the zone. Check emitters by removing them and inspecting for debris. Soak them in a weak acid solution if they are clogged with calcium carbonate. Use a chlorine solution if they are clogged with organic matter. Problem three: pump not starting. Possible causes include no power, a tripped circuit breaker, a faulty capacitor, a burned motor, or a faulty pressure switch. Check the power supply first. Use a multimeter to test voltage. Check the circuit breaker and reset if tripped. Test the capacitor with a multimeter. If the motor is burned, replace it or rewind it. For solar pumps, check the panels for shade, dust or damage. Check the controller for error codes. Problem four: water hammer. This is a loud banging noise in the pipes when a valve closes suddenly. It can burst pipes and damage fittings. Install air release valves at high points. Install a slow closing valve. Install a surge tank if needed. Problem five: algae growth in drip lines. This happens when water is stored in open tanks or when laterals are transparent. Use opaque pipes or bury the laterals. Add chlorine at a concentration of one to two parts per million. Flush the lines regularly. Problem six: rodent damage. Rats and mice chew drip tape and cables. Use metal conduit for cables. Bury drip tape deeper. Use traps or rodenticides carefully, keeping them away from children and animals. Problem seven: clogged emitters from silt. This is common in river water. Install a sand filter and a disk filter. Clean them often. Use a settling tank before the pump. Problem eight: leaking pipes. For PVC, cut out the damaged section and replace with a coupling. For HDPE, use a compression coupling or a fusion joint. For drip tape, use a barbed connector and a figure eight end. For a temporary fix, use a rubber sleeve and hose clamps. Always carry repair materials in your vehicle. Now let us talk about repair techniques. For a cracked pump casing, you can sometimes weld it if it is cast iron, but often it is better to replace it. For a burned motor, rewinding is possible in many African cities, but check the cost against a new pump. For a damaged solar panel, replace it with a panel of the same voltage and wattage. For a faulty controller, check the warranty before opening it. For a broken valve, replace the valve body or the handle. For a clogged filter, backwash or replace the filter element. Always use original spare parts when possible. In remote areas, you may need to improvise. For example, you can make a temporary filter from a cloth bag. You can repair a drip tape with a piece of bicycle inner tube and a clamp. You can use a car battery to test a pump motor. But never compromise on safety. If you are not sure, ask for help. Now let us discuss water quality testing. Test for pH, salinity, iron, manganese and bacteria. High iron causes brown staining and clogging. High salinity damages crops. Bacteria form slime that clogs emitters. Test at least once per season. Send samples to a laboratory if possible. If not, use a portable meter. Adjust the system accordingly. For high iron, use aeration and settling. For high salinity, leach the soil with extra water. For bacteria, chlorinate. Finally, train the farmer to do daily checks. A farmer who cleans the filter every day will save you a repair call every week. A farmer who reports a leak immediately will prevent a major blowout. A farmer who keeps a logbook will help you diagnose problems faster. Your job is not just to fix things but to build the farmer capacity to maintain the system. In the next lesson we will cover business skills, because as an irrigation technician you are also a businessperson. Before you move on, create a troubleshooting flowchart for the ten most common problems. Laminate it and keep it in your toolkit. Practice repairing a drip tape, a PVC pipe and a pump capacitor. Time yourself. The faster you are, the more farms you can serve. See you in Lesson 5.

5. Module 9 Lesson 5: Business Skills, Customer Service and Career Growth

You now have the technical skills to design, install, maintain and repair irrigation systems. But technical skills alone will not pay your bills. You must also run your work as a business. This final lesson covers pricing, customer service, record keeping, marketing, and career growth for the African irrigation technician. Start with pricing. Many technicians undercharge because they do not know their costs. Calculate your direct costs: fuel, transport, spare parts, tools, phone airtime and labour. Calculate your indirect costs: vehicle maintenance, insurance, training, office expenses and taxes. Add a profit margin of at least twenty percent. Then quote a price that covers everything. Do not compete only on price. Compete on quality, reliability and speed. A farmer will pay more for a technician who answers the phone, arrives on time and fixes the problem the first time. Offer different service packages. A basic package might include a site visit, a system check and a written report. A premium package might include a full design, installation supervision and a one year maintenance contract. Maintenance contracts are gold. They give you recurring income and they keep the system working. Charge a monthly or seasonal fee. Visit the farm at least once per month. Clean filters, check pressures, flush lines and train the farmer. This prevents emergencies and builds trust. Next, customer service. In Africa, word of mouth is the most powerful marketing. A happy farmer tells ten neighbours. An unhappy farmer tells everyone. So treat every customer with respect. Listen to their problems. Do not interrupt. Repeat back what you heard to confirm. Explain what you will do and how much it will cost. Give a written quote. Do not start work until the customer agrees. Arrive on time. If you are delayed, call ahead. Keep your vehicle and tools clean. Wear a uniform or a branded shirt. This shows professionalism. After the job, ask the customer to inspect the work with you. Show them what you did. Show them how to maintain it. Give them a written report with photos. Ask for feedback. If there is a problem, fix it immediately. Do not argue. Your reputation is worth more than any single job. Record keeping is essential. Keep a customer database with names, phone numbers, farm locations, crop types, system details and service history. Use a notebook or a simple spreadsheet on your phone. Record every sale, every expense and every payment. Track your income and expenses monthly. Know your profit. Pay your taxes. Register your business if required. Open a bank account. Keep personal and business money separate. This discipline will help you grow. Marketing can be simple and cheap. Create a business card with your name, phone number and services. Give it to every farmer you meet. Ask for referrals. Offer a small discount to customers who refer a new client. Join farmer groups and cooperatives. Attend agricultural shows and field days. Give a free demonstration of drip irrigation. Post before and after photos on social media if you have internet. Use WhatsApp groups for farmers in your area. Share tips about irrigation scheduling. Become the go to expert. Build relationships with agricultural extension officers, agro dealers and pump suppliers. They can refer customers to you. They can also provide training and spare parts. Consider offering a mobile service. Many farmers cannot transport a pump to a workshop. If you have a vehicle or a motorcycle, you can go to them. Charge a call out fee. This is common in many African countries. Be transparent about the fee. Now let us talk about career growth. You can grow in several directions. You can become a specialist in solar pumping. Solar is the fastest growing segment in Africa. Get certified by a reputable training provider. Learn to size solar arrays, install controllers and troubleshoot. You can become a designer. Learn computer aided design software. Offer design services to NGOs, government projects and commercial farms. You can become a trainer. Teach other technicians. Write a manual. Run workshops. You can become a supplier. Stock and sell drip tape, filters, valves and pumps. You can become a contractor. Bid for large irrigation projects. Hire a team. Manage multiple sites. You can also work for an NGO or a government agency. Many organisations need irrigation technicians for food security projects. Whatever path you choose, keep learning. Technology changes. New emitters, new pumps, new controllers and new methods appear every year. Read manuals. Watch videos. Attend training. Join professional associations. Network with other technicians. Share your knowledge. In Africa, the demand for irrigation is growing because of climate change, population growth and the need for year round food production. A skilled irrigation technician is a valuable person. You can help farmers double or triple their yields. You can help communities become food secure. You can build a successful business. But remember that success takes time. Be patient. Be honest. Be reliable. Treat every farm as if it were your own. If you do these things, you will never lack work. Finally, let us review the whole module. In Lesson 1 you learned the components. In Lesson 2 you learned site assessment and design. In Lesson 3 you learned installation and safety. In Lesson 4 you learned maintenance and troubleshooting. In Lesson 5 you learned business and customer service. Together these five lessons give you the foundation to work as a professional irrigation technician. But the real learning happens in the field. So go out there. Find a farmer. Offer to check their system. Do a good job. Ask for a testimonial. Use that testimonial to get the next customer. Repeat. Before you finish this module, complete a final project. Choose a real farm. Do a full site assessment. Design a system. Install a small demonstration plot. Maintain it for one month. Keep records. Write a report. Present it to your instructor or your peers. This project will prove that you are ready to work. Congratulations on completing Module 9. I wish you success in your career. Remember that water is life. Your work brings water to crops, income to farmers and food to communities. Do it with pride and professionalism. See you in the next module.

This practical reference lesson consolidates the numbers, tables and checklists an irrigation technician needs on site. It is designed to be printed, laminated and carried in the toolbox. ⚠ SAFETY WARNING: CHEMICAL AND WATERBORNE HAZARDS

Irrigation technicians frequently handle chlorine, acids, fertilisers and pesticides for system cleaning and fertigation. These chemicals can cause severe skin burns, eye damage and respiratory injury. WHO guidelines for the safe use of wastewater and excreta in agriculture (WHO, 2006) require that any water used on edible crops meets microbiological standards, and that workers wear gloves, eye protection and respirators when handling treatment chemicals. Never mix chlorine with acid — toxic chlorine gas is released. Always flush lines before fertigation and store chemicals in labelled, locked containers away from children and livestock. If chemical contact occurs, rinse with clean water for 15 minutes and seek medical help immediately. In malaria-endemic areas, standing water in tanks and furrows can breed mosquitoes; cover tanks and drain stagnant pools weekly.

Quick Reference Checklist for Field Visits

  1. Before leaving the workshop: confirm you have pressure gauge, flow meter, TDS/EC meter, pH strips, pipe cutter, spare emitters, fittings, filter screens, chlorine test kit, gloves, goggles and a first-aid kit.
  2. At the water source: record source type, static water level, pump make/model, suction condition and any visible contamination.
  3. At the pump and filter: measure inlet and outlet pressure; a drop greater than 0.5 bar across the filter means it needs cleaning or replacement.
  4. Along the mainline: walk the full length; check for leaks, exposed pipe, damaged valves and illegal connections.
  5. At the laterals: measure emitter flow at the first, middle and last emitter on three laterals; variation above 20% indicates a pressure or clogging problem.
  6. In the root zone: dig a small profile to check wetted depth; for most vegetables, target 30–60 cm. Adjust run time accordingly.
  7. With the farmer: agree on a maintenance schedule (daily visual check, weekly filter flush, monthly chlorination, seasonal acid treatment) and write it on a laminated card.
  8. Before leaving: photograph the installation, record all readings in the job sheet, and quote any recommended repairs with parts and labour separated.

Business-First Notes: Turning Diagnostics into Revenue

In the African agribusiness context, the irrigation technician is not only a repair person but a service business. Each field visit should generate at least one of three revenue streams: a maintenance contract, a repair job, or a design/upgrade consultation. FAO estimates that irrigated horticulture in sub-Saharan Africa can generate gross margins two to four times higher than rainfed staple crops (FAO, 2019). A technician who can demonstrate a 30% water saving and a 20% yield increase through proper maintenance can justify a service fee of KES 3,000–8,000 per visit, or an annual contract of KES 25,000–60,000 per hectare. ILRI studies in Kenya and Ethiopia show that farmers who receive regular technical support are 40% more likely to continue using improved irrigation technology (ILRI, 2018). This is the business case for structured after-sales service.

Pricing should cover: travel (KES 500–2,000), labour (KES 1,000–3,000 per day), consumables (10–20% markup), and profit margin (20–30%). Always provide a written quote with a validity period of 14 days. For larger installations, offer a phased payment plan: 50% deposit, 30% on delivery of materials, 20% on commissioning. This reduces cash-flow risk for both technician and farmer.
