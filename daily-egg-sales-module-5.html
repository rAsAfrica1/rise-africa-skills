1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Housing is the foundation of a profitable layer flock. In Africa, where temperatures can soar above 35C and rain can flood open runs, a well-designed chicken house keeps your birds healthy, calm, and laying steadily. If hens are stressed by heat, cold, drafts, or predators, they stop laying or produce thin-shelled eggs. That means lost income every single day. A good house also protects your investment from theft and wild birds that carry disease.

In this module, you will learn how to build or adapt a simple, low-cost house that works in your climate. We focus on the tropics and subtropics: use local materials like mud bricks, timber offcuts, bamboo, thatch, and corrugated iron sheets. You do not need expensive equipment. What you need is correct orientation, adequate space, good ventilation, and strong security.

Consider these real numbers: a flock of 100 layers needs about 20 square metres of floor space inside the house (0.2 m per bird). If you keep them in a crowded space, they will peck each other, eat less, and lay fewer eggs. In hot regions, the house should face east-west so the long side avoids the midday sun. The roof should overhang by at least 60 cm to block sun and rain. The floor should be raised 2030 cm above ground level to prevent flooding and dampness.

Why does this matter for daily egg sales? Because consistent lay means consistent supply. Customershotels, shops, and neighbourswant eggs every day. If your hens stop laying for a week due to heat stress or a predator attack, you lose those sales and your reputation. A proper house is not a one-time cost; it is the engine of your daily income. In the next lessons, you will gather tools, build step by step, avoid common mistakes, and practice with a real exercise. By the end, you will have a house that keeps your hens laying all year round.

2. Tools and Materials

To build a layer house for 100 birds, you need basic tools and materials that are available in most African towns or can be improvised. Gather these before you start:

Tools: hand saw, hammer, tape measure (5 m), spirit level (or a clear plastic tube filled with water), shovel, hoe, machete, pliers, wheelbarrow, and a carpenter's square. A power drill is helpful but not essential. For roofing, you will need a ladder and a roofing hammer. If you are mixing concrete, add a bucket and a trowel.

Materials for a 20 m house (4 m x 5 m):
- 8 wooden posts (10 cm x 10 cm x 2.4 m) for corners and mid-walls. Use treated timber or local hardwood like mvule or iroko.
- 20 rafters (5 cm x 7.5 cm x 3 m) for the roof frame.
- 12 corrugated iron sheets (2 m long) or 30 bundles of thatch for roofing.
- 4 rolls of chicken wire (1 m high, 10 m long) with 2.5 cm mesh for walls.
- 2 kg of 50 mm nails, 1 kg of roofing nails with rubber washers.
- 6 hinges and 4 latches for doors and windows.
- 10 bags of sand, 3 bags of cement, and 20 litres of water for a concrete floor (optional but recommended).
- 1 plastic drinker (10 L) and 1 feeder (10 kg capacity) per 50 birds.
- 2 bags of wood shavings or dry grass for litter.

For a low-cost version, use bamboo poles for rafters, mud bricks for walls (leaving openings for ventilation), and thatch for roofing. In East Africa, many farmers use eucalyptus poles and makuti thatch. In West Africa, raffia palm or zinc sheets are common. Always treat timber against termites by painting used engine oil or applying a borate solution.

Budget estimate: In Kenya, a 100-bird house costs about KES 25,00035,000 (USD 200280) if you buy new materials. Using local materials can cut that to KES 10,000. Do not skimp on wire mesh or nailspredators like snakes and mongooses can push through weak walls. Buy from a reputable agro-vet shop to avoid poor-quality wire that rusts quickly. Store cement and nails in a dry place before building.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps to build a 4 m x 5 m layer house for 100 hens. Work with two or three people to make it easier.

Step 1: Choose the site. Pick a spot that is slightly raised, well-drained, and shaded by trees (but not directly under branches that could fall). The house should face east-west so the long side gets morning sun and afternoon shade. Keep it at least 10 m away from other poultry houses to reduce disease spread.

Step 2: Mark the foundation. Use pegs and string to mark a rectangle 4 m by 5 m. Check corners with a tape measure: diagonals should be equal (6.4 m each). Dig holes 50 cm deep at each corner and every 2 m along the walls. Place posts in holes, fill with concrete (1 part cement: 3 parts sand: 4 parts gravel) or rammed earth. Ensure posts are vertical using a spirit level. Let concrete cure for 3 days.

Step 3: Build the floor. If using concrete, mix 1:3:5 (cement:sand:gravel) and pour a 5 cm slab. Slope it slightly (1 cm per metre) towards a drainage channel outside. For a cheaper floor, spread a 10 cm layer of compacted gravel, then cover with dry grass litter. Raise the floor 20 cm above ground by building a low plinth of stones or bricks.

Step 4: Erect walls. Nail chicken wire (1 m high) around the posts, leaving 30 cm gaps at the bottom for a solid wall of mud bricks or timber boards up to 30 cm high. This stops drafts and predators. Leave a 60 cm wide opening on the east side for a door and another on the west for a window. Cover window with wire mesh. For extra ventilation in hot climates, leave a 30 cm gap between the top of the wire and the roof, covered with wire mesh.

Step 5: Put on the roof. Fix rafters every 60 cm, sloping from the centre ridge to the eaves. Overhang the roof by 60 cm on all sides. Nail corrugated sheets or thatch. Ensure no leakswet litter causes disease. Add a ridge cap.

Step 6: Install fittings. Hang the door with hinges and a latch. Place feeders and drinkers evenlyone drinker per 50 birds, one feeder per 25 birds. Add perches: 20 cm per bird, made from 5 cm x 5 cm timber, placed 60 cm above floor and 30 cm apart. Finally, spread 5 cm of wood shavings or dry grass as litter. Your house is ready after 1 week of airing to remove smells.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Even experienced farmers make mistakes when building layer houses. Here are the most common ones in Africa and how to fix them.

Mistake 1: Too small space. Many farmers crowd 200 birds into a 20 m house. This causes pecking, reduced laying, and disease. Fix: Provide at least 0.2 m per bird inside and 0.3 m in the run. For 100 birds, build 20 m. If you already have a small house, reduce flock size or extend the house by adding a lean-to.

Mistake 2: Poor ventilation. In hot climates, a closed house becomes an oven. Hens pant, eat less, and stop laying. Fix: Leave open spaces covered with wire mesh at least 30 cm high along the top of walls. Ensure cross-ventilationopenings on opposite sides. Add a ridge vent in the roof. Never block all openings to keep the house warm at night; instead, use curtains that can be rolled up during the day.

Mistake 3: Roof leaks. A leaky roof wets the litter, causing ammonia build-up and respiratory disease. Fix: Overlap roofing sheets by 15 cm, nail with rubber washers, and repair holes immediately. For thatch, add a new layer every year before the rainy season.

Mistake 4: Weak predator protection. Snakes, mongooses, and hawks kill chicks and hens. Fix: Bury wire mesh 30 cm into the ground around the perimeter. Use strong 2.5 cm mesh, not chicken wire with large holes. Cover windows and vents with wire. Lock doors at night.

Mistake 5: No perches. Hens naturally roost at night. Without perches, they sleep on the floor in droppings, getting dirty eggs and disease. Fix: Install wooden perches 5 cm wide, 60 cm above floor, 30 cm apart, allowing 20 cm per bird. Make them removable for cleaning.

Mistake 6: Concrete floor too smooth. Smooth floors become slippery when wet, causing leg injuries. Fix: Brush the surface before it dries to create a rough texture, or add a thin layer of sand.

Mistake 7: Wrong orientation. A house facing north-south in the tropics gets full sun on the long side, overheating. Fix: Orient east-west. If you cannot, plant shade trees on the sunny side or add a shade cloth over the roof.

Mistake 8: Using toxic materials. Treated wood with creosote can poison birds. Fix: Use only borate-treated timber or natural rot-resistant woods like neem. Let any paint or treatment dry completely before introducing birds.

Fix these mistakes earlythey cost little but save your flock and your daily egg income.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Building and maintaining a layer house involves safety risks. Follow these practices to protect yourself, your workers, and your birds.

Personal safety during construction: Wear gloves when handling wire mesh and timber. Use goggles when cutting or hammering. Wear strong boots to avoid nail punctures. When working on the roof, use a sturdy ladder and have someone hold it. Never work alone in hot suntake breaks and drink water. If using power tools, keep them dry and check cables for damage.

Bird safety and biosecurity: Keep the house clean and dry. Remove wet litter daily and replace with fresh shavings. Clean drinkers and feeders every morning with soap and water, then rinse. Disinfect the house every 3 months with a 1% bleach solution (1 part bleach to 50 parts water). Quarantine new birds for 2 weeks before adding them to the flock. Limit visitorsthey can carry disease on shoes. Provide a footbath with disinfectant at the entrance.

Structural safety: Check posts for termite damage every 6 months. Replace weak posts immediately. Ensure the roof is secure before the rainy season. Fix any loose wire mesh that could trap a bird's leg. Keep the door latch strong enough to stop thieves but easy for you to open. Do not store chemicals, fuels, or tools inside the chicken house.

Best practices for consistent lay: Maintain a routine. Feed and water at the same times daily (e.g., 7 AM and 4 PM). Collect eggs at least twice a daymorning and eveningto prevent breakage and dirty eggs. Keep the house at a comfortable temperature: below 30C. In extreme heat, wet the roof or floor to cool it. In cold highlands, add curtains at night. Provide 1416 hours of light per day; if natural light is short, use a solar lamp for 2 hours at dusk.

Record keeping: Keep a simple notebook. Write down daily egg count, feed used, deaths, and any repairs. This helps you spot problems early. For example, if egg production drops by 10% in one week, check for heat, mites, or a draft. Finally, train anyone who helps you. Show them how to handle birds gently, clean properly, and report anything unusual. A safe, well-run house means happy hens and steady egg sales.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it is time to apply what you have learned. This exercise will guide you to plan and build a small model or a real house section. You can do it alone or with a group.

Exercise 1: Site assessment. Visit your proposed site. Measure the area (length and width). Check for: shade (trees), drainage (does water pool?), wind direction, and distance to neighbours. Write down your findings. Draw a simple sketch of the site, marking north. Decide the best orientation for your house (east-west). Take a photo if you have a phone.

Exercise 2: Materials list and budget. Using the tools and materials lesson, make a list for a house that fits your flock size. For example, if you have 50 birds, you need 10 m (2 m x 5 m). Adjust quantities. Visit a local agro-vet or hardware shop and price each item. Write the total. Compare with your budget. If too expensive, identify three ways to reduce cost (e.g., use bamboo, thatch, or second-hand timber).

Exercise 3: Build a scale model. Use cardboard, sticks, and string to build a 1:10 scale model of your planned house. Include walls, roof, door, window, and perches. This helps you visualise the design and spot problems before building full-size. Show it to a neighbour or extension officer for feedback.

Exercise 4: Construct a real section. If you are ready, build one wall section or a small pen (2 m x 2 m) for 10 birds. Follow the step-by-step method. Use local materials. After completion, check: Is the wire mesh secure? Does the roof leak? Are there gaps for ventilation? Fix any issues.

Exercise 5: Daily monitoring plan. Write a simple checklist for the first week after birds move in. Include: morning check (feed, water, egg count), afternoon check (temperature, litter dryness), evening check (lock doors, count birds). Record any problems and how you fixed them.

Submit your work: Take photos of your model or real house, your materials list, and your checklist. Share with your trainer or group. Discuss what was easy and what was hard. This practical experience is the best way to learn. Remember, a well-built house is the backbone of daily egg sales. Start small, do it right, and expand as your flock grows.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Housing for Consistent Lay in African Layer Flocks

In a daily egg sales operation, housing is not a construction project it is a revenue-protection tool. Every egg you sell depends on a hen that feels safe, cool, dry, and undisturbed. In most African layer systems, poor housing is the single largest cause of drop in lay, cracked eggs, and mortality. This Practical Reference lesson gives you the numbers, dimensions, and decisions you need to build or upgrade housing that keeps your flock laying consistently and keeps your egg sales predictable.

Use this module as a field manual. Keep it beside you when you plan a new pen, when you buy materials, and when you inspect your houses each week. The goal is simple: a hen that is comfortable lays an egg almost every day; a hen that is stressed stops laying, and your daily sales collapse.

Why Housing Drives Daily Egg Sales

Layer hens are sensitive to heat, crowding, draughts, and predators. In sub-Saharan Africa, the biggest housing pressure is heat. When ambient temperature rises above 30C, feed intake drops, egg size falls, and shell quality weakens. When night temperatures stay above 25C, hens cannot recover from daytime heat stress, and lay rate falls the next day. Good housing buffers these shocks.

Housing also controls disease. A dry floor, good ventilation, and clean drinkers reduce ammonia, respiratory disease, and coccidiosis. Fewer sick birds means fewer missed eggs and lower medicine costs both directly improve your profit per bird per day.

Finally, housing protects eggs. Nest boxes that are dark, clean, and private reduce floor eggs, dirty eggs, and cracked eggs. In a daily egg sales business, a cracked egg is a lost sale, not a discounted sale. Housing quality is therefore a direct revenue decision.

SAFETY WARNING: Heat Stress and Predator Risk

Heat stress kills layers fast. In many African regions, afternoon temperatures inside poorly ventilated houses exceed 35C. At this level, hens pant, stop eating, and can die within hours. Never house layers in a closed, low-roofed building without ridge ventilation and open side walls. Provide shade trees, roof insulation (thatch or reflective sheet), and cool drinking water at all times.

Predators and theft are business risks. Snakes, mongooses, wild cats, and rodents enter through gaps. Use 1 cm wire mesh on all openings, bury the perimeter fence 30 cm deep, and lock the house at night. A single predator attack can wipe out weeks of egg revenue.

Never use untreated timber or old battery cages that may carry disease or sharp edges. Disinfect all second-hand materials before use.

Quick Reference Checklist: Housing for Consistent Lay

  1. Space: Confirm at least 0.15 m per bird on deep litter; reduce flock if overstocked.
  2. Ventilation: Open side walls on at least two sides; ridge vent at the roof peak; no draughts at bird level.
  3. Roof: Insulated or thatched roof with 60 cm overhang to block direct sun and rain.
  4. Floor: Dry, 10 cm deep litter; remove wet patches weekly; never let ammonia rise above 20 ppm.
  5. Nest boxes: One 30 30 35 cm box per 5 hens, dark and clean, lined with soft dry material.
  6. Feeders and drinkers: 1012 cm feeder space and 2.5 cm drinker space per bird; clean drinkers daily.
  7. Light: 1416 hours of light per day; use a low-watt bulb or natural daylight plus timer in dark seasons.
  8. Security: 1 cm mesh on all openings, buried perimeter fence, locked door at night, and a simple record of any predator sightings.
