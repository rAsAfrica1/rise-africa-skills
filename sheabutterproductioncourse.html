6-Month Business Seed Curriculum

MODULE 1: Shea Tree Identification & Sustainable Harvesting (Month 1)

  1. Shea Tree (Vitellaria paradoxa) Basics — Native to Sahel belt of West/Central Africa (Mali, Burkina Faso, Ghana, Côte d'Ivoire, Nigeria, Cameroon, Niger), grows wild and cultivated, lifespan 200-300 years, mature height 15-25m, produces nuts from year 15-20 onwards
  2. Tree Identification & Scouting — Locate wild shea trees on communal lands, identify ripe fruit (green to brown-yellow skin, 1.5-3cm oval nut inside), look for established gathering sites in your area, map productive tree locations, note tree density (5-50 trees/hectare in wild savanna)
  3. Nut Production Cycle — Flowering March-April (in Sahel), fruit development May-August, nut maturation August-October, natural drop September-November, peak harvest September-November (4-6 month window), yield 10-40kg nuts/tree/year depending on climate/age
  4. Sustainable Harvesting Practices — Never cut/damage trees (they regenerate for centuries), collect fallen nuts only, respect community harvesting agreements, avoid over-harvesting from single trees (rotate collection), train collectors to not damage branches reaching for nuts, document collection by tree to track productivity
  5. Harvest Logistics & Transport — Collection buckets/bags (25kg capacity each), gloves for hand sorting, drying mats or tarp (transport to storage), transport to processing site within 5-7 days of collection (mold prevention), storage in cool dry place (moisture <12%)
  6. Collection Economics — Hired collectors paid $0.30-0.60/kg nuts collected (or flat $5-8/day), household collection: 10-20kg per person per day during peak season (15-30 people needed to collect from 100 trees), total harvest 1000-4000kg nuts from 100 productive trees across 4-month season
Module 1 Quiz (10 questions): Shea tree lifespan, native regions, harvest season timeline, nut yield range per tree, identification of ripe nut, collection period (months), sustainable harvesting rules, collector payment rate, transport timeline post-collection, mold prevention humidity level.

MODULE 2: Nut Drying & Storage (Month 2)

  1. Post-Harvest Handling (First 7 Days) — Sort fresh nuts (remove debris, damaged nuts), rinse if muddy (optional, adds drying time), spread on drying ground or raised drying beds, initial sun-drying 3-5 days (nuts darken, kernel inside hardens), stir every 1-2 days for even drying
  2. Drying Methods & Timeline — Sun-drying (7-10 days to target <12% moisture, cheapest but weather-dependent), shade-drying in bags (14-21 days, slower but prevents cracking), kiln-drying (2-3 days, $2-5/kg cost, only for commercial scale), target final moisture 8-12% for storage and processing
  3. Moisture Content Testing — Simple hand test: dry nut should be hard and rattle inside shell, no mold smell, professional moisture meter if available (aim for 8-12% MC), over-dried nuts (<8%MC) become brittle (kernel losses in cracking), under-dried (>12%MC) develop mold and aflatoxin risk
  4. Drying Infrastructure Setup — Drying ground or raised beds (40m × 6m handles ~2-3 tonnes nuts), tarpaulin covers (rain protection, cost $100-150), shelter structure with airflow (prevent mold), labour: 1-2 people monitoring/stirring daily
  5. Storage & Preservation — Dried nuts stored in jute bags (25kg each), stacked on pallets (avoid ground moisture), well-ventilated storage (24-28°C optimal), protected from rodents and insects (stock must be monitored weekly), shelf life dried nuts 12-18 months if stored properly
  6. Batch Tracking & Quality Assurance — Label each bag (date, tree origin if tracking, quantity), separate by harvest lot (quality may vary), document drying timeline and moisture readings, reject any lot showing mold (must be burned, not sold), keep drying records for traceability
Module 2 Quiz (10 questions): Drying timeline sun-dry days, target moisture %, hand moisture test, over-dried consequences, mold prevention humidity, drying infrastructure size, storage temperature, batch labeling importance, shelf life dried nuts, aflatoxin risk factors.

MODULE 3: Kernel Extraction & Nut Cracking (Month 3)

  1. Nut Cracking Methods — Hand cracking (stone/hammer, labour-intensive, 20-40 nuts/hour per person, risk of kernel damage), manual vise/lever press (~$50-100, 50-80 nuts/hour, better control), mechanical drum sheller (~$1500-3000, 200-400 nuts/hour, commercial scale), hydraulic press (~$2000-4000, 300+ nuts/hour)
  2. Cracking Technique & Kernel Extraction — Shell thickness varies (3-8mm), strike along equatorial seam for clean break, extract kernel with spoon/knife (minimize breakage), kernels range 10-20g per nut, dried kernel weight ~8-15% of total nut weight (1kg nuts yields ~100-120g dried kernel)
  3. Hand Cracking Economics (Small Scale) — Cost: 1 person cracks 30 nuts/hour (2-hour shift = 60 nuts), from 1 tonne dried nuts (3000 nuts) = 50 hours labour, cost $50-100 labour (depending on wage), yields ~300-360kg kernels, operator can process own harvest or take commissions from community
  4. Kernel Quality & Sorting — Good kernel: cream/tan colour, no mold, hard/dry, whole or large pieces, bad kernels: dark, soft, mold spots, rejected, loss rate 5-15% (damage during cracking or pre-existing rot)
  5. Kernel Drying & Storage (Pre-Pressing) — Freshly cracked kernels contain 8-12% moisture, spread on drying mats 2-3 days for final moisture 6-8%, store in jute bags in dry place, lasts 6-12 months if kept dry and cool, ready for pressing into butter
  6. Small-Scale Setup Investment — Manual cracking stones: $10-30, vise/press: $50-150, drying mats: $50-80, storage bags (50 pieces): $30-40, small facility (20m×10m): $200-500 rent/setup, total modest startup $340-900 for hand or semi-mechanical operation
Module 3 Quiz (10 questions): Hand cracking rate (nuts/hour), shell thickness range, kernel weight per nut, kernel yield from 1kg nuts, nut cracking along seam, kernel quality signs, rejection criteria, kernel moisture target %, storage lifespan, labour cost per tonne cracked.

MODULE 4: Shea Butter Extraction & Refining (Month 4)

  1. Traditional Cooking & Hand-Pounding Method — Kernels roasted 30-45 minutes (releases oils, develops flavour, colour becomes darker), cooled slightly, ground by hand (mortar and pestle or stone grinder, 5-10kg takes 2-3 hours with 2-3 people), kneading by hand in water to separate oil from solids, cream rises to surface
  2. Oil Recovery by Traditional Method — Yield: 40-48% oil from raw kernel weight (e.g., 100kg kernels → 40-48kg crude shea butter), water-soluble milk used in traditional cooking, residue (press cake) used as animal feed or fertiliser, labour-intensive but produces premium artisanal butter
  3. Mechanical Pressing Method — Roasted kernels fed into screw press (electric or manual), first cold-press yields pale yellow oil 45-50%, residue re-pressed yields darker oil, total recovery 45-52%, faster processing (50-100kg/day small press), mechanical butter quality consistent
  4. Butter Purification & Clarification — Crude shea butter (contains 2-5% water, fine particles, plant debris), gentle heating (70-80°C, NOT hot) to melt and settle impurities 12-24 hours, decant clear butter into clean containers, strain through muslin cloth if needed, final product pure ivory/cream coloured butter
  5. Butter Grading & Quality Standards — Grade A (premium): pale yellow, smooth, >99.5% pure, <0.5% water, FFA <2%, used for cosmetics/high-end food, price $8-12/kg, Grade B (commercial): slightly darker, 98-99% pure, <1% water, FFA <3%, used for cooking, price $5-7/kg, rejected: mold, rancid smell, high FFA >5%
  6. Processing Yield & Economics Example —
    Input: 100kg dried kernels (6-8% moisture)
    Mechanical press yield: 48kg crude shea butter (48% recovery)
    After clarification: 47kg pure shea butter (1kg loss to impurities = 2%)
    Cost: labour $5, fuel/utilities $3, materials $2 = $10 processing cost
    Revenue: 47kg × $7/kg (commercial grade) = $329, minus $10 cost = $319 profit (gross margin ~97%)
Module 4 Quiz (10 questions): Roasting temperature & time, hand-pounding labour hours, oil recovery % by method, clarification temperature, butter grading criteria, FFA content standard, Grade A price, Grade B price, yield loss to impurities %, total labour cost per 100kg input.

MODULE 5: Packaging, Value-Addition & Market Quality (Month 5)

  1. Packaging & Storage Options — Bulk containers: 20L jerry cans (~$3 each, holds 20kg butter at 1.0 density), 5-gallon buckets (~$2, holds 4.5kg), food-grade drums (200L ~$30, holds 200kg, export-ready), small retail: 250g plastic tubs (~$0.15, premium appearance), 1kg jars (~$0.30, value-added presentation)
  2. Labeling & Branding — Legal requirements: product name, ingredients (100% shea butter or additives listed), weight/volume, producer name/address, batch number, manufacturing & best-before date, country of origin, contact info, storage instructions (cool dry place, use within 12-24 months)
  3. Value-Added Products (Premium Markets) —
    — Scented shea butter (add essential oils: 1-2% lavender, rosemary, shea-vanilla blend, price +50% premium)
    — Whipped shea butter (blend with oils/butters, lighter texture, price +40% premium)
    — Cosmetic creams (mix with beeswax, essential oils, cosmetic emulsifier, price +100-200% premium)
    — Soap production (saponification with lye, gentle bars, niche market, price +80% premium)
  4. Quality Testing & Food Safety — Moisture: <1% in finished product (use moisture meter), Free Fatty Acids (FFA): <2% using titration test kit (~$50 one-time investment), Microbial testing: contract with lab if exporting (pathogenic bacteria, aflatoxin screening, $20-50 per sample), shelf stability at room temperature 24 months minimum
  5. Export Quality Compliance — Cosmetic grade (ISO 9001, HACCP if available), organic certification (if using traditional/organic methods, 2-3 year documentation), fat composition analysis (palmitic ~27%, stearic ~45%, oleic ~21%, linoleic ~7% - standard for shea butter), testing cost ~$100-300 per batch if certifying for export
  6. Pricing Strategy & Market Segments —
    — Bulk wholesale: $4-6/kg (to distributors, minimum 50kg orders)
    — Local retail: $7-9/kg (in containers to local markets, beauty shops)
    — Value-add retail: $12-18/kg (branded, scented, cosmetic-grade, online/boutique)
    — Premium export: $15-25/kg (certified organic/cosmetic, small orders, international retailers)
Module 5 Quiz (10 questions): Labeling requirements, packaging container options, value-add scent premium %, whipped butter profit margin, soap production base, moisture % target finished product, FFA standard limit, microbial testing purpose, export certification types, wholesale pricing tier.

MODULE 6: Business Planning & Scaling Shea Production (Month 6)

  1. Full Business Model Development — Executive summary: buying shea nuts from harvesters vs. harvesting yourself, processing to butter, selling wholesale or value-added retail, target market (local/national/export), time to profitability (can be immediate if buying dried nuts)
  2. Capital & Operating Cost Summary —
    Setup (one-time): Cracking equipment $50-300 + press $200-500 + drying mats $50 + storage/processing space $200-500 = $500-1300 total startup
    Operating (per 100kg kernels): Raw kernel cost $150-200 + labour $15-25 (cracking, pressing, clarification) + utilities $5-10 + packaging $5-10 = $175-245 input
    Output: 47kg premium shea butter = $329-470 revenue (at $7-10/kg), gross profit $84-295, margin 48-60%
  3. Volume & Scaling Path — Start small: buy 50-100kg dried shea nuts/month (local market), learn processing, build local buyer base, scale to 500kg/month once workflow efficient and buyers secure, eventually 2-5 tonnes/month at full commercial scale with hired help
  4. Supplier Relationships & Cost Control — Source dried kernels from local collectors (cheaper than whole nuts), negotiate bulk discounts (5% reduction at 500kg+, 10% at 2000kg+), contract with steady suppliers to avoid price spikes, build reputation as reliable buyer for quality kernels
  5. Market Development Strategy —
    Phase 1 (Months 1-3): Sell locally via markets, beauty shops, restaurants (wholesale $5-6/kg, build reputation for quality)
    Phase 2 (Months 4-6): Develop value-added scented/creamed products, online presence (Etsy, social media, whatsapp business)
    Phase 3 (Months 7-12): Target organic certification or cosmetic-grade testing, explore export opportunities, negotiate with regional distributors
  6. Risk Management & Sustainability — Harvest sustainability: never damage trees, rotate collection sites, support reforestation if possible (shea trees declining in many regions), crop pricing risk: lock in purchases with community harvesters early season, quality control: regular FFA and moisture testing, contingency: maintain 1-month buffer stock in case supply disrupted
Module 6 Quiz (10 questions): Startup equipment cost, operating cost per 100kg, gross profit margin %, monthly volume target year 1, kernel supplier cost, wholesale price per kg, retail value-add price, scaling timeline, certification benefit to price, sustainability concern for shea trees.

Final Business Seed Project: Your Shea Butter Production Business Plan

Due at end of course (Month 6)

  1. Executive Summary (1 page): Your shea butter business vision. Processing scale (monthly kg target), market focus (wholesale, retail, value-add, export), timeline to first sale and profitability (can be rapid if buying processed inputs).
  2. Market Analysis (2 pages): Research shea butter prices and demand in your region. Identify 3-5 buyer categories: local bulk buyers, beauty shops, online retailers, exporters. Document pricing by channel (wholesale vs. retail). Note seasonal demand variations and competitor offerings.
  3. Supply Chain Strategy (2 pages): Map your kernel supply sources (local collectors, regional markets, dried kernel suppliers). Document prices, reliability, quality grading, payment terms, bulk discounts available. Plan supplier negotiations and contingency if primary source fails.
  4. Operations & Processing Plan (2 pages):
    — Processing method choice (hand-pounding vs. mechanical press) with rationale and cost.
    — Labour requirements (your time or hired staff hours needed per kg).
    — Daily/weekly processing timeline (e.g., Monday: crack, Tuesday: dry, Wednesday: press, Thursday-Friday: clarify & package).
    — Quality control steps (moisture testing, FFA if possible, sensory grading).
    — Scaling plan: start 10kg/week, grow to 50kg/week by month 6.
  5. Financial Projections (2 pages):
    — Detailed cost per kg: kernel cost, labour, utilities, packaging, transport to buyer.
    — Revenue projections: Month 1-6, assuming gradual volume ramp and market penetration.
    — Break-even analysis (often <1 month if buying pre-dried kernels).
    — Profit margin by market segment (wholesale, retail, value-add).
    — Gross margin calculation: (Revenue - COGS) / Revenue.
    — Cash flow: when you pay suppliers vs. when you receive payment from buyers.
  6. Market Entry & Growth Strategy (1 page): How you'll find first customers (local markets, shops, online), pricing strategy by segment, differentiation (quality, packaging, organic claim, value-add products), scaling to 500kg/month and beyond, export potential if targeting.
