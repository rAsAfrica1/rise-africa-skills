1. Identifying Fall Armyworm in Maize

Fall armyworm (FAW) is a major pest of maize in Africa. The larva is the damaging stage. Look for small holes in young leaves, often in the whorl. As the plant grows, you may see ragged holes and large amounts of moist sawdust-like frass (excrement) around the whorl. The larva has a distinctive inverted Y-shaped mark on its head and four dark spots arranged in a square on the second-to-last abdominal segment. FAW can be confused with other caterpillars like African armyworm or stem borers. To confirm, check the head and tail: FAW has a light-colored inverted Y on the head and four dots on the tail. Scout your field twice a week, especially when maize is 2-6 weeks old. Check 10 plants in 5 different spots. If you find 2 or more larvae per 10 plants, consider action. Handpick larvae when few. Apply recommended biopesticides like Bacillus thuringiensis or neem-based products early. Chemical pesticides like emamectin benzoate or chlorantraniliprole can be used, but rotate to avoid resistance. Always follow label instructions and wear protective gear.

2. Recognizing Cassava Mosaic and Brown Streak Viruses

Cassava mosaic disease (CMD) and cassava brown streak disease (CBSD) are the two most destructive viral diseases of cassava in Africa. CMD causes yellow-green mosaic patterns on leaves, leaf distortion, and stunted growth. The virus is spread by whiteflies and through infected cuttings. CBSD causes yellowing along leaf veins, but the real damage is in the roots: brown, corky dry rot that makes tubers inedible. CBSD is also spread by whiteflies and infected cuttings. To identify CMD, look for mosaic patterns, especially on young leaves. For CBSD, pull up a few plants and check roots for brown streaks or rot. There is no cure for infected plants. Management relies on using certified disease-free cuttings, uprooting and burning infected plants, and controlling whiteflies with neem oil or imidacloprid. Plant resistant varieties like TME 419 or TMS 30572. Scout fields every two weeks. If you see symptoms on more than 5% of plants, take action. Always source cuttings from reputable suppliers.

3. Tomato Leafminer and Other Common Tomato Pests

Tomato leafminer (Tuta absoluta) is a devastating pest. The adult is a small moth. The larvae mine inside leaves, creating blotchy, irregular mines. They also bore into stems and fruits, causing rot. Scout by checking leaves for mines and fruits for entry holes. Use pheromone traps to monitor adult moths: hang 20 traps per hectare. If you catch more than 20 moths per trap per week, start control. Other common tomato pests include whiteflies (vector of viruses), aphids, and spider mites. Whiteflies cause yellowing and transmit tomato yellow leaf curl virus. Aphids cause leaf curling and secrete honeydew. Spider mites cause stippling and webbing. For control, use neem oil, insecticidal soap, or recommended chemicals like abamectin for mites, imidacloprid for whiteflies. Rotate crops and remove weeds that host pests. Practice integrated pest management: combine cultural, biological, and chemical methods. Always follow pre-harvest intervals.

4. Weeds That Threaten African Farms: Striga and Speargrass

Striga, also called witchweed, is a parasitic weed that attacks cereals like maize, sorghum, and millet. It attaches to the host roots and sucks nutrients, causing stunting and yellowing. Striga thrives in poor soils and dry areas. Speargrass (Imperata cylindrica) is a aggressive perennial grass with sharp leaves and deep rhizomes. It competes for water and nutrients and is hard to remove by hand. To manage Striga, practice crop rotation with legumes like cowpea or groundnut, which stimulate Striga seeds to germinate but do not support them. Pull Striga before it flowers. Apply nitrogen fertilizer to boost crop vigor. For speargrass, repeated tillage during dry season exposes rhizomes to sun. Mulching with thick organic matter can suppress it. Hand-weed before speargrass sets seed. Herbicides like glyphosate can be used, but careful not to harm crops. Always integrate methods for best results.

5. Using Local Knowledge to Scout for Pests

Local knowledge is a powerful tool for pest scouting. Farmers often know signs of pest outbreaks before scientists do. For example, in many African communities, farmers observe certain birds or insects that indicate pest presence. They also know plant indicators: when certain wild plants flower, pests may appear. Use this knowledge alongside systematic scouting. Start by walking your field in a zigzag pattern. Check 10 plants in 5 spots. Look for chewed leaves, holes, frass, discoloration, or wilting. Record what you see. Talk to elders and experienced farmers about pest cycles. Use simple tools: a hand lens, a notebook, and a stick to open plant whorls. For fall armyworm, check early morning or late evening when larvae are active. For diseases, look for symptoms on leaves and stems. Share findings with neighbors. If you see something unusual, take a photo and ask an extension officer. Combine local knowledge with modern methods for best results.

6. When to Act: Economic Thresholds for Small Farms

Economic threshold (ET) is the pest density at which control measures are justified to prevent economic loss. For small farms, ET helps avoid unnecessary pesticide use. For fall armyworm in maize, ET is 2 larvae per 10 plants. For tomato leafminer, ET is 20 moths per pheromone trap per week. For cassava mosaic, ET is 5% of plants showing symptoms. For weeds, ET depends on crop and weed species; generally, if weeds cover 10% of the field, act. To calculate ET, estimate the cost of control and the value of crop loss. If control cost is less than potential loss, act. Scout regularly to monitor pest levels. Use simple counts: for aphids, check 10 leaves per plant; if more than 50 aphids per leaf, treat. Always consider natural enemies: if many ladybirds are present, aphid numbers may decline without spraying. Keep records to make better decisions each season.

<h2>1. Fall Armyworm (FAW) in Maize Identification &amp; Early Action</h2>

<p>FAW (<em>Spodoptera frugiperda</em>) is now endemic across most of sub-Saharan Africa. Early instars "window" leaves (papery transparent patches); later instars produce ragged holes and moist frass in the whorl. The inverted-Y mark on the head and four raised spots in a square on the second-to-last abdominal segment distinguish FAW from native armyworms and stem borers.</p>

<h2>2. Cassava Mosaic Disease (CMD) &amp; Cassava Brown Streak Disease (CBSD)</h2>

<p>CMD shows as yellow-green mosaic mottling, leaf distortion, and stunted growth; CBSD causes yellowing along veins and brown necrosis in storage roots, making them unfit for market. Both are spread by whiteflies and, more importantly, through infected cuttings. There is no cure once a plant is infected the business response is prevention through clean planting material and roguing.</p>

<h2>3. Tomato Leafminer (<em>Tuta absoluta</em>) and Common Tomato Pests</h2>

<p><em>Tuta absoluta</em> larvae mine leaves, stems, and fruit, causing up to 100% loss in unprotected tomato. Scout for blotch-shaped mines and pin-hole fruit entry points. Other key pests: whitefly (TYLCV vector), thrips (spotted wilt), and <em>Helicoverpa armigera</em> (fruit borer). Use pheromone traps (34 per acre) for early warning.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Cultural:</strong> Rotate with cereals; remove crop residue; use insect-proof nursery nets.</li>
<li><strong>Biological:</strong> <em>Nesidiocoris tenuis</em> predator; Bt var. <em>kurstaki</em>; neem oil (azadirachtin 0.30.5%).</li>
<li><strong>Chemical (rotate modes of action):</strong> Chlorantraniliprole, Spinetoram, Emamectin benzoate. Never spray the same MoA twice in a row.</li>
<li><strong>Market note:</strong> MRL violations on export tomatoes are costly keep spray records and respect pre-harvest intervals.</li>
</ul>

<h2>4. Weeds: Striga and Speargrass (<em>Imperata cylindrica</em>)</h2>

<p><strong>Striga</strong> (<em>Striga hermonthica</em>, <em>S. asiatica</em>) parasitises maize, sorghum, millet, and upland rice. It causes "witchweed" stunting and can cut yields 40100% on degraded soils. <strong>Speargrass</strong> is a rhizomatous perennial that regenerates from root fragments tillage spreads it.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Striga:</strong> Use resistant/tolerant varieties (e.g., IITA Striga-resistant maize); apply nitrogen (Striga thrives in low-N soils); rotate with legumes (cowpea, soybean); hand-pull before flowering; consider <em>Fusarium oxysporum</em> biocontrol where registered.</li>
<li><strong>Speargrass:</strong> Repeated slashing before seed set; shade with dense cover crops (mucuna, pigeon pea); avoid deep tillage that fragments rhizomes; glyphosate spot treatment only where safe and legal.</li>
</ul>

<h2>5. Using Local Knowledge to Scout for Pests</h2>

<p>Smallholder scouts often detect problems earlier than formal protocols. Institutionalise this by:</p>
<ol>
<li>Training 23 lead farmers per village as "first detectors" with pictorial ID cards.</li>
<li>Linking farmer observations to extension SMS/WhatsApp groups and county/ district agriculture officers.</li>
<li>Recording indigenous indicators e.g., ant activity, bird flocking, changes in leaf colour after rains alongside standard counts.</li>
<li>Holding monthly field days to verify reports and update action thresholds.</li>
</ol>

<h2>6. When to Act: Economic Thresholds for Small Farms</h2>

<p>An economic threshold (ET) is the pest density at which the value of yield saved exceeds the cost of control. For African smallholders, ETs must account for labour cost, input access, and market price volatility. Simple rule: <strong>spray only when expected loss &gt; 2 cost of control</strong>.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>FAW in maize:</strong> 20% whorl damage in vegetative stage (FAO, 2018).</li>
<li><strong>Tomato leafminer:</strong> 12 live larvae per plant or 5% infested leaflets.</li>
<li><strong>Whitefly (tomato):</strong> 5 adults per leaflet triggers TYLCV risk action.</li>
<li><strong>Striga:</strong> Any emergence before maize tasselling warrants hand-pulling and N top-dress.</li>
<li><strong>Cassava:</strong> Zero tolerance for CMD/CBSD symptoms rogue immediately.</li>
</ul>

<div style="border:3px solid #b30000; background:#fff5f5; padding:16px; margin:20px 0;">
<h3 style="color:#b30000; margin-top:0;"> SAFETY WARNING Pesticide Handling</h3>
<p style="margin-bottom:0;">Always wear gloves, long sleeves, boots, and a face mask when handling or spraying agrochemicals. Never eat, drink, or smoke during application. Respect the pre-harvest interval (PHI) on the label selling produce before PHI can cause illness and market rejection. Store pesticides in a locked, ventilated place away from children, food, and animal feed. Dispose of empty containers by triple-rinsing and puncturing never reuse for water or food. In case of poisoning, contact your local poison information centre or health facility immediately and carry the product label. Follow national pesticide registration (e.g., PCPB in Kenya, NAFDAC in Nigeria, DALRRD in South Africa) and only use products approved for your crop.</p>
</div>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Scout weekly</strong> using the W-pattern; record counts on paper or phone app.</li>
<li><strong>Identify before you spray</strong> use pictorial guides for FAW, CMD, CBSD, <em>Tuta</em>.</li>
<li><strong>Check thresholds</strong> do not spray below ET; protect natural enemies.</li>
<li><strong>Rotate modes of action</strong> to prevent resistance (IRAC groups).</li>
<li><strong>Use clean planting material</strong> certified cassava cuttings, disease-free tomato seedlings.</li>
<li><strong>Rogue out</strong> virus-infected cassava and tomato plants immediately; burn, do not compost.</li>
<li><strong>Manage weeds early</strong> Striga before flowering; speargrass before rhizome spread.</li>
<li><strong>Keep spray records</strong> (date, product, dose, PHI) for market traceability and MRL compliance.</li>
</ol>
