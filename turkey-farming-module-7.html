1. Growth Stages and Targets

From week 5 to market, your turkeys go through three clear stages: grower (512 weeks), developer (1318 weeks), and finisher (1924 weeks or more, depending on breed). Each stage has target weights and feed needs. For a broad-breasted white, aim for 57 kg live weight at 12 weeks, 912 kg at 18 weeks, and 1418 kg at 24 weeks. Local or crossbreed turkeys grow slower: expect 34 kg at 12 weeks and 68 kg at 24 weeks. Weigh 10 birds every two weeks using a hanging scale or a bathroom scale (weigh yourself, then hold the bird). Record weights in a notebook. If average weight is more than 10% below target, check feed quality, space, and disease. At 8 weeks, separate males and females if you canmales grow faster and need more feed. In hot African climates, turkeys eat less during midday; provide most feed early morning and late afternoon. By 16 weeks, start evaluating which birds to keep for breeding or sell as heavy roasters. Cull slow-growing, lame, or sick birds early to save feed. A turkey that reaches 2 kg at 8 weeks but only 4 kg at 16 weeks is not profitablesell it as a small bird or use it for home consumption. Keep a simple chart on the wall: date, age, average weight, feed consumed. That chart tells you if you are on track.

2. Space Allowances

Turkeys need more space than chickens. Overcrowding causes feather pecking, cannibalism, poor growth, and disease. For grow-out pens, give 0.5 square meters per bird from 5 to 12 weeks, 1.0 square meter from 13 to 18 weeks, and 1.5 square meters from 19 weeks to market. If you keep turkeys on deep litter, use dry wood shavings, rice husks, or chopped maize cobsnever wet or mouldy litter. In free-range systems, allow 1015 square meters per bird so they can graze. But even free-range birds need a sheltered house at night: 0.30.5 square meters per bird inside. Ventilation is critical. In hot climates, build open-sided houses with wire mesh walls and a solid roof. Air must move freely but not blow directly on the birds. If you smell ammonia, you need more ventilation or fresh litter. Feeders: allow 10 cm of linear feeder space per bird; drinkers: 2 cm per bird. For 50 birds at 16 weeks, you need about 50 square meters of floor space, 5 meters of feeder, and 1 meter of drinker. Use local materials: bamboo, mud bricks, thatch, or corrugated iron. Raise the house floor 1520 cm above ground to prevent flooding. Add perchesturkeys like to roost. Provide 20 cm of perch length per bird, 60 cm above the floor. Clean and disinfect the house between batches. If you see birds panting and holding wings away from the body, they are too hotincrease shade and airflow immediately.

3. Feeding for Optimal Growth

Turkeys need high-protein feed for fast growth. From 5 to 8 weeks, feed a grower mash with 2426% crude protein. From 9 to 16 weeks, use 2022% protein. From 17 weeks to market, use 1618% protein finisher feed. You can mix your own using locally available ingredients: maize (energy), soybean meal or groundnut cake (protein), fish meal (protein and minerals), and limestone or oyster shell (calcium). A simple 100 kg grower mix: 50 kg maize, 30 kg soybean meal, 10 kg fish meal, 5 kg wheat bran, 3 kg limestone, 1 kg salt, 1 kg vitamin-mineral premix. For finisher, reduce soybean to 20 kg and increase maize to 60 kg. Always mix well. Feed twice a dayearly morning and late afternoon. Give only what birds can finish in 20 minutes to avoid waste and spoilage. A turkey eats about 0.5 kg feed per day at 8 weeks, 1 kg at 16 weeks, and 1.5 kg at 24 weeks. Provide clean water at all times; a turkey drinks 23 times its feed weight in water. In hot weather, add electrolytes (1 teaspoon salt + 1 teaspoon baking soda per 5 litres) once a week. Never feed mouldy maizeit causes aflatoxin poisoning, which stunts growth and kills. Store feed in dry, raised containers. If you cannot afford commercial premix, use crushed termite mound soil or wood ash as a mineral source, but commercial premix is safer. Weigh feed weekly to track consumptionif feed intake drops, check for disease or heat stress.

4. Health Monitoring

Walk through your turkeys at least twice a day. Look for signs of illness: drooping wings, ruffled feathers, closed eyes, greenish diarrhoea, coughing, sneezing, or sudden deaths. Isolate any sick bird immediately in a separate pen with clean water and feed. Common turkey diseases in Africa include Newcastle disease, fowl pox, coccidiosis, and blackhead. Vaccinate against Newcastle at 7 days (if not done earlier) and repeat every 3 months. For fowl pox, vaccinate at 8 weeks. Coccidiosis is controlled with amprolium in drinking water for 5 days, but prevention is better: keep litter dry and avoid overcrowding. Blackhead is a parasite that affects turkeys more than chickensdo not keep turkeys with chickens, especially where chickens have access to soil. Deworm every 8 weeks with levamisole or albendazole. External parasites (lice, mites) are treated with dusting powder or ash. Keep a health record: date, symptoms, treatment, outcome. If more than 2 birds die in a week, call a vet or extension officer. In many African countries, government veterinary services are free or cheap. Also watch for injuries: turkeys can break legs if handled roughly. Provide soft bedding. If a bird has a wound, clean with warm salty water and apply iodine. Always wash your hands and boots before entering the penyou can carry disease from other farms. Quarantine new birds for 3 weeks before mixing with your flock.

5. Handling and Moving Turkeys

Turkeys are strong and can injure themselves or you if handled wrongly. Never lift a turkey by its wings, legs, or neck. The correct method: approach calmly from the side, place one hand under the breast and the other hand over the back, holding the wings against the body. Lift smoothly and hold the bird close to your body for support. For large toms (over 10 kg), use a catching hook or a cloth sling. Move birds in the cool of the dayearly morning or evening. Avoid chasing; it causes stress and heat exhaustion. Use a catching pen or a corner of the house with a temporary fence made of bamboo or netting. To move turkeys from one pen to another, herd them slowly with a wooden board or a stick, not by shouting. If you must transport them, use crates with ventilation holes. Give each bird 0.1 square meters of crate space. Do not stack crates more than three high. Cover the crate with a light cloth to reduce stress, but ensure airflow. Transport in a vehicle with open sides. For short distances, carry birds in baskets lined with soft grass. After moving, give water with vitamins for 2 days. Handle birds only when necessaryevery handling event slows growth. Train your helpers on correct handling. If a bird struggles, put it down gently, let it calm, then try again. Never lift more than one bird at a time. For weighing, use a hanging scale with a cloth sling; it is quick and safe.

6. Pre-Market Preparation

Two weeks before selling, start preparing your turkeys for market. First, decide your market: live birds, dressed (slaughtered and plucked), or cut pieces. Live birds are easiestyou need no special equipment. Dressed birds fetch higher prices but require a clean slaughter area, sharp knives, hot water (6065C) for plucking, and cold storage or immediate sale. If selling live, stop feeding 12 hours before transport to reduce defecation, but always provide water. Weigh each bird and sort into weight classes: small (under 5 kg), medium (58 kg), large (812 kg), extra-large (over 12 kg). Buyers pay more for uniform sizes. Clean the birds feet and vent area with a damp cloth. For dressed birds, starve 8 hours, then slaughter by severing the jugular vein. Bleed fully, then scald and pluck. Remove head and feet. Chill in clean water with ice or in a refrigerator. If you have no cold storage, sell immediately. Package in clean plastic bags or wrap in banana leaves. Label with weight and date. Transport in clean crates or baskets. For live birds, tie legs loosely with soft ropenever wire. Market early in the morning when it is cool. Take a few birds to show buyers before the main batch. Set a price based on local market rates: check prices at nearby markets or ask other farmers. Do not underpriceturkey meat is premium. Keep records of sales and expenses to know your profit. After selling, clean and disinfect all crates and the slaughter area.

<h2>Practical Reference: Turkey Grow-Out and Finishing</h2>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the core operational knowledge needed to take a turkey flock from brooding through to market readiness in an African small-to-medium commercial setting. It is written for the farm manager or lead stockperson who must make daily decisions on space, feed, health, handling and pre-market preparation. The focus is on measurable targets, realistic African conditions (heat stress, variable feed quality, limited cold chain, informal market buyers) and the commercial bottom line: feed conversion, mortality, uniformity and carcass value.</p>

<p>Turkeys are not chickens. They grow faster, eat more, need more space, are more sensitive to heat and stress, and are more prone to leg and respiratory problems. Treating them like broilers is one of the most common and costly mistakes on African farms. The following sections give you the numbers, the timings and the checkpoints to run a professional grow-out and finishing phase.</p>

<h3>Growth Stages and Targets</h3>

<p>Commercial turkey production in Africa typically follows three phases: brooding (04 weeks), grow-out (512 weeks) and finishing (13 weeks to market). Market weight depends on the target buyer. Local informal markets often prefer 57 kg live weight (1416 weeks), while hotels, supermarkets and festive markets pay a premium for 812 kg birds (1822 weeks). Choose your target weight early, because it determines your feed programme, space and cash-flow cycle.</p>

<p>Uniformity matters more than maximum weight. A flock where most birds fall within 10% of the average weight sells faster and more profitably than a flock with a few giants and many runts. Weigh a sample of 510% of birds every week, at the same time of day, and record the average and the spread.</p>

<h3>Space Allowances</h3>

<p>Understocking wastes money; overstocking kills birds and profits. Turkeys need roughly twice the floor space of broilers at the same age. In African open-sided or naturally ventilated houses, add 1015% extra space to compensate for heat and humidity. Litter must be dry, 510 cm deep, and raked daily to prevent caking wet litter is the single biggest cause of footpad dermatitis and respiratory disease in turkeys.</p>

<h3>Feeding for Optimal Growth</h3>

<p>Feed is 6575% of your production cost. Every point of feed conversion you save is direct profit. Use a phased feeding programme: starter crumbles (04 weeks, 28% crude protein), grower pellets (512 weeks, 2224% CP), and finisher pellets (13 weeks to market, 1820% CP). In many African markets, pre-mixed turkey feeds are scarce or expensive, so you may need to mix your own. If you do, source consistent-quality maize, soybean meal, fishmeal or sunflower cake, and always add a commercial vitamin-mineral premix and limestone or oyster shell for calcium.</p>

<p>Key practical rules: feed at the same times daily, keep feeders one-third full to reduce spillage, and never let feed sit in the trough for more than a few hours in hot weather it moulds. Clean water must be available at all times; a turkey drinks roughly 1.52 times its feed intake by weight. In heat, water intake can double. A flock without clean water for 6 hours in 35C heat will lose weight and may die.</p>

<p style="margin:0;color:#5a0000;">Turkeys are highly susceptible to heat stress. Do <strong>not</strong> handle, move, vaccinate or transport turkeys during the hottest part of the day (11:0016:00) when temperatures exceed 30C. Move birds only in the early morning or late evening. Never crowd birds into a small pen for sorting or loading crowding in heat causes rapid death from suffocation and heat stroke within minutes. Always provide shade, ventilation and cool drinking water during any handling operation. If birds are panting heavily with wings held away from the body, stop all handling immediately and cool the house.</p>

<h3>Health Monitoring</h3>

<p>Walk the flock at least twice daily early morning and late evening. You are looking for behaviour, not just symptoms. Healthy turkeys are active, curious, evenly spread, with bright eyes and clean vents. Sick birds huddle, sit on their hocks, have drooping wings, ruffled feathers, or stay near the drinker. Record any change in feed or water intake: a 10% drop in water intake is often the first sign of disease, 2448 hours before visible symptoms.</p>

<p>Core vaccination programme (adapt to local veterinary advice): Newcastle disease (day 7 and 21), fowl pox (week 46), and infectious bursal disease where present. Deworm every 68 weeks with a poultry-safe anthelmintic. Keep a simple health log: date, observation, action, outcome. This log is also your evidence for buyers and lenders.</p>

<h3>Handling and Moving Turkeys</h3>

<p>Turkeys bruise easily and panic when handled roughly. Move birds calmly, using low, slow movements. Never catch a turkey by the wing, leg or neck. Hold the bird with both hands one supporting the breast, the other controlling the legs or use a catching frame for groups. For short moves within the farm, use a clean, ventilated crate or a low-sided pen. For transport to market, use crates with 2530 cm headroom, load in the cool hours, and do not stack more than three crates high in hot weather.</p>

<h3>Pre-Market Preparation</h3>

<p>Market readiness is a commercial decision, not just a biological one. Start preparing 710 days before sale. Withdraw any medication according to the label withdrawal period this is a legal and food-safety requirement. Switch to finisher feed with no growth promoters. Check that birds are clean: wipe muddy feet and soiled feathers with a damp cloth. Sort birds into uniform weight groups so buyers can choose. Fast birds for 812 hours before slaughter or transport (water available), which reduces gut content and contamination risk. Arrange transport and buyer confirmation 48 hours ahead. If selling live, present birds in clean crates with shade; if selling dressed, ensure your slaughter and chilling process meets local food-safety rules.</p>
