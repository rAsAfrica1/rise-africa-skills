Lessons

1. L1: Removing the Petrol Tank and Lines

Before you start, park the engine on a flat, shaded slab. Put on safety glasses and nitrile gloves. Petrol is dangerous: no smoking, no phone, no grinding nearby. You will need a 10mm, 12mm, and 14mm spanner set, a flathead screwdriver, a fuel-safe container with a lid, and a fire extinguisher. First, disconnect the battery negative terminal. Then find the fuel tank, usually under the rear seat or boot. Remove the fuel pump fuse or relay and run the engine until it stalls. This burns most petrol in the lines. Now loosen the fuel filler cap to release pressure. Place the container under the tank drain plug. Slowly open the drain plug with a 14mm spanner. Let all petrol drain out. If there is no drain plug, disconnect the fuel line at the tank outlet and let it drain. Next, remove the tank straps or bolts. They are often 12mm or 14mm. Lower the tank carefully. Disconnect the fuel gauge wires and the filler neck hose. Pull the tank out. Now remove the old steel fuel lines running from the tank to the engine. They are usually clipped to the chassis. Use a 10mm spanner to undo the fittings. If they are rusty, cut them with a hacksaw and replace with new copper or rubber fuel hose. For irrigation, you will not use petrol. You will convert to diesel or use a direct gravity feed from a raised tank. So remove all petrol lines completely. Clean up any spilled petrol with sand or rags, and dispose of them safely. Store the old tank away from sparks. This job takes about 2 hours. Take your time and work in open air.

2. L2: Installing a Diesel Fuel Tank and Filter

Diesel is safer than petrol but still needs care. For irrigation, you want a tank that holds at least 20 litres for a full day's pumping. A plastic 25-litre jerrycan works if you fit a bottom outlet. Better: use a small steel tank from a scrap truck. Mount it higher than the engine so gravity feeds the fuel. You will need a 12mm drill bit, a bulkhead fitting, a 10mm fuel hose, hose clamps, and a diesel filter assembly. First, decide where to mount the tank. On a frame above the engine, at least 30cm higher than the fuel injection pump. Drill a hole in the tank bottom for the outlet fitting. Use a step drill to avoid cracking plastic. Fit a brass bulkhead fitting with rubber washers on both sides. Tighten gently. Next, install a diesel filter. A standard spin-on filter like a Fleetguard FF5052 is good. Mount it on the engine frame, close to the injection pump. Connect the tank outlet to the filter inlet with 10mm fuel hose. Use hose clamps on every connection. Then connect the filter outlet to the injection pump inlet. If your engine is a petrol engine converted to diesel, you will need a diesel injection pump and injectors. That is a big jobconsider swapping to a small diesel engine instead. For now, assume you have a diesel engine. Bleed the system: open the bleed screw on the filter housing and pump the hand primer until diesel comes out with no bubbles. Then tighten. Start the engine. Check for leaks. If you see diesel dripping, tighten the clamps. This setup gives you a clean, reliable fuel supply for irrigation.

3. L3: Converting a Petrol Engine to Run on Kerosene or Paraffin

In many African villages, kerosene (paraffin) is cheaper and easier to find than petrol. You can convert a small petrol engine to run on kerosene, but it needs modifications. Kerosene has a lower octane rating, so the engine will knock if you just pour it in. You must lower the compression ratio. That means adding a thicker head gasket or a spacer plate. For a typical 5hp engine, a 1mm copper spacer works. You also need to preheat the fuel. Kerosene does not vaporise as easily as petrol. So wrap a copper coil around the exhaust pipe and run the fuel line through it. This heats the kerosene before it reaches the carburetor. You will also need to enlarge the main jet in the carburetor by 10-15%. Use a 1.2mm drill bit to open the jet. Start the engine on petrol, let it warm up, then switch to kerosene using a two-way valve. Do not shut off the petrol until the engine is hot. When stopping, switch back to petrol for one minute to flush kerosene out of the carburetor. Otherwise, the kerosene will leave gum deposits. For irrigation, this conversion can save money, but kerosene is not always available. Also, kerosene produces less powerexpect a 10% drop. Use it only if you have a steady supply. Always store kerosene in a clearly marked red container, away from children. Check the spark plug often; kerosene fouls plugs faster. Clean or replace every 50 hours.

4. L4: Fitting a Gravity-Feed Fuel System from a Raised Tank

Gravity feed is the simplest fuel system for irrigation. No fuel pump, no electricity, no carburetor float problems. You just need a tank raised high enough to push fuel into the engine. For a diesel engine, you need about 0.5 psi of pressure. That means the tank bottom must be at least 1 metre above the injection pump. For a petrol engine with a carburetor, you need 1.5 to 2 psi, so raise the tank 2 to 3 metres. Mount a 200-litre drum on a strong wooden or metal stand. Use 50mm x 50mm angle iron for the legs. Weld or bolt it well200 litres of fuel weighs 160kg. Fit a 15mm ball valve at the bottom of the drum. Then run a 10mm copper or rubber fuel hose down to the engine. Install an inline fuel filter just before the carburetor or injection pump. This catches rust and dirt. Use a clear plastic filter so you can see when it is dirty. Connect the hose to the engine fuel inlet. If your engine has a mechanical fuel pump, you can leave it in placeit will just pull fuel through. But if the pump is faulty, bypass it. For gravity feed to work, the tank must be vented. Drill a 2mm hole in the filler cap. Otherwise, a vacuum will stop the flow. Test the system: open the valve and watch fuel fill the filter. Start the engine. If it runs lean, raise the tank higher. If it floods, lower it. This system is cheap and reliable. Just remember to check the fuel level daily.

5. L5: Adjusting the Carburetor for Continuous Pumping

An irrigation pump runs at a steady speed for hours. A car engine carburetor is set for changing speeds. You need to adjust it for constant load. First, locate the carburetor. It is the metal part with the air filter on top. You will see two screws: one for idle speed (usually a large screw that stops the throttle) and one for idle mixture (a small screw on the side). For continuous pumping, you want the engine to run at about 1500 to 1800 RPM. That is fast enough to pump water but not so fast that it overheats. Start the engine and let it warm up. Turn the idle speed screw in until the engine runs at your target RPM. Use a tachometer or a cheap digital tachometerabout $15 online. Now adjust the idle mixture screw. Turn it in slowly until the engine starts to stumble, then turn it out 1.5 turns. That is usually the best setting. If the engine surges up and down, the mixture is too lean. Turn the mixture screw out 1/4 turn at a time until it runs smooth. If it runs rich (black smoke, fouled plug), turn it in. For continuous running, you may need to clean the carburetor every 100 hours. Use carburetor cleaner spray and a thin wire to clear the jets. Also, check the float level. If the float is too high, fuel will flood the engine. Bend the float tab slightly to adjust. Set it so the fuel level is just below the bowl gasket. Finally, lock the throttle in position with a cable or a piece of wire. You do not want it moving while you are away. Test under load: connect the pump and run for 30 minutes. Adjust as needed.

6. L6: Safety and Maintenance of the Converted Fuel System

Fuel system safety is not optional. One spark can burn your farm. Always keep a dry powder fire extinguisher within 10 metres of the engine. Never refuel while the engine is running. Let it cool for 10 minutes first. Store fuel in a locked, ventilated shed away from the pump. Use only metal containers for petrol; plastic can build static. For diesel and kerosene, plastic is okay if it is marked. Check all fuel lines every week. Look for cracks, leaks, or loose clamps. Replace any rubber hose that is hard or brittle. Use fuel hose, not water hosewater hose will dissolve. Every 100 hours, change the fuel filter. Every 500 hours, drain the tank and clean it out. Rust and water settle at the bottom. Install a water separator if you can. It looks like a small glass bowl under the filter. Drain it daily. Keep the carburetor or injection pump clean. Dirt is the number one killer of fuel systems. Use a funnel with a fine mesh when filling. In dusty conditions, cover the fuel cap with a cloth. If you smell fuel while the engine runs, stop immediately and find the leak. Do not use tape as a permanent fix. For long-term storage, drain all fuel from the tank and lines. Run the engine until it stops from lack of fuel. Then spray a little oil into the spark plug hole or intake. This prevents rust. Write down the date of every filter change and tank cleaning. A simple notebook works. Teach everyone who uses the pump these rules. One careless moment can destroy your investment.

7. Practical Reference


Practical Reference: Fuel System Conversion for Irrigation Pumping



Converting a stationary petrol car engine into a diesel, kerosene, or gravity-fed irrigation pump is one of the fastest routes to reliable water access for smallholder and commercial farms across Africa. The fuel system is the heart of that conversion. Get it right and you unlock lower running costs, longer engine life, and a pump that runs all day without constant attention. Get it wrong and you risk fire, engine seizure, or a pump that stalls every twenty minutes during the dry season when every drop counts.



This practical reference consolidates the key technical decisions, measurements, and safety practices from the seven lessons in this module. It is written for the technician, farm mechanic, or agripreneur who needs to make a conversion work profitably not just theoretically. All figures assume a typical 1.01.6 litre four-cylinder petrol engine (e.g., Toyota 4K, Nissan A-series, or similar) commonly available in African second-hand markets and workshops.

SAFETY WARNING FIRE AND EXPLOSION RISK


Petrol, kerosene, and diesel vapours are highly flammable. Never convert a fuel system near an open flame, welding equipment, or running engine. Always disconnect the battery before working on fuel lines. Store fuel in approved containers away from the pump site. Fit a fire extinguisher (dry powder, minimum 2 kg) within 3 metres of the engine. In gravity-feed systems, ensure the tank vent is clear and the shut-off valve is accessible from outside the pump house. A fuel leak on a hot exhaust manifold can ignite within seconds inspect all joints with soapy water before every start-up. In many African jurisdictions, fuel storage above 200 litres requires local authority notification.




Quick Reference Checklist




  1. Drain and purge the original petrol tank, lines, and carburetor completely before installing any new fuel system. Residual petrol will contaminate diesel or kerosene.

  2. Mount the tank at least 1.5 m above the carburetor for gravity feed, or install a low-pressure lift pump (24 psi) for diesel systems. Secure with vibration-resistant brackets.

  3. Fit a primary filter (30-micron) before the lift pump and a secondary filter (5-micron) before the carburetor or injector pump. In dusty African conditions, change filters every 250 hours.

  4. Use copper or steel lines for all fuel plumbing never garden hose or PVC. Route lines away from exhaust components and secure every 30 cm with clips.

  5. Adjust the carburetor for continuous load: richer mixture, float level per table above, and idle speed set to 18002200 RPM under pump load.

  6. Install a shut-off valve at the tank outlet and a second one before the carburetor. Label both clearly. Test operation weekly.

  7. Check for leaks with soapy water at every joint before first start-up and after every 50 hours of operation. Never use a match or lighter to check for leaks.

  8. Keep a logbook of fuel consumption, filter changes, and carburetor adjustments. This data is essential for calculating cost per cubic metre of water pumped the key profitability metric for any irrigation enterprise.
