1. Routine Morning and Evening Checks for Guinea Fowl and Quail

Start your day with the birds before they start theirs. At first light, walk to the pens quietly. Count every bird. For guinea fowl, a flock of 20 should all be present; for quail, a cage of 30 should show no empty spaces. Look for any bird sitting apart, panting, or with drooping wings. Check water first: a guinea fowl drinks about 250 ml per day, a quail about 50 ml. In hot weather, refill waterers twice daily. Ensure water is clean and coolnot hot from the sun. Next, check feed troughs. Adult guinea fowl eat 80100 g of feed per day; quail eat 2530 g. Remove any wet or mouldy feed immediately. Look at droppings: healthy droppings are firm and grey-green with a white cap. Watery or bloody droppings mean sicknessisolate that bird. In the evening, before sunset, do a second check. Count birds again. Guinea fowl may roost in trees if free-ranging; ensure they come back to the pen. Quail are ground birds; they should be inside the cage. Collect eggs: guinea fowl eggs are thick-shelled and often hidden in grass; quail eggs are small and may fall through wire floors if not collected promptly. Check for predators: snakes, rats, and hawks are common. Repair any holes in netting. Lock doors. Note any unusual behaviour in a notebook. This routine takes 15 minutes morning and evening but prevents most losses. Consistency is keybirds thrive on predictability.

2. Cleaning and Disinfecting Cages and Pens

Dirty cages cause disease. Clean weekly, disinfect monthly, and do a deep clean every three months. For quail cages, remove birds to a holding box. Scrape droppings with a putty knife or flat stick. Use a wire brush to scrub perches and corners. Mix 1 part bleach to 10 parts water (or use 2 tablespoons of washing soda per litre of hot water). Scrub all surfaces. Rinse thoroughly with clean waterbleach residue can burn birds feet. Let dry in the sun; sunlight kills many germs. For guinea fowl pens, remove old bedding (wood shavings, straw, or sand). Replace with fresh, dry material. Sprinkle agricultural lime on the floor to reduce ammonia smell and kill parasites. If using sand, sieve it and sun-dry. Disinfect feeders and waterers daily by washing with soap and rinsing. Once a week, soak them in disinfectant for 10 minutes. For free-range guinea fowl, move their night shelter every few weeks to new ground to avoid parasite build-up. Quail are sensitive to ammonia; if you smell ammonia, clean immediately. Wear gloves and a nose mask when cleaning to protect yourself. After cleaning, wash your hands and change clothes. Keep a cleaning schedule on the wall: Mondaywaterers, Tuesdayfeeders, Wednesdaycage floors, etc. This prevents skipped tasks. Remember: a clean environment reduces stress, respiratory infections, and coccidiosis. Healthy birds grow faster and lay more eggs.

3. Managing Guinea Fowl Free-Range Behavior Without Losing Birds

Guinea fowl love to roam. They can fly up to 2 metres high and walk 1 kilometre a day. Without management, you will lose birds to predators, cars, or theft. Start by training them to a home pen. Keep young guinea fowl confined for the first 68 weeks. Feed them inside the pen at the same time each morning and evening. Use a distinctive calllike shaking a tin of maizeso they associate that sound with food. After 8 weeks, let them out in the late afternoon for short periods. They will return to roost if the pen is safe and has feed. Always provide a secure night shelter. A simple hut with perches 1.5 metres high works well. Close the door at dusk after all birds are inside. Count them. If any are missing, search nearby treesthey often roost high. To reduce wandering, plant tall grasses or shrubs around the pen as a visual barrier. Provide a dust bath area inside the pen; guinea fowl use dust to clean feathers and reduce parasites. Keep a dog trained to guard poultry, or use a donkeythey chase predators. Never let guinea fowl free-range during planting season; they eat young crops. In areas with hawks, keep birds in a covered run. If you must free-range, do it after 10 a.m. when hawks are less active. Always have a rooster or a few older guinea fowl as leaders; they teach younger ones to come home. Losing birds is costlyeach guinea fowl is worth money and time. Management is better than replacement.

4. Stress Reduction: Handling, Noise, and Weather Extremes

Stress kills birds quietly. Guinea fowl and quail are easily frightened. Sudden noise, rough handling, or extreme heat can cause them to stop eating, stop laying, or die. Handle birds only when necessary. To catch a quail, use a small net or cup both hands gently around the body, keeping wings folded. Never grab by the head or legs. For guinea fowl, use a catching hook or a large net. Hold them with both hands supporting the breast and wings. Work quietly and slowly. Keep children and dogs away from pens. Loud noisesmotorbikes, radios, shoutingcause panic. Quail may pile up in a corner and suffocate. Place pens away from roads and noisy areas. Provide hiding spots: small boxes, leafy branches, or shade cloth. For heat stress, offer cool water with electrolytes (1 teaspoon of salt and 1 teaspoon of sugar per litre). Misting the roof or providing shallow pans for foot cooling helps. In cold weather, add extra bedding and block drafts. Guinea fowl are hardy but quail are sensitive below 10C. Use a heat lamp for quail chicks, but adults can tolerate 15C if dry. During storms, bring free-range guinea fowl inside early. Play a radio softly at night to mask sudden sounds. Keep a routine: same feeding times, same person. Birds recognise faces. A calm environment means more eggs: a stressed quail stops laying for days. Reduce stress, increase profit.

5. Record Keeping for Growth, Egg Production, and Feed Consumption

You cannot manage what you do not measure. Keep a simple notebook or a wall chart. Record daily: number of eggs collected (for guinea fowl and quail separately), amount of feed given (in kilograms), and any dead or sick birds. Weigh a sample of 5 birds every two weeks to track growth. For quail, adult weight should be 150200 g; for guinea fowl, 1.31.6 kg. If weights drop, check feed quality or disease. Egg production: a good quail lays 250300 eggs per year; a guinea fowl lays 80150. If your quail lay fewer than 20 eggs per 30 birds per day, something is wrongmaybe feed, light, or stress. Feed conversion ratio (FCR) = feed eaten divided by weight gained or eggs produced. For quail, FCR for eggs should be about 2.5 kg feed per 1 kg eggs. If FCR rises, you are wasting money. Record expenses: feed, medicine, bedding, labour. At the end of each month, calculate profit: (eggs sold + birds sold) minus expenses. Use a simple table with columns: Date, Eggs, Feed (kg), Deaths, Notes. Keep records for at least one year to see seasonal patterns. Share records with your vet if disease occurs. Good records help you get loans or grants. They also show you which pen or breed performs best. Start todayeven a rough tally is better than none.

6. Seasonal Care: Preparing for Rainy and Dry Seasons

Africas seasons are tough on poultry. In the rainy season, mud and dampness cause disease. In the dry season, heat and dust cause stress. Prepare early. Before rains, check roofs for leaks. Raise pens on a 30 cm platform of stones or bricks to prevent flooding. Use free-draining bedding like sand or wood shavings. Never let water pool inside. Store feed in airtight drums to prevent mould. Mouldy feed causes aflatoxin poisoningbirds die suddenly. Vaccinate against Newcastle disease before rains, as it spreads in wet conditions. For guinea fowl, provide a covered dry area; they hate getting wet. Quail cages should have a solid roof and plastic sheeting on the windward side. After rains, check for snails and slugsthey carry gapeworm. Collect eggs quickly; wet eggs spoil. In the dry season, heat is the enemy. Provide shade: trees, shade cloth, or a thatched roof. Give cool water at least three times a day. Add vitamin C (1 g per litre) to water to reduce heat stress. Wet the ground around pens to cool the air. Never overcrowdreduce stocking by 20% in peak heat. For quail, mist the roof but not the birds. For guinea fowl, let them free-range early morning and late evening only. Watch for signs of heat stroke: panting, wings held out, lying down. Move such birds to a cool, shaded area and give water with electrolytes. In both seasons, keep a emergency fund for extra feed, water, or medicine. Prepare now, lose less later.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Daily Care & Management of Adult Guinea Fowl and Quail

In African smallholder and semi-commercial poultry systems, adult guinea fowl and quail are often kept as complementary enterprises: guinea fowl for meat, eggs, tick control, and security alarm behaviour; quail for rapid egg and meat turnover. The difference between a profitable flock and a loss-making one is rarely the breed it is the daily routine. This lesson gives you a practical, business-first reference for managing adult birds so that mortality stays low, egg output stays stable, and feed conversion remains economically viable.

Use this module as a field manual. Keep a printed copy in your poultry house, and train every worker on the morning and evening checks before they handle birds alone.

1. Routine Morning and Evening Checks

Do the same checks at the same time every day. Birds and workers both settle into the rhythm, and problems are caught early when they are cheap to fix.

Morning (06:0008:00): Enter quietly. Count birds before they scatter. Look for dead birds, blood, diarrhoea, or drooped wings. Refill clean water first, then feed. Collect eggs immediately quail eggs in particular crack or get eaten if left. Open vents and remove wet litter.

Evening (17:3018:30): Count again. Guinea fowl must be inside before dark they will roost in trees if allowed. Refill waterers for the night. Check for predators (snakes, rats, wild cats). Lock the house. Note anything unusual.

2. Cleaning and Disinfecting Cages and Pens

Dirty housing is the single largest driver of mortality in African smallholder flocks. Quail are especially sensitive to ammonia from droppings; guinea fowl tolerate more, but both suffer respiratory disease in wet, soiled bedding.

Daily: remove wet litter and spilled feed. Weekly: scrape perches, scrub drinkers with soap and clean water, sun-dry them. Monthly: full clean-out remove all litter, wash floors and walls, apply disinfectant, dry completely before re-stocking. Rotate disinfectants to avoid resistance. Wood ash, lime, and sunlight are free, effective tools where commercial disinfectants are expensive.

3. Managing Guinea Fowl Free-Range Behaviour Without Losing Birds

Guinea fowl are semi-wild. They fly, roam, and roost in trees which is why many African farmers lose 2040% of their flock each year. The solution is routine, not force.

Train birds from a young age: feed them at the same spot every morning and evening, and call them with the same sound. Keep them confined for the first 23 weeks after purchase or hatch so they imprint on the homestead. Clip one wing of the strongest fliers only if necessary never both wings, as birds need balance. Provide tall trees or poles inside the compound as safe roosting options so they don't wander. Always close the house at dusk.

4. Stress Reduction: Handling, Noise, and Weather Extremes

Stress costs money: it suppresses laying, weakens immunity, and increases cannibalism. Quail are far more stress-sensitive than guinea fowl a loud noise or rough handling can stop egg production for 37 days.

Handle birds gently with both hands, supporting the body. Never chase birds. Move slowly. Keep children and dogs away from the pen. During heatwaves, provide shade, cool water, and misting. During cold or rainy spells, block draughts, dry the litter, and increase energy in the feed.

SAFETY WARNING DISINFECTANTS AND MEDICATION

Never mix disinfectants (especially bleach with ammonia-based products) toxic gases can kill you and your birds. Always wear gloves and a nose mask when cleaning pens or applying chemicals. Store all medicines, vaccines, and disinfectants in a locked cabinet away from children, feed, and water. Follow withdrawal periods strictly before selling eggs or meat for human consumption. If you suspect a notifiable disease (e.g., avian influenza), report immediately to your local veterinary officer do not sell or move birds.

6. Seasonal Care: Preparing for Rainy and Dry Seasons

Rainy season: The biggest risks are wet litter, coccidiosis, and flooded pens. Raise the floor on a platform, dig drainage channels, store feed on pallets above ground, and vaccinate before the rains start. Collect eggs more frequently so they don't get muddy.

Dry season: Heat stress and dust are the enemies. Provide deep shade (trees, shade cloth), cool drinking water several times a day, and dust-bathing areas. In the Sahel and northern savannah, heat can push quail mortality above 15% without intervention.

Transition periods: Watch for sudden temperature swings these trigger respiratory disease in both species. Increase vitamin C and electrolytes in water during stress periods.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Count all birds morning and evening record any loss immediately.
  • Provide clean, cool water before feed every morning.
  • Collect eggs at least twice daily; store in a cool, clean place.
  • Remove wet litter and spilled feed daily to prevent ammonia and mould.
  • Close guinea fowl and quail houses before dusk no exceptions.
  • Handle birds calmly; keep noise, dogs, and strangers away from pens.
  • Record mortality, eggs, feed, and sales in your logbook every day.
  • Inspect for predators, leaks, and draughts weekly; repair immediately.
