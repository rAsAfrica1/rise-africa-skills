Lesson 9.1~11 min

Reading Dung, Skin and Appetite: Your Daily Five-Minute Health Check

In this lesson
  • Score pen dung consistency on a four-point scale and record it as a daily herd health indicator
  • Identify the five nutrition-linked warning signs visible on a pig before a veterinarian is needed
  • Calculate the cost of a single lost weaner and use it to justify daily observation time

Most pig diseases on a small farm announce themselves in the feed trough and the dung channel long before a pig looks sick. A producer who walks the pens every morning with a notebook catches problems while they still cost a few dollars. A producer who only reacts when a pig is down is paying vet bills and burying stock.

Start with dung. Use a simple four-point score and write it on a card nailed to each pen: 1 = firm pellets or a firm log, 2 = soft but holding shape, 3 = loose, piles spreading, 4 = watery, running, often yellow or grey. Score the pen, not the pig, by looking at the whole floor. A pen that moves from a 1 or 2 up to a 3 for two mornings in a row is telling you something changed: a new bag of maize, a wet feed sack, a broken water nipple, or a batch of piglets that were weaned too abruptly. In weaners, watery yellow scour in the first two weeks after weaning is almost always enterotoxigenic E. coli, and the reference standards treat it as a feeding problem first and a drug problem second.

Next, appetite. Feed refusal is the single most sensitive signal you have. Deoxynivalenol, the mould toxin also called vomitoxin, starts reducing voluntary feed intake at 1 to 3 parts per million, well below the 5 ppm maximum considered safe for swine, and long before any pig vomits. So if pigs walk away from feed they ate happily last week, and nothing else changed, suspect the grain.

Then skin and coat. Rough, staring hair and a dull coat point at vitamin A shortage, which the standards list as causing reduced growth, poor coat quality and reduced immunity. Pale ears, pale snout and pale inner eyelids in suckling piglets point at iron-deficiency anaemia. Piglets are born with only about 40 mg of total body iron and need roughly 7 mg a day, so a piglet on a concrete or hard floor with no soil access runs out inside a week. Target haemoglobin is above 9 g per 100 ml; below that the piglet is already borderline anaemic and growing slowly.

Fourth, look at the vulva of young gilts. Swelling and reddening in gilts that are not in heat is the classic sign of zearalenone, the oestrogenic mould toxin, and repeated exposure around 1 ppm can cause uterine prolapse in young pigs. Fifth, watch breathing. Laboured breathing across a whole pen after a feed change is a serious sign; fumonisin above 100 ppm causes porcine pulmonary oedema.

Worked example. A farmer in Kiambu County, Kenya, runs 12 sows and weans about 220 piglets a year. She loses 8 weaners a year to scour. If a weaned piglet is worth in the range of 30 to 50 USD at weaning, and we take 40 USD as her local figure, that is 8 x 40 = 320 USD a year walking out of the farm. Her total morning inspection time is 15 minutes across 10 pens. Over a year that is 15 x 365 = 5,475 minutes, about 91 hours. If catching problems two days earlier halves her scour losses, she saves 160 USD for 91 hours of walking she was doing anyway. She was already in the pens; the notebook is what was missing.

Build the routine like this:

  1. Same time every morning, before feeding, while pigs are still lying down.
  2. Count pigs standing versus lying. Pigs that stay down when feed arrives are the ones to examine.
  3. Score dung per pen and write the number on the card.
  4. Check every drinker actually flows. Dehydration cuts feed intake faster than any disease.
  5. Note anything you changed in the last 48 hours: new feed batch, new pen mates, weather, transport.

The notebook matters more than the pen. Three weeks of dung scores next to feed batch dates will tell you which supplier's maize is making your pigs sick, and that is information no drug can buy you.

Stopping Post-Weaning Diarrhoea with Fibre, Lactose and Feed Form

In this lesson
  • Sequence insoluble and soluble fibre across the three nursery phases to reduce scour
  • Set lactose inclusion levels for each weaner phase using published ranges
  • Compare the cost of a pelleted creep feed against the value of the extra weaning weight it buys

Post-weaning diarrhoea, or PWD, is the biggest single cause of loss between weaning and 20 kg on most African small and medium pig units. The piglet is taken off milk, mixed with strangers, moved to a new pen and offered dry feed all in one day. Its stomach acid is weak, its gut enzymes are immature, and E. coli takes the opportunity. Nutrition can remove most of that opportunity without a single antibiotic.

Fibre first, because it is the cheapest lever you have and you almost certainly already own the ingredients. Insoluble fibre, such as wheat bran, maize bran or oat hulls, speeds up passage of digesta through the gut. Faster passage means pathogenic bacteria have less time to colonise and less time to produce toxin, and published work consistently shows lower PWD incidence. The catch is that insoluble fibre reduces nutrient digestibility in pigs under 10 kg because their enzyme systems are immature, and inclusion above 5 percent in weaner diets can depress growth. Soluble fibre, such as pectin from fruit residues or guar gum, does the opposite: it ferments in the hindgut to volatile fatty acids including butyrate, which feed the gut lining cells and tighten the gut barrier. But soluble fibre also thickens gut contents and slows passage, which in some trials makes scour worse.

So you sequence them:

  1. Days 0 to 7 after weaning: lean on insoluble fibre at roughly 2 to 3 percent of the diet. Speed of passage is what you want in the first week.
  2. Days 7 to 14: move to a mix of about half soluble and half insoluble, letting the gut microbes adapt.
  3. After day 14: increase the soluble fraction to drive volatile fatty acid production and long-term gut health.

Hold total crude fibre in weaner diets to about 2 to 4 percent maximum. Grower and finisher diets tolerate 4 to 6 percent, and gestating sows do well on 6 to 8 percent because the bulk keeps a restricted-fed sow satisfied.

Lactose is the second lever. Include 5 to 10 percent lactose in creep and early nursery diets. It is easily fermented and feeds lactic acid bacteria, which lowers gut pH and crowds out E. coli. Dried whey is the usual carrier at 5 to 8 percent of the diet, supplying both lactose and protein. Taper lactose down through phases 2 and 3 as the piglet's own maltase and isomaltase enzymes develop. Lactose sources cost more than cereal, so this is a phase-1 investment, not a permanent one.

Feed form is the third lever and the one small producers most often skip. Pellets of 3 to 4 mm outperform meal on palatability, intake and feed conversion, with under 10 percent fines against variable fines in meal. Pellet quality matters: aim for a durability index above 80 percent and under 20 percent fines at the feeder. Keep pelleting temperature below 170 degrees Fahrenheit, about 77 degrees Celsius, or you scorch ingredients and damage protein through the Maillard reaction. Adding 3 to 4 percent fat protects nutrients during pelleting and improves palatability.

Worked example from Nigeria. A producer in Ogun State weans 40 piglets per batch. Creep feed costs in the region of 0.50 to 0.80 USD per kilogram against 0.35 to 0.45 for a plain nursery diet. Take 0.65 and 0.40. If each piglet eats 1.0 kg of creep over four weeks, the extra cost is 40 x 1.0 x (0.65 - 0.40) = 10 USD for the batch. Published work says piglets eating at least 0.8 kg of creep gain an extra 10 to 20 g per day before weaning and show 5 to 15 percent lower PWD incidence. If that saves just one 40 USD weaner in the batch, the 10 USD spend returns four times over, before counting the growth.

The rule of thumb: creep feeding pays when piglets actually eat at least 0.8 to 1.0 kg each. If your creep trays come out full, you are burning money and should fix palatability, feeder placement and pellet size before spending more.

Zinc Oxide and Copper: Doses, Legal Status and Safer Substitutes

In this lesson
  • State the pharmacological and nutritional dose ranges for zinc oxide and copper in pig diets
  • Explain why zinc oxide is banned as a growth promoter in the EU and what that means for export-linked producers
  • Calculate the inclusion weight of zinc oxide needed to reach a target ppm in a one-tonne batch

Zinc oxide and copper sulphate sit in an awkward place: both work, both are cheap, and both are being restricted worldwide. If you produce pork for a domestic African market you may still be able to use them. If you sell into a chain with European buyers, or if your national regulator follows EU rules, you need to know exactly where the lines are.

Start with the numbers. Zinc as a nutrient is required at about 100 ppm in nursery phase 1, 80 to 100 ppm in later nursery, grower and finisher, and 100 to 125 ppm for sows and boars. Pharmacological zinc oxide for post-weaning diarrhoea control is a different animal entirely: 2,000 to 3,000 ppm in phase 1 for the first two to three weeks after weaning, tapering to 1,000 to 1,500 ppm in phase 2 and 125 to 250 ppm in phase 3. At those levels published work reports a 40 to 60 percent reduction in PWD incidence in challenged environments, roughly 4 to 7 percent better average daily gain and 5 to 10 percent better feed conversion.

Copper follows the same pattern. Nutritional requirement is only 5 to 6 ppm baseline, with 15 to 20 ppm typical in nursery diets and 15 ppm for sows and boars under PIC guidelines. The pharmacological, bacteriostatic range is 125 to 250 ppm, where copper suppresses gram-negative bacteria in the gut. Above 250 ppm you are into chronic toxicity risk. Copper sulphate is the common source and is more bioavailable than oxide forms.

The regulatory position matters. In the European Union, zinc oxide has been banned as a growth promoter since the 2022 regulations, and only nutritional levels of 80 to 150 ppm are permitted. North America still permits pharmacological zinc oxide for PWD control under 2024-2025 guidelines. The reasons for the ban are environmental accumulation of zinc in pig manure and a suspected link to antimicrobial resistance development. Many African regulators lag behind but buyers do not, so check both.

There is a nutritional trap as well. High dietary calcium above 1.0 percent reduces zinc absorption, and wide calcium to phosphorus ratios beyond about 1.5 to 1 interfere with phosphorus absorption. A farmer who over-adds limestone to save on dicalcium phosphate can create a zinc deficiency and a phosphorus problem at the same time.

Worked calculation. You want 2,500 ppm zinc in a 1,000 kg batch of phase 1 weaner feed. 2,500 ppm means 2,500 mg per kg, which is 2.5 g per kg, which is 2.5 kg of elemental zinc per tonne. Feed-grade zinc oxide is roughly 72 to 80 percent zinc; take 75 percent as a working figure and check the label on your bag. You need 2.5 / 0.75 = 3.33 kg of zinc oxide per tonne. Now subtract the zinc your premix already supplies. If your premix delivers 100 ppm, that is 0.1 kg elemental zinc per tonne, so you need (2.5 - 0.1) / 0.75 = 3.2 kg of zinc oxide per tonne. Getting 3.2 kg evenly through 1,000 kg by hand is the hard part: pre-mix it into 20 kg of maize meal, then mix that into the batch, or you will poison one pen and under-dose another.

A producer in Lilongwe, Malawi, supplying a supermarket chain that audits against European standards decided pharmacological zinc was not worth the contract risk. He moved to a combination approach: insoluble fibre in week one, formic acid at the low end of its 0.35 to 1.4 percent range, and dried whey for lactose. Published work suggests well-implemented alternatives close most of the gap, with a typical 0 to 3 percent performance difference against antibiotic growth promoters when management is good, and 5 to 15 percent when management is poor. His scour rate stayed acceptable; his contract stayed intact.

The honest summary: pharmacological zinc and copper work, they are being phased out, and the alternatives in the next lesson require better management rather than a bigger cheque.

Organic Acids, Probiotics and Essential Oils: Building a Practical Gut-Health Package

In this lesson
  • Select the right organic acid for each nursery phase based on its mechanism
  • Compare probiotic, prebiotic and essential-oil options on expected performance response
  • Cost a gut-health additive package per tonne of feed and per pig produced

Once you decide to reduce or remove pharmacological zinc and antibiotic growth promoters, you need a package, not a single product. No one additive replaces them. The published performance gap is 0 to 3 percent when alternatives are used well alongside good management, and 5 to 15 percent when management is poor. Read that carefully: the additives do not rescue a dirty, overcrowded, badly ventilated pen.

Organic acids are the foundation. A weaned piglet cannot acidify its own stomach properly, so pH sits around 4.5 to 5.5 instead of the 3.5 to 4.5 that kills E. coli. Formic acid at 0.35 to 1.4 percent of the diet pulls stomach pH down to roughly 3.5 to 4.0 and is bactericidal to gram-negative pathogens including ETEC and Salmonella. Expect around 4 to 7 percent better average daily gain and 5 to 10 percent better feed conversion in challenged conditions. The drawbacks are real: formic acid corrodes mixers and augers and irritates the airways of whoever is mixing, so buy it as a buffered or salt form if you can and always use gloves and a mask. Lactic acid at 0.5 to 1.0 percent does a similar pH job and is particularly effective against ETEC-F18, with 10 to 15 percent PWD reduction reported in susceptible herds. Propionic acid at 0.5 to 1.0 percent gives less pH effect but inhibits mould growth in stored feed, which is a second benefit in humid climates.

Butyric acid is different and often misused. At 0.2 to 0.5 percent, usually as sodium or calcium butyrate for stability, it is not there for pH. It is the energy source for the cells lining the large intestine, it strengthens the gut barrier by increasing tight junction expression, and it is anti-inflammatory. Its benefits accumulate, so use it in mid and late nursery rather than day one. A sound sequence is: phase 1 formic or lactic acid for pH, phases 2 and 3 shift to butyrate for barrier and energy.

Probiotics come next. Bacillus subtilis at 1 to 5 billion CFU per kg is spore-forming, which means it survives pelleting, and it improves protein and amino acid digestibility. Lactobacillus plantarum at 1 to 10 billion CFU per kg produces lactic acid and antimicrobial peptides and competitively excludes ETEC. Saccharomyces cerevisiae yeast at 0.1 to 0.5 percent brings beta-glucans that stimulate immunity. Expect 5 to 15 percent average daily gain improvement in challenged environments and much less in clean ones. Prebiotics are more modest: mannan oligosaccharides at 0.1 to 0.2 percent bind E. coli and Salmonella so they cannot stick to the gut wall, fructooligosaccharides at 0.5 to 1.0 percent feed Lactobacillus, and both give roughly 3 to 8 percent gain improvement, best combined with something else.

Essential oils are the strongest performing plant option. A blend of carvacrol, thymol and cinnamaldehyde at 0.05 to 0.15 percent, meaning 100 to 150 mg per kg of diet, produced in 2023 work a 15 to 25 percent average daily gain improvement over days 1 to 14 and 10 to 18 percent over days 1 to 42, with 5 to 10 percent better feed conversion. The mechanisms are direct antimicrobial action, taller intestinal villi, stronger tight junctions and higher digestive enzyme activity. Because essential oils oxidise quickly, microencapsulated products work more consistently, at a 1.5 to 2 times cost premium.

Worked costing. A producer in Kampala, Uganda, mixes one tonne of phase 2 weaner feed. She adds lactic acid at 0.7 percent, which is 7 kg per tonne, Bacillus subtilis at the manufacturer's rate, and an encapsulated essential oil blend at 0.1 percent, which is 1 kg per tonne. Suppose she pays 2.00 USD per kg for the acid, 12.00 for the probiotic dose per tonne and 25.00 per kg for the encapsulated oil. Her additive cost per tonne is (7 x 2.00) + 12.00 + (1 x 25.00) = 14 + 12 + 25 = 51 USD per tonne. One tonne of phase 2 feed carries roughly 45 piglets through that phase at about 22 kg of feed each, so the cost is 51 / 45 = 1.13 USD per piglet. Against a weaner worth 30 to 50 USD, saving one piglet in every 40 pays for the package outright. Use your own local prices; these are illustrative and vary widely.

Buy on evidence rank, not on the sales pitch. The best-studied categories are probiotics, organic acids and phytogenics. Enzymes and immunostimulants have moderate evidence. Antimicrobial peptides and bacteriophages are still emerging with limited herd-level data.

Mycotoxins in Maize, Cassava and Oilseed Cake: Limits, Testing and Blending Maths

In this lesson
  • Recall the safe feed limits for aflatoxin, DON, zearalenone and fumonisin in swine
  • Calculate a safe blending ratio for contaminated grain using a dilution equation
  • Design a low-cost store management routine that keeps grain below 15 percent moisture

In hot, humid regions mycotoxins are not an occasional problem, they are a permanent tax on pig performance. Maize, groundnut cake and cottonseed cake are the usual carriers. The toxins do not smell, do not always show as visible mould, and are not destroyed by cooking or normal processing. Prevention and dilution are your only practical tools on a small farm.

Know the limits. Aflatoxin: 20 parts per billion for breeding animals and pregnant sows, 20 ppb for grower pigs in the 20 to 50 lb range, and 200 ppb for finishers over 100 lb, which reflects better metabolic clearance in bigger pigs. Above 2,000 ppb you get severe liver dysfunction, haemorrhages, jaundice and sudden death; 500 to 2,000 ppb causes chronic toxicity and liver lesions; even 100 to 300 ppb sustained causes immunosuppression. Deoxynivalenol: 5 ppm maximum for swine of all ages, with feed refusal already at 1 to 3 ppm, reduced growth at 5 to 10 ppm, complete refusal and vomiting above 10 ppm, and severe digestive lesions above 20 ppm. Zearalenone: no official FDA limit, but industry recommends 1 ppm or less; 1 ppm repeated in young gilts can cause uterine prolapse, 5 to 10 ppm reduces fertility and causes swollen vulva and early embryo loss, above 30 ppm in sows causes embryo loss, above 40 ppm in boars reduces libido and sperm quality. Fumonisin: 10 ppm maximum, with growth reduction at 10 ppm and fatal porcine pulmonary oedema above 100 ppm. Ochratoxin A has no official swine limit; a precautionary guideline borrowed from poultry is under 1 ppb, with chronic kidney damage from 100 to 1,000 ppb.

Prevention beats everything. Dry grain to 15 percent moisture or below. Store below 15 degrees Celsius where possible, which is difficult in the tropics, so focus instead on low humidity, good airflow, pallets off the floor, and preventing condensation on iron roofs. Never store new grain on top of old residue. Test high-risk sources such as maize and barley before purchase where you can. Lateral flow rapid test kits give an indicative answer in minutes; HPLC laboratory testing is the gold standard with a two to seven day turnaround.

When you are already holding contaminated grain, dilution is the standard mitigation. The equation is simple: (weight A x concentration A) + (weight B x concentration B) divided by total weight = final concentration.

Worked example from Zambia. A farmer near Chipata buys 3 tonnes of maize and a rapid test reads about 60 ppb aflatoxin. His finisher pigs are over 100 lb so their limit is 200 ppb and he could feed it, but he also has gestating sows whose limit is 20 ppb. He has 5 tonnes of clean maize testing below detection, call it 2 ppb. If he blends all 8 tonnes: (3 x 60) + (5 x 2) = 180 + 10 = 190, divided by 8 tonnes = 23.75 ppb. Still above the 20 ppb sow limit. So he asks how much clean maize he needs with 3 tonnes of the 60 ppb lot to reach 20 ppb. Let X be tonnes of clean maize: (180 + 2X) / (3 + X) = 20, so 180 + 2X = 60 + 20X, so 120 = 18X, so X = 6.67 tonnes. He does not have that much. His better decision: send all 3 contaminated tonnes to the finisher pens where the 200 ppb limit applies, keep the clean maize for the sow and weaner rations, and buy a clay-based binder for the finisher feed. Binders such as bentonite, montmorillonite or yeast cell wall extract cost roughly 5 to 20 USD per tonne of premix and reduce bioavailability by about 30 to 50 percent, which is helpful but is not a licence to feed heavily contaminated grain.

The critical management rule: never feed suspect grain to breeding stock or young pigs. They are the most sensitive and the most valuable. Route questionable batches to older finishers where the tolerance is genuinely higher, and record which batch went to which pen so that if performance drops you can trace it.

Iron, Selenium and Vitamin E: The Quiet Deficiencies That Cost You Pigs

In this lesson
  • Implement an iron protocol for suckling piglets in concrete-floor systems
  • Explain how selenium and vitamin E work together and set correct dietary levels
  • Mix a low-cost oral iron creep supplement from ingredients available locally

Three deficiencies account for a large share of unexplained poor performance on small pig units: iron in suckling piglets, selenium in boars and sows, and vitamin E under stress. None of them produce a dramatic outbreak. They produce pale piglets, poor conception, weak litters and pigs that grow slowly for no obvious reason.

Iron first. A newborn piglet has about 40 mg of total body iron and needs roughly 7 mg daily. Sow milk supplies very little. On soil, piglets root and pick up enough iron incidentally. On concrete or slats, they do not, and anaemia appears within one to two weeks. Requirements are 60 to 200 mg per kg of diet for creep and nursing piglets, 80 to 100 for weaners, 40 to 100 for growers, 40 to 80 for finishers and 80 for sows and boars.

The standard solution is injectable iron dextran given intramuscularly on days 1 to 4 of life. Dose 100 mg where weaning is at three weeks or earlier, and 150 to 200 mg for later weaning. Efficacy is about 95 percent in preventing anaemia. If injections are not practical, there is a published homemade oral option: mix sow lactation ration at 88 percent, ferrous sulphate at 10 percent and maize or other vegetable oil at 2 percent, and place it in shallow trays inside the creep area. Piglets consume it readily and haemoglobin is maintained adequately. The oil keeps the powder from separating and improves palatability.

Worked example. A farmer in Kaduna State, Nigeria, weans piglets at 28 days on a concrete floor and cannot reliably buy iron dextran. She needs 10 kg of oral supplement for the month. That is 8.8 kg of sow lactation ration, 1.0 kg of ferrous sulphate and 0.2 kg of vegetable oil. If lactation ration costs 0.50 USD per kg, ferrous sulphate 3.00 per kg and oil 2.00 per kg, the batch costs (8.8 x 0.50) + (1.0 x 3.00) + (0.2 x 2.00) = 4.40 + 3.00 + 0.40 = 7.80 USD for 10 kg, or 0.78 per kg. Spread across 60 piglets that is 0.13 USD per piglet. One anaemic piglet lost is worth 30 to 50 USD. Use your own local prices, but the ratio will not change much.

Selenium and vitamin E work as one system and must be discussed together. Selenium is the cofactor for glutathione peroxidase, an enzyme that protects cell membranes and sperm from lipid peroxidation. Vitamin E is the fat-soluble antioxidant working alongside it. Give one without the other and you waste both. Growing pigs need 0.15 to 0.30 mg selenium per kg of diet, sows 0.20 to 0.30, and reproducing boars 0.30 to 0.50 because of semen quality demands. The documented boar response to 0.5 ppm selenium against unsupplemented controls is striking: sperm motility rose from 60.4 to 87.9 percent, normal morphology from 24.2 to 61.9 percent, and fertilisation rate from 73 to 99 percent. If you keep a boar and your conception rate is disappointing, check selenium before you replace the boar.

Selenium source matters. Inorganic sodium selenite is absorbed rapidly but leaves minimal tissue reserves. Organic selenomethionine from selenium yeast is incorporated into body protein and mobilised during stress, which is why modern systems prefer it for boars. Note that EU regulation caps selenised yeast at 0.2 mg selenium per kg of complete feed. Selenium is genuinely toxic in excess, so never guess and never double-dose two premixes that both contain it.

Vitamin E requirements are 30 to 44 IU per kg for weaners, 22 to 30 for growers, 11 to 22 for finishers, and 44 for sows and boars. Under immune challenge or stress the standard 11 to 16 IU per kg is inadequate and 44 IU per kg or more is appropriate, with research levels reaching 100 to 200 IU per kg. Practical stress periods on an African farm are obvious: weaning, mixing groups, long transport to market on a hot day, and the hottest weeks of the year. Raising vitamin E for two weeks around those events is cheap insurance.

One last mineral note. Vitamin A at 2,000 to 2,200 IU per kg for weaners down to 1,300 to 1,600 for finishers, and 2,000 to 4,000 for breeding stock, matters for immunity and coat quality, and excess has minimal toxicity in swine, so this is one place where a generous margin is safe.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. At what level of deoxynivalenol (DON) does voluntary feed intake in pigs already begin to fall?

Feed refusal is a subclinical effect starting at 1-3 ppm, below the 5 ppm level treated as the swine maximum. That is why sudden loss of appetite is an early mould-toxin warning.

2. A suckling piglet's haemoglobin is measured at 8 g per 100 ml of blood. What does this indicate?

The target is above 9 g per 100 ml. A reading below that indicates borderline iron-deficiency anaemia and calls for iron supplementation.

3. Roughly how much total body iron is a newborn piglet born with?

Newborn pigs carry about 40 mg total body iron and require around 7 mg per day, so reserves are exhausted quickly on floors with no soil access.

4. Swelling and reddening of the vulva in young gilts that are not in heat most strongly suggests which feed problem?

Zearalenone is an oestrogenic mycotoxin. Repeated exposure around 1 ppm in young pigs can even cause uterine prolapse.

5. Why should the daily pen walk happen before feeding rather than after?

Observing before feed arrives lets you see which pigs do not get up for their meal, which is the earliest and cheapest sign of illness.

6. In the first seven days after weaning, which fibre type is preferred and why?

Insoluble fibre at 2-3 percent accelerates digesta passage, giving pathogens less time to colonise and produce toxin. Soluble fibre comes in later once microbes have adapted.

7. What is the recommended lactose inclusion in creep and early nursery diets?

5-10 percent lactose supports lactic acid bacteria and pH reduction. It is tapered in later phases as maltase and isomaltase develop.

8. Above what inclusion level can insoluble fibre depress growth in weaner diets?

Inclusion above about 5 percent in weaner diets can depress growth because young pigs cannot digest fibre efficiently.

9. What is the recommended maximum pelleting temperature to avoid ingredient damage?

Above roughly 170 degrees F, ingredients scorch and the Maillard reaction damages protein quality, which defeats the purpose of a high-quality creep feed.

10. Creep feeding is generally economic when piglets consume at least how much?

The performance benefits in published trials appear at intakes at or above roughly 0.8 kg per piglet. Below that, the extra cost of creep feed is not recovered.

11. What is the pharmacological zinc oxide level used in nursery phase 1 for PWD control?

Phase 1 pharmacological levels are 2,000-3,000 ppm for the first two to three weeks post-weaning, then tapered down through later phases.

12. Under EU rules since 2022, zinc may be included in pig feed at what levels?

The EU banned zinc oxide as a growth promoter but still permits zinc at nutritional levels of 80-150 ppm.

13. Copper becomes a chronic toxicity risk above which level?

The bacteriostatic range is 125-250 ppm; above 250 ppm chronic copper toxicity becomes a real risk.

14. You need 2.5 kg of elemental zinc per tonne and your zinc oxide is 75 percent zinc. Roughly how much product do you add per tonne?

2.5 divided by 0.75 gives about 3.33 kg of zinc oxide product per tonne, before subtracting zinc already supplied by the premix.

15. Why does over-adding limestone to a pig ration risk a zinc problem?

High dietary calcium above 1.0 percent reduces zinc absorption, so excess limestone can cause a functional zinc deficiency even when the premix looks adequate.

16. Which acid is chosen mainly for gut barrier function and colonocyte energy rather than lowering stomach pH?

Butyric acid, usually as a stable salt at 0.2-0.5 percent, feeds the large intestine lining and strengthens tight junctions. It is not primarily a pH tool.

17. What is the reported performance gap between antibiotic growth promoter use and AGP-free production when alternatives and management are both good?

With optimal alternatives the gap is 0-3 percent. It widens to 5-15 percent where management is suboptimal.

18. Which probiotic is spore-forming and therefore survives the pelleting process?

Bacillus subtilis forms spores, making it stable through pelleting, and it also improves protein and amino acid digestibility.

19. An essential oil blend is added at 0.1 percent of the diet. How much product goes into one tonne of feed?

0.1 percent of 1,000 kg is 1 kg per tonne. Getting that small quantity mixed evenly requires pre-mixing into a carrier.

20. Besides lowering pH, what practical second benefit does propionic acid offer in a humid climate?

Propionic acid inhibits mould growth in feed, a useful extra where storage humidity is a constant risk.

21. What is the aflatoxin limit for pregnant sows and breeding animals?

Breeding animals and pregnant sows are held to 20 ppb. Finishers over 100 lb have a higher 200 ppb limit because of better metabolic clearance.

22. You blend 3 tonnes of maize at 60 ppb aflatoxin with 5 tonnes at 2 ppb. What is the resulting concentration?

(3 x 60) + (5 x 2) = 190, divided by 8 tonnes gives about 23.75 ppb, which is still above the 20 ppb sow limit.

23. How effective are mycotoxin binders such as bentonite or yeast cell wall extract?

Binders typically reduce bioavailability by 30-50 percent depending on toxin and binder type, at roughly 5-20 USD per tonne of premix. They reduce risk but do not make contaminated grain safe.

24. Fumonisin above which level causes fatal porcine pulmonary oedema?

10 ppm is the recommended maximum with growth reduction; above 100 ppm severe pulmonary oedema, cyanosis and death occur.

25. What target moisture content makes stored grain reasonably safe from mould growth?

Drying to 15 percent moisture or below is the standard target for safe storage, combined with low humidity and no condensation.

26. When should injectable iron dextran be given to piglets?

Iron dextran is given intramuscularly on days 1-4, before reserves run out. Waiting for visible pallor means growth has already been lost.

27. In the published homemade oral iron supplement, what percentage is ferrous sulphate?

The recipe is 88 percent sow lactation ration, 10 percent ferrous sulphate and 2 percent corn or vegetable oil, fed in shallow creep trays.

28. Which nutrient works synergistically with selenium in the glutathione peroxidase antioxidant system?

Vitamin E is the fat-soluble antioxidant partner. Inadequate vitamin E reduces the benefit of selenium supplementation, and vice versa.

29. Why is organic selenium (selenomethionine from Se-yeast) often preferred for boars?

Organic selenium builds tissue reserves that can be mobilised under stress, whereas inorganic sodium selenite turns over rapidly with minimal retention.

30. A boar herd has poor conception rates. Which selenium level would be appropriate for the boar diet?

Reproducing boars require 0.30-0.50 mg selenium per kg of diet, higher than growing pigs, specifically to support semen quality.

Step 4: inspect and photograph your grain store, note moisture feel, mould smell, insect damage and how long grain has been held.
