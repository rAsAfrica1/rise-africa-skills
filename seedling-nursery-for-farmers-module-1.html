1. Overview of Nursery Farming

A seedling nursery is a place where you raise young plants from seeds or cuttings until they are strong enough to be transplanted into the field. In Africa, most smallholder farmers buy seedlings from commercial nurseries or receive them from projects, but you can also grow your own. Nursery farming is not complicated. It requires a small plot of land, water, and daily care. You can start with as little as 1 meter by 2 meters of space. The goal is to produce healthy seedlings that survive transplanting and give you a good harvest.

Nurseries are important because they give seeds a protected start. In the open field, seeds face birds, insects, drought, and weeds. In a nursery, you control water, shade, and pests. For example, tomato seedlings raised in a nursery for 3 to 4 weeks can be transplanted when they have 4 to 6 true leaves. This gives them a head start over direct-seeded plants. Nursery farming also allows you to grow crops that need a long season, such as onions or peppers, in areas with short rains.

There are different scales of nursery farming. A home nursery is for your own garden. A community nursery serves a group of farmers. A commercial nursery sells seedlings to others. All three use the same basic techniques: good seeds, fertile soil mix, proper watering, and protection from sun and pests. The difference is mainly in size and management.

Starting a nursery requires planning. You need to know which crops you want to grow, when to plant, and where to get seeds. You also need a reliable water source. Many farmers fail because they water too little or too much. A simple rule is to keep the soil moist but not soaked. You can test by squeezing a handful of soil; it should hold together but not drip water.

In this module, you will learn the benefits of growing your own seedlings, the types of nurseries, basic requirements, how to choose a site, and how to set up a simple nursery. By the end, you will be able to start your own nursery with local materials and minimal cost.

2. Benefits of Growing Your Own Seedlings

Growing your own seedlings saves money. A tray of 100 tomato seedlings from a commercial nursery can cost 5,000 to 10,000 shillings or more. If you grow your own, you spend only on seeds, soil, and water. For example, a packet of improved tomato seeds costs about 2,000 shillings and can produce over 200 seedlings. That is a saving of 80% or more. You can also share or sell extra seedlings to neighbours.

You control the quality. When you buy seedlings, you do not know how old they are or whether they were raised with the right care. Some may be diseased or weak. When you grow your own, you choose healthy seeds, use clean soil, and water properly. You can also harden off seedlings before transplanting, which means gradually exposing them to sun and wind so they survive the shock of moving to the field. Hardening off for 3 to 5 days can increase survival rates by 50%.

You get better timing. Many farmers miss the start of the rains because they wait for seedlings from others. With your own nursery, you can sow seeds 3 to 4 weeks before the rains and transplant as soon as the soil is wet. This means your crop matures earlier, and you can sell when prices are high. For example, early maize can fetch double the price of late maize.

You can grow varieties that are not available locally. Maybe you want a drought-tolerant sorghum or a disease-resistant tomato. By raising your own seedlings, you can try new varieties from research stations or seed companies. You can also produce seedlings for high-value crops like kale, spinach, or watermelon that are not commonly sold as seedlings.

Finally, nursery farming is a skill that creates business opportunities. Once you learn how to raise healthy seedlings, you can start a small commercial nursery. Many women and youth in Africa earn income by selling seedlings to other farmers. You can start with a few trays and expand as you gain experience. The investment is low, and the demand for good seedlings is always high.

3. Types of Nurseries: Home, Community, Commercial

There are three main types of nurseries: home, community, and commercial. Each has its own purpose, size, and management. A home nursery is the smallest. It is usually a small bed or a few trays near your house. You grow seedlings for your own garden. You might produce 50 to 200 seedlings at a time. A home nursery can be as simple as a wooden box filled with soil, placed under a tree for shade. You water it with a watering can or a bucket with small holes. The advantage is low cost and easy access. You can check your seedlings every day.

A community nursery is run by a group of farmers, often a cooperative or a women's group. It is larger than a home nursery, maybe 10 meters by 10 meters. The group shares the work and the cost. They might produce 5,000 to 20,000 seedlings per season. A community nursery often has a fence, a water tank, and a shade net. The group sells seedlings to members at a low price and to outsiders at a higher price. The money is used to buy more seeds and maintain the nursery. This type builds teamwork and allows farmers to access improved varieties that they could not afford alone.

A commercial nursery is a business. It produces seedlings for sale on a large scale. It may have greenhouses, irrigation systems, and paid workers. Commercial nurseries produce tens of thousands of seedlings per season. They focus on high-demand crops like tomatoes, onions, cabbages, and peppers. They often sell in trays or plastic bags. To start a commercial nursery, you need more capital, but the profits can be high. You must also know your market and have reliable water.

Which type should you choose? Start with a home nursery. Learn the basics. Then, if you have success, you can expand to a community nursery with neighbours. If you see a strong demand, you can turn it into a commercial nursery. Many successful commercial nursery owners in Africa started with a small home nursery. The key is to master the skills first, then grow.

4. Basic Requirements for a Nursery

To start a nursery, you need five basic things: water, soil, seeds, containers, and shade. Water is the most critical. You need a reliable source that is close to the nursery. A borehole, well, river, or piped water is ideal. You will need about 5 to 10 liters of water per square meter per day, depending on the weather. In hot areas, you may need to water twice a day, morning and evening. A watering can with a fine rose is best because it does not wash away seeds. You can also use a bucket with small holes in the lid.

Soil for nursery should be loose, fertile, and well-drained. Do not use ordinary garden soil alone because it can become hard and may contain weed seeds or diseases. A good mix is 3 parts soil, 2 parts compost or well-rotted manure, and 1 part sand. This gives nutrients and good drainage. You can sterilize the soil by spreading it on a black plastic sheet under the sun for 2 to 3 days. This kills many pests and weed seeds.

Seeds must be good quality. Buy certified seeds from a reputable agro-dealer or save seeds from your best plants. Check the expiry date on the packet. Old seeds may not germinate. For most vegetables, you need 100 to 200 seeds per gram. For example, tomato seeds are small; 1 gram has about 300 seeds. Sow seeds at the right depth: generally, 2 to 3 times the diameter of the seed. Tiny seeds like lettuce should be sown on the surface and pressed gently.

Containers can be trays, plastic bags, or even old tins and banana leaves. The important thing is that they have drainage holes. You can make seed beds directly on the ground, but they are harder to manage. Seed trays with cells are good because they reduce root disturbance when transplanting. You can buy trays or make your own from wood. Plastic bags of 10 cm by 15 cm are common for tree seedlings.

Shade is needed to protect young seedlings from strong sun and heavy rain. You can use a shade net, palm fronds, or grass. A simple shade structure is a frame of sticks covered with a 50% shade net. In the first week after sowing, keep seedlings under shade. Then gradually reduce shade so they get used to full sun. This is part of hardening off.

5. Site Selection Criteria

Choosing the right site for your nursery is important. A bad site can mean lost seedlings and wasted money. Here are the key things to look for. First, water. The site must be close to a reliable water source. You do not want to carry water long distances. A nursery needs water every day. If the site is more than 50 meters from water, you will waste time and energy. Also, the site should not be in a place that floods. Seedlings can drown or get fungal diseases if water stands.

Second, sunlight. Most seedlings need bright light but not harsh, direct sun all day. A site that gets morning sun and afternoon shade is ideal. In very hot areas, you may need 50% shade. In cooler areas, more sun is better. Avoid sites under big trees that drip water or drop leaves, as this can damage seedlings. Also, avoid areas where water collects, like at the bottom of a slope.

Third, soil. The site should have good soil that drains well. If the soil is heavy clay, you can improve it with compost and sand, or use raised beds. Avoid sites where crops like tomatoes, peppers, or potatoes were grown in the last 2 years, because they may carry diseases like bacterial wilt. A fresh site or one that has been fallow is best.

Fourth, accessibility. The nursery should be easy to reach. You will need to visit it daily. If it is far from your home, you may neglect it. Also, if you plan to sell seedlings, the site should be near a road or market so customers can reach you. But do not put it too close to a road where dust and animals can cause damage. A fence is important to keep out goats, chickens, and other animals.

Fifth, size. Start small. A site of 2 meters by 3 meters can produce 500 to 1,000 seedlings in trays. You can expand later. Make sure you have room for a water tank, a shed for tools, and space to work. Also, consider the wind. Strong winds can dry out seedlings and tear shade nets. A natural windbreak, like a hedge, can help. Finally, check the land ownership. If you do not own the land, get permission from the owner. For a community nursery, choose a central location that all members can access.

6. Setting Up a Simple Nursery

Setting up a simple nursery can be done in one day with local materials. Here is a step-by-step guide. First, clear the site. Remove grass, stones, and roots. Level the ground. If the site slopes, make terraces or raised beds to prevent erosion. Mark out a bed of 1 meter wide and 2 to 3 meters long. Keep paths of 50 cm between beds so you can work comfortably.

Second, build a shade structure. Use strong sticks or poles to make a frame 1.5 to 2 meters high. Cover it with a 50% shade net, or use palm fronds or grass. Leave the sides open for air but make sure the top is covered. In very hot areas, you may need shade on the sides too. Secure the net with ropes or nails. Make sure it does not touch the seedlings.

Third, prepare the seed bed or trays. If using a bed, dig the soil to 20 cm deep and mix in compost. Make a raised bed of 15 cm high. If using trays, fill them with your soil mix: 3 parts soil, 2 parts compost, 1 part sand. Sterilize the soil mix by solarization if possible. Fill trays to the top, firm gently, and water before sowing.

Fourth, sow the seeds. Make small furrows 1 cm deep for medium seeds, or broadcast tiny seeds on the surface. Cover with a thin layer of soil or compost. Water gently with a fine rose. Label each tray or row with the crop name and date. This helps you track growth and know when to transplant.

Fifth, manage water and pests. Water every morning and evening for the first week. After that, water once a day if the weather is cool, twice if hot. Check for pests like aphids, whiteflies, and cutworms. Handpick them or spray with a simple soap solution: mix 1 tablespoon of liquid soap in 1 liter of water. For fungal diseases, avoid overwatering and remove affected seedlings.

Sixth, harden off seedlings before transplanting. One week before transplanting, reduce watering and gradually remove the shade. Start with 2 hours of sun on day 1, then increase daily. This makes seedlings strong. Transplant when they have 4 to 6 true leaves, usually 3 to 4 weeks after sowing. Water the seedlings before transplanting to keep the roots intact. With this simple setup, you can produce healthy seedlings for your farm or for sale.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Introduction to Seedling Nursery

This practical reference is written for African farmers and agripreneurs who want to treat seedling production as a business, not just a garden chore. Whether you are raising 200 tomato seedlings for your own plot or 20,000 for sale, the principles are the same: healthy seed, clean media, correct watering, and a market you can actually reach. Use this module as a working document. Print it, laminate it, and keep it in the nursery shed.

1. Nursery Siting: The Five Non-Negotiables

Site selection is the cheapest decision you will ever make and the most expensive one to get wrong. A nursery placed badly will lose you money through disease, water hauling, and heat stress. Before you buy a single tray, walk the site and score it on these five criteria.

3. Basic Requirements: The Minimum Viable Nursery

You do not need a greenhouse to start. You need clean water, a clean medium, clean seed, and a structure that moderates temperature. The list below is the minimum for a functional nursery. Anything beyond this is an upgrade, not a prerequisite.

  • Water: tank or drum (200 L minimum), watering can with fine rose, drip line if possible.
  • Media: sterilised topsoil, river sand, and compost or coco-peat in a 1:1:1 mix. Never use fresh manure.
  • Containers: seedling trays (128200 cells), polythene tubes (for trees), or raised beds for bare-root.
  • Shade: 4050% shade net on a simple wooden or metal frame.
  • Seed: certified or quality-assured seed from a registered agro-dealer.
  • Records: a simple notebook or phone app for sowing date, germination %, and sales.

SAFETY WARNING SOIL STERILISATION AND AGROCHEMICALS

Never sterilise soil by burning a heap next to the nursery: fire risk, smoke damage to seedlings, and loss of beneficial organisms. If using chemical sterilants (e.g. formalin, methyl bromide alternatives), wear gloves, a mask, and eye protection, and work outdoors. Methyl bromide is banned or restricted in most African countries check with your national plant protection office. Store all agrochemicals in a locked cabinet away from seed, food, and children. Wash hands and tools after handling treated media. If you feel dizzy or your skin burns, stop immediately and seek medical help.

4. Setting Up a Simple Nursery: Step by Step

The following sequence is the one taught in most African extension services. It works in both humid and dry zones with minor adjustments.

  1. Clear and level the site. Remove grass, roots, and stones. A 3 m 6 m plot handles roughly 5,000 seedlings in trays.
  2. Build the frame. Use treated poles or galvanised pipe. Ridge height 2.2 m, side height 1.8 m for easy walking and airflow.
  3. Fix the shade net. 40% in highlands, 50% in hot lowlands. Leave the sides open for ventilation; add a windbreak on the windward side.
  4. Construct raised beds. 1 m wide, 20 cm high, 30 cm paths between beds. This improves drainage and reduces soil-borne disease.
  5. Prepare the medium. Mix topsoil, sand, and compost 1:1:1. Sieve to remove stones. Sterilise by solarisation (cover with clear plastic for 46 weeks) where possible.
  6. Fill and sow. Fill trays or tubes, firm gently, sow 12 seeds per cell at the recommended depth, cover lightly, and water with a fine rose.
  7. Manage. Water twice daily in hot weather, once in cool weather. Scout for pests and diseases daily. Harden off seedlings 57 days before transplanting.
  8. Record and sell. Track germination, losses, and sales. Price by seedling, not by tray, once you have a market.

5. The Business Case: Why Seedlings Beat Seed for Many African Farmers

A farmer who buys certified tomato seed at USD 0.05 per seed and raises it in a home nursery can produce a transplant worth USD 0.150.40 on the local market. The margin comes from germination care, uniformity, and reduced field losses. For a commercial nursery selling 50,000 vegetable seedlings per season at USD 0.10 each, gross revenue is USD 5,000 with media, labour, and water typically consuming 4050%. The economics work when (a) you sell in bulk to agro-dealers or cooperatives, (b) you specialise in 23 high-demand crops, and (c) you keep losses below 15%.

Tree seedlings follow a different curve: longer cycle (39 months), higher unit price (USD 0.201.50), and stronger demand from government, NGO, and carbon projects. In the Sahel and East Africa, grafted fruit tree seedlings (mango, avocado, citrus) command premium prices and are a reliable commercial niche.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Water source confirmed for dry season (tank or borehole, not just a stream).
  • Shade net 4050% installed with open sides for airflow.
  • Raised beds built, 1 m wide, 20 cm high, with 30 cm paths.
  • Media mixed 1:1:1 (topsoil : sand : compost), sieved and sterilised.
  • Certified seed purchased from a registered agro-dealer; sowing date recorded.
  • Daily scouting for pests, damping-off, and nutrient deficiency.
  • Buyer identified before sowing agro-dealer, cooperative, or project.
  • Records kept for germination %, losses, and sales per season.
