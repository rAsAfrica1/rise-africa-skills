Lessons

1. Identifying Common Cabbage Pests

Cabbage is attacked by several pests that can reduce your harvest by half or more if you don't act early. The most damaging are caterpillars: diamondback moth (DBM), cabbage looper, and cabbage white butterfly. DBM larvae are small (1-2 cm), light green, and wriggle violently when touched. They eat small holes in leaves, often from the underside. Cabbage loopers are larger (3-4 cm), green with white stripes, and move by looping. They chew large irregular holes. Cabbage white butterfly larvae are velvety green and also chew big holes. Another major pest is aphids: tiny (1-3 mm), soft-bodied, green or grey, clustering on new shoots and leaf undersides. They suck sap, causing curled leaves and transmitting viruses. You'll also see whiteflies (1-2 mm, white, fly up when disturbed) and thrips (slender, 1-2 mm, cause silvery streaks). Cutworms (3-4 cm, greasy grey) cut seedlings at ground level at night. Flea beetles (2-3 mm, shiny black) make small shot-holes in young leaves. To scout, check 10 random plants per field twice a week. Look on leaf undersides, in the growing point, and on the soil around stems. Use a hand lens (10x) for small pests. Record what you find. Early detection means you can use less pesticide and save money.

2. Integrated Pest Management (IPM) Principles

IPM means using many methods together to keep pests below damaging levels, not trying to kill every insect. It saves money, protects your health, and keeps beneficial insects alive. The steps are: prevention, monitoring, and intervention only when needed. First, prevent pests: rotate cropsâdon't plant cabbage where you grew cabbage, broccoli, or kale in the last 2 years. Use certified seed and resistant varieties like 'Copenhagen Market' or 'Green Challenger' if available. Space plants 45-60 cm apart to improve airflow. Control weeds because they host pests. Use row covers (fine mesh netting) over young plants to block butterflies and aphids. Second, monitor: scout your field twice a week. Check 10 plants per field. Count pests and note which ones. Set action thresholds: for DBM, treat if you find 2 or more small larvae per plant; for aphids, treat if 20% of plants have colonies. Third, intervene: start with the least harmful methods. Handpick caterpillars and drop them in soapy water. Spray a strong jet of water to knock off aphids. Use biological sprays like Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) for caterpillars, or neem oil for aphids and whiteflies. Only use chemical pesticides as a last resort, and choose ones that are less toxic to bees and natural enemies. Always follow label instructions. Rotate chemicals with different modes of action to prevent resistance. IPM is not about doing nothingâit's about doing the right thing at the right time.

3. Biological Control of Cabbage Pests

Biological control uses living organismsâpredators, parasites, and pathogensâto reduce pests. It's cheap, safe, and sustainable. For cabbage, the most important natural enemies are: parasitic wasps (tiny, 1-2 mm) that lay eggs inside DBM and aphids; ladybird beetles (both adults and larvae) that eat aphids; hoverfly larvae that eat aphids; and spiders and ground beetles that eat cutworms. You can buy some of these, but in Africa it's often easier to conserve what you already have. To encourage natural enemies: avoid broad-spectrum insecticides like lambda-cyhalothrin, which kill everything. Plant flowering plants near your cabbageâe.g., marigolds, sunflowers, coriander, and sweet alyssumâto provide nectar and pollen for adult parasitic wasps and hoverflies. Provide shelter with hedgerows or mulch. You can also apply biological sprays. Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) is a bacterium that kills caterpillars when they eat treated leaves. It comes as a powder or liquid; mix 1-2 grams per litre of water and spray in the evening. It only works on caterpillars, not aphids. For aphids and whiteflies, use neem oil (azadirachtin): mix 5 ml per litre of water with a few drops of soap, spray every 7-10 days. Beauveria bassiana is a fungus that infects aphids, whiteflies, and thrips; mix 2-5 grams per litre and spray. Always spray in the evening to avoid killing bees and to prevent sunlight from degrading the product. Reapply after rain. Biological control works best when started early, before pest numbers explode.

4. Common Cabbage Diseases and Symptoms

Cabbage gets several diseases that can wipe out your crop. Learn to recognise them early. Black rot: caused by bacteria. Leaves turn yellow in V-shaped patterns from the edges, then turn brown and dry. The veins turn black. It spreads in wet weather and enters through wounds. Clubroot: a soil-borne fungus. Plants wilt in the day and recover at night. Roots swell into ugly clubs. It survives in soil for up to 20 years. Downy mildew: fungus-like. Yellow patches on upper leaf surface, white fluffy growth on the underside. Spreads in cool, wet weather. Alternaria leaf spot: fungus. Brown spots with concentric rings (like a target) on older leaves. Spreads by wind and rain. Blackleg: fungus. Stem base turns black and shrivels; plants collapse. Powdery mildew: fungus. White powdery patches on leaves, usually in dry, warm weather. Fusarium wilt: fungus. Leaves yellow and wilt on one side; stem inside turns brown. To identify, look at: where symptoms start (old or new leaves), pattern (spots, blights, wilts), and signs (fluff, powder, black veins). Use a hand lens. If unsure, take a photo and ask an extension officer. Prevention is key: rotate crops, use resistant varieties, don't work in wet fields, remove infected plants immediately, and disinfect tools. Once a plant is severely diseased, pull it out and burn itâdo not compost.

5. Fungicides and Bactericides Application

When diseases threaten your cabbage, you may need to spray fungicides or bactericides. But they only work if used correctly. First, identify the diseaseâfungicides don't kill bacteria, and bactericides don't kill fungi. For fungal diseases like downy mildew, alternaria, or powdery mildew, use fungicides. Common active ingredients in Africa: mancozeb (e.g., Dithane M-45), chlorothalonil (e.g., Bravo), copper hydroxide (e.g., Kocide), and metalaxyl (e.g., Ridomil). For bacterial diseases like black rot, use copper-based bactericides (copper hydroxide or copper oxychloride) or a mix of copper and mancozeb. Always read the label: check dose, safety, and pre-harvest interval (PHI). For example, mancozeb PHI is 7 days; copper PHI is 1-2 days. Mix only what you need. Use clean water. Wear protective clothing: gloves, boots, mask, and long sleeves. Spray in the early morning or late evening when it's cool and calm. Cover both sides of leaves, especially undersides. Use a knapsack sprayer with a fine nozzle. Spray every 7-14 days depending on disease pressure and rain. Rotate chemicals with different modes of action to prevent resistanceâdon't use the same product all season. For example, alternate mancozeb with copper. Never spray when it's about to rain. Store chemicals in a locked cabinet away from food and children. Triple-rinse empty containers and puncture them. Never reuse containers. Dispose of them safely. Remember: fungicides and bactericides are tools, not cures. Use them with IPM.

6. Preventive Measures and Sanitation

The best way to manage pests and diseases is to prevent them from starting. Sanitation is your first line of defence. Start with clean land: remove all crop debris from previous crops. Burn or bury infected plant materialâdo not compost it. Clean your tools: wash shovels, hoes, and pruning shears with soapy water and then disinfect with 10% bleach solution (1 part bleach to 9 parts water) between fields. Use certified disease-free seed. If saving your own seed, only take from healthy plants. Treat seeds before planting: soak in hot water at 50Â°C for 20 minutes, then cool in cold water. This kills black rot bacteria. Practice crop rotation: don't plant cabbage or its relatives (broccoli, cauliflower, kale, turnip) in the same spot for at least 2 years. Use raised beds or ridges to improve drainageâmost diseases need wet soil. Water at the base of plants, not on leaves. Use drip irrigation or a watering can. If using overhead irrigation, water early in the day so leaves dry before night. Mulch with straw or plastic to prevent soil splash onto leaves. Control weeds because they host pests and diseases. Remove volunteer cabbage plants. Space plants correctly (45-60 cm) for airflow. Stake or prune lower leaves that touch the ground. Inspect your field twice a week. Remove and destroy any plant with disease symptoms immediately. If you see just a few pests, handpick them. Keep a clean field borderâmow grass and remove rubbish. Finally, keep records: note when pests appear, what you sprayed, and the results. This helps you plan better next season. Good sanitation costs little but saves a lot.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Pest and Disease Management for Cabbage Farming in Africa

This practical reference consolidates the core field actions for managing cabbage pests and diseases in African smallholder and commercial operations. It is designed as a decision-support tool: use it during scouting, before spraying, and when planning preventive sanitation. The focus is on protecting marketable yield, reducing input costs, and meeting buyer quality standards.

Why this matters for your business

Cabbage is a high-value leafy vegetable in East and Southern Africa, but it is also one of the most pest-sensitive crops. Diamondback moth, aphids, and cabbage whitefly can reduce marketable heads by 30â60% if uncontrolled. Fungal diseases such as black rot and downy mildew can cause total field loss within two weeks under humid conditions. Each spray costs money; each rejected crate costs margin. IPM is therefore a commercial discipline, not an environmental luxury.

Identifying Common Cabbage Pests

Scout twice weekly from transplanting. Walk a W-pattern and inspect 10 plants per bed. Key pests:

  • Diamondback moth (Plutella xylostella): small green larvae that wriggle when touched; cause "window-paning" holes in leaves. Resistant populations are common in Kenya, Tanzania, and South Africa.
  • Cabbage aphid (Brevicoryne brassicae): grey-green colonies on leaf undersides; cause leaf curl and transmit viruses.
  • Cabbage whitefly (Aleyrodes proletella): white adults and scale-like nymphs; sooty mould reduces photosynthesis and market value.
  • Cutworms and armyworms: cut seedlings at soil level; most damaging within 2 weeks of transplanting.
  • Cabbage webworm and loopers: webbing and large irregular holes; common in warm, dry periods.

Integrated Pest Management (IPM) Principles

IPM combines cultural, biological, and chemical tools in a sequence that protects natural enemies and delays resistance. The sequence is: prevent â monitor â identify â act â record. Never spray on a calendar schedule. Spray only when scouting confirms the threshold. Rotate active ingredients between chemical groups. Keep a spray log with date, product, rate, and pest stage.

Biological Control of Cabbage Pests

Africa has strong natural enemy populations. Conserve them by avoiding broad-spectrum insecticides early in the season.

  • Diamondback moth: Diadegma semiclausum and Cotesia plutellae parasitoids are effective in Kenya and Tanzania. Release or conserve them.
  • Aphids: Ladybirds, lacewings, and hoverflies reduce colonies rapidly. Avoid pyrethroids.
  • Whitefly: Encarsia and Eretmocerus parasitoids; also use yellow sticky traps for monitoring.
  • Soil pests: Entomopathogenic nematodes and Beauveria bassiana can suppress cutworms.

Fungicides and Bactericides Application

Apply only after correct diagnosis. Fungicides do not cure bacterial diseases. Use the following rules:

  • Calibrate sprayer before each season; replace worn nozzles.
  • Spray early morning or late afternoon to reduce evaporation and bee harm.
  • Cover both leaf surfaces; cabbage heads need thorough coverage.
  • Respect pre-harvest intervals (PHI). Common PHIs: mancozeb 7 days, chlorothalonil 7 days, metalaxyl 7â14 days. Always read the label.
  • Rotate chemical groups to prevent resistance. Do not mix more than two products unless the label allows it.

â  RED SAFETY WARNING

Pesticides are hazardous. Always wear a long-sleeved shirt, trousers, boots, gloves, and a face mask. Never spray against the wind. Never eat, drink, or smoke while spraying. Store pesticides in a locked cabinet away from children, food, and animal feed. Triple-rinse empty containers and puncture them; never reuse for water or food. If symptoms of poisoning occur (headache, dizziness, vomiting, blurred vision), stop work, move to fresh air, and seek medical help immediately. In Kenya, contact the Pest Control Products Board (PCPB) or a local health facility. In South Africa, contact the Poisons Information Centre. Keep the product label for the doctor.

Preventive Measures and Sanitation

  • Rotation: Do not plant cabbage or other brassicas in the same bed for at least 3 years. Rotate with maize, beans, or cereals.
  • Certified seed: Buy seed from licensed agro-dealers. Avoid farm-saved seed from diseased fields.
  • Transplant hygiene: Use clean trays; harden seedlings; discard weak or yellow seedlings.
  • Field sanitation: Remove crop debris, volunteer brassicas, and weeds that host pests. Burn or compost diseased material away from the field.
  • Spacing and airflow: Plant at recommended spacing (45â60 cm between rows, 30â45 cm within rows) to reduce humidity.
  • Irrigation: Avoid overhead watering late in the day. Drip or furrow irrigation reduces leaf wetness and disease spread.
  • Tool hygiene: Disinfect knives, hoes, and boots with 10% bleach solution when moving between fields.
  • Record keeping: Log every scouting observation, spray, and harvest. Use records to negotiate with buyers and plan next season.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Scout twice weekly using a W-pattern; record pest counts and disease incidence.
  2. Identify the pest or disease correctly before spending money on chemicals.
  3. Spray only when the economic threshold is reached; rotate chemical groups.
  4. Conserve natural enemies by avoiding broad-spectrum insecticides early in the season.
  5. Use certified seed and resistant varieties where available.
  6. Remove and destroy diseased plants and crop debris immediately.
  7. Maintain a spray log with date, product, rate, PHI, and operator name.
  8. Wear full protective equipment and follow the red safety warning above.
