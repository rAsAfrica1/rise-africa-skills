Lessons

1. Identifying Clay Deposits in Your Region

Start by asking local potters, brickmakers, or farmers where they find clay. In many African regions, clay appears along riverbanks, in termite mounds, or where roads cut through hills. Look for areas where the soil is sticky when wet and cracks when dry. You can also check for changes in vegetationclay soils often support different plants than sandy soils. A simple test: dig a small hole 30 cm deep and squeeze a handful of moist soil. If it holds together and can be rolled into a sausage without breaking, it likely contains clay.

Once you suspect a deposit, collect samples from different spots within a 10-meter area. Use a hoe and shovel to dig 50 cm down to reach less weathered material. Avoid topsoil, which is full of roots and organic matter. Place each sample in a separate labeled bucket or plastic bag. Note the location, depth, and color. In Africa, common clay colors are reddish-brown (iron-rich), gray, and white (kaolin). Red clay is good for tiles and bricks; white clay is better for ceramics.

Test the clay's behavior by making a small ball and letting it dry in the shade. If it cracks badly, it may be too sandy or too plastic. Mix a little water to form a thick paste and rub it between your fingersgritty means sand, smooth means fine clay. If the sample is very sticky, it has high plasticity and will need temper (like sand or grog) to reduce shrinkage.

Finally, check local regulations. In some countries, digging clay on community land requires permission from elders or local government. Always ask before you dig. Sustainable harvesting means taking only what you need and refilling holes. Record your findings in a notebookthis becomes your personal clay map. With practice, you'll identify good deposits within a few hours of walking your area.

2. Testing Clay Quality: Plasticity, Shrinkage, and Firing

To turn local clay into tiles or ceramics, you must test three key properties: plasticity, shrinkage, and firing behavior. Start with a simple plasticity test. Take 100 grams of dry clay, add water slowly, and knead until it forms a smooth, workable ball. Roll it into a coil 1 cm thick. Wrap it around your fingerif it bends without cracking, plasticity is good. If it cracks, the clay is short and needs more aging or mixing with a more plastic clay.

Next, test shrinkage. Make a rectangular bar 10 cm long, 2 cm wide, and 1 cm thick. Mark a 10 cm line on it with a ruler. Let it air-dry in the shade for 3 days. Measure the line again. If it shrinks more than 10% (i.e., less than 9 cm), the clay will crack during drying. You can reduce shrinkage by adding 20-30% sand or grog (crushed fired clay). For tiles, aim for 6-8% shrinkage.

Firing test: Make three small tiles, 5 cm x 5 cm x 1 cm. Let them dry completely. Fire them in a kiln or open pit. If you don't have a kiln, dig a pit 50 cm deep, place tiles inside, cover with wood, and light a fire. Let it burn for 2 hours, then cool slowly. After firing, check for cracks, warping, or color change. A good tile rings when tapped and doesn't crumble. If it crumbles, the firing temperature was too low or the clay has too much sand.

Record all results. For example, if your clay shrinks 12% and cracks, mix it with 25% river sand and retest. Keep a log of each test batch. This data helps you adjust recipes for consistent tiles. In Africa, many potters use simple pit firing, but for durable tiles, you need temperatures around 900-1000C. Test your clay at different temperatures if possible. Always wear gloves and a mask when handling dry clay to avoid dust inhalation.

3. Digging and Extracting Clay Sustainably

Before digging, get permission from landowners or local authorities. In many African communities, clay is a shared resource, so explain your purpose and agree on a small area. Choose a site where clay is close to the surfacelook for exposed banks or termite mounds. Dig a test pit 1 meter by 1 meter to check depth and quality. Use a pickaxe, shovel, and hoe. Remove topsoil (first 20-30 cm) and set it aside for refilling later.

Clay often lies in layers. Dig until you reach a consistent color and textureusually 50 cm to 2 meters deep. Avoid mixing different layers; keep them separate. Use a machete or wire to cut clay into blocks about 30 cm x 30 cm. Lift blocks with a shovel and place them on a tarpaulin or sacks. Don't dig more than you need for a few monthsclay improves with aging. A small pickup truck can carry about 1 cubic meter (roughly 1.5 tonnes) of wet clay.

Sustainability matters. After extracting, refill the hole with topsoil and compact it. Plant local grasses or trees to prevent erosion. Never dig near water sources or on steep slopesit causes landslides. If you dig on farmland, leave a border of untouched soil to protect crops. In dry regions, avoid digging in the rainy season when holes collapse.

Work with a team of 2-3 people for safety. Wear boots, gloves, and a hat. Watch for snakes and scorpions. If the clay is very hard, wet it slightly and let it soften overnight. Once extracted, cover the clay with plastic or wet sacks to keep it moist. Transport it to your workshop within a few days. Remember: sustainable harvesting means the deposit can recover. Take only what you need, and always leave the site better than you found it.

4. Cleaning and Soaking Clay to Remove Impurities

Raw clay contains stones, roots, and sand that ruin tiles. Cleaning starts with drying. Spread clay on a concrete floor or tarpaulin in the sun for 2-3 days. Break large lumps with a wooden mallet or hammer. Once dry, crush the clay into small pieces (2-3 cm) using a pestle or a heavy stone. Remove visible stones, roots, and debris by hand.

Next, soak the clay. Fill a plastic drum or basin with waterabout 3 parts water to 1 part clay by volume. Add the crushed clay slowly, stirring with a stick. Let it soak for 24-48 hours. The clay will break down into a slurry. Stir again, then let it settle for 2 hours. Heavy sand and stones sink to the bottom; organic matter floats. Skim off floating debris with a sieve or your hand.

For finer cleaning, pour the slurry through a sieve. Use a 1-2 mm mesh screen (like a mosquito net or window screen) stretched over a wooden frame. Pour the slurry through, catching the fine clay in another container. Discard the residue left in the sieve. Repeat if needed. For high-quality ceramics, you can use a 0.5 mm sieve. This process removes most impurities. If the clay is very sandy, you may need to add a deflocculant like sodium silicate (1 teaspoon per 10 liters) to help separate particles, but this is optional for tiles.

After sieving, let the clay slurry settle for 12-24 hours. Pour off the clear water on top. The thick clay at the bottom is ready for wedging. If you need dry clay, spread the slurry on a porous surface (like a plaster slab) and let it dry to a plastic consistency. Always test a small batch after cleaningroll a coil and check for grit. Clean clay feels smooth and plastic. Store it in a sealed container or plastic bag to prevent drying out.

5. Wedging and Kneading Clay for Consistency

Wedging removes air pockets and makes clay uniform. Air pockets cause tiles to crack or explode in the kiln. There are two main methods: spiral wedging and ram's head wedging. For small batches, use spiral wedging. Place a ball of clay on a table. Press the heel of your hand into the clay, push forward, then roll it back. Repeat, rotating the clay 90 degrees each time. Do this for 5-10 minutes until the clay feels smooth and consistent.

Ram's head wedging is better for large amounts. Cut the clay into slices with a wire, stack them, and press down hard with both hands, folding the clay like kneading bread. Repeat 20-30 times. You can also use a pug mill if available, but manual wedging is fine for small-scale production. A good test: cut the wedged clay with a wire. If the cut surface is smooth with no holes, it's ready.

Consistency matters for tiles. Different batches of clay can vary in moisture and plasticity. To blend, mix clay from different batches together during wedging. Add water sparinglya spray bottle helps. If clay is too wet, let it dry on a plaster slab for a few hours. If too dry, wrap it in a wet cloth overnight. Aim for a moisture content of 15-20%clay should feel like stiff cookie dough.

For large batches, use a foot-powered pug mill or a simple dough mixer. But always finish with hand wedging to check quality. Wedging is hard worktake breaks. In Africa, many potters wedge on a wooden plank or concrete floor. Keep your work area clean; grit from the floor can contaminate clay. After wedging, shape the clay into balls or blocks and cover with plastic. Use within a few days, or store for later. Proper wedging saves hours of frustration during throwing or pressing.

6. Storing Clay for Future Use

Clay improves with age. Storing it properly allows bacteria to grow, which increases plasticity. After wedging, shape clay into balls or blocks of a size you can handletypically 1-2 kg for tiles. Wrap each ball tightly in plastic bags or cling film. Remove as much air as possible. You can also store clay in a sealed plastic drum with a lid. Add a little water (a cup per 10 kg) to keep it moist. Place the drum in a cool, shaded area, away from direct sun.

For long-term storage (months), bury the clay in a pit lined with plastic. Cover with wet sand or soil. This keeps temperature and moisture stable. In dry African climates, check every 2 weeks and add water if needed. In humid areas, watch for moldmold is harmless but can cause allergies. Wear a mask when handling moldy clay. If clay dries out, don't throw it away. Break it into pieces, soak in water for 24 hours, and re-wedge.

Label each batch with the date, source, and any additives (like sand or grog). This helps you track performance. For example, "Batch 3: 20% sand, aged 2 months." Keep a logbook. Store clay away from cement or lime, which can contaminate it. Also keep it away from fuels or chemicals.

If you produce tiles regularly, keep a rotating stock: use the oldest clay first. A simple shelf system works. For a small workshop, 50-100 kg of stored clay is enough for a week's work. Always test a small piece before using a new batchmake a test tile and fire it. Proper storage means you always have workable clay, even in the dry season. It also reduces waste and saves money. Remember, clay is a living materialtreat it well, and it will reward you with strong, beautiful tiles.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Sourcing and Preparing Local Raw Materials for Tiles & Ceramics

This practical reference supports the Rise AFRICA Skills module Sourcing and Preparing Local Raw Materials for the course Tiles & Ceramics. It is written for artisans, small-scale tile producers, and agribusiness-adjacent entrepreneurs who want to turn locally available clay into reliable, sellable ceramic products. The focus is business-first: reduce input costs, improve product consistency, and build a repeatable production system that can serve construction markets, hospitality, and retail buyers across Africa.

Across sub-Saharan Africa, clay is abundant but highly variable. A deposit that works for one product may fail for another. The practical skill is not simply finding clay, but testing, processing, and storing it so that every batch meets the same standard. This lesson consolidates the core steps from the six module lessons into one operational reference.

Why Clay Quality Determines Business Viability

In tile and ceramic production, raw material costs can represent 2040% of total production cost. When clay is sourced locally and processed correctly, margins improve significantly. However, inconsistent clay leads to cracking, warping, colour variation, and rejected batches losses that small producers cannot absorb. The practical reference below gives you a repeatable system for turning local deposits into production-ready material.

Step 1: Identifying Clay Deposits in Your Region

Look for clay in riverbanks, road cuttings, termite mounds, and areas where vegetation is sparse or water pools after rain. In many African regions, clay deposits are associated with sedimentary basins, floodplains, and weathered granite zones. Test pits of 12 metres depth are usually sufficient for small-scale extraction.

Record GPS coordinates, depth, colour, and estimated volume for each deposit. A simple deposit log helps you return to reliable sources and negotiate land access with local authorities or landowners. In countries such as Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya, and South Africa, artisanal mining permits or community agreements may be required.

Step 2: Testing Clay Quality Plasticity, Shrinkage, and Firing

Before committing to a deposit, run three core tests:

  • Plasticity: Roll a moist clay sample into a 3 mm coil and wrap it around your finger. If it cracks, plasticity is low; if it holds without cracking, plasticity is acceptable.
  • Shrinkage: Form a 10 cm bar, mark it, dry it slowly, and measure linear shrinkage. Then fire it and measure again. Total shrinkage above 12% increases cracking risk.
  • Firing: Fire small test tiles at your target temperature (typically 9001100C for terracotta and tiles). Observe colour, hardness, and deformation.

Step 3: Digging and Extracting Clay Sustainably

Extract clay in a way that protects the site and your future supply. Avoid digging deeper than 2 metres without support, and never undermine roads, buildings, or riverbanks. In many African contexts, community consent and land rehabilitation are both legal and reputational requirements.

Best practices include:

  • Dig in strips or terraces rather than a single deep pit.
  • Stockpile topsoil separately for site rehabilitation.
  • Avoid extraction during heavy rains to prevent collapse and runoff.
  • Backfill or replant exhausted areas with local vegetation.
SAFETY WARNING Excavation and Silica Dust

Clay extraction and dry processing generate respirable crystalline silica dust, which can cause silicosis and lung cancer. Always wear a fitted N95 or FFP2 respirator when digging, crushing, or sieving dry clay. Keep the work area ventilated. Never enter an unsupported pit deeper than 1.5 metres. In Africa, where artisanal mining is common, ensure workers are trained on pit safety, dust control, and emergency procedures. Wet-processing methods reduce dust significantly and are strongly recommended.

Step 4: Cleaning and Soaking Clay to Remove Impurities

Impurities such as stones, roots, and iron nodules cause defects in tiles and ceramics. The most reliable method for small producers is slaking and sieving:

  1. Break dry clay into lumps of 510 cm.
  2. Soak in water for 2448 hours until it breaks down into slurry.
  3. Pass the slurry through a 12 mm sieve or mesh.
  4. Allow the slurry to settle, then remove excess water.
  5. Dry the clay to a workable consistency (leather-hard or plastic).

For higher-value tile production, a second finer sieve (0.5 mm) improves surface finish. In areas with high iron content, a simple magnetic separation step can reduce firing discolouration.

Step 5: Wedging and Kneading Clay for Consistency

Wedging removes air pockets and homogenises moisture. It is the difference between a batch that fires evenly and one that fails. Use the spiral wedging or ram's head method:

  • Spiral wedging: push the clay forward and down, then fold and repeat in a spiral motion.
  • Ram's head: press the clay down and forward, then fold back and repeat.
  • Aim for 50100 wedging strokes per 5 kg batch, depending on clay stiffness.
  • Test with a wire cut: the cross-section should show no air pockets.

Step 6: Storing Clay for Future Use

Proper storage prevents moisture loss, contamination, and batch inconsistency. Store clay in:

  • Sealed plastic bags or bins.
  • Damp hessian-covered piles in a shaded area.
  • Labeled batches with date, source, and moisture condition.

In hot, dry African climates, evaporation is rapid. A simple shade structure or covered storage room protects clay for weeks or months. For business continuity, keep at least one month of tested, wedged clay in stock.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Deposit identified? GPS logged, depth recorded, land access confirmed.
  2. Plasticity tested? 3 mm coil holds without cracking.
  3. Shrinkage measured? Dry <8%, firing <6%.
  4. Firing test done? Test tiles fired at target temperature, no bloating or deformation.
  5. Extraction sustainable? Strip or terrace method, topsoil stockpiled, site rehabilitation planned.
  6. Clay cleaned? Soaked 2448 hours, sieved through 12 mm mesh.
  7. Wedged properly? 50100 strokes per 5 kg, wire cut shows no air pockets.
  8. Stored and labelled? Batch date, source, moisture condition recorded; one month stock maintained.

Business takeaway: In African tile and ceramic markets, the producers who win are those who control raw material quality. By standardising deposit identification, testing, extraction, cleaning, wedging, and storage, you reduce waste, improve product consistency, and build a brand that buyers trust. Treat clay as a strategic asset not just dirt.
