1. Packing and Transporting Food

When you cook from home, packing and transporting food safely is critical. Use insulated coolers or thick cardboard boxes lined with newspaper or bubble wrap. For hot food, wrap pots in towels and place in a cooler. For cold food, use ice packs or frozen water bottles. Never mix hot and cold items in the same container. Label each container with the dish name and date. For a 50-person event, you might need 4 large coolers: one for hot mains, one for cold salads, one for drinks, and one for desserts. Pack sauces and dressings separately in leak-proof jars. Use sturdy plastic containers with tight lids rather than foil, which tears. Transport in a vehicle with a flat boot or back seat. Secure containers with seatbelts or rope so they don't slide. If travelling more than 30 minutes, avoid opening coolers. In hot African climates, keep food out of direct sun. For weddings or funerals, where food may sit for hours, use chafing dishes with sterno fuel. Always carry a basic toolkit: spare lids, cling film, paper towels, and a thermometer. Check that hot food stays above 60C and cold below 5C. If you must hold food, use a insulated bag. Plan your route to avoid potholes and traffic. For large orders, make two trips rather than overloading. Remember: food safety is your reputation. One case of food poisoning can end your business. So invest in good coolers and never cut corners on temperature control.

2. On-Site Setup and Organization

Arrive at the venue at least 2 hours before serving time. First, walk the space: identify where guests will sit, where the buffet will go, and where you can set up a prep area. You need a table for food, a separate table for drinks, and a waste bin. If there is no kitchen, bring a folding table and a clean cloth. Set up a 'backstage' area for your bags, empty containers, and extra supplies. Arrange food in the order guests will serve themselves: plates first, then mains, sides, sauces, and cutlery last. Use risers (upside-down boxes) to create height and save space. For a 50-person event, use two serving lines to avoid queues. Label each dish clearly with a card. If serving hot food, set up chafing dishes with water and fuel. For cold items, nest bowls in larger bowls filled with ice. Keep a trash bag tied to the table for scraps. Have a hand sanitizer station. Organize your staff: one person per serving line, one to replenish, one to clear tables. If you are alone, pre-plate salads and desserts so you only serve mains. Use a checklist: tables, chairs, linen, plates, cutlery, glasses, napkins, serving spoons, cooler, fuel, matches, bin bags, cleaning cloths. Test everything before guests arrive. In many African events, guests arrive late, so keep food hot or cold properly. Do not leave food uncovered. A clean, organized setup shows professionalism and makes service smoother.

3. Serving and Staffing Considerations

For small events (20-100 guests), you need 1 server per 25 guests for buffet, or 1 per 15 for plated. If you are the only staff, choose buffet. Train helpers on portion sizes: a main is 150-200g of meat, 1 cup of rice, 2 tablespoons of sauce. Use a standard serving spoon (60ml) for sides. For plated service, serve from the left, clear from the right. In African settings, many guests prefer generous portions, so plan 10% extra. Serve hot food hot: keep chafing dishes at 60C. Cold food at 5C. If using local servers, pay them fairly: in Kenya, a server might earn KES 800-1500 per event. Brief them on allergies and dietary needs. Have a designated person for drinks so the buffet line moves. For outdoor events, use food covers to keep flies off. If serving alcohol, check local laws and have non-alcoholic options. For children, offer smaller plates and simple foods. For elderly, ensure soft options. Always serve yourself last. Monitor food levels; when a dish is half empty, replace it with a full one. Do not let guests serve themselves with their own cutlery. Provide tongs or spoons for each dish. Keep a clean cloth to wipe spills. After the event, pack leftovers in clean containers within 2 hours. If you have excess, offer to staff or donate. Good serving keeps guests happy and your business recommended.

4. Timeline and Coordination

Create a timeline working backwards from serving time. For a lunch event at 1 PM: start cooking at 6 AM, pack by 10 AM, arrive at 11 AM, set up by 12 PM, serve at 1 PM. For a 50-person event, cooking takes 3-4 hours. Prep vegetables the night before. Marinate meat overnight. Make sauces and desserts a day ahead. On event day, stick to the schedule. Use a timer for each dish. If you are late, call the client. Coordinate with venue staff: confirm access time, power, water, and parking. If you need help, hire 1-2 assistants and give them a written schedule. For a wedding, you may need to serve at 3 PM, so start early. For a funeral, food may be needed immediately after burial, so have everything ready. Use a checklist for each step. Keep your phone charged. Have a backup plan if traffic delays you: call ahead and ask someone to set up tables. If you are cooking at home, ensure you have enough fuel (gas or charcoal). For large orders, cook in batches. Label each batch with time. Do not leave food in the danger zone (5-60C) for more than 2 hours. If you must hold, use insulated containers. After the event, schedule cleanup and return of equipment. Confirm payment terms before the event. A good timeline reduces stress and ensures you deliver on time.

5. Emergency Contingency Plans

Even with good planning, emergencies happen. Have a plan for power cuts: if you rely on electricity, buy a small gas burner or charcoal stove as backup. For refrigeration failure, use coolers with ice. If you run out of food, have a quick dish like pasta or rice that cooks in 20 minutes. Keep extra canned tomatoes, onions, and spices. If a dish burns, do not serve it; apologize and offer an alternative. For spills, carry a mop and bucket. If a guest gets sick, have a first aid kit and know the nearest clinic. For transport breakdown, have a backup vehicle or call a taxi. If you are late, call the client and explain. For bad weather, have tarpaulins or move indoors. If a staff member does not show, you must step in or call a backup. For missing equipment, borrow from the venue or use disposable plates. Always carry a spare gas cylinder, matches, and a knife. For large events, have a fire extinguisher. In case of fire, turn off gas and use extinguisher. For food allergies, ask guests beforehand and label dishes. If you drop a dish, clean it up and replace with another. Keep a list of emergency contacts: client, venue, staff, supplier. After the event, review what went wrong and improve. Insurance is optional but recommended for large events. A calm response turns a crisis into a minor hiccup.

6. Cleanup and Breakdown

Cleanup starts as you serve. Clear empty plates and glasses every 30 minutes. Keep a bin for waste and a tub for dirty dishes. After the event, pack leftovers in clean containers within 2 hours. Do not leave food out. Scrape plates into bin, stack dishes. If you have a sink, wash with hot soapy water. If not, take dishes home to wash. Wipe tables and chairs. Sweep the floor. Remove all your equipment. Check for personal items. Return borrowed items. If you used chafing dishes, empty water and fuel safely. Clean them before storing. For linen, shake off crumbs and bag. For coolers, wipe and dry. Do not leave trash at the venue unless agreed. Take it with you. If you hired staff, pay them and give feedback. Count your equipment against a checklist. Report any damages to the client. For a 50-person event, cleanup takes 1-2 hours with 2 people. If you are alone, it may take 3 hours. Charge for cleanup if it is extensive. After cleanup, send a thank-you message to the client. Ask for a testimonial. Store equipment properly: clean, dry, and in a dry place. Review your performance: what went well, what to improve. Keep records of expenses and income. Cleanup is not glamorous, but it leaves a lasting impression. A clean venue means clients will call you again.

<h2>Practical Reference: Logistics and Event Execution</h2>

<p>In home-based catering across Africa, the difference between a profitable event and a costly one is rarely the food itselfit is the logistics. A caterer in Accra, Nairobi, or Lusaka can cook brilliantly and still lose money through late delivery, spoilage during transport, understaffing at the buffet, or a chaotic breakdown that leaves equipment behind. This practical reference consolidates the operational knowledge you need to move food safely, set up efficiently, coordinate staff and timelines, handle emergencies, and break down cleanlyall while protecting your margin and your reputation.</p>

<p>Treat this module as your field manual. Each section gives you concrete decisions to make, numbers to plan with, and checklists to adapt to your own business. The goal is repeatable execution: the same reliable standard whether you are serving 30 guests at a family funeral in Kumasi or 150 at a corporate launch in Lagos.</p>

<h3>Packing and Transporting Food</h3>

<p>Transport is where most home caterers lose control. In many African cities, roads are uneven, traffic is unpredictable, and ambient temperatures can exceed 35C. Your packing strategy must assume delays. Use insulated cool boxes for cold items and insulated hot bags or cambro-style containers for hot food. Never mix raw and cooked items in the same box. Label every container with the dish name, quantity, and preparation time.</p>

<p>For hot food, preheat insulated containers with boiling water for 10 minutes before loading. For cold food, pre-chill cool boxes with ice packs or frozen water bottles. Pack sauces and stews in sealed containers with a secondary layer of cling film or a lid to prevent leaks. Carry a basic spill kit: paper towels, disinfectant, and spare bags. If you are transporting in a private car, use the boot for equipment and the passenger area for temperature-sensitive foodbut never place hot food directly on seats without a protective mat.</p>

<p>Plan your route and departure time backwards from the serving time. Add a 30-minute buffer for traffic and parking. In cities like Nairobi or Lagos, that buffer may need to be 60 minutes during peak hours. Confirm parking access with the client in advance; carrying 20 kg of food 500 metres is a recipe for spills and delays.</p>

<h3>On-Site Setup and Organization</h3>

<p>Arrive early enough to set up without rushingideally 90 minutes before serving for events up to 100 guests. Your setup should follow a logical flow: staging area, preparation zone, serving line, and waste station. In many African venues, space is limited, so vertical organisation matters. Use folding tables, stackable crates, and labelled boxes so you can find equipment without unpacking everything.</p>

<p>Set up the serving line so guests move in one direction. Place plates and cutlery at the start, main dishes in the middle, and sauces, drinks, and condiments at the end. This reduces congestion and speeds service. Keep a separate table for staff supplies: serving spoons, gloves, napkins, and cleaning cloths. If the venue has no running water, bring two bucketsone with soapy water and one with clean rinse waterplus hand sanitiser.</p>

<p>Protect food from dust, flies, and direct sun. Use mesh food covers or clean cloths. In outdoor settings, position the buffet under a canopy or shade. Never place food on the ground. Check that all electrical equipment (chafing dishes, hot plates, blenders) has a safe power source and that cables are taped down or covered to prevent tripping.</p>

<h3>Serving and Staffing Considerations</h3>

<p>Staffing ratios matter for both service quality and food safety. For a plated meal, one server per 1012 guests is a reasonable starting point. For a buffet, one server per 2530 guests, plus one dedicated person to replenish dishes and one to manage the waste station. If you are working alone, simplify the menu and use self-service where culturally appropriate.</p>

<p>Train your staff on three non-negotiables: hand hygiene, no bare-hand contact with ready-to-eat food, and prompt removal of empty or contaminated dishes. Brief them on the menuwhat each dish contains, including allergens like peanuts, shellfish, or dairy. In many African contexts, guests may ask about ingredients for dietary or religious reasons; your staff should be able to answer confidently.</p>

<p>Pay attention to portion control. Serving spoons should be the correct sizetoo large and you run out of food; too small and service slows down. Monitor the buffet every 1520 minutes and replace dishes rather than topping up, especially for hot food that has been sitting out.</p>

<h3>Timeline and Coordination</h3>

<p>A written timeline is your single most important coordination tool. Build it backwards from the serving time and include every task: shopping, prepping, cooking, chilling, packing, loading, travelling, setting up, serving, and cleaning. Share it with your staff and the client. Use a simple formattime, task, person responsibleand keep it visible in the kitchen and at the venue.</p>

<p>Coordination with the client should cover access times, parking, power availability, water, and any venue restrictions. Confirm these in writing at least 48 hours before the event. On the day, assign one person as the point of contact for the client so you are not interrupted while cooking or serving. If you are working with external suppliersrentals, decor, or additional staffconfirm their arrival times and contact numbers the day before.</p>

If food has been sitting out beyond 2 hours, discard itdo not attempt to reheat and serve. Reheating does not destroy all toxins produced by bacteria. This rule protects your clients, your reputation, and your business from legal liability.</p>

<h3>Emergency Contingency Plans</h3>

<p>Emergencies happen: power cuts, late deliveries, staff illness, vehicle breakdowns, or sudden increases in guest numbers. Prepare a one-page contingency plan for each event. Keep a list of backup suppliers, a spare gas cylinder or charcoal stove, and a basic first aid kit. If power fails, have insulated containers ready to hold hot food and a plan to move to a backup cooking method.</p>

<p>For staff no-shows, cross-train at least one person to cover critical roles. For transport breakdowns, identify a backup vehicle or a taxi service that can carry insulated boxes. For food shortages, have a simple backup dishlike a large pot of rice or a vegetable stewthat can be prepared quickly from pantry staples. Communicate proactively with the client if an issue arises; most clients appreciate honesty and a solution more than silence.</p>

<h3>Cleanup and Breakdown</h3>

<p>Cleanup starts before the event ends. Assign a staff member to clear tables and manage waste throughout the service so you are not faced with a mountain of work at the end. Pack equipment in the reverse order of setup: clean and dry items first, then heavy equipment, then food containers. Check the venue for any items you broughtserving spoons, cloths, and extension cables are commonly left behind.</p>

<p>Dispose of waste responsibly. Separate food waste from recyclables where possible. If you are leaving leftovers with the client, transfer them to clean containers and advise on storage and reheating. If you are taking leftovers back, chill them quickly and label them. Never leave food waste at the venue unless the client has agreed to it in advance.</p>

<p>Do a quick debrief with your team: what went well, what went wrong, and what to change next time. Record these notes in a simple log. Over time, this log becomes your competitive advantagea library of lessons that makes every event smoother and more profitable.</p>
