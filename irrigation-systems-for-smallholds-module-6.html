1. Components of a Drip Irrigation System

A drip irrigation system delivers water directly to plant roots through a network of tubes and emitters. The main components are: water source (well, tank, or tap), pump (if needed), filter, mainline, sub-mainline, drip lines (laterals), emitters, and fittings (connectors, valves, end caps). For a smallholder in Africa, a typical system might use a 200-litre drum elevated 1 metre to provide gravity pressure. You'll need a filter to prevent cloggingâa 120-mesh screen filter is common. The mainline is usually 32mm or 25mm PVC or PE pipe, while drip lines are 16mm. Emitters can be built into the drip line (inline) or inserted separately (online). Valves control water flow to different sections. End caps close the drip lines. A simple system for a 10m x 10m vegetable plot might cost $50-$100. Start with a kit that includes all parts. Remember: without a filter, emitters clog quickly, especially with dirty water. Also, a pressure regulator (1-2 bar) protects the system. In Africa, many farmers use gravity-fed systems from tanks to save on pumps. You can also use a bucket kit for very small plots. The key is to match components to your water source and field size. Always use black polyethylene tubing to prevent algae growth. Keep spare parts like extra emitters and connectors. This system saves water and labour compared to watering cans.

2. Choosing Drip Emitters and Tubing

Choosing the right emitters and tubing is crucial for efficient drip irrigation. Emitters come in different flow rates: 1, 2, 4, or 8 litres per hour (L/h). For vegetables like tomatoes, peppers, and onions, 2 L/h emitters spaced 30-40 cm apart are ideal. For sandy soil, use higher flow (4 L/h) because water spreads less. For clay soil, lower flow (1-2 L/h) prevents runoff. Tubing: drip lines are typically 16mm diameter. You can buy inline drip tape (thin wall, cheap, lasts 1-2 seasons) or thick-walled drip line (lasts 5-10 years). In Africa, UV-resistant drip tape is important because sunlight degrades cheap tape quickly. Space emitters according to crop spacing: for maize, 30 cm; for leafy greens, 20 cm. You can also use adjustable online emitters if you want to change flow. But they are more expensive and prone to clogging. For a smallholder, start with a 16mm inline drip line with 2 L/h emitters at 30 cm spacing. That gives about 6.6 emitters per metre. For a 10m row, that's 66 emitters, delivering 132 L/h. If you have 10 rows, you need 1320 L/h. Check your water source can supply that. Tubing connectors: use barbed fittings for 16mm. Always use a punch tool to make holes for online emittersânever a nail. Also, consider pressure-compensating (PC) emitters if your field slopes or laterals are long. They cost more but ensure even watering. For most smallholders, non-PC is fine if laterals are under 30m. Buy from reputable dealers to avoid clogging from poor quality.

3. Designing a Simple Drip Layout for Vegetables

Designing a drip layout starts with a sketch of your plot. Measure length and width. For vegetables, rows are typically 30-50 cm apart. Let's design for a 10m x 10m plot with tomatoes. Rows: 10m long, spaced 50 cm apart, so 20 rows. Each row gets one drip line. Total drip line length = 20 x 10m = 200m. Use 16mm drip line with 2 L/h emitters at 30 cm spacing. Each row has 33 emitters (10m / 0.3m â 33). Total emitters = 20 x 33 = 660. Water demand: 660 x 2 L/h = 1320 L/h. If you run the system for 1 hour, you apply 1320 litres. That's about 13.2 mm of water over 100 mÂ² (since 1 mm = 1 L/mÂ², and 100 mÂ² = 100 L per mm). Tomatoes need about 5-7 mm per day. So you might run for 30-40 minutes daily. Now, mainline: from water source to the field. Use 32mm PVC or PE. Sub-mainline runs along the top of rows. Connect drip lines to sub-mainline with barbed connectors and a small valve for each row (optional). Or use a manifold. For gravity systems, elevate tank 1-2m. For 1320 L/h, you need a tank outlet of at least 25mm. If using a pump, choose one that delivers 1500 L/h at 1 bar. Always include a filter after the pump or tank. Also, install a pressure regulator if pressure exceeds 2 bar. End each drip line with a flush valve for cleaning. This layout is simple and scalable. For a 20m x 20m plot, double everything. Keep laterals under 30m to avoid pressure loss. If slope is steep, use pressure-compensating emitters or run laterals along contours.

4. Installing a Drip System: Step-by-Step

Installing a drip system is straightforward if you follow these steps. Step 1: Gather toolsâpipe cutter, punch tool, barbed connectors, filter, valves, end caps, and Teflon tape. Step 2: Position your water source. If using a tank, place it on a stand at least 1m high. Step 3: Install the filter and mainline. Connect filter to tank outlet. Use 32mm PVC pipe for mainline. Run it to the field. Step 4: Lay out sub-mainline. This is a 16mm or 25mm pipe that runs along the top of your rows. Connect it to mainline with a reducer and valve. Step 5: Install drip lines. Unroll drip line along each row. Cut to length, leaving 30cm extra. Connect one end to sub-mainline using a barbed connector. Fold the other end and secure with an end cap or figure-8 clamp. Step 6: Punch holes for online emitters only if using blank tubing. For inline drip line, skip this. Step 7: Flush the system before installing emitters. Remove end caps and run water for 2 minutes to clear debris. Step 8: Install end caps. Step 9: Test the system. Turn on water and check for leaks and even flow. Adjust pressure regulator if needed. Step 10: Bury or secure drip lines lightly with soil or pegs to prevent movement. For a 10m x 10m plot, this takes 2-3 hours. Always use a punch tool for holesânever a nail. Use Teflon tape on threaded connections. In Africa, protect drip lines from animals by fencing or raising them slightly. After installation, run the system daily and check for clogged emitters. Keep a repair kit with extra connectors and emitters. If you have a pump, install a pressure gauge to monitor. Remember: a well-installed system saves water and increases yields.

5. Filtering and Maintaining Drip Systems

Filters are the most important part of a drip system because emitters clog easily. Use a 120-mesh screen filter for clean water, or a 200-mesh for very clean water. If your water source is a river or dam, use a sand filter first, then a screen filter. Clean the screen filter weekly: remove the screen, rinse with clean water, and replace. If you see a pressure drop, clean it immediately. For sand filters, backwash every 2-4 weeks. Maintenance also includes flushing drip lines. Once a month, remove end caps and run water for 5 minutes to flush out sediment. If emitters clog, remove them and soak in a 1% chlorine solution (household bleach) for 30 minutes, then rinse. For inline drip tape, you can inject chlorine at 1-2 ppm continuously for 30 minutes to clear organic matter. But be careful: too much chlorine damages crops. Use a fertilizer injector or a simple bucket with a valve. Also, check for leaks: walk the system weekly and fix with repair couplings. In Africa, rodents and insects can chew drip lines. Use traps or raise lines on stakes. After harvest, drain and store drip lines in a cool, dark place. If you use drip tape, it may last only one season; thick-walled drip line lasts 5-10 years. Keep a log of maintenance activities. A well-maintained system can last a decade. Remember: clean water in, clean water out. Never use dirty water without filtration. If you have algae, use a 0.5% copper sulfate solution, but only for non-crop periods. Always follow safety guidelines when handling chemicals.

6. Fertigation: Injecting Nutrients through Drip

Fertigation is the practice of injecting soluble fertilizers into your drip system. It saves labour and delivers nutrients directly to roots. Common fertilizers: urea, potassium nitrate, calcium nitrate, and NPK blends. Use only fully soluble fertilizersânever use granular or organic fertilizers that can clog emitters. A simple venturi injector costs $20-$50 and works with a pump or gravity system. For gravity, use a bucket with a valve and a small pump, or a bypass tank. Mix fertilizer at recommended rates: for tomatoes, apply 5-10 g of NPK (20-20-20) per plant per week during fruiting. Dissolve in a bucket of water, then inject over 30 minutes. Always inject after a plain water flush to avoid burning roots. Then flush again with clean water for 10 minutes. Never mix calcium with phosphate or sulfate fertilizersâthey precipitate and clog. Use a separate injection for each. In Africa, many farmers use urea (46-0-0) for leafy greens: 3-5 g per plant per week. For fertigation, you need a filter after the injector to catch any undissolved particles. Also, use a backflow preventer to protect your water source. Fertigation schedule: apply nutrients 2-3 times per week during peak growth. Monitor plant leaves for deficiency symptoms. If you see yellowing, adjust. Keep a log of fertilizer types and amounts. Over-fertilization can burn plants and pollute groundwater. Start with half the recommended rate and increase. Always wear gloves and goggles when handling fertilizers. After fertigation, flush the system thoroughly. This method can increase yields by 20-30% compared to broadcasting. It's ideal for smallholders because it saves fertilizer and water.

2. Component Reference: What to Buy and Why

Your system's reliability is set by the quality of four components: the filter, the emitters, the pressure regulator and the connectors. Everything else can be replaced cheaply. Do not economise on these four.

On a smallholder block in Kenya, Zambia, Ghana or Senegal, the most common failure is not pump failure â it is emitter clogging caused by unfiltered water. A 120-mesh screen filter costs a fraction of a full lateral replacement and will prevent the majority of clogging problems.

3. Sizing the System: Water Demand and Emitter Numbers

Designing a drip layout is arithmetic, not intuition. The three numbers you need are: the crop's peak daily water demand (mm/day), the emitter flow rate (L/h), and the number of emitters per plant. Get these wrong and you will either starve the crop or over-irrigate and leach nutrients.

A practical rule for African smallholder vegetable production: most vegetables need 4â7 mm/day at peak demand. On a 1,000 mÂ² plot, 5 mm/day equals 5,000 litres per day. If your emitters deliver 2 L/h and you have 1 emitter per plant at 30 cm spacing in rows 60 cm apart, you have roughly 5,555 plants per 1,000 mÂ² â that is 11,110 L/h if all run at once, far more than you need. This is why you irrigate in blocks, not all at once.

4. Installation Sequence: The Order That Prevents Rework

  1. Survey and mark. Mark the water source, head control position, block boundaries and crop rows with pegs. Confirm the slope.
  2. Install the head control. Pump, filter, pressure regulator, valves and a flush point â all on a single accessible manifold.
  3. Lay the mainline and sub-main. Bury mainlines where tractors or livestock cross; leave sub-mains on the surface for small plots.
  4. Flush the mains before connecting laterals. Run water through the mains for 5â10 minutes to remove construction debris. This single step prevents most early clogging.
  5. Install laterals. Run them along the crop row, emitter side up, and cap the ends with flush valves.
  6. Pressure-test and flush. Run the whole system, check every connection, and flush each lateral for 1â2 minutes.
  7. Commission and record. Measure flow at the end of the furthest lateral and record it in a logbook. This becomes your maintenance baseline.

Never inject fertilisers, pesticides or any chemical into a drip system without a functioning backflow prevention device (non-return valve and/or air gap). Without it, chemicals can siphon back into your drinking water source, your borehole or your neighbour's supply. Always wear gloves, eye protection and a mask when handling fertigation chemicals. Never exceed the pressure rating of the lowest-rated component â over-pressurised PVC and HDPE lines can burst and cause serious injury. Isolate and depressurise the system before repairing any fitting.

5. Maintenance and Fertigation: Protecting the Investment

Drip systems fail slowly, then suddenly. The maintenance regime that prevents this is simple and cheap:

  • Flush laterals weekly â open end caps for 1â2 minutes per block.
  • Clean the filter every 1â3 days depending on water source; replace screens when torn.
  • Acid-treat or chlorinate monthly if using surface water, to control algae and mineral scale. Use food-grade citric acid or approved chlorine products only.
  • Check emitter uniformity quarterly by measuring flow at the first and last emitter on a lateral. A variation above 15% signals a problem.
  • Record everything â run-times, flush dates, filter cleanings, chemical doses.

Fertigation â injecting soluble nutrients through the drip system â is where drip irrigation becomes a genuine business multiplier. Split applications of nitrogen and potassium directly to the root zone can raise nutrient use efficiency from roughly 30â40% (broadcast) to 60â80% (fertigation), which means you spend less on fertiliser for the same or better yield. The rule is simple: inject only fully soluble fertilisers, inject in the middle third of the irrigation cycle, and flush the lines with clean water for 10â15 minutes afterwards. Never leave fertiliser sitting in the laterals.
