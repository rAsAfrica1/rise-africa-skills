1. Calf Milk Replacer: Composition, Mixing and Quality

Calf milk replacer is a powder made from dairy by-products (skim milk, whey) or plant proteins (soy), formulated to mimic cow milk. In Africa, local suppliers include Bidco (Kenya), Nutri Feeds (South Africa), and imported brands like Denkavit (Netherlands) and MaxCare (New Zealand). Prices range from KES 500-800 per kg in Kenya, NGN 3,000-5,000 per kg in Nigeria, and ZAR 60-100 per kg in South Africa. A good replacer contains 20-28% crude protein, 15-20% fat, and less than 0.2% crude fibre. High-quality replacers use milk proteins (casein, whey) which are more digestible than soy. According to Teagasc (Ireland), calves fed milk-protein replacers have 10-15% better growth rates than those on soy-based replacers. FAO recommends using replacers with at least 22% protein for calves under 3 weeks. Mixing is critical: use a scale to weigh powder. A typical mixing rate is 125-150 g of powder per litre of water, aiming for 12.5-15% solids. For example, to make 1 litre of 12.5% solution, add 125 g powder to 875 ml water. Water must be clean and heated to 45-50 degrees Celsius for mixing, then fed at 38-40 degrees Celsius. Never use boiling water as it destroys vitamins and curdles proteins. Use a thermometer; if not available, test on wrist - it should feel warm, not hot. Mix thoroughly with a whisk until no lumps. Feed immediately or store in a refrigerator for up to 24 hours. Common mistakes in Africa: using too little powder to save money (resulting in weak solution and poor growth), mixing with cold water (causing clumping and digestive upsets), and using dirty containers (leading to bacterial growth). Always clean feeding equipment with hot water and detergent after each feeding. Store powder in a cool, dry place, sealed tightly. Buy from reputable suppliers; cheap powders often contain high fibre (from plant hulls) which calves cannot digest. Check the label for protein, fat, fibre, and mixing instructions. According to NAP (National Academies Press), calf milk replacer should provide 20% of dry matter intake from fat for energy. In Africa, where ambient temperatures are high, calves may need more water; ensure fresh water is available at all times after 2 weeks of age. For smallholders, buying in bulk (25 kg bags) reduces cost. In Kenya, a 25 kg bag of quality replacer costs KES 12,000-20,000. In Nigeria, NGN 75,000-125,000. In South Africa, ZAR 1,500-2,500. Always check expiry date; expired powder loses vitamin potency and may have mould. If mixing for multiple calves, use a clean bucket and measure per calf to avoid over- or under-feeding. Weigh calves weekly to adjust feeding rates. A well-mixed replacer should look like milk, not watery or pasty. If it separates, it is not mixed properly. Use a blender if available. For organic farms, some choose raw milk, but replacer is more consistent and biosecure. Remember: consistency in mixing and feeding is key to healthy calves.

2. Feeding Schedule and Volumes for Calves

A calf's feeding schedule must match its age, weight, and growth stage. According to Teagasc, calves should receive 10-12% of body weight in milk replacer per day during the first week, increasing to 15-20% by week 4. For a 40 kg calf, that is 4-4.8 litres per day in week 1, rising to 6-8 litres by week 4. Divide into 2-3 feedings. In Africa, where labour may be limited, twice-daily feeding is common: morning and evening. For example, a 40 kg calf gets 2 litres in the morning and 2 litres in the evening in week 1. By week 2, increase to 2.5 litres per feeding (5 litres total). By week 3, 3 litres per feeding (6 litres total). By week 4, 3.5 litres per feeding (7 litres total). Never feed more than 2 litres per feeding for calves under 3 weeks, as their abomasum (true stomach) cannot handle larger volumes; excess milk spills into the rumen and causes bloat or scours. Feeding times should be consistent, ideally 12 hours apart. If feeding three times a day, space evenly (e.g., 6 am, 12 pm, 6 pm). Water: from day 3, offer fresh clean water in a separate bucket. Calves need water to develop rumen and digest starter feed. According to FAO, calves drink 1-2 litres of water per day in the first week, increasing to 4-6 litres by week 4. In hot African climates, water intake can be 20-30% higher. Starter feed: introduce calf starter (18-20% protein) from day 4-7. Offer a handful (50-100 g) daily, increasing to 500 g by week 4. By week 6, calves should eat 1-1.5 kg starter per day. Weigh calves weekly to adjust milk replacer. A 50 kg calf at week 4 needs 7.5-10 litres per day. If calf is scouring, reduce volume by 25% and feed electrolytes. Common mistakes: feeding too much at once (causes scours), skipping feedings (causes stress and poor growth), and not adjusting for weight gain. In Kenya, many farmers feed 2 litres twice a day regardless of age, leading to underfeeding. Use a chart: Week 1: 4-5 L/day; Week 2: 5-6 L; Week 3: 6-7 L; Week 4: 7-8 L; Week 5: 8-9 L; Week 6: 9-10 L. Then wean gradually over 1-2 weeks by reducing milk replacer and increasing starter. Weaning age: 6-8 weeks for dairy calves, 8-10 weeks for beef calves. According to MaxCare (NZ), calves should be weaned when eating 1.5-2 kg starter per day for 3 consecutive days. In Africa, where starter may be expensive, weaning at 8 weeks is practical. Always provide shade and clean bedding. In hot weather, feed during cooler hours (early morning, late evening). In cold weather (e.g., South African highlands), increase energy by adding 10-20% more powder. Monitor body condition: ribs should be covered but not visible. If calves are thin, increase feed. If fat, reduce. Use a scale or weigh tape. Record daily intake. For smallholders, group calves by age and weight to simplify feeding. But avoid mixing large and small calves; small ones get bullied. If using automatic feeders, set to 4-6 litres per day per calf, but monitor. According to Royal Canin, for orphaned calves, feed colostrum replacer first (first 6 hours), then milk replacer. Never feed milk replacer to a calf that has not received colostrum. Colostrum is essential for immunity. In Africa, many calves die from failure of passive transfer. Ensure colostrum within 2 hours of birth, 2 litres, and again at 12 hours. Then start milk replacer at 24 hours. Feeding schedule is not just about volume; it is about timing, temperature, and consistency. Keep records: date, age, weight, volume fed, health notes. This helps identify problems early.

3. Mixing and Feeding Equipment for Calves

Proper equipment ensures calves get the right amount of milk replacer at the right temperature. Essential items: a digital scale (0-5 kg capacity, accurate to 1 g), a thermometer (0-100 degrees Celsius), a whisk or blender, a clean bucket (10-20 litres), calf bottles or nipple buckets, and a measuring jug. In Africa, these are available in agrovet shops or online. Costs: scale KES 2,000-4,000, thermometer KES 500-1,000, nipple bucket KES 1,500-3,000. If too expensive, share among farmers. Mixing: weigh powder accurately. For 1 litre of 12.5% solution, weigh 125 g. For 2 litres, 250 g. Use a jug to measure water. Heat water to 45-50 degrees Celsius. Add powder to water (not water to powder) to avoid lumps. Whisk for 1-2 minutes until fully dissolved. Check temperature with thermometer; should be 38-40 degrees Celsius for feeding. If too hot, add cold water or wait. If too cold, reheat gently (do not boil). Never microwave as it heats unevenly. Feed using a nipple bottle or bucket with nipple. Nipples should be clean and soft. For group feeding, use a milk bar with multiple nipples, but ensure each calf gets its share. Clean equipment after each feeding: rinse with lukewarm water, wash with hot water and detergent, sanitise with bleach solution (1 tablespoon per 4 litres), rinse well, air dry. According to Teagasc, dirty equipment causes 80% of scours cases. In Africa, water quality is a concern. Use boiled and cooled water for mixing if tap water is unsafe. Boiling kills bacteria. Store boiled water in clean containers. Common mistakes: using the same bucket for mixing and feeding without cleaning, not drying equipment (bacteria grow in moisture), and using cracked nipples (harbour bacteria). Replace nipples every 2-3 months. For smallholders, have two sets of equipment: one for use, one for cleaning. Rotate. If a calf is sick, use separate equipment to avoid spread. According to FAO, hygiene is critical in calf rearing. In Kenya, many farmers use plastic jerrycans as buckets; ensure they are food-grade. Avoid containers that held chemicals. For feeding, elevate the nipple to calf's mouth level; do not let calf suck air. If using a bucket, train calf to drink by dipping fingers in milk and letting it suck. This may take 2-3 days. For newborn calves, use a bottle with a teat. Feed at a steady rate; do not force. After feeding, wipe calf's mouth with clean cloth. Monitor for coughing or milk coming out of nose, which indicates feeding too fast or too much. Adjust nipple hole size. In hot climates, mix only enough for one feeding; do not store mixed milk replacer for more than 1 hour at room temperature. If refrigerated, use within 24 hours and reheat to 38-40 degrees Celsius. Never refreeze. For cost savings, buy powder in 25 kg bags and store in airtight containers in a cool, dark place. In Nigeria, a 25 kg bag of Denkavit costs NGN 80,000-120,000. In South Africa, ZAR 1,800-2,500. In Kenya, KES 15,000-20,000. Always check for caking or off-smell; if present, discard. Use within 3 months of opening. Record batch numbers for traceability. According to NAP, equipment should be designed for easy cleaning. For smallholders, simple is best: a 10-litre bucket, a whisk, and a nipple bottle. Train all workers on proper mixing and cleaning. Mistakes cost lives. A clean, well-mixed feed is the foundation of calf health.

4. Health, Scours and Weaning Management

Scours (diarrhoea) is the leading cause of calf death in Africa, often due to improper milk replacer feeding. Causes: overfeeding, inconsistent mixing, dirty equipment, cold milk, and stress. Symptoms: loose watery faeces, dehydration, sunken eyes, weakness. According to Teagasc, 50% of scours cases are nutritional, 30% infectious, 20% environmental. Treatment: reduce milk replacer volume by 50% for 1-2 days, feed electrolytes (e.g., Diakur, Lectade) between feedings. Electrolyte recipe: 1 litre clean water, 1 teaspoon salt, 6 teaspoons sugar, 1/2 teaspoon baking soda. Feed 2 litres per day in addition to milk. Do not stop milk entirely; calves need energy. If scours persists >2 days, consult vet. Antibiotics only if bacterial. Prevention: consistent feeding times, correct temperature (38-40 degrees Celsius), clean equipment, and gradual changes. In Africa, many farmers feed cold milk from refrigerator; warm it to 38 degrees Celsius. Also, do not feed milk replacer with high fibre (>0.5%) as it causes gut irritation. According to FAO, calves should be vaccinated for rotavirus and coronavirus if available. In Kenya, vaccines cost KES 500-1,000 per dose. Weaning: start at 6-8 weeks. Reduce milk replacer by 25% each week while increasing starter. For example, week 6: 8 litres/day; week 7: 6 litres; week 8: 4 litres; week 9: 2 litres; week 10: 0. Ensure starter intake is 1.5-2 kg/day before weaning. According to MaxCare, weaning should be gradual over 2-3 weeks. Abrupt weaning causes growth check. In Africa, where starter may be limited, wean at 10-12 weeks. Use high-quality starter (18-20% protein, 3% fat, 10% fibre). Provide fresh water always. After weaning, continue starter for 2 weeks. Monitor weight: calves should gain 400-600 g/day. If not, adjust. Common mistakes: weaning too early (calves not eating enough starter), weaning too late (costly), and not providing water (calves cannot digest starter without water). In Nigeria, many farmers wean at 4 weeks, leading to stunted calves. According to Royal Canin, for orphaned calves, weaning should be based on starter intake, not age. Health: watch for pneumonia (coughing, rapid breathing). Treat with vet advice. Ensure good ventilation but no drafts. In hot climates, provide shade. In cold, provide bedding. Disbudding and castration at 2-4 weeks. Deworm at 4 weeks. Vaccinate for clostridial diseases at 4 weeks. Record all treatments. In South Africa, calf mortality can be 10-20% on small farms; with good management, reduce to <5%. Key: colostrum, hygiene, consistent feeding, and gradual weaning. According to NAP, calf mortality is directly linked to management. For smallholders, simple protocols work: feed at same times, weigh powder, clean equipment, and monitor daily. If a calf is sick, isolate. Use a separate bucket. Wear gloves. Disinfect. In Kenya, many farmers use herbal remedies; these may help but do not replace veterinary care. Always consult a vet for sick calves. Scours can kill in 24 hours; act fast. Electrolytes are cheap and save lives. Keep a stock. Weaning is a critical period; plan ahead. Buy starter before weaning. Store in dry place. Feed fresh. Do not let it mould. Mouldy feed causes poisoning. In Africa, aflatoxin is a risk. Buy from reputable suppliers. Check for mould. If present, discard. Health and weaning are intertwined; healthy calves wean easily. Sick calves do not. So focus on prevention.

5. Costs, Local Suppliers and Common Mistakes in Africa

Calf milk replacer is a significant cost for African smallholders. In Kenya, a 25 kg bag of quality replacer (e.g., Bidco, Denkavit) costs KES 12,000-20,000. In Nigeria, NGN 75,000-125,000. In South Africa, ZAR 1,500-2,500. This translates to KES 480-800 per kg, NGN 3,000-5,000 per kg, ZAR 60-100 per kg. Feeding one calf for 8 weeks requires about 25-30 kg powder, costing KES 12,000-24,000, NGN 75,000-150,000, ZAR 1,500-3,000. This is expensive for many smallholders. Alternatives: use fresh cow milk if available, but ensure it is not needed for human consumption. If using milk, feed 10% of body weight per day. For a 40 kg calf, 4 litres. But milk is also costly. Some farmers use a combination: milk replacer for first 2 weeks, then fresh milk. However, switching can cause scours. Do it gradually over 3 days. Local suppliers: In Kenya, Bidco, Nutri Feeds, Unga Farm Care. In Nigeria, Vital Feed, Chikun, Premier Feeds. In South Africa, Nutri Feeds, Epol, Meadow Feeds. In Tanzania, Interchick, Tanfeeds. In Uganda, Ugachick, Biyinzika. Always buy from licensed agrovets to avoid counterfeit. Counterfeit powder often has high fibre, low protein, and may contain melamine. Check packaging: sealed, with batch number, expiry date, and nutritional analysis. If price is too low, be suspicious. According to FAO, counterfeit feed is a growing problem in Africa. Common mistakes: 1) Using too little powder (e.g., 50 g per litre instead of 125 g) to save money. This results in watery milk, poor growth, and scours. 2) Mixing with cold water, causing lumps and digestive upset. 3) Feeding cold milk (below 35 degrees Celsius) which causes gut slowdown. 4) Not cleaning equipment, leading to bacterial growth. 5) Overfeeding (more than 2 litres per feeding for young calves) causing bloat. 6) Sudden changes in diet or schedule. 7) Using dirty water. 8) Storing powder in damp place; it cakes and grows mould. 9) Not weighing calves; feeding same amount regardless of weight. 10) Weaning too early or too late. According to Teagasc, these mistakes account for 70% of calf health problems. To reduce costs: buy in bulk with other farmers. Form a cooperative. In Kenya, groups can buy 25 kg bags at wholesale price. In Nigeria, similar. Also, consider using milk replacer only for calves that cannot get colostrum or when cow milk is scarce. For beef calves, suckling is best. For dairy, replacer allows more milk for sale. Calculate: if a cow produces 10 litres/day, feeding 4 litres to calf leaves 6 litres for sale. At KES 50/litre, that is KES 300/day. Milk replacer costs KES 480/kg; 0.5 kg/day for 4 litres costs KES 240. So profit is KES 60/day. But if milk price is low, replacer may not be profitable. Do the math. According to NAP, economics of calf rearing depend on milk price and replacer cost. In South Africa, milk price is ZAR 5-7/litre, replacer ZAR 60-100/kg. Feeding 0.5 kg/day costs ZAR 30-50, milk value ZAR 20-28 for 4 litres. So replacer may be more expensive than milk. But it frees milk for sale. Consider opportunity cost. In Africa, many smallholders use replacer to save milk for family or sale. But if replacer is poor quality, calves suffer. Invest in quality. According to Denkavit, a good replacer pays for itself in growth and health. For smallholders, start with a small trial: buy 5 kg, test on one calf. Monitor weight gain. If good, scale up. Also, consider local alternatives: some farmers make homemade replacer from eggs, milk, and oil. But these are not balanced and can cause deficiencies. Not recommended. Use commercial replacer. In Kenya, the Kenya Dairy Board regulates feed quality. In Nigeria, NAFDAC. In South Africa, Act 36 of 1947. Report counterfeit. Finally, train yourself and workers. Attend extension workshops. Read labels. Keep records. A well-managed calf is profitable. A poorly managed one is a loss. According to Royal Canin, for puppies and kittens, similar principles apply: use species-specific milk replacer, mix correctly, feed at body temperature, and follow schedule. But this course focuses on calves. For other young animals, see other modules.
