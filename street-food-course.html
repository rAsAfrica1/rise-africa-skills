Practical Reference

This reference block is designed to be used as a working tool, not a reading exercise. Print it, laminate it, and keep it in the store, the packhouse, or the sales vehicle. Every figure below is drawn from published public sources so that you can quote it to buyers, lenders, and regulators with confidence. Where African conditions differ from global averages, we flag it. Where money is at stake, we say so directly.

The chart above is the single most useful diagnostic you can share with a farmer group or a lender. Note the sequencing logic in Table 2. The cheapest items â crates, shade, hermetic bags, a moisture meter â also have the fastest payback. A cold room only makes sense once you have a reliable market that will pay a premium for quality and a supply volume that keeps the unit full. Empty cold rooms are the most expensive mistake in African horticulture. Do not buy refrigeration to solve a handling problem.

â  SAFETY WARNING â AFLATOXIN AND PESTICIDE RESIDUES

Aflatoxin is a cancer-causing mould toxin that develops in poorly dried and poorly stored maize, groundnut, and other grains. It cannot be seen, smelled, or tasted, and it is not destroyed by normal cooking. Grain stored above 13â14% moisture content, or in damp conditions, is high risk. Dry to safe moisture before storage, use hermetic or well-sealed bags, keep bags off damp floors, and never blend visibly mouldy grain into a clean lot for sale. Rejected consignments and delisted suppliers are the commercial consequence; liver disease is the human one.

Pesticides: always observe the pre-harvest interval printed on the label, wear gloves and a mask during application, never apply near water sources, and never use a banned or unregistered product to chase a buyer's cosmetic standard. Residue violations close export markets for whole countries, not just one farm. Store all chemicals locked, labelled, and away from food, feed, and children.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Harvest cool and early. Pick in the early morning or late afternoon, never in the midday heat, and move produce into shade within 30 minutes of harvest.
  2. Switch from sacks to crates. Sacks compress and bruise; rigid crates stacked no more than four high cut handling loss immediately and are reusable for years.
  3. Grade and sort at the field edge. Remove damaged, diseased, and over-ripe units before they contaminate a clean lot. One rotten tomato spoils the box.
  4. Test moisture before storage. Use a moisture meter on every grain lot. Maize and groundnut above 13â14% moisture must be dried further before bagging.
  5. Keep records that a lender will accept. Log harvest date, volume, grade, loss, buyer, and price per lot. This is your evidence base for credit and for negotiating contracts.
  6. Match investment to volume. Do not finance a cold room until you have a contracted buyer and enough throughput to keep it at least 70% full.
  7. Train the people who handle the produce. Most loss happens at the hands of casual labour. A two-hour handling session at the start of each season pays for itself in the first week.
  8. Verify every input and every buyer. Check pesticide registration, check buyer payment history, and put payment terms in writing before delivery.
