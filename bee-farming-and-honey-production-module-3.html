1. Types of Hives: Langstroth, Top Bar, Warre

When choosing a hive, you need to consider your budget, available materials, and how you plan to manage your bees. The three most common hives in Africa are the Langstroth, Top Bar, and Warre. Each has advantages and challenges.

The Langstroth hive is the most widely used worldwide. It consists of stacked rectangular boxes with removable frames. The bees build comb on the frames, which can be easily lifted out for inspection and honey harvesting. A standard Langstroth hive has a brood box (where the queen lays eggs) and supers (for honey storage). It requires precise construction because bee space must be exactly 3/8 inch (9.5 mm) to prevent bees from gluing frames together with propolis or building extra comb. Langstroth hives produce the most honey and are easiest to manage, but they are expensive to buy and require accurate woodworking skills.

The Top Bar hive is a simpler, low-cost alternative. It is a single long box with sloped sides and top bars resting across the top. Bees build natural comb hanging from the bars. You can inspect each comb by lifting a bar. Top Bar hives are easy to build with local materials and are good for beginners. However, the comb is fragile and can break during harvest, and honey yields are lower than Langstroth.

The Warre hive is a vertical, stackable hive similar to Langstroth but with top bars instead of frames. It is designed to mimic a hollow tree, with boxes added to the bottom rather than the top. Warre hives are less intrusive for bees and require less frequent inspections. They are popular in sustainable beekeeping but are harder to find and manage for maximum honey production.

In Africa, many beekeepers start with Top Bar hives because they are affordable and can be made from locally available timber. As you gain experience, you may upgrade to Langstroth for higher yields. Choose the hive that matches your resources and goals.

2. Materials and Construction

To build a durable hive, you need the right materials. The best wood for hives is seasoned timber that is not treated with toxic chemicals. In Africa, good options include cypress, cedar, mahogany, or eucalyptus. Avoid softwoods like pine that rot quickly, and never use pressure-treated wood because the chemicals can kill bees. You will need planks of 1-inch (2.5 cm) thickness for the boxes, and 1-inch by 1-inch strips for frames and top bars.

Essential tools for hive construction include a saw (hand saw or circular saw), hammer, nails or screws, wood glue, measuring tape, square, and sandpaper. For Langstroth hives, you also need a table saw or router to cut precise grooves for frames. A bee space of 3/8 inch (9.5 mm) is critical: if spaces are smaller, bees fill them with propolis; if larger, they build burr comb. So measure twice and cut once.

Start by cutting all pieces to size according to your hive plan. For a Langstroth brood box, the standard dimensions are 19 7/8 inches long, 16 1/4 inches wide, and 9 5/8 inches high (internal). Assemble the boxes using waterproof wood glue and galvanized nails or screws. Reinforce corners with metal brackets or cleats. For Top Bar hives, cut the sides at a 30-degree angle to mimic natural tree hollows, and make top bars 1 3/8 inches wide to allow bee space between combs.

After assembly, sand rough edges and apply a protective finish. Use linseed oil or exterior latex paint on the outside only. Never paint the inside. Allow the hive to dry completely before introducing bees. Proper construction ensures your hive lasts for years and keeps bees healthy.

3. Assembling a Langstroth Hive

Assembling a Langstroth hive is straightforward if you follow these steps. You will need the pre-cut wooden parts: bottom board, brood box, supers, inner cover, outer cover, frames, and foundation. Also gather nails, wood glue, hammer, and a hive tool.

Step 1: Build the brood box. Lay out the four sides of the brood box. Apply wood glue to the ends and nail them together to form a rectangular box. Use at least three nails per corner. Check for squareness by measuring diagonally; both diagonals should be equal. Repeat for any supers you have.

Step 2: Assemble the frames. Each frame consists of a top bar, bottom bar, and two side bars. Nail or glue them together. Then insert a sheet of foundation (wax or plastic) into the frame. The foundation gives bees a guide to build straight comb. For wax foundation, use a wire support or embed it with a heated tool. For plastic foundation, simply snap it into the frame.

Step 3: Attach the bottom board. The bottom board is a solid base with a landing board at the entrance. Nail it to the bottom of the brood box. Some beekeepers use a screened bottom board for better ventilation and pest control.

Step 4: Add the inner cover and outer cover. The inner cover has a hole for feeding and ventilation. Place it on top of the highest box. The outer cover is a weatherproof lid that fits over the inner cover.

Step 5: Install the entrance reducer. This is a small wooden strip that narrows the entrance to help the bees defend against robbers and pests. Use the smallest opening for new colonies.

Finally, place the assembled hive on a hive stand (see next lesson). Your Langstroth hive is now ready for bees.

4. Hive Stands and Placement

A hive stand raises your hive off the ground, protecting it from dampness, termites, and predators like ants and small animals. In Africa, a good hive stand is essential because of heavy rains and diverse pests. You can build a simple stand using four wooden posts (2x4 inches) set into the ground, with crossbeams to support the hive. The stand should be about 18-24 inches (45-60 cm) high. This height deters ants and keeps the hive dry. For extra protection, place the legs in cans of used motor oil or water to stop ants from climbing.

Choose a location for your hive that gets morning sun and afternoon shade. In hot African climates, full sun can melt wax and stress bees. A spot under a tree that provides dappled shade is ideal. Ensure the hive entrance faces away from prevailing winds and is not in a low area where water collects. Good air drainage prevents fungal diseases.

Place the hive where bees have easy access to water, nectar, and pollen. A source of clean water within 100 meters is important, especially during dry seasons. You can provide a shallow water dish with stones for bees to land on. Avoid placing hives near noisy roads, human traffic, or livestock pens, as vibrations disturb bees.

Keep hives at least 3-5 feet apart to prevent drifting of bees between colonies. If you have multiple hives, arrange them in a row or circle with entrances facing different directions. This reduces competition and disease spread. Also, ensure the hive is level; use a spirit level and adjust the stand. A tilted hive can cause comb to be built crooked.

Finally, secure your hive stand against strong winds. In some areas, you may need to tie the hive down with straps. A well-placed and sturdy stand is the foundation of a productive apiary.

5. Protective Clothing and Tools

Beekeeping requires protective clothing to prevent stings and keep you safe. The essential items are a veil, gloves, and a bee suit. A veil protects your face and neck. You can buy a hat with a veil or make one from mosquito netting and a wide-brimmed hat. Ensure the veil is dark-colored; bees are less attracted to dark colors. A full bee suit covers your body and is usually made of thick cotton or synthetic material. In hot African climates, choose a ventilated suit with mesh panels to allow airflow. If a full suit is too expensive, wear light-colored, thick clothing (like overalls) and tuck trousers into boots. Never wear dark, furry, or woolly clothing because bees may attack.

Gloves protect your hands. Leather gloves are durable but can be clumsy. Rubber or nitrile gloves allow better dexterity but may get sticky with propolis. Always keep gloves clean and free of holes. Wash them regularly to remove alarm pheromones from previous stings.

You also need a hive tool, which is a small metal pry bar used to separate boxes and frames. It is essential for inspections. A bee brush is a soft brush to gently move bees aside. A smoker is a metal can with a bellows that produces cool smoke to calm bees. Use dry, clean fuel like dried grass, cardboard, or pine needles. Light the smoker before opening the hive and puff smoke at the entrance and under the lid.

Other useful tools include a frame grip for lifting frames, a uncapping knife for harvesting honey, and a bucket with a lid for holding tools. Always work with confidence and move slowly. Avoid crushing bees, as this releases alarm pheromones that trigger stinging. With proper gear and calm handling, beekeeping is safe and enjoyable.

6. Setting Up an Apiary Site

An apiary is a place where you keep your beehives. Choosing the right site is crucial for bee health and honey production. Start by selecting an area with abundant bee forage. Bees need nectar and pollen from flowers, trees, and crops. Good sources in Africa include acacia, citrus, sunflower, eucalyptus, and indigenous wildflowers. Aim for a site with diverse flowering plants that bloom at different times of the year.

Your apiary should be accessible for you to manage the hives but away from frequent human activity. A distance of at least 100 meters from homes, schools, and livestock is ideal. This reduces the risk of stings and disturbance. Also, avoid areas prone to flooding or strong winds. A gentle slope with good drainage is best. In hot regions, provide shade during the hottest part of the day, but ensure morning sun reaches the hives to warm them and encourage early foraging.

Water is essential. Bees need a reliable source of clean water within 100-200 meters. If none exists, create a shallow water station with floating sticks or stones so bees can drink without drowning. Keep the water fresh to prevent mosquito breeding.

Plan the layout of your hives. If you have more than one, space them 3-5 feet apart and arrange them in a way that entrances face different directions. This reduces drifting and competition. You can also paint hives different colors to help bees identify their home. Keep the grass around hives short to reduce pests and allow air circulation, but leave some tall vegetation as a windbreak.

Finally, secure your apiary with a fence to keep out livestock and thieves. In some areas, you may need to guard against honey badgers and elephants. A strong fence and perhaps a guard dog can help. Visit your apiary regularly to monitor hive health, check for pests, and ensure everything is in order. A well-set apiary will reward you with healthy bees and good honey harvests.

<h2>Practical Reference: Hive Selection, Assembly, and Apiary Setup for African Beekeepers</h2>

<p>Beekeeping in Africa is not a hobby. It is a business with a low entry cost, high market demand for honey and beeswax, and growing opportunities in pollination services, propolis, and value-added products. The difference between a profitable apiary and a failed one often comes down to decisions made before the first bee arrives: which hive you choose, how you assemble it, where you place it, and what protective equipment you use. This practical reference gives you the field-tested specifications, materials, and setup steps you need to move from planning to production.</p>

<h3>Choosing the Right Hive for Your Market</h3>

<p>Three hive types dominate modern African beekeeping: the Langstroth, the Top Bar, and the Warre. Each has a different business case.</p>

<p><strong>Langstroth:</strong> The global standard for commercial honey production. Its movable frames allow centrifugal extraction, which means you can harvest honey without destroying comb. This is the only hive type that reliably supports repeat harvests from the same colony. Best for beekeepers targeting bulk honey sales, supermarkets, and export markets.</p>

<p><strong>Top Bar:</strong> Cheaper to build and easier to manage for beginners. Bees build natural comb from a simple wooden bar. Harvesting usually destroys the comb, which reduces wax yield and stresses the colony. Best for low-capital startups and regions where timber is expensive but labour is cheap.</p>

<p><strong>Warre:</strong> A vertical, top-bar hive designed for natural beekeeping. It mimics a hollow tree and requires minimal intervention. Honey harvest is lower per hive, but the wax quality is excellent. Best for premium, organic, or niche markets.</p>

<h3>Materials and Construction</h3>

<p>Hive construction in Africa must balance durability, cost, and bee health. Timber choice matters more than most beginners realise. Softwoods like cypress and pine are easy to work but rot quickly in humid tropical conditions. Hardwoods like mvule, mahogany, and eucalyptus last longer but are heavier and more expensive.</p>

<p>Treat all timber with beeswax or food-grade linseed oil. Never use creosote, engine oil, or lead-based paint. Bees detect and reject chemical odours, and contaminated honey fails export tests.</p>

<h3>Assembling a Langstroth Hive</h3>

<p>Assembly is straightforward if you follow the sequence. Rushing this step leads to gaps that robbers and pests exploit.</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Prepare the bottom board.</strong> Attach the floor to the stand or base. Ensure it slopes slightly forward (about 5 degrees) so rainwater and condensation drain out.</li>
<li><strong>Assemble the brood chamber.</strong> Nail or screw the four sides. Use wood glue at joints for a bee-tight seal. Check that corners are square.</li>
<li><strong>Build frames.</strong> Assemble 10 frames per box. Fit foundation (wax or plastic) into each frame. Nail top bars and bottom bars securely.</li>
<li><strong>Fit frames into the brood chamber.</strong> Space them evenly. Leave no gaps wider than 6 mm, or bees will build burr comb.</li>
<li><strong>Add the queen excluder.</strong> Place it flat on top of the brood chamber. This keeps the queen below and honey above.</li>
<li><strong>Stack the honey super.</strong> Add one or two supers with frames. Each super holds 10 frames.</li>
<li><strong>Fit the inner cover and roof.</strong> The inner cover prevents bees from propolising the roof. The roof must overhang the box by at least 5 cm on all sides.</li>
</ol>

<p>A well-assembled Langstroth hive should be bee-tight except for the entrance. Run your hand along every joint. If you feel a gap, seal it.</p>

<h3>Hive Stands and Placement</h3>

<p>Never place a hive directly on the ground. Ground-level hives attract ants, termites, cane rats, and small hive beetles. A stand raises the hive to 4060 cm and protects it from flooding and predators.</p>

<p>Placement rules:</p>
<ul>
<li>Face the entrance away from prevailing winds and human traffic.</li>
<li>Provide partial shade. Full sun overheats the hive; deep shade encourages pests and dampness.</li>
<li>Keep hives 12 metres apart to reduce drifting and disease spread.</li>
<li>Place hives on a slight slope, never in a waterlogged depression.</li>
<li>Keep hives away from animal pens, kitchens, and footpaths.</li>
</ul>

<div style="border:2px solid #b00020; background:#fff5f5; padding:12px; margin:16px 0; font-family:Arial; font-size:14px;">
<strong style="color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING:</strong> Never place hives near schools, markets, or residential footpaths. In many African countries, beekeeping near public spaces requires local authority approval. Bee stings can cause anaphylactic shock, which is life-threatening. Always keep a first-aid kit with antihistamines and epinephrine (if available) at the apiary. Wear protective clothing at all times during inspections. Do not inspect hives at night or during storms.
</div>

<h3>Setting Up an Apiary Site</h3>

<p>Site selection determines colony health, honey yield, and your ability to work safely. A good apiary site has four characteristics:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Forage within 23 km.</strong> Bees fly up to 3 km for nectar and pollen. Ensure flowering plants, crops, or trees are available year-round.</li>
<li><strong>Water source nearby.</strong> Bees need water for cooling and diluting honey. A clean stream, pond, or water trough within 500 m is ideal.</li>
<li><strong>Wind protection.</strong> Use hedges, trees, or a simple windbreak. Strong winds reduce foraging and can topple hives.</li>
<li><strong>Security and access.</strong> Fence the apiary to keep out livestock and thieves. Ensure you can reach it by foot or vehicle in all seasons.</li>
</ol>

<p>Clear the site of tall grass and debris. This reduces pests and fire risk. Mark hive positions before you bring in colonies so you can space them correctly from day one.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul>
<li>Choose hive type based on market: Langstroth for commercial honey, Top Bar for low-cost entry, Warre for premium natural products.</li>
<li>Use treated, food-safe timber. Never use creosote, engine oil, or lead paint.</li>
<li>Assemble Langstroth hives bee-tight with 10 frames per box and a queen excluder between brood and honey supers.</li>
<li>Raise every hive on a stand 4060 cm above ground. Never place hives directly on soil.</li>
<li>Place hives in partial shade, facing away from wind and foot traffic, 12 m apart.</li>
<li>Wear a full bee suit, veil, and gloves during every inspection. Keep a first-aid kit at the apiary.</li>
<li>Ensure forage and water are within 23 km of the apiary. Fence the site for security.</li>
<li>Inspect hives weekly during the first month, then every 710 days during the active season.</li>
</ul>
