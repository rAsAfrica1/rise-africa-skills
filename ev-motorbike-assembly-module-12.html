1. Torque Check of Critical Fasteners

After assembling an EV motorbike, you must check the tightness of every critical bolt and nut. Loose fasteners can cause accidents. Use a calibrated torque wrench. For this module, you need a torque wrench that reads from 5 Nm to 100 Nm. Start with the front and rear axle nuts. The front axle nut should be tightened to 60 Nm. The rear axle nut to 80 Nm. Check the brake caliper bolts: front caliper bolts to 25 Nm, rear caliper bolts to 25 Nm. The handlebar clamp bolts to 20 Nm. The battery mounting bolts to 15 Nm. The motor mount bolts to 40 Nm. Always tighten in a star pattern if there are multiple bolts, like on the wheel. Use a torque wrench, not a regular spanner. If you don't have a torque wrench, borrow one from a nearby garage. In Africa, many workshops have torque wrenches for petrol bikes; they work the same. After tightening, mark each bolt with a paint pen so you know it's been checked. Do a final check after the first 50 km of riding, as some bolts may settle. Never overtighten, as you can strip threads or break the bolt. If you feel the wrench click, stop. If a bolt is stripped, replace it with a high-tensile steel bolt of the same size. Record all torque values in a logbook. This is your proof of quality. A well-torqued bike is a safe bike.

2. Fluid and Lubricant Top-Off

EV motorbikes have fewer fluids than petrol bikes, but you still need to check and top off several. First, brake fluid. Most EV bikes use DOT 3 or DOT 4 brake fluid. Check the reservoir on the handlebar and near the rear brake. The fluid should be between the MIN and MAX lines. If low, top up with fresh fluid from a sealed bottle. Never mix DOT 3 and DOT 4. Use a clean cloth to wipe any spills, as brake fluid damages paint. Second, battery coolant. Some high-performance EV bikes have liquid-cooled batteries and motors. Check the coolant level in the overflow tank. Use a 50/50 mix of distilled water and ethylene glycol coolant. If you live in a very hot area, use a 60/40 mix. Do not use tap water; it causes corrosion. Third, chain lubricant. If your bike has a chain drive, clean the chain with a brush and kerosene, then apply chain lube. Spin the wheel and spray the inside of the chain. Let it dry for 10 minutes. Fourth, check the gear oil in the reduction gearbox. Some EVs have a small gearbox that needs 80W-90 gear oil. Check the level with the dipstick or fill plug. Top off if needed. Fifth, check the brake pads and discs for wear. Replace pads if less than 2 mm thick. Finally, check tire pressure. Front tire: 28 psi, rear tire: 32 psi. Adjust for load. In Africa, roads can be rough, so slightly lower pressure improves grip. Always use a pressure gauge, not your thumb. Record all top-offs in your logbook.

3. Software Update and Calibration

Modern EV motorbikes have a controller, battery management system (BMS), and sometimes a display. These need software updates and calibration. First, connect your laptop or smartphone to the bike's diagnostic port. This is usually a USB or CAN bus connector under the seat. Use the manufacturer's app or software. If you don't have it, download it from the manufacturer's website. For many African brands, the software is free. Update the firmware to the latest version. This fixes bugs and improves performance. After updating, calibrate the throttle. Turn the key on, but don't twist the throttle. The system will learn the zero position. Then twist the throttle fully and hold for 3 seconds. Release. The system learns the full position. Next, calibrate the battery. Fully charge the battery, then ride until it is 20% remaining. Then fully charge again. This helps the BMS learn the true capacity. Also calibrate the speedometer. Use a GPS app on your phone to check actual speed. If the speedometer reads 40 km/h but GPS says 45 km/h, adjust the wheel size setting in the software. Finally, check for error codes. The display may show a wrench icon. Use the software to read and clear codes. If a code persists, check the sensor or wiring. Common codes: P0A0F (motor overheat), P0B0F (battery undervoltage). Fix issues before handover. Document the software version and calibration date in the logbook.

4. Cleaning and Detailing

A clean bike shows quality and makes the customer happy. Start by washing the bike. Use a bucket of water and a mild car shampoo. Do not use a high-pressure hose, as water can get into the battery, controller, and motor. Use a soft sponge or cloth. Wash from top to bottom. Rinse with a gentle stream of water. Dry with a microfiber cloth. Next, clean the battery pack and motor with a damp cloth only. Never spray water directly on electrical components. For the chain, use a degreaser and a brush, then rinse and dry, then apply chain lube. For the brakes, clean the calipers with brake cleaner and a toothbrush. Do not get brake cleaner on painted surfaces. For the body panels, use a plastic polish to restore shine. For the seat, use a vinyl cleaner. For the tires, use a tire shine, but avoid the tread. After cleaning, inspect for any damage: cracks, loose bolts, frayed cables. Touch up paint chips with matching paint. Replace any missing decals. Finally, polish the metal parts with chrome polish. In Africa, dust is a problem, so use a dust cover when parked. A clean bike also helps you spot leaks or cracks. Take pride in your work. A detailed bike sells itself. Handover a bike that looks new.

5. Documentation and Warranty Registration

Every EV motorbike you assemble must have proper paperwork. Start with the assembly checklist. This is a form that lists every step you did: torque checks, fluid top-offs, software updates, cleaning. Sign and date it. Next, fill out the warranty card. The customer gets one copy, you keep one. The warranty covers the battery for 2 years or 20,000 km, whichever comes first. The motor and controller for 1 year. The frame for 3 years. Write the serial number of the bike, the battery, and the motor. These are usually on a sticker or engraved. Take photos of the serial numbers. Next, create an invoice or receipt. Include the date, customer name, phone number, address, bike model, color, and price. If the customer paid a deposit, note the balance. Also, give the customer the owner's manual. If you don't have a printed manual, download a PDF and put it on a USB stick or send via WhatsApp. Next, register the bike with the local authorities if required. In many African countries, you need a license plate and insurance. Help the customer with this. Finally, keep a copy of all documents in a file. This protects you if there is a dispute. Also, record the customer's details in a database for future service reminders. Send a reminder after 500 km for the first service. Good documentation builds trust and repeat business.

6. Customer Handover and Basic Training

The handover is your chance to teach the customer how to use and care for their new EV motorbike. Start by showing them the controls: key ignition, throttle, brakes, lights, horn, and turn signals. Show them how to turn it on and off. Explain the battery gauge: green means full, yellow means half, red means charge soon. Show them how to charge: plug into a standard 220V socket, using the charger provided. Charge for 6-8 hours for a full charge. Do not leave it plugged in for more than 24 hours. Show them how to check tire pressure and brake fluid. Teach them the daily checks: tires, brakes, lights, battery level. Show them how to adjust the mirrors and handlebar. Explain the regenerative braking: when you release the throttle, the motor slows the bike and charges the battery. This saves energy. Warn them not to touch the battery terminals or open the battery box. If they have a problem, they should call you, not try to fix it themselves. Give them your phone number and the workshop address. Do a test ride with them: you ride first, then they ride in a safe area. Watch them start, stop, and turn. Correct any mistakes. Finally, give them the keys, documents, and a small toolkit. Wish them safe riding. A good handover creates a happy customer who will recommend you to others.

<section>
<h2>Final Inspection and Handover Practical Reference</h2>

<p>Final inspection is where an assembled electric motorbike stops being a pile of parts and becomes a saleable asset. In the African e-mobility market Nairobi boda boda riders, Kigali delivery fleets, Kampala commercial riders, Lagos last-mile operators the handover moment determines whether you get repeat business, warranty claims, or a damaged brand. This lesson gives you the shop-floor sequence, torque values, fluid specs, software steps, and the paperwork that protects both the customer and your margin. Treat it as your pre-delivery inspection (PDI) playbook.</p>

<p>Business reality: a single missed fastener or uncalibrated controller can cost you a full warranty replacement, a stranded rider, and lost fleet contracts worth tens of thousands of dollars. Every step below exists because someone, somewhere, paid for skipping it.</p>

<h3>1. Torque Check of Critical Fasteners</h3>
<p>Torque is not "tight enough." It is a measured value. On an e-motorbike the critical joints are: axle nuts, brake caliper bolts, steering stem nut, motor mount bolts, battery pack mounting bolts, and handlebar clamp bolts. Under-torque causes vibration loosening and catastrophic failure; over-torque strips threads, cracks castings, and deforms brake discs.</p>

<p>Use a calibrated torque wrench never a rattle gun on critical joints. Record every value on the PDI sheet. In dusty African conditions, apply thread-locker (medium strength, e.g., Loctite 243) on motor mounts and caliper bolts, and re-check after the first 100 km.</p>

<h3>2. Fluid and Lubricant Top-Off</h3>
<p>Electric motorbikes have fewer fluids than ICE bikes, but the ones they have matter. Check and top off: brake fluid (DOT 3 or DOT 4, never mix), hydraulic brake lines for leaks, wheel bearing grease, suspension fork oil (if applicable), chain lubricant (on chain-drive models), and gearbox oil in hub or mid-drive motors that require it.</p>

<p>In hot, dusty African operating conditions, brake fluid absorbs moisture faster. Test with a brake fluid tester; replace if water content exceeds 3%. Use only sealed, fresh containers opened fluid in a dusty workshop is contaminated fluid.</p>

Do not open the reservoir in a dusty environment without cleaning the cap first. Never mix DOT 3 and DOT 4 unless the OEM explicitly allows it. For high-voltage battery packs (48V72V), always disconnect the main fuse and wear insulated gloves before touching terminals. A shorted pack can cause arc flash, fire, or fatal shock. In African workshops with unreliable grounding, verify your earth connection before any electrical work.</p>

<h3>3. Software Update and Calibration</h3>
<p>Modern e-motorbikes have a controller (BLDC or FOC), a BMS, and often a display/telematics unit. Before handover you must: (1) check the controller firmware version against the latest OEM release; (2) update via the manufacturer's app or cable; (3) calibrate the throttle (zero and full position); (4) calibrate the brake cut-off switch; (5) verify speedometer accuracy against GPS; (6) check BMS cell balance and state of health (SoH).</p>

<p>Calibration matters commercially: a mis-calibrated throttle causes jerky acceleration, which riders interpret as a defective bike. A mis-reading speedometer creates legal and trust problems. Log the firmware version and calibration values on the PDI sheet this is your evidence if a warranty dispute arises.</p>

<h3>4. Cleaning and Detailing</h3>
<p>A clean bike sells. But cleaning is also inspection: dirt hides cracks, leaks, and loose fasteners. Use low-pressure water only never a pressure washer near the controller, motor connectors, display, or battery compartment. Use a soft brush and pH-neutral cleaner. Dry with compressed air or microfiber. Apply a protective spray to the frame and a silicone dressing to rubber parts.</p>

<p>In dusty African conditions, pay special attention to the air intake of the controller cooling system, the motor cooling fins, and the brake caliper area. Clogged cooling fins cause controller overheating a leading cause of warranty returns in hot climates.</p>

<h3>5. Documentation and Warranty Registration</h3>
<p>This is where most African assemblers lose money. Incomplete paperwork means denied warranty claims. You need: a completed PDI checklist (signed), a warranty registration card with frame number (VIN), motor number, battery serial number, controller serial number, customer name, phone, ID, purchase date, and dealer stamp. Register the warranty in the OEM's system within 48 hours. Give the customer a physical copy and keep a digital copy.</p>

<p>Also prepare: owner's manual (in the customer's language where possible), service schedule card, and a first-service reminder. For fleet customers (delivery companies, boda boda SACCOs), provide a fleet handover sheet listing every unit's serial numbers.</p>

<h3>6. Customer Handover and Basic Training</h3>
<p>The handover is a sales and retention event, not a formality. Spend 2030 minutes with the customer. Cover: (1) how to charge safely correct voltage, charging location, charging time, and what not to do; (2) how to read the display and battery indicator; (3) daily pre-ride checks (brakes, tyres, lights, throttle response); (4) what to do in a fault who to call, what not to touch; (5) warranty terms and service intervals; (6) basic troubleshooting (reset procedure, fuse location).</p>

<p>For African riders, emphasize charging safety: many use shared or informal power sources. Explain the risk of overloading sockets, using wrong chargers, and charging in wet conditions. A short, practical demonstration beats a long lecture. Give them a laminated quick-start card in their language.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li> Torque-check all critical fasteners with a calibrated wrench; record values.</li>
<li> Top off brake fluid, lubricate chain, check for leaks.</li>
<li> Update controller/BMS firmware; calibrate throttle, brake cut-off, speedometer.</li>
<li> Clean and detail using low-pressure water; inspect for cracks and loose parts.</li>
<li> Complete PDI sheet, warranty card, and register warranty within 48 hours.</li>
<li> Confirm frame, motor, battery, and controller serial numbers match documents.</li>
<li> Deliver 2030 minute customer training on charging, riding, and fault response.</li>
<li> Provide owner's manual, service card, and first-service reminder.</li>
</ul>
