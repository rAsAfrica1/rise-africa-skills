1. Arc Welding Basics

Arc welding is the most common way to join metal on a small farm or workshop. You need a stick welder (also called SMAW), electrodes (rods), a chipping hammer, a wire brush, and a welding helmet with a dark lens (shade 10 or 11). For farm tools like hoes, ploughs, and gates, use 6013 or 7018 electrodes. 6013 is easy to strike and works on dirty metal; 7018 gives stronger welds but needs clean metal and dry storage.

Start by cleaning your metal with a grinder or wire brush. Rust, paint, and oil cause weak welds. Set your welder to about 90â120 amps for 3.2 mm electrodes on mild steel. Clamp your ground clamp to bare metal close to the weld. Strike the arc by tapping or scratching the electrode like a match. Once the arc starts, hold a 15â20Â° angle and move steadily. The sound should be like frying bacon. If it pops or sputters, adjust your amps or arc length.

For a good bead, keep the arc length about the diameter of the electrode core. Move at a speed that gives a bead twice as wide as the electrode. Too fast gives a thin, weak weld; too slow burns through thin metal. Practice on scrap steel first. Run straight beads, then lap welds, then T-joints. For farm repairs, a T-joint is common when attaching a handle to a tool head.

Safety is critical. Wear a leather apron, gloves, and closed shoes. Never weld near fuel, paint, or dry grass. Have a bucket of water or sand nearby. After welding, chip off the slag with your chipping hammer and brush the weld clean. Inspect for holes, undercut, or cracks. If you see defects, grind them out and weld again. A good weld is smooth, even, and fully fused at the edges.

With practice, you can repair wheelbarrows, make gates, and build tool frames. Start with simple projects like a welding table or a fire grate. Keep your rods dryâstore them in a sealed container with a handful of rice or silica gel. A basic stick welder costs about $100â200 and pays for itself quickly on a farm.

2. Gas Welding and Cutting

Gas welding uses a flame from burning acetylene and oxygen to melt and join metal. The same setup with a cutting torch can slice through steel up to 100 mm thick. You need an oxygen cylinder, an acetylene cylinder, regulators, hoses (red for acetylene, green or black for oxygen), a welding torch, a cutting torch, spark lighter, goggles, and gloves. This is great for thin metal (0.5â3 mm), brazing, and heating stuck bolts.

Set up safely. Acetylene is unstable above 15 psi, so never set the acetylene regulator above 15 psi. Oxygen can be up to 40 psi for cutting. Open the acetylene valve slightly and light it with a spark lighter. Then slowly open the oxygen valve until the flame becomes neutralâa sharp blue cone with a slight feather. A neutral flame is best for welding steel. A carburizing flame (more acetylene) adds carbon; an oxidizing flame (more oxygen) burns steel.

For welding, hold the torch at a 45Â° angle and move the puddle forward. Add filler rod with your other hand. Keep the inner cone of the flame just above the metal. For thin sheet, use a small tip (size 0) and low pressure. For cutting, heat the metal until it is bright red, then press the oxygen lever. The oxygen jet blows molten metal away. Move steadily along your cut line. You can cut curves, straight lines, and even bolt holes.

Gas welding is slower than arc welding but gives you more control on thin materials. It is ideal for repairing aluminium irrigation pipes, brazing copper fittings, and heating bearings. In rural Africa, gas welding is common in small workshops because it is versatile. However, acetylene and oxygen are expensive and need careful storage. Keep cylinders upright, away from heat, and secure them with a chain.

Always leak-test hoses and connections with soapy waterânever a flame. Wear goggles with a shade 4 or 5 lens. After work, close both cylinder valves, bleed the hoses, and back off the regulator screws. A complete gas welding kit costs $300â500, but you can start with a small portable set. Practice on scrap metal until you can run a clean bead without burning holes.

3. MIG and TIG Welding

MIG (Metal Inert Gas) and TIG (Tungsten Inert Gas) welding are cleaner and faster than stick welding, but they need more equipment and skill. MIG uses a spool of wire that feeds automatically through a gun. TIG uses a tungsten electrode and a separate filler rod, giving very precise control. Both use shielding gasâusually argon or a mix of argon and CO2âto protect the weld from air.

MIG welding is great for thin sheets (0.8â6 mm) and production work. You need a MIG welder, gas cylinder, regulator, wire (0.6â1.0 mm), and a helmet with shade 10â13. Set voltage and wire speed according to the thickness chart on the machine. For 1 mm steel, try 18 volts and wire speed 3â4 m/min. Hold the gun at a 10â15Â° angle and push away from the weld. The sound should be a steady buzz. MIG is easy to learn but the wire can bird-nest if tension is wrong. Keep your tip clean and replace it when worn.

TIG welding is slower but produces beautiful welds on aluminium, stainless steel, and thin tubes. You need a TIG torch, tungsten electrode (2% thoriated for steel, pure for aluminium), filler rod, and argon gas. Set the machine to DC for steel, AC for aluminium. Sharpen the tungsten to a point. Hold the torch like a pencil, create a puddle, then dip the filler rod. TIG is perfect for repairing aluminium irrigation pipes and dairy equipment. It takes practice to coordinate both hands.

In Africa, MIG and TIG are common in urban fabrication shops but rare on small farms due to cost. A basic MIG welder costs $300â600; a TIG welder $500â1000. However, if you do a lot of aluminium or stainless work, they pay off. You can also hire a portable MIG welder for specific jobs. Always use the correct shielding gas: pure argon for TIG, 75% argon/25% CO2 for MIG on steel. Never weld in windy conditionsâthe gas blows away. Keep your metal clean; MIG and TIG are less forgiving of rust than stick welding.

Start by practicing on scrap. For MIG, run beads on 2 mm steel. For TIG, fuse edges without filler first. Once you can make consistent beads, try butt joints and T-joints. Safety: wear a respirator when welding galvanized metal (zinc fumes are toxic). Always have good ventilation.

4. Fabricating Frames and Structures

Fabricating frames for farm equipmentâlike trailers, gates, tool racks, and animal pensârequires planning, measuring, cutting, and welding. Start with a drawing on paper or cardboard. Decide on dimensions: for a gate, a common size is 3 m wide by 1.2 m high. Use square steel tubing (25 mm x 25 mm x 2 mm wall) or angle iron (30 mm x 30 mm x 3 mm). For heavy trailers, use 50 mm x 50 mm x 3 mm square tubing.

Measure twice, cut once. Use a tape measure, square, and soapstone or marker. Cut with an angle grinder with a cutting disc (1 mm thick) or a hacksaw for small pieces. For accurate 90Â° cuts, use a mitre box or a speed square. Clean the ends with a grinder. Clamp pieces to a flat welding table or concrete floor. Tack weld (small welds) at corners first, then check for square by measuring diagonals. If diagonals are equal, the frame is square.

Weld the frame in a sequence to avoid warping. Weld opposite corners first, then the others. Use a stitch weld (2 cm weld, 2 cm gap) for thin tubing to prevent burn-through. For strong joints, use a full weld on all sides. After welding, grind smooth. For a gate, add hinges and a latch. For a trailer, weld the axle mounts and a hitch. Always wear gloves and a helmet.

Reinforce critical points. Add gussets (triangular plates) at corners. Use a diagonal brace on gates to prevent sagging. For a tool rack, weld hooks or shelves. If you need to bend tubing, use a pipe bender or heat with a torch. For holes, use a drill press or a hole saw. Deburr all edges.

In Africa, many fabricators use recycled steel from old cars or bed frames. That is fine, but clean off paint and rust first. Test your frame by loading it. A good gate should hold 100 kg without bending. Paint with red oxide primer then enamel. With practice, you can build wheelbarrows, chicken coops, and even a small trailer. Start with a simple project like a welding table or a bike rack.

5. Bending and Rolling Metal

Bending and rolling let you shape metal into curves, cylinders, and angles without cutting and welding. You need a bending machine (brake), a roll bender, or simple hand tools like a pipe bender, hammers, and a vice. For farm tools, you might bend handles, make rings for animal traps, or roll sheet metal for feed troughs.

For bending flat bar or tubing, use a manual pipe bender. Mark your bend line with soapstone. For a 90Â° bend in 20 mm tubing, heat the area with a torch until red, then pull the handle slowly. For cold bending, use a hydraulic bender. Always bend against the grain if possible. For sheet metal up to 1.5 mm, you can use a hand brake or even a piece of angle iron clamped in a vice. Place the sheet between two angles and hammer the edge over. For a clean bend, use a mallet, not a steel hammer.

Rolling creates cylinders. A roll bender has three rollers. Adjust the top roller down gradually, passing the sheet back and forth. For a 200 mm diameter cylinder from 1 mm steel, start with a flat sheet 628 mm long (Ï x diameter). Roll a little at a time. For thicker metal, pre-heat with a torch. You can also roll rings from round bar for hinges or decorative work. In rural Africa, many workshops use homemade roll benders made from car jacks and steel pipes.

Bending and rolling require patience. Always overbend slightly because metal springs back. For a 90Â° bend, bend to 92â95Â°. Test on scrap first. If you bend too far, you can sometimes straighten with a hammer and dolly. For tight curves, use a series of small bends or heat with a torch. Never bend cast ironâit cracks. Aluminium bends easily but work-hardens; anneal it by heating and quenching.

Safety: wear gloves and eye protection. Hot metal looks the same as cold metal. Use tongs. Keep your work area clean. After bending, check dimensions. For a feed trough, roll a half-cylinder and weld end caps. For a tool handle, bend a loop at the end. Practice on scrap until you can make consistent bends. A simple pipe bender costs $50â100; a roll bender $200â500. You can also hire a local workshop to roll large pieces.

6. Finishing and Painting Metal Tools

Finishing and painting protect metal tools from rust and make them last. After welding, grinding, and bending, your tool has slag, scale, and sharp edges. Start by cleaning: chip off slag with a chipping hammer, then grind welds smooth with a 115 mm angle grinder and a 60-grit flap disc. Remove all rust with a wire brush or a grinder. For tight spots, use a wire wheel on a drill. Wipe with a clean rag and degreaser (acetone or petrol).

Next, apply a primer. Red oxide primer is cheap and works well on steel. Brush or spray a thin coat. Let it dry for 4â6 hours. For aluminium, use a zinc chromate primer. For galvanized steel, use a special etch primer or vinegar wash first. Primer stops rust and helps paint stick. If you are in a humid area, prime the same day you clean the metal.

Then paint with a topcoat. Enamel paint (oil-based) is durable and available in most African towns. Use a brush for small tools or a spray gun for larger frames. Apply two thin coats rather than one thick coat. Let each coat dry 24 hours. For handles, use a different colour to show grip area. For cutting edges (like hoes), do not paintâkeep them oiled. You can also use powder coating if you have a local shop, but it is more expensive.

For a professional finish, sand lightly between coats with 400-grit paper. Wipe dust. After the final coat, let it cure for 48 hours before use. For tools that get heavy wear, like ploughs, use a two-part epoxy paint. For animal pens, use non-toxic paint if animals may chew. Always paint in a well-ventilated area, away from sparks. Wear a respirator.

Maintenance: touch up scratches immediately. Keep tools oiled with used engine oil or vegetable oil. Store under cover. A well-painted tool can last 5â10 years. In Africa, many farmers skip painting, but a painted tool fetches a better price at market. You can also brand your tools with a stencil or a welded initial. Finishing is the step that turns a rough fabrication into a sellable product. Practice on a simple hoe or rake before moving to complex frames.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Welding and Metal Fabrication for African Agribusiness

Tool making for agriculture is a business. Whether you are fabricating ox-drawn ploughs in Mbeya, repairing maize sheller drums in Nakuru, or building poultry house frames in Ogun State, a working knowledge of welding and metal fabrication translates directly into revenue. This practical reference consolidates the essentials from the module so that artisans, workshop owners, and farm mechanics can quote jobs accurately, work safely, and deliver tools that survive real field conditions, not showroom conditions.

Across sub-Saharan Africa, the demand for fabricated agricultural tools is steady: replacement plough shares, harrows, seed drill frames, animal-drawn carts, maize shellers, feed mixers, and irrigation stands. Many of these are made in small workshops using arc welding, oxy-acetylene cutting, and increasingly MIG welding where electricity is reliable. The economics favour local fabrication because imported tools are expensive and spare parts scarce. Your competitive edge is quality control, turnaround time, and understanding the farm environment: dust, moisture, rough handling, and repairs done with minimal equipment.

This reference is organised around the six lessons in the module, with data tables, a safety warning, a quick checklist, and free further reading. Treat it as your workshop bench companion.

Arc Welding Basics (SMAW)

Shielded Metal Arc Welding remains the backbone of rural fabrication because the equipment is cheap, portable, and tolerant of generator power. For agricultural tools, use E6013 electrodes for general fabrication and E7018 for high-stress components such as plough beams and hitch points. Keep electrodes dry; in humid coastal workshops, store them in a sealed container with silica gel or warm them before use. A common mistake is running too long an arc, which causes spatter and poor penetration. For 6 mm plate typical of plough components, a 3.2 mm electrode at 110â130 A gives reliable penetration.

Gas Welding and Cutting

Oxy-acetylene is invaluable for cutting thick sections, heating seized bolts, and brazing. In many African workshops, it is the only practical way to cut steel beyond 10 mm without a plasma cutter. Set a neutral flame for steel welding and a slightly oxidising flame for cutting. Always check hoses for cracks and use flashback arrestors; acetylene is unstable above 15 psi. Gas cutting is also the fastest way to shape plough shares and harrow tines from plate.

MIG and TIG Welding

MIG (GMAW) is faster and cleaner than arc welding, making it ideal for production runs of gates, crates, and frames. It requires shielding gas (COâ or Ar/COâ mix) and stable power. TIG (GTAW) produces high-quality welds on thin sheet and aluminium, useful for irrigation fittings and dairy equipment, but is slower and demands more skill. In areas with unreliable grid power, a generator-rated inverter MIG welder is a sound investment.

Fabricating Frames and Structures

Agricultural structuresâpoultry houses, greenhouses, drying racks, and cattle crushesâdepend on square, rigid frames. Always tack-weld and check diagonals before completing welds. Use jigs for repetitive work: a simple steel jig for a maize sheller frame can cut assembly time by half and improve consistency. Gussets at corners add strength cheaply. For outdoor structures, specify galvanised or painted steel and avoid trapped moisture pockets.

Bending and Rolling Metal

Bending is essential for making tool handles, wheel rims, and curved plough components. A hydraulic pipe bender or a simple H-frame press with dies handles most workshop needs. For rolling, a slip roller forms cylinders for feed troughs and water tanks. Always account for springback: over-bend by 2â5 degrees depending on material thickness. Heat bending with oxy-acetylene is practical for one-off jobs.

Finishing and Painting Metal Tools

Finishing determines tool life and customer satisfaction. Remove slag, spatter, and scale with a chipping hammer and wire brush, then grind smooth. For agricultural tools, a zinc-rich primer followed by two coats of enamel or epoxy gives good corrosion resistance. In coastal or humid regions, hot-dip galvanising is worth the cost for high-value items. Sharp edges on hand tools should be ground and tempered correctly to avoid brittleness.

Never weld without a proper welding helmet with at least a shade 10 lens for arc welding. "Arc eye" (photokeratitis) is extremely painful and can cause permanent damage. Always weld in a well-ventilated area or use a fume extractor; galvanised steel releases toxic zinc fumesâgrind it off or weld outdoors with a respirator. In African workshops, ensure your generator is earthed and cables are undamaged. Keep a dry powder fire extinguisher within reach and never weld near fuel, thinners, or dry crop residues. Wear leather gloves, apron, and closed shoes. Gas cylinders must be secured upright and flashback arrestors fitted.
