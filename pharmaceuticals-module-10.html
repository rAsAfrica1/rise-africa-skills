Lessons

1. Cold Chain Management and Logistics

In Africa's heat, many medicines like vaccines, insulin, and some antibiotics must stay between 2C and 8C. A break in the cold chain can make them useless or dangerous. You need a reliable refrigerator, a thermometer, and a way to keep things cold during transport. In cities, use a vaccine fridge with a temperature logger. In rural areas, a solar direct-drive (SDD) fridge is best because it doesn't need batteries or fuel. For transport, use a cold box with ice packs. Pre-condition ice packs in a freezer at -20C for at least 24 hours. A standard 5-litre cold box can hold 2 litres of vaccines and stay cold for 24-48 hours if packed correctly: line the sides with ice packs, put vaccines in the middle, and fill empty spaces with bubble wrap. Always put a thermometer inside the box. If the temperature goes above 8C, mark the vaccines as 'do not use' and call your supervisor. Never freeze vaccines that shouldn't be frozen (like DPT or hepatitis B). Freezing can ruin them. Keep a temperature log: record the temperature twice a day, morning and evening. If you see a reading outside 2-8C, act immediately. For longer trips, use a refrigerated truck or a 4x4 with a 12V car fridge. In remote areas, plan your route to avoid midday heat. Use a 'shake test' for freeze-sensitive vaccines: if you suspect freezing, shake the vial and compare to a non-frozen vial. If it's cloudy, discard it. Train your staff on cold chain basics every 6 months. Remember: a broken cold chain wastes money and can kill patients. Simple tools and discipline keep medicines safe.

2. Wholesale Distribution and Inventory Control

Wholesale distribution means buying medicines in bulk from manufacturers or importers and selling them to pharmacies, clinics, and hospitals. To succeed, you need to control inventory carefully. Start with a simple system: use a stock card or a free app like 'Stock Management' on your phone. Record every item in and out. Set a reorder level for each product. For example, if you sell 100 paracetamol tablets per week, and it takes 2 weeks to get new stock, your reorder level is 200 tablets plus a safety stock of 50. So when stock hits 250, you order more. Use the FIFO method: first in, first out. Always put new stock behind old stock on the shelf. Check expiry dates every month. Remove anything expiring in 3 months and sell it at a discount or return it. Keep a 'fast movers' list: antimalarials, antibiotics, painkillers, and ORS. These should never run out. Slow movers like rare vaccines can be ordered on demand. For storage, keep medicines off the floor on pallets, away from sunlight, and in a dry room below 30C. Use a hygrometer to check humidity; keep it under 60%. Do a physical count every 3 months. Compare with your records. If there's a difference, investigate. Theft and expiry are your biggest losses. Train staff to check expiry dates when receiving goods. For distribution, use a simple route plan: deliver to nearby clinics on Mondays, far ones on Wednesdays. Keep a delivery log with signatures. If you use a motorcycle, use a lockable box. Never leave medicines in a hot car. A good wholesale business runs on accurate records and fast turnover.

3. Track and Trace Systems and Serialization

Track and trace means following a medicine from the factory to the patient. Serialization gives each pack a unique number, like a fingerprint. In Africa, many countries are adopting GS1 standards. A GS1 DataMatrix barcode is a small square code that holds the product code, batch number, expiry date, and a serial number. You can scan it with a smartphone app like 'GS1 Barcode Scanner' or a handheld scanner. Why does this matter? It stops counterfeiters and helps recalls. If a batch is bad, you can find exactly where it went. To start, ask your supplier for serialized products. When you receive a box, scan the barcode. Your system should record the serial numbers. When you sell to a pharmacy, scan again. This creates a chain. In Kenya, the Pharmacy and Poisons Board uses a system called 'PPB Track and Trace'. In Nigeria, NAFDAC has a similar scheme. For a small business, you don't need a big system. Use a spreadsheet: columns for product name, batch, serial numbers, date received, date sold, and buyer. Keep it on a computer or phone. For high-value items like antiretrovirals, scan every unit. For low-value items, scan the outer box. If you find a product without a serial number, treat it as suspicious. Report it to your national drug authority. Train your staff to scan and verify. A simple rule: no scan, no sale. This protects your reputation and your customers. In the future, blockchain and mobile apps will make this easier, but today, a disciplined paper or spreadsheet system is enough.

4. Counterfeit Drug Prevention

Counterfeit drugs are fake medicines that may contain no active ingredient, wrong ingredients, or even poison. In Africa, the WHO estimates 1 in 10 medical products is substandard or falsified. You can protect yourself and your customers. First, buy only from licensed suppliers. Ask for a certificate of analysis and a wholesale license. Check the packaging: misspellings, poor printing, and wrong fonts are red flags. Compare with a known genuine sample. Look at the batch number and expiry datethey should be printed clearly, not stamped over. Use a simple test: for tablets, check if they dissolve in water within 30 minutes. For syrups, check the smell and colour. But these are not foolproof. Better tools: a 'GPHF Minilab' is a small kit that tests for common drugs like paracetamol, artemether, and antibiotics. It costs about $500 and can be used in a pharmacy. You can also use a 'TruScan' handheld device that uses light to check if the medicine is genuine. In Nigeria, NAFDAC has a 'Green Page' to verify products. In Kenya, use the PPB's online portal. Train your staff to check every delivery. If you suspect a fake, quarantine it, document everything, and report to the national authority. Never sell it. Warn your customers: if a drug doesn't work, it might be fake. Encourage them to buy from you, not from street vendors. Keep a list of known counterfeit products from your regulator. Remember: your reputation is your business. One fake drug can destroy it.

5. Global Sourcing and Procurement

Global sourcing means buying medicines from manufacturers in other countries. This can save money, but it comes with risks. Start by identifying reliable suppliers. Use platforms like 'Dun & Bradstreet' to check a company's credit and history. Or ask other pharmacists in your network. For essential medicines, the WHO prequalification list is a good starting point. It lists manufacturers whose products meet quality standards. You can also buy from UNICEF's Supply Division or the Global Fund's wambo.org. When you find a supplier, request a proforma invoice. It should include: product name, strength, batch number, expiry date, quantity, unit price, and Incoterms (like FOB or CIF). Incoterms define who pays for shipping and insurance. For small orders, use air freight; for large orders, sea freight is cheaper but slower. Always use a letter of credit (LC) for large payments. An LC is a bank guarantee that you pay only when the goods are shipped. For small orders, use a wire transfer but only after checking references. Watch out for hidden costs: customs duties, clearing fees, and storage at the port. In Africa, clearing can take weeks. Hire a customs agent. Keep all documents: bill of lading, packing list, certificate of analysis, and import permit. In Kenya, you need a permit from the Pharmacy and Poisons Board. In Nigeria, from NAFDAC. Never import without a license. Also, consider local sourcing firstit's faster and supports your economy. But for specialized drugs, global sourcing is necessary. Build relationships with 2-3 suppliers to avoid shortages. Always inspect goods on arrival. If something is wrong, document it and claim insurance.

6. Reverse Logistics and Returns

Reverse logistics is the process of moving goods back from the customer to the seller or manufacturer. In pharmaceuticals, returns happen for many reasons: expired stock, damaged goods, wrong items, or recalls. A good returns system protects your money and your reputation. Start with a clear policy: no returns without a receipt and original packaging. For expired goods, many suppliers offer credit notes if you return them before the expiry date. For example, if you return 6 months before expiry, you might get 80% credit; 3 months before, 50%; after expiry, nothing. So track expiry dates monthly. Use a 'returns log' with columns: date, product, batch, quantity, reason, and action. For damaged goods, take photos and file a claim with your supplier or insurer. For recalls, act immediately: contact all customers who bought the batch, quarantine remaining stock, and return to the supplier. In Africa, national regulators like NAFDAC or PPB issue recall notices. You must comply. For unsold goods that are still good, you can donate to clinics or NGOs, but get a receipt for tax purposes. For disposal of expired drugs, never throw them in the bin or flush them. Use a licensed waste disposal company. They incinerate at high temperature. In Kenya, the National Environment Management Authority (NEMA) licenses such companies. For small quantities, you can use a 'drug destruction kit' that neutralizes the drug. Keep records of disposal for 5 years. Reverse logistics also includes returning reusable cold boxes and ice packs. Train your staff to handle returns quickly. A smooth returns process builds trust with suppliers and customers.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Pharmaceutical Supply Chain & Distribution in Africa

This practical reference distils the operational essentials of moving medicines from manufacturer to patient across African markets. It is written for founders, general managers, and supply chain leads running or scaling pharmaceutical distribution businesses where margins are thin, cold chain failures are expensive, and counterfeit risk is a daily commercial threat. Use it as a working document: the tables, checklist, and diagram are designed to be printed, pinned, and acted on.

1. Cold Chain Management and Logistics

Cold chain is not a support function it is a product integrity guarantee and a commercial liability. In most African markets, the dominant cold chain products are vaccines, insulin, biologics, and certain antiretrovirals. The WHO recommends the "+2C to +8C" range for most vaccines, with some products requiring -20C or -70C (e.g., certain COVID-19 vaccines).

Key operational controls: validated cold rooms and refrigerators with continuous temperature monitoring (data loggers with alarm thresholds), qualified packing configurations for cold boxes, and documented excursion management. Power instability is the single biggest risk budget for solar direct-drive refrigerators, backup generators, and voltage stabilizers. For last-mile delivery, use conditioned vaccine carriers with conditioned ice packs, and never allow direct contact between ice packs and product.

2. Wholesale Distribution and Inventory Control

Wholesale distribution in Africa is fragmented. A distributor typically serves pharmacies, clinics, hospitals, and informal medicine outlets. Inventory control must balance service level against working capital pharmaceuticals are expensive to hold and expire.

Adopt FEFO (First Expired, First Out) rather than FIFO. Track batch numbers and expiry dates at SKU level. Use ABC-XYZ classification: A items are high-value/high-volume (e.g., antimalarials, antibiotics), Z items are erratic demand (e.g., rare biologics). Set reorder points based on lead time variability, not just average lead time port clearance in Mombasa, Lagos, or Durban can add 26 weeks of uncertainty.

3. Track and Trace Systems and Serialization

Serialization assigns a unique identifier to each saleable unit. In Africa, the regulatory landscape is maturing: Nigeria's NAFDAC introduced a traceability framework; Kenya's Pharmacy and Poisons Board is implementing a track-and-trace system; and the Africa Medicines Agency (AMA) is pushing harmonization. GS1 standards (GTIN + serial number + batch + expiry) are the global baseline.

Business case: serialization reduces counterfeit infiltration, enables precise recalls, and supports donor compliance (e.g., Global Fund, USAID, UNICEF require traceability). Cost: aggregation and data carrier printing add 13% to unit cost, but recall and fraud savings are typically higher. Start with high-risk, high-value SKUs.

4. Counterfeit Drug Prevention

The WHO estimates that 1 in 10 medical products in low- and middle-income countries is substandard or falsified. In Africa, the economic cost is estimated at USD 23 billion annually. Prevention is layered: verified sourcing, serialization, tamper-evident packaging, and field verification tools (e.g., SMS short codes, mobile scanning apps).

SAFETY WARNING Counterfeit and Substandard Medicines

Never source pharmaceuticals from unverified suppliers, informal markets, or online vendors without regulatory authorization. Counterfeit medicines can contain no active ingredient, wrong dosage, or toxic substances they cause treatment failure, antimicrobial resistance, and death. Always verify batch numbers, expiry dates, and serial numbers against the manufacturer or regulator. Report suspected counterfeits to NAFDAC (Nigeria), PPB (Kenya), SAHPRA (South Africa), or your national medicines regulator immediately. Keep a documented chain of custody for every batch.

5. Global Sourcing and Procurement

African pharmaceutical distributors source from India, China, Europe, and increasingly from local manufacturers (e.g., Aspen in South Africa, Fidson in Nigeria, Universal Corporation in Kenya). Procurement strategy must balance price, lead time, quality, and regulatory approval.

Key procurement levers: framework agreements with pre-qualified suppliers (WHO PQ, Global Fund QA), volume consolidation across regions, and Incoterms selection (CIF vs. FOB vs. DDP) that matches your risk capacity. Always verify that the product is registered in your market an unregistered product, even if genuine, is illegal to distribute.

6. Reverse Logistics and Returns

Reverse logistics in pharmaceuticals covers expired stock, damaged goods, temperature excursions, recalls, and customer returns. It is often neglected but is a major cost and compliance risk. A documented returns policy must specify: what can be returned (usually only unopened, in-date, with verified chain of custody), what cannot (temperature-excursion products, opened units), and how returns are destroyed or credited.

For recalls, you need batch-level traceability to identify affected units within hours, not days. Build a recall simulation into your annual QA calendar. Partner with licensed waste disposal contractors for safe destruction never allow returned medicines to re-enter the market through informal channels.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Temperature loggers on every cold shipment download and review data within 24 hours of arrival.
  2. FEFO enforced in the warehouse batch and expiry tracked at SKU level in your WMS.
  3. Supplier verification file WHO PQ, Global Fund QA, or national regulator approval on record for every supplier.
  4. Serialization on high-risk SKUs GTIN + serial + batch + expiry printed and scanned at every handover.
  5. Customs pre-clearance pack ready invoice, packing list, certificate of analysis, registration certificate, import permit.
  6. Excursion SOP posted quarantine, assess, document, and decide (release/reject) within defined timelines.
  7. Recall simulation run annually target: identify and isolate affected batch within 4 hours.
  8. Reverse logistics register every return logged, dispositioned, and destroyed or credited with documentation.
