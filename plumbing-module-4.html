1. Module 4 Lesson 1: Introduction to Drainage and Wastewater Systems in African Buildings

Welcome to Module 4, Lesson 1. In this lesson we focus on drainage and wastewater systems, which are the backbone of any plumbing installation in African residential, commercial, and institutional buildings. Without proper drainage, even the best water supply system becomes useless. You will learn the principles of gravity drainage, the components of a drainage system, and how to design and install systems that work reliably in African conditions. First, understand the basic principle: wastewater flows downhill by gravity. Every drain pipe must have a continuous fall toward the point of disposal, whether that is a septic tank, a soakaway, a municipal sewer, or a biogas digester. The standard fall for small diameter pipes (50mm to 110mm) is between 1 in 40 and 1 in 60. For larger pipes (160mm and above), a fall of 1 in 100 to 1 in 150 is sufficient. In Africa, many buildings are single storey or low rise, so gravity drainage is almost always possible. However, in hilly areas or where the sewer is higher than the building, you may need a pumping station or a lifting station. That is a more advanced topic we will cover later. The main components of a drainage system are: soil pipes (which carry human waste from toilets), waste pipes (which carry greywater from sinks, showers, and baths), vent pipes (which allow air into the system to prevent vacuum and trap seal loss), traps (which hold a water seal to block sewer gases), inspection chambers (also called manholes or rodding eyes), and the final disposal point. In many African cities, the municipal sewer network is unreliable or nonexistent. Therefore, most buildings rely on on-site disposal systems such as septic tanks, soakaways, or biodigesters. You must know how to size and install these correctly. A septic tank is a watertight chamber that separates solids from liquids. The solids settle at the bottom as sludge, while the liquid (effluent) flows out to a soakaway or drain field. The tank must be sized based on the number of users and the daily wastewater flow. A common rule of thumb in Africa is 200 litres per person per day for domestic use. For a family of six, that is 1200 litres per day. The septic tank should have a retention time of at least 24 hours, so the minimum volume is 1200 litres plus space for sludge accumulation. A practical size for a family of six is 2.5 to 3 cubic metres. The tank should be divided into two compartments: the first compartment takes two thirds of the volume, and the second compartment takes one third. This improves settling. The inlet and outlet should have T-pipes or baffles to prevent scum and sludge from escaping. The tank must be watertight to prevent groundwater contamination. In areas with high water tables, you may need to anchor the tank to prevent it from floating when empty. Soakaways are pits filled with stones or rubble where effluent seeps into the ground. They work well in sandy or loamy soils but not in clay or waterlogged areas. The size of the soakaway depends on the soil percolation rate. A simple test: dig a hole 300mm square and 300mm deep, fill it with water, and measure how long it takes to drain. If it drains in less than 30 minutes, the soil is good. If it takes more than 2 hours, the soil is poor and you need a different solution. In clay soils, consider a raised drain field or a mound system. In rocky areas, you may need to use a biodigester or a cesspool that is emptied regularly. Vent pipes are critical. Every building must have at least one main vent pipe that extends above the roof. This prevents trap seals from being siphoned out when water flows. Without proper venting, you will get bad smells and slow drainage. The vent pipe should be at least 50mm diameter for small buildings and 75mm or 100mm for larger ones. It should terminate at least 300mm above the roof and away from windows. Traps come in many shapes: P-traps, S-traps, bottle traps, and drum traps. In Africa, the P-trap is most common because it is compact and easy to clean. Every fixture must have a trap, except toilets which have built-in traps. The trap seal should be between 50mm and 75mm deep. If the seal is too shallow, it evaporates quickly in hot climates. If it is too deep, it slows drainage. Inspection chambers are access points for cleaning blockages. They should be placed at every change of direction, at every junction, and at intervals of not more than 15 metres on straight runs. In small buildings, you can use rodding eyes instead of full manholes. Rodding eyes are small access caps that allow you to push a rod through the pipe. They are cheaper and easier to install. Now, let us talk about materials. In Africa, the most common drainage pipes are uPVC (unplasticized polyvinyl chloride) and HDPE (high-density polyethylene). uPVC is rigid, cheap, and easy to join with solvent cement. HDPE is flexible, resistant to chemicals, and good for underground applications where ground movement is expected. Cast iron is still used in some older buildings but is expensive and heavy. Asbestos cement pipes are dangerous and should be avoided. When installing underground pipes, always bed them on a layer of sand or fine gravel to protect them from rocks. The trench should be wide enough for you to work comfortably. The pipe should be laid with the socket facing uphill so that water flows smoothly. After laying, test the system by filling it with water and checking for leaks. For above-ground pipes, use brackets to support them every 1.2 metres for vertical pipes and every 1 metre for horizontal pipes. Slope horizontal pipes correctly. Never install a horizontal pipe with a fall of less than 1 in 100. In hot African climates, uPVC pipes expand and contract. Leave a small gap at each joint or use expansion joints on long runs. Also, protect pipes from sunlight because UV radiation makes uPVC brittle. Paint them or cover them with a UV-resistant wrap. Finally, consider the business aspect. As a plumber, you can earn good money by specializing in drainage systems. Many homeowners and landlords do not understand drainage, so they rely on you. Offer a complete service: design, installation, and maintenance. Charge for your expertise, not just your labour. A well-designed drainage system prevents costly repairs and health hazards. In the next lesson, we will look at how to size and install septic tanks and soakaways in detail. Before you move on, complete the practical exercise: draw a drainage layout for a three-bedroom house with two bathrooms, a kitchen, and a laundry. Show the soil pipes, waste pipes, vent pipe, traps, inspection chambers, and the connection to a septic tank and soakaway. Calculate the fall for each pipe run. This will prepare you for the next lesson.

2. Module 4 Lesson 2: Designing and Installing Septic Tanks, Soakaways, and Biodigesters

In Lesson 2 we dive deep into on-site wastewater disposal systems. This is one of the most important skills for a plumber working in Africa because municipal sewer connections are often unavailable, unreliable, or too expensive. You will learn how to design, size, and install septic tanks, soakaways, and biodigesters. We will also cover maintenance and troubleshooting. Let us start with septic tanks. A septic tank is a settling chamber that separates solids from liquids. The solids form a sludge layer at the bottom, and the lighter materials form a scum layer at the top. The middle layer is relatively clear effluent that flows out to a soakaway or drain field. The tank must be watertight and made of durable materials such as concrete, fibreglass, or plastic. In Africa, concrete is the most common because it is cheap and locally available. However, concrete tanks can crack if not reinforced properly. Fibreglass and plastic tanks are lighter and easier to install but more expensive. The size of the septic tank depends on the number of users and the daily flow. Use the following formula: Total volume = (Number of users x Daily flow per user x Retention time) + Sludge storage volume. For domestic use, assume 150 to 200 litres per person per day. Retention time should be at least 24 hours. Sludge storage should be for at least 2 years. A practical example: a family of eight. Daily flow = 8 x 150 = 1200 litres. Retention volume = 1200 litres. Sludge volume = 1200 x 2 = 2400 litres. Total = 3600 litres or 3.6 cubic metres. Add 20 percent for safety, so 4.3 cubic metres. A tank of 4.5 cubic metres is suitable. The tank should be divided into two compartments. The first compartment should be twice the size of the second. So for 4.5 cubic metres, first compartment = 3.0 cubic metres, second = 1.5 cubic metres. The inlet should be at least 100mm above the water level. The outlet should be at least 50mm below the water level. Use T-pipes or baffles at both inlet and outlet to prevent scum and sludge from escaping. The tank should have a manhole cover for inspection and emptying. The cover should be airtight to prevent odours and mosquito breeding. Now, soakaways. A soakaway is a pit that allows effluent to seep into the ground. It can be a simple pit filled with stones, or a more engineered structure with perforated pipes. The size depends on the soil type and the volume of effluent. Test the soil percolation rate first. Dig a hole 300mm square and 300mm deep. Fill with water and let it drain. Then refill and measure the time it takes to drop by 25mm. If it takes less than 5 minutes, the soil is very permeable. If it takes 5 to 30 minutes, it is moderate. If it takes more than 30 minutes, it is poor. For a family of eight producing 1200 litres per day, a soakaway in moderate soil should have a surface area of at least 10 square metres. That means a pit 3 metres by 3 metres and 2 metres deep. Fill the pit with 50mm to 100mm stones or broken bricks. Cover with a geotextile fabric to prevent soil clogging. Then backfill with soil. The effluent should enter the soakaway through a distribution box or a perforated pipe. Never connect a soakaway directly to a septic tank outlet without a distribution box, because the flow will be uneven. In clay soils, soakaways do not work. You need an alternative. Options include: a raised drain field (also called a mound system), a biodigester, or a cesspool that is emptied regularly. A raised drain field is a bed of sand and gravel built above the ground. Effluent is pumped or gravity-fed to the bed and seeps through the sand into the ground. It requires a pump and a larger area. A biodigester is a sealed tank that uses anaerobic bacteria to break down waste. It produces biogas (mainly methane) which can be used for cooking or lighting. Biodigesters are becoming popular in Africa because they reduce waste and produce energy. There are two main types: fixed dome and floating drum. The fixed dome is cheaper and more common. It consists of an underground concrete dome with an inlet and outlet. The gas collects at the top and is piped to a stove. The effluent that comes out is a nutrient-rich liquid fertilizer. You can use it on crops. A biodigester needs a constant temperature of 20 to 35 degrees Celsius. In cold areas, you may need to insulate it. It also needs water. The ideal ratio is one part waste to one part water. Do not use bleach or antibiotics because they kill the bacteria. Now, installation steps for a septic tank. Step 1: Excavate the hole. The hole should be at least 300mm larger than the tank on all sides. Step 2: Prepare the base. Compact the soil and add a 100mm layer of concrete (1:3:6 mix). Step 3: Build the tank. If using concrete, build the walls and floor with reinforced concrete. Use a 1:2:4 mix. Make sure the walls are at least 150mm thick. Step 4: Install the inlet and outlet pipes. Use 110mm uPVC pipes. The inlet should have a T-pipe inside the tank. The outlet should have a T-pipe as well. Step 5: Install the partition wall. Leave a gap at the bottom for sludge movement and a gap at the top for scum. Step 6: Cast the cover slab. Use reinforced concrete with a manhole opening. Step 7: Waterproof the tank. Use a cement-based waterproof coating or a plastic liner. Step 8: Backfill carefully. Do not damage the tank. Step 9: Connect the house drainage to the inlet. Step 10: Connect the outlet to the soakaway or drain field. Step 11: Test the system. Fill the tank with water and check for leaks. Maintenance is critical. Septic tanks need to be emptied every 2 to 5 years depending on usage. Do not wait until the tank is full. If the sludge level reaches more than one third of the tank depth, empty it. Use a vacuum truck or a manual pump. Never enter a septic tank because it contains toxic gases. Soakaways need to be inspected every year. If the ground is soggy or the drains are slow, the soakaway may be clogged. You can try to restore it by adding enzymes or by digging a new soakaway. Biodigesters need to be fed daily with a consistent mix. Check the gas pressure regularly. If the gas pressure drops, there may be a leak or the bacteria may be upset. Add a little cow dung or sewage sludge to restart the process. Business tip: Many African households and institutions need septic tanks and biodigesters. You can specialize in this area and charge a premium. Offer a package that includes design, installation, and a maintenance contract. A maintenance contract gives you recurring income. Also, consider building pre-cast concrete tanks or plastic tanks that you can sell. This is a scalable business. In the next lesson, we will cover venting, traps, and how to prevent smells and blockages. Before you move on, complete this exercise: design a septic tank and soakaway for a small guesthouse with 20 guests. Assume 150 litres per guest per day. Calculate the tank volume, the soakaway size, and draw a simple layout. This will test your understanding.

3. Module 4 Lesson 3: Venting, Traps, and Preventing Blockages and Smells

In Lesson 3 we focus on the details that make a drainage system work well: venting, traps, and blockage prevention. These are the areas where most plumbing problems occur in African buildings. A system that is not vented properly will have slow drains, gurgling sounds, and bad smells. A system with wrong traps will lose its water seal and allow sewer gases into the building. A system that is not protected against blockages will require frequent and expensive repairs. Let us start with venting. The purpose of a vent pipe is to allow air into the drainage system so that water can flow freely. Without air, water flowing down a pipe creates a vacuum behind it. This vacuum can suck the water out of traps, allowing sewer gases to enter the building. It can also slow down the flow. Every building must have at least one main vent pipe, also called a soil and vent pipe. This pipe connects to the highest point of the drainage system and extends above the roof. The vent pipe should be the same diameter as the soil pipe or at least 50mm for small buildings. For a typical African house, a 75mm or 100mm vent pipe is common. The vent pipe should terminate at least 300mm above the roof and at least 3 metres away from any window or air intake. If this is not possible, use an air admittance valve (AAV) instead. An AAV is a one-way valve that allows air into the system but does not let sewer gases out. AAVs are cheaper and easier to install than a full vent pipe, but they are not a complete substitute. They can fail over time. Use them only where a full vent pipe is not practical. There are two types of venting: wet venting and dry venting. In wet venting, the vent pipe also carries wastewater. This is common in small buildings. In dry venting, the vent pipe carries only air. Dry venting is better because it is less likely to clog. If you use wet venting, make sure the vent pipe is sized correctly and has a continuous fall. Now, traps. A trap is a fitting that holds a water seal to block sewer gases. Every fixture must have a trap, except toilets which have built-in traps. The most common trap in Africa is the P-trap. It has a U-shaped bend that holds water. The depth of the water seal should be between 50mm and 75mm. If the seal is too shallow, it will evaporate quickly in hot weather. If it is too deep, it will slow drainage. In hot African climates, you may need to add a small amount of oil to the trap to slow evaporation, or use a trap with a deeper seal. S-traps are similar to P-traps but they have a horizontal outlet. They are prone to self-siphoning, which means the water seal can be sucked out. Avoid S-traps if possible. Bottle traps are compact and easy to clean. They are common under sinks. Drum traps are used in some older buildings. They have a large water seal but are difficult to clean. When installing traps, make sure they are accessible for cleaning. Do not bury them in walls or floors. Use a trap with a cleanout plug. Also, make sure the trap is not installed backwards. The flow should go from the fixture into the trap, then down to the drain. Now, blockages. The most common causes of blockages in African plumbing systems are: grease and food from kitchen sinks, hair and soap from bathrooms, sanitary items from toilets, and tree roots in underground pipes. To prevent blockages, follow these rules. First, never pour grease or oil down the sink. Collect it in a container and dispose of it in the rubbish. Second, install a strainer in every sink and shower drain to catch hair and food particles. Third, never flush sanitary items, nappies, or wipes down the toilet. Even if the packet says flushable, do not flush them. They do not break down and they cause major blockages. Fourth, plant trees away from drainage lines. Tree roots are attracted to water and can crack pipes. If you must plant trees, use a root barrier. Fifth, install inspection chambers at every change of direction and at every junction. This makes it easy to rod the drain if it blocks. Sixth, use the correct fall. If the fall is too flat, solids will settle. If the fall is too steep, the water will flow away too fast and leave solids behind. The ideal fall for a 110mm pipe is 1 in 40 to 1 in 60. For a 50mm pipe, use 1 in 30 to 1 in 40. Now, how to clear a blockage. First, try a plunger. A plunger works well for toilets and sinks. Make sure there is enough water in the bowl to cover the plunger cup. Push and pull vigorously. Second, use a drain snake or auger. This is a flexible cable that you feed into the drain. Turn it clockwise to break up the blockage. Third, use a high-pressure water jetter. This is a professional tool that blasts water at high pressure. It is very effective for grease and roots. Fourth, use chemicals. Be careful with chemicals because they can damage pipes and are dangerous to handle. Use them only as a last resort. Never mix different chemicals. Now, let us talk about smells. Bad smells in a building usually come from a lost trap seal, a blocked vent, or a broken pipe. To find the source, check each trap. If a trap is dry, refill it with water. If a trap is leaking, replace it. If the vent is blocked, clear it. You can also use a smoke test. A smoke test involves blowing smoke into the drainage system and seeing where it comes out. This is a professional technique. In Africa, many buildings have open drains or poorly sealed inspection chambers. These can also cause smells. Make sure all inspection chambers have airtight covers. If you have a septic tank, check the inlet and outlet T-pipes. If they are missing or damaged, smells will escape. Also, check the vent pipe on the septic tank. Some tanks have a vent pipe that is separate from the house vent. Make sure it is not blocked. Business tip: Blockage clearing and smell diagnosis are high-demand services. Many homeowners do not know how to fix these problems. You can charge a call-out fee plus labour. Offer a preventive maintenance service where you visit every six months to check traps, vents, and inspection chambers. This gives you steady income and prevents emergencies. In the next lesson, we will cover the installation of underground drainage pipes, including trenching, bedding, and testing. Before you move on, complete this exercise: inspect a building and identify all the traps, vents, and inspection chambers. Check if any traps are dry or leaking. Check if the vent pipe is clear. Write a short report with your findings and recommendations. This will prepare you for the next lesson.

4. Module 4 Lesson 4: Underground Drainage Installation, Trenching, Bedding, and Testing

In Lesson 4 we cover the practical steps for installing underground drainage pipes. This is heavy work but it is also where you can earn good money because many plumbers avoid it. Underground drainage requires careful planning, correct materials, and proper testing. In Africa, the soil conditions vary widely, from sandy coastal areas to rocky highlands to clay valleys. You must adapt your methods to the local conditions. Let us start with planning. Before you dig, you need to know the layout. Draw a plan showing the position of the building, the drainage pipes, the inspection chambers, the septic tank, and the soakaway. Mark the depth of each pipe. The minimum depth for underground drainage is 300mm for small pipes and 450mm for pipes under driveways or areas with heavy traffic. In cold areas, you may need to go deeper to prevent freezing, but in most of Africa, freezing is not a concern. However, in hot areas, you need to protect pipes from heat and UV radiation if they are above ground. For underground pipes, use uPVC or HDPE. uPVC is rigid and strong. HDPE is flexible and resistant to ground movement. In areas with expansive clay soils, HDPE is better because it can flex without cracking. In rocky areas, use uPVC with a protective layer of sand. Now, trenching. Dig the trench to the required depth and width. The width should be at least 300mm wider than the pipe diameter. For a 110mm pipe, the trench should be at least 400mm wide. This gives you room to work. The bottom of the trench should be smooth and free of rocks. If the soil is soft, you may need to compact it. If the soil is rocky, remove the rocks and add a 100mm layer of sand or fine gravel. This is called bedding. Bedding protects the pipe from point loads and allows it to settle evenly. For HDPE pipes, you can use a sand bedding of 100mm below and 150mm above the pipe. For uPVC pipes, use 100mm below and 100mm above. After bedding, lay the pipe. Start from the lowest point and work uphill. This ensures that water flows in the right direction. The socket should face uphill. Apply solvent cement to the joint and push the pipe into the socket with a quarter turn. Hold for 30 seconds. For HDPE pipes, use butt fusion or electrofusion welding. This requires special equipment. If you do not have it, use compression fittings. Now, the fall. Use a spirit level and a string line to check the fall. The fall should be continuous and even. Do not create dips or high points. A dip will collect water and solids, causing a blockage. A high point will create a air lock. For a 110mm pipe, a fall of 1 in 60 means that for every 60 metres of pipe, the pipe drops 1 metre. That is about 17mm per metre. For a 50mm pipe, a fall of 1 in 40 means 25mm per metre. Use a laser level for accuracy. After laying the pipe, install inspection chambers. An inspection chamber is a box or a manhole that allows access to the pipe. It should be at every change of direction, at every junction, and at intervals of not more than 15 metres on straight runs. In small buildings, you can use a rodding eye instead of a full chamber. A rodding eye is a small cap that you can remove to push a rod through. It is cheaper and easier to install. For larger buildings, use a full inspection chamber with a cover. The chamber should be at least 450mm x 450mm for a 110mm pipe. The cover should be airtight and strong enough to walk on. Now, testing. After installation, you must test the system before backfilling. There are two tests: the water test and the air test. The water test is the most common. Plug the lowest end of the pipe and fill the system with water. Let it stand for 30 minutes. Check for leaks. If the water level drops, there is a leak. Mark the leak and repair it. Then refill and test again. The air test is used in dry areas where water is scarce. Plug both ends and pump air into the pipe until the pressure reaches 100mm of water gauge. Wait for 5 minutes. If the pressure drops, there is a leak. Repair and retest. After testing, backfill the trench. Use fine soil or sand for the first 300mm above the pipe. Do not use large rocks or rubble. Compact the soil in layers of 150mm. Do not use a mechanical compactor directly over the pipe. Use a hand tamper. If the pipe is under a driveway, add a concrete slab over the pipe for protection. Now, special considerations for Africa. In many African cities, the water table is high during the rainy season. If you dig a trench and it fills with water, you need to dewater it. Use a pump or a bucket. Do not lay pipes in water. Also, in areas with black cotton soil, the soil expands when wet and shrinks when dry. This can crack pipes. Use HDPE pipes or add a layer of sand around the pipe to allow movement. In areas with termites, use termite-resistant materials or treat the soil. Termites can eat through some plastic pipes. In areas with high traffic, use cast iron or concrete pipes for the top section. Business tip: Underground drainage installation is a specialized skill. You can charge more than general plumbing. Offer a complete service that includes excavation, pipe laying, testing, and backfilling. You can also hire a small excavator to speed up the work. In many African cities, manual labour is cheap, but an excavator can do the work of 20 men. If you can afford it, buy a used excavator. It will pay for itself in a few jobs. Also, consider offering a warranty on your work. A 5-year warranty on underground pipes gives customers confidence. In the next lesson, we will cover the maintenance and repair of drainage systems, including how to handle emergencies. Before you move on, complete this exercise: dig a 3-metre test trench in your area. Check the soil type, the water table, and the presence of rocks. Lay a 3-metre length of 110mm uPVC pipe with a fall of 1 in 60. Test it with water. Write a report on your findings. This will give you practical experience.

5. Module 4 Lesson 5: Drainage System Maintenance, Repair, and Business Growth

In Lesson 5 we focus on maintenance, repair, and how to grow your plumbing business through drainage services. This is the lesson that turns your technical skills into money. In Africa, there is a huge demand for reliable drainage services because many systems are poorly installed or poorly maintained. If you can offer fast, professional, and affordable service, you will never lack work. Let us start with maintenance. A drainage system needs regular maintenance to prevent blockages, leaks, and smells. You should offer a maintenance contract to your customers. A typical contract includes a visit every three to six months. During each visit, you should: inspect all traps and refill any that are dry; check all vent pipes for blockages; open inspection chambers and check for sludge or debris; check the septic tank sludge level; check the soakaway for signs of clogging; test all fixtures for slow drainage; and check for leaks in exposed pipes. Write a checklist and give a copy to the customer. This shows professionalism and helps you remember what to do. For septic tanks, the main maintenance task is emptying. The tank should be emptied every 2 to 5 years. Do not wait until it is full. If the sludge level is more than one third of the tank depth, it is time to empty. Use a vacuum truck. In many African cities, there are private vacuum truck operators. You can partner with them and earn a commission for every referral. Or you can buy your own vacuum truck if you have the capital. A small vacuum truck can cost between 20,000 and 50,000 US dollars. It is a big investment but it can generate high income. For soakaways, if the ground is soggy or the drains are slow, the soakaway may be clogged. You can try to restore it by adding a biological additive that breaks down organic matter. If that does not work, you may need to dig a new soakaway. For biodigesters, check the gas pressure and the pH. The ideal pH is between 6.8 and 7.4. If the pH is too low, add a little lime. If the gas pressure is low, check for leaks. Also, make sure the inlet is not blocked. Now, repairs. The most common repairs are: fixing leaks in pipes, replacing broken traps, clearing blockages, and repairing inspection chambers. When you go to a repair job, always carry a basic toolkit: a plunger, a drain snake, a pipe wrench, a hacksaw, solvent cement, replacement traps, and a torch. For leaks, first find the source. If the leak is in a joint, you can try tightening it or applying a sealant. If the pipe is cracked, cut out the damaged section and replace it with a new piece. Use a coupling to join the new piece. For blockages, use a plunger first. If that does not work, use a drain snake. If that does not work, use a high-pressure jetter. If you do not have a jetter, you can hire one. For broken inspection chamber covers, replace them with new ones. Make sure the new cover is airtight. Now, emergencies. Drainage emergencies include: sewage backing up into the building, a collapsed pipe, or a septic tank overflow. When you get an emergency call, respond quickly. Time is critical. First, stop the flow. If sewage is backing up, tell the customer to stop using water. Then, find the blockage and clear it. If the pipe is collapsed, you may need to dig it up and replace it. If the septic tank is overflowing, pump it out. Always wear protective clothing: gloves, boots, and a mask. Sewage contains harmful bacteria and viruses. After the job, wash your hands and tools with disinfectant. Now, business growth. Drainage services are a great way to grow your plumbing business. Here are some strategies. First, specialize. Become known as the drainage expert in your area. Use a slogan like 'No blockage too tough'. Second, offer a 24-hour emergency service. Many plumbers do not offer this. If you do, you can charge a premium. Third, build relationships with real estate agents, landlords, and property managers. They always need reliable plumbers. Give them your card and offer a discount for repeat business. Fourth, use social media. Create a WhatsApp business account and a Facebook page. Post photos of your work before and after. Ask satisfied customers to leave a review. In Africa, word of mouth is powerful. Fifth, train your staff. If you have apprentices, teach them drainage skills. This allows you to take on more jobs. Module 4 Lesson 6: Practical Reference Guide for Drainage and Wastewater Systems

This practical reference lesson consolidates the technical knowledge from Module 4 into a field-ready guide for plumbing professionals working across African urban and peri-urban settings. Whether you are installing a septic tank in Kumasi, repairing a blocked drain in Nairobi, or advising a client on biodigester maintenance in Kampala, this lesson provides the figures, standards, and business insights you need to deliver reliable work and grow your plumbing enterprise.

Drainage and wastewater management is not merely a technical service it is a public health intervention and a business opportunity. Across sub-Saharan Africa, less than 40% of the population has access to safely managed sanitation services (WHO/UNICEF JMP, 2022). This gap represents both a health crisis and a market. Plumbing professionals who master drainage systems position themselves to serve households, institutions, and commercial clients who urgently need safe, affordable solutions.

SAFETY WARNING Biological and Chemical Hazards

Wastewater contains pathogenic bacteria, viruses, protozoa, and helminths that cause cholera, typhoid, hepatitis A, and schistosomiasis. Never enter a septic tank or soakaway pit without:

  • Full personal protective equipment (PPE): waterproof gloves, boots, goggles, and N95 respirator
  • Confined space entry permit and atmospheric testing for hydrogen sulphide (HS) and methane (CH)
  • A minimum of two persons present, with one trained observer outside the tank
  • Forced ventilation for at least 30 minutes before entry

Hydrogen sulphide is lethal at concentrations above 700 ppm. Methane is explosive at 5-15% concentration in air. Septic tank gases have killed experienced plumbers. When desludging, use vacuum tankers operated by licensed contractors. Always wash hands with soap and clean water after handling wastewater equipment. Vaccinate against hepatitis A and typhoid where available.

Source: WHO Guidelines on Sanitation and Health (2018); US OSHA Confined Space Standard 29 CFR 1910.146.

Business-First Approach: Turning Drainage Expertise into Revenue

In the African context, drainage services are chronically undersupplied. A plumber who can design, install, and maintain complete drainage systems not just fix leaks commands premium rates and recurring income. Here is how to structure your business around Module 4 competencies:

1. Package services for institutions. Schools, clinics, churches, and small hotels need septic tank installation, desludging coordination, and drainage maintenance. Offer annual maintenance contracts (AMCs) that include quarterly inspections, drain cleaning, and priority emergency response. A single school contract can generate 3-5 times the revenue of ad-hoc residential work.

2. Sell the health benefit. When quoting for a household septic system, explain that proper drainage prevents cholera, typhoid, and child diarrhoea. Parents will pay for safety. Use the WHO data: improved sanitation reduces diarrhoeal disease by 32% (WHO, 2022). Frame your work as health infrastructure, not just plumbing.

3. Become the biodigester specialist. Biodigesters produce biogas for cooking, saving households 30-50% on charcoal or LPG costs. In Kenya, Uganda, and Tanzania, demand for biodigesters is growing rapidly. Training in biodigester installation (e.g., through SNV or GIZ programmes) can add a high-margin service line. A single 6m biodigester installation can earn KES 80,000-150,000 (USD 600-1,100) in labour and supervision fees.

4. Offer drain cleaning as a subscription. Monthly or quarterly drain cleaning for restaurants, butcheries, and food processing businesses prevents blockages and odour complaints. Charge a flat monthly fee. This creates predictable cash flow and positions you as a trusted service provider.

5. Document your work with photos and reports. For commercial clients, provide before-and-after photos, a simple inspection report, and recommendations. This professionalism justifies higher rates and differentiates you from informal labourers.

Quick Reference Checklist: Drainage System Installation and Maintenance

  1. Site assessment: Confirm water table depth (minimum 1.5m below soakaway base), soil percolation rate, and distance from water sources (minimum 30m from wells, 15m from buildings).
  2. Permits and approvals: Obtain local authority approval for septic tank installation. In Kenya, this is the County Public Health Department; in Ghana, the Environmental Health and Sanitation Directorate.
  3. Materials check: Use uPVC pipes (BS EN 1401 or equivalent), rubber ring joints, and solvent cement appropriate for the pipe material. Never use thin-wall pipes underground.
  4. Trench preparation: Trench width = pipe diameter + 300mm. Bedding = 100mm of sand or fine gravel, free of sharp stones. Backfill in 150mm layers, compacting each layer.
  5. Gradient verification: Use a spirit level and string line. Check gradient every 3m. Minimum 1 in 60 for 110mm pipes. Too steep causes liquid to outrun solids; too flat causes blockages.
  6. Testing: Water test each section for 30 minutes before backfilling. Air test at 38mm water gauge for 3 minutes for soil stacks. Record results.
  7. Ventilation: Every soil stack must terminate above roof level with a cowl or durgo valve. This prevents trap siphonage and odour entry.
  8. Handover and documentation: Provide the client with a simple diagram of the system, maintenance schedule, and your contact details. Schedule a 6-month follow-up inspection.
