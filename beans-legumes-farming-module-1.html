Lesson 1

Why Every Cereal Farmer Needs a Legume

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Explain why a cereal-only rotation fails
  • Understand what legumes contribute beyond their own crop value
  • See the legume as both a crop and a soil input

A farm that grows only cereals — maize, sorghum, millet, rice — mines the soil. Every harvest removes nitrogen, phosphorus and organic matter. Yields fall. Farmers compensate by buying more fertiliser, which costs more each year, and the cycle continues until the land is exhausted or the farmer is broke.

Legumes break this cycle. They:

  • Fix nitrogen from the air (via root bacteria) — the only non-purchased nitrogen source in agriculture
  • Return nitrogen to the soil through roots, leaf drop and crop residue
  • Break pest and disease cycles of cereals
  • Break Striga's life cycle in sorghum and millet fields
  • Provide a second income stream — often at premium prices
  • Provide household protein — meat is expensive, beans are cheap

The nitrogen fixation is the headline. A good bean crop fixes 40–80 kg of nitrogen per hectare, available to the following cereal. That is worth $40–100 of purchased urea, produced by the plant itself.

Lesson 2

The Six Legumes and What Each Is For

~11 min
In this lesson
  • Distinguish the six main legume crops by use, yield and market
  • Match a legume to your rainfall and soil
  • Choose the one that fits your rotation best

Common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris)

The dominant food legume in East and southern Africa. Two growing habits — bush and climbing. Cycle 75–100 days. Yield 0.8–2 t/ha (bush), 2–4 t/ha (climbing). Sensitive to waterlogging and drought. Prefers well-drained, fertile soils. High market demand in urban areas.

Cowpea (Vigna unguiculata)

The most drought-tolerant of the six. Grown across the Sahel and southern Africa. Cycle 60–90 days. Yield 0.5–1.5 t/ha. Dual purpose — the grain for food, the haulm for livestock feed. Essential rotation partner in dry zones.

Groundnut (Arachis hypogaea)

High-value oil and food crop. Cycle 90–150 days. Yield 0.8–2 t/ha. Requires calcium and well-drained, sandy soil. Susceptible to aflatoxin if not dried properly. Strong market — oil, peanut butter, and export.

Soybean (Glycine max)

The highest nitrogen fixer of the African legumes. High in protein (35–40%). Cycle 90–130 days. Yield 1.5–2.5 t/ha. Strong demand from feed mills, soya processors, and export. Requires inoculation for best results.

Pigeon pea (Cajanus cajan)

Perennial legume. Cycle 150–280 days. Yield 0.8–1.5 t/ha. Drought-tolerant, deep-rooted, and can be intercropped with cereals. Fixes nitrogen and provides a second harvest. Popular in East Africa and India (where it is called tur).

Green gram / mung bean (Vigna radiata)

Short-cycle legume. Cycle 60–75 days. Yield 0.5–1 t/ha. Fast rotation crop, good in double-cropping systems. Premium price for the dried grain.

Lesson 3

Nitrogen Fixation — the Free Fertiliser

~11 min
In this lesson
  • Understand how nitrogen fixation works
  • Use rhizobium inoculant correctly
  • Value the nitrogen contribution in money terms

Legumes form a partnership with soil bacteria called rhizobia. The bacteria invade the root, form nodules (small pink or white bumps on the roots), and convert atmospheric nitrogen into a form the plant can use. The plant gives the bacteria sugar; the bacteria give the plant nitrogen. Both benefit.

Why inoculation matters

Not all soils have the right rhizobia. A soil that has grown beans for years usually does. A soil that has not, or a soil where the wrong strain is present, will not fix nitrogen efficiently. Inoculation — coating seed with the correct rhizobium strain before planting — is the single most reliable way to boost fixation.

How to inoculate

  1. Buy fresh inoculant matched to the crop (bean inoculant for beans, soybean inoculant for soybean). Different crops need different rhizobium species.
  2. Moisten seed slightly with clean water (or a sugar solution for stickiness).
  3. Mix inoculant with seed at the rate on the packet. Cover all seeds.
  4. Plant immediately. Inoculant dies on dry seed within hours.
  5. Do not mix with fertiliser, fungicide or insecticide — they kill the bacteria.
  6. Keep the inoculant cool and out of direct sun.

The value in money

A 1-hectare soybean crop that fixes 100 kg N/ha is worth about $60–120 at common urea prices. A bean crop that fixes 60 kg N/ha is worth $40–70. Over a 4-year rotation, that compounds to real money.

Lesson 4

The Three Markets — Food, Feed, Export

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Name the three main legume markets
  • Match the legume to the correct market
  • Understand the export premium

Food market

Households, urban millers, market vendors. Common beans, cowpeas, and green grams are the main food legumes. Prices vary with season and region. Beans typically sell for $0.60–1.50/kg in African markets, depending on type and season.

Feed market

Feed mills and livestock producers buy soybean meal, groundnut cake and cowpea haulm. Prices track the protein content and global oilseed market. Soybean is the dominant feed legume in Africa; demand has grown rapidly with the poultry industry.

Export market

Africa exports significant volumes of beans, groundnuts, pigeon peas and green grams to Europe, the Middle East and India. Prices are 20–50% above local market when specification is met. Requirements: uniform grain size, moisture below 12%, aflatoxin below the buyer's threshold, and often certification (organic, fair trade, or GLOBALG.A.P.).

Lesson 5

Choosing Your Legume for the Rotation

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Match legume to rainfall zone, soil type and market access
  • Plan a 4-year rotation that balances nitrogen, cash and food
  • Avoid the mistake of planting a legume with no buyer

Match by rainfall

  • Under 400 mm: cowpea, pigeon pea
  • 400–600 mm: cowpea, green gram, groundnut, pigeon pea, short-season beans
  • 600–900 mm: common bean, groundnut, soybean
  • Over 900 mm: common bean, soybean, green gram

Match by soil

  • Sandy, well-drained: groundnut
  • Loam to clay loam: common bean, soybean
  • Poor, dry, exhausted: cowpea, pigeon pea
  • High organic matter: any legume, all respond well

Match by market

  • Strong local food market: common bean, cowpea
  • Feed mill nearby: soybean, groundnut
  • Export market access: groundnut (aflatoxin risk), pigeon pea, green gram, common bean

The 4-year rotation

A pattern that works for most smallholders:

  • Year 1: Cereal (maize, sorghum, millet)
  • Year 2: Legume (beans, cowpea, groundnut)
  • Year 3: Cereal (different from Year 1)
  • Year 4: Legume (different from Year 2)

Add a short-cycle legume in a double-crop system if rainfall allows. Rotating two cereals and two legumes breaks every major pest and disease cycle.

Knowledge check — Module 1

1. What do legumes fix from the air?

Correct: nitrogen. Via rhizobium bacteria in root nodules.

2. Which legume fixes the most nitrogen?

Correct: soybean. 60–120 kg N/ha — the highest of the African legumes.

3. Why inoculate seed before planting?

Correct: soils may lack the right rhizobium. Inoculation guarantees efficient fixation.

4. What is the value of 100 kg N/ha fixed by a legume?

Correct: $60–120. At common urea prices, this is real money over a rotation.

5. Which legume suits a dry zone under 400 mm rainfall?

Correct: cowpea or pigeon pea. Both are drought-tolerant options for the Sahel and dry southern Africa.

Module 1 capstone

Write a one-page legume plan for your farm. Include: (1) which legume you will grow and why; (2) the market you are aiming at; (3) your rainfall zone and whether the legume fits; (4) the legume's place in your rotation; (5) whether you will inoculate seed and where you will buy the inoculant.
