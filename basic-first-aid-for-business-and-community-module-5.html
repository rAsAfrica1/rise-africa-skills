1. Types of Bleeding: Arterial, Venous, Capillary

Bleeding is classified by which blood vessels are damaged. Knowing the difference helps you act fast. Capillary bleeding is the most common: small cuts, scrapes, or abrasions. Blood oozes slowly and is usually bright red. It often stops on its own or with light pressure. Venous bleeding comes from veins, which carry blood back to the heart. It flows steadily and is dark red. Because veins are under low pressure, bleeding can be controlled with direct pressure, but it can still be serious if a large vein is cut. Arterial bleeding is the most dangerous. Arteries carry oxygen-rich blood from the heart under high pressure. Blood spurts in pulses, matching the heartbeat, and is bright red. This type of bleeding can quickly become life-threatening.

In a business or community setting in Africa, you might see these types of bleeding from common injuries: a kitchen worker cuts a finger with a knife (capillary or venous), a farmer steps on a hoe (venous or arterial), or a child falls on a bottle (arterial if a major vessel is cut). Your first step is to identify the type by looking at the flow. If it spurts, it's arterial. If it flows steadily, it's venous. If it oozes, it's capillary. But don't waste timeif you're unsure, treat it as arterial.

Always wear gloves if available. If not, use a clean plastic bag over your hands to protect yourself from bloodborne diseases like HIV and hepatitis. For capillary bleeding, wash the wound with clean water and soap, then apply a sterile dressing or clean cloth and press gently. For venous bleeding, apply firm direct pressure with a clean cloth or gauze. Do not peekhold pressure for at least 5-10 minutes. For arterial bleeding, apply hard direct pressure immediately and call for emergency help. If bleeding is from an arm or leg and pressure doesn't stop it, you may need a tourniquet (see Lesson 3).

Remember: never use dirty cloths or leaves on a woundthey cause infection. Use the cleanest material you have. In rural areas, boiled and cooled water is best for cleaning. Keep the person calm and lying down if bleeding is severe. Elevate the injured limb above the heart if possible, unless you suspect a fracture. Always seek medical help for arterial bleeding, deep wounds, or if bleeding doesn't stop after 10 minutes of pressure. Training your staff and community members to recognize these types saves lives.

2. Direct Pressure and Pressure Bandages

Direct pressure is the simplest and most effective way to stop most bleeding. It works by pressing the bleeding vessel against a bone or firm tissue, allowing a clot to form. For any bleeding except minor capillary oozing, start with direct pressure. Put on gloves if you have them. If not, use a plastic bag or clean cloth barrier. Place a sterile gauze pad or the cleanest cloth available directly over the wound. Press firmly with your fingers or palm. Do not remove the cloth to checkif it soaks through, add more layers on top and keep pressing. Hold for at least 10 minutes without lifting. For severe bleeding, press as hard as you can with your body weight if needed.

Once bleeding is controlled, you need to secure the dressing with a pressure bandage. A pressure bandage is a strip of cloth or commercial bandage wrapped tightly over the dressing to maintain pressure. You can use a triangular bandage, a roll of gauze, or a clean strip of cloth (like a bedsheet torn into strips). Start wrapping from the side farthest from the heart, moving toward the heart. Wrap firmly but not so tight that you cut off circulation. Check for signs of too much pressure: fingers or toes turning blue, cold, or numb, or the person complaining of tingling. If that happens, loosen the bandage immediately.

In Africa, many businesses and homes have limited first aid supplies. You can improvise: use a clean T-shirt, a kanga, or a chitenge as a dressing and bandage. For a pressure bandage, a necktie, belt, or strip of cloth works. But never use wire, rope, or thin cord directly on skinthey cause damage. If the wound is on a limb, elevate it above the heart after bandaging. Keep the person still and monitor for bleeding through the bandage. If blood seeps through, add more padding and press againdo not remove the original dressing.

After applying a pressure bandage, seek medical help if the wound is deep, caused by a bite, or if bleeding continues. For minor cuts, you can clean and bandage yourself. Always wash your hands before and after. If you don't have soap, use ash or a alcohol-based hand rub. Remember, a pressure bandage is temporarychange it daily or if it gets wet or dirty. Watch for infection: redness, swelling, pus, or fever. In community settings, teach others this skill. A simple pressure bandage can mean the difference between life and death.

3. Tourniquet Application: When and How

A tourniquet is a tight band used to stop severe bleeding from an arm or leg when direct pressure fails. It should only be used as a last resort because it cuts off all blood flow and can cause tissue damage if left too long. Use a tourniquet when: bleeding from a limb is life-threatening and spurting (arterial), direct pressure and pressure bandages have not stopped it, and you cannot get medical help quickly. Never use a tourniquet on the head, neck, or torso. Never use it for minor bleeding. In Africa, where transport to a hospital may take hours, a tourniquet can save a life if applied correctly.

To apply a tourniquet, first put on gloves if available. If not, use a barrier. Choose a commercial tourniquet like a CAT (Combat Application Tourniquet) if you have one. Otherwise, improvise with a wide, strong strip of cloth (at least 5 cm wide)a folded triangular bandage, a belt, or a strip of bedsheet. Do not use wire, rope, or shoelacesthey cut into skin. Place the tourniquet 5-7 cm above the wound, but not on a joint. If the wound is on a joint, place it above the joint. Wrap the band tightly around the limb twice and tie a half-knot. Place a stick or pen on top and tie a full knot. Twist the stick to tighten until bleeding stops. This should be painfulthat's normal. Secure the stick so it doesn't unwind. Write the time of application on the person's skin or a piece of tape.

Once applied, do not loosen or remove the tourniquet. Cover it with a blanket to keep it from being bumped. Get the person to a hospital immediately. If you can, note the time and tell the medical staff. A tourniquet can be left in place for up to 2 hours without permanent damage, but after that, the risk of amputation increases. If the person is in shock, keep them warm and lying down. Do not give them anything to eat or drink. Monitor their breathing. If they become unconscious, check for breathing and start CPR if needed.

After the emergency, seek training. Many organizations in Africa offer first aid courses. Practice applying a tourniquet on a dummy or a limb (not a person) to build confidence. Remember: a tourniquet is a temporary measure. It buys time. Always follow up with professional medical care. In community settings, share this knowledgeit can prevent deaths from farm accidents, road crashes, or violence.

4. Treating Minor Cuts, Scrapes, and Abrasions

Minor cuts, scrapes, and abrasions are common in homes, farms, and businesses. They involve capillary bleeding and can be treated at home if you follow steps to prevent infection. First, wash your hands with soap and clean water. If you don't have soap, use ash or an alcohol-based hand rub. Put on gloves if available; if not, use a clean plastic bag. Stop any bleeding by applying gentle direct pressure with a clean cloth or gauze for a few minutes. Most minor bleeding stops quickly.

Next, clean the wound. Rinse it under clean running water for at least 5 minutes. If you don't have running water, use a clean container and pour water over the wound. Wash around the wound with mild soap, but try not to get soap inside the woundit can irritate. Use a clean cloth to gently wipe away dirt. If there is embedded debris like gravel or splinters, remove it with tweezers that have been cleaned with alcohol or boiled. If you can't remove it, leave it and seek medical help. After cleaning, pat the area dry with a clean cloth. Apply a thin layer of antibiotic ointment if you have itthis helps prevent infection and keeps the wound moist. If not, you can use honey, which has natural antibacterial properties.

Cover the wound with a sterile bandage or clean cloth. For scrapes, a non-stick dressing is best. Change the dressing daily or whenever it gets wet or dirty. If the wound is on a hand, you can wear a clean plastic bag over it when working to keep it dry. For minor cuts, you can use butterfly bandages or strips of tape to pull the edges together. Do not use cotton wool directly on the woundfibers can stick and cause infection. Keep the wound clean and dry. Watch for signs of infection: increased redness, swelling, warmth, pain, pus, or fever. If any appear, see a health worker.

In Africa, many people use traditional remedies like leaves or soil. Avoid thesethey cause tetanus and other infections. Tetanus is a serious risk with any wound, especially puncture wounds. If the person hasn't had a tetanus vaccine in the last 5 years, they should get one within 48 hours. Also, check if the wound was caused by a dirty object or an animal bitethese need medical attention. For minor wounds, healing takes a few days to a week. Keep the area clean and protect it from dirt. Teach children to wash their hands and clean their cuts. A simple first aid kit with gauze, bandages, and antiseptic can prevent many problems.

5. Managing Nosebleeds and Dental Injuries

Nosebleeds are common and usually not serious. They can be caused by dry air, picking the nose, a blow to the face, or high blood pressure. To manage a nosebleed: sit the person upright and lean them slightly forward. This prevents blood from running down the throat, which can cause nausea. Tell them to pinch the soft part of the nose, just below the bony bridge, with their thumb and index finger. Hold for 10-15 minutes without letting go. Breathe through the mouth. Do not tilt the head back. After 10 minutes, release and check if bleeding has stopped. If not, pinch again for another 10 minutes. You can also apply a cold compress (ice wrapped in cloth) to the bridge of the nose to constrict blood vessels. If bleeding continues after 20 minutes, or if it's very heavy, seek medical help.

After a nosebleed, tell the person not to blow their nose, bend over, or lift heavy objects for several hours. Avoid hot drinks and spicy foods. If nosebleeds happen often, see a doctor. In Africa, dry harmattan winds can cause nosebleeds. Using a little petroleum jelly inside the nostrils can help keep them moist. For children, keep their fingernails short to prevent picking.

Dental injuries include knocked-out teeth, broken teeth, and cuts to the lip or tongue. For a knocked-out permanent tooth, act fastyou have about 30 minutes to save it. Pick up the tooth by the crown (the white part), not the root. If it's dirty, rinse it gently with milk or saline (not water) for a few seconds. Do not scrub it. Try to put it back into the socket and have the person bite down on a clean cloth to hold it. If you can't reinsert it, put the tooth in a container with milk, saline, or the person's own saliva (if they are conscious and can spit). Do not put it in water. Get to a dentist immediately.

For a broken tooth, save the pieces in milk or saline. Rinse the mouth with warm water. Apply a cold compress to the face to reduce swelling. For bleeding from the lip or tongue, apply direct pressure with a clean cloth. If bleeding doesn't stop, seek help. For a baby tooth that is knocked out, do not try to reinsert itit can damage the permanent tooth. Just control bleeding and see a dentist. In all dental injuries, avoid aspirin for pain as it can increase bleeding. Use paracetamol instead. Always follow up with a dental professional.

6. Infection Prevention and Wound Dressing

Infection is the biggest risk after any wound. In Africa, where access to antibiotics may be limited, prevention is critical. The first step is hand hygiene. Wash your hands with soap and clean water for at least 20 seconds before and after touching a wound. If soap is not available, use an alcohol-based hand rub or ash. Wear gloves if you have them. If not, use a clean plastic bag over your hands. Never touch a wound with bare hands if you can avoid it.

Clean the wound thoroughly. For most wounds, rinse with clean running water for 5-10 minutes. You can use boiled and cooled water if tap water is not safe. Avoid using hydrogen peroxide or alcohol directly on the woundthey damage tissue and slow healing. Mild soap is okay for cleaning around the wound, but rinse it off well. If the wound is dirty, use a soft brush or cloth to remove debris. For puncture wounds, do not probe deeplyjust clean the surface and seek medical help. After cleaning, apply a thin layer of antibiotic ointment if available. If not, honey can be used as an alternative. Cover the wound with a sterile dressing or clean cloth. For wounds that are oozing, use a non-stick pad. Change the dressing daily or whenever it becomes wet or dirty. When changing, wash your hands again and use a new clean dressing.

Watch for signs of infection: redness spreading from the wound, swelling, warmth, pain that gets worse, pus or foul smell, and fever. If you see these, get medical help. Do not wait. In the meantime, keep the wound clean and elevated if possible. Tetanus is a serious infection that can be prevented by vaccination. If the person hasn't had a tetanus booster in 5 years, they should get one within 48 hours of a dirty wound. Also, consider rabies if bitten by an animalseek immediate care.

In community settings, promote hygiene: ensure safe water, use latrines, and keep wounds covered. Teach people not to use soil, leaves, or cow dung on wounds. These cause tetanus and other infections. A simple first aid kit should include gloves, gauze, bandages, antiseptic, and scissors. Store it in a clean, dry place. For businesses, have a designated first aider and keep a log of incidents. After any wound, follow up: check healing daily, and if it's not improving in 2-3 days, see a health worker. Remember, a clean wound heals faster and leaves less scarring.

<h2>Practical Reference: Bleeding Control and Wound Care for African Businesses and Communities</h2>

<p>In African agribusiness, construction, transport, and community settings, bleeding injuries are common and often occur far from formal healthcare. A worker cuts a hand on a machete, a farmer steps on a hoe, a market trader suffers a nosebleed, or a child scrapes a knee on a school playground. The first responder is usually a supervisor, colleague, family member, or community health volunteer. This practical reference lesson gives you clear, field-tested steps to control bleeding, treat wounds, prevent infection, and decide when to refer to a clinic or hospital. It is written for business owners, farm managers, community leaders, and anyone who wants to protect people and productivity.</p>

<p>Remember: severe bleeding can kill within minutes. Your priority is to stop the bleeding, keep the airway open, and get help. For minor wounds, your priority is to clean, cover, and prevent infection. Always wear gloves or use a clean plastic bag as a barrier when available. In Africa, where resources may be limited, improvisation with clean cloth, boiled water, and soap can save lives.</p>

<h3>Types of Bleeding: Arterial, Venous, Capillary</h3>
<p>Understanding the type of bleeding helps you choose the right response. Arterial bleeding is bright red and spurts with each heartbeat. It is life-threatening and requires immediate direct pressure and, if severe, a tourniquet. Venous bleeding is dark red and flows steadily. It can be serious but usually responds to direct pressure and elevation. Capillary bleeding is bright red and oozes slowly. It is common in minor cuts and scrapes and usually stops with gentle pressure and a dressing.</p>

<h3>Direct Pressure and Pressure Bandages</h3>
<p>Direct pressure is the first and most effective way to stop most bleeding. Put on gloves if available. Place a clean cloth, gauze, or plastic bag over the wound. Press firmly with your hand. Do not lift the cloth to check the wound; if blood soaks through, add more cloth on top and keep pressing. For a limb, elevate it above heart level if you can. Once bleeding is controlled, wrap a pressure bandage firmly over the dressing. The bandage should be tight enough to maintain pressure but not so tight that it cuts off circulation. Check for signs of poor circulation: pale, cold, or blue fingers or toes, numbness, or tingling. If these occur, loosen the bandage.</p>

<h3>Tourniquet Application: When and How</h3>
<p>A tourniquet is a last resort for life-threatening arterial bleeding on an arm or leg when direct pressure and pressure bandages fail. Do not use a tourniquet on the head, neck, or torso. Use a commercially made tourniquet if available, such as a CAT (Combat Application Tourniquet). If not, use a wide, strong cloth and a stick as a windlass. Place the tourniquet 57 cm above the wound, never directly over a joint. Tighten until bleeding stops. Note the time of application and write it on the patient's skin or a piece of tape. Do not cover the tourniquet. Get the patient to a hospital immediately. In African rural settings, where transport may take hours, a tourniquet can be life-saving, but it must be applied correctly and the patient must be referred urgently.</p>

<div style="background-color:#ffe6e6; border-left:6px solid #cc0000; padding:15px; margin:20px 0;">
<strong style="color:#cc0000;"> RED SAFETY WARNING:</strong> A tourniquet is only for life-threatening bleeding that cannot be controlled by direct pressure. Applying a tourniquet incorrectly can cause permanent nerve damage, limb loss, or death. Never use a tourniquet for minor bleeding, nosebleeds, or bleeding on the head, neck, or torso. Once applied, do not loosen or remove it. Write down the time of application. Seek emergency medical care immediately. In areas with limited ambulance services, arrange transport to the nearest clinic or hospital without delay.

<h3>Treating Minor Cuts, Scrapes, and Abrasions</h3>
<p>Minor wounds are common in farms, workshops, kitchens, and homes. Wash your hands and wear gloves if possible. Rinse the wound with clean running water for at least 5 minutes. Wash around the wound with soap, but avoid getting soap deep into the wound. Remove dirt or debris with clean tweezers. Apply an antibiotic ointment if available. Cover with a sterile bandage or clean cloth. Change the dressing daily or when wet or dirty. Watch for signs of infection: increased redness, swelling, warmth, pain, pus, or fever. In Africa, where tetanus is a risk, ensure the patient's tetanus vaccination is up to date. If the wound is deep, dirty, or caused by a rusty object, refer to a clinic for tetanus prophylaxis.</p>

<h3>Managing Nosebleeds and Dental Injuries</h3>
<p>Nosebleeds are common and usually not serious. Have the person sit upright and lean slightly forward. Pinch the soft part of the nose, just below the bony bridge, for 1015 minutes. Do not tilt the head back, as this can cause blood to run down the throat. Apply a cold compress to the bridge of the nose if available. If bleeding continues after 20 minutes, or if the person is taking blood thinners, seek medical help. For dental injuries, such as a knocked-out tooth, handle the tooth by the crown, not the root. Rinse gently with saline or milk. If possible, reinsert the tooth into the socket and have the person bite down gently on a clean cloth. If not, place the tooth in milk or saline and get to a dentist within 30 minutes. For a broken tooth, rinse the mouth with warm water and apply a cold compress to reduce swelling.</p>

<h3>Infection Prevention and Wound Dressing</h3>
<p>Infection is a major risk in African communities where access to clean water and antibiotics may be limited. Prevent infection by washing your hands before and after wound care, using clean gloves, and cleaning the wound thoroughly. Use boiled and cooled water if clean running water is not available. Apply a sterile dressing or clean cloth. Change the dressing daily and whenever it becomes wet or dirty. Do not use cotton wool directly on the wound, as fibers can stick. If the wound becomes infected, seek medical care. Signs of infection include redness spreading, swelling, warmth, throbbing pain, pus, and fever. For business owners, keeping a well-stocked first aid kit and training staff in basic wound care reduces downtime and healthcare costs.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>1. Assess the scene:</strong> Ensure safety for yourself and the injured person. Wear gloves or use a plastic bag.</li>
<li><strong>2. Identify bleeding type:</strong> Arterial (spurting, bright red), venous (steady, dark red), capillary (oozing).</li>
<li><strong>3. Apply direct pressure:</strong> Press firmly with clean cloth or gauze for 1015 minutes. Do not lift to check.</li>
<li><strong>4. Elevate and bandage:</strong> Raise limb above heart if possible. Wrap pressure bandage firmly but not too tight.</li>
<li><strong>5. Use tourniquet only if life-threatening:</strong> Apply 57 cm above wound, not over joint. Note time. Do not loosen.</li>
<li><strong>6. Clean minor wounds:</strong> Rinse with clean water, wash around with soap, cover with sterile dressing.</li>
<li><strong>7. Manage nosebleeds and dental injuries:</strong> Pinch nose, lean forward. For knocked-out tooth, store in milk and see dentist.</li>
<li><strong>8. Prevent infection and refer:</strong> Change dressings daily. Watch for signs of infection. Refer to clinic if severe, dirty, or tetanus risk.</li>
</ul>
