1. Making Maize Shellers

A maize sheller saves hours of hand-shelling. Start with a wooden or metal drum, 200-litre oil drum works well. Cut a rectangular opening 25 cm by 15 cm on the side. Inside, weld or bolt 8 to 12 metal teeth (10 mm round bars, 5 cm long) in a spiral pattern along the drum's inner surface. The shaft runs through the centre on two bearings (pillow block bearings, 25 mm bore). Attach a hand crank or a pulley for a 1-2 HP electric motor. The clearance between the teeth and the drum wall should be 2-3 cm for dry maize. For wet maize, increase to 4 cm to avoid crushing. Build a hopper on top (30 cm x 30 cm) and a discharge chute at the bottom. Test with 5 kg of dry maize: you should shell 20-30 kg per hour by hand, or 100-150 kg per hour with a motor. Adjust the teeth if kernels break too much. Use hardened bolts for teeth to last longer. Grease the bearings every 50 hours of use. This design is simple enough for a village welder to make with a grinder, welding machine, and drill.

2. Fabricating Grain Mills and Grinders

Grain mills turn maize, millet, or sorghum into flour. The simplest is a plate mill: two cast iron or steel discs, 20-25 cm diameter, with grooves cut radially. One disc is stationary, the other rotates. The gap between them adjusts from 0.5 mm (fine flour) to 2 mm (coarse meal). Use a 1.5-3 HP electric motor or a 5-7 HP diesel engine. Build a wooden or metal frame to hold the discs and motor. A hopper feeds grain into the centre of the rotating disc. The centrifugal force pushes grain outward, grinding it between the plates. For a hammer mill, use a rotor with 4-8 swinging hammers (10 mm thick steel) inside a drum. A 3 mm screen at the bottom controls particle size. Hammer mills are better for dry grains and can also crush groundnuts or cassava chips. For either type, ensure the drive belt is tight and pulleys aligned. Use a 1:3 ratio (motor pulley 5 cm, mill pulley 15 cm) to get 800-1200 RPM for plate mills. Test with 1 kg of maize: you should get 20-50 kg per hour. Wear safety glasses and ear protection. Clean the mill after each use to prevent mould.

3. Building Fruit Presses and Crushers

For mango, pineapple, or palm fruit, a manual press extracts juice. Build a cylindrical basket from hardwood slats (5 cm wide, 1 cm thick) held by three metal hoops. The basket is 40 cm tall, 30 cm diameter. Inside, a wooden or metal plunger fits snugly, pushed by a screw jack (car jack, 2-ton capacity) or a lever arm. Place a perforated plate at the bottom (5 mm holes) and a juice tray underneath. For crushing, use a separate crusher: a wooden or metal drum with rotating blades (4 blades, 20 cm long) powered by a hand crank or 1 HP motor. Load 10 kg of fruit, crush for 2 minutes, then transfer to the press. Apply pressure slowly over 5 minutes; too fast and juice splashes. You get 4-6 litres of juice from 10 kg of mangoes. For palm fruit, use a digester (a vertical cylinder with rotating paddles) to loosen the fruit before pressing. Sterilise all parts with boiling water before use. Use food-grade stainless steel for the press plate and tray to avoid rust. Clean immediately after use with soap and water.

4. Making Threshing Machines

A thresher separates grain from stalks for rice, wheat, sorghum, or beans. Build a drum 60 cm long, 40 cm diameter, from 2 mm steel sheet. Weld 4 rows of 8 teeth (10 mm round bars, 8 cm long) in a spiral. The drum spins inside a concave: a curved metal grate with 10 mm gaps, set 2-3 cm below the teeth. A 5-7 HP diesel engine drives the drum at 500-700 RPM via a belt and pulley. Feed crop into the inlet (20 cm x 20 cm) at the top. The teeth beat the grain loose; grain falls through the concave onto a sieve (5 mm mesh), while straw exits the back. For rice, use a lower speed (400 RPM) to avoid breaking grains. For maize, use a higher speed (800 RPM) and larger gaps. Build a blower fan (30 cm diameter, 4 blades) to separate chaff from grain. Test with 10 kg of sorghum: you should thresh 100-200 kg per hour. Wear gloves and safety glasses. Lubricate bearings daily. Adjust the concave gap for different crops: 15 mm for rice, 25 mm for maize. Keep the machine on level ground.

5. Constructing Drying Racks and Solar Dryers

Drying racks keep crops off the ground, preventing mould and animal damage. Build a wooden frame 2 m long, 1 m wide, 1.5 m high. Use 5 cm x 5 cm timber for legs and crossbars. Stretch insect-proof mesh (1 mm holes) across the top as a tray. Add three shelves, 40 cm apart. For a solar dryer, build a box 2 m x 1 m x 0.5 m deep, with a clear plastic or glass top (4 mm thick). Paint the inside black to absorb heat. Cut vents at the bottom (10 cm x 10 cm) and top (10 cm x 10 cm) with mesh to keep insects out. The dryer reaches 50-70Â°C, drying 10 kg of sliced mangoes in 2-3 days instead of 7-10 days in the open. For grains, use a rack with a clear roof but open sides. Turn crops every 4 hours for even drying. Test moisture with a cheap moisture meter: maize should be 13-14% for storage. Clean racks after each use. In humid areas, add a small fan (12V, 0.5A) powered by a solar panel (20W) to speed drying.

6. Designing Storage Bins and Silos

A good storage bin keeps grain dry and pest-free. For small farms, build a metal bin from 0.5 mm galvanised sheet: 1.5 m diameter, 2 m tall, holding about 2 tonnes of maize. Cut sheets into 1.5 m x 1 m pieces, roll into a cylinder, and rivet or bolt seams. Add a conical roof (30Â° slope) and a bottom cone (45Â° slope) for easy emptying. Weld a 10 cm diameter outlet pipe with a sliding gate. For a silo, use concrete blocks or ferrocement: a 2 m diameter, 3 m tall structure holds 8-10 tonnes. Line the inside with waterproof cement plaster. Add a ventilation pipe (10 cm diameter) at the top and a small fan for aeration. Before storing, clean and dry the bin, then dust with 1% malathion powder (10 g per square metre). Fill grain to 90% capacity. Check monthly for moisture and insects. For hermetic storage, use airtight plastic bags (e.g., GrainPro) inside the bin. In Africa, keep bins off the ground on a concrete plinth and under shade to reduce heat. Repair any holes immediately.

Maize shellers, hammer mills, threshers, and presses cause severe injuries. Before any build or operation: (1) Fit guards over all belts, pulleys, and rotors â no exposed moving parts. (2) Install an emergency stop and a lockable isolator on every motorised unit. (3) Never clear a jam or reach into a hopper while the machine is running or still spinning down. (4) Welders must wear a full-face shield, leather apron, and gauntlet gloves; ensure fume extraction in enclosed workshops. (5) Test every machine at low load before full-power operation, and balance rotors to prevent vibration failure. (6) Provide a written safety briefing and PPE with every sale â this protects your customers and your business reputation.
