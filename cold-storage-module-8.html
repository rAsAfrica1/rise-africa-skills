Lessons

1. Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)

In cold storage, PPE is your first line of defence against frostbite, chemical burns, and physical injuries. You need insulated gloves, a thermal jacket, thermal trousers, and non-slip boots. For ammonia areas, keep a full-face respirator with ammonia cartridges, chemical splash goggles, and a face shield. Ear protection is essential because compressors and fans can exceed 85 decibels. Always check PPE before each shift: look for tears, cracks, or missing straps. In Africa, where heat outside can be 35C and inside -25C, layering is key. Wear moisture-wicking base layers, then fleece, then a windproof outer layer. Never wear loose clothing that can catch on machinery. For refrigerant handling, add cryogenic gloves and an apron. Store PPE in a clean, dry locker room. Replace worn items immediatelydon't wait for a purchase order. A simple rule: if you can see your skin through a glove, it's useless. Train every worker to inspect and don PPE in under two minutes. Keep a spare set for visitors. Remember, PPE only works if you wear it correctly: seal the respirator with a fit test, tighten boot laces, and fasten all zippers. In many African facilities, dust and humidity degrade gear quickly, so inspect weekly and replace every six months for gloves and filters. Document all inspections in a logbook. Your life depends on this geartreat it like your most important tool.

2. Ammonia and Refrigerant Leak Response

Ammonia is common in African cold stores because it's cheap and efficient, but it's deadly. You can smell it at 5 parts per million (ppm); at 300 ppm, it's immediately dangerous. If you smell ammonia, don't panic. First, hold your breath and walk upwind to fresh air. Never runrunning increases inhalation. Alert others by shouting 'Ammonia leak!' and activate the nearest alarm. If you're trained and have a full-face respirator with ammonia cartridges, you can shut the emergency valve, usually a red quarter-turn valve near the compressor. Otherwise, evacuate immediately. For other refrigerants like R-404A or R-134a, they're heavier than air and displace oxygen. If you feel dizzy, leave at once. Use a leak detector or soap bubbles to find leaksnever a lighter. Once safe, call your supervisor and the fire brigade if large. Treat anyone exposed: move to fresh air, flush eyes with water for 15 minutes, and seek medical help. For skin contact with liquid refrigerant, warm the area with lukewarm waterdo not rub. In Africa, many facilities lack gas masks, so keep a 5kg bag of lime or baking soda to neutralise small ammonia spills. But never enter a confined space without a buddy and a rescue line. After any leak, ventilate the area with fans for at least 30 minutes before restarting. Document the incident and replace all filters. Practice a leak drill every three months. Speed saves lives: a 10-second delay can cause permanent lung damage.

3. Fire Prevention and Suppression

Cold storage fires often start from electrical faults, overheated compressors, or welding sparks. In Africa, many facilities use diesel generators, so keep fuel stored 10 metres away. Prevent fires by cleaning lint from condenser coils weekly, checking wiring for rodent damage, and never storing flammable chemicals like paint or solvents inside. Install smoke detectors and a fire alarm that can be heard over machinery noise. For suppression, use a dry powder or CO2 extinguishernever water on electrical fires. Place extinguishers every 15 metres and check the pressure gauge monthly. A simple rule: PASSPull, Aim, Squeeze, Sweep. For small fires, aim at the base. If a fire is bigger than a wastebasket, evacuate and call the fire brigade. In cold rooms, fires can smoulder due to low oxygen, so always investigate burning smells. Train every worker to use an extinguisher once a year. Keep a sand bucket near welding areas. For ammonia fires, never use waterit reacts violently. Use CO2 or dry chemical. If you see smoke, feel the door with the back of your hand. If hot, don't open. In many African settings, fire brigades may take 20 minutes, so your first response is critical. Have a fire blanket for small pan fires. After any fire, check for hidden embers in insulation. Replace all extinguishers that have been used, even partially. Keep a log of all inspections and drills. A fire drill every six months is mandatory.

4. Emergency Evacuation Plans

A cold storage facility has unique evacuation challenges: heavy doors, limited exits, and freezing temperatures. Your plan must be written, posted at every exit, and practiced. First, map all exitsat least two per room, and keep them clear of pallets and boxes. Mark exits with glow-in-the-dark signs that work when power fails. Assign a floor warden for each shift. When the alarm sounds, stop work immediately, turn off machinery if safe, and walkdon't runto the nearest exit. In cold rooms, move quickly but carefully; icy floors cause falls. If you're trapped, use your phone or radio to call for help and bang on pipes. For ammonia leaks, evacuate upwind and uphill. For fires, stay low to avoid smoke. Once outside, go to the assembly pointa painted circle or sign at least 50 metres from the building. The warden takes a headcount using a staff list. Never re-enter until the fire brigade or safety officer says it's safe. In Africa, where many workers speak different languages, use pictograms and simple words like 'Exit' and 'Assembly Point'. Practice evacuations every three months, including one at night. Time your drills: everyone should be out in under 3 minutes. Keep a whistle and torch on your person. If you have visitors, brief them on exits before entering. After any evacuation, review what went wrong and update the plan. A good plan is simple, practiced, and known by all.

5. Lockout/Tagout Procedures

Lockout/Tagout (LOTO) prevents machines from starting while you're repairing them. In cold storage, this applies to compressors, fans, conveyors, and electrical panels. The rule: every energy sourceelectric, pneumatic, hydraulic, or thermalmust be isolated. First, notify all affected workers that you're shutting down. Then, turn off the main disconnect switch. Apply your personal padlock and a tag with your name and date. Never use someone else's lock. For electrical panels, use a lockout hasp that holds multiple locks. For valves, use a gate valve lockout. Test the machine by trying to start itif it doesn't move, it's safe. If it starts, stop and find another energy source. In Africa, many facilities have old equipment without standard disconnects, so use a chain and padlock through the breaker handle. Always keep the key with younever leave it in the lock. When the job is done, remove your lock only after all workers are clear. Replace guards, then notify others before re-energising. Tagout alone is not enoughtags can fall off. Always use a lock. Train every maintenance worker on LOTO before they touch any machine. Keep a LOTO kit with locks, tags, hasps, and a logbook. Inspect locks monthly for rust. A simple rule: if you didn't lock it, don't touch it. LOTO saves livesover 100 workers die each year in Africa from unplanned machine start-ups. Don't be a statistic.

6. First Aid and CPR Basics

In cold storage, common injuries are cuts, frostbite, chemical burns, and cardiac arrest from electric shock or ammonia exposure. Your first aid kit must include sterile gauze, bandages, burn gel, eye wash, scissors, and a CPR mask. Check it monthly and replace expired items. For frostbite, move the person to a warm room (20-25C). Do not rub the area. Soak in warm water (37-39C) for 20-30 minutes. If blisters form, cover loosely. For chemical burns from ammonia, flush with water for at least 15 minutes. Remove contaminated clothing. For cuts, apply direct pressure with a clean cloth. For CPR, if someone is unresponsive and not breathing, call for help and start chest compressions: push hard and fast in the centre of the chest, at least 100-120 compressions per minute, 5-6 cm deep. Use an AED if available. In Africa, ambulances may take time, so you are the first responder. For electric shock, turn off power before touching the victim. For heat exhaustion (from working in hot outside areas), move to shade and give water. For hypothermia (from cold rooms), wrap in blankets and give warm drinks. Never give alcohol. Train at least two people per shift in first aid and CPR. Recertify every two years. Keep a written incident report. Practice scenarios monthly. Remember: your actions in the first 5 minutes can save a life. Stay calm, act fast, and never put yourself in danger.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Safety and Emergency Procedures for Cold Storage in African Agribusiness

Cold storage is the backbone of post-harvest value addition across Africafrom flower exports in Kenya and Ethiopia to citrus in South Africa, fish in Senegal, and horticulture in Ghana. But ammonia refrigeration, diesel generators, and confined spaces create serious hazards. This practical reference gives you the checklists, tables, and procedures your team needs to protect people, product, and profit.

Use this module alongside your facility's standard operating procedures (SOPs). It is designed for supervisors, technicians, and safety officers working in small to medium cold rooms (10500 tonnes) and larger packhouses.

PPE is your last line of defencenot your first. Engineering controls (ventilation, leak detection) and administrative controls (permits, training) must come first. In many African facilities, PPE is expensive and imported; therefore, focus on the highest-risk tasks: ammonia handling, freezer entry, and generator maintenance.

Ammonia and Refrigerant Leak Response

Ammonia (R717) is the most common industrial refrigerant in African cold stores because it is cheap, efficient, and locally available. But it is toxic and flammable at high concentrations. A leak can kill within minutes in confined spaces. Your response must be rehearsed monthly.

Immediate actions on ammonia leak:

  1. Do not enter the area without full PPE and SCBA (self-contained breathing apparatus).
  2. Activate the emergency alarm and evacuate non-essential personnel upwind.
  3. Shut off the nearest isolation valve if safe to do so.
  4. Ventilate the area using explosion-proof fans if available.
  5. Call the fire brigade and emergency services. In many African cities, give clear landmarksstreet names may be unreliable.
  6. Use water spray to knock down ammonia vapour (never direct water into liquid ammonia).
  7. Account for all personnel at the muster point.
RED SAFETY WARNING: Ammonia Exposure

Ammonia concentrations above 300 ppm cause immediate throat irritation; above 5,000 ppm can be fatal. Never attempt to rescue a colleague without SCBAyou will become a second casualty. In facilities without SCBA, evacuate and wait for trained emergency services. Store ammonia emergency kits (SCBA, chemical suits, ammonia test strips) in a clearly marked, accessible location outside the compressor room.

Fire Prevention and Suppression

Cold storage fires often start in compressor rooms (electrical faults, oil leaks) or from diesel generators. Ammonia itself is flammable at 1528% concentration in air. Fire prevention is cheaper than firefighting.

Key controls: no smoking, hot work permits, regular electrical inspections, and clean compressor rooms. For suppression, use CO or dry chemical extinguishers for electrical fires; never use water on live electrical equipment. In ammonia areas, use water spray only to absorb vapour.

Emergency Evacuation Plans

Every cold store must have a written evacuation plan, posted at exits and in local languages (English, French, Portuguese, Swahili, etc.). The plan must include:

  • Primary and secondary escape routes (keep them clear).
  • Muster point locationupwind and at least 50 m from the building.
  • Headcount procedure and buddy system.
  • Roles for fire wardens, first aiders, and shutdown operators.
  • Contact numbers for fire brigade, ambulance, and refrigeration contractor.

Conduct drills every 3 months. In many African facilities, staff turnover is high; new workers must be drilled within their first week.

Lockout/Tagout (LOTO) Procedures

LOTO prevents accidental start-up of compressors, fans, and conveyors during maintenance. It is legally required in South Africa (OHS Act 85 of 1993) and strongly recommended elsewhere.

Six-step LOTO:

  1. Notify all affected workers.
  2. Shut down equipment using normal stop procedure.
  3. Isolate all energy sources (electrical, pneumatic, hydraulic, thermal).
  4. Apply locks and tags to each isolation point.
  5. Release stored energy (bleed pressure, block moving parts).
  6. Verify zero energy state by attempting a start.

First Aid and CPR Basics

In remote African locations, emergency services may take 3060 minutes. Your team must be trained in:

  • CPR (30 chest compressions : 2 breaths for adults).
  • Recovery position for unconscious breathing casualties.
  • Ammonia exposure: move to fresh air, flush eyes/skin with water for 15 minutes.
  • Frostbite: warm affected area in 3739C water, do not rub.
  • Severe bleeding: apply direct pressure and elevate.

Keep first aid kits stocked and accessible. Check expiry dates quarterly.

Quick Reference Checklist (Post at Every Cold Store)

  • PPE worn correctly for the task (see Table 1).
  • Ammonia detectors tested weekly; alarm audible.
  • Fire extinguishers inspected monthly; no obstructions.
  • Escape routes clear; muster point marked and upwind.
  • LOTO locks and tags available; only trained staff use them.
  • First aid kit stocked; expiry dates checked quarterly.
  • Emergency contact numbers posted in local language(s).
  • Drills conducted every 3 months; records kept.
