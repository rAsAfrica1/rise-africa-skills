Lesson 10.1~11 min

Vitamin C and Vitamin E: The Two Vitamins That Decide Disease Resistance

In this lesson
  • State the vitamin C and vitamin E requirements for grow-out and broodstock fish
  • Explain why unstabilised vitamin C is worthless in a hot store within weeks
  • Cost stabilised vitamin forms against the mortality and growth losses they prevent

If you only ever fix two micronutrients in your feed, fix vitamin C and vitamin E. They are the two the tropics destroy fastest and the two whose absence shows up as disease.

Vitamin C. Tilapia, catfish and carp all require 100-200 mg per kg of feed at grow-out, and broodstock need 200-300 mg/kg. Fish store no vitamin C at all: it is water-soluble and must arrive in every meal. Fish fed adequate vitamin C show about 50 percent fewer infections and 10-20 percent higher broodstock fecundity. Deficiency shows as poor growth, haemorrhages under the skin and around the fins, spinal deformity and a general collapse of resistance to whatever bacteria are already in the pond.

Here is the problem that catches most African farmers. Ordinary ascorbic acid loses 50-90 percent of its potency within 2-4 weeks of storage at ambient temperature. Your feed bag may have been formulated correctly at the mill and be nutritionally empty of vitamin C by the time you open it. The answer is a stabilised form:

  • Ascorbyl phosphate, water-soluble, 20-30 percent more expensive, retains 70-95 percent of potency at six months
  • Ascorbyl palmitate, fat-soluble, 30-40 percent more expensive, retains 60-90 percent at six months

The cost of stabilisation is USD 0.02-0.05 per kg of feed. If unstabilised vitamin C is all you can get, then buy feed in small lots and use it within eight weeks.

Vitamin E. The requirement is 100-200 mg/kg for normal feeds and 200-400 mg/kg for high-lipid broodstock feeds. Vitamin E loses 30-50 percent of its potency over six months in feed. Its job is to protect the polyunsaturated fats in the feed and in the fish from oxidation. That means the more oil you put in a feed, the more vitamin E it needs. A feed above 10 percent lipid, or above 1 percent EPA plus DHA, needs the higher end of the range. Deficiency causes muscular dystrophy and greatly increased disease susceptibility. Cost of adequate vitamin E is roughly USD 0.02-0.10 per kg of feed depending on whether you use synthetic or natural tocopherol.

Worked example, Cameroon. A farmer near Douala buys 2 tonnes of tilapia feed per cycle at USD 0.70/kg, so USD 1,400. Adding a stabilised vitamin C at the top of the range, USD 0.05/kg, costs 2,000 x 0.05 = USD 100 per cycle. Suppose his current cycle loses 12 percent of fish to bacterial infection out of 1,000 kg of expected harvest, so 120 kg lost. If halving infections recovers even 60 kg, at an assumed farm-gate price of USD 2.00/kg (verify your own market) that is USD 120 recovered against USD 100 spent, before counting the growth improvement in the survivors. The margin is thin at the top of the price range and much better at USD 0.02/kg.

That calculation is the honest way to think about vitamins. They are not magic. They are an insurance premium with a measurable payout, and the payout is largest where mortality is currently high.

Two practical points. First, ask your feed supplier in writing which form of vitamin C the feed contains and what the manufacture date is. A supplier who cannot answer is telling you something. Second, if you mix your own feed, buy a vitamin premix rather than individual vitamins; a complete premix costs about USD 0.03-0.05 per kg of finished feed and is the single highest-value line item in a farm-made ration.

Lesson 10.2~11 min

Reading Deficiency Signs on the Fish in Your Net

In this lesson
  • Identify the specific deficiency each visible sign points to
  • Distinguish nutritional deficiency from water quality damage and infection
  • Run a structured 30-fish sampling inspection and record what you find

Your fish are the cheapest diagnostic tool on the farm. Deficiency signs are specific, and once you know them you can read a feed problem off a net full of fish in ten minutes.

Amino acid deficiency signs:

  • Lysine: fin erosion, gill hyperplasia (thickened, ragged gill tissue), slow growth. Lysine is the first-limiting amino acid in almost every plant-based feed, at 1.8-2.0 percent of the diet for tilapia fry and 1.2-1.5 percent at grow-out.
  • Methionine: cataracts and cloudy eyes, loss of skin colour. Methionine is the lowest amino acid in soybean meal, at only 0.4-0.5 percent, against a tilapia fry requirement of 0.9-1.0 percent methionine plus cystine.
  • Tryptophan: skeletal deformity, specifically scoliosis (sideways curve) and lordosis (downward curve of the spine), plus jumpy, hyperexcitable behaviour.
  • Arginine: poor growth, weak immune response, poor wound healing. Fish need two to three times more arginine per kg of body weight than mammals, which is a genuine and often overlooked difference.
  • Threonine: anaemia and pale gills, gut hypoplasia, reduced disease resistance.

Vitamin deficiency signs:

  • Vitamin A: eye opacity progressing to blindness, gill damage.
  • Vitamin D3: soft skeleton and poor calcium-phosphorus regulation, most visible in fry. Note that fish cannot make vitamin D from sunlight the way land animals do, so it must come from the feed regardless of how sunny the pond is.
  • Vitamin E: muscular dystrophy and a general rise in disease.
  • Vitamin C: haemorrhages, poor growth, high infection rate.
  • Riboflavin: cataracts, gill erosion, poor growth.
  • Niacin: dermatitis and poor growth.
  • Vitamin B12: anaemia, poor growth, reduced appetite. This is the specific risk of an all-plant feed, because B12 comes from bacteria and animal proteins.
  • Choline: fatty liver and poor growth.

Now separate these from other causes. Fin erosion also comes from ammonia above 0.1 mg/L and from crowding. Pale gills also come from nitrite poisoning. Dark colour and listlessness point to general stress or infection rather than a specific nutrient. The distinguishing question is whether the sign is spread evenly across the population, which suggests feed, or concentrated in a few weak fish, which suggests infection or bullying.

Worked inspection, Malawi. A farmer at Zomba nets 30 tilapia averaging 120 g. He finds 11 fish with visible fin erosion, 4 with cloudy eyes and 2 with curved spines. That is 37 percent fin erosion and 13 percent cataract, spread across all sizes. He checks his feed: a home mix of 45 percent soybean meal, 30 percent maize, 15 percent rice bran and 10 percent local fish scrap, with no synthetic amino acids. The pattern fits lysine and methionine shortage precisely, because soybean meal is lysine-moderate and methionine-poor and maize contributes almost none.

The fix costs very little. Supplemental DL-methionine at 0.2-0.3 percent of the feed and L-lysine at around 0.3-0.4 percent together cost about USD 0.02-0.05 per kg of feed and return a 2 to 5 times payback through 5-10 percent better growth and an FCR improvement of 0.05-0.10. On 2 tonnes of feed that is USD 40-100 spent for a real and visible change in the next sampling.

Make sampling routine. Once a month, net 30 fish, weigh them as a group to get an average, and inspect every fish against this list. Write the counts down. Trends matter more than single observations.

Lesson 10.3~11 min

Feed Storage, Rancidity and Nutrient Loss in Hot Climates

In this lesson
  • Identify the storage conditions that destroy vitamins and turn feed lipids rancid
  • Calculate the value lost when feed is bought in bulk and stored too long
  • Set a purchase and storage routine that matches your consumption rate

Feed does not stay the feed you bought. In a hot store, it loses potency week by week, and the nutrients that go first are the expensive ones.

What degrades, and how fast:

  • Vitamin C, unstabilised: 50-90 percent lost in 2-4 weeks
  • Vitamin E: 30-50 percent lost in six months
  • Vitamin A: about 50 percent lost in six months
  • Vitamin D3: about 30 percent lost in six months
  • Riboflavin, niacin, biotin: relatively stable, above 80 percent retained

The fats go too. Rice bran is the classic trap: it carries 15-18 percent lipid and oxidises rapidly, so it should be used fresh within two to three weeks unless it has been heat-stabilised, in which case it lasts two to three months for an extra USD 0.02-0.05 per kg. Fish oil and fish meal are also high-risk and need antioxidant protection. Rancid fat is not just a lost nutrient; oxidised lipid actively destroys vitamin E in the same bag and reduces feed intake.

How to tell. A rancid feed smells sharp, like old paint or stale nuts, rather than nutty and clean. It may look darker or oily where the bag touched it. Caked or clumped feed has taken up moisture and is on its way to mould. Fines and dust at the bottom of the bag indicate poor pellet durability; the target durability index is above 80 percent, and below 60 percent means the pellet is breaking into powder that fouls your water instead of feeding your fish.

Worked example, Nigeria. A farmer in Oyo State buys 3 tonnes of feed at once at USD 0.65/kg because the bulk price is 8 percent below the 500 kg price, saving 3,000 x 0.65 x 0.08 = USD 156. But he uses only 500 kg a month, so the last bag is opened six months later. Over that time vitamin E has lost 30-50 percent, vitamin A about 50 percent, and if the vitamin C was unstabilised it is effectively gone from most of the stock. The saving of USD 156 is offset by feeding a nutritionally degraded product for four of the six months. Buying in 1 tonne lots every two months costs him the discount but keeps the feed inside the potency window. This is a judgement call, not an automatic answer: if you can store cool and dry and the feed uses stabilised vitamin C, the bulk discount may well win.

Storage rules that cost almost nothing:

  1. Store on pallets or timber, never on bare concrete or soil, so moisture does not wick into the bags.
  2. Keep bags off walls, with air moving between stacks.
  3. Store in the coolest, darkest room available. Heat and light drive both vitamin loss and fat oxidation.
  4. First in, first out. Mark every bag with the delivery date in marker pen on arrival.
  5. Keep the store rodent-proof and bird-proof. Contaminated feed spreads disease and rodents chew bags open, letting in moisture.
  6. Never store feed next to fuel, pesticide or fertiliser. Feed absorbs odours and chemicals.
  7. Buy in lots that match roughly eight weeks of consumption unless your feed is stabilised and your store is genuinely cool and dry.

One last discipline. Open one bag at a time and keep it closed between feeds. An open bag in a humid store can begin to mould within days, and mouldy feed is not merely wasted, it is the mycotoxin problem covered in the next lesson.

Lesson 10.4~11 min

Mycotoxins in Maize and Groundnut Cake: Protecting Tilapia

In this lesson
  • Rank the main mycotoxins by the risk they pose to tilapia, catfish and carp
  • Apply safe inclusion limits and visual screening to local maize and groundnut cake
  • Choose practical mitigation such as binders, ingredient limits and fermentation

Mycotoxins are fungal poisons that grow in grain and oilseed cake stored warm and damp. Every African fish farmer who buys local maize or groundnut cake is exposed, and Nile tilapia is the most sensitive of the farmed species.

The tolerance limits that matter:

  • Aflatoxin B1: tilapia safe below 10 ppb, and because tilapia is notably sensitive many advisers target below 5 ppb; channel catfish below 20 ppb; carp below 15 ppb. Common in maize and groundnut meal at 5-50 ppb in the tropics. Causes liver tumours, immune suppression and loss of appetite.
  • Fumonisin: tilapia below 50 ppb, channel catfish below 100 ppb, carp below 80 ppb. Found in maize at 15-500 ppb in poor harvest seasons. Causes fatty liver and tissue necrosis.
  • Ochratoxin A: tilapia below 5 ppb, channel catfish below 10 ppb, carp below 8 ppb. Found in barley, wheat and dried products. Damages kidneys and suppresses immunity.
  • Deoxynivalenol (vomitoxin): tilapia below 500 ppb, channel catfish below 1,000 ppb, carp below 800 ppb. Found in wheat and barley. Reduces feed intake and causes weight loss.
  • T-2 toxin: tilapia below 5 ppb. Causes haemorrhage and gut necrosis.

You will rarely be able to test. So manage by source, by inspection and by limits.

Visual and sensory screening. Reject maize with visibly mouldy, discoloured, shrivelled or blue-green dusted kernels. Reject groundnut cake that smells musty or shows greenish-yellow powder. Reject any ingredient that has been rained on and dried slowly. Buy from mills that dry grain properly and store on pallets. The fungus grows where moisture and warmth meet, so grain dried to below about 13 percent moisture and stored dry is far lower risk.

Inclusion limits as a safety measure. Because tolerance is a matter of dose, limiting maize to under 20 percent of the feed reduces the total fumonisin and aflatoxin load even if the batch is imperfect. Adsorbents such as bentonite clay can be added to bind some toxins; the reference data supports bentonite as a mitigation for fumonisin in particular.

Fermentation helps for one toxin specifically: deoxynivalenol falls 30-50 percent with fermentation. Do not assume fermentation solves aflatoxin.

Worked example, Kenya. A farmer at Machakos formulates a tilapia grow-out feed with 30 percent maize bought at a local market after a wet harvest. Several sacks show mould. He has two choices. He can reject the maize, buying clean maize at an assumed USD 0.05/kg more, which on a 2 tonne feed batch containing 600 kg of maize costs 600 x 0.05 = USD 30 extra. Or he can use it and risk immune suppression and appetite loss across a pond worth several hundred dollars. USD 30 to remove the risk from a batch is not a difficult decision, and it is far cheaper than a mortality event.

A second local hazard is cyanobacteria in the pond itself. Over-fertilised, nutrient-heavy ponds grow Microcystis and Anabaena, which release microcystin (liver damage, immune suppression, bioaccumulation) and anatoxin-a (neurological signs and rapid death at high bloom density). Prevention is the same discipline as water quality management: do not over-fertilise, keep N:P at roughly 3:1 to 5:1, aerate, and if a heavy scum-forming bloom appears, isolate the pond, drain and clean it before restocking.

The underlying rule for both feed and water is the same: the cheapest mycotoxin management is not buying the contaminated ingredient in the first place.

Lesson 10.5~11 min

Gut Health: Anti-Nutritional Factors, Fermentation and Probiotics

In this lesson
  • Name the main anti-nutritional factors in plant feed ingredients and their effects
  • Run a simple on-farm fermentation of soybean meal to reduce phytate
  • Judge whether probiotics and immunostimulants are worth their cost on your farm

A plant-based feed is cheaper, but plants defend themselves chemically. Understanding those defences is the difference between a soybean feed that performs at 98 percent of a fish meal feed and one that performs at 75 percent.

The main anti-nutritional factors:

  • Phytate (phytic acid, IP6). Binds phosphorus, calcium, zinc, iron and copper, reducing their availability by 50-80 percent. Soybean meal has 0.6-0.7 percent total phosphorus but only about 30 percent of it is available. Rice bran has 1.5-2.0 percent total P with only 20-30 percent available.
  • Trypsin inhibitors. Block protein digestion. Inactivated by heat, which is why steamed or extruded soybean meal digests 30-40 percent better than raw.
  • Oligosaccharides. Indigestible sugars in soy and pulses. Cause a 5-10 percent FCR penalty in pulse-heavy diets through bloating and reduced digestion.
  • Lectins and other compounds, largely handled by the same heat treatment.

Three practical treatments, in ascending cost:

  1. Fermentation. The smallholder answer. Mix soybean meal with water at 2 parts meal to 1 part water, add a Lactobacillus source, which can be plain yoghurt or whey, seal it in a container or bag for 48-72 hours at ambient temperature between 20 and 30 C, then sun-dry on a tarpaulin. Result: phytate down 30-50 percent, methionine bioavailability up 10-15 percent, better palatability. Cost USD 0.05-0.08 per kg. The fermented product keeps two to three weeks.
  2. Heat treatment or extrusion. Inactivates trypsin inhibitors and gelatinises starch, improving soybean meal digestibility 30-40 percent and reducing the synthetic amino acid you need by around 10 percent. Cost USD 0.05-0.15 per kg. Usually only available if you have or can hire a mill.
  3. Phytase enzyme. Added at 500-1,000 FTU per kg of feed, cost USD 0.03-0.10 per kg, it breaks 40-50 percent of the phytic acid and recovers roughly 30-40 percent of the bound phosphorus. That cuts your supplemental phosphorus need by 30-50 percent and reduces the phosphorus you dump into the pond. Return is about 1.5 to 3 times the cost.

Worked example, Uganda. A farmer at Jinja mixes 1 tonne of feed per month containing 400 kg of soybean meal. He ferments the soy at USD 0.06 per kg of soy: 400 x 0.06 = USD 24 per tonne of feed. The reference data values the combined benefit of fermentation, meaning the phytase-like effect plus reduced oligosaccharides plus better digestibility, at roughly USD 0.10-0.20 per kg. Even at the bottom of that range, applied only to the 400 kg fermented, the benefit is 400 x 0.10 = USD 40 against USD 24 spent. Plus an FCR gain of 0.05-0.10 which, on a tonne of feed, is worth another USD 0.03-0.08 per kg of growth.

Probiotics and immunostimulants sit lower on the priority list. Probiotics such as Bacillus and Lactobacillus cost USD 0.02-0.10 per kg of feed and return roughly 1 to 2 times through 2-5 percent growth gain and reduced disease. Immunostimulants such as beta-glucans cost USD 0.05-0.20 per kg and return about 0.5 to 1 times, meaning their value is in preventing disease rather than adding growth. Medicinal herbs such as turmeric and garlic cost USD 0.01-0.05 per kg with uncertain returns.

The spending order is therefore clear. First the mandatory tier: mineral premix at USD 0.03-0.05, vitamin premix at USD 0.03-0.05 and salt. Then the high-return tier: lysine and methionine at USD 0.02-0.05 with a 2 to 5 times return, and phytase at USD 0.03-0.08 with a 1.5 to 3 times return. Only then consider probiotics. Skip fish oil for grow-out, skip choline and lecithin supplements which the plant meals already supply, and skip specialty amino acids like taurine which are not limiting for freshwater fish.

Lesson 10.6~11 min

A Nutrition-Based Biosecurity and Mortality Reduction Plan

In this lesson
  • Build a written prevention plan covering feed, water, stock movement and records
  • Calculate what a percentage point of mortality costs your farm in USD
  • Set thresholds that trigger a specific action rather than waiting and hoping

Disease treatment on a smallholder fish farm is usually late, expensive and unreliable. Prevention is neither. This lesson assembles everything in this module into a plan you can write on one page and pin to the store wall.

Start by pricing mortality, because that is what makes the plan worth doing. Take a pond you expect to harvest at 500 kg. At an assumed farm-gate price of USD 2.00/kg, which you must replace with your own verified market price, the pond is worth USD 1,000. One percentage point of mortality after the fish have reached full size is 5 kg, or USD 10. A 15 percent mortality event is USD 150 gone, and you have already paid for the feed those fish ate.

Now the plan, in five parts.

1. Feed quality control. Buy from a supplier who will tell you the manufacture date and the vitamin C form. Reject visibly mouldy or musty ingredients. Keep maize below 20 percent of the ration. Store on pallets, first in first out, in lots of roughly eight weeks of consumption. Smell every opened bag. Threshold: any rancid or musty bag is removed from the store, not fed.

2. Nutrient adequacy. Feed the right protein for the stage rather than the highest protein available: tilapia grow-out 25-32 percent, African catfish grow-out 28-35 percent, fingerlings higher. Include a mineral and vitamin premix at about USD 0.06-0.10 per kg combined. Add DL-methionine and L-lysine to any plant-heavy home mix. Threshold: if a 30-fish sample shows more than a few percent with fin erosion or cataracts, review amino acids immediately.

3. Water quality. Ammonia below 0.05 mg/L for tilapia and African catfish, below 0.1 mg/L for channel catfish and carp. Nitrite below 0.5 mg/L for tilapia and carp, below 1 mg/L for channel catfish, with 1-2 g/L salt as insurance. Dissolved oxygen above 5 mg/L for tilapia and channel catfish grow-out, above 4 mg/L preferred for African catfish. Threshold: any reading outside these bands triggers a 20-30 percent feed reduction that same day.

4. Stock and movement control. Buy fingerlings from one known hatchery rather than several. Hold new stock separately where you can. Never move nets, buckets or boots between a sick pond and a healthy one without rinsing and drying them. Remove dead fish daily and bury them away from the water, never back into the pond.

5. Records. One exercise book, one line per pond per day: date, temperature, dawn observation, feed given, any test result, mortalities counted, action taken. Monthly, add the 30-fish sample weight and deficiency counts.

Worked example, Zambia. A farmer near Siavonga has been losing about 18 percent of each cycle across four ponds harvesting 400 kg each, so 1,600 kg total and about 288 kg lost, worth USD 576 at the assumed USD 2.00/kg. She implements the plan. Her costs are: stabilised vitamin C and premix upgrade at USD 0.05 per kg on 3,200 kg of feed, so USD 160; amino acid supplementation at USD 0.03 per kg, so USD 96; ammonia and nitrite test kits at an assumed USD 50; salt for nitrite protection at an assumed USD 40. Total roughly USD 346. If mortality falls from 18 percent to 9 percent she recovers 144 kg, worth USD 288, which does not yet cover the cost. But the same changes also improve FCR by around 0.05-0.10, and on 3,200 kg of feed a 0.1 FCR gain means roughly 160 kg more fish from the same feed, worth another USD 320. Together the plan pays.

That is the honest picture. No single intervention pays for itself dramatically. The combination of vitamin adequacy, amino acid balance, clean ingredients and disciplined feeding does, because each one removes a different cause of the same loss.

1. Why must vitamin C be supplied in every meal?

Vitamin C is water-soluble with no body storage, so a continuous dietary supply is required.

2. Ordinary ascorbic acid in feed stored at tropical ambient temperature loses:

Unstabilised vitamin C degrades very fast, so stabilised forms or fresh feed within eight weeks are needed.

3. A high-lipid broodstock feed requires more vitamin E because:

More PUFA means more peroxidation risk, so broodstock feeds need 200-400 mg/kg rather than 100-200.

4. Adequate dietary vitamin C is associated with roughly:

Fish fed 100-200 mg/kg show about 50 percent fewer infections and 10-20 percent higher broodstock fecundity.

5. For a farmer mixing feed at home, the highest-value single purchase is usually:

A complete vitamin premix is cheap per kg of finished feed and prevents the deficiencies that cause most preventable losses.

6. Cataracts and loss of skin colour in tilapia most specifically suggest a deficiency of:

Methionine deficiency causes cataracts and depigmentation, and methionine is the amino acid lowest in soybean meal.

7. Scoliosis and lordosis in fish point to a shortage of:

Tryptophan deficiency causes skeletal deformities and hyperexcitability, although vitamin C deficiency can also affect the skeleton.

8. Why can fish not rely on sunlight for vitamin D?

Unlike land animals, fish have no functional skin synthesis pathway, so dietary vitamin D3 is obligatory.

9. An all-plant feed with no animal protein carries a particular risk of deficiency in:

B12 is produced by bacteria and found in animal proteins and fish meal, so plant-only diets need it supplemented.

10. A deficiency sign spread evenly across all sizes of fish in a sample most likely indicates:

Even distribution across the population points to something every fish is exposed to, which is usually the feed.

11. Rice bran should normally be used within:

Its 15-18 percent lipid oxidises quickly; heat-stabilised rice bran lasts 2-3 months for a small extra cost.

12. A sharp, paint-like smell from a feed bag indicates:

Oxidised lipid smells sharp rather than nutty, and it destroys vitamin E in the same bag while reducing intake.

13. A pellet durability index below 60 percent means:

Low durability produces dust and fines, wasting feed and degrading water quality.

14. Storing feed bags directly on a concrete floor is a problem because:

Ground moisture moves into the feed, leading to caking, mould and mycotoxin risk.

15. A bulk purchase discount of 8 percent may still be a bad deal if:

Nutrient degradation over a long storage period can cost more than the discount saves.

16. Which farmed species is most sensitive to aflatoxin B1?

Tilapia is notably susceptible, with a safe limit below 10 ppb and a practical target below 5 ppb.

17. Fumonisin contamination is most associated with:

Fumonisin occurs in maize at 15-500 ppb in poor harvest seasons, which is why maize is limited to under 20 percent of feed.

18. Fermentation is documented to reduce which mycotoxin by 30-50 percent?

DON falls 30-50 percent with fermentation; other toxins are not reliably reduced this way.

19. Microcystin in a pond comes from:

Microcystis blooms in over-fertilised ponds release microcystin, causing liver damage and immune suppression.

20. The cheapest mycotoxin control on a smallholder farm is:

Sourcing and visual screening cost nothing and remove most of the risk before it enters the feed.

21. Phytate reduces the availability of minerals by roughly:

Phytic acid binds P, Ca, Zn, Fe and Cu, cutting availability by 50-80 percent in plant-based feeds.

22. On-farm fermentation of soybean meal typically runs for:

48-72 hours with a Lactobacillus source at ambient 20-30 C reduces phytate 30-50 percent.

23. Heat treatment of soybean meal is important mainly because it:

Heat destroys trypsin inhibitors and gelatinises starch, improving digestibility by 30-40 percent.

24. Which supplement gives the highest documented return per dollar for a smallholder?

Amino acid balancing costs USD 0.02-0.05 per kg and returns 2 to 5 times through growth and FCR gains.

25. Which of these is listed as poor value in a smallholder grow-out feed?

Fish oil is recommended only for broodstock; grow-out should use vegetable oil blends instead.

26. On a pond expected to harvest 500 kg at an assumed USD 2.00/kg, one percentage point of late mortality costs about:

One percent of 500 kg is 5 kg, and 5 x USD 2.00 is USD 10, plus the feed those fish already consumed.

27. Which is the correct grow-out crude protein range for Nile tilapia?

25-32 percent is sufficient at grow-out; higher protein wastes money and increases ammonia output.

28. Dead fish should be:

Daily removal and burial away from water prevents disease spread and ammonia loading.

29. A single reading of ammonia above the safe threshold should trigger:

Cutting the ration reduces both the nitrogen input and the oxygen demand while you address the cause.

30. The honest case for a nutrition-based prevention plan is that:

Each measure removes a different cause of loss; together the mortality reduction plus an FCR gain of 0.05-0.10 makes the economics work.

Module 10 capstone

Run a feed-safety and fish-health audit on your own farm over three weeks. Photograph or sketch your feed store and record its temperature at midday, the date printed on your feed, how long a bag typically lasts you, and whether any bag shows caking, mould or a rancid smell. Take a sample of at least 30 fish from one pond and examine every one for the specific signs taught in this module: fin erosion, cataract or eye opacity, skeletal curvature, pale gills, dark colour and body condition. Record the count of each sign. Then price a full corrective plan in USD: cost of a stabilised vitamin C feed or premix, cost of improving your store (pallets, ventilation, smaller purchase lots), cost of replacing any suspect maize or groundnut cake, and cost of a fermentation setup for your plant ingredients. Present a single page listing what you found, what it is most likely costing you in growth and mortality, and which two corrective actions give the best return for the money you actually have.
