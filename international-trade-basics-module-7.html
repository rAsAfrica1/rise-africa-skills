Lessons

1. Cash in Advance

Cash in advance means the buyer pays before the seller ships. For an African exporter, this is the safest method: you get money upfront, no risk of non-payment. But many international buyers, especially in Europe or North America, will refuse. They don't want to tie up cash or trust a new supplier. So cash in advance is usually only for small orders, first-time transactions, or when the buyer desperately needs your product.

How do you get paid? Common tools: international wire transfer (SWIFT), mobile money like M-Pesa for regional trade, or online platforms like Payoneer or Wise. For example, a Kenyan coffee exporter might ask a German buyer for 100% advance on a $5,000 sample order. The buyer sends via SWIFT to the exporter's bank in Nairobi. Fees: $20-$50 per wire, plus currency conversion. Mobile money is cheaper for small amounts but limited to about $5,000 per transaction in many countries.

To protect yourself, always request payment in a hard currency like USD or EUR to avoid inflation risk. Use a bank that can confirm receipt quickly. Never ship until funds clearnot just show in your account. Once you have a track record, you can offer better terms like 50% advance, 50% on shipment. But for now, cash in advance builds your cash flow and eliminates risk. Just be prepared for buyers to negotiate. If they insist on credit, move to a letter of credit or documentary collection.

2. Open Account

Open account means you ship first and get paid later, typically 30, 60, or 90 days after delivery. It's the most competitive terms you can offer, but also the riskiest. As an African exporter, you're essentially giving your buyer a free loan. If they don't pay, you have little recourse except legal action, which is expensive and slow across borders.

Why would you ever agree? Because buyers demand it. In many industriestextiles, agriculture, consumer goodsopen account is standard. If you refuse, a competitor from Asia or Europe will offer it. For example, a Ghanaian cocoa exporter might sell to a Dutch trader on 60-day open account. The exporter ships 20 tons, worth $60,000, and waits two months. During that time, they still need to pay farmers, transport, and staff. Cash flow can kill you.

To manage risk, use trade credit insurance. Organizations like ATI (African Trade & Investment Development Insurance) or Euler Hermes cover up to 90% of losses if the buyer defaults. Cost: 0.3% to 1% of invoice value. Also, run credit checks on buyers through Dun & Bradstreet or local chambers of commerce. Start with small orders and short terms. Use factoring: sell your invoices to a bank at a discount (e.g., 2-5% per month) to get cash now. For intra-African trade, the AfCFTA might help, but enforcement is still weak. Open account works best with trusted, long-term buyers.

3. Documentary Collections: D/P and D/A

Documentary collections are a middle ground between cash in advance and open account. Your bank sends shipping documents to the buyer's bank, which releases them only when the buyer pays (D/P) or accepts a time draft (D/A). D/P means Documents against Payment: the buyer must pay immediately to get the documents. D/A means Documents against Acceptance: the buyer signs a promise to pay in 30, 60, or 90 days.

For an African exporter, D/P is safer. Example: A Nigerian exporter sells $20,000 of sesame seeds to a Chinese buyer. The exporter ships, then gives documents (bill of lading, invoice, packing list) to their bank in Lagos. That bank sends them to the buyer's bank in Shanghai. The Chinese bank releases documents only after the buyer pays $20,000. Then the buyer can claim the goods. If the buyer refuses, the goods sit at the port, and the exporter must find a new buyer or pay return freight. D/A is riskier: the buyer gets goods before paying, so you're basically giving credit. But D/A can help you compete.

Costs: bank charges of $100-$300 per collection, plus courier fees. The main pitfall: banks don't guarantee payment. They only facilitate. If the buyer defaults, you must sue. Also, documents must be perfectany discrepancy can delay release. Always use a reliable bank like Ecobank, Standard Bank, or Access Bank that has correspondent relationships. For intra-African trade, the Pan-African Payment and Settlement System (PAPSS) is reducing costs, but documentary collections still rely on traditional banking.

4. Letters of Credit: Process and Pitfalls

A letter of credit (LC) is a bank's promise to pay you, the exporter, as long as you present exactly the right documents. The buyer's bank issues the LC, and your bank advises it. This reduces risk for both sides: you know you'll get paid if you comply; the buyer knows they'll get goods if documents are correct.

Process: 1) Buyer and seller agree on LC terms in the sales contract. 2) Buyer's bank issues LC and sends to seller's bank. 3) Seller ships goods and prepares documents (bill of lading, commercial invoice, packing list, certificate of origin, inspection certificate). 4) Seller presents documents to their bank. 5) Seller's bank checks documents and forwards to buyer's bank. 6) Buyer's bank pays seller's bank (at sight or after 30/60/90 days). 7) Buyer's bank releases documents to buyer, who claims goods.

Pitfalls: LC is all about documents, not goods. If there's a typosay, "sesame seeds" vs. "sesame seed"the bank can reject. You then fix and resubmit, paying fees. Common discrepancies: late shipment, missing signatures, inconsistent descriptions. Costs: 1-2% of LC value, plus amendment fees ($50-$100 each). For an African exporter, use LCs for large orders ($50,000+). Example: A Kenyan flower exporter sells to a UK supermarket on a 60-day LC. The exporter must ship exactly on time and present documents within 21 days. If not, the supermarket can refuse. Always ask for an irrevocable, confirmed LC from a reputable bank. Confirmation adds cost but protects against buyer's bank or country risk.

5. Bank Guarantees and Standby Letters of Credit

Bank guarantees and standby letters of credit (SBLCs) are similar: they are promises by a bank to pay if you fail to meet a contractual obligation. They're used when a buyer or seller demands extra security. For African exporters, you might need a performance guarantee to win a big contract, or you might receive an SBLC from a buyer as payment security.

Example: A South African construction company bids for a road project in Zambia. The Zambian government requires a 10% performance guaranteesay, $500,000. The company's bank issues a guarantee: if the company fails to complete the road, the bank pays the government $500,000. The company pays a fee (1-3% per year) and may need to pledge collateral. Similarly, an SBLC can secure payment: a buyer in the US issues an SBLC to a Ghanaian cocoa exporter. If the buyer doesn't pay after 60 days, the exporter can claim against the SBLC.

Key differences: Bank guarantees are common in Africa and Europe; SBLCs are more common in the US. Both are on-demand: the beneficiary can claim without proving default, though this can be challenged in court. Pitfalls: If you're the exporter and you receive an SBLC, check that it's issued by a reputable bank and is irrevocable. If you're providing a guarantee, beware of unfair callingthe buyer might claim even if you performed. Always use clear, specific terms: amount, expiry, conditions. For intra-African trade, the African Development Bank and Afreximbank support guarantee facilities. Costs: 1-5% annually. Don't confuse these with insurance; they are bank promises, not insurance policies.

6. Managing Foreign Exchange Risk

Foreign exchange (FX) risk is the danger that currency fluctuations wipe out your profit. As an African exporter, you often get paid in USD or EUR but incur costs in local currency. If the local currency strengthens, your export earnings buy less. If it weakens, you gainbut volatility makes planning hard.

Example: A Tanzanian coffee exporter sells 10 tons for $50,000, payable in 90 days. Today, the exchange rate is 2,300 TZS/USD, so you expect 115 million TZS. But in 90 days, the rate might be 2,200 TZS/USD, giving you only 110 million TZSa 5 million loss. To manage this, use forward contracts. Your bank (e.g., CRDB, NMB) can lock in a rate today for a future date. If the rate moves against you, the bank covers the difference. Cost: a small premium or spread. For example, a 90-day forward might cost 1-2% of the amount.

Other tools: currency options give you the right (not obligation) to exchange at a set rate, but cost more (2-5%). Natural hedging: match your currency inflows and outflows. If you buy equipment in USD, your USD earnings offset that. For small exporters, mobile money platforms like M-Pesa now offer FX hedging in some corridors. Always invoice in a stable currency. Avoid currencies with high inflation, like the Nigerian naira or Zimbabwean dollar. For intra-African trade, the PAPSS system reduces FX exposure by settling in local currencies. But for now, forwards are your best bet. Talk to your bank earlydon't wait until payment is due.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: International Payment Methods for African Exporters and Importers

This practical reference consolidates the six core payment methods covered in this module into a single decision-support tool. It is written for African agribusiness managers, trade finance officers, and export desk staff who must choose, negotiate, and document payment terms under real commercial pressure. The guidance assumes you are dealing with cross-border transactions where counterparty trust, currency convertibility, and documentary compliance all carry material risk.

African trade finance operates in a distinctive environment. Intra-African trade is dominated by informal and semi-formal channels, while extra-African trade relies heavily on correspondent banking relationships that are increasingly subject to de-risking. According to the African Development Bank, the continent's trade finance gap is estimated at USD 81 billion, with SMEs and agribusinesses disproportionately affected. Choosing the right payment instrument is therefore not merely a commercial preference it is a risk management decision that determines whether you get paid at all.

Foreign Exchange Risk Management: Tools and Considerations

African currencies are among the most volatile in the world. The Nigerian naira, Kenyan shilling, Ghanaian cedi, and South African rand have all experienced significant depreciation against hard currencies in recent years. For agribusiness exporters who invoice in USD or EUR but incur costs in local currency, FX risk can erode margins faster than any other single factor.

CRITICAL SAFETY WARNING: Documentary Discrepancies and Fraud Risk

Under a Letter of Credit, banks pay against documents, not goods. A single discrepancy a misspelled name, a missing signature, a date outside the permitted range can cause the bank to refuse payment. In African trade corridors, document discrepancies are the leading cause of LC rejection, affecting an estimated 5070% of first presentations according to ICC data. Always have your documents checked by a trade finance specialist before presentation. Never ship goods against an unconfirmed LC from a bank you have not verified. Never accept an LC issued by a bank in a jurisdiction with weak enforcement. Never release original documents to a buyer without ensuring payment or a bank guarantee is in place. If you are an importer, never pay against a copy of a bill of lading insist on original documents or an electronic bill of lading through a trusted platform.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Assess counterparty risk first. Before choosing a payment method, verify the buyer's or seller's registration, trade references, and bank standing. Use the payment method as a risk mitigation tool, not a substitute for due diligence.
  2. Match the instrument to the relationship. Use Cash in Advance for new or high-risk counterparties; move to Documentary Collections or LC as trust builds; reserve Open Account for established, repeat relationships with strong credit history.
  3. Confirm the LC is confirmed and irrevocable. An unconfirmed LC from a bank in a weak jurisdiction offers little protection. Insist on confirmation from a prime bank or a confirmed LC from a bank with a correspondent relationship in your country.
  4. Check every document against LC terms before shipment. Create a document checklist from the LC and verify each item names, dates, amounts, ports, descriptions before presenting to the bank. One discrepancy can delay payment by weeks.
  5. Understand your FX exposure and hedge it. If you invoice in USD but pay costs in local currency, quantify your exposure and use forward contracts or natural hedges. Do not assume the exchange rate will move in your favour.
  6. Know your bank's correspondent network. African banks rely on correspondent relationships for USD and EUR clearing. Confirm your bank has a reliable correspondent and that your payment corridor is not subject to de-risking or sanctions screening delays.
  7. Document everything and keep records. Retain copies of all LC documents, collection instructions, bank guarantees, and correspondence for at least five years. In disputes, documentary evidence is your primary protection.
  8. Use bank guarantees for performance obligations. When you advance payment or require performance, insist on a bank guarantee or standby LC from a reputable bank. Do not rely on personal guarantees or unsecured promises in cross-border trade.
