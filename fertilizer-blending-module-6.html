1. Receiving and Storage of Raw Materials

When raw materials arrive at your blending site, you must inspect them before unloading. Check the delivery truck for cleanliness and any signs of moisture. Ask the driver for the delivery note and weighbridge ticket. Weigh the truck on a platform scale if available; if not, use a weighbridge at a nearby depot. Record the gross weight, tare weight, and net weight of each material. For example, a 30-ton truck carrying urea should have a net weight of about 30,000 kg. If the weight is off by more than 1%, reject the load or call your supplier.

After weighing, take a sample from at least five random spots in the load using a spear sampler. Mix these samples together and test for moisture, granule size, and nutrient content if you have a test kit. For urea, moisture should be below 0.5%. For DAP, granule size should be 2-4 mm. If the material is lumpy or wet, do not store itâit will clog your blender. Store only dry, free-flowing material.

Store raw materials in a dry, well-ventilated shed with a concrete floor. Keep bags on pallets at least 10 cm off the floor to prevent moisture from the ground. Stack bags no more than 10 high to avoid crushing. Leave a 1-meter gap between stacks and the wall for inspection and air circulation. Separate different materials by at least 2 meters to prevent cross-contamination. For example, keep urea away from ammonium sulphate because they react and release moisture.

Label every stack clearly with the material name, date received, and supplier. Use a permanent marker on a board or write directly on the bags. Keep a logbook recording all deliveries: date, supplier, material, weight, moisture, and any remarks. This helps you trace problems later. If you store in bulk, use a covered hopper or silo with a moisture-proof liner. Never store fertilizer in the open sun or rainâit will cake and become unusable.

2. Material Handling and Transport

Moving raw materials from storage to the blender requires care to avoid spills, dust, and segregation. For small-scale operations, use manual handling: a wheelbarrow, a hand truck, or a sack trolley. For 50-kg bags, two workers can lift and carry, but use a trolley to prevent back injury. For larger operations, use a front-end loader or a conveyor belt. A conveyor belt with a 500 mm width can move 20-30 tons per hour. Adjust the belt speed to match your blending rate.

When using a loader, avoid dropping materials from a height. Dropping causes dust and separates particles by size. Instead, lower the bucket close to the pile and tip gently. For conveyor belts, install a skirt rubber at the transfer points to contain dust. Use a dust mask (N95 or equivalent) and safety goggles when handling dusty materials like phosphate rock or limestone. Wear gloves to protect your skin from urea and ammonium nitrate, which can cause irritation.

Transport materials in clean, dry containers. Never use the same wheelbarrow for different materials without cleaning it first. A 5-kg residue of DAP in a wheelbarrow can contaminate 500 kg of urea. Use color-coded containers: red for nitrogen, blue for phosphorus, green for potassium. This simple system prevents mistakes. If you use a tractor-trailer, line the bed with a tarpaulin to prevent leaks and moisture.

Plan your route to minimize travel distance. Arrange storage so that the most-used materials are closest to the blender. For example, if you blend 70% urea, 20% DAP, and 10% MOP, keep urea nearest. Use a flow chart on the wall showing the path from storage to weighing to mixing. Train workers to follow the same path every time. This reduces time and errors. Never allow workers to eat, drink, or smoke in the handling areaâfertilizer dust can be harmful if ingested.

3. Sequencing of Ingredient Addition

The order in which you add ingredients affects blend quality and mixing time. Always start with the largest quantity ingredient. For a typical NPK blend, urea is often the largest component (e.g., 400 kg), followed by DAP (200 kg), MOP (150 kg), and fillers (250 kg). Add urea first to the mixer. Then add DAP. Then add MOP. Finally add fillers and micronutrients. This sequence prevents light materials from floating on top and heavy materials from settling at the bottom.

If you add small amounts of micronutrients (e.g., zinc sulphate or borax), pre-mix them with a small portion of filler or urea before adding to the main batch. For example, mix 5 kg of zinc sulphate with 20 kg of urea in a separate bucket, then add to the mixer. This ensures even distribution. Never add micronutrients directly to the top of a full mixerâthey will stay in one spot.

For materials that tend to clump, like ammonium sulphate, add them after the urea but before the DAP. The urea granules help break up clumps as they tumble. If you use a ribbon blender, add materials while the ribbon is turning. This prevents the mixer from stalling. For a horizontal paddle mixer, start the mixer, then add urea, then DAP, then MOP, then fillers. Wait 30 seconds between each addition to let the previous material spread.

Record the sequence on a whiteboard next to the mixer. Write the order: 1. Urea, 2. DAP, 3. MOP, 4. Filler, 5. Micronutrients. Use a checklist and tick each step. If you are blending organic materials like compost or poultry manure, add them last because they are bulky and light. They will coat the granules and reduce dust. Never add liquids (like water or molasses) until all dry ingredients are mixedâliquids should be sprayed slowly while the mixer runs.

4. Mixing Time and Speed Optimization

Mixing time and speed determine whether your blend is uniform. For a horizontal ribbon blender, run at 20-30 rpm. For a vertical screw mixer, run at 200-300 rpm. For a rotary drum mixer, run at 15-20 rpm. Always check the manufacturer's manual. If you run too fast, you may crush granules and create dust. If you run too slow, materials will not mix. A good test: after 3 minutes of mixing, take 10 samples from different spots in the mixer. Test each sample for nitrogen content. If the difference between highest and lowest is more than 5%, mix longer.

For most dry blends, 3-5 minutes of mixing is enough. For blends with micronutrients at low concentrations (e.g., 0.5% zinc), mix for 5-7 minutes. For organic blends with bulky materials, mix for 7-10 minutes. Do not over-mixâafter 10 minutes, granules may break down and segregate. Use a timer. Set it for the required time and stop the mixer when it rings. Never rely on guesswork.

Speed optimization also depends on fill level. Do not fill the mixer more than 70% of its volume. For a 1-ton mixer, use 700 kg of material. Overfilling reduces mixing efficiency and can burn out the motor. Underfilling (less than 30%) also reduces mixing because materials just slide. Aim for 50-70% fill.

Check mixer blades regularly. Worn blades reduce mixing efficiency. Replace ribbon blades when they are worn by more than 5 mm. For paddle mixers, replace paddles when they are worn by 10 mm. Lubricate bearings every 50 hours of operation. Keep a logbook: date, batch number, mixing time, speed, and any issues. If you notice lumps in the final blend, increase mixing time by 1 minute and check for moisture in raw materials.

5. Discharge and Packaging

After mixing, discharge the blend quickly to prevent segregation. Open the discharge gate fully. For a ribbon blender, the gate is at the bottom. For a rotary drum, tilt the drum or use a discharge chute. Let the material flow into a hopper or directly into bags. Do not let the blend sit in the mixer for more than 5 minutes after mixingâvibration can cause heavier particles to sink. If you must wait, run the mixer at low speed (10 rpm) to keep materials suspended.

For packaging, use woven polypropylene bags with a liner if the fertilizer is hygroscopic (like urea). Common bag sizes are 25 kg, 50 kg, and 100 kg. For smallholder farmers, 25-kg bags are easier to handle. Fill bags using a manual scale or an automatic weighing machine. For manual filling, use a funnel and a scale. Weigh each bag to within Â±0.5% of the target weight. For a 50-kg bag, that means 49.75 to 50.25 kg. Seal bags with a sewing machine or a heat sealer. For sewing, use a portable bag closer with nylon thread. Stitch at least 5 cm from the top.

Label each bag with the blend formula (e.g., 20-10-10), net weight, production date, and your company name. Use a permanent marker or printed labels. Store filled bags on pallets in a dry area. Stack no more than 10 bags high. Keep bags away from walls and floors. For transport, load bags onto a truck with a tarpaulin cover. Do not throw bagsâdropping can cause breakage and dust.

If you produce bulk blends for tractor-mounted spreaders, load directly into a spreader or a bulk truck. Use a conveyor or a front-end loader. Cover the load with a tarpaulin during transport. Clean the discharge area after each batch. Sweep up spilled fertilizer and return it to the mixer if it is clean. Never mix spilled material with a different formulaâit will contaminate the next batch.

6. Housekeeping and Dust Control

Dust is the biggest housekeeping challenge in fertilizer blending. It affects worker health, causes equipment failure, and contaminates products. Control dust at the source. Install dust extraction hoods over the mixer, discharge point, and bagging station. Connect them to a cyclone or baghouse filter. For small operations, use a simple fan and ducting to pull dust away from workers. Wear N95 dust masks, goggles, and coveralls. Wash hands and face after work.

Clean up spills immediately. Use a broom and dustpan, not a compressed air hoseâcompressed air spreads dust. For fine dust, use a vacuum with a HEPA filter. Sweep floors at least twice per day: once in the morning and once after the last batch. Use a damp mop for fine dust. Keep a dedicated broom and dustpan for each material area to avoid cross-contamination. For example, a broom used for urea should not be used for DAP.

Store raw materials and finished bags in a way that minimizes dust. Keep bags sealed. Do not tear bags openâcut them neatly with a knife. Use a bag opener that catches the dust. For bulk materials, cover piles with tarpaulins. Keep the storage area well-ventilated with fans or open windows. If you use a shed, install ridge vents to let hot air and dust escape.

Maintain equipment regularly. Check for leaks in ducting and seals on mixers. Replace worn gaskets. Lubricate bearings to prevent overheating, which can ignite dust. Keep a fire extinguisher (dry chemical, ABC type) near the blending area. Train all workers on housekeeping procedures. Create a cleaning schedule: daily, weekly, and monthly tasks. For example, daily: sweep floors, empty dust bins. Weekly: clean mixer interior, check filters. Monthly: inspect ducting, replace filters. Post the schedule on the wall. A clean site is a safe site and produces better fertilizer.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Blending Process Operations

This practical reference is written for African fertilizer blenders, toll manufacturers, and agribusiness operators who need to run a bulk blending plant profitably. Whether you operate a 5 t/hr batch blender in Nakuru, a 20 t/hr continuous line in Kaduna, or a mobile blending unit serving cooperatives in Zambia's Copperbelt, the same operational fundamentals determine your margins: raw material quality in, dust and losses controlled, correct sequencing, correct mixing time, and clean, well-labelled bags out. This module gives you the numbers, tolerances, and checklists you need on the plant floor.

1. Receiving and Storage of Raw Materials

Every tonne of raw material you receive carries moisture, caking risk, and quality variability. In humid tropical and coastal markets â Mombasa, Lagos, Dar es Salaam, Beira â hygroscopic materials such as urea, ammonium nitrate, and some NPK compounds absorb moisture rapidly. Store them in a dry, ventilated, concrete-floored warehouse with pallets at least 100 mm off the floor and 500 mm off walls. Keep urea and ammonium nitrate in separate, clearly signed bays. Never stack more than the safe bag height (typically 10â12 bags for 50 kg woven PP bags, or per manufacturer guidance).

On receipt, record supplier, batch number, tonnage, moisture (target <0.5% for most prills), and granule size distribution. Reject or segregate caked, wet, or off-spec material immediately â one bad batch can ruin an entire day's blend and trigger customer claims. In West Africa, where imported raw materials often arrive via Lagos or Tema with long transit times, inspect every truck before offloading.

2. Material Handling and Transport

Use front-end loaders with clean buckets, enclosed screw conveyors, or belt conveyors with skirtboards and covers. Open belts and bucket elevators generate dust and segregate particles by size â a hidden cause of off-spec blends. For urea and ammonium nitrate, use stainless or coated mild steel contact surfaces to limit corrosion and caking. Keep transfer points short, minimize free-fall height (ideally <500 mm drop), and fit dust socks or local extraction at every transfer.

In many African plants, manual bag handling still dominates. If so, enforce 25 kg or 50 kg bag limits, provide trolleys and pallet jacks, and rotate workers to reduce fatigue injuries. Where diesel or grid power is unreliable, design gravity-fed or low-power conveying routes so production continues during outages.

3. Sequencing of Ingredient Addition

Sequence matters. A practical rule for bulk blending: add the largest-volume, densest, and least hygroscopic material first, then the minor ingredients, then the most hygroscopic or sticky material last. This reduces segregation and coating loss. For a typical NPK blend:

  1. Load filler or bulk carrier (e.g., limestone, dolomite) first.
  2. Add DAP, MAP, or TSP.
  3. Add MOP (potassium chloride) or SOP.
  4. Add urea or ammonium sulphate.
  5. Add micronutrients, coated products, or biologicals last.
  6. Never mix urea directly with ammonium nitrate or with freshly limed material without expert guidance.

4. Mixing Time and Speed Optimization

Under-mixing gives nutrient variability; over-mixing causes segregation and dust. For a typical horizontal ribbon or paddle blender, mix 2â4 minutes at 20â30 rpm. For vertical auger blenders, 3â6 minutes. Test your coefficient of variation (CV) on N, P, and K at ten points in the discharge â target CV <5% for N and K, <10% for P. If CV is high, increase time in 30-second increments and retest; do not simply run longer, as segregation can worsen beyond the optimum.

5. Discharge and Packaging

Discharge quickly and completely. Standing blended fertilizer segregates, especially when particle size and density differ between urea and phosphate granules. Bag within 15â30 minutes of mixing. Use calibrated net weighers or volumetric fillers with checkweighing. Label every bag with product name, grade (e.g., 23:10:5+4S), net weight, batch number, production date, and manufacturer. In markets regulated by bodies such as the Fertilizer and Pesticide Authority equivalents â KEPHIS in Kenya, NAFDAC in Nigeria, or the Zambia Compulsory Standards Agency â incorrect labelling can stop sales.

6. Housekeeping and Dust Control

Dust is the biggest hidden cost and the biggest safety risk in blending. Fertilizer dust is combustible, irritant, and corrosive. Use local exhaust ventilation at transfer points, weigh hoppers, and bagging stations. Fit bag filters with regular cleaning schedules. Sweep or vacuum â never dry-sweep with compressed air. Keep floors dry; wet fertilizer is slippery and corrosive.

â  SAFETY WARNING â Dust Explosion and Chemical Hazard

Ammonium nitrate, urea dust, and many blended fertilizers can form explosive dust clouds in enclosed spaces. Never allow dust accumulation on beams, ledges, or equipment. Prohibit smoking, welding, and open flames in storage and blending areas. Ground and bond all conductive equipment. Wear dust masks (N95 or better), sealed goggles, gloves, and long sleeves. Ammonium nitrate must be stored separately from fuels, oils, and organic materials. In case of fire, evacuate and call emergency services â do not attempt to fight an ammonium nitrate fire with water jets in enclosed spaces. Follow national regulations (e.g., Kenya OSHA 2007, Nigeria FEPA, South Africa SANS 10234).
