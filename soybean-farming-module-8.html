1. Major Insect Pests: Aphids, Beetles, Caterpillars

Soybean in Africa is attacked by three main groups of insects: aphids, beetles, and caterpillars. Aphids are tiny (1-3 mm), soft-bodied, and cluster on young leaves and stems. They suck sap, causing leaves to curl, yellow, and stunt growth. They also transmit viruses. Check weekly from 2 weeks after planting. A heavy infestation is when you find 50 or more aphids per plant on 20% of plants. In cool highlands, aphids can wipe out 40% of yield if untreated.

Beetles include blister beetles, bean leaf beetles, and flea beetles. Blister beetles are 1-2 cm long, black or grey with stripes. They chew leaves and flowers. Bean leaf beetles are red or yellow with black spots and chew round holes in leaves. Flea beetles are small (2-3 mm) and jump when disturbed; they cause shot-hole damage. A threshold for bean leaf beetles is 5 beetles per plant during pod fill. Blister beetles also release a blistering chemical, so wear gloves when handling them.

Caterpillars are the larvae of moths and butterflies. The most damaging are pod borers (Helicoverpa and Spodoptera species), armyworms, and loopers. They chew leaves, bore into pods, and eat seeds. Armyworms march in groups and can defoliate a field in 2 days. Scout by shaking plants over a white cloth or bucket. Threshold: 2-3 large caterpillars per row meter, or 20% defoliation before flowering. Pod borers are worse after flowering; 1 larva per plant can reduce yield by 10%.

In Africa, these pests are worse in warm, dry spells and where soybeans follow soybeans. Rotate with maize or sorghum to break cycles. Plant early to avoid peak pest populations. Keep field edges clean because pests often start there. Learn to identify each pest correctly; a photo guide from your extension officer helps. Early action saves the crop.

2. Scouting Techniques and Economic Thresholds

Scouting means walking your soybean field regularly to count pests and damage. Do it at least once a week from emergence to pod fill. Walk in a zigzag or W pattern, stopping at 10 spots per hectare. At each spot, inspect 5 plants. Look at the whole plant: upper and lower leaves, stems, flowers, and pods. Use a hand lens (10x) to see small pests like aphids and mites. Carry a notebook and record date, pest, number per plant, and damage level.

For aphids, count aphids on 3 leaves per plant (top, middle, bottom). Economic threshold: 50 aphids per leaf on 20% of plants. For beetles, count beetles per plant. Threshold for bean leaf beetle: 5 per plant during pod fill. For caterpillars, shake plants over a white cloth (1m x 1m) and count dislodged larvae. Threshold: 2-3 large caterpillars per row meter, or 20% defoliation before flowering. For pod borers, inspect 20 pods per plant; threshold is 1 larva per plant or 5% pod damage.

Economic threshold (ET) is the pest level at which the cost of control equals the value of yield loss. If pest numbers are below ET, do not spray; you save money and protect beneficial insects. If above ET, act immediately. Example: if a farmer expects 2 tonnes per hectare and soybeans sell for 500 USD per tonne, the crop is worth 1000 USD. If caterpillars at 3 per meter cause 10% loss (100 USD), and spraying costs 30 USD, then spraying is profitable. But if pest numbers cause only 2% loss (20 USD), spraying loses money.

Keep scouting records to see trends. After rain, pest numbers often drop. After dry spells, they rise. Share records with neighbours to warn them. Train two people per farm to scout so you always have data. Use a smartphone app like 'Pest Scout' if available, or a simple notebook. Scouting takes 30-60 minutes per hectare but prevents crop loss.

3. Biological Control and Beneficial Insects

Biological control uses living organisms to eat or kill pests. In Africa, many natural enemies already exist in soybean fields. Protect them. The main beneficial insects are ladybird beetles (ladybugs), lacewings, hoverflies, parasitic wasps, and spiders. Ladybird beetles eat aphids: one larva can eat 50 aphids per day. Lacewing larvae eat aphids, caterpillars, and mites. Parasitic wasps lay eggs inside caterpillars; the wasp larvae kill the caterpillar from inside. Spiders eat beetles and moths.

To encourage beneficial insects, avoid broad-spectrum insecticides like lambda-cyhalothrin, which kill both pests and beneficials. If you must spray, use selective insecticides like Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) for caterpillars, or insecticidal soap for aphids. These spare most beneficials. Plant flowering plants around your field, such as sunflower, cowpea, and marigold. These provide nectar and pollen for adult beneficials. Leave some wild grasses at field edges; they host alternative prey and shelter.

You can also buy beneficial insects. In Kenya, South Africa, and Nigeria, companies sell ladybird beetles and parasitic wasps for aphid and caterpillar control. Release them when pest numbers are low, early in the morning or evening. One release of 10,000 ladybirds per hectare can control aphids for 2-3 weeks. For caterpillars, release Trichogramma wasps (egg parasitoids) at 50,000 per hectare weekly for 3 weeks. These wasps kill pest eggs before they hatch.

Another biological control is using bacteria and fungi. Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) is a bacterium that kills caterpillar larvae when eaten. Mix 1-2 grams per litre of water and spray on leaves. It is safe for humans, animals, and beneficials. Beauveria bassiana is a fungus that infects aphids and beetles; mix 2 grams per litre and spray. These biopesticides work best when applied in the evening, as sunlight kills them. Store them in a cool, dark place. Use within 6 months. Always check expiry date.

Biological control is cheap, safe, and sustainable. But it works slower than chemicals. Start early and monitor. Combine with other methods like resistant varieties and crop rotation.

4. Chemical Insecticide Use and Safety

Chemical insecticides are powerful but dangerous. Use them only when pest numbers are above economic threshold and other methods have failed. Always read the label before buying. Choose an insecticide registered for soybean in your country. Common ones include lambda-cyhalothrin (for beetles and caterpillars), imidacloprid (for aphids), and chlorantraniliprole (for pod borers). Never use banned chemicals like DDT, lindane, or monocrotophos.

Wear protective clothing: long-sleeved shirt, long trousers, rubber boots, gloves, goggles, and a face mask. Mix chemicals outdoors, away from children and animals. Use a clean bucket and a measuring cup. Follow the label rate exactly. For example, lambda-cyhalothrin 5% EC is mixed at 10-20 ml per 20 litres of water. Too little won't kill pests; too much wastes money and harms the crop. Spray in the early morning or late evening when bees are not flying. Never spray on windy days or before rain.

Calibrate your sprayer before use. Fill with water, spray a known area (e.g., 100 m), and measure how much water you used. Adjust so you apply 200-300 litres per hectare. Spray the whole plant, especially the undersides of leaves where aphids and caterpillars hide. Keep a steady walking speed. Clean the sprayer after use: rinse three times with clean water and pour the rinse water into a pit away from wells and streams.

After spraying, wash your hands and face with soap. Shower and change clothes. Wash clothes separately from family laundry. Store chemicals in a locked cabinet, away from food and feed. Never store in soda bottles. Dispose of empty containers by puncturing them and burying in a pit 50 cm deep, away from water sources. If someone shows signs of poisoning (headache, vomiting, blurred vision), call a doctor immediately. Take the chemical label to the clinic. Keep a first aid kit with activated charcoal if advised by a health worker. Never eat, drink, or smoke while spraying.

5. Integrated Pest Management (IPM) Strategies

Integrated Pest Management (IPM) combines many methods to keep pests below economic thresholds with the least harm to people and the environment. IPM is not a single action but a year-round plan. It starts with good crop husbandry: plant certified, pest-resistant soybean varieties like 'TGx' lines that resist aphids and pod borers. Rotate soybeans with cereals like maize or sorghum every season to break pest life cycles. Plant early and at recommended spacing (60 cm between rows, 5 cm within rows) to reduce humidity that favours aphids.

Sanitation is key. Remove crop residues after harvest because they harbour beetles and caterpillars. Plough deeply to bury pupae. Keep field edges free of weeds like blackjack and pigweed, which host aphids and beetles. Use trap crops: plant a border of cowpea or sunflower around your soybean. Pests attack the trap crop first, then you can spray or destroy just that border. Intercrop soybean with maize or sorghum; this confuses pests and increases beneficial insects.

Monitor weekly using scouting and economic thresholds. Only spray when thresholds are reached. Choose selective insecticides like Bt or insecticidal soap first, and broad-spectrum chemicals only as a last resort. Rotate chemicals with different modes of action to prevent resistance. For example, if you used lambda-cyhalothrin this season, use chlorantraniliprole next season. Never mix chemicals unless the label says it is safe.

Combine biological control: release ladybirds for aphids, Trichogramma wasps for caterpillars. Use biopesticides like Beauveria bassiana. Keep a field diary of pest outbreaks, weather, and actions. After harvest, evaluate what worked. Share with neighbours. IPM reduces costs by 30-50% compared to calendar spraying. It also protects bees, which pollinate soybeans and increase yield by 10-15%. Healthy soil and healthy plants resist pests better. Add compost and apply fertiliser based on soil test. Strong plants tolerate some pest damage without yield loss.

6. Managing Pests in Storage

After harvest, soybean seed can be attacked by storage pests like cowpea weevil (Callosobruchus maculatus), grain moths, and flour beetles. These pests bore into seeds, reducing quality and germination. A heavy infestation can destroy 50% of stored seed in 6 months. Prevent them before storage. Dry soybean to 12% moisture or less. Use a moisture meter; if not available, bite a seed: it should crack, not be soft. Clean the storage area: remove old grain, dust, and cobwebs. Repair cracks and holes.

Use hermetic storage bags like Purdue Improved Crop Storage (PICS) bags or GrainPro bags. These are triple-layer plastic bags that cut off oxygen, killing pests without chemicals. Fill the bag, tie it tightly, and store on pallets away from walls. For 100 kg of seed, use one PICS bag. Check monthly for holes or tears. If you see live insects, sun-dry the seed for 2-3 days on a tarpaulin, then re-bag. Sun-drying kills eggs and larvae.

For small quantities, mix seed with wood ash or diatomaceous earth (DE) at 1 kg per 100 kg of seed. DE is a powder that scratches insect skins, causing them to dry out. It is safe for humans. You can also use botanical powders: neem seed powder (2 kg per 100 kg), or dried pepper and garlic. These repel insects but do not kill all. For larger stores, use approved storage insecticides like Actellic (pirimiphos-methyl) dust at 1 kg per 100 kg of grain. Wear gloves and mask when mixing. Do not use on seed for planting if the label says not to.

Store different crops separately. Keep storage room cool, dry, and dark. Inspect every 2 weeks for the first 3 months, then monthly. Look for live insects, webbing, or holes in seeds. If you find moths flying, use pheromone traps. For weevils, a simple trap: a jar with a little soapy water placed near bags; weevils fly to it and drown. Never store soybean near cowpea or beans, as weevils cross-infest. Sell or use older seed first. Keep records of storage dates and pest sightings.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Insect Pest Management in Soybean Farming

In African soybean production, insect pests can cause yield losses ranging from 20% to over 80% if left unmanaged. For the agribusiness operator, the goal is not zero pests it is protecting profit margins through timely, cost-effective interventions. This practical reference consolidates the key decision points, thresholds, and control options covered across this module into a single working document you can carry to the field, share with farm managers, or use in training sessions with outgrowers and cooperative members.

Soybean is increasingly important across Africa from Nigeria and Ghana to Zambia, Malawi, South Africa, and Ethiopia as both a food and cash crop, and as a source of protein meal for the growing poultry and livestock feed industry. Insect pests attack every stage: seedlings, vegetative growth, flowering, pod fill, and stored grain. Each stage demands a different response. Understanding when a pest population justifies the cost of control is the single most important business skill in pest management.

Scouting and Economic Thresholds

Scouting is the foundation of profitable pest management. Walk your fields in a W or zigzag pattern, inspecting at least 1020 sampling points per field. Check the underside of leaves, growing tips, flowers, and pods. Record pest counts and beneficial insect counts separately. Scout at least weekly from emergence through pod fill, and more frequently during flowering and pod fill when damage translates most directly to yield loss.

An economic threshold (ET) is the pest population density at which the cost of control equals the value of the crop lost. Below the threshold, spraying destroys profit. Above it, delaying costs yield. Thresholds vary by crop value, control cost, and region always use locally validated figures where available.

Biological Control and Beneficial Insects

Africa's soybean fields host a wide range of natural enemies ladybird beetles (ladybugs), lacewings, hoverflies, parasitic wasps, spiders, and predatory bugs. These organisms suppress aphids, caterpillars, and other pests at no cost to the farmer. The business case for conserving them is straightforward: every beneficial insect you protect is a free pest-control worker.

Broad-spectrum insecticides especially pyrethroids and organophosphates kill beneficials along with pests, often triggering pest resurgence and secondary outbreaks. Where possible, choose selective products, spot-spray only affected areas, and avoid spraying during peak beneficial activity (early morning or late evening).

Augmentative biological control is also available in some African markets. Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) products target caterpillars specifically and are safe for beneficials and humans. Trichogramma wasps are used against pod borers in some regions. Neem-based botanicals (azadirachtin) offer a locally available, low-toxicity option that disrupts pest feeding and growth while sparing many natural enemies.

Chemical Insecticide Use and Safety

Chemical insecticides remain a necessary tool, but they are the most expensive and most dangerous option. Use them only when scouting confirms the threshold has been crossed and biological control is insufficient. Always rotate active ingredients to prevent resistance, and never exceed label rates.

SAFETY WARNING READ BEFORE SPRAYING
  • Always wear personal protective equipment (PPE): gloves, long sleeves, trousers, boots, eye protection, and a face mask.
  • Never eat, drink, or smoke while handling or applying insecticides.
  • Mix and apply only in well-ventilated areas, away from children, livestock, and water sources.
  • Respect the pre-harvest interval (PHI) on the label spraying too close to harvest leaves illegal residues and can cause rejected loads.
  • Store insecticides in original labeled containers, locked away from food, feed, and children.
  • If poisoning is suspected, contact a medical facility immediately and bring the product label.
  • Dispose of empty containers safely never reuse them for food or water.

Integrated Pest Management (IPM) Strategies

IPM is a decision framework, not a product. It combines cultural, biological, and chemical tools in a sequence that minimizes cost and environmental impact while protecting yield. For African soybean farmers, the most practical IPM sequence is:

  1. Prevent: Use resistant or tolerant varieties where available. Rotate with cereals (maize, sorghum, millet) to break pest cycles. Plant on time to avoid peak pest flights. Manage weeds that host pests.
  2. Monitor: Scout weekly. Record pest and beneficial counts. Use pheromone traps where available for pod borers and stink bugs.
  3. Decide: Compare counts to economic thresholds. If below threshold, do not spray.
  4. Biological first: If above threshold, consider Bt, neem, or release of beneficials before broad-spectrum chemicals.
  5. Chemical last: Use selective, labeled products at the correct rate. Rotate modes of action. Respect PHI.
  6. Evaluate: After harvest, review what worked. Adjust next season's plan.

Managing Pests in Storage

Post-harvest losses from bruchids, weevils, and grain moths can reach 2030% in African farm storage. For a soybean business, stored grain is working capital protecting it is protecting cash. Key practices:

  • Harvest promptly at physiological maturity to reduce field infestation.
  • Dry grain to 1213% moisture before storage damp grain invites pests and mould.
  • Clean and repair storage structures before filling. Remove old grain residues.
  • Use hermetic storage bags (e.g., Purdue Improved Crop Storage PICS bags) to suffocate pests without chemicals.
  • Store in cool, dry, well-ventilated spaces, off the floor and away from walls.
  • Inspect monthly. If live insects exceed 25 per kg, treat with approved storage insecticide or re-dry and re-bag.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Scout fields weekly from emergence to pod fill record pests and beneficials.
  • Know your economic thresholds before you buy any insecticide.
  • Identify pests correctly not every insect is a pest.
  • Protect beneficial insects: avoid broad-spectrum sprays unless thresholds are crossed.
  • Wear full PPE when handling or applying any insecticide.
  • Respect pre-harvest intervals and rotate active ingredients.
  • Dry grain to 1213% moisture and use hermetic storage bags.
  • Inspect stored grain monthly; treat if live insects exceed 25 per kg.
