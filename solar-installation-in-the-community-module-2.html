1. Conducting a Site Survey for Solar Potential

A site survey is the first step to see if solar will work for a home or business. You will need a notebook, pencil, tape measure, compass, and a smartphone with a solar app like Solar Pathfinder or Sun Surveyor. Start by talking to the owner about their electricity needs: how many lights, phones, fridge, TV, or machines they use daily. Ask about their monthly energy bill if they have one, or estimate by listing appliances and their wattage. For example, a small home might use 5 LED lights (5W each), a phone charger (10W), and a radio (20W) for 4 hours a day: total about 50W x 4h = 200Wh per day. Write this down.

Next, walk the property and note possible mounting spots: roof, ground, or pole. Measure the area available. For a roof, measure length and width in meters. Check the roof material: corrugated iron, tiles, or thatch. Thatch is not suitable for solar panels. For ground mount, check soil type and if the area floods in rainy season. Use your compass to find true north (or south if you are in southern Africa). In most of Africa, panels should face north in the north hemisphere and south in the south hemisphere. But near the equator, a shallow angle works.

Take photos of the site from different angles, especially where you plan to put panels. Note any tall trees, buildings, or hills that could cast shadows. Also check where the battery and inverter will go they need a dry, ventilated space. Measure the distance from panels to battery to estimate cable length. Cable runs over 10 meters may need thicker wire to reduce power loss.

Finally, record the location using GPS coordinates or a nearby landmark. Note the local climate: rainy season, dust, or extreme heat. These affect panel performance. A good survey takes 1-2 hours and gives you all the data to design a system that fits the site and the user's budget.

2. Evaluating Solar Irradiance and Shading

Solar irradiance is the amount of sunlight energy hitting a surface, measured in kilowatt-hours per square meter per day (kWh/m/day). In Africa, this varies from about 4 kWh/m/day in cloudy highlands to over 6 kWh/m/day in the Sahara and Kalahari. You can find your local value using free online tools like Global Solar Atlas or NASA POWER. For a quick estimate, assume 5 kWh/m/day for most of sub-Saharan Africa. This means a 300W panel can produce about 1.5 kWh per day (300W x 5 hours = 1500Wh). But shading can cut this by half or more.

Shading is the biggest killer of solar production. Even a small shadow on one panel can reduce the output of the whole string. To evaluate shading, use a Solar Pathfinder or a smartphone app like Sun Surveyor. Stand where you will mount the panels and look at the sky. Identify trees, buildings, or poles that cast shadows. Note the time of day and year when shadows fall on the spot. For example, a tree to the east will shade panels in the morning; a building to the west shades in the afternoon. In the northern hemisphere, the sun is in the south, so shadows point north. In the southern hemisphere, the opposite.

Measure the height of obstacles and their distance from the panels. A simple rule: if an obstacle is twice as tall as its distance from the panel, it will shade the panel when the sun is low. For example, a 10m tree 5m away will shade the panel when the sun is below 63 degrees. In Africa, the sun is high most of the year, but in winter it is lower. Check shading at 9am, 12pm, and 3pm on a clear day. Also consider seasonal changes: in the rainy season, clouds reduce irradiance even without shadows.

If shading is unavoidable, you can move the panels, trim trees (with permission), or use micro-inverters or power optimizers to reduce the impact. But the best solution is to avoid shading at the design stage. Record all shading data in your survey notes. A site with heavy shading may need a ground mount in an open area instead of a roof mount.

3. Assessing Roof and Ground Mount Options

Choosing between roof and ground mount depends on space, shading, roof condition, and cost. Roof mount saves ground space and often uses existing structure, but it can be harder to install and maintain. Ground mount is easier to clean and angle, but needs land and may be vulnerable to theft or animals.

For roof mount, first check the roof type. Corrugated iron sheets are common in Africa and can support solar panels if the purlins (the horizontal beams) are strong. You will need to find the rafters and use lag bolts or special roof hooks. Never drill through the roof without sealing use rubber washers and sealant to prevent leaks. For tile roofs, use tile hooks that replace a tile. For concrete roofs, use anchor bolts. The roof should be less than 10 years old; if it is old or rusted, repair or replace it first. Also check the roof pitch: panels work best at an angle equal to your latitude (e.g., 10 near the equator, 30 in South Africa). If the roof is too steep or flat, you may need tilted mounts.

For ground mount, you need a clear area with good sun. The soil should be stable not sandy or waterlogged. Use concrete footings or ground screws to anchor the mounting frame. Keep panels at least 50cm above ground to avoid dust, water, and animals. In areas with theft risk, build a fence or use tamper-proof bolts. Ground mounts allow you to adjust the tilt seasonally for maximum output. But they require more cable to reach the battery, which adds cost.

Consider wind loads: in coastal or windy areas, use heavier frames and more anchors. Also think about access for cleaning and maintenance. A ground mount is easier to clean, which is important in dusty regions. If the roof is shaded or weak, ground mount is better. If land is scarce, roof mount is the only option. Always discuss with the owner about their preference and budget. A typical 1kW system needs about 6-8 square meters of roof or ground space.

4. Structural and Electrical Considerations

Before installing solar panels, you must ensure the structure can handle the weight and wind. A single solar panel weighs about 15-20 kg. A 1kW system (3-4 panels) adds 60-80 kg to the roof, plus mounting rails. That is like having an extra person standing on the roof. Check the roof trusses: they should be spaced no more than 1.2 meters apart and made of treated timber or steel. If they are weak, add bracing. For ground mounts, the frame must withstand wind speeds of at least 120 km/h. Use galvanized steel or aluminum to prevent rust. Concrete footings should be at least 60cm deep.

Electrical considerations start with voltage and current. Most solar panels produce 12V or 24V DC. You will need a charge controller to regulate the voltage to the battery. The controller must match the panel voltage and battery voltage. For example, a 12V battery needs a 12V panel and a 12V controller. If you use a 24V system, you can use two 12V panels in series. Calculate the total current: a 300W panel at 12V produces about 25A. Use cables rated for at least 1.25 times that current. For a 25A current, use 4mm copper cable for runs up to 5 meters. Longer runs need thicker cable (6mm or 10mm) to avoid voltage drop.

Safety is critical. Install a DC circuit breaker or fuse between the panel and controller, and between controller and battery. Use a grounding rod for the panel frames and mounting structure to prevent electric shock. All connections must be in waterproof junction boxes. Never connect panels directly to a battery without a controller it will overcharge and damage the battery.

Also plan for the inverter if you need AC power. The inverter should be sized to handle the total wattage of appliances you want to run at once. For example, a 500W inverter can run a laptop, lights, and a small TV. Place the inverter and battery in a dry, ventilated area away from living spaces because they produce hydrogen gas. Label all cables clearly. A well-planned electrical system is safe, efficient, and easy to maintain.

5. Permitting and Community Approvals

In many African countries, installing solar panels requires permits and approvals from local authorities. The rules vary by country, region, and even by city. Always check with the local government office, energy ministry, or utility company before you start. In some places, small off-grid systems need no permit, but grid-tied systems or systems above a certain size (e.g., 1kW) require approval. For example, in Kenya, the Energy Regulatory Commission (ERC) requires licensing for systems above 1kW. In South Africa, you need a Certificate of Compliance (CoC) from a registered electrician for any grid-tied system.

Start by visiting the local council or municipal office. Ask for the requirements for solar installation. You may need to submit a site plan, electrical diagram, and proof of ownership or permission from the landowner. If you are renting, get written permission from the landlord. For community projects, you may need approval from a village committee or chief. In some areas, you must inform the utility company if you plan to feed excess power into the grid. That requires a bi-directional meter and a grid-tie agreement.

Community approvals are just as important. Talk to neighbors about your plans. They may have concerns about glare, noise from inverters, or access. Address these early. In some communities, you may need to present your project at a community meeting. Bring a simple drawing and explain the benefits: clean energy, lower bills, and local jobs. If the system is on a shared roof or land, get written consent from all users.

Keep copies of all permits and approvals. They protect you if there is a dispute later. Also check if there are any tax incentives or subsidies for solar in your area. Some governments offer rebates or reduced import duties on solar equipment. A little paperwork upfront saves big trouble later. If you are unsure, hire a local solar consultant who knows the regulations. Never install without the necessary permits you could be fined or forced to remove the system.

6. Creating a Site Assessment Report

A site assessment report is a written document that summarizes your findings and recommends a solar system design. It helps the client understand what they are buying and protects you as the installer. The report should be clear, simple, and include photos and diagrams. Write it in the local language if needed, or use simple English.

Start with a cover page: client name, date, location, and your name. Then include a summary of the client's energy needs: list appliances, their wattage, hours used per day, and total daily energy in watt-hours (Wh). For example, 5 lights (5W x 4h = 100Wh), phone charger (10W x 2h = 20Wh), radio (20W x 4h = 80Wh) = 200Wh/day. Add a 20% safety margin, so design for 240Wh/day.

Next, describe the site: roof or ground area, dimensions, orientation, and shading analysis. Include photos with arrows showing north and any obstacles. State the solar irradiance value you used (e.g., 5 kWh/m/day). Then recommend a system size: for 240Wh/day, you need a 100W panel (100W x 5h = 500Wh, which is more than enough; but you can use a 50W panel if you have a smaller battery). Calculate battery size: for 240Wh, a 12V 20Ah battery (240Wh) gives one day of storage. Add an extra day for cloudy weather, so 40Ah. Recommend a charge controller rated for the panel's current (e.g., 10A for a 100W panel).

Include a cost estimate: panels, battery, controller, inverter, cables, mounting, and labor. List permits needed. Add a simple wiring diagram. Finally, give a timeline: survey (1 day), design (1 day), installation (1-2 days), testing (1 day). End with your contact details and a place for signatures. The report should be 2-3 pages. Give a copy to the client and keep one for your records. A good report builds trust and ensures everyone agrees on the plan before you start digging or drilling.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Solar Site Assessment and Planning

In African community solar projects, the difference between a system that pays for itself in four years and one that underperforms for a decade is almost always decided before the first panel is mounted. A disciplined site assessment is the cheapest insurance a solar business can buy. This practical reference consolidates the field methods, data sources, and documentation standards you need to move from a client enquiry to a bankable site assessment report whether you are sizing a 3 kW rooftop system for a rural clinic in Kisumu or a 50 kW ground-mount array for an agro-processing hub in Ghana's Ashanti Region.

Treat this lesson as your field manual. Each section maps to one of the six core lessons in this module, but here the focus is on what you actually do on site, what you record, and how you turn it into a proposal that wins the job.

1. Conducting a Site Survey for Solar Potential

Start with a desk study using free tools: Google Earth Pro for roof geometry, Global Solar Atlas for GHI, and OpenStreetMap for access roads. Then verify on the ground. Record GPS coordinates at the proposed array centre, note the roof type (mabati/iron sheets, clay tile, concrete slab, thatch), and measure usable area with a tape. Photograph every obstruction: water tanks, TV aerials, neighbouring buildings, and critically in many African communities mature mango, neem, or jacaranda trees whose canopies will grow.

For ground-mount sites, check soil type (a simple hand auger test), water table depth in the rainy season, and flood history. Ask the landowner about grazing patterns goats and cattle destroy unsecured ground-mount arrays across the continent.

2. Evaluating Solar Irradiance and Shading

Africa's advantage is its resource: most of the continent receives 4.56.5 kWh/m/day of global horizontal irradiation (GHI). But GHI on a flat map is not the irradiance on a tilted panel. Use the Global Solar Atlas to pull monthly GHI and DNI for your exact coordinates, then apply a tilt factor for your latitude. For a fixed-tilt system in sub-Saharan Africa, a tilt equal to latitude minus 510 usually optimises annual yield while shedding dust in the rainy season.

Shading is where most assessments fail. Use a Solmetric SunEye, a Horizon compass app, or the free Sun Surveyor app to capture the horizon profile. Aim for zero shading between 9am and 3pm year-round. A single palm frond casting a shadow across one panel string can cut string output by 30% or more.

3. Assessing Roof and Ground Mount Options

Roof mounts dominate urban and peri-urban African projects because they avoid land disputes and reduce cable runs. But not every roof can take the load. A solar array adds roughly 1218 kg/m to a roof; older mabati sheets on timber purlins may need reinforcement. Check purlin spacing, sheet gauge, and whether the roof has been re-sheeted in the last decade.

Ground mounts suit rural clinics, schools, and agro-processing sites with available land. They allow optimal tilt and easier cleaning, but require perimeter fencing, anti-theft bolts, and a clear drainage plan. In flood-prone areas, raise the mounting frame at least 300 mm above the highest recorded flood line.

4. Structural and Electrical Considerations

Structural: for roof mounts, calculate the additional dead load and confirm the roof can take wind uplift coastal and highland areas can see 100+ km/h gusts. For ground mounts, specify concrete ballast or screw piles depending on soil. Never rely on sandbags alone in areas with seasonal winds.

Electrical: map the cable route from array to inverter to battery to distribution board. Keep DC cable runs under 30 m where possible to limit voltage drop; use 4 mm or 6 mm PV cable with UV-resistant sheathing. Inverter and battery placement should be shaded, ventilated, and above flood level. In off-grid community systems, size the battery bank for at least two days of autonomy for critical loads (vaccine fridges, lighting, water pumps).

SAFETY WARNING DC Arc Flash and Roof Fall Hazards

Solar DC circuits cannot be switched off at the panel they remain live whenever exposed to light. A DC arc flash can reach 3,000C and is a leading cause of solar installer fatalities. Always isolate, test, and use insulated tools rated for 1,000 V DC. On roof work, use fall arrest harnesses anchored to a certified point, never work alone, and stop work in winds above 38 km/h or during rain. In many African jurisdictions, working at height without a harness is both illegal and uninsurable.

5. Permitting and Community Approvals

Permitting in Africa is rarely a single window. Expect at least three layers: local government (county or district planning), the national utility or energy regulator (for grid-tied or net-metering systems), and community-level consent (chief, elders, landowner, or school board). Start the process early approvals can take 412 weeks. Keep a signed consent letter from the landowner and a minute of the community meeting in your project file. These documents protect you if ownership disputes arise later.

For grid-tied systems, confirm the utility's interconnection requirements some African utilities require a visible lockable AC isolator and a type-tested inverter. For off-grid systems, permitting is lighter but land use consent is still essential.

6. Creating a Site Assessment Report

Your report is a sales document as much as a technical record. Structure it as: (1) executive summary with recommended system size and estimated yield; (2) site description with GPS, photos, and shading analysis; (3) structural and electrical findings; (4) permitting status and required approvals; (5) bill of materials and indicative cost; (6) financial summary payback period, avoided diesel or grid cost, and CO offset. Keep it under 15 pages, use clear photos, and always state your assumptions (irradiance source, performance ratio, tariff). A clear report wins repeat business and referrals in tight-knit African communities.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Desk study done: GPS coordinates, Global Solar Atlas GHI pulled, satellite imagery reviewed.
  2. Site visit completed: roof type/gauge, usable area, ground soil and flood history recorded with photos.
  3. Shading mapped: horizon profile captured 9am3pm, tree growth and future obstructions noted.
  4. Structural check: roof load capacity or ground foundation design confirmed by a qualified person.
  5. Electrical route planned: cable lengths, inverter/battery siting, and voltage drop calculated.
  6. Permits and consents: council, utility, and community approvals identified with timelines.
  7. Safety plan: fall arrest, DC isolation, and lone-working rules documented before mobilisation.
  8. Report delivered: system size, yield estimate, cost, payback, and assumptions in writing.
