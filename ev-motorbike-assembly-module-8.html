1. Routing Main Wiring Harness

The main wiring harness is the central nervous system of your EV motorbike. It carries power and signals to every electrical part. Start by laying the harness on a clean table. Check each connector for damage. In Africa, dust and heat can crack plastic, so inspect carefully. You will need: cable ties, electrical tape, a 10mm spanner, and a multimeter. Begin at the front, near the headlight. Feed the harness through the frame's steering head, following the same path as the brake cables. Keep it away from sharp edges and hot surfaces like the motor or controller. Use cable ties every 15-20 cm to secure it. Do not overtighten; you want the harness to move slightly with the suspension. Next, route the harness along the main frame tube towards the battery box. Avoid pinching it under the seat or side panels. In many African workshops, rats chew wires, so consider wrapping the harness in split loom tubing for extra protection. For the rear section, guide the harness to the taillight and turn signals. Leave enough slack at the steering and rear suspension points so the wires don't stretch when the bike moves. Connect the main harness to the controller and battery. The controller usually has a multi-pin connector; push it in until it clicks. The battery connections are red for positive, black for negative. Use a multimeter to check for continuity between the harness ends before plugging everything in. A common mistake is routing the harness too tight, causing wires to break inside the insulation. After routing, turn the handlebars left and right to ensure no pulling. Secure all loose ends with cable ties. Finally, test that all lights and horn work before closing panels. A well-routed harness lasts for years, even on rough African roads.

2. Connecting Headlight and Taillight

Headlights and taillights are critical for safety, especially when riding at night on unlit roads. You need: a 12V headlight (typically 35W), a taillight (5W), a screwdriver, wire strippers, and electrical tape. First, identify the headlight wires. Most have three: white for low beam, blue for high beam, and black for ground. The taillight usually has two: red for brake/tail and black for ground. On your EV, the main harness provides a 12V output for lighting. Check your controller or DC-DC converter for a lighting wire, often yellow or brown. Strip 5mm of insulation from each wire. Twist the copper strands tightly. Use a soldering iron if available; if not, crimp connectors are fine. For the headlight, connect the white wire to the low beam terminal, blue to high beam, and black to ground. If your headlight has a single beam, just connect the live wire and ground. For the taillight, connect the red wire to the tail light terminal and black to ground. Some taillights have a separate brake light wire (often green). Connect that to the brake switch on the rear brake pedal. Test by turning on the ignition. The headlight should come on in low beam. Switch to high beam and check. Press the brake pedal; the brake light should brighten. If nothing works, check the fuse. A 10A fuse is common. Use a multimeter to verify 12V at the headlight connector. In dusty conditions, seal connections with dielectric grease to prevent corrosion. Mount the headlight securely to the fork brackets. Aim it slightly down so you don't blind oncoming traffic. Taillight should be visible from 50 meters. After installation, wrap all connections with self-amalgamating tape for waterproofing. Test again after a short ride to ensure nothing loosens.

3. Installing Turn Signals and Horn

Turn signals and a horn make you visible and heard in traffic. You need: four turn signal units (12V, 10W each), a horn (12V, 1.5A), a flasher relay, wire, and a 10mm spanner. Start with the turn signals. Each signal has two wires: positive (often red or green) and ground (black). Mount the front signals on the headlight bracket or fork ears. The rear signals go on the tail bracket. Ensure they face outward and slightly back. Route the wires along the main harness. Connect the positive wires to the flasher relay output. The flasher relay has three terminals: one to the turn signal switch, one to ground, and one to the signals. The switch on the handlebar has left and right outputs. Connect left front and left rear positives together to the left switch wire, and right front and right rear to the right switch wire. Ground all signal grounds to the frame. For the horn, mount it near the front, facing down so water doesn't collect. Connect the positive wire to the horn button on the handlebar, and the negative to ground. The horn button usually has two wires: one from the horn, one to ground. Test the horn: press the button, it should sound loudly. If not, check the fuse and connections. For turn signals, turn on the ignition and flip the switch left. The left signals should blink about 60-120 times per minute. If they don't blink, replace the flasher relay. In Africa, many riders skip turn signals, but they are legally required in most countries. Use cable ties to secure wires away from moving parts. After installation, test all signals and horn with the engine off. Then start the bike and test again. A weak horn often means a bad ground. Clean the frame contact point with sandpaper. Finally, apply dielectric grease to all connectors to keep dust and moisture out.

4. Wiring Dashboard and Display

The dashboard shows speed, battery level, and indicators. You need: a 12V display unit, a speed sensor, a multimeter, and small screwdrivers. First, identify the display wires. Common colors: red for positive (12V), black for ground, green for speed signal, yellow for battery level, and blue for turn indicator. Some displays use a multi-pin connector; check your manual. Mount the display on the handlebar using the provided bracket. Ensure it's visible and doesn't interfere with controls. Route the wires along the main harness to the controller area. Connect the red wire to a switched 12V source, such as the ignition wire. Connect black to ground. The speed sensor usually mounts near the front wheel; it picks up a magnet on the wheel spoke. Connect the sensor's signal wire to the display's green wire. For battery level, the display needs a signal from the battery management system (BMS) or a shunt. If your EV has a simple voltage-based display, connect the yellow wire to the main battery positive after the fuse. For turn indicators, connect the blue wire to the left and right signal positives via diodes (1N4007) to prevent backfeed. Test the display: turn on ignition, it should light up. Check speed by spinning the wheel; the display should show a reading. If not, adjust the sensor gap to 2-3mm from the magnet. Battery level should show full if the battery is charged. If the display flickers, check for loose connections. Use heat shrink tubing on all splices. In dusty environments, seal the display's back with silicone. After wiring, secure all wires with cable ties. Take a short ride to verify speed accuracy; compare with a GPS app. Adjust the wheel size setting in the display if needed. A well-wired dashboard gives you confidence on long trips.

5. Connecting 12V Accessory System

A 12V accessory system lets you power a phone charger, GPS, or extra lights. You need: a 12V socket (cigarette lighter style), a 10A fuse, 16 AWG wire, ring terminals, and a relay (optional). First, find a good mounting spot for the socket, like under the seat or on the handlebar. Drill a hole if needed. Connect the red wire from the socket to a 12V positive source. It's best to use a relay that switches on with the ignition, so accessories don't drain the battery when the bike is off. The relay has four pins: 30 (positive from battery), 87 (to socket positive), 85 (ground), and 86 (ignition switched positive). Connect a 10A fuse between the battery positive and relay pin 30. Connect pin 87 to the socket's red wire. Connect pin 85 to ground. Connect pin 86 to a wire that has 12V when ignition is on, such as the headlight wire. Connect the socket's black wire to ground. Use 16 AWG wire for up to 10A. Strip wires, crimp ring terminals, and secure with screws. Test: turn on ignition, plug in a phone charger, it should work. If not, check the fuse and relay. In Africa, accessory sockets often get dusty; use a rubber cap when not in use. For extra lights, connect them via a separate switch and relay to avoid overloading the main circuit. Never exceed the fuse rating. If you add many accessories, upgrade the battery or alternator. Secure all wires away from heat and moving parts. Use split loom for protection. After installation, check that the socket doesn't short when wet. Apply dielectric grease to the terminals. A good accessory system makes your EV motorbike more useful for daily work.

6. Testing Electrical Continuity

Testing continuity ensures your wiring is correct and safe. You need a multimeter set to continuity mode (beep) or resistance (ohms). First, disconnect the battery to avoid damage. For each circuit, test from the power source to the component. For example, to test the headlight circuit, place one probe on the headlight positive wire and the other on the corresponding pin at the switch. The multimeter should beep. If not, there's a break. Trace the wire visually; look for cuts, corrosion, or loose connectors. Common issues: broken wire inside insulation, dirty ground points, or a blown fuse. Test grounds by placing one probe on the component's ground wire and the other on a clean metal part of the frame. It should beep. If not, clean the ground connection with sandpaper. For switches, test continuity when the switch is on; it should beep, and when off, no beep. For the horn, test from the horn button to the horn positive. For turn signals, test from the flasher relay to each signal. Use a helper to operate switches while you test. If you find a break, repair by soldering and heat shrinking. Do not just twist wires. After testing all circuits, reconnect the battery and test with power. Use a multimeter to check voltage at each component: 12V for lights, horn, and accessories. If voltage is low, check for high resistance in connections. In Africa, heat and dust cause corrosion; clean and protect connections with dielectric grease. Test continuity after every repair. Keep a wiring diagram handy. A simple continuity test can save hours of troubleshooting. Always test before riding, especially after modifying wiring. A short circuit can cause a fire. If you smell burning, disconnect the battery immediately. Regular testing keeps your EV reliable.

Practical Reference: Electrical Wiring and Lighting for EV Motorbike Assembly

In the African e-mobility market, an electric motorbike is only as reliable as its wiring loom. Riders in Nairobi, Kigali, Lagos, and Accra depend on these machines for deliveries, passenger transport, and daily commuting. A loose connector or a poorly routed harness does not just cause a breakdown â it costs the operator income, damages the brand's reputation, and can create fire and shock hazards. This practical reference consolidates the essential wiring and lighting tasks for EV motorbike assembly, with a business-first focus on repeatable quality, reduced warranty claims, and faster assembly times.

Whether you are assembling 5 bikes per day or 50, the principles are the same: route before you connect, test before you close, and document every step so that any technician can reproduce the build. 1. Routing the Main Wiring Harness

The main harness is the spine of the electrical system. Route it along the frame's upper rail, away from sharp edges, hot surfaces, and moving suspension components. Use rubber grommets at every frame pass-through and secure the loom with UV-resistant zip ties every 150â200 mm. In Africa, where roads can be rough and dust ingress is high, add a protective split conduit or spiral wrap on exposed sections. Leave a service loop near the steering head so the harness does not stretch when the handlebars turn lock-to-lock.

Business impact: A harness routed correctly at assembly takes 15â20 minutes. A harness that fails in the field due to chafing can take 3â5 hours to diagnose and replace, plus lost rider income. Training your assembly team on a single routing standard reduces rework and warranty claims.

2. Connecting Headlight and Taillight

Most EV motorbikes use 12V LED lighting powered through the DC-DC converter. Connect the headlight's positive lead to the switched 12V output from the fuse box, and the negative lead to a common ground. The taillight typically has three wires: ground, running light, and brake light. The brake light signal comes from the brake switch on the handlebar or foot pedal. Ensure the brake light activates before the brake pads engage â this is a safety and regulatory requirement in most African markets.

3. Installing Turn Signals and Horn

Turn signals must be mounted symmetrically and angled outward for visibility. Connect the positive lead from the flasher relay to each signal's positive terminal, and ground the negative. Test the flash rate: too fast indicates a blown bulb or incorrect relay; too slow indicates a wiring resistance issue. The horn should be mounted with its opening facing downward to prevent water ingress during rain â critical in tropical African climates. Use a relay if the horn draws more than 3A to avoid burning the handlebar switch contacts.

4. Wiring the Dashboard and Display

The dashboard receives power from the 12V accessory circuit and communicates with the motor controller via CAN bus or discrete signal wires. Connect the ignition-switched 12V line first, then the ground, then the communication wires. Never connect or disconnect the dashboard while the system is powered â this can damage the display or the controller. Secure the dashboard connector with a locking clip or a small zip tie to prevent vibration-induced disconnection on rough roads.

5. Connecting the 12V Accessory System

Many African riders add phone chargers, USB ports, or auxiliary lights. Provide a dedicated 12V accessory circuit with its own fuse (typically 10A) and a clearly labeled connector. Do not tap into the lighting circuit for accessories â this overloads the wiring and causes flickering lights or blown fuses. A dedicated accessory bus also makes it easy to add or remove devices without disturbing the main harness.

6. Testing Electrical Continuity

Before powering up the system, perform a continuity test on every circuit. Set your multimeter to the continuity or resistance mode. With the battery disconnected, touch the probes to the two ends of each wire. A reading near 0Î© indicates good continuity; infinite resistance indicates a break. Also test for shorts: measure resistance between the positive wire and the frame ground. Any reading below 1Î© indicates a short that must be fixed before power-up. Document your readings on a build sheet â this creates a traceable quality record and helps diagnose future issues.
