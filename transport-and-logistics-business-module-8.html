Lessons

1. Costing and Pricing of Logistics Services

To price your logistics services profitably, you must know your costs. Start by listing all direct costs for a trip: fuel, driver wages, tolls, loading fees, and vehicle maintenance. For example, a trip from Lagos to Ibadan (130 km) might use 40 litres of diesel at 1,200 per litre = 48,000. Driver wage: 10,000. Tolls: 2,000. Loading: 5,000. Total direct cost: 65,000. Now add indirect costs: vehicle insurance, licensing, office rent, and your own salary. If monthly indirect costs are 300,000 and you make 20 trips per month, each trip carries 15,000 indirect cost. So total cost per trip = 80,000. To make a 20% profit, price at 96,000. But check competitors: if they charge 85,000, you may need to reduce costs or find a niche.

For multiple services, use activity-based costing. For example, if you also offer warehousing, allocate rent, utilities, and labour to storage per pallet. A 100-square-metre warehouse costing 200,000 monthly can hold 200 pallets, so each pallet costs 1,000 per month. Add handling and admin, then price per pallet.

Always calculate your break-even point. If fixed costs are 500,000 monthly and each trip contributes 30,000 after variable costs, you need 17 trips to break even. Use simple spreadsheets like Excel or Google Sheets to track costs. Update prices regularly as fuel prices change. In Africa, fuel can fluctuate weekly, so build a fuel surcharge clause into contracts. For cross-border haulage, include customs clearing fees, border delays, and currency exchange costs. Remember, pricing too low kills your business; pricing too high loses customers. Test prices with a few clients before rolling out.

2. Budgeting and Financial Forecasting

A budget is a plan for how you will spend and earn money. Start by estimating your income for the next three months. For a logistics business, income comes from trips, storage, and other services. If you have 5 trucks and each does 15 trips monthly at 100,000 per trip, monthly income = 7,500,000. But be realistic: not all trucks work every day. Use past data or industry averages. Then list expenses: fuel, salaries, rent, maintenance, insurance, loan repayments. For example, fuel might be 40% of income (3,000,000), salaries 20% (1,500,000), maintenance 10% (750,000), rent and utilities 5% (375,000), insurance 3% (225,000), loan repayment 10% (750,000). Total expenses = 6,600,000, leaving profit of 900,000 (12%).

Forecasting is predicting future income and expenses. Use a simple method: take last year's figures and adjust for growth or inflation. If inflation is 15%, increase costs by 15%. If you plan to buy a new truck, add loan repayments and additional fuel. Create a cash flow forecast: month by month, when cash comes in and goes out. For example, a client may pay 30 days after delivery, so you need enough cash to cover expenses before payment arrives.

Use tools like Excel templates or mobile apps like Wave or QuickBooks. In Africa, where bank loans are expensive, budgeting helps you avoid borrowing. Review your budget monthly. If fuel prices rise, cut non-essential spending. Involve your team: drivers can suggest fuel-saving routes. A good budget is flexible and realistic. Always keep a contingency of 10% for unexpected costs like breakdowns or fines.

3. Cash Flow Management and Working Capital

Cash flow is the movement of money in and out of your business. Even if you are profitable on paper, you can run out of cash if clients pay late. For example, you complete a trip worth 500,000, but the client pays after 60 days. Meanwhile, you must pay for fuel, driver, and tolls immediately. If you don't have cash reserves, you can't take the next job. Working capital is the money you need to cover day-to-day operations: current assets (cash, receivables, fuel stock) minus current liabilities (payables, short-term loans).

To manage cash flow, invoice promptly and follow up on payments. Offer a 2% discount for early payment (e.g., within 10 days). Use mobile money or bank transfers to speed up receipts. For large clients, request a deposit of 30-50% upfront. Keep a cash flow forecast showing weekly inflows and outflows. If you see a shortfall, arrange an overdraft or delay non-essential purchases. In Africa, where many businesses operate informally, keep a separate bank account for business and personal use.

Build a cash buffer of at least one month's expenses. If monthly expenses are 6,000,000, aim to have 6,000,000 in reserve. You can also use invoice factoring: sell your unpaid invoices to a financier for a fee (e.g., 5% of invoice value) to get cash quickly. For working capital, negotiate longer payment terms with suppliers (e.g., 30 days) and shorter terms with customers. Monitor your cash conversion cycle: days inventory + days receivables - days payables. If it's too long, you tie up cash. For example, if you hold fuel for 10 days, collect receivables in 45 days, and pay suppliers in 30 days, your cycle is 25 days. Reduce it by collecting faster or paying later.

4. Investment Appraisal for Logistics Assets

When buying a new truck, you need to know if it's worth the money. Investment appraisal compares the cost of the asset with the future cash flows it will generate. Simple methods include payback period and return on investment (ROI). For example, a used truck costs 15,000,000. It generates 500,000 net cash per month (after all expenses). Payback period = 15,000,000 / 500,000 = 30 months (2.5 years). If you expect the truck to last 5 years, it's a good investment. ROI = (annual net cash / cost) x 100 = (6,000,000 / 15,000,000) x 100 = 40% per year. Compare this to leaving money in a bank (e.g., 10% interest) or buying a different asset.

For more accuracy, use net present value (NPV) or internal rate of return (IRR). NPV discounts future cash flows to today's value. If interest rates are 20%, 500,000 next year is worth 416,667 today. Sum the discounted cash flows over the truck's life and subtract the initial cost. If NPV is positive, invest. IRR is the discount rate that makes NPV zero. If IRR > your cost of capital (e.g., loan interest), invest.

In Africa, consider risks: fuel price volatility, currency devaluation, and political instability. Use a higher discount rate for risky investments. Also consider alternatives: leasing instead of buying, or hiring subcontractors. For example, leasing a truck might cost 800,000 per month, but you avoid maintenance and depreciation. Compare total lease cost over 3 years (28,800,000) with buying (15,000,000 + maintenance). Often, buying is cheaper if you have the capital. Always include salvage value: a truck bought for 15,000,000 might sell for 5,000,000 after 5 years. Use Excel's NPV and IRR functions to calculate. Never invest without a clear payback plan.

5. Key Financial Metrics and KPIs

To run a profitable logistics business, track key financial metrics. These are numbers that tell you how well you're doing. Start with gross profit margin: (revenue - direct costs) / revenue x 100. If a trip earns 100,000 and direct costs are 70,000, gross margin is 30%. Aim for at least 25-30% in logistics. Net profit margin: (revenue - all costs) / revenue x 100. If all costs are 90,000, net margin is 10%. Compare with industry averages.

Other important metrics: cost per kilometre. If a truck's monthly costs (fuel, driver, maintenance, insurance) are 1,200,000 and it runs 5,000 km, cost per km = 240. You can then price per km to ensure profit. Revenue per truck: total revenue / number of trucks. If you have 5 trucks and revenue is 7,500,000, each truck earns 1,500,000. Track this monthly to see if trucks are underutilised. Fuel efficiency: kilometres per litre. If a truck uses 40 litres for 130 km, efficiency = 3.25 km/l. Improve by driver training and maintenance.

Also monitor days sales outstanding (DSO): average days to collect payment. If DSO is 60 days, you need more working capital. Aim for 30 days. Current ratio: current assets / current liabilities. A ratio above 1.5 is healthy. Debt-to-equity ratio: total debt / equity. Keep it below 2:1 to avoid risk. Use a simple dashboard in Excel or a notebook. Review weekly. In Africa, where cash is king, also track cash balance daily. Set targets: e.g., increase gross margin to 35%, reduce DSO to 45 days. Share metrics with your team and reward improvements. These numbers guide decisions: if cost per km rises, find cheaper fuel or routes.

6. Risk Management and Insurance

Logistics is risky: accidents, theft, fuel price spikes, currency changes, and client defaults. Risk management means identifying risks, reducing them, and transferring them through insurance. First, list all risks: vehicle accidents, cargo theft, driver injury, fire, flood, and political unrest. For each, assess likelihood and impact. A major accident could cost 10,000,000 in vehicle damage and legal fees. If you can't afford that, you need insurance.

In Africa, common insurances for logistics: commercial vehicle insurance (third party, fire, theft), goods in transit insurance (covers cargo), and workers' compensation. For a truck worth 15,000,000, comprehensive insurance might cost 600,000 per year (4% of value). Goods in transit insurance might cost 0.5% of cargo value per trip. For a 5,000,000 cargo, that's 25,000 per trip. Shop around: use local brokers and compare quotes. Also consider microinsurance for small operators.

Beyond insurance, reduce risk: train drivers in defensive driving, install GPS trackers (cost 50,000 per truck) to prevent theft, and do regular maintenance. Diversify clients: don't rely on one big customer. Keep contracts clear on liability and payment terms. For cross-border, buy political risk insurance or use letters of credit. Build an emergency fund: 3-6 months of expenses. If a truck breaks down, you can repair it without borrowing. Also have a business continuity plan: if a key driver leaves, who replaces them? Document procedures. In Africa, where insurance claims can be slow, keep copies of all documents and photos of incidents. Work with a reputable insurer. Finally, review risks quarterly and adjust your insurance as your business grows.

<h2>Practical Reference: Financial Management in Logistics</h2>

<p>This practical reference is a desk-side guide for logistics operators hauliers, clearing agents, courier firms, boda-boda distribution networks and warehousing businesses working across African corridors. It pulls together the six lessons in this module (costing and pricing, budgeting and forecasting, cash flow and working capital, investment appraisal, KPIs, and risk and insurance) into one usable reference. The aim is simple: help you protect margin, keep cash moving, and make asset decisions that survive real African operating conditions fuel volatility, currency swings, long receivables cycles and informal competition.</p>

<p>Use it alongside your own management accounts. Where you have data, use it. Where you don't, the tables below give you benchmark ranges to sanity-check your numbers. Treat every figure as a starting hypothesis, not a law.</p>

<h3>1. Costing and Pricing: Know Your True Cost Per Kilometre</h3>

<p>Most logistics businesses in Africa underprice because they cost only the visible trip fuel, driver, tolls and ignore depreciation, tyres, maintenance cycles, insurance, licensing, and overheads. The industry-standard method is <strong>activity-based costing</strong>: build a cost per kilometre (or per tonne-kilometre, or per delivery) that includes every cost driver, then add a target margin.</p>

<p>The core formula for a vehicle:</p>
<p><code>Cost per km = (Fixed costs annual km) + (Variable costs km)</code></p>

<p>Fixed costs include insurance, licensing, financing, and driver base pay. Variable costs include fuel, tyres, maintenance, and trip allowances. Once you know your cost per km, pricing becomes a margin decision, not a guess.</p>

<p><strong>Pricing in practice:</strong> African markets are price-sensitive and often informal. Quote three tiers spot rate, contract rate (volume commitment), and dedicated fleet rate so you can compete without destroying margin. On cross-border corridors (MombasaKampala, DurbanLusaka, LagosAbidjan), always price in a fuel adjustment clause and a currency clause. Fuel can move 2040% in a year; ignoring it is how hauliers go bankrupt.</p>

<h3>2. Budgeting and Financial Forecasting</h3>

<p>Build your budget bottom-up, not top-down. Start with fleet capacity (trucks available days realistic utilisation), then revenue, then costs. Use a 12-month rolling forecast and revise quarterly. In high-inflation environments, re-forecast monthly.</p>

<p>Three scenarios are enough: <strong>base</strong>, <strong>downside</strong> (fuel +25%, volume 20%), and <strong>upside</strong>. The downside scenario is the one that keeps you solvent.</p>

<h3>3. Cash Flow Management and Working Capital</h3>

<p>Logistics is a cash-hungry business. You pay for fuel, wages, tolls and tyres <em>now</em>; customers pay in 30, 60 or 90 days sometimes never. The cash conversion cycle is your survival metric:</p>

<p><code>Cash cycle = Days Inventory + Days Receivable Days Payable</code></p>

<p>For a haulier, inventory is minimal (spares), so the cycle is driven by receivables and payables. If you collect in 75 days and pay in 30, you are financing 45 days of operations from your own pocket. On a $1.2m revenue base, that's roughly $150,000 of working capital tied up.</p>

<div style="border:3px solid #c00000; background:#fff5f5; padding:14px 18px; margin:18px 0; border-radius:6px;">
<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0; color:#c00000; font-weight:bold; font-size:16px;"> SAFETY &amp; COMPLIANCE WARNING</p>
<p style="margin:0; color:#7a0000;">Never sign a contract that requires you to fund fuel and driver costs for more than 45 days without either (a) a mobilisation payment of at least 30% upfront, or (b) a confirmed invoice-discounting facility. In several African markets, prolonged non-payment by a single large client is the leading cause of haulage insolvency. Also verify that your insurance covers cross-border transit, cargo liability, and third-party bodily injury in every country on your route a gap here can bankrupt you faster than any cash-flow squeeze.</p>
</div>

<p><strong>Practical tools:</strong> a 13-week rolling cash flow forecast; a receivables ageing report reviewed weekly; and a rule that no single customer exceeds 30% of revenue. Where factoring or invoice discounting is available (Kenya, Nigeria, South Africa, Ghana), use it selectively it costs 25% but converts 60-day receivables into same-week cash.</p>

<h3>4. Investment Appraisal for Logistics Assets</h3>

<p>Before buying a truck, warehouse racking or a telematics system, run the numbers. Use <strong>Net Present Value (NPV)</strong> as the primary test, with <strong>Internal Rate of Return (IRR)</strong> and <strong>payback period</strong> as supporting checks. In African contexts, apply a higher discount rate (1825%) to reflect currency, political and counterparty risk.</p>

<h3>5. Key Financial Metrics and KPIs</h3>

<p>Track a small dashboard monthly. The metrics that matter most in African logistics:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Revenue per kilometre</strong> your pricing health.</li>
<li><strong>Cost per kilometre</strong> your efficiency health.</li>
<li><strong>Gross margin per trip</strong> target 2035%.</li>
<li><strong>Vehicle utilisation (%)</strong> target 7085% loaded km.</li>
<li><strong>Days Sales Outstanding (DSO)</strong> target under 60 days.</li>
<li><strong>Fuel cost as % of revenue</strong> benchmark 2835%.</li>
<li><strong>Maintenance cost per km</strong> benchmark $0.10$0.20.</li>
<li><strong>On-time delivery rate</strong> target above 90%.</li>
</ul>

<p>If DSO rises above 75 days, stop chasing volume and start chasing cash. If utilisation falls below 65%, you have a sales problem or a routing problem diagnose before you buy more trucks.</p>

<h3>6. Risk Management and Insurance</h3>

<p>Logistics risk in Africa clusters into five areas: <strong>cargo loss/damage</strong>, <strong>vehicle accident and third-party liability</strong>, <strong>fuel and currency volatility</strong>, <strong>political and border disruption</strong>, and <strong>counterparty (client) default</strong>. Each needs a mitigation.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Cargo:</strong> Goods-in-transit insurance, plus carrier's liability cover. Verify coverage in every transit country.</li>
<li><strong>Vehicle:</strong> Comprehensive cover with third-party extension; ensure drivers are licensed and tested.</li>
<li><strong>Fuel/currency:</strong> Contractual adjustment clauses; where available, forward contracts or fuel hedging.</li>
<li><strong>Political/border:</strong> Diversify routes and clients; keep 30 days of operating cash.</li>
<li><strong>Client default:</strong> Credit checks, credit limits, deposits, and invoice discounting.</li>
</ul>

<p>Insurance is not a cost it is the price of staying in business. Review policies annually against actual route and cargo profile, and never let a policy lapse for a premium dispute.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Calculate your fully loaded cost per kilometre including depreciation, tyres and overheads before quoting any rate.</li>
<li>Build a 12-month rolling budget with base, downside and upside scenarios; revise monthly in high-inflation markets.</li>
<li>Maintain a 13-week rolling cash flow forecast and review receivables ageing weekly.</li>
<li>Cap any single customer at 30% of revenue; insist on mobilisation payments for contracts over 45 days.</li>
<li>Run NPV and payback on every asset purchase above your capital threshold; lease if NPV is negative.</li>
<li>Track eight core KPIs monthly: revenue/km, cost/km, gross margin, utilisation, DSO, fuel %, maintenance/km, on-time delivery.</li>
<li>Review insurance cover annually for cargo, liability, and cross-border transit never let it lapse.</li>
<li>Keep at least 30 days of operating cash as a buffer against fuel spikes, border delays and payment defaults.</li>
</ol>
