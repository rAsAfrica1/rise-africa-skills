1. Irrigation System Components and Selection for African Farms

Welcome to Module 5, the heart of your training as an Agricultural Irrigation Technician. In this first lesson we focus on the hardware that turns water into income. Every component you install must survive heat, dust, poor roads, and intermittent power. Your job is not to sell the most expensive kit but the kit that keeps working when the farmer needs it most. Start with the water source. In Africa we see boreholes, shallow wells, rivers, dams, and municipal lines. Each has different flow, pressure, and reliability. A borehole with a solar pump may give steady flow during the day but nothing at night. A river may flood in the rainy season and dry to a trickle in the dry season. Before you select any component, measure the source. Measure flow in litres per minute, static water level, and drawdown. Use a bucket and stopwatch if you lack a flow meter. Record everything in a notebook and later in a simple spreadsheet. The pump is the engine of the system. Surface pumps suit shallow wells and rivers. Submersible pumps go deep into boreholes. Solar pumps are now common because fuel is expensive and grid power is unreliable. When choosing a pump, match it to the total head, which is the vertical lift plus friction losses in pipes and fittings. A common mistake is buying a pump that is too small, then adding a long hose and wondering why pressure drops. Teach the farmer to read the pump curve. The curve shows flow at different heads. Select a pump that gives the required flow at the actual head, not the maximum head on the box. Next, pipes and fittings. In Africa, uPVC and HDPE are the workhorses. uPVC is rigid and good for buried mains. HDPE is flexible, UV resistant, and easy to lay over rough ground. Use the correct pressure class. A class 6 pipe is fine for low pressure but will burst if you run a high pressure pump. Always use the same size or larger pipe than the pump outlet. Reducing pipe size increases friction and wastes energy. Fittings should be of good quality. Cheap fittings crack in the sun and leak at joints. Use solvent cement correctly for uPVC and compression fittings for HDPE. For surface lines that animals or tractors cross, use galvanised or thick-walled pipe. Filters protect the system. Sand filters remove organic matter from rivers and dams. Screen filters catch sand from boreholes. Disc filters are compact and good for drip. A blocked filter reduces pressure and starves crops. Install filters before the main line and make them easy to open and clean. Show the farmer how to flush the filter every day or every second day depending on water quality. Valves control flow. Gate valves isolate sections for repair. Ball valves give quick on and off. Air release valves prevent air locks at high points. Pressure reducing valves protect drip lines from high pressure. Non-return valves stop water flowing back and contaminating the source. Each valve must be accessible. Do not bury valves without a valve box. Emitters and outlets deliver water to the crop. Sprinklers are good for fodder, maize, and vegetables. They need higher pressure and are affected by wind. Drip lines are efficient but need filtration and careful management. In Africa, drip is popular for high-value crops like tomatoes, peppers, and flowers. Micro-sprinklers sit between the two and work well for orchards. When selecting emitters, consider the soil. Sandy soil needs higher flow and shorter runs. Clay soil needs lower flow and longer runs to avoid runoff. The control head is the brain of the system. It may be a simple manual manifold or an automated controller with valves and a timer. In areas with power cuts, use battery-powered controllers or solar controllers. Keep the control head under shade if possible. Label every valve and line. A labelled system saves hours during repairs. Water storage is often overlooked. A tank or reservoir lets you pump when power or sun is available and irrigate when the crop needs it. Size the tank for at least one day of peak irrigation. For a one hectare vegetable plot using 40 cubic metres per day, a 40 to 50 cubic metre tank is a good start. Tanks can be plastic, concrete, or lined earth. Plastic tanks are easy to install but can be expensive. Concrete tanks last long but need skilled labour. Lined earth pans are cheap but lose water to evaporation and seepage. Cover tanks to reduce evaporation and algae. Now think about the business. A farmer does not buy components for fun. They buy them to increase yield, reduce labour, and grow in the dry season when prices are high. Your selection must show a payback. For example, a solar pump may cost more than a diesel pump but saves fuel every day. A drip system may cost more than furrow but saves water and labour and improves quality. Prepare a simple cost and benefit sheet for each option. Include purchase price, installation, spare parts, fuel or power, labour, and expected yield increase. Farmers trust numbers more than words. Also consider local availability. If a pump model has no spare parts within 200 kilometres, it is a bad choice even if it is cheap. Build relationships with local suppliers. Keep a list of prices and stock. During installation, follow standards. Dig trenches deep enough to protect pipes from ploughs. Use sand bedding for uPVC. Pressure test every line before burying. Mark the layout with a simple map. Train the farmer and one family member on daily checks. A system that is not maintained will fail. Finally, document everything. Take photos, write serial numbers, and leave a simple operation manual in the local language if possible. Your reputation as a technician depends on systems that still work two seasons later. In the next lesson we will look at pump installation and maintenance in detail. Before then, visit a local farm and identify every component on an existing system. Write down its condition and one improvement you would recommend. That practical habit will make you a better technician and a better businessperson.

2. Pump Installation, Power Options, and Maintenance in African Conditions

Pumps are the most critical and most abused part of an irrigation system. In this lesson you will learn how to install, power, and maintain pumps so they last through dusty dry seasons and unpredictable power. Start with safety. Water and electricity kill. Always isolate power before working on a pump. Use a qualified electrician for mains wiring. For solar, treat panels and batteries with respect. Never work alone on a deep borehole. Use a tripod or crane and a trained assistant. Before installation, check the pump. Read the manual. Confirm voltage, phase, head, flow, and outlet size. Check that the impeller turns freely. For submersible pumps, check the motor winding with a megohmmeter if available. A low insulation reading means the motor is wet or damaged. Do not install a faulty pump. It will fail and you will be blamed. For surface pumps, place the pump as close to the water as possible. Keep suction lift low. A suction lift above 7 metres is risky. Use a foot valve and strainer. Prime the pump before starting. A pump running dry will destroy its seal and impeller in minutes. For submersible pumps, lower the pump at least one metre below the lowest water level but not so deep that it sits in sand or mud. Use a safety rope. Install a non-return valve above the pump. Use a torque arrestor if the pump is large. The riser pipe must be strong. In Africa, galvanised riser pipes are common but heavy. HDPE riser pipes are lighter and cheaper but need proper clamping. Use stainless steel or brass fittings where possible. Cheap galvanised fittings rust and seize. The wellhead must be sealed to prevent contamination. Install a sanitary seal and a vent. Now power options. Grid power is convenient but unreliable in many areas. Diesel and petrol pumps are powerful but fuel is expensive and engines need constant service. Solar pumps are now the best choice for many small and medium farms. They are quiet, need little maintenance, and the fuel is free after purchase. But solar has limits. It only pumps when the sun shines. You need storage or a hybrid system for night irrigation. When sizing solar, calculate the daily water requirement in cubic metres. Then calculate the energy needed. A rough formula is energy in kilowatt hours equals water volume in cubic metres times total head in metres divided by 367 times pump efficiency. Use a pump efficiency of 40 to 60 percent for small pumps. Then size the solar array to produce that energy in the available sun hours. In most of Africa, use 4 to 5 peak sun hours per day. Add 30 percent for losses and cloudy days. Use a solar controller or inverter that matches the pump. Do not connect panels directly to a pump unless the pump is designed for direct solar. Batteries are expensive and need care. If you use batteries, use deep cycle or lithium. Keep them in a cool, ventilated place. Check water levels in lead acid batteries every month. Clean terminals. For hybrid systems, a changeover switch lets you run from solar, grid, or generator. Label the switch clearly. Maintenance is where most technicians fail. A pump that is installed well but not maintained will still fail. Create a maintenance schedule. Daily: check for leaks, unusual noise, and vibration. Check the suction strainer. Record flow and pressure. Weekly: clean solar panels. Check battery water and terminals. Lubricate bearings where required. Monthly: check electrical connections. Test the non-return valve. Flush the system. Every three months: inspect the pump impeller and wear rings. Check the motor insulation. Replace worn parts. Annually: pull the submersible pump for a full service. Replace seals and bearings. Clean the borehole if needed. Train the farmer to do daily and weekly checks. Give them a simple checklist with pictures. A farmer who checks the system every morning will catch problems early. Common pump problems and solutions. No water: check power, check for air lock, check suction strainer, check water level. Low flow: check for leaks, worn impeller, blocked filter, or wrong voltage. High pressure: check for closed valves or blocked lines. Noise and vibration: check for loose mounting, worn bearings, or cavitation. Cavitation happens when the pump tries to pull more water than the source can give. It sounds like gravel in the pump. Fix it by reducing flow or lowering the pump. Overheating: check voltage, ventilation, and load. A pump that runs hot will fail soon. In Africa, lightning is a real threat. Install surge protection on electrical pumps. Ground the system properly. For solar, use surge protectors on the array and controller. Dust is another enemy. Keep panels clean. Cover pumps and controllers when not in use. Rodents chew wires. Use conduit or armoured cable. Now think about the business of pump services. You can earn money by installing pumps, servicing them, and selling spare parts. Offer an annual service contract. For a small fee, you visit the farm four times a year and do checks. This gives the farmer peace of mind and gives you steady income. Keep a stock of common spares: seals, bearings, impellers, capacitors, float switches, and foot valves. Build a relationship with a supplier in the capital city. Order parts before the dry season when demand is high. Price your services fairly. A service call should cover transport, labour, and a small profit. Do not overcharge. A farmer who trusts you will call you again and recommend you to neighbours. Document every service. Write the date, pump model, problem, action, and parts used. Leave a copy with the farmer. This record helps you diagnose future problems and proves your work. Finally, consider the end of life. When a pump is beyond repair, advise the farmer on a replacement. Do not just sell the same model. Review the farm's water needs, power situation, and budget. Sometimes a smaller pump with a bigger tank is better than a bigger pump. Sometimes a solar pump is better than a diesel pump even if the upfront cost is higher. Your advice should be based on numbers, not commission. In the next lesson we will cover pipes, fittings, and layout design. Before then, practice installing a small surface pump with a foot valve and a suction pipe. Time yourself. The faster and cleaner you work, the more jobs you can complete in a week. That is how you build a successful irrigation business.

3. Pipe Layout, Fittings, and Hydraulic Design for Efficient Irrigation

Pipes are the veins of an irrigation system. If they are too small, too long, or poorly joined, the crop suffers and the farmer loses money. In this lesson you will learn how to design and install pipe layouts that deliver the right amount of water to every plant. Start with the water requirement. Before you draw any pipe, calculate how much water the farm needs. Use the crop, the soil, and the climate. For vegetables in a hot area, a common figure is 5 to 8 millimetres per day. One millimetre on one hectare is 10 cubic metres. So 6 millimetres on one hectare is 60 cubic metres per day. That is 60,000 litres. If you irrigate for 4 hours, you need 15 cubic metres per hour, which is 250 litres per minute. Write this number down. It drives every pipe size decision. Now calculate the flow for each section. The main line carries all the water. The sub-main carries water to a block. The lateral carries water to the plants. Each section must be sized for the flow it carries. Use a pipe sizing chart. For uPVC and HDPE, a velocity of 1 to 1.5 metres per second is good. Above 2 metres per second, friction and water hammer increase. Below 0.5 metres per second, sediment settles. For a flow of 250 litres per minute, a 50 millimetre pipe gives about 2.1 metres per second, which is a bit high. A 63 millimetre pipe gives about 1.3 metres per second, which is good. So the main line should be 63 millimetres. The sub-main may be 50 millimetres. The laterals may be 16 or 20 millimetres for drip. Do not guess. Use the chart. If you do not have a chart, use a simple rule: for every 10 litres per minute, use 10 millimetres of pipe diameter for short runs. For long runs, go one size bigger. Now friction loss. Water rubbing against the pipe wall loses pressure. The longer the pipe and the smaller the diameter, the greater the loss. Fittings add loss too. A 90 degree elbow is like adding several metres of pipe. A tee is like adding a few metres. A valve adds more. Use long radius elbows where possible. Avoid sharp turns. Keep the layout simple. A straight line is better than a zigzag. If the farm is on a slope, use the slope to your advantage. Put the tank uphill and let gravity help. But remember that pressure increases with depth. Every 10 metres of height gives about 1 bar of pressure. That is good for sprinklers but may be too much for drip. Use a pressure reducing valve if needed. For flat farms, use a booster pump if pressure is low. Now design the layout. Start with a map of the farm. Mark the water source, the tank, the pump, and the blocks. Draw the main line from the source to the tank or from the pump to the blocks. Keep the main line as short and straight as possible. Put the control head at a central point. This reduces pipe length and makes operation easier. Divide the farm into blocks. Each block should have similar soil, crop, and slope. Each block should have its own valve. This lets you irrigate one block while repairing another. For drip, keep lateral runs under 100 metres. For sprinklers, keep them under 200 metres. Longer runs need bigger pipes or higher pressure. Use a looped main line for large farms. A loop gives two paths for water, reducing friction and improving pressure at the ends. It also lets you isolate a break without shutting down the whole farm. Now fittings. Use the right fitting for the job. For uPVC, use solvent cement fittings. Clean the pipe and fitting with primer. Apply cement to both surfaces. Push and twist a quarter turn. Hold for 30 seconds. Do not touch for 24 hours. For HDPE, use compression fittings or electrofusion. Compression fittings are easy but can leak if not tightened properly. Electrofusion is strong but needs a machine. For small farms, compression is fine. Use brass or stainless steel fittings at the pump and valves. Plastic threads strip easily. Use thread seal tape or paste. Do not over tighten. Support pipes every 2 to 3 metres. Use saddles or blocks. Bury main lines at least 30 centimetres deep. Deeper if tractors cross. Use sand bedding to protect the pipe. Mark the route with stones or a simple fence. Now valves and air. Install a gate valve at the pump outlet. Install a non-return valve after the pump. Install a pressure gauge after the non-return valve. Install an air release valve at every high point. Air trapped in the pipe reduces flow and causes noise. Install a drain valve at every low point. This lets you empty the system for repairs or frost. In Africa, frost is rare but draining is still useful for cleaning. Install a filter after the pump and before the main line. For drip, install a screen filter and a sand filter if the water is dirty. Install a pressure reducing valve before the drip laterals. Drip needs 1 to 1.5 bar. Sprinklers need 2 to 3 bar. Now testing. Before you bury any pipe, pressure test it. Close all valves. Fill the system. Raise the pressure to 1.5 times the working pressure. Hold for one hour. Check for leaks. Fix any leak and test again. Do not bury a leaking pipe. It will waste water and cause erosion. After testing, flush the system. Open the ends and let water run until it is clear. This removes dirt and cement pieces. Then install the emitters. Now think about the business. A good pipe layout saves money on pipes, fittings, and pumping. A bad layout wastes water and energy. Show the farmer the difference. For example, a 50 millimetre main line instead of a 63 millimetre line may save a little on pipe but will cost more in pumping every day. Over a season, the bigger pipe pays for itself. Also consider future expansion. Leave a spare valve and a capped tee at the end of the main line. The farmer may want to add a block later. A little extra now saves a lot later. Keep a simple drawing of the layout. Mark pipe sizes, valve positions, and block names. Laminate it and give it to the farmer. This drawing is your signature. It shows you are a professional. In the next lesson we will cover emitters, sprinklers, and drip systems in detail. Before then, practice drawing a layout for a one hectare farm with a borehole, a tank, and four blocks. Calculate the flow and choose pipe sizes. This exercise will make you confident on real jobs.

4. Emitter Selection, Drip, and Sprinkler Systems for African Crops

The emitter is where water meets the plant. Choosing the right emitter and managing it well is the difference between a good harvest and a wasted season. In this lesson you will learn about drip, sprinkler, and micro-sprinkler systems and how to match them to African crops and conditions. Start with the soil. Soil decides how fast water enters and how far it spreads. Sandy soil takes water quickly but holds little. Water moves down, not sideways. On sand, use higher flow emitters and shorter irrigation times, more often. Clay soil takes water slowly. Water spreads sideways. On clay, use lower flow emitters and longer times to avoid runoff. Loam is in between and is the easiest to manage. Test the soil with a simple jar test or a fist test. A jar test: put soil in a jar, add water, shake, and let it settle. Sand settles first, then silt, then clay. The layers show the percentages. A fist test: squeeze moist soil in your hand. If it forms a ball that holds together, it has good clay. If it crumbles, it is sandy. Now drip systems. Drip is the most efficient way to irrigate in Africa. It saves water, reduces weeds, and keeps leaves dry, which reduces disease. Drip is ideal for tomatoes, peppers, onions, cabbage, and other vegetables. It is also good for orchards and vines. There are two main types: inline drip tape and online drip lines. Inline drip tape is thin and cheap. It is good for one or two seasons. It is easy to lay and roll up. Online drip lines are thicker and last longer. They have emitters inserted at intervals. They are better for perennial crops. When choosing drip, look at emitter flow. Common flows are 1, 2, 4, and 8 litres per hour. For sandy soil, use 4 or 8 litres per hour. For clay, use 1 or 2 litres per hour. Look at emitter spacing. Common spacings are 20, 30, and 40 centimetres. Match the spacing to the plant spacing. For tomatoes at 40 centimetres, use 30 or 40 centimetre spacing. For maize, drip is less common but can work with 40 centimetre spacing. Now design the drip block. Keep lateral runs under 100 metres. Keep pressure at 1 to 1.5 bar. Use a pressure reducing valve. Use a filter. Drip emitters are small and clog easily. Use a screen filter of 120 mesh or finer. If the water is dirty, use a sand filter first. Flush the laterals regularly. Open the end of each lateral and let water run for a few minutes. Do this weekly. If the water is very dirty, do it daily. Now sprinklers. Sprinklers are good for field crops like maize, wheat, and fodder. They are also good for pasture. They cover a large area and are easy to see if they are working. But they use more water than drip and are affected by wind. In windy areas, use low angle sprinklers or irrigate at night. There are many types. Impact sprinklers are strong and simple. They work well with dirty water. Rotating sprinklers are quieter and more uniform. Micro-sprinklers are small and good for orchards and nurseries. When choosing sprinklers, look at flow, pressure, and wetted diameter. A common impact sprinkler gives 1 to 2 cubic metres per hour at 2 to 3 bar and covers 20 to 30 metres. Space them at 50 to 60 percent of the wetted diameter. For example, if the diameter is 24 metres, space them 12 to 15 metres apart. Use a triangular spacing for better uniformity. Now design the sprinkler block. Calculate the flow for the block. If you have 20 sprinklers at 1.5 cubic metres per hour, the block needs 30 cubic metres per hour. That is 500 litres per minute. Size the pipes for that flow. Use a main line of 75 or 90 millimetres. Use sub-mains of 50 or 63 millimetres. Use risers of 20 or 25 millimetres. Keep the pressure at the sprinkler within 10 percent of the design. Use a pressure gauge at the end of the line. If pressure is low, reduce the number of sprinklers per block or increase pipe size. Now micro-sprinklers. These are small sprinklers or sprayers that wet a small area. They are good for trees, vines, and vegetables. They use less water than sprinklers and are easier to inspect than drip. They need filtration but less than drip. Use a screen filter of 80 to 100 mesh. Keep pressure at 1.5 to 2 bar. Now management. Irrigation scheduling is the key to success. Do not irrigate by habit. Irrigate when the crop needs it. Use a simple soil moisture test. Dig a hole 15 centimetres deep. Squeeze the soil. If it forms a ball and leaves a slight moisture mark on your hand, it is fine. If it crumbles, irrigate. If it drips, wait. You can also use a tensiometer or a moisture meter if available. For drip, irrigate little and often. For sprinklers, irrigate longer and less often. This encourages deep roots. Now fertigation. Drip and micro-sprinklers can apply fertiliser with the water. This saves labour and improves efficiency. Use soluble fertilisers. Inject them before the filter. Use a simple venturi injector or a dosing pump. Flush the system after fertigation. Do not mix calcium and phosphate fertilisers. They form a precipitate and clog emitters. Now maintenance. Drip emitters clog from dirt, algae, and chemical precipitates. Flush laterals weekly. Clean filters daily. Use chlorine or acid occasionally if needed. But be careful. Chlorine can harm plants if too strong. Use 1 to 2 parts per million. Acid can damage pipes. Use only if you know what you are doing. Sprinklers clog from dirt and insects. Clean nozzles regularly. Check for worn bearings. Replace worn parts. Micro-sprinklers clog from dirt and algae. Clean them in a bucket of water. Now the business. Drip and sprinklers are not just hardware. They are a service. Offer to design, install, and maintain the system. Offer a training session for the farmer and workers. Show them how to flush, clean filters, and check emitters. A farmer who knows how to maintain the system will get more value and will recommend you. Also consider the cost. Drip costs more per hectare than sprinklers but saves water and labour. In areas with water scarcity or high labour costs, drip pays back quickly. In areas with plenty of water and cheap labour, sprinklers may be better. Do the numbers for the farmer. Show the payback period. For example, a drip system may cost 2,000 US dollars per hectare and save 500 US dollars per year in water and labour. That is a four year payback. If the crop is high value, the payback is faster. Finally, think about the future. Climate change is making rainfall less reliable. Irrigation is becoming more important. Farmers who invest in efficient systems will survive. Your job is to help them choose the right system and keep it working. In the next lesson we will cover water sources, storage, and scheduling. Before then, visit a farm with drip and a farm with sprinklers. Compare them. Write down the advantages and disadvantages. This will help you advise farmers better.

5. Water Sources, Storage, Scheduling, and Business Growth

Water is the most limiting factor in African agriculture. In this final lesson of Module 5 you will learn how to find, store, and schedule water, and how to grow your business as an irrigation technician. Start with water sources. The best source is the one that is reliable, clean, and close to the farm. Boreholes are common but expensive to drill. A good borehole gives water all year. Test the water for salinity and iron. High salinity harms crops. High iron clogs emitters. If salinity is high, use tolerant crops or blend with other water. If iron is high, use aeration and filtration. Shallow wells are cheaper but may dry up in the dry season. Rivers and streams are good but may be seasonal. Dams and ponds store rainwater. They lose water to evaporation and seepage. Line them with clay or plastic. Municipal water is convenient but expensive and may be unreliable. Rainwater harvesting is underused. Roofs, roads, and rock catchments can collect a lot of water. A 100 square metre roof can collect 80 cubic metres from 800 millimetres of rain. That is enough for a small garden. Teach farmers to harvest rain. Now storage. Storage lets you pump when energy is cheap and irrigate when the crop needs it. It also lets you settle sediment and cool water. Size the tank for at least one day of peak demand. For a one hectare vegetable farm, 40 to 60 cubic metres is a good start. For a small garden, 5 to 10 cubic metres is enough. Use plastic tanks for small volumes. Use concrete or lined earth for large volumes. Cover tanks to reduce evaporation and algae. Put the tank uphill if possible. Gravity saves pumping. If the tank is downhill, use a booster pump. Now scheduling. Scheduling is deciding when and how much to irrigate. The goal is to keep the soil moisture in the root zone between field capacity and wilting point. Field capacity is when the soil holds all the water it can against gravity. Wilting point is when plants can no longer extract water. The difference is available water. Do not let the soil dry below 50 percent of available water for most crops. Use a simple check. Dig a hole. Feel the soil. If it is dry at 15 centimetres, irrigate. If it is moist, wait. Use a calendar as a guide but not as a rule. Weather changes. Crops change. Soil changes. A good technician checks the soil every day. For drip, irrigate every day or every second day. For sprinklers, irrigate every 3 to 7 days. Apply enough water to wet the root zone. For shallow rooted crops like onions, the root zone is 30 centimetres. For deep rooted crops like maize, it is 60 centimetres. One millimetre of water wets one centimetre of soil if the soil is dry. So 30 millimetres wets 30 centimetres. But soil type matters. Sandy soil holds less water, so apply less and more often. Clay soil holds more, so apply more and less often. Now water quality. Test the water for pH, salinity, and specific ions. pH should be 6.5 to 8.5. Salinity should be below 2 decisiemens per metre for most crops. Sodium and chloride should be low. If water is bad, choose tolerant crops or mix with good water. Do not use water with high boron. It harms many crops. Now the business. You are not just a technician. You are a problem solver. Farmers pay for solutions, not for parts. Listen to the farmer. What is the problem? Is it low yield, high labour, dry season hunger, or poor quality? Then offer a solution. Maybe it is a solar pump. Maybe it is drip. Maybe it is just a better schedule. Do not sell what you have. Sell what they need. Build a simple business plan. What services will you offer? Installation, maintenance, training, and design. What is your target market? Smallholder farmers, commercial farmers, NGOs, or government projects. What is your price? Cost plus a fair profit. What is your advantage? Speed, quality, local knowledge, or spare parts. Keep records. Every job should have a file. Write the farmer's name, location, phone number, crop, water source, system design, and date. Take photos. This helps you remember and helps you prove your work. Ask for referrals. A happy farmer will tell neighbours. Offer a small discount for referrals. Build a network. Work with borehole drillers, solar installers, and agro-dealers. They can refer clients to you. Join a farmer group or cooperative. Offer a group discount. Train farmers. A training session can be a source of income and a way to get installation jobs. Charge a small fee or ask the group to pay. Use a simple manual with pictures. Teach in the local language. Now think about growth. Start small. Do good work. Save money. Buy tools. A good pipe wrench, a hacksaw, a measuring tape, a level, a pressure gauge, and a multimeter are essential. Buy a small pump for testing. Buy a solar panel and controller for demonstrations. As you grow, hire an assistant. Train them well. Pay them fairly. Expand to nearby districts. Keep learning. Attend training. Read manuals. Join online groups. Irrigation technology is changing. Solar pumps, drip tape, and sensors are getting cheaper. Be the first to know. Now risk management. Farmers may not pay on time. Ask for a deposit. Use a simple contract. Write the scope, price, and payment schedule. Do not do work without a deposit. Weather can ruin a job. Plan for rain. Keep tarps and pumps to dewater trenches. Accidents can happen. Wear gloves and boots. Use safe practices. Insure your business if possible. Finally, think about impact. Your work helps farmers grow more food, earn more money, and send children to school. It helps communities survive drought. It is a noble profession. Do it with pride and honesty. In this module you have learned about components, pumps, pipes, emitters, and water management. You are now ready to go out and practice. Start with a small job. Do it well. Then do another. Your reputation will grow. Your business will

<h2>Practical Reference: Field Commissioning, Troubleshooting and Operating Records for Irrigation Systems in African Conditions</h2>

<p>This reference lesson is written for the working irrigation technician and the small agribusiness that employs them. It assumes you already know how to size a pump, lay pipe, and choose emitters. What it does not assume is that you have a system for turning a newly installed irrigation kit into a reliable, profitable, season-after-season asset. In African conditions variable grid power, dusty air, hard water, and tight cash cycles the difference between a system that pays for itself in one season and one that becomes a repair bill is commissioning discipline, a fault-finding routine, and written records. This module gives you all three, with the numbers and sources to back them up.</p>

<p>The diagram above is the mental map for every job you do. Water moves from source to pump, through filtration and valves, down the mainline and laterals, out of the emitters, and into the root zone. Every fault you will ever be called to fix sits on one of those blocks. The loop at the bottom is the part most technicians skip: after you fix or install, you must re-measure pressure, flow, and uniformity, and you must write it down. That written record is what turns a repair into a business asset it lets you quote maintenance contracts, prove your work, and predict failures before the farmer loses a crop.</p>

<p>Note the salinity and pH rows. In many African irrigation schemes the Nile Delta, the Senegal River valley, parts of the Rift Valley, and coastal aquifers salinity is the silent yield killer. FAO 29 (Ayers &amp; Westcot) remains the standard reference for interpreting irrigation water quality, and it is free to download. If your EC reading is above 2 dS/m, do not just install the system and walk away; advise the farmer on leaching fraction, crop choice, and drainage. That advice is billable expertise.</p>

<p>Every row in Table 2 is a chance to sell a service, not just a part. The technician who only fixes the fault earns once. The technician who documents the fault, explains the cause, and offers a preventive plan earns every season. In African markets where farmers often buy equipment from informal suppliers with no after-sales support, this is your competitive edge.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #c62828; background:#fdecea; padding:12px; margin:16px 0;">
<strong style="color:#c62828;">SAFETY WARNING Chemicals, Fertigation, and Water Treatment</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Irrigation water is not drinking water. When you handle chlorine, acids, or fertilizer concentrates for fertigation or line cleaning, you are handling hazardous chemicals. Always wear gloves, eye protection, and a mask when mixing; never mix acid with chlorine it releases toxic gas; store chemicals locked away from children and animals; and never allow backflow of chemicals into a drinking-water source. Install a backflow preventer or air gap. Follow WHO guidelines for safe use of chemicals in water and the product label the label is the law. If a farmer reports illness or a chemical spill, stop work, isolate the area, and seek medical advice immediately.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist Field Commissioning</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Before start-up:</strong> confirm water source is clear, pump is primed, and all valves are in the correct position.</li>
<li><strong>Pressure:</strong> record pressure at the pump, at the filter, and at the farthest emitter; compare with design.</li>
<li><strong>Flow:</strong> measure emitter output at 1015 points across the block; calculate variation and DU.</li>
<li><strong>Filtration:</strong> check filter pressure differential; backwash if above 0.5 bar; record the reading.</li>
<li><strong>Water quality:</strong> test EC and pH; note any salinity risk and advise on leaching or crop choice.</li>
<li><strong>Leaks:</strong> walk the mainline and laterals; mark and repair all visible leaks before handover.</li>
<li><strong>Records:</strong> complete a commissioning sheet with date, readings, photos, and farmer signature.</li>
<li><strong>Training:</strong> show the farmer how to flush, backwash, and read the pressure gauge; leave a one-page schedule.</li>
</ol>

<p>That checklist is not bureaucracy. It is the evidence that protects you if a farmer later claims the system was faulty, and it is the baseline against which you diagnose future problems. A commissioning sheet with real numbers is worth more than a verbal promise. In many African markets, word-of-mouth reputation is the main marketing channel for technicians a signed, well-documented handover is what gets you referred to the next farm.</p>

<h3>Operating Records and the Business Case</h3>
<p>Keep a simple logbook for each client: date, water source level, pump hours, pressure readings, filter backwash, emitter checks, and any repairs. After one season you will be able to show the farmer the trend for example, that filter differential is creeping up, or that pressure at the far end is falling. That trend is your early warning and your sales pitch for a maintenance contract. In a continent where irrigation can raise yields by 50100% compared with rain-fed farming, protecting that investment with a small annual service fee is an easy business case to make. FAO's irrigation manuals emphasise exactly this: monitoring and evaluation are not optional extras, they are part of the system.</p>

<p>Finally, price your services for the African reality: smallholder cash flow is seasonal. Offer a package commissioning plus two service visits plus a water-quality test priced as a single fee, and time it to the start of the season when the farmer has income from the previous harvest or from a cooperative loan. That is the business-first approach this module is built around.</p>
