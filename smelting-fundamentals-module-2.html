Lessons

1. Ore Types and Characteristics

Before you smelt anything, you need to know what you're working with. In Africa, common smelting ores include iron oxides like hematite (Fe2O3) and magnetite (Fe3O4), copper sulfides like chalcopyrite (CuFeS2), and lead sulfides like galena (PbS). Each behaves differently in the furnace. Hematite is reddish and relatively easy to reduce, while magnetite is black, magnetic, and denser. Chalcopyrite is brassy yellow and needs roasting to remove sulfur before smelting. Galena is silvery-grey and heavy, but it releases toxic lead fumes, so you must handle it carefully.

You can identify ores with simple tests. Use a streak plate: hematite leaves a red-brown streak, magnetite leaves a black streak. A magnet picks up magnetite but not hematite. For sulfides, roast a small sample on a charcoal fire: chalcopyrite gives off sulfur dioxide (rotten egg smell) and turns into copper oxide. Galena melts easily and gives off white lead oxide fumes. Always work outdoors or with good ventilation.

Ore grade matters. A good iron ore contains 50-65% iron. Copper ore might be only 0.5-2% copper, so you need to concentrate it. In Africa, many small-scale operations mine laterite (iron-rich soil) or alluvial deposits. Know your ore's hardness: quartz-rich ores are abrasive and wear out crushers quickly. Clay-rich ores stick and clog equipment. Test hardness with a knife: if it scratches, it's soft; if it scratches the knife, it's hard.

Moisture content affects handling. Wet ore is heavy and can freeze in cold highlands or turn to mud in rainy seasons. Dry ore is dusty and can be lost to wind. Always store ore under cover. Finally, check for harmful elements like arsenic or phosphorus. High phosphorus in iron ore makes brittle metal. High arsenic in copper ore is dangerous. If you can't test in a lab, send a sample to a local university or geological survey.

2. Crushing and Grinding Techniques

Crushing reduces ore from large rocks to small pieces. Start with a jaw crusher: feed rocks no larger than 80% of the jaw opening. For a 100 mm x 150 mm jaw crusher, feed rocks up to 80 mm. The output is typically 10-20 mm. If you don't have a jaw crusher, use a sledgehammer on a steel plate. Wear safety glasses and gloves. Break ore into pieces about the size of a fist.

Next, grind the ore to powder. A ball mill is best: a rotating steel cylinder with steel balls inside. For a small operation, a 1 m diameter mill turning at 30-40 rpm with 50 kg of 50 mm steel balls will grind 100 kg of ore per hour to less than 1 mm. If you lack a ball mill, use a stamp mill (common in Zimbabwe and South Africa) or a mortar and pestle for tiny batches. For gold ore, you need finer grinding: 75 microns (0.075 mm) or less to liberate gold particles. A ball mill can do this in 2-4 hours.

Control the particle size. After crushing, screen the ore. Oversize goes back to the crusher. After grinding, check with a sieve: 200 mesh (0.074 mm) is a common target. If too coarse, grind longer. If too fine, you waste energy and create slimes that are hard to handle. Add water during grinding to reduce dust and prevent overheating. The mixture of ore and water is called pulp. Keep pulp density at 60-70% solids by weight.

Maintenance is critical. Check crusher jaws for wear every 50 hours. Replace worn plates. In ball mills, check ball charge: add balls to maintain 40-50% of mill volume. Lubricate bearings daily. In Africa, power can be unreliable, so consider a diesel-powered mill. Always lock out equipment before maintenance. Train workers to never put hands into a running crusher. Use a rod to clear blockages.

3. Screening and Classification

Screening separates ore by size using woven wire mesh or punched plates. A vibrating screen is most efficient: it shakes at 800-1200 rpm with a stroke of 3-5 mm. For small operations, a manual screen (a frame with mesh) works. Common mesh sizes: 10 mm for coarse, 5 mm for medium, 1 mm for fine. Always screen before crushing to remove fines that would otherwise clog the crusher. After crushing, screen again to send oversize back to the crusher.

Classification separates particles by settling velocity in water or air. A hydrocyclone is a cone-shaped device that uses centrifugal force. Feed pulp at 50-100 kPa pressure. Coarse particles go to the underflow (bottom), fine particles to the overflow (top). A 100 mm hydrocyclone can handle 5-10 m3/h. For gold recovery, a cyclone can cut at 75 microns. If you don't have a cyclone, use a settling tank: let pulp stand for 30 minutes; coarse sand settles first, then silt, then clay. Decant the muddy water.

In Africa, many artisanal miners use a simple sluice box for classification and concentration. A sluice is a long trough with riffles. Feed pulp at a steady rate. Heavy particles (gold, cassiterite) settle behind riffles; light particles wash away. Angle the sluice at 5-15 degrees. Use a gentle water flow. Check riffles every 30 minutes and clean them out.

Efficiency matters. Overloading a screen reduces efficiency: never feed more than 80% of screen capacity. For a 1 m x 2 m screen, capacity is about 10-20 t/h per mm of aperture. Keep screens clean: brush or wash them. Replace torn mesh immediately. For classification, monitor cyclone pressure and underflow density. If underflow is too wet, adjust the apex valve. Always wear a dust mask when handling dry screened ore. Silica dust causes silicosis.

4. Concentration Methods: Gravity Separation

Gravity separation uses density differences to separate heavy minerals from light waste. It works best for ores with a density difference of at least 2.5 g/cm3. Gold (19.3 g/cm3) is easy to separate from quartz (2.65 g/cm3). Cassiterite (7.0 g/cm3) separates from sand. But galena (7.5 g/cm3) and sphalerite (4.0 g/cm3) are close, so gravity alone may not work.

The simplest tool is a gold pan. Fill the pan with crushed ore and water. Swirl gently. Light particles wash over the edge; gold settles at the bottom. Practice with known samples. A good panner can recover 90% of gold coarser than 100 mesh. For larger scale, use a sluice box. Build a sluice 3 m long, 30 cm wide, with riffles every 20 cm. Line the bottom with a blanket or miners' moss. Feed pulp at 20-50 L/min. Angle at 10 degrees. Clean the sluice every 2 hours.

A shaking table is more efficient. It has a tilted deck with riffles that shakes side-to-side. Feed pulp evenly. Heavy particles move along the riffles to one side; light particles wash off the other. A small table (1 m x 2 m) handles 0.5-2 t/h. Adjust stroke length (10-20 mm) and speed (250-300 rpm). In Africa, tables are used for gold, tin, and tantalum. They are expensive but pay off in recovery.

Jigs are another option. A jig pulses water through a bed of ore. Heavy particles settle; light particles rise. A 0.5 m2 jig handles 1-3 t/h. Use a screen with 2-5 mm openings. Control water flow: too fast and you lose fine gold; too slow and separation is poor. Always pre-screen ore to remove slimes (less than 0.5 mm). Slimes interfere with gravity separation. For best results, classify ore into narrow size ranges before gravity separation. Test with a small sample first.

5. Concentration Methods: Flotation

Flotation separates minerals by making some particles water-repellent (hydrophobic) so they attach to air bubbles and float. It works for sulfide ores like chalcopyrite, galena, and sphalerite. You need a flotation cell, reagents, and aeration. A simple cell can be a 200 L drum with an impeller and air inlet. For small-scale, use a 20 L bucket and a hand mixer.

Reagents are key. Collectors make minerals hydrophobic. For copper sulfide, use potassium amyl xanthate (PAX) at 20-50 g/t. For lead, use sodium isopropyl xanthate. Add a frother like pine oil (20-50 g/t) to stabilize bubbles. Add a depressant like sodium cyanide (50-100 g/t) to keep unwanted minerals from floating. Add a pH modifier: lime (CaO) to raise pH to 10-11 for copper, or soda ash to lower pH. Always add reagents in order: pH modifier, then collector, then frother. Mix for 5-10 minutes.

Operate the cell. Feed pulp at 25-35% solids. Aerate at 0.5-1.0 m3/min per m3 of cell volume. Agitate at 800-1200 rpm. Bubbles rise and carry sulfide minerals to the surface. Skim the froth into a launder. Collect for 5-15 minutes. The froth is concentrate; the remaining pulp is tailings. For a 20 L bucket, use 1-2 g of collector, 1 g of frother, and 5 g of lime. Run for 10 minutes. You should see a mineralized froth.

Safety first. Many flotation reagents are toxic. Wear gloves, goggles, and a respirator. Never eat or smoke near reagents. Store reagents in sealed containers away from children. In Africa, flotation is used at large mines like Kansanshi in Zambia and Tenke Fungurume in DRC. For artisanal miners, flotation is complex, but it can be done with training. Always test ore in a lab to determine reagent dosages. Too much reagent wastes money and can kill fish if tailings are released. Treat tailings in a pond before discharge.

6. Drying and Roasting of Ores

Drying removes moisture before smelting. Wet ore causes explosions in furnaces and wastes heat. Sun-dry ore on a concrete pad or black plastic sheet. Spread ore in a layer 5-10 cm thick. Rake it every 2 hours. In hot African sun, ore dries in 1-3 days. For faster drying, use a rotary dryer: a horizontal cylinder heated by a diesel burner. Feed ore at 10-20% moisture; output at 1-2% moisture. A 1 m diameter, 5 m long dryer handles 5-10 t/h. Temperature: 200-400C. Do not overheat: sulfides can ignite.

Roasting drives off sulfur, carbon dioxide, and water from ores. It is essential for sulfide ores. For chalcopyrite, roast at 500-700C in air. The reaction: 2CuFeS2 + 3O2 2CuO + 2FeO + 2SO2. The sulfur dioxide gas is toxic and corrosive. Always roast outdoors or in a fume hood. Build a simple roasting hearth: a shallow pit with a grate and a chimney. Layer ore 5-10 cm deep over a wood or charcoal fire. Stir every 15 minutes. Roast for 2-4 hours until the ore turns from yellow to dark grey or red. For galena, roast at 600-800C to form lead oxide (PbO).

Control temperature. Too low: sulfur remains. Too high: ore melts and sticks. Use a thermocouple or color: dull red is about 500C, cherry red is 700C. In Africa, roasting is often done in open fires, but this releases SO2. To reduce pollution, use a retort or scrubber. A simple scrubber: pass gases through a drum of lime water (Ca(OH)2). The lime neutralizes SO2 to form gypsum (CaSO4).

Safety and health. Roasting produces toxic gases: SO2, arsenic trioxide, and lead fumes. Wear a respirator with acid gas cartridges. Never roast in an enclosed space. Keep children and animals away. After roasting, let the ore cool before handling. Store roasted ore in sealed bags to prevent re-absorption of moisture. For best smelting results, roast ore to less than 0.5% sulfur. Test with a simple sulfide test: add a few drops of hydrochloric acid; if it smells like rotten eggs, sulfur remains.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Ore Preparation and Handling for African Smelters

Ore preparation is where smelter economics are won or lost. In African operations whether you are running a copper concentrator in Zambia, a gold CIL plant in Ghana, or a ferrochrome furnace in South Africa the cost of getting ore from the pit to the furnace feed chute typically accounts for 4060% of total processing cost. This practical reference consolidates the key parameters, equipment selection logic, and operating targets you need to design, audit, or troubleshoot an ore preparation circuit. It is written for plant managers, metallurgists, and business owners who need to make capital and operating decisions, not for academic study.

Why Ore Preparation Determines Smelter Profitability

Every tonne of ore you mine and handle carries a cost. Every tonne you reject before the furnace is a tonne you do not pay to smelt. The business case for ore preparation rests on three levers:

  • Grade uplift: Concentration raises the grade from, say, 0.8% Cu in run-of-mine to 2530% Cu in concentrate a 30-fold reduction in mass to smelt.
  • Furnace stability: Consistent particle size and moisture content prevent furnace upsets, reduce refractory wear, and lower specific energy consumption.
  • Logistics: Concentrate is far cheaper to transport per contained metal unit than raw ore, which matters enormously in landlocked African jurisdictions where road and rail costs are high.

Key Ore Characteristics That Drive Circuit Design

Before selecting equipment, characterise your ore. The following parameters determine everything downstream:

  • Hardness (Bond Work Index): Measured in kWh/t. African copper ores in the Zambian Copperbelt typically range from 1216 kWh/t; gold ores in Ghana and Tanzania often 1420 kWh/t. Higher Wi means more grinding energy and larger mills.
  • Abrasion index: Determines liner and media wear rates a major cost driver in remote African operations where spares logistics are expensive.
  • Liberation size: The grind size at which valuable minerals separate from gangue. This sets your target P80 and mill power.
  • Mineralogy: Sulphide vs oxide vs mixed. Sulphides suit flotation; oxides often need gravity, leaching, or roasting. Transition ores (common in DRC and Zambia) require flexible circuits.
  • Moisture and clay content: High clay ores (common in West African lateritic gold deposits) cause handling, screening, and chute blockage problems.
  • Bulk density and angle of repose: Critical for stockpile, conveyor, and bin design.

Crushing and Grinding: Equipment Selection Logic

Comminution is the single largest energy consumer in a concentrator often 5070% of plant electricity. In African operations where grid power is unreliable or expensive (diesel-generated power in remote DRC or Tanzanian sites can exceed USD 0.25/kWh), energy efficiency is a direct profitability lever.

Crushing circuits typically follow a three-stage flow: primary jaw or gyratory (ROM to 150 mm), secondary cone (to 50 mm), tertiary cone or HPGR (to 12 mm). For high-tonnage operations (above 5 Mtpa), gyratory primaries are preferred. For smaller or mobile operations, jaw crushers dominate.

Grinding circuits are either SAG-ball (autogenous/semi-autogenous) or multi-stage crush-ball. SAG circuits suit hard, competent ores and large tonnages. For African operations with variable ore hardness common in transitional oxide-sulphide zones a SAG mill with variable speed drive and pebble crushing gives operational flexibility.

HPGR (High Pressure Grinding Rolls) is increasingly adopted in African iron ore and diamond operations for its energy efficiency (1530% savings vs conventional tertiary crushing) and lower media consumption.

Screening and Classification

Screening separates by size; classification separates by settling velocity (hydrocyclones, spiral classifiers). Key operating targets:

  • Screen efficiency: Target 8595% for fine screens; below 80% indicates blinding, overload, or worn panels.
  • Hydrocyclone cut point: Typically 75150 m for base metals. Cyclone efficiency is sensitive to feed pressure (target 100150 kPa) and pulp density (target 4560% solids).
  • Circulating load: Target 250350% in closed-circuit ball milling. Excessive circulating load signals over-grinding or poor classification.

Concentration Methods: Gravity and Flotation

Gravity separation exploits density differences. It is low-cost, chemical-free, and effective for gold (free milling), tin, tungsten, chromite, and iron ore. Equipment includes jigs, spirals, shaking tables, and Knelson/Falcon concentrators. In African artisanal and small-scale gold operations, gravity concentration is often the primary recovery method. For large operations, gravity is used as a pre-concentration step ahead of flotation or leaching.

Flotation exploits surface chemistry differences. It dominates sulphide concentration copper, lead, zinc, nickel, PGM. In Zambia and DRC, flotation is the backbone of copper-cobalt concentrate production. Key variables: grind size (liberation), pulp pH, reagent dosage (collector, frother, depressant), aeration rate, and froth depth. Flotation recovery of copper sulphides typically ranges from 8593%; PGM flotation in South Africa achieves 8088% recovery.

Drying and Roasting

Drying reduces moisture to levels acceptable for downstream smelting or transport. Rotary dryers are common; flash dryers suit fine concentrates. Target moisture: 68% for smelter feed, below 1% for some roasting or briquetting applications. In humid African climates (e.g., DRC, Ghana), drying is often essential before transport to prevent cargo liquefaction a serious shipping hazard.

Roasting is a thermal treatment in air or controlled atmosphere. It is used to:

  • Oxidise sulphides (dead roasting) e.g., zinc sulphide to zinc oxide before leaching.
  • Remove volatiles (arsenic, sulphur) important for refractory gold ores.
  • Induce phase changes (magnetising roasting for iron ore).
  • Generate SO for acid plants common in Zambian and South African copper smelters.

Roasting is energy-intensive and requires careful gas handling. In African operations, roasting is typically integrated with acid plants to capture SO and produce sulphuric acid a saleable by-product that improves project economics.

⚠ SAFETY WARNING ORE PREPARATION HAZARDS

Ore preparation exposes workers to crystalline silica dust (respirable <10 m), a confirmed carcinogen causing silicosis and lung cancer. In Southern African mining, silicosis rates remain a major occupational health issue. Mandatory controls: water suppression on crushers and conveyors, local exhaust ventilation, enclosed transfer points, and fit-tested P2/N95 respirators where dust exceeds 0.05 mg/m (8-hour TWA). Rotating equipment crushers, mills, conveyors causes entanglement and crushing injuries; lock-out/tag-out (LOTO) is non-negotiable. Roasting generates SO and arsenic trioxide; gas monitoring and SCBA are required. Grinding media can eject from mills never approach a running mill. Comply with South African Mine Health and Safety Act (MHSA) and Zambian Mining Regulations. Report all incidents; near-misses predict fatalities.

Business-First Operating Targets

For an African smelter or concentrator, track these KPIs weekly:

  • Specific energy consumption (kWh/t): Benchmark against design; a 10% overshoot on a 5 Mtpa plant at USD 0.12/kWh costs over USD 600,000 annually.
  • Grind size P80: Consistency matters more than absolute value; variability above 10% destabilises flotation.
  • Recovery and concentrate grade: The grade-recovery curve defines your revenue; optimise for net smelter return (NSR), not recovery alone.
  • Reagent consumption (g/t): Flotation reagents are a top-3 operating cost; benchmark against similar African operations.
  • Availability and utilisation: Target 92% mechanical availability, 85% utilisation. Unplanned downtime in remote sites is disproportionately costly due to spares lead times.
  • Water and power per tonne: In water-scarce South Africa and power-constrained Zambia, these are strategic constraints.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Characterise the ore first. Bond Wi, abrasion index, liberation size, mineralogy, and moisture before you buy equipment.
  2. Match grind size to liberation. Over-grinding wastes energy and creates slimes that hurt flotation; under-grinding loses recovery.
  3. Design for variability. African orebodies often transition oxide-to-sulphide; build flexibility into the circuit (variable speed drives, reagent dosing ranges).
  4. Control dust and water. Silica dust kills; water scarcity constrains. Balance both with closed-circuit water recovery and dust suppression.
  5. Benchmark energy and reagents. Compare kWh/t and g/t against published African peer data quarterly.
  6. Maintain spares critically. In remote sites, hold 612 months of critical spares (mill liners, screen panels, cyclone parts, reagents).
  7. Train operators on grade-recovery economics. Operators who understand NSR make better decisions than those chasing recovery alone.
  8. Audit safety monthly. LOTO compliance, dust monitoring, and PPE use safety failures destroy licence to operate and morale.
