1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Distribution and route sales are the backbone of a bakery business. You can bake the best bread in town, but if it doesn't reach customers, you make no money. In many African cities and towns, people buy bread from kiosks, small shops, or directly from vendors who move through neighborhoods. A well-planned route ensures your bread is fresh, on time, and available where people are. Without a system, you waste fuel, time, and bread. With a system, you can double your sales in a month.

Think about your typical customer. In Nairobi, someone might buy bread at 6 AM from a vendor at a bus stop. In Lagos, a woman sells bread from a wheelbarrow near a market. In Accra, a delivery van drops bread at 10 small shops before 8 AM. Each situation needs a different approach, but the principle is the same: get the right amount of bread to the right place at the right time. That is distribution.

Route sales mean you or your salespeople follow a set path each day. You visit the same customers at the same time. This builds trust. Customers know when to expect you. You also learn exactly how much bread each stop needs, so you reduce waste. For example, if Mama Ada's shop sells 20 loaves every morning, you bring 20, not 30. That saves you money.

Why does this matter for a small bakery? Because bread is perishable. It loses freshness within hours. If you deliver late, customers may refuse it. If you deliver too much, you lose money on unsold loaves. A good route system also helps you plan production. You know how much to bake because you know your route demand. This module will teach you the tools, steps, and best practices to set up your own distribution and route sales system. You will learn how to choose vehicles, plan routes, handle money, and avoid common mistakes. By the end, you will be able to design a route that works for your bakery, whether you use a bicycle, motorbike, or van.

2. Tools and Materials

To run a successful distribution and route sales operation, you need the right tools. Start with a reliable transport method. For small bakeries, a bicycle with a rear rack and large basket can carry up to 50 loaves. A motorbike with a insulated box can carry 100-150 loaves. A small van or pickup can carry 500+ loaves. Choose based on your daily production and distance. In many African cities, motorbikes are best because they are cheap to run and can navigate traffic and narrow roads.

Next, you need insulated containers or boxes. Bread stays fresh longer if kept warm or at least protected from sun and rain. You can use locally made wooden boxes lined with foam, or buy plastic insulated crates. For example, a 60-liter cooler box can hold about 40 loaves. Label each box with your bakery name and phone number for free advertising.

You also need a record-keeping system. A simple notebook and pen work, but a smartphone is better. Use a notebook to record each stop: customer name, amount ordered, amount delivered, amount returned, and payment. At the end of the day, tally sales. A small calculator helps. If you have a smartphone, use a free app like Google Sheets to track. Also, keep a cash box or pouch for money. Never mix personal money with business money.

Other useful items: a weighing scale (to check loaf weights if needed), plastic bags for customers, a hand sanitizer or water for hygiene, a spare tire or repair kit for your vehicle, and a rain coat. In rainy season, you must protect bread from getting wet. A simple tarpaulin or plastic sheet can cover your boxes.

Finally, consider a uniform or apron with your bakery logo. This makes you look professional and builds trust. In many African markets, customers buy from people they recognize. A clean apron and a smile are free but powerful tools. Keep your vehicle clean and well-maintained. A breakdown on route means lost sales and spoiled bread. So set aside a small amount each week for fuel, repairs, and replacements. With these tools, you are ready to plan your route.

3. Step by Step Method

Step 1: Map your area. Draw a simple map of your neighborhood or town. Mark potential customers: shops, kiosks, schools, offices, bus stops, and residential areas. Use Google Maps on your phone if available. Identify main roads and shortcuts.

Step 2: Group customers into a route. Start from your bakery. Plan a loop that covers all stops without doubling back. For example, go east first, then south, then west, then north back to bakery. This saves fuel and time. Aim to finish the route in 2-3 hours for morning delivery.

Step 3: Set a timetable. Decide what time you leave and how long you spend at each stop. For morning bread, leave by 5:30 AM so you reach shops by 6:00 AM. Spend no more than 5 minutes per stop. If a customer needs more time, schedule them later.

Step 4: Load your vehicle correctly. Put heavier boxes at the bottom, lighter on top. Keep bread away from exhaust fumes. Use insulated boxes. Count loaves as you load. For example, if you have 10 stops and each gets 20 loaves, load 200 loaves. Double-check.

Step 5: Follow the route. Greet each customer, deliver the agreed amount, and collect payment. If a customer wants extra, sell if you have spare. If they want less, note it and adjust tomorrow. Always give a receipt or mark in your notebook. Keep money in a secure pouch.

Step 6: Handle returns. At the end of the route, count unsold loaves. If you have 5 left, you can sell them at a discount on the way back or to a nearby shop. Never return stale bread to the bakery for next day; it lowers quality. Record returns to adjust production.

Step 7: Reconcile and report. Back at the bakery, count cash, compare with notebook. Note any discrepancies. Report to the owner or manager. Discuss which stops are growing or shrinking. Adjust route for next day.

Step 8: Improve continuously. Every week, review your route. Can you add a stop? Can you combine two stops? Are you spending too much time in traffic? Ask customers for feedback. A simple "How can we serve you better?" can reveal new opportunities. With this step-by-step method, you will build a reliable distribution system that grows your sales.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Overloading the vehicle. Carrying too many loaves can damage bread, cause accidents, and slow you down. Fix: Know your vehicle's capacity. For a motorbike, never exceed 150 loaves. Use a second trip if needed.

Mistake 2: No fixed route. Wandering around hoping to find customers wastes fuel and time. Fix: Plan a route and stick to it. Use a map and timetable. After one week, you will see the benefit.

Mistake 3: Late delivery. If you arrive after 8 AM, shops may have already bought from another supplier. Fix: Start early. Load the night before if possible. Set an alarm. Aim to be at the first stop by 6 AM.

Mistake 4: Poor record keeping. Not writing down sales leads to theft, confusion, and wrong production. Fix: Use a notebook or phone app. Record every transaction immediately. Reconcile daily.

Mistake 5: Ignoring customer feedback. If a shop says your bread is often stale, don't argue. Fix: Check your delivery time and storage. Maybe you need to deliver earlier or use better insulation.

Mistake 6: Mixing money. Using sales cash for personal expenses ruins the business. Fix: Keep a separate cash box. Deposit business money daily or weekly.

Mistake 7: Not maintaining the vehicle. A breakdown on route means lost sales. Fix: Do daily checks: tires, fuel, oil. Carry a repair kit. Service regularly.

Mistake 8: Delivering wrong quantities. Bringing 30 loaves when only 20 are needed creates waste. Fix: Confirm orders the day before. Keep a customer order book. Adjust based on history.

Mistake 9: Poor hygiene. Dirty vehicle or hands turn customers away. Fix: Clean your vehicle daily. Wear a clean apron. Use hand sanitizer.

Mistake 10: Giving credit without tracking. Selling on credit is okay, but if you don't record it, you lose money. Fix: Use a credit book. Set a limit. Follow up weekly.

By avoiding these common mistakes, you will run a smoother, more profitable distribution system. Remember, small fixes lead to big gains.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety first. As a route salesperson, you face risks: road accidents, theft, and health issues. Always wear a helmet if on a motorbike or bicycle. Use reflective clothing in early morning or evening. Follow traffic rules. Do not overload, as it affects balance and braking. In many African cities, roads can be dangerous. Be defensive. Assume other drivers may not see you.

Protect your money. Carry cash in a secure pouch, not in your pocket. Avoid displaying large amounts. If possible, use mobile money (like M-Pesa, MTN MoMo, or Airtel Money) for payments. This reduces theft risk and makes record-keeping easier. If you must carry cash, vary your route slightly each day to avoid being predictable.

Food safety is critical. Bread must be kept clean and covered. Do not place bread on the ground or on dirty surfaces. Use insulated boxes. Wash your hands before handling bread. If you sneeze or cough, turn away. Never sell bread that has been dropped or contaminated. Customers trust you; keep that trust.

Best practices for customer service: Greet warmly. Be on time. Smile. Listen to complaints. If a customer is unhappy, offer a replacement or refund. Word of mouth is powerful. A happy customer tells others. An unhappy one tells more.

Best practices for efficiency: Keep your vehicle organized. Load in reverse order of delivery: last stop's bread at the front. Use a checklist. At each stop, deliver, collect payment, and mark the notebook. Do not linger. If a customer wants to chat, be polite but brief.

Best practices for growth: Track your sales per stop. Identify top customers. Offer them a small discount for bulk orders. Ask for referrals. Add new stops only if they fit your route. Review your route monthly. What works in rainy season may not work in dry season.

Finally, take care of yourself. Eat breakfast. Carry water. Wear comfortable shoes. Fatigue leads to mistakes. If you feel sick, rest. Your health is your business. By following these safety and best practices, you will build a reliable, respected route sales operation that supports your bakery and your community.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it's time to put what you learned into practice. This exercise will help you design your own distribution and route sales plan. You will need a notebook, pen, and if possible, a phone with Google Maps. If you don't have a phone, use a paper map or draw your own.

Step 1: List 10 potential customers within 5 km of your bakery. Include shops, kiosks, schools, and offices. Write their names and locations. If you don't know them, visit and introduce yourself. Ask if they would buy bread daily. Note how many loaves they might need.

Step 2: Draw a route on your map. Start at your bakery. Connect all 10 stops in a loop. Try to avoid busy roads during peak hours. Estimate travel time between stops. For example, if stops are 500 meters apart, it takes about 2 minutes on a motorbike. Add 5 minutes per stop for delivery and payment. Total time should be under 3 hours.

Step 3: Create a timetable. Set a start time (e.g., 5:30 AM). Assign a time for each stop. For example: Stop 1: 6:00 AM, Stop 2: 6:10 AM, etc. Make sure you finish by 8:00 AM so shops are ready for morning customers.

Step 4: Calculate daily bread needs. Add up all orders. If total is 150 loaves, ensure your vehicle can carry that. If not, plan two trips or reduce stops.

Step 5: Simulate one day. Walk or drive the route at the planned times. Note any problems: traffic, road conditions, long delays. Adjust your timetable.

Step 6: Create a record sheet. Make columns: Stop name, Order (loaves), Delivered, Returned, Cash received, Notes. Use this every day.

Step 7: After one week, review. Which stops were profitable? Which were not? Talk to customers. Ask if they need more or less. Adjust your route and quantities.

This exercise is not just theory. Do it for real. You will learn more from one week of actual route sales than from any book. Start small, with 5 stops if needed. Then grow. With a good plan, you can build a distribution system that sells your bread reliably and profitably. Good luck!

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Distribution and Route Sales for African Bakeries

This practical reference consolidates everything you need to run a profitable daily bread distribution route in an African market. Whether you are supplying kiosks in Nairobi, table-top vendors in Lagos, or tuck shops in Accra, the principles of route sales are the same: get fresh bread to the right points at the right time, collect cash reliably, and keep your cost per loaf low enough to leave a margin. This lesson gives you the tools, numbers, and checklists you can print and pin to your delivery van wall.

1. Understanding Your Route Economics

Before you load a single crate, you must know your numbers. Route sales in African bakery businesses typically operate on thin margins (820% net), so a small error in fuel, spoilage, or credit can wipe out your daily profit. The table below shows typical cost drivers for a small bakery running a single delivery route in an urban African market.

Fuel (petrol/diesel) 1218 3035% Varies with route distance and traffic
Driver/loader wages 815 2028% Often 1 driver + 1 loader
Vehicle maintenance & depreciation 610 1518% Higher on bad roads
Bread spoilage/returns 49 1015% Target below 5% of load
Packaging & crates 36 810% Reusable crates reduce cost
Licenses, insurance, misc. 24 58% County/municipal permits

Source: Adapted from ILO (2019), "Small Business Management in the Food Processing Sector," International Labour Organization, Geneva, and TechnoServe (2021), "Bakery Business Toolkit for East Africa."

The key insight: fuel and labour together make up over half your route cost. This means route density the number of stops per kilometre is your most powerful profit lever. A route with 40 stops in 15 km is far more profitable than one with 15 stops in 40 km, even if the second route sells the same number of loaves.

3. Vehicle and Load Configuration

Your vehicle choice determines your route reach and cost. In most African markets, the three common options are: a motorcycle with side carrier (low cost, short range), a three-wheeler or pickup (medium cost, medium range), and a small van or truck (higher cost, long range). 4. Loading and Stock Control

Load in reverse stop order: the last stop's bread goes in first, the first stop's bread goes in last. This saves 510 minutes per route and reduces handling damage. Use a simple load sheet that lists each stop, the quantity ordered, and the expected cash. Count loaves before departure and after return. Any difference is either spoilage, theft, or a recording error all three must be investigated daily.

SAFETY WARNING: Never allow a driver or loader to travel on the roof of a vehicle or sit on an unsecured load of bread crates. In 2022, road traffic injuries were the leading cause of death for African men aged 1544 (WHO, 2023). Ensure all loads are strapped, the vehicle is not overloaded, and the driver has a valid licence and rest before departure. Overloading is the most common cause of bread delivery accidents in African urban markets.

5. Cash Handling and Credit Control

Cash is the lifeblood of route sales. In many African markets, vendors expect credit for the first few deliveries. This is normal, but it must be controlled. Use a simple credit book: name, date, amount, and signature or thumbprint. Set a hard credit limit per vendor (e.g., no more than 2 days' worth of bread). If a vendor exceeds the limit, stop supply until they pay. This sounds harsh, but it protects your working capital. A bakery with 30% of its revenue tied up in unpaid credit cannot buy flour for tomorrow.

6. Quick Reference Checklist

  • Before departure: Count loaves loaded, check load sheet against orders, confirm cash float (coins and small notes for change).
  • Vehicle check: Tyres, fuel, brakes, lights, and load straps. Do not depart if any is faulty.
  • Route sequence: Follow the planned stop order. Do not skip stops or add unplanned detours without recording them.
  • At each stop: Deliver the exact quantity ordered. Get a signature or thumbprint for credit sales. Collect cash immediately for cash sales.
  • Returns: Record any unsold or damaged bread at the point of return. Do not mix returns with fresh stock.
  • End of route: Reconcile cash collected against load sheet. Investigate any shortage above 1% of sales value.
  • Daily report: Submit sales sheet, cash, and returns to the bakery manager before leaving for the day.
  • Weekly review: Check route profitability per stop. Drop or renegotiate stops that cost more to serve than they earn.

7. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Overloading to avoid a second trip. Fix: Calculate the true cost of a second trip vs. the risk of an accident or fines. A second trip is almost always cheaper than a crash.

Mistake 2: Giving unlimited credit to "loyal" vendors. Fix: Set a credit limit in writing. Enforce it consistently. Loyalty is built on reliable supply, not on debt.

Mistake 3: No daily reconciliation. Fix: Reconcile every day, even if it takes 20 minutes. Daily reconciliation catches theft and errors before they become habits.

Mistake 4: Ignoring route density. Fix: Map your stops. If two stops are 5 km apart and each buys 10 loaves, consider dropping one or finding a closer vendor.

8. Practical Exercise

Choose one day this week. Before departure, record the following on a single sheet of paper: (1) total loaves loaded, (2) number of stops, (3) total kilometres driven, (4) total cash collected, (5) total credit given, (6) total returns. At the end of the day, calculate your cost per loaf delivered (total route cost loaves sold) and your revenue per kilometre. Compare these to the benchmarks in Tables 1 and 2. If your cost per loaf is above 30% of your selling price, your route is not viable adjust your stops, vehicle, or pricing before the next run.

Key takeaway: Route sales is not just driving and dropping bread. It is a daily financial operation. Count your loaves, control your credit, know your cost per loaf, and review your route every week. Do this consistently, and your bakery will grow from a single route to a distribution network.
