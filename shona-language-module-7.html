1. Modes of Transportation

In Zimbabwe, getting around means knowing the right Shona words for the transport you need. The most common public transport is the kombi, a minibus taxi. You will hear people say "Ndiri kuenda kumba nekombi" (I am going home by kombi). A large bus is called bhazi, and a bicycle is bhasikoro. For walking, the verb is kufamba. If you are driving a car, it's motokari. When you want to ask how someone is traveling, say "Uri kuenda sei?" (How are you going?). The answer might be "Ndiri kufamba" (I am walking) or "Ndiri kubhazi" (I am taking the bus).

For hiring a taxi, you say "Ndinoda tekisi" (I need a taxi). In cities like Harare or Bulawayo, you can also use ride-hailing apps, but the Shona term remains tekisi. If you are on a bicycle, you say "Ndiri kubhasikoro". To say "I go by car", use "Ndinofamba nemotokari". Notice the preposition ne- which means "by" or "with". It changes to n- before vowels: nekombi, nebhasikoro, nemotokari.

When you want to say "I want to go to...", use "Ndinoda kuenda ku...". For example, "Ndinoda kuenda kuchiteshi" (I want to go to the station). The word chiteshi means station. For airport, it's nhandare yendege. If you are traveling with someone, say "Tiri kuenda" (We are going). To ask "Are you going?", say "Uri kuenda here?" The here at the end makes it a question.

Practice these phrases with a friend. Start by naming the transport you see every day. Then make sentences: "Ndiri kuenda kuchikoro nekombi" (I am going to school by kombi). "Mai vangu vanofamba nemotokari" (My mother travels by car). The more you use these words, the faster they stick. Remember that in Shona, the verb comes after the subject and the object follows the verb. So "I am going home" is "Ndiri kuenda kumba", not "Ndiri kumba kuenda". Keep it simple and speak out loud.

2. Asking for and Giving Directions

When you are lost in Zimbabwe, you need to ask "Ndiri kupi?" (Where am I?). To ask for directions to a place, say "Ndinoda kuenda ku..." (I want to go to...). For example, "Ndinoda kuenda kuchiteshi chebhazi" (I want to go to the bus station). If you want to ask "Where is the market?", say "Musika uri kupi?" The word kupi means where. You can also ask "Nzira yekuenda kumusika ndeyipi?" (Which is the way to the market?).

When giving directions, use these key words: kuruboshwe (left), kurudyi (right), kumberi (forward), kumashure (behind), pedyo (near), kure (far). For example, "Famba kumberi, woenda kuruboshwe" (Go forward, then turn left). The word woenda means "then go". To say "Turn right", say "Tendeuka kurudyi". If you need to say "It is near the bank", say "Iri pedyo nebhanga". The preposition ne- again means "with" or "near".

If you are giving directions to a driver, you might say "Mira pano" (Stop here). "Enderera mberi" means "Continue straight". "Dzokera shure" means "Go back". To ask "How far is it?", say "Iri kure here?" The answer could be "Iri kure" (It is far) or "Iri pedyo" (It is near). You can also use "maminitsi" for minutes: "Iri maminitsi mashanu" (It is five minutes away).

Practice by giving directions from your home to the nearest shop. Use these phrases: "Buda pamba, famba kumberi, woenda kuruboshwe pauchisvika pamugwagwa" (Leave home, go forward, then turn left when you reach the road). Listen to how people give directions in your area. Often they use landmarks like "pa tree" (by the tree) or "pa corner" (at the corner). In Shona, you can say "pamuti" (by the tree) or "pakona" (at the corner). The more you practice, the easier it becomes to navigate any Zimbabwean city or village.

3. Locations and Landmarks

To talk about locations in Shona, you need words for places and how to say where they are. Common places include: musika (market), chikoro (school), chipatara (hospital), bhanga (bank), chiteshi (station), nhandare (airport or stadium), hotera (hotel), uori (toilet), and imba (house). For example, "Musika uri pedyo nechikoro" (The market is near the school). The word pedyo means near, and kure means far.

When you want to say "at" or "in" a place, use the prefix pa- or ku-. For example, "Ndiri pachikoro" (I am at school), "Ndiri kumusika" (I am at the market). The difference is subtle: pa- often means "at" a specific point, while ku- means "to" or "from" a general direction. But in everyday speech, they are sometimes used interchangeably. To say "inside", use mukati. "Mukati meimba" means inside the house. "Pamusoro" means on top, and "pasi" means below.

Landmarks are very important in Zimbabwe. People often give directions using landmarks like a big tree, a church, a filling station, or a roundabout. For example, "Chiteshi chiri pedyo nechechi" (The station is near the church). "Kona iyi ndeye bhanga" (This corner is for the bank). To ask "Where is the toilet?", say "Uori uri kupi?" If you need to say "The hospital is far from here", say "Chipatara chiri kure nepanapa".

Practice by naming all the important places in your neighborhood. Then make sentences: "Imba yangu iri padyo nechikoro" (My house is near the school). "Bhanga riri kurudyi kwechiteshi" (The bank is to the right of the station). Use the possessive to say "my", "your", etc. For example, "imba yangu" (my house), "chikoro chako" (your school). The possessive changes depending on the noun class, but for now, learn these common ones. The more you describe your surroundings, the better you will become at giving and understanding locations.

4. Buying Tickets and Schedules

When you travel in Zimbabwe, you often need to buy a ticket. In Shona, a ticket is tikiti. To ask for a ticket, say "Ndinoda tikiti" (I want a ticket). If you need a return ticket, say "Ndinoda tikiti yekudzokera" (I want a return ticket). A one-way ticket is "tikiti yekumboenda" (ticket for going only). At the bus station, you will hear "Tikiti inodhura marii?" (How much does the ticket cost?). The answer might be "Inodhura madhora mashanu" (It costs five dollars). In Zimbabwe, they use US dollars and local currency, so you might hear "madhora" for dollars.

To ask about the schedule, say "Bhazi rinosimuka riini?" (What time does the bus leave?). The word riini means when. The answer could be "Rinosimuka na7" (It leaves at 7). For time, Shona uses "na" before the hour: na7, na8, etc. To ask "What time does it arrive?", say "Rinosvika riini?" If you want to know the duration, ask "Rinotora nguva yakareba sei?" (How long does it take?). The answer might be "Maawa maviri" (Two hours).

At the ticket counter, you might say: "Ndinoda tikiti yekuenda kuHarare" (I want a ticket to Harare). "Ndinoda kuenda mangwanani" (I want to go in the morning). "Ndinoda kuenda masikati" (I want to go in the afternoon). "Ndinoda kuenda manheru" (I want to go in the evening). If you need to change your ticket, say "Ndinoda kuchinja tikiti yangu" (I want to change my ticket). To ask for a discount, you can say "Mungandipewo discount?" (Can you give me a discount?). But be polite.

Practice by role-playing with a friend. One person is the ticket seller, the other is the traveler. Use phrases like "Mhoro, ndinoda tikiti" (Hello, I want a ticket). "Kuenda kupi?" (Going where?). "Kuenda kuBulawayo" (Going to Bulawayo). "Imarii?" (How much?). "Madhora gumi" (Ten dollars). "Ndinoda maviri" (I want two). "Ndipewo change" (Give me change). This will help you feel confident when you actually travel.

5. At the Bus Station or Airport

Bus stations in Zimbabwe, like Harare's Mbare Musika or Bulawayo's Egodini, are busy places. You will hear many Shona phrases. To ask where the bus to a certain place is, say "Bhazi rekuenda kuMasvingo riri kupi?" (Where is the bus going to Masvingo?). The word rekuenda means "of going". If you are looking for the ticket office, ask "Hofisi yetikiti iri kupi?" (Where is the ticket office?). At the airport, called nhandare yendege, you will need to check in. "Ndinoda check-in" (I want to check in). To ask about your flight, say "Ndege yangu inosimuka riini?" (What time does my plane leave?).

When you are at the station, you might need to ask for help. "Ndinoda rubatsiro" (I need help). "Ndine mabag ari kuremera" (I have heavy bags). "Mungandibatsire here?" (Can you help me?). If you are looking for a specific bus company, say "Ndinoda bhazi reIntercape" (I want the Intercape bus). To ask if the bus is full, say "Bhazi razara here?" (Is the bus full?). The answer could be "Hongu, razara" (Yes, it's full) or "Kwete, harisati razara" (No, it's not full yet).

At the airport, you will go through security. "Ndinofanira kupfuura security" (I must go through security). To ask where the gate is, say "Gedhi riri kupi?" (Where is the gate?). If your flight is delayed, you might hear "Ndege yanonoka" (The plane is late). To ask about luggage, say "Mukwende wangu uri kupi?" (Where is my luggage?). The word mukwende means luggage. If you lose something, say "Ndakarasikirwa ne..." (I lost...). For example, "Ndakarasikirwa nepasipoti yangu" (I lost my passport).

Practice these phrases before you travel. Listen to announcements at the station or airport. Often they are in English and Shona. Repeat after the speaker. If you don't understand, ask "Ndapota, dzokorora" (Please repeat). Or "Ndapota, taura zvishoma nezvishoma" (Please speak slowly). The more you expose yourself to these situations, the more comfortable you will become. Don't be afraid to make mistakes; people appreciate when you try to speak Shona.

6. Describing a Journey

After a trip, you will want to tell others about your journey. In Shona, a journey is rwendo. To say "I traveled to...", use "Ndakaenda ku...". For example, "Ndakaenda kuVictoria Falls" (I went to Victoria Falls). To describe how you traveled, add the transport: "Ndakaenda nekombi" (I went by kombi). If the journey was long, say "Rwendo rwakanga rwakareba" (The journey was long). If it was short, "Rwendo rwakanga rwupfupi" (The journey was short).

To talk about what you saw, use "Ndakaona..." (I saw...). For example, "Ndakaona nzizi" (I saw rivers). "Ndakaona makomo" (I saw mountains). "Ndakaona mhuka" (I saw animals). To say "It was beautiful", say "Zvakanaka" or "Zvainakidza" (It was enjoyable). If the journey was tiring, say "Ndakaneta" (I am tired). To ask someone about their journey, say "Rwendo rwako rwakanga rwakanaka here?" (Was your journey good?). The answer could be "Hongu, rwakanga rwakanaka" (Yes, it was good) or "Kwete, rwakanga rwakaoma" (No, it was tough).

You can also describe the weather during your journey. "Kwakanaya mvura" (It rained). "Kwaipisa" (It was hot). "Kwaitonhora" (It was cold). To say "We arrived safely", say "Tasvika zvakanaka". If there was a delay, say "Takanonoka" (We were late). To talk about the people you met, say "Ndakasangana nevanhu vakanaka" (I met good people). The verb kusangana means to meet.

Practice by telling a friend about a recent trip. Use these phrases: "Ndakaenda ku..." "Ndakafamba ne..." "Ndakaona..." "Rwendo rwakanga..." "Ndakanzwa..." (I felt...). For example, "Ndakaenda kuMutare nekombi. Rwendo rwakanga rwakareba asi rwakanaka. Ndakaona makomo akanaka. Ndakanzwa kufara" (I went to Mutare by kombi. The journey was long but nice. I saw beautiful mountains. I felt happy). The more you practice, the more natural it will become. Try to use past tense correctly: the prefix -aka- indicates past tense for most verbs. So "I went" is "ndakaenda", "I saw" is "ndakaona", "I felt" is "ndakanzwa". Keep your sentences simple and clear.

<h3>2. Asking for and Giving Directions Kubvunza nzira</h3>
<p>Direction-giving in Shona is landmark-based rather than street-number-based, which mirrors how most Zimbabwean towns are navigated. A driver will say "enderera mberi" (continue straight) and "tendeuka kuruboshwe" (turn left) using physical markers a filling station, a school, a bridge not GPS coordinates.</p>

<h3>3. Locations and Landmarks Nzvimbo nezviratidzo</h3>
<p>Landmarks carry commercial weight. In agricultural trade, "kuchiteshi chebhazi" (at the bus station), "kumusika" (at the market), "kuchipatara" (at the hospital) and "kubhanga" (at the bank) are reference points that determine delivery timing. When directing a haulage driver to a farm, always give the nearest growth point (Guruve, Mvurwi, Bindura) plus a physical landmark a grain depot, a grinding mill, or a dip tank.</p>

<h3>4. Buying Tickets and Schedules Kutenga matikiti</h3>
<p>At a ticket window, clarity saves money. Key phrases: <em>"Ndipa tikiti rimwe kuenda kuBulawayo"</em> (Give me one ticket to Bulawayo); <em>"Bhazi rinobuda rini?"</em> (What time does the bus depart?); <em>"Mune nzvimbo here?"</em> (Do you have seats available?). Always confirm whether the quoted fare includes luggage ("mutoro") agricultural traders moving produce are frequently charged extra for sacks.</p>

<strong style="color:#c0392b; font-size:15px;"> SAFETY WARNING Yambiro yekuchengetedza</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0; color:#222; font-size:14px;">Never hand cash to an unlicensed tout ("mukorokoza") or board an unregistered vehicle at an informal rank. Confirm the vehicle registration, the driver's name, and the official fare at the ticket office before departure. For cross-border trips (Beitbridge, Forbes, Nyamapanda), carry your passport, vehicle registration and customs documentation at all times; unofficial "facilitation fees" at border posts are illegal and should be reported to the Zimbabwe Revenue Authority (ZIMRA). Keep a photocopy of all cargo manifests separate from the originals.</p>

<h3>5. At the Bus Station or Airport Kuchiteshi</h3>
<p>Zimbabwe's main gateways are Robert Gabriel Mugabe International Airport (Harare), Joshua Mqabuko Nkomo International Airport (Bulawayo) and Victoria Falls Airport. At a bus terminus or airport, the following phrases are essential:</p>
<ul>
<li><em>"Ndege yacho inosvika rini?"</em> When does the plane arrive?</li>
<li><em>"Ndine mutoro unorema"</em> I have heavy luggage.</li>
<li><em>"Nzvimbo yekumirira iripi?"</em> Where is the waiting area?</li>
<li><em>"Ndapota, ratidza chiteshi chebhazi"</em> Please show me the bus station.</li>
</ul>

<h3>6. Describing a Journey Kutsanangura rwendo</h3>
<p>Being able to describe a completed journey is essential for reporting to suppliers, insurers and logistics partners. A structured Shona journey description uses past tense markers: <em>"Ndakaenda"</em> (I went), <em>"Ndakasvika"</em> (I arrived), <em>"Ndakatenga tikiti"</em> (I bought a ticket), <em>"Bhazi rakanonoka maawa maviri"</em> (The bus was delayed two hours). For cargo incidents, the phrase <em>"Mutoro wangu wakanganisika"</em> (My cargo was damaged) is the correct opener when filing a claim.</p>
