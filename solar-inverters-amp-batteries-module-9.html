1. Solar for a Small Retail Shop: Lights, Fridge, POS

A small retail shop in Africa typically needs power for LED lights, a refrigerator for cold drinks, and a point-of-sale (POS) machine or phone for mobile money. Start by listing each appliance and its wattage. For example: 5 LED bulbs at 10W each = 50W, one fridge at 150W (but it cycles on and off, so average 50W per hour), and a POS machine at 50W. Total running watts = 250W. But you must account for surge: the fridge compressor may surge to 600W for a few seconds. So your inverter must handle at least 600W continuous, but a 1kVA inverter (800W) is safer. For daily energy, multiply running watts by hours used. Lights: 50W Ã 6 hours = 300Wh. Fridge: 50W average Ã 24 hours = 1200Wh. POS: 50W Ã 8 hours = 400Wh. Total daily energy = 1900Wh (1.9kWh). To size the battery, decide how many days of backup you want. You need at least a 12V 400Ah battery or a 24V 200Ah system. For solar panels, assume 4 peak sun hours per day in most of Africa. To generate 1900Wh, you need 1900/4 = 475W of panels. Round up to 500W (e.g., two 250W panels). Use a 30A MPPT charge controller. Always add 20% safety margin. So final: 1kVA inverter, 24V 200Ah lithium battery (or 2Ã12V 200Ah lead-acid in series), 600W solar panels, 40A MPPT. Mount panels facing north (in southern hemisphere) or south (northern hemisphere) at angle equal to your latitude. Use proper DC cables and fuses. This system will keep your shop running through cloudy days if you manage loads carefully.

2. Solar for a Barbershop or Salon: Clippers, Fans, Music

A barbershop or salon uses clippers (50-100W each), fans (60-80W), music system (100W), and maybe a small water heater or hair dryer (1000-2000W). 3. Solar for a Welding or Carpentry Workshop: High Surge Tools

Welding machines and carpentry tools like circular saws, planers, and compressors have very high surge currents. A typical welding machine draws 3000-5000W continuous and can surge to 8000W or more. A circular saw (1500W) may surge to 4500W. So you cannot use a small inverter. You need a heavy-duty inverter, often 48V or higher, with a continuous rating of at least 5000W and surge of 10000W. For a workshop, list all tools and their running watts. Example: welder 4000W, circular saw 1500W, drill 800W, lights 200W. But you rarely run all at once. Still, the welder alone needs 4000W continuous. So inverter should be 5kW continuous, 10kW surge. For daily energy, estimate hours: welder 2h Ã 4000W = 8000Wh, saw 1h Ã 1500W = 1500Wh, drill 1h Ã 800W = 800Wh, lights 4h Ã 200W = 800Wh. Total = 11100Wh (11.1kWh). Battery bank: use lithium for high discharge. A 48V 200Ah lithium gives 9600Wh usable. You might need two in parallel for 19.2kWh. Or use 48V 300Ah. Solar panels: 11100Wh / 4 sun hours = 2775W. Round to 3000W (twelve 250W panels). Charge controller: 3000W / 48V = 62.5A, so use an 80A MPPT. Wiring: use large cables, at least 25mmÂ² for battery to inverter, and proper fuses. For welders, consider a separate high-frequency inverter or a generator backup. Many workshops in Africa use a 5kVA hybrid inverter with 48V 200Ah lithium and 3000W panels, but they run the welder only during sunny hours and use a generator for heavy continuous welding. Always consult a professional for such high-power systems. Safety: ensure proper grounding and ventilation for batteries.

4. Solar for a Poultry Farm: Incubators, Lights, Water Pumps

A poultry farm needs constant power for incubators (critical), lights, and water pumps. Incubators are the most sensitive: they must run 24/7 and cannot lose power for more than a few hours. A typical small incubator uses 200-400W, but it cycles on and off, so average 100-200W. Lights: 10 LED bulbs at 10W = 100W, used 12 hours. Water pump: 500W, used 2 hours per day. List all loads. Example: incubator 300W (average 150W), lights 100W, pump 500W. Total running watts if all on = 900W. Surge: pump may surge to 1500W. So inverter should be at least 1.5kVA continuous, 3kVA surge. For daily energy: incubator 150W Ã 24h = 3600Wh, lights 100W Ã 12h = 1200Wh, pump 500W Ã 2h = 1000Wh. Total = 5800Wh (5.8kWh). Battery: for one day backup, use lithium. 48V 150Ah gives 7200Wh usable. Or 24V 300Ah gives 7200Wh. Solar panels: 5800Wh / 4 sun hours = 1450W. Round to 1600W (e.g., eight 200W panels). Charge controller: 1600W / 48V = 33.3A, use 40A MPPT. For incubators, you need a pure sine wave inverter to avoid damaging the thermostat. Also, consider a backup generator or a second battery bank for redundancy. In Africa, many poultry farms use a 3kVA hybrid inverter with 48V 200Ah lithium and 2000W panels. Water pumps can be run during sunny hours to save battery. Always monitor battery voltage and set a low-voltage cutoff to protect the battery. Incubators should have their own dedicated circuit. Use proper ventilation for the battery room.

6. Calculating Return on Investment for Your Solar Setup

To calculate ROI, you need to know your total system cost, your monthly savings from not buying fuel or grid electricity, and the system's lifespan. Start by adding up all costs: solar panels, inverter, batteries, charge controller, mounting, cables, and installation. For example, a small shop system (1kVA inverter, 24V 200Ah lithium, 600W panels) might cost $1500 in many African countries. Next, estimate your monthly energy savings. If you previously used a generator, calculate fuel cost per month. A small generator uses about 1 liter per hour, running 6 hours a day = 180 liters per month. At $1.2 per liter, that's $216 per month. If you used grid electricity, calculate your monthly bill for the same loads. Suppose you paid $50 per month. So savings = $216 - $50 = $166 per month if you switch from generator to solar. But with solar, you have no fuel cost, so savings = $216 per month. Now, ROI = total cost / monthly savings = $1500 / $216 = 6.94 months. That means the system pays for itself in about 7 months. After that, it's pure profit. However, you must account for battery replacement. Lithium batteries last 5-10 years, lead-acid 2-3 years. If you use lead-acid, you might replace them every 3 years at $400, so add that to annual cost. Also, consider maintenance and occasional inverter replacement. For a more accurate ROI, use the formula: ROI = (Total Cost + (Replacement Cost Ã Years)) / (Monthly Savings Ã 12 Ã Years). For example, over 5 years with lithium: total cost $1500, no replacement, savings $216Ã12Ã5 = $12,960. ROI = 1500/12960 = 0.116, or 11.6% per year. That's a good return. For a barbershop with a $3000 system saving $300 per month, ROI = 3000/300 = 10 months. Always include a 10% contingency for unexpected costs. Use a simple spreadsheet to compare. Many African businesses see ROI in 6-18 months. The key is to accurately measure your current energy expenses and size the system correctly to avoid overspending.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Sizing Solar Systems for Different African Business Types

This reference lesson turns the six business scenarios in this module into a single, practical sizing and decision toolkit. Use it as a lookup sheet when a customer walks into your shop, when you quote a client, or when you check whether a proposed system will actually pay for itself. The numbers here are working estimates for African conditions: high ambient temperatures, variable grid quality, and equipment that is often imported and repaired locally rather than replaced.

The core rule for every business type is the same: size the battery bank around daily energy use, size the inverter around the single largest surge load, and size the panels around the worst month of sunshine. Most failed solar installations in Africa fail on one of those three points, not on the panel brand.

Never size a battery bank below the inverter's minimum DC input voltage, and never connect a lithium and lead-acid bank in parallel. In high-temperature African environments, battery rooms must be ventilated above 30Â°C and cables must be rated for the full surge current, not the running current. A welding workshop drawing 5 kVA surge through undersized DC cables is a fire risk, not a sizing error. Always install a DC circuit breaker and a surge protection device on the PV side.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • List every load, including phone chargers. Small loads left off the list are the most common cause of a system that runs flat by 6 p.m.
  • Measure surge, not just running watts. Fridges, pumps, welders, and saws decide your inverter size; lights and clippers do not.
  • Use worst-month peak sun hours. A system sized on the best month will fail in the cloudy season.
  • Add a 25% loss margin for heat, cable losses, dust on panels, and inverter efficiency.
  • Match battery chemistry to the business. Lithium for daily deep cycling (kiosk, retail); lead-acid only where budget forces it and maintenance is reliable.
  • Confirm the inverter's continuous and surge ratings separately before quoting. A 3 kVA inverter may only surge to 6 kVA for a few seconds.
  • Calculate payback against the real displaced cost â grid tariff, diesel per litre, or kerosene per week â not against a retail price you wish you could charge.
  • Plan for expansion. A retail shop today is a retail shop with a second fridge next year. Leave one spare breaker and 20% panel headroom.
