1. Identifying Through-Hole Components

Through-hole components are the traditional electronic parts with long metal leads that go through holes in a circuit board. You will see them in almost every repair job across Africa, from radios in Accra to solar charge controllers in Nairobi. Start by sorting them into groups: resistors, capacitors, diodes, transistors, ICs, connectors, and switches. Resistors are small cylinders with coloured bands. A 10k ohm resistor has bands brown, black, orange, gold. Capacitors come in two main types: ceramic discs (small, no polarity) and electrolytic cans (larger, with a white stripe marking the negative lead). Diodes are black cylinders with a silver band at one end that band marks the cathode. Transistors look like little black half-cylinders with three legs; the flat side shows the part number like BC547. ICs are black rectangular chips with 8, 14, or 16 legs. Connectors are plastic headers or screw terminals. Switches are mechanical. Use a cheap digital multimeter set to continuity or resistance to confirm values. For example, a good 100 ohm resistor reads about 100 ohms. A bad capacitor may read zero or infinite. Always check the part number printed on the component. In many African markets, components are salvaged from old TVs and computers, so markings may be faded. Clean the part with a cloth and use a magnifying glass. Keep a notebook listing each components name, value, and polarity. This habit saves hours during assembly. Remember: polarity matters for electrolytic capacitors, diodes, LEDs, and ICs. Non-polar parts like resistors and ceramic capacitors can go either way. Practice identifying 20 random components from a mixed bag until you can name them in under 10 seconds each.

2. Component Preparation and Lead Forming

Before you insert any component, you must prepare its leads. This step prevents damage and ensures a proper fit. Start with a pair of small flush cutters and a lead-forming tool or a simple pair of round-nose pliers. For resistors and diodes, bend the leads to match the hole spacing on your board. A common spacing is 0.4 inches (10 mm). Hold the component body with one hand and use pliers to bend the leads 90 degrees downward. Do not bend right at the body leave a small gap of about 1 mm to avoid cracking the seal. For capacitors, especially electrolytics, keep the leads straight unless the board requires a specific bend. Clean the leads with fine sandpaper or a pencil eraser to remove oxidation. In humid African climates, oxidation is common, and dirty leads cause bad solder joints. If leads are too long, trim them to about 5 mm below the board after insertion but wait to trim until after soldering. For ICs, never bend the legs. Instead, use a DIP insertion tool or gently straighten them on a flat surface. If a leg is bent, use tweezers to realign it. For transistors, identify the emitter, base, and collector before forming. A BC547 has a flat side; the middle leg is base, the left is collector, right is emitter when viewed from the front. Form leads to fit the boards triangle pattern. Always wear safety glasses when cutting leads flying metal can injure your eyes. Use a lead-forming jig if available; otherwise, a small vice and pliers work fine. After forming, place all prepared components in a clean tray sorted by value. This preparation step may take 10 minutes per 20 components, but it speeds up assembly and reduces rework. In many African workshops, technicians skip this step and later struggle with misaligned parts. Dont be that technician.

3. Inserting and Securing Components

Insertion is where your prepared components meet the board. Start with the lowest-profile parts: resistors, diodes, and small ceramic capacitors. Place the board on a flat surface or in a holder. Insert each components leads through the correct holes. For polarized parts, match the positive lead to the square pad or the hole marked with a plus sign. On most boards, the square pad is positive for electrolytic capacitors and the round pad is negative. For diodes, the banded end goes to the pad marked with a line or a K. For ICs, align the notch or dot with the notch on the silkscreen. Push the component down until it sits flush against the board no gap larger than 1 mm. If a component stands off the board (like power resistors), use a spacer or leave a 2-3 mm gap for cooling. Once inserted, bend the leads outward at about 45 degrees to hold the part in place. This is called clinching. Do not bend them flat against the board that makes rework difficult. For multi-lead parts like ICs, insert one row first, then gently push the other row in. If a lead wont go, do not force it check for a bent leg or wrong hole. Use a small screwdriver to guide leads. After all components are inserted, flip the board over and check that each part is seated properly. You can use a piece of foam or a soft cloth to hold components in place while flipping. Secure large or heavy components with a small dab of silicone or hot glue after soldering, especially in high-vibration environments like solar installations in dusty areas. In African workshops, a common mistake is inserting tall components first, which makes it hard to reach shorter ones. Always work from short to tall. Practice inserting 10 resistors, 2 capacitors, and 1 IC on a scrap board until you can do it without thinking.

4. Soldering Through-Hole Components

Soldering is the heart of electronics assembly. You need a soldering iron with a fine tip, 60/40 or lead-free solder (0.8 mm diameter is ideal), a wet sponge or brass wool, and a fume extractor or small fan. Set your iron to 350C for leaded solder or 380C for lead-free. Let it heat for 5 minutes. Clean the tip on the sponge, then apply a small amount of solder to tin the tip. For each joint, follow this method: touch the iron tip to both the pad and the component lead at the same time. Count one second. Then feed solder into the joint not onto the iron tip. The solder should melt and flow around the lead and pad like a small volcano. Remove solder first, then remove the iron. The whole process should take 2-3 seconds. A good joint is shiny, smooth, and concave. A bad joint looks dull, blobby, or ball-shaped. For African learners, practice on a scrap board with 20 resistors. Common problems: too little heat (cold joint), too much solder (bridge), or moving the component before solder cools. If you see a bridge between two pads, use solder wick or a desoldering pump to remove excess. Keep your tip clean every 3-4 joints. Do not use too much pressure you can lift pads. For ground planes or large copper areas, you may need a higher wattage iron (40W or more). After soldering, trim the leads flush with the board using flush cutters. Hold the lead so it doesnt fly. Inspect each joint with a magnifying glass. In many African settings, power fluctuations can affect iron temperature; use a surge protector or a voltage stabilizer. If you are soldering in a humid environment, keep the board dry. Practice until you can solder 30 joints in 5 minutes with 90% perfect. That speed and quality will get you hired.

5. Inspecting Through-Hole Solder Joints

Inspection is not optional it is how you catch defects before they cause failures. After soldering, let the board cool for 2 minutes. Use a magnifying glass (10x) and bright light. Start by checking each joint visually. A good joint has a shiny, smooth, concave fillet that wets both the pad and the lead. The solder should look like a tiny volcano. A cold joint is dull, lumpy, and may have a crack around the lead. A cold joint often results from insufficient heat or dirty surfaces. A solder bridge is a blob of solder connecting two adjacent pads this causes short circuits. Use a multimeter set to continuity to check for bridges between neighbouring pads. A disturbed joint looks like a frozen drip it happens if the component moved while solder was cooling. A blowhole or pinhole indicates trapped gas or contamination. For each defect, mark it with a small piece of tape or a marker. Then check component placement: is the part flush? Are polarized parts oriented correctly? Look for leads that are too long or too short. Use a dental pick or tweezers to gently tug each lead a good joint will not move. If it moves, its a cold joint. In African repair shops, a common defect is insufficient solder on ground pads because the copper plane sucks heat away. Reheat those joints with a hotter iron and add a little solder. Also check for splashes of solder on the board remove them with a brush. After inspection, clean the board with isopropyl alcohol and a toothbrush to remove flux. Flux residue can cause corrosion over time, especially in humid coastal areas like Mombasa or Lagos. A clean board is easier to inspect and more reliable. Document your findings: count the number of defects per 100 joints. A good target is less than 2 defects per 100. If you find more, review your soldering technique. Inspection takes 5 minutes for a small board, but it saves hours of troubleshooting later.

6. Rework and Touch-Up of Through-Hole Assemblies

Rework means fixing defects found during inspection. Touch-up means improving a joint that is not perfect but not yet failed. You need a soldering iron, solder sucker or solder wick, flux pen, tweezers, and a heat-resistant mat. Start by identifying the defect. For a cold joint, reheat the joint with the iron and add a tiny amount of fresh solder. The flux in the new solder will clean the joint. Hold the iron for 1-2 seconds, then remove. For a solder bridge, use solder wick: place the wick over the bridge, press with the iron, and the wick will absorb excess solder. Alternatively, use a solder sucker heat the bridge and quickly suck the molten solder. For a component that needs replacement, first remove solder from all pads using wick or sucker. Then gently pull the component out with tweezers while heating one lead at a time. Do not force it you can lift pads. If a pad lifts, you may need to run a jumper wire to the next component. For touch-up, if a joint looks dull but is electrically good, add a little flux and reheat. Do not add more solder unless needed. After rework, clean the area with isopropyl alcohol. Always test the board after rework with a multimeter to ensure no shorts and correct continuity. In African workshops, rework is common because components are often salvaged. Practice rework on a scrap board: create 10 cold joints, then fix them. Time yourself a skilled technician can fix a cold joint in 10 seconds. Remember: never rework a joint more than 3 times repeated heating can damage the pad and the component. If a pad is damaged, use a small wire to bridge to the nearest connected point. Keep your iron tip clean and tinned. After rework, re-inspect the joint. A good rework is invisible it looks like a factory joint. Finally, log what you fixed and why. This helps you improve your assembly process and avoid repeating mistakes.

<h2>Practical Reference: Through-Hole Component Assembly</h2>

<p>Through-hole assembly remains the backbone of electronics manufacturing across Africa from small workshops in Kumasi and Nairobi to contract manufacturers supplying solar inverters, radio transmitters, and agricultural sensor boards. Unlike surface-mount work, through-hole assembly is forgiving of modest equipment, tolerates high-vibration environments, and produces mechanically robust joints that survive heat, dust, and humidity. For an African SME, mastering through-hole assembly is a direct route to revenue: repair contracts, low-volume production runs, and prototype services for universities and agritech startups.</p>

<p>This practical reference condenses the six lessons in this module into a single working document. Use it on the bench, print it for the workshop wall, or adapt it into a training handout for apprentices.</p>

<h3>1. Identifying Through-Hole Components</h3>
<p>Through-hole parts are classified by function and by lead count. Resistors, capacitors, and diodes are axial or radial; ICs come in DIP (dual in-line package); connectors and relays are typically large and mechanically stressed. In the African market, component sourcing is the first business constraint: counterfeit electrolytics and recycled ICs are common. Build a verified supplier list and inspect incoming stock with a multimeter and capacitance meter before accepting a consignment.</p>

<h3>2. Component Preparation and Lead Forming</h3>
<p>Leads must be cleaned, straightened, and formed to match the hole pitch. Use lead-forming pliers or a simple jig to bend leads without stressing the component body. Oxidation on recycled leads is the leading cause of cold joints in African workshops clean with isopropyl alcohol or a soft eraser before insertion.</p>

<h3>3. Inserting and Securing Components</h3>
<p>Insert components from the non-solder side, working from lowest profile to tallest. Bend leads outward at 3045 to hold parts in place during soldering. For heavy parts (transformers, large relays), use mechanical fasteners or silicone adhesive in addition to solder vibration from generators and vehicles will otherwise crack joints.</p>

<h3>4. Soldering Through-Hole Components</h3>
<p>Use a temperature-controlled iron at 350370 C with lead-free SAC305 or, where permitted, Sn63/Pb37. Apply heat to both pad and lead for 12 seconds, feed solder, then remove solder first, then iron. Target a shiny, concave fillet on both sides of the board. In humid coastal environments, use no-clean flux with adequate solids to prevent dendritic growth.</p>

Wash hands thoroughly after handling leaded solder never eat or drink at the bench. In shared workshops, keep a dedicated soldering zone away from food preparation and child-accessible areas. Store solder and flux in sealed containers; dispose of dross and used tips as hazardous waste, not general refuse.</p>

<h3>5. Inspecting Through-Hole Solder Joints</h3>
<p>Inspect at 510 magnification or with a loupe. Acceptable joints show a bright, concave fillet wetting both pad and lead, with lead outline visible. Reject: cold joints (dull, blobby), insufficient solder (no fillet), excess solder (convex ball), bridges between pads, disturbed joints (frosty, cracked), and lifted pads. For Class 2 and Class 3 work, follow IPC-A-610 acceptance criteria exactly this is what export customers and telecoms operators will audit.</p>

<h3>6. Rework and Touch-Up</h3>
<p>Rework is a business cost, not a failure. Use a solder sucker or desoldering braid to remove excess solder; use flux-cored wire and a clean tip for touch-up. Never reheat a joint more than twice without removing and re-preparing the lead repeated heating damages pads and components. Document every rework in a simple logbook; this becomes evidence of quality control when bidding for larger contracts.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li>Verify component value, polarity, and pin-1 orientation against the BOM and silkscreen before insertion.</li>
<li>Clean oxidized leads with isopropyl alcohol or a soft eraser.</li>
<li>Form leads to match hole pitch never bend directly at the component body.</li>
<li>Insert lowest-profile parts first; secure tall or heavy parts mechanically.</li>
<li>Set iron to 350370 C for lead-free, 315345 C for leaded solder.</li>
<li>Heat pad and lead together for 12 s before feeding solder.</li>
<li>Inspect every joint at 510 magnification against IPC-A-610 criteria.</li>
<li>Log all rework; retrain on any recurring defect pattern.</li>
</ul>
