1. Sun-Drying and Air-Drying: Traditional Methods

Sun-drying and air-drying are the oldest ways to preserve meat without electricity. In many African communities, these methods are still used for making biltong, jerky, and dried fish. The goal is to remove moisture so bacteria and mould cannot grow. Meat should be cut into thin strips, about 12 cm thick, to dry quickly. Lean cuts like beef silverside or game meat work best. Trim off all fat because fat goes rancid. Cut along the grain for chewy biltong or across the grain for tender jerky.

Before drying, meat must be salted or brined. A basic dry rub is 2 parts salt to 1 part sugar, with spices like black pepper, coriander, or chilli. Rub it into the meat and leave it in a cool place for 1224 hours. The salt draws out water and creates an environment where harmful microbes cannot survive. After salting, wipe off excess salt with a clean cloth or rinse briefly and pat dry.

Hang the strips in a well-ventilated area. Direct sunlight is good for the first day to speed drying, but too much sun can cook the meat and make it hard. In hot, dry climates, meat can dry in 35 days. In humid areas, it may take 710 days. Use a simple drying rack made from clean wooden sticks or wire mesh, covered with mosquito netting to keep flies off. Air must circulate around each piece. Turn the strips daily.

Watch for signs of spoilage: slimy texture, bad smell, or green/black mould. A little white mould is normal and can be wiped off with vinegar. When fully dry, the meat should be hard and brittle, snapping when bent. Store in a paper bag or cloth sack in a cool, dry place. Properly dried meat can last 23 months without refrigeration. In humid conditions, store in an airtight container with a desiccant packet or keep in the freezer if available.

2. Building a Solar Dryer for Meat

A solar dryer speeds up drying and protects meat from dust, flies, and rain. You can build a simple cabinet dryer using local materials: wood, plywood, or metal sheets, and a clear plastic or glass cover. The dryer has three main parts: a solar collector, a drying chamber, and a chimney. The collector is a flat box painted black inside to absorb heat. Cover it with clear plastic or glass. Air enters at the bottom, heats up, and rises into the drying chamber where meat is placed on racks.

To build a small dryer for family use, you need: 4 wooden posts (2 m tall), plywood or metal sheets for walls, a clear plastic sheet (UV-resistant), wire mesh for racks, and black paint. Make the collector about 1 m long, 0.5 m wide, and 0.2 m deep. The drying chamber can be 1 m tall, 0.5 m wide, and 0.5 m deep. Angle the collector at 3045 degrees facing the sun. Paint the inside black.

Place meat on mesh racks inside the chamber. The racks should be at least 10 cm apart for airflow. The chimney at the top lets moist air escape. A small fan can be added for better circulation, but it works without one. In full sun, the dryer can reach 5070C, which dries meat in 13 days. This is faster than open-air drying and safer.

Use the dryer during the day. Bring meat inside at night or close the vents to keep out insects and moisture. Clean the dryer regularly to prevent mould. Solar dryers are affordable, easy to build, and work well in most African climates. They reduce losses from pests and rain, and produce higher-quality dried meat.

3. Smoking with Local Woods: Acacia, Mopane, and Mesquite

Smoking meat adds flavour and helps preserve it by drying the surface and depositing antimicrobial compounds from smoke. In Africa, you can use many local hardwoods. Acacia, mopane, and mesquite are excellent choices. Never use softwoods like pine or eucalyptus, which contain harmful resins. Also avoid woods that are toxic, such as oleander.

Acacia is widespread in savanna regions. It burns hot and gives a mild, sweet smoke. Mopane is common in southern Africa and produces a strong, earthy flavour; it's ideal for game meat. Mesquite, though invasive in some areas, burns long and gives a sweet, smoky taste. Collect dry, seasoned wood. Green wood produces too much creosote, which is unhealthy.

To smoke meat, you need a smoker or a simple pit. A pit smoker: dig a hole 1 m deep, line with stones, and start a fire at the bottom. Let it burn down to embers. Place meat on sticks or wire mesh 6080 cm above the embers. Cover the pit with a metal sheet or wet burlap, leaving a small vent. Smoke for 48 hours for hot smoking (6080C) or 13 days for cold smoking (2030C). Hot smoking cooks and dries the meat; cold smoking only flavours and dries.

For cold smoking, keep the fire small and use a separate firebox with a pipe to carry smoke into the chamber. This prevents heat from cooking the meat. You can also use a metal drum: build a fire at the bottom, hang meat at the top, and control airflow. Smoke until the meat is firm and dry. Store in a cool, dry place. Smoked meat keeps for weeks without refrigeration if kept dry.

4. Combining Salting, Smoking, and Drying

The best preservation uses a combination of salting, smoking, and drying. This 'hurdle technology' makes it very hard for bacteria to survive. Salt reduces water activity, smoke adds antimicrobial compounds, and drying removes moisture. Together, they create a shelf-stable product that can last months without refrigeration.

Start with a salt cure. For 1 kg of meat, use 3040 g of salt (about 34% of meat weight). Mix with 10 g sugar and spices. Rub into meat and cure for 2448 hours in a cool place (below 10C if possible, or in a refrigerator). After curing, rinse briefly and pat dry.

Next, smoke the meat. Hot smoking at 6080C for 46 hours or cold smoking at 2030C for 12 days. Smoking also dries the surface. After smoking, move to a drying stage. Air-dry in a solar dryer or well-ventilated room until the meat is hard and brittle. This may take 13 days in a dryer or 510 days in open air.

The final product should have a moisture content below 30% (ideally 2025%). You can test by weight: weigh a piece before drying and after; it should lose at least 50% of its weight. Store in paper or cloth bags in a cool, dry place. If humidity is high, use airtight containers with desiccant. Properly made, this meat can last 36 months. Always check for mould or off-smells before eating.

5. Controlling Insects and Pests During Drying

Insects and pests can ruin drying meat. Flies lay eggs that become maggots, and beetles and ants eat the meat. The biggest threat is the blowfly, which is attracted to the smell of raw meat. To prevent infestation, use physical barriers. Cover drying meat with fine mesh netting (mosquito net) or cheesecloth. Ensure the netting does not touch the meat, as flies can lay eggs through it.

A solar dryer is excellent because it is enclosed. The heat also deters insects. If drying outdoors, hang meat high off the ground, at least 1.5 m, and away from rubbish or animal pens. Keep the area clean. Use traps: flypaper or bait stations around the drying area. Some people use natural repellents like neem leaves or wood ash spread around the drying rack.

During storage, insects like weevils and moths can attack dried meat. Store in airtight containers or vacuum-sealed bags. Add bay leaves or dried chilli to repel insects. Inspect regularly. If you see small holes or powdery dust (frass), discard the infested meat.

For long-term storage, freezing or refrigeration is best, but without it, keep meat in a cool, dark, dry place. You can also re-dry meat if it becomes soft. A simple method: heat meat in an oven at 70C for 30 minutes to kill eggs and larvae. This also reduces moisture. Always maintain hygiene: wash hands, clean tools, and keep animals away. A clean workspace and proper drying conditions are your best defence against pests.

6. Testing for Dryness: Moisture Content and Shelf Stability

Proper drying is critical for shelf stability. If meat is not dry enough, mould and bacteria will grow. The target moisture content for dried meat is 2030%. You can test dryness without a moisture meter using simple methods.

First, the bend test: take a piece of meat and bend it. Fully dried meat should snap or crack, not bend like rubber. If it bends, it needs more drying. Second, the weight test: weigh a piece before drying and after. It should lose at least 50% of its weight. For example, 1 kg of fresh meat should weigh 500 g or less when dry. Third, the touch test: dry meat feels hard and leathery, with no soft spots. Cut a piece open; the inside should be dry, not moist.

If you have a moisture meter, aim for 2025% moisture. If not, use these practical tests. Another method: place a piece in a sealed jar with a little salt. If the salt clumps, the meat is still too moist.

Once dry, cool the meat completely before packaging. Store in paper bags or cloth sacks in a cool, dry, dark place. For humid climates, use airtight containers with silica gel desiccant. Check stored meat weekly for signs of moisture or mould. If mould appears, wipe with vinegar and re-dry. Properly dried meat can last 23 months at room temperature, or 612 months in the freezer. Always label with date and use oldest first.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Drying and Smoking Preserving Without Refrigeration

In much of Africa, the cold chain is expensive, unreliable, or simply absent. For a small meat business a butcher in Gaborone, a goat processor in Kajiado, a biltong maker in Windhoek drying and smoking are not nostalgia. They are working capital. They convert a perishable carcass into a shelf-stable, transportable, higher-margin product that can reach urban markets, roadside stalls, and export buyers without a single kilowatt of refrigeration.

This lesson is a practical reference. It covers the physics, the numbers, the equipment, and the commercial logic you need to dry and smoke meat reliably and legally. The goal is not to make a museum piece. The goal is to make a product you can sell, repeat, and scale.

1. The Science in One Paragraph

Drying and smoking preserve meat by lowering water activity (aw), not just water content. Bacteria need aw above roughly 0.90; moulds and yeasts tolerate down to about 0.60. Properly dried and smoked meat sits at aw 0.600.85, which is why it keeps for months at ambient temperature. Salt lowers aw chemically; heat and airflow remove water physically; smoke deposits antimicrobial phenols and creates an oxygen-barrier surface. Combine all three and you have a robust, low-cost preservation system.

2. Sun-Drying and Air-Drying: Traditional Methods

Open sun-drying is the oldest method and still the cheapest. In the Sahel, Sudan, and northern Kenya, meat is cut into thin strips (12 cm), salted, and hung on lines or laid on rocks or woven mats. Air-drying relies on ambient temperature, low humidity, and wind. It works best in dry-season conditions: relative humidity below 60%, daytime temperatures above 30C, and steady breeze.

The commercial problem with open drying is variability. A batch that dries in two days in June may take five in September, and five days of exposure means flies, dust, rain risk, and inconsistent texture. For a business selling to supermarkets or export buyers, variability is a rejection risk. Traditional methods are the foundation but they must be upgraded.

3. Building a Solar Dryer for Meat

An indirect solar dryer costs roughly USD 80250 in materials and can be built by a local artisan in two days. It typically doubles or triples drying speed versus open air and eliminates fly contact, which alone can cut losses by 2040%.

Basic design:

  • Collector: a shallow box (1.5 m 1 m 15 cm) with a black metal absorber plate, insulation underneath (sawdust, straw, or foam), and a clear glazing cover (glass or UV-stabilised polythene).
  • Drying chamber: a vertical or inclined box above or beside the collector, fitted with mesh trays (food-grade stainless or plastic). Capacity: 515 kg fresh meat per batch.
  • Airflow: cool air enters at the base of the collector, heats, rises through the chamber, and exits via a chimney or upper vent. Natural convection is usually sufficient; a small 12V fan (solar-powered) improves consistency.
  • Vent control: adjustable vents at inlet and outlet let you manage temperature and humidity. Target 4560C in the chamber hot enough to dry fast, cool enough to avoid cooking the meat.

Keep the collector and chamber dark inside to reduce light-induced fat oxidation. Clean trays between batches with hot water and food-safe sanitiser.

Smoke does three jobs: it dries, it deposits antimicrobial and antioxidant compounds, and it builds flavour. Wood choice matters commercially because flavour drives repeat purchase.

5. Combining Salting, Smoking, and Drying

The most shelf-stable African meat products biltong, drowors, kilishi, nyama choma-style dried beef use a hurdle sequence: salt or brine first, then smoke, then dry. Each step lowers aw and adds antimicrobial protection. The sequence matters: salt before smoke, smoke before or during drying. Never dry unsalted meat for long-term storage.

6. Controlling Insects and Pests During Drying

Flies, beetles, and ants are the biggest cause of batch rejection. The blowfly (Calliphora spp.) and housefly (Musca domestica) lay eggs on moist meat within hours; larvae (maggots) render a batch unsellable. Control is a combination of exclusion, speed, and chemistry.

  • Physical exclusion: mesh screens (12 mm aperture) on all openings; solar dryers are inherently protected.
  • Speed: the faster aw drops below 0.90, the less time pests have. Pre-salting accelerates this.
  • Smoke: phenols are insect-repellent; a light smoke early in drying reduces fly landings.
  • Spices: black pepper, chilli, garlic, and cloves have documented repellent activity; traditional recipes use them for good reason.
  • Hygiene: clean drying area, no offal or blood pools nearby, covered waste bins.
  • Storage: once dry, vacuum-seal or store in airtight containers with a desiccant sachet; freeze for 48 h if beetle eggs are suspected.

SAFETY WARNING FOOD SAFETY AND LEGAL COMPLIANCE

Never dry meat that has not been chilled or cooked promptly after slaughter. Meat left above 4C for more than 2 hours can harbour Salmonella, E. coli O157:H7, and Listeria; drying does not destroy these pathogens it only stops them growing. Use only potable water for brining, food-grade salt (not industrial or iodised table salt in excess), and clean utensils. Do not use chemically treated timber, painted wood, or plastic that is not food-grade in smokehouses; burning these releases toxic compounds. If you sell meat products, you must comply with your national food safety authority (e.g. KEBS in Kenya, SABS/SANS in South Africa, BOBS in Botswana, NAFDAC in Nigeria) and, for export, with the importing countrys veterinary and labelling requirements. When in doubt, test do not guess.

7. Testing for Dryness: Moisture Content and Shelf Stability

Looks dry is not a specification. Buyers and regulators want numbers. The two practical measures are moisture content (MC) and water activity (aw).

  • Moisture content: target 2035% for biltong, 1525% for kilishi, 2540% for drowors (wet basis). Measure by oven-drying a sample at 105C for 46 hours and weighing before/after. A cheap kitchen scale and a domestic oven can give you a working number.
  • Water activity: aw meters cost USD 150400; a shared or cooperative meter is a good investment. Target aw 0.85 for ambient shelf stability; 0.70 for long transport.
  • Field test: bend a strip it should crack, not snap cleanly, and show no moist pink centre. Squeeze a piece: no free moisture should appear.
  • Shelf-stability test: keep a sample at ambient for 7 days in a sealed bag. If mould, off-odour, or slime appears, the batch is under-dried or under-salted.

Record every batch: date, raw weight, salt %, smoke time, drying time, final weight, final MC or aw. This record is your quality system and your sales evidence.

8. Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Start clean: potable water, food-grade salt, sanitised trays and knives.
  2. Salt first: brine or dry-salt before smoke or dry; never skip this step.
  3. Control temperature: solar dryer 4560C; hot smoke 7085C; cold smoke below 30C.
  4. Exclude insects: mesh screens, fast drying, light smoke, clean surroundings.
  5. Use safe wood: acacia, mopane, or mesquite; never treated, painted, or resinous softwood.
  6. Measure dryness: target MC 1535% and aw 0.85; record every batch.
  7. Package properly: vacuum-seal or airtight container with desiccant; label with date and batch number.
  8. Comply and document: meet national food safety standards; keep records for buyers and regulators.

The same kilogram, dried and smoked into a branded, shelf-stable product, can sell for USD 1530 in urban or export markets. The equipment is cheap, the skills are learnable, and the market is growing. The constraint is consistency and compliance. Build the dryer, standardise the recipe, measure the moisture, and document the batch. That is how a traditional method becomes a business.
