1. Overview of Beef Primal Cuts

When a beef carcass is split into wholesale cuts, we call these primal cuts. In Africa, depending on the region and market, you'll find variations, but the main primals are: chuck, rib, brisket, shank, plate, flank, loin (short loin and sirloin), round, and variety meats. Each primal has distinct muscle structure, fat cover, and bone. Knowing these helps you decide how to break them down further for retail or food service.

Start by identifying the four quarters: forequarter and hindquarter, each split into two. The forequarter includes chuck, rib, brisket, shank, and plate. The hindquarter includes flank, loin, and round. In many African butcheries, whole carcasses are bought and broken down in-house. You'll need a sturdy butcher block, a bone saw, a breaking knife (10-12 inch), a boning knife (6 inch), a sharpening steel, and a meat hook. Always wear a chainmail glove on your non-knife hand for safety.

The chuck is the shoulder area, heavily exercised, so it's tough but flavorful. Great for stews, ground beef, and slow-cooked dishes like potjiekos. The rib is the upper back, containing the ribeye. In Africa, ribeye is often sold as a premium steak or used for braai. The brisket is the chest, with lots of connective tissue; perfect for slow roasting or corning. The shank is the leg, full of collagen; ideal for soups and osso buco. The plate is the belly, used for short ribs or ground beef. The flank is the abdominal muscle, lean and tough; used for stir-fry or London broil. The loin is the back, yielding tender steaks like sirloin, T-bone, and fillet. The round is the rear leg, lean and tough; used for roasts, steaks (like rump), and ground beef. Variety meats include liver, kidney, heart, tongue, and tripe.

In African markets, primal cuts are often further processed to meet local preferences. For example, in South Africa, biltong is made from silverside (round). In Nigeria, beef is often sold as bone-in pieces for soups. Understanding primals allows you to maximize yield and minimize waste. A typical beef carcass yields about 55-60% usable meat after fabrication. The rest is fat, bone, and trim. Always keep your knives sharp and your workspace clean to prevent contamination. Practice identifying each primal on a whole carcass or a chart until you can name them without hesitation.

2. Breaking Down a Beef Forequarter

The forequarter is the front half of the carcass, comprising about 55% of the beef. It includes the chuck, rib, brisket, shank, and plate. Breaking it down starts by separating the chuck from the rib. Use a bone saw to cut through the spine between the 5th and 6th ribs. Then, remove the brisket and shank by cutting along the natural seams. The shank is the front leg, cut off at the knee joint. The brisket is the chest, separated by cutting along the cartilage.

Next, break the chuck into sub-primals: the shoulder clod, chuck roll, and chuck tender. The shoulder clod is the large muscle on top; it can be cut into steaks (like flat iron) or used for pot roast. The chuck roll is under the blade bone; it's well-marbled and great for ground beef or stew. The chuck tender is a small, cone-shaped muscle near the shoulder; it's tender enough for quick cooking but often used for stir-fry. The rib section is separated into ribeye and rib bones. The ribeye can be cut into steaks (ribeye, rib steak) or boned out for roasting. The rib bones are meaty and can be sold as short ribs.

The plate is the lower belly, separated from the rib by cutting along the costal cartilage. It contains the skirt steak and short ribs. The skirt steak is a thin, flavorful cut ideal for grilling; in Africa, it's often used for braai or asada. Short ribs are cut into 2-inch pieces and are great for slow cooking. The brisket is divided into the point (fattier) and flat (leaner). The flat is often corned or smoked; the point is used for burnt ends or ground beef. The shank is cut into osso buco pieces or used for soup bones.

Throughout the process, use a meat hook to move heavy pieces. Keep your knives sharp; a dull knife is dangerous. Work on a clean surface and sanitize between steps. In African butcheries, forequarter cuts are often sold as bone-in pieces for traditional dishes. For example, in Kenya, goat and beef forequarter are used for nyama choma. Yield from a forequarter is about 70-75% usable meat after trimming. Practice breaking down a forequarter in under 30 minutes with minimal waste.

3. Breaking Down a Beef Hindquarter

The hindquarter is the rear half of the carcass, about 45% of the beef. It includes the flank, loin, and round. Start by separating the flank from the loin by cutting along the natural seam. The flank is a thin, lean cut; remove the membrane and use for stir-fry or ground beef. Then, separate the loin from the round by cutting between the last lumbar vertebra and the first sacral vertebra. Use a bone saw to cut through the spine.

The loin is divided into short loin and sirloin. The short loin contains the tenderloin, strip loin, and T-bone. The tenderloin is the most tender cut; remove it by cutting along the backbone. It can be sold whole or cut into fillet steaks. The strip loin is the top muscle; cut into New York strip steaks. The T-bone includes both strip and tenderloin, with a T-shaped bone. The sirloin is behind the short loin; it contains the top sirloin, bottom sirloin, and tenderloin tip. Top sirloin is a lean, flavorful steak; bottom sirloin (tri-tip) is great for grilling. In Africa, sirloin is often used for braai or as a roast.

The round is the rear leg, divided into top round (inside), bottom round (outside), eye of round, and knuckle. Top round is lean and tough; it's often used for roast beef or steaks like London broil. Bottom round is used for rump roasts or ground beef. Eye of round is a cylindrical muscle; it's uniform and used for deli roast beef. The knuckle is between the top and bottom round; it's used for ground beef or stew. The shank (hind shank) is cut off at the hock and used for soup bones or osso buco.

Use a breaking knife and bone saw. A meat hook helps move the heavy hindquarter. Always cut away from yourself. In African butcheries, hindquarter cuts are often sold as bone-in pieces for stews or grilled. For example, in South Africa, rump is popular for braai. Yield from a hindquarter is about 75-80% usable meat. Practice breaking down a hindquarter in under 40 minutes. Remember to trim excess fat and silverskin to improve presentation and texture.

4. Producing Steaks from the Loin

The loin is the most valuable part of the beef carcass, yielding tender steaks like fillet, strip, T-bone, and sirloin. To produce steaks, start with a whole loin (short loin and sirloin). First, remove the tenderloin from the short loin. Use a boning knife to cut along the backbone, separating the tenderloin from the strip loin. Trim the tenderloin of silverskin and fat. Cut into steaks of desired thickness: 2-3 cm for fillet mignon, 4-5 cm for chateaubriand. In Africa, fillet is often sold whole for special occasions or cut into medallions for grilling.

Next, the strip loin. Remove the bone if making boneless strip steaks, or leave it for bone-in strip. Trim the fat cap to about 0.5 cm. Cut into steaks 2.5-3 cm thick. For T-bone steaks, cut the short loin into steaks that include both strip and tenderloin, with the T-shaped bone. Use a bone saw to cut through the bone. T-bone steaks are typically 3-4 cm thick. In South Africa, T-bone is a popular braai cut.

The sirloin is separated from the short loin. The top sirloin can be cut into steaks (2.5 cm thick) or left whole for roasting. The bottom sirloin (tri-tip) is a triangular muscle; it can be cut into steaks or left whole for grilling. The tri-tip is popular in African braais for its flavor and tenderness when cooked correctly. For sirloin steaks, trim excess fat and silverskin. Cut against the grain for tenderness.

Always use a sharp knife; a dull knife will tear the meat. For consistent thickness, use a steak cutter or a ruler. In African butcheries, steaks are often cut to order. Yield from a whole loin: about 12-15 steaks (depending on thickness). Practice cutting uniform steaks. Remember to keep your workspace clean and sanitize between cuts. After cutting, steaks can be vacuum-sealed or wrapped in butcher paper for sale. Properly stored at 0-4C, steaks last 3-5 days. For longer storage, freeze at -18C.

5. Cutting Roasts from the Round and Chuck

The round and chuck are tough but flavorful, ideal for roasts. From the round, you can produce top round roast, bottom round roast, eye of round roast, and rump roast. Start with the top round (inside). Trim excess fat and silverskin. Cut into roasts of 1.5-2 kg, or cut into steaks like London broil. Top round roast is lean and best cooked low and slow, then sliced thin. In Africa, it's often used for pot roast or sliced for sandwiches.

The bottom round (outside) is divided into the flat and the eye. The flat can be cut into rump roasts (1.5-2 kg) or used for stew. The eye of round is a cylindrical muscle; cut into uniform roasts (1-1.5 kg) for deli roast beef. The knuckle is between top and bottom round; it can be cut into roasts or used for ground beef. The hind shank is cut into osso buco pieces for soup.

From the chuck, you can produce chuck roasts, shoulder roasts, and blade roasts. The chuck roll is a large muscle; cut into 2-3 kg roasts. The shoulder clod can be cut into roasts (2-3 kg) or steaks. The blade roast is from the blade bone area; it's well-marbled and great for pot roast. In African cooking, chuck roast is often used for stews like Nigerian beef stew or South African potjiekos. Cut roasts against the grain for tenderness. Trim fat to 0.5 cm. For even cooking, tie roasts with butcher twine.

Use a breaking knife and bone saw. For boneless roasts, remove bones carefully. Yield from round: about 70% usable meat. From chuck: about 65%. Practice cutting uniform roasts. Store roasts at 0-4C for up to 5 days, or freeze at -18C for up to 6 months. In African butcheries, roasts are often sold bone-in for flavor. Always label with cut and date.

6. Identifying and Preparing Variety Meats

Variety meats, also called offal, include liver, kidney, heart, tongue, tripe, oxtail, and sweetbreads. In African cuisine, these are highly valued and used in many traditional dishes. For example, in Nigeria, liver is used in peppered liver, kidney in kidney stew, and tripe in shaki. In South Africa, oxtail is a delicacy, and tongue is often pickled or boiled.

Start with liver. Beef liver is dark red, smooth, and slightly firm. Remove any bile ducts or green spots. Soak in milk or salt water for 30 minutes to reduce bitterness. Cut into slices or cubes. In Africa, liver is often fried with onions and peppers. Kidney: beef kidneys are bean-shaped, dark red, with a strong flavor. Remove the white core and soak in salted water. Cut into cubes. Heart: beef heart is muscular, lean, and tough. Trim fat and valves, then cut into cubes or slices. It's great for stews or grilling. Tongue: beef tongue is covered in a tough skin. Boil for 2-3 hours, then peel off skin. Slice and serve hot or cold. In African markets, tongue is often sold pickled.

Tripe: beef tripe is the stomach lining, usually honeycomb or blanket. It requires extensive cleaning: wash, scrape, and boil for 2-3 hours until tender. In Africa, tripe is used in soups and stews, like Nigerian shaki. Oxtail: the tail of the beef, cut into segments. It's rich in collagen and great for slow-cooked stews. Soak in water, then brown and simmer for 3-4 hours. Sweetbreads: thymus or pancreas, creamy and delicate. Soak in water, blanch, then remove membrane. Saut or grill.

Always handle variety meats with care; they are highly perishable. Keep refrigerated at 0-4C and use within 2 days. Clean thoroughly to avoid contamination. In African butcheries, variety meats are often sold fresh or frozen. Learn to identify each by appearance, texture, and smell. Practice preparing each type. For example, to clean tripe, use salt and lime juice to scrub, then rinse. Yield from variety meats is about 90% after cleaning. They are nutritious and affordable, making them important for food security.

Practical Reference: Beef Fabrication for African Butcheries

This practical reference supports the Butchery course module Beef Fabrication on the RISE AFRICA skills platform. It is written for butchers, meat traders, and small abattoir operators who need to turn a carcass into saleable cuts with minimal waste, consistent yield, and safe handling. In most African markets, beef is sold either as bone-in cuts for stewing and braising or as trimmed primal cuts for grilling and roasting. The margin difference between a poorly broken carcass and a well-fabricated one can be 1530% of revenue, so fabrication skill is a business skill.

The lessons in this module cover the full sequence: Overview of Beef Primal Cuts, Breaking Down a Beef Forequarter, Breaking Down a Beef Hindquarter, Producing Steaks from the Loin, Cutting Roasts from the Round and Chuck, and Identifying and Preparing Variety Meats. This reference consolidates the practical knowledge into checklists, tables, and a visual guide you can print and pin in the cutting room.

1. Primal Cuts and Their Commercial Value

The carcass is first split into two sides, then broken into primal cuts. In African butcheries, the split between forequarter and hindquarter is usually made between the 5th and 6th rib or between the 12th and 13th rib depending on market preference. The hindquarter carries the high-value loin and round; the forequarter carries the chuck, brisket, and shank, which are slower-moving but essential for stewing and braising markets.

2. Breaking Down the Forequarter and Hindquarter

The forequarter is separated from the hindquarter at the 5th6th rib or 12th13th rib. The forequarter is then split into chuck, rib, brisket, shank, and plate. The hindquarter is split into loin, sirloin, round, flank, and shank. The key business decision is where to make the split: a higher split (12th rib) gives more loin to the hindquarter and more chuck to the forequarter, which suits markets that value grilling cuts. A lower split (5th rib) suits markets that value stewing beef and mince.

When breaking the forequarter, always remove the shank first, then separate the brisket and plate along the natural seam. The chuck is separated from the rib at the 5th rib. When breaking the hindquarter, remove the flank first, then separate the round from the loin at the hip joint. The tenderloin is removed from the loin before portioning steaks.

3. Producing Steaks from the Loin

The loin is the highest-value primal. It is divided into short loin (striploin), sirloin, and tenderloin. Steaks are cut perpendicular to the muscle grain at 24 cm thickness depending on market preference. In many African markets, 2.5 cm is standard for grilling. The tenderloin is trimmed of silver skin and chain before portioning into fillet steaks. Yield from the loin is typically 7075% after trimming; the trim goes to mince or sausage.

SAFETY WARNING Cold chain and knife hygiene

Beef must be held at or below 4C during fabrication and 18C or below for frozen storage. In hot African climates, work in short cycles and return cuts to cold storage immediately. Never leave carcasses or cuts at ambient temperature for more than 2 hours. Use separate colour-coded boards for raw meat and ready-to-eat products. Sanitise knives and surfaces with 70% alcohol or approved food-grade sanitiser between species and between carcasses. Wash hands with soap and potable water before and after handling. Failure to control temperature and hygiene is the leading cause of meat spoilage and foodborne illness in small butcheries.

4. Cutting Roasts from the Round and Chuck

The round is divided into topside, silverside, knuckle, and shank. Topside and silverside are the main roasting cuts; they are also the preferred cuts for biltong and dry-cured products in Southern Africa. The chuck is divided into chuck roll, chuck tender, and blade. Chuck roll is ideal for slow roasting and pulled beef; blade is used for stewing and mince. Roasts are typically cut at 1.52.5 kg for household markets and 35 kg for catering.

When cutting roasts, remove excess fat and connective tissue but retain a thin fat cap for moisture. Tie roasts with butcher's twine at 34 cm intervals to maintain shape during cooking. Label with cut name, weight, and date. In business terms, roasts from the round and chuck allow you to sell lower-value primals at a higher price per kilogram than stewing cubes, improving overall carcass realisation.

5. Variety Meats (Offal)

Variety meats liver, kidney, heart, tongue, oxtail, tripe, and head meat are high-demand products in many African markets and can add 812% to carcass revenue if handled well. They must be inspected, cleaned, and chilled quickly. Liver and kidney should be sold fresh; tongue and tripe require scalding and cleaning. Oxtail is a premium item in stew markets. Do not discard offal: it is a profit centre, not a waste stream.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Verify carcass inspection stamp and cold chain records before fabrication begins.
  2. Confirm the forequarter/hindquarter split point (5th6th rib or 12th13th rib) against customer demand.
  3. Separate primals along natural seams never saw through muscle where a seam exists.
  4. Trim silver skin, excess fat, and bruising; reserve trim for mince or sausage.
  5. Cut steaks perpendicular to muscle grain at the thickness specified in Table 2.
  6. Tie and label roasts with cut name, weight, and date; keep fat cap thin.
  7. Clean and chill variety meats immediately; label species and date.
  8. Record yields per primal and compare against Table 1 to control shrinkage and theft.
