1. Preparing the Pond Bottom for Stocking

Before you fill your pond with water and fish, the bottom must be prepared properly. A good pond bottom holds water, supports natural fish food, and does not release harmful gases. Start by draining the pond completely and letting it dry for 7 to 14 days. Sunlight kills many disease organisms and parasites. If you cannot drain, pump out as much water as possible and use a rototiller or hoe to break up the top 10-15 cm of soil. Remove all debris: leaves, branches, rocks, and any dead animals. These rot and consume oxygen.

Next, test the soil pH. Use a simple soil test kit from an agro-vet shop. If pH is below 6.5, you will need to lime (covered in Lesson 2). If the soil is very sandy, add 10-15 cm of clay soil to improve water retention. For muddy bottoms, add 5-10 cm of coarse sand to firm it up. Level the bottom so it slopes gently toward the drainabout 1% slope (1 cm drop per meter). This helps you drain completely later.

After leveling, compact the soil by walking behind a roller or tamping with a heavy wooden post. This reduces seepage. If you have access to cow dung or compost, spread a 5 cm layer and work it into the top 10 cm of soil. This feeds natural plankton once water is added. But do not use fresh manureit carries diseases. Use only well-rotted manure.

Finally, fill the pond with clean water to a depth of 20-30 cm and let it sit for 3-5 days. This allows any remaining harmful gases to escape. Then top up to your desired depth. Stock fish only after the water turns a light green colour, which shows that plankton is growing. If the water stays clear, add 1 kg of NPK fertilizer per 100 square meters to kick-start the food chain. Always test water temperature and pH before stocking.

2. Soil Treatment and Liming

Liming is one of the cheapest and most effective ways to improve pond bottom health. It raises soil pH, kills parasites, and releases nutrients like phosphorus and potassium from the mud. Use agricultural limestone (calcium carbonate) or dolomite (calcium magnesium carbonate). Do not use quicklime (calcium oxide) or hydrated limethey are too caustic and can kill fish.

First, test your soil pH. If pH is between 5.5 and 6.5, apply 200-300 kg of limestone per 1000 square meters (0.1 hectare). If pH is below 5.5, use 400-500 kg per 1000 square meters. Spread the lime evenly over the dry pond bottom using a shovel or a broadcast spreader. Then mix it into the top 10-15 cm of soil with a hoe or rototiller. Do not just dump it in one spot.

If your pond is already full, you can still lime the water. Use 100-200 kg per 1000 square meters, but first mix the lime in a bucket of water and pour it evenly over the surface. Never add lime directly to water with fishit can burn their gills. For water application, use only agricultural limestone, not quicklime.

After liming, wait at least 7 days before stocking fish. The pH will rise, and you want it to stabilize. Test pH again: it should be between 6.5 and 8.5. If it is still low, apply another 100 kg per 1000 square meters. You can also use wood ash from cooking fires as a substituteit contains calcium and potassium. Apply 100-200 kg per 1000 square meters, but only from clean wood, not charcoal briquettes with chemicals.

Remember: liming is not a one-time job. Test soil pH every 6 months. If you use a lot of feed, the bottom will become acidic over time. Regular small applications of 50-100 kg per 1000 square meters every 3 months keep the bottom healthy. Always wear gloves and a dust mask when handling limeit dries your skin and irritates your lungs.

3. Installing Bottom Drains and Sludge Removal

A bottom drain is a pipe at the lowest point of your pond that lets you remove sludge and old water without draining the whole pond. This is critical for keeping the bottom clean and fish healthy. For a small pond (up to 500 square meters), use a 50-75 mm diameter PVC pipe. For larger ponds, use 110 mm or bigger. The drain should be set into a concrete sumpa small pit 30-40 cm deep and 50 cm wideat the deepest end of the pond. Slope the pond bottom toward this sump at 1-2%.

Install a standpipe or a valve on the drain outlet. A standpipe is a vertical pipe that you can remove to lower the water level. A valve (gate or ball valve) is easier to control. Put a screen or grate over the drain inlet to stop fish from escaping. For sludge removal, you can also use a siphon hosea 25-50 mm flexible hose that you start by sucking water, then let gravity pull sludge out. Siphon into a bucket or a nearby garden bed.

To remove sludge without a drain, use a sludge pump or a trash pump. These cost money, but you can rent one from a farm supply shop. A cheaper method is manual: use a long-handled scoop or a bucket on a rope. Work in the cool morning hours. Remove 2-5 cm of the top sludge layer every 2-4 weeks. Do not remove all the mudsome is needed for beneficial bacteria.

After removing sludge, add fresh water to replace what you took out. If you have a bottom drain, open the valve for 10-15 seconds each day to flush out settled waste. This small daily flush prevents large sludge build-up. For ponds with no drain, consider installing one before the next stocking cycle. The cost of a 50 mm PVC pipe and fittings is low compared to the loss of fish from poor water quality. Always keep a bucket of clean water nearby to wash your hands and tools after handling sludge.

4. Using Biofilters and Substrates

A biofilter is a place where good bacteria live. These bacteria convert fish waste (ammonia) into less harmful nitrate. In ponds, the bottom itself is a biofilter if it has the right substratematerial like gravel, sand, or coconut fibre. You can also build a separate biofilter tank using a drum or a wooden box filled with gravel, sand, and charcoal. Water from the pond is pumped through this filter and back into the pond.

For a simple in-pond biofilter, add 5-10 cm of clean gravel (5-10 mm size) over 20% of the pond bottom. The gravel gives bacteria a huge surface area. You can also hang bundles of plastic mesh, old mosquito nets, or coconut husks in the water. These are called substrates. Bacteria grow on them and eat ammonia. For every 1000 litres of water, you need about 1 square meter of substrate surface area. So a 10,000-litre pond needs 10 square meters of substrateabout 10 square meters of netting or 20 coconut husks.

To start a biofilter, you need to seed it with bacteria. You can buy a commercial starter from an aquaculture shop, or use 1 litre of water from an established healthy pond. Add this to your new pond. It takes 2-4 weeks for the bacteria to grow enough to handle fish waste. During this time, do not stock many fish. Add only a few fish, or feed very lightly.

Clean your biofilter gently. Never scrub it with soap or chlorinethat kills the bacteria. If the gravel gets clogged with sludge, rinse it in pond water, not tap water. Tap water has chlorine that kills bacteria. For a drum biofilter, backwash it every 2 weeks by opening a valve at the bottom and letting water run out until it runs clear. Replace 10% of the gravel every 6 months. In Africa, many farmers use old rice sacks filled with charcoal and gravel as cheap biofilters. These work well if water flows through them slowly. Always keep the biofilter wetdry bacteria die in hours.

5. Managing Benthic Zones for Fish Health

The benthic zone is the bottom layer of your pondthe mud, water, and all the tiny creatures living there. A healthy benthic zone is full of insects, worms, and snails that fish eat. But if it becomes polluted, it breeds disease and produces toxic gases like hydrogen sulphide (rotten egg smell) and methane. You need to manage this zone actively.

First, keep the bottom oxygenated. Oxygen from the surface does not easily reach the bottom in deep ponds. So keep your pond depth at 1.0-1.5 meters for most tilapia and catfish. If deeper, use a paddle wheel aerator or a simple splash aerator (a pump that sprays water into the air). Run it for 2-3 hours at night when oxygen is lowest. You can also add 1-2 kg of potassium permanganate per 1000 square meters every 2 weeks. This chemical releases oxygen into the mud. But do not overdoseit can kill fish.

Second, control organic matter. Uneaten feed and fish waste sink and rot. Feed only what fish can eat in 15 minutes. Remove any leftover feed with a fine net after feeding. Every week, use a pond vacuum or a manual scoop to remove the top 1-2 cm of sludge from the deepest part. If sludge smells like rotten eggs, you have a problem. Add 100-200 g of agricultural gypsum per square meter to bind the sulphide. Gypsum is cheap and safe.

Third, encourage beneficial benthic life. Add 10-20 kg of well-rotted cow dung per 100 square meters once a month. This feeds worms and insect larvae that fish love. But do not overdo ittoo much manure consumes oxygen. Also, add 5-10 kg of crushed oyster shells or eggshells per 100 square meters. These provide calcium and raise pH slightly. Finally, avoid using insecticides or herbicides near the pond. They kill the benthic food chain. If you must treat a disease, use only approved aquaculture medicines. Test your benthic zone by smelling the mud and looking for small worms. If you see bubbles rising from the mud, that is methaneincrease aeration immediately.

6. Techniques for Pond Bottom Cleaning

Cleaning the pond bottom is not a one-time job. It must be done regularly to keep fish healthy. Here are practical techniques you can use, from cheapest to most expensive.

Manual cleaning: Drain the pond completely. Use a shovel, hoe, and buckets to remove the top 5-10 cm of sludge. This is hard work but costs nothing but your time. Do it during the dry season when you are not stocking fish. Spread the sludge on a garden bedit is good fertilizer. After removing sludge, let the bottom dry for 7-14 days until it cracks. Then level and compact it again. For a 100-square-meter pond, two people can finish in one day.

Partial cleaning without draining: Use a siphon hose. Fill a 25-50 mm hose with water, cover both ends, put one end in the pond sludge and the other end in a bucket below pond level. Release the ends and let gravity pull sludge out. Move the hose around the bottom. You can also use a sludge pump (a small petrol or electric pump with a filter) to suck sludge directly. Rent one for a day if you cannot buy. Another method is to use a long-handled pool net to scoop floating debris and sludge from the surface of the mud.

Biological cleaning: Add 5-10 kg of molasses per 1000 square meters every 2 weeks. Molasses feeds beneficial bacteria that break down sludge. Also, add 1-2 kg of commercial sludge-eating bacteria (like those used in septic tanks) per 1000 square meters once a month. These are available at aquaculture shops. For a cheap alternative, use 1 litre of sour milk or 500 g of yeast mixed with 10 litres of water, poured over the bottom.

Preventive cleaning: Never overfeed. Use floating feed so you can see and remove leftovers. Keep the pond bottom sloped and drained. Install a bottom drain if possible. Test water quality weekly for ammonia, nitrite, and pH. If ammonia is above 0.5 mg/litre, do a 30% water change and add molasses. Clean your biofilter regularly. Finally, rotate crops: every 2-3 years, drain the pond completely, dry it, and grow vegetables on the bottom for one season. This breaks disease cycles and rejuvenates the soil.

<h2>Practical Reference: Pond Bottom and Substrate Management for African Fish Farms</h2>

<p>In African pond aquaculture whether you are running a smallholder tilapia pond in Kisumu, a catfish grow-out operation in Delta State, or a commercial <em>Clarias</em> and <em>Oreochromis</em> polyculture in Ghana's Volta Region the pond bottom is where profitability is made or lost. Feed represents 6070% of operating costs. The bottom is where uneaten feed, faeces, and dead algae accumulate, consume oxygen, and release ammonia and hydrogen sulphide that suppress growth and trigger disease. Managing the substrate is not a technical nicety; it is a direct lever on feed conversion ratio (FCR), survival, and your cost per kilogram of fish sold.</p>

<p>This practical reference condenses the six core lessons of this module into field-ready procedures, data, and checklists. Use it during pond preparation, between cycles, and as a diagnostic tool when fish stop feeding or mortalities spike.</p>

<h3>1. Preparing the Pond Bottom for Stocking</h3>
<p>The single highest-return activity on any African fish farm is a proper dry-out between cycles. After harvest, drain completely, remove the sludge layer (the top 515 cm of black, anoxic mud), and let the bottom dry until it cracks. Sun-drying oxidises organic matter, kills pathogens and fish parasites, and mineralises nutrients. On sandy or lateritic soils common in West and Central Africa, drying may take 37 days; on heavy clay soils in the Nile Delta or Malawi's Shire Valley, allow 714 days.</p>

<p>Where complete draining is not possible (common in valley-bottom ponds or where water rights are shared), pump out as much as possible, apply agricultural lime at higher rates, and use mechanical agitation to resuspend and flush sludge through the drain.</p>

<h3>2. Soil Treatment and Liming</h3>
<p>African pond soils are frequently acidic (pH 4.56.5), especially in the humid forest zones of Nigeria, Cameroon, and the Congo Basin, and in leached sandy soils of the Sahel. Acidic bottoms reduce phosphorus availability, suppress natural food production, and stress fish. Liming raises pH, increases alkalinity, and improves the efficiency of every fertiliser naira or cedi you spend.</p>

<p>Apply agricultural limestone (CaCO) or dolomite (CaMg(CO)) to the dry bottom and incorporate into the top 10 cm. Do not use quicklime (CaO) or hydrated lime (Ca(OH)) as a routine soil treatment they are caustic and dangerous to handle. Test soil pH with a simple field kit or send samples to a national agricultural research station (e.g. NARS in Nigeria, CSIR in Ghana, KALRO in Kenya).</p>

<h3>3. Installing Bottom Drains and Sludge Removal</h3>
<p>A well-designed bottom drain is the cheapest sludge-management tool you will ever install. For new ponds, slope the bottom 12% toward a monk or krantz structure with a drain pipe sized for full emptying in 48 hours. For existing ponds, retrofitting a central drain is possible but disruptive schedule it between cycles.</p>

<p>During the cycle, partial draining from the bottom (510% of volume weekly) removes the most concentrated sludge and ammonia load. This is far more effective than surface overflow alone. In African contexts where water is scarce or expensive, recirculating the drained water through a settlement basin before reuse can cut water bills by 3050%.</p>

<div style="background:#fdecea; border-left:6px solid #c0392b; padding:12px 16px; margin:16px 0; font-family:Arial; font-size:14px;">
<strong style="color:#c0392b;"> SAFETY WARNING HYDROGEN SULPHIDE AND ANAEROBIC SLUDGE</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Never enter a drained or partially drained pond to remove sludge without first testing the air for hydrogen sulphide (HS) and ensuring forced ventilation. HS is a colourless gas with a rotten-egg smell at low concentrations, but it deadens the sense of smell at higher concentrations and can cause rapid unconsciousness and death. Several African farm workers have been killed entering sludge pits and pond bottoms. Always work in pairs, use a rope harness, ventilate for at least 30 minutes, and never rely on smell as a warning. Wear gloves and boots sludge may harbour <em>Aeromonas</em>, <em>Edwardsiella</em>, and parasitic cercariae that cause schistosomiasis.</p>
</div>

<h3>4. Using Biofilters and Substrates</h3>
<p>Biofilters and artificial substrates convert toxic ammonia into less harmful nitrate and provide surface area for beneficial bacteria and periphyton. In Africa, low-cost options dominate: bamboo poles, palm fronds, rice straw bundles, and plastic mesh. These "periphyton substrates" can increase tilapia yields by 2040% without extra feed, according to studies in Malawi, Egypt, and Ghana.</p>

<p>For intensive systems (catfish, tilapia with aeration), a simple trickling biofilter or moving-bed bioreactor using locally available plastic media can maintain total ammonia nitrogen (TAN) below 1 mg/L. Budget-conscious farmers can use crushed granite, gravel, or even discarded plastic bottles cut into rings.</p>

<h3>5. Managing Benthic Zones for Fish Health</h3>
<p>The benthic (bottom) zone is where most pathogens and parasites complete their life cycles. <em>Streptococcus agalactiae</em>, <em>Aeromonas hydrophila</em>, and the ciliate <em>Ichthyophthirius</em> all thrive in organically rich bottoms. The practical rule: keep the redox potential of the top 5 cm above +100 mV. In plain language, keep the bottom brown, not black.</p>

<p>Techniques that work in African conditions:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Weekly bottom inspection</strong> with a simple Secchi-disk-on-a-stick or a bottom sampler. If the mud smells of rotten eggs, you have a problem.</li>
<li><strong>Probiotics</strong> <em>Bacillus</em> spp. products are increasingly available in Nigeria, Kenya, and South Africa. Apply according to label; they compete with pathogens and break down organic matter.</li>
<li><strong>Stocking density discipline</strong> do not exceed 35 fish/m for extensive tilapia, 1020/m for semi-intensive with aeration. Overstocking is the fastest route to benthic collapse.</li>
<li><strong>Feed management</strong> use floating pellets, feed to satiation twice daily, and stop feeding when fish stop rising. Uneaten feed is the primary sludge source.</li>
</ul>

<h3>6. Techniques for Pond Bottom Cleaning</h3>
<p>Between cycles, the standard sequence is: drain dry remove sludge lime refill. For ponds that cannot be fully drained, use one or more of the following:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Mechanical agitation:</strong> drag a chain or weighted net across the bottom to resuspend sludge, then flush through the drain.</li>
<li><strong>Sludge pumps:</strong> small diesel or solar-powered pumps can lift the bottom 10 cm of sludge out of the pond. Cost: USD 200800 depending on capacity.</li>
<li><strong>Biological digestion:</strong> apply commercial sludge-digesting bacteria (e.g. <em>Bacillus subtilis</em>, <em>B. licheniformis</em>) at 510 g/m weekly during the cycle.</li>
<li><strong>Manual removal:</strong> for small ponds (&lt; 500 m), shovelling out the top 5 cm of sludge is labour-intensive but effective. Budget 12 person-days per 100 m.</li>
</ul>
