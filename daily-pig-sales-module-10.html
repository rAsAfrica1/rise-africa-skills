1. Stage 1: 5 Sows, 2 Pigs Per Week

Stage 1 is where you prove the system works with minimal risk. You keep 5 sows and sell 2 pigs per week. That means each sow produces about 0.4 pigs per week, or roughly 2 pigs every 5 weeks. With a 3-week batch farrowing schedule, you group sows so that you get a steady flow. For 5 sows, you can farrow 2 sows every 6 weeks. Each sow gives birth to 8-10 piglets. After weaning at 4 weeks, you raise the piglets to 8-10 weeks or 60-70 kg, then sell. With 5 sows, you'll have about 40-50 piglets per cycle. To sell 2 per week, you need 8 pigs per month. That's achievable if you keep 2 sows farrowing every 6 weeks. Your main costs: feed (about 2 kg per sow per day plus piglet feed), housing (simple pens with concrete floors and iron sheets), and health (dewormers, vaccines). In Africa, you can start with locally available materials: mud bricks, timber, and thatch for roofing. But invest in a good boar or use artificial insemination (AI) to avoid inbreeding. Record keeping is critical: use a notebook to track farrowing dates, number of piglets born, and sales. At this stage, you're learning the system. Sell to local butcheries, hotels, or neighbors. Price per kg live weight in many African countries ranges from $2 to $3. Your goal is to cover costs and save for expansion. Don't rush. Master batch farrowing: all sows in a group farrow within the same week, so you can manage them together. This reduces labor and improves efficiency. After 6 months, you should have enough savings to move to Stage 2.

2. Stage 2: 15 Sows, 1 Pig Per Day

At Stage 2, you have 15 sows and sell 1 pig per dayâthat's 7 pigs per week or about 30 per month. To achieve this, you need a batch farrowing schedule with 3 groups of 5 sows each. Each group farrows every 7 weeks (since gestation is 114 days, about 16 weeks, plus weaning). Actually, with 15 sows, you can farrow 5 sows every 5 weeks. Each sow gives 8-10 piglets, so 5 sows give 40-50 piglets. You raise them to 70-80 kg and sell. To sell 1 per day, you need 30 pigs per month. With 5 sows farrowing every 5 weeks, you get about 45 piglets per batch, which is 9 pigs per weekâenough for daily sales. You'll need better housing: separate pens for farrowing, weaning, and finishing. Use concrete floors for easy cleaning. Feed costs become significant: each sow eats 2.5 kg per day, so 15 sows eat 37.5 kg daily. Plus piglet feed. You'll need to mix your own feed to cut costs: use maize, soybean meal, fish meal, and additives. In Africa, you can source these locally. Water is essential: automatic drinkers or simple nipple drinkers. Health program: vaccinate against swine fever, deworm every 3 months. Hire one worker to help with daily chores. Record keeping: use a wall chart or simple software like Excel. Track feed conversion, mortality, and sales. Marketing: establish contracts with local hotels, restaurants, and butcheries. You can also sell to neighbors. At this stage, you should be profitable. Reinvest profits into expanding to Stage 3. Aim to reach 15 sows within 1-2 years.

3. Stage 3: 30 Sows, 2 Pigs Per Day

Stage 3: 30 sows, selling 2 pigs per dayâ14 per week, 60 per month. This requires a well-organized batch farrowing system. Divide sows into 6 groups of 5. Each group farrows every 6 weeks. With 30 sows, you can farrow 5 sows every 3 weeks. That gives 5 sows x 8 piglets = 40 piglets per batch. You need 60 pigs per month, so you need about 1.5 batches per month. That means you should farrow every 2 weeks. So organize 6 groups of 5 sows, each group farrowing every 12 weeks (since 6 groups x 2 weeks = 12 weeks). Each sow produces 2 litters per year. This is intensive. Housing: you need pens for 30 sows, plus piglets at different stages. Build a grow-out house for 200 pigs. Use concrete floors, slatted floors for waste management. Feed: 30 sows eat 75 kg per day. Plus piglets. You'll need a feed mixer and storage. Consider buying feed ingredients in bulk: maize, soy, fish meal. Vaccination program: swine fever, erysipelas, deworming. Biosecurity: footbaths, limited visitors. Labor: 2-3 workers. Record keeping: use a computer or smartphone app to track individual sow performance. Marketing: you need consistent buyers. Contract with a slaughterhouse or multiple butcheries. Price negotiation: sell by weight, not per head. At this stage, you can start adding value: process pork into sausages, bacon, etc. But focus on production first. Finances: you'll need a loan or savings to expand. Keep detailed records to show banks. Your goal is to reach 30 sows within 3-4 years. Reinvest profits to move to Stage 4.

4. Stage 4: 60 Sows, 5 Pigs Per Day

Stage 4: 60 sows, 5 pigs per dayâ35 per week, 150 per month. This is a commercial operation. You need a strict batch farrowing schedule. Divide sows into 12 groups of 5. Each group farrows every 6 weeks. That means 2 groups farrow every week. Each group gives 40 piglets, so 80 piglets per week. You need 35 pigs per week for sale, so you have surplus for replacement gilts or expansion. Housing: separate buildings for gestation, farrowing, nursery, and finishing. Farrowing crates for sows to reduce piglet mortality. Climate control: fans or open sides for ventilation. Feed: 60 sows eat 150 kg per day. You need a feed mill on site. Mix your own feed using a hammer mill and mixer. Store ingredients in silos. Water: automatic drinkers. Health: full vaccination program, regular vet visits. Biosecurity: shower-in, shower-out, no visitors without permission. Labor: 5-6 workers, including a manager. Record keeping: use software like PigCHAMP or simple Excel. Track individual sow productivity, feed conversion, mortality. Marketing: you need contracts with large buyers: supermarkets, processors, export. Consider forming a cooperative with other farmers to negotiate better prices. At this stage, you can also sell breeding stock. Finances: you'll need significant capital. Prepare a business plan for banks or investors. Your goal is to reach 60 sows within 5-7 years. Reinvest profits to reach Stage 5.

5. Stage 5: 120 Sows, 10 Pigs Per Day

Stage 5: 120 sows, 10 pigs per dayâ70 per week, 300 per month. This is a large-scale commercial pig farm. You need a highly efficient batch farrowing system. Divide sows into 24 groups of 5. Each group farrows every 6 weeks, so 4 groups farrow every week. That gives 160 piglets per week. You need 70 pigs per week for sale, so you have extra for breeding or expansion. Housing: modern buildings with automated feeding and watering systems. Farrowing crates, nursery pens with heated floors, finishing pens. Waste management: biogas plant or composting. Feed: 120 sows eat 300 kg per day. You need a large feed mill and storage silos. Mix feed using least-cost formulation software. Health: full-time veterinarian, strict biosecurity. Labor: 10-12 workers, including managers, veterinarians, and technicians. Record keeping: advanced software for production and financial tracking. Marketing: direct contracts with processors, supermarkets, and exporters. You may also process your own pork products. At this scale, you can influence local prices. Finances: you need substantial investment. Prepare a detailed business plan with cash flow projections. Consider partnerships or investor funding. Your goal is to reach 120 sows within 10 years. This stage requires dedication, but the profits are high. You can also train other farmers and become a hub for pig production in your region.

6. Choosing Your Stage

Choosing your stage depends on your resources, skills, and market. Start small and grow. Many farmers fail because they jump to a larger stage without mastering the basics. Stage 1 requires little capital: you can start with 5 sows using local materials. You need about $2,000 to $5,000. Stage 2 needs $10,000 to $20,000. Stage 3 needs $30,000 to $50,000. Stage 4 needs $100,000 to $200,000. Stage 5 needs over $500,000. But these are estimates; costs vary by country. Consider your market: can you sell 2 pigs per week? If you live near a city, demand is higher. If you are in a remote area, start with Stage 1 and find local buyers. Your skills: do you have experience with pigs? If not, start at Stage 1 and learn. Your feed supply: can you get cheap feed? If you can grow your own maize and soy, you can scale faster. Your labor: do you have family to help? At Stage 1, you can do it alone. At Stage 3, you need hired labor. Your goal: do you want a small income or a large business? Be realistic. Many successful farmers in Africa started with 2 sows and grew gradually. Use records to decide when to expand. When your sows are consistently producing 8-10 piglets and you have a steady market, move to the next stage. Don't expand too fast. Each stage requires more management. Join a pig farmers' association for support. Attend training. Visit other farms. The batch farrowing system works at any scale, but you must adjust the number of sows per group. For Stage 1, 2 sows per group; Stage 2, 5 sows per group; Stage 3, 5 sows per group; Stage 4, 5 sows per group; Stage 5, 5 sows per group. The key is consistency. Choose your stage based on your capacity and grow step by step.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: The Five Stages of a Batch Farrowing Pig Business

This Practical Reference lesson is your working document. It is designed to sit beside your notebook, your calculator, and your farm records. It does not repeat the theory of the five stages â it gives you the numbers, the benchmarks, the tables, and the checklists you need to choose a stage and run it. In African pig production, the difference between a profitable farm and a struggling one is rarely the breed or the feed brand. It is almost always the match between the farmer's chosen stage and the farmer's real capacity: land, capital, labour, water, feed supply, and market access.

The batch farrowing system is the engine behind every stage in this course. Instead of breeding sows continuously and having pigs of all ages mixed together, you group sows so that they farrow, wean, and finish in batches. This gives you predictable weekly or daily sales, better disease control, easier feed planning, and stronger bargaining power with buyers. Each stage below is defined by two numbers: the number of productive sows and the number of pigs you aim to sell per week or per day. Those two numbers drive everything else â housing, feed, labour, and cash flow.

How the Five Stages Fit Together

Think of the five stages as a staircase, not a ladder. You do not have to climb every step. You choose the step that fits your current resources and your target market. Stage 1 is a side business that can run alongside crops or a job. Stage 5 is a commercial operation that needs full-time management, a reliable feed supply chain, and formal market contracts. The most common mistake in African pig production is jumping from Stage 1 to Stage 3 or 4 without the feed, water, and housing to support it. That is how farms collapse.

â  SAFETY WARNING â Biosecurity and Feed Risk

African Swine Fever (ASF) has no vaccine and no cure. It kills up to 100% of infected pigs. Never introduce new pigs into your herd without a 30-day quarantine period in a separate pen. Never feed kitchen waste or swill unless it has been boiled at 90Â°C for at least 30 minutes. Never allow visitors, buyers, or other farmers into your pens without clean boots and clothing. ASF outbreaks in East and Southern Africa have wiped out entire farms overnight. Biosecurity is not optional at any stage â but at Stage 3 and above, one outbreak can destroy years of investment.

Choosing Your Stage: A Business-First Decision

Do not choose a stage because it sounds impressive. Choose it because the numbers work. Work through these questions honestly:

1. Market first. Who is buying? A Stage 1 farm can sell 2 pigs a week to neighbours, local butcheries, and roadside pork joints. A Stage 3 farm needs a reliable buyer for 2 pigs a day â that is a restaurant, a hotel chain, a processing plant, or a municipal market. If you do not have that buyer, do not build that stage.

2. Feed second. Can you secure feed at a stable price for at least six months? If you rely on buying from a mill that runs out of stock, you are exposed. Many successful African pig farmers at Stage 3 and above grow or contract part of their own maize and soya, or join a feed cooperative.

3. Water third. A sow needs 10â20 litres of clean water per day; a growing pig needs 5â10 litres. At Stage 3, that is 300â600 litres per day. Do you have a borehole, a tank, or a reliable piped supply? Water shortages cause abortion, poor growth, and death.

4. Labour fourth. Stage 1 can be done before and after work. Stage 3 needs at least one full-time person who knows pigs. Stage 5 needs a manager, a stockman, and a feed and records person. If you cannot pay and supervise that labour, stay smaller.

5. Capital fifth. Estimate your total setup cost â housing, water, feed store, breeding stock, and three months of working capital. If you cannot fund it from savings, grants, or a loan you can repay from pig sales, reduce your stage.

6. Records sixth. You cannot manage a stage you cannot measure. Keep a simple record of breeding dates, farrowing dates, pigs born, pigs weaned, pigs sold, feed used, and money in and out. At Stage 1 this can be a notebook. At Stage 3 and above, use a simple spreadsheet or a farm record book.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Market confirmed: I have a named buyer or buyers for my target weekly or daily sales before I expand.
  • Feed secured: I have a reliable feed source and at least one month of feed in store at all times.
  • Water secured: I have enough clean water every day for the stage I am running, even in the dry season.
  • Housing ready: Pens are clean, dry, well-ventilated, and separated by age group before new pigs arrive.
  • Biosecurity in place: Quarantine pen, footbath, restricted visitor policy, and no raw swill feeding.
  • Records kept: Breeding, farrowing, mortality, feed, and sales records are updated weekly.
  • Cash reserve: I have at least three months of operating costs available before I move to the next stage.
  • Labour available: I have trained, reliable labour for the daily routine, including weekends and holidays.
