Lessons

1. Selecting Breeding Stock

To start breeding rabbits, you need healthy, productive parents. Choose does (females) that are at least 6 months old and bucks (males) at least 7 months. Look for animals with bright eyes, clean noses, and no drooling. Check their teeththey should be aligned, not overgrown. Feel their body: firm, not fat or thin. A good doe has a wide, well-developed belly and at least 8 teats. A good buck has two large, firm testicles that descend by 5 months. Avoid breeding from rabbits that have had repeated litters of fewer than 4 kits, or that have kindled (given birth) with difficulty. In Africa, many farmers use New Zealand White, California White, or local crosses. These breeds adapt well to heat and are good mothers. If you buy from a market, quarantine new rabbits for 30 days before mixing with your herd. Feed them well during quarantine: 100g of pellets per day plus fresh greens like sweet potato vines or amaranth. Watch for signs of illness like diarrhoea or coughing. Only select from rabbits that have never been sick. Record the parents performance: number of kits born, number weaned, and mothering ability. A doe that weans 6 or more kits per litter is excellent. A buck that mates quickly and successfully with at least 3 does is a keeper. Never breed brother to sister or father to daughterthis causes weak kits and deformities. Instead, exchange bucks with a neighbour every 1218 months to bring in new blood. Finally, cull (remove) any rabbit that has produced two consecutive litters with stillborn kits or that refuses to mate. Good selection is the foundation of a profitable rabbitry.

2. Mating and Gestation Management

Rabbits are ready to mate when the doe is in heather vulva becomes pink, swollen, and moist. This happens every 1416 days, but in warm African climates, does may be ready year-round. Always take the doe to the bucks cage, never the other way around. The bucks territory makes him more confident. Mating takes seconds: the buck mounts, falls off, and grunts. After that, he may fall overthis is normal. Leave the doe for another mating 68 hours later to increase litter size. One buck can serve 810 does. Do not mate rabbits in the hottest part of the day; early morning or evening is best. After mating, put the doe back in her own cage. Gestation lasts 2832 days, average 31. Mark the mating date on a card on the cage. During pregnancy, feed the doe 120150g of pellets daily plus unlimited hay and greens. Provide a nest box on day 27a wooden box 40cm x 30cm x 30cm with soft bedding like dried grass or shredded paper. Keep the cage clean and quiet. Handle the doe gently; stress can cause abortion. Check for pregnancy signs: by day 14, you can feel grape-sized embryos by gently palpating her bellybut only if you are experienced, otherwise you may injure the kits. A simple method is to watch for nesting behaviour: the doe pulls fur from her belly and carries straw in her mouth. If she does not kindle by day 34, consult a vet or experienced farmer. In Africa, many farmers use a mating chart to track dates. If a doe fails to conceive after two matings, give her a rest for 2 weeks and try again. Keep records: date mated, buck used, expected kindling date. This prevents inbreeding and missed pregnancies.

3. Kindling and Newborn Kit Care

Kindling (giving birth) usually happens at night or early morning and takes 1530 minutes. The doe will pull fur from her belly and chest to line the nest. Do not disturb her. Stay away but check quietly from a distance. If she kindles during the day, leave her alone. After kindling, count the kitsbut only after the doe has left the nest to eat or drink. Wear clean hands or use a small spoon to move kits. Newborn kits are blind, deaf, and hairless. They weigh 3080g. A healthy litter has 412 kits. If the doe has more than 10 kits, you may need to foster some to another doe that kindled within 3 days. To foster, remove the foster doe from her cage, rub the new kits with her bedding, and place them in her nest. Watch herif she accepts them, she will nurse them. If she rejects them, return them to the original mother. Keep the nest box dry and warm. In cold weather, add extra bedding. In hot weather, ensure ventilation but no draughts. The doe nurses once or twice a day, usually at night, for 35 minutes. You may not see itthats normal. Check kits daily for full bellies. If their bellies are wrinkled, they are not getting milk. A doe with 8 kits needs 180200g of pellets daily plus unlimited water. Give her fresh greens like carrot tops, kale, or sweet potato leaves. If a doe dies or rejects her litter, you can hand-feed kits with goat milk using a syringe (without needle) every 23 hours for the first week, then every 4 hours. Keep the kits warma hot water bottle wrapped in a towel works. Kits open their eyes at 1012 days and start eating solid food at 3 weeks. By 4 weeks, they will nibble pellets and hay. Always handle kits gently and minimally for the first two weeks. Stress can cause the mother to cannibalise or abandon them.

4. Weaning and Raising Young Rabbits

Weaning means separating kits from their mother. Do this at 46 weeks old, when kits weigh 500700g and are eating solid food well. In Africa, many farmers wean at 5 weeks to give the doe a rest and rebreed her sooner. But weaning too early (before 4 weeks) causes stress and death. Before weaning, ensure kits are eating the same pellets and hay as the mother. Provide a shallow dish of pellets and a water bottle with a sipper. At weaning, move kits to a clean, dry cage in groups of 46 of the same size. Do not mix littersfighting and disease spread. Keep the cage away from drafts and direct sun. For the first week after weaning, feed the same food they had with the mother. Change feed gradually over 7 days. A common mistake is overfeedingthis causes diarrhoea. Give 3050g of pellets per kit per day, split into two meals. Add fresh greens like blackjack, amaranth, or sweet potato leaves, but introduce one at a time. Clean water must be available 24/7. Weigh kits weekly. Healthy kits gain 3040g per day. At 8 weeks, they should weigh 1.21.5kg. Separate males from females at 1012 weeks to prevent unwanted mating. Males can be sold for meat at 2.53kg (1216 weeks). Keep the best females as future breeders. Watch for signs of illness: dull coat, droopy ears, loss of appetite, or diarrhoea. Isolate sick kits immediately. Coccidiosis is common after weaningtreat with amprolium in water for 5 days. Provide a mineral lick and keep bedding dry. If you plan to sell, market at 3kg for best price. Record weaning weights and dates. This helps you identify fast-growing lines. Good weaning management reduces mortality from 30% to under 10%.

5. Record Keeping for Breeding

Record keeping is not paperwork for its own sakeit is your memory and your profit guide. Without records, you cannot know which doe produces the most kits, which buck is infertile, or when to expect kindling. Use a simple notebook or a wall chart. Each cage gets a card. On the card write: rabbit ID (e.g., DOE-01), date of birth, parent IDs, and date acquired. For breeding, record: date mated, buck ID, expected kindling date (31 days later), actual kindling date, number born alive, number born dead, number weaned. Also note any problems: difficult birth, mastitis, cannibalism. For each kit, record: birth weight (optional), weaning weight, and any illness. At the end of each year, calculate for each doe: total kits born, total weaned, and average weaning weight. A good doe weans 2030 kits per year. A poor doe weans fewer than 10cull her. For bucks, record: number of does mated, number of successful pregnancies, and litter sizes. A buck that fails to impregnate 3 does in a row should be culled. In Africa, many farmers use a simple colour code: green for good, yellow for watch, red for cull. Put a coloured tag on the cage. Keep a monthly summary: total does bred, total kits born, total deaths, total sold. This helps you plan feed purchases and sales. If you have a smartphone, use a free app like Rabbit Manager or Farm Records. But paper works fine if kept dry and safe. Review records every month. Look for patterns: are more kits born in certain months? Do some does have small litters? Adjust feeding or culling. Records also help when selling breeding stockbuyers pay more for proven parents. Finally, keep health records: dates of deworming, vaccinations (if any), and treatments. This prevents drug residues in meat. Good records turn a hobby into a business.

6. Common Reproductive Problems and Solutions

Reproductive problems cost money. The most common is failure to conceive. Causes: doe too fat or too thin, heat stress, or buck infertility. Solution: feed a balanced diet (1618% protein pellets), provide shade and ventilation, and check the bucks testicles for size and firmness. If a doe fails after two matings, give her 2 weeks rest, then try a different buck. Another problem is kindling difficulties (dystocia). The doe strains for over 30 minutes with no kits. This happens if kits are too large or positioned wrong. Gently massage her belly, or if trained, assist by pulling kits with clean fingers. If she still fails, call a vet. Cannibalismthe doe eats her kitsis often due to stress, lack of water, or a noisy environment. Provide a quiet nest box, plenty of water, and do not handle newborns for the first 3 days. Mastitis (hot, swollen teats) is caused by bacteria. Treat with antibiotics prescribed by a vet, and apply warm compresses. Kits may need fostering. Stillbirths are common if the doe is too fat or has a long kindling. Reduce feed for obese does before mating. Infertility in bucks: heat stress above 30C can cause temporary sterility for 46 weeks. Keep bucks cool with fans or wet sacks. If a buck is permanently infertile, cull him. Pseudopregnancy: the doe acts pregnant but kindles nothing. This happens after a sterile mating. Give her 18 days rest, then rebreed. Retained kits: if a doe kindles some kits but stops, a kit may be stuck. Seek help. Abscesses on the reproductive tract: usually from fighting or poor hygiene. Clean with iodine and give antibiotics. To prevent problems, keep cages clean, provide a balanced diet, avoid overcrowding, and maintain a breeding schedule. Do not breed does under 6 months or over 3 years. Replace breeding stock every 2 years. Keep records to spot problems early. If a doe has three consecutive failed pregnancies, cull her. A healthy rabbitry has a kindling rate above 80% and a weaning rate above 70%.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Breeding and Reproduction in Rabbit Farming

In African rabbit farming, reproduction is the engine of the business. A doe that kindles eight healthy kits every 6070 days can produce 4050 marketable rabbits per year. A doe that kindles four kits and loses half before weaning produces less than 10. The difference is not luck it is management. This practical reference consolidates the key technical decisions, benchmarks, and record-keeping habits that separate profitable rabbitries from subsistence-level operations across Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana, Tanzania, Uganda, and South Africa.

Whether you are a smallholder with five does or a semi-commercial unit with fifty, the principles are the same: select healthy stock, manage mating intervals, prepare nests, monitor kindling, wean on schedule, and record everything. This module gives you the reference data, diagrams, and checklists to do exactly that.

Reproductive Anatomy and the Breeding Cycle

Rabbits are induced ovulators the doe releases eggs only after mating stimulation. This means a single mating can result in pregnancy, but the timing of mating relative to the doe's receptivity is critical. A doe in heat (vulva pink to red and swollen) will accept the buck readily; a doe not in heat (vulva pale or white) will refuse or fight. Understanding this cycle allows you to plan matings rather than guess.

Reproduction is nutritionally expensive. A lactating doe producing 8 kits can drink 23 litres of water per day and consume 200300 g of feed. Underfeeding during gestation and lactation is the single biggest cause of small litters, weak kits, and high pre-weaning mortality in African rabbitries.

Selecting Breeding Stock

Selection is the foundation of every successful rabbitry. A doe with poor mothering instincts will lose kits regardless of how well you feed her. A buck with low libido or poor semen quality will waste your time and feed. Select on these criteria:

  • Does: Choose from litters of 8+ kits. Look for calm temperament, good body condition (not fat, not thin), 45 pairs of functional nipples, and a history of successful kindling in their dam.
  • Bucks: Choose from litters of 6+ kits. Check for two descended testicles, good body condition, and active mating behaviour. Avoid bucks from litters with a history of kindling problems.
  • Age at first mating: Does 56 months (medium breeds) or 68 months (large breeds). Bucks 67 months. Never mate underweight animals they will produce weak litters.
  • Record review: Never select breeding stock without reviewing the dam's production records. A doe that consistently kindles 8+ kits and weans 7+ is worth ten does that kindle 4 and wean 2.

Mating and Gestation Management

Always take the doe to the buck's cage. The doe is territorial in her own cage and may attack the buck. Mating should occur within 510 minutes; if the buck mounts and falls off, mating is usually successful. Allow 23 services in one session for higher conception rates.

Pregnancy diagnosis can be done by palpation at day 1214. Feel for marble-sized embryos in the lower abdomen. If the doe is not pregnant, re-mate after 7 days. False pregnancy (pseudopregnancy) lasts 1618 days a doe that refuses the buck and nests but does not kindle is likely pseudopregnant.

Kindling and Newborn Kit Care

Prepare a nest box 35 days before kindling (day 2528 of gestation). Provide clean straw or wood shavings. Kindling usually occurs at night and lasts 1530 minutes. Do not disturb the doe during kindling. After kindling, check the nest once remove dead kits, count live kits, and ensure the doe has pulled fur to keep kits warm.

Kits are born blind, deaf, and hairless. They depend entirely on the doe's milk for the first 3 weeks. A doe nurses only once or twice per day (usually early morning and late evening). If kits are scattered or cold, warm them slowly and return them to the nest. Fostering kits to another doe that kindled within 23 days is possible if you mask the scent by rubbing the kits with the foster doe's fur.

SAFETY WARNING: Handling Pregnant and Lactating Does

Pregnant and lactating does are highly sensitive to stress. Never handle a doe roughly, chase her, or expose her to extreme heat or cold during gestation and lactation. Stress can cause abortion, cannibalism of kits, or abandonment. Always approach quietly, support the doe's belly and hindquarters, and never lift a pregnant doe by the ears. In hot African climates, ensure shade and ventilation heat stress above 30C can cause pregnancy loss and reduced milk production. If a doe kindles on the wire floor instead of the nest box, move kits to the nest box immediately and warm them. If a doe cannibalises her kits, it is usually due to stress, inadequate water, or nutritional deficiency address the cause before re-breeding her.

Weaning and Raising Young Rabbits

Wean kits at 46 weeks (2842 days). Weaning weight should be 500600 g. Early weaning (before 4 weeks) increases mortality; late weaning (after 8 weeks) stresses the doe and delays re-breeding. At weaning, move kits to a grow-out cage in groups of 46 per cage. Separate males from females at 810 weeks to prevent unwanted pregnancies.

Provide a creep feeder with a high-protein (1718%) starter feed from 3 weeks onward. Kits will nibble on solid food from 23 weeks but rely on milk until 4 weeks. After weaning, transition gradually to a grower ration (1617% protein) over 710 days.

Record Keeping for Breeding

You cannot manage what you do not measure. Every breeding rabbitry should maintain the following records:

  • Doe cards: Doe ID, date mated, buck used, date kindled, kits born alive, kits born dead, kits weaned, weaning weight, date re-mated.
  • Buck cards: Buck ID, date of birth, number of does mated, conception rate, date retired.
  • Nest box records: Date nest box provided, date kindling, litter size, kit mortality, date weaned.
  • Feed records: Daily feed consumption per cage, feed cost, feed conversion ratio.
  • Health records: Vaccinations, treatments, mortality events, culling decisions.

Use a simple notebook or a spreadsheet. In Kenya, KALRO provides free rabbit record templates. In Nigeria, the Federal Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Development has extension materials with record-keeping formats. The key is consistency record every mating, every kindling, every death.

Common Reproductive Problems and Solutions

  • Doe not conceiving: Check body condition (too fat or too thin), check for heat signs, re-mate after 7 days, check buck fertility.
  • Small litters: Increase feed quantity and protein before mating, ensure doe is not over-mated, check buck age.
  • Abortion: Usually caused by stress, heat, or disease. Remove the cause, rest the doe for 30 days, re-mate.
  • Cannibalism: Caused by stress, inadequate water, or nutritional deficiency. Provide plenty of clean water, reduce disturbances, supplement with minerals.
  • Mastitis: Swollen, hot, painful mammary glands. Cull affected does treatment is rarely economic.
  • Pseudopregnancy: Doe nests but does not kindle. Re-mate after 18 days. Do not cull unless repeated.
  • Retained kits: Doe kindles some kits but retains others. Consult a veterinarian; manual removal may be necessary.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Select breeding stock from litters of 6+ kits with calm temperament and good body condition.
  • Mate does at 56 months (medium breeds) or 68 months (large breeds); bucks at 67 months.
  • Always take the doe to the buck's cage; allow 23 services per mating session.
  • Palpate for pregnancy at day 1214; re-mate non-pregnant does after 7 days.
  • Provide a clean nest box with straw 35 days before kindling (day 2528).
  • Check nest once after kindling; remove dead kits, count live kits, ensure fur cover.
  • Wean kits at 46 weeks (500600 g); separate males and females at 810 weeks.
  • Record every mating, kindling, weaning, and mortality event no exceptions.
