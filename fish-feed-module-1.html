Lesson 1.1~11 min

Fish Digestive System and Metabolism

In this lesson
  • Explain how a fish's cold-blooded metabolism changes feed intake as water temperature changes
  • Describe why fish excrete most surplus protein as ammonia rather than urea, and what that costs you
  • Match gut type and mouth size to feed form for tilapia and African catfish

A fish is not a small pig in water. Two facts about its body change every feeding decision you will make on your farm.

The first is that a fish is cold-blooded. Its body temperature is the water temperature, so its whole metabolism speeds up and slows down with the pond. Nile tilapia grow well at 25-30C and tolerate 15-35C. African catfish are optimal at 20-28C and tolerate a remarkable 8-42C, which is why they survive in shallow, warm, poorly oxygenated ponds where tilapia struggle. Common carp sit at 18-28C. Below about 20C tilapia feed conversion collapses from roughly 1.6-2.0 in warm water to 3.0-5.0, and appetite falls to almost nothing. Feed given to a cold fish is largely feed wasted. Every 1C below optimal costs roughly 3-5 percent in feed conversion efficiency.

The second is that a fish lives in the water it excretes into. Only about 28-34 percent of the protein you feed is retained as fish flesh. The other 66-72 percent is stripped of its nitrogen and that nitrogen leaves as ammonia across the gills. Land animals spend energy converting nitrogen to urea because they must store and pass it; a fish simply dumps it into the pond, which is cheap for the fish and expensive for you. A 100 kg standing crop of tilapia puts out roughly 50-80 g of nitrogen a day. That is the reason overfeeding protein is not just a waste of money but an active poisoning of your own water.

Now the gut itself. Tilapia are omnivores with a long, coiled intestine, often six or more times body length, a very acidic stomach that helps break algae cells, and no true pancreas as a separate organ. That long gut is why tilapia handle plant material and starch far better than catfish. African catfish are omnivorous but lean carnivorous, with a shorter gut and a real stomach that handles animal protein well and starch poorly. Carp have no stomach at all, so they need small frequent meals and cannot handle a large acid-dependent protein load in one sitting.

None of this works if the feed does not fit the mouth. The rule of thumb is that pellet diameter should be about 60 percent of mouth width. Fry under two weeks have a mouth under 1 mm and need 0.8-1.5 mm particles. Early fingerlings at two to eight weeks take 1.5-3 mm crumble. Fingerlings at eight to twelve weeks take 2-4 mm. Juveniles take 3-6 mm and grow-out fish 4-8 mm, with 6-8 mm preferred for tilapia and 5-6 mm for catfish. Feed a pellet that is too large and intake drops and the fish stresses; feed one that is too small and you lose feed to sorting and to the water.

Worked example. Yaa runs a 0.15 ha pond outside Kumasi, Ghana with about 1,200 tilapia averaging 120 g, so roughly 144 kg of fish. At 28C at medium density the reference feeding rate is 3-4 percent of body weight per day. Take 3.5 percent: 144 x 0.035 = 5.04 kg of feed a day. Her feed costs USD 0.70/kg, so USD 3.53 a day. In August a cold spell drops the pond to 22C for a week. At 20-25C the rate falls to 2-3 percent. Feeding 2.5 percent instead gives 3.6 kg a day, USD 2.52. If she had kept feeding at the warm-water rate she would have spent an extra USD 1.01 a day, roughly USD 7 over the week, on feed that mostly rotted and raised her ammonia. That is the whole of this lesson in one number.

Module 9 covers pond and tank design and how to measure water. Here the point is narrower: read the thermometer before you weigh the feed.

Protein and Amino Acid Requirements

In this lesson
  • State the crude protein target for your species at each life stage
  • Identify lysine and methionine as the limiting amino acids in plant-based fish feed
  • Calculate the cost of correcting an amino acid deficiency with synthetic supplements

Protein is the most expensive thing in your feed bag and the single easiest thing to get wrong in both directions. Too little and the fish stops growing. Too much and you are paying premium prices to manufacture ammonia.

Start with crude protein by stage, expressed as percent of dry matter. Nile tilapia need 50-56 percent as fry and larvae, 40-45 percent as fingerlings, 30-40 percent as juveniles, 25-32 percent at grow-out and 30-40 percent for broodstock. African catfish need 45-50 percent as fry, 40-45 percent as fingerlings, 35-40 percent as juveniles, 28-35 percent at grow-out and 35-40 percent for broodstock. Common carp need 45-50 percent as fry, 35-42 percent as fingerlings, 30-36 percent as juveniles and 25-30 percent at grow-out. Channel catfish, the American species most of the published research uses, sit at 45-50 percent fry down to 25-28 percent grow-out.

The pattern is the same for all four: protein requirement falls steeply as the fish grows. A farmer who feeds one 35 percent protein pellet from fingerling to harvest is underfeeding protein at the start and overfeeding it at the end.

But total protein is only half the story. What the fish actually needs is ten indispensable amino acids: threonine, valine, leucine, isoleucine, methionine, tryptophan, lysine, histidine, arginine and phenylalanine. Growth stops at whichever one runs out first, no matter how much crude protein is on the label. Tilapia fry need 1.8-2.0 percent lysine in the dry diet, dropping to 1.2-1.5 percent at grow-out; African catfish need 1.6-1.8 percent. Methionine plus cystine runs 0.9-1.0 percent for tilapia fry and 0.8-0.9 percent for African catfish. One striking difference from land animals: fish need two to three times more arginine per kg of body weight than mammals do, 1.5-1.7 percent for tilapia fry.

The practical problem is that the cheap protein sources are exactly the ones short of these amino acids. Fish meal at 60-65 percent protein carries 2.2-2.4 percent lysine and 0.9-1.1 percent methionine and digests at 90-95 percent, but costs USD 1.50-3.00/kg. Soybean meal at 44-48 percent protein carries 1.6-1.8 percent lysine and only 0.4-0.5 percent methionine at 75-85 percent digestibility, but costs USD 0.30-0.50/kg. Methionine is the first thing to run out in any soy-heavy African feed.

Worked example. Joseph mixes his own tilapia grow-out feed near Mwanza, Tanzania, using mostly soybean meal, maize and a little local fish by-product. He is targeting a 30 percent protein feed. On a soy-dominated mix his methionine lands near 0.45 percent while the ideal-protein pattern for his fish wants closer to 0.65-0.75 percent total methionine plus cystine. He adds DL-methionine at 0.2 percent of the mix. DL-methionine costs USD 3-6/kg; take USD 5. Two kg of DL-methionine per tonne of feed costs 2 x 5 = USD 10 per tonne, which is USD 0.01/kg of feed. The reference puts amino acid supplementation at USD 0.02-0.05/kg with a return of 2 to 5 to 1, through 5-10 percent better growth and an FCR improvement of 0.05-0.10. On a 2-tonne cycle that USD 20 of methionine buys him roughly 100 kg of extra feed-equivalent efficiency. It is the cheapest improvement available to a small mixer.

A second discipline: protein must be read against energy. If digestible energy rises from 3,000 to 3,500 kcal/kg, the amino acid percentages should fall by about 10 percent, because the fish eats less feed to meet its energy needs. Lesson 4 of Module 12 in the earlier part of this course deals with pricing feed; here the point is that a protein number without an energy number tells you nothing.

Deficiency signs are worth memorising. Lysine short: fin erosion, gill hyperplasia, slow growth. Methionine short: cataracts and loss of colour. Tryptophan short: bent spines, scoliosis and lordosis. Arginine short: poor growth and weak immune response. Threonine short: anaemia and gut damage.

Lipid Requirements and Fatty Acids

In this lesson
  • Set total lipid percentage by species and life stage
  • Explain why freshwater fish can partly make EPA and DHA from cheaper vegetable oils
  • Compare fish oil and vegetable oil on cost per kg of essential fatty acid delivered

Oil does two jobs in fish feed. It supplies concentrated energy, which spares protein from being burned for fuel, and it supplies essential fatty acids the fish cannot make from scratch. Get it right and your protein goes further; get it wrong and you either build fatty fish or you get weak, disease-prone stock.

Targets first, as percent of the dry diet. Tilapia fry and fingerlings need 8-12 percent total lipid with 0.8-1.5 percent EPA plus DHA. Tilapia grow-out drops to 5-8 percent lipid with only 0.3-0.8 percent EPA plus DHA. African catfish sit at 7-10 percent lipid with 0.5-1.0 percent EPA plus DHA. Common carp are the leanest, 4-6 percent lipid and 0.3-0.5 percent EPA plus DHA. Broodstock of every species jump to 12-18 percent lipid and 1.0-2.0 percent EPA plus DHA, which is covered in Module 11 where broodstock management sits.

The omega-3 to omega-6 ratio also matters: roughly 1:1 to 1:2 for tilapia fry, widening to 1:2 to 1:5 at grow-out. Grow-out fish are forgiving. Fry and broodstock are not.

Here is the fact that saves African farmers real money. Freshwater fish, unlike marine species, can convert dietary alpha-linolenic acid into EPA and DHA themselves. Conversion is inefficient, roughly 5-15 percent for EPA and only 2-5 percent for DHA, so you still need about 0.5-1.0 percent alpha-linolenic acid in the diet. But it means tilapia, catfish and carp do not need expensive marine oil at grow-out. Linseed oil is about 55 percent alpha-linolenic acid, rapeseed about 10 percent and soybean oil about 7 percent.

Now the money. Fish oil costs USD 2.00-3.50/kg and carries 15-25 percent EPA plus DHA. Vegetable oil costs USD 0.50-1.00/kg and carries none, though it supplies the alpha-linolenic acid the fish converts. Fish oil digests at over 95 percent in tilapia, vegetable oils at 88-92 percent, so final absorption is broadly similar. The reference recommends fish oil for fry and broodstock only, vegetable oil for grow-out, and for grow-out a blend of 3-5 percent fish oil with 3-5 percent vegetable oil as the best cost-performance compromise.

Worked example. Grace formulates catfish grow-out feed near Abeokuta, Nigeria and needs 8 percent total lipid in a 1,000 kg batch, which is 80 kg of oil. Option one, all fish oil at USD 2.75/kg: 80 x 2.75 = USD 220, adding USD 0.22 to every kg of feed. Option two, all vegetable oil at USD 0.75/kg: 80 x 0.75 = USD 60, or USD 0.06/kg. Option three, the recommended blend, 4 percent fish oil (40 kg at USD 2.75 = USD 110) plus 4 percent vegetable oil (40 kg at USD 0.75 = USD 30) = USD 140, or USD 0.14/kg. Moving from all fish oil to the blend saves USD 80 per tonne while still delivering roughly 7-12 percent EPA plus DHA in the oil fraction, comfortably above the 0.5-1.0 percent of diet African catfish need. Moving all the way to vegetable oil saves USD 160 per tonne and is defensible at grow-out but not for the hatchery.

One warning. Oil goes rancid, and rancid oil destroys vitamins and damages fish. Fish oil oxidises fastest and needs antioxidant protection, which in practice means vitamin E. Fish fed more than 10 percent lipid or more than 1 percent EPA plus DHA should get elevated vitamin E, 200-400 mg/kg for high-lipid broodstock feeds against 100-150 mg/kg for standard feeds, at a cost of about USD 0.02-0.10/kg. Rice bran, cheap and widely used across East Africa at USD 0.05-0.15/kg, is 15-18 percent lipid and oxidises fast: use it fresh within two to three weeks, or buy heat-stabilised bran which lasts two to three months for an extra USD 0.02-0.05/kg.

Carbohydrate Utilization in Fish

In this lesson
  • Set maximum starch and fibre inclusion by feeding type for tilapia, catfish and carp
  • Explain how gelatinisation changes starch digestibility and pellet stability
  • Cost the saving from replacing protein with cheap local starch without losing growth

Carbohydrate is the cheapest energy you can buy and the most misused ingredient in farm-made fish feed. Maize, cassava, rice bran and wheat cost USD 0.20-0.40/kg against USD 1.50-3.00/kg for soybean meal or fish meal. Used correctly, starch spares protein and binds the pellet. Used carelessly, it passes straight through the fish and fouls the pond.

How much a fish can handle depends on what it evolved to eat. Carnivorous fish, including catfish fry, take under 10 percent crude fibre and under 15 percent soluble carbohydrate; they digest gelatinised starch at only 50-70 percent and raw starch at 20-40 percent. Omnivorous fish, including tilapia fingerlings, take 10-15 percent fibre and 20-30 percent soluble carbohydrate, digesting gelatinised starch at 70-85 percent and raw starch at 40-60 percent. Herbivorous fish, including carp juveniles, take 15-20 percent fibre and 30-40 percent soluble carbohydrate, digesting gelatinised starch at 85-95 percent.

The single most important word there is gelatinised. Raw maize starch is only 30-40 percent digestible to a fish. Steam-extruded maize is 75-85 percent digestible. Starch digestibility improves by 50-100 percent when the starch is cooked under heat and pressure. That is the same physical change that makes a pellet hold together in water. A farmer grinding raw maize into a cold-pressed pellet is throwing away half the energy he paid for and producing a pellet that falls apart.

Water stability follows directly. Fish meal with plain wheat flour holds 2-4 hours and loses 20-30 percent of its protein and energy to leaching within two hours. Fish meal with gelatinised corn starch holds 4-8 hours and loses only 10-15 percent, for about 5 percent extra cost. Adding 1-2 percent soy lecithin gives 6-12 hours and 5-10 percent loss for 10-15 percent extra cost. A proper binder such as alginate, CMC or bentonite gives 8-24 hours and under 5 percent loss for 5-10 percent extra. Commercial aquaculture feed targets more than 8 hours of stability. In a hand-fed pond, stability under 4 hours means 20-30 percent of nutrients leach out before the fish eat, and with 10-15 percent uneaten feed on top you are losing 30-45 percent of what you bought.

There is also a limit set by the fish's blood chemistry. Carnivorous fish have poor gluconeogenic control and their blood glucose can run 150-400 mg/dL after a meal against 80-120 for a mammal. In practice keep dietary glucose below 200 g/kg for catfish fingerlings and below 300 g/kg for tilapia and carp.

Worked example. Samuel mixes tilapia feed near Jinja, Uganda. His current 32 percent protein mix relies heavily on soybean meal at USD 0.40/kg. He decides to lift maize from 15 to 22 percent and drop soybean meal by 7 percentage points, holding protein at 30 percent, which is still inside the 25-32 percent grow-out band. Per tonne he now buys 70 kg less soybean meal, saving 70 x 0.40 = USD 28, and 70 kg more maize at USD 0.22/kg, costing USD 15.40. Net saving USD 12.60 per tonne, or USD 0.0126/kg. That is real but modest, and it only holds if he cooks the starch. If he pellets cold and the maize stays raw, digestibility falls from about 75 percent to about 35 percent, he loses roughly 10-20 percent on FCR, and at FCR 1.8 rising to 2.0 on a 1,000 kg feed batch he loses about 55 kg of fish worth roughly USD 1.50/kg at farm gate, or USD 83. The saving is wiped out four times over. Cheap starch only pays if it is cooked.

A small pellet mill of 2-3 kW costs USD 500-1,500 and pays back in six to twelve months on a farm mixing its own feed. Steam or par-boiling the grain before mixing is the poor farmer's version of the same trick.

Vitamin and Mineral Requirements

In this lesson
  • Identify vitamin C and phosphorus as the two nutrients most often deficient in farm-made fish feed
  • Explain how phytate locks up phosphorus and how phytase or fermentation releases it
  • Budget a vitamin and mineral premix into your feed cost per kg

Vitamins and minerals are a tiny share of feed weight and a large share of whether the feed works. Two of them deserve most of your attention: vitamin C and phosphorus.

Vitamin C first. Fish need 100-200 mg/kg of feed, and broodstock 200-300 mg/kg. Fish store none of it, so a gap shows up fast as poor growth, haemorrhages and rising infection rates. The problem is that plain ascorbic acid loses 50-90 percent of its potency in two to four weeks at tropical ambient temperature. If you buy a bag of feed made two months ago and stored in a hot store, the vitamin C on the label may be almost gone. Stabilised forms solve this: ascorbyl phosphate retains 70-95 percent at six months for 20-30 percent more cost, ascorbyl palmitate retains 60-90 percent for 30-40 percent more. The cost impact is only USD 0.02-0.05/kg of feed. Fish fed adequate vitamin C show around 50 percent fewer infections and broodstock fecundity 10-20 percent higher. If you cannot buy stabilised vitamin C, buy feed in smaller lots and use it within eight weeks.

Vitamin D3 is the second fish-specific surprise. Unlike land animals, fish cannot make vitamin D from sunlight; there is no functional pathway in the skin. Dietary D3 at 1,000-2,000 IU/kg is obligatory, and a shortage shows as soft skeletons in fry. Vitamin A runs 8,000-20,000 IU/kg, vitamin E 100-200 mg/kg, vitamin K 5-10 mg/kg, thiamine and riboflavin 10 mg/kg each, niacin 60-100 mg/kg, choline 1,000-2,000 mg/kg. Vitamin B12 needs only 0.01 mg/kg but is produced by bacteria and found mostly in fish meal and other animal protein, so a purely plant-based feed risks anaemia and poor growth unless supplemented, at a trivial USD 0.001-0.005/kg.

Now phosphorus, the mineral that decides your feed's real value. Tilapia need 0.6-0.8 percent, African catfish 0.6-0.7 percent, carp and channel catfish 0.5-0.7 percent. Calcium requirement is 0.8-1.2 percent for tilapia, with an optimal calcium to phosphorus ratio of 1.5-2.0 to 1, wider than in mammals because fish absorb calcium from the water. In hard water above 100 mg/L calcium the dietary calcium need is often already met. Phosphorus is not, because natural fresh water carries almost none.

The difficulty is phytate. Plant ingredients store phosphorus as phytic acid, which binds 60-80 percent of it and also ties up zinc, iron and copper, cutting their availability by 50-80 percent. Fish meal is 1.2-1.5 percent total phosphorus at about 85 percent available. Soybean meal is 0.6-0.7 percent total phosphorus at only about 30 percent available. Rice bran looks phosphorus-rich at 1.5-2.0 percent but delivers only 20-30 percent. A plant-based diet therefore usually needs 0.3-0.5 percent added inorganic phosphate.

There are three ways out, and two of them suit a small farm. Phytase enzyme at 500-1,000 FTU/kg costs USD 0.05-0.10/kg of feed, recovers about 30-40 percent of the bound phosphorus and cuts your supplemental phosphate need by 30-50 percent, with a return of 1.5 to 3 to 1. Fermenting plant ingredients with lactic acid bacteria for 24-72 hours cuts phytate by 30-50 percent and also improves copper and zinc availability, for USD 0.05-0.10/kg in labour and containers. Simply choosing lower-phytate ingredients is the third route.

Worked example. Tendai mixes catfish feed near Bulawayo, Zimbabwe. His tier-one premix costs are USD 0.03-0.05/kg for a mineral premix and the same again for a vitamin premix, so call it USD 0.08/kg for both, plus salt at 2-3 percent which costs almost nothing and also protects against nitrite toxicity. On a 1,000 kg batch that is USD 80. He then adds phytase at USD 0.07/kg, another USD 70, and drops supplemental dicalcium phosphate by 40 percent. His premix and enzyme bill is USD 150 per tonne, about USD 0.15/kg on a feed costing perhaps USD 0.45/kg to make. It looks heavy until he compares it with the alternative, which is a feed the fish cannot fully use.

One caution for intensive systems: copper above 0.1 mg/L in the water kills algae and zooplankton, and zinc above 0.3 mg/L impairs nitrification. Supplement to requirement, not beyond.

Water Quality and Nutrition Interaction

In this lesson
  • Calculate how much ammonia your daily feed ration puts into the pond
  • Apply written rules for cutting the ration when oxygen or ammonia move out of range
  • Choose a protein level that matches the water quality your system can actually maintain

Feed and water are one system. The feed you put in becomes the ammonia you must get out, and the water you have decides how much feed the fish can safely eat. Module 9 of this course covers pond and tank design, aeration equipment and how to take readings; this lesson is only about the link between the feed bag and the water chemistry.

Start with the arithmetic of ammonia. Around 66-72 percent of the protein you feed leaves the fish as nitrogen. A standing crop of 100 kg of tilapia eating a normal ration produces roughly 50-80 g of nitrogen a day. Channel catfish and carp on 25-28 percent protein produce 40-60 g a day per 100 kg. The safe unionised ammonia threshold is under 0.05 mg/L for Nile tilapia and African catfish, and under 0.1 mg/L for channel catfish and carp.

What those numbers mean in fish terms is worth learning by heart. At 0.02 mg/L ammonia you already lose 2-5 percent of growth through mild immune suppression. At 0.05 mg/L the gills are irritated and growth drops 10-15 percent. At 0.1 mg/L tilapia lose 25-30 percent of growth, stop eating properly and start showing fin erosion. Above 0.2 mg/L fish die within days. Ammonia at 0.1 mg/L also worsens FCR by 5-10 percent all by itself, so you pay twice: once in lost growth and once in wasted feed.

The cheapest lever is dietary protein. Every 5 percent reduction in dietary protein cuts ammonia output by 15-20 percent. That is why matching protein to life stage, as in Lesson 2, is a water-quality decision as much as a cost decision. The other levers are protein quality, since well-balanced amino acids mean less protein burned for energy, and digestibility, since a feed above 80 percent digestible leaves less nitrogen in the faeces and less uneaten pellet rotting on the bottom.

Oxygen works the other way round: it limits how much the fish can eat. Tilapia grow-out want more than 5 mg/L and become critical at 2.0 mg/L; below 5 mg/L feed intake falls and FCR worsens by 15-30 percent. Tilapia fry want more than 6 mg/L. Channel catfish grow-out want more than 5 mg/L, critical at 3.0 mg/L. African catfish, being air-breathers, survive down to 1.0 mg/L but still prefer more than 4 mg/L and grow poorly below it. Feeding itself consumes oxygen: fish fed to satiation use 30-40 percent more oxygen, and feeds above 40 percent protein raise post-feeding oxygen demand by 50-100 percent. In a low-aeration pond, feeding a heavy meal at dawn is asking the fish to burn oxygen at the hour the pond has least.

Nitrite is the third gauge. Safe operating levels are under 0.5 mg/L for tilapia and carp and under 1 mg/L for channel catfish. Salt at 1-2 g/L reduces nitrite toxicity by about half by competing for gill chloride channels, and costs almost nothing.

Write these adjustment rules on the feed store wall and follow them without arguing with yourself:

  1. If ammonia is above 0.1 mg/L or dissolved oxygen is below 5 mg/L, cut the ration 20-30 percent.
  2. If water temperature falls below 20C, cut the ration by half.
  3. If fish show disease signs, cut the ration 30-50 percent.
  4. In low-aeration ponds, cut the ration 20-30 percent at dawn and after sunset, and feed the full share at midday.

Worked example. Kwame farms tilapia in cages at Kpando on Lake Volta, Ghana, with about 400 kg of fish in one cage on a 30 percent protein feed at 3 percent of body weight, so 12 kg of feed a day at USD 0.72/kg, USD 8.64. His hand-held meter reads 4.2 mg/L dissolved oxygen at 06:30 after a still, cloudy night. Rule one applies: he cuts 25 percent, feeding 9 kg instead of 12, saving USD 2.16 that day and, more importantly, avoiding a 15-30 percent FCR penalty on the whole ration. Over a 180-day cycle, if that happens on 30 mornings, the cut costs him nothing in growth because the fish could not have used the feed anyway, and it saves 3 kg x 30 = 90 kg of feed, or USD 64.80.

Do not treat these cuts as losses. They are the difference between feeding fish and feeding bacteria.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. Why does a fish excrete surplus nitrogen as ammonia rather than urea?

Ammonia is cheap to excrete for an animal surrounded by water, which is why overfeeding protein directly loads your pond.

2. Approximately what share of dietary protein ends up as fish flesh?

Only 28-34 percent is retained; the rest is deaminated and excreted as ammonia.

3. Nile tilapia grow best at which water temperature?

Tilapia are optimal at 25-30C, tolerating 15-35C but growing rapidly only above 25C.

4. Correct pellet diameter is roughly what fraction of mouth width?

About 60 percent. Undersized pellets are lost to sorting; oversized pellets reduce intake and stress the fish.

5. Which species tolerates the widest temperature range and the poorest oxygen?

African catfish tolerate 8-42C and can air-breathe, surviving down to about 1 mg/L dissolved oxygen.

6. Crude protein for Nile tilapia at grow-out should be about:

Grow-out tilapia need 25-32 percent; 50-56 percent is the fry requirement.

7. Which amino acid is usually lowest in soybean meal?

Soybean meal carries only 0.4-0.5 percent methionine, the lowest of the common plant proteins.

8. How many indispensable amino acids do fish require?

Ten: threonine, valine, leucine, isoleucine, methionine, tryptophan, lysine, histidine, arginine and phenylalanine.

9. Compared with mammals, fish require how much arginine per kg body weight?

Arginine requirement in fish is 2-3 times that of mammals, a genuine species difference.

10. Adding DL-methionine at 0.2 percent of a tonne of feed, at USD 5/kg, costs:

0.2 percent of 1,000 kg is 2 kg; 2 x USD 5 = USD 10 per tonne, or USD 0.01/kg of feed.

11. Total lipid for tilapia grow-out feed should be about:

Grow-out tilapia need 5-8 percent; 12-18 percent is the broodstock level.

12. Why can freshwater fish use vegetable oil where marine fish cannot?

Freshwater species convert ALA to EPA at 5-15 percent and DHA at 2-5 percent, so preformed marine oil is less critical.

13. Which oil is richest in alpha-linolenic acid?

Linseed oil is roughly 55 percent ALA, far above rapeseed at 10 percent and soybean at 7 percent.

14. Filling 8 percent lipid in a 1,000 kg batch with vegetable oil at USD 0.75/kg costs:

8 percent of 1,000 kg is 80 kg; 80 x 0.75 = USD 60, or USD 0.06 per kg of feed.

15. Rice bran at 15-18 percent lipid should be used within:

Unstabilised rice bran goes rancid in two to three weeks; heat-stabilised bran lasts two to three months.

16. Gelatinising maize starch changes its digestibility to fish from about:

Cooking under heat and pressure roughly doubles starch digestibility, from 30-40 percent raw to 75-85 percent.

17. Maximum soluble carbohydrate for a catfish fry diet is about:

Carnivorous fish including catfish fry tolerate under 15 percent soluble carbohydrate and under 10 percent crude fibre.

18. A pellet that disintegrates in under 4 hours in a hand-fed pond loses roughly:

Stability under 4 hours means 20-30 percent nutrient leaching, before you add 10-15 percent uneaten feed.

19. Which binder gives the longest water stability?

Proper binders give 8-24 hours with under 5 percent leaching, for about 5-10 percent extra cost.

20. A small 2-3 kW pellet mill typically costs:

USD 500-1,500, with a six to twelve month payback on a farm that mixes its own feed.

21. Why can fish not rely on sunlight for vitamin D?

Fish have no functional 7-dehydrocholesterol pathway in the skin, so dietary D3 at 1,000-2,000 IU/kg is obligatory.

22. Unstabilised vitamin C in feed loses 50-90 percent of its potency within:

Two to four weeks at ambient tropical temperature, which is why stabilised ascorbyl phosphate or small frequent purchases matter.

23. Approximately what proportion of soybean meal phosphorus is available to fish?

Phytate binds most of it; soybean meal delivers only about 30 percent of its 0.6-0.7 percent total phosphorus.

24. Phytase at 500-1,000 FTU/kg recovers roughly how much bound phosphorus?

About 30-40 percent, which cuts the supplemental inorganic phosphate requirement by 30-50 percent.

25. Copper in pond water becomes dangerous to algae and zooplankton above:

Above 0.1 mg/L copper kills algae and zooplankton, which can crash pond oxygen.

26. Safe unionised ammonia for Nile tilapia is:

Under 0.05 mg/L. At 0.1 mg/L tilapia lose 25-30 percent of growth and stop feeding properly.

27. Cutting dietary protein by 5 percentage points reduces ammonia output by roughly:

Every 5 percent reduction in dietary protein cuts ammonia output 15-20 percent, provided amino acids stay balanced.

28. Dissolved oxygen below 5 mg/L worsens tilapia FCR by about:

Feed intake falls and conversion worsens 15-30 percent, which is why the ration should be cut when oxygen drops.

29. Adding salt at 1-2 g/L to pond or tank water mainly protects against:

Chloride competes with nitrite at the gill, roughly halving nitrite toxicity, and salt costs almost nothing.

30. In a low-aeration pond, when should the ration be reduced by 20-30 percent?

Oxygen is lowest at dawn and falls again after sunset; feed the full share at midday instead.

Build a one-page nutrient profile of the feed you are using right now. Collect the feed bag label or, if you mix your own, the recipe with every ingredient by weight. Write down crude protein percent, lipid percent, pellet size in mm and price per kg in USD from a real local supplier. Using the tables in this module, decide whether that protein level matches the species and life stage in your pond, and calculate the protein-to-energy ratio roughly by dividing your estimated digestible energy in kcal/kg by the grams of protein per kg of feed; state whether it falls inside 8-10 kcal per gram of protein. Then measure the water: take temperature at dawn and mid-afternoon for seven days and one ammonia reading, and note the daily feed weight beside each reading. Finish with three written decisions: the protein level you will buy or mix next cycle, the pellet size you will move to, and the feeding rule you will apply when temperature or oxygen drops. Cost each decision in USD per kg of feed and per cycle for your own pond.
