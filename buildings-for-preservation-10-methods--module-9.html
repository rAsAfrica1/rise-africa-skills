1. Cleaning Masonry: Methods and Risks

Masonry cleaning removes dirt, soot, and biological growth from brick, stone, or concrete. But cleaning always carries risk: you can strip the protective patina, open pores, or dissolve the binder. In Africa, common soiling includes red dust from laterite, bird droppings, mould from humid coastal areas, and smoke from cooking fires. Before you start, test a small area. Use a 1:10 dilution of non-ionic detergent (like Triton X-100) in warm water. Apply with a soft natural-bristle brush, not wire. Work from bottom up to avoid streaks. Rinse with low-pressure water (under 300 psi) and a sponge. Never use high-pressure jets (over 1000 psi) on historic masonryâthey erode mortar and sandstone. For heavy soot, a poultice of kaolin clay and ammonium carbonate (1:1 by weight) can be applied 5â10 mm thick, covered with plastic, and left 24 hours. But ammonium salts can leave harmful residues; rinse thoroughly with deionised water if available. In dry regions like the Sahel, avoid wet cleaning during harmattan windsâdust will stick. In coastal areas, clean after the rainy season to remove salt. Always wear nitrile gloves, goggles, and a dust mask (N95). Work in shade; direct sun dries chemicals too fast. After cleaning, let the masonry dry 48 hours before applying any protective treatment. Remember: the goal is not to make it look new, but to remove harmful deposits while preserving historic fabric. If in doubt, leave itâdirt is often less damaging than aggressive cleaning.

2. Chemical Cleaning: Poultices and Solvents

Chemical cleaning uses poultices to draw out stains and solvents to dissolve coatings. A poultice is a paste that holds a cleaning agent against the surface. For masonry, mix kaolin clay or diatomaceous earth with a solvent: for oil stains, use acetone or xylene; for rust, use oxalic acid (10% solution); for biological growth, use quaternary ammonium (1:40 dilution). Apply 6â10 mm thick, cover with plastic, and leave 12â24 hours. Remove before it dries completelyâscrape into a bag and dispose as hazardous waste. For timber, use a poultice of white spirit and fuller's earth to remove wax or oil. For metal, solvents like acetone remove grease, but avoid chlorinated solvents on aluminium. In Africa, many solvents are expensive or unavailable. Local alternatives: citrus-based degreasers (d-limonene) work on light oils; vinegar (5% acetic acid) removes hard water spots on glass and glazed tile. Always test on a hidden area. Work outdoors or with cross-ventilation; solvents are flammable and toxic. Wear nitrile gloves, goggles, and a respirator with organic vapour cartridges. Do not mix chemicalsâespecially bleach and ammonia, which produce toxic gas. After poulticing, rinse with clean water and neutralise acids with baking soda solution (1 cup per litre). For stubborn soot on limestone, a poultice of ammonium carbonate and kaolin (1:3) is effective, but rinse 3â4 times. Store chemicals in original labelled containers, away from children and livestock. Dispose of residues according to local regulationsânever pour down storm drains.

3. Mechanical Cleaning: Abrasive and Laser

Mechanical cleaning uses physical force to remove dirt. Abrasive methods include dry grit blasting, wet grit blasting, and micro-abrasion. Grit blasting with silica sand is banned in many countries due to silicosis risk; use garnet or crushed glass instead. For historic masonry, use low pressure (2â4 bar) and a fine abrasive (0.1â0.5 mm). Keep the nozzle 300â500 mm from the surface and move continuously. Never blast mortar joints directlyâthey erode quickly. In Africa, sandblasting is common but often too aggressive. A safer alternative is the Jos/Toronto method: a fine mist of water and air at 1â2 bar with a swirl nozzle, which gently lifts dirt. For timber, use hand scrapers and bristle brushes; power sanders remove too much material. For metal, use wire brushes (brass for soft metals, steel for iron) and fine abrasive pads. Laser cleaning is emerging but expensive. A 100-watt pulsed Nd:YAG laser can remove soot from stone without touching the surface. However, lasers can cause microfractures and are not portable for large areas. In Africa, laser cleaning is rare; focus on manual and low-pressure methods. Always wear PPE: goggles, N95 mask, ear protection. Work in a contained area; use tarps to catch debris. After mechanical cleaning, vacuum with a HEPA filter to remove fine dust. Never use wire brushes on historic brickâthey scratch the fired skin. Test a small area first. If the surface becomes powdery or discoloured, stop. Mechanical cleaning is fast but irreversibleâuse it only when gentler methods fail.

4. Cleaning Timber and Metal Surfaces

Timber and metal need gentler cleaning than masonry. For timber, start with a soft brush and vacuum to remove loose dirt. For painted timber, use a pH-neutral detergent (1:20 dilution) and a damp clothânever soak the wood. For bare timber, remove grime with a 50:50 mix of white vinegar and water, then rinse and dry immediately. Avoid caustic soda or bleachâthey raise the grain and bleach colour. For mould on timber, use 70% isopropyl alcohol in a spray bottle; leave 10 minutes, then wipe. In humid African climates, mould returns quickly; improve ventilation and apply a borate treatment (e.g., Tim-bor) after cleaning. For metal, identify the type first. Ferrous metals (iron, steel) rust; use a wire brush or sandpaper (120â240 grit) to remove loose rust, then apply a rust converter (phosphoric acid). For non-ferrous metals (copper, brass, bronze), use a paste of lemon juice and salt, or commercial metal polish. Never use abrasive cleaners on gilded or painted metal. For aluminium, use a non-abrasive pad and mild soap. After cleaning, rinse with distilled water to avoid water spots. Dry immediately with a soft cloth. Apply a microcrystalline wax (e.g., Renaissance wax) to protect metal from tarnish; for timber, use a breathable oil like linseed or tung oil. In coastal areas, metal corrodes fastâclean and wax every 6 months. In dusty regions, cover metal with breathable fabric when not in use. Always test on a hidden area. Store brushes and cloths separately for ferrous and non-ferrous metals to avoid cross-contamination.

5. Protecting Surfaces After Cleaning

After cleaning, surfaces are vulnerable. Dirt and pollutants can reattach quickly, and some cleaning methods leave residues that attract new soiling. Protection starts with thorough rinsing. For masonry, rinse with deionised water if available; if not, use rain water or filtered water. Avoid tap water high in saltsâit can leave efflorescence. Let the surface dry 48â72 hours. Then apply a breathable water repellent, such as a silane/siloxane blend (e.g., 5% active ingredient). Do not use film-forming sealers (acrylic, polyurethane)âthey trap moisture and cause spalling. Apply with a low-pressure sprayer or brush, two coats, wet-on-wet. For timber, after cleaning and drying, apply a UV-resistant oil or stain. In Africa, linseed oil is cheap but can grow mould; tung oil is better. For metal, apply a microcrystalline wax or a clear lacquer (for indoor only). For all surfaces, consider a sacrificial coating: a reversible layer that can be removed later. For example, on historic stone, a lime wash (1:3 lime putty to water) can be applied and renewed every 5 years. It protects and is reversible. After protection, monitor for 6 months. Check for water beading (good) or dark patches (moisture trapped). In dusty areas, apply a anti-soiling treatment like a fluoropolymer, but these are costly. Always keep a record of products used and dates. Train local staff to reapply as needed. Remember: protection is not permanentâit is a maintenance cycle. Clean gently, protect wisely, and monitor often.

6. Case Study: Cleaning a Soot-Damaged Cathedral

In 2019, a 19th-century stone cathedral in Nairobi, Kenya, suffered a fire that deposited thick black soot on limestone walls and timber pews. The soot was acidic and would etch the stone if left. A team of 12 local trainees, supervised by a conservator, used a three-step process. First, dry cleaning: HEPA vacuums with soft brush attachments removed loose soot from all surfaces. Second, chemical poultice: for the limestone, they mixed kaolin clay with ammonium carbonate (1:1) and applied 8 mm thick, covered with plastic, left 18 hours. They worked in 1 mÂ² sections, from bottom up. After removal, they rinsed with deionised water three times, testing pH with litmus paper until neutral. For the timber pews, they used a poultice of fuller's earth and white spirit (1:2) to draw out embedded soot, then wiped with 70% isopropyl alcohol. Third, protection: after 72 hours drying, they applied a silane/siloxane water repellent to the stone and a tung oil finish to the timber. Challenges included limited water (they used 200 litres total, recycled for rinsing) and high humidity (they ran dehumidifiers for a week). Results: 95% of soot removed without damaging the stone's patina. Cost: about $3,500 USD for materials and 200 labour hours. Lessons: test small areas first; never use high-pressure water; control moisture; and train local people. The cathedral now has a maintenance plan: clean light soiling every 2 years, inspect for soot every 6 months. This case shows that with simple tools and careful methods, even severe soot damage can be reversed in an African context.

1. Cleaning Masonry: Methods and Risks

Masonry in Africa rarely fails because it is dirty; it fails because cleaning removes the sacrificial layer that protected it. Coral rag on the Swahili coast, laterite in West Africa, and lime-mortared sandstone in the Sahel all share one property: they are porous and salt-active. 2. Chemical Cleaning: Poultices and Solvents

Poultices work by capillary action: a moist paste draws soluble salts and soiling out of the pore structure as it dries. The technique is old, cheap, and unforgiving. The two failure modes are too wet (drives salts deeper) and too dry (nothing moves). For salt-loaded coral or lime, use a clay-kaolin or paper-pulp poultice with deionised water, applied 10â20 mm thick, covered to slow drying, and removed at the leather-hard stage.

Solvents are for organic soiling â paints, oils, graffiti. In Africa, solvent supply chains are the constraint: import lead times and customs clearance can add 3â6 weeks. Specify products available locally where possible, or price the lead time into your programme. Always check the safety data sheet (SDS) and confirm VOC limits against the client's site rules and local environmental regulations.

3. Mechanical Cleaning: Abrasive and Laser

Abrasive cleaning is fast and cheap, which is exactly why it is over-used. On carved stone, abrasive blasting erases tool marks â the very evidence that gives a building its heritage value and, in commercial terms, its listing status and grant eligibility. The rule: if the surface has any carving, moulding, or historic finish, abrasive is off the table unless a trial panel proves otherwise.

Laser cleaning (Nd:YAG, Q-switched) is the premium option. It is dry, precise, and ideal for soot, metal, and delicate stone. In Africa, the constraint is equipment access: machines are typically hired from South Africa or Europe, with mobilisation costs of USD 3,000â8,000. For a cathedral or museum contract, this is justifiable; for a single faÃ§ade, it rarely is. Price laser as a specialist sub-contract, not as an in-house service.

4. Cleaning Timber and Metal Surfaces

Timber. Never wet-clean historic timber. Moisture raises the risk of fungal decay and dimensional movement, and in tropical Africa, termites follow damp wood. Dry-brush with soft bristles, vacuum with HEPA filtration, and use a conservation-grade poultice only for localised stains. If the timber is painted, test for lead before any abrasion â pre-1970s paint in many African cities contains lead.

Metal. Ferrous metal: remove corrosion products by hand, apply a rust converter only where specified, and protect immediately. Non-ferrous (copper, brass, bronze): do not polish to a bright finish unless the client's brief and the heritage authority explicitly require it â patina is part of the significance. In coastal Africa, salt-laden air means any bare metal will re-corrode within weeks; protection must be applied the same day as cleaning.

5. Protecting Surfaces After Cleaning

Cleaning without protection is a maintenance liability you have just handed to the client. Match the protection to the substrate:

  • Masonry: breathable water repellent (silane/siloxane), never a film-forming acrylic. Test for colour change.
  • Timber: breathable oil or microporous stain; avoid polyurethane on historic joinery.
  • Metal: wax or lacquer for non-ferrous; primer plus topcoat for ferrous.
  • All: document the protection product, batch, and application date in the O&M manual â this is a billable deliverable and protects you from future blame.

6. Case Study: Cleaning a Soot-Damaged Cathedral

A 19th-century stone cathedral in an African capital suffered a major fire. Soot and pyrolytic carbon bonded to limestone, lime plaster, and stained glass. The client's insurer required documented method statements and a conservation-accredited supervisor.

Approach: (1) Dry HEPA vacuuming to remove loose soot; (2) poultice cleaning on salt-loaded limestone; (3) laser cleaning on carved capitals and stained-glass surrounds; (4) micro-abrasion only on flat, unadorned plinth stone; (5) breathable water repellent after 14 days of drying.

Commercial outcome: the laser sub-contract was 22% of the cleaning budget but unlocked the heritage grant, which covered 40% of the total project. The lesson for African contractors: the premium method is often the one that makes the project fundable. Price the method against the funding, not just the labour.
