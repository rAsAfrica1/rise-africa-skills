1. Common Pig Diseases and Symptoms

As a pig farmer in Africa, you must know the common diseases that can wipe out your herd. The most deadly is African Swine Fever (ASF). There is no vaccine. Symptoms include high fever (41C+), loss of appetite, red skin blotches on ears and belly, vomiting, diarrhea, and sudden death. If you see these, call a vet immediately. ASF spreads fast; cull and bury deep (2 meters) or burn. Another major killer is swine erysipelas. Look for diamond-shaped red patches on the skin, high fever (40-41C), and stiff walking. It responds to penicillin (e.g., 1 ml per 10 kg body weight, injected into the muscle for 3 days).

Piglets often get colibacillosis (E. coli diarrhea) within the first week. Symptoms: watery yellow or white diarrhea, dehydration, and death within 2-3 days. Treat with oral electrolytes and antibiotics like neomycin (follow label). Prevent by ensuring clean farrowing pens and vaccinating sows before farrowing. Another common problem is pneumonia, caused by bacteria like Pasteurella or viruses. Signs: coughing, labored breathing, fever, and poor growth. Use antibiotics such as oxytetracycline (20 mg/kg body weight, injected daily for 3-5 days). Improve ventilation and reduce dust.

Parasites also cause disease. Mange (sarcoptic mange) makes pigs rub against walls, causing thick crusts on ears and flanks. Treat with ivermectin injection (1 ml per 33 kg body weight, under the skin). Roundworms cause pot-belly, coughing, and poor growth. Deworm every 3-4 months with fenbendazole or ivermectin in feed. Always check with your local vet or extension officer for correct dosages.

Early recognition saves money. Walk through your pens daily. Look for pigs that are separated from the group, not eating, or lying down more than usual. Check their eyes (discharge), nose (dry or runny), skin (redness, lumps), and feces (blood, mucus, worms). Record any changes. If more than 2 pigs show the same signs, isolate them and call a vet. Do not wait. In Africa, where veterinary services may be far, your daily observation is the first line of defense.

2. Vaccination Programs and Protocols

Vaccines are cheap insurance against deadly diseases. In Africa, the most important vaccines for pigs are against swine erysipelas, classical swine fever (CSF), and foot-and-mouth disease (FMD) where available. African Swine Fever has no vaccine, so you must rely on biosecurity. Work with your vet to design a program based on local disease threats. A typical schedule: vaccinate piglets at 6-8 weeks against erysipelas and CSF. Give a booster 3-4 weeks later. Sows should be vaccinated 2-4 weeks before farrowing to pass immunity to piglets through colostrum.

Store vaccines in a refrigerator between 2C and 8C. Never freeze them unless the label says so. Use a cooler box with ice packs when transporting. Before injecting, check the expiry date and shake the bottle gently. Use a clean needle and syringe for each pig. For piglets, use a 20-gauge needle; for adult pigs, 16- or 18-gauge. Inject into the neck muscle behind the ear, not the ham. The correct dose is on the labelusually 1-2 ml per pig. Do not inject into fat; you want the vaccine in the muscle.

Follow the cold chain strictly. If a vaccine gets warm, it loses potency. Discard opened bottles after 24 hours unless the label says otherwise. Never mix different vaccines in one syringe unless instructed. Record every vaccination: date, vaccine name, batch number, and pig ID. This helps if a disease breaks out. If you see swelling at the injection site, that's normal. But if a pig collapses or has difficulty breathing, it may be an allergic reactioncall a vet immediately.

Vaccination alone is not enough. It must go with good nutrition, clean water, and reduced stress. Stressed pigs respond poorly to vaccines. So, handle pigs calmly, avoid mixing groups, and provide shade. In many African countries, government vets offer free or subsidized vaccines for CSF and erysipelas. Ask your local agriculture office. If you cannot afford all vaccines, prioritize breeding sows and piglets, as they are most vulnerable. A simple rule: vaccinate before disease strikes, not after.

3. Parasite Control and Deworming

Parasites steal feed and cause disease. In pigs, the main internal parasites are roundworms (Ascaris suum), whipworms, and nodular worms. External parasites include mange mites and lice. A heavy worm burden can reduce growth by 20-30% and cause coughing, diarrhea, and pot-belly. You may see worms in feces or a dull, rough coat. Mange causes intense itching, crusty skin, and weight loss. Control starts with clean pens. Remove manure daily. Keep floors dryparasite eggs need moisture. Provide a concrete floor if possible; dirt floors harbor worms.

Deworm regularly. For piglets, deworm at 2 weeks, 6 weeks, and 10 weeks of age. For adult pigs, deworm every 3-4 months. Use drugs like ivermectin (injection: 1 ml per 33 kg body weight under the skin) or fenbendazole (in feed: 5 mg per kg body weight for 3 days). Albendazole is also effective (10 mg/kg). Always follow label instructions. Rotate drugs to prevent resistance. If you use ivermectin, it also kills mange mites and lice. For mange only, you can use amitraz spray or dip, but follow safety precautions.

Treat the whole herd at once, not just sick pigs. Otherwise, untreated pigs reinfect the rest. Mark your calendar and deworm all pigs on the same day. After deworming, you may see worms in feces for a day or twothat's normal. Clean pens thoroughly after deworming to remove eggs. If you have a small herd (less than 10 pigs), you can deworm individually. For larger herds, mix dewormer into feed. Make sure each pig gets the right dose; dominant pigs may eat more. Divide pigs into groups by size and feed the medicated feed in troughs with enough space.

Prevent re-infection. Quarantine new pigs for 30 days and deworm them before mixing. Keep pigs off wet, muddy areas. Rotate pastures if you have them. Provide clean water and feed from troughs, not on the ground. Control flies and rats, which can carry parasite eggs. If you see persistent itching or weight loss after deworming, consult a vetmaybe you have a resistant parasite. Regular deworming is one of the cheapest ways to improve pig health and profit.

4. Biosecurity Measures to Prevent Disease

Biosecurity means keeping diseases out of your farm. In Africa, where African Swine Fever is rampant, it is your only defense. Start with a fence around your pig pen. A simple wire fence or wooden palisade works. Keep a footbath at the entrance with disinfectant (e.g., 2% caustic soda or commercial virkon). Change it weekly. Anyone entering must dip their boots. Do not allow visitors who own pigs or have visited other farms within 48 hours. If they must enter, provide clean boots and overalls. Never let them bring pork products onto the farm.

Quarantine all new pigs for at least 30 days. Keep them in a separate pen, far from your main herd. Feed and water them last. Wear separate boots and clothes. After 30 days, if they show no signs of disease, you can mix them. But before mixing, deworm and vaccinate them. If any pig dies during quarantine, burn or bury it deep (2 meters) and disinfect the area. Never sell or eat a pig that died suddenlyit could be ASF.

Control traffic. Vehicles that transport pigs or feed can carry viruses. Load and unload pigs at the gate, not inside your farm. If a truck must enter, disinfect its wheels and chassis. Use your own vehicle if possible. Keep feed in a closed store; do not let rodents or birds contaminate it. Store feed on pallets, not on the floor. Clean water is criticaluse a borehole or treated water. Do not let pigs drink from stagnant pools.

Maintain clean pens. Remove manure daily. Wash floors with water and disinfectant every week. Let pens dry before putting pigs back. Provide separate tools for each pen; do not share shovels or buckets between sick and healthy pigs. If you have a disease outbreak, cull sick pigs immediately. Disinfect the entire pen with 2% caustic soda or 1% formalin. Leave it empty for at least 2 weeks. Burn or bury all bedding and manure. Remember, ASF virus survives in dead pigs, pork products, and on boots for days. One mistake can kill your whole herd. So, be strict. No exceptions.

5. Recognizing and Treating Sick Pigs

A sick pig costs you money, so act fast. Every morning, walk through your pens and look for these signs: a pig that doesn't come to feed, stands apart, lies down more than usual, or has a rough coat. Check its eyesdischarge or redness. Check its nosedry or runny. Check its skinred patches, lumps, or crusts. Check its fecesdiarrhea, blood, or worms. Check its breathingcoughing or panting. Take its temperature with a digital thermometer (normal is 38.5-39.5C). If it's above 40C, it has a fever. Isolate any sick pig immediately in a hospital pen with clean bedding, easy access to water, and soft feed.

Treat based on symptoms. For fever and unknown cause, start with a broad-spectrum antibiotic like oxytetracycline (20 mg/kg body weight, injected into the neck muscle daily for 3-5 days). For diarrhea, give oral rehydration solution (1 teaspoon salt, 1 tablespoon sugar in 1 liter water) and an antibiotic like neomycin in water. For pneumonia, use tylosin or enrofloxacin (follow label). For wounds, clean with iodine and apply an antibiotic spray. Never use human antibiotics without vet advicedosages differ. Always complete the full course, even if the pig looks better after 2 days. Stopping early breeds resistant bacteria.

Supportive care matters. Provide fresh water with electrolytes. Offer soft, palatable feed like cooked maize porridge with some milk. Keep the pig warm and dry. Reduce stresshandle gently. If the pig is not eating, you may need to force-feed with a syringe (10-20 ml of porridge every 4 hours). Record all treatments: date, drug, dose, and pig ID. If no improvement after 3 days, call a vet. Some diseases like ASF have no treatment; you must cull to save the rest. Know when to give up: if a pig is down, cannot stand, and has a high fever with skin blotches, it may be ASF. Do not sell or eat it. Bury deep.

Prevention is better than cure. Keep pens clean, provide good feed, and reduce overcrowding. A healthy pig rarely gets sick. But when it does, quick action saves money. Keep a basic medicine kit: thermometer, syringes, needles, antibiotics, dewormer, electrolytes, and disinfectant. Store in a cool, dry place. Check expiry dates monthly. Train yourself and your workers to spot signs early. In Africa, where vets may be far, your observation and first aid can mean the difference between one dead pig and a dead herd.

6. Working with Veterinarians and Diagnostic Labs

You cannot do everything alone. A good veterinarian is your best partner. In Africa, find a vet through your local agriculture office, veterinary council, or farmers' cooperative. Establish a relationship before an emergency. Ask them to visit your farm at least twice a year for a health check. During the visit, they can take blood samples, check for parasites, and advise on vaccinations. Keep their phone number saved. When you call, be ready to describe symptoms clearly: how many pigs are sick, their ages, what you see (fever, diarrhea, skin patches), and what you have already tried. This helps them decide if they need to come or can prescribe over the phone.

Diagnostic labs are crucial for confirming diseases like ASF, CSF, and erysipelas. Your vet can collect samples: blood in EDTA tubes for viruses, swabs from noses or rectums, and pieces of tissue from dead pigs (liver, spleen, lymph nodes) in a clean plastic bag on ice. Never freeze fresh samples unless instructed. Send them to a government or private lab. In many countries, the national veterinary lab offers subsidized testing. Results may take 3-7 days. While waiting, isolate sick pigs and disinfect. Do not move pigs off the farm. If ASF is confirmed, you must cull all pigs and disinfect. The government may compensate you in some countries.

Work with your vet to create a herd health plan. This includes vaccination schedules, deworming dates, biosecurity protocols, and a list of emergency contacts. Review it every 6 months. If you have a disease outbreak, your vet can help you report it to authorities. In many African countries, ASF is a notifiable diseaseyou must report it. Failure to report can lead to spread and legal penalties. Also, ask your vet about training opportunities. Many NGOs and government programs offer short courses on pig health. Attend them.

Finally, keep good records. Write down every treatment, vaccination, and vet visit. Note the date, drug, dose, and pig ID. This helps your vet see patterns and make better decisions. If you sell pigs, buyers may ask for health records. Good records also help you claim insurance or compensation. Remember, a vet is not just for emergenciesthey are a teacher and advisor. Treat them with respect, pay them fairly, and follow their advice. A strong vet-farmer relationship leads to healthier pigs and better profits.

<h2>Practical Reference: Pig Health &amp; Disease Management in African Pig Enterprises</h2>

<p>This practical reference is the working document for the <strong>Health and Disease Management</strong> module of the <em>Rise AFRICA Skills</em> Pig Farming course. It is written for the commercial smallholder and emerging pig producer who treats pigs as a business, not a hobby. Every disease event has a cost: dead animals, slow growth, condemned carcasses, drug bills, and lost market confidence. The purpose of this lesson is to give you a fast, field-ready system for detecting problems early, acting correctly, and protecting the margin on every pig you sell.</p>

<p>Across sub-Saharan Africa, pig production is one of the fastest-growing livestock enterprises, driven by urban demand for pork in Nigeria, Uganda, Kenya, Rwanda, Ghana, South Africa, and beyond. But growth is constrained by disease. African Swine Fever (ASF) alone has wiped out entire herds in Nigeria, Uganda, and South Africa in recent years. Classical Swine Fever, African Swine Fever, porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome (PRRS), foot-and-mouth disease (FMD), swine erysipelas, colibacillosis, coccidiosis, and internal parasites all reduce productivity. The economics are brutal: a sow that loses a litter to neonatal diarrhoea costs you roughly 812 piglets, or the equivalent of several months of feed. A single ASF outbreak can close your business permanently.</p>

<p>This reference covers six operational areas: recognising common diseases and symptoms; vaccination programmes and protocols; parasite control and deworming; biosecurity; treating sick pigs; and working with veterinarians and diagnostic laboratories. It is deliberately practical. Keep it in the pig house, not in an office drawer.</p>

<h3>3. Parasite Control and Deworming</h3>
<p>Internal parasites (Ascaris, Strongyles, Trichuris) and external parasites (mange mites, lice, ticks) silently steal your profit. A heavily parasitised grower can lose 1530% of expected daily gain, meaning you feed the worm instead of the pig. Deworm all pigs on a fixed calendar, not when you remember. Rotate dewormer active ingredients (e.g., ivermectin, albendazole, levamisole) to reduce resistance. Treat mange with ivermectin injections or pour-on, and repeat after 14 days to kill newly hatched mites. Keep pens dry moisture favours coccidia and worm eggs. Clean and disinfect farrowing pens between batches.</p>

<h3>4. Biosecurity Measures to Prevent Disease</h3>
<p>Biosecurity is the cheapest disease control you will ever buy. In African smallholder systems, the biggest risks are: visitors entering pens, new pigs introduced without quarantine, sharing of buckets and boots between farms, feeding of uncooked swill (kitchen waste), and contact with wild pigs or free-ranging pigs. Practical measures:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Quarantine all new pigs for 30 days</strong> in a separate pen, away from the main herd.</li>
<li><strong>Control entry:</strong> one gate, footbath with disinfectant, dedicated boots and overalls.</li>
<li><strong>Never feed uncooked swill</strong> ASF survives in raw meat scraps. Boil swill for at least 30 minutes if used at all.</li>
<li><strong>Fence the pig house</strong> to keep out wild pigs, dogs, and unauthorised people.</li>
<li><strong>All-in / all-out</strong> where possible; clean and disinfect between batches.</li>
<li><strong>Control rodents and flies</strong> they carry pathogens mechanically.</li>
<li><strong>Record everything:</strong> visitors, purchases, treatments, deaths.</li>
</ul>

<h3>5. Recognising and Treating Sick Pigs</h3>
<p>Check your pigs at least twice daily. A healthy pig is alert, eats eagerly, has a cool dry snout, pink skin, and normal faeces. Signs of illness: off-feed, isolation, laboured breathing, coughing, diarrhoea, lameness, skin lesions, abortion, or sudden death. When you find a sick pig: isolate it, record its temperature (normal is 38.539.5C), and call your vet. Do not guess with antibiotics. Use only veterinary-prescribed medicines, follow dosage by body weight, and respect the <strong>withdrawal period</strong> before slaughter this protects consumers and your market access. Keep a treatment register: date, pig ID, drug, dose, withdrawal end date.</p>

<h3>6. Working with Veterinarians and Diagnostic Labs</h3>
<p>Your veterinarian is a business partner, not an emergency contact. Build the relationship before you have a crisis. Register with a licensed vet or the district veterinary office. Establish a routine herd health visit every 36 months. When disease strikes, collect samples correctly: blood in EDTA tubes for PCR, tissue (spleen, tonsil, lymph nodes) in formalin or chilled for laboratory diagnosis, and swabs for bacterial culture. In many African countries, samples can be submitted to national veterinary laboratories (e.g., NVRI in Nigeria, KALRO and the Central Veterinary Laboratory in Kenya, NAADS/ MAAIF laboratories in Uganda, or the Onderstepoort Veterinary Institute in South Africa). Always ask the lab for sample submission guidelines before collecting. Accurate diagnosis saves money treating the wrong disease wastes drugs and loses pigs.</p>

<li>Walk the herd twice daily and record any sick pig immediately.</li>
<li>Isolate sick pigs in a quarantine pen away from the main herd.</li>
