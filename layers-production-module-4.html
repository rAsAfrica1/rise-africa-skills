1. Understanding the Pre-lay Phase: Feeding for Future Production

The pre-lay phase is the period from about 16 weeks of age until the first egg is laid, usually around 18 to 20 weeks depending on breed and management. This is the most critical window in the entire layer feeding programme because the bird is undergoing rapid physiological change. She is still growing skeletal frame, medullary bone is being laid down as a calcium reserve, the reproductive tract is developing, and the first follicles are being recruited. In Africa, where feed costs can be 65 to 75 percent of total production cost, mistakes in the pre-lay phase are expensive because they reduce peak production and persistency, and you cannot recover that lost income later. The goal of pre-lay feeding is simple: arrive at point of lay with a pullet that is the correct body weight, has a well developed frame, has sufficient calcium reserves, and is ready to sustain a long laying cycle. If the pullet is underweight at point of lay, she will lay smaller eggs, reach a lower peak, and drop out of production earlier. If she is overweight, she risks prolapse, fatty liver, and poor persistency. Both errors cost money. The first practical step is to switch from grower feed to pre-lay feed at around 16 to 17 weeks, or when the flock reaches about 5 percent production in some systems. The pre-lay diet should contain 16 to 17 percent crude protein, 0.9 to 1.0 percent calcium, and 0.45 to 0.5 percent available phosphorus. The calcium level is deliberately higher than grower feed but lower than layer feed, because the bird needs to start building medullary bone without overloading the kidneys before the shell glands are fully active. In many African markets, pre-lay feed is not always available as a separate product. In that case, a practical approach is to blend grower feed with layer feed at a ratio of about 50 to 60 percent grower to 40 to 50 percent layer for 10 to 14 days, then move fully to layer feed at first egg. This blending must be done carefully and mixed thoroughly to avoid nutrient segregation. Body weight monitoring is non negotiable. Weigh a representative sample of at least 100 birds every week from 16 weeks onward. Use a digital scale accurate to 10 grams. Compare the average weight to the breed standard. If the flock is more than 5 percent below standard, do not delay the feed transition; instead, increase the daily feed allocation by 3 to 5 grams per bird and consider a higher energy diet. If the flock is more than 5 percent above standard, do not restrict feed severely; instead, hold the allocation steady and increase fibre slightly. In Africa, heat stress is a major factor. During hot months, birds eat less during the day, so shift the main feeding to early morning and late evening when temperatures are cooler. Provide cool drinking water at all times; a layer drinks about 200 to 250 millilitres per day, and intake doubles in heat. Water quality matters: total dissolved solids should be below 1000 parts per million, and bacterial contamination must be controlled. A simple practice is to add 1 to 2 millilitres of household bleach per 20 litres of drinking water if the source is questionable, but never exceed this and always mix well. Lighting is part of the pre-lay programme because it drives sexual maturity. In open sided houses in Africa, natural day length varies. The rule is simple: never decrease light during rearing, and never increase light before the flock is ready. If birds are underweight at 16 weeks, delay any light stimulation until they reach target weight. If they are on target, begin a step up lighting programme at 17 to 18 weeks, adding 30 to 45 minutes per week until you reach 16 hours of total light per day. This should be done gradually to avoid a sudden surge of double yolks or prolapse. Vaccination and health must be completed before lay. Ensure the flock has received Newcastle, infectious bronchitis, and egg drop syndrome vaccines according to your local veterinary schedule. Deworm 2 to 3 weeks before expected lay. Check for external parasites such as mites and lice, because they reduce feed intake and cause stress. A stressed pullet does not eat well and will not reach peak. Finally, train the birds to the nest boxes before lay begins. Place nest boxes at least one week before the first egg, with clean bedding such as wood shavings or rice husks. Collect floor eggs promptly to discourage the habit. In summary, pre-lay feeding is about preparation. Get the body weight right, transition the feed gradually, manage heat and water, control light, and keep the birds healthy. A well prepared pullet will reward you with a strong peak and a long, profitable laying cycle.

2. Managing the Peak Phase: Feeding for Maximum Egg Output

The peak phase covers roughly 26 to 35 weeks of age, when the flock should reach 90 to 95 percent production for most commercial layers, and sometimes higher for brown egg breeds under good management. This is the most demanding period for the bird and for the farmer. The bird is laying almost every day, gaining a little body weight, and maintaining her frame. Nutrient demand is at its highest. In Africa, where temperatures often exceed 30 degrees Celsius, the challenge is that feed intake drops just when nutrient needs rise. The key principle of peak feeding is to maximise nutrient intake, not just feed intake. This means using a dense diet with high energy, high protein, and balanced amino acids. A typical peak layer diet should contain 17 to 18 percent crude protein, 0.75 to 0.8 percent available phosphorus, 3.8 to 4.2 percent calcium, and 0.45 to 0.5 percent methionine. Energy should be 2750 to 2850 kilocalories per kilogram of metabolisable energy. If the local feed mill cannot supply this, you can improve the diet by adding 1 to 2 percent vegetable oil or tallow, and by using synthetic amino acids such as methionine and lysine. These additions are cost effective because they increase egg size and persistency. Feed allocation at peak should be 110 to 120 grams per bird per day for white egg layers and 115 to 125 grams for brown egg layers, depending on body weight and temperature. Do not restrict feed during peak. A common mistake in Africa is to reduce feed to save money when egg prices are low. This is a false economy. If you cut feed at peak, the bird will mobilise body reserves, lose weight, and drop production. She may never recover fully. Instead, if costs are high, focus on feed efficiency: use good quality ingredients, avoid wastage, and control rodents and birds. Feed wastage of 5 grams per bird per day across 1000 birds is 5 kilograms per day, which is 150 kilograms per month. That is real money. Use feeders that are the correct height and depth, and do not fill them more than one third full. In hot weather, feed early in the morning and again in the late afternoon. Provide electrolytes and vitamin C in drinking water during heat waves. A simple homemade electrolyte solution is 1 teaspoon of salt, 1 teaspoon of baking soda, and 1 tablespoon of honey or sugar per litre of clean water, given for 4 to 6 hours per day during extreme heat. Always provide plain water alongside. Water is the most important nutrient at peak. A laying hen drinks 200 to 300 millilitres per day, and up to 400 millilitres in heat. Ensure drinkers are clean and placed in shade. Check water flow rate: nipple drinkers should deliver at least 60 to 80 millilitres per minute. If flow is low, birds will not drink enough and production will fall. Egg size and shell quality are also critical at peak. Calcium must be provided in the correct particle size. Use a mix of fine limestone and coarse limestone or oyster shell. Coarse particles stay in the gizzard longer and release calcium during shell formation at night. A practical ratio is 60 percent fine and 40 percent coarse. If shell quality is poor, check calcium and phosphorus levels, and also check for disease such as infectious bronchitis. In Africa, many farmers use local limestone that may be high in magnesium or other impurities. Test your limestone source if possible. Magnesium above 0.5 percent can reduce shell quality. Record keeping at peak is essential. Track daily egg production, feed intake, water intake, mortality, and egg weight. A sudden drop in feed or water intake is the first sign of disease or heat stress. A sudden increase in water intake can indicate diarrhoea or kidney problems. Weigh eggs weekly. Egg weight should increase gradually from about 50 grams at start of lay to 60 to 65 grams by 30 weeks. If egg weight is low, increase methionine and energy. If eggs are too large, reduce energy slightly or check for double yolks. Mortality at peak should be below 0.5 percent per month. If it is higher, investigate immediately. Common causes in Africa include heat stress, colibacillosis, fowl typhoid, and internal parasites. Work with a veterinarian. Finally, do not change the diet suddenly at peak. Any change should be gradual over 3 to 5 days. Keep the best feed for the best birds. The peak phase is where you make your profit. Feed them well, keep them cool, give them clean water, and they will pay you back with a high, persistent lay.

3. Post-peak Phase: Maintaining Persistency and Managing Egg Quality

The post-peak phase begins after about 35 to 40 weeks of age, when production starts to decline gradually. A well managed flock should drop only 0.5 to 1 percent per week, reaching about 65 to 70 percent at 70 weeks and 50 to 55 percent at 90 weeks. The goal of post-peak feeding is to maintain persistency, control egg size, and keep shell quality high while reducing feed cost per egg. In Africa, this phase is often neglected because farmers focus on peak. But post-peak is where many flocks either stay profitable or lose money. The first principle is to reduce nutrient density gradually, not suddenly. As production falls, the bird needs less protein and energy, but she still needs calcium for shell quality. A typical post-peak diet should contain 15 to 16 percent crude protein, 0.65 to 0.7 percent available phosphorus, 4.0 to 4.2 percent calcium, and 0.35 to 0.4 percent methionine. Energy can drop to 2650 to 2750 kilocalories per kilogram. Feed allocation should be reduced by 2 to 3 grams per bird per day every 4 to 6 weeks, but only if body weight is stable and production is declining normally. Do not reduce feed if the flock is underweight or if egg weight is dropping too fast. Weigh birds every 4 weeks. Body weight should remain stable or increase slightly. If birds lose weight, increase feed. If they gain too much, reduce energy slightly. Egg size control is important because very large eggs are more likely to crack and are often downgraded. In the post-peak phase, egg size naturally increases. To control it, reduce protein and energy slightly, but keep calcium high. Do not reduce calcium. Shell quality is the biggest challenge in post-peak. As the bird ages, her ability to absorb and mobilise calcium declines. Use coarse limestone at 50 percent of total calcium source. Provide additional oyster shell in a separate feeder if possible. Keep phosphorus at the correct level; too much phosphorus reduces calcium absorption. Vitamin D3 is essential for calcium metabolism. Ensure the premix contains at least 3000 international units of vitamin D3 per kilogram of feed. In Africa, where birds are often exposed to strong sunlight, vitamin D3 is still needed because the bird cannot synthesise enough from sunlight alone, especially in confined houses. Heat stress continues to be a factor. Provide shade, ventilation, and cool water. In open houses, plant trees or use shade cloth. In closed houses, use fans and foggers. Avoid handling birds during the hottest part of the day. Feed during cool hours. Add vitamin C at 200 to 300 milligrams per litre of water during heat waves. Water quality must be maintained. Clean drinkers daily. Use a water sanitiser such as chlorine or hydrogen peroxide according to instructions. Check for biofilm in pipes. A simple practice is to flush lines weekly and use a descaler every 3 months. Disease challenges in post-peak include egg drop syndrome, infectious bronchitis, and bacterial infections such as E. coli and Salmonella. Vaccinate according to your schedule. If production drops suddenly by more than 5 percent in a week, investigate immediately. Check feed quality, water, light, and disease. Do not wait. Mortality in post-peak should be below 0.5 percent per month. If it rises, cull sick and non laying birds. Culling is difficult but necessary. A non laying bird eats feed and gives nothing. Identify her by checking the vent, comb colour, and pubic bone spacing. Cull at least once per month. Egg collection and handling also affect quality. Collect eggs at least 3 to 4 times per day. Store eggs in a cool place, ideally 15 to 18 degrees Celsius, with 70 to 80 percent humidity. Do not wash eggs unless necessary; if you must, use warm water with a sanitiser and dry immediately. In many African markets, eggs are sold fresh, so shell cleanliness matters. Keep nests clean and replace bedding regularly. Finally, plan the end of the cycle. Decide when to sell the flock. Most flocks are sold at 72 to 80 weeks when production falls below 50 percent or when egg prices are low. Some farmers keep flocks to 90 weeks if feed is cheap and eggs are profitable. Calculate your cost per egg every month. If cost per egg exceeds the selling price, it is time to sell. Post-peak feeding is about balance. Reduce costs without hurting the bird. Keep shells strong. Keep the flock healthy. And know when to stop. That is how you protect your profit.

4. Practical Feed Formulation and Ingredient Selection for African Layer Diets

Feed is the largest cost in layer production, often 65 to 75 percent of total expenses. In Africa, feed ingredient prices fluctuate widely due to season, fuel costs, and import restrictions. A farmer who understands feed formulation can save money and improve performance. This lesson covers practical feed formulation for pre-lay, peak, and post-peak phases using locally available ingredients. The first step is to know the nutrient requirements of your birds. These vary by age, body weight, production level, and temperature. Use breed standards as a guide, but adjust based on your own records. The most important nutrients are metabolisable energy, crude protein, essential amino acids such as methionine and lysine, calcium, available phosphorus, and vitamins and minerals. Energy is the driver of feed intake. If energy is low, birds eat more. If energy is high, they eat less. In hot climates, high energy diets help because birds cannot eat enough to meet their needs. Common energy sources in Africa include maize, sorghum, millet, cassava chips, wheat bran, and vegetable oils. Maize is the gold standard with about 3300 kilocalories per kilogram. Sorghum is similar but may contain tannins. Use low tannin varieties or add 0.5 percent sodium bicarbonate. Cassava chips are cheaper but low in protein and can contain cyanide if not properly dried. Use only well dried cassava and limit to 20 percent of the diet. Wheat bran is a good source of fibre and phosphorus but low in energy. Vegetable oil, such as palm oil or sunflower oil, is an excellent energy source at 9000 kilocalories per kilogram. Add 1 to 3 percent to increase energy density. Protein sources include soybean meal, groundnut cake, sunflower cake, fish meal, and blood meal. Soybean meal is the best plant protein with 44 to 48 percent crude protein and good amino acid profile. Groundnut cake has 40 to 45 percent protein but is often contaminated with aflatoxin. Test your groundnut cake and use a binder such as bentonite or a commercial toxin binder. Sunflower cake has 28 to 32 percent protein and high fibre; limit to 10 percent in layer diets. Fish meal is excellent with 60 to 65 percent protein but expensive and variable in quality. Use only good quality fish meal and limit to 5 percent to avoid fishy eggs. Blood meal has 80 percent protein but poor amino acid balance; use up to 3 percent. Calcium sources include limestone, oyster shell, and marble chips. Limestone should contain at least 38 percent calcium. Oyster shell is better for coarse particles. In Africa, limestone is often cheap and locally available. Test for magnesium and other impurities. Phosphorus sources include dicalcium phosphate, bone meal, and rock phosphate. Dicalcium phosphate is the best with 18 to 22 percent phosphorus and good availability. Bone meal is variable and may carry disease; use only from reputable sources. Rock phosphate has high fluorine and should be avoided unless defluorinated. Vitamins and minerals are supplied through a premix. Use a layer premix that provides vitamins A, D3, E, K, B complex, and trace minerals such as zinc, manganese, iron, copper, iodine, and selenium. The premix should also contain methionine, lysine, and sometimes choline. Follow the manufacturer's inclusion rate, usually 0.25 to 0.5 percent. When formulating, use the Pearson square or a simple computer program. Start with the energy and protein targets. Then balance calcium and phosphorus. Then add amino acids. Then add premix. Always mix thoroughly. A horizontal mixer or a drum mixer is best. Mix for at least 10 minutes. If mixing by hand, mix on a clean concrete floor in layers and turn at least 3 times. Store feed in a cool, dry place. Use within 4 weeks. Mouldy feed causes disease and reduces production. In Africa, aflatoxin is a major risk. Use a toxin binder in the feed, especially if using groundnut cake or maize that may be contaminated. Common binders include bentonite, zeolite, and yeast cell wall products. Use according to instructions. Water quality also affects feed utilisation. Test your water for salinity, nitrates, and bacteria. If water is high in salt, reduce salt in the diet. If water is contaminated, treat it. Finally, keep records of feed formulation and performance. If you change an ingredient, monitor egg production, egg weight, and shell quality for 2 weeks. If performance drops, change back. Do not make multiple changes at once. Good feed formulation is a skill. It takes practice. But it is one of the best investments you can make in your layer business.

5. Troubleshooting Common Feeding Problems in African Layer Flocks

Even with a good feeding programme, problems occur. This lesson covers the most common feeding related problems in African layer flocks and how to solve them. The first problem is low feed intake. If birds are not eating enough, production drops. Causes include heat stress, poor feed quality, disease, and social stress. In heat stress, birds pant and drink more but eat less. Solution: feed early morning and late evening, provide cool water, add electrolytes, and increase nutrient density. If feed quality is poor, check for mould, rancidity, or dust. Mouldy feed smells musty and may contain aflatoxin. Reject it. If disease is present, look for other signs such as diarrhoea, respiratory distress, or drop in water intake. Treat with a veterinarian. Social stress from overcrowding, poor lighting, or predator attacks also reduces intake. Provide more space, shade, and calm environment. The second problem is high feed intake with low production. This often means the diet is low in energy or protein, or the bird is not absorbing nutrients. Check the feed formulation. Increase energy and protein. Check for internal parasites such as worms. Deworm. Check for coccidiosis. Treat. Check for mycotoxins. Use a binder. The third problem is poor shell quality. Causes include low calcium, low phosphorus, low vitamin D3, high phosphorus, heat stress, and disease. Check the calcium level. Use coarse limestone. Add oyster shell. Check phosphorus. Ensure vitamin D3 is at least 3000 international units per kilogram. In heat stress, add vitamin C and electrolytes. If disease is present, treat. The fourth problem is small egg size. Causes include low protein, low methionine, low energy, and early lay. Increase methionine to 0.45 percent. Increase energy. If birds are laying too early, delay light stimulation. The fifth problem is large egg size with cracks. Causes include high protein, high energy, and age. Reduce protein and energy slightly. Keep calcium high. Add coarse limestone. The sixth problem is dirty eggs. Causes include dirty nests, diarrhoea, and poor egg handling. Clean nests. Replace bedding. Treat diarrhoea. Collect eggs frequently. The seventh problem is sudden drop in production. Causes include disease, heat stress, feed change, water shortage, and light change. Check water first. Then check feed. Then check for disease. If you changed feed, change back. If light changed, restore. The eighth problem is high mortality. Causes include heat stress, disease, and cannibalism. Provide shade and ventilation. Treat disease. Reduce stocking density. Provide enrichment such as hanging vegetables or pecking blocks. The ninth problem is feed wastage. Causes include poor feeder design, overfilling, and rodents. Use correct feeders. Fill one third. Control rodents. The tenth problem is water quality. Causes include high salts, bacteria, and algae. Test water. Clean drinkers. Use sanitiser. In Africa, many of these problems are linked to heat stress and poor feed quality. The best solutions are preventive: use good quality feed, provide cool water, manage shade and ventilation, keep records, and act quickly when you see a problem. A good farmer walks the flock every day. Look at the birds. Look at the feed. Look at the water. Look at the eggs. Look at the droppings. The droppings tell you a lot. Normal droppings are brown with white caps. Green droppings mean the bird is not eating. Yellow droppings mean liver problems. Watery droppings mean diarrhoea or kidney problems. Bloody droppings mean coccidiosis. Use your eyes and your records. If you see a problem, act immediately. Do not wait. A small problem becomes a big problem in a few days. Finally, work with other farmers. Join a cooperative or a farmer group. Share knowledge. Buy feed together to reduce cost. Sell eggs together to get better prices. In Africa, community is strength. Use it. With good feeding and good management, your layers will reward you with high production and good profit.

<h2>Practical Reference: Layer Flock Health, Biosecurity and Record-Keeping for African Egg Enterprises</h2>

<p>By module four, most African layer farmers have mastered the technical basics of feeding. Yet the difference between a farm that breaks even and one that builds real wealth usually comes down to three non-negotiable disciplines: keeping birds alive and productive (health), keeping disease out (biosecurity), and knowing your numbers (records). This practical reference consolidates those disciplines into a single working document you can print, laminate and keep in the poultry house. Everything here is designed for the realities of African production small to medium flocks, mixed housing, limited veterinary access, and the constant pressure of feed costs that consume 6575% of total expenditure.</p>

<p>Think of this lesson as your farm's operating manual. It does not replace your veterinarian, but it ensures you ask the right questions, act fast when mortality spikes, and can prove to a buyer, bank or insurer that your enterprise is professionally run. In the African egg business, buyers increasingly demand consistency of supply and quality. Records and biosecurity are what make that consistency possible.</p>

<h3>Why Health, Biosecurity and Records Drive Profit</h3>

<p>In African layer production, preventable disease and poor record-keeping routinely destroy 1530% of potential revenue. A Newcastle disease outbreak can wipe out 5090% of an unvaccinated flock within a week. Coccidiosis in growing pullets can permanently reduce peak production by 510 percentage points. And without records, farmers cannot detect these losses early, cannot prove creditworthiness to lenders, and cannot negotiate contracts with supermarkets, hotels or institutional buyers who require reliable weekly volumes.</p>

<p>The three pillars reinforce each other. Biosecurity reduces the disease pressure your flock faces. Health management keeps the birds you have performing at their genetic potential. Records tell you whether either is actually working and convert that knowledge into money through better pricing, lower mortality and tighter feed control.</p>

<strong style="color:#b71c1c;"> SAFETY WARNING HEALTH, CHEMICALS AND FOOD SAFETY</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#4a0000;">Never use antibiotics as a substitute for biosecurity, and never sell eggs from birds under antibiotic treatment or within the withdrawal period. Misuse of antimicrobials contributes to antimicrobial resistance (AMR), a major One Health threat in Africa, and residues in eggs can cause regulatory rejection and harm consumers. Always:</p>
<ul style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#4a0000;">
<li>Use only veterinary-prescribed medicines, at the correct dose and duration.</li>
<li>Observe the full withdrawal period before selling eggs or meat.</li>
<li>Store vaccines and drugs at recommended temperatures (usually 28C).</li>
<li>Wear gloves and a mask when handling disinfectants, formaldehyde or fumigants.</li>
<li>Dispose of dead birds by deep burial or incineration never sell or feed them to other animals.</li>
<li>Wash hands and change boots before and after entering poultry houses.</li>
</ul>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#4a0000;font-size:13px;">Sources: WHO (2021) <em>Antimicrobial Resistance Fact Sheet</em>; FAO/OIE/WHO (2022) <em>One Health Joint Plan of Action</em>.</p>

<h3>Building a Simple Record-Keeping System</h3>

<p>You do not need software. A hardcover exercise book and a pen, used consistently, will outperform a sophisticated app used sporadically. Record these five numbers every day:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Mortality</strong> number of dead birds and suspected cause.</li>
<li><strong>Eggs collected</strong> total, plus cracked/soiled/dirty.</li>
<li><strong>Feed issued</strong> kilograms per house or per flock.</li>
<li><strong>Water consumed</strong> if you have a meter; a sudden drop is an early disease signal.</li>
<li><strong>Notable events</strong> weather, visitors, vaccinations, medication, power cuts.</li>
</ol>

<p>At the end of each week, calculate: hen-day production %, feed conversion ratio, mortality %, and cost per egg. At the end of each month, compare against Table 2. This discipline alone typically improves profitability by 1020% within two production cycles because it exposes feed wastage, disease trends and underperforming houses before they become crises.</p>

<h3>Biosecurity on a Realistic African Budget</h3>

<p>You do not need a laboratory-grade facility. You need consistent barriers:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>One entrance.</strong> Fence the compound; lock the gate. Visitors sign in.</li>
<li><strong>Footbath at every house door.</strong> Refill with fresh disinfectant every 23 days; sunlight and organic matter neutralise it fast.</li>
<li><strong>Dedicated boots and overalls.</strong> Never wear farm clothes into town or to other farms.</li>
<li><strong>All-in / all-out where possible.</strong> If not, at least keep age groups in separate houses with separate equipment.</li>
<li><strong>Rodent and wild bird control.</strong> Mesh openings, store feed in closed bins, clear vegetation around houses.</li>
<li><strong>Quarantine new birds</strong> for 21 days before mixing with the main flock.</li>
<li><strong>Dead bird disposal</strong> deep pit or incinerator, far from water sources and houses.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul>
<li> Vaccination schedule posted on the house wall; cold chain verified weekly.</li>
<li> Daily records of mortality, eggs, feed and water completed before dusk.</li>
<li> Weekly calculation of hen-day production, FCR and mortality %.</li>
<li> Footbath at every house entrance, refreshed every 23 days.</li>
<li> Visitor log in use; no entry without boots and overalls.</li>
<li> Monthly comparison of flock performance against Table 2 benchmarks.</li>
<li> Veterinary contact and nearest diagnostic lab saved in phone and on wall.</li>
<li> Antibiotic use logged with dose, dates and withdrawal period observed.</li>
</ul>
