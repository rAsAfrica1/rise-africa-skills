1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Locking methods are what stop nuts, bolts, and screws from shaking loose. On a moving machineâlike a maize grinder, a boda boda, or a generatorâvibration is constant. Without locking, a nut can back off in minutes. That leads to parts falling off, oil leaks, or worse: injury. In Africa, where machines often run long hours on rough roads or uneven ground, locking is not optional. It is the difference between a repair that lasts and one that fails before the customer pays you.

There are two main types of locking: mechanical and chemical. Mechanical locking uses physical devicesâlock washers, split pins, lock nuts, and tab washers. Chemical locking uses liquid threadlockers, like Loctite. Each has its place. For example, a split pin on a wheel hub nut is standard on many trucks. A threadlocker is better for small screws inside a pump that you cannot easily reach again.

The cost of not locking is high. A single loose bolt on a grinding mill can crack the frame. A loose nut on a motorcycle axle can cause a crash. In many African workshops, comebacksâwhen a customer returns with the same problemâoften come from missing or wrong locking. Learning to lock properly saves time, money, and reputation.

This module will teach you the tools, methods, mistakes, and safety rules for locking. You will practice on real bolts and nuts. By the end, you will be able to choose the right locking method for any job, from a simple door hinge to a heavy-duty truck suspension. Remember: a bolt without locking is a bolt waiting to fail.

2. Tools and Materials

To do locking work, you need a few basic tools and materials. Most are cheap and available in local markets or hardware shops. Start with a good set of spannersâboth open-ended and ringâsized 8mm to 24mm. You will also need a socket set with a ratchet handle. For locking, you often need two spanners: one to hold the bolt head and one to turn the nut.

For mechanical locking, you need: split pins (also called cotter pins) in sizes 2mm, 3mm, and 4mm diameter. Lock washersâboth split-ring and starâin common sizes like M6, M8, M10, and M12. Tab washers, which are thin metal plates with tabs you bend over. And lock nuts, such as nylon-insert nuts (nyloc) or all-metal prevailing torque nuts. A small hammer and a pin punch help you bend tabs or tap split pins.

For chemical locking, you need threadlocker. Loctite 243 (blue) is medium strengthâgood for most bolts you might need to remove later. Loctite 271 (red) is high strengthâfor permanent joints. In Africa, you can find local brands like ABRO or Permatex. Always check the expiry date. Also get a wire brush and clean rags to remove oil and dirt before applying.

Safety gear: safety glasses, gloves, and a wire brush for cleaning threads. A torque wrench is not mandatory but very helpfulâit ensures you tighten to the correct setting, so locking devices work properly. For example, a nyloc nut needs to be tightened to a specific torque to grip the nylon insert. Without a torque wrench, you might under- or over-tighten.

Keep your tools organised in a roll or box. In many African workshops, lost tools cost more time than the job itself. A simple magnetic tray for nuts and bolts prevents them from falling into dirt or engine bays.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps for any locking job. First, clean the threads. Use a wire brush to remove rust, old threadlocker, or grease. Dirty threads cause false torque readings and weak locking. Wipe with a rag. If using a chemical locker, the surface must be dry and oil-free.

Second, choose your locking method. For a bolt that will be removed often, use a split washer or nyloc nut. For a permanent joint, use red threadlocker or a tab washer. For a critical joint like a steering arm, use a split pin and a castellated nut. Match the size: a 10mm bolt needs a 10mm lock washer or a M10 nyloc nut.

Third, assemble and tighten. If using a threadlocker, apply 2â3 drops to the bolt threads, then tighten to the recommended torque. For a nyloc nut, tighten until the nylon insert gripsâusually you feel resistance. For a split pin, tighten the nut to the correct torque, then align the hole in the bolt with a slot in the nut. Insert the split pin through the hole, bend one leg over the bolt end and the other around the nut. Use pliers to bend neatly.

Fourth, check your work. Try to turn the nut by handâit should not move. For tab washers, bend one tab against a flat on the nut and another against a flat on the part. For lock wire (safety wire), twist it in the tightening direction so it pulls the bolt tight. Use lock wire pliers if available; otherwise, twist by hand with pliers.

Fifth, test under load. If possible, run the machine briefly and re-check. Vibration can settle parts. A good locking job will not loosen. If it does, you missed a step. In Africa, where machines work hard, always double-check critical fasteners like wheel nuts, engine mounts, and blade bolts.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Reusing a split pin or lock washer. Split pins fatigue and break; lock washers lose tension. Fix: always use new locking devices. They cost pennies compared to a breakdown. In many African markets, you can buy a packet of 100 split pins for less than a loaf of bread.

Mistake 2: Over-tightening a nyloc nut. This strips the nylon insert or damages the bolt. Fix: use a torque wrench. For an M10 nyloc, typical torque is 45â50 Nm. If you don't have a torque wrench, tighten until the nylon grips, then a quarter turn moreâno more.

Mistake 3: Using red threadlocker on a bolt you need to remove. Red Loctite requires heat (about 250Â°C) to break. Fix: use blue Loctite for removable joints. If you already used red and need to remove, heat the bolt with a blowtorch carefully, then turn.

Mistake 4: Not cleaning threads before threadlocker. Oil or grease stops the locker from curing. Fix: clean with brake cleaner or acetone, let dry, then apply. In dusty workshops, cover the bolt after application.

Mistake 5: Bending tab washers incorrectly. If you bend the tab back and forth, it breaks. Fix: bend each tab once, firmly, with pliers. If it breaks, replace the washer.

Mistake 6: Ignoring lock wire direction. If you twist lock wire the wrong way, it can loosen the bolt. Fix: always twist in the direction that tightens the bolt. Practice on a bench first.

Mistake 7: Using the wrong size split pin. A loose pin falls out. Fix: choose a pin that fits snugly in the hole. If it is too small, use the next size up. In Africa, many roadside repairs fail because the mechanic used a nail or piece of wire instead of a proper split pin. Never do that.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety starts before you pick up a tool. Wear safety glassesâthreadlocker can splash into your eyes. Wear gloves when handling lock wire or split pins; they have sharp ends. Work in a well-ventilated area if using chemical lockers; fumes are harmful. In many African workshops, ventilation is poor, so open a window or work outside.

Best practice 1: Always use the correct torque. Over-tightening stretches bolts; under-tightening lets them loosen. A torque wrench is a wise investment. If you don't have one, borrow or rent one for critical jobs like cylinder heads or wheel hubs.

Best practice 2: Match locking method to the job. For high-vibration areas like engine mounts, use both a lock washer and a threadlocker for extra security. For parts that get hot, like exhaust bolts, use all-metal lock nutsânylon inserts melt. For parts that get wet, use stainless steel locking devices to avoid rust.

Best practice 3: Inspect after assembly. Give the nut a firm tug with a spanner. If it moves, redo it. For lock wire, ensure the wire is tight and twisted neatly. For split pins, check that both legs are bent properly.

Best practice 4: Keep records. In a busy workshop, note which locking method you used on each job. If a customer returns, you can check. This also helps you learn what works.

Best practice 5: Train others. If you are a supervisor, show your apprentices how to lock properly. In Africa, skills are passed by hand. A single well-trained mechanic can prevent many accidents.

Finally, never take shortcuts. A missing split pin or a dab of grease instead of threadlocker might save five minutes now, but it can cost a life later. Locking is a small step that makes a big difference.

6. Practical Exercise

This exercise will give you hands-on practice with three common locking methods. You need: a vice, a set of spanners, a torque wrench (if available), a split pin, a nyloc nut, a tab washer, a bolt and nut (M10), a threadlocker (blue), a wire brush, and safety glasses. Work on a bench in a well-lit area.

Exercise 1: Split pin locking. Take an M10 bolt and a castellated nut. Tighten the nut to 45 Nm. If the hole in the bolt does not align with a slot, tighten a little more until it does. Insert a 3mm split pin through the hole. Bend one leg over the bolt end and the other around the nut using pliers. Check that the pin is snug and cannot fall out. Now try to loosen the nut by handâit should not move. Then remove the pin with pliers and repeat until you can do it in under 2 minutes.

Exercise 2: Nyloc nut and threadlocker. Clean the threads of an M10 bolt with a wire brush. Apply two drops of blue threadlocker to the bolt threads. Thread on a nyloc nut and tighten to 45 Nm. Wait 30 minutes for the threadlocker to cure. Then try to loosen the nut with a spanner. It should require force. Now remove the nut, clean the threads, and repeat without threadlocker. Compare the difference. This shows how chemical locking adds security.

Exercise 3: Tab washer. Place a tab washer on a bolt, then the part, then a plain nut. Tighten to 45 Nm. Bend one tab up against a flat on the nut, and another tab down against a flat on the part. Use a small hammer and punch. Check that the nut cannot turn. Then try to remove the nut without bending the tabs backâyou will see it is locked.

After each exercise, inspect your work. Ask yourself: Is it clean? Is it tight? Will it survive vibration? In Africa, where machines work hard, these skills are in demand. Practice until you can do all three without thinking. Then teach someone else.

A fastener that loosens on a tractor steering linkage, a trailed implement drawbar, or a grain auger drive can cause a catastrophic accident. Never reuse a split pin that is bent, corroded, or has lost its spring. Never substitute a plain nut for a lock nut on a safety-critical joint. Never operate equipment with missing or loose locking hardware â even for a short test run. If you are unsure of the correct locking method or torque, stop and consult the manufacturer's manual or a qualified mechanic. In African field conditions, dust, heat, and vibration accelerate loosening â inspect locking hardware before every season and after every 50 hours of operation.

Quick Reference Checklist â Locking Methods

  1. Identify the joint type: Is it safety-critical (steering, brakes, drawbar) or non-critical (guards, covers)? Safety-critical joints require positive locking (split pin, tab washer, or new lock nut).
  2. Check the manual: Confirm the specified locking method and torque for that specific fastener on that specific machine. Do not guess.
  3. Inspect before reuse: Replace any split pin, nylon lock nut, or tab washer that shows wear, corrosion, or deformation. These are single-use items in most cases.
  4. Clean the threads: Dust, oil, and rust prevent correct torque and reduce threadlocker effectiveness. Wire-brush and degrease before assembly.
  5. Apply the right torque: Use a calibrated torque wrench. Under-torque causes loosening; over-torque stretches the bolt and destroys its locking ability.
  6. Use the correct threadlocker strength: Medium strength (removable) for serviceable joints; high strength (permanent) only where specified. Never use threadlocker on plastic or nylon-insert nuts.
  7. Verify after assembly: Tug-test split pins, confirm tab washers are bent flat against the nut, and re-check torque after the first 10 hours of operation.
  8. Record and schedule: Log locking hardware inspections in your maintenance book. In dusty or high-vibration conditions, inspect every 50 hours or weekly during peak season.
