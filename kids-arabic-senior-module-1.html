1. Introduction to Arabic Script and Directionality

Arabic is written from right to left, and the script is cursive, meaning most letters connect to the next. This is different from Latin scripts where letters stand alone. In Arabic, the shape of a letter changes depending on its position in a word: initial, medial, final, or isolated. For example, the letter Baa () looks like a boat with a dot below when isolated, but when connected at the start it becomes , in the middle , and at the end . Learning these four forms is essential for reading and writing. In Africa, Arabic is used daily in countries like Egypt, Sudan, Chad, Nigeria, Senegal, and Tanzania, not only for religious texts but also in newspapers, street signs, and business contracts. As a vocational learner, you will encounter Arabic in trade, tourism, and diplomacy. To start, get a lined notebook and a pen. Practice writing each letter in all four positions. Use free online tools like 'Arabic Keyboard' to type and see the letters change. Spend 10 minutes daily copying the alphabet chart. By the end of this lesson, you should be able to write your name in Arabic and recognize the direction of a paragraph. Remember, practice writing from right to left until it feels natural. A good exercise: take a simple Arabic word like 'kitab' (book) and write it letter by letter, noting how each letter connects. This foundation will make the rest of the module easier.

2. Letters from Alif to Kha: Shapes and Sounds

The first group of Arabic letters includes Alif (), Baa (), Taa (), Thaa (), Jeem (), Haa (), and Kha (). Alif is a vertical stroke and does not connect to the following letter; it sounds like the 'a' in 'father'. Baa is a boat shape with one dot below, sounding like 'b' in 'book'. Taa is similar but with two dots above, sounding like 't' in 'table'. Thaa has three dots above, sounding like 'th' in 'think'. Jeem has a dot inside its loop, sounding like 'j' in 'jam'. Haa is a smooth loop without a dot, a deep 'h' from the throat. Kha has a dot above the loop, sounding like the 'ch' in Scottish 'loch'. In Africa, these letters appear in names like 'Khadija' () and 'Thabo' (). To practice, write each letter five times in isolation, then in the initial, medial, and final forms. Use a pronunciation app like 'Arabic Alphabet' or watch YouTube videos by 'ArabicPod101'. Record yourself saying each sound and compare with a native speaker. Pay attention to the difference between Haa () and Kha (); the first is softer, the second raspy. A common mistake is to confuse Baa and Taa; remember the dots: one below for Baa, two above for Taa. Practice writing words like 'bab' () and 'tamr' (). Spend 15 minutes daily on these seven letters before moving on. By the end, you should be able to read and write simple words like 'ahlan' () and 'khubz' ().

3. Letters from Dal to Dad: Shapes and Sounds

This group covers Dal (), Dhal (), Ra (), Zay (), and Dad (). Dal is a simple angle shape, sounding like 'd' in 'door'. Dhal has a dot above, sounding like 'th' in 'this'. Ra is a curved stroke, sounding like a rolled 'r' as in Spanish. Zay has a dot below, sounding like 'z' in 'zebra'. Dad is a loop with a dot, a heavy 'd' from the back of the mouth. These letters do not connect to the following letter, so they only have two forms: isolated and final. For example, Dal at the start of a word stands alone but connects to the previous letter if any. In Africa, these letters are common in words like 'dar' (house) and 'zaman' (time). To master them, write each letter 10 times, then write words like 'dars' () and 'zayt' (). Use a mirror to watch your mouth for Dad; it is pronounced with the tongue pressed against the upper gums, creating a deep sound. Practice with a native speaker via apps like 'Tandem' or 'HelloTalk'. A useful drill: say 'dal-dhal' and 'ra-zay' pairs to hear the difference. Many learners confuse Dal and Dad; remember Dad is heavier and emphatic. Spend 10 minutes daily. By the end, you should read and write 'wad' () and 'dhad' ().

4. Letters from Ta to Qaf: Shapes and Sounds

This set includes Ta (), Za (), Ayn (), Ghayn (), Fa (), and Qaf (). Ta is an emphatic 't', pronounced with the tongue pressed down, sounding deeper than Taa. Za is the emphatic 'th' or 'z', like a heavy 'th' in 'that'. Ayn is a pharyngeal sound, a constriction in the throat, often described as the sound when lifting something heavy. Ghayn is similar but with a gargle, like the French 'r'. Fa is a loop with a dot, sounding like 'f' in 'fish'. Qaf is a deep 'k' from the back of the throat, like the 'c' in 'cup' but further back. In Africa, these letters appear in words like 'qahwa' (coffee) and 'ghali' (expensive). To practice, write each letter in all positions. For Ayn and Ghayn, use a mirror and place your hand on your throat to feel the vibration. Listen to native speakers on YouTube channels like 'Learn Arabic with Maha'. A common mistake is to substitute Ayn with a glottal stop; instead, practice saying 'a-a-a' while tightening your throat. Spend 15 minutes daily. By the end, you should be able to read and write 'qalb' (heart) and 'ghurfah' (room).

5. Letters from Kaf to Ya: Shapes and Sounds

The final group includes Kaf (), Lam (), Meem (), Noon (), Ha (), Waw (), and Ya (). Kaf is a 'k' sound, like in 'kite'. Lam is an 'l', like in 'lion'. Meem is a small circle with a tail, sounding like 'm' in 'moon'. Noon is a bowl with a dot above, sounding like 'n' in 'noon'. Ha is a small loop, a light 'h' from the lungs. Waw is a loop with a tail, sounding like 'w' in 'water' or 'u' in 'put'. Ya is a curve with two dots below, sounding like 'y' in 'yes' or 'ee' in 'see'. In Africa, these letters are in names like 'Kofi' () and 'Lamine' (). To practice, write each letter in all forms. Pay attention to Ha () versus Haa (); Ha is lighter. Use flashcards or apps like 'Anki' for memorization. Practice reading simple words: 'kalb' (dog), 'laban' (milk), 'mawz' (banana), 'nahr' (river), 'hawa' (air), 'wadi' (valley), 'yad' (hand). Spend 10 minutes daily. By the end, you should be able to read and write these letters in connected text. A good exercise is to write a short sentence like 'ana fi al-bayt' (I am in the house) using all the letters learned so far.

6. Short Vowels (Harakat) and Sukun

Arabic short vowels, called harakat, are marks placed above or below letters to indicate the vowel sound. There are three: Fatha () is a diagonal stroke above, pronounced as 'a' in 'cat'. Kasra () is a diagonal stroke below, pronounced as 'i' in 'sit'. Damma () is a small loop above, pronounced as 'u' in 'put'. Sukun () is a small circle above, indicating no vowel. These marks are essential for correct pronunciation, especially in the Quran and for learners. In everyday Arabic, newspapers and books often omit them, but for a beginner, they are crucial. In Africa, harakat are used in teaching materials and children's books. To practice, write a letter with each vowel: Ba (), Bi (), Bu (), and B (). Then combine with letters to form words: 'kataba' () means 'he wrote'. Notice how each consonant has a vowel except the last. Use a pencil to add harakat to words in a text. Listen to native speakers on 'Forvo' or 'YouTube' to hear the difference between 'bata' () and 'bita' (). A common mistake is to confuse Fatha and Kasra; remember Fatha is above, Kasra below. Spend 15 minutes daily. By the end, you should be able to read fully vocalized Arabic texts, like children's stories. Practice with the word 'shukran' () and 'ahlan wa sahlan' ( ).

Practical Reference: Arabic Alphabet & Pronunciation for African Agribusiness Professionals



This practical reference consolidates the six lessons in the Arabic Alphabet and Pronunciation module into a single, working document. It is designed for African agribusiness owners, traders, exporters, and cooperative managers who need to read Arabic script confidently enough to handle supplier labels, shipping documents, customs forms, and client correspondence across North Africa and the Middle East. Arabic is spoken by over 420 million people and is an official language in 25 countries, including major agricultural trade partners of African nations such as Egypt, Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Libya, Sudan, and Mauritania. For a Kenyan avocado exporter shipping to Cairo, a Nigerian sesame trader negotiating with a Saudi buyer, or a Ghanaian cocoa cooperative dealing with Moroccan processors, the ability to read Arabic script is a direct commercial advantage.



Business-first principle: you do not need to become a fluent Arabic speaker to gain value from this module. You need to recognise letters, understand directionality, and decode short vowel markers so you can read product names, weights, dates, and place names on documents. This reference is your desk-side companion.

SAFETY WARNING Arabic Script & Commercial Documents


Do not rely on machine translation or unaided reading of Arabic script for legally binding documents, phytosanitary certificates, customs declarations, or letters of credit. Arabic script omits short vowels in most adult texts, and a single misread letter can change a quantity, a date, or a product name. For example, (ann) means "ton" while (n) means "clay" a costly confusion in a fertiliser or cement shipment. Always have a qualified human translator or a bilingual trade agent verify critical documents before signing or shipping. In African trade corridors with North Africa (e.g., NigeriaMorocco, KenyaEgypt, GhanaAlgeria), document errors can trigger customs delays of 721 days and demurrage charges. When in doubt, request an English or French version of the document alongside the Arabic original.




Quick Reference Checklist




  1. Directionality: Always read Arabic right to left, top to bottom. Numbers are read left to right within Arabic text a common trap on invoices.

  2. Letter shapes: Remember that most letters have four forms isolated, initial, medial, and final. Learn the isolated form first, then the connected forms.

  3. Non-connecting letters: Alif (), Dal (), Dhal (), Ra (), Zay (), Waw (), and their families do not connect to the following letter. This creates visual breaks in words.

  4. Harakat: If you see fatha (), kasra (), or damma (), pronounce the short vowel. If you see sukun (), pronounce the consonant with no vowel.

  5. Product labels: Look for the letters (d) for dir (export), (ww) for wazn (weight), and (t) for trkh (date).

  6. Business vocabulary drill: Memorise 10 high-frequency agribusiness words per week start with dir (export), wrid (import), sir (price), kammiyya (quantity), jawda (quality).

  7. Document verification: Never sign an Arabic-language contract without a bilingual review. Keep a glossary of your top 20 trade terms in Arabic, English, and French.

  8. Practice source: Use free official resources such as the Qatar Foundation's Al-Jazeera Learning Arabic or the British Council's Arabic resources to reinforce reading daily for 15 minutes.
