1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Aluminium welding is a high-demand skill across Africa. From repairing aluminium pots and pans in local markets to fabricating window frames, roof trusses, and boat hulls, the need is everywhere. But aluminium is not steel. It melts at around 660C, much lower than steel's 1,370C, and it conducts heat about five times faster. That means heat travels away from the weld zone quickly, so you need more amps and faster travel speeds. If you weld aluminium like steel, you will burn through or get a cold, weak joint.

Another challenge is the oxide layer. Aluminium instantly forms a tough oxide skin that melts at 2,070Cfar higher than the metal itself. This oxide must be removed before welding, or you will get porosity, lack of fusion, and a dirty weld. That is why aluminium welding requires either mechanical cleaning (stainless steel brush) or chemical cleaning, and why AC TIG is so effective: the alternating current breaks up the oxide layer.

In Africa, aluminium is common in transport (truck bodies, canoe repairs), construction (roofing sheets, window frames), and household goods. Many workshops in Nairobi, Lagos, or Accra already have basic arc welders, but few can weld aluminium well. Mastering this skill can double your income because few competitors can do it. You can repair expensive aluminium radiators, fabricate custom gates, or build fishing boats. The key is understanding the metal's behaviour and using the right tools.

This module will teach you advanced techniques: TIG welding with AC, MIG welding with spool guns, proper filler selection, and how to avoid common pitfalls. We will focus on practical methods you can apply with equipment available in most African cities. By the end, you will be able to produce strong, leak-free aluminium welds that look professional. Remember: aluminium demands speed, cleanliness, and the right heat. Let's get started.

2. Tools and Materials

To weld aluminium properly, you need specific tools. First, a welding machine capable of AC/DC. For TIG, an AC/DC inverter TIG welder is ideal200 amps minimum. For MIG, you need a spool gun or a push-pull gun because aluminium wire is soft and will birdnest in a standard torch. In many African cities, you can find these at welding supply shops in industrial areas, or order from online suppliers like Amazon or local distributors. Expect to pay $500$1,500 for a good AC/DC TIG machine.

Filler metals: For most aluminium alloys, use 4043 (5% silicon) or 5356 (5% magnesium). 4043 is more forgiving, flows well, and is good for castings and general work. 5356 is stronger and more ductile, better for structural applications and anodized parts. Never use steel filler on aluminium. Buy filler rods in 1.6mm, 2.4mm, or 3.2mm diameters. For MIG, use 0.8mm or 1.0mm wire on a spool gun.

Tungsten electrodes: For AC TIG, use pure tungsten (green) or zirconiated (white) for transformer machines; for inverter machines, use lanthanated (gold) or ceriated (grey) in 2.4mm. You need a dedicated stainless steel wire brushnever use it on steeland a dedicated file or carbide burr for cleaning. Acetone or alcohol for degreasing. A stainless steel scratch brush and a stainless steel wire brush are different: use a hand brush for light cleaning, and a power brush for heavy oxide.

Safety gear: Welding helmet with shade 1013, leather gloves, flame-resistant jacket, and proper ventilation. Aluminium welding produces ozone and metal fumeswork outdoors or use a fume extractor. Also, you need a good quality regulator and argon gas (100% argon for TIG and MIG). Flow rate: 1015 CFH for TIG, 2030 CFH for MIG. In Africa, argon is available from gas suppliers like BOC or local affiliates. A cylinder rental and refill costs about $50$100 per month. Finally, get a dedicated aluminium work table or clean steel surfacenever weld aluminium on a dirty bench.

3. Step by Step Method

Step 1: Clean the metal. Aluminium must be perfectly clean. Wipe with acetone to remove oil and grease. Then use a dedicated stainless steel wire brush to remove the oxide layer. Brush in one direction only, and do not use the brush on steel. For thick oxide, use a carbide burr or grinding wheel, but avoid grinding wheels that have been used on steelcontamination causes porosity. Clean within 10 minutes of welding; oxide reforms fast.

Step 2: Set up your machine. For AC TIG, set the amperage based on thickness: 1.6mm aluminium needs 6080 amps; 3mm needs 100130 amps; 6mm needs 180220 amps. Set AC frequency to 60120 Hz (higher for thin material). Set balance (cleaning action) to 3035% electrode positive. For MIG, use spray transfer mode if possible: voltage 2228V, wire speed 610 m/min. Use 100% argon gas.

Step 3: Prepare tungsten. Grind the tungsten to a point for AC TIGbut for aluminium, a slightly blunted tip works better. Use a dedicated grinder for tungsten only. Stickout should be 36mm. For MIG, use a spool gun with a teflon liner to prevent wire binding.

Step 4: Tack weld. Clamp parts firmly. Use a higher amperage for tacking to ensure penetration. Tack every 2550mm. Check alignment. For thin material, use a heat sink (aluminium or copper block) to prevent distortion.

Step 5: Weld. For TIG, start with a foot pedal or torch switch. Create a puddle quickly, then add filler rod. Move fastaluminium conducts heat away, so you need speed. Use a push angle of 1015 degrees. For MIG, push the gun at a 1015 degree angle. Maintain a short arc. Travel speed: 200400 mm/min for TIG, 400800 mm/min for MIG. Watch the puddle; if it looks dull and sluggish, increase amperage. If it boils, reduce. For TIG, add filler with a dabbing motion, not continuous. For MIG, use a slight back-and-forth motion to help fusion.

Step 6: Finish and inspect. After welding, clean the weld with a stainless brush. Check for cracks, porosity, or lack of fusion. A good aluminium weld is bright and shiny with a stacked dimes appearance for TIG, or a smooth bead for MIG. If you see black soot, that is oxide from too much cleaning actionreduce balance. If you see porosity, clean better and check gas flow.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Welding dirty aluminium. Aluminium oxide melts at 2,070C, so if you don't remove it, you get a dirty, porous weld. Fix: Clean with acetone and a stainless steel brush immediately before welding. Never use a brush that has touched steel.

Mistake 2: Using the wrong polarity. For TIG, you need AC, not DC. DC will not break up the oxide layer. For MIG, use DCEP (electrode positive). Fix: Set your machine to AC for TIG, and check polarity for MIG.

Mistake 3: Too much heat or too little. Aluminium conducts heat fast, so beginners often use too little amperage and get cold laps. Others use too much and burn through. Fix: Start with recommended amperage for thickness. If the puddle is sluggish, increase by 10 amps. If it collapses, reduce. Use a foot pedal for TIG to control heat.

Mistake 4: Wrong filler metal. Using 4043 on a 5356 base can cause cracking. Fix: Match filler to base alloy. For 6061, use 5356. For castings, use 4043. If unsure, use 4043 for general work.

Mistake 5: Poor gas coverage. Aluminium is sensitive to contamination. Too little gas flow causes porosity; too much causes turbulence. Fix: Set flow to 1015 CFH for TIG, 2030 CFH for MIG. Use a gas lens for TIG to improve coverage.

Mistake 6: Contaminated tungsten. If the tungsten touches the puddle, it contaminates the weld. Fix: Stop, remove tungsten, break off contaminated tip, regrind, and restart. Keep a dedicated grinder.

Mistake 7: Travel speed too slow. Slow travel overheats the metal, causing burn-through and wide, weak welds. Fix: Move faster. For TIG, aim for 200400 mm/min. For MIG, 400800 mm/min. Practice on scrap.

Mistake 8: Not using a heat sink. Thin aluminium warps easily. Fix: Clamp a thick aluminium or copper block behind the weld to absorb heat. Or use a chill bar.

Mistake 9: Ignoring safety. Aluminium welding produces ozone and fumes. Fix: Weld in a well-ventilated area or use a fume extractor. Wear a respirator if needed.

Mistake 10: Skipping practice. Aluminium is unforgiving. Fix: Practice on scrap pieces of the same thickness and alloy before welding your project. Spend at least an hour practicing beads.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety first. Aluminium welding produces intense ultraviolet light, ozone, and metal fumes. Always wear a welding helmet with a shade 1013 lens. For TIG, use shade 1011; for MIG, shade 1113. Never look at the arc without protection. Wear leather gloves, a flame-resistant jacket, and closed-toe shoes. Avoid synthetic clothingit melts and sticks to skin.

Ventilation is critical. Aluminium welding releases ozone and nitrogen oxides, which can cause respiratory irritation. Work outdoors or in a well-ventilated shop. If indoors, use a fume extractor or a fan to blow fumes away from your breathing zone. A respirator with P100 filters is recommended for prolonged welding. In Africa, many workshops are open-air, which helps, but still position yourself upwind.

Electrical safety: Check your cables and torch for damage. Ensure proper grounding. Do not weld in wet conditions. Use a GFCI if available. Keep your work area dry and organized. Store gas cylinders upright and secured. Argon is an asphyxiantif it leaks in a confined space, it displaces oxygen. Always open the cylinder valve slowly and check for leaks with soapy water.

Fire safety: Aluminium welding produces sparks and hot spatter. Keep a fire extinguisher nearby (dry powder or CO2). Remove flammable materials from the area. Never weld near fuel, paint, or solvents. Have a bucket of sand or water ready. After welding, check for hot slag and let parts cool before handling.

Best practices: Maintain your equipment. Clean your torch tip, replace worn parts, and check gas lines. Use the right amperage and travel speed. Keep your tungsten sharp and clean. Practice good posture to avoid fatigue. Take breaks to prevent eye strain and heat stress. Stay hydratedwelding in hot African climates can cause dehydration.

Finally, respect the metal. Aluminium is soft and can be easily damaged. Use proper clamping to avoid dents. Do not overheat. If you are unsure, practice on scrap. Always inspect your weldslook for cracks, porosity, and incomplete fusion. A good weld is strong and leak-free. Remember, safety is not just about you; it's about everyone in your workshop. Set a good example.

6. Practical Exercise

Exercise: TIG weld a 3mm aluminium butt joint. You will need two pieces of 3mm aluminium plate (100mm x 50mm each), 4043 filler rod (2.4mm), acetone, stainless steel brush, AC/DC TIG welder, argon gas, and safety gear.

Step 1: Clean both plates with acetone, then brush with a stainless steel brush in one direction. Clamp them on a clean aluminium or steel table with a 1mm gap between them. Use a heat sink (aluminium block) behind the joint to prevent warping.

Step 2: Set your TIG machine to AC, 120 amps, frequency 80 Hz, balance 30% electrode positive. Set gas flow to 12 CFH. Use a 2.4mm lanthanated tungsten, ground to a blunt point. Stickout 4mm.

Step 3: Tack weld at both ends and in the middle. Use 140 amps for tacking to ensure penetration. Check alignment.

Step 4: Weld the joint. Start at one end. Create a puddle, then add filler. Use a push angle of 15 degrees. Move at a steady speedabout 250 mm/min. Add filler with a dabbing motion. Watch the puddle: it should be shiny and fluid. If it looks dull, increase amps by 10. If it boils, reduce. Weld the entire length. Do not stop in the middle; if you must, overlap the restart by 10mm.

Step 5: Inspect. After cooling, brush the weld with a stainless brush. Look for a smooth, shiny bead with no porosity or cracks. If you see black soot, reduce balance to 25%. If you see lack of fusion, increase amps or slow down slightly.

Step 6: Test strength. Cut the weld crosswise and inspect penetration. You should see full penetration with no voids. If not, adjust and try again. Repeat this exercise at least three times. Then try a T-joint and a lap joint. For MIG, use a spool gun with 1.0mm 4043 wire, voltage 24V, wire speed 7 m/min. Practice beads on scrap first.

This exercise builds muscle memory. In Africa, you can sell this skill: repair aluminium boats, fabricate window frames, or fix truck bodies. Charge by the inch or by the job. A 3mm butt joint like this is the foundation for all aluminium welding. Master it, and you can tackle any project.

<h3>Why Aluminium Is Differentand Why It Matters for African Fabricators</h3>

<p>
Aluminium has an oxide layer (AlO) that melts at roughly 2,072C, while the base metal melts at about 660C. This oxide layer
must be removed or disrupted during welding, or you will get incomplete fusion, porosity, and weak joints. Aluminium also has
high thermal conductivityabout five times that of steelso heat dissipates quickly, requiring higher heat input and faster
travel speeds than many welders expect. Its thermal expansion is roughly twice that of steel, which increases distortion risk
and makes fixturing and tack sequencing critical.
</p>

<p>
In African markets, these technical facts translate directly into business outcomes. A workshop that can consistently produce
leak-tight aluminium fuel tanks, structurally sound canopies, or food-grade processing equipment can serve sectors where
aluminium's corrosion resistance and weight advantages are decisive: transport, marine, food and beverage, and solar mounting.
The alternativeimporting finished aluminium componentsis expensive, slow, and vulnerable to supply-chain disruption.
</p>

<h3>Step-by-Step Advanced Method</h3>

<ol>
<li><strong>Identify the alloy.</strong> Use a spark test, chemical marker, or mill certificate. Never assume scrap aluminium is 6061. Wrong filler selection is the most common cause of cracking.</li>
<li><strong>Clean mechanically and chemically.</strong> Remove oxides with a dedicated stainless steel brush (never used on steel), then wipe with acetone or isopropyl alcohol. Do this within 30 minutes of welding.</li>
<li><strong>Set up AC TIG.</strong> Select AC, set frequency 60120 Hz, balance 6575% EN, and amperage from Table 2. Use a 2% thoriated or lanthanated tungsten with a balled or pointed tip depending on your machine.</li>
<li><strong>Tack strategically.</strong> Use short tacks spaced to control distortion. For long seams, use backstep tacking and consider a heat sink or chill bar.</li>
<li><strong>Weld with consistent travel.</strong> Maintain a short arc, push the puddle, and feed filler smoothly. Watch the puddle, not the arc. Increase travel speed as the workpiece heats up.</li>
<li><strong>Control distortion.</strong> Use clamps, fixtures, and balanced weld sequences. For thin material, consider skip welding or alternating sides.</li>
<li><strong>Inspect and finish.</strong> Check for porosity, incomplete fusion, and cracks. Clean the weld, remove discoloration if required, and apply post-weld treatment where specified.</li>
</ol>

<div style="border:2px solid #b03a2e;background:#fdecea;padding:14px;margin:18px 0;border-radius:6px">
<strong style="color:#b03a2e"> SAFETY WARNING:</strong>
Aluminium welding produces intense ultraviolet radiation and can generate ozone and metal fume. Always use a welding helmet with
appropriate shade (minimum shade 1013 for TIG), proper ventilation or fume extraction, and flame-resistant clothing. Never weld
aluminium containers that have held fuel, solvents, or unknown chemicals without proper purging and testing. In many African
workshops, ventilation is inadequateinvest in local exhaust ventilation or weld outdoors where safe. Incomplete purging of tanks
has caused fatal explosions in fabrication yards.
</div>

<h3>Common Mistakes and Fixes</h3>

<ul>
<li><strong>Porosity:</strong> Usually caused by contamination, moisture, or insufficient gas flow. Fix by cleaning, using dry argon, and checking gas lines for leaks.</li>
<li><strong>Cracking:</strong> Often from wrong filler or excessive restraint. Use 4043 for crack-sensitive alloys or 5356 for higher strength; preheat thick sections if needed.</li>
<li><strong>Incomplete fusion:</strong> Caused by low heat input, fast travel, or oxide entrapment. Increase amperage, slow down, and ensure proper cleaning.</li>
<li><strong>Distortion:</strong> Caused by high heat input and poor fixturing. Use tacks, clamps, and balanced sequences; consider pulse welding.</li>
<li><strong>Tungsten contamination:</strong> Dipping the tungsten in the puddle. Fix by maintaining arc length and practicing puddle control.</li>
</ul>
