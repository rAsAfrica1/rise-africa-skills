Vegetable Farming

A complete 6-month program building a production LINE, not one crop — beds, nurseries, succession planting, safe IPM and grading that turn 500 m² into weekly income all year.

⏱️ 6 Months (multiple crop cycles)📊 Beginner → Business-ready🌍 Hybrid: Online + Offline🎓 Certificate on completion

Course Overview

Why 6 months? Because vegetables are not one crop — they are a production LINE. Leafy greens (rape, covo, tsunga, spinach, lettuce) harvest in 4–8 weeks; brassicas (cabbage, kale, broccoli) in 10–14 weeks; carrots and beetroot in 12–16. Six months lets you run several overlapping crops and learn the real skill: a bed always in production and something to sell every single week. That weekly cash is what makes vegetables the best cash-flow farming a beginner can do.

Structure: 6 monthly modules → lessons → practical assignments → a 10-question quiz each module → final business project.

How to study (hybrid): Online — modules on your phone, sessions in the Study Diary, questions to the rAs AI assistant. Offline — save this page (Print → Save as PDF); the practicals happen at the beds with a watering can and a notebook.

⚠️ Read first: the three rules that protect your investment

  • Vegetables are eaten raw or barely cooked — pesticide discipline is food safety. Registered products only, at label rates, and NEVER pick inside the pre-harvest interval. Leafy greens harvested every few days are the easiest crop in the world to poison a customer with.
  • Water quality is not optional. Irrigating salad crops with sewage-contaminated water spreads cholera and typhoid. Use clean water sources, and never irrigate leafy crops with raw wastewater.
  • Never spend before the Iron-Clad Price Check: seed, manure and nets from 3 suppliers. Vegetable margins live and die on input cost per bed.
MONTH 1 — The Vegetable Business, Crop Choice & Site ▾

Lesson 1.1 — Why vegetables beat most crops for a beginner

  • Speed: money in 6–10 weeks, not 6 months. A small plot recycles capital 4–8 times a year while a maize field turns over once.
  • Small area, real income: 500 m² of intensively grown vegetables, sold well, can out-earn a hectare of grain — vegetables are farmed by the square metre, not the hectare.
  • Constant demand: every household buys greens weekly; schools, hospitals, lodges and supermarkets buy daily.
  • The trade-off you must accept: vegetables need WATER and ATTENTION every week of the year. No irrigation, no vegetable business.

Lesson 1.2 — The crop menu and their clocks

CropSeed to harvestHarvest styleNote
Rape / covo / tsunga (African greens)4–6 weeksRepeat-pick leaves for 2–4 monthsThe cash-flow backbone; huge everyday demand
Spinach (Swiss chard)6–8 weeksRepeat-pick for monthsHardy, heat-tolerant, long earner
Lettuce6–8 weeksSingle cutPremium buyers (lodges, supermarkets); needs cool/shade in heat

  • Beginner mix that works: 60% fast greens (weekly cash), 30% cabbage/brassica (bulk lump sums), 10% experiment (lettuce/broccoli for premium buyers).

Lesson 1.3 — Site, soil and water

  • Full sun (6+ hours), gentle slope, deep soil, pH 6.0–7.0; near the house for security and near the water source for sanity.
  • Water need: roughly 4–6 mm/day at peak — for a 100 m² bed block that's about 400–600 litres daily. Test your source's dry-season reliability BEFORE planting.
  • Fence against goats, chickens and thieves — an unfenced vegetable garden is a donation.
  • Rotation groups from day one: leafy (brassicas) → fruiting (tomato/beans) → root (carrot/onion) → legume, never repeating a family within 2–3 seasons on the same bed.

Lesson 1.4 — Market first, seed second

  • Walk your market: which greens sell fastest, in what bundle size, at what price, and who is short of supply on which days?
  • Institutional buyers (schools, hospitals, lodges, canteens) want RELIABLE weekly volumes and will pay steadily — they are the small grower's best contract and are usually under-served.
  • Write your target: "X kg of greens and Y heads of cabbage per week to named buyers" — then design the bed plan to produce exactly that.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 1): (1) Market survey: 5 buyers (2 institutional), with prices, volumes and buying days. (2) Test your water source's dry-season flow and measure your available area. (3) Draft the bed plan and rotation groups on paper with your 60/30/10 crop mix.

MONTH 2 — Beds, Soil Fertility & the Nursery ▾

Lesson 2.1 — Building beds that work

  • Standard bed: 1–1.2 m wide (so you can reach the middle from both sides without stepping on it), any length, paths 40–50 cm. NEVER walk on a bed — compacted soil is dead soil.
  • Raised beds (15–25 cm) where drainage is poor or rain is heavy; sunken beds in very dry areas to hold water. Same width rules.
  • Double-dig or deep-loosen new beds once (fork to 30 cm) — root crops in particular repay it; after that, minimum disturbance keeps structure and organic matter.

Lesson 2.2 — Feeding the soil

  • Compost/manure at 3–5 kg per m² (30–50 t/ha) worked into each bed before planting — vegetables are heavy feeders and organic matter is what holds water and nutrients in sandy African soils.
  • Basal fertiliser for hungry crops (cabbage, spinach): a compound at roughly 30–50 g/m², banded and covered — never left touching seed or roots.
  • Liquid feeds for repeat-pick greens: manure tea (a sack of manure steeped in a drum for 1–2 weeks, diluted to weak-tea colour) applied fortnightly keeps leaves coming — the cheapest yield boost in vegetable growing.
  • Compost making is the whole system's engine — see the rAs Composting course; a grower who makes compost cuts input costs permanently.

Lesson 2.3 — The nursery for transplanted crops

  • Direct-sown: carrot, beetroot, radish, beans, and often African greens. Transplanted: cabbage, kale, broccoli, lettuce, onion, tomato.
  • Nursery bed 1 m wide with sterilised or fresh clean soil + compost; shade at 40–50% for the first 2 weeks; insect net where whitefly/aphid virus pressure exists.
  • Seedlings transplant at 4–6 true leaves (3–5 weeks for brassicas); harden off 5–7 days by reducing water and removing shade.
  • Sow little and often — a nursery sown every 2–3 weeks feeds the continuous production line that Module 3 builds.

Lesson 2.4 — Seed: quality and quantity

  • Certified, dated seed from registered dealers; store what's left cool, dark and dry in a sealed jar (viability falls fast in African heat).
  • Rough guides per 100 m²: greens 30–60 g direct-sown; cabbage 3–5 g in nursery (≈ 300–400 seedlings); carrot 60–100 g direct-sown.
  • Open-pollinated varieties let you save seed on some crops (see the rAs Seed Saving course); hybrids give uniformity and vigour — decide crop by crop.

MONTH 3 — Planting, Spacing & the Succession System ▾

Lesson 3.1 — Spacing (yield is a spacing decision)

CropSpacingPlants per 10 m² bed
Rape/covo/tsunga30 × 30 cm≈110
Spinach/chard30 × 30 cm≈110
Lettuce25–30 × 25–30 cm≈110–160
Cabbage50–60 × 40–50 cm≈35–50
Kale45 × 45 cm≈50
CarrotRows 20–25 cm, thin to 4–5 cm≈800–1,000
BeetrootRows 25–30 cm, thin to 8–10 cm≈350–450
  • Thinning is not optional for root crops: unthinned carrots produce a bed of hairy sticks. Thin at 3–4 true leaves, in the evening, watering after.

Lesson 3.2 — Transplanting and direct sowing technique

  • Transplant late afternoon; water the nursery first; plant to the same depth (brassicas can go slightly deeper), firm gently, water immediately.
  • Direct sowing: shallow furrows (seed depth ≈ 2–3× seed diameter — tiny seeds barely covered), water gently with a fine rose, and keep the surface moist until emergence.
  • Mulch between plants once established: grass, leaves or straw — water saved, weeds suppressed, soil cooled, splash-disease reduced.

Lesson 3.3 — Succession: the weekly-income engine

  • The rule: plant a NEW small batch every 2–3 weeks rather than one big planting. Ten beds planted on the same day give one glut and nine idle weeks; ten beds staggered give ten harvests.
  • Build a bed calendar: each bed gets a row per month showing crop, planting date, expected first harvest, and expected clearing date — this one page is the difference between a garden and a business.
  • Replant a cleared bed within days: add compost, replant a DIFFERENT family, and the ground never rests idle in the growing season.

Lesson 3.4 — Weeding and water routine

  • Weed early and shallow — vegetables lose more to weeds in their first 3 weeks than at any other time; a hoe used weekly beats a machete used monthly.
  • Water deeply and less often rather than a daily sprinkle: shallow watering grows shallow roots that collapse the first hot day. Morning watering; evening acceptable; midday wasteful.
  • Check moisture by finger: 3–5 cm down should feel damp. Leafy crops wilt visibly when short — but chronic mild stress (which you can't see) is what silently costs yield.

MONTH 4 — Pests & Diseases the Safe Way ▾

Lesson 4.1 — The pest gallery

PestSignsManagement

NematodesStunting, galled rootsRotation (marigold, grasses), organic matter, resistant varieties

Lesson 4.2 — Diseases

  • Damping off (seedlings collapse at soil line): sterile media, don't overwater, thin sowing, good airflow.
  • Downy/powdery mildew (yellow patches with fuzz beneath / white powder on top): spacing and airflow, water at the base, morning watering, resistant varieties.
  • Black rot in brassicas (V-shaped yellow leaf-edge lesions with black veins): certified seed, rotation, remove infected plants, avoid working plants when wet.
  • Bacterial and fungal leaf spots: sanitation, rotation, mulch to stop soil splash, and copper-based products where registered.

Lesson 4.3 — Integrated pest management (IPM), the vegetable grower's method

  • Prevention layer: rotation, healthy soil, correct spacing, clean seed, insect netting, and removing crop residues that host pests.
  • Monitoring layer: scout twice weekly, undersides of leaves, and act on thresholds — a few aphids are food for ladybirds, not an emergency.
  • Biological/low-toxicity layer FIRST: soap sprays, neem, Bt for caterpillars, garlic/chilli extracts, sticky traps, hand-picking on small plots.
  • Chemical layer LAST: registered product for the identified pest, correct dose, rotate groups, and always the PHI. On vegetables picked every 3 days, choose products with SHORT pre-harvest intervals or don't spray at all.

Lesson 4.4 — Organic and low-cost protection recipes

  • Soap spray: pure soap (not detergent with additives) at roughly 1–2 tablespoons per 5 L water for aphids, whitefly and mites — spray in cool hours, rinse-sensitive crops tested first.
  • Neem: crushed seed/leaf extract steeped overnight, strained, sprayed weekly — repels and disrupts many chewing and sucking pests.
  • Companion planting: onions/garlic among brassicas confuse pests; marigold suppresses nematodes; flowering herbs feed the predators that work free for you.
  • Physical: insect net over hoops on brassica beds is the single most effective diamondback-moth solution and needs no chemicals at all.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 4): (1) Scout twice weekly for a month and keep a pest log with counts, not impressions. (2) Make and test a soap spray and a neem spray on infested plants; record results after 3 and 7 days. (3) Build one netted brassica bed and compare its damage against an unnetted bed.

MODULE 5 — Harvest, Quality & Post-Harvest Handling ▾

Lesson 5.1 — Harvesting each crop right

  • Repeat-pick greens: take the outer mature leaves and leave the growing point — a rape or spinach plant harvested correctly feeds you for 2–4 months; stripped bare, it dies.
  • Cabbage: cut when the head is firm to a squeeze; over-mature heads split. Leave a few wrapper leaves for protection.
  • Lettuce: cut whole at full size before bolting (heat makes it shoot up and turn bitter).
  • Carrots/beetroot: lift when shoulders show at the size your market wants; water the bed first to lift without breaking.
  • Time of day: harvest in the COOL early morning. Vegetables picked at midday have already lost water and shelf life before they reach the crate.

Lesson 5.2 — The cold-chain-free freshness rules

  • Into shade within minutes; never leave harvested greens in the sun — 30 minutes of sun costs a day of shelf life.
  • Wash only in CLEAN water (contaminated wash water infects a whole batch), drain well, and never pack wet greens into sealed bags — they rot.
  • Sprinkle-and-cover with damp sacking for transport of leafy greens; use ventilated crates, never stuffed sacks.
  • Evaporative cooling (clay-pot or charcoal cooler — see the rAs Cold Storage course) can add 2–4 days of life without electricity.

Lesson 5.3 — Grading and bundling for price

  • Grade every harvest: A-grade (uniform, clean, undamaged) for premium buyers; B-grade for local/market sale; damaged for home or livestock. Mixing grades drags your whole price to the lowest item in the crate.
  • Bundle to the local standard — customers buy by the familiar bundle, so match it and make yours visibly fuller/fresher than your competitor's.
  • Presentation is free money: clean leaves, neat bundles, uniform crates. The same vegetables presented well earn 20–40% more from the same buyer.

Lesson 5.4 — Reducing losses

  • Harvest to ORDER wherever possible — cut what is sold, not what is ready; standing crops keep better in the ground than in a heap.
  • Process or preserve surplus: solar-dried greens and vegetable powders (rAs Solar Drying), fermented/pickled vegetables, or feed to livestock — never dump.
  • Track losses weekly (harvested vs sold) — the number always surprises new growers and always shrinks once measured.

MODULE 6 — Marketing, Economics & Your Business Plan ▾

Lesson 6.1 — Where to sell and what each demands

  • Roadside/farm gate: zero transport, good prices, limited volume — great for greens.
  • Market vendors: volume and speed, lower price; build 2–3 relationships and supply reliably.
  • Institutions (schools, hospitals, lodges, canteens): steady weekly orders and stable prices — they need consistency more than cheapness. Approach with a written weekly supply offer.
  • Supermarkets: highest standards (grading, packaging, spray records, invoicing) and slower payment — the goal for a maturing business.
  • Direct/WhatsApp box scheme: weekly mixed vegetable boxes to households — highest price per kg, builds loyal customers, and your production plan makes the box.

Lesson 6.3 — Records that run the garden

  • Bed calendar (crop, dates), harvest book (kg per bed per week), sales book (channel, price, buyer), and the input book. Four cheap notebooks that turn a garden into a business.
  • Compute cost per kg and margin per bed each month — you'll quickly discover which crops actually pay on YOUR land and market, and the plan shifts toward them.

  • Assemble: site and water assessment, bed layout and rotation plan, 12-month succession calendar, input budget with quotes, IPM protocol, harvest/grading standards, buyer list with weekly volumes and prices, risk plan (water failure, pest outbreak, price glut), and the expansion path (more beds, netting, shade structure, or a box scheme).
  • Submit with photos and your four record books for your rAs certificate — this plan is exactly what horticulture support schemes and institutional buyers ask to see.

📖 Glossary

  • Succession planting — staggered small plantings for continuous harvest.
  • Bed calendar — the per-bed schedule of crops and dates.
  • Hardening off — toughening seedlings before transplant.
  • Thinning — removing surplus seedlings to correct spacing.
  • Repeat-pick — harvesting outer leaves over months.
  • IPM — integrated pest management; chemicals last.
  • Bt — biological caterpillar control.
  • PHI — pre-harvest interval after spraying.
  • Bolting — premature flowering (bitter lettuce, split cabbage).
  • Manure tea — liquid feed steeped from manure.
