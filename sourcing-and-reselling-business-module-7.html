Lessons

1. Choosing Shipping Carriers and Services

Choosing the right shipping carrier is critical to your reselling profit. In Africa, options vary by country. In Nigeria, use GIG Logistics, Kwik Delivery, or Sendbox for local deliveries. In Kenya, pick Pickup Mtaani, Sendy, or G4S. In South Africa, The Courier Guy, PostNet, and Pargo are reliable. For international shipping, DHL, FedEx, and UPS are common but expensive. Always compare rates: a 1kg package from Lagos to Accra via DHL costs about $50, while local courier GIG charges around 3,000 ($4) for same-city delivery. For small items, use registered mail through your national postal serviceits cheaper but slower. Consider speed: customers expect delivery within 2-5 days locally. Offer standard (3-5 days) and express (1-2 days) options. Track everything: use carriers with online tracking and SMS updates. Negotiate rates once you ship over 20 packages per month. Also, check insurance: for high-value items, pay extra 1-2% of item value. Test carriers with a few shipments before committing. Ask other resellers in Facebook groups like Jumia Sellers or Takealot Resellers for recommendations. Always keep receipts and tracking numbers. Avoid cash-on-delivery unless you trust the carrier; many lose money on returns. Remember, reliable shipping builds repeat customers.

2. Calculating Shipping Costs and Labels

To price shipping, you need three numbers: weight, dimensions, and destination. Use a digital scale (5,000 on Jumia) and a tape measure. For local shipping in Nigeria, most couriers charge by weight: first 1kg costs 1,500-2,500, each additional kg 500. Volumetric weight applies for bulky items: (length x width x height in cm) / 5000. For example, a box 30x20x10cm = 6000/5000 = 1.2kg, so you pay for 2kg. Always round up. For international, DHL charges about $30 for 0.5kg to UK. Use online calculators: DHL Express, FedEx, or local courier websites. Create labels using free tools: Sendbox, ShipStation, or even Google Docs with a barcode. Include: sender address, receiver address, phone numbers, tracking number, and weight. Print on adhesive paper or tape a paper label securely. For FBA, you must use Amazons Partner Carrier or approved couriers and print FBA labels from Seller Central. Never guess costsundercharging eats your profit. Add 10% buffer for fuel surcharges and remote area fees. For returns, pre-print return labels and include them in the package. Keep a shipping log in Excel: date, order ID, carrier, cost, tracking. Review monthly to spot overcharges. Many resellers lose money by not weighing accurately. Buy a scale today.

3. Packaging Best Practices for Safe Delivery

Packaging protects your product and your reputation. In Africa, rough handling is common, so over-pack. Use double-walled cardboard boxes for fragile items. For electronics, wrap in bubble wrap (2,000 per roll) and use foam peanuts or shredded paper. For clothing, use poly mailers (500 for 50)theyre waterproof and cheap. For liquids, seal in a ziplock bag, then wrap in bubble wrap, and mark This Side Up. Use strong tape: packing tape (1,000 per roll) not cellotape. For international shipping, customs may open packages, so include a packing slip inside with item description and value. Never use old newspapers as paddingthey get soggy and ink rubs off. For FBA, follow Amazons strict rules: no loose items, poly bag items smaller than 5cm, and suffocation warning labels. Use a thermal label printer (50,000) for professional labels. For local deliveries, use courier-provided bags if available. Test your packaging: drop the box from waist heightif it survives, good. Add Fragile stickers. For high-value items, use tamper-evident bags. Keep packaging costs low: buy in bulk from Alibaba or local suppliers like Packaging Nigeria. Reuse boxes from your own online orders. Always include a thank-you card and return instructions. Good packaging reduces damage claims and returns, saving you money and stress.

4. Using Fulfillment by Amazon (FBA)

FBA lets you store products in Amazon warehouses; they pick, pack, and ship for you. This is powerful for African resellers selling to the US, UK, or Germany. But it has strict rules. First, you need an Amazon Seller Central account (professional plan $39.99/month). Create a shipment plan: choose products, print FBA labels (using thermal printer), and box them. Each box must have an FBA box ID label. Ship via Amazons partnered carriers (DHL, UPS) or use a freight forwarder like Shipbymail or MyUS. From Nigeria, shipping 10kg to Amazon US costs about $150 via DHL. Amazon charges fulfillment fees: for a 1kg standard-size item, about $5.50. Storage fees: $0.75 per cubic foot per month (Jan-Sep) and higher in Q4. You must comply with expiration dates, safety, and labeling. For African sellers, the biggest challenge is customs and import duties. Use a freight forwarder who handles customs. Also, you must have a US tax ID (EIN) or use Amazons tax calculation service. FBA can boost sales with Prime badge, but only if you manage inventory well. Avoid long-term storage fees: keep 2-3 months of stock. Use Amazons inventory performance index (IPI) to monitor. If you sell in Europe, you need VAT registration. Start small: test with 20-30 units. Many Africans succeed with FBA by selling African prints, shea butter, or crafts. But calculate all fees before committing.

5. International Shipping and Customs

International shipping from Africa is expensive and paperwork-heavy. You need a commercial invoice, packing list, and customs declaration. For each shipment, declare the correct valueunder-declaring is illegal and can lead to seizure. Use HS codes (Harmonized System) for your products. For example, shea butter HS code 1515.90. Customs duties vary: US charges 0-10% depending on item; EU charges 0-17% plus VAT (20% in UK). African exporters can use AGOA (US) or EU-ACP agreements for reduced duties, but you need certificates of origin. For shipping, use DHL, FedEx, or freight forwarders like Bollore Logistics or Seko Logistics. For small packages under 2kg, registered mail via your post office is cheapest (e.g., Nigeria Post EMS to UK: 15,000 for 1kg, 7-14 days). Always include a commercial invoice in a clear plastic pouch on the outside. Mark Commercial Sample only if true. For returns, customs can be a nightmareoften cheaper to refund and let customer keep item. Use tools like Zonos or Avalara for duty calculation. Consider using a 3PL in the destination country to avoid customs delays. For African sellers, a common route is to ship to a US address (like a freight forwarder) then reship. Always keep tracking and proof of export. Customs delays of 2-4 weeks are normal. Factor in 15-20% extra for duties and taxes. Never lie on customs formsit can destroy your business.

6. Handling Returns and Refunds Efficiently

Returns are part of reselling. In Africa, return rates are lower (2-5%) than in the US (15-30%), but you must have a policy. State clearly: Returns accepted within 7 days if item is unused and in original packaging. Buyer pays return shipping. For defective items, you pay return shipping. Use a return authorization (RA) number. For local returns, use a courier like GIG or Pickup Mtaani to pick up from customer. For international, its often cheaper to refund without returnespecially for low-value items. For FBA, Amazon handles returns; you can choose to have items returned to you or disposed. Set up automated refunds in your store (Shopify, WooCommerce) to save time. When a return arrives, inspect within 48 hours. If resellable, restock; if not, claim insurance or write off. Keep a returns log: order ID, reason, action, cost. Analyze reasons: wrong size, damaged, not as described. Fix root causes: better photos, accurate descriptions, stronger packaging. For refunds, use the same payment method. In Nigeria, Paystack and Flutterwave make refunds easy. For cash-on-delivery orders, refund via bank transfer. Always communicate with the customera happy return experience can turn a negative into a loyal customer. Offer a discount code on next purchase. Track return rate per product; if a product has >10% returns, stop selling it. Efficient returns management protects your profit and reputation.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Shipping and Fulfillment for African Sourcing and Reselling Businesses

Shipping and fulfillment is where most African reselling businesses either protect their margins or lose them. Whether you are sourcing from Guangzhou, Dubai, or Nairobi and reselling into Lagos, Accra, Johannesburg, or Nairobi, the decisions you make about carriers, packaging, customs, and returns determine your reputation and your profit. This practical reference gives you the operational detail you need to move goods reliably and affordably.

Choosing Shipping Carriers and Services

African resellers typically choose between three carrier categories: international express couriers (DHL, FedEx, UPS, Aramex), postal and consolidated freight services (national postal operators, EMS, freight forwarders), and regional last-mile providers (GIG Logistics, Kwik, Sendy, Bwala, Speedaf). Express couriers offer speed and tracking but cost 35 more than postal or consolidated options. Freight forwarders consolidate multiple sellers' cargo into one container or air pallet, cutting per-kilogram cost dramatically for bulk orders.

For small, high-value items such as phone accessories or cosmetics, express is often worth it. For bulky, low-margin goods such as household plastics or fabric, sea freight via a forwarder is the only viable route. Many Nigerian and Kenyan resellers use a hybrid: air freight for fast-moving SKUs and sea freight for slow-moving bulk.

Calculating Shipping Costs and Labels

Carriers charge by volumetric weight or actual weight, whichever is greater. The formula for air freight is: (Length Width Height in cm) 5000 = volumetric kg. For sea freight, divide by 6000 or use CBM (cubic meter) pricing. Always compare the two before booking. A box of pillows may weigh 2 kg but occupy 0.05 CBM, making it expensive to ship by air.

Labels must include sender and receiver details, tracking number, weight, and customs declaration. Mislabeled parcels are the leading cause of customs delays at Murtala Muhammed, JKIA, and OR Tambo. Use thermal label printers or the carrier's own label portal to avoid smudged handwriting.

Packaging Best Practices for Safe Delivery

Packaging is not just protection; it is brand experience and cost control. Use double-walled corrugated boxes for fragile items, bubble wrap for electronics, and moisture barriers (poly bags or silica gel) for goods crossing humid coastal regions. Avoid oversized boxes: you pay for air, not for emptiness. Label "FRAGILE" and "THIS SIDE UP" in English and French for Francophone markets.

For cosmetics and liquids, seal caps with tape and place in leak-proof bags. For fashion, use poly mailers to reduce volumetric weight. Always include a packing slip with order number and a return address inside the parcel.

Using Fulfillment by Amazon (FBA)

FBA is available in select African markets through Amazon's global programs, but most African resellers use it via a US or UK entity. You ship inventory to an Amazon fulfillment center, and Amazon handles storage, packing, shipping, and customer service. Fees include storage, pick-and-pack, and weight-based fulfillment fees. FBA is powerful for Prime eligibility and Buy Box wins, but it is capital-intensive and requires accurate inventory forecasting.

For African sellers, FBA works best for lightweight, high-margin products already compliant with destination-country regulations. Always calculate FBA fees against your landed cost before committing inventory.

International Shipping and Customs

Customs is the single biggest risk in cross-border reselling. Every shipment needs a commercial invoice, packing list, HS code, and certificate of origin where applicable. Duties and VAT are calculated on CIF value (Cost + Insurance + Freight). In Nigeria, the Nigeria Customs Service applies duty rates from 5% to 35% plus 7.5% VAT. Kenya's KRA uses EAC CET rates. South Africa's SARS applies ad valorem duties plus 15% VAT.

Under-declaring value to reduce duty is illegal and can lead to seizure, fines, and blacklisting. Use a licensed customs agent and keep records for at least five years.

SAFETY AND LEGAL WARNING:

Never ship prohibited or restricted goods such as counterfeit branded products, unregistered pharmaceuticals, ivory, or undeclared cash. In Nigeria, Kenya, and South Africa, customs authorities can seize goods, impose fines up to 300% of duty payable, and pursue criminal charges. Counterfeit goods also expose you to trademark lawsuits from brands. Always verify HS codes and import permits with a licensed customs agent before shipping.

Handling Returns and Refunds Efficiently

Returns are inevitable. A clear, fair policy reduces disputes and protects your reputation. Offer a 714 day return window for defective items, require photo evidence, and process refunds within 48 hours of receiving the returned item. For low-value items, it is often cheaper to issue a partial refund and let the customer keep the product than to pay return shipping.

Track return reasons in a simple spreadsheet: sizing, damage, wrong item, or buyer's remorse. Patterns reveal sourcing or packaging problems you can fix upstream.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Compare volumetric vs actual weight before booking any shipment.
  • Confirm HS codes and duty rates with a licensed customs agent before importing.
  • Use double-walled boxes and moisture barriers for fragile or humid-route goods.
  • Print thermal labels with tracking numbers; never handwrite labels.
  • Keep commercial invoices, packing lists, and receipts for at least five years.
  • Set a clear 714 day return policy and process refunds within 48 hours.
  • Track return reasons monthly to fix sourcing and packaging issues.
  • Never under-declare value or ship counterfeit or restricted goods.
