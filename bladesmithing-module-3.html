1. Knife Anatomy: Point, Edge, Spine, and Tang

A knife is made of distinct parts, each with a job. The point is the sharp tip, used for piercing. The edge is the sharpened cutting side. The spine is the thick, unsharpened back of the blade, giving strength. The tang is the part of the steel that extends into the handle. In Africa, you see these parts on a Moroccan koummya or a Congolese machete. The point can be dropped (curved down to the edge), clip (a notch on the spine), or spear (centered). The edge can be plain or serrated. The spine can be straight or curved. The tang can be full (extends all the way through the handle), half, or hidden (a stick tang). For a strong working knife, a full tang is best. For a light slicer, a half tang works. The handle scales (wood, bone, or horn) are attached to the tang with rivets or epoxy. In many African knives, the tang is burned into a wooden handle for a tight fit. Knowing these parts helps you design a knife that works. For example, a skinning knife needs a deep belly (curved edge) and a drop point. A bushcraft knife needs a strong spine for batoning (splitting wood). Measure the tang length to match your handle material. A typical full tang is 1012 cm long. The spine thickness is usually 35 mm for a sturdy knife. The edge angle is about 20 degrees per side for general use. Start by sketching these parts on paper before you touch steel.

2. Designing a Blade: Profile and Dimensions

Before you cut steel, draw your blade profile on paper. Use graph paper or a cardboard template. The profile is the outline of the blade. Start with the overall length: a small knife is 1518 cm, a medium knife 2025 cm, a large knife 3040 cm. For a general-purpose knife, 22 cm overall with a 10 cm blade works well. The blade width (from edge to spine) is typically 2.54 cm. The spine thickness is 35 mm. The point shape depends on use: a drop point for skinning, a clip point for utility, a spear point for piercing. The edge curve (belly) helps slicing. A straight edge is good for chopping. Draw the profile with a pencil, then cut it out and trace it onto a piece of mild steel or cardboard. Hold it in your hand to check the feel. Adjust the handle length: for a full-hand grip, the handle should be 1012 cm long. The finger guard (if any) should be 12 cm. In Africa, you can use old leaf springs from cars (5160 steel) or files (1095 steel). These are good blade steels. Draw the profile so the grain of the steel runs lengthwise. Avoid sharp inside cornersthey cause cracks. Round all curves. Once you are happy, cut the template from thin plywood or metal. This is your pattern. You will use it to mark steel. Keep the design simple for your first knife: a straight spine, a curved edge, and a drop point. That is easy to grind and works for many tasks.

3. Marking and Layout on Steel

To transfer your design to steel, you need a few tools: a scriber (or a sharp nail), a center punch, a hammer, a permanent marker, and layout fluid (or shoe polish, or a marker). Clean the steel with sandpaper or a rag. If using layout fluid, spray or brush a thin coat and let it dry. Otherwise, color the area with a permanent marker. Place your template on the steel and trace around it with your scriber. Use a center punch to mark any holes (for handle pins) and the start of the cutting line. For a full tang knife, mark the tang shape too. If you are making a hidden tang, mark the centerline of the tang. Double-check your measurements: blade length, handle length, and overall length. A common mistake is not leaving enough steel for the handle. Mark the plunge line (where the bevel starts) about 23 cm from the handle. Mark the edge centerline along the blade. Use a ruler to keep lines straight. For curved sections, use a French curve or a flexible ruler. If you are recycling a leaf spring, first anneal it (heat to 800C and cool slowly in ash) to make it soft and easy to mark and cut. Mark on both sides if you can, or use a marker on one side and flip the steel to check alignment. Once marked, punch all holes. Now you are ready to cut. Remember: measure twice, cut once. In many African workshops, a simple scriber made from an old file works perfectly. Keep your lines visiblere-mark if they rub off.

4. Cutting and Shaping the Profile

Cutting the profile from steel can be done with a hacksaw, an angle grinder, a plasma cutter, or a chisel and hammer (for thin steel). For most beginners, an angle grinder with a cut-off wheel is fastest. Clamp the steel securely to a workbench. Wear safety glasses, gloves, and ear protection. Cut along your marked line, leaving about 12 mm of extra steel. Do not cut exactly on the lineyou will grind to the line later. For straight cuts, use a hacksaw with a 24 TPI blade. For curves, use an angle grinder or a die grinder with a carbide burr. If you have access to a bandsaw with a metal-cutting blade, that is ideal. After rough cutting, use a bench grinder or an angle grinder with a grinding wheel to shape the profile to your line. Keep the steel cool by dipping it in water frequentlyoverheating can ruin the steel's hardness. For a professional look, file the edges smooth with a mill file. Check your progress often by holding the blade to the light or comparing to your template. For a hidden tang, cut the tang narrower than the blade. For a full tang, cut the handle shape as well. Once the profile is shaped, deburr all edges. If you are making a knife from a file, be careful not to overheat itif it turns blue, you have lost hardness. In that case, you will need to re-heat-treat later. After cutting, your blade should match your template. Now you can move to grinding bevels. In rural Africa, many smiths use a simple hacksaw and files to cut and shape, which takes longer but works well.

5. Introduction to Grinding: Bevels and Plunge Lines

The bevel is the angled cut from the spine down to the edge. It creates the cutting edge. The plunge line is where the bevel stops near the handle. A clean plunge line looks professional and prevents cracks. To grind a bevel, you can use a belt grinder (best), an angle grinder, or a file. For beginners, a file is safe and cheap. Clamp the blade edge-up in a vise. Draw a line on the blade where you want the bevel to start (the plunge line). For a flat grind, file at a consistent angle, usually 48 degrees per side for a thin knife, or 1015 degrees for a heavy chopper. Count your strokes and check often. For a scandi grind (popular in bushcraft), the bevel is a single flat angle of about 1215 degrees per side. For a convex grind, use a belt grinder with a slack belt. The plunge line should be perpendicular to the edge or slightly angled. Use a file guide or a piece of angle iron to keep the file straight. Work slowly, alternating sides to keep the edge centered. Do not grind the edge too thinleave about 0.5 mm for the final sharpening. If using a belt grinder, use a 60120 grit belt for rough grinding, then 220400 for refining. Keep a bucket of water nearby to cool the steel. Overheating will ruin the heat treatment. After grinding, the bevel should be even on both sides. Check by looking down the edge. A common mistake is grinding past the plunge line into the handle. Mark your plunge line clearly and stop there. In Africa, many smiths use a simple file jig made from wood and a clamp. Practice on scrap steel first.

6. Design Considerations for Function and Aesthetics

A good knife balances function and beauty. Function comes first: the knife must cut well, feel comfortable, and last. For function, consider the task. A chef's knife needs a thin, hard edge (6062 HRC) and a comfortable handle. A machete needs a tough, soft edge (5558 HRC) to resist chipping. A hunting knife needs a sharp point and a deep belly for skinning. The handle should fit your hand: not too thick, not too thin. For most hands, a handle circumference of 1012 cm is good. Use materials that are available: hardwood (ebony, mahogany, olive), bone, horn, or even micarta made from cloth and resin. For aesthetics, you can add a brass guard, a mosaic pin, or a file-worked spine. But do not sacrifice strength for looks. A fancy handle that breaks is useless. In Africa, traditional knives often have carved handles and leather sheaths. You can incorporate local patterns or materials. For example, use a piece of warthog tusk for a handle. Or etch a pattern on the blade with vinegar and salt. The finish of the blade matters: a satin finish (400800 grit) hides scratches, while a mirror polish (2000 grit) shows them. For a working knife, a satin finish is practical. The sheath should be designed to hold the knife safely. Leather or kydex works. Always test your knife: cut rope, carve wood, and check comfort. Adjust the design if needed. Remember, a knife is a toolit should serve you well. But a beautiful knife brings pride. In your design, aim for a tool that works hard and looks good doing it.

<h2>Practical Reference: Blade Design, Layout &amp; Shaping for the African Workshop</h2>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the six lessons in the <strong>Blade Design and Layout</strong> module into a single working document you can pin to the wall of your forge. It is written for the small-scale African bladesmith who is building a business not a hobby and who needs repeatable numbers, not vague advice. Whether you are forging from leaf-spring steel in Kumasi, stock-removing from an old saw blade in Nakuru, or grinding machetes in Lubumbashi, the geometry, layout and grinding principles below apply. Get them right and your knives will cut better, last longer, and sell at a higher margin.</p>

<h3>1. Knife Anatomy: The Vocabulary of Value</h3>

<p>Every knife you sell is judged by six anatomical features. Know them, and you can explain your price.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Point</strong> the piercing end. The profile (clip, drop, spear, tanto) determines whether the knife is for skinning, slicing or stabbing.</li>
<li><strong>Edge</strong> the cutting geometry. A thin edge (1520 per side) slices; a thick edge (2530) chops and batons.</li>
<li><strong>Spine</strong> the unsharpened back. It stiffens the blade and can be squared for fire-steel striking.</li>
<li><strong>Tang</strong> the extension of the blade into the handle. Full tang = strongest, most marketable; stick tang = lighter, traditional.</li>
<li><strong>Ricasso &amp; Plunge Line</strong> the flat section above the edge and the transition into the bevel. Clean plunge lines signal craftsmanship.</li>
<li><strong>Handle &amp; Pins</strong> scales, bolsters and fasteners. In African markets, hardwood (ebony, mvule, mukwa) and bone command premium prices.</li>
</ul>

<h3>2. Designing a Blade: Profile and Dimensions</h3>

<p>Design on paper before you touch steel. A 1:1 drawing on card stock becomes your template trace it onto every blank so your batch is consistent. Consistency is what turns a forge into a brand.</p>

<p>For the African market, four profiles cover 90% of demand:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Drop-point hunter</strong> skinning and general bushcraft. Blade 100130 mm, width 3035 mm.</li>
<li><strong>Machete / panga</strong> agriculture and land clearing. Blade 400600 mm, width 4560 mm, edge 2025.</li>
<li><strong>Chef's knife</strong> urban kitchens, growing middle class. Blade 180210 mm, width 4550 mm.</li>
<li><strong>Utility / paring</strong> high-volume, low-cost sellers. Blade 80100 mm, width 2025 mm.</li>
</ul>

<p>Keep the blade-to-handle ratio around 60:40 for hunters and 70:30 for machetes. A handle shorter than 100 mm will not fit a working hand; longer than 130 mm and you lose control.</p>

<h3>3. Marking and Layout on Steel</h3>

<p>Layout is where accuracy becomes profit. A mis-marked blank wastes steel, gas and grinding belts. Use a fine-tip <strong>layout dye</strong> (or a permanent marker), a <strong>scriber</strong> (an old hacksaw blade ground to a point works), and a <strong>steel rule</strong>. Mark centre lines first, then profile, then the plunge-line position.</p>

<p>Always work from a datum one edge or the centre line so errors do not compound. Mark both sides of the blank; flipping errors are the most common cause of asymmetric blades.</p>

<h3>5. Introduction to Grinding: Bevels and Plunge Lines</h3>

<p>The bevel is the knife's engine. There are three common grinds:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Flat grind</strong> strongest, easiest to learn, best for machetes and working knives.</li>
<li><strong>Scandi grind</strong> zero-bevel, excellent for bushcraft; popular in Southern Africa.</li>
<li><strong>Hollow grind</strong> thinnest slicing geometry; requires a contact wheel; best for chef's knives.</li>
</ul>

<p>The <strong>plunge line</strong> is where the bevel meets the ricasso. A crisp, vertical plunge line is the single clearest sign of a skilled maker. Establish it with the edge of your grinding belt, keep the blade perpendicular to the belt, and use a file guide (a hardened steel clamp) for your first 20 knives.</p>

<strong style="color:#b71c1c;"> SAFETY WARNING Grinding and Cutting</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#b71c1c;">Angle grinders and belt grinders cause more workshop injuries than any other tool in a bladesmith's shop. <strong>Always</strong> wear impact-rated safety glasses, a face shield when grinding, hearing protection and a P2/N95 dust mask (metal dust and belt dust are carcinogenic). Never wear gloves near a spinning belt or wheel they can catch and pull your hand in. Keep a bucket of clean water and a first-aid kit within arm's reach. Check your grinder's guard and disc rating before every session. In many African markets, cheap unrated cutting discs shatter buy rated discs only, and never exceed the RPM stamped on the disc. If you run a generator, ensure it is earthed and that cables are not trailing through water.</p>
</div>

<h3>6. Design Considerations for Function and Aesthetics</h3>

<p>Function sells the first knife; aesthetics sell the second. Balance the two:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Function:</strong> match steel thickness to use (2 mm for paring, 45 mm for machetes); match handle material to climate (stabilised wood resists humidity better than raw ebony); match edge angle to task (see Table 2).</li>
<li><strong>Aesthetics:</strong> a clean plunge line, even bevels, symmetrical handle pins and a hand-rubbed finish (400800 grit) command 2040% higher prices in urban African markets and for export.</li>
<li><strong>Branding:</strong> stamp or etch your mark on the ricasso. A maker's mark turns a knife into a product and a product into a business.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li> Draw the profile 1:1 on card; confirm blade-to-handle ratio (60:40 hunter, 70:30 machete).</li>
<li> Mark centre line and profile on both sides of the blank with dye and scriber.</li>
<li> Cut 12 mm outside the line; never cut to the line.</li>
<li> Keep steel cool dip every 35 seconds; if you can't touch it, stop.</li>
<li> Establish the plunge line with a file guide; keep the blade perpendicular to the belt.</li>
<li> Grind bevels to the angle in Table 2 for the intended use.</li>
<li> Check symmetry by eye and with a straight edge before heat treatment.</li>
<li> Wear eye, ear and respiratory protection; keep water and first-aid within reach.</li>
</ol>

<p><strong>Business takeaway:</strong> In the African bladesmithing market, the makers who win are those who standardise. Draw once, template once, mark the same way every time, grind the same angles every time. Consistency reduces waste, shortens training time for apprentices, and lets you quote a price with confidence. Your layout table is your production line treat it like one.</p>
