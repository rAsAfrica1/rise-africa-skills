1. Lesson 1: Choosing Between Concrete, Block, Ferrocement, and Plastic Tanks for African Conditions

When a client in Nairobi, Lagos, Accra, or Johannesburg asks you to build a water tank, the first business decision is not how to build it but what to build. Every material choice carries a different cost curve, lifespan, labour requirement, and risk profile. Getting this wrong can wipe out your margin or destroy your reputation. This lesson gives you a decision framework you can use on site with real numbers. Start with the four main options: reinforced concrete (cast in situ), concrete block (masonry), ferrocement (thin wire-reinforced mortar), and rotationally moulded plastic. Each has a sweet spot. Reinforced concrete suits large capacities from 10,000 litres upward, especially where you need underground or partially buried tanks, heavy roof loads, or fire reserve storage. A 20,000 litre cast concrete tank in Kenya costs roughly KES 180,000 to KES 280,000 in materials and labour depending on reinforcement prices and whether you use ready-mix or site-mixed concrete. In Nigeria, a similar tank runs NGN 1.8 million to NGN 3.2 million. Block tanks are cheaper on materials but slower and more skill-dependent. A 10,000 litre block tank in Ghana costs around GHS 9,000 to GHS 14,000, while the same capacity in South Africa using cement blocks runs ZAR 18,000 to ZAR 28,000. Ferrocement is the artisan's friend. It uses cement mortar over layers of chicken wire or welded mesh on a steel skeleton. A 5,000 litre ferrocement tank can be built for KES 45,000 to KES 70,000 in Kenya, NGN 450,000 to NGN 700,000 in Nigeria, GHS 3,500 to GHS 5,500 in Ghana, and ZAR 7,000 to ZAR 12,000 in South Africa. It is ideal for rural schools, clinics, and household rainwater harvesting where transport of heavy materials is expensive. Plastic tanks are the fastest and most predictable. A 5,000 litre rotational moulded tank costs KES 35,000 to KES 55,000 in Kenya, NGN 350,000 to NGN 550,000 in Nigeria, GHS 3,000 to GHS 5,000 in Ghana, and ZAR 6,000 to ZAR 10,000 in South Africa. They are excellent for above-ground household storage but degrade under intense UV in 8 to 15 years unless stabilised, and they cannot be buried unless specifically rated. Your decision matrix should weigh five factors: capacity, site access, soil type, water source, and client budget. For a 5,000 litre household tank with good road access, plastic wins on speed and predictability. For a 20,000 litre school tank with no road access beyond a footpath, ferrocement or block wins because you can carry materials in head loads or on motorbikes. For a 50,000 litre community tank, reinforced concrete wins because it is the only option that can be engineered for structural loads and buried partially for cooling. Soil matters enormously. Black cotton soil, common in parts of Kenya, Uganda, and Tanzania, expands and contracts violently. A plastic tank on a poorly designed base will crack or tilt. A concrete ring beam foundation is mandatory. In sandy coastal soils like Mombasa or Lagos, you need a reinforced base slab to prevent settlement. In rocky areas like Jos or Pretoria, excavation costs rise but foundation costs fall. Water source determines water quality requirements. Rainwater is slightly acidic and low in dissolved solids, which is gentle on concrete but can leach lime from fresh mortar. Borehole water high in sulphates or chlorides attacks ordinary Portland cement. In those cases you need sulphate-resisting cement or a plastic liner. Plastic tanks are inert but can impart taste if low-grade resins are used. Always ask for food-grade polyethylene certification. Labour is your biggest variable. In Kenya, a skilled mason earns KES 1,200 to KES 2,000 per day. In Nigeria, NGN 8,000 to NGN 15,000. In Ghana, GHS 80 to GHS 150. In South Africa, ZAR 350 to ZAR 650. Ferrocement is labour-intensive but uses semi-skilled labour. Reinforced concrete needs skilled steel fixers and formwork carpenters. Block tanks need skilled masons who can lay true and plumb. Plastic needs almost no on-site labour beyond base preparation. Your profit margin lives in this difference. A ferrocement tank built by a trained team of three in five days can yield 30 to 40 percent gross margin. A concrete tank with hired formwork and ready-mix might yield 15 to 20 percent. Plastic resale yields 10 to 15 percent but with almost no risk. Safety note: never enter an excavated tank pit deeper than 1.2 metres without shoring or battering. Collapse kills more tank builders than any other cause. Always check for underground cables and pipes before digging. Use gloves when handling cement and wire mesh. Keep a first aid kit on site. Finally, always write a one-page specification before quoting. State capacity, material, foundation type, wall thickness, reinforcement schedule, inlet and outlet sizes, lid type, and warranty. This protects you from disputes and positions you as a professional, not a casual labourer. The client who sees a written spec will pay more and refer you faster. That is the business lesson: the material choice is technical, but the specification is commercial. Master both and you control the project.

2. Lesson 2: Site Preparation, Foundations, and Base Slabs for Concrete and Block Tanks

A tank is only as good as its foundation. In Africa, most tank failures I have investigated from Kisumu to Kumasi to Polokwane trace back to a bad base, not bad walls. This lesson walks you through site preparation, excavation, and base slab construction for concrete and block tanks with real costs and practical field methods. Start with a site survey. You need four things: a level area at least one metre larger than the tank diameter on all sides, a water source for construction within reasonable distance, a safe distance from latrines and animal pens (minimum 15 metres and preferably uphill), and vehicle access for materials. If the site is on a slope, you will need cut and fill. Never build a tank on fill soil without compacting it in 150mm layers and proving it with a plate compactor or a heavy hand rammer. In Kenya, hiring a plate compactor costs KES 3,000 to KES 5,000 per day. In Nigeria, NGN 25,000 to NGN 40,000. In Ghana, GHS 250 to GHS 400. In South Africa, ZAR 600 to ZAR 1,200. This is money well spent. Excavation for an underground or partially buried tank must be battered or shored. For a 3 metre deep pit in stable soil, batter the sides at 45 degrees. In loose soil, use timber shoring or steel trench boxes. A cubic metre of manual excavation in Kenya costs KES 800 to KES 1,500. In Nigeria, NGN 6,000 to NGN 12,000. In Ghana, GHS 60 to GHS 120. In South Africa, ZAR 250 to ZAR 500. Machine excavation is cheaper per cubic metre but needs access. A 20-tonne excavator in Kenya costs KES 8,000 to KES 12,000 per hour including operator and fuel. For a 20,000 litre tank, you will excavate roughly 25 to 30 cubic metres if buried. That is 3 to 4 hours of machine time or 20 to 30 man-days. The foundation itself depends on tank type. For an above-ground concrete or block tank, you need a reinforced ring beam or a full base slab. A full base slab is better because it distributes load evenly and prevents differential settlement. For a 10,000 litre tank, the base slab should be at least 150mm thick with a 200mm thick compacted hardcore sub-base. Reinforcement should be A142 mesh or 10mm bars at 200mm centres both ways. Concrete grade should be C20/25, which is a 1:2:4 mix by volume or 1:1.5:3 if you want extra strength. In Kenya, ready-mix C25 costs KES 12,000 to KES 15,000 per cubic metre delivered. In Nigeria, NGN 120,000 to NGN 160,000. In Ghana, GHS 1,200 to GHS 1,600. In South Africa, ZAR 2,500 to ZAR 3,500. Site-mixed concrete is cheaper but quality control is harder. Use a concrete mixer, not a wheelbarrow and shovel, for anything larger than 1 cubic metre. For a block tank, you can build a reinforced concrete ring beam instead of a full slab if the tank is small and above ground. A ring beam 300mm wide by 300mm deep with four 12mm bars and 8mm links at 200mm centres is adequate for a 5,000 litre tank on stable soil. But if the soil is black cotton or filled ground, use a full slab. Waterproofing the base is critical. Apply a damp-proof membrane under the slab. Use 500 micron polythene sheeting overlapped 300mm and taped. On top of the slab, before walls go up, apply a cementitious waterproof coating or a bituminous membrane if the tank will hold potable water. Do not use solvent-based bitumen inside a potable water tank. Use food-grade epoxy or cementitious coating. In Kenya, a 20kg bag of cementitious waterproof coating costs KES 4,500 to KES 7,000. In Nigeria, NGN 45,000 to NGN 70,000. In Ghana, GHS 450 to GHS 700. In South Africa, ZAR 900 to ZAR 1,400. One bag covers roughly 1.5 to 2 square metres in two coats. Drainage around the tank is not optional. You need a French drain or a graded apron sloping away from the tank at 2 percent minimum. In heavy clay soils, install a perimeter drain filled with gravel and wrapped in geotextile. This prevents hydrostatic pressure from saturating the soil and pushing against the tank walls, which is a common cause of block tank cracking. Safety notes: never leave an open excavation uncovered overnight. Use barriers and warning lights. Check for gas in deep pits before entry. Provide a ladder for access. Keep spoil heaps at least 1 metre from the pit edge to avoid surcharge loading. Wear boots, gloves, and hard hats. For concrete work, protect your skin from wet cement, which causes chemical burns. Now let us talk money. A complete foundation for a 10,000 litre concrete tank in Kenya costs roughly KES 35,000 to KES 55,000 including excavation, hardcore, mesh, concrete, and waterproofing. In Nigeria, NGN 350,000 to NGN 550,000. In Ghana, GHS 3,500 to GHS 5,500. In South Africa, ZAR 7,000 to ZAR 11,000. This is 20 to 30 percent of the total tank cost. Do not cut corners here. A failed foundation means a failed tank and a refund you cannot afford. Always take photographs of every stage before covering up. These photos are your proof of quality and your defence if a client claims poor workmanship. Also, record the concrete cube test results if you are doing structural work. In Kenya, a cube test costs KES 2,500 to KES 4,000 per set. In Nigeria, NGN 25,000 to NGN 40,000. In Ghana, GHS 250 to GHS 400. In South Africa, ZAR 500 to ZAR 900. It is cheap insurance. Finally, plan your material delivery. Cement must be stored off the ground on pallets and covered. Sand and aggregate must be on hard standing or tarpaulin to avoid contamination. Rebar must be off the ground and free of mud. A clean site is a profitable site. Clients notice. And clients who notice quality pay on time and refer you to their neighbours. That is how you build a tank business, one solid foundation at a time.

3. Lesson 3: Ferrocement Tank Construction Step by Step

Ferrocement is the most underrated tank technology in Africa. It uses cement mortar reinforced with layers of wire mesh over a steel skeleton. The result is a thin, strong, crack-resistant shell that can be built with semi-skilled labour and carried to remote sites. A well-built ferrocement tank lasts 30 to 50 years with minimal maintenance. This lesson gives you the full construction sequence with real numbers for Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana, and South Africa. Start with design. For a cylindrical tank, the wall thickness is typically 25mm to 40mm. The steel skeleton consists of vertical bars, usually 6mm or 8mm, spaced at 300mm to 400mm around the circumference. Horizontal bars, also 6mm or 8mm, are spaced at 300mm vertically. The skeleton is tied with 1.6mm galvanised tie wire. Over this skeleton you wrap layers of chicken wire or welded mesh. Chicken wire is cheaper but weaker. Welded mesh, such as 25mm x 25mm x 1.6mm, is stronger and easier to work with. For a 5,000 litre tank, you need roughly 15 to 20 square metres of mesh per layer, and you should use at least two layers, preferably three. For a 10,000 litre tank, double those quantities. The base is a reinforced concrete slab as described in Lesson 2. The skeleton is anchored into the slab using starter bars. For a 5,000 litre tank, use 12 vertical starter bars of 8mm diameter, embedded 300mm into the slab and projecting 600mm above it. The slab should be at least 100mm thick with A142 mesh. Once the slab has cured for at least 7 days, you can erect the skeleton. Build a central pipe or a temporary frame to hold the top ring in place. The top ring is usually a 6mm or 8mm bar bent into a circle and tied to the vertical bars. Check plumb and level constantly. A tank that is out of plumb by more than 10mm over 2 metres will look bad and may crack. Now apply the first layer of mortar. The mix is critical. Use 1 part cement to 2 parts clean sand, with a water-cement ratio of 0.4 to 0.45. That means roughly 20 litres of water per 50kg bag of cement. The mortar should be stiff enough to hold on the mesh without sagging but wet enough to work into the wire. Apply from the outside first, pressing mortar through the mesh. Then apply from the inside. The first layer should be 10mm to 15mm thick. Leave it rough to key the next layer. Cure for 24 hours, then apply the second layer of mesh and mortar. The second layer should bring the total thickness to 25mm to 30mm. For a 5,000 litre tank, you will use roughly 15 to 20 bags of cement for the walls, plus 5 to 8 bags for the base. In Kenya, a 50kg bag of cement costs KES 650 to KES 800. In Nigeria, NGN 7,000 to NGN 9,000. In Ghana, GHS 70 to GHS 90. In South Africa, ZAR 120 to ZAR 160. Sand costs vary widely. In Kenya, a 7-tonne lorry of river sand costs KES 25,000 to KES 35,000. In Nigeria, NGN 250,000 to NGN 350,000. In Ghana, GHS 2,500 to GHS 3,500. In South Africa, ZAR 4,000 to ZAR 6,000. You need roughly 2 to 3 cubic metres of sand for a 5,000 litre tank. Mesh costs are also significant. A roll of chicken wire in Kenya costs KES 4,000 to KES 6,000. In Nigeria, NGN 40,000 to NGN 60,000. In Ghana, GHS 400 to GHS 600. In South Africa, ZAR 800 to ZAR 1,200. Welded mesh is more expensive but worth it. A sheet of 25mm welded mesh in Kenya costs KES 3,500 to KES 5,000. In Nigeria, NGN 35,000 to NGN 50,000. In Ghana, GHS 350 to GHS 500. In South Africa, ZAR 700 to ZAR 1,000. After the walls are built, you need a roof or lid. Ferrocement tanks usually have a domed or flat lid. A domed lid is stronger and sheds water. Build a temporary formwork of sand or timber, cover it with mesh, and apply mortar. The lid must have an access hatch of at least 600mm x 600mm for cleaning and inspection. The hatch cover should be insect-proof and lockable. Inlet and outlet pipes must be installed before the final mortar layer. Use PVC pipes with rubber gaskets or solvent cement joints. The inlet should be at the top, the outlet 100mm above the base, and a drain at the base. Fit a first-flush diverter on the inlet if the tank collects rainwater. This simple device, costing KES 2,000 to KES 4,000 in Kenya, NGN 20,000 to NGN 40,000 in Nigeria, GHS 200 to GHS 400 in Ghana, and ZAR 400 to ZAR 800 in South Africa, dramatically improves water quality and reduces cleaning frequency. Waterproofing and finishing are next. After the final mortar layer has cured for 7 days, apply a cementitious waterproof coating or a food-grade epoxy. Two coats are standard. Then fill the tank with water and let it stand for 24 hours. Check for leaks. If you find a leak, drain, patch with a stiff mortar mix, and re-coat. Do not ignore small leaks. They become big leaks. Curing is the most neglected step. Ferrocement must be cured wet for at least 14 days, preferably 21. Cover the tank with wet burlap or plastic sheeting and keep it moist. In hot climates, cure at night and early morning to avoid rapid drying. A tank that dries too fast will crack and leak. Safety notes: wire mesh has sharp edges. Wear heavy gloves. When mixing mortar, wear a dust mask to avoid cement dust inhalation. When working inside the tank, ensure adequate ventilation. Never work alone inside a confined space. Have someone outside with a rope. Use a ladder, not a bucket, to get in and out. Now let us talk business. A 5,000 litre ferrocement tank in Kenya can be sold for KES 90,000 to KES 130,000. Your material cost is roughly KES 45,000 to KES 70,000. Labour for a team of three for five days at KES 1,500 per day is KES 22,500. That leaves a gross margin of KES 22,500 to KES 62,500. In Nigeria, the same tank sells for NGN 900,000 to NGN 1,300,000 with material costs of NGN 450,000 to NGN 700,000. In Ghana, GHS 9,000 to GHS 13,000 with material costs of GHS 3,500 to GHS 5,500. In South Africa, ZAR 18,000 to ZAR 26,000 with material costs of ZAR 7,000 to ZAR 12,000. The key to profit is repeatable process. Build a jig for the skeleton. Standardise your mesh cutting. Train your team on the same mix every time. Take photos of every tank. Use those photos to sell the next one. Ferrocement is not just a construction method. It is a business model for reaching rural clients who cannot afford plastic or concrete. Master it and you own that market.

4. Lesson 4: Concrete Block Tank Construction, Mortar, and Reinforcement

Concrete block tanks are the workhorse of African water storage. They are familiar to every mason, materials are available in every town, and they can be built incrementally as money allows. But they fail more often than any other tank type because of poor mortar, inadequate reinforcement, and bad curing. This lesson gives you the technical details to build block tanks that last 40 years and the business details to price them profitably. Start with block selection. You can use solid concrete blocks, hollow concrete blocks, or stabilised soil blocks. Solid blocks are strongest but heaviest. Hollow blocks are lighter and allow vertical reinforcement to be grouted into the cores. Stabilised soil blocks, made with 5 to 10 percent cement and compressed, are cheapest but weakest and should only be used for above-ground tanks under 5,000 litres. For a 10,000 litre tank, use hollow concrete blocks of 150mm or 200mm thickness with a compressive strength of at least 3.5 N per square millimetre. In Kenya, a 200mm hollow block costs KES 60 to KES 90. In Nigeria, NGN 500 to NGN 800. In Ghana, GHS 5 to GHS 8. In South Africa, ZAR 12 to ZAR 20. You need roughly 12.5 blocks per square metre of wall. For a 10,000 litre tank with a 3 metre diameter and 1.5 metre wall height, the wall area is roughly 14 square metres, so you need about 175 blocks. Add 10 percent for breakage. Mortar is where most block tanks fail. The mix should be 1 part cement to 4 parts sand for load-bearing walls, and 1 part cement to 5 parts sand for non-load-bearing walls. Never use a 1:6 mix or weaker. Use clean, sharp sand, not soft plaster sand. Add a plasticiser or lime to improve workability if needed. Do not add too much water. The mortar should be stiff enough to hold a block without sagging. For a 10,000 litre tank, you need roughly 1.5 cubic metres of mortar. That is about 8 to 10 bags of cement and 1.5 cubic metres of sand. In Kenya, that costs KES 6,000 to KES 8,000 for cement and KES 6,000 to KES 9,000 for sand. In Nigeria, NGN 60,000 to NGN 90,000 for cement and NGN 60,000 to NGN 90,000 for sand. In Ghana, GHS 600 to GHS 900 for cement and GHS 600 to GHS 900 for sand. In South Africa, ZAR 1,000 to ZAR 1,600 for cement and ZAR 1,000 to ZAR 1,800 for sand. Reinforcement is not optional. Every block tank must have vertical reinforcement in the cores and horizontal reinforcement in the mortar joints. For a 10,000 litre tank, use 12mm vertical bars at 400mm centres, tied to starter bars in the base slab. Use 6mm horizontal bars every third course, laid in the mortar. Fill the cores containing vertical bars with concrete or strong mortar, 1:3 mix, compacted with a rod to remove air pockets. This creates a reinforced concrete column inside the block wall. Without it, the tank will crack under water pressure. The wall thickness matters. A 150mm block wall is adequate for tanks up to 5,000 litres. For 10,000 to 20,000 litres, use 200mm blocks or double-skin the wall with a 50mm gap filled with mortar. For tanks over 20,000 litres, you need an engineer to design the reinforcement. Do not guess. A collapsed tank can kill. Build the walls in stages. Lay no more than 1 metre of height per day. This allows the mortar to set and prevents the wall from bulging. Check plumb and level every three courses. Use a spirit level and a plumb bob. If the wall is out of plumb by more than 5mm over 1 metre, take it down and rebuild. It is cheaper to rebuild now than to repair a leak later. After the walls are up, apply a render coat of 1:3 cement-sand mortar, 15mm thick, to the inside. This is your waterproofing base. Then apply a cementitious waterproof coating or a food-grade epoxy. Do not rely on the render alone. Water will find a way through. For the outside, apply a 10mm render and paint with a weather-resistant masonry paint. This protects the block from rain and UV. The lid or roof is critical. A block tank needs a reinforced concrete roof or a domed ferrocement lid. A flat concrete roof should be at least 100mm thick with A142 mesh and a manhole of 600mm x 600mm. A domed lid is cheaper and stronger but needs formwork. In Kenya, building a domed lid for a 10,000 litre tank costs KES 15,000 to KES 25,000 in materials and labour. In Nigeria, NGN 150,000 to NGN 250,000. In Ghana, GHS 1,500 to GHS 2,500. In South Africa, ZAR 3,000 to ZAR 5,000. Curing is non-negotiable. After rendering, keep the tank wet for 14 days. Fill it with water after 7 days and let it stand. Check for leaks. If you find a leak, drain, cut out the defective area, and patch with a stiff mortar mix and waterproof coating. Do not just paint over it. Safety notes: block walls are heavy. Do not stack blocks more than 1.5 metres high on a scaffold. Use a proper scaffold with guardrails. Wear gloves when handling blocks and cement. When mixing mortar, wear a dust mask. When working inside the tank, ensure ventilation and never work alone. Now let us talk money. A 10,000 litre block tank in Kenya costs roughly KES 120,000 to KES 180,000 to build and sells for KES 180,000 to KES 250,000. In Nigeria, build cost is NGN 1.2 million to NGN 1.8 million and selling price is NGN 1.8 million to NGN 2.5 million. In Ghana, build cost is GHS 12,000 to GHS 18,000 and selling price is GHS 18,000 to GHS 25,000. In South Africa, build cost is ZAR 25,000 to ZAR 38,000 and selling price is ZAR 38,000 to ZAR 55,000. Your margin is 30 to 40 percent if you control labour and materials. The biggest risk is variation orders. Clients change their mind about capacity, position, or finishes. Always quote a fixed scope and price. Charge extra for changes. Put it in writing. A block tank is a simple structure, but it is not a simple business. Treat it like a business and it will pay you well.

5. Lesson 5: Waterproofing, Testing, Maintenance, and Business Pricing for Concrete and Block Tanks

You can build a beautiful tank and still lose money if it leaks, if the client refuses to pay, or if you cannot maintain it. This final lesson covers waterproofing, leak testing, maintenance, and the business systems that turn tank construction from a hustle into a profitable enterprise. Waterproofing is where amateurs cut corners and professionals win. There are three main approaches: cementitious coatings, epoxy coatings, and integral waterproofers. Cementitious coatings are the most common in Africa. They are powdered products mixed with water and applied in two or three coats. They bond to damp concrete, are breathable, and are relatively cheap. In Kenya, a 20kg bag costs KES 4,500 to KES 7,000 and covers 1.5 to 2 square metres in two coats. In Nigeria, NGN 45,000 to NGN 70,000. In Ghana, GHS 450 to GHS 700. In South Africa, ZAR 900 to ZAR 1,400. Epoxy coatings are more expensive but more durable and easier to clean. They are ideal for potable water tanks because they are inert and do not impart taste. In Kenya, a 5kg kit costs KES 8,000 to KES 12,000 and covers 5 to 8 square metres. In Nigeria, NGN 80,000 to NGN 120,000. In Ghana, GHS 800 to GHS 1,200. In South Africa, ZAR 1,600 to ZAR 2,400. Integral waterproofers are added to the concrete mix. They are cheap and easy but less reliable than surface coatings. Use them as a backup, not a primary defence. The application process matters as much as the product. The surface must be clean, free of dust, oil, and loose material. It must be damp, not wet. Mix the product exactly as instructed. Apply with a brush or roller, not a trowel, for the first coat. Let it cure for the recommended time, usually 4 to 6 hours, then apply the second coat at right angles to the first. Cure the final coat by misting with water for 3 to 7 days. Do not let it dry too fast. Leak testing is mandatory. After the tank has cured for 7 days, fill it with water to the maximum level. Mark the water level on the wall. Leave it for 24 hours. If the level drops more than 10mm, you have a leak. Drain the tank and find the leak. Look for damp patches, efflorescence, or cracks. Patch with a stiff mortar mix, 1:2 cement-sand with a bonding agent, and re-coat. Then test again. Do not hand over a leaking tank. It will cost you more to fix later and damage your reputation. Maintenance is your after-sales service and your recurring revenue. Every tank needs cleaning every 6 to 12 months, depending on water source. Rainwater tanks need cleaning annually. Borehole tanks may need cleaning every 6 months. Charge for this service. In Kenya, tank cleaning costs KES 5,000 to KES 15,000 depending on size. In Nigeria, NGN 50,000 to NGN 150,000. In Ghana, GHS 500 to GHS 1,500. In South Africa, ZAR 1,000 to ZAR 3,000. This is high-margin work because your costs are labour and transport only. Use a soft brush, a mild chlorine solution, and clean water. Never use harsh chemicals that can damage the coating. Inspect the inlet, outlet, overflow, and lid. Replace damaged screens and gaskets. Check the base and walls for cracks. A small crack repaired now is a major leak prevented later. Business pricing is where most tank builders fail. They quote a lump sum without breaking down costs. Then they discover they forgot the waterproofing or the lid and their margin disappears. Use a costing sheet. List every material: cement, sand, aggregate, mesh, rebar, blocks, waterproofing, pipes, fittings, lid, labour, transport, and tools. Add 10 percent contingency. Add 15 to 20 percent profit. Then quote. For a 10,000 litre concrete tank in Kenya, a full cost breakdown might look like this: excavation KES 15,000, base slab KES 35,000, walls KES 45,000, lid KES 20,000, waterproofing KES 15,000, pipes and fittings KES 8,000, labour KES 35,000, transport KES 7,000, contingency KES 18,000. Total cost KES 198,000. Add 20 percent profit, quote KES 238,000. In Nigeria, the same tank might cost NGN 1.95 million and sell for NGN 2.35 million. In Ghana, GHS 19,500 cost and GHS 23,500 selling price. In South Africa, ZAR 40,000 cost and ZAR 48,000 selling price. Payment terms protect you. Ask for 50 percent deposit before starting, 30 percent when walls are complete, and 20 percent on handover. Never start work without a deposit. Never hand over a tank without full payment. Put this in a simple contract. Include the scope, the price, the payment schedule, the completion date, and a 12-month warranty on workmanship. Exclude damage from misuse, earthquakes, or third-party interference. Safety notes for the final phase: when testing, never overfill a tank beyond its design capacity. Water weighs 1 tonne per cubic metre. A 10,000 litre tank holds 10 tonnes of water. That is a lot of load. Ensure the foundation can take it. When cleaning, use a ladder and a safety harness if the tank is deep. Never enter a tank alone. Test for gas if the tank has been sealed. Use chlorine safely. Wear gloves and eye protection. Now let us talk growth. The tank business is not just about building new tanks. It is about maintenance contracts, cleaning services, repairs, and upgrades. A client who buys a tank from you will need cleaning, repairs, and maybe a second tank. Keep a database of every client. Call them after 6 months. Offer a cleaning service. That is how you build recurring revenue. Also, train your team. A mason who can build a ferrocement tank, a block tank, and a concrete tank is worth more than one who can only do one. Pay them well. In Kenya, a skilled tank mason earns KES 1,500 to KES 2,500 per day. In Nigeria, NGN 10,000 to NGN 20,000. In Ghana, GHS 100 to GHS 200. In South Africa, ZAR 400 to ZAR 800. Pay on time. Provide safety gear. Treat them with respect. They are your production line. Finally, document everything. Take photos of every stage. Keep receipts. Write down your mix ratios. Record your costs. After every project, review what went well and what went wrong. Adjust your pricing. Improve your process. The tank construction business in Africa is growing because water scarcity is growing. Every household, school, clinic, and farm needs storage. If you build well, price fairly, and maintain your tanks, you will never lack work. That is the lesson. Build like a professional. Price like a business. Maintain like a partner. And you will prosper.
