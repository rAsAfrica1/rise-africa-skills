Lesson 8.1~12 min

Feed Conversion Ratio and Profitability

In this lesson
  • Calculate feed conversion ratio correctly, including feed eaten by birds that died
  • Compute the production efficiency factor and use it to compare batches fairly
  • Convert an FCR improvement into money per batch on your own flock

Feed conversion ratio is the number that connects nutrition to money. It is kilograms of feed divided by kilograms of live weight produced, and every other efficiency figure on a poultry farm is downstream of it.

Calculate it honestly, because the common shortcut flatters you. The correct figure is total feed issued to the house, including feed eaten by birds that later died, divided by the total live weight of birds sold. Take 500 broilers placed, 25 deaths, 475 sold at 2.05 kg average. Live weight sold = 973.75 kg. Total feed issued to the house = 1,770 kg. FCR = 1,770 / 973.75 = 1.82. If you had divided only by the feed you think the surviving birds ate, you would have reported 1.78 and been wrong by an amount worth real money.

Now value it. At feed of $0.52/kg, that 0.04 difference in FCR across 973.75 kg of live weight is 39 kg of feed, about $20 per batch, or $240 a year on six cycles. Bigger movements matter more. Improving from FCR 1.95 to 1.80 on the same 973.75 kg saves 146 kg of feed, about $76 a batch.

FCR alone can mislead, because a bird taken to a heavier weight always has a worse FCR and a bird sold early always has a better one. To compare batches fairly you need an index that accounts for weight, age and mortality together. The standard industry measure is the production efficiency factor, sometimes called EPEF:

EPEF = (liveability % x average live weight in kg) / (age in days x FCR) x 100.

Work two batches. Batch A: liveability 95%, 2.05 kg, 42 days, FCR 1.78. (95 x 2.05) = 194.75. (42 x 1.78) = 74.76. 194.75 / 74.76 = 2.605, times 100 = 261. Batch B: liveability 91%, 1.90 kg, 42 days, FCR 1.95. (91 x 1.90) = 172.9. (42 x 1.95) = 81.9. 172.9 / 81.9 = 2.111, times 100 = 211. Batch A is clearly the better batch, and the index tells you by how much even though both ran 42 days.

For layers the equivalent measures are feed per kilogram of eggs and feed per dozen. Hy-Line Brown reports 2.06-2.15 kg of feed per kilogram of eggs from peak to 60 weeks, and 1.23-1.28 kg of feed per dozen eggs over the same window. Across an extended 20-100 week cycle those figures move to 2.15-2.24 kg per kilogram of eggs and 1.55-1.61 kg per dozen, because efficiency declines with age. Lohmann Brown-Classic reports 2.0-2.2 kg of feed per kilogram of egg mass over the cycle. Compare your own flock with the range for its age, not with the peak figure.

What actually moves FCR? In order of usual impact on a small African farm:

  1. Feed wastage. Brim-filled feeders and feeders set too low can waste several per cent of everything you mix. This is pure FCR loss with no biological cause at all.
  2. Energy density. A ration diluted with bran below the Ross figures of 2,975 kcal/kg starter, 3,050 grower and 3,100 finisher forces the bird to eat more kilograms for the same weight.
  3. Heat. A bird that stops eating in the afternoon and eats hard at night converts worse and finishes lighter.
  4. Gut health. Coccidiosis, necrotic enteritis and mycotoxins all raise FCR before they raise mortality. Organic acids give a published 2-8% FCR improvement depending on acid type and gut health status, and NSP enzymes 3-7% where the ration justifies them.
  5. Mortality and its timing. A bird that dies at day 38 has eaten almost all of its feed and produces nothing. Late mortality damages FCR far more than early mortality.
  6. Amino acid balance. Excess nitrogen above the amino acid requirement is an economic loss and increases nitrogen excretion - you are paying for protein the bird cannot use.

Keep the record that makes this possible. One sheet per batch: birds placed, deaths by day, every bag of feed issued with its weight, sample weights every week, and final numbers and weight sold. Without feed issued in kilograms you cannot calculate FCR at all, and a farm that cannot calculate FCR is guessing.

A Tanzanian example: a Mbeya producer running 800 broilers assumed his FCR was around 1.8 because that was what the feed bag suggested. When he weighed every bag issued for one full cycle he found 3,120 kg of feed against 1,504 kg sold - an FCR of 2.07. Roughly half the gap turned out to be spillage from six overfilled feeders and a torn bag stored on a damp floor. Measuring cost him nothing and found about $130 per cycle.

Lesson 8.2~11 min

Cost Analysis for Broiler Production

In this lesson
  • Build a complete cost sheet for one broiler batch including every non-feed input
  • Calculate cost per bird sold and cost per kilogram of live weight
  • Identify which single cost line to attack first on your own farm

Most small broiler producers know their feed bill and their sale price and nothing in between. That gap is where the profit disappears. This lesson builds a full batch account so you can see every dollar. Broader questions of financing growth and long-term business strategy belong to Module 12; here we stay on one batch.

Take 500 day-old chicks in a 60 square metre house, sold at 42 days. Mortality 5%, so 475 sold at 2.05 kg average, FCR 1.82 on feed issued. Use these illustrative prices and replace every one with your own.

Costs:

  1. Day-old chicks: 500 x $0.80 = $400.00
  2. Feed: 1,770 kg issued x $0.52 = $920.40
  3. Vaccines and medication: $30.00
  4. Litter, wood shavings or rice husk: $20.00
  5. Brooding fuel or heat: $20.00
  6. Labour, casual and family valued honestly: $60.00
  7. Water and electricity: $15.00
  8. Transport of inputs and birds: $20.00
  9. House depreciation, repairs and equipment: $30.00

Total cost = $1,515.40.

Revenue at $4.20 per bird = 475 x $4.20 = $1,995.00. Margin = $479.60 per batch, which is $1.01 per bird sold, or $0.96 per bird placed.

Now the ratios that tell you where to work. Cost per bird sold = 1,515.40 / 475 = $3.19. Cost per kilogram of live weight = 3.19 / 2.05 = $1.56. Feed is 920.40 / 1,515.40 = 60.7% of total cost. Chicks are 26.4%. Everything else together is under 13%. In markets where day-old chicks are cheaper, feed rises to the 65-75% share more commonly quoted.

That ranking dictates strategy. Cutting your labour bill in half saves $30. Cutting feed cost by 8% saves $74. Cutting mortality from 5% to 3% adds 10 birds at $4.20 - $42 of revenue and slightly less feed. Attack feed and mortality; the small lines are not where the money is.

Run the sensitivities, because this is what protects you when prices move.

If maize rises 25% and maize is 55% of your ration by weight, your feed cost per kilogram rises from $0.52 to roughly $0.575. The batch feed bill goes from $920.40 to $1,017.75 - $97 straight off the margin, taking it to $382. You would need $0.21 more per bird to stand still.

If FCR slips from 1.82 to 2.00, feed issued rises to 1,947 kg, costing $1,012 - a $92 loss for the same birds sold.

If mortality rises from 5% to 12%, you sell 440 birds instead of 475. Revenue falls to $1,848 while feed cost barely moves, because most of those birds died late. Margin falls to roughly $350.

If sale price falls from $4.20 to $3.80, revenue drops $190 and the margin nearly halves. Note how exposed a broiler batch is to price: a 10% price fall wipes out 40% of the margin. This is why knowing your cost per bird before you negotiate matters more than any other single figure in this course.

Break-even is the figure to write on the wall. At $1,515.40 of cost and 475 birds sold, you break even at $3.19 per bird, or $1.56 per kilogram live. Below that you are subsidising your buyer. Calculate this before every batch, because your cost changes as feed prices move.

One more discipline: value family labour. If you and your household spend 45 minutes a day on the flock for 42 days, that is about 31 hours. Valued at even $1.20 an hour, it is $38. Leaving it out of the account makes the enterprise look more profitable than it is and hides the fact that at some scale you must hire.

A Nigerian example: an Abeokuta producer running 500 birds every seven weeks believed he was making about $2 a bird because he compared sale price with feed cost only. A full account showed $1.05. That was still a real business, but it changed his decisions immediately - he stopped taking a $3.60 gate price he had been accepting when a trader arrived early, because he could now see it was below his break-even in a high-maize month.

Cost Analysis for Layer Production

In this lesson
  • Build a monthly cost account for a laying flock including pullet depreciation
  • Calculate cost per dozen eggs and compare it with your selling price
  • Decide when a flock has stopped paying and should be culled

A layer flock is a different kind of business from a broiler batch. The capital is tied up for over a year, income arrives daily rather than in a lump, and the single biggest hidden cost is the pullet you have already paid for. So the layer account is built monthly, and it must include pullet depreciation.

Take 200 point-of-lay pullets bought at $5.50 each, expected to lay for 12 months before culling. Feed at $0.39/kg. Assume 80% lay and 110 g of feed per bird per day.

Monthly costs:

  1. Feed: 200 birds x 0.110 kg x 30 days = 660 kg, at $0.39 = $257.40
  2. Pullet depreciation: $5.50 spread over 12 months = $0.46 per bird per month x 200 = $92.00
  3. Labour: $50.00
  4. Vaccines, medication and vitamins: $10.00
  5. Egg trays and packaging: $12.00
  6. Transport to market: $20.00
  7. Water, electricity and lighting: $8.00

Total monthly cost = $449.40.

Production: 200 x 0.80 x 30 = 4,800 eggs = 400 dozen. Feed used per dozen = 660 / 400 = 1.65 kg. Compare that against Hy-Line Brown, which reports 1.23-1.28 kg per dozen at peak to 60 weeks and 1.55-1.61 kg per dozen over a full 20-100 week cycle. At 1.65 kg you are slightly outside even the full-cycle range, which tells you there is feed wastage, low lay or both. That single comparison is the most useful diagnostic a layer producer has.

Cost per dozen = $449.40 / 400 = $1.12. Feed alone per dozen = $257.40 / 400 = $0.64. At a selling price of $1.55 per dozen the margin is $0.43 per dozen, or $172.00 a month, which is $0.86 per hen per month.

Now test the account against reality.

If lay drops from 80% to 65%, eggs fall to 325 dozen. Feed barely changes, because hens eat by appetite, not by output. Cost per dozen rises to $449.40 / 325 = $1.38 and the margin per dozen collapses from $0.43 to $0.17 - a 60% fall in profit from a 19% fall in production. This is the central fact of layer economics: your costs are almost fixed and your income is not.

If feed rises 15% to $0.4485/kg, the monthly feed bill goes to $296.01 and the monthly margin falls by $38.61.

If you buy cheaper pullets at $4.20 but they lay 8% less, you save $0.11 per bird per month in depreciation and lose about 32 dozen eggs worth $49.60. The cheap pullet is expensive.

Culling is the decision this account exists to inform. A hen is worth keeping while her egg income exceeds her feed cost plus her share of the other running costs. In the example, non-feed running costs excluding pullet depreciation are $100 a month across 200 birds, or $0.50 per hen. Feed is $1.29 per hen per month. So each hen must produce more than $1.79 a month, which at $1.55 a dozen is about 13.9 eggs, roughly 46% lay, just to cover cash costs. Below that she is losing money every day even before you count what you paid for her. Late-cycle feed conversion is also worse: Hy-Line reports 2.15-2.24 kg of feed per kilogram of eggs over the extended cycle against 2.06-2.15 at peak.

Three adjustments protect a late-cycle flock. Shift the limestone particle split from 40% fine and 60% coarse to 25% fine and 75% coarse, because from week 77 hens need 4.7 g of calcium a day against 4.0 g at peak and shells thin without it. Move to a lower-protein phase diet, since Hy-Line Layer 5 at 110 g of intake needs only 16.23% crude protein against 19.83% at peak on 90 g. And count your cull value: 200 spent hens sold locally at even $2.50 each is $500, which recovers close to half your pullet investment.

A Kenyan example: a Thika producer with 300 hens kept a flock to 95 weeks because the birds were still laying and she disliked culling. Building this account showed she was at 51% lay and covering feed but not the rest, and losing about $60 a month. She culled, sold the hens for $735, and restocked. Her first month with new pullets at 88% lay returned $310. The birds had not been failing dramatically; the arithmetic had simply turned negative without anyone noticing.

Lesson 8.4~12 min

Marketing and Distribution

In this lesson
  • Compare the net price per bird or per dozen across each available sales channel
  • Cost the delivery and losses that sit between farm gate and buyer
  • Choose a channel mix that fits your batch size and cash needs

Marketing decisions are usually made emotionally - the trader who turns up, the price a neighbour mentions - when they should be made with a calculator. The right question is never which channel pays the highest price. It is which channel leaves the most money in your hand after the costs of reaching it, and pays you soon enough to buy the next batch of chicks. Broader questions of building a brand, contracts and long-term market development belong to Module 12; specialty and premium markets are covered in Module 11 Lesson 6.

Build a net-price comparison for the 475-bird batch from Lesson 2, where break-even was $3.19 per bird.

Channel A, farm gate to a trader. Price $4.20 per bird, no transport, no losses, cash on collection. Net $4.20. Margin over break-even $1.01 per bird, $479.60 per batch.

Channel B, live birds to a town market 30 km away. Price $4.90. Transport $28 return in a hired pickup. Crate hire and loading help $10. Losses and downgrades in transit, 2% of 475 birds is roughly 9 birds worth $44. Your own day $12. Total deduction $94 over 475 birds, so $0.20 per bird. Net $4.70 per bird. Margin $1.51 per bird, $717 per batch.

Channel C, dressed birds to restaurants and households. Price $6.30 per dressed bird. Dressing loss is real - a 2.05 kg live bird yields substantially less dressed weight, so compare on the finished unit you actually sell. Processing labour, water, packaging and ice at roughly $0.75 per bird, plus delivery of $35 per batch, plus the risk of unsold stock without cold storage. Net roughly $5.48 per bird if everything sells. Margin $2.29 per bird - but only if you can genuinely sell 475 dressed birds within the few days your cold chain allows.

Channel D, a supermarket or institutional contract. Price $5.60 with 30-day payment. There is no transport advantage, there are specification requirements, and the 30-day delay means you must finance the next batch of chicks and feed out of savings. The price is good; the working capital demand is the cost.

The lesson from that table is that the highest headline price is rarely the best net price, and that payment timing is a cost even when it is not a deduction. If waiting 30 days forces you to skip a placement, the loss is a whole batch margin.

For eggs the same arithmetic applies per dozen. Farm gate at $1.40 with no costs; local shops at $1.55 with $20 a month of transport across 400 dozen, so $0.05 a dozen, netting $1.50; hotels and restaurants at $1.85 but with washing, grading, delivery twice a week and a credit period, netting perhaps $1.68 after $0.17 of handling and transport. Add breakages: 2% of 4,800 eggs is 96 eggs, which is $12.40 at $1.55 a dozen. Egg trays cost money and are lost to customers unless you charge a deposit.

Three practical rules follow.

  1. Never sell below break-even because a buyer arrived unexpectedly. Know your figure before the pickup pulls in.
  2. Spread channels. Selling everything to one trader gives you no price information and no fallback. A common workable mix is a reliable base channel for most of the batch and a higher-margin channel for a portion, sized to what you can genuinely service.
  3. Time your batch to the market, not to your convenience. Demand for chicken and eggs spikes around festivals, holidays and month-ends when wages are paid. A 42-day broiler cycle can be counted backwards from a known date.

A Zambian example: a Lusaka producer with 600 broilers per cycle sold everything at the gate at $4.10 for two years. She trialled taking 150 birds a cycle to a market 22 km away and dressing 60 for two guesthouses. Gate sales still took the bulk of the flock, but the two extra channels added roughly $180 a cycle in net margin and, more importantly, told her every six weeks what the market price actually was - information her single trader had never had any reason to give her.

Lesson 8.5~12 min

Vertical Integration Opportunities

In this lesson
  • List the integration steps open to a small poultry producer and what each one saves
  • Calculate the break-even volume for milling your own feed
  • Judge whether rearing your own pullets or processing your own birds pays at your scale

Vertical integration means doing for yourself something you currently buy. Each step saves somebody else's margin and adds their work and their risk to yours. This lesson sets out the options and the break-even arithmetic for each. Whether integration is the right growth strategy for your business, and how to finance and sequence it, is dealt with in Module 12 Lessons 4 and 5.

Four steps are realistically open to a small African producer.

Step one: mill and mix your own feed. This is almost always the first and largest saving. Commercial compounded broiler feed might cost $0.62/kg where the ingredients to make the same ration cost $0.52/kg. That $0.10/kg is the whole prize. A hammer mill and a small mixer might cost $2,500 installed. Break-even volume = 2,500 / 0.10 = 25,000 kg of feed. A 500-bird broiler batch uses about 1,770 kg, so you need roughly 14 batches to repay the equipment. Running six cycles a year with one house, that is over two years. Running 260 birds a month across staggered houses, about 930 kg a month, you repay in roughly 27 months. Add the running costs - fuel or electricity at perhaps $0.012/kg, maintenance, screens, and your own time - and the true saving is nearer $0.08/kg, which pushes break-even to about 31,000 kg. Below roughly 800-1,000 kg a month, buy your feed and spend the capital elsewhere.

Step two: rear your own point-of-lay pullets instead of buying them. A pullet bought at $5.50 can be reared from a day-old chick at about $1.10. Cumulative feed to 17 weeks is commonly in the region of 5.5-6.5 kg per pullet - treat that as approximate and measure your own - so at $0.38/kg for rearing feed that is roughly $2.09-$2.47. Add vaccines, brooding fuel and labour of perhaps $0.80, and allow 6% mortality, and a home-reared pullet lands near $4.40. The saving is around $1.10 a bird. For 200 pullets that is $220. Against that you need a separate house for 17 weeks, a brooding setup, a vaccination programme you can execute properly, and the discipline to hit the Lohmann Brown-Classic 17-week target of 1.42 kg body weight. An underweight pullet at point of lay never fully recovers, and a flock that comes into lay light will cost you far more than $220 across the cycle. Rear your own only when you can weigh birds and hit the target.

Step three: process your own birds. Dressing adds price but it also adds a cold chain, a water supply, waste disposal and food-safety exposure. The margin per bird in the previous lesson looked attractive at roughly $2.29 over break-even, but that assumed every dressed bird sold within days. Without reliable refrigeration, unsold dressed birds are a total loss where unsold live birds simply eat for another day. Start with a small, fixed, pre-ordered number.

Step four: brood chicks for other farmers, selling four- or five-week-old birds. This uses brooding equipment you already own during the periods it would otherwise sit idle and shifts the highest-mortality phase of production onto your skills. It works only where other producers nearby want it, and it makes your biosecurity everyone else's problem too - which raises the standard you must keep.

Three cautions apply to every step.

  1. Integration converts a variable cost into a fixed cost. A mill you own must be paid for whether or not you have birds. If your production is irregular, integration increases your risk rather than reducing it.
  2. Each step needs a skill you may not have. Milling needs weighing discipline and mixer uniformity testing; pullet rearing needs vaccination technique; processing needs hygiene.
  3. Quality can fall. A home mix that misses the Ross specifications of 2,975 kcal/kg and 1.32% digestible lysine in starter will cost more in feed conversion than the $0.10/kg you saved on ingredients. The saving is only real if the ration is right, which is why Module 6 comes before this one.

A Kenyan example: a Kikuyu producer running 1,200 layers and 500 broilers per cycle bought a hammer mill and mixer for about $2,300. She was already using roughly 4,600 kg of feed a month across both enterprises, so at an $0.08/kg net saving she repaid the equipment in about seven months. Her neighbour, running one 400-bird broiler batch every ten weeks and using about 570 kg a month, bought the same equipment on the same advice and had not repaid it after four years. Same machine, same saving per kilogram, completely different answer - because volume, not enthusiasm, decides integration.

Lesson 8.6~12 min

Scaling and Business Growth

In this lesson
  • Calculate whether one additional house or batch actually adds margin
  • Identify the constraint that will break first when you grow
  • Set the performance conditions that must be met before you expand

This lesson covers one decision only: the first expansion. Should you add a second house, or a second batch, or more birds in the house you already have? Longer-run questions - how to sequence several expansions, how to finance them, when to formalise the business - are handled in Module 12 Lesson 6. Here we stay on the arithmetic of the next step.

Start with the honest test, which most producers skip. Expansion is only justified when the existing unit is already performing. If your current 500-bird house runs at FCR 2.05, 12% mortality and a production efficiency factor around 180, a second house will simply reproduce those numbers at twice the cost. Fixing the first house is almost always the higher-return investment. Set yourself conditions and write them down: mortality under 6%, FCR under 1.85, a full batch account completed for three consecutive batches, and a break-even price you can state from memory. If you cannot meet all four, expand later.

Now the arithmetic of a second house. From Lesson 2, one 500-bird batch produced $479.60 of margin on $1,515.40 of cost. A second identical house does not simply double that, and the differences run both ways.

What improves. Buying 1,000 chicks instead of 500 may earn a small discount, perhaps $0.03 each, worth $30. Buying ingredients in larger lots typically saves 3-6% - on $920 of feed per house that is $28-55 per house per batch. Your transport per bird falls because the same trip carries more. And your own time per bird falls, because checking 1,000 birds does not take twice as long as checking 500.

What worsens. You will pass the point where family labour is enough and must hire, adding perhaps $80-120 a month. Water supply, which was adequate for 500 birds, may need a bigger tank or a second line. Your buyer may not absorb 950 birds at once, so you may have to accept a lower price on the surplus or stagger sales. And your risk concentrates: a disease event now takes 1,000 birds, not 500.

Build the case. Second house construction at, say, $1,400 for a simple 60 square metre open-sided structure with mesh, feeders and drinkers. Extra margin per batch, allowing $40 of bulk savings but $70 of extra hired labour, is roughly $479.60 + $40 - $70 = $449.60 per batch. At six batches a year that is $2,698 a year. The building repays in about six months of operation. That looks obvious - until you check the working capital.

Working capital is the constraint that breaks most expansions. To run two houses you must hold enough cash to buy 1,000 chicks and roughly 3,540 kg of feed before any bird is sold - about $800 of chicks and $1,840 of feed, plus other inputs. Call it $2,900 tied up per cycle against about $1,515 today. If you also spent $1,400 on the building, you need roughly $2,800 of new money at once. Many producers build the house, run out of cash at week three, and feed a diluted ration - which destroys the FCR that justified the expansion in the first place.

So the correct sequence is: build the house only after you can fund a full cycle of feed for both houses. If you cannot, expand in a smaller step. Options in order of capital required: raise your existing batch modestly if your density and season allow it; run more batches a year by cutting turnaround time, which needs no new building at all; or add a small second house of 250 birds rather than 500.

That middle option deserves attention. If you currently run five batches a year with three weeks of turnaround, tightening cleaning and chick ordering to a 14-day downtime can give you six batches. That is a 20% increase in annual margin - roughly $480 a year in the worked example - for no capital at all. Look for that before you look for a builder.

Finally, decide what you will watch. Set three numbers to review after every batch in the new arrangement: production efficiency factor, cost per bird sold, and margin per batch. If any of them is worse than the single-house figures for two consecutive batches, stop and diagnose before growing further.

A Ugandan example: a producer near Wakiso with one 500-bird house planned a second. Rather than build immediately, she first cut her turnaround from 24 days to 14, going from five to six batches a year, and used the extra $470 of annual margin plus savings to fund the feed for a second house the following year. When she did build, she had the working capital to run both houses properly from day one. Her production efficiency factor in the new house matched the old one within two batches - which is the real test of whether an expansion worked.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. What is the correct denominator when calculating flock FCR?

Feed eaten by birds that later died is real feed you paid for. Using total feed issued over total live weight sold gives the true, less flattering figure.

2. Liveability 95%, weight 2.05 kg, 42 days, FCR 1.78. What is the production efficiency factor?

(95 x 2.05) / (42 x 1.78) x 100 = 194.75 / 74.76 x 100 = 261.

3. Why does late mortality damage FCR more than early mortality?

Feed consumption is cumulative. A death at day 3 wastes a few hundred grams; a death at day 38 wastes several kilograms of the most expensive feed in the cycle.

4. Why can FCR alone mislead when comparing two batches?

FCR naturally worsens as birds get heavier. The production efficiency factor combines liveability, weight, age and FCR so batches of different lengths can be compared fairly.

5. Which FCR loss has no biological cause at all?

Spilled feed is counted as issued but never eaten. It is pure loss and is usually the largest and cheapest FCR problem to fix on a small farm.

6. In the worked batch, which two lines should a producer attack first?

Feed is about 61% of cost and chicks 26%. Halving labour saves $30; cutting feed cost 8% saves $74 and cutting mortality from 5% to 3% adds around $42 of revenue.

7. Why should family labour be costed into the batch account?

Unpaid family time is a real input. Costing it keeps the account honest and reveals whether the enterprise can survive when the work has to be paid for.

8. Total cost is $1,515.40 and 475 birds are sold. What is the break-even price per bird?

$1,515.40 divided by 475 birds sold gives $3.19. Any sale below that price is subsidising the buyer.

9. Why does high late mortality hurt the account more than high early mortality?

Feed cost is cumulative. When mortality rose to 12% in the example, revenue fell by $147 while feed cost barely changed, because those birds had already eaten most of their feed.

10. Maize rises 25% and is 55% of the ration by weight. What happens to feed cost per kg?

A 25% rise on the 55% of the ration that is maize raises the total by roughly 0.25 x 0.55 = 13.75%, taking $0.52 to about $0.575 per kilogram.

11. Why does a 19% fall in lay cause a 60% fall in profit per dozen?

Feed intake is driven by appetite, not by output. When production drops, the monthly cost barely moves, so cost per dozen rises sharply and margin collapses.

12. What is pullet depreciation and why must it be in the account?

A $5.50 pullet laying for 12 months costs $0.46 a month. Omitting it makes the flock look profitable when it may only be covering feed.

13. A flock uses 660 kg of feed and produces 400 dozen eggs. What is feed per dozen?

660 / 400 = 1.65 kg per dozen, slightly worse than the full-cycle Hy-Line range of 1.55-1.61, which points to wastage or low lay.

14. A cheaper pullet that lays 8% less is usually a bad buy because what?

In the example, saving $0.11 per bird per month cost around 32 dozen eggs worth $49.60. Pullet quality is bought once and paid for every day.

15. Which adjustment should a late-cycle flock get first?

From around week 77 hens need 4.7 g of calcium a day and benefit from a larger coarse fraction for overnight shell formation. Protein requirement falls rather than rises.

16. Why is the highest headline price often not the best channel?

The town market at $4.90 nets $4.70 after transport, crates, transit losses and your own time. Compare net price per unit, not headline price.

17. Why is a 30-day payment term a real cost even though nothing is deducted?

Cash timing determines whether you can restock. A price premium that stops you placing the next flock is a loss, not a gain.

18. What is the main non-price benefit of selling through more than one channel?

A single trader has no reason to tell you the market price. Multiple channels give continuous price information and reduce dependence on one relationship.

19. Why should a broiler batch be timed backwards from a known demand date?

The cycle length is known and fixed. Counting back 42 days from a demand peak is free and can add several per cent to the price you receive.

20. What must you know before a trader arrives unexpectedly at the gate?

Break-even changes with feed prices and mortality. Knowing this batch's figure is the only defence against accepting a price below cost under time pressure.

21. Equipment costs $2,500 and the net saving is $0.08/kg. What volume repays it?

$2,500 divided by $0.08 per kilogram is 31,250 kg of feed. The headline $0.10/kg saving falls to about $0.08 once fuel, maintenance and your time are counted.

22. Why does integration increase risk for a producer with irregular production?

Buying feed costs nothing when you have no birds. Owning a mill costs its capital repayment regardless, so irregular production makes ownership worse, not better.

23. What is the main risk of rearing your own pullets?

The Lohmann target of 1.42 kg at 17 weeks is the quality control point. An underweight pullet never fully recovers, and the lost production far exceeds the roughly $1.10 saved per bird.

24. Why is processing your own birds riskier than selling them live?

Dressing removes your ability to hold stock. Without a reliable cold chain, any bird not sold within days is written off entirely.

25. Why did the same mill repay in seven months for one producer and never for another?

The saving is per kilogram, so the payback period depends entirely on throughput. Volume, not enthusiasm, decides whether integration pays.

26. What is the first test before building a second house?

A second house reproduces the first house's performance at twice the cost. If the current unit is running poorly, fixing it returns more than expanding does.

27. Which constraint most often breaks an expansion?

Two houses need roughly $2,900 tied up per cycle against $1,515 for one. Producers who build first and run out of cash at week three end up diluting the ration.

28. How can annual margin rise about 20% with no capital outlay?

Shorter downtime between flocks fits an extra batch into the year. In the worked example that is roughly $480 of extra annual margin for no new building.

29. Which cost reliably rises when you move from one house to two?

Bulk buying lowers feed and chick prices slightly and transport per bird falls, but hired labour of perhaps $80-120 a month is a new fixed cost.

30. Which three numbers should be reviewed after every batch in a new expansion?

Those three capture biological performance, cost control and profitability. Two consecutive batches worse than the single-house figures means stop and diagnose.

Module 8 capstone

Close the books on one complete batch and prove the numbers. Week 1: open a batch record for your current or next flock, listing birds placed, chick cost, and the opening stock of every input. Record daily mortality, daily feed issued in kilograms and daily eggs or weekly sample weights from day one. Week 2: collect and file every receipt - feed ingredients, vaccines, litter, brooding fuel, labour, transport, trays, electricity - and allocate each one to the batch. Week 3: at sale or at the end of the month, calculate feed conversion ratio, and for broilers calculate the production efficiency factor using the formula in Lesson 1; for layers calculate kilograms of feed per dozen eggs and compare it with the Hy-Line range of 1.23-1.28 kg at peak and 1.55-1.61 kg across a full cycle. Week 4: produce a one-page batch account showing total cost, cost per bird sold or per dozen eggs, revenue by channel, and margin per batch. Then identify the single largest cost line, state one change that would reduce it, and calculate how much that change must save before it repays itself. Present real figures from your own farm, not the example figures in these lessons.
