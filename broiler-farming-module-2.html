1. Site Selection and Orientation for Broiler Houses

Choosing the right site for your broiler house is the first step to healthy birds and good profits. In Africa, you need a site that is well-drained, away from swamps or areas that flood. The ground should slope slightly (1-2%) so water runs away from the house. Avoid low-lying areas where cold air and moisture collect. Also, keep the site at least 100 meters away from other poultry farms, markets, and busy roads to reduce disease spread and stress. The site should have easy access to clean water and electricity (or space for solar panels).

Orientation matters for natural ventilation. In most of Africa, the sun is strong. Build the house with its long axis running east to west. This reduces direct sunlight on the side walls, keeping the house cooler. If you build north-south, the sun will heat the long sides and make the inside too hot. Also, consider the prevailing wind direction. The house should face the wind so that fresh air blows through. In hot climates, a east-west orientation with open sides allows cross ventilation.

Clear the site of bushes and trees at least 10 meters around the house to reduce rodents, snakes, and wild birds that carry diseases. The area should be fenced to keep out stray animals. Make sure there is space for a footbath at the entrance and a storage area for feed. A good site also has room for future expansion. Remember, a bad site will cost you money in repairs, disease treatment, and poor growth. Take time to select the best spot before you build.

2. Design and Construction of Broiler Houses

A broiler house must be strong, well-ventilated, and easy to clean. The most common design in Africa is the open-sided house with wire mesh walls and a roof. The floor should be concrete or packed earth covered with litter (wood shavings or rice husks). Concrete is best because it can be washed and disinfected. The walls can be made of local materials like mud bricks, cement blocks, or timber, but they must be smooth to prevent hiding places for pests. The height of the walls should be at least 2.5 meters to allow good airflow.

The roof is critical. Use corrugated iron sheets or thatch (thatch is cooler but flammable). Provide a overhang of at least 1 meter to keep rain out. For a house of 1000 birds, you need about 100 square meters (10 birds per square meter). The width should not exceed 10 meters for natural ventilation. The length can be 10 to 30 meters. Inside, you need a central walkway and feeders and drinkers spaced evenly.

Construct a concrete apron around the house to prevent rats from burrowing and to keep the area dry. Install wire mesh (1-inch holes) on the sides to keep out birds and rodents. The mesh should be removable or have shutters for adjusting ventilation. The floor should slope towards a drain for easy cleaning. Use treated timber for trusses to resist termites. Nails and screws should be galvanized to prevent rust. A well-built house will last 10-15 years and protect your investment.

3. Ventilation Systems: Natural and Mechanical

Ventilation removes heat, moisture, ammonia, and carbon dioxide from the broiler house. Without it, birds suffocate or get sick. Natural ventilation uses wind and open sides. In Africa, most small farms rely on natural ventilation because it's cheap and doesn't need electricity. For natural ventilation, the house should have open sides with wire mesh and adjustable curtains. The roof should have a ridge vent to let hot air escape. The width of the house should not exceed 10 meters so air can move across. In hot weather, open all curtains; in cold, close them to keep warmth.

Mechanical ventilation uses fans to move air. This is for large farms or areas with little wind. You need exhaust fans on one end and air inlets on the other. A common setup is tunnel ventilation: fans pull air from one end to the other at a speed of 2-3 meters per second. For 1000 birds, you need about 2-3 fans of 50 cm diameter. Mechanical ventilation requires reliable electricity, so have a backup generator or solar system. It also uses more energy, so insulate the house well.

Monitor ventilation by checking for ammonia smell (should not sting your eyes) and humidity (litter should not be wet). In hot climates, use foggers or sprinklers on the roof to cool the air. Always provide 24-hour ventilation, but avoid drafts directly on chicks. Adjust based on bird age: chicks need warm, fresh air; adult birds need more airflow. Poor ventilation causes respiratory diseases and poor feed conversion. Invest in good ventilation for healthy birds.

4. Feeding and Drinking Equipment Setup

Broilers need constant access to feed and clean water. For feeding, you can use tube feeders or trough feeders. Tube feeders are best because they reduce waste and keep feed clean. For 1000 birds, you need about 30 tube feeders (each 15 kg capacity) or 20 linear meters of trough. Place feeders evenly so all birds can eat. The feeder height should be adjusted as birds grow: the bottom of the feeder should be at the level of the birds' backs. This reduces spillage. For chicks, use flat trays or small feeders for the first week.

Drinking equipment includes bell drinkers, nipple drinkers, or troughs. Nipple drinkers are the most hygienic and save water, but they need a pressurized system. Bell drinkers are common and easy: one bell drinker (40 cm diameter) serves 80-100 birds. For 1000 birds, use 10-12 bell drinkers. Place drinkers and feeders alternately so birds don't have to travel far. Clean and refill drinkers at least twice a day. In hot weather, birds drink 2-3 times more water, so provide extra drinkers.

Set up the brooding area with feeders and drinkers close together for the first week. Use 1 feeder and 1 drinker per 100 chicks. As birds grow, spread equipment out. Always provide 24-hour access to feed and water. Use a feed formula that matches the birds' age: starter (0-3 weeks), grower (4-5 weeks), finisher (6 weeks+). Water must be clean and free from bacteria. Add vitamins and electrolytes in hot weather. Proper equipment setup reduces stress and improves growth.

5. Brooding Equipment and Setup

Brooding is the first 7-14 days of a chick's life. Chicks cannot regulate their body temperature, so you must provide artificial heat. Common brooding equipment includes charcoal stoves, kerosene lamps, gas brooders, or electric bulbs. For 100 chicks, use one 100-watt bulb or a charcoal stove that heats a 2-meter diameter area. In Africa, many farmers use charcoal stoves because they are cheap and available. But you must ensure good ventilation to avoid carbon monoxide poisoning. Electric brooders are cleaner but need reliable power.

Set up the brooding area with a brooder guardâa circular cardboard or metal sheet about 50 cm highâto keep chicks near the heat and prevent drafts. The floor should have 5-10 cm of clean litter (wood shavings). Spread feed on flat trays or paper for the first 3 days. Provide water in shallow drinkers (1 per 50 chicks). Preheat the brooder 24 hours before chicks arrive to 35Â°C at chick level. Reduce temperature by 3Â°C each week until it matches room temperature (around 25Â°C).

Monitor chick behavior: if they huddle under the heat, it's too cold; if they spread away, it's too hot; if they are evenly spread, it's just right. Check the brooder temperature with a thermometer at chick height. Adjust by raising or lowering the heat source or opening vents. Brooding is intensive but critical: chicks that are cold or stressed will die or grow poorly. Keep the brooder dry and clean. After 2 weeks, you can remove the brooder guard and expand the space. Always have a backup heat source in case of power failure.

6. Biosecurity Infrastructure and Footbaths

Biosecurity means keeping diseases out of your farm. The first line of defense is a footbath at the entrance of each house. A footbath is a shallow container (e.g., a plastic basin or concrete trough) filled with disinfectant. Every person must dip their feet before entering. Use a disinfectant like glutaraldehyde or bleach (1:100 dilution). Change the solution daily or when dirty. Also, provide boots and overalls for workers. Keep a separate pair of boots for each house to avoid cross-contamination.

Other biosecurity infrastructure includes a perimeter fence to keep out wild animals and unauthorized people. A gate with a lock is essential. Provide a hand-washing station with soap and water at the entrance. Install a vehicle dip or spray at the farm entrance if you have many visitors. For feed storage, keep it in a closed room with rodent-proof walls. Store feed on pallets, not on the floor. Have a designated area for dead bird disposal (incinerator or deep pit) away from the houses.

Clean and disinfect the house between flocks. Remove all litter, wash with detergent, then apply disinfectant. Let it dry for 3-5 days before bringing new chicks. Use a footbath at each house entrance and at the feed store. Limit visitors; if they must enter, they should wear protective clothing. Keep records of all visitors and any disease events. A simple footbath and strict hygiene can save you from losing your entire flock. In Africa, where veterinary services may be far, prevention is your best medicine. Invest in biosecurity from day one.

Practical Reference: Broiler Housing & Equipment in African Conditions

This practical reference consolidates the six lessons in the Housing and Equipment module into a single field-ready guide. It is written for the African broiler producer who must make capital-efficient decisions under heat stress, variable power supply, and tight biosecurity pressure. Every recommendation here is framed around one question: what protects margin per bird? Housing and equipment are not costs to minimise â they are the production system that determines feed conversion, mortality, and market weight.

1. Site Selection and Orientation

Site selection is the cheapest decision you will ever make and the most expensive one to get wrong. In most of sub-Saharan Africa, the dominant environmental constraint is heat, not cold. A house oriented eastâwest with the long axis running eastâwest exposes the large side walls to the full arc of the sun and creates uneven heating. The standard recommendation is to orient the long axis eastâwest in hot climates so that the gable ends face east and west and the side walls receive oblique rather than direct solar load â but orientation must be balanced against prevailing wind. If the prevailing wind is northâsouth, orienting the ridge northâsouth maximises cross-ventilation. The rule of thumb: prioritise prevailing wind for natural ventilation, then minimise solar load on the long walls.

Other site criteria: well-drained soil (avoid black cotton soils that waterlog), at least 100 m from other poultry houses and 300 m from main roads to reduce dust and disease entry, reliable all-weather access for feed trucks, and a slope of 2â3% for drainage. Water quantity and quality matter more than most producers assume â a broiler house of 1,000 birds consumes roughly 100â150 litres per day in hot weather.

3. Ventilation Systems

Natural ventilation relies on wind pressure and thermal buoyancy. In open-sided tropical houses, it is usually sufficient â but only if the house is oriented correctly, has no obstruction within 10 m of the windward side, and has ridge openings or a raised ridge cap. The critical failure mode in Africa is still air at midday combined with 38 Â°C ambient, when the stack effect is weak and birds suffer heat stress. Mechanical ventilation (fans) is a backup, not a replacement. Tunnel ventilation with 1â2 fans of 0.5â1.0 m diameter per 1,000 birds can move air at 1.5â2.5 m/s across the flock. Solar-powered fans with battery backup are increasingly viable and avoid dependence on unreliable grid power.

4. Feeding and Drinking Equipment

Feed and water are 65â75% of broiler production cost. Equipment that spills feed or leaks water destroys margin. The two dominant systems in African broiler production are round tube feeders (hanging, adjustable height) and linear trough feeders; and bell drinkers or nipple lines. Nipple drinkers are more hygienic, reduce litter wetting, and cut labour, but require reliable water pressure (0.3â1.0 bar) and filtration â a common failure point where water is pumped from boreholes with sediment.

Drinkers: one bell drinker per 80â100 birds, or one nipple per 8â10 birds. 5. Brooding Equipment and Setup

Brooding is the highest-risk phase: the first 7 days determine flock uniformity and final live weight. The chick needs a floor temperature of 32â34 Â°C at day 1, reduced by roughly 3 Â°C per week until 21â24 Â°C at week 3â4. In Africa, the two practical brooding systems are charcoal/wood stove brooders (cheap, widely used, but risk of carbon monoxide and fire) and gas or electric infrared brooders (cleaner, controllable, higher capital). A third option â solar-powered brooder with heat storage â is emerging in East Africa and suits off-grid farms.

Set up a brooding ring (cardboard or metal) 2â3 m diameter for 250â300 chicks, with feed and water at the edge, not under the heat source. Use a "chick behaviour" check: if chicks crowd under the heat source, it is too cold; if they spread to the ring edge, too hot; if evenly distributed, correct. Provide 23 hours of light for the first 7 days, then reduce. Pre-heat the house 24 hours before chick arrival â cold floors kill chicks faster than cold air.

6. Biosecurity Infrastructure and Footbaths

Biosecurity is the cheapest insurance in broiler farming. The infrastructure layer includes: a perimeter fence, a single controlled entry point, a changing area with boots and overalls, wheel dips for vehicles, footbaths at every house door, and a carcass/mortality disposal pit or incinerator. Footbaths must contain a fresh disinfectant solution â the common failure is a footbath that is dirty, dry, or contains the wrong concentration. Use a proven disinfectant (e.g., glutaraldehyde, quaternary ammonium, or sodium hypochlorite) at the manufacturer's dilution, and refresh at least daily, more often in muddy conditions.

All-in/all-out management, with a 7â14 day clean-out between batches, breaks disease cycles. In African markets where live-bird traders and middlemen enter farms, control human traffic strictly: no visitors into houses, no shared crates, and vehicle wheels disinfected at the gate. Record every entry â a simple logbook is a biosecurity tool, not paperwork.
