Lessons

1. Designing Your Operational Workflow

Your operational workflow is the step-by-step path your product or service takes from raw materials to the customer's hands. For a small African business, this must be simple, written down, and visible to your team. Start by listing every step in order. For example, if you bake bread, your workflow might be: buy flour, mix dough, proof, bake, cool, package, deliver. Write each step on a card and arrange them on a table. This is your first workflow map.

Next, assign time and cost to each step. If mixing takes 20 minutes and baking takes 40 minutes, you know your total production time per batch. If you can bake 50 loaves per batch, and each loaf sells for 50 KES, you can calculate revenue per batch. Identify bottlenecks: the step that slows everything down. If your oven can only bake 30 loaves at a time, that's a bottleneck. You might need a second oven or longer hours.

Use simple tools to document your workflow. A notebook and pen work fine. If you have a smartphone, use free apps like Google Keep or Trello to list steps and check them off. For more complex operations, draw a flowchart on a large paper or a whiteboard. Keep it where workers can see it. Train each worker on their specific steps. For example, one person mixes, another bakes, another packages. This division of labour speeds up production.

Review your workflow weekly. Ask: Where did we waste time? Where did we waste money? Adjust. If you find that delivering to distant customers takes too long, consider grouping deliveries by area. If you're a tailor, your workflow might be: take measurements, cut fabric, sew, iron, deliver. Measure how long each takes and see if you can combine steps. Always look for ways to reduce steps without reducing quality. A smooth workflow means more output, happier customers, and higher profit.

2. Supply Chain and Vendor Management

Your supply chain is the network of suppliers, transporters, and warehouses that get materials to you. In Africa, this can be challenging due to poor roads, unreliable suppliers, and currency fluctuations. But with good planning, you can keep your business running. Start by identifying your key inputs. If you run a restaurant, you need vegetables, meat, cooking oil, and gas. List all suppliers for each input. Don't rely on one supplier; have at least two for critical items. For example, if you buy tomatoes from one farmer and they have a bad harvest, you need a backup.

Build relationships with vendors. Visit their farms or shops. Agree on prices, delivery schedules, and quality standards. Put it in writing, even if it's a simple handwritten agreement. For example, 'Supplier will deliver 20 kg of tomatoes every Monday by 8 AM at 100 KES per kg.' Pay on time to build trust. If you can, negotiate bulk discounts. Buying 100 kg of rice at once might get you a 10% discount. But don't overbuy perishables.

Manage risks. In many African cities, traffic can delay deliveries. So order early. If you import goods, consider currency risk. If the dollar rises, your costs go up. You might set aside a small buffer of cash. Also, check quality upon delivery. Reject spoiled goods immediately. Keep records of every purchase: date, supplier, quantity, price, and quality. Use a notebook or a free app like Wave or Excel. This helps you track price trends and supplier performance.

Finally, pay attention to transport. If you pick up goods yourself, calculate fuel and time. Sometimes it's cheaper to pay a supplier to deliver. If you use a boda boda, agree on a rate. For larger loads, consider sharing a truck with another business. Always have a backup plan: if your main supplier fails, who will you call? Keep a list of alternative vendors. Good supply chain management means no stockouts, less waste, and satisfied customers.

3. Technology and Tools for Operations

Technology can make your small business run faster and cheaper. You don't need expensive equipment. Start with a smartphone. Almost every African entrepreneur has one. Use it for more than calls and WhatsApp. For record-keeping, download free apps like Wave (for invoicing) or Kobo Collect (for data). If you sell products, use a simple point-of-sale app like Loyverse. It tracks sales, inventory, and profits. You can get a basic Android phone for under $50.

For communication, use WhatsApp groups to coordinate with staff and suppliers. Send daily updates, share photos of goods, and confirm orders. For payments, mobile money like M-Pesa, Airtel Money, or MTN MoMo is essential. It's safe, fast, and widely accepted. Set up a business account if possible. For online presence, create a free Facebook page or Instagram account. Post photos of your products with prices. Many customers will message you to order.

For operations, consider cloud storage. Google Drive is free and lets you store documents, photos, and spreadsheets. You can access them from any phone. Use Google Sheets to track inventory, expenses, and customer lists. If you have a computer, learn basic Excel. For inventory, a simple barcode scanner app can help, but for small shops, a notebook works. If you deliver, use Google Maps to plan routes and avoid traffic.

Don't forget power. In areas with unreliable electricity, invest in a small solar panel and power bank to keep your phone charged. A solar kit costs about $30. For larger operations, a generator might be needed, but calculate fuel costs. Also, protect your data: use a password on your phone and back up to Google Drive weekly. Train your staff on using these tools. For example, show a salesperson how to record sales in Loyverse. Technology should simplify, not complicate. Start with one tool, master it, then add another.

4. Organizational Structure and Roles

Even a one-person business needs an organizational structure. As you grow, you'll need to define who does what. Start by listing all the tasks in your business: buying, production, sales, delivery, record-keeping, customer service. Now group similar tasks into roles. For a small bakery, you might have: Baker (production), Salesperson (front counter), and Delivery Person. If you're solo, you do all three. But write down each role and its responsibilities. This helps you delegate and avoid confusion.

Draw an organogram. It's a simple chart showing who reports to whom. At the top is you, the owner. Below are the roles. If you have a manager, they might oversee workers. For a small business, keep it flat: owner and workers. For example, a tailoring shop: Owner (you) handles design and customer orders; Tailor 1 sews; Tailor 2 sews; Apprentice does ironing and cleaning. Each person knows their job.

Define each role in writing. For each role, list 3-5 key duties. For example, Salesperson: greet customers, record sales in notebook, handle cash, keep display clean. This prevents overlap and gaps. When hiring, use these role descriptions to find the right person. Also, decide on pay. In many African countries, minimum wage varies. In Kenya, for example, the minimum wage for a general worker is about 15,000 KES per month. Pay fairly but within your budget. Consider incentives: a salesperson might get a commission of 5% on sales above a target.

Review your structure every 6 months. As you grow, you might need a supervisor or an accountant. Don't create roles you don't need. Keep it simple. Also, ensure clear communication. Hold a short daily meeting (5 minutes) to plan the day. Write down who is responsible for what. This accountability makes your business run smoothly. Remember, a good structure lets you step away without everything falling apart.

5. Hiring and Training Staff

Hiring the right people is critical. Start by writing a simple job description. For example, 'We need a sales assistant. Duties: greet customers, record sales, keep shop clean. Must be able to read and write, and be honest.' Post the job where people will see it: WhatsApp groups, local notice boards, church, or market. Ask for references. When you interview, ask practical questions: 'If a customer complains about a product, what do you do?' 'How would you record a sale?' Look for honesty and willingness to learn, not just experience.

Once hired, train them. Don't assume they know how to do things your way. Spend the first day showing them around. Explain your workflow. Demonstrate each task. For example, show how to use the mobile money app to receive payments. Let them practice while you watch. Give feedback. Use a checklist: Did they greet the customer? Did they record the sale correctly? Repeat training for a week. For more complex tasks, like baking, you might need a longer apprenticeship. Consider pairing a new hire with an experienced worker.

Pay attention to motivation. Pay on time. Provide a clean, safe workplace. Offer small incentives: a bonus for perfect attendance, or a free lunch. In many African cultures, respect and recognition matter. Praise good work publicly. If someone makes a mistake, correct privately. Also, invest in cross-training. If your salesperson can also do basic bookkeeping, you're less vulnerable if someone is absent.

Keep records for each employee: name, contact, start date, pay rate, and any warnings. Use a notebook or a simple Excel sheet. Follow labour laws: give written contracts, pay minimum wage, provide leave as required. In Kenya, for example, employees are entitled to 21 days of annual leave. Even if you're small, treat staff fairly. Happy staff lead to happy customers. And remember, you can't do everything alone. Hiring and training well is an investment in your business's future.

6. Legal and Regulatory Compliance

Every business must follow the law. In Africa, regulations vary by country, but some basics apply. First, register your business. In Kenya, you can register a sole proprietorship online via the eCitizen portal for about 1,000 KES. In Nigeria, register with the Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC). Registration gives you a legal identity, allows you to open a bank account, and helps you avoid fines. You'll need a business name, address, and ID.

Next, get necessary licenses. If you sell food, you need a health certificate from the local health department. If you operate a bar, you need a liquor license. If you import goods, you need an import license. Check with your local county or municipal office. Fees vary: a health certificate might cost 2,000 KES per year. Also, pay taxes. Even small businesses must pay tax. In Kenya, if your turnover is below 5 million KES, you can use the presumptive tax regime. Keep records of all sales and expenses. File returns annually. The Kenya Revenue Authority (KRA) has an online portal. If you don't pay, you face penalties.

Compliance also means following labour laws. Give written contracts. Pay minimum wage. Deduct and remit statutory deductions like NSSF and NHIF in Kenya. For employees, you must also provide a safe working environment. If you have a shop, ensure fire extinguishers and clear exits. For food businesses, maintain hygiene: wash hands, cover food, and keep premises clean.

Finally, protect your brand. Register your trademark if you can. It costs about 10,000 KES in Kenya. Also, get insurance: fire, theft, or liability. It might seem expensive, but it protects you from shocks. Keep all documents in a safe place. Use a folder or a cloud folder. Review compliance every year. Laws change, so stay informed. Visit your local business office or check government websites. Being compliant builds trust with customers, suppliers, and banks. It also lets you sleep at night.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Operations & Management Plan

This practical reference consolidates the six lessons in the Operations and Management Plan module. It is written for African agribusiness founders and managers who need to move from idea to a bankable, fundable, and legally compliant operating model. Use it as a working document: copy the tables into your business plan, adapt the workflow diagram, and walk the checklist before you submit to lenders, grant committees, or investors.

1. Designing Your Operational Workflow

Your workflow is the sequence of activities that turns inputs into a saleable product. In African agribusiness, the biggest operational risks are seasonal cash-flow gaps, post-harvest losses, and unreliable input quality. A practical workflow must specify, for each stage: the activity, the responsible role, the equipment or facility, the quality standard, and the record produced. The diagram above shows five stages with two feedback loops: input quality and cost data flow back from production, and market demand signals flow back from distribution. Both loops should be reviewed monthly.

2. Supply Chain and Vendor Management

Diversify vendors to reduce single-source risk. For seed, feed, and agrochemicals, insist on certified suppliers and keep batch records. For logistics, negotiate volume-based rates with at least two transporters. Where cooperatives or outgrower schemes are used, formalise the relationship with written supply agreements stating quantity, quality, price mechanism, and delivery windows.

3. Technology and Tools for Operations

Technology should solve a specific operational bottleneck, not add complexity. Common high-return tools in African agribusiness include mobile money for payments, SMS or USSD for farmer communication, simple inventory apps, and solar-powered cold storage or irrigation. Choose tools with offline capability, local language support, and low data requirements.

4. Organizational Structure and Roles

Structure follows strategy and scale. A start-up may run with four roles: Managing Director, Operations Manager, Finance/Admin Officer, and Field/Production Supervisor. As you grow, separate sales, quality assurance, and HR. Document each role with a one-page job description: purpose, key tasks, KPIs, and reporting line. Avoid overlapping responsibilities, which cause conflict and control failures.

5. Hiring and Training Staff

Hire for reliability and train for skill. In rural Africa, referrals and community reputation are strong signals. Use structured interviews with practical tasks. Provide induction on safety, hygiene, and record-keeping. Budget 2-5% of payroll for annual training. Cross-train staff so operations continue during leave or absence.

6. Legal and Regulatory Compliance

Compliance is not optional if you want bank finance or export markets. At minimum: register the business, obtain a tax identification number, comply with labour law, and secure sector permits (e.g., NAFDAC in Nigeria, KEBS in Kenya, Ghana Standards Authority). For export, check phytosanitary and destination-market requirements.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Workflow mapped stage-by-stage with responsible roles and quality standards.
  2. At least two vendors per critical input, with written supply agreements.
  3. Technology chosen to solve a specific bottleneck; offline capability confirmed.
  4. Organogram and one-page job descriptions for every role.
  5. Hiring process includes practical assessment and reference checks.
  6. Training budget set at 2-5% of payroll; induction covers safety and records.
  7. Business registration, tax ID, and sector permits current and filed.
  8. Monthly review of feedback loops: input quality, costs, demand signals.
