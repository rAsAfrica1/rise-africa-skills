Lesson 8.1~12 min

Equipment and Facility Planning

In this lesson
  • Specify equipment and capital cost for three realistic scales of production
  • Calculate realistic monthly capacity from mixer size and batch cycle time
  • Lay out a facility with a one-way flow from raw materials to finished goods

Most producers buy equipment in the wrong order and end up with a bottleneck they cannot afford to fix. Decide your monthly volume first, then buy the machine that sets your capacity, then buy everything else to match it.

There are three realistic scales, and the jump between them is large.

Scale one, the kitchen unit, producing 20 to 40 kilograms a day. A double gas burner at USD 60, heavy pots, a 1.5 horsepower meat mincer at USD 220, a cabinet drier or converted oven at USD 400, a 50 kilogram platform scale at USD 45, a balance reading to 0.01 g for micro-ingredients at USD 180, a hand impulse sealer at USD 65, and stainless or sealed work tables at USD 150. About USD 1,200 to 1,600. This is where nearly every producer should start, because it lets you learn the recipes and find customers before you owe anybody money.

Scale two, the small commercial unit, producing 400 to 600 kilograms a day. A 5 horsepower hammer mill at USD 900, a 200 kilogram horizontal ribbon mixer at USD 1,800, a 5 horsepower mincer at USD 750, a flat-die pellet mill rated 200 kilograms an hour at USD 2,200, a batch drier at USD 2,500, a 300 kilogram platform scale at USD 250, a sealer with a date coder at USD 400, bins and pallets at USD 500 and a spares kit at USD 400. That is about USD 9,700 in machinery, plus roughly USD 1,500 for electrical installation, guards and a starter panel, giving about USD 11,200. This is the scale most of this course is written for.

Scale three, extrusion. A single-screw extruder with its drier and coater starts around USD 45,000 and needs three-phase power, a trained operator and a very different sales volume. Do not go there until you are consistently selling everything scale two can make.

Now calculate capacity honestly, because this is what you will promise a retailer. A 200 kilogram mixer with a full cycle of weighing, grinding, mixing, pelleting, drying, cooling and packing might complete four batches in a working day, which is 800 kilograms a day in theory. Realistic output after cleaning, changeovers, downtime and quality holds is commonly 60 to 70 percent of theory, so plan on 480 to 560 kilograms a day. Over 22 working days that is 10,500 to 12,300 kilograms a month. Work out your own figure before you sign any supply agreement.

Power is a cost people forget. A 5 horsepower hammer mill draws about 3.7 kilowatts, a 3 horsepower mixer about 2.2, a pellet mill about 7.5, giving roughly 14 kilowatts when all are running. Six hours of running is 84 kilowatt hours, which at USD 0.18 a unit is USD 15.10 a day. Producing 500 kilograms that day, electricity is USD 0.030 a kilogram. If your supply is unreliable, cost a generator into the plan rather than discovering it later.

Layout is free to get right on paper and expensive to fix in concrete. Design a one-way flow with no crossing paths: raw material store, then weighing, then grinding and mixing, then forming and drying, then cooling, then packing, then finished goods store. Raw material and finished product must never share a door or a shelf. For a scale two unit, about 60 square metres works: raw store 15, processing 20, drying 10, packing 8, finished store 12 and a small office and wash area. Add smooth washable floors that slope to a drain, walls that can be wiped, insect screens on every opening, and pallets or timber that keep every sack at least 15 centimetres off the floor and 30 centimetres from the wall so you can sweep and inspect behind them.

Hygiene is a schedule, not an attitude. Daily: sweep and wipe all contact surfaces, empty and brush out the mixer, wash tools, remove waste from the building. Weekly: open and clean inside the mixer and the mill, check for rodent signs, clean drains. Monthly: deep clean the raw store, check insect screens, service and grease equipment. Write it on a card, sign it, and keep the cards.

An example from Nairobi, Kenya. A producer moved from her kitchen into a rented 70 square metre unit at USD 180 a month. She bought the mixer, mill and sealer first at USD 3,100, kept using a gas drier for six months, and only bought the pellet mill once she was selling 1,200 kilograms a month. Staging the purchases meant her capital outlay never exceeded what her sales could service, and the pellet mill was bought from cash flow rather than debt.

Lesson 8.2~13 min

Regulatory Compliance and Labelling

In this lesson
  • State correctly what standing AAFCO nutrient profiles have in African countries
  • Name the national bodies to approach and the questions to ask them
  • Control net weight, batch coding and date marking as production tasks

Everything this course teaches about nutrient profiles comes from AAFCO, the Association of American Feed Control Officials. Be blunt about what that means. AAFCO is a United States model. Its profiles are the most widely used technical reference in the world and they are a sound basis for formulation anywhere, but AAFCO has no legal force in Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana, Tanzania, Uganda, Zambia, Zimbabwe, Malawi, South Africa or anywhere else on the continent. Do not print an AAFCO statement on a bag because a course told you to, and do not assume that meeting AAFCO makes you compliant at home.

Who you actually ask depends on your country, and pet food may sit under animal feed law, agricultural inputs, a standards body, a veterinary authority, or almost nothing. The bodies to approach include the Kenya Bureau of Standards, the Standards Organisation of Nigeria and NAFDAC, the Ghana Standards Authority, the Tanzania Bureau of Standards, and in South Africa the registrar acting under the Fertilizers, Farm Feeds, Agricultural Remedies and Stock Remedies Act, Act 36. Which of those applies to you, what registration costs, what testing they demand and what wording they require are questions only your own regulator can answer. Telephone them, visit them, and write down what they say with the date and the name of the officer. That hour is free and it is the most valuable hour in this module. The business-planning side of compliance, including budgeting for registration and choosing a legal structure, is covered in Module 12 Lesson 2; what follows here is the part that happens on the production floor.

Premises come before paperwork in most systems. Expect an inspector to look for a building with a hard washable floor, a one-way flow, a separate raw material store, screened openings, a hand-washing point, rodent and insect control, staff in clean protective clothing, a water source they consider potable, and records. Nothing on that list is expensive. All of it is difficult to add later, which is why Lesson 1 came first.

The label is a production output and must be controlled like one. What travels internationally is the logic rather than the wording. In most systems a pack is expected to carry the product name and the species and life stage it is for, the net weight, a guaranteed analysis showing at least minimum crude protein, minimum crude fat, maximum crude fibre and maximum moisture, a full ingredient list in descending order by weight, feeding directions, your business name and physical address, and a batch code with a date. Confirm every one of those against your own regulator rather than against this list.

The adequacy claim carries the real risk. If you state that a food is complete and balanced for a life stage, you are asserting that it meets a published profile. Using AAFCO as your reference, an adult dog food must actually deliver at least 18.0 percent crude protein, 5.5 percent fat, 0.5 percent calcium and 0.4 percent phosphorus on a dry matter basis, and an adult cat food 26.0 percent protein, 9.0 percent fat, 0.6 percent calcium and 0.5 percent phosphorus, plus 0.10 percent taurine. Your guaranteed analysis is a promise you can be tested against, so declare minima you comfortably exceed, not the exact calculated value.

Three production controls make the label true.

  1. Net weight. Check-weigh at least one bag in twenty and record it. If your 5 kilogram bags average 4.92 kilograms you are short-filling and can be prosecuted; if they average 5.15 you are giving away 3 percent of your product, which on 2000 kilograms a month at USD 0.85 variable cost is USD 51 a month. Set the filler to a small positive tolerance and monitor it.
  2. Batch coding. Every bag gets a code that ties it to one batch record. A simple scheme is product letter, year, day of year, batch number, so DG-25-118-2. Print it, do not write it by hand, and never reuse a code.
  3. Date marking. A best-before date derived from your own shelf-life trial as described in Module 7 Lesson 6, not copied from an imported bag.

One more warning that is squarely a labelling matter. Do not market grain-free as a health claim, and do not treat containing grain as protective. The United States regulator received 524 reports of diet-associated dilated cardiomyopathy between January 2014 and 30 April 2019, 515 canine and 9 feline, including 119 dog deaths, and named 16 brands by report frequency, but stated that adverse event numbers by themselves do not establish a causal relationship, called it a complex scientific issue that may involve multiple factors, and in December 2022 said it did not intend to publish further updates until there was meaningful new scientific information. Nothing there has been proven either way, so claim nothing in either direction.

An example from Abuja, Nigeria. A producer visited the standards office with a sample bag, a formulation sheet and a list of eight written questions. She left with the correct registration route, a fee schedule, the required label elements and the name of an officer she could call. The visit cost her a morning and a taxi fare. She had previously been quoted three different answers by three suppliers, all wrong.

Pricing Strategy and Profitability

In this lesson
  • Separate variable and fixed costs and calculate a true cost per kilogram
  • Calculate break-even volume and profit at three production levels
  • Include depreciation and yield loss in a production costing

Market-facing pricing, meaning what competitors charge and what customers will pay, is worked through in Module 12 Lesson 1 and Lesson 5. What follows is the production side of the same question: what your food actually costs to make, and what volume you must reach before the business pays you.

Split every cost into variable and fixed. Variable costs change with each kilogram made. Fixed costs are there whether you produce or not, and they are the reason volume matters so much.

Variable cost per kilogram, using the formulation costed in Module 7 Lesson 2:

  1. Ingredients USD 0.72.
  2. Packaging USD 0.069, being a printed 5 kilogram bag at USD 0.28, a label at USD 0.04 and a seal and code at USD 0.01, spread over 5 kilograms, plus amortised print plates.
  3. Energy USD 0.030, from 14 kilowatts for six hours at USD 0.18 a unit over 500 kilograms.
  4. Direct labour USD 0.032, being two workers at USD 8 a day producing 500 kilograms.

Total variable cost USD 0.851, call it USD 0.85 a kilogram.

Now fixed costs a month: rent USD 180, one supervisor salary USD 250, standing electricity and water USD 25, licences and insurance spread monthly USD 40, testing USD 120, fixed transport USD 100, and depreciation. Depreciation matters and most small producers ignore it. Equipment costing USD 11,200 written off over five years is 11,200 divided by 60 = USD 187 a month. That money is the fund that replaces the mixer when it dies. Total fixed cost is USD 902, call it USD 900.

Set a wholesale price of USD 1.95 a kilogram, with the shop retailing at about USD 2.60. Contribution per kilogram is 1.95 minus 0.85 = USD 1.10.

Break-even is fixed cost divided by contribution: 900 divided by 1.10 = 818 kilograms a month. Below that you are losing money however busy you feel.

Now see what volume does to cost per kilogram. At 1000 kilograms, fixed cost per kilogram is 900 divided by 1000 = USD 0.90, so total cost is USD 1.75 and profit is 1.95 minus 1.75 = USD 0.20 a kilogram, USD 200 a month. At 2000 kilograms, fixed cost per kilogram is USD 0.45, total cost USD 1.30, profit USD 0.65 a kilogram, USD 1,300 a month. At 4000 kilograms, fixed cost per kilogram is USD 0.225, total cost USD 1.075, profit USD 0.875 a kilogram, USD 3,500 a month. The price never changed. Volume did all the work.

Two adjustments make the figures honest.

Yield loss. You do not sell every kilogram you buy. Between spillage, dust, screen rejects, flush material for limited-ingredient runs and the odd rejected batch, losing 3 to 5 percent is normal. If your true yield is 96 percent, your effective ingredient cost is 0.72 divided by 0.96 = USD 0.75 a kilogram, and variable cost becomes USD 0.88. Break-even rises to 900 divided by 1.07 = 841 kilograms.

Working capital. Selling on 30 or 45 day terms means every kilogram sold locks up its variable cost for that period. At 2000 kilograms a month with 45 day terms, that is roughly 2000 x 0.88 x 1.5 = USD 2,640 of cash permanently tied up. Profitable businesses fail here.

One pricing rule that belongs on the production side: never quote a price on a batch size you have not made. Your cost per kilogram falls with batch size, so a price quoted from a 200 kilogram costing will not hold on a 50 kilogram special run. Price small runs separately, including the mill flush and the extra cleaning time.

An example from Harare, Zimbabwe. A producer was selling 700 kilograms a month at USD 1.95 and could not understand why there was never money at month end. Working through the same table showed her break-even was 818 kilograms, so she was losing about USD 130 a month before drawings. She added two shops rather than raising the price, reached 1,400 kilograms in four months and moved to about USD 640 a month of profit on exactly the same recipe and the same equipment.

Lesson 8.4~12 min

Market Research and Customer Needs

In this lesson
  • Convert observed demand into a production schedule and a required capacity
  • Choose pack sizes from how customers actually buy rather than from convenience
  • Plan for seasonality and for the minimum viable batch

Counting the market, sizing the opportunity and deciding who your customer is are worked through in Module 12 Lesson 1. The job here is narrower and it is a production job: turning what you learn about customers into pack sizes, batch sizes and a schedule your equipment can actually meet.

Start by collecting four numbers from every outlet you supply or intend to supply. How many kilograms of dog and cat food do they sell in a month? In what pack sizes? How often do they want delivery? And how long do they take to pay? Those four answers, from ten outlets, are your production plan. Everything else is opinion.

Pack size is decided by cash, not by preference. Where customers buy weekly and pay in cash, small packs sell and big packs sit. But small packs cost more per kilogram to fill. A printed 5 kilogram bag at USD 0.28 plus a label at USD 0.04 and a seal at USD 0.01 is USD 0.33, or USD 0.066 a kilogram. A 1 kilogram bag might cost USD 0.19 all in, which is USD 0.19 a kilogram, nearly three times as much, and it takes five times as many fills for the same weight. If you sell in 1 kilogram packs, your price per kilogram must reflect that rather than absorb it. A practical answer for many producers is two sizes only, one small pack for the weekly cash buyer and one 10 or 20 kilogram sack for kennels, breeders, security firms and veterinary clinics, which are the accounts that make your volume predictable.

Now work backwards from demand to a schedule. Suppose ten outlets take 1,800 kilograms a month between them, split 70 percent adult dog food, 20 percent puppy and 10 percent cat. That is 1,260, 360 and 180 kilograms. With a 200 kilogram mixer that is roughly six dog batches, two puppy batches and one cat batch a month. Nine batches over 22 working days is easy. But note the cat batch: 180 kilograms is one batch, and one batch a month means the product sits in your store for up to four weeks before it is all sold. Check that against the shelf life you established, and consider making 100 kilograms twice rather than 200 once.

Minimum viable batch is a real constraint. Below about half your mixer's rated capacity, mixing quality falls and your fixed changeover cost per kilogram becomes punishing. For a 200 kilogram mixer, treat 100 kilograms as your floor. If a product cannot sell 100 kilograms within its shelf life, it is not yet a product, it is a special order to be priced as one.

Sequence the schedule for safety, not convenience. Run cat food and any limited-ingredient or single-protein product first on clean equipment, before dog foods containing common proteins. Run puppy and growth diets separately and check calcium, remembering the 1.8 percent dry matter ceiling for any food that may be fed to puppies that will exceed 70 pounds as adults. Leave the highest-fat product until last, because fat is the hardest residue to clean out.

Seasonality is worth measuring rather than guessing. In many African markets pet food sales move with school terms, salary dates, festive periods and the rains. Ask each outlet which two months of the year are their best and which two are their worst, and write it down. If December is 40 percent above average, that is 2,520 kilograms in the month and you need to know now whether your equipment and your cash can carry it.

One customer need that is specific to production. Ask each outlet what they do with damaged or unsold bags. If the answer is that they push them to the back and sell them late, your dating and stock rotation are about to become a reputation problem, and you should be rotating their shelves yourself on every delivery.

An example from Kigali, Rwanda. A producer surveyed eight outlets and found that 60 percent of her volume went in 2 kilogram packs to walk-in customers who paid cash, while 40 percent went in 20 kilogram sacks to three kennels who paid at 30 days. She kept the 2 kilogram pack at a higher price per kilogram to carry its packaging cost, negotiated 14 day terms with the kennels in exchange for a 4 percent discount, and set her production schedule to make the kennel volume in the first week of each month so her cash gap was shortest.

Distribution and Supply Chain

In this lesson
  • Calculate the cost of a delivery drop and set a minimum order size
  • Compare owning a vehicle with using third-party transport
  • Protect product quality and stock rotation between your store and the shelf

A pet food business is a logistics business with a mixer attached. Product that sits in a hot shop, arrives damaged, or is delivered in uneconomic quantities will destroy margins that took a year of formulation work to build.

Start by costing a drop. Suppose one delivery run covers eight shops over 60 kilometres. Fuel at 8.5 kilometres a litre is 7.1 litres, which at USD 1.35 is USD 9.60. Half a day of a driver at USD 10 a day is USD 5.00. Vehicle maintenance, tyres and insurance allocated at USD 0.10 a kilometre is USD 6.00. The run costs USD 20.60. If it delivers 350 kilograms, that is USD 0.059 a kilogram, and USD 2.58 a drop.

Now set a minimum order. If your contribution is USD 1.10 a kilogram, a 25 kilogram order contributes USD 27.50 against a drop cost of USD 2.58, which is comfortable. A 5 kilogram order contributes USD 5.50 against the same USD 2.58 and is barely worth stopping for. Set a written minimum order, commonly 20 or 25 kilograms, and let smaller customers collect or combine.

Compare owning with hiring. A used pickup at USD 6,500 depreciated over five years is USD 108 a month before fuel, driver, insurance and repairs, realistically USD 350 to 450 a month all in for a modest delivery load. A motorcycle courier in most African cities charges USD 2 to 4 for a city drop, and a shared truck to an upcountry town might be USD 25 to 60. If you are making fewer than about 3,000 kilograms a month, hiring almost always wins, and it converts a fixed cost into a variable one, which is exactly what a small business needs. Buy a vehicle when you can show that the monthly hire bill exceeds the all-in cost of running your own.

Route rather than react. Group customers by area and give each area a fixed day: south side Tuesday, north side Thursday. Fixed days let shops plan their orders, halve your kilometres, and let you check shelves on a schedule. Telephone or message every shop the day before to confirm the order so you never carry stock you do not sell.

Protect the product in transit and on the shelf. Load with the heaviest sacks at the bottom, keep bags out of direct sun, and never carry pet food with fuel, agrochemicals, cement or cleaning products, because taint and contamination are both real and neither can be undone. Cover the load. In the rain, wet woven bags will mould even if the inner liner holds.

Rotate the shelf yourself on every visit. Bring old stock to the front, check dates, look for split bags and rodent damage, and record what you find. If a shop has stock older than about two-thirds of its shelf life, either take it back and replace it or stop supplying that shop. A rancid bag with your name on it costs more than the bag.

Set terms deliberately, because credit is the largest hidden cost in distribution. Every kilogram sold on 45 day terms locks up its variable cost of USD 0.88 for a month and a half. At 2,000 kilograms a month that is roughly 2,000 x 0.88 x 1.5 = USD 2,640 permanently tied up, which is money you cannot spend on ingredients. Offering a 3 or 4 percent discount for payment in 7 days is usually cheaper than borrowing, and much cheaper than running out of maize.

Second-source everything critical. One supplier for your main protein meal, one transporter, one bag printer and one premix supplier is four ways to be shut down. For each, write the name of an alternative and telephone them once a quarter so the relationship exists before you need it. Keep two months of cover on anything scarce, and remember from Module 6 Lesson 6 that stock cover is capital sitting in a store.

An example from Mombasa, Kenya. A producer delivered on demand to fourteen outlets and was driving roughly 900 kilometres a month. She grouped them into three fixed routes on three fixed days, dropped two outlets that ordered under 10 kilograms and moved them to collection, and cut mileage to about 480 kilometres. Fuel and vehicle cost fell by about USD 55 a month, and because she was now on each shelf every week, her out-of-date returns fell to nearly nothing.

Lesson 8.6~13 min

Building a Brand and Customer Trust

In this lesson
  • Use traceability and batch records as the foundation of customer trust
  • Write and rehearse a recall procedure for a small producer
  • Handle a complaint in a way that produces evidence rather than argument

Brand identity, naming, pack design and marketing are worked through in Module 12 Lesson 5. What follows is the half of a brand that is made in the production room: whether you can prove what is in the bag, whether you can find it again, and what you do on the day something goes wrong.

Trust in pet food is unusual because the customer cannot inspect the product, the consumer cannot speak, and the consequences are slow. That means trust has to be built from things a customer can verify. There are four, and none of them costs much.

The first is traceability. Every bag carries a printed batch code. Every code ties to a one-page batch record holding the date, the formulation version, every ingredient with its quantity and its supplier lot number, the mixing time, the operator and the checker, the number of bags made and where they went. Every batch has a 200 gram retained sample kept for the shelf life plus a month. That is the whole system and it fits in a box file.

The reason to have it is that large companies with laboratories and quality managers have got this wrong. Vitamin D formulation errors caused multiple dry food recalls in 2018 across brands from a single contract manufacturer, one major brand issued canned recalls in January, March and May 2019, and regulators issued warning letters in 2019. What made those recalls possible at all was traceability. Vitamin D is the nutrient you are most likely to poison an animal with by accident, and the dog safe upper limit of 3000 IU per kilogram of dry matter is not far above a normal inclusion.

The second is a rehearsed recall procedure. Write it on one page and practise it once a year.

  1. Stop production and quarantine all remaining stock of the affected product.
  2. Look up the batch code and pull the batch record and the retained sample.
  3. List every customer who received bags from that batch and how many.
  4. Telephone each one, in order of volume, and ask them to withdraw the bags and hold them.
  5. Tell your regulator if the fault is a safety fault, and write down who you spoke to and when.
  6. Collect the stock, count it against the record, and store it separately and labelled.
  7. Find the cause using the batch record and the retained sample, and write down what you changed so it cannot happen again.
  8. Tell affected customers what you found. This step is the one that turns a recall into a reputation.

Run the drill on paper with a real batch code and time yourself. If it takes more than an hour to produce the list of who received that batch, your records are not good enough.

The third is honest claims. Anything you print must be either measured or referenced. You can print a guaranteed analysis, because you can be tested against it. You can state that a food meets a named profile for a named life stage, if it actually does. You cannot claim it improves cognition, prevents kidney disease or cures allergies. And do not market grain-free as a health claim or containing grain as protective, because no causal link has been demonstrated in either direction. In a market with weak enforcement, refusing to make unprovable claims is the only durable advantage available, because it is the one competitors will not copy.

The fourth is how you handle a complaint. Do not argue and do not refund immediately. Collect. Ask for the batch code from the bag, the date of purchase, how much is left, what the animal is, what it ate before, when signs started and what they were. Ask to keep a sample of the remaining food. Then open your own retained sample from that batch and compare. Write it all in a complaints log with a date and an outcome. Most complaints resolve as a transition problem, a storage problem at the shop, or something unrelated to the food. A few are real, and those are the ones your records let you fix. If an animal is unwell, tell the owner to see a veterinarian first, before any discussion about the food.

Two cheap things build visible trust. Let customers see the production room, by appointment, with clean floors and labelled bins. And put your physical address and a working telephone number on every bag. A producer who can be found is a producer who can be trusted.

An example from Gaborone, Botswana. A producer received two complaints of loose stools from one week. She pulled both batch codes, found they were the same batch, opened the retained sample, and found the mixing time on that record was five minutes instead of the standard ten because a new worker had run it. She withdrew the 34 remaining bags, replaced them free, retrained the worker, and added a second signature line to the batch record. She then told both customers exactly what had happened. Both are still buying.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. What should be decided before buying any equipment?

Buying in the wrong order produces a bottleneck you cannot afford to fix. Volume determines the capacity machine, and everything else is matched to it.

2. A 200 kg mixer completing four batches a day gives what realistic monthly output over 22 days?

800 kg a day in theory, at 60 to 70 percent utilisation, is 480 to 560 kg a day, or 10,500 to 12,300 kg over 22 days.

3. What is the correct flow through a small production unit?

A one-way flow with no crossing paths prevents raw material contaminating finished product. Raw and finished stock must never share a door or a shelf.

4. At 14 kW running for six hours at USD 0.18 per unit, producing 500 kg, what is the electricity cost per kilogram?

84 kWh at USD 0.18 is USD 15.10 for the day, divided by 500 kg is USD 0.030 per kilogram.

5. How far off the floor and from the wall should sacks be stored?

Keeping stock off the floor and away from walls lets you sweep, inspect for rodents and allow air movement, and it is one of the first things any inspector checks.

6. What legal force do AAFCO nutrient profiles have in African countries?

AAFCO is a United States model. It is a sound formulation reference anywhere, but compliance must be confirmed with your own national regulator.

7. Which body regulates farm feeds in South Africa?

Act 36 and its registrar are the South African route. Other countries have their own bodies, and only your own regulator can tell you what applies.

8. Why should a guaranteed analysis declare minima you comfortably exceed?

If a sample tests below your declared minimum you have mislabelled the product. Declaring the exact calculated value leaves no room for normal variation.

9. What is a sound batch code scheme?

A printed unique code such as DG-25-118-2 ties every bag to one batch record, which is what makes a targeted withdrawal possible.

10. What is the correct position on grain-free claims?

The regulator received 524 reports including 119 dog deaths but stated that adverse event numbers alone do not establish causation, and has published no finding. Neither claim is defensible.

11. What is the break-even volume at USD 900 fixed cost, USD 1.95 price and USD 0.85 variable cost?

Contribution is 1.95 minus 0.85 = USD 1.10, and 900 divided by 1.10 is 818 kilograms a month.

12. Why must depreciation appear in the fixed costs?

USD 11,200 over five years is USD 187 a month. Ignoring it makes the business look profitable until the mixer fails and there is no money to replace it.

13. At 4000 kg a month, what is the total cost per kilogram?

Fixed cost per kilogram is 900 divided by 4000 = USD 0.225, added to USD 0.85 variable, giving USD 1.075.

14. Your true yield is 96 percent. What happens to the ingredient cost per kilogram sold?

0.72 divided by 0.96 is USD 0.75. Spillage, dust, screen rejects and flush material all have to be carried by the kilograms you actually sell.

15. Why should a 50 kilogram special run not be priced from a 200 kilogram costing?

Cleaning time, flush material and setup are the same regardless of batch size, so small runs must be quoted separately.

16. Which four questions turn customer research into a production plan?

Those four answers from ten outlets give you the volume, the pack mix, the schedule and the cash gap. Everything else is opinion.

17. Why must a 1 kilogram pack be priced higher per kilogram?

Small packs carry a much higher packaging and labour cost per kilogram, which must be reflected in the price rather than absorbed.

18. What is a sensible minimum batch on a 200 kilogram mixer?

Below about half rated capacity, mixing quality falls and changeover cost per kilogram becomes punishing. If a product cannot sell 100 kg within its shelf life, price it as a special order.

19. In what order should products be run through shared equipment?

Running sensitive and single-protein products first prevents carryover, and fat is the hardest residue to clean, so it belongs at the end of the run.

20. A December peak is 40 percent above an average 1,800 kg month. What must you check?

Seasonality has to be measured from the outlets and planned for, because both production capacity and working capital have to stretch in the peak month.

21. A run costs USD 20.60 and delivers 350 kg to eight shops. What is the cost per kilogram?

20.60 divided by 350 is USD 0.059 per kilogram. The cost per drop is 20.60 divided by 8, which is USD 2.58.

22. Why set a minimum order size?

The drop cost is the same whatever the order size, so small orders should be collected or combined rather than delivered.

23. When does owning a delivery vehicle become sensible?

Hiring converts a fixed cost into a variable one, which suits a small business. Ownership only wins once volume makes the fixed cost cheaper per kilogram.

24. What must never be carried with pet food?

Taint and chemical contamination cannot be undone once they occur, and a contaminated load is a total loss at best.

25. You find stock in a shop that is older than two-thirds of its shelf life. What should you do?

A rancid or out-of-date bag carries your name. Rotating shelves on every delivery visit is cheaper than the reputation damage.

26. What are the three parts of a small producer's traceability system?

A printed code on every bag, a one-page record per batch and a retained sample together let you confirm what was made and withdraw only the affected bags.

27. How long should a recall drill take to produce the list of who received a batch?

If listing the recipients of a given batch code takes more than an hour, the records are not adequate. The drill should be run on paper once a year.

28. What is the first thing to do when a customer complains about a food?

Collecting evidence turns a complaint into information. Compare it against your retained sample and log it. If the animal is unwell, the owner should see a veterinarian first.

29. Why did large manufacturers still have to recall product for vitamin D errors?

Those companies had laboratories and quality managers. The failures were formulation and premix errors, and batch traceability is what limited the damage.

30. Which claim can a small producer defensibly print?

A guaranteed analysis is measurable and verifiable. Health claims of that kind are not supported by the verified standards and cannot be defended.
