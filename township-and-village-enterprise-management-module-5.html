1. Understanding Township and Village Enterprises in Africa

Welcome to Module 5 of Township and Village Enterprise Management. This lesson introduces the concept of township and village enterprises, or TVEs, and explains why they matter for African economies. A TVE is a business owned and operated at the local level, often by a community, a cooperative, a family, or a small group of entrepreneurs. It is rooted in a specific town, village, or peri-urban area and uses local resources, labour, and knowledge to produce goods or services for nearby markets and beyond. In Africa, TVEs take many forms: grain milling operations in rural Kenya, tailoring cooperatives in Ghana, fish smoking and packaging enterprises in Senegal, furniture workshops in Tanzania, shea butter processing groups in Burkina Faso, and solar kiosks in off-grid Nigerian communities. What unites them is their local ownership, their reliance on local supply chains, and their potential to create jobs and income where people already live. The first practical step for any manager or promoter is to understand the local economic ecosystem. Ask: What resources exist here? What skills do people have? What is currently imported from outside that could be produced locally? What do households and businesses spend money on every week? A TVE succeeds when it solves a real local problem or meets a steady local demand. For example, if a village spends large sums transporting maize to a distant mill, a local milling enterprise can capture that value. If farmers lose income because they sell fresh tomatoes cheaply during harvest, a small drying or processing unit can extend shelf life and raise margins. The business-first mindset means starting with the market, not with the machinery. Too many TVEs fail because promoters buy equipment first and look for customers later. Instead, talk to potential buyers, estimate volumes, agree on prices, and only then invest. Legal and governance structures matter. TVEs can be registered as sole proprietorships, partnerships, limited liability companies, cooperatives, or community-based organisations. Each has advantages. Cooperatives give members shared ownership and democratic control, which builds trust in rural areas. Companies offer clearer liability protection and easier access to formal finance. Many successful African TVEs combine a formal company with a cooperative supply base, so farmers or producers own the raw material side while a professional management team runs the processing and sales side. Governance should include a clear constitution, defined roles for directors and managers, regular meetings, written records, and transparent financial reporting. Without these, even profitable enterprises collapse when founders disagree or when members suspect favouritism. Financing is another core issue. TVEs rarely get bank loans easily because they lack collateral, formal accounts, and credit history. Practical alternatives include savings groups, village banks, rotating credit associations, matching grants from NGOs or government programmes, and phased reinvestment of profits. The key is to start small, prove the business model, and grow with internally generated funds before taking on debt. A TVE that buys one sewing machine and sells twenty school uniforms can use the profit to buy a second machine. That is safer than borrowing heavily for ten machines before demand is proven. Market access is the next pillar. Local markets are the foundation, but growth often comes from linking to larger buyers: schools, hospitals, hotels, supermarkets, construction companies, and export aggregators. To win these contracts, TVEs need consistent quality, reliable volumes, simple packaging, and basic documentation such as invoices and delivery notes. Managers should learn to negotiate contracts, calculate unit costs, and set prices that cover materials, labour, transport, utilities, and a margin for reinvestment. Record keeping does not require expensive software. A cash book, a stock register, a debtors list, and a simple profit and loss sheet are enough to start. Finally, TVEs must manage risk. Common risks in Africa include currency fluctuations, drought, pests, power cuts, theft, and sudden changes in government policy. Practical responses include keeping a small emergency fund, diversifying products, sourcing from multiple suppliers, using solar backup, and joining associations that lobby for better local conditions. In summary, a TVE is a locally rooted business that turns local resources and demand into jobs and wealth. Its success depends on market research, sound governance, patient financing, reliable quality, and disciplined record keeping. In the next lesson, we will look at how to identify and validate a viable TVE business idea using simple tools that work in low-resource settings.

2. Identifying and Validating a Viable TVE Business Idea

Every successful township and village enterprise begins with a viable idea. But what makes an idea viable in an African local context? It must meet real demand, use accessible resources, fit local skills and culture, and generate enough margin to sustain the business and reward its owners. This lesson gives you a practical process for finding, testing, and validating a TVE business idea before you spend significant money. The first step is opportunity scanning. Walk through your community and observe. What goods are brought in from outside? What services do people travel far to get? What waste or by-product is thrown away that could be turned into value? What complaints do people repeat? For example, if women walk five kilometres to fetch clean water, a water kiosk or purification enterprise may work. If farmers burn rice husks, a briquette-making enterprise could turn waste into cooking fuel. If students leave the village for computer training, a small digital skills centre could serve them. Keep a notebook and record at least twenty observations. Then rank them by frequency of need, willingness to pay, and your ability to deliver. The second step is defining the customer. A common mistake is saying everyone is the customer. Instead, name a specific group: smallholder maize farmers within ten kilometres, secondary school students, market traders, pregnant mothers, construction contractors, or guesthouses. For each group, describe their buying behaviour. How much do they buy? How often? What price do they pay now? Where do they buy? What quality do they expect? What would make them switch to your product? If you cannot answer these questions, you are not ready to invest. The third step is estimating demand with simple arithmetic. Suppose you want to start a poultry enterprise selling eggs to local households and shops. Count the number of households in your area, estimate how many buy eggs regularly, and multiply by average weekly consumption. Then check how many eggs currently come from outside. If the local supply covers only part of demand, there is room. If the market is already saturated, you need a different angle such as organic eggs, cheaper packaging, or delivery. Do the same for any product: soap, bread, bricks, furniture, solar chargers, or milling services. Use conservative numbers, not optimistic guesses. The fourth step is testing the idea cheaply. Before buying equipment, run a small pilot. If you plan to sell processed fruit juice, buy fruit, make a small batch, and sell it at a market stall or to neighbours. If you plan to make school uniforms, sew five samples and show them to two schools. If you plan to offer transport, rent a vehicle for a week and track demand. A pilot reveals real costs, real prices, and real customer reactions. It also builds confidence and gives you evidence to show lenders or partners. The fifth step is calculating unit economics. For every unit sold, what is the selling price? What are the direct costs: raw materials, packaging, labour, transport, fuel, and commission? What is left as gross margin? Then add fixed costs: rent, licenses, salaries, loan interest, and depreciation. How many units must you sell each month to break even? If the break-even volume is higher than what the local market can absorb, the idea is not viable at that scale. You may need to raise prices, reduce costs, find additional markets, or choose a different product. The sixth step is checking resource availability. Do you have reliable access to raw materials throughout the year? Is there a risk of seasonal shortage? Can you hire or train workers with the right skills? Is there electricity, water, or transport infrastructure? If not, can you work around it with solar, rainwater harvesting, or bicycle delivery? Many TVEs fail because they assume inputs will always be available. Build relationships with at least two suppliers for every critical input. The seventh step is assessing competition and substitutes. Who else offers this product or service? What are their strengths and weaknesses? What do customers say about them? Can you compete on price, quality, convenience, credit terms, or customer service? Sometimes the best strategy is not to compete head-on but to serve a niche that larger businesses ignore, such as small-quantity packaging for low-income households or same-day delivery for perishable goods. The eighth step is checking legal and regulatory requirements. Does your business need a license, health permit, environmental clearance, or tax registration? What local bylaws apply? Ignoring these can lead to fines or closure. Visit the local government office, ask questions, and budget for compliance. The ninth step is seeking feedback from trusted advisors. Share your idea with experienced business people, extension officers, cooperative leaders, or mentors. Ask them what could go wrong. Their questions will expose blind spots. The tenth step is making a go or no-go decision. Write a one-page summary: the customer, the product, the price, the costs, the break-even point, the main risks, and the first three actions. If the numbers work and the risks are manageable, proceed with a small launch. If not, adjust or abandon the idea. Abandoning a weak idea early is a success, not a failure, because it saves money and time. Remember that validation is continuous. Even after launch, keep talking to customers, tracking sales, and adjusting. A TVE that listens to its market will survive; one that assumes it knows best will struggle. In the next lesson, we will cover operations management, including production planning, quality control, and supply chain management for local enterprises.

3. Operations Management for Township and Village Enterprises

Operations management is the engine room of a township or village enterprise. It covers how you produce goods or deliver services, how you manage quality, how you handle inputs and inventory, and how you organise daily work. In African TVEs, operations must be practical, low-cost, and resilient to disruptions such as power cuts, fuel shortages, and seasonal supply changes. This lesson provides a step-by-step approach to setting up and running efficient operations. Start with process mapping. Write down every step from raw material to finished product or service delivery. For a bakery, the steps might be: buy flour, sugar, yeast, and oil; store ingredients; mix dough; knead; shape; prove; bake; cool; package; sell; and deliver. For a tailoring enterprise: take measurements; buy fabric; cut; sew; finish; iron; package; and deliver. Mapping helps you see where delays, waste, and costs occur. It also helps you assign tasks to specific people and estimate how long each step takes. Next, design the layout of your workspace. A good layout reduces movement, prevents contamination, and improves safety. Keep raw materials separate from finished goods. Place frequently used tools within easy reach. Ensure there is enough light and ventilation. In food processing, separate washing, cutting, cooking, and packaging areas. In workshops, keep walkways clear and store flammable materials safely. A simple layout change can raise output without any new equipment. Production planning follows. Estimate demand for the coming week or month based on past sales and known orders. Then calculate how much raw material you need and how many hours of labour. Create a simple schedule: what will be produced, by whom, and by when. Build in buffer time for breakdowns and late deliveries. If demand is seasonal, plan for peak periods by hiring temporary workers or running extra shifts. If demand is steady, aim for a smooth workflow to avoid idle time. Quality control is essential for repeat customers. Define what good quality means for your product: size, weight, colour, taste, strength, durability, or cleanliness. Write simple standards and check every batch. For example, a soap enterprise might check that each bar weighs 200 grams, lathers well, and is wrapped neatly. A furniture workshop might check that joints are tight, surfaces are smooth, and dimensions match the order. Use a checklist and record results. When a defect appears, find the cause and fix it rather than just discarding the product. Train workers on quality standards and give them authority to stop production if something is wrong. Supply chain management is about getting the right inputs at the right time, price, and quality. Identify your critical inputs and find at least two suppliers for each. Build relationships based on reliability, not just lowest price. Negotiate bulk discounts where possible, but do not overbuy if storage is poor or if the input spoils. For seasonal inputs, consider contract farming or advance purchase agreements with local producers. Keep a simple supplier record with contact details, prices, lead times, and performance notes. Inventory management balances having enough stock with avoiding waste. Use a basic stock card for each item: date received, quantity, date issued, balance. Set reorder levels so you know when to buy more. For perishable goods, use first-in-first-out. For expensive items, keep them locked and record every movement. Conduct a physical count every week or month and compare it with records. Differences may indicate theft, spoilage, or recording errors. Maintenance keeps equipment running. Create a maintenance schedule for every machine: daily cleaning, weekly checks, monthly servicing, and annual overhaul. Train an operator to do basic repairs and keep a small kit of spare parts, belts, fuses, and lubricants. When a machine breaks down, having spares on hand can save days of lost production. For enterprises dependent on electricity, invest in a solar backup or a generator if affordable, and prioritise critical equipment. Health and safety protect your workers and your business. Provide clean water, soap, and protective clothing such as gloves, aprons, and masks. Train workers on safe handling of chemicals, sharp tools, and hot surfaces. Keep a first aid kit and display emergency numbers. Report and investigate accidents. A safe workplace reduces absenteeism, compensation claims, and reputational damage. Labour management is about getting the best from your team. Write clear job descriptions and daily tasks. Train workers on skills and safety. Pay fairly and on time. Recognise good performance with bonuses, public praise, or extra leave. Address poor performance privately and promptly. In many TVEs, family and community relationships affect work. Separate family roles from business roles where possible, and apply rules consistently. Encourage workers to suggest improvements; they often see problems that managers miss. Record keeping supports all operations. Keep daily production records, sales records, stock records, and expense records. Review them weekly to spot trends: rising costs, falling yields, or slow-moving products. Use the numbers to make decisions, not guesses. Finally, build resilience. Identify the top five risks to your operations: power outage, water shortage, supplier failure, theft, or transport disruption. For each, write a simple contingency plan. For example, if power fails, use a manual backup or solar system. If a supplier fails, have a second supplier ready. If theft is a risk, improve lighting, locks, and stock checks. Operations management is not about complex systems; it is about doing simple things consistently well. Map your processes, plan your production, control quality, manage supplies, maintain equipment, protect workers, and keep records. These habits turn a small TVE into a reliable, profitable enterprise. In the next lesson, we will focus on marketing and sales, including how to reach customers, set prices, and build trust in local and regional markets.

4. Marketing and Sales for Local and Regional Markets

Marketing and sales are how a township or village enterprise turns production into income. Many TVE owners believe marketing means advertising, but in local African markets it is much more: it is understanding customers, offering value, communicating clearly, and making it easy for people to buy. This lesson covers practical marketing and sales strategies that work with limited budgets and strong community relationships. Begin with the customer. Who buys your product or service? Where do they live, work, and shop? What problems do they want solved? What price can they afford? What makes them choose one supplier over another? For a maize milling enterprise, customers might be households that want fine flour, restaurants that need consistent supply, and traders who buy in bulk. Each group has different needs. Households may value convenience and small packs; restaurants may value credit terms and delivery; traders may value low prices and large volumes. Your marketing must speak to each group differently. Next, define your value proposition. This is a short statement of why a customer should buy from you rather than someone else. Examples: the only mill in the village that opens at 6am so farmers can process before market; the tailoring shop that delivers school uniforms within three days; the poultry farm that sells fresh eggs daily and replaces broken ones free. A strong value proposition is specific, believable, and important to the customer. Then set your price. Pricing is not just cost plus profit. Consider what customers are willing to pay, what competitors charge, and what value you deliver. If your product is better quality, you may charge a premium. If you are new, you may price slightly lower to attract trial, but avoid being the cheapest forever because it attracts price-sensitive customers and leaves no margin for improvement. Calculate your unit cost carefully, including materials, labour, transport, packaging, rent, utilities, and a share of equipment. Add a margin that allows reinvestment and a reserve for hard times. Review prices regularly as costs change. Promotion in local markets relies on trust and visibility. Word of mouth is the most powerful tool. Deliver good quality on time and ask satisfied customers to tell others. Use community gatherings, churches, mosques, markets, and cooperative meetings to introduce your product. Offer free samples where possible. Put up simple signs at your premises and at key locations such as market entrances. Use local radio if affordable, especially for reaching farmers and rural households. For youth and urban customers, use WhatsApp groups, Facebook pages, and SMS messages. Keep messages clear: what you sell, where you are, your opening hours, and your phone number. Build a simple brand. A brand is more than a name or logo; it is the promise customers associate with you. Choose a name that is easy to remember and pronounce locally. Use consistent colours and packaging. If you sell food, ensure packaging is clean and labelled with weight, ingredients, and contact details. If you offer services, wear neat uniforms and arrive on time. Consistency builds trust, and trust brings repeat business. Sales is the process of turning interest into payment. Train yourself and your staff to greet customers politely, ask questions, listen, and recommend the right product. Do not pressure people. Instead, explain benefits and answer objections. For example, if a customer says your soap is more expensive, explain that it lasts longer and is gentler on the skin. If a customer worries about quality, offer a small trial pack or a money-back guarantee. Keep a customer list with names, phone numbers, and purchase history. Follow up with reminders, special offers, or new product announcements. Distribution is how customers get your product. Options include selling at your premises, at markets, through agents, through retailers, or by delivery. Choose channels that match customer habits. If customers shop daily at a market, a stall there may outsell a shop in a side street. If restaurants need early morning delivery, offer it. If rural customers cannot travel, work with village agents who buy in bulk and resell. Each channel has costs and risks. Calculate the cost of delivery, agent commissions, and credit. Avoid channels that tie up too much cash or require large discounts. Credit is a major issue in African TVEs. Selling on credit can increase sales but also creates bad debts. Set clear rules: who qualifies, how much, for how long, and what happens if they do not pay. Keep a debtors book and follow up politely but firmly. Consider requiring a deposit or a guarantor for large amounts. For small repeat customers, a loyalty card or a small discount for cash payment can encourage prompt settlement. Customer service is a competitive advantage. Answer phone calls. Reply to messages. Handle complaints quickly and fairly. If a customer is unhappy, listen, apologise, and fix the problem. A complaint handled well can create a more loyal customer than one who never complained. Train staff to treat every customer with respect, regardless of how small the purchase. In many communities, reputation is everything. Competition should be studied, not feared. Visit competitors, observe their prices, quality, and service. Identify gaps you can fill: longer hours, home delivery, smaller packs, better packaging, or friendlier service. Do not copy blindly; adapt to your strengths. If a large competitor moves in, focus on niches they ignore, such as personalised service or remote villages. Finally, measure your marketing and sales. Track the number of customers, average sale value, repeat purchase rate, and cost of reaching a new customer. Ask customers how they heard about you. Use this information to invest more in what works and stop what does not. Marketing is not a one-time event; it is a daily habit of understanding customers, communicating value, and making buying easy. A TVE that masters this will grow from serving a few neighbours to supplying regional markets. 5. Financial Management and Access to Finance for TVEs

Financial management is the difference between a busy enterprise and a profitable one. Many township and village enterprises handle money informally, mixing personal and business funds, keeping no records, and making decisions based on memory. This lesson explains how to manage money properly, keep simple records, control costs, plan cash flow, and access finance for growth. Start with separation. Open a separate bank account or mobile money wallet for the business. Do not use business money for personal expenses unless you record it as a salary or drawing. This discipline makes it possible to know whether the enterprise is actually making a profit. Pay yourself a fixed monthly amount if funds allow, rather than taking money randomly. Next, keep records. You do not need accounting software to start. Use a cash book with two columns: money in and money out. Record every transaction on the day it happens. Keep receipts and invoices in a file or envelope. Use a stock book to record purchases and sales of goods. Use a debtors book to track money owed to you and a creditors book to track money you owe. At the end of each month, total the columns. Money in minus money out gives your cash movement. To calculate profit, you also need to account for stock and unpaid bills, but the cash book is the foundation. Costing is essential for pricing and profitability. Divide costs into direct and indirect. Direct costs are those that rise with each unit produced: raw materials, packaging, and piece-rate labour. Indirect costs are those that do not change immediately with output: rent, salaries, electricity, licenses, and depreciation. Calculate the direct cost per unit. Then add a share of indirect costs based on expected volume. For example, if monthly indirect costs are 500,000 shillings and you expect to sell 1,000 units, each unit must carry 500 shillings of indirect cost. Add your desired profit margin. If the resulting price is higher than customers will pay, you must reduce costs, increase volume, or change the product. Review costs monthly because fuel, transport, and raw material prices change often. Cash flow is the movement of money in and out of the business. A profitable enterprise can still fail if it runs out of cash. Plan cash flow by listing expected cash inflows and outflows for the next three months. Inflows include sales, loans, grants, and member contributions. Outflows include purchases, wages, rent, utilities, loan repayments, taxes, and transport. Identify months when outflows exceed inflows. Prepare for those months by building a cash reserve, delaying non-essential purchases, negotiating supplier credit, or arranging a short-term loan. Keep a minimum cash buffer equal to one month of essential expenses. Profit is not the same as cash. If you sell on credit, you may record a profit but have no money in hand. If you buy stock, you may have cash but no profit yet. Understand both. Use your records to calculate gross margin, net profit, and return on investment. Gross margin is sales minus direct costs. Net profit is gross margin minus indirect costs. Return on investment is net profit divided by the total money invested. Compare these ratios over time and against similar businesses. Access to finance is a common challenge. Banks often require collateral, formal accounts, and a track record. TVEs can improve their chances by registering the business, keeping clean records, opening a bank account, and building a savings history. Mobile money statements can serve as evidence of transactions. Start with small loans and repay on time to build a credit record. Explore alternative sources. Savings groups and village banks offer small loans based on group trust. Cooperatives may lend to members from a common fund. Government programmes and NGOs sometimes provide matching grants, subsidised loans, or guarantee schemes for agriculture, youth, and women-led enterprises. Value chain funds from buyers or processors may offer advance payments or input credit. Diaspora remittances or family loans can help, but treat them professionally with written agreements. Before borrowing, calculate whether the loan will generate enough extra profit to cover the interest and repayment. If you borrow to buy a machine, estimate the additional income it will produce. If the additional income is less than the loan cost, do not borrow. Avoid borrowing for consumption or for assets that do not generate income. When you do borrow, match the loan term to the investment. Short-term loans for working capital, such as buying raw materials, should be repaid within the production cycle. Long-term loans for equipment should be repaid over several years. Keep loan repayments within a safe share of monthly cash flow, ideally not more than thirty percent. Financial controls prevent loss and fraud. Require two signatures for large payments. Reconcile bank and mobile money statements monthly. Conduct physical stock counts. Separate the roles of ordering, receiving, and paying where possible. If one person handles everything, arrange for regular independent checks. Train staff on financial policies and consequences of theft. Lead by example: do not take money without recording it. Budgeting helps you plan. Prepare an annual budget with expected sales, costs, and profit. Break it into monthly targets. Compare actual results with the budget each month. If sales are below target, investigate why and adjust. If costs are above budget, find savings. Use the budget to decide on investments, hiring, and expansion. Finally, plan for taxes and compliance. Know which taxes apply to your business, such as value added tax, income tax, or local levies. Keep records that support your tax returns. Pay on time to avoid penalties. If you do not understand tax rules, seek advice from a tax officer, accountant, or business association. Good financial management builds trust with lenders, investors, and partners. It also gives you the information you need to make smart decisions. A TVE that knows its costs, controls its cash, keeps clean records, and borrows wisely will grow steadily and survive shocks. This concludes Module 5. You now have a practical foundation in understanding TVEs, validating ideas, managing operations, marketing and sales, and financial management. Apply these lessons step by step, and your enterprise will create lasting value for your community.

<h2>Practical Reference: Record Keeping and Simple Enterprise Analysis for Township and Village Enterprises</h2>

<p>For a township or village enterprise (TVE) in Africa, the difference between a business that survives and one that quietly collapses is rarely about the quality of the product. It is almost always about whether the owner knows, at any given moment, what the business owns, what it owes, what it earns and what it spends. Record keeping is not an administrative chore imposed by donors or tax authorities; it is the owner's own instrument of control. This practical reference lesson shows how a small TVE a poultry unit, a grain mill, a tailoring shop, a soap-making group can keep usable records with a notebook, a pen and a simple cash box, and how to convert those records into decisions.</p>

<h3>Why Records Come First</h3>

<p>Most TVEs in Africa operate on memory. The owner remembers who owes money, roughly what feed cost last month, and approximately how many customers came. Memory fails precisely when it matters: at the moment of a price negotiation, a loan application, or a disease outbreak. Records do four things that memory cannot. They reveal the true cost of a product, they expose theft and waste, they support access to finance (banks and SACCOs require at least three to six months of records), and they allow the owner to compare this month with last month.</p>

<p>The rule for a TVE is simplicity. A single hardcover exercise book, ruled into columns, kept daily, is worth more than a sophisticated software package that is updated once a quarter. The records must be written at the moment of the transaction, not reconstructed at the end of the week.</p>

<h3>The Five Records Every TVE Must Keep</h3>

<p>Keep these five, and nothing else, until the business is large enough to justify more:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Daily cash book.</strong> Two columns money in, money out plus a one-line description and a running balance. Written the same day.</li>
<li><strong>Debtors and creditors list.</strong> Who owes the business, and whom the business owes. Updated weekly.</li>
<li><strong>Stock or production record.</strong> For a poultry unit: birds, feed used, eggs or birds sold, deaths. For a mill: grain in, flour out. For tailoring: cloth, thread, garments completed.</li>
<li><strong>Asset register.</strong> Every tool, machine, animal or building owned, with the date bought and the price paid.</li>
<li><strong>Wage and labour record.</strong> Who worked, how many hours or days, and how much was paid.</li>
</ol>

<div style="border:3px solid #b00020;background:#fdecec;padding:14px 16px;margin:20px 0;font-family:Arial,sans-serif;">
<strong style="color:#b00020;font-size:16px;"> SAFETY WARNING Health, Food and Chemical Hazards</strong>
<p style="margin:10px 0 0 0;color:#3a0a0a;">If your TVE handles food, live animals, or agricultural chemicals, records are a safety tool, not just a financial one. Keep a separate <strong>chemical and medicine register</strong> recording every vaccine, antibiotic, pesticide or fumigant used: product name, date, dose, animal or batch treated, and <strong>withdrawal period</strong> before sale. Selling meat, eggs or milk before the withdrawal period ends leaves drug residues that harm consumers and can lead to market bans and prosecution. Store all chemicals in a locked, labelled container away from feed, food and children. Use gloves and wash hands after handling. Report any unusual animal deaths immediately to a veterinary officer. WHO and FAO classify many agricultural pesticides as highly hazardous; never repackage them into food or drink containers.</p>
</div>

<h3>How to Analyse What You Have Recorded</h3>

<p>Once a month, take thirty minutes and answer five questions from your books:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Did I make a profit?</strong> Total money in, minus total money out, for the month. If negative, which cost line was largest?</li>
<li><strong>What is my cost per unit?</strong> Total costs divided by units produced (birds, bags of flour, garments). Compare with the selling price.</li>
<li><strong>How much do customers owe me?</strong> If debtors exceed one month's sales, tighten credit terms.</li>
<li><strong>What did I lose?</strong> Deaths, breakages, spoilage, theft. Losses above 5% need investigation.</li>
<li><strong>What will I change next month?</strong> Write one decision and one action with a date.</li>
</ol>

<p>This is the entire discipline. It is not accounting for accountants. It is the owner's dashboard.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul style="font-family:Arial,sans-serif;">
<li> Write every transaction in the cash book the same day it happens no exceptions.</li>
<li> Keep one book per enterprise; never mix household and business money.</li>
<li> Update the debtors and creditors list every Friday.</li>
<li> Record stock or production daily (birds, eggs, grain, cloth, soap).</li>
<li> Register every asset with date and purchase price in the asset book.</li>
<li> Keep a separate chemical and medicine register with withdrawal periods.</li>
<li> Close the month within seven days: total, compare, and write one decision.</li>
<li> Store records in a dry, safe place; keep at least three years of books.</li>
</ul>
