1. Obtaining Bees: Packages, Nucs, Swarms

To start beekeeping, you need bees. There are three main ways to get them: packages, nucleus colonies (nucs), and swarms. Each has pros and cons, and the best choice depends on your budget, location, and experience.

A package is a box of bees with a mated queen and about 1.5 to 2 kg of worker bees (roughly 10,000 to 15,000 bees). Packages are sold by suppliers and shipped through the post. They are convenient but can be stressful for the bees, and the queen may be rejected. In Africa, packages are less common because shipping is expensive and bees may overheat. However, some commercial beekeepers in South Africa and Kenya sell them. Expect to pay $80 to $150 per package.

A nucleus colony (nuc) is a small, working hive with 4 to 5 frames of comb, brood, bees, and a mated queen. Nucs are the best option for beginners because they are already established and ready to grow. You can buy a nuc from a local beekeeper for $100 to $200. Inspect the nuc before buying: check for a healthy queen, no signs of disease, and at least 3 frames of brood. In Africa, many beekeepers sell nucs in standard Langstroth or top-bar hives.

Catching a swarm is free but requires skill. Swarms are groups of bees that have left an overcrowded hive with a queen. They hang in clusters on tree branches, fences, or buildings. You can catch them by shaking them into a box or using a swarm trap. Swarm catching is best done in the early morning or evening when bees are calm. Always wear protective gear. Swarms are usually gentle because they have no hive to defend, but they may carry diseases. Quarantine them and treat for mites if needed.

In Africa, local bee races like Apis mellifera scutellata are aggressive but productive. If you buy bees, source them from a reputable local breeder to ensure they are adapted to your climate. Never import bees illegally; it can introduce pests and diseases. For beginners, a nuc is the safest start. If you are patient and want to save money, learn to catch swarms. Packages are a last resort due to cost and stress. Whichever method you choose, prepare your hive before the bees arrive.

2. Installing Package Bees

Installing package bees is a delicate process. You have a box of bees with a queen in a small cage. The goal is to get them into your hive with minimal stress and ensure the queen is accepted.

First, prepare your hive. Choose a sunny, sheltered spot with windbreaks. Set up a brood box with frames, leaving out 2-3 frames to make space. Have a feeder ready with 1:1 sugar syrup (1 kg sugar to 1 litre water). Wear a bee suit, gloves, and use a smoker.

When the package arrives, keep it in a cool, dark place. Spray the package with a little sugar syrup to calm the bees. Do not open it indoors. Take the package to the hive site. Remove the feeder can and the queen cage. The queen cage is usually hanging inside. Plug the hole of the queen cage with a small marshmallow or candy. This allows the bees to eat through and release her slowly. If you use a marshmallow, make sure it is plain and not coated in chocolate.

Now, shake the bees into the hive. Give the package a sharp downward shake to dislodge the bees. Pour them into the brood box. Some bees will fly, but they will orient to the hive. Place the queen cage between two frames, with the candy side up. Then gently put the frames back, leaving a gap for the queen cage. Close the hive.

Feed the bees immediately with sugar syrup. Use a feeder inside the hive or an entrance feeder. Continue feeding until they have drawn out at least 6 frames of comb. This can take 4-6 weeks. Check the hive after 3 days to see if the queen is released. If not, release her manually. After a week, check for eggs. If you see eggs, the queen is laying. Remove the empty package and any dead bees. In Africa, install packages in the early morning or late afternoon to avoid heat stress. Provide shade if temperatures exceed 35C. Keep the entrance small to prevent robbing. With proper care, the colony will grow and you can add a second box in 6-8 weeks.

3. Installing a Nucleus Colony

Installing a nucleus colony (nuc) is easier than a package because the bees already have comb, brood, and a queen. They are less likely to abscond. Here is how to do it.

First, prepare your hive. Choose a permanent location with morning sun and afternoon shade. Set up a brood box with frames. You will transfer the 4-5 nuc frames into the hive. Have a feeder ready with 1:1 sugar syrup. Wear protective gear.

Bring the nuc to the hive site in the evening or early morning when bees are calm. Smoke the entrance lightly. Open the nuc box. If the frames are fixed with propolis, use a hive tool to loosen them. Carefully lift each frame and place it into the brood box in the same order. Keep the brood frames together. If you have fewer frames than the hive, fill the empty spaces with foundation or drawn comb. Do not leave empty gaps; the bees will build burr comb.

Once all frames are transferred, close the hive. Leave the entrance open. The bees will orient to the new location. Feed the colony with sugar syrup for the first 2-3 weeks. This helps them draw new comb and raise brood. Check the hive after 3 days. Look for the queen and eggs. If the queen is not laying, check again in a week. If she is still not laying, you may need to replace her.

In Africa, nucs are often installed in top-bar hives. In that case, transfer the combs gently and attach them to top bars with string or wire. Ensure the combs are straight. After installation, reduce the entrance to prevent robbing by other bees. Keep the hive shaded if it is very hot. Do not disturb the colony for a week after installation. Then inspect weekly. Within 4-6 weeks, the colony should fill the brood box. Add a super when 7-8 frames are drawn. Nuc installation is the most reliable way to start beekeeping. With proper care, your colony will thrive.

4. Catching and Hiving Swarms

Catching a swarm is an exciting and free way to get bees. Swarms are usually gentle because they have no hive to defend. But you must act quickly and safely.

First, identify a swarm. It looks like a cluster of bees hanging from a branch, fence, or wall. Swarms usually appear in spring or early summer, after rain. They may stay for a few hours to a few days. If you find one, prepare your equipment: a bee suit, gloves, a smoker, a cardboard box or a swarm trap, and a hive ready with frames.

Approach the swarm calmly. Smoke lightly to calm the bees. If the swarm is on a branch, hold a box underneath and shake the branch sharply. Most bees will fall into the box. If the swarm is on a wall, use a brush or your gloved hands to gently scoop them into the box. Do not crush bees. Once the queen is in the box, the rest will follow. Leave the box near the swarm until evening so stragglers can join.

After dark, when all bees are inside, close the box and take it to your hive. Open the hive and shake the bees onto the frames. Or, if you used a swarm trap, transfer the frames. Close the hive and leave them alone for a week. Feed them sugar syrup to help them settle. In Africa, swarms may be Africanized (aggressive). Always wear protective gear and work at night or early morning. If the swarm is in a dangerous place, call an experienced beekeeper.

Swarms can carry pests like varroa mites. After hiving, monitor for mites and treat if necessary. Also, check for diseases like American foulbrood. If you see symptoms, destroy the colony to prevent spread. Catching swarms is cost-effective but requires patience. Set up swarm traps (a box with old comb and lemongrass oil) in trees around your apiary. Check traps weekly during swarm season. With practice, you can build your apiary for free.

5. Feeding New Colonies

New colonies need extra food to build comb, raise brood, and survive. In Africa, nectar flow may be seasonal, so feeding is essential. The main feed is sugar syrup. Use white sugar, not brown or raw sugar, as it can cause dysentery. Mix 1 kg sugar with 1 litre of clean water for spring and summer feeding. For autumn, use 2 kg sugar to 1 litre water (thicker syrup) to help them store food.

Feed using a hive-top feeder, entrance feeder, or a plastic bag with small holes. Place the feeder inside the hive to prevent robbing. If using an entrance feeder, reduce the entrance. Feed in the evening to avoid attracting other bees. Provide 500 ml to 1 litre of syrup per week for a new colony. Stop feeding when they have drawn 6-8 frames of comb and there is a nectar flow.

You can also feed pollen substitute if natural pollen is scarce. Mix soy flour, brewer's yeast, and sugar syrup into a patty. Place it on the top bars. Pollen is needed for brood rearing. In dry areas, provide a water source nearby. Bees need water to dilute honey and cool the hive. Put a shallow container with stones and water near the apiary.

Do not feed honey from unknown sources; it can carry diseases. Never feed during a honey flow if you plan to harvest honey, as it can mix with your crop. In Africa, some beekeepers feed cassava flour or maize meal as pollen substitute, but these are not ideal. Stick to sugar syrup and commercial pollen substitutes if available. Monitor the colony's weight by lifting the hive from behind. If it feels light, feed more. Overfeeding can cause the queen to lay excessively and lead to swarming. Balance feeding with natural forage. Always keep records of feeding dates and amounts. Healthy, well-fed colonies are more resistant to pests and diseases.

6. First Inspections and Record Keeping

After installing your bees, you must inspect the hive regularly to ensure the colony is healthy and growing. The first inspection should be 3-7 days after installation, depending on the method. For packages, check after 3 days to see if the queen is released. For nucs, check after 5-7 days. For swarms, leave them alone for a week.

When inspecting, wear protective gear and use a smoker. Work from the side of the hive, not in front. Open the hive gently. Remove the outer cover and inner cover. Use a hive tool to loosen frames. Lift out one frame at a time. Look for: the queen (or eggs), brood in all stages (eggs, larvae, capped), food stores (honey and pollen), and signs of pests or diseases. Check for queen cells; if you see them, the colony may be preparing to swarm. In Africa, watch for small hive beetles and ants. Keep the inspection short"no more than 15 minutes. Do not crush bees.

Record everything you see. Use a notebook or a simple form. Write the date, time, weather, number of frames with brood, number of frames with food, queen seen (yes/no), eggs seen (yes/no), and any problems. Also note any actions taken, like feeding or adding a super. Records help you track the colony's progress and predict problems. For example, if you see no eggs for two inspections, the queen may be failing. If you see queen cells, you may need to split the colony.

In Africa, many beekeepers use a hive logbook. Keep it in a safe place. Also, mark the hive with a number. Inspect every 7-10 days during the active season. In hot weather, inspect early morning or evening. In cold weather, inspect less often. After the first month, you can reduce inspections to every 2 weeks if the colony is strong. Regular inspections and good records are the keys to successful beekeeping. They help you catch problems early and ensure a good honey harvest.

7. Practical Reference


Practical Reference: Starting and Managing a Colony



For the African beekeeper, the difference between a hobby hive and a profitable apiary often comes down to how well a colony is started. A strong, well-fed, disease-free colony can produce 20"40 kg of honey per year under good conditions, plus beeswax, propolis and pollination services. A poorly started colony may abscond within weeks, taking your investment with it. This reference brings together the practical techniques for obtaining, installing, feeding and first-inspecting colonies, with an eye on the realities of African beekeeping: limited access to commercial suppliers, the availability of wild swarms, and the need to keep costs low while maximising returns.



Obtaining Bees: Packages, Nucs and Swarms



There are three main ways to start a colony in Africa: buy a package, buy a nucleus (nuc), or catch a wild swarm. Each has different costs, risks and timelines.



Packages are boxes of 1"1.5 kg of worker bees with a mated queen in a separate cage. They are common in North America and Europe but rare in most of Africa because of transport costs and the difficulty of keeping bees alive in transit. If you do find a supplier, expect to pay a premium and install the package immediately.



Nucleus colonies (nucs) are small, established colonies of 4"5 frames with brood, bees, a laying queen and food stores. They are the gold standard for starting because they are already a functioning colony. In Africa, nucs are increasingly available from trained beekeepers, cooperatives and projects such as those supported by the African Beekeepers Association. A good nuc costs more than a package but establishes faster and is less likely to abscond.



Swarms are free but unpredictable. In many African regions, swarm season coincides with the start of the rains or the flowering of key trees. Catching a swarm requires a swarm box or bait hive, lemongrass oil or beeswax as attractant, and patience. The advantage is zero purchase cost; the disadvantage is that the swarm may carry pests or diseases, and its genetics are unknown.





Installing Package Bees


If you are using a package, prepare a hive with drawn comb or foundation, remove the queen cage, and place the package in the hive. The queen cage should be hung between frames with the candy plug exposed; workers will release her over 2"3 days. Shake the bees into the hive, close it, and feed sugar syrup immediately. In Africa, packages are rarely cost-effective because local nucs and swarms are cheaper and better adapted. If you do use a package, install it in the late afternoon to reduce drifting and absconding.



Installing a Nucleus Colony


Nuc installation is straightforward. Place the nuc box beside the new hive, smoke lightly, and transfer frames one by one into the same relative position. Ensure the queen is not left behind. Fill the remaining space with frames of foundation or drawn comb. Close the hive and feed. Do not open for 5"7 days to let the colony settle. In African conditions, nucs installed at the start of a nectar flow establish fastest. A well-installed nuc can be ready for honey supering in 6"8 weeks.



Catching and Hiving Swarms


Swarm catching is the most common entry point for African beekeepers. Use a bait hive: a wooden box or hollow log rubbed with beeswax and lemongrass oil, placed at least 3"4 metres above ground in a shaded, sheltered spot. Swarms are most common at the start of the rainy season and during heavy flowering. When a swarm arrives, leave it for 2"3 days to settle, then transfer frames or combs into a permanent hive in the evening. Wear protective gear; Africanised or aggressive strains are common in some regions.




SAFETY WARNING:
Never handle bees without a veil, gloves and long clothing. African bee strains can be highly defensive. Work in the late afternoon or early evening when bees are calmer. Do not disturb a colony in cold, wet or windy weather. If you are allergic to bee stings, do not keep bees. Always have a smoke source and a clear escape route. In areas with Africanised bees, maintain a 30-metre safety zone from homes, schools and livestock pens.


Feeding New Colonies


New colonies need sugar syrup (1:1 by weight) to draw comb and raise brood. Feed continuously for the first 4"6 weeks, or until the colony has drawn at least 6"8 frames. In dry seasons or areas with low nectar, feed a 2:1 syrup to build stores. Never feed honey from unknown sources"it can carry disease spores. In Africa, where sugar may be expensive, consider planting bee forage (sunflower, moringa, acacia, citrus) around the apiary to reduce feeding costs.

First Inspections and Record Keeping


First inspection should happen 5"7 days after installation. Look for: queen presence (eggs or young larvae), brood pattern, food stores, and signs of pests or disease. Keep a simple record card for each hive: date, weather, queen seen, brood frames, stores, actions taken. In Africa, where apiaries may be remote, a notebook and pencil are more reliable than a smartphone. Record keeping helps you track colony performance, predict swarming, and make business decisions about honey harvesting and hive expansion.

Quick Reference Checklist



  • Choose your start method: Nuc if you can afford it, swarm if you cannot. Packages are rarely cost-effective in Africa.

  • Prepare the hive before bees arrive: Clean, shaded, water nearby, entrance facing away from wind.

  • Feed 1:1 syrup for 4"6 weeks: Stop when 6"8 frames are drawn.

  • Inspect after 5"7 days: Look for eggs, brood, stores and pests.

  • Keep records: Date, queen, brood, stores, actions. Use a notebook.

  • Wear protective gear: Veil, gloves, long clothing. Smoke before opening.

  • Watch for absconding: If bees cluster outside, check for pests, hunger or overheating.

  • Plan for the flow: Add supers 2"3 weeks before the main nectar flow.
