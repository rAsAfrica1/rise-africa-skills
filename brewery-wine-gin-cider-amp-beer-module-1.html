1. Choosing the Right Location: Access to Water, Market, and Transport in Your Town

When choosing a location for your small brewery, water is your first priority. You need a reliable source of clean waterâat least 500 litres per day for a micro-brewery producing 100 litres of beer or wine. In many African towns, municipal water is intermittent, so budget for a borehole (cost: $1,500â$3,000) or a 5,000-litre storage tank ($300â$500). Test your water for iron and chlorine; high iron ruins beer flavour. If you're in Kenya, contact the Water Resources Authority for permits; in Nigeria, the state water corporation; in South Africa, your local municipality.

Second, be close to your market. Selling to bars, restaurants, and individuals within a 5 km radius cuts transport costs. A location near a busy market or transport hub (like a taxi rank or bus park) increases foot traffic. In Lagos, a shop in Ikeja or Yaba works; in Nairobi, industrial area or Westlands; in Johannesburg, Maboneng or Soweto. Avoid residential areas with noise restrictionsâbreweries run pumps and bottling lines.

Third, transport access. You need a road that can handle a 3-ton truck for deliveries of raw materials (malt, sugar, yeast) and finished products. If your road floods in rainy season, that's a problem. Also consider power supply: brewing requires heating and cooling. A location with stable electricity (or space for a generator) is crucial. In many African cities, power outages are common, so a 5 kVA generator ($800â$1,200) is a must.

Finally, check zoning laws. In Kenya, you need a Trade License from the county and a change of user if the area is residential. In Nigeria, you need a Business Premises Registration with the Corporate Affairs Commission. In South Africa, zoning certificates from the municipality. Talk to neighbours before signing a leaseâsmells from fermentation and waste water can cause complaints. A good location balances water, market, transport, and legal compliance. Spend time scouting; a bad location will cost you more in the long run.

2. Licensing and Permits for Alcohol Production in Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa

Alcohol production is heavily regulated. In Kenya, you need a license from the Kenya Revenue Authority (KRA) for excise duty, and a permit from the county government. The Alcoholic Drinks Control Act (2010) requires a manufacturer's license from the county alcoholic drinks control committee. Fees vary: a small-scale producer license costs about KES 50,000 per year. You also need a KRA Excise License (KES 10,000) and a Health Certificate from the public health office. Apply at your county offices; processing takes 4â8 weeks.

In Nigeria, the key regulator is the National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC). You must register your product and facility. The process includes an inspection, product testing, and a registration fee of about NGN 100,000â200,000. You also need a license from the Nigeria Customs Service for excise duty, and a state liquor license from the Ministry of Trade. In Lagos, the Lagos State Liquor Licensing Authority issues permits. Expect 2â3 months for approvals.

In South Africa, the South African Revenue Service (SARS) handles excise duties, and the Liquor Products Act (1989) governs production. You need a manufacturing license from the Department of Agriculture, Land Reform and Rural Development. The cost is around ZAR 5,000â10,000. You also need a liquor license from the provincial liquor authority (e.g., Gauteng Liquor Board). For small-scale producers, there's a micro-manufacturing license with lower fees. The process involves submitting a business plan, facility layout, and proof of water quality.

Common to all three countries: you need a company registration (e.g., Kenya's eCitizen, Nigeria's CAC, South Africa's CIPC), tax compliance, and health permits for employees. Hire a local consultant if possibleâthey know the shortcuts. Budget $500â$2,000 for all licenses, and start early; delays can kill your launch.

5. Hiring and Training Your First Two Employees

Your first two hires should be a brewer's assistant and a sales/delivery person. For the brewer's assistant, look for someone with basic numeracy and a strong work ethicânot necessarily brewing experience. In Kenya, hire from local technical colleges (e.g., Kenya Polytechnic) or through word of mouth. In Nigeria, check Yaba College of Technology; in South Africa, TVET colleges. Pay: Kenya KES 15,000â25,000/month; Nigeria NGN 50,000â80,000; South Africa ZAR 4,000â6,000. Add housing and transport allowances.

Training: spend the first week on sanitation. Teach them to wash all equipment with hot water and food-grade sanitiser (e.g., Star San). Show them how to take hydrometer readings and record them. For brewing, demonstrate the mashing process: heat water to 65Â°C, add malt, stir, rest for 1 hour. Then boil with hops for 1 hour, cool, and pitch yeast. For gin, teach distillation: ferment sugar wash, then run through still, discarding foreshots (first 50 ml) and tails. For wine, crush fruit, add yeast, and rack after 2 weeks. Use a notebook to log every batchâdate, ingredients, temperatures, readings.

Sales/delivery person: train them to approach bars, restaurants, and individuals. Give them samples and a price list. Teach them to use a simple invoice book. In Kenya, use M-Pesa for payments; in Nigeria, bank transfer or cash; in South Africa, SnapScan or cash. Set a daily sales target: e.g., 20 litres per day. Pay a commission of 10% on sales above target.

Safety: train both on fire safety (keep a fire extinguisher near the still), and proper handling of chemicals. Provide gloves and boots. Hold weekly 15-minute meetings to review production and sales. Treat them wellâpay on time, provide lunchâto reduce turnover. Your team is your biggest asset.

6. Health and Safety Basics: Sanitation, Fire Safety, and Waste Disposal

Sanitation is non-negotiable. Every surface that touches your brew must be cleaned with hot water (80Â°C) and a food-grade sanitiser. Use a no-rinse acid sanitiser like Star San (mix 30 ml per 20 litres of water). In Kenya, buy from brewing suppliers in Nairobi; in Nigeria, from Lagos; in South Africa, from Brewcraft. Clean fermenters immediately after useâdried residue harbours bacteria. Use a separate brush for each vessel. Sterilise bottles by soaking in sanitiser for 2 minutes, then drain. Your hands must be washed with soap before handling equipment. Keep a bucket of sanitiser nearby.

Fire safety: Distilling produces highly flammable alcohol vapour. Never distill near an open flame. Use an electric hot plate or a gas burner with a closed flame. Keep a dry powder fire extinguisher (5 kg, $30) within 3 metres of the still. In Kenya, buy from Safety Kenya; in Nigeria, from Lagos Safety; in South Africa, from Fireco. Store alcohol in sealed containers away from heat. If you smell gas, turn off the burner and ventilate. Have a fire blanket for small fires. Train your employees to use the extinguisherâPASS technique: Pull, Aim, Squeeze, Sweep.

Waste disposal: Brewing produces large volumes of wastewater (5â10 litres per litre of beer) and solid waste (spent grain, yeast, fruit pulp). Never dump wastewater into storm drainsâit kills fish and plants. In Kenya, check with NEMA for disposal guidelines; in Nigeria, with NESREA; in South Africa, with the Department of Environmental Affairs. Spent grain can be sold as animal feed (KES 10/kg in Kenya, NGN 50/kg in Nigeria, ZAR 2/kg in South Africa). Yeast slurry can be composted. Fruit pulp can be composted or fed to pigs. Wastewater can be used to irrigate non-edible crops after settling. Keep a waste log. Fines for illegal dumping are heavy: up to KES 1 million in Kenya. Do it rightâit protects your community and your business.

This practical reference gives you a working checklist for the six lessons in this module. It is written for founders who are already thinking about cash flow, licensing, suppliers, and staffânot for hobbyists. Use it alongside the lesson videos and worksheets. 1. Choosing the Right Location: Water, Market, Transport

Location is a commercial decision before it is a production decision. In most African towns, the winning site sits on a reliable water source, within reach of a market you can serve weekly, and on a road that a 3-tonne truck can use in the rainy season. Water quality matters more than water volume: brewing and distilling concentrate any contaminants, so test for nitrates, iron, and coliforms before signing a lease. The WHO Guidelines for Drinking-water Quality (4th edition) provide the reference limits you can show a landlord or water utility.

Market access means distance to your first 20 paying customers, not distance to the capital city. A 40 km trip on a tarmac road is cheaper than 8 km on a rutted track. Transport cost per crate or per 20 L jerrycan should be calculated before you commit. If your town has a growing hospitality strip, a university, or a mining camp, those are your anchor buyers.

3. Sourcing Affordable Equipment

Buy locally where possible. A fermenter fabricated in Nairobi, Lagos, or Johannesburg can be repaired by the same workshop, while an imported unit may sit idle for months waiting for a spare seal. For a micro-brewery producing 200â500 L per week, the core kit is: a hot liquor tank, mash tun, kettle, fermenters (food-grade stainless or HDPE), a still if you are making gin or spirits, a bottle filler or capper, and a clean-in-place (CIP) pump.

Ask every supplier for three things: material certificate (food-grade 304 stainless or HDPE), spare parts list, and a written warranty. For stills, confirm the copper or stainless grade and that the design is legal in your jurisdictionâhome-made stills may breach excise rules. Bottling gear should match your bottle format; mismatched caps are a hidden cost.

4. Budgeting for a Micro-Brewery in Local Currency

Build your budget in the currency you actually pay in, then add a 15% contingency. A realistic 500 L/week micro-brewery in an African town typically needs start-up capital for equipment, licences, rent deposit, raw materials, packaging, and three months of wages. Use the categories below as a template and fill in your own quotes.

  • Equipment: fermenters, kettle, still, bottling line, CIP, refrigeration.
  • Licences and compliance: business registration, tax, alcohol licence, health permits.
  • Premises: rent deposit, water connection, drainage, electrical upgrade.
  • Raw materials: malt, hops, yeast, sugar, botanicals, bottles, caps, labels.
  • People: two salaries, training, protective equipment.
  • Working capital: three months of fixed costs before break-even.

Track unit economics from day one: cost per litre, cost per crate, and gross margin per sales channel. If your cost per litre is above the local wholesale price, no amount of marketing will fix the business.

5. Hiring and Training Your First Two Employees

Your first two hires should cover production and sales. Hire a brewer or production assistant with documented food or beverage experience, and a sales/delivery person who knows your town. Train both on standard operating procedures (SOPs): cleaning, fermentation records, bottling hygiene, and safe handling of chemicals and COâ.

Put job descriptions and training records in writing. In many African jurisdictions, written contracts and statutory deductions (PAYE, NSSF, NHIF or equivalents) are required. Budget for PPE: boots, gloves, aprons, and eye protection. Cross-train so that a single absence does not stop production.

6. Health and Safety Basics: Sanitation, Fire, Waste

Sanitation is your product quality system. Use a documented cleaning schedule, food-grade sanitisers, and separate equipment for raw and finished product. Fire safety means extinguishers serviced annually, no smoking near the still, and COâ monitoring in fermentation rooms. Waste disposal must be agreed with your local authorityâspent grain can go to livestock, but effluent with high biological oxygen demand cannot be discharged into drains without treatment.
