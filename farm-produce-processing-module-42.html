Costing, Pricing & Profit Per Unit

8 lessons · 30 quiz questions · Business

1 Cost per unit breakdown

You cannot price a product unless you know exactly what it costs. Break every product into four cost categories:

  • Direct materials — raw produce, packaging, labels, caps, boxes
  • Direct labour — wages for people making the product
  • Overheads — rent, electricity, water, equipment depreciation, admin
  • Waste — rejected batches, spoiled materials, spillage

Example (500 g peanut butter jar): materials $0.80, labour $0.20, overhead $0.15, waste $0.05 = total cost $1.20. Selling at $2.00 gives $0.80 gross profit per jar (40% margin).

💡 Recalculate every cost when prices change — raw materials shift monthly. Stale numbers lose money.

2 Raw materials, packaging, labour

Raw materials: Biggest cost for most food products. Buy in bulk, sign farmer contracts, buy in season.

Packaging: Glass jars cost 5× more than plastic. Labels cost 5–15 cents each. Every cent per unit adds up over thousands of units.

Labour: Calculate as "wage per hour ÷ units per hour." If a worker earns $3/hour and fills 60 jars/hour, labour cost is $0.05 per jar.

Never forget the hidden costs: cleaning time, machine setup, waste disposal. These add 5–10%.

3 Overheads and waste

Overheads are costs that don't change with volume — but per-unit they shrink as you scale.

Monthly overheads to track:

  • Rent or mortgage on facility
  • Electricity, water, gas
  • Equipment maintenance and depreciation
  • Insurance
  • Licences and permits
  • Office/admin costs

Divide total overheads by monthly units produced = overhead per unit.

Waste: Track what you throw away. Rotten produce, burnt batches, mislabelled jars. Target under 5% waste. Above 10% means a process problem.

4 Break-even calculation

Break-even is the number of units you must sell to cover costs and start making profit.

Formula: Break-even units = Fixed costs ÷ (Selling price − Variable cost per unit)

Example: rent + wages + overheads = $3,000 per month. Selling price per jar = $2.00. Variable costs (materials + packaging) = $1.10. Contribution margin = $0.90 per unit. Break-even = 3,000 ÷ 0.90 = 3,334 jars per month.

Every jar above 3,334 is profit. Below that number, you're losing money.

5 Retail vs wholesale pricing

Two prices for the same product, depending on who buys it.

Retail price: What consumers pay. Highest margin, most work (you sell one jar at a time).

Wholesale price: What shops pay. Usually 30–40% lower. Bigger volume, less work.

Rule of thumb: Wholesale = 60–70% of retail. If retail is $5, wholesale is $3–3.50. The shop then sells at $5 and keeps the difference as their margin.

Serve both: wholesale for steady cash flow, retail for margin. Best: build your own brand and sell direct online, matching retail price without a shop's cut.

6 Discounts that hurt

Discounts are a trap. Every discount cuts straight into profit.

Example: You make $0.80 profit at $2.00 price. A 20% "small discount" ($0.40 off) cuts your profit in half. Sell twice as many to make the same profit.

Safe discounts:

  • Bulk discount for 10+ units (buyer commits more money)
  • Pre-payment discount (cash today)
  • Seasonal clearance (old stock)

Never discount: Repeat loyal customers (they'll expect it forever), or as a default when someone asks.

7 Raising prices without losing customers

Costs rise. You must raise prices eventually.

  1. Give notice — tell customers 30 days before
  2. Explain why — raw material cost, packaging cost, currency
  3. Adjust one product at a time — never all at once
  4. Bundle or add value — same price, more product or bonus
  5. Watch for drops — if sales fall over 30%, review

Most customers understand. Those who leave were buying on price alone — not loyal anyway.

8 Monthly profit report

Every month, produce a simple one-page report:

  • Total sales (units and revenue)
  • Cost of materials
  • Cost of labour
  • Overheads
  • Gross profit and net profit
  • Top 3 products by margin
  • Worst 3 products (consider dropping)
  • Cash at month end

Use a spreadsheet. Update weekly, review monthly. What gets measured gets improved.

💰 Most food businesses fail because owners don't know their true cost per unit. Do this one thing and you'll beat 80% of competitors.
