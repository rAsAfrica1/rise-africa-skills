Module 4

The Top-Bar Hive — Kenyan and Tanzanian Designs

A top-bar hive is simpler to build than a Langstroth, better suited to African bees, and easier to inspect. It is the correct choice for a smallholder starting out. This module is the full build, both variants.

  • Distinguish the Kenyan top-bar hive from the Tanzanian design
  • Cut and assemble a hive body to correct bee-space dimensions
  • Cut top bars that lift out cleanly and hold comb

Lesson 4.1

Kenyan vs Tanzanian — the two patterns

Top-bar hives are horizontal boxes with a row of removable bars across the top. Two patterns dominate Africa:

FeatureKenyan (KTBH)Tanzanian (TTBH)
Body shapeSloped sides (trapezoid in cross-section)Straight sides (rectangular in cross-section)
SlopeWalls slope inward from top to bottom at ~60°Vertical walls
Bar length~480 mm~750 mm
Body length~900 mm~1,000 mm
Body width at top~450 mm~450 mm
Body depth~300 mm~300 mm
AdvantageSloped sides discourage bees attaching comb to walls (a natural comb shape)Simpler to cut — no angles
DisadvantageMore complex carpentry — the slope must be accurateBees attach comb to walls more often

The Kenyan pattern is preferred by most experts because the sloped sides genuinely reduce comb attachment. But the Tanzanian pattern is easier to build and works well if you inspect regularly and detach comb from the walls.

For most smallholders, build the Kenyan. The extra carpentry cost is small, and the management benefit over years is real.

Lesson 4.2

The hive body — cut and assemble

Kenyan top-bar hive body dimensions

PartQtyLengthWidthThickness
Long side wall2900 mm350 mm25 mm
Short end wall2450 mm350 mm25 mm
Bottom board1900 mm450 mm20 mm

Side walls are cut to a trapezoid — top width 450 mm, bottom width 350 mm, height 300 mm. The trapezoid shape is what gives the hive its sloped sides.

Cutting the slope

  1. Start with a rectangular board 900 × 300 mm.
  2. Mark the top edge at 450 mm width (centred).
  3. Mark the bottom edge at 350 mm width (centred).
  4. Cut along the marked lines to produce the trapezoid.

Assembly

  1. Dry-fit the sides and ends. Check square.
  2. Glue and screw — 4–5 screws per joint.
  3. Attach the bottom board, flush with the sloped sides.
  4. Seal all joints with exterior wood filler.
  5. Cut the entrance slot: 300 mm wide, 15 mm high, at the bottom of one end wall.
  6. Cut the bar rest: a 20 mm wide × 15 mm deep groove in the top edge of both side walls, spanning the full length.

The bar rest — critical dimension

The bar rest must be exactly 20 mm wide and 15 mm deep. If too wide, the bees propolise. If too narrow, the bars rock. Check by trial-fitting one bar before finishing.

The sloped-side rule: the slope must be at 60° or shallower. Steeper than 60° and the bees treat it as a wall and attach comb to it. This is the single design feature that distinguishes the Kenyan hive from the Tanzanian.

Lesson 4.3

Cutting top bars

Top bars are the working surface of the top-bar hive. They carry the comb, they lift out for inspection, and their spacing decides bee space.

Dimensions

DimensionValue
Length480 mm (KTBH) or 750 mm (TTBH)
Width32 mm
Thickness20 mm
Spacing (centre-to-centre)35 mm — bee space

Cutting steps

  1. Cut all bars to the same length — 480 mm for KTBH.
  2. Cut to the same width — 32 mm — with a table saw or a rip fence.
  3. Cut a shallow chamfer or a saw kerf along the bottom edge of each bar. This is the comb guide — see below.
  4. Sand the top surfaces of the bars smooth. They are what you touch during inspection.
  5. Apply a beeswax and linseed oil mix to the underside of the bars (do not paint).

Comb guides

The bees need a starting line so they build comb in the right place, hanging from the centre of each bar. Three options:

  • Saw kerf: a 3 mm wide, 3 mm deep cut along the bottom centre of the bar. Simplest. Works well in most conditions.
  • Wax strip: a 15 mm wide strip of beeswax foundation fixed along the bottom centre. Best comb guidance. Adds cost and prep time.
  • Chamfered edge: a chamfer cut along both bottom edges, leaving a ridge in the centre. The ridge is the guide. Used in some traditional hives.

For most smallholders, a simple saw kerf is enough. The bees reliably use it as a starting line.

How many bars?

At 35 mm spacing over 900 mm length (with 30 mm clearance at each end), the KTBH fits 24–26 bars. Cut 28 — two spares are always useful.

Lesson 4.4

The follower board

A follower board is a solid vertical board that sits inside the hive body, moving along its length as the colony grows. It divides the hive into an active section (where the bees live) and an empty section (where they will expand into).

Why the follower matters

  • Keeps the colony warm. A small colony in a large cavity spends energy heating unused space. The follower confines them.
  • Prevents comb-building in the wrong place. Bees build comb from the follower outward, not from the entrance end backward.
  • Simplifies expansion. As the colony grows, you move the follower one bar further away.
  • Allows smaller colonies to thrive. A nucleus colony in a top-bar hive without a follower frequently absconds.

Follower board dimensions

  • Width: 32 mm (matches top bar)
  • Length: 480 mm (spans the bar rest)
  • Height: 280 mm (fits inside the body, leaves a small bee space at the bottom)

Positioning

Install the follower at the entrance end, 3–4 bars in, when you first introduce a swarm. Move it outward one bar at a time as the colony expands. By the end of a strong season, it moves 15–20 bars down the hive.

Lesson 4.5

The roof, stand and finishing

Roof

The roof covers the full bar row and overhangs by 80–100 mm on all sides. Dimensions: 1,050 × 600 × 12 mm top board with a 60 mm rim.

  • Top board: plywood or timber, covered with galvanised sheet.
  • Rim: 60 mm tall, on all four sides, under the top board. Sits over the hive body, keeps rain off the bar rest.
  • Overhang: 80–100 mm all round.
  • Metal covering: galvanised sheet, wrapped and stapled. Non-optional in Africa; without it, the roof rots in 2 years.

Stand

A top-bar hive is long. It needs a stand that supports the full length without sagging. Two options:

  • Two trestles under the ends. Simple. Four legs, two cross beams.
  • Four legs directly under the hive. Sturdy but harder to level.

Legs must be at least 300 mm above the ground. Treat the legs (not the hive body) with wood preservative or use naturally durable timber.

Finishing

  • Paint the exterior only. Never the interior. Bees propolise the inside.
  • Do not paint the bar rest. Bees contact this surface; paint flakes into the honey.
  • Light colour on the roof reflects heat in hot climates.
  • Seal the top of the walls with exterior sealant where the bars rest, so water cannot enter the joint.
The top-bar rules: 32 mm bar width; 35 mm spacing; 20 mm bar rest; 60° slope on the sides; follower board in place; 80 mm overhang. Miss any of these and the hive will either propolise shut, sag, or lose its comb.

Knowledge check — Module 4

1. What is the main design difference between a Kenyan and a Tanzanian top-bar hive?

Correct: sloped vs straight sides. The Kenyan slope (60°) discourages bees attaching comb to the walls.

2. What is the correct top bar spacing?

Correct: 35 mm. Bee space — same as the Langstroth. Wrong spacing breaks the whole system.

3. What is the purpose of a follower board?

Correct: confine the colony. A small colony in a large cavity spends energy heating empty space and often absconds.

4. What angle should the slope on a Kenyan hive's sides be?

Correct: 60°. Steeper than 60° and the bees treat the wall as vertical and attach comb to it.

5. What is a comb guide?

Correct: starting line for comb. Simplest is a saw kerf; wax strip is best quality; chamfer is traditional.
