1. Roasting and Braising for Crowds

Roasting and braising are your best friends when cooking for 20 to 50 people. Roasting uses dry heat in an oven, while braising combines searing and slow cooking in liquid. For roasting, preheat your oven to 180Â°C (350Â°F). Use large roasting pans or disposable aluminum trays. For chicken pieces, season with salt, pepper, and paprika, then roast for 40-45 minutes until internal temperature reaches 75Â°C (165Â°F). For beef or goat, roast at 160Â°C (320Â°F) for 2-3 hours until tender. Always use a meat thermometer to avoid overcooking.

Braising is perfect for tough cuts like beef chuck, goat shoulder, or pork shoulder. Sear the meat in batches over high heat in a heavy pot with oil. Remove meat, sautÃ© onions, garlic, and tomatoes until soft. Add meat back, cover with stock or water, and simmer on low heat for 2-3 hours. For a crowd, use a large cast-iron pot or a slow cooker. Season with local spices like ginger, chili, and curry powder. Braised dishes can be made a day ahead and reheated, saving time on event day.

For roasting vegetables like potatoes, carrots, and butternut, cut into uniform pieces, toss with oil, salt, and herbs, and roast at 200Â°C (400Â°F) for 30-40 minutes, stirring halfway. This ensures even cooking. When cooking for a crowd, don't overcrowd the pan; use multiple pans if needed. Roasting and braising allow you to cook large quantities with minimal attention, leaving you free to handle other tasks. Remember to rest roasted meats for 10-15 minutes before carving to keep juices in. Braised dishes taste even better the next day, so consider making them ahead. Always keep food at safe temperatures: hot food above 60Â°C (140Â°F) and cold food below 5Â°C (40Â°F). With practice, you'll master these techniques and impress your clients.

2. SautÃ©ing and Pan-Searing

SautÃ©ing and pan-searing are quick, high-heat methods that give food a delicious brown crust. Use a heavy skillet or frying pan, preferably cast iron or stainless steel. For sautÃ©ing, heat 1-2 tablespoons of oil over medium-high heat until it shimmers. Add ingredients in a single layer; don't overcrowd. For onions, cook for 5-7 minutes until golden. For vegetables like bell peppers and zucchini, cook for 3-4 minutes until tender-crisp. Stir occasionally to ensure even cooking. For a crowd, work in batches to maintain high heat.

Pan-searing is ideal for proteins like beef steak, chicken breast, or fish fillets. Pat the meat dry with paper towels, season with salt and pepper. Heat 1 tablespoon of oil in the pan until smoking slightly. Place meat in the pan and sear for 3-4 minutes per side for a 2 cm thick steak, or until desired doneness. Use a meat thermometer: 60Â°C (140Â°F) for medium-rare beef, 75Â°C (165Â°F) for chicken. After searing, let meat rest for 5 minutes before slicing. For a crowd, sear in batches and finish in the oven at 180Â°C (350Â°F) if needed.

To make a simple pan sauce, after searing meat, remove it from the pan. Add chopped onions or garlic and sautÃ© for 1 minute. Pour in 1 cup of stock or water, scrape the brown bits, and simmer for 2-3 minutes. Add a spoon of butter or oil for richness. This sauce can be served over the meat. SautÃ©ing and pan-searing are fast, so prep all ingredients before you start. Use oils with high smoke points like sunflower, canola, or groundnut oil. Avoid olive oil for high-heat searing as it burns easily. Clean your pan between batches to prevent burning. These techniques add flavor and texture that your guests will love. Practice with small quantities first, then scale up for events.

3. Steaming and Poaching

Steaming and poaching are gentle cooking methods that preserve moisture and nutrients, perfect for delicate foods like fish, chicken, and vegetables. Steaming uses steam from boiling water to cook food. Use a steamer basket or a colander set over a pot of boiling water. For vegetables like carrots, broccoli, and green beans, steam for 5-8 minutes until tender but still bright. For fish fillets, steam for 8-10 minutes until opaque and flaky. For chicken pieces, steam for 20-25 minutes until internal temperature reaches 75Â°C (165Â°F). Add herbs, garlic, or lemon slices to the water for extra flavor.

Poaching cooks food submerged in liquid at a low temperature, around 70-80Â°C (160-175Â°F). The liquid can be water, stock, or milk. For chicken breast, poach in seasoned stock for 15-20 minutes until cooked through. For fish, poach in water with lemon and herbs for 8-10 minutes. For eggs, poach in simmering water for 3-4 minutes. Poaching is great for making moist, shredded chicken for salads or sandwiches. For a crowd, use a large pot and poach in batches. Keep the liquid at a gentle simmer; boiling will make meat tough.

Steaming and poaching are healthy because they don't require added fat. They are also forgiving: food is less likely to burn. For events, you can steam vegetables ahead and refresh them in a hot pan with a little oil. Poached chicken can be made a day ahead and stored in the fridge. When reheating, add a splash of stock to keep it moist. Use a thermometer to ensure safe temperatures. Steaming and poaching are excellent for clients who want lighter options. They also work well for foods that might dry out with other methods. Invest in a good steamer pot or a multi-tier steamer to increase capacity. Remember to season the cooking liquid well, as it flavors the food. With these techniques, you can offer healthy, delicious dishes that stand out.

4. Grilling and Smoking

Grilling and smoking add smoky, charred flavors that are popular for events. Grilling cooks food quickly over direct heat, while smoking uses low, indirect heat and wood smoke. For grilling, use a charcoal or gas grill. Heat charcoal until covered with grey ash, about 20-30 minutes. For gas, preheat to medium-high (200-230Â°C/400-450Â°F). Grill chicken pieces for 15-20 minutes, turning every 5 minutes, until internal temperature reaches 75Â°C (165Â°F). Grill beef steaks for 4-5 minutes per side for medium-rare. Grill vegetables like corn, peppers, and eggplant for 5-10 minutes, brushing with oil to prevent sticking.

Smoking is done in a smoker or a covered grill with wood chips. Soak wood chips (like hickory or apple) in water for 30 minutes. Set up indirect heat: coals on one side, food on the other. Maintain a temperature of 100-120Â°C (225-250Â°F). Smoke chicken for 2-3 hours, pork shoulder for 6-8 hours, and beef brisket for 8-12 hours. Use a meat thermometer to check doneness. Smoking requires patience but yields tender, flavorful meat. For a crowd, smoke large cuts ahead and reheat gently with a little stock.

For grilling and smoking, season meats with dry rubs or marinades. A simple dry rub: 2 tablespoons paprika, 1 tablespoon salt, 1 tablespoon black pepper, 1 tablespoon garlic powder. Apply generously and let sit for 30 minutes. For marinades, mix oil, acid (lemon juice or vinegar), and spices. Marinate chicken for at least 2 hours, beef overnight. Always oil the grill grates to prevent sticking. Use tongs to turn meat, not a fork, to keep juices in. For events, consider grilling in batches and holding in a warm oven at 80Â°C (175Â°F). Smoking can be done the day before and reheated. These methods require practice, but they create impressive, smoky flavors that clients love. Start with small batches and adjust seasoning as you go.

5. Baking and Pastry Basics

Baking and pastry are essential for catering events, from breads to cakes and pies. Start with accurate measurements: use a kitchen scale for flour, sugar, and butter. For bread, combine 500g flour, 10g salt, 7g yeast, and 350ml warm water. Knead for 10 minutes until smooth, let rise for 1 hour, shape, and bake at 220Â°C (425Â°F) for 25-30 minutes. For a crowd, make multiple loaves or use large sheet pans for focaccia. Bread can be baked ahead and frozen; reheat at 180Â°C (350Â°F) for 10 minutes.

For cakes, the creaming method works well: beat 250g butter and 250g sugar until fluffy, add 4 eggs one at a time, then fold in 250g flour and 2 teaspoons baking powder. Bake at 180Â°C (350Â°F) for 20-25 minutes for cupcakes, or 30-35 minutes for a 20cm round cake. For a crowd, bake in large rectangular pans and cut into squares. For pies and tarts, make a simple shortcrust: 300g flour, 150g cold butter, pinch of salt, and 1 egg yolk mixed with 2 tablespoons cold water. Chill for 30 minutes before rolling. Fill with fruit or custard and bake at 200Â°C (400Â°F) for 30-40 minutes.

Pastry basics include choux, puff, and shortcrust. For choux, boil 250ml water with 100g butter, add 150g flour, and beat in 4 eggs. Pipe and bake at 200Â°C (400Â°F) for 20 minutes. For puff pastry, you can buy ready-made to save time. Always preheat your oven and use parchment paper. For events, focus on items that hold well: cookies, brownies, and pound cake. Avoid delicate items that melt. Practice recipes in small batches, then scale up. Use local ingredients like cassava flour for gluten-free options, or plantain for fritters. Baking requires precision, so measure carefully. With practice, you can offer fresh breads and desserts that set your catering apart.

6. Holding and Reheating Foods

Holding and reheating foods safely is critical for catering. Hot food must be held at 60Â°C (140Â°F) or above, and cold food at 5Â°C (40Â°F) or below. Use chafing dishes, slow cookers, or insulated containers for hot food. For cold food, use ice baths or refrigerated trays. Never leave food in the danger zone (5-60Â°C/40-140Â°F) for more than 2 hours. When holding, stir food occasionally to distribute heat evenly. For large quantities, use multiple smaller containers rather than one big pot. This ensures even heating and makes serving easier.

Reheating must bring food back to 75Â°C (165Â°F) quickly. Use an oven, microwave, or stovetop. For rice, add a splash of water and reheat in a covered pot until steaming. For meats, reheat in a 160Â°C (320Â°F) oven with a little stock to prevent drying. For stews and soups, reheat on the stovetop over medium heat, stirring often. Avoid reheating more than once. If you must, cool quickly and refrigerate. For fried foods, reheat in a hot oven at 200Â°C (400Â°F) for 5-10 minutes to regain crispiness. Never reheat in a slow cooker on low, as it takes too long and risks bacterial growth.

Plan your cooking schedule so that foods are ready close to serving time. Cook items that hold well (braises, casseroles, baked goods) first, and items that need last-minute cooking (grilled meats, sautÃ©ed vegetables) last. Use a food thermometer to check temperatures. Label and date all prepared foods. For events, consider renting or buying insulated food carriers. If you don't have chafing dishes, use thick pots with lids and towels for insulation. Always keep raw and cooked foods separate. When in doubt, throw it out. Proper holding and reheating ensure food safety and quality, protecting your reputation and your clients' health.

Practical Reference: Cooking Techniques for Small Events

Master the technique that gives the best yield at the lowest risk for your specific menu, and you protect your profit per plate.

Roasting works when you have a reliable oven or a charcoal/wood-fired drum. These are your flavour-building and finishing techniques. SautÃ© onions, peppers, and tomato purÃ©e in batches â never overload the pan, or you steam instead of brown. Steaming and Poaching

Steaming is the highest-yield moist method: no fat loss, no water-soluble nutrient loss into cooking liquid, and easy portion control. Smoking (hot or cold) preserves flavour and can be done in a converted drum. Note that smoking is not a substitute for refrigeration; smoked foods still require temperature control.

Baking and Pastry Basics

Baking is the least forgiving technique because it depends on precise ratios and consistent oven temperature. For small events, focus on a narrow range: one reliable bread, one cake, one biscuit, and one savoury pastry. Mandazi, chapati, and simple sponge cakes have the best margin-to-risk ratio. Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Plan backwards from service time. Decide what is cooked fresh on site and what is cooked ahead and reheated.
  2. Calibrate your thermometer weekly. Ice water should read 0Â°C; boiling water 100Â°C (adjust for altitude).
  3. Batch by technique, not by dish. Roast all proteins together, then braise all sauces â this saves fuel and labour.
  4. Pre-chill cooked food in shallow trays. Deep pots cool too slowly and risk the danger zone.
  5. Label every container with cook time and use-by time. FIFO (first in, first out) prevents waste and error.
  6. Test one small batch of any new recipe before the event. Never debut a dish at a paying function.
  7. Keep a written service timeline. Note when each dish leaves the fire and when it must be served or discarded.
  8. Cost every technique per portion. Know your fuel, labour, and ingredient cost before you quote a client.
