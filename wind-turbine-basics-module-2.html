1. Measuring Wind Speed and Direction

Wind speed and direction are the two most important things to measure for any wind project. You need to know how fast the wind blows and from which direction, on average, over at least one full year. In Africa, many sites have strong seasonal winds, like the trade winds in Kenya or the Harmattan in West Africa. For a small wind turbine, you want an average wind speed of at least 4-5 meters per second (m/s) at hub height. A good site might have 6-8 m/s. You measure wind speed with an anemometer and direction with a wind vane. These are usually mounted on a tower or a simple pole at the same height as the turbine hub. For a small turbine, that could be 10-20 meters high. You also need a data logger to record the readings every 10 minutes or so. Inexpensive options include the Davis Instruments Vantage Pro2 or a homemade Arduino-based system using a cup anemometer. If you cannot afford a tower, you can start with a handheld anemometer and take readings at different times of day, but this is less accurate. For a proper assessment, you need at least 12 months of data to capture seasonal changes. In many African countries, the windiest months are during the dry season, while the rainy season may have calm periods. Record both speed and direction at the same time so you can later make a wind rose. Also note the maximum gust speeds, as turbines must survive extreme winds. Simple tools like a windsock can give a rough idea of direction and speed, but they are not precise. Always calibrate your instruments and check for obstacles like trees or buildings that can distort the wind. If you are near the coast, salt spray can damage sensors, so choose marine-grade equipment.

2. Wind Rose Diagrams and Site Analysis

A wind rose is a circular diagram that shows how often the wind blows from each direction and how strong it is. It is one of the most useful tools for wind site assessment. To make a wind rose, you need at least a few months of wind direction and speed data. You can use free software like WRPLOT View from Lakes Environmental, or even a spreadsheet. The wind rose has spokes for each of the 16 compass directions (N, NNE, NE, etc.). The length of each spoke shows the percentage of time the wind comes from that direction. Colors on the spoke show wind speed ranges. For example, a long spoke to the east means the wind often comes from the east. In many parts of Africa, the prevailing wind is from the east or northeast (trade winds), but coastal areas may have sea breezes that change direction between day and night. When you analyze a site, you want the wind rose to show a dominant direction that is consistent. If the wind comes from all directions equally, the site is poor for a wind turbine because the turbine cannot always face the wind. You also want to avoid placing a turbine in a valley or behind a hill that blocks the prevailing wind. Use the wind rose to orient your turbine: most small turbines are upwind, meaning they face into the wind. If the wind rose shows a strong prevailing direction, you can point the turbine that way. Also, check for turbulence: if the wind rose shows many different directions, the site may be turbulent, which reduces energy production and stresses the turbine. In Africa, you can get wind rose data from global databases like the Global Wind Atlas (globalwindatlas.info), which provides free maps and wind roses for any location. Use that as a starting point, but always verify with on-site measurements.

3. Anemometers and Wind Vanes

Anemometers measure wind speed, and wind vanes measure wind direction. There are several types, but for small wind projects in Africa, cup anemometers and propeller anemometers are most common. Cup anemometers have three or four cups that spin with the wind; the faster they spin, the higher the wind speed. They are rugged and relatively cheap. A good entry-level cup anemometer is the NRG Systems #40, but it costs a few hundred dollars. A cheaper option is the Inspeed Vortex or a homemade one using a small DC motor and cups. Propeller anemometers have a propeller that points into the wind and a tail vane to keep it facing the wind. They can measure both speed and direction in one unit, like the Davis 6410. Ultrasonic anemometers have no moving parts and are very accurate but expensive. Wind vanes are simple: a flat fin that points downwind, with a counterweight. They are often combined with anemometers on the same mount. When installing, place the anemometer at the top of a tower or pole, at least 10 meters above the ground and away from obstacles. The standard height for wind measurement is 10 meters, but for a small turbine you might measure at hub height. You also need to record data. A simple data logger like the HOBO or a Raspberry Pi can store readings. In Africa, power supply can be an issue, so choose a logger with low power consumption or solar power. Calibration is important: check your anemometer against a known reference every year. Also, ensure the anemometer is level and the vane is balanced. If you are using a handheld anemometer, take readings at the same spot and height each time. Remember that wind speed increases with height, so measuring at 2 meters will give much lower readings than at 10 meters. Always note the height of your measurements.

4. Estimating Annual Energy Production

Estimating annual energy production (AEP) tells you how much electricity your wind turbine will generate in a year. This is crucial for deciding if a project is worth it. The basic formula is: AEP = 0.5 air density swept area wind speed cubed efficiency hours in a year. But wind speed varies, so you need a wind speed distribution. The most common is the Weibull distribution, which uses two parameters: k (shape) and c (scale). You can calculate these from your wind data using free software like WindRose or a spreadsheet. For a small turbine, a simpler method is to use the average wind speed and a capacity factor. Capacity factor is the actual energy produced divided by the energy if the turbine ran at full power all the time. For small wind turbines, capacity factors are typically 0.15 to 0.30 (15-30%). So if you have a 1 kW turbine and the average wind speed is 5 m/s, you might get 1 kW 0.25 8760 hours = 2190 kWh per year. But that is rough. Better: use the power curve of your turbine. The power curve shows the output at each wind speed. You multiply the hours the wind blows at each speed by the power output at that speed, then sum up. You can get wind speed data in bins (e.g., hours per year at 0-1 m/s, 1-2 m/s, etc.) from your measurements or from the Global Wind Atlas. In Africa, air density is lower at high altitudes (e.g., Addis Ababa at 2350 m has about 20% lower air density than sea level), so you must adjust. Air density = pressure / (287.05 temperature in Kelvin). Use average temperature and pressure for your site. Also, account for losses: downtime, soiling, and electrical losses can reduce output by 10-20%. So multiply your gross AEP by 0.8 to get net AEP. Many online calculators, like the one from the Danish Wind Industry Association, can help. Always be conservative: it is better to underestimate than to overpromise.

5. Wind Shear and Turbulence Intensity

Wind shear is how wind speed changes with height. It is important because you measure wind at one height (say 10 m) but your turbine hub might be at 20 m. Wind shear is described by the shear exponent (alpha). A common value is 0.14 for open land, but it can be 0.2 to 0.4 in areas with trees or buildings. You calculate alpha using the formula: alpha = log(V2/V1) / log(H2/H1), where V1 and V2 are wind speeds at heights H1 and H2. For example, if wind at 10 m is 5 m/s and at 20 m is 5.8 m/s, alpha = log(5.8/5) / log(20/10) = 0.21. To get accurate AEP, you need to extrapolate your measured wind speed to hub height using this alpha. In Africa, wind shear can be high in savanna areas with scattered trees, but low over smooth desert or water. If you cannot measure at two heights, use a default alpha of 0.14 for open terrain, but be aware this adds uncertainty. Turbulence intensity (TI) is the standard deviation of wind speed divided by the mean wind speed, usually expressed as a percentage. For example, if mean wind speed is 6 m/s and standard deviation is 1.2 m/s, TI = 20%. Low TI (below 10%) is good; high TI (above 20%) is bad because it causes fatigue loads and reduces turbine life. Turbulence is caused by obstacles, rough terrain, and thermal effects. In cities or near trees, TI can be very high. For small wind turbines, you want a site with TI less than 15% at hub height. You can measure TI with a fast-response anemometer (like a sonic or a 3-cup with high sampling rate) and a data logger that records at least 1 Hz. If you cannot measure TI, avoid sites with many obstacles. Also, note that turbulence increases with wind speed up to a point, then decreases. For wind resource assessment, always report both wind shear and turbulence, as they affect both energy production and turbine survival.

6. Using Wind Atlases and Software Tools

Before you spend money on measuring equipment, use free wind atlases to get a first idea of the wind resource in your area. The Global Wind Atlas (globalwindatlas.info) is the best starting point. It covers the whole world, including Africa, with a resolution of about 1 km. You can zoom into your site, see the average wind speed at 10 m, 50 m, 100 m, and 200 m, and download wind roses and data. It also shows capacity factors for different turbine types. Another useful tool is the DTU Wind Energy Global Wind Atlas, which is the same. For Africa, the African Wind Atlas (africanwindatlas.org) might have more local data, but it is not always updated. Also, the SWERA (Solar and Wind Energy Resource Assessment) project has maps for many African countries. These atlases are based on computer models, not actual measurements, so they can be off by 10-20%. Use them to identify promising areas, then verify with on-site measurements. For software, you can use free tools like WAsP (Wind Atlas Analysis and Application Program) for small projects, but it is complex. A simpler tool is RETScreen (from Natural Resources Canada), which has a wind energy module. You input your site coordinates, and it pulls data from NASA and other sources to estimate energy production. It also does financial analysis. Another free tool is HOMER (Hybrid Optimization of Multiple Energy Resources) for off-grid systems. For small wind turbines, you can use the free online calculator from the Danish Wind Industry Association (windpower.org) or the American Wind Energy Association (AWEA) small wind calculator. If you have measured data, you can use Excel or Google Sheets to make your own analysis. There are also mobile apps like Windfinder and Windy for real-time wind forecasts, but these are not for resource assessment. Always remember that atlases and software are just estimates. The only way to know for sure is to measure at your site for at least a year. But these tools can save you time and money by helping you avoid obviously bad sites.

<h2>Practical Reference: Wind Resource Assessment for African Projects</h2>

<p>Wind resource assessment is the single most important investment decision in any wind energy project. For African developers, the challenge is sharper: measurement campaigns cost money that must be justified against uncertain grid access, limited meteorological infrastructure, and financing that demands bankable data. This lesson gives you the reference material formulas, thresholds, sources, and checklists that you will use repeatedly when scoping, commissioning, or reviewing a wind measurement campaign.</p>

<p>Treat this as your working desk reference. The numbers and standards below are drawn from international best practice and adapted for the African context, where terrain complexity, data scarcity, and dust loading all change how you apply them.</p>

<h3>1. What You Are Actually Measuring</h3>

<p>Wind resource assessment produces four outputs that financiers and turbine suppliers need:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Wind speed frequency distribution</strong> usually modelled as Weibull with shape parameter <em>k</em> and scale parameter <em>A</em>.</li>
<li><strong>Wind direction distribution</strong> summarised in a wind rose (see the module lesson on Wind Rose Diagrams).</li>
<li><strong>Vertical wind shear profile</strong> described by the power law exponent or the logarithmic law with roughness length z.</li>
<li><strong>Turbulence intensity (TI)</strong> the ratio of the standard deviation of wind speed to the mean wind speed, critical for turbine class selection.</li>
</ul>

<p>The power law relationship is the workhorse formula:</p>

<p style="font-family: monospace; background:#f4f4f4; padding:8px;">V / V = (z / z)<sup></sup></p>

<p>For most African sites, a default of 0.143 (the 1/7 power law) is used when no measurement data exists. But this default is dangerous. Coastal sites in Namibia or Somalia can show below 0.10, while bushland or urban-fringe sites in Kenya or Nigeria can exceed 0.30. Getting wrong by 0.05 can change annual energy production (AEP) estimates by 510%.</p>

<h3>3. Safety Warning</h3>

<div style="border:2px solid #c0392b; background:#fdecea; padding:12px; margin:16px 0;">
<strong style="color:#c0392b;"> RED SAFETY WARNING Met Mast Installation and Maintenance</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Met mast erection and any work at height above 3 metres must follow a documented fall-protection plan. In many African jurisdictions, enforcement of working-at-height regulations is weak, and developers have been fined or shut down after incidents. Before any mast work: (1) verify the mast is de-energised and locked out; (2) use a full-body harness with double lanyard anchored to a certified fall-arrest point; (3) never work alone minimum two-person crew with radio contact; (4) check lightning risk daily, especially in East and Central Africa where afternoon storms are common; (5) secure all tools and sensors with lanyards to prevent dropped objects. A single dropped anemometer from 60 m is lethal. Budget for certified rope-access technicians it is cheaper than a fatality investigation.</p>
</div>

<h3>4. Interpreting the Numbers for African Sites</h3>

<p>When you receive a wind resource report, apply these business-first tests before accepting the AEP figure:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Measurement duration:</strong> At least 12 months of valid data, with &gt;90% data recovery. Less than this and you are guessing.</li>
<li><strong>MCP correlation:</strong> Measurecorrelatepredict against a long-term reference (reanalysis or nearby airport). Without MCP, you cannot claim a bankable P50.</li>
<li><strong>Shear check:</strong> If &gt; 0.25 or &lt; 0.10, investigate. It may indicate sensor error, terrain effects, or a genuinely unusual site.</li>
<li><strong>Turbulence check:</strong> Compare measured TI at 15 m/s against the turbine class you intend to buy. If TI exceeds the class limit, you need a higher-class turbine or a lower hub height.</li>
<li><strong>Loss assumptions:</strong> Apply at least 1015% total losses (availability, electrical, wake, curtailment, icing/dust). African dust and grid instability often push losses higher than European defaults.</li>
</ul>

<h3>5. Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ol>
<li><strong>Define the question first.</strong> Are you screening (reanalysis is fine) or seeking finance (12+ months of on-site measurement required)?</li>
<li><strong>Check data recovery.</strong> Reject any campaign below 90% valid data it cannot support a bankable P50.</li>
<li><strong>Verify sensor calibration.</strong> All anemometers must have a valid calibration certificate traceable to a recognised standard (e.g., MEASNET).</li>
<li><strong>Compute shear from at least two heights.</strong> Never extrapolate from a single measurement height.</li>
<li><strong>Compare TI to IEC class.</strong> If measured TI exceeds the class limit, change turbine class or hub height.</li>
<li><strong>Run MCP.</strong> Correlate your short-term data with at least 10 years of reference data before claiming long-term AEP.</li>
<li><strong>Apply realistic losses.</strong> Use 1020% total losses for African sites, not the 58% often quoted from European experience.</li>
<li><strong>Document everything.</strong> Financiers will audit your raw data, calibration certificates, and MCP methodology. If it is not written down, it did not happen.</li>
</ol>
