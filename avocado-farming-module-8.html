1. Common Insect Pests (e.g., Mites, Thrips)

Avocado trees in Africa are attacked by several insect pests that reduce yield and fruit quality. The most common are mites, thrips, and fruit flies. Mites are tiny (0.2â0.5 mm) and feed on leaves, causing yellow stippling and leaf drop. In dry conditions, populations explode. Check weekly by tapping a branch over a white sheet of paper; if you see 5â10 mites per leaf, treat. Thrips are slender, 1â2 mm long, and feed on flowers and young fruit, causing scarring and fruit drop. Use yellow sticky traps to monitor: hang 5 traps per acre. If you catch more than 10 thrips per trap per week, spray with neem oil (5 ml per litre of water) or insecticidal soap. Fruit flies lay eggs in ripening fruit, leading to maggots and rot. Use protein bait traps (e.g., GF-120) or hang methyl eugenol traps at 4 per acre. For all pests, encourage natural enemies like ladybirds and predatory mites by planting flowering plants such as sunflower or marigold between rows. Avoid broad-spectrum pesticides that kill beneficial insects. Always scout early morning or late evening when pests are active. In Africa, the avocado lace bug also causes leaf bronzing; control by pruning infested shoots and applying horticultural oil. Regular monitoring and early action are key to keeping these pests below economic thresholds.

2. Major Diseases (e.g., Phytophthora Root Rot, Anthracnose)

Phytophthora root rot is the most destructive avocado disease in Africa. It is caused by a soil-borne fungus that attacks roots, causing wilting, yellow leaves, and dieback. The tree may die within 2â3 years. Prevent it by planting on raised mounds (30â40 cm high) and using resistant rootstocks like 'Dusa' or 'Bounty'. Ensure good drainage; avoid overwatering. If symptoms appear, drench the soil with potassium phosphonate (e.g., 2 ml per litre of water) around the drip line every 3 months. Anthracnose is a fungal disease that causes dark, sunken spots on fruit, flowers, and leaves, especially during wet weather. It leads to post-harvest rot. Control by pruning for air circulation, removing infected debris, and spraying copper hydroxide (2 g per litre) at flowering and again 2 weeks later. Cercospora spot causes small, dark spots on leaves and fruit; use copper-based fungicides. Powdery mildew appears as white powder on leaves; spray with potassium bicarbonate (5 g per litre) or sulphur. In Africa, bacterial canker (caused by Xanthomonas) is emerging; avoid overhead irrigation and disinfect pruning tools with 10% bleach. Always buy certified disease-free seedlings. Rotate crops if intercropping. For all diseases, sanitation is critical: remove and burn infected plant parts. Keep records of outbreaks to adjust your management plan each season.

3. Integrated Pest Management (IPM) Strategies

Integrated Pest Management (IPM) combines cultural, biological, and chemical methods to control pests and diseases with minimal harm to people and the environment. Start with prevention: choose resistant varieties, plant on raised beds, and maintain proper spacing (7â8 m between trees) for airflow. Prune regularly to remove dead wood and open the canopy. Monitor your orchard weekly using a scouting plan: walk in a zigzag pattern, inspect 10 trees per acre, and record pest numbers. Set action thresholds: for example, treat mites only when 10% of leaves have mites. Use biological controls first: release predatory mites (e.g., Phytoseiulus persimilis) for spider mites, or encourage lacewings by planting dill and fennel. For thrips, introduce predatory mites like Amblyseius swirskii. If chemicals are needed, choose selective pesticides like neem oil, spinosad, or insecticidal soap. Apply them in the evening to protect bees. Rotate chemicals with different modes of action to prevent resistance. For diseases, use cultural practices like mulching, proper irrigation (drip, not overhead), and crop rotation. Sanitize tools between trees. Keep a farm diary of all actions and results. IPM reduces costs, protects health, and sustains yields. In Africa, many farmers have successfully used IPM to cut pesticide use by 50% while increasing marketable fruit. Train your workers on scouting and safe handling. Review your IPM plan every season.

4. Biological Control Methods

Biological control uses living organisms to suppress pests. It is safe, sustainable, and cheap once established. For avocado pests in Africa, several natural enemies are effective. Predatory mites (e.g., Phytoseiulus persimilis) eat spider mites. You can buy them from commercial insectaries or conserve existing populations by avoiding broad-spectrum pesticides. Ladybirds and lacewing larvae feed on thrips and aphids. Plant border crops like sunflower, cowpea, and marigold to attract these beneficial insects. Parasitic wasps (e.g., Encarsia formosa) control whiteflies. For fruit flies, release sterile males or use parasitoids like Fopius arisanus. For diseases, antagonistic fungi such as Trichoderma harzianum can be applied to soil to compete with Phytophthora root rot. Mix 5 g of Trichoderma powder per litre of water and drench around the root zone every 3 months. Bacillus subtilis can be sprayed on leaves to prevent anthracnose. To establish biological control, reduce pesticide use, provide habitat (hedgerows, cover crops), and introduce beneficials early in the season. Monitor pest and beneficial populations with sticky traps and visual counts. If you must spray, use selective products like neem or Bt (Bacillus thuringiensis) that spare natural enemies. Biological control may take a few seasons to work, but it builds a self-regulating ecosystem. Many African farmers have reduced costs by 30% using these methods. Always source beneficial insects from reputable suppliers and follow release instructions.

5. Safe Use of Pesticides

Pesticides can be dangerous if not used correctly. Always read the label before opening a container. Wear protective clothing: long sleeves, trousers, rubber boots, gloves, goggles, and a respirator mask. Never eat, drink, or smoke while handling pesticides. Mix chemicals in a well-ventilated area, away from children and animals. Use the correct dose: for example, for copper hydroxide, mix 2 g per litre of water. Overdosing wastes money and harms the environment. Apply pesticides only when pests reach the action threshold and weather is calm (wind speed below 10 km/h). Spray in the early morning or late evening to avoid bee activity. Never spray before rain; check the forecast. Calibrate your sprayer: measure output per minute and adjust walking speed. Keep a dedicated sprayer for herbicides only; never use it for insecticides. After spraying, wash your hands and face with soap, and change clothes. Store pesticides in a locked cabinet, away from food and feed. Triple-rinse empty containers, puncture them, and dispose of them in a designated pit. Never reuse pesticide containers. If someone shows signs of poisoning (headache, dizziness, vomiting), call a doctor immediately and show the label. Keep a first aid kit with activated charcoal. In Africa, many farmers have been harmed by improper use; follow these rules to protect yourself, your workers, and your family. Consider organic options like neem oil, which is safer but still requires care.

6. Monitoring and Scouting Techniques

Monitoring and scouting are the backbone of pest and disease management. Scout your avocado orchard at least once a week. Walk in a zigzag or W pattern, inspecting 10 trees per acre. For each tree, check 5 random branches: look at leaves (top and bottom), flowers, and fruit. Record what you see: pest type, number, and damage. Use a hand lens (10x) to identify mites and thrips. For mites, tap a branch over white paper; count mites that fall. For thrips, use yellow sticky traps: hang 5 per acre at canopy height, and count weekly. For fruit flies, use protein bait traps or methyl eugenol traps, 4 per acre. For diseases, look for symptoms: yellowing leaves (root rot), dark spots (anthracnose), or white powder (powdery mildew). Keep a notebook or use a mobile app like 'Plantix' to log data. Set action thresholds: e.g., treat mites when 10% of leaves have mites; treat thrips when 10 thrips per trap per week. Also monitor beneficial insects: if you see ladybirds or predatory mites, delay spraying. After rain, scout more often for fungal diseases. Train your workers on scouting; give them a simple form to fill. Review records monthly to spot trends. Early detection saves money and prevents outbreaks. In Africa, farmers who scout regularly report 20â30% higher marketable yields. Make scouting a fixed weekly task, like watering. Use the data to decide when to spray, prune, or release biologicals.

Practical Reference: Pest and Disease Management in African Avocado Farming

Avocado is one of Africa's fastest-growing export crops. Kenya, Tanzania, South Africa, Ethiopia, and Rwanda are all expanding production to serve European, Middle Eastern, and Asian markets. But pests and diseases routinely destroy 20â40% of potential yield on African farms â and for export-oriented growers, a single rejected container can wipe out a season's profit. This practical reference gives you the working knowledge to protect both your trees and your margins.

This module assumes you are managing avocado as a business, not a hobby. Every recommendation here is designed to protect yield, meet market access requirements (especially GlobalG.A.P. and EU MRL standards), and reduce long-term input costs through Integrated Pest Management (IPM).

Common Insect Pests

African avocado orchards face a recurring pest complex. Mites (Oligonychus punicae and Eriophyes spp.) cause bronzing and leaf drop, especially in hot, dusty conditions common in Kenya's Makueni and Tanzania's Mbeya regions. Thrips (Scirtothrips perseae and Heliothrips haemorrhoidalis) scar fruit skin, triggering downgrades at packhouses. False codling moth (Thaumatotibia leucotreta) is a major phytosanitary barrier for EU exports â a single larva found in a consignment can close a market.

Other significant pests include fruit flies (Ceratitis spp.), mealybugs, and the avocado lace bug. For business planning, assume pest pressure peaks during the warm dry season and immediately after fruit set.

Major Diseases

Phytophthora root rot (Phytophthora cinnamomi) is the single most destructive avocado disease worldwide, and it thrives in poorly drained African soils â a common problem on smallholder plots. Trees wilt, leaves yellow, and roots turn black. Once established, it is nearly impossible to eradicate; prevention through drainage and resistant rootstocks (e.g., Dusa, Duke 7) is the only reliable business strategy.

Anthracnose (Colletotrichum gloeosporioides) causes post-harvest black lesions that destroy fruit value during shipping. It is especially severe in humid highland zones like Ethiopia's Jimma and Kenya's Kisii. Cercospora spot, scab, and stem-end rot also reduce marketable yield.

Integrated Pest Management (IPM) Strategies

IPM is not a philosophy â it is a cost-control system. The core principle: intervene only when economic damage is likely, and use the cheapest effective method first. For an African avocado business, IPM protects margins in three ways: it reduces chemical spend, delays resistance, and keeps export certification intact.

Step 1 â Prevention (cultural): Plant certified clean seedlings. Space trees for airflow. Manage irrigation to avoid waterlogging. Remove and destroy fallen fruit weekly â this alone breaks the false codling moth and fruit fly cycle.

Step 2 â Monitoring: Scout weekly. Use pheromone traps for moths, yellow sticky traps for thrips, and protein baits for fruit flies. Record counts. This data is your decision trigger.

Step 3 â Biological and mechanical: Conserve natural enemies. Release predators where justified. Use orchard sanitation, pruning, and physical barriers.

Step 4 â Chemical (last resort): Choose the least toxic product that works. Rotate active ingredients. Respect pre-harvest intervals (PHI) and maximum residue limits (MRLs) required by your buyer.

Biological Control Methods

Africa has strong biological control assets. Cryptolaemus montrouzieri (mealybug destroyer) is commercially available in Kenya and South Africa. Predatory mites (Phytoseiulus persimilis, Neoseiulus californicus) control spider mites. Orius bugs suppress thrips. Trichogramma wasps parasitise moth eggs.

For false codling moth, the Sterile Insect Technique (SIT) is now deployed commercially in South Africa and pilot-tested in Kenya through ICIPE. For fruit flies, the Diachasmimorpha longicaudata parasitoid is used in integrated programmes. Biologicals are cheaper long-term but require patience â they work over weeks, not hours.

Safe Use of Pesticides

Pesticides are the highest-risk input on your farm â for your workers, your family, your export licence, and your soil. In Africa, misuse is common because of weak enforcement and informal supply chains. Do not be part of the problem.

Monitoring and Scouting Techniques

Scouting is the intelligence system of your farm. Without it, you spray blind â wasting money and risking residues. A practical scouting protocol for African avocado orchards:

  • Walk a W-pattern through each block, inspecting 20 trees per hectare.
  • Check 4 zones per tree: new shoots, fruit clusters, underside of leaves, and trunk base.
  • Record on a simple form (or mobile app like Plantix or FAO's Fall Armyworm app adapted for avocado).
  • Use traps: 1 pheromone trap per 2 ha for moths; 4 yellow sticky traps per ha for thrips; 2 fruit fly traps per ha.
  • Sample fruit at 2-week intervals from fruit set to harvest â cut open 10 fruits per block to check for internal larvae.
  • Review weekly with your team. Compare counts to thresholds in Table 1.
  • Keep records for 2 years â required for GlobalG.A.P. and most export buyers.

  1. Scout every 7 days using the W-pattern; record counts on a standard form.
  2. Check pheromone and sticky traps weekly; replace lures per manufacturer schedule.
  3. Remove and destroy fallen fruit at least twice weekly.
  4. Verify soil drainage before planting; use resistant rootstocks in high-risk blocks.
  5. Confirm every pesticide is registered and approved by your buyer before purchase.
  6. Wear full PPE and observe PHI and re-entry intervals without exception.
  7. Conserve beneficial insects â avoid broad-spectrum sprays during flowering.
  8. Keep spray, scouting, and harvest records for at least 2 years for certification audits.
