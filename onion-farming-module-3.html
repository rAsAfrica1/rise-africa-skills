1. Soil Requirements for Onions

Onions grow best in loose, well-drained soil that is rich in organic matter. The ideal soil is sandy loam or loam, which allows the bulbs to expand easily and prevents waterlogging. Avoid heavy clay soils because they hold water and can cause bulb rot. If your soil is clay, you must improve it by adding compost, sand, or planting on raised beds. Onions are shallow-rooted, so they need soil that is at least 30 cm deep and free of rocks and hard clods. Before planting, test your soil drainage: dig a hole 30 cm deep, fill it with water, and see how long it takes to drain. If water remains after 2 hours, drainage is poor. In that case, you must plant on raised beds or install drainage channels. Onions also prefer a soil pH between 6.0 and 6.8. If your soil is too acidic (below 5.5), add agricultural lime at 2-4 tonnes per hectare. If too alkaline (above 7.5), add gypsum or elemental sulphur. In many parts of Africa, soils are sandy and low in nutrients. You can still grow onions by adding plenty of compost and fertilizing regularly. Always choose a field that has not grown onions, garlic, or leeks for at least 2-3 years to avoid soil-borne diseases like white rot. Good soil preparation is the foundation of a healthy onion crop.

2. Soil Testing and pH Adjustment

Before you plant onions, you must know your soil's pH and nutrient levels. You can buy a simple soil test kit from an agricultural supply store or send a sample to a local extension office. To take a sample, use a clean spade to dig 15-20 cm deep in at least 10 spots across your field. Mix the soil in a bucket, then take about 500g for testing. If you cannot afford a lab test, you can use a DIY pH test kit with colour indicators. Onions grow best at pH 6.0-6.8. If your soil is too acidic (pH below 6.0), apply agricultural lime. The amount depends on your soil type: for sandy soil, apply 2 tonnes per hectare; for loam, 3 tonnes; for clay, 4 tonnes. Spread lime evenly and mix it into the top 15 cm of soil at least one month before planting. If your soil is too alkaline (pH above 7.5), add gypsum at 2 tonnes per hectare or elemental sulphur at 500 kg per hectare. You can also add organic matter like compost or manure, which helps buffer pH. Remember that onions are sensitive to high acidity, which can cause poor growth and small bulbs. Adjusting pH takes time, so test early and correct before planting. In Africa, many soils are acidic due to heavy rainfall and erosion. Regular testing every 2-3 years will help you maintain the right pH for good yields.

3. Organic Matter and Compost Incorporation

Organic matter is the key to healthy onion soil. It improves soil structure, holds water, and provides nutrients. The best organic matter for onions is well-rotted compost or manure. Fresh manure can burn plants and introduce weeds, so always compost it for at least 3 months. To make compost, pile layers of dry leaves, grass, kitchen scraps, and animal manure. Turn the pile every 2 weeks and keep it moist. In 2-3 months, it will turn dark and crumbly. Apply compost at 10-20 tonnes per hectare (about 1-2 kg per square metre). Spread it evenly over the soil and mix it into the top 15-20 cm with a hoe or fork. If you don't have compost, you can use well-rotted cow, goat, or poultry manure. Poultry manure is rich in nitrogen, so use it at 5 tonnes per hectare. In sandy soils, organic matter helps retain water and nutrients; in clay soils, it improves drainage. You can also grow green manure crops like cowpeas or mucuna, then plough them into the soil before planting onions. This adds nitrogen and organic matter. Remember that onions have shallow roots, so they benefit from a loose, crumbly soil. Incorporate organic matter at least 2-3 weeks before planting to allow it to settle. In Africa, where synthetic fertilizers may be expensive, compost is a cheap and effective way to boost soil fertility and get bigger onion bulbs.

4. Fertilizer Recommendations: N-P-K

Onions need balanced fertilizers, but they are heavy feeders of nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K). A common recommendation is to apply a basal fertilizer at planting, such as NPK 10-20-10 or 12-24-12, at 300-400 kg per hectare. This provides phosphorus for root development and potassium for bulb formation. Nitrogen is needed for leaf growth, but too much nitrogen late in the season can cause thick necks and poor storage. Apply half of the nitrogen at planting and the other half as a side-dress 4-6 weeks later. Use ammonium sulphate (21% N) or urea (46% N) for side-dressing. For example, apply 100 kg of ammonium sulphate per hectare at side-dressing. Potassium is crucial for bulb size and quality; if your soil is low in potassium, apply muriate of potash (0-0-60) at 100-150 kg per hectare. In sandy soils, split applications are better because nutrients leach away. You can also use organic fertilizers like poultry manure or compost tea. A simple rule: for every 10 tonnes of compost, you get about 50 kg N, 20 kg P, and 50 kg K. Always base your fertilizer application on soil test results. Over-fertilizing can burn plants and pollute water. Under-fertilizing leads to small bulbs. In Africa, where fertilizers may be costly, combine small amounts of synthetic fertilizer with compost for best results. Apply fertilizers when the soil is moist, and water after application to dissolve nutrients.

5. Micronutrient Management

Onions need small amounts of micronutrients like zinc, boron, manganese, copper, and iron. These are needed in tiny quantities but are essential for healthy growth. Deficiencies can cause poor bulb formation, yellowing leaves, and reduced yields. In many African soils, zinc and boron are the most common deficiencies. Zinc deficiency shows as yellowing between the veins of younger leaves and stunted growth. To correct, apply zinc sulphate at 20-30 kg per hectare before planting, or spray a 0.5% zinc sulphate solution on leaves. Boron deficiency causes hollow stems, thick necks, and brown spots on bulbs. Apply borax at 10-15 kg per hectare, but be careful: too much boron is toxic. Manganese deficiency causes grey speck on leaves; spray with manganese sulphate at 0.5%. Copper deficiency leads to pale, twisted leaves; apply copper sulphate at 10 kg per hectare. Iron deficiency shows as yellow leaves with green veins; spray with chelated iron. The best way to manage micronutrients is through organic matter. Compost and manure contain a wide range of micronutrients. Regular addition of compost reduces the need for synthetic micronutrients. If you suspect a deficiency, send leaf samples to a lab for testing. In Africa, where lab services may be limited, you can use visual symptoms as a guide. Always follow recommended rates; excess micronutrients can harm plants and soil. Foliar sprays are quick fixes, but soil application with organic matter is a long-term solution.

6. Raised Bed and Flatbed Preparation

Onions can be grown on flatbeds or raised beds, depending on your soil and climate. Flatbeds are common in dry areas with good drainage. To prepare a flatbed, plough or dig the soil to a depth of 20-30 cm, remove rocks and weeds, and level the surface. Make beds 1-1.2 metres wide so you can reach the centre from both sides. Leave paths of 30-40 cm between beds. Raised beds are better in wet areas or where drainage is poor. They are also easier to manage and reduce soil compaction. To make a raised bed, dig soil from the paths and pile it onto the bed to a height of 15-30 cm. Mix in compost and fertilizer. The bed should be 1-1.2 metres wide and any length. Raised beds warm up faster in cool weather and drain excess water. In Africa, raised beds are especially useful during the rainy season. For either bed type, break up clods to a fine tilth so onion seeds or seedlings can emerge easily. Onions are often transplanted, so prepare the bed 2-3 weeks before transplanting. Apply a pre-planting fertilizer and mix it into the top 10 cm. Water the beds thoroughly a day before planting. If you use drip irrigation, install lines along the beds. Mulching with straw or grass after planting helps conserve moisture and suppress weeds. Good bed preparation saves labour and leads to uniform onion stands.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Soil Preparation and Fertility for Onion Farming in Africa

Onion (Allium cepa L.) is one of Africa's most commercially reliable horticultural crops, with strong demand across urban markets in Kenya, Nigeria, Tanzania, Ethiopia, Senegal, and South Africa. But onion is also one of the least forgiving crops when soil conditions are wrong. Unlike maize or cassava, onions cannot compensate for poor soil through vigorous vegetative growth. Bulb size, uniformity, storage life, and market grade are all set in the first 6–8 weeks after transplanting — and they are set by soil conditions.

This Practical Reference consolidates the six lessons in this module into a field-ready guide. It assumes you are farming for market: you want uniform Grade 1 bulbs, low cull rates, and a crop that stores. Everything below is written with that commercial objective in mind.

1. Soil Requirements: What Onions Actually Need

Onions perform best on well-drained sandy loams, loams, or silt loams with a pH of 6.0–6.8. They are extremely sensitive to soil compaction, waterlogging, and salinity. A compacted pan at 15–20 cm will produce flattened, split, or double bulbs regardless of how much fertilizer you apply.

Key physical criteria:

  • Depth: Minimum 30 cm of friable soil; 45 cm preferred for long-day varieties.
  • Drainage: Infiltration rate above 15 mm/hour. Onions die in standing water within 48 hours.
  • Texture: Sandy loam to silt loam. Heavy clay requires raised beds and organic amendment.
  • Salinity: ECe below 1.2 dS/m. Onion yield drops sharply above 1.2 dS/m.
  • Previous crop: Avoid fields that grew onions, garlic, leeks, or shallots in the last 3 years (white rot, nematodes).

2. Soil Testing and pH Adjustment

Do not guess. A basic soil test through a national agricultural research institute or a private lab costs the equivalent of USD 15–40 and will save you far more than that in wasted fertilizer. Test before land preparation, at least 6–8 weeks before transplanting, so lime has time to react.

Onion is sensitive to acidity. Below pH 5.8, phosphorus locks up, molybdenum becomes unavailable, and aluminium toxicity stunts roots. Above pH 7.5, micronutrients (especially iron, manganese, zinc, boron) become unavailable and bulb quality suffers.

3. Organic Matter and Compost Incorporation

Organic matter is the single highest-return investment in onion soil preparation. It improves structure, water-holding capacity, cation exchange capacity, and microbial activity. For onions, target 2–4% organic matter in the top 20 cm.

Well-decomposed cattle or poultry manure at 10–20 t/ha, or quality compost at 15–25 t/ha, should be incorporated 3–4 weeks before transplanting. Never use fresh manure — it burns roots, introduces weed seeds, and can carry E. coli and Salmonella, which will fail your produce at supermarket or export gate.

In West Africa, where soils are often sandy and low in organic carbon, split applications (half before planting, half at 4 weeks) work better than a single dose. In East Africa's highland areas, incorporate compost in the dry season so it breaks down before the rains.

4. Fertilizer Recommendations: N-P-K

Onion has a shallow, inefficient root system. It needs steady, moderate nutrition — not heavy doses. Split applications are essential; a single large dose of nitrogen will produce thick necks, delayed maturity, and poor storage.

Stop all nitrogen 3–4 weeks before harvest. Late N is the leading cause of thick-necked, non-storing bulbs — the most common cause of rejected consignments in African onion supply chains.

5. Micronutrient Management

Onion responds strongly to boron, zinc, manganese, and copper. Deficiencies are common in sandy, leached, or high-pH soils.

  • Boron: 1–2 kg/ha borax in basal. Deficiency causes hollow stem and split bulbs. Do not exceed 3 kg/ha — boron toxicity is worse than deficiency.
  • Zinc: 10–15 kg/ha zinc sulphate, or foliar 0.5% ZnSO₄ at 4 and 7 weeks.
  • Manganese: Foliar 0.5% MnSO₄ on high-pH soils.
  • Copper: Foliar 0.2% CuSO₄ where deficiency is confirmed.

6. Raised Bed vs Flatbed Preparation

Choose your bed type based on drainage, rainfall, and irrigation method — not tradition.

  • Raised beds (15–25 cm high, 90–120 cm wide): Essential in high-rainfall areas (East African highlands, coastal West Africa), on heavy clay, and where drip irrigation is used. Better drainage, warmer soil, easier harvest, higher marketable yield.
  • Flatbeds: Suitable in dry, well-drained sandy soils with furrow irrigation. Cheaper to establish but risky in intense rainfall.

Regardless of bed type, prepare soil 25–30 cm deep, break clods, and level the surface. Onion seed and transplants need a fine, firm tilth — not powder, not lumps. Firm the bed with a light roller before transplanting.

⚠ SAFETY WARNING — Fertilizer, Lime, and Manure Handling

  • Always wear gloves, eye protection, and a dust mask when handling lime, ammonium nitrate, urea, or boron products. Lime causes severe eye and skin burns.
  • Never mix calcium ammonium nitrate (CAN) or ammonium nitrate with fuels, oil, or organic matter — risk of fire and explosion. Store separately in a dry, ventilated place.
  • Do not apply fresh poultry or cattle manure to onion beds. It carries Salmonella, E. coli, and parasite eggs, and will fail produce safety audits.
  • Boron is toxic at low overdose. Measure precisely — do not "estimate by eye."
  • Keep all agrochemicals and fertilizers out of reach of children and away from water sources.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Test soil 6–8 weeks before planting — pH, organic matter, P, K, and EC.
  2. Correct pH to 6.0–6.8 with lime (acid) or elemental S (alkaline); allow time to react.
  3. Apply 15–25 t/ha well-decomposed compost or manure and incorporate to 20 cm.
  4. Deep-rip or plough to 25–30 cm to break compaction pans.
  5. Form beds suited to your drainage — raised in wet areas, flat in dry sandy soils.
  6. Apply basal N-P-K (30-60-40 kg/ha) at transplanting, placed 5 cm from roots.
  7. Split top-dress N into two applications at 3–4 and 6–7 weeks; stop N 3–4 weeks before harvest.
  8. Include boron and zinc in the programme; monitor for deficiency symptoms weekly.
