1. Determining Harvest Maturity Indices

Harvesting at the right time is the difference between fruit that sells and fruit that spoils. For mango, the most reliable index is the number of days from flowering: 90100 days for early varieties like Tommy Atkins, 110120 days for Kent and Keitt. Also check shoulder shape: mature mangoes have a full, rounded shoulder, not a pointed tip. For pineapple, tap the fruita dull, solid sound means mature; a hollow sound means immature. The eyes should be flat and the base color should change from green to yellow. For papaya, look at the skin: 1/4 yellow is ideal for transport, 1/2 yellow for local market. For banana, count days from shooting: 8090 days for most varieties, and check the fingersthey should be full, not angular. For avocado, maturity is not about softness; it's about oil content. A mature Hass avocado has 810% oil. You can test by picking one fruit, letting it ripen at room temperature, and tastingif it's creamy, the tree is ready. For citrus like oranges, use a refractometer to check Brix (sugar content). Oranges should have at least 10% Brix. For guava, the fruit changes from dark green to light green and develops a sweet aroma. Always test a few fruits before harvesting the whole tree. In Africa, where temperatures are high, fruits mature faster. So check every 23 days. Never harvest after rainfruit is turgid and bruises easily. Early morning is best. Use a refractometer (cost about $20) and a firmness tester (penetrometer) for accurate readings. If you don't have tools, use your senses: color, smell, and ease of detachment. For mango, mature fruit snaps off easily with a short stem. For pineapple, the fruit detaches with a gentle twist when ripe. Remember, maturity for harvest is not the same as ripeness for eating. Most fruits are harvested mature but unripe, then ripened later.

2. Harvesting Tools and Techniques

Using the right tools prevents damage and keeps fruit fresh longer. For tall trees like mango and avocado, use a harvesting pole with a clipper head and a catching bag. A good pole extends 35 meters and costs about $3050. The clipper cuts the stem, and the bag catches the fruit gently. For shorter trees, use a sharp pair of pruning shears or a curved harvesting knife. Never pull fruit off the treethat tears the skin and opens the door to rot. For pineapple, wear thick gloves and twist the fruit sharply. For banana, use a machete or sharp knife to cut the bunch, leaving a 1520 cm stalk. For papaya, use a sharp knife and leave a 23 cm stem. For citrus, use a clipper that cuts flush, leaving a small button. Always harvest in the cool of the dayearly morning or late afternoon. Never harvest during the heat of the day (11am3pm) because fruit is hot and respires faster. Use clean tools: dip them in a 10% bleach solution (1 part bleach to 9 parts water) between trees to prevent spreading disease. Wear gloves to protect your hands and the fruit. For ground fruits like pineapple and melon, bend at the knees, not the waist, to avoid back injury. Place fruit gently into a clean, lined harvest bin. Never throw or drop fruit. A drop from 30 cm can cause internal bruising that shows up days later. For mango, leave a 510 mm stem to prevent sap burn. For avocado, leave a 5 mm stem. For banana, handle the bunch by the stalk, not the fingers. After harvest, move fruit to shade immediately. Field heat is the enemy. In Africa, where sun is intense, a simple shade cloth or tree shade can reduce temperature by 510C. If you harvest 100 kg of mango, you can lose 10 kg to bruising if you're careless. So invest in good tools and train your workers. A sharp knife is safer than a dull one because it requires less force.

3. Post-Harvest Physiology and Ripening

After harvest, fruits are still alive. They breathe (respire), lose water, and ripen. Understanding this helps you control the process. Fruits are either climacteric or non-climacteric. Climacteric fruitsbanana, mango, papaya, avocado, guavacontinue to ripen after harvest. They produce ethylene gas, which triggers ripening. Non-climacteric fruitspineapple, citrus, melondo not ripen further after harvest; they are as ripe as they will ever be. So harvest them at full maturity. For climacteric fruits, you can speed up ripening by exposing them to ethylene gas. A simple method: put fruits in a closed container with a ripe banana or apple (which release ethylene). At 2025C, mangoes ripen in 35 days. At 15C, it takes 710 days. For commercial ripening, use a ripening chamber with ethylene gas at 100 ppm for 2448 hours. But for small-scale farmers, a plastic tent or a room with a heater works. Never use calcium carbideit's dangerous and illegal in many countries. Instead, use ripe fruit or ethylene gas from a cylinder. Respiration rate increases with temperature. At 25C, mangoes respire 3 times faster than at 10C. So cooling after harvest is critical. For every 10C reduction, shelf life doubles. For example, mango stored at 25C lasts 5 days; at 10C, it lasts 1014 days. But be careful: tropical fruits are chilling-sensitive. Mango, banana, and papaya should not be stored below 1012C. Pineapple can go to 7C. Avocado to 4C. If you store them too cold, they get chilling injury: black spots, failure to ripen, off-flavors. Also, fruits lose water. A 5% weight loss makes them look shriveled. So keep humidity high (8595% RH) for most tropical fruits. Use perforated plastic bags or humidifiers. Ethylene can also be a problem: it speeds ripening of sensitive fruits like lettuce and cucumber. So store ethylene-producing fruits (banana, mango) away from ethylene-sensitive ones. For ripening, keep fruits in a single layer, not piled, to allow air circulation. Turn them gently every day. When they reach the desired ripeness, cool them to slow down further ripening.

4. Cleaning, Grading, and Packing

Cleaning, grading, and packing add value and reduce losses. After harvest, clean fruits gently. For mango, wipe with a soft cloth to remove sap and dirt. Never wash with water unless you dry them thoroughlymoisture encourages rot. For pineapple, brush off soil and remove any dried leaves. For banana, remove any damaged fingers. For citrus, wash with clean water and a soft brush, then dry in shade. Grading means sorting by size, color, and quality. Use simple tools: a sizing ring or a caliper. For mango, grade into three sizes: small (200300g), medium (300500g), large (500800g). For export, only perfect fruit with no blemishes. For local market, grade 1 (good), grade 2 (slight defects), grade 3 (processing). Remove any bruised, diseased, or overripe fruit immediatelyone rotten fruit can spoil the whole box. Packing protects fruit during transport. Use clean, new boxes. For mango, use a single layer in a shallow box with paper lining. For banana, pack in a ventilated box, 1015 kg per box. For pineapple, pack upright in a box with a cushion. For papaya, wrap each fruit in newspaper or tissue. Never pack more than 20 kg per box to avoid crushing. Use cushioning materials like shredded paper, banana leaves, or foam. In Africa, many farmers use wooden crates or plastic crates. Plastic crates are better because they are easy to clean and ventilate. But they are expensive. A cheaper option is a cardboard box with holes for ventilation. Label each box with the fruit type, grade, weight, and harvest date. Keep records. For export, you need to follow standards: GlobalG.A.P. or organic certification. For local markets, simple grading is enough. After packing, store in a cool, shaded place. Never leave boxes in the sun. If you pack 100 boxes, you can sell them for 2030% more than ungraded fruit. So grading is worth the effort. Also, use clean gloves when handling fruit after cleaning to avoid recontamination.

5. Storage and Transport Conditions

Proper storage and transport keep fruit fresh from farm to market. The key factors are temperature, humidity, and ventilation. For most tropical fruits, the ideal storage temperature is 1015C. But each fruit has its own range: mango 1012C, banana 1315C, papaya 1012C, pineapple 710C, avocado 47C, citrus 510C. If you don't have a cold room, use evaporative cooling. A simple charcoal cooler: a double-walled brick structure with wet sand between the walls. It can reduce temperature by 510C. Or use a shaded, well-ventilated shed. Humidity should be 8595% for most fruits. You can increase humidity by placing wet sacks on the floor or using a humidifier. But avoid free water on the fruit. Ventilation is important to remove ethylene and heat. Use fans or open vents. For transport, use refrigerated trucks if possible. But in Africa, many farmers use open trucks. If so, cover the load with a white tarpaulin to reflect sun, but leave sides open for air. Never transport in a closed, hot truck. For short distances (under 2 hours), harvest early morning and transport immediately. For longer distances, use refrigerated trucks or trains. For export, use refrigerated containers (reefers) set at the correct temperature. During transport, avoid mixing fruits with strong-smelling goods like onions or diesel. Also, don't stack boxes too highmaximum 5 layers for cardboard boxes. Use pallets to keep boxes off the floor. For storage, keep fruits in a single layer on shelves. Check daily for signs of rot. Remove any spoiled fruit immediately. For banana, store in a ventilated room at 1315C. For mango, store at 1012C for up to 2 weeks. For pineapple, store at 710C for 23 weeks. For papaya, 1012C for 12 weeks. Remember, storage life depends on maturity at harvest. The more mature the fruit, the shorter the storage life. So harvest at the right stage. Also, keep records of temperature and humidity. A simple thermometer and hygrometer cost about $10 each. Use them. If you see condensation on the fruit, reduce humidity. If fruit looks shriveled, increase humidity.

6. Practical: Harvesting and Packing Fruits

This practical exercise will guide you through harvesting and packing 20 mangoes and 10 pineapples. You will need: a harvesting pole with clipper, a sharp knife, pruning shears, clean gloves, a 10% bleach solution, clean cloths, a sizing ring, cardboard boxes, shredded paper, and a thermometer. Step 1: Prepare tools. Dip all cutting tools in bleach solution for 1 minute. Wear gloves. Step 2: Harvest mangoes. Look for mature fruit: full shoulders, color change from green to light green or yellow, and a snap when you lift the fruit. Use the pole to cut the stem, leaving 5 mm. Catch the fruit gently. Place in a lined bin. Do not drop. Step 3: Harvest pineapples. Choose fruit with a dull sound when tapped and 1/4 yellow at the base. Wear thick gloves. Twist the fruit sharply. Place in a bin. Step 4: Clean. Wipe mangoes with a soft cloth to remove sap. Brush soil off pineapples. Step 5: Grade. Use a sizing ring for mangoes: small (200300g), medium (300500g), large (500800g). For pineapples, grade by weight: small (0.81.2 kg), medium (1.21.8 kg), large (1.82.5 kg). Remove any bruised or diseased fruit. Step 6: Pack. For mangoes, line a cardboard box with shredded paper. Place mangoes in a single layer, stem down. Do not pack more than 12 mangoes per box. For pineapples, pack upright in a box with a cushion. Do not pack more than 6 pineapples per box. Step 7: Label. Write the fruit type, grade, weight, and date on each box. Step 8: Store. Keep boxes in a shaded, ventilated place. Check temperature: it should be 1015C. If not, move to a cooler spot. Step 9: Record. Note the number of fruits harvested, grades, and any losses. Step 10: Evaluate. After 3 days, check for spoilage. If you followed the steps, spoilage should be less than 5%. If higher, identify where you went wrong. This practical teaches you that careful handling and grading can reduce losses and increase income. Repeat this exercise for other fruits like papaya and banana.

7. Practical Reference

<section>
<h2>Practical Reference: Harvesting and Packing Fruits</h2>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the field and packhouse steps that determine whether a tropical fruit crop reaches market in premium condition. For African agribusinesses whether supplying local supermarkets, regional exporters, or airfreight buyers in Europe and the Gulf the margin between profit and loss is decided in the hours between detachment and dispatch. Fruit that is harvested at the right maturity, handled with clean and appropriate tools, cooled quickly, graded consistently, and packed to withstand the journey will earn repeat orders. Fruit that is bruised, over-ripe, or inconsistently graded will be discounted, rejected, or claimed against.</p>

<p>Use this lesson as a bench-side and field-side reference. It assumes you have completed the earlier lessons on maturity indices, harvesting tools, post-harvest physiology, cleaning/grading/packing, and storage/transport. Here we bring them together into a single operating procedure you can adapt for mango, avocado, papaya, pineapple, banana, passion fruit, or citrus. Always confirm specific maturity standards, maximum residue limits (MRLs), and phytosanitary requirements with your buyer and your national plant protection organisation before harvest.</p>

<h3>Step 1: Pre-Harvest Readiness Check</h3>
<p>Before a single fruit is cut, confirm four things: (1) the block is at the correct maturity index for the intended market for example, mango for airfreight export is typically harvested at 810% dry matter or specific gravity 1.011.02, while local market mango may be left to colour-break; (2) the previous spray has passed its pre-harvest interval (PHI); (3) harvest labour, crates, and transport are scheduled for the coolest part of the day; and (4) the packhouse is clean, cooled, and staffed. A missed PHI is a food-safety and trade failure, not a minor inconvenience.</p>

<h3>Step 2: Harvesting Technique</h3>
<p>Harvest in the early morning. Use sharp, clean secateurs or pole harvesters. Leave a short peduncle (25 mm) on mango and avocado to reduce latex or sap bleed onto the fruit surface, but trim it flush enough that it does not puncture neighbouring fruit in the crate. Never drop fruit into a bucket or bin. Use a picking bag with a soft base, or lower fruit by hand into a padded crate. For pineapple and banana, cut the stalk with a curved knife and carry bunches on the shoulder or on a padded trolley never drag them.</p>

<p>Field crates should be shallow ( 20 kg) and ventilated. Do not use sacks, which crush and heat fruit. Keep harvested fruit in shade immediately; a simple shade net or tree canopy can reduce pulp temperature by several degrees before the fruit even reaches the packhouse.</p>

<h3>Step 3: Receiving, Cleaning, and Grading</h3>
<p>At the packhouse, record the field lot, harvest time, and pulp temperature of each crate. Pre-cool as quickly as possible forced-air cooling is the most practical option for most African packhouses. Clean fruit with potable water and a food-grade sanitiser (e.g., 100150 ppm free chlorine for many fruits, but check crop-specific tolerance). Do not soak fruit; dip or spray briefly, then drain. Grade on a clean, padded table under good light. Grade by size, colour, shape, and freedom from defects. Remove and separately record all rejects this data drives field management decisions next season.</p>

<h3>Step 4: Packing</h3>
<p>Pack only sound, dry, correctly graded fruit. Use clean, ventilated cartons sized to the buyers specification. Line with food-grade liners where required (e.g., for avocado and mango to reduce water loss). Count accurately. Label with product, variety, grade, count, net weight, lot number, and country of origin. Stack cartons on pallets to allow airflow; do not over-stack. Keep packed fruit in the cold room until loading.</p>

<p>For transport, pre-cool the reefer or insulated truck before loading. Set the thermostat to the target pulp temperature, not the air temperature alone. Load pallets to allow air circulation around the load. Record the temperature at loading and at arrival; a simple data logger pays for itself in one disputed consignment. For domestic African markets where cold chain is unreliable, harvest at a slightly less advanced maturity and sell quickly freshness and appearance drive repeat sales.</p>

<h3>Step 6: Record Keeping and Traceability</h3>
<p>Every consignment should carry a lot number that links back to the field block, harvest date, harvest team, pack date, and packhouse operator. This is now a baseline requirement for most export buyers and is increasingly required for supermarket supply in Africa. A simple paper log or a spreadsheet is enough to start; the discipline of recording is what matters. Traceability protects your business when a buyer raises a quality claim you can identify the cause and fix it rather than losing the account.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol style="line-height:1.7;">
<li><strong>Maturity confirmed:</strong> Block sampled and tested against the buyers maturity index; PHI respected.</li>
<li><strong>Tools ready:</strong> Sharp, clean secateurs/knives; padded crates 20 kg; shade available; first-aid kit on site.</li>
<li><strong>Harvest timing:</strong> Early morning; fruit kept in shade; field-to-cool time 2 hours.</li>
<li><strong>Cooling:</strong> Pre-cool to crop-specific target (see Table 1); record pulp temperature at receiving.</li>
<li><strong>Cleaning and grading:</strong> Potable water and correct sanitiser dose; grade under good light; rejects recorded separately.</li>
<li><strong>Packing:</strong> Clean, ventilated, correctly sized cartons; accurate count and label; pallets not over-stacked.</li>
<li><strong>Storage and transport:</strong> Correct set point and RH; reefer pre-cooled; temperature logged from packhouse to arrival.</li>
<li><strong>Traceability:</strong> Lot number links field, harvest date, pack date, and operator; records retained for at least one season.</li>
</ol>
