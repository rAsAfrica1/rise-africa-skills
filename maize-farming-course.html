Maize Farming

A complete 6-month season program built around one mission: closing the gap from 1 tonne to 4+ tonnes per hectare — population, feeding, the eight-week weed war, fall armyworm scouting, and selling on the price curve.

⏱️ 6 Months (one season)📊 Beginner → Business-ready🌍 Hybrid: Online + Offline🎓 Certificate on completion

Course Overview

Why 6 months? Because that is one maize season: land preparation before the rains, planting with the first effective rains, the weeding-and-feeding battle of the first eight weeks, the pest watch through grain fill, and harvest at black layer. This course walks the season with you — and its whole mission is one number: taking you from the smallholder average of under 1 tonne/ha toward the 4–8+ tonnes/ha the same land can produce with correct plant population, feeding and weed control.

Structure: 6 monthly modules → lessons → practical assignments → a 10-question quiz each month → final business project.

How to study (hybrid): Online — modules on your phone, sessions in the Study Diary, questions to the rAs AI assistant. Offline — save this page (Print → Save as PDF); the practicals happen in the field with a hoe and a string line.

⚠️ Read first: the three rules that protect your investment

  • The first 6–8 weeks decide the yield. A maize crop weedy or hungry in its first two months has already lost half its harvest — no late rescue exists. Plan labour and fertiliser for THAT window before you plant a single seed.
  • Fall armyworm is beaten early or not at all. Scout from week one; once larvae are deep in the funnel, sprays barely reach them. Month 5 is your battle manual.
  • Mouldy maize can carry aflatoxin — an invisible poison. Never eat or sell visibly moulded grain; dry to 12.5% and store clean (see the rAs Grain Storage course). Handle pesticides with gloves, label-doses and pre-harvest intervals, always.
MONTH 1 — Planning, Varieties & the Yield Gap ▾

Lesson 1.1 — The yield gap is the business plan

  • African smallholder average: 0.8–1.5 t/ha. Research stations and good commercial farmers on the SAME rainfall: 5–10 t/ha. The gap is not the weather — it is plant population, feeding, weeds and timing, all of which this course controls.
  • The economics of the gap: costs per hectare are broadly similar whether you harvest 1 t or 4 t — the extra tonnes are nearly pure profit. Yield IS the margin.

Lesson 1.2 — Choosing the variety (match maturity to rainfall)

Maturity classDays to maturityWhere it wins
Ultra-early / early90–115Short/erratic rain areas — escapes drought, lower ceiling yield
Medium115–135The all-rounder for most regions
Late135–160+Long reliable rains/irrigation — highest yield potential
  • Hybrids out-yield open-pollinated varieties (OPVs) by 20–50% but seed must be BOUGHT FRESH every season — replanted hybrid grain collapses in yield and uniformity.
  • OPVs yield less but seed can be saved 2–3 seasons — the low-cash option done knowingly.
  • Also match: drought-tolerant lines for dry zones, streak-virus-resistant where the disease bites, and the GRAIN the market wants (white for meal, yellow for feed).

Lesson 1.3 — The season budget and input list

  • Per hectare shopping list (conventional): 25 kg seed, 150–300 kg basal compound fertiliser, 150–250 kg nitrogen top dressing (AN/urea), pest scout kit, and the labour plan for weeks 1–8.
  • Certified seed from sealed, dated bags at registered dealers — fake seed is a whole-season theft; check the certification tag.
  • Conservation-agriculture route (basins + mulch + manure — Lesson 2.3) substitutes labour and organic matter for part of the cash — proven for low-capital farmers.

Lesson 1.4 — Field maths every grower must own

  • Target plant population: 37,000–53,000 plants/ha rainfed (higher end with good rain/fertility; ~44,000 is the classic 90×25 cm; 75×25 cm ≈ 53,000 for well-fed fields).
  • One plant = one cob (usually): population × cob weight IS your yield. 44,000 plants × 150 g cobs ≈ 6.6 t/ha ceiling; gaps and runts eat it from there.
  • Seed rate ~25 kg/ha at 1–2 seeds/station covers the population plus gap-filling.

(2) Count plants in 3 rows of a neighbour's last crop and compute their real population vs 44,000. (3) Write the full per-hectare input budget with 3 dealer quotes.

MONTH 2 — Land Preparation & Soil Fertility ▾

Lesson 2.1 — Know your soil first

  • Maize wants pH 5.5–7.0. Below 5.5 (very common on old African fields), phosphorus locks up and aluminium poisons roots — lime at 500–2,000 kg/ha (soil-test guided; extension offices test cheaply) is often the single highest-return input a farmer ever buys, lasting 3–5 seasons.
  • The finger test: sandy soils (drain fast, feed hungry — split fertiliser more), clays (hold water and food, drain slowly), loams (the prize).
  • Organic matter is the bank account: every season's manure, compost and residues raise water-holding and feeding power — burning residues is burning next year's yield.

Lesson 2.2 — Conventional preparation

  • Plough/dig 15–20 cm after early rains soften ground (or dry-plough with draft power), then harrow to a medium tilth — cloddy beds lose seeds, powder-fine beds cap and crust.
  • Mark rows with a string line and pegs at your chosen spacing — straight, correctly spaced rows are what make the 44,000-plant target real instead of theoretical.
  • On slopes: rows on the contour, and grass strips/bunds every 10–20 m — one storm on a downhill-ploughed slope removes topsoil that took centuries to build.

Lesson 2.3 — Conservation agriculture (the low-cash high-work option)

  • Planting basins: 15×15×15 cm holes at row spacing, dug in the DRY season — each catches rain and holds a handful of manure + a capful of basal fertiliser exactly where the plant needs it.
  • Mulch the field with residues (soil covered = weeds suppressed, moisture kept, temperature buffered) and never burn.
  • Documented results across Southern/Eastern Africa: basin systems commonly double smallholder yields in drier years because every basin is a mini water harvest.

Lesson 2.4 — Basal feeding: the foundation meal

  • Basal compound (e.g. NPK types sold locally for maize) at 150–300 kg/ha placed IN the row/station at planting — banded placement beats broadcasting by putting food at the roots, not the weeds.
  • Manure/compost 5–10 t/ha where available replaces part of the compound and feeds the soil itself — the combination beats either alone.
  • Never let seed touch fertiliser directly (it burns germination): fertiliser in the station, a little soil over it, then the seed.

MONTH 3 — Planting: the Highest-Stakes Week ▾

Lesson 3.1 — When to plant

  • Plant with the first EFFECTIVE rains: ~20–30 mm that wet the soil to hoe depth, with the season genuinely started (your extension office and elders both know the false-start showers).
  • Every week of late planting after the optimum window costs roughly 5–10% of yield in most rainfed systems — early (but not false-start) planting is free yield.
  • Dry planting just before reliable rains works for experienced growers with certain seasons — beginners wait for the real rain.

Lesson 3.2 — How to plant

  • Spacing: your Month-1 choice (90×25 cm classic; 75×25 cm intensive). One seed per station at high seed quality, two where germination is doubtful (thin later to one).
  • Depth 3–5 cm — into moisture, firmed over. Too shallow dries out; deeper than ~7 cm exhausts the seedling before it surfaces.
  • Basal fertiliser in the station first (soil pinch, then seed) or banded beside the row.
  • Where seed wasn't dressed: dealer-bought seed dressing protects against soil pests and birds pulling seedlings.

Lesson 3.3 — Germination and gap filling

  • Emergence in 5–10 days. Walk the rows at day 10: count 20-station samples — below 90% stand, GAP-FILL immediately with soaked seed (later gap fills become runts that steal water and give nothing).
  • Common stand-killers to diagnose: crusting after hard rain (break gently), cutworms (seedlings felled at night — soil-level collar damage), birds (pulled seedlings with seed gone), planting too deep/dry.

Lesson 3.4 — Intercropping done right

  • Maize + beans/cowpeas in the row middles: legumes fix nitrogen, cover soil against weeds, and pay a second harvest from the same land — plant legumes 1–2 weeks after maize so they don't compete early.
  • Maize + pumpkins: living mulch that smothers weeds between rows.
  • Keep maize population at target — intercrops ADD to the plan, never replace maize stations.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 3): (1) Plant your plot to the string line at your spacing, fertiliser-soil-seed order, logging the date and rain that triggered it. (2) Day-10 stand count on 20-station samples; compute % and gap-fill same day. (3) Plant your legume intercrop 1–2 weeks behind the maize.

MONTH 4 — Weeding & Top Dressing: the Yield Window ▾

Lesson 4.1 — The critical weed-free period

  • Maize must be CLEAN from emergence to ~week 8 — weeds in that window steal the water, nitrogen and light that set the cob size, and no later weeding refunds it. Yield losses from early weeds run 30–60%.
  • Weeding calendar that wins: first weeding week 2–3, second week 5–6, third only if needed pre-tasseling. Hoe shallow near rows (deep hoeing cuts maize's shallow feeder roots).
  • Mulch and intercrops (Months 2–3) are weedings you did in advance; herbicide options (e.g. pre-emergence products) exist for larger areas — label rates, sprayer calibration and extension advice required.

Lesson 4.2 — Striga: the witchweed exception

  • The pretty pink/purple flowering weed attached to maize roots is striga — it parasitises the crop underground long before flowering, and hand-weeding at flowering is too late for this season (still pull and BURN it before seeding: one plant sheds 50,000+ seeds that last years).
  • Beat it over seasons: rotation/intercrop with cowpea or groundnut (their roots trigger suicide germination), heavy manuring (striga loves poor soils), and striga-tolerant/"push-pull" systems where extension supports them.

Lesson 4.3 — Top dressing: feeding the cob

  • Nitrogen top dressing (AN or urea) 150–250 kg/ha, SPLIT: half at knee height (4–6 weeks), half at ~tasseling — this feeds cob formation and grain fill exactly when demand peaks.
  • Placement: a bottle-cap dose 5–10 cm from each plant, covered lightly; ideally onto moist soil before rain. Urea left on a dry surface loses much of its nitrogen to the air.
  • Read the crop: pale yellow-green leaves and a firing (yellowing) V up the lower leaf midrib = nitrogen hunger; purple seedlings = phosphorus (a basal/pH problem to fix next season).

Lesson 4.4 — Moisture management mid-season

  • The unforgivable stress window: tasseling/silking. Drought in those 2–3 weeks aborts pollination and can halve yield — everything (mulch, basins, weed control) exists to bank water for that fortnight.
  • Signs of stress to log: leaf rolling by mid-morning (rolling at midday is normal); use logs to pick better varieties/dates next season.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 4): (1) Execute weedings 1 and 2 on calendar; photograph rows before/after. (2) Apply split top dressing with the bottle-cap method and log dates/rain. (3) Scout for striga weekly; pull-and-burn any flowering plants and mark the patches for next season's rotation.

MONTH 5 — Pests & Diseases: the Scout's War ▾

Lesson 5.1 — Fall armyworm (FAW): the front line

  • Recognise it: ragged "windowpane" leaf feeding, wet sawdust-like frass in the funnel, and the caterpillar's inverted "Y" on the head with four square dots on the rear segment.
  • Scout from week 1: walk a W-pattern, check 10 plants at 5 stops (50 plants) twice weekly. Action threshold: ~20% of young plants showing fresh damage.
  • Hit larvae EARLY (small, exposed) — late larvae hide deep in the funnel where sprays can't reach. Direct spot treatment into funnels beats blanket spraying: registered products per label, OR proven low-cost options (soil/ash pinch into funnels kills many larvae; neem solutions; hand-crushing on small plots at dawn).
  • Nature fights with you: birds, ants and wasps eat FAW — which is one more reason against blanket overspraying.

Lesson 5.2 — The rest of the pest gallery

PestSignsResponse
Stalk borersPinholes in whorl leaves, "dead heart" centre, tunnelled stemsEarly planting, funnel treatment at first pinholes, destroy stalk residues after harvest (they overwinter there)
CutwormsSeedlings cut at soil level overnightEarly weeding (removes egg sites), dig around felled plants at dawn and kill; seed dressing helps

Lesson 5.3 — Diseases

  • Maize streak virus: yellow-white streaks along young leaves, stunted plants — carried by leafhoppers; answer is resistant varieties and early planting (no in-season cure; remove badly stunted plants).
  • Grey leaf spot & rusts: rectangular grey lesions / orange pustules on leaves in humid seasons — resistant varieties, rotation and residue management; fungicides only pay on high-yield commercial stands.
  • Cob rots (the aflatoxin gateway): favoured by late harvest, insect-damaged cobs and drought stress — harvest on time, dry fast, and NEVER mix mouldy cobs into food or sale grain.

Lesson 5.4 — Safe chemical use (when you do spray)

  • Registered products for the named pest only, at label dose — half doses breed resistant pests, double doses poison you and the field.
  • Full cover: gloves, long sleeves, no eating/smoking, wash after; spray in still evening air; keep children and livestock out per label re-entry times.
  • Respect PRE-HARVEST INTERVALS on the label — days that must pass between last spray and harvest; triple-rinse and puncture empty containers, never reuse them for food or water.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 5): (1) Run the W-pattern FAW scout twice weekly for a month; chart % damaged plants against the 20% threshold. (2) Treat funnels on one affected block (ash/soil or labelled product) and compare damage a week later vs untreated. (3) Rogue out streak-stunted plants and log their positions.

MONTH 6 — Harvest, Storage, Marketing & Your Business Plan ▾

Lesson 6.1 — Harvest at the right moment

  • Physiological maturity: husks dry and brown, and the black layer — a dark spot at each kernel's base where it joins the cob (twist a kernel out and look). Grain is then ~30–35% moisture.
  • Field-dry standing or harvest and dry on racks/tarpaulins to the storage target: 12.5% moisture (the trader's meter, or the salt-jar and rattle tests from the rAs Grain Storage course).
  • Every week cobs stand over-ripe in the field is a week of borer, termite, rodent and rot losses — harvest promptly once dry enough to handle.

Lesson 6.2 — Shelling, storage and the aflatoxin line

  • Shell clean (hand, sheller ring, or machine), winnow, and SORT: mouldy, discoloured and insect-holed grain OUT — fed to nothing that lays eggs or gives milk for you, and never sold into food.
  • Store at 12.5% in clean containers: hermetic bags/sealed drums beat open sacks decisively (see the rAs Grain Storage course for the full system — this lesson is its doorway).
  • Reserve and label your household food stock FIRST, then seed money stock, then the trading stock.

Lesson 6.3 — Selling: the calendar is the price

  • Maize prices follow a yearly curve: LOWEST at harvest glut, typically rising 30–80% into the lean months before the next harvest. Stored, safe grain is a savings account with interest — if storage is genuinely sound.
  • Channels: grain traders at the gate (fast, lowest), grain marketing boards/depots where they operate (posted prices, quality standards), millers (volume contracts), and retailing by the bucket in lean months (highest per-kg of all).
  • Sell by the KG on a scale — bucket and bag sales without weights systematically favour the buyer.
