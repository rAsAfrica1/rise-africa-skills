1. Identifying Major Pig Breeds

In Africa, pig farming relies on a mix of local and exotic breeds. The most common exotic breeds are Large White, Landrace, Duroc, Hampshire, and Pietrain. Large White (also called Yorkshire) is white with erect ears, known for large litters (10-12 piglets) and good mothering. Landrace is white with droopy ears and a long body, producing up to 14 piglets per litter but sensitive to stress. Duroc is red or brown with droopy ears, fast-growing and hardy, often used as a terminal sire. Hampshire is black with a white belt, lean and muscular. Pietrain is white with black spots, extremely muscular but stress-prone and poor mothering. Local African breeds like the Ashanti Black in Ghana or the Mukono in Uganda are smaller, slower-growing but resistant to diseases and heat. To identify a breed, look at ear shape (erect vs droopy), coat colour, body length, and litter size records. For example, if you see a white sow with droopy ears and a long body, it's likely Landrace. A red boar with droopy ears is Duroc. Keep a notebook to record each pig's breed, ear tag number, and performance. In markets, ask the seller about the breed and check teeth for age (piglets have small teeth, adults have worn teeth). Crossbreeds are common; they may show mixed colours and ear shapes. Start with at least two breeds to compare. For small farms, Large White x Landrace crosses are popular for good litter size and growth. Always buy from a reputable breeder who keeps records. Avoid buying pigs with unknown backgrounds, as they may carry diseases or poor genetics. In hot climates, breeds with dark coats like Duroc tolerate sun better, but they need shade and water. Local breeds are best for low-input systems; exotic breeds need balanced feed and vet care. Your choice depends on your market: if you sell to hotels, lean meat from Pietrain crosses is preferred; for local markets, hardy crossbreds work well.

2. Comparing Maternal and Terminal Breeds

Maternal breeds are chosen for their ability to produce and raise many piglets. They have good mothering instincts, large litters, and enough milk. Examples: Large White and Landrace. A good maternal sow weans 10-12 piglets per litter, with low piglet mortality. Terminal breeds are chosen for fast growth, feed efficiency, and meat quality. They are used as sires in crossbreeding to produce market pigs. Examples: Duroc, Hampshire, Pietrain. A terminal boar passes on rapid growth and lean meat to his offspring. In a commercial operation, you keep maternal line sows (e.g., Large White x Landrace) and mate them with a terminal boar (e.g., Duroc) to get piglets that grow fast and have good carcass quality. This is called a terminal cross. Never keep offspring from terminal crosses for breeding, because they may have poor mothering and inconsistent traits. Maternal breeds often have more backfat and are less muscular, while terminal breeds are leaner and more muscular. For example, a Pietrain boar will give piglets with high lean percentage but may cause stress-related deaths during transport. In Africa, where feed costs are high, using a terminal sire like Duroc improves feed conversion ratio (FCR) from 3.5 to 3.0, meaning less feed per kg gain. However, terminal breeds may need better housing and health care. Maternal breeds are more tolerant of poor management. If you are a smallholder with limited resources, start with a maternal breed like Large White and use a local boar for hardiness. If you supply to a premium market, invest in a terminal boar. Record performance: weigh piglets at birth, weaning (28 days), and 70 days. Compare litters from different sows. A sow that weans 10 piglets at 7 kg each is better than one weaning 8 at 6 kg. Keep sows that consistently perform. Replace sows after 6-8 litters. For boars, check libido and semen quality. Use a terminal boar for no more than 2 years. In hot climates, terminal breeds like Duroc handle heat better than Pietrain. Always match breed choice to your market and resources.

3. Selecting Breeding Stock Based on Performance

Selecting breeding stock based on performance means choosing pigs that have proven they can produce well. You need records. Start with a simple notebook or a smartphone app like PigChamp. Record for each sow: ear tag number, breed, date of birth, number of piglets born alive, number weaned, and weaning weight. For boars: growth rate, feed efficiency, and libido. Key performance indicators (KPIs) for sows: litter size (target 10-12 born alive), weaning weight (target 6-8 kg at 28 days), and farrowing interval (target 150-160 days). For boars: average daily gain (target 700-900 g/day from 30 to 90 kg), feed conversion ratio (target 2.8-3.2), and backfat thickness (target 12-15 mm at 90 kg). To measure backfat, use a backfat scanner or ultrasound; if not available, condition score by hand: ribs easily felt but not visible is ideal. Select replacement gilts from sows that consistently wean 10+ piglets. Gilts should reach puberty by 6-7 months and weigh 100-110 kg at first mating. Avoid gilts from litters with high mortality or slow growth. For boars, choose from litters with high growth and low backfat. Check testicles: two large, firm testicles of equal size. Check legs and feet for soundness. In Africa, where data may be scarce, use visual appraisal: a good gilt has a long body, 12-14 teats (spaced evenly), and a strong back. A good boar has a wide chest, strong legs, and active libido. Buy from breeders who provide performance records. If no records, ask to see parents and siblings. Select at 5-6 months of age. Never select pigs that have been sick or treated frequently. Keep replacement rate at 20-25% per year. Cull sows with poor mothering, small litters, or lameness. Cull boars with low libido or poor semen. Remember: performance is influenced by environment and management. A pig with good genetics can still perform poorly if fed poorly. So, provide balanced feed (16-18% protein for growers) and clean water. Weigh pigs monthly to track growth. Use a scale or weigh tape. If a pig is not meeting targets, check feed, health, and housing before blaming genetics.

4. Evaluating Conformation and Soundness

Conformation refers to the shape and structure of a pig's body; soundness means the pig can move and stand without pain. Good conformation and soundness are critical for breeding stock because they affect longevity, fertility, and ease of management. Start with legs and feet. A sound pig has straight, strong legs with even toes. Check for swollen joints, cracked hooves, or splayed legs. In Africa, where pigs often walk on hard, uneven ground, leg problems are common. A boar with weak legs cannot mount a sow; a sow with bad legs cannot farrow comfortably. Next, look at the back. A good breeding pig has a long, wide, and slightly arched back. A dipped or roached back indicates weakness. The body should be deep, with well-sprung ribs (ribs that curve outward) to allow room for the heart and lungs. For sows, a good underline means at least 12 evenly spaced, prominent teats. Inverted or blind teats are disqualifying because piglets cannot suckle. Check the vulva: it should be well-developed and not infantile. For boars, check testicles: two large, firm, symmetrical testicles in a well-descended scrotum. The sheath should be small and free of discharge. Evaluate the head: a good pig has a broad snout and bright eyes. Avoid pigs with runny noses, coughing, or skin lesions. When evaluating a pig, have it walk on a flat surface. Watch for limping, stiffness, or crossing over. Score each trait from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent). Only select pigs with scores of 4 or 5 in legs, back, and underlines. In hot climates, pigs with dark coats and loose skin tolerate heat better. Also check teeth: a pig with overshot or undershot jaw may have trouble eating. For breeding, avoid closely related pigs (inbreeding) to prevent poor conformation. If you are buying at a market, ask the seller to walk the pig. A pig that refuses to walk may have foot problems. Always quarantine new pigs for 30 days and observe for soundness. Remember, conformation is partly genetic and partly environmental. Provide good nutrition and soft bedding to prevent leg injuries.

5. Understanding Genetics and Heritability

Genetics is the study of how traits are passed from parents to offspring. In pig farming, understanding genetics helps you improve your herd. Every pig has genes, which come in pairs: one from the sire, one from the dam. Some genes are dominant (they show up even if only one copy is present), others are recessive (they show up only if both copies are the same). For example, the gene for white coat colour is dominant over black. Heritability is a measure of how much of a trait's variation is due to genetics versus environment. It ranges from 0 to 1. A high heritability (above 0.4) means the trait is mostly genetic and can be improved by selection. Examples: backfat thickness (0.5), growth rate (0.3), litter size (0.1). Litter size has low heritability, so selecting for it is slow; better to improve management (feed, health). To improve a trait, you need to select pigs with superior genes and cull those with poor genes. But you must also provide good environment. For instance, even a pig with high genetic potential for growth will not grow well if underfed. In Africa, many farmers use local breeds that have low growth but high disease resistance. Crossbreeding with exotic breeds can combine traits. However, crossbreeding does not change heritability; it just creates new gene combinations. To make genetic progress, keep records and use estimated breeding values (EBVs). EBVs predict the genetic merit of a pig for a trait. For example, an EBV for growth of +50 g/day means the pig's offspring will grow 50 g/day faster than average. Without EBVs, use performance records of the pig and its relatives. Select boars from litters with high growth and low backfat. Select gilts from sows with large litters. Avoid inbreeding: mating closely related pigs (e.g., father-daughter) increases the chance of recessive defects like hermaphrodism or cleft palate. Keep a herd book to track pedigrees. Rotate boars every 2 years. If you have a small herd, bring in a new boar from a different farm every 2-3 years. Remember, genetics is a long-term investment. It takes 3-5 generations to see major changes. Start with the best stock you can afford and keep records.

6. Crossbreeding Strategies for Commercial Production

Crossbreeding is mating pigs of different breeds to produce offspring with superior traits. This is called hybrid vigour or heterosis. For commercial production, crossbreeding can increase litter size, growth rate, and health. The most common strategy is the two-way cross: mating a Large White sow to a Landrace boar, or vice versa. The resulting F1 gilts are kept as mothers, and they are mated to a terminal boar like Duroc. This is a three-way cross: (Large White x Landrace) sow x Duroc boar. The piglets from this cross are all for market. They grow fast, have lean meat, and good feed efficiency. Another strategy is the rotational cross: use two or three breeds in rotation, e.g., breed 1 boar on breed 2 sows, then breed 2 boar on their daughters, and so on. This maintains hybrid vigour but requires more breeds. For small farms, a simple two-breed cross is easiest. For example, use a local sow (hardy) and an exotic boar (fast growth). The offspring will be hardier than pure exotic and faster-growing than pure local. In Africa, where heat and disease are challenges, crossbreeding with local breeds can improve survival. However, you must manage nutrition and health carefully. Crossbred pigs need balanced feed (16% protein for growers, 14% for finishers) and regular deworming. Record performance of crossbreds: weigh at birth, weaning, and market. Compare with purebreds. If crossbreds are not better, check management. Avoid keeping crossbred gilts for breeding if they come from a terminal cross, as they may not mother well. Always buy terminal boars from reputable breeders. In hot climates, use Duroc or local boars for heat tolerance. For market, crossbred pigs often fetch higher prices due to lean meat. Plan your crossbreeding: decide on maternal line (e.g., Large White x Landrace) and terminal line (e.g., Duroc). Keep 1 boar per 10-15 sows. Replace boars every 2 years. Keep records of each cross. If you have a small herd, consider artificial insemination (AI) to access superior genetics without keeping a boar. AI costs about $10-20 per dose in many African cities. Crossbreeding is not a magic bullet; it must be combined with good feeding, housing, and health.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Pig Breeds and Selection for African Pig Enterprises

This practical reference consolidates the core decision-support material from the module Pig Breeds and Selection. It is written for the commercial pig producer in Africa who must choose breeds, select breeding stock, and design crossbreeding systems that deliver measurable profit not simply good-looking animals. Every recommendation here is anchored to real farm economics: feed conversion, litter size, growth rate, carcass value, and market preference.

Pig production in Africa is growing rapidly, driven by urban demand for pork in Nigeria, Uganda, Kenya, South Africa, Ghana, and Zambia. But profitability is squeezed by high feed costs (6075% of total production cost), disease pressure (especially African Swine Fever), and inconsistent genetics. Breed and selection decisions are therefore among the highest-leverage choices a pig farmer makes. A sow that weans 2 extra piglets per litter or a boar that improves feed conversion by 0.3 units can change the entire enterprise margin.

1. Identifying Major Pig Breeds in African Production Systems

African pig farms typically work with three categories of genetics: exotic improved breeds, local indigenous pigs, and crossbreds. The dominant exotic breeds are Large White (Yorkshire), Landrace, Duroc, Hampshire, and Pietrain. Local breeds include the Nigerian Indigenous Pig, the Mukono pig of Uganda, the Windsnyer of southern Africa, and various village pigs across West and Central Africa.

2. Comparing Maternal and Terminal Breeds

Maternal breeds are selected for reproductive performance: number born alive, litter weaning weight, mothering ability, and longevity. Large White and Landrace are the classic maternal lines. Terminal breeds Duroc, Hampshire, Pietrain, and increasingly specialised terminal lines are selected for growth rate, feed conversion, and carcass leanness.

For African commercial farms, the practical rule is: use maternal genetics in the sow herd and terminal genetics in the boar (or terminal semen) for the slaughter generation. This captures hybrid vigour and complementary traits. Do not use terminal boars over terminal sows you lose reproductive efficiency and produce fewer, more variable litters.

3. Selecting Breeding Stock Based on Performance

Selection must be based on recorded performance, not appearance alone. In African conditions, where recording is often informal, prioritise the traits with the highest economic weight and the easiest measurement:

  • Number born alive per litter the single biggest driver of sow productivity.
  • Litter weaning weight combines milk yield and piglet survival.
  • Average daily gain to 90 kg determines time to market and feed cost.
  • Feed conversion ratio directly controls profitability given high feed prices.
  • Backfat thickness / loin depth where carcass payment is by lean percentage.
  • Structural soundness and teat count functional traits that extend productive life.

Use a simple selection index. Even a basic index with three or four traits is far better than selecting on a single trait, because single-trait selection causes deterioration in other economically important traits. For example, selecting only for leanness in a tropical environment can reduce appetite, litter size, and heat tolerance.

4. Evaluating Conformation and Soundness

Conformation assessment is a practical filter before performance records are available. For breeding stock, check:

  • Legs and feet: straight, strong pasterns; no buck knees, cow hocks, or splayed toes. Unsound legs are the leading cause of early culling in sows.
  • Teats: at least 1214 evenly spaced, functional teats; inverted or blind teats reduce rearing capacity.
  • Back and loin: long, well-muscled, without sharp shoulders or weak loin.
  • Underline: deep, with good capacity for feed intake and pregnancy.
  • Vulva and reproductive tract: well-developed, correctly positioned vulva in gilts.
  • Behaviour: calm temperament; aggressive or nervous animals are difficult and dangerous to manage.

In tropical African conditions, also assess heat tolerance and skin condition. Pietrain and heavily muscled lines are more stress-prone (halothane gene sensitivity) and less suited to hot, humid environments without excellent housing and cooling.

5. Understanding Genetics and Heritability

Heritability (h) tells you how much of the variation in a trait is passed from parents to offspring. It determines how fast you can improve a trait through selection.

Key business takeaway: do not expect fast genetic progress in litter size through selection alone. Crossbreeding and good sow management deliver faster returns. Focus direct selection pressure on growth, feed efficiency, and carcass traits, where heritability is moderate to high.

6. Crossbreeding Strategies for Commercial Production

Crossbreeding exploits heterosis (hybrid vigour) and combines complementary breed strengths. For African commercial farms, the most practical systems are:

  • Two-way terminal cross: Large White or Landrace sow Duroc boar. Simple, produces fast-growing, hardy slaughter pigs. Best entry point for small and medium farms.
  • Three-way terminal cross: Large White Landrace F1 sow Duroc or Pietrain boar. Higher heterosis in the sow, excellent growth and carcass in the progeny. Requires good record-keeping and replacement management.
  • Rotational cross: Alternating boars of two or three breeds. Retains some heterosis and allows on-farm replacement without buying purebred gilts. Suitable where AI or purebred boars are expensive or unreliable.
  • Local exotic cross: Indigenous sow improved exotic boar. Improves growth and carcass while retaining hardiness and disease tolerance. Useful in low-input systems and for premium local-market products.

Practical rules for African conditions: keep a closed or semi-closed nucleus where possible to reduce disease risk; use AI to access superior terminal genetics without maintaining multiple boars; and always match the cross to the market some African markets still prefer fattier pork, while supermarket and export chains pay premiums for lean carcasses.

SAFETY AND BIOSECURITY WARNING

African Swine Fever (ASF) is a devastating, incurable pig disease present in many African countries. It spreads through contaminated feed, vehicles, people, clothing, and pig-to-pig contact and through the purchase of live pigs or semen from unknown sources. Never introduce new breeding stock without a quarantine period of at least 30 days, away from the main herd. Buy only from reputable, disease-free breeders with health records. Do not feed uncooked swill or kitchen waste. Disinfect boots, vehicles, and equipment. If sudden deaths or high fever occur, report immediately to veterinary authorities and do not move animals off the farm. A single biosecurity lapse can destroy an entire pig enterprise.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Define your production objective first: maternal replacement, terminal slaughter pigs, or both.
  • Use maternal breeds (Large White, Landrace) for the sow herd; terminal breeds (Duroc, Hampshire, Pietrain) for the boar.
  • Select breeding stock on recorded performance litter size, weaning weight, daily gain, FCR, backfat not looks alone.
  • Check conformation and soundness: legs, feet, at least 12 functional teats, correct underline and vulva.
  • Match breed and cross to your market: lean carcass for formal buyers, hardier crossbreds for local markets.
  • Use crossbreeding to capture heterosis, especially for low-heritability traits like litter size.
  • Keep simple records for every sow and boar; cull consistently on performance and structural faults.
  • Enforce strict biosecurity and a 30-day quarantine for all incoming stock ASF has no cure.
