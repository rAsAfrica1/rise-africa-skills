Module 5

Humidity Control, Misting and Water Supply

Mushrooms are 90% water. The grow room must hold 85–92% humidity — not 80%, not 95%. This module is the water system that achieves it, and the discipline that keeps it stable.

Module 5 of 12Written methodKnowledge check + capstone
By the end of this module you will be able to
  • Choose the correct humidification method for your scale
  • Design a misting system that holds 85–92% without wetting the crop
  • Ensure the water supply is clean and adequate for your operation
  • Prevent the three problems that ruin mushroom humidity — over-watering, contamination, and uneven distribution
  • Automate humidity control cheaply and reliably
  • Produce a humidity system design for your own mushroom house

Lesson 5.1

Why humidity matters so much

A mushroom is 90–92% water. It grows by absorbing water from the substrate and losing water to the air. The balance between these two determines how fast it grows, how big it gets, and whether it develops properly.

What happens at each humidity level

Relative humidityEffect on the crop
Under 75%Fruiting bodies dry out, crack, stop growing. Crop loss.
75–85%Slow growth, small mushrooms, poor yield.
85–92%Optimal for most species. Fast, even growth, good yield.
92–95%Acceptable for some species. Risk of bacterial blotch starting.
Over 95%Condensation forms on the mushrooms. Bacterial blotch, brown spots, contamination. Crop loss.

The narrow band

The correct range is only 7 percentage points wide. Below and the mushrooms dry. Above and they rot. Every humidifier, controller, and air exchange in the room exists to hold within that band, 24 hours a day, for weeks.

The humidity rule: 85–92%. Above or below and yield falls fast. A humidity controller is not optional for commercial mushroom growing.

Lesson 5.2

The three humidification methods

MethodHow it worksCostBest for

For a small African mushroom house (10–30 m² of grow space), misting nozzles or an ultrasonic humidifier is the correct choice. Both are effective and available.

Misting nozzles — the details

  • Pressure: 4–7 bar for fine mist. Too low and droplets are too large; they wet the crop and cause bacterial blotch.
  • Nozzle size: 0.3–0.5 mm orifice for fine mist.
  • Placement: high on one wall, aimed upward and across the room, not down onto the crop.
  • Water quality: filtered. Impurities block nozzles and contaminate the crop.
  • Timer control: usually 10–30 seconds every 5–15 minutes, adjusted seasonally.

If your water source is chlorinated (municipal supply), let it stand in an open tank for 24 hours before use — chlorine dissipates. Or use a carbon filter.

If your water source is surface water (river, dam), filter through sand and cartridge filters, and consider UV treatment or a small chlorinator. Mushrooms are extremely sensitive to contamination.

Water quantity

A mushroom house uses water for:

  • Humidification: 5–20 L per hour of operation, depending on room size
  • Wash-down: 20–50 L per crop cycle
  • Substrate prep (pasteurisation): 100–500 L per tonne of substrate

Estimate your weekly water need and confirm your source can supply it year-round. During dry season, spring or river sources drop. Boreholes are the most reliable.

Lesson 5.4

Automatic humidity control

Manual misting fails. Nobody can mist a room 24/7 for weeks at a time. Automatic control is required for a commercial operation.

Components

  • Humidity sensor — a digital hygrometer with 3% accuracy. $30–100.
  • Controller — turns the humidifier on and off at set humidity levels. $60–250.
  • Solenoid valve — a 12V or 24V valve that opens the water line when the controller signals. $30–100.
  • Humidifier — one of the three types from Lesson 5.2.
  • Timer (backup) — a simple 15-minute timer as a secondary control to prevent the humidifier running continuously if the sensor fails.

Setpoints

  • On at 85% — start the humidifier when humidity drops below 85%.
  • Off at 92% — stop when humidity reaches 92%.
  • Alarm at 80% and 95% — audible warning if humidity leaves the safe band.

Sensors and calibration

  • Cheap sensors drift over time. Calibrate monthly against a known-good reference (a second sensor you trust).
  • Position sensors at mushroom level (mid-height), not at ceiling or floor.
  • Shield the sensor from direct water mist. A small perforated plastic cover is enough.

The backup timer

If the humidity sensor fails, the humidifier may run continuously and soak the crop. A simple mechanical timer (e.g., mist for 30 seconds every 10 minutes) as a hard limit on runtime protects the crop. This costs $10 and is worth having.

Lesson 5.5

The three humidity disasters

1. Over-misting the crop

Water droplets sitting on mushroom caps cause bacterial blotch (Pseudomonas tolaasii), a brown spot that ruins the crop. Prevention:

  • Use fine nozzles (0.3–0.5 mm) at correct pressure.
  • Aim mist upward, not down onto the crop.
  • Do not mist during the last 2 hours before harvest.
  • Ensure air circulation moves the mist around, not settling.

2. Contaminated water

If the water is not clean, every misting spreads contamination. Once bacterial blotch is established, whole crops are lost. Prevention:

  • Test water source annually.
  • Filter all water to the misting system.
  • Chlorinated water: let stand 24 hours before use, or carbon filter.
  • Clean the misting system every 2 weeks.

3. Uneven distribution

Humidity pockets — dry in one corner, dripping in another — are caused by:

  • Poor air circulation. Solution: a small circulation fan moving air slowly (0.2–0.5 m/s).
  • Too few nozzles. Rule: 1 nozzle per 4–6 m² of floor.
  • Nozzles blocked. Inspect and clean monthly.
  • Shelving blocking airflow. Leave 200 mm clearance behind shelves.
The humidity rule: right range (85–92%), right nozzle, right water, right airflow. Four things. Automatic controller and backup timer on top. This is the system that decides the crop.

Knowledge check — Module 5

1. What is the correct humidity range for oyster mushrooms?

Correct: 85–92%. Below and mushrooms dry. Above and bacterial blotch takes over.

2. Why must misting nozzles produce a fine mist?

Correct: fine mist evaporates. 0.3–0.5 mm nozzles at 4–7 bar. Aim upward, not at the crop.

3. Why must chlorinated water be left to stand before use in a mushroom house?

Correct: chlorine damages mycelium. Let stand 24 hours in an open tank or use a carbon filter.

4. What is the correct setpoint for a humidity controller?

Correct: on at 85%, off at 92%. Alarm at 80% and 95% as a safety net.

5. What is the correct number of nozzles per square metre?

Correct: 1 per 4–6 m². Fewer nozzles cause uneven humidity; more waste water.

Practical assignment — Module 5

Produce a humidity system design for your mushroom house.

  1. Humidification method chosen (misting, ultrasonic, centrifugal) and why.
  2. Number of nozzles or unit size, with the arithmetic based on room area.
  3. Water source and treatment (filter, chlorine dissipation, UV or other).
  4. Water quantity calculation — misting, wash-down, substrate prep — total weekly need.
  5. Humidity controller — sensor type, controller model, setpoints (on, off, alarm low, alarm high).
  6. Backup timer setting to prevent continuous misting if the sensor fails.
  7. Air circulation fan — size, position, airflow rate.
  8. Cleaning and maintenance schedule for the misting system.
  9. Two supplier quotations for the humidification equipment and the humidity controller.
  10. One paragraph (150 words) on the biggest humidity risk at your site and how you will control it.

Marks are weighted toward points 2 and 10. Nozzle sizing is the technical foundation; the paragraph is where you commit to a humidity strategy.
