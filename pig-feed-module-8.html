Lesson 8.1~11 min

Feed Conversion and Productivity

In this lesson
  • Calculate a true feed conversion ratio that includes mortality and wastage
  • Convert feed conversion into feed cost per kilogram of gain
  • Rank the practical interventions that improve feed conversion by their return

Feed is normally between two thirds and three quarters of the cost of producing a pig, so feed conversion ratio is the single most important production number on the farm. It is also the number most often quoted wrongly, because most producers calculate the ratio they wish they had rather than the one they have.

Feed conversion ratio is total feed used divided by total liveweight gained. The word total is doing the work. Total feed means everything delivered to the pen, including what was spilled, what the rats took, and the feed eaten by pigs that later died. Total gain means the weight actually sold, minus the weight you started with, for the animals that survived. A dead pig ate feed and produced no gain, and if you leave it out of the calculation you flatter yourself and misprice your pork.

Worked example. A producer in Mzuzu, Malawi, starts 100 pigs at 25 kg each. Ninety-four are sold at an average of 98 kg. Six died at various points. Over the batch she delivered 24,600 kg of feed.

  1. Weight in for the pigs sold: 94 x 25 = 2,350 kg.
  2. Weight out: 94 x 98 = 9,212 kg.
  3. Gain: 9,212 minus 2,350 = 6,862 kg.
  4. True feed conversion ratio: 24,600 divided by 6,862 = 3.58.

If she had ignored mortality and assumed 100 pigs finished, her gain would look like 100 x 98 minus 100 x 25 = 7,300 kg and her ratio would read 24,600 divided by 7,300 = 3.37. That six percent difference is the whole margin on many farms.

Now convert it into money. Feed cost per kilogram of gain equals feed price per kilogram times feed conversion ratio. At 0.42 USD per kg her real figure is 0.42 x 3.58 = 1.504 USD per kg of gain, against the 1.415 the flattering calculation suggested.

Where does improvement come from? In rough order of return for a small producer:

  1. Reduce mortality. In the example, saving three of the six deaths would add 3 x 73 = 219 kg of gain for the same feed, dropping the ratio to 24,600 divided by 7,081 = 3.47.
  2. Stop wastage. Feeders that spill, wet feed left to sour, and rodent losses are pure ratio. Feeder adjustment costs nothing.
  3. Phase feed. The published finisher ladder runs from 1.05 percent SID lysine at 27 to 40 kg down to 0.65 percent at 120 to 130 kg. Feeding one flat diet either wastes protein at the end or starves growth at the start.
  4. Fix water. Dehydration cuts feed intake faster than almost anything, so a blocked nipple is a feed conversion problem.
  5. Control mould. Deoxynivalenol reduces voluntary intake from 1 to 3 ppm, well below the 5 ppm maximum.
  6. Consider additives with published effect sizes. Essential oil blends at 100 to 150 mg per kg show 5 to 10 percent feed conversion improvement, organic acids 5 to 10 percent, enzymes 2 to 5 percent, and zinc oxide at nursery levels 5 to 10 percent where permitted.
  7. Add fat, but only when the oil price justifies it. One percent added fat improves the ratio by about 0.04 points, and Module 7 Lesson 6 works the test.

Also watch the ratio's own weight dependence. Feed conversion worsens steadily as pigs get heavier, because energy efficiency for gain declines with body weight. That is why holding pigs beyond your market weight is expensive, a point developed in Lesson 5.

Second worked example. The same producer measures a 5 percent feed wastage from worn feeders. Five percent of 24,600 kg is 1,230 kg of feed, worth 1,230 x 0.42 = 516.60 USD in one batch. Two new feeder pans and an afternoon adjusting the gates cost her 90 USD. Her ratio moves from 3.58 to 23,370 divided by 6,862 = 3.41, and her feed cost per kilogram of gain falls from 1.504 to 1.432 USD. Across a year of three batches that is roughly 1,550 USD, from equipment maintenance alone.

Lesson 8.2~11 min

Cost Analysis and Break-Even

In this lesson
  • Separate variable from fixed costs in a pig enterprise
  • Calculate break-even price per kilogram and break-even number of pigs
  • Use gross margin per pig to test whether a price offer is worth accepting

Break-even analysis answers two questions that keep producers awake: what price do I need, and how many pigs do I need? Both are simple arithmetic once costs are split correctly.

Variable costs change with every extra pig. They are the weaner or the breeding cost, the feed, veterinary and medicine, transport to market, market levies and commissions, and any casual labour paid per pig. Fixed costs do not change when you add one pig: buildings and their repair, permanent labour, licences, interest on loans, electricity for lights and pumps, and depreciation on equipment. The distinction matters because only variable costs tell you whether to sell one more pig, while fixed costs tell you how many pigs you must sell to stay in business.

Worked example. A producer in Ndola, Zambia, finishes pigs bought as weaners.

Variable costs per pig:

  • Weaner at 25 kg: 40.00 USD
  • Feed: 75 kg of gain at a feed conversion ratio of 3.2 is 240 kg, at 0.42 USD per kg = 100.80 USD
  • Health and medicine: 4.00 USD
  • Transport and market levy: 4.00 USD
  • Total variable cost: 148.80 USD per pig

He sells at 100 kg liveweight for 1.80 USD per kg, which is 180.00 USD.

Gross margin per pig: 180.00 minus 148.80 = 31.20 USD.

His fixed costs for the year are 9,000 USD: permanent labour 4,800, building repair and depreciation 2,400, licences and insurance 600, electricity and water 700, interest 500.

  1. Break-even number of pigs: fixed costs divided by gross margin per pig, so 9,000 divided by 31.20 = 288.5, meaning 289 pigs a year before he earns anything.
  2. He currently sells 400 pigs. Profit: 400 x 31.20 minus 9,000 = 12,480 minus 9,000 = 3,480 USD a year.
  3. Fixed cost per pig at 400 pigs: 9,000 divided by 400 = 22.50 USD.
  4. Full cost per pig: 148.80 plus 22.50 = 171.30 USD.
  5. Break-even price per kilogram: 171.30 divided by 100 kg = 1.71 USD per kg.

That 1.71 figure is the number to carry in your head. Any offer below it loses money over the year. But note the second, lower number: 148.80 divided by 100 = 1.49 USD per kg is his variable cost per kilogram. In the short run, with pigs already fed and a building already paid for, an offer between 1.49 and 1.71 still contributes something to fixed costs and beats not selling. It is not a position to live in, but it is a position to survive a bad month in.

Notice how sensitive the whole thing is to feed conversion. If his ratio slips from 3.2 to 3.6, feed rises to 75 x 3.6 x 0.42 = 113.40 USD, variable cost becomes 161.40, gross margin falls to 18.60, and break-even volume jumps from 289 to 9,000 divided by 18.60 = 484 pigs. A 12.5 percent slip in feed conversion raises his break-even herd size by 68 percent. This is why Lesson 1 comes first.

And notice the effect of scale on fixed costs. At 600 pigs his fixed cost per pig would be 9,000 divided by 600 = 15.00 USD and his break-even price 163.80 divided by 100 = 1.64 USD per kg, assuming the buildings can hold them. That is the real argument for scaling, and Lesson 6 tests whether it holds.

Build the habit of recomputing this every quarter. Maize prices in most African markets are strongly seasonal, so a break-even calculated at harvest is wrong by the lean season. Keep one page in the notebook with variable cost per pig at the top, fixed costs for the year below, and the two break-even figures at the bottom, and rewrite it every time feed price moves more than about ten percent.

Breeding Herd Economics

In this lesson
  • Calculate the annual cost of keeping one sow including feed, replacement and boar share
  • Convert that cost into a cost per weaner using pigs weaned per sow per year
  • Test how sensitive weaner cost is to productivity and sow longevity

A breeding herd only makes sense if it produces weaners more cheaply than you could buy them. Most small producers have never tested this, and a meaningful minority are running a sow unit that quietly loses money against the local weaner price.

Start with what a sow eats in a year. Using the figures developed in Module 5:

  • Gestation: about 250 kg per pregnancy, from 90 days at 2.0 kg plus 24 days at 2.9 kg.
  • Lactation: 21 days at about 6.4 kg is 134 kg.
  • Weaning to service: roughly 6 days at about 3.5 kg is 21 kg, an approximate figure.
  • Per reproductive cycle: 250 plus 134 plus 21 = 405 kg.
  • At 2.2 litters per sow per year: 405 x 2.2 = 891 kg, call it 900 kg.

At a blended price of 0.44 USD per kg across gestation and lactation diets, that is 900 x 0.44 = 396 USD of feed per sow per year.

Now the rest of her costs.

  1. Gilt replacement. From Module 5, a developed gilt costs about 215 USD. Spread over the number of litters she produces: at six litters, 215 divided by 6 = 35.83 USD per litter, or 35.83 x 2.2 = 78.83 USD per sow-year.
  2. Boar share. One boar for twenty sows, eating 912.5 kg a year at 0.45 USD, is 410.63 USD, plus his own replacement. Per sow: about 410.63 divided by 20 = 20.53, call it 25.00 USD with replacement.
  3. Health, vaccination and breeding costs: an estimated 45.00 USD per sow-year on many small units. Use your own figure.

Total annual sow cost: 396.00 plus 78.83 plus 25.00 plus 45.00 = 544.83 USD.

Now productivity. Pigs weaned per sow per year is litters per year multiplied by piglets weaned per litter. At 2.2 litters and 9.5 weaned, that is 2.2 x 9.5 = 20.9 weaners.

Cost per weaner: 544.83 divided by 20.9 = 26.07 USD.

If the local weaner price is 40 USD, the breeding herd is generating 40 minus 26.07 = 13.93 USD of value per weaner, or 13.93 x 20.9 = 291.14 USD per sow per year, before her share of housing and labour. That is a real business.

Now test the sensitivity, because this is where the lesson lives.

  • At 8 weaned per litter and 2.0 litters, productivity is 16.0 weaners. Cost per weaner: 544.83 divided by 16 = 34.05 USD. Value generated collapses to 5.95 per weaner, or 95.20 per sow-year.
  • At 11 weaned and 2.3 litters, productivity is 25.3. Cost per weaner: 544.83 divided by 25.3 = 21.54 USD, and value generated rises to 18.46 per weaner, or 467.04 per sow-year.

So moving from 16 to 25.3 weaners per sow per year multiplies the sow's contribution nearly fivefold on almost the same cost base. Almost every lever that does this is nutritional and covered in Module 5: late gestation feeding for colostrum, ad libitum lactation feeding and heat management for intake, generous weaning-to-service feeding to shorten non-productive days, and breeding-specification minerals to keep sows to parity six instead of parity three.

One more test. Take the sow longevity arithmetic from Module 5 Lesson 6. If she is culled at three litters instead of six, her replacement charge doubles from 78.83 to 157.67 USD a sow-year, total cost rises to 623.67, and cost per weaner at 20.9 rises from 26.07 to 29.84 USD. Across a twenty-sow herd producing 418 weaners a year, that is 418 x 3.77 = 1,575.86 USD, matching the figure calculated in Module 5 from the other direction.

Worked example. A producer in Bulawayo, Zimbabwe, runs fifteen sows and weans 17 pigs per sow per year. Her cost per weaner is 544.83 divided by 17 = 32.05 USD against a local weaner price of 38 USD. She is making 5.95 per weaner, or 89.25 per sow. She calculates that lifting weaning numbers by two per sow per year, through late gestation feeding and better lactation intake alone, would take her to 19 weaners at 28.68 USD each, raising her margin to 9.32 per weaner and 177.08 per sow, nearly doubling her breeding herd's contribution for the cost of a second scoop mark and a shade cloth.

Vertical Integration Strategies

In this lesson
  • Identify the margin available at each link in the pig value chain
  • Choose which link to integrate first based on capital, skill and risk
  • Recognise when integration adds cost rather than margin

Vertical integration means capturing a margin that currently belongs to someone else, by doing that step yourself. In pig production the chain runs roughly: feed ingredients, feed manufacture, breeding and weaner production, growing and finishing, slaughter, butchery and processing, and retail. Most small producers occupy one or two links and buy or sell at every boundary.

Each boundary carries a margin. The practical question is not whether integration is good in principle but which single step is worth taking next on your farm, and the honest answer is usually determined by capital, skill and risk rather than by the size of the margin.

Look at each link.

  1. Feed manufacture. The margin is real, since a bought compound feed usually costs meaningfully more than the ingredients plus mixing. But this is a working capital and storage question above all, and the break-even calculation for on-farm milling is worked in full in Module 12 Lesson 5. Do not duplicate that here; take the answer from there.
  2. Weaner production. Module 3 of this course covers the biology and Lesson 3 above covers the economics. On the figures worked there, a weaner costing 26.07 USD to produce against a market price of 40 USD captures about 13.93 USD per pig. The catch is that breeding is a skill, not a purchase, and a herd that weans 16 rather than 21 pigs per sow per year captures almost nothing.
  3. Growing and finishing. Most producers already do this. The margin is the difference between the weaner price and the finished pig value less feed, which in Lesson 2 was 31.20 USD per pig of gross margin.
  4. Slaughter and butchery. This is where the largest visible gap sits, and where the most producers get hurt. A 100 kg live pig at 1.80 USD per kg is 180 USD. Dressing percentage is usually in the region of 72 to 75 percent, an approximate figure that varies with breed and condition, so about 73 kg of carcass. If pork sells at 3.50 USD per kg retail, that carcass is worth 73 x 3.50 = 255.50 USD, apparently 75.50 USD more.

But count the costs the trader was covering. Slaughter fee and inspection, transport of a carcass rather than a live pig, chilling, which requires reliable power, cutting labour and skill, packaging, unsold cuts and trimming loss, spoilage risk, licensing, and the working capital tied up while you sell over several days instead of taking one payment at the gate. On many small operations those costs consume most of the 75.50 USD, and the residual is payment for a considerable amount of extra work and risk. It can still be worth doing, particularly where the producer has a reliable outlet, but it must be costed line by line before the equipment is bought.

The practical order for most small producers is: first control your feed quality and cost, then your weaner supply, then finishing performance, and only then consider slaughter and retail. Integrating downstream before your production is consistent simply exports your problems to a customer who will not return.

There is one more integration direction worth naming: sideways, into supply. Growing your own maize, soya or forage, or contracting a neighbour to grow it, removes the seasonal price swing that damages most feed budgets. It also carries agronomic risk and, critically, storage and drying obligations. Module 9 sets the mycotoxin limits that make or break this: grain must be dried to 15 percent moisture or below and stored cool and dry, or the saving is lost to aflatoxin and deoxynivalenol.

Worked example. A producer near Kigali, Rwanda, finishes 400 pigs a year and buys all his weaners at 40 USD. He considers two integrations. Option one, a 20-sow breeding unit, would produce about 418 weaners a year and capture roughly 13.93 USD each, or 5,822 USD a year, but requires farrowing housing, gilts and about eighteen months before it runs properly. Option two, a small butchery, might capture part of a 75.50 USD per pig gap on perhaps 100 pigs a year sold as cuts, but only if he can find and hold the retail customers, which is the subject of Module 11. He chooses breeding first, because it feeds a business he already understands and its output is guaranteed a home in his own finishing pens. That logic, matching integration to what you already control, is usually the right one.

Lesson 8.5~12 min

Marketing Premium Pork

In this lesson
  • Compare local marketing channels on net USD received rather than headline price
  • Calculate the cost of holding pigs beyond their target market weight
  • Choose a baseline channel mix that matches your volume and cash needs

This lesson deals with baseline commodity marketing: getting a fair price for ordinary good pork through the channels available to a small producer. The separate question of building a genuine premium, through eating quality, heritage genetics, story and direct customer relationships, is the whole subject of Module 11, and this lesson does not attempt to cover it. Get the baseline right first; a premium built on an unreliable base does not survive.

There are four common channels and each has a different net value.

  1. Farm-gate live sale to a trader. Highest convenience, lowest price. The trader takes transport, market risk, payment risk and time off your hands, and charges for all four. Payment is usually immediate, which has real value.
  2. Live sale delivered to an abattoir or larger buyer. Better headline price, but you pay transport, you carry shrinkage in transit, and payment terms are often 7 to 30 days.
  3. Carcass sale to a butcher. Better again, but requires slaughter access, inspection and often chilling, and you carry the dressing percentage risk.
  4. Direct sale of cuts to households, restaurants or a shop. Highest price per kilogram, highest cost and effort, slowest to build.

Compare them properly, on net received per pig, not on headline price.

Worked example. A producer in Dodoma, Tanzania, has a 100 kg pig and three real offers.

  • Trader at the gate: 1.70 USD per kg live. Net: 170.00 USD, paid today, no other cost.
  • Abattoir 60 km away: 1.95 USD per kg live. Transport for a load of six pigs costs 60 USD, so 10.00 per pig. Weight loss in transit and lairage of about 2 percent means the pig weighs 98 kg on their scale: 98 x 1.95 = 191.10, minus 10.00 transport = 181.10 USD, paid in 21 days.
  • Butcher wanting carcasses: 2.60 USD per kg carcass. At 73 percent dressing that is 73 kg, or 189.80 USD, minus slaughter and inspection fee of 12.00 and transport of 10.00 = 167.80 USD, paid in 7 days.

On these numbers the abattoir wins at 181.10, the trader is second at 170.00 and the butcher, despite the highest headline price per kilogram, is last at 167.80. Change the dressing percentage to 75 and the butcher moves to 173.00; change transport to a shared load and it moves again. This is why the calculation must be done with your own numbers, and redone when any of them changes.

Also value the payment terms honestly. Twenty-one days of delay on 181.10 USD, on a farm that must buy feed weekly, is not free. If you would have to borrow to bridge it, add the interest. If it would force you to sell a second pig early, add that cost too.

Now timing, which is the marketing decision producers most often get wrong. Feed conversion worsens as pigs get heavier, so every extra kilogram past your market weight costs more feed than the one before it.

Worked example. The same producer considers holding pigs from 100 to 115 kg to catch a festival price. In that weight range feed conversion is poor, roughly 4.0. So 15 kg of gain needs 15 x 4.0 = 60 kg of feed, at 0.42 USD = 25.20 USD. The extra revenue at 1.80 USD per kg is 15 x 1.80 = 27.00 USD. The margin on those fifteen kilograms is 27.00 minus 25.20 = 1.80 USD, before counting the extra weeks of pen occupancy, water, labour and risk. Meanwhile the published late-finisher specification tells you the pig is also depositing more backfat, with a modern market target of about 15 to 19 mm at the tenth rib, so heavier is not automatically better paid. Unless the festival price is genuinely higher, holding pigs is a way to work for nothing.

Build a channel mix rather than a single channel. A practical baseline for a small producer is to sell the bulk through whichever wholesale channel nets most, keep a small direct trade going to hold prices honest and learn what customers want, and never become dependent on a single buyer. One buyer means one price, set by them.

Finally, record every sale: date, number, live weight, channel, price per kilogram, deductions, and date paid. After a year that book will tell you which channel actually pays, which is almost never the one people remember as paying best.

Scaling and Growth Strategy

In this lesson
  • Identify the single binding constraint that limits your current output
  • Calculate the capital and working capital needed to double a sow herd
  • Apply a readiness test before committing to a scale-up

This lesson answers one question only: when should a producer go from N sows to 2N? The wider work of business planning, financing options, batch farrowing and writing a bankable plan belongs to Module 12, which builds on what you decide here.

Start with the honest observation that most failed expansions were not failures of ambition but of sequence. The farm doubled its animals before it doubled its feed cash, its farrowing places, its labour or its market, and the new pigs arrived to find one of those four missing.

So find your binding constraint first. Ask four questions and answer each with a number.

  1. Farrowing places. How many farrowing pens do you have, and how many litters can each produce a year? A pen occupied for five weeks per litter can theoretically produce about ten litters a year, less cleaning and slack. Multiply pens by litters to get your farrowing ceiling.
  2. Feed cash. What is the peak cash requirement before a new batch produces revenue? A pig is sold roughly five months after weaning, so you fund feed for months before income arrives.
  3. Labour. How many hours a day does the current herd take, and who does the extra?
  4. Market. Did you sell every pig you produced last year at your target price, or did some go cheap because you had to move them?

The smallest of those four numbers is your real capacity. Expanding anything else first buys you nothing.

Worked example. A producer in Iringa, Tanzania, runs 10 sows and wants 20. Her farrowing ceiling is fine, she has the pens. Her market is proven: she sold every pig last year at target price and turned buyers away twice. Labour is manageable with one additional part-time worker. So her binding constraint is cash. She prices the move.

Capital costs:

  • 10 gilts at 215 USD each: 2,150
  • Additional gestation and dry sow housing: 3,000
  • Extra feeders, drinkers and fencing: 600
  • Total capital: 5,750 USD

Working capital. Her ten extra sows will eat 10 x 900 = 9,000 kg a year at 0.44 USD, which is 3,960 USD. But she must fund the first cycle before any of their progeny is sold. Their first litters wean about five months after the gilts are served, and those weaners are sold about five months after that, so she funds roughly ten months of sow feed plus the growing feed for the first batch before revenue arrives.

  • Ten months of extra sow feed: 3,960 x 10 divided by 12 = 3,300
  • First batch of 10 x 20.9 = 209 weaners, growing feed at 240 kg each for the ones that reach sale in that window, say half of them at 105 pigs: 105 x 240 x 0.42 = 10,584
  • Total working capital gap: about 13,884 USD

So the real cost of the move is 5,750 plus 13,884 = 19,634 USD, of which only 29 percent is the visible capital. This is the number that surprises producers. The housing is not the hard part.

What does she get? Ten extra sows producing 209 weaners which she finishes at a gross margin of 31.20 USD per pig, less the additional weaner production cost already counted, gives roughly 209 x 31.20 = 6,521 USD of extra gross margin a year, against additional fixed costs of perhaps 1,800 USD for the extra labour and building depreciation, leaving about 4,721 USD. Against 19,634 USD committed, that is a payback of a little over four years on the full commitment, or under a year once the working capital cycles.

Apply a readiness test before committing. A workable one: expand only when you can answer yes to all five.

  1. You sold everything you produced last year at or above your target price.
  2. Your feed conversion ratio and your pigs weaned per sow per year are both at or above your own three-year best.
  3. You have written a break-even calculation in the last quarter and know your break-even price per kilogram.
  4. You can fund the full working capital gap, not just the capital cost, from savings, a facility or a firm commitment.
  5. Your mortality is stable or falling.

If any answer is no, fix that first. Improving productivity in the herd you already own is almost always cheaper than doubling it, as Lesson 3 shows: moving from 16 to 25 weaners per sow per year multiplies a sow's contribution nearly fivefold at no capital cost at all. Doubling a badly performing herd simply doubles the losses. Module 12 takes it from here.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. 94 pigs go from 25 kg to 98 kg on 24,600 kg of feed. What is the true feed conversion ratio?

Gain is 94 x 98 minus 94 x 25, or 6,862 kg, and 24,600 divided by 6,862 gives 3.58.

2. Why must feed eaten by pigs that later died be included in the calculation?

The feed was bought and consumed. Leaving it out produces a flattering ratio and leads to mispricing the pork.

3. What happens to feed conversion as pigs get heavier?

Net energy for gain requirement rises with body weight, so heavier pigs convert less efficiently, which is why holding pigs past market weight is costly.

4. A 5% feed wastage on 24,600 kg at $0.42/kg costs how much per batch?

Five percent of 24,600 kg is 1,230 kg, and 1,230 multiplied by 0.42 gives 516.60 USD.

5. Which intervention typically returns most for a small producer?

Every death is feed already spent with no output, so mortality reduction moves the ratio harder than most additive or ration changes.

6. Which of these is a fixed cost in a pig enterprise?

Building depreciation does not change when one more pig is produced, while feed, weaners and per-pig levies all do.

7. Fixed costs are $9,000 and gross margin is $31.20 per pig. What is the break-even number of pigs?

9,000 divided by 31.20 gives 288.5, so 289 pigs must be sold before the enterprise earns anything.

8. Variable cost is $148.80 and fixed cost per pig is $22.50 at 400 pigs. What is the break-even price for a 100 kg pig?

Full cost is 171.30 USD for 100 kg, giving 1.71 USD per kilogram liveweight.

9. Why might a producer accept a price between his variable cost per kg and his break-even price in the short run?

With the pig already fed and the building already paid for, any price above variable cost makes a contribution to fixed costs, though it is not sustainable.

10. How often should a break-even calculation be redone?

Seasonal maize prices move the calculation substantially, so a break-even worked out at harvest is wrong by the lean season.

11. A sow costs $544.83 a year and weans 20.9 pigs. What is the cost per weaner?

544.83 divided by 20.9 gives 26.07 USD per weaner produced.

12. What is a sow's approximate annual feed use on the figures in this lesson?

About 405 kg per reproductive cycle multiplied by 2.2 litters gives roughly 891 kg, rounded to 900 kg per year.

13. If a sow is culled at three litters instead of six, what happens to her replacement charge per sow-year?

The same 215 USD gilt cost is recovered over half as many litters, doubling the annual replacement charge and raising cost per weaner.

14. Which lever most cheaply raises pigs weaned per sow per year?

These raise output on almost the same cost base, whereas buying more sows adds the full cost base again for the same output per sow.

15. At 2.2 litters per year and 9.5 weaned per litter, how many pigs are weaned per sow per year?

2.2 multiplied by 9.5 gives 20.9 pigs weaned per sow per year.

16. A 100 kg live pig at 72-75% dressing gives roughly what carcass weight?

At about 73 percent dressing, a 100 kg live pig yields roughly 73 kg of carcass, though the figure varies with breed and condition.

17. Why is the apparent $75.50 gap between live and retail value usually much smaller in practice?

The trader's margin pays for real costs and risks. Those must be costed line by line before assuming the gap is profit.

18. What is the recommended order of integration for most small producers?

Integrating downstream before production is consistent exports your problems to customers. Control inputs and performance first.

19. Where is the full break-even calculation for on-farm feed milling handled?

Module 12 Lesson 5 works the mill-or-buy break-even in detail, so it is cross-referenced rather than duplicated here.

20. What is the main risk of backward integration into growing your own grain?

Grain must be dried to 15 percent moisture or below and stored cool and dry, or aflatoxin and deoxynivalenol losses exceed the price saving.

21. A pig sells at $1.95/kg at an abattoir but loses 2% weight in transit and costs $10 to transport. What is the net on a 100 kg pig?

98 kg at 1.95 USD gives 191.10, less 10.00 USD transport, leaving 181.10 USD net.

22. Holding a pig from 100 to 115 kg at a late-finisher FCR of 4.0 requires how much feed?

15 kg of gain multiplied by a feed conversion ratio of 4.0 gives 60 kg of feed.

23. Why should payment terms be counted in a channel comparison?

Delayed payment has a real cost in interest or in forced early sales, so it belongs in the net-received calculation alongside transport and deductions.

24. Where is the premium and heritage marketing angle developed?

Module 11 covers specialty pork, heritage breeds and premium markets in full. This lesson deliberately stays on baseline commodity channels.

25. What is the danger of selling through a single buyer?

A channel mix keeps prices honest and preserves a fallback, which is why even a small direct trade alongside a wholesale channel is worth maintaining.

26. What is usually the largest part of the cost of doubling a sow herd?

In the worked example, working capital was 13,884 USD against 5,750 USD of visible capital, roughly 71 percent of the total commitment.

27. Ten extra sows eating 900 kg each per year at $0.44 per kg cost how much annually?

10 multiplied by 900 kg gives 9,000 kg, and 9,000 multiplied by 0.44 USD gives 3,960 USD a year.

28. Which of these is a valid reason NOT to expand yet?

Doubling a herd that is underperforming doubles the losses. Fix productivity in the herd you own before adding to it.

29. How is a farm's real capacity ceiling determined?

The smallest of the four is binding. Expanding any of the others first buys nothing until the binding constraint is removed.

30. Where is the full treatment of financing, batch farrowing and business planning found?

Module 12 covers financing options, batch farrowing throughput and writing a bankable plan, building on the scale-up decision made here.
