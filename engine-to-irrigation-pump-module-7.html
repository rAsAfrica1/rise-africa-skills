7. Fabricating the Frame and Mounts

The frame is the skeleton of your pump unit. It holds the engine, the pump, the radiator, and the fuel tank in rigid alignment. A good frame lasts 20+ years. A bad one flexes, cracks, and eventually fails — usually during harvest season when you need it most. This module shows you exactly how to build it, step by step.

Module 7 of 12 · 6 lessons · ~60 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end · Covers petrol and diesel builds

Lesson 7.1 — Frame design principles — layout, strength, portability

Before you cut steel, decide what kind of frame you need. Three layouts cover 95% of projects.

LayoutBest forProsCons
Linear (inline)Direct coupling, compact unitsShort belt, minimal footprintLong frame
V-shapeBelt drive with radiator on the sideShort overall lengthHarder to fabricate
ParallelBelt drive, long radiator, fuel tank on the far endBalanced weight, easy to moveWide footprint

For a first build, the parallel layout is recommended. Engine on one end, pump in the middle, radiator and fuel tank on the far end. The weight balances for easier moving.

Frame sizing example for a small-medium project (4Y petrol engine, 5 kW pump):

  • Total length: 1,800 mm
  • Width: 800 mm
  • Height: 600 mm (engine base plate)
  • Steel: 60×60×6 mm angle iron for the main rails; 40×40×5 mm for cross-bracing

Strength requirements:

  • Support at least 3× the total weight (engine + pump + full tank + frame)
  • Resist the torque reaction of the engine under full load
  • Absorb vibration without cracking at weld points
  • Handle a lift by 2 people or a small crane at the balance point

Petrol-specific notes

  • Petrol engines are lighter (80–150 kg for 1.6–2.0 L)
  • Less torque reaction — lighter frame OK
  • More vibration at high RPM — need softer rubber mounts
  • Fuel tank must be well-vented (petrol vapour)

Diesel-specific notes

  • Diesel engines are heavier (150–250 kg for 2.5–3.0 L)
  • Much higher torque reaction at low RPM — need stronger frame
  • Less vibration at working speed — harder rubber mounts OK
  • Fuel tank can be simpler (diesel is less volatile)
The layout rule: Sketch your layout on paper before buying steel. Mark the engine, pump, radiator, fuel tank, battery, and control panel. Measure every dimension. If it does not fit on paper, it will not fit in steel.
Practical exercise 7.1: Sketch your frame layout to scale on paper. Mark all components. Note every dimension. Use this drawing to order steel.

For a diesel build over 3.0 L, use the heavier sizing. Torque reaction from a large diesel can twist a light frame on startup.

Never use thin-wall steel tube for the main rails. It looks strong but collapses under load. Minimum 5 mm wall for angle iron, 3 mm for square tube used as the main rail. If in doubt, go heavier.
Practical exercise 7.2: Take your drawing to 2 steel merchants. Get quotes for the full material list. Ask them to cut to length if possible (small fee, saves you work).

Lesson 7.3 — Cutting steel — precise measurements

  1. Measure twice, cut once. Mark every cut with a permanent marker or soapstone. Use a square to check the line is true.
  2. Cut with an angle grinder (metal cutting disc, 1 mm or 3 mm thick). Or use a chop saw for cleaner cuts.
  3. Deburr every cut with a grinding wheel. Sharp edges cut skin and resist welding.
  4. Lay out all pieces on a flat concrete floor before welding. Check the assembly matches your drawing.
  5. Number each piece with a marker, matching your drawing. This prevents mix-ups during welding.
  6. Check dimensions of the assembled rails. Diagonal measurements must be equal — this proves it is square.

Square check: measure from one corner to the opposite corner of the frame. Then measure the other two corners. The two numbers must be identical (within 2 mm). If not, the frame is not square — adjust before welding.

The cut rule: Cut all pieces first, then weld. Do not cut-weld-cut-weld. Measure everything, cut everything, check fit, then weld. This saves time and prevents mistakes.
Practical exercise 7.3: Cut all frame pieces to length. Lay them out on the ground. Check square by measuring diagonals. Only proceed to welding when square.

Lesson 7.4 — Welding the frame — order matters

Welding is where the frame becomes rigid. Order determines whether it stays square.

  1. Tack-weld the corners only — one small weld (10 mm) at each joint. Do not weld the whole joint yet.
  2. Check square again. The frame will move slightly when tacked. Adjust if needed.
  3. Tack-weld the cross-braces in place. Check square again.
  4. Weld the main frame corners — full welds at each joint. Alternate sides to prevent warping (weld one corner, then the opposite corner).
  5. Weld the cross-braces — full welds at both ends of each brace.
  6. Grind the welds smooth on the outside for a clean finish.
  7. Fit and weld the mounting plates. These are the drilled plates where the engine and pump will bolt down.
  8. Fit and weld the radiator bracket. Position must allow airflow in front, hose routing at the bottom.
  9. Fit and weld the fuel tank platform (if tank mounted on the frame).
  10. Fit and weld the control panel bracket — where you will mount the ignition switch, start button, gauges, and kill switch.
  11. Fit and weld the lifting eyes — 2 or 4 lifting points at the balance point.
  12. Final check. Everything welded, square, and level.

Petrol build

  • Lighter mounting plates OK (8 mm plate)
  • Mount locations for: coil, distributor, fuel pump
  • Battery tray for smaller battery (35–55 Ah typical)

Diesel build

  • Heavier mounting plates (10 mm plate)
  • Mount for lift pump and primary fuel filter
  • Water separator for diesel (essential)
  • Battery tray for larger battery (75–100 Ah typical)
  • Glow plug timer relay bracket
Never weld near fuel lines or a fuel tank. Remove the fuel tank and any fuel-carrying component from the frame before welding. Welding sparks can ignite fuel vapour even at some distance. This is a life-safety rule.
Practical exercise 7.4: Complete the full welding sequence. Then check the frame with a spirit level and a tape. Correct any warp before painting.

Lesson 7.5 — Engine mounts and vibration isolation

The engine must sit on rubber mounts. Solid mounting destroys the frame and the engine.

Mount typeUseCost
Original car mountsBest — designed for the engineReuse ($0)
Universal rubber bobbin mountAftermarket, general purpose$5–$15 each
Captive rubber mountPrevents engine lifting off under load$10–$25 each
Marine engine mountsHigh-end, excellent isolation$30–$80 each

Fitting order:

  1. Position engine on the frame with a crane or 4 helpers.
  2. Mark the mount hole locations on the frame.
  3. Lift the engine off and drill the frame holes at the marked locations.
  4. Fit the mounts to the engine first (using original bolt pattern).
  5. Lower the engine so the mounts rest on the frame.
  6. Bolt through the frame with grade 8.8 bolts and nyloc nuts.
  7. Check that mounts are level. Shim if needed.
  8. Tighten to spec (typically 40–60 Nm for M10–M12 bolts).
  9. Test by hand: rock the engine gently. It should move 3–5 mm against the rubber, not solid.

Petrol engines

Petrol engines vibrate at higher frequency. Use softer rubber mounts for better isolation.

Diesel engines

Diesel engines shake harder at low RPM. Heavier rubber mounts handle this better. Consider captive mounts to prevent engine lift under load.

The mount rule: Always use rubber. Always use all original mount points. Always torque to spec. A solid-mounted engine cracks the frame within 100 hours of running.
Practical exercise 7.5: Source rubber mounts (reuse if possible). Bolt the engine down. Check for solid contact at any point — there should be none. All contact through rubber.

Lesson 7.6 — Painting and rust protection

Bare steel rusts in weeks. A painted frame lasts 15–20 years.

  1. Clean the frame — remove all welding slag, dust, and grease with a wire brush and degreaser.
  2. Sand any rough spots with 80-grit sandpaper.
  3. Wipe down with a clean cloth and mineral spirits. Let dry.
  4. Apply a primer coat. Zinc-rich primer is best. Spray or brush on evenly. Let dry per label.
  5. Apply 2 top coats of industrial enamel or vehicle paint. Colour is your choice — yellow is common for visibility, red for safety, black or dark grey for classic look.
  6. Let dry fully (24–48 hours depending on paint).
  7. Touch up around bolt holes after assembly to prevent rust at edges.

Rust protection checklist:

  • Primer before topcoat — never skip
  • Two topcoats minimum for outdoor use
  • Include a third coat at weld joints and corners
  • Paint inside the frame rails if accessible
  • Annual touch-up of any rust spots during maintenance
Practical exercise 7.6: Paint your frame completely. Total paint cost: $15–$40. Total time: 4–6 hours including drying. Do this before installing engine and pump — it is much harder to paint once everything is on.
