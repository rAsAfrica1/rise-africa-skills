1. Temperature and Humidity Control

Oyster and button mushrooms need steady temperatures between 20C and 25C for spawn run and 15C to 20C for fruiting. In hot African climates, you can cool a grow room by hanging wet burlap sacks on the walls and using a fan to blow air over them. This evaporative cooling can drop the temperature by 5C to 8C. For every 1000 bags, use at least one 60-watt fan for air movement. To raise temperature in cold highlands, use a small charcoal stove outside the room with a pipe to bring warm air in, but never let smoke enter.

Humidity must stay at 85% to 95% during fruiting. A simple hygrometer costs about $5 and is essential. If humidity is low, mist the floor and walls with clean water using a knapsack sprayer. Do not spray directly on mushrooms as it causes rot. If humidity is too high, increase ventilation. You can make a cheap evaporative cooler by placing a porous sack over a fan and dripping water on it. In dry Sahel regions, hang wet cloths and place shallow pans of water on the floor.

Monitor temperature and humidity at least three times a day: morning, midday, and evening. Record readings in a notebook. If temperature goes above 28C, mushrooms may abort or die. If below 10C, growth stops. Adjust by opening or closing vents, using fans, or adding a small heater. For small-scale farms, a digital thermometer-hygrometer combo costs $10 and gives accurate readings. Keep it at mushroom level, not near the door.

In Africa, many farmers use thatched roofs which naturally insulate. If you use a polythene grow house, add a shade net on top to reduce heat. In the evening, open vents to let cool air in. Always ensure fresh air exchange because high CO2 reduces humidity control. With practice, you will learn to read signs: mushrooms with long stems and small caps mean high CO2 and low light; dry caps mean low humidity. Adjust daily.

2. CO2 and Fresh Air Exchange

Mushrooms breathe like humans: they take in oxygen and release carbon dioxide (CO2). During spawn run, CO2 levels can be high (up to 20,000 ppm) without harm, but during fruiting, CO2 must stay below 1000 ppm. Above 1000 ppm, you get long stems and tiny caps. Above 5000 ppm, fruiting stops. You cannot see or smell CO2, so you need a CO2 monitor. A basic handheld CO2 meter costs $100-$200, but for small farms, you can use a simple test: if you feel dizzy or the air smells stale, CO2 is too high. Better to buy a monitor if you can afford it.

Fresh air exchange (FAE) is the method to lower CO2. For a 3m x 4m grow room, install two 30cm exhaust fans: one low on one wall, one high on the opposite wall. Run them for 5 minutes every hour. This replaces the air without drying out the room too much. In African settings, you can use a chimney effect: a vertical pipe from the floor to the roof with a cap that opens. Hot air rises and pulls fresh air in through low vents. This works well in hot climates.

Do not ventilate too much or humidity drops. Balance is key. During fruiting, aim for 4 to 6 air exchanges per hour. You can test with a smoke pencil or incense stick: hold it near the exhaust and see how fast smoke is pulled out. If it takes more than 30 seconds to clear, increase fan speed or open more vents. For small operations, simply opening a door and using a fan for 10 minutes every 2 hours can work.

Monitor CO2 with a monitor placed at mushroom level. If you cannot afford one, watch the mushrooms: if they have thin, long stems and small caps, CO2 is too high. If caps are dry and cracked, FAE is too high and humidity is low. Adjust by running fans less or more. In humid coastal areas, you may need more FAE to prevent mold. In dry areas, reduce FAE and add humidity. Always keep a log of fan times and CO2 readings to learn your room's pattern.

3. Light Cycles for Fruiting

Mushrooms do not need light to grow, but they need light to know when to fruit. Most species, like oyster and shiitake, need a light cycle of 12 hours on and 12 hours off to trigger fruiting. The light does not need to be strong: a 20-watt fluorescent tube or a 10-watt LED bulb per 4 square meters is enough. Direct sunlight is harmful because it dries the mushrooms and raises temperature. Use indirect natural light from a window or a shaded area, or artificial light on a timer.

In Africa, many grow houses use natural light from translucent roof sheets. If you use a dark room, install a timer switch (costs $10) to turn lights on and off. Set it for 6am to 6pm. If you cannot afford a timer, do it manually at the same time each day. Consistency matters more than exact timing. During spawn run, keep the room dark or in low light because light can cause premature pinning. Cover windows with black plastic or cloth.

For fruiting, blue light (wavelength 450-495 nm) works best. You can buy blue LED strips for $5 per meter. Place them 1.5 meters above the bags. Do not use incandescent bulbs because they produce heat and waste electricity. If using natural light, ensure it is not direct sun. A simple test: if you can read a newspaper comfortably, the light is enough. If you need to squint, it is too bright.

Monitor light with a cheap lux meter ($20) or a smartphone app. Aim for 200-500 lux at mushroom level. Too little light gives long stems and pale caps. Too much light dries the surface. In hot climates, use light during the cooler evening hours to avoid heat. Always combine light with proper humidity and CO2 control. If you see mushrooms leaning toward the light, they are getting too little from one side; rotate bags or add a second light source. Keep a record of light hours and fruiting response to find your best cycle.

4. Monitoring Equipment and Sensors

You cannot manage what you do not measure. Start with three basic tools: a digital thermometer-hygrometer ($10), a CO2 monitor ($100-$200), and a light meter ($20 or a free smartphone app). For small farms, a simple min-max thermometer ($5) tells you the highest and lowest temperatures since you last checked. Place sensors at mushroom level, about 30cm above the bags, not near doors or fans. Calibrate your hygrometer using the salt method: put it in a sealed container with a cup of salt and a little water for 12 hours; it should read 75%. If not, adjust or replace.

For larger farms (over 1000 bags), invest in a data logger ($50-$150) that records temperature, humidity, and CO2 every 30 minutes. You can download data to a phone or computer. This helps you see patterns at night when you are asleep. In Africa, power cuts are common, so choose a logger with battery backup. Some sensors connect to a GSM module and send SMS alerts if conditions go out of range. That costs about $200 but saves crops.

Do not rely on one sensor. Use at least two: one in the center, one near the edge. If they differ by more than 2C or 10% humidity, you have uneven conditions. Fix by adding fans or rearranging bags. For CO2, a handheld meter is fine for spot checks. If you cannot afford one, use a chemical CO2 test kit ($30) that changes color. For light, a lux meter app like 'Lux Light Meter' works on most smartphones. Check monthly for accuracy.

Keep sensors clean. Dust and spores can block readings. Wipe with a damp cloth weekly. Replace batteries every 3 months. Store your equipment in a dry box with silica gel when not in use. Train one person to be responsible for daily readings and equipment care. In humid rooms, use a plastic bag with a hole for the sensor to protect the electronics. Always record readings in a logbook immediately. If a sensor fails, you still have your eyes and nose: smell for stale air, feel for dampness, look at mushroom shape. But sensors give you early warning before problems appear.

5. Automation Systems

Automation saves time and prevents human error. Start small: a timer plug ($10) for your lights and fans is the simplest automation. Set lights for 12 hours on, 12 off. Set exhaust fans for 5 minutes every hour during fruiting. For humidity, a humidistat ($30) can turn on a misting system when humidity drops below 85%. You can build a simple misting system with a water pump ($20), a timer, and micro-sprinklers ($10). Use a 12V pump connected to a car battery for areas with unreliable power. A float valve in a reservoir keeps water topped up.

For temperature control, a thermostat ($25) can turn on a fan when it gets too hot or a heater when too cold. In hot African regions, a thermostat controlling an evaporative cooler (wet pad + fan) works well. For cold highlands, a thermostat can turn on a small electric heater or a charcoal stove with a fan. Always use a relay if switching high-power devices. If you are not confident with wiring, hire an electrician. Do not overload circuits.

For advanced farmers, an Arduino or Raspberry Pi kit ($50-$100) can monitor temperature, humidity, CO2, and light, and control fans, lights, and pumps. You need basic coding or a pre-made script. Many free tutorials exist online. A GSM module ($30) can send SMS alerts if conditions go wrong. This is useful if you live far from the farm. But keep it simple: a failed automation can kill your crop faster than manual control. Always have a manual override.

Maintenance is critical. Check timers monthly to ensure they are still set correctly. Clean sensors and misting nozzles with vinegar every 2 weeks to remove mineral buildup. Test your backup battery monthly. Keep spare parts: a spare timer, pump, and sensor. In dusty areas, cover control boxes with a cloth. Write down your automation settings and keep them in a safe place. Train a worker to check the system daily. If power is unstable, use a surge protector ($15). Start with one automated function, master it, then add another. Do not automate everything at once.

6. Record Keeping and Data Logging

Good records turn a hobby into a business. Use a simple notebook or a spreadsheet on your phone. For each day, record: date, time, temperature, humidity, CO2 (if available), light hours, fan on/off times, any misting, and observations like pinning, harvest weight, or problems. Write down everything. A 5-minute daily entry will show you patterns. For example, you might notice that humidity drops every afternoon when the sun hits the roof. Then you can add shade or mist more at that time.

Use a standard form. Draw columns in your notebook: Date | Time | Temp | Hum | CO2 | Light | Actions | Notes. Fill it in three times a day: morning, midday, evening. At the end of each week, review. Calculate averages and ranges. If temperature varies more than 5C in a day, you need better insulation or automation. If humidity drops below 80% regularly, add more misting. Keep a separate harvest log: date, number of bags harvested, total weight, and quality grade. This tells you which conditions give the best yield.

For multiple grow rooms, use a whiteboard or a shared spreadsheet. Label each room. In Africa, many farmers use WhatsApp groups to share daily readings with a mentor. Take a photo of your log and send it. You can also use free apps like Google Sheets on a smartphone. If you have no smartphone, use a paper log and transfer to a computer at a local internet caf once a week. Store logs in a dry place. Keep them for at least one year to compare seasons.

Data logging with a sensor logger is even better. Set it to record every 30 minutes. Download weekly. Look for spikes at night or during power cuts. If you see a problem, fix it and note the change. For example, if CO2 spikes every night when fans are off, set a timer to run fans for 5 minutes every 2 hours at night. Always write down what you changed and when. This is your farm's memory. Share your records with other farmers to learn together. At the end of each crop cycle, review all data and decide what to improve for next time. Good records lead to better mushrooms and more profit.

<h2>Practical Reference: Environmental Control and Monitoring for Mushroom Farming</h2>

<p>In mushroom farming, your crop is your climate. Unlike field crops that can tolerate swings in weather, <em>Pleurotus</em> (oyster), <em>Lentinula</em> (shiitake), and <em>Agaricus</em> (button) mushrooms demand tight control of temperature, humidity, carbon dioxide (CO), fresh air exchange, and light. For African agribusinesses whether you are fruiting oyster mushrooms in a converted shipping container in Lagos, a mud-brick grow house in Kigali, or a polytunnel in Nairobi the difference between profit and loss is often a few degrees Celsius or a few hundred parts per million of CO.</p>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the numbers, equipment, and record-keeping practices you need. Treat it as your operating manual: print it, laminate it, and keep it inside the grow room.</p>

<h3>1. Temperature and Humidity Setpoints</h3>

<p>Temperature and relative humidity (RH) are the two variables you will adjust most often. During <strong>colonisation</strong> (spawn run), mushrooms prefer warmer, more humid conditions; during <strong>fruiting</strong>, they need a cooler, slightly drier trigger. Getting this shift right is what signals the mycelium to pin.</p>

<h3>2. CO and Fresh Air Exchange</h3>

<p>Mushrooms breathe. During colonisation, CO can safely rise to 5,00010,000 ppm because it suppresses competing moulds. During fruiting, however, CO above 1,000 ppm produces long, thin stems and small caps a classic sign of poor fresh air exchange (FAE). In African grow rooms, FAE is often the most neglected parameter because farmers focus on humidity and forget that air must be <em>exchanged</em>, not just humidified.</p>

<h3>3. Light Cycles for Fruiting</h3>

<p>Mushrooms do not photosynthesise, but they do need light as a <em>signal</em> for direction and pin formation. Oyster and shiitake mushrooms fruit best with 12 hours of indirect light at 2001,000 lux (roughly the light of a dimly lit room). Direct sunlight is harmful: it dries the substrate and raises temperature. In African grow rooms, a simple 12-hour LED or fluorescent cycle, or natural daylight through a shaded window, is sufficient. Button mushrooms are more tolerant of low light and can fruit in near-darkness.</p>

<h3>4. Monitoring Equipment and Sensors</h3>

<p>You cannot manage what you do not measure. A basic monitoring kit for an African mushroom farm includes:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Digital thermo-hygrometer</strong> (US$1025): measures temperature and RH. Place at fruiting height, not near the door.</li>
<li><strong>CO monitor</strong> (US$80200): essential for commercial fruiting rooms. Handheld or wall-mounted.</li>
<li><strong>Data logger</strong> (US$30150): records readings every 1560 minutes to SD card or cloud.</li>
<li><strong>Light meter</strong> (US$2050): confirms lux levels; smartphone apps can approximate.</li>
<li><strong>Thermostat/hygrostat controller</strong> (US$40120): switches fans and humidifiers automatically.</li>
</ul>

<p>For low-cost setups, the Arduino-based <em>OpenMushroom</em> or Raspberry Pi with DHT22 and MH-Z19 sensors can be built for under US$60 and is widely documented in open-source communities. In areas with unreliable grid power, pair sensors with a small solar panel and battery.</p>

<h3>5. Automation Systems</h3>

<p>Automation reduces labour and prevents human error. A basic automated grow room uses:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Temperature controller</strong> switches exhaust fan or evaporative cooler.</li>
<li><strong>Humidity controller</strong> switches ultrasonic fogger or misting system.</li>
<li><strong>CO controller</strong> switches fresh air fan.</li>
<li><strong>Timer</strong> controls light cycle and fan intervals.</li>
</ul>
<p>In Kenya, Uganda, and Nigeria, solar-powered controllers are increasingly common because they buffer against power cuts. A simple rule: automate the fan and humidifier first, then add CO and light control as revenue allows.</p>

<h3>6. Record Keeping and Data Logging</h3>

<p>Records turn a hobby into a business. Every grow room should maintain:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Daily log:</strong> date, time, temperature, RH, CO, light hours, fan/humidifier status.</li>
<li><strong>Batch log:</strong> substrate type, spawn date, flush dates, yield per bag, contamination events.</li>
<li><strong>Input log:</strong> water, electricity, labour, and consumables per batch.</li>
<li><strong>Sales log:</strong> buyer, quantity, price, and channel.</li>
</ul>
<p>Use a simple notebook or a free spreadsheet template (Google Sheets, LibreOffice Calc). For commercial farms, a cloud logger that sends SMS alerts when temperature exceeds setpoints is worth the investment. Data lets you calculate cost per kilogram, identify your best-performing substrate, and prove consistency to buyers a key requirement for supplying supermarkets and hotels.</p>

<p style="margin:8px 0 0;color:#7f1d1d;">High CO levels in sealed grow rooms can cause dizziness, headaches, and unconsciousness. Never enter a room with CO above 5,000 ppm without ventilation. Always install a CO alarm if using sealed rooms. In African conditions with frequent power surges, use a surge protector on controllers and data loggers.</p>

<ol>
<li>Check temperature and RH at least twice daily morning and evening.</li>
<li>Keep fruiting room at 1822 C and 8590 % RH for oyster mushrooms.</li>
<li>Maintain CO below 1,000 ppm during fruiting; below 800 ppm for premium quality.</li>
<li>Run exhaust fan for 610 air exchanges per hour during fruiting.</li>
