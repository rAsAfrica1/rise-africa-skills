1. Designing and Fitting a Guard

A guard protects your hand and gives the knife balance. Start with mild steel or brass, 35 mm thick. For a hidden-tang knife, you need a slot that matches the tang. Measure the tang width and thickness with calipers. Transfer these to the guard stock. Drill a series of holes along the slot outline, then file to shape. Use a chainsaw file for tight corners. The fit should be snugtap it on with a mallet, not force it. For a stick tang, you'll need a smaller slot. In Africa, many smiths use recycled car leaf springs for guards. They're tough and take a good finish. If you're using brass, it's softer and easier to file. Always wear safety glasses when filing. Test the fit by sliding the guard onto the tang. It should go on with light taps and stay in place. If it's too tight, file a little more. Too loose, and you'll need to peen the tang or use solder. For a full-tang knife, the guard is often a piece of metal sandwiched between the scales. In that case, you drill holes for pins. The guard should be slightly wider than the blade to protect your fingers. A good rule: guard width = blade width + 10 mm. Once fitted, mark the blade where the guard sits. You'll need to file a shoulder on the blade for the guard to sit against. This prevents the guard from sliding up. Use a file guide to keep the shoulder square. Take your timea poorly fitted guard ruins the look of the knife. After fitting, you can shape the guard. Round the edges with a file, then sand to 400 grit. Leave the final polish until after assembly. Remember, the guard is a safety feature. It must be strong. If you're unsure, make it thicker. A 5 mm guard is better than a 3 mm one that bends.

2. Soldering and Pinning Guards

Once your guard fits snugly, you need to secure it. Two methods: soldering and pinning. Soldering is common for hidden tangs. Use lead-free silver solder (like Stay-Brite) and flux. Clean the tang and guard slot with sandpaper and acetone. Apply flux to both parts. Heat the guard with a propane torch until the flux sizzles. Touch the solder to the jointit should flow like water. Don't overheat; brass can melt. For a strong joint, use a soldering pick to draw the solder into the seam. Let it cool, then file off excess. If you don't have silver solder, you can use electrical solder, but it's weaker. Pinning is for full-tang knives or guards with a hole. Drill a hole through the guard and tang (if full tang) or through the guard and a stub tang. Use a drill press for accuracy. Pin material: brass rod, copper, or mild steel. Cut a pin slightly longer than the hole. Peen one end, insert, then peen the other. File flush. For a hidden tang, you can pin through the guard and tang if the tang is thick enough. In Africa, many smiths use copper wire from old electrical cables for pins. It's soft and easy to peen. Always countersink the holes slightly so the peened pin locks in. After pinning, file the pin flush with the guard. Then sand the guard to 400 grit. Check for gapsif you see light through the joint, add more solder or a second pin. For a really strong guard, combine both: pin it, then solder. That's how many traditional African knives are made. Practice on scrap pieces first. Soldering takes finesse. Keep your torch moving. And always work in a ventilated areaflux fumes are toxic.

3. Creating Bolsters from Metal and Micarta

A bolster is a thick piece of metal or micarta at the front of the handle, behind the guard. It adds strength and looks. For metal bolsters, use brass, copper, or stainless steel. 610 mm thick is good. Cut a piece slightly larger than the handle. For a hidden tang, you need a slot. For a full tang, you can use two pieces (one on each side) or one piece with a slot. Micarta bolsters are lighter and easier to shape. You can make micarta from denim or canvas and epoxy. Cut layers, soak in epoxy, clamp between boards, and let cure for 24 hours. Then cut and shape like wood. For metal, start by drilling a series of holes for the slot. Use a file to connect them. Fit the bolster to the tang. It should be tight. If using micarta, you can drill and file the same way. Shape the bolster with a belt grinder or files. Round the front edge so it flows into the guard. A common shape is a rounded rectangle. Sand to 400 grit. For a polished look, go to 800 then buff. But remember, bolsters take a beating. A satin finish (400 grit) hides scratches better. In Africa, many smiths use hardwood bolsters, but micarta is more durable. If you use metal, make sure it's corrosion-resistant. Brass tarnishes; stainless is better for humid climates. You can also use a bolster to hide a poorly fitted guard. Just make it slightly larger. When fitting, tap it on with a mallet. If it's too tight, file the slot. Too loose, and you'll need to peen the tang or use epoxy. For a full-tang knife, you can pin the bolster through the tang. Two pins are enough. Countersink the holes. After assembly, file the pins flush. Then sand the bolster and handle together for a seamless transition.

4. Pommel Construction and Installation

The pommel is the cap at the end of the handle. It balances the knife and can be decorative. For a hidden tang, the pommel is often a metal block with a hole for the tang. The tang goes through, and you peen it over the pommel. That's called a peened pommel. Start with a piece of brass or steel, 1015 mm thick. Drill a hole that matches the tang. Slide it on. The tang should stick out about 35 mm. Heat the tang with a torch until red, then let it cool slowly. This anneals it so it's soft. Then peen the end with a ball-peen hammer. Work around the edges to mushroom it over the pommel. File flush. For a full-tang knife, the pommel is often part of the handle material. You can add a metal butt cap. Cut a piece of metal, shape it to match the handle, and epoxy and pin it. Another option: a threaded pommel. Weld a threaded rod to the tang, then screw on a nut or decorative knob. That's common on tactical knives. For a stick tang, you can use a pommel that's pinned. Drill through the pommel and tang, then pin. In Africa, many traditional knives have a simple pommel: a piece of hardwood or bone. But metal is more durable. When installing, make sure the pommel is square to the handle. If it's crooked, the knife looks bad. Use a file to adjust. After installation, shape the pommel to match the handle. Round the edges. Sand to 400 grit. If you peened the tang, file the peened area smooth. Then polish. A good pommel should feel comfortable in your palm. It shouldn't have sharp edges. Test the balance: the knife should balance at the index finger for a chef's knife, or at the guard for a fighter. You can adjust by making the pommel heavier or lighter. A heavier pommel moves the balance back.

5. Finishing Metal Furniture

Metal furnitureguards, bolsters, pommelsneeds finishing to look good and resist rust. Start with files: cross-cut for rough shaping, then mill file for smoothing. Then sandpaper. For brass, start at 220 grit, then 400, 600, 800, 1000, 1500, 2000. Sand in one direction, then change direction 90 degrees with each grit. That removes scratches. For a mirror polish, use a buffing wheel with white rouge. But mirror finishes show fingerprints and scratches. A satin finish (400600 grit) is more practical. For steel, same process. If you want a brushed finish, use a Scotch-Brite belt on a belt grinder. For a rustic look, leave it at 220 grit. After sanding, clean with acetone. Then apply a protective coat. For brass, lacquer prevents tarnish. For steel, oil or wax. In humid African climates, rust is a problem. So use stainless steel if you can. If not, keep the metal oiled. You can also blue or parkerize steel. Cold bluing is available in small bottles. It's not as durable as hot bluing, but it works. For a traditional look, leave the metal to patina naturally. Many African smiths prefer a dark patina from use. To get an even patina, soak in vinegar or lemon juice. But that can pit the metal. Test on scrap first. When finishing, don't forget the inside of the guard slot. Sand it as best you can. A clean slot means the guard fits better. After finishing, assemble the knife. If you used epoxy, clean up squeeze-out with acetone before it cures. Then do a final polish on the whole knife. A finished knife should have no sharp edges on the furniture. Run your finger along the guardit should be smooth. That's the mark of a professional.

6. Assembling a Full-Tang Knife with Bolsters

Full-tang knives are strong and popular. Assembly is straightforward but needs care. Start with your blade, which should be heat-treated and finished. The tang should be flat and clean. Your handle scales: wood, micarta, or bone. Cut them slightly oversized. For bolsters, you'll have two pieces (one per side) or one piece with a slot. Fit the bolsters first. They should sit flush against the blade's ricasso. If there's a gap, file the front of the scales or the back of the bolster. Use a file guide to keep things square. Once the bolsters fit, drill holes for pins through the bolster and tang. Use a drill press. Clamp everything. Drill one hole, insert a pin, then drill the next. That keeps alignment. Do the same for the handle scales. You'll need holes for pins and maybe a lanyard tube. Countersink all holes slightly. Now mix epoxy. Use a slow-cure epoxy (like West System) for strength. Coat the tang, the inside of the scales, and the bolsters. Assemble: bolsters first, then scales. Insert pins. Clamp with spring clamps or a vise. Don't over-tightenyou'll squeeze out too much epoxy. Wipe off squeeze-out with acetone. Let cure for 24 hours. After curing, shape the handle. Use a belt grinder or files. Work the bolsters and scales together so they blend. Don't create a step. Sand progressively: 80, 120, 220, 400. Then finish to your desired grit. For a durable finish, use tung oil or Danish oil on wood. For micarta, sand to 600 and buff. For a full-tang knife, you can also peen the pins. That adds mechanical strength. Peen one side, then the other. File flush. Then do final sanding. Test the knife: it should feel balanced and comfortable. The bolsters should be smooth. The handle should have no gaps. If you see gaps, fill with epoxy mixed with sawdust. Let cure, then sand. That's how you assemble a full-tang knife with bolsters. Practice on a cheap blade first. It takes time, but the result is a knife that will last a lifetime.

<h2>Practical Reference: Guards, Bolsters, and Pommels for the African Bladesmith</h2>

<p>In the African custom knife market, the handle furniture is where a blade stops being a tool and becomes a product. A hunter in Namibia will pay a premium for a knife that feels balanced and will not loosen after a season in the bush. A collector in Lagos or Nairobi buys the fit and finish first, the steel second. For the working smith, guards, bolsters, and pommels are also a business decision: they add 3060% to the labour time of a knife but can double or triple the selling price, and they are the single biggest source of warranty returns when done badly.</p>

<p>This reference covers the six core skills in the module: designing and fitting a guard, soldering and pinning guards, creating bolsters from metal and Micarta, pommel construction and installation, finishing metal furniture, and assembling a full-tang knife with bolsters. It assumes you are working from a small workshop with a forge or belt grinder, hand tools, and access to either imported 416 stainless or locally available brass, copper, and mild steel.</p>

<h3>1. Designing and Fitting a Guard</h3>

<p>The guard protects the hand and locks the handle to the tang. In the African market, the two dominant styles are the double guard (common on Bowies and hunting knives sold to safari clients) and the single finger guard (common on working knives for farmers and butchers). Design rule: the guard slot must be cut 0.050.1 mm undersize relative to the tang, then filed to a press fit. A guard that slides on with finger pressure will loosen with humidity changes, and in coastal markets like Mombasa or Durban, that happens within weeks.</p>

<p>Layout the slot with a height gauge and scribe lines on both sides. Drill a series of holes along the slot, then file to the line with a pillar file. Check fit with layout dye: the tang should scrape the dye evenly. For a hidden-tang or stick-tang knife, the guard is usually slotted and the tang passes through; for a full-tang knife, the guard is often a separate block that butts against the ricasso.</p>

<h3>2. Soldering and Pinning Guards</h3>

<p>A press-fit guard still needs mechanical and chemical locking. The standard sequence is: fit, flux, pin, solder, clean. For guards, use a low-temperature silver-bearing solder (typically 46% silver, melting around 220250C) or a lead-free plumbing solder where food contact is a concern. High-temperature silver solder (45%+ silver) is reserved for stainless-to-stainless joints where corrosion resistance matters.</p>

<p>Pin the guard with 1.52 mm brass or 416 stainless pins, drilled through the guard and tang, then peened. Two pins are standard for a double guard; one pin plus solder is acceptable for a small finger guard. Solder flows by capillary action into the joint; keep the heat moving and stop as soon as the solder flashes. Overheating will draw the temper from the tang and can discolour the blade.</p>

<p>After soldering, file the joint flush and inspect under bright light. A hairline gap that is invisible indoors will show as a dark line once the knife is finished. In humid African markets, that gap becomes a rust starter.</p>

<h3>3. Creating Bolsters from Metal and Micarta</h3>

<p>Bolsters are the front and rear blocks that reinforce the handle. Metal bolsters (brass, copper, 416 stainless, mild steel) are traditional and command higher prices. Micarta bolsters are lighter, corrosion-proof, and increasingly popular for working knives sold to farmers, butchers, and outdoor guides.</p>

<p>For metal bolsters, cut oversize blocks, drill pin holes, and shape with files and a belt grinder. For Micarta, stack layers with epoxy, clamp, cure 24 hours, then cut and shape the same way. Micarta dust is a respiratory hazardsee the safety box below.</p>

<h3>4. Pommel Construction and Installation</h3>

<p>The pommel closes the handle and provides the rear balance point. Three common constructions:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Pin-through pommel:</strong> a metal block drilled and pinned through the tang. Strongest, simplest, most common on full-tang knives.</li>
<li><strong>Threaded pommel:</strong> the tang is threaded and the pommel screws on. Allows field disassembly but requires precise threading and a locking mechanism.</li>
<li><strong>Hidden-tang pommel:</strong> the tang is inserted into a drilled pommel block and epoxied. Used on stick-tang knives.</li>
</ul>

<p>For a full-tang knife, the pommel is usually a metal or Micarta block pinned through the tang with two pins and epoxied. The rear pin should be at least 8 mm from the pommel edge to avoid tear-out. File the pommel flush with the handle scales, then shape the rear contourrounded for hunters, flat for tactical, angled for chef's knives.</p>

<h3>5. Finishing Metal Furniture</h3>

<p>Finishing is where the price is made. The sequence for brass, copper, and stainless is: file to shape, sand progressively (220, 400, 800, 1200 grit), then polish with a stitched buff and compound. For a satin finish, stop at 800 grit and use a Scotch-Brite belt. For a mirror finish, go to 2000 grit then buff with white compound.</p>

<p>Stainless 416 work-hardens; use sharp belts and light pressure. Brass and copper are soft and load beltsuse a belt lubricant or wax. After polishing, degrease with acetone and apply a thin coat of Renaissance wax or a locally available microcrystalline wax to slow tarnish in humid climates.</p>

<strong style="color:#b00020"> SAFETY WARNING Micarta and metal dust</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0">Micarta contains phenolic resin and fibres. Grinding and sanding Micarta produces fine dust that is a respiratory and skin irritant. Always wear a P2/N95 respirator, eye protection, and gloves when shaping Micarta. Work in a ventilated area or use a dust extractor. Never blow Micarta dust with compressed airit will aerosolise into the breathing zone. The same rule applies to brass and copper dust, which are toxic if inhaled over time. In small African workshops without extraction, work outdoors or with a fan moving air away from your face, and wash hands and forearms before eating or smoking.</p>
</div>

<h3>6. Assembling a Full-Tang Knife with Bolsters</h3>

<p>The full-tang assembly sequence, in order:</p>
<ol>
<li>Finish the blade to 90%all grinding done, but leave final sharpening until after assembly.</li>
<li>Drill tang holes for pins. Countersink slightly on both sides for epoxy keys.</li>
<li>Fit and solder the guard. Clean up the joint.</li>
<li>Fit the front bolster. Pin and epoxy in place. Clean up.</li>
<li>Prepare handle scales. Drill matching pin holes.</li>
<li>Mix epoxy (slow-cure, 24-hour, not 5-minute). Coat tang and scale mating surfaces.</li>
<li>Insert pins, clamp, and cure 24 hours at room temperature. In hot African climates, cure in the shadedirect sun can cause epoxy to bubble.</li>
<li>Shape handle and bolsters together with files and belts. This is where the transition lines are set.</li>
<li>Fit the pommel. Pin and epoxy.</li>
<li>Final sanding and finishing of all furniture.</li>
<li>Final sharpening and edge finishing.</li>
<li>Clean, wax, and photograph for sale.</li>
</ol>

<p>Business note: a full-tang knife with bolsters and a soldered guard takes roughly 814 hours of labour for a competent smith. In the East African custom market, such a knife sells for 38 times the price of a simple stick-tang working knife. The furniture is the margin.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li>Guard slot cut 0.050.1 mm undersize; press fit confirmed with layout dye.</li>
<li>Guard pinned with at least one 1.52 mm pin before soldering.</li>
<li>Solder flashed quickly; no prolonged heat on the tang.</li>
<li>Bolster blocks drilled, pinned, and epoxied; no gaps at the ricasso.</li>
<li>Handle scales drilled to match tang holes; epoxy is slow-cure, not 5-minute.</li>
<li>Pommel pins at least 8 mm from the pommel edge.</li>
<li>All furniture sanded through the correct grit sequence for its material.</li>
<li>Final finish waxed; knife photographed and logged for sale.</li>
</ul>
