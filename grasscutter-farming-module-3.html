1. Natural Diet of Grasscutters

Grasscutters are strict herbivores. In the wild, they eat a wide variety of grasses, leaves, roots, fruits, and bark. They are not rodents that eat meat or insects. Their natural diet is high in fibre and low in fat. In Africa, common wild foods include elephant grass, guinea grass, cassava leaves, sweet potato leaves, banana leaves, and the bark of certain trees. They also eat fruits like mango, pawpaw, and pineapple when available. Grasscutters are crepuscular, meaning they feed mainly at dawn and dusk. This is when they are most active and when you should offer most of their food. They have a large caecum (a pouch in the gut) that ferments fibre, similar to a rabbit. This means they need a constant supply of roughage to keep their gut healthy. If you feed too much soft, starchy food, they can get diarrhoea and die. In a farm setting, you must mimic this natural diet. Offer a mix of fresh grasses, legumes, and kitchen vegetables. Avoid mouldy or sprayed plants. Collect forage from clean areas away from roadsides where exhaust fumes and chemicals settle. A good rule: 70% grasses and leaves, 20% legumes and fruits, 10% supplementary feed like pellets or grains. Never feed meat, fish, or oily foods. Grasscutters also need to gnaw to wear down their teeth, so include tough stalks and branches. This natural feeding pattern will keep your animals healthy and reduce feed costs.

2. Nutrient Requirements for Different Stages

Grasscutters need different amounts of nutrients depending on their age and purpose. Newborn grasscutters (pups) drink only their mother's milk for the first two weeks. After that, they start nibbling soft grasses. From weaning (about 4 weeks) to 3 months, they need high protein for rapid growth. A diet with 16-18% crude protein is ideal. You can achieve this by mixing legumes like lucerne or moringa leaves with grasses. From 3 to 6 months, they are growing steadily and need 14-16% protein. Pregnant and lactating females need extra energy and protein: 16-18% protein and plenty of calcium for milk production. Add crushed oyster shells or bone meal to their feed. Breeding males need 14-16% protein to maintain fertility. Adult maintenance (non-breeding) requires only 12-14% protein. Fibre is critical at all stages: at least 20-30% crude fibre. Too little fibre causes digestive problems. Energy needs are moderate; grasscutters do not need high-energy feeds like maize in large amounts. Excess energy leads to fat deposits and poor breeding. Vitamins and minerals: always provide a mineral lick or mix. Calcium and phosphorus in a 2:1 ratio are important for bone and teeth development. Salt (sodium chloride) should be available as a lick. Vitamin A from green leafy plants is essential for eyesight and immunity. Vitamin D from sunlight is made naturally if they get some sun. Vitamin E from fresh greens supports reproduction. A simple rule: young and breeding animals get more protein and minerals; adults get more fibre. Adjust rations every month by observing body condition. If animals look thin, increase protein. If they look fat, reduce energy feeds and increase grasses.

3. Common Feed Ingredients and Formulation

You can feed grasscutters using locally available ingredients. The main ingredients are roughages (grasses, leaves, stalks), concentrates (grains, legumes, oilseed cakes), and supplements (minerals, vitamins). Common roughages: elephant grass, guinea grass, napier grass, cassava leaves (dried to remove cyanide), sweet potato vines, banana leaves, plantain leaves, and water hyacinth (washed). Legumes: lucerne, moringa, groundnut haulms, cowpea leaves, and soya bean leaves. Concentrates: maize (whole or crushed), sorghum, millet, wheat bran, rice bran, palm kernel cake, groundnut cake, and soya bean meal. For a simple home-made pellet or mash, mix by weight: 40% maize, 20% wheat bran, 15% groundnut cake, 10% soya bean meal, 10% dried cassava leaves, 4% bone meal, 1% salt. This gives about 16% protein. For young animals, increase groundnut cake and soya bean meal to 20% each and reduce maize to 30%. For adults, reduce protein sources and increase wheat bran to 30%. Always grind ingredients into a coarse meal. Mix thoroughly. You can add a little water to make a crumbly mash, but do not make it too wet. Feed the mash in clean troughs. Do not store mixed feed for more than 3 days in a dry place, as it can grow mould. Mouldy feed causes poisoning. You can also buy commercial rabbit pellets as a base, but they are often too low in fibre for grasscutters. If you use pellets, mix with chopped grasses. A good formulation is not expensive: use what you grow or can buy cheaply at local markets. Always introduce new feeds gradually over 5-7 days to avoid digestive upsets.

4. Feeding Schedule and Rations

Grasscutters do best on a routine. Feed them twice a day: early morning (6-7 am) and late afternoon (4-5 pm). This matches their natural feeding times. Give fresh green forage first, then a small amount of concentrate. A typical adult grasscutter (2-3 kg) eats about 300-500 grams of fresh forage per day. That is roughly one large handful of grasses and leaves. They also need about 50-100 grams of concentrate (mash or pellets). Pregnant and lactating females need double the concentrate: 100-150 grams. Young weaned grasscutters (1-2 months) eat 100-200 grams of forage and 20-30 grams of concentrate per day. Growing grasscutters (3-6 months) eat 250-400 grams forage and 40-60 grams concentrate. Always provide more forage than they can finish; they will eat what they need. Remove leftovers before the next feeding to prevent mould. Never feed only concentrate; always mix with roughage. If you feed only grasses, they may lose weight. If you feed only pellets, they may get diarrhoea. A good ratio: 70-80% forage, 20-30% concentrate by weight. For a colony of 10 adults, you need about 4-5 kg of fresh forage and 1 kg of concentrate daily. Plan your forage collection accordingly. During the dry season, when fresh grass is scarce, you can use dried grasses, hay, or silage. Soak dried grasses in clean water for 30 minutes before feeding to soften them. You can also feed kitchen scraps like carrot tops, cabbage leaves, and fruit peels, but avoid onions, garlic, and citrus peels. Always provide fresh drinking water. Adjust rations based on body condition: if animals are thin, increase concentrate; if fat, reduce it. Keep a simple record of how much you feed and how much they eat.

5. Supplements and Additives

Even with a good diet, grasscutters may need supplements to stay healthy. The most important is a mineral lick. You can make one by mixing equal parts of bone meal, crushed oyster shells, and salt. Add a little clay or red soil. Put it in a shallow dish or hang a block in the cage. They will lick it as needed. This provides calcium, phosphorus, and sodium. For vitamins, fresh green leaves are usually enough. But in the dry season, when greens are scarce, add a commercial vitamin premix for rabbits or poultry (follow the label, usually 1-2 grams per kg of feed). Do not overdose; too many vitamins can be toxic. Another useful supplement is brewer's yeast (1-2% of feed) for B vitamins, especially for pregnant females. For digestion, you can add a little charcoal or kaolin clay to the feed (1% of feed) to prevent diarrhoea. Some farmers add probiotics (e.g., Lactobacillus) to improve gut health. You can make a simple probiotic by fermenting rice water or milk with a little sugar for 2 days, then mixing 10 ml per litre of drinking water. For strong bones and teeth, provide cuttlebone or old bones (boiled and dried) for gnawing. Avoid adding antibiotics routinely; use only when prescribed by a vet. Never add growth hormones; they are illegal and harmful. Salt licks should always be available. If animals are off feed, check for mould or spoilage. Supplements should not replace good forage. They are called supplements for a reason. Always introduce any new supplement slowly over a week. Observe animals for any negative reactions like diarrhoea or refusal to eat. Keep records of what you supplement and when.

6. Water Management and Hygiene

Water is the most important nutrient. Grasscutters need clean, fresh water every day. They drink about 100-200 ml per adult per day, more in hot weather or when lactating. Use clean water from a borehole, well, or tap. Do not give stagnant or dirty water. Provide water in shallow, heavy bowls that they cannot tip over. Or use nipple drinkers designed for rabbits. Change water at least twice a day, morning and evening. Wash the bowls with clean water and a little soap, then rinse well. Dirty water spreads diseases like coccidiosis and diarrhoea. In hot African climates, water can grow algae quickly. Keep waterers in the shade. If you use a trough, clean it daily. Never let animals walk in their water. Place waterers away from feeding areas to avoid contamination with feed and droppings. Hygiene of feed and feeders is also critical. Feed troughs should be cleaned daily. Remove leftover feed before it spoils. Store feed in dry, airtight containers to prevent mould, rats, and insects. Mouldy feed contains aflatoxins that can kill grasscutters. Never feed mouldy or fermented feed. Forage should be washed if dusty or from areas with animal droppings. But do not soak leafy greens for too long; they lose vitamins. If you collect forage from the wild, check for snails, slugs, or insects that can carry parasites. Rinse in salt water (1 tablespoon per litre) for 5 minutes, then rinse with clean water. Keep the cage dry. Wet floors cause foot rot and respiratory problems. Provide good drainage. Clean cages weekly and disinfect monthly with a mild solution (e.g., 1% bleach), then rinse well and let dry before returning animals. Sick animals should be isolated and given fresh water with electrolytes (a pinch of salt and sugar in water). Always have water available; grasscutters can die from dehydration within a day.

<h2>Practical Reference: Feeding &amp; Nutrition for Grasscutter (Greater Cane Rat) Farming</h2>

<p>Grasscutter farming (<em>Thryonomys swinderianus</em>) is one of the most bankable micro-livestock enterprises in West and Central Africa. The animals convert cheap, locally available roughage into high-value meat with minimal capital outlay but only if the feeding programme is run like a business input, not an afterthought. Feed typically represents <strong>6075% of total production cost</strong> in grasscutter units, so every decision about what you put in the trough directly moves your gross margin. This Practical Reference consolidates the module's six lessons into an operating document you can print, laminate, and pin inside your feed store.</p>

<h3>1. Natural Diet What the Animal Is Built For</h3>
<p>Grasscutters are hindgut-fermenting herbivores that in the wild select young, tender grasses, reeds, sedges, and the succulent parts of cassava, sugarcane, sweet potato vines, and legumes. They are <strong>selective browsers of high-moisture roughage</strong>, not grazers of dry straw. That single biological fact drives your entire feed-cost strategy: your cheapest and most effective feed is fresh, chopped, young green matter harvested daily, not imported pellets.</p>

<h3>2. Nutrient Requirements by Stage</h3>
<p>Requirements shift sharply across the production cycle. Breeding does and growing weaners are the two groups where under-feeding translates directly into lost revenue.</p>

<h3>3. Common Feed Ingredients &amp; Formulation</h3>
<p>Formulate around what is cheapest and most available in your locality. A practical West African ration uses <strong>cassava peels or maize bran</strong> as the energy base, <strong>soybean or groundnut cake</strong> for protein, and <strong>fresh forage</strong> as the bulk. Never rely on a single ingredient.</p>

<h3>4. Feeding Schedule &amp; Rations</h3>
<p>Feed <strong>twice daily</strong> morning (07:0008:00) and late afternoon (16:0017:00). Fresh forage goes in first; concentrate follows. Remove unconsumed fresh material within 12 hours to prevent mould and mycotoxin build-up. A working rule: <strong>adult grasscutter = 400600 g fresh forage + 60100 g concentrate per day</strong>; weaners start at 100 g forage + 25 g concentrate and scale up weekly. Lactating does get free-choice concentrate plus unlimited fresh forage.</p>

<h3>5. Supplements &amp; Additives</h3>
<p>Supplements are insurance, not decoration. A mineralvitamin premix at 1.5% of the concentrate covers calcium, phosphorus, and trace minerals that forages alone cannot guarantee. Salt at 0.5% maintains electrolyte balance. Where available, add a coccidiostat or probiotic during weaning stress. Do <strong>not</strong> add antibiotics as growth promoters this is now banned in most African regulatory frameworks and destroys your premium market positioning.</p>

<div style="border-left:6px solid #b00020;background:#fdecec;padding:14px 18px;margin:20px 0;border-radius:4px;">
<strong style="color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING Mycotoxins and Cassava Cyanide</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Cassava peels and maize bran are the two most common grasscutter feed ingredients in Africa and also the two most common sources of <strong>cyanogenic glycosides and aflatoxins</strong>. Inadequate drying causes <em>Aspergillus</em> mould and aflatoxin contamination, which causes liver damage, poor growth, and abortion in does. Fresh cassava must be <strong>sun-dried for at least 4872 hours until crisp</strong> before inclusion, and any feed with visible mould, musty smell, or caking must be discarded immediately never diluted into a batch. Store feed on pallets in a dry, ventilated room, use within 30 days, and never feed mouldy material to pregnant or lactating animals. Aflatoxin poisoning is cumulative and irreversible; prevention is the only control.</p>
</div>

<h3>6. Water Management &amp; Hygiene</h3>
<p>Grasscutters drink less than rabbits but water quality still drives feed conversion. Provide <strong>clean, fresh water ad libitum</strong>, changed twice daily. Use nipple drinkers or shallow bowls that cannot be fouled with forage. Wash drinkers with clean water daily and disinfect weekly. In hot, humid conditions, water intake rises sharply a lactating doe may drink 300500 ml/day. Dehydration shows first as reduced feed intake, then as rough coat and weight loss; by the time you see it, you have already lost a week of growth.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li> Weigh a sample of feed weekly don't estimate by eye; feed cost is your biggest line item.</li>
<li> Harvest fresh forage <em>daily</em>; wilted or fermented forage reduces intake and risks mould.</li>
<li> Match ration to stage weaners and lactating does get the highest protein (1618% CP).</li>
<li> Sun-dry cassava peels 4872 hours before inclusion; discard any mouldy batch immediately.</li>
<li> Feed twice daily at fixed times; remove leftovers within 12 hours.</li>
<li> Provide clean water twice daily; scrub drinkers weekly.</li>
<li> Include 1.5% mineralvitamin premix and 0.5% salt in every concentrate batch.</li>
<li> Record feed in, animal weight out, and cost per kg gain review monthly.</li>
</ul>
