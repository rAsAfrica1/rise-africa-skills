Lessons

1. Procurement Planning

Procurement planning means deciding what you need to buy, when you need it, and how you will buy it. Start by reviewing your project schedule and budget. For example, if you are building a 50,000-litre water tank for a community in Kisumu, you need cement, steel, pipes, and a contractor. List every item or service, estimate quantities, and set a required-by date. Use a simple table: item, quantity, unit, required date, estimated cost. Total the estimated costs and compare with your project budget. If the total exceeds your budget, you must adjust scope or find cheaper alternatives.

Next, decide the procurement method. For small purchases under $500, you can buy directly from a local supplier. For $500 to $5,000, get at least three quotations. For contracts above $5,000, use a formal tender or request for proposal (RFP). In many African countries, public projects require open tendering above a threshold set by law, such as 5 million Kenyan shillings. Always check your donor or government procurement rules.

Create a procurement schedule that shows when you will advertise, when bids close, when you will evaluate, and when you will sign contracts. A common mistake is to start procurement too late. If you need a supplier in 4 weeks, you must start the process 8 weeks earlier. Use tools like Gantt charts in Microsoft Project or free Trello boards to track deadlines.

Finally, identify risks. What if the supplier delays? What if prices rise? Plan for a 10% contingency on all procurement costs. For critical items, identify a backup supplier. Document your plan and get approval from your project sponsor or finance officer. A good procurement plan saves time, money, and prevents corruption.

2. Contract Types (Fixed-Price, Cost-Reimbursable, T&M)

A contract type determines how you pay the vendor and who carries the risk. The three main types are fixed-price, cost-reimbursable, and time and materials (T&M). Choose the right one based on how clear your requirements are and how much risk you can manage.

Fixed-price contracts set a total price for a well-defined scope. For example, you agree to pay a contractor 2,500,000 Kenyan shillings to build a 3-bedroom house. The contractor bears the risk of cost overruns. This type works best when you have detailed drawings and specifications. If the scope changes, you must issue a change order with a new price. Fixed-price contracts are common in construction and equipment supply.

Cost-reimbursable contracts pay the vendor for all actual costs plus a fee. For instance, a consultant charges $500 per day plus travel and accommodation. You bear the risk of cost overruns. Use this type when the scope is uncertain, such as research or emergency repairs. You must audit invoices carefully. Many donors limit cost-reimbursable contracts to a not-to-exceed amount.

Time and materials (T&M) contracts pay a fixed hourly or daily rate plus the cost of materials. For example, a mechanic charges $30 per hour plus parts. This type is good for small repairs or when you need flexibility. However, costs can escalate quickly, so set a maximum budget and require daily timesheets.

In Africa, fixed-price is preferred for most goods and works because it provides budget certainty. Cost-reimbursable is used for complex services like consulting. T&M is common for maintenance. Always put the contract type in writing and include payment terms, penalties for delay, and dispute resolution.

3. Selecting Vendors and Bidders

Selecting the right vendor is critical. Start by defining your evaluation criteria before you receive bids. Common criteria include price (30-40%), technical capability (30-40%), experience (10-20%), and delivery time (10%). For example, if you need 500 bags of cement, price might be 50%, quality 30%, and delivery 20%. Write these criteria in your tender document so all bidders know how they will be judged.

Advertise your opportunity widely. Use local newspapers, radio, and online platforms like Tenders Kenya or NGO tender portals. For small purchases, invite at least three known suppliers to quote. For larger contracts, issue a formal request for proposal (RFP) or invitation to bid (ITB). Give bidders at least two weeks to respond; for complex works, give four weeks.

When bids arrive, form an evaluation committee of at least three people. One should be technical, one financial, and one from management. Evaluate each bid against the criteria. Use a scoring sheet: for each criterion, give a score from 1 to 5, multiply by the weight, and sum. Do not just choose the lowest pricea cheap bid may fail to deliver. Check that bidders are registered, have tax compliance certificates, and can provide references.

Conduct due diligence. Visit previous projects or call references. For a catering contract, ask for a food handling certificate. For construction, check the contractor's class of registration with the National Construction Authority. Avoid vendors with a history of delays or corruption. Document your selection and keep a record of why you rejected other bids. This protects you if a bidder complains.

4. Administering Contracts

Once a contract is signed, administration begins. Your job is to ensure the vendor delivers on time, at the agreed quality, and within budget. Start by holding a kick-off meeting. Review the scope, schedule, payment terms, and communication plan. Assign a contract managerone person responsible for day-to-day oversight. For a school feeding program, the contract manager might be the school bursar.

Keep a contract file with the signed agreement, all change orders, invoices, delivery notes, and correspondence. Use a simple log in Excel or Google Sheets to track deliverables. For example, if a supplier must deliver 100 desks by March 15, record the date, quantity, and condition. Inspect goods upon delivery. If 10 desks are broken, note it on the delivery note and ask for replacement within 7 days.

Manage payments carefully. Never pay in full upfront. Typical terms are 30% advance, 40% on delivery, and 30% after acceptance. For services, pay monthly against approved timesheets. Always require a receipt or invoice with a tax identification number. In many African countries, you must withhold a percentage (e.g., 5% in Kenya) as retention money for defects liability.

Handle changes formally. If the vendor requests a change, issue a written change order signed by both parties. Uncontrolled changes cause disputes. If the vendor delays, send a written warning. If delays continue, you may terminate the contract and hire a backup. Document everything. Good contract administration prevents fraud and ensures value for money.

5. Vendor Performance Monitoring

Vendor performance monitoring means regularly checking whether your suppliers and contractors are meeting their obligations. Do not wait until the end of the contract. Set up a simple monitoring system from day one. For each vendor, define key performance indicators (KPIs) such as on-time delivery, quality defect rate, responsiveness, and cost adherence. For a transport vendor, KPIs might be: 95% on-time pickup, less than 2% damaged goods, and response to queries within 4 hours.

Collect data continuously. Use a vendor scorecarda table where you rate the vendor each month on a scale of 1 to 5 for each KPI. For example, if a supplier delivers 90% on time, give a 3. If they deliver 100%, give a 5. Share the scorecard with the vendor monthly. This feedback helps them improve and gives you evidence if you need to terminate.

Conduct site visits or spot checks. For a construction project, visit the site weekly to check progress against the schedule. Take photos and write brief notes. For a catering contract, taste the food unannounced. If you find problems, issue a corrective action request (CAR) in writing. Give the vendor a deadline to fix the issue, e.g., 5 working days. Follow up to confirm the fix.

At the end of each quarter, hold a performance review meeting. Discuss what went well and what needs improvement. If a vendor consistently scores below 3, put them on a performance improvement plan. If no improvement after 30 days, start looking for a replacement. Keep all records. Good monitoring reduces risk and builds stronger relationships with reliable vendors.

6. Closing Procurements

Closing procurements means formally completing each contract and settling all obligations. It is not just paperworkit protects your project from future claims and helps you learn. Start by verifying that all deliverables have been received and accepted. For a contract to supply 1,000 textbooks, check that all books arrived, are in good condition, and match the order. Get a signed acceptance certificate from the person who received the goods.

Next, settle final payments. Ensure all invoices are paid, but hold back retention money if the contract requires it. For example, in Kenya, you might withhold 5% for 6 months to cover defects. After the defects liability period, release the retention if no issues arise. Also confirm that the vendor has paid their subcontractors and workers. Ask for proof of payment to avoid labor disputes.

Conduct a procurement audit. Review the contract file: was the process competitive? Were there any unauthorized changes? Did the vendor perform as agreed? Write a short close-out report with lessons learned. For example, note that a supplier was late because the delivery route was floodednext time, choose a supplier closer to the site. Share this report with your team and finance department.

Finally, update your records. Mark the contract as closed in your procurement register. Return any unused materials or equipment. If the vendor performed well, add them to your approved vendor list for future projects. If they performed poorly, note that too. Proper closure frees up your team to focus on other tasks and ensures you have a clean audit trail for donors or government inspectors.

<h2>Practical Reference: Procurement and Vendor Management in African Agribusiness Projects</h2>

<p>In African agribusiness, procurement is rarely a back-office exercise. A single delayed input order can wipe out a planting window; a poorly written contract can turn a fertiliser supplier dispute into a season-long cash-flow crisis. This Practical Reference gives project managers, cooperative leaders, and agribusiness entrepreneurs a working toolkit for planning procurement, choosing contract types, selecting vendors, administering contracts, monitoring performance, and closing procurements with examples drawn from maize, cocoa, horticulture, poultry, and agro-processing value chains across the continent.</p>

<h2>1. Procurement Planning: Anchor to the Season, Not the Calendar</h2>
<p>In African agriculture, procurement planning must be driven by the biological clock of the crop or herd, not by a quarterly budget cycle. A maize project in Zambia's Central Province needs certified seed and basal fertiliser delivered before the first reliable rains (typically late November). A Kenyan horticulture exporter shipping French beans to Europe must align packaging procurement with harvest peaks and air-freight slots. Planning starts with a <strong>procurement statement of work (SOW)</strong> that specifies quantity, quality grade, delivery point, and timing for example, "50 MT of NPK 23:21:0+4S, bagged in 50 kg woven polypropylene, delivered to Mkushi depot by 15 November."</p>
<p>Make-or-buy analysis is critical. Should a poultry project buy day-old chicks or invest in a hatchery? Should a cocoa cooperative buy a fermentary or contract a licensed processor? Decisions hinge on volume, capital availability, and risk tolerance. Where foreign exchange is scarce as in Nigeria, Ghana, or Malawi plan for import lead times of 816 weeks and secure letters of credit early.</p>
<p>Consolidate demand across cooperatives or out-grower schemes to unlock volume discounts. In Ethiopia's coffee sector, unions aggregate member cooperative purchases of jute bags and organic inputs, reducing unit costs by 1020%.</p>

<h2>3. Selecting Vendors and Bidders</h2>
<p>Vendor selection in African agribusiness must balance price with reliability, quality, and local content. A cheap fertiliser supplier who delivers three weeks late is more expensive than a slightly pricier one who delivers on time.</p>
<p>Use a two-envelope or weighted-criteria evaluation. Typical weights: technical capability 40%, price 30%, delivery reliability 15%, after-sales support 10%, local content/ESG 5%. For donor-funded projects, follow the funder's rules World Bank, AfDB, and EU each have distinct thresholds and methods (shopping, national competitive bidding, international competitive bidding).</p>
<p>Due diligence matters. Verify vendor registration, tax compliance certificates, and physical premises. In several African markets, counterfeit inputs are a real risk; require batch certificates of analysis (CoA) and, where possible, inspect samples before award.</p>

<h2>4. Administering Contracts</h2>
<p>Contract administration is where procurement value is won or lost. Assign a contract manager, keep a payment milestone tracker, and hold monthly vendor review meetings. In agribusiness, key administration tasks include:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Delivery inspection:</strong> check quantity, quality, and packaging against the SOW before signing goods-received notes.</li>
<li><strong>Payment discipline:</strong> pay on time to preserve vendor goodwill late payment is the number-one complaint of African SMEs supplying larger projects.</li>
<li><strong>Change control:</strong> any scope change must be documented and priced before work proceeds.</li>
<li><strong>Documentation:</strong> keep contracts, amendments, inspection reports, and correspondence in a single file for audit and closure.</li>
</ul>

<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#7f0000;font-size:14px;">Never procure or accept agrochemicals, veterinary drugs, or seed without verifying registration with the national regulator (e.g., Kenya's PCPB, Nigeria's NAFDAC/NAQS, South Africa's DALRRD). Counterfeit or unregistered inputs have caused crop failures, livestock deaths, and human poisoning across Africa. Insist on batch certificates of analysis, keep safety data sheets (SDS), and train handlers in PPE use and safe storage. Non-compliant procurement can void insurance and expose the project to legal liability.</p>

<h2>5. Vendor Performance Monitoring</h2>
<p>Monitor vendors continuously, not only at contract end. Use a simple scorecard with KPIs such as on-time delivery rate, quality rejection rate, responsiveness, and price stability. For a horticulture exporter, a packaging supplier's on-time delivery rate below 95% during harvest is a red flag. For a dairy processor, milk collection vendors must meet cold-chain temperature logs.</p>
<p>Escalate formally: first a written reminder, then a corrective action request, then a contract remedy (liquidated damages, replacement, or termination). Document everything. In donor-funded projects, unresolved vendor disputes can trigger audits and funding suspensions.</p>

<h2>6. Closing Procurements</h2>
<p>Closure is not just filing paperwork. It includes:</p>
<ul>
<li>Verifying all deliverables are accepted and final payments made.</li>
<li>Reconciling the contract against the budget and procurement plan.</li>
<li>Capturing lessons learned which vendors performed, which clauses worked, what caused delays.</li>
<li>Issuing vendor performance evaluations for future reference.</li>
<li>Archiving records for the period required by the funder or national law (often 57 years).</li>
</ul>
<p>In cooperatives and out-grower schemes, closure should also include a member feedback session farmers are the ultimate end-users of procured inputs and their experience is the best indicator of procurement success.</p>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>
<ol style="line-height:1.7;">
<li><strong>Season-aligned plan:</strong> Does the procurement schedule match planting, harvest, or production windows?</li>
<li><strong>Clear SOW:</strong> Are quantity, quality, delivery point, and timing specified in writing?</li>
<li><strong>Right contract type:</strong> Does the contract type match scope certainty and risk allocation?</li>
<li><strong>Vendor due diligence:</strong> Are registration, tax, and quality certifications verified?</li>
<li><strong>Weighted evaluation:</strong> Is vendor selection based on documented criteria, not relationships?</li>
<li><strong>Contract administration:</strong> Are inspections, payments, and changes documented and on time?</li>
<li><strong>Performance KPIs:</strong> Are vendors monitored against measurable delivery and quality targets?</li>
<li><strong>Proper closure:</strong> Are final payments, evaluations, and lessons-learned records completed and archived?</li>
</ol>
