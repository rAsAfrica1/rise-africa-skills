Lessons

1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Welding is the backbone of metal furniture making. Without it, you cannot join metal tubes, rods, or sheets into strong, durable frames for chairs, tables, beds, or gates. In Africa, where metal furniture is in high demand for homes, schools, offices, and outdoor spaces, welding skills can turn simple scrap metal into products that sell for 5 to 10 times the cost of the raw materials. For example, a basic metal chair made from 2 meters of square tube (costing about 5000 Naira or 300 KES) can sell for 15,000 to 25,000 Naira or 1500 to 2500 KES after welding and finishing.

This module teaches you basic welding specifically for furniture. You will learn arc welding (stick welding), which is the most common and affordable method in Africa. It uses a welding machine that runs on 220V single-phase power (or a generator), a welding electrode (rod), and a ground clamp. No gas cylinders are needed, making it ideal for small workshops. You will learn to weld mild steel, which is what most furniture is made fromsquare tubes, round tubes, angle iron, and flat bars.

Why does welding matter for furniture? Because furniture must bear weight, resist shaking, and look neat. A poorly welded joint will crack when someone sits on it, leading to returns, lost customers, and safety risks. A well-welded joint is stronger than the metal itself. With practice, you can weld a chair frame in 30 minutes and earn a good profit. This module will take you from zero to confident welder, focusing on practical steps, safety, and fixing common mistakes. By the end, you will complete a simple stool or small table using your new skills.

2. Tools and Materials

To start welding furniture, you need a basic set of tools and materials. The core equipment is a welding machine. In Africa, the most common is a 200-amp inverter welder that runs on 220V single-phase power. Brands like Powercraft, Ingco, or generic Chinese models cost between 50,000 and 120,000 Naira (or 5,000 to 12,000 KES). You also need a welding helmet or handheld face shield with a dark lens (shade 10 or 11), leather gloves, a chipping hammer, a wire brush, and a pair of pliers. For safety, wear a leather apron, closed shoes, and a cap.

Electrodes are the filler metal. For furniture, use mild steel electrodes like E6013 (general purpose) or E7018 (stronger). Common sizes are 2.5mm and 3.2mm diameter. A 2.5mm rod is good for thin tubes (1-2mm wall thickness), while 3.2mm is for thicker metal (3mm and above). Buy a 5kg packit costs about 3,000 to 5,000 Naira (300-500 KES). You also need a ground clamp and cable, and a electrode holder. These usually come with the machine.

For cutting metal, you need an angle grinder with a cutting disc (4.5-inch or 9-inch) and a grinding disc for smoothing welds. A hacksaw can work for thin tubes but is slow. You also need a measuring tape, try square, scriber or marker, and a workbench or sturdy table. Clamps help hold pieces in place. For the metal itself, buy mild steel square tubes (e.g., 20mm x 20mm x 1.5mm), round tubes, or angle iron from a local metal dealer. A 6-meter length of 20mm square tube costs about 2,500 Naira (250 KES). Finally, keep a fire extinguisher or bucket of sand nearby, and ensure good ventilation.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps to weld a simple furniture joint, such as a 90-degree corner between two square tubes. First, prepare the metal: cut two pieces to length using your angle grinder. Clean the ends with a wire brush or grinder to remove rust, paint, or oil. Bare, shiny metal is essential for a good weld. Next, set up your work area: place the metal on a fireproof surface (not wood or plastic). Ensure good ventilationopen doors or weld outside. Put on your safety gear: helmet, gloves, apron, and closed shoes.

Second, set your welding machine. For 2.5mm electrode on 1.5mm thick tube, set the amperage to about 70-90 amps. For 3.2mm electrode on 3mm thick metal, set 100-130 amps. Too high will burn holes; too low will not penetrate. Clamp the ground clamp to the metal piece you are welding. Insert the electrode into the holder, touching the metal to strike an arc. Practice striking: tap the electrode like a match, then lift slightly to maintain a short arc (about 2-3mm gap).

Third, tack weld first. Hold the two pieces at 90 degrees using a try square and clamp. Make small tack welds (just a quick touch) at each corner. Check the angle, then weld the full joint. For a butt joint (end to end), use a slight back-and-forth motion to fill the gap. For a T-joint (one tube on another), angle the electrode 45 degrees and push the puddle. Move steadily, watching the molten pool. After welding, let it cool, then chip off slag with a chipping hammer and brush clean. Grind smooth if needed. Practice on scrap metal until your beads are even and penetrate well.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Beginners often make mistakes that weaken furniture or make it look ugly. Here are the most common and how to fix them. First, slag inclusion: this happens when you move too fast or don't clean between passes. Slag (the glassy coating on the weld) gets trapped inside, causing weak spots. Fix: clean thoroughly with a chipping hammer and wire brush after each pass, and slow down your travel speed. Second, porosity: small holes in the weld caused by moisture, dirt, or too long an arc. Fix: clean metal, use dry electrodes (store in a dry place), and keep a short arc. Third, undercut: a groove melted into the base metal along the weld edge. This weakens the joint. Fix: reduce amperage, use a shorter arc, and pause at the edges to fill.

Fourth, burn-through: holes melted through thin metal. This happens with too high amperage or slow travel. Fix: lower amps, use a smaller electrode (2.5mm), and move faster. Fifth, spatter: small metal balls stuck to the workpiece. Fix: adjust amperage and angle, use anti-spatter spray (or a little oil), and chip off after. Sixth, incomplete fusion: the weld doesn't melt into the base metal. Fix: increase amperage, slow down, and ensure the arc melts both edges. Seventh, warping: the metal bends due to heat. Fix: clamp pieces firmly, weld in short sections, and alternate sides.

Finally, a common mistake is not practicing enough. Welding is a muscle memory skill. Spend at least 30 minutes a day on scrap metal. If your weld looks like a row of blobs, you are moving too fast. If it's a flat, wide pancake, you are too slow. Aim for a uniform, slightly convex bead with fine ripples. Always inspect your work: a good weld should have no cracks, holes, or undercut. If in doubt, cut the joint open and check penetration.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Welding is safe if you follow rules. The biggest dangers are electric shock, burns, eye damage, and fumes. First, electric shock: never weld in wet conditions. Wear dry gloves and boots. Check cables for damage. Do not touch the electrode or metal with bare skin while the machine is on. Use a properly grounded outlet. Second, burns: the arc reaches 3,500C. Wear leather gloves, apron, and closed shoes. Never wear synthetic clothingit melts. Keep a bucket of water or sand nearby for small fires. Third, eye damage: the bright arc emits UV and IR rays that can blind you. Always wear a welding helmet with a proper shade lens (shade 10-11 for arc welding). Never look at the arc with naked eyes. Even a quick flash can cause 'arc eye'painful like sand in your eyes.

Fourth, fumes: welding produces toxic gases and metal fumes. Weld in a well-ventilated areaopen doors, use a fan to blow fumes away, or weld outside. If welding galvanized metal (zinc-coated), avoid it or wear a respirator; zinc fumes cause metal fume fever. Fifth, fire: keep flammable materials away. Have a fire extinguisher or sand bucket. Never weld near petrol, paint, or wood. Sixth, proper handling: let hot metal cool before touching. Use pliers or tongs. Mark hot metal with a chalk or keep a wet rag nearby.

Best practices: Inspect your equipment daily. Keep your work area clean and organized. Use the right amperage for the metal thickness. Practice on scrap first. Always wear PPE. Never weld alonehave someone nearby in case of emergency. After welding, turn off the machine and unplug it. Store electrodes in a dry place. Finally, respect the metal: a good weld is a sign of a skilled craftsman. Take your time, and don't rush. Safety is not optionalit's part of the job.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it's time to apply what you've learned. This exercise will guide you to build a simple metal stool. You will need: 4 pieces of 20mm square tube, each 45cm long (for legs), 4 pieces of 20mm square tube, each 30cm long (for the seat frame), and 1 piece of flat bar or metal sheet 30cm x 30cm for the seat top. You also need your welding machine, 2.5mm electrodes, angle grinder, measuring tape, try square, clamps, and safety gear.

Step 1: Cut all pieces to length. Clean ends with a grinder. Step 2: Lay the four 30cm pieces into a square frame on a flat surface. Use a try square to ensure 90-degree corners. Clamp them. Step 3: Tack weld each corner. Check for squareness by measuring diagonalsthey should be equal. If not, adjust before full welding. Step 4: Weld all four corners fully. Use a 2.5mm electrode at 80 amps. Let cool, chip slag, and grind smooth. Step 5: Attach the legs. Place each 45cm leg at a corner of the frame, ensuring they are perpendicular to the frame. Tack weld, check with a square, then weld fully. Step 6: Weld the seat top (flat bar or sheet) onto the frame. Tack first, then weld around the edges. Step 7: Grind all welds smooth. Step 8: Clean with a wire brush and paint with anti-rust paint.

Inspect your work: the stool should sit flat without wobbling. Welds should be strong and neat. Test by standing on itif it holds, you've succeeded. If a weld breaks, cut it out and re-weld. Practice this exercise at least three times. Each time, aim for faster, cleaner welds. Once you master the stool, you can scale up to chairs, tables, and beds. Remember: every professional welder started with simple projects. Keep practicing, and you will build furniture that lasts for years.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Basic Welding for Metal Furniture

This practical reference is the go-to page for a working welder, a workshop owner, or a trainee in a metal furniture business. It pulls together the numbers, settings, checks, and sources you need on the shop floor from a one-man operation in Kibera or Katwe to a growing workshop supplying schools, hotels, and hardware shops across the region. The goal is simple: weld furniture that holds, looks clean, and makes you money without burning your workshop down.

In African metal furniture markets, most production still runs on manual metal arc welding (MMAW, "stick") using 2.5 mm and 3.2 mm electrodes on 1225 mm square tube, angle iron, and flat bar. Stick welding is cheap, portable, and works with generators where the grid is unreliable. But it punishes poor preparation: rusty tube, damp electrodes, and wrong amperage are the top three causes of weak joints. Get the basics right and you can build beds, gates, chairs, tables, trolleys, and frames that survive years of hard use.

1. Electrode and Amperage Selection

Most furniture workshops in Africa run E6013 (rutile) electrodes because they strike easily, run smoothly on low-cost AC machines, and give a neat bead for visible furniture joints. E6011 is used for dirty or galvanised material and for deeper penetration on thicker sections. For critical load-bearing joints (bed frames, gate hinges), E7018 gives higher strength but needs a DC machine or a good AC welder and dry storage.

Electrode Diameter Material thickness Current (AC/DC) Typical furniture use
E6013 2.0 mm 1.53 mm 4070 A Thin tube, chair frames, decorative work
E6013 2.5 mm 35 mm 70100 A Square tube 2025 mm, table frames
E6013 3.2 mm 58 mm 100140 A Angle iron, bed frames, gate frames
E6011 2.5 mm 38 mm 70110 A Rusty, painted, or galvanised stock
E7018 3.2 mm 612 mm 110150 A (DC+ preferred) High-strength load-bearing joints

Source: Compiled from Lincoln Electric, Stick Electrode Selection Guide (lincolnelectric.com), and ESAB, Welding Handbook MMAW (esab.com). Always confirm settings on your own machine with a test coupon.

2. Joint Preparation and Fit-Up

Furniture joints are mostly T-joints, corner (mitre) joints, and butt joints. The single biggest quality gain in any African metal furniture workshop is cutting square and cleaning to bare metal before welding. A 100 mm grinder with a flap disc removes mill scale, rust, and galvanising. Leave a small root gap (12 mm) on butt joints and bevel material over 6 mm thick. Tack first, check square with a set square, then weld.

Table 2: Common furniture joints preparation, technique, and business impact
Joint Preparation Technique Business impact if done well
T-joint (frame to leg) Clean both faces; no gap 45 drag angle, 2 passes on thick tube Strong beds and tables; fewer warranty returns
Corner / mitre (door, window frame) Cut at 45, clean, tack at both ends Tack, check square, weld inside corner first Square frames sell faster; less rework
Butt joint (rail extension) Bevel over 6 mm; 12 mm root gap Root pass, then cover pass Safe load-bearing rails; repeat customers
Lap joint (bracket, plate) Clean overlap; clamp tight Weld edges, not centre Reliable brackets; fewer callbacks

Source: Compiled from the American Welding Society, Welding Handbook, 10th ed., Vol. 1 (aws.org), and Skills for Africa / TVET practical welding curricula used in Kenya, Uganda, and Ghana.

SAFETY WARNING ARC EYE FLASH AND FUMES

Never weld without a proper welding helmet with a shade 1013 filter. "Arc eye" (photokeratitis) is extremely painful and can cause permanent damage. Never look at the arc with the naked eye, and never let apprentices or bystanders watch without protection. Weld only in a ventilated area or with a fume extractor galvanised metal releases toxic zinc oxide fumes. In many African workshops, welding is done in shared, enclosed spaces; a simple open door, roof vent, or fan positioned behind the welder can save lungs. Keep a dry powder or CO fire extinguisher within reach, and never weld near petrol, paint thinners, or LPG cylinders.

3. Step-by-Step Method (Shop Floor)

  1. Measure and mark all cuts with a steel rule and scriber. Cut with a hacksaw or angle grinder; deburr.
  2. Clean all joint areas to bare, shiny metal with a grinder and flap disc. Wipe off oil and dust.
  3. Set up and clamp the frame on a flat welding table or concrete floor. Check square and level.
  4. Tack weld at the ends of each joint (23 tacks per joint). Re-check square before full welding.
  5. Set the machine to the amperage in Table 1 for your electrode and thickness. Test on scrap.
  6. Weld with a 1020 work angle, 7080 travel angle, and a steady drag or slight weave. Keep arc length short (about the electrode core diameter).
  7. Chip and brush each pass. Inspect for slag inclusion, porosity, and undercut.
  8. Grind visible joints smooth if the piece is for painting or powder coating.
  9. Finish with primer and topcoat (or leave raw if the client wants a rustic look).
  10. Price and deliver see the business note below.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

  • Porosity (gas holes): damp electrodes, dirty metal, or arc too long. Fix: store electrodes in a dry sealed container, clean the metal, shorten the arc.
  • Slag inclusion: not chipping between passes. Fix: chip and brush every pass.
  • Undercut: amperage too high or travel too fast. Fix: reduce amps or slow down.
  • Lack of penetration: amps too low, gap too tight, or travel too fast. Fix: increase amps, add root gap, slow travel.
  • Distortion: too much heat in one area. Fix: tack first, weld in short runs, alternate sides, allow cooling.
  • Sticking electrode: amps too low or arc length too short at start. Fix: increase amps, strike like a match.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety is not a formality it is a business cost. One arc-eye injury or a workshop fire can close a small enterprise. Minimum PPE: welding helmet (shade 1013), leather gloves, leather apron, safety boots, and eye protection for grinding. Use screens to protect others. Keep cables and electrode holders in good condition; replace damaged insulation. Never weld on a wet floor or with wet gloves. In shared workshops, agree a "welding zone" and a fire watch for 30 minutes after finishing.

6. Practical Exercise

Task: Build a simple 400 400 450 mm stool frame from 20 mm square tube (or 20 20 2 mm angle).

  1. Cut 4 legs (450 mm), 4 top rails (400 mm), and 4 bottom rails (400 mm).
  2. Clean all ends and lay out one side frame (2 legs + 2 rails).
  3. Tack, check square, weld. Repeat for the other side.
  4. Join the two sides with the remaining rails. Check square and level.
  5. Grind visible joints, prime, and paint.
  6. Load-test with 100 kg for 1 minute. Inspect for cracks.

Business note: A stool like this sells for roughly KES 1,5002,500 (USD 1220) in Nairobi, or GHS 120200 in Accra, depending on finish. With good jigs and batch production, a two-person workshop can make 812 per day. The same skills scale to beds, gates, school desks, and market stalls the real money in African metal furniture is in repeat contracts with schools, hotels, and hardware distributors, not one-off pieces.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Metal cleaned to bare, shiny steel before welding
  • Electrode dry, correct type and diameter for the job
  • Amperage set and tested on scrap
  • Frame tacked and checked square before full welding
  • Short arc, correct travel angle, steady speed
  • Slag chipped and brushed after every pass
  • PPE on: helmet, gloves, apron, boots, eye protection
  • Fire extinguisher within reach; area ventilated; fire watch after finishing
