1. Preventive Maintenance Schedules for African Conditions

Welcome to Module 11, Lesson 1. In this lesson we focus on building and following preventive maintenance schedules that actually work in African operating environments. Whether you run a maize mill in Kenya, a quarry in Ghana, a generator fleet in Nigeria, or a tractor hire service in Tanzania, the principle is the same: machines fail when maintenance is reactive, and reactive maintenance costs three to five times more than planned maintenance. Your goal as an operator or owner is to move from breakdown thinking to calendar and hour based thinking. Start by creating a machine register. For every asset, record the make, model, serial number, year of manufacture, engine or motor rating, and the date it entered service. Then attach a maintenance logbook, either paper or a simple phone app. In many African workshops, the biggest problem is not lack of skill but lack of records. Without records you cannot predict failure, you can only react to it. Next, classify your machines by criticality. A machine that stops the whole production line is critical. A backup pump is less critical. Spend your maintenance budget first on critical machines. For each critical machine, obtain the manufacturer manual. If the manual is missing, search online using the model number, or contact the local dealer. If no manual exists, build a schedule from first principles using these intervals: daily checks before start-up, weekly checks, monthly services, quarterly services, and annual overhauls. Daily checks include engine oil level, coolant level, fuel level and water separator, hydraulic oil level, tyre pressure and condition, battery terminals, fan belt tension, air filter restriction indicator, and any unusual noise or vibration. In dusty conditions, which are common across the Sahel, East Africa, and Southern Africa dry seasons, air filters clog much faster than in Europe or Asia. Check the air filter daily and clean it every 50 to 100 hours depending on dust. Replace it when the restriction indicator shows red or when light cannot pass through the pleats. Fuel quality is another African reality. Diesel often contains water, dirt, and sulphur. Install a water separator and drain it daily. Change fuel filters every 250 hours or sooner if you see power loss. Keep fuel storage tanks clean and never fuel machines directly from drums that have been sitting open. Use a funnel with a fine mesh. Lubrication is the cheapest insurance you can buy. Grease all nipples every 50 hours on earthmoving equipment and every 100 hours on stationary plant. Use the correct grease grade as specified. Over-greasing can damage seals, so follow the manual. Engine oil and filter changes should happen every 250 hours for most diesel engines, but reduce to 200 hours in very dusty or hot conditions. Record the hour meter reading at every service. Hydraulic systems need attention too. Check hydraulic oil level daily, inspect hoses for cracks and leaks weekly, and change hydraulic filters every 500 hours. Contamination is the number one killer of hydraulic pumps and valves. Keep couplings clean and use a filter cart when transferring oil. Cooling systems in hot climates work harder. Check coolant concentration every month. Use a 50 50 mix of antifreeze and clean water. In areas with hard water, use distilled or softened water to prevent scale. Clean the radiator fins with compressed air or a soft brush every 250 hours. Never open a hot radiator cap. Belts and hoses should be inspected weekly. Replace any belt that is cracked, glazed, or frayed. Keep spare belts and hoses on site because in remote areas you may wait days for delivery. Electrical systems suffer from dust, heat, and vibration. Check battery electrolyte level monthly, clean terminals, and tighten connections. Test alternator output every quarter. Keep wiring harnesses secured away from hot surfaces. Tyres and undercarriage are major cost centres. Check tyre pressure weekly when tyres are cold. Rotate tyres according to the manual. For tracked machines, check track tension weekly and inspect sprockets, idlers, and rollers for wear. A loose track can derail and cause expensive damage. Now let us talk about scheduling. Create a wall chart or whiteboard in the workshop. List each machine and its next service date or hour. Assign responsibility to a named person. In small businesses, the owner often does maintenance personally. That is fine, but write it down. Use a simple traffic light system: green means service not due, amber means due within 50 hours, red means overdue. Never let a machine go red. If you operate a fleet, consider a maintenance management app. Many are free or low cost and work offline. They send reminders and keep history. This is especially useful when machines are scattered across sites. Finally, budget for maintenance. Set aside 3 to 5 percent of the machine purchase price per year for maintenance. For older machines, increase to 8 percent. Put this money in a separate account. When a breakdown happens, you will have cash to buy parts and pay labour. Preventive maintenance is not a cost; it is a profit centre because it keeps your machines earning. In the next lesson we will cover troubleshooting common diesel engine faults. Before you move on, write down three daily checks you will start doing tomorrow on your most critical machine. Then write the name of the person responsible. That is your first step to a reliable fleet.

2. Troubleshooting Diesel Engines in the Field

Welcome to Lesson 2. Diesel engines power most machinery in Africa, from generators and water pumps to tractors, trucks, and excavators. When a diesel engine fails, downtime costs money. Your ability to diagnose quickly and correctly saves hours and thousands in parts. This lesson gives you a practical troubleshooting method you can use in the field with basic tools. First, understand the four things a diesel engine needs to run: air, fuel, compression, and correct timing. If any one is missing, the engine will not start or will run poorly. Most field failures are fuel or air related. Start with the simplest checks. If the engine will not start, ask: is there fuel in the tank? Is the fuel valve open? Is the battery charged? Does the engine crank at normal speed? If it cranks slowly, the battery or starter is weak. If it does not crank at all, check the safety switches, fuses, and ignition circuit. If it cranks but does not fire, check fuel delivery. Crack the injector lines one at a time and crank the engine. If fuel pulses out, the injection pump is delivering. If not, the problem is upstream: fuel filter, lift pump, or injection pump. In many African settings, dirty fuel is the culprit. Replace the fuel filter and bleed the system. Bleeding is critical. Air in the fuel system will stop a diesel engine. Locate the bleed screws on the filter housing and injection pump. Open them and pump the hand primer until bubble free fuel flows. Close them and try to start. If the engine starts but runs rough, check for air leaks on the suction side of the fuel system. Loose hose clamps and cracked hoses are common. Next, check the air intake. A clogged air filter will cause black smoke, low power, and high fuel consumption. Remove the filter and inspect it. If it is heavily dusted, clean or replace it. Check the intake hoses for cracks or loose clamps. A turbocharged engine with a failed turbo will produce blue or black smoke and very low power. Remove the intake hose and spin the turbo impeller by hand. It should spin freely without touching the housing. If it wobbles or scrapes, the turbo needs repair or replacement. Compression problems are more serious. If the engine has fuel and air but still will not start, or if it runs with heavy blow-by and blue smoke, you may have worn rings, valves, or a blown head gasket. A quick test is to remove the oil filler cap while the engine runs. If you feel strong pulses of air, you have blow-by. A compression tester will confirm. In the field, you can also do a leak-down test. These tests require some skill and tools. If you suspect compression loss, stop running the engine and call a mechanic. Continuing to run will cause more damage. Overheating is another common problem. If the temperature gauge rises, stop the machine immediately. Check the coolant level, fan belt, radiator fins, and thermostat. In dusty conditions, the radiator can be blocked externally. Clean it with compressed air or water. Check the water pump for leaks and bearing play. A failed thermostat can cause overheating or overcooling. Replace it if in doubt. Never add cold water to a hot engine; let it cool first. Oil pressure warnings are serious. If the oil light comes on, stop the engine. Check the oil level. If it is low, top up. If the level is correct, the oil pump, relief valve, or bearings may be worn. Do not run the engine until you find the cause. Low oil pressure can destroy an engine in minutes. Excessive smoke tells you a lot. Black smoke means too much fuel or not enough air. Check the air filter, turbo, and injectors. White smoke when warm means unburnt fuel, often from a faulty injector or low compression. Blue smoke means burning oil, usually worn rings or valve guides. Use these clues to narrow your diagnosis. Unusual noises also help. A knocking sound deep in the engine may be a bearing. A ticking sound may be a valve or injector. A squealing sound may be a belt or bearing. A hissing sound may be a leaking gasket or hose. Learn the normal sounds of your engine so you can detect changes. Fuel injectors need periodic testing and cleaning. In many African workshops, injector test benches are available. Remove injectors every 2000 to 3000 hours and have them tested. Clean or replace as needed. Never mix injector parts between cylinders. Injection pump timing is critical. If the engine is hard to start, lacks power, or knocks, the timing may be off. This requires a skilled technician with a timing tool. Do not attempt to adjust the injection pump without the manual and proper tools. Battery and charging problems are common. A diesel engine needs a strong battery to crank and to power the fuel solenoid. Check battery voltage with a multimeter. A fully charged battery should read 12.6 volts for a 12 volt system or 25.2 volts for a 24 volt system. If lower, charge it. Check the alternator output. It should be 13.8 to 14.4 volts for 12 volt systems. Clean and tighten all connections. In hot climates, batteries fail faster. Replace them every two to three years. Keep a spare battery or jump pack on site. Now let us put this into a field method. Use the acronym FUEL: Fuel, Air, Compression, and Electrical. Check in that order. Fuel first because it is the most common problem. Then air. Then compression. Then electrical. This saves time. Keep a small toolbox with you: spanners, screwdrivers, pliers, a multimeter, a fuel primer, spare filters, and a torch. Keep a notebook to record symptoms and fixes. Over time you will build a personal fault library. Finally, know your limits. Some repairs need a workshop, a crane, or a specialist. Trying to fix a major internal fault in the field can make it worse. Your job is to diagnose accurately and decide whether to repair on site or call for help. In the next lesson we will cover hydraulic system maintenance and repair. Before you move on, write down the last three breakdowns you experienced and identify which of the four needs was missing. That exercise will sharpen your diagnostic thinking.

3. Hydraulic System Maintenance and Repair

Welcome to Lesson 3. Hydraulic systems power excavators, loaders, tractors, presses, and many stationary machines. In Africa, hydraulic failures are often caused by contamination, heat, and poor maintenance. Repairing hydraulics is expensive, so prevention is the best strategy. This lesson covers how hydraulic systems work, how to maintain them, and how to diagnose common faults. A basic hydraulic system has a reservoir, a pump, valves, hoses, cylinders, and a motor or actuator. The pump creates flow, not pressure. Pressure is created by resistance to flow. When a cylinder meets a load, pressure rises. The relief valve protects the system by limiting maximum pressure. If the relief valve is stuck, pressure can rise too high and burst hoses or damage the pump. If it is stuck open, the system will be weak. Understanding this helps you diagnose. The number one enemy of hydraulics is contamination. Dirt, water, and metal particles cause wear in pumps, valves, and cylinders. In dusty African conditions, contamination enters through dirty couplings, damaged breathers, and poor oil handling. Your first defence is clean oil. Store hydraulic oil in sealed drums. Use a clean pump and filter when transferring. Never use a dirty bucket. Clean the area around the filler cap before opening. Use a filter cart if available. Check the reservoir breather monthly and replace it if clogged. Water in hydraulic oil causes corrosion and reduces lubricity. Check for water by draining a small amount from the bottom of the reservoir into a clear container. If you see separation, drain and replace the oil. In humid coastal areas, water ingress is common. Keep the reservoir full to reduce condensation. Temperature is the second enemy. Hydraulic oil overheats when the system is overloaded, when the cooler is blocked, or when the relief valve is bypassing. Hot oil thins out, reduces lubrication, and damages seals. Check the oil cooler daily. Clean the fins with compressed air. Ensure the fan is working. If the system runs hot, check the relief valve setting and the load. Do not keep running a hot system. Let it cool and find the cause. Filters must be changed on schedule. Most hydraulic systems have a return filter and sometimes a suction strainer. Change the return filter every 500 hours or when the indicator shows. Clean the suction strainer every 1000 hours. Use only the specified filter micron rating. A wrong filter can cause cavitation or bypass. Hoses and fittings need weekly inspection. Look for cracks, blisters, abrasion, and leaks. A pinhole leak can spray oil at high pressure and cause injury. Never use your hand to check for leaks; use a piece of cardboard. Replace any hose that shows damage. When replacing hoses, use the correct pressure rating and compatible fittings. Keep spare hoses and fittings on site. Cylinders need attention too. Check for leaks at the rod seal and the piston seal. A leaking rod seal drips oil and allows dirt in. Replace seals as soon as they leak. Check the rod for scoring or bending. A bent rod will damage the seal and the cylinder. Store spare cylinders upright and capped. Now let us diagnose common faults. If the system is slow or weak, check the oil level first. Low oil causes cavitation and poor performance. Then check the filter and suction strainer. A clogged filter will starve the pump. Next, check the relief valve. If it is stuck open, pressure will be low. Test with a pressure gauge at a test port. Compare to the specification. If pressure is low, adjust or replace the relief valve. If pressure is correct but the system is weak, the pump may be worn. A worn pump produces low flow at high pressure. You can test flow with a flow meter. If flow is low, replace or rebuild the pump. If a single function is weak, the problem is likely in that circuit: a worn cylinder, a leaking valve, or a damaged hose. Swap hoses or valves with another function to isolate the fault. If the system is noisy, check for air in the oil. Air causes a whining or cracking sound. Bleed the system at the highest point. Check the suction line for leaks. A loose clamp on the suction side will draw air in without leaking oil out. Tighten all clamps. If the noise is a knocking sound, the pump may be worn or misaligned. Check the coupling and alignment. If the system overheats, check the cooler, the relief valve, and the oil level. Also check for a restricted return line. A kinked or clogged return hose will cause high pressure and heat. If a cylinder drifts or settles under load, the piston seal is leaking internally. This is common on older machines. Rebuild the cylinder with a new seal kit. If a valve spool is stiff, the valve may be contaminated or the linkage may be misaligned. Clean the valve and lubricate the linkage. Do not force a stiff lever; you may bend the linkage. Preventive maintenance for hydraulics includes daily checks of oil level and leaks, weekly checks of hoses and fittings, monthly checks of the breather and cooler, and scheduled filter changes. Keep a log of oil analysis if you can. Oil analysis costs a little but tells you the condition of the oil and the machine. In many African cities, oil analysis labs are available. Take a sample every 500 hours. Finally, train your operators. Most hydraulic damage is caused by operators who ignore warning signs, run the machine with low oil, or use the wrong oil. Teach them to report leaks, noises, and heat immediately. A small leak fixed today prevents a major failure tomorrow. In the next lesson we will cover electrical systems and fault finding. Before you move on, inspect one hydraulic machine and write down the condition of its hoses, oil level, and cooler. Fix one small issue today.

4. Electrical Systems and Fault Finding

Welcome to Lesson 4. Electrical systems on machinery can seem mysterious, but with a logical approach you can diagnose most faults. In Africa, electrical problems are common because of dust, heat, vibration, poor wiring, and voltage fluctuations. This lesson covers basic electrical theory, tools, and a step by step fault finding method. Start with the basics. Electricity flows in a circuit from a power source, through a load, and back to the source. The source may be a battery, an alternator, or a mains supply. The load may be a motor, a light, a solenoid, or a sensor. A circuit needs a complete path. If the path is broken, the load will not work. A switch opens and closes the path. A fuse or circuit breaker protects the circuit from too much current. Voltage is the pressure, current is the flow, and resistance is the restriction. Use a multimeter to measure all three. A cheap multimeter is enough for most field work. Learn to use it. Measure voltage with the circuit powered. Measure resistance with the circuit off and the component disconnected. Measure current by putting the meter in series with the load. Never measure resistance on a live circuit. Before you touch any electrical system, disconnect the battery negative terminal unless the manual says otherwise. This prevents shorts and sparks. Wear safety glasses. Remove rings and watches. Use insulated tools. Now let us build a fault finding method. Start with the symptom. Does the machine not start? Does a light not work? Does a fuse blow? Does a motor run slow? Write down the symptom and when it happens. Then check the simple things first. Is the battery charged? Are the terminals clean and tight? Is the main switch on? Is the fuse blown? Is the emergency stop released? Many faults are simple. If the battery is weak, the engine will crank slowly or not at all. Check voltage. A 12 volt battery should read 12.6 volts at rest. If it reads below 12.0 volts, charge it. If it will not hold a charge, replace it. Check the alternator. Start the engine and measure voltage at the battery. It should rise to 13.8 to 14.4 volts. If not, the alternator or regulator is faulty. Check the belt tension and the wiring. If a fuse blows repeatedly, do not just replace it with a bigger fuse. That can cause a fire. Find the short. Disconnect the load and test. Use a multimeter to check for continuity to ground on the positive wire. If there is continuity, the wire is shorted. Trace the wire and repair or replace it. If a single light or accessory does not work, check the bulb, the switch, and the ground. A bad ground is a very common fault. Clean the ground connection and tighten it. If a motor runs slow or hot, check the voltage at the motor. If voltage is low, the wire may be too thin or the connections may be corroded. Check the current draw. If it is higher than the nameplate, the motor may be failing or the load may be too high. If a solenoid clicks but does not engage, the battery may be weak or the solenoid may be faulty. Test the solenoid with a multimeter. If the coil has no resistance, it is open. If it has very low resistance, it may be shorted. Replace it. Wiring in African conditions often suffers from heat and vibration. Look for cracked insulation, corroded connectors, and loose terminals. Use heat shrink tubing and dielectric grease on connections. Secure wires with clips and conduit. Avoid running wires near hot surfaces or sharp edges. Use the correct gauge wire for the load. Undersized wire causes voltage drop and heat. Keep a wiring diagram for each machine. If you do not have one, draw one as you trace circuits. Label wires and components. This saves time later. Use colour codes consistently. In many African workshops, wiring is a mess of mixed colours. Standardise your work. Now let us talk about batteries. Lead acid batteries need maintenance. Check the electrolyte level monthly and top up with distilled water. Clean the terminals with a wire brush and apply petroleum jelly. Keep the battery secure. Vibration damages plates. In hot climates, batteries fail faster. Test them every six months with a load tester. Replace them every two to three years. Keep a spare battery or a jump pack. Solar and inverter systems are increasingly common on farms and remote sites. These systems have their own maintenance needs. Check solar panel cleanliness monthly. Dust reduces output. Check the charge controller settings. Check the battery bank water level if flooded. Do not mix old and new batteries. Use the correct cable size. If the inverter alarms, check the load and the battery voltage. Finally, safety. Electricity can kill. Never work alone on live circuits. Use lockout tagout when working on machinery. Keep a fire extinguisher rated for electrical fires nearby. Do not use water on electrical fires. Train your team on basic electrical safety. In the next lesson we will cover workshop management and spare parts planning. Before you move on, take a multimeter and test the battery and charging system on one machine. Write down the readings. That practice will build your confidence.

5. Workshop Management and Spare Parts Planning

Welcome to Lesson 5. A well run workshop keeps machines working and profits flowing. In Africa, workshops face unique challenges: limited access to spare parts, unreliable power, dust, heat, and a shortage of skilled technicians. This lesson covers how to organise your workshop, manage spare parts, and lead a maintenance team. Start with layout. A good workshop has separate areas for cleaning, diagnosis, repair, and storage. Keep dirty jobs away from clean jobs. Have a dedicated wash bay. Have a clean bench for electrical and hydraulic work. Keep tools organised on shadow boards or in labelled drawers. A place for everything and everything in its place saves hours of searching. Lighting is important. Use LED lights where possible. They are efficient and last long. If mains power is unreliable, install a solar system with a battery bank. This keeps lights and small tools running. Ventilation is critical in hot climates. Use roof vents, fans, and open doors. But keep dust out of clean areas. Use curtains or plastic strips. Flooring should be concrete if possible. It is easy to clean and supports heavy machines. If concrete is too expensive, use compacted gravel and keep it level. Now let us talk about tools. You do not need every tool, but you need the right tools. Start with a good set of spanners, sockets, screwdrivers, pliers, hammers, and a torque wrench. Add a multimeter, a hydraulic pressure gauge, a compression tester, and a bearing puller. Buy quality tools from known brands. Cheap tools break and injure. Keep a tool register. Sign tools out and in. Replace broken tools immediately. Power tools need care. In dusty conditions, clean them after use. Use an air compressor to blow out dust. Check cords and plugs for damage. Use a residual current device on mains power. If power is unstable, use a voltage stabiliser. Now spare parts. This is where most African workshops struggle. Parts may take weeks to arrive. You must plan. Start by listing the parts that commonly fail on each machine. Use your maintenance records to identify them. For each part, decide whether to stock it, order it on demand, or fabricate it. Stock high use, low cost items: filters, belts, hoses, seals, bearings, fuses, bulbs, and lubricants. Keep a minimum and maximum level for each. When stock reaches minimum, order more. Use a simple bin card or a spreadsheet. Record every part in and out. Do not let parts sit on the shelf without a record. For expensive parts like injectors, pumps, and control units, keep one spare if the machine is critical. Otherwise, order on demand. Build relationships with suppliers. Have at least two suppliers for critical parts. Keep their contacts on the wall. Join a local business association or online group where you can ask for parts. In many African cities, WhatsApp groups connect buyers and sellers. Use them. Consider fabricating simple parts. A local welder or machinist can make brackets, pins, bushes, and guards. This saves time and money. Keep a stock of raw materials: steel plate, round bar, angle iron, and bolts. Now let us talk about people. A workshop is only as good as its team. Hire technicians with the right attitude, not just the right certificates. Train them continuously. Use the manuals and this course. Encourage them to report problems early. Create a culture where safety and quality matter. Assign clear responsibilities. One person should be in charge of tools, another of parts, another of records. Hold a short meeting every morning. Review the day s jobs, safety issues, and parts needs. Keep a job card for every repair. The job card should include the machine, the complaint, the diagnosis, the work done, the parts used, the time taken, and the technician s name. This helps you track costs and improve. Use a whiteboard or a simple computer. If you have no computer, use a notebook. The important thing is to record. Now let us talk about safety. Every workshop should have a first aid kit, a fire extinguisher, and a spill kit. Train everyone on their use. Wear personal protective equipment: boots, gloves, glasses, and hearing protection. Use jack stands, not just a jack, when working under machines. Never work under a raised load supported only by hydraulics. Use lockout tagout when repairing machinery. Keep the workshop clean. A clean workshop is a safe workshop. Finally, let us talk about business. Your workshop is not just a cost centre; it is a service provider. If you have capacity, offer maintenance services to other businesses. This brings in revenue and keeps your team sharp. Track your costs: labour, parts, tools, and overhead. Set your prices to cover costs and make a profit. Invoice promptly. Keep financial records. Separate business and personal money. In many African small businesses, mixing money is a major cause of failure. Open a business bank account or mobile money account. Use it for all business transactions. Now let us put it all together. A well managed workshop has a clean layout, the right tools, a spare parts plan, a trained team, and good records. It follows safety rules and tracks costs. It plans maintenance instead of reacting to breakdowns. It builds relationships with suppliers and customers. It uses simple systems that work in local conditions. You do not need a big budget to start. You need discipline and consistency. Start with one improvement today. Clean a bench. Label a shelf. Write a bin card. Train one person. Small steps lead to big results. This concludes Module 11. You now have the foundation to operate and repair machinery effectively in African conditions. Review the five lessons. Apply one action from each. Keep learning. Keep improving. Your machines will reward you with reliable service and better profits.

â  SAFETY WARNING â FUEL, OIL AND BATTERY HANDLING

Diesel, engine oil, hydraulic fluid and battery acid are hazardous. Observe the following without exception:

  • Never siphon diesel or petrol by mouth. Fuel aspiration causes chemical pneumonitis and can be fatal; use a hand pump only. Seek immediate medical care if fuel is swallowed or inhaled.
  • Battery electrolyte contains sulphuric acid. Wear eye protection and gloves. If acid contacts skin or eyes, flush with clean water for at least 15 minutes and seek medical attention immediately.
  • Used engine oil is a suspected carcinogen. Avoid prolonged skin contact; wash hands with soap, not petrol or solvent.
  • Store all fuels, oils and chemicals in labelled, closed containers away from children, livestock and water sources. Never burn used oil or filters in the open.
  • Run engines only in ventilated areas. Carbon monoxide from petrol and diesel engines is odourless and lethal.

Safety guidance based on WHO chemical safety and occupational health principles and MSD Veterinary Manual guidance on hydrocarbon exposure.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Daily before start-up: check engine oil, coolant level, fuel water trap, tyre pressure and visible leaks. Record hours.
  • Daily after work: dump dust from air pre-cleaner, grease high-load nipples, and park on level ground.
  • Every 200 hours: change engine oil and oil filter; inspect belts and hoses for cracking.
  • Every 250 hours: replace fuel filters, drain water separator, check valve clearance if due.
  • Every 500 hours: service air filter, inspect hydraulic hoses, test brakes and steering play.
  • Every 600â800 hours: change hydraulic and transmission oil; clean strainers and magnets.
  • Annually before peak season: flush cooling system, test injectors, inspect clutch and PTO, calibrate hour meter and record card.
  • Stock control: keep minimum stock of filters, oil, belts, hoses and one spare water pump; reorder at 30 per cent remaining stock.
