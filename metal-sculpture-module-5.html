1. Introduction to Casting: Sand Casting and Lost Wax

Casting is a 6000-year-old method for shaping metal by pouring it molten into a hollow mold. In sand casting, you pack a special sand mixture around a pattern (a replica of your sculpture), remove the pattern, and pour metal into the empty space. The sand is mixed with clay and water to hold its shape. A typical mix is 90% silica sand, 8% bentonite clay, and 2% water. For a small aluminum sculpture, you can use a two-part flask (cope and drag) made from wooden boxes. Lost wax casting is older and better for fine detail. You carve the sculpture in wax, encase it in a plaster-like ceramic shell, then heat it so the wax melts out (lost). Molten metal replaces the wax. In Africa, lost wax is famous in Benin City, Nigeria, where brass casters have used it since the 13th century. For your first project, sand casting is cheaper and faster. You can build a flask from scrap wood, and use a bucket of foundry sand. Lost wax needs more materials: microcrystalline wax, ceramic slurry, and a kiln for burnout. But lost wax gives you undercuts and thin sections. Start with sand casting for simple shapes like medallions or small figures. Both methods require safety gear: leather gloves, face shield, and a leather apron. Never pour metal over concreteit can explode from moisture. Use a dry sand bed or steel plate.

2. Pattern Making and Mold Preparation

A pattern is a copy of your sculpture, but slightly larger to allow for metal shrinkage. Aluminum shrinks about 1.3% when it cools, so if you want a final piece 10 cm long, make the pattern 10.13 cm. Patterns can be made from wood, plastic, or metal. For simple shapes, carve a pattern from medium-density fiberboard (MDF) or pine. Sand the pattern smooth and apply a coat of shellac to seal it. You need a draft angletaper the sides by 1 to 2 degrees so you can pull the pattern out without breaking the sand. For a two-part mold, you need a parting line. Place the pattern on a flat board, then pack sand around it in the drag (bottom flask). Flip it over, place the cope (top flask), and pack more sand. Before packing, dust the pattern with talcum powder or parting dust so sand doesn't stick. Ram the sand firmly with a mallettoo loose and the mold collapses; too hard and gases can't escape. After packing, remove the pattern by tapping it gently and lifting straight up. Cut a sprue hole (for pouring) and a riser (for gas escape) with a dowel. For lost wax, you dip the wax tree in ceramic slurry and stucco (fine sand) repeatedly7 to 10 coats, drying between each. Then you melt out the wax in a kiln at 150C, then fire the shell at 700C. In Africa, many foundries use local clay mixed with cow dung for mold makingit works if you test it first.

3. Melting and Pouring Metal

Aluminum is the easiest metal to melt for sculpture. It melts at 660C. You can use a charcoal furnace made from a 20-litre metal bucket lined with clay and sand. Use charcoal or coke as fuel, and a blower (hair dryer or hand-cranked fan) to force air into the fire. A cruciblea clay-graphite cupholds the metal. Never use a steel pot; it will melt. Put scrap aluminum (old car rims, window frames, cans) into the crucible. Cans are too thin and oxidize; better to use cast aluminum scrap. Heat until the metal is fully liquid and shiny like mercury. Skim off dross (impurities) with a steel spoon. Pouring is the most dangerous step. Wear a face shield, leather gloves, and a leather apron. Preheat your mold to 200300C to remove moistureif you pour into a cold mold, steam can explode molten metal back at you. Pour in one steady stream, keeping the crucible close to the sprue. Don't stop halfway. After pouring, wait until the metal turns from red to gray (about 1015 minutes for a small casting). Then break the sand mold away with a hammer. For lost wax, quench the shell in water after it coolsthe ceramic shatters off. In Africa, many artisans melt aluminum in small charcoal furnaces in open-air workshops. Always work outdoors or in a well-ventilated shed. Never pour near water, concrete, or flammable materials.

4. Cleaning and Finishing Castings

After the casting cools, you need to remove the sprue, riser, and any sand or ceramic stuck to the surface. Use a hacksaw or angle grinder with a cutting disc to cut off the sprue. Then grind the stubs flush with a grinding wheel or flap disc. For detailed areas, use a rotary tool (Dremel) with a carbide burr. Sand the surface progressively: 80 grit, then 120, 220, 400, and 800 if you want a polished finish. For a matte finish, stop at 220. Aluminum casts often have small pits and porosity. You can fill these with epoxy mixed with aluminum powder, or weld them with a TIG welder if you have one. For lost wax castings, the surface is usually smoother, but you may need to chase fine details with a graver. After sanding, clean the piece with a wire brush and soapy water. If you want a patina, you can heat the metal and apply wax or oil, or use chemical patinas like liver of sulfur (for darker tones) or ferric nitrate (for brown). In Africa, many sculptors use local materials: palm oil heated on the fire gives a rich black finish. Or bury the casting in wet ash for a weekit darkens naturally. Always wear a dust mask when sanding or grinding, and safety glasses. Finally, sign your work with a metal stamp or engrave your initials. A finished casting should have no sharp edgesfile them smooth.

5. Safety in Foundry Work

Foundry work can kill you if you ignore safety. Molten aluminum is 660Cit will burn through flesh in a second. Never wear synthetic clothing (polyester, nylon) because it melts onto skin. Wear cotton or wool, leather boots, leather gloves, a leather apron, and a face shield. Tie back long hair. Work in a well-ventilated areaoutdoors is best. Carbon monoxide from charcoal furnaces can kill you in a closed room. Keep a bucket of dry sand or a fire extinguisher nearby. Never use water on a metal fireit explodes. For small spills, smother with dry sand. Before pouring, check your mold for moisture. Any dampness causes steam explosions. Preheat molds to at least 200C. When melting, never look directly into the crucibleuse a face shield. Use long-handled tongs to move the crucible. Never leave a hot crucible unattended. After pouring, let the mold cool completely before breaking it open. Hot metal can hide under sand. When grinding or sanding, wear a dust mask and safety glasses. Aluminum dust is flammablekeep it away from sparks. In Africa, many foundries operate informally. Insist on basic safety: gloves, face shield, and a clear work area. Never work alone. Have a partner who can call for help. If you get a burn, cool it with clean water for 20 minutes and seek medical help. Prevention is cheaper than a hospital bill.

6. Project: Cast Aluminum Sculpture

For this project, you will cast a small aluminum sculpture using sand casting. Choose a simple shape: a turtle, a mask, or a geometric form. First, make a pattern from MDF. Carve or sand it to shape, leaving a 1.3% extra size for shrinkage. Seal it with shellac. Build a two-part flask from wood: a bottom (drag) and top (cope) that fit together with alignment pins. Place the pattern in the drag, pack foundry sand around it, and ram it firm. Flip, place the cope, and pack sand again. Remove the pattern, cut a sprue and riser. Melt about 1 kg of aluminum scrap in a clay-graphite crucible in a charcoal furnace. Preheat the mold to 250C. Pour the molten aluminum into the sprue in one steady stream. Wait 15 minutes, then break the sand away. Cut off the sprue with a hacksaw. Grind and sand the surface: 80, 120, 220 grit. For a finish, heat the sculpture with a torch and rub with palm oilit will turn black and glossy. Or leave it raw and polish with a wire brush. Sign your work. This project takes about 4 hours. If you don't have a furnace, you can use a local blacksmith's forge or a community foundry. In Africa, many artisans cast aluminum from recycled car parts. Your total cost: sand (free if you dig your own), clay (free), charcoal (about $2), and scrap aluminum (free or cheap). The skills you learn here can become a businesscast aluminum sculptures sell in markets and galleries. Practice with simple shapes before attempting complex ones.

<h2>Practical Reference: Metal Casting for African Sculpture Studios</h2>

<p>Metal casting turns scrap aluminium, brass, and bronze into sellable art. In African studiosfrom the brass casters of Benin City to the aluminium recyclers of Kumasi and the foundries around Johannesburgcasting is a business, not just a craft. This Practical Reference gives you the numbers, materials, and process controls you need to run a casting operation that produces consistent, profitable sculpture.</p>

<p>The core economics are simple: your input is scrap metal (often 3060% cheaper than virgin ingot), your output is a finished sculpture that can sell for 550 the metal value depending on design, finish, and market. The margin lives in your yield rate, your mould cost per piece, and your finishing quality.</p>

Your choice determines your pricing model.</p>

<h2>2. Pattern Making and Mould Preparation</h2>

<p>A pattern is a replica of your sculpture, oversized by the shrinkage allowance. For aluminium, pattern shrinkage is <strong>1.3%</strong>; for bronze, <strong>1.5%</strong>; for brass, <strong>1.6%</strong>. Get this wrong and your finished piece is undersizeda fatal error for edition work.</p>

<p><strong>Pattern materials:</strong> In African workshops, patterns are often carved from hardwood (mahogany, iroko), cast in epoxy, or 3D-printed in PLA. For sand casting, add draft angles of 12 per side so the pattern lifts cleanly. For lost wax, carve the wax directly or print a master and make a silicone mould for wax injection.</p>

<p><strong>Sand preparation:</strong> Green sand is a mix of silica sand, bentonite clay (binder), water, and sometimes coal dust. A workable recipe for small foundries: 100 parts sand, 812 parts bentonite, 35 parts water. Test by squeezing a handfulit should hold shape but crumble when broken. Too wet causes steam defects; too dry causes mould collapse.</p>

<p><strong>Ramming:</strong> Ram sand in three layers (bottom, middle, top), each about 2530 mm, using a rammer or mallet. Under-ramming causes metal penetration; over-ramming traps gases. Vent the mould with a wire before closing.</p>

<h2>3. Melting and Pouring</h2>

<p>Most African sculpture studios melt aluminium in a <strong>crucible furnace</strong> fired by charcoal, LPG, or waste oil. Aluminium melts at <strong>660C</strong>; pour temperature is typically <strong>700750C</strong>. Bronze (Cu-Sn) melts at 9501000C; pour at 10501150C.</p>

<p><strong>Pouring technique:</strong> Preheat the crucible, skim dross from the melt, and pour in one continuous stream into the sprue. Do not stop mid-pourcold shuts and misruns result. Keep the sprue full so the riser feeds shrinkage.</p>

<h2>4. Cleaning and Finishing</h2>

<p>After the casting cools (212 hours depending on mass), break out the sand, cut off the sprue and risers with an angle grinder or hacksaw, and grind the gates flush. Then:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Shot blasting or wire brushing</strong> to remove sand and scale.</li>
<li><strong>Filing and sanding</strong> (80 400 grit) for a smooth surface.</li>
<li><strong>Polishing</strong> with a buffing wheel and compound for a mirror finish, or leave a patina for a rustic look.</li>
<li><strong>Patination</strong> with liver of sulphur (for bronze/brass) or potassium permanganate (for aluminium) to create browns, blacks, and greens that command higher prices.</li>
<li><strong>Clear-coat</strong> with lacquer or wax to prevent oxidationcritical in humid coastal markets.</li>
</ul>

<p>Finishing is where your margin lives. A raw casting might sell for $20; the same piece finished and mounted on a hardwood base can sell for $150$400 in gallery or export markets.</p>

<p>Worked example for a 3 kg cast aluminium sculpture:</p>

<li>Scrap aluminium: 5 kg at $1.50/kg = <strong>$7.50</strong> (yield 60%)</li>
<li>Mould materials (sand, bentonite, wax): <strong>$4.00</strong></li>
<li>Fuel (LPG/charcoal): <strong>$3.00</strong></li>
<li>Labour (2 hours at $3/hr): <strong>$6.00</strong></li>
<li>Finishing and base: <strong>$5.00</strong></li>
<li><strong>Total cost: $25.50</strong></li>
<li>Wholesale price (3): <strong>$76.50</strong> | Retail (6): <strong>$153</strong></li>

<p>At 20 pieces per month, that is $1,530$3,060 in revenue against $510 in materials and laboura viable micro-enterprise. Export and gallery channels can push unit prices higher.</p>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>
<ol>
<li> Pattern oversized by shrinkage allowance (Al 1.3%, bronze 1.5%, brass 1.6%).</li>
<li> Sand mixed to correct moistureholds shape, crumbles when broken.</li>
<li> Mould rammed in 3 layers, vented, and closed with sprue aligned.</li>
<li> Metal preheated, dross skimmed, poured in one continuous stream.</li>
<li> Pour temperature within range (Al 700750C, bronze 10501150C).</li>
<li> Full PPE worn: face shield, leather apron, gauntlets, boots, cotton cap.</li>
<li> Dry sand bucket and extinguisher within 3 m; never pour alone.</li>
<li> Casting cooled fully before knockout; sprue and gates ground flush.</li>
<li> Finished, patinated, and clear-coated before sale or export.</li>
</ol>
