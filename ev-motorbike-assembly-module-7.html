Lessons

1. Tire Mounting on Rim

Mounting a tire on a rim for an electric motorbike is a hands-on job you can do with basic tools. First, inspect the rim for dents or sharp edges that could puncture the tube. Clean the rim with a wire brush and apply a thin layer of rubber lubricant or soapy water to the tire bead and rim edges. This helps the tire slide on without damage. Place the rim on a sturdy surface or use a rim holder. If you have a tire changing stand, great; if not, kneel on the tire to keep it in place.

Start by pushing one side of the tire onto the rim. Use two tire levers, working opposite each other. Insert the first lever under the bead and hook it onto a spoke. Insert the second lever about 10 cm away and work your way around. Be careful not to pinch the inner tube. For tubeless tires, you need a good seal; for tube-type, insert the tube partially inflated (just enough to hold shape) into the tire before mounting the second bead.

Once the first bead is on, insert the valve stem through the rim hole. If it's a tube, feed the valve through and secure it with the nut. Then mount the second bead, again using levers, working slowly to avoid pinching the tube. When the tire is fully on, check that the tube is not trapped between the tire and rim. Inflate slowly to about 20 psi (1.4 bar) to seat the beads. You should hear a pop as the bead seats. Then deflate and re-inflate to the recommended pressure, usually 28-32 psi (2-2.2 bar) for motorbike tires.

In Africa, many workshops use simple tools: two or three tire levers, a rubber mallet, and a hand pump. Avoid using screwdrivers as levers; they can damage the tire bead. If you struggle, warm the tire in the sun for 10 minutes to make it more pliable. Always check the tire direction arrow; mounting backwards affects grip and water dispersion. After mounting, spin the wheel to check for wobbles. A well-mounted tire will spin true without side-to-side movement.

2. Balancing Wheels

Wheel balancing prevents vibrations that can cause fatigue, uneven tire wear, and unsafe handling. For an electric motorbike, smooth operation is crucial because the motor delivers instant torque. You need a balancing stand or a simple homemade balancer: an axle supported by two stands with bearings. Remove the wheel from the bike and mount it on the balancer. Spin the wheel gently and let it stop on its own. The heavy spot will settle at the bottom.

Mark the heavy spot with chalk. Add balancing weights opposite the heavy spot, on the rim. Start with 10-gram weights. You can buy adhesive wheel weights or use lead-free alternatives. Clean the rim area with alcohol before sticking weights. Spin again; if the wheel stops randomly, it's balanced. If it still settles, add more weight. For motorbike wheels, you rarely need more than 30 grams. In Africa, some mechanics use small fishing weights or even coins taped to the rim as a temporary fix, but proper adhesive weights are cheap and reliable.

Static balancing is usually enough for motorbike wheels up to 120 km/h. For higher speeds, dynamic balancing is better, but that requires a spin balancer, which is rare in rural workshops. Focus on static balancing. Check balance after mounting a new tire or repairing a puncture. Also, ensure the valve stem is not contributing to imbalance; if you have a long metal valve, it can add weight. Use a short rubber valve if possible.

After balancing, reinstall the wheel and test ride at low speed. If you feel handlebar vibration, rebalance. Remember, a balanced wheel reduces stress on bearings and suspension, extending the life of your EV motorbike. It's a 15-minute job that makes a big difference.

3. Installing Wheel Bearings

Wheel bearings allow the wheel to spin smoothly on the axle. Worn bearings cause wobbling, noise, and can damage the axle. For EV motorbikes, bearings must handle the weight of batteries and motor. First, remove the old bearings using a bearing puller or a long punch and hammer. Tap evenly around the inner race to drive it out. Clean the hub thoroughly with a rag and inspect for cracks or wear. If the hub is damaged, replace it.

Get new bearings of the correct size. Check your motorbike manual or measure the old bearing: inner diameter, outer diameter, and width (e.g., 6202-2RS). Use sealed bearings (2RS) to keep out dust and water, which is important on African roads. Apply a thin layer of high-temperature grease to the bearing outer race and the hub bore. Never grease sealed bearings; they are pre-lubricated.

To install, use a bearing driver or a socket that matches the outer race. Tap the bearing in squarely. A common mistake is hitting the inner race, which damages the bearing. Tap gently with a rubber mallet until the bearing is fully seated against the hub shoulder. Install the spacer tube between bearings if your wheel has one. Then install the second bearing the same way. After installation, the wheel should spin freely with no play. If it feels rough, you may have seated it crooked; remove and try again.

In many African workshops, a large socket and a hammer are used as a bearing driver. That works if you're careful. Alternatively, heat the hub slightly with a heat gun (not open flame) to expand it, making installation easier. After installing, pack some grease between the bearings and the seals. Reassemble the wheel and check for side-to-side play by grabbing the tire and shaking. Any movement means bearings are loose or worn. Replace if needed.

4. Mounting Front Wheel to Forks

Mounting the front wheel requires attention to alignment and brake components. Start by supporting the motorbike so the front wheel is off the ground. Use a centre stand or a block under the motor. Remove the axle nut and slide the axle out. Clean the axle with a rag and apply a light coat of grease. Inspect the fork dropouts for cracks or damage. If you have a disc brake, remove the caliper first to avoid damaging the brake pads.

Lift the wheel into place between the fork legs. Make sure the brake disc slides between the brake pads. Insert the axle from the correct side (usually left, but check your manual). The axle should slide through the wheel hub, spacers, and fork legs. Do not force it; if it's tight, check that spacers are in the right order. Spacers are critical: they centre the wheel and prevent fork damage. Typically, there's a spacer on each side, but some bikes have a speedometer drive on one side.

Once the axle is through, install the axle nut and tighten it finger-tight. Then tighten the pinch bolts on the fork legs. For most motorbikes, the axle nut torque is 50-70 Nm (37-52 lb-ft) and pinch bolts 15-20 Nm (11-15 lb-ft). Use a torque wrench if available; if not, tighten firmly but don't overdo it. Over-tightening can crush the fork legs. Spin the wheel to check for free rotation and brake drag. If the brake rubs, you may need to centre the caliper.

After tightening, pump the front brake a few times to seat the pads. Check that the wheel is centred between the forks. If it's off, adjust spacers. Finally, lower the bike and test ride slowly. In Africa, where roads can be rough, ensure all bolts are tight and use thread-locking compound on the axle nut. A properly mounted front wheel is essential for safe steering.

5. Mounting Rear Wheel to Swingarm

The rear wheel of an EV motorbike often includes the hub motor, so mounting is more complex. First, support the bike so the rear wheel is off the ground. Remove the chain guard and disconnect the brake rod or cable if drum brake. For disc brakes, remove the caliper. Loosen the axle nut and chain adjuster bolts. Slide the wheel forward to release the chain from the sprocket, then lift the wheel out.

When installing, first ensure the swingarm is clean and the chain adjusters are free. Place the wheel into the swingarm, aligning the brake disc with the caliper slot or the drum brake shoes with the drum. Insert the axle from the left side, making sure spacers are in place. The rear axle usually has a spacer on each side and sometimes a tensioner block. Do not forget the chain adjuster plates. Slide the axle through and install the nut finger-tight.

Now adjust the chain tension. With the bike on the ground, the chain should have 20-30 mm of slack at the tightest point. Rotate the wheel to find the tightest spot. Adjust the chain adjuster bolts evenly on both sides. Use the marks on the swingarm to keep the wheel aligned. Tighten the axle nut to 60-80 Nm (44-59 lb-ft). Then tighten the adjuster lock nuts. Spin the wheel to check for free movement and brake drag. If the wheel binds, check that spacers are correct and the brake isn't rubbing.

For hub motor wheels, the axle often has a torque arm to prevent spinning. Ensure the torque arm is securely bolted to the swingarm. Also, check the motor cable for damage and secure it with zip ties. In Africa, where loads can be heavy, use high-quality bearings and check chain tension regularly. After mounting, test ride and re-check chain tension, as it may change after the first ride. A well-mounted rear wheel ensures efficient power transfer and safety.

6. Checking Wheel Alignment and Spacers

Wheel alignment ensures the front and rear wheels are in line, which is critical for stability and tire wear. Misalignment causes the bike to pull to one side and can lead to accidents. Start by placing the bike on a level surface. Use a straight edge, like a long plank or string, against the rear wheel. The string should touch the front and rear of the rear tire and then extend to the front tire. The gap between the string and the front tire should be equal on both sides. If not, adjust the rear wheel alignment using the chain adjusters.

Spacers are small but vital. They keep the wheel centred and prevent bearings from being crushed. Always check that spacers are installed in the correct order. A missing or wrong spacer can cause the wheel to sit off-centre, leading to brake rub, bearing failure, and poor handling. When you remove a wheel, lay out the spacers in the order they came off. If you're unsure, consult a parts diagram. Common spacers include a left and right side spacer, and sometimes a speedometer drive or torque arm spacer.

Check for play by grabbing the wheel at the top and bottom and trying to rock it. Any movement indicates worn bearings or loose axle. Also, spin the wheel and watch for side-to-side wobble; if the rim wobbles, it may need truing. For alignment, you can also use a laser alignment tool, but a string line is cheap and effective. In African workshops, a simple method is to sight down the chain from behind; the chain should run straight from the rear sprocket to the front sprocket. If it's angled, the rear wheel is misaligned.

After adjusting, tighten all bolts to spec and re-check. Take a test ride on a straight road and note if the bike tracks straight. If it pulls, re-adjust. Proper alignment and spacers extend tire life and ensure safe riding. Remember, a small misalignment can become a big problem at speed. Always double-check your work.

â  SAFETY WARNING â WHEEL AND TIRE ASSEMBLY

Never inflate a tire that has not been fully seated on the rim. A partially seated bead can burst off the rim under pressure and cause severe injury â this is the most common serious workshop accident in tire fitting. Inflate in small increments (20 kPa / 3 psi), check bead seating around the full circumference, and keep hands and face clear of the tire sidewall. Use a clip-on air chuck with an extension hose, never hold the valve by hand. Always wear eye protection. If a bead will not seat, deflate completely, re-lubricate, and start again â do not force it. For tubeless EV rims, inspect for rim damage before mounting; a bent rim lip is a failure waiting to happen at speed.

Step-by-Step Practical Workflow

1. Tire mounting on rim. Inspect the rim for dents, cracks, and corrosion. Fit the tube lightly inflated inside the tire, align the valve with the rim hole, and work the first bead over the rim by hand, using levers only for the final section. Lubricate the bead with soapy water. Seat the second bead, then inflate slowly to the recommended pressure. Check that the tire bead line is even all around.

2. Balancing wheels. Mount the wheel on a balance stand with the axle supported on low-friction bearings. Let it settle; the heavy point drops to the bottom. Add weights opposite the heavy point until the wheel stays put at any position. Residual imbalance should be â¤ 5 g. Re-check after tire seating.

3. Installing wheel bearings. Remove old bearings with a drift or blind bearing puller. Clean the hub bore. Press new sealed bearings squarely using a driver that contacts the outer race only. Seat fully against the shoulder. Fit the spacer tube and dust seals. Spin the axle â it should turn smoothly with no lateral play.

4. Mounting front wheel to forks. Fit any required spacers and the speedometer drive (if fitted). Insert the axle from the correct side, slide the wheel into the fork dropouts, and loosely fit the axle nut. Compress the fork a few times to settle the axle, then torque the axle nut to spec. Check that the wheel spins freely and the brake disc aligns with the caliper.

5. Mounting rear wheel to swingarm. Fit spacers, chain/belt adjusters, and the brake disc or drum into position. Insert the axle and align the wheel in the swingarm. Set chain or belt tension to the manufacturer's specification (typically 20â30 mm slack). Torque the axle nut. Re-check that the wheel spins true and the brake operates correctly.

6. Checking wheel alignment and spacers. Confirm all spacers are present and on the correct side â a missing spacer is a common cause of brake drag and bearing failure. Check rear wheel alignment using a straight edge along the swingarm or by measuring from the swingarm pivot to the axle on both sides. Verify rim runout with a dial gauge: radial â¤ 1.0 mm, lateral â¤ 0.8 mm. Spin the wheel and listen for bearing noise.

Quality Control and Business Impact

Every wheel that leaves your bench should pass three checks: spin test (free rotation, no noise), torque verification (paint-mark or re-check), and runout measurement. Document these on a simple assembly sheet. In fleet contracts â for example, supplying 50 units to a delivery operator â a documented QC process is often the difference between winning the tender and losing it. Riders in Africa ride hard; your reputation is built on wheels that do not fail.
