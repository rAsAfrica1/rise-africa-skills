1. Determining Harvest Maturity Indices

Knowing exactly when to harvest cabbage is critical. If you harvest too early, heads are small and loose; too late, they split and rot. For most African varieties, maturity is reached 70 to 120 days after transplanting, depending on the cultivar and climate. But days alone are not enough. You must check the head itself.

A mature cabbage head is firm and solid when you squeeze it. The outer leaves should be thick and waxy, and the head should feel heavy for its size. The bottom leaves may start to yellow and dry, but the head remains tight. Another sign: when you cut a mature head, the core is short and the internal leaves are crisp. If the head feels spongy or the leaves are still loose, wait a few more days.

In hot weather, cabbages mature faster and may split if left too long. In cool highlands, they take longer but hold longer. Check fields every 23 days once heads start to form. For market, harvest when heads are fully firm but before they crack. For storage, harvest slightly less mature to reduce splitting. Use a refractometer? Not necessary. Your hand and eye are enough.

A simple test: cut one or two representative heads. If the cut surface is solid white or pale green with no gaps, and the core is less than 3 cm long, it's ready. Also, the head should not have started to bolt (flower stalk). Bolting makes the head bitter and unmarketable. So watch for a central stalk pushing up.

In Africa, many farmers harvest by size: a head that fits in two hands (about 12 kg) is ideal for local markets. For export, smaller heads (0.81.2 kg) are preferred. Know your market. Always harvest in the cool of the dayearly morning or late afternoonto reduce field heat. This simple practice extends shelf life by days.

2. Harvesting Tools and Techniques

Harvesting cabbage is not just pulling heads off. You need the right tools and a clean technique to avoid damage and disease. The basic tool is a sharp knife or a machete. A pruning saw also works for tough stems. Do not twist or tear the head; that creates wounds where rot enters.

Before you start, clean your tools with a disinfectantdiluted bleach (1 part bleach to 9 parts water) or rubbing alcohol. This prevents spreading diseases like black rot from plant to plant. Wear gloves to protect your hands and reduce bruising. Harvest during the coolest part of the day. If you must harvest in heat, keep heads in shade immediately.

To cut: hold the head with one hand, and with the other, cut the stem about 23 cm below the head. Leave 23 wrapper leaves attachedthey protect the head during transport. Do not cut too close to the head or you expose the core. For a whole plant harvest, cut at ground level. For a partial harvest (if you want a second crop), cut higher, leaving the lower leaves to photosynthesize. But most cabbage is harvested once.

After cutting, place heads gently into crates or basketsnever throw them. A plastic crate (like a tomato crate) is ideal because it stacks and ventilates. If using baskets, line with clean cloth or paper to prevent bruising. Do not pile more than 34 heads high. Field heat is the enemy: move harvested heads to shade within 30 minutes. If you have a cool room, pre-cool to 510C within 2 hours. In rural areas, a shaded shed with wet sacks can help.

Never leave harvested cabbage in the sun. Even one hour of direct sun can cause wilting and sunscald. Also, do not wash cabbage in the field unless you have clean water and will dry it properly. Excess moisture promotes rot. A simple rule: harvest, shade, cool, transport. That sequence keeps quality high.

3. Field Sorting and Grading

Sorting and grading right in the field saves time and money. You separate marketable heads from rejects and classify by size and quality. This ensures you get the best price and reduces waste. Do it immediately after harvest, in a shaded area.

First, remove any heads with obvious defects: splits, insect damage, disease spots, or soft spots. These are culls. Culls can be sold for processing (e.g., sauerkraut) or fed to livestock, but never mixed with good heads. Next, grade the good heads into categories. Common grades: Grade A (excellent, firm, uniform, 11.5 kg), Grade B (good, slightly less uniform, 0.81 kg), and Grade C (small or slightly misshapen, for local market). For export, standards are stricter: heads must be 0.81.2 kg, compact, with 23 wrapper leaves, no blemishes.

Use a simple sizing ring or a scale. A sizing ring is a circle of wire or plastic that heads must pass through. For example, a 12 cm diameter ring for small heads, 15 cm for medium, 18 cm for large. Or weigh a sample: 1 kg is a good medium. Grade by weight and firmness. Firmness: squeeze gentlyif it gives, it's not Grade A.

Sorting also means removing loose outer leaves. But leave 23 wrapper leaves for protection. Trim the stem to 12 cm. Do not over-trim; that shortens shelf life. Work on a clean tarpaulin or table. Never sort on bare groundsoil carries pathogens. Use clean crates for each grade. Label them clearly. If you have many workers, assign one person per grade to speed up.

After sorting, keep grades separate. Mixing grades lowers your price. A buyer for Grade A will not want Grade C mixed in. Also, record how many crates of each grade you have. This helps you plan sales and negotiate. In African markets, Grade A often goes to supermarkets or exporters, Grade B to urban markets, Grade C to local vendors or processing. Know your buyer's requirements before you harvest.

4. Packaging and Storage Requirements

Proper packaging and storage keep cabbage fresh for weeks instead of days. Cabbage is a hardy vegetable, but it still needs care. The goal is to reduce respiration, water loss, and mechanical damage.

For packaging, use clean, ventilated crates or boxes. Plastic crates (like those for tomatoes) are excellent because they stack, allow airflow, and are easy to clean. If using cartons, ensure they have holes for ventilation. Never use closed plastic bagsthey trap moisture and cause rot. For local market, you can use woven baskets lined with paper, but stack only 23 layers. For export, use waxed cartons with 510 kg capacity. Each head should be snug but not crushed. You can wrap heads individually in newspaper or tissue paper to reduce bruising and moisture loss. This is common for high-value markets.

Storage temperature is key. Cabbage stores best at 02C with 9095% relative humidity. At these conditions, it can last 36 months. Without refrigeration, you can store for 24 weeks in a cool, dark, well-ventilated room. In rural Africa, a traditional storage structure like a ventilated shed or a pit can work. A pit storage: dig a pit 1 m deep, line with straw, place heads in layers, cover with straw and soil, leave a vent. This keeps temperature stable. But check regularly for rot.

Do not store cabbage with apples, bananas, or other ethylene-producing fruits. Ethylene speeds up aging. Also, keep away from strong-smelling chemicals. For short-term storage, a cool room with a fan and wet floor can help. If you have a solar cooler, even better. Always pre-cool before storage. Remove any damaged heads before storing. Inspect stored cabbage every week and remove rotting heads immediately. A single rotting head can spread disease to the whole batch. Good storage means good monitoring.

5. Post-Harvest Diseases and Control

Post-harvest diseases can destroy your harvest within days. The most common are bacterial soft rot, black rot, and gray mold. These are caused by pathogens that enter through wounds or natural openings. Prevention is better than cure.

Bacterial soft rot: caused by Erwinia bacteria. It turns heads into a smelly, slimy mess. It enters through cuts, bruises, or insect damage. To control: handle gently, avoid overwatering before harvest, and never wash with dirty water. If you see a soft rot head, remove it immediatelydo not compost near the field. Black rot: caused by Xanthomonas bacteria. It causes yellow V-shaped lesions on leaves and blackened veins. It spreads through seed and crop debris. Use certified disease-free seed, rotate crops, and remove infected plants. Gray mold (Botrytis): appears as fuzzy gray growth, especially in cool, humid storage. It enters through wounds. Control by avoiding excess moisture and keeping storage ventilated.

Other diseases: Alternaria leaf spot (dark spots), downy mildew (white growth on underside). These are mostly field diseases but can continue in storage. The key is to start with healthy heads. Do not harvest wet cabbagewait until dew dries. Use clean tools and crates. Sanitize storage rooms with bleach solution before loading. Keep storage temperature low (02C) and humidity moderate (9095%).

If you see disease in storage, act fast. Remove affected heads. Increase ventilation. Do not wash heads after harvest unless you can dry them thoroughly. For organic control, some farmers use a dip in hot water (50C for 2 minutes) to reduce surface pathogens, but this can damage heads if not done carefully. Chemical control is limited post-harvest; most fungicides are for field use. So focus on sanitation and handling. A clean harvest is a healthy harvest.

6. Transportation and Market Preparation

Transportation is the final step that gets your cabbage to buyers in good condition. Poor transport ruins all your hard work. Plan ahead to minimize damage and delay.

First, prepare heads for market. Trim stems to 12 cm, remove loose outer leaves but leave 23 wrapper leaves. Grade and pack in clean, ventilated crates. If you are selling to supermarkets or exporters, follow their specific packaging requirements: they may want heads wrapped, labeled, and stacked in waxed cartons. For local markets, simple crates or baskets are fine. Label each crate with your farm name, grade, and weight. This builds trust.

Loading is critical. Do not throw or drop crates. Stack them so they do not crush the bottom layer. Use a truck with a covered bed or a tarpaulin to protect from sun and rain. If open truck, cover with a white tarpaulin to reflect heat. Never transport cabbage with strong-smelling goods like fish or chemicalscabbage absorbs odors. Also, do not mix with ethylene-producing fruits (bananas, apples). If possible, transport in the cool of the day or at night. For long distances, pre-cool heads and use refrigerated trucks. If not available, use ice packs in crates or wet sacks on top to keep cool.

Time matters. The shorter the transport, the better. For local markets, deliver within 24 hours. For distant markets, aim for 48 hours maximum without cooling. Upon arrival, unload immediately into shade. If you are selling at a market, keep heads in shade and sprinkle water on the ground (not on heads) to cool the air. Display heads neatly; do not pile too high. For export, you need a cold chain from farm to port. Work with a logistics company that understands perishables.

Finally, keep records: date harvested, grade, weight, destination, and any problems. This helps you improve. A good transport plan means higher prices and repeat buyers.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Harvesting and Post-Harvest Handling of Cabbage in Africa

In African smallholder and emerging commercial cabbage production, post-harvest losses routinely reach 3050% between field and market (FAO, 2022). Every percentage point recovered is direct margin. This practical reference consolidates the decision rules, tools, numbers, and market preparation steps that determine whether a cabbage crop earns money or becomes compost. Treat it as a bench manual: use it in the field, at the shed, and when negotiating with buyers.

Business framing: cabbage is a high-volume, low-unit-value crop. Profit comes from head weight marketable percentage price per kg, minus transport and shrinkage. Post-harvest handling is therefore a revenue function, not a chore. A 1-hectare crop yielding 40 t/ha at 35% loss sells 26 t; at 12% loss it sells 35.2 t. At USD 0.30/kg, that difference is USD 2,760 per hectare often more than the entire input budget.

1. Determining Harvest Maturity Indices

Cabbage is non-climacteric: it does not ripen or improve after cutting. Harvest decisions are therefore final. The commercial maturity index is head firmness plus target head weight for the buyer's specification, not calendar age alone.

  • Firmness test: squeeze the head. A market-ready head resists thumb pressure and does not spring back.
  • Head weight: 1.01.5 kg for fresh market; 1.52.5 kg for processing or wholesale bulk.
  • Days after transplant: 6590 days for most open-pollinated and hybrid varieties in tropical highlands; 5570 days in cool highland areas.
  • Wrapper leaves: 34 tight, green outer leaves still attached; these protect the head in transit.
  • Split risk: harvest before heads split after rain. Splitting is irreversible and drops the head to cull grade.
  • Time of day: harvest before 10:00 or after 16:00. Field heat at midday can raise head temperature by 1015 C, accelerating respiration and decay.

2. Harvesting Tools and Techniques

Cut cleanly, leave a short stem, and never drop heads. A sharp curved harvest knife or secateurs produces a clean cut that heals faster than a torn stem.

SAFETY WARNING PESTICIDE RESIDUE AND HARVEST INTERVAL

Never harvest cabbage before the pre-harvest interval (PHI) stated on the pesticide label. Cabbage is a leafy brassica with high residue retention. Selling cabbage inside the PHI can cause buyer rejection, market bans, and serious consumer health harm. Record every spray date and product. If the PHI is unknown, do not sell the crop until a residue test or extension officer confirms it is safe. This is a legal, commercial, and ethical requirement not optional.

4. Packaging and Storage Requirements

Cabbage respires and loses water. Packaging must allow airflow and protect against compression. The goal is to hold heads at 04 C and 9598% relative humidity where cold storage exists, or to minimise heat and water loss where it does not.

  • Best: rigid plastic crates, 1520 kg capacity, stackable, ventilated.
  • Acceptable: well-ventilated woven baskets or mesh bags for short-distance local sales.
  • Avoid: sealed polypropylene sacks and overfilled bags they cause compression, heat build-up, and anaerobic rot.
  • Pre-cooling: move to shade immediately; evaporative cooling (wet charcoal or sand floor, fan-driven) can drop temperature 510 C in dry African conditions.
  • Storage life: 36 weeks at 0 C; 12 weeks at 10 C; 35 days at 2530 C.
  • Stacking: never more than 4 crates high; leave a central air channel.

5. Post-Harvest Diseases and Control

The main post-harvest diseases are Alternaria leaf spot, Botrytis grey mould, bacterial soft rot (Erwinia), and Sclerotinia rot. Most enter through cuts, bruises, and excess moisture.

  • Sanitation: clean crates and knives between harvests; remove crop debris from the shed.
  • Moisture control: do not wash cabbage for storage unless you can dry and cool it immediately.
  • Temperature: cold is the single most effective control; every 10 C reduction roughly halves decay rate.
  • Culling: remove infected heads at once one rotting head contaminates a whole crate.
  • Chemical control: only approved post-harvest fungicides, used strictly per label; many African markets now reject treated produce, so confirm buyer requirements first.

6. Transportation and Market Preparation

Transport is where the largest avoidable losses occur. Heat, vibration, and compression do the damage.

  • Timing: load and move in the coolest hours early morning or night.
  • Vehicle: covered, ventilated truck or pickup; never seal a load in a closed metal container in the sun.
  • Loading: crates stacked no more than 4 high, secured against movement; use a tarpaulin for shade, not for sealing.
  • Market preparation: trim outer leaves only at the point of sale to keep protection during transit; present uniform Grade 1 heads facing the buyer; label weight and grade.
  • Contracts: agree grade, weight, packaging, and delivery window in writing with wholesalers, supermarkets, or processors before harvest.
  • Price risk: stagger planting so harvest does not coincide with market gluts; cabbage prices in many African urban markets fall 3050% during peak season.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Harvest when heads are firm and at target weight before splitting; cut before 10:00 or after 16:00.
  2. Use a sharp curved knife or secateurs; leave a 23 cm stem; never drop or throw heads.
  3. Move harvested heads into shade within 15 minutes; pre-cool where possible.
  4. Sort and grade at the field edge into Grade 1, Grade 2, Grade 3, and cull.
  5. Pack in rigid, ventilated crates never sealed sacks; stack no more than 4 high.
  6. Respect the pesticide pre-harvest interval and record every spray date.
  7. Transport in the coolest hours in a covered, ventilated vehicle; keep loads secure.
  8. Confirm buyer grade, weight, packaging, and delivery terms in writing before harvest.
