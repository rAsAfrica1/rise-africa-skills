1. Determining Harvest Maturity

Harvesting citrus at the right time is critical for taste, shelf life, and market price. In Africa, most citrus is harvested by hand, so you must know when to start. The easiest test is colour: for oranges, the skin should change from green to a uniform orange or yellow-orange. But colour alone can fool yousome varieties stay green even when ripe. A more reliable method is the Brix test. Use a refractometer (costs about $20) to measure sugar content. For oranges, aim for 10-12% Brix; for lemons, 7-8%. Also check acidity with a simple acid test kit. The ratio of Brix to acid should be at least 8:1 for sweet oranges. Another field test: pick a fruit and taste it. If it's sweet and juicy, it's ready. For export, you also need to check firmnesssoft fruit bruises easily. Walk your orchard every 3 days as harvest approaches. In Kenya, for example, Valencia oranges are ready 8-10 months after flowering. Don't harvest too earlyimmature fruit shrivels and lacks juice. Don't wait too longoverripe fruit drops and rots. Start with a small test pick, then harvest the whole block within a week.

2. Harvesting Techniques and Tools

How you pick citrus affects how long it lasts. Always use sharp, clean tools. The best tool is a pair of curved harvesting snips (also called clippers). They cost about $10-15. Never pull fruit off the treethat tears the skin and leaves a wound where rot enters. Instead, cut the stem close to the fruit, leaving a tiny button (about 2mm). For high branches, use a pole pruner with a basket to catch fruit. Wear soft cotton gloves to avoid scratching the peel. Harvest in the cool of the dayearly morning or late afternoon. Never harvest wet fruit; wait until dew dries. Work from the outside of the tree inward, and from bottom to top. Place fruit gently into a picking bag or a plastic bucket lined with a soft cloth. Don't drop fruit into the bucketeach drop causes bruising that shows up later as brown spots. In South Africa, commercial farms use picking bags with a strap over the shoulder and a false bottom to empty fruit slowly into bins. For smallholders, a 20-litre bucket works fine. Do not throw fruit into a pile. After picking, move fruit to shade immediately. A tree can yield 50-100 kg of fruit per season, so plan your labour. One picker can harvest about 200-300 kg per day if fruit is easy to reach.

3. Field Handling and Transport

Once picked, citrus starts to lose quality. Field heat is the enemy. Move fruit to a shady area within 30 minutes. Never leave fruit in the suntemperatures above 30C cause rapid water loss and sunburn. Use clean plastic crates (not sacks) for transport. Sacks crush fruit and cause bruising. A standard crate holds 15-20 kg. Stack crates no more than 5 high to avoid crushing the bottom layer. If you use a pickup truck, cover the load with a white tarpaulin to reflect heat. Avoid driving on rough roadsvibration causes internal damage. For long distances (over 2 hours), use a refrigerated truck if possible, but if not, transport at night when it's cooler. In many African countries, farmers lose 20-30% of fruit due to poor transport. To reduce losses, harvest only what you can transport within 4 hours. If you must store fruit overnight in the field, put it in a ventilated shed, not a closed room. Never mix fruit from different trees or different maturity stagesone rotten fruit can spoil the whole batch. Before loading, sort out any fruit with cuts, soft spots, or signs of decay. Feed those to animals or process them immediately. Clean your crates after every use with soap and water, then dry them in the sun. Dirty crates carry fungal spores that cause blue and green mould.

4. Packinghouse Operations and Grading

A packinghouse is where fruit is cleaned, graded, and packed for market. Even a smallholder can set up a simple shed with a concrete floor, a water tank, and sorting tables. First, dump fruit gently into a water bath with a mild detergent (2 ml per litre) to remove dust and insects. Do not soak for more than 2 minutes. Then rinse with clean water. Next, dry the fruit with a soft cloth or a fan. Grading is sorting by size, colour, and quality. Use a sizing ring or a simple wooden gauge. For oranges, sizes are: small (60-65 mm), medium (65-72 mm), large (72-80 mm), and jumbo (over 80 mm). Remove all fruit with blemishes, soft spots, or green patches. These go to juice or animal feed. For export, you must also wax the fruit to slow water loss. Food-grade wax (carnauba or shellac) costs about $5 per litre and covers 200 kg of fruit. Apply a thin coat with a soft brush or sponge. Then pack into clean, ventilated cartons. Each carton holds 15 kg or 18 kg. Label with variety, size, and farm name. In Kenya, a 15-kg carton of export-quality oranges can fetch $10-15, while ungraded fruit sells for $3-5. Proper grading can double your income. Always keep records of how many cartons you pack per day.

5. Storage Conditions and Shelf Life

Citrus can be stored for weeks if you control temperature and humidity. The ideal storage for oranges and lemons is 5-8C with 85-90% relative humidity. For limes, 10-12C. If you don't have a cold room, you can use a charcoal coolera simple structure with wet charcoal walls and a fan. This can keep fruit at 10-15C in hot climates. Never store citrus with apples, bananas, or tomatoesthese produce ethylene gas that speeds up ripening and decay. Also, keep fruit away from onions and potatoes; they absorb odours. In a well-ventilated room, citrus can last 2-3 weeks. In a cold room, 6-8 weeks. Check stored fruit every 3 days. Remove any fruit that shows soft spots or mould. One rotten fruit can spread spores to the whole box. For small quantities, you can store in a clean plastic bag with holes (to allow air) in the bottom of a refrigerator. But do not wash fruit before storagemoisture encourages mould. If you sell at local markets, harvest only what you can sell in 5 days. For longer storage, consider processing. Remember: shelf life depends on how you handled fruit in the field. Bruised fruit will rot first. So always handle gently from tree to store.

6. Value-Added Processing (Juice, Marmalade, etc.)

Processing turns low-grade or surplus citrus into higher-value products. Juice is the easiest. You need a manual or electric juicer. For 10 kg of oranges, you get about 5 litres of juice. Pasteurize by heating to 80C for 15 minutes, then bottle in clean glass bottles. Add 1 teaspoon of citric acid per litre to preserve colour. Juice can be sold fresh or frozen. Marmalade is made from the peel and pulp. Use equal weights of fruit and sugar. Slice fruit thinly, soak overnight in water, then boil with sugar until it sets (about 105C). Pour into sterilized jars. A 500g jar sells for $3-5 in local markets. Dried peel is another product: dry orange peel in the sun for 3-4 days, then grind into powder for baking or tea. Citrus essential oil can be extracted by cold pressing the peel, but this requires a press. For small-scale, focus on juice and marmalade. In Nigeria, women's groups process citrus into juice and marmalade and sell to supermarkets. You need a food-grade kitchen, clean water, and proper labelling. Always check local food safety regulations. Processing also reduces wastefruit that is too small or blemished can still be used. Start small: process 20 kg per day, test the market, then scale up.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Harvesting & Post-Harvest Handling of Citrus in Africa

Citrus is one of Africa's most commercially viable fruit crops from the Western Cape and Limpopo in South Africa, through Zimbabwe's Mazowe Valley, to Egypt's Nile Delta and Morocco's Souss plains. Yet the money is not made in the orchard alone. It is made or lost in the 72 hours after the fruit leaves the tree. Poor harvesting and post-harvest handling can destroy 2040% of a harvest in many African value chains, turning a profitable season into a break-even exercise.

This lesson is a practical, business-first reference for growers, packhouse managers, and agripreneurs. It covers maturity decisions, harvesting technique, field handling, packinghouse operations, storage, and value addition. Use it as a checklist to protect margin at every step.

1. Determining Harvest Maturity the Margin Decision

Harvest too early and fruit is sour, undersized, and rejects in export or processing markets. Harvest too late and fruit drops, dehydrates, or arrives soft. Maturity is measured by three signals:

  • Total soluble solids (TSS / Brix): sugar content measured with a refractometer. Export oranges typically require 911 Brix depending on variety and market.
  • Titratable acidity (TA): acid level. The TSS:TA ratio is the true commercial maturity index. For oranges, a ratio of 8:1 to 12:1 is common; for lemons, higher acid is acceptable.
  • External colour and firmness: rind colour break (from green to yellow/orange) and fruit firmness. Colour charts (e.g., the Citrus Colour Chart) are used in packhouses.

Business tip: Buy a handheld refractometer (US$3080) and a titrator kit. A single missed maturity window can downgrade an entire block from export to juice a price drop of 5070% per tonne.

2. Harvesting Techniques and Tools

Citrus rind is easily bruised. Bruises do not show immediately but become decay (Penicillium mould) within 714 days. The rule is: clip, don't pull.

  • Use sharp, curved harvesting clippers (not fingers, not knives that cut into the rind).
  • Leave a short stem (12 mm) too long a stem punctures neighbouring fruit; too short leaves an open wound.
  • Harvest in the cool of the day (early morning or late afternoon) to reduce field heat.
  • Never drop fruit into bins from height place gently.
  • Use padded picking bags and lined field bins (plastic or wooden, clean and dry).

3. Field Handling and Transport

Field heat is the enemy. Fruit harvested at 3035C in African summer conditions respires and deteriorates quickly. Rapid cooling within 46 hours is ideal, but in many smallholder operations this is not possible. Practical mitigations:

  • Harvest early morning; move fruit to shade immediately.
  • Do not stack field bins more than 23 high.
  • Use clean, dry, smooth bins never fertiliser bags or dirty sacks.
  • Transport on smooth roads; avoid overloading; use covered vehicles.
  • Deliver to packhouse within 1224 hours of harvest.

4. Packinghouse Operations and Grading

The packinghouse is where value is either protected or destroyed. Key steps:

  1. Receival & pre-grading: remove rotten, damaged, and undersized fruit immediately. One rotten fruit can infect a whole bin.
  2. Washing & sanitising: use potable water with approved sanitiser (e.g., chlorine at 100150 ppm).
  3. Waxing: food-grade wax reduces moisture loss and improves shine. Optional for local markets, essential for export.
  4. Grading: by size (counts), colour, and defects. Use manual sizing rings or electronic graders.
  5. Packing: clean, ventilated cartons with correct count and weight.
  6. Palletising & cold room: stack for airflow; cool to 48C for oranges, 1012C for lemons.

5. Storage Conditions and Shelf Life

Cold storage is capital-intensive but essential for export and extended local marketing. For smallholders and agripreneurs without cold rooms, alternatives include:

  • Evaporative cooling chambers (ZECC): low-cost brick-and-sand structures that reduce temperature by 510C. Widely used in Kenya, Tanzania, and Nigeria.
  • Zero-energy cool chambers: similar principle, using wet sand and ventilation.
  • Rapid market turnover: sell within 35 days to local markets rather than storing.
  • Contract processing: divert surplus to juice or marmalade processors immediately.
SAFETY WARNING Chemical & Sanitiser Handling

Post-harvest chemicals (fungicides such as imazalil, thiabendazole, or chlorine-based sanitisers) are hazardous. Always wear gloves, goggles, and a respirator when mixing or applying. Never exceed label rates. Store chemicals in locked, ventilated, labelled cabinets away from fruit, food, and children. In many African markets, Maximum Residue Limits (MRLs) are strictly enforced by importers non-compliant fruit is rejected at the port at the exporter's cost. Keep a spray and dip log for every batch. If you cannot verify MRL compliance, do not export.

6. Value-Added Processing (Juice, Marmalade, etc.)

Processing turns rejects and surplus into revenue. In Africa, the most viable small-to-medium options are:

  • Fresh juice: requires pasteurisation, bottling, and cold chain. High margin, high compliance burden.
  • Concentrate: capital-intensive; usually done by large processors.
  • Marmalade & jams: lower capital, longer shelf life, strong local and export niche (e.g., Kenyan and South African brands).
  • Dried peel / essential oil: citrus peel oil (limonene) and dried peel have cosmetic and food industry demand.
  • Animal feed: citrus pulp is a valuable livestock feed ingredient.

Business tip: Before investing in processing, secure at least one off-take buyer. Processing without a buyer simply converts fruit waste into capital waste. Start with marmalade or dried peel low equipment cost, long shelf life, and easier regulatory pathway.

  1. Test Brix and TSS:TA ratio before harvesting each block.
  2. Use sharp clippers; leave 12 mm stem; never pull or drop fruit.
  3. Harvest early morning; move to shade within 30 minutes.
  4. Use clean, lined, dry field bins; never overstack.
  5. Deliver to packhouse within 1224 hours.
  6. Remove all rotten fruit at receival one rotten fruit spoils a bin.
