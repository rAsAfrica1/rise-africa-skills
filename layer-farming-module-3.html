1. Preparing the Brooder House: Sanitation and Setup

Before your chicks arrive, the brooder house must be cleaned and disinfected thoroughly. Start by removing all old litter, feed, and dust. Scrape floors and walls, then wash with soapy water. Rinse and let dry. Disinfect using a mixture of 1 part bleach to 10 parts water, or use commercial disinfectants like Virocid or TH4. Spray walls, floor, and ceiling. For every 10 square feet, use about 1 gallon of disinfectant solution. After disinfection, close the house and fumigate with potassium permanganate and formalin (use 20g potassium permanganate + 40ml formalin per 10 cubic feet). Seal for 24 hours, then open and air for 2 days.

Next, set up the brooder. Use a clean, dry absorbent litter like wood shavings or rice husks, 3-4 inches deep. Never use sawdust or moldy litter. Create a brooder ring using cardboard or metal sheets, about 2 feet high, to keep chicks near heat and feed. For 100 chicks, provide a ring of about 8-10 feet diameter. Install a heat source: for 100 chicks, use a 250-watt infrared bulb or a charcoal stove (jiko) with a guard. If using charcoal, ensure proper ventilation to avoid carbon monoxide. Place the heat source at one end, and spread feed and water around it.

Provide feeders and drinkers. For 100 chicks, use 4 tube feeders (each 5 kg capacity) and 4 drinkers (each 3-4 liters). Place them alternating around the brooder. Use clean, fresh water with glucose and vitamins for the first 2 days. Add a chick guard (a wire mesh or cardboard) to prevent chicks from crowding the heat source.

Check temperature before chicks arrive: 95F (35C) at chick level. Use a thermometer. If too hot, raise the heat source; if too cold, lower it. Also, ensure the house is rodent-proof and bird-proof. Cover windows with wire mesh.

Finally, prepare a brooding record book to track temperature, mortality, feed, and water. Have a first-aid kit with antibiotics and vitamins. Now your brooder is ready for day-old chicks.

2. Temperature and Humidity Control for Chicks

Temperature is the most critical factor in brooding. Day-old chicks need 95F (35C) for the first week. Reduce by 5F (2.8C) each week until you reach 70F (21C) by week 5. Use a thermometer placed at chick level, not high up. If you see chicks huddling under the heat source, they are cold; increase temperature. If they spread out away from heat, they are hot; lower it. If they are evenly spread and active, temperature is just right.

In Africa, many farmers use charcoal stoves (jikos) or infrared bulbs. For 100 chicks, one 250-watt bulb is enough. Hang it 18-24 inches above the litter. For charcoal, use a stove with a metal guard and adjust air vents to control heat. Always have a backup heat source, like a hurricane lamp, in case of power cuts.

Humidity should be 40-60% for the first week, then 50-70% later. Too low humidity causes dehydration and pasty butt; too high causes wet litter and respiratory problems. To increase humidity, hang wet sacks or place shallow pans of water near the heat source. To decrease, improve ventilation.

Ventilation is vital but avoid direct drafts on chicks. Open windows on one side only, and use a wire mesh. In hot climates, open more during the day.

Monitor chick behavior every 2-3 hours. Adjust heat accordingly. At night, temperature drops, so ensure the brooder is well insulated. Use a curtain or sack around the brooder ring to retain heat.

Keep a record of daily temperature and humidity. A simple thermometer-hygrometer costs little and saves lives. If you cannot afford one, use your hand: place the back of your hand at chick level; if it feels hot after 10 seconds, it's too hot.

Remember, cold chicks pile up and suffocate; hot chicks pant and dehydrate. Both lead to death. Consistent temperature prevents stress and disease.

3. Feeding and Watering Day-Old Chicks

When chicks arrive, give them water first. Use clean, fresh water with 5% glucose (50g per liter) and a multivitamin (follow label) for the first 2 days. This rehydrates and gives energy. Use shallow drinkers (like egg trays or jar lids) for the first 3 days to prevent drowning. Later, use 3-4 liter drinkers. For 100 chicks, provide 4 drinkers. Wash and refill at least twice daily.

After 2 hours, offer feed. Use commercial chick mash (starter) with 20-22% protein. If unavailable, mix your own: 10 kg maize meal, 3 kg soybean meal, 1 kg fish meal, 0.5 kg minerals and vitamins. For the first 3 days, spread feed on clean paper or flat trays to encourage eating. Then switch to tube feeders. For 100 chicks, use 4 feeders. Fill feeders halfway to avoid waste.

Feed chicks ad libitum (free choice) for the first 6 weeks. Each chick eats about 10-15g per day in week 1, increasing to 40-50g by week 6. Provide feed early morning and late afternoon. Remove stale feed daily.

Add grit (crushed granite) at 2 weeks, a handful per 100 chicks, to aid digestion. Also, provide insoluble grit if using homemade feed.

Water quality: never give dirty water. Clean drinkers with soap and rinse well. Add 1 teaspoon of bleach per 10 liters if water is suspect.

Monitor crop fill: 24 hours after feeding, at least 95% of chicks should have full crops. If not, adjust feed or water.

Keep feed in a dry, cool place, away from rodents. Use within 4 weeks.

A simple rule: 1 kg of feed per chick for the first 6 weeks. So for 100 chicks, buy 100 kg of starter feed. Always have enough.

Happy, well-fed chicks will grow into strong layers.

4. Vaccination and Health Monitoring in Brooding

Vaccination is essential to prevent deadly diseases. For layers, follow this schedule: Day 1: Marek's (in hatchery) and Newcastle (ND) + Infectious Bronchitis (IB) via eye drop or spray. Day 7: ND + IB booster. Day 14: Gumboro (IBD). Day 21: ND + IB booster. Day 28: Gumboro booster. Week 6: Fowl Typhoid. Week 8: ND + IB + EDS. Always buy vaccines from a reputable supplier and keep them cold (2-8C). Use within 2 hours of mixing.

Administer vaccines correctly: eye drop for ND/IB, oral for Gumboro (in water), and injection for Marek's. For water vaccination, withhold water for 2 hours, then mix vaccine in clean water (no chlorine) and provide to chicks. Use 1 liter per 100 chicks.

Health monitoring: Check chicks daily for signs of illness: dullness, ruffled feathers, coughing, sneezing, diarrhea, or blood in droppings. Isolate sick chicks immediately. Common problems: coccidiosis (bloody droppings) treat with amprolium; respiratory infections treat with antibiotics like oxytetracycline; pasty butt (blocked vent) clean with warm water.

Keep records of vaccinations, medications, and mortality. A mortality rate above 5% in brooding is a warning.

Biosecurity: Limit visitors, use footbaths with disinfectant at entrance, and wash hands before entering.

If you see many chicks dying, consult a vet or extension officer. Do not guess.

Vaccines are cheap; diseases are expensive. A vial of Newcastle vaccine costs little and protects 1000 chicks.

Always use sterile equipment. For injections, use a new needle per chick or sterilize between uses.

Healthy chicks are active, have bright eyes, and eat well. Monitor them at least three times a day.

5. Managing Stocking Density and Space Allowance

Stocking density affects health and growth. For brooding, allow 0.5 square feet per chick (about 0.05 m) for the first 2 weeks, then 1 square foot per chick from week 3 to 6. So for 100 chicks, provide 50 square feet (4.6 m) initially, then 100 square feet (9.3 m) by week 6. Overcrowding causes stress, pecking, and disease.

Use a brooder ring to confine chicks initially, then expand as they grow. For 100 chicks, start with a ring of 8-10 feet diameter. After 2 weeks, remove the ring and let them use the whole brooder house.

Provide sufficient feeders and drinkers. For 100 chicks: 4 tube feeders (5 kg) and 4 drinkers (4 L) for weeks 1-2; double to 8 each by week 6. Place feeders and drinkers evenly, not more than 3 feet from any chick.

Ventilation must match density. More birds need more air. Use wire mesh windows and open vents. If you smell ammonia, increase ventilation or reduce density.

Litter management: Keep litter dry and loose. Stir daily and add fresh shavings if wet. Wet litter causes coccidiosis and respiratory issues.

In hot climates, reduce density by 10-20% to prevent heat stress. In cold, you can keep slightly higher density but ensure no crowding around heat source.

A simple rule: if chicks cannot all eat at once, you have too many birds or too few feeders.

Plan for expansion: As chicks grow, they need more space. If you started with 100 chicks in a 10x10 ft house, by week 6 you need 100 sq ft, which is exactly 10x10 ft. So a 10x10 ft house can hold 100 layers up to 6 weeks.

Always provide a little extra space to reduce stress.

6. Transitioning from Brooder to Layer House

At 6-8 weeks, chicks are ready to move to the layer house. But transition must be gradual to avoid stress. Start by reducing brooder temperature to 70F (21C) by week 6. If chicks are fully feathered and active, they can move.

Before moving, prepare the layer house: clean and disinfect it, provide fresh litter (3-4 inches), and set up feeders and drinkers. For layers, provide 4-6 inches of feeder space per bird and 1-2 inches of drinker space. For 100 birds, use 6 tube feeders and 6 drinkers.

Move chicks in the cool of the day (early morning or evening). Use crates or boxes, and handle gently. Place them in the new house and confine them to a small area for 2-3 days with feed and water. This helps them learn where to eat and drink.

Continue feeding chick mash until week 8, then switch to grower mash (16-18% protein). Mix the two feeds gradually over 3 days to avoid digestive upset.

Provide perches: layers need 6-8 inches of perch space per bird. Install wooden perches 2-3 feet high.

Lighting: From week 6 to 18, provide 12 hours of light per day. Use natural light or a bulb.

Monitor for stress: signs include reduced eating, pecking, and drop in egg production later. Keep records.

Vaccinate before moving if not done: at week 8, give ND + IB + EDS.

After moving, check birds daily for 2 weeks. Ensure they have access to feed and water.

A smooth transition ensures your pullets grow into healthy layers that start laying at 18-20 weeks.

Remember, moving is stressful; do it once, and do it right.

<h2>Practical Reference: Chick Brooding Management for African Layer Enterprises</h2>

<p>Brooding is the single most capital-sensitive phase of layer farming. In African conditions high ambient temperatures, erratic power, mixed-quality day-old chicks, and limited veterinary access the first 14 days determine whether a flock becomes a profitable laying unit or a cash drain. This reference consolidates the operational benchmarks, numbers, and decision rules you need to run brooding as a business process, not a guessing game. It is designed for farm managers, extension officers, and agripreneurs running 200 to 20,000 birds in commercial or semi-commercial systems.</p>

<h2>1. Preparing the Brooder House: Sanitation and Setup</h2>

<p>Sanitation is not a cost it is insurance against a 2040% first-week mortality event that can erase an entire cycle's margin. The standard sequence is: dry clean wash disinfect rest set up.</p>

<p><strong>Dry clean:</strong> Remove all old litter, dust, cobwebs, and feed residues. Dust carries <em>Aspergillus</em> spores and <em>Salmonella</em>. <strong>Wash:</strong> Use a detergent to break organic film disinfectants fail on dirty surfaces. <strong>Disinfect:</strong> Rotate actives (glutaraldehyde, quaternary ammonium, or sodium hypochlorite) to prevent resistance. <strong>Rest:</strong> Leave the house empty 714 days. <strong>Fumigate</strong> only if you have trained personnel and proper PPE; in many African markets, formaldehyde fumigation is restricted use fogging disinfectants instead.</p>

<p>Litter should be 58 cm deep, dry, dust-free, and absorbent. Wood shavings, rice husks, or chopped maize cobs work well; avoid wet sawdust and mouldy straw. Pre-warm the house to 3335C at chick level <em>before</em> chicks arrive never introduce day-olds into a cold house.</p>

<h2>2. Temperature and Humidity Control for Chicks</h2>

<p>Temperature is the number-one driver of first-week mortality and feed conversion. Chicks cannot thermoregulate until roughly day 14, so you are their thermostat. The rule is simple: <strong>start at 3335C at chick level and reduce 23C per week</strong> until 2022C by week 5, then hold.</p>

<p>Do not trust wall thermometers alone measure at chick height (5 cm above litter) and at multiple points. Behaviour is your best sensor: chicks spread evenly = correct; huddled under heat = too cold; pressed to walls and panting = too hot; noisy and scattered = draughts.</p>

<p>Humidity should sit at 6070% in week 1, dropping to 5060% thereafter. In dry Sahelian or highland conditions, humidity falls below 40% and chicks dehydrate; use shallow water trays or misting. In humid coastal West Africa, humidity above 75% with high heat causes wet litter, coccidiosis, and respiratory stress increase ventilation, not heat.</p>

<p>Power reliability is the biggest African constraint. Budget for a backup heat source: charcoal brooder, kerosene lamp, or gas brooder. Never rely on a single grid connection.</p>

<h2>3. Feeding and Watering Day-Old Chicks</h2>

<p>Water first, feed second. Chicks lose 10% of body water in the first 24 hours after hatch and cannot recover without immediate access. Provide water within 30 minutes of placement, at 2024C, with 5% glucose or commercial chick electrolyte for the first 68 hours. Add vitamins A, D, E for the first 3 days.</p>

<p>Use shallow drinkers with marbles or inverted bottle bases to prevent drowning. Clean and refill 34 times daily. Feed a quality chick starter crumble at 2022% crude protein from day 1. Do not restrict feed in week 1 target 1214 g/bird/day by day 7. Use flat tray feeders for the first 3 days, then transition to hanging feeders.</p>

<p>Feed conversion in brooding is the baseline for your whole flock's economics. A chick that gains 6070 g by day 7 and 130150 g by day 14 is on track for a productive layer. Underweight pullets at 18 weeks will lay smaller eggs and peak lower.</p>

<h2>4. Vaccination and Health Monitoring in Brooding</h2>

<p>Vaccination schedules vary by country, but the core brooding vaccines in African layer systems are:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Day 1:</strong> Marek's disease (hatchery) and Newcastle disease (ND) via eye drop or spray</li>
<li><strong>Day 710:</strong> ND + Infectious Bronchitis (IB) via eye drop or drinking water</li>
<li><strong>Day 1418:</strong> Gumboro (IBD) via drinking water</li>
<li><strong>Day 2128:</strong> ND booster</li>
</ul>

<p>Use only vaccines from cold-chain-verified sources. Check the freeze indicator. Reconstitute and use within 2 hours. Withdraw water 12 hours before drinking-water vaccination, and add skimmed milk powder (2 g/L) to neutralise chlorine.</p>

<p>Monitor daily for: watery or bloody droppings (coccidiosis), gasping or coughing (ND, IB, mycoplasma), huddling (chilling), and uneven growth (feed or water access). Weigh a sample of 20 birds weekly and record. A 10% drop in expected body weight is an early warning investigate before mortality appears.</p>

<h2>5. Managing Stocking Density and Space Allowance</h2>

<p>Overstocking is the most common and most expensive mistake in African brooding. It causes wet litter, ammonia build-up, coccidiosis, and uneven growth. The rule: <strong>double the space allowance every 2 weeks</strong> from the Table 2 benchmarks. For a 1,000-bird flock, plan for at least 20 m at day 7 and 80 m by day 35.</p>

<p>Expand the brooder ring daily from day 3. Remove the ring entirely by day 1014 in warm climates. Ensure 24-hour access to feed and water; never let a chick travel more than 3 metres to a drinker.</p>

<h2>6. Transitioning from Brooder to Layer House</h2>

<p>Transition at 56 weeks, when chicks are fully feathered and outside temperatures are stable. Steps:</p>
<ol>
<li>Prepare the layer house with clean litter, feeders, and drinkers same equipment type if possible.</li>
<li>Move in the cool of the early morning or late evening to reduce heat stress.</li>
<li>Maintain the same feed and water routine for 35 days.</li>
<li>Continue the vaccination and weighing schedule.</li>
<li>Introduce layer grower feed from week 78, transitioning over 57 days.</li>
</ol>

<p>Record weight and uniformity at transfer. A flock with 80% of birds within 10% of the target weight at 18 weeks will peak above 90% hen-day production. Below 70% uniformity, expect a poor and short laying peak.</p>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>
<ul>
<li> Disinfect and rest the brooder house for at least 7 days before chick arrival.</li>
<li> Pre-heat to 3335C at chick level; verify with a calibrated thermometer at 5 cm height.</li>
<li> Provide water with glucose/electrolytes within 30 minutes of placement; feed within 1 hour.</li>
<li> Use 58 cm dry litter; never wet or mouldy.</li>
<li> Follow the national vaccination schedule; maintain cold chain and use within 2 hours of reconstitution.</li>
<li> Weigh 20 sample birds weekly and record; investigate a 10% deviation.</li>
<li> Expand brooder ring daily and double space every 2 weeks; remove ring by day 1014.</li>
<li> Have a backup heat source and a written mortality log review daily.</li>
</ul>
