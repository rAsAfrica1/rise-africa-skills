1. Introduction to Steel Fixing and Rebar Fundamentals

Welcome to Module 10 of your Steel Fixing training. This module is designed for site supervisors, foremen, steel fixers, and construction business owners who want to master the practical and commercial aspects of rebar work in African construction markets. Steel fixing, also known as rebar installation, is the backbone of reinforced concrete structures. Without correctly placed and tied reinforcement, concrete cannot resist tension, and buildings, bridges, culverts, and water tanks will crack or collapse. In this first lesson, we cover the fundamentals: what rebar is, why it matters, the types available in African markets, and how to read basic bar schedules. Rebar, or reinforcing bar, is a steel bar with deformations or ribs that bond with concrete. The ribs are not decorative; they transfer stress between the concrete and the steel. In Africa, common rebar diameters range from 6 mm to 40 mm. Small bars (6, 8, 10 mm) are used for stirrups, links, and slab mesh. Medium bars (12, 16, 20 mm) are used for beams and columns. Large bars (25, 32, 40 mm) are used for heavy foundations, bridge piers, and industrial slabs. You must know the difference between mild steel and high-yield steel. Mild steel is softer and bends easily, often used for links. High-yield steel has greater tensile strength and is used for main reinforcement. In many African countries, imported rebar from Turkey, China, India, and South Africa competes with local mills. Always check mill certificates and test for corrosion, straightness, and rib pattern. A common business mistake is buying cheap, underweight rebar. If a 12 mm bar weighs less than 0.888 kg per metre, it is likely substandard. You can verify weight with a simple scale and a one-metre cut. This protects your reputation and prevents structural failure. Reading a bar schedule is a core skill. A bar schedule lists bar mark, diameter, shape, length, number required, and bending dimensions. For example, a beam may have top bars, bottom bars, and links. The schedule tells you that bar mark B1 is 16 mm diameter, 5.2 m long, with 90-degree bends at both ends, and you need 8 pieces. You must cross-check the schedule against the structural drawings. If the drawing shows a 20 mm bar but the schedule says 16 mm, stop work and ask the engineer. Never guess. In Africa, site conditions vary greatly. You may work in dusty Sahel conditions, humid coastal areas, or high-altitude regions. Rebar must be stored off the ground on timber or concrete blocks, covered with tarpaulin if rain is expected, and kept away from salt or chemicals. Rust is acceptable if it is surface rust, but deep pitting reduces bond strength. Clean bars with a wire brush before placing. Safety is also fundamental. Wear gloves, safety boots, and eye protection. When cutting with a hacksaw or shear, secure the bar. When bending, use a proper bending machine or a sturdy bending jig. Manual bending of large bars causes back injuries. Plan your work area so that cut bars do not create trip hazards. Finally, think like a business owner. Steel fixing is not just labour; it is a profit centre. Estimate your labour, bar wastage, tie wire, and chair consumption. A 5 percent wastage on a 10-tonne project is half a tonne of steel, which can be a significant loss. Train your team to cut accurately, reuse offcuts, and keep a daily record of bars delivered and installed. In the next lesson, we will cover setting out, bar bending schedules, and cutting lists in detail. Before you move on, complete this practical task: obtain a set of structural drawings for a small building, identify five bar marks, and calculate the total weight of steel required. This will prepare you for real site work.

2. Setting Out, Cutting, and Bending Rebar Accurately

In this lesson, we focus on the hands-on skills that determine whether your steel fixing is profitable and structurally sound: setting out, cutting, and bending. In African construction, where projects often run on tight budgets and tight schedules, accuracy in these steps saves money and prevents rework. Setting out means transferring the positions of bars from the drawings to the actual work area. For foundations, you mark the grid lines on the ground or on the blinding concrete. For columns, you mark the starter bar positions on the footing. For slabs, you mark the spacing of bars along the edges. Use a chalk line, measuring tape, and spray paint. Always set out from a fixed reference point, such as a grid line or a column centre. Never set out from a previous bar that may have moved. Check diagonals to ensure squareness. If the diagonals are not equal, your setting out is wrong, and every bar after that will be misplaced. Cutting rebar seems simple, but it is where waste happens. You need a cutting list derived from the bar schedule. Group bars by diameter and length. Use a bar cutting machine if available; it gives clean cuts and high output. If cutting manually with a hacksaw, use a blade with the correct teeth per inch for steel, and lubricate with water or cutting oil. For large bars, a shear or abrasive saw is better. Always cut on a sturdy bench or sawhorse. Measure twice and cut once. Mark the cut length with a soapstone or paint marker. Allow for bends: if a bar has a 90-degree bend, the cutting length is not the same as the sum of the straight legs. You must subtract the bend deduction. For a 90-degree bend, the deduction is typically 2 times the bar diameter. For a 135-degree bend, it is 3 times the diameter. For a 180-degree hook, it is 4 times the diameter. These are standard values, but always confirm with the engineer or the bending schedule. Bending rebar correctly is critical. Bends must be smooth and not crack the steel. The minimum bend diameter depends on the bar size and grade. For high-yield bars, a common rule is 4 times the bar diameter for stirrups and 6 times for main bars. If you bend a 16 mm bar around a pin smaller than 64 mm, you may crack it. Use a proper bar bending machine or a bending jig with correct pin sizes. For manual bending, use a bending table with pins and a lever pipe. Never bend a bar by heating it with a torch unless the engineer approves, because heat changes the steel properties. In Africa, many small contractors use manual bending for 8 mm and 10 mm links. This is acceptable if the pins are correct. For 12 mm and above, use a machine. Bending links requires a link bending jig. A link is a closed loop, usually rectangular or square, with 135-degree hooks at the corners. The hooks must be bent around the main bars. The length of the hook is typically 10 times the bar diameter, but not less than 75 mm. For example, a 10 mm link needs a hook of at least 100 mm. When bending links, ensure all links are identical. Use a template or a jig. If links vary in size, they will not fit over the main bars, and you will waste time and steel. Cutting and bending also require quality control. Check the first bar of each type against the schedule. If it is correct, continue. If not, stop and adjust. Keep a sample bar for each mark. At the end of each day, count the bars cut and bent, and compare with the cutting list. This helps you track progress and detect theft or waste. Business tip: invest in a good bar bending machine and a bar cutting machine. In many African cities, you can rent them or buy Chinese or Indian models at reasonable prices. A machine increases output by 5 to 10 times compared to manual labour. It also reduces injuries and improves accuracy. If you cannot afford a machine, train your workers to use manual jigs safely and efficiently. Another tip: organise your cutting and bending yard. Keep bars of different diameters separate. Label bundles with the bar mark and length. Store bent bars on timber pallets, not on the ground. This prevents rust and damage. Use a simple colour code: paint the ends of bars with different colours for different marks. This speeds up installation. Finally, consider the African context. In remote areas, power for machines may be unreliable. Have a generator or a manual backup. In hot climates, workers tire quickly; schedule heavy cutting and bending for early morning. Provide drinking water and shade. In coastal areas, salt air accelerates rust; apply a rust-inhibiting primer if bars will be stored for more than a few weeks. In the next lesson, we will cover installation, tying, and spacing of rebar in foundations, columns, beams, and slabs. Before that, practice this: take a bar schedule, calculate the cutting length for a 12 mm bar with a 90-degree bend at one end and a 135-degree hook at the other. Then bend a sample and check it against the drawing. This hands-on practice will make you a more effective steel fixer.

3. Installation, Tying, and Spacing of Rebar

Now that you can set out, cut, and bend rebar, this lesson teaches you how to install it correctly. Installation is where the structure takes shape. Poor installation leads to concrete cover problems, congestion, and structural weakness. We will cover foundations, columns, beams, slabs, and walls, with a focus on African site conditions. Start with foundations. Footings and raft foundations require bottom and top reinforcement. Bottom bars are placed first, on concrete cover blocks or chairs. Cover blocks ensure the required concrete cover, usually 50 mm for foundations in contact with soil. In Africa, cover blocks are often made on site with a 1:3 cement-sand mortar, or you can buy plastic spacers. Do not use stones or wood, as they rot or crush. Bottom bars are spaced according to the drawing, typically 150 mm or 200 mm centre to centre. Tie them with 1.6 mm or 2 mm annealed tie wire. Use a double tie at intersections for heavy bars. After bottom bars, place top bars on chairs or bar supports. The chairs must be strong enough to support the weight of workers and concrete. For raft foundations, you may need two layers of top and bottom bars. Ensure the vertical distance between layers is correct. Next, columns. Column reinforcement consists of vertical main bars and horizontal links. The main bars are usually 12 mm to 32 mm, and links are 8 mm or 10 mm. The number of main bars and link spacing are on the drawing. For example, a 300 mm x 300 mm column may have 4 no. 16 mm main bars and 8 mm links at 150 mm centres. Starter bars from the foundation must be long enough for a lap splice. Lap length depends on bar diameter and concrete grade; a common rule is 40 times the bar diameter for tension bars. For a 16 mm bar, lap is 640 mm. Always stagger laps; do not put all laps at the same level. Links must be placed at the correct spacing, and the first link should be within 50 mm of the floor or beam. Use a link spacing rod or a measuring tape. Tie links to main bars with tie wire at every intersection. For columns, use a plumb bob or spirit level to keep bars vertical. Brace the column cage with timber or steel props so it does not move during concreting. Beams are next. A beam has top bars, bottom bars, and links. Bottom bars carry tension at midspan; top bars carry tension at supports. Links resist shear. Place bottom bars first, then links, then top bars. The links must be spaced correctly and tied to both top and bottom bars. For deep beams, you may need side bars and cross ties. Ensure the concrete cover is maintained with cover blocks or spacers. In African markets, many beams are cast on site with timber formwork. Check that the formwork is strong and does not deflect. If the beam is wide, use chairs to support top bars. Slabs are large areas of reinforcement. A typical slab has bottom bars in two directions and top bars over supports. The bottom bars are placed on cover blocks, and top bars are supported on chairs or bar supports. Spacing is usually 150 mm or 200 mm. For a one-way slab, main bars run in the short direction; distribution bars run in the long direction. For a two-way slab, both directions are main. Tie bars at every intersection, but you can skip some ties in the middle if the spacing is tight. However, always tie the perimeter and around openings. Openings for pipes and ducts must have trimmer bars. Do not cut main bars without engineer approval. Walls, such as retaining walls and shear walls, have vertical and horizontal bars. Vertical bars are placed first, then horizontal bars. Use cover blocks on both faces. For tall walls, use spacers to keep the two layers apart. Tying is a skill. Use a tie wire reel and a hook. A simple tie is a loop that holds two bars together. A cross tie is stronger. For heavy bars, use a double tie or a figure-of-eight tie. Do not overtighten, as you may break the wire. Do not leave sharp ends that can injure workers. Cut the ends or bend them into the concrete. Spacing is critical. Use a spacing bar or a template. A spacing bar is a piece of timber or steel with nails or notches at the required spacing. You place it along the bars and move it as you tie. This ensures uniform spacing. Check spacing with a tape measure every few metres. If spacing is wrong, the structural capacity is reduced. In Africa, labour is often abundant but supervision may be weak. As a supervisor, you must check installation daily. Take photos. Keep a checklist: cover, spacing, lap length, link spacing, tie security, and cleanliness. Remove dust, mud, and oil from bars before concreting. If bars are rusty, wire-brush them. If they are coated with epoxy, handle carefully to avoid damage. Business tip: train your steel fixers to work in teams. A typical team has one lead fixer, two helpers, and one tie wire cutter. Pay by output, not by day, to increase productivity. Provide incentives for zero rework. Keep a daily record of bars installed and compare with the bar schedule. This helps you claim for variations and avoid disputes. In the next lesson, we will cover quality control, inspection, and common mistakes. Before that, practice installing a small column cage and a beam cage in your yard. Measure cover, spacing, and lap length. Ask a colleague to inspect it. This practice will build your confidence and speed.

4. Quality Control, Inspection, and Common Mistakes

Quality control is what separates a professional steel fixing business from an amateur one. In this lesson, we cover inspection procedures, common mistakes, and how to fix them. We also discuss documentation and how to work with engineers and consultants in African construction projects. Inspection starts before installation. Check the bar schedule against the structural drawings. Check the delivered bars: diameter, length, straightness, and surface condition. Use a calliper or a micrometer to measure diameter. A 12 mm bar should measure 12 mm plus or minus 0.3 mm. If it is 11.5 mm, it is underweight and may not meet the required strength. Check the mill certificate. If there is no certificate, send a sample to a testing laboratory. In many African countries, laboratories are available in major cities. The cost is small compared to the risk of failure. During installation, inspect the following: cover, spacing, lap length, link spacing, number of bars, and tie security. Cover is the most common problem. If cover is too small, the steel will corrode and concrete will spall. If cover is too large, the effective depth is reduced, and the beam or slab may crack. Use cover blocks of the correct size. For foundations, cover is 50 mm to 75 mm. For columns and beams, cover is 25 mm to 40 mm. For slabs, cover is 20 mm to 25 mm. Measure cover with a cover meter or a simple depth gauge. If you do not have a cover meter, break out a small area after concreting to check, but this is destructive. Better to check before concreting. Spacing errors are also common. A worker may start with correct spacing but drift. Use a spacing bar and check every few metres. If spacing is wrong, adjust before tying. Lap length errors are dangerous. A lap that is too short will slip under load. A lap that is too long wastes steel. Use the correct lap length from the drawing or the code. For example, in many African countries, the code is based on British Standards or Eurocode. A common lap length for high-yield bars in tension is 40 times the diameter. For compression, it is 30 times the diameter. Always stagger laps. Link spacing errors are common in columns and beams. Links must be spaced correctly and tied to main bars. If a link is missing, shear capacity is reduced. Count the links and check spacing. Tie security: if ties are loose, bars may move during concreting. Use enough ties. A common rule is to tie at every intersection for bars larger than 16 mm, and every second intersection for smaller bars. But always tie the perimeter and around openings. Common mistakes include: using the wrong diameter bar, using rusty bars without cleaning, forgetting starter bars, placing bars in the wrong direction, and not providing chairs for top bars. Another mistake is cutting bars without engineer approval. If you cut a main bar to make way for a pipe, you must add trimmer bars. Never cut without approval. Documentation is essential. Keep a daily site diary. Record the date, weather, number of workers, bars installed, and any instructions from the engineer. Take photos of every stage: before tying, after tying, and before concreting. Use a checklist for each element. This protects you if there is a dispute. In Africa, many projects have payment delays. Good documentation helps you claim for work done. It also helps you train new workers. Working with engineers: engineers and consultants may visit the site. Be respectful and cooperative. If they ask for a change, get it in writing. If you see a design error, raise it politely. Do not argue in front of workers. Keep a copy of all drawings and schedules. If the engineer gives a verbal instruction, confirm it by email or WhatsApp and keep a screenshot. Business tip: offer a quality guarantee. Tell your client that you will fix any defect within 12 months. This builds trust and helps you win more work. Also, invest in a small testing kit: a tape measure, calliper, cover meter, and a spring balance for tie wire tension. These tools are affordable and show professionalism. Finally, learn from mistakes. After each project, hold a debrief with your team. Discuss what went well and what went wrong. Calculate your wastage and your rework cost. Use this information to improve your next project. In the next lesson, we will cover business management, pricing, and scaling your steel fixing enterprise. Before that, inspect a recently completed slab or beam. Check cover, spacing, and ties. Write a short report. This practice will sharpen your eye for detail.

5. Business Management, Pricing, and Scaling Your Steel Fixing Enterprise

This final lesson focuses on the business side of steel fixing. You already know the technical skills. Now you need to run a profitable enterprise. We will cover estimating, pricing, labour management, equipment, and growth strategies for African markets. Estimating starts with the bar schedule. Calculate the total weight of steel required. Weight per metre for common bars: 6 mm = 0.222 kg, 8 mm = 0.395 kg, 10 mm = 0.617 kg, 12 mm = 0.888 kg, 16 mm = 1.579 kg, 20 mm = 2.466 kg, 25 mm = 3.854 kg, 32 mm = 6.313 kg, 40 mm = 9.864 kg. Multiply the length by the weight per metre. Add 5 to 10 percent for wastage, laps, and offcuts. Then add tie wire. A common estimate is 1 kg of tie wire per 100 kg of rebar. Add cover blocks: about 4 to 6 per square metre of slab, and 2 to 4 per metre of beam or column. Add chairs for top bars. Then calculate labour. A skilled steel fixer can install 500 kg to 1000 kg per day, depending on complexity. A team of four can install 2 to 4 tonnes per day. Estimate the number of days and multiply by the daily wage. In Africa, daily wages vary. In Nigeria, a steel fixer may earn 5,000 to 10,000 naira per day. In Kenya, 800 to 1,500 shillings. In South Africa, 300 to 600 rand. Always pay fairly but competitively. Add overheads: transport, fuel, tools, rent, and supervision. Add profit: 15 to 30 percent. Then quote. Do not quote too low. A low quote may win the job but ruin your business. Pricing can be per tonne, per square metre, or per bar. Per tonne is common for large projects. Per square metre is common for slabs. Per bar is common for small jobs. Choose the method that suits your client. Always put your quote in writing. Include scope, exclusions, payment terms, and validity period. Payment terms: ask for a mobilisation advance of 30 to 50 percent. This covers your initial costs. Then progress payments. Do not work for months without payment. If the client delays, stop work and issue a notice. Keep records of all payments. Labour management: hire reliable workers. Train them. Pay on time. Provide safety gear. Create a team culture. Reward good performance. Do not tolerate theft or laziness. Keep a register of workers and their daily attendance. Use a simple payroll system. If you have many workers, consider using a mobile money service to pay wages. This reduces cash handling and theft. Equipment: start with basic tools: tape measures, hammers, tie wire hooks, cutting pliers, spirit levels, and spanners. Then invest in a bar cutting machine and a bar bending machine. A good machine costs 1,000 to 5,000 USD, depending on capacity. You can rent or buy used. A machine increases your capacity and reduces labour. Also invest in a generator if power is unreliable. A small generator costs 200 to 500 USD. Buy good quality tools. Cheap tools break and cause injuries. Scaling: to grow, you need to take on larger projects. Build a reputation for quality and reliability. Network with contractors, engineers, and architects. Join construction associations. Attend trade fairs. Use social media to showcase your work. Take photos and videos. Create a simple website or a WhatsApp business profile. Bid for government projects. Many African governments have local content policies that favour local contractors. Register your business. Get a tax identification number. Keep proper books. Hire an accountant part-time. Consider diversifying: you can also supply rebar, provide formwork, or do concrete works. But do not diversify too fast. Master steel fixing first. Risk management: steel fixing has risks. Injuries, theft, non-payment, and price fluctuations. Mitigate by training, security, contracts, and buying steel early. If steel prices are rising, buy in bulk. If they are falling, buy as needed. Keep a cash reserve for three months of expenses. Finally, think about legacy. Train apprentices. Many young people in Africa need skills. By training them, you build a loyal workforce and contribute to your community. Write down your processes. Create a training manual. This makes your business less dependent on you. In conclusion, steel fixing is a noble and profitable trade. With technical skill and business sense, you can build a successful enterprise. Remember: quality first, safety always, and profit with purpose. This concludes Module 10. Complete the final assessment: prepare a quote for a small building with 5 tonnes of rebar. Include steel, labour, tie wire, cover blocks, overheads, and profit. Present it to your instructor or a colleague for feedback. Good luck.

6. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Rebar Detailing, Estimation, and Site Documentation for African Construction Enterprises

Steel fixing is not only a trade it is a business. In most African markets, a steel fixing enterprise wins or loses money not at the tying stage but at the estimation, detailing, and documentation stage. A bar bending schedule (BBS) that is wrong by 5% can wipe out the margin on an entire slab. A missing delivery note can delay payment for months. This Practical Reference lesson gives you the field-ready tables, formulas, and paperwork habits that turn a gang of fixers into a contracting business that banks reliably.

The figures below are drawn from international standards and development-agency practice that apply directly to projects across Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana, Tanzania, Rwanda, Uganda, and South Africa. Use them as your default reference sheet on site and in the office.

1. Unit Weight and Cutting Length Reference

The single most useful number in steel fixing is the unit weight of bar. The formula is W = D/162.2 kg/m (D in mm), derived from the density of steel (7,850 kg/m). This is the same formula used in British Standard BS 8666 and in most African national building codes. Memorise the table below it is your estimating engine.

Cutting length rule of thumb: Cutting length = clear span + 2 anchorage + 2 hook allowance 2 bend deduction. For a standard 90 bend, deduct 2d; for a 135 stirrup hook, add 10d per hook. These deductions are standard in BS 8666 and are what your client's engineer will use to check your BBS. If you over-order by ignoring bend deductions, you carry the loss. If you under-order, you delay the pour.

SAFETY WARNING REBAR AND CHEMICAL HAZARDS

Rebar work exposes fixers to serious injury and long-term health harm. Always: wear leather gloves and safety boots when handling rebar cut ends are razor-sharp and a 12 mm bar can cause deep puncture wounds; wear eye protection when cutting with angle grinders (metal fragments cause permanent eye damage); use ear protection when using power benders or grinders; never stand under a suspended bundle of rebar; and secure all vertical starter bars with caps or bent-over ends to prevent impalement. Where epoxy coatings, form release agents, or curing compounds are used, follow the manufacturer's safety data sheet, ensure ventilation, and wash skin immediately on contact these are chemical hazards, not just "site dust." Report any cut, puncture, or eye injury for medical review the same day.

3. Site Documentation That Gets You Paid

In African construction, disputes are usually about measurement and variation, not quality. The enterprise that documents daily wins. Keep four records on every job:

  1. Bar Bending Schedule (BBS) signed by the engineer before cutting. This is your contract for quantity.
  2. Daily Site Diary date, weather, gang size, work done, kg fixed, delays, instructions received verbally. A verbal instruction not written down is a free variation for the client.
  3. Delivery Notes and Weighbridge Tickets every tonne received, with supplier, vehicle, and time. Reconcile weekly against BBS.
  4. Measurement Sheets and Interim Payment Certificates measured kg, rate, amount, signed by the resident engineer or clerk of works. Never proceed to the next floor without a signed measurement for the last one.

Photograph every stage: before pour, after fixing, with a dated marker. In markets where payment disputes go to adjudication, a dated photo with a visible tape measure is worth more than a verbal assurance.

4. Wastage, Lap, and Ordering Control

Standard lap lengths for deformed bars in tension are typically 40d to 50d depending on concrete grade and bar diameter (BS 8110 / Eurocode 2 practice). A 16 mm bar needs roughly 640800 mm lap. If you order exactly the BBS quantity with no allowance, you will run short because of cutting waste, bend errors, and site damage. Use these allowances:

  • Cutting and bending wastage: 23% for straight bars, 46% for stirrups and links.
  • Lap allowance: already in the BBS do not double-count.
  • Site damage and theft: 12% on open sites; higher where security is weak.
  • Total order allowance: 58% above net BBS weight for most building jobs.

Order in full 12 m stock lengths where possible. Cutting 12 m into 6 m creates offcuts; nesting cuts on paper before cutting steel saves 35% of material cost which, on a 20-tonne job, is a full tonne of steel.

5. Quick Reference Checklist

  • BBS prepared, checked against structural drawings, and signed by the engineer before any cutting.
  • Bar diameter, grade, and coating verified against delivery notes and mill certificates on arrival.
  • Cutting list nested to minimise offcuts; bend deductions (2d per 90 bend, 10d per hook) applied.
  • Lap lengths confirmed (typically 40d50d) and marked on site before fixing.
  • Cover blocks, spacers, and chairs in place and checked against specified cover (e.g., 2550 mm).
  • Daily site diary completed with kg fixed, gang size, delays, and verbal instructions.
  • Measurement sheet signed by clerk of works or resident engineer before concrete pour.
  • Dated photographs of every fixed element taken before pour and filed with the payment claim.
