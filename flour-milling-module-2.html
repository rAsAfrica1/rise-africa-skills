1. Structure of the Wheat Kernel

The wheat kernel, also called a grain, is a small seed that contains three main parts: the bran, the germ, and the endosperm. Understanding these parts is the first step to milling good flour. The bran is the hard outer layer that protects the seed. It makes up about 14-15% of the kernel weight. The bran is rich in fibre, vitamins, and minerals, but it does not mill into fine flour easily. The germ is the tiny embryo at the bottom of the kernel. It is about 2-3% of the weight and contains oil, vitamins, and proteins. The germ can go rancid quickly if not stored properly, so many millers remove it to make flour with a longer shelf life. The endosperm is the largest part, about 82-84% of the kernel. It is mostly starch and protein, and it is what we grind into white flour.

In Africa, wheat kernels are usually smaller and harder than those grown in Europe or North America. For example, a typical kernel from Ethiopia or Kenya weighs about 30-40 milligrams. When you inspect wheat, you can see the creasea deep groove along one side of the kernel. This crease traps dirt and small stones, so cleaning is very important before milling. The brush end, opposite the germ, has fine hairs called brush. These hairs can also hold dust.

To see the structure yourself, take a few wheat kernels and soak them in water for 24 hours. Then cut them lengthwise with a sharp knife. You will see the bran as a dark outer layer, the endosperm as a white starchy area, and the germ as a small yellow-brown part at the bottom. This simple test helps you understand what your mill is doing when it separates bran from endosperm.

When you mill, you want to break the kernel so that the endosperm breaks into small pieces while the bran stays in larger flakes. This makes it easier to sift out the bran. If you mill too hard, the bran shatters into tiny bits that mix with the flour, making it darker and speckled. So knowing the kernel structure helps you adjust your mill settings for better flour.

2. Chemical Composition of Wheat

Wheat kernels are made of water, carbohydrates, proteins, fats, minerals, and vitamins. The exact amounts vary with variety, growing conditions, and storage. On average, a dry wheat kernel contains about 70-75% carbohydrates, 10-15% protein, 2-3% fat, 1-2% minerals (ash), and 12-14% water. These numbers matter to millers because they affect how the wheat mills and how the flour behaves.

Carbohydrates are mainly starch, which is the energy store of the seed. Starch is what makes flour thicken and gel when heated. The protein in wheat is special because it can form gluten when mixed with water. Gluten gives dough its elasticity and strength. Hard wheats, like those grown in the highlands of Ethiopia and Kenya, have more protein (12-14%) than soft wheats (8-10%). That is why hard wheats are used for bread and soft wheats for cakes and biscuits.

Fat is mostly in the germ. Even though it is only 2-3% of the kernel, fat can go rancid and give flour a bad smell. That is why whole wheat flour has a shorter shelf life than white flour. Minerals, measured as ash, are mostly in the bran. Ash content tells you how much bran is in the flour. White flour has low ash (0.4-0.6%), while whole wheat flour has high ash (1.5-2.0%).

In Africa, wheat is often grown in areas with high temperatures and drought. This can reduce protein content and make the grain harder. For example, wheat from Sudan or northern Nigeria may have lower protein but still mill well if handled correctly. To check protein, you can send a sample to a lab, but a simple field test is to chew a few kernels: hard, glassy kernels usually have more protein than soft, floury ones.

Understanding composition helps you decide how to mill. High-protein wheat needs more water during tempering (adding water before milling) to soften the bran and toughen the endosperm. Low-protein wheat may need less water. By knowing what is in your wheat, you can adjust your mill and get the flour you want.

3. Wheat Classes and Varieties

Wheat is not just one crop. There are many classes and varieties, each with different milling and baking qualities. In Africa, the most common classes are hard red winter, hard red spring, soft red winter, durum, and soft white. These names tell you about the kernel's hardness, colour, and the season it is grown.

Hard red winter wheat is grown in places with cold winters, like the highlands of Ethiopia and Kenya. It has high protein and is good for bread. Hard red spring wheat is grown in cooler areas and also has high protein. Soft red winter wheat has lower protein and is used for cakes, biscuits, and pastries. Durum wheat is the hardest and is used for pasta like macaroni and spaghetti. Soft white wheat is low in protein and is used for noodles and flatbreads.

In Africa, local varieties are also important. For example, in Ethiopia, farmers grow improved varieties like 'Kakaba' and 'Danda'a', which are hard wheats with good bread-making quality. In Kenya, 'Kenya Nyati' and 'Kenya Fahari' are popular. In South Africa, 'SST' varieties are common. These varieties are bred for drought tolerance, disease resistance, and good milling quality.

When you buy wheat for milling, ask for the class and variety. If you want to make bread, choose hard wheat with 12-14% protein. For cakes, choose soft wheat with 8-10% protein. If you mix them, you get medium protein, which is good for all-purpose flour. You can also blend different varieties to get the protein level you need.

A simple test to tell hard from soft wheat: take a kernel and bite it. Hard wheat is vitreous (glassy) and shatters; soft wheat is opaque and crushes easily. Another test is to cut the kernel: hard wheat looks glassy inside, soft wheat looks floury. Knowing your wheat class helps you set your mill and predict how the flour will bake.

4. Wheat Grading and Specifications

Wheat grading is a system to judge the quality of a batch of wheat. It looks at factors like moisture, test weight, foreign matter, broken kernels, and damaged kernels. In Africa, many countries follow national grading standards, often similar to those used in Europe or the USA. For example, Kenya has grades like Grade 1, Grade 2, and Grade 3, based on test weight and defects.

Moisture content is critical. If wheat is too wet (above 14%), it can grow mould and rot. If it is too dry (below 10%), it becomes brittle and shatters during milling, making too much bran powder. The ideal moisture for safe storage is 12-13%. You can check moisture with a moisture meter, but a simple field method is to bite a kernel: if it is hard and cracks, it is dry; if it is soft and chewy, it is too wet.

Test weight is the weight of a fixed volume of wheat, usually expressed in kilograms per hectolitre (kg/hl). High test weight (above 76 kg/hl) means plump, heavy kernels with more endosperm and less bran. Low test weight (below 70 kg/hl) means thin, shrivelled kernels with less flour. In Africa, a good wheat should have a test weight of at least 75 kg/hl.

Foreign matter includes stones, dirt, seeds, and chaff. Grade 1 wheat should have less than 1% foreign matter. Broken kernels and damaged kernels (sprouted, insect-damaged, or diseased) should also be low. Too many broken kernels make flour specky and reduce yield.

To grade wheat, you need a sample, a scale, a sieve, and a moisture meter. Take a 1 kg sample, remove foreign matter by hand or sieve, and weigh the clean wheat. Then calculate percentages. If you are buying wheat, always ask for a grading certificate or test the wheat yourself. Good grading means better flour and less waste.

5. Impact of Kernel Properties on Milling

The properties of the wheat kernel directly affect how it mills and the quality of the flour. Hardness, size, moisture, and protein content are the most important. Hard wheat is harder to grind and requires more energy, but it produces flour with good bread-making quality. Soft wheat grinds easily but gives weaker flour. If you mill hard wheat too finely, you can damage the starch and make the flour sticky.

Kernel size matters. Large kernels have more endosperm and less bran, so they give higher flour yield. Small kernels have more bran relative to endosperm, so yield is lower. In Africa, wheat kernels are often smaller due to drought stress. You can adjust your mill to handle smaller kernels by reducing the gap between the stones or rollers. If the gap is too wide, the kernels pass through without breaking; if too narrow, you get too much bran powder.

Moisture content affects milling too. Dry wheat shatters, creating fine bran particles that are hard to sift out. Wet wheat gums up the mill and can clog screens. The ideal moisture for milling is 14-15% for hard wheat and 13-14% for soft wheat. This is achieved by tempering: adding water to the wheat and letting it sit for 12-24 hours. Tempering makes the bran tough and the endosperm soft, so they separate cleanly.

Protein content influences gluten strength. High-protein wheat needs more water in tempering to develop gluten properly. Low-protein wheat needs less. If you mill low-protein wheat for bread, you may get a flat loaf. You can blend it with high-protein wheat to improve it.

Finally, damaged kernels (sprouted, insect-damaged) produce poor flour. Sprouted kernels have high enzyme activity that breaks down starch, making sticky dough. Always remove damaged kernels before milling. By understanding these properties, you can adjust your mill and tempering to get the best flour from your wheat.

6. Hands-on Wheat Inspection

Hands-on inspection is the best way to judge wheat quality before you buy or mill. You do not need a laboratory. With a few simple tools and your senses, you can check moisture, test weight, foreign matter, and kernel condition. This lesson shows you how.

First, collect a representative sample. Take small handfuls from different parts of the bag or truck. Mix them together. You need about 1 kg for a good test. Look at the sample: good wheat should be uniform in colour, with no mould, no insects, and no bad smell. If you see black spots, it may be diseased. If it smells musty, it is mouldy.

Next, check moisture. Bite a few kernels. If they are hard and crack between your teeth, moisture is likely below 13%. If they are soft and chewy, moisture is above 14%. For a more accurate test, use a moisture meter. In Africa, you can buy small moisture meters for about $50-$100. If you don't have one, use the bite test and also squeeze a handful: if kernels stick together, it's too wet.

Check test weight. Fill a 1-litre container with wheat, level it off, and weigh it. A good test weight is 750-800 grams per litre (75-80 kg/hl). If it weighs less than 700 grams, the wheat is light and will give low flour yield. You can also use a 1-pint container and convert.

Remove foreign matter. Spread a sample on a clean surface and pick out stones, dirt, seeds, and chaff. Weigh the clean wheat and calculate the percentage of foreign matter. Grade 1 wheat should have less than 1%. Also look for broken kernels and damaged kernels. Count them in a 100-kernel sample. More than 5% broken means poor quality.

Finally, do a simple milling test. Grind a small amount in a coffee grinder or hand mill. Feel the flour: it should be smooth, not gritty. If it is gritty, the bran is not separated well. By doing these checks, you can avoid buying bad wheat and get better flour.

<p>This lesson is the working reference for the module <strong>Wheat Kernel Anatomy and Composition</strong>. It is written for mill managers, quality controllers, procurement officers and production supervisors who buy, test and mill wheat in African markets. Everything here is designed to be used at the intake pit, in the lab, and in commercial negotiation. The goal is simple: reduce risk, protect extraction rate, and defend the margin between what you pay for wheat and what you sell as flour.</p>

Practical kernel inspection is therefore not an academic exercise; it is a commercial control point.</p>

<h3>Why Kernel Anatomy Drives Your Milling Economics</h3>

<p>The wheat kernel has three commercially distinct parts. The <strong>endosperm</strong> (8285% of kernel weight) is what you sell as flour. The <strong>bran</strong> (1215%) is the by-product you sell as animal feed, but it also dilutes flour if poorly separated. The <strong>germ</strong> (23%) contains oil and enzymes that reduce flour shelf life and cause rancidity a critical issue in hot, humid African storage conditions.</p>

<p>Every milling decision tempering time, roll gap, sifter configuration is an attempt to separate endosperm from bran cleanly while minimising germ contamination. Kernel hardness, vitreousness, moisture and test weight all predict how easily that separation will happen. Your inspection protocols must capture these variables before the wheat enters the mill.</p>

<h3>Hands-On Wheat Inspection: The Practical Sequence</h3>

<p>At intake, run a repeatable sequence. <strong>Step 1: sampling.</strong> Use a spear or diverter sampler to take at least 5 sub-samples per truck; never inspect only the top of the load. <strong>Step 2: visual check.</strong> Look for colour, mould, insect damage, sprouting and foreign matter (stones, glass, metal, ergot). <strong>Step 3: moisture.</strong> Use a calibrated moisture meter; record every load. <strong>Step 4: test weight.</strong> Use a standard 1-litre or 0.5-litre measure. <strong>Step 5: physical grading.</strong> Sieve for broken kernels, shrivelled grain and foreign material. <strong>Step 6: protein and vitreousness.</strong> Use NIR if available, or a cut kernel test for vitreousness. <strong>Step 7: documentation.</strong> Record results against the supplier and truck number.</p>

<p>For small and medium mills without a full lab, the practical minimum kit is: moisture meter, test weight measure, set of sieves, hand lens, knife for cut kernel test, and a reference sample library. This kit can be assembled for under US$1,500 and pays back within a single disputed truckload.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #b00020;background:#fdecec;padding:14px;margin:20px 0;border-radius:6px;">
<strong style="color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING Grain Dust and Fumigation Hazards</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Wheat dust is combustible and can cause explosions in enclosed handling areas. Always use dust extraction and avoid open flames near intake pits, elevators and bins. If wheat has been fumigated (common with phosphine tablets in African storage), never enter a silo or sealed container without gas monitoring and a permit-to-work procedure. Phosphine is highly toxic. Wear appropriate PPE dust mask, eye protection and gloves during all sampling and sieving. Mouldy grain may carry mycotoxins; avoid inhaling dust from suspect loads and reject visibly mouldy wheat at intake.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Sample properly:</strong> minimum 5 sub-samples per truck, from different depths, combined into one representative sample.</li>
<li><strong>Check moisture first:</strong> reject or segregate loads above 14% moisture; schedule drying before storage.</li>
<li><strong>Measure test weight:</strong> below 72 kg/hl triggers a price or rejection discussion with the supplier.</li>
<li><strong>Inspect visually:</strong> colour uniformity, mould, insects, stones, glass, metal, ergot and sprouted kernels.</li>
<li><strong>Run the cut kernel test:</strong> assess vitreousness and endosperm texture to predict milling behaviour.</li>
<li><strong>Test protein:</strong> confirm against contract specification; record for blending decisions.</li>
<li><strong>Document and retain:</strong> keep a sealed reference sample of every accepted load for at least 90 days.</li>
<li><strong>Communicate results:</strong> feed inspection data back to procurement and production daily, not monthly.</li>
</ol>

<h3>Business-First Takeaways for African Millers</h3>

<p>Quality inspection is a profit centre, not a cost centre. Every load accepted below specification transfers value from your mill to your supplier. In markets where wheat is imported, blended with local grain, and traded through multiple intermediaries, the mill that inspects rigorously negotiates from strength. Build a simple intake database supplier, truck, moisture, test weight, protein, foreign matter, decision and within one season you will know which suppliers consistently deliver value and which ones cost you money.</p>

<p>Kernel anatomy is not theory. The bran you fail to separate becomes feed instead of flour. The germ you fail to remove becomes rancidity in a hot warehouse. The endosperm you fail to extract becomes lost revenue. Practical inspection connects the biology of the kernel to the commercial reality of the mill. Master it, and you control your margin.</p>
