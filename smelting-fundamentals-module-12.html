Lessons

1. Direct Reduction Processes

Direct reduction (DR) turns iron ore into metallic iron without melting it. You use a reducing gasmostly hydrogen and carbon monoxideto strip oxygen from iron ore at 8001000C. The product is called direct reduced iron (DRI) or sponge iron. In Africa, natural gas is the main reducing agent. Nigeria, Algeria, and Egypt have gas reserves, so DR plants make sense there. A typical MIDREX plant uses a shaft furnace. Iron ore pellets go in the top. Hot gas (about 850C) flows up through the bed. The gas comes from reforming natural gas with CO2 and steam. The oxygen in the ore combines with hydrogen and carbon, leaving iron. The DRI drops out the bottom with 9094% iron content. You can feed DRI to an electric arc furnace (EAF) to make steel. That avoids the blast furnace route, which needs coke and sinter. For a small operation in Africa, a rotary kiln DR is cheaper. You use coal instead of gas. The kiln is a long rotating tube. Iron ore and coal are mixed and heated to 10001100C. The coal produces CO that reduces the ore. The product is cooled and magnetically separated. A 50,000-tonne-per-year rotary kiln costs about $1520 million. Thats affordable for a regional steel mill. But watch out: DRI can re-oxidize if wet. Store it dry and use it within a few weeks. Also, the reduction gas must be cleaned of dust and sulphur. A venturi scrubber and a desulphurization unit are standard. In Africa, direct reduction works best where you have cheap gas or coal and a nearby EAF. Countries like South Africa, Egypt, and Nigeria already run DR plants. You can learn to operate a DR shaft by monitoring gas temperature, pressure, and metallization. Metallization is the percentage of iron that is metallic. Aim for 9295%. If it drops, check gas flow or ore quality. This is hands-on work: you adjust valves, read gauges, and test samples every hour. Direct reduction is a bridge between mining and steelmaking, and Africa has the raw materials to do it.

2. Smelting Reduction Technologies

Smelting reduction (SR) melts iron ore and reduces it in one vessel. You skip the sinter and coke plants. Instead, you inject coal and oxygen into a molten bath. The coal burns and produces CO, which reduces the ore. The heat melts the iron. The product is liquid hot metal, like from a blast furnace, but you can use cheaper coal and fines. The two main SR processes are COREX and HIsarna. COREX uses a shaft furnace to pre-reduce ore, then a melter-gasifier where coal is gasified and final reduction happens. HIsarna uses a cyclone converter furnace: ore fines are injected with oxygen, and coal is added to a molten bath. In Africa, SR is attractive because you can use non-coking coal. South Africa has huge coal reserves, and companies like ArcelorMittal South Africa have tested SR. A small SR unit, say 100,000 tonnes per year, costs around $80120 million. Thats higher than DR but lower than a full blast furnace complex. The key operating parameters are bath temperature (14501550C), oxygen flow, and coal feed rate. You must control the foaming slag. If the slag foams too much, it can block the vessel. You add dolomite or lime to keep slag basicity (CaO/SiO2) between 1.0 and 1.3. For a learner, the daily tasks include checking the oxygen lance, measuring bath temperature with a disposable thermocouple, and tapping the iron. Tapping is done every 23 hours. You drill a clay plug and let the metal flow into a ladle. Then you plug it with a mud gun. Safety is critical: molten metal at 1500C will burn through anything. Wear full aluminized gear. SR plants are rare in Africa, but the technology is proven in India and China. For African steelmakers, SR offers a way to use local coal and ore fines, which are often wasted. You can train on a pilot plant or a computer simulator. The main challenge is refractory life. The melter-gasifier lining lasts 612 months. You must monitor wear and patch it with gunning mix. If you master SR, you can work in any advanced steel plant.

3. Plasma Smelting

Plasma smelting uses a plasma torch to generate very high temperaturesup to 5000C. A plasma torch works by passing gas (argon, nitrogen, or air) through an electric arc. The gas becomes ionized and conducts electricity. You aim the plasma at a furnace charge. The heat melts and reduces the ore almost instantly. In Africa, plasma smelting is used for ferroalloys, titanium, and even waste recycling. For example, South Africas Mintek has a plasma pilot plant for ferrochrome. A plasma furnace is usually a DC arc furnace with a hollow graphite electrode. You feed ore fines, reductant (coal or coke), and flux into the plasma zone. The plasma gas is often argon for reactive metals, or air for iron. The main advantage is you can use fine ores without agglomeration. You also get very high temperatures, so you can smelt refractory ores. A 1 MW plasma torch costs about $50,000100,000. A 5 MW furnace can produce 10,000 tonnes of ferroalloy per year. Operating a plasma furnace requires monitoring arc voltage (80200 V) and current (10005000 A). You control the torch power by adjusting current. The plasma gas flow is 1050 Nm3/h. You must keep the furnace pressure slightly positive to prevent air ingress. The biggest challenge is electrode life. Graphite electrodes erode and must be replaced every 100200 hours. You also need a reliable power supply. In Africa, grid instability means you need a backup generator or a captive power plant. For a learner, start by understanding the DC circuit. You can practice on a small 50 kW plasma unit. Youll learn to strike the arc, adjust gas flow, and tap the molten metal. Plasma smelting is not for high-volume ironmaking, but it is perfect for high-value metals like chromium, manganese, and titanium. Africa has 70% of the worlds chrome ore, so plasma smelting of ferrochrome is a big opportunity. You can work in a smelter in Rustenburg or Middelburg. The skills you need: electrical safety, refractory repair, and gas handling. If you can operate a plasma furnace, you are in demand.

4. Microwave-Assisted Smelting

Microwave-assisted smelting uses microwaves (2.45 GHz) to heat and reduce ore. Unlike conventional heating, microwaves heat the material from the inside. This saves energy and speeds up reduction. In Africa, microwave smelting is still experimental, but it works well for small-scale operations. You can build a microwave furnace from a domestic microwave oven (1000 W) for testing. For production, you need a industrial microwave cavity (5100 kW). The ore is mixed with a reductant like charcoal or coal. The mixture is placed in a crucible that is transparent to microwaves (e.g., alumina or quartz). The microwaves are absorbed by the ore and carbon. The ore heats to 10001200C in minutes. Reduction happens fast because the carbon is in direct contact. You can smelt iron, copper, and even gold from concentrates. A 30 kW microwave furnace can process 50 kg per hour. Thats small but ideal for artisanal mining communities. The key parameters are microwave power, exposure time, and particle size. You want ore particles less than 1 mm. You also need to control the atmospheresometimes you add nitrogen or argon to prevent oxidation. The main advantage is low capital cost. A 30 kW unit costs about $30,00050,000. You can run it on solar panels and batteries. In Africa, that means off-grid smelting. But there are challenges: microwaves can cause arcing if the ore is too conductive. You must tune the cavity. Also, the crucible must be microwave-transparent. For a learner, start by testing different ores in a kitchen microwave. Measure weight loss and check for metallic prills. Then scale up. You can use a graphite susceptor to help heating. The future is hybrid: microwave pre-reduction followed by conventional melting. This cuts energy by 3050%. In Africa, microwave smelting could help small miners process their own ore instead of selling raw. You can train at a technical college or a maker space. The skills are electrical safety, microwave handling, and basic metallurgy. If you master this, you can build your own smelter.

5. Recycling and Secondary Smelting

Recycling and secondary smelting turn scrap metal into new metal. In Africa, this is the biggest smelting sector. You collect scrapaluminium, copper, lead, steeland melt it in a furnace. The product is secondary metal, which uses 90% less energy than primary smelting. For aluminium, you need a reverberatory furnace or a rotary furnace. The melting point is 660C. You add flux (salt cake) to remove oxides. The salt cake is a mixture of NaCl and KCl. It covers the melt and prevents oxidation. You also use chlorine or argon to degas the melt. A 5-tonne rotary furnace costs $50,000100,000. You can run it on diesel, gas, or waste oil. In Nigeria, thousands of small foundries melt aluminium scrap into pots, pans, and car parts. For copper, you use a crucible furnace or an induction furnace. You melt copper at 1085C. You must remove insulation and other contaminants. You can use a slagging agent like borax. For lead, you recycle car batteries. You break the batteries, separate the lead grids, and smelt them in a short rotary furnace. Lead fumes are toxic, so you need a fume hood and a baghouse. The biggest challenge in Africa is feedstock quality. Scrap is often mixed and dirty. You need to sort by alloy. Use a magnet for steel, and a spark test for aluminium vs. zinc. You also need to manage dross. Dross is the waste skimmed off the melt. It contains metal and oxides. You can reprocess dross in a rotary salt furnace to recover more metal. For a learner, start by building a simple charcoal-fired crucible furnace. Melt 5 kg of aluminium cans. Youll learn to skim dross and pour ingots. Then scale up. Secondary smelting is a business: you can buy scrap, smelt it, and sell ingots. In Africa, demand for aluminium and copper is high. You can make cooking pots, roofing sheets, or electrical wire. The key is to keep your furnace hot and your metal clean. Always wear a face shield and gloves. Never add wet scrap to molten metalit explodes. With practice, you can run a profitable recycling smelter.

6. Future Trends and Innovations

The future of smelting in Africa is about green energy, digital control, and small-scale innovation. Hydrogen smelting is coming. Instead of coal, you use green hydrogen to reduce iron ore. The byproduct is water, not CO2. Swedens HYBRIT project is leading, but Africa can follow. South Africa has platinum and hydrogen potential. You can use solar-powered electrolyzers to make hydrogen. A pilot plant in Morocco is testing hydrogen DR. The key is cheap renewable electricity. Another trend is digital twins. A digital twin is a computer model of your furnace. You feed it real-time datatemperature, pressure, gas flowand it predicts problems. You can use it to train operators without risking a real furnace. In Africa, you can run a digital twin on a laptop. Free software like Python and OpenFOAM can simulate smelting. You also see more use of sensors and IoT. Wireless thermocouples and gas analyzers send data to your phone. You can monitor a furnace from anywhere. That helps with remote plants. Another trend is microwave and plasma hybrids. You pre-reduce with microwaves, then finish in a plasma furnace. This cuts energy by 40%. For artisanal miners, small modular smelters are emerging. A 10-tonne-per-day unit can fit in a shipping container. It uses solar power and a induction furnace. You can smelt gold, copper, or tin. The cost is $100,000200,000. Thats affordable for a cooperative. Finally, there is a push for zero-waste smelting. You recover all metals and turn slag into cement or road base. In South Africa, some smelters sell slag to brick makers. For a learner, the future is bright if you learn digital skills. You need to know how to read sensor data, adjust parameters, and maintain equipment. You also need to understand energy. In Africa, the sun is free. So solar smelting is a real opportunity. Start by building a small solar furnacea Fresnel lens can melt copper. Then scale up. The smelters of tomorrow will be clean, smart, and local. You can be part of that.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Advanced Smelting Technologies in African Metallurgy

This practical reference module consolidates the core operational knowledge from the six lessons in Advanced Smelting Technologies. It is designed for plant managers, metallurgical engineers, entrepreneurs, and technical decision-makers who need actionable guidance on selecting, deploying, and optimising advanced smelting routes within African industrial contexts. The focus is on commercial viability, energy access, feedstock realities, and regulatory compliance across the continent.

Why Advanced Smelting Matters for African Industry

Africa holds approximately 30% of global mineral reserves, yet captures a disproportionately small share of downstream metals value. Traditional blast furnace routes require large-scale coke production, high capital intensity, and reliable grid power conditions often absent in many African jurisdictions. Advanced smelting technologies offer alternative pathways: direct reduction using natural gas or hydrogen, smelting reduction that bypasses coke entirely, plasma systems that stabilise power quality, and microwave-assisted routes that reduce energy intensity for specific feedstocks. For African producers, these technologies can lower capital thresholds, improve feedstock flexibility, and align with decarbonisation mandates increasingly demanded by European and Asian off-takers.

SAFETY WARNING Plasma and Microwave Systems

Plasma smelting operates at arc temperatures exceeding 5,000C with high-voltage DC or AC power supplies. Microwave-assisted systems use 2.45 GHz magnetrons that present serious burn and radiation hazards if interlocks are bypassed. In African operations where grid stability varies and maintenance intervals may be extended, the following are non-negotiable: (1) all plasma torch and magnetron enclosures must be interlocked with power isolation; (2) PPE must include arc-rated face shields, aluminised suits, and RF-monitoring badges; (3) no lone working during plasma or microwave campaigns; (4) emergency shutdown drills must be conducted monthly and logged. Failure to comply has resulted in fatalities in both European and Asian installations African plants must not treat these as optional.

Business-First Decision Framework

Before committing capital to any advanced smelting route, African project developers should work through the following commercial filters:

  1. Feedstock security: Can you secure a consistent, assayed feedstock for 10+ years? Direct reduction demands high-grade pellets; smelting reduction tolerates lower-grade lump ore; recycling depends on scrap collection infrastructure.
  2. Energy contract: Plasma and microwave routes are electricity-intensive. Without a long-term PPA below USD 50/MWh, plasma economics rarely close. Gas-based DRI requires firm gas supply available in Nigeria, Mozambique, Tanzania, and North Africa.
  3. Off-take alignment: European and Asian buyers increasingly require verified low-carbon intensity. DRI with hydrogen and recycling routes offer the strongest decarbonisation narratives for export markets.
  4. Logistics and infrastructure: Rail, port, and road access determine whether DRI/HBI can reach export markets or feed local rolling mills. Landlocked operations should prioritise smelting reduction or recycling to minimise transport of gaseous or fragile products.
  5. Skills and maintenance: Plasma and microwave systems require specialised technicians. Budget for OEM training, spare parts supply chains, and remote diagnostics especially where local technical capacity is thin.
  6. Regulatory and ESG compliance: South Africa's carbon tax, Nigeria's flare-gas penalties, and evolving EU CBAM rules all influence technology choice. Recycling and hydrogen-DRI typically score best on ESG screens.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Feedstock assay verified confirm Fe grade, gangue, and moisture against technology spec.
  • Energy supply contracted gas, grid, or renewable PPA secured for 10+ years.
  • Off-take agreement signed including carbon intensity specifications.
  • Logistics route validated rail, road, and port capacity confirmed.
  • OEM support package training, spares, and remote diagnostics included.
  • Safety systems audited plasma/microwave interlocks, PPE, and emergency drills verified.
  • Environmental permits obtained emissions, waste, and water use approved.
  • Financial model stress-tested sensitivity to energy price, feedstock cost, and metal price cycles.
