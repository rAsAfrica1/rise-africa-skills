Lessons

1. Shielded Metal Arc Welding (SMAW)

Shielded Metal Arc Welding (SMAW), commonly called stick welding, is the most widely used welding process in African workshops because it is cheap, portable, and works well outdoors. The equipment is simple: a welding machine (AC or DC), an electrode holder, work clamp, and electrodes. Electrodes come in sizes like 2.5 mm, 3.2 mm, and 4.0 mm. For general steel fabrication, E6013 (for sheet metal and general work) and E7018 (for stronger joints) are common. The electrode is coated with flux that burns to create a shielding gas and slag, protecting the weld from air.

To strike an arc, drag the electrode across the metal like striking a match, then lift slightly to maintain a gap equal to the electrode diameter. For a 3.2 mm electrode, set the machine to about 90130 amps on DC electrode positive (DCEP). Travel speed should be steady, moving forward at about 24 mm per second. Keep a work angle of 90 to the plate and a drag angle of 1015 in the direction of travel. For a flat butt joint on 6 mm plate, use a root gap of 1.52 mm and a 6070 included angle.

In Africa, power supply can be unreliable, so many welders use engine-driven welders or inverters that work on 220V single phase. Always wear a welding helmet with at least shade 10, leather gloves, and a leather apron. Ventilation is criticalweld in open air or use a fan. Clean slag after each pass with a chipping hammer and wire brush. Practice on scrap steel before working on a job. A common mistake is long arcing, which causes spatter and poor penetration. Keep the arc tight. For vertical-up welding, use a triangle motion and lower amperage by 1015%.

2. Gas Metal Arc Welding (GMAW)

Gas Metal Arc Welding (GMAW), also called MIG (Metal Inert Gas) welding, uses a continuous wire electrode fed through a gun, shielded by a gas. It is faster and easier to learn than stick welding, making it popular for production work in African fabrication shops. The equipment includes a MIG welder, a gas cylinder (usually 75% argon + 25% CO2 for steel), a regulator, and a wire feeder. Common wire sizes are 0.8 mm and 1.0 mm. For mild steel, use ER70S-6 wire.

Set the voltage and wire feed speed according to the thickness of the metal. For 3 mm steel, try 1819 volts and wire speed around 45 m/min. For 6 mm steel, use 2123 volts and 67 m/min. The gas flow rate should be 1215 litres per minute. Hold the gun at a 1015 push angle (pushing away from the weld) for better visibility. Keep a contact tip to work distance (CTWD) of 1015 mm. Too much stick-out causes porosity.

GMAW works best indoors because wind blows away the shielding gas. In Africa, many workshops use a simple curtain or weld inside a shed. Clean the metal to bare steelno rust, paint, or oil. For a fillet weld on 5 mm plate, use a 0.8 mm wire at 19 volts and 5 m/min, travel speed about 300 mm/min. Listen for a steady sizzling sound like frying bacon. If it pops, increase voltage or wire speed. Replace the contact tip when it wears oval. Always turn off the gas when done and coil the hose neatly. Practice on thin sheet first to avoid burn-through.

3. Flux-Cored Arc Welding (FCAW)

Flux-Cored Arc Welding (FCAW) is like MIG welding but uses a tubular wire filled with flux instead of a separate shielding gas (though some wires require gas). It is ideal for outdoor work and windy conditions because the flux protects the weld. In African workshops, FCAW is common for heavy fabrication like truck frames, gates, and structural steel. The equipment is similar to MIG: a wire feeder, gun, and power source. Use self-shielded wire like E71T-11 (no gas needed) or gas-shielded wire like E71T-1 (requires CO2).

For self-shielded FCAW, set the polarity to DC electrode negative (DCEN) for most wires. For 1.2 mm wire, use 2024 volts and wire speed 58 m/min. For gas-shielded, use DCEP and 75% argon/25% CO2 at 1520 L/min. The gun angle should be 515 drag (pulling) to allow the flux to cover the weld. CTWD should be 1525 mm. Travel speed for a 6 mm fillet weld is about 250350 mm/min.

FCAW produces slag that must be chipped and brushed after each pass. It has deeper penetration than MIG, so it is good for thick steel. But it creates more smokealways weld in a well-ventilated area or use a respirator. For a butt joint on 10 mm plate, use a 60 V-groove, root gap 2 mm, and multiple passes. Clean between passes. A common problem is wire feeding issues; check the drive rolls and liner. Keep the gun cable straight. In Africa, FCAW is often used with a generator because it tolerates voltage fluctuations better than MIG. Practice on scrap to get the sound and speed right.

4. Gas Tungsten Arc Welding (GTAW)

Gas Tungsten Arc Welding (GTAW), or TIG (Tungsten Inert Gas) welding, uses a non-consumable tungsten electrode and a separate filler rod. It is slower and requires more skill but produces the highest quality welds, often used for stainless steel, aluminium, and thin materials. In African workshops, TIG is common for food-grade equipment, dairy tanks, and decorative work. The equipment includes a TIG torch, tungsten electrode (2% thoriated for steel, pure tungsten for aluminium), a gas lens, and a foot pedal or amperage control. Shielding gas is 100% argon.

For DC TIG on steel, use DCEN (electrode negative). Grind the tungsten to a sharp point. For 1.6 mm tungsten, set amperage to 60100 A for 3 mm steel. For aluminium, use AC and a balled tungsten. Hold the torch at a 7080 angle and add filler rod at 1520 to the work. Keep a short arc (13 mm). Travel speed is slow, about 100150 mm/min. Clean the metal to bright finishany oil or oxide will cause porosity.

TIG welding is done in a still environment because even a small breeze disrupts the gas shield. Use a gas flow of 812 L/min. For a butt joint on 2 mm stainless steel, use 1.6 mm filler rod (ER308L) and 60 A. Pulse the foot pedal to control heat. A common mistake is dipping the tungsten into the weld poolstop, regrind, and restart. In Africa, TIG is often used for repair work because it is precise. Practice on flat plate first: run beads without filler, then add filler. Keep the tungsten clean and the metal shiny.

5. Weld Symbols and Joint Design

Weld symbols are a universal language on engineering drawings. In African fabrication shops, you must read them to build correctly. The basic symbol is a horizontal line with an arrow pointing to the joint. On the line, you place the weld symbol: a triangle for fillet, a V for butt, a square for square butt, and a circle for all-around. A flag on the arrow means field weld. A tail on the line gives the process (e.g., SMAW).

The size of a fillet weld is given on the left of the symbol (e.g., 6 mm). The length and pitch are on the right (e.g., 50-150 means 50 mm weld, 150 mm pitch). For a V-groove, the included angle is on the left (e.g., 60). The arrow side and other side are indicated by the symbol above or below the line. If the symbol is above, weld on the arrow side; below, weld on the other side. Both sides? Put symbols on both.

Joint design is about preparing the edges. For a butt joint on 6 mm plate, grind a 60 V-groove with a 2 mm root face and 2 mm root gap. For a T-joint, no bevel is needed for fillet welds. For a corner joint, you may need a 45 bevel. Always clean the joint to bare metal. Tack weld every 150200 mm to hold alignment. Use a square and tape measure. In Africa, many jobs are repaired, so you often copy existing joints. But for new work, follow the drawing. If no drawing, use a 60 V for butt joints and 6 mm fillets for T-joints. A common mistake is welding too smallcheck the throat thickness. A 6 mm fillet has a throat of 4.2 mm. Use a weld gauge to check.

6. Weld Inspection and Testing

Weld inspection ensures the joint is strong and safe. In Africa, many failures come from poor inspection. You can do basic tests yourself. First, visual inspection: look for cracks, porosity, undercut, overlap, and spatter. Use a magnifying glass and a weld gauge. Measure the leg length and throat. A 6 mm fillet should have a throat of at least 4.2 mm. Check for uniform ripple pattern and proper penetration.

For destructive testing, cut a sample and bend it. A root bend test: cut a 200 mm long piece, grind the weld flush, and bend it 180 around a 40 mm radius. If the weld cracks or tears, it fails. A nick break test: cut a notch in the weld, hit it with a hammer, and look at the fracture. Porosity or slag inclusion will show. These tests are done on test coupons, not on the actual job.

Non-destructive testing (NDT) includes dye penetrant (for surface cracks), magnetic particle (for surface and near-surface defects), ultrasonic (for internal defects), and radiographic (X-ray). In Africa, dye penetrant is cheap and common: clean the weld, spray red dye, wait 10 minutes, wipe, spray developer, and cracks show as red lines. Magnetic particle uses a yoke and iron powder. Ultrasonic requires a trained technician. For critical jobs like pressure vessels, hire a certified inspector.

Always document your inspection. Record date, welder, joint, and results. If a weld fails, cut it out and re-weld. Never cover up a defect. In many African markets, word of mouth is everythinggood welds bring more work. Practice inspection on your own welds. Keep a file of test results. Use a checklist: cracks? porosity? undercut? size? penetration? If all pass, the weld is good.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Welding and Joining for Steel Fabrication

This practical reference is written for steel fabrication workshops, artisan training centres, and small-to-medium enterprises (SMEs) across Africa that want to win and deliver profitable welding contracts. Whether you are fabricating gates, roof trusses, water tanks, trailers, or structural steel for a commercial building, the welding process you choose and how you control it determines whether your product passes inspection, satisfies the client, and earns repeat business. This module covers the practical essentials of SMAW, GMAW, FCAW, GTAW, weld symbols and joint design, and weld inspection and testing. It is designed as a shop-floor companion for apprentices, journeymen, and workshop owners who need to make informed technical and commercial decisions.

Across Africa, welding is one of the most in-demand metal trades. Rapid urbanisation, infrastructure development, mining, agriculture, and oil and gas projects all depend on fabricated steel. Yet many workshops lose money because of rework, rejected welds, or unsafe practices. This reference helps you avoid those losses by getting the fundamentals right the first time.

1. Shielded Metal Arc Welding (SMAW)

SMAW, commonly called "stick welding," is the most widely used process in African fabrication workshops. It requires only a welding machine, electrode holder, earth clamp, and coated electrodes. It is forgiving of wind, rust, and dirt, which makes it ideal for site work and rural locations where power and gas supply may be unreliable. Typical electrodes include E6013 (general purpose), E7018 (low-hydrogen, structural), and E6011 (deep penetration, dirty steel).

Business tip: SMAW has low equipment cost but higher labour and consumable cost per metre. Use it for short runs, repairs, and site work. For long production runs, GMAW or FCAW will reduce your cost per joint.

2. Gas Metal Arc Welding (GMAW)

GMAW (MIG/MAG) uses a continuous solid wire electrode and a shielding gas. It offers high deposition rates and is easy to learn, making it popular in production shops fabricating gates, frames, and furniture. In Africa, CO is the most common shielding gas because it is cheaper than argon mixes, though argon/CO mixes give smoother arcs on thin material.

Business tip: GMAW requires clean material and good gas supply. Invest in a reliable regulator and keep nozzles clean. It is best for indoor, high-volume work.

3. Flux-Cored Arc Welding (FCAW)

FCAW uses a tubular wire with a flux core, sometimes with additional shielding gas (dual-shield) or self-shielded. It combines the productivity of GMAW with the portability and wind resistance of SMAW. It is excellent for outdoor structural work, heavy plate, and repair of earthmoving and agricultural equipment.

Business tip: Self-shielded FCAW is ideal for remote sites, farms, and mines where gas cylinders are impractical. It is a strong differentiator for workshops serving the agricultural and mining sectors.

4. Gas Tungsten Arc Welding (GTAW)

GTAW (TIG) uses a non-consumable tungsten electrode and an inert gas (usually argon). It produces the highest quality welds and is essential for stainless steel, aluminium, and thin materials. It is slower and requires more skill, so it is used for high-value work such as food-processing equipment, dairy tanks, and architectural stainless steel.

Business tip: GTAW is a premium service. If you can TIG weld stainless steel hygienically, you can charge higher rates for dairy, brewery, and pharmaceutical fabrication work.

5. Weld Symbols and Joint Design

Understanding weld symbols and joint design is essential for reading drawings and communicating with clients, engineers, and inspectors. The five basic joints are butt, lap, T, corner, and edge. Weld symbols on drawings tell you the type, size, length, and location of the weld. Misreading a symbol leads to rework, which destroys profit margins.

Business tip: Always confirm the drawing revision before cutting material. A single misunderstood symbol can waste thousands of shillings or naira in steel and labour.

6. Weld Inspection and Testing

Inspection protects your business reputation. Common methods include visual inspection (VT), dye penetrant testing (PT), magnetic particle testing (MT), ultrasonic testing (UT), and radiographic testing (RT). For most fabrication work, visual inspection and a few destructive tests (nick-break, bend test) are sufficient. For structural and pressure work, third-party NDT may be required.

Business tip: Build a simple weld quality checklist and train one person as your internal inspector. Documenting your inspection gives clients confidence and helps you win larger contracts.

SAFETY WARNING ARC WELDING HAZARDS

Arc welding exposes operators to serious hazards. In African workshops, the most common causes of injury and long-term disability are:

  • Arc eye (photokeratitis): Never weld without a proper welding helmet with the correct shade lens (Shade 1013 for SMAW/FCAW; Shade 913 for GMAW; Shade 1013 for GTAW). Even a brief flash can cause painful eye damage.
  • Fumes and gases: Weld in a well-ventilated area or use fume extraction. Manganese and hexavalent chromium fumes from stainless steel are toxic. Never weld galvanised steel without respiratory protection.
  • Electric shock: Work with dry gloves, dry boots, and an insulated electrode holder. Never weld in wet conditions. Ensure the machine is properly earthed.
  • Fire and burns: Keep a fire extinguisher and sand bucket nearby. Remove flammable materials within 10 metres. Wear leather apron, gloves, and safety boots.
  • Gas cylinder safety: Store cylinders upright, secured, and away from heat. Use flashback arrestors on oxy-fuel sets.

Employer responsibility: Provide PPE, train workers, and enforce safety rules. A serious accident can shut down your business and destroy lives.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Confirm the drawing and weld symbols before cutting or welding. Check revision number and dimensions.
  2. Prepare the joint correctly: clean rust, oil, paint, and moisture; set correct root gap and bevel angle.
  3. Select the right process and parameters for the material thickness, position, and service conditions.
  4. Use dry, correct electrodes or filler wire and store low-hydrogen electrodes in a heated quiver.
  5. Wear full PPE: welding helmet with correct shade, leather gloves, apron, safety boots, and respiratory protection when needed.
  6. Weld with correct technique: travel angle, arc length, travel speed, and current setting.
  7. Inspect every weld visually and remove slag between passes. Document any defects and rework immediately.
  8. Test critical welds using appropriate NDT or destructive methods before delivering to the client.
