1. Pearson square and algebraic methods for two-ingredient mixes

The Pearson square is a simple tool to mix two ingredients to hit a target nutrient level, like protein. Suppose you want a 16% crude protein (CP) mix using maize (9% CP) and soybean meal (44% CP). Draw a square: put 16 in the centre. Top left: 9 (maize). Bottom left: 44 (soybean meal). Difference across diagonals: 44 - 16 = 28 parts maize; 16 - 9 = 7 parts soybean meal. Total parts = 35. So maize is 28/35 = 80%, soybean meal is 7/35 = 20%. For 100 kg feed: 80 kg maize, 20 kg soybean meal. This gives 0.8*9 + 0.2*44 = 7.2 + 8.8 = 16% CP. Always check your math.

For three or more ingredients, use algebra. Let x = kg of maize, y = kg of soybean meal, z = kg of limestone. You need two equations: x + y + z = 100 (total weight) and 0.09x + 0.44y + 0z = 16 (protein). But you have three unknowns and two equations; you need a third constraint, like fixing limestone at 2 kg. Then x + y = 98 and 0.09x + 0.44y = 16. Solve: from first, x = 98 - y. Substitute: 0.09(98 - y) + 0.44y = 16 8.82 - 0.09y + 0.44y = 16 0.35y = 7.18 y = 20.51 kg. Then x = 77.49 kg. So mix: 77.49 kg maize, 20.51 kg soybean meal, 2 kg limestone. Check protein: 0.09*77.49 + 0.44*20.51 = 6.97 + 9.02 = 15.99% 16%.

In Africa, common ingredients are maize, sorghum, cottonseed cake, sunflower cake, and urea. Always use locally available feeds. For example, in Kenya, use maize and cottonseed cake (36% CP) to make 14% CP: Pearson square: 14 centre, 9 top left (maize), 36 bottom left. Differences: 36-14=22 parts maize, 14-9=5 parts cottonseed cake. Total 27 parts. Maize = 22/27 = 81.5%, cottonseed = 5/27 = 18.5%. For 50 kg: 40.75 kg maize, 9.25 kg cottonseed cake. Thats a practical mix for growing steers.

Remember: Pearson square only works for two ingredients and one nutrient. For more nutrients (energy, fibre, minerals), use simultaneous equations or linear programming. Always weigh ingredients accurately using a scale, not volume. Mix thoroughly. Store in dry place.

2. Simultaneous equations for multi-ingredient formulations

When you have more than two ingredients, you need simultaneous equations. Suppose you want 100 kg of feed with 16% CP and 2.5% fat, using maize (9% CP, 4% fat), soybean meal (44% CP, 1% fat), and fishmeal (60% CP, 8% fat). Let x = kg maize, y = kg soybean meal, z = kg fishmeal. You have three equations: x + y + z = 100 (total weight); 0.09x + 0.44y + 0.60z = 16 (protein); 0.04x + 0.01y + 0.08z = 2.5 (fat). Solve by substitution or elimination. From first: x = 100 - y - z. Substitute into protein: 0.09(100 - y - z) + 0.44y + 0.60z = 16 9 - 0.09y - 0.09z + 0.44y + 0.60z = 16 0.35y + 0.51z = 7. Substitute into fat: 0.04(100 - y - z) + 0.01y + 0.08z = 2.5 4 - 0.04y - 0.04z + 0.01y + 0.08z = 2.5 -0.03y + 0.04z = -1.5 0.03y - 0.04z = 1.5. Now solve: from protein, y = (7 - 0.51z)/0.35. Plug into fat: 0.03*(7 - 0.51z)/0.35 - 0.04z = 1.5 (0.21 - 0.0153z)/0.35 - 0.04z = 1.5 0.6 - 0.0437z - 0.04z = 1.5 -0.0837z = 0.9 z = -10.75. Negative, impossible. So adjust targets: reduce fat to 2% or add a fourth ingredient like limestone (0% CP, 0% fat). This shows you can't always hit exact targets with few ingredients.

In practice, use more ingredients to give flexibility. For example, add wheat bran (15% CP, 3% fat) and limestone. Then you have more variables than equations, so infinite solutions. You then choose the cheapest mix or use linear programming. For manual work, fix two ingredients and solve for two others. For instance, fix 2 kg limestone and 5 kg fishmeal, then solve for maize and soybean meal to meet 16% CP in 100 kg: x + y = 93; 0.09x + 0.44y + 0.60*5 = 16 0.09x + 0.44y = 13 solve: from x = 93 - y, 0.09(93 - y) + 0.44y = 13 8.37 - 0.09y + 0.44y = 13 0.35y = 4.63 y = 13.23 kg, x = 79.77 kg. So mix: 79.77 kg maize, 13.23 kg soybean meal, 5 kg fishmeal, 2 kg limestone. Check protein: 0.09*79.77 + 0.44*13.23 + 0.60*5 = 7.18 + 5.82 + 3 = 16%. Good.

Always check that all ingredients sum to 100 kg and nutrients meet targets. Use a calculator or spreadsheet. In Africa, fishmeal may be expensive; replace with cottonseed cake or sunflower cake. Adjust equations accordingly.

3. Linear programming concepts for least-cost formulation

Linear programming (LP) is a mathematical method to find the cheapest mix of ingredients that meets nutrient requirements. You need: (1) a list of available ingredients with their nutrient composition and cost per kg; (2) nutrient requirements (e.g., 16% CP, 2.5% fat, 0.8% calcium); (3) limits on ingredients (e.g., maize max 70%, fishmeal max 5%). The LP solver then calculates the exact amounts of each ingredient to minimize total cost while meeting all constraints.

For example, you have maize (9% CP, cost $0.30/kg), soybean meal (44% CP, $0.60/kg), fishmeal (60% CP, $1.20/kg), limestone (0% CP, $0.10/kg). You want 100 kg with at least 16% CP and at least 0.8% calcium. Limestone is 38% calcium. You also limit fishmeal to 5 kg max. The LP might choose: maize 62 kg, soybean meal 30 kg, fishmeal 5 kg, limestone 3 kg. Cost = 62*0.30 + 30*0.60 + 5*1.20 + 3*0.10 = 18.6 + 18 + 6 + 0.3 = $42.90. Protein: 0.09*62 + 0.44*30 + 0.60*5 + 0*3 = 5.58 + 13.2 + 3 = 21.78% (exceeds 16%, but that's okay if cost is minimized? Actually LP minimizes cost, so it might use less protein if cheaper. But if protein is a minimum constraint, it will meet exactly or exceed if cheaper. Here, it might reduce soybean meal and add more maize if protein allows? But protein min is 16, so it could use less soybean meal. Let's recalc: if maize 70 kg, soybean 26 kg, fishmeal 4 kg, limestone 3 kg: protein = 6.3 + 11.44 + 2.4 = 20.14% still high. To get exactly 16, need less protein. Actually, LP would find cheapest combination that meets 16% CP. Since maize is cheaper, it will use as much maize as possible but protein constraint limits maize. So it will use maize up to the point where protein from other ingredients meets 16. So the solution above might not be optimal. The LP solver does this automatically.

In Africa, you can use free software like LP Solver in Excel (Solver add-in) or online tools. For small-scale, you can use the Pearson square for two ingredients, but LP is better for many ingredients. Start by listing all ingredients and their costs. Then set constraints. The LP will give you the least-cost formula. Always check that the formula is practical: does it mix well? Are ingredients available? Adjust limits accordingly.

Remember: LP only works if you have accurate nutrient and cost data. Update regularly. Use local ingredients to reduce cost.

4. Using spreadsheet software for feed formulation

Spreadsheet software like Microsoft Excel or Google Sheets is a powerful tool for feed formulation. You can set up a simple linear programming model using the Solver add-in. First, create a table with ingredients in rows and columns for cost per kg, nutrient composition (CP, fat, fibre, calcium, etc.), and maximum/minimum limits. For example, list maize, soybean meal, fishmeal, limestone, and salt. In separate cells, enter the target nutrient levels for 100 kg of feed.

Next, create a column for the amount of each ingredient (in kg). Initially, put any number (e.g., 10 kg each). Then create formulas to calculate total weight (sum of all ingredient amounts), total cost (sum of amount * cost), and total nutrients (sum of amount * nutrient % for each nutrient). For instance, protein = SUMPRODUCT(amount_range, protein_range). Then use Solver: set objective to minimize total cost, by changing ingredient amounts. Add constraints: total weight = 100; total protein >= 16; total fat >= 2.5; total calcium >= 0.8; each ingredient amount >= 0 and <= its limit. Solver will find the optimal amounts.

For example, in Excel: A1:A5 ingredients, B1:B5 cost, C1:C5 protein %, D1:D5 amount (initially 10). E1 = SUM(D1:D5) total weight. F1 = SUMPRODUCT(B1:B5,D1:D5) total cost. G1 = SUMPRODUCT(C1:C5,D1:D5)/E1 protein %. Then Solver: Set Objective F1 to Min, By Changing D1:D5, Subject to: E1 = 100, G1 >= 0.16, D1:D5 >= 0, D1:D5 <= limits. Run Solver. It will adjust amounts. For instance, it might give maize 65 kg, soybean meal 25 kg, fishmeal 5 kg, limestone 3 kg, salt 2 kg. Cost $40.50. Protein 16.2%.

In Africa, you can use free Google Sheets with Solver add-on (OpenSolver). Or use Excel on a computer. If no computer, use a calculator and the algebraic methods. But spreadsheet saves time and reduces errors. Practice with simple examples. Start with two ingredients, then add more. Always verify results manually. Save your spreadsheet as a template for future use. Adjust ingredient costs and nutrient values as they change. This is a key skill for commercial feed production.

5. Setting nutrient constraints and ingredient limits

Nutrient constraints are the minimum or maximum levels of nutrients your feed must meet. For cattle and steers, key nutrients are crude protein (CP), total digestible nutrients (TDN), fibre (crude fibre), fat, calcium, phosphorus, and sometimes vitamins and minerals. For example, a growing steer may need 12-16% CP, 60-70% TDN, and 0.5-0.8% calcium. You set these as constraints in your formulation. If you are using Pearson square or algebra, you target one nutrient at a time. With linear programming, you set all constraints simultaneously.

Ingredient limits are the maximum or minimum amounts of each ingredient you can use. For example, you might limit maize to 70% because too much maize can cause acidosis. Limit fishmeal to 5% due to cost and taste. Limit cottonseed cake to 20% because of gossypol toxicity. Limit urea to 1% for safety. These limits are based on nutritional knowledge and practical experience. In Africa, common limits: maize max 70%, soybean meal max 30%, fishmeal max 10%, limestone max 3%, salt max 0.5%. Always check local recommendations.

To set constraints in a spreadsheet, you add rows for each nutrient with target values and rows for each ingredient with min/max. For example, in Excel, you have a cell for protein target (e.g., 16). Then in Solver, you add constraint that protein cell >= 16. For ingredient limits, you add constraints that each amount cell <= max limit and >= min limit (often 0). You can also set a minimum for some ingredients, like at least 2% limestone to provide calcium.

When constraints are too tight, no solution exists. For example, if you require 20% CP but only have maize and limestone, impossible. So you must have enough protein sources. Also, if you set maximum limits too low, you may not meet nutrient needs. So balance. Start with realistic targets based on animal requirements. Use tables from FAO or local extension services. For steers in Africa, a typical feedlot diet might be 70% maize, 15% cottonseed cake, 10% wheat bran, 3% molasses, 2% limestone and salt. That gives about 14% CP. Adjust as needed.

Always test your formulated feed with animals if possible. Observe weight gain and health. Adjust constraints accordingly. Keep records.

6. Evaluating and adjusting formulated diets

After you formulate a diet, you must evaluate it. First, check that all nutrients meet the requirements. Calculate the actual nutrient levels from the ingredient amounts. For example, if your mix has 65 kg maize (9% CP), 20 kg soybean meal (44% CP), 10 kg wheat bran (15% CP), 3 kg limestone (0% CP), 2 kg salt (0% CP), then total CP = 0.09*65 + 0.44*20 + 0.15*10 = 5.85 + 8.8 + 1.5 = 16.15%. That meets 16% target. Check other nutrients: fat, fibre, calcium, phosphorus. Use a feed composition table.

Second, evaluate cost. Calculate cost per kg of feed. Compare with alternative formulas. If cost is too high, adjust ingredients. For example, replace part of soybean meal with cottonseed cake (36% CP) if cheaper. But watch for gossypol; limit cottonseed cake to 20%. Use linear programming to find least-cost. If no computer, try manual adjustments: reduce expensive ingredients and increase cheaper ones, then recalculate nutrients.

Third, evaluate practicality. Can you mix the ingredients easily? Are they available locally? For example, fishmeal may be scarce in inland areas. Use local protein sources like groundnut cake, sunflower cake, or brewers grains. Check if the feed is palatable. Cattle may refuse feed with too much urea or fishmeal. Start with small amounts and observe.

Fourth, test with animals. Feed the formulated diet to a few steers for 2-4 weeks. Monitor feed intake, weight gain, and health. If weight gain is below target, increase energy or protein. If animals have diarrhoea, reduce maize or add fibre. If they are off-feed, check for toxins or poor mixing. Adjust the formula accordingly. Keep records of feed ingredients, costs, and animal performance.

Finally, remember that formulation is iterative. What works in one season may not work in another due to ingredient availability and price changes. Re-evaluate regularly. Use simple tools like Pearson square for quick adjustments. For example, if maize price rises, you might switch to sorghum or cassava. Always aim for the cheapest balanced diet that meets animal needs. In Africa, using locally available by-products can reduce cost significantly. Share your formulas with other farmers for feedback.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Building, Checking and Adjusting Cattle & Steer Rations

A feed formulation is only as good as its weakest assumption. In African beef and dairy operations, where maize bran, cottonseed cake, molasses, urea, and locally milled by-products vary batch to batch, the difference between a profitable ration and a losing one is often 23 percentage points of crude protein (CP) or a single misunderstood dry matter (DM) figure. This Practical Reference gives you the working numbers, the checks, and the adjustment rules that turn a formulation on paper into kilos of gain and litres of milk on the ground.

Treat every formulation as a business decision: the cheapest ration that meets the animal's nutrient needs and the mill's physical limits is the target not the "best" ration in a textbook. Least-cost thinking is the core commercial discipline of feed formulation.

1. The Two-Ingredient Workhorse: Pearson Square in Practice

The Pearson Square remains the fastest field method for blending two feeds to hit a target nutrient level (usually CP, sometimes energy or a mineral). It works only with two nutrient-bearing ingredients plus a diluent or concentrate, and it is only as accurate as your feed analysis.

Worked example (Zimbabwe / Zambia style): You want a 16% CP concentrate from cottonseed cake (CSC, 36% CP) and maize bran (10% CP).

  • Target in the centre: 16
  • Difference across: 36 16 = 20 parts maize bran; 16 10 = 6 parts CSC
  • Total parts = 26. CSC share = 6 26 = 23.1%; maize bran = 20 26 = 76.9%

Check: (0.231 36) + (0.769 10) = 8.3 + 7.7 = 16.0% CP. Correct.

2. Simultaneous Equations for Multi-Ingredient Mixes

When you need to hit two nutrient targets at once typically CP and energy (TDN or ME) with two ingredients, use simultaneous equations. With three or more ingredients and multiple constraints, move directly to linear programming (Section 3).

Example: Blend maize grain (ME 13.8 MJ/kg DM, CP 9%) and soybean meal (ME 13.2 MJ/kg DM, CP 44%) to reach 14% CP and at least 13.5 MJ/kg ME in a 100 kg batch.

  • Let x = kg maize, y = kg soybean meal. x + y = 100
  • 0.09x + 0.44y = 14 (CP equation)
  • Substitute x = 100 y: 0.09(100 y) + 0.44y = 14 9 + 0.35y = 14 y = 14.3 kg soybean meal; x = 85.7 kg maize
  • ME check: (0.857 13.8) + (0.143 13.2) = 11.83 + 1.89 = 13.72 MJ/kg

In practice, add a mineralvitamin premix at 0.51.0% and a rumen buffer (e.g., 0.5% sodium bicarbonate) on top of the energyprotein blend, then re-check that the premix dilution has not pulled CP below target.

4. Linear Programming and Spreadsheet Formulation

Least-cost linear programming (LP) minimises total ingredient cost subject to constraints. In Excel, use Solver (Data Solver) with:

  • Decision variables: kg of each ingredient in the mix
  • Objective: minimise SUMPRODUCT(quantity, price per kg)
  • Constraints: CP target, ME target, Ca:P ratio between 1.5:1 and 2.5:1, fibre maximum, ingredient inclusion limits (e.g., urea 1% of DM, molasses 10%, CSC 20% for monogastric-sensitive or gossypol-sensitive animals)
  • Non-negativity: all quantities 0

For multi-ingredient problems, LP will always find the cheapest feasible mix but it will exploit every loose constraint. Tighten constraints deliberately, not by accident.

SAFETY WARNING Urea and Gossypol

Urea: Never exceed 1% of total dry matter intake, and never feed urea to non-ruminants or to starving animals. Introduce over 710 days. Urea toxicity kills within hours symptoms include bloat, staggering, and rapid breathing. Always mix thoroughly and provide free access to clean water.

Cottonseed cake: Limit to 20% of the concentrate for growing cattle and 10% for breeding bulls, due to gossypol. Do not feed to pigs or poultry. Store dry mouldy CSC risks aflatoxin, which passes into milk.

6. Evaluating and Adjusting Formulated Diets

After formulation, run a four-point check before committing to a mill run:

  1. Nutrient check: Recalculate CP, ME, Ca, P and compare against Table 2. Allow 5% tolerance.
  2. Physical check: Bulk density, palatability, mixing uniformity, and moisture (target <12% for stored meal).
  3. Economic check: Cost per kg DM and cost per kg gain or per litre of milk. If cost per kg gain exceeds the local liveweight price, reformulate.
  4. Animal response check: Monitor intake, body condition score, and dung consistency for 14 days. Adjust by 1 percentage point CP or 2% TDN at a time.

Adjustment rules of thumb:

  • Low intake check mould, urea level, and fibre; add 35% molasses for palatability.
  • Poor gain raise TDN by 23 points before raising CP; energy is usually the first limiting factor in African rations.
  • Loose dung reduce urea or increase fibre (hay/silage).
  • Milk fat drop increase effective fibre, reduce rapidly fermentable starch.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Use local laboratory or near-infrared (NIR) feed analysis never rely on book values alone.
  • Pearson Square only for two-ingredient blends; switch to LP for three or more.
  • Always check Ca:P ratio (1.5:1 to 2.5:1) deficiencies cause poor growth and milk fever.
  • Cap urea at 1% DM; cap CSC at 20% (10% for bulls); never feed urea to non-ruminants.
  • Include a mineralvitamin premix at 0.51.0% of the mix.
  • Calculate cost per kg gain or per litre of milk not just cost per tonne of feed.
  • Run a 14-day animal response trial before scaling up.
  • Record every batch: ingredients, prices, analysis, and animal performance.
