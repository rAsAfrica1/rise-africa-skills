1. Determining Ripeness and Harvest Timing

Tomatoes do not ripen all at once. You must check your field every day once the first fruits start to change colour. For fresh market, harvest when tomatoes are 50-70% red or pink. For transport to distant markets, pick at the 'breaker' stage, when the fruit just shows a hint of yellow or pink. For processing or local sales, wait until fully red but still firm.

Use the calendar and your eyes. Count days from transplanting: most varieties are ready 60-90 days after transplanting. But weather changes this. In hot, dry areas like northern Nigeria or Senegal, tomatoes ripen faster. In cooler highlands like Kenya's Rift Valley, they take longer. Walk your rows every morning. Look for colour change on the bottom of the fruit first. Gently squeeze a few fruits. A ripe tomato gives slightly to thumb pressure but springs back. A soft, mushy tomato is overripe and will rot quickly.

Check sugar content if you want top price. A simple refractometer (handheld, about $20) measures Brix. For fresh market, aim for 4-6 Brix. For processing, 8-10 Brix. But most smallholders rely on taste and feel. Taste a tomato from each block. If it is sweet and juicy, harvest that block.

Harvest in the cool of the day: early morning or late afternoon. Never harvest in the heat of midday. Hot fruits respire faster and bruise easily. If you pick for a distant market, pick slightly underripe. For a nearby market or home use, pick fully ripe. Always harvest before heavy rain, because wet fruits crack and grow mould. If rain is coming, pick early and store in a shaded, ventilated shed.

Finally, plan your harvest over several days. Do not strip the whole field at once. Pick only ripe and breaker fruits, leaving green ones to grow. This spreads your labour and your sales. It also reduces waste. A good rule: harvest every 2-3 days for fresh market, every 5-7 days for processing.

2. Harvesting Techniques to Avoid Damage

Tomatoes bruise easily. A bruise is a wound that invites rot. So handle every fruit like an egg. Use your thumb and forefinger to gently twist the fruit until the stem separates from the vine. Do not pull hard. If the stem stays attached, use a small pair of sharp scissors or pruning shears to cut the stem, leaving a 1 cm stub. Never yank the vine or shake the plant. That damages other fruits and the plant itself.

Harvest into clean, smooth containers. Do not use rough sacks or baskets with sharp edges. Plastic crates (like the 20 kg ones used for bananas) are best. If you use baskets, line them with soft cloth or newspaper. Never overfill a container. Fill only to 2/3 capacity so fruits do not crush each other. A full 20 kg crate of tomatoes can bruise the bottom layer under its own weight. So use shallow crates (15-20 cm deep) or stack no more than 3 layers of fruit.

Keep harvested fruits in the shade immediately. A tomato in direct sun at 35C can lose 5% of its weight in 2 hours. That is money lost. Use a shade tree, a simple thatched roof, or a white tarpaulin stretched over a frame. Never leave crates on hot ground or in a closed vehicle. Air must move around the fruits.

Do not wash tomatoes right after harvest unless you are selling within 24 hours. Washing removes the natural waxy bloom that protects the fruit. If you must wash, use clean water with a little chlorine (1 teaspoon of bleach per 10 litres of water), then dry in the shade. Never soak tomatoes. Soaking spreads disease from one fruit to all.

Remove any damaged, cracked, or diseased fruits as you pick. Put them in a separate bucket. Do not mix them with good fruits. One rotten tomato can spoil a whole crate in a day. Finally, train your workers. Show them how to twist, not pull. Pay by the crate, not by the hour, but check for bruises. A good picker fills a crate with zero bruised fruits.

3. Grading and Sorting for Market

Grading means separating tomatoes by size, colour, and quality. Sorting means removing bad ones. Both add value. A graded crate of uniform, red, firm tomatoes can fetch 30-50% more than a mixed crate. Buyers pay for consistency.

Start by sorting out rejects. Remove any fruit that is cracked, bruised, soft, green (unless you sell green tomatoes), diseased, or has insect holes. These go to processing, animal feed, or compost. Never sell them as fresh. Then grade the good fruits into three classes. Class 1: fully red, firm, uniform size, no blemishes. Class 2: slightly uneven colour or shape, but still firm and edible. Class 3: ripe but soft, or with minor blemishes, for local market or processing.

Use simple tools. A wooden or plastic sizing ring (a circle with a diameter of 4 cm for small, 5 cm for medium, 6 cm for large) helps you sort by size. Or use your hand: small tomatoes fit in a closed fist, medium in an open palm, large beyond. For colour, use a colour chart from your extension officer, or just your eyes. In Africa, many buyers prefer deep red, but some markets want pink or orange. Ask your buyer.

Grade into separate crates. Label each crate with class and date. Keep crates in the shade. Do not mix classes. A crate of Class 1 tomatoes should have no more than 5% defects. If you find more, re-sort.

Grading also helps you plan sales. Sell Class 1 to supermarkets, hotels, or exporters. Sell Class 2 to local markets and roadside vendors. Send Class 3 to processors or make sauce. This way, nothing is wasted. You can also grade by ripeness: pick breaker tomatoes for long-distance transport, and fully ripe ones for same-day sale. Always grade in a cool, shaded area. Never grade in the sun. A simple grading table under a tree works well. Train your workers to grade consistently. One bad crate can ruin your reputation.

4. Packaging and Storage Best Practices

Packaging protects tomatoes from bruising, dirt, and moisture loss. Use clean, ventilated plastic crates (20 kg or 10 kg). Wooden crates can splinter and damage fruits. If you use cartons, make sure they have holes for air. Never use closed plastic bags or sacks. Tomatoes need to breathe. Line crates with newspaper or soft leaves to cushion fruits. Do not line with plastic.

Pack in layers. Put stem-side down to avoid puncturing neighbours. Do not pack more than 3 layers deep. For long-distance transport, use smaller crates (10 kg) to reduce crushing. Fill crates to the top but do not press down. Leave 2 cm of space for air movement. Label each crate with your farm name, grade, and harvest date. This builds trust with buyers.

Storage: tomatoes are best stored at 12-15C and 85-90% relative humidity. In most African farms, you do not have a cold room. But you can build a simple evaporative cooler: a wooden frame covered with wet burlap or sand. Air blowing through the wet cloth cools the inside by 5-10C. Or use a shaded, well-ventilated room with a damp floor. Never store tomatoes in a closed, hot room. At 30C, tomatoes ripen twice as fast as at 15C.

For short storage (1-2 weeks), keep fruits in a cool, dark place. Check daily for rotten fruits and remove them. Do not store tomatoes with onions, bananas, or avocados. These produce ethylene gas, which speeds ripening and spoilage. Store tomatoes alone.

For longer storage (3-4 weeks), pick breaker fruits, pack in crates, and keep at 12C. If you have no cooler, harvest only what you can sell in 3 days. Never store tomatoes in the sun or in a hot car. If you must transport, do it at night or early morning. Use a white reflective tarpaulin over the load. Finally, keep records: date picked, date sold, weight, and price. This helps you plan better next season.

5. Post-Harvest Diseases and Disorders

Even with good handling, tomatoes can rot after harvest. The main diseases are: bacterial soft rot (wet, smelly, mushy spots), fungal rots like early blight and late blight (brown or black lesions with white mould), and anthracnose (sunken, dark circles). These come from wounds, dirty containers, and high humidity. The main disorders are: chilling injury (brown spots and watery flesh after storage below 10C), sunscald (white, papery patches from sun exposure), and cracking (from uneven watering).

To prevent diseases, start in the field. Spray copper-based fungicide every 7-10 days during wet weather. Remove diseased fruits and plants immediately. Do not compost them near the field. At harvest, use clean crates. Wash crates with 10% bleach solution and dry them in the sun. Never put tomatoes on the ground. Use a tarpaulin or clean table.

After harvest, keep fruits dry. Do not wash unless necessary. If you wash, use chlorinated water (1 teaspoon bleach per 10 litres) and dry in shade. Store at 12-15C. Do not refrigerate below 10C. If you see one rotten fruit, remove it at once. One soft rot fruit can release enzymes that rot the whole crate in 24 hours.

For chilling injury, never store tomatoes in a fridge set below 10C. If you have no choice, wrap them in newspaper and put in the warmest part of the fridge. For sunscald, pick fruits before they get too much sun. Provide shade. For cracking, water evenly. Do not let the soil dry out completely and then flood it. Mulch helps.

If you sell to a market, ask the buyer about their storage. Many buyers keep tomatoes in hot, closed rooms. Educate them. A simple shade net and a wet floor can cut losses by half. Finally, keep a notebook. Record every disease you see, the weather, and what you did. This helps you predict and prevent next time.

6. Value-Added Products: Sauces, Drying, and Canning

When you have too many tomatoes, or when prices are low, turn them into value-added products. You can double or triple your income. Three easy products: tomato sauce, dried tomatoes, and canned tomatoes.

Tomato sauce: Wash and chop 10 kg ripe tomatoes. Remove stems and bad spots. Blend or crush. Add 1 kg onions, 100 g garlic, 200 g ginger, 500 ml oil, 200 g salt, 100 g sugar, and spices (chilli, curry, black pepper). Simmer in a large pot for 1-2 hours until thick. Stir often to prevent burning. Pour hot sauce into clean glass bottles or jars. Seal and boil the filled bottles in water for 20 minutes to sterilise. Cool and label. Sauce keeps for 6-12 months in a cool, dark place. Sell for 3-5 times the price of fresh tomatoes.

Dried tomatoes: Use firm, ripe tomatoes. Wash, remove stems, and cut into 5 mm slices or halves. Remove seeds if you like. Spread on clean trays or drying racks. Sun-dry for 3-5 days, turning daily. Cover with mesh to keep flies off. Or use a solar dryer (a wooden box with a glass top and vents). Dry until leathery, not brittle. Pack in clean, dry jars or plastic bags. Store in a cool, dark place. Dried tomatoes keep for 6-12 months. Add to soups, stews, or sell as a snack.

Canned tomatoes: Use fully ripe, firm tomatoes. Wash, blanch in boiling water for 30 seconds, then peel. Pack whole or crushed into clean glass jars. Add 1/2 teaspoon salt per jar. Fill with hot tomato juice or water, leaving 1 cm headspace. Seal and process in boiling water for 40 minutes. Cool, check seals, and label. Canned tomatoes keep for 12-18 months.

Always use clean equipment and sterilised jars. Work in a clean, shaded area. Label with product name and date. Sell at local markets, hotels, or online. With 10 kg of tomatoes (worth $5 fresh), you can make 5 litres of sauce worth $25. That is real profit.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Harvesting and Post-Harvest Handling for Tomato Profitability

In tomato farming, the margin between profit and loss is often decided after the fruit has coloured. A grower may produce 30 tonnes per hectare, but if 25% is lost to bruising, sunscald, or premature rot, the effective yield drops to 22.5 tonnes. Across sub-Saharan Africa, post-harvest losses for tomatoes routinely range between 30% and 50%, according to FAO and regional horticultural studies. For an agribusiness, every percentage point recovered is direct revenue. This practical reference consolidates the core decisions: when to pick, how to pick, how to grade, how to pack, and how to add value when fresh market prices collapse.

Determining Ripeness and Harvest Timing

Tomatoes are climacteric fruit they continue to ripen after harvest. This single biological fact drives every harvest decision. For distant urban markets (Nairobi, Lagos, Accra, Johannesburg), pick at mature green or breaker stage. For nearby markets or direct-to-consumer sales, pick at pink to light red. For processing into sauce or paste, pick fully red ripe for maximum sugar and lycopene content.

Harvest timing within the day matters. Pick in the early morning after dew has dried, or late afternoon. Never harvest during peak heat (11:0015:00) fruit temperature is high, respiration is rapid, and field heat accelerates deterioration. The ideal fruit temperature at harvest is below 25C.

Harvesting Techniques to Avoid Damage

Tomato skins bruise easily. A bruise that looks minor at harvest becomes a sunken brown lesion within 48 hours and a disease entry point within 72 hours. Key rules:

  • Use both hands: cup the fruit and lift with a slight twist; never pull. Pulling tears the stem and creates a wound.
  • Leave the calyx (green sepals) attached for fresh market it signals freshness and reduces water loss.
  • Harvest into shallow crates (maximum 23 fruit deep), never into buckets, sacks, or gunny bags. Depth creates compression bruises at the bottom.
  • Never drop fruit into containers. Place gently.
  • Keep harvested fruit in shade immediately. A simple shade net or tree canopy reduces fruit temperature by 510C.
  • Harvest with clean hands and clean tools. Dirty crates carry fungal spores from previous harvests.

Grading and Sorting for Market

Grading is not cosmetic it is commercial. Buyers pay premiums for uniformity. Supermarkets, hotels, and processors specify grade standards. Sorting removes defective fruit before it contaminates healthy fruit in the same container.

Packaging and Storage Best Practices

Packaging protects fruit from compression, vibration, and contamination during transport. The standard for tomatoes is a ventilated plastic crate (2025 kg capacity) or a fibreboard carton with ventilation holes (1015 kg). Wooden crates are acceptable if smooth and clean. Never use polypropylene sacks they cause compression, restrict airflow, and trap heat.

SAFETY WARNING Pesticide Residue and Post-Harvest Handling

Never harvest tomatoes within the pre-harvest interval (PHI) stated on any pesticide label. Residues on harvested fruit can exceed maximum residue limits (MRLs), causing buyer rejection, export bans, and health risks. If you apply fungicide or insecticide, record the date and respect the PHI commonly 314 days depending on the product. When handling fruit after spraying, wear gloves and wash hands. For processing (sauce, canning), fruit must be washed in potable water and any fruit with visible mould discarded mouldy tomatoes can contain mycotoxins that survive cooking.

Post-Harvest Diseases and Disorders

The most common post-harvest problems in African tomato supply chains are:

  • Bacterial soft rot (Erwinia spp.): Water-soaked, foul-smelling lesions. Enters through wounds. Control: avoid bruising, keep fruit dry, sanitise crates.
  • Alternaria rot (Alternaria alternata): Dark brown sunken lesions at stem end. Control: harvest at correct maturity, avoid overhead irrigation before harvest.
  • Rhizopus rot: Soft, watery decay with white-black fuzzy mould. Spreads rapidly in warm, humid conditions. Control: rapid cooling, remove infected fruit immediately.
  • Grey mould (Botrytis cinerea): More common in cool, humid storage. Control: maintain proper ventilation, avoid condensation.
  • Sunscald: Not a disease but a disorder pale, blistered areas from direct sun exposure. Control: maintain leaf cover, harvest before peak heat.
  • Chilling injury: Pitting, water-soaked areas, failure to ripen. Caused by storing mature green tomatoes below 10C. Control: respect temperature minimums.
  • Blossom-end rot: Calcium deficiency disorder dark, sunken area at blossom end. Control: soil calcium management, consistent irrigation.

Value-Added Products: Sauces, Drying, and Canning

When fresh market prices fall below production cost common during peak harvest gluts processing converts loss into revenue. Three viable options for African agribusinesses:

Tomato sauce: Requires red ripe fruit, washing, chopping, cooking, sieving, and adding preservatives (salt, sugar, vinegar, or permitted preservatives). A small-scale operation can produce 100200 litres per day with basic equipment (pulper, cooking pot, bottles, capping machine). Shelf life: 1218 months if properly bottled and heat-treated.

Solar drying: Sliced tomatoes (68mm) dried in a solar dryer for 23 days to below 10% moisture. Dried tomato flakes or powder have a growing market in urban centres and can be stored for 612 months in airtight packaging. This is the lowest-capital value-addition option.

Canning: Requires higher capital (retort, cans, seamer) but produces the longest shelf life (2+ years) and access to institutional buyers (schools, army, hospitals). Best suited for cooperatives or established processors.

For all value-added products, food safety compliance is non-negotiable. In Kenya, KEBS certification is required for retail sale. In Nigeria, NAFDAC registration. In Ghana, FDA Ghana approval. Budget for these certifications before launching.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Harvest at the right stage for the right market mature green for long distance, pink/light red for local sale, red ripe for processing.
  2. Pick in the cool of the day early morning or late afternoon, never in peak heat.
  3. Use shallow, ventilated crates never sacks, buckets, or deep containers.
  4. Handle fruit like eggs two hands, gentle twist, no dropping, no squeezing.
  5. Grade and sort immediately after harvest remove all defective fruit before packing.
  6. Cool and shade within 30 minutes of harvest field heat is the enemy.
  7. Respect storage temperatures 1215C for green fruit, never below 10C.
  8. Keep records harvest date, grade, buyer, price, and losses. Data drives better decisions next season.
