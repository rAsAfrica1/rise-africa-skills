1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Welding codes and standards are the rulebooks that tell you how to make a weld that won't fail. In Africa, many welders learn by watching others, but without codes, you risk building a bridge that cracks, a pipeline that leaks, or a trailer that breaks on the highway. Codes like AWS D1.1 (structural steel), ASME Section IX (pressure vessels), and ISO 9606 (welder qualification) set minimum requirements for materials, procedures, and inspection. They exist because welds fail when they are too small, too cold, or made with the wrong filler. For example, a 6mm fillet weld on a 10mm plate may be fine for a gate, but for a crane support, AWS D1.1 might require an 8mm fillet. If you ignore that, the weld can tear under load. In many African countries, local authorities adopt international codes, but enforcement is weak. That means your reputation depends on you following the code even when no one checks. Knowing codes also helps you win contracts: mining companies, oil and gas firms, and government projects often demand certified welders who can pass tests like the 3G or 4G plate test. This module will teach you the basics of codes, how to read a welding procedure specification (WPS), and how to apply standards in your daily work. You don't need a library of booksjust a few key rules and the habit of checking before you strike an arc. By the end, you'll understand why a code-compliant weld is safer, stronger, and more profitable.

2. Tools and Materials

To work with welding codes, you need the right tools and documents. First, get a copy of the relevant code. For structural steel, AWS D1.1 is common; for pressure piping, ASME B31.3. In Africa, you can buy printed copies from engineering bookshops in cities like Johannesburg, Lagos, or Nairobi, or download PDFs from official sites (some are free for students). Next, you need a welding procedure specification (WPS) and procedure qualification record (PQR). These are documents that tell you the exact settings: amperage, voltage, travel speed, electrode type, and preheat. Your employer or client should provide them. If not, you can write your own using code templates. You also need measuring tools: a fillet weld gauge (to check leg length), a undercut gauge, and a magnifying glass for visual inspection. A digital thermometer or temperature crayons (e.g., Templistik) check preheat and interpass temperatures. For destructive tests, you need a hacksaw, grinding wheel, and bend tester. For non-destructive testing (NDT), you may hire a technician with dye penetrant or ultrasonic equipment. Common materials: mild steel plates (S275 or A36), electrodes like E7018 for low hydrogen, and filler wires like ER70S-6. Always check mill certificates for steel chemistry and strength. In Africa, counterfeit electrodes are commonbuy from reputable suppliers and look for the AWS classification printed on the electrode. Keep a logbook of your welds, including date, WPS number, and inspection results. This record proves you follow codes and helps you improve. Without these tools, you are guessing, and guessing leads to rejects.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps to weld according to a code. Step 1: Read the WPS. Identify the base metal, thickness, joint design, welding process (SMAW, MIG, TIG), filler metal, amperage range, voltage range, travel speed, preheat, and interpass temperature. For example, a WPS might say: 6mm fillet, E7018, 110-140 amps, 20-24 volts, 2-3 mm/s travel, preheat 100C. Step 2: Prepare the joint. Clean off rust, oil, and paint at least 25mm from the weld zone. Use a grinder if needed. Step 3: Set up your machine. Set amperage and voltage within the WPS range. Use a calibrated meter if possible. Step 4: Preheat if required. Use a torch or heating blanket. Check with a temperature crayonif it melts, you're at temperature. Step 5: Weld. Maintain the correct travel speed and electrode angle (usually 10-15 drag for SMAW). Keep arc length short. For multi-pass welds, clean slag between passes and control interpass temperature (max 250C for low hydrogen). Step 6: Inspect. After cooling, use a fillet gauge to check leg size. Look for undercut, porosity, cracks. If the code requires NDT, arrange it. Step 7: Record. Write down the WPS number, your name, date, and any repairs. If a weld fails inspection, you must repair it following a repair WPS. In Africa, many jobsites lack preheat ovens, so you can use a charcoal fire with a steel plate to heat the part evenlybut never exceed the max interpass. Always let the weld cool naturally unless the code says otherwise. This method works for any code if you adapt the numbers.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Using the wrong electrode. Many welders grab E6013 because it's easy, but for code work, low hydrogen E7018 is often required. Fix: Check the WPS. If it says E7018, use it, and store it in a heated rod oven (or a sealed container with silica gel). Mistake 2: Ignoring preheat. On thick steel (over 25mm), cold welding causes cracking. Fix: Preheat to 100-150C using a torch or oven. Test with a temperature crayon. Mistake 3: Too fast travel. This causes narrow, convex beads and lack of fusion. Fix: Slow down and watch the puddle wet out at the edges. Mistake 4: Wrong fillet size. A 6mm fillet on a 10mm plate may be under-sized for the load. Fix: Use a fillet gauge. If the leg is 5mm, add another pass. Mistake 5: Poor interpass temperature control. Welding continuously can overheat the steel, causing grain growth and reduced toughness. Fix: Let it cool to below 250C between passes. Mistake 6: No cleaning between passes. Slag inclusions cause rejects. Fix: Chip and brush every pass. Mistake 7: Skipping documentation. Without a WPS and records, you can't prove compliance. Fix: Keep a logbook and copy of the WPS on site. Mistake 8: Using damaged cables or loose connections. This causes voltage drop and poor welds. Fix: Inspect cables daily. In Africa, power fluctuations are commonuse a voltage stabilizer if possible. If a weld fails inspection, don't just grind and re-weld; find the root cause. Was it amperage? Travel speed? Moisture in electrode? Fix the cause, then repair. Remember: codes are minimums, not maximums. Doing it right the first time saves money and lives.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Welding codes also cover safety. The main hazards are electric shock, fumes, arc eye, burns, and fire. For electric shock, ensure your machine is grounded and use dry gloves. Never weld in wet conditions. For fumes, especially with galvanized steel or stainless, use a respirator and ventilate the area. In Africa, many workshops are open-air, which helps, but still position yourself upwind. Arc eye comes from UV radiationalways wear a welding helmet with at least a shade 10 lens for arc welding. For gas welding, shade 5. Keep a fire extinguisher nearby and a bucket of sand. Never weld near flammable liquids. Best practice: follow the code's safety section (e.g., AWS Z49.1). Wear leather gloves, apron, and safety boots. Use screens to protect others. For confined spaces, use a gas monitor and a spotter. Also, codes require welder qualification. You should test every 6 months or when you change process. In Africa, many welders skip this, but a qualified welder can command higher pay. Keep your certification card. Another best practice: use the correct amperage. Too high burns through; too low causes lack of fusion. Check the WPS. Finally, maintain your equipment. Replace worn liners, tips, and nozzles. A clean machine runs cooler and welds better. If you work at height, use a harness. If you weld in a tank, have a rescue plan. Safety is not optionalit's part of the code. A safe welder is a productive welder.

6. Practical Exercise

For this exercise, you will make a fillet weld on a T-joint and check it against a code. You need: two steel plates 10mm thick, 100mm x 50mm each; E7018 electrodes 3.2mm; a SMAW machine; a fillet weld gauge; a wire brush; a chipping hammer; a temperature crayon (100C); and a hacksaw. Step 1: Clean the plates. Step 2: Tack weld the plates to form a T. Step 3: Preheat to 100C using a torch. Check with crayon. Step 4: Set amperage to 120A (within WPS range). Step 5: Weld a 6mm fillet along the joint. Use a 10-15 drag angle, short arc. Step 6: Let it cool. Clean slag. Step 7: Measure the fillet leg with a gauge. It should be 6mm 1mm. If under, add a pass. Step 8: Inspect for undercut (max 0.5mm), porosity, cracks. Step 9: Cut the weld cross-section with a hacksaw. Polish with a file. Look for fusion at the root. If there is lack of fusion, your travel was too fast or amperage too low. Step 10: Write a short report: WPS number, amperage, preheat, leg size, defects found, and repair action. This exercise mimics a real code test. In Africa, you can do this in a workshop with minimal tools. Repeat until you can consistently pass. Then try a 3G (vertical) test. That's what employers pay for.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Welding Codes and Standards for African Fabrication Businesses

In the African welding trade, the difference between a shop that wins contracts and one that stays local is often not the quality of the weld bead it is whether the business can prove that the weld meets a recognised code. Mining houses in Zambia's Copperbelt, oil and gas operators in Nigeria's Niger Delta, and infrastructure contractors on Kenya's Standard Gauge Railway all ask the same question before awarding a fabrication package: Which welding code are you working to, and can you show me the procedure and the welder qualification to match?

This Practical Reference lesson gives you the working knowledge to answer that question. It covers the codes most commonly referenced across Africa, the data you need on your desk, and the commercial reasons compliance pays. Treat it as a bench-side reference for running a compliant welding operation, not as a substitute for the full code documents, which must be purchased or licensed from the issuing body.

Why Codes and Standards Are a Business Asset

A welding code is a written set of rules for design, procedure, qualification, fabrication, inspection and acceptance. A standard is a document that defines how a material, process or test is performed. Together they create the traceability that clients, insurers and regulators demand. For an African fabricator, three commercial benefits follow immediately:

  • Access to higher-value tenders. Most mines, oil and gas majors, and public infrastructure authorities require compliance with a recognised code as a pre-qualification condition. Without it, your bid is not evaluated.
  • Reduced rework and liability. A qualified Welding Procedure Specification (WPS) and Procedure Qualification Record (PQ) remove guesswork. Repairs on a pressure vessel or structural node can cost more than the original weld, and failed welds can trigger insurance disputes.
  • Workforce mobility. A welder qualified to a recognised code for example, under ISO 9606 or ASME Section IX can carry that qualification across borders and projects, which raises the earning power of your team and reduces your recruitment risk.

The Core Documents You Must Control

Before any arc is struck on a coded job, four documents should be in place and available to the inspector:

  1. Welding Procedure Specification (WPS). The written recipe: base metal, filler, process, current, voltage, travel speed, preheat, interpass temperature, shielding gas and joint details.
  2. Procedure Qualification Record (PQR). The evidence that the WPS produces a sound weld, based on destructive testing of a test coupon.
  3. Welder Qualification Certificate. Proof that each welder has passed a test to the relevant code for the position, process and material in use.
  4. Inspection and Test Plan (ITP). The schedule of inspections, non-destructive testing (NDT) and hold points agreed with the client.

SAFETY WARNING HIGH RISK

Never weld to an unqualified or expired WPS, and never allow a welder to work outside the limits of their qualification. On pressurised or structural work, an out-of-scope weld can fail catastrophically, causing fire, explosion, structural collapse or loss of life. In many African jurisdictions, welding on pressure equipment without a valid WPS and qualified welder can also void insurance and breach occupational health and safety law. If in doubt, stop and verify the documentation before striking an arc.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Confirm the exact code, edition and addenda specified in the contract before quoting.
  • Ensure a current WPS and supporting PQR exist for every joint, process and material combination.
  • Verify each welder's certificate covers the process, position, material and thickness in use.
  • Check filler metal certificates and base metal certificates against the WPS.
  • Confirm preheat, interpass temperature and post-weld heat treatment requirements.
  • Agree the ITP and hold points with the client's inspector before starting production.
  • Record NDT results and non-conformances, and close them out with signed dispositions.
  • Archive WPS, PQR, welder certs, NDT reports and material certs for the contract retention period.

Business takeaway: In the African market, welding codes are not bureaucracy they are the passport to better-paying work. A shop that controls its WPS, PQR, welder certificates and NDT records can bid for mining, oil and gas, and infrastructure contracts that unqualified competitors cannot touch. Build the documentation system once, train your team to maintain it, and it will pay for itself on the first coded contract you win.
