1. Receiving and Inspection Procedures

When a delivery truck arrives at your cold store, your job is to check the load quickly and accurately before it enters the storage area. Start by checking the truck's temperature recorder or a handheld thermometer. For frozen goods, the core temperature should be -18Â°C or colder. For chilled products, it should be between 0Â°C and 4Â°C. If the temperature is too high, reject the load or record the deviation and notify your supervisor. Next, inspect the packaging. Look for torn cartons, wet boxes, or signs of thawing and refreezing, such as ice crystals inside plastic bags. Count the number of cartons or crates and compare with the delivery note. Use a clipboard and pen to record actual quantities received. If there are discrepancies, note them on the delivery note and have the driver sign.

After the initial check, move the products to a receiving area or directly to the cold room, but never leave them on the dock for more than 30 minutes. Use a pallet jack or trolley to move goods quickly. While moving, check for pests or contamination. Look for insects, rodent droppings, or unusual smells. If you find any, isolate the affected items and inform your supervisor immediately. For fresh produce like fruits and vegetables, check for bruises, mould, or overripeness. For meat and fish, check colour and smell. Any product that looks or smells bad should be rejected.

Once the load is accepted, record everything in a receiving log. Include the date, time, supplier name, product type, quantity, temperature, and any issues found. This log helps with traceability and also helps you track supplier performance. If a supplier often sends poor-quality goods, you can use the log as evidence. Also, keep the delivery note and any temperature records for at least one year. In many African countries, health inspectors may ask for these records during a visit.

Finally, train your receiving staff to work fast but carefully. In hot climates, every minute counts. Set a target of 20 minutes from truck arrival to storage for frozen goods. Use a checklist to avoid missing steps. A simple checklist can be: check temperature, check packaging, count items, check for pests, record in log, move to cold room. Following these steps protects your products and your business.

2. Storage Temperature and Humidity Control

Temperature and humidity are the two most important factors in cold storage. Different products need different conditions. For example, frozen meat and fish need -18Â°C or colder. Chilled meat needs 0Â°C to 2Â°C. Dairy products like milk and cheese need 1Â°C to 4Â°C. Fruits and vegetables have different needs: leafy greens like spinach need 0Â°C to 2Â°C and high humidity (90-95%), while bananas need 13Â°C to 15Â°C and lower humidity. If you store them together, they will spoil faster. So, group products by their temperature and humidity requirements.

To control temperature, use a reliable thermometer or a data logger. Check the temperature at least twice a day, once in the morning and once in the afternoon. Record the readings in a logbook. If the temperature is too high or too low, adjust the thermostat or call a technician. Never ignore a temperature alarm. In many African cold stores, power cuts are common. Have a backup generator or a plan to move products to another cold room. Also, keep doors closed as much as possible. Every time you open the door, cold air escapes and warm air enters. Use strip curtains or plastic doors to reduce air exchange.

Humidity control is also critical. Too much humidity can cause mould and spoilage. Too little humidity can cause weight loss in fruits and vegetables. Use a hygrometer to measure humidity. For most chilled products, aim for 85-90% relative humidity. For frozen products, humidity is less critical because the low temperature prevents microbial growth, but you should still avoid ice buildup. If humidity is too high, use a dehumidifier or improve ventilation. If too low, you can place open containers of water or use a humidifier. In dry African climates, humidifiers are often needed for fresh produce.

Regular maintenance of your cold room is essential. Clean the evaporator coils every month to ensure efficient cooling. Check door seals for gaps. A simple test: close the door on a piece of paper; if you can pull it out easily, the seal is bad. Replace worn seals. Also, ensure that the cold room is not overloaded. Air must circulate around products. Leave at least 10 cm of space between pallets and walls, and 30 cm below the ceiling. Stack boxes with gaps for air flow. By controlling temperature and humidity, you can extend shelf life and reduce losses.

3. Product Placement and Rotation (FIFO/FEFO)

How you place products in your cold room affects both quality and efficiency. Always store products on pallets or shelves, never directly on the floor. The floor is colder and can cause freezer burn or contamination. Use pallets that are clean and dry. Stack boxes neatly, but do not stack them too high. A good rule is to keep stacks at least 30 cm below the ceiling to allow air circulation. Also, leave 10 cm between pallets and walls. This prevents condensation and makes cleaning easier.

Group similar products together. For example, keep all frozen meat in one area, all dairy in another, and all fruits and vegetables in a separate section. This makes it easier to find products and reduces the risk of cross-contamination. For example, raw meat should never be stored above ready-to-eat foods. If meat drips, it can contaminate the food below. So, always place raw meat on the bottom shelf. Use colour-coded labels or signs to mark different zones.

Rotation is critical. You must use FIFO (First In, First Out) and FEFO (First Expired, First Out). FIFO means the oldest stock is used first. FEFO means the stock with the earliest expiry date is used first. In practice, FEFO is more important for perishable goods. When new stock arrives, move older stock to the front and place new stock behind. Use a marker to write the received date and expiry date on each box. For products without a date, use a sticker with the date you received it. Train your staff to always pick from the front. This prevents products from expiring in the back of the cold room.

Conduct regular checks for expired or damaged products. At least once a week, go through the cold room and remove any items that are past their expiry date or show signs of spoilage. Record these in a waste log. If you find expired products, investigate why they were not used in time. Maybe the rotation was not followed, or the stock was too large. Adjust your ordering accordingly. Also, keep a map of your cold room showing where each product is stored. This helps new staff find things quickly and ensures that rotation is consistent. By following FIFO and FEFO, you reduce waste and ensure that customers get fresh products.

4. Order Picking and Dispatch

Order picking is the process of collecting products from the cold room to fulfil customer orders. It must be done quickly and accurately to maintain the cold chain. Before you start picking, review the order. Check the product codes, quantities, and any special instructions. Use a picking list or a handheld device if available. If you use paper, attach it to a clipboard. Wear warm clothing, such as a jacket, gloves, and a hat, because you will be in and out of the cold room. Also, wear non-slip boots to prevent accidents.

When picking, always follow FIFO/FEFO. Pick from the front of the shelf, where the oldest stock is. Check the expiry date on each item. Do not pick products that are expired or damaged. If an item is missing or not enough, note it on the picking list and inform your supervisor. Use a trolley or pallet jack to move products. Do not carry too much at once. For frozen items, work quickly to minimise time out of the cold room. A good target is to pick a pallet in 10-15 minutes. If you take longer, the products may start to thaw.

After picking, move the products to a dispatch area. This area should be cold, but not as cold as the storage room. For frozen goods, keep the dispatch area at -15Â°C or colder. For chilled goods, keep it at 0Â°C to 4Â°C. If you don't have a cold dispatch area, load the products directly into the delivery vehicle. The vehicle must be pre-cooled to the correct temperature before loading. Check the vehicle's temperature recorder. If it's not working, use a handheld thermometer. Load products quickly and close the doors. Do not leave products on the dock.

Documentation is key. Record what was picked, the batch numbers, and the destination. Use a dispatch note that lists the products, quantities, and temperatures. Have the driver sign the dispatch note. Also, record the time the vehicle left. Keep a copy for your records. If a customer complains about quality, you can trace the batch and see if there was a temperature issue during dispatch. Finally, clean the picking area and return any unused products to the cold room immediately. Following these steps ensures that orders are correct and products arrive in good condition.

5. Sanitation and Pest Control

A clean cold store is a safe cold store. Sanitation prevents bacteria, mould, and pests from contaminating your products. Start with a cleaning schedule. Every day, sweep the floors and wipe down surfaces. Every week, deep clean the cold room. Use cleaning agents that are safe for food areas, such as food-grade sanitisers. Do not use strong-smelling chemicals because they can taint the products. For floors, use a mixture of warm water and a mild detergent. For walls and shelves, use a sanitiser. Always rinse with clean water and dry. Never leave puddles; they can become breeding grounds for bacteria.

Pay special attention to drains and corners. These are common places for mould and pests. Flush drains weekly with hot water and a drain cleaner. Check for leaks and fix them immediately. Moisture attracts pests. Also, clean the evaporator coils and fans. Dust and dirt reduce efficiency and can spread contaminants. Use a vacuum or a soft brush. If you have a walk-in freezer, remove ice buildup regularly. Ice can hide dirt and pests. Use a plastic scraper, not a metal one, to avoid damaging the surfaces.

Pest control is critical. Common pests in cold stores include rodents, cockroaches, and flies. Rodents can chew through packaging and contaminate food with droppings and urine. Cockroaches carry bacteria. Flies can enter when doors are open. To prevent pests, seal all cracks and gaps. Install door sweeps and keep doors closed. Use insectocutors (electric fly killers) near entrances. For rodents, set traps and bait stations outside the cold room, not inside. Check them weekly. If you see signs of pests, such as droppings, gnaw marks, or live insects, act immediately. Call a professional pest control company. Do not use sprays inside the cold room while products are present.

Train all staff in hygiene. They must wash hands before entering the cold room and after using the toilet. Wear clean uniforms and gloves. Do not eat or smoke in the cold room. Keep a cleaning log and a pest control log. Record what was cleaned, when, and by whom. Also, record any pest sightings and the action taken. In many African countries, health inspectors require these logs. By keeping your cold store clean and pest-free, you protect your products and your reputation.

6. Documentation and Traceability

Documentation is the backbone of cold storage operations. It helps you track products from receiving to dispatch, and it is essential for food safety and customer trust. Start with a receiving log. When a truck arrives, record the date, time, supplier, product name, quantity, temperature, and any issues. Use a notebook or a simple spreadsheet. If you have a computer, use it, but paper is fine as long as it is kept dry and organised. Assign a batch number to each delivery. A batch number can be the date plus a sequence, like 20250315-01. Write this number on the delivery note and on the products themselves.

Next, keep a storage log. Record the temperature and humidity of each cold room at least twice a day. Note the time, the readings, and your initials. If there is a deviation, write what you did to correct it. For example, if the temperature rose to -12Â°C, note that you checked the door and called the technician. Also, keep a cleaning log and a pest control log. These logs show that you are following good practices. In many countries, these records are required by law. Keep them for at least one year, or longer if your customers require it.

When you pick an order, record the batch numbers of the products you picked. This is called a picking log. Include the order number, customer name, product, quantity, and batch number. When you dispatch, create a dispatch note. It should list the products, quantities, batch numbers, and the temperature of the vehicle. The driver signs it, and you keep a copy. If a customer complains about a product, you can use the batch number to trace it back to the supplier and the receiving date. This is called traceability. It helps you identify the source of a problem and prevent it from happening again.

Finally, keep your records organised. Use a file for each month. Label folders clearly. If you use a computer, back up your data regularly. Train your staff to fill in logs correctly. A missing or incomplete log can cause problems during an inspection or a customer audit. Also, consider using a simple inventory management system on a phone or computer. Many free apps are available. But even a paper system works if it is consistent. The key is to record everything and keep it safe. Good documentation protects your business and your customers.

Practical Reference: Cold Storage Operations in African Agribusiness

Cold storage is the backbone of high-value agricultural trade in Africa. Whether you are handling avocado for export from Kenya, tilapia from Lake Volta, or vaccines and horticultural produce moving through Ghana, Nigeria, or South Africa, the difference between profit and loss is often a few degrees and a few hours. This Practical Reference consolidates the operational standards that keep product saleable, compliant with buyers, and safe for consumers. It is written for warehouse managers, supervisors, and owners who need to run a cold room as a business â not just as a building.

Every lesson in this module feeds into one commercial outcome: maintaining product value from gate to gate. A cold store that receives poorly, controls temperature loosely, or loses traceability will destroy margins through rejections, weight loss, and spoilage. The procedures below are drawn from internationally recognised cold chain standards and adapted for the realities of African logistics â intermittent power, long road distances, and mixed-product loads.

1. Receiving and Inspection Procedures

Receiving is your first and cheapest quality control point. Reject at the door, not in the cold room. Before unloading, verify the purchase order, supplier details, and the vehicle's pre-cooling record. Check the pulp temperature of a representative sample â not just the air temperature â using a calibrated probe thermometer. For fresh produce, a pulp temperature above the agreed maximum (often 4â8 Â°C depending on commodity) is grounds for rejection or conditional acceptance. Inspect for physical damage, pest presence, off-odours, and condensation. Record everything on a Goods Received Note (GRN) and photograph anomalies. In many African markets, informal suppliers may resist documentation; make the GRN a condition of payment to enforce discipline.

2. Storage Temperature and Humidity Control

Temperature and humidity are the two variables that determine shelf life. Different commodities have different optima: leafy vegetables need 0â2 Â°C and 95â98% RH, while bananas ripen best at 13â15 Â°C and 85â90% RH. Mixing incompatible products in one room is a common and costly error. Install calibrated data loggers at multiple points â near the door, at the evaporator, and in the warmest corner â and review records daily. In Africa, power instability is the biggest threat. A backup generator, thermal mass (such as water drums), and a written power-failure protocol are essential. Never store products directly against walls or on the floor; use pallets and maintain 10â15 cm clearance for air circulation.

3. Product Placement and Rotation (FIFO/FEFO)

FIFO (First In, First Out) and FEFO (First Expired, First Out) are not administrative preferences â they are profit protection tools. FEFO is mandatory for products with expiry dates such as dairy, meat, and pharmaceuticals. Label every pallet with receipt date, expiry date, batch number, and commodity code. Place older stock at the front and newest at the back. Use racking where possible to separate incompatible products and prevent cross-contamination. In shared cold stores common in African urban markets, enforce a written placement map and refuse ad-hoc storage that breaks segregation.

4. Order Picking and Dispatch

Picking is where cold chain integrity is most often broken. Minimise door-open time by consolidating orders and using a staging area that is itself temperature-controlled or insulated. Verify product temperature at dispatch and record it on the delivery note. For export loads, pre-cool the truck and use a temperature recorder that travels with the consignment. In many African corridors â such as the MombasaâNairobi or LagosâKano routes â road delays are common, so dispatch temperature must account for transit time. Never dispatch product that has been temperature-abused; it will fail at destination and damage your buyer relationship.

5. Sanitation and Pest Control

Cold rooms are not immune to pests. Rodents, cockroaches, and even birds can enter through damaged seals and drains. Implement a cleaning schedule with food-safe sanitisers, keep floors dry, and maintain a 50 cm pest-control perimeter around the building. Document every cleaning and pest-control activity. In humid African climates, mould is a persistent threat; control humidity and inspect pallets weekly. A clean cold store also reduces energy costs because evaporator coils stay efficient.

6. Documentation and Traceability

Traceability is increasingly a market access requirement, not just good practice. Buyers in the EU, UK, and Middle East require proof of origin, temperature history, and handling records. Keep a simple but complete system: GRNs, temperature logs, cleaning records, dispatch notes, and batch tracking. Digital tools are becoming affordable â even a shared spreadsheet with daily backups can work. The goal is to trace any pallet from farm to buyer within minutes, and to recall it if needed.
