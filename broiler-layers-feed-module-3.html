Lesson 3.1~11 min

Pre-Lay Pullet Nutrition (0-16 weeks)

In this lesson
  • State the Lohmann body weight targets and phase nutrient levels for rearing pullets
  • Explain why frame size at 16-17 weeks determines lifetime egg output
  • Calculate the feed needed to rear a pullet and the cost per point-of-lay bird

Everything a laying hen will ever do is decided before she lays her first egg. The rearing period builds skeleton, digestive capacity, muscle and the medullary bone reserve she will draw on for shell formation for the next eighteen months. A pullet that arrives at point of lay underweight will never make it up, because from that moment her body is committed to egg production rather than growth. This is why experienced producers judge a rearing unit by its weights and its uniformity, not by its mortality.

The Lohmann Brown-Classic targets give you the frame. Rearing ends at 17 weeks with a target body weight of 1.42 kg. Getting there means progressive weekly gains: 0.08-0.12 kg total gain across weeks 1-3, then 0.20-0.25 kg per week through the grower phase of weeks 4-8, then 0.18-0.22 kg per week across the developer phase of weeks 9-17. Falling behind in any one of those windows shows up at 17 weeks and cannot be corrected later.

The nutrient plan follows the phases. Lohmann sets starter calcium at 0.90-1.00% with available phosphorus 0.42-0.45% for weeks 1-3, grower calcium at 0.85-0.95% with available phosphorus 0.40-0.42% for weeks 4-8, and developer calcium at 0.90% with available phosphorus 0.37-0.40% for weeks 9-17. Energy for the starter is 2,860 kcal/kg (12.0 MJ). Notice that calcium stays low and roughly flat through rearing - around 1% or below. A pullet is not a layer, and feeding layer-level calcium of nearly 4% to a growing pullet damages the kidneys and gives you nothing in return.

What you are buying in this phase is appetite and gut capacity. A pullet reared on a diet that forces her to eat a reasonable volume develops the digestive capacity to handle the 110 g a day she will need later. Rearing on a very concentrated, low-volume diet produces a hen that physically cannot eat enough at peak. This is why many rearing programmes deliberately raise fibre in the developer phase.

Uniformity matters as much as the average. Weigh 30 to 50 randomly caught pullets, calculate the average, and count how many fall within plus or minus 10% of it. A flock where 80% or more sit inside that band will come into lay together and peak sharply. A flock at 60% will straggle into production over several weeks, and you will feed a full house for a partial egg return. The commonest causes of poor uniformity are too little feeder space and inconsistent feed delivery, both cheap to fix.

Cost the rearing. Published intake for a brown pullet to 17 weeks is roughly 6 kg of feed. For 200 pullets that is about 1,200 kg. At an average $0.50/kg across the three rearing diets, feed is $600, or $3.00 per bird. Add a day-old pullet chick at about $1.10, plus vaccination and brooding fuel of perhaps $0.60, and your point-of-lay bird has cost around $4.70 before she has laid an egg. Against a bought point-of-lay pullet at, say, $6.50, home rearing can pay - but only if your 17-week weight and uniformity match what the seller delivers. If your pullets reach only 1.20 kg, you have saved $1.80 and lost far more in eggs.

Mycotoxin control is important here. Young birds are highly sensitive: chicks of 0-4 weeks carry an aflatoxin limit of 0.005 ppm against 0.02 ppm for older birds, and an ochratoxin limit of 0.05 ppm against 0.10 ppm. Module 10 covers grain sampling and storage in detail; the rule for rearing is to use your best grain here, not your worst.

A pullet rearer in Jos, Nigeria supplying 500-bird batches was losing customers because his birds came into lay late. Weighing showed a 17-week average of 1.24 kg against the 1.42 kg target, and only 58% of birds within 10% of the average. He had two feeder lines for 500 birds. Adding a third line and moving from a single daily feed to two feeds lifted his next batch to 1.39 kg with 76% uniformity, at a one-off cost of under $70.

Early Lay Nutrition (16-30 weeks)

In this lesson
  • Time the pre-lay and layer feed changes against sexual maturity, not the calendar alone
  • State the pre-lay calcium level and explain what it prepares
  • Calculate daily nutrient delivery from feed intake in grams

The window from 16 to 30 weeks is the steepest change a hen ever goes through. In about eight weeks she moves from laying nothing to laying an egg on most days, her body weight is still rising, and her calcium demand multiplies several times over. Two feed decisions in this window shape the whole cycle: when to move to the pre-lay diet, and when to move to the full layer diet.

The pre-lay diet exists to prepare the skeleton. Lohmann specifies pre-lay calcium at 2.00-2.50% with available phosphorus 0.38-0.42%, fed from about 18 weeks until roughly 5% production. That level sits between the developer's 0.90% and layer phase 1's 3.8-4.2%. Its purpose is to let the hen lay down medullary bone - the labile calcium reserve inside the long bones that she will draw on every night when the shell gland is working and no feed is in the gut. A hen moved straight from developer to full layer feed misses that preparation and starts her cycle borrowing from structural bone, which is where cage layer fatigue and late-cycle skeletal fragility begin.

Equally, do not leave her on pre-lay too long. Once about 5% of the flock is laying, calcium demand jumps and the hen needs the full layer level. The signal to switch is production, not a date on the calendar. Watch the nest boxes and the first eggs on the floor.

The layer specification at this stage is high. Hy-Line Brown at peak production, on a 90 g daily intake, calls for 19.83% crude protein, 0.92% SID lysine and 0.83% SID methionine plus cystine. Calcium is 3.8-4.1% of the diet, delivering about 4.0 g per bird per day, with a calcium to phosphorus ratio of 8.4-11.0:1 - a ratio that would be dangerous in a broiler and is essential in a layer. The limestone particle split at peak is 40% fine to 60% coarse, which Lesson 5 explains in detail. NRC 1994, expressed as daily amounts at 100 g intake, gives 15,000 mg crude protein, 690 mg lysine, 300 mg methionine, 580 mg methionine plus cysteine, 470 mg threonine, 160 mg tryptophan, 700 mg arginine, 700 mg valine and 650 mg isoleucine.

Here is the arithmetic that matters most and is done least. Requirements are daily, but feeds are formulated per kilogram. Suppose your hens eat 95 g a day and your feed contains 3.7% calcium. Daily calcium is 0.095 kg x 3.7% = 0.095 x 0.037 = 0.0035 kg, which is 3.5 g. Against the 4.0 g target you are 0.5 g short every day - enough to cost you shell quality within a month. To fix it, either raise dietary calcium to 4.0 / 0.095 / 10 = 4.2% or raise intake. Run the same calculation for lysine: at 95 g intake, a 0.87% lysine feed delivers 95 x 0.0087 = 0.83 g, or 830 mg, comfortably above the NRC 690 mg but below the Hy-Line peak figure once digestibility is accounted for.

Body weight must keep rising through early lay. Lohmann shows layer body weight stabilising at 1.80-2.00 kg during peak production, having come from 1.42 kg at 17 weeks. A hen that stops gaining or loses weight during early lay is being under-fed and will drop off peak early. If your birds are not gaining, check feed intake in grams per bird per day before you change the formula.

Cost the change. For 300 hens eating 95 g a day, monthly feed is 300 x 0.095 x 30 = 855 kg. Moving from a $0.44/kg pre-lay to a $0.48/kg layer feed adds 855 x $0.04 = $34.20 a month. If it lifts hen-day production from 82% to 88%, that is 300 x 0.06 = 18 extra eggs a day, or 540 a month. At $0.13 an egg that is $70 - double the feed cost increase.

A producer in Nakuru, Kenya moved her 400 Lohmann pullets straight from developer to layer feed at 18 weeks with no pre-lay stage. Production reached only 84% at peak and shell quality was poor by 40 weeks. On the following batch she introduced a 2.2% calcium pre-lay diet for three weeks at a total extra cost of about $18 for the flock; peak reached 92%.

Peak Layer Nutrition (30-60 weeks)

In this lesson
  • Hold nutrient delivery constant as feed intake changes with season and age
  • Benchmark feed conversion against published kg feed per dozen figures
  • Calculate feed cost per dozen eggs and margin at local prices

From about 30 to 60 weeks the hen is doing the job you bought her for. Production is at or near its ceiling, egg weight is climbing, and the flock is consuming feed steadily. This is the phase where small nutritional errors are invisible for weeks and then appear all at once as a production drop you cannot recover.

The published benchmark to measure yourself against is feed conversion. Hy-Line Brown from peak to 60 weeks runs 2.06-2.15 kg of feed per kilogram of eggs, or 1.23-1.28 kg of feed per dozen eggs. Lohmann Brown-Classic gives 2.0-2.2 kg feed per kilogram of egg mass across the cycle. Over a longer 20-100 week run the Hy-Line figures loosen to 2.15-2.24 kg/kg and 1.55-1.61 kg per dozen, because later birds eat more per egg.

Calculate yours. For 300 hens eating 110 g a day, monthly feed is 300 x 0.110 x 30 = 990 kg. At 88% hen-day production, monthly eggs are 300 x 0.88 x 30 = 7,920 eggs, which is 660 dozen. Feed per dozen is 990 / 660 = 1.50 kg. Against the Hy-Line 1.23-1.28 kg benchmark you are well behind - the likely causes are feed wastage, an over-diluted feed, or production below what your record suggests. At $0.48/kg, your feed cost per dozen is 1.50 x $0.48 = $0.72. If eggs sell at $1.55 a dozen, feed leaves you $0.83 a dozen before every other cost. Cutting feed per dozen to 1.30 kg would save 0.20 x $0.48 = $0.096 a dozen, or $63 a month on 660 dozen.

The nutritional job in this phase is to hold daily delivery constant while intake moves. Hy-Line's own specification shifts with intake: 19.83% crude protein, 0.92% SID lysine and 0.83% met+cys at 90 g a day, dropping to 16.23% crude protein, 0.75% lysine and 0.68% met+cys at 110 g a day in the later Layer 5 phase for production below 85%. The daily amounts are similar; the concentration changes because the grams change. In practice, on a small farm this means you must actually weigh what your birds eat, because heat, feed form and season move intake by 10-20% and your formula should move with it.

Heat is the main disruptor. When house temperature rises above about 30C, intake falls, and if the diet stays the same the hen simply gets less of everything - including calcium, which shows first as thin shells. The responses are to concentrate the diet, feed at the cool ends of the day, and raise dietary electrolyte balance using the bands from Module 1 Lesson 4. Environmental conditions, including temperature, humidity and stocking density, are explicitly recognised in the literature as shifting layer feed intake and amino acid requirements, with adjustment of dietary amino acid concentration as the compensation.

Calcium stays at 3.8-4.1% of diet, about 4.0 g per bird per day, with a 40% fine to 60% coarse limestone split. Do not let this slip. Also do not creep the protein upward hoping for bigger eggs; excess nitrogen above the amino acid requirement is economic loss and raises nitrogen excretion and house ammonia, which then damages the respiratory tract.

Mycotoxins deserve a mention because layers are more exposed than broilers - they eat the same grain store for a year or more. Ochratoxin A at 0.5 ppm has been associated with a 50-75% production decline in layers, against a regulatory limit of 0.10 ppm. If production drops sharply across a whole house with no disease signs, suspect the feed. Module 10 covers testing and storage.

A layer unit in Kampala, Uganda with 800 hens saw production slide from 89% to 78% over six weeks during the hot season with no mortality change. Intake had fallen from 108 g to 92 g a day - the hens were simply eating 16 g less of the same feed, losing about 0.6 g of calcium and a proportional share of every other nutrient daily. Re-formulating the same ingredients to a higher density and shifting feeding to 5.30am and 6.30pm brought production back to 86% within a month.

Late Lay Nutrition (60+ weeks)

In this lesson
  • Adjust calcium level and particle size for the ageing hen
  • Decide between continuing, molting or culling using your own numbers
  • Calculate the break-even production rate at your local egg and feed price

After about 60 weeks the hen changes in two ways that matter to you. Her egg gets bigger while the amount of shell she can deposit does not, so shell thickness falls. And she becomes less efficient at absorbing and mobilising calcium. The result is more cracked eggs, more downgrades, and a slow slide in saleable production. Late-lay nutrition is about managing that decline profitably rather than pretending it is not happening.

The calcium answer is counter-intuitive: give her more, and give it coarser. Hy-Line's decline phase from about week 77 specifies a daily calcium intake of 4.7 g per bird, up from 4.0 g at peak, at a dietary concentration of 3.92-4.70% depending on feed intake. More importantly the particle split changes from 40% fine to 60% coarse, to 25% fine to 75% coarse. Coarse particles of about 2-4 mm are retained in the gizzard and released slowly, which is exactly what an ageing hen forming shell overnight needs. Fine particles are absorbed rapidly and go mainly to bone maintenance.

Protein and energy move the other way. Hy-Line's Layer 5 phase, for production below 85% at 110 g daily intake, gives 16.23% crude protein, 0.75% SID lysine and 0.68% SID methionine plus cystine - considerably lower than the peak figures of 19.83%, 0.92% and 0.83%. The older hen eats more grams, so the same daily amino acid delivery comes from a less concentrated feed. Feeding peak-level protein to a 70-week flock wastes money and raises house ammonia.

Feed conversion worsens and you must plan for it. Over an extended 20-100 week run, Hy-Line figures are 2.15-2.24 kg feed per kg of eggs and 1.55-1.61 kg per dozen, against 2.06-2.15 and 1.23-1.28 to 60 weeks. Lohmann notes the same drift, with later-cycle birds at 2.15-2.24 kg feed per kg of eggs.

Now the decision. Work out your break-even production rate. For 300 hens eating 115 g a day at $0.48/kg, daily feed cost is 300 x 0.115 x $0.48 = $16.56. At $0.13 per saleable egg, you need 16.56 / 0.13 = 128 saleable eggs a day just to cover feed - that is 42.5% hen-day production if every egg sells at full price. Allow 8% cracks and downgrades and you need about 46% production. Below that, the flock is losing money on feed alone before any other cost. Above it, keep going. This single calculation is the most useful thing a layer producer can do, and it changes every time feed or egg prices move.

The third option is an induced molt, which rests the flock and rejuvenates the reproductive tract for a second cycle. Published protocols use a fasting phase of 7-14 days followed by a molt diet fed for 28-42 days, with metabolisable energy reduced 15-25% against standard layer feed, crude protein cut to 8-12% from the usual 16-18%, calcium restricted to support feather regrowth and follicle regression, and phosphorus maintained for bone integrity. Calcium supplementation during the fasting period has been shown to increase post-molt production. Recovery to 90% production takes 4-6 weeks, post-molt production typically returns to 85-95% of pre-molt levels, flock life is extended by a further 6-8 months, and shell quality in the second cycle is often better than at the end of the first. Molting is stressful and its acceptability differs by market and by country; it is common in cage systems and declining in the EU. Check what your buyers and your regulator accept before planning one.

A producer in Lilongwe, Malawi with 600 hens at 68 weeks was seeing 11% cracked eggs. He was still feeding the same peak-formulated layer mash with finely ground limestone. Splitting his limestone purchase so that three quarters was coarse grit-sized and raising dietary calcium to about 4.4% cost him roughly $9 a month on 2,070 kg of feed. Cracks fell to under 5%, recovering about 37 saleable eggs a day - worth close to $145 a month.

Eggshell Quality and Calcium Metabolism

In this lesson
  • Describe how a hen assembles a shell overnight and where the calcium comes from
  • Set limestone particle size correctly for the flock's age
  • Cost the saleable eggs lost to poor shells and the price of fixing it

A hen puts about 2.2-2.5 g of calcium into every eggshell. She does it mostly at night, over roughly the last fifteen hours before the egg is laid, at a time when she is not eating. Where that calcium comes from, and how fast it arrives, is the whole story of shell quality.

There are two sources. The first is the gut, supplied by calcium still being released in the gizzard. The second is medullary bone - a spongy calcium reserve inside the long bones, built during the pre-lay phase and topped up daily. If gut supply runs out overnight, the hen strips her own skeleton. Do that for months and you get thin shells, fragile bones and eventually cage layer fatigue.

This is why particle size is not a detail. Coarse calcium particles of about 2-4 mm are retained in the gizzard and released slowly over hours, supplying the shell gland through the night. Fine particles are absorbed rapidly and go mainly to bone maintenance. The published recommendation is 40% fine to 60% coarse at peak production, shifting to 25% fine to 75% coarse in late production and decline, where shell quality is prioritised over bone reserve. A producer grinding all his limestone to a fine powder because it mixes better is guaranteeing thin shells, no matter how much calcium is in the analysis.

Amount matters alongside form. Hy-Line specifies about 4.0 g of calcium per bird per day at peak, at 3.8-4.1% of the diet, and 4.7 g per day in the decline phase at 3.92-4.70%. The calcium to phosphorus ratio in a layer runs 8.4-11.0:1, which reflects how far shell demand dominates. Available phosphorus in Lohmann layer phase 1 is 0.34-0.42%; too little phosphorus impairs bone turnover, and too much interferes with calcium.

Vitamin D3 is the third leg. It drives calcium absorption from the gut and bone mineralisation. Where a layer diet is specified at 4,000-5,000 IU/kg, a premix that has lost potency in a hot store will show up as poor shells before it shows up as anything else. The 25-OH D3 metabolite is absorbed more efficiently than D3 because it bypasses liver hydroxylation, and is roughly four to five times more biologically active per unit weight, with recommended use at 2,760-5,529 IU/kg where it partly or fully replaces D3. Manganese at layer levels of 60-80 mg/kg and zinc at 80-100 mg/kg also participate in shell matrix formation.

Heat wrecks shells through two routes. It cuts feed intake, so daily calcium falls, and it drives panting, which blows off carbon dioxide and reduces the bicarbonate available for the carbonate half of calcium carbonate. Raising dietary electrolyte balance into the 300-350 mEq/kg band for 32-35C conditions helps on both counts, and an evening feed timed to put calcium in the gizzard before the night's shell formation is one of the most effective free interventions available - many producers offer a separate coarse limestone or oyster shell top-dress in the late afternoon for exactly this reason.

Cost the problem. Take 500 hens at 85% production, laying 425 eggs a day. If 9% are cracked or thin-shelled and sold at half price or not at all, that is 38 eggs a day. At $0.13 each, the full loss is about $4.97 a day, or $149 a month. Now the fix: switching half your limestone to coarse grade usually costs nothing extra per kilogram, and raising dietary calcium from 3.7% to 4.2% on a flock eating 500 x 0.110 x 30 = 1,650 kg a month means about 8 kg more limestone, worth perhaps $1.50. Even a premix upgrade at $0.005/kg of feed adds only $8.25 a month. The economics are not close.

A farmer in Kigali, Rwanda with 350 hens at 55 weeks was running 10% seconds. Sieving a sample of her feed showed almost no particles above 1 mm - her supplier ground everything fine. She began buying crushed limestone grit separately and offering it in a small trough each afternoon at about 4 g per bird. Within three weeks seconds fell below 5%, recovering roughly 18 saleable eggs a day, worth around $70 a month against a grit cost near $6.

Flock Uniformity and Performance Monitoring

In this lesson
  • Set up a weekly record system that a small producer can actually maintain
  • Calculate uniformity, hen-day production and feed per dozen from your own data
  • Diagnose whether a production problem is nutritional, environmental or management

The producers who make money from layers are not the ones with the best feed. They are the ones who know their numbers weekly and act on them within days. A flock that drifts for a month before anyone notices has usually lost more value than a year of feed improvements would recover.

Four records carry almost all the information you need, and together they take about ten minutes a day. First, eggs collected daily, converted to hen-day production: eggs divided by live hens, times 100. Second, feed delivered, converted to grams per bird per day. Third, mortality with a cause where you can identify one. Fourth, a weekly weighing of 30 randomly caught birds. Write them on a wall chart, not a notebook that goes in a drawer.

Uniformity is the record most often skipped and the most diagnostic. Weigh 30 birds, calculate the average, then count how many fall within plus or minus 10% of it. Divide by 30 and multiply by 100. A flock at 80% or above is uniform, will come into lay together and peak sharply. A flock at 60% has a large tail of under-developed birds that will never pay their way. During rearing, uniformity against the Lohmann 17-week target of 1.42 kg tells you whether your feeding programme is working long before egg numbers can.

Read the numbers together, because each one alone is ambiguous. Production falling while feed intake in grams stays constant points to a nutritional problem - the concentration is wrong, or a premix has lost potency, or the calcium level has slipped. Production falling while intake in grams also falls points to environment or palatability - heat, water shortage, stale feed, a mould smell. Production steady but seconds rising points at calcium form and particle size, or ageing birds needing the late-lay adjustments from Lesson 4. Mortality rising alongside falling intake points at disease or water, and Module 9 covers the nutrition-health relationship in full.

Benchmarks make your numbers mean something. Hy-Line Brown gives 2.06-2.15 kg feed per kg of eggs and 1.23-1.28 kg per dozen from peak to 60 weeks, loosening to 2.15-2.24 and 1.55-1.61 over a 20-100 week run. Lohmann Brown-Classic gives 2.0-2.2 kg feed per kg egg mass overall, with body weight stabilising at 1.80-2.00 kg in peak production and reaching about 2.06 kg at the end of a long cycle. Compare yourself weekly, not annually.

Work an example. You keep 400 hens. This week you collected 2,296 eggs and delivered 308 kg of feed. Hen-day production is 2,296 / (400 x 7) = 2,296 / 2,800 = 82%. Feed per bird per day is 308,000 g / 2,800 bird-days = 110 g. Eggs in dozens are 2,296 / 12 = 191.3. Feed per dozen is 308 / 191.3 = 1.61 kg - at the loose end even of the extended-cycle benchmark. At $0.48/kg that is $0.77 of feed in every dozen. If eggs sell at $1.55 a dozen you keep $0.78 before all other costs. Bring feed per dozen down to 1.40 kg and you gain 0.21 x $0.48 = $0.10 a dozen, which on 191 dozen a week is $19, or nearly $1,000 a year on a 400-bird flock.

Sampling technique decides whether your numbers are true. Catch birds where you are standing rather than chasing the ones you can reach, because slow and weak birds are always easiest to catch and will bias your average downward. Weigh at the same time of day each week, ideally before the morning feed. Use the same scale.

A women's poultry group in Mzuzu, Malawi running 250 hens between them started a single shared wall chart with four columns - eggs, feed, deaths, and a Sunday bird weight. Within two months they identified that production always dipped three days after a particular feed delivery, traced it to a supplier substituting a cheaper grain without telling them, and changed supplier. No laboratory, no consultant - just four numbers written down every day.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. The Lohmann Brown-Classic body weight target at the end of rearing (17 weeks) is:

1.42 kg at 17 weeks is the published pullet maturity indicator. Body weight at end of production is about 2.06 kg.

2. Calcium in a 9-17 week developer diet should be about:

Lohmann sets developer calcium at 0.90% with 0.37-0.40% available phosphorus. Layer-level calcium is only introduced at the pre-lay stage.

3. Poor pullet uniformity at 17 weeks results in:

A wide weight spread means birds mature at different times, so you carry the cost of a full house for a partial egg return.

4. A rearing diet that is very concentrated and low in volume risks producing:

Gut capacity is developed during rearing. A pullet never asked to eat volume may not manage the 110 g a day she needs in late production.

5. Aflatoxin limit for birds aged 0-4 weeks is:

Young birds are the most sensitive class at 0.005 ppm, against 0.02 ppm for broilers, layers and breeders - so rearing feed should use your cleanest grain.

6. Pre-lay diet calcium is set at about:

Lohmann sets pre-lay at 2.00-2.50% calcium, between the developer's 0.90% and layer phase 1's 3.8-4.2%, to allow medullary bone formation.

7. The correct signal to move from pre-lay to full layer feed is:

Calcium demand jumps at the onset of lay. Production, not the calendar, is the trigger - watch the nest boxes and the first floor eggs.

8. At 95 g daily intake, a feed with 3.7% calcium delivers:

0.095 kg x 0.037 = 0.0035 kg, or 3.5 g - half a gram short of the roughly 4.0 g daily target at peak.

9. A hen that loses weight in early lay will most likely:

Body weight should continue rising to about 1.80-2.00 kg during peak. Weight loss signals under-feeding and predicts an early decline.

10. NRC 1994 daily lysine for a laying hen at 100 g intake is:

NRC 1994 gives 690 mg lysine per day at 100 g intake, equal to 0.69% of the diet, alongside 300 mg methionine and 580 mg met+cys.

11. Hy-Line Brown feed conversion to 60 weeks is about:

The published benchmark is 1.23-1.28 kg per dozen, or 2.06-2.15 kg of feed per kilogram of eggs, from peak to 60 weeks.

12. 300 hens eating 110 g/day and laying at 88% give feed per dozen of:

990 kg of feed a month divided by 660 dozen eggs equals 1.50 kg per dozen - well above the published benchmark, pointing to waste or dilution.

13. When hot weather cuts layer feed intake, the correct response is to:

Requirements are daily. Fewer grams means every nutrient must be more concentrated, alongside cool-hour feeding and electrolyte adjustment.

14. Raising crude protein above the amino acid requirement mainly causes:

Nitrogen above the ideal amino acid profile is wasted money, and the excreted nitrogen raises house ammonia which damages the respiratory tract.

15. A whole-house production drop with no mortality or disease signs should first make you suspect:

Sudden flock-wide production loss without clinical signs points to feed - either intake has fallen or the grain is contaminated. Ochratoxin A at 0.5 ppm can cut production 50-75%.

16. Daily calcium for a hen in the decline phase from about week 77 is:

Hy-Line specifies 4.7 g/bird/day in decline, up from about 4.0 g at peak, at a dietary concentration of 3.92-4.70%.

17. Limestone particle split in late lay should shift to:

25% fine to 75% coarse. Coarse 2-4 mm particles stay in the gizzard and release calcium slowly through the night when the shell is being formed.

18. Crude protein for Hy-Line Layer 5 (production below 85%, 110 g intake) is:

16.23% crude protein with 0.75% SID lysine, because the older hen eats more grams and needs a less concentrated feed for the same daily delivery.

19. 300 hens eating 115 g/day at $0.48/kg, with eggs at $0.13, break even on feed at about:

Daily feed cost is $16.56; divided by $0.13 that needs 128 eggs, or about 42.5% hen-day production - nearer 46% once cracks are allowed for.

20. A published induced molt protocol uses a molt diet with crude protein of:

Molt diets cut protein to 8-12% from the usual 16-18%, reduce energy 15-25%, restrict calcium and maintain phosphorus for bone integrity.

21. Roughly how much calcium goes into one eggshell?

About 2.2-2.5 g per egg, deposited mainly overnight, which is why gut calcium supply and medullary bone reserve both matter.

22. Coarse limestone particles work because they are:

2-4 mm particles stay in the gizzard and dissolve slowly, feeding the shell gland overnight. Fine particles are absorbed quickly and go mainly to bone maintenance.

23. Grinding all limestone to a fine powder will:

Total calcium in the analysis says nothing about overnight availability. Without a coarse fraction the hen strips her own bone to finish the shell.

24. Panting in heat harms shell quality because it:

Shell is calcium carbonate. Excessive panting depletes the carbon dioxide and bicarbonate supply, and heat also cuts intake and so daily calcium.

25. 25-OH vitamin D3 is roughly how much more biologically active than D3 per unit weight?

It is approximately 4-5 times more biologically active, and recommended at 2,760-5,529 IU/kg where it partly or fully replaces D3.

26. Uniformity is calculated as the percentage of birds:

Weigh 30 randomly caught birds, find the average, and count how many sit within 10% either side. 80% or more is a uniform flock.

27. Production falling while feed intake in grams stays constant most likely indicates:

If the bird is still eating the same grams, the problem is in what those grams contain - concentration, premix potency or calcium level.

28. 2,296 eggs from 400 hens over 7 days is a hen-day production of:

2,296 divided by (400 x 7 = 2,800) equals 0.82, or 82% hen-day production.

29. When sampling birds for weighing you should:

Easy-to-catch birds are disproportionately slow or weak, which biases the flock average downward and can send you chasing a problem that does not exist.

30. Production steady but the percentage of cracked and thin eggs rising points to:

Shell defects with unchanged egg numbers point at calcium amount and form, vitamin D3 potency, or the age-related shift to a 25:75 fine-to-coarse split and 4.7 g of daily calcium.

Module 3 capstone

Build a full production and cost record for your own laying flock over one calendar month. Week 1: weigh 30 randomly caught hens, calculate the flock average and the percentage falling within plus or minus 10% of it, and compare against the Lohmann 17-week target of 1.42 kg or the appropriate age target. Record your daily feed intake per bird in grams and your hen-day production percentage every day. Week 2: collect 30 eggs across the day, weigh them, and record any that are thin, cracked or misshapen as a percentage. Week 3: analyse your layer feed against the Hy-Line peak figures - 3.8-4.1% calcium, about 4.0 g calcium per bird per day, 0.92% SID lysine at 90 g intake - and check the fine to coarse limestone split by hand-sieving a sample. Week 4: price your feed per kilogram, calculate kilograms of feed per dozen eggs against the Hy-Line benchmark of 1.23-1.28 kg/dozen to 60 weeks, and work out your feed cost per dozen and your margin per dozen at this month's local egg price. Finish with three ranked corrections and their dollar cost.
