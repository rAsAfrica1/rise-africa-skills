Lessons

1. Corner Joints: Crimping, Screwing, and Welding

Corner joints are the backbone of any aluminum window or door. In Africa, where power can be unreliable, most workshops use mechanical joints: crimping and screwing. Crimping is fast and strong for standard windows. You need a crimping tool (manual or pneumatic), a hammer, and a steel block. First, cut your aluminum profiles at 45 degrees using a mitre saw. Clean the cut ends with a file. Insert a corner cleat (usually aluminum or plastic) into both profile ends. Then, using the crimping tool, press the outer walls of the profile into the cleat. Do this on both sides of the joint. For a stronger joint, add two self-tapping screws (usually 4.8mm x 25mm) through the profile into the cleat. Screwing alone is common for doors and heavy windows. Pre-drill with a 3.5mm bit to avoid cracking the aluminum. Welding aluminum is rare in small workshops because it requires a TIG welder and argon gas, which is expensive. But for high-end projects, welding gives a seamless finish. If you weld, use 4043 filler rod and clean the joint with a stainless steel brush before welding. After welding, grind the joint smooth. For most African workshops, crimping and screwing are the go-to methods. Always check the joint for squareness using a carpenter's square. A good corner joint should have no gaps and be perfectly 90 degrees. Test by trying to twist the frame; if it moves, tighten or add more screws. Remember, a weak corner will cause the whole window to sag, leading to problems with opening and closing.

2. Using Cleats and Corner Connectors

Cleats and corner connectors are the hidden heroes of aluminum assembly. They hold the frame together and keep it square. There are three main types: corner cleats (also called corner keys), T-connectors, and mullion connectors. For standard windows, you use a corner cleat that fits inside the hollow profile. These come in different sizes, so match the cleat to your profile's internal dimensions. In Africa, you'll find cleats made of aluminum, nylon, or zinc alloy. Nylon is cheap but can break under heavy load; aluminum is stronger. To install, cut your profiles at 45 degrees, then slide the cleat into both ends. You may need to tap it gently with a rubber mallet. Once in, crimp or screw as described in Lesson 1. For doors, use a heavier cleat with a screw port. T-connectors join a vertical and horizontal profile at 90 degrees, like where a window mullion meets the frame. These often have a flat plate that screws into both profiles. Mullion connectors are used when you have multiple windows side by side. They allow you to join two frames without a visible gap. Always apply a small amount of silicone sealant inside the joint before assembly to prevent water leaks. After joining, check for alignment: the faces of the profiles should be flush. If not, loosen and adjust. A common mistake is using a cleat that's too small, which leads to a loose joint. Always buy cleats from a reputable supplier and keep them dry to avoid corrosion. In coastal areas, use stainless steel screws to prevent rust.

3. Applying Sealants and Gaskets

Sealants and gaskets keep water and air out of your aluminum windows and doors. In Africa, heavy rain and dust make this step critical. You need two main products: silicone sealant and rubber gaskets (also called weatherstrips). Silicone sealant comes in cartridges and is applied with a caulking gun. Use neutral-cure silicone, not acetic-cure (which smells like vinegar and can corrode aluminum). Before applying, clean the surfaces with a cloth and alcohol to remove dust and oil. Cut the nozzle at a 45-degree angle to control the bead. For corner joints, apply a bead of silicone inside the joint before assembling, then another bead on the outside after assembly. Smooth it with a wet finger or a plastic spatula. Gaskets are rubber strips that fit into grooves on the profile. They come in different shapes: EPDM, silicone, or PVC. EPDM is best for African sun because it resists UV. To install, cut the gasket slightly longer than needed, then press it into the groove with a roller or a flat screwdriver. Don't stretch it; let it sit for a few minutes to relax. For sliding windows, use a fin gasket on the frame and a brush gasket on the sash. For casement windows, use a bubble gasket on the frame. After installation, test by spraying water on the window from a hose. If water leaks inside, add more sealant or replace the gasket. Remember, a good seal saves energy and prevents mold. In dusty areas, check gaskets every six months and replace if cracked.

4. Assembling Sliding Window Frames

Sliding windows are the most common in African homes because they save space and allow good ventilation. Assembling them requires precision. Start by cutting your frame profiles: the outer frame (top, bottom, and sides) and the sash profiles (the moving panels). Use a mitre saw set at 45 degrees for the outer frame corners. For the bottom track, you need a profile with a raised rail to guide the rollers. Assemble the outer frame first using corner cleats and screws. Check that the frame is square by measuring diagonals; they must be equal. Next, assemble the sashes. Each sash is a rectangle made of four profiles joined at 45 degrees. Use a smaller cleat for sashes. Install rollers on the bottom of each sash; typically two rollers per sash, positioned at the ends. The rollers should be adjustable so you can raise or lower the sash later. Then, slide the sashes into the frame. The inner sash goes in first, then the outer sash. Make sure the sashes slide smoothly. If they stick, adjust the rollers or check for debris in the track. Install a top guide and a bottom track cover. Finally, add a locking mechanism (a latch or a crescent lock) on the meeting stiles. For security, use a keyed lock. Test the window by sliding it back and forth. It should move with one finger. If it's too tight, the frame might be twisted; loosen the corner screws and re-square. In coastal areas, use stainless steel rollers to prevent rust.

5. Assembling Casement Window Frames

Casement windows open outward like a door. They are popular in Africa for their tight seal against rain and wind. Assembly starts with the outer frame, which is a rectangle joined at 45 degrees with cleats and screws. The frame must be perfectly square. Next, assemble the sash (the opening part). The sash is also a rectangle, but it has a rebate to hold the glass. Cut the sash profiles at 45 degrees and join with cleats. Then, install the hinges. Casement hinges are usually friction stays (also called restrictors) that allow the window to open to different angles. Mark the hinge positions on the frame and sash: typically, hinges go 100mm from the top and bottom of the sash. Drill holes and screw the hinges in place. Make sure the sash opens and closes smoothly. If it rubs, adjust the hinge arms. Next, install the handle and locking mechanism on the opposite side. For security, use a multi-point lock. Then, install the glass. Casement windows use a glazing bead (a small profile that snaps into the sash) to hold the glass. Apply a bead of silicone around the glass before snapping the bead in place. Finally, check the seal: close the window and look for gaps. Adjust the hinges or add weatherstrip if needed. In hot climates, consider adding a trickle vent for airflow. Test the window by opening and closing it several times. It should stay open at any angle and close tightly.

6. Assembling Hinged and Sliding Door Frames

Door frames are bigger and heavier than window frames, so they need stronger joints. For hinged doors, the outer frame is assembled with corner cleats and screws, but you must use larger cleats and more screws. The frame is usually fixed to the wall with anchor bolts. The door leaf (the moving part) is assembled similarly, but it needs a reinforced corner joint because it will carry the weight of the door. Use a corner bracket or a heavy-duty cleat. For hinged doors, install three hinges: top, middle, and bottom. The middle hinge carries most of the weight. Use stainless steel hinges for coastal areas. For sliding doors, the assembly is different. The outer frame has a top track and a bottom track. The bottom track must be strong enough to support the door's weight, so use a heavy-duty profile with a stainless steel rail. The door panels are assembled like sliding window sashes but larger. Install rollers on the bottom of each panel; use at least two rollers per panel, rated for the door's weight. For heavy doors, use four rollers. The top of the panel has guides that run in the top track. After assembly, adjust the rollers so the door slides smoothly and closes flush with the frame. Install a lock on the leading edge. For security, use a mortise lock or a hook lock. Test the door by sliding it back and forth; it should not wobble. If it does, adjust the rollers or add a floor guide. Always check that the frame is level and plumb before fixing to the wall.

<h2>Practical Reference: Assembly and Joinery Techniques for Aluminium Windows &amp; Doors</h2>

<p>This practical reference supports the RISE AFRICA Skills module <strong>Assembly and Joinery Techniques</strong> within the course <strong>Aluminium Windows &amp; Doors</strong>. It is written for workshop owners, fabricators, apprentices, and technical trainers who need to assemble frames accurately, reduce rework, and protect margin. In many African markets, aluminium fabrication is a high-competition, low-margin business. A frame that leaks, rattles, or fails on site can wipe out the profit from the whole job. The techniques below focus on repeatable quality: correct corner joints, proper cleats and connectors, sealant discipline, and frame-specific assembly logic for sliding windows, casement windows, hinged doors, and sliding doors.</p>

<p>Use this as a bench-side guide. It assumes you already have cutting, machining, and glazing capability and now need to turn cut profiles into square, watertight, and durable assemblies that installers can fit quickly.</p>

<h2>1. Corner Joints: Crimping, Screwing, and Welding</h2>

<p>Corner joints are the backbone of every aluminium frame. The three common methods in African fabrication shops are crimping, screwing, and welding. Each has a business implication: crimping is fast and low-cost but depends on correct cleat and die selection; screwing is flexible and repairable but slower and more visible; welding gives the strongest, cleanest joint but requires capital equipment and skilled operators. Choose based on product type, client expectation, and volume.</p>

<p><strong>Crimping</strong> uses a corner crimping machine to push the outer walls of the profile into a cleat or corner connector. It is the standard for high-volume window production. The critical variables are cut accuracy, cleat type, and crimping pressure. If the mitre is not exactly 45, the crimper will close the gap visually but leave internal stress that shows up as a cracked corner or water leak later.</p>

<p><strong>Screwing</strong> is common in smaller workshops and for on-site repairs. Self-drilling screws or machine screws pass through the profile into a corner connector or aluminium block. It is strong enough for many residential applications if pilot holes are correct and screws are not over-torqued. The risk is galvanic corrosion if steel screws are used with aluminium in coastal or humid regions. Use stainless steel or aluminium-specific fasteners.</p>

<p><strong>Welding</strong> (TIG or MIG for aluminium) produces a continuous joint and is used for high-end architectural work, security frames, and heavy doors. It requires cleaning, correct filler wire, and post-weld finishing. In many African markets, welding capacity is a differentiator for premium contracts such as banks, hotels, and embassies. The business case is higher price per unit, not higher volume.</p>

<h2>2. Using Cleats and Corner Connectors</h2>

<p>Cleats and corner connectors transfer load across the mitre. They are not optional accessories; they are structural. In Africa, where many profiles are imported from China, Turkey, or South Africa, the connector must match the profile series exactly. A loose connector will allow the frame to twist during glazing and transport. A connector that is too tight can crack the profile during crimping.</p>

<p>Best practice is to dry-fit the connector, check that the mitre closes without force, and confirm that the connector sits below the glazing line. For sliding windows, the connector must not interfere with the track. For doors, use heavier connectors or corner blocks because the door leaf carries dynamic load from opening and closing.</p>

<h2>3. Applying Sealants and Gaskets</h2>

<p>Sealants and gaskets are where profit leaks. A frame that is assembled square but sealed poorly will generate callbacks during the rainy season. In coastal cities such as Mombasa, Dar es Salaam, Lagos, and Durban, salt and humidity accelerate sealant failure. Inland high-UV regions such as Nairobi, Kampala, and Gaborone, sunlight degrades cheap gaskets quickly.</p>

<p>Use neutral-cure silicone or MS polymer sealant compatible with aluminium and any powder coating. Avoid acid-cure silicone on bare aluminium because it can corrode the surface. Gaskets should be EPDM or silicone, not cheap PVC, for long-life performance. When assembling sliding windows, the gasket must allow the sash to move without binding. When assembling casement windows, the gasket must compress evenly against the frame when the window is closed.</p>

Follow the manufacturer safety data sheet (SDS) for every chemical on the bench.</p>

<h2>4. Assembling Sliding Window Frames</h2>

<p>Sliding windows are the volume product in most African residential projects. The assembly sequence matters: assemble the outer frame first, check diagonals, then install the track and interlock. The frame must be square before the track is fixed, otherwise the sashes will bind or rattle. Use a flat assembly table or a clean concrete floor with timber battens. Never assemble on uneven ground.</p>

<p>After crimping or screwing the corners, measure both diagonals. They should be within 2 mm for a standard window. If not, adjust before proceeding. Install the bottom track with drainage slots clear. Apply sealant at the corner where the track meets the frame, but do not block the drainage path. Fit the sashes, check the overlap, and adjust the rollers. A well-assembled sliding window should open with one finger and close without lifting.</p>

<h2>5. Assembling Casement Window Frames</h2>

<p>Casement windows are outward or inward opening. They require accurate corner joints because the sash carries wind load. The frame must be square and the sash must be square. Use corner connectors that match the profile and, for larger sashes, consider a welded or mechanically reinforced corner. Fit the hinges after the frame is assembled and squared. Check that the sash sits flush against the gasket around the full perimeter.</p>

<p>In high-wind coastal areas, casement sashes need additional fixing points. Friction stays or restrictors should be installed according to the manufacturer's instructions. If the sash is too heavy for the hinge, the window will drop and leak. This is a common failure in multi-storey buildings in Lagos and Nairobi. Calculate sash weight before choosing hinges.</p>

<h2>6. Assembling Hinged and Sliding Door Frames</h2>

<p>Doors are the highest-stress assemblies. A hinged door frame must resist the leverage of the leaf, especially if the door is heavy or used frequently. Use heavier corner connectors, and consider welding or reinforcing the bottom corners. The frame must be anchored to the structure at multiple points, not just at the corners. If the frame twists after installation, the door will drag.</p>

<p>Sliding door frames require a level track and a strong bottom rail. The track must be fixed to a solid substrate, not just to a thin floor finish. In many African projects, sliding doors fail because the track is fixed to a weak screed. Use mechanical fixings into the structural slab. Check that the rollers are rated for the leaf weight, and leave a service gap for adjustment.</p>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>
<ul>
<li>Confirm profile series, cleat, and connector match before cutting.</li>
<li>Check mitre angle and cut length; reject any profile with visible burrs or distortion.</li>
<li>Dry-fit the corner joint and confirm the mitre closes without force.</li>
<li>Measure both diagonals after assembly; correct before fixing track or hinges.</li>
<li>Use stainless steel or aluminium-specific fasteners; never mix steel screws with aluminium in wet or coastal areas.</li>
<li>Apply sealant and gaskets according to the system manual; keep drainage paths clear.</li>
<li>Check sash or leaf movement by hand before glazing and before delivery.</li>
<li>Record batch, profile supplier, and assembler for every frame to trace defects.</li>
</ul>
