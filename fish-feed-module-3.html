Lesson 3.1~11 min

Fingerling Feed Requirements

In this lesson
  • State fingerling protein, lipid, energy and mineral targets for tilapia and African catfish
  • Select the correct pellet size and form as fish move from 2 g to 30 g
  • Calculate the cost per fingerling of feeding to specification

The fingerling stage, roughly from a few grams to about 50 g, is where a farm either builds a fast, uniform, healthy batch or quietly wastes half a season. Feed conversion is at its best here and never gets better, so this is the stage to spend properly.

The targets. Nile tilapia fingerlings need 40-45 percent crude protein; African catfish fingerlings 40-45 percent; common carp fingerlings 35-42 percent; channel catfish fingerlings 35-40 percent. Lipid runs 8-12 percent for tilapia fingerlings with 0.8-1.5 percent EPA plus DHA, and 7-10 percent for African catfish with 0.5-1.0 percent. Digestible energy for a channel catfish fingerling diet sits at 3,000-3,300 kcal/kg giving a protein-to-energy ratio of 8.5-9.5 kcal per gram of protein; African catfish sit at 3,200-3,400 kcal/kg. Phosphorus is 0.6-0.8 percent for tilapia and 0.6-0.7 percent for African catfish, calcium 0.8-1.2 percent for tilapia, with a calcium to phosphorus ratio of 1.5-2.0 to 1. Vitamin C stays at 100-200 mg/kg and should be a stabilised form.

Amino acids still bind the result. Tilapia lysine falls from 1.8-2.0 percent as fry towards 1.2-1.5 percent at grow-out, so a fingerling diet sits between; African catfish need 1.6-1.8 percent lysine and 0.8-0.9 percent methionine plus cystine. Arginine at 1.4-1.6 percent for African catfish is higher than any land animal needs and is easy to miss in a maize-heavy mix.

Pellet size runs on a schedule you should write on the store wall. From two to eight weeks the mouth is 1-2 mm and the fish takes 1.5-3 mm crumble. From eight to twelve weeks the mouth is 2-3 mm and the fish takes 2-4 mm, often a 50:50 crumble and pellet mix. From twelve to twenty-four weeks the mouth is 3-5 mm and the fish takes 3-6 mm standard pellet, with under 5 percent waste to sorting when the size is right. Changing size a week early costs intake; changing a month late costs growth to sorting waste.

Floating or sinking matters here too. Floating pellet lets you watch the fish and judge appetite, which is genuinely valuable at fingerling stage, but 15-25 percent goes uneaten against 5-10 percent for sinking pellet, and floating feed costs 5-15 percent more to make. Sinking feed suits bottom-feeding catfish and carp; the reference notes that catfish juveniles are often fed a feed that floats initially and sinks after satiation. In deep tanks and raceways, sinking is preferred because surface-congregating fish deplete oxygen at the top.

Worked example. Blessing raises tilapia fingerlings near Kariba, Zimbabwe. She stocks 10,000 fry at 2 g and grows them to 30 g. Total weight gain is 10,000 x 0.028 kg = 280 kg. At a fingerling FCR of 1.4, in the middle of the 1.3-1.5 band for tilapia fingerlings at 27-30C, she needs 280 x 1.4 = 392 kg of feed. A 42 percent protein crumble at USD 1.05/kg costs 392 x 1.05 = USD 411.60. Divided across 10,000 fish that is USD 0.041 per fingerling in feed, before mortality. If she loses 10 percent, the cost per surviving fingerling rises to USD 0.046. Against a market of USD 0.03-0.10 per fingerling, she has a business only if she keeps survival high and FCR near 1.4. If she cuts corners and buys a 30 percent protein grow-out feed at USD 0.75/kg to save money, FCR drifts towards 1.8, feed needed rises to 504 kg, cost is USD 378, barely cheaper, and the fish take longer and come out uneven. The cheap feed did not save her money; it cost her time.

The general rule at this stage is that protein quality buys FCR, and FCR is the whole economics of the stage. Buy the amino acid balance, not the headline protein number.

Lesson 3.2~11 min

Juvenile Feed Formulation

In this lesson
  • Build a juvenile diet from locally available African ingredients
  • Balance amino acids using soy, canola and a small fish meal or insect share
  • Compare farm-made and commercial juvenile feed on cost per kg of gain

Juveniles, roughly 50 g to 200 g, are where feed volume starts to be serious money. A tonne of feed at this stage is a real purchase, and it is the stage where a farmer who can mix competently earns a genuine margin over one who cannot.

The targets loosen slightly from fingerling stage. Nile tilapia juveniles need 30-40 percent crude protein, African catfish 35-40 percent, common carp 30-36 percent and channel catfish 32-35 percent. The reference is blunt that at this stage protein quality matters more than quantity. Digestible energy sits around 3,000-3,300 kcal/kg for tilapia and channel catfish and 3,200-3,400 for African catfish, and the protein-to-energy ratio should land at 8-10 kcal per gram of protein. Lipid runs 5-8 percent for tilapia and 7-10 percent for African catfish.

Now the ingredients an African mixer actually has. Soybean meal at 44-48 percent protein, 1.6-1.8 percent lysine, 0.4-0.5 percent methionine, 75-85 percent digestibility, USD 0.30-0.50/kg. Canola meal at 36-40 percent protein, 1.2-1.4 percent lysine, 0.5-0.6 percent methionine, 70-80 percent digestibility, USD 0.40-0.60/kg. Cottonseed meal 40-45 percent protein, USD 0.35-0.50/kg. Sunflower meal 38-42 percent protein, USD 0.40-0.60/kg. Maize at USD 0.18-0.25/kg. Rice bran at 11-13 percent protein and 15-18 percent lipid for USD 0.12-0.18/kg, useful as an energy filler at 5-10 percent inclusion but rancid within two to three weeks unless stabilised. Moringa leaf meal at 18-25 percent protein, free or USD 0.10-0.20/kg if dried locally, with good amino acid balance but 70-75 percent digestibility and enough fibre that it should stay under 10 percent of feed protein; trials found fish on 10 percent moringa grew comparably to a full soy control. Black soldier fly larvae meal at 40-45 percent protein and USD 1.00-1.50/kg.

The complementarity trick is worth learning. Soybean meal is lysine-rich and methionine-poor; canola is the reverse pattern, lysine-poor with similar methionine. A blend of 60 percent soybean meal, 30 percent canola and 10 percent fish meal in the protein fraction gives a balanced amino acid profile at 20-25 percent lower cost than a 50:50 soy and fish meal diet. Add DL-methionine at 0.2-0.3 percent on top.

Worked example, a 1,000 kg tilapia juvenile diet targeting 34 percent protein, mixed near Mbeya, Tanzania. Soybean meal 400 kg at USD 0.42 = USD 168, protein contribution 400 x 0.46 = 184 kg. Canola meal 150 kg at USD 0.50 = USD 75, contributing 150 x 0.38 = 57 kg. Local artisanal fish meal 80 kg at USD 1.20 = USD 96, contributing 80 x 0.55 = 44 kg. Maize 200 kg at USD 0.22 = USD 44, contributing 200 x 0.09 = 18 kg. Rice bran 80 kg at USD 0.15 = USD 12, contributing 80 x 0.12 = 9.6 kg. Soybean oil 40 kg at USD 1.00 = USD 40. Vitamin premix 13 kg and mineral premix 30 kg, together about USD 80. DL-methionine 3 kg at USD 5 = USD 15 and L-lysine 4 kg at USD 3 = USD 12. Total protein 312.6 kg in 1,000 kg, so 31.3 percent, just inside the 30-40 percent band but at the bottom; lifting fish meal to 100 kg and cutting maize to 180 kg gets closer to 33 percent. Total cost is USD 542, or USD 0.54/kg, against a commercial juvenile pellet at USD 0.80-1.20/kg. On 1,000 kg that is a saving of USD 260-660.

But count the costs the spreadsheet hides. Hand mixing and small-scale pelleting add 20-30 percent in indirect labour cost. A cold-pressed pellet with raw starch loses 10-20 percent on FCR and falls apart in the water. So the saving is real only if you gelatinise the starch, bind the pellet and value your own time honestly. The reference's cost-reduction roadmap is a sensible order: buy premixes and hand-mix first for a 15-20 percent saving with no capital, ferment plant ingredients next for another 5-10 percent on USD 50-100 of containers, then consider a USD 500-1,500 pellet mill, and join a cooperative for bulk buying, which alone gives 10-15 percent for no cash at all.

Lesson 3.3~11 min

Feeding Strategy and Growth Optimization

In this lesson
  • Set a daily ration as a percentage of body weight for your species, size and temperature
  • Split that ration into a feeding schedule that suits your labour and oxygen supply
  • Recalculate the ration from sample weights every two weeks

A feed bag does not grow fish. A ration schedule does. The three decisions are how much, how often and when, and all three change as the fish grow and the water changes.

How much, first. Feeding rate is expressed as a percentage of standing body weight per day and falls as fish get bigger and as water gets colder. For tilapia at 27-30C the reference gives 2-3 percent at low density, 3-4 percent at medium density and 4-5 percent at high density, noting that at high density you should reduce the satiation level by about 20 percent because crowding stresses the fish. At 20-25C tilapia rates drop to 1-2 percent low density, 2-3 percent medium and 2-3.5 percent high. Channel catfish at 25-28C take 1.5-2.5 percent low, 2.5-3.5 percent medium and 3-4 percent high density, and the guidance is explicit that catfish have less appetite than tilapia and you should underestimate rather than overestimate. At 15-20C catfish drop to 1-2.5 percent. Carp at 20-25C sit at 1.5-2.5 percent low, 2.5-3.5 percent medium and 3-4 percent high.

How often. In extensive ponds, manual broadcast once or twice daily with 15-25 percent waste and FCR 2.5-3.5. Semi-intensive, two to three times daily to satiation with 10-15 percent waste and FCR 1.8-2.2. Intensive, three to four times daily on a strict schedule with a spreader, 5-10 percent waste and FCR 1.5-1.8. Tanks, two to four times daily with an observer, 5-8 percent waste and FCR 1.4-1.7. The pattern is that more frequent, more observed feeding buys better conversion. In a stage where FCR runs 1.4-1.9, a 0.2 improvement on a tonne of feed is worth roughly 100 kg of extra fish.

When. Oxygen decides this. Feeding to satiation raises oxygen use 30-40 percent, and high-protein feeds raise post-feeding demand 50-100 percent. In low-aeration systems, under about 1 kW per 100 cubic metres of pond, reduce feeding 20-30 percent at dawn and after sunset and put the main ration in the middle of the day. In intensive ponds at 50-150 kg/ha, cut 15-20 percent at dawn and dusk and feed the full ration at midday.

The worked ration example from the reference is worth copying exactly. Take 1,000 tilapia averaging 50 g, so 50 kg of standing biomass, at 30C. Target 3.5 percent of body weight a day, which is 1.75 kg of feed. Split it into five equal feeds of 0.35 kg at 06:00, 10:00, 14:00, 17:00 and 18:00. Then apply the adjustment rules: if ammonia goes above 0.1 mg/L or dissolved oxygen falls below 5 mg/L, reduce 20-30 percent; if water temperature falls below 20C, reduce by half; if the fish look diseased, reduce 30-50 percent.

Worked example on money. Esther farms tilapia at Lake Bosomtwe, Ghana with 4,000 juveniles averaging 90 g, so 360 kg standing biomass, at 28C and medium density. At 3.5 percent she feeds 12.6 kg a day at USD 0.78/kg, USD 9.83. Two weeks later she samples 30 fish and finds the average is 118 g, so biomass is 472 kg and the ration should be 16.5 kg a day. Had she not resampled, she would have kept feeding 12.6 kg, underfeeding by 3.9 kg a day, roughly 24 percent, and her fish would have grown slower than her feed budget assumed. The opposite error is worse: farmers who never resample after a mortality event keep feeding for fish that are no longer there, and pay for it in ammonia. Resample every two weeks and recalculate. It takes twenty minutes.

Demand feeders deserve a mention. Field trials report FCR improving from 1.5 to 1.3, growth up substantially and production cycles shortened by about four weeks. Equipment costs USD 500-2,000 per feeder and the payback on a small pond is one to two years, so they suit intensive farms and rarely make sense below 5 kg/ha.

Disease Prevention Through Nutrition

In this lesson
  • Set vitamin C and E levels for fingerlings around stocking, grading and transport
  • Apply a stress-feeding protocol before and after handling
  • Recognise nutritional deficiency signs that are commonly mistaken for infection

Nutrition will not cure a disease outbreak. What it does is decide how much stress a fingerling can absorb before an ordinary pathogen becomes an outbreak. Module 10 of this course covers fish health, diagnosis and treatment in full; this lesson stays narrowly on the fingerling stage, where handling stress is concentrated and where the nutritional levers are cheap and specific.

Vitamin C is the first lever. Requirement is 100-200 mg/kg of feed, and fish store none of it, so supply must be continuous. Fish fed adequate vitamin C show roughly 50 percent fewer infections. Deficiency shows as poor growth, haemorrhages and rising infection rates. The catch is stability: plain ascorbic acid loses 50-90 percent of potency in two to four weeks at ambient tropical temperature. Ascorbyl phosphate retains 70-95 percent at six months for 20-30 percent more cost, ascorbyl palmitate 60-90 percent for 30-40 percent more, and the total impact on feed price is only USD 0.02-0.05/kg. Around stocking and grading, work at the top of the range rather than the bottom.

Vitamin E is the second. Requirement is 100-200 mg/kg, rising to 200-400 mg/kg where lipid is above 10 percent or EPA plus DHA above 1 percent, at USD 0.02-0.10/kg. Deficiency shows as muscular dystrophy and increased disease. Vitamin E and vitamin C work together: E protects membranes from oxidation and C regenerates E, so a feed short of one wastes the other.

Other nutritional signs worth recognising before you reach for a treatment. Lysine deficiency causes fin erosion and gill hyperplasia, which looks exactly like bacterial fin rot. Methionine deficiency causes cataracts and loss of pigment. Tryptophan deficiency causes bent spines, scoliosis and lordosis, often blamed on genetics. Riboflavin deficiency causes eye cataracts and gill erosion. Vitamin A deficiency causes eye opacity and gill damage. Threonine deficiency causes anaemia and gut damage with reduced disease resistance. Vitamin D3 deficiency, which fish cannot correct with sunlight, gives soft skeletons in fry. If a whole batch shows the same sign at the same time and no fish are dying acutely, suspect the feed before you suspect a pathogen.

The stress-feeding protocol around handling. Grading, counting and transport all mean netting, crowding and air exposure, and a fish that has just been handled has raised oxygen demand and a suppressed immune response. Practical steps: stop feeding for a day before you handle so the gut is empty, which cuts oxygen demand and reduces fouling in transport water; add salt at 1-2 g/L to holding and transport water, which also halves nitrite toxicity; resume feeding at a reduced rate the day after handling and build back over two to three days rather than going straight to full ration; and if fish already show disease signs, cut the ration 30-50 percent while you diagnose, because a sick fish that will not eat turns full rations into ammonia.

Worked example. Isaac grades 30,000 tilapia fingerlings at Mpanda, Tanzania. His feed is USD 1.00/kg, standing biomass 600 kg, normal ration 3.5 percent, so 21 kg a day. He upgrades to a stabilised vitamin C version for the two weeks either side of grading, adding USD 0.04/kg. Over 28 days at 21 kg a day that is 588 kg of feed and USD 23.52 of extra cost. His usual post-grading mortality is 6 percent, which is 1,800 fish. If the better vitamin status and the stress-feeding protocol cut that to 3 percent, he saves 900 fingerlings. At USD 0.06 each that is USD 54, more than double the cost, and he also avoids the growth check that the surviving fish would have suffered.

The honest summary is that vitamins C and E, a day of fasting before handling, and salt in the transport water are among the cheapest interventions available to a fish farmer, and they matter most in exactly the two weeks around grading and stocking.

Lesson 3.5~11 min

Water Quality Management at High Stocking

In this lesson
  • Estimate the nitrogen your daily ration adds at your stocking density
  • Decide whether to reduce protein, reduce ration or increase exchange
  • Recognise where published stocking bands sit relative to real African practice

At high stocking density, feed and water stop being two subjects. Every extra kilogram of feed is extra nitrogen in a volume that is already carrying more fish than it comfortably should. Module 9 deals with aeration, exchange and monitoring equipment; this lesson deals only with how the ration must change when density is high.

Start with the honest part. The published bands describe extensive ponds at 1-5 kg/ha, semi-intensive at 5-50 kg/ha, intensive at 50-150 kg/ha, static tanks at 100-200 kg per cubic metre and RAS at 200-500 kg per cubic metre or more. Many African farms carry standing crops far above the pond bands. A quarter-hectare pond holding 3,000 tilapia at 250 g is 750 kg on 0.25 ha, which is 3,000 kg/ha, twenty times the top of the intensive band. Farmers do this and some of them succeed, but they succeed by aerating, exchanging water frequently and monitoring daily, and the ones who do not do those things lose fish in the first hot, still week of the season. Treat the published bands as the level at which the system is forgiving, not as a legal limit, and be clear with yourself about which side of that line you are on.

The nitrogen arithmetic. About 66-72 percent of fed protein leaves as nitrogen. A standing crop of 100 kg of tilapia or African catfish on 28-32 percent protein feed puts out 50-80 g of nitrogen a day; channel catfish and carp on 25-30 percent protein put out 40-60 g. Scale that. A 750 kg standing crop of tilapia is producing 375-600 g of nitrogen a day into whatever water you have. Safe unionised ammonia for tilapia and African catfish is below 0.05 mg/L, and for channel catfish and carp below 0.1 mg/L. At 0.1 mg/L tilapia lose 25-30 percent of growth and FCR worsens 5-10 percent. High density by itself also costs conversion: above about 100 fish per cubic metre in tilapia, FCR worsens 10-15 percent through stress and competition.

You have three levers and they are not equally good. Reducing dietary protein by 5 percentage points cuts ammonia output 15-20 percent and is nearly free if you keep amino acids balanced; that is the first thing to try, and it is why matching protein to life stage matters so much at high density. Improving digestibility above 80 percent cuts both faecal nitrogen and uneaten feed, which needs better ingredients and cooked starch. Increasing exchange or aeration works but costs water, fuel and capital. Reducing the ration works instantly and always, and costs growth.

The standing rules at intensive density: 25-40 percent water exchange two to three times a week, daily oxygen monitoring, reduce the ration 15-20 percent at dawn and dusk and feed full at midday, and cut 20-30 percent whenever ammonia is above 0.1 mg/L or oxygen below 5 mg/L. At ultra-intensive density in tanks and raceways, cut 10-15 percent whenever oxygen drops below 5 mg/L or ammonia exceeds 0.1 mg/L, and remember that a biofilter is destroyed by overfeeding faster than by anything else.

Worked example. Daniel runs tank culture of African catfish at Mwea, Kenya: four tanks of 8 cubic metres, 900 kg of fish in total, on a 32 percent protein feed at 2.5 percent, so 22.5 kg a day at USD 0.85/kg, USD 19.13. His nitrogen output is roughly 9 x 65 g = 585 g a day. He drops to a 28 percent protein feed of equal amino acid quality, cutting nitrogen output by about 15 percent to 500 g a day, and the cheaper feed costs USD 0.76/kg, saving USD 2.03 a day, USD 60 a month. African catfish grow-out sits at 28-35 percent protein so 28 percent is inside the band, though at the bottom. The gain is double: less ammonia to remove and less money spent. The risk is that if his amino acid balance is not maintained, growth falls and he loses more than he saved, which is exactly why lysine and methionine supplementation at USD 0.02-0.05/kg is the companion decision.

Lesson 3.6~12 min

Grading and Uniformity

In this lesson
  • Explain how feeding practice creates or prevents size spread in a batch
  • Plan a grading schedule that fits pellet size changes and market windows
  • Value uniformity in USD at your own farm gate price

Two ponds can carry the same total kilograms and be worth very different money. One holds fish that are all close to 400 g and can be sold in a single lot at a single price. The other holds a spread from 150 g to 700 g, where the small fish are unsaleable, the large fish are past their best conversion, and the buyer discounts the lot. Uniformity is a nutrition outcome before it is a handling one.

How feeding creates spread. First, particle size. When the pellet is at the top of the acceptable range, the largest fish take it comfortably and the smallest struggle, so the gap widens every day. Feeding a 50:50 mix of crumble and pellet at the 8-12 week transition is the standard answer. Second, feeding frequency and distribution. One large feed in one corner of the pond is taken by the fish that get there first, which are the biggest. Three or four feeds spread across the water surface give the smaller fish repeated chances. Third, ration level. Underfeeding always widens the spread because the dominant fish take their full share first; the shortfall falls entirely on the smallest.

There is a species dimension. African catfish are strongly cannibalistic when size differences open up, which is why catfish hatcheries grade early and often. Tilapia are less cannibalistic but reproduce in the pond, so a mixed-sex grow-out fills with unwanted small fish that eat feed and produce ammonia without ever reaching market size. Module 11 covers seed production and sex control; the feeding consequence is that every recruit in your pond is eating feed you budgeted for saleable fish.

Grading schedule. Tie it to your pellet size steps, because you are handling the fish anyway and because each new size suits a narrower weight range. A workable pattern is to grade once at the crumble-to-pellet transition around 8-12 weeks, once more in mid-juvenile phase, and once before the final grow-out pellet. Each grading is a stress event, so apply the protocol from Lesson 4: fast for a day beforehand, salt at 1-2 g/L in holding water, resume feeding at a reduced rate and build back over two to three days, and use feed at the top of the vitamin C range for the fortnight either side.

Worked example. Aisha harvests 2,500 tilapia at Kafue, Zambia, total 900 kg, so an average of 360 g. Her buyer pays USD 1.80/kg for fish over 300 g and USD 1.10/kg for anything smaller, which he takes reluctantly. In an ungraded batch, suppose 30 percent of the fish, 750 fish, are under 300 g and weigh on average 220 g, so 165 kg. The remaining 1,750 fish average 420 g, so 735 kg. Revenue is 735 x 1.80 plus 165 x 1.10 = USD 1,323 plus USD 181.50 = USD 1,504.50. Now suppose grading and better feed distribution during juvenile phase cut the undersized share to 12 percent, 300 fish at 260 g, so 78 kg, and 2,200 fish at 373 g, so 822 kg. Revenue is 822 x 1.80 plus 78 x 1.10 = USD 1,479.60 plus USD 85.80 = USD 1,565.40. That is USD 60.90 more from the same total weight of fish, plus the option to hold the small fish over to the next cycle rather than dumping them. Across four ponds and two cycles a year, roughly USD 490.

A practical measurement. Weigh 30 fish individually rather than in bulk once per grading, and note the heaviest and the lightest. If the heaviest is more than twice the lightest, your batch is ragged and you should grade. If you can calculate it, a coefficient of variation, being the standard deviation divided by the mean, is the professional version of the same check, and watching it fall over a season is the clearest evidence that your feeding practice has improved.

One last honest note. Grading costs fish. Every handling event risks mortality, and a farm that grades too often on a poorly aerated pond will lose more to handling than it gains in price. Grade with purpose, at the transitions you were going to handle anyway, and never in the hottest part of the day.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. Crude protein for a tilapia fingerling diet should be:

Fingerlings need 40-45 percent, between the 50-56 percent fry level and the 25-32 percent grow-out level.

2. Typical FCR for tilapia fingerlings at 27-30C is:

1.3-1.5, the best conversion of the whole production cycle, which is why this stage rewards good feed.

3. Uneaten feed with floating pellets typically runs:

Floating pellets waste 15-25 percent against 5-10 percent for sinking, though they let you observe appetite.

4. Growing 10,000 fingerlings from 2 g to 30 g at FCR 1.4 requires about:

Gain is 280 kg; 280 x 1.4 = 392 kg of feed.

5. The optimal calcium to phosphorus ratio for tilapia is:

1.5-2.0 to 1, wider than in mammals because fish also absorb calcium directly from the water.

6. Crude protein for a tilapia juvenile diet should be:

Juvenile tilapia need 30-40 percent, with protein quality mattering more than the headline number.

7. Soybean meal and canola meal complement each other because:

Blending them balances the amino acid profile and cuts cost 20-25 percent against a soy and fish meal diet.

8. Moringa leaf meal should be limited to about:

It is only 70-75 percent digestible and fibrous, so it works as a partial substitute below 10 percent of feed protein.

9. Hand mixing and small-scale pelleting add roughly what indirect cost?

Labour for hand mixing and small-scale pelleting adds 20-30 percent, which must be counted against the ingredient saving.

10. Which step gives 10-15 percent feed cost reduction for almost no cash?

Cooperative bulk buying costs organisation rather than capital and delivers 10-15 percent immediately.

11. A medium-density tilapia pond at 28C should be fed about:

3-4 percent at medium density and 27-30C; the rate falls to 2-3 percent at 20-25C.

12. 1,000 tilapia averaging 50 g fed at 3.5 percent of body weight need:

50 kg standing biomass x 0.035 = 1.75 kg of feed per day, best split into four or five feeds.

13. In a low-aeration pond, the ration should be reduced 20-30 percent:

Oxygen is lowest at dawn and drops again after sunset; the main ration belongs in the middle of the day.

14. How often should you resample and recalculate the ration?

Fish gain weight fast at this stage; a fortnightly 30-fish sample keeps the ration matched to real biomass.

15. Demand feeders in trials improved FCR from:

Reported FCR improvement was 1.5 to 1.3, about 13 percent, with the production cycle shortened by roughly four weeks.

16. Why must vitamin C be supplied continuously in fish feed?

Vitamin C is water soluble and not stored, so a gap in supply shows up quickly as poor growth and infection.

17. Fin erosion and gill hyperplasia across a whole batch may indicate a deficiency of:

Lysine deficiency causes fin erosion and gill hyperplasia, which is easily mistaken for bacterial fin rot.

18. Before netting fish for grading you should:

An empty gut lowers oxygen demand and reduces fouling of transport water, cutting handling losses.

19. Salt at 1-2 g/L in transport water helps mainly by:

Chloride competes with nitrite at the gill, roughly halving its toxicity, and eases osmoregulatory stress.

20. Cutting post-grading mortality from 6 to 3 percent on 30,000 fingerlings worth USD 0.06 saves:

900 saved fish x USD 0.06 = USD 54, against about USD 24 for the upgraded vitamin C.

21. The published stocking band for an intensive earthen pond is:

50-150 kg/ha. Many African ponds run well above this, but only with aeration, exchange and daily monitoring.

22. Cutting dietary protein 5 percentage points reduces nitrogen output by about:

15-20 percent, and it is nearly free provided lysine and methionine are kept balanced.

23. Stocking above about 100 tilapia per cubic metre typically worsens FCR by:

Crowding stress and competition for feed cost 10-15 percent in conversion efficiency.

24. A 900 kg standing crop of African catfish produces roughly how much nitrogen daily?

At 50-80 g per 100 kg of fish, nine hundred kilograms produces roughly 450-720 g of nitrogen a day.

25. In a recirculating system, the fastest way to destroy the biofilter is:

Biofilters are sensitive to nutrient overload; overfeeding destabilises nitrification faster than anything else.

26. Underfeeding a pond widens size spread because:

The largest fish feed first and fully, so any shortfall is borne entirely by the smallest fish.

27. Which species is most strongly cannibalistic when size differences open up?

African catfish are strongly cannibalistic, which is why catfish hatcheries grade early and often.

28. A simple field test that a batch needs grading is:

A more than twofold spread in a 30-fish sample means the batch is ragged and grading is overdue.

29. Grading should preferably be scheduled:

Each handling event costs fish, so grade at the transitions you were going to handle anyway, and not in the heat of the day.

30. Selling 822 kg at USD 1.80 and 78 kg at USD 1.10 gives revenue of about:

822 x 1.80 = USD 1,479.60 plus 78 x 1.10 = USD 85.80, giving USD 1,565.40.

Module 3 capstone

Take one fingerling batch through a full juvenile phase and produce a performance sheet for it. Record stocking number, stocking date, average weight from a 30-fish sample, and the feed you use with its protein percentage, pellet size and price per kg in USD from a real supplier. Weigh a 30-fish sample every two weeks and record total feed given between samples so you can calculate FCR for each period as feed divided by weight gain. Grade the batch at least once, count and weigh each grade, and calculate the coefficient of variation as standard deviation divided by mean, or if that is not practical, record the weight of the largest and smallest fish in your sample. Track dissolved oxygen at dawn twice a week and ammonia weekly, noting the ration beside each. Close with one page stating: FCR achieved per period, the pellet sizes used and when you changed, your grading outcome, your worst water reading and what you had fed, cost of feed per kg of fish produced, and three changes for the next batch priced in USD.
