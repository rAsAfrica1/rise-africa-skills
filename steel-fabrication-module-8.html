Lessons

1. Grinding and Surface Preparation

Before you paint or coat steel, you must prepare the surface. Dirt, rust, mill scale, and oil will make any finish fail. Start by inspecting the steel. Look for rust (red or brown), mill scale (dark grey flaky layer), weld spatter, and sharp edges. Use a wire brush or scraper to remove loose rust and dirt. For heavier rust, use a chipping hammer. Then, use an angle grinder with a grinding wheel (typically 115mm or 230mm diameter) to grind down welds, smooth edges, and remove mill scale. Hold the grinder at a 15-30 degree angle and move it steadily. Don't press too hard; let the tool do the work. For corners and tight spots, use a die grinder or a file. After grinding, switch to a sanding disc (40-80 grit) to smooth the surface. Finally, clean the surface with a solvent like acetone or methylated spirits to remove oil and dust. In Africa, where humidity and heat can be high, work in the shade and avoid leaving bare steel overnight. A properly prepared surface should look uniformly grey or white metal, with no shiny spots (which indicate oil) and no dark patches (rust). This step takes time but ensures your coating lasts for years.

2. Shot Blasting and Sandblasting

Shot blasting and sandblasting are methods to clean steel by firing abrasive particles at high speed. Sandblasting uses silica sand, while shot blasting uses steel shot or grit. Both remove rust, mill scale, and old paint, leaving a rough profile that helps coatings stick. In Africa, sandblasting is common but dangerous: silica dust causes silicosis. Always wear a respirator, goggles, and heavy gloves. Work outdoors or in a well-ventilated area. For small jobs, a portable sandblaster (like a pressure pot) works with a compressor delivering at least 100 PSI. Hold the nozzle 300-500mm from the surface and move in steady passes. For large structures, use a shot blasting machine with a closed cabinet or a centrifugal wheel system. After blasting, the steel should be a uniform grey-white. Blast to SA 2.5 or SA 3 standard (near-white or white metal). Immediately apply a primer within 4 hours to prevent flash rust, especially in humid coastal areas like Mombasa or Lagos. If you don't have equipment, you can hire a mobile blasting service. Remember: never use sand from a river or beach without testing for salt; salt causes rust. Use clean, dry abrasive. Safety first: no blasting near people or animals.

3. Painting and Coating Systems

Painting steel is not just about colour; it's about protection. A good coating system has three parts: primer, intermediate coat, and topcoat. Primer sticks to the steel and prevents rust. Use a zinc-rich primer for harsh environments, or an epoxy primer for general use. Apply by brush, roller, or spray. For brush, use a 50mm or 75mm natural bristle brush. For spray, use a conventional or airless sprayer. Mix paint thoroughly and thin only as recommended. Apply the primer at 75-100 microns dry film thickness (DFT). Let it dry (usually 4-6 hours at 25C). Then apply an intermediate coat, often epoxy or polyurethane, at 100-150 microns DFT. Finally, the topcoat (polyurethane or enamel) gives colour and UV resistance, at 50-75 microns DFT. Total DFT should be 200-300 microns. In Africa, use paints that resist heat and humidity. Avoid painting in direct sun or when humidity is above 85%. Check with a wet film gauge and dry film gauge. Clean tools with thinner. Store paint away from heat. If you see runs, sags, or brush marks, sand and recoat. A good paint job lasts 5-10 years.

4. Galvanizing and Metalizing

Galvanizing coats steel with zinc to prevent rust. There are two main types: hot-dip galvanizing and metalizing (also called zinc spraying). Hot-dip galvanizing involves dipping prepared steel into molten zinc at about 450C. The zinc reacts with the steel to form a tough alloy layer. This is done in a factory, so you must send your steel to a galvanizing plant. The coating is typically 50-100 microns thick and lasts 20-50 years, even in coastal areas. For large structures like transmission towers or boat trailers, hot-dip is best. Metalizing, on the other hand, can be done on-site. You use a wire arc or flame spray gun to melt zinc wire and spray it onto the steel. The surface must be blasted to SA 2.5 first. The zinc coating is 100-200 microns thick, but it's porous, so you must seal it with a thin coat of epoxy or polyurethane. Metalizing is good for repairs or for very large objects that can't be dipped. In Africa, hot-dip galvanizing plants are in major cities like Johannesburg, Nairobi, and Lagos. Metalizing equipment is portable but expensive. Both methods require safety gear: respirator, gloves, and face shield. Never weld galvanized steel without removing the zinc first; the fumes are toxic.

5. Powder Coating

Powder coating is a dry finishing process. Instead of liquid paint, you spray electrostatically charged powder onto a grounded metal part. The powder sticks, then you bake it in an oven at 180-200C for 10-20 minutes. The powder melts and forms a smooth, hard film. Powder coating is tougher than wet paint and comes in many colours. It's great for furniture, fencing, and automotive parts. To powder coat, first clean and pretreat the steel. Blast or phosphate the surface. Then hang the part on a conveyor or rack. Use a powder spray gun (electrostatic) to apply the powder. Aim for 60-100 microns thickness. Too thin and you'll see bare spots; too thick and it may orange-peel. After spraying, cure in an oven. In Africa, powder coating is common in cities like Cairo, Durban, and Accra. Small shops use batch ovens; large factories use conveyor lines. If you don't have an oven, you can't powder coat properly. Some try to use a heat gun, but results are poor. Safety: wear a dust mask and avoid breathing powder. Keep the area clean; powder is flammable. Powder coating is not good for parts that will be welded later; the coating will burn. Also, it's hard to touch up; you need to re-coat the whole part.

6. Quality Inspection of Finishes

After finishing, you must inspect the coating to ensure it will protect the steel. Start with a visual check. Look for runs, sags, drips, brush marks, holidays (missed spots), and foreign particles. The colour should be uniform. For thickness, use a dry film thickness (DFT) gauge. Take 5-10 readings per square meter. The average should meet the spec, and no single reading should be less than 80% of the spec. For example, if spec is 200 microns, no reading below 160 microns. For adhesion, use a cross-hatch or pull-off test. Cross-hatch: cut a grid with a knife, apply tape, and pull. If paint comes off, adhesion is poor. Pull-off: glue a dolly, pull with a gauge, and note the force. For galvanizing, check thickness with a magnetic gauge and look for bare spots. For powder coating, check for orange peel, pinholes, and colour match. Also check for cure: wipe with MEK (methyl ethyl ketone) if the coating softens, it's undercured. Document all results. In Africa, humidity and heat can affect curing, so test often. If a coating fails, you must remove it and redo. Quality inspection saves money in the long run.

<h2>Practical Reference: Finishing and Surface Treatment for Steel Fabrication</h2>

<p>In steel fabrication, finishing is not cosmetic it is the difference between a product that lasts three rainy seasons and one that lasts twenty years. Across Africa, where humidity, coastal salt, harmattan dust, and intense UV radiation punish unprotected steel, surface treatment is a core business function. A fabricator in Mombasa, Tema, or Dar es Salaam who masters finishing wins contracts in construction, marine, mining, and agro-processing. One who does not loses them to corrosion, rework, and reputation damage.</p>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the six lessons in this module into a working field guide: grinding and surface preparation, shot blasting and sandblasting, painting and coating systems, galvanizing and metalizing, powder coating, and quality inspection. It is written for the workshop owner, supervisor, and technician who needs to quote accurately, train staff, and deliver a finish that passes inspection the first time.</p>

<h3>Why Surface Preparation Determines Everything</h3>

<p>Industry data consistently shows that over 70% of premature coating failures trace back to inadequate surface preparation, not to the paint itself. In African workshops, the three most common preparation failures are: (1) skipping degreasing before blasting, (2) blasting to the wrong profile, and (3) applying coatings on damp steel during the rainy season. Each is preventable with discipline and a few inexpensive tools.</p>

<p>Grinding and mechanical preparation (wire brushing, needle gunning) is acceptable for small repairs and welds, but it rarely achieves the cleanliness required for long-life systems. For structural steel exposed to C4 or C5 environments coastal Kenya, Nigeria's Niger Delta, South African coastal zones abrasive blasting to Sa 2 is the baseline. For galvanizing, ISO 1461 requires thorough cleaning and, in some cases, a specific surface condition; powder coating demands a chemical pretreatment or blast profile that the powder can mechanically key into.</p>

<h3>Choosing the Right Finishing Route</h3>

<p>The finishing method must match the service environment, the client's specification, and the shop's capability. A fabricator without a blast booth can still win work by partnering with a blasting subcontractor or by specifying hand-tool cleaning for low-exposure jobs. A fabricator with a galvanizing line can command premium pricing for transmission towers, agricultural equipment, and water infrastructure.</p>

<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0;font-weight:bold;color:#c0392b"> SAFETY WARNING Blasting and Coating Hazards</p>
<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0">Silica sand blasting is a serious health hazard. Respirable crystalline silica causes silicosis, an incurable lung disease. In many African countries, dry sandblasting is restricted or banned. Use only approved abrasives (garnet, steel grit, aluminium oxide) and always wear a supplied-air respirator not a dust mask. Shot blasting and sandblasting also generate sparks, static, and high noise levels. Painting and coating involve flammable solvents, isocyanates, and heavy metals (zinc, lead in older paints). Galvanizing kettles operate above 445C with molten zinc; metalizing uses arc or flame spray at extreme temperatures.</p>
<p style="margin:0">Mandatory controls: supplied-air respirators for blasters and sprayers, full face shields, hearing protection above 85 dB, flame-retardant coveralls, local exhaust ventilation in paint booths, and a no-smoking zone within 15 m of any solvent or molten metal. Train every operator on the Safety Data Sheet (SDS) for each product before first use.</p>

<h3>Quality Inspection: The Business Case</h3>

<p>Inspection is not an overhead it is the evidence that justifies your price. A client who sees a calibrated DFT gauge, a surface profile comparator, and a signed inspection report knows they are buying a system, not a guess. The most common inspection failures in African fabrication shops are: coating too thin (to save material), coating too thick (leading to cracking), missed welds and edges, and inadequate cure before handling.</p>

<p>Core inspection tools and their use:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Surface profile comparator</strong> (ISO 8503) verifies blast profile before coating.</li>
<li><strong>Dry film thickness (DFT) gauge</strong> (ISO 2808) magnetic or eddy-current; measure 5 points per 10 m minimum.</li>
<li><strong>Wet film thickness gauge</strong> checks application rate in real time.</li>
<li><strong>Adhesion test</strong> (ISO 4624 pull-off or ISO 2409 cross-cut) confirms bond to substrate.</li>
<li><strong>Holiday / pinhole detector</strong> for immersion and pipeline coatings.</li>
<li><strong>Salt contamination test</strong> (ISO 8502-6) critical for coastal and offshore work.</li>
</ul>

<p>For galvanizing, inspection follows ISO 1461: coating thickness, appearance, adhesion, and freedom from defects. For powder coating, check film thickness, gloss, adhesion, and cure (solvent rub or MEK test). For metalizing, check bond strength and porosity.</p>

<h3>Africa-Specific Considerations</h3>

<p><strong>Climate:</strong> High humidity and temperature swings affect cure and adhesion. In West Africa's coastal belt, steel can flash-rust within hours of blasting. Best practice: blast and coat the same day, and use dehumidification where possible. In the Sahel, UV degradation of topcoats is the dominant failure mode specify UV-stable polyurethane or acrylic topcoats.</p>

<p><strong>Supply chain:</strong> Imported coatings and abrasives carry freight, duty, and long lead times. Build stock buffers for critical projects. Local alternatives such as garnet from South Africa or steel grit from regional suppliers can reduce cost and lead time.</p>

<p><strong>Skills:</strong> Certified blasters, sprayers, and inspectors are scarce. Invest in training and retain trained staff. The rise AFRICA skills framework encourages documented competency and portable certification.</p>

<p><strong>Standards and clients:</strong> Major clients mining houses, oil and gas, government infrastructure increasingly require ISO 12944, ISO 1461, or NACE/AMPP compliance. Being able to document your process is a competitive advantage.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Confirm exposure category</strong> (C1C5-M or Im) and client specification before quoting.</li>
<li><strong>Degrease and remove salts</strong> before any abrasive blasting; test for chloride if coastal.</li>
<li><strong>Verify blast profile</strong> with a comparator; target Sa 2 and 4075 m for most systems.</li>
<li><strong>Check ambient conditions</strong> steel temperature must be at least 3C above dew point; humidity per product data sheet.</li>
<li><strong>Measure DFT</strong> at 5 points per 10 m; record and compare to specification.</li>
<li><strong>Inspect edges, welds, and corners</strong> these fail first; stripe coat before full coat.</li>
<li><strong>Allow full cure</strong> before handling, stacking, or transport; follow the data sheet.</li>
<li><strong>Document everything</strong> batch numbers, conditions, readings, photos and issue an inspection report with delivery.</li>
</ol>
