1. Brooder Setup and Temperature Requirements

A brooder is a warm, dry, draft-free space for ducklings from day-old to about 3 weeks old. In Africa, you can use a simple wooden box, a plastic crate, or a corner of a room enclosed with cardboard. The floor should be covered with absorbent bedding like wood shavings or dry grass. Never use slippery surfaces like newspaper alone as it can cause leg damage. The brooder must have a heat source. A 100-watt bulb hung 45 cm above the floor works for 10-15 ducklings. For larger groups, use a 250-watt infrared lamp. In rural areas without electricity, use a charcoal stove (jiko) placed outside the brooder with a metal pipe to carry heat inside, or fill plastic bottles with hot water and wrap them in cloth. Always ensure the heat source is secure and cannot be knocked over. Temperature is critical. Week 1: 32-35C. Week 2: 29-32C. Week 3: 24-27C. Week 4: 21-24C. After that, they can do without extra heat if nights are warm. Measure temperature with a thermometer placed at duckling level. If no thermometer, watch the ducklings: if they huddle directly under the heat, they are cold; if they spread to the corners, they are too hot; if they move freely and sleep spread out, the temperature is just right. Adjust by raising or lowering the bulb or opening vents. Provide a draft guard using sacks or cardboard around the brooder base, but allow air to enter near the top. Always give ducklings enough space: 0.5 square feet per bird in week 1, increasing to 1 square foot by week 3. Overcrowding leads to wet bedding, disease, and stress. Keep the brooder clean and dry. Check ducklings at least 4 times a day for the first week, especially at night. In hot African climates, brooders may only need heat at night after the first week. Always have a backup heat source in case of power cuts.

2. Feeding and Watering Ducklings

Ducklings need constant access to clean water and feed from day one. Use shallow water containerslike a lid or a shallow bowlso they can drink without getting soaked. Ducklings love to splash, so place waterers on a raised wire mesh or a shallow tray with pebbles to prevent wet bedding. Change water at least 3 times a day, more if dirty. For feed, start with a commercial duck starter crumble (20-22% protein). If unavailable, mix your own: 2 parts maize meal, 1 part soybean meal, 1 part fishmeal, plus 1% mineral and vitamin premix. Add finely chopped greens like amaranth, cabbage, or duckweed from day 3. From week 2, you can also offer chopped hard-boiled eggs or termites as a protein boost. Feed ducklings freely (ad libitum) for the first 2 weeks. After that, feed 4 times a day: morning, midday, afternoon, and evening. A rough guide: each duckling eats about 30g of feed per day in week 1, 60g in week 2, 100g in week 3, and 150g in week 4. Always provide grit (crushed charcoal or small stones) from week 2 to help digestion. Never feed mouldy or stale feed. Store feed in a dry, airtight container. In Africa, many farmers use fermented feed (like silage) to improve gut healthmix feed with water and let it ferment for 24 hours before feeding. This also reduces waste. Ducklings also need niacin for strong legs. If using chick starter, add 1 tablespoon of brewer's yeast per 10 ducklings daily, or add crushed groundnut leaves. Signs of niacin deficiency: bowed legs, reluctance to walk. Provide water 24/7. Ducklings can drown in deep water, so use shallow dishes. In hot weather, add electrolytes (a pinch of salt and sugar in 1 litre of water) to prevent dehydration. Always wash waterers and feeders daily with soap and clean water to prevent disease.

3. Bedding and Cleanliness for Ducklings

Ducklings produce a lot of wet droppings, so bedding must absorb moisture and be changed often. Good bedding materials include wood shavings (not sawdustit can cause respiratory problems), chopped straw, dry grass, rice husks, or shredded paper. Avoid sand or soil as they can cause impaction if eaten. Spread bedding 5-8 cm thick. For the first week, change bedding daily. After that, you can change every 2-3 days if you stir it and remove wet spots daily. Always remove wet bedding immediatelydampness leads to chilling, respiratory disease, and foot problems. A simple rule: if you can squeeze water out of the bedding, it is too wet. In humid African regions, you may need to change bedding twice a day. Keep the brooder well-ventilated but draft-free. Use a wire mesh cover on top to keep out rats and snakes. Clean feeders and waterers daily with warm soapy water, then rinse and dry. Once a week, scrub the entire brooder with a disinfectant like bleach solution (1 part bleach to 10 parts water). Rinse thoroughly and let dry before adding new bedding. Remove any dead birds immediately and dispose of them far from the brooder. Wash your hands before and after handling ducklings. If you notice a bad smell, it means bedding is dirty or ventilation is poorfix it at once. For outdoor pens, use a deep litter system: start with 10 cm of bedding, add more as needed, and clean out completely every 2-3 months. This builds beneficial bacteria. In Africa, many farmers use raised wire floors to let droppings fall through, but this can hurt ducklings' feet. A solid floor with good bedding is better. Always keep a footbath with disinfectant at the entrance of the brooder to prevent bringing in disease. Cleanliness is the cheapest medicine you can give your ducklings.

4. Introducing Ducklings to Water

Ducklings can swim from day one, but they must not get chilled. In nature, mother duck leads them to water, but in a brooder, you must introduce water carefully. For the first 3 days, provide only shallow water for drinkinga lid or a shallow dish with pebbles. Do not let them swim yet; their down is not waterproof and they can become soaked and die of cold. From day 4, you can offer a shallow pan of warm water (25-30C) for 5-10 minutes, supervised. The water should be no deeper than 2-3 cm. After swimming, immediately return them to the brooder to dry off under the heat. Never let them swim in cold water or in a draft. In Africa, use water from a clean sourcewell, borehole, or rainwater. Avoid stagnant water that may contain parasites. From week 2, you can allow longer swims (15-20 minutes) once a day if the weather is warm. Always provide a ramp or shallow edge so they can get out easily. Ducklings can drown if exhausted. After swimming, they will preen and spread oil from their preen glandthis makes them waterproof. Do not wash them; they need to do it themselves. If a duckling gets soaked and chilled, dry it with a towel and put it under the heat lamp immediately. In hot climates, swimming helps them cool down, but always supervise. By week 4, they can have access to a small pond or basin for most of the day, but ensure they have a dry area to rest. Never use chlorinated water for swimming; it can irritate their eyes. If you have a natural pond, check for predators like snakes, monitor lizards, or large fish. Fence the area. Introduce ducklings to water gradually and always provide a warm, dry place to return to.

5. Common Duckling Health Issues

The most common duckling diseases are duck viral hepatitis, duck plague, and aspergillosis. But with good management, you can prevent most problems. Watch for these signs: dullness, ruffled feathers, loss of appetite, watery or bloody droppings, coughing, sneezing, and sudden deaths. Early action saves lives. Duck viral hepatitis affects ducklings under 3 weeks. It spreads fast and kills up to 90%. There is no cure, but you can vaccinate breeding ducks and give ducklings a vaccine at day 1 if available. In Africa, vaccine may not be easy to get, so focus on biosecurity: keep wild birds out, disinfect boots and equipment, and isolate new birds. Duck plague (duck enteritis) causes diarrhoea, dehydration, and high mortality. Vaccinate if possible. Aspergillosis is a fungal infection from mouldy feed or bedding. Prevent by storing feed dry and changing bedding often. Symptoms: gasping, rapid breathing. Remove affected ducklings and improve ventilation. Another common issue is niacin deficiency (bowed legs). Add brewer's yeast or crushed groundnut leaves to feed. Splayed leg can also be caused by slippery floorsuse good bedding. Coccidiosis causes bloody droppings; treat with amprolium or sulpha drugs in water. Always consult a vet if unsure. Parasites: worms and lice. Deworm every 2-3 months with piperazine or levamisole. For lice, dust with wood ash or diatomaceous earth. In hot climates, heat stress can kill ducklings. Provide shade, cool water, and ventilation. In cold, they chill easilykeep brooder warm. Always isolate sick ducklings immediately. Use a separate hospital box with heat and easy access to water and feed. Disinfect after handling sick birds. Keep records of any illness and treatment. A healthy duckling is active, has bright eyes, clean vent, and eats well. Check your flock at least twice a day. If you see any problem, act fast.

6. Transitioning Ducklings to Outdoor Housing

Ducklings can move outdoors at 4-6 weeks old, once they are fully feathered and the weather is warm. In Africa, if nights are above 20C, you can move them at 4 weeks. If colder, wait until 6 weeks. Before moving, prepare the outdoor pen. It should have a dry, sheltered area with bedding, and an open area for foraging. Fence with wire mesh buried 30 cm deep to prevent digging predators. Provide a pond or water trough for swimming, but also a dry resting area. The pen should be secure from dogs, cats, snakes, and thieves. Introduce ducklings to the outdoors gradually. Start by taking them out for 1-2 hours on a warm day, supervised. Increase time over a week. At night, bring them back to the brooder for the first few days. Then leave them out if the night temperature is above 18C. Always provide the same feed and water they are used to. Moving is stressful, so add vitamins or electrolytes to water for 3 days. Watch for bullyingolder ducks may peck at younger ones. If mixing with adults, do it slowly and provide hiding places. For the first week outdoors, check them often, especially at night. Make sure they can get back into shelter if it rains. In rainy season, provide a raised dry area. Ducks are hardy, but sudden changes can cause illness. Keep the outdoor pen clean: remove wet bedding, change water daily. Provide shade with a simple roof of palm fronds or corrugated iron. In hot climates, ensure plenty of shade and cool water. In cold, provide a windbreak. After 8 weeks, they can be considered adults. Continue to feed a grower ration (16-18% protein) until they start laying or are sold. Transitioning is a critical stepdo it slowly and watch your ducks closely.

<h2>Practical Reference: Duckling Care &amp; Rearing in African Smallholder and Commercial Systems</h2>

<p>Ducklings are the highest-risk, highest-return stage of any duck enterprise. A brooder that loses 15% of a 200-duckling batch to chilling, wet bedding, or contaminated water wipes out the margin on the surviving birds. In much of Africa from Kiambu County, Kenya to Ogun State, Nigeria and the Volta Region of Ghana duck production is dominated by Muscovy, Pekin, and Khaki Campbell crosses kept under low-input conditions. This reference consolidates the practical thresholds you need to hit, the numbers behind them, and the business consequences of missing them.</p>

<p>Treat this module as your operating manual: brooder temperature and setup, feeding and watering, bedding and hygiene, first-water introduction, disease triage, and the transition to outdoor housing. Every figure below is drawn from FAO, university extension, or industry sources you can verify yourself.</p>

<h3>1. Brooder Setup and Temperature Requirements</h3>

<p>Ducklings cannot thermoregulate for roughly the first 1418 days. The brooder must deliver a temperature gradient a warm zone and a cooler zone so birds self-regulate. The classic rule is 3032C in week one, dropping about 3C per week until ambient temperature is reached around week 45.</p>

<p>In off-grid African settings, a charcoal <em>jiko</em> or kerosene lantern inside a guarded ring works, but the temperature must be measured not guessed. A cheap digital thermometer placed at duckling back-height (about 5 cm above litter) is the single best investment you will make in this module.</p>

<h3>2. Feeding and Watering Ducklings</h3>

<p>Ducklings should be on feed and water within 24 hours of hatch. Use a shallow waterer (a jar lid or chick fount with marbles) for the first 35 days to prevent drowning and wetting. Starter feed should be 1820% crude protein for meat breeds and 1618% for layers. Never feed medicated chick starter containing amprolium or similar coccidiostats intended for chickens ducks consume far more feed and can overdose.</p>

<p>Always provide grit (crushed limestone or shell) after week 2 if you supplement with kitchen scraps or greens. Ducklings on pasture or free-range need grit to digest fibrous material.</p>

<h3>3. Bedding and Cleanliness</h3>

<p>Ducklings produce wet droppings far wetter than chicks. Bedding must be absorbent, changed frequently, and never allowed to cake. Wet litter is the number one cause of respiratory disease and chilling in African brooder houses.</p>

<p>Use wood shavings, rice husks, or chopped maize stover. Avoid sawdust (dusty, respiratory risk) and avoid newspaper alone (slippery, causes splayed legs). In humid coastal West Africa, add a thin layer of agricultural lime under bedding to suppress ammonia.</p>

<h3>4. Introducing Ducklings to Water</h3>

<p>Ducklings are waterfowl, but they are not waterproof until about 34 weeks when the preen gland matures. Before that, they chill rapidly if soaked. Introduce water in a shallow dish (12 cm deep) from day 2, supervised, for 510 minutes at a time. Never leave a deep water source unattended before week 4.</p>

<p>From week 4, provide a shallow "training pool" a basin 1015 cm deep for 1520 minutes per day. Full pond access should wait until week 68, when plumage is fully waterproofed and the bird can preen effectively.</p>

<h3>5. Common Duckling Health Issues</h3>

<p>The five conditions that kill African ducklings most often are: <strong>chilling</strong>, <strong>wet feather / pneumonia</strong>, <strong>coccidiosis</strong>, <strong>botulism</strong> (from stagnant water), and <strong>niacin deficiency</strong> (leg weakness). Niacin is critical ducks need 23 the niacin of chickens. If you feed chick starter, supplement with brewer's yeast or a niacin premix.</p>

<p>Botulism is a real risk where ducklings access stagnant, rotting water. Never let them drink from standing puddles, especially in warm weather.</p>

<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0;font-weight:bold;color:#b71c1c;font-size:1.05em"> SAFETY WARNING Botulism and Water Quality</p>
<p style="margin:0;color:#4e1a1a">Never allow ducklings to drink from stagnant ponds, puddles, or water that has held dead plant material for more than 24 hours. <em>Clostridium botulinum</em> toxin causes rapid paralysis and death, often within 1224 hours. If you see flaccid paralysis, drooping neck, or difficulty breathing, remove all birds from the water source immediately and replace with clean, chlorinated water. There is no practical on-farm treatment prevention is the only control. In West African and East African smallholder systems, this is the single most common cause of sudden duckling mortality after week 3.</p>
</div>

<h3>6. Transitioning Ducklings to Outdoor Housing</h3>

<p>Move ducklings outdoors at 46 weeks if night temperatures stay above 18C. In highland areas (e.g., Kenya's Rift Valley, Ethiopia, Rwanda), wait until week 68. The transition should be gradual: 2 hours outdoors on day 1, increasing by 2 hours daily over 710 days.</p>

<p>Outdoor housing must provide: shade, predator-proof fencing (especially against mongoose, snakes, and wild birds), a shallow drinking trough, and a dry sleeping area with 0.30.5 m per bird. Pekin and Muscovy need more space than Khaki Campbell. For a commercial flock of 100 birds, plan 4050 m of run plus 1520 m of shelter.</p>

<p>Business note: the cost of a well-built brooder and outdoor shelter is recovered within one cycle if mortality stays under 5%. A poorly managed transition typically costs 1020% of the flock a direct hit to your gross margin.</p>
