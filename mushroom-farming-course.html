Mushroom Farming

A complete 12-week program — two full oyster-mushroom cycles from boiling drum to standing orders. No land, no rain, under $200 to start; hygiene is the whole game.

⏱️ 3 Months (two grow cycles)📊 Beginner → Business-ready🌍 Hybrid: Online + Offline🎓 Certificate on completion

Course Overview

Why 3 months / two grow cycles? Mushrooms are the fastest crop in this academy: oyster mushrooms go from bagging to first harvest in 4–5 weeks, then flush again every 7–10 days. Three months lets you run TWO complete cycles — the first to learn, the second to profit from your corrections. You need no land, no rain and almost no capital: a dark room, chopped straw, and discipline about cleanliness.

Structure: 6 modules → lessons → practical assignments → a 10-question quiz each module → final business project.

How to study (hybrid): Online — modules on your phone, sessions in the Study Diary, questions to the rAs AI assistant. Offline — save this page (Print → Save as PDF); the practicals happen at the boiling drum and in the grow room.

⚠️ Read first: the three rules that protect your investment

  • Mushroom farming is a hygiene business wearing a farming costume. The green mould you can't see today is the ruined bag next week. Wash hands, tools and room — every single time.
  • Buy spawn ONLY from a reputable laboratory/supplier. Old, warm-stored or contaminated spawn is the #1 cause of failed first crops. Fresh spawn is white, smells pleasantly of mushroom, and was kept cool.
  • Never sell wild-picked mushrooms alongside your farmed ones, and never guess at wild species — poisonings destroy the market for every grower in a district. You sell cultivated oyster mushrooms, full stop.
MODULE 1 (Weeks 1–2) — Fungi Science, Species & the Market ▾

Lesson 1.1 — What a mushroom actually is

  • The mushroom you eat is only the FRUIT. The organism is the mycelium — a white thread network that digests the substrate (straw, sawdust) from inside. Your whole job is: feed the mycelium, protect it from competitors, then shock it into fruiting.
  • Fungi don't photosynthesise — no sunlight needed for growth, which is why a spare room, shed or thatched hut outproduces a field.
  • The lifecycle you manage: spawn (seed) → colonisation/incubation (white takeover of the bag, 2–4 weeks, dark) → pinning (baby mushrooms) → fruiting (harvest flushes) → spent substrate (compost/animal feed).

Lesson 1.2 — Choose your species

SpeciesDifficultyWhy / why not
Oyster (Pleurotus spp.)EASIEST — this course's trackGrows on cheap straw/agri-waste, tolerant, fast (4–5 wks), heat-tolerant strains exist for Africa
Button (Agaricus)HardNeeds composted manure substrate + casing soil + cool rooms — a factory crop, not a starter
ShiitakeMedium-hardHardwood logs/sawdust, months to fruit — premium but slow; a year-2 addition
  • Within oysters: grey/pearl oyster (fast, productive), pink oyster (loves heat, stunning market colour, short shelf life), king oyster (premium, fussier). Start grey/pearl.

Lesson 1.3 — The market before the mushroom

  • Fresh oyster mushrooms retail at $3–8/kg in most African towns — a meat-priced vegetable with almost no local competition and rising health demand.
  • Buyers: supermarkets and greengrocers (consistency wins shelf space), restaurants/lodges (weekly standing orders), health-conscious households via WhatsApp groups, and vegetarians/church communities (big repeat buyers).
  • Dried mushrooms at 10:1 concentration sell for $20–40/kg, never spoil, and post anywhere — your no-refrigeration insurance policy.

Lesson 1.4 — Sourcing spawn (the seed of the business)

  • Spawn = sterilised grain colonised with pure mycelium, from a laboratory. Find suppliers via agriculture colleges, mushroom farmer groups and agro-dealers; order fresh, transport cool, use within 2–4 weeks (fridge-stored).
  • Spawn rate: 5–10% of WET substrate weight — 1 kg of spawn plants 10–20 kg of prepared substrate ≈ 4–7 bags.
  • Advanced growers later make their own spawn (a sterile-lab skill) — buy it for your first year.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Module 1): (1) Survey 3 mushroom buyers: current source, price/kg, weekly volume they'd take — in writing. (2) Locate 2 spawn suppliers with prices and freshness policy. (3) Inventory free substrate within reach: maize stover, wheat/grass straw, cotton waste, bean trash — weigh what you can gather in a day.

MODULE 2 (Weeks 2–3) — The Grow Room & Equipment ▾

Lesson 2.1 — Two rooms (or one room, two phases)

  • Incubation space: dark, 20–28°C, still air — a cupboard, corner or shelf-lined room where bags sit undisturbed while mycelium colonises.
  • Fruiting room: indirect light (reading-light level), high humidity (80–90%), fresh air exchange, 18–28°C for common oyster strains — a room with shade-cloth windows, a wet floor and hanging space.
  • Small growers run one room and change conditions by phase; committed growers split them so cycles overlap continuously.

Lesson 2.2 — Building the fruiting room cheaply

  • Any mud-brick, thatch or block room works; thatch and earth floors actually hold humidity beautifully.
  • Humidity kit: wet the floor and walls 2–3× daily, hang wet sacks, or a hand mister — evaporation does the work; a wet charcoal wall panel is the deluxe village version.
  • Ventilation: opposite low+high vents with mosquito-net screens (insects carry contaminants and lay eggs in mushrooms); stale CO₂-rich air = long stems, tiny caps.
  • Hanging systems: bags hung from roof poles on wires, or stacked on slatted shelves — hanging uses air on all sides and beats shelf crowding.

Lesson 2.4 — Hygiene protocol (the real crop protection)

  • Clean room BEFORE every cycle: sweep, wash walls/floor with soap then dilute bleach/disinfectant, dry, THEN bring in bags.
  • People rules: washed hands (or gloves), no entering after handling soil/compost/animals, sick visitors admire from the door.
  • The one-way rule: contaminated bags leave immediately and are emptied FAR from the grow room (never opened inside — green mould sheds millions of spores).

MODULE 3 (Weeks 3–4) — Substrate, Pasteurisation & Spawning ▾

Lesson 3.1 — Substrate: the mushroom's food

  • Best oyster substrates: chopped wheat/grass straw, maize stover, cotton seed hulls/waste (a champion), bean trash, dry banana leaves — clean, dry, mould-free material chopped to 2–5 cm.
  • Boosters (optional): 5–10% wheat/maize bran raises yields but also contamination risk — add only once your hygiene is proven.
  • Never use: mouldy/rained-on material, sawdust from treated timber, anything sprayed recently with fungicide (it kills your crop — it's a fungus).

Lesson 3.2 — Pasteurisation (killing the competition)

  • Purpose: kill competitor moulds and pests in the substrate while leaving it hospitable for your spawn.
  • Hot-water method (the standard): submerge chopped substrate in water at 60–80°C for about 1 hour (drum over fire; weigh it down; thermometer, not guesswork). Below 60°C competitors survive; a rolling boil for hours wastes fuel and nutrients.
  • Alternatives: drum steaming (sacks of substrate over boiling water, 1–2 h), or cold lime-water soak (builder's lime, ~1%, 12–18 h) where fuel is scarce.
  • Drain on the clean tarpaulin to 65–70% moisture — the squeeze test: a hard-squeezed handful yields only a few drops between the fingers. Streaming water = too wet (rot); no drops and springy = too dry (stalled mycelium).
  • Cool to below 30°C before spawning — hot substrate cooks the spawn.

Lesson 3.3 — Spawning and bagging (the sterile hour)

  • This is the contamination-critical hour: washed hands/gloves, disinfected surface, clean bags, spawn bag opened only now.
  • Layer method: 5–8 cm substrate, sprinkle spawn, repeat to fill a 30×45 cm bag (3–5 kg wet), finishing with spawn; or mix spawn through at 5–10% and fill.
  • Compress gently, tie the neck, and punch 8–12 small holes (or 1–2 cm slits) around the bag — mycelium needs to breathe; mushrooms will later fruit from these holes.
  • Label every bag: date + substrate + spawn batch. Records find problems; memory doesn't.

Lesson 3.4 — Into incubation

  • Bags to the dark incubation space, 20–28°C, not touching, undisturbed. No watering, no opening, no visitors — just a daily glance for green invaders.

MODULE 4 (Weeks 4–7) — Incubation, Fruiting & Contamination War ▾

Lesson 4.1 — Watching the white takeover

  • Days 3–7: white fuzz spreads from every spawn grain. Days 14–28: bags turn fully white — colonisation complete. Faster in warmth (25–28°C), slower when cool.
  • Fully colonised bags feel firm, smell sweetly of mushroom, and may show tiny knots at the holes — pins are coming.

Lesson 4.2 — The contamination war

InvaderLooks likeAction
Green mould (Trichoderma)Green patches spreading fastBag OUT immediately, emptied far away; review pasteurisation temp and spawning hygiene
Black/pin mouldsBlack or grey fuzzSame — out; usually substrate too wet or poorly pasteurised
Sour/wet rot (bacteria)Slimy patches, sour smellOut; substrate was too wet — recalibrate the squeeze test
Flies/gnats & mitesInsects at holes, larvae tunnelsScreens, hygiene, remove infested bags; never spray insecticide on fruiting bags
  • Score your cycle: 0–10% bags lost = good hygiene; 10–25% = fixable process faults; above 25% = stop, deep-clean, re-check spawn source and pasteurisation before wasting more substrate.

Lesson 4.3 — Triggering the fruit

  • Move fully white bags to the fruiting room and shock them: light (indirect), fresh air, humidity 80–90%, and a few degrees' temperature drop — the mycelium reads "surface reached, rains came" and fruits.
  • Widen or add fruiting slits if pins crowd; mist AROUND bags and the room 2–3× daily — never waterlog pins directly (they yellow and die).
  • Pins appear 3–7 days after the shock; pin-to-harvest is just 4–7 days — oyster clusters double daily. Check twice a day near harvest.

Lesson 4.4 — Reading the mushrooms

  • Perfect harvest stage: caps flattening but edges still slightly curled DOWN. Once edges turn up and release spore dust, quality, weight and shelf life all fall.
  • Long stems + small caps = more fresh air needed; dry cracked caps = more humidity; yellowing mush = overwatering directly on clusters.

MODULE 5 (Weeks 7–10) — Harvest, Flushes & Yields ▾

Lesson 5.1 — Harvesting right

  • Grip the whole cluster at the base and twist-pull cleanly (or cut flush with a clean knife) — stumps left behind rot and invite flies.
  • Harvest into shallow baskets/trays, never deep piles (they bruise under their own weight); mushrooms are 90% water and bruise like ripe fruit.
  • Morning harvest, sold same day fresh — or straight to the dryer.

Lesson 5.2 — Flushes: the repeating crop

  • After the first flush, remove stumps, rest the bag, keep humidity — the next flush pins in 7–14 days. Expect 3–4 flushes per bag: roughly 50% of total yield in flush 1, 30% flush 2, 20% after.
  • Yield maths (biological efficiency): good oyster production returns 50–100% of the substrate's DRY weight as fresh mushrooms — a 4 kg wet bag (~1.4 kg dry) yields 0.7–1.4 kg of mushrooms over its life.
  • After the last flush, the spent substrate is excellent compost, worm-farm feed, or cattle feed supplement — sell or use it; nothing is waste.

Lesson 5.3 — Post-harvest handling

  • Fresh shelf life: 2–3 days ambient, 5–7 refrigerated. Pack in paper-lined baskets or perforated punnets — sealed plastic sweats them into slime.
  • Drying: slice large ones, dry whole small ones on mesh in a solar dryer (see the rAs Solar Drying course) to cracker-crisp; store airtight. 10 kg fresh → ~1 kg dried.
  • Value-added: mushroom powder (dried + pounded — soup seasoning), and pre-packed "soup kits" with dried vegetables.

Lesson 5.4 — The continuous production line

  • The professional rhythm: spawn a new batch of bags EVERY week or two → after month two you harvest every single week forever, from overlapping cohorts.
  • Plan backwards from buyers: a restaurant wanting 5 kg/week needs ~15–20 active bags in staggered rotation.

MODULE 6 (Weeks 10–12) — Selling, Economics & Your Business Plan ▾

Lesson 6.1 — Selling a crop many people have never cooked

  • The tasting rule: cooked samples (fried with onion, salt) at markets, churches and offices convert sceptics faster than any poster — mushrooms sell on taste and on "meat substitute" economics.
  • Include a simple recipe slip in every pack; repeat customers are made in the kitchen, not the market stall.
  • Pitch lines that work: protein-rich meat alternative at half meat's price; grown clean, indoors, spray-free.
