Commercial Pig Farming

A complete 6-month program that follows one real production cycle — from empty plot to your first pigs sold. Theory, hands-on practicals, business planning and quizzes every month.

⏱️ 6 Months (8–12 hrs/week) 📊 Beginner → Business-ready 🌍 Hybrid: Online + Offline 🎓 Certificate on completion

Course Overview

Why 6 months? Because that is one real pig production cycle: a weaner bought in Month 2–3 reaches slaughter weight (60–90 kg) around 5–6 months of age. You study each month's material while your own pigs (or your planning) go through exactly that stage. By graduation you have either sold your first pigs or hold a complete, costed plan ready for funding.

Structure: 6 monthly modules → lessons → practical assignments → a 10-question quiz each month → final business project.

How to study (hybrid): Online — work through each month on your phone, log sessions in the Study Diary, ask the rAs AI assistant anything. Offline — save this page (Print → Save as PDF) and do the practicals in the real world: at your site, at other farms, at the market. The practicals are the course.

⚠️ Read first: the three rules that protect your investment

  • African Swine Fever (ASF) has NO vaccine and NO cure. It kills nearly 100% of infected pigs. Your only protection is biosecurity — Month 4 is life-or-death reading. Never feed uncooked kitchen/hotel swill; it is the classic ASF entry route.
  • Never spend before the Iron-Clad Price Check: call at least 3 suppliers for every purchase — piglets, feed, cement, medicine. This habit alone saves 30–50% of startup cost.
  • Pigs eat money: feed is 60–70% of total production cost. Every lesson on feeding is a lesson on profit.
MONTH 1 — Foundations, Planning & Pig Science ▾

Lesson 1.1 — The pig industry and your opportunity

  • Pork is the most consumed meat on earth (~35% of global meat), and African demand is growing faster than supply in most countries — urban butcheries import what local farmers fail to produce.
  • Pigs convert feed to meat better than cattle: a good pig needs roughly 2.5–3.5 kg of feed per 1 kg of live weight gain (FCR), versus 6–8 kg for beef.
  • Pigs are prolific: one sow can produce 18–25+ weaners per year (2 litters of 8–14 piglets) — no other farm mammal multiplies capital that fast.
  • The flip side: pigs are unforgiving of bad feeding, dirty water and poor biosecurity. This course exists to keep you out of the 50%+ of new pig ventures that fail in year one.

Lesson 1.2 — Pig biology every farmer must know

  • Gestation: 3 months, 3 weeks, 3 days (≈114 days) — the easiest fact in farming to remember.
  • Heat (oestrus) cycle: every 21 days; standing heat lasts 8–36 hours — the sow stands rigid when pressed on the back.
  • Weaning: commercially at 21–35 days; the sow returns to heat 4–7 days after weaning.
  • Growth benchmarks: birth 1.2–1.6 kg → weaner (8 weeks) 12–20 kg → slaughter (5–6 months) 60–90 kg.
  • Pigs cannot sweat — they control heat through shade, water and wallows. Heat stress above ~32°C cuts growth and fertility.

Lesson 1.3 — Choosing your production system

Lesson 1.4 — Breeds for African conditions

  • Large White: the workhorse — big litters, fast growth, good mothering; needs decent feeding and shade.
  • Landrace: long body, excellent mothers, bacon-type carcass; often crossed with Large White.
  • Duroc: hardy, fast-growing, good meat quality; common as a terminal sire (father of slaughter pigs).
  • Indigenous breeds (e.g. Mukota, local blacks): heat- and disease-tolerant, survive on rougher feed, but slower growth and smaller litters — excellent for low-input and crossbreeding programs.
  • Practical rule: a Large White × Landrace sow put to a Duroc boar is the classic commercial cross; a Large White × indigenous cross is the smart low-budget starter.

Lesson 1.5 — Site selection

  • Well-drained land, slight slope, shade available; pigs + waterlogged mud = disease.
  • Reliable water: a growing pig drinks 5–10 L/day; a lactating sow 20–30 L/day. No water security, no pig farm.
  • Distance: far enough from homes for smell/regulations (check your local authority), close enough for security.
  • Biosecurity by location: away from other piggeries, roads used by pig transporters, and wild pig areas if possible.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 1): (1) Visit two working pig farms and one butchery; record their breeds, feed sources, pig prices and biggest problems. (2) Walk your intended site with a checklist: water source, drainage, shade, distances — photograph and file it. (3) Open your farm record book: every cost from today goes in it.

MONTH 2 — Housing, Equipment & Biosecurity Setup ▾

Lesson 2.1 — Housing principles

  • A pig house must deliver five things: dry floor, shade/ventilation, feed and water access, easy cleaning, and separation of groups.
  • Orientation: long axis east–west so the sun never bakes the pens; open sides with roof overhangs for airflow in hot climates.
  • Floors: rough-finished concrete sloped 2–3% to a drainage channel — smooth concrete causes leg injuries, earth floors harbour worms and disease.
  • Roof height 2.4–3 m for cooling; thatch or insulated sheets beat bare metal sheeting in heat.

Lesson 2.2 — Space requirements (the numbers)

Class of pigFloor space per animal
Weaner (10–25 kg)0.3–0.5 m²
Grower (25–60 kg)0.6–0.8 m²
Finisher (60–90 kg)0.8–1.2 m²
Dry sow / boar pen6–9 m² (boar pen strong-walled, 1.2 m high)
Farrowing pen (sow + litter)7–9 m² with creep area & guard rails
  • Overcrowding = fighting, tail-biting, slow growth and disease. When in doubt, give more space.

Lesson 2.3 — Low-cost construction options

  • Brick/concrete pens: the gold standard — hygienic, durable; budget the concrete floor first, walls can start as poles + offcuts and be upgraded.
  • Pole-and-thatch with concrete floor: proven starter design at a fraction of full-brick cost.
  • Deep-litter option: 30–50 cm of dry maize stover/sawdust bedding composts manure in place — warmer, low smell, but needs generous space and dry climate management.
  • Farrowing guard rails: a 20–25 cm rail around farrowing pen walls stops the sow crushing piglets — the cheapest piglet-saver ever built.

Lesson 2.4 — Feeders, drinkers and the wallow

  • Trough space: 30 cm per grower so all eat at once (pigs bully); concrete or half-drum troughs bolted down.
  • Nipple drinkers save water and stay clean; otherwise fixed concrete water troughs cleaned daily.
  • A shallow wallow or sprinkle area is air-conditioning for pigs — plan it into hot-climate housing.

Lesson 2.5 — Building biosecurity INTO the farm

  • One gate in, footbath (disinfectant) at the entrance, refreshed every 1–2 days.
  • Perimeter fence keeping out stray pigs, dogs and unsupervised visitors — the #1 physical ASF barrier.
  • Quarantine pen 30+ m from the main houses for every new arrival, 2–4 weeks.
  • Vehicles (especially pig buyers' trucks) stay OUTSIDE the fence; loading ramp at the boundary.
✅ Iron-Clad Price Check: price cement, poles, roofing, troughs and fencing at 3 suppliers each before buying — building materials show the biggest supplier price gaps of anything in this course.

MONTH 3 — Buying Stock & Mastering Feeding ▾

Lesson 3.1 — Buying your first pigs

  • Buy 8-week weaners of 12–20 kg from a reputable, disease-free farm — never from open markets or unknown traders (ASF risk).
  • Health checks at purchase: bright eyes, curly tail, smooth skin (no mange scabs), no coughing/scouring, active and curious.
  • Start small: 5–10 growers is the right first cycle — big enough to learn economics, small enough that mistakes are survivable.
  • Transport calmly in a clean, disinfected vehicle; straight into your quarantine pen for 2 weeks with deworming on arrival.

Lesson 3.2 — What pigs must get from feed

StageCrude proteinFeed/day (approx.)Ration name
Creep (piglets 2–8 wks)18–20%ad lib small amountsCreep/starter
Weaner/grower (12–60 kg)16–18%1.0 → 2.5 kg risingGrower
Finisher (60–90 kg)13–15%2.5–3.0 kgFinisher
Dry sow / boar13–14%2.0–2.5 kgSow & boar
Lactating sow15–17%2 kg + 0.4 kg per piglet (5–8 kg)Lactation

Lesson 3.3 — Commercial feed vs home mixing

  • Commercial complete feeds are safest for beginners: correct protein, minerals and vitamins guaranteed — your job is only clean water and correct amounts.
  • Home mixing cuts cost 20–40% once you have volume. A classic grower mix: ~60–65% maize meal, 15–20% soybean meal (or 20–25% sunflower cake with lysine attention), 10–15% wheat/maize bran, plus a commercial pig premix (vitamins/minerals) at the bag's stated rate, salt 0.25–0.5%.
  • Golden rule of home mixing: always include a proper pig premix — energy and protein without micro-nutrients gives lame, slow pigs.
  • Free/cheap supplements that WORK: brewery spent grain (wet, feed fresh), vegetable market waste (clean, raw), sweet potato vines, lucerne. They stretch feed but cannot replace the balanced ration for growth stock.

Lesson 3.4 — The swill rule (this saves your farm)

  • Kitchen, restaurant and hotel food waste ("swill") is the classic route for African Swine Fever and other diseases entering a farm.
  • If you ever use food waste: it must contain NO meat products and be boiled vigorously for at least 30 minutes — and in many countries feeding swill is restricted or illegal. Check your regulations. The safest policy is simply: don't.

Lesson 3.5 — Feeding management that shows in the bank

  • Feed twice daily at fixed times; pigs thrive on routine and fight less.
  • Weigh or tape-measure pigs monthly (heart-girth tape gives ±5% of weight) and track growth: growers should gain 500–700 g/day, finishers up to 800 g/day.
  • Adjust feed weekly as pigs grow; running out of feed for even 2 days visibly sets growth back.
  • Clean water 24/7 — a pig eating dry feed without water simply stops eating.
✅ Iron-Clad Price Check: feed is 60–70% of your cost — get 3 quotes for complete feeds AND price the home-mix ingredients per kg of protein before choosing your feeding strategy.

MONTH 4 — Health, Disease Prevention & Biosecurity Discipline ▾

Lesson 4.1 — African Swine Fever: the farm-killer

  • ASF is a viral disease with near-100% death rate, NO vaccine and NO treatment anywhere in the world.
  • Signs: sudden deaths, high fever (40.5–42°C), purple-red blotches on ears/belly/legs, huddling, loss of appetite, bloody diarrhoea.
  • Spread: infected pigs, uncooked pork/swill, contaminated vehicles, boots, equipment, and in some regions soft ticks and wild pigs.
  • Your response plan: isolate, stop ALL movement on/off the farm, and report immediately to your veterinary authority — ASF is notifiable by law. Never sell sick pigs into the market; it destroys every farm downstream.

Lesson 4.2 — The everyday diseases and their handling

ProblemSignsPrevention / action
Worms (internal parasites)Poor growth, pot belly, rough coatDeworm on arrival, then every 2–3 months; clean concrete floors
Mange (skin mites)Scratching, grey crusty skin, thickened earsIvermectin-type treatment per vet/label; treat all pigs at once
Piglet scours (diarrhoea)Wet yellow/white diarrhoea in young pigletsWarm dry creep area, sow hygiene, oral rehydration fast; vet if bloody
PneumoniaCoughing, fast breathing, feverVentilation without draughts, no overcrowding; vet-directed antibiotics
Erysipelas ("diamond skin")Fever, diamond-shaped skin patchesVaccine available in many countries; vet treatment works if fast

Lesson 4.3 — The health routine calendar

  • Daily: observe every pig eating — the pig that skips a meal is your early-warning system; check water flow; remove dung.
  • Weekly: footbath refresh audit, rodent check, record weights of a sample pen.
  • Every 2–3 months: deworm the herd; disinfect empty pens between batches (wash → dry → disinfect → rest days).
  • Per your vet's local advice: vaccinations available in your country (e.g. erysipelas; sow vaccines for piglet scours) — build the farm's own calendar with a local vet or para-vet once and follow it.

Lesson 4.4 — Medicines: use like a professional

  • Buy only registered veterinary products, from registered outlets; record every treatment: pig ID, product, dose, date.
  • Respect withdrawal periods — the days between last treatment and legal slaughter printed on every label. Selling pork inside withdrawal is illegal and dangerous.
  • Never guess antibiotic doses; wrong dosing breeds resistant bacteria and wastes money. When unsure, one call to a para-vet costs less than one dead pig.

Lesson 4.5 — Biosecurity as a daily habit

  • One set of boots and overalls that never leave the farm; visitors wear yours, not theirs.
  • No pork products enter the pig area — a sandwich can carry ASF virus.
  • Control rats and birds around feed stores; buy feed from mills that don't collect swill.
  • Downtime rule: if you visit another piggery or market, 48 hours and a full wash before entering your own pens.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 4): (1) Write your one-page Farm Health Calendar (deworming dates, disinfection days, vet contacts) and pin it in the feed room. (2) Do a biosecurity self-audit with a 15-point checklist and fix the 3 worst gaps. (3) Save your national veterinary services' ASF reporting number in your phone today.

MONTH 5 — Growth Management, Records & Breeding Basics ▾

Lesson 5.1 — Managing growers to slaughter weight

  • Group pigs by size; move slow-growers to a "hospital/catch-up" pen with extra care rather than letting them be bullied.
  • Target: 85–95 kg live weight at 5–6 months for the mainstream pork market; check what YOUR buyer wants — some butchers pay best at 70–80 kg.
  • Dressing percentage (carcass ÷ live weight) runs 70–75%: a 90 kg pig yields a 63–68 kg carcass. All pricing maths flows from this number.
  • Cut feed 12–24 hours (water always available) before transport/slaughter — calmer pigs, cleaner slaughter, less weight-loss stress.

Lesson 5.2 — The records that run the farm

  • Feed register: every bag in, every kg fed — this single book reveals your true FCR and whether theft/wastage is eating profit.
  • Pig register: ID (ear-notch or tag), source, weights monthly, treatments, sale price.
  • Money book: every cent in and out, weekly totals — Month 6 turns this into your business plan.
  • Rule: what is not written down did not happen. Lenders, buyers and your future self only believe records.

Lesson 5.3 — Breeding basics (your Year-2 expansion)

  • Gilts (young females) are ready at 7–8 months and 100–120 kg — breed on the second or third heat, never the first.
  • Heat detection: restlessness, swollen vulva, mounting others, and the standing reflex under back pressure; serve twice, 12 hours apart, during standing heat.
  • Boar ratio 1:15–20 sows; or use AI (artificial insemination) where services exist — better genetics without keeping a boar.
  • Flushing: feeding gilts extra energy 10–14 days before service increases litter size.

Lesson 5.4 — Farrowing and saving piglets

  • Move the washed sow to the farrowing pen 5–7 days before her 114-day due date; provide chopped bedding.
  • At birth: clear membranes from nostrils, dip navels in iodine, ensure every piglet suckles colostrum within 6 hours — colostrum is their only immune system.
  • Warmth is survival: piglets need 30–32°C in week one (lamp, box or deep bedding in the creep area) while the sow needs it cooler.
  • Routine days 1–3: iron injection (piglets are born iron-deficient), clip needle teeth only if fighting injuries appear; record litter size.
  • Target: wean 8–12 piglets per litter; crushing and chilling — both preventable — cause most losses.

Lesson 5.5 — Manure: from problem to product

  • One grower produces ~1.5–2.5 kg of manure daily — a 10-pig unit makes several tonnes of premium fertiliser a year.
  • Compost 6–8 weeks before use on vegetables; fresh pig manure burns crops and carries pathogens.
  • Manure sales or your own vegetable plot typically add 5–10% to farm income; a biogas digester is the advanced upgrade (see the rAs Biogas Technology course).

(2) Visit a butchery: get their preferred live weight, carcass price/kg and payment terms in writing. (3) Start a compost heap with your manure and bedding.

MONTH 6 — Selling, Economics & Your Business Plan ▾

Lesson 6.1 — Where the money is: market channels

  • Butcheries and abattoirs: volume buyers, price per kg carcass — build 2–3 relationships before your pigs are ready, not after.
  • Live sales to other farmers (weaners/breeding gilts): breeding stock earns 1.5–2.5× pork price per kg.
  • Direct pork sales (where licensed): highest margin, highest work — requires slaughter through an approved abattoir and health inspection in most countries.
  • Events and festive seasons: pork demand spikes at holidays — time your batches to finish then.

Lesson 6.2 — The full cost of a pig (worked example)

  • The lesson inside the table: at these margins, feed price and FCR decide everything. Cutting feed cost/kg by 15% (home mixing) or improving FCR from 3.5 to 3.0 roughly doubles margin per pig.

Lesson 6.3 — Pricing and negotiation

  • Know your break-even per kg before any negotiation: total cost ÷ expected carcass kg.
  • Sell by scale weight, never by eye — a hanging scale pays for itself on the first sale.
  • Get butcher price quotes weekly in your record book; sell to the best of three, not the nearest of one.

Lesson 6.4 — Growing the enterprise

  • The proven ladder: 5–10 growers → 20–30 growers with home mixing → 2–4 sows farrow-to-finish → sell weaners AND pork.
  • Reinvest the first cycles: better floors, feed store, a scale, then breeding stock.
  • Contracts: schools, mines, supermarkets and processors want RELIABLE monthly supply — batch planning (new weaners every month) is what unlocks them.

Lesson 6.5 — Final project: your bankable business plan

  • Using your six months of real records, assemble: farm description, production system, 12-month batch calendar, full costings (your numbers, not this course's), market letters/quotes from 2 buyers, risk plan (ASF response, feed price spikes), and 3-year growth ladder.
  • This document is your certificate project AND your loan/investor application — one piece of work, two uses.

📖 Glossary

  • FCR — Feed Conversion Ratio: kg of feed per kg of live weight gained.
  • Weaner — piglet separated from the sow, ~8 weeks, 12–20 kg.
  • Gilt — a female pig before her first litter; sow after.
  • Farrowing — a sow giving birth.
  • Creep area — warm piglet-only corner of the farrowing pen.
  • Standing heat — the period a female stands rigid for mating; the service window.
  • Dressing % — carcass weight ÷ live weight (70–75% in pigs).
  • Withdrawal period — legal wait between medication and slaughter.
  • Biosecurity — all measures keeping disease off the farm.
  • ASF — African Swine Fever: fatal, untreatable, notifiable pig virus.
