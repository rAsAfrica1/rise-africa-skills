1. Water Requirements at Different Growth Stages

Sweet potatoes need about 500-700 mm of water over the whole growing season, but how that water is delivered matters as much as the total. In the first 3-4 weeks after planting, the vines are small and roots are shallow. Keep the top 15 cm of soil moist but not soaked. Too much water now can rot the slips. A light watering every 2-3 days in dry weather is enough. If you see the leaves wilting in the morning, that is a sign to water.

From week 5 to week 10, the plants grow fast and start forming storage roots. This is the most critical stage for water. The soil should be kept evenly moist to a depth of 30 cm. In sandy soils, you may need to water every 2 days. In clay soils, every 4-5 days. A simple test: push a finger into the soil. If it feels dry at 5 cm, it is time to irrigate. Lack of water now means small, woody roots.

From week 11 to week 15, the roots swell and fill with starch. Water needs are still high but start to drop in the last 2 weeks. Too much water near harvest can cause roots to crack or rot. Reduce irrigation when you see the leaves start to yellow naturally. Stop watering completely 10-14 days before you plan to harvest. This helps the skin set and improves storage life.

In Africa, rainfall is often irregular. If you rely on rain, plant at the start of the rainy season. If you have irrigation, you can plant earlier or in the dry season. A rain gauge costs little and helps you track how much rain you get. For every 1 mm of rain, you get 1 litre per square metre. So if your crop needs 25 mm per week, that is 25 litres per square metre per week. Adjust your irrigation accordingly. Always check the soil before wateringnever water on a schedule alone.

2. Irrigation Systems: Drip, Sprinkler, and Furrow

Drip irrigation is the most efficient for sweet potatoes. It saves water by delivering it directly to the roots. A basic drip kit for a 100 square metre plot costs about $20-30 and includes a tank, filter, main line, and drip tape. You can also make your own using a 20-litre bucket with small holes punched in the bottom, placed near each plant. Drip works well on small plots, saves up to 60% water compared to furrow, and reduces weeds because only the root zone gets wet. Clean the filter weekly and check for clogged emitters.

Sprinkler irrigation mimics rainfall. It is good for sandy soils and for cooling plants in extreme heat. A small sprinkler can cover a 10-metre circle. But sprinklers lose water to evaporation, especially in hot afternoons. Water early morning or late evening to reduce losses. Also, wet leaves can encourage fungal diseases. If you use sprinklers, water in the morning so leaves dry before night. Sprinklers are better for large fields but require a pump or pressurized tank. A treadle pump can work if you have a water source nearby.

Furrow irrigation is the cheapest and oldest method. You dig small channels between rows and let water flow. It works best on flat land with clay or loam soil. But it uses a lot of water and can cause erosion. To reduce water use, make furrows 20-30 cm deep and 50-75 cm apart. Let water run for a short time, then stop. Do not let water stand for more than a few hours. Furrow irrigation can waterlog the soil and cause root rot if not managed well. It is not recommended for sandy soils because water drains too fast.

Choose your system based on your budget, water source, and soil. For most smallholders in Africa, drip or a combination of drip and furrow is best. Always level your land so water spreads evenly. And remember, no system works if you do not maintain itcheck for leaks, clogs, and worn parts regularly.

3. Scheduling Irrigation Based on Soil Moisture

Scheduling irrigation means watering at the right time, not just on a fixed day. The best way is to check soil moisture. You can do this with your hands. Dig a small hole 10 cm deep. Take a handful of soil and squeeze it. If it forms a ball and leaves a slight moisture mark on your palm, it is perfect. If it crumbles, it is too drywater now. If it drips water, it is too wetwait. Do this test every 2-3 days in the root zone.

For more accuracy, use a simple tensiometer. It is a tube with a gauge that measures how tightly water is held by the soil. You can buy one for $30-50 or make a DIY version with a plastic bottle and a porous cap. Install it 15-20 cm deep. When the gauge reads 30-40 centibars, it is time to irrigate. For sweet potatoes, keep soil moisture between 50-70% of field capacity. That means the soil should feel like a wrung-out sponge.

Another method is to use a rain gauge and a water balance sheet. Record daily rainfall and irrigation. Subtract water lost through evaporation (about 3-5 mm per day in hot weather). When the balance drops below 20 mm, irrigate. You can find evaporation rates from your local weather station or use a simple pan evaporimeter. A Class A pan costs about $50 but lasts for years.

In Africa, many farmers use the 'look and feel' method. Watch your plants. Sweet potato leaves fold slightly in the afternoon heat, but if they are still wilted in the early morning, they need water. Also, check the soil at 5 cm and 15 cm. If both are dry, water deeply. Deep watering encourages deep roots, which makes plants more drought-tolerant. Avoid light, frequent wateringit leads to shallow roots and weak plants. Water early in the morning to reduce evaporation and disease. Keep a simple notebook to track your observations and adjust your schedule with the seasons.

4. Managing Drought Stress and Waterlogging

Drought stress happens when the soil lacks enough water for the plant to grow. Sweet potatoes are fairly drought-tolerant, but long dry spells reduce yields. The first sign is wilting leaves in the morning. Later, leaves turn yellow and drop. Storage roots become small and woody. To manage drought, mulch heavily (see lesson 5), reduce plant spacing so leaves shade the soil, and water deeply but less often. If you have limited water, prioritize the critical stages: root initiation (week 4-6) and root bulking (week 10-14). During these times, even a little water makes a big difference.

You can also plant drought-tolerant varieties like 'Tanzania' or 'Mogamba'. These mature faster and need less water. If you expect a dry season, plant on ridges to concentrate water near roots. Dig small basins around each plant to catch rain. And consider using greywater from your householdsoapy water is fine for sweet potatoes as long as it is not too salty.

Waterlogging is the opposite problem: too much water in the soil. This happens after heavy rains or over-irrigation. Sweet potatoes hate wet feet. When soil is waterlogged, roots cannot breathe and rot. You will see yellow leaves, stunted growth, and soft, smelly roots. To prevent waterlogging, plant on raised beds or ridges, especially in clay soils. Dig drainage channels between beds to carry excess water away. If your field floods, open a trench to the lowest point.

If waterlogging occurs, stop irrigating immediately. Remove standing water by digging small drains. Do not walk on wet soilit compacts and makes things worse. After the soil dries a bit, loosen the top 5 cm with a fork to let air in. If roots have started to rot, harvest what you can and replant. In flood-prone areas, choose a different plot or grow sweet potatoes in raised containers. Always check drainage before planting: dig a 30 cm hole, fill with water, and see how long it takes to drain. If it takes more than 2 hours, improve drainage with organic matter or raised beds.

5. Mulching to Conserve Soil Moisture

Mulching is covering the soil with a layer of dry material. It is the cheapest way to save water. A good mulch layer reduces evaporation by up to 70%. That means you water less and your plants suffer less in dry spells. For sweet potatoes, apply mulch 5-10 cm thick around the plants, but keep it 5 cm away from the stems to avoid rot. You can use dry grass, straw, maize stalks, banana leaves, or even black plastic. Organic mulches also add nutrients as they break down.

Apply mulch right after planting or when the vines start to spread. First, water the soil well, then spread the mulch. Do not mulch on dry soilit will keep the soil dry. In Africa, dry grass from roadsides or crop residues are free. But avoid mulching with material that has seeds, like fresh weeds, or you will create a weed problem. Also, do not use diseased plant material.

Mulching has other benefits. It keeps soil temperature stable, so roots do not get too hot. It suppresses weeds, which compete for water. It prevents soil erosion from heavy rain. And it adds organic matter, improving soil structure so it holds more water. In sandy soils, mulch is especially important because sand drains fast.

If you use plastic mulch, choose black or silver-black. Black plastic warms the soil in cool areas and blocks weeds. Silver-black reflects sunlight and repels aphids. Plastic mulch costs about $10 for a 30-metre roll, but it lasts for 2-3 seasons if you handle it carefully. Lay it tight over raised beds and cut holes for planting. Remove it after harvest and store for reuse. For organic farmers, stick to straw or grass.

One caution: in very wet areas, mulch can keep soil too moist and cause rot. If you have heavy rains, reduce mulch thickness or remove it temporarily. Always check soil moisture under the mulchit can be dry even if the surface looks wet. Mulch is a tool, not a rule. Use it wisely based on your climate and soil.

6. Water Quality and Salinity Considerations

Not all water is good for sweet potatoes. Water with high salt content can damage your crop. Salinity is a problem in many parts of Africa, especially in dry areas where irrigation water evaporates and leaves salts behind. Sweet potatoes are moderately sensitive to salt. If the salt level is too high, leaves turn yellow and roots become small and misshapen. You can test your water with a simple electrical conductivity (EC) meter. For sweet potatoes, keep EC below 1.5 dS/m. If it is above 3.0, do not use that water without treatment.

Where does salty water come from? Wells in coastal areas, boreholes in dry regions, and recycled water. You can also get salinity from over-fertilizingtoo much fertilizer adds salts. To manage salinity, first test your water and soil every season. If salt is high, mix with fresh water if possible. Irrigate with extra water to leach salts below the root zone. But do not over-irrigate, or you will waterlog the soil. Add organic matter like compost or manure to help flush salts and improve drainage.

Other water quality issues: dirty water can clog drip lines. Always filter water from rivers or ponds. Use a simple cloth filter or a sand filter. Water with too much chlorine (from treated pipes) can harm beneficial soil life. Let chlorinated water sit in an open tank for 24 hours before use. Water with oil or chemicals should never be used.

In Africa, many farmers use water from streams or shallow wells. These can contain bacteria that cause root rot. If you suspect disease, water at the base of the plant, not on leaves. And avoid using water that has run off from other farms where pesticides were applied.

If you must use slightly salty water, plant salt-tolerant varieties like 'Ejumula' or 'SPK004'. Apply gypsum to the soilit helps replace sodium with calcium. But prevention is better: choose a clean water source, protect it from contamination, and monitor quality regularly. A simple test kit for EC and pH costs about $20 and can save your whole crop.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Water Quality and Salinity Considerations for Sweet Potato Irrigation in Africa

Water is the single most limiting input for sweet potato production across most of sub-Saharan Africa, yet many growers irrigate without ever testing what is actually in the water. For commercial sweet potato enterprises — whether supplying urban markets, processors, or export buyers — poor water quality quietly erodes yield, root quality, and shelf life long before visible symptoms appear. This lesson gives you the practical numbers, thresholds, and decision rules you need to protect both your crop and your investment.

Sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas) is moderately salt-sensitive. Published salinity tolerance data place its threshold electrical conductivity (ECe) at roughly 1.5 dS/m, with yield declining about 11% per additional dS/m beyond that point (Maas & Hoffman, 1977; FAO Irrigation and Drainage Paper 29). In plain terms: if your irrigation water is above 1.5 dS/m and you are not leaching salts, you are losing marketable roots. For a 10-hectare enterprise yielding 15 t/ha, a 20% salinity-driven loss equals 30 tonnes — real money.

Key Water Quality Parameters and Thresholds

Test your water source at least once per season — ideally before planting — at a certified laboratory. The four parameters that matter most for sweet potato are salinity (ECw), sodium hazard (SAR), specific ion toxicity (chloride and boron), and biological contamination where water contacts edible roots.

Leaching Requirement: The Business Case for Flushing Salts

The leaching requirement (LR) is the fraction of applied water that must pass below the root zone to keep salts from accumulating. It is calculated as:

LR = ECw ÷ (5 × ECe − ECw)

For sweet potato, use ECe = 1.5 dS/m as your target. If your water is ECw = 1.0 dS/m, then LR = 1.0 ÷ (7.5 − 1.0) = 0.154, meaning roughly 15% extra water must be applied beyond crop demand. On a 10-hectare farm using 500 mm of irrigation, that is an additional 75 mm — a real cost in pumping and labour, but far cheaper than losing a fifth of your harvest.

⚠ SAFETY WARNING — Do Not Irrigate With Untested or Contaminated Water

Never irrigate sweet potato with untreated wastewater, industrial effluent, or water from sources downstream of mining, tanneries, or fuel depots. Heavy metals (lead, cadmium, arsenic) accumulate in edible roots and cannot be washed off — they create a food safety hazard and can cause your produce to be rejected at market or export inspection. Coliform contamination from sewage poses direct human health risk where roots are eaten raw or lightly cooked. If you cannot verify water quality with a laboratory test, do not use the source for a food crop. Where water is stored on-farm, cover tanks to prevent algal growth and mosquito breeding, and keep livestock out of the water source.

Practical Management Rules for African Conditions

  • Test before you plant. Borehole and river water quality shifts seasonally. Test at the start of each dry season when you rely most on irrigation.
  • Match irrigation method to water quality. Drip irrigation places water directly at the root zone with less evaporative concentration than furrow or sprinkler, and is the preferred method where ECw exceeds 1.0 dS/m.
  • Leach deliberately, not accidentally. Apply the calculated leaching fraction during the vegetative stage, when the crop can tolerate it, not during root bulking.
  • Monitor soil ECe. A simple 1:1 soil-to-water extract using a handheld EC meter gives a workable field estimate; convert to ECe using a locally calibrated factor.
  • Blend water sources where possible. Mixing a high-salinity borehole with a low-salinity canal or rainwater tank can bring blended ECw below threshold at low cost.
  • Improve drainage first. Leaching only works if salts can move downward. On heavy clay or compacted soils, install drains or plant on raised beds before attempting leaching.
  • Choose tolerant varieties. Where salinity is unavoidable, select locally adapted varieties with better salt tolerance and shorter cycles, and target fresh-market rather than long-storage contracts.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Water source tested at an accredited lab within the last 12 months — ECw, SAR, chloride, boron, and biological parameters recorded.
  2. ECw compared against sweet potato threshold (1.5 dS/m) and expected yield loss calculated before planting decisions are made.
  3. Leaching requirement calculated and built into the seasonal irrigation budget.
  4. Irrigation method selected to minimise salt concentration at the root zone (drip preferred where ECw > 1.0 dS/m).
  5. Soil ECe monitored at least monthly during the irrigation season.
  6. Drainage verified — no waterlogging, no salt crusting on the surface.
  7. Water storage covered, livestock excluded, and no untreated wastewater or industrial effluent used.
  8. Records kept of water source, test results, volumes applied, and yield outcomes for each block.
