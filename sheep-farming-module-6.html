1. Reproductive Anatomy and Cycles

To breed sheep successfully, you must understand the basic parts of the ram and ewe and how their cycles work. The ram's testicles produce sperm and testosterone. A healthy ram has two firm, equal-sized testicles. The penis is inside a sheath and extends during mating. The ewe has two ovaries that release eggs (ova), a uterus where lambs grow, a cervix, and a vagina. The vulva is the external opening. In Africa, most sheep are seasonal breeders, but tropical breeds like the Red Maasai and Djallonk can breed year-round if feed is good.

The ewe's estrous cycle lasts about 17 days (range 1419). She is in heat (estrus) for 2436 hours. During heat, she may stand still when the ram nudges her, wag her tail, and have a swollen, reddish vulva. Ovulation happens 2430 hours after heat begins. If she is not mated, she returns to heat in about 17 days. Pregnancy (gestation) lasts about 147 days (range 142152).

Rams are not seasonal like ewes, but their fertility drops in extreme heat. A ram can serve 510 ewes in a 17-day cycle. Young rams (under 18 months) should serve fewer ewes. To check if a ewe is pregnant, you can use a ram harness with a crayon: mark the ram's chest with a crayon, and when he mounts a ewe, she gets marked. If she returns to heat, she was not pregnant. Or use ultrasound after 40 days.

Practical signs of heat: ewe stands for the ram, seeks him out, and may bleat more. In hand-mating, bring the ewe to the ram when she shows these signs. Record the date. Count 147 days later for expected lambing. Keep a breeding calendar. In Africa, many farmers use a teaser ram (a vasectomized ram) to detect heat. A teaser ram cannot impregnate but will mount and mark ewes in heat. This helps you plan mating and save feed.

Feed is the biggest factor. Flush ewes (give extra feed) 23 weeks before mating to increase ovulation rate. Give 200300g of maize or sorghum per ewe per day, plus good pasture. For rams, keep them in good body condition (score 3 out of 5) but not fat. Trim their hooves and treat for worms before mating. A healthy ram and well-fed ewes give more lambs.

2. Selection of Breeding Rams and Ewes

Choosing the right breeding stock is the cheapest way to improve your flock. Never keep a ram or ewe just because it is available. Select for health, fertility, mothering ability, and growth. In Africa, adapt to local conditions: choose animals that tolerate heat, ticks, and poor feed. Start with your best 20% of ewes and a proven ram.

For a ram: buy or keep a ram that is at least 1218 months old, with two large, firm testicles. Check for equal size, no lumps, and no signs of disease. His scrotum should be free of wounds. Look at his feet: no foot rot, no long hooves. He should walk easily. His body condition score should be 34 (on a 15 scale). A ram that is too fat cannot mate well. Check his eyes for anemia (pale inside eyelids means worms). Treat him for internal parasites 34 weeks before mating. A good ram has a strong libido: when you put him with a ewe in heat, he should mount quickly. Avoid rams that are aggressive to people or too lazy.

For ewes: select ewes that have lambed before and raised twins. A ewe that produces twins and feeds them well is valuable. Check her udder: two teats, no lumps, no mastitis. She should have a good body condition (score 3). Avoid ewes with bad teeth, chronic lameness, or a history of abortion. Age matters: ewes 26 years old are most productive. Replace ewes after 67 years. In Africa, keep ewes that shed parasites naturally or need fewer dewormings.

Record keeping is key. Ear tag or notch each animal. Write down: date of birth, sire, dam, number of lambs born, weaning weight. Without records, you cannot improve. Use a simple notebook or a smartphone app. Many African farmers use the 'Flock Record' book. Select rams from twin births, from mothers that lamb easily. Do not keep a ram from a difficult birth or from a ewe that produces singles only.

Finally, avoid inbreeding. Do not use a ram on his own daughters or sisters. Bring in a new ram every 23 years. If you cannot buy a new ram, swap rams with a neighbour. Inbreeding causes small, weak lambs and lower fertility. Crossbreeding with local breeds can increase hybrid vigour, but keep pure lines if you want consistent traits. Always quarantine a new ram for 3 weeks before mixing with your flock.

3. Mating Systems and Timing

Mating systems determine how many ewes a ram serves and how you manage breeding. The three main systems are hand mating, pasture mating, and artificial insemination (AI). For most African smallholders, hand mating and pasture mating are practical. AI requires trained technicians and liquid nitrogen, so it is less common but available in some areas.

Hand mating: you bring a ewe in heat to a ram for a single service. Use a teaser ram or check ewes twice daily (morning and evening) for heat signs. When a ewe stands for the teaser, move her to the breeding ram. Let the ram serve her once or twice. Record the date. Then return her to the ewe group. This system lets you know exact mating dates, so you can predict lambing. It also saves the ram's energy. A ram can hand-mate 23 ewes per day, but give him rest days. Hand mating works well for small flocks (1050 ewes).

Pasture mating: you run the ram with the ewes for 68 weeks. This is less labour but you do not know exact mating dates. Use a ram with a marking harness (crayon on chest) to identify mated ewes. Change crayon colour every 17 days to see returns to heat. A single ram can serve 3050 ewes in a 6-week period if he is healthy. For 100 ewes, use 23 rams. In Africa, pasture mating is common but leads to a spread-out lambing season, which makes management harder.

Timing: ewes cycle every 17 days. If you want lambs at a certain time, plan mating 147 days before. For example, to lamb in the dry season when feed is scarce, avoid mating. Better to mate so lambs are born when grass is plenty. In many African regions, mate in AprilMay for lambs in SeptemberOctober (after rains). Or mate in NovemberDecember for lambs in AprilMay. Avoid mating in extreme heat (over 35C) because rams become infertile for 68 weeks.

Flushing: 23 weeks before mating, give ewes extra feed (200300g grain per day). This increases ovulation and twins. Also, give rams a good diet. During mating, keep rams cool and provide shade and water. Do not overuse a ram: a young ram (1218 months) should serve no more than 10 ewes in a season. An older ram can serve 3050. Watch for ram exhaustion: if he lies down often or loses weight, rest him. Rotate rams if possible. Always have a backup ram.

4. Pregnancy Management

Once a ewe is mated, pregnancy lasts about 147 days (5 months). Good management during pregnancy ensures healthy lambs and avoids abortion. The first 3 months are early pregnancy: the embryo implants and grows slowly. The last 2 months are rapid growth: 70% of lamb weight is gained then. So feed matters most in the last 6 weeks.

Confirm pregnancy: A ewe that does not return to heat after 1721 days is likely pregnant. You can also use ultrasound after 40 days (costs $25 in many African towns). A ram harness with crayon helps: if she is not marked again, she is probably pregnant. Watch for signs: she may become quieter, her belly grows, and her udder develops in the last month.

Feeding: In early pregnancy, feed normal pasture. In late pregnancy (last 6 weeks), increase energy and protein. Give 300500g of maize or sorghum per ewe per day, plus good hay or legume hay (e.g., lucerne). Add minerals: a salt lick with calcium and phosphorus. In Africa, many soils lack selenium and copper, so use a mineral supplement. Provide clean water always. Do not feed mouldy hay or spoiled silagethis causes abortion. Avoid sudden feed changes.

Health care: Deworm ewes 23 weeks before lambing, but use a safe dewormer (consult a vet). Vaccinate against clostridial diseases (e.g., pulpy kidney) 46 weeks before lambing. This protects the lamb through colostrum. Treat for external parasites (ticks, lice) with a spray or dip. Handle ewes gently: no rough herding, no dog chasing. Stress can cause abortion. Keep ewes away from rams after mating to avoid injury.

Housing: In the last month, move ewes to a clean, dry pen at night. Provide shade and ventilation. Avoid overcrowding. A ewe needs about 1.52 square metres. Bed with dry straw. In hot climates, ensure good airflow. In cold areas, protect from wind. Check ewes daily for signs of lambing: restlessness, pawing, separating from flock, swollen vulva, and clear mucus. Have a lambing kit ready: clean towels, iodine, scissors, lubricant, and gloves. If a ewe aborts, isolate her and dispose of the fetus and placenta safely. Abortion can spread disease. Always wash hands after handling aborted material.

5. Lambing and Assistance

Lambing is the most critical time. Most ewes lamb without help, but you must be ready. Signs of lambing: the ewe separates from the flock, paws the ground, lies down and gets up, and has a swollen vulva with mucus. She may bleat and look at her flank. First-stage labour lasts 26 hours. Second-stage (pushing) should take 3060 minutes. If she pushes hard for over an hour with no lamb, you must assist.

Prepare a clean lambing pen: 1.5m x 1.5m, dry bedding, fresh water. Wash your hands and arms with soap and water. Put on clean gloves. Use a lubricant (KY jelly or vegetable oil). If the lamb is coming normally, you will see two front feet and a nose. If not, check the position. Common problems: one leg back, head back, or breech (tail first). For a leg back, push the lamb back gently and bring the leg forward. For a head back, push the lamb back and cup the head to bring it forward. For breech, push back and pull the hind legs. Always pull during a contraction, and pull downwards towards the ewe's hocks. Never pull hard. If you cannot correct it in 10 minutes, call a vet or experienced neighbour.

After lambing, clear the lamb's nose and mouth of mucus. If the lamb is not breathing, rub it vigorously with a towel and swing it gently (head down) to clear fluid. Tickle its nose with a straw. If the ewe does not lick the lamb, dry it yourself. Dip the navel in iodine (7% tincture) to prevent infection. Make sure the lamb suckles colostrum within 1 hour. Colostrum is thick, yellow, and rich in antibodies. If the lamb does not suckle, squeeze a little colostrum into its mouth or use a bottle. A lamb needs 200ml of colostrum in the first 6 hours.

Watch for problems: retained placenta (if not passed in 6 hours, call vet), uterine infection (smelly discharge, fever), and mastitis (hot, hard udder). For a ewe that rejects her lamb, restrain her and let the lamb suckle. Sometimes a ewe with twins will reject one. In Africa, many farmers use a 'lambing box' to keep the ewe and lamb together for 12 days. Keep records: date, lamb sex, weight, and any assistance given. Good lambing management saves lives.

6. Neonatal Care

Neonatal care means caring for the lamb from birth to 2 weeks old. This is when most deaths happen. In Africa, up to 30% of lambs die before weaning, mostly from cold, starvation, and disease. Simple steps can cut that to under 10%.

First, ensure the lamb breathes and is dry. If the ewe does not lick it, rub it with a clean cloth. Put it under a heat lamp or in a warm box if the weather is cold or wet. Lambs cannot regulate body temperature well for the first 2 days. In hot climates, provide shade. In cold, provide dry bedding and windbreaks.

Colostrum is vital. The lamb must drink within the first hour, and at least 200ml in the first 6 hours. Colostrum contains antibodies that protect against disease. If the ewe has no milk or rejects the lamb, use frozen colostrum from another ewe (thaw gently) or a commercial colostrum replacer. You can also milk a healthy ewe and bottle-feed. If the lamb is weak, tube-feed with a stomach tube (ask a vet to show you). Never feed cow's milk in the first dayit lacks the right antibodies and can cause scours.

After the first day, make sure the lamb suckles every 23 hours. A hungry lamb bleats and looks for the udder. Check the ewe's udder for milk. If the ewe has mastitis, treat it and feed the lamb by bottle. Keep the lamb with the ewe in a clean, dry pen for 23 days. Then let them join the flock. Ear tag or notch the lamb for identification. Record birth weight (use a hanging scale). A healthy lamb weighs 24 kg at birth. Weigh again at 2 weeks to check growth.

Health: Dip the navel in iodine at birth. Watch for joint ill (swollen joints), naval ill (swollen navel), and scours (diarrhoea). Scours can be caused by dirty pens or overfeeding. Treat with electrolytes and consult a vet. Vaccinate lambs at 68 weeks against clostridial diseases. Deworm at 46 weeks if needed. Provide a creep feed area: a small pen with hay and grain that only lambs can enter. This helps them grow faster. In Africa, many farmers use a 'lamb creep' made of sticks. Always provide clean water. Keep predators away: use a secure night pen. Finally, handle lambs gently to reduce stress. A well-cared lamb will be a productive adult.

<h2>Practical Reference: Sheep Breeding and Reproduction</h2>

<p>Sheep breeding is one of the highest-leverage decisions on an African smallholder or commercial farm. A single ram can influence the genetics of an entire flock for years, and a missed oestrus cycle can cost a producer an entire season of lamb revenue. Yet many farmers treat breeding as a background activity something that "just happens" rather than a managed, measurable business process.</p>

<p>This Practical Reference lesson consolidates the core operational knowledge from the Sheep Breeding and Reproduction module into a single working document. It is designed for farm managers, extension officers and agribusiness advisors who need to make fast, evidence-based decisions on: reproductive anatomy and cycles, ram and ewe selection, mating systems and timing, pregnancy management, lambing assistance, and neonatal care all within African production systems.</p>

<p>The commercial logic is straightforward. A well-managed ewe should produce 1.21.8 lambs per year under good conditions, weaned at 34 months. If a 50-ewe flock lifts its lambing rate from 80% to 130%, that is an additional 25 lambs per year at a conservative market value of US$4080 per weaner in most African markets, that is US$1,0002,000 in additional gross income from better breeding management alone. The inputs required are knowledge, a record book and disciplined timing, not expensive technology.</p>

<h3>1. Reproductive Anatomy and Cycles What You Must Know Operationally</h3>

<p>Ewes are seasonally polyoestrous in many temperate breeds, but most indigenous African breeds (e.g., Red Maasai, Djallonk, Nguni, Balami, WAD) are either aseasonal or show only mild seasonality, meaning they can breed year-round if nutrition is adequate. This is a major commercial advantage in tropical Africa: it allows producers to target lambing for festive seasons (Eid, Christmas, Easter) when prices peak.</p>

<p>The oestrous cycle in ewes averages 17 days (range 1419). Oestrus itself lasts 2436 hours. Ovulation occurs 2430 hours after the onset of oestrus. The ram's sperm survives 2448 hours in the ewe's reproductive tract, while the ewe's egg is viable for only 1218 hours. This narrow window is why mating timing matters commercially: a ram left with ewes continuously will catch most cycles, but hand-mating requires precision.</p>

<p>Puberty in ewe lambs is reached at 612 months depending on breed and nutrition, but for business purposes, ewes should not be mated until they reach 6065% of mature body weight (typically 3040 kg for indigenous breeds, 4555 kg for crossbreds). Mating immature ewes produces small lambs, difficult births and stunted dams a net loss.</p>

<h3>2. Selection of Breeding Rams and Ewes</h3>

<p>Selection is where genetics meet economics. A ram contributes 50% of the genetics to every lamb in the flock; a ewe contributes 50% to her own lambs. For a 50-ewe flock, one ram is therefore as genetically influential as 25 ewes. This asymmetry means ram selection deserves disproportionate attention.</p>

<p>Key selection criteria for rams: scrotal circumference (30 cm in mature indigenous rams; 33 cm in crossbreds), libido (willingness to serve), structural soundness (feet, legs, jaw), body condition score 3.03.5, and critically proven fertility or a breeding soundness examination. A ram that fails to serve is a silent, expensive failure. In many African markets, a proven ram costs 24 a cull ram, but the return on that premium is realised within a single lambing season.</p>

<p>For ewes: select for mothering ability, udder soundness (two functional teats), body condition, and previous lambing performance. Ewes that consistently produce twins or triplets under local conditions are genetic gold. Cull ewes that fail to conceive twice, abort repeatedly, or produce weak lambs.</p>

<h3>3. Mating Systems and Timing</h3>

<p>Four mating systems are relevant in African production:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hand mating:</strong> Ram is introduced to individually identified ewes in oestrus. Highest control, highest labour. Best for recording parentage and for targeted lambing.</li>
<li><strong>Pen mating:</strong> Small group of ewes (1015) with one ram for a defined period. Good balance of control and labour.</li>
<li><strong>Pasture/group mating:</strong> Ram runs with the flock continuously. Lowest labour, lowest control, no parentage records.</li>
<li><strong>Artificial insemination (AI):</strong> Increasingly available in South Africa, Kenya, Ethiopia and Nigeria. Allows use of superior genetics without owning a ram. Requires synchronisation and technical support.</li>
</ul>

<p>Timing rule of thumb: the "AM/PM rule" ewes detected in oestrus in the morning are mated that evening; ewes detected in the evening are mated the following morning. This places mating approximately 1218 hours after onset, aligning with ovulation.</p>

<p>Flushing increasing ewe nutrition (energy) 23 weeks before and during mating can lift ovulation rate by 1020% and is one of the cheapest interventions available. In practice, this means supplementing with 200300 g of concentrate per ewe per day, or moving ewes to better pasture.</p>

<h3>4. Pregnancy Management</h3>

<p>Gestation in sheep averages 147 days (range 145152). Pregnancy diagnosis is possible from day 3540 using ultrasound, or from day 6070 by experienced palpation. In the African context, where ultrasound is often unavailable, body condition monitoring and observation of oestrus return (a ewe that returns to heat 1721 days after mating is not pregnant) are practical alternatives.</p>

<p>Nutrition is the single biggest determinant of pregnancy outcome. The last 6 weeks of gestation (when 70% of foetal growth occurs) is when underfeeding causes low birth weights, weak lambs and pregnancy toxaemia. Ewes carrying twins need 2030% more energy than singles. In practice, this means supplementing with 300500 g of concentrate per ewe per day in late gestation, plus good-quality forage.</p>

<p>Vaccination timing matters: clostridial vaccines (e.g., pulpy kidney, tetanus) should be given 46 weeks before lambing so that antibodies pass into colostrum. Deworming should be done at mating and again 4 weeks before lambing, using a product safe for pregnant ewes.</p>

This is the most common avoidable cause of ewe death in African smallholder flocks.</p>

<h3>5. Lambing and Assistance</h3>

<p>Normal lambing takes 30120 minutes per lamb. The ewe should be observed quietly from a distance. Intervene only if: no progress after 1 hour of active straining, malpresentation (head back, legs back, breech), or if the ewe is exhausted.</p>

<p>When assisting: wash hands, use lubricant, correct the presentation, and pull only during the ewe's contractions, using gentle traction in a downward arc. Never pull with force this causes vaginal tears and lamb death. After delivery, clear the lamb's mouth and nose, and ensure it breathes. Dip the navel in iodine (27%) to prevent joint ill and navel ill.</p>

<p>For African producers, the most common lambing problems are: (1) malnutrition-induced weak contractions, (2) malpresentation in single large lambs from overfed ewes, and (3) lack of clean, dry birthing areas leading to infection.</p>

<h3>6. Neonatal Care</h3>

<p>The first 24 hours determine whether a lamb survives. Colostrum the first milk, rich in antibodies must be consumed within 6 hours of birth, ideally within 12 hours. Lambs that do not receive adequate colostrum are 510 more likely to die in the first month. For orphaned or rejected lambs, use frozen/thawed colostrum from another ewe, or a commercial colostrum replacer.</p>

<p>Practical neonatal checklist: ensure the lamb is dry, warm, and has suckled; identify and record the lamb (ear tag or paint mark); ensure the ewe has passed the placenta within 6 hours; provide clean water and hay to the ewe; and observe for mastitis or metritis.</p>

<p>Lamb mortality in African smallholder flocks often runs 1530%, but well-managed flocks can hold it below 8%. The difference is almost entirely management: colostrum, hygiene, shelter and nutrition.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Condition score ewes and rams 3 weeks before mating.</strong> Target BCS 2.53.5. Flush ewes with 200300 g concentrate/day.</li>
<li><strong>Use the AM/PM rule.</strong> Ewe in heat AM mate PM; ewe in heat PM mate next AM.</li>
<li><strong>Keep ram:ewe ratios correct.</strong> 1:2540 for pasture mating; 1:1015 for pen mating; never overwork a ram.</li>
<li><strong>Vaccinate ewes 46 weeks pre-lambing</strong> (clostridial vaccine) and deworm 4 weeks pre-lambing.</li>
<li><strong>Boost late-gestation nutrition.</strong> 300500 g concentrate/day in the last 6 weeks; twins need more.</li>
<li><strong>Watch for pregnancy toxaemia.</strong> Off-feed, dull, acetone breath in late gestation = emergency. Call vet, drench glucose.</li>
<li><strong>Ensure colostrum within 6 hours of birth.</strong> Dip navel in iodine. Record lamb and dam.</li>
<li><strong>Keep records.</strong> Mating date, sire, lambing date, lambs born/alive/weaned. Records turn breeding into a business.</li>
</ol>
