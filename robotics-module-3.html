1. Proprioceptive Sensors: Encoders, IMUs

Proprioceptive sensors measure the robot's own statejoint angles, wheel rotation, orientation, acceleration. The two workhorses are encoders and IMUs.

**Encoders** count wheel or motor shaft rotations. A common incremental encoder like the Omron E6B2-CWZ6C gives 360 pulses per revolution (PPR). If your wheel is 65 mm diameter, circumference = 65 204 mm. Each pulse = 204/360 0.57 mm of travel. In Arduino, use an interrupt pin to count pulses: `attachInterrupt(digitalPinToInterrupt(2), countPulse, RISING);`. For direction, read a second channel (quadrature). In Africa, encoders are cheap on AliExpress or from local electronics shops in Nairobi or Lagos. Mount them on motor shafts with a slotted disc and optical sensor if you can't afford sealed units.

**IMUs** (Inertial Measurement Units) combine accelerometer, gyroscope, and sometimes magnetometer. The MPU-6050 (about $3) is popular. It gives acceleration in g and angular velocity in /s. But raw data drifts: a gyro might drift 12 per minute. So you must calibrate and filter. For a robot moving on uneven ground, the accelerometer helps correct pitch and roll, but vibration from motors adds noise. Use a low-pass filter: `angle = 0.98*(angle + gyro*dt) + 0.02*accel_angle`. This is a complementary filter, simple and effective on an Arduino.

**Practical tips for Africa:** Power stability matters. Use a separate 5V regulator for sensors to avoid brownouts. Keep wires short to reduce noise. If you can't get an IMU, a smartphone with a sensor-logging app can serve as a temporary IMU via Bluetooth. For encoders, old printer or scanner mechanisms often contain optical encoders you can salvage.

**Calibration:** Before each run, keep the robot still and average 500 accelerometer readings to find offset. Subtract that offset in code. For gyro, average 500 readings and subtract. This takes 2 seconds and cuts drift dramatically.

**Testing:** Write a sketch that prints encoder counts and IMU angles every 100 ms. Push the robot by hand and verify counts increase in the right direction. Rotate it 90 and check the gyro integrates to ~90. Adjust as needed.

2. Exteroceptive Sensors: Ultrasonic, Infrared, LiDAR

Exteroceptive sensors measure the world outside the robot. The three you'll use most are ultrasonic, infrared (IR), and LiDAR.

**Ultrasonic** sensors like the HC-SR04 ($2) emit a 40 kHz chirp and time the echo. Distance = (echo time speed of sound) / 2. Speed of sound 343 m/s at 20C. In code: `distance_cm = duration * 0.0343 / 2;`. Range is 2 cm to 400 cm, but accuracy drops beyond 200 cm. Soft surfaces (cloth, foam) absorb sound, giving false long readings. In dusty or humid environments, the sensor can fail. Mount it at least 10 cm above ground to avoid ground reflections. For outdoor robots in Africa, protect it from direct sun and rain.

**Infrared** sensors like the Sharp GP2Y0A21YK0F ($10) output an analog voltage inversely proportional to distance (1080 cm). They are faster than ultrasonic but affected by sunlight and black surfaces (which absorb IR). Use them for edge detection or short-range obstacle avoidance. Calibrate with a lookup table: measure voltage at 10, 20, 30, 40, 60, 80 cm and fit a curve.

**LiDAR** (e.g., RPLIDAR A1, $100) spins a laser and gives 360 distance scans up to 12 m. It's precise but costs more and needs a clear line of sight. In dusty conditions, keep the lens clean. For outdoor use, a 2D LiDAR can't see holes or overhangspair it with ultrasonic.

**Practical Africa-focused tips:** Buy sensors from local distributors like Robu.in (India) or Mouser (ships to Africa) or from Kenyan shops like Nerokas. For low budget, build your own IR proximity sensor with an IR LED and photodiode. For ultrasonic, waterproof the transducer with a thin plastic film. Always test in the actual environment: a market stall with metal roofs causes multipath echoes.

**Fusion idea:** Use ultrasonic for long range and IR for short range. If ultrasonic reads >200 cm but IR reads 30 cm, trust IR. This simple rule prevents collisions.

3. Vision Sensors: Cameras and Depth Sensors

Cameras give robots rich information: colors, shapes, motion. Depth sensors add distance per pixel. For African robotics, start with a cheap USB webcam (Logitech C270, $20) or a Raspberry Pi Camera Module ($15).

**Camera basics:** Resolution matters. 640480 is enough for line following or color tracking. Frame rate: 30 fps is standard. On a Raspberry Pi, use `picamera` or OpenCV. For color tracking, convert to HSV color space (hue, saturation, value). A red object has hue around 010 or 170180. Write a mask: `mask = cv2.inRange(hsv, (0,100,100), (10,255,255))`. Then find contours and compute centroid. This runs at 1015 fps on a Pi 3.

**Depth sensors:** The Microsoft Kinect v1 (used, $30) gives RGB + depth at 640480. The Intel RealSense D435 ($150) is newer but pricier. For a low-cost option, use two webcams for stereo visionbut calibration is hard. A simpler trick: use a single camera and known object size. If a soccer ball is 22 cm diameter and appears 50 pixels wide, distance = (22 cm focal_length_px) / 50. Focal length in pixels = (image_width_px real_focal_mm) / sensor_width_mm. For a Pi Camera v2, focal length = 3.04 mm, sensor width = 3.67 mm, so at 640 px width, focal_px 530. Then distance = 22 530 / 50 233 cm.

**Africa-specific challenges:** Lighting varies from harsh sun to dim huts. Use auto-exposure or add a bright LED ring. Dust on lenses is commoncarry a microfiber cloth. For outdoor robots, a camera with global shutter (like OV5647) avoids motion blur. Power: a Pi 4 needs 5V 3A; use a good UBEC.

**Practical project:** Build a line follower using a Pi Camera. Threshold for black line on white floor. Compute error from center and steer. This is more robust than IR sensors on reflective floors. For depth, mount the camera at 45 down to see obstacles ahead.

4. Sensor Calibration and Noise Filtering

Raw sensor data is noisy and biased. Calibration removes systematic errors; filtering removes random noise.

**Calibration steps:**
1. **Offset:** For analog sensors, read 100 times with no stimulus (e.g., no object in front of IR). Average and subtract. For IMU, keep still and average.
2. **Scale:** For distance sensors, measure known distances (10, 20, 30 cm) and record output. Fit a linear or polynomial equation. For example, Sharp IR: voltage = 1.2 / (distance + 0.2) 0.1. Use Excel or Python to fit.
3. **Cross-axis:** For magnetometer, rotate 360 and find min/max on each axis. Hard iron offset = (max+min)/2. Soft iron scale = (max-min)/2.

**Noise filtering:**
- **Moving average:** Keep last N readings in an array, output average. N=5 for fast response, N=20 for smooth. In Arduino: `avg = avg * 0.8 + new_reading * 0.2;` (exponential moving average).
- **Median filter:** Take 5 readings, sort, pick middle. Great for spikes (e.g., ultrasonic glitches).
- **Low-pass RC filter:** Hardware: resistor + capacitor. For 10 Hz cutoff, R=1k, C=16F. This smooths analog signals before ADC.
- **Kalman filter:** Covered in L5, but a simple 1D Kalman for distance: predict = last_estimate + velocity*dt; update = predict + K*(measurement - predict). K depends on noise.

**Africa-focused practicalities:** Power supply noise is common. Add a 100F capacitor across sensor VCC and GND. Keep sensor wires away from motor wires. Use shielded cable for long runs. For outdoor robots, temperature changes affect sensorsrecalibrate every hour if precision matters. In dusty environments, clean sensor lenses before calibration.

**Testing:** Write a sketch that prints raw and filtered values. Wave your hand in front of an ultrasonic sensor. The raw value jumps; the filtered value should be smooth. If it lags too much, reduce N. For IMU, plot angle over timedrift should be less than 1 per minute after calibration.

5. Sensor Fusion with Kalman Filters

No single sensor is perfect. Sensor fusion combines data to get a better estimate. The Kalman filter is the most common method.

**Why fuse?** An IMU gives fast orientation but drifts. A magnetometer gives absolute heading but is noisy and affected by motors. An encoder gives distance but slips. Fusing them gives smooth, accurate state estimation.

**1D Kalman filter (for distance):**
- State: position and velocity.
- Predict: `pos = pos + vel*dt; vel = vel;`
- Update: `K = P / (P + R); pos = pos + K*(measurement - pos); P = (1-K)*P;`
Here P is estimate uncertainty, R is measurement noise. Tune R by measuring variance of sensor readings when stationary. For ultrasonic, R might be 4 cm. For encoder, R = 0.1 cm.

**2D Kalman for orientation (IMU + magnetometer):**
Use a complementary filter firstit's simpler. `angle = 0.98*(angle + gyro*dt) + 0.02*mag_angle`. This works well on Arduino. For full Kalman, use a library like `KalmanFilter` or `Madgwick`. The Madgwick filter runs on a Pi or ESP32 and fuses accel, gyro, mag at 100 Hz.

**Practical implementation on Arduino:**
```cpp
float Q_angle = 0.001; // process noise
float Q_bias = 0.003;
float R_measure = 0.03;
float angle = 0, bias = 0, P[2][2] = {{0,0},{0,0}};
// ... in loop:
float rate = gyro - bias;
angle += dt * rate;
P[0][0] += dt * (dt*P[1][1] - P[0][1] - P[1][0] + Q_angle);
// ... update with accel angle
```
This is a 1D Kalman for pitch/roll. For yaw, add magnetometer.

**Africa-focused tips:** Use an ESP32 instead of Arduino for more processing power. Keep the filter loop time consistentuse `micros()` for dt. If motors cause magnetic interference, mount the magnetometer on a mast 20 cm above the robot. For outdoor robots, add GPS to the fusion for global position. Test by driving a square path: the fused estimate should close the loop within 10 cm.

**Common mistakes:** Wrong R values cause sluggish or jittery response. Start with R = variance of sensor. If the estimate lags, increase Q. Always validate with a known trajectory.

6. Lab: Building a Distance-Measuring Sensor Array

In this lab, you'll build a sensor array that measures distance in three directions: front, left, right. You'll use three HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensors, an Arduino Uno, and a breadboard. Total cost: about $15.

**Materials:**
- 3 HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensors
- 1 Arduino Uno (or ESP32)
- 1 breadboard, jumper wires
- 1 5V power supply (e.g., 9V battery + regulator or USB power bank)
- Optional: 3 IR sensors for short-range backup

**Wiring:**
Each HC-SR04 has VCC, GND, Trig, Echo. Connect VCC to 5V, GND to GND. Trig and Echo to digital pins: front (Trig=2, Echo=3), left (Trig=4, Echo=5), right (Trig=6, Echo=7). Use a separate 5V regulator for the sensors if your Arduino browns out.

**Code:**
```cpp
const int trigPins[3] = {2,4,6};
const int echoPins[3] = {3,5,7};
float distances[3];
void setup() { Serial.begin(9600); for(int i=0;i<3;i++){ pinMode(trigPins[i], OUTPUT); pinMode(echoPins[i], INPUT); } }
void loop() {
for(int i=0;i<3;i++){
digitalWrite(trigPins[i], LOW); delayMicroseconds(2);
digitalWrite(trigPins[i], HIGH); delayMicroseconds(10);
digitalWrite(trigPins[i], LOW);
long duration = pulseIn(echoPins[i], HIGH);
distances[i] = duration * 0.0343 / 2;
delay(50); // avoid cross-talk
}
Serial.print("F:"); Serial.print(distances[0]);
Serial.print(" L:"); Serial.print(distances[1]);
Serial.print(" R:"); Serial.println(distances[2]);
delay(100);
}
```
**Calibration:**
Place a wall at 30 cm. Adjust the code if readings are off. For better accuracy, take 5 readings per sensor, sort, and use the median. This reduces noise from echoes.

**Mounting:**
Mount the sensors on a cardboard or 3D-printed bracket. Front sensor points straight ahead; left and right point 45 outward. Keep sensors at least 10 cm apart to avoid cross-talk. Use rubber grommets to reduce vibration.

**Testing in Africa:**
Test in a corridor, a market aisle, or outdoors. Note how dust and wind affect readings. For outdoor use, add a thin plastic film over the transducers (but not too thickit attenuates sound). If you see erratic readings, add a 100F capacitor across VCC and GND of each sensor.

**Extension:**
Add a servo to sweep the front sensor 180 for a scanning sonar. Or fuse the three distances with a simple rule: if any distance < 20 cm, stop the robot. This is the foundation of obstacle avoidance.

<h3>2. Exteroceptive Sensors: Ultrasonic, Infrared, LiDAR</h3>
<p>Exteroceptive sensors measure the external world. Ultrasonic ($315) is the workhorse for obstacle detection in dusty African environments it works in dust, rain, and darkness, unlike infrared. IR sensors ($210) are cheap but fail in direct sunlight and on dark surfaces. LiDAR ($100 for 2D RPLIDAR A1, $500+ for 3D) gives you accurate range and angular data but struggles with dust and requires a clean lens.</p>
<p>For a Nairobi-based agri-robot startup, the practical stack is: ultrasonic ring for safety stop, 2D LiDAR for SLAM, and a downward-facing IR for row following. Total sensor BOM under $400. A 3D LiDAR at $2,000+ is only justified if you are selling to mining or autonomous vehicle customers with corresponding budgets.</p>

<h3>3. Vision Sensors: Cameras and Depth Sensors</h3>
<p>Cameras are the highest-information-density sensor but the most computationally demanding. A $30 Raspberry Pi Camera v3 plus a $15 Coral USB accelerator can run lightweight detection models (MobileNet, YOLOv5-nano) at 1530 FPS. Stereo depth (Intel RealSense D435i, $350) gives you RGB-D at 30 FPS but is sensitive to sunlight and requires calibration. Time-of-Flight sensors (VL53L5CX, $15) are cheap but short-range (4 m).</p>
<p>For African agriculture, thermal cameras (FLIR Lepton, $200) are transformative for crop stress detection and livestock monitoring but they require radiometric calibration, which is rarely available locally. Budget for a calibration service or a return-to-factory cycle.</p>

<h3>4. Sensor Calibration and Noise Filtering</h3>
<p>Calibration is where most African robotics projects fail. Sensors arrive with factory calibration that assumes 25C and 50% humidity. In reality, you will operate at 3545C and 2080% humidity. You must recalibrate:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>IMU:</strong> Six-position static calibration for accelerometer; rate table or manual rotation for gyro. Do this at the ambient temperature of your deployment site.</li>
<li><strong>Camera:</strong> Intrinsic calibration with a checkerboard (OpenCV <code>calibrateCamera</code>); extrinsic calibration for stereo pairs.</li>
<li><strong>LiDAR:</strong> Range offset and angular offset calibration against a known target at 1 m, 5 m, 10 m.</li>
<li><strong>GNSS:</strong> Antenna lever-arm calibration relative to the IMU.</li>
</ul>
<p>Noise filtering: use a median filter (window 35) for ultrasonic to reject outliers; a low-pass Butterworth (cutoff 1020 Hz) for IMU; and a bilateral filter for depth images. Never filter before calibration you will mask the bias you need to remove.</p>

<h3>5. Sensor Fusion with Kalman Filters</h3>
<p>The Kalman filter is the standard algorithm for combining noisy sensor data into a single best estimate. For a differential-drive robot, you fuse wheel odometry (high rate, drifts) with IMU (high rate, drifts differently) and LiDAR/GNSS (low rate, absolute). The filter predicts the state using the motion model, then corrects it using the measurement.</p>
<p>Practical implementation: use the <code>robot_localization</code> ROS package (EKF and UKF nodes) it is free, well-tested, and handles the common case of fusing odometry, IMU, and GPS. For African deployments, tune the process noise covariance higher than you would in a lab, because your environment is more dynamic (rough terrain, temperature swings, vibration).</p>
<p>Business note: sensor fusion is what turns a $500 sensor kit into a $5,000 robot. The software is the moat. Invest in your filter tuning and calibration pipeline it is the difference between a demo and a product.</p>

<h3>6. Lab: Building a Distance-Measuring Sensor Array</h3>
<p>This lab builds a three-sensor array (ultrasonic + IR + LiDAR) with a Kalman filter that outputs a fused distance estimate. Hardware: Arduino Mega or Raspberry Pi Pico, HC-SR04, Sharp GP2Y0A21, RPLIDAR A1, and a 9-DOF IMU. Software: Python with <code>pyserial</code>, <code>numpy</code>, and a simple 1D Kalman filter.</p>
<p>Steps:</p>
<ol>
<li>Wire all sensors to the microcontroller; log raw data at 50 Hz for 60 seconds.</li>
<li>Calibrate each sensor against a tape measure at 0.5 m, 1 m, 2 m, 3 m.</li>
<li>Implement a 1D Kalman filter: state = distance, process noise Q = 0.01, measurement noise R per sensor from calibration.</li>
<li>Compare fused output to ground truth. Target: 2 cm accuracy at 2 m.</li>
<li>Test in direct sunlight, in dust (flour), and at 40C (oven or heat lamp).</li>
</ol>
<p>Deliverable: a CSV log, a plot of raw vs. fused distance, and a one-page report on which sensor failed under which condition.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li> Specify sensors for your actual operating temperature range, not the datasheet's 25C lab condition.</li>
<li> Budget 1520% of sensor cost for calibration tooling and time.</li>
<li> Always include at least one sensor that works in dust, rain, and darkness (ultrasonic is the cheapest).</li>
<li> Calibrate IMU and camera at the deployment site, not in an air-conditioned office.</li>
<li> Use median filtering for ultrasonic; low-pass for IMU; bilateral for depth.</li>
<li> Fuse at least two independent modalities (e.g., odometry + IMU + LiDAR) before trusting any single source.</li>
<li> Validate your sensor stack against a tape measure and a stopwatch before writing any SLAM code.</li>
<li> Document sensor drift over a 4-hour run this is what your customer will experience.</li>
</ul>
