1. Calculating Cost of Goods Sold for Each Product

To know if you are making a profit, you must first know exactly what each product costs you to make. This is called Cost of Goods Sold (COGS). COGS includes every ingredient and packaging item that goes directly into one unit of your product. For example, if you make a 50g tin of body butter, you need to add up the cost of shea butter, coconut oil, essential oil, the tin, and the label. Suppose you buy 1kg of shea butter for 5000 Naira. You use 30g per tin. That's 150 Naira. Coconut oil: 1 litre for 2000 Naira, you use 20ml, so 40 Naira. Essential oil: 10ml for 1500 Naira, you use 5 drops (about 0.25ml), so 37.50 Naira. The tin costs 100 Naira, label 20 Naira. Total COGS per tin = 150 + 40 + 37.50 + 100 + 20 = 347.50 Naira. Round to 348 Naira. You must do this for every product: lip balm, hair oil, etc. Keep a notebook or use a simple Excel sheet on your phone. List all ingredients and packaging, their purchase price, and the amount used per unit. Then divide the purchase price by the number of units you can make from that purchase. For instance, if a 500g bag of beeswax costs 3000 Naira and you use 5g per lip balm, then cost per balm = (3000/500)*5 = 30 Naira. Be precise. Don't forget small items like pipettes, gloves, or cleaning supplies that are used per batchthese are overheads, not COGS, but they affect your total cost. COGS is only the direct materials. Once you have COGS per unit, you can set a price that covers all costs and gives profit. Without accurate COGS, you might think you're making money when you're actually losing. Review your COGS every time supplier prices change. In Africa, prices fluctuate, so update monthly. Use a simple formula: COGS = sum of (cost of ingredient / amount used per unit). This is the foundation of all pricing decisions.

2. Setting Wholesale and Retail Prices

Once you know your Cost of Goods Sold (COGS), you can set your wholesale and retail prices. Wholesale is the price you sell to shops or resellers who buy in bulk. Retail is the price you sell directly to consumers, usually at markets or online. A common rule is to multiply COGS by 2 for wholesale and by 3 or 4 for retail. For example, if your body butter COGS is 348 Naira, wholesale price = 348 x 2 = 696 Naira. Retail price = 348 x 3 = 1044 Naira, round to 1000 Naira for simplicity. This gives you a profit margin. But you must also consider what the market will pay. In many African markets, customers are price-sensitive. Check what similar products sell for in your local market or on Jumia. If your retail price is too high, you may not sell. If too low, you won't cover overheads and profit. For wholesale, you need to offer a discount to resellers so they can make a profit too. They will typically double the wholesale price to set their retail. So if you sell wholesale at 700 Naira, they sell at 1400 Naira. That might be too high. So adjust: maybe wholesale 600 Naira, retail 900 Naira. Your profit per unit at wholesale = 600 - 348 = 252 Naira. At retail = 900 - 348 = 552 Naira. You need to decide how much you want to earn. Also consider production volume: selling 100 units at 200 Naira profit each gives 20,000 Naira, while selling 50 units at 500 Naira profit gives 25,000 Naira. Sometimes lower price with higher volume is better. But never sell below COGS. That's a loss. Set a minimum price. Also, offer tiered wholesale pricing: buy 10-49 units at 700 Naira, 50-99 at 650, 100+ at 600. This encourages larger orders. Always communicate your prices clearly. Use a price list. Review prices every 3-6 months as costs change. Remember, your time is also valuableinclude a labor cost in your pricing if you pay yourself. But for now, focus on materials and overhead. With practice, you'll find the sweet spot where customers buy and you profit.

3. Managing Overhead Costs

Overhead costs are all the expenses that are not directly tied to making a specific product. They include rent for your workspace, electricity, internet, transportation to buy supplies, packaging materials for shipping, marketing, and even your own salary if you pay yourself. These costs must be covered by your sales, or you will not be profitable. To manage overhead, first list every overhead expense you have each month. For example: rent for a small room = 20,000 Naira, electricity = 5,000 Naira, data/internet = 3,000 Naira, transport = 4,000 Naira, marketing (flyers, social media ads) = 5,000 Naira. Total overhead = 37,000 Naira per month. Now you need to allocate this overhead to each product. If you produce and sell 200 units per month, overhead per unit = 37,000 / 200 = 185 Naira. Add this to your COGS to get total cost per unit. So body butter total cost = 348 + 185 = 533 Naira. Then your retail price of 1000 Naira gives profit of 467 Naira. But if you only sell 100 units, overhead per unit = 370 Naira, total cost = 718 Naira, profit only 282 Naira. So increasing sales volume reduces overhead per unit. To manage overhead, look for ways to reduce costs: share a workspace with another entrepreneur, buy packaging in bulk, use free social media instead of paid ads, combine trips to the market. Keep track of all overhead expenses in a notebook or app like Excel or a simple expense tracker. At the end of each month, compare total overhead to total sales. If overhead is more than 30% of sales, you need to cut costs or increase prices. Also, avoid unnecessary expenses. Every Naira saved on overhead is extra profit. Remember, overhead is fixed in the short term, so you must sell enough to cover it. Set a target: sell at least enough units to cover overhead + COGS + desired profit. This is your break-even point. Calculate it: break-even units = total overhead / (price - COGS). For body butter: 37,000 / (1000 - 348) = 37,000 / 652 = 57 units. So you must sell at least 57 units to cover overhead. Beyond that, profit starts. Manage overhead tightly, and you'll be more profitable.

4. Creating Bundles and Special Offers

Bundles and special offers are powerful ways to increase sales and profitability. A bundle is when you combine two or more products and sell them at a slightly lower price than if bought separately. For example, a 'Lip Care Bundle' with one lip balm and one body butter. If lip balm COGS is 150 Naira and body butter COGS is 348 Naira, total COGS = 498 Naira. If you sell them separately, retail might be 500 Naira for lip balm and 1000 Naira for body butter, total 1500 Naira. A bundle price of 1300 Naira gives the customer a 200 Naira discount, but you still make profit: 1300 - 498 = 802 Naira profit, compared to 1500 - 498 = 1002 Naira if sold separately. You sacrifice 200 Naira but may sell more bundles because of the perceived value. Bundles also move slow-selling products. For instance, if hair oil is not selling well, bundle it with a popular body butter. Special offers include 'Buy 2 Get 1 Free' or 'Free delivery for orders above 5000 Naira'. These can boost sales volume. But be careful: 'Buy 2 Get 1 Free' means you give away one unit. If your profit margin is 50%, giving one free may wipe out profit on the other two. Calculate: if each unit profit is 500 Naira, selling 2 gives 1000 Naira profit, but giving one free costs you 348 Naira (COGS), so net profit = 652 Naira. Still positive, but lower. Only run such offers if you can afford it and if it brings in new customers. Another popular offer is 'First 10 customers get 10% off'. This creates urgency. Use social media to announce offers. In Africa, WhatsApp status and Facebook groups are great for this. Also, create holiday bundles for Christmas, Ramadan, or Valentine's Day. Track which offers work by recording sales before, during, and after. Don't run too many offers at once, or customers will wait for discounts. Use bundles to increase average order value. If a customer buys one lip balm for 500 Naira, offer a bundle of 3 for 1200 Naira instead of 1500. They save 300 Naira, you sell more. Always ensure your bundle price covers COGS + overhead + profit. Never bundle at a loss. With creativity, bundles can significantly boost your income.

5. Tracking Expenses and Income

To run a profitable cosmetics business, you must track every Naira that comes in and goes out. This is called bookkeeping. Without it, you won't know if you're making a profit or loss. Start simple: get a notebook and divide each page into two columns: 'Income' and 'Expenses'. Or use a free app like Excel, Google Sheets, or a mobile app like 'Expense Manager'. Record every sale immediately: date, product, quantity, amount. For example, '5 Jan 2025, 3 body butters, 3000 Naira'. Record every expense: ingredients, packaging, transport, rent, data, etc. For example, '5 Jan 2025, shea butter 1kg, 5000 Naira'. At the end of each week, total income and expenses. Subtract expenses from income to get profit. If profit is negative, you spent more than you earned. You need to adjust. At the end of the month, do a full review. Calculate your total revenue, total COGS, total overhead, and net profit. Your net profit = revenue - (COGS + overhead). For example, if you sold 100 body butters at 1000 Naira each, revenue = 100,000 Naira. COGS per unit = 348 Naira, total COGS = 34,800 Naira. Overhead for the month = 37,000 Naira. Net profit = 100,000 - 34,800 - 37,000 = 28,200 Naira. That's your profit. Now you can see if you're meeting goals. Also track inventory: how many units you have left. This helps you plan production. Keep receipts for all purchases. Even if you buy from a local market without a receipt, write it down. Use a separate bank account or mobile money wallet for business to avoid mixing personal and business funds. At the end of each month, reconcile: does your cash balance match your records? If not, find the discrepancy. Tracking helps you identify problems early: maybe a particular product is not selling, or an ingredient cost has risen. You can then adjust prices or find cheaper suppliers. It also helps when applying for loans or grants. Remember, what gets measured gets managed. Make tracking a daily habit. Spend 10 minutes each evening to record the day's transactions. Over time, you'll have a clear picture of your business's financial health.

6. Scaling Production for Higher Profit

Scaling production means making more products to sell more and increase profit. But scaling requires planning. First, ensure your current production is profitable and you have steady demand. If you sell 100 units a month and consistently sell out, you can scale. To scale, you need to increase efficiency. Buy ingredients in bulk to reduce cost per unit. For example, if you buy shea butter in 25kg drums instead of 1kg bags, the price per kg drops. Suppose 1kg costs 5000 Naira, but 25kg drum costs 100,000 Naira, which is 4000 Naira per kg. That saves 1000 Naira per kg. For 100 body butters using 30g each, you need 3kg, saving 3000 Naira. That's extra profit. Also, negotiate with suppliers for discounts. Buy packaging in larger quantities. Invest in simple equipment: a digital scale, a hand mixer, a sealing machine. These reduce labor time and increase output. For example, a sealing machine can seal 100 lip balm tubes in 10 minutes instead of 1 hour by hand. That frees you to make more products or sell. Consider hiring one or two assistants to help with production and packaging. Pay them fairly, but ensure the increased output covers their wages. For example, if you pay an assistant 20,000 Naira per month, they must help you produce at least 40 extra units (at 500 Naira profit each) to cover their cost. That's 10 units per week. Usually, an assistant can help you double production. Also, standardize your recipes so anyone can follow them. Write down exact measurements and steps. This ensures consistency. When scaling, you may need a larger workspace. Look for a small production room or share with others. Marketing must also scale: use social media ads, attend trade fairs, approach more shops for wholesale. Keep tracking expenses and income as you grow. Reinvest a portion of profits into more inventory and equipment. Don't scale too fastincrease gradually. If you double production, make sure you can sell the extra units. Otherwise, you'll have dead stock. Set a goal: increase production by 20% each month. With careful planning, scaling can significantly increase your profit and turn your small business into a thriving enterprise.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Costing, Pricing & Profitability for African Cosmetics Producers

This practical reference consolidates the numbers work behind every jar of body butter, tube of lip balm and bottle of hair oil you sell. It is written for small and growing producers in Africa from a kitchen in Kumasi to a co-operative workshop in Nairobi where cash is tight, inputs are imported at volatile exchange rates, and one underpriced product can quietly drain the profit of an entire batch. Use it alongside the lessons on Cost of Goods Sold, wholesale and retail pricing, overheads, bundles, expense tracking and scaling.

The golden rule is simple: you cannot price what you have not cost. Every figure below should be recalculated each time a supplier price changes, because in most African markets shea butter, carrier oils, fragrance, packaging and freight can move 1040% within a single season.

2. Turning COGS into Prices

With a COGS of $1.10 per unit, you now apply margin logic:

  • Wholesale price = COGS (1 wholesale margin). At a 40% margin: 1.10 0.60 = $1.83. Round to $1.85.
  • Retail price = wholesale (1 retail margin). At a 50% retail margin: 1.85 0.50 = $3.70. Round to $3.75.
  • Contribution per unit at retail = 3.75 1.10 = $2.65, which must cover overheads and profit.

In many African markets, informal retailers expect 2540% off retail, while formal stockists in malls or pharmacies demand 4050%. Build this into your wholesale tier rather than discounting at the point of sale, which erodes trust with your retail partners.

3. Managing Overhead Costs

Overheads are every cost that is not directly tied to a unit: rent, electricity, generator fuel, internet, market stall fees, packaging design, accounting, licences (e.g. NAFDAC in Nigeria, KEBS in Kenya, Ghana FDA), and transport to markets. Allocate them per unit using a simple driver for example, split monthly overheads by the number of units produced. If overheads are $300/month and you produce 500 units, that is $0.60 per unit on top of COGS. If your contribution per unit is below $0.60, you are losing money on every sale.

SAFETY & COMPLIANCE WARNING

Never sell cosmetics without checking your national regulator: NAFDAC (Nigeria), KEBS (Kenya), Ghana FDA, South African NRCS/SABS, or your country's equivalent. Unregistered products can be seized, fined and destroyed, and mislabelled preservatives or essential oils can cause burns, allergies or microbial contamination. Always: (1) use a preservative in any water-containing product; (2) label every batch with ingredients, batch number and expiry; (3) keep a safety data sheet for every fragrance and essential oil; (4) never use unverified "natural" claims that imply medicinal treatment. Compliance costs belong in your overheads not as an afterthought.

4. Bundles, Special Offers and Scaling

Bundles raise average order value without discounting your hero product. Example: a "Glow Trio" of lip balm + body butter + hair oil priced at $9.50 when sold separately at $3.75 + $3.75 + $4.00 = $11.50. The bundle gives the customer 17% off but increases your cash per sale and reduces per-unit packaging and delivery costs. Only run bundles where the contribution margin of the bundle still exceeds the sum of individual contributions minus the discount.

Scaling profitably means buying inputs in larger volumes, negotiating with shea co-operatives (e.g. in northern Ghana, Burkina Faso, Nigeria) and moving from retail-only to a wholesale + retail mix. As volume rises, your per-unit overhead falls but so may your per-unit price if you chase volume with discounts. Track contribution margin, not revenue, as your north star.

5. Tracking Expenses and Income

Keep three simple ledgers: Sales (date, product, quantity, channel, price), Direct Costs (materials, packaging, labour per batch) and Overheads (rent, power, transport, fees). Reconcile weekly. Use a mobile tool such as Wave, Zoho Books, or even a structured Google Sheet. In markets with mobile money (M-Pesa, MTN MoMo, Opay), record each transaction with the reference number so you can match cash to stock.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Recalculate COGS every time a supplier price changes (at least monthly).
  2. Include packaging, labour and a fair share of overheads not just raw materials.
  3. Set wholesale margin at 3545% and retail margin at 4555% for African retail realities.
  4. Know your break-even units per month and check them against actual sales.
  5. Price bundles so the bundle contribution beats the sum of individual contributions.
  6. Record every sale and expense weekly cash flow kills more African cosmetics brands than competition.
  7. Register with your national regulator and budget the fees as overhead.
  8. Review prices quarterly against exchange-rate and freight movements.
