Module 7

🐠 Practical Fish Feed Formulation

The arithmetic module. Build an ingredient table with your own prices, then use Pearson's Square and simple balancing to produce real, copyable formulas for larvae, fingerlings, market fish and broodstock, with pellet binding, water stability and least-cost substitution worked out step by step.

What you will be able to do after this module

  • Build an ingredient table carrying protein, lysine, methionine, digestibility, inclusion limit and price
  • Set the protein, lipid and particle size targets for fry of your species
  • Set stage-correct protein targets for fingerlings and juveniles of each species
  • Formulate a 25-35 percent protein grow-out feed with mostly local plant ingredients
  • Formulate a 35-40 percent protein, 12-18 percent lipid conditioning feed
  • Substitute ingredients on equal protein rather than equal weight

Lesson 7.1~11 min

Fish Feed Ingredient Database

In this lesson
  • Build an ingredient table carrying protein, lysine, methionine, digestibility, inclusion limit and price
  • Calculate cost per protein point to compare ingredients honestly
  • Set the inclusion ceilings that every later formula must respect

Formulation is arithmetic on a table. If the table is wrong, everything downstream is wrong, so build it before you build a formula.

Here is the working database. Figures are crude protein percentage, lysine percentage, methionine percentage, digestibility in fish, and 2026 price per kilogram.

  1. Fish meal: 60-65 CP, 2.2-2.4 lysine, 0.9-1.1 methionine, 90-95 percent digestible, USD 1.50-3.00. Local artisanal fish meal at African landing sites is USD 0.05-0.15/kg.
  2. Soybean meal: 44-48 CP, 1.6-1.8 lysine, 0.4-0.5 methionine, 75-85 percent, USD 0.30-0.50.
  3. Cottonseed cake: 40-45 CP, 1.4-1.6 lysine, 0.4-0.5 methionine, 65-75 percent, USD 0.35-0.50.
  4. Sunflower cake: 38-42 CP, 1.2-1.4 lysine, 0.5-0.6 methionine, 70-80 percent, USD 0.40-0.60.
  5. Canola or rapeseed meal: 36-42 CP, 1.2-1.6 lysine, 0.5-0.6 methionine, 68-80 percent, USD 0.35-0.60.
  6. BSFL meal: 40-45 CP, 25-35 percent lipid, 85-90 percent digestible, USD 1.00-1.50, or USD 0.10-0.25 if you produce it yourself.
  7. Moringa leaf meal: 18-25 CP, 70-75 percent digestible, USD 0.10-0.20 or free if dried on farm.
  8. Maize, ground: about 9 CP, USD 0.18-0.25.
  9. Wheat: about 12 CP, USD 0.20-0.28, and a better binder than maize.
  10. Rice bran: 11-13 CP with 15-18 percent lipid, USD 0.10-0.18.
  11. Copra or coconut cake: 20-24 CP, 12-15 percent lipid, 60-70 percent digestible, USD 0.20-0.35.
  12. Brewer's yeast: 35-45 CP, 8-12 percent lipid, USD 0.30-0.60.
  13. Vegetable oil: USD 0.80-1.20.
  14. Vitamin premix, mineral premix: USD 0.03-0.05/kg of finished feed each.

Groundnut cake, maize bran and blood meal are common in African markets but the verified data set gives no composition for them. Treat them as approximate: groundnut cake is a mid-forties protein oilseed cake, maize bran is a low-protein high-fibre energy ingredient, blood meal is a very high protein animal meal that is poor in isoleucine. Get an actual analysis from your supplier or your national feed laboratory before you build a formula around any of them, and until then keep each under 10 percent of a mix.

Now the comparison that matters. Never compare ingredients on price per kilogram; compare them on price per protein point, meaning the cost of one kilogram of crude protein.

  • Soybean meal at USD 0.42/kg and 46 percent protein: 0.42 divided by 0.46 equals USD 0.91 per kg of protein.
  • Cottonseed cake at USD 0.40/kg and 42 percent: 0.40 / 0.42 = USD 0.95.
  • Imported fish meal at USD 2.20/kg and 62 percent: 2.20 / 0.62 = USD 3.55.
  • Local fish meal at USD 0.55/kg and 55 percent: 0.55 / 0.55 = USD 1.00.
  • Maize at USD 0.22/kg and 9 percent: 0.22 / 0.09 = USD 2.44 per kg of protein, which is why maize is bought for energy and never for protein.
  • Moringa leaf meal dried on farm at USD 0.10/kg and 21 percent: 0.10 / 0.21 = USD 0.48, the cheapest protein on the list, but capped by fibre.

That single column reorders your shopping. Local fish meal at USD 1.00 per kilogram of protein is better value than imported fish meal at USD 3.55 and competitive with soya, while carrying better amino acids and available phosphorus.

Finally, write the inclusion ceilings into the table, because least-cost arithmetic will always try to break them.

  • Moringa leaf meal: under 10 percent of feed protein.
  • Copra cake: under 15 percent of the mix.
  • Maize: under 20 percent, for fumonisin safety.
  • Rice bran: 5-10 percent, for fibre and rancidity.
  • Brewer's yeast: 10-20 percent.
  • BSFL meal: 30-50 percent.
  • Crude fibre: under 10 percent for catfish fry, 10-15 percent for tilapia fingerlings, 15-20 percent for carp.

Worked example, Nigeria. A farmer in Oyo State priced soybean meal at USD 0.45 and cottonseed cake at USD 0.36 and assumed cottonseed was the bargain. On protein points, soya is 0.45 / 0.46 = USD 0.98 and cottonseed is 0.36 / 0.42 = USD 0.86, so cottonseed does win, but its digestibility is 65-75 percent against 75-85 percent, so per kilogram of digestible protein the two are nearly identical. He blends them and stops worrying about it.

Lesson 7.2~12 min

Larval Feed Formulation

In this lesson
  • Set the protein, lipid and particle size targets for fry of your species
  • Run Pearson's Square to hit a 45-56 percent crude protein target
  • Adapt a dry formula into a crumble or powder a first-feeding fry can physically eat

Fry feed is the hardest formula to make and the one where mistakes cost most, because a fry that misses its first two weeks never catches up.

The targets. Nile tilapia fry need 50-56 percent crude protein; African catfish and channel catfish fry 45-50 percent; carp 45-50 percent. Lipid is 8-12 percent for tilapia fry and 10-12 percent for channel catfish fry, with EPA plus DHA at 0.8-1.5 percent for tilapia and 0.5-1.0 percent for catfish. Digestible energy is 3,300-3,500 kcal/kg with a protein to energy ratio of 6-7 kcal per gram of protein, which is deliberately tighter than the 8-10 used for grow-out. Vitamin C sits at 100-200 mg/kg and vitamin D3 at 1,000-2,000 IU/kg, and D3 must come from the feed because fish cannot make it from sunlight.

Particle size is a physical constraint, not a preference. Fry under two weeks have a mouth width below 1 mm and need particles of 0.8-1.5 mm, often simply powder. The rule of thumb is that pellet diameter should be about 60 percent of mouth width. Too small and the fry waste energy sorting; too large and they cannot take it at all.

Now the arithmetic. Pearson's Square balances two components to a target. Put the target in the middle, the two component protein values at the left corners, and subtract diagonally, always taking the smaller from the larger.

Work a 50 percent crude protein tilapia fry feed. Component one is fish meal at 62 percent protein. Component two is a basal energy mix; take equal parts maize at 9, wheat at 12 and rice bran at 12, which averages (9 + 12 + 12) / 3 = 11 percent protein.

Draw the square: 62 top left, 11 bottom left, 50 in the middle.

  • Top right: 50 minus 11 = 39 parts of fish meal.
  • Bottom right: 62 minus 50 = 12 parts of basal.
  • Total parts: 39 + 12 = 51.
  • Fish meal: 39 / 51 = 76.5 percent.
  • Basal: 12 / 51 = 23.5 percent.

Check it: (0.765 x 62) + (0.235 x 11) = 47.4 + 2.6 = 50.0 percent. Correct.

Now make it a real recipe for a 10 kg batch. Reserve 2 percent for premix and 1 percent for salt, so 97 percent is available for the square result. Fish meal 76.5 percent of 97 = 7.42 kg. Basal 23.5 percent of 97 = 2.28 kg, split as 0.76 kg maize, 0.76 kg wheat, 0.76 kg rice bran. Vitamin premix 0.1 kg, mineral premix 0.1 kg, salt 0.1 kg. That totals 10.0 kg.

Lipid check. Fish meal and rice bran bring lipid with them, and rice bran is 15-18 percent fat. If the mix falls short of 10 percent lipid, add oil, remembering that each percentage point of lipid adds 30-50 kcal/kg of digestible energy. For fry, part of that oil should be fish oil to supply the 0.8-1.5 percent EPA plus DHA, because vegetable oils contain none and fry convert plant omega-3 to DHA at only 2-5 percent efficiency.

Cost it. Using local fish meal at USD 0.55/kg: 7.42 x 0.55 = USD 4.08. Basal at about USD 0.20/kg: 2.28 x 0.20 = USD 0.46. Premixes and salt at roughly USD 0.30. Total USD 4.84 for 10 kg, or USD 0.48/kg. With imported fish meal at USD 2.20/kg the same batch costs 7.42 x 2.20 = USD 16.32 plus USD 0.76, so USD 1.71/kg. That single ingredient decision changes fry feed cost by more than three times.

For African catfish fry at 45 percent protein, rerun the square: fish meal parts = 45 - 11 = 34, basal parts = 62 - 45 = 17, total 51, so fish meal 66.7 percent and basal 33.3 percent. Five protein points lower cuts the fish meal share by ten points.

Processing. Cook the starch fraction, because raw starch digests at 20-40 percent in a carnivorous fry against 50-70 percent gelatinised. Mix into a stiff dough, steam it, dry it, then grind and sieve to under 1 mm for first feeding and 1.5 mm from two weeks. Module 11 Lesson 4 covers the feeding schedule for the first eight weeks; this lesson is only about what goes in the bag.

Lesson 7.3~11 min

Fingerling and Juvenile Feed Formulation

In this lesson
  • Set stage-correct protein targets for fingerlings and juveniles of each species
  • Use a two-stage Pearson's Square with a blended protein concentrate
  • Balance lysine and methionine with supplements once the square is solved

Fingerling feed is where most farm-mixed feed is actually used, and where the arithmetic starts to pay because you can substitute plant protein without hurting growth.

The targets. Nile tilapia fingerlings need 40-45 percent crude protein and juveniles 30-40 percent. African catfish fingerlings need 40-45 and juveniles 35-40. Channel catfish fingerlings 35-40 and juveniles 32-35. Carp fingerlings 35-42 and juveniles 30-36. Digestible energy is 3,000-3,300 kcal/kg with a protein to energy ratio of 8.5-9.5 for channel catfish fingerlings. Pellet diameter is 1.5-3 mm for early fingerlings, 2-4 mm at 8-12 weeks and 3-6 mm for juveniles, often as a 50:50 crumble and pellet mix during the transition.

The method is Pearson's Square again, but with a blended protein concentrate instead of a single ingredient, because no single plant meal reaches a fingerling target on its own.

Step one: build the concentrate. Take 70 percent soybean meal at 46 and 30 percent fish meal at 62. Its protein is (0.70 x 46) + (0.30 x 62) = 32.2 + 18.6 = 50.8 percent.

Step two: build the basal. Equal parts maize at 9 and rice bran at 12 gives 10.5 percent.

Step three: run the square for a 40 percent tilapia fingerling feed.

  • Concentrate parts: 40 minus 10.5 = 29.5.
  • Basal parts: 50.8 minus 40 = 10.8.
  • Total: 40.3.
  • Concentrate: 29.5 / 40.3 = 73.2 percent.
  • Basal: 10.8 / 40.3 = 26.8 percent.

Check: (0.732 x 50.8) + (0.268 x 10.5) = 37.2 + 2.8 = 40.0 percent. Correct.

Step four: break the concentrate back into ingredients and make room for the additives. Reserve 4 percent of the batch for vitamin premix, mineral premix, salt and oil, leaving 96 percent for the square. Concentrate 73.2 percent of 96 = 70.3 percent of the batch, of which soya is 70 percent, so 49.2 percent, and fish meal 30 percent, so 21.1 percent. Basal 26.8 percent of 96 = 25.7 percent, split 12.9 maize and 12.8 rice bran.

For a 100 kg batch: soybean meal 49.2 kg, fish meal 21.1 kg, maize 12.9 kg, rice bran 12.8 kg, vegetable oil 2.0 kg, vitamin premix 1.0 kg, mineral premix 1.0 kg, salt 1.0 kg. Total 101.0 kg, so trim 1 kg off the maize to land at 100.

Step five: check the amino acids, because crude protein is not the whole story. Lysine contributed: soya 49.2 kg at 1.7 percent gives 0.84 kg; fish meal 21.1 kg at 2.3 percent gives 0.49 kg; total 1.33 kg in 100 kg, so 1.33 percent of the diet. Tilapia at grow-out need 1.2-1.5 percent, so that passes. Methionine: soya at 0.45 percent gives 0.22 kg; fish meal at 1.0 percent gives 0.21 kg; total 0.43 percent. Tilapia fry need 0.9-1.0 percent methionine plus cystine and the working total for practical feeds is 0.65-0.75 percent, so this formula is short. Add DL-methionine at 0.25 percent of the batch, 250 g, costing about USD 1.00 at USD 3-6/kg. That is the classic cheap fix: amino acid supplementation costs USD 0.02-0.05/kg of feed and returns 2 to 5 to 1 through 5-10 percent better growth and an FCR gain of 0.05-0.10.

Cost the batch, Kenyan prices. Soya 49.2 x 0.45 = USD 22.14. Fish meal 21.1 x 0.55 = USD 11.61. Maize 11.9 x 0.22 = USD 2.62. Rice bran 12.8 x 0.15 = USD 1.92. Oil 2.0 x 1.00 = USD 2.00. Premixes and salt USD 3.00. Methionine USD 1.00. Total USD 44.29 for 100 kg, or USD 0.44/kg, against retail pellet at USD 0.60-0.80/kg.

Worked example, Kenya. A farmer at Sagana mixes 400 kg of this feed a month. At USD 0.44/kg he spends USD 176 against USD 280 at a retail USD 0.70/kg, saving USD 104 a month, USD 1,248 a year, on one formula sheet and a day of mixing every fortnight.

Lesson 7.4~12 min

Market-Size Fish Feed Formulation

In this lesson
  • Formulate a 25-35 percent protein grow-out feed with mostly local plant ingredients
  • Check the protein to energy ratio before accepting a formula
  • Choose a binder and test the pellet for water stability and durability

Grow-out feed is the largest volume you will ever mix, so a one-cent saving per kilogram matters, and it is also the stage where fish can tolerate the most plant protein.

The targets. Nile tilapia grow-out needs 25-32 percent crude protein, African catfish 28-35, channel catfish 25-28, carp 25-30. Digestible energy is 3,000-3,300 kcal/kg for tilapia and 3,200-3,400 for African catfish. Lipid is 5-8 percent for tilapia grow-out and 7-10 for African catfish. Pellet diameter is 4-8 mm, with 6-8 mm preferred for tilapia and 5-6 mm for catfish.

Formulate a 30 percent tilapia grow-out feed with no imported fish meal at all. Concentrate: 50 percent soybean meal at 46 and 50 percent cottonseed cake at 42, giving (0.5 x 46) + (0.5 x 42) = 44 percent. Basal: equal maize at 9 and rice bran at 12, so 10.5 percent.

Square for 30:

  • Concentrate parts: 30 minus 10.5 = 19.5.
  • Basal parts: 44 minus 30 = 14.
  • Total: 33.5.
  • Concentrate: 19.5 / 33.5 = 58.2 percent.
  • Basal: 14 / 33.5 = 41.8 percent.

Check: (0.582 x 44) + (0.418 x 10.5) = 25.6 + 4.4 = 30.0 percent. Correct.

For a 100 kg batch, reserving 5 percent for premix, salt, oil and binder: concentrate 58.2 percent of 95 = 55.3 kg, so 27.7 kg soya and 27.6 kg cottonseed cake. Basal 41.8 percent of 95 = 39.7 kg, but rice bran is capped at 5-10 percent for fibre and rancidity, so take 9 kg rice bran and 20 kg maize, holding maize under the 20 percent mycotoxin ceiling, and make up the remaining 10.7 kg with maize bran or wheat. Then 3 kg oil, 1 kg vitamin premix, 1 kg mineral premix, 1 kg salt.

Now the protein to energy check, which is the step most farm mixers skip. The optimum is 8-10 kcal of digestible energy per gram of protein. A 30 percent protein feed at 3,000 kcal/kg gives 3,000 / 300 = 10.0 kcal per gram, right at the top of the band. Below 8 the fish deposits fat and wastes feed; above 10 it burns protein for energy, which is expensive and produces ammonia. If your formula lands above 10, add oil, since each percentage point of lipid adds 30-50 kcal/kg. If it lands below 8, cut oil or raise protein.

Pellet binding and water stability decide whether the fish eat what you paid for. In a manual feeding pond, stability under four hours means 20-30 percent of the nutrients leach into the water before the fish get them, and another 10-15 percent is lost uneaten to the sediment, so total waste reaches 30-45 percent. Commercial specification is over eight hours of water stability.

The binder options, with their documented stability, leaching at two hours and cost:

  1. Fish meal plus wheat flour, low starch: 2-4 hours, 20-30 percent leaching. Baseline cost. Poor in warm water.
  2. Fish meal plus gelatinised maize starch: 4-8 hours, 10-15 percent leaching, about 5 percent more cost. Cooking the starch is what makes this work.
  3. Soy lecithin at 1-2 percent: 6-12 hours, 5-10 percent leaching, 10-15 percent more cost.
  4. Alginate, CMC or bentonite binder: 8-24 hours, under 5 percent leaching, 5-10 percent more cost.
  5. Oil-coated pellet: 12-24 hours, under 3 percent leaching, 15-20 percent more cost, with a rancidity risk.

The cost adder for real stability is USD 0.10-0.30/kg. For a farm mixing at USD 0.40/kg that is significant, but losing 30 percent of a USD 0.40 feed costs USD 0.12/kg, so a bentonite or gelatinised-starch route usually pays.

Test durability too. The durability index is the percentage of pellets still intact after ten minutes of tumbling, and the target is above 80 percent. Below 60 percent the feed breaks into fines that never get eaten and instead foul the water.

Worked example, Zambia. A farmer at Mkushi mixes 1,000 kg of the formula above. Soya 277 kg at USD 0.45 = USD 124.65. Cottonseed 276 kg at USD 0.38 = USD 104.88. Maize 200 kg at USD 0.22 = USD 44. Rice bran 90 kg at USD 0.15 = USD 13.50. Maize bran 107 kg at USD 0.17 = USD 18.19. Oil 30 kg at USD 1.00 = USD 30. Premixes and salt USD 30. Total USD 365.22, so USD 0.37/kg. Adding bentonite and cooking the starch takes him to about USD 0.45/kg and cuts leaching from 20-30 percent to under 5 percent. He is still USD 0.25/kg below retail feed.

Broodstock Feed Formulation

In this lesson
  • Formulate a 35-40 percent protein, 12-18 percent lipid conditioning feed
  • Add lipid, phospholipid and fish oil in the right order without unbalancing protein
  • Cost a broodstock batch and express it as cost per fry produced

A broodstock formula is a fingerling formula with more fat, more vitamins and one deliberate purchase of fish oil. The arithmetic is the same; the sequence is what matters, because adding oil dilutes protein and you have to allow for it.

The targets from Module 5: 35-40 percent crude protein, 12-18 percent lipid, EPA plus DHA 1.5-2.5 percent, phospholipid 3-5 percent, vitamin C 200-300 mg/kg, vitamin E 200-400 mg/kg.

Formulate a 38 percent protein, 15 percent lipid African catfish broodstock feed for a 100 kg batch.

Step one: decide the non-protein reservations first, because they take space out of the square. Oil 8 kg, soy lecithin 4 kg, vitamin premix 1.5 kg, mineral premix 1.5 kg, salt 1 kg. That is 16 kg reserved, leaving 84 kg for the protein and basal fraction.

Step two: correct the target for dilution. If 84 kg must supply all the protein for a 100 kg batch at 38 percent, that 84 kg must itself test at 38 / 0.84 = 45.2 percent crude protein.

Step three: build the concentrate. Take 50 percent fish meal at 62 and 50 percent soybean meal at 46, giving (0.5 x 62) + (0.5 x 46) = 54 percent. Basal is maize and rice bran in equal parts at 10.5 percent.

Step four: run the square on 45.2.

  • Concentrate parts: 45.2 minus 10.5 = 34.7.
  • Basal parts: 54 minus 45.2 = 8.8.
  • Total: 43.5.
  • Concentrate: 34.7 / 43.5 = 79.8 percent.
  • Basal: 8.8 / 43.5 = 20.2 percent.

Step five: convert to kilograms of the 84 kg fraction. Concentrate 79.8 percent of 84 = 67.0 kg, so 33.5 kg fish meal and 33.5 kg soybean meal. Basal 20.2 percent of 84 = 17.0 kg, so 8.5 kg maize and 8.5 kg rice bran.

The full recipe for 100 kg: fish meal 33.5, soybean meal 33.5, maize 8.5, rice bran 8.5, oil 8.0, soy lecithin 4.0, vitamin premix 1.5, mineral premix 1.5, salt 1.0.

Check the protein: (33.5 x 0.62) + (33.5 x 0.46) + (8.5 x 0.09) + (8.5 x 0.12) = 20.77 + 15.41 + 0.77 + 1.02 = 37.97 kg in 100 kg, so 38.0 percent. Correct.

Check the lipid. The 8 kg of added oil is 8 percent. Rice bran at 15-18 percent lipid contributes 8.5 x 0.165 = 1.4 kg. Fish meal and soya bring roughly a further 2-3 kg between them, and lecithin adds its own. The mix lands in the 12-15 percent region, at the lower end of the 12-18 target, which is appropriate for phase one conditioning; for phase two raise oil to 11-12 kg and rerun the dilution correction, since 100 minus 20 reserved is 80 kg, needing 38 / 0.80 = 47.5 percent in the protein fraction.

The fish oil decision. Of the 8 kg of oil, make 3-4 kg fish oil. Fish oil is 15-25 percent EPA plus DHA, so 3.5 kg contributes roughly 0.5-0.9 kg to a 100 kg batch, which is 0.5-0.9 percent of the diet, with fish meal supplying more. A 50:50 fish and vegetable oil blend is documented at 7-12 percent EPA plus DHA in the oil fraction. Push toward the higher fish oil share if you are chasing the top of the 1.5-2.5 percent target, and hold vitamin E at 300-400 mg/kg to stop it oxidising.

Cost, Ghanaian prices. Fish meal 33.5 kg at USD 0.90 = USD 30.15. Soya 33.5 at USD 0.45 = USD 15.08. Maize 8.5 at USD 0.22 = USD 1.87. Rice bran 8.5 at USD 0.15 = USD 1.28. Vegetable oil 4.5 at USD 1.00 = USD 4.50. Fish oil 3.5 at USD 3.00 = USD 10.50. Lecithin 4 at USD 2.00 = USD 8.00. Premixes at broodstock rate and salt USD 6.00. Total USD 77.38 for 100 kg, USD 0.77/kg. That sits well below the USD 1.50-3.00/kg quoted for a bought broodstock diet.

Worked example, Ghana. A hatchery near Akosombo needs 277 kg for one conditioning run on 33 kg of broodstock. At USD 0.77/kg that is USD 213. If the run produces 250,000 fry, feed cost per fry is 213 / 250,000 = USD 0.00085, inside the documented USD 0.001-0.005 band, against a fry value of USD 0.03-0.10.

Lesson 7.6~12 min

Least-Cost Fish Feed Formulation

In this lesson
  • Substitute ingredients on equal protein rather than equal weight
  • Apply constraints so that a cheap formula stays a usable formula
  • Rank feed cost changes by their effect on cost per kilogram of fish, not per kilogram of feed

Least-cost formulation is not about finding the cheapest bag. It is about finding the cheapest way to deliver a fixed nutrient specification, and then checking that the fish can still eat and digest it.

The method has four steps, and they must be done in order.

  1. Fix the specification. Crude protein, lipid, digestible energy, lysine, methionine, phosphorus. These do not move. A 30 percent tilapia grow-out feed at 3,000 kcal/kg with 1.2 percent lysine is the target regardless of what ingredients cost this month.
  2. List every ingredient with its protein, price and inclusion ceiling. Ceilings are not negotiable either: maize under 20 percent, rice bran 5-10 percent, copra under 15 percent, Moringa under 10 percent of feed protein, BSFL 30-50 percent, fibre under 10-15 percent depending on species.
  3. Substitute on equal protein, never equal weight. If you remove 10 kg of soybean meal at 46 percent, you have removed 4.6 kg of protein. Replacing it with cottonseed cake at 42 percent needs 4.6 / 0.42 = 11.0 kg, not 10 kg. Replacing it with maize bran at roughly 10 percent would need 46 kg, which will not fit in the batch. That single arithmetic step is what separates a formula from a guess.
  4. Recheck the constraints after every substitution: protein to energy back in the 8-10 band, lysine and methionine still met, fibre under the ceiling, and the pellet still binding.

Worked substitution, Uganda. A farmer near Masaka runs the 30 percent grow-out formula from Lesson 4 at USD 0.37/kg. Soya rises from USD 0.45 to USD 0.60/kg. His 277 kg of soya per tonne now costs USD 166 instead of USD 125, pushing the tonne to USD 0.41/kg.

Option A: replace 100 kg of soya with cottonseed cake at USD 0.38. Protein removed is 46 kg, so cottonseed needed is 46 / 0.42 = 109.5 kg. Cost change is minus 100 x 0.60 = minus USD 60, plus 109.5 x 0.38 = plus USD 41.61, a saving of USD 18.39 per tonne, or USD 0.018/kg. But cottonseed is now 385 kg per tonne, 38.5 percent of the mix, and its digestibility is 65-75 percent against soya at 75-85, so he must watch growth.

Option B: introduce his own BSFL meal at USD 0.20/kg, produced on farm, replacing 100 kg of soya. Protein removed 46 kg; BSFL at 42 percent needs 46 / 0.42 = 109.5 kg. Cost change is minus USD 60, plus 109.5 x 0.20 = plus USD 21.90, a saving of USD 38.10 per tonne, or USD 0.038/kg. BSFL is 85-90 percent digestible, better than the soya it replaced, and brings 25-35 percent lipid so he can cut 3-4 kg of oil, saving another USD 3-4. He must add DL-methionine because BSFL is methionine-poor.

Option C: add Moringa leaf meal dried on his own trees, replacing 60 kg of soya. Protein removed 27.6 kg; Moringa at 21 percent needs 131 kg, which is 13 percent of the mix and pushes Moringa past the 10 percent of feed protein ceiling and the fibre ceiling with it. Rejected on constraint, not on price.

Now the most important test, and the one that reorders everything. Cost per kilogram of feed is not the objective. Cost per kilogram of fish produced is. That is feed cost per kilogram multiplied by FCR.

  • Formula at USD 0.37/kg with FCR 1.8: 0.37 x 1.8 = USD 0.67 per kg of fish.
  • Cheaper formula at USD 0.33/kg but raw, ungelatinised, so FCR worsens 10-20 percent to 2.05: 0.33 x 2.05 = USD 0.68 per kg of fish. The saving has vanished.
  • Formula at USD 0.45/kg with binder, cooked starch and balanced amino acids, FCR 1.55: 0.45 x 1.55 = USD 0.70. Slightly more expensive per kilogram of fish but with less waste in the pond, better water quality and fewer losses.

This is why chasing the cheapest bag is a trap. A feed that saves USD 0.04/kg and costs 0.25 of FCR loses money. Module 8 takes this arithmetic all the way to margin per pond per cycle.

Three rules to close on. First, change one thing at a time and give it a full cycle before judging it, comparing against a pond fed the old formula. Second, keep a formula sheet with the date and prices on it so you can see what actually changed. Third, never let least-cost arithmetic break an inclusion ceiling, because the money you save on the mix comes straight back as poor growth, fouled water and dead fish.

Module 7 capstone

Formulate, mix, feed and cost one 50 kg batch of feed for the class of fish you have most of. Start from your own priced ingredient table. Choose your target crude protein from the species and stage figures in this module, set your protein concentrate mix and your basal energy mix, and run Pearson's Square to get the two proportions. Break the concentrate share back out into individual ingredients, subtract 2 percent for premix and 1 percent for salt, and add oil to reach your lipid target. Write the full recipe in kilograms for a 50 kg batch, then price every line and calculate cost per kilogram of finished feed. Cook or gelatinise the starch fraction, mix wet, pellet or crumble to the right diameter for your fish's mouth width, and dry to under 10 percent moisture. Before you feed it, run a water stability test: put ten pellets in a glass of pond water and record whether they hold together at 30 minutes, 1 hour, 2 hours and 4 hours. Feed the batch to one pond or tank, weigh a sample of fish before and after, record the feed used, and calculate your FCR and your cost per kilogram of fish gain. Compare that figure with what the same gain would have cost you on bought feed. Keep the recipe, the cost and the FCR together on one page, because that page is the basis of every formulation decision you make afterwards.
