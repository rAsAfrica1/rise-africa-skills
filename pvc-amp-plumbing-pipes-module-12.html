1. Bidding for Larger Contracts (Schools, Clinics, Churches)

When you bid for a school, clinic, or church job, you are not just selling pipesyou are selling reliability. These institutions need a plumber who can handle a big system: multiple toilets, sinks, a kitchen, maybe a water tank. The first step is to visit the site and measure everything. Count the number of toilets, sinks, and floor drains. Note the distance from the main water line to each fixture. For a typical school with 10 toilets, 6 sinks, and 2 water tanks, you might need 50 meters of 25mm PVC pipe, 30 meters of 50mm PVC pipe, 20 elbows, 10 tees, and 5 ball valves. Use a tape measure and a notebook. Then calculate materials cost using current prices from your local supplier. Add 15% for waste and mistakes. Next, estimate labor: a helper and you can install about 5 fixtures per day. For 18 fixtures, thats 4 days. Add 2 days for trenching and testing. Your bid should include materials, labor, transport, and a 20% profit margin. For example, if materials cost 500,000 shillings and labor is 200,000, your total cost is 700,000. Add 20% profit = 840,000 shillings. Present your bid in writing with a clear breakdown. Schools and clinics often require a formal quotation on letterhead. Include your phone number and a deadline for acceptance. Always ask for 50% upfront to buy materials. This protects you from spending your own money. Finally, follow up politely after one week. Many jobs are won by the plumber who shows up on time and communicates clearly.

2. Hiring and Training Apprentices

You cannot scale up alone. You need apprenticesyoung people willing to learn. Start by hiring one or two apprentices from your community. Look for someone who is punctual, honest, and not afraid of hard work. A good apprentice does not need experience; you will train them. Pay them a daily wage, not a monthly salary, so they understand that work equals money. In many African countries, a fair daily wage for an apprentice is 5,000 to 10,000 shillings (or equivalent). Train them in stages. First week: tool safety and names. Show them how to use a pipe cutter, a deburring tool, and a measuring tape. Second week: cutting and joining PVC pipes. Teach them the correct amount of gluetoo much makes a mess, too little leaks. Third week: installing simple fixtures like a tap or a toilet. Always demonstrate first, then let them try while you watch. Correct mistakes immediately. Never let an apprentice work alone on a pressurized line until they have proven they can do it right three times. Create a simple checklist for each task: measure, cut, clean, apply glue, twist, hold for 30 seconds. Have them repeat the checklist aloud. After one month, give them a small test: install a sink drain. If they pass, increase their responsibility. Also teach them customer service: greet the client, wear clean clothes, clean up after work. Your reputation depends on their behavior. Treat them wellprovide water, gloves, and a safe working environment. A loyal apprentice will become your best worker and eventually a partner.

3. Managing Multiple Jobs and Scheduling

When you have three jobs at oncea house repair, a school bid, and a clinic installationyou need a schedule. Use a simple notebook or a whiteboard. Write each job on a separate line. For each job, list the tasks and how many days each will take. For example: Job A (house repair) needs 2 days. Job B (school bid) needs 1 day for site visit and 1 day for quote. Job C (clinic) needs 5 days. Total = 9 days. Now look at your calendar. Can you overlap? You can visit the school in the morning and work on the house in the afternoon. But you cannot be in two places at once. So assign your apprentices. If you have two apprentices, send one to the house to do prep work (cutting pipes) while you and the other apprentice work at the clinic. Use your phone to set alarms for start and stop times. At the end of each day, spend 10 minutes updating your schedule. What got done? What is delayed? If a job is delayed, call the client immediately. Do not wait for them to call you. For larger jobs, create a simple Gantt chart on paper: draw bars for each task across days. This helps you see conflicts. Always keep one day per week free for emergenciesleaks, callbacks, or material pickups. Never promise a completion date you cannot meet. It is better to say I will finish by Friday and finish on Thursday. Also, keep a list of your tools and where they are. A missing pipe wrench can stop a whole job. Finally, charge a deposit for each job to cover materials. This keeps cash flowing even when you are juggling multiple sites.

4. Sourcing Materials Directly from Manufacturers

Buying from a local hardware store is convenient, but you pay a middleman markup. To scale up, go directly to the manufacturer or a regional distributor. For example, in Kenya, you can contact companies like Kenpoly or Plastics Kenya. In Nigeria, try Interplast or Europlast. In South Africa, look for DPI Plastics or Marley. Call them and ask for a price list for bulk orders. A typical manufacturer sells 25mm PVC pipes in bundles of 10. If you buy 10 bundles (100 pipes), you might get a 15% discount compared to retail. For a school job needing 50 pipes, that saves you money. But you must buy in bulkoften a minimum order of 50,000 shillings or more. Plan ahead: combine orders for multiple jobs. For example, if you have three jobs coming up, calculate total pipes, fittings, and glue needed. Then place one large order. You will need transporta pickup truck or a boda boda with a trailer. Hire a truck for 20,000 to 50,000 shillings depending on distance. Always inspect the goods before paying. Check for cracks, warped pipes, or missing fittings. Ask for a receipt and a warranty. Some manufacturers offer credit terms: pay 50% now, 50% in 30 days. This helps your cash flow. Build a relationship with the sales manager. Visit the factory, shake hands, and explain that you are a growing plumbing business. They may give you better prices or priority during shortages. Also, ask about training on new products like PPR or CPVC. Finally, store materials in a dry, shaded place. PVC degrades in strong sunlight. Keep pipes off the ground on wooden pallets.

5. Financing Growth: Savings Groups and Micro-Loans

To take on bigger jobs, you need money for materials and labor before the client pays. Two common sources in Africa are savings groups (like SACCOs, VSLA, or chama) and micro-loans. A savings group is a circle of trusted people who contribute money regularly. For example, 20 members each put in 10,000 shillings per month. The total pot is 200,000 shillings. Members can borrow from the pot at a low interest rate, say 5% per month. To join, you must be known in the community and attend meetings. Use the loan to buy materials for a school bid. When the school pays you, repay the loan plus interest. The advantage is low interest and no collateral. The disadvantage is the loan size is limited. A micro-loan from a bank or mobile money service (like M-Shwari, Kiva, or Tala) can be largerup to 500,000 shillings. But interest rates are higher, often 10-20% per month. Only take a micro-loan if you have a signed contract and a clear repayment plan. For example, if you borrow 200,000 shillings at 10% for 3 months, you repay 260,000. Your job must earn at least 300,000 profit to be worth it. Always keep a simple record book: date, amount borrowed, amount repaid, balance. Never mix business and personal money. Start small: borrow only what you need for materials. As you build a credit history, you can access larger loans. Also, consider asset financing: some suppliers will give you materials on credit if you have a good relationship. That is free financing. Finally, save 10% of every payment into a separate account for emergencies. This prevents you from borrowing when a tool breaks.

6. Final Project: Plan and Install a Complete Plumbing System for a Small House

For your final project, you will plan and install a complete plumbing system for a small house with one bathroom, one kitchen, and an outdoor tap. The house has a water tank on a 2-meter stand. You will use PVC pipes for drainage and PPR or PVC for supply. First, draw a floor plan. Mark the kitchen sink, bathroom sink, toilet, shower, and outdoor tap. Measure the distance from the tank to each fixture. For supply, use 25mm pipe for the main line and 20mm for branches. You need a ball valve at the tank outlet and a check valve to prevent backflow. For drainage, use 110mm pipe for the toilet, 50mm for sinks and shower, and 40mm for the outdoor tap drain. Slope all horizontal drainage pipes at 1 cm per meter (1%) so waste flows. Install a P-trap under each sink and a floor drain in the bathroom. Cut pipes with a pipe cutter or hacksaw, deburr the ends, and apply PVC solvent cement. Twist the pipe a quarter turn and hold for 30 seconds. For PPR, use a heat fusion tool. Test the system: cap all openings, fill with water, and pressurize to 5 bar for 30 minutes. Check for leaks. Then install fixtures: toilet, taps, showerhead. Connect the toilet to the 110mm pipe with a wax ring or rubber gasket. Finally, backfill trenches and clean up. Your total material list: 20m of 25mm PPR, 15m of 20mm PPR, 10m of 110mm PVC, 15m of 50mm PVC, 10 elbows, 5 tees, 2 ball valves, 1 check valve, 3 P-traps, 1 toilet, 2 sinks, 1 shower, 1 outdoor tap, and 1kg of solvent cement. Budget: 600,000 shillings. Time: 5 days with one helper. This project proves you can handle a full housethe first step to community projects.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Scaling Up from Single Jobs to Community Projects

Moving from one-off household repairs to community-scale contracts schools, clinics, churches, small housing developments is the single biggest income jump available to a trained plumber in Africa. It is also the point where most tradespeople fail, not because their pipework is bad, but because their business systems are not ready. This reference lesson gives you the practical tools: how to price and bid, how to build a crew, how to schedule multiple sites, how to cut out the middleman on materials, and how to finance the jump without destroying your cash flow.

Work through it in the order below. Each section builds on the last. The final project at the end of the module tests all six skills together.

1. Bidding for Larger Contracts (Schools, Clinics, Churches)

Institutional clients buy differently from households. A homeowner negotiates on price and trust. A school board, clinic administrator or church committee buys on written scope, compliance and reliability. They usually need three quotes, they pay by bank transfer or cheque, and they will hold retention money (commonly 510%) until defects are fixed.

Your bid must contain five things: (1) a scope statement describing exactly what you will install, (2) a bill of quantities (BoQ) with pipe sizes, fittings, tanks, pumps and labour separated, (3) a timeline with milestones, (4) your compliance documents (registration, tax PIN, NCA or local authority registration where required), and (5) terms payment schedule, retention, defects liability period, and validity of the quote (typically 30 days, because pipe prices move).

Price using a build-up, not a guess. Take material cost, add 1015% for wastage and small fittings, add labour at a realistic daily rate, add transport and equipment, add 10% overhead, then add profit of 1525%. Institutional work carries more risk than domestic work price for it.

Never bid below your material cost to "win the job and negotiate later." Institutional clients rarely renegotiate upward, and you will fund the project from your own pocket.

2. Hiring and Training Apprentices

You cannot scale on your own hands. The African plumbing sector has a deep informal apprenticeship tradition use it deliberately rather than accidentally. A workable crew structure for community projects is: 1 lead plumber (you or a trusted senior), 12 skilled plumbers, 23 apprentices/helpers.

Recruit apprentices from local vocational training centres (NITA-registered in Kenya, NABTEB in Nigeria, or equivalent TVET institutions). Pay a stipend, not "exposure." Train on the job against a written checklist of competencies: pipe threading, solvent welding, pressure testing, reading drawings, and critically customer conduct and safety. Keep a simple training log per apprentice. This protects you legally and produces a plumber you can trust on a site without you.

Pay structure that works: skilled plumbers on daily rate plus a completion bonus; apprentices on a lower daily rate with a raise tied to demonstrated competency. Never pay the whole crew a flat rate it kills incentive and hides poor performers.

3. Managing Multiple Jobs and Scheduling

The killer of growing plumbing businesses is overlapping commitments. Two clients expecting you on the same morning means one of them fires you. The fix is a simple scheduling discipline:

  • One master board (physical or WhatsApp group) showing every active job, its stage, and who is assigned.
  • Never start a new job until the previous one has passed pressure testing and handover.
  • Assign a crew leader to each site so you are not the single point of failure.
  • Book material deliveries to arrive the day before work starts, not the morning of.
  • Build a 23 day buffer into every institutional timeline African supply chains and weather demand it.

Track three numbers weekly: jobs completed, days per job, and cash collected. If days per job is rising, you have a scheduling or crew problem, not a pricing problem.

4. Sourcing Materials Directly from Manufacturers

Buying from a local hardware shop at retail prices is the fastest way to lose your margin. Once you are doing community-scale work, go direct to manufacturers or regional distributors of PPR, PVC, HDPE and GI pipes (for example, companies like Plastics Pipe Institute members operating across East and West Africa, or regional manufacturers of PPR systems).

Direct sourcing typically saves 1530% on pipe and fittings. Requirements: buy in volume, pay promptly (or negotiate 30-day terms once you have a track record), and collect with your own transport. Always demand a manufacturer's certificate of conformity and batch numbers institutional clients increasingly require it, and counterfeit pipe is a serious problem in the region.

5. Financing Growth: Savings Groups and Micro-Loans

Institutional jobs pay after completion, but materials must be bought before. That gap is where growth is won or lost. Three financing routes dominate in Africa:

  • Savings groups (SACCOs, chamas, stokvels, esusu): Rotating savings and credit associations are the cheapest and most accessible capital. Contribute weekly, borrow 23 your contribution at low interest, repay over 36 months. Ideal for buying a welding machine or a first bulk material order.
  • Micro-loans (MFIs): Faster and larger than savings groups, but interest of 24% per month adds up. Use only for jobs with confirmed payment dates. Never borrow for a bid you have not won.
  • Supplier credit: Once you have a track record, negotiate 30-day terms with your manufacturer. This is the cheapest growth capital of all it is free if you pay on time.

Rule of thumb: never take a loan whose repayment exceeds 30% of the expected contract value, and never finance a job where the client has not signed a written agreement with a payment schedule.

SAFETY WARNING Pressure Testing and Confined Spaces

Never pressure-test a newly installed system with people standing near open fittings, and never enter a septic tank, manhole or inspection chamber without a harness, a second person on the surface, and forced ventilation. Hydrogen sulphide in African septic systems can kill in seconds. Institutional sites often have deep chambers treat every one as a confined space. Also: PPR welding tools reach 260C; keep apprentices away from the heating element and never leave a welder plugged in unattended. On school and clinic sites, isolate the water supply before any cut-in and tag the valve.

6. Final Project: Plan and Install a Complete Plumbing System for a Small House

The capstone for this module is a full design-and-install for a small two-bedroom house (roughly 6080 m). You must produce, and be assessed on, all six artefacts:

  1. A scaled layout drawing showing incoming main, tank position, pump, distribution manifold, and every fixture.
  2. A bill of quantities with pipe sizes, fittings, sanitaryware and labour, priced using the build-up method above.
  3. A written bid document with timeline, payment schedule and defects liability terms.
  4. A crew plan naming each worker, their role and their daily rate.
  5. A material sourcing plan showing at least one direct-from-manufacturer order with certificate of conformity.
  6. A financing plan showing how materials are funded before the client pays, and how the loan (if any) is repaid.

Installation must pass a pressure test at 1.5 working pressure for at least 30 minutes with no drop, and a drainage flow test. Hand over with a written defects liability period of 6 months.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Bid includes written scope, BoQ, timeline, compliance docs and 30-day validity.
  • Profit margin is 1525% never bid at material cost.
  • Crew has a named lead, skilled plumbers and apprentices with a training log.
  • Master job board shows every active site, stage and assigned crew.
  • Materials ordered direct from manufacturer with certificate of conformity.
  • Financing arranged before materials are purchased; loan repayment under 30% of contract value.
  • Pressure test at 1.5 working pressure, 30 minutes, zero drop, documented.
  • Written handover with defects liability period and retention terms.
