1. The Kota: Bread, Chips, Fillings

A kota is a quarter loaf of bread hollowed out and filled with chips, then layered with anything from cheese and polony to Russian sausage, egg, and chakalaka. To start, buy a fresh white loaf and cut it into quarters. For each kota, take one quarter and remove the soft inside, leaving a thick shell. Save the removed bread for making breadcrumbs or croutons. You need a deep fryer or a large pot with oil heated to 180C. Cut potatoes into 1cm thick chips, rinse in cold water, and dry well. Fry them for 5 minutes until soft, then remove and drain. Just before serving, fry again for 2-3 minutes until golden and crispy. This double-frying gives you the perfect crunch. While chips fry, prepare fillings. Common options: Russian sausages sliced lengthwise, fried eggs, grated cheese, polony, and atchar (mango pickle). For a classic kota, layer chips first, then add a slice of cheese, a fried egg, and a few slices of Russian. Top with atchar and a dash of vinegar or peri-peri sauce. For a vegetarian version, use grilled mushrooms, peppers, and cheese. To keep costs low, buy bread in bulk from a bakery, potatoes in 10kg bags, and sausages from a wholesaler. A single kota can sell for R25-R40 depending on fillings. Your profit margin is highest when you use cheap fillings like polony and egg. Set up a stall near a taxi rank or school. Keep your oil clean by straining it daily. Serve kotas in a paper bag or wrapped in newspaper. Always wear gloves and keep a clean workspace. With a small gas burner, a pot, and a few utensils, you can start with under R1000. Practice making 20 kotas a day until you can assemble one in under 2 minutes. Speed and consistency bring customers back.

2. Bunny Chow: Durban Style

Bunny chow is a half or quarter loaf of bread hollowed out and filled with curry. It originated in Durban's Indian community. To make it, you need a firm white bread loaf, not too soft. Cut a loaf in half for a 'half bunny' or in quarters for a 'quarter bunny'. Scoop out the inside, leaving a 2cm wall. Keep the scooped bread to dip in curry. The curry is the heart. For a basic chicken curry, fry chopped onions in oil until golden. Add ginger-garlic paste, then curry powder (mild or hot), turmeric, and cumin. Add chicken pieces (bone-in is traditional) and coat with spices. Add chopped tomatoes and cook until soft. Add potatoes cut into chunks. Pour in water or stock to cover, then simmer for 30-40 minutes until chicken is tender and potatoes are cooked. For a vegetarian version, use beans, lentils, or mixed vegetables. Adjust salt and add fresh coriander. To assemble, place the hollowed bread on a piece of paper, fill with curry, and top with the 'virgin' bread lid you removed. Serve with extra gravy on the side. Common in Durban, you can also find lamb, beef, or tripe bunny chow. For business, cook curry in large pots. A half bunny sells for R50-R80. Your cost per portion depends on meat; chicken is cheaper than lamb. Buy spices in bulk from Indian spice shops. Use a heavy-bottomed pot to prevent burning. Keep curry hot in a cooler box or on a low flame. Sell at markets, taxi ranks, or outside factories during lunch. Offer a 'quarter' for R30-R40 for students. Always provide a fork or spoon, and napkins. Hygiene is critical: wash hands, use clean utensils, and keep raw meat separate. With practice, you can make 50 bunny chows from one large pot. Start with one curry recipe and perfect it.

3. Gatsby and Foot-Long Sandwiches

A gatsby is a foot-long sandwich from Cape Town, typically filled with chips, meat, cheese, and sauce. It's a shareable meal. To make one, buy a long, soft bread roll (like a hoagie roll) about 30cm long. Slice it open lengthwise but not all the way through. The classic filling is masala steak, but you can use polony, Russian, or even just chips and cheese. For masala steak, slice beef thinly, marinate in a mix of masala spice, garlic, and oil for 30 minutes. Fry in a hot pan until cooked. For chips, double-fry as for kotas. To assemble, spread butter or mayonnaise on the bread. Layer chips, then meat, then grated cheese, then sliced tomatoes, onions, and lettuce. Add sauces: peri-peri, tomato sauce, or a vinegar-based sauce. For a foot-long sandwich, you can also do a 'chip roll' with just chips, cheese, and sauce. Sell gatsbys as a whole for R60-R100 or cut into four portions for R20 each. To make it a business, buy bread rolls in bulk from a bakery, and use cheap cuts of beef like chuck or stewing steak. Tenderise by pounding or slow cooking. For a vegetarian gatsby, use roasted vegetables, falafel, or just chips and cheese. Key tools: a large frying pan or griddle, a deep fryer, and a sharp knife. Wrap in foil or paper for takeaway. Sell at sports events, beaches, or busy streets. Keep ingredients in separate containers for quick assembly. A gatsby is heavy, so one per two people is common. Advertise 'Gatsby Special' with a combo of chips and a drink. Train yourself to assemble in under 3 minutes. Maintain a clean station. With a small investment in a griddle and fryer, you can start selling gatsbys from a cart.

4. Shawarma and Wraps African Style

Shawarma is a Middle Eastern wrap that has been adapted across Africa. In many African cities, you'll find shawarma with chicken, beef, or even goat, wrapped in flatbread with vegetables and sauces. To make it, you need flatbreads (pita or tortilla), meat, and a sauce. For chicken shawarma, marinate boneless chicken thighs in yogurt, lemon juice, garlic, cumin, coriander, and paprika for at least 2 hours. Cook on a griddle or in a pan until charred and cooked through. Slice into strips. For a beef version, use thin strips of steak and cook quickly over high heat. The sauce is key: mix mayonnaise, yogurt, garlic paste, and lemon juice. Add chili for heat. To assemble, warm a flatbread, spread sauce, add meat, then top with shredded lettuce, sliced tomatoes, onions, and pickles. Roll tightly, tucking in the sides, then wrap in foil or paper. For a vegetarian option, use grilled halloumi, falafel, or roasted vegetables. In Africa, you can also use local spices like suya or berbere for a twist. Sell shawarmas for R40-R60 each. Your cost per wrap is low if you buy chicken in bulk and make your own flatbreads (flour, water, salt, oil). A simple dough: 2 cups flour, 1/2 cup water, 1 tsp salt, 2 tbsp oil. Knead, rest, roll thin, and cook on a dry pan. For business, set up a vertical rotisserie if you can afford it, but a flat griddle works. Keep meat hot and assemble to order. Offer a 'shawarma combo' with chips and a drink. Train staff to roll tight wraps so they don't fall apart. Sell at night markets, outside clubs, or busy taxi ranks. Hygiene: use gloves, keep raw meat cold, and clean the griddle after each batch. With practice, you can make 30 shawarmas per hour. Start with chicken, as it's cheapest and most popular.

5. Grilled Cheese and Egg Sandwiches

Grilled cheese and egg sandwiches are simple, cheap, and popular for breakfast or a quick lunch. To make one, you need sliced bread, butter or margarine, cheese, and eggs. For a basic grilled cheese, butter two slices of bread on one side. Place cheese between the unbuttered sides. Heat a pan over medium heat. Place the sandwich in the pan and cook until golden brown on both sides and cheese is melted. For an egg sandwich, fry or scramble an egg and place it inside the sandwich before grilling. You can also add tomato, onion, or bacon if you want to upsell. For a business, these sandwiches cost very little: a loaf of bread (R15) gives 10-12 slices, cheese (R30 for 200g) gives about 8 slices, and eggs (R40 per dozen) give 12. So one sandwich costs about R5-R7. Sell for R15-R25. To increase profit, offer add-ons: extra cheese, ham, or a fried egg for R5 more. Use a sandwich press or a flat griddle. A sandwich press speeds up cooking and gives even browning. If you don't have one, use a heavy pan and press with a spatula. For volume, pre-cook eggs in a large pan and keep warm. Assemble sandwiches to order. Sell at schools, offices, or construction sites in the morning. Wrap in paper or foil. Offer a combo with coffee or tea. For a vegan version, use vegan cheese and scrambled tofu. Keep your station clean: wipe the press after each use. Store bread in a sealed container to keep fresh. Buy cheese in bulk blocks and slice yourself to save money. With a small investment in a sandwich press (R500-R1000), you can start. Practice making a perfect grilled cheese: not burnt, cheese fully melted. This is a low-risk, high-turnover business. You can also offer delivery to nearby offices. Start with 20 sandwiches per day and grow.

6. Vegan and Vegetarian Options

Offering vegan and vegetarian options expands your customer base and can be more profitable because plant proteins are often cheaper than meat. For kotas, replace meat with grilled mushrooms, peppers, and onions. Use vegan cheese or skip cheese. For bunny chow, make a bean or lentil curry: fry onions, add curry powder, tomatoes, and cooked beans or lentils. Simmer until thick. For gatsbys, use roasted vegetables like butternut, sweet potato, and eggplant. For shawarmas, use falafel (chickpea balls) or grilled halloumi (if vegetarian, not vegan). For grilled cheese, use vegan cheese and scrambled tofu with turmeric for colour. Key ingredients: dried beans, lentils, chickpeas, rice, and seasonal vegetables. Buy from local markets for best prices. For a vegan kota, layer chips, then a bean patty or grilled mushrooms, then atchar and sauce. For a vegan bunny chow, use a mix of potatoes, carrots, and peas in a spicy gravy. For wraps, make a chickpea salad with mashed chickpeas, vegan mayo, and spices. Label your options clearly: 'Vegan' or 'Vegetarian'. Many customers will pay the same price as meat versions, so your profit margin is higher. Use separate cooking surfaces or clean well between meat and vegan to avoid cross-contamination. For frying, use vegetable oil, not animal fat. For sauces, check for fish sauce or dairy. Offer a 'Vegan Combo' with a drink. Market your vegan options on social media and with signs. In African cities, veganism is growing, especially among young people. You can also cater to religious groups who avoid certain meats. Start by adding one vegan item to your menu, like a vegan kota. Test it for a week and see sales. Adjust spices to local taste. With beans and lentils, you can make large batches cheaply. A vegan kota can cost you R8 to make and sell for R30. That's a 73% margin. Train your staff on vegan handling. Keep separate utensils. This is a smart way to grow your street food business.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Kotas, Bunny Chow & Street Sandwiches

This practical reference consolidates the operational knowledge behind South Africa's most bankable street sandwiches: the kota (a quarter loaf hollowed and stuffed with chips, polony, egg, cheese, atchar and sauces), bunny chow (Durban's hollowed half-loaf filled with curry), and the wider family of street sandwiches gatsby, foot-longs, shawarma-style wraps, grilled cheese and egg sandwiches, and vegan/vegetarian variants. For a street-food business, these are not merely recipes; they are a margin engine. Bread and potatoes are among the cheapest calorie carriers in Africa, while the perceived value of a loaded, hand-held meal is high. The gap between ingredient cost and selling price is where your profit lives.

Use this module as a costing, prep and safety manual. Every figure below should be adapted to your own market, supplier and currency, but the structure batch prep, portion control, food safety, and speed of assembly is universal across Johannesburg, Durban, Cape Town, Lagos, Nairobi and Accra.

1. The Economics of the Loaded Sandwich

The kota and bunny chow share a business logic: a low-cost starch base (bread, potatoes, rice) is hollowed or layered, then filled with a small quantity of high-flavour protein, sauce and vegetables. The customer perceives abundance; the operator controls cost through portion discipline. The table below shows a representative cost breakdown for a standard kota in a South African township market. Business insight: The protein line is your largest cost and your biggest margin risk. If polony prices spike, either reduce the portion to 50 g and compensate with extra atchar and sauce, or introduce a "cheese-only" vegetarian kota at a lower price point. Never absorb cost silently re-engineer the recipe or adjust price in small increments.

2. Bunny Chow: Durban's Curry-in-Bread

Bunny chow originated in Durban's Indian community and is now a national icon. A half or quarter loaf is hollowed, the soft bread removed and reserved, and the cavity filled with mutton, chicken, bean or vegetable curry. The "virgin" bunny is vegetarian; the "full house" includes extra gravy and salad. For a street-food business, bunny chow is attractive because curry can be batch-cooked in a large pot, portioned with a ladle, and served fast. The critical control points are curry temperature (above 60C for hot holding), bread freshness, and consistent gravy viscosity so the loaf does not disintegrate.

SAFETY WARNING: Deep-Frying and Hot Curry Handling

Deep-frying chips and hot-holding curry are the two highest-risk operations in this module. Hot oil causes severe burns, and oil that is too old or too hot can flash into fire. Curry held below 60C for more than two hours can grow Bacillus cereus and other pathogens.

  • Never leave a deep-fryer unattended. Keep a fire blanket or lid within arm's reach never use water on an oil fire.
  • Change frying oil when it darkens, foams or smells acrid; filter daily if volume justifies it.
  • Hot-hold curry above 60C or cool rapidly and reheat to 75C before serving. Do not reheat more than once.
  • Use long tongs and a splash guard. Wear closed shoes and an apron no bare feet or loose sleeves.
  • Keep raw meat separate from ready-to-eat bread, salad and cheese. Use colour-coded boards.
  • Wash hands after handling money, raw meat or waste, and before touching bread or garnish.

4. Vegan and Vegetarian Options

Plant-based kotas and wraps are not a niche sideline they are a margin opportunity. Beans, lentils, chickpeas, spinach, butternut and mushroom cost less per portion than meat, and they appeal to students, health-conscious customers and religious dietary requirements. A well-seasoned bean curry in a bunny chow, or a kota filled with spiced chickpeas, grilled veg and vegan mayo, can be sold at a similar price to a meat version with a higher gross margin. Keep separate tongs and boards for vegan prep to avoid cross-contact with meat fats, which matters to observant customers.

5. Quick Reference Checklist

  • Cost every item weekly: update your ingredient prices and recalculate margin before changing portion sizes.
  • Batch-prep before service: pre-fry chips, pre-cook protein, pre-hollow bread and pre-chop salad to hit 4560 second assembly.
  • Portion with tools, not eyes: use scoops, ladles and scales so every kota and bunny chow is consistent.
  • Hot-hold above 60C and cold-hold below 5C: check with a probe thermometer at least every two hours.
  • Protect your oil: filter, test temperature, and never leave the fryer unattended.
  • Offer a vegan option daily: it lowers your protein cost and widens your customer base.
  • Package for travel: foil or lined paper keeps bread from going soggy and lets customers eat on the move.
  • Track sales by item: know your top three sellers and your slowest; cut or re-engineer the slow ones.

The operators who succeed are not the ones with the most elaborate recipes they are the ones with disciplined costing, fast assembly, strict food safety and a clear read on what their customers actually buy. Master these, and the module becomes a repeatable profit system rather than a set of recipes.
