Lesson 1

Reading the Legume Price Calendar

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Understand the legume price cycle
  • Use storage to sell into high-price months
  • Plan production timing for the peaks

Legume prices follow the same seasonal curve as other crops: low at harvest, rising through the dry season, highest in the "hungry gap" before the next harvest. The curve is steeper for legumes than for cereals because legume supply is more seasonal.

Typical pattern

  • Harvest season (2–3 months): lowest prices, glut
  • Post-harvest with storage: prices rise 30–60% as household stocks deplete
  • Late dry season: prices at peak, often 2–3x harvest price
  • Before next harvest: highest prices, but stock is exhausted

Using storage to capture the price curve

Hermetic storage enables you to hold your crop and sell into higher prices. If you harvest beans in May at $0.60/kg and store to December when they sell for $1.20/kg, the storage gains you $0.60/kg — a 100% return in 7 months, without chemicals or losses.

Lesson 2

The Four Buyer Types

~11 min
In this lesson
  • Name and price the four legume buyers
  • Match grade and variety to the correct buyer
  • Understand payment risk

Buyer 1 — Local market vendors

Buy at the market, small lots, cash. Prices are market-set. Easiest channel, lowest price. Volume: 20–500 kg per week.

Buyer 2 — Urban retail and supermarkets

Buy under contract in specific grades and standard packaging. Pay 30–50% above market. Require business registration and 30–60 day terms. Volume: 1–10 tonnes per week.

Buyer 3 — Processors and millers

Buy for flour, canned goods, peanut butter or tofu. Prices track commodity markets. Require volume and consistency. Payment on delivery or 30 days. Volume: 5–50 tonnes per year.

Buyer 4 — Exporters and aggregators

Buy for regional or international export. Highest prices when specification met (uniform grade, low moisture, aflatoxin-tested, sometimes organic or fair-trade certified). Payment on delivery or 30–60 days. Volume: 50+ tonnes per year, often through a farmer group.

Buyer 5 — Feed mills (soybean and groundnut cake)

Buy soybean and groundnut for feed processing. Prices track the protein market. Volume: 50+ tonnes per year.

Lesson 3

The Export Premium

~11 min
In this lesson
  • Understand what export buyers require
  • Prepare grain that meets export specification
  • Access export channels via groups or aggregators

What export buyers want

  • Uniform grain size. Graded, not mixed. Different sizes sell at different prices.
  • Low moisture. 12% or lower. Tested at intake.
  • Clean. Free of stones, dirt, foreign matter, weed seeds.
  • Aflatoxin tested. For groundnut, cowpea and other susceptible crops. Below the buyer's threshold (typically 4–10 ppb for food).
  • Consistent quality. Same size, same colour, same moisture across deliveries.
  • Documentation. Invoices, packing lists, phytosanitary certificates, sometimes organic or fair-trade certification.
  • Volume. Most exporters buy in 20-tonne container loads, sometimes in 5-tonne lots through aggregators.

How small farmers access export markets

  1. Join a farmer group. Aggregate volume to meet the minimum.
  2. Work with an aggregator. A trader who buys from many farmers, grades, and sells to exporters.
  3. Partner with a certified producer. Grow under contract for someone who holds the certification.
  4. Get certified as a group. Group organic or fair-trade certification is possible at the cooperative level.

    5. The export premium is real but the quality bar is high. A single failing delivery ends the relationship.

Net margin

$200–1,000 per hectare depending on yield and price realisation. Add the nitrogen credit to the next cereal crop: $60–120. Add the crop residue value (5–10 t/ha at $20–30/t) as fodder or compost: $100–300. Total system value: $360–1,400 per hectare.

Lesson 5

Group Selling and the Year-Round Plan

~11 min
In this lesson
  • Join or form a group for collective selling
  • Plan a 12-month legume production calendar
  • Decide when to scale

Why groups work

Most high-value buyers — processors, exporters, institutional buyers — want volume that a single smallholder cannot supply. A group of 20 farmers, each with 1 tonne of beans, controls 20 tonnes. That is a container load.

Making a group work

  1. Common quality standard — agree moisture, cleanliness, grade before pooling
  2. Single negotiator — one person talks to the buyer, not 20
  3. Written agreement — who brought what, at what grade, for what share
  4. One delivery, one invoice, one payment
  5. Transparent split by contribution
  6. Dispute procedure for rejects and short payments

The 12-month plan

  • Main season: plant at rains onset, harvest 3–4 months later
  • Storage: store 50–70% of the crop in hermetic bags for the off-season price
  • Second season (if rainfall allows): plant a short-cycle legume after the cereal harvest
  • Off-season: sell stored crop into the price peak
  • Next season: seed saved from the best plants, replaced with certified every 3 years

When to scale

  • Two profitable cycles with records
  • Confirmed buyers for larger volume
  • Storage capacity upgraded
  • Working capital for the larger inputs

Legumes scale well because the market has grown with the poultry and feed industry. A farmer who produces 10 tonnes of soybean this year can produce 30 tonnes next year if the buyer relationship is in place.

Knowledge check — Module 6

1. When are legume prices highest?

Correct: late dry season. 2–3x harvest price when household stocks deplete.

2. What does hermetic storage enable?

Correct: holding stock. +100% revenue if you sell 6+ months later.

3. What is the biggest requirement for export buyers?

Correct: consistent specification. A single failing delivery ends the relationship.

4. How does a smallholder access export markets?

Correct: group or aggregator. Exporters need container-load volumes.

5. When should you scale up the legume business?

Correct: after two profitable cycles with infrastructure. Same discipline as all scaling.
