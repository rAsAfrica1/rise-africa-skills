Yam Processing

8 lessons · 30 quiz questions · Tubers & Roots

1 Yam varieties (white, yellow, water)

Yam is a staple across West and Central Africa. There are over 600 species, but three matter commercially:

White yam (Dioscorea rotundata): The most common. Firm flesh, good for pounded yam, yam flour and chips. Stores 3–4 months.

Yellow yam (Dioscorea cayenensis): Deep yellow flesh, stronger flavour. Preferred for yam flour (amala) and for fufu.

Water yam (Dioscorea alata): Higher moisture, softer when cooked. Best for yam chips and instant poundo. Stores shorter.

Choose a variety based on your end product. For flour and poundo → white or yellow. For chips → water yam works but dries slower.

2 Cleaning and peeling

Yam comes out of the ground covered in soil and often with roots and hairs.

  1. Wash in clean water to remove soil and stones — gritty yam flour is a customer complaint
  2. Cut off the two ends (the head and tail have more fibre)
  3. Peel with a sharp knife — remove 3–5 mm of skin, no more
  4. Inspect for rot or brown spots — cut them out fully, don't leave any
  5. Rinse again in clean water

Yam oxidises quickly — peeled pieces turn brown within 1–2 hours. Keep them in water with a squeeze of lemon if you're not processing immediately.

💡 Wear gloves. Yam sap is sticky and causes itching for some people.

3 Yam flour production

Yam flour (called elubo in Nigeria) is the biggest industrial use of yam. It's turned into a smooth paste (amala) by stirring into hot water.

Process:

  1. Slice peeled yam into 5 mm chips
  2. Parboil in hot water for 3–5 minutes (this gelatinises starch and improves texture)
  3. Cool quickly in cold water
  4. Sun-dry or solar-dry for 3–5 days until brittle (moisture under 12%)
  5. Mill in hammer mill
  6. Sieve through 250 micron screen
  7. Package in 1 kg, 5 kg, 25 kg bags

Yield: 100 kg fresh yam → 30–35 kg flour. Loss comes from skin, water, and drying.

4 Yam chips and crisps

Fried yam chips are a popular snack in Nigeria, Ghana and Cameroon. This is a higher-margin product than flour.

Steps:

  1. Peel and slice yam thinly (2–3 mm) — a mandolin or slicer gives even cuts
  2. Soak slices in salt water (1 tbsp salt per litre) for 20 minutes
  3. Drain and pat dry — wet chips explode in oil
  4. Fry at 170–180°C for 3–4 minutes until golden
  5. Drain on paper, salt while hot
  6. Cool, then pack in sealed foil or poly bags

Yield: 100 kg fresh yam → 25–30 kg chips. Shelf life: 3–6 months in sealed packaging.

💰 Yam chips sell for $4–$8 per kg retail — 3–4× the price of fresh yam.

5 Pounded yam flour (poundo)

Poundo yam is a dehydrated version of pounded yam — it rehydrates in hot water to a smooth, stretchy paste. It's a huge market in Nigerian cities and diaspora communities.

Process:

  1. Peel and cut yam into chunks
  2. Boil until soft (30–40 minutes)
  3. Pound or mash while hot into a smooth paste
  4. Spread thinly on trays and dry (sun or oven) — takes 2–3 days sun-drying
  5. Mill to a fine powder
  6. Sieve and package

Key quality point: no lumps, no black specks, rehydrates smooth. Sells for $5–$10 per kg in cities and diaspora markets.

6 Yam flakes drying

Yam flakes are an intermediate product — dried yam pieces used by food companies to make instant products.

They're made by parboiling, chipping, drying, and lightly crushing. Similar to flour production but stopping before milling.

Buyers: food manufacturers, instant food companies, army and institutional catering.

Price: $2–$4 per kg in bulk.

7 Packaging and preservation

Yam products attract moisture and pests if stored badly.

Packaging:

  • Flour — laminated poly bags with zip closure for retail; 25 kg poly-lined sacks for bulk
  • Chips — foil-laminated pouches, nitrogen-flushed for long shelf life
  • Poundo — resealable pouches with oxygen absorbers

Storage: Cool (under 25°C), dry (under 60% humidity), away from direct sun. Keeps 12 months in good packaging.

Compliance: Label with product name, ingredients (yam, water, salt if added), net weight, best-before date, batch code, producer contact, and any certifications (e.g. NAFDAC in Nigeria).

8 Market channels for yam products

Where to sell yam products:

  • Open markets — direct to consumers, fast turnover, low margin
  • Supermarkets — need consistent packaging and supply; higher margin
  • Diaspora shops — premium prices for poundo and flour (UK, US, Canada)
  • Restaurants and caterers — bulk sales of poundo and pounded yam paste
  • Food companies — bulk supply of flour and flakes
  • Export — poundo yam and flour to African shops in Europe and North America
💰 Highest margins: poundo for diaspora. Highest volume: flour and chips for local markets.
