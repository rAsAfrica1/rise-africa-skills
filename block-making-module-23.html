Coloured Concrete Block

8 lessons · 30 quiz questions · Easy difficulty

1 Iron oxide pigments

Colour comes from iron oxide pigments — the same pigments used in paints, plastics and cosmetics. They're stable, safe, and produce consistent colours that don't fade.

Common colours and their oxide sources:

  • Red/terracotta: Iron oxide red (Fe₂O₃)
  • Yellow/sandstone: Iron oxide yellow (FeOOH)
  • Black/charcoal: Iron oxide black (Fe₃O₄)
  • Brown: Iron oxide brown (mixture)
  • Green: Chromium oxide (Cr₂O₃)
  • Blue: Cobalt oxide (rare and expensive)
  • White: Titanium dioxide (TiO₂)

Iron oxides cost $2–$8 per kg. Chromium and cobalt oxides cost 3–5× more. Titanium dioxide is mid-range.

💡 Only iron oxide pigments are stable in alkaline concrete. Some organic pigments fade within months — always test before committing.

2 Pigment dose per batch

The right dose is the smallest amount that gives the target colour.

Standard dose by cement weight:

  • Red/terracotta: 3–5%
  • Yellow: 4–7%
  • Black/charcoal: 2–4%
  • Green: 4–6%
  • White: 5–10%

Example: a 100 kg cement batch with 5% red oxide = add 5 kg pigment. That colours about 400 blocks.

Overdosing wastes money and can weaken the block (pigment particles disrupt the cement matrix). Never exceed 10% by cement weight.

Underdosing gives pale colours that look washed-out. Test a small batch first — colour looks different in the final cured block than in the wet mix.

3 Mixing pigment evenly

Uneven pigment = streaky, patchy colour. This is the #1 cause of rejected coloured blocks.

Correct method:

  1. Weigh pigment accurately (use a scale, not "eyeballing").
  2. Mix pigment dry with a small amount of cement first — 2–3 minutes in a bucket or small mixer.
  3. Add this colour premix to the main batch.
  4. Mix the full batch 2–3 minutes longer than an uncoloured batch.

Total mixing time: 6–9 minutes. Longer than plain concrete because the pigment must be evenly distributed through every grain of sand.

Never add pigment to the mixer last — it forms clumps that show up as dark spots on the cured block.

4 Colourfastness testing

Not all pigments are equal. Some fade in sunlight or are attacked by alkaline cement.

Test before full production:

  1. Make 10 sample blocks with a new pigment.
  2. Cure normally (7 days minimum).
  3. Place 5 samples in direct sun (roof or open yard) for 30 days.
  4. Keep 5 samples in dark storage.
  5. Compare colour after 30 days.

    6. If the sun-exposed samples are noticeably lighter, the pigment is fading. Reject it and try another supplier.

    Iron oxides pass this test easily. Organics usually fail. Some cheaper "iron oxide" pigments are diluted with fillers — always test.

5 Moulds for colour blocks

Coloured blocks use the same moulds as plain concrete blocks. No special mould needed.

But: clean the mould carefully between colours. Residue from a red batch will contaminate the next yellow batch and turn it orange.

Best practice:

  • Have one mould set per colour if you're doing volume.
  • If sharing moulds, clean with a wire brush between colours.
  • Rinse with clean water if changing from dark to light colours.
  • Start with light colours first, dark colours later — easier cleanup.

Multi-cavity moulds work fine. Standard mould cost: $200–$800 for a 6-cavity.

6 Curing without discolouration

Curing affects colour. Wet curing gives richer, more uniform colour. Dry curing (or uneven curing) gives pale, patchy colour.

Rules for coloured blocks:

  • Cover with plastic for the first 3 days.
  • Keep uniformly damp — no dry spots.
  • Spray with clean water (not borehole water with iron or minerals).
  • Avoid direct sun in the first 24 hours.
  • Do not touch or move blocks for 24 hours.

Efflorescence (white salt bloom) is more visible on coloured blocks than on plain grey. It's usually harmless and washes off — but it looks bad and hurts sales. Prevent with clean water and consistent curing.

7 Colour catalogue

Building a colour range increases sales — customers want choice.

Starter colour range (5 colours):

  • Terracotta (red)
  • Sandstone (yellow)
  • Charcoal (black)
  • Buff (light yellow-tan)
  • Ivory (off-white)

Extended range (10 colours):

  • Add: Forest green, Slate blue (if affordable), Chocolate brown, Champagne, Graphite

Print a colour catalogue with real photos of cured blocks (not computer-generated colours). Show prices per colour — some are more expensive due to pigment cost.

Consider one "signature colour" for your brand. Something distinctive that customers remember.

8 Selling decorative blocks

Coloured blocks sell for 2–3× the price of plain grey blocks.

Who buys:

  • Homeowners wanting visible decorative walls (garden walls, boundary walls, feature walls)
  • Landscapers for garden designs
  • Restaurants, hotels for exterior theming
  • Commercial projects with brand colours
  • Churches and community buildings

Pricing (Africa, 2026):

  • Standard coloured block: $1.20–$2.50 per block
  • Premium colours (green, white): $2.00–$3.50 per block
  • Custom colours: $3.00–$5.00 per block

Cost to make: $0.50–$0.90 per block (materials + pigment + labour). Margin: 55–70%.

Selling tools:

  • Sample board with all colours mounted
  • Photos of finished walls
  • Colour chart with prices
  • Recommendation service — help customers choose the right colour for their project
💰 Coloured blocks are the easiest margin-lift for any block yard. Same equipment, one extra ingredient. Highest ROI upgrade available.
