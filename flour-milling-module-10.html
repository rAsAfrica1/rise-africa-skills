1. Mill Production Planning and Scheduling

Production planning starts with knowing your mill's capacity. A small roller mill in Kenya might process 500 kg of wheat per hour. If you run one 8-hour shift, that's 4,000 kg per day. But you need to schedule cleaning, maintenance, and breaks. Plan for 7 hours of actual milling per shift. That gives 3,500 kg daily. If you sell 50 kg bags of flour, that's 70 bags per day. Your weekly plan should match orders. For example, if a bakery needs 200 bags per week, you need 3 days of milling. Use a simple wall chart or a notebook to write down orders, wheat stock, and milling days. Many African millers use a 'first in, first out' rule for wheat. Schedule harder wheat first because it takes more power. Also, group similar flour types to reduce changeover time. Changeover from white flour to whole wheat takes about 30 minutes for cleaning. So, if you have multiple orders, mill all white flour first, then whole wheat. Power availability is a big issue. In Nigeria, many millers run generators during grid outages. Schedule milling during times when electricity is stable, often early morning. Keep a generator on standby but only run it when needed to save fuel. Labor: assign one person to feed wheat, one to monitor the mill, and one to bag flour. Train them to watch for jams. A jam can stop production for 20 minutes. So, preventive checks every hour. Finally, keep a daily log: wheat used, flour produced, downtime reasons. This helps you improve next week. For example, if you see frequent jams on Mondays, maybe the wheat is too wet from weekend rain. Adjust by drying wheat first.

2. Inventory Management for Wheat and Flour

Inventory management is about having enough wheat and flour without wasting money. Start by calculating your usage. If you mill 3,500 kg of wheat per day, you need 17,500 kg per week (5 days). But wheat prices fluctuate. In Tanzania, wheat prices are lower after harvest in August. So, buy in bulk then. But you need storage. A 20-tonne warehouse can hold about 400 bags of 50 kg. Ensure the floor is raised on pallets to prevent moisture. Use rat traps and keep cats if needed. For flour, it's perishable. Flour stays good for 3 months if kept dry and cool. But in humid areas like Mombasa, it can spoil in 6 weeks. So, produce to order. Don't overstock. Use a simple card system: each bag of wheat gets a tag with date and weight. When you use it, record it. For flour, stack bags on pallets, not directly on floor. Keep a minimum stock level: say, 2 days of wheat and 1 day of flour. That's your safety stock. Reorder when you hit that level. Use a notebook or a free app like 'Inventory Simple' on your phone. Count stock every Friday. Compare with records. If there's a difference, investigate. Maybe theft, spillage, or moisture loss. Also, rotate stock: first in, first out. Old wheat can get weevils. You can prevent weevils by adding diatomaceous earth (food grade) at 1 kg per tonne. For flour, sell older stock first. If you have a walk-in customer, sell the oldest bag. Finally, keep records for at least one year. This helps with loans and taxes.

3. Cost Analysis and Yield Optimization

To make a profit, you must know your costs. Let's break down a 50 kg bag of flour. First, wheat cost: In Kenya, wheat might be 40 KES per kg. But you don't use 50 kg of wheat to make 50 kg of flour. Extraction rate is key. For white flour, you get about 75% extraction. So, to get 50 kg flour, you need 66.7 kg wheat. That wheat costs 66.7 x 40 = 2,668 KES. Then milling costs: electricity, labor, maintenance, bags, and overhead. Suppose electricity is 500 KES per hour, and you mill 500 kg per hour. For 66.7 kg, that's 0.133 hours, so 66.7 KES. Labor: if you pay a worker 500 KES per day and they produce 70 bags, that's 7.14 KES per bag. Bags cost 30 KES each. Maintenance: set aside 2% of revenue, say 50 KES. Total cost: 2,668 + 66.7 + 7.14 + 30 + 50 = 2,821.84 KES. If you sell at 3,200 KES, profit is 378 KES per bag. But yield optimization can improve this. If you adjust the mill to get 78% extraction, you need only 64.1 kg wheat, saving 104 KES. Also, reduce downtime. If you mill 6 hours instead of 5, you spread fixed costs. Train staff to reduce waste. Spilled flour is money lost. A 1% spill on 50 kg is 0.5 kg, worth about 32 KES. So, sweep floors and use all flour. Also, consider by-products: bran sells for animal feed at 20 KES per kg. From 66.7 kg wheat, you get about 16.7 kg bran, worth 334 KES. That offsets costs. So, net cost becomes 2,821.84 - 334 = 2,487.84 KES. Profit now 712 KES per bag. Keep a daily cost sheet. Review weekly.

4. Staff Training and Supervision

Your staff are your most important asset. In a small mill, you might have 3-5 workers. They need training on safety, machinery, and quality. Start with safety: dust from flour can explode. So, no smoking, no open flames. Workers must wear masks and ear protection. Train them to lock out the mill before cleaning. In Nigeria, many accidents happen because someone turns on the mill while another is inside. So, use a padlock and key system. Only the supervisor has the key. For machinery, train on start-up and shut-down procedures. For a roller mill, start the rolls before feeding wheat. Shut down after all wheat is out. Teach them to listen for unusual noises. A bearing squeal means it needs grease. A knock means a loose bolt. Quality training: show how to check flour fineness. Rub a pinch between fingers. If it feels gritty, adjust the rolls. Also, teach moisture testing. A simple moisture meter costs about $100. If wheat is above 14% moisture, it can clog the mill. So, dry it first. Supervision: don't micromanage. Set clear daily targets: e.g., 70 bags. Check progress every 2 hours. Use a whiteboard to show targets and actual. Praise good work. If someone is slow, find out why. Maybe they need better tools. Rotate jobs so everyone can do every task. That way, if one person is absent, others cover. Hold a 15-minute meeting every Monday. Discuss last week's problems and solutions. Keep a training log: date, topic, who attended. This helps for audits. Finally, pay fairly and on time. A happy worker is a productive worker. In many African mills, high turnover is a problem. So, invest in your team.

5. Regulatory Compliance and Food Safety

Food safety is not optional. In most African countries, mills must register with the national food safety authority. For example, in Kenya, it's KEBS (Kenya Bureau of Standards). In Nigeria, NAFDAC. You need a license to sell flour. To get it, your mill must meet hygiene standards. Start with the building: walls should be smooth and washable. Floors should be concrete, not dirt. There should be no cracks where pests hide. Lighting must be bright enough to see dirt. Ventilation to remove dust. Water supply: clean water for cleaning. Handwashing stations with soap. Toilets must be separate from milling area. Pest control: use traps, not poison near flour. Keep a pest control log. Every week, check for rodent droppings. If found, clean and set traps. Personal hygiene: workers must wash hands before handling flour. Wear clean uniforms. No jewelry. Hairnets. If sick, stay home. For the product: flour must be free of foreign matter. Use magnets to catch metal bits. Sift flour before bagging. Also, label correctly. Include: product name, weight, ingredients, production date, expiry date, and your contact. For export, you need more: aflatoxin testing, moisture content, protein content. Keep records of every batch: wheat source, milling date, who milled it. If a customer complains, you can trace back. Also, train staff on allergens. If you mill different grains, clean between runs. Wheat is a common allergen. So, if you also mill maize, clean thoroughly. Finally, expect inspections. An inspector may come unannounced. Keep your records ready. If you fail, you get a warning or fine. So, do it right from the start.

6. Preventive Maintenance Programs

Preventive maintenance (PM) saves money. A breakdown costs 10 times more than a planned repair. Start with a schedule. For a small mill, you need daily, weekly, monthly, and yearly tasks. Daily: before start-up, check oil levels in the gearbox. Grease bearings. Check belt tension. Listen for unusual noises. After shutdown, clean the mill. Remove flour dust. Dust causes fires and clogs. Weekly: inspect rolls for wear. Use a micrometer to measure diameter. If worn more than 0.5 mm, regrind or replace. Check sifter screens for holes. A hole lets bran into white flour. Replace if torn. Monthly: change oil in the gearbox. Check electrical connections for loose wires. Tighten bolts on the mill frame. Vibration can loosen them. Clean the magnet. Test the emergency stop button. Yearly: overhaul the mill. Replace worn bearings. Check the main shaft for cracks. Repaint rusty areas. Also, maintain your generator. Change oil every 100 hours. Clean air filter every 50 hours. Keep spare parts: bearings, belts, screens, fuses. In Africa, getting parts can take weeks. So, stock critical spares. Keep a maintenance log: date, task, who did it, parts used. This helps predict failures. For example, if a bearing fails every 6 months, you know to replace it at 5 months. Train your staff to do daily checks. But for complex tasks, hire a technician. Budget for PM: set aside 3% of your revenue. For a mill making 1 million KES per month, that's 30,000 KES. Spend it on parts and labor. Don't skip PM. A mill that runs smoothly produces more flour and lasts longer.

<h2>Practical Reference: Mill Management and Operations</h2>

<p>Running a flour mill in Africa is a business of thin margins, volatile wheat prices, unreliable power, and demanding customers. Whether you operate a 20 t/day roller mill in Nakuru, a 100 t/day plant in Lagos, or a small hammer mill in Lilongwe, the difference between profit and loss usually sits in management discipline, not in the milling hardware. This practical reference pulls together the operating knowledge that mill managers, production supervisors, and owner-operators need to keep a mill running, compliant, and profitable. It is written for the African context: imported wheat, local maize and cassava substitution, frequent power interruptions, informal and formal distribution channels, and regulatory environments that vary widely from country to country.</p>

<p>Use this module alongside the six lessons in Mill Management and Operations. Each section below gives you the operational logic, the numbers you should be tracking, and the decisions you will actually face on the mill floor.</p>

<h3>Production Planning and Scheduling</h3>

<p>Production planning starts with demand, not with the mill. In most African markets, demand is split between a few large industrial bakers, many small and medium bakeries, and a growing retail packaged-flour segment. Each channel has different requirements for flour specification, packaging, delivery frequency, and payment terms. A mill that plans production around a single blended "all-purpose" flour often loses the premium bakery accounts to competitors who can supply consistent bread flour at 12.513.5% protein and consistent biscuit flour at 910% protein.</p>

<p>Build a weekly production plan from confirmed orders plus a rolling forecast. Schedule long runs of the same flour grade to minimise changeover losses and cleaning downtime. In a typical roller mill, a grade change can cost 3090 minutes of lost output and generate off-spec flour that must be reworked or sold at a discount. Group grades by similarity (bread flour first, then biscuit, then self-raising) and clean the mill between incompatible products.</p>

<p>Power reliability is the single biggest scheduling constraint in most African mills. If you run on grid power with frequent outages, plan high-load operations (roll stands, purifiers, pneumatic conveying) during the most reliable hours and schedule cleaning, maintenance, and packing during peak outage windows. If you run a diesel generator, calculate the cost per kWh and reflect it in your flour price. Many mills in Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya, and Zambia now run hybrid solar-diesel systems to stabilise costs.</p>

<h3>Inventory Management for Wheat and Flour</h3>

<p>Wheat inventory is working capital. Every tonne of wheat sitting in your silo is cash you cannot use elsewhere, plus storage cost, plus quality risk. Standard practice is to hold 48 weeks of wheat cover, depending on import lead times and local supply reliability. In landlocked countries such as Uganda, Rwanda, and Zambia, where imported wheat can take 610 weeks from order to delivery, you may need 812 weeks of cover. In coastal cities such as Mombasa, Tema, or Durban, 46 weeks is usually enough.</p>

<p>Flour inventory is different. Flour is perishable in the sense that quality declines with time, especially in hot, humid conditions. Aim to hold no more than 714 days of finished flour, and rotate stock strictly first-in-first-out. Packaged flour stored above 30C and 65% relative humidity can develop mould, insect infestation, and off-flavours within weeks. Keep warehouses ventilated, use pallets, and never store flour directly against concrete walls.</p>

<h3>Cost Analysis and Yield Optimisation</h3>

<p>Flour milling economics are dominated by three cost lines: wheat, energy, and labour. In most African mills, wheat accounts for 7080% of total production cost, energy 815%, and labour 510%. That means a 1% improvement in extraction rate is usually worth more than a 10% cut in labour cost. Track extraction rate, flour yield, and mill balance weekly.</p>

<p>Extraction rate is the percentage of clean wheat that becomes saleable flour. Modern roller mills typically achieve 7276% extraction for white flour, with the balance going to bran, pollard, and shorts. In African mills running older equipment or variable wheat quality, 6872% is common. Every percentage point of extraction on a 50 t/day mill is roughly 500 kg of extra flour per day, or about 150 tonnes per year a material revenue line.</p>

<p>Yield optimisation levers include: wheat blending (mixing high and low protein wheats to hit specification at lowest cost), conditioning (tempering wheat to optimal moisture for clean separation of bran and endosperm), roll gap and roll wear management, sifter and purifier efficiency, and reducing flour losses in packing and conveying. Weigh everything: wheat in, flour out, bran out, and waste. If your mass balance does not close within 1%, you have a leak physical or accounting.</p>

<h3>Staff Training and Supervision</h3>

<p>A mill is only as good as the people running it. In many African mills, the biggest operational risk is not equipment failure but operator error: wrong roll gap, missed lubrication, incorrect tempering time, poor hygiene. Invest in structured training and clear supervision.</p>

<p>Minimum training areas for mill operators: wheat intake and quality testing (moisture, protein, test weight, foreign matter), tempering and conditioning, roll stand operation and adjustment, sifter and purifier monitoring, packing and palletising, and food safety and hygiene. Millers should also understand the business: how their extraction rate and downtime affect cost per tonne.</p>

<p>Supervision should be visible and data-driven. Use daily shift handover logs, hourly production checks, and weekly performance reviews. Recognise and reward operators who hit extraction and downtime targets. In many mills, a simple bonus tied to extraction rate and downtime has a bigger impact than new equipment.</p>

<h3>Regulatory Compliance and Food Safety</h3>

<p>Flour is a staple food and one of the most regulated products in every African country. Compliance is not optional it is a licence to operate. Key requirements typically include: business and mill registration, food safety certification (HACCP or ISO 22000 in larger mills), product labelling (ingredients, allergens, batch code, expiry date, country of origin), fortification compliance (many countries mandate vitamin and mineral premix in wheat and maize flour), aflatoxin and pesticide residue limits, and weights and measures verification.</p>

<p>Fortification is a major compliance issue across Africa. Countries including Kenya, Nigeria, Tanzania, Uganda, Zambia, Zimbabwe, and South Africa mandate fortification of wheat and maize flour with iron, zinc, folic acid, and B vitamins. Non-compliance can result in fines, product seizure, and loss of contracts with institutional buyers such as schools and hospitals. Maintain premix dosing records, calibrate feeders, and test finished flour regularly.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #c0392b;background:#fdecea;padding:12px 16px;margin:16px 0;border-radius:6px;">
<strong style="color:#c0392b;"> SAFETY WARNING Dust Explosion and Confined Space Hazards</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Flour dust is combustible. A cloud of flour dust in the right concentration, with an ignition source (hot bearing, static discharge, welding spark, faulty electrical equipment), can cause a devastating explosion. In African mills, common ignition sources include unguarded electrical panels, welding during maintenance without permits, and overheated bearings. Strictly enforce: no smoking or open flames; hot work permits for all welding and cutting; regular cleaning to prevent dust accumulation; explosion venting or suppression on bins, cyclones, and dust collectors; and bonded/grounded equipment to prevent static. Never enter a silo, bin, or hopper without confined space entry procedures, atmospheric testing, and a standby person. Several fatalities occur every year in African mills from preventable dust explosions and confined space entry.</p>
</div>

<h3>Preventive Maintenance Programs</h3>

<p>Unplanned downtime is the most expensive downtime. A preventive maintenance (PM) programme keeps rolls sharp, bearings cool, sifters clean, and conveyors running. Build your PM schedule around manufacturer intervals, operating hours, and actual condition monitoring.</p>

<p>Core PM tasks in a flour mill: daily lubrication checks and cleaning; weekly inspection of belts, chains, and bearings; monthly roll gap and roll wear inspection; quarterly sifter and purifier screen inspection; annual overhaul of major equipment. Keep a maintenance log for every machine. Track mean time between failures (MTBF) and mean time to repair (MTTR). In mills with poor PM, MTTR is often 23 times longer than in mills with good PM, because spare parts are not in stock and technicians are not trained.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Plan production weekly</strong> from confirmed orders plus rolling forecast; group flour grades to minimise changeovers.</li>
<li><strong>Hold 412 weeks of wheat cover</strong> depending on import lead time; hold no more than 714 days of finished flour.</li>
<li><strong>Weigh and test every intake</strong> moisture, protein, test weight, foreign matter, aflatoxin where relevant.</li>
<li><strong>Track extraction rate daily</strong>; target 7476% for white flour and close mass balance within 1%.</li>
<li><strong>Train and supervise operators</strong> on roll gap, tempering, sifter monitoring, hygiene, and food safety.</li>
<li><strong>Maintain fortification records</strong> and calibrate premix feeders; test finished flour regularly.</li>

<p style="margin-top:20px;font-size:0.9em;color:#333;"><strong>Business takeaway:</strong> In African flour milling, the mills that survive and grow are not necessarily the ones with the newest equipment. They are the ones that plan production tightly, control wheat and flour inventory, measure extraction and cost relentlessly, train and supervise staff, stay compliant with food safety and fortification rules, and maintain their machines before they break. Management discipline is the real competitive advantage.</p>
