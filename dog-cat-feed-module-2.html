Lesson 2.1~12 min

Growth Rate and Caloric Requirements

In this lesson
  • Calculate daily energy for a puppy or kitten using resting energy and the correct growth multiplier
  • Explain why fastest growth is not best growth and how energy density drives the problem
  • Convert a growth energy requirement into grams per day and into a monthly cost for the owner

A growing animal is the most demanding customer you will ever formulate for, and the one where mistakes last a lifetime. Bone is being laid down, organs are maturing and body weight can multiply many times over in months. Everything you get wrong in this window is written into the adult skeleton.

Energy first. The resting energy requirement is 70 multiplied by body weight in kilograms raised to the power 0.75, calculated on the animal's weight today, not its expected adult weight. The growth multipliers are 3.0 for a puppy from weaning to four months, 2.0 from four months to adult, and 2.5 for a kitten from weaning right through to adulthood.

Work a puppy through. Take a mixed-breed puppy weighing 6 kilograms at three months.

  1. Resting energy is 70 multiplied by 6 to the power 0.75. Six to the power 0.75 is 3.83, so resting energy is 268 kcal per day.
  2. Under four months the multiplier is 3.0, giving 268 multiplied by 3 = 805 kcal per day.
  3. On a growth food at 4000 kcal per kilogram, that is 805 divided by 4000 = 0.201 kilograms, about 201 grams a day.
  4. At five months the same puppy weighs 10 kilograms. Resting energy is 394 kcal, the multiplier drops to 2.0, and the requirement is 787 kcal, that is 197 grams. The puppy is heavier and eats slightly less. Owners find this counter-intuitive and will overfeed unless your feeding table shows it.
  5. At USD 1.90 per kilogram for a growth food, 201 grams a day is USD 0.38, about USD 11.50 a month.

Now a kitten weighing 1.5 kilograms. One point five to the power 0.75 is 1.36, so resting energy is 95 kcal. The kitten multiplier of 2.5 gives 238 kcal per day. On a dry food at 3800 kcal per kilogram that is 63 grams a day; on a wet food at 1000 kcal per kilogram it is 238 grams, roughly three 85 gram pouches. The gap between those two numbers is why wet and dry feeding guides can never be the same table.

The harder lesson is that these are starting points, not targets to be exceeded. Free feeding an energy-dense growth diet produces a puppy that grows as fast as its appetite allows, and rapid growth is precisely the condition under which excess dietary calcium does skeletal damage in large breeds. The published guidance combines a calcium ceiling with restraint on growth rate, because the two act together. Lesson 2 of this module deals with the calcium side in detail.

So how do you know the rate is right? Not from a growth chart you cannot source. The honest answer to give a customer is that a growing animal should be lean enough that the ribs can be felt easily under light pressure without being visible, that weight should be recorded weekly on the same scale, and that a veterinarian should assess body condition at each vaccination visit. Do not print a target weight-for-age curve in your feeding guide unless you have a published source for the breed, because you will be blamed for whatever the animal does.

Nutrients rise alongside energy. Dog growth requires at least 22.5 percent protein and 8.5 percent fat on a dry matter basis against 18.0 and 5.5 for adults, and cat growth requires 30.0 percent protein against 26.0, with fat unchanged at 9.0 percent. Zinc rises from 80 to 100 milligrams per kilogram in dogs, iron from 40 to 88, copper from 7.3 to 12.4. A growth food is not an adult food with more of it fed.

A producer in Abuja, Nigeria selling to breeders learned to print two columns in every feeding guide, grams per day and cups per day, and to give away a cheap 100 gram kitchen scale with every 15 kilogram bag. The scale cost USD 4 wholesale and eliminated the single most common complaint, which was puppies gaining too fast on volume-measured food. It also gave the customer a reason to keep buying from the producer who took them seriously.

Finally, remember which animals are outside this lesson. Diets for a puppy with a diagnosed condition belong in module 9, and feeding the pregnant or lactating dam that produced the litter belongs in module 11.

That is the beginning of your feeding table.

Lesson 2.2~12 min

Calcium and Phosphorus in Growing Animals

In this lesson
  • Apply the correct calcium ceiling to a growth diet depending on the label claim you intend to make
  • Calculate how a bone-bearing ingredient changes total dietary calcium in a 100 kilogram batch
  • Explain to a breeder why topping up a growth food with a calcium supplement causes harm

This is the lesson that prevents the most serious injury a small pet food producer can cause. Calcium in a growth diet is bounded both below and above, and the upper bound is the one people breach, usually with the best intentions.

The minima are calcium 1.2 percent and phosphorus 1.0 percent of dry matter for dogs at growth and reproduction, and calcium 1.0 percent and phosphorus 0.8 percent for kittens. The calcium to phosphorus ratio must sit between 1 to 1 and 2 to 1 for dogs, with an optimum commonly given as 1.2 to 1.4 to 1. For growing cats the European nutritional maximum is 1.5 to 1.

Now the ceiling, quoted exactly. A maximum of 1.8 percent applies to formulas that may be fed to large size puppies, meaning those weighing 70 pounds or more as mature lean adults. For other life stages, including non-large-size growth formulas, the maximum calcium is 2.5 percent of dry matter. Supplementation above 3 percent has been reported to cause severe osteochondrosis, and the practical recommended band is 1 to 3 percent.

The reason is physiological. Adult dogs absorb calcium largely through regulated active transport and can turn absorption down when intake is high. Large and giant breed puppies have limited ability to downregulate intestinal calcium absorption and continue passive absorption at high intakes, so dietary excess becomes systemic excess. The result is disturbed endochondral ossification and developmental orthopaedic disease: osteochondrosis, retained cartilaginous cores, hypertrophic osteodystrophy. The 1.8 percent figure and the 70 pound threshold are regulatory; the mechanism as described is standard teaching rather than something verified here, but the constraint is not optional either way.

Vitamin D follows the same logic. The dog safe upper limit was cut from 5000 to 3000 IU per kilogram of dry matter in 2016 because Great Dane puppies fed 4000 IU per kilogram showed disrupted endochondral ossification. A growth diet is the wrong place to be generous with either nutrient.

Work through how a batch drifts. Suppose your growth formula sits at 1.60 percent calcium on a dry matter basis, comfortably inside the 1.8 percent ceiling.

  1. You substitute 2 kilograms per 100 of a new meat and bone meal whose certificate of analysis declares 24 percent calcium, replacing 2 kilograms of a poultry meal at 4 percent calcium.
  2. Calcium added is 2 multiplied by 0.24 = 0.48 kilograms per 100 kilograms, that is 0.48 percentage points.
  3. Calcium removed is 2 multiplied by 0.04 = 0.08 kilograms, that is 0.08 percentage points.
  4. Net change is plus 0.40 points, so the diet moves from 1.60 to 2.00 percent as fed. On a dry matter basis at 92 percent dry matter that is 2.17 percent.
  5. The diet is still legal for a small breed growth claim at 2.5 percent, and illegal for the large size dogs claim at 1.8 percent. One substitution, made for cost reasons, has silently voided your label.

The breeder problem is the other half. Many breeders add bone meal, eggshell powder or a calcium tablet to a good growth diet because they believe more calcium builds better bone. Explain the arithmetic in front of them. A quarter of a teaspoon of a calcium supplement declaring 400 milligrams of calcium, added to a 200 gram daily ration, adds 0.4 grams to roughly 184 grams of dry matter, that is another 0.22 percentage points. Two such doses a day take a compliant 1.7 percent diet past 2.1 percent. Never top-dress a large breed puppy diet with calcium, and print that sentence on the bag.

A producer in Blantyre, Malawi supplying a small group of German Shepherd and Rhodesian Ridgeback breeders decided to assay calcium on every incoming meat and bone meal consignment rather than trusting a supplier average. The assay cost about USD 25 per consignment and at roughly one consignment a month adds USD 300 a year, which on 60 tonnes of production is USD 0.005 per kilogram. That is the cheapest liability insurance in this course.

Where a puppy already shows lameness, a swollen joint or a reluctance to move, that is a veterinary case, not a formulation adjustment. Say so plainly to the owner and let the veterinarian direct any dietary change, with module 9 covering therapeutic formulation once a diagnosis exists.

Do this today: find the certificate of analysis for every ingredient in your growth formula that contains bone, write down its declared calcium percentage, and recalculate total dietary calcium on a dry matter basis. Write the answer next to 1.8 percent and decide, in writing, whether your label claim is still true.

Immune Development Through Nutrition

In this lesson
  • Identify the growth-stage nutrients whose minima rise and relate them to normal development
  • Calculate a trace mineral correction from a premix or a single mineral source
  • State plainly which immune claims a producer may not make on a pack

This is a lesson about restraint as much as nutrition. The pet food market is full of immune claims, and almost none of them can be defended from a nutrient profile. What you can say honestly is that a diet meeting the growth minima supports normal development, and that a diet failing them does not. That is a smaller claim and a true one.

Start with what actually changes at growth. Comparing the dog growth and reproduction column with adult maintenance, zinc rises from 80 to 100 milligrams per kilogram of dry matter, iron from 40 to 88, copper from 7.3 to 12.4 and manganese from 5.0 to 7.2. Selenium stays at 0.35 milligrams per kilogram with a maximum of 2, and vitamin E stays at 50 IU per kilogram. For cats, iron, zinc, manganese and selenium do not change between growth and maintenance, but copper in extruded diets rises from 5 to 15 milligrams per kilogram, iodine from 0.6 to 1.8, and vitamin A doubles from 3332 to 6668 IU per kilogram. Vitamin E for cats stays at 40 IU per kilogram.

Those are the numbers you are entitled to talk about. Zinc, copper, selenium and vitamins A and E all have well-established roles in tissue integrity and normal defence, and their minima are published. What is not in our verified reference set is any dose-response data showing that exceeding a minimum improves immune function, or any figure for a probiotic, a beta-glucan or a nucleotide in dog or cat food. Do not print a percentage improvement in immunity. Do not name a colony-forming unit count you cannot support with a supplier certificate and a stability study.

Correct a real shortfall step by step. Suppose your puppy formula analyses at 62 milligrams of zinc per kilogram of dry matter against a growth minimum of 100.

  1. The shortfall is 38 milligrams per kilogram, that is 38 grams of elemental zinc per tonne.
  2. Your zinc oxide supplier declares 72 percent zinc.
  3. Product required is 38 divided by 0.72 = 52.8 grams per tonne.
  4. At USD 2.60 per kilogram for feed-grade zinc oxide, that is 0.0528 multiplied by 2.60 = USD 0.14 per tonne. The cost of the fix is nothing; the cost of not knowing you needed it is a litter with poor coats and slow healing.
  5. In practice, deliver this through your assayed premix rather than as a separate weighing, because a 52 gram addition to a tonne is beyond the accuracy of most small mixers.

The genuinely important immune input in the first days of life is colostrum, not a supplement, and that belongs to the dam. Module 11 lesson 6 covers colostrum and neonate quality, and module 11 lesson 3 covers feeding the lactating dam whose milk is doing this work. Your product enters the story at weaning, and the honest positioning is that it continues what the dam started rather than replacing it.

Food safety is the immune subject a producer really controls. A weaned puppy or kitten has a developing gut and a small margin for error, and pathogen contamination in a raw or poorly dried product is a real hazard rather than a theoretical one. Control moisture at the dryer, hold retained samples from every batch, keep raw material and finished product physically separated, and clean between species. These are the things you can put in a batch record and show a customer.

A producer in Mombasa, Kenya working in a humid coastal climate found the practical limit on shelf life was not nutrient loss but mould in bags stored on concrete floors. Moving to pallets, buying a simple moisture meter for about USD 120 and testing every batch before bagging cut returns sharply. That single instrument did more for the health of the animals eating the product than any claimed immune ingredient would have.

Where a puppy or kitten is repeatedly unwell, has persistent diarrhoea or is failing to thrive, that is a veterinary matter. Vaccination, parasite control and diagnosis are not nutritional decisions, and a producer who implies otherwise is doing harm.

Lesson 2.4~12 min

Weaning Transition Strategies

In this lesson
  • Write a weaning schedule that moves a litter from milk to solid food without a digestive setback
  • Calculate the total feed a litter consumes from the start of weaning to eight weeks
  • Price a weaning pack for a breeder and explain what it saves them

Weaning is where your product first meets the animal, and where a producer can win a breeder for years or lose them in a week. The commercial opportunity is real: breeders buy in bulk, buy repeatedly and talk to each other. The technical requirement is a diet that is nutritionally a growth diet but physically a gruel.

The sequence, in approximate terms because exact ages vary with species, breed and litter, is that puppies and kittens begin showing interest in solid food from around three to four weeks and are usually fully weaned somewhere between six and eight weeks. Treat those as husbandry norms rather than hard standards, and let the breeder and their veterinarian judge the individual litter.

The practical method is a gruel that is thinned at first and thickened over about two weeks.

  1. Days one to three: blend one part growth food with three parts warm water by weight, and offer it four times daily in a shallow tray the litter can walk to.
  2. Days four to seven: move to one part food to two parts water.
  3. Days eight to eleven: one part food to one part water.
  4. Days twelve to fourteen: offer moistened food that holds its shape, plus free access to clean drinking water at all times.
  5. From then on, dry food in four meals a day, reducing to three by twelve weeks.

The nutrient specification does not change through any of this. What the litter eats must already meet the growth minima on a dry matter basis, dog protein 22.5 percent and fat 8.5 percent, cat protein 30.0 percent and fat 9.0 percent, with calcium and phosphorus at 1.2 and 1.0 for puppies and 1.0 and 0.8 for kittens. Adding water changes the moisture, not the dry matter analysis, which is exactly why dry matter is the basis your specification sheet is written on.

Cost a litter properly, because breeders think in litters. Take six puppies weaned from four to eight weeks, averaging 3 kilograms body weight over that period.

  1. Resting energy at 3 kilograms is 70 multiplied by 3 to the power 0.75. Three to the power 0.75 is 2.28, so resting energy is 160 kcal.
  2. At the under-four-months multiplier of 3.0 the requirement is 479 kcal per puppy per day, though early in weaning much of that still comes from the dam's milk.
  3. Assume solid food supplies a quarter of energy in week one of weaning, half in week two, three quarters in week three and all of it in week four. Average over the four weeks is 62.5 percent, that is 299 kcal per puppy per day from your food.
  4. Over 28 days for six puppies that is 299 multiplied by 28 multiplied by 6 = 50,232 kcal.
  5. At 4000 kcal per kilogram that is 12.6 kilograms of food for the whole litter. At USD 1.90 per kilogram, USD 23.90.

That number sells. A breeder who is told a litter of six costs about USD 24 to wean on a properly formulated growth food, against the cost of a failed weaning and a veterinary visit, will listen. Sell it as a weaning pack: 15 kilograms of growth food, a printed schedule, a shallow stainless tray and a kitchen scale. At an ingredient and packaging cost of roughly USD 33 you can retail the pack around USD 48 and it is still cheaper than what the breeder is doing now.

The dam matters too. During lactation her energy requirement runs from 3.0 times resting energy to more than 6.0 depending on litter size, and a queen needs resting energy plus 30 to 90 percent per kitten in the first six weeks. Feeding her properly is covered in module 11 lesson 3, and it is the other half of any conversation with a breeder.

A producer in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania found that the single change that reduced weaning complaints most was printing the gruel ratios by weight rather than by volume on the back of every bag, in Kiswahili and English. Water added by eye varies enormously between households, and a litter given a gruel that is too thin simply does not take in enough energy.

One caution to put in writing: never introduce a new food and deworm or vaccinate on the same day. If diarrhoea follows, nobody can tell which change caused it, and your product will be blamed by default.

Lesson 2.5~12 min

Breed-Specific Considerations

In this lesson
  • Decide whether your growth product may carry a large size dogs claim and what that costs you
  • Adjust energy density and kibble form for very small and very large breeds
  • Distinguish breed differences you can document from breed claims you cannot

Breed marketing is seductive and mostly unsupportable. There is one breed-related constraint in the published profiles that genuinely changes a formulation, and a handful of practical differences in feeding that are real but not quantified. Knowing which is which keeps you out of trouble.

The one that matters is size at maturity. A growth formula that may be fed to large size puppies, defined as those weighing 70 pounds or more as mature lean adults, must not exceed 1.8 percent calcium on a dry matter basis, against 2.5 percent for other growth formulas. That is a hard line drawn around a real disease process, and it is the only breed-linked number in the nutrient profiles.

It gives you a commercial choice. Option one is a single growth product formulated at or below 1.8 percent calcium, which may be fed to any puppy and carries the wider claim. Option two is two products, a general growth diet with more formulation freedom and a separate large breed growth diet. For a producer with one extruder and limited working capital, option one is almost always right: one recipe, one premix, one label, and the broadest possible market. The cost is that you cannot use bone-heavy meals freely, and you must assay calcium on every incoming consignment as covered in lesson 2 of this module.

Energy density is the second lever. Large and giant breed puppies should not be encouraged to grow at maximum speed, so a lower energy density with a fixed measured ration is preferable to a very dense food fed free choice. Work the arithmetic for a puppy that will mature at 40 kilograms and currently weighs 18 kilograms at five months.

  1. Resting energy is 70 multiplied by 18 to the power 0.75. Eighteen to the power 0.75 is 8.74, so resting energy is 612 kcal.
  2. At the four months to adult multiplier of 2.0, the requirement is 1224 kcal per day.
  3. On a dense growth food at 4200 kcal per kilogram that is 291 grams a day.
  4. On a moderate growth food at 3600 kcal per kilogram it is 340 grams a day.
  5. The same energy, but 49 grams more food, which means more chewing time, a fuller stomach and less scope for the animal to overeat before it feels satisfied. For a giant breed that is a real advantage, and the moderate food is usually cheaper per kilogram as well.

For very small breeds the issues are different and practical rather than regulatory. A toy breed puppy has a small stomach and a high energy requirement per kilogram, so it needs frequent small meals of an energy-dense food and a kibble it can physically pick up. There is no published nutrient profile difference for toy breeds; what you are managing is meal frequency, kibble size and the risk of a puppy going too long between meals. Advise four meals a day to twelve weeks and let the owner's veterinarian advise on any individual animal that is unusually small or slow to gain.

For cats, our verified reference set contains no breed-specific nutrient differences at all. Kittens of every breed are formulated to the same growth column. If you are asked about a large breed such as the Maine Coon, the honest answer is that the growth period is longer, so the kitten diet is fed for longer, not that the nutrient requirements differ.

Be equally honest about what you do not know. Coat colour, skull shape, sensitive stomachs and joint predisposition are all used in breed marketing, and none of them appears as a nutrient requirement in the published profiles. You may say your food meets the growth minima and, if true, that it respects the large size calcium ceiling. You may not say it is formulated for the specific needs of a named breed unless you can show what those needs are.

A producer in Pretoria, South Africa selling into a Boerboel and Rhodesian Ridgeback breeder network built the whole business on one claim: every bag states the calcium percentage on a dry matter basis and the batch assay date. Nothing else about the pack is unusual. Breeders of giant dogs care about exactly that number, and a competitor selling on breed imagery could not match a stated figure with a date beside it.

Feeding Schedule and Portion Control

In this lesson
  • Build a feeding table in grams per day from energy requirements and your food's energy density
  • Quantify the error introduced when an owner measures food by volume instead of weight
  • Plan the transition from a growth diet to an adult diet without a growth spurt or a gut upset

A perfect formulation delivered in the wrong quantity is a bad diet. Portion control is the part of nutrition that happens in the customer's kitchen, and the only tools you have there are your feeding table, your packaging and whatever you can teach in one conversation.

Build the table from first principles rather than copying a competitor. For each body weight, calculate resting energy as 70 multiplied by weight to the power 0.75, multiply by the life stage factor, then divide by your own measured energy density.

  1. A 5 kilogram puppy under four months: resting energy 234 kcal, times 3.0 equals 703 kcal, divided by 4000 kcal per kilogram equals 176 grams per day.
  2. A 10 kilogram puppy under four months: resting energy 394, times 3.0 equals 1181 kcal, that is 295 grams per day.
  3. A 20 kilogram puppy over four months: resting energy 662, times 2.0 equals 1324 kcal, that is 331 grams per day.
  4. A 3 kilogram kitten: resting energy 159, times 2.5 equals 399 kcal, divided by 3800 equals 105 grams per day.
  5. A 4 kilogram kitten: resting energy 198, times 2.5 equals 495 kcal, that is 130 grams per day.

Split those daily totals into meals. Four meals a day to about twelve weeks, three meals to about six months and two meals thereafter is a workable pattern for puppies, and kittens do well on three to four small meals. The number of meals does not change the daily total, and saying so on the pack prevents the common error of feeding the table amount at every meal.

Now the volume problem, which is the single largest source of error in the field. Kibble bulk density varies with size, shape and fat coating, so a cup of one food is not a cup of another. Measure your own.

  1. Weigh an empty 250 millilitre cup, fill it level with your kibble and weigh it again. Suppose the kibble weighs 113 grams.
  2. Your 10 kilogram puppy needs 295 grams a day, that is 295 divided by 113 = 2.6 cups.
  3. An owner told to feed three cups delivers 339 grams, that is 15 percent more energy than the animal needs, every day.
  4. Over a month that surplus is roughly 1.3 kilograms of extra food and, more importantly, a growth rate you did not intend.
  5. If you later change kibble size or fat coating, the grams per cup change and every cup instruction you ever printed becomes wrong. This is why grams are the primary instruction and cups are the fallback.

Body condition is the correction that overrides the table. Teach owners to feel the ribs with flat fingers: easily felt under a thin layer, not visible from across the room and not buried under fat. Reassess every two weeks in a growing animal and adjust by about ten percent at a time. Where an owner cannot tell, or where the animal is gaining or losing unexpectedly, the assessment belongs with a veterinarian.

Transition to the adult diet at the end of growth. For most dogs that is around twelve months and later in giant breeds; for cats, around twelve months as well. Move over seven to ten days, replacing roughly a quarter of the ration every two to three days. Expect the daily grams to fall, because the multiplier drops from 2.0 to 1.6 for a neutered adult dog and from 2.5 to 1.2 to 1.4 for a neutered cat. Neutering itself lowers the requirement, and an animal neutered at the same time it moves onto adult food can gain weight quickly if the portion is not reduced.

A producer in Gaborone, Botswana printed a feeding table in grams with a second column showing the equivalent number of level 250 millilitre cups for that specific product, plus the sentence stating the grams per cup as measured on that batch. When the kibble die was changed, the number was reprinted. That discipline cost nothing and made the feeding guide trustworthy, which is more than most imported bags on the same shelf can say.

Everyday portion control is prevention. Managing an animal that is already obese, or one with a diagnosed condition, is a different job covered in module 9 lesson 5.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. A 6 kilogram three-month-old puppy needs roughly how many kcal per day?

Resting energy is 70 times 6 to the power 0.75, that is 268 kcal, and the under-four-months multiplier of 3.0 gives 805 kcal.

2. Why can a puppy's daily grams of food fall as it passes four months?

Resting energy rises with weight, but the multiplier falling from 3.0 to 2.0 can more than offset it, so daily intake plateaus or dips. Owners need this shown in the feeding table.

3. What is the kitten energy multiplier from weaning to adulthood?

Kittens sit at 2.5 times resting energy for the whole growth period, unlike puppies which step down at four months.

4. Why is free feeding an energy-dense growth diet risky in large breed puppies?

Growth rate and calcium excess compound each other, which is why guidance pairs a calcium ceiling with restraint on energy intake in large breed growth.

5. What should you avoid printing in a growth feeding guide?

An unsourced growth curve is a claim you cannot defend and will be blamed for. Body condition assessment and weekly weighing are the honest alternative.

6. A diet labelled for growth including large size dogs must keep calcium at or below what level?

The 1.8 percent ceiling applies to formulas that may be fed to puppies weighing 70 pounds or more as mature lean adults.

7. Why are large breed puppies more vulnerable to excess dietary calcium than adult dogs?

Adults reduce absorption when intake is high; growing large breed puppies continue passive absorption, so dietary excess becomes systemic excess and disturbs bone formation.

8. Swapping in 2 kg per 100 of a 24 percent calcium meal for a 4 percent calcium meal changes dietary calcium by how much?

It adds 0.48 points and removes 0.08 points, a net rise of 0.40 percentage points, enough to push a 1.60 percent diet to 2.00 percent.

9. A breeder asks whether to add bone meal to your large breed growth food. What is the correct answer?

Top-dressing pushes calcium past the ceiling the formulation was designed to respect, and excess calcium in a large breed puppy causes skeletal disease rather than preventing it.

10. A puppy in your customer's litter is lame with a swollen joint. What should you do?

Lameness in a growing puppy is a clinical case. The diagnosis belongs to a veterinarian, and any therapeutic diet follows the diagnosis rather than replacing it.

11. Which trace mineral minimum rises from 80 to 100 milligrams per kilogram between adult and growth in dogs?

Zinc rises from 80 to 100 milligrams per kilogram of dry matter. Iron goes from 40 to 88 and copper from 7.3 to 12.4 over the same step.

12. Your puppy formula is 38 mg per kilogram short of zinc. Zinc oxide declares 72 percent zinc. How much per tonne?

38 divided by 0.72 gives 52.8 grams of product per tonne. That is too small to weigh accurately on most small mixers, so it belongs in the premix.

13. Which immune claim may a small producer make honestly?

Meeting published minima is verifiable from your own analysis. Percentage improvements, prevention claims and anything implying a substitute for veterinary care are not supportable.

14. What is the main immune input in the first days of life?

Colostrum is the dam's contribution and is covered in module 11. A weaning diet continues that work rather than replacing it.

15. Which control most directly protects a young animal from a pathogen risk in your product?

Contamination is a manufacturing hazard controlled by drying, separation, cleaning and record keeping, not by a nutrient addition.

16. What is a sensible starting gruel ratio at the beginning of weaning?

Start thin at one to three by weight and thicken over about two weeks to moistened food that holds its shape.

17. Does adding water to a growth food change whether it meets the AAFCO growth minima?

Profiles are expressed on a dry matter basis, so adding water alters the as-fed figures but not the dry matter analysis.

18. Six puppies weaned over four weeks on a 4000 kcal per kilogram food need roughly how much food in total?

About 299 kcal per puppy per day from solid food, over 28 days for six puppies, is 50,232 kcal, which at 4000 kcal per kilogram is 12.6 kilograms.

19. Why should a new food never be introduced on the same day as a vaccination or deworming?

Separating changes preserves the ability to attribute cause, and protects your product from being blamed for something it did not do.

20. Why print gruel ratios by weight rather than by volume?

Household estimates of volume vary enormously. A ratio by weight gives a repeatable energy intake for the litter.

21. Seventy pounds as a mature lean adult is approximately what in kilograms?

Seventy pounds is roughly 32 kilograms, and that threshold decides whether the 1.8 percent calcium ceiling applies to a growth formula.

22. Why does a single growth diet at or below 1.8 percent calcium suit a small producer?

Respecting the tighter ceiling gives the broadest legitimate claim from a single product, which matters when working capital and machine time are limited.

23. An 18 kg puppy at five months needs about how many kcal per day?

Resting energy is 612 kcal and the four months to adult multiplier is 2.0, giving 1224 kcal per day.

24. What do the published profiles say about breed-specific nutrient requirements in kittens?

There are no breed-specific feline nutrient differences in the profiles. A slower-maturing breed is simply fed the growth diet for longer.

25. Which claim may you print without further evidence?

A measured figure with a batch date is verifiable. Breed-need and disease-prevention claims cannot be supported from the published profiles.

26. A 10 kilogram puppy under four months on a 4000 kcal per kilogram food needs about how much per day?

Resting energy 394 kcal times 3.0 gives 1181 kcal, and 1181 divided by 4000 is 0.295 kilograms.

27. Why are grams the primary feeding instruction and cups only a fallback?

A cup of one food is not a cup of another, and changing the die or the fat coating changes grams per cup, invalidating every cup instruction printed before.

28. How should meal frequency relate to the daily ration?

The daily total is fixed by energy requirement; the number of meals only decides how it is split. Feeding the full amount per meal is a common and serious overfeeding error.

29. What happens to a dog's energy multiplier at the transition to adult food after neutering?

Growth from four months uses 2.0 and a neutered adult uses 1.6, so the ration must fall or the animal gains weight quickly.

30. What overrides the feeding table for an individual animal?

Tables are starting points. Body condition is the feedback signal, adjusted by about ten percent at a time, with a veterinarian consulted where the picture is unclear.

Module 2 capstone

Design, cost and stress-test one growth diet you could put on sale within three months. Step 1: decide exactly what the label will claim, growth and reproduction, or growth including large size dogs at 70 pounds or more as adults, or kitten. That sentence sets every constraint that follows. Step 2: write the AAFCO growth minima for your species down the left of a page: protein, fat, calcium, phosphorus, the calcium to phosphorus ratio, zinc, vitamin A and vitamin D, plus taurine and arachidonic acid if it is a kitten diet. Step 3: build the recipe with real local ingredients and real quoted prices, and calculate every one of those nutrients on a dry matter basis from certificates of analysis. Step 4: if the label claims large size dogs, prove your calcium sits at or below 1.8 percent of dry matter with a written margin for consignment variation in any bone-bearing meal. Step 5: calculate metabolisable energy with the modified Atwater route and build a feeding table in grams per day for five body weights, using resting energy times 3.0 under four months, times 2.0 from four months, or times 2.5 for kittens. Step 6: write the weaning instructions that go on the pack, including the gruel ratio and the days over which it is thinned. Step 7: cost one litter through to eight weeks at your prices, and one puppy from eight weeks to six months, so you can quote a breeder a real number. Ask a veterinarian to read the finished feeding table and the label claim before you commit to print.
