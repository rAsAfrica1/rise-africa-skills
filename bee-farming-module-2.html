1. Anatomy of the Honey Bee

A honey bee's body has three main parts: head, thorax, and abdomen. The head carries two large compound eyes, each made of thousands of tiny lenses, plus three simple eyes (ocelli) that detect light intensity. Two antennae smell and feel. The mouthparts form a tongue (proboscis) for sucking nectar. The thorax is the flight engine: two pairs of wings and three pairs of legs. The hind legs have a pollen basket (corbicula) Ã¢Â€Â“ a flat, hairy area where the bee packs pollen. The front legs have a notch for cleaning antennae. The abdomen holds the honey stomach (crop) for carrying nectar, the wax glands (on the underside of segments 4-7), and the stinger (only in females).

Workers, queens, and drones differ. Workers are female, 12-15 mm long, with pollen baskets and wax glands. The queen is longer (18-20 mm) with a smooth, curved stinger and no pollen baskets. Drones are male, 15-17 mm, with big eyes that meet at the top, no stinger, and no pollen baskets. In Africa, local races like Apis mellifera scutellata are slightly smaller and more defensive than European bees.

Practical check: When you open a hive, look for these parts. A worker's pollen basket looks like a shiny, slightly flattened area on the hind leg Ã¢Â€Â“ you'll see it loaded with orange, yellow, or white pollen. The queen's abdomen extends beyond her wing tips. Drones have blunt abdomens and no stinger Ã¢Â€Â“ you can pick them up safely (but they're useless for honey).

Knowing anatomy helps you: 1) Identify castes quickly during inspections. 2) Understand why smoke calms bees Ã¢Â€Â“ it masks alarm pheromones from the sting. 3) See if a colony is queenless (no eggs, no queen, only drones). 4) Recognize diseases Ã¢Â€Â“ for example, deformed wings indicate varroa or viruses. 5) Handle frames gently Ã¢Â€Â“ crushing bees releases alarm pheromone and triggers stinging.

Use a magnifying glass or a smartphone macro lens to study bees on a frame. In Africa, many farmers keep bees in top-bar hives or log hives; you can still observe anatomy when bees cluster on the comb. Always wear a veil and gloves Ã¢Â€Â“ African bees are especially defensive. Practice identifying the three castes until you can do it in seconds. That skill saves time and reduces stings.

2. Life Cycle of the Honey Bee

The honey bee goes through four stages: egg, larva, pupa, and adult. This is complete metamorphosis. The queen lays eggs in hexagonal wax cells. A fertilized egg becomes a female (worker or queen); an unfertilized egg becomes a drone. Eggs hatch in 3 days into tiny white larvae. For the first 3 days, all larvae eat royal jelly (a milky secretion from nurse bees). After day 3, worker and drone larvae switch to a mix of honey and pollen (bee bread), while queen larvae continue on royal jelly alone.

Timeline in days: Worker: egg 3, larva 6, pupa 12 Ã¢Â€Â“ total 21 days. Queen: egg 3, larva 5.5, pupa 7.5 Ã¢Â€Â“ total 16 days. Drone: egg 3, larva 6.5, pupa 14.5 Ã¢Â€Â“ total 24 days. The queen emerges first, then workers, then drones. A queen cell is large, peanut-shaped, and hangs vertically. Worker cells are horizontal and small. Drone cells are slightly larger and have domed caps.

In Africa, with warm temperatures (25-35Ã‚Â°C), development is at the faster end. In cooler highlands, it may take a day or two longer. The queen mates once in her life, usually with 10-20 drones, then stores sperm for years. She can lay up to 1,500-2,000 eggs per day in peak season. A strong colony can have 30,000-60,000 bees.

Practical application: To check if a colony is queenright, look for eggs Ã¢Â€Â“ tiny white specks standing upright in cell bottoms. If you see eggs, the queen was there within the last 3 days. If you see only larvae and capped brood but no eggs, the queen may be failing or dead. To boost a weak colony, you can add a frame of capped brood from a strong hive Ã¢Â€Â“ those bees will emerge in 1-2 weeks. To raise a new queen, you can graft 1-day-old larvae into queen cups Ã¢Â€Â“ but this requires practice.

Beekeepers in Africa often use local materials: a sharp knife to cut comb, a bucket to hold brood, and a smoker to calm bees. Never shake a frame with eggs Ã¢Â€Â“ they dislodge and die. Keep records: note the date you see eggs, then predict when new workers will emerge (21 days later). That helps you time inspections and honey harvests. A colony with no eggs for 2 weeks is likely queenless Ã¢Â€Â“ order a new queen or combine with a queenright hive.

3. Communication and Pheromones

Honey bees talk mostly through chemicals called pheromones and through dances. The queen produces queen mandibular pheromone (QMP). It tells workers she is present, prevents them from raising a new queen, and attracts drones for mating. When a queen is removed, QMP drops, and within hours workers start building emergency queen cells. You can buy synthetic QMP lures to stabilize a queenless colony, but they are not a replacement for a real queen.

Alarm pheromone is released when a bee stings or is crushed. It smells like bananas. It triggers other bees to sting the same spot. That's why you must not crush bees during inspection. Use gentle smoke Ã¢Â€Â“ it masks alarm pheromone and makes bees retreat. African bees (Apis mellifera scutellata) are very sensitive to alarm pheromone; a single sting can set off the whole colony. Always wear a full suit and use a smoker.

Nasonov pheromone is released by workers at the entrance fanning their wings. It guides foragers home and helps a swarm settle. You can use a Nasonov lure (or a few drops of lemongrass oil) to attract a swarm into a bait hive. Lemongrass oil mimics Nasonov pheromone and is cheap and available in African markets.

Brood pheromone is a mix from larvae and pupae. It tells nurse bees how many larvae need feeding. If brood is low, foragers switch to collecting pollen. If brood is high, they focus on nectar. You can use this: if you want more honey, keep brood moderate; if you want more bees, allow the queen to lay freely.

The waggle dance communicates distance and direction to flowers. A bee dances in a figure-eight; the angle of the straight run relative to vertical (gravity) shows the angle from the sun. The duration of the waggle shows distance: about 1 second per 1,000 meters. In Africa, with diverse flora, dances help colonies exploit scattered resources. You can observe dances on a horizontal frame Ã¢Â€Â“ but it's easier on a vertical comb. Practice watching bees at the entrance: if many return with pollen, they've found a good source. If they dance vigorously, a rich patch is nearby. Use that to place your hives near flowering crops like sunflower, citrus, or acacia.

4. Foraging and Pollination Behavior

Forager bees fly out to collect nectar, pollen, propolis, and water. A worker starts foraging at about 3 weeks old. She can visit 50-100 flowers per trip and make 10-15 trips per day. In Africa, flight range is up to 3-5 km, but most foraging happens within 1-2 km of the hive. A strong colony can collect 20-30 kg of nectar per day during a good flow. Nectar is carried in the honey stomach; pollen in the pollen baskets; propolis in the pollen baskets (mixed with wax); water in the honey stomach.

Pollination happens when pollen sticks to the bee's body and is transferred to the next flower. Bees are excellent pollinators because they are flower-faithful Ã¢Â€Â“ they visit the same species repeatedly. That improves fruit set. In Africa, key bee-pollinated crops include coffee, avocado, mango, sunflower, cotton, macadamia, and many vegetables. A single strong hive per hectare can increase yields by 20-50% in some crops. For example, in Kenya, coffee farms with beehives report up to 30% higher yields.

To attract foragers, plant bee-friendly flowers near your hives. Good options: sunflower, lavender, basil, mint, acacia, citrus, and indigenous trees like Croton and Syzygium. Avoid pesticides Ã¢Â€Â“ they kill bees. If you must spray, do it in the evening when bees are inside, and use bee-safe products. Provide a clean water source Ã¢Â€Â“ a shallow tray with stones Ã¢Â€Â“ so bees don't drown.

Practical foraging management: Place hives facing east to catch morning sun. Keep hives on stands 50-100 cm high to avoid pests like ants and toads. Inspect during mid-morning when many foragers are out Ã¢Â€Â“ fewer bees in the hive means calmer inspections. If you see bees returning with heavy pollen loads, the colony is raising brood. If they return with clear nectar, a honey flow is on. During dearth (dry season), bees may rob other hives Ã¢Â€Â“ reduce entrances and avoid leaving honey exposed.

You can train bees to a specific crop by placing hives in the field just as flowers open. Move hives at night when all bees are inside, and close the entrance. In Africa, migratory beekeeping is common: beekeepers move hives to follow flowering seasons Ã¢Â€Â“ e.g., from acacia to sunflower to citrus. That maximizes honey and pollination income. Always leave enough honey for the bees Ã¢Â€Â“ at least 10-15 kg per hive Ã¢Â€Â“ especially before a dry season.

5. Seasonal Colony Dynamics

A honey bee colony changes with seasons. In Africa, seasons are often wet and dry rather than cold and hot. The colony's size, brood rearing, and honey storage follow flower availability. During the wet season (or spring in cooler regions), flowers bloom, nectar and pollen are abundant, and the queen lays heavily. The colony grows from a few thousand bees to 30,000-60,000. Drones appear. Swarming may happen. Beekeepers call this the build-up season.

In the dry season (or winter), flowers disappear. The queen reduces laying, drones are kicked out, and the colony shrinks to 5,000-15,000 bees. Bees live longer (up to 6 months) to survive. They cluster to keep warm (in highlands) or to cool the hive (in hot lowlands). They consume stored honey. If stores are low, they abscond Ã¢Â€Â“ the whole colony leaves to find a better place. Absconding is common in African bees, especially after drought, fire, or pest attack.

Practical management: In the build-up season, add supers (honey boxes) early Ã¢Â€Â“ before the flow starts. Check every 7-10 days for swarm cells. Give the queen space to lay by adding empty frames. In the dry season, reduce entrances to prevent robbing. Remove empty supers and store them dry. Feed sugar syrup (1 part sugar to 1 part water) if honey stores are below 5 kg. Never feed during a honey flow Ã¢Â€Â“ it contaminates honey. In Africa, many beekeepers harvest honey twice a year: once after the main wet season and once after a secondary flow.

Record keeping: Note the date of first drone brood, first swarm cell, and first honey harvest. After 2-3 years, you'll know your local pattern. For example, in Uganda, the main flow is March-May and September-November. In South Africa's Western Cape, it's September-December. Adjust your management to your area.

Pests and diseases also follow seasons. Varroa mites build up in the wet season. Small hive beetles thrive in warm, humid conditions. Ants invade during dry spells. Inspect monthly for pests. Use integrated pest management: strong colonies, screened bottom boards, and minimal chemicals. In Africa, many beekeepers use physical methods: grease bands on hive stands, wood ash around the base, and trapping beetles with oil traps. Always keep hives in partial shade to reduce heat stress. A well-managed colony can produce 20-40 kg of honey per year in good conditions.

6. Swarming: Causes and Management

Swarming is when a colony splits: the old queen leaves with half the workers to find a new home, and the remaining bees raise a new queen. It is natural reproduction. In Africa, swarming often happens at the start of the wet season when nectar and pollen are abundant. Causes include: overcrowding (no empty cells for the queen to lay), poor ventilation, old queen (low pheromone), and genetic tendency. African bees swarm more often than European bees Ã¢Â€Â“ sometimes 2-3 times per year.

Signs of impending swarming: 1) Queen cells Ã¢Â€Â“ peanut-shaped, hanging from the bottom of frames. 2) Drones Ã¢Â€Â“ many drones appear. 3) Reduced laying Ã¢Â€Â“ the queen's abdomen shrinks because she stops laying before swarming. 4) Bees cluster on the entrance Ã¢Â€Â“ a 'beard' of bees hanging outside. 5) The queen starts making a piping sound. Once you see capped queen cells, swarming is 1-2 days away.

Management options: 1) Prevent overcrowding Ã¢Â€Â“ add empty frames or a super early. 2) Remove queen cells every 7-10 days during swarm season. But you must remove all Ã¢Â€Â“ if you miss one, they swarm. 3) Split the colony yourself Ã¢Â€Â“ make an artificial swarm. Move the old queen with 3-4 frames of brood and bees to a new hive. Leave the queen cells in the old hive to raise a new queen. This controls swarming and increases your hives. 4) Use a bait hive Ã¢Â€Â“ a box with lemongrass oil or old comb Ã¢Â€Â“ to catch swarms. Place it 2-3 meters high, in shade, facing east. 5) Clip the queen's wings Ã¢Â€Â“ but this is controversial; it doesn't stop swarming, just delays it, and the queen may be lost.

In Africa, swarm catching is a common way to get free bees. Build a bait hive from a wooden box or a clay pot. Rub the inside with beeswax or lemongrass oil. Place it near flowering trees. Check weekly during swarm season. When a swarm moves in, leave it for 3-4 days to settle, then transfer to a permanent hive at night.

If you want to avoid swarming entirely, keep young queens (replace every 1-2 years) and give plenty of space. But remember: swarming is how bees reproduce. Many African beekeepers allow some swarming to increase their apiary. Just manage it so you don't lose half your honey production. A swarm that leaves takes 50-60% of the workers and reduces honey yield for that season. Plan ahead: in the build-up season, inspect every 7 days for queen cells. If you find them, act immediately.

Reading the Bee: What Anatomy Tells You About Your Business


Every anatomical feature maps to a management decision. The proboscis length determines which flowers your bees can work Ã¢Â€Â” African honey bees have relatively shorter tongues than some European races, which is why they favour open, shallow flowers such as Acacia, Balanites, Croton, and Calliandra. The pollen baskets on the hind legs tell you at a glance whether your colony is brooding Ã¢Â€Â” a forager returning with heavy pollen loads signals an active queen and a colony that will need space. The compound eyes and antennae drive the olfactory navigation that lets bees return to your apiary from 3Ã¢Â€Â“5 km away, which is why apiary siting near consistent forage is a commercial decision, not an aesthetic one.

African honey bee races (A. m. scutellata, A. m. adansonii, A. m. monticola, A. m. jemenitica) develop faster than European races. Communication, Pheromones, and Colony Health


Queen mandibular pheromone (QMP) suppresses worker ovary development and holds the colony together. When a queen ages or fails, QMP declines, workers build emergency cells, and the colony may become aggressive or abscond. Alarm pheromone (iso-pentyl acetate) is released when a bee stings Ã¢Â€Â” this is why you must smoke gently and avoid crushing bees; one crushed bee recruits dozens of defenders. Nasonov pheromone, released at the entrance, guides returning foragers and swarm clusters to the hive. In African contexts, absconding is often triggered by disturbance, food shortage, or pest pressure (ants, honey badgers, wax moths) Ã¢Â€Â” not by disease alone.

African subspecies are significantly more defensive than European races. Always wear a full bee suit, gloves, and closed footwear. Never inspect without smoke. Work from the side or rear of the hive, never in the flight path. Keep hives at least 100 m from homesteads, livestock kraals, and footpaths Ã¢Â€Â” in many African countries this is a legal requirement. If you are allergic to bee venom, do not work with African bees without medical clearance and an adrenaline auto-injector on site. Suspend inspections during the hottest part of the day, during dearth periods, and after any colony disturbance.

Swarming: Causes and Management


Swarming is reproduction at colony level. In African conditions, the triggers are: congestion of the brood nest, declining queen pheromone, abundant forage, and warm dry weather. Signs include queen cups along comb edges, drone brood increase, reduced foraging, and bearding. Management options: (1) pre-emptive splitting before queen cells are sealed; (2) adding supers or drawn comb to relieve congestion; (3) clipping the queen's wing (only where legal and appropriate); (4) setting bait hives to capture swarms as a low-cost colony multiplication strategy. For commercial beekeepers, swarming is not a failure Ã¢Â€Â” it is an opportunity to grow hive numbers without purchase cost.



Quick Reference Checklist



  1. Before every inspection: suit on, smoker lit and tested, hive tool and spare super ready.

  2. Check the queen: look for eggs and young larvae Ã¢Â€Â” absence means queenless within 9 days.

  3. Check space: if 7+ frames are drawn and brood is on 6, add a super immediately.

  4. Check for swarm cells: queen cups with larvae along comb bottoms = split within 7 days.

  5. Check pests: ants, wax moth, small hive beetle, and varroa (where present) Ã¢Â€Â” record and act.

  6. Check stores: honey and pollen frames; feed sugar syrup only in dearth, never during flow.

  7. Record everything: date, colony ID, brood frames, stores, queen status, action taken.

  8. Harvest only capped honey: uncapped honey ferments and destroys your brand reputation.
