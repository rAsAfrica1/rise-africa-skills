1. Choosing Paints for Different Surfaces: Mud, Stone, Wood, Metal

Mud walls need breathable paint. Use lime wash or clay paint. Mix 1 part lime putty with 3 parts water. Add 1 cup of salt per 20 liters to help it stick. For stone, use silicate paint or lime wash. Stone is porous, so avoid plastic paints that trap moisture. Wood needs flexible paint. Use linseed oil paint or acrylic exterior paint. For new wood, prime with 1 part linseed oil and 1 part turpentine. Metal needs rust protection. Use red oxide primer first, then enamel paint. In Africa, the sun is harsh. Choose paints with UV inhibitors. For mud walls, test a small area first. If the paint peels, add more lime. For wood, sand lightly before painting. For metal, remove rust with a wire brush. Buy paint from local suppliers. In Kenya, Crown Paints and Basco are common. In Nigeria, Berger Paints. For traditional finishes, use natural pigments like ochre, charcoal, or cow dung. Mix with clay and water. These are cheap and locally available. Always check the label for drying time. Most paints dry in 2-4 hours in hot weather. Apply two coats for best results. Stir paint well before use. Never paint in direct sun or when rain is expected. For mud, apply in the morning when walls are cool. For metal, paint in the shade to avoid bubbles. Remember: the right paint saves money and lasts longer.

2. Surface Preparation: Cleaning, Sanding, Priming

Surface preparation is 80% of a good paint job. First, clean the surface. For mud walls, brush off loose dirt with a stiff broom. Mix 1 cup of vinegar in 5 liters of water to kill mold. For stone, scrub with a wire brush and water. For wood, wash with soapy water, then rinse. For metal, remove rust with a wire brush or sandpaper (80-grit). Wipe with turpentine to remove grease. Second, sand. For wood, sand with 120-grit paper, then 220-grit. Always sand with the grain. For metal, sand until shiny. For mud, smooth cracks with a mud and straw mix. Let dry for 2 days. Third, prime. Primer helps paint stick and blocks stains. For mud, use lime primer: 1 part lime, 2 parts water. For wood, use an oil-based primer. For metal, use red oxide primer. For stone, use a silicate primer. Apply primer with a brush. Let dry for 4-6 hours. In Africa, dust is a problem. Wet the ground before sweeping to keep dust down. Work in the shade. If you paint in the sun, the paint dries too fast and cracks. Always check for moisture. Press a plastic sheet on the wall for 24 hours. If moisture appears, wait. Never paint a damp wall. For wood, fill holes with wood filler. For metal, fill dents with epoxy. After priming, sand lightly with 220-grit. Wipe with a tack cloth. Now you are ready to paint. Good preparation means the paint lasts 5-10 years instead of 2.

3. Applying Paint: Brushes, Rollers, and Sprayers

Choose the right tool for the job. Brushes are best for small areas, corners, and detailed work. Use a 2-inch brush for trim and a 4-inch brush for walls. For mud and stone, use a wide brush with stiff bristles. For wood, use a soft brush. For metal, use a small brush. Rollers cover large areas fast. Use a 9-inch roller with a 10mm nap for smooth walls. For rough stone or mud, use a 20mm nap roller. Sprayers are fastest for big jobs. Use an airless sprayer for exterior walls. But sprayers need thinning. Add 10% water to acrylic paint. Always wear a mask when spraying. For brushes and rollers, use the 'wet edge' method: paint in 1-meter sections, then blend. Don't let the edge dry. For mud walls, apply paint in one direction. For wood, paint with the grain. For metal, paint in thin coats. Two thin coats are better than one thick coat. In hot weather, add a paint extender to slow drying. Mix 1 cup of glycerin per 4 liters of paint. For rollers, dip halfway, then roll on a grid. For brushes, dip one-third of the bristles. Tap off excess. Start at the top and work down. For sprayers, keep the nozzle 30cm from the surface. Move in steady lines. Overlap by 50%. Clean tools immediately. For water-based paint, wash with soap and water. For oil-based, use turpentine. Store brushes in a plastic bag in the fridge overnight. This saves cleaning time. Practice on a board first. A good painter uses less paint and gets a better finish.

4. Traditional Finishes: Earth Plasters and Natural Pigments

Traditional finishes are cheap, beautiful, and breathable. Earth plasters are made from clay, sand, and straw. Mix 3 parts clay soil, 1 part sand, and 1 part chopped straw. Add water until thick like peanut butter. Apply with a trowel or hand. First coat: 1cm thick. Let dry for 2 days. Second coat: 5mm thick. Smooth with a wet sponge. For a shiny finish, burnish with a stone or plastic trowel. Natural pigments come from rocks, plants, and insects. Ochre (red, yellow, brown) is found in many African soils. Charcoal gives black. Cow dung gives grey-brown. Mix pigments with clay slip: 1 part pigment, 2 parts clay, 3 parts water. Apply with a brush or rag. For a mottled look, use a sponge. For a solid color, apply two coats. In West Africa, people use 'banco' finish: mud, cow dung, and shea butter. In East Africa, 'lime wash' with ochre is common. These finishes last 2-5 years. They need reapplication after heavy rains. But they are free or very cheap. They also keep houses cool. To make lime wash: 1 part lime putty, 3 parts water, 1 cup salt per 20 liters. Add pigment. Apply with a brush. Let dry. For a water-resistant finish, add 1 cup of boiled cassava starch per 10 liters. Traditional finishes are good for mud and stone. Do not use on metal or wood. For wood, use linseed oil. For metal, use enamel. Always test a small area. If it rubs off, add more clay. If it cracks, add more sand. These finishes are part of African heritage. They are sustainable and locally sourced.

5. Protective Coatings for Weather Resistance

In Africa, weather is tough: sun, rain, dust, and wind. Protective coatings make buildings last. For mud walls, use lime wash with casein (milk protein). Mix 1 liter of milk with 1 liter of lime putty and 5 liters of water. This forms a water-resistant layer. Apply two coats. For stone, use a silicone-based water repellent. Brush it on. It lets stone breathe but stops rain. For wood, use linseed oil or polyurethane. Linseed oil is natural. Mix 1 part oil with 1 part turpentine. Apply three coats. Let dry 24 hours between coats. For metal, use epoxy or polyurethane paint. First, remove rust. Then apply red oxide primer. Then two coats of enamel. For roofs, use aluminum paint or bitumen. For walls, add a UV stabilizer to paint. In hot climates, white or light colors reflect heat. In rainy areas, use dark colors and add fungicide. For thatch, use a fire retardant. Mix 1 part borax, 1 part boric acid, and 10 parts water. Spray on. For concrete, use acrylic sealer. Apply with a roller. For all coatings, check the drying time. In humid weather, wait longer. In dry heat, work fast. Always wear gloves and a mask. Store coatings away from children. Reapply every 2-3 years. A good coating saves money on repairs. It also keeps the building cool in summer and warm in winter. Test a small area first. If it peels, try a different product. Local hardware stores stock these. Ask for 'weather guard' or 'damp proof' paint.

6. Hands-On: Painting a Small Building Exterior

Let's paint a small building exterior, step by step. You need: 20 liters of exterior paint, 5 liters of primer, 2 brushes (4-inch and 2-inch), 1 roller with 20mm nap, 1 roller tray, sandpaper (80 and 120 grit), a wire brush, a scraper, a ladder, a drop cloth, and a mask. First, clear the area. Move plants and furniture. Cover the ground with a drop cloth. Second, clean the walls. For mud, brush off loose dirt. For wood, wash with soapy water. For metal, remove rust. Let dry for 2 days. Third, repair cracks. For mud, fill with mud and straw. For wood, fill with wood filler. For metal, fill with epoxy. Sand smooth. Fourth, prime. Apply one coat of primer with a brush. Start at the top. Work in 1-meter sections. Let dry 4 hours. Fifth, paint. Stir the paint. Pour into a tray. Use a roller for large areas. Use a brush for corners and trim. Apply two coats. Let dry 4 hours between coats. For mud, use a brush only. For metal, use a brush. For wood, use a roller then brush. In hot sun, paint in the morning. In rain, wait. Sixth, clean up. Wash brushes with soap and water. Store paint in a sealed can. Check the finish. Look for missed spots. Touch up with a brush. Now your building is protected. It will last 5-10 years. You saved money by doing it yourself. You can teach others. Remember: preparation is key. Never skip priming. Always use two coats. Keep a small can of paint for touch-ups. Congratulations! You have finished Method 9.

<h2>Practical Reference: Painting and Surface Finishing for African Buildings</h2>

<p>In African construction, surface finishing is not merely cosmetic. It is a business decision. A well-finished building resists rain, sun, dust, and insects; it commands higher rent; it lasts longer between maintenance cycles; and it protects the asset that generates income. This Practical Reference distils Method 9 into actionable guidance for contractors, facility managers, and property owners working with mud, stone, wood, and metal surfaces across the continent.</p>

<p>Whether you are finishing a rural clinic in Tanzania, a guesthouse in Ghana, or a warehouse in Kenya, the principles are the same: choose the right coating for the substrate, prepare the surface properly, apply it correctly, and protect it from the weather. The details differ by material and climate, but the business logic does not.</p>

<h3>Why Surface Finishing Matters for African Buildings</h3>

<p>Africa's building stock faces a punishing environment: intense ultraviolet radiation, seasonal heavy rains, high humidity in coastal and tropical zones, and dust-laden harmattan winds in the Sahel. Unprotected mud walls erode; untreated timber warps and is attacked by termites; bare metal corrodes within months. According to the Global Alliance for Buildings and Construction, buildings in Africa account for a growing share of energy use, and the durability of the envelope directly affects operational costs and asset value.</p>

<p>From a business perspective, finishing is a revenue-protection activity. A coat of paint that costs a few hundred dollars can extend the life of a wall by years, reduce repair bills, and keep tenants satisfied. The return on investment is often measured in months, not decades.</p>

<h3>Surface Preparation: Cleaning, Sanding, Priming</h3>

<p>Preparation consumes 6070% of a quality paint job's labour. Skipping it is the fastest way to waste money on paint that peels within a year.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Cleaning:</strong> Remove dust, grease, mould, and loose particles. In dusty Sahelian conditions, wash walls with clean water and a stiff brush; allow to dry fully. In humid coastal areas, treat mould with a diluted bleach solution and rinse.</li>
<li><strong>Sanding:</strong> Smooth rough surfaces with sandpaper or abrasive blocks. For wood, sand along the grain. For metal, remove rust with a wire brush or sandpaper until bare metal is exposed.</li>
<li><strong>Priming:</strong> Apply a primer suited to the substrate. Primers seal porous surfaces, improve adhesion, and block stains. On mud walls, a lime-based primer is preferable to acrylic. On metal, red oxide primer is the standard in most African markets.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Applying Paint: Brushes, Rollers, and Sprayers</h3>

<p>The application method affects finish quality, speed, and paint consumption. In African markets, brushes and rollers are the most accessible and cost-effective for small to medium projects. Sprayers offer speed on large surfaces but require masking, ventilation, and higher initial equipment cost.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Brushes:</strong> Best for edges, corners, and detailed work. Natural-bristle brushes suit oil-based paints; synthetic bristles suit water-based paints. In many African towns, local brush makers produce affordable options inspect for bristle shedding before purchase.</li>
<li><strong>Rollers:</strong> Ideal for large flat surfaces. Use short-nap rollers for smooth walls and long-nap for rough masonry. Rollers reduce labour time by 3050% compared to brushes.</li>
<li><strong>Sprayers:</strong> Fastest for large areas and complex shapes. Airless sprayers are common in commercial painting. They require thinner paint, careful masking, and respiratory protection.</li>
</ul>

<p>Whichever method you choose, apply two thin coats rather than one thick coat. Thin coats dry faster, adhere better, and produce a more uniform finish. Work in shade where possible; painting in direct sun causes rapid drying and brush marks.</p>

<h3>Traditional Finishes: Earth Plasters and Natural Pigments</h3>

<p>Africa has a rich tradition of earth plasters and natural pigments that predates synthetic paints by centuries. These finishes are not inferior they are often better suited to local climates and budgets. Earth plasters use clay, sand, and natural fibres (straw, dung, or sisal) to create durable, breathable surfaces. Natural pigments from ochre, charcoal, and plant extracts provide colour without volatile organic compounds (VOCs).</p>

<p>In the Sahel, earth plasters are used to protect mud-brick buildings from rain erosion. In West Africa, indigo and kola nut dyes have been used to colour walls. In Southern Africa, cow dung and termite mound soil are traditional additives that improve water resistance. These methods are low-cost, locally sourced, and generate income for skilled artisans.</p>

<p>For modern applications, earth plasters can be stabilised with lime or cement to improve durability. The <em>UN-Habitat Building Materials and Construction Technologies</em> handbook provides detailed mix ratios and application techniques.</p>

<h3>Protective Coatings for Weather Resistance</h3>

<p>Weather resistance is the single most important performance requirement in most African climates. UV radiation degrades paint films; rain drives moisture into walls; humidity accelerates corrosion. Protective coatings must address all three.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>UV stability:</strong> Choose acrylic or silicate paints with UV inhibitors. Avoid cheap oil-based paints that chalk and fade quickly.</li>
<li><strong>Water repellency:</strong> Silicone-based additives or masonry paints reduce water absorption while remaining breathable.</li>
<li><strong>Corrosion protection:</strong> On metal, use a three-coat system: primer, intermediate coat, and topcoat. Epoxy and polyurethane coatings offer the best performance in coastal areas.</li>
<li><strong>Fungal resistance:</strong> In humid zones, add fungicide to paint or use paints with built-in mould resistance.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Hands-On: Painting a Small Building Exterior</h3>

<p>This step-by-step guide assumes a small single-storey building with mud, stone, or rendered walls. Budget: 25 days for a 50100 m exterior, depending on surface condition and labour availability.</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Assess and repair:</strong> Inspect walls for cracks, loose plaster, and damp patches. Repair before painting. Allow repairs to cure fully.</li>
<li><strong>Clean:</strong> Wash walls with water and a stiff brush. Remove mould with diluted bleach. Rinse and allow to dry for 2448 hours.</li>
<li><strong>Mask and protect:</strong> Cover windows, doors, and ground with plastic sheeting or old cloth. Mask edges with tape.</li>
<li><strong>Prime:</strong> Apply primer with a brush or roller. Work from top to bottom. Allow to dry per manufacturer instructions.</li>
<li><strong>Apply topcoat:</strong> Apply two thin coats of exterior paint. Use a roller for large areas and a brush for edges. Maintain a wet edge to avoid lap marks.</li>
<li><strong>Clean up:</strong> Remove masking tape while paint is still slightly tacky. Clean brushes and rollers with appropriate solvent or water. Dispose of waste responsibly.</li>
</ol>

<p>For a typical 80 m exterior in East Africa, material costs range from USD 150400 depending on paint quality and local prices. Labour costs vary by region. The business case is straightforward: a well-painted exterior reduces repair costs, improves tenant satisfaction, and extends the building's service life by 510 years.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li> Identify substrate correctly (mud, stone, wood, or metal) before selecting paint.</li>
<li> Clean surface thoroughly; remove dust, grease, mould, and loose particles.</li>
<li> Repair cracks and defects; allow repairs to cure before painting.</li>
<li> Apply the correct primer for the substrate; never skip priming.</li>
<li> Use two thin coats rather than one thick coat for better adhesion and finish.</li>
<li> Work in shade or cooler hours to avoid rapid drying and brush marks.</li>
<li> Wear personal protective equipment (gloves, respirator, eye protection).</li>
<li> Clean tools and dispose of paint waste responsibly after the job.</li>
</ul>
