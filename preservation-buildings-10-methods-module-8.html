Module 8 — Method 7: Fermentation Rooms and Crocks

Salt, acid and time — and the room that lets them work without letting the wrong microbes in.

Learning outcomes

  • Explain how fermentation preserves food — lactic acid, pH drop and salt.
  • Choose and manage crocks, drums and airlocks.
  • Design a fermentation room with the correct temperature, humidity and ventilation.
  • Calculate salt ratios and brine strength for reliable ferments.
  • Monitor pH and recognise the difference between good and bad fermentation.
  • Prevent the faults that ruin batches.

1. How fermentation preserves

Fermentation is not a single method. It is a family of controlled microbial processes that produce acids, alcohols or both, dropping the pH low enough that spoilage and pathogenic organisms cannot grow.

Ferment typeMain microbeProductTarget pH
Lactic acid — vegetablesLactobacillus speciesSauerkraut, kimchi, pickled cucumbers, turnips3.3–3.8
Lactic acid — cerealLactobacillus, yeastOgi, garri, kenkey, sourdough3.5–4.2
Lactic acid — proteinLactobacillus, BacillusIru, ogiri, dawadawa (fermented seeds)5.0–6.0 (salt and ammonia also protect)
AlcoholicYeastPalm wine, fruit wines, vinegar base3.0–4.0 (after secondary acidification)
Acetic — vinegarAcetobacterVinegar from alcoholic base2.5–3.5

Why pH matters more than salt

Clostridium botulinum will not grow below pH 4.6. Most lactic ferments aim for pH 3.3–3.8 — well below that line. Salt alone does not guarantee safety; the pH drop does.

Target: vegetable ferments reach pH 3.5 or below within 5–7 days at 18–22 °C.

Essential features of any vessel

  1. Food-grade material. No recycled containers that held chemicals, no galvanised metal, no untreated metal in contact with brine.
  2. Airlock or water seal. CO₂ must escape; air must not enter. A simple bubbler airlock or a water-seal rim both work.
  3. Weight or follower. The ferment must stay submerged. Use a clean stone, a glass weight, a zip bag of brine, or a purpose-made follower.
  4. Headspace. Leave 20–25 % of the volume free — the ferment will rise and foam.
  5. Wide mouth. You must be able to reach in to clean and to pack the vegetables.
  6. Easy to sanitise. Smooth surfaces, no scratches, no crevices.

3. The fermentation room

Fermentation is slow and temperature-sensitive. The room is a controlled environment, not a corner of the kitchen.

ParameterTargetWhy
Temperature — vegetable ferments18–22 °CBelow 15 °C is too slow and risks spoilage; above 25 °C is too fast and softens texture
Temperature — protein ferments (iru, ogiri)28–35 °CBacillus species need warmer conditions
Temperature stability± 2 °CFluctuation causes uneven fermentation
Relative humidity70–80 %Prevents the brine from evaporating and the top drying
LightDark or dimUV degrades colour and can inhibit some microbes
Ventilation4–6 ACH, with CO₂ vented highCO₂ is heavier than air and can build up to dangerous levels
FloorSloped to drain, acid-resistantBrine spills are acidic and corrosive
WallsWashable to 1.5 mBrine spray and spills

CO₂ can kill

Fermentation produces large volumes of carbon dioxide. In a sealed or poorly ventilated room, CO₂ can displace oxygen and cause unconsciousness or death. Never enter a fermentation room that has been closed for hours without ventilating it first, and never put your head into a fermenting vessel. Fit a high-level vent and a low-level air inlet, and keep the door open when working in the room.

Room layout

  1. Prep area — wash, chop, salt. Stainless bench, potable water, drainage.
  2. Packing area — pack into crocks or drums, add brine, fit follower and airlock.
  3. Fermentation area — the temperature-controlled part. Insulated if possible, with a small heater or cooling unit and a thermostat.
  4. Monitoring station — pH meter, thermometer, notebook. Sampling tools, all sanitised.
  5. Finishing area — decant, pack into retail jars, label. Separated from the raw prep area.
  6. Storage — cool, dark, stable. Finished product at 4–10 °C for extended shelf life.

4. Salt ratios and brine strength

Salt selects for the right microbes and against the wrong ones. It must be measured by weight, not by spoon or handful.

Salt calculations — three common methods

1. Dry salt method (sauerkraut, kimchi)
Salt = 2–2.5 % of the shredded vegetable weight.
10 kg cabbage × 0.02 = 200 g salt. Massage until brine forms.

2. Brine method (cucumbers, whole vegetables)
Brine % = salt weight / (salt + water weight) × 100.
For 5 % brine in 10 L water (water ≈ 10 kg):
salt = 0.05 × 10 / (1 − 0.05) = 526 g salt

3. Salt-to-water ratio (quick reference)
2 % brine = 20 g salt per litre of water
3 % brine = 30 g/L
5 % brine = 50 g/L
10 % brine = 100 g/L (for long-stored or warm-climate ferments)

ProductSalt methodTarget %Typical ferment time
SauerkrautDry salt on shredded cabbage2.0–2.5 %2–4 weeks
KimchiDry salt, then paste and pack2.5–3.0 %3 days to 3 weeks
Pickled cucumbersBrine5 %1–3 weeks
Pickled turnips, carrotsBrine3–5 %1–2 weeks
Iru, ogiriDry salt after fermentation5–10 % added at end2–4 days warm ferment
Ogi, garriSoak, ferment, then processNo added salt2–4 days

Use non-iodised salt

Iodine and anti-caking agents can inhibit lactic bacteria and cause off flavours and soft textures. Use pure sea salt, pickling salt or kosher salt — anything without iodine or anti-caking additives.

5. Monitoring pH

pH is the single best indicator that a ferment is working correctly. Check it daily for the first week.

DayExpected pHWhat to do if different
05.5–6.5 (raw vegetable)—
15.0–5.5If no drop, check temperature and salt level
34.0–4.5If above 4.5, add a starter from a previous batch or increase temperature
5–73.5–3.8If above 4.0, discard — do not sell
14+3.3–3.6Stable; move to cool storage

pH measurement

  • Use a calibrated digital pH meter, not strips, for anything you will sell.
  • Calibrate with pH 4.0 and 7.0 buffer solutions before each session.
  • Take a sample of liquid (brine), not solid vegetable. Rinse the probe with distilled water between samples.
  • Record pH for every batch. This is your evidence of a safe product.

6. Good fermentation vs bad

SignGood fermentationBad fermentation
SmellSour, tangy, cleanRotting, faecal, cheesy, solvent
SurfaceClear brine, maybe a thin white film (kahm yeast)Fuzzy mould in green, black, blue or pink
ColourSlightly duller than rawBrown, black, or dark patches
TextureFirm, crispSoft, slimy, mushy
BubblesSteady small bubbles in the first weekNo bubbles at all, or violent bubbling after week 1
pHFalling steadily to 3.5Stuck above 4.5 after 5 days

When in doubt, throw it out

Fuzzy mould (not the flat white film of kahm yeast) means discard the whole batch. Do not skim it off. The mould's threads extend into the ferment even if you cannot see them. Slimy or soft texture with an off smell is also discard. There is no safe salvage for a spoiled ferment.

Room sizing — worked example

Production target: 100 kg of sauerkraut per week.
Fermentation time: 21 days = 3 weeks.
Working stock: 3 weeks × 100 kg = 300 kg in fermentation at any time.

In 50 L crocks, each holding ~40 kg of packed cabbage:
Number of crocks = 300 / 40 = 7.5, round up to 8 crocks.

Floor space per crock (including working space around it): 1.0 m².
Fermentation room floor area: 8 m² minimum. Add packing and prep areas: total building 20–25 m².

9. Regulatory and safety notes

  • Fermented products are usually classified as food and require business registration and inspection.
  • Keep a pH record for every batch. Inspectors and buyers may ask for it.
  • Test each new recipe for pH, salt % and microbiological safety before selling.
  • Label with product name, ingredients, net weight, your business details, batch code, best-before and storage instruction.
  • Fermented products are usually stored cool (4–10 °C) after fermentation to slow further acid development.
  • CO₂ safety: fit a monitor, ventilate before entering, never lean into an open vessel.
