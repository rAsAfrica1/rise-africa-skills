1. Corrugated Fiberboard: Grades and Selection

Corrugated fiberboard is the workhorse of export packaging. It is made of a fluted layer (the wavy part) glued between flat linerboards. In Africa, you will find it in three common grades: single wall (one flute), double wall (two flutes), and triple wall (three flutes). For export, double wall is the minimum for most products, and triple wall for heavy or fragile items. The flute type matters: A-flute is thick (about 5mm) and gives good cushioning; B-flute is thinner (3mm) and gives better crush resistance; C-flute is a compromise (4mm). For stacking strength, use B-flute or C-flute. For cushioning, use A-flute.

The key number is the Edge Crush Test (ECT) rating. This measures how much force a box can take on its edge before crushing. For export by sea, you need at least 32 ECT for boxes under 30 kg, and 44 ECT for heavier loads. In Africa, many suppliers sell boxes by 'bursting strength' (Mullen test), but ECT is more reliable for stacking. Ask your supplier for the ECT value. If they cannot provide it, test a sample: stack boxes 2 meters high in a humid room for 24 hours and see if they deform.

Humidity is your enemy. Corrugated board loses up to 50% of its strength at 90% relative humidity. For export from coastal cities like Mombasa or Lagos, always ask for moisture-resistant liners (e.g., kraft liner with a water-resistant coating) or use a plastic inner liner. Also, avoid printing that covers more than 40% of the box surface, as ink can weaken the board. Store boxes flat, off the floor, in a dry room.

When selecting a box, calculate the stacking strength needed: weight of boxes above x safety factor (3 for sea freight). For example, if you stack 5 boxes of 20 kg each, the bottom box must support 100 kg. A 44 ECT double-wall box can handle about 150 kg in ideal conditions, but with humidity, choose triple wall or add corner boards. Always test a sample shipment before committing to a full order.

2. Wooden Crates and Pallets: ISPM 15 Compliance

Wooden crates and pallets are strong, cheap, and repairable, but they carry a hidden risk: pests. The international standard ISPM 15 (International Standards for Phytosanitary Measures No. 15) requires all wood packaging material (except processed wood like plywood) to be treated and marked. If you export without compliance, your shipment can be refused, fumigated at your cost, or destroyed. In Africa, many small exporters lose money because they ignore this rule.

ISPM 15 requires either heat treatment (HT) or methyl bromide fumigation (MB). Heat treatment means heating the wood core to 56Â°C for at least 30 minutes. Fumigation uses methyl bromide gas, but it is being phased out due to ozone damage. Heat treatment is preferred. After treatment, the wood must be marked with a stamp: IPPC logo, country code (e.g., KE for Kenya, NG for Nigeria), producer code, and treatment code (HT or MB). The stamp must be on at least two opposite sides of the crate or pallet. You cannot make your own stamp; it must be applied by an approved treatment provider.

In practice, you have two options: buy pre-treated wood or pallets from a certified supplier, or send your wood to a treatment facility. In Kenya, for example, the Kenya Plant Health Inspectorate Service (KEPHIS) licenses treatment providers. Expect to pay about $2-5 per pallet for heat treatment. For crates, the cost depends on size. Always keep the certificate of treatment; some countries ask for it.

When building a crate, use nails, not screws, for speed. For heavy machinery, use 2.5 cm thick boards and reinforce corners with diagonal bracing. Pallets come in standard sizes: 1200x800 mm (Euro pallet) and 1200x1000 mm (standard pallet). For air freight, use lighter crates (plywood or Oriented Strand Board) because weight costs money. For sea freight, solid wood is fine. Always check the destination country's rules: Australia and New Zealand are very strict, while some African countries have weaker enforcement, but do not risk it. Compliance is cheap; non-compliance is expensive.

3. Plastic and Foam Packaging for Protection

Plastic and foam packaging protect against moisture, shock, and vibration. In Africa, you will find polyethylene (PE) bags, polypropylene (PP) woven sacks, polyethylene terephthalate (PET) bottles, and expanded polystyrene (EPS) foam. Each has a role. For dry goods like grains or powders, use PE bags (thickness 80-100 microns) inside a woven PP sack for strength. For liquids, use HDPE (high-density polyethylene) jerrycans or PET bottles. For fragile items, use EPS foam or bubble wrap.

Bubble wrap is measured by bubble size: small (10 mm) for light items, large (30 mm) for heavy items. Use at least two layers. For extra protection, use foam sheets (PE or PU) cut to shape. A common mistake is to wrap an item tightly; leave a 2-3 cm gap between the item and the box wall, filled with foam or paper. This 'floating' effect absorbs shock. For electronic items, use anti-static bags (pink or silver) to prevent static damage.

Moisture control is critical. Silica gel desiccant bags absorb humidity inside a sealed package. Use 10-20 grams of silica gel per cubic meter of package volume. For long sea voyages, use a desiccant with a humidity indicator card. Also, seal packages with heat sealers (for PE bags) or good quality tape. Do not use cheap tape that peels in heat; use acrylic or hot-melt tape.

Cost and availability vary. In many African cities, plastic packaging is available in local markets, but quality can be inconsistent. Test thickness with a micrometer: PE bags should be at least 80 microns for export. Foam can be purchased in sheets and cut with a hot wire cutter. For small-scale exporters, consider using recycled plastic (e.g., shredded PET) as cushioning, but ensure it is clean and dry. Always test a packed sample by dropping it from 1 meter onto concrete; if the product survives, your packaging is adequate.

4. Metal Packaging for Durability

Metal packaging is used for durability, barrier properties, and long shelf life. Common types are tin-plated steel (tinplate), aluminum, and stainless steel. Tinplate cans are used for food, chemicals, and paints. Aluminum is lighter and corrosion-resistant, used for aerosols, beverage cans, and foil. Stainless steel is for high-value or corrosive products. In Africa, metal packaging is often imported, so cost is higher than plastic or paper, but it offers unmatched protection.

For export, the key issues are corrosion and denting. Corrosion happens when moisture and oxygen meet the metal. To prevent it, choose cans with an internal lacquer (epoxy or phenolic) for acidic foods like tomatoes or pineapple. For dry products, no lacquer is needed. For export by sea, humidity can rust the outside of cans; apply a thin oil film or use VCI (vapor corrosion inhibitor) paper. VCI paper releases a chemical that protects metal surfaces. Wrap each item or line the box with VCI paper.

Denting occurs during handling. Use cans with a bead (a raised ring) for strength. For large cans (e.g., 20 liters), use a thicker gauge: 0.23 mm for small cans, 0.32 mm for large. Always pack cans in a box with dividers so they do not touch. For bottles, use metal caps with a tamper-evident ring. For drums, use a locking ring and gasket. Test by dropping a full drum from 1.2 meters onto its edge; it should not leak.

Metal packaging is recyclable and can be reused if cleaned. In Africa, informal recycling of aluminum is common, but for export, use new or reconditioned containers. Reconditioned drums must be cleaned and tested for leaks. Always check destination regulations: some countries ban certain metals (e.g., lead solder). For food contact, ensure the metal is food-grade. If you are packing liquids, leave 5-10% headspace for expansion. Metal is heavy, so calculate freight costs: a 20-liter steel drum weighs about 2 kg empty, while a plastic jerrycan weighs 0.5 kg. Choose metal only when protection justifies the weight.

5. Sustainable and Biodegradable Materials

Buyers in Europe, North America, and increasingly in Africa demand sustainable packaging. Biodegradable materials break down naturally, reducing waste. Common options are paper, cardboard, molded pulp, bioplastics (PLA, starch-based), and natural fibers like jute, sisal, and coconut coir. In Africa, you have abundant agricultural residues: banana leaves, maize husks, and sugarcane bagasse. These can be processed into packaging.

Paper and cardboard are the easiest. Use recycled paper for inner wrapping and corrugated board for boxes. Ensure the paper is not coated with plastic; look for '100% recycled' or 'compostable' labels. Molded pulp (made from recycled paper) is excellent for egg trays, fruit trays, and protective corners. It is cheap and widely available in Africa. For example, a molded pulp tray for 30 eggs costs about $0.10. Jute bags are strong and reusable; they are ideal for coffee, cocoa, and grains. A 50 kg jute bag costs $1-2. Sisal fibers can be woven into mats or used as cushioning.

Bioplastics like PLA (polylactic acid) are made from corn starch or sugarcane. They look like plastic but compost in industrial facilities. However, they need specific conditions (58Â°C, 60% humidity) to break down. In many African countries, industrial composting is rare, so PLA may not degrade in a landfill. Starch-based foams (e.g., packing peanuts) are water-soluble and compostable at home. They are made from cassava or maize starch. You can make them locally: mix starch, water, and a little vinegar, then expand with heat. They are cheap but not strong for heavy items.

When using sustainable materials, test for strength and moisture. Paper loses strength when wet; add a wax coating (beeswax or paraffin) for water resistance. For export, check destination rules: some countries require biodegradable packaging to be certified (e.g., OK Compost). Also, consider the carbon footprint of importing bioplastics; local materials like jute or molded pulp have lower transport emissions. Always label clearly: 'biodegradable' or 'compostable' to avoid confusion. Remember, sustainable does not mean weak; with proper design, it can match conventional packaging.

6. Material Selection Based on Product and Route

Choosing the right packaging material depends on two things: the product and the route. Start by listing product characteristics: weight, fragility, moisture sensitivity, value, and shape. Then list route conditions: mode of transport (sea, air, road), duration, humidity, temperature, and handling. For example, a 20 kg bag of coffee beans going by sea from Uganda to Europe needs a moisture barrier and stacking strength. A 2 kg glass jar of honey going by air from Tanzania to Dubai needs cushioning and light weight.

For sea freight, expect high humidity (up to 90%) and long transit (3-6 weeks). Use moisture-resistant materials: plastic-lined jute bags, VCI paper, or desiccants. For air freight, weight is critical; use lightweight materials like foam and thin corrugated board. For road transport within Africa, dust and vibration are main risks; use sealed bags and cushioning. For rail, shock is higher; use double-wall corrugated or wooden crates.

Create a decision matrix. Score each material (corrugated, wood, plastic, metal, sustainable) on cost, protection, weight, availability, and compliance. For a fragile ceramic vase, corrugated box with foam inserts scores high on protection but medium on cost. For a heavy machine part, wooden crate with ISPM 15 treatment scores high on protection but low on weight. For a food product, metal cans score high on barrier but low on cost. Use the matrix to justify your choice.

Always test your packaging. The standard test is ISTA (International Safe Transit Association) 3A, which simulates drops, vibration, and compression. You can do a simplified version: drop test from 1 meter (6 faces), vibration test on a truck for 1 hour, and compression test by stacking boxes 2 meters high for 24 hours. If the product survives, you are ready. Also, consider cost: packaging should be 5-10% of product value. If it is more, redesign. Finally, involve your buyer: ask for their packaging requirements. Many buyers have specific rules (e.g., no Styrofoam, specific pallet size). Meeting their needs prevents rejections.

Material Selection by Product Type

Tea, coffee, and cocoa (Kenya, Uganda, Ghana, Ethiopia, Rwanda): These are moisture-sensitive, high-value dry commodities. Multi-wall paper sacks with internal moisture barrier or foil-lined corrugated cartons are standard. For specialty and premium grades, buyers increasingly demand recyclable or biodegradable inner liners. Never use untreated wood as primary contact for food.

Fresh horticulture (Kenya, Ethiopia, Tanzania, South Africa): The cold chain dictates packaging. Ventilated corrugated cartons with wax coating or HDPE crates are common. Punnets and trays must allow airflow. For air-freighted flowers, lightweight corrugated with minimal void space reduces freight cost â every kilogram counts.

Processed foods and sauces (Nigeria, Ghana, South Africa): Glass jars and metal cans require secondary protection. Corrugated partitions, foam inserts, or molded pulp trays prevent breakage. Metal packaging is preferred for long shelf-life products and where barrier properties are critical.

Cashew, sesame, shea, and other bulk agricultural commodities (West Africa): Jute or woven polypropylene bags are traditional, but export buyers increasingly specify food-grade liners and palletised, stretch-wrapped unit loads. Wooden pallets must be ISPM 15 compliant.

Handicrafts, textiles, and light manufactured goods (across Africa): Corrugated cartons with moisture barrier are usually sufficient. For high-value items, add tamper-evident seals and consider plastic or metal outer packaging to deter theft.

Sustainable Materials: Opportunity and Caution

European and UK buyers are pushing hard on packaging sustainability. Biodegradable and compostable materials â PLA, starch-based films, molded pulp, recycled paperboard â can be a competitive differentiator for African exporters. However, two cautions apply. First, verify the buyer's certification requirements: "biodegradable" means different things in different markets, and some buyers require specific EN 13432 or ASTM D6400 certification. Second, sustainable materials often have lower moisture and impact resistance than conventional options â test them on your actual route before committing a full shipment.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Match material to product: moisture-sensitive goods need barrier packaging; fragile goods need shock absorption; heavy goods need structural strength.
  2. Match material to route: sea freight demands humidity resistance; air freight demands lightweight; road freight demands durability against vibration.
  3. Verify ISPM 15 compliance for all wooden pallets, crates, and dunnage â check the IPPC stamp, country code, treatment method, and producer number.
  4. Confirm buyer specifications before ordering packaging â many EU and UK retailers have approved supplier lists and material restrictions (especially on EPS foam and single-use plastics).
  5. Test the full pack: conduct a drop test, stack test, and (where relevant) humidity test on a sample before the first commercial shipment.
  6. Calculate total packaging cost including material, labour, treatment, compliance certification, and any return logistics for reusable crates.
  7. Document everything: keep treatment certificates, material safety data sheets, and food-contact certifications ready for customs and buyer audits.
  8. Review annually: packaging regulations, buyer requirements, and material costs change â what worked last season may fail this season.
