1. Distinguishing ferrous and non-ferrous metals: steel, iron, aluminum, copper

When you break cars and scrap metal, the first big split is ferrous vs non-ferrous. Ferrous metals contain iron and stick to a magnet. Steel and iron are ferrous. Non-ferrous metals like aluminum, copper, brass, and lead do not stick to a magnet (with a few exceptions like stainless steel). This matters because non-ferrous metals sell for much more per kilogram. In many African scrapyards, clean copper can fetch 5 to 10 times the price of steel. So your profit depends on separating them properly.

Start with a strong magnet. A cheap fridge magnet is too weak; get a neodymium magnet or an old speaker magnet. Test every piece. If it sticks, it's ferrous. If it doesn't, it's non-ferrous. But be careful: some stainless steel is weakly magnetic. If a piece is shiny and resists rust but sticks slightly, it might be stainless steel, which is ferrous but often worth more than regular steel. Keep a separate pile for it.

For non-ferrous, look at color and weight. Aluminum is light and silvery. Copper is reddish-brown and heavy for its size. Brass is yellow-gold and also heavy. Lead is dull grey and very heavy. In Africa, common sources: car radiators (copper/brass), engine blocks (aluminum), wiring (copper), and wheel rims (aluminum). Car bodies are steel. Cast iron is used in engine blocks and brake drums; it's magnetic but brittle and breaks with a loud snap.

At your yard, set up separate bins or areas for: steel, cast iron, stainless steel, aluminum, copper, brass, and other (lead, zinc, etc.). Label them clearly. Train your workers to test with a magnet before throwing anything in a bin. One mistakeâlike mixing a copper wire into the steel pileâcan cost you money because the steel mill will reject contaminated loads or pay you lower grade. Also, keep a small file or grinder to check spark tests later. Start simple: magnet first, then color, then weight. With practice, you'll identify metals in seconds.

2. Using a magnet and spark test to identify common metals

A magnet is your first tool, but it only tells you if iron is present. To tell steel from cast iron, or to identify different grades of steel, you need a spark test. This is a skill every scrapyard worker in Africa should learn. You'll need a bench grinder or an angle grinder with a grinding wheel. Wear safety glasses and glovesâsparks are hot.

Hold the metal against the spinning wheel with moderate pressure. Watch the sparks that fly off. The color, length, and shape of the sparks tell you what you have. Here's a simple guide:

- **Cast iron**: Short, dull red sparks that are thick and break close to the wheel. They often have a dotted or broken line.
- **Mild steel** (most car bodies): Long, straight sparks that are yellow-orange and end in a small fork. They travel a good distance.
- **High-carbon steel** (like springs, axles): Bright yellow sparks that burst into many small forks at the end, like a star.
- **Stainless steel**: Very short, dark red sparks with almost no forks. They barely travel.
- **Aluminum**: No sparks at all. It just melts and gums up the wheel. Never grind aluminum on a wheel you use for steelâit clogs.
- **Copper, brass**: No sparks.

In Africa, you'll often find mixed scrap from old cars, trucks, and machinery. A magnet will pull out the ferrous, but the spark test helps you sort steel into grades. For example, a car body is mild steel, but a leaf spring is high-carbon steel, which sells for a higher price. Separate them. Also, use the spark test to check if a piece is actually steel or just coated with something. A magnet might stick to a zinc-coated bolt, but the spark test will show it's steel underneath.

Practice on known samples: get a piece of cast iron (old brake drum), mild steel (car door), high-carbon steel (old file or spring), and stainless steel (kitchen sink or car trim). Grind each and memorize the spark pattern. Keep a small grinder at your sorting table. In busy yards, you can also use a file: cast iron files easily and produces black dust; mild steel files with medium resistance; high-carbon steel resists the file. But spark test is faster once you know it. Always wear eye protectionâsparks can cause fires in dry grass, so work in a clear area.

3. Sorting copper, brass, and aluminum for higher scrap prices

Copper, brass, and aluminum are the money-makers in a scrapyard. But not all copper is the same. Scrapyards and exporters pay different prices for different grades. If you mix them, you get the lowest price. Sorting properly can double your income from these metals.

**Copper**: The highest price goes to bright, bare copper wire (no insulation, no paint). Next is #1 copperâclean, unalloyed copper tubing or pipe, no solder, no fittings. Then #2 copperâcopper with solder, paint, or slight oxidation. Lowest is copper with heavy contamination or insulation. In Africa, common sources: car wiring, plumbing pipes, radiators, and electric motors. To get top price, strip wire with a wire stripper or a knife. Burn insulation? Don'tâburning copper creates toxic fumes and many buyers reject burnt wire. Instead, strip it manually or use a wire stripping machine. For radiators, separate the copper tubes from the brass tanks. Use a magnet to check for steel ends.

**Brass**: Brass is an alloy of copper and zinc. It's yellow and non-magnetic. Common sources: car radiators (brass tanks), faucets, door handles, and ammunition casings. Brass prices are lower than copper but still good. Sort brass by type: yellow brass (most common), red brass (higher copper content, more valuable), and plumbing brass (may contain lead). Remove any steel screws or plastic parts. A magnet helps here.

**Aluminum**: Light and silvery. Common sources: car rims, engine blocks, transmissions, and window frames. Grades: clean aluminum (no paint, no steel bolts) gets the best price; painted aluminum (like rims) is lower; aluminum with steel attachments (like engine blocks with steel bolts) is lowest. Use a magnet to find steel bolts and remove them. For engine blocks, remove the steel crankshaft and pistons if possibleâthat's a big chunk of steel that will downgrade your aluminum. Also, separate cast aluminum (engine parts) from sheet aluminum (body panels). Cast aluminum is thicker and often worth more.

In your yard, set up separate bins: copper #1, copper #2, brass, clean aluminum, painted aluminum, and aluminum with steel. Weigh each bin and keep records. When you sell, call different buyersâprices vary. In many African cities, exporters pay more than local foundries. But don't mix grades to make weight; you'll lose money. Clean copper wire can sell for 5 to 8 USD per kg, while mixed copper might get 2 USD. The extra work pays off.

4. Recognizing precious metals in catalytic converters and electronics

Catalytic converters and old electronics contain precious metals like platinum, palladium, rhodium, gold, and silver. These are worth far more than copper or aluminum. But you need to know how to recognize them and handle them safely. In Africa, many scrapyards overlook these items or sell them cheaply to middlemen. With a little knowledge, you can make serious money.

**Catalytic converters**: These are found in the exhaust system of petrol and diesel cars, usually near the engine. They look like a small metal canister with a honeycomb ceramic inside. The honeycomb is coated with platinum, palladium, and rhodium. Prices vary by car model. A used catalytic converter from a small car might sell for $50 to $200 USD; from a truck or luxury car, up to $500 or more. To identify: look for a bulge in the exhaust pipe with heat shields and sensors (oxygen sensors). Cut it off with a hacksaw or angle grinder. Do not break the ceramic inside unless you know how to extract the metalsâmost yards sell whole converters to specialized buyers. Weigh it and check online for prices based on the serial number or car model. Store them in a dry, secure place.

**Electronics**: Old computers, phones, and TVs contain gold, silver, and copper. The most valuable parts are the circuit boards (motherboards, graphics cards) and connectors. Gold is used in tiny amounts on contacts and pins. You can recognize gold by its yellow color and non-magnetic property. But beware: not all that glitters is goldâsome contacts are brass or gold-plated. A magnet won't stick to gold. For silver, look for older switches and relays. In Africa, e-waste is common. Collect circuit boards separately. Do not burn them to recover metalsâit releases toxic fumes and is illegal in many countries. Instead, sell boards to e-waste recyclers who use safe methods. Prices: a kilogram of high-grade circuit boards can fetch $5 to $20 USD, depending on gold content.

Safety first: catalytic converters contain ceramic fibers that can irritate lungs. Wear a dust mask when cutting. Electronics may contain lead and mercury. Wash hands after handling. Store precious metal items in a locked container. Keep records of where each came from. Build relationships with buyers who specialize in catalytic converters and e-waste. In many African countries, there are formal buyers in cities like Lagos, Nairobi, and Johannesburg. Don't sell to just anyoneâget a quote from at least two buyers.

5. Separating different grades of steel and iron for sale

Steel and iron are the backbone of any scrapyard, but not all steel is equal. Mills and foundries pay different prices for different grades. If you mix them, you get the lowest price. Learning to separate grades can increase your revenue by 20-30%. In Africa, common steel grades from cars include: mild steel (car bodies), high-carbon steel (springs, axles), cast iron (engine blocks, brake drums), and stainless steel (trim, exhaust).

**Mild steel**: This is your basic steel. It's magnetic, rusts easily, and is not hardened. Car bodies, frames, and panels are mild steel. It sells for the lowest price per kg, but it's the most common. Keep it cleanâremove plastic, rubber, and non-ferrous attachments. A magnet sticks strongly.

**High-carbon steel**: This is harder and stronger. It's used in springs, axles, drive shafts, and some tools. It's magnetic. To identify, use a spark test: bright yellow sparks with many forks. Or try to file itâit resists more than mild steel. High-carbon steel sells for a higher price because it can be recycled into new springs or tools. Separate it from mild steel. Common mistake: throwing leaf springs into the car body pile. Don't.

**Cast iron**: This is brittle and breaks with a loud snap. It's used in engine blocks, brake drums, and exhaust manifolds. It's magnetic. Cast iron is not steelâit has more carbon. It sells for a different price, often lower than steel but sometimes higher for clean engine blocks. To identify: hit it with a hammerâit cracks. Or look at the broken surface: it's grainy, not smooth. Keep cast iron separate. Remove any steel bolts or brass fittings.

**Stainless steel**: This is shiny, resists rust, and is weakly magnetic (some types are non-magnetic). It's used in car trim, exhaust systems, and kitchen equipment. Stainless steel sells for much more than mild steelâoften 3 to 5 times more. Use a magnet: if it sticks weakly or not at all, and it's shiny, it's likely stainless. But beware: some stainless is magnetic. A spark test gives short, dark red sparks. Keep stainless in a separate bin. Common sources: old pots, sinks, and car exhaust tips.

In your yard, set up bins for: mild steel, high-carbon steel, cast iron, and stainless steel. Use a magnet and spark test to sort. Weigh each bin separately. When selling, ask buyers for their grade specifications. Some buyers want shredded steel, others want heavy melting scrap. Clean your steelâremove dirt, concrete, and plastic. Contaminated steel gets rejected or downgraded. Also, keep your steel dry to prevent rust, which adds weight but lowers quality. A simple roof or tarp helps.

6. Storing sorted metals to prevent contamination and theft

You've sorted your metalsânow you must store them properly. Poor storage leads to contamination, theft, and lost money. In Africa, scrapyards face unique challenges: rain, dust, pests, and security. A little planning goes a long way.

**Contamination**: Each metal type must be stored separately. Use labeled bins, drums, or designated areas on concrete or hard ground. Never store metals on bare dirtâmud and stones mix in and lower value. For non-ferrous metals like copper and aluminum, keep them under a roof or tarp to prevent oxidation and dust. For steel and iron, rust is not a big issue for weight, but dirt and oil can contaminate. If you store different grades together, you'll have to re-sort later, which wastes time. Use old paint buckets or oil drums for small pieces. For larger items, build simple wooden bins or use old shipping containers if available. Label everything clearly in the local language and English.

**Preventing theft**: Scrap metal is valuable, and theft is common. Non-ferrous metals like copper and brass are especially targeted. Store them in a locked container or a secure room. If you don't have a building, use heavy-duty chain and padlocks on bins. Keep the yard fenced and well-lit at night. In many African cities, hiring a night guard is affordable and worth it. Also, don't advertise what you have. Keep high-value metals out of sight. Weigh your metals regularly and keep recordsâif weights drop, you have a theft problem. Consider marking your metal with a unique paint or stamp to deter thieves and help police identify stolen goods.

**Organizing for sale**: Store metals in a way that makes loading easy. Keep a clear path for trucks. Stack steel in piles that can be lifted with a magnet crane or forklift. For non-ferrous, use sacks or drums that can be weighed and loaded quickly. Keep a small office or shed with your scales, records, and tools. A simple table for sorting and a grinder for spark tests. Also, keep a first aid kitâcuts and burns happen.

**Environmental and safety**: Store batteries and e-waste separately in sealed containers to prevent acid leaks. Never burn cables or electronics in the openâit's toxic and illegal. Keep a fire extinguisher or sand bucket near flammable materials. In dry season, sparks from grinding can start fires. Clear grass around your storage area. Finally, train your workers on storage rules. A clean, organized yard is a profitable yard. It shows buyers you are professional, and they may pay you more.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Metal Identification and Sorting

In the African scrap trade, the difference between a profitable load and a wasted trip often comes down to one skill: knowing exactly what you are buying, sorting, and selling. A mixed load of "scrap metal" might fetch a low bulk price, but the same load sorted into clean copper, aluminium, brass, and graded steel can multiply your revenue by two to five times. This Practical Reference lesson gives you the field-tested methods used by scrapyard operators from Lagos to Nairobi to Johannesburg to identify metals quickly, sort them correctly, and protect your margins.

Why Metal Identification Is a Business Skill

Metal prices are not set by weight alone. They are set by grade, purity, and contamination level. A kilogram of clean copper wire is worth far more than a kilogram of mixed cable with plastic insulation. A kilogram of aluminium engine block is worth more than a kilogram of aluminium foil mixed with steel screws. When you can identify and sort metals accurately, you negotiate from a position of knowledge. You stop selling "mixed scrap" and start selling specific commodities to specific buyers, including foundries, refineries, and export aggregators.

Across Africa, the informal scrap sector employs millions of people. The operators who consistently earn the best margins are those who treat sorting as a production line, not an afterthought. This lesson covers the core identification methods, the sorting categories that buyers actually pay premiums for, and the storage practices that prevent contamination and theft.

Ferrous vs Non-Ferrous: The First Split

Every load should be divided into two primary streams: ferrous (contains iron, magnetic) and non-ferrous (no iron, non-magnetic, higher value). Ferrous metals include steel and iron. Non-ferrous metals include aluminium, copper, brass, lead, zinc, and precious metals. The magnet test is the fastest first filter. If a magnet sticks strongly, the metal is ferrous. If it does not stick, it is non-ferrous. If it sticks weakly or only in spots, it may be stainless steel or a mixed assembly that needs further dismantling.

However, the magnet test alone is not enough. Some stainless steels are weakly magnetic or non-magnetic, yet they are ferrous alloys. Some non-ferrous metals are plated onto ferrous bases, such as chrome-plated steel or brass-plated zinc. For these, you need a second test: the spark test, which reveals the metal's behaviour under a grinding wheel.

Using a Magnet and Spark Test

Magnet test: Use a strong neodymium magnet on a string or a magnetic pickup tool. Test each piece in several spots. Steel and iron will pull the magnet firmly. Aluminium, copper, brass, and lead will not attract it. Stainless steel may show weak attraction depending on its nickel content. If a piece is painted or dirty, scrape a small area first.

Spark test: This is the field method for distinguishing steel grades and confirming ferrous content. Press the metal against a bench grinder or angle grinder in a safe, well-ventilated area away from dry grass, fuel, and people. Observe the spark stream:

  • Low-carbon steel: long, straight, yellow-orange sparks with few bursts.
  • High-carbon steel: shorter sparks with more bursts and a brighter white-yellow colour.
  • Cast iron: short, dull red sparks that stay close to the wheel.
  • Stainless steel: short, dark red sparks with few or no bursts.
  • Aluminium: no sparks; the metal may smear or gum the wheel.
  • Copper and brass: no sparks; they may glow but do not throw a spark stream.

Always wear eye protection, gloves, and a dust mask during spark testing. Never spark-test near fuel, solvents, or dry vegetation.

Always test outdoors on a non-flammable surface, keep a fire extinguisher within reach, and never test near fuel storage or vehicle fuel tanks. When dismantling catalytic converters or electronics, be aware that some components contain hazardous materials such as lead, mercury, and cadmium. Wear nitrile gloves, eye protection, and a P2/P3 respirator. Never burn cable insulation or electronic boards to recover copper; this releases toxic fumes and is illegal in many African countries. Store acids, batteries, and e-waste separately from general scrap.

Recognizing Precious Metals in Catalytic Converters and Electronics

Catalytic converters contain platinum, palladium, and rhodium in a ceramic honeycomb. These are among the highest-value scrap items per kilogram. Electronics contain gold, silver, palladium, and copper in circuit boards, connectors, and chips. The key business point is simple: do not sell these as mixed scrap. Separate them and sell to specialist buyers who assay and pay for precious metal content.

For catalytic converters, cut the outer shell carefully and remove the ceramic core. The core is the valuable part. Store cores in sealed containers to prevent dust loss. For electronics, remove batteries, capacitors, and toner cartridges before storing boards. Never burn boards or cables. Use a reputable e-waste buyer who provides a written assay or weight-based payment for precious metal content.

Storing Sorted Metals to Prevent Contamination and Theft

Sorting is only profitable if the sorted material stays clean and secure. Contamination happens when copper is stored with steel, when aluminium is mixed with brass, or when wet scrap is left in the open. Theft happens when high-value metals are visible, unsecured, or stored in the same area as low-value scrap.

Best practices for African scrapyard operations:

  • Use separate labelled bins, drums, or bays for each metal grade. Paint the labels clearly.
  • Keep non-ferrous metals indoors or under lock and key. Copper and brass are frequent theft targets.
  • Store catalytic converter cores and electronic boards in sealed, locked containers.
  • Keep scrap dry. Wet steel rusts and loses value. Wet aluminium can oxidise and contaminate.
  • Never store batteries, fuel tanks, or sealed containers with general scrap. Drain and remove fluids first.
  • Weigh and record each grade separately. Use a simple notebook or phone app to track daily intake and sales.
  • Install basic security: locked gates, lighting, and if possible, a camera or guard. Organise a neighbourhood watch with other yard operators.
  • Train every worker on the magnet and spark tests. A single mis-sorted load can cost you a full day's profit.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Magnet first: Ferrous sticks, non-ferrous does not. Test every piece in multiple spots.
  2. Spark test second: Use a grinder to confirm steel grade and separate cast iron from steel.
  3. Sort non-ferrous by colour and weight: Reddish = copper, yellow = brass, silver-light = aluminium.
  4. Never mix copper with brass or aluminium. Clean separation earns premium prices.
  5. Remove insulation, plastic, and rubber before selling copper wire. Bare bright copper pays the most.
  6. Separate catalytic converters and electronic boards. Sell them to specialist buyers, not mixed scrap dealers.
  7. Store each grade in a labelled, dry, secure container. Prevent contamination and theft.
  8. Record weights and grades daily. Know your numbers before you negotiate.
