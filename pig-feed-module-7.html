Module 7

🐷 Practical Swine Feed Formulation

Step-by-step formulation for each pig category, using Pearson's Square and simple ration balancing with ingredients a small African producer can actually buy. Covers building your own ingredient database, worked starter, grower, finisher and breeding rations you can copy, calculating your own mineral and vitamin premix inclusions, and choosing between rations on cost per kilogram of gain rather than cost per bag.

What you will be able to do after this module

  • Build an ingredient card recording price, composition and supplier for every local feedstuff
  • Balance a starter ration to a crude protein target using Pearson's Square
  • Formulate a finisher ration to a published phase specification using Pearson's Square
  • Formulate a gestation ration using a two-ingredient basal mix inside Pearson's Square
  • Calculate limestone and dicalcium phosphate inclusions from calcium and phosphorus targets
  • Compare two rations on feed cost per kilogram of gain rather than cost per kilogram of feed

Lesson 7.1~12 min

Swine Feed Ingredient Database

In this lesson
  • Build an ingredient card recording price, composition and supplier for every local feedstuff
  • Calculate cost per kilogram of protein and cost per megacalorie of energy for ranking ingredients
  • Distinguish book values from measured values and know when each is safe to use

You cannot formulate a ration without knowing what is in your ingredients and what they cost today. Almost every failed farm-mixed feed traces back to one of two things: using a composition figure from a book that does not match the sack in the store, or using a price from three months ago. The fix is a small, disciplined ingredient database that you own and update.

Make one card per ingredient. On each card record: the ingredient name, the supplier and their phone number, the price per kilogram in USD and the date of that price, the pack size, approximate crude protein, approximate metabolisable energy in kcal per kg, approximate lysine, calcium and phosphorus, and a note on any limit that applies. Keep the cards in one envelope or one page of a notebook, and rewrite the price every time you buy.

Where do the composition numbers come from? Published swine nutrient standards give you the animal's requirements in detail, but they do not publish a table of African ingredient compositions, so any composition figure you use is either a book value or a measured value. Book values are averages from tables and are fine for making a first draft of a ration. Measured values come from a laboratory analysis of your own batch and are what you need before you commit a whole season's mixing to an unfamiliar ingredient. As a working rule: use book values for maize, soybean meal, wheat bran and limestone, which are relatively consistent, and pay for analysis on oilseed cakes, by-products, insect meals and anything from a new supplier, where processing varies enormously.

As approximate book starting values, commonly used across the region and requiring local verification: maize around 9 percent crude protein, 3,350 kcal ME per kg and 0.24 percent lysine. Cassava meal around 2 to 3 percent protein and similar energy to maize. Wheat bran around 15 percent protein but high fibre and low energy. Rice bran variable and prone to rancidity. Soybean meal around 44 to 46 percent protein and 2.8 percent lysine. Full-fat roasted soya around 36 percent protein with high energy. Sunflower cake around 28 to 32 percent protein but lower lysine. Cottonseed cake around 35 to 40 percent protein with gossypol limits. Fishmeal 50 percent and up with excellent amino acids. Limestone around 38 percent calcium. Dicalcium phosphate around 24 percent calcium and 18 percent phosphorus. Treat every one of these as a starting point, not a fact.

The two calculations that make the database useful are cost per unit of nutrient.

  1. Cost per kilogram of protein equals price per kg divided by protein as a decimal. Soybean meal at 0.60 USD and 45 percent gives 0.60 divided by 0.45 = 1.33 USD per kg of protein. Sunflower cake at 0.35 and 30 percent gives 1.17. Fishmeal at 1.20 and 55 percent gives 2.18.
  2. Cost per megacalorie of energy equals price per kg divided by ME in Mcal per kg. Maize at 0.35 USD and 3.35 Mcal gives 0.104 USD per Mcal. Cassava at 0.22 and an approximate 3.2 Mcal gives 0.069.

Those two columns, updated monthly, tell you instantly which direction to push a ration when prices move. But remember the caution from Module 6: cheapest protein is not the same as cheapest lysine, and a ration built on the cheapest protein source per kilogram of protein can still fail the pig.

Add a third column: maximum inclusion. Write the limit on the card so you never have to remember it. Crude fibre ceilings of 2 to 4 percent for weaners, 4 to 6 percent for growers and finishers, 6 to 8 percent for gestating sows and 3 to 4 percent for lactating sows constrain how much bran or cake any ration can hold. Fishmeal and plasma sit at 3 to 5 percent in starter diets. Mycotoxin limits belong on the card too: aflatoxin 20 ppb for breeding animals and young pigs, 200 ppb for finishers over about 45 kg; deoxynivalenol 5 ppm with intake falling from 1 to 3 ppm; zearalenone 1 ppm or below; fumonisin 10 ppm.

Worked example. A producer in Arusha, Tanzania, keeps eleven ingredient cards. In January maize is 0.35 USD per kg and cassava chips 0.30, so cassava costs 0.30 divided by 3.2 = 0.094 USD per Mcal against maize at 0.104, a marginal advantage not worth the reformulation. By June, after the maize harvest has been sold on and prices have risen to 0.46, maize costs 0.46 divided by 3.35 = 0.137 USD per Mcal while cassava has held at 0.30, or 0.094. Now cassava is 31 percent cheaper per unit of energy, and the substitution arithmetic from Module 6 is worth doing. Without the cards she would only have noticed that feed had become expensive.

Lesson 7.2~12 min

Piglet and Starter Feed Formulation

In this lesson
  • Balance a starter ration to a crude protein target using Pearson's Square
  • Check and correct the lysine content of a completed starter ration
  • Specify the lactose, specialty protein and physical form requirements of a starter diet

Starter feed is the most expensive feed on the farm per kilogram and the smallest by tonnage, which is exactly why it is worth formulating properly. A weaner that stalls costs you weeks of finisher time later.

The published targets for nursery phase 1, pigs of 5 to 7.5 kg, are SID lysine 1.30 to 1.40 percent and energy 3,300 to 3,400 kcal ME per kg. Phase 2, 7.5 to 11.5 kg, drops to 1.20 to 1.30 percent lysine at about 3,300 kcal. Phase 3, 11.5 to 23 kg, drops again to 1.08 to 1.15 percent at about 3,200 kcal. Calcium is 0.85 percent, total phosphorus 0.70 percent, STTD phosphorus 0.45 to 0.63 percent, iron 80 to 100 mg per kg, vitamin A 2,000 to 2,200 IU per kg and vitamin E 30 to 44 IU per kg. Crude fibre must stay at 2 to 4 percent.

Specialty ingredients matter here more than anywhere. Lactose is included at 5 to 10 percent in creep and early nursery diets and tapered as the pig's own maltase and isomaltase develop. Dried whey at 5 to 8 percent supplies both lactose and protein. Spray-dried porcine plasma at 3 to 5 percent supplies over 85 percent digestible protein plus immunoglobulins, at a cost premium of 0.80 to 1.20 USD per kg. Fishmeal at 3 to 5 percent gives 88 to 92 percent protein digestibility and excellent palatability. Soy protein concentrate at 3 to 8 percent reduces allergenicity against plain soybean meal.

Now the formulation. Use Pearson's Square to hit a crude protein target, then check lysine and fix it.

Step 1. Choose a crude protein target of 22 percent for phase 2. Draw a square. Put 22 in the middle, maize at 9 percent top left, soybean meal at 45 percent bottom left.

Step 2. Subtract diagonally, always taking the smaller from the larger. 45 minus 22 = 23 parts of maize. 22 minus 9 = 13 parts of soybean meal. Total parts = 23 + 13 = 36.

Step 3. Convert to percentages. Maize = 23 divided by 36 = 63.9 percent. Soybean meal = 13 divided by 36 = 36.1 percent. Check: 63.9 x 9 percent = 5.75 and 36.1 x 45 percent = 16.25, totalling 22.0 percent. The square works.

Step 4. Make room for the starter extras. Reserve 10 percent for dried whey, 4 percent for fishmeal, 3 percent for oil and 3 percent for minerals, premix, salt and lysine, which is 20 percent. Scale the maize and soybean meal into the remaining 80 percent: maize = 0.80 x 63.9 = 51.1 percent; soybean meal = 0.80 x 36.1 = 28.9 percent.

Step 5. Check lysine, using approximate lysine values of 0.24 percent for maize, 2.8 percent for soybean meal, 4.5 percent for fishmeal and 0.9 percent for dried whey.

  • Maize: 51.1 x 0.0024 = 0.123 kg
  • Soybean meal: 28.9 x 0.028 = 0.809 kg
  • Fishmeal: 4 x 0.045 = 0.180 kg
  • Whey: 10 x 0.009 = 0.090 kg
  • Total: 1.202 kg per 100 kg, so 1.20 percent total lysine.

Step 6. Convert to digestible. Total lysine overstates SID lysine, and on a diet of this type SID sits around 90 percent of total, so about 1.08 percent SID. Your target is 1.20 to 1.30 percent for phase 2, so you are short by roughly 0.15 percentage points.

Step 7. Correct with L-lysine hydrochloride, which supplies about 78 percent lysine. 0.15 divided by 0.78 = 0.19 kg per 100 kg, or 1.9 kg per tonne. Take that 0.19 kg out of the maize.

Finally, physical form. Pellets of 3 to 4 mm are preferred for creep and early nursery because palatability and intake are best and waste is lowest, with a durability index above 80 percent and fines below 20 percent at the feeder. Keep pelleting temperature below 170 degrees Fahrenheit to avoid scorching ingredients, and include 3 to 4 percent fat to protect nutrients through the die and improve palatability. If you cannot pellet, meal is acceptable but expect lower intake.

Worked example. A producer in Eldoret, Kenya, prices this ration per 100 kg: maize 50.9 kg at 0.35 = 17.82; soybean meal 28.9 at 0.60 = 17.34; whey 10 at 1.10 = 11.00; fishmeal 4 at 1.20 = 4.80; oil 3 at 1.30 = 3.90; minerals, premix and salt 3 at 0.90 = 2.70; L-lysine 0.19 at 3.00 = 0.57. Total 58.13 USD per 100 kg, or 0.58 USD per kg. That sits inside the published nursery diet range and well below creep feed at 0.50 to 0.80 USD per kg, and it is worth every cent for the four weeks she feeds it.

Lesson 7.3~12 min

Growing and Finishing Feed Formulation

In this lesson
  • Formulate a finisher ration to a published phase specification using Pearson's Square
  • Verify a ration against the protein to energy ratio for the correct weight band
  • Adjust fat sources in the final weeks to control carcass fat quality

Growing and finishing feed is where the tonnage is, and therefore where most of the money is. A one cent per kilogram improvement here is worth more than a ten cent improvement in starter. It is also the easiest place to formulate well, because the ingredients are simple and the targets are published in a clear ladder.

The published six-phase finisher framework runs like this. Phase 1, 27 to 40 kg: 1.05 percent SID lysine at 3,350 kcal ME per kg, protein to energy ratio 3.1. Phase 2, 40 to 60 kg: 0.95 percent at 3,300, ratio 2.9. Phase 3, 60 to 80 kg: 0.85 percent at 3,250, ratio 2.6. Phase 4, 80 to 100 kg: 0.75 percent at 3,200, ratio 2.3. Phase 5, 100 to 120 kg: 0.70 percent at 3,150, ratio 2.2. Phase 6, 120 to 130 kg: 0.65 percent at 3,100, ratio 2.1. Each phase runs two to three weeks except the last. Calcium and phosphorus also fall with weight: 0.65 percent calcium and 0.32 percent STTD phosphorus at 27 to 60 kg, 0.55 and 0.28 at 60 to 100 kg, and 0.46 and 0.26 at 100 to 130 kg.

Most small producers cannot run six diets. Two or three is realistic. If you run two, split at about 60 kg and use the phase 2 and phase 4 specifications. If you run three, split at 60 and 100 kg.

Formulate phase 3, the 60 to 80 kg diet, as a worked example.

Step 1. Set a crude protein target of 16 percent, which is a workable level for that lysine specification with maize and soybean meal.

Step 2. Pearson's Square. Maize 9, soybean meal 45, target 16. Diagonals: 45 minus 16 = 29 parts maize; 16 minus 9 = 7 parts soybean meal; total 36 parts.

Step 3. Percentages. Maize = 29 divided by 36 = 80.6 percent. Soybean meal = 7 divided by 36 = 19.4 percent. Check: 80.6 x 0.09 = 7.25 and 19.4 x 0.45 = 8.73, totalling 15.98, near enough 16 percent.

Step 4. Reserve 4 percent for limestone, dicalcium phosphate, salt, premix and synthetic lysine. Scale into 96 percent: maize = 0.96 x 80.6 = 77.4 percent; soybean meal = 0.96 x 19.4 = 18.6 percent.

Step 5. Check lysine at 0.24 percent for maize and 2.8 percent for soybean meal.

  • Maize: 77.4 x 0.0024 = 0.186 kg
  • Soybean meal: 18.6 x 0.028 = 0.521 kg
  • Total: 0.707 kg per 100 kg, or 0.71 percent total lysine, roughly 0.64 percent SID.

Step 6. Correct the shortfall. Target 0.85 percent SID minus 0.64 achieved = 0.21 percentage points. L-lysine HCl at 78 percent lysine: 0.21 divided by 0.78 = 0.27 kg per 100 kg, or 2.7 kg per tonne. Take it out of the maize.

Step 7. Verify the protein to energy ratio. Divide SID lysine percentage by energy in Mcal per kg: 0.85 divided by 3.25 = 2.62 g of lysine per Mcal, which matches the published 2.6 for this phase. The optimal band across finishing is 3.0 to 3.5 g per Mcal for high lean growth genetics in earlier phases, falling through the ladder above. If your calculated ratio is far from the published figure, one of your two numbers is wrong.

Two further levers. Adding fat raises energy density and improves feed conversion by roughly 0.04 points per one percent of fat added, which is worth checking against the price of oil, as Module 8 does. And in the final four to six weeks, limit unsaturated fat sources. High inclusions of distillers grains above about 15 percent, or high-linoleic oils, produce soft pork fat and poor bacon quality. Shift to saturated sources such as tallow or palm oil, or simply reduce the unsaturated source, in the last phase.

Worked example. A producer in Chipata, Zambia, prices this phase 3 ration per 100 kg: maize 77.1 kg at 0.35 = 26.99; soybean meal 18.6 at 0.60 = 11.16; limestone 1.0 at 0.10 = 0.10; dicalcium phosphate 1.2 at 0.90 = 1.08; salt 0.4 at 0.25 = 0.10; premix 0.4 at 3.00 = 1.20; L-lysine 0.27 at 3.00 = 0.81. Total 41.44 USD per 100 kg, or 0.414 USD per kg. At a feed conversion ratio of 3.2 over that phase, his feed cost per kilogram of gain is 0.414 x 3.2 = 1.32 USD.

Breeding Stock Feed Formulation

In this lesson
  • Formulate a gestation ration using a two-ingredient basal mix inside Pearson's Square
  • Set separate specifications for gestation, lactation and boar diets
  • Balance fibre, energy and lysine for a restricted-fed breeding animal

Breeding diets break the simple two-ingredient Pearson's Square because a gestating sow needs something a maize and soybean meal mix cannot give her: bulk. Her published crude fibre allowance is 6 to 8 percent, against 4 to 6 for finishers, and the reason is satiety in a restricted-fed animal. So you need a third ingredient, and the way to handle that in Pearson's Square is to build a basal mix first and treat it as one ingredient.

Recall the targets from Module 5. Gestation: 12 to 13 percent crude protein, 0.50 to 0.60 percent SID lysine, 0.85 to 0.95 percent calcium, 0.30 to 0.35 percent STTD phosphorus, 6 to 8 percent crude fibre, fed at about 2.0 kg a day to days 90 and 2.9 kg thereafter. Lactation: 14 to 16 percent crude protein, about 60 g of SID lysine a day, 0.38 to 0.42 percent STTD phosphorus with at least 22 g a day, 3 to 4 percent crude fibre, fed ad libitum to about 6.4 kg a day. Boar: 13 to 14 percent crude protein, 0.62 percent SID lysine, 0.80 percent calcium, 0.40 percent available phosphorus, fed at about 2.5 kg a day.

Formulate the gestation diet.

Step 1. Build the basal. Choose 70 parts maize and 20 parts wheat bran, 90 parts in total. Its crude protein is (70 x 9 + 20 x 15) divided by 90 = (630 + 300) divided by 90 = 10.33 percent. Because bran is high in fibre, this basal also carries the bulk the sow needs.

Step 2. Pearson's Square with two ingredients: the basal at 10.33 percent and soybean meal at 45 percent, target 13 percent. Diagonals: 45 minus 13 = 32 parts basal; 13 minus 10.33 = 2.67 parts soybean meal; total 34.67 parts.

Step 3. Percentages. Basal = 32 divided by 34.67 = 92.3 percent. Soybean meal = 2.67 divided by 34.67 = 7.7 percent. Check: 92.3 x 0.1033 = 9.53 and 7.7 x 0.45 = 3.47, totalling 13.0 percent.

Step 4. Break the basal back into its parts. Maize is 70 of 90, so 0.923 x 70 divided by 90 = 71.8 percent of the diet. Bran is 20 of 90, so 0.923 x 20 divided by 90 = 20.5 percent. Add soybean meal 7.7 percent and the three sum to 100.

Step 5. Reserve 4 percent for limestone, dicalcium phosphate, salt, premix and lysine. Scale: maize 68.9 percent, bran 19.7 percent, soybean meal 7.4 percent.

Step 6. Check lysine using 0.24 percent for maize, an approximate 0.55 percent for wheat bran, and 2.8 percent for soybean meal.

  • Maize: 68.9 x 0.0024 = 0.165
  • Bran: 19.7 x 0.0055 = 0.108
  • Soybean meal: 7.4 x 0.028 = 0.207
  • Total: 0.480 kg per 100 kg, or 0.48 percent total lysine, roughly 0.42 percent SID.

Step 7. Correct to the middle of the 0.50 to 0.60 percent band. Take 0.53 minus 0.42 = 0.11 percentage points. L-lysine HCl at 78 percent: 0.11 divided by 0.78 = 0.14 kg per 100 kg, or 1.4 kg per tonne.

Step 8. Sanity-check daily delivery. At 2.0 kg a day and 0.53 percent SID lysine she receives 0.0053 x 2,000 = 10.6 g, sitting at the top of the published 8 to 10 g band for days 0 to 90. In late gestation at 2.9 kg she receives 15.4 g, above the 12 to 14 g quoted, which is the intended safety margin during mammary development.

For the lactation diet, run the same method but with a target of 16 percent crude protein, no bran or very little because fibre must stay at 3 to 4 percent, and added fat to lift energy toward 3,362 kcal ME per kg. Check lysine by division rather than by percentage: divide 60 g by your measured daily intake, exactly as Module 5 Lesson 4 sets out. For the boar diet, target 13.5 percent crude protein and 0.62 percent SID lysine, and remember his amino acid pattern needs methionine plus cystine at about 70 percent of lysine and threonine at about 74 percent, so a maize and single-cake diet will need attention on sulphur amino acids.

Worked example. A producer in Mbarara, Uganda, prices the gestation ration per 100 kg: maize 68.8 at 0.35 = 24.08; wheat bran 19.7 at 0.20 = 3.94; soybean meal 7.4 at 0.60 = 4.44; limestone 1.5 at 0.10 = 0.15; dicalcium phosphate 1.2 at 0.90 = 1.08; salt 0.4 at 0.25 = 0.10; breeding premix 0.9 at 3.20 = 2.88; L-lysine 0.14 at 3.00 = 0.42. Total 37.09 USD per 100 kg, or 0.371 USD per kg. At 250 kg per gestation that is 92.75 USD of feed per sow per pregnancy.

Lesson 7.5~12 min

Vitamin and Mineral Premix for Pigs

In this lesson
  • Calculate limestone and dicalcium phosphate inclusions from calcium and phosphorus targets
  • Work out the concentration a premix must carry at a given inclusion rate
  • Value phytase against the dicalcium phosphate it replaces

The last four percent of a ration decides whether the other ninety-six percent works. Minerals and vitamins are cheap in absolute terms and catastrophic when wrong, and they are the part of farm mixing most often guessed at.

Set the targets first. For a gestation diet: total calcium 0.90 percent, total phosphorus around 0.55 percent, STTD phosphorus 0.32 percent, calcium to digestible phosphorus ratio between 2.0 and 2.3 to 1, salt around 0.4 percent. Vitamins for breeding stock: A 2,000 to 4,000 IU per kg, D3 800 IU, E 44 IU, K 0.50 mg. Trace minerals for breeding stock: iron 80 mg per kg, zinc 100 to 125 ppm, copper 15 ppm, manganese 25 mg, selenium 0.20 to 0.30 mg, iodine 0.14 to 0.20 mg. Choline 1.00 to 1.25 g per kg. B vitamins at thiamine 1.0, riboflavin 3.0, niacin 10, pantothenic acid 10, pyridoxine 1.5, cobalamin 0.01, folic acid 0.3 and biotin 0.05 mg per kg.

Now the phosphorus arithmetic, which is where most producers go wrong. Take the gestation ration from Lesson 4: about 689 kg maize, 197 kg wheat bran and 74 kg soybean meal per tonne. Using approximate phosphorus values of 0.28 percent for maize, 1.0 percent for bran and 0.65 percent for soybean meal:

  • Maize: 689 x 0.0028 = 1.93 kg
  • Bran: 197 x 0.010 = 1.97 kg
  • Soybean meal: 74 x 0.0065 = 0.48 kg
  • Total plant phosphorus: 4.38 kg per tonne, or 0.44 percent.

That looks close to the 0.55 percent target. But roughly 70 to 80 percent of phosphorus in plant feeds is bound as phytate and unavailable. Take 25 percent as digestible and you have 4.38 x 0.25 = 1.10 kg of usable phosphorus, against a STTD target of 0.32 percent, which is 3.2 kg per tonne. You are short 2.1 kg of available phosphorus.

Dicalcium phosphate supplies about 18 percent phosphorus in a highly available form and about 24 percent calcium. So: 2.1 divided by 0.18 = 11.7 kg of dicalcium phosphate per tonne. That also delivers 11.7 x 0.24 = 2.81 kg of calcium.

Now calcium. The target of 0.90 percent means 9 kg per tonne. Plant ingredients supply very little, call it 0.5 kg. Dicalcium phosphate has given 2.81 kg. The gap is 9 minus 0.5 minus 2.81 = 5.69 kg, which must come from limestone at about 38 percent calcium: 5.69 divided by 0.38 = 15.0 kg of limestone per tonne.

Check the ratio: calcium 9 kg against STTD phosphorus 3.2 kg is 2.8 to 1, slightly above the 2.0 to 2.3 target, so trim limestone toward 12 kg and recheck. This is normal. Formulation is iteration, not a single pass.

Salt at 0.4 percent is 4 kg per tonne. That leaves the vitamin and trace mineral premix, typically included at 2.5 kg per tonne. The premix must therefore be concentrated enough that 2.5 kg delivers the whole requirement. Work backwards for any nutrient:

  1. Vitamin E at 44 IU per kg of diet means 44 x 1,000 = 44,000 IU per tonne.
  2. Divide by the premix inclusion: 44,000 divided by 2.5 = 17,600 IU per kg of premix.
  3. Do the same for selenium: 0.25 mg per kg of diet is 250 mg per tonne, divided by 2.5 kg gives 100 mg per kg of premix.

That is how you check whether a premix on offer is actually suitable for breeding stock or is a growing-pig product being sold to you at a breeding-stock price. Ask for the specification sheet and do the division.

Phytase changes the economics. Microbial phytase at 500 to 2,500 FTU per kg releases an additional 0.10 to 0.20 percent available phosphorus. Take 0.15 percent, which is 1.5 kg per tonne, and it directly replaces 1.5 divided by 0.18 = 8.3 kg of dicalcium phosphate. At 0.90 USD per kg that is 8.3 x 0.90 = 7.47 USD saved per tonne. If phytase costs you 2 to 3 USD per tonne at the recommended dose, the net saving is around 4.50 to 5.50 USD per tonne, plus less phosphorus in the manure.

Worked example. A producer in Blantyre, Malawi, mixes 30 tonnes a year. Adding phytase at a net 5.00 USD per tonne saving gives 150 USD a year, and letting her cut dicalcium phosphate from 11.7 to 3.4 kg per tonne also frees up cash tied in an expensive imported input. She checks her premix specification sheet and finds it lists 8,000 IU of vitamin E per kilogram; at 2.5 kg per tonne that delivers 8,000 x 2.5 divided by 1,000 = 20 IU per kg of diet, well short of the 44 IU her sows need. She either raises the inclusion to 5.5 kg per tonne or changes premix.

Lesson 7.6~12 min

Least-Cost Formulation for Swine

In this lesson
  • Compare two rations on feed cost per kilogram of gain rather than cost per kilogram of feed
  • Apply nutritional and practical constraints before minimising cost
  • Run a simple substitution test to decide whether an ingredient change is worth making

Least-cost formulation means finding the cheapest mix that still meets every requirement. The second half of that sentence is the part producers forget. A cheaper bag of feed that produces worse pigs is not a saving, and the only way to know which you have is to compare on cost per kilogram of gain.

The governing calculation is simple. Feed cost per kilogram of gain equals feed cost per kilogram multiplied by feed conversion ratio.

Worked example. A producer in Kaduna, Nigeria, is offered two options for her finisher pigs.

  • Ration A, her current mix: 0.42 USD per kg, and her records show a feed conversion ratio of 3.2.
  • Ration B, a cheaper mix using more maize bran and less soybean meal: 0.38 USD per kg, but on a trial pen it converts at 3.6.

Ration A: 0.42 x 3.2 = 1.344 USD per kg of gain. Ration B: 0.38 x 3.6 = 1.368 USD per kg of gain.

Ration B is 9.5 percent cheaper per kilogram of feed and 1.8 percent more expensive per kilogram of pork. On 200 pigs each gaining 75 kg, that is 200 x 75 x 0.024 = 360 USD a year lost by buying the cheaper feed. It also takes longer, so pens turn over more slowly, which costs again.

So the method is: constrain first, then minimise.

Write your constraints down before you touch the price list. For a finisher ration they are: SID lysine at the published level for the weight band, from 1.05 percent at 27 to 40 kg down to 0.65 percent at 120 to 130 kg; methionine plus cystine at 56 to 58 percent of lysine, threonine 62 to 64 percent, tryptophan 18 to 19 percent; energy 3,100 to 3,350 kcal ME per kg by phase; calcium and STTD phosphorus by weight band, from 0.65 and 0.32 percent down to 0.46 and 0.26; crude fibre 4 to 6 percent maximum; and the protein to energy ratio in the published band for the phase.

Add practical constraints the software will never know: maximum inclusion of any ingredient you have not tested, mycotoxin limits, availability for the whole batch rather than one week, and whether you can physically mix it evenly by hand.

The substitution test is the everyday tool. Whenever a price changes, ask one question: does swapping ingredient X for ingredient Y save more than it costs in nutrients I must replace? Work it in four steps.

  1. Calculate the saving from the swap in USD per 100 kg.
  2. Calculate the nutrients lost, principally protein, lysine and energy.
  3. Cost the ingredients needed to put those nutrients back.
  4. Net the two. If the answer is less than about 1 USD per 100 kg, do not bother, because the risk and effort are not worth it.

Module 6 Lesson 2 works a full example of this for maize and cassava. The same method applies to every pair.

One more discipline: mix accuracy. A perfectly formulated ration mixed badly is a badly formulated ration. Weigh, do not scoop, for anything included below 5 percent. Premix, salt, limestone and synthetic lysine must be pre-blended with a few kilograms of maize before going into the main batch, or they will not distribute. On a hand-mixed floor batch, shovel the pile over at least three times, and always in the same pattern.

And keep a trial discipline. Never change more than one thing at a time, always run a control pen on the old ration, always weigh at the start and end, and always record all feed delivered including spillage. Two pens of ten pigs over four weeks will tell you more about your own farm than any table.

Second worked example. The same producer considers adding one percent oil to her finisher ration. Published guidance says one percent added fat improves feed conversion by about 0.04 points. Her ration is 0.42 USD per kg and her pigs convert at 3.2, so improving to 3.16 saves 0.04 kg of feed per kilogram of gain, worth 0.04 x 0.42 = 0.0168 USD per kg of gain. Across 200 pigs gaining 75 kg each, that is 200 x 75 x 0.0168 = 252 USD saved. The cost: one percent of her total feed. Those pigs eat 200 x 75 x 3.2 = 48,000 kg, so one percent is 480 kg of oil. At 1.30 USD per kg that is 624 USD. She would spend 624 to save 252. The answer is no, at that oil price. If oil fell to 0.50 USD per kg the cost would be 240 USD and the answer would flip. Write the arithmetic down and the decision makes itself.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. Soybean meal costs $0.60 per kg at 45% protein. What is the cost per kilogram of protein?

0.60 divided by 0.45 gives 1.33 USD per kilogram of protein.

2. When is it worth paying for a laboratory analysis rather than using book values?

Consistent commodities such as maize and limestone are safe on book values, but processing variation in cakes and by-products makes measured values essential.

3. Maize at $0.46 per kg and 3.35 Mcal ME per kg costs how much per megacalorie?

0.46 divided by 3.35 gives 0.137 USD per megacalorie, which is what makes cassava at 0.094 attractive at that price.

4. What third column should every ingredient card carry alongside price and composition?

Fibre ceilings, specialty protein inclusion rates and mycotoxin limits constrain formulation, and writing them on the card means they get checked at purchase.

5. Why is the cheapest protein per kilogram not always the right choice?

Requirements are set as SID lysine. A cake that is cheap per kilogram of crude protein may still leave the ration lysine deficient.

6. Using Pearson's Square with maize at 9% CP and soybean meal at 45% CP for a 22% target, how many parts of maize?

45 minus 22 gives 23 parts of maize, and 22 minus 9 gives 13 parts of soybean meal, for 36 parts in total.

7. 23 parts maize out of 36 total parts is what percentage of the mix?

23 divided by 36 gives 63.9 percent maize, with soybean meal at 13 divided by 36, or 36.1 percent.

8. A starter ration is 0.15 percentage points short of its SID lysine target. How much L-lysine HCl at 78% lysine is needed per tonne?

0.15 divided by 0.78 gives 0.19 kg per 100 kg, which is 1.9 kg per tonne.

9. Why is lactose included at 5-10% in early nursery diets and then reduced?

Young pigs digest lactose readily and it supports Lactobacillus, but as small intestinal enzymes mature other carbohydrates become usable and the cost premium is no longer justified.

10. What fat inclusion is recommended in a pelleted starter and why?

Three to four percent fat protects nutrients through the pelleting process and improves palatability, both of which raise intake in the critical first weeks.

11. Using Pearson's Square with maize at 9% and soybean meal at 45% for a 16% target, what percentage is maize?

45 minus 16 gives 29 parts maize and 16 minus 9 gives 7 parts soybean meal, so maize is 29 divided by 36, or 80.6 percent.

12. A 60-80 kg diet has 0.85% SID lysine and 3.25 Mcal ME per kg. What is its protein to energy ratio?

0.85 divided by 3.25 gives 2.62 g of lysine per megacalorie, matching the published 2.6 for that phase.

13. What is the published SID lysine target for a 120-130 kg finishing pig?

The final phase drops to 0.65 percent SID lysine on about 3,100 kcal ME per kg, the lowest point of the ladder.

14. Why should unsaturated fat sources be limited in the final four to six weeks?

High unsaturated fat inclusion raises iodine value, producing soft fat and poor bacon quality, so producers shift to saturated sources late in finishing.

15. A ration costs $0.414 per kg and the pigs convert at 3.2. What is the feed cost per kilogram of gain?

0.414 multiplied by 3.2 gives 1.32 USD of feed for every kilogram of liveweight gain.

16. A basal of 70 parts maize at 9% CP and 20 parts bran at 15% CP has what crude protein?

(70 x 9 + 20 x 15) divided by 90 gives 930 divided by 90, or 10.33 percent.

17. Why can a plain maize and soybean meal mix not serve as a gestation diet?

Gestating sows are fed restricted amounts and need bulk. A third, fibrous ingredient such as bran is required to reach the published fibre allowance.

18. With basal at 10.33% and soybean meal at 45%, what percentage of a 13% CP diet is soybean meal?

The diagonals give 2.67 parts soybean meal and 32 parts basal, so soybean meal is 2.67 divided by 34.67, or 7.7 percent.

19. A gestation diet at 0.53% SID lysine fed at 2.0 kg per day delivers how much lysine?

0.0053 multiplied by 2,000 g gives 10.6 g of digestible lysine per day.

20. How should lactation diet lysine be checked?

The published 0.77 to 1.34 percent band is simply 60 g divided by intake, so the correct percentage depends on what your sows actually eat.

21. A ration is short 2.1 kg of available phosphorus per tonne. How much dicalcium phosphate at 18% P is needed?

2.1 divided by 0.18 gives 11.7 kg of dicalcium phosphate per tonne.

22. A diet needs 44 IU of vitamin E per kg and the premix goes in at 2.5 kg per tonne. What concentration must the premix carry?

44,000 IU are needed per tonne, and dividing by the 2.5 kg premix inclusion gives 17,600 IU per kilogram of premix.

23. Why does plant phosphorus that meets the total phosphorus target still leave a deficit?

Only about a quarter of plant phosphorus is digestible without enzyme help, so the STTD figure, not the total, is what must be met.

24. Phytase releasing 0.15% available phosphorus replaces how much dicalcium phosphate per tonne?

1.5 kg of phosphorus divided by the 0.18 phosphorus content of DCP gives 8.3 kg replaced.

25. Calcium at 9 kg and STTD phosphorus at 3.2 kg per tonne gives what ratio, and what should be done?

9 divided by 3.2 is 2.8 to 1, above the 2.0 to 2.3 target, so limestone comes down and the calculation is run again. Formulation is iterative.

26. Ration A costs $0.42/kg at FCR 3.2 and Ration B costs $0.38/kg at FCR 3.6. Which is cheaper per kilogram of gain?

Multiplying price by feed conversion gives 1.344 for A and 1.368 for B, so the cheaper bag produces the more expensive pork.

27. What is the correct order of operations in least-cost formulation?

Least cost means the cheapest mix that still meets every requirement. Constraints come first or the result is simply a cheap ration that fails.

28. 200 pigs each gain 75 kg at FCR 3.2. How much feed is that?

200 multiplied by 75 gives 15,000 kg of gain, and multiplying by 3.2 gives 48,000 kg of feed.

29. Why must ingredients included below 5 percent be weighed and pre-blended?

Small inclusions such as premix, salt and synthetic lysine cannot mix evenly if added directly to a large batch, so they are pre-blended with a few kilograms of maize.

30. What is the correct trial discipline for testing a new ration?

Only a single change against a control pen with recorded weights and feed will tell you what actually caused a difference on your own farm.

Module 7 capstone

Formulate, mix and test your own complete ration set. Step 1: build an ingredient card for every feedstuff available within a day's travel of your farm, recording supplier, price per kilogram in USD, approximate crude protein, approximate energy, lysine where known, and the date of the price. Step 2: using Pearson's Square, formulate one starter, one grower, one finisher and one gestation ration to the nutrient targets given in this module, showing every step of your arithmetic on paper. Step 3: check each ration for lysine, calcium, phosphorus and crude fibre against the published targets and adjust with synthetic lysine, limestone, dicalcium phosphate and premix until each specification is met. Step 4: cost each ration per 100 kg and per tonne at today's prices. Step 5: mix one 100 kg batch of your grower ration by hand or by mixer, weighing every ingredient, and feed it to one pen for four weeks with start and end weights recorded. Step 6: calculate your feed cost per kilogram of gain for that batch, compare it with the ration you were using before, and write a one-paragraph decision on whether to adopt it, revise it, or abandon it.
