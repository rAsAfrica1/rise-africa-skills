1. Feeding the Dry Sow

A dry sow is a sow that is not pregnant and not lactating. She still needs good care to get back into condition for the next breeding. Feed her a maintenance diet of about 2 kg per day of a good sow ration. This ration should have 14% protein and be high in fibre, like maize bran or wheat bran. If she is too thin, increase to 2.5 kg; if too fat, reduce to 1.5 kg. Always provide plenty of clean waterat least 10 litres per day. In hot African climates, sows may eat less, so feed early morning and late evening when it is cooler. Group-housed dry sows need space to avoid bullying; give each sow at least 2 square metres. If you keep them in individual stalls, make sure they can lie down and stand up comfortably. Watch for signs of heat (oestrus) every daya dry sow that is not cycling may need a boar nearby or a change in feed. Flushing (increasing feed to 3 kg per day) for 10-14 days before mating can increase ovulation rate. But do not overfeed, as fat sows have difficult births. Keep a record of each sow's feed and body condition. In Africa, many farmers use local feeds like sweet potato vines, cassava leaves, and kitchen waste, but these must be balanced with a commercial or home-mixed concentrate. A simple home mix: 50% maize, 20% bran, 20% protein concentrate (like soy or fish meal), 10% minerals and salt. Always store feed in a dry place to prevent mould. A dry sow that is well managed will give you many healthy litters.

2. Feeding the Pregnant Sow

Once a sow is confirmed pregnant, her feeding changes. For the first 80 days of pregnancy, feed her about 2 kg per day of a 14% protein sow ration. Do not overfeed, because too much energy can cause the embryos to die. From day 80 to farrowing (about 114 days), increase to 2.5-3 kg per day. This is when the piglets grow fast and the sow needs extra nutrients. Use a ration with 16% protein and added calcium and phosphorus for strong bones. Provide 15-20 litres of water daily. In the last week before farrowing, reduce feed slightly to 2 kg and add bran to prevent constipation. A pregnant sow should have a body condition score of 3 out of 5not too thin, not too fat. Check her udder: it should start to develop and may leak milk a few days before birth. Keep her in a clean, quiet pen away from other sows. In Africa, many farmers use group housing for pregnant sows, but individual pens are better to prevent fighting and ensure each sow gets her share. If you use local feeds, make sure they are not mouldymouldy feed can cause abortion. Add a mineral supplement if your soil is deficient in selenium or zinc. Weigh feed with a simple scale or use a tin can as a measure (a standard 2-litre tin holds about 1.5 kg of maize). Record feed intake daily. A sow that is too thin will have small piglets; a sow that is too fat will have difficulty farrowing. Adjust feed based on her condition. Always consult a vet if you see signs of illness. Good feeding during pregnancy means strong, healthy piglets and a sow that recovers quickly after birth.

3. Feeding the Lactating Sow

A lactating sow is feeding piglets, so she needs a lot of energy and protein. Feed her free-choice, meaning she can eat as much as she wants, but start slowly after farrowing. On day 1 after birth, give only 1 kg of feed. Increase by 0.5 kg each day until she is eating 2 kg plus 0.5 kg for each piglet she is nursing. For example, a sow with 10 piglets should eat 2 + (10 x 0.5) = 7 kg per day. Use a high-energy, 18% protein lactation ration. Provide unlimited clean watershe may drink 20-30 litres daily. If she does not eat enough, she will lose body condition and produce less milk. In Africa, you can supplement with local feeds like cooked sweet potato, pumpkin, or milk from cattle, but always include a balanced concentrate. Feed her 2-3 times a day, and remove any leftover feed to prevent spoilage. Keep the farrowing pen cooluse shade, ventilation, or wet the floor. A hot sow will eat less. Watch her udder for mastitis (hard, hot, painful). If she has mastitis, she may refuse to feed piglets. Consult a vet. Also, ensure piglets have access to creep feed from 2 weeks old to reduce demand on the sow. Weigh the sow weekly or use body condition scoring. If she is losing too much weight, increase feed. If she is too fat, reduce slightly. After weaning, reduce feed to 2 kg per day to help her dry off. Good lactation feeding means heavy piglets and a sow that returns to heat quickly. Remember, a sow that is well fed will produce 20-30 piglets per year. Poor feeding means fewer, smaller piglets and longer time to rebreed.

4. Body Condition Scoring

Body condition scoring (BCS) is a simple way to check if your sow is too thin, too fat, or just right. Use a scale of 1 to 5, where 1 is very thin and 5 is very fat. A score of 3 is ideal. To score, look at the sow from the side and behind. Feel her backbone, ribs, and hip bones. If you can easily feel the bones with light pressure, she is thin (score 2). If you cannot feel them even with firm pressure, she is fat (score 4 or 5). If you can feel them with firm pressure but not see them, she is ideal (score 3). Also check the tailhead and pin bones. In Africa, where feed can be scarce, many sows are too thin. A thin sow will have weak piglets and poor milk. A fat sow will have difficulty giving birth and may crush piglets. Score your sows every month. Keep a record for each sow. If a sow scores 2, increase her feed by 0.5-1 kg per day. If she scores 4, reduce feed by 0.5 kg and increase exercise. For pregnant sows, aim for score 3 at mating, then maintain until day 80, then allow slight increase. For lactating sows, they will naturally lose condition; aim to have them at score 3 when weaned. Use a simple chart with pictures to help you. You can draw the scores on a paper or use a notebook. In group housing, score each sow individually because dominant sows may eat more. If you cannot feel the bones, use a measuring tape to check girth. A sow with a girth of more than 1.5 metres may be too fat. Body condition scoring is free and takes only a few minutes. It helps you adjust feeding and prevent problems. Train your workers to do it. Good BCS means healthy sows and more profit.

5. Culling Old Sows

Culling means removing a sow from the breeding herd. You cull old sows when they are no longer productive. Most sows stay productive for 6-8 litters, or until they are 4-5 years old. Signs it is time to cull: she has fewer than 8 piglets per litter, she has difficulty farrowing, she has poor milk, she has lost body condition and does not recover, she has repeated health problems like mastitis or lameness, or she is aggressive and injures other sows. Also cull if she fails to conceive after two matings. In Africa, where feed is expensive, keeping a low-producing sow wastes money. A good sow should wean at least 20 piglets per year. If she weans fewer than 16, consider culling. To cull, first confirm she is not pregnant. Sell her to a butcher or another farmer. In some areas, you can sell cull sows for meatthey are often used for sausages or minced meat. Before selling, check local regulations and ensure she is healthy. Do not sell a sow with disease. If you cannot sell, you may slaughter for home consumption. After culling, replace her with a gilt (young female pig) that you have raised or bought. Keep records of each sow's performance: number of piglets born alive, weaned, and weaning weight. Use a notebook or a simple spreadsheet. This helps you decide which sows to keep. Culling is not failureit is good management. It keeps your herd productive and profitable. Plan to cull 20-25% of your sows each year. This means if you have 10 sows, replace 2-3 each year. Always have replacement gilts ready. In batch farrowing, culling must be done between batches to avoid disruption. A well-culled herd has healthy, productive sows that give you daily pig sales.

6. Managing the Boar

A boar is a male pig used for breeding. A good boar is calm, has strong legs, and comes from a healthy line. Keep one boar for every 10-15 sows. In Africa, many farmers cannot afford artificial insemination, so a boar is essential. Feed your boar a special boar ration with 14% protein. Give him 2-2.5 kg per day. If he is used for mating often, increase to 3 kg. Do not overfeed, as fat boars have poor libido and difficulty mating. Provide plenty of water and exercise. A boar needs spaceat least 6 square metres. If he is tethered, give him a chain and a comfortable pen. Boars can be aggressive, so handle them calmly and never turn your back. Use a pig board (a flat board) to move him safely. Check his feet and legs regularly; lame boars cannot mate. Trim his tusks if they grow too long. A boar can mate from 8 months old, but wait until he is 10-12 months for heavy use. Use him for at most 2 matings per week when young, and up to 4-5 per week when mature. Always bring the sow to the boar's pen for matingthis reduces fighting. Mating takes 5-10 minutes. If the boar is not interested, check for heat in the sow or health problems in the boar. In hot climates, boars may be less fertile; provide shade and cool water. Rotate boars every 2 years to prevent inbreeding. If you have only one boar, replace him after 2 years with a new one from a different farm. Keep records of each mating: date, sow, and boar. This helps you predict farrowing dates. A well-managed boar will give you many healthy litters. Remember, a boar is half your herd's genetics, so take good care of him.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Sow and Boar Feeding & Management Benchmarks for African Pig Enterprises

This practical reference consolidates the feeding and management decisions that determine whether a batch-farrowing pig unit in Africa makes money or loses it. In a batch system, every sow is either dry, pregnant, or lactating at a predictable time so feed, labour, and housing can be planned like a factory production line. The financial logic is simple: a sow that weans 1011 strong piglets per litter, twice a year, at low feed cost per piglet weaned, is profitable. A sow that weans 6 piglets after eating 1.2 tonnes of feed per year is a loss-making asset that should have been culled three parities ago.

Use the figures below as working benchmarks, not gospel. Adjust to your own feed prices, local maize and soya availability, and market weights (many African markets prefer 6080 kg live weight "porkers" rather than 100110 kg). Track your own numbers for at least three parities before making big culling decisions.

Feeding the Dry Sow

The dry period is short typically 57 days from weaning to service. The goal is to flush the sow so she ovulates more eggs, then get her served promptly. Feed a maintenance-plus ration of 2.02.5 kg/day of a sow diet containing 1314% crude protein (CP). In the 35 days before service, "flushing" with an extra 0.51.0 kg/day can raise ovulation rate, especially in thin sows (BCS 2). Do not over-flush fat sows they have more difficulty conceiving. Ensure continuous access to clean water and a mineral premix, particularly in areas where soil is selenium- or zinc-deficient (common in parts of East and Southern Africa).

Feeding the Pregnant Sow

Gestation is 114 days (range 112116). The critical rule: do not overfeed the pregnant sow. Overfeeding causes fat sows, difficult farrowing, low milk yield, and crushed piglets. Feed 2.22.8 kg/day of a 1415% CP gestation diet, split into two meals. The first 80 days is maintenance only; the last 30 days is when 60% of foetal growth occurs, so increase carefully to 2.83.0 kg/day in the final three weeks, especially for thin sows. Use a simple body condition score (see below) to fine-tune.

Feeding the Lactating Sow

Lactation is where money is made or lost. A sow suckling 10 piglets produces 68 litres of milk per day and needs 5.56.5 kg of a 1618% CP diet daily. The simple rule of thumb used across African extension services: 2 kg maintenance + 0.4 kg per suckling piglet. A sow with 10 piglets needs about 6 kg/day. Feed to appetite do not restrict. If the sow loses more than 10 kg during lactation, she will take longer to return to heat and will wean fewer piglets in the next litter. Provide unlimited clean water; a lactating sow drinks 2030 litres per day. In hot climates, feed in the cool of early morning and evening to boost intake.

SAFETY WARNING Aflatoxin and Mouldy Feed

Never feed sows maize or groundnut cake that is mouldy, musty, or discoloured. Aflatoxin B1 common in poorly stored African maize and groundnuts causes liver damage, abortions, stillbirths, and low milk yield. It also passes into pork, creating a human food safety risk. Store feed on pallets in a dry, ventilated room, use within 4 weeks, and never feed damp or heated feed. If you suspect aflatoxin, discard the batch; do not "dilute" it with good feed. Wear gloves and a dust mask when handling suspect feed.

Body Condition Scoring (BCS)

BCS is the single most useful management tool for a sow herd. Score each sow at weaning, at service, at day 60 of gestation, and at farrowing. Use a 15 scale by feeling the backbone, ribs, and hip bones. Target BCS 3 (ribs not seen but easily felt; hip bones rounded).

Culling Old Sows

Culling is a business decision, not an emotional one. Keep a sow only while she earns her keep. Cull when:

  • She has weaned fewer than 7 piglets in two consecutive litters.
  • She has failed to conceive after two services (repeat breeder).
  • She has had two consecutive difficult farrowings or a prolapse.
  • She is BCS 1 or BCS 5 at weaning and does not recover.
  • She has poor udders, mastitis history, or has crushed piglets more than once.
  • She has reached parity 78 (in most African herds, cull at parity 67).

Replace culled sows with home-bred or purchased gilts so that 2025% of the breeding herd is replaced each year. In batch systems, plan replacements to enter the batch at the same time as the rest of the group.

Managing the Boar

A working boar is expensive to keep and easy to ruin. Feed a 14% CP ration at 2.53.0 kg/day, but reduce to 2.02.5 kg/day for two weeks before heavy service to keep him active. Boars need exercise walk them daily. Keep them cool; heat stress above 30C reduces sperm quality for 46 weeks. Use one boar for no more than 2 services per week when young (under 12 months) and up to 45 per week when mature. Rotate boars every 1218 months to avoid inbreeding. In African conditions with limited AI access, a healthy boar is a critical asset treat him like the most valuable animal on the farm.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Weigh or measure feed daily do not estimate by eye.
  2. Score every sow at weaning, service, day 60, and farrowing; target BCS 3.
  3. Lactating sows: 2 kg + 0.4 kg per piglet per day, fed to appetite.
  4. Clean, cool water available 24/7 2030 litres/day for lactating sows.
  5. Store feed dry, on pallets, use within 4 weeks; reject any mouldy batch.
  6. Deworm sows before service and treat for mange and lice.
  7. Cull any sow that weans fewer than 7 piglets in two consecutive litters.
  8. Replace 2025% of the breeding herd each year with healthy gilts.
