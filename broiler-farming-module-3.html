1. Pre-Placement Preparation of Broiler House

Before your chicks arrive, you must prepare the brooder house thoroughly. Start by cleaning out all old litter, dust, and cobwebs. Wash the floor, walls, and ceiling with soap and water, then disinfect with a product like Virocid or formalin. Allow the house to dry completely. Next, spread fresh litter such as wood shavings, rice husks, or chopped straw to a depth of 5-8 cm. Avoid sawdust from treated wood. Level the litter and remove any clumps. For the brooder, set up a hover or brooder guard. A common method in Africa is using a charcoal stove or gas brooder, but electric brooders are also used. Place the brooder at one end of the house, with a guard made of cardboard or metal sheets about 40-50 cm high, creating a circle of 2-3 meters diameter for 250 chicks. Pre-heat the brooder 24 hours before chick arrival to reach a temperature of 32-35Â°C at chick level. Use a thermometer at chick height. Also, provide feed and water. Use clean, shallow chick feeders and drinkers. Fill drinkers with clean water and add glucose or vitamins for the first day. Place feed in feeders 2-3 hours before arrival. Ensure adequate ventilation without drafts. Check that all equipment is working. Finally, keep a record of all steps. This preparation reduces stress and disease, giving your chicks a strong start.

2. Brooding Temperature and Humidity Control

Temperature is critical for chick survival. For the first week, maintain 32-35Â°C at chick level. Reduce by 2-3Â°C each week until you reach 20-24Â°C at week 5. Use a thermometer placed at chick height, not on the wall. In Africa, many farmers use charcoal stoves or kerosene brooders. Adjust the stove vents to control heat. If using electric brooders, use a thermostat. Watch the chicks: if they huddle under the brooder, it's too cold; if they spread away, it's too hot; if they are evenly spread, it's just right. Humidity should be 60-70% for the first week. Low humidity causes dehydration and pasty vents. High humidity leads to wet litter and respiratory problems. To increase humidity, place shallow pans of water near the brooder. To decrease, improve ventilation. In dry areas like the Sahel, you may need to hang wet sacks or use a humidifier. In humid areas like the coast, use fans or open vents. Always avoid drafts. Check temperature at least three times a day: early morning, midday, and evening. Adjust as needed. Keep a record. Remember, chicks cannot regulate their own body temperature for the first two weeks, so you are their thermostat. A sudden drop can cause chilling and death. A sudden rise can cause dehydration. Be consistent.

3. Lighting Programs for Broilers

Lighting affects feeding, growth, and behavior. For broilers, use a continuous light program for the first 3 days: 23 hours of light and 1 hour of darkness. This helps chicks find feed and water. Use a bright light (30-40 lux) initially, then reduce to 10-20 lux by day 7. After day 7, switch to a program of 18 hours light and 6 hours dark, or 20 hours light and 4 hours dark. The dark period allows rest and reduces stress. In Africa, power outages are common, so have a backup like a solar lamp or generator. If using natural light, supplement with artificial light in the evening and morning. Use energy-saving bulbs or LED lights. Place lights evenly, about 2-2.5 meters above the floor. Avoid bright spots that cause crowding. For the last week before slaughter, you can increase light to 20 hours to boost feed intake. But too much light can cause leg problems and ascites. Always provide a period of darkness. Monitor chicks: if they are not eating, increase light intensity. If they are panting and crowded, reduce. Keep a lighting schedule and stick to it. Sudden changes can stress birds. Record any power failures and adjust. Good lighting leads to better feed conversion and uniformity.

4. Stocking Density and Space Allocation

Stocking density is the number of birds per square meter. For broilers, the recommended density is 10-12 birds per square meter in open-sided houses in Africa, but this depends on ventilation. In hot climates, use 8-10 birds per square meter to reduce heat stress. In controlled environment houses, you can go up to 15 birds per square meter, but this requires good ventilation and cooling. Overstocking causes poor growth, disease, and mortality. Understocking wastes space and money. Calculate the floor area: length x width. For example, a 10m x 10m house = 100 square meters. At 10 birds per square meter, you can keep 1000 birds. But also consider feeder and drinker space. Each bird needs 5-7 cm of feeder space and 2-3 cm of drinker space. For 1000 birds, that's 50-70 meters of feeder and 20-30 meters of drinker. Use linear feeders and drinkers, or round ones. Provide enough to avoid competition. Also, provide space for equipment like brooders and feed bags. As birds grow, they need more space. You may need to expand the brooder area or remove partitions. In Africa, many farmers use deep litter system. Ensure litter is dry and loose. If you see overcrowding, reduce numbers or expand space. Plan ahead: order chicks according to your house capacity. Never exceed recommended density. It's better to have fewer birds that grow well than many that die.

5. Early Chick Care and Placement Techniques

When chicks arrive, they are tired and thirsty. Handle them gently. First, count them and check for any dead or weak ones. Then, place them in the brooder. Dip each chick's beak into water to teach them to drink. You can add glucose or vitamins to the water for the first 6 hours. Use shallow drinkers with clean water. For the first 2 hours, provide only water, then add feed. Use chick starter feed with 22-24% protein. Place feed in shallow trays or on paper for the first 3 days to encourage eating. Sprinkle some feed on the paper. Make sure the brooder temperature is 32-35Â°C. Observe the chicks: if they are active and spread out, they are comfortable. If they huddle, increase heat. If they pant, reduce heat. Provide a brooder guard to keep them near heat and feed. The guard should be about 40 cm high and placed in a circle. After 3 days, you can start removing the guard gradually. Ensure there is enough light for them to see feed. For the first week, keep them in the brooder area. Then expand space as they grow. Always provide fresh, clean water. Change water at least twice a day. Clean feeders and drinkers daily. Keep records of mortality and feed intake. In Africa, many farmers lose chicks due to poor placement. Take your time and be gentle. A good start leads to a healthy flock.

6. Monitoring Chick Behavior and Health Indicators

Daily observation is key to successful brooding. Spend at least 15 minutes in the morning and evening watching your chicks. Look for signs of comfort: chicks should be evenly spread, active, and eating and drinking. If they are huddled under the brooder, they are cold. If they are spread away from the brooder and panting, they are hot. If they are crowded in corners, there may be a draft. If they are chirping loudly and continuously, they are distressed. Check droppings: normal droppings are firm and brownish with a white cap. Watery or bloody droppings may indicate disease. Pasty vent is a sign of dehydration or illness. Check for any lameness, sneezing, or coughing. Monitor feed and water consumption: a sudden drop can indicate a problem. Weigh a sample of chicks weekly to track growth. For broilers, target weights: week 1: 150-200g, week 2: 400-500g, week 3: 800-1000g, week 4: 1.4-1.6kg. If below, check feed quality and temperature. Also, check mortality: remove dead birds immediately and record. A mortality rate above 1% per week is a warning. In Africa, common issues include coccidiosis, Newcastle disease, and heat stress. Vaccinate according to schedule. Keep the house clean and litter dry. If you see abnormal behavior, act quickly. Consult a vet if needed. Good monitoring prevents losses.

3. Pre-Placement Preparation â The 72-Hour Countdown

Chicks arriving on a cold, damp or poorly ventilated floor will never recover full genetic potential. The preparation sequence is non-negotiable:

  • 72 hours before: wash, disinfect and dry the house. Use a footbath at every entrance. Whitewash walls if possible.
  • 48 hours before: spread 5â8 cm of clean, dry litter (wood shavings, rice husks or chopped maize cobs). Never use mouldy or chemically treated material.
  • 36 hours before: switch on brooders and pre-heat the house to 33â35Â°C at chick level. In off-grid farms, test the charcoal stove or kerosene brooder and confirm fuel supply for 48 hours.
  • 24 hours before: place drinkers and feeders inside the brooding ring. Fill drinkers with clean water plus glucose and electrolytes for the first 6 hours after arrival.
  • 6 hours before: verify temperature with a thermometer placed at chick height (not hanging at human height). Check for draughts using a candle or smoke pencil.
  • On arrival: count chicks, record mortality in transit, and place them gently near â not under â the heat source so they can self-regulate.
â  SAFETY WARNING â CARBON MONOXIDE AND FIRE RISK

Charcoal, kerosene and gas brooders produce carbon monoxide (CO), an odourless gas that kills chicks silently and can kill farm workers. Never operate a fuel-based brooder in a fully sealed house. Provide at least 0.5 mÂ² of adjustable ventilation per 100 chicks and never block all vents at night, even in cold highland areas. Keep a CO alarm in the house if using gas. Keep a fire extinguisher or sand bucket within 5 m of any open-flame brooder. Store fuel outside the poultry house. In many African markets, electric brooders are safer but vulnerable to power cuts â always have a backup heat source and a plan to move chicks to a smaller, well-insulated room if power fails for more than 2 hours.

4. Temperature, Humidity and Chick Behaviour â Reading the Flock

Thermometers tell you the air temperature. Chick behaviour tells you the effective temperature at bird level. Train every worker to read the flock before touching any dial:

  • Chicks spread evenly, active, chirping softly: conditions are correct.
  • Chicks huddled under the heat source, loud distress chirping: too cold. Raise brooder temperature or reduce draughts.
  • Chicks pressed against the ring walls, panting, wings spread: too hot. Lower heat or increase ventilation.
  • Chicks clustered in corners or along one wall: draught. Find and seal the gap.
  • Chicks wet and chilled, litter damp: drinkers too close to heat or too high humidity. Move drinkers and increase ventilation.

In African conditions, the biggest brooding risk is not steady cold but temperature swing â 34Â°C at 2 pm and 18Â°C at 4 am in highland zones, or 38Â°C daytime heat in the Sahel. Use curtains, roof insulation (thatch or reflective foil) and night-time brooder reinforcement to flatten the curve. Record temperature at 6 am, 1 pm and 9 pm daily in a simple notebook or mobile app; this data is your early warning system.

5. Lighting, Stocking Density and Early Chick Care

Lighting programmes in the tropics must balance feed intake with heat stress. A common practical schedule:

  • Days 1â3: 23 hours light, 30â40 lux. Chicks must find feed and water.
  • Days 4â7: 20 hours light, 20â30 lux.
  • Days 8â14: 18 hours light, 10â15 lux. Begin dark periods to reduce metabolic heat and stress.
  • Days 15 onward: 18â20 hours light depending on target weight and heat stress risk.

Stocking density should be adjusted for climate: in hot, humid coastal zones, reduce density by 10â15% compared to temperate recommendations. For a 1,000-bird brooding ring, a 3â4 m diameter ring is adequate for the first 7 days; expand to full house by day 10â14. Always provide at least 2 drinkers and 2 feeders per 100 chicks in the first week to ensure access and reduce competition.

Early chick care includes: dipping beaks in water on arrival (especially after long transport), providing glucose-electrolyte water for the first 6 hours, using flat tray feeders for days 1â5, and checking crops at 24 hours â at least 95% of chicks should have full, soft crops. If not, investigate water access, temperature or feed quality immediately.
