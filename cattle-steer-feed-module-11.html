Module 11

🐄 Health and Disease Prevention Through Nutrition

The metabolic and nutritional diseases that cost African cattle producers the most money, and the cheap feeding decisions that prevent them.

What you will be able to do after this module

  • Identify the pasture and animal conditions that create hypomagnesaemia risk
  • Explain why over-feeding calcium before calving causes rather than prevents milk fever
  • Explain negative energy balance and how it produces ketosis in early lactation
  • Link low effective fibre and rapid concentrate increases to abomasal displacement risk
  • Explain how selenium, vitamin E, zinc and vitamin A support udder defence

Lesson 11.1~12 min

Grass Tetany Prevention

In this lesson
  • Identify the pasture and animal conditions that create hypomagnesaemia risk
  • Calculate the daily magnesium supplement needed for a lactating cow at risk
  • Set up a magnesium programme that starts before the risk period rather than during it

Grass tetany, or hypomagnesaemia, kills cattle quickly and almost always kills the best ones: mature lactating cows on the most attractive pasture on the farm. It is a nutritional emergency created entirely by a mismatch between magnesium demand and magnesium availability, and it is preventable for a few cents a head per day.

The mechanism is not simply low magnesium in the grass. It is low magnesium combined with high potassium, high nitrogen, high phosphorus and high calcium in rapidly growing forage, all of which interfere with magnesium absorption across the rumen wall. That is why the disease appears on lush, well-fertilised, fast-growing pasture rather than on poor dry grazing. Cattle also have almost no readily mobilisable magnesium reserve, unlike calcium; the supply must arrive in the diet every single day.

Demand rises sharply with lactation. A cow in late pregnancy needs roughly 7 to 9 grams of magnesium a day. The same cow in lactation needs roughly 18 to 21 grams a day, because magnesium leaves in the milk. Expressed as a diet percentage, requirements are 0.10 to 0.12 percent of dry matter for growing cattle, 0.12 to 0.15 percent for gestating cattle, and 0.20 to 0.25 percent for lactating cattle.

Blood levels tell the story. Normal serum magnesium is above 2.0 mg/dL. From 1.5 to 2.0 mg/dL the animal is at tetany risk. Below 1.5 mg/dL she is in clinical tetany, showing muscle tremors, hyperexcitability, staggering, convulsions and death if untreated. Treatment is intravenous calcium and magnesium gluconate and requires a veterinarian; by the time you are treating, you have already lost the economic argument.

Read the pasture, not just the cow. The published forage risk indicators are a potassium to calcium-plus-magnesium ratio above 2.2, a potassium to magnesium ratio above 10 to 1, or forage magnesium below 0.15 percent of dry matter. Any of these on a forage report during a lush growth period means act now.

Worked example. A herd of 30 lactating cows is going onto fresh, heavily grown pasture after the first rains. Target supplementation is 0.6 oz of magnesium oxide per head per day, which is roughly 17 grams of magnesium oxide, delivering magnesium in the range needed to lift the diet toward 0.20 to 0.25 percent. For 30 cows that is 30 x 17 = 510 grams a day, about 0.51 kg. Over a 60-day risk period that is 30.6 kg of magnesium oxide. If magnesium oxide costs in the region of 1.20 USD per kg locally, the whole herd's protection for the season costs about 37 USD, or 1.22 USD per cow. One dead cow worth 400 to 600 USD pays for that programme roughly a dozen times over.

The practical difficulty is that magnesium oxide is bitter and cattle will not eat it free-choice in sufficient quantity. The published options are a high-magnesium free-choice mineral at 8 to 10 percent magnesium, or mixing magnesium oxide into a carrier the cattle want, typically molasses, maize bran or a small daily concentrate allowance. Free-choice alone is unreliable, and unreliable is the same as absent when the disease is acute.

Timing is everything. Start supplementation 3 to 4 weeks before the risk period begins, not on the day the first cow goes down. The risk window is the flush of lush growth, which in temperate systems is March to May and in African systems is the first weeks after the rains break on well-fertilised or manured pasture. Frozen or very cold conditions also raise risk by reducing intake, which is less relevant in most of Africa but matters at altitude.

Other published prevention strategies: delay grazing until the sward is at least 6 inches high, so cattle are eating slightly more mature and less watery grass; reduce nitrogen fertiliser in the risk seasons, since high N drives the potassium and nitrogen interference; and integrate legumes into pastures, since legumes carry more magnesium than grasses.

Concrete African context. Dairy farms on the manured, intensively grazed plots of the Kenyan and Rwandan highlands are a textbook tetany setting: heavy manure application raises soil potassium, the pasture grows explosively after the rains, and the highest-yielding cows go out first. Farmers there who lose a cow typically attribute it to poisoning or snakebite. The pattern to look for is a lactating cow, on new lush grass, found dead or convulsing within days of a pasture change. If that has happened on your farm even once, put a magnesium programme in place before the next rains.

Summary rules:

  1. Supplement magnesium at 0.20 to 0.25 percent of diet dry matter for lactating cows during risk periods.
  2. Start 3 to 4 weeks early. During is too late.
  3. Do not trust free-choice alone; carry the magnesium in something cattle want to eat.
  4. Manage the pasture too: graze later, fertilise less with nitrogen, add legumes.

Lesson 11.2~12 min

Milk Fever and Hypocalcaemia Management

In this lesson
  • Explain why over-feeding calcium before calving causes rather than prevents milk fever
  • Apply DCAD principles to a pre-calving ration using locally available anionic salts
  • Distinguish clinical from subclinical hypocalcaemia and quantify the cost of each

Milk fever, properly parturient paresis, is an acute collapse in blood calcium at calving. The cow's demand for calcium jumps almost instantly as she starts producing colostrum, and if her body is not primed to mobilise calcium from bone and absorb it from the gut, blood calcium crashes. Highest risk is 24 to 72 hours after calving.

The counter-intuitive core of prevention is this: feeding a lot of calcium before calving makes milk fever more likely, not less. When dietary calcium is plentiful before calving, the cow absorbs what she needs passively and her parathyroid hormone response goes quiet. At calving, when she suddenly needs to mobilise bone calcium fast, that machinery is switched off and takes days to restart. A cow that has been mildly calcium-challenged before calving has an active PTH system ready to respond.

Blood calcium defines the three states. Normal is above 8 mg/dL. Subclinical hypocalcaemia is 7 to 8 mg/dL, where the cow looks fine but has impaired immune function, and that is where most of the money is lost: more retained placentas, more metritis, more mastitis, poorer intake and slower return to cycling. Clinical milk fever is below 7 mg/dL, giving recumbency, seizures and death risk. Treatment is intravenous calcium gluconate or chloride, a veterinary job.

Risk factors are over-feeding calcium pre-calving, high dietary anions without cation balance, breed (Jersey at higher risk than Holstein), and age, with older cows at higher risk than first-calvers.

The main prevention tool is DCAD, dietary cation-anion difference. The goal is to make the pre-calving diet mildly acidifying, which triggers a controlled mild metabolic acidosis, which raises PTH activity, which increases blood calcium availability at the moment of calving. The published target for pre-calving diets is a negative DCAD of -5 to -10 mEq per 100 g of dry matter, which is the same as -50 to -100 mEq per kg. It is achieved by increasing anions, chiefly sulfur and chloride, relative to cations, chiefly potassium and sodium.

The practical constraint on African farms is that anionic salts are unpalatable and DCAD management requires knowing the mineral content of your forage, particularly potassium. High-potassium forage, which is exactly what heavily manured smallholder plots produce, works directly against you. If you cannot test forage or source anionic salts, the two remaining levers still matter a great deal: avoid feeding high-calcium and high-potassium forages such as lucerne to dry cows, and ensure adequate magnesium, because magnesium is required for the PTH response to work at all. The published periparturient magnesium level is 0.40 to 0.50 percent of diet dry matter.

Calcium levels themselves: post-calving, the lactating requirement is 0.30 to 0.45 percent of diet dry matter for dairy cattle, and gestating dairy cattle sit at 0.15 to 0.30 percent. Note that some published guidance quotes much higher pre-calving calcium figures alongside DCAD systems; treat any single pre-calving calcium figure with caution and prioritise the principle, which is do not let a dry cow eat a calcium-rich diet without a deliberate DCAD strategy behind it.

Worked example on cost. A 40-cow dairy herd with a 6 percent clinical milk fever rate loses about 2.4 cases a year. Direct treatment is perhaps 25 to 40 USD a case in veterinary time and drugs. But subclinical hypocalcaemia typically affects several times as many cows as clinical cases do, and each subclinical cow tends to produce less, breed back later and get sick more often. If subclinical cases cost even 60 USD each in lost milk and delayed conception, and 10 cows in a 40-cow herd are affected, that is 600 USD a year, invisible, with no dead cow to explain it. Against that, a deliberate dry-cow feeding programme costs a few dollars per cow.

Concrete African context. Smallholder dairy farms across the Kenyan and Ugandan highlands commonly feed dry cows whatever forage is spare, often the high-potassium Napier from the plot nearest the boma that receives all the manure. The single highest-value change, requiring no purchase, is to separate the dry cows and feed them the poorer, lower-potassium forage from the far end of the farm, saving the good forage for milking cows where it earns money. That one management move improves both groups.

Also remember the periparturient window generally: from 5 weeks before calving to 3 weeks after, increase vitamin E and selenium supplementation, ensure adequate vitamin D, and use a DCAD formulation pre-calving where you can. This window is the most concentrated source of both risk and opportunity in the whole dairy year.

Lesson 11.3~12 min

Ketosis and Energy Balance

In this lesson
  • Explain negative energy balance and how it produces ketosis in early lactation
  • Use body condition score to manage energy reserves across the dry period
  • Design a three-week transition step-up onto the milking diet

Ketosis is what happens when a cow's energy output exceeds her energy intake and she starts dismantling her own body fat faster than her liver can process it. Glycogen depletes, fat mobilises, ketone bodies accumulate, and she goes into a metabolic acidosis. In dairy cows this peaks in the first one to three weeks after calving. In beef cows in late pregnancy the equivalent problem is pregnancy toxaemia, driven by the fetus taking energy faster than a poorly fed cow can supply it.

The numbers that define it: normal blood glucose is 40 to 60 mg/dL, and ketosis risk begins below 40 mg/dL with elevated blood ketones, specifically beta-hydroxybutyrate above 1.0 mmol/L. Subclinical ketosis has elevated ketones without visible signs, and it still reduces fertility and production. Clinical signs are reduced appetite that can be subtle, a drop in milk yield, weight loss despite apparent intake, and rarely neurological signs such as incoordination.

The trap is that the causes are set weeks earlier. The dominant one is body condition at calving. A cow that comes into calving too fat has a poor appetite immediately after calving, mobilises fat aggressively, and is the classic ketosis case. The published target is a body condition score of 3.0 to 3.5 on a 5-point scale at calving, and gain in late pregnancy should be limited, with over-feeding above roughly 1 lb per day of gain in late pregnancy identified as a risk.

Body condition can be converted into energy, which makes it a real management number rather than a subjective one. One BCS unit is roughly 9.4 percent of body weight in energy storage, with an energy value of 6.3 Mcal of retained energy per kg, and it requires 5.6 Mcal NEL per kg to put on. For a 500 kg cow, one BCS unit is about 47 kg of body tissue, which is about 296 Mcal of retained energy, and would take roughly 263 Mcal NEL to replace through feeding. That is a very large number, and it explains why the cheap time to put condition on a cow is during late lactation on grass, and the expensive time is after calving on purchased feed.

Prevention has five components:

  1. Pre-calving condition. Hit BCS 3.0 to 3.5, and avoid rapid late-pregnancy gain.
  2. Transition diet. Use a 3-week step-up onto the milking diet with high-quality forage and a gradual concentrate increase, rather than a switch on calving day.
  3. Early lactation energy. Feed a high energy density diet for the first 2 to 4 weeks, focused on NEL. This is where good forage is worth most.
  4. Intake management. Monitor dry matter intake closely, because anything reducing intake, including illness, lameness, dirty water or an uncomfortable lying area, raises ketosis risk. Fresh water and a clean feed bunk are part of the ketosis programme.
  5. Niacin supplementation at 15 to 30 g per day pre-calving may improve periparturient energy balance; this is emerging research rather than settled practice.

Worked example. A 500 kg cow calves at BCS 4.0 rather than 3.25, so 0.75 BCS units over target, roughly 35 kg of extra body fat. She calves, her intake is depressed, and she mobilises. Suppose she develops subclinical ketosis, drops 3 litres a day for 30 days, and conceives 25 days later than she should. At 0.40 USD per litre, the milk alone is 3 x 30 x 0.40 = 36 USD. A day of extra open time on a smallholder dairy is commonly valued in the range of 2 to 4 USD; at 3 USD, 25 days is 75 USD. Total roughly 111 USD from one cow, caused by feeding her too well in the dry period. Feeding less, in that window, would have made money.

Treatment after diagnosis is to increase energy density and supply propylene glycol as a gluconeogenic precursor; severe cases need veterinary intervention with intravenous dextrose.

Concrete African context. Smallholders often do the opposite of the right thing at both ends: they feed the dry cow generously because she is 'resting and building the calf', then cut back after calving because feed is scarce. That is precisely backwards. The dry cow needs adequate but restrained feeding to hold BCS 3.0 to 3.5; the fresh cow needs the best feed on the farm. If your conserved silage is limited, the fresh cows get it. Nobody else.

A simple monitoring habit: body condition score every cow at drying off, at calving, and at 30 days in milk, and write the three numbers on her card. A cow dropping more than about one full unit between calving and 30 days is telling you she is in serious negative energy balance.

Lesson 11.4~12 min

Displaced Abomasum and Abomasal Ulcers

In this lesson
  • Link low effective fibre and rapid concentrate increases to abomasal displacement risk
  • Set a minimum forage inclusion that protects rumen fill and abomasal position
  • Cost the decision between a surgical case and a feeding change

The abomasum is the true stomach, sitting under the rumen. When the rumen is full of coarse forage it presses down and holds the abomasum in place. When the rumen is empty or full of finely ground concentrate, the abomasum can fill with gas, float upward and become displaced, usually to the left. The cow goes off feed, drops milk sharply, and the standard resolution is surgery. On most African smallholder farms surgery is not available, so a displaced abomasum is effectively a cull decision.

Note on evidence: the specific epidemiology of displaced abomasum is not detailed in the reference standards used for this course. What is well established in those standards, and what the prevention advice below rests on, is the effect of low dietary fibre, rapid concentrate increases and negative energy balance on rumen function. Treat the mechanism described here as the widely accepted explanation and the fibre and transition numbers as the verified part.

The risk profile is consistent across sources: fresh cows in the first few weeks after calving, on diets low in effective fibre, that have had rapid concentrate increases, and that are in negative energy balance. Every one of those is the same list that produces ketosis, which is why the two conditions travel together. A cow with ketosis has depressed intake, an empty rumen, and therefore a displaced abomasum waiting to happen.

Prevention is therefore not a separate programme. It is the same fibre and transition discipline that prevents acidosis and ketosis, applied strictly to fresh cows.

  1. Hold the forage floor. Forage NDF must not fall below 15 to 16 percent of total diet dry matter, and working targets in early lactation are 19 to 25 percent forage NDF with total diet NDF of 25 to 38 percent. Many practical guidelines also set a floor of around 50 percent forage in a lactating ration on a dry matter basis for herds without close nutritional monitoring, which is a sensible conservative rule for a smallholder.
  2. Keep the chop long enough. Effective fibre depends on particle length, not just on NDF percentage. Forage ground or chopped too fine loses its ability to keep the rumen mat intact.
  3. Never make abrupt diet changes. Keep changes between feeding groups under 10 to 15 percent of diet dry matter, and step up concentrate over 3 weeks.
  4. Protect intake in fresh cows. Anything that reduces intake, whether milk fever, lameness, a dirty water trough or overcrowding at the feed face, empties the rumen and raises risk.
  5. Cap the concentrate at roughly 2 to 3 lb per 100 lb body weight per day, and remember that better forage lowers the concentrate needed.

Abomasal ulcers arise from the same background: high grain, low forage, and the acid load that comes with it. Feed management, not medication, is the control.

Worked example on economics. A crossbred dairy cow is worth, in many East African markets, in the range of 500 to 900 USD. Take 700 USD. If she develops a displaced abomasum at 12 days in milk and no surgeon is available, you salvage her at cull value, perhaps 250 to 350 USD. The loss is therefore roughly 350 to 450 USD plus the lost lactation. Against that, the prevention costs nothing at all: it is chopping coarser, stepping concentrate up over three weeks instead of three days, and putting a slab of long hay in front of fresh cows every morning. One prevented case in five years pays for a lifetime of discipline.

Concrete African context. Zero-grazing units in Kiambu and Nyeri counties in Kenya frequently feed a chopped Napier and dairy meal mix, and the pressure from feed cost pushes farmers to raise the meal share and chop finer to reduce refusals. Both moves reduce effective fibre. A practical countermeasure used successfully in those systems is a simple one: keep a rack of long dry hay or dried maize stover available to fresh cows at all times, separate from the mixed ration. Cows regulate their own fibre intake reasonably well when long forage is genuinely available, and the rack costs a morning's carpentry.

Signs to act on early: a fresh cow that goes off feed within the first 3 weeks, drops milk sharply, has reduced or scanty dung, and shows a slab-sided or hollow left flank. Call for advice at that point rather than waiting.

Lesson 11.5~12 min

Mastitis Prevention Through Nutrition

In this lesson
  • Explain how selenium, vitamin E, zinc and vitamin A support udder defence
  • Calculate a selenium and vitamin E supplementation plan for the periparturient window
  • Check a mineral programme against maximum tolerable levels before increasing any dose

Mastitis is usually treated as a hygiene problem, and hygiene is indeed the first line. But a cow's ability to resist and clear an intramammary infection is an immune function, and immune function is a nutritional output. The published standards are explicit that vitamin E and selenium are critical for immune function and mastitis prevention across the periparturient window, defined as 5 weeks before calving through to 3 weeks after.

Four nutrients carry most of the weight.

Selenium is an antioxidant cofactor that supports neutrophil function, the white cells that clear bacteria from the udder. The published beef and dairy requirement is 0.10 mg/kg of diet dry matter, and dairy guidance for immunity specifically cites 0.3 mg/kg DM. The maximum tolerable level is 5 ppm, so there is a wide but not unlimited safety margin. Selenium deficiency is regionally common because it tracks soil selenium, and large parts of East and Southern Africa sit on selenium-poor soils. Selenium deficiency also causes white muscle disease in calves.

Vitamin E works with selenium and the two should be considered together. The published optimal ratio is 1 mg of selenium per 100 IU of vitamin E. Requirements are 16 IU per lb of feed dry matter for growing cattle, 25 to 30 IU per lb of dry matter for lactating dairy, and 400 to 500 IU per day for stressed calves. Critically, vitamin E declines in stored feed at about 2 to 3 IU per lb per year, following the same pattern as vitamin A, so a herd on year-old hay is on a falling vitamin E supply exactly when nobody is looking.

Zinc supports skin and epithelial integrity, including the teat canal, which is the physical barrier the pathogen must cross. The requirement is 30 mg/kg of diet DM for both beef and dairy, and the maximum tolerable level is 500 ppm. Zinc deficiency shows as poor hoof, skin and coat quality. Note the interaction: excess zinc above 500 ppm inhibits copper absorption and creates a secondary copper deficiency.

Vitamin A supports epithelial integrity throughout the body. The requirement for a 1,200 lb lactating cow is 54,000 IU per day, against 34,000 IU for a gestating cow. Deficiency causes skin lesions, poor coat quality and immune compromise. The risk case is stored feed: beta-carotene degrades 33 to 50 percent per year, so year-one hay carries 50 to 67 percent of original vitamin A and year-two hay only 25 to 50 percent. Brown hay is severely depleted. If your cattle are on old or weathered hay or dormant dry-season pasture, they need supplementation.

Body condition matters too. Poor condition means the cow is in negative energy balance and stress-related immunosuppression follows, so the ketosis prevention work in Lesson 3 is also mastitis prevention.

Worked example. A 30-cow dairy herd is on year-old hay through a 5-month dry season. Vitamin A requirement for lactating cows is 54,000 IU per day each. Across 30 cows for 150 days that is 30 x 54,000 x 150 = 243 million IU. A typical injectable or in-feed vitamin ADE product is priced per dose or per kg; the practical route on most African farms is a fortified mineral premix that carries vitamins A, D and E along with the trace minerals. Costed at, say, 0.05 to 0.10 USD per cow per day for a good fortified mineral, the 150-day dry season protection costs 30 x 150 x 0.075 = 337 USD for the herd, roughly 11 USD a cow. A single clinical mastitis case, counting discarded milk, treatment and the risk of a lost quarter, commonly runs well past that. If the programme prevents even a handful of cases it pays.

Before increasing any mineral, check it against the maximum tolerable levels: copper 40 ppm, zinc 500 ppm, manganese 2,000 ppm, selenium 5 ppm, cobalt 25 ppm, iodine 50 ppm, molybdenum 5 ppm. Also check the interactions, because they defeat naive supplementation: excess molybdenum above 5 ppm binds copper and causes deficiency even with adequate copper in the ration; dietary sulfur above 0.20 percent of DM binds copper as sulfide; and high-sulfate water counts toward that total. Stress from transport or illness increases trace mineral requirements.

Concrete African context. Selenium-deficient soils across parts of Kenya, Tanzania, Malawi and Zimbabwe mean that a generic imported mineral formulated for a selenium-adequate region may still leave cattle short. The practical step is to read the guaranteed analysis on the bag rather than trusting the brand, confirm the selenium and vitamin E figures, and where possible ask your local veterinary or agricultural office whether soil selenium is known to be low in your district. A mineral that omits selenium is not a mineral programme.

Lesson 11.6~12 min

Bloat and Frothy Bloat Prevention

In this lesson
  • Distinguish frothy bloat from free-gas bloat and describe what causes each
  • Apply grazing management rules that prevent bloat on legume-rich pasture
  • Recognise a bloat emergency and know what to do in the minutes available

Bloat is the accumulation of fermentation gas in the rumen faster than the animal can belch it out. Because the distended rumen presses on the diaphragm and the great vessels, a severe case can kill in under an hour. It is one of the few nutritional emergencies where minutes matter.

There are two forms. Free-gas bloat occurs when the animal physically cannot eructate, from an obstruction, from being cast on its back, or from a nerve problem. Frothy bloat, the far more common form on legume pasture, occurs when soluble plant proteins and fine particles stabilise the gas into a foam. The gas is there, but it is trapped in bubbles that cannot be belched. That distinction determines treatment: releasing free gas helps a free-gas case and does very little for a frothy one, which needs an anti-foaming agent to break the bubbles.

Note on evidence: the specific reference standards compiled for this course do not cover bloat or anti-bloat products in detail. The management guidance below reflects widely accepted veterinary and extension practice, and any product dose must be taken from the current product label and your veterinarian, not from a course figure. Where this lesson gives numbers about legume feeding and forage structure, those come from the verified fibre and forage material used throughout this module.

Risk conditions are consistent and worth memorising:

  1. Lush, wet, rapidly growing legume pasture, especially lucerne and clover, and especially first thing in the morning when there is dew or after rain.
  2. Hungry animals turned onto that pasture, so they eat fast and take a large load before rumen adaptation.
  3. Sudden changes onto legume-rich grazing with no transition.
  4. Finely ground or finely chopped feed with little effective fibre, which produces more fine particles to stabilise foam.

The prevention rules are almost all free:

  • Never turn hungry cattle onto legume pasture. Fill them on dry hay or grass first, so they graze slowly rather than gorging.
  • Avoid grazing legume pasture when it is wet with dew or rain. Wait until midday when the sward has dried.
  • Introduce legume grazing gradually over 7 to 10 days, starting with short periods.
  • Keep dry, long-stem forage available alongside legume grazing at all times.
  • Keep the legume share of the sward moderate rather than pure. This is the same guidance given for integrating fodder legumes at 20 to 30 percent of forage dry matter.
  • Where anti-foaming agents such as poloxalene are locally available and registered, they are used preventively during high-risk grazing periods. Follow the label dose and veterinary advice; do not use a remembered figure.
  • Oils and fats, including vegetable oil, act as crude anti-foaming agents and are sometimes used in emergencies and preventively. Note that adding significant oil to a ruminant diet reduces fibre digestion, so this is a short-term tool, not a feeding strategy.

Emergency recognition and response. A bloated animal shows obvious distension on the left flank, standing above and behind the last rib, with the animal restless, reluctant to move, breathing rapidly with an extended neck, salivating and often kicking at the belly. Act immediately: stop grazing and move the animal off the pasture at a slow walk if it can walk, keep it standing and moving gently rather than lying down, and call the veterinarian. Passing a stomach tube can relieve free-gas bloat. Emergency trocarisation is a last-resort veterinary procedure with real complication risk and should not be a routine farmer action.

Worked example. A farm has 3 hectares of lucerne under irrigation and 25 head of cattle. The manager wants to use the lucerne because it is the best protein on the farm and displaces roughly 0.7 kg of cottonseed cake per cow per day, worth 0.28 USD, so 7 USD a day across 25 head, about 2,500 USD a year. That value is real and worth capturing. The safe protocol costs nothing: feed hay in the boma before turnout, turn out only after 10 am when the dew has lifted, start with 45 minutes on day one and build over 10 days, keep a hay rack on the field edge, and never turn out after heavy rain. One dead cow at 700 USD wipes out three months of the gain, so the discipline pays for itself the first time.

Concrete African context. Irrigated lucerne blocks are increasingly common on medium-sized dairy and beef farms in South Africa, Zimbabwe, Kenya and Egypt, and bloat losses on newly established blocks are a recurring first-season problem. The pattern is nearly always the same: the block finally comes into production, the manager turns the whole herd out onto it in the early morning to make use of it, and animals die by mid-morning. Fill them first, turn out late, build up slowly.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. How much magnesium does a lactating cow require daily during a high-risk grazing period?

Lactating cows need 18-21 g/day compared with 7-9 g/day in late pregnancy, because magnesium is lost in milk.

2. Which forage condition indicates high grass tetany risk?

A K:Mg ratio above 10:1, a K:(Ca+Mg) ratio above 2.2, or forage Mg below 0.15% DM all mark a high-risk pasture.

3. When should magnesium supplementation begin?

Cattle have no meaningful magnesium reserve, so supplementation must be in place before the lush growth period starts.

4. Why is free-choice magnesium oxide alone unreliable?

Magnesium oxide is unpalatable. Published practice is to carry it in molasses, bran or a daily concentrate, or use an 8-10% Mg free-choice mineral as a supporting measure.

5. Which pasture management step reduces tetany risk?

Delaying grazing, reducing spring and autumn nitrogen, and integrating legumes (which are higher in magnesium) are the published pasture measures.

6. Why does over-feeding calcium before calving increase milk fever risk?

Plentiful dietary calcium before calving lets the cow absorb passively, so her PTH-driven mobilisation system is inactive exactly when she suddenly needs it.

7. What is the published pre-calving DCAD target?

A negative DCAD of -5 to -10 mEq per 100 g DM, equal to -50 to -100 mEq/kg, produces the mild metabolic acidosis that primes the calcium response.

8. A cow has a blood calcium of 7.5 mg/dL and looks normal. What is her status?

7-8 mg/dL is subclinical hypocalcaemia. She shows no signs but has impaired immunity, which drives retained placenta, metritis and mastitis.

9. Which mineral must be adequate for the parathyroid hormone response to work?

Periparturient magnesium of 0.40-0.50% of diet DM supports PTH function; low magnesium undermines any calcium prevention strategy.

10. Which forage should be avoided for dry cows on a smallholder farm?

High potassium works directly against a negative DCAD. The good forage should go to milking cows where it earns income, and dry cows get the leaner material.

11. What is the target body condition score at calving?

BCS 3.0-3.5 on a 5-point scale. Over-conditioned cows have depressed fresh-cow appetite and mobilise fat aggressively, which is the classic ketosis case.

12. Which blood ketone level indicates ketosis risk?

Ketosis risk is defined by blood glucose below 40 mg/dL together with beta-hydroxybutyrate above 1.0 mmol/L.

13. When is ketosis risk highest in a dairy cow?

Early lactation, 1-3 weeks post-calving, is peak risk because milk output rises faster than dry matter intake recovers.

14. A farmer has limited good silage. Which group should receive it?

Fresh cows are in negative energy balance and are where energy density prevents ketosis and buys milk. Dry cows need adequate but restrained feeding.

15. Which supplement is used after a ketosis diagnosis as a glucose precursor?

Post-diagnosis management is to raise energy density and supply propylene glycol as a gluconeogenic precursor, with intravenous dextrose in severe cases.

16. What physically holds the abomasum in position in a healthy cow?

A rumen full of coarse forage keeps the abomasum in place. An empty rumen or one full of fine concentrate allows the gas-filled abomasum to float and displace.

17. Which cow is at highest risk of displaced abomasum?

Fresh cows with low effective fibre, rapid concentrate increases and negative energy balance carry the highest risk, which is why DA and ketosis often occur together.

18. Beyond NDF percentage, what else determines effective fibre?

Finely chopped forage can meet an NDF percentage while failing to stimulate chewing and maintain the rumen mat.

19. A conservative practical forage floor for a lactating ration without close monitoring is:

Around 50% forage on a dry matter basis is a safe working rule for herds that are not being monitored by a nutritionist.

20. Which early sign in a fresh cow should prompt action?

A sudden intake and milk drop with reduced dung in the first weeks after calving is the classic early presentation and needs advice immediately.

21. What is the published optimal ratio of selenium to vitamin E?

1 mg selenium per 100 IU vitamin E is the published ratio for maximum immune benefit.

22. What is the maximum tolerable level of selenium in cattle diets?

The requirement is 0.10 mg/kg with 0.3 mg/kg cited for dairy immunity, and the maximum tolerable level is 5 ppm.

23. Excess dietary molybdenum above 5 ppm causes which problem?

Molybdenum binds copper. High sulfur above 0.20% DM does the same by forming copper sulfide, so both defeat naive copper supplementation.

24. Which period is defined as the periparturient window for vitamin E and selenium supplementation?

The published window is 5 weeks before calving through 3 weeks after, when immune function and mastitis risk are most nutritionally sensitive.

25. Why does year-old hay create a mastitis-relevant risk?

Beta-carotene falls 33-50% per year and vitamin E declines about 2-3 IU per lb per year, so old hay quietly leaves cattle short on both.

26. What distinguishes frothy bloat from free-gas bloat?

Frothy bloat traps gas in foam stabilised by soluble plant proteins and fine particles, so releasing free gas alone does little; an anti-foaming agent is needed.

27. Which is the highest-risk moment for turning cattle onto lucerne?

Hungry cattle plus wet, lush legume is the classic frothy bloat setting, because they gorge fast on foam-forming material.

28. What is the correct first action before turning cattle onto legume pasture?

Filling animals with dry forage first slows intake on the legume and reduces the sudden fermentable load.

29. How long should the introduction to legume grazing take?

A gradual build-up over 7-10 days allows the rumen microbial population and the animal to adapt.

30. What is a limitation of using oil as an anti-foaming measure?

Oils break foam but significant dietary oil interferes with rumen fibre digestion, so it is an emergency or short-term measure rather than a feeding strategy.

Module 11 capstone

Mineral and Transition Programme Audit for your own herd. Step 1: list every animal on the farm by class: growing stock, dry pregnant cows, cows in the first 30 days after calving, cows in mid and late lactation, bulls. Step 2: write down exactly what mineral supplementation each class currently receives, including the brand, the guaranteed analysis printed on the bag, the amount actually consumed per head per day (weigh what you put out and what remains after a week), and the cost in USD. Step 3: compare the guaranteed analysis against the published requirements: calcium 0.35-0.65% of diet DM for growing and lactating cattle, phosphorus 0.20-0.35%, magnesium 0.10% growing, 0.12-0.15% gestating and 0.20-0.25% lactating, potassium 0.60-0.70%, sodium 0.06-0.10%, plus copper 10 mg/kg, zinc 30 mg/kg, manganese 20-40 mg/kg, selenium 0.10 mg/kg, cobalt 0.10-0.15 mg/kg and iodine 0.50 mg/kg. Mark every gap. Step 4: check nothing exceeds the maximum tolerable levels: copper 40 ppm, zinc 500 ppm, manganese 2,000 ppm, selenium 5 ppm, cobalt 25 ppm, iodine 50 ppm, molybdenum 5 ppm. Step 5: write out your current transition plan for cows calving in the next 90 days and check it against the 5-weeks-before to 3-weeks-after periparturient window, the 3-week step-up to the milking diet, and a target body condition score of 3.0-3.5 at calving. Step 6: record the last three years of losses to grass tetany, milk fever, ketosis, retained placenta and mastitis, put a USD figure on each using your own replacement and treatment costs, and compare that total against the annual cost of fixing the gaps you found. Present the audit as a one-page costed action plan with dates.
