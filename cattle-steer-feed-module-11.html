Lessons

1. Feedlot layout and bunk management

A well-designed feedlot saves you labour, reduces waste, and keeps cattle healthy. Start with the pen: allow 1520 square metres per animal for a 450 kg steer, with a 23% slope for drainage. In hot African climates, orient pens east-west so cattle get shade during the hottest part of the day. Provide at least 3 metres of shade per animal if you have trees or shade cloth. The feed bunk should be on the highest, driest part of the pen, with a concrete apron 2.53 metres wide to prevent mud. Allow 3045 cm of bunk space per head so all cattle can eat at once. A common mistake is too little bunk space, which causes aggressive feeding and acidosis. Water troughs must be clean and provide 4060 litres per head per day; place them opposite the bunk to encourage exercise. Bunk management means keeping feed fresh and consistent. Feed twice daily at the same times, e.g., 7 am and 3 pm. Clean out old feed before adding new. Use a feed bunk reader or simply a stick to measure how much feed is left before the next feeding. The goal is 05% orts (leftover feed) on a dry matter basis. If you find more than 10% orts, reduce the next feed by 510%. If the bunk is empty, increase by 5%. Never let the bunk stay empty for more than an hour, as this causes hunger and gorging. For small-scale feedlots in Africa, a simple two-row bunk with a central alley works well. Use locally available materials like treated poles and galvanised sheets, but ensure no sharp edges. Keep the area around the bunk clean to avoid attracting rodents and birds, which spread disease.

2. Step-up and step-down feeding programs

When cattle first arrive at the feedlot, their rumen is not used to high-grain diets. You must adapt them slowly over 2128 days using a step-up program. Start with a receiving diet of 4050% roughage (e.g., maize stover, hay) and 5060% concentrate. Feed this for the first 35 days. Then increase concentrate by 1015% every 34 days. For example: Day 14: 50% concentrate; Day 58: 60%; Day 912: 70%; Day 1316: 80%; Day 1721: 8590%. By day 2128, cattle should be on the finishing diet (8590% concentrate). Always provide long-stem roughage (e.g., 0.51 kg per head per day) during the step-up to stimulate chewing and saliva production. Watch for signs of acidosis: loose manure, reduced feed intake, or cattle kicking at their belly. If these appear, hold the concentrate level for a few days. Step-down feeding is used when cattle are sold or when you need to reduce feed costs. For example, if maize prices rise, you can step down the concentrate by 5% every 3 days, replacing it with roughage, but never drop below 50% concentrate for growing cattle. In Africa, where feed quality varies, test your roughage and concentrate for protein and energy. A simple rule: for every 10% increase in concentrate, add 0.5% calcium and 0.3% salt to the diet to prevent urinary calculi. Use a feed mixer wagon or mix by hand in small operations. Weigh ingredients accuratelya 10% error in concentrate can cause acidosis. Keep records of each step to know what works for your herd.

3. Monitoring feed intake and performance

To make money in a feedlot, you must know how much feed your cattle eat and how well they grow. Dry matter intake (DMI) is the key number. For a 300 kg steer gaining 1.2 kg per day, expect DMI of 2.53% of body weight, i.e., 7.59 kg of dry matter per day. For a 450 kg steer gaining 1.5 kg per day, DMI is 2.22.5% of body weight, i.e., 1011 kg per day. Measure feed offered and feed left over (orts) daily. Subtract orts from offered to get actual intake. Use a scale to weigh feeddo not guess. For small feedlots, a hanging scale or platform scale works. Record daily feed intake per pen. If intake drops by more than 10% from the previous day, check for acidosis, bloat, or water problems. Weigh cattle every 28 days. Use a livestock scale or weigh tape. Calculate average daily gain (ADG): (final weight initial weight) number of days. For example, if a steer goes from 300 kg to 384 kg in 70 days, ADG = 84 70 = 1.2 kg per day. Feed conversion ratio (FCR) = feed intake weight gain. A good FCR is 5:1 to 7:1 on a dry matter basis. If FCR is above 8:1, your diet is too expensive or cattle are sick. In Africa, use a simple notebook or smartphone app like FarmTrack or Livestock Manager to record data. Also monitor manure: loose manure means too much concentrate; dry, firm manure means too little. Check water intake: cattle drink 35 litres per kg of dry matter intake. If water intake drops, intake will drop. Finally, calculate cost per kg of gain: total feed cost total weight gain. This tells you if your feeding strategy is profitable.

4. Managing acidosis and bloat

Acidosis and bloat are the two biggest killers in feedlots. Acidosis happens when cattle eat too much grain too fast. The rumen pH drops below 5.5, killing good bacteria and causing inflammation. Signs: loose, grey manure, reduced feed intake, cattle kicking at their belly, and in severe cases, staggering and death. To prevent acidosis, follow the step-up program strictly. Never increase concentrate by more than 10% every 3 days. Add buffers like sodium bicarbonate at 0.51% of the diet, or magnesium oxide at 0.20.4%. Provide long-stem roughage (e.g., 0.51 kg per head per day) to stimulate chewing and saliva, which naturally buffers the rumen. If acidosis occurs, remove all concentrate for 24 hours and feed only hay. Then reintroduce concentrate slowly. For bloat, which is gas trapped in the rumen, signs include a swollen left side, laboured breathing, and drooling. Bloat is often caused by lush legumes like alfalfa or clover, or by high-grain diets with too little fibre. Prevent bloat by limiting legume hay to 20% of the diet, or by feeding poloxalene (Bloat Guard) at 24 g per 100 kg body weight per day. In Africa, where alfalfa is expensive, use grass hay and add 10% molasses to improve palatability. If an animal bloats, walk it briskly for 1015 minutes. If that fails, use a stomach tube to release gas. In severe cases, a vet may need to trocarise (puncture the rumen). Always have a vets number on hand. Keep a first-aid kit with sodium bicarbonate, mineral oil, and a stomach tube. Record every case to identify problem pens or feeds.

5. Feed delivery and weighing accuracy

Accurate feed delivery is the difference between profit and loss. A 5% error in feed weighing can cost you 5% of your feed budget. For a 100-head feedlot eating 10 kg per head per day, that is 50 kg of feed wasted or underfed daily. Use a calibrated scale. For total mixed rations (TMR), a feed mixer wagon with a scale is ideal, but for small-scale operations, a platform scale or hanging scale works. Weigh each ingredient separately. For example, if the diet is 70% maize, 20% soybean meal, 5% molasses, and 5% minerals, weigh 70 kg maize, 20 kg soybean, 5 kg molasses, and 5 kg minerals for a 100 kg batch. Mix thoroughly for at least 5 minutes. If mixing by hand, mix on a concrete floor with a shovel, turning the pile three times. Deliver feed at the same time each day. Use a feed cart or wheelbarrow. Distribute feed evenly along the bunk so all cattle have equal access. Do not dump feed in one spot. After feeding, check for leftover feed (orts). If orts are more than 5%, reduce the next feeding by the same amount. Weigh orts to know exactly how much. Clean the scale weekly and check zero. For accuracy, use a scale with 0.5 kg increments. In Africa, many feedlots use a simple beam scale or a digital luggage scale for small batches. Always calibrate with a known weight, like a 20 kg bag of cement. Keep a log of feed delivered per pen. If a pen consistently has high orts, check the dietmaybe it is too fine or too dusty. If a pen has no orts, they may be hungryincrease feed by 5%. Never guess; weigh everything.

6. Record keeping and data analysis for feedlot efficiency

You cannot improve what you do not measure. Record keeping is the backbone of a profitable feedlot. Start with a simple notebook or a spreadsheet on a phone or computer. For each pen, record daily: date, feed offered (kg), feed refused (orts, kg), number of cattle, and any health issues. Weekly, record cattle weights (or weigh a sample of 10 cattle). Monthly, calculate key performance indicators (KPIs): average daily gain (ADG), dry matter intake (DMI), feed conversion ratio (FCR), and cost per kg of gain. For example, if a pen of 20 steers eats 200 kg of feed per day (10 kg per head) and gains 24 kg per day (1.2 kg per head), FCR = 200 24 = 8.3:1. If your target is 6:1, you need to improve. Analyse data by pen, by diet, and by season. In Africa, rainy seasons may reduce intake due to mud; dry seasons may increase dust and reduce intake. Use records to adjust feeding times or add water to reduce dust. Calculate break-even price: total costs (feed, labour, health, bedding) total weight gain. If break-even is 2.50 USD per kg and market price is 2.80 USD, you make a profit. Use simple software like Excel or free apps like Feedlot Manager or Herdwatch. For small-scale farmers, a wall chart with daily feed and weekly weights works. Review records every week with your team. Identify pens with low ADG or high FCR and investigate: is it the diet, disease, or bunk management? Keep records for at least 3 years to see trends. Finally, use records to negotiate better prices for feed and cattle. Data is power.

7. Practical Reference


Practical Reference: Feedlot Management and Feeding Strategies



In African feedlots from the Highveld of South Africa to the peri-urban fattening units of Kenya, Zambia and Botswana margin is made or lost in the bunk. Feed represents 7085% of the cost of gain, so every kilogram of feed delivered must convert into saleable liveweight. This practical reference consolidates the operational essentials of feedlot layout, bunk management, step-up/step-down programmes, intake monitoring, acidosis control, feed delivery accuracy and record keeping into a single working document for feedlot managers, nutritionists and pen riders.



The guiding business principle is simple: measure, then manage. Feedlots that weigh every load, record every refusal and review close-out data per pen consistently outperform those that run on intuition. In African markets where feed grain and protein concentrate prices are volatile, the difference between a 6:1 and a 7:1 feed conversion ratio can be the difference between profit and loss.



Feedlot Layout and Bunk Management



A functional feedlot layout separates traffic flows: incoming cattle, feed delivery, manure removal and outgoing slaughter cattle should never cross unnecessarily. The feed alley must be wide enough for a mixer wagon or tractor-drawn feed buggy to travel in one direction, with a turning bay at each end. Bunk height and reach-over design determine how easily cattle access feed and how much is wasted.



Bunk space per animal depends on body weight and feeding frequency. As a rule of thumb, allow 250300 mm of linear bunk space per head for cattle up to 300 kg, and 300400 mm for heavier cattle. Under-feeding at the bunk creates competition, increases variation in intake and slows compensatory gain. Over-allocation wastes feed and encourages sorting.





Step-Up and Step-Down Feeding Programmes



Newly received cattle must be adapted from a high-forage receiving diet to a high-concentrate finishing diet. This is achieved through a step-up programme, typically over 1428 days depending on the starting weight, prior nutrition and health status. Each step increases concentrate by 510 percentage points while reducing roughage. The objective is to allow rumen papillae and microbial populations to adapt without triggering acidosis.



A step-down programme is used when cattle are sold, when feed quality changes, or when acidosis risk escalates. It involves reversing the concentrate:roughage ratio to stabilise rumen function before resuming the step-up. Step-down is also used during heat stress events, when intake naturally drops and a more fibrous diet reduces metabolic heat load.



In African feedlots where roughage such as maize stover, wheat straw or hay is locally available, the step-up can be economically advantageous because it uses cheaper fibre early in the feeding period. However, if roughage is scarce or expensive, a shorter step-up with careful bunk management may be justified but only where the manager can monitor intake daily.

Monitoring Feed Intake and Performance



Daily dry matter intake (DMI) is the single most useful early-warning indicator in a feedlot. A drop in intake across a pen even 10% can signal acidosis, heat stress, water failure, or a feed delivery error. Conversely, a sudden increase in intake may indicate under-feeding the previous day or a bunk-reading error.



Performance should be tracked per pen, not just per feedlot. Key metrics include average daily gain (ADG), feed conversion ratio (FCR), cost of gain, days on feed, and close-out margin. In African feedlots, where cattle are often sourced from communal or smallholder systems with variable genetics, ADG can range from 0.8 kg/day to 1.6 kg/day. FCR typically ranges from 5.5:1 to 8:1 depending on diet energy density and cattle type.

Managing Acidosis and Bloat



Acidosis occurs when rumen pH drops below 5.6 for extended periods, usually because of rapid fermentation of starch from grain. Sub-acute ruminal acidosis (SARA) is more common and more costly than acute acidosis because it depresses intake and fibre digestion without obvious symptoms. Bloat is a separate but related condition, often triggered by frothy legume-rich diets or sudden changes in feed.



Control measures include gradual step-up, adequate effective fibre, ionophores (where registered), buffering agents (sodium bicarbonate), consistent feeding times, and avoiding sudden changes in feed ingredient or particle size. In African feedlots where maize meal, hominy chop, molasses and brewers' grain are common energy sources, the risk of rapid starch fermentation is high and step-up discipline is non-negotiable.




SAFETY WARNING ACIDOSIS AND BLOAT EMERGENCY

Acute acidosis and bloat can kill cattle within hours. If you observe staggering, laboured breathing, profuse diarrhoea, or a distended left flank, act immediately: remove all concentrate from the bunk, provide long-stem roughage and fresh water, and contact a veterinarian. Never attempt to relieve bloat by inserting a trocar unless trained and authorised incorrect placement can cause fatal peritonitis. Do not increase concentrate in the diet until all affected animals have recovered and intake has stabilised for at least 48 hours.




Feed Delivery and Weighing Accuracy



Feed delivery errors are a hidden profit leak. A 5% over-delivery on a 10,000-tonne annual feed budget is 500 tonnes of wasted feed often more than the entire net margin of a medium-sized African feedlot. Weighing accuracy depends on scale calibration, loader operator discipline, and mixer wagon condition.



Best practice includes:



  • Calibrate feed scales monthly and after any major repair.

  • Verify mixer wagon accuracy by weighing a known load of a single ingredient.

  • Record actual delivered weight versus target for every load.

  • Inspect bunks before feeding never feed on top of stale or spoiled feed.

  • Keep a bunk-reading log with a consistent 04 scale (0 = empty, 4 = full).



In many African feedlots, feed is delivered by tractor-drawn mixer wagons or by hand. Hand-feeding increases risk of inconsistency; where possible, invest in a weigh box or load cells on the wagon. The payback period is often less than one year through reduced over-feeding alone.



Record Keeping and Data Analysis for Feedlot Efficiency



Records are the foundation of feedlot profitability. At minimum, a feedlot should maintain:



  • Pen-level daily feed delivery and refusal records.

  • Individual or pen-level entry weight, exit weight and days on feed.

  • Health treatment records (animal ID, date, product, dose, withdrawal period).

  • Feed ingredient receipts, batch numbers and nutrient analyses.

  • Close-out summaries per pen: ADG, FCR, cost of gain, mortality, margin.



Data analysis should be routine, not annual. Weekly review of intake and bunk scores allows early correction. Monthly review of close-out data identifies systemic issues for example, a pen that consistently underperforms may indicate poor cattle sourcing, inadequate bunk space, or a health challenge. In African contexts where record-keeping may be paper-based, a simple notebook or spreadsheet is sufficient if used consistently.



Quick Reference Checklist



  1. Bunk space: 250400 mm per head depending on weight and feeding frequency.

  2. Step-up: 1428 days, increasing concentrate by 510 percentage points per step.

  3. Daily intake check: Investigate any pen-level drop greater than 10%.

  4. Acidosis watch: Loose dung, reduced intake, laminitis act before it spreads.

  5. Scale calibration: Monthly, and after any repair or ingredient change.

  6. Bunk reading: Use a consistent 04 scale before every feeding.

  7. Close-out records: Calculate ADG, FCR and cost of gain per pen.

  8. Water: Ensure adequate trough space and flow rate water drives intake.
