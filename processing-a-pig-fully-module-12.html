1. Calculating the true cost of processing one pig

To know if you are making a profit, you must know the true cost of processing one pig. Many small butchers forget hidden costs and end up losing money. Start by listing every expense for one pig. Example: You buy a live pig for 150,000 shillings (about $60). Transport to your facility costs 10,000. Slaughter fee if you use a local abattoir is 20,000. Water and electricity for cleaning and chilling: 5,000. Packaging materials (plastic bags, trays, labels): 8,000. Labor for you and one helper for a day: 15,000. Market fees or stall rent: 7,000. Total cost = 215,000 shillings. Now weigh the carcass. A typical pig yields about 70% of live weight. If the pig weighed 80 kg live, you get 56 kg of meat. So your cost per kg is 215,000 ÃƒÂ· 56 = 3,839 shillings per kg. If you sell at 5,000 per kg, you make 1,161 per kg profit. But if you sell at 3,500, you lose money. Always calculate before you set prices. Also track variable costs like feed if you keep pigs longer. Use a simple notebook or a free app like Excel on your phone. Update your costs every month because prices change. This calculation helps you negotiate better with suppliers and set fair prices that keep you in business.

2. Keeping simple records of sales and expenses

Good records are the difference between a hobby and a real business. You don't need a computer. A hardcover notebook and a pen work fine. Divide each page into two columns: Date, Description, Money In, Money Out, Balance. Every time you sell pork, write the date, what you sold (e.g., 5 kg ribs), and the amount received. Every time you spend, write the date, what you bought (e.g., 2 pigs from Mr. Okafor), and the amount paid. At the end of each day, total your money in and money out. At the end of the week, add up all sales and all expenses. Subtract expenses from sales to get your profit. For example, if you sold 40 kg at 5,000 each = 200,000. Expenses: pig cost 150,000, transport 10,000, packaging 8,000 = 168,000. Profit = 32,000. Keep receipts in a separate envelope or plastic bag. If you use a phone, apps like "Cash Book" or even a simple WhatsApp message to yourself can work. But paper is reliable. Review your records weekly to spot patterns: Are sales higher on market days? Are costs rising? This helps you plan. Also, records prove your income if you want a loan from a microfinance bank. Without records, you are guessing. With records, you control your business.

3. Reinvesting profits to buy more pigs or equipment

Profit is not for spending on personal things first. To grow, you must reinvest. Start by setting a rule: every time you make a profit, put at least 50% back into the business. For example, if you make 32,000 shillings profit from one pig, save 16,000 to buy the next pig. Use the rest for your needs. But if you want to grow faster, reinvest 70% or more. What can you buy? More pigs: one pig costs 150,000. If you save 16,000 per week, in 10 weeks you can buy another pig without borrowing. Equipment: a used chest freezer costs 200,000. A meat grinder costs 80,000. A weighing scale costs 30,000. These save time and improve quality. For example, a grinder lets you make sausages, which sell at a higher price. A freezer lets you store meat longer and sell when prices are high. Also reinvest in feed if you keep pigs. Buy feed in bulk to get discounts. Keep a separate "business savings" box or mobile money account. Do not mix business and personal money. Every month, review your profits and decide what to buy next. Prioritize tools that increase your capacity or reduce costs. For example, a bicycle for delivery costs 50,000 and saves transport fees. Reinvesting is how a one-pig operation becomes a ten-pig operation.

4. Finding reliable pig suppliers and negotiating prices

Your profit depends on buying pigs at a good price. Start by visiting local pig farms. Ask other butchers where they buy. Go to livestock markets early in the morning. Look for healthy pigs: clear eyes, smooth skin, no coughing. Avoid very thin or lame pigs. Build relationships with 2-3 reliable farmers. Tell them you will buy regularly. Ask for a discount for bulk or repeat purchases. For example, if a farmer sells one pig for 150,000, offer 140,000 if you buy two. Or ask for a free transport if you buy three. Always weigh the pig before buying. A weighing scale is essential. If you can't weigh, use a measuring tape: heart girth (around the chest) and length from ear to tail. 5. Marketing your pork products via WhatsApp and local radio

You have pork to sell, but people must know. WhatsApp is free and powerful. Create a business WhatsApp account. Save your customers' numbers. Post photos of fresh pork, cuts, and prices. Use status updates every morning: "Fresh pork today! 5,000 per kg. Free delivery in town." Ask customers to share your number with friends. Create a group for regular buyers. Offer a small discount for orders via WhatsApp. For example, 4,800 per kg if you order before 10 am. Local radio is also effective. Many community radio stations charge 5,000-10,000 shillings for a 30-second ad. Run ads on market days. Say: "Buy fresh pork from [Your Name]. Call 07XX-XXXXXX. We deliver." Repeat your phone number clearly. Also, put posters at butcheries, bars, and restaurants. Offer free samples to potential bulk buyers like hotels. Word of mouth is strong: give good service and ask happy customers to tell others. Track which marketing works: ask new customers how they heard about you. If WhatsApp brings more sales, spend more time there. If radio works, renew the ad. Marketing is not a one-time thing. Do it daily. A simple photo of your pork with a price tag can bring orders. Keep your promises: if you say delivery by 2 pm, be there. Your reputation is your best marketing.

6. Scaling up: hiring help and expanding to a small abattoir

When you sell more pigs than you can handle alone, hire help. Start with one part-time worker. Pay fairly: for example, 10,000 shillings per day for slaughter and cleaning. Train them on hygiene and safety. Hire someone you trust. As sales grow, hire a second person for delivery or sales. But before hiring, calculate if the extra profit covers the wages. If you process 5 pigs a week and make 30,000 profit per pig, that's 150,000. Hiring one helper at 50,000 per week leaves 100,000 profit. That's good. Next, consider a small abattoir. You can build a simple slaughter slab with concrete floor, water, and drainage for 500,000 shillings. Or rent a space and equip it. A small abattoir lets you slaughter more pigs, meet hygiene standards, and sell to hotels and supermarkets. You may need a license from local health authorities. Visit your local government office to ask about permits. Also, invest in a chest freezer and a delivery bicycle or motorbike. Keep records of all new costs. Scaling up means more responsibility: pay taxes, insure your workers, and maintain equipment. But it also means more profit. Start small: first hire one helper, then save for equipment, then build the abattoir. Don't rush. Grow step by step.

Pigs can carry Taenia solium (pork tapeworm), Trichinella, Salmonella, E. coli and Streptococcus suis. These pass to people through raw or undercooked pork, contaminated knives, tables and hands. Never sell raw blood or raw offal. Cook all pork products to an internal temperature of at least 71 Â°C (160 Â°F). Wash hands, knives and surfaces with soap and clean water between the live animal and the meat. Do not slaughter sick pigs for sale â report them to a veterinary officer. Keep pigs away from human toilets and untreated water. If you or a worker develop fever, severe headache or muscle pain after handling pigs, see a health worker and mention the pig contact. Source: WHO, Taenia solium fact sheet; FAO/WHO Codex Alimentarius meat hygiene codes; MSD Veterinary Manual, zoonoses chapter.

Quick Reference Checklist â Before, During and After Processing

  1. Weigh the live pig on a scale or use a reliable weight tape, and record the figure before money changes hands.
  2. Confirm the pig is healthy: no fever, no diarrhoea, no skin lesions, no coughing. Reject sick animals â do not "process and hope".
  3. Use potable water only for scalding, washing carcass and cleaning equipment. Dirty water is the fastest way to ruin a batch.
  4. Separate clean and dirty work: different knives, tables and aprons for live animal handling, gut removal and meat cutting.
  5. Chill fast: move the carcass to 0â4 Â°C within two hours. If you have no cold room, sell or cook the same day and tell buyers it is fresh, not chilled.
  6. Weigh and price every product stream â lean meat, offal, head, trotters, blood, fat, bones. Do not give away what you can sell.
  7. Record every batch in the Table 2 format. One line per pig. Review weekly for dressing percentage and gross margin.
  8. Clean and disinfect all tables, knives, floors and drains at the end of every processing day, and log it.
