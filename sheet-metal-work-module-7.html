1. Safety in Welding and Soldering

Welding and soldering are high-risk activities. Before you strike an arc or light a torch, put on your personal protective equipment (PPE). For arc welding, you need a welding helmet with a shade 10 or 11 lens for most sheet metal work, leather gloves, a leather apron, and boots. For gas welding, use goggles with a shade 5 lens. Never wear synthetic clothingit melts and sticks to skin. Cotton coveralls are best.

Ventilation is critical. Welding fumes contain metal oxides and gases that can damage your lungs. Work outdoors or in a well-ventilated area. If you must work indoors, use a fume extractor or a fan blowing fumes away from your breathing zone. In Africa, many workshops are open-air, which helps, but be aware of wind blowing fumes back at you.

Fire safety: Keep a fire extinguisher (dry powder or CO2) within reach. Remove all flammable materialspaint, solvents, rags, paperfrom a 10-meter radius. Sheet metal can get hot enough to ignite nearby wood or cloth. After welding, stay in the area for at least 30 minutes to watch for smoldering fires.

Gas cylinders require special care. Store oxygen and acetylene cylinders upright and secured with a chain or strap. Keep them at least 6 meters apart or separated by a fire-resistant wall. Never use oil or grease on oxygen fittingsit can cause an explosion. Open acetylene cylinder valves only 1/4 to 1/2 turn so you can shut them off quickly in an emergency.

Electric shock is a risk with arc welding. Check that your welding machine is grounded. Don't weld in wet conditions. Wear dry gloves and stand on a dry rubber mat if the floor is concrete. If you feel a tingle, stop immediately and have the machine checked.

Finally, protect your eyes from flying chips when chipping slag. Wear safety glasses under your helmet. When you finish, clean up metal scraps and sharp edges. Good housekeeping prevents cuts and trips.

2. Oxy-Acetylene Welding of Sheet Metal

Oxy-acetylene welding is ideal for sheet metal because you control the heat precisely. You need an oxygen cylinder, an acetylene cylinder, regulators, hoses (red for acetylene, green or black for oxygen), a welding torch, and a spark lighter. For sheet metal 0.5 mm to 2 mm thick, use a small tip, such as a size 0 or 1. Set the oxygen pressure at 0.2 bar and acetylene at 0.2 bar. Adjust the flame to a neutral flamea sharp inner cone with no acetylene feather.

Start by cleaning the metal. Remove rust, oil, and paint with a wire brush or grinder. Fit the pieces together with a small gap (about half the thickness of the metal) for butt joints. Tack weld every 25 mm to hold alignment. Use a copper or carbon block behind the joint to absorb heat and prevent warping.

Hold the torch at a 45-degree angle to the joint. Move the flame in a small circular motion to preheat the metal. When a molten pool forms, dip the filler rod (same material as the base metal) into the leading edge of the pool. Add filler only as neededtoo much causes lumps. Move forward steadily. For thin sheets, use a technique called 'forehand welding' where the torch points in the direction of travel and the filler rod follows.

Control distortion. Sheet metal warps easily. Use clamps or clecos to hold parts. Weld short sections (25-50 mm) and let them cool before continuing. You can also use a heat sink, like a wet rag, next to the weld. After welding, clean the joint with a wire brush and check for cracks or porosity.

In Africa, oxy-acetylene is common because it's versatileyou can also use it for brazing, cutting, and heating. But acetylene is expensive. If you can't afford it, consider MIG or TIG welding. Always leak-test hoses with soapy water before lighting up. Never use a match or lighteruse a spark lighter. When finished, close both cylinder valves, bleed the hoses, and back off the regulator screws.

3. Gas Tungsten Arc Welding (TIG)

TIG welding (Gas Tungsten Arc Welding) gives you the cleanest, most precise welds on sheet metal. It's perfect for thin stainless steel, aluminum, and even copper. You need a TIG machine with a foot pedal or hand amperage control, a tungsten electrode (2% thoriated for steel, pure tungsten for aluminum), a ceramic cup, and argon gas. For sheet metal 1 mm thick, set the amperage to about 40-60 amps. Use a 1/16-inch tungsten and a #6 or #7 cup. Argon flow should be 10-15 CFH (cubic feet per hour).

Prepare the metal. TIG requires absolutely clean metal. Wipe with acetone or alcohol to remove oil. For aluminum, brush with a stainless steel wire brush dedicated to aluminum only. Fit the joint tightlyno gap needed for TIG. Tack weld every 20-30 mm. Use a copper backing bar to prevent burn-through on thin sheets.

Hold the torch at a 70-80 degree angle, almost vertical. Strike an arc by touching the tungsten to the work and lifting, or use high-frequency start. Form a molten pool. Dip the filler rod (same alloy as base metal) into the front edge of the pool. Move forward in small steps, adding filler with each dip. For thin sheet, you can often weld autogenously (without filler) for a clean seam. Keep the tungsten sharp and not touching the pool. If it touches, stop, regrind, and restart.

TIG is slow but produces beautiful welds. It's great for visible parts like motorcycle fuel tanks or kitchen equipment. In Africa, TIG is used in workshops that make stainless steel sinks, dairy equipment, and aluminum boats. The main challenge is argon gas supplyit can be expensive or hard to find in remote areas. If argon is scarce, you might use a MIG welder with CO2, but TIG gives better quality.

Practice on scrap metal. Start with mild steel, then try stainless. Aluminum is trickier because it conducts heat away fastyou need more amps and a pedal to control heat. Always wear a welding helmet with a shade 10-12 lens. Keep your tungsten clean and your gloves dry.

4. Gas Metal Arc Welding (MIG)

MIG welding (Gas Metal Arc Welding) is the easiest welding process to learn and the fastest for sheet metal. It uses a continuously fed wire electrode and a shielding gas. For sheet metal 1-2 mm thick, use a 0.6 mm or 0.8 mm mild steel wire. Set the voltage to 16-18 volts and wire feed speed to 3-4 meters per minute. Use a shielding gas of 75% argon and 25% CO2 (called C25) at 12-15 CFH. For aluminum, use 100% argon and a spool gun or Teflon liner.

Set up your machine. Check the polarity: for solid wire with shielding gas, use DCEP (electrode positive). Install the wire spool, feed it through the liner and gun, and set the tension so the wire doesn't slip but also doesn't crush. Use a contact tip that matches your wire size. For thin sheet, use a 0.6 mm tip with 0.6 mm wire. Clean the metalremove rust, oil, and paint. Fit the joint with a small gap for butt welds, or overlap for lap welds. Tack weld every 25-50 mm.

Hold the gun at a 10-15 degree angle, pushing the weld (forehand) or pulling (backhand). For thin sheet, push is better for penetration control. Keep a stick-out of 6-10 mm. Move steadily, watching the molten pool. For thin metal, use a series of tack welds (stitch welding) to prevent burn-through. Weld 10-15 mm, then skip 10-15 mm, then weld again. Let each section cool slightly.

MIG is great for production work. In Africa, MIG welders are common in car repair shops, metal furniture makers, and construction. The wire and gas are widely available in cities. If you can't get shielding gas, you can use flux-cored wire (FCAW) which doesn't need gas, but it's messier. Always wear a welding helmet with a shade 10-11 lens. Use pliers to cut the wire between welds. Clean the nozzle regularly with anti-spatter spray.

Practice on scrap. Adjust voltage and wire speed until you get a smooth, crackling sound like frying bacon. If it pops or sputters, adjust. Keep your gloves dry and your work area clean.

5. Soldering and Brazing Techniques

Soldering and brazing join metals without melting the base metal. Soldering uses filler metal that melts below 450C, while brazing uses filler that melts above 450C but below the base metal's melting point. For sheet metal, common fillers are 50/50 tin-lead solder (for electrical) or silver solder (for strength). Brazing often uses brass or bronze rods with flux.

Start by cleaning the metal. Remove all oxide with sandpaper or a wire brush. For soldering, apply fluxrosin for electronics, acid flux for sheet metal. Acid flux is corrosive, so clean it off after. For brazing, use borax or commercial brazing flux. Heat the joint with a propane torch or oxy-acetylene torch. For soldering, a small propane torch is enough. For brazing, you need more heatuse oxy-acetylene with a neutral flame.

Heat the joint, not the filler. Touch the filler to the joint; if it melts, the joint is hot enough. For soldering, apply solder to the joint and let it flow. Don't move the parts until the solder solidifies. For brazing, heat the joint to a dull red, then dip the brazing rod into flux and touch it to the joint. The filler should flow into the gap by capillary action. Use a tight joint0.05 to 0.1 mm clearance for soldering, 0.1 to 0.2 mm for brazing.

Soldering is used for electrical connections, radiator repair, and sealing seams. Brazing is stronger and used for bicycle frames, HVAC tubing, and sheet metal assemblies. In Africa, brazing is common in refrigeration repair and car radiators. You can use a charcoal fire with a blowpipe for brazing if you don't have a torchthis is a traditional method.

After joining, clean off flux with hot water and a wire brush. For soldering, a 60/40 tin-lead solder melts at 190C. For brazing, a typical bronze rod melts at 900C. Always wear safety glasses and gloves. Work in a ventilated areaflux fumes are harmful. Practice on scrap copper and steel. A good solder joint is shiny and smooth; a good braze joint is golden and filleted.

6. Spot Welding and Resistance Welding

Spot welding and resistance welding use electric current and pressure to join sheet metal without filler. Spot welding is the most commonit's fast and perfect for overlapping thin sheets (0.5-3 mm). You need a spot welder with copper electrodes. The machine passes a high current (1000-10000 amps) through the metal for a short time (0.1-0.5 seconds). Resistance at the joint generates heat, melting and fusing the metal.

Set up your spot welder. For 1 mm mild steel, use a welding current of about 5000 amps and a squeeze time of 0.2 seconds. Adjust the electrode forcetoo little and you get sparks; too much and you dent the metal. Clean the metal first. Remove paint, rust, and oil. Overlap the sheets by at least 10 mm. Clamp them tightly. Position the electrodes on either side of the joint. Press the foot pedal or trigger. Hold for the set time. Release. You should see a small indentation and a discolored spot. Test the weld by twisting the sheets apartthe weld should hold.

Spot welding is used in car bodies, appliance manufacturing, and metal furniture. In Africa, many small workshops use foot-operated spot welders for making metal boxes, shelves, and car repairs. If you don't have a spot welder, you can use a carbon arc torch or a MIG welder to make plug welds, but spot welding is faster and cleaner.

Resistance welding also includes seam welding (using rollers) and projection welding. Seam welding makes continuous leak-proof seams for fuel tanks and radiators. Projection welding joins nuts and studs to sheet metal. These processes need specialized machines.

Safety: Spot welders use high currentnever touch the electrodes during operation. Wear dry gloves and stand on a dry floor. Keep the electrodes clean with a file or emery cloth. Replace them when they become pitted. The weld nugget should be 4-5 mm diameter for 1 mm sheet. If the weld is weak, increase current or time. If it burns through, decrease. Practice on scrap. A good spot weld is strong and leaves a slight indentation. Always unplug the machine before changing electrodes.

<h2>Practical Reference: Welding &amp; Soldering in Sheet Metal An African Workshop Guide</h2>

<p>This practical reference is written for the sheet metal entrepreneur, the artisan fabricator, and the technician running a small workshop in Nairobi, Lagos, Accra, or Johannesburg. <p>Across much of sub-Saharan Africa, sheet metal demand is rising with urban housing, cold-chain logistics, and food processing. <h3>Why This Matters for African Businesses</h3>
<p>Sheet metal work is one of the fastest routes into formal manufacturing for small African enterprises. A two-person workshop with a MIG set, a guillotine, and a bending brake can supply HVAC ducting to a growing hotel sector, fabricate poultry drinkers for agribusiness, or produce stainless steel tables for food processing. Welding and soldering quality directly determines whether you win the next order or absorb the cost of a failed tank, a leaking duct, or a cracked gate hinge.</p>

<p>Three commercial realities shape process choice in most African markets:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Power reliability.</strong> TIG and MIG require stable voltage. Where the grid fluctuates, an inverter MIG with a generator or a voltage stabiliser is often cheaper than downtime.</li>
<li><strong>Gas logistics.</strong> Argon and CO cylinders are available in major cities but expensive inland. Oxy-acetylene remains the most widely available gas process across the continent.</li>
<li><strong>Skill supply.</strong> TIG welders command higher wages. Training an apprentice on MIG or oxy-acetylene is faster and cheaper for routine sheet metal work.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Process Notes for the Workshop Floor</h3>

<p><strong>Oxy-Acetylene Welding.</strong> The most forgiving process for thin sheet in areas with unreliable power. Use a neutral flame for mild steel, a slightly oxidising flame for brass, and a carburising flame for aluminium. Keep the tip size matched to thickness a #0 or #1 tip for 1 mm sheet. Pre-heat the joint edges, use a small weaving motion, and add filler only when a molten pool forms. Distortion is the main enemy: tack every 5075 mm and use a copper backing bar where possible.</p>

<p><strong>TIG (GTAW).</strong> The premium process for stainless and aluminium. In Africa, TIG is the standard for food-grade stainless steel dairy tanks, brewing vessels, abattoir tables. Use DCEN for steel and stainless, AC for aluminium. Keep tungsten sharpened to a point for DC, balled for AC. Argon flow 812 L/min. Clean the joint with a stainless wire brush dedicated to stainless only carbon steel contamination causes rust bleed.</p>

<p><strong>MIG (GMAW).</strong> The production workhorse for mild steel ducting, gates, and trailers. Use 0.8 mm wire for 12 mm sheet, 1.01.2 mm for thicker. CO is cheaper than Ar/CO mix but gives more spatter; mix gives smoother beads and is worth the extra cost on visible work. Set wire speed and voltage by test coupon, not by guesswork. Keep contact tip and nozzle clean spatter build-up causes wire feed problems, the most common MIG fault in busy workshops.</p>

<p><strong>Soldering and Brazing.</strong> Soldering (below 450 C) suits electrical enclosures, gutters, and low-stress joints. Brazing (above 450 C) suits copper pipework, HVAC, and dissimilar metals. Cleanliness is everything: degrease, then mechanically clean, then flux immediately before heating. For copper pipework, capillary brazing with silicon bronze or silver-bearing filler gives strong, leak-free joints. In food and potable water applications, use lead-free solder and lead-free flux.</p>

<p><strong>Spot and Resistance Welding.</strong> Fast, clean, and no filler ideal for lap joints in appliance housings, automotive panels, and ventilation fittings. The key variables are current, electrode force, and weld time. On thin galvanised sheet, use higher current and shorter time. Electrode caps must be dressed regularly; worn caps cause undersized welds and rejected work. Resistance welding needs a stable power supply if your workshop runs on a generator, size it at least 2.5 the welder's rated kVA to handle inrush current.</p>

<h3>Quoting and Quality Control</h3>
<p>Quote by the metre of weld, not by the hour, once you know your deposition rate. For MIG on 1.5 mm mild steel, a competent welder deposits roughly 1.52.5 kg of wire per 8-hour day on ducting work. Add 1520 % for setup, tacking, and clean-up. Inspect every joint visually: look for undercut, porosity, incomplete fusion, and spatter. For pressure or water-tight work, dye penetrant or a simple water-immersion test catches leaks before the client does. Document your settings on a job card repeat jobs become faster and more profitable.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Match process to thickness and power.</strong> 0.52 mm: oxy-acetylene, soldering, or spot. 13 mm: TIG or MIG. 36 mm: MIG spray or multi-pass TIG.</li>
<li><strong>Check gas and consumables before starting.</strong> Cylinder pressure, wire spool, contact tip, tungsten, flux, and filler rod running out mid-job wastes time and gas.</li>
<li><strong>Clean the joint.</strong> Remove oil, paint, rust, and zinc. Stainless gets a dedicated stainless brush only.</li>
<li><strong>Set up ventilation and PPE first.</strong> Forced-air extraction for galvanised or painted steel; helmet, gloves, apron, boots, and respirator as required.</li>
<li><strong>Tack before you weld.</strong> Tack every 5075 mm to control distortion, especially on thin sheet and long seams.</li>
<li><strong>Test your settings on a scrap coupon.</strong> Adjust voltage, wire speed, gas flow, and travel speed before touching the customer's job.</li>
<li><strong>Inspect and pressure-test.</strong> Visual check for undercut, porosity, and incomplete fusion; water or dye test for leak-tight work.</li>
<li><strong>Record settings and consumables used.</strong> A simple job card builds your quoting accuracy and your reputation for repeatable quality.</li>
</ol>
