1. Nutrient Requirements of Sheep

Sheep need six key nutrients: water, energy, protein, minerals, vitamins, and fiber. Water is the most critical. A dry ewe drinks 48 liters per day; a lactating ewe can drink 1015 liters. In hot African conditions, always provide clean water twice daily. Energy comes mainly from carbohydrates in pasture, hay, and grains. A dry ewe needs about 810% protein in her diet; a lactating ewe needs 1416%. Growing lambs need 1618% protein. Energy is measured in Total Digestible Nutrients (TDN). For maintenance, sheep need 5060% TDN; for late pregnancy, 6065%; for lactation, 6570%. Fiber is essential for rumen health. At least 30% of the diet should be roughage like grass or hay. Minerals like calcium, phosphorus, and salt are needed in small amounts. Provide a free-choice mineral lick. Vitamins A, D, and E are important; green pasture provides these. In dry seasons, supplement with commercial sheep premix. A simple rule: watch body condition. A sheep with a body condition score of 2.53.5 (on a 5-point scale) is healthy. You can feel the spine and ribs but not see them. Adjust feed based on age, pregnancy, and lactation. Overfeeding concentrates can cause acidosis; underfeeding causes weight loss and poor reproduction. Always introduce new feeds gradually over 710 days. For African smallholders, use locally available feeds: maize bran, cottonseed cake, sunflower cake, and legume hays like lucerne or groundnut hay. Mix them to meet protein and energy needs. Clean water, good pasture, and a balanced mineral supplement will keep your sheep productive.

2. Pasture Management and Grazing

Good pasture is the cheapest feed for sheep. In Africa, common pasture grasses include Rhodes grass, Napier grass, star grass, and Kikuyu. Legumes like desmodium, stylo, and lucerne add protein. Rotational grazing is key. Divide your land into 46 paddocks using wire or live fences. Graze each paddock for 35 days, then rest it for 2130 days. This prevents overgrazing and parasite buildup. Stocking rate: 1 sheep per 0.40.8 hectares on improved pasture; on poor rangeland, 1 per 24 hectares. In dry areas, reduce stocking. Always provide a mineral lick and clean water in each paddock. Avoid grazing wet pastures early in the morning when dew is heavythis reduces bloat and worm infection. For zero-grazing systems, cut and carry fresh grass and legumes daily. A mature ewe eats 24 kg of fresh forage per day. Mix grasses with legumes at a ratio of 70:30. Improve pasture by broadcasting seeds, applying manure or compost, and controlling weeds. In East Africa, many farmers use 'tumbukiza' pits for Napier grass to conserve moisture. During dry seasons, conserve excess grass as hay or silage. Rotational grazing also helps break the life cycle of internal parasites like Haemonchus contortus (barber's pole worm). If you see sheep with pale gums, bottle jaw, or diarrhea, move them to a clean paddock and deworm. Never let sheep graze too shortleave 810 cm of grass height. Overgrazed pastures take longer to recover and invite weeds. For small flocks, a simple electric fence with two wires works well. Train sheep to electric fences by putting them in a small paddock for a few days. Always check fences for breaks. A well-managed pasture can carry 1015 ewes per hectare in high-rainfall areas with good management.

3. Hay and Silage Production

Hay and silage are conserved feeds for dry seasons. Hay is dried grass or legumes; silage is fermented, high-moisture forage. To make good hay, cut grass at early flowering stage when protein is highest. Dry it in the sun for 23 days, turning it daily. Aim for 1520% moistureif you twist a handful, it should break, not drip. Store hay in a dry, ventilated shed on pallets to avoid mold. A ewe eats 1.52.5 kg of hay per day. Legume hays like lucerne or groundnut hay are richer in protein (1520%) than grass hay (810%). For silage, chop fresh grass or maize stalks to 23 cm lengths. Pack tightly into a pit or silo, excluding air. Add molasses (23% of fresh weight) to improve fermentation. Cover with plastic sheet and soil. Silage is ready in 46 weeks. Good silage smells sweet and sour, not rotten. A ewe can eat 35 kg of silage per day. In Africa, maize silage is common. Harvest maize at dough stage (when kernels are soft but not dry). For smallholders, a 2x2x2 meter pit holds about 4 tons of silage, enough for 10 ewes for 3 months. Always test silage before feeding: if it is slimy, black, or smells like ammonia, discard it. Introduce silage gradually over a week. Hay and silage can be supplemented with concentrates. For example, feed 1 kg of hay plus 0.3 kg of maize bran to a lactating ewe. Store hay and silage away from rodents and moisture. In humid areas, use hay sheds with raised floors. Never feed moldy hayit can cause listeriosis or abortion. Invest in a simple moisture meter or use the salt test: mix a handful of chopped hay with salt; if salt sticks, moisture is too high. Proper conservation saves money and keeps sheep healthy year-round.

4. Concentrate Feeding and Rations

Concentrates are energy- or protein-rich feeds like grains, oilseed cakes, and by-products. They are expensive, so use them strategically. Common African concentrates: maize, sorghum, millet, maize bran, wheat bran, cottonseed cake, sunflower cake, and soybean meal. A simple ration for a lactating ewe: 1.5 kg hay + 0.5 kg maize bran + 0.2 kg cottonseed cake + mineral lick. This provides about 16% protein and enough energy. For growing lambs, use 18% protein: 1 kg hay + 0.4 kg maize + 0.3 kg soybean meal. Always introduce concentrates slowly over 710 days to avoid acidosis. Start with 100 g per day and increase to full ration. Feed concentrates twice daily, after roughage. Never feed more than 0.5 kg of grain at one time per sheep. Mix feeds thoroughly. You can make a home-made ration: 50% maize bran, 30% cottonseed cake, 15% molasses, 5% mineral premix. This is a 14% protein mix. For dry ewes, 0.20.3 kg per day is enough. For rams during breeding, feed 0.50.8 kg of a 14% protein ration. Use a feed trough to prevent waste and contamination. Store concentrates in dry, rat-proof containers. Aflatoxin in moldy maize or cottonseed can kill sheep. Buy from reputable suppliers and check for mold. In East Africa, many farmers use 'sheep pellets' from companies like Unga or Pembe. If you mix your own, weigh ingredients with a simple scale. For 10 ewes, a weekly ration: 10 kg maize bran, 6 kg cottonseed cake, 3 kg molasses, 1 kg mineral premix. Mix well and feed 0.5 kg per ewe per day. Adjust based on body condition. If sheep get too fat, reduce concentrates. If they lose weight, increase. Always provide clean waterconcentrates increase water needs. Avoid feeding urea to sheep unless properly mixed; urea toxicity is fatal. For smallholders, concentrate feeding is most cost-effective during late pregnancy and early lactation.

5. Mineral and Vitamin Supplements

Minerals and vitamins are needed in small amounts but are critical for health, growth, and reproduction. The most important minerals for sheep are salt (sodium and chlorine), calcium, phosphorus, selenium, copper, zinc, and cobalt. In many African soils, copper and selenium are deficient. However, sheep are sensitive to copper toxicitynever feed copper supplements meant for cattle. Provide a sheep-specific mineral lick free-choice. A good lick contains 1520% salt, 810% calcium, 46% phosphorus, and traces of zinc, manganese, and selenium. You can buy commercial blocks or make your own: mix 5 kg salt, 2 kg dicalcium phosphate, 1 kg limestone, 0.5 kg trace mineral premix, and 0.5 kg molasses. Put in a wooden box with a roof. Sheep will lick 1030 g per day. Vitamins A, D, and E are important. Green pasture provides beta-carotene (vitamin A precursor). In dry seasons, supplement with a vitamin premix or injectable vitamins. Vitamin E and selenium work together; a deficiency causes white muscle disease in lambs. In selenium-deficient areas (e.g., parts of Kenya, Tanzania, and South Africa), give ewes an injection of vitamin E/selenium 24 weeks before lambing. Vitamin B complex is synthesized in the rumen, so usually not needed. However, thiamine (B1) deficiency can occur with high-concentrate diets, causing polioencephalomalacia. If sheep show circling, head pressing, or blindness, give thiamine injection. Always provide clean waterminerals need water for absorption. Salt blocks are not enough; they lack other minerals. Loose mineral mixes are better. Store minerals in a dry place. Do not mix minerals with concentrates unless you use a premix. Over-supplementation can be toxic, especially copper and selenium. Follow label directions. For organic or free-range systems, provide mineral licks year-round. Watch for signs of deficiency: poor growth, brittle bones, poor fertility, and white muscle disease. A simple blood test can confirm deficiencies, but visual signs and response to supplementation are often enough. Consult your local vet or extension officer for specific recommendations.

6. Feeding Different Age Groups

Feeding sheep by age and stage is key to success. Newborn lambs: colostrum within the first hour is critical. Colostrum provides antibodies and energy. If a lamb cannot suckle, tube-feed 50 ml per kg body weight of warm colostrum. For the first 3 days, lambs need 200300 ml of milk per day, increasing to 11.5 liters by 4 weeks. From 1 week, offer creep feed: a mix of 18% protein pellets, maize bran, and molasses. Provide fresh water. Wean lambs at 812 weeks when they eat 200 g of creep feed per day. After weaning, feed a grower ration: 16% protein, 0.51 kg per day plus hay. Growing lambs need 0.20.4 kg of concentrate per day. For ewes: dry ewes need 0.20.3 kg concentrate plus pasture. In late pregnancy (last 6 weeks), increase to 0.50.7 kg of a 14% protein rationthis prevents pregnancy toxemia. After lambing, lactating ewes need 0.81.2 kg of a 16% protein ration plus hay. Rams: during breeding, feed 0.50.8 kg of a 14% protein ration. In off-season, 0.20.3 kg. Always provide mineral licks and clean water. For orphan lambs, use cow milk replacer or goat milk; feed 45 times daily. Never feed cold milk. In Africa, many farmers use 'kienyeji' (local) sheepthey are hardy but still need supplements during dry seasons. A simple rule: feed the best to the most productive. Lactating ewes and growing lambs get the most concentrates. Dry ewes and rams get less. Adjust based on body condition score. If a lamb is thin, increase feed. If a ewe is too fat, reduce. Introduce new feeds gradually. Always have roughage available. For sheep on zero-grazing, cut and carry fresh grass and legumes. For range sheep, provide supplements in troughs. Monitor weight monthly. A healthy lamb gains 150200 g per day. If not, check for worms and adjust ration. Proper age-based feeding ensures high survival and fast growth.

<h2>Practical Reference: Sheep Nutrition and Feeding</h2>

<p>Feeding is the single largest cost in any sheep enterprise typically 6070% of total production costs in African smallholder and commercial flocks. A ewe that is underfed at mating produces fewer lambs; a lamb that is underfed at weaning takes months longer to reach market weight. In business terms, every kilogram of feed that does not convert into meat, milk, or wool is money lost. This lesson is a working reference: keep it on the farm, use it when mixing rations, buying feed, or planning grazing. It assumes you already understand the six core lessons in this module and now need practical numbers, checklists, and decision rules for African conditions.</p>

<p>The guiding principle is <strong>match feed supply to animal demand</strong>. Demand changes with age, weight, pregnancy, lactation, and season. Supply changes with rainfall, pasture quality, and what you can afford to buy. Profitable sheep farming is the business of closing that gap at the lowest cost per unit of production not the highest level of feeding.</p>

<h3>Building a Practical Ration Worked Example</h3>

<p>Suppose you keep 50 mature ewes (average 50 kg) in late pregnancy in a dry-season setting. Table 1 tells you each ewe needs about 1.6 kg DM/day at 1214% crude protein and 6065% TDN. Your available feed is maize stover (free, but only 35% CP and 4045% TDN) and you can buy cottonseed cake (35% CP) and maize grain (9% CP, 82% TDN).</p>

<p>A simple mix per ewe per day: 1.0 kg maize stover + 0.4 kg maize grain + 0.2 kg cottonseed cake. This delivers roughly 1.35 kg DM, 1112% CP, and 5860% TDN close enough for a tropical breed. The cost per ewe per day is the business question: if cottonseed cake costs USD 0.30/kg and maize grain USD 0.25/kg, the supplement costs about USD 0.16/ewe/day, or USD 8/day for 50 ewes. Over a 60-day late-pregnancy period that is USD 480. If that investment prevents 10 lamb losses and adds 3 kg birth weight per lamb across 50 lambs, the return is typically 35 times the feed cost. That is the business case for supplementary feeding not animal welfare alone.</p>

<div style="border:3px solid #c62828; background:#ffebee; padding:16px; margin:20px 0; border-radius:6px;">
<h3 style="color:#c62828; margin-top:0;"> SAFETY WARNING Copper Toxicity and Urea Poisoning</h3>
<p><strong>Copper:</strong> Sheep are highly sensitive to copper. Never feed sheep mineral supplements or concentrates formulated for cattle, goats, or pigs these often contain copper levels that are safe for other species but toxic to sheep. Chronic copper poisoning causes liver damage and sudden death. Use only sheep-specific mineral licks. If you keep sheep and cattle together, provide separate mineral sources.</p>
<p><strong>Urea:</strong> Urea can be a cheap non-protein nitrogen source for ruminants, but incorrect use kills sheep within hours. Never feed urea to hungry sheep, never exceed 1% of the total ration, and always introduce it gradually over 23 weeks with adequate energy feed. Sheep in poor condition or on low-energy diets are at highest risk. If in doubt, do not use urea the risk to your flock and your business is too high.</p>
<p><strong>Mouldy feed:</strong> Never feed mouldy hay, silage, or concentrates. Moulds produce mycotoxins that cause abortion, reduced fertility, and liver damage. In humid African conditions, store feed off the ground on pallets, keep it dry, and use within 3 months.</p>
</div>

<h3>Seasonal Feeding Calendar Africa Context</h3>

<p>Most of sub-Saharan Africa has a distinct dry season of 36 months. Your feeding plan must be built around this. In the wet season, accumulate body condition and make hay or silage. In the dry season, draw down those reserves and supplement strategically. The critical periods are:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Flush feeding (23 weeks before mating):</strong> Improve nutrition to increase ovulation rate. Even modest supplementation with 200300 g/day of a concentrate can increase lambing percentage by 1020%.</li>
<li><strong>Early pregnancy (first 3 months):</strong> Maintain, do not overfeed. The embryo needs little extra energy, but underfeeding causes early embryo loss.</li>
<li><strong>Late pregnancy (last 6 weeks):</strong> The most critical period. 70% of foetal growth happens now. Underfeeding causes low birth weight, weak lambs, and pregnancy toxaemia in ewes.</li>
<li><strong>Lactation (first 68 weeks):</strong> Highest demand of the entire cycle. A ewe suckling twins needs 22.5 times maintenance. This is when concentrate feeding pays best.</li>
<li><strong>Weaning and dry period:</strong> Reduce feed to maintenance. This is your chance to recover body condition and reduce costs.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ol style="font-size:15px; line-height:1.8;">
<li><strong>Weigh or condition-score your ewes monthly.</strong> Body condition score (BCS) 2.53.0 at mating, 3.03.5 at lambing. If you cannot weigh, use the 15 BCS scale by hand it is free and accurate enough for decisions.</li>
<li><strong>Plan supplementary feed purchases 3 months before the dry season.</strong> Prices rise when everyone else is buying. Buy early, store properly, and you cut feed costs by 2040%.</li>
<li><strong>Never feed cattle or pig minerals to sheep.</strong> Copper toxicity kills. Keep species-specific mineral licks available at all times, especially for lactating ewes.</li>
<li><strong>Test your forage quality once per season.</strong> A simple laboratory analysis (USD 2050) tells you exactly what to supplement. Without it, you are guessing and likely overspending or underfeeding.</li>
<li><strong>Introduce any new feed over 710 days.</strong> Sudden changes cause acidosis, bloat, and production loss. Mix new feed with old at increasing ratios.</li>
<li><strong>Provide clean water at all times.</strong> A lactating ewe drinks 48 litres per day. Water restriction reduces feed intake and milk production more than any other single factor.</li>
<li><strong>Keep a simple feed record per flock.</strong> Record what you feed, how much, and the cost. Compare against lamb growth and ewe condition. This is how you find your most profitable ration.</li>
<li><strong>Make hay or silage when pasture is abundant.</strong> The cheapest feed you will ever have is the grass you conserved yourself in the wet season. Target 12 tonnes of hay per 10 ewes for dry-season security.</li>
</ol>
