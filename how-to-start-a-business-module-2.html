2. Step 2 — Validate Before You Invest

Most people spend months building something and then hope people buy it. This module teaches the opposite. You will learn how to prove demand with almost no money and almost no risk, before you commit a single dollar to equipment, stock, or a website.

Module 2 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~50 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 2.1 — Talking to 20 potential customers before spending a cent

The single most valuable thing you can do before starting a business is talk to 20 real people who might buy from you. Not your friends. Not your family. People who fit the profile of a paying customer and who have no obligation to be polite.

This is called customer discovery. It is free. It is fast. And it will save you months of wasted effort if the answer is "I wouldn't buy that" or "I already have someone who does that cheaper."

Here is how to do it properly.

Step 1 — Identify who you are talking to. Write down the three types of people who would most benefit from your product. For example, if you want to start a bakery: (a) office workers near your location who buy breakfast, (b) mothers who buy bread for family, (c) small shops who would stock your loaves.

Step 2 — Approach them naturally. Do not walk up with a clipboard. Go where they already are. The office worker at the coffee stand in the morning. The mother at the market. The shopkeeper during a quiet hour. Introduce yourself honestly: "Hi, I am thinking of starting a bakery nearby and I want to ask your opinion. Do you have 5 minutes?"

Step 3 — Ask about their current behaviour, not your idea. The biggest mistake beginners make is asking "would you buy my thing?" People say yes to be polite. Instead ask what they already do:

  • Where do you buy bread today?
  • How often do you buy it?
  • What do you pay?
  • What would make you switch to a new bakery?
  • What is missing from where you buy now?

Behaviour is truth. If they already buy bread from someone for the same price, that is a real market. If they say "I never buy bread," that is real data too — and it might mean your bakery location is wrong.

The 20-person rule: Do not commit a single dollar to your business until you have had 20 real conversations with potential customers. 20 is the minimum before patterns emerge. 5 is a hobby. 50 is a business plan.
Practical exercise 2.1: Write down the three types of customers you would sell to. For each type, list 3 places you could naturally talk to them this week. Total: 9 conversation opportunities. Get started today.

Lesson 2.2 — The pre-sale test — sell it before you make it

The strongest validation is a pre-sale. Someone gives you money for something that does not yet exist, based on your promise. If they will do this, the market exists. If nobody will, you have not found a real business yet.

This is not a scam and it is not dishonest. Big companies do it all the time — from Kickstarter campaigns to airlines selling tickets on planes that do not exist yet. The only rule is: if you take money, you must deliver within the promised window, or return the money in full. Always.

Here is how to do a pre-sale on almost no capital:

  • Simple offer. "I am opening a cake business next month. If you pre-order a cake this week, you pay $12 today and I deliver on [date]. Normal price will be $15." You have not built anything yet — no kitchen, no equipment. Just a promise and a date.
  • WhatsApp status or Facebook post. Not a fancy website. A message to your contacts. A post on the community group where you already exist. If you cannot get 5 pre-orders from the people who already know you, getting 50 strangers will be much harder.
  • Small deposit, not full price. Asking for 20–50% deposit lowers the barrier and is still a real signal. If they will not give you even 50% for something they have never seen, they are not a customer yet.
  • Cap the pre-sale. Take only the number of orders you can realistically fulfill. Ten cakes, not a hundred. Then deliver every single one perfectly, because those ten customers are your future testimonials and referrals.
Never take a pre-sale you cannot deliver. If you cannot make the cakes, do not sell the cakes. Taking money and failing to deliver is a scam, and it destroys both your reputation and possibly your legal standing. Pre-sales are a tool for validation — not for funding.

Pre-sales work for products. For services, the equivalent is a signed agreement or a paid consultation. For courses, it is a paid enrolment before the course launches. Every business has a version of this test. Find yours and run it before you spend on anything else.

Practical exercise 2.2: Write out a pre-sale offer you could make this week. What is the product, what is the price, what is the delivery date, and how will you contact the first 20 people? Send the offer to at least 10 people by the end of this week.

Lesson 2.3 — Competitor pricing research

You need to know exactly what your competitors charge and what they deliver for that price. Without this, you are pricing blind — and pricing blind is how new businesses either overcharge and get no customers, or undercharge and lose money on every sale.

Here is the simple way to research competitor pricing in one day.

Step 1 — List the competitors. Find 5–10 businesses that sell something similar to what you want to sell. Same product, same area, same customer type. Ideally within 5 km of where you plan to operate.

Step 2 — Visit each one and record four things. Price of the closest item to yours. What is included (size, quality, extras). How busy they were when you visited. What customers complained about, if you overheard anything.

Step 3 — Build a simple table.

CompetitorPriceWhat is includedBusy?Weakness
Shop A$1.20Small loaf, plainVeryLong queues
Shop B$1.50Large loaf, wholewheatMediumPoor location
Shop C$1.00Stale by 2pmLowQuality drops

Now you can see the landscape. Shop A is doing well on volume but customers wait. Shop B has a premium product but not enough foot traffic. Shop C is cheap but inconsistent. Every one of these weaknesses is an opportunity for you.

Pricing rule: Never be the cheapest just to win customers. The cheapest price attracts the customers who will leave you the moment someone cheaper appears. Compete on quality, reliability, or convenience — not price.

Write down your target price based on the range you found. If everyone charges $1.20 and you can make a better loaf for $1.40, that is a valid position — but only if you can tell customers why the extra 20 cents is worth it in one sentence. Quality of ingredients, freshness, delivery, personalisation, support — pick one.

Practical exercise 2.3: Spend half a day visiting 5 competitors in your area. Fill out the table with real numbers. Note their prices, what they include, and one weakness each. Your target price at the end.

Lesson 2.4 — Free samples and prototypes

Once you know what your competitors charge, you need to prove your product is worth your price. Free samples are the fastest way to do this — and they are much cheaper than any advertising.

But free samples do not mean "give it away to anyone." A good sample is targeted and deliberate.

Rule 1 — Sample the right people, not the easy people. Handing out samples to friends and family feels nice but teaches you nothing. They will say it is delicious to make you happy. Sample strangers who match your target customer profile. That is where you get real data.

Rule 2 — Give 20 samples in one session. Not 3. Not 5. Twenty. This is enough to start seeing patterns: does everyone love it, or do 6 out of 20 say it is too sweet, too small, too expensive? Patterns need numbers.

Rule 3 — Have a follow-up question ready. After they taste, ask: "On a scale of 1 to 10, how likely are you to buy this at $X?" If 15 out of 20 say 8 or higher, you have a product. If most say 6 or lower, you need to change something. Price, size, flavour, packaging — one of them is off.

Rule 4 — Ask for the sale at the end. The best sample sessions end with "Do you want to buy one now for $X?" A small percentage will say yes. That is your second strongest validation signal, after pre-sales.

Do not sample for too long. Sampling is validation, not a business model. If after 100 samples over 3 sessions you still have not converted a single person to a paid customer, something is wrong. Step back and rethink the product, price, or audience.

Prototypes work the same way for physical products. Build one rough version, show it to 20 people, ask for honest feedback, and improve. Do not spend money on tooling, packaging, or inventory until the prototype is genuinely loved by at least 15 out of 20 potential customers.

Practical exercise 2.4: Make or source 20 units of your product this week. Sample them to 20 real potential customers over 2 days. Record their rating (1–10) and whether they bought. Calculate your "love rate" — the percentage who rated 8 or higher.

Lesson 2.5 — The 3 signs to walk away from an idea

Validation is not just about finding reasons to move forward. It is also about finding reasons to walk away. Every failed small business is one where the founder ignored an obvious warning sign, because they had already fallen in love with the idea.

Here are the three signs that mean you should stop and rethink.

Sign 1 — Nobody will pay, even at a discount. You offered 20 samples and 0 people bought. You pre-sold to nobody. You gave a 50% off launch deal and got 1 sale. The market does not exist at any price you can afford. Walk away from this specific idea. There are hundreds of businesses — this one is not the one.

Sign 2 — You cannot make it profitably. To sell at a competitive price, you would need to spend more on materials than the item sells for. Or you would need to work 16 hours a day to earn $10. This is not a business — it is a volunteer job. Either find a way to lower costs, raise quality so you can charge more, or find a different product.

Sign 3 — Every customer wants something different. You tested with 20 people. Each one wants a different flavour, size, colour, or feature. You cannot satisfy all of them, and any one version only pleases 3 or 4. This means your market is too fragmented to serve with one product. Either narrow your customer type sharply, or pick a different product.

Walk-away is not failure. It is the cheapest lesson you will ever get. It cost you a few hours and a few samples. The alternative is spending 6 months and $3,000 to learn the same lesson the hard way.

The best entrepreneurs kill ideas fast. They test cheaply, they ask honestly, and when the data says stop — they stop. Then they test the next idea. Two or three quick failures are worth far more than one slow one.

Practical exercise 2.5: After your validation tests, honestly ask yourself if any of the three walk-away signs apply. If yes, write out why, in one page. If no, write out which of the positive signals you have seen, and how strong they are.

Lesson 2.6 — The 3 signs to double down

Just as important as knowing when to walk away: knowing when to double down. If you see these three signs, it is time to invest more — with confidence.

Sign 1 — People pay before you deliver. You ran a pre-sale and got real money. Not promises. Not "I will definitely buy." Actual payments. This is the single strongest signal you can get. If 10 people paid you money for something that did not exist yet, they are telling you the market exists — go make the thing.

Sign 2 — Customers refer their friends. After you delivered your first 10 orders, at least 2 or 3 new customers came from people telling their friends and family. That is organic word of mouth, the only marketing that genuinely works for small businesses in the early days. If it is already happening at this scale, it will compound.

Sign 3 — Customers ask for more. They ask if you can also make this larger size. Also deliver to this area. Also offer this service. Also do this special order. When customers start asking for extensions, they are telling you what to build next — and they will pay for it.

SignalWeakStrong
Pre-sales0–2 pay8+ pay before delivery
ReferralsNone yet2+ from first 10 customers
Customer requestsNobody asks for moreMultiple ask for extensions

When two or three of these signals are strong, you have earned the right to invest. Buy the equipment. Rent the space. Hire the helper. Register the business. Your validation work has produced real evidence — not hope.

Practical exercise 2.6: Write down which of the three signs you have seen, and how strongly. If two or more are strong, write a plan for what to invest in next. If none are strong, plan another round of validation — more samples, more pre-sales, more conversations.
