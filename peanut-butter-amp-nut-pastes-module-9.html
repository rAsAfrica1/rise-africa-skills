1. Identifying Your Target Customers: Households, Shops, Restaurants

Before you sell a single jar, you need to know who will buy your peanut butter. Your customers fall into three main groups: households, shops, and restaurants. Each group has different needs, buying habits, and budgets. Start by looking at your own community. How many families live nearby? Are there small grocery stores, kiosks, or supermarkets? Are there hotels, cafes, or street food vendors? Write down every potential customer you can think of. For households, focus on families who cook at home. They buy in small quantitiesoften 250g or 500g jarsand care about taste, price, and health. A mother of three might buy one jar per week. If you sell to 50 families, that's 50 jars a week. For shops, think about local dukas, minimarts, and market stalls. They buy in bulkmaybe 10 to 20 jars at a timeand want a good profit margin. Offer them a wholesale price, say 20% less than retail. Restaurants, hotels, and catering services use peanut butter in sauces, sandwiches, and desserts. They need consistent supply and larger containers, like 5kg buckets. Visit them with samples and a price list. To identify your best targets, ask yourself: Who has money to spend? Who buys regularly? Who is easy to reach? For example, if you live near a school, teachers and parents are good household targets. If there's a busy market, shops there are ideal. Don't try to sell to everyone at once. Pick one group firstmaybe householdsand master that. Then expand. Keep a notebook with names, phone numbers, and what they buy. This list becomes your gold mine. Remember, in Africa, word of mouth is powerful. A happy household customer will tell friends. A shop that sells your product will reorder. A restaurant that uses your peanut butter will recommend you. So treat every customer like a partner.

2. Selling at Local Markets: Setting Up an Attractive Stall

A market stall is one of the best ways to sell your nut pastes. But with so many stalls, yours must stand out. First, choose the right market. Go for one with high foot trafficnear a bus park, a mosque, a church, or a school. Visit on different days to see when people shop. In many African markets, mornings are busy for fresh produce, while evenings attract workers buying dinner ingredients. Set up your stall early, before the crowds arrive. Your stall should be clean, colourful, and organised. Use a wooden table or a sturdy box covered with a bright clothgreen, yellow, or red attract attention. Arrange your jars in neat rows. Use small labels with clear prices written in bold. If you have different sizes (250g, 500g, 1kg), group them. Add a small bowl of open peanut butter with a spoon for tasting. People love to taste before they buy. Bring a clean spoon and small pieces of bread or crackers for sampling. Keep your area tidyno dirty cloths or empty boxes lying around. A clean stall says your product is safe and high quality. Price your jars competitively. Check what others charge. If a 500g jar of imported peanut butter costs 5,000 shillings, you can sell yours for 3,500 to 4,000 shillings and still make a profit. Offer a small discount for buying two jars. Have a notebook to record sales and customer contacts. Smile and greet everyone who passes. Say, 'Good morning! Would you like to taste my fresh peanut butter?' Don't sit silently. Engage people. If someone buys, thank them and ask them to tell friends. At the end of the day, count your money and note what sold best. Over time, you'll learn what works. A good stall can sell 30 to 50 jars a day. That's a solid income. And many customers will become regulars.

3. Using Social Media and WhatsApp to Promote Your Product

Social media and WhatsApp are powerful tools to sell your nut pastes, even if you have no money for advertising. Start with WhatsApp. Create a broadcast list of all your contactsfriends, family, past customers. Send a short message with a clear photo of your jars. Write something like: 'Fresh peanut butter, no additives, 500g for 3,500 shillings. Free delivery within town. Reply to order.' Use WhatsApp Status to post daily photos or short videos of you making the peanut butter, packing orders, or customers enjoying it. People trust what they see. For Facebook, join local buy-and-sell groups in your area. Post your product with a price and a phone number. Respond quickly to comments and messages. On Instagram, use hashtags like #PeanutButterAfrica #NaturalNutPaste #MadeInKenya (or your country). Post behind-the-scenes content: roasting peanuts, grinding, filling jars. Show the processit builds trust. TikTok is great for short, fun videos. Record a 15-second clip of you stirring a jar, or a customer tasting and smiling. Add a trending sound. You can also collaborate with small food influencers or bloggers. Offer them a free jar in exchange for an honest review. For all platforms, keep your message consistent: healthy, tasty, locally made. Use a simple logo or your photo as a profile picture. Always include your phone number and location. Respond to every inquiry within an hour if possible. If someone asks a question, answer politely and ask for their order. Use WhatsApp to confirm orders and share delivery updates. You can also create a simple catalogue using WhatsApp Business appit's free. List your products, prices, and delivery options. Many customers prefer to order via WhatsApp because it's personal. Remember, social media is not just about selling; it's about building relationships. Share tips about healthy eating, recipes using peanut butter, or stories about your business. The more you engage, the more people will buy. Aim to post at least 3 times a week. Consistency beats perfection.

4. Building Relationships with Retailers and Distributors

Selling directly to consumers is good, but to grow, you need retailers and distributors. Retailers are shop owners who buy your jars and resell them. Distributors buy in large quantities and supply many shops. Building strong relationships with them means steady orders and less time selling. Start by identifying potential retailers: small supermarkets, dukas, kiosks, health food stores, and even pharmacies. Visit them in person. Dress neatly, carry samples, and a price list. Introduce yourself and your product. Explain why your peanut butter is betternatural, no preservatives, good taste. Offer a wholesale price. For example, if you retail a 500g jar at 4,000 shillings, sell to retailers at 3,200 shillings. That gives them 800 shillings profit per jar. They will be motivated to push your product. For distributors, look for businesses that already supply shops with other goodslike cooking oil, rice, or soap. They have vans and routes. Offer them a special distributor price, say 2,800 shillings per jar, and a minimum order of 50 jars. Provide them with free display stands or posters. Always deliver on time. If you promise a delivery on Tuesday, be there on Tuesday. Keep records of each retailer's orders, payments, and feedback. Follow up regularlyvisit or call every two weeks. Ask: 'How are sales? Do you need more?' If a retailer has slow sales, offer to help by doing a tasting demo at their shop. Build trust by being reliable and honest. If a jar has a problem, replace it immediately. Word spreads fast among shop owners. A good relationship with one retailer can lead to introductions to others. Also, consider offering a small commission to shop assistants who recommend your product. This motivates them. Over time, you can have 20 or 30 retailers selling for you, and you focus on production. That's how you scale.

5. Offering Free Samples and Demonstrations

Free samples and demonstrations are the fastest way to turn a stranger into a customer. People are more likely to buy something they have tasted and seen. So plan to give out small samples regularly. For samples, use small spoons or pieces of bread. A 500g jar can provide about 30 small tastes. That costs you one jar, but can lead to many sales. For example, if you give 30 tastes and 10 people buy a jar, you've made a profit. Choose high-traffic locations: outside a busy shop, at a market entrance, near a school gate at closing time, or at a church after service. Always ask permission from the shop owner or market leader first. Set up a small table with your jars, a bowl of peanut butter, and a pack of disposable spoons or bread pieces. Wear clean clothes and a smile. Greet people warmly: 'Good afternoon! Would you like to taste my fresh peanut butter? It's natural and healthy.' When they taste, watch their reaction. If they smile, say: 'A 500g jar is only 3,500 shillings. Would you like one?' If they hesitate, offer a small discount for buying today. Keep the demonstration lively. You can also show how to use peanut butter in a quick recipelike spreading on bread or adding to porridge. For restaurants and hotels, offer a free 1kg sample for their chef to try. Follow up after three days to ask for feedback and an order. For shops, offer a free jar for the owner to taste, and a small display stand. Track your samples: note how many you give and how many sales result. Aim for at least a 30% conversion rate. If you give 50 samples, you should get 15 sales. If not, adjust your pitch or location. Always collect phone numbers of people who taste but don't buy. Send them a WhatsApp message the next day: 'Thank you for tasting. Here's a special offer for you.' This personal touch works. Demonstrations also build trust because people see you are proud of your product. Do them at least twice a week.

6. Participating in Trade Fairs and Food Exhibitions

Trade fairs and food exhibitions are golden opportunities to showcase your nut pastes to a large audience, including potential distributors, retailers, and even exporters. They happen in cities and towns across Africalike the Nairobi International Trade Fair, the Lagos International Trade Fair, or local agricultural shows. To participate, start by finding out about upcoming events. Ask at your local chamber of commerce, ministry of trade, or farmers' association. Some fairs are free for small businesses; others charge a fee, maybe 5,000 to 20,000 shillings for a small booth. If the fee is too high, consider sharing a booth with another food producer. Once you register, prepare your booth to stand out. Use bright colours, a clean table, and a banner with your business name and phone number. Bring plenty of samplessmall tasting spoons and bread pieces. Have jars for sale and wholesale price lists. Also bring business cards or flyers with your contact details. During the fair, be active. Don't sit behind the table. Stand in front, greet people, and offer tastes. Talk about your product's benefits: no additives, locally made, supports farmers. Collect contact details of everyone interesteduse a notebook or a simple form. Ask: 'Would you like to stock my product in your shop?' If they say yes, note their name and number. After the fair, follow up within three days. Send a WhatsApp message: 'Thank you for visiting my booth. Here is my price list. Let's do business.' Many people who taste at fairs become long-term customers or retailers. Also, attend workshops or networking sessions at the fair. Meet other food producers, suppliers, and buyers. Exchange ideas. You might find a distributor who covers a region you can't reach. Trade fairs also give you feedback. Listen to what people say about your taste, packaging, and price. Use that to improve. Even if you only get a few orders, the exposure and contacts are worth it. Aim to participate in at least one fair per year. It can transform your business.

<h1>Practical Reference: Marketing and Selling Your Nut Pastes</h1>
<p>This practical reference supports the RISE AFRICA skills module <strong>Marketing and Selling Your Nut Pastes</strong>. It is written for small and growing agribusinesses in Africa that process groundnut (peanut), cashew, sesame, or mixed nut pastes and want to sell profitably into households, shops, restaurants, markets, and beyond. The focus is on actions you can take this week: identifying who buys, setting up a stall that attracts attention, using WhatsApp and social media, building retailer relationships, running demonstrations, and showing up well at trade fairs.</p>

<h2>1. Identifying Your Target Customers: Households, Shops, Restaurants</h2>
<p>Your product is not for "everyone." The fastest route to sales is to choose two or three customer groups and tailor your packaging, price, and message to them. In most African markets, nut pastes sell through four main channels: direct-to-household, small retail shops (dukas, kiosks, minimarts), food service (restaurants, hotels, caterers, school feeding programmes), and institutional buyers (NGOs, processors, exporters).</p>
<p><strong>Households</strong> buy in small volumes frequently. They care about taste, freshness, hygiene, and price per spoon or per small jar. <strong>Shops</strong> care about shelf life, margin, consistent supply, and packaging that does not leak or attract pests. <strong>Restaurants</strong> care about consistency, bulk pricing, food safety documentation, and reliable delivery schedules. <strong>Distributors</strong> care about volume, credit terms, and whether the product moves off shelves.</p>
<p>A simple segmentation exercise: list every customer you sold to in the last month. Group them by channel. Calculate revenue and gross margin per channel. You will usually find that one channel pays better than the others. Double down there before expanding.</p>

<h2>2. Selling at Local Markets: Setting Up an Attractive Stall</h2>
<p>At a busy market, buyers decide in seconds. An attractive stall signals quality and trust. Use height, colour, and cleanliness. Stack jars or tubs on a raised table covered with a clean cloth. Use a bright banner with your brand name in large letters. Display open sample jars so buyers can smell and taste. Keep a small hand-washing station (jerrycan with tap, soap, clean towel) this is both a hygiene requirement and a powerful sales signal.</p>
<p>Price clearly. Use a chalkboard or printed price card showing price per jar, per sachet, and per bulk tub. Offer a "market special" bundle (e.g., buy two small jars, get one sachet free). Keep your money separate from food. Never handle cash and then touch product without washing hands. Train one assistant to serve while you engage buyers.</p>

<h2>3. Using Social Media and WhatsApp to Promote Your Product</h2>
<p>WhatsApp is the highest-ROI channel for most African nut paste businesses. Build a broadcast list or WhatsApp Business catalogue with clear product photos, prices, and delivery options. Post three times a week: a product photo, a customer testimonial, and a short video of your production or a recipe (e.g., groundnut sauce, peanut butter sandwiches, nut paste in porridge).</p>
<p>On Facebook and Instagram, use local language and English. Tag your location so nearby buyers find you. On TikTok, short recipe videos and "behind the scenes" clips of roasting and grinding perform well. Always include a call to action: "Order on WhatsApp: 07XX XXX XXX" or "Free delivery within 5 km."</p>
<p>Track what works. Note which posts generate orders. Respond within one hour during business hours. Save every customer number and segment them: households, shops, restaurants. Send monthly promotions to shops, recipe ideas to households.</p>

<h2>4. Building Relationships with Retailers and Distributors</h2>
<p>Retailers and distributors multiply your sales, but they need reasons to stock you. Prepare a one-page sell sheet: product name, sizes, wholesale price, recommended retail price, shelf life, and your contact. Offer a starter pack of mixed sizes. Agree on clear terms: delivery day, payment terms (cash on delivery is safest for new relationships), and a return policy for damaged stock.</p>
<p>Visit shops weekly. Check shelf placement, restock, and talk to the shopkeeper about what customers say. Offer a small incentive for prominent placement (e.g., a free display stand). For distributors, provide marketing support: posters, sample sachets, and a WhatsApp group for orders.</p>

<h2>5. Offering Free Samples and Demonstrations</h2>
<p>Samples convert strangers into buyers. At markets and shops, offer a small taste on a clean spoon or a piece of bread. Keep a log of who tasted and what they said. Run demonstrations at community events, churches, mosques, markets, and school open days. Show how to use nut paste in local dishes. A 10-minute demonstration with 30 tasters can generate 1015 immediate sales and several repeat customers.</p>
<p>For restaurants and hotels, prepare a free sample pack with a recipe card. Follow up within 48 hours. For shops, leave a free sample jar for the shopkeeper's family they become your best salespeople.</p>

<h2>6. Participating in Trade Fairs and Food Exhibitions</h2>
<p>Trade fairs put you in front of buyers you cannot reach by walking the market. In Africa, relevant events include national agricultural shows, SIAL, Africa Food Show, and regional trade fairs. Prepare: branded booth, product samples, sell sheets, business cards, and a visitor log. Collect contact details and follow up within one week.</p>
<p>Before the fair, set a goal: e.g., 20 distributor leads, 5 restaurant trials, 100 sample tastings. After the fair, send a WhatsApp message to every contact with a photo of your product and a special offer. Fairs are expensive; measure return on investment by counting orders and leads generated.</p>

<h3 style="color:#b71c1c;margin-top:0"> SAFETY WARNING Food Safety and Aflatoxin Control</h3>
<p style="margin-bottom:0;color:#333">Peanuts and other nuts can carry <strong>aflatoxins</strong>, which are invisible, tasteless, and dangerous at low levels. Aflatoxin is a serious health risk (liver cancer) and can destroy your business if buyers test your product and reject it. <strong>Never sell nut paste made from mouldy, discoloured, or damp nuts.</strong> Buy from trusted suppliers, sort and remove damaged nuts, store raw nuts in dry, well-ventilated conditions below 10% moisture, and test your finished product where possible. Use clean, food-grade packaging and label with a production and expiry date. If you suspect contamination, discard the batch do not blend it with good stock. Food safety is your licence to operate.</p>
