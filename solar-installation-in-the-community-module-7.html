1. Pre-Commissioning Checks

Before you turn on any solar system, you must do a thorough pre-commissioning check. This step prevents damage and ensures safety. Start by inspecting all mechanical connections: panels, mounting brackets, and cable entries. Look for loose bolts, corrosion, or water ingress. In dusty African conditions, check that all conduit and cable glands are sealed tight.

Next, verify electrical connections. Use a multimeter to check continuity on all DC cables. Ensure positive and negative are not reversed. Measure the open-circuit voltage (Voc) of each string. For a typical 24V panel, Voc should be around 44V. If a string reads significantly lower, there may be a bad connection or a faulty panel. Also check that the combiner box fuses and breakers are correctly rated and seated.

Battery checks are critical. For lead-acid batteries, check electrolyte levels and top up with distilled water if needed. Measure the specific gravity with a hydrometer â it should be around 1.265 for a fully charged cell. For lithium batteries, check the voltage and ensure the BMS is functioning. Confirm that battery cables are tight and properly torqued.

Finally, inspect the inverter and charge controller. Ensure they are mounted in a shaded, ventilated area. Check that all settings match the system design: battery type, charge voltage, and load priorities. Do a visual check for any loose wires or debris inside enclosures. Document all readings on a checklist. This pre-check takes about an hour but saves days of troubleshooting later.

2. Start-Up Procedures for Solar Systems

Starting up a solar system in the correct order is essential to avoid sparks, damage, or electric shock. Follow these steps carefully. First, ensure all loads are switched off. This means turning off the main breaker in the distribution board and unplugging any appliances.

Next, connect the battery bank to the charge controller and inverter. Always connect the battery first â this powers up the controller and lets it recognise the system voltage. Use a torque wrench to tighten battery terminals to the manufacturer's specification, typically 5-7 Nm for M8 bolts. If you connect panels first, the controller may be damaged.

Now, turn on the DC breaker between the battery and the controller. Check that the controller display shows battery voltage and no error codes. Then, switch on the PV array breaker. The controller should start charging. Measure the charging current with a clamp meter â it should be roughly 10-20% of the battery capacity in amps. For a 200Ah battery, expect 20-40A.

After the controller is stable, turn on the inverter. Listen for any abnormal humming or beeping. Check the inverter display for input voltage and output voltage. It should show around 230V AC for a standard African household. Finally, turn on loads one by one, starting with small loads like lights, then larger ones like fridges. Monitor the inverter for overload warnings. If everything works, your system is commissioned. Always wear PPE: insulated gloves and safety glasses.

3. Measuring Voltage, Current, and Power

To test a solar system, you need to measure voltage, current, and power accurately. Use a digital multimeter (DMM) for voltage and a clamp meter for current. For power, you can calculate it as voltage times current, or use a watt meter.

Measuring voltage: Set your multimeter to DC volts for solar panels and batteries. For AC volts, measure at the inverter output. To measure panel open-circuit voltage (Voc), disconnect the panel from the controller and place the probes on the panel terminals. For a 12V panel, Voc is about 22V; for 24V, about 44V. For battery voltage, measure across the terminals. A fully charged 12V lead-acid battery should read 12.7V; a 24V system should read 25.4V.

Measuring current: Use a clamp meter around a single cable. Set it to DC amps for the PV or battery side, and AC amps for the load side. Clamp only one wire â if you clamp both positive and negative, the magnetic fields cancel and you get zero. For a 300W panel, expect around 8-9A in full sun. For a 1kW inverter, AC current at 230V is about 4.3A.

Measuring power: Multiply voltage by current. For example, if your panel voltage is 18V and current is 5A, power is 90W. You can also use a plug-in power meter for AC loads. Compare your measured power to the panel's rated power. If it's less than 70% on a clear day, there may be shading, dirt, or a faulty panel. Always take measurements at noon on a sunny day for consistency. Record all values in a logbook.

4. Performance Ratio and Efficiency Calculations

Performance ratio (PR) tells you how well your solar system is performing compared to its ideal output. It accounts for losses from heat, dust, wiring, and inverter inefficiency. A good PR for a small off-grid system in Africa is 0.7 to 0.8 (70-80%).

To calculate PR, you need the actual energy produced (kWh) and the theoretical energy the panels could produce. Theoretical energy = panel rating (kW) Ã peak sun hours (PSH). For most of Africa, PSH is 4.5 to 6.0. For example, a 1kW system in Kenya with 5 PSH should produce 5 kWh per day. If it actually produces 3.8 kWh, PR = 3.8 / 5 = 0.76 or 76%.

Efficiency of the panels themselves is different. It's the ratio of power output to solar irradiance. For a 300W panel with an area of 1.6 mÂ², at 1000 W/mÂ² irradiance, efficiency = 300 / (1000 Ã 1.6) = 18.75%. Typical panels are 15-20% efficient. You can measure irradiance with a pyranometer, but for field work, use a simple solar power meter.

Battery efficiency also matters. Lead-acid batteries are 80-85% efficient; lithium is 95%. To calculate round-trip efficiency, divide energy out by energy in. If you put 1 kWh into a battery and get 0.85 kWh out, efficiency is 85%. Inverter efficiency is usually 90-95%. Multiply all efficiencies to get system efficiency. For example, 0.95 (panels) Ã 0.85 (battery) Ã 0.92 (inverter) = 0.74 or 74%. If your PR is below 0.6, investigate shading, dirty panels, or faulty components. Keep records to track performance over time.

5. Troubleshooting Common Issues

Even well-installed solar systems can have problems. Here are common issues and how to fix them. First, low power output. Check for shading â even a small shadow on one panel can reduce string output by 50%. Clean panels with water and a soft brush; dust in the Sahel can cut output by 20% in a week. Also check for loose connections. Use a multimeter to measure voltage at each panel and compare to others.

Second, battery not charging. If the controller shows no charging, check the PV breaker and fuses. Measure the PV input voltage at the controller â it should be higher than battery voltage by at least 5V. If the battery is hot or gassing, it may be overcharging; check the controller settings. For lead-acid, equalise every 3 months. If the battery won't hold charge, it may be sulfated â replace it.

Third, inverter alarms. Common alarms include overload, over-temperature, and low battery. If overload, reduce loads â turn off high-power appliances like irons or kettles. If over-temperature, improve ventilation or clean dust from fans. If low battery, check charging as above. Never ignore alarms; they protect your equipment.

Fourth, flickering lights or frequent shutdowns. This often means loose AC wiring or a bad neutral. Tighten all terminals in the distribution board. Check that the inverter output voltage is stable (230V Â±10%). If it fluctuates, the inverter may be faulty.

Fifth, ground faults. If the system trips a residual current device (RCD), there may be a short circuit or water in a junction box. Isolate sections and test with a megohmmeter. Always follow safety procedures: turn off all power before working on wiring.

6. Documenting Commissioning Results

Documentation is not just paperwork â it's proof that your system works and a tool for future maintenance. After commissioning, you must record all measurements and observations. Use a standard commissioning sheet. Include the date, location, installer name, and system specifications (panel wattage, battery capacity, inverter rating).

Record all electrical readings: open-circuit voltage (Voc) for each string, battery voltage, charging current, inverter output voltage and frequency. Also note environmental conditions: temperature, cloud cover, and time of day. For example, 'Voc string 1: 88.2V, battery: 25.6V, charging: 12A, inverter out: 230V, 50Hz, sunny, 32Â°C.'

Take photos of the installation: panels, wiring, controller settings, and battery bank. These help if you need to troubleshoot later. Also document any issues found during commissioning and how they were fixed. For instance, 'Replaced faulty MC4 connector on panel 3.'

Give a copy of the commissioning report to the client. Explain the key readings and what they mean. Show them how to check basic parameters daily. Also leave a maintenance log where they can record any problems. Finally, file a copy for your own records. In many African countries, you may need to submit a commissioning certificate to the utility or for warranty. Keep everything organised â it saves time and builds trust.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Commissioning & Performance Testing for Solar Installations

This practical reference is written for technicians, installers and small EPC businesses working on solar PV systems in African communities â from 200 W off-grid home systems to 5â10 kW hybrid mini-grid installations. Commissioning is the point where your technical work meets your commercial reputation. A system that is correctly commissioned on day one generates fewer call-backs, protects your margin, and builds the word-of-mouth that wins your next contract. Use this lesson as a field manual: it consolidates the checks, measurements, formulas and documentation you need to hand over a system that performs as promised.

Why Commissioning Is a Business Activity, Not Just a Technical One

In many African markets, solar companies compete on trust. A client in Kisumu, Kumasi or Gaborone who has paid for a 3 kW hybrid system expects the lights to stay on. If the system underperforms because of a loose MC4 connector, a shaded array, or a mis-set charge controller, the cost is not only the return visit â it is the referral you lose. Commissioning evidence (photos, measurement sheets, signed handover) also supports warranty claims with suppliers and can be required by financiers, NGOs or government programmes such as those funded under the Africa Minigrids Program (AMP) or national rural electrification agencies.

Pre-Commissioning Checks

Before you energise anything, verify the installation against the design. Walk the site with the as-built drawing and check:

  • Mechanical integrity: mounting rails torqued, roof penetrations sealed, array tilt and azimuth as designed, no shading between 9 a.m. and 3 p.m.
  • DC wiring: correct polarity at every string, string voltages balanced, cable glands tight, UV-rated conduit used outdoors.
  • Earthing and lightning protection: array frame, combiner box and inverter bonded to a single earth electrode with measured resistance typically below 5â10 Î© depending on local code.
  • Labels and signage: DC/AC warnings, string numbering, emergency shutdown instructions in a language the client reads.
  • Battery bank (if present): correct terminal torque, ventilation, temperature sensor fitted, state of charge recorded.
  • Inverter and controller settings: battery type, charge profile, grid code (if grid-tied), and anti-islanding enabled.

Start-Up Procedures

Follow the manufacturer's sequence â never improvise. A typical hybrid system start-up is:

  1. Confirm all DC and AC isolators are open and the inverter is off.
  2. Measure array open-circuit voltage (Voc) and confirm polarity on every string.
  3. Close the DC combiner isolator and check the inverter's DC input reading matches your meter (within ~2%).
  4. Close the AC output isolator (and grid breaker if grid-tied); confirm the inverter detects grid voltage and frequency within limits (e.g. 230 V Â±10%, 50 Hz Â±1 Hz in most African grids).
  5. Switch the inverter on and let it complete its self-test and synchronisation.
  6. For battery systems, connect the battery breaker last, verify charge current direction, and set the correct charge profile.
  7. Apply load gradually and observe for alarms, unusual noise or heating.

PV arrays generate voltage whenever exposed to light and cannot be switched off at the source. A single string of 8â10 modules can produce 300â500 V DC â enough to cause fatal shock or sustain a dangerous arc. Always:

  • Isolate and lock off DC before working on conductors; use a DC-rated isolator, never an AC breaker.
  • Wear insulated gloves, use 1000 V CAT III/IV rated instruments, and work with a second person present.
  • Cover the array with an opaque sheet if you must work on live DC conductors.
  • Never assume a "dead" string is safe â measure it. On hot African afternoons, irradiance and voltage can rise within seconds after cloud cover clears.

Troubleshooting Common Issues

Most commissioning faults fall into a handful of categories. Match the symptom to the likely cause before you start swapping parts:

  • Low or zero array voltage: reversed polarity, blown string fuse, open MC4, or a disconnected string in the combiner.
  • One string reads much lower than others: shading, soiling, a cracked module, or a bypass diode failure.
  • Inverter trips on grid fault: grid voltage or frequency outside the configured window â common on weak rural networks; check grid code settings with the utility.
  • Battery not charging: wrong charge profile, blown battery fuse, BMS communication fault, or temperature sensor disconnected.
  • PR below 0.70: soiling, undersized cable causing voltage drop, inverter clipping, or a mismatch between array and inverter.
  • Intermittent output: loose terminal, thermal derating, or a failing DC isolator.

Documenting Commissioning Results

Your commissioning report is a commercial asset. It should include: site and client details; system description and serial numbers; as-built single-line diagram; all measurement values with instrument model and calibration date; photos of the array, labels, earthing and inverter display; a signed client handover and training record; and a maintenance schedule. Keep a digital copy and give the client a printed folder â this is often the first professional documentation they have received, and it differentiates you from informal installers.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Walk the site and verify array tilt, azimuth, shading and mechanical fixings against the design.
  2. Confirm DC polarity, string balance and earth continuity before energising.
  3. Record string Voc and Isc with a calibrated meter under stable irradiance.
  4. Verify inverter DC and AC readings match your instruments within Â±2%.
  5. Confirm grid voltage, frequency and anti-islanding settings (grid-tied systems).
  6. Check battery charge profile, BMS status and ventilation (battery systems).
  7. Calculate Performance Ratio and compare against the design target.
  8. Complete the commissioning report, photograph everything, and train the client on shutdown and basic checks.
