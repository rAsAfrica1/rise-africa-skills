Sourcing, costing and formulating with insect meal, plant proteins and unusual animal proteins, including what the regulators have and have not approved, and how to keep a diet complete when the protein source changes.

Lesson 6.1~12 min

Insect Protein for Companion Animals

In this lesson
  • State the current regulatory position on black soldier fly larvae in pet food and its limits
  • Calculate cost per kilogram of protein for insect meal against conventional meals
  • List the quality specifications to demand from an insect meal supplier

Insect meal is the most promising alternative protein for African pet food producers, for one simple reason: the raw material is already here. Black soldier fly is farmed commercially in Kenya, Uganda, Ghana, South Africa and elsewhere, often on brewery and market waste, and the fly does not need imported inputs.

Start with what is actually approved, because this is where most sales pitches overreach. Dried black soldier fly larvae was tentatively approved in August 2021 for use in salmonid, poultry and swine feed and in adult dog food. Read that carefully. It covers adult dog food only. The reviewing body was not at that time reviewing black soldier fly larvae for adult cat food, and growth and puppy food is not covered either. Black soldier fly larvae is also the only insect product that has successfully gone through that ingredient definition process, so cricket meal and mealworm meal do not have the same standing. Whether any of this has changed since 2021 is not established in the verified reference, so confirm it yourself before you print anything.

The definition also tells you what to demand from a supplier. The ingredient must be dried by artificial means to no more than 10 percent moisture, and it must be labelled with a minimum crude protein and a minimum crude fat. If your supplier cannot give you those three figures on a document, you are not buying a defined ingredient, you are buying dried insects. No maximum inclusion level is specified in the definition, which does not mean you can use any amount; it means nobody has set a limit and you must set your own from trials.

In Europe, insect proteins from seven species were authorised for farmed fish, poultry and pig feed under a 2017 regulation. Whether that regulation authorises pet food use as distinct from food-producing animals is not clear from the verified reference and should be confirmed against the original legal text before any European claim. Substrate is the other legal issue: farmed insects are fed plant-based substrates such as spent brewery grains, fruits, vegetables and other vegetable or cereal by-products, and in Europe manure and catering waste are excluded. Ask your supplier in writing what the larvae were reared on, and keep the answer. In an African context this is your food safety control as much as a legal one.

Nutritionally, insect meal typically runs around 55 percent protein with average digestibility around 80 percent, varying with species and processing. Both dogs and cats will eat it, with palatability preferences differing between the two species. Two caveats you should treat as unconfirmed but plan around: chitin contributes nitrogen and therefore inflates crude protein measured by the Kjeldahl method while being poorly digestible, so crude protein may overstate available protein; and methionine and lysine profiles vary by species. Both are reasons to specify an amino acid analysis rather than a protein number alone.

Now cost it properly. Cost per kilogram of protein is price divided by protein expressed as a decimal. Insect meal at USD 1.90 per kilogram and 55 percent protein is 1.90 divided by 0.55 = USD 3.45 per kilogram of crude protein. Fish meal at USD 1.55 and 60 percent protein is 1.55 divided by 0.60 = USD 2.58. Poultry by-product meal at USD 1.10 and 58 percent is USD 1.90. Soybean meal at USD 0.62 and 44 percent is USD 1.41. On crude protein alone, insect meal is the most expensive of the four. Now adjust for digestibility using the 80 percent figure: digestible protein is 55 x 0.80 = 44 percent, so the cost per kilogram of digestible protein is 1.90 divided by 0.44 = USD 4.32. For comparison, apparent crude protein digestibility of a bone protein meal in a Beagle trial ran between 78.0 and 82.4 percent depending on inclusion, so the rendered meals are in a similar digestibility band and keep their price advantage. Insect meal today is a premium ingredient, not a cheap one, and it must earn its place through a marketing story, a hypoallergenic positioning or a supply advantage rather than through price.

An example from Kisumu, Kenya. A producer buys black soldier fly meal from a local farm at USD 1.90 a kilogram delivered, and includes it at 8 percent in an adult dog food, contributing 8 x 0.55 = 4.4 percentage points of crude protein at an ingredient cost of 0.08 x 1.90 = USD 0.152 per kilogram of feed. She sells that food at a 35 percent premium as a locally sourced sustainable line to two Nairobi pet shops. The premium, not the ingredient cost, is what makes the line work.

Lesson 6.2~12 min

Plant-Based Protein Blending

In this lesson
  • Blend two plant proteins to hit a target crude protein using Pearson's Square
  • Identify the limiting amino acids in a high-plant dog formulation
  • Explain why a plant-based cat food cannot be made safe from the verified standards

Plant proteins are the cheapest protein you can buy in most African markets, and they are also the easiest way to build a diet that looks right on the label and is wrong in the animal. This lesson is about using them correctly, not about avoiding them.

Deal with cats first, and briefly, because the answer is settled. Cats are obligate carnivores. The British Veterinary Association's position is that it is much harder or even impossible to meet their nutritional needs without feeding them meat. Look at why, using the profile. Taurine is required at 0.10 percent of dry matter in dry food and 0.20 percent in canned, and it is absent from plants. Preformed vitamin A is required at 3332 IU per kilogram for adult maintenance because cats cannot cleave beta-carotene. Arachidonic acid is required at 0.02 percent and is absent from vegetable oils. Niacin is required at 60 mg per kilogram and cannot be met through tryptophan. Arginine is required at 1.04 percent, higher than any other domestic species you will formulate for. Each of these can in theory be added synthetically, but the objection is bioavailability and the absence of large-scale long-term studies. Do not make a vegan cat food.

Dogs are a different case. Dogs are omnivores, and the profile lists no taurine, no arachidonic acid and no preformed vitamin A requirement for them. That is why the dog case is arguable where the cat case is not. But the same veterinary position warns that it is much easier to get the balance of essential nutrients wrong than to get it right, and concludes that the jury is still very much out. The honest commercial position is to use plant protein as part of a mixed diet, not to sell a vegan dog food as a health product.

Now the practical work. In a high-plant dog formulation the binding constraints are the sulphur amino acids and lysine. The dog adult minima are methionine plus cystine 0.65 percent of dry matter, methionine alone 0.33 percent, lysine 0.63 percent, threonine 0.48 percent and tryptophan 0.16 percent. Cereals are short of lysine; oilseed meals are short of methionine. Blending the two covers more ground than either alone, and synthetic DL-methionine and L-lysine are cheap insurance.

Use Pearson's Square to hit a protein target with two ingredients. Suppose you want a 24 percent crude protein mixed portion from soybean meal at 44 percent and maize at 9 percent. Draw the square: put 24 in the middle, 44 top left, 9 bottom left. Subtract diagonally, always taking the smaller from the larger. 24 minus 9 = 15 parts of soybean meal. 44 minus 24 = 20 parts of maize. Total 35 parts. So soybean meal is 15 divided by 35 = 42.9 percent of the blend and maize is 20 divided by 35 = 57.1 percent. Check it: 0.429 x 44 = 18.9, plus 0.571 x 9 = 5.1, total 24.0 percent. The method is worked in full in Module 7 Lesson 2 and you will use it constantly.

Cost that blend. Soybean meal at USD 0.62 and maize at USD 0.41 gives 0.429 x 0.62 = 0.266 plus 0.571 x 0.41 = 0.234, that is USD 0.50 per kilogram of blend at 24 percent protein, which is 0.50 divided by 0.24 = USD 2.08 per kilogram of crude protein. Compare that with fish meal at USD 2.58. The saving is real, which is exactly why plant inclusion creeps up in small operations.

Know the penalty. A systematic review of 17 studies found soybean meal reduced dry matter digestibility in most studies, increased crude protein digestibility in several, decreased fat digestibility in multiple studies, and reduced gross energy digestibility across most trials. No maximum inclusion consensus exists, because the methods were too varied to set one. Soy also carries oligosaccharides that drive flatulence and stool softening and trypsin inhibitors that need heat inactivation, which is standard teaching but not confirmed in the verified reference. Practically: heat-treat your soy, watch stool scores, and do not let plant protein climb just because the spreadsheet says it can.

Two more constraints for a high-plant dog diet: phytate binds zinc and iron, so check against the dog minima of 80 mg per kilogram zinc and 40 mg per kilogram iron for adults; and vitamin B12 must come from animal material or supplement, at 0.028 mg per kilogram for dogs.

An example from Lusaka, Zambia. A producer replaced 6 percent fish meal with 10 percent soybean meal plus 0.15 percent DL-methionine. Protein held, the ingredient cost fell by about USD 0.055 per kilogram of feed, and three customers reported softer stools in week two. She reduced the swap to 6 percent soy and the stools recovered. That is the correct process: change one thing, measure, adjust.

Lesson 6.3~12 min

Novel Protein and Allergy Management

In this lesson
  • Define what makes a protein novel for a specific animal in a specific market
  • Design a mill cleaning and sequencing routine that protects a single-protein product
  • Cost the production penalty of a genuinely single-protein run

A novel protein is one the animal has never eaten. That is the whole definition, and it means novelty is a property of the individual animal and of your local market, not of the ingredient. Where diagnosis is concerned, see Module 9 Lessons 1 and 2, which cover food allergy against food intolerance and the elimination trial that a veterinarian runs. This lesson is about the part you own: sourcing a protein nobody in your town is feeding, and producing it without contamination.

Start with the market survey, because it decides everything. Walk three pet shops and one supermarket and write down the named protein in every dog and cat food on the shelf. In most African cities the list is short: chicken, beef, fish, lamb and a great deal of unnamed meat and animal derivatives. Anything not on that list is a candidate. Rabbit, duck, goat, camel, tilapia offcuts, guinea fowl and insect meal are all realistic in different countries. Insect meal has a particular advantage here, because almost no animal has previously eaten it, though remember from Lesson 1 that its approval covers adult dog food only.

The hard part is not the ingredient. It is proving the food contains nothing else. A limited-ingredient diet that carries traces of chicken from the previous batch is worthless to the animal it was bought for, and the owner will find out within two weeks. Three controls make this workable in a small mill.

  1. Sequence your production. Run the single-protein product first, at the start of the week, on clean equipment, before any chicken or beef batch. Never run it after.
  2. Flush the mill. Push 15 to 20 kilograms of the same cereal used in the novel diet through the mixer, mill and conveyor, then discard or divert that flush to a general-purpose product. On a 200 kilogram batch a 15 kilogram flush is 7.5 percent of the batch weight and its full cost must be carried by the novel product.
  3. Dedicate what you can. Separate scoops, separate buckets, colour-coded and stored apart. Scoops cost a few dollars and are the commonest cross-contamination route in a small operation.

Cost the penalty honestly. Suppose your standard dog food costs USD 0.92 a kilogram in ingredients. A rabbit-based novel diet with rabbit trimming at USD 2.40 a kilogram at 22 percent inclusion, cassava flour, oil and premix, comes to USD 1.46 a kilogram. Add the flush: 15 kilograms of cereal at USD 0.41 is USD 6.15 spread over a 200 kilogram batch, that is USD 0.031 per kilogram. Add an extra hour of cleaning labour at USD 1.50 and that is USD 0.0075 per kilogram. Total USD 1.50 a kilogram, against USD 0.92 for the standard food, a 63 percent higher cost. Batches are also smaller, because demand is smaller, so your fixed costs per kilogram rise as well. Price accordingly; do not sell a novel protein diet at a normal margin.

Supply is the second risk and it is worse than for ordinary ingredients. Rabbit and duck supply in most African cities is thin, seasonal and dependent on one or two farmers. Before you launch, get a written monthly volume commitment, identify a second supplier, and decide in advance what you will tell customers if supply fails. Changing the protein in a novel protein diet without telling the customer is the single fastest way to destroy the product, because the animal will react and the owner will blame you correctly.

One labelling point that carries real risk. If you claim a single protein, the ingredient list must be true and short, and you must be able to show a batch record proving what went in. Keep a retained sample of every batch of a novel diet, 200 grams in a sealed labelled bag, for at least the shelf life of the product. If a customer reports a reaction, that sample is the only thing that lets you answer.

An example from Arusha, Tanzania. A producer runs a goat-based limited-ingredient dog food in 120 kilogram batches once a month, always on the first Monday, always first after a full clean. She buys goat trimmings from two butchers under a standing arrangement at USD 1.85 a kilogram, freezes what she cannot use, and sells the product only through two veterinary clinics so that every buyer has been advised by a vet. Her price is USD 3.20 a kilogram against USD 2.10 for her standard line, and the clinics keep the product credible.

Lesson 6.4~12 min

Nutritional Completeness in Alternative Formulas

In this lesson
  • Audit an alternative-protein recipe against every species-specific requirement
  • Calculate whether a blended protein diet meets the amino acid minima
  • Decide when a formulation must be verified by laboratory analysis rather than by calculation

Every time you change the protein source you change more than protein. This lesson is the checklist that stops an alternative formula from quietly failing, and the arithmetic that proves it does not.

Work through the cat requirements first, because the cat is unforgiving. Adult maintenance needs 26.0 percent crude protein and 9.0 percent crude fat on a dry matter basis, and note that unlike the dog the fat minimum does not fall for adult maintenance. Then the five cat-specific items: taurine at 0.10 percent of dry matter for extruded food and 0.20 percent for canned; arachidonic acid at 0.02 percent, which must come pre-formed from animal fat because vegetable oils contain none; preformed vitamin A at 3332 IU per kilogram, since beta-carotene cannot be converted; niacin at 60 mg per kilogram, which tryptophan will not supply; and arginine at 1.04 percent, far above the dog's 0.51 percent. Any alternative protein that reduces animal fat or animal tissue in a cat food puts at least three of those five at risk at once.

The canned taurine figure is doubled to 0.20 percent for a reason worth knowing: moist processing and higher gut losses reduce taurine bioavailability. If you move a product from dry to wet format, the taurine specification changes even though the recipe looks the same.

For dogs the audit is shorter but not trivial. Adult maintenance is 18.0 percent protein and 5.5 percent fat, calcium 0.5 and phosphorus 0.4 percent with a phosphorus maximum of 1.6, zinc 80 and iron 40 mg per kilogram, vitamin A 5000 IU, vitamin D between 500 IU and the ceiling of 3000 IU per kilogram of dry matter, and vitamin E 50 IU. The amino acids to watch when plant or insect protein replaces meat are lysine at 0.63 percent, methionine plus cystine at 0.65 percent, threonine at 0.48 and tryptophan at 0.16.

Now do the amino acid arithmetic properly, because most small producers skip it. Suppose a dog food contains 25 percent soybean meal, 10 percent black soldier fly meal and 30 percent maize. Take lysine contents of 2.75 percent in soybean meal, 3.0 percent in insect meal and 0.25 percent in maize, using your supplier's own analysis rather than a book. Contributions are 25 x 0.0275 = 0.688, 10 x 0.030 = 0.300, and 30 x 0.0025 = 0.075. Total 1.063 percent lysine as fed. Convert to dry matter at 10 percent moisture by dividing by 0.90: 1.181 percent, comfortably above the 0.63 percent minimum. Repeat the same three lines for methionine plus cystine, and this is where plant-heavy diets usually fail; if the total is below 0.65 percent on a dry matter basis, add DL-methionine. At USD 4.80 a kilogram, adding 0.1 percent of the diet costs 0.001 x 4.80 = USD 0.0048 per kilogram of feed, which is trivial against the risk.

There is a limit to what calculation can do. Published tables and supplier declarations describe an average ingredient, not your sack. For an alternative protein you have never used, calculation gets you a candidate recipe and nothing more. Send a sample of the finished food to a laboratory for crude protein, crude fat, moisture, ash, crude fibre, calcium and phosphorus as a minimum, and for a cat food add taurine. Budget roughly USD 60 to USD 140 for a proximate panel plus minerals in most African commercial laboratories, and treat it as the cost of being allowed to make a completeness claim at all.

One cautionary case shows why. There is an established example of a single-cell cultured protein tested in adult dogs at 0, 4, 6 and 8 percent inclusion over six months, where apparent total tract digestibility of organic matter, protein and energy all exceeded 80 percent, and the conclusion was that inclusion up to 8 percent of the total diet was safe and provided sufficient nutrition. But copper digestibility in that same study was only around 20 percent. A single mineral behaving badly inside an otherwise excellent ingredient is exactly the sort of thing you will not predict from a protein number.

An example from Kampala, Uganda. A producer moved a cat food from 12 percent fish meal to 6 percent fish meal plus 8 percent insect meal to cut cost. Protein held at 30 percent. The laboratory report showed taurine at 0.07 percent, below the 0.10 percent minimum, because the fish meal had been carrying it. She added supplemental taurine at 0.05 percent of the diet, costing about USD 0.03 per kilogram of feed, and retested. Without the analysis she would have shipped a deficient cat food for months.

Lesson 6.5~12 min

Transition Protocols

In this lesson
  • Write a day-by-day transition schedule from an old diet to an alternative-protein diet
  • Run a stepwise inclusion trial before committing a new ingredient to full production
  • Handle feline food neophobia without abandoning a sound formulation

A good formulation rejected by the animal is a failed product. Transition is the step where most alternative protein launches are lost, and it costs almost nothing to do properly.

Use a seven to ten day changeover as the standard instruction on every pack and in every conversation. A workable schedule for a dog eating 300 grams a day: days 1 and 2, 225 g old and 75 g new, a quarter new; days 3 and 4, 150 g and 150 g; days 5 and 6, 75 g and 225 g; day 7 onward, 300 g new. For a sensitive animal or an unusual protein, stretch it to ten or fourteen days by holding each step two extra days. Give the owner the grams, written down. Percentages get misread; weights do not.

Instruct the owner to score stools at every step, using the same 1 to 5 scale used elsewhere in this course, where 1 is very hard and 5 is liquid. If stool loosens by more than one point, go back a step for three days and then move forward more slowly. The point of a staged transition is not politeness, it is that you get an early, cheap signal before the whole household is affected.

Cats need a different approach. Cats are neophobic about food and will reject an abrupt change outright, sometimes refusing to eat at all, which in an overweight cat is genuinely dangerous. Start a cat transition at ten percent new food, not twenty-five, and take fourteen days. Warm the food slightly to release aroma, feed in a clean shallow dish away from the litter tray, and never leave the new food down all day to become stale. If a cat has refused all food for more than 24 hours, that is a veterinary matter, not a feeding matter.

Now the trial you run before the product ever reaches a customer. Do not go from zero to full inclusion of a new protein. Use a stepwise inclusion design, which is exactly how a cultured single-cell protein was assessed in adult dogs: inclusion at 0, 4, 6 and 8 percent of the diet, over a six month safety phase, with digestibility and health parameters measured. You cannot run a six month controlled study, but you can run the same shape at a small scale.

  1. Make three small batches at, say, 0, 5 and 10 percent inclusion of the new ingredient, 20 kilograms each.
  2. Recruit at least six healthy adult animals whose owners agree in writing, two per level, and feed for 21 days after transition.
  3. Record body weight at day 0, 10 and 21; stool score daily; food refusal events; and coat and skin comments.
  4. If the 10 percent group loosens or refuses, your practical ceiling is 5 percent, whatever the literature says.
  5. Write the result down and keep it. That page is your evidence when a customer or a vet asks why you use the ingredient.

Budget it. Three 20 kilogram batches at about USD 1.20 a kilogram in ingredients is USD 72. Six owners given free food for a month costs you the same USD 72 in retail terms but buys you six reference customers. A basic proximate analysis on the chosen level adds USD 60 to USD 140. The whole exercise is under USD 250 and it is the difference between a product you can defend and a guess.

Palatability deserves one specific test. Set two identical bowls, old food and new food, weigh both, offer for fifteen minutes, and weigh what is left. Do it for three days, swapping left and right each day so the animal is not simply choosing a side. If the new food is consistently eaten at less than half the rate of the old, fix palatability before launch, usually with fat coating or a palatant, and remember that dried allium seasonings must never be used because onion and garlic are toxic, with cats the most susceptible species at around 5 g/kg of raw onion and garlic three to five times more toxic than onion.

An example from Nakuru, Kenya. A producer launched an insect protein dog food with no transition advice and had four bags returned in the first month for loose stools. She reprinted the bag with a seven day gram-by-gram schedule on the back panel, added a card in every bag, and returns fell to zero on the next 60 bags. The reprint cost USD 140. The four returned bags plus the reputation cost more.

Lesson 6.6~12 min

Cost Analysis and Supply Chain

In this lesson
  • Compare protein sources on cost per kilogram of digestible protein rather than price per kilogram
  • Adjust ingredient prices to a dry matter basis before comparing them
  • Build a supply plan that survives a two-month failure of any single ingredient

Alternative proteins live or die on arithmetic that most small producers never do. Price per kilogram of ingredient tells you almost nothing. Three corrections turn it into a number you can decide on.

Correction one, dry matter. You pay for water. An ingredient at 12 percent moisture delivers 0.88 kilograms of dry matter per kilogram bought; at 30 percent moisture it delivers 0.70. A wet brewery by-product at USD 0.18 a kilogram and 72 percent moisture costs 0.18 divided by 0.28 = USD 0.64 per kilogram of dry matter, which is not cheap at all once you have paid to transport and dry it.

Correction two, protein concentration. Divide price by protein as a decimal. Work a full table at this week's prices. Fish meal USD 1.55 at 60 percent protein is USD 2.58 per kilogram of protein. Poultry by-product meal USD 1.10 at 58 percent is USD 1.90. Soybean meal USD 0.62 at 44 percent is USD 1.41. Black soldier fly meal USD 1.90 at 55 percent is USD 3.45. Sunflower cake USD 0.34 at 28 percent is USD 1.21. Cottonseed cake USD 0.40 at 32 percent is USD 1.25.

Correction three, digestibility. Insect meal averages about 80 percent digestibility, and a bone protein meal in a controlled Beagle trial showed apparent crude protein digestibility between 78.0 and 82.4 percent depending on inclusion. So insect meal at 55 x 0.80 = 44 percent digestible protein costs 1.90 divided by 0.44 = USD 4.32 per kilogram of digestible protein. Where you have no digestibility figure, say so and do not guess. Ask your supplier; if they cannot answer, that itself is information.

Now the trap. The cheapest column is never the answer, because amino acid quality, ash, palatability and stool quality are not in the price. Sunflower cake at USD 1.21 per kilogram of protein looks superb until you remember it is short of lysine and high in fibre. The correct use of the table is to rank candidates, then test the top two or three against the constraints in Lesson 4.

Supply chain is where alternative proteins really cost money. Run this checklist on every new ingredient before you commit a recipe to it.

  1. How many suppliers exist within one day's travel? If the answer is one, you have a single point of failure.
  2. What months of the year is it available? Write the twelve months down and mark the gaps.
  3. What is the minimum order, and can you store it? An ingredient sold in 1000 kilogram lots is useless if you make 200 kilogram batches and have nowhere dry to keep it.
  4. How much does the price move? Ask for the price on the same date twelve months ago.
  5. What happens to your recipe if it disappears for eight weeks? Write the substitute recipe now, and check it against the profile now.

Stock cover arithmetic makes the point. If you use 8 percent insect meal in a food and you make 2000 kilograms a month, you need 160 kilograms a month. Two months of cover is 320 kilograms, which at USD 1.90 is USD 608 of cash sitting in a store. That capital is the real price of using a scarce ingredient, and it must appear in your working capital plan alongside the figures in Module 12 Lesson 6.

A hedge worth using: design your recipe so that no single alternative ingredient exceeds about 10 percent of the formula. At that level a supply failure is a reformulation you can absorb, not a shutdown. Above 20 percent, your business is that supplier's business.

An example from Tamale, Ghana. A producer costed three protein routes for the same 25 percent protein dog food. Route A, poultry by-product meal only, ingredient cost USD 0.94 a kilogram. Route B, 8 percent insect meal replacing part of the poultry meal, USD 1.03. Route C, insect meal at 20 percent, USD 1.31. She sold route B as a sustainability line at USD 2.60 against USD 2.10, so the extra USD 0.09 of cost bought USD 0.50 of price. Route C did not pay, because the customer would not accept a further increase and the single insect supplier could not commit to 400 kilograms a month.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. What use was dried black soldier fly larvae tentatively approved for in August 2021?

The approval covered salmonid, poultry and swine feed and adult dog food. Cat food was not under review and growth diets are not covered.

2. What moisture specification is written into the ingredient definition?

The definition requires the ingredient to be dried by artificial means to no more than 10 percent moisture and labelled with minimum crude protein and crude fat.

3. Insect meal at USD 1.90 per kilogram and 55 percent protein costs how much per kilogram of crude protein?

1.90 divided by 0.55 is USD 3.45. USD 4.32 is the figure after adjusting for 80 percent digestibility, and USD 2.58 is fish meal.

4. Why might crude protein overstate the value of insect meal?

Chitin adds to Kjeldahl crude protein without being well digested, so an amino acid analysis is worth more than a protein number alone. Treat this as a planning assumption rather than a confirmed figure.

5. Why must you ask what the larvae were reared on?

Farmed insects are reared on plant-based substrates such as spent brewery grains and vegetable by-products, with manure and catering waste excluded in the EU. Getting the answer in writing protects you legally and on safety.

6. Which nutrients make a plant-based cat diet unsafe from the profile alone?

All four are required by cats and are absent or unusable from plant sources, and the cat cannot substitute beta-carotene for vitamin A or tryptophan for niacin.

7. Using Pearson's Square for a 24 percent blend from 44 percent soybean meal and 9 percent maize, how many parts of soybean meal?

24 minus 9 is 15 parts of soybean meal, and 44 minus 24 is 20 parts of maize, giving 35 total parts and 42.9 percent soybean meal.

8. What did the systematic review find about soybean meal and digestibility?

The review found mixed effects and explicitly declined to set a maximum inclusion because the methodologies were too heterogeneous.

9. Why is the dog case for plant protein arguable where the cat case is not?

Those are exactly the nutrients that plants cannot supply to a cat. Dogs are omnivores, though the veterinary position remains that balance is easy to get wrong.

10. A producer swaps fish meal for soy and customers report softer stools. What is the right response?

Soy contributes oligosaccharides that soften stool. Changing one variable, measuring the result and adjusting is the correct method for a small producer.

11. What makes a protein novel?

Novelty is a property of the animal's own dietary history and of what is on sale locally, which is why a market survey comes before the ingredient choice.

12. When should a single-protein batch be produced?

Running it first on clean equipment prevents carryover from a previous batch. A limited-ingredient diet contaminated with chicken is worthless to the animal it was bought for.

13. A 15 kg cereal flush at USD 0.41 per kilogram on a 200 kg batch adds how much per kilogram of product?

15 x 0.41 = USD 6.15, divided by 200 kg = USD 0.031 per kilogram. The full cost of the flush belongs to the novel product.

14. Your rabbit supplier fails for two months. What is the correct action?

Changing the protein without telling the customer causes a reaction in the animal and destroys the product's credibility. Supply continuity must be planned before launch.

15. Where does the diagnosis of a food allergy belong?

Diagnosis is clinical work covered in Module 9. The producer owns the formulation, the contamination control and the batch record.

16. Why is the canned taurine minimum 0.20 percent against 0.10 percent for dry food?

The doubled figure compensates for reduced bioavailability in moist products, so moving a product from dry to wet changes the specification.

17. A diet has 25 percent soybean meal at 2.75 percent lysine, 10 percent insect meal at 3.0 percent and 30 percent maize at 0.25 percent. What is lysine as fed?

0.688 plus 0.300 plus 0.075 is 1.063 percent as fed. Dividing by 0.90 for 10 percent moisture gives 1.181 percent on a dry matter basis.

18. Which nutrient behaved poorly in the cultured protein safety study despite good overall results?

Organic matter, protein and energy all exceeded 80 percent apparent digestibility, but copper was around 20 percent, which is the kind of surprise calculation will not catch.

19. When is laboratory analysis of the finished food essential?

Published tables describe an average ingredient, not your sack. Analysis is the cost of being entitled to make a completeness claim.

20. Replacing half the fish meal in a cat food with insect meal held protein at 30 percent but taurine fell to 0.07 percent. What happened?

Animal ingredients carry taurine. Removing them while holding crude protein constant leaves a deficiency that only analysis or supplementation will catch.

21. What is the standard changeover period for a dog moving to a new food?

A staged seven to ten day transition gives an early signal from stool scores before the change is complete, and can be stretched for sensitive animals.

22. Why should a cat transition start at 10 percent rather than 25 percent?

Food neophobia in cats can lead to outright refusal, which is dangerous in an overweight cat. A slower fourteen day transition is safer.

23. What inclusion levels were used in the published cultured protein safety study?

The study used 0, 4, 6 and 8 percent over a six month safety phase and concluded that up to 8 percent of the total diet was safe in adult dogs.

24. In a two-bowl palatability test, why swap the bowls left and right each day?

Position preference will otherwise be mistaken for food preference. Weigh both bowls before and after a fifteen minute offer, over three days.

25. Which flavouring must never be used to improve palatability?

Allium species are toxic, cats are the most susceptible, and garlic is three to five times more toxic than onion. Dried powders in flavourings and broths are the concentrated hazard.

26. A wet by-product costs USD 0.18 per kilogram at 72 percent moisture. What is the cost per kilogram of dry matter?

Dry matter is 28 percent, so 0.18 divided by 0.28 is USD 0.64 per kilogram of dry matter. You pay to transport and dry the water.

27. Which of these has the lowest cost per kilogram of crude protein at the prices given?

0.62 divided by 0.44 is USD 1.41, against USD 2.58, USD 3.45 and USD 1.90 for the others.

28. Why is the cheapest cost per kilogram of protein not automatically the right choice?

The table ranks candidates. The winner still has to pass the amino acid and completeness checks and a stool quality trial.

29. You use 8 percent of an ingredient in 2000 kg of feed a month. How much do you need for two months of cover?

8 percent of 2000 kg is 160 kg a month, so two months is 320 kg, which at USD 1.90 is USD 608 of working capital tied up in a store.

30. What is the practical reason to keep any single alternative protein under about 10 percent of the formula?

Exposure to a thin, seasonal or single-supplier ingredient is a business risk. Limiting inclusion limits the damage when supply fails.

Module 6 capstone

Build a costed alternative-protein reformulation of one product you already make or intend to make. Step 1: list every protein ingredient in your current recipe with its inclusion percentage, its crude protein percentage, the price you were quoted this week in USD per kilogram, and the supplier's name and telephone number. Step 2: find at least three alternative protein sources you can actually buy within a day's travel. At least one must be an insect meal, an oilseed cake or a by-product stream that you have not used before. For each, record the price, the crude protein on the label or from a laboratory, the moisture, the minimum order quantity, the lead time and how many months of the year it is available. Step 3: calculate cost per kilogram of crude protein for every ingredient, old and new, by dividing price by protein as a decimal. Rank them. Step 4: build one reformulated recipe that replaces at least 20 percent of your current protein with an alternative source, and check it against the profile: for a dog food, 18.0 percent crude protein and 5.5 percent fat on a dry matter basis, and for a cat food 26.0 percent protein, 9.0 percent fat, 0.10 percent taurine, 0.02 percent arachidonic acid, 60 mg per kilogram niacin and 3332 IU per kilogram preformed vitamin A. Step 5: cost a 100 kilogram batch of the old and new recipes side by side and state the saving or the extra cost per kilogram. Step 6: write a one-page supply risk note saying what you will do if the new ingredient becomes unavailable for two months. Submit the ingredient table, both recipes, both costings and the risk note.
