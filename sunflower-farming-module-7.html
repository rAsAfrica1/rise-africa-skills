1. Common Weeds in Sunflower Fields

Weeds are the silent thieves of your sunflower harvest. In Africa, the most common weeds in sunflower fields include striga (witchweed), blackjack (Bidens pilosa), pigweed (Amaranthus spp.), goosegrass (Eleusine indica), and couch grass (Cynodon dactylon). Striga is especially devastating in dry areas, attaching to sunflower roots and stunting growth by up to 70%. Blackjack and pigweed germinate fast and compete for water and nutrients, reducing yields by 30-50% if uncontrolled. Couch grass spreads through underground rhizomes, forming dense mats that smother young sunflower seedlings.

Identify weeds early. Striga appears as small purple or pink flowers close to the ground; it emerges 4-6 weeks after planting. Blackjack has yellow flowers and sticky seeds that cling to clothing. Pigweed has red stems and tiny green flowers. Goosegrass grows in clumps with finger-like seed heads. Couch grass has long, white underground stems. Scout your field weekly, especially in the first 6 weeks after planting, because sunflower is most vulnerable to competition during this period.

Weed seeds can remain viable in soil for years. Striga seeds survive up to 20 years, so rotation with non-host crops like cowpea or groundnut helps reduce the seed bank. Keep field edges clean because weeds spread from borders. Remove weeds before they flower and set seedone pigweed plant can produce 100,000 seeds. In Africa, many farmers hand-pull striga, but this must be done before flowering to prevent seed production. Mulching with crop residues suppresses blackjack and pigweed. For couch grass, deep ploughing exposes rhizomes to sun, killing them.

Prevention is cheaper than control. Use certified sunflower seed free of weed seeds. Clean tools and machinery between fields. Plant in rows to make hoeing easier. A dense sunflower canopy (plant population of 40,000-60,000 plants per hectare) shades out late-emerging weeds. Remember, early weed control in the first 4-6 weeks gives the biggest yield boost. A weed-free field can yield 1.5-2 tonnes per hectare, while a heavily infested field may yield less than 0.5 tonnes. Act early, act often.

2. Cultural and Mechanical Weed Control

Cultural and mechanical methods are the backbone of weed management for African sunflower farmers, especially where herbicides are expensive or unavailable. Start with land preparation: plough and harrow two to three times before planting to kill germinating weeds. A good seedbed free of weeds gives sunflower a head start. Plant when soil moisture is adequate so sunflower germinates quickly and outcompetes weeds.

Hand hoeing is the most common mechanical method. Hoe when weeds are smallbefore they reach 10 cm tall. In sunflower, the critical period for weed control is the first 4-6 weeks after emergence. Two hoeings are usually enough: first at 2-3 weeks after planting, second at 5-6 weeks. Hoe shallowly (3-5 cm deep) to avoid damaging sunflower roots. In rows spaced 75 cm apart, you can use a hand hoe or a push hoe. For larger fields, animal-drawn cultivators (e.g., ox-drawn) save labour. A single ox-drawn cultivator can weed one hectare in a day, compared to 10-15 person-days with hand hoes.

Mulching is a powerful cultural method. Spread 2-3 tonnes of crop residues (maize stover, grass, or rice straw) per hectare between sunflower rows. This smothers weeds, conserves moisture, and adds organic matter. Mulching reduces weed emergence by 60-80%. But avoid mulching with materials that contain weed seeds or rhizomes. Intercropping with legumes like cowpea or groundnut also suppresses weeds and fixes nitrogen. Plant sunflower and legumes in alternating rows; the legume canopy covers the ground and reduces weed growth.

Crop rotation breaks weed cycles. Rotate sunflower with cereals like maize or sorghum, which have different growth habits and allow different weed control practices. Avoid planting sunflower after sunflower, as this builds up sunflower-specific weeds like striga. Competitive varieties: choose sunflower varieties with vigorous early growth and large leaves that shade weeds. In Africa, open-pollinated varieties like 'Record' or 'Peredovik' are popular, but hybrid varieties often have better competitive ability. Finally, keep field borders and irrigation channels clean. Weeds from these areas spread into your crop. A combination of timely hoeing, mulching, and rotation can reduce weed pressure by 70-90% without any herbicide.

3. Herbicide Selection and Application Timing

Herbicides can be a powerful tool, but they must be chosen and timed correctly. For sunflower in Africa, common herbicides include pendimethalin (e.g., Stomp), S-metolachlor (e.g., Dual Gold), and oxyfluorfen (e.g., Goal). These are pre-emergence herbicides applied to the soil before weeds emerge. For post-emergence control, farmers can use quizalofop-p-ethyl (e.g., Targa) for grass weeds, or imazamox (e.g., Beyond) for broadleaf weeds, but only on herbicide-tolerant sunflower varieties. Always check the label for sunflower registration.

Timing is critical. Pre-emergence herbicides must be applied within 24-48 hours after planting, before weeds germinate. Apply to moist soil and incorporate lightly with a rake or harrow if the label recommends it. Pendimethalin at 1.5-2.0 litres per hectare in 200-300 litres of water gives good control of pigweed, blackjack, and goosegrass. S-metolachlor at 1.0-1.5 litres per hectare controls grasses and some broadleaves. Do not apply pre-emergence herbicides if heavy rain is expected within 24 hours, as they may leach away.

Post-emergence herbicides are applied when weeds are small (2-4 leaves) and sunflower is at the 2-4 leaf stage. Quizalofop-p-ethyl at 0.5-1.0 litres per hectare controls couch grass, goosegrass, and other grasses. Apply with a knapsack sprayer using a flat fan nozzle. Spray in the morning or evening when wind is low (less than 10 km/h) to avoid drift. Do not spray when sunflower is stressed by drought or waterlogging. Always add a surfactant if recommended.

Safety first: wear gloves, boots, and a mask. Never eat or smoke while spraying. Store herbicides away from food and children. Calibrate your sprayer before use: spray water on a 10 m x 10 m area, measure output, and adjust. A common mistake is under-dosing, which allows weeds to survive, or over-dosing, which harms sunflower. Follow the label exactly. In Africa, counterfeit herbicides are common; buy from reputable agro-dealers. Rotate herbicides with different modes of action to prevent resistance. For example, use pendimethalin (Group K1) one season and S-metolachlor (Group K3) the next. Always keep records of what you applied, when, and at what rate.

4. Integrated Weed Management Strategies

Integrated Weed Management (IWM) combines cultural, mechanical, biological, and chemical methods to control weeds effectively and sustainably. For African sunflower farmers, IWM means not relying on one method alone. Start with prevention: use clean seed, clean equipment, and avoid moving infested soil between fields. Rotate cropssunflower after maize or sorghum breaks weed cycles. Plant cover crops like cowpea or lablab during the off-season to smother weeds and improve soil.

Mechanical control remains central. Two timely hoeings (at 2-3 and 5-6 weeks after planting) give 70-80% weed control. For larger farms, animal-drawn or tractor-mounted cultivators are efficient. Mulching with 2-3 tonnes per hectare of crop residues reduces weed emergence by 60-80% and conserves moisture. Intercropping sunflower with legumes like groundnut or cowpea suppresses weeds and provides extra income. A study in Tanzania showed that sunflower-cowpea intercropping reduced weed biomass by 50% compared to sole sunflower.

Chemical control is used strategically. Apply pre-emergence herbicide (e.g., pendimethalin) at planting, then follow with one hand weeding at 4 weeks if needed. This reduces herbicide use and costs. For post-emergence, use selective herbicides like quizalofop-p-ethyl for grasses. Always rotate herbicide groups to prevent resistance. Combine herbicide with mechanical weeding: apply pre-emergence, then hoe once. This gives better control than either alone.

Biological control is limited for sunflower, but you can encourage natural enemies of weeds. For example, some insects feed on striga seeds. In West Africa, farmers use the fungus Fusarium oxysporum to control striga, but this is not yet widely available. A more practical biological method is to plant trap crops like cowpea that stimulate striga seeds to germinate without a host, then destroy the cowpea before striga attaches. This reduces the striga seed bank.

Monitor and adapt. Scout weekly. Keep records of weed species, control methods, and results. If one method fails, combine others. IWM is not a one-time fix; it is a season-long approach. The goal is to keep weeds below the economic thresholdusually 5-10 weeds per square metre for sunflower. With IWM, you can achieve 90% weed control, increase yields by 30-50%, and reduce herbicide costs. Train your workers on each method. Share knowledge with neighbours. IWM works best when the whole community manages weeds together.

5. Herbicide Resistance Management

Herbicide resistance occurs when weeds survive a herbicide that once controlled them. In Africa, resistance is growing, especially in pigweed (Amaranthus) and blackjack (Bidens pilosa) to herbicides like atrazine and glyphosate. Resistance happens when the same herbicide or same mode of action is used repeatedly. For example, if you apply pendimethalin every year, some pigweed plants may develop resistance. These survivors produce seeds, and soon the whole population is resistant. Resistance is inherited, so it spreads quickly.

To manage resistance, rotate herbicides with different modes of action. Herbicides are grouped by numbers (e.g., Group K1, K3, B, etc.). Never use the same group more than once per season. For sunflower, rotate pendimethalin (Group K1) with S-metolachlor (Group K3) or oxyfluorfen (Group E). If you use a post-emergence herbicide like quizalofop-p-ethyl (Group A), do not use another Group A herbicide in the same season. Mix herbicides with different modes of action if the label allowsthis is called a tank mix. For example, pendimethalin plus S-metolachlor gives broader control and reduces resistance risk.

Use multiple control methods. Do not rely on herbicides alone. Combine with hand weeding, mulching, crop rotation, and competitive varieties. Weeds that survive herbicide should be removed by hand before they set seed. This is called 'rogueing'. In Africa, many farmers rogue resistant weeds, which is effective if done before flowering. Keep records of which herbicides you use and where. If you notice a weed patch that survives spraying, mark it and hand-pull those weeds. Do not let them produce seeds.

Reduce herbicide use by using banded application: spray only in the crop row and hoe between rows. This cuts herbicide use by 50% and reduces selection pressure. Use full recommended ratesdo not under-dose, as sub-lethal doses allow weeds to survive and develop resistance. Calibrate your sprayer regularly. Buy herbicides from reputable dealers to avoid counterfeit products. If you suspect resistance, contact your extension officer. They can help test for resistance. In the long run, integrated weed management is the best strategy against resistance. A diverse approach keeps weeds guessing and preserves herbicide effectiveness for future seasons.

6. Pre-emergence and Post-emergence Herbicide Use

Pre-emergence herbicides are applied to the soil before weeds and sunflower emerge. They form a chemical barrier that kills germinating weed seeds. For sunflower, common pre-emergence herbicides include pendimethalin (Stomp), S-metolachlor (Dual Gold), and oxyfluorfen (Goal). Apply within 24-48 hours after planting. Use a knapsack sprayer with a flat fan nozzle, calibrated to deliver 200-300 litres per hectare. For pendimethalin, use 1.5-2.0 litres per hectare. For S-metolachlor, use 1.0-1.5 litres per hectare. Incorporate lightly into the top 2-3 cm of soil with a rake or harrow if the label recommends. Do not apply if the soil is very dry or if heavy rain is expected within 24 hours. Pre-emergence herbicides control pigweed, blackjack, goosegrass, and some grasses. They do not control established weeds.

Post-emergence herbicides are applied after weeds and sunflower have emerged. They are used when pre-emergence control is insufficient or when weeds escape. For grass weeds like couch grass and goosegrass, use quizalofop-p-ethyl (Targa) at 0.5-1.0 litres per hectare. Apply when grasses are 2-4 leaves and sunflower is at 2-4 leaves. For broadleaf weeds, use imazamox (Beyond) at 0.5-0.75 litres per hectare, but only on imidazolinone-tolerant sunflower varieties. Apply with a surfactant. Spray in the morning or evening when wind is low. Avoid spraying when sunflower is stressed. Post-emergence herbicides are most effective on small weeds. If weeds are larger than 10 cm, hand weeding may be better.

Timing and calibration are critical. For pre-emergence, apply before weeds germinate. For post-emergence, apply when weeds are small. Calibrate your sprayer: measure a 10 m x 10 m plot, spray water, and collect output from one nozzle. Adjust pressure or speed to get the correct volume. A common mistake is to spray too fast, leaving gaps. Walk at a steady pace. Use a shield to protect sunflower from non-selective herbicides. Always wear protective clothing. Store herbicides safely. Rotate between pre- and post-emergence herbicides with different modes of action to prevent resistance. For example, use pendimethalin (Group K1) pre-emergence, then quizalofop-p-ethyl (Group A) post-emergence. This combination gives broad-spectrum control and reduces resistance risk. Keep records of application dates, rates, and weather conditions. If you follow these steps, you can achieve 85-95% weed control and protect your sunflower yield.

<h3>2. Cultural and Mechanical Weed Control</h3>
<p>In African smallholder and commercial systems, mechanical control (hand hoeing, tractor row cultivation) remains the backbone of weed management. Key practices include:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Timely hand weeding:</strong> Two weedings at 23 weeks and 56 weeks after emergence protect yield.</li>
<li><strong>Row cultivation:</strong> Inter-row cultivation with a tractor or ox-drawn cultivator at 34 weeks.</li>
<li><strong>Competitive varieties:</strong> Taller, faster-canopy hybrids suppress weeds better than open-pollinated varieties.</li>
<li><strong>Crop rotation:</strong> Rotating sunflower with cereals (maize, sorghum, wheat) breaks weed cycles, especially <em>Striga</em>.</li>
<li><strong>Mulching:</strong> Crop residue mulch (24 t/ha) reduces weed emergence by 4060%.</li>
<li><strong>Stale seedbed:</strong> Irrigate or wait for weed flush, then kill weeds before sowing.</li>
</ul>

<p>Herbicide use is growing rapidly among commercial sunflower growers in South Africa, Zambia, and Tanzania. Selection must match the weed spectrum, soil type, and sunflower growth stage. Always check national registration (e.g., South Africa's Act 36 of 1947, Kenya's PCPB, Tanzania's TPRI).</p>

<h3>4. Integrated Weed Management (IWM) Strategies</h3>
<p>No single tactic controls all weeds. IWM combines cultural, mechanical, chemical, and biological approaches to keep weed populations below economic thresholds while protecting soil health and delaying resistance. For African agribusinesses, IWM reduces input costs by 1530% compared to herbicide-only programmes and stabilises yields across seasons.</p>
<p>Core IWM pillars for sunflower:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Prevention:</strong> Clean seed, clean equipment, and quarantine of <em>Parthenium</em>-infested fields.</li>
<li><strong>Cultural:</strong> Crop rotation, competitive varieties, optimal plant density (55,00065,000 plants/ha), and balanced fertilisation.</li>
<li><strong>Mechanical:</strong> Timely cultivation and hand weeding during the critical period.</li>
<li><strong>Chemical:</strong> Pre- and post-emergence herbicides with rotation of modes of action.</li>
<li><strong>Monitoring:</strong> Weekly scouting, weed maps, and record-keeping for decision-making.</li>
</ol>

<h3>5. Herbicide Resistance Management</h3>
<p>Herbicide resistance is now confirmed in <em>Amaranthus</em>, <em>Bidens</em>, and <em>Echinochloa</em> populations in South Africa and Zimbabwe. Resistance develops when the same mode of action (MoA) is used repeatedly. To manage resistance:</p>
<ul>
<li>Rotate HRAC groups every season (e.g., K1 A B K3).</li>
<li>Use tank mixes or pre-mixes with multiple effective MoAs.</li>
<li>Include non-chemical tactics in every season.</li>
<li>Apply herbicides at full label rate, at the correct growth stage, and with proper calibration.</li>
<li>Keep records of herbicide use per field for at least 5 years.</li>
<li>Destroy weed escapes before they set seed one <em>Amaranthus</em> plant can produce 500,000 seeds.</li>
</ul>

<h3>6. Pre-emergence and Post-emergence Herbicide Use</h3>
<p><strong>Pre-emergence</strong> herbicides form a soil barrier that kills germinating weed seeds. They must be applied to moist, well-prepared soil within 03 days of sowing and before sunflower emergence. Rainfall or irrigation of 1020 mm within 7 days activates the herbicide. Avoid application on cloddy or dry soil efficacy drops sharply.</p>
<p><strong>Post-emergence</strong> herbicides are applied after sunflower and weeds emerge, typically at the 24 leaf stage. Timing is critical: too early stresses the crop, too late allows weeds to compete. Use directed sprays where possible to avoid crop injury. Always add a recommended surfactant and calibrate the sprayer before each session.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist Field Officers &amp; Farm Managers</h3>
<ol>
<li> Scout fields weekly from emergence to canopy closure (06 weeks).</li>
<li> Apply pre-emergence herbicide within 3 days of sowing on moist soil.</li>
<li> Conduct first hand weeding or row cultivation by 23 weeks after emergence.</li>
<li> Apply post-emergence herbicide at 24 leaf stage, calibrated and with surfactant.</li>
<li> Rotate herbicide modes of action (HRAC groups) every season.</li>
<li> Maintain crop rotation with cereals to break <em>Striga</em> and nutsedge cycles.</li>
<li> Wear full PPE and observe pre-harvest intervals and buffer zones.</li>
<li> Record all herbicide applications, rates, dates, and weed escapes for 5 years.</li>
</ol>
