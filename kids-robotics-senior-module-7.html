1. Odometry and Dead Reckoning

Odometry is how a robot estimates its position by counting wheel rotations. On a typical African maker robot like a 2WD chassis with TT motors and a caster wheel, you attach an incremental encoder to each motor shaft. The encoder gives pulses per revolution (PPR). For example, a common cheap encoder gives 20 PPR. With a gear ratio of 1:48, one full wheel turn gives 960 pulses. If your wheel diameter is 65 mm, circumference = Ï Ã 65 â 204 mm. So each pulse = 204 / 960 â 0.2125 mm. That's your resolution. In code, you count pulses, multiply by 0.2125, and you have distance traveled. To get heading, you need to know the distance each wheel traveled. If left wheel goes 100 mm and right wheel goes 120 mm, and your wheelbase (distance between wheels) is 150 mm, then the robot turned by (120-100)/150 = 0.133 radians â 7.6 degrees to the left. You integrate these small turns over time to get your x, y, and theta. This is dead reckoning: estimating your current pose from a known start and measured motions. The problem is drift. Small errors in wheel diameter, uneven floors, or wheel slip accumulate. On a dirt road in a farm, slip can be 20% or more. So after 10 meters, your robot might think it's at 12 meters. To reduce drift, calibrate your wheel diameter by driving a straight 5 meters and measuring actual distance, then adjust. Use a gyroscope (like MPU6050) to correct heading drift. Combine wheel odometry with gyro using a simple complementary filter: trust gyro for short-term changes, odometry for long-term. In practice, odometry alone is good for short trips (under 5 meters) on smooth surfaces. For longer runs, you need landmarks or GPS. But odometry is cheap, fast, and always available. Start with it, then add sensors. In your lab, you'll test odometry on a tiled floor and on grass to see the difference. Record the error at 1, 3, and 5 meters. That data will guide your sensor fusion later.

2. Wheeled Locomotion and Kinematics

Wheeled locomotion is the most common way to move a robot on flat ground. The two main types are differential drive and Ackermann steering. Differential drive uses two independently driven wheels, usually with a caster or omni wheel for balance. It's simple, cheap, and can turn in place. Ackermann steering is like a car: two front wheels steer, rear wheels drive. It's better for high speed and rough terrain but can't turn in place. For African conditions, differential drive is popular because it handles uneven ground and is easy to build with locally available parts. Kinematics is the math that relates wheel speeds to robot motion. For differential drive, let v_L and v_R be the linear speeds of left and right wheels. The robot's linear speed v = (v_L + v_R)/2. The angular speed Ï = (v_R - v_L)/L, where L is the wheelbase. Conversely, given desired v and Ï, you set v_L = v - (Ï L)/2 and v_R = v + (Ï L)/2. For example, if you want to go straight at 0.2 m/s, set both wheels to 0.2 m/s. To turn left at 0.5 rad/s with L=0.15 m, v_L = 0.2 - (0.5*0.15)/2 = 0.1625 m/s, v_R = 0.2 + 0.0375 = 0.2375 m/s. These speeds translate to motor PWM values. You'll need to calibrate: find the minimum PWM that moves the robot, and the maximum before wheels slip. A typical TT motor with 1:48 gear runs well at 6V with PWM 100-255 (8-bit). For precise control, use PID on each wheel's encoder. Also consider wheel slip: on loose sand, wheels spin without moving. To reduce slip, use rubber tires with tread, add weight over drive wheels, and accelerate slowly. For omni-directional robots, you use mecanum or omni wheels, but they are expensive and hard to find in local markets. Stick with differential drive for your first robot. In the lab, you'll program a function that takes v and Ï and sets motor speeds, then test it by driving a square path: forward 1 m, turn 90Â°, repeat. Measure how close you return to start. That's your kinematic accuracy.

3. Path Planning: A*, Dijkstra, RRT

Path planning finds a route from start to goal without hitting obstacles. The three common algorithms are Dijkstra, A*, and RRT. Dijkstra's algorithm explores all directions equally, like pouring water on a flat surface. It guarantees the shortest path but is slow because it checks many cells. A* is Dijkstra with a heuristic: it uses a guess of distance to goal to focus the search. For a grid map, the heuristic is often Manhattan distance (|x1-x2| + |y1-y2|) or Euclidean distance. A* is faster and still optimal if the heuristic never overestimates. RRT (Rapidly-exploring Random Tree) is different: it randomly samples points in the space and builds a tree, connecting each new point to the nearest existing point. It's good for high-dimensional spaces and robots with complex motions, but the path is not optimal and can be jerky. For a mobile robot in a maze, A* on a grid is the go-to. You represent the environment as a 2D grid (e.g., 1 cell = 10 cm). Mark obstacles as blocked. Run A* to get a list of cells. Then smooth the path by removing unnecessary waypoints. In Africa, you might map a warehouse in Lagos or a farm in Kenya. Use a grid of 0.5 m cells. A* will find a path in milliseconds on a laptop, but on a microcontroller like Arduino, it might be too heavy. Use a Raspberry Pi or ESP32 with enough RAM. For dynamic obstacles, you need replanning. If a person walks into the path, stop, update the grid, and replan. RRT is better for continuous spaces, like a drone flying through trees, but for ground robots, grid-based A* is simpler. In the lab, you'll implement A* in Python on a 10x10 grid with obstacles. Then you'll convert the path to motor commands. Test with a small robot on a floor with cardboard boxes as obstacles. Measure time to goal and path length. Compare with Dijkstra to see the speed difference. A* is usually 2-5 times faster.

4. Obstacle Avoidance and Local Planning

Obstacle avoidance keeps your robot from crashing when the map is wrong or something new appears. Local planning uses sensors to react in real time. The most common sensor is ultrasonic (HC-SR04). It gives distance to an object by timing sound pulses. Range is 2 cm to 4 m, but accuracy drops on soft surfaces like cloth. Infrared (IR) sensors are cheaper but only detect close objects (2-30 cm) and are affected by sunlight. For outdoor use, add a lidar (e.g., RPLIDAR A1) which gives 360Â° scans up to 12 m. The simplest avoidance algorithm is the Braitenberg vehicle: each sensor drives a motor. For example, if left sensor sees an obstacle, turn right. If both see obstacles, back up and turn. A more robust method is the Vector Field Histogram (VFH): build a polar histogram of obstacles, find gaps, and steer toward the largest gap. Another is the Dynamic Window Approach (DWA): sample possible velocities (v, Ï) in a short time window, simulate the robot's motion, and pick the one that avoids obstacles and gets closer to the goal. DWA works well on differential drive robots. In Africa, you might have unpredictable obstacles: goats, potholes, people. So you need a safety margin. Set a stop distance of 30 cm for ultrasonic. If distance < 30 cm, stop and turn. For narrow corridors, use side sensors. In code, read sensors every 50 ms. Use a state machine: if front clear, go forward; if front blocked, turn left or right based on which side is clearer. Add a buzzer to warn people. In the lab, you'll program a robot to navigate a corridor with randomly placed boxes. Use three ultrasonic sensors (left, center, right). The robot should reach the end without touching. Then add a moving obstacle (a person) and see how it reacts. Tune the stop distance and turn speed. Remember: local planning is reactive, so it can get stuck in a dead end. Combine with a global planner to escape.

5. SLAM: Simultaneous Localization and Mapping

SLAM is when a robot builds a map and figures out where it is at the same time. It's a chicken-and-egg problem: to map, you need to know your position; to know your position, you need a map. SLAM solves it by using landmarks. The robot moves, observes features (walls, corners, pillars), and updates both its position and the map. The two main types are EKF-SLAM (Extended Kalman Filter) and GraphSLAM. EKF-SLAM keeps a state vector of robot pose and landmark positions, and updates it with sensor data. It works for small maps (up to a few hundred landmarks) but is computationally heavy. GraphSLAM builds a graph of robot poses and landmarks, then optimizes the whole graph. It's more accurate and scales better. For a beginner, use a lidar and a ROS package like GMapping or Cartographer. On a Raspberry Pi 4 with RPLIDAR A1, you can run GMapping at 5 Hz. The output is a 2D occupancy grid: each cell is free, occupied, or unknown. You can then use that map for navigation with AMCL (Adaptive Monte Carlo Localization). In Africa, SLAM is useful for mapping buildings, mines, or farms. But lidar is expensive (over $100). A cheaper alternative is a camera and ORB-SLAM, but it needs good lighting and texture. For outdoor sun, use a depth camera like Intel RealSense. In the lab, you'll use a pre-built robot with a lidar and ROS. Drive it around a room slowly (0.2 m/s). Watch the map build in RViz. Then save the map and use it to navigate to a goal. Note the loop closure: when you return to start, the map should correct itself. If you don't have lidar, simulate SLAM in Python with a simple 2D world. The key takeaway: SLAM is powerful but needs good sensors and computation. Start with a known map and localization (AMCL) before attempting full SLAM.

6. Lab: Autonomous Navigation in a Maze

In this lab, you'll build a robot that can navigate a maze autonomously. You'll use a differential drive robot with encoders, three ultrasonic sensors, and an Arduino or Raspberry Pi. The maze is made of cardboard walls, 30 cm high, on a 2m x 2m floor. The goal is to go from start to finish without hitting walls. First, calibrate your odometry: drive straight 1 m and adjust the wheel diameter in code until the error is under 2 cm. Then calibrate your turn: command a 90Â° turn and measure actual angle; adjust the wheelbase parameter. Next, implement a simple wall-following algorithm: keep a set distance (20 cm) from the right wall. Use a PID controller on the right sensor to adjust steering. If front sensor sees a wall within 30 cm, turn left. This works for many mazes. For more complex mazes, use a flood-fill algorithm: treat the maze as a grid, mark visited cells, and always move to the unvisited neighbor with the lowest distance to goal. You'll need to map the maze as you go. Use a 2D array (e.g., 10x10) and update it with sensor readings. For each cell, store walls (N, S, E, W). When you enter a cell, check sensors and update walls. Then compute the shortest path to goal using A* on the known map. Move one cell at a time. This is a simplified SLAM. In practice, you'll run into issues: sensor noise, wheel slip, and timing. Use a 50 ms loop. Add a buzzer that beeps when turning. Test with a simple maze first (3x3), then increase size. Record your success rate over 10 runs. Aim for 80% success. If you fail, check your sensor thresholds and motor deadband. For power, use a 7.4V LiPo battery with a voltage regulator. Keep wires tidy to avoid snags. This lab ties together odometry, kinematics, path planning, and obstacle avoidance. It's the core of mobile robotics. After this, you can add a camera for line following or a lidar for full SLAM.

Practical Reference: Mobile Robot Navigation for African Agribusiness




Mobile robot navigation is not an abstract robotics topic in African agribusiness; it is the difference between a machine that moves seed, feed, or harvested produce efficiently and one that gets stuck in a muddy row, misses a waypoint, or wastes costly battery capacity. This practical reference distils the navigation stack into deployable decisions for farm robots, warehouse AGVs, and field scouting platforms. It assumes you are building or procuring wheeled robots for tasks such as automated weeding, feed distribution, soil sampling, or produce transport on smallholder and commercial farms.




In African contexts, navigation systems must tolerate dust, uneven terrain, unreliable GPS, and intermittent connectivity. The most successful deployments combine low-cost wheel odometry, robust local planning, and lightweight SLAM rather than relying on expensive, high-precision sensors alone. The following sections give you the engineering reference points, calibration data, and safety rules needed to specify, test, and operate mobile robots in agricultural environments.





Why This Matters for African Agribusiness



African farms and agri-logistics operations face three navigation realities: labour availability is seasonal and variable, field terrain is often unstructured, and imported robotics must justify their cost within one to three harvest cycles. Mobile robot navigation directly affects unit economics. A robot that localises poorly will overlap spray or fertiliser passes, wasting inputs that can represent 30â40% of operating cost. A robot that cannot replan around a fallen branch or a goat will stall and require human intervention, eroding the labour-saving value proposition.



The practical reference below is written for technical managers, integrators, and farm operators who need to evaluate robot navigation performance, calibrate odometry, and choose planning algorithms that work under African field conditions. It focuses on wheeled platforms because they are the dominant form factor for agricultural AGVs, autonomous tractors, and greenhouse robots in African markets.

Wheeled Locomotion and Kinematics: Selection Guide



The choice of wheeled locomotion determines the navigation algorithms you can use and the terrain your robot can handle. Differential drive is the most common in African agri-robotics because it is mechanically simple, turns in place, and suits narrow rows. Ackermann steering is preferred for larger field robots that must cover long distances efficiently. Skid-steer is robust but causes high soil disturbance and odometry error, making it suitable mainly for hard surfaces or tracked conversions.

Path Planning: A*, Dijkstra, and RRT



Global planners compute a route from current pose to goal on a known map. Dijkstra's algorithm guarantees the shortest path but explores uniformly, making it slow on large farm maps. A* adds a heuristic and is the default for grid-based farm navigation because it is fast and optimal when the heuristic is admissible. RRT (Rapidly-exploring Random Tree) is preferred for high-dimensional or non-holonomic robots, such as Ackermann tractors, because it handles kinematic constraints naturally.



In African farm layouts, grid resolution matters. A 0.25 m grid is sufficient for row crops but generates large maps for a 50-hectare field. Use A* with a coarse 1 m grid for inter-row transit and switch to a fine local planner for in-row alignment. RRT* can be used offline to generate smooth tractor paths that respect minimum turning radius, reducing soil compaction and fuel use.



Obstacle Avoidance and Local Planning



Local planners react to unmapped obstacles such as people, animals, fallen branches, or parked equipment. The Dynamic Window Approach (DWA) is widely used on differential-drive robots because it samples feasible velocity commands and scores them for obstacle clearance, goal heading, and speed. Vector Field Histogram (VFH) is lighter computationally and works well on low-power African-designed robots using Raspberry Pi or similar single-board computers.



For agribusiness, local planning must prioritise not damaging crops. A robot that swerves into a maize row to avoid a chicken is a net loss. Configure costmaps with high inflation around planted rows and lower cost for bare furrows. Set maximum deceleration so that a sudden stop does not tip a tall payload, such as a full harvest crate.



SLAM: Simultaneous Localization and Mapping



SLAM builds a map while tracking pose. For African farms, LiDAR SLAM (e.g., Cartographer, LIO-SAM) performs well in orchards and greenhouses where GNSS is weak. Visual SLAM (ORB-SLAM3) is cheaper but struggles in dusty or low-texture fields. A practical hybrid is GNSS RTK for open fields and LiDAR SLAM for barns, packhouses, and tree canopies.



Map maintenance is an operational cost often overlooked. Fields change with seasons, crop growth, and irrigation infrastructure. Budget for monthly map updates during peak season. Store maps in a versioned repository and tag them with crop stage, so the robot does not navigate using last season's row geometry.



Lab: Autonomous Navigation in a Maze



The module lab uses a maze to simulate row-crop navigation. Students build a differential-drive robot, implement odometry, run A* on a known maze map, and then test SLAM by removing the map and relying on LiDAR. The maze should include dead ends, narrow corridors (30 cm), and movable obstacles to mimic African farm conditions. Assessment criteria: completion time, collision count, and pose error at goal.



Quick Reference Checklist



  1. Calibrate odometry before every season. Measure actual wheel radius under load and update the kinematic model; soil and tyre wear change effective radius by 3â8%.

  2. Fuse at least two pose sources. Never rely on wheel encoders alone; add IMU and either GNSS RTK or LiDAR SLAM.

  3. Match planner to drive type. Use A* + DWA for differential drive; use RRT* or curvature planners for Ackermann.

  4. Set costmap inflation for crop rows. Protect plants with high-cost zones and keep emergency stop zones clear.

  5. Test in the actual field, not just the lab. Dust, mud, vibration, and temperature affect sensors and wheel slip.

  6. Version and date your maps. Retire maps when crop stage or field layout changes.

  7. Verify emergency stop and watchdog. Hardware E-stop within 1 m; software halt if sensor data is stale >300 ms.

  8. Log navigation data for cost analysis. Track path length, overlap, interventions, and energy per hectare to justify ROI.
