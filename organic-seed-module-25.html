🧠 Brain Opener

Every seed business needs money at some point - to buy better seed, rent more land, or bridge a cash gap. The question is not whether you need finance, but which kind and from whom.

  • What are the five sources of finance for a seed business?
  • When should you borrow and when should you not?
  • What do lenders look for in a small farm?
🎁 Reveal a strong answer

The five sources are personal savings, family and friends, grants, microfinance, and development finance. Borrow only when the money will generate more than the cost of the loan. Lenders look for records, cash flow, and repayment history.

📖 Nyari Finds the Right Lender

Nyari needed 2,000 dollars. She wanted to rent an extra hectare, buy better seed, and hire two workers for the season. Her business plan showed the money would return 5,000 dollars in one season.

She went to her bank. The loan officer asked for three things: three years of financial records, land title as collateral, and a guarantor with a formal salary.

Nyari had none of these. Her farm was on family land without a title. Her records were two years old. Her only guarantors were farmers.

The bank said no.

She went to a microfinance institution. They offered 1,500 dollars at 36 percent annual interest. She calculated: on a one-season loan of 8 months, the interest would be 360 dollars. That was affordable if her plan worked.

But they also required a group guarantee - three other people to co-sign. Nyari asked two farmers and her aunt. They agreed.

She took the loan. She paid it back in 7 months. Interest paid: 315 dollars. Profit from the extra hectare: 2,700 dollars.

She learned three lessons. First, banks want collateral; microfinance wants trust. Second, interest rates matter but cash flow matters more. Third, borrowing for a business that will pay back is not debt - it is leverage.

💡 The Big Idea

Different finance for different stages

A seed business at year one has different needs than one at year five. The right source of finance changes as the business grows. Start with savings. Add microfinance for specific returns. Access grants when you have a plan. Use development finance for scale. Match the tool to the stage.

📚 Three Deep Lessons

1

The five sources of finance

1. Personal savings. Best. No interest. No obligation. Start here for anything under 500 dollars.

2. Family and friends. Informal loans at low or zero interest. Write a simple agreement - even with family. Protects the relationship.

3. Grants and subsidies. Government programmes, NGOs, and development agencies. Free money for specific purposes. Requires a strong application and reporting.

4. Microfinance. Small loans from specialized institutions. Interest 20 to 40 percent annually. Group guarantees or collateral. Best for 500 to 5,000 dollar needs.

5. Development finance. Larger loans from impact investors, agricultural banks, and development finance institutions. Interest 10 to 20 percent. Collateral required. Best for 5,000 dollar and above.

2

When to borrow and when not to

Borrow when the money will earn more than the interest you pay. If you borrow 1,000 dollars at 30 percent for one year, you pay 300 in interest. If the 1,000 dollars returns 2,000 dollars, you profit 700. Borrow.

Do not borrow for personal needs. Do not borrow to cover losses from last season. Do not borrow without a written plan. Do not borrow more than you can repay even in a bad season.

The general rule: total loan repayments should never exceed 30 percent of expected annual revenue. Above that, you are over-leveraged.

3

What lenders look for

Lenders are not buying your dream. They are buying your repayment ability. Show them four things.

Records. Written production and sales history. Even a notebook log is better than nothing.

Cash flow. A monthly plan showing money in and out.

Collateral or guarantee. Land title, equipment, savings, or group co-signers.

Track record. Previous loans repaid. Previous seasons completed. Previous buyers satisfied.

If you have none of these, build them first. Work with savings for one season. Keep excellent records. Repay any small loan on time. Then approach a lender with evidence.

🛠 Hands-On - Prepare a Loan Application Pack

You need

  • A notebook
  • A pen
  • Your records from last season
  1. Write a one-page summary of your business.
  2. Write a one-page cash flow plan for the next 12 months.
  3. Write a one-page list of your assets and liabilities.
  4. Write a one-page description of what you will do with the loan.
  5. Write a one-page explanation of how you will repay.
  6. Collect any receipts, invoices, or sales records from last season.
  7. Write the names and numbers of three potential guarantors.
  8. Put everything in a folder. Label it Loan Pack 1.
Why it works: A one-inch folder of clear documents will get you further than a perfect memory. Lenders want paper.

🎮 Game - Pitch to a Lender

4 to 12 players - 25 min

One player is the lender. The others pitch a loan request of 1,000 dollars with a specific use. The lender can ask three questions. Score each pitch on: clarity of use, evidence of ability to repay, strength of plan. Highest score gets the loan.

🤖 AI-Era Skill

AI can help you write a loan application and evaluate loan offers. But you must judge whether to take on the debt.

Try this prompt: "I run a small organic seed business in [country]. I am considering a 1,000 dollar microfinance loan at 30 percent annual interest. My expected return from using the money is 2,500 dollars over 8 months. Should I take the loan? Walk me through the decision."

Do the maths yourself. 1,000 dollars at 30 percent for 8 months is 200 dollars interest. Net profit from the loan: 2,500 minus 1,000 minus 200 equals 1,300 dollars. The loan makes sense if the plan is realistic.

🌍 Real Stories from Africa

Zambia FISP programme

Zambia Food Input Support Programme provides subsidised inputs to smallholder farmers. Farmers who use FISP support to produce seed earn 40 percent more than those who do not. The programme is free money - but you must apply through the local agricultural office.

Kenya agricultural finance

Kenyan agricultural finance institutions offer loans specifically for seed production. Interest rates range from 12 to 24 percent. Requirements include a business plan, land records, and previous production records. First-time borrowers can use group guarantees.
