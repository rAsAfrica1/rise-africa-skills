1. Selecting and Sourcing the 33 Ingredients

Welcome to Module 6, the heart of your organic manure enterprise. By now you understand that a 33 ingredient manure is not a gimmick; it is a deliberate strategy to feed the soil a balanced diet of carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium, sulphur, trace minerals, and living microbes. In this first lesson we focus on selecting and sourcing those ingredients in an African context, where what is available in Kisumu may differ from what is available in Kumasi or Kano. The business first principle is simple: your input cost determines your profit margin. If you pay market price for every ingredient, your manure will be expensive and you will struggle to compete with cheap chemical fertiliser. Your advantage is that most of the 33 ingredients are waste streams that other people want to get rid of. You must become a waste hunter. Start by mapping your local area. Walk through markets, abattoirs, poultry farms, breweries, sugar factories, coffee processing plants, sawmills, and municipal dumps. Each of these produces materials you need. The 33 ingredients can be grouped into six families. First, carbon rich bulk materials: maize stover, rice husks, sugarcane bagasse, sawdust, coffee husks, and dry grass. Second, nitrogen rich green materials: tithonia, comfrey, lucerne, moringa leaves, bean trash, and fresh kitchen waste. Third, animal manures: cattle, goat, sheep, poultry, rabbit, and pig dung. Fourth, mineral and ash sources: wood ash, bone meal, rock phosphate, limestone, gypsum, and dolomite. Fifth, microbial and activator sources: forest topsoil, termite mound soil, compost from a previous batch, and fermented plant juice. Sixth, special additives: urine, molasses, seaweed or lake weed, fish waste, and biochar. That gives you a working list of 33. Now, sourcing rules. Rule one: never pay cash for more than three ingredients. The rest must be free or bartered. For example, a poultry farmer will give you litter if you help him clean the house. A market woman will give you rotten tomatoes if you carry them away. A sawmill will give you sawdust if you take it off their premises. Rule two: secure volume before you promise delivery. Many trainers fail because they collect one bag of this and one bag of that, then cannot fulfil a five tonne order. Identify one anchor ingredient that you can get in tonnes, such as cattle manure from a zero grazing unit or rice husks from a mill. Build your recipe around that anchor. Rule three: test your ingredients. In Africa, many soils are acidic, so wood ash and limestone are gold. But some ashes from burnt plastics or treated timber are toxic. Never use ash from batteries, painted wood, or municipal waste that contains plastic. Rule four: dry and store properly. Wet ingredients rot and lose nitrogen. Dry your materials under shade, not in direct sun, because sunlight kills microbes and volatilises nitrogen. Store in sacks on pallets in a dry shed. Rule five: keep records. For every ingredient, write the date, source, quantity, cost, and moisture level. This record becomes your production bible. A practical sourcing calendar for a typical East African operation looks like this. January to March, dry season, collect ash, bone meal, and dry stover. April to June, long rains, collect green tithonia, comfrey, and weeds before they seed. July to September, harvest season, collect maize cobs, rice husks, and coffee husks. October to December, short rains, collect animal manure and make compost. Your goal by the end of this lesson is to have a written list of at least 20 local suppliers, with phone numbers, for your 33 ingredients. That list is a business asset. Without it, you are just a gardener. With it, you are a manufacturer. Remember, the 33 ingredient manure is not about having exactly 33 at all times. It is about diversity. If you have 25 ingredients, your manure will still be excellent. If you have 10, you are making ordinary compost. So push for diversity, but do not let perfection stop you from starting. Start with what you have, then add one new ingredient every month. That is how you build a resilient, profitable, and truly African organic manure business.

2. Building the Carbon and Nitrogen Base

In this lesson we move from sourcing to building the foundation of your 33 ingredient manure: the carbon and nitrogen base. Every compost pile, every manure heap, and every soil amendment lives or dies by the carbon to nitrogen ratio. If you get this wrong, your pile will either sit cold for months or turn into a smelly, fly infested mess. The target is a C to N ratio between 25 and 30 to 1. In practical African terms, that means roughly three parts dry brown material to one part green or wet material by volume. But because you are using 33 ingredients, you must think in layers, not in a single mix. Start with a bottom layer of coarse carbon material, about 15 centimetres deep. Use maize cobs, sugarcane bagasse, or rice husks. This layer allows air to enter from the ground and prevents the pile from waterlogging during heavy rains. Next, add a layer of animal manure, about 5 centimetres. Cattle, goat, or poultry manure is best because it already contains microbes. Then add a layer of green material, about 10 centimetres. Tithonia, comfrey, fresh weeds, or kitchen waste. Then sprinkle a thin layer of mineral ingredients: wood ash, bone meal, rock phosphate, and a handful of soil from a termite mound or forest floor. Repeat these layers until the pile is one and a half metres high. The width should be at least one and a half metres, and the length can be as long as you want. Do not make the pile too small, because small piles lose heat and do not compost properly. Now, the 33 ingredients come into play in the layering. You do not need to put all 33 in every layer. Instead, distribute them across the pile. For example, put sawdust and coffee husks in the carbon layers. Put moringa leaves, bean trash, and seaweed in the green layers. Put bone meal, limestone, and gypsum in the mineral layers. Put fish waste and urine in the centre of the pile where heat is highest. Put molasses diluted in water as a microbial stimulant when you water the pile. This is where the art meets the science. A common mistake in Africa is using too much fresh manure and too little carbon. The result is ammonia smell, which means you are losing nitrogen into the air. If you smell ammonia, add more carbon immediately. Another mistake is using too much sawdust from treated timber. Treated sawdust contains chemicals that kill microbes. Only use sawdust from untreated wood, and even then, use it sparingly because it breaks down slowly. Moisture is the next critical factor. Your pile should feel like a wrung out sponge. If you squeeze a handful and water drips out, it is too wet. If it feels dry and dusty, it is too dry. In dry season, you may need to water the pile every three days. In rainy season, you may need to cover it with a tarpaulin or banana leaves to prevent nutrients from leaching. Turn the pile every seven to ten days. Turning introduces oxygen, mixes the ingredients, and prevents anaerobic pockets. On a commercial scale, use a tractor with a front loader or a simple pitchfork if you are small. The pile should reach a temperature of 55 to 65 degrees Celsius within three days. If it does not heat up, your pile is too dry, too wet, or lacking nitrogen. Add urine or fresh green material and turn again. After three to four weeks, the pile will cool down. This is when you add the final 33 ingredients that are heat sensitive: biochar, seaweed, and microbial inoculants. Biochar is especially important because it acts like a sponge for nutrients and provides homes for microbes. You can make biochar from maize cobs, rice husks, or coconut shells using a simple kiln. Crush it and add it at 5 to 10 percent of the total volume. After adding the final ingredients, let the pile cure for two to three weeks. The final product should be dark, crumbly, and smell like forest soil. It should not smell like manure or rot. If it does, it needs more curing time. Now, let us talk business. A well built carbon and nitrogen base means you use less labour, less water, and less time. That translates to lower production cost per tonne. If you are selling to farmers, they will judge your manure by its smell, texture, and results. A farmer who applies your manure and sees maize leaves turn dark green within two weeks will come back and buy ten more bags. A farmer who gets a smelly, wet product will never return. So treat the carbon and nitrogen base as your factory floor. Keep it clean, keep it balanced, and keep records of every layer you add. In the next lesson we will add the mineral and biological ingredients that make your manure truly 33 in one.

3. Adding Minerals, Microbes, and Activators

Now that your carbon and nitrogen base is built, it is time to add the ingredients that turn ordinary compost into a complete soil food. In this lesson we cover minerals, microbes, and activators. These are the ingredients that most African farmers ignore, and that is why their compost gives poor yields. You will not make that mistake. Let us start with minerals. The big four are calcium, phosphorus, potassium, and magnesium. In many African soils, calcium and magnesium are depleted, which makes the soil acidic and locks up other nutrients. Your job is to add them back. Wood ash is your cheapest source of potassium and calcium. But not all ash is equal. Ash from hardwood trees is best. Ash from maize cobs and rice husks is also good. Avoid ash from plastics, batteries, or painted wood. Apply ash at 2 to 5 percent of the total pile volume. Bone meal is your best source of phosphorus and calcium. You can make it yourself by burning animal bones in a pit, then crushing them into powder. Do not use bones from diseased animals. Rock phosphate is another phosphorus source, but it is slow release. Use it if you can get it from a local quarry or agrodealer. Limestone and dolomite provide calcium and magnesium. If your soil is acidic, use more limestone. If your soil is magnesium deficient, use dolomite. Gypsum provides calcium and sulphur without changing soil pH. It is excellent for reclaiming sodic soils, which are common in irrigation schemes. Now, the trace minerals. These are needed in tiny amounts but are critical for plant health. Seaweed or lake weed is your best source. In coastal Africa, collect seaweed from the beach. In inland Africa, collect lake weed from Lake Victoria, Lake Malawi, or Lake Tanganyika. Dry it, crush it, and add it at 1 to 2 percent of pile volume. Fish waste is another trace mineral source. You can get fish frames, scales, and guts from markets. Bury them deep in the pile to avoid smell and flies. Urine is a powerful activator and mineral source. Human urine, cattle urine, or goat urine can be diluted 1 to 5 with water and sprinkled on the pile. Urine adds nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium. Molasses is a microbial stimulant. Mix one litre of molasses in twenty litres of water and spray on the pile. The sugar feeds bacteria and fungi, speeding up decomposition. Now, microbes. You cannot see them, but they are the workers in your manure factory. You need three types: bacteria, fungi, and actinomycetes. You get them from forest topsoil, termite mound soil, and old compost. Collect a sack of forest soil from under a mature tree. Mix it with water to make a slurry, then strain it and spray the liquid on your pile. Termite mound soil is rich in actinomycetes and minerals. Add a few handfuls to each layer. Old compost from a previous batch is your best inoculant. Always keep a sack of finished compost to start your next pile. You can also make your own microbial brew. Take one kilogram of fresh cow dung, one kilogram of molasses, and twenty litres of water. Mix in a drum and let it ferment for seven days. Stir daily. The liquid will smell sweet and slightly alcoholic. Dilute it 1 to 10 with water and spray on your pile. This brew multiplies beneficial microbes and speeds up composting. Another activator is fermented plant juice. Collect young leaves of tithonia, comfrey, and moringa. Pound them, mix with equal weight of brown sugar or molasses, and ferment in a jar for seven days. Strain and dilute 1 to 500 with water. Spray on the pile. This is a powerful growth stimulant for both microbes and plants. When you add these minerals, microbes, and activators, you must do it at the right time. Add minerals like rock phosphate and bone meal early, because they break down slowly. Add ash and limestone early too, but not together with ammonium based ingredients, because they can cause nitrogen loss. Add microbes and activators after the pile has cooled down, because high heat kills them. Add biochar at the end, because it will absorb nutrients and provide microbial habitat. A practical schedule for a one tonne pile is this. Day one: build carbon and nitrogen layers. Day three: add bone meal, rock phosphate, and ash. Day seven: turn and add more green material. Day fourteen: turn and add limestone and gypsum. Day twenty one: turn and add forest soil slurry. Day twenty eight: add biochar, seaweed, and microbial brew. Day thirty five: cure. Day fifty: screen and bag. This schedule is not rigid. Adjust it to your climate and materials. In dry areas, add more water. In wet areas, add more carbon. The business point is this: minerals and microbes are what make farmers see results. A farmer who uses your manure will not see the 33 ingredients. He will see taller maize, greener leaves, and bigger tomatoes. That visible result is your marketing. So do not skip the minerals. Do not skip the microbes. They are the difference between a bag of dirt and a bag of gold.

4. Fermentation, Curing, and Quality Control

In this lesson we focus on the final stages of production: fermentation, curing, and quality control. These stages determine whether your 33 ingredient manure is safe, effective, and sellable. Many producers rush this stage and end up with a product that burns plants, smells bad, or contains weed seeds. You will not rush. Fermentation is the active breakdown phase. It starts when you build the pile and ends when the pile cools down. During fermentation, temperatures rise to 55 to 65 degrees Celsius. This heat kills pathogens like E. coli and salmonella, and it kills most weed seeds. If your pile does not reach this temperature, pathogens and weed seeds survive. To ensure proper fermentation, follow these rules. First, pile size must be at least one cubic metre. Smaller piles lose heat. Second, moisture must be 50 to 60 percent. Too dry and microbes stop working. Too wet and the pile goes anaerobic and smells. Third, turn the pile every seven days. Turning moves the outside material to the inside, so everything gets heated. Fourth, do not add fresh material after day twenty one, because it will restart fermentation and delay curing. Curing is the maturing phase. After the pile cools, beneficial fungi and actinomycetes take over. They break down tough materials like lignin and cellulose, and they produce humus. Humus is the dark, stable organic matter that gives soil its structure and water holding capacity. Curing takes two to four weeks. During curing, keep the pile moist but not wet. Turn it once a week. Do not add new ingredients. The pile will shrink by 30 to 50 percent. That is normal. When curing is complete, the manure should be dark brown to black, crumbly, and smell like forest floor. It should not smell like ammonia, rot, or alcohol. If it smells like ammonia, it is too wet or too rich in nitrogen. Add carbon and cure longer. If it smells like rot, it is too wet. Turn and add dry material. If it smells like alcohol, it is fermenting anaerobically. Turn and add oxygen. Now, quality control. You cannot sell manure that you have not tested. Start with simple field tests. The jar test: put a handful of manure in a jar of water, shake, and let it settle. Good manure will have a dark layer of humus on top, a clear middle layer, and a small layer of sand at the bottom. If the water is cloudy and brown, the manure is not fully cured. The seed germination test: plant ten maize or bean seeds in a small pot of your manure mixed with soil. If fewer than eight germinate, your manure is too salty or too acidic. The smell test: good manure smells like earth. Bad manure smells like ammonia or rot. The texture test: good manure crumbles in your hand. Bad manure is sticky or dusty. For commercial production, you need laboratory tests. In most African countries, you can send samples to a government soil laboratory or a university. Test for pH, organic matter, nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium, and heavy metals. The ideal pH for most crops is 6.0 to 7.0. If your manure is too acidic, add limestone. If it is too alkaline, add gypsum or more organic matter. Heavy metals like lead and cadmium must be below national limits. If you use municipal waste or industrial waste, test for heavy metals. Never sell manure that exceeds limits. Now, packaging and labelling. Your manure must be dry enough to store. Moisture should be below 20 percent. If it is too wet, it will grow mould in the bag. Dry it under shade on a concrete floor or tarpaulin. Turn it daily. When it is dry, screen it through a 5 millimetre sieve to remove stones and sticks. Pack in 50 kilogram bags or 25 kilogram bags. Use woven polypropylene bags with a liner to prevent moisture. Label each bag with your brand name, weight, batch number, production date, and expiry date. Expiry is usually two years if kept dry. Add a simple instruction leaflet: apply 2 to 4 tonnes per hectare for maize, 3 to 5 tonnes for vegetables, and 1 to 2 tonnes for fruit trees. Include your phone number. Now, the business side. Quality control is your reputation. One bad batch can destroy your brand. So test every batch. Keep samples from every batch for six months. If a farmer complains, you can test the sample and find out what went wrong. Offer a money back guarantee. That builds trust. In Africa, trust is the most valuable currency. A farmer who trusts your manure will tell ten other farmers. A farmer who does not trust you will tell one hundred. So do not cut corners. Ferment properly. Cure properly. Test properly. Package properly. That is how you build a manure business that lasts.

5. Pricing, Marketing, and Scaling the Business

In this final lesson we move from production to profit. You have learned how to source 33 ingredients, build the carbon and nitrogen base, add minerals and microbes, and ferment and cure. Now you must sell. The first rule of pricing is know your cost per kilogram. Add up all your costs: labour, transport, bags, labels, rent, water, and depreciation on tools. Divide by the number of kilograms produced. For example, if you spend 200 dollars to produce one tonne, your cost is 0.20 dollars per kilogram. Your selling price should be at least 1.5 to 2 times your cost. So sell at 0.30 to 0.40 dollars per kilogram. That is 15 to 20 dollars per 50 kilogram bag. In many African markets, chemical fertiliser sells for 30 to 50 dollars per 50 kilogram bag. Your organic manure should be cheaper, but not too cheap. If you sell too cheap, farmers think it is low quality. Price it at 60 to 70 percent of chemical fertiliser price. That gives you a profit and gives farmers a bargain. But remember, organic manure is not a direct substitute for chemical fertiliser in terms of nutrient concentration. It is a soil conditioner and a slow release nutrient source. So you must market it correctly. Do not say it will replace chemical fertiliser. Say it will reduce your chemical fertiliser use by half and improve your soil for years. That is a truthful and powerful message. Now, marketing. In Africa, word of mouth is the best marketing. So start with demonstration plots. Select five respected farmers in your area. Give them free manure for one season. Ask them to compare a small plot with manure and a small plot without. Take photos. Measure yields. At harvest, invite other farmers to see the difference. That is your marketing event. Serve food and drinks. Let the host farmer speak. Then sell your manure on the spot. Another marketing tool is the farmer field day. Work with local agricultural extension officers. They have credibility and networks. Offer to train farmers on composting and soil health. At the end of the training, sell your manure. Another tool is radio. In many African rural areas, radio is still king. Buy a five minute slot on a local station. Speak in the local language. Talk about soil health, not just manure. Give a phone number. Another tool is WhatsApp groups. Create a group for your customers. Share photos, tips, and success stories. Answer questions. That builds loyalty. Now, distribution. You can sell directly from your production site. You can sell through agrodealers. You can sell through cooperatives. You can sell through NGOs and government programmes. Each channel has different margins. Direct sales give you the highest margin but require you to handle retail. Agrodealers give you volume but take 20 to 30 percent margin. Cooperatives give you access to many farmers but may delay payment. NGOs and government programmes give you large orders but require paperwork and quality certification. Start with direct sales and agrodealers. Then add cooperatives. Then pursue NGO and government contracts. Now, scaling. To scale, you must standardise your recipe and your process. Write a production manual. Train your workers. Use the same recipe every time. Invest in equipment: a cement mixing floor, a shredder, a sieve, a weighing scale, a sealing machine. These reduce labour and increase output. Consider buying a small tractor with a front loader if you produce more than 50 tonnes per year. Consider making pellets if you want to sell to urban gardeners and landscapers. Pellets are easier to transport and apply. Consider making liquid manure extracts for foliar feeding. These sell at a premium. Consider licensing your brand to producers in other regions. You provide the recipe, the training, and the brand. They produce and sell. You take a royalty. That is how you grow without owning every factory. Finally, keep records. You need to know your production cost, your sales, your profit, and your customer list. Use a simple notebook or a phone app. Without records, you cannot grow. With records, you can get a loan from a bank or an investor. In Africa, many banks are now lending to agribusinesses. But they need to see records. So start today. Your 33 ingredient manure is not just a product. It is a movement to restore African soils, reduce dependence on imported fertiliser, and create jobs. You are not just a producer. You are a soil doctor, a business person, and a leader. Go and build.

Module 6: Practical Reference Field Guide to the 33-Ingredient Organic Manure

This lesson is a working reference for the operator, not a theory chapter. By Module 6 you have already selected ingredients, built your carbonnitrogen base, added minerals and microbes, and run fermentation and curing. What follows is the desk-side and field-side companion: what each ingredient class actually contributes, how much to use, what it costs in an African market, and where the safety lines sit. Keep this lesson printed and pinned in the store room. Every figure below is sourced so you can defend your formulation to a buyer, a regulator, or a cooperative board.

Business reality first: a 33-ingredient manure is a premium product, not a bulk commodity. In Kenya, Ghana, Nigeria, and Zambia, well-branded organic amendments sell at 24 the price of raw cattle manure per kilogram because they solve a specific problem depleted soils, poor water retention, low microbial activity and because the buyer can read a label. Your margin comes from formulation discipline, not from volume. The tables below are your cost and dosage control sheet.

SAFETY WARNING READ BEFORE HANDLING RAW MANURE OR MINERAL AMENDMENTS

Raw poultry litter, cattle slurry, and untreated sewage sludge can carry Salmonella, E. coli O157:H7, Campylobacter, and helminth eggs. WHO guidelines require that manure applied to food crops be composted or otherwise treated to reduce pathogens before field use. Do not apply raw manure to leafy vegetables or root crops eaten raw. Wear gloves, boots, and a dust mask when turning or bagging. Wash hands and tools after handling. Rock phosphate and wood ash are respiratory irritants keep dust down with light watering and never bag in a closed room without ventilation. Keep all amendments out of reach of children and away from drinking-water sources by at least 30 m.

Source: WHO, Guidelines for the safe use of wastewater, excreta and greywater, Volume 2 (2006); FAO/WHO, Microbiological risk assessment of fresh produce (2019).

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Weigh and log every ingredient. A 33-ingredient batch fails when one input is guessed. Record kg, supplier, and date for each of the 33 lines.
  2. Test C:N before you build. Target 2530:1. If above 35:1, add poultry litter or slurry; if below 20:1, add maize stover or rice husk.
  3. Moisture at 5060% during fermentation, 4050% at curing. Squeeze test: a handful should drip one or two drops, not stream.
  4. Turn on temperature, not on the calendar. Turn when the pile hits 6065 C, then again when it falls below 45 C. Three to five turns is normal.
  5. Run the maturity test before bagging. A seed germination test at 80% or better on cress or maize means the manure is safe to sell.
  6. Check pH. Finished product should sit at 6.57.5. Wood ash pushes it up; rock phosphate and gypsum pull it down.
  7. Bag with a label that states N-P-K, organic matter %, and batch date. Buyers pay for information, not just for manure.
  8. Store on pallets, under cover, off the floor. Moisture re-entry after curing restarts fermentation and ruins shelf life.
