1. Pressure Testing Your System with a Hand Pump

After you finish installing a PVC water line, you must pressure test it before covering trenches or connecting fixtures. A hand pump (hydrostatic test pump) is the standard tool. For most residential systems in Africa, test at 1.5 times the working pressure. If your pump delivers 40 psi (2.8 bar) normal pressure, test at 60 psi (4.1 bar). Never exceed 80 psi for PVC. Close all outlets, fill the system with water, and pump until the gauge reads your target. Wait 30 minutes. If pressure drops more than 10%, you have a leak. Mark the gauge reading, then walk the entire line looking for wet spots, drips, or hissing. Common leak points: threaded joints, solvent-welded elbows, and tees. If no visible leak but pressure drops, isolate sections with ball valves and retest each part. Use a hand pump with a built-in check valve and a 0-100 psi gauge. In hot climates, water expands, so test early morning or evening. Record the starting pressure, time, and final pressure on a checklist. If the test passes, release pressure slowly by opening a drain valve. Never leave a pressurized system overnight without checking. For large farms or estates, test in sections of 50-100 meters. A simple soapy water solution brushed on joints will bubble if air escapes. Remember: a failed test now saves you from digging up a driveway later. Always test before backfilling.

2. Identifying and Fixing Common Leaks (Joints, Cracks, Threads)

Leaks waste water and money. The three most common leaks in PVC plumbing are at solvent-welded joints, cracked pipes, and threaded connections. First, dry the area and wrap toilet paper around suspect points. A wet spot shows the leak. For a leaking solvent joint, cut out the fitting with a hacksaw, leaving 5 cm of pipe on each side. Use a coupling and new fittings, clean with PVC cleaner, apply PVC cement, and twist a quarter turn. Hold for 30 seconds. For a crack in a straight pipe, cut out the cracked section and replace with a coupling and new pipe. If the crack is near a fitting, replace the fitting. For threaded leaks, unscrew the fitting, clean old Teflon tape or paste, apply 3-4 wraps of new Teflon tape clockwise, and tighten by hand plus one quarter turn with a wrench. Do not overtightenPVC threads crack easily. For a quick temporary fix on a pinhole leak, use a rubber patch and hose clamp, but replace properly within a week. In Africa, many leaks come from poor cementing: not enough cement, dirty surfaces, or moving the joint while curing. Always let solvent welds cure 24 hours before pressure testing. For underground leaks, dig a hole 30 cm wider than the pipe, dry the area, and use a repair coupling. Keep a leak repair kit: hacksaw, couplings, cement, Teflon tape, and a small torch for drying. Teach customers to check their water meter when all taps are offif it spins, there's a leak.

3. Dealing with Water Hammer and Noise in Pipes

Water hammer is the loud banging or knocking sound when you close a tap quickly. It happens because water moving at speed suddenly stops, sending a shockwave through the pipe. In PVC systems, this can crack joints or burst pipes over time. The main cause is high water velocity. Keep velocity below 1.5 meters per second (5 feet per second). For a 25 mm (1 inch) pipe, that's about 20 liters per minute. If your pump or main line is too powerful, install a pressure-reducing valve (PRV) set to 50 psi (3.5 bar). Another cause is loose pipesthey vibrate and knock against walls or each other. Secure pipes every 1 meter with plastic clamps or metal saddles. Use rubber inserts to absorb vibration. A water hammer arrestor (a small air chamber) near quick-closing valves like washing machine taps or flush valves absorbs the shock. You can make a simple arrestor with a 30 cm vertical pipe capped at the top, installed on a tee. For existing noise, first check for loose straps and tighten them. Then install a PRV if pressure is above 60 psi. If noise continues, add arrestors at each fixture. In Africa, many homes have direct pump systems without a pressure tank. Adding a 24-liter pressure tank with a bladder smooths flow and reduces hammer. Also, ensure all air is purged from the systemair pockets cause sputtering and noise. Open the highest tap and let water run until no air comes out. Finally, teach customers to close taps slowly, not slam them.

4. Preventing Backflow and Contamination

Backflow is when dirty water flows backwards into your clean water supply. This can happen if the main water pressure drops (e.g., during a power cut or pipe burst) and a contaminated sourcelike a bucket, a garden hose in a puddle, or a chemical tanksucks water back into the pipes. To prevent this, install a backflow preventer. The simplest is an air gap: leave a 2 cm gap between the tap and the highest water level in a sink or tank. For hose bibs, use a vacuum breaker (a small brass fitting that screws onto the tap). For whole-house protection, install a double-check valve assembly on the main line after the meter. In Africa, many homes fill rooftop tanks from the mains. If the mains pressure drops, tank water can siphon back. Install a check valve on the inlet pipe to the tank. Also, never connect a garden hose directly to a chemical sprayer without a backflow preventer. For boreholes, ensure the pump inlet is above any potential contamination source. Test for contamination by taking a water sample to a lab for coliform bacteria. If positive, shock chlorinate the well or tank with 1 liter of household bleach per 1000 liters of water, let sit 12 hours, then flush. Always store drinking water in clean, covered containers. Mark all pipes: blue for potable water, green for recycled, yellow for gas. Train customers to never leave a hose submerged in a bucket or pool. A simple backflow preventer costs less than $10 and can save a family from typhoid or cholera.

5. Documenting Your Work with Photos and Checklists

Good documentation protects you and your customer. Before you start, take photos of the existing plumbing, walls, and floors. Note any cracks or damage. During installation, photograph each stage: trench depth, pipe layout, joints before covering, and pressure test gauge readings. Use your phone cameraset it to show date and time. After completing, take photos of the final work, including labels on pipes and valves. Create a simple checklist for each job: 1) Pressure test passed? 2) All joints leak-free? 3) Pipes secured every 1 meter? 4) Backflow preventer installed? 5) Water hammer addressed? 6) System flushed and air purged? 7) Customer walkthrough done? Sign and date the checklist. Give a copy to the customer and keep one for your records. For large jobs, use a notebook or a free app like Google Keep to log daily progress. Include material receipts, test results, and any changes from the original plan. If a dispute arises, your photos and checklist are evidence. In Africa, where cash jobs are common, a written record builds trust. Also, photograph the water meter reading before and after to show no hidden leaks. For warranty, state clearly: 1-year warranty on labor, 6 months on PVC joints if pressure tested. Keep a folder for each customer. At the end, email or WhatsApp the photos and checklist to the customer. This professionalism wins referrals. Never skip documentationit takes 10 minutes but saves hours of arguing.

6. Customer Walkthrough and Handover

The final step is walking the customer through the system. Start by showing them the main shut-off valve and how to turn it off in an emergency. Test it together. Show them the pressure gauge and explain normal pressure (e.g., 40-50 psi). Point out all isolation valves for different zones. Demonstrate how to drain the system if they leave for a long time. Explain the pressure test results and show the checklist. Teach them to check for leaks monthly: turn off all taps, watch the water meterif it moves, call you. Show them how to open a tap slowly to avoid water hammer. For outdoor systems, show them how to winterize if applicable, but in most of Africa, explain how to protect pipes from sun with paint or insulation. Discuss water quality: if they have a tank, show them how to clean it every 6 months with bleach. Give them a simple maintenance schedule: check for leaks monthly, clean aerators every 3 months, test pressure annually. Provide your contact details and warranty terms in writing. Ask if they have any questions. Then, do a final live test: turn on all taps, flush toilets, run the pump, and check for noise or leaks. Let the customer see everything working. Finally, have them sign the handover form and give them a copy of all documents. A good walkthrough turns a customer into a repeat client. In Africa, word-of-mouth is powerfulhappy customers bring more jobs. Always leave the site clean and remove all debris. Your reputation is built on these final 30 minutes.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Troubleshooting and Quality Control for PVC & Plumbing Systems

In African plumbing and small-scale construction markets, your reputation is built on one thing: systems that don't leak, don't fail, and don't call you back. A plumber in Kumasi, a contractor in Nairobi, or a maintenance technician in Lagos who can pressure-test, diagnose, and document work properly charges more, wins repeat contracts, and avoids costly rework. This Practical Reference lesson gives you the field standards, data, and checklists to do exactly that and to prove it to your client.

Quality control is not a cost. It is a profit centre. Every leak you catch before handover is money saved on labour, materials, transport, and lost goodwill. Every checklist and photo you document is evidence that protects you in disputes and wins you the next job.

1. Pressure Testing Your System with a Hand Pump

Pressure testing is the single most important quality gate in plumbing. Test before backfilling, before closing walls, and before handover. For PVC and most rigid plastic pipe systems, the standard practice is a hydrostatic test: fill the line, purge air, pressurise with a hand pump, and hold.

Key field rules:

  • Test pressure: Typically 1.5 the system's maximum working pressure, or a minimum of 1.01.5 bar for low-pressure domestic lines. Never exceed the pipe's rated pressure PVC can shatter.
  • Hold time: Minimum 3060 minutes for small domestic systems; longer for larger or buried mains.
  • Temperature correction: Plastic pipes expand and contract with heat. In hot African conditions, pressure can rise in the sun and drop at night. Test during stable temperatures where possible.
  • Air purge: Trapped air gives false readings and dangerous stored energy. Purge all air before pressurising.
  • Safety: Never stand in line with a pressurised plastic pipe end-cap or fitting. Use proper test plugs and restrain all caps.

2. Identifying and Fixing Common Leaks

Leaks in PVC and plumbing systems almost always occur at joints, cracks, and threaded connections. Knowing where to look saves hours.

3. Dealing with Water Hammer and Noise in Pipes

Water hammer is the banging or knocking sound when a valve closes suddenly or a pump stops. It is not just noise it is destructive pressure spikes that crack pipes, blow joints, and damage fittings. In African systems with intermittent supply and high pump pressures, water hammer is a leading cause of premature failure.

Solutions:

  • Install water hammer arrestors near quick-closing valves and appliances.
  • Use slow-closing valves instead of quick-closing types where possible.
  • Add air chambers (vertical capped pipe sections) at the end of long runs.
  • Secure all pipes with clamps at recommended spacing loose pipes amplify noise.
  • Check pump non-return valves and ensure they close smoothly.

4. Preventing Backflow and Contamination

Backflow is when contaminated water flows backwards into the clean supply. In African contexts, this often happens when mains pressure drops and a dirty source (tank, well, or irrigation line) siphons back. The result is waterborne disease a serious health and business risk.

Prevention measures:

  • Install air gaps at all tanks and cisterns the most reliable backflow prevention.
  • Use double check valves or reduced pressure zone (RPZ) devices on mains connections.
  • Never cross-connect potable and non-potable lines.
  • Label all pipes clearly (blue = potable, other colours = non-potable).
  • Test backflow preventers annually.
RED SAFETY WARNING PRESSURISED PLASTIC PIPES

Never stand in line with a pressurised PVC pipe end-cap, test plug, or fitting. Plastic pipes store enormous energy and can shatter, causing severe injury or death. Always restrain caps, use proper test plugs, and keep all personnel clear during pressure testing. Never use compressed air to test plastic pipes use water. In hot African conditions, pressure can rise dangerously in the sun; test during cooler hours and monitor the gauge continuously.

5. Documenting Your Work with Photos and Checklists

Documentation is your business insurance. It proves you did the work correctly, protects you in disputes, and gives clients confidence. In African markets where word-of-mouth is everything, a professional handover pack sets you apart.

What to document:

  • Before and after photos of every installation stage.
  • Pressure test readings (time, pressure, duration) with a photo of the gauge.
  • Materials used brand, size, batch numbers where available.
  • Leak repairs photo of the fault and the fix.
  • Signed checklist from the client at handover.

Use a simple smartphone and a printed or digital checklist. Store backups in the cloud or send to the client via WhatsApp or email. This is low-cost, high-impact professionalism.

6. Customer Walkthrough and Handover

The handover is your final quality gate and your best marketing moment. Walk the client through the system:

  • Show the main isolation valve and how to shut off water in an emergency.
  • Explain the pressure test results and show the documentation.
  • Demonstrate how to check for leaks and what to do if one appears.
  • Provide a simple maintenance schedule (e.g., check valves every 6 months).
  • Get a signed handover checklist and take a photo of the completed work with the client.

Quick Reference Checklist (68 points)

  1. Purge air from the system before pressure testing.
  2. Test at 1.5 working pressure (minimum 1.01.5 bar) and hold 3060 minutes.
  3. Inspect all joints, threads, and fittings for leaks before backfilling.
  4. Install water hammer arrestors and secure pipes at correct spacing.
  5. Verify backflow prevention air gaps, check valves, no cross-connections.
  6. Photograph every stage and record pressure test readings.
  7. Complete a signed handover checklist with the client.
  8. Provide a maintenance schedule and emergency shut-off demonstration.
