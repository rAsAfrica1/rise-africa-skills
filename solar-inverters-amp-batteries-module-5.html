1. Choosing Solar Panels: Monocrystalline vs Polycrystalline

When you buy solar panels in Africa, you'll see two main types: monocrystalline (mono) and polycrystalline (poly). Mono panels are made from single-crystal silicon, so they look black and uniform. Poly panels are made from multiple silicon crystals, giving them a blue, speckled look. The key difference is efficiency: mono panels convert more sunlight into electricity per square meter. A typical mono panel is 20-22% efficient, while poly is 15-17%. This means for the same roof space, mono gives you more power. For example, a 300W mono panel might be 1.6m x 1m, while a 300W poly panel is 1.9m x 1m. If you have limited space, mono is better.

But mono panels cost more per watt. In many African markets, a 300W mono panel costs about $150-$200, while a 300W poly costs $120-$160. However, prices are falling, and the gap is shrinking. Also, mono panels perform slightly better in high heat, which matters in hot African climates. Poly panels are more affordable and work fine if you have plenty of space. For a small home with a 1kW system, you might need three 300W mono panels or four 300W poly panels. That's a big difference in roof space and mounting cost.

When choosing, consider your budget, available space, and climate. If you live in a dusty area, both types need regular cleaningâdust can cut output by 20% or more. Look for panels with at least 25-year performance warranty. Brands like Canadian Solar, Jinko, and Trina are common in Africa and reliable. Avoid cheap no-name panels; they often underperform and fail early. Check the label for voltage and current ratings: you need to match them to your charge controller. Voc (open-circuit voltage) and Isc (short-circuit current) are critical. Always buy from a reputable dealer who can provide test data. In the end, mono is best for tight spaces and high heat; poly is a budget-friendly choice for larger areas.

2. Mounting Panels on Roofs and Ground in African Sun

Mounting solar panels correctly is crucial for safety and performance. In Africa, you must consider strong sun, high temperatures, dust, and occasional strong winds. For roof mounting, first check your roof type: corrugated metal, tiles, or concrete. Metal roofs are easiestâuse Z-brackets with stainless steel bolts and rubber washers to prevent leaks. For tiles, use tile hooks that slide under the tiles. Never drill through tiles without sealing. For ground mounts, use galvanized steel or aluminum frames set in concrete footings. The panels should face the equator: north in the Southern Hemisphere, south in the Northern Hemisphere. Tilt angle should equal your latitude for year-round performance. For example, in Nairobi (1Â°S), tilt 5-10Â°; in Johannesburg (26Â°S), tilt 26Â°.

Airflow is critical. Panels get hotâup to 70Â°C in direct sunâand efficiency drops as temperature rises. Leave at least 10cm gap between the panel and roof or ground for air circulation. Mount panels at least 30cm above the ground to avoid dust, water, and animals. Use corrosion-resistant materials: stainless steel bolts, aluminum rails, and UV-resistant cable ties. In coastal areas, salt air corrodes quickly, so use marine-grade hardware. For wind, ensure the mounting can withstand at least 120 km/h gusts. In dusty regions, angle panels steeper (10-15Â° more than latitude) so dust slides off with rain.

Always use lock washers and tighten bolts securely, but don't overtighten and crack the panel frame. Connect the panel frames to a ground rod to prevent electric shock. For multiple panels, use rails to create a secure array. Avoid mounting panels flat on a roofâthey won't self-clean and will overheat. If you must, clean them weekly. In areas with heavy dust, consider a ground mount that's easier to clean. Remember, a poorly mounted panel can fly off in a storm or cause roof leaks. Take your time, use good tools (drill, wrench, level), and follow the manufacturer's instructions. If unsure, hire an experienced installerâit's worth the cost.

3. PWM vs MPPT Charge Controllers: Which to Use and Why

A charge controller sits between your solar panels and battery. It prevents overcharging and manages power flow. Two main types: PWM (Pulse Width Modulation) and MPPT (Maximum Power Point Tracking). PWM is simple and cheapâoften $10-$30 for a 30A unit. It works by connecting the panel directly to the battery when charging, then pulsing to reduce power as the battery fills. But this means the panel voltage is pulled down to the battery voltage. For example, a 100W panel with 18V Vmp connected to a 12V battery will only deliver about 12V x 5.5A = 66W. You lose about 30% of potential power. PWM is fine for small systems (under 200W) where cost matters more than efficiency.

MPPT is more complex and expensiveâ$50-$200 for a 30A unit. It uses electronics to track the panel's maximum power point, converting excess voltage into extra current. So a 100W panel at 18V and 5.5A can charge a 12V battery at about 7.5A (100W / 12V = 8.3A, minus losses). That's up to 30% more power. MPPT also allows you to use higher-voltage panels (e.g., 24V or 36V) with a 12V battery, which reduces cable losses. In hot climates, MPPT performs better because it adjusts to voltage changes. For any system over 200W, MPPT is worth the extra cost. It pays for itself in extra energy harvest, especially in winter or cloudy conditions.

When choosing, match the controller's voltage and current ratings to your system. A 30A MPPT can handle up to 30A output, so at 12V that's 360W max. Always buy a controller with at least 20% headroom. Look for features like temperature compensation, low-voltage disconnect, and USB ports. Brands like Victron, Epever, and Renogy are reliable. Avoid cheap controllers that claim MPPT but are actually PWM. Read reviews. For a typical African home with a 300W panel and 12V battery, an MPPT controller will give you noticeably more charging, especially on cloudy days. If you're on a tight budget and have a small panel, PWM is okay, but MPPT is the better long-term investment.

4. Wiring Panels to Charge Controller and Battery Safely

Wiring solar panels to a charge controller and battery requires care to avoid shocks, fires, and damaged equipment. First, gather tools: wire strippers, crimping tool, multimeter, screwdrivers, and appropriate cables. Use flexible solar cable (often 4mmÂ² or 6mmÂ²) with UV-resistant insulation. For a 300W panel at 12V, current is about 25A, so 6mmÂ² cable is safe for runs up to 5 meters. For longer runs, use thicker cable to reduce voltage drop. Always connect in this order: battery to controller first, then panels to controller. This lets the controller sense the battery voltage and set itself. Never connect panels firstâit can damage the controller.

Before wiring, cover the panels with a cloth to stop power generation. Connect the battery to the controller's battery terminals, observing polarity: red to positive, black to negative. Use ring terminals and tighten securely. Then connect the panels: positive to controller's PV+, negative to PV-. If using multiple panels, connect them in series or parallel depending on your controller's voltage limit. For a 12V system with a PWM controller, panels are usually parallel (positive to positive, negative to negative). For MPPT, series may be better to increase voltage and reduce current. Check your controller's max PV voltageânever exceed it. Use a combiner box with fuses for parallel strings. Fuses should be rated 1.25 times the panel's Isc.

After wiring, check all connections with a multimeter. Measure battery voltage at the controller terminalsâit should match the battery. Then uncover panels and check charging current. If zero, troubleshoot. Always use proper fusing: a fuse between battery and controller (e.g., 30A) protects against short circuits. Also, ground the controller and panel frames. Keep wires away from water and sharp edges. Use conduit where possible. Never work on live wires. If unsure, hire a qualified electrician. Safety first: DC arcs are dangerous. Double-check polarity before connecting. A wrong connection can fry your controller and battery. Take photos before disconnecting anything for future reference.

5. Preventing Overcharging and Deep Discharge

Overcharging and deep discharge are the two biggest killers of solar batteries. Overcharging happens when the charge controller fails to stop charging when the battery is full. This causes the battery to heat up, lose water (in flooded types), and corrode plates. In sealed batteries, it can cause swelling and explosion. Deep discharge happens when you drain the battery too low, often below 50% state of charge (SoC) for lead-acid, or below 20% for lithium. This shortens battery life dramatically. A lead-acid battery cycled to 50% might last 500 cycles; to 80% depth of discharge (DoD), only 200 cycles. So preventing both is key.

Your charge controller handles overcharge protection. Set the correct charging parameters: for a 12V flooded lead-acid battery, absorption voltage is about 14.4V, float 13.2V. For sealed (AGM/Gel), absorption 14.1V, float 13.2V. For lithium (LiFePO4), absorption 14.2-14.6V, float 13.6V. Many controllers have preset battery typesâselect the right one. Also set equalization if needed (only for flooded). Temperature compensation is important: in hot climates, reduce voltages by about 0.03V per Â°C above 25Â°C. Without this, you risk overcharging in heat. Check your controller manual for settings. Use a multimeter to verify voltages at the battery.

To prevent deep discharge, use a low-voltage disconnect (LVD) feature on your controller or inverter. Set LVD to 11.5V for lead-acid (about 50% SoC) or 10.5V for lithium (about 20% SoC). When voltage drops, the LVD cuts off loads. Also, monitor your battery with a shunt-based battery monitor (e.g., Victron BMV) to see SoC accurately. Avoid running heavy loads at night. Size your battery bank to have at least 2 days of autonomy. For example, if you use 1kWh per day, a 12V 200Ah battery (2.4kWh) gives you about 50% usable, so 1.2kWhâenough for one day. Add more batteries for backup. Regularly check water levels in flooded batteries and top up with distilled water. Keep terminals clean and tight. With proper settings and monitoring, your batteries can last 5-7 years instead of 2-3.

6. Troubleshooting Common Panel and Controller Issues

Even with good installation, problems happen. Here's how to troubleshoot common issues. First, if your system isn't charging, check the obvious: are panels clean? Dust, bird droppings, or shade can cut output by 50% or more. Clean with water and a soft brush early morning. Next, check connections: loose or corroded wires cause voltage drop. Use a multimeter to measure panel voltage at the controller. If it's zero, check panel cables and fuses. If voltage is low (e.g., 12V instead of 18V), a panel may be faulty or shaded. Test each panel individually. For parallel panels, one bad panel can drag down the whole array. Disconnect and test each one.

If the controller shows no lights, check battery voltage. If battery is dead (below 10V), the controller may not start. Charge the battery with a grid charger first. If controller is hot or smells burnt, it's likely damagedâreplace it. If it's not charging, check settings: is it set to the correct battery type? Is the PV voltage within range? Some controllers need PV voltage to be at least battery voltage +5V to start. For MPPT, if PV voltage is too low, it won't charge. Also, check for error codes (blinking lights). Common codes: overvoltage, overtemp, reverse polarity. Fix accordingly.

If your battery is overcharging, the controller may be faulty or set to wrong voltage. Measure battery voltage during charging; if it exceeds 14.8V for lead-acid, stop charging and adjust settings. If battery is not holding charge, it may be old or sulfated. Load test it: a healthy 12V battery should read 12.6V fully charged, and not drop below 10.5V under load. If it drops quickly, replace it. For panels, check for cracks or hot spots using a thermal camera or by feeling for unusually hot areas. Bypass diodes can fail, causing partial panel failure. If a panel's voltage is zero, check the junction box for loose connections. Always turn off the system before working on it. Keep a log of voltages and currents to spot trends. Most issues are due to loose connections, dirty panels, or wrong settings. Regular maintenance prevents 80% of problems.

Mounting in African Sun

Roof mounts must respect the roof: corrugated iron needs proper Z-brackets and sealing washers, tile roofs need hooks, and thatch or shingle requires stand-off rails. Ground mounts need concrete footings or driven posts, and â critically â clearance of at least 0.5â1 m to allow airflow and to survive flash floods in the rainy season. In dusty regions, tilt panels 5â10Â° above the latitude-optimal angle so rain and washing water self-clean. PWM vs MPPT: The Controller Decision

A PWM controller simply connects the array to the battery for part of each cycle, forcing the panel to operate near battery voltage. An MPPT controller tracks the array's maximum power point and converts the surplus voltage into extra current. The gain is roughly the ratio of array Vmp to battery voltage, minus conversion losses (typically 92â98%).

Solar arrays cannot be switched off by daylight. A single 550 W panel can produce over 50 V open-circuit and several amps; a series string can exceed 600 V DC, which is lethal and does not self-extinguish like AC. Cover panels with an opaque sheet before working on wiring. Never short a battery to test it â a 12 V 200 Ah lithium bank can deliver thousands of amps and will weld tools, melt jewellery and cause arc flash. Use insulated tools, remove metal watches and rings, and wear safety glasses. Ventilated lead-acid batteries release explosive hydrogen during charging. Lithium batteries must use a BMS and a charge controller set to the exact chemistry profile â wrong settings cause thermal runaway and fire. Install a DC-rated isolator and a fuse or breaker on the battery positive. In lightning-prone regions, bond array frames and mount a DC surge protection device. If you are not trained for DC work, do not open the controller or battery enclosure.

Preventing Overcharging and Deep Discharge

Overcharge cooks lead-acid batteries and stresses lithium. Deep discharge below 50% state of charge (lead-acid) or below the BMS low-voltage cutoff (lithium) shortens life dramatically. Set the controller's absorption, float and equalisation voltages to the battery manufacturer's data sheet â not to a generic default. Use temperature compensation if the controller supports it, because African battery rooms routinely swing between 15 Â°C at night and 40 Â°C+ by afternoon. For lithium, disable equalisation and set the low-temperature charge cutoff if the BMS does not already do it. Size the battery so daily depth of discharge stays under 50% for lead-acid and under 80% for LiFePOâ. A battery monitor with a shunt gives you the state of charge and is worth every dollar on a commercial installation.

Troubleshooting Common Panel and Controller Issues

Work through the system in order: array, wiring, controller, battery, load. Measure open-circuit voltage at the array, then at the controller input, then the battery voltage at the controller terminals. A big drop between array and controller means a broken connector, blown fuse or corroded MC4. A controller that is hot and showing low output may be in derating due to ambient temperature â improve ventilation. If the battery never reaches float, either the array is undersized, there is a parasitic load, or the absorption voltage is set too low. If the controller shows PV voltage but no charge current, suspect reverse polarity, a failed controller or a battery that is already full. In dusty conditions, a 30â40% output loss from soiling is normal â clean panels monthly or more often during harmattan or dry season.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Size the array first from daily kWh demand, then pick mono or poly on price per watt and available area.
  2. Choose MPPT for arrays over 200 W or any long cable run; PWM only for tiny 12 V kits.
  3. Match controller voltage and current to the array Voc (with cold-temperature margin) and battery bank voltage.
  4. Calculate voltage drop and keep it under 3% on the PV side and under 2% on the battery side.
  5. Fuse and isolate the battery positive and the PV positive; use DC-rated devices only.
  6. Set charge voltages to the battery data sheet, enable temperature compensation, and disable equalisation for lithium.
  7. Bond and earth array frames and mount a DC surge protection device in lightning-prone areas.
  8. Clean, inspect and log monthly: panel soiling, connector corrosion, terminal torque and state of charge.
