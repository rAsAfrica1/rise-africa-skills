1. Introduction to Curtain Walls and Shopfronts

Curtain walls and shopfronts are large aluminum and glass systems that cover building facades or storefronts. Unlike windows, they don't support the building's weight; they hang off the structure. In Africa, these are common in malls, banks, and office buildings in cities like Nairobi, Lagos, and Johannesburg. To fabricate a basic shopfront, you'll need 2" x 4" aluminum tubes (or 50mm x 100mm), 6mm tempered glass, and gaskets. Start by measuring the opening accurately using a laser measure or steel tape. Cut the vertical mullions (uprights) and horizontal rails (transoms) with a mitre saw at 45-degree angles for corners. Use a router to cut weep holes for drainage. Assemble with corner cleats and stainless steel screws. For curtain walls, you need a pressure plate system: the glass sits on setting blocks, then a pressure plate compresses the gasket. A typical curtain wall panel is 1.5m wide by 3m high. You'll need a spider crane or a team of four to lift panels into place. Always check with a spirit level and plumb bob. In Africa, where temperatures can exceed 40C, use thermal breaks (see Lesson 3) to prevent expansion issues. Safety: wear gloves, safety glasses, and harnesses when working at height. A successful shopfront installation can earn $50-$100 per square meter in labour, so mastering this opens lucrative contracts.

2. Fabricating Large Sliding Doors and Bi-Fold Doors

Large sliding doors and bi-fold doors are popular for patios, balconies, and open-plan living in African homes. A typical sliding door can be 3m wide and 2.4m high, using 2mm thick aluminum profiles. For sliding doors, you need top and bottom tracks, rollers, and a locking mechanism. Start by cutting the frame: verticals at 2.4m, horizontals at 1.5m each for a two-panel door. Use a mitre saw with a 45-degree blade for clean corners. Assemble with corner stakes and screws. Install rollers on the bottom of each panel; top rollers guide the panel. For bi-fold doors, you need a track system with hinges and pivots. A four-panel bi-fold might have two panels folding to each side. Cut panels at 600mm wide each. Use heavy-duty hinges every 600mm. In Africa, where dust and heat are issues, use nylon rollers instead of steel to reduce noise and wear. Test the door by sliding it back and forth; adjust rollers with a screwdriver until it moves smoothly. For large openings, add a mid-rail for strength. A well-made sliding door can cost $200-$400 in materials, and you can charge $500-$800 installed. Always use tempered glass for safety. If the door is wider than 2.4m, consider a bottom track with a drainage channel to prevent water pooling during rainy seasons.

3. Using Thermal Breaks for Energy Efficiency

Thermal breaks are barriers between the inside and outside of an aluminum frame that reduce heat transfer. In hot African climates, they keep cool air in and heat out, cutting air conditioning costs by up to 30%. A thermal break is typically a polyamide strip inserted between two aluminum profiles. To make a thermal-broken window, you need a 'thermal break' profile, which is two aluminum sections joined by a reinforced plastic strip. You can buy these profiles pre-made, or use a 'pour and debridge' method: pour polyurethane into a channel, let it cure, then cut away the aluminum bridge. For a small workshop, pre-made profiles are easier. When fabricating, cut and assemble as normal, but ensure the thermal break is not damaged. Use a thermal break saw blade (carbide-tipped) to avoid melting the plastic. Install low-E glass (with a metallic coating) to further improve efficiency. In Africa, where electricity is expensive, this is a selling point: tell customers they'll save on bills. Test with a thermal camera or simply touch the frame on a hot day: a thermal-broken frame will be cooler. Cost: thermal break profiles cost about 20-30% more than standard, but you can charge 40-50% more. For a typical 1.2m x 1.5m window, the extra cost is $20-$30, but the customer saves $100+ per year on cooling. Always seal gaps with silicone to prevent air leaks.

4. Automating Windows and Doors

Automation adds motors, sensors, and controls to windows and doors for convenience, security, and energy efficiency. In Africa, automated gates and windows are increasingly common in upscale homes and businesses. Start with simple automation: a motorized sliding gate or a window opener. For windows, you need a linear actuator (12V or 24V), a remote control, and a transformer. A basic kit costs $80-$150. Install the actuator on the window frame, connecting it to the sash with a bracket. Wire it to a control box with a receiver. Test the limit switches so it stops at full open and close. For doors, use a sliding door motor (e.g., for a 2m wide door, a 600kg capacity motor). Mount the motor on the top track, attach a belt or rack to the door. Add safety sensors (infrared beams) to prevent crushing. In Africa, power outages are common, so include a battery backup (12V 7Ah) and a solar panel (20W) to keep it running. Automation can also be integrated with smart home systems like Google Home or Alexa using Wi-Fi modules. For commercial buildings, automatic sliding doors with motion sensors are standard. Cost: a basic automated window costs $200-$300 installed; a sliding gate $500-$1000. Learn basic electrical wiring: use a multimeter to check voltage, and always fuse the circuit. Safety: never put fingers near moving parts during testing. Automation is a high-value skill that can double your income.

5. Expanding to Multiple Crews and Locations

Scaling from a one-man workshop to multiple crews and locations requires systems, trust, and cash flow management. In Africa, successful aluminum businesses often start in a garage and grow to serve several cities. First, document your processes: write step-by-step guides for measuring, cutting, assembling, and installing. Use a checklist for each job. Then hire and train apprentices. A good rule: one experienced fabricator can supervise two apprentices. Pay daily or weekly; in many African countries, daily pay is expected. For a new location, start with a small rented space ($100-$300 per month) and a pickup truck for deliveries. Buy tools in bulk to save: a mitre saw ($200), a drill ($50), and a welding machine ($300). Use a central workshop for cutting and a satellite assembly team for installations. Track jobs with a simple notebook or a free app like Trello. Cash flow: require 50% deposit before ordering materials, 40% on delivery, and 10% after installation. Keep a reserve for slow months. Insurance: get liability insurance for accidents. In Kenya, for example, a growing aluminum company might have crews in Nairobi, Mombasa, and Kisumu. Communication is key: use WhatsApp groups for daily updates. Don't expand too fast; add one crew every 6-12 months. A crew of four can install 10 windows per day. With three crews, you can handle large contracts like schools or hospitals. Always pay your workers fairly to reduce turnover.

6. Staying Updated with New Products and Trends

The aluminum industry changes fast: new profiles, finishes, and smart features appear yearly. To stay competitive in Africa, you must keep learning. Follow manufacturers like Hydro, SAPA, and local suppliers on social media. Attend trade shows such as the Big 5 Construct in Kenya or Nigeria. Subscribe to magazines like 'Aluminium International Today' or join online forums. Key trends: slimline profiles for minimal sightlines, matte black and bronze finishes, and integrated blinds between glass panes. Also, sustainability: recycled aluminum is growing. In Africa, where solar gain is high, low-E glass and thermal breaks are in demand. For automation, voice control and app-based systems are hot. To test new products, order samples and try them in your workshop. For example, try a new gasket that resists UV better. Network with other fabricators: join a local association. Use YouTube to watch installation videos. When you learn something new, train your crew. Keep a library of catalogs and price lists. Update your quotes with current material costs; aluminum prices fluctuate with global markets. A simple habit: spend 30 minutes each week reading industry news. This helps you advise customers on the best options and charge premium prices. For instance, if you master a new 'lift and slide' door system, you can charge 20% more. Remember, your reputation grows when you offer the latest solutions. Don't be afraid to experiment on small projects before offering to clients.

<h2>Practical Reference: Scaling Up and Advanced Techniques in Aluminium Windows &amp; Doors</h2>

<p>This practical reference supports the RISE AFRICA Skills module <strong>Scaling Up and Advanced Techniques</strong> for the course <strong>Aluminium Windows &amp; Doors</strong>. It is written for fabricators, installers, workshop owners and site supervisors who already handle standard windows and doors and now want to move into larger, more technical and more profitable work: curtain walls, shopfronts, large sliding and bi-fold doors, thermal breaks, automation, multi-crew operations and staying current with a fast-changing product market.</p>

<p>In most African markets from Lagos and Accra to Nairobi, Johannesburg and Dar es Salaam the aluminium fabrication sector is growing quickly because of urban construction, retail expansion, higher-end residential demand and the need for energy-conscious buildings. The businesses that win are not only those that can weld and install, but those that can <strong>price correctly, standardise production, manage multiple sites and guarantee quality</strong>. This module gives you the technical and commercial reference material to do that.</p>

<h3>1. Curtain Walls and Shopfronts: The Commercial Gateway</h3>

<p>Curtain walls are non-structural, self-supporting external cladding systems, usually made of aluminium framing with glass, that hang off the building structure. Shopfronts are simpler, ground-floor glazed systems for retail units. Both are high-value jobs that require accurate surveying, structural understanding and careful sequencing. In Africa, curtain wall and shopfront demand is concentrated in malls, banks, offices, hotels, hospitals and fuel-station forecourts.</p>

<p>Key technical points for fabrication and installation:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Design loads:</strong> wind load is the governing factor in many coastal and high-rise African cities (Mombasa, Dar es Salaam, Luanda, Cape Town). Check local wind speed maps and structural engineer requirements.</li>
<li><strong>Movement:</strong> allow for thermal expansion, seismic movement and building deflection. Use slotted connections and expansion joints, not rigid welds to the slab.</li>
<li><strong>Drainage:</strong> curtain wall systems are drained and ventilated. Weep holes, baffles and pressure-equalised chambers must be installed exactly as the system supplier details.</li>
<li><strong>Glass:</strong> specify laminated, toughened or double-glazed units according to safety, thermal and acoustic requirements. In hot climates, solar-control glass reduces cooling load dramatically.</li>
<li><strong>Anchors:</strong> use tested anchors into concrete or steel. In many African projects, anchor pull-out tests are required by the engineer.</li>
</ul>

<h3>2. Fabricating Large Sliding Doors and Bi-Fold Doors</h3>

<p>Large sliders (2.4 m to 6 m openings) and bi-folds (up to 6 m or more) are common in high-end residential estates, guest houses and lodges across Africa. They are heavier, more sensitive to alignment and more profitable than standard windows. Mistakes are expensive: a poorly aligned 4 m slider will drag, leak and sag.</p>

<p>Practical rules:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Track support:</strong> the bottom track must be level and fully supported. On suspended slabs, use a continuous structural base, not spot shims.</li>
<li><strong>Weight calculation:</strong> glass weight is roughly 2.5 kg per m per mm of thickness. A 6 m 10 mm toughened panel weighs about 150 kg. Rollers must be rated above this with a safety margin.</li>
<li><strong>Reinforcement:</strong> long mullions and stiles need steel or aluminium inserts to resist wind and self-weight deflection.</li>
<li><strong>Drainage:</strong> bottom tracks must have drainage slots and sill heights designed for local rainfall intensity.</li>
<li><strong>Bi-fold stacking:</strong> plan the stack position, hinge direction and traffic door clearly on the shop drawing before fabrication.</li>
</ul>

<h3>3. Using Thermal Breaks for Energy Efficiency</h3>

<p>Thermal breaks separate the inner and outer aluminium profiles with a non-conductive material (usually polyamide). This reduces heat transfer, condensation and cooling load. In hot African climates, thermal breaks matter most when the building is air-conditioned or when the client wants lower energy bills. In cooler highland regions (Nairobi, Addis Ababa, Johannesburg winter), they reduce heating demand and condensation.</p>

<p>Commercial note: thermal break systems cost more in materials and require different fabrication (crimping or rolling the polyamide strip). Price them as a premium product, not a default, unless the specification demands it. Many African projects now require U-values or green-building certification (EDGE, IFC, Green Star SA), which makes thermal breaks a sales advantage.</p>

<h3>4. Automating Windows and Doors</h3>

<p>Automation is growing in Africa for security, accessibility, smoke ventilation and high-end residential convenience. Common systems include automatic sliding doors for malls and hospitals, motorised windows for smoke exhaust, and smart home integration.</p>

<p>Key considerations:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Power reliability:</strong> always specify battery backup or manual override. Grid interruptions are common.</li>
<li><strong>Safety sensors:</strong> automatic doors must have presence and motion sensors to prevent injury.</li>
<li><strong>Integration:</strong> choose systems that work with local voltages (220240 V, 50 Hz) and are serviceable locally.</li>
<li><strong>Warranty and spares:</strong> importers should stock motors, controllers and sensors. A door that cannot be repaired becomes a liability.</li>
</ul>

<h3>5. Expanding to Multiple Crews and Locations</h3>

<p>Scaling from one crew to several is a business systems problem, not just a technical one. The fabricators who succeed standardise drawings, cutting lists, quality checks and site reporting. They also separate fabrication from installation so that crews can work in parallel.</p>

<p>Recommended operating model:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Central fabrication hub</strong> with fixed machinery and trained cutters.</li>
<li><strong>Mobile installation crews</strong> (35 people) with their own tools, vehicles and checklists.</li>
<li><strong>Site supervisor</strong> responsible for measuring, snagging and client sign-off.</li>
<li><strong>Storekeeper</strong> controlling profiles, glass, gaskets and hardware to prevent theft and waste.</li>
<li><strong>Weekly production meeting</strong> to review jobs, cash flow and quality issues.</li>
</ul>

<h3>6. Staying Updated with New Products and Trends</h3>

<p>Aluminium systems change fast: new thermal break profiles, slim sightlines, concealed hardware, smart glass, solar shading and low-E coatings. African fabricators can stay current through supplier training, trade fairs (Big 5 Construct, Africa Build), manufacturer technical manuals and online catalogues. The businesses that invest in learning win the specification work.</p>

<strong style="color:#c0392b"> SAFETY WARNING Glass Handling, Lifting and Curtain Wall Installation</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#7b241c">Large glass panels, curtain wall units and automated doors are heavy and dangerous. In African site conditions, the biggest risks are manual lifting, unstable scaffolding, unsecured panels in wind, and lack of PPE. Always use mechanical lifting equipment (vacuum lifters, hoists, scissor lifts) for panels above 50 kg. Never lift glass by hand above shoulder height. Secure all panels against wind before leaving site. Use cut-resistant gloves, safety boots, helmets and eye protection. Ensure scaffolding is inspected and tagged. Train every crew member on emergency response and first aid. A single dropped curtain wall panel can kill no deadline justifies unsafe lifting.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Survey twice, fabricate once:</strong> measure openings, levels, plumb and structural openings before cutting.</li>
<li><strong>Confirm system and glass specification:</strong> wind load, thermal break, safety glass, U-value and colour.</li>
<li><strong>Check anchor and fixing design:</strong> pull-out capacity, edge distance, corrosion protection.</li>
<li><strong>Verify drainage and ventilation:</strong> weep holes, baffles, sill heights, gaskets and sealants.</li>
<li><strong>Use lifting equipment for heavy panels:</strong> vacuum lifters, hoists or crane; never manual lift above 50 kg.</li>
<li><strong>Sign off quality before invoicing:</strong> operation, alignment, locking, sealing, snag list closed.</li>
<li><strong>Document every job:</strong> drawings, cutting lists, delivery notes, installation photos, warranty.</li>
<li><strong>Train crews continuously:</strong> new products, safety, automation, thermal systems and customer care.</li>
</ol>
