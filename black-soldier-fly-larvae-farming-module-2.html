Lessons

1. Selecting a Suitable Site

Choosing the right site for your Black Soldier Fly (BSF) colony is the foundation of your farm. In Africa, you need a location that is warm, shaded, and protected from heavy rain and strong winds. BSF thrive between 25C and 30C. If temperatures drop below 20C, breeding slows; above 35C, adults die quickly. So avoid direct afternoon sun. A site under a tree canopy or a simple shade net (70% shade) works well. In hotter regions like the Sahel, use a double-layer shade net or a grass-thatched roof.

Your site must be close to your waste source. BSF larvae eat kitchen waste, market refuse, and animal manure. If you have to carry waste more than 100 metres daily, you will soon give up. Also, ensure a water source nearby for cleaning and humidity control. But avoid low-lying areas that flood during rains. A gentle slope helps with drainage. In many African cities, a small backyard (3m x 5m) is enough for a starter colony.

Security and access matter. BSF enclosures attract ants, rats, and birds. Ants can wipe out a colony in days. So place your site on a concrete slab or hard-packed ground, and use ant barriers like ash or used engine oil around the legs of your enclosures. Keep the site fenced or locked if possible. Also, neighbours may complain about smell if you mismanage waste. So site your colony at least 10 metres away from homes and 20 metres from wells or boreholes to avoid contamination.

Finally, consider expansion. Start small but leave room to add more cages or bins. A site that allows you to grow from one cage to ten without moving is ideal. Also, check local bylaws. Some municipalities require permits for insect farming. Visit your local agriculture office or council to confirm. With the right site, your BSF colony will be productive and low-maintenance.

2. Designing Adult Fly Enclosures

Adult BSF need a secure, well-ventilated enclosure to mate and lay eggs. A simple design used across Africa is a cube-shaped cage made from wooden frames and mesh. The standard size for a starter colony is 1.5m x 1.5m x 1.5m. Use untreated timber (avoid termite-prone wood) and cover it with insect-proof mesh. The mesh size should be 1-2mm to keep out ants, small flies, and lizards, but allow air flow. Shade netting (70%) can be used on the sides for ventilation, but the top must be solid mesh to prevent escape.

The cage must have a door for access. Use a zipper or a simple latch. Inside, hang a black plastic sheet or cardboard as a resting surface for adults. BSF adults are attracted to bright light, so place the cage in a shaded area but with indirect sunlight. Do not put it in full sun, as adults overheat. Also, provide a shallow dish of water with a sponge or cotton wool for the flies to drink. Without water, they die in 2-3 days.

For egg collection, place a small container with attractant (see Lesson 5) near the top of the cage. The container should have small holes (2-3mm) so females can lay eggs but not escape. A common design uses a plastic bucket with a lid, filled with moist chicken feed or fruit waste. The females lay egg clusters in the crevices. You can also use corrugated cardboard strips as egg-laying sites.

Ensure the cage is easy to clean. Line the floor with a removable tray or plastic sheet. Adult flies produce frass and dead flies, which must be removed every 2-3 days to prevent disease. In humid African climates, mould can grow on mesh; clean with a mild bleach solution monthly. Finally, secure the cage to the ground with stakes or weights. Wind can topple it. A well-designed enclosure costs about $30-$50 in materials and can last 2-3 years if maintained.

3. Attracting Wild BSF to Start a Colony

You do not need to buy BSF to start. In most African regions, wild BSF are present, especially in warm, humid areas. To attract them, you need to create a breeding trap. The simplest method is to use a black plastic bucket (20-30 litres) with a lid. Cut a hole (5cm diameter) in the lid and insert a piece of PVC pipe or a funnel made from a plastic bottle. The funnel should point inward, so flies can enter but not easily find their way out. Inside the bucket, place a strong attractant: fermented fruit waste, fish waste, or a mixture of water and molasses. The smell attracts BSF adults.

Hang the bucket in a shaded area near your future colony site. Check it daily. You will likely catch blowflies and houseflies first, but after a few days, BSF adults will appear. They are black, slender, and have a distinctive wasp-like waist. Once you see BSF inside, you can transfer them to your adult enclosure (Lesson 2). But be careful: wild BSF may carry parasites. So quarantine them for 2-3 days and provide only clean water and sugar solution.

Another method is to set up a 'bait station' using a shallow tray filled with moist chicken feed or palm kernel cake. Place it in a shady spot. Female BSF will lay eggs in the crevices of the tray. Collect the eggs using a soft brush or spoon and place them in a hatching container with moist feed. This is less risky than trapping adults. In West Africa, farmers often use a mixture of cassava peels and water to attract BSF. In East Africa, banana and mango waste works well.

Timing matters. BSF are most active during the warm rainy season. Start your attraction efforts then. If you see no BSF after two weeks, you may need to buy eggs or neonates (Lesson 4). Also, avoid using pesticides near your attractant; they kill BSF. Once you have a few adults, they will multiply quickly. A single female lays 500-900 eggs, so a small starter colony can grow fast.

4. Purchasing BSF Eggs or Neonates

If wild BSF are scarce or you want to start faster, buying eggs or neonates (newly hatched larvae) is a good option. In Africa, you can buy from established BSF farms, agricultural research institutes, or online suppliers. Prices vary: a gram of eggs (about 10,000-15,000 eggs) costs $5-$20, while neonates are sold by weight or count. For a starter colony, buy 5-10 grams of eggs or 50-100 grams of neonates. Always buy from a reputable supplier to avoid diseases or low hatch rates.

When purchasing, ask for eggs that are 1-2 days old. They should be light cream to yellowish and look like tiny rice grains. If they are brown or black, they are too old or dead. Neonates are small, white, and wriggling. Check for movement under a magnifying glass. Avoid suppliers who cannot show you their breeding stock or who sell eggs in dirty containers. In many African countries, you can find BSF suppliers through farmer groups, WhatsApp groups, or agricultural shows.

Shipping is critical. Eggs and neonates are fragile. They must be transported in a cool, ventilated container. Use a small plastic box with moist paper towel or coco peat. Do not expose them to direct sun or temperatures above 30C. If shipping takes more than 2 days, opt for neonates, as they survive longer. On arrival, immediately transfer them to a rearing bin with moist feed (e.g., blended kitchen waste). Keep the feed at 60-70% moisture. Neonates will start feeding within hours.

If you buy eggs, place them on a fine mesh or cloth over moist feed. They hatch in 2-4 days. Do not bury them; they need air. After hatching, the neonates will drop into the feed. Keep the bin covered with a lid that allows air but keeps out ants. Expect 70-90% hatch rate if eggs are fresh. If you buy neonates, they are already feeding, so you save time. But they are more expensive. For a beginner, buying 10 grams of eggs is cheaper and gives you a larger colony. Always quarantine new stock for 3 days to check for mites or disease.

5. Creating a Breeding Habitat

A breeding habitat for BSF adults must mimic their natural environment: warm, humid, and with plenty of egg-laying sites. Inside your adult enclosure (Lesson 2), you need to provide three things: water, food, and egg-laying material. For water, use a shallow dish with a sponge or cotton wool soaked in clean water. Change it daily to prevent bacteria. For food, adult BSF do not eat solid food; they need sugar water or honey solution (1 part honey to 10 parts water). Put this in a small jar with a cotton wick. Without sugar, adults live only 3-5 days; with it, they live 7-10 days and lay more eggs.

Egg-laying material is the most important. Female BSF lay eggs in dry, dark crevices near a food source. A common method is to use a plastic container (e.g., 1-litre ice cream tub) with a lid. Cut small holes (2-3mm) in the lid and fill the container with moist chicken feed or fruit waste. The smell attracts females. They lay eggs in the holes. You can also use corrugated cardboard strips (2cm wide) stacked together and tied with a string. Soak the cardboard in a little water and place it near the food. Females lay eggs in the corrugations.

Place the egg-laying container near the top of the cage, in a shaded corner. Do not place it on the floor; ants and other pests can reach it. Check for eggs every morning. They are laid in clusters of 500-900. Collect them gently with a soft brush or spoon and transfer to a hatching bin. If you leave them, they will hatch in the cage and the neonates will fall to the floor and die. So daily collection is key.

Maintain humidity at 60-70% inside the cage. In dry African regions, mist the cage with water twice a day. But avoid wetting the egg-laying material; too much moisture causes mould. Also, keep the cage clean. Remove dead flies and old food daily. A well-maintained breeding habitat can produce 5-10 grams of eggs per day from a cage with 500-1000 adults. That is enough to start a large larvae-rearing operation.

6. Maintaining Optimal Colony Conditions

To keep your BSF colony productive, you must control temperature, humidity, light, and hygiene. Temperature should stay between 25C and 30C. If it drops below 20C, adults stop mating. In cooler African highlands (e.g., Kenya, Ethiopia), use a small greenhouse or polytunnel to trap heat. In hot regions, use shade nets and misting. Humidity should be 60-70%. Buy a cheap hygrometer (less than $5) to monitor. If humidity is below 50%, adults dehydrate; above 80%, mould grows. Adjust by misting or opening vents.

Light is critical. BSF adults mate in bright light, preferably sunlight. But they also need shade to rest. So place your cage where it gets at least 4-6 hours of indirect sunlight. Do not use artificial light at night; it disrupts their cycle. Also, keep the cage away from strong winds. Wind prevents mating and can damage wings. A simple windbreak made from palm fronds or a tarp works.

Hygiene prevents disease. Every day, remove dead flies, old food, and frass. Every week, clean the cage with a mild disinfectant (e.g., 1% bleach solution) and rinse well. Never use insecticides. Ants are the biggest threat. Apply a barrier of ash, diatomaceous earth, or used engine oil around the cage legs. If you see mites (small red or white dots on flies), isolate affected flies and clean the cage thoroughly.

Feeding and watering must be consistent. Provide fresh sugar water every 2 days. Replace water sponges daily. Egg-laying containers should be checked and refilled with fresh attractant every 3 days. Record your observations: number of adults, eggs collected, and any deaths. This helps you spot problems early. With good conditions, a colony can last 6-12 months. You will need to add new blood every 3-4 months by attracting wild BSF or buying new eggs to prevent inbreeding. Inbreeding reduces egg hatch rates and larval vigour. So always keep your colony fresh.

<h2>Practical Reference: Setting Up Your BSF Colony</h2>

<p>Black Soldier Fly (BSF) farming is one of the fastest-return agribusiness ventures in Africa. A well-managed colony can convert 1 tonne of organic waste into 250300 kg of fresh larvae in 1418 days, and those larvae sell as high-protein poultry, fish, and pig feed at 2040% below the cost of imported fishmeal or soy. But the business case collapses if your colony fails to breed reliably. This Practical Reference gives you the numbers, designs, and operating parameters to build a colony that produces eggs week after week, in African conditions, with locally available materials.</p>

<h3>1. Selecting a Suitable Site</h3>
<p>BSF adults need warmth (2732C), shade, and protection from wind and rain. In most African cities, a shaded veranda, a converted shipping container, or a covered shed works well. Avoid low-lying areas that flood, and keep the colony at least 30 m from residential kitchens to prevent nuisance complaints. The site must have water access for cleaning and a well-drained floor for leachate.</p>

<h3>2. Designing Adult Fly Enclosures</h3>
<p>Adult flies are housed in a cage or netted enclosure. A practical size for a small commercial colony is 2 m 2 m 2 m (8 m), which can hold 10,00020,000 adults. Use UV-resistant shade netting (4060% shade) on the sides and clear plastic or polycarbonate on the roof to let light in while keeping rain out. Provide a 1 m high entry door with a double-net vestibule to prevent escapes.</p>

<h3>3. Attracting Wild BSF to Start a Colony</h3>
<p>You do not need to buy eggs to start. Wild BSF are present across most of sub-Saharan Africa. Place 510 kg of fresh food waste (fruit peels, brewers' spent grain, market waste) in a shallow container inside the enclosure. Add a small amount of decomposing material to release the volatile compounds that attract gravid females. Within 37 days, wild females will lay egg clusters in the corrugated cardboard or wood crevices you provide. Transfer these eggs to a nursery tray.</p>

<h3>4. Purchasing BSF Eggs or Neonates</h3>
<p>If you need to scale quickly, buy from a reputable supplier. In Kenya, ICIPE and several private hatcheries sell BSF eggs at KES 5,0008,000 per gram (approx. 20,00030,000 eggs). In Nigeria, prices range from 15,00025,000 per gram. Always request a hatchability guarantee (80%) and a viability certificate. Neonates (newly hatched larvae) are easier to start with but cost 23 more per unit and must be used within 24 hours.</p>

<h3>5. Creating a Breeding Habitat</h3>
<p>BSF adults need a specific oviposition substrate. The standard design is a corrugated cardboard or wood strip (23 mm gaps) placed vertically above a food attractant. Females lay eggs in the dry crevices. Keep the substrate shaded and slightly moist (6070% RH). Replace the strips every 23 days and move egg clusters to a hatching tray with moist feed (e.g., chicken mash or fruit waste) at 2830C.</p>

<h3>6. Maintaining Optimal Colony Conditions</h3>
<p>Monitor temperature, humidity, and light daily. BSF adults are most active at 2732C and 6070% RH. Below 20C, mating stops. Above 35C, adults die within hours. Provide 1214 hours of light per day; natural light is sufficient in most of Africa, but supplement with LED if you are in a shaded or indoor facility.</p>

<strong style="color:#b71c1c;"> SAFETY WARNING: Biosecurity and Handling</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#b71c1c;">BSF larvae and adults can carry pathogens if fed contaminated waste (e.g., meat, dairy, or faecal sludge). Never feed raw meat or manure to your colony unless you have a validated composting step. Wear gloves and a dust mask when handling large quantities of larvae or frass. Keep the colony away from food preparation areas. If you experience allergic reactions (skin rash, respiratory irritation), stop work and consult a doctor. In malaria-endemic areas, ensure the enclosure does not collect standing water.</p>
