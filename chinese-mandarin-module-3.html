1. Numbers 0100 and Phone Numbers

In Mandarin, numbers are built logically from 0 to 99. Start with 010: lng (0), y (1), r (2), sn (3), s (4), w (5), li (6), q (7), b (8), ji (9), sh (10). For 1119, say sh + digit: shy (11), sh'r (12), etc. For 2099, say digit + sh + digit: rsh (20), rshy (21), snshw (35), jishb (98). For 100, say ybi. Practice by counting everyday items: 5 mangoes (w g mnggu), 12 eggs (sh'r g jd n).

Phone numbers are crucial for business in Africa. In Mandarin, you read each digit separately. For example, 0712345678 becomes lng q y r sn s w li q b. Note: 0 is often read as 'dng' in phone numbers (from 'zero' in English), but 'lng' is also fine. Practice with your own number: write it down, then say each digit in Mandarin. Repeat until fluent.

Use numbers in prices: 50 shillings = wsh xinlng. 1000 naira = yqin n il. In markets, bargaining is common. Say 't i gu le' (too expensive) and offer a lower price: 'snsh ku i, xng ma?' (30 yuan, okay?). Learn to understand numbers quickly when spoken. Listen to Chinese media or use apps like HelloChinese to drill numbers. Practice with a partner: one says a number in Mandarin, the other writes it down. Do this daily for 10 minutes.

For phone numbers, remember that Chinese mobile numbers are 11 digits, often starting with 1. But in Africa, you'll use local numbers. The key is to be able to say and understand any sequence. Write your own phone number in Mandarin and practice saying it aloud. Also, learn to ask for someone's number: 'n de di nhu h om sh dusho?' (What is your phone number?). Respond: 'w de di nhu h om sh...' (My phone number is...).

Finally, practice listening to numbers in context. When you buy airtime, the vendor might say the price. When you exchange money, you'll hear amounts. Start with small numbers and build up. Use flashcards or a notebook to review daily. Consistency is key. In a few weeks, you'll handle numbers confidently in business and daily life.

2. Days of the Week and Months

In Mandarin, days of the week are easy: they are 'week' (xngq) plus a number. Monday is xngqy (week one), Tuesday xngq'r, Wednesday xngqsn, Thursday xngqs, Friday xngqw, Saturday xngqli, and Sunday is xngqtin or xngqr (week day/sun). So if you know numbers 16, you know the days. Practice by saying the day for each date on a calendar. For example, if today is Tuesday, say 'jntin xngq'r'.

Months are even simpler: they are 'month' (yu) plus a number. January is yyu (one month), February ryu, March snyu, April syu, May wyu, June liyu, July qyu, August byu, September jiyu, October shyu, November shyyu, December sh'ryu. So you only need to know numbers 112. Practice by listing the months in order, then saying your birthday month.

To say 'today is Monday', say 'jntin xngqy'. For 'tomorrow is Tuesday', say 'mngtin xngq'r'. For 'yesterday was Sunday', say 'zutin xngqtin'. These phrases are useful for scheduling. In Africa, many business meetings are set by day and date. If someone says 'meet on Friday', you can confirm: 'xngqw, du ma?' (Friday, right?).

Also learn to ask 'what day is it today?' 'jntin xngqj?' (Today is what day of the week?). The answer will be one of the days. Similarly, ask 'what month is it?' 'xi nz i sh j yu?' (Now is what month?). Practice with a friend: ask and answer these questions daily. Use a Chinese calendar app or write the days and months in your notebook. Repetition will make them automatic.

In business, knowing days and months helps with planning. For example, 'wmen xngqsn ji nmi n' (We meet on Wednesday). 'Snyu shw r sh jizh rq' (March 15 is the deadline). Practice making simple sentences with days and months. Combine with numbers from Lesson 1. For instance, 'wyu rshs r' (May 24th). This builds a foundation for dates and scheduling.

3. Telling Time: Hours and Minutes

To tell time in Mandarin, use 'din' for hour and 'fn' for minutes. The structure is: number + din + number + fn. For example, 3:00 is sn din. 3:15 is sn din shw fn. 3:30 is sn din snsh fn, but more commonly sn din b n (half past three). 3:45 is sn din sshw fn. For 'a.m.' and 'p.m.', use 'sh ngw' (morning, before noon) and 'xi w' (afternoon, after noon). So 9:00 a.m. is sh ngw ji din. 2:00 p.m. is xi w ling din. Note: 2 o'clock is 'ling din', not 'r din', when telling time.

To ask 'what time is it?', say 'xi nz i j din?' (Now what time?). The answer: 'xi nz i sn din shw fn' (Now 3:15). For 'what time do you...?', say 'n j din...?' For example, 'n j din qchung?' (What time do you get up?). Practice answering with your daily routine: 'w q din qchung' (I get up at 7). 'W b din ch zof n' (I eat breakfast at 8). 'W ji din sh ngbn' (I start work at 9).

In Africa, punctuality varies, but in business, being on time is appreciated. If you have a meeting at 10:30, say 'sh din b n'. If it's 10:45, say 'sh din sshw fn' or 'ch shw fn shy din' (15 minutes to 11). The latter is less common but useful to understand. Also learn 'b n' (half), 'k' (quarter, 15 minutes). So 10:15 is 'sh din y k' or 'sh din shw fn'. 10:45 is 'sh din sn k' or 'ch y k shy din'.

Practice with a clock or phone. Set random times and say them in Mandarin. Use a timer: every hour, say the time aloud. When scheduling, confirm times: 'wmen ling din ji n, xng ma?' (We meet at 2, okay?). If you need to change, say 'wmen sn din ji n ba' (Let's meet at 3). Listen to Chinese audio for time expressions. Apps like Duolingo or ChinesePod have listening exercises. With daily practice, telling time becomes second nature.

4. Dates: Year, Month, Day

In Mandarin, dates are written and spoken from largest to smallest: year, month, day. The format is: number + nin (year) + number + yu (month) + number + r (day). For example, May 24, 2025 is r lng r w nin w yu rshs r. Note: years are read digit by digit. 2025 is 'r lng r w', not 'rqin rshw'. For months, use the numbers 112 + yu. For days, use the number + r. So '2025 nin 5 yu 24 r' means May 24, 2025.

To ask 'what's the date today?', say 'jntin j yu j r?' (Today what month what day?). The answer: 'jntin w yu rshs r' (Today May 24th). To ask the year, say 'jntin sh j nin?' (Today is what year?). In Africa, you often need to write dates on forms. Practice writing your birth date: 'w sh y ji ji lng nin sn yu shw r chshng' (I was born on March 15, 1990). Note: 1990 is 'y ji ji lng', with 0 as 'lng'.

When scheduling, combine with days of the week. For example, 'xngqw, w yu rshs r' (Friday, May 24th). Or 'snyu shw r, xngqsn' (March 15th, Wednesday). Practice saying dates for upcoming holidays or events. In business, deadlines are crucial: 'qng z i li yu snsh r qin wnchng' (Please complete before June 30th). Learn to understand dates when spoken quickly. Listen to news or announcements.

Also learn to ask 'when?' 'shnme shhu?' (What time?). For example, 'n shnme shhu li?' (When are you coming?). Answer with a date: 'w w yu rshs r li' (I come on May 24th). Practice with a calendar: point to a date and say it in Mandarin. Write important dates in your planner in Mandarin. This reinforces memory. Use online resources like ChineseClass101 for date exercises. Consistency is key.

5. Scheduling: Making Appointments

Making appointments in Mandarin requires combining days, dates, and times. Start with a polite request: 'wmen yu g shjin ba' (Let's set a time). Or 'n shnme shhu yu kng?' (When are you free?). To propose a specific time: 'wmen xngqsn xi w sn din ji n, ho ma?' (Let's meet Wednesday afternoon at 3, okay?). If you need to reschedule: 'dubq, wmen gi d o xngqs ba' (Sorry, let's change to Thursday). Confirm: 'ho, xngqs sh ngw sh din, y yn wi dng' (Okay, Thursday 10 a.m., it's a deal).

In African business contexts, appointments may be more flexible, but using precise Mandarin shows professionalism. Learn phrases for location: 'z i nl ji n?' (Where to meet?). 'Z i w de gngs' (At my office). 'Z i kfi di n' (At the coffee shop). If you're running late, say 'w hu chd o sh fnzhng' (I'll be 10 minutes late). If you need to cancel: 'w yu sh, bnng li le' (I have something, can't come). Always apologize: 'zhn b hoysi' (I'm really sorry).

Practice with a partner: one proposes a time, the other accepts or suggests another. Use real scenarios: meeting a supplier, scheduling a language exchange, planning a call. Write down the appointment in Mandarin: 'xngqw, s yu sh'r r, xi w ling din, z i w de b ngngsh' (Friday, April 12th, 2 p.m., at my office). This reinforces dates and times. Also learn to ask for confirmation: 'n qudng ma?' (Are you sure?). 'Wmen hi ji n ma?' (Are we still meeting?).

Use tools: set reminders in your phone with Mandarin labels. For example, 'xngqy ji din, kihu' (Monday 9 a.m., meeting). When you receive a message in Mandarin, practice translating it. For instance, 'mngtin xi w sn din, z i lodfng ji n' (Tomorrow 3 p.m., meet at the usual place). Over time, you'll handle appointments smoothly. Remember to be polite: use 'qng' (please) and 'xixie' (thank you).

6. Asking and Telling Age

Asking age in Mandarin is straightforward: 'n du d le?' (How old are you?). This is informal and used for peers or younger people. For formal situations, say 'nn du d ninj?' (What is your age?). The answer uses the pattern: number + su. For example, 'w rshw su' (I am 25 years old). For children under 10, you can also say 'w w su le' (I am 5). Note: 'le' indicates a change of state, often used with age.

In Africa, age is sometimes a sensitive topic, but in Chinese culture, it's common to ask. However, be respectful. If someone asks your age, answer clearly. Practice numbers 1100 with 'su'. For example, 'w snsh su' (I am 30). 'T ssh'r su' (He is 42). To ask about someone else: 't du d le?' (How old is he/she?). For a child: 'n rzi j su le?' (How old is your son?).

Learn to talk about age in context. For business, age may indicate experience. Say 'w yjng gngzu sh nin le' (I have worked for 10 years). Or 'w cng sh zhge hngy rsh nin le' (I have been in this industry for 20 years). When meeting new people, you might exchange ages. Practice with a friend: ask and answer. Also learn to say 'w b t d ling su' (I am two years older than him). 'T b w xio sn su' (She is three years younger than me).

For official documents, you might need to write your birth date and age. Practice writing: 'w y ji b w nin chshng, jnnin snshji su' (I was born in 1985, this year I am 39). Use a calculator to check. In conversations, listen for 'su' to catch age. Also learn 'ninj' (age, years) in formal contexts. For example, 'nn de ninj sh?' (Your age?). Respond politely: 'w wsh su' (I am 50). Practice with audio resources. Knowing how to discuss age helps build relationships and shows respect.

<h2>Practical Reference: Numbers, Dates, and Time in Mandarin for African Agribusiness</h2>

<p>In cross-border agribusiness, a single misunderstood number can cost a shipment, a contract, or a season. Whether you are negotiating the price of cashew in Abidjan, confirming a cold-chain pickup in Nairobi, or scheduling a buyer call in Guangzhou, Mandarin numbers, dates, and time expressions are operational tools not academic trivia. This Practical Reference consolidates the module into a working desk guide for African traders, exporters, cooperative managers, and logistics officers who deal with Chinese suppliers, buyers, and freight forwarders.</p>

<p>Chinese numbers are highly regular, which is good news for busy professionals. Once you learn 0100, you can form phone numbers, prices, quantities, ages, and dates. The main traps are tone changes, the <em>ling</em> vs. <em>r</em> distinction for "two," and the fact that Chinese dates run from largest unit to smallest (year month day), the opposite of many African and European conventions. Master these and you remove most scheduling errors before they happen.</p>

<p>For African exporters, the highest-value application of numbers is the phone number. Chinese business cards, WeChat profiles, and freight documents list numbers in digit strings. Read them digit by digit. Note that in phone numbers, <em>y</em> (1) is usually pronounced <em>yo</em> to avoid confusion with <em>q</em> (7). So +86 138 0013 8000 is read "yo sn b, lng lng yo sn, b lng lng lng." Practise reading your own Kenyan, Nigerian, Ghanaian, or South African number aloud in Mandarin so you can dictate it to a Chinese counterpart without error.</p>

<p>Dates follow the year month day sequence. 2025315 is 15 March 2025. In business writing, this is unambiguous and preferred over the slash format, which differs between China (YYYY/MM/DD) and many African countries (DD/MM/YYYY). When you confirm a shipment date, always write the full Chinese form in the contract or email, then repeat it verbally. This single habit prevents the classic "03/04" dispute that has delayed many cocoa, coffee, and sesame consignments.</p>

<p>Telling time uses (din) for the hour and (fn) for minutes. is 3:15. (sn din b n) is 3:30. For business, add (sh ngw, morning), (xi w, afternoon), or (wnshang, evening). "" is 3 p.m. Because China Standard Time is UTC+8, a 3 p.m. Beijing call is 10 a.m. in Nairobi (UTC+3), 9 a.m. in Lagos (UTC+1), and 7 a.m. in Accra (UTC+0). Always state the time zone in writing, and repeat the Chinese time aloud to confirm.</p>

<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#5a0010">Never confirm a payment amount, bank account number, or contract date by voice alone in a language you are still learning. Numbers such as 4 (s) and 10 (sh), or 1 (y) and 7 (q), are easily misheard on poor mobile connections common in rural sourcing areas. Always follow up with a written message (WeChat, email, or SMS) showing the digits and the full date in both Chinese and your local format. For payments, verify the beneficiary account through an independent channel before transfer. Fraudsters exploit language gaps and time-zone pressure slow down and double-check.</p>

<p>Scheduling an appointment combines date, time, and polite request patterns. A practical formula is: + time + + ? (Wmen [time] ji nmi n, ho ma?) "Shall we meet at [time]?" For example, ? (Let's meet Thursday at 2 p.m., OK?). To reschedule: ? (Sorry, can we change it to Friday?). In African agribusiness, where harvest timing, port congestion, and weather shift plans, the ability to reschedule clearly in Mandarin is as valuable as the ability to book.</p>

<p>Asking and telling age uses (su). ? (N du d ?) is informal; ? (Nn du d ninj?) is polite. Answer: (I am 35). Age matters in two business contexts: verifying the age of a counterpart for relationship-building, and verifying the age of machinery or livestock. For equipment, say (This machine has been used for five years). For livestock, (This cow is three years old). Precision here affects valuation and insurance.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Read phone numbers digit by digit</strong> and say 1 as <em>yo</em>, not <em>y</em>.</li>
<li><strong>Use (ling)</strong> before measure words (tonnes, containers, kilograms), not (r).</li>
<li><strong>Write dates as </strong> in all contracts and emails to remove ambiguity.</li>
<li><strong>State the time zone</strong> every time you book a call; Beijing is UTC+8.</li>
<li><strong>Confirm numbers in writing</strong> after every voice call, especially payments and account details.</li>
<li><strong>Use / / </strong> to avoid a.m./p.m. confusion.</li>
<li><strong>Practise rescheduling phrases</strong> (?) before you need them under pressure.</li>
<li><strong>Repeat back</strong> the date, time, quantity, and price your counterpart states, then ask ? (correct?).</li>
