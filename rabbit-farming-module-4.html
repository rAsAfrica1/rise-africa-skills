1. Nutritional Requirements for Different Life Stages

Rabbits have different nutritional needs as they grow from kits to adults. A newborn kit drinks only its mother's milk for the first 3 weeks. After that, you can start offering small amounts of soft hay and pellets. From 3 to 6 weeks, kits should have unlimited access to a high-quality pellet feed with 16-18% protein and 12-14% fibre. They also need fresh water at all times. By 6 to 8 weeks, they can eat the same feed as adults but should still get extra protein for growth. A good grower pellet has 15-16% protein and 15-17% fibre. From 8 weeks to 6 months, feed them 1/4 to 1/2 cup of pellets per day, plus unlimited hay. For breeding does, feed a diet with 17-18% protein and 15-17% fibre. They need extra energy during pregnancy and lactation. A lactating doe can eat 2-3 times more than a dry doe. Give her unlimited pellets and hay. For bucks, a maintenance diet with 12-14% protein and 15-17% fibre is fine. Feed them 1/4 to 1/2 cup of pellets daily. For pet rabbits, a lower protein pellet (12-14%) and lots of hay is best to prevent obesity. Always provide fresh, clean water. In Africa, many farmers use locally available feeds like sweet potato vines, carrot tops, and moringa leaves to supplement pellets. But remember, pellets should be the main source of balanced nutrition. Adjust feed amounts based on the rabbit's body condition. If a rabbit feels bony, increase feed; if it feels too fat, reduce pellets and increase hay. Always introduce new feeds slowly over 7-10 days to avoid digestive upset. Clean feed bowls daily to prevent mold and bacteria. Store feed in a cool, dry place away from pests. Never feed moldy or spoiled feed. By matching nutrition to life stage, you will raise healthy, productive rabbits.

2. Types of Feed: Pellets, Hay, Greens, and Supplements

Rabbits need a balanced diet of pellets, hay, greens, and sometimes supplements. Pellets are the easiest way to ensure they get all the nutrients they need. Buy pellets that are fresh, smell sweet, and are not dusty. A good pellet for adult rabbits has 12-14% protein, 15-17% fibre, and 1-2% fat. For growing rabbits, choose 15-16% protein. Store pellets in a sealed container in a cool, dry place and use within 3 months. Hay is essential for digestion and wears down their teeth. Grass hay like timothy, orchard grass, or local grasses is best. Avoid alfalfa hay for adult rabbits because it is too high in calcium and protein; it can cause bladder stones. Give rabbits unlimited hay every day. Greens provide vitamins and variety. Safe greens include carrot tops, beet tops, kale, romaine lettuce, parsley, cilantro, and dandelion leaves. In Africa, you can use sweet potato vines, amaranth leaves, and moringa leaves. Wash all greens thoroughly to remove pesticides and dirt. Introduce new greens one at a time in small amounts to avoid diarrhoea. Supplements are not usually needed if the rabbit eats a balanced diet. However, you can give a salt lick or mineral block. Some farmers add a little vegetable oil to feed for extra energy in cold weather. Never give supplements without advice from a vet or extension officer. Avoid feeding bread, chocolate, sweets, corn, beans, or potatoes. These can cause serious digestive problems. Also, never feed mouldy or spoiled feed. Always provide fresh, clean water. A rabbit drinks about 50-150 ml of water per kg of body weight daily. In hot African climates, they may drink more. Check water bottles twice a day and clean them regularly. By combining pellets, hay, and safe greens, you will keep your rabbits healthy and productive.

3. Feeding Schedules and Rations

Feeding your rabbits on a consistent schedule helps them stay healthy and grow well. Adult rabbits should be fed twice a day: once in the morning and once in the evening. Give each adult 1/4 to 1/2 cup of pellets per day, split into two meals. Also provide unlimited hay at all times. Pregnant and lactating does need more feed. Give them unlimited pellets and hay, and check their feed bowls three times a day. Growing rabbits (kits) from 3 weeks to 6 months should have unlimited pellets and hay. After 6 months, switch to adult rations. For breeding bucks, feed 1/4 to 1/2 cup of pellets daily. Always feed at the same times each day. Rabbits are creatures of habit and sudden changes in feeding time can stress them. Use a measuring cup to ensure you give the right amount. Overfeeding pellets can lead to obesity and diarrhoea. Underfeeding causes poor growth and weak babies. Adjust the amount based on the rabbit's body condition. If a rabbit feels too thin, increase pellets slightly. If it feels too fat, reduce pellets and increase hay. In hot weather, feed more in the cool morning and evening. In cold weather, rabbits need more energy, so you can increase pellets slightly. Always provide fresh, clean water. Change water daily and clean bottles or bowls. Never let feed sit in the bowl for more than 24 hours, especially in humid climates, as it can grow mould. Store feed in airtight containers. Keep a simple record of how much each rabbit eats. If a rabbit suddenly stops eating, it is a sign of illness. Check with a vet or experienced farmer. In Africa, many farmers feed kitchen greens in the morning and pellets in the evening. That works well. Just make sure greens are fresh and washed. By following a strict schedule and measuring rations, you will save money and keep your rabbits healthy.

4. Safe and Toxic Plants for Rabbits

Rabbits can eat many plants, but some are poisonous. Knowing the difference can save your rabbits' lives. Safe plants include most leafy greens: carrot tops, beet tops, kale, romaine lettuce, parsley, cilantro, dandelion leaves, and spinach (in small amounts). In Africa, you can also feed sweet potato vines, amaranth leaves, moringa leaves, and blackjack (Bidens pilosa). Always wash plants thoroughly to remove dirt, pesticides, and animal waste. Introduce new plants slowly, one at a time, over several days. Watch for diarrhoea or refusal to eat. Toxic plants include many common garden and wild plants. Never feed your rabbits: tomato leaves and stems, potato leaves and stems, rhubarb leaves, avocado leaves and bark, foxglove, lily of the valley, buttercup, poinsettia, oleander, and nightshade. Also avoid onion, garlic, leek, and chives. These can cause anaemia. Some plants cause mild stomach upset, while others are deadly even in small amounts. If you are not sure about a plant, do not feed it. Keep a list of safe and toxic plants in your rabbitry. Teach everyone who feeds the rabbits. If a rabbit eats a toxic plant, it may show signs like drooling, vomiting (rare in rabbits), diarrhoea, weakness, or convulsions. Contact a vet immediately. There is no home remedy for most plant poisonings. Prevention is best: only feed plants you know are safe. Fence off your rabbit area from wild toxic plants. When collecting wild greens, avoid roadsides where herbicides may be sprayed. Also avoid plants growing near factories or garbage dumps. In many African villages, farmers use local plants like moringa, sweet potato vines, and amaranth. These are safe and nutritious. But always confirm with an experienced rabbit farmer or extension officer. Never assume a plant is safe because another animal eats it. Rabbits have different digestive systems. When in doubt, throw it out. Your rabbits depend on you for their safety.

5. Watering Systems and Hygiene

Clean water is the most important nutrient for rabbits. A rabbit can survive longer without food than without water. Adult rabbits drink about 50-150 ml of water per kilogram of body weight daily. In hot African climates, they may drink up to 200 ml per kg. Lactating does need even more. Always provide fresh, clean water at all times. There are two main watering systems: bottles and bowls. Bottles with sipper tubes are best because they keep water clean and prevent spillage. Hang bottles so the sipper tube is at the rabbit's shoulder height. Check bottles twice a day to make sure they are not blocked or leaking. Clean bottles with a bottle brush and hot soapy water every week. Rinse well. Bowls are cheaper but easily soiled with feed, hay, and droppings. If you use bowls, use heavy ceramic ones that rabbits cannot tip over. Clean and refill bowls at least twice a day. In very hot weather, change water three times a day. Never let water sit for more than 24 hours. Bacteria and algae grow quickly in warm water. If you have many rabbits, consider an automatic watering system. These use a large tank and pipes with nipples. They save labour and keep water clean. But they need regular cleaning and maintenance. Check all nipples daily for leaks or blockages. Flush the system every month with a mild disinfectant, then rinse thoroughly. Hygiene is critical. Dirty water spreads diseases like coccidiosis and pasteurellosis. Always wash your hands before and after handling water equipment. Keep water containers off the floor to prevent contamination from urine and droppings. Store water in clean, covered containers. If you use well or river water, boil it or treat it with chlorine (follow instructions). Rainwater is good if collected cleanly. In dry seasons, water may be scarce. Plan ahead: store enough water for your rabbits. A rabbit deprived of water for 24 hours can become severely ill. If a rabbit stops drinking, check the water source first. Then check for illness. By keeping water clean and plentiful, you prevent many health problems and keep your rabbits productive.

6. Body Condition Scoring and Diet Adjustment

Body condition scoring (BCS) is a simple way to check if your rabbit is getting the right amount of feed. You do this by feeling the rabbit's spine and hips. A rabbit in good condition has a smooth, rounded spine that you can feel but not see. The hips are slightly rounded. You should not be able to see bones sticking out. Use a scale of 1 to 5: 1 = emaciated (bones very prominent), 2 = thin, 3 = ideal, 4 = overweight, 5 = obese. To score, place the rabbit on a flat surface. Run your hand along its back and sides. Feel the spine, ribs, and hips. If the spine feels sharp and the ribs are easy to feel, the rabbit is thin (score 1-2). If the spine is smooth and ribs are covered with a little fat, it is ideal (score 3). If you cannot feel the spine and the rabbit feels like a soft ball, it is overweight (score 4-5). Check your rabbits every two weeks. Keep a record for each rabbit. Adjust feed based on the score. For a thin rabbit (score 1-2), increase pellets by 1/4 cup per day and add a small amount of safe greens. Also check for illness or dental problems. For an overweight rabbit (score 4-5), reduce pellets to 1/8 cup per day and increase hay. Do not starve the rabbit; reduce gradually. Encourage exercise. For an ideal rabbit (score 3), keep the current ration. Pregnant and lactating does should have a BCS of 3-4. If they are too thin, they may not produce enough milk. If too fat, they may have trouble kindling. Growing kits should be score 3. In Africa, where feed may be limited, BCS helps you use feed efficiently. You can also adjust for seasons: in hot weather, rabbits may eat less, so monitor BCS closely. In cold weather, they need more energy, so increase feed if they lose condition. Always provide fresh water. If a rabbit's BCS changes suddenly, check for illness. Regular BCS is a cheap, effective tool for managing your rabbitry. It takes only a minute per rabbit and can save you money on feed and vet bills.

<h3>3. Feeding Schedules and Watering</h3>

<p>Rabbits are crepuscular they eat most actively at dawn and dusk. A practical African schedule:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>06:0007:00:</strong> Fresh greens and/or pellets (first main meal)</li>
<li><strong>12:0013:00:</strong> Check water; top up hay if needed</li>
<li><strong>17:0018:00:</strong> Second main meal (pellets or forage)</li>
<li><strong>Ad libitum:</strong> Hay and clean water at all times</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Water:</strong> A lactating doe drinks 23 litres daily in hot climates. Use nipple drinkers or clean ceramic bowls not open troughs that get contaminated. In rural settings, a simple 5-litre jerrycan with nipple fittings serves 46 rabbits for 24 hours.</p>

<h3>4. Body Condition Scoring (BCS) and Diet Adjustment</h3>

<p>BCS is your most practical on-farm tool. Score each rabbit 15 by feeling the spine and ribs:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>BCS 1 (emaciated):</strong> Spine and ribs sharp increase pellets by 30%, check for disease/parasites.</li>
<li><strong>BCS 2 (thin):</strong> Spine palpable, ribs easily felt add 20% feed, supplement with lucerne.</li>
<li><strong>BCS 3 (ideal):</strong> Spine and ribs felt with slight pressure maintain ration.</li>
<li><strong>BCS 4 (fat):</strong> Spine barely palpable reduce pellets by 15%, increase hay.</li>
<li><strong>BCS 5 (obese):</strong> Spine not palpable reduce feed by 25%, especially in breeding does.</li>
</ul>

<p>Score every rabbit monthly and before breeding. A doe at BCS 45 will have difficulty kindling and may produce weak kits. A doe at BCS 12 will not conceive reliably.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol style="background:#f1f8e9; padding:16px 16px 16px 36px; border-radius:6px;">
<li>Weigh feed daily don't guess. A 150 g ration is not "a handful."</li>
<li>Provide clean, cool water <em>ad libitum</em>; check twice daily in hot weather.</li>
<li>Feed hay or fresh forage every day fibre prevents gut stasis and enteritis.</li>
<li>Wilt fresh greens 24 hours before feeding to reduce moisture and bloat risk.</li>
<li>Never feed mouldy, dusty, or spoiled feed aflatoxin kills rabbits quickly.</li>
<li>Body condition score every rabbit monthly; adjust rations individually.</li>
<li>Keep a simple feed record: date, quantity, feed type, and any health observations.</li>
<li>Introduce new feeds gradually over 57 days; sudden changes cause diarrhoea.</li>
</ol>
