Lessons

1. Site Assessment and Risk Analysis

Before you start any roofing job, you must assess the site. Walk around the building and look for hazards: power lines, trees, uneven ground, and weak walls. Check the roof itself: is it steep? Are there rotten boards? In Africa, many roofs are on single-story buildings, but you still need to be careful. Use a ladder to get a closer look, but never climb on a roof that looks unsafe. Look for signs of termites, rust, or water damage. Also check the weather: rainy season can make roofs slippery and dangerous. Talk to the building owner about any special risks, like nearby schools or markets where children play. Write down all hazards and plan how to avoid them. For example, if there are power lines, keep a distance of at least 3 meters. If the roof is steep, use roof jacks or scaffolding. Also consider the sun: in hot climates, work early morning or late afternoon to avoid heat stroke. Always have a first aid kit on site and a phone to call for help. Remember, a safe site is a productive site.

2. Roof Deck Preparation and Inspection

The roof deck is the base for your roofing sheets. It must be clean, dry, and structurally sound. Start by removing all debris: old nails, leaves, and dirt. Use a broom and a scraper. Check for loose boards or panels. If you find rotten wood, replace it. For a wooden deck, use a hammer to tap boards; a dull sound means rot. Replace any board that is soft or broken. For a metal deck, check for rust; wire-brush it and paint with anti-rust primer. Make sure the deck is level. Use a spirit level to check for dips. If there are dips, you may need to add shims or sister boards. Also, check that the deck is securely fixed to the trusses. Tighten any loose nails or screws. In Africa, termites are a big problem. Treat the wood with termite poison before installing sheets. Also, ensure there is proper ventilation under the deck to prevent moisture buildup. If the deck is concrete, check for cracks and repair them with mortar. Once the deck is clean and solid, you are ready for underlayment. Never skip this step; a bad deck will ruin your roofing sheets.

3. Underlayment and Insulation Installation

Underlayment is a protective layer between the roof deck and the roofing sheets. It keeps out water and dust. In Africa, use a breathable underlayment like Tyvek or a locally available bitumen-based felt. Start at the bottom of the roof and roll it out horizontally. Overlap each layer by at least 10 cm. Nail or staple it down, but don't overdo it; too many holes can cause leaks. For insulation, use materials like fiberglass or polystyrene boards. Insulation keeps the building cool in hot climates and reduces noise from rain. Place insulation boards between the rafters or on top of the deck, depending on your design. If you use foil-faced insulation, the shiny side should face up to reflect heat. Make sure insulation is not compressed; it needs air space to work. In areas with heavy rain, add a second layer of underlayment at the eaves. Also, install drip edges at the edges of the roof to direct water away from the fascia. Always wear gloves and a mask when handling insulation. After installation, check for tears or gaps. Repair any damage with tape. This step is critical for a long-lasting roof.

4. Erecting Scaffolding and Access Platforms

Scaffolding gives you a safe platform to work on the roof. For most single-story buildings, you can use simple trestle scaffolding or a mobile tower. For taller buildings, you need a proper scaffold system. In Africa, many roofers use wooden poles tied with rope, but this is dangerous. Instead, use steel scaffolding with couplers and base plates. Set the base on firm ground; use wooden planks under the legs to spread the load. Make sure the scaffold is level and plumb. Use diagonal braces for stability. The platform should be at least 60 cm wide and have guardrails at 1 meter and 0.5 meter heights. Also, install toe boards to prevent tools from falling. For access, use a ladder that extends 1 meter above the platform. Secure the ladder at the top. If you are working on a steep roof, install roof jacks and walk boards. Always inspect scaffolding daily before use. Check for loose connections, cracks, or rust. Never overload the scaffold; keep materials distributed evenly. In windy conditions, tie the scaffold to the building. Remember, falls are the biggest cause of roofing accidents. Take your time and build it right.

5. Material Storage and Handling on Site

Roofing sheets are expensive and can be damaged easily. Store them properly on site. Keep sheets off the ground on wooden pallets or beams. Cover them with a tarpaulin to protect from rain and sun. In hot climates, metal sheets can expand and warp if left in direct sun. Stack sheets flat, not on edge, to prevent bending. For long sheets, support them at 1-meter intervals. Never drag sheets; carry them vertically with two people. Use gloves to avoid cuts. When lifting sheets to the roof, use a hoist or a rope with a helper. Do not throw sheets; they can dent or injure someone. Keep nails, screws, and other fixings in a dry, secure place. Use a container or a locked shed to prevent theft. In Africa, theft is common, so store materials in a locked area. Also, keep underlayment rolls upright to avoid crushing. Insulation boards should be kept dry; if they get wet, they lose effectiveness. Plan your material delivery so you don't have excess on site. Order only what you need for the day. At the end of each day, clean up scraps and store tools. This keeps the site safe and materials in good condition.

6. Weather Considerations and Scheduling

Weather can make or break a roofing job. In Africa, you must plan around rainy seasons and extreme heat. Check the forecast daily. If rain is expected, do not start installing sheets. Water can get trapped under sheets and cause rust. Also, wet roofs are slippery. Work only on dry days. In hot climates, start early morning and finish by noon. Take breaks in the shade and drink plenty of water. Heat stroke is a real danger. If you must work in the afternoon, use a hat and light clothing. For windy conditions, do not work on the roof if winds exceed 30 km/h. Sheets can act like sails and cause falls. Secure all materials before leaving the site. Schedule your work so that the roof is watertight before the rainy season. If you are in a region with two rainy seasons, plan around them. Also, consider the availability of labor. In some areas, workers are busy during harvest season. Book your team in advance. Have a backup plan for bad weather: work on interior tasks or prepare materials. Always communicate with the building owner about delays. A well-planned schedule saves time and money.

<h2>Practical Reference: Preparation and Site Setup for Roofing Sheet Installation</h2>

<p>In African roofing projects, preparation and site setup determine whether a job finishes on time, on budget, and without injury. This practical reference distils the essentials for site assessment, roof deck inspection, underlayment and insulation, scaffolding, material handling, and weather scheduling. It is written for contractors, supervisors, and tradespeople working with corrugated metal, IBR, or stone-coated steel sheets in urban and rural settings.</p>

<p>Use this module alongside the six core lessons. Each section below gives field-ready guidance, data, and checklists you can apply immediately. Where local conditions vary coastal humidity, highveld hail, or dusty Sahelian winds adapt the numbers to your site's actual exposure.</p>

<h2>Site Assessment and Risk Analysis</h2>

<p>Begin with a structured walkover. Record access routes, ground slope, soil bearing capacity, overhead power lines, and proximity to occupied buildings. In many African cities, informal structures and shared boundaries create access constraints that dictate crane or manual lifting choices. Measure the roof pitch and span; these determine sheet length, overlap, and fixing pattern.</p>

<p>Risk analysis must be documented. Use a simple matrix: likelihood severity. High-risk items include working at height without fall arrest, unguarded roof edges, and manual handling of long sheets in wind. In malaria-endemic regions, standing water around the site is a health risk drain it before work starts.</p>

<h2>Roof Deck Preparation and Inspection</h2>

<p>Inspect the deck for straightness, level, and structural integrity. Timber trusses must be treated against termites and borer a major issue in tropical Africa. Steel purlins require rust treatment and correct spacing (typically 9001200 mm for 0.5 mm IBR sheets). Check that purlins are square to the ridge; cumulative error causes sheet misalignment and leaks.</p>

<p>Clean the deck of debris, nails, and loose material. Any protruding nail heads must be punched below the surface. If the deck is concrete, ensure it is dry and free of release agents. For re-roofing, remove old sheets carefully they may contain asbestos in older buildings. Follow national asbestos regulations (e.g., South Africa's Asbestos Regulations, 2001).</p>

<h2>Underlayment and Insulation Installation</h2>

<p>Underlayment protects against wind-driven rain and condensation. In hot African climates, a reflective foil or anti-glare membrane also reduces heat gain. Install underlayment horizontally, starting from the eaves, overlapping at least 100 mm and lapping over the ridge. Secure with cap nails or adhesive according to manufacturer instructions.</p>

<p>Insulation choices vary: bulk insulation (fibreglass, cellulose) for thermal resistance; reflective foil for radiant heat. In coastal areas, use corrosion-resistant materials. Ensure insulation does not block ventilation paths trapped moisture rots timber and corrodes steel.</p>

<h2>Erecting Scaffolding and Access Platforms</h2>

<p>Scaffolding must be erected by competent persons. In many African markets, modular steel scaffolding is available for hire; bamboo scaffolding is still used but requires rigorous inspection. Base plates must sit on firm ground use sole boards on soft soil. Guardrails at 1 m and 2 m, toe boards, and safe ladder access are mandatory.</p>

<p>For single-storey roofs, mobile tower scaffolds or roof ladders may suffice. For multi-storey, independent tied scaffolds are required. Never rely on the roof sheet itself as a working platform. Inspect scaffolding daily before use and after any storm.</p>

<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#7b241c;">Working at height is the leading cause of fatalities in roofing. In South Africa alone, the construction sector records hundreds of fall-related incidents annually. <strong>Never work on a roof without fall arrest or edge protection.</strong> Harnesses must be anchored to a certified point, not to the roof sheet. Scaffold guardrails must be in place before any work starts. If you cannot provide fall protection, stop work. No deadline justifies a life.</p>

<h2>Material Storage and Handling on Site</h2>

<p>Roofing sheets are easily damaged. Store them on a raised, level platform never directly on the ground. Use timber dunnage at 1 m intervals. Cover with tarpaulin but allow ventilation to prevent condensation. In dusty regions, keep sheets wrapped until installation. Stack height should not exceed 1.5 m to avoid bending and toppling.</p>

<p>Handle sheets with gloves edges are sharp. Use two people for sheets longer than 3 m. In windy conditions, carry sheets horizontally and low. Never drag sheets across each other; this scratches coatings and accelerates corrosion. Keep a small inventory log to track deliveries and usage.</p>

<h2>Weather Considerations and Scheduling</h2>

<p>Rain, wind, and extreme heat dictate scheduling. Metal sheets become dangerously hot in direct sun schedule heavy lifting for early morning or late afternoon. Wind above 40 km/h makes sheet placement unsafe. Rain creates slip hazards on scaffolding and wet decks.</p>

<p>In West Africa, the rainy season (JuneSeptember) can halt work for days. Plan roof covering before the rains or after. In East Africa, the long rains (MarchMay) and short rains (OctoberDecember) require similar planning. Use a simple weather log and daily forecast review. Build contingency days into the programme typically 1520% of total duration.</p>
