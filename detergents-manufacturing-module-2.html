1. Structure and classification of surfactants

A surfactant molecule has two ends: a hydrophilic (water-loving) head and a hydrophobic (water-hating) tail. The tail is usually a hydrocarbon chain from oils or petrochemicals. When you mix a surfactant with water, the heads dive into the water while the tails stick out. This is why surfactants can lift grease and dirt from clothes or dishes.

Surfactants are classified by the charge on their head group. Anionic surfactants have a negative charge. They are the workhorses in laundry powders and dish liquids. Common examples are linear alkylbenzene sulfonate (LAS) and sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS). Cationic surfactants have a positive charge. They are used in fabric softeners and disinfectants. Nonionic surfactants have no charge. They are excellent at removing oily stains and are often mixed with anionics. Amphoteric surfactants have both positive and negative charges depending on pH. They are mild and used in shampoos and baby products.

In Africa, you can make anionic surfactants from locally available materials. For example, you can sulfonate benzene with sulfuric acid to make LAS. But this requires careful handling of strong acids. A safer start is to buy pre-made surfactant concentrates from chemical suppliers in Nairobi, Lagos, or Johannesburg. Look for products labeled 'LABSA' (linear alkylbenzene sulfonic acid) or 'SLES' (sodium lauryl ether sulfate).

To test the type of surfactant you have, do a simple foam test. Put 1 gram of surfactant in a clean 500 ml bottle, add 200 ml of water, and shake for 10 seconds. Anionic surfactants produce lots of foam that lasts. Nonionics produce less foam. Cationics produce little foam. This helps you decide which surfactant to use in your formula.

Always wear gloves and goggles when handling surfactants. They can irritate skin and eyes. Store them in labeled plastic containers away from sunlight. For a small-scale detergent business, start with anionic and nonionic surfactants. They cover most cleaning needs and are readily available in 25 kg drums or 200 kg barrels.

2. Micelle formation and critical micelle concentration

When you add a surfactant to water, the molecules first spread out individually. As you add more, they start to cluster into tiny balls called micelles. In a micelle, the hydrophobic tails point inward, away from water, and the hydrophilic heads point outward, into the water. This happens at a specific concentration called the critical micelle concentration (CMC).

Why does CMC matter? Below the CMC, the surfactant does not clean well because there are not enough micelles to trap dirt. Above the CMC, cleaning power increases rapidly. For most anionic surfactants like LAS, the CMC is around 0.1 to 0.5 grams per liter (100-500 ppm) in pure water. But in hard water, which contains calcium and magnesium ions, the CMC can be higher because some surfactant is used up reacting with these ions.

In Africa, water hardness varies widely. In cities like Accra or Cairo, water may be very hard (over 300 mg/L as CaCO3). In rural areas, rainwater is soft. To find the right amount of surfactant, do a simple jar test. Fill a 1-liter jar with your local water. Add 0.5 grams of surfactant, shake, and observe foam. Keep adding 0.1 gram increments until you get a stable foam that lasts 5 minutes. That amount is close to the CMC for your conditions.

For a typical laundry detergent, you want the surfactant concentration in the wash water to be well above the CMC, often 1-2 grams per liter. That means if you use 50 liters of water per wash, you need 50-100 grams of surfactant. If your detergent powder is 15% surfactant, you need about 333-667 grams of powder per wash. Adjust based on soil level and water hardness.

Remember, micelles also help solubilize oils. Once micelles form, they can trap oily dirt inside their hydrophobic cores and carry it away in the rinse water. So achieving CMC is essential for effective cleaning. Always test your detergent with local water and local dirt (e.g., red clay soil or palm oil stains) to find the best formulation.

3. Hydrophilic-lipophilic balance (HLB) concept

The hydrophilic-lipophilic balance (HLB) is a number from 0 to 20 that tells you whether a surfactant prefers water (hydrophilic) or oil (lipophilic). A low HLB (3-6) means the surfactant is oil-soluble and good for water-in-oil emulsions. A high HLB (8-18) means it is water-soluble and good for oil-in-water emulsions. For detergents, you generally want surfactants with HLB between 10 and 15.

Each surfactant has its own HLB value. For example, sodium lauryl sulfate (anionic) has an HLB of about 40, but it is often used at low levels. Nonionic surfactants like alcohol ethoxylates have HLB values that depend on the number of ethylene oxide (EO) units. A nonionic with 6 EO units might have HLB 12, while one with 10 EO units might have HLB 14. You can blend surfactants to get the right HLB for your cleaning task.

In Africa, you can calculate HLB using simple formulas. For a blend, HLB_mix = (HLB1 x weight1 + HLB2 x weight2) / (weight1 + weight2). Suppose you want an HLB of 12 for a dishwashing liquid. You have two nonionics: one with HLB 10 and one with HLB 14. Mix equal parts: (10+14)/2 = 12. That blend will work well.

But HLB is not the only factor. Temperature, water hardness, and the type of dirt also matter. For example, in hot climates, nonionic surfactants may become less soluble and cloud out. That is why many African detergent makers use a mix of anionic and nonionic surfactants. Anionics like LAS have good cleaning power but can be sensitive to hard water. Nonionics are more tolerant of hard water but may foam less.

To apply HLB, start with a simple recipe: 10% LAS, 5% nonionic (HLB 12), 2% sodium carbonate, and water to 100%. Test it on oily plates and greasy clothes. If it does not clean well, adjust the nonionic HLB by blending different ethoxylates. Keep records of your trials. Over time, you will develop a feel for what works in your local market.

4. Anionic surfactants: LAS, AES, AS

Anionic surfactants are the most widely used in detergents because they are excellent at removing dirt and are relatively cheap. The three main types are LAS (linear alkylbenzene sulfonate), AES (alcohol ether sulfate), and AS (alcohol sulfate).

LAS is the backbone of many laundry powders. It is made by sulfonating linear alkylbenzene with sulfur trioxide or oleum, then neutralizing with sodium hydroxide. In Africa, you can buy LAS as a powder (sodium salt) or as a liquid (LABSA, which is the acid form). LABSA is 90-96% active and must be neutralized carefully. To neutralize, slowly add LABSA to a 20% sodium hydroxide solution while stirring. Keep the temperature below 40Â°C to avoid darkening. The result is a thick paste or liquid with about 30% active LAS. Use 5-15% LAS in your detergent formula.

AES, also known as SLES (sodium lauryl ether sulfate), is milder than LAS and produces richer foam. It is common in dishwashing liquids and shampoos. AES is made by ethoxylating lauryl alcohol (from coconut or palm kernel oil) and then sulfating. In Africa, you can buy SLES as a 70% paste. To use, dilute it with water to 28-30% active and add preservative. A typical dish liquid has 10-20% SLES.

AS, or sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS), is a simple sulfate with no ethoxylation. It is a good foamer but can be harsh on skin. It is often used in toothpaste and industrial cleaners. You can make SLS by sulfating lauryl alcohol with chlorosulfonic acid, but this is dangerous. It is safer to buy SLS powder or needles (90% active) and dissolve it in water.

When using anionic surfactants, avoid mixing with cationic surfactants because they will neutralize each other and form a precipitate. Also, anionic surfactants are sensitive to hard water. Add a builder like sodium carbonate or zeolite to soften the water. In Africa, many small-scale producers use locally available soda ash (sodium carbonate) from Lake Magadi in Kenya or Lake Natron in Tanzania. A simple laundry powder formula: 15% LAS, 10% sodium carbonate, 50% sodium sulfate, 5% sodium silicate, and 20% water (spray dried or mixed). Test with local dirt and water.

5. Nonionic surfactants: alcohol ethoxylates, alkylphenol ethoxylates

Nonionic surfactants have no charge. They are excellent at removing oily and greasy dirt, and they work well in hard water. The two main types are alcohol ethoxylates (AEs) and alkylphenol ethoxylates (APEs).

Alcohol ethoxylates are made by reacting fatty alcohols (from coconut or palm oil) with ethylene oxide. The number of ethylene oxide (EO) units determines the properties. A typical laundry surfactant has 7-9 EO units. It is a liquid or paste at room temperature. In Africa, you can buy AEs under trade names like Neodol or Lutensol. For a dishwashing liquid, use 5-10% of an AE with 7 EO. For laundry, use 3-5% of an AE with 9 EO. AEs are biodegradable and generally considered safe.

Alkylphenol ethoxylates (APEs), such as nonylphenol ethoxylates (NPEs), were once very common but are now restricted in many countries because they break down into toxic compounds. In Africa, some markets still sell them, but it is better to avoid them. If you must use them, handle with care and follow local regulations. A safer alternative is to use AEs or fatty acid ethoxylates.

To use nonionic surfactants, you often need to blend them with anionics. For example, a heavy-duty laundry liquid: 10% LAS, 5% AE (7 EO), 2% sodium carbonate, 1% sodium chloride (to adjust viscosity), and water to 100%. Mix LAS and AE first, then add water slowly. Nonionics can be thick, so warm them to 40Â°C to make pouring easier. Do not overheat above 60Â°C as they can degrade.

Nonionics are also good for making emulsifiable concentrates, like a degreaser for engines. Mix 20% AE, 10% butyl glycol, and 70% water. Spray on greasy engine parts, wait 5 minutes, and rinse. In Africa, this can be a profitable product for auto mechanics.

One challenge with nonionics is cloud point. As temperature rises, they become insoluble and the solution turns cloudy. The cloud point for an AE with 7 EO is about 50-60Â°C. In hot African climates, this can be a problem. To raise the cloud point, blend with an anionic surfactant or add a hydrotrope like sodium xylene sulfonate. Always store nonionics in a cool, shaded place.

6. Cationic and amphoteric surfactants

Cationic surfactants have a positive charge on their head group. They are used mainly for fabric softening, antistatic properties, and disinfection. Common examples are quaternary ammonium compounds (quats) like benzalkonium chloride and cetrimonium chloride. In Africa, you can buy these as 50% or 80% active liquids. A typical fabric softener contains 5-10% cationic surfactant, 1% fragrance, and 0.1% dye, with water to 100%. Mix the cationic into warm water (40Â°C) slowly to avoid lumps. Do not mix with anionic surfactants, as they will form a scum.

Cationic surfactants are also good disinfectants. A 0.1% solution of benzalkonium chloride can kill many bacteria and viruses. This is useful for making surface cleaners for hospitals or food processing areas. However, they are not effective against all germs, so for heavy-duty disinfection, use bleach or hydrogen peroxide instead.

Amphoteric surfactants have both positive and negative charges. They are mild and foam well, making them ideal for shampoos, baby washes, and hand soaps. The most common is cocamidopropyl betaine (CAPB), derived from coconut oil. In Africa, you can buy CAPB as a 30% active liquid. A simple shampoo formula: 15% SLES, 5% CAPB, 2% glycerin, 0.5% preservative, and water to 100%. Adjust pH to 5.5 with citric acid. This shampoo is gentle and produces rich foam.

Amphoteric surfactants are also used in hard water because they are not affected by calcium and magnesium ions. They can be blended with anionics to reduce irritation. For example, a hand dishwashing liquid with 10% LAS and 5% CAPB is milder than one with only LAS.

When handling cationic and amphoteric surfactants, wear gloves and goggles. Cationics can be irritating to skin and eyes. Store them in plastic containers, as they can corrode metal. For a small-scale business, start with a fabric softener and a mild shampoo. These products have good demand in African markets. Source your raw materials from local suppliers or import from China or India in 200 kg drums. Always test your final product for stability, foam, and cleaning performance before selling.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Surfactants for Detergent Manufacturing in Africa

This practical reference consolidates the key technical decisions a detergent formulator or small-scale manufacturer in Africa must make when selecting surfactants. It assumes you have already worked through the earlier lessons on surfactant structure, micelle formation and CMC, HLB, and the four main surfactant classes. Here we focus on what matters on the factory floor and in the market: which surfactants to buy, how much they cost relative to performance, how they behave in hard water, and how to blend them into a product that sells.

Africa's detergent market is growing fast, driven by urbanisation, rising incomes, and a large informal sector. But manufacturers face specific constraints: hard water in many regions (especially groundwater in the Sahel, East Africa, and parts of Southern Africa), high import costs for petrochemical feedstocks, inconsistent electricity, and price-sensitive consumers who buy in small sachets. Surfactant choice is therefore not just a chemistry question â it is a business decision. The right surfactant system balances cleaning performance, cost per wash, local water chemistry, and regulatory compliance.

Choosing Surfactants: Cost, Performance, and Local Water

The first practical rule is that no single surfactant does everything. Anionic surfactants give strong detergency and foaming but are sensitive to hard water. Nonionics tolerate hard water and remove oily soil well but foam little. Cationics are mainly used for fabric softening and antimicrobial action, not cleaning. Amphoterics are mild and foam well, making them ideal for hand dishwashing and personal care, but they cost more. A typical African powder detergent uses 15â25% total surfactant, mostly LAS with some nonionic and sometimes a foam booster.

Water hardness is the single biggest technical variable across the continent. In soft-water cities (e.g., parts of coastal West Africa, Kampala, Kigali), LAS works efficiently. In hard-water areas (e.g., Nairobi, Dakar, Windhoek, much of northern Nigeria), LAS alone leaves scum and underperforms. Here you must either add a builder (zeolite, sodium carbonate, citrate) at 20â40% of the formulation or shift part of the surfactant load to nonionics and AES, which are more tolerant of CaÂ²âº and MgÂ²âº. The cost trade-off is real: nonionics cost more per kilogram than LAS, but they let you reduce total surfactant without losing cleaning, which can lower cost per wash.

Another Africa-specific factor is the informal sachet market. Consumers often buy 10â50 g sachets and wash by hand in buckets. This favours formulations with good foam (a visible signal of cleaning) and quick dissolution. LAS and AES both foam well; adding a small amount of amphoteric or a foam booster like cocamide DEA improves foam stability in hard water. For machine washing â still a minority of households but growing in South Africa, Egypt, and urban Kenya â low-foam nonionic-rich systems are preferred.

â  SAFETY WARNING â Surfactant Handling

Concentrated surfactants and their intermediates can cause serious skin and eye irritation. LAS acid (linear alkylbenzene sulfonic acid) is strongly corrosive and must be neutralised before use; never handle it without acid-resistant gloves, goggles, and a face shield. Ethoxylation intermediates and alkylphenol ethoxylates may contain trace 1,4-dioxane and ethylene oxide residues â ensure supplier certificates of analysis and adequate ventilation. Cationic quaternaries are toxic to aquatic life and must not be discharged untreated into rivers or wetlands. Always store surfactants in labelled, closed containers away from food, and keep Material Safety Data Sheets (MSDS) on site. In many African jurisdictions, workplace safety and effluent rules are enforced â check with your national environmental authority before discharging wash water.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Test your water first. Measure hardness (mg/L as CaCOâ) before choosing a surfactant system. A simple titration kit costs little and prevents costly reformulation.
  2. Match surfactant class to job. Anionic for cleaning and foam, nonionic for oily soil and hard water, cationic for softening and sanitising, amphoteric for mildness and foam stability.
  3. Respect the CMC. Below CMC, cleaning is poor. Formulate above CMC but avoid wasteful excess â usually 0.1â0.5% active surfactant in the wash liquor is enough.
  4. Use HLB as a guide, not a law. For oily soil, target HLB 10â13; for emulsification and hard-water tolerance, blend anionic and nonionic to reach the required HLB.
  5. Build for hardness. Add zeolite, sodium carbonate, or citrate at 20â40% in hard-water regions; without builders, surfactant performance collapses.
  6. Watch cost per wash, not cost per kg. A slightly more expensive nonionic can reduce total surfactant load and lower your unit cost.
  7. Check regulatory status. Alkylphenol ethoxylates are restricted in the EU and increasingly elsewhere; prefer alcohol ethoxylates for export or eco-labelled products.
  8. Keep MSDS and batch records. Document surfactant supplier, batch, and concentration for traceability, quality control, and safety compliance.
