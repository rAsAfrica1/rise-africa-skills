1. Common Cattle Diseases

In Africa, cattle face several diseases that can wipe out your herd if you don't act fast. The most common are foot-and-mouth disease (FMD), lumpy skin disease, anthrax, blackleg, and East Coast fever. FMD causes blisters on the mouth and hooves, making cattle drool and limp. It spreads like wildfire through saliva, so isolate sick animals immediately. Lumpy skin disease gives cattle fever and skin lumps, reducing milk and meat value. Anthrax and blackleg are soil-borne killers; anthrax can even infect you, so never open a carcass suspected of anthraxcall a vet. East Coast fever, spread by brown ear ticks, kills calves within 3 weeks if untreated. Other frequent problems include mastitis (udder infection), pneumonia (often after transport), and foot rot from muddy pens. Recognizing these early saves money. For example, a cow with pneumonia breathes fast (over 40 breaths per minute) and coughs. Mastitis makes the udder hot and milk lumpy. In Africa, many diseases are preventable with vaccines. For FMD, vaccinate every 6 months in endemic areas. For blackleg and anthrax, vaccinate once a year before the rainy season. Always buy cattle from trusted sources and quarantine new animals for 30 days. If you see sudden deaths, report to the local veterinary office immediatelythey may declare a quarantine zone. Remember, a dead cow is a total loss, but a treated cow recovers. Keep a simple notebook: date, symptoms, action. This helps you and your vet spot patterns. Don't rely on guesswork; use a vet's diagnosis. Many drugs are available at agrovet shops, but wrong use creates resistance. For example, overusing antibiotics for viral diseases like FMD does nothing. Focus on prevention: clean water, good grazing, and tick control. That's your first line of defense.

2. Vaccination Protocols

Vaccination is the cheapest insurance for your cattle. In Africa, you must follow a schedule based on your region's diseases. For blackleg and anthrax, vaccinate all cattle once a year, preferably 2-4 weeks before the rainy season when outbreaks peak. For foot-and-mouth disease (FMD), in endemic areas, vaccinate every 6 months. In some countries, FMD vaccine is provided free by the government; check with your local vet. For lumpy skin disease, vaccinate annually, especially in wet areas. For brucellosis, vaccinate heifers at 4-8 months oldthis protects them for life. Always buy vaccines from a reputable agrovet and check the expiry date. Keep vaccines cold: use a cooler box with ice packs. Never freeze vaccines unless the label says so; freezing kills many vaccines. Use a clean needle and syringe for each animal, or at least disinfect between animals if using the same syringe. For a herd, use a new needle every 10 animals. Inject under the skin (subcutaneous) for most vaccines, but some are intramuscularread the label. For example, blackleg vaccine is given under the skin on the neck. After vaccination, watch for side effects like swelling or fever; these usually pass in a day. Record each vaccination: date, vaccine name, batch number, and animal ID. This helps when selling or during disease outbreaks. If you miss a booster, restart the schedule. Don't vaccinate sick animals; wait until they recover. Also, don't mix vaccines unless instructed. In hot climates, vaccinate in the cool of the morning. If you have many cattle, work with your vet to plan a herd health calendar. Many African governments run free vaccination campaigns for anthrax and FMDtake advantage. A vaccinated herd means more milk, more calves, and less stress.

3. Parasite Control

Parasites steal your cattle's feed and can kill them. The two main types are internal (worms) and external (ticks, flies, lice). Internal worms cause diarrhea, weight loss, and a rough coat. In Africa, the most deadly is the barber's pole worm (Haemonchus), which sucks blood and causes anemiacheck the eyelid: if it's pale white instead of pink, the animal needs deworming. Deworm cattle every 3-6 months, depending on rainfall. Use drugs like albendazole or ivermectin. Rotate drugs yearly to prevent resistance. Dose by weight: a 250 kg cow needs 5 ml of a 10% albendazole suspension. Underdosing is worse than not treatingit breeds resistant worms. For external parasites, ticks are the biggest threat. They spread East Coast fever, heartwater, and redwater. Control ticks by dipping or spraying every week in wet seasons, every two weeks in dry. Use acaricides like amitraz or cypermethrin. Dip tanks are common in communal areas; if you don't have one, use a knapsack sprayer to wet the whole body, especially ears, udder, and tail. Don't forget the inside of the ears and under the tail. Tick grease for ears is also good. Flies cause irritation and spread pinkeye. Use fly traps or insecticide-impregnated ear tags. Lice cause hair loss and itching; treat with ivermectin or dusting powder. Keep pens dry and rotate grazing to break worm cycles. Don't overgraze; tall grass harbors fewer worms. For calves, deworm at 2 weeks old if they show signs. Pregnant cows: use safe dewormers like fenbendazole, avoid ivermectin in first trimester. Always read labels. If you see ticks, act immediately. A heavy tick load can kill a calf in days. Remember, healthy cattle need less medicine. Provide mineral licks with copper and zinc to boost immunity. And always consult your vet for a parasite control plan tailored to your area.

4. Biosecurity Measures

Biosecurity means keeping diseases out of your farm. It's cheaper than treating sick animals. Start with a fence: keep your cattle separate from neighbors' herds. If you share grazing, diseases spread. When you buy new cattle, quarantine them for at least 30 days in a separate pen, far from your herd. Watch for signs of illnesscoughing, diarrhea, lameness. Test for diseases like brucellosis and tuberculosis if possible. Limit visitors: only essential people enter your cattle area. Provide boots and overalls for visitors, or at least a footbath with disinfectant (e.g., 2% sodium hypochlorite) at the gate. Vehicles can carry germs; park them away from pens. Keep feed and water clean. Store feed in sealed bins to prevent rodents and birds. Don't feed kitchen scraps that may contain meat. Water should be from a clean source; if using a stream, fence it off to stop animals defecating in it. Isolate sick animals immediately. Use a separate pen and separate tools. After handling sick animals, wash hands and change clothes. Dispose of dead animals properly: burn or bury deep (2 meters) with lime. Never sell meat from a dead animal. For calves, use individual pens to reduce contact. Clean and disinfect pens regularly: remove manure, scrub with soap, then apply disinfectant. Let pens dry in the sun. Control insects and rodentsthey carry diseases. Keep a record of all treatments and movements. If you attend a livestock market, don't wear the same clothes and boots back to your farm. Change and disinfect. Vaccinate and deworm on schedule. If you see sudden deaths, call a vet and don't move animals. A simple footbath and quarantine pen can save your whole herd. Many African farms lose money because they skip these steps. Be strict: biosecurity is a daily habit, not a one-time job.

5. Recognizing Signs of Illness

Cattle can't tell you when they're sick, so you must watch for signs. A healthy cow is alert, eats well, and has a shiny coat. First, check the basics: appetite, rumination, and behavior. A sick cow often separates from the herd, stands with its head down, or lies down more. It may stop chewing cud. Take its temperature: normal is 38.0-39.5C. Fever above 39.5C means infection. Use a digital thermometer with lubricant in the rectum. Next, look at the eyes: dull, sunken eyes mean dehydration. Pale eyelids (white instead of pink) indicate anemia from worms or blood parasites. Check the nose: dry muzzle or nasal discharge is a sign of respiratory disease. Breathing rate: normal is 10-30 breaths per minute. If over 40, it's stressed or sick. Listen for coughing, especially after exercise. Check the mouth for blisters (FMD) or drooling. Look at the feet: lameness, swelling, or wounds. Foot rot smells bad. Examine the udder: hot, hard quarters with lumpy milk mean mastitis. Check the skin for lumps (lumpy skin disease) or ticks. Look at manure: diarrhea (watery) or blood means gut problems. Hard, dry manure means constipation or dehydration. Urine: dark red or brown may mean redwater. Also watch for sudden deaththat's an emergency. Other signs: grinding teeth (pain), kicking at belly (colic), or straining to defecate. Keep a daily routine: walk through your herd every morning and evening. Use a notebook to record any abnormal signs. If you see one sick animal, check the whole herd. Early treatment saves money. For example, a cow with mastitis treated within 12 hours recovers faster. If you're unsure, take a photo or video and send to your vet. Don't wait for the animal to go down. Act fast.

6. Working with Veterinarians

A good veterinarian is your best partner in cattle farming. In Africa, you may have access to government vets, private vets, or community animal health workers. Build a relationship before an emergency. Invite your vet to visit your farm at least twice a year for a herd health check. They can spot problems early and advise on vaccination and deworming schedules. When you call a vet, be ready with information: number of animals, age, symptoms, when they started, and any treatments given. Have your records handy. If you can't reach a vet, contact your local veterinary office or use mobile apps like VetAfrica. For emergencies like anthrax, call immediatelydon't touch the carcass. Vets can also help with pregnancy diagnosis, artificial insemination, and surgery. Always follow their prescription exactly: dose, duration, and withdrawal period. For example, if a vet gives oxytetracycline, give the full course even if the cow looks better. Stopping early breeds resistance. Ask questions: what disease is it? How did it spread? How can I prevent it? A good vet will explain. Pay promptly and keep a receipt. If you can't afford a private vet, government vets are cheaper or free for notifiable diseases. Join a farmer group to share the cost of vet visits. Also, learn basic skills: how to give injections, how to check temperature, how to apply a tick grease. Your vet can train you. Store medicines as directedsome need refrigeration. Never buy drugs from unlicensed sellers; they may be fake. If a vet prescribes a drug you don't have, ask for alternatives. Keep a vet contact list on your phone. In remote areas, a vet may take days to arrive, so have a first-aid kit with antibiotics, anti-inflammatory drugs, and wound spray. But remember, you are not a vetdon't guess. A wrong diagnosis wastes money and kills cattle. Work together, and your herd will thrive.

<h2>Practical Reference: Cattle Health &amp; Disease Management in African Agribusiness</h2>

<p>In cattle farming, disease is not simply a veterinary problem it is a business risk. A single outbreak of foot-and-mouth disease or East Coast fever can wipe out a year's cash flow, close market access, and destroy the herd genetics you have spent years building. This Practical Reference lesson gives you the working knowledge to protect your investment: the diseases that matter most in Africa, the vaccination and parasite-control calendars that keep herds productive, the biosecurity habits that stop pathogens at the gate, the early signs that tell you something is wrong before it becomes an outbreak, and how to work with veterinarians as a business partner rather than an emergency expense.</p>

<p>Treat every health decision as a financial decision. Prevention costs a fraction of treatment, and treatment costs a fraction of lost production, lost markets, and lost animals. Your goal is not zero disease it is a herd that stays profitable under African conditions.</p>

<h2>Common Cattle Diseases That Hit African Herds Hardest</h2>

<p>Africa's cattle diseases fall into three commercial categories: those that kill animals (mortality risk), those that reduce milk and weight gain (productivity risk), and those that block trade (market access risk). Foot-and-mouth disease, for example, rarely kills adult cattle but can shut down your access to formal markets for months, making it arguably the most expensive disease on the continent. East Coast fever, by contrast, kills up to 90% of susceptible exotic calves. Know which category each threat falls into so you can prioritise spending.</p>

<h2>Biosecurity Measures</h2>

<p>Biosecurity is the cheapest and most powerful tool you own. It is the set of everyday practices that stop disease entering your farm and stop it spreading if it does. For a commercial operation, biosecurity is also a market-access requirement buyers, abattoirs, and exporters increasingly demand documented health and movement records.</p>

<p>Core measures: maintain a single controlled entry point with a footbath and vehicle wheel dip; quarantine all incoming animals for at least 2130 days; keep visitors and their vehicles away from animal housing; never share needles, syringes, or equipment between farms without disinfection; dispose of carcasses and placenta by deep burial or burning; control ticks, flies, and rodents; and keep written records of every animal movement, treatment, and death.</p>

<h2>Recognising Signs of Illness</h2>

<p>Early detection is where you save money. A skilled stockman walking the herd daily will spot problems hours to days before they become emergencies. Train every worker on the basic check: <strong>appetite, attitude, temperature, and output</strong>. A cow that is off-feed, standing apart, or not chewing cud is telling you something.</p>

<p>Warning signs to act on: sudden drop in milk yield; refusal to eat or drink; standing with head down or hunched back; rapid or laboured breathing; drooling or mouth lesions; swollen joints or lameness; diarrhoea or blood in dung; abortion or retained placenta; cloudy or bloody urine; sudden weight loss; and any animal that dies without a clear cause. Take the rectal temperature normal adult cattle run 38.039.3C. Record it and call your veterinarian with the number, not just a description.</p>

<h2>Working with Veterinarians</h2>

<p>Your veterinarian is a business partner, not a firefighter. Move from reactive call-outs to a scheduled relationship: agree an annual herd health plan, a vaccination and parasite-control calendar, and a monthly or quarterly visit for routine checks. A retained veterinarian who knows your herd will spot trends rising somatic cell counts, recurring lameness, poor growth that a one-off emergency visit cannot.</p>

<p>Keep a farm file with animal IDs, treatments, withdrawal dates, and vaccination records. When you call, give the vet: number affected, age, symptoms, temperature, and duration. This saves diagnostic time and money. Ask for written prescriptions, respect drug withdrawal periods for meat and milk, and never self-prescribe antibiotics antimicrobial resistance is a growing threat to both animal and human health across Africa.</p>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>
<ul>
<li>Walk the herd daily and check appetite, attitude, temperature, and output record anything unusual.</li>
<li>Confirm your vaccination calendar with the District Veterinary Officer and match FMD vaccine to local serotype.</li>
<li>Dip or spray for ticks every 24 weeks in the wet season and rotate acaricide classes.</li>
<li>Quarantine every new animal for 2130 days and test for brucellosis and TB before mixing.</li>
<li>Maintain one controlled entry point with footbath, wheel dip, and visitor log.</li>
<li>Never open a suddenly dead animal report it and let the vet investigate.</li>
<li>Keep written records of treatments, vaccinations, movements, and deaths.</li>
<li>Schedule a routine herd health visit, not just emergency call-outs.</li>
</ul>
