1. Lesson 1: Setting the Daily Feeding Schedule for Piglets, Calves, Kids and Lambs

Feeding frequency is the single most important variable a smallholder controls after colostrum. Piglets, calves, goat kids and lambs have different stomach capacities and different daily energy needs, so a single schedule cannot serve all species. This lesson gives you the numbers you need to build a schedule for each species, and explains why frequency matters. Piglets: A newborn piglet weighs 1.2 to 1.6 kg and has a stomach capacity of roughly 25 to 30 ml. It cannot drink more than about 30 ml per feed in the first 24 hours. Sow-reared piglets suckle 20 to 30 times per day. Orphan or fostered piglets on milk replacer should therefore be fed every 2 hours for the first 3 days, which means 10 to 12 feeds per day. From day 4 to day 7, reduce to 8 feeds per day. From week 2, 6 feeds per day is adequate. From week 3, 4 feeds per day plus creep feed. Piglet milk replacer is usually mixed at 125 to 150 g powder per litre of water, giving about 4.7 to 5.0 MJ metabolisable energy per litre. A 1.5 kg piglet needs about 250 to 300 ml of reconstituted milk per day in week 1, rising to 500 to 700 ml by week 3. That is roughly 17 to 20 percent of body weight per day in week 1, falling to 12 to 15 percent by week 3 as creep feed replaces milk. Calves: A dairy calf weighs 35 to 45 kg at birth. The abomasum holds about 2 litres, but the oesophageal groove reflex means milk bypasses the rumen only if the calf drinks slowly from a teat or nipple. Teagasc (Ireland) recommends feeding calves twice daily, at 8 to 10 percent of body weight per day in the first week, rising to 15 percent by week 3. For a 40 kg calf that is 3.2 to 4.0 litres per day in week 1, split into two feeds of 1.6 to 2.0 litres. Whole milk or calf milk replacer mixed at 125 to 150 g per litre is standard. Calf milk replacer should contain 20 to 22 percent crude protein and 15 to 20 percent fat for calves under 3 weeks. Feed at 38 to 39 degrees Celsius. Never feed more than 2 litres in one feed to a calf under 3 weeks, because overflow into the rumen causes bloat and scours. Goat kids and lambs: A kid or lamb weighs 2.5 to 4.5 kg at birth. Stomach capacity is 100 to 150 ml. Feed every 3 to 4 hours for the first week, which is 5 to 6 feeds per day. From week 2, 4 feeds per day. From week 3, 3 feeds per day. Daily volume is 10 to 15 percent of body weight in week 1, rising to 20 percent by week 3. For a 3 kg kid that is 300 to 450 ml per day in week 1, split into 5 feeds of 60 to 90 ml. Goat and lamb milk replacer is mixed at 150 to 200 g per litre, higher than calf replacer because kids and lambs need more fat and protein. Feed at 38 to 40 degrees Celsius. Cold milk causes abomasal stasis and scours in kids and lambs. Africa context: In Kenya, piglet and calf milk replacers are sold by Unga, Pembe, and imported brands such as Denkavit and MaxCare (NZ). A 20 kg bag of calf milk replacer costs KES 8,000 to 12,000 in Nairobi. In Nigeria, Premier Feeds and Vital Feed sell calf replacer at NGN 45,000 to 70,000 per 25 kg. In South Africa, Meadow Feeds and Afgri sell calf milk replacer at ZAR 900 to 1,400 per 25 kg. Goat and lamb replacer is harder to find; many farmers use calf replacer with added cream or vegetable oil, but this is risky. If you must use calf replacer for kids, add 30 ml of clean vegetable oil per litre and feed within 2 hours of mixing. Common mistakes: feeding once a day to save labour, which causes hunger scours and poor growth; feeding too much at one time, which causes milk to sit in the rumen and ferment; and feeding cold milk, which slows digestion. A simple rule: never increase volume by more than 10 percent per day, and never increase concentration above the manufacturer's recommendation without veterinary advice.

2. Lesson 2: Calculating Volumes by Body Weight and Mixing Powder Correctly

Getting the volume right is a calculation, not a guess. This lesson shows you how to calculate daily milk volume from body weight, how to convert that into powder and water, and how to mix accurately without scales. The formula: Daily milk volume in ml = body weight in kg x percentage of body weight x 1000. For a 40 kg calf at 10 percent, that is 40 x 0.10 x 1000 = 4,000 ml, or 4 litres per day. For a 3 kg kid at 15 percent, that is 3 x 0.15 x 1000 = 450 ml per day. For a 1.5 kg piglet at 18 percent, that is 1.5 x 0.18 x 1000 = 270 ml per day. For a 5 kg puppy at 15 percent, that is 5 x 0.15 x 1000 = 750 ml per day. For a 0.5 kg kitten at 20 percent, that is 0.5 x 0.20 x 1000 = 100 ml per day. These percentages are starting points. Royal Canin and university extension services recommend adjusting by body condition: if the animal is gaining less than 150 g per day for a calf, or 50 g per day for a kid, increase volume by 10 percent. If it is scouring, reduce volume by 20 percent and increase frequency. Mixing powder: Always read the bag. Calf milk replacer is typically 125 to 150 g per litre. Piglet replacer is 125 to 150 g per litre. Goat and lamb replacer is 150 to 200 g per litre. Puppy and kitten replacer, such as Royal Canin Baby Dog Milk or Baby Cat Milk, is mixed at 1 part powder to 2 parts water by volume for puppies, and 1 part powder to 3 parts water for kittens in the first week. If you have no scale, use a measuring cup. One level cup of calf milk replacer powder weighs about 120 to 130 g, depending on the brand. One level cup of goat replacer weighs about 140 to 150 g. Weigh once with a kitchen scale, mark the cup, and use that mark every time. Water: Use clean water that has been boiled and cooled to 40 to 45 degrees Celsius. Never use hot water straight from the tap or from a borehole without boiling, because bacteria such as E. coli and Salmonella are common in rural water supplies. Add powder to water, not water to powder, and whisk for 30 seconds until there are no lumps. Let the mixture stand for 2 minutes, then check the temperature with a thermometer. Feed at 38 to 39 degrees Celsius for calves, 38 to 40 degrees Celsius for kids and lambs, 37 to 38 degrees Celsius for piglets, and 37 to 38 degrees Celsius for puppies and kittens. If you have no thermometer, test a drop on your wrist. It should feel warm, not hot. Africa context: In Kenya, a 50 kg bag of maize germ or maize meal is often used as a cheap filler in home-made milk replacer. This is dangerous for young animals because they cannot digest starch before 3 weeks of age. FAO recommends that home-made replacers contain only milk powder, oil, and a vitamin-mineral premix. A simple home-made calf replacer: 1 litre of fresh cow milk, 100 ml of clean water, 20 g of skimmed milk powder, and 5 ml of cod liver oil. Mix and feed at 38 degrees Celsius. This is cheaper than commercial replacer but must be used within 1 hour. In Nigeria, a 400 g tin of evaporated milk costs NGN 1,200 to 1,800. It can be used for puppies and kittens in an emergency, mixed 1 part evaporated milk to 2 parts boiled water, but it lacks enough fat and calcium for long-term use. In South Africa, a 1 kg bag of Royal Canin Baby Dog Milk costs ZAR 450 to 600. Common mistakes: using a scoop from another product, which changes the concentration; adding more powder to make the milk richer, which causes scours because the gut cannot absorb the extra solids; and mixing with cold water, which leaves lumps that block teats and cause refusal. Always mix fresh. Milk replacer that has been mixed and left at room temperature for more than 2 hours should be thrown away, not refrigerated and reused.

3. Lesson 3: Hygiene of Buckets, Bottles, Teats and Feeding Equipment

Hygiene is not optional. A calf or kid that drinks from a dirty bucket is being dosed with bacteria with every swallow. This lesson gives you a cleaning protocol that works on a small farm with limited water. The principle: milk is a perfect food for bacteria. At 38 degrees Celsius, bacteria in milk replacer double every 20 minutes. A bucket that is rinsed but not scrubbed can carry 10 million bacteria per square centimetre. That is enough to cause scours in a 3-day-old calf. Step 1: Rinse immediately after feeding. As soon as the animal finishes, rinse the bucket, bottle, and teat with warm water at 40 to 45 degrees Celsius. Do not let milk dry on the equipment. Dried milk is hard to remove and protects bacteria from disinfectants. Step 2: Wash with hot water and detergent. Use water at 60 degrees Celsius or hotter if you can heat it. Add a small amount of dishwashing liquid or washing soda. Scrub with a bottle brush. Pay attention to the teat, the inside of the bottle neck, and the corners of the bucket. Step 3: Disinfect. Use a chlorine solution made from 1 tablespoon of household bleach (3.5 percent sodium hypochlorite) in 5 litres of clean water. Soak equipment for 2 minutes. Do not rinse after disinfecting if you will use the equipment within 2 hours. If you will store it, rinse with boiled and cooled water and hang upside down to dry. Step 4: Dry. Bacteria need moisture. Hang buckets and bottles upside down on a rack in the sun. Ultraviolet light from the sun kills many bacteria. Do not dry with a cloth, because cloths carry bacteria. Step 5: Store. Store dry equipment in a clean, closed cupboard or plastic box. Do not store on the floor. Do not store with animal feed or manure. Teats: Teats are the hardest part to clean. Use a teat brush or a clean toothbrush. Check for cracks and holes. Replace teats every 2 to 3 weeks for calves, every 1 to 2 weeks for kids and lambs, and every week for piglets. A cracked teat holds milk and bacteria. In Kenya, replacement teats cost KES 50 to 150 each from agrovets in Nakuru or Nairobi. In Nigeria, they cost NGN 500 to 1,500. In South Africa, ZAR 20 to 60. Water quality: If your water is not chlorinated, boil it for 1 minute and cool it before mixing milk replacer. Do not use water from a dam, river, or open well without boiling. FAO reports that contaminated water is a leading cause of neonatal diarrhoea in smallholder farms in East Africa. Hand hygiene: Wash your hands with soap before mixing milk and before feeding. If you have been handling manure or sick animals, change your clothes or wear a clean apron. A person who has been in the calf pen should not mix milk for a newborn without washing hands. Pest control: Flies carry bacteria from manure to milk. Keep feeding areas clean, remove spilled milk and feed, and use fly traps or screens. Rats and mice contaminate stored powder. Keep powder in a sealed container, not in the paper bag it came in. Africa context: In many rural areas, water is scarce. You can reuse wash water for the first rinse, but the final rinse and disinfection must use clean water. If you have no bleach, use boiling water at 100 degrees Celsius for 5 minutes. This kills most bacteria but does not remove milk residue, so scrub first. Sun-drying is free and effective. In Ethiopia and Kenya, farmers who hang bottles in direct sun for 3 hours report fewer cases of scours. Common mistakes: using the same bucket for milk and for manure or feed; rinsing with cold water only; leaving teats in the bucket of milk between feeds; and mixing a large batch of milk in the morning and feeding it all day. Mix only what you need for one feed.

4. Lesson 4: Feeding Puppies and Kittens - Frequency, Volume and Hygiene for Small Animals

Puppies and kittens are not small calves. They have different energy needs, different stomach capacities, and different risks. This lesson covers orphan puppies and kittens from birth to weaning, with numbers from Royal Canin, NAP (National Academies Press), and university extension services. Newborn puppies: A puppy weighs 200 to 500 g at birth, depending on breed. A small breed puppy may weigh 150 g. Stomach capacity is 5 to 10 ml per feed in the first week. Feed every 2 hours for the first 5 days, which is 12 feeds per day. From day 6 to day 14, feed every 3 hours, which is 8 feeds per day. From week 3, feed every 4 hours, which is 6 feeds per day. From week 4, feed every 6 hours, which is 4 feeds per day, plus starting solid food. Daily volume: 15 to 20 percent of body weight in week 1, falling to 10 to 12 percent by week 4. For a 300 g puppy at 15 percent, that is 300 x 0.15 = 45 ml per day, split into 12 feeds of 3.75 ml. That is less than a teaspoon per feed. Use a syringe without a needle or a small pet nursing bottle. Royal Canin Baby Dog Milk is mixed at 1 part powder to 2 parts water by volume. Feed at 37 to 38 degrees Celsius. Newborn kittens: A kitten weighs 80 to 120 g at birth. Stomach capacity is 2 to 4 ml per feed in the first week. Feed every 2 hours for the first 5 days, then every 3 hours from day 6 to day 14, then every 4 hours from week 3. Daily volume: 20 to 25 percent of body weight in week 1, falling to 15 percent by week 4. For a 100 g kitten at 20 percent, that is 20 ml per day, split into 12 feeds of 1.6 ml. Use a 1 ml syringe or a kitten nursing bottle. Royal Canin Baby Cat Milk is mixed at 1 part powder to 3 parts water by volume in the first week, then 1 part powder to 2 parts water from week 2. Feed at 37 to 38 degrees Celsius. Hygiene for puppies and kittens: These animals are very vulnerable to bacteria. Wash bottles, syringes, and teats in hot soapy water, then soak in a bleach solution (1 teaspoon of bleach in 1 litre of water) for 2 minutes, then rinse with boiled and cooled water. Sterilise by boiling for 5 minutes if possible. Do not use the same syringe for different litters without sterilising. Do not feed a puppy or kitten that is cold. A cold puppy cannot digest milk. Warm it first with a hot water bottle wrapped in a towel, or a heat lamp, until its body temperature is 37 to 38 degrees Celsius. Then feed. After feeding, stimulate urination and defecation by rubbing the genital area with a warm, damp cotton ball. This is what the mother does. Do this after every feed for the first 3 weeks. Weaning: Start offering soft food at 3 weeks. For puppies, mix puppy milk replacer with puppy kibble soaked in warm water. For kittens, mix kitten milk replacer with canned kitten food. By 6 weeks, puppies and kittens should be eating solid food and drinking water. Reduce milk feeds gradually over 2 weeks. Africa context: In Kenya, puppy milk replacer is sold at pet shops in Nairobi and Mombasa. A 300 g tin of Royal Canin Baby Dog Milk costs KES 2,500 to 3,500. In Nigeria, it costs NGN 15,000 to 25,000 in Lagos or Abuja. In South Africa, it costs ZAR 350 to 500. If you cannot find or afford commercial replacer, a home-made emergency formula for puppies is: 1 cup of whole milk, 1 egg yolk, 1 teaspoon of vegetable oil, and 1 drop of liquid multivitamin. Mix and feed at 37 degrees Celsius. This is not a complete diet and should not be used for more than 3 days. For kittens, use 1 cup of whole milk, 1 egg yolk, and 1 teaspoon of vegetable oil. Again, short-term only. Common mistakes: feeding cow milk to puppies and kittens, which causes diarrhoea because they are lactose intolerant; feeding too fast, which causes milk to enter the lungs (aspiration pneumonia); and not stimulating urination and defecation, which causes death within 24 hours.

5. Lesson 5: Troubleshooting Feeding Problems and Adjusting for African Conditions

Even with good schedules and clean equipment, problems happen. This lesson covers the most common feeding problems in piglets, calves, kids, lambs, puppies, and kittens, and how to fix them. Problem 1: Scours (diarrhoea). Scours is the number one killer of young animals on smallholder farms. Causes: overfeeding, cold milk, dirty equipment, sudden change in milk replacer, or infection. Action: reduce milk volume by 20 percent for 24 hours, but do not stop feeding. Offer oral rehydration solution (ORS) between feeds. A simple ORS: 1 litre of clean water, 1 teaspoon of salt, 6 teaspoons of sugar. Feed 100 to 200 ml per calf or kid per day, in small amounts. For piglets, 20 to 30 ml per feed. For puppies and kittens, 5 to 10 ml per feed. If scours continues for more than 2 days, or if there is blood, call a vet. Problem 2: Bloat. Bloat happens when milk enters the rumen instead of the abomasum. Causes: feeding too fast, feeding too much, or feeding from a bucket instead of a teat. Action: for calves, feed from a nipple bottle or teat bucket. If bloat occurs, do not feed for 12 hours. Massage the left side of the abdomen. If the calf is in distress, call a vet. For kids and lambs, bloat is less common but can happen with overfeeding. Reduce volume and increase frequency. Problem 3: Refusal to drink. Causes: milk too cold, milk too hot, dirty teat, or illness. Action: check temperature with a thermometer. It should be 38 to 39 degrees Celsius. Check the teat for cracks or smell. If the animal is sick, check for fever, nasal discharge, or laboured breathing. Problem 4: Poor growth. Causes: not enough volume, not enough concentration, or internal parasites. Action: weigh the animal weekly. A calf should gain 400 to 600 g per day. A kid or lamb should gain 100 to 200 g per day. A piglet should gain 150 to 250 g per day. A puppy should gain 5 to 10 percent of body weight per day. A kitten should gain 10 to 15 g per day. If growth is below these numbers, increase volume by 10 percent and check for worms. Deworm according to local veterinary advice. Problem 5: Milk replacer not available. In remote areas, commercial replacer may be out of stock or too expensive. Options: use fresh cow or goat milk if available. For calves, feed 4 to 5 litres per day of whole milk, split into 2 feeds. For kids and lambs, feed 500 to 800 ml per day, split into 4 feeds. For piglets, feed 300 to 500 ml per day, split into 6 feeds. For puppies and kittens, use the emergency formula in Lesson 4. If you use fresh milk, you must still follow hygiene rules. Fresh milk can carry tuberculosis and brucellosis, so boil it and cool it to 38 degrees Celsius before feeding. Africa context: In Kenya, a 20 kg bag of calf milk replacer costs KES 8,000 to 12,000. In Nigeria, NGN 45,000 to 70,000 per 25 kg. In South Africa, ZAR 900 to 1,400 per 25 kg. These prices are out of reach for many smallholders. FAO recommends that farmers form groups to buy in bulk and share transport costs. In Kenya, dairy cooperatives in Kiambu and Nakuru buy milk replacer in 500 kg pallets and sell to members at 20 to 30 percent below retail. In Nigeria, the Poultry Association of Nigeria and the Nigerian Veterinary Medical Association sometimes organise bulk purchases. In South Africa, Afgri and Meadow Feeds have branch networks that reduce transport costs. Another option: use waste milk from the dairy herd. Waste milk is milk that cannot be sold, such as milk from cows with mastitis or milk with antibiotic residues. Do not feed milk with antibiotic residues to calves that will be slaughtered soon, because it can cause antibiotic resistance. But for calves that will be raised for breeding, waste milk is a good option if pasteurised. Pasteurise at 63 degrees Celsius for 30 minutes, then cool to 38 degrees Celsius. Common mistakes: feeding milk replacer to a newborn before colostrum. Colostrum is the first milk, rich in antibodies. A calf, kid, lamb, piglet, puppy, or kitten must receive colostrum within the first 6 hours of life. If the mother is dead or not producing milk, use frozen colostrum from another animal, or a commercial colostrum replacer. Do not use milk replacer as a substitute for colostrum. Another mistake: mixing milk replacer with too little water. A thick mixture causes dehydration and scours. Always follow the bag. If you have no bag, use 125 g per litre for calves and piglets, 150 g per litre for kids and lambs, and 1 part powder to 2 parts water for puppies and kittens.
