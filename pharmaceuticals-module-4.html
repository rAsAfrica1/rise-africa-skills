1. Dosage Form Design: Tablets, Capsules, Injectables

When you design a medicine, you decide how it will enter the body. Tablets are the most common because they are cheap to make and easy to swallow. A basic tablet has the active ingredient plus fillers like lactose or microcrystalline cellulose, a binder like starch paste, a disintegrant like croscarmellose sodium to help it break up in water, and a lubricant like magnesium stearate to stop sticking to the machine. In Africa, you might use cassava starch as a binderit works well and is locally available. You compress the powder in a tablet press using force of 5 to 20 kilonewtons. For a 500 mg paracetamol tablet, you might use 10 kN.

Capsules are gelatin or vegetarian shells filled with powder, pellets, or liquid. They are easier to swallow and protect sensitive drugs from light and air. A two-piece capsule is filled on a capsule filling machine. You can make hard capsules in small batches using manual filling plates that hold 100 to 300 capsules. For a 250 mg amoxicillin capsule, you fill the body with powder and snap on the cap. Softgels are sealed with liquid insidegood for oils like vitamin A.

Injectables are sterile liquids or powders for injection. They must be pyrogen-free and sterile. You dissolve the drug in water for injection, filter through a 0.22 micron filter, fill into glass vials or ampoules, and seal. Then you sterilize by autoclaving at 121C for 15 minutes or by dry heat at 160C for 2 hours. In Africa, many small facilities make intravenous fluids like normal saline. You must test for sterility and bacterial endotoxins. A common problem is particlesalways inspect vials against a black and white background.

Each dosage form has its own equipment and skills. Start with tablets if you have a tablet press, or capsules if you have a filling plate. Injectables require a cleanroom and autoclave, which is a bigger investment. Choose based on your market: tablets for chronic diseases, capsules for antibiotics, injectables for hospitals.

2. Excipients and Their Functions

Excipients are everything in a medicine except the active drug. They help with manufacturing, stability, and how the drug is released in the body. In Africa, you can source many excipients locally: cassava starch, maize starch, talc, and calcium carbonate. But some like magnesium stearate and microcrystalline cellulose are often imported.

Fillers add bulk so the tablet is big enough to handle. Lactose is common but many Africans are lactose intolerantso for chewables or dispersibles, use mannitol or sorbitol. For a 100 mg tablet, you might add 200 mg of filler. Binders hold powders together. Starch paste (10% w/v in water) is cheap and effective. You mix it into the powder to form granules. Disintegrants make the tablet break apart in the stomach. Croscarmellose sodium swells to 10 times its volume. Use 2-5% of tablet weight. Without it, a tablet might pass through you unchanged.

Lubricants stop powder from sticking to punches and dies. Magnesium stearate at 0.5-1% is standard, but too much slows dissolution. Mix it last, for only 2-5 minutes. Glidants like colloidal silicon dioxide improve flow. A good powder flows like dry sandif it clumps, add 0.5% glidant.

For liquid medicines, you need solvents, preservatives, and sweeteners. Water is the main solvent, but you must use purified water. Preservatives like sodium benzoate at 0.1% stop mold. Sweeteners like sucrose or sorbitol mask bitter tastes. For injectables, you use water for injection and may add sodium chloride to match blood osmolarity.

Coatings protect drugs and control release. A film coating uses hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC) in a solvent. You spray it onto tumbling tablets in a coating pan. Enteric coatings resist stomach acid and dissolve in the intestineuse cellulose acetate phthalate. In Africa, humidity can ruin tablets, so a simple film coat can extend shelf life.

Always check excipient compatibility with your drug. Mix them and store at 40C and 75% relative humidity for one month. If color or smell changes, find a different excipient.

3. Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP)

GMP is a set of rules to ensure your medicine is safe and effective. It covers everything from building design to worker hygiene. In Africa, many countries follow WHO GMP guidelines. You don't need a fancy building, but you must control contamination.

Start with premises. Walls and floors should be smooth, washable, and non-shedding. No wooden surfaces in production areas. Separate rooms for weighing, mixing, and packaging. Air should flow from clean to less clean areas. Use a simple HVAC system with HEPA filters if you make sterile products. For oral solids, you can use window screens and positive air pressure. Water must be purifieduse reverse osmosis or distillation. Test it for conductivity and microbes weekly.

Personnel are key. Everyone must wear clean uniforms, hair covers, and shoe covers. No jewelry or watches. Wash hands before entering. Sick workers stay home. Train everyone on hygiene and GMP annually. Keep training records.

Equipment must be calibrated and cleaned. A tablet press needs its pressure gauge checked monthly. Balances must be calibrated daily with standard weights. After making a batch, clean all equipment with detergent and water, then rinse. For sensitive drugs like antibiotics, use dedicated equipment or clean thoroughly to avoid cross-contamination.

Documentation is the backbone of GMP. Every batch must have a batch record: who made it, what ingredients, what equipment, and test results. Write down everythingif it isn't written, it didn't happen. Keep records for at least one year after expiry.

Quality control is separate from production. The QC lab tests raw materials and finished products. They must have the authority to reject a batch. In Africa, many small manufacturers fail GMP because of poor documentation and cleaning. Start simple: write a master formula, keep a logbook, and clean visibly. Then improve.

Finally, GMP is not a one-time thing. Do internal audits every six months. Invite a consultant or local regulator to inspect. Fix problems before they become recalls.

4. Quality Control and Quality Assurance

Quality control (QC) is testing the product. Quality assurance (QA) is the whole system that ensures quality. In a small African pharmaceutical company, you might have one person doing both, but you must separate testing from production decisions.

QC tests start with raw materials. For each incoming batch of active ingredient, test identity, purity, and potency. Use simple methods: melting point, thin-layer chromatography (TLC), or titration. For paracetamol, you can do a UV spectrophotometer reading at 243 nm. For excipients, check pH, moisture, and particle size. If you can't test, get a certificate of analysis from a reputable supplier and verify identity.

During production, do in-process checks. For tablets, check weight every 15 minutes. Weight variation should be within 5% for tablets under 250 mg. Check hardness using a hardness testeraim for 4-8 kg/cm. Friability: tumble 10 tablets in a friabilator for 4 minutes at 25 rpm; they should lose less than 1% weight. Disintegration: place 6 tablets in a basket, raise and lower in water at 37C. Uncoated tablets should disintegrate within 15 minutes.

For capsules, check fill weight and lock length. For injectables, check fill volume, pH, and sterility. Sterility test takes 14 days using soybean-casein digest medium. Bacterial endotoxin test (LAL) is faster but needs a kit.

Finished product testing includes assay (how much drug is present), dissolution (how fast it dissolves), and uniformity of dosage units. For a 500 mg paracetamol tablet, assay should be 95-105% of label claim. Dissolution: 80% in 30 minutes in pH 5.8 buffer.

QA covers documentation, training, audits, and deviations. If a batch fails, investigate why. Write a deviation report. Correct the problem. Keep samples of every batch for one year after expiry in a retain room.

In Africa, many regulators require a certificate of analysis from an accredited lab. If you don't have a lab, partner with a university or a regional lab. Always keep control samples. A simple QA system: write standard operating procedures (SOPs) for each task, train staff, and review records weekly.

5. Scale-Up and Process Validation

Scale-up means making a bigger batch without changing the product. You might start with 1 kg in the lab, then move to 100 kg in production. The challenge is that mixing, heating, and drying times change. You must validate that the large batch is the same as the small one.

First, understand your critical process parameters. For tablets, these include mixing time, granulation liquid amount, drying temperature, and compression force. In the lab, you might mix for 5 minutes. In a 500 L blender, you need 15 minutes to get the same uniformity. Do a mixing uniformity test: take 10 samples from different locations, assay them. The relative standard deviation (RSD) should be less than 5%.

For granulation, if you use a high-shear mixer, the impeller speed and chopper speed matter. In small scale, you might run at 300 rpm. In large scale, you might need 150 rpm but for longer. Do a trial with placebo first to find the right conditions.

Drying: a fluid bed dryer at lab scale dries 1 kg in 10 minutes at 60C. At 100 kg, it might take 30 minutes. Check moisture contentaim for 1-3% for tablets. Use a moisture analyzer.

Compression: a rotary tablet press has different turret speed. At small scale, you might run at 20 rpm. At large scale, 40 rpm. You must adjust the fill depth and compression force to get the same tablet weight and hardness. Do a compression profile: run at different forces and test hardness and disintegration.

Process validation has three stages: 1) Process design (lab studies), 2) Process qualification (three consecutive successful batches at production scale), 3) Continued process verification (ongoing monitoring). You must write a validation protocol before you start. It states what you will test, how many samples, and acceptance criteria. For a tablet, you might take 30 tablets every 15 minutes and test weight, hardness, and thickness.

In Africa, many companies skip validation and then get recalls. Regulators like NAFDAC in Nigeria or SAHPRA in South Africa require validation data for registration. Start simple: validate your largest batch size. Then you can make smaller batches without revalidation if you keep the same equipment and process.

Document everything. A validation report should include raw data, deviations, and conclusion. If a batch fails, investigate and fix. Then revalidate.

6. Packaging, Labeling, and Stability Testing

Packaging protects the medicine from light, moisture, and physical damage. In Africa, humidity and heat are big threats. A tablet that absorbs moisture can swell, crack, or grow mold. So choose packaging carefully.

Primary packaging touches the product: blister packs, bottles, ampoules, vials. For tablets, a blister pack with aluminum foil backing and PVC or PVDC film is good. PVC alone allows moisture in; PVDC is better. In a humid climate, use aluminum-aluminum blisters. For bottles, use high-density polyethylene (HDPE) with a desiccant like silica gel. For liquids, use glass or PET bottles with child-resistant caps. For injectables, use Type I borosilicate glass vials with rubber stoppers and aluminum crimps.

Secondary packaging is the carton or box. It holds the primary package and carries information. Tertiary packaging is the shipping case.

Labeling must be clear and in a language people understand. In Africa, many countries require English, French, or Portuguese, plus local languages if possible. A label must have: generic name, brand name, strength, dosage form, batch number, manufacturing date, expiry date, storage conditions, and manufacturer's name and address. For a 500 mg paracetamol tablet, you might write: "Paracetamol 500 mg Tablets. Store below 25C. Protect from light. Batch: 2024A01. Exp: 06/2026." Include a package insert with indications, dosage, side effects, and contraindications.

Stability testing tells you how long the medicine lasts. You store samples at different conditions: 25C/60% RH (long-term), 30C/65% RH (intermediate), and 40C/75% RH (accelerated). Test at 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 18, 24 months. For accelerated, test at 0, 3, and 6 months. Test appearance, assay, dissolution, and microbial limits. If the drug degrades more than 5% in 6 months at 40C/75% RH, the shelf life will be short. For a typical tablet, you might get 24 months at 25C/60% RH. In Africa, where temperatures often exceed 30C, you may need to label "Store below 30C" and use more protective packaging.

Always do a real-time stability study for your product. Keep at least three batches. Write a stability protocol. If you change packaging or supplier, redo stability. A simple stability chamber can be a refrigerator with a humidity controller, but a proper chamber is better. Partner with a university lab if you don't have one.

<h2>Practical Reference: Pharmaceutical Formulation and Manufacturing in Africa</h2>

<p>This practical reference is designed for African agribusiness professionals, entrepreneurs, and technical managers entering or expanding into pharmaceutical manufacturing. Whether you are setting up a small-scale tablet production line in Kenya, compounding veterinary injectables in Nigeria, or packaging oral rehydration salts in Ghana, this module provides the operational benchmarks, regulatory touchpoints, and quality systems you need to move from formulation to market. The focus is on cost-effective, compliant production that meets African regulatory requirements and serves both human and veterinary markets.</p>

<h3>Dosage Form Design: Tablets, Capsules, Injectables</h3>
<p>Dosage form selection drives your entire manufacturing footprint. Tablets dominate African pharmaceutical markets due to low cost, stability, and high-volume production capability. Capsules offer flexibility for sensitive actives but require controlled humidity (typically below 45% RH) a challenge in humid coastal cities like Mombasa or Lagos. Injectables demand sterile facilities, water for injection (WFI) systems, and rigorous environmental monitoring; they are capital-intensive but command higher margins, especially for veterinary vaccines and antiparasitics relevant to livestock agribusiness.</p>

<p>For African manufacturers, the practical decision matrix is: tablets for high-volume essential medicines (antimalarials, antibiotics, ORS), capsules for nutraceuticals and herbal products where local botanical actives are sensitive to compression heat, and injectables for high-value veterinary biologics or where cold-chain distribution is feasible.</p>

<h3>Excipients and Their Functions</h3>
<p>Excipients are the backbone of formulation economics. In Africa, sourcing excipients locally where possible reduces import dependency and forex exposure. Common excipients include diluents (lactose, microcrystalline cellulose), binders (povidone, starch paste), disintegrants (croscarmellose sodium, sodium starch glycolate), lubricants (magnesium stearate), and glidants (colloidal silicon dioxide). For injectables, you need WFI, pH adjusters, tonicity agents, and preservatives (for multi-dose vials).</p>

<p><strong>Business insight:</strong> Magnesium stearate is the most common lubricant but over-lubrication reduces dissolution. In humid African climates, starch-based binders can support microbial growth specify low-bioburden grades. For oral rehydration salts (ORS), the excipient profile is simple (glucose, sodium chloride, potassium chloride, trisodium citrate) but sourcing food-grade versus pharma-grade materials directly impacts regulatory approval and cost.</p>

<h3>Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP)</h3>
<p>GMP is not optional it is the price of market access. In Africa, WHO GMP compliance is required for tendering to national health systems, export to regulated markets, and prequalification by WHO. Key pillars include: qualified personnel, validated premises and equipment, documented procedures, and robust quality systems. For agribusiness entrants, the practical starting point is a clean, segregated facility with controlled airflow, validated water systems, and a documented batch record system.</p>

<p><strong>Business case:</strong> A small-scale manufacturer in Uganda investing in GMP-compliant facility upgrades can access Global Fund and UNICEF tenders contracts that are otherwise closed to non-compliant producers. The cost of GMP compliance is typically 1525% of capital expenditure but unlocks 35x revenue potential through institutional procurement.</p>

<h3>Quality Control and Quality Assurance</h3>
<p>QC tests the product; QA builds the system that prevents defects. In African settings, QC labs need: HPLC for assay and dissolution, stability chambers, and microbiological testing for non-sterile and sterile products. QA covers batch record review, deviation management, change control, and supplier qualification.</p>

<p><strong>Practical tip:</strong> Many African manufacturers outsource QC testing to contract labs (e.g., in South Africa, Kenya, or Egypt) while building internal QA systems. This hybrid model reduces capital costs while maintaining compliance. However, for injectables, internal sterility testing and environmental monitoring are non-negotiable.</p>

<h3>Scale-Up and Process Validation</h3>
<p>Scale-up is where formulation meets engineering. A formula that works in a 10 kg lab blender may fail in a 500 kg production blender due to shear, mixing time, and granulation endpoint differences. Process validation (PV) demonstrates that your process consistently delivers a product meeting specifications. The three-batch validation approach is standard: three consecutive successful batches at production scale.</p>

<h3>Packaging, Labeling, and Stability Testing</h3>
<p>Packaging is a critical quality attribute, not an afterthought. In Africa, packaging must withstand high humidity, temperature fluctuations, and long distribution chains. Blister packaging (Alu-Alu or Alu-PVC) offers superior moisture protection versus strip packaging or bottles. Labeling must comply with national regulations including language requirements (English, French, Portuguese, Arabic, Swahili), batch number, expiry date, and storage conditions.</p>

<p>Stability testing follows ICH climatic zones. Most of Africa falls under Zone IVb (hot and very humid) 30C 2C / 75% RH 5% RH. This is stricter than Zone I (temperate) and Zone II (subtropical). Products developed for European markets may fail in African conditions if stability studies were not conducted under Zone IVb conditions.</p>

For injectables and vaccines, cold-chain breaches can render products unsafe implement temperature monitoring from manufacturing to point of use. Non-compliance can result in regulatory recall, patient harm, and loss of license.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Formulation:</strong> Confirm dosage form suitability for African climate (Zone IVb stability data mandatory).</li>
<li><strong>Excipients:</strong> Qualify all suppliers; test incoming materials for identity, purity, and microbial limits.</li>
<li><strong>GMP:</strong> Maintain clean, segregated areas; validate HVAC, water, and compressed air systems.</li>
<li><strong>QC:</strong> Establish validated analytical methods; participate in proficiency testing schemes.</li>
<li><strong>QA:</strong> Implement batch record review, deviation management, and change control before commercial release.</li>
<li><strong>Validation:</strong> Complete three consecutive successful process validation batches at production scale.</li>
<li><strong>Packaging:</strong> Use moisture-barrier packaging (Alu-Alu) for hygroscopic products; verify label compliance with national regulators.</li>
<li><strong>Stability:</strong> Conduct real-time and accelerated studies under Zone IVb conditions; monitor ongoing stability annually.</li>
</ol>
