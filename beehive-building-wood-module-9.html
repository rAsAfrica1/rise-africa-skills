Module 9 — Hive Stands, Bases and Apiary Layout

The foundation, the floor and the site plan — the three things that decide whether your apiary is manageable.

Learning outcomes

  • Build a hive stand that keeps hives dry, level and pest-free.
  • Choose between solid, screened and slatted bases and know when each is correct.
  • Lay out an apiary for access, airflow, shade and biosecurity.
  • Control ants, termites, hive beetles and other crawl-in pests with stand design.
  • Number and record hives so you can manage more than ten without confusion.

1. The hive stand

A hive stand does five jobs. Miss any one and you will pay for it every inspection.

  1. Lift the hive off the ground — 400–500 mm. Keeps splash, damp and crawling pests away.
  2. Keep the hive level side-to-side and slightly tilted front-to-back — 5–10 mm fall toward the entrance so rain and condensate drain out.
  3. Carry the weight — a full 10-frame Langstroth can weigh 60–90 kg. A stand must hold 150 kg with no flex.
  4. Resist rot and termites — the reason most stands fail within three years.
  5. Break the pest path — legs that ants and beetles cannot cross.

Sizing the timber for a rail stand

Assume one hive = 80 kg loaded, plus the stand top rail carrying 4 hives between posts.
Load per span (2 posts, 2 hives each side) — worst case a span carries 2 hives = 160 kg = 1 570 N.

For a simply supported beam with two point loads, approximate as a UDL for sizing:
M = wL² / 8

For a 1.5 m span carrying 1 570 N as UDL:
w = 1 570 / 1.5 = 1 047 N/m
M = 1 047 × 1.5² / 8 = 294 N·m

Timber allowable bending stress (treated softwood) ≈ 8 N/mm². Required section modulus:
Z = 294 000 / 8 = 36 750 mm³

A 100 × 100 mm timber has Z = 100 × 100² / 6 = 166 667 mm³ — ample. A 50 × 100 mm on edge has Z ≈ 83 000 mm³ — also fine. A 50 × 50 mm post is not adequate as a horizontal rail but is fine as a vertical post in compression.

2. Bases

The base is the floor of the hive. It is the single most modified part in beekeeping and the choice matters.

Base typeAdvantagesDisadvantagesUse when
Solid baseWarm, simple, cheap, no mesh to clogPoor ventilation; moisture builds up; no varroa drop monitoringCool climates; winter; small colonies
Screened base with sliderVentilation; varroa drop count; moisture escape; can be closedMore complex; mesh can be blocked by propolis and debrisMost situations — the modern default
Slatted baseAirflow under brood; debris falls clearLess common; harder to source; bees may build burr comb belowHumid, hot climates
Deep base with landing boardLanding space; keeps entrance clear of grassRain can pool if not drainedAny climate if drained

Base construction details

  • Mesh spec: galvanised #8 mesh (about 3 mm aperture). Fine enough for varroa, coarse enough for bees not to get stuck.
  • Slider tray: a removable board under the mesh. Fitted = closed base; removed = fully screened.
  • Entrance: full width × 20 mm clear height. Fit an entrance reducer with at least three settings (fully open, 15 mm, 8 mm).
  • Landing board: 75–100 mm deep, sloping slightly down and out so rain runs off.
  • Drainage: the floor of the base tilts 5 mm toward the front. Any water that enters can only leave at the entrance.
  • Joins: external joins sealed or painted; internal surfaces left bare so bees can propolise them.

3. Apiary layout

Layout affects airflow, sun, shade, drift between hives, and how easily you work.

Spacing rules

ElementMinimumPreferredReason
Hive to hive in a row0.6 m1.0 mWorking space; reduces drift
Row to row2.5 m3–4 mForaging bees fly up and over, not into the next row
Hive to boundary5 m10 mNeighbour relations and bee flight lines
Hive to water source—20–100 mClose enough to find, far enough to keep traffic away
Hive to track1 m2 mVehicle and trolley access
Hive entrance to windbreak2 m4 mLift-off and landing space

Orientation and drift

  • Face entrances away from prevailing wind and toward morning sun — bees start foraging earlier.
  • In the tropics, an east or south-east facing entrance with afternoon shade is ideal.
  • In cool climates, a south or south-east facing entrance in full sun.
  • To reduce drift (bees entering the wrong hive), offset rows, vary hive colour or entrance markers, and leave 1 m between hives.

Paint the entrances differently

Alternating colours, or a distinctive mark above each entrance (a shape, a stripe), helps returning foragers find their own hive. It also helps you know which hive you are standing in front of when the numbers fade.

4. Pest control through stand design

PestHow it reaches the hiveDesign control
AntsClimb the stand legsOil traps: a cup around each leg, filled with used engine oil or mineral oil; keep the cup clear of debris
TermitesAttack timber posts and hive bodies from the groundMetal or concrete posts; treated timber; keep 100 mm clearance between soil and any timber
Small hive beetleCrawls up the stand; larvae drop to soil to pupateStand on hard, dry ground or gravel; screened base; keep ground clear of fallen comb
Wax mothFlies in, lays in weak coloniesStrong colonies, no stored comb in the open, regular inspections
RodentsClimb into weak hives in cold weatherReduce entrances in cold season; metal entrance guard
Lizards, toadsSit at the entrance and pick off foragersKeep grass short around the stand; raise the hive 500 mm

Ants are a symptom, not just a pest

A strong colony keeps ants out. If ants are entering freely, the colony is probably weak, queenless or stressed. Treat the cause, not just the oil cup.

5. Ground preparation

  • Clear all vegetation within 1 m of the stand. Bare earth or gravel — no long grass.
  • Level the ground, or dig footings for permanent posts.
  • Slope the ground away from the hives so rainwater does not pool under the stand.
  • Gravel or crushed stone under the stand makes hive beetle larvae and dropped comb easier to spot and remove.
  • Leave a 2 m clear strip along the apiary for walking and moving equipment.

Numbering system

Simple numbering

H01, H02, H03 ... — painted on the front of each base, and on a small marker at the stand.

For larger apiaries, group by yard or site: A-01, A-02, B-01, B-02.

Keep a single master sheet listing every hive, its number, its site, its queen origin and its current status.

7. Biosecurity between hives and apiaries

  • Clean tools between hives — a flame or a bucket of washing soda solution.
  • Never move frames between hives unless you know they are disease-free.
  • Keep a spare, clean hive tool and gloves for visiting other apiaries.
  • If you keep more than one apiary, visit the healthiest first and the weakest last.
  • Record every movement of a hive, a box or a queen. If disease appears, you will need that record.
