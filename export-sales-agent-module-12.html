1. Export Sales Agent Fundamentals for African Markets

Welcome to Module 12, the capstone of your Export Sales Agent training. This module brings together everything you have learned and sharpens it into a practical operating system for selling African products and services across borders. As an export sales agent, you are the bridge between a producer in Lagos, Nairobi, Accra, Cairo, or Johannesburg and a buyer in London, Dubai, Guangzhou, or New York. Your job is not simply to move goods. Your job is to create trust, reduce risk, and make the transaction easy for both sides. In Africa, export sales is rarely just about price. It is about reliability, documentation, logistics, and relationships. A buyer who trusts you will pay a little more. A buyer who does not trust you will disappear after the first delayed shipment. This lesson covers the core mindset, the legal and commercial basics, and the daily habits that separate top-performing export agents from order-takers. First, understand the three pillars of export sales: product readiness, market fit, and transaction capability. Product readiness means the supplier can produce consistent quality at volume, with packaging that survives long-distance freight and meets destination labeling rules. Market fit means there is real demand in the target country, not just a hopeful guess. Transaction capability means you can quote, negotiate, document, finance, ship, and resolve problems without losing the buyer. Many African exporters fail not because their product is bad but because one of these three pillars is weak. Your role is to test all three before you promise anything to a buyer. Start with a supplier audit. Visit the factory or farm if possible. Check production capacity, quality control, raw material sourcing, and lead times. Ask for samples from at least three different production batches. If the supplier cannot provide consistent samples, do not take the order. Next, build a buyer profile. For each target market, identify the importer type, the end user, the typical order size, the preferred Incoterms, and the payment method. A buyer in Rotterdam may expect CIF and a letter of credit. A buyer in Dubai may prefer FOB and a 30 percent deposit with the balance against documents. A buyer in Nairobi buying from Tanzania may want goods delivered by road with payment on delivery. You must know these patterns before you quote. Documentation is the backbone of export sales. Without correct documents, goods sit at ports, incur demurrage, and destroy trust. The core documents are the commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading or airway bill, certificate of origin, phytosanitary certificate for agricultural products, quality certificate, and insurance certificate. Depending on the product and destination, you may also need a fumigation certificate, a halal certificate, a health certificate, or a CITES permit. In Africa, many delays happen because a certificate of origin is filled incorrectly or a phytosanitary certificate is missing. Build a checklist for each product and each destination. Never assume the freight forwarder will fix documentation errors. You are the agent. You own the transaction. Payment risk is the biggest fear for both African suppliers and foreign buyers. Suppliers fear not getting paid. Buyers fear paying for goods that never arrive or arrive in poor condition. Your job is to structure payment terms that balance risk. Common options include advance payment, letter of credit, documentary collection, open account, and escrow. For new buyers, start with a deposit plus balance against documents. For repeat buyers with good history, you can move to open account terms with credit insurance. In Africa, mobile money and fintech platforms are increasingly used for smaller cross-border transactions, especially within ECOWAS and the East African Community. Know the rules of the African Continental Free Trade Area, because they can reduce tariffs and make your offer more competitive. But also know that AfCFTA implementation varies by country and customs office. Do not promise duty-free treatment until you have confirmed the tariff line and the rules of origin. Logistics in Africa requires patience and contingency planning. Port congestion in Lagos, Durban, Mombasa, and Tema can add weeks. Border posts such as Beitbridge, Chirundu, and Kasumbalesa can be slow. Airfreight from Nairobi, Addis Ababa, Johannesburg, and Cairo is faster but expensive. Your job is to give the buyer realistic transit times, not optimistic ones. Under-promise and over-deliver. If you say 30 days, plan for 45 and communicate updates every week. Silence is the enemy of trust. Finally, develop a daily routine. Morning: check emails and WhatsApp messages from buyers and suppliers. Midday: follow up on shipments, documents, and payments. Afternoon: prospect new buyers and research market prices. Evening: update your pipeline and send status reports. Keep a simple CRM, even if it is a spreadsheet. Track every buyer, every quote, every shipment, and every payment. Your reputation is your most valuable asset. In Africa, word travels fast. One bad shipment can close doors in an entire market. One excellent shipment can open doors you never imagined. Treat every container as if your career depends on it, because it does.

2. Building Buyer Relationships Across African and Global Markets

In export sales, relationships are not a soft skill. They are the infrastructure of the business. In Africa, where formal contract enforcement can be slow and expensive, trust often carries more weight than a legal document. A buyer who trusts you will accept a delay, a price adjustment, or a change in specification. A buyer who does not trust you will use every small problem as a reason to walk away. This lesson teaches you how to build, maintain, and repair buyer relationships across cultures, time zones, and business norms. Start with the first contact. Whether you meet a buyer at a trade fair in Cairo, send a cold email to an importer in Hamburg, or get an introduction through a mutual contact in Nairobi, your first message must answer three questions: who are you, what can you supply, and why should the buyer care. Do not send a long company profile. Send a short, specific message with a clear product, a clear price range, and a clear next step. For example: We supply roasted cashew nuts from Ghana, grade W320, 20 metric tons per month, FOB Tema, with phytosanitary and quality certificates. Can I send you our spec sheet and a sample? That message respects the buyer's time and makes it easy to reply. Once a buyer responds, move quickly. In export sales, speed signals seriousness. If a buyer asks for a quotation, send it within 24 hours. If you need more information, send a partial quote and ask the missing questions. Do not disappear for a week and then reappear with a perfect quote. By then, the buyer has moved on. Your quote should include product specification, packaging, quantity, unit price, currency, Incoterm, port of loading, port of discharge, lead time, payment terms, validity period, and document list. Ambiguity creates disputes. Clarity creates confidence. Cultural intelligence matters. African markets are diverse, and so are global buyers. A buyer in Japan may value detailed specifications and punctuality above all. A buyer in the Gulf may value personal connection and hospitality. A buyer in Europe may value sustainability certificates and traceability. A buyer in China may value volume, speed, and price competitiveness. A buyer in another African country may value flexibility on payment and delivery. Learn the difference between direct and indirect communication. In many African and Asian cultures, a direct no is avoided. You may hear we will see or it is difficult instead of no. Learn to read the signal and ask clarifying questions politely. Do not pressure a buyer into a yes. Instead, remove obstacles one by one. Trust is built in small moments. Remember the buyer's name and their assistant's name. Remember their holidays and their busy seasons. Send a short message after a shipment arrives to ask about quality. If there is a problem, respond immediately with a solution, not an excuse. In Africa, the phrase the customer is king is taken seriously. But the deeper truth is that the customer is a partner. You are not begging for orders. You are offering value. That mindset changes how you negotiate. When you negotiate, focus on interests, not positions. The buyer's position may be your price is too high. The buyer's interest may be I need a landed cost that lets me make 30 percent margin. If you understand the interest, you can offer options: a different packaging size, a different Incoterm, a different payment term, or a different grade. Do not simply cut your price. Cutting price without changing anything else teaches the buyer that your first price was not honest. Instead, trade concessions. If the buyer wants a lower price, ask for a larger volume, a longer contract, or faster payment. Every concession should have a counterpart. Manage your pipeline like a farmer manages a field. Some buyers are ready to harvest. Some are still growing. Some are just seeds. Do not spend all your time on the ready buyers and ignore the seeds. In export sales, the sales cycle can be three to twelve months. You need a steady flow of new contacts to replace the buyers who go quiet. Use trade fairs, chambers of commerce, export promotion agencies, LinkedIn, and referrals. In Africa, referrals are gold. Ask your best buyer for an introduction to one other importer in their network. Ask your freight forwarder which buyers are active in your product. Ask your bank which letters of credit are being opened for your commodity. Build a reputation as someone who delivers. Then the buyers will come to you. Handle complaints with speed and grace. Every exporter will have a shipment that goes wrong. The container is delayed. The quality is slightly off. The documents have an error. What matters is not that the problem happened. What matters is how you respond. Acknowledge the problem within 24 hours. Investigate within 48 hours. Offer a solution within 72 hours. Follow up until the buyer confirms satisfaction. A buyer who sees you solve a problem well becomes more loyal than a buyer who never had a problem. Finally, think long term. The export agent who survives and thrives is the one who is still in business after ten years. That means you do not chase one-time profits at the expense of relationships. You do not misrepresent quality. You do not hide costs. You do not blame the supplier, the shipping line, or the customs officer. You take ownership. In Africa, reputation is built over years and lost in a day. Protect it. Your buyers are not just customers. They are your partners in bringing African value to the world. Treat them that way, and they will keep coming back.

3. Pricing, Quoting, and Negotiating Export Deals in Africa

Pricing is where export sales agents either make their money or lose their shirt. In Africa, pricing is complicated by currency volatility, inflation, logistics costs, and informal fees. A price that works today may not work next month. A quote that looks profitable on paper may collapse when the naira, cedi, shilling, or rand moves. This lesson teaches you how to build a price, how to quote it, and how to negotiate without giving away your margin. Start with the cost build-up. Your price must cover the product cost, packaging, inland transport, export documentation, customs clearance, port charges, freight, insurance, and your commission. If you are quoting CIF, you must include freight and insurance to the destination port. If you are quoting FOB, you stop at the port of loading. If you are quoting DDP, you must include import duties and delivery to the buyer's door. Most African exporters start with FOB because it is simpler and lower risk. But some buyers prefer CIF because it gives them one price. Know the difference and be able to quote both. Build a spreadsheet that calculates your break-even price for each Incoterm. Include a line for currency fluctuation. If your costs are in local currency and your revenue is in US dollars or euros, you are exposed to exchange rate movements. You can manage this by pricing in hard currency, by adding a currency adjustment clause, or by hedging with a bank. For small exporters, the simplest approach is to quote in US dollars and review your price every 30 days. Do not quote in local currency unless the buyer insists and you have a plan to convert quickly. Packaging is a hidden cost. In Africa, packaging quality varies widely. If you are exporting agricultural products, you need food-grade packaging that meets destination rules. If you are exporting manufactured goods, you need packaging that survives rough handling and long transit. Do not let the supplier use cheap packaging to save money. A damaged shipment costs more than good packaging. Freight is the biggest variable. Ocean freight rates from African ports can swing by 50 percent or more in a year. Airfreight is even more volatile. Get at least two quotes from freight forwarders for every shipment. Ask about surcharges, demurrage, detention, and terminal handling charges. These can add thousands of dollars. If you are quoting CIF, build in a buffer of 10 to 15 percent on freight. If you are quoting FOB, tell the buyer that freight is not included and give them a rough estimate so they are not surprised. Documentation costs are small but important. Certificates of origin, phytosanitary certificates, quality certificates, and legalization fees can add up. Include them in your price. Do not absorb them silently. Your commission must be clear. As an export sales agent, you may work on a percentage of the invoice value, a fixed fee per shipment, or a markup on the product price. Whatever the model, put it in writing with your supplier. A common model in Africa is 3 to 7 percent of the FOB value for the agent. For high-value or high-risk markets, it can be 10 percent or more. Do not work for free. Do not work on promises. Get a signed agency agreement that states your commission, your territory, your product, and your duration. Now, quoting. A good quote is clear, complete, and time-bound. It includes the product name, specification, grade, packaging, quantity, unit price, total price, currency, Incoterm, port of loading, port of discharge, lead time, payment terms, document list, and validity. It also includes your contact details and a polite call to action. Do not send a quote with missing information. Do not send a quote that expires in 90 days if your costs change weekly. Use a 15-day or 30-day validity. If the buyer asks for a longer validity, explain that you can hold the price for 30 days and then review. Negotiation is not a fight. It is a conversation about value. Before you negotiate, know your walk-away point. Know your target price. Know your concession plan. If the buyer asks for a 10 percent discount, do not say yes immediately. Ask what volume they are considering. Ask what payment terms they can offer. Ask if they can commit to a quarterly contract. Then offer a smaller discount in exchange for something you need. For example: I can offer 5 percent off if you increase the order to two containers and pay 50 percent deposit. That is a professional trade, not a surrender. In Africa, negotiation often includes relationship building. Do not rush to the price. Ask about the buyer's business, their market, their challenges. Understand their landed cost and their margin. If you can help them make more money, price becomes less sensitive. Sometimes the best negotiation move is to change the specification. Offer a different grade, a different packaging size, or a different origin that meets their needs at a lower cost. Sometimes the best move is to change the payment terms. A buyer who cannot pay a 50 percent deposit may accept a letter of credit. A buyer who cannot open a letter of credit may accept a smaller trial order with advance payment. Be creative. Be flexible. But never be desperate. Desperation destroys price. If you cannot meet the buyer's target, say so politely and explain why. Offer an alternative. Keep the relationship alive. A no today can become a yes tomorrow. Watch your margins. In export sales, it is easy to focus on revenue and ignore profit. A 100,000 dollar order with a 2 percent margin is not better than a 20,000 dollar order with a 15 percent margin. The first order carries more risk, more paperwork, and more stress. Know your numbers. Review your profit after every shipment. If a product or a market is not profitable, fix it or leave it. Finally, protect yourself against non-payment. Use letters of credit, documentary collections, credit insurance, or advance payment. Do not ship on open account to a new buyer unless you can afford to lose the money. In Africa, payment delays are common. Build a cash buffer. Do not depend on one buyer. Do not depend on one product. Diversify your risk. Pricing and negotiation are skills. They improve with practice. Study your competitors. Study your buyers. Study your costs. The more you know, the more confident you become. Confidence, backed by numbers, is the best negotiation tool you have.

4. Export Documentation, Compliance, and Risk Management

Documentation is the difference between a smooth export and a costly disaster. In Africa, where customs systems vary widely and border processes can be unpredictable, a single missing or incorrect document can delay a shipment for weeks. This lesson covers the essential documents, the compliance rules you must know, and the risk management tools that protect your business. Start with the commercial invoice. This is the buyer's record of what they bought and the customs authority's record of value. It must include the seller's and buyer's full names and addresses, the invoice number and date, the product description, the quantity, the unit price, the total value, the currency, the Incoterm, the country of origin, and the payment terms. Do not use vague descriptions like general goods or samples. Be specific. If the invoice is wrong, customs may revalue the goods, charge penalties, or seize the shipment. The packing list is the companion to the invoice. It lists the number of packages, the contents of each package, the weight, and the dimensions. It helps the buyer and the customs officer verify the shipment. The bill of lading is the transport document for sea freight. It is issued by the shipping line or the freight forwarder. It serves as a receipt, a contract, and a document of title. The buyer needs the original bill of lading to collect the goods. If it is delayed or lost, the goods sit at the port. The airway bill is the equivalent for air freight. It is not a document of title, but it is still essential for customs clearance. The certificate of origin proves where the goods were produced. It is required for preferential tariff treatment under trade agreements such as AfCFTA, the EU's Everything But Arms scheme, AGOA, and various bilateral agreements. If you claim preferential treatment without a valid certificate, the buyer may have to pay full duty, and they will not be happy. The phytosanitary certificate is required for plants, seeds, and agricultural products. It is issued by the national plant protection organization in the exporting country. It confirms that the goods are free from pests and diseases. Without it, agricultural shipments can be rejected at the border. The health certificate is required for animal products, processed foods, and some cosmetics. It is issued by the relevant government authority. The quality certificate confirms that the goods meet the agreed specification. It may be issued by the supplier, an independent inspection company, or a government lab. The insurance certificate proves that the goods are covered against loss or damage. If you are quoting CIF or CIP, you must provide it. Other documents may include a fumigation certificate, a halal certificate, a kosher certificate, a CITES permit for endangered species, a radiation certificate, and a non-GMO certificate. Build a document checklist for each product and each destination. Update it every time a rule changes. Do not rely on memory. Compliance is not optional. It is the price of entry. In Africa, compliance rules are changing fast. Many countries are digitizing customs through systems like ASYCUDA, TradeNet, and single windows. This can speed up clearance, but it also means that errors are captured instantly. Make sure your supplier and your freight forwarder are registered and compliant. Check that your product meets the destination's labeling rules. In the European Union, for example, food products must have labels in the local language, with ingredients, allergens, nutrition information, and best-before dates. In the Gulf, halal certification is often required. In the United States, FDA registration and prior notice are required for food imports. In China, GACC registration is required for food exporters. If you ignore these rules, your buyer will face problems, and you will lose the account. Risk management is about identifying, reducing, and transferring risk. The main risks in export sales are payment risk, delivery risk, quality risk, currency risk, and political risk. Payment risk is the risk that the buyer does not pay. Reduce it with advance payment, letters of credit, documentary collections, credit insurance, or export factoring. Delivery risk is the risk that the goods are lost, damaged, or delayed. Reduce it with reliable freight forwarders, cargo insurance, and realistic lead times. Quality risk is the risk that the goods do not meet the buyer's expectations. Reduce it with pre-shipment inspection, clear specifications, and samples. Currency risk is the risk that exchange rates move against you. Reduce it with hard currency pricing, forward contracts, or short quote validity. Political risk is the risk that a country's government changes rules, blocks payments, or restricts trade. Reduce it with political risk insurance, diversification, and careful market selection. Use contracts. A written contract is not a sign of distrust. It is a sign of professionalism. Your contract should state the product, quantity, quality, price, Incoterm, payment terms, delivery date, documents, inspection method, dispute resolution, and governing law. If the buyer refuses to sign a contract, be careful. You can still proceed with a proforma invoice and a letter of credit, but you have less protection. Keep records. Every email, every WhatsApp message, every invoice, every bill of lading, every payment receipt. In a dispute, records are your evidence. Store them in the cloud and back them up. Finally, build a network of professionals. A good freight forwarder, a good customs broker, a good banker, and a good lawyer are worth their weight in gold. In Africa, relationships with these professionals can save you days and thousands of dollars. Treat them well. Pay them on time. Give them accurate information. They will help you when you need it most. Documentation and compliance are not glamorous. But they are the foundation of a sustainable export business. Master them, and you will sleep well at night.

5. Scaling Your Export Sales Agency in Africa

You have learned the fundamentals, the relationships, the pricing, and the documentation. Now it is time to think about scale. How do you grow from a one-person operation handling a few containers a year to a professional export sales agency with multiple products, multiple markets, and a reliable income? This lesson covers the systems, the team, the technology, and the strategy for scaling in the African context. First, specialize. The export agents who scale fastest are not generalists. They are specialists in one product category or one market. A specialist in shea butter from West Africa knows the buyers, the grades, the regulations, and the competition. A specialist in coffee from Ethiopia knows the washing stations, the cupping scores, and the specialty roasters. A specialist in leather goods from Morocco knows the tanneries, the fashion brands, and the trade fairs. Specialization builds credibility. It also reduces your learning curve and your marketing cost. Once you dominate one niche, you can add adjacent products or markets. But do not start by trying to sell everything to everyone. Second, build systems. A scalable agency runs on repeatable processes. Create a standard operating procedure for each stage of the export cycle: supplier onboarding, buyer prospecting, quoting, order confirmation, documentation, logistics, payment, and after-sales follow-up. Write these procedures down. Use checklists. Use templates. Use a customer relationship management tool, even if it is a simple spreadsheet or a free tool like HubSpot or Zoho. The goal is to make your business less dependent on your memory and more dependent on your systems. When you are sick or traveling, the business should still function. Third, build a team. You cannot scale alone. Your first hires should be people who free up your time for selling and relationship building. Consider a documentation officer who prepares invoices, packing lists, certificates, and shipping instructions. Consider a logistics coordinator who manages freight forwarders, customs brokers, and transporters. Consider a sales assistant who handles prospecting, follow-up, and customer service. In Africa, talent is abundant, but training is essential. Hire for attitude and train for skill. Pay fairly. Provide clear targets and incentives. Treat your team with respect. A loyal team is your competitive advantage. Fourth, use technology. Africa is a global leader in mobile technology. Use WhatsApp Business for buyer communication. Use mobile money and fintech platforms for payments. Use cloud accounting software like QuickBooks or Xero. Use export management software or a simple shared drive for documents. Use video calls for supplier audits and buyer meetings. Use social media and LinkedIn for prospecting. Use trade data platforms like ImportGenius, Panjiva, or Trade Map to identify buyers and track competitors. Technology reduces cost, increases speed, and improves accuracy. But do not let technology replace personal relationships. Use it to support them. Fifth, diversify your markets. Africa's traditional export markets are Europe, North America, and Asia. But intra-African trade is growing, especially under AfCFTA. Consider selling to buyers in South Africa, Nigeria, Kenya, Egypt, Morocco, and Ghana. Consider the Gulf markets of Dubai, Saudi Arabia, and Qatar. Consider Turkey, India, and China. Each market has different rules, different tastes, and different payment norms. Diversification reduces your risk. If one market slows down, another can keep you busy. Sixth, manage your finances carefully. Export sales can be cash-hungry. You may need to pay suppliers before buyers pay you. You may need to fund freight, insurance, and documentation. Build a cash reserve. Establish a line of credit with your bank. Use export finance products such as pre-shipment finance, post-shipment finance, and invoice discounting. Keep your personal and business finances separate. Pay your taxes. In Africa, tax compliance is increasingly enforced, and a clean record opens doors to government support and bank credit. Seventh, build your brand. As an export sales agent, your brand is your reputation. Create a professional website. Create a company profile. Attend trade fairs. Speak at industry events. Write articles or post updates on LinkedIn. Join export associations and chambers of commerce. Get certifications such as ISO 9001, HACCP, or Fairtrade if relevant to your product. A strong brand attracts better buyers and better suppliers. It also gives you pricing power. Eighth, measure your performance. Track your key numbers: number of new buyers contacted, number of quotes sent, conversion rate, average order value, gross margin, payment collection time, and customer retention rate. Review these numbers monthly. Identify what is working and what is not. Double down on what works. Cut what does not. Use data to make decisions, not feelings. Ninth, manage risk as you grow. More buyers mean more exposure. More shipments mean more chances for problems. Review your insurance. Review your contracts. Review your payment terms. Do not let a single large order put your business at risk. If a buyer asks for a volume you cannot handle, consider partnering with another agent or supplier. Consider using a confirming house or a trade finance partner. Consider joint ventures. Growth should be sustainable, not reckless. Tenth, think about the future. The African export landscape is changing. The AfCFTA is creating a single market of over 1.3 billion people. Digital platforms are connecting buyers and sellers directly. Sustainability and traceability are becoming mandatory in many markets. Climate change is affecting agricultural supply. Geopolitical shifts are changing trade routes. As an export sales agent, you must adapt. Learn continuously. Attend webinars. Read trade publications. Talk to buyers and suppliers. Travel when you can. The agents who thrive are the ones who see change as opportunity, not threat. Finally, remember why you started. Export sales is not just about making money. It is about connecting African producers to global markets. It is about creating jobs, building industries, and telling a positive story about African quality and reliability. Every container you ship is a statement. Every buyer you satisfy is an ambassador. Every supplier you support is a partner in development. You are not just an agent. You are a bridge. Build that bridge strong, and it will carry you far. This concludes Module 12. You now have the knowledge, the tools, and the mindset to succeed as an Export Sales Agent. The rest is action. Start today. Contact one new buyer. Improve one document. Fix one price. Build one relationship. Small steps, repeated daily, create extraordinary results. Good luck, and go sell Africa to the world.

<div style="border:2px solid #b00020; background:#fdecea; padding:14px; border-radius:6px; margin:20px 0;">
<strong style="color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING HEALTH, FOOD, AND CHEMICAL COMPLIANCE</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Live animal and meat exports carry serious zoonotic and food-safety risks. Never ship animals or products without a valid veterinary health certificate and, where required, an import permit from the destination country. Antibiotic residues above maximum residue limits (MRLs) will trigger rejection and possible blacklisting. Withdrawal periods must be observed strictly: for example, oxytetracycline requires a 28-day withdrawal in cattle before slaughter. Highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) and foot-and-mouth disease (FMD) outbreaks can close borders with zero notice check the WOAH (OIE) WAHIS dashboard before every consignment. Chemical disinfectants and fumigants must be used per label; misuse is a public health offence and voids your export licence. If in doubt, stop the shipment and consult the national veterinary authority.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist for Export Sales Agents</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Verify buyer import permit validity</strong> check issue date, product scope, and expiry before quoting.</li>
<li><strong>Confirm disease-free status</strong> of the source zone on WOAH WAHIS and national veterinary bulletins.</li>
<li><strong>Match HS codes precisely</strong> misclassification causes duty overpayment or seizure (e.g., 0102 live cattle vs 0201 beef).</li>
<li><strong>Inspect cold-chain data loggers</strong> at loading and unloading; retain records for 24 months.</li>
<li><strong>Check withdrawal periods</strong> for all veterinary drugs used on source animals.</li>
<li><strong>Secure SPS and phytosanitary certificates</strong> from the national plant/animal health authority.</li>
<li><strong>Calculate landed cost</strong> including duty, VAT, port charges, and demurrage buffer (minimum 5% of CIF).</li>
<li><strong>Confirm payment instrument</strong> (LC, CAD, or advance) is irrevocable and confirmed by a prime bank.</li>
</ul>
