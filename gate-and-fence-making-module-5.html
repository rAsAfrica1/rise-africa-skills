Lessons

1. Types of Metal Fences: Chain Link, Aluminum, Steel

When choosing a metal fence, consider cost, durability, and local availability. Chain link is the cheapest and most common for security and boundaries. It comes in galvanized (silver) or vinyl-coated (green, black) finishes. Standard chain link fabric is 1.2m to 2.4m high, with wire gauge 9 to 12 (lower gauge = thicker wire). In Africa, galvanized chain link is widely sold in rolls of 10m or 15m. It is easy to install but can sag if not tensioned properly. Use it for schools, homes, and farms.

Aluminum fences are lightweight, rust-proof, and stylish. They are popular for residential estates and commercial compounds. Aluminum comes in powder-coated colors (black, bronze, white) and in panels or pickets. It costs 2-3 times more than chain link, but needs no painting and lasts decades. However, aluminum is softer than steel, so it can be bent or cut by thieves. Use it for front boundaries where appearance matters.

Steel fences are the strongest. Common types include welded mesh, expanded metal, and palisade (vertical bars with pointed tops). Steel is used for high-security areas: prisons, banks, and industrial sites. It can be galvanized or painted. Mild steel rusts quickly if not protected, so always use galvanized or powder-coated steel. Steel palisade fences are typically 1.8m to 3m high, with bars spaced 100mm to 150mm apart. They are heavy and require strong posts and concrete footings.

In Africa, scrap metal and local welders can make custom steel fences at low cost. But ensure welds are ground smooth and painted with red oxide primer plus enamel. For coastal areas, salt accelerates rust, so choose aluminum or hot-dip galvanized steel. For inland rural areas, chain link with concrete posts is economical. Always buy from a reputable supplier and check wire thickness with a gauge. A good fence balances security, cost, and maintenance. Remember: the fence is only as strong as its posts and footings, so never skimp on those.

2. Setting Metal Posts and Concrete Footings

Metal posts carry the entire fence, so they must be set deep and straight. For a 1.8m high fence, dig holes 600mm deep and 300mm diameter. In sandy or loose soil, go 800mm deep. Space posts 2.4m to 3m apart for chain link, and 1.8m to 2.4m for heavy steel palisade. Use a shovel, post-hole digger, or auger. In rocky ground, a digging bar and hoe work. Mark post positions with string and pegs first.

Use concrete mix of 1 part cement, 2 parts sand, 3 parts gravel (1:2:3). For one post hole, you need about 2 bags of cement for 10 posts. Mix dry, then add water until it holds shape when squeezed. Pour 100mm of concrete at the bottom, then insert the post. Check vertical with a spirit level on two sides. Brace with wooden stakes and string. Fill hole with concrete, tamping with a rod to remove air pockets. Slope the top of concrete away from the post so water runs off.

For metal posts, use galvanized pipe (50mm diameter for chain link, 75mm for heavy gates). If using steel angle iron, set the angle facing inward. For aluminum fences, posts are usually square tubes (50x50mm) with base plates bolted to concrete. In that case, cast a concrete footing 300x300x300mm with anchor bolts. Let concrete cure for at least 3 days before attaching fence. In hot climates, cover with wet sack and keep damp for 24 hours to prevent cracking.

In Africa, many installers use local sand and gravel. Wash sand to remove clay. Use clean water, not salty or muddy. If you are near a river, pit sand is better than river sand for concrete. For every 50kg cement bag, use 4 head pans of sand and 8 head pans of gravel. Always wear gloves and mask when mixing cement. After curing, backfill around the concrete with soil and compact. A well-set post will not move even if you hang on it. Test each post before proceeding.

3. Assembling Chain Link Fence Framework

The framework includes top rail, bottom rail (optional), and tension wire. For a standard 1.2m high fence, use a top rail of 32mm galvanized pipe. For 1.8m, use 40mm. Bottom rail is recommended for high-traffic areas or where soil shifts. Cut rails to fit between posts, allowing 50mm overlap into each post. Use eye bolts or rail ends to attach. Most posts have pre-drilled holes or you can drill with a 10mm bit.

Start by installing the top rail. Slide it through the top post caps or clamp it with rail clamps. Tighten bolts with a spanner. The rail should be level. Use a string line from one end post to the other to check. If the fence line is long, install in sections of 30m to allow for expansion. For corners, use corner posts with brace bands. Add a diagonal brace from the corner post to the next post for rigidity.

Next, install the bottom tension wire. Use 4mm galvanized wire. Run it through holes near the bottom of each post, about 100mm above ground. Tighten with a turnbuckle at one end. This wire keeps the chain link fabric from lifting. For fences without bottom rail, the tension wire is essential. For fences with bottom rail, you can still add tension wire for extra support.

Finally, add the top tension wire if using a top rail? Actually, chain link fabric is usually tied to top rail with tie wires. But for a taut fence, run a tension wire along the top rail as well, especially for long spans. Use wire tensioners every 15m. Tighten until the wire twangs when plucked. Do not over-tighten or you will bend posts. In Africa, many use locally made turnbuckles from scrap. Make sure all bolts are galvanized or painted to prevent rust. After assembly, check that all rails are straight and all bolts are tight. A loose framework will cause the fabric to sag.

4. Installing Chain Link Fabric and Tension Wire

Chain link fabric comes in rolls. Unroll it along the fence line, standing it upright. Start at one end post. Attach the fabric to the end post using a tension bar (a metal bar that slides through the last diamond) and tension bands. The tension bar should be vertical and bolted to the post with 5mm bolts. Then pull the fabric tight by hand along the top rail. Use a come-along or fence puller to stretch it. Attach a tension bar at the other end and pull until the fabric is taut. You should not be able to push it more than 25mm.

For long runs, stretch in sections of 30m. Use a turnbuckle on the tension wire to fine-tune. After stretching, secure the fabric to the top rail with tie wires every 300mm. Tie wires are 2mm galvanized wire; twist them tight with pliers. Also tie to the bottom tension wire every 300mm. For posts in between, use post ties (wire clips) to attach fabric to each post. This prevents the fabric from pulling away.

Cut the fabric with bolt cutters or a hacksaw. Wear gloves; the cut ends are sharp. For corners, bend the fabric around the corner post and overlap by one diamond. Use a tension bar at the corner. For gates, leave an opening of the gate width plus 50mm. The fabric should not touch the ground; keep it 50mm above to prevent rust from soil moisture. In Africa, some installers bury the bottom 100mm in a concrete strip to prevent digging under. That is good for security but may rust faster. Use galvanized fabric if burying.

After installation, walk along the fence and check for loose ties. Tighten any sagging areas. If the fabric is too tight, it may tear at the ties. Adjust turnbuckles. For coastal areas, apply cold galvanizing spray to cut ends and scratches. A well-installed chain link fence will last 10-15 years in inland Africa, 5-8 years near the coast. Always keep tension wire tight; re-tighten after one month as the fence settles.

5. Building and Hanging Metal Gates

Metal gates must be strong and square. For a 3m wide gate, use a frame of 40x40mm square tubing or 50mm angle iron. Weld the frame into a rectangle. Add a diagonal brace from bottom hinge corner to top latch corner to prevent sagging. For chain link gates, weld a chain link fabric to the frame or use a tension bar. For steel gates, weld vertical bars or mesh inside the frame. Space bars 100-150mm apart. Grind all welds smooth.

Hinges: Use heavy-duty weld-on hinges or bolt-on hinges. For a 3m gate, use three hinges (top, middle, bottom). Weld the hinge halves to the gate frame and the post. The gate should swing freely. For double gates, use a drop bolt or a cane bolt at the bottom center to secure when closed. For single gates, install a latch or a sliding bolt. In Africa, many use locally made hinges from car parts or thick flat iron. Ensure the hinge pin is at least 12mm diameter.

Hanging the gate: With help, lift the gate into position. Support it with blocks so it is level and has 50mm clearance from the ground. Weld or bolt the hinges to the post. Check that the gate opens and closes without binding. If it binds, adjust hinges or add washers. The gate should not drag. For heavy gates, install a wheel at the bottom or a jockey wheel. For double gates, ensure the two leaves meet in the middle with a 10mm gap.

Finish: Paint the gate with red oxide primer then two coats of enamel. For galvanized gates, use zinc-rich paint. Add a lock box or chain and padlock. For security, weld a piece of flat iron over the hinge pins so they cannot be removed. In Africa, many gates are made from scrap metal. That is fine, but ensure the frame is not twisted. Use a spirit level and tape measure. A well-built gate will last many years. Remember to grease hinges every 6 months. If the gate sags, tighten the diagonal brace or add a turnbuckle from the top hinge corner to the bottom latch corner.

6. Rust Prevention and Metal Finishing

Rust is the number one enemy of metal fences, especially in Africa where humidity and salt are high. Start by removing all rust and mill scale with a wire brush or angle grinder with a flap disc. For heavy rust, use a rust converter (phosphoric acid) before painting. Wipe with a clean cloth. Then apply a primer. For steel, use red oxide primer or zinc chromate primer. For galvanized metal, use a galvanized-specific primer (etch primer) or just clean with vinegar and paint with direct-to-metal paint.

Topcoat: Use enamel paint or polyurethane paint. For outdoor fences, use two coats. Allow 4 hours drying between coats. In hot sun, paint early morning or late afternoon to avoid blistering. For chain link fabric, you can spray with cold galvanizing spray (zinc-rich) in a can. This is expensive but effective for small areas. For large fences, use a paint sprayer or roller. Mix paint with thinner according to instructions. Do not paint over dirt or oil; it will peel.

For aluminum fences, they are usually powder-coated at the factory. If scratched, touch up with matching touch-up paint. Do not paint aluminum with regular steel paint; it will not adhere. For galvanized steel, it can be left unpainted (it will weather to a dull gray) or painted for color. If painting galvanized, wait 6 months for it to weather, or wash with a mild acid (vinegar) to promote adhesion.

Maintenance: Inspect your fence every 6 months. Touch up any scratches or rust spots immediately. Keep the bottom of the fence clear of vegetation and soil. In coastal areas, wash with fresh water monthly to remove salt. For gates, oil hinges and latches. If you see white rust (zinc oxide) on galvanized metal, brush it off and apply zinc-rich paint. In Africa, many use used engine oil as a cheap rust preventative. It works for a few months but attracts dust. Better to use proper paint. A well-maintained metal fence can last 20 years or more. Teach your clients to do simple touch-ups.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Metal Fence Construction for African Agribusiness

Metal fencing is the backbone of secure land, livestock, and crop protection across Africa. Whether you are enclosing a poultry unit in Ogun State, a macadamia orchard in Kenya, or a feedlot in Mpumalanga, the difference between a fence that lasts 25 years and one that collapses in 5 comes down to three things: correct material selection, proper post setting, and disciplined rust prevention. This Practical Reference module consolidates the six lessons in the Metal Fence Construction course into a single working document you can take to site, to the hardware supplier, and to the bank.

Treat this as a business tool. A well-built metal fence reduces theft losses, controls animal movement, protects high-value genetics, and raises the resale value of your land. A poorly built fence is a recurring expense that eats profit every season.

1. Material Selection by Application

Africa's climate and security realities should drive your choice of metal fence type. Coastal areas (Mombasa, Durban, Lagos, Dar es Salaam) demand hot-dip galvanised or powder-coated systems because salt spray attacks bare steel within 18 months. Inland highveld and savannah areas face intense UV and seasonal moisture, which favour aluminium or heavy-gauge galvanised steel. High-theft corridors peri-urban livestock areas, road-adjacent orchards, mining towns need steel with anti-climb geometry and secure gate hardware.

2. Setting Metal Posts and Concrete Footings

Post setting is where most African fence projects fail. The rule is simple: hole depth should be one-third of the above-ground post height for line posts, and one-quarter of post height plus 100 mm for terminal and corner posts. In black cotton soil (vertisols) common in Tanzania's Rift Valley and parts of Zambia, widen the hole and add a 50 mm gravel base to prevent concrete from cracking during the wet season.

Concrete mix: use a 1:3:6 (cement:sand:aggregate) mix for footings, or a ready-mix 20 MPa concrete. In remote areas, a 1:4:8 mix with clean river sand and 20 mm crushed stone is acceptable if cured properly. Always crown the concrete slightly above ground level and slope it away from the post so water runs off this single detail adds years to post life.

3. Assembling the Chain Link Framework

The framework terminal posts, line posts, top rail, bottom tension wire, and diagonal braces carries all the fabric tension. Install in this order:

  1. Set all terminal (corner, end, gate) posts first, then line posts.
  2. Fit top rail through post caps or rail-end fittings, joining with sleeves or expansion couplings.
  3. Install diagonal braces on every terminal post and every gate post.
  4. Run bottom tension wire and anchor with tension bands at each terminal.
  5. Stretch fabric, then tension top rail and bottom wire together.

4. Installing Fabric and Tension Wire

Chain link fabric must be stretched, not merely hung. Use a fence stretcher (come-along) and pull until the fabric is taut enough that a hand cannot deflect it more than 25 mm. Attach to terminal posts with tension bands and brace bands, and to line posts with wire ties at every post. Bottom tension wire should sit 5075 mm above ground to prevent animals pushing under the fence.

5. Building and Hanging Metal Gates

Gates are the highest-stress component. Build gate frames from 40 mm 40 mm square tubing or 50 mm round pipe, with a diagonal brace welded corner-to-corner. Hinge points must be welded to a gate post that is itself set in a footing at least 900 mm deep. For farm vehicle access, use a double-leaf gate with a drop rod into a concrete receiver in the ground.

SAFETY WARNING WELDING AND CUTTING

Welding and angle-grinding galvanised steel releases zinc oxide fumes that cause metal fume fever flu-like symptoms that can put a worker out for 2448 hours. Always weld galvanised steel in a well-ventilated area or outdoors, wear a P2/P3 respirator, and grind back the zinc coating 50 mm on each side of the weld zone before welding. Never weld in a closed shed or near stored fuel, feed, or agrochemicals. In rural African workshops where ventilation is poor, this is the single most common serious injury in fence construction.

6. Rust Prevention and Metal Finishing

Every cut end, weld, and scratch is a future rust point. The finishing protocol is:

  • Wire-brush all welds and cut ends to bare metal.
  • Apply a zinc-rich cold galvanising compound (95% zinc) to welds, cuts, and scratches.
  • For coastal sites, overcoat with an epoxy primer and a polyurethane topcoat.
  • For inland sites, a good quality enamel or acrylic metal paint is sufficient.
  • Re-inspect and touch up every 24 months this is cheaper than replacing posts.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Confirm fence line, corners, and gate positions with pegs and string before digging.
  • Check soil type widen footings in black cotton or sandy soils.
  • Set terminal and gate posts first; cure concrete 4872 hours before loading.
  • Crown concrete footings and slope away from posts to shed water.
  • Install diagonal braces on every terminal and gate post.
  • Stretch fabric taut (max 25 mm deflection) and tie at every line post.
  • Weld gate frames with diagonal brace; hang with heavy-duty hinges.
  • Cold-galvanise all welds and cuts; paint within 24 hours.
