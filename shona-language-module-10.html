1. School Subjects and Supplies

In Shona, school subjects are called zvidzidzo. The most common subjects you will talk about are: Mathematics (Masvomhu), English (Chirungu), Shona (ChiShona), Science (Sainzi), Agriculture (Zvekurima), and History (Nhoroondo). To say 'I study Mathematics', you say 'Ndiri kudzidza Masvomhu.' If you want to ask 'What subject do you like?', say 'Unoda chidzidzo chipi?' The answer might be 'Ndinoda Sainzi' (I like Science).

When talking about school supplies, you need words like: book (bhuku), pen (peni), pencil (pensera), ruler (rula), eraser (rabha), and exercise book (bhuku rekunyora). For example, 'Ndine bhuku reMasvomhu' means 'I have a Mathematics book.' To ask for a pen, say 'Ndipe peni.' If you need to borrow a ruler, say 'Ndinokumbira rula.' In a classroom, you might hear 'Vanhu vose, torai mabhuku enyu' (Everyone, take your books).

In many African schools, supplies are shared or scarce. You might need to say 'Handina peni' (I don't have a pen) or 'Ndapota, ndipe pensera' (Please, give me a pencil). Teachers often say 'Chengetedzai mabhuku enyu' (Take care of your books). Knowing these phrases helps you participate in class and ask for what you need. Practice naming the subjects you study and the supplies you use every day. Try to use them in simple sentences like 'Ndiri kunyora nepeni' (I am writing with a pen).

When you go to a shop to buy supplies, you can say 'Ndinoda kutenga bhuku' (I want to buy a book). The shopkeeper might ask 'Unoda bhuku ripi?' (Which book do you want?). You can reply 'Bhuku reChiShona' (A Shona book). Prices are in dollars or local currency, so you might hear 'Iri madhora mashanu' (It is five dollars). Knowing these words makes shopping for school easier. Also, learn the days of the week: Muvhuro (Monday), Chipiri (Tuesday), Chitatu (Wednesday), China (Thursday), Chishanu (Friday). You can say 'Ndine chidzidzo cheSainzi neChipiri' (I have Science on Tuesday). This helps you talk about your timetable.

2. Classroom Phrases

In a Shona classroom, you will hear many common phrases. The teacher might say 'Mangwanani vana' (Good morning children). You respond 'Mangwanani mudzidzisi' (Good morning teacher). To ask for permission to enter, say 'Ndapota, ndinopinda here?' (Please, may I come in?). If you are late, you might say 'Ndine urombo nekunonoka' (I am sorry for being late). The teacher may say 'Gara pasi' (Sit down). To ask a question, raise your hand and say 'Ndine mubvunzo' (I have a question). Then ask your question. If you don't understand, say 'Handisi kunzwisisa' (I don't understand). The teacher might repeat: 'Inzwai zvakanaka' (Listen carefully).

When you need to go to the toilet, say 'Ndapota, ndinoda kuenda kuchimbuzi' (Please, I need to go to the toilet). To ask for a turn to speak, say 'Ndapota, nditaure' (Please, let me speak). If you want to answer, say 'Ndinoziva mhinduro' (I know the answer). The teacher may say 'Simudza ruoko' (Raise your hand). When working in groups, you might hear 'Shandai pamwe chete' (Work together). To ask for help, say 'Ndibatsireiwo' (Please help me). The teacher might say 'Edza zvakare' (Try again).

Other useful phrases include: 'Verenga' (Read), 'Nyora' (Write), 'Teerera' (Listen), 'Tarisa' (Look). For example, 'Verenga bhuku rako' (Read your book). 'Nyora mhinduro' (Write the answer). 'Teerera kumudzidzisi' (Listen to the teacher). If you finish early, you can say 'Ndapedza' (I have finished). The teacher may say 'Makaipa' (Good) or 'Wakaita zvakanaka' (You did well). To ask for clarification, say 'Chii zvinoreva izvi?' (What does this mean?).

In group work, you might say 'Ngatitangei' (Let's start). 'Ini ndichaita chii?' (What will I do?). 'Ndapedza chikamu changu' (I finished my part). If you disagree, say 'Handibvumirani' (I don't agree). To agree, say 'Ndinobvuma' (I agree). At the end of class, the teacher may say 'Munodzidza zvakanaka' (Study well). You respond 'Tichaedza' (We will try). When leaving, say 'Chisarai zvakanaka' (Goodbye, stay well) or 'Fambai zvakanaka' (Go well). These phrases help you communicate respectfully and effectively in class. Practice them with a friend to build confidence.

3. Talking About Jobs and Professions

In Shona, jobs and professions are called mabasa. To ask 'What work do you do?', say 'Unoita basa rei?' or 'Unoshanda kupi?' (Where do you work?). Common professions include: teacher (mudzidzisi), doctor (chiremba), nurse (mukoti), farmer (murimi), driver (mutyairi), builder (muvaki), trader (mutengesi), and engineer (injiniya). For example, 'Baba vangu murimi' (My father is a farmer). 'Amai vangu mudzidzisi' (My mother is a teacher). To say 'I am a teacher', say 'Ndiri mudzidzisi'. If you are a student, say 'Ndiri mudzidzi'.

When talking about work, you can say 'Ndinoshanda kuchikoro' (I work at a school). 'Ndinoshanda mubhizimusi' (I work in business). To ask about someone's job, say 'Basa rako nderei?' (What is your job?). The answer might be 'Ndiri mukoti' (I am a nurse). If you want to say 'I want to be a doctor', say 'Ndinoda kuva chiremba'. To ask 'What do you want to be?', say 'Unoda kuva chii?' You can answer 'Ndinoda kuva mutyairi' (I want to be a driver).

In African contexts, many people have multiple jobs. You might say 'Ndinoshanda mubhizimusi remhuri' (I work in a family business). Or 'Ndinorima mumunda' (I farm in the field). To ask about work hours, say 'Unoshanda maawa mangani?' (How many hours do you work?). You might answer 'Ndinoshanda maawa masere pazuva' (I work eight hours a day). To talk about salary, say 'Mubhadharo wangu ndewe...' (My salary is...). But be careful, this is sensitive. You can also say 'Ndinowana mari shoma' (I get little money).

When describing your job, use verbs: 'Ndinodzidzisa vana' (I teach children). 'Ndinorapa varwere' (I treat patients). 'Ndinotengesa zvinhu' (I sell things). 'Ndinovaka dzimba' (I build houses). To ask 'Do you like your job?', say 'Unoda basa rako here?' Answer 'Hongu, ndinoda basa rangu' (Yes, I like my job) or 'Kwete, handidi basa rangu' (No, I don't like my job). These phrases help you talk about work in everyday conversations. Practice by describing what you do or what you want to do.

4. Job Interview Basics

A job interview in Shona is called kubvunzurudzwa kwebasa. When you arrive, greet the interviewer: 'Mangwanani' (Good morning) or 'Masikati' (Good afternoon). Then say 'Ndine urombo nekusvika nguva' (Sorry for arriving on time) if you are early, or 'Ndine urombo nekunonoka' (Sorry for being late) if late. The interviewer may say 'Gara pasi' (Sit down). They might ask 'Unonzi ani?' (What is your name?). Answer 'Ndinonzi [Your Name]'. They may ask 'Une makore mangani?' (How old are you?). Answer 'Ndine makore makumi maviri' (I am twenty years old).

Common interview questions include: 'Unoshanda kupi parizvino?' (Where do you work now?). 'Wakamboshanda kupi?' (Where have you worked before?). 'Une ruzivo rwei?' (What experience do you have?). To answer, say 'Ndine ruzivo rwekutengesa' (I have experience in selling). 'Ndakamboshanda kuchitoro' (I worked at a shop). 'Ndinokwanisa kushanda nesimba' (I can work hard). If asked 'Unoda basa rino here?' (Do you want this job?), say 'Hongu, ndinoda basa rino' (Yes, I want this job). To ask about salary, say 'Mubhadharo ungava wakadii?' (What will the salary be?). But it's better to wait until they offer.

You may be asked 'Unokwanisa kushanda maawa akawanda here?' (Can you work many hours?). Answer 'Hongu, ndinokwanisa' (Yes, I can). Or 'Ndinoita zvandinokwanisa' (I do what I can). To talk about your strengths, say 'Ndine unyanzvi hwekutaura nevamwe' (I have skill in talking to others). 'Ndiri munhu anochengeta nguva' (I am a person who keeps time). If you don't understand a question, say 'Ndapota, dzokororai mubvunzo' (Please repeat the question). At the end, you can ask 'Ndichaziva sei kana ndabudirira?' (How will I know if I succeeded?). The interviewer might say 'Tichakufonera' (We will call you). Thank them: 'Ndatenda nebasa renyu' (Thank you for your work). Then say 'Chisarai zvakanaka' (Goodbye). Practice these phrases with a friend to prepare.

5. Workplace Vocabulary

In the workplace, you need words for tools, actions, and people. Common terms: boss (sabhuku or mukuru), colleague (shamwari yebasa), salary (mubhadharo), work (basa), office (hofisi), factory (fekitari), farm (munda), shop (chitoro). To say 'I work in an office', say 'Ndinoshanda muhofisi'. 'I work on a farm' is 'Ndinoshanda mumunda'. If you are a builder, you use tools like hammer (sando), nails (zvipikiri), and saw (saha). A farmer uses hoe (badza), plough (gejo), and seeds (mbeu).

Actions at work: to start work (kutanga basa), to finish (kupedza basa), to rest (kuzorora), to help (kubatsira), to clean (kutsvaira), to carry (kutakura), to write (kunyora), to read (kuverenga), to speak (kutaura), to listen (kuteerera). For example, 'Ndinotanga basa na7 mangwanani' (I start work at 7 am). 'Ndinopedza basa na5 masikati' (I finish work at 5 pm). 'Ndinodya masikati na12' (I eat lunch at 12). To ask for help, say 'Ndibatsireiwo' (Please help me). To offer help, say 'Ndinogona kubatsira here?' (Can I help?).

Safety and rules: 'Chengetedza' (Be careful). 'Usaite izvi' (Don't do this). 'Ita izvi' (Do this). 'Pfeka ngowani' (Wear a helmet). 'Pfeka magirazi ekuchengetedza' (Wear safety glasses). If you are injured, say 'Ndakuvara' (I am injured). Call for help: 'Batsirai! Ndakuvara!' (Help! I am injured!). To report a problem, say 'Ndine dambudziko' (I have a problem). The supervisor might say 'Zvakanaka, ndichazvitarisa' (Okay, I will look into it).

Communication with colleagues: 'Mangwanani, makadini?' (Good morning, how are you?). 'Ndiripo, wakadini?' (I am fine, how are you?). 'Tichaonana mangwana' (See you tomorrow). 'Mushande zvakanaka' (Work well). If you need to leave early, say 'Ndapota, ndinoda kuenda kumba nekukurumidza' (Please, I need to go home early). To ask about payday, say 'Mubhadharo unouya rini?' (When does salary come?). These phrases help you navigate the workplace respectfully and effectively.

6. Writing a Simple CV

A CV in Shona is called rezume or tsamba yebasa. Even a simple CV should have: your name (zita rako), address (kero), phone number (nhamba yefoni), email (email), education (dzidzo), work experience (ruzivo rwebasa), and skills (unyanzvi). Start with your personal details at the top. For example: 'Zita: Tendai Moyo. Kero: 123 Murambinda, Zimbabwe. Foni: 0771234567. Email: tendai@example.com.'

Next, write about your education. Use the phrase 'Dzidzo' as a heading. List your schools and qualifications. For example: 'Chikoro chepuraimari: Murambinda Primary, 2005-2012. Chikoro chesekondari: Murambinda Secondary, 2013-2016. Chikoro chepamusoro: Harare Polytechnic, 2017-2019, Diploma in Marketing.' If you did not finish school, write what you completed: 'Ndakapedza giredhi 7' (I finished grade 7). Then write 'Ruzivo rwebasa' (Work experience). List jobs you have done, even informal ones. For example: 'Mutengesi, Chitoro chaMai Chikafu, 2018-2020. Basa: kutengesa, kubhadhara, kuchengeta mari.' (Seller, Mrs. Chikafu's Shop, 2018-2020. Duties: selling, paying, keeping money.)

Under 'Unyanzvi' (Skills), list what you can do. For example: 'Kutaura Chirungu neChiShona' (Speaking English and Shona), 'Kushanda nekombuta' (Working with a computer), 'Kutyaira mota' (Driving a car), 'Kubika' (Cooking). If you have certificates, mention them: 'Ndine chitupa chekutyaira' (I have a driver's license). Also include 'Zvinangwa' (References) if you have people who can speak for you. Write their names and phone numbers: 'Mudzidzisi wangu: Mr. Ncube, 0779876543.'

Keep your CV to one page. Use clear handwriting or type it if possible. Use simple sentences. For example: 'Ndakashanda kuchitoro kwemakore maviri. Ndine ruzivo rwekutengesa. Ndinokwanisa kushanda nesimba.' (I worked at a shop for two years. I have experience in selling. I can work hard.) Before you submit, check for mistakes. Ask a friend who knows Shona well to read it. Then you can say 'Ndatenda' (Thank you) when you hand it in. A good CV helps you get an interview.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Education and Work in Shona

This practical reference module equips learners with the Shona language tools needed to navigate education and workplace environments in Zimbabwe and the wider Southern African region. Whether you are a farmer registering for an agricultural extension course, a trader preparing a CV for a job at a agro-processing firm, or a manager conducting interviews in Shona, this lesson provides the vocabulary, phrases, and cultural context to communicate effectively. The module is designed for business-first application: every phrase and term is chosen because it has direct utility in real economic activity.

Why Shona Matters in African Agribusiness

Shona is spoken by over 10 million people, primarily in Zimbabwe, and is one of the 16 official languages of that country. In the agricultural sector, Shona is the primary language of communication for smallholder farmers, extension officers, agro-dealers, and informal traders. Understanding Shona terminology for education and work allows agribusiness professionals to:

  • Train farm workers on safety and production protocols in their first language
  • Conduct job interviews and performance reviews with Shona-speaking staff
  • Write CVs and job applications that resonate with local employers
  • Understand classroom and training environments when attending agricultural colleges
  • Build trust with farming communities through culturally appropriate communication

Never sign employment contracts or legal documents in Shona without understanding every clause. Agricultural work contracts may include hidden deductions, unsafe working conditions, or unfair termination clauses. Always request a copy of any contract, have it translated if needed, and consult a trusted advisor or the Zimbabwe Congress of Trade Unions (ZCTU) before signing. If a potential employer pressures you to sign immediately or refuses to provide written terms, treat this as a serious red flag. Your rights are protected under Zimbabwe's Labour Act (Chapter 28:01).
