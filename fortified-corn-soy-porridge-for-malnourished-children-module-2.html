1. Where to buy quality corn and soybeans in local markets

Start by visiting at least three different markets or traders in your area. In many African countries, the best quality corn and soybeans come directly from farmers or from market vendors who specialize in grains. Look for vendors who sell in bulk, not those who repackage small quantities. Ask other food processors, millers, or nutrition centers where they buy their ingredientsthey often know the most reliable sources.

When you go to the market, inspect the grain yourself. Good corn kernels should be uniform in color, hard, and free of cracks. Avoid corn that looks dusty, has a musty smell, or shows any green or black spotsthese are signs of mold. Soybeans should be clean, round, and free of shriveled or damaged seeds. Never buy grain that is already ground into flour; you cannot check its quality. Always buy whole grains.

Bring a small clean container and a moisture meter if you have one. A simple moisture meter costs about $20$50 and can save you from buying wet grain that will mold. If you don't have a meter, squeeze a few kernels between your teethgood dry corn should be hard and shatter, not soft or chewy. For soybeans, bite oneit should be hard and dry, not rubbery. Aim for moisture content below 13% for corn and below 12% for soybeans.

Buy from farmers who have a reputation for good storage. Ask if they use hermetic bags or proper drying. Visit their storage if possible. Avoid buying from open-air piles on the ground, especially during the rainy season. If you see insects, weevils, or cobwebs, move on. A good supplier will let you take a sample home to test before you commit to a large purchase. Start with a small trial batch, test it for aflatoxin (see Lesson 2), and only then buy in bulk.

2. How to check for aflatoxin in corn and groundnuts

Aflatoxin is a poison produced by mold that grows on corn and groundnuts, especially when they are dried or stored poorly. It can cause liver cancer and stunted growth in children. You cannot see, smell, or taste aflatoxin, so you must test for it. The good news is that simple, low-cost test kits are available in many African countries.

The most common test is the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit, which costs about $1$3 per sample. You can buy these from agricultural supply stores or from companies like Romer Labs or Neogen. To use it, grind a small sample of grain (about 100 grams) in a clean coffee grinder or mortar and pestle. Mix it with the provided solvent, shake, and let it settle. Then add a few drops to the test strip or well. After 510 minutes, a color change tells you if aflatoxin is present. Compare the color to the chart provided. A safe level for children's food is below 10 parts per billion (ppb); anything above 20 ppb should be rejected.

If you cannot afford test kits, you can use a black light (UV lamp) in a dark room. Aflatoxin glows a bright greenish-yellow under UV light. Spread a thin layer of ground corn or groundnuts on a clean surface and shine the light. Pick out any glowing kernels or particles. However, this method only detects high levels and is not reliable for low-level contamination. It is better than nothing, but not a substitute for a proper test.

Always test each new batch of corn or groundnuts before you use them, especially if you buy from a new supplier. Keep a record of test results. If a batch tests positive, do not use it for children's porridge. You can try to reduce aflatoxin by soaking the grain in water with 1% lime (calcium hydroxide) for 24 hours, then rinsing and dryingbut this only reduces, not eliminates, the toxin. The safest action is to reject the batch and find a new supplier. Train your workers to recognize the signs of moldy grain and to never mix good grain with suspect grain.

3. Proper drying and storage of corn and soybeans to prevent mold

Mold grows when grain is too wet. To prevent mold, you must dry corn and soybeans to a safe moisture level: below 13% for corn and below 12% for soybeans. You can test moisture with a cheap moisture meter (available for $20$50) or by the salt method: mix a handful of grain with dry salt in a sealed jar, shake for 2 minutes, and if the salt sticks to the sides, the grain is still too wet.

Drying is best done on a clean concrete floor or on a black plastic tarp in full sun. Spread the grain in a thin layer, no more than 5 cm deep, and rake it every 30 minutes. In hot, sunny weather, corn dries in 23 days and soybeans in 12 days. Bring the grain indoors at night or cover it with a tarp to prevent dew or rain from wetting it again. Never dry grain on bare groundit picks up dirt, moisture, and mold spores.

After drying, clean the grain by winnowing or sieving to remove broken kernels, dust, and foreign matter. Broken kernels mold faster. Then store the grain in airtight containers. The best options are hermetic storage bags (like GrainPro or PICS bags), metal silos, or plastic drums with tight-fitting lids. These exclude air and stop insects and mold. If you use jute or polypropylene sacks, stack them on wooden pallets, not directly on the floor, and keep them in a dry, well-ventilated room. Never store grain in a room that has leaks or floods.

Add a desiccant like dried neem leaves or silica gel packets to absorb moisture. Check stored grain every two weeks. Look for clumping, a musty smell, or discoloration. If you find mold, remove the affected grain immediately and dry the rest again. For long-term storage, keep the room temperature below 25C and relative humidity below 65%. A simple thermometer and hygrometer cost less than $15. Proper drying and storage can keep your grain safe for 612 months.

4. Working with local farmers to get consistent supply

To run a reliable porridge business, you need a steady supply of good-quality corn and soybeans. The best way is to work directly with local smallholder farmers. Start by visiting farmers in your area during planting season. Introduce yourself and explain that you need a consistent quantity of grain every month. Ask about their farm size, what they grow, and how they store their harvest. Look for farmers who already dry and store their grain well.

Offer a fair price and a clear contract. A simple written agreement (even handwritten) stating the quantity, quality, price, and delivery date protects both of you. For example, you might agree to buy 500 kg of corn and 200 kg of soybeans every month at a set price. Pay a small deposit (1020%) at planting time to help the farmer buy seeds or fertilizer, and pay the rest on delivery. This builds trust and ensures the farmer prioritizes your order.

Provide training and support. Teach farmers how to dry grain to below 13% moisture, how to use hermetic storage bags, and how to avoid aflatoxin. You can organize a short workshop or share printed guides. If you can, buy a moisture meter and let farmers test their grain before selling. Farmers who learn these skills will produce better grain, and you will have less waste. Consider forming a farmer group of 510 farmers so you can buy in bulk and share transport costs.

Plan for the off-season. Corn and soybeans are harvested once or twice a year. To have supply all year, you need to buy enough at harvest and store it properly. Work with farmers to store a portion of their harvest for you, or rent a community warehouse. Offer a bonus for farmers who deliver clean, dry, mold-free grain. Keep records of each farmer's deliveries, quality, and payment. This helps you identify your best suppliers and plan for next season. Consistent supply means consistent production, which is key to keeping malnourished children fed.

5. Negotiating prices for bulk purchases at harvest time

Harvest time is when prices are lowest, so this is the best time to buy in bulk. But you must negotiate wisely. Before you go to the market, find out the current market price. Ask other buyers, check radio announcements, or call a few farmers. Know the price per kilogram for both corn and soybeans. In many African countries, corn prices drop by 3050% at harvest compared to the lean season. For example, if corn is $0.50/kg in the lean season, it might be $0.25$0.35/kg at harvest.

When you negotiate, buy in large quantities. Farmers and traders give discounts for bulk. For instance, buying 1,000 kg might get you a 10% discount, while buying 5,000 kg could get you 20%. Bring cash or a mobile money transfer to pay immediatelymany sellers offer a lower price for cash. Always inspect the grain before agreeing on a price. If you find mold, insects, or high moisture, ask for a lower price or reject the lot. Be polite but firm. Remember that you are not just buying grain; you are building a relationship.

Consider forming a buying group with other porridge producers or nutrition centers. Pool your money and buy a full truckload (1030 tonnes). This gives you more bargaining power and reduces transport costs per kilogram. You can also negotiate with farmers' cooperatives, which often have better quality control and can supply larger volumes. Ask for a sample before you commit. Test the sample for aflatoxin (see Lesson 2). If it passes, sign a simple contract with the price, quantity, and delivery date.

Timing is critical. The best prices are within 24 weeks after harvest, before traders start hoarding. If you wait too long, prices rise. Also, consider buying directly from farmers rather than through middlemenyou save 1020%. But if you buy from farmers, you may need to arrange transport and storage. Always keep a record of your purchases, including price, quantity, and supplier. This helps you compare prices next season and negotiate better. A good deal at harvest means affordable porridge for children all year.

6. Setting up a small storage room to keep ingredients safe

A proper storage room protects your corn and soybeans from moisture, pests, and mold. You don't need a big buildinga small room (2m x 3m) can store up to 2 tonnes of grain if organized well. Choose a room that is dry, well-ventilated, and away from kitchens or bathrooms. It should have a solid floor (concrete is best), walls without cracks, and a ceiling to keep out dust and rain. If you don't have a dedicated room, you can use a corner of a warehouse or a secure shed.

Start by cleaning the room thoroughly. Remove all old grain, dust, and cobwebs. Repair any leaks or holes. Paint the walls with whitewash (lime and water) to deter insects and make it easier to see dirt. Install shelves or pallets to keep bags off the floor. Wooden pallets are good, but plastic pallets are better because they don't absorb moisture. Stack bags at least 10 cm away from walls and 50 cm from the ceiling to allow air circulation. Never stack bags higher than 10 layers for jute or 15 layers for hermetic bags.

Control temperature and humidity. Aim for below 25C and below 65% relative humidity. Use a thermometer and hygrometer (cost: $10$20) to monitor daily. If humidity is too high, use a dehumidifier or place bowls of quicklime (calcium oxide) in corners to absorb moisture. Ventilate the room in the early morning or evening when the air is dry. Avoid opening the room during hot, humid afternoons. Install mesh screens on windows and vents to keep out insects and rodents. Set traps for rats and mice.

Store different grains separately. Keep corn in one section and soybeans in another to prevent cross-contamination and make inventory easier. Use hermetic bags or airtight drums for long-term storage. Label each bag with the date, supplier, and moisture level. Rotate stockuse the oldest grain first. Inspect the room weekly for signs of mold, insects, or water damage. Keep a broom, dustpan, and flashlight handy. A well-maintained storage room can keep your ingredients safe for 612 months, ensuring you always have quality grain for your porridge.

<h2>Practical Reference: Sourcing and Storing Corn and Soybeans Locally</h2>

<p>Reliable raw material supply is the backbone of a fortified corn-soy porridge business. A single batch of mouldy maize can wipe out a week's profit, poison your customers, and destroy the reputation you have worked to build. This Practical Reference module gives you the field-tested tools to buy well, test for aflatoxin, dry and store safely, lock in consistent supply from farmers, negotiate bulk prices at harvest, and set up a small storage room that protects both your ingredients and your margins.</p>

<h3>Where to Buy Quality Corn and Soybeans in Local Markets</h3>
<p>Buy from organised aggregation points rather than roadside heaps. In most African markets the best sources are:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Primary markets and grain bulking centres</strong> e.g., Nakawa and Kibuye in Kampala, Mile 12 in Lagos, Wakulima in Nairobi, and district-level bulking stores in Tanzania and Zambia.</li>
<li><strong>Farmer cooperatives and producer groups</strong> they aggregate, sort, and often have better post-harvest handling than individual traders.</li>
<li><strong>Registered agro-dealers and certified seed/grain stockists</strong> traceable supply, though usually higher priced.</li>
<li><strong>Commodity exchanges</strong> e.g., the Ethiopia Commodity Exchange (ECX) and the Africa Exchange Holdings network, where grade and moisture are documented.</li>
</ul>
<p>For soybeans, prefer white or cream varieties with uniform size and no split or discoloured beans. For maize, choose hard, flinty white or yellow varieties with tight husks and no visible mould, discolouration, or musty smell.</p>

<h3>How to Check for Aflatoxin in Corn and Groundnuts</h3>
<p>Aflatoxin is produced by <em>Aspergillus flavus</em> and <em>Aspergillus parasiticus</em> moulds, which thrive in hot, humid conditions and on poorly dried grain. It is invisible, odourless, and heat-stable cooking does not destroy it. Chronic exposure causes liver cancer and stunting in children.</p>

<p><strong>Field tests you can run today:</strong></p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Visual and smell check.</strong> Reject grain with green-yellow mould, black specks, or a musty, earthy odour.</li>
<li><strong>Moisture meter.</strong> Safe storage moisture: 13.5% for maize, 12% for soybeans, 8% for groundnuts.</li>
<li><strong>Rapid test kits.</strong> ELISA or lateral flow kits (e.g., from Romer Labs, Charm, or Neogen) cost US$515 per test and detect aflatoxin in 1020 minutes.</li>
<li><strong>Black light (UV 365 nm).</strong> Bright greenish-yellow fluorescence suggests <em>Aspergillus</em> presence a screening, not confirmation, tool.</li>
<li><strong>Laboratory confirmation.</strong> Use national bureaus of standards or accredited labs for high-value or export batches.</li>
</ol>

<p>Drying is the single most important mould-prevention step. Dry maize to 13.5% moisture and soybeans to 12% before storage. Use a moisture meter do not rely on the "bite test" alone.</p>

<h3>Working with Local Farmers for Consistent Supply</h3>
<p>Spot-market buying is cheap but unreliable. Contract farming gives you volume, quality, and traceability. Practical steps:</p>
<ul>
<li>Identify 1030 smallholders within 50 km of your processing site.</li>
<li>Sign simple written agreements specifying variety, moisture, aflatoxin limits, delivery date, and price formula.</li>
<li>Provide inputs on credit (certified seed, drying tarpaulins, hermetic bags) and deduct at delivery.</li>
<li>Train farmers on post-harvest handling this is the cheapest aflatoxin control you will ever buy.</li>
<li>Pay promptly. Reputation is your supply chain.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Setting Up a Small Storage Room to Keep Ingredients Safe</h3>
<p>A 3 m 4 m room can hold 58 tonnes if properly designed.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Site:</strong> raised floor, no water pooling, away from toilets and livestock.</li>
<li><strong>Structure:</strong> cement floor, plastered walls, insulated roof, sealed door with insect screen.</li>
<li><strong>Ventilation:</strong> cross-flow vents with mesh; no direct sunlight on grain.</li>
<li><strong>Palletisation:</strong> stack bags on wooden pallets 10 cm off the floor, 30 cm off walls.</li>
<li><strong>Containers:</strong> hermetic bags (e.g., GrainPro, PICS) for maize and soybeans; food-grade drums for groundnuts.</li>
<li><strong>Monitoring:</strong> thermometer/hygrometer, monthly moisture checks, first-in-first-out stock rotation.</li>
<li><strong>Pest control:</strong> no rodenticides near food; use traps and sealed containers.</li>
</ul>

<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Aflatoxin causes liver cancer and stunting in children. Never "dilute" contaminated grain with clean grain mixing spreads the toxin. Never feed rejected maize or groundnuts to children or livestock. Reject any batch with visible mould, musty smell, or a positive rapid test above 10 ppb for children's food. Wear gloves and a dust mask when handling suspect grain. Store rejected material in clearly labelled red bags and dispose of it according to national regulations do not sell it on the open market.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Buy only from traceable sources: cooperatives, bulking centres, or registered dealers.</li>
<li>Test every incoming batch for moisture (maize 13.5%, soy 12%, groundnuts 8%).</li>
<li>Run a rapid aflatoxin test on every new supplier's first three deliveries, then every 10th batch.</li>
<li>Reject any grain with visible mould, discolouration, or musty smell no exceptions.</li>
<li>Buy 7080% of annual volume within 6 weeks of harvest when prices are lowest.</li>
<li>Store on pallets in hermetic bags in a dry, ventilated, pest-proof room.</li>
<li>Rotate stock first-in-first-out and re-check moisture monthly.</li>
<li>Keep written contracts and payment records with every farmer supplier.</li>
</ol>
