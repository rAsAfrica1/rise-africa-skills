1. Manual Cutting with Tin Snips and Shears

Tin snips are your basic cutting tool for sheet metal up to about 1.2 mm thick. In Africa, you'll find three main types: straight-pattern snips for straight cuts and gentle curves, left-cut and right-cut aviation snips for tight curves, and long-handled shears for thicker gauge. Buy good quality snips with forged blades, not stamped ones they last longer and cut cleaner. Brands like Stanley, Irwin, or local equivalents from South Africa or Kenya work well.

Start by marking your cut line with a scriber or fine marker. Clamp the sheet to your workbench using G-clamps or a vice, with the cut line just off the edge. For straight cuts, hold the snips perpendicular to the sheet and open them wide enough to bite about 5-10 mm of metal. Squeeze the handles firmly and steadily don't force or twist, or you'll bend the metal and dull the blades. For curves, use left-cut snips for cutting clockwise and right-cut for counter-clockwise. Take small bites, especially on curves.

When cutting, wear gloves sheet metal edges are razor sharp. Also wear safety glasses; small slivers fly off. If the snips jam, back up slightly and try again. Never cut across a previous cut or you'll damage the blades. For thicker sheets (1.5-2 mm), use a bench shear or Beverly shear instead tin snips will struggle and ruin your hands.

Maintain your snips by oiling the pivot regularly and sharpening the blades with a fine file. Store them dry to prevent rust. In many African workshops, you'll also see 'aviation snips' with compound leverage these cut with less effort and are great for long cuts. Practice on scrap pieces first: cut straight lines, then circles, then squares. Within an hour you'll get the feel. Remember: let the tool do the work; forcing it is the fastest way to ruin both the metal and the snips.

2. Using Bench Shears and Beverly Shears

Bench shears and Beverly shears are lever-operated machines that cut sheet metal up to 3 mm thick. They are bolted to a workbench or stand. A bench shear has a straight blade and is used for straight cuts and trimming. A Beverly shear (also called a throatless shear) has a curved blade and a rotating head, allowing you to cut curves, circles, and irregular shapes. Both are common in African metal workshops because they are hand-powered, durable, and don't need electricity.

To use a bench shear: clamp it securely to a sturdy bench. The blade gap should be set to about 5% of the metal thickness for 1 mm sheet, gap = 0.05 mm. Too wide and the metal folds; too tight and the blade wears. Mark your cut line, then insert the sheet between the blades, aligning the line with the blade edge. Pull the handle down firmly and steadily. For long cuts, feed the metal through in one smooth motion. Never cut round bar or wire it will chip the blade.

For a Beverly shear, the process is similar but you can rotate the sheet to follow curves. The throat depth (distance from blade to frame) determines how tight a curve you can cut typical throat is 150-200 mm. To cut a circle, drill a starter hole, insert the blade, and rotate the sheet while operating the handle. Keep the metal flat against the bed. Beverly shears are ideal for cutting auto body panels or ductwork. In Nigeria and Ghana, you'll find locally made copies of Beverly shears that work well if the blade is hardened.

Safety: always wear gloves and eye protection. Keep fingers clear of the blade use a push stick for small pieces. Lubricate the pivot and blade with machine oil daily. Sharpen blades with a flat stone or have them professionally ground when they start leaving burrs. A well-maintained shear will last for decades. Practice on 1 mm mild steel: cut straight lines, then a 100 mm circle. You'll quickly learn the rhythm.

3. Power Shearing: Guillotine and Jump Shears

When you need to cut many sheets quickly and accurately, power shears are the answer. A guillotine shear (also called a squaring shear) has a long straight blade that comes down hydraulically or mechanically. It can cut sheets up to 3-6 mm thick and 2-3 meters long. A jump shear is a smaller, foot-operated or pneumatic shear for lighter work. These machines are found in larger African metal fabrication shops, often in industrial areas of Lagos, Nairobi, or Johannesburg.

Operating a guillotine: first set the back gauge to your desired cut width. The back gauge is a stop that ensures repeatable cuts. For example, to cut 100 strips of 50 mm width from a 1 m sheet, set the back gauge to 50 mm. Clamp the sheet against the back gauge and hold it down with the hold-down foot or hydraulic clamp. Then step on the foot pedal or press the button the blade descends and cuts. Keep hands clear of the blade! Use a squaring arm to align the sheet. For angled cuts, set the blade angle (most guillotines allow 0.5 to 2 rake). A rake angle reduces cutting force but can distort thin sheets; use 0.5 for thin sheets.

Jump shears are simpler: you feed the sheet and step on a pedal. They are good for cutting small pieces, notching, and trimming. Capacity is usually up to 2 mm. Always check the blade clearance: for 1 mm steel, clearance should be 0.05-0.1 mm. Too much clearance causes burrs and bending.

Safety is critical: never put your hands under the blade. Use a hand pad or push block. Wear safety glasses and gloves. Keep the machine guarded if the guard is missing, don't use it. In many African workshops, old guillotines lack guards; retrofit a simple acrylic guard. Maintenance: oil the blades and guides daily, check hydraulic fluid monthly. A dull blade will crack the sheet instead of cutting. Sharpen or replace blades when they leave a rough edge. With practice, you can cut 100 sheets in an hour far faster than hand shears.

4. Notching and Piercing Operations

Notching is cutting a small piece out of the edge or corner of a sheet. Piercing is punching a hole without drilling. Both are common in sheet metal work for making brackets, ducting, and electrical panels. In Africa, you'll use hand notchers, nibblers, or a punch press. For light work, a hand notcher (like a Beverly shear with a corner notch attachment) works. For production, a notching machine with a V-shaped blade is used.

To notch a corner: mark the notch size, e.g., 10 mm x 10 mm. Insert the sheet into the notcher and align the marks with the blade. Pull the handle the blade cuts out the corner. For a 90 notch, use a right-angle notcher. For round notches, use a rotary notcher or a hole saw. Always deburr the notch with a file. Notching is faster than drilling and cutting, and leaves a clean edge.

Piercing: use a punch and die set. A hand punch (like a Roper Whitney No. 5) can punch up to 3 mm steel. Insert the sheet between the punch and die, align the center mark, and squeeze the handle. For larger holes, use a knockout punch (e.g., Greenlee) that requires a wrench. For many holes, use a turret punch or a drill press with a hole saw. In African workshops, a common method is to drill a pilot hole then use a chassis punch. Always support the sheet to prevent distortion.

Safety: notching and piercing create sharp slugs and burrs. Wear gloves and safety glasses. Keep fingers away from the blade or punch. For punch presses, use a guard and never reach into the die area. Lubricate punches and dies with cutting oil. Check for wear a dull punch will crack the sheet. In many shops, you'll make your own notcher from an old file and a lever but be careful; hardened files can shatter. Better to buy a proper tool. Practice on 1 mm aluminium: notch a 10 mm corner, then punch a 6 mm hole. You'll see how these operations save time and improve accuracy.

5. Nibbling and Rotary Cutting Tools

Nibbling is a cutting process that uses a small punch and die to remove metal in small bites, like a sewing machine. It is ideal for cutting curves, internal cutouts, and complex shapes in sheet metal up to 2 mm thick. A nibbler can be hand-held (electric or pneumatic) or a stand-mounted machine. Rotary cutting tools include rotary shears and circle cutters, which use rotating blades to cut circles and straight lines.

Hand-held nibblers: these look like a drill with a cutting head. You drill a starter hole (about 10 mm), insert the nibbler, and move it along your cut line. The punch moves up and down rapidly, chipping away metal. Feed speed should be slow and steady too fast and you'll break the punch. For curves, rotate the tool as you go. In Africa, electric nibblers from Bosch or Makita are common. They cut at about 1-2 meters per minute. Always wear eye and ear protection nibbling is noisy. Keep the cut line visible; use a guide fence for straight cuts.

Rotary shears: these have two rotating blades that cut as you push the tool. They are great for long straight cuts and gentle curves. The blades are usually hardened steel. You can also get a rotary circle cutter a compass-like tool with a cutting wheel. Set the radius, clamp the center, and rotate the tool around. It cuts circles up to 300 mm diameter in 1 mm steel. For larger circles, use a trammel attachment.

Safety: rotary tools can grab gloves never wear loose gloves. Use a push stick for small pieces. Unplug the tool before changing blades. Lubricate the cutting head with a few drops of oil every hour. In many African workshops, you'll see homemade rotary shears made from old gears they work but are dangerous. Buy a proper tool. Practice on 1 mm mild steel: cut a 100 mm circle with a rotary cutter, then use a nibbler to cut an internal square. You'll find nibbling is slower than shearing but far more versatile for complex shapes.

6. Laser and Plasma Cutting Overview

Laser and plasma cutting are advanced thermal cutting processes. They use a focused beam of light (laser) or a jet of ionized gas (plasma) to melt and blow away metal. In Africa, these machines are found in larger fabrication shops, often in cities like Johannesburg, Cairo, or Lagos. They are not for beginners, but you should know what they do and when to use them.

Plasma cutting: a plasma torch blows a high-temperature plasma arc through the metal. It cuts any conductive metal steel, stainless, aluminium, copper up to 50 mm thick (depending on power). A typical hand-held plasma cutter (e.g., Hypertherm) uses compressed air and 220V power. To cut, you clamp the work lead to the metal, put on the torch tip, press the trigger, and move at a steady speed. The cut is fast and leaves a narrow kerf (cut width). For 6 mm steel, you can cut at 1-2 meters per minute. Plasma cutting produces fumes use ventilation. Wear a welding helmet with shade 5-8. In many African workshops, plasma cutters are used for cutting car panels, gates, and art pieces.

Laser cutting: a laser beam (CO2 or fiber) melts and vaporizes metal. It is extremely precise kerf as small as 0.1 mm. It cuts up to 20 mm steel (fiber laser) but is usually for thin sheets (0.5-6 mm). Laser cutters are expensive and need clean power and cooling. They are used for intricate parts, signage, and automotive panels. In Africa, laser cutting services are available in major cities; you send a DXF file and get cut parts.

Safety: both processes are dangerous. Never look at the laser beam it can blind you. Plasma produces UV radiation and hot sparks. Keep a fire extinguisher nearby. Never cut sealed containers or galvanized metal (toxic fumes). For beginners, focus on manual and power shearing first. But if you have access to a plasma cutter, practice on 3 mm scrap: set the amperage to 30A, air pressure to 5 bar, and cut a straight line. You'll see the speed and versatility.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Cutting and Shearing Techniques for African Sheet Metal Workshops

In African sheet metal enterprises from Nairobi's Kamukunji jua kali clusters to Lagos' Ladipo fabrication yards and Ghana's Suame Magazine cutting is where profitability is won or lost. Every millimetre of wasted sheet, every premature blade change, and every rework caused by a distorted edge eats directly into the thin margins of a typical job shop. This practical reference consolidates the six core lessons of this module into shop-floor decisions: which tool for which job, what settings to use, and how to keep material yield high and scrap low. It is written for the workshop owner, the foreman, and the apprentice who must deliver a finished product today, on budget, with the tools actually available.

1. Choosing the Right Cutting Method

Cutting processes fall into three economic tiers. Manual tools (tin snips, aviation shears, hand nibblers) have near-zero capital cost and are ideal for short runs, site work, and curved profiles in 0.51.2 mm mild steel or 0.7 mm galvanised sheet. Bench-mounted shears the treadle guillotine and the Beverly shear sit in the middle: modest capital outlay (often USD 150900 in local markets), high throughput on straight cuts and curves respectively, and they are the backbone of most African job shops. Power shears, guillotines, notchers, nibblers, and CNC laser/plasma tables represent the industrial tier, justified only when monthly throughput and tolerance demands exceed what manual and bench equipment can sustain.

The decision rule is simple: match the tool to the longest straight cut, the tightest radius, and the required edge quality. A 2 m straight cut in 1.5 mm sheet is uneconomic on tin snips (distortion, operator fatigue, poor edge) but trivial on a treadle guillotine. A 40 mm internal radius in 1 mm sheet is impossible on a guillotine but natural on a Beverly shear or a hand nibbler.

SAFETY WARNING Shearing and Cutting Hazards

Shearing operations cause severe lacerations, amputations, and eye injuries. In African workshops where guarding is often removed for speed or repaired locally, the risk multiplies. Never place fingers within 100 mm of the blade line. Always use a hold-down or push stick for short offcuts. Guillotine and jump shears must have functional foot guards, two-hand controls, and rear guards. Nibblers and rotary shears eject hot chips wear safety glasses and a face shield, not just glasses. Never cut galvanised or coated sheet without respiratory protection (zinc fume and coating dust). Isolate power before any blade change or clearance adjustment, and lock out the breaker. Keep the floor dry and clear of offcuts; sheet metal edges are razor-sharp even when "finished."

Reference: OSHA 29 CFR 1910.212 General Requirements for All Machines; UK HSE Sheet Metal Work: Safeguarding (PPIS5).

4. Notching, Piercing, Nibbling, and Rotary Cutting

Notching and piercing create internal features and corner reliefs that guillotines cannot produce. Corner notchers (manual or powered) remove a right-angle bite in one stroke; piercing punches create holes and slots. In African shops, a hand-operated corner notcher is often the highest-ROI tool after the guillotine, because it eliminates the slow, inaccurate practice of hand-filing corners a common source of rework on ductwork, panels, and brackets.

Nibblers and rotary shears cut by progressive punching or by continuous rotary blades. They excel at internal cutouts (e.g., fan openings, control panel windows) and long curves. Hand nibblers cost USD 80300 and run on single-phase power or even hand power; they are the practical choice where a laser or plasma table is unavailable. The trade-off is edge quality: nibbled edges carry a burr and a slight scallop, so allow 0.51 mm for finishing.

5. Laser and Plasma: When to Step Up

Laser and plasma cutting are the industrial tier. Plasma is the more accessible entry point in Africa: a 4060 A plasma cutter costs USD 6002,500, runs on single-phase or generator power, and cuts 612 mm mild steel. It is the practical choice for structural brackets, gates, and machine parts. Laser (CO or fibre) delivers superior edge quality and kerf width (0.10.3 mm) but demands stable three-phase power, clean dry air or nitrogen, and a capital outlay of USD 25,000+. For most African job shops, outsourcing laser cutting to a local service bureau and keeping plasma in-house is the profit-maximising strategy until volume justifies ownership.

6. Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Match tool to cut: longest straight cut and tightest radius determine the method not habit.
  2. Set blade clearance first: 58% of sheet thickness for mild steel; check before every material change.
  3. Support the sheet: use a back gauge or support table so the offcut does not fall and drag the edge.
  4. Never cut beyond rated thickness: overloading a shear cracks blades and voids any warranty.
  5. Allow for kerf and burr: add 0.51 mm finishing allowance on nibbled or plasma-cut edges.
  6. Inspect blades daily: a nicked blade marks every subsequent cut rotate or sharpen before it costs you a job.
  7. Guard and isolate: guards on, power off and locked out before any blade or clearance adjustment.
  8. Track yield: record sheet usage vs. finished parts weekly; a 5% yield gain is often larger than a price increase.
