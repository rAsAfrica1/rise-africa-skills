Lessons

1. Tracking Income and Expenses

To grow your machine business, you must know exactly where your money comes from and where it goes. Start by recording every sale, no matter how small. Use a simple notebook or a free app like Wave or Excel. For example, if you run a welding machine, write down each job: date, client, amount. Also record expenses: fuel, electrodes, transport, and any repairs. At the end of each day, total your income and expenses. This takes 10 minutes but gives you control.

Separate business and personal money. Open a separate bank account or mobile money wallet for the business. Deposit all income there and pay expenses from it. This prevents you from spending business cash on personal needs. In Kenya, M-Pesa is perfect for this. In Nigeria, use a dedicated bank account. Even a small tin box labeled 'Business' works if you are disciplined.

Track everything for one month to see your patterns. You might discover that you spend 30% on transport or that certain jobs are not profitable. Use a simple table: columns for date, description, money in, money out, and balance. At month end, add up all income and all expenses. The difference is your profit or loss. If you lost money, find expenses to cut. If you profited, decide how much to reinvest.

Many African entrepreneurs fail because they mix cash. They think they are making money but actually are losing it. For instance, a tailor might earn 50,000 CFA per month but spend 60,000 on fabric and personal items. Without tracking, they would not know. By tracking, they can adjust prices or reduce waste.

Finally, review your records weekly. Ask: Did I earn more than I spent? Which expense was highest? Can I buy materials cheaper? This habit builds a strong foundation for scaling. Remember, you cannot manage what you do not measure.

2. Calculating Profit per Machine

Profit per machine is not just income minus the purchase price. You must include all costs: fuel, maintenance, operator wages, and depreciation. Let's say you bought a Chinese block-making machine for 500,000 Naira. It produces 100 blocks per day, selling at 200 Naira each. Daily income = 20,000 Naira. Daily expenses: cement and sand = 8,000, fuel = 2,000, operator = 3,000, maintenance = 1,000. Total daily cost = 14,000. Daily profit = 6,000 Naira. Monthly (26 days) = 156,000 Naira.

But you also must account for the machine's wear. If the machine lasts 5 years (about 1,560 working days), its daily depreciation = 500,000 / 1,560 = 320 Naira. Subtract that from daily profit: 6,000 - 320 = 5,680 Naira. That is your true profit per day. Over a month, that's 147,680 Naira.

Calculate profit per machine for each machine you own. If you have a welding machine and a grinding machine, do separate calculations. This tells you which machine earns more per hour. For example, a sewing machine might earn 10,000 CFA per day after costs, while a popcorn machine earns 5,000 CFA. You might decide to focus on the more profitable one.

Use a simple formula: Profit = (Income - Variable Costs) - Depreciation. Variable costs are those that change with production, like materials and fuel. Fixed costs like rent are shared across machines. If you have one workshop, allocate rent by machine usage. For instance, if the block machine uses 70% of space, assign 70% of rent to it.

Track this monthly. If profit per machine drops, investigate: Are material prices up? Is the machine breaking down? Are you underpricing? Adjust accordingly. Knowing profit per machine helps you decide when to replace or add a machine. It also helps you set prices that ensure you actually make money, not just stay busy.

3. Reinvesting for Growth: Adding Your Second Machine

When your first machine is profitable, consider adding a second. But do not rush. First, ensure your first machine runs smoothly and you have saved at least 3 months of operating expenses as an emergency fund. Then, calculate the cost of the new machine, including installation, training, and initial materials. For example, a second sewing machine might cost 150,000 CFA. You need another 50,000 CFA for thread, needles, and a small table. Total 200,000 CFA.

How to fund it? Reinvest a portion of your profits. If you make 100,000 CFA profit per month, save 50,000 CFA each month. In 4 months, you have 200,000 CFA. Alternatively, use a savings group (like a tontine or chama) or a microfinance loan. But avoid high-interest loans; if the loan payment is more than 30% of your monthly profit, it's too risky.

Before buying, test demand. Can you sell the extra output? If your first machine already meets all local demand, a second machine will just sit idle. Look for new markets: a nearby town, a construction site, or a school. For instance, a tailor with one machine might serve individual clients. With two machines, she can take bulk orders from schools for uniforms.

When you add the second machine, you may need to hire help. Factor in wages. If you hire an operator for 40,000 CFA per month, your profit per machine must cover that. Recalculate: If each machine earns 80,000 CFA profit before wages, after paying one operator, total profit = 160,000 - 40,000 = 120,000 CFA. That's still more than one machine alone (80,000). So it's worth it.

Finally, keep records separate for each machine. This helps you see if the second machine is as profitable as the first. If not, find out why and fix it. Scaling is not just about buying more; it's about earning more per machine.

4. Hiring and Managing Staff

Hiring staff is a big step. Start by writing a simple job description: what the person will do, hours, and pay. For a machine operator, tasks might include: operate machine safely, maintain it daily, record output, and keep the area clean. Pay fairly but affordably. In many African countries, a machine operator earns between 30,000 and 60,000 CFA per month, depending on skill. Check local rates. You can also pay per output: for example, 50 CFA per block made. This motivates hard work.

When hiring, test skills. Ask the candidate to operate the machine for an hour. Watch if they follow safety rules. Check references from previous employers. Start with a trial period of one month. If they perform well, offer a contract. If not, let them go politely. Always have a written agreement, even if simple. It should state pay, hours, and grounds for dismissal.

Manage staff by setting clear expectations. Show them how to record daily output in a notebook. Check the records weekly. Praise good work; correct mistakes immediately. Train them on machine care: daily oiling, cleaning, and reporting strange noises. A well-trained operator reduces breakdowns. For example, a maize mill operator who greases bearings daily can double the machine's life.

Pay on time. If you promise Friday, pay Friday. This builds trust. Also, provide safety equipment: gloves, goggles, or masks. In many countries, it's legally required. Even if not, it prevents accidents and keeps your business running.

As you grow, you may need a supervisor. Promote your best operator. Pay them extra to oversee others. But keep numbers small: one supervisor for every 5 workers. Avoid hiring family unless they are qualified; it can cause problems. Treat staff with respect. They are key to your scaling success. A happy worker is a productive worker.

5. Insurance for Your Machines and Business

Insurance protects you from losing everything. In many African countries, you can insure machines against fire, theft, and damage. For example, a carpentry workshop in Ghana can insure its machines for 2% of their value per year. If machines are worth 10,000 Ghana Cedis, annual premium is 200 Cedis. That's small compared to replacing a 5,000 Cedi machine. Contact local insurers like SIC in Ghana, or NIC in Kenya. Ask for a 'business assets' policy.

Also consider business interruption insurance. If a fire stops your work for a month, this pays your lost income. Premiums vary, but it's worth it if you have loans to repay. For smaller businesses, a savings fund can act as self-insurance. Save 5% of monthly profit into a 'repair and replace' fund. After a year, you might have enough to cover minor incidents.

Health insurance for you and your staff is also important. If you get sick, the business stops. In Nigeria, NHIS offers cheap plans. In Kenya, NHIF. Enroll yourself and your employees. It costs a few thousand shillings per month but prevents huge hospital bills that could bankrupt you.

Property insurance for your workshop or shop is also wise. If you rent, your landlord may have insurance, but it may not cover your equipment. Get your own. Take photos of your machines and keep receipts. If a loss occurs, you'll need proof. Report incidents to police and insurer immediately. Keep a copy of the policy in a safe place.

Insurance may seem like an extra cost, but it's a safety net. One fire or theft can destroy years of work. Talk to other business owners about their experiences. Many will tell you they wish they had insured. Start with the most valuable machine. As you grow, expand coverage. It's a sign of a serious, scaling business.

6. Planning for Replacement and Upgrades

Every machine wears out. Plan for replacement before it breaks down. Keep a log of each machine's purchase date, cost, and expected lifespan. For example, a Chinese generator might last 5 years if serviced well. After 3 years, start saving for a new one. If a new generator costs 300,000 CFA, save 100,000 CFA per year, or about 8,500 CFA per month. Put this in a separate 'machine replacement' account.

Upgrades can also boost profit. Maybe your current machine is slow. A newer model might produce 30% more. Calculate the return on investment (ROI). If a new machine costs 500,000 CFA and adds 20,000 CFA profit per month, it pays back in 25 months. That's about 2 years. If you plan to use it for 5 years, it's a good investment. But only upgrade if demand exists. Otherwise, you'll just have debt.

When replacing, sell the old machine. Even broken machines have scrap value. A welding machine might fetch 20,000 CFA for parts. Use that to reduce the cost of the new one. Also, time your purchase. Prices may drop during promotions or when the exchange rate is favorable. For imported Chinese machines, watch currency fluctuations. Buy when your local currency is strong.

Keep maintenance records. A well-maintained machine lasts longer and fetches a better resale price. Train staff to report issues early. A small repair now prevents a major breakdown later. Set aside 2% of monthly revenue for maintenance.

Finally, think about technological upgrades. Newer Chinese machines may be more energy-efficient or have digital controls. But don't buy fancy features you don't need. Focus on reliability and parts availability. Join online forums or WhatsApp groups for machine owners. Learn from others' experiences. Planning ahead ensures you always have working machines and never miss orders due to breakdowns.

Why This Module Matters

Across sub-Saharan Africa, small-scale processing and manufacturing equipment is often the difference between subsistence activity and real income. The challenge is rarely the machine itself. It is the financial discipline around it: knowing your true costs, pricing for profit, separating business and personal money, and planning for the day the machine breaks down or demand outgrows capacity. This reference gives you the tools to do that with numbers, not guesswork.

1. Tracking Income and Expenses

The first discipline is a simple cash book. Record every inflow (customer payments, mobile money receipts) and every outflow (fuel, spare parts, transport, wages, loan instalments). Use a notebook, a spreadsheet, or a mobile app such as Wave or Kobo. The key rule: record the same day, not at month-end.

Separate business and personal money. Open a dedicated mobile money wallet or bank account for the machine business. This prevents the most common failure mode in African small enterprises: revenue disappearing into household spending before costs are covered.

3. Reinvesting for Growth: Adding Your Second Machine

Adding a second machine is a scaling decision, not an emotional one. Before buying, confirm three things: (1) demand exceeds current capacity for at least three consecutive months; (2) you have a reserve fund covering three months of operating costs; (3) you have a trained operator or can hire one.

Financing options in Africa include SACCOs, village savings and loan associations (VSLAs), mobile-based credit (M-Shwari, Tala), and equipment leasing. Compare total cost of credit, not just interest rate. A 24% annual rate over 18 months may be cheaper than a 15% rate with a 5% arrangement fee.

4. Hiring and Managing Staff

Your first hire should be an operator who can run the machine when you are sourcing, selling, or resting. Pay a base wage plus a production bonus. This aligns incentives and reduces theft and negligence. Keep a simple written contract, even if informal, stating duties, hours, pay, and notice period.

Train on safety, hygiene, and basic maintenance. In many African markets, customers choose the mill or workshop that is clean, punctual, and trustworthy. Staff behaviour is your brand.

5. Insurance for Your Machines and Business

Insurance is often overlooked but protects against shocks. Options include:

  • Equipment insurance â covers fire, theft, and accidental damage.
  • Business interruption cover â pays when the machine is down.
  • Micro-insurance products â offered by Turaco, Pula, and some SACCOs in Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, and Nigeria.
  • Personal accident cover â for you and your staff.

Even a basic policy can prevent a single breakdown from wiping out a year of savings. Ask your SACCO or mobile money provider about bundled micro-insurance.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Record every income and expense the same day in a cash book or app.
  2. Keep business and personal money in separate accounts or wallets.
  3. Calculate profit monthly per machine, including depreciation.
  4. Maintain a reserve fund covering at least three months of operating costs.
  5. Before adding a second machine, confirm demand, reserve, and operator readiness.
  6. Hire staff with written terms and a production-linked bonus.
  7. Buy at least basic equipment and personal accident insurance.
  8. Set aside a fixed monthly amount for machine replacement and upgrades.
