1. Why Brooding Determines Your Profit: The First 14 Days

Brooding is the single most important phase in broiler production. The first 14 days of a chick's life determine up to 80 percent of its final performance. If you get brooding right, you will see faster growth, lower mortality, better feed conversion, and higher profit. If you get it wrong, no amount of good feed or medication later can fully recover the losses. In Africa, where day-old chicks are expensive and feed costs keep rising, every chick that dies in the first week is money lost twice: the cost of the chick and the feed it would have converted into meat. This lesson explains why brooding matters, what the three critical factors are, and how to think about brooding as an investment rather than a cost. Brooding is the artificial provision of warmth, water, feed, light, and clean air to young chicks whose bodies cannot yet regulate temperature. A day-old chick has a body temperature of about 40 degrees Celsius but very little feather cover and a high surface area to body weight ratio. It loses heat quickly. In nature, the mother hen provides warmth and teaches the chicks to eat and drink. In commercial production, you are the mother hen. Your job is to create an environment where the chick spends almost no energy on keeping warm and almost all energy on eating, drinking, and growing. Every calorie the chick burns to stay warm is a calorie not converted into body weight. That is why temperature control is the foundation of brooding. Water is the second pillar. Chicks lose water rapidly through respiration and excretion. If they do not find water within the first few hours, they dehydrate, their organs shut down, and they die. Even mild dehydration reduces feed intake, slows growth, and weakens the immune system. In many African farms, water quality is poor, water lines are blocked, or drinkers are placed too far from the heat source. These small mistakes cost large sums. Light is the third pillar. Light controls chick activity, feeding behavior, and the development of the digestive system. Too little light and chicks sleep instead of eating. Too much light and they become restless, waste energy, and may develop leg problems. The correct light program changes with age and must be managed together with temperature and water. Think of brooding as a business system. You invest in gas, electricity, charcoal, or kerosene for heat. You invest in clean water, vitamins, and electrolytes. You invest in bulbs and electricity for light. These are not costs to minimize blindly. They are inputs that produce a return. A chick that reaches 14 days at the correct weight will likely reach market weight faster and with less feed. A chick that is stunted at 14 days will never catch up. In Africa, the brooding period is especially challenging because of unreliable electricity, high fuel costs, and hot daytime temperatures that can quickly overheat a brooder. Many smallholder farmers brood in rooms that are also used for storage or living. This creates drafts, dust, and inconsistent temperatures. The successful farmer plans brooding before the chicks arrive. They clean and disinfect the brooder house. They test the heat source for 24 hours. They place drinkers and feeders correctly. They buy a thermometer and use it. They check water quality. They prepare a brooding chart on the wall. They do not guess. The three factors of temperature, water, and light interact. If the temperature is too low, chicks huddle and do not drink. If the temperature is too high, chicks pant and move away from feed. If water is too far or too dirty, chicks do not drink enough and eat less. If light is too dim, chicks cannot find feed and water. If light is too bright, chicks crowd in corners and suffocate. The brooder manager must observe chick behavior continuously. Chicks are the best thermometer. When they are comfortable, they spread evenly across the floor, some eating, some drinking, some resting. When they are cold, they pile near the heat source and chirp loudly. When they are hot, they move away from the heat source, pant, and spread their wings. When there is a draft, they crowd on one side. When they are hungry, they peck at the floor and at each other. Learning to read chick behavior is a skill that saves money every day. For African farmers, the business case for good brooding is clear. A well-brooded flock can achieve a feed conversion ratio of 1.5 to 1.6. A poorly brooded flock may reach 1.9 or worse. On a 1000-bird farm, that difference can mean hundreds of kilograms of extra feed. At current feed prices, that is a large sum. Mortality in the first week should be below 1 percent. Many farms lose 5 to 10 percent because of cold, dehydration, or poor hygiene. Each dead chick is a direct loss. The first 14 days also set the stage for disease resistance. Chicks that are stressed by cold or hunger have weaker immune systems. They respond poorly to vaccines. They are more likely to get coccidiosis, colibacillosis, and respiratory infections. Good brooding reduces medication costs and improves survival. This module will cover temperature, water, and light in detail. Each lesson gives practical steps for African conditions, including how to brood without electricity, how to use local materials, and how to monitor performance. The goal is not perfection but consistency. Chicks need a stable environment. A farmer who checks the brooder every two hours for the first three days will outperform a farmer who checks once a day, even if the second farmer has better equipment. Brooding is a hands-on business. Your presence and attention are as important as your equipment. Before you buy chicks, ask yourself: Can I provide reliable heat for 14 days? Can I provide clean water at the right temperature? Can I provide the correct light program? If the answer is no, fix those problems first. The chicks will reward you with fast growth and high profit. The next lesson covers temperature management in detail, including how to set up a brooder, how to measure temperature, and how to adjust for day and night, weather, and chick behavior.

2. Temperature Management: The Invisible Profit Driver

Temperature is the most critical factor in brooding. A chick that is too cold burns energy to stay warm and grows slowly. A chick that is too hot pants, drinks excessively, eats less, and may die from heat stress. The goal is to keep chicks in their thermoneutral zone, the range of temperature where they do not need to spend energy on heating or cooling. For day-old broiler chicks, that zone starts at about 32 to 34 degrees Celsius at chick level. Every week, the required temperature drops by about 2 to 3 degrees Celsius until it reaches about 20 to 22 degrees Celsius by week five. In Africa, ambient temperatures can be high during the day and low at night. This means you must adjust your brooder constantly. The temperature you measure at the wall is not the temperature the chicks feel. You must measure at chick level, about 5 to 10 centimeters above the litter, in several locations. Use a minimum-maximum thermometer or a digital thermometer with a probe. If you cannot afford a thermometer, use your hand and observe chick behavior, but a thermometer is a small investment that pays for itself in the first flock. Setting up a brooder starts before chicks arrive. Choose a clean, dry, draft-free room. If you use a coal or charcoal stove, place it in a metal container to prevent fire and carbon monoxide. If you use a gas brooder, check for leaks. If you use electricity, have a backup plan because power cuts are common in many African countries. A simple backup is a charcoal stove or a kerosene lamp inside a metal drum with a lid. Another option is a hot water bottle or plastic bottles filled with hot water and wrapped in cloth, placed around the brooder ring. These are temporary but can save chicks during a power cut. Create a brooder ring using cardboard, metal sheets, or wooden boards. The ring keeps chicks near the heat source and prevents drafts. Start with a ring about 1 meter in diameter for 100 chicks. Expand the ring as chicks grow. The ring should be about 40 to 50 centimeters high. Cover the floor with clean, dry litter such as wood shavings, rice husks, or chopped straw. Never use wet or moldy litter. Litter depth should be 5 to 8 centimeters. Pre-heat the brooder for at least 24 hours before chicks arrive. This ensures the floor and walls are warm, not just the air. Chicks lose heat to cold floors. A cold floor can kill chicks even if the air is warm. Place the thermometer at chick level. Check the temperature every two hours for the first three days, then every four hours until day 14. Record the readings. Adjust the heat source by raising or lowering it, opening or closing vents, or adding or removing heat. Do not wait for chicks to show signs of distress. Anticipate changes. At night, temperatures drop. In many African regions, night temperatures can fall below 15 degrees Celsius. You may need to increase heat at night. During the day, especially in hot regions, you may need to reduce heat or increase ventilation. Watch the chicks. If they are spread evenly and are quiet, the temperature is right. If they are huddled under the heat source and chirping loudly, they are cold. If they are crowded at the edges of the ring, away from the heat, they are hot. If they are crowded on one side, there is a draft. If they are panting and lying with wings spread, they are too hot. If they are inactive and have droopy wings, they may be sick or too cold. Adjust the temperature gradually. Sudden changes cause stress. If chicks are cold, increase heat slightly and check again in 30 minutes. If they are hot, reduce heat slightly and increase ventilation. Never open all vents at once in cold weather. Use curtains or baffles to control airflow. In Africa, many farmers brood in open-sided houses. This makes temperature control difficult. You can use plastic sheeting or tarpaulin to create a temporary brooding area within the house. Seal the area to prevent drafts but allow some ventilation at the top. Carbon dioxide and ammonia must escape. Ammonia burns chick eyes and respiratory tracts. If you smell ammonia, increase ventilation. Humidity also matters. If humidity is too low, chicks dehydrate. If too high, litter becomes wet and ammonia builds up. Aim for 60 to 70 percent relative humidity in the first week. You can increase humidity by placing shallow pans of water near the heat source. You can reduce humidity by increasing ventilation and adding dry litter. In hot climates, heat stress is a major risk. Chicks cannot sweat. They pant to cool down. Panting uses energy and causes water loss. If the temperature exceeds 35 degrees Celsius, chicks may die. Provide extra drinkers, add electrolytes to water, and increase ventilation. In very hot weather, you may need to brood at night when it is cooler and reduce heat during the day. Some farmers in West Africa brood chicks in the evening so they can use the cooler night hours to establish feeding and drinking. This is a smart strategy. But you must still provide warmth at night if temperatures drop. A common mistake is to assume that because Africa is hot, chicks do not need heat. This is false. Day-old chicks need 32 to 34 degrees Celsius regardless of the outside temperature. Even in hot regions, nights can be cold. And chicks in an open house can be chilled by drafts. Always provide a heat source and monitor chick behavior. Another mistake is to use a single heat source for too many chicks. A single 100-watt bulb can heat about 30 to 50 chicks in a well-insulated brooder. A charcoal stove can heat 100 to 200 chicks depending on the size and the room. Do not overcrowd. Overcrowding causes uneven heating, poor access to feed and water, and stress. Expand the brooder ring as chicks grow. By day 14, chicks need about twice the floor space they needed on day one. Temperature management is not just about heat. It is about creating a stable, comfortable environment. This requires observation, record-keeping, and flexibility. Keep a notebook. Write down the temperature, the time, the weather, and what you did. After a few flocks, you will know exactly how your brooder behaves. You will be able to predict problems before they happen. That is the mark of a professional farmer. Remember, every degree of temperature stress costs you money. Cold chicks eat more feed but grow slower. Hot chicks eat less and may die. The right temperature is the cheapest growth promoter you can buy. Invest in a thermometer, a backup heat source, and your own attention. Your chicks will pay you back with fast, efficient growth.

3. Water and Nutrition: The Lifeline of Chicks

Water is the most important nutrient for broiler chicks. A chick can survive several days without feed but only a few hours without water. Water makes up about 70 percent of a chick's body. It carries nutrients, regulates temperature, removes waste, and supports every metabolic process. If chicks do not drink enough, they will not eat enough. Feed intake and water intake are directly linked. For every gram of feed a chick eats, it needs about 1.8 to 2 grams of water. If water is limited, growth stops. In Africa, water quality and availability are major challenges. Many farms rely on wells, boreholes, rivers, or rainwater. These sources can be contaminated with bacteria, viruses, parasites, and chemicals. Chicks are very sensitive to water quality. Dirty water causes diarrhea, dehydration, and disease. Clean water is not a luxury. It is a basic requirement for profit. Before chicks arrive, clean and disinfect all drinkers. Use a chlorine solution or a commercial disinfectant. Rinse thoroughly with clean water. Never use drinkers that have been used for other animals without disinfecting them. Place drinkers at chick level so that chicks can reach them easily. For day-old chicks, use shallow drinkers or chick founts. Do not use deep buckets that chicks can fall into and drown. Add clean, fresh water. The water should be at room temperature, about 20 to 25 degrees Celsius. Cold water chills chicks. Hot water discourages drinking. In hot weather, provide cool water. In cold weather, provide slightly warm water. You can add electrolytes and vitamins to the first water. This helps chicks recover from transport stress and start eating. Use a commercial chick starter vitamin or a simple solution of glucose and electrolytes. Do not add too much. Follow the instructions. Too many vitamins can be toxic. Too much glucose can cause diarrhea. For the first three days, add vitamins and electrolytes to the water. After that, provide plain clean water. If you use well water, test it for bacteria and minerals. High levels of iron, sulfur, or salt can reduce water intake. If the water smells bad or looks cloudy, do not use it. Boil it or treat it with chlorine. Chlorine treatment: add 2 to 3 drops of household bleach per liter of water, mix, and let it stand for 30 minutes. This kills most bacteria. But do not overdose. Too much chlorine is toxic. A better option is to use a commercial water sanitizer. Water lines and drinkers must be cleaned daily. Biofilm, a slimy layer of bacteria, builds up quickly in warm water. This biofilm can harbor diseases and block nipples. Clean drinkers with a brush and disinfectant every day. Rinse well. If you use nipple drinkers, check that they are working. Chicks may not find nipples immediately. For the first two days, use supplemental chick founts in addition to nipples. Place them near the feed and heat source. Water should be available at all times. Never let drinkers run dry. Chicks can die from dehydration in a few hours. Check drinkers at least four times a day. In hot weather, check more often. Chicks drink more when they are hot. If you cannot provide water continuously, you are not ready to brood chicks. Feed is the second part of the nutrition equation. Chicks need a high-quality starter feed with 20 to 22 percent protein. In Africa, many farmers mix their own feed using maize, soybean meal, fish meal, and additives. This can be cheaper but risky. If the mix is not balanced, chicks grow slowly and get sick. If you mix your own feed, use a proven formula and add a vitamin-mineral premix. Do not use moldy or old ingredients. Aflatoxin in maize and groundnuts is a serious problem in Africa. It causes liver damage, poor growth, and death. Buy feed from a reputable supplier or test your ingredients. Feed should be fresh and free of dust and mold. Store feed in a dry, cool place. Use it within two weeks. Place feeders at chick level. Use shallow trays or chick feeders for the first week. Do not overfill feeders. Chicks waste feed by scratching it out. Fill feeders only one-third full. Clean feeders daily. Remove wet or moldy feed. Feed intake should increase every day. A day-old chick eats about 10 to 12 grams of feed per day. By day 7, it eats about 25 to 30 grams. By day 14, about 50 to 60 grams. If feed intake is lower, something is wrong. Check temperature, water, light, and disease. Water and feed must be close together. Chicks should not have to walk more than 1 meter to find water or feed in the first week. As they grow, you can move them further apart. But always ensure easy access. In hot weather, chicks may drink more and eat less. This reduces growth. To encourage eating, provide feed during the coolest parts of the day, early morning and late evening. You can also wet the feed slightly to increase palatability, but do not let it sour. Wet feed grows mold quickly. Only wet what the chicks will eat in 30 minutes. In cold weather, chicks eat more to stay warm. This increases feed costs. Make sure the brooder is warm enough so that feed is used for growth, not heating. Water and feed are the raw materials of meat production. Every kilogram of feed and every liter of water should be converted into body weight. If water is dirty or feed is poor, conversion is inefficient. You lose money. If water and feed are clean and balanced, conversion is efficient. You make money. The best farmers in Africa are fanatical about water and feed quality. They test water. They buy fresh feed. They clean drinkers and feeders daily. They record how much feed and water the flock consumes. They compare with standard figures. If consumption drops, they investigate immediately. You should do the same. Start by providing clean water and high-quality starter feed. Monitor intake. Adjust as needed. Your chicks will reward you with fast growth and good health. Remember, water is not just a drink. It is a management tool. Use it well.

4. Lighting Programs for Broilers: From Day One to Market

Light is a powerful tool in broiler production. It controls when chicks eat, how much they eat, how active they are, and how efficiently they convert feed into meat. In Africa, where electricity is often unreliable or expensive, lighting programs must be practical and flexible. You can use natural light, artificial light, or a combination. The goal is to give chicks enough light to find feed and water, but not so much that they waste energy. For the first three days, chicks need bright light 24 hours a day. This helps them find feed and water and adapt to their new environment. Use a light intensity of about 20 to 30 lux. That is roughly equivalent to a 60-watt bulb per 20 square meters, placed about 2 meters above the chicks. If you do not have electricity, use a bright lantern or a solar lamp. Do not use a single bulb in a corner. Light should be evenly distributed. Shadows confuse chicks and reduce feeding. After day three, you can start reducing light intensity and duration. A common program is: days 1 to 3, 24 hours of light; days 4 to 7, 20 hours of light and 4 hours of darkness; days 8 to 14, 18 hours of light and 6 hours of darkness; days 15 to 21, 16 hours of light and 8 hours of darkness; days 22 to market, 14 to 16 hours of light and 8 to 10 hours of darkness. The dark period is important. It allows chicks to rest, reduces stress, and improves feed conversion. It also saves electricity. Some farmers fear that darkness will reduce feed intake and growth. But research shows that a dark period of 6 to 8 hours per day does not reduce final weight and may improve health and welfare. In hot climates, a dark period during the hottest part of the day can reduce heat stress. Chicks rest instead of panting. In cold climates, a dark period at night saves fuel because you can reduce heat slightly. However, you must ensure that chicks can find feed and water when the lights come on. The dark period should be truly dark. If there is light from outside, use curtains or black plastic. If you cannot achieve complete darkness, do not worry. Even a dim light is better than no dark period. But try to make it as dark as possible. Light intensity should be reduced gradually. Sudden changes cause stress. Start with bright light and reduce over a week. Use dimmers if you have them. If not, use fewer bulbs or move bulbs higher. You can also use different wattage bulbs. For example, use 60-watt bulbs for the first week, then switch to 40-watt, then 25-watt. In Africa, many farmers use compact fluorescent lamps (CFLs) or light-emitting diodes (LEDs). These are more efficient and last longer. A 15-watt LED can replace a 60-watt incandescent bulb. This saves money on electricity. If you have solar power, use LED lights. They use less power and are reliable. If you have no electricity, use natural light. Broilers can be raised with natural daylight if the day length is sufficient. In many African countries, day length is about 12 hours. That is not enough for maximum growth. You may need to supplement with artificial light in the morning and evening. A simple solution is to use a kerosene lantern or a solar lamp for 4 to 6 hours in the evening. This extends the feeding period. But be careful with kerosene fumes. They can harm chicks. Use a lantern with a chimney and place it outside the brooder, shining light through a window or opening. Do not put a lantern inside the brooder. Another option is to use a generator, but fuel costs are high. Only use a generator if you have no other option and if the cost is justified by the flock size. For small flocks, manual lighting with lanterns is fine. For large flocks, invest in solar or grid electricity. Light color also matters. Chicks prefer white or blue light. Red light can reduce cannibalism. If you have a problem with feather pecking, use red bulbs. But red light may reduce feed intake. Use red light only if necessary. Green light may stimulate growth. But the evidence is mixed. Stick with white light for most situations. Light should be evenly distributed. Avoid dark corners. Chicks will crowd in bright areas and suffocate. Use multiple bulbs spaced evenly. If you use a single bulb, place it in the center of the brooder. But for large brooder rings, use two or more bulbs. Check bulbs daily. Replace burnt-out bulbs immediately. Keep bulbs clean. Dust reduces light output. In hot weather, lights add heat. Use LED lights which produce less heat. In cold weather, incandescent bulbs provide both light and heat. This is a bonus. But do not rely on lights for heat. Use a proper heat source. Light and temperature interact. If the brooder is too hot, chicks will avoid the light if it is also hot. If the brooder is too cold, chicks will crowd under the light. Observe chick behavior. If they are spread evenly, the light and temperature are good. If they are crowded under the light, they are cold. If they are crowded away from the light, they are hot. Adjust accordingly. A good lighting program saves money and improves performance. It reduces electricity costs, reduces stress, and improves feed conversion. It also helps you manage heat and cold. In Africa, where resources are limited, a simple lighting program using natural light plus a few hours of artificial light can work well. The key is consistency. Chicks need a routine. They learn when to eat and when to rest. A sudden change in lighting causes stress. So plan your program and stick to it. Write it down. Post it on the wall. Check it every day. If you have a power cut, do not panic. Chicks can tolerate a few hours of darkness. But if the power cut is long, use a backup light. A solar lamp or a battery-powered lantern is a good investment. In summary, light is not just about seeing. It is about managing growth. Use bright light for the first three days. Then reduce intensity and duration gradually. Provide a dark period of 6 to 8 hours per day after the first week. Use energy-efficient bulbs. Keep lights clean and evenly distributed. Observe chicks and adjust. Your goal is to maximize feed intake during light hours and allow rest during dark hours. This will give you the best growth and the best profit.

5. Troubleshooting Brooding Problems: A Practical Guide for African Farmers

Even with the best planning, problems happen. Chicks get cold. Water lines block. Power goes out. Disease strikes. The difference between a profitable flock and a loss is how quickly you identify and fix the problem. This lesson gives you a practical troubleshooting guide for the most common brooding problems in Africa. It covers temperature, water, light, and chick behavior. It also covers record-keeping and decision-making. Use it as a reference when things go wrong. Problem 1: Chicks are huddling and chirping loudly. This means they are cold. Check the thermometer at chick level. If it is below 32 degrees Celsius in the first week, increase heat. Raise the heat source or add another heat source. Check for drafts. Seal gaps in walls, doors, and windows. Check the litter. If it is wet, replace it with dry litter. Cold, wet litter pulls heat from chicks. If you use a charcoal stove, add more charcoal. If you use a gas brooder, increase the flame. If you use electricity, check that the bulbs are working. If the power is out, use a backup heat source. Do not wait. Chicks can die from cold within hours. Problem 2: Chicks are panting and spreading away from the heat source. This means they are too hot. Check the thermometer. If it is above 35 degrees Celsius, reduce heat. Lower the heat source or turn it off. Increase ventilation. Open vents or windows slightly. Use a fan if available, but do not blow air directly on chicks. Provide cool water. Add electrolytes. In hot weather, you may need to brood at night and reduce heat during the day. If chicks are panting heavily, they are in danger. Act immediately. Problem 3: Chicks are crowded on one side of the brooder. This means there is a draft. Find the source. It could be a gap under the door, a hole in the wall, or a window that does not close. Seal it with plastic, cardboard, or cloth. Check the brooder ring. If it is not secure, chicks may push it aside. Make sure the ring is tight. Problem 4: Chicks are not eating or drinking. This can be caused by several things. Check the temperature. If chicks are cold or hot, they will not eat. Check the water. Is it clean? Is it fresh? Is it at room temperature? Check the drinkers. Are they working? Are they placed correctly? Chicks need to find water within the first few hours. If they do not, they dehydrate. Dip the beaks of a few chicks into the water to teach them. Check the feed. Is it fresh? Is it the right size? Chicks need small crumbles, not large pellets. Check the light. Is it bright enough? Chicks need light to find feed and water. If the light is too dim, they will not eat. Check for disease. If chicks are sick, they will be inactive and have droopy wings. Consult a veterinarian. Problem 5: Chicks are dying. Mortality in the first week should be below 1 percent. If it is higher, investigate immediately. Common causes include cold, dehydration, starvation, and disease. Check the temperature, water, and feed. Look at the dead chicks. Are they dehydrated? Their skin will be dry and their eyes sunken. Are they cold? They will be huddled together. Are they sick? They may have diarrhea or respiratory signs. Send samples to a laboratory if possible. In Africa, many farmers do not have access to laboratories. In that case, use your observation skills. Check the history. Did you change feed? Did you change water? Did you have a power cut? Did you introduce new birds? Keep records. Records help you identify patterns. Problem 6: Power cuts. Power cuts are common in many African countries. They can be devastating for brooding. You must have a backup plan. Options include a charcoal stove, a kerosene lantern, a solar lamp, a battery-powered fan, and a generator. The best backup depends on your budget and the size of your flock. For small flocks, a charcoal stove in a metal container is cheap and effective. For larger flocks, a generator or solar system is better. Test your backup before you need it. Keep fuel or batteries on hand. During a power cut, reduce ventilation to conserve heat. But do not seal the brooder completely. Carbon monoxide from charcoal stoves is deadly. Always allow some fresh air. Problem 7: Water quality. If water is dirty, chicks will get sick. Test your water source. If it is contaminated, treat it. Boiling is effective but expensive for large volumes. Chlorination is cheaper. Use 2 to 3 drops of household bleach per liter of water. Let it stand for 30 minutes. Do not use bleach that contains perfume or other additives. Use plain unscented bleach. Alternatively, use a commercial water sanitizer. Clean drinkers daily. Do not let algae grow in drinkers. Algae can produce toxins. Problem 8: Ammonia build-up. If you smell ammonia, the litter is wet and ventilation is poor. Ammonia burns chick eyes and lungs. It causes blindness, respiratory disease, and poor growth. Remove wet litter. Add dry litter. Increase ventilation. Do not let ammonia levels rise. If you can smell it, it is already too high. Problem 9: Chicks are pecking each other. This is called cannibalism. It can be caused by too much light, overcrowding, hunger, or boredom. Reduce light intensity. Use red light. Increase feeder and drinker space. Provide more ventilation. Add vitamins and minerals to the water. If it continues, you may need to debeak. But debeaking is stressful and should be a last resort. Prevention is better. Problem 10: Poor growth. If chicks are not growing as expected, check the basics. Are they eating and drinking enough? Is the temperature correct? Is the light correct? Is the feed quality good? Are they sick? Weigh a sample of chicks every week. Compare with standard weights. For example, a broiler chick should weigh about 180 to 200 grams at day 7 and about 450 to 500 grams at day 14. If they are below these weights, find the cause. Common causes of poor growth include cold stress, poor feed quality, disease, and overcrowding. Record-keeping is your most powerful troubleshooting tool. Keep a daily log. Write down the date, age of chicks, temperature, water consumption, feed consumption, mortality, and any observations. Also write down what you did. If you change something, note it. After a few flocks, you will see patterns. You will know that when the temperature drops below 28 degrees Celsius at night, mortality increases. You will know that when you use a certain feed, growth is slower. You will know that when you clean drinkers twice a day, disease is lower. Records turn guesswork into management. They help you make decisions based on data, not emotion. In Africa, where margins are thin, data-driven decisions are the difference between profit and loss. Finally, do not be afraid to ask for help. Talk to other farmers. Join a cooperative. Consult extension officers. Use your phone to search for information. Many African farmers have solved the same problems you face. Learn from them. But always verify advice. What works in one region may not work in another.

6. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: The Brooding Data Sheet Benchmarks, Records and Decision Rules for African Broiler Farms

Brooding is where your money is made or lost. A broiler chick costs money from the moment it hatches, and the first 14 days decide whether that money returns a profit or becomes a loss. Yet most small and medium-scale African broiler farmers run brooding on instinct: "the chicks look cold", "the house feels warm", "the feed is finished, so I buy more". Instinct does not survive a bad batch. Numbers do.

This lesson is a practical reference a bench sheet. It gives you the real figures that commercial integrators and veterinary services use, the record-keeping format that turns those figures into decisions, and the simple rules that tell you when to act before chicks die. Treat it as a laminated card you keep in the brooder house.

Why Benchmarks Matter More Than Experience

Experience tells you what happened last cycle. Benchmarks tell you what should happen this cycle, so you can see a problem while it is still cheap to fix. A chick that is 10 g below target at day 7 will not recover that gap by day 35 it will cost you the same feed and produce less meat. In a market where feed is 6575% of total production cost, that is a direct loss on every bird.

The three numbers that matter most in the first 14 days are:

  • Average body weight at day 7 and day 14 the single best predictor of final slaughter weight.
  • Mortality accumulated to day 14 should be under 2% in a well-managed house, and under 1% is achievable.
  • Feed conversion ratio (FCR) at day 14 a chick that converts poorly early rarely converts well later.

Everything else temperature, water intake, humidity, litter condition, lighting is a lever you pull to move these three numbers.

Reading the Chicks, Not the Thermometer

A thermometer tells you the air temperature. The chicks tell you whether that temperature is right. This is the fastest diagnostic you have, and it costs nothing:

  • Chicks spread evenly across the brooder, active and eating temperature is correct.
  • Chicks huddled tight under the heat source, chirping loudly too cold. Raise temperature 12C and check for draughts.
  • Chicks pressed against the walls, as far from heat as possible, panting too hot. Lower temperature and increase ventilation.
  • Chicks clustered in one corner only draught from that direction. Find and block it.

In many African brooder houses, the real problem is not the temperature setting but uneven distribution: one end of the house is 5C hotter than the other. Chicks then spend energy moving between zones instead of eating. Fix airflow and heat distribution before you touch the thermostat.

The One-Page Record Sheet

You cannot manage what you do not measure. Every brooder house should have a daily record sheet with these columns:

  1. Date and age of flock (days)
  2. Minimum and maximum temperature (read twice daily, morning and afternoon)
  3. Relative humidity (a cheap hygrometer is enough)
  4. Mortality count number removed, with a note of symptoms if any
  5. Culls removed small, weak or injured birds
  6. Feed issued (kg) and feed remaining in feeder
  7. Water consumed (litres) from a simple in-line meter or by counting refills
  8. Litter condition dry / damp / wet
  9. Observations chick distribution, panting, huddling, abnormal droppings

Weigh a sample of 50100 birds at day 7 and day 14. Do it at the same time of day, before feeding. Record the average and the range. This single habit separates profitable farms from struggling ones.

Decision Rules: When to Act

Use these rules to convert your records into action:

  • If day 7 weight is below 160 g: check brooder temperature at chick level, water line height and flow rate, and feeder space. Do not simply add more feed.
  • If water intake drops more than 20% in one day: inspect the entire water line immediately. In hot weather this is often the first sign of a blockage or a disease event.
  • If mortality exceeds 0.5% in a single day after day 3: call your veterinarian. Do not wait for the next scheduled visit.
  • If litter is wet under drinkers by day 5: adjust drinker height and pressure, and top up with dry litter. Wet litter causes hock burns, footpad lesions and respiratory disease.
  • If uniformity is below 70% at day 14: review feeder and drinker placement, stocking density, and whether all birds can reach feed within 3 metres.

SAFETY WARNING Vaccines, Disinfectants and Chemicals

Brooding involves handling live vaccines (Newcastle disease, infectious bronchitis, Gumboro), disinfectants and sometimes antibiotics. These are health and food-safety hazards if mishandled.

  • Store vaccines at 28C in a dedicated fridge. Never store vaccines with food or drinks.
  • Use the correct diluent and administer within 2 hours of reconstitution. Discard leftovers do not save them.
  • Wear gloves and eye protection when mixing disinfectants such as glutaraldehyde, formaldehyde or chlorine-based products. Work in a ventilated area.
  • Respect withdrawal periods for any medication. Selling birds before the withdrawal period ends leaves drug residues in meat and is illegal in most markets.
  • Dispose of dead birds by deep burial or incineration, away from water sources and dwellings. Never feed dead birds to other animals.
  • Wash hands and change clothes after handling sick or dead birds, especially before preparing food.

Sources: WHO Antimicrobial resistance and use in food-producing animals; FAO/OIE/WHO Good practices for biosecurity in the pig and poultry sectors (2010); MSD Veterinary Manual Poultry vaccination principles.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Temperature checked at chick level, twice daily, and matched to the age-based target in Table 1.
  • Chicks observed for distribution: spread evenly = correct; huddled = too cold; against walls = too hot.
  • Water available, clean, and within 2 metres of every chick. Flow rate checked at drinker height.
  • Feed available in shallow trays or chick feeders for the first 7 days, refreshed at least 4 times daily.
  • Daily record sheet completed: temperature, humidity, mortality, culls, feed, water, litter condition.
  • Sample weighing done at day 7 and day 14 50100 birds, same time of day.
  • Litter dry and friable; wet patches corrected within 24 hours.
  • Vaccination schedule on the wall, with fridge temperature checked daily.
