1. Delivering Products by Bicycle, Motorbike, or Pickup

When you start selling tissues and paper products, you need a reliable way to get them to your customers. The three most common delivery vehicles in African cities and towns are bicycles, motorbikes, and pickups. Each has its place. A bicycle is cheap to buy and maintain, needs no fuel, and can carry up to 30 kg if you use a rear rack and saddlebags. Use it for short distances under 5 km and small orders like 10 to 20 tissue packs. A motorbike costs more but moves faster and can carry up to 80 kg with a strong rear box. It is ideal for 515 km deliveries and medium orders. A pickup truck is for bulk orders 200 kg or more and can cover 2050 km. You can hire one when needed instead of buying.

Before you load, always check the weather. Paper products are ruined by rain. Invest in a waterproof tarpaulin or large plastic bags. For bicycles, use a covered basket or panniers. For motorbikes, a lockable top box keeps goods dry and secure. For pickups, tie down the load with ropes and cover with a tarp. Never stack heavy items on top of soft tissue rolls they will crush and become unsellable.

Plan your delivery route to avoid backtracking. If you have 10 customers, group them by area. Deliver to the furthest point first, then work your way back. This saves fuel and time. For a motorbike, a full tank of 5 litres costs about 5,0007,000 shillings (or equivalent) and can cover 150 km. Charge delivery fees that cover your costs: for a 5 km bicycle delivery, charge 1,000 shillings; for a 10 km motorbike delivery, charge 2,500 shillings; for a pickup, charge 10,000 shillings plus fuel.

Maintain your vehicle. For bicycles, check tyre pressure weekly and oil the chain monthly. For motorbikes, change engine oil every 1,500 km and check brakes. For pickups, service every 5,000 km. A breakdown means lost sales and spoiled goods. Keep a basic tool kit: spanner, tyre pump, patch kit, and spare inner tube. Always carry a phone with mobile money and a contact number for a local mechanic. With good planning and care, your delivery vehicle becomes a money-making asset, not a headache.

2. Working with Local Transporters for Bulk Orders

When you land a big order say 500 tissue packs for a hotel or 1,000 rolls for a supermarket your bicycle or motorbike won't cut it. You need a local transporter. These are individuals or small companies with pickups, lorries, or tuk-tuks who move goods for a fee. They are found at markets, transport stages, or through word of mouth. Building good relationships with them is key to growing your business.

Start by identifying 34 reliable transporters in your area. Ask other shop owners who they use. When you meet a transporter, agree on clear terms: price per trip, loading and offloading help, and time of delivery. For a 1-tonne pickup within 20 km, expect to pay 30,00050,000 shillings. For a 3-tonne lorry within 50 km, 100,000150,000 shillings. Always negotiate, but don't squeeze too hard a fair price ensures they handle your goods with care. Get a written agreement or at least a WhatsApp message confirming the deal, including date, time, and price.

Before loading, inspect the truck: is it clean, dry, and free of sharp edges? Paper products absorb moisture and smells. If the truck previously carried fish or cement, insist on a clean tarpaulin lining. Load boxes neatly, with heavier ones at the bottom. Use straps to secure the load. Count every box with the transporter present and note the number on the delivery note. Both of you sign. This prevents disputes later.

During transit, stay in touch. Call the driver halfway to confirm progress. If you are not travelling with the goods, ensure the receiver knows the expected arrival time and has your number. On arrival, the receiver should count and inspect the goods before signing. Any damage or shortage must be noted on the delivery note immediately. Take photos as evidence.

Pay the transporter promptly after delivery is confirmed. A happy transporter will prioritise your future orders, offer discounts, and even help you find backhaul loads to reduce costs. Treat them as partners, not just service providers. Over time, you can negotiate a monthly contract for regular deliveries, which saves money and guarantees availability.

3. Setting Up a Simple Distribution Route

A distribution route is simply a plan for where you will deliver and in what order. Without a route, you waste fuel, time, and energy. With a good route, you can serve more customers in less time and make more profit. Start by listing all your customers shops, hotels, schools, market stalls and mark them on a map or a hand-drawn sketch. Group them by area: for example, 'Market Road', 'Town Centre', 'Industrial Area'.

Next, decide how often you will visit each area. If you have many customers in one area, visit that area on a fixed day each week. For example, Monday: Market Road; Tuesday: Town Centre; Wednesday: Industrial Area; Thursday: repeat Market Road; Friday: new customer hunting. This schedule lets customers know when to expect you, so they can prepare their orders and cash. It also reduces your travel distance because you cover one area at a time.

For each route, plan the order of stops to avoid crossing back and forth. Start with the furthest stop, then work back towards your base. Use a notebook or a simple app like Google Maps to estimate distances. If you use a motorbike, aim for no more than 40 km per day. For a bicycle, 15 km. For a pickup, 80 km. Calculate your fuel or energy cost and set a minimum order size per stop for example, 5 packs of tissues to make the trip worthwhile.

Keep a delivery log. Each day, record: customer name, items delivered, amount collected, and any issues. This log helps you track which customers are growing, which are not paying, and which areas need more attention. After one month, review your routes. Are some stops too far for the profit? Can you combine two small stops into one? Adjust as needed.

Finally, always have a backup plan. If a road is flooded or a customer is closed, move to the next stop and come back later. Inform the customer via call or SMS. A reliable route builds trust and turns one-time buyers into regular customers. Start simple, be consistent, and refine over time.

4. Managing Cash on Delivery and Mobile Money Payments

Most small tissue and paper product sales in Africa are cash on delivery (COD) or mobile money. COD means the customer pays when you hand over the goods. Mobile money means they send money via M-Pesa, Airtel Money, MTN MoMo, or similar. Both have risks: fake notes, robbery, and failed transfers. You need simple systems to protect your money.

For COD, always carry a small cash box or a locked bag. Never flash large amounts of cash. If a customer pays with a large note, count the change carefully in front of them. Use a calculator or your phone to confirm totals. For amounts over 50,000 shillings, ask for mobile money instead. If you must accept cash, meet in a safe place a shop, not a dark street. After each delivery, record the amount received in your log and reconcile at the end of the day. Deposit cash into your bank or mobile money account the same day or next morning. Don't keep it at home.

For mobile money, always confirm the payment before releasing goods. Ask the customer to show you the confirmation SMS or the transaction ID. Call your own phone to check the balance if needed. Beware of fake SMS screenshots they are common. The safest method is to dial your mobile money balance code (e.g., *144# for M-Pesa) and verify the money is in your account. If the network is down, ask the customer to pay cash or reschedule delivery. Never accept a promise to pay later unless you know the customer well and have a signed credit agreement.

Set clear payment terms. For regular customers, you might allow payment within 7 days, but only if they have a history of paying on time. For new customers, insist on COD or mobile money upfront. Keep a record of every transaction: date, customer, items, amount, payment method, and balance (if any). Use a simple notebook or a free app like Wave or Keeper. At the end of each week, total your sales and compare with cash and mobile money received. Any shortage means you need to tighten your process.

Train anyone who delivers for you. They must follow the same rules: no goods without payment, no cash kept overnight, and always verify mobile money. A small mistake can wipe out your profit for the day. Be firm but polite. Most customers respect a business that is organised and secure.

5. Storing Finished Goods to Avoid Moisture and Pests

Tissues and paper products are like sponges they absorb moisture from the air. Damp paper grows mould, smells bad, and loses strength. Pests like rats, cockroaches, and silverfish also love to chew and nest in paper. Proper storage is not optional; it is the difference between selling at full price and throwing away stock. You don't need a fancy warehouse. A dry, clean room with good airflow works.

First, choose the right room. It should be above ground level, with no leaks, and away from kitchens, bathrooms, or washing areas. If you store in a shop, keep goods on shelves at least 30 cm off the floor and 10 cm away from walls. This allows air to circulate and prevents moisture from seeping in. Use wooden pallets or plastic crates to raise boxes. Never stack boxes directly on a damp floor or against a cold wall.

Control moisture. If your area is humid or rainy, use cheap desiccants like silica gel packets (save them from new shoe boxes or buy in bulk) or charcoal in open containers. Place them near the stock. You can also run a fan for an hour a day to keep air moving. If you have electricity, a dehumidifier is excellent but expensive. A simple hygrometer (costs about 10,000 shillings) tells you the humidity level. Keep it below 60%. If it rises, open windows on dry days or use a fan.

Prevent pests. Keep the room clean no food crumbs, no old cardboard lying around. Seal cracks and holes with cement or steel wool. Use traps, not poison, near paper goods. Cats are good for rats but may tear paper, so avoid them in the storage room. Inspect your stock weekly. Look for tiny holes, droppings, or a musty smell. If you find pests, isolate the affected boxes and treat the room with a safe insecticide (follow instructions) or call a pest control service. For small infestations, you can freeze paper products for 48 hours to kill eggs and larvae but only if you have a large freezer and the goods are sealed in plastic.

Rotate your stock. Use the 'first in, first out' (FIFO) method. Older boxes go out first. Write the date received on each box with a marker. Never store goods for more than 3 months if you can help it. Check your stock weekly for any signs of damage. A little effort in storage saves you from big losses. Remember: dry, clean, and pest-free means your tissues stay soft and sellable.

6. Handling Returns and Damaged Goods Professionally

Even with the best care, some goods will be returned or damaged. A customer might complain that tissues are torn, wet, or the wrong type. A box might fall off a truck. How you handle these situations determines whether you keep that customer and your reputation. The rule is simple: be professional, be fair, and follow a clear process.

First, listen to the customer. Let them explain the problem without interrupting. Thank them for bringing it to your attention. If the return is due to your error wrong item, damaged in transit, or expired you must make it right. Offer a replacement or a refund, whichever the customer prefers. If the damage is minor (e.g., a few crushed rolls in a pack), you might offer a discount on their next order instead. But never argue or blame the customer in public. That destroys trust.

For damaged goods, inspect them immediately. Take photos. If the damage happened during delivery, note it on the delivery note and inform your transporter. If you have insurance or a good relationship with the transporter, you may recover part of the cost. If the damage is due to poor storage, learn from it and improve. Separate damaged goods from good stock. Do not try to sell damaged tissues at full price it will backfire. You can sell them at a discount to a local market or use them for cleaning rags, but be honest about the condition.

Create a simple returns policy. Write it down and share it with regular customers. For example: 'Returns accepted within 3 days for damaged or wrong items. Goods must be in original packaging. Refund or replacement within 7 days.' This sets expectations and reduces disputes. For cash refunds, get a signed receipt from the customer. For replacements, record the exchange in your log.

Track returns. If a particular product keeps getting returned, stop buying it. If a particular customer returns items too often, have a polite conversation. Maybe they need a different product or better storage advice. Use returns as feedback to improve your business. A customer who sees you handle a problem well becomes more loyal. A customer who feels cheated tells everyone. Always choose the professional path, even when it costs you a little. In the long run, your reputation is worth more than one box of tissues.

<h2>Practical Reference: Distribution &amp; Logistics for Tissues &amp; Paper Products</h2>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the six lessons in the Distribution and Logistics module into one working document you can print, laminate, and keep at the dispatch point. It is written for small and growing tissue and paper product businesses in Africa the kind of operation running one or two pickups, a handful of motorbikes, and a network of kiosks, salons, hotels, and mini-marts. The assumption throughout is simple: <strong>distribution is a cost centre that becomes a profit centre when routes, cash, and stock handling are controlled.</strong> Paper is heavy, bulky, and vulnerable to moisture and pests. Those three facts drive every decision below.</p>

<h3>1. Choosing the Vehicle: Bicycle, Motorbike, or Pickup</h3>

<p>The right vehicle is determined by load weight, distance, road surface, and unit economics not by what is available. Tissues are low-value, high-volume goods, so the cost per delivered carton matters more than speed alone.</p>

<p><strong>Bicycle:</strong> Best for last-mile drops under 5 km in dense urban or peri-urban areas market stalls, salons, small shops. A reinforced rear rack or trailer carries 4080 kg (roughly 48 cartons of facial tissue, fewer for jumbo toilet rolls). Fuel cost is zero, which is decisive when margins are thin. Downside: slow, weather-exposed, and limited to flat terrain.</p>

<p><strong>Motorbike:</strong> The workhorse of African last-mile distribution. Carries 80150 kg with a proper box or side panniers, moves fast through traffic, and handles rough roads. Fuel and maintenance are the main costs. Ideal for delivering to hotels, restaurants, and clinics on scheduled routes.</p>

<p><strong>Pickup (bakkie):</strong> For bulk orders, wholesale drops, and inter-town routes. A 1-tonne pickup moves 200400 cartons per trip. Use it for high-volume customers and to resupply motorbike riders acting as sub-distributors. Never use a pickup for single-carton drops you will burn profit on fuel and time.</p>

<h3>2. Working with Local Transporters for Bulk Orders</h3>

<p>When an order exceeds your own fleet, contract local transporters boda boda associations, taxi van operators, or independent truck owners. The commercial rules that protect your margin:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Agree a per-trip rate, not per-hour.</strong> Per-hour billing rewards slow loading and punishes you.</li>
<li><strong>Insist on a written waybill</strong> listing cartons, SKUs, and destination. Verbal handovers are how stock disappears.</li>
<li><strong>Pay 50% on loading, 50% on confirmed delivery</strong> with a signed delivery note or photo proof.</li>
<li><strong>Build a roster of 34 transporters</strong> so one breakdown does not stall your distribution.</li>
<li><strong>Check insurance.</strong> Most informal transporters carry none; for high-value loads, use a registered transport company.</li>
</ul>

<h3>5. Storing Finished Goods to Avoid Moisture and Pests</h3>

<p>Paper is hygroscopic it absorbs moisture from the air. In humid coastal or rainy-season conditions, unprotected stock can become unsellable in under a week. Practical controls:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Raise stock off the floor</strong> on pallets or wooden slats (minimum 15 cm).</li>
<li><strong>Keep 30 cm clearance from walls</strong> to allow air circulation and prevent pest harbourage.</li>
<li><strong>Use pallets, not direct stacking,</strong> and never stack cartons more than 5 high compression damages embossed tissue.</li>
<li><strong>Control humidity:</strong> use silica gel sachets in sealed storage, or a dehumidifier in humid climates. Target 4555% relative humidity.</li>
<li><strong>Pest control:</strong> monthly inspection, sealed bins, no food waste in the store, rodent traps at entry points.</li>
<li><strong>FIFO (First In, First Out):</strong> date every carton on arrival and dispatch oldest first.</li>
</ul>

<h3>6. Handling Returns and Damaged Goods Professionally</h3>

<p>Returns are a cost, but handled well they protect your brand and your margins. The rule: <strong>separate the return decision from the delivery decision.</strong> Riders collect returns; the warehouse manager decides credit, replacement, or write-off.</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Log every return</strong> on a return note: customer, date, SKU, quantity, reason (damaged in transit, moisture, wrong item, expired).</li>
<li><strong>Photograph the goods</strong> on collection this settles disputes.</li>
<li><strong>Quarantine returns</strong> in a marked area; never mix with sellable stock.</li>
<li><strong>Classify:</strong> resellable (re-stock), repairable (re-pack), or write-off (dispose responsibly).</li>
<li><strong>Root-cause:</strong> if the same customer returns repeatedly, investigate it may be a storage problem on their side, not a defect.</li>
<li><strong>Close the loop:</strong> issue credit note or replacement within 48 hours to keep the customer.</li>
</ol>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist Daily Dispatch &amp; Return Routine</h3>
<ul>
<li> Confirm today's route sheet and customer list before loading.</li>
<li> Check load weight against vehicle limit (bicycle 80 kg, motorbike 150 kg, pickup 1,000 kg).</li>
<li> Verify stock is dry, pest-free, and FIFO-dated before loading.</li>
<li> Issue waybill with carton count, SKUs, and destination.</li>
<li> Confirm rider has MoMo till number and receipt book.</li>
<li> Collect cash/MoMo and reconcile against waybill at end of day.</li>

<li> Report any moisture, pest, or damage issue to the warehouse manager immediately.</li>
</ul>
