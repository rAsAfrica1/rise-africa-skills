1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Point of lay is when your pullets start laying eggs, usually around 18 to 20 weeks old. This is the moment you've been waiting forâyour first eggs and your first income. But it's also a critical transition. The birds are still growing while starting to lay, so their bodies are under stress. If you manage them poorly now, they may never reach peak production, and you'll lose money. Peak production for most layer breeds in Africa (like Isa Brown, Lohmann, or local improved breeds) is around 90% layâthat means 9 eggs per 10 hens per day. That peak usually comes at 28 to 32 weeks of age. Your goal in this module is to get your flock from point of lay to that peak as quickly and smoothly as possible, and then hold it there. Why does it matter? Because a hen that starts laying well and reaches peak will give you 300 to 320 eggs in her first year. A hen that is poorly managed might give you only 200. That difference of 100 eggs per hen, at say 300 shillings per egg in your local market, is 30,000 shillings per hen per year. For a flock of 100 hens, that's 3 million shillings lost. So the steps you take in the next few weeks directly determine your profit. In this module, you'll learn exactly what to do: how to feed, water, light, and handle your birds to push them to peak. You'll also learn common mistakes that keep African farmers stuck at 50% lay, and how to fix them. By the end, you'll have a practical plan to get your flock to 90% lay and keep it there for months.

2. Tools and Materials

To manage your flock from point of lay to peak, you need a few basic tools and materials. Most are cheap and available in local agro-vet shops or markets. First, you need a reliable feed supply. Layer mash or pellets with 16-18% protein and 3.5-4.5% calcium are essential. In many African countries, you can buy commercial layer feed from companies like Ugachick in Uganda, Kenchic in Kenya, or similar. If you mix your own feed, you'll need maize, soybean meal, fish meal, limestone, and a mineral premix. A simple kitchen scale or a feed scoop that holds exactly 1 kg will help you measure daily ration. Second, you need clean water. A 20-litre bucket or a plastic drinker is fine. For 100 hens, provide at least 2 drinkers and 2 feeders to avoid crowding. Third, lighting. If your hens are indoors and daylight is less than 14 hours, you need a bulb (LED or fluorescent) and a timer or a person to switch it on and off. A simple 40-watt bulb per 20 square meters is enough. Fourth, a thermometer to check coop temperatureâideally 18-24Â°C. Fifth, a record book or notebook to track daily egg count, feed intake, and any deaths. Sixth, gloves and boots for cleaning. Seventh, a disinfectant like Virkon or bleach for cleaning drinkers and feeders. Finally, a small first-aid kit with antibiotics, vitamins, and dewormers. You don't need expensive equipment. Many successful farmers use locally made feeders from old plastic containers and drinkers from cut jerrycans. The key is consistency and cleanliness. Make sure you have these items before your pullets start laying, so you're not caught unprepared. A good practice is to stock up on feed for at least two weeks, so you never run out.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps to move your flock from point of lay to peak production. Step 1: Prepare the coop. Clean and disinfect the laying house at least one week before you expect the first eggs. Provide one nest box for every 5 hens, filled with soft bedding like wood shavings or dry grass. Make sure the coop is well-ventilated but not drafty. Step 2: Adjust feed. Switch from grower feed to layer feed gradually over 7 days. Start with 25% layer feed mixed with 75% grower, then increase every two days. By day 7, they should be on 100% layer feed. Give each hen about 110-120 grams of feed per day. For 100 hens, that's 11-12 kg per day. Split into two feedings: morning and afternoon. Step 3: Provide clean water at all times. A laying hen drinks about 200-250 ml per day. In hot weather, they drink more. Never let drinkers go empty. Step 4: Manage lighting. Hens need 14-16 hours of light per day to lay well. If natural daylight is less, use artificial light. For example, if sunrise is 6:30 am and sunset is 6:30 pm, that's 12 hours. You need 2-4 more hours. Turn on lights at 5:00 am and off at 7:00 am, then on again from 6:00 pm to 8:00 pm. Use a timer to keep it consistent. Step 5: Collect eggs at least twice a dayâmorning and evening. This prevents eggs from being broken or eaten. Step 6: Monitor weight and health. Weigh a few hens weekly. They should gain about 100-150 grams per week until 30 weeks. Watch for signs of disease: pale combs, ruffled feathers, or drop in feed intake. Step 7: Record everything. Write down daily egg count, feed used, and any problems. At peak, you should see 90 eggs per 100 hens. If you're below 70%, check your feeding, lighting, and water. Step 8: Keep stress low. Avoid moving birds, loud noises, or introducing new birds. Stress can drop production by 10-20% quickly. Follow these steps consistently, and your flock should reach peak by 30 weeks.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Many farmers fail to reach peak production because of avoidable mistakes. Here are the most common ones and how to fix them. Mistake 1: Feeding too little or wrong feed. Some farmers continue with grower feed or give kitchen scraps only. This leads to low protein and calcium, so hens lay small eggs or stop laying. Fix: Use commercial layer feed with 16-18% protein and 4% calcium. If mixing your own, ensure correct ratios. Give 110-120g per hen daily. Mistake 2: Not enough water. A hen without water for even a few hours can stop laying for days. Fix: Provide at least 2 drinkers per 100 hens and check them twice daily. Clean drinkers with bleach solution once a week. Mistake 3: Poor lighting. In many African regions, daylight is 12 hours year-round. Without extra light, hens may lay at 50-60% instead of 90%. Fix: Add artificial light to reach 14-16 hours total. Use a timer to avoid forgetting. Mistake 4: Overcrowding. More than 5 hens per square meter causes stress and disease. Fix: Provide at least 0.2 square meters per hen in the coop, and 0.5 square meters in the run. Mistake 5: Dirty nest boxes. Eggs get dirty or broken, and hens may eat eggs. Fix: Clean nest boxes daily, replace bedding weekly, and collect eggs at least twice a day. Mistake 6: Sudden changes. Changing feed brand, moving the flock, or adding new birds can cause a drop in lay. Fix: Make changes gradually over a week. Mistake 7: Ignoring parasites. Worms and mites reduce egg production. Fix: Deworm every 3 months and treat mites with dust or spray. Mistake 8: Not recording. Without records, you can't tell if production is dropping. Fix: Keep a simple notebook. Write down daily egg count and feed intake. If you see a drop, act immediately. By avoiding these mistakes, you'll reach peak faster and keep it longer.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety and best practice protect both you and your hens. Start with personal safety. Always wear gloves and boots when cleaning the coop or handling sick birds. Chicken manure can carry bacteria like Salmonella. Wash your hands with soap after any contact. If you use chemicals like disinfectants or insecticides, follow the instructions and keep them away from feed and water. For the hens, biosecurity is key. Keep visitors out of the coop unless necessary. If you must enter, use footbaths with disinfectant. Never bring in new birds without a 2-week quarantine. This prevents diseases like Newcastle or avian flu from wiping out your flock. Clean drinkers and feeders daily with clean water. Once a week, scrub them with a mild bleach solution (1 tablespoon per 4 litres) and rinse well. Replace bedding in nest boxes weekly. Collect eggs promptly and store them in a cool place. Best practice for peak production includes: consistent routine. Feed, water, and light at the same times every day. Hens are creatures of habit. Any change stresses them. Keep temperature stable. If it's too hot (above 30Â°C), hens eat less and lay fewer eggs. Provide shade, ventilation, and cool water. If it's too cold, add bedding and block drafts. Handle hens gently. Rough handling can cause injuries or stress. When you need to catch a hen, do it at night when they are calm. Use a net or hold them by the body, not the wings or neck. Provide a balanced diet. Besides layer feed, you can give small amounts of greens like kale or amaranth for vitamins, and crushed oyster shells for extra calcium. But don't overdo scrapsâthey dilute the feed. Finally, keep records. This is a safety tool: if you see a drop in production or feed intake, you can catch disease early. A simple notebook with daily egg count, deaths, and any symptoms is enough. By following these practices, you'll have a healthy flock and a profitable egg business.

Count the hours of natural daylight in your area. If it's less than 14 hours, plan to add artificial light. Share your results with a fellow farmer for accountability. This hands-on practice will build your skills and confidence.

3. Step-by-Step Practical Method

  1. Prepare the layer house 7 days before transfer. Clean, disinfect, repair nest boxes, check feeders and drinkers, set up perches. Remove all old litter. African layer houses are often open-sided â check wire mesh for holes and shade netting for tears.
  2. Transfer pullets at 16â17 weeks. Move in the cool of early morning or late evening. Add vitamin/electrolyte to water for 3 days. Do not mix flocks from different sources.
  3. Start layer feed at 18 weeks or at first egg, whichever comes first. Transition over 5â7 days by mixing. Never switch abruptly â this causes a drop in intake and delays peak.
  4. Increase day length gradually. From 12 hours at 18 weeks to 16 hours by 28â30 weeks. Use a timer or a fixed schedule. Never decrease day length during lay.
  5. Weigh a sample of 20â30 birds weekly. Body weight uniformity above 80% is the single best predictor of peak. Underweight pullets must be fed separately or given extra feed.
  6. Collect eggs at least 3 times daily. In African heat, eggs left in nests crack and spoil. Morning, midday, and late afternoon collection is standard.
  7. Grade and sell daily. Sort by size and shell quality. Sell cracked eggs immediately (or use for home consumption) â never mix them into sale trays.
  8. Record everything. Use Table 2 as your template. Review weekly. Compare hen-day % against Table 1.
â  SAFETY WARNING â Heat Stress and Ammonia

In many African layer houses, afternoon temperatures exceed 32 Â°C and ammonia from wet litter can exceed 25 ppm. Both kill peak production silently. Never close ventilation to "keep the birds warm" â layers need airflow. Never allow water lines to run dry for even 2 hours during lay; a single dry afternoon can cost 3â5% of peak and take 10 days to recover. If you smell ammonia at head height, remove wet litter and increase ventilation immediately. Wear a dust mask when cleaning litter and wash hands after handling dead birds. If you use a generator for lighting or pumping, keep fuel away from the house and never refuel a running generator near birds.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

  • Mistake: Switching to layer feed too late (after 20 weeks). Fix: Start layer feed at 18 weeks or at first egg.
  • Mistake: Sudden light increase (e.g. 12 h to 16 h in one week). Fix: Add 30â45 minutes per week.
  • Mistake: Underfeeding during ramp-up. Fix: Feed ad libitum from 18 to 28 weeks; do not restrict.
  • Mistake: Dirty or insufficient nest boxes. Fix: One nest per 5 hens, clean litter daily.
  • Mistake: Selling eggs before grading. Fix: Grade daily; cracked eggs sold separately at a discount.
  • Mistake: Ignoring mortality until it is 1% per week. Fix: Post-mortem or vet visit at any sudden rise.
