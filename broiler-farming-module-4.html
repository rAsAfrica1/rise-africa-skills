Lessons

1. Nutrient Requirements of Broilers by Growth Stage

Broilers need different amounts of nutrients as they grow. In the first 10 days (starter phase), chicks need high protein (22-24%) and high energy (3000-3100 kcal/kg) for rapid organ and muscle development. A common mistake is feeding too little protein early, which stunts growth permanently. From day 11 to 24 (grower phase), protein drops to 20-21% and energy stays around 3100 kcal/kg. From day 25 to market (finisher phase), protein drops to 18-19% and energy increases to 3200 kcal/kg to promote fattening. These numbers are for standard commercial broilers like Cobb 500 or Ross 308. If you raise slower-growing local breeds, reduce protein by 1-2%. You must also provide essential amino acids: lysine, methionine, and threonine. Maize and soybean meal are the main ingredients, but they lack enough methionine, so you add synthetic methionine (1-2 kg per tonne of feed). Minerals like calcium and phosphorus are critical for bone growth: starter needs 1% calcium and 0.45% available phosphorus; finisher needs 0.9% calcium and 0.35% phosphorus. Too much calcium in starter causes kidney damage. Vitamins and trace minerals (zinc, iron, copper, manganese, selenium) are added via premix at 0.25-0.5% of the diet. Salt (sodium chloride) at 0.2-0.3% provides sodium and chloride. Always check the feed label or mix your own using a formula from a trusted extension officer. Weigh feed daily and adjust based on bird weight and feed conversion ratio (FCR). A good FCR at market is 1.6-1.8 kg feed per kg live weight. If FCR is above 2.0, your nutrient balance or feed management is off.

2. Feed Ingredients and Formulation Basics

Formulating broiler feed means mixing energy sources, protein sources, minerals, and additives in correct proportions. In Africa, the cheapest energy source is yellow maize (60-70% of the diet). Other energy sources include sorghum, millet, cassava chips, and wheat bran, but they have lower energy or anti-nutritional factors. For protein, soybean meal (44-48% protein) is best, but it is expensive. You can replace up to 50% of soybean meal with sunflower cake, groundnut cake, or cottonseed cake, but cottonseed has gossypol which is toxic to chickslimit to 5% in starter. Fishmeal (60-65% protein) is excellent but costly; use 2-5% in starter only. Animal by-products like blood meal and meat-and-bone meal are risky due to disease and variable quality. Always use a Pearson square or a computer program like WinFeed or FeedSoft to balance nutrients. A simple starter formula (100 kg): maize 55 kg, soybean meal 30 kg, fishmeal 5 kg, wheat bran 5 kg, limestone 1 kg, dicalcium phosphate 1.5 kg, salt 0.3 kg, premix 0.25 kg, methionine 0.15 kg, lysine 0.1 kg, and vegetable oil 1.7 kg. Mix thoroughly for 10 minutes in a horizontal mixer or on a clean concrete floor with a shovel. Never mix on bare groundyou lose nutrients and introduce pathogens. Test your ingredients for moisture (should be below 12%) and mould before use. Buy from reputable suppliers and keep records of batch numbers. Formulation is not guesswork: use a known formula and adjust only when you have laboratory analysis. If you cannot afford a lab, use book values but be aware of variability. For small flocks, buying ready-made commercial feed is safer and often cheaper than mixing your own.

3. Feeding Programs: Starter, Grower, Finisher

A feeding program tells you what feed to give, when, and how much. Broilers are usually fed three diets: starter (day 1-10), grower (day 11-24), and finisher (day 25 to market). Starter feed is crumbled or mashed for easy eating. Give chicks unlimited feed (ad libitum) for the first 7 days, but clean feeders twice daily to avoid mouldy leftovers. From day 8 to 10, switch gradually to grower by mixing 50:50 for two days. Grower feed is usually pellets or mash. Continue ad libitum, but monitor feed intake: a healthy broiler eats about 50-60 g/day at 3 weeks. Finisher feed is given from day 25 until sale, often with a withdrawal period of 5-7 days before slaughter if the feed contains medication. Never restrict feed unless directed by a vet; restricted feeding reduces weight gain and extends grow-out. A typical broiler eats 3.5-4.0 kg of feed to reach 2.0-2.5 kg live weight in 35-42 days. If you use local breeds, expect lower weights and longer time. Use feeders that prevent spillage: tube feeders with a lip, or tray feeders for chicks. Provide one feeder per 50 birds for chicks and one per 30 birds for older birds. Adjust feeder height so the lip is at the level of the birds' backs. Clean and disinfect feeders weekly. Water is also part of the program: provide clean water ad libitum, and add electrolytes or vitamins on hot days. Record daily feed consumption and compare with breed standards. If intake drops suddenly, check for disease, heat stress, or feed quality problems. Always keep a feeding chart on the wall of your broiler house.

4. Feed Presentation and Feeding Management

Feed presentation means the physical form of feed: mash, crumbles, or pellets. Chicks (0-10 days) do best on crumbles because they are easy to pick up and digest. Mash (finely ground) can cause pasting in the beak and waste. Pellets (for grower and finisher) reduce waste and increase intake, but they cost more to produce. If you cannot pellet, use a coarse mashnot powder. Always sift out fine powder before feeding; powder causes respiratory problems and is wasted. Feeding management includes how you distribute feed. For the first 3 days, spread feed on clean paper or tray feeders every 2-3 hours to encourage eating. After day 4, use tube feeders. Fill feeders to one-third capacity to prevent spillage. Feed at the same times daily: early morning and late afternoon are best in hot climates. Never let feeders run empty for more than 2 hours. Remove any wet or mouldy feed immediatelyit can kill your birds. Provide one feeder per 50 chicks for the first week, then one per 30 birds. Place feeders evenly so all birds can eat at once. Use a feed scoop to measuredon't guess. A simple rule: 100 broilers at 4 weeks eat about 6 kg of feed per day. Weigh a sample of birds weekly to check growth. If birds are underweight, increase feed access or check for disease. If overweight, reduce energy slightly. Keep a record of feed used per pen. Good feeding management reduces feed waste by 10-15%, which directly increases profit.

5. Water Quality and Sanitation

Water is the most important nutrient for broilers. A bird can survive days without feed but only hours without water. Broilers drink 1.5 to 2 times as much water as feed by weight. At 30C, a 2 kg broiler drinks about 300 ml per day. Water must be clean, cool (10-20C), and free of pathogens. Never give water from a stagnant pond or dirty well. Test your water source for bacteria (E. coli, Salmonella) and minerals (iron, manganese, nitrate). If you cannot test, treat all water with chlorine at 2-3 ppm (add 1 gram of bleaching powder per 1000 litres, wait 30 minutes). Use a chlorine test kit to confirm. Clean water lines and drinkers daily. For nipple drinkers, flush lines weekly with a 3% hydrogen peroxide solution or commercial line cleaner. For bell drinkers, scrub with a brush and disinfectant, then rinse well. Provide one drinker per 80-100 birds. Place drinkers at the edge of the feeder area, not under feeders. Adjust height so the water level is at the bird's beak. In hot weather, add electrolytes (sodium, potassium, chloride) and vitamin C to water to reduce heat stress. Never add antibiotics to water without veterinary advice. Sanitation also means cleaning feed spills and wet litter, which breed bacteria and flies. Keep a footbath with disinfectant at the entrance of each house. Change footbath daily. Wash hands before entering. If you see diarrhoea or dirty vents, check water quality immediately. Poor water quality reduces feed intake, causes disease, and increases mortality. A simple rule: if you would not drink it, do not give it to your birds.

6. Feed Storage and Mycotoxin Prevention

Feed storage protects nutrients and prevents mould growth. Store feed in a cool, dry, dark room with good ventilation. Ideal temperature is below 25C and humidity below 65%. Use pallets to keep bags off the floor and 30 cm away from walls. Never store feed on bare concretemoisture wicks up. Stack bags no more than 10 high to avoid crushing and heat buildup. Use the first-in, first-out rule: old feed goes out first. Buy only enough feed for 2-3 weeks because vitamins degrade over time. If you mix your own feed, use within 7 days. Mycotoxins are poisons produced by moulds, especially aflatoxin from Aspergillus flavus. They cause liver damage, poor growth, and death. To prevent mycotoxins: buy maize and other ingredients with less than 12% moisture; test with a moisture meter (cost $20-50). Reject any grain with visible mould, discolouration, or musty smell. Store grains in airtight silos or bags with moisture barrier. Add a mould inhibitor like propionic acid (0.5-1 kg per tonne) to finished feed. If you suspect mycotoxins, add a toxin binder (e.g., bentonite clay or yeast cell wall) at 1-2 kg per tonne. Never feed mouldy feedit is better to discard than to lose your whole flock. Clean storage rooms monthly: sweep, remove old bags, and spray with insecticide to control beetles and weevils. Rodents eat and contaminate feed; set traps and seal holes. Keep a log of feed purchases and storage dates. If you see clumping, caking, or white powder on feed, do not use it.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Nutrition & Feeding for Profitable Broiler Farming in Africa

Feed is the single largest cost in broiler production typically 6575% of total operating costs. In African markets where feed can account for up to 75% of production expenses and where feed conversion efficiency determines whether a batch is profitable or loss-making, the practical reference below is designed as a field-ready guide for farm managers, extension officers, and agripreneurs. It focuses on decisions that directly affect cost per kilogram of live weight, mortality, and market weight consistency.

This module assumes you are raising commercial broilers (e.g., Ross 308, Cobb 500, Arbor Acres, or locally adapted strains such as the Koekoek or Sasso in semi-intensive systems). Whether you run a 500-bird backyard unit in Uganda or a 20,000-bird environmentally controlled house in South Africa, the principles of nutrient density, feed form, water quality, and storage hygiene remain the same.

1. Nutrient Requirements by Growth Stage What Actually Matters

Broilers are not fed a single diet. Their nutrient needs shift rapidly as body composition changes. The three-phase system (starter, grower, finisher) is the minimum commercial standard; many integrated operations in South Africa and Nigeria now use four or five phases to better match amino acid supply to bird demand and reduce feed cost per kg. The critical nutrients are metabolisable energy (ME), crude protein (CP), essential amino acids (lysine, methionine, threonine), calcium, available phosphorus, and sodium.

In tropical African conditions, high ambient temperatures reduce feed intake by 1020%. The practical response is not simply to increase protein percentage, but to increase nutrient density (energy and amino acids per kg of feed) so that birds meet requirements despite eating less. This is a business decision: denser feed costs more per tonne but improves FCR and reduces days to market.

2. Feed Ingredients and Formulation Basics The African Reality

Formulation is where profit is made or lost. In most African markets, maize and soybean meal are the dominant energy and protein sources, but their prices and availability fluctuate seasonally. Successful producers use least-cost formulation software or at minimum a spreadsheet-based linear programme, and they maintain a portfolio of alternative ingredients: wheat bran, rice bran, cassava chips, sorghum, sunflower cake, cottonseed cake, fishmeal, and locally produced full-fat soy.

Key constraints: maize quality (aflatoxin risk), soybean meal protein content (4448%), and the presence of anti-nutritional factors in cottonseed and cassava. Always analyse ingredients before formulation a 2% difference in maize protein or a 5% difference in soybean meal quality can shift FCR by 0.10.2 points, which at scale is significant money.

3. Feeding Programs: Starter, Grower, Finisher

The phase-change decision should be based on body weight and feed intake, not calendar days alone. A common practical rule: switch to grower when average body weight reaches 180200 g (typically day 1012), and to finisher when birds reach 1.01.2 kg (typically day 2428). In hot climates, birds may reach these weights later; forcing an early switch to a lower-protein diet can permanently reduce final weight.

Feed restriction is rarely justified in commercial broiler production except for breeder stock. However, in the first 7 days, ensuring chicks find feed and water quickly is critical the yolk sac provides nutrition for the first 4872 hours, but early feed intake drives gut development and immune function. A good target: 100% of chicks eating within 24 hours of placement.

4. Feed Presentation and Feeding Management

Feed form matters. Crumbs or mini-pellets for starter reduce waste and improve intake. Pellets for grower and finisher improve FCR by 38% compared with mash, but they must be durable (low fines). In many African markets, pelleting capacity is limited, so mash diets are common in that case, ensure consistent particle size and avoid excessive fines.

Feeder space: 2.5 cm per bird for tube feeders (02 weeks), increasing to 5 cm by market age. Feed depth should not exceed one-third of the feeder trough. Never let feeders run empty for more than 23 hours during daylight; empty feeders trigger competitive feeding and increase mortality. Weigh feed weekly and calculate FCR per batch this is your most important business KPI.

5. Water Quality and Sanitation

Water is the most overlooked nutrient. Broilers drink 1.62.0 times their feed intake by weight. A 2 kg bird at 42 days may drink 300400 ml per day in tropical conditions. Water quality directly affects gut health, medication efficacy, and mortality.

Key parameters: pH 6.57.5, total dissolved solids <1,000 ppm, chlorine residual 23 ppm at drinker level, and no faecal coliforms. In many African farms, borehole water is safe but storage tanks and drinker lines are the contamination point. Clean and sanitise drinkers daily; flush lines weekly; use a water sanitiser (chlorine, hydrogen peroxide, or commercial blend) according to manufacturer instructions.

6. Feed Storage and Mycotoxin Prevention

Mycotoxins especially aflatoxin B1, fumonisin, and zearalenone are a major cause of poor performance, immunosuppression, and liver damage in African poultry. The risk is highest in warm, humid conditions and where maize is harvested and stored poorly.

Practical prevention: buy feed or raw materials from suppliers who test for mycotoxins; store feed on pallets in a dry, ventilated room; keep moisture below 12%; use feed within 46 weeks; and consider a broad-spectrum mycotoxin binder (e.g., hydrated sodium calcium aluminosilicate or yeast cell wall products) in high-risk periods. Never use mouldy feed the cost of a binder is far lower than the cost of a lost batch.

SAFETY WARNING AFLATOXIN:

Never feed mouldy, musty, or discoloured maize or feed to broilers. Aflatoxin B1 is a potent liver toxin and carcinogen. It causes reduced weight gain, increased mortality, and residues in meat that pose a human health risk. If you suspect contamination, stop feeding immediately, isolate the batch, and have a sample tested at a certified laboratory. Do not attempt to "dilute" contaminated feed with clean feed this only spreads the risk. Store all feed in dry, ventilated conditions and use within 46 weeks.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Weigh feed weekly and calculate FCR per batch target 1.51.7 in tropical conditions.
  • Analyse ingredients before formulation; never assume maize or soybean meal quality is consistent.
  • Match phase to body weight switch to grower at ~180200 g, finisher at ~1.01.2 kg.
  • Check water quality monthly: pH 6.57.5, chlorine 23 ppm, no faecal coliforms.
  • Clean drinkers daily and flush lines weekly; sanitise tanks monthly.
  • Store feed below 12% moisture on pallets, away from walls, in a ventilated room.
  • Use a mycotoxin binder during high-risk periods and test suspect batches.
  • Record everything feed in, eggs/meat out, mortality, and medication to make data-driven decisions.
