1. Why 33 Ingredients Beat Single-Source Manure

Welcome to Module 1 of the Organic Manure with 33 Ingredients training. This first lesson explains why blending many feedstocks produces a superior product for African soils and a stronger business for you. Most farmers in Africa have used cattle manure, poultry litter, or compost alone. These work, but they are unbalanced. Cattle manure is low in nitrogen and slow to release. Poultry litter is high in nitrogen and phosphorus but can burn crops and often carries high salt and antibiotic residues. Goat and sheep manure sits in between. Crop residues are mostly carbon. Kitchen waste is variable. None of these alone gives a crop everything it needs across a full season. A 33-ingredient blend solves that problem by combining materials that complement each other. Think of it like a football team. One striker cannot win the match. You need defenders, midfielders, and a goalkeeper. In the same way, your manure needs carbon sources, nitrogen sources, phosphorus sources, potassium sources, calcium and magnesium sources, trace mineral sources, and biological activators. When you mix them correctly, the whole becomes far more powerful than the sum of the parts. Let us look at what 33 ingredients actually means in practice. The number is not magic. It is a practical target that forces you to gather diversity. A typical blend might include cattle manure, poultry litter, goat manure, sheep manure, rabbit droppings, pig manure, horse manure, donkey manure, and camel manure where available. Then you add crop residues like maize stover, rice straw, wheat straw, sorghum stalks, millet stalks, and bean haulms. Then green matter like tithonia, comfrey, moringa leaves, neem leaves, lantana, and water hyacinth where it is safe to use. Then mineral and ash sources like wood ash, charcoal dust, bone meal, rock phosphate, gypsum, limestone, and dolomite. Then biological activators like effective microorganisms, molasses, yeast, and indigenous microorganism cultures. Then kitchen and market waste like banana peels, cassava peels, potato peels, eggshells, fish waste, and coffee husks. Not every farmer will have all 33 on day one. That is fine. The goal is to move from three or four ingredients to as many as you can reliably source. Why does diversity matter so much? First, nutrient balance. African soils are often deficient in nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, sulphur, boron, zinc, and organic matter. A single manure cannot fix all of these. A blend can. Second, carbon to nitrogen ratio. Microbes need the right C:N ratio to decompose efficiently. Too much carbon and decomposition stalls. Too much nitrogen and you lose ammonia to the air. By mixing high-carbon materials like straw with high-nitrogen materials like poultry litter, you hit the sweet spot of around 25 to 30 to 1. Third, microbial diversity. Different ingredients carry different microbes. A wide blend inoculates your soil with a broad community that can suppress diseases, cycle nutrients, and build soil structure. Fourth, buffering capacity. A diverse blend resists shocks. If one ingredient is missing or of poor quality, the others compensate. Fifth, market advantage. Buyers pay more for a product that visibly outperforms ordinary manure. You can brand it, bag it, and sell it in towns and cities. Now let us talk business. In many African countries, a 50 kg bag of ordinary cattle manure sells for the equivalent of two to five US dollars. A well-made 33-ingredient organic manure can sell for ten to twenty US dollars per 50 kg bag, and sometimes more, because farmers see results. Your cost per bag depends on how well you source free or cheap materials. If you collect crop residues after harvest, use kitchen waste from markets, and raise your own poultry or rabbits, your main costs are labour, transport, and packaging. A small operation producing 200 bags per month can generate a meaningful income. Let me give you a realistic example. A youth group in central Uganda started with cattle manure only. They sold 100 bags a month at three dollars each, earning 300 dollars. After training, they added poultry litter, maize stover, wood ash, bone meal, tithonia, and molasses. Their production cost rose slightly, but their selling price rose to twelve dollars per bag. They sold 300 bags a month because demand grew. That is 3,600 dollars per month in revenue. The difference was not more land or more animals. It was better blending. You might be thinking, is 33 ingredients too complicated? The answer is no, because you do not need to measure each one precisely. You need categories. Aim for roughly 40 percent bulky carbon materials, 30 percent nitrogen-rich manures, 15 percent green matter, 10 percent mineral and ash sources, and 5 percent activators and kitchen waste. Within each category, use whatever is available. This gives you flexibility. It also means you can start today with what you have and improve over time. Another key point is consistency. A customer who buys your manure expects the same quality every time. That means you need simple records. Write down what you put in, how much, when you turned the pile, and how it performed. After a few cycles, you will have your own proven recipe. You can then train others and even franchise your brand. Let us also address safety and legality. Some materials need care. Pig manure and poultry litter should be composted thoroughly to kill pathogens. Water hyacinth can carry heavy metals in polluted water, so source it from clean areas. Neem leaves are good but can slow decomposition if used in large amounts. Ash should be used moderately because it raises pH. Always compost for at least four to six weeks, turning regularly, until the pile heats up and then cools down. The final product should smell earthy, not foul. It should be dark, crumbly, and free of visible worms or larvae. In summary, the 33-ingredient approach is not about chasing a number. It is about building a complete, balanced, living fertilizer that African soils and crops respond to. It gives you a competitive product, a repeatable process, and a business that can grow. In the next lesson, we will look at sourcing and preparing your 33 ingredients step by step. Before you move on, write down five ingredients you can access this week for free. That is your starting point.

2. Sourcing and Preparing the 33 Ingredients

Now that you understand why diversity matters, let us get practical about sourcing and preparing your 33 ingredients. This lesson is the backbone of your production. If you get sourcing right, everything else becomes easier. We will go category by category, with Africa-specific tips on where to find materials, how to collect them, and how to prepare them for composting. First, animal manures. These are your nitrogen and phosphorus powerhouses. Cattle manure is available in most rural areas. Collect it fresh, but do not let it dry out completely in the sun because you lose nitrogen. Store it under shade or mix it into your pile within a few days. Poultry litter is excellent but high in nitrogen and salts. If you keep chickens, collect the litter with the bedding. If you buy from a commercial farm, ask how long it has been sitting. Fresh litter should be composted for at least six weeks. Goat and sheep manure are often available in dry areas. They are rich in nutrients and easy to handle because they come in pellets. Rabbit droppings are a hidden treasure. They can be applied almost directly, but for a 33-ingredient blend, add them to the pile. Pig manure is high in nitrogen but must be composted thoroughly because of parasites. Horse and donkey manure are high in carbon because of the bedding. They are good for structure. Camel manure is available in the Sahel and is similar to cattle manure but drier. Second, crop residues. These are your carbon sources. Maize stover, rice straw, wheat straw, sorghum stalks, and millet stalks are abundant after harvest. The problem is that farmers often burn them. Instead, collect them. Chop them into pieces of 5 to 10 centimetres. Smaller pieces decompose faster. Bean haulms, groundnut haulms, and cowpea haulms are better than cereal straw because they have more nitrogen. If you can, mix both. Do not use diseased crop residues. If you see mould or signs of disease, leave them out or compost them separately at high temperature. Third, green matter. This is your biological activator and nutrient booster. Tithonia, also called Mexican sunflower, grows along roadsides and in fallow land. It is rich in nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium. Comfrey is a deep-rooted plant that mines minerals from the subsoil. Moringa leaves are high in nitrogen and make excellent compost. Neem leaves are good but use them at no more than 10 percent of the pile because they contain compounds that can slow microbial activity. Lantana is invasive in many areas, so using it for compost is a good way to control it. Water hyacinth is abundant in lakes and rivers, but only use it if the water is not heavily polluted with industrial waste or sewage. Fourth, mineral and ash sources. These provide calcium, magnesium, phosphorus, potassium, and trace elements. Wood ash is rich in potassium and calcium. Use it from clean wood, not from treated timber or plastics. Charcoal dust is a good source of carbon and helps hold nutrients. Bone meal is made by burning and crushing animal bones. It is rich in phosphorus and calcium. Rock phosphate is mined and sold in some countries. It is slow-release, so grind it fine. Gypsum provides calcium and sulphur. Limestone and dolomite provide calcium and magnesium. These materials are often available at agro-dealer shops or from construction sites. Fifth, biological activators. These speed up decomposition and improve the final product. Effective microorganisms, or EM, can be bought or made at home. Molasses is a food source for microbes. Yeast is another activator. Indigenous microorganism cultures can be made by burying cooked rice in a forest or under a tree for a few days, then mixing it with sugar or molasses. These are cheap and effective. Sixth, kitchen and market waste. Banana peels, cassava peels, potato peels, eggshells, fish waste, and coffee husks are all valuable. Collect them from your own kitchen, from neighbours, or from markets. Fish waste should be buried deep in the pile to avoid smells and flies. Coffee husks are acidic, so use them moderately or add ash to balance. Now let us talk about preparation. You do not need expensive machinery. A machete, a shovel, a watering can, and a tarpaulin are enough to start. Here is a simple workflow. Step one, collect and sort. Keep carbon materials, nitrogen materials, green matter, minerals, and activators separate. Step two, chop and crush. Smaller particles decompose faster. Chop stalks, crush bones, and grind rock phosphate. Step three, mix. Lay down a layer of carbon materials, then a layer of nitrogen materials, then green matter, then a thin layer of minerals, then sprinkle activators. Repeat until the pile is one to one and a half metres high. Step four, water. The pile should be as moist as a squeezed sponge. Too dry and decomposition stops. Too wet and it becomes anaerobic and smelly. Step five, cover. Use a tarpaulin, banana leaves, or grass to keep moisture in and rain out. Step six, turn. Turn the pile every week for the first three weeks, then every two weeks. Turning adds oxygen and mixes materials. Step seven, monitor temperature. A good pile heats up to 55 to 65 degrees Celsius in the first week. If it does not heat up, it may be too dry, too wet, or lacking nitrogen. Add poultry litter or green matter and water. Step eight, cure. After four to six weeks, the pile will cool down. Let it cure for another two weeks. The final product should be dark, crumbly, and smell like forest soil. Let us talk about sourcing at scale. If you want to produce hundreds of bags per month, you need reliable supply chains. Build relationships with poultry farms, cattle markets, and crop farmers. Offer to clean their barns or collect their residues for free. In some cases, you may need to pay a small fee, but it is still cheaper than buying finished compost. Work with market vendors to collect vegetable waste. Work with schools and restaurants to collect food waste. Use a pickup truck or a motorcycle trailer to transport materials. If you are in a city, look for sawdust from timber yards, coffee husks from coffee processing plants, and rice husks from rice mills. These are often free or very cheap. Quality control is essential. Do not use materials that are contaminated with heavy metals, pesticides, or plastics. Do not use human waste unless you are trained in safe handling and the product is properly composted. Do not use diseased animal carcasses. Keep records of where each material came from and when you collected it. This helps you trace problems if a batch fails. Finally, think about storage. Once your manure is ready, store it in a dry, shaded place. Bag it in 50 kg bags or smaller if you are selling to urban gardeners. Keep bags off the ground on pallets or stones. Do not stack them too high because the weight can compact the product. Label your bags with the date, the batch number, and the ingredients. This builds trust with customers. In the next lesson, we will dive into the composting process itself, including how to manage heat, moisture, and aeration for a perfect finish. For now, make a list of every ingredient you can access within 20 kilometres of your farm or workshop. You will be surprised how many you already have.

3. The Composting Process: Heat, Moisture, and Aeration

In this lesson, we focus on the engine room of your production: the composting process. You can have all 33 ingredients, but if you do not manage heat, moisture, and aeration, you will end up with a poor product. Composting is a biological process. Billions of bacteria, fungi, and other microorganisms break down organic matter into humus. Your job is to create the conditions where they thrive. Let us start with the pile. Build your pile on a flat, well-drained surface. If possible, lay down a layer of coarse material like maize stalks or twigs to allow air to enter from the bottom. The ideal pile size is one to one and a half metres high and one and a half to two metres wide. If the pile is too small, it will not heat up. If it is too large, air cannot reach the centre and it becomes anaerobic. In hot African climates, you may need to make the pile slightly larger to hold moisture, or slightly smaller to avoid overheating. Now, heat. Within two to three days of building a good pile, the temperature should rise to 55 to 65 degrees Celsius. You can measure this with a compost thermometer or a long metal rod. If you do not have a thermometer, push a metal rod into the centre, leave it for ten minutes, and pull it out. If it is too hot to hold, the pile is active. This heat is important because it kills weed seeds, pathogens, and parasite eggs. It also speeds up decomposition. If the pile does not heat up, something is wrong. The most common reasons are too dry, too wet, too little nitrogen, or too small a pile. Fix the problem by adding water, turning the pile, adding poultry litter or green matter, or adding more material. If the pile gets too hot, above 70 degrees Celsius, it can kill the beneficial microbes and lose nitrogen. Turn the pile to cool it down. In very hot weather, you may need to turn more frequently or add water. Next, moisture. The pile should be as moist as a squeezed sponge. If you squeeze a handful of material, a few drops of water should appear, but it should not drip. If it is too dry, microbes slow down and the pile will not heat up. Add water gradually while turning. If it is too wet, the spaces between particles fill with water, air cannot enter, and the pile becomes anaerobic. Anaerobic piles smell like rotten eggs or ammonia. To fix a wet pile, turn it and add dry carbon materials like straw, sawdust, or rice husks. In the rainy season, cover the pile with a tarpaulin or banana leaves to prevent it from getting waterlogged. In the dry season, water the pile every few days, especially when you turn it. Aeration is the third pillar. Microbes need oxygen. Without it, decomposition slows and bad smells develop. Turning the pile is the main way to add oxygen. Turn every week for the first three weeks, then every two weeks until the pile is finished. When you turn, move the outside of the pile to the inside and the inside to the outside. This ensures that all materials are exposed to the heat and microbes. If you have a large operation, you can use a pitchfork or a small tractor with a front-end loader. But even with hand tools, you can manage a pile of one to two cubic metres. Another way to improve aeration is to include coarse materials like maize stalks, twigs, or rice husks in the mix. These create air pockets. Do not compact the pile by walking on it or piling heavy materials on top. Now let us talk about the stages of composting. Stage one is the mesophilic stage, which lasts one to three days. Moderate-temperature microbes start breaking down sugars and starches. Stage two is the thermophilic stage, which lasts one to three weeks. High-temperature microbes take over and break down proteins, fats, and cellulose. This is when the pile heats up. Stage three is the cooling stage, which lasts one to two weeks. The pile cools down as the easy food is used up. Stage four is the maturation or curing stage, which lasts two to four weeks. Fungi and actinomycetes break down tough materials like lignin and cellulose. The final product is stable humus. During the process, you will notice the pile shrink by 30 to 50 percent. This is normal. You will also notice a change in smell. A young pile smells like ammonia or vinegar. A finished pile smells like earth. You will notice a change in colour. A young pile is a mix of browns and greens. A finished pile is dark brown to black. You will notice a change in texture. A young pile is rough and recognizable. A finished pile is crumbly and the original materials are hard to identify. Let us talk about troubleshooting. If the pile smells like ammonia, it has too much nitrogen. Add carbon materials like straw or sawdust and turn. If it smells like rotten eggs, it is too wet and anaerobic. Turn and add dry materials. If it smells like vinegar, it is too acidic and may be anaerobic. Turn and add ash or lime. If the pile is not heating up, check moisture, add nitrogen, and turn. If the pile is attracting flies, bury food waste deeper and cover the pile. If the pile is attracting rodents, remove meat and dairy from the mix or bury them deep. If the pile is too dry, add water while turning. If the pile is too wet, add dry materials and turn. Now let us talk about time. In warm African climates, a well-managed pile can be ready in four to six weeks. In cooler highland areas, it may take eight to ten weeks. Do not rush. Immature compost can damage crops because it continues to decompose in the soil and competes with plants for nitrogen. It can also carry pathogens. Test for maturity by planting a few bean or maize seeds in a small pot of the compost. If they grow well, the compost is ready. If they turn yellow or die, it needs more time. Another test is to put a handful of compost in a jar of water. If it floats, it is immature. If it sinks, it is mature. You can also smell it. If it smells like earth, it is ready. If it smells like manure or ammonia, it is not. Let us talk about scaling up. If you want to produce 500 bags per month, you need a systematic approach. Build multiple piles in a rotation. Start a new pile every week. Turn and monitor each pile according to its age. Use a simple record book to track the date, ingredients, temperature, moisture, and turning schedule. Train your workers on the same system. Use colour-coded tags or signs to identify each pile. This way, you always have mature compost ready for bagging. Finally, think about safety. Wear gloves and boots when handling manure and compost. Wash your hands after working. If you have open cuts, cover them. Avoid breathing dust from dry compost. If you are immunocompromised, be extra careful. In the next lesson, we will look at quality control, testing, and packaging. But before you move on, go outside and check your pile. Is it heating? Is it moist? Does it need turning? Your pile is a living thing. Treat it well, and it will reward you.

4. Quality Control, Testing, and Packaging

You have sourced your ingredients and managed your compost pile. Now you need to make sure your final product is consistent, safe, and attractive to customers. This lesson covers quality control, simple testing methods, and packaging. Quality control starts before you build the pile. If you use contaminated materials, you will get contaminated compost. So, inspect every batch of ingredients. Look for plastics, glass, metal, and stones. Remove them. Look for signs of disease in crop residues. Do not use them. Look for chemical contamination. If a field was recently sprayed with herbicides, do not use the crop residues. Herbicides can persist in compost and damage crops. If you are buying manure from a commercial farm, ask about antibiotics and other chemicals. Poultry litter often contains antibiotic residues. Proper composting breaks down most of these, but not all. If you are certified organic, you need to follow your certifier rules. Now let us talk about testing. You do not need a laboratory to do basic quality control. You can use your senses and simple tests. First, smell. Good compost smells like earth or forest floor. It should not smell like manure, ammonia, vinegar, or rotten eggs. Second, feel. Good compost is crumbly and moist, like a wrung-out sponge. It should not be soggy or dusty. Third, look. Good compost is dark brown to black. The original materials should be mostly unrecognizable. There should be no visible food waste, no worms, and no larvae. Fourth, the bag test. Put a handful of compost in a clear plastic bag. Seal it and leave it in a warm place for three days. Open it. If it smells sour or foul, the compost is not mature. If it smells earthy, it is ready. Fifth, the seed test. Plant ten bean or maize seeds in a pot of your compost. Plant ten more in a pot of ordinary soil as a control. Water them and wait two weeks. If the compost seeds grow as well as or better than the soil seeds, your compost is good. If they grow poorly, the compost is immature or has high salts. Sixth, the jar test. Put a handful of compost in a jar, fill it with water, and shake it. Let it settle. If the compost floats, it is immature. If it sinks, it is mature. If the water turns dark brown, that is normal. If it turns green or smells bad, there is a problem. Seventh, the pH test. You can buy cheap pH strips or a pH meter. Most crops prefer a pH between 6 and 7. If your compost is too acidic, add wood ash or lime. If it is too alkaline, add more green matter or coffee husks. Eighth, the moisture test. Squeeze a handful of compost. It should feel like a wrung-out sponge. If it drips, it is too wet. If it crumbles, it is too dry. For bagging, you want the moisture content around 30 to 40 percent. Now let us talk about nutrient content. You can send a sample to a laboratory for a full analysis. This costs money, but it is worth it if you are selling to commercial farmers or if you want to make nutrient claims on your label. A basic analysis will tell you nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium, sulphur, organic matter, pH, and moisture. It will also tell you if there are heavy metals or pathogens. In many African countries, laboratories at universities or agricultural research institutes offer this service. If you cannot afford a lab test, you can still do on-farm trials. Apply your compost to a small plot and compare it with a plot that uses ordinary manure or no manure. Measure the yield. Take photos. Use the results in your marketing. Now let us talk about packaging. Packaging is your silent salesman. It protects the product, makes it easy to handle, and communicates quality. For small-scale sales, use 50 kg woven polypropylene bags. These are cheap and widely available. For urban gardeners and home users, use 5 kg, 10 kg, or 25 kg bags. These are easier to carry and store. If you can afford it, use laminated bags with a clear window so customers can see the product. Print your label with the following information: product name, net weight, ingredients, nutrient analysis if available, application instructions, production date, batch number, and your contact details. If you are certified organic, include the certification logo. If you are not certified, do not use the word organic in a way that implies certification. Instead, say natural or chemical-free if that is accurate. Your label should be in the local language and in English or French, depending on your market. Use simple words and pictures. Many farmers have low literacy, so pictures of crops and application rates are very helpful. Now let us talk about branding. A good brand name is short, memorable, and positive. It should reflect the benefits of your product. Examples include Green Gold, Soil Boost, Harvest Plus, and Farm Rich. Avoid names that are too generic or too complicated. Register your brand if possible. In many countries, you can register a trademark with the national intellectual property office. This protects your name and logo. Now let us talk about pricing. Your price should cover your costs and give you a profit. Calculate your cost per bag by adding the cost of ingredients, labour, transport, packaging, and overheads. Then add a profit margin of 20 to 50 percent. Check the prices of competing products. If your product is better, you can charge more. If you are new, you may need to charge slightly less to attract customers. But do not compete on price alone. Compete on quality and results. Offer a money-back guarantee if the product does not perform. This builds trust. Now let us talk about storage. Store your bags in a dry, shaded, well-ventilated place. Keep them off the ground on pallets or stones. Do not stack them more than ten bags high. Keep them away from chemicals, fuel, and animal feed. Rotate your stock so that older bags are sold first. Check for pests like rats and insects. Use traps or natural repellents. Do not use rat poison near your product. Now let us talk about customer education. Many farmers do not know how to use organic manure properly. Give them a simple leaflet with application rates for common crops. For maize, apply 2 to 4 tonnes per hectare, or 200 to 400 grams per planting hole. For vegetables, apply 1 to 2 tonnes per hectare, or 100 to 200 grams per plant. For fruit trees, apply 5 to 10 kilograms per tree per year. Tell them to mix the manure into the soil, not just leave it on the surface. Tell them to apply it before planting or as a top dressing. Tell them to water after applying. Offer a training day at your production site. Show them how you make the manure. Let them see the pile. Let them smell the finished product. This transparency builds trust and creates loyal customers. Finally, keep records. Record every batch: date, ingredients, quantities, turning dates, temperatures, and test results. Record every sale: date, customer, quantity, price, and feedback. This data helps you improve your product and your business. In the next lesson, we will look at marketing, sales, and scaling your organic manure business. But before you move on, take a sample of your compost and do the seed test. It is the simplest way to know if your product is ready for market.

5. Marketing, Sales, and Scaling Your Manure Business

You have a great product. Now you need to sell it. This lesson covers marketing, sales, and scaling your organic manure business in an African context. We will start with the mindset. You are not just selling manure. You are selling healthier soils, higher yields, and better incomes for farmers. You are selling food security and climate resilience. When you talk to customers, lead with benefits, not features. Do not say my manure has 33 ingredients. Say your maize will grow taller and your beans will fill better. Do not say it is composted. Say it is safe and will not burn your crops. Do not say it is organic. Say it is chemical-free and improves your soil for years. Now let us talk about your target market. There are several segments. First, smallholder farmers. They are the largest group. They are price-sensitive but loyal if they see results. They often buy in small quantities. Second, commercial farmers. They buy in bulk and are willing to pay more for quality. They want lab tests and consistent supply. Third, urban gardeners. They grow vegetables, flowers, and lawns. They buy small bags and are willing to pay a premium for convenience. Fourth, institutions. Schools, churches, hospitals, and prisons have gardens and farms. They buy in bulk. Fifth, agro-dealers. They sell to farmers and can help you reach a wider market. Sixth, exporters. Organic export markets are growing, but they require certification and strict quality control. Start with the segment you can serve best. If you are in a rural area, focus on smallholder farmers and agro-dealers. If you are near a city, focus on urban gardeners and institutions. Now let us talk about your value proposition. Why should a farmer buy from you instead of using free cattle manure? Because your product is balanced, weed-free, pathogen-free, and ready to use. Because it gives better results. Because it is convenient. Because you provide training and support. Write down your value proposition in one sentence. For example: Our 33-ingredient organic manure gives you healthier crops and higher yields without chemicals, and we support you with free training. Now let us talk about pricing strategy. You can use cost-plus pricing, competitive pricing, or value-based pricing. Cost-plus means you add a fixed margin to your cost. Competitive means you match or beat competitors. Value-based means you charge based on the results you deliver. For example, if your manure increases a farmer yield by 20 percent, and that extra yield is worth 100 dollars, you can charge 20 to 30 dollars for the amount needed. This is a powerful approach because it ties your price to the farmer profit. But you need data to prove your results. So, run field trials and document them. Now let us talk about promotion. You do not need a big budget. You need creativity and consistency. First, demonstration plots. Plant a plot with your manure and a plot without. Put up a sign. Invite farmers to see the difference. This is the most powerful marketing tool in agriculture. Second, field days. Organize a day when farmers can visit your site, see your piles, and learn how to use the product. Offer a small discount for purchases on that day. Third, radio. In many rural areas, radio is the most trusted medium. Buy a short spot or appear on a farming program. Speak in the local language. Fourth, WhatsApp and social media. Create a WhatsApp group for your customers. Share photos, videos, and tips. Use Facebook and TikTok to reach younger farmers. Fifth, word of mouth. Happy customers are your best salespeople. Ask them to tell their neighbours. Offer a referral discount. Sixth, partnerships. Work with extension officers, NGOs, and farmer cooperatives. They can introduce you to groups of farmers. Seventh, branding. Use a consistent logo, colours, and message. Paint your bags, your signboard, and your vehicle with the same design. Now let us talk about sales. There are several channels. Direct sales from your production site. Sales through agro-dealers. Sales at markets and trade fairs. Sales through cooperatives. Online sales for urban customers. Choose the channels that fit your capacity. If you are small, focus on direct sales and one or two agro-dealers. If you are larger, expand to more channels. Train your salespeople. They should know the product, the application rates, and the benefits. They should be able to answer common questions. They should be polite and reliable. Keep a record of every sale. Follow up with customers after two weeks to ask how the product is working. This shows you care and gives you feedback. Now let us talk about scaling. Scaling means increasing production and sales without sacrificing quality.

This Practical Reference lesson is your bench-side companion. It complements the earlier lessons by giving you the exact numbers, ratios, and safety rules you need when you are standing in front of a pile of raw materials with a shovel in your hand and a buyer waiting for a delivery. In African agribusiness, manure is not a waste product â it is a manufactured input, and like any manufactured input it must be consistent, safe, and priced correctly. Whether you are producing for smallholder vegetable growers in Kenya, maize farmers in Zambia, or urban garden centres in Nigeria, the same principles apply: know your ingredient categories, know your target carbon-to-nitrogen ratio, know your moisture and temperature windows, and never compromise on pathogen reduction. Ingredient Categories in the 33-Ingredient System

The 33 ingredients are not a random collection. They fall into six functional categories, each doing a specific job in the final product. Understanding these categories lets you substitute locally available materials without breaking the recipe.

  • Carbon bulking agents â maize stover, rice husks, sawdust, coffee husks, sugarcane bagasse, banana pseudostem. These provide energy for microbes and structure for aeration.
  • Nitrogen activators â poultry litter, cattle slurry, goat droppings, rabbit manure, urine, fish waste, blood meal. These drive the thermophilic phase.
  • Mineral and ash sources â wood ash, bone meal, rock phosphate, limestone, gypsum, kitchen ash. These supply phosphorus, calcium, potassium, and pH buffering.
  • Microbial inoculants â mature compost, forest litter, termite mound soil, effective microorganisms (EM), fermented plant extracts.
  • Moisture and binder agents â water, molasses, cassava starch, clay loam soil.

â ï¸ SAFETY WARNING â Pathogen and Chemical Hazards

Organic manure carries real biological and chemical risks. Treat every batch as potentially hazardous until it has completed the thermophilic phase.

  • Pathogens: Raw poultry litter, cattle slurry, and human-contact materials can carry Salmonella, E. coli O157:H7, Campylobacter, and Cryptosporidium. These cause diarrhoeal disease, which kills over 400,000 African children under five each year (WHO, 2023). Never apply raw manure to vegetables eaten uncooked.
  • Personal protective equipment: Wear gloves, boots, and a dust mask when turning piles. Wash hands with soap after handling manure, even if you wore gloves.
  • Chemical contamination: Manure from animals treated with antibiotics or dewormers can carry residues. Observe withdrawal periods. Do not use manure from animals that died of unknown causes.
  • Heavy metals: Poultry litter from intensive operations can accumulate zinc, copper, and arsenic from feed additives. If you are producing for certified organic markets, test annually.
  • Ammonia gas: Confined composting areas can accumulate ammonia, which damages lungs. Ensure ventilation. Never enter a sealed composting shed without breathing protection.
  • Children and pregnant women: Keep them away from raw manure handling areas. Toxoplasmosis and listeriosis are real risks.

Reference: WHO (2006) Guidelines for the Safe Use of Wastewater, Excreta and Greywater in Agriculture; MSD Veterinary Manual (2023) Zoonotic Diseases of Livestock.

Quick Reference Checklist â Daily Operations

  1. Temperature check: Insert a 50 cm compost thermometer into three points of the pile. Record all three. If any point is below 45Â°C or above 70Â°C, act immediately.
  2. Moisture squeeze test: Grab a handful from 30 cm deep. It should feel like a wrung-out sponge â one or two drops, no more.
  3. Odour check: Ammonia smell means too much nitrogen or too little carbon. Rotten-egg smell means anaerobic conditions â turn immediately.
  4. Turning schedule: Turn every 3â5 days during weeks 1â3. Move outer material to the centre and centre material to the outside.
  5. Record keeping: Log date, temperature, moisture, turning, and any additives. Buyers and certifiers will ask for these records.
  6. PPE compliance: Gloves, boots, and dust mask on before you touch the pile. No exceptions.
  7. Maturity test: After 6 weeks, bag a sample in a sealed jar for 24 hours. If it smells earthy and not sour, it is ready. If it smells sour or ammonia-like, it is not.
  8. Bagging moisture: Air-dry to 20â30% moisture before bagging. Wet compost in sealed bags becomes anaerobic and unsellable.
