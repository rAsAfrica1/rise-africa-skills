1. Simple processing: drying, milling, packaging

Simple processing turns raw crops into products that last longer, weigh less, and sell for more. Start with drying. For vegetables like tomatoes, peppers, and leafy greens, wash, slice to 5mm thickness, and dry on raised racks in full sun for 2-3 days until brittle. In humid areas, use a solar dryer: a wooden box with a glass top and vent holes. Dry until moisture is under 10%a piece should snap, not bend. For grains like maize and cassava, drying on a clean tarpaulin for 2-3 days reduces mould and allows safe storage.

Milling adds value by turning whole grains into flour. A diesel-powered hammer mill costs about $800-$1,500 in many African cities. You can rent time at a local mill for $0.05-$0.10 per kg. Mill maize into fine flour for porridge, or cassava into high-quality cassava flour (HQCF). Sift flour through a 1mm sieve for uniform texture. One 50kg bag of maize (cost $15) yields about 45kg of flour after milling and sifting. Sell that flour in 1kg or 2kg sealed bags for $1.50-$2.00 per kgtripling your income.

Packaging protects your product and makes it attractive. Use food-grade plastic bags, sealed with a simple impulse sealer ($50-$100). For flour, a 1kg bag with a printed label showing your farm name, weight, and production date builds trust. For dried vegetables, use clear jars or zip-lock pouches. Always label with the product name, net weight, and your phone number. A simple inkjet printer for labels costs $100-$200.

Start small: dry 20kg of tomatoes, mill 50kg of maize, package in 1kg units. Test the market at a local shop or market stall. Track your costs: raw material, fuel, packaging, and your time. If you sell 20kg of dried tomatoes at $5 per kg, that's $100 from tomatoes that would have sold fresh for $10. The same principle works for any cropdry, mill, package, and sell for more.

2. Making tomato paste, fruit juice, or peanut butter

Tomato paste: Use ripe, red tomatoes. Wash, remove stems, and blend into puree. Cook in a large stainless steel pot over medium heat, stirring constantly to prevent burning. After 1 hour, the puree thickens. Add 1 teaspoon of salt and 1 tablespoon of lemon juice per litre of puree to preserve colour and flavour. Continue cooking until a spoon leaves a clear trail. Pour hot paste into sterilised glass jars, leaving 1cm headspace. Seal and boil jars in a water bath for 20 minutes. One 20kg crate of tomatoes (cost $5) yields about 4kg of paste. Sell 250g jars for $2-$3 each$32-$48 total.

Fruit juice: Use mangoes, oranges, or pineapples. Wash, peel, and remove seeds. Blend fruit with clean water (1 part fruit to 2 parts water). Strain through a clean cloth. Add sugar to taste (about 100g per litre) and 1 teaspoon of citric acid per litre as a preservative. Heat to 80C for 5 minutes (pasteurise), then pour into sterilised bottles. Cap immediately and cool. One 20L bucket of mangoes (cost $3) makes about 15 litres of juice. Sell 500ml bottles for $1.50 each$45 total.

Peanut butter: Use raw groundnuts. Roast in a dry pan or oven at 160C for 15-20 minutes until fragrant. Rub off skins (winnowing works). Grind in a mill or heavy blender until smooth. Add 1 teaspoon of salt and 1 tablespoon of honey or sugar per kg for taste. Add 1 tablespoon of palm oil per kg to prevent separation. Pack into clean jars. One 10kg bag of groundnuts (cost $10) yields about 9kg of peanut butter. Sell 500g jars for $3-$4 each$54-$72 total.

Key rules: Always work cleanwash hands, sterilise jars by boiling, and use clean water. Label with product name, weight, and date. Test small batches first. These products have a shelf life of 3-6 months if stored in a cool, dark place. Start with one product, master it, then expand.

3. Adding value to cassava, maize, or groundnuts

Cassava: Fresh cassava roots sell for $0.20-$0.30 per kg. Process into gari, a fermented dried cassava product. Peel roots, wash, and grate finely. Press out excess water using a screw press or heavy stones for 24 hours. Ferment the pressed mash for 2-3 days in a sack. Sieve to remove lumps, then roast in a dry pan over low heat, stirring constantly until dry and crispy (about 30 minutes per 5kg). One 100kg bag of cassava (cost $20) yields about 25kg of gari. Sell 1kg bags for $1.50-$2.00$37.50-$50 total. Also try high-quality cassava flour (HQCF): peel, wash, grate, press, dry, and mill. HQCF sells for $1.20-$1.50 per kg.

Maize: Turn maize into roasted maize flour (for porridge) or corn flakes. For roasted flour, soak maize for 12 hours, drain, roast in a pan until golden brown, then mill. It sells for $1.80-$2.20 per kg. For corn flakes, make a thick porridge, spread thin on a tray, steam for 10 minutes, cut into flakes, and dry. Packaging in 500g bags sells for $2-$3. One 50kg bag of maize (cost $15) yields about 40kg of roasted flour$72-$88 total.

Groundnuts: Beyond peanut butter, make roasted groundnuts for snacks. Sort, roast at 160C for 15 minutes, cool, and pack in airtight bags. Add salt or spices. One 10kg bag (cost $10) yields 9kg of roasted nuts. Sell 250g packs for $1.50$54 total. Also make groundnut oil: grind nuts, mix with water, boil, and skim off oil. One 10kg bag yields about 3 litres of oil. Sell 1L bottles for $4-$5$12-$15, plus the residue cake for animal feed.

Key steps: Always sort out spoiled pieces. Use clean water and equipment. For cassava, proper fermentation reduces cyanide. For maize, dry thoroughly to avoid aflatoxins. Label your products with weight, date, and your contact. Start with 50kg batches to learn, then scale up as you find buyers.

4. Packaging value-added products for sale

Packaging is not just a containerit protects your product, tells your story, and makes buyers willing to pay more. Start with food-grade materials. For dry products like flour, gari, or roasted nuts, use clear plastic bags (0.5mm thick) or kraft paper bags with a plastic liner. For liquids like juice or oil, use glass bottles or PET plastic bottles with tight caps. For pastes like tomato paste or peanut butter, use glass jars with metal lids. You can buy these in bulk from packaging suppliers in major citiesa pack of 100 1kg plastic bags costs $5-$10, 100 glass jars cost $30-$50.

Sealing is critical. For plastic bags, use an impulse sealer ($50-$100) or a simple candle and pliers to melt the edge. For bottles and jars, ensure the cap is screwed tight and check for leaks. For extra protection, shrink-wrap or use a heat gun to seal the neck. Always leave 1-2cm headspace for liquids and pastes to allow for expansion.

Labelling builds trust. Include: product name, net weight (e.g., 500g), your farm or business name, phone number, production date, and expiry date. For example: 'Mama Asha's Peanut Butter, 500g, Made in Arusha, Tel: 0712-345-678, Best before: Dec 2025.' You can print labels at a local print shop for $0.10-$0.20 each, or use a simple inkjet printer with sticker paper. A QR code linking to your WhatsApp catalogue is a bonus.

Presentation matters. Group small units into a display box or basket. For market stalls, use clear containers so buyers see the product. For retail shops, offer a shelf-ready box of 12 units. Pricing: packaging adds costa 1kg bag of flour costs $0.10 for bag and label. Factor that into your price. If your flour costs $1.00 to produce and $0.10 to package, sell at $1.50 for a 50% margin. Always test with a small batch: package 20 units, show to 5 potential buyers, and ask what they think. Adjust size, label, and price based on feedback.

5. Calculating costs and prices for processed goods

To make a profit, you must know your true costs. Start with raw materials. For example, to make 1kg of gari, you need about 4kg of fresh cassava. If cassava costs $0.25 per kg, raw material cost is $1.00. Add processing costs: fuel or firewood ($0.20), labour (your timevalue it at $0.50 per hour; 1 hour per kg), and packaging ($0.15 for bag and label). Total cost per kg = $1.85. If you sell at $2.50 per kg, your profit is $0.65 per kg. For 100kg, profit is $65.

For tomato paste: 20kg tomatoes ($5), salt and lemon ($0.50), jars ($0.30 each for 4 jars = $1.20), fuel ($1.00), labour (2 hours = $1.00). Total cost = $8.70 for 4 jars of 250g. Cost per jar = $2.18. Sell at $3.00 per jar, profit $0.82 per jar, $3.28 total. That seems low, but you can scale up: 100kg tomatoes gives 20 jars, profit $16.40.

Include overheads: equipment depreciation. If a mill costs $1,000 and lasts 5 years, that's $200 per year. If you mill 1,000kg per year, add $0.20 per kg. Also include transport to market ($0.10 per kg) and market fees ($0.05 per kg). Update your cost sheet monthly.

Pricing strategy: Use cost-plus pricingadd a markup of 30-50% to your total cost. Check competitor prices. If your gari costs $1.85 and competitors sell at $2.20, price at $2.40 to be competitive but profitable. Offer discounts for bulk (e.g., 10kg for $22 instead of $24). Always calculate break-even: if total fixed costs are $100 per month, you need to sell enough units to cover that. For gari at $0.65 profit per kg, you need 154kg to break even. Keep records in a notebook: date, batch size, costs, sales, profit. Review weekly to adjust prices and find savings.

6. Finding buyers for processed products

Start local. Your first buyers are neighbours, local shops, and market vendors. Visit 10 small shops within 5km. Bring samples (small 100g packs) and a price list. Ask: 'Would you stock my gari at $2.50 per kg? I'll give you a 10% discount.' Offer to leave 5 units on consignmentthey pay after selling. This builds trust. One shop might sell 20kg per week, earning you $50.

Target institutions. Schools, hospitals, and restaurants buy in bulk. A school with 500 students needs 50kg of maize flour per week. Approach the head teacher or bursar with a sample and a quote: 'I can supply 50kg of flour at $1.80 per kg, delivered every Monday.' That's $90 per week. Also try hotels and lodgesthey need peanut butter, juice, and tomato paste. Offer a consistent quality and reliable delivery.

Use digital tools. Create a WhatsApp Business account with a catalogue of your products, prices, and photos. Share your status with local groups. Join Facebook groups for farmers and buyers in your region. Post in marketplaces like Jumia or Kilimall if you have packaged goods. For example, a peanut butter seller in Ghana got 20 orders per week from a WhatsApp group of 200 members.

Attend trade fairs and farmers' markets. The Nairobi Agricultural Show or local county fairs attract thousands of buyers. Rent a stall for $20-$50, display your products, and collect contacts. Follow up within 48 hours with a call or message. Also approach NGOs and government programmes that buy from small-scale processorse.g., World Food Programme's local procurement.

Keep records of every buyer: name, contact, product, quantity, price, and delivery date. Follow up monthly. Ask for referrals: 'Do you know anyone else who needs flour?' Word of mouth is powerful. Start with 5 buyers, deliver on time, and your reputation will grow. Aim to have at least 3 regular buyers before expanding production.

<h2>Practical Reference: Value Addition to Increase Income</h2>

<p>Value addition is the single most reliable way for African smallholder farmers and agripreneurs to escape the trap of selling raw commodities at harvest-time prices. When you dry, mill, package, or process a crop before selling it, you capture a larger share of the final consumer price, reduce post-harvest losses, and build a business that can serve buyers year-round. This lesson gives you the practical numbers, safety rules, and market routes you need to turn raw produce into income.</p>

<h3>Why Processing Pays: The Margin Logic</h3>
<p>Raw groundnuts sold at farm gate in Northern Nigeria may fetch 600900 per kg, while a jar of locally produced peanut butter retails at 2,5004,000 per 500g equivalent to 5,0008,000 per kg. Even after deducting oil, jars, labels, labour, and transport, the processor's margin is typically 24 the raw commodity margin. The same logic applies to cassava (fresh roots vs. garri or high-quality cassava flour), maize (grain vs. fortified maize meal), and tomatoes (fresh vs. paste).</p>

<p>The catch: value addition only works if you (1) control quality, (2) understand your true costs, and (3) have a buyer before you scale. This reference covers all three.</p>

<h3>Simple Processing: Drying, Milling, Packaging</h3>
<p>You do not need expensive machinery to start. The three lowest-barrier processing steps are:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Drying:</strong> Sun-drying on raised racks or tarpaulins reduces moisture to safe storage levels (maize 13.5%, groundnuts 9%, cassava chips 12%). This alone can lift price 2050% because buyers pay a premium for aflatoxin-safe, mould-free grain.</li>
<li><strong>Milling:</strong> A hammer mill or plate mill (US$800US$3,000) turns maize, cassava, millet, or groundnuts into flour. Milling on demand for neighbours is itself a profitable service business.</li>
<li><strong>Packaging:</strong> Moving from open sacks to sealed 1kg, 2kg, or 5kg branded bags signals quality and lets you sell into supermarkets, schools, and urban markets.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Making Tomato Paste, Fruit Juice, or Peanut Butter</h3>
<p>These three products are proven entry points across Africa because demand is constant and raw material is locally available.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Tomato paste:</strong> Wash, blend, cook down, add preservative (citric acid or benzoate within legal limits), hot-fill into sterilised jars. Yield: ~5kg fresh tomatoes 1kg paste.</li>
<li><strong>Fruit juice:</strong> Mango, pineapple, or baobab. Extract, pasteurise at 8590C for 1530 seconds, hot-fill. Shelf life 612 months if sealed.</li>
<li><strong>Peanut butter:</strong> Roast, cool, de-skin, grind with salt and optional sugar/oil. No preservative needed if moisture is low and jars are clean. This is the highest-margin, lowest-tech option for many cooperatives.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Packaging Value-Added Products for Sale</h3>
<p>Packaging is not decoration it is part of the product. Buyers judge quality by the package before they taste. Key requirements:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Food-grade materials only:</strong> PET jars, laminated foil pouches, or glass. Never reuse chemical containers.</li>
<li><strong>Legal labelling:</strong> Product name, net weight, ingredients, production and expiry dates, batch number, producer address, and where required, NAFDAC (Nigeria), KEBS (Kenya), or Ghana FDA registration number.</li>
<li><strong>Nutrition and barcode:</strong> Barcodes (EAN-13) are mandatory for supermarket listings; nutrition panels build trust.</li>
<li><strong>Unit sizing:</strong> Offer 250g, 500g, and 1kg to serve both low-income daily buyers and bulk household or institutional buyers.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Calculating Costs and Prices for Processed Goods</h3>
<p>Most small processors fail because they price from the market backward instead of costing forward. Use this formula:</p>
<p style="background:#f1f8e9;padding:10px;border-left:4px solid #558b2f;"><strong>Selling price = (Total cost per unit) (1 target profit margin)</strong></p>
<p>Total cost per unit includes: raw material, processing (fuel, labour, water), packaging, transport, marketing, and overhead allocation (rent, depreciation, licenses).</p>

<strong style="color:#b71c1c;"> SAFETY WARNING Aflatoxin and Food Safety</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0;">Groundnuts and maize are highly susceptible to <strong>aflatoxin</strong>, a poisonous mould toxin that causes liver cancer and stunting in children. Never process or sell mouldy, discoloured, or musty-smelling groundnuts or maize no amount of roasting or processing removes aflatoxin. Dry crops to safe moisture (groundnuts 9%, maize 13.5%) immediately after harvest, store on pallets away from walls, and use hermetic bags. In tomato paste and juice production, always hot-fill and sterilise jars to prevent <em>Clostridium botulinum</em> and yeast spoilage. Register with your national food safety authority (NAFDAC, KEBS, Ghana FDA) before selling to the public unregistered food products can be seized and producers prosecuted.</p>
</div>

<h3>Finding Buyers for Processed Products</h3>
<p>Processed goods need different buyers than raw commodities. Target these channels in order of ease:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Local retail:</strong> Neighbourhood shops, kiosks, and open markets lowest barrier, lowest margin.</li>
<li><strong>Institutional buyers:</strong> Schools, hospitals, hotels, restaurants, and the military buy in bulk and pay reliably. Prepare a one-page product sheet and sample.</li>
<li><strong>Supermarkets:</strong> Shoprite, Carrefour, Naivas, and local chains require barcodes, consistent supply, and registration. Margin is lower but volume is high.</li>
<li><strong>Export and diaspora markets:</strong> African food shops in Europe and North America pay premium prices for authentic products (garri, peanut butter, baobab juice). Requires export certification.</li>
<li><strong>Online and social commerce:</strong> WhatsApp Business, Jumia, Kilimall, and Facebook Marketplace are increasingly effective for packaged foods in urban areas.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Source raw material from known, aflatoxin-tested suppliers; reject mouldy or damp crops.</li>
<li>Dry every crop to its safe moisture level before storage or processing.</li>
<li>Use only food-grade packaging, clean jars, and sealed lids; label with dates and batch numbers.</li>
<li>Calculate true cost per unit (raw + processing + packaging + overhead) before setting price.</li>
<li>Target a minimum 3040% gross margin to absorb transport, spoilage, and credit sales.</li>
<li>Register your product with the national food safety authority before retail sale.</li>
<li>Secure at least one confirmed buyer or purchase order before scaling production.</li>
<li>Keep a simple daily record of production, sales, and expenses to track real profit.</li>
</ol>
