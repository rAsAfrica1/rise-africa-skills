1. Lesson 1: Safe Mixing and Hygiene of Milk Replacer

Mixing milk replacer correctly is the first line of defence against disease and poor growth in young animals. Whether you are rearing a bucket-fed calf, a pen of piglets, or an orphaned puppy, the principles are the same: clean water, correct powder-to-water ratio, accurate temperature, and immediate consumption. The FAO (Food and Agriculture Organization) estimates that up to 30 percent of pre-weaning mortality in smallholder systems is linked to poor hygiene and incorrect reconstitution of milk replacer. Teagasc (Ireland) research shows that calves fed milk replacer mixed at the wrong concentration (too dilute) gain 15-20 percent less weight by weaning. The golden rule: always read the label, but common ratios are 125-150 grams of powder per litre of water for calves, 100-125 g per litre for goat kids and lambs, and 150-200 g per litre for piglets. For puppies and kittens, use species-specific products like Royal Canin Baby Dog Milk or Baby Cat Milk at 1 scoop (approx 5 g) per 30 ml of warm water. Never guess. Use a digital kitchen scale (cost 1,500-3,000 KES or 8,000-15,000 NGN or 200-400 ZAR) because volume scoops vary with compaction. Water must be clean and safe. In many African smallholder settings, borehole or rainwater is preferred over surface water. If only surface water is available, boil it and cool to the correct mixing temperature. The ideal mixing temperature is 45-50 degrees Celsius for most powders; this dissolves the powder without destroying heat-sensitive vitamins. Once mixed, feed at 38-39 degrees Celsius for calves, 38-40 for piglets, and 37-38 for puppies and kittens. Use a thermometer (200-500 KES). Feeding too hot (above 42 C) causes abomasal or gastric burns and refusal; too cold (below 35 C) slows digestion and causes bloat or diarrhoea. Mix only what the animal will consume in one feeding. Discard any leftover within 1 hour. Do not store mixed milk replacer at room temperature for more than 30 minutes because bacteria multiply rapidly. Refrigerate if necessary at 4 C and reheat to 38 C, but never reheat more than once. Hygiene: wash all buckets, bottles, nipples, and mixing jugs with hot water and detergent after every feeding. Once daily, use a disinfectant approved for food contact (e.g., chlorine solution at 200 ppm). Rinse thoroughly. Teagasc recommends a three-step cleaning: rinse, wash with detergent, then disinfect. In Africa, many farmers use local black soap or dishwashing liquid; that is acceptable if rinsed well. Common mistakes: using hot water from a thermos without measuring temperature; adding powder to cold water and then heating (creates lumps and hot spots); using the same bucket for mixing and feeding without rinsing; mixing a day's supply at once and leaving it in the sun. Cost note: a 25 kg bag of calf milk replacer costs 8,000-12,000 KES in Kenya, 45,000-70,000 NGN in Nigeria, or 1,200-1,800 ZAR in South Africa. Buying the cheapest powder often means lower fat and protein, leading to poor growth. Look for 20-22 percent protein and 15-20 percent fat for calves. For piglets, 22-24 percent protein and 8-12 percent fat. For goat kids and lambs, 20-24 percent protein and 20-25 percent fat. For puppies and kittens, use only products labelled for that species; Royal Canin and Denkavit both publish detailed mixing tables. Always store powder in a cool, dry place, sealed, away from rodents. Use within 4-6 weeks of opening. Never feed mouldy or caked powder. If you cannot afford commercial milk replacer, whole cow or goat milk can be used for calves and kids, but it must be fed at 10-12 percent of body weight daily and may need supplementation with vitamins and minerals. However, whole milk is not balanced for piglets or puppies. For those species, use commercial replacer or consult a veterinarian. Finally, keep records: date, batch, amount mixed, temperature, and animal response. This helps trace problems. Safety is not expensive; it is a habit.

2. Lesson 2: Safe Feeding Volumes, Frequencies, and Body Weight Percentages

Overfeeding and underfeeding are both dangerous. Overfeeding causes diarrhoea, bloat, and in severe cases, death from acidosis or aspiration. Underfeeding causes starvation, poor immunity, and stunted growth. The key is to feed a percentage of body weight per day, split into several meals. For calves, the standard is 10-12 percent of body weight in milk or milk replacer per day. A 40 kg calf should receive 4.0-4.8 litres per day, split into 2-3 feedings. Teagasc recommends 2 feedings per day for calves under 3 weeks, then 1-2 feedings. For goat kids and lambs, feed 10-15 percent of body weight per day. A 5 kg kid should get 500-750 ml per day, split into 3-4 feedings. For piglets, feed 200-300 ml per kg body weight per day? No, that is too high. Correct: piglets should receive 15-20 percent of body weight in milk replacer per day. A 2 kg piglet needs 300-400 ml per day, split into 4-6 feedings. For puppies, feed 15-20 percent of body weight per day. A 1 kg puppy needs 150-200 ml per day, split into 4-6 feedings. For kittens, feed 20-25 percent of body weight per day. A 0.5 kg kitten needs 100-125 ml per day, split into 4-6 feedings. These are starting points; adjust based on body condition and growth. Weigh animals weekly. If a calf gains less than 400-500 g per day, increase volume slightly or check powder concentration. If diarrhoea occurs, reduce volume by 20 percent for one day and offer electrolytes. Never feed more than 5 percent of body weight in a single meal. For a 40 kg calf, that is 2 litres maximum per feeding. For a 2 kg piglet, 100 ml maximum. For a 1 kg puppy, 50 ml maximum. Exceeding this causes milk to sit in the stomach, ferment, and cause bloat. Frequency: newborn animals need frequent small meals. Calves: 2-3 times daily. Kids and lambs: 3-4 times daily for first 2 weeks, then 2-3 times. Piglets: 6-8 times daily for first week, then 4-6 times. Puppies and kittens: 6-8 times daily for first 2 weeks, then 4-6 times. As they grow, reduce frequency. By weaning, most species can be fed 2 times daily. Always feed at the same times each day. Sudden changes in volume or frequency cause digestive upset. When increasing volume, do so gradually over 3-4 days. For example, increase by 10 percent per day. When mixing, use the correct powder-to-water ratio as in Lesson 1. Do not add extra powder to make a richer mix; that causes diarrhoea. Do not dilute to save money; that causes malnutrition. Common mistakes in Africa: feeding calves once a day to save labour, leading to hunger and poor growth; feeding piglets from a trough without measuring, leading to overfeeding and waste; feeding puppies cow milk, which causes diarrhoea because of lactose. Cow milk has 4.8 percent lactose; puppies need 3.5 percent or less. Use only species-specific replacer. Cost: feeding a calf 4 litres per day for 8 weeks requires about 25 kg of powder, costing 8,000-12,000 KES or 45,000-70,000 NGN or 1,200-1,800 ZAR. That is an investment. Underfeeding to save powder costs more in lost growth and mortality. For goat kids and lambs, 10 kg of powder feeds one kid for 6 weeks, costing 4,000-6,000 KES. For piglets, 5 kg of powder feeds a litter of 10 for 3 weeks, costing 3,000-5,000 KES. For puppies, a 2 kg bag of Royal Canin Baby Dog Milk costs 6,000-9,000 KES and feeds one puppy for 3-4 weeks. Always provide fresh clean water alongside milk replacer after 1 week of age. Calves, kids, and lambs need water for rumen development. Piglets, puppies, and kittens need water for hydration. Do not mix water into milk replacer beyond the recommended ratio. If an animal is scouring, do not feed milk; feed an electrolyte solution (e.g., 1 litre water + 5 g salt + 20 g glucose) for 12-24 hours, then reintroduce milk at half strength. Consult a veterinarian if diarrhoea persists beyond 24 hours. Finally, monitor body condition score. For calves, aim for a score of 2.5-3.0 on a 5-point scale. For piglets, check ribs and spine; they should be palpable but not prominent. For puppies and kittens, you should feel ribs but not see them. Adjust feeding accordingly. Safety is about consistency and observation.

3. Lesson 3: Safe Water, Electrolytes, and Rehydration

Water is the most overlooked nutrient in young animal feeding. A calf that is scouring can lose 5-10 percent of body weight in water in a single day. Without rehydration, it will die. The same applies to piglets, kids, lambs, puppies, and kittens. This lesson covers safe water, electrolyte preparation, and rehydration protocols. First, clean water. All animals must have access to fresh, clean water from day 1. For calves, kids, and lambs, water supports rumen development. For piglets, puppies, and kittens, water prevents dehydration. In Africa, water quality varies. If you would not drink it, do not give it to young animals. Boil surface water for 5 minutes, then cool. Use a clean container. Change water at least twice daily. Do not allow animals to drink from stagnant pools or shared troughs that are contaminated with faeces. Teagasc recommends testing water for coliforms if you have recurrent diarrhoea. Simple test kits cost 2,000-4,000 KES. Second, electrolytes. When an animal has diarrhoea, it loses sodium, potassium, chloride, and bicarbonate. Plain water is not enough. Use a commercial electrolyte (e.g., Denkavit Diakur, Royal Canin Rehydration Support, or MaxCare Calf Electrolyte). If unavailable, make a homemade solution: 1 litre clean water + 5 grams table salt + 20 grams glucose (or 40 grams sugar) + 2 grams baking soda (optional). Mix well. Feed at 38-39 C. For calves, offer 2-4 litres per day in addition to milk, or replace one milk feeding with electrolytes. For piglets, offer 100-200 ml per day via bottle or trough. For puppies and kittens, offer 50-100 ml per day via syringe. Do not force-feed; let them drink. If they refuse, consult a veterinarian. Third, rehydration protocol. If an animal is 5 percent dehydrated (slightly sunken eyes, skin tent lasts 2-3 seconds), give electrolytes equal to 5 percent of body weight. For a 40 kg calf, that is 2 litres. Give half immediately, half after 4 hours. If 8-10 percent dehydrated (eyes sunken, skin tent lasts 5+ seconds, cold extremities), give 10 percent of body weight (4 litres for a 40 kg calf) over 24 hours, plus veterinary care. For piglets, a 2 kg piglet at 5 percent dehydration needs 100 ml; at 10 percent, 200 ml. For puppies, a 1 kg puppy needs 50 ml or 100 ml. Always give electrolytes between milk feedings, not mixed with milk, because the high salt can curdle milk. Wait 30-60 minutes after electrolytes before feeding milk. Fourth, common mistakes. Many farmers continue feeding milk during diarrhoea, which worsens it. Stop milk for 12-24 hours, feed electrolytes, then reintroduce milk at half strength. Another mistake: using too much salt in homemade solution. 5 grams per litre is correct; 10 grams causes salt poisoning. Another: using dirty water to mix electrolytes. Another: giving cold electrolytes. Another: not offering water to calves on milk replacer. Calves on milk replacer still need water. Fifth, cost. Commercial electrolyte sachets cost 200-500 KES each (or 1,000-2,500 NGN, or 30-70 ZAR). A 40 kg calf may need 4-8 sachets over 2 days, costing 800-4,000 KES. Homemade solution costs less than 100 KES per litre. But if you use homemade, ensure accurate weighing. A teaspoon of salt is about 5 grams; a tablespoon of sugar is about 20 grams. Use a scale if possible. Sixth, prevention. The best rehydration is prevention. Follow hygiene, correct mixing, and correct volumes. Vaccinate dams against rotavirus and coronavirus (available for cattle and pigs). Ensure colostrum intake in the first 6 hours of life. Colostrum provides antibodies and energy. For orphans, use a colostrum replacer (e.g., Denkavit Colostrum or MaxCare Colostrum) at 10 percent of body weight in the first 6 hours. Seventh, when to call a vet. If diarrhoea is bloody, if the animal is recumbent, if it has a fever above 40 C, or if it does not improve after 24 hours of electrolytes, call a veterinarian. Do not use antibiotics without prescription. Overuse of antibiotics leads to resistance and is illegal in many countries. Finally, keep records of water intake, electrolyte use, and faecal consistency. This helps you spot problems early. Safe water and electrolytes save lives.

4. Lesson 4: Safe Storage, Handling, and Cost Management of Milk Replacer

Milk replacer is expensive. In Africa, a 25 kg bag of calf milk replacer costs 8,000-12,000 KES in Kenya, 45,000-70,000 NGN in Nigeria, or 1,200-1,800 ZAR in South Africa. For piglets, a 10 kg bag costs 5,000-8,000 KES. For goat kids and lambs, a 10 kg bag costs 4,000-6,000 KES. For puppies and kittens, a 2 kg bag of Royal Canin or Denkavit costs 6,000-9,000 KES. Poor storage wastes money and harms animals. This lesson covers safe storage, handling, and cost management. First, storage. Milk replacer powder is hygroscopic; it absorbs moisture from the air. Store in a cool, dry place, away from direct sunlight. Ideal temperature is below 25 C. Humidity should be below 60 percent. In many African smallholder settings, this means storing in a sealed plastic container or original bag inside a dry cupboard or room. Do not store on the floor; use a pallet or shelf. Do not store near chemicals, fuel, or pesticides. Do not store in the kitchen where steam and smoke can contaminate. Once opened, use within 4-6 weeks. Write the opening date on the bag. If the powder is caked, smells sour, or changes colour, discard it. Do not feed mouldy powder; mycotoxins can cause liver damage and death. Second, handling. Always use a clean, dry scoop. Do not use a wet scoop; moisture introduces bacteria. Do not return unused powder to the bag; discard it. Do not mix with dirty hands. Wash hands before handling. If you transfer powder to a smaller container, label it with the product name, batch number, and expiry date. Keep the original label for reference. Third, cost management. The cheapest powder is not always the best value. Compare cost per kilogram of dry matter and protein content. A 25 kg bag at 10,000 KES is 400 KES per kg. If it has 20 percent protein, that is 2,000 KES per kg of protein. A 25 kg bag at 12,000 KES with 22 percent protein is 2,182 KES per kg of protein. The second is slightly more expensive per kg of protein but may give better growth. Teagasc recommends calculating cost per kilogram of gain. For calves, a good target is 300-400 KES per kg of gain. If you are spending more, review your feeding programme. Buy in bulk if you have storage. A 25 kg bag is cheaper per kg than a 5 kg bag. But do not buy more than you can use in 6 weeks. Share a bulk order with neighbours to get a discount. In Kenya, suppliers like Unga Farm Care, Cooper K-Brands, and Bidco sell milk replacer. In Nigeria, suppliers like Vital Feed, Chikun, and Premier Feed Mills. In South Africa, suppliers like Meadow Feeds, Epol, and Afgri. For puppies and kittens, Royal Canin and Denkavit are available through veterinary clinics. Fourth, common mistakes. Buying milk replacer without checking protein and fat levels. Using calf milk replacer for piglets (too low in protein and fat). Using whole milk for puppies (causes diarrhoea). Mixing milk replacer with hot water from a thermos without measuring temperature. Storing powder in a hot car. Leaving the bag open. Using a wet scoop. Feeding leftover mixed milk. These mistakes cost money and lives. Fifth, record keeping. Keep a simple notebook: date, amount of powder used, amount of water, number of animals fed, and any health problems. This helps you calculate cost per animal per day. For example, a calf fed 4 litres per day at 125 g per litre uses 500 g of powder per day. Over 8 weeks (56 days), that is 28 kg. At 400 KES per kg, that is 11,200 KES. If the calf gains 40 kg, cost per kg gain is 280 KES. If it gains only 20 kg, cost per kg gain is 560 KES. The difference is often due to poor hygiene, incorrect mixing, or disease. Sixth, alternatives. If commercial milk replacer is too expensive, consider using whole cow or goat milk for calves, kids, and lambs. But whole milk is not balanced for piglets, puppies, or kittens. For those species, you must use commercial replacer or consult a veterinarian for a homemade formula. A homemade formula for puppies (used only in emergencies) is 1 litre whole milk + 1 egg yolk + 1 teaspoon cod liver oil + 1 teaspoon glucose. But this is not complete and should not be used long-term. Royal Canin and Denkavit both warn against homemade formulas because of nutritional imbalances. Seventh, safety. Never use milk replacer past its expiry date. Never use a bag that has been wet. Never feed powder that has been contaminated with rodent droppings. Never mix milk replacer with antibiotics unless prescribed. Never feed milk replacer to a different species without checking the label. Safe storage and handling protect your investment and your animals.

5. Lesson 5: Safe Weaning and Transition from Milk Replacer

Weaning is the most stressful period in a young animal's life. Done correctly, it saves money and prevents disease. Done incorrectly, it causes growth checks, diarrhoea, and death. This lesson covers safe weaning and transition from milk replacer for calves, piglets, goat kids, lambs, puppies, and kittens. First, when to wean. Calves: wean at 8-10 weeks if they are eating at least 1 kg of calf starter per day and drinking water. Goat kids and lambs: wean at 6-8 weeks if they are eating 200-300 g of creep feed per day. Piglets: wean at 3-4 weeks if they are eating 200-300 g of creep feed per day. Puppies: wean at 6-8 weeks onto solid food. Kittens: wean at 6-8 weeks onto solid food. Do not wean early to save money; early weaning increases mortality. Do not wean late; it wastes milk replacer. Second, how to wean. Weaning should be gradual over 1-2 weeks. For calves, reduce milk replacer by 20 percent every 2-3 days while increasing starter feed. For example, a calf on 4 litres per day: day 1-3, 3.2 litres; day 4-6, 2.4 litres; day 7-9, 1.6 litres; day 10-12, 0.8 litres; day 13, stop. Always offer fresh starter and water. For goat kids and lambs, reduce milk by 25 percent every 3 days. For piglets, reduce milk by 25 percent every 2 days. For puppies and kittens, mix milk replacer with solid food, gradually increasing solid and decreasing liquid over 7-10 days. Third, what to feed during weaning. Calves: calf starter (18-20 percent protein) and hay. Goat kids and lambs: creep feed (16-18 percent protein) and hay. Piglets: creep feed (18-20 percent protein). Puppies: puppy food soaked in warm water or milk replacer. Kittens: kitten food soaked in warm water or milk replacer. Always provide clean water. Teagasc recommends that calves eat 1 kg of starter before weaning. If they eat less, delay weaning. Fourth, common mistakes. Weaning abruptly. Weaning during illness or extreme weather. Weaning and moving animals at the same time. Weaning and vaccinating at the same time. Weaning and dehorning at the same time. These combinations cause stress and disease. Do one change at a time. Another mistake: not providing enough feeder space. Each calf needs 30-45 cm of feeder space. Each piglet needs 10-15 cm. Each kid or lamb needs 20-30 cm. Another mistake: not cleaning feeders. Feeders must be cleaned daily. Another mistake: feeding mouldy or dusty feed. Another mistake: not providing water. Calves need 4-6 litres of water per day at weaning. Fifth, cost. Weaning saves money because milk replacer is expensive. A calf on 4 litres per day costs 400-500 KES per day. A calf on starter and hay costs 100-150 KES per day. So weaning at 8 weeks instead of 12 weeks saves 8,000-12,000 KES per calf. But weaning too early costs more in mortality and poor growth. The goal is to wean when the animal is ready, not when the calendar says so. Sixth, health during weaning. Watch for diarrhoea, pneumonia, and poor growth. Weigh animals weekly. If a calf loses weight for 2 consecutive weeks, check feed quality and quantity. If diarrhoea occurs, treat with electrolytes as in Lesson 3. If pneumonia occurs (coughing, rapid breathing, fever), call a veterinarian. Vaccinate before weaning, not during. Deworm before weaning, not during. Seventh, special considerations for Africa. In many smallholder systems, calves are weaned onto poor-quality hay and maize stover. This causes poor growth. Instead, offer good-quality hay, legume hay (e.g., lucerne, desmodium), and a balanced starter. For goat kids and lambs, offer browse and creep feed. For piglets, offer a balanced creep feed with 18-20 percent protein. For puppies and kittens, offer commercial puppy or kitten food. If commercial feed is too expensive, make a homemade mix: for calves, 50 percent maize meal, 30 percent soybean meal, 15 percent wheat bran, 5 percent minerals and vitamins. This is not as good as commercial starter but is better than nothing. For piglets, 50 percent maize, 30 percent soybean, 15 percent fish meal, 5 percent minerals. For puppies and kittens, do not make homemade food; use commercial food. Eighth, record keeping. Record weaning date, weight, feed intake, and any health problems. This helps you improve next time. Ninth, safety. Never wean an animal that is sick. Never wean during extreme heat or cold. Never wean and transport at the same time. Never wean and mix with new animals at the same time. Always provide clean water and clean feed. Always monitor. Safe weaning is gradual, clean, and stress-free.
