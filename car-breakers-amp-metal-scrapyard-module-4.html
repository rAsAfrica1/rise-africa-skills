Lessons

1. Basic tools for dismantling: wrenches, sockets, and cutting torches

To dismantle a car safely and efficiently, you need the right tools. Start with a basic socket set: 1/4-inch drive for small bolts (8-12mm), 3/8-inch for most engine and body bolts (10-19mm), and 1/2-inch for stubborn suspension and chassis bolts (17-24mm). In Africa, many scrapyards use second-hand tools from markets like Kariakoo in Tanzania or Ladipo in Nigeria. Buy a set of combination wrenches (8-22mm) and a breaker bar for rusted nuts. Penetrating oil like WD-40 is essentialspray and wait 10 minutes before turning.

For cutting, an angle grinder with a 4.5-inch cutting disc (1mm thick) is the most versatile tool. Use it to cut exhaust pipes, seized bolts, and body panels. Always wear safety goggles and gloves. A reciprocating saw (like a Sawzall) with a metal blade is faster for thick sections. If you have access to oxy-acetylene cutting torches, they cut through 10mm steel in seconds, but they are expensive and require training. In many African yards, a simple hacksaw with a 24-teeth-per-inch blade works for small pipes.

Lifting tools matter: a 2-ton hydraulic jack and axle stands are minimum. For engines, a engine hoist (cherry picker) is ideal, but a chain block and a strong tree branch can work in rural areas. Always support the vehicle on stands before crawling underneath.

Organize your tools in a bucket or a wooden box. Keep a magnetic tray for boltslosing a 14mm bolt can stop a job. For electrical connectors, a set of plastic trim removal tools (or a flat screwdriver wrapped in tape) prevents broken clips. A multimeter (cheap ones cost $10) helps test alternators and starters before removal. Finally, keep a notebook and marker to label parts as you gothis saves hours later.

2. Removing high-demand parts: alternators, starters, and radiators

Alternators, starters, and radiators are the fastest-selling used parts in African scrapyards. Learn to remove them without damage.

**Alternator:** Disconnect the battery negative terminal first. Remove the drive belt by loosening the tensioner (usually a 14mm or 17mm bolt). Unplug the electrical connector (press the tab) and remove the main power wire (10mm nut). Then remove the mounting boltstypically two 14mm bolts. If seized, apply penetrating oil and tap with a hammer. On Toyota Corollas (common in Africa), the alternator often comes out from the top after removing the air intake. Test with a multimeter: a good alternator should show 13.5-14.5 volts when spinning.

**Starter:** Disconnect the battery. Remove the negative cable from the starter solenoid (12mm or 13mm nut). Then remove the positive cable (also 12mm). Unbolt the starterusually two 14mm boltsand pull it out. On many Japanese cars, you may need to remove the intake manifold bracket. A starter that clicks but doesn't crank is often just worn brushes; you can sell it as a rebuildable core for $15-30.

**Radiator:** Drain the coolant into a bucket (1.5-2 gallons). Disconnect the upper and lower hoses (use pliers on the clamps). Remove the fan shroud (10mm bolts). Unbolt the radiator mounts (usually 10mm or 12mm). Lift it out carefullydo not bend the fins. A used radiator for a Toyota Hilux sells for $40-80 in Kenya or Ghana. Pressure test it with a radiator tester (or just fill with water and look for leaks).

Always label the bolts in a bag with the part name. Store alternators and starters indoors to prevent rust. Radiators should be stored upright, not flat, to avoid bending the core.

3. Extracting engines and transmissions for resale or rebuild

Pulling an engine and transmission is the biggest job in a scrapyard, but it pays wella used Toyota 2E engine sells for $300-500 in West Africa, and a 5-speed manual gearbox for $150-250. Follow this method.

First, drain all fluids: engine oil (10-15 liters), coolant (5-7 liters), and transmission oil (2-3 liters). Remove the battery and the hood (bonnet). Label every wire and hose you disconnect with masking tape and a markertake photos with your phone if you have one. Disconnect the fuel lines (clamp them to prevent spillage), throttle cable, and all electrical connectors (injectors, sensors, alternator, starter). Remove the radiator and fan.

Support the transmission with a jack or a strong strap. Remove the driveshaft (4 bolts at the differential, 4 at the transmission). For front-wheel-drive cars, remove the CV axles (32mm nut at the hub, then pry out). Unbolt the engine mountsusually two on the sides and one on the transmission. On a rear-wheel-drive car, you can pull the engine and transmission together as one unit. Use an engine hoist (cherry picker) rated at 1 ton. If no hoist, use a chain block and a sturdy tree branchbut never get under the load.

Lift slowly, checking for anything still attached. Once out, separate the transmission from the engine (remove the bellhousing boltsusually 6 to 8 bolts, 14mm or 17mm). Store the engine on a tire or wooden pallet, not directly on the ground. For rebuilds, sell the engine as a core if it has a blown head gasketrebuilders pay $100-200. A good running engine can be sold as-is. Keep the ECU and wiring harness with the enginethey add value. Label the engine with its make, model, and year using a paint marker.

4. Salvaging interior components: seats, dashboards, and electronics

Interior parts are lightweight, easy to remove, and sell quickly. A clean set of seats for a Toyota Hiace can fetch $80-150 in Uganda or Zambia. Dashboards, radios, and ECUs are also in demand.

**Seats:** Remove the four bolts holding each seat (usually 14mm or 17mm). For front seats, slide them back to access the rear bolts, then forward for the front. Disconnect any electrical connectors under the seat (for power seats or airbag sensors). Lift the seat outtwo people make it easier. Check for tears or stains; clean with soap and water. Store seats indoors to prevent sun damage.

**Dashboards:** This is delicate. Remove the negative battery cable first. Take out the glovebox and lower panels (Phillips screws). Unbolt the steering column (4 bolts, 12mm) and lower it. Disconnect all wiring harnesses behind the dashboardlabel them. Remove the radio (special tools or two U-shaped wires). The dashboard is held by 6-10 bolts (10mm) along the top and sides. Pull it out slowly; it may catch on the heater core. A cracked dashboard is still sellable for $20-50; a perfect one for $100+.

**Electronics:** The ECU (engine control unit) is usually behind the glovebox or under the passenger seat. Unplug it carefullynever pull the wires. Radios, amplifiers, and speakers are easy to remove. Test them with a 12V battery before selling. Airbags are dangerousdo not remove them unless you are trained; sell the car with airbags intact or call a professional. Keep small parts like knobs, switches, and fuses in labeled zip-lock bags. A complete interior from a popular car can earn you $200-400 if sold as a set.

5. Removing body panels, doors, and windows without damage

Body panels, doors, and windows are high-value itemsa used door for a Toyota Hilux sells for $100-200 in Nigeria, and a windshield for $50-150. But they damage easily. Follow these steps.

**Doors:** Open the door and remove the interior trim panel (clips or screws). Disconnect the wiring harness for the window motor, lock, and speakersthere is usually a rubber boot between the door and body; pull it back and unplug the connector. Remove the door hinge bolts (usually 12mm or 14mm, two per hinge). Have a helper hold the door while you remove the last bolt. Never let it dropbent hinges make the door worthless. Label the door with its position (front left, rear right) using masking tape.

**Body panels (fenders, hoods, bumpers):** For bolt-on panels, remove all bolts (10mm, 12mm, or 14mm). For welded panels, use a spot weld cutter (drill bit) or an angle grinder to cut the weldsbut this damages the panel. Only do this if the panel is rare. For plastic bumpers, unclip the sides and remove the bolts underneath. Store panels flat or standing on edge, not leaning, to avoid warping.

**Windows:** For side windows, remove the door trim and unbolt the window regulator (3-4 bolts, 10mm). Slide the glass out. For windshields, use a windshield removal tool (a wire with handles) or a cold chisel and hammerbut this often cracks the glass. A better method: use a utility knife to cut the urethane seal, then push the glass out from inside with your feet (wear shoes). Two people are needed. If the glass breaks, sweep up immediatelyglass shards are dangerous. Sell windshields only if they have no cracks or chips. Store them upright in a rack with cardboard between them.

6. Organizing and labeling removed parts for easy inventory

A disorganized scrapyard loses money. You forget what you have, parts get damaged, and customers leave because you can't find anything. A simple system fixes this.

**Labeling:** Use a permanent marker or paint pen to write on the part itselfmake, model, year, and part name. For example: "Toyota Corolla 2005 Alternator". For small parts, put them in a zip-lock bag with a paper label inside. For bolts, tape them to the part or put them in a bag labeled with the part name. Never rely on memory.

**Shelving and storage:** Build wooden shelves or use metal racks (old bed frames work). Keep heavy parts (engines, transmissions) on the floor on pallets. Medium parts (alternators, starters, radiators) on middle shelves. Small parts (ECUs, sensors, bolts) in labeled plastic bins on top shelves. Group by system: engine parts together, transmission parts together, body parts together. For doors and panels, build a rack with vertical slotslike a file cabinetso they don't fall.

**Inventory log:** Keep a notebook (or a simple Excel sheet on a phone) with columns: Part name, Make/Model, Year, Condition, Price, Location (shelf number). Update it every time you add or sell a part. In Africa, many successful scrapyards use a chalkboard on the wall listing high-demand parts and prices. Take photos of expensive parts and keep them on your phoneyou can send them to customers via WhatsApp.

**Security and weather:** Cover engines and electronics with tarps to keep dust and rain off. Store alternators and starters indoors if possible. Use old engine oil to wipe bare metal partsit prevents rust for months. Check your inventory monthly and re-label anything faded. A well-organized yard can double your sales because customers trust you and return.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Dismantling and Parts Removal

In the African auto-recycling business, dismantling is not a demolition job it is inventory creation. Every hour spent removing an alternator, radiator, or dashboard cleanly and labelling it correctly converts a wreck into sellable stock for mechanics, panel beaters, and rebuilders across your city and beyond. This lesson gives you a practical, business-first reference for dismantling and parts removal in a commercial scrapyard, with methods that work with the tools available in most African workshops.

Your economics are simple: a vehicle bought for scrap value can yield 310 times its purchase price if high-demand parts are removed intact, tested, cleaned, and stored properly. The difference between a profitable yard and a break-even yard is usually technique and organisation, not equipment.

1. Basic tools for dismantling: wrenches, sockets, and cutting torches

Start with a core toolkit. In most African yards, a 1/2-inch drive socket set (824 mm), combination wrenches (824 mm), adjustable wrenches, a breaker bar, screwdrivers (flat and Phillips), pliers, a hacksaw, a hammer, and a pry bar cover 80% of jobs. Add a torque wrench for engine and suspension work, and a reciprocating saw or angle grinder for seized bolts. A cutting torch (oxy-acetylene) is powerful but should be a last resort it destroys resale value on any part you cut through.

Business tip: buy impact sockets and a cordless impact wrench if budget allows. An impact wrench removes rusted bolts in seconds rather than minutes, and time saved is money earned. In many African markets, Chinese and Indian tool brands (e.g., Total, Ingco, Bosch DIY range) offer acceptable quality at a fraction of premium prices.

2. Removing high-demand parts: alternators, starters, and radiators

These three parts are the bread and butter of any African scrapyard. Alternators and starters are rebuilt locally in most cities and sell fast; radiators are in constant demand because of heat and poor roads.

Alternator: Disconnect the battery first. Remove the drive belt (release tensioner or loosen adjustment bolt). Unbolt the mounting bracket and disconnect the electrical connector and charge wire. Label the wire positions with tape before removal.

Starter: Disconnect the battery. Remove the heat shield if present. Unbolt the starter (usually 23 bolts) and disconnect the solenoid wires. Keep the bolts with the starter in a labelled bag.

Radiator: Drain coolant into a container (recycle or sell to radiator repairers). Disconnect hoses, fan shroud, and transmission cooler lines if automatic. Lift the radiator straight up to avoid bending the core. Store upright.

Business tip: Test alternators and starters before selling. A simple bench test with a battery and multimeter takes 5 minutes and lets you sell "tested working" at a 3050% premium over "untested."

3. Extracting engines and transmissions for resale or rebuild

Engines and gearboxes are your highest-value items often worth more than the rest of the vehicle combined. In many African markets, a used Toyota 2KD or Nissan QD32 engine can sell for the equivalent of 36 months of yard labour costs.

Preferred method (engine hoist): Remove the bonnet, disconnect battery, drain fluids, remove radiator and fan, disconnect wiring harness (label every connector), remove exhaust manifold and drive shaft, unbolt engine mounts and bell housing, then lift slowly with an engine crane. Keep the crane level and use a load leveller if available.

Field method (no crane): Use a chain block anchored to a sturdy overhead beam or a reinforced A-frame. Never work under a suspended engine. In smaller yards, some operators drop the engine and gearbox together from below after removing the front crossmember this is faster but requires care not to damage the oil pan or sump.

Transmissions: Support the gearbox with a jack before unbolting the crossmember. Remove the drive shaft, clutch slave cylinder, and speedometer cable. Lower carefully. For automatics, keep the torque converter with the gearbox and plug the fluid lines to prevent leakage.

4. Salvaging interior components: seats, dashboards, and electronics

Interior parts are increasingly profitable in Africa, especially for popular models (Toyota, Nissan, Isuzu, Hyundai, Kia). Dashboards, instrument clusters, ECUs, radios, airbags (handle with care), and seats all have resale value.

Seats: Unbolt the four mounting bolts and disconnect any wiring (seatbelt sensors, electric adjustment). Remove carefully torn seat covers reduce value by 50%.

Dashboard: Remove the steering wheel, instrument cluster, and centre console first. Then unbolt the dashboard frame. Label and bag every screw and clip buyers pay more for complete assemblies with hardware.

Electronics: The ECU, body control module, radio, and sensors are high-value. Remove the ECU last after disconnecting the battery and waiting 10 minutes (to avoid airbag deployment risk). Store electronics in anti-static bags or dry boxes.

5. Removing body panels, doors, and windows without damage

Body panels and doors are bulky but profitable, especially for panel beaters who prefer original panels over aftermarket copies.

Panels: Locate all bolts, clips, and spot welds. Use a plastic trim tool or flat screwdriver wrapped in tape to avoid scratching paint. Drill out spot welds with a spot-weld drill bit do not chisel them off, as this deforms the panel.

Doors: Support the door with a jack or a helper before unbolting the hinges. Disconnect the wiring loom through the rubber boot. Label the door (front left, rear right, etc.).

Windows: For windshields and rear glass, use a windshield removal wire or a cold knife. For side windows, lower the glass and unbolt the regulator. Store glass upright on a padded rack never flat, as it cracks under its own weight.

6. Organising and labelling removed parts for easy inventory

This is where most African scrapyards lose money. A part without a label is a part you cannot sell quickly. Adopt a simple system:

  • Tag every part with vehicle make, model, year, engine size, part name, and condition.
  • Use a numbering system (e.g., T-ALT-2024-001 for Toyota alternator).
  • Photograph each part and store photos on a phone or computer for WhatsApp/Facebook sales.
  • Store by category in labelled bays: engines, electrical, body, interior, fluids.
  • Keep a stock book or spreadsheet with date in, price, and date out.

In markets where buyers come from neighbouring countries (e.g., Uganda, Rwanda, DRC, Zambia), a well-labelled yard with a WhatsApp catalogue can sell parts before the buyer even arrives.

SAFETY WARNING READ BEFORE YOU START

Always disconnect the battery before removing electrical parts. Never work under a vehicle supported only by a jack use axle stands. Drain all fluids (fuel, oil, coolant, AC gas) before cutting or grinding. Fuel vapours and refrigerant gases are explosive and toxic. Wear gloves, safety glasses, and steel-toe boots. When using a cutting torch, keep a fire extinguisher within reach and never cut near the fuel tank or brake lines. In African yards, heat exhaustion is a real risk work in shade, drink water, and take breaks.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Photograph the vehicle and record make, model, year, and VIN before touching anything.
  2. Disconnect the battery and remove it first store safely.
  3. Drain all fluids into labelled containers for recycling or resale.
  4. Remove high-value parts first: alternator, starter, radiator, ECU, radio.
  5. Label every part immediately with vehicle details and condition.
  6. Bag and tag all bolts, clips, and brackets with the part they belong to.
  7. Store parts by category in dry, secure bays engines upright, glass vertical.
  8. Update your stock book or spreadsheet the same day.
