Module 5

Roofs, Rain Protection and Bee Access

A fixed hive cannot be moved, so the roof cannot be replaced with a new building. It must be designed for the weather for the life of the hive. This module is the roof and the rain.

Module 5 of 12Written methodKnowledge check + capstone
By the end of this module you will be able to
  • Design a removable roof that keeps rain out for decades
  • Choose between metal roof, concrete slab, and timber roof
  • Provide bee access without inviting robbers
  • Manage ventilation and condensation under the roof
  • Design a rain-shadow drainage system around the hive
  • Complete the roof and rain protection for a masonry hive

Lesson 5.1

The three roof types

TypeWeightLifespanCostBest for
Metal on timber frame5–15 kg15–25 yearsLowMost hives; easily lifted by one or two people
Concrete slab50–120 kg40+ yearsMediumHives inspected infrequently; needs two people to lift
Corrugated metal on brick gable10–25 kg20 yearsMediumLong apiary rows; wind-exposed sites

For most fixed hives, corrugated metal on a timber frame is the correct choice. Light, cheap, easy to lift, and adequate lifespan.

Design principles

  • Overhang 200 mm on all four sides — more than a wooden hive because a masonry hive cannot be moved to a new position if the roof fails.
  • Slope 5° for rain runoff. Not flat.
  • Metal covering (galvanised or aluminium). Wrapped and stapled over the timber frame.
  • Ventilation gap of 20 mm between top bars and the underside of the roof.
  • Lifting handles on both ends — a bar or a rope to lift the roof when it is set aside.

Lesson 5.2

Roof construction

Frame

  • Timber frame: 4 boards, 20 mm thick, forming a rectangle 20 mm larger than the hive external dimensions.
  • Cross bracing: one or two centre battens to prevent sagging.
  • Height of the frame: 60–80 mm. Sits over the top of the walls, covering the top-bar notches.

Metal covering

  • Galvanised iron sheet, gauge 30 or thicker.
  • Overhangs the frame by 100 mm on all sides — total 200 mm over the hive walls.
  • Folded down at the edges and around corners to prevent wind lifting.
  • Fixed with roofing screws through the crown of the corrugation — not the valley.
  • Every screw with a sealing washer.

Aluminium alternative

Aluminium sheet does not rust, lasts longer, is lighter — but costs 2–3× more. Worth it in coastal areas or for a premium installation.

Concrete slab roof (alternative)

For a hive that will rarely be opened, a concrete slab roof is possible:

  • 100 mm thick concrete slab reinforced with a light steel mesh.
  • Cast in place with a slight slope for drainage.
  • Lifting handles cast in — steel bars or loops.
  • Two people required to lift.
  • Roof must not bear on the top bars — must rest on the walls only.

Lesson 5.3

Bee access and entrance design

Entrance size

A small entrance is easier for bees to defend against robbers, wasps, and other insects. Recommended for a masonry hive:

  • Width: 300–400 mm. Enough for the colony's traffic.
  • Height: 15–20 mm. Bees pass freely; larger intruders cannot.
  • Position: at the base of one end wall, sloping slightly down toward the outside so rain runs out, not in.

Entrance reducer

A removable wooden or metal bar that can narrow the entrance to 50 mm × 10 mm in robbing season, or in cold weather. Slots into position at the front of the entrance.

Alighting board

A small platform outside the entrance — 100 mm wide, 200 mm long — helps loaded bees land. Optional but improves traffic flow.

Guard against larger intruders

  • Rodents — the 15–20 mm entrance height is enough to block rats and mice.
  • Honey badgers, snakes — masonry walls are enough. Wooden hives are vulnerable; masonry is not.
  • Humans — the entrance is small; thieves cannot easily reach in.

Lesson 5.4

Ventilation and condensation

Masonry holds moisture. Without ventilation, the interior of a masonry hive stays damp and mouldy, which the colony cannot tolerate for long.

Ventilation openings

  • Two slots at the top of the walls — 200 × 10 mm, meshed, on opposite sides.
  • Roof ventilation gap — 20 mm between top bars and roof underside.
  • Optional roof vent — a small screened opening in the roof peak for hot climates.

Condensation control

  • Condensation forms on the underside of the roof in cool, humid nights. It drips onto the cluster.
  • Solutions: an insulated ceiling (25 mm foam between frame and roof), or a ventilated inner cover that lets moist air escape.
  • In very humid areas, a thin layer of absorptive material (cloth, sisal) under the roof soaks up condensation and releases it slowly.

The 20 mm rule

Between the top of the top bars and the underside of the roof: 20 mm. Too little and bees propolise the roof shut. Too much and they build burr comb in the gap.

Lesson 5.5

Rain-shadow and drainage around a fixed hive

Rain hits the ground and splashes back up. For a masonry hive, that splash is a source of water ingress. Manage it:

Rain shadow

  • Roof overhang 200 mm creates a rain shadow that keeps rain off the walls.
  • Drip edge on the metal roof — a slight downward turn — prevents water clinging and running back under the roof.
  • Gutter and downpipe on the low side of the roof, discharging into the perimeter drain. Optional but valuable in high-rainfall areas.

Perimeter drain

  • V-shaped drain around the hive, 200 mm deep, 300 mm wide.
  • Drain runs downhill to a discharge point 3–5 m from the hive.
  • Do not let water pool anywhere within 1 m of the hive base.

Ground level

  • The hive base is at least 150 mm above surrounding ground.
  • Ground slopes away from the hive on all sides.
  • No vegetation touching the hive walls — grass is trimmed to 200 mm away.
The rain rule: a masonry hive cannot be moved to avoid a bad rainy season. The roof, overhang, rain shadow, and drainage must all work together to keep the hive interior dry — every season, for decades.

Knowledge check — Module 5

1. What is the correct roof overhang for a masonry hive?

Correct: 200 mm. More than a wooden hive because the hive cannot be moved if the roof fails.

2. What is the correct entrance height for a masonry hive?

Correct: 15–20 mm. Enough for bees, small enough to defend and to block rodents.

3. Why does a masonry hive need more ventilation than a wooden one?

Correct: moisture buildup. Block and brick absorb and release water. Without ventilation, the hive stays damp.

4. What causes condensation under the roof of a hive?

Correct: warm air meets cool roof. Solution: insulated ceiling or ventilated inner cover.

5. What is a rain shadow?

Correct: protected zone under the overhang. The reason a 200 mm overhang is worth building.
