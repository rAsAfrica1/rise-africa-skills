1. Spacing and Orchard Layout

Spacing determines how many avocado trees you can plant per hectare and how easy it will be to manage them. For most African smallholder conditions, a spacing of 5 meters by 5 meters (5m x 5m) is a good starting point. This gives you about 400 trees per hectare. If you want to intercrop with beans or maize during the first three years, you can widen to 6m x 6m, giving about 278 trees per hectare. Remember that avocado trees grow large; overcrowding reduces airflow, increases disease, and lowers yields.

Before planting, measure your land using a tape measure and pegs. Start by marking a baseline along one edge of the field. Use the 3-4-5 method to make a right angle: measure 3 meters along the baseline, 4 meters perpendicular, and adjust until the diagonal is 5 meters. This ensures your rows are straight. Then mark planting positions at your chosen spacing. Use wooden pegs or sticks painted white for visibility.

Consider the slope of your land. On gentle slopes, plant in straight rows. On steeper slopes (more than 10%), plant along the contour to reduce soil erosion. You can also use a triangular layout (also called hexagonal) where trees are staggered. This gives each tree more space and can increase yields per hectare by about 10%. For triangular spacing at 5m between trees, the distance between rows is about 4.33m.

Leave access paths every 20-30 meters so you can carry harvests and spray equipment. Also, keep avocado trees away from buildings and large trees that may shade them. Avocados need full sun for at least 6 hours daily. If you are planting different varieties for pollination, plan to put pollinizer trees in every third row. Good layout saves labour and boosts production.

2. Digging and Preparing Planting Holes

Proper hole preparation gives your avocado seedling a strong start. Dig holes at least 60 cm wide and 60 cm deep. If your soil is hard or rocky, make them 80 cm wide and 80 cm deep. Start digging at least one month before the rainy season so the soil has time to settle and any organic matter can decompose. Use a hoe, spade, and pickaxe if the ground is tough. Keep topsoil (the dark top 20-30 cm) separate from subsoil.

Mix the topsoil with well-rotted manure or compost. A good ratio is one wheelbarrow of manure to two wheelbarrows of topsoil. Add 200 grams of rock phosphate or bone meal per hole to support root development. If you have acidic soil (pH below 5.5), add 200 grams of agricultural lime. Mix everything thoroughly. Do not add fresh manureâit burns roots.

Refill the hole with the topsoil mixture first, then the subsoil. Leave a slight mound (about 10 cm high) at the surface because the soil will settle. Do not plant immediately; let the hole rest for at least two weeks after refilling. This allows the soil to settle and prevents the seedling from sinking too deep.

If you are planting on a slope, make a small basin on the downhill side to catch water. In dry areas, line the hole with plastic or banana leaves to conserve moisture. Test your soil drainage: fill the hole with water and see if it drains within 24 hours. If not, you may need to plant on raised mounds. Good hole preparation reduces transplant shock and gives roots room to grow.

3. Transplanting Seedlings

Transplant your avocado seedlings at the start of the rainy season when the soil is moist but not waterlogged. Choose a cloudy day or late afternoon to reduce heat stress. Water the seedling in its nursery bag thoroughly two hours before transplanting. This keeps the root ball intact.

Carefully cut the nursery bag away with a sharp knife or razor blade. Do not pull the seedling outâthat damages roots. If the seedling is in a biodegradable pot, you can plant it whole, but tear the bottom first. Inspect the roots: if they are circling the pot, gently loosen them with your fingers. If the taproot is coiled, trim it slightly with clean secateurs.

Place the seedling in the centre of the hole. The top of the root ball should be level with the surrounding soil, or slightly higher (2-3 cm) to allow for settling. Do not bury the graft unionâthe swollen part where the scion meets the rootstock. If buried, the tree may produce suckers and die. Backfill with the soil mixture, firming gently with your hands to remove air pockets. Do not stamp on the soil.

Water immediately with 10-20 litres of water, even if it is raining. This settles the soil around the roots. Create a small basin around the seedling to hold water. Mulch with dry grass, straw, or banana leaves, keeping mulch 10 cm away from the trunk to prevent rot. If the sun is intense, shade the seedling with a small branch or palm frond for the first two weeks. Monitor daily for wilting. Transplanting shock can last 7-10 days; keep the soil moist but not soggy.

4. Pollination and Pollinizer Varieties

Avocado trees have a unique flowering behaviour called protogynous dichogamy. Each flower opens twice: first as female (receptive to pollen) and later as male (shedding pollen). The timing depends on the variety, which is classified as Type A or Type B. Type A varieties (e.g., Hass, Pinkerton) open as female in the morning and male in the afternoon. Type B varieties (e.g., Fuerte, Bacon) open as female in the afternoon and male the next morning. For good pollination, you need both types in the orchard.

Plant at least one pollinizer tree for every 8 to 10 main crop trees. For example, if you plant Hass (Type A), interplant with Fuerte or Bacon (Type B). Place pollinizer rows every third row, or plant a pollinizer every 10th tree within a row. Pollinizers should be clearly marked so you don't remove them during pruning.

Bees are the main pollinators. Encourage them by planting flowering plants like sunflower, lavender, or basil between rows. Avoid spraying insecticides during flowering. If you have few bees, you can hand-pollinate using a small soft brush, but this is labour-intensive. In most African orchards, natural pollinators are sufficient if you have both types and avoid harsh chemicals.

Wind also helps, but avocado pollen is heavy and not carried far. Therefore, pollinizers must be within 30 meters of the main variety. If you have a small plot, you can graft a branch of a Type B variety onto a Type A tree. This saves space. Remember that some varieties like Hass produce very little pollen, so a dedicated pollinizer is essential. Good pollination increases fruit set by up to 30%.

5. Initial Training and Staking

After transplanting, your avocado seedling needs training to develop a strong framework. Start by removing any side shoots that appear below the graft union. These are suckers from the rootstock and will never produce good fruit. Also remove any flowers or small fruits in the first yearâthis allows the tree to put energy into root and leaf growth.

Stake the seedling if it is tall or in a windy area. Use a sturdy wooden stake (2-3 meters long) or bamboo. Drive it into the ground 30 cm away from the trunk to avoid damaging roots. Tie the tree to the stake with soft cloth or rubber strips in a figure-eight loop. This allows the trunk to move slightly but prevents it from falling. Check ties every month and loosen them as the trunk grows.

Prune to shape the tree. In the first year, select 3-4 strong branches that will form the main scaffold. Remove any crossing or weak branches. Cut them back to a bud facing outward. The goal is an open centre so sunlight reaches all parts. Do not prune heavily in the first year; just remove what is necessary.

Remove weeds around the base and keep a clear circle of 1 meter diameter. Mulch again after pruning. If the tree is grafted, ensure the graft union remains 15-20 cm above the soil. If it sinks, lift the tree gently and replant. Training in the first two years determines the tree's shape for decades. A well-trained tree is easier to harvest and spray.

6. Irrigation Setup After Planting

Young avocado trees need consistent moisture, especially in the first two years. In dry areas, irrigation is essential. The simplest method is a basin system: create a circular basin around each tree, 1 meter in diameter, with a raised edge. Fill the basin with 20-30 litres of water twice a week during dry spells. This is labour-intensive but cheap.

For larger orchards, install drip irrigation. Use a 16 mm drip line with emitters spaced 50 cm apart, delivering 2-4 litres per hour. Run the system for 1-2 hours every two days. Drip saves water and reduces weeds. You can connect it to a tank or a borehole. Add a filter to prevent clogging. If you cannot afford drip, use a hose or watering can.

Mulch heavily around the tree, leaving a 10 cm gap around the trunk. Mulch reduces evaporation by up to 70%. Use dry grass, straw, or wood chips. Avoid fresh manure as mulchâit can burn roots. In very dry areas, you can bury a plastic bottle with small holes near the roots and fill it weekly; water seeps out slowly.

Monitor soil moisture by digging 10 cm down. If it feels dry, water. Overwatering causes root rot, so ensure the soil drains well. In the rainy season, stop irrigation to avoid waterlogging. As trees mature, they need less frequent but deeper watering. By year three, you can water once a week. Always water in the morning or evening to reduce evaporation.
