1. Numbers 1-10

In Shona, the numbers from one to ten are the foundation for all counting. Start by memorising these: 1 imwe, 2 piri, 3 tatu, 4 ina, 5 shanu, 6 tanhatu, 7 nomwe, 8 sere, 9 pfumbamwe, 10 gumi. Notice that some numbers change depending on what you are counting. For example, when counting people, you say 'munhu mumwe' (one person) but for objects you might say 'chinhu chimwe' (one thing). The numbers themselves stay the same, but the noun class prefix changes. For everyday use, focus on the basic forms first. Practice by counting everyday items around you: 'mbudzi imwe' (one goat), 'mombe piri' (two cows), 'huku tatu' (three chickens). In markets like Mbare in Harare or Kariakoo in Dar es Salaam, you will hear these numbers used constantly. When buying tomatoes, a vendor might say 'madomasi mashanu' (five tomatoes). Listen carefully to how the numbers attach to the noun. To master 1-10, repeat them aloud daily. Write them on a piece of paper and stick it on your wall. Use your fingers to count: one finger for imwe, two for piri, and so on. Try counting your steps as you walk: 'imwe, piri, tatu...' up to gumi. Then start again. This repetition builds muscle memory. Also, learn the question 'Mangani?' which means 'How many?' If someone asks 'Mangani?' you can respond with the number. For example, if you have three oranges, you say 'matatu' (three). Remember that numbers are essential for bargaining. If a seller says 'gumi' (ten), you can counter with 'shanu' (five). Practice with a friend: one person holds up fingers, the other says the number in Shona. Do this for five minutes each day. Within a week, you will know 1-10 without thinking.

2. Numbers 11-20

Once you know 1-10, numbers 11-20 follow a simple pattern in Shona. For 11 to 19, you say 'gumi ne' (ten and) followed by the unit. So 11 is 'gumi neimwe' (ten and one), 12 is 'gumi nepiri' (ten and two), 13 is 'gumi netatu', 14 is 'gumi neina', 15 is 'gumi neshanu', 16 is 'gumi netanhatu', 17 is 'gumi nenomwe', 18 is 'gumi nesere', 19 is 'gumi nepfumbamwe'. For 20, the word is 'makumi maviri' (twenty). Notice that 'makumi' means 'tens' and 'maviri' means 'two' so literally 'two tens'. This pattern continues for all tens: 30 is 'makumi matatu', 40 is 'makumi mana', 50 is 'makumi mashanu', etc. But for this lesson, focus on 11-20. Practice by counting from 11 to 20 aloud: 'gumi neimwe, gumi nepiri, gumi netatu, gumi neina, gumi neshanu, gumi netanhatu, gumi nenomwe, gumi nesere, gumi nepfumbamwe, makumi maviri.' Repeat until fluent. Use real-life contexts: if you are at a bus terminus and the conductor says 'gumi neshanu' (15), that might be the fare in Zimbabwean dollars or rand. If you are counting people at a funeral, you might say 'vanhu gumi nemaviri' (twelve people). Note that when counting people, 'gumi nemaviri' is used, not 'gumi nepiri', because 'vanhu' (people) takes the 'va-' prefix. So you say 'vanhu gumi nemaviri' (twelve people). Similarly, for money, you might say 'madhora gumi nemashanu' (fifteen dollars). To practice, write down random numbers between 11 and 20 and say them in Shona. Then, listen to Shona radio stations or watch Shona news, and try to catch numbers in the 11-20 range. You can also play a game: toss a ball and count each toss in Shona from 11 to 20. This makes learning active and fun. After a few days, you will be comfortable with these numbers.

3. Tens and Hundreds

Counting in tens and hundreds is essential for handling money, measuring goods, and telling time. In Shona, tens are formed with 'makumi' (tens) plus the unit. For example, 10 is 'gumi', 20 is 'makumi maviri', 30 is 'makumi matatu', 40 is 'makumi mana', 50 is 'makumi mashanu', 60 is 'makumi matanhatu', 70 is 'makumi manomwe', 80 is 'makumi masere', 90 is 'makumi mapfumbamwe', and 100 is 'zana'. Notice that from 20 onwards, the word 'makumi' is used, and the unit follows. For 100, the word is 'zana'. For hundreds beyond 100, you combine 'zana' with the tens and units. For example, 200 is 'mazana maviri' (two hundreds), 300 is 'mazana matatu', 400 is 'mazana mana', 500 is 'mazana mashanu', 600 is 'mazana matanhatu', 700 is 'mazana manomwe', 800 is 'mazana masere', 900 is 'mazana mapfumbamwe', and 1000 is 'churu' (or 'churu chimwe'). To say a number like 345, you say 'mazana matatu nemakumi mana neshanu' (three hundreds, four tens, and five). This is similar to English but with 'ne' (and) connecting the parts. Practice by writing down numbers and saying them in Shona. For example, 456: 'mazana mana nemakumi mashanu netanhatu'. Start with round numbers: 10, 20, 30... up to 100. Then 200, 300... up to 1000. Then mix them. Use money: if something costs 250 rand, you say 'mazana maviri nemakumi mashanu'. In markets, you will hear prices like 'zana' (100) or 'mazana maviri' (200). When bargaining, you might say 'mazana maviri' and the seller might say 'mazana maviri nemakumi mashanu' (250). Knowing these numbers helps you avoid being overcharged. Also, when counting large quantities like bags of maize, you might say 'mazana matatu' (300 bags). To master this, practice counting by tens: 'gumi, makumi maviri, makumi matatu...' up to 'zana'. Then count by hundreds: 'zana, mazana maviri, mazana matatu...' up to 'churu'. Do this daily for a week. You can also use a calculator to check your pronunciation. With consistent practice, you will handle tens and hundreds with ease.

4. Counting Objects

Counting objects in Shona requires matching the number to the noun class of the object. Shona has different noun classes, and the number word changes its prefix accordingly. For example, when counting people (class 2), you say 'vanhu vaviri' (two people). For animals like goats (class 9), you say 'mbudzi mbiri' (two goats). For fruits like oranges (class 5), you say 'maranjisi maviri' (two oranges). For long objects like trees (class 3), you say 'miti miviri' (two trees). For small objects like stones (class 7), you say 'matombo maviri' (two stones). The key is to learn the noun class of the object and then use the appropriate number prefix. The numbers from one to five have different prefixes for each class, but from six onwards, the numbers are the same for all classes. For example, 'tanhatu' (six) is used for all classes. So, to count objects, first identify the noun class. Common classes: class 1 (people) 'munhu mumwe' (one person), 'vanhu vaviri' (two people); class 2 (plural of people) 'vanhu vaviri'; class 3 (trees) 'muti mumwe', 'miti miviri'; class 4 (plural of class 3) 'miti miviri'; class 5 (fruits) 'ranjisi rimwe', 'maranjisi maviri'; class 6 (plural of class 5) 'maranjisi maviri'; class 7 (small objects) 'chinhu chimwe', 'zvinhu zviviri'; class 8 (plural of class 7) 'zvinhu zviviri'; class 9 (animals) 'mbudzi imwe', 'mbudzi mbiri'; class 10 (plural of class 9) 'mbudzi mbiri'. Practice by picking up objects around you and counting them. For example, count your fingers: 'minwe miviri' (two fingers). Count books: 'mabhuku maviri' (two books). Count chairs: 'zvigaro zviviri' (two chairs). At the market, count tomatoes: 'madomasi mashanu' (five tomatoes). Count maize cobs: 'chibage chimwe' (one maize cob), 'chibage zviviri' (two maize cobs). To get the noun class right, listen to how native speakers count. If unsure, you can often use the generic 'chinhu' (thing) and say 'zvinhu zviviri' (two things). But for fluency, learn the specific class prefixes. Make a list of common objects and their noun classes. Practice counting them daily. For example, count the number of plates in your house: 'ndiro imwe' (one plate), 'ndiro mbiri' (two plates). Count the number of cups: 'mukombe mumwe' (one cup), 'mikombe miviri' (two cups). This hands-on practice will make counting objects natural.

5. Telling Time Basics

Telling time in Shona is straightforward once you know numbers. The basic question is 'Inguvai?' which means 'What time is it?' The answer starts with 'I' (it is) followed by the hour and minutes. Shona uses a 12-hour clock, but you can specify morning, afternoon, or evening. For example, 7:00 is 'I7' or 'Inguva yechinomwe' (the seventh hour). 8:00 is 'I8' or 'Inguva yesere'. To say 7:30, you say 'I7:30' or 'Hafu pashure pa7' (half past 7). But more commonly, you say '7 nehafu' (7 and a half). For 7:15, you say '7 nechikamu chimwe chechina' (7 and a quarter) or simply '7:15'. For 7:45, you say '8 asara maminitsi gumi nemashanu' (15 minutes to 8) or '7:45'. In everyday conversation, people often use English numbers for time, but you can use Shona numbers. For example, 'Iva nemaminitsi mashanu' means 'It is five minutes'. To ask 'At what time?' you say 'Nguvai?' or 'Ini nga ndione' (let me see). Practice by looking at a clock and saying the time in Shona. Start with whole hours: 1:00 'I1' or 'Inguva yekutanga'; 2:00 'I2' or 'Inguva yechipiri'; 3:00 'I3' or 'Inguva yechitatu'; 4:00 'I4' or 'Inguva yechina'; 5:00 'I5' or 'Inguva yeshanu'; 6:00 'I6' or 'Inguva yetanhatu'; 7:00 'I7' or 'Inguva yechinomwe'; 8:00 'I8' or 'Inguva yesere'; 9:00 'I9' or 'Inguva yepfumbamwe'; 10:00 'I10' or 'Inguva yegumi'; 11:00 'I11' or 'Inguva yegumi neimwe'; 12:00 'I12' or 'Inguva yegumi nembiri'. For minutes, you say the hour plus 'namaminitsi' (and minutes). For example, 5:20 is '5 namaminitsi makumi maviri' (5 and twenty minutes). 5:40 is '6 asara maminitsi makumi maviri' (20 minutes to 6). In rural areas, people often use the sun's position: 'Mangwanani' (morning), 'Masikati' (afternoon), 'Manheru' (evening). But for precise time, use the clock. Practice by setting a clock to random times and saying them in Shona. Also, listen to Shona radio for time announcements. Soon, you will tell time confidently.

6. Using Numbers in Daily Life

Numbers are everywhere in daily life, and using them in Shona will make you more fluent and confident. Start with shopping. In a market, you ask 'Imarii?' (How much?) and the vendor replies with a number. For example, 'Mazana maviri' (200). You can bargain: 'Ndingakupa zana' (I can give you 100). If you are buying multiple items, you say 'Ndinoda matatu' (I want three). At the bus rank, you ask 'Imarii kuenda kuHarare?' (How much to Harare?) and the conductor says 'Mazana mashanu' (500). You pay and say 'Heino mazana mashanu' (Here is 500). When giving directions, you use numbers: 'Famba mamita zana' (Walk 100 metres), 'Tendeuka kuruboshwe' (Turn left), 'Ipa makiromita mashanu' (It's five kilometres). In cooking, you measure with numbers: 'Kapu imwe yehupfu' (One cup of mealie meal), 'Mazai maviri' (Two eggs), 'Mvura ina' (Four waters). At home, you count family members: 'Mhuri yedu ine vanhu vatanhatu' (Our family has six people). You tell your age: 'Ndine makore makumi maviri' (I am 20 years old). You count money: 'Ndine madhora zana' (I have 100 dollars). You use numbers for phone numbers: 'Nomboro yangu ndeye 0771 234 567' but in Shona, you might say 'Zvirongwa zviviri, zvinomwe, zvinomwe, imwe...' (digits). In farming, you count bags of fertiliser: 'Mabhegi mashanu' (Five bags). You count livestock: 'Mombe gumi' (Ten cattle). In health, you say 'Ndine makore makumi matatu' (I am 30 years old) at the clinic. You measure medicine: 'Mishonga miviri' (Two pills). To practice, make a daily habit of using Shona numbers in real situations. When you buy something, say the price in Shona. When you count items, do it in Shona. When you tell time, do it in Shona. Keep a small notebook and write down numbers you encounter. For example, today you might write: 'Tomato madomasi mashanu 50c'. Review your notes. Also, practice with a friend: one person says a number in English, the other translates to Shona quickly. This builds speed. Over time, using numbers in daily life will become automatic.

<h2>Practical Reference: Numbers and Counting in Shona for African Agribusiness</h2>

<p>In Zimbabwe and across the wider Shona-speaking business corridor, number fluency is not a cultural nicety it is a commercial tool. Whether you are buying maize from smallholder farmers in Mashonaland, negotiating cattle prices in Masvingo, or reconciling stock in a Bulawayo hardware store, the ability to count, quote, and verify numbers in Shona reduces errors, builds trust, and closes deals faster. This Practical Reference module consolidates the six lessons in this course into a working toolkit for entrepreneurs, traders, and agribusiness operators.</p>

<p>The Shona language is spoken by roughly 10.7 million people, primarily in Zimbabwe, with significant communities in Mozambique, Zambia, and South Africa (Ethnologue, 2023). In agribusiness, numbers appear in every transaction: bags of fertiliser, litres of diesel, kilograms of seed, head of cattle, and rand or USD totals. Learning to handle these numbers in Shona is a direct investment in market access.</p>

<h4 style="margin:0 0 8px;color:#c62828;"> SAFETY WARNING: Number Errors in Agrochemical Handling</h4>
<p style="margin:0;font-size:14px;color:#333;">Misreading or miscommunicating numbers in Shona can be dangerous when handling agricultural chemicals. A confusion between <strong>gumi</strong> (10) and <strong>zana</strong> (100) in pesticide dilution, or between <strong>maviri</strong> (2) and <strong>matatu</strong> (3) in millilitres per litre, can lead to crop failure, poisoning, or fatality. Always confirm quantities in writing and repeat the number back in Shona and English before mixing or applying any agrochemical. Where labels are in English only, translate aloud with a second worker present. Never rely on memory alone when measuring paraquat, dimethoate, or any restricted-use substance.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<p>Use this checklist before any Shona-language transaction involving numbers. It is designed for busy traders who need to verify quickly and move on.</p>
<ul style="font-family:Arial,sans-serif;font-size:14px;line-height:1.8;">
<li><strong>Confirm the noun class</strong> before counting cattle use <em>imwe, mbiri, nhatu</em>, not <em>motsi, piri, tatu</em>.</li>
<li><strong>Repeat the number back</strong> in Shona and English to eliminate ambiguity in noisy markets.</li>
<li><strong>Write the figure down</strong> spoken Shona numbers can sound similar (e.g., <em>shanu</em> vs <em>sere</em>).</li>
<li><strong>Check tens and hundreds</strong> carefully: <em>makumi mashanu</em> (50) is not <em>mazana mashanu</em> (500).</li>
<li><strong>Use hand signals</strong> alongside speech where literacy or hearing is a barrier.</li>
<li><strong>Verify time</strong> using both Shona and clock terms <em>nguva</em> (time), <em>awa</em> (hour), <em>miniti</em> (minute).</li>
<li><strong>For agrochemicals, always double-check</strong> with a second person and a written label.</li>
<li><strong>Record the transaction</strong> in a ledger immediately, using numerals, not words.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Telling Time and Daily Business Scheduling</h3>
<p>Time-telling in Shona traditionally follows the movement of the sun, but modern business uses clock time. Key terms include: <em>nguva</em> (time), <em>awa</em> (hour), <em>miniti</em> (minute), <em>mangwanani</em> (morning), <em>masikati</em> (afternoon), <em>manheru</em> (evening). A meeting at 8 AM is <em>nguva yemasere mangwanani</em>. A delivery at 3 PM is <em>nguva yematatu masikati</em>. In livestock auctions, the opening time is often announced as <em>nguva yepfumbamwe mangwanani</em> (9 AM). Being able to state and understand these times prevents missed deliveries and late payments both common causes of lost business in African agricultural supply chains.</p>

<h3>Using Numbers in Daily Business Life</h3>
<p>Numbers are embedded in every agribusiness function. When you negotiate the price of a tonne of maize, you say <em>churu chimwe</em> (one thousand). When you agree on a 10% deposit, you say <em>chikamu chegumi</em>. When you count stock at the end of the day, you use <em>kuverenga</em> (to count). When you forecast next season's planting, you use <em>kuronga</em> (to plan) with numbers. The trader who can move fluidly between Shona and English numbers has a measurable advantage: faster negotiations, fewer disputes, and stronger relationships with suppliers who may not speak English confidently.</p>
