1. Keeping your baking area clean and safe

A clean baking area is your first defence against sickness and spoilage. Start by sweeping floors and wiping tables before you begin. Use a mix of 1 tablespoon of bleach in 1 litre of water to sanitise surfaces. Keep a bucket of this solution and a clean cloth dedicated to food surfaces. Wash mixing bowls, spoons, and baking tins with hot soapy water, then rinse and air dry. Never use the same cloth for floors and tables.

Organise your space so raw cassava and finished bread do not mix. Have separate areas for peeling, grinding, and baking. If space is tight, work in batches: finish one step, clean up, then start the next. Store ingredients like flour, sugar, and yeast in sealed containers off the floor. Keep pests out by sealing cracks and using covered bins for waste. Empty waste bins daily.

Personal safety matters too. Keep a first aid kit with plasters, clean water, and antiseptic. Wear closed shoes to protect feet from hot trays and spills. Tie back hair and avoid loose clothing near flames. Have a fire extinguisher or a bucket of sand near the oven. Never leave hot oil or open flames unattended.

Train your workers on these rules. Post a simple checklist on the wall: wash hands, sanitise surfaces, check temperatures, store properly. Do a deep clean every week: scrub floors, wash curtains, and clean behind equipment. A clean bakery builds trustcustomers will return when they see you care about hygiene.

2. Safe handling of cassava to remove toxins

Cassava contains natural toxins called cyanogenic glycosides. If not removed, they can cause serious illness. The good news: proper processing removes almost all of it. Start by choosing sweet cassava varieties when possiblethey have less toxin. But even sweet cassava needs processing. Never eat raw cassava.

The most reliable method is soaking and fermentation. Peel the cassava, cut into chunks, and soak in clean water for at least 48 hours. Change the water every 12 hours. This allows enzymes to break down the toxins. After soaking, grind the cassava into a wet paste. Then press the paste to remove excess wateruse a clean cloth or a screw press. The liquid that comes out contains toxins, so discard it safely away from food areas.

Next, dry the pressed cassava. Spread it thinly on a clean mat or tray in the sun for 2-3 days, turning it daily until it is completely dry and crumbly. Alternatively, roast it in a dry pan over low heat for 20-30 minutes, stirring constantly, until it smells nutty and is dry. This heat further reduces toxins.

For bread, you will mix this processed cassava flour with other ingredients. Always taste a small amount of the processed flour firstit should not be bitter. If it is bitter, do not use it; repeat the soaking and drying. Remember: fermentation, pressing, and drying are not optional. Skipping steps can leave poison in your bread. Teach your workers these steps and supervise them until they are reliable.

3. Hygiene rules for you and your workers

Your hands carry germs that can make customers sick. Wash hands with soap and running water for at least 20 seconds before starting work, after using the toilet, after handling waste, and after touching your face or phone. Keep fingernails short and clean. No nail polish or fake nailsthey trap dirt. Cover any cuts with a waterproof plaster. If you have diarrhoea, vomiting, or fever, stay home. You can spread illness even if you feel slightly unwell.

Wear clean clothes and an apron. Tie back long hair or wear a cap. Avoid jewellery on hands and arms. If you sneeze or cough, turn away and cover your mouth with your elbow, then wash hands. Do not eat, drink, or smoke in the baking area. Spit and saliva carry bacteria.

Set up a handwashing station near the work area: a container with a tap, soap, and disposable paper towels or a clean cloth that is changed daily. If you have workers, train them on these rules and lead by example. Post simple pictures showing when to wash hands. Check hands regularlylook for dirt under nails or open sores.

Keep a clean uniform or apron for each worker. Wash aprons frequently. Provide a place to store personal items away from food. Sick workers should not handle food. Cross-training helps: if one worker is sick, another can step in. A healthy team bakes safe bread.

4. Preventing mold and spoilage in bread

Mold grows on bread when it is warm, damp, and exposed to air. To prevent it, cool bread completely before packaging. Never wrap warm breadtrapped steam creates moisture that encourages mold. Use clean, dry packaging: paper bags, plastic bags, or reusable containers. For longer shelf life, vacuum sealing or adding a desiccant packet can help, but these may be costly. A simple trick: add a slice of dry bread or a paper towel to absorb moisture.

Store bread in a cool, dry place away from sunlight. Ideal temperature is 18-22C. Do not store near onions, garlic, or strong-smelling foodsbread absorbs odours. If you live in a humid area, consider using a sealed container with a small amount of food-grade silica gel. Check bread daily for signs of mold: fuzzy green, white, or black spots. If you see mold, throw away the entire loaf. Do not cut off the moldy partmold roots may already be throughout the bread.

Spoilage also comes from bacteria and yeast. To reduce risk, keep your work area clean, use clean utensils, and avoid touching bread with bare hands after baking. Use tongs or clean gloves. If you sell at market, keep bread covered and out of direct sun. On hot days, sell quickly or keep in a cooler with ice packs.

For longer storage, you can dry bread into crumbs or freeze it. Freeze in airtight bags for up to 3 months. Thaw at room temperature, not in the sun. Always label with the date you baked. Use older bread first. A simple rule: if it smells strange or looks off, throw it out. Never sell spoiled breadit ruins your reputation and can make people sick.

5. Labeling products with dates and ingredients

A label tells customers what is in your bread and when it was made. It builds trust and helps you track quality. Every package should have: product name (e.g., Cassava Bread), your business name and contact, list of ingredients in order of weight (heaviest first), net weight, and date baked. Also include a 'best before' dateusually 3-5 days after baking for fresh bread, or up to 2 weeks for dry bread. If you use preservatives, list them. If not, say 'no preservatives'.

Write clearly with a permanent marker or print stickers. Use a date code like DD/MM/YYYY. For example: Baked: 15/06/2025, Best Before: 20/06/2025. If you sell at market, you can write on the bag or attach a small card. For wholesale, use printed labels. Keep a record of each batch: date, ingredients, and any problems. This helps if a customer complains.

Ingredients must be listed accurately. Common allergens like wheat, soy, or eggs must be declared. If your bread contains cassava, say so. If you add sugar, salt, or oil, list them. Do not make false claims like 'organic' unless certified. Honest labeling protects you from legal issues and keeps customers loyal.

Train workers to label every package. Check labels before selling. A missing date can lead to selling old bread. A missing ingredient can cause an allergic reaction. Use a simple notebook to record batches. If you have many products, use coloured stickers for different days. Labeling is not just a ruleit is good business.

6. Meeting local food safety requirements

Every country has food safety rules. In many African countries, you need a food handler's certificate, a business permit, and a health inspection. Visit your local council or health department to find out what applies to you. In Kenya, for example, you need a food hygiene certificate from the public health office. In Nigeria, you may need to register with NAFDAC if you package food. Ask questionsofficials are there to help.

Key requirements usually include: clean premises with handwashing facilities, safe water, proper waste disposal, and pest control. You must store food at safe temperatures and keep records of suppliers. Your workers may need medical checks for typhoid and other diseases. Inspectors will look at your kitchen, storage, and labels. They may take samples for testing. If you fail, you get a list of corrections and a deadline to fix them.

To prepare, keep a folder with your certificates, training records, and cleaning schedules. Do a self-inspection monthly using a checklist. Fix problems before an inspector arrives. For example, repair leaking pipes, replace cracked tiles, and ensure lights have covers. Keep a thermometer to check fridge and oven temperatures. Record these daily.

Meeting requirements may cost money, but it opens markets. Many shops and hotels only buy from certified producers. It also protects you from fines and closure. Join a local food safety workshop or ask a health officer to visit for advice. Work with other bakers to share costs, like buying a shared thermometer or paying for training. Safe food is everyone's business.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Food Safety & Quality Control for Cassava Bread Businesses

Cassava bread whether you call it farinha, gari bread, kpokpo garri loaf, or a composite wheatcassava pan loaf is one of the fastest-growing bakery categories in West, Central, and East Africa. It cuts your flour cost by 1530%, it uses local raw material, and it speaks to customers who want African food that is affordable and familiar. But it also carries two risks that wheat bread does not: cyanogenic compounds in poorly processed cassava, and rapid mould and spoilage in a moist, low-preservative product sold in hot, humid conditions.

This reference module is written for the owneroperator of a small or medium cassava bakery. It is not a laboratory manual. It is a set of decisions you can make this week that will (a) keep your customers alive and well, (b) keep you out of trouble with local food authorities, and (c) protect the reputation that lets you charge a fair price. Food safety is a business asset. A single poisoning incident or a mould complaint on social media can close a bakery faster than any competitor.

SAFETY WARNING CYANIDE (HCN) POISONING

Cassava roots contain linamarin, which releases hydrogen cyanide when eaten raw or under-processed. Never sell or taste raw cassava dough, half-baked cassava bread, or cassava flour that has not been fully fermented and dried. Symptoms of acute cyanide poisoning include headache, dizziness, vomiting, rapid breathing, and collapse these can appear within 30 minutes. Long-term consumption of under-processed cassava causes konzo, an irreversible paralysis, especially in children and women of childbearing age. If a customer or worker shows these symptoms after eating cassava bread, call emergency services immediately and keep the batch for testing. Always ferment grated cassava for at least 24 hours, press out the juice, dry to below 12% moisture, and bake to a core temperature of at least 90 C.

Quick Reference Checklist (post this on your bakery wall)

  1. Wash hands with soap and running water before touching cassava, dough, or baked bread and after using the toilet, handling waste, or touching your face.
  2. Ferment grated cassava for at least 24 hours in a clean, covered container. Never skip fermentation to save time.
  3. Press and drain cassava mash thoroughly; dispose of juice away from food preparation areas.
  4. Dry cassava flour to below 12% moisture (it should crumble, not clump, in your hand) before mixing dough.
  5. Bake to a core temperature of at least 90 C use a probe thermometer on at least one loaf per batch.
  6. Cool bread on raised racks until room temperature before packing. Never bag warm bread.
  7. Label every loaf with production date, best-before date (23 days for soft bread, 57 for dry), and full ingredient list.
  8. Clean and sanitise tables, mixers, and utensils at the start and end of every shift; keep waste bins covered and emptied daily.
