1. Pre-Slaughter Handling and Welfare

Proper pre-slaughter handling reduces stress, prevents injuries, and improves meat quality. In Africa, ostriches are often transported from farm to slaughter point. Use a well-ventilated truck with partitions to prevent birds from piling. Load at night or early morning when temperatures are below 25Â°C. Avoid rough handlingânever lift birds by the wings or neck. Use a hood or blindfold to calm them. Provide water but withdraw feed 12 hours before slaughter to reduce gut content and contamination risk.

On arrival, keep birds in a lairage (holding pen) with shade, clean bedding, and water for 2â4 hours to rest. Overcrowding causes heat stress and bruising. Allow 1.5 square meters per bird. Check for injuries; separate any bird with broken limbs or wounds.

Welfare matters for quality. Stressed birds produce dark, firm, dry meat with a high pH, which spoils faster and tastes poor. Also, stressed birds may damage hides and feathers, reducing by-product value.

Use calm, quiet handling. Move birds in small groups of 3â5 using a flag or stick, not electric prods. A well-designed race with solid sides prevents baulking.

Inspect birds for signs of diseaseâdo not slaughter sick birds for human consumption. Follow local veterinary regulations. If you see diarrhoea, nasal discharge, or lethargy, isolate and consult a vet.

Finally, plan slaughter for cool hours. In hot African climates, work before 10 AM or after 4 PM. Have all equipment ready: knives, stunning device, bleeding hooks, and scalding tanks. Being organised reduces stress on both birds and workers. Remember: humane handling is not just ethicalâit directly affects your profit from meat, leather, and feathers.

2. Slaughter Methods and Equipment

Ostrich slaughter requires specific methods to ensure quick death, proper bleeding, and worker safety. The most common method in Africa is stunning followed by bleeding. Use a captive bolt stunner (penetrating or non-penetrating) on the forehead. For ostriches, a .22 calibre blank cartridge captive bolt is effective. Aim at the intersection of lines from the eyes to the opposite ear. This renders the bird unconscious immediately.

Alternatively, electrical stunning can be used with 250â400 volts for 3â5 seconds. But this is less common due to equipment cost.

After stunning, immediately bleed the bird. Hang it by the legs using a shackle or hoist. Make a deep cut on the neck just behind the jaw, severing the carotid arteries and jugular veins. Bleed for 5â7 minutes. Collect blood in a container if you plan to use it for blood meal or fertiliser.

Essential equipment: captive bolt stunner with cartridges, sharp bleeding knives (15â20 cm blade), honing steel, shackles, hoist or gambrel, bleeding cone (optional), and a clean apron.

For small-scale operations, you can bleed without stunning if you are skilled, but this is less humane and may cause stress. Always ensure the bird is dead before further processingâcheck for no corneal reflex and no heartbeat.

After bleeding, proceed to skinning. Keep knives sharp; a dull knife tears hides and increases contamination. Sterilise knives between birds in 82Â°C water for 2 minutes.

Record slaughter weight, time, and any abnormalities. A typical adult ostrich yields 35â40 kg of meat, 1.2â1.5 square meters of hide, and 1.5â2 kg of feathers. Proper slaughter sets the stage for high-quality products.

3. Skinning and Leather Preservation

Ostrich hide is valuableâa single good hide can fetch $100â$300 in international markets. Skinning must be done carefully to avoid cuts. Start by removing the wings and legs at the joints. Then make a midline cut from the vent to the neck. Use a sharp skinning knife to separate the hide from the carcass, working downwards. Avoid nicking the hide; even small cuts reduce grade.

For the neck and legs, use a pulling motion with your hands and knife. The hide is thick, so take your time. Once removed, lay the hide flat on a clean surface.

Preservation begins immediately. Salt the hide with coarse salt (sodium chloride) at a rate of 1 kg salt per 1 kg hide weight. Rub salt into the flesh side, then fold the hide flesh-to-flesh and roll it. Store in a cool, shaded area for 24â48 hours.

Alternatively, you can air-dry the hide on a frame. Stretch it on a wooden frame with nails or ropes, flesh side out. Dry in the shade for 5â7 days until stiff. Avoid direct sun, which cracks the hide.

For better quality, use a salt- alum cure: mix 1 kg salt, 0.5 kg alum, and 0.1 kg borax in 10 litres water. Soak the hide for 24 hours, then dry. This prevents bacterial growth and makes the hide softer.

After drying, store hides in a dry, ventilated room away from rodents. Stack them flat with newspaper between layers. Never store wet hides togetherâthey will rot.

In Africa, many hides are lost to poor preservation. Invest in a simple drying rack and salt. Sell to tanneries or export directly. Good hides are used for boots, bags, and jackets. Quality pays.

4. Meat Cutting and Packaging

Ostrich meat is lean, red, and low in cholesterol. After bleeding and skinning, eviscerate the carcass: cut open the abdomen, remove intestines, stomach, heart, liver, and lungs. Keep the liver and heart for sale if healthy. Rinse the carcass with clean water (potable) and chill immediately to below 4Â°C.

Cutting: Ostrich has three main primal cutsâfan fillet (tender, from the thigh), round (from the hind leg), and drum (from the lower leg). Use a sharp boning knife. First, remove the legs and separate the thigh muscles. The fan fillet is the most expensive cut; trim it carefully. Cut the round into steaks or roasts. The drum is tougher; use for mince or stew.

Typical yields: fan fillet 2â3 kg, round 8â10 kg, drum 4â5 kg, and trimmings 5â7 kg. Total meat per bird: 35â40 kg.

Packaging: For local sale, wrap cuts in cling film or butcher paper. Label with cut name, weight, and date. For longer storage, vacuum-pack and freeze at -18Â°C. Frozen meat keeps 9â12 months.

In Africa, many small processors sell fresh meat within 24 hours. Use cool boxes with ice for transport. Never leave meat in the sun.

Hygiene is critical. Wash hands, knives, and surfaces with hot soapy water. Wear clean aprons and gloves. Keep raw meat away from cooked foods.

Value addition: Make mince, sausages, or biltong (dried meat). Biltong is popular in Southern Africa. Slice meat into strips, marinate in vinegar, salt, and spices, then air-dry for 3â5 days. Package in sealed bags. This extends shelf life and increases profit.

5. Feather Harvesting and Cleaning

Ostrich feathers are a valuable by-product, used in fashion, crafts, and dusters. A mature bird yields 1.5â2 kg of feathers. Harvest after slaughter, before scalding. Pluck the feathers by hand, pulling with the grain (from tail to head). The best feathers come from the wings and tailâthese are long and fluffy. Body feathers are shorter and used for fillers.

After plucking, separate feathers by size and quality. Wash them in warm water (40Â°C) with mild detergent. Gently agitate to remove dirt and oil. Rinse in clean water. Then disinfect by soaking in a solution of 1 tablespoon bleach per 4 litres of water for 10 minutes. Rinse again.

Drying: Lay feathers on a mesh screen in a shaded, well-ventilated area. Turn them daily. Drying takes 2â3 days. Do not use direct sunlightâit makes feathers brittle.

Once dry, sort into grades: Grade A (long, unbroken, clean), Grade B (slightly damaged), Grade C (short or dirty). Bundle Grade A feathers in groups of 50 and tie with string. Store in a dry box with mothballs to prevent insect damage.

In Africa, feathers are sold to craft markets, hat makers, or exported. Grade A ostrich feathers can sell for $0.50â$2 each. A bundle of 50 may fetch $25â$100.

You can also make feather dusters by attaching feathers to a wooden handle. These sell well locally.

Do not waste feathersâthey add income. Even damaged feathers can be used for compost or as protein meal after grinding. Proper cleaning and grading ensure top prices.

6. By-Product Utilization: Fat, Oil, and Bones

Ostrich slaughter yields more than meat. Fat, oil, and bones can be processed into valuable products.

Fat: Ostrich fat is rendered to make oil. Collect fat from the abdomen and around organs. Cut into small pieces and heat in a large pot at 80â100Â°C. Stir occasionally. The fat melts and separates from impurities. After 1â2 hours, strain through a cloth into clean containers. The resulting oil is rich in omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids. It is used in cosmetics, soaps, and traditional medicine. A single ostrich yields 2â4 kg of fat, producing 1.5â3 litres of oil. Bottle in dark glass and store in a cool place. Sell to cosmetic makers or use for leather conditioning.

Bones: After deboning, collect bones. They can be boiled to make bone broth, rich in collagen. Or dry and grind into bone meal, a high-phosphorus fertiliser. To make bone meal, boil bones for 2 hours, dry in sun for 3 days, then crush with a hammer or grinder. Sieve to fine powder. Apply to gardens at 100 g per square metre.

Blood: Collect blood during bleeding. Cook it or dry it to make blood meal, a nitrogen-rich fertiliser. Mix with bone meal for a balanced organic fertiliser.

Other by-products: The head, feet, and intestines can be cleaned and sold as traditional delicacies or pet food. The gizzard lining is used in some medicines.

In Africa, adding by-product processing increases income by 20â30%. Invest in a rendering pot, grinder, and drying racks. Sell oil, bone meal, and blood meal locally or to processors. Nothing should go to waste.

Ostriches are highly susceptible to stress. Poor handling 24â48 hours before slaughter causes glycogen depletion, leading to dark, firm, and dry (DFD) meat with a high pH. Ostrich feathers are graded by size, colour, and curl. White feathers from the wings and tail are most valuable.
