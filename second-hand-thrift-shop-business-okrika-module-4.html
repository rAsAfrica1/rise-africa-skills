Lessons

1. Washing and Sanitizing Second-Hand Clothes Safely

When you buy bales of okrika, the first step is to wash and sanitize every item before selling. This removes dirt, germs, and odors. Start by sorting clothes by color: whites, lights, and darks. Never mix a red shirt with white ones, or you will end up with pink whites. For a standard load, use a large plastic basin or a washing machine if you have one. Fill it with cold water. Add one cup of powdered detergent and half a cup of disinfectant like Dettol or a local bleach (e.g., Jik) for whites only. For colored clothes, use a color-safe disinfectant like Savlon. Let the clothes soak for 30 minutes. Then scrub gently with a soft brush, focusing on collars, cuffs, and underarms. Rinse twice in clean water until no soap remains. For stubborn odors, add half a cup of white vinegar to the final rinse. Wring gently and hang to dry in direct sunlight for at least 4 hours. Sunlight kills bacteria and bleaches stains naturally. If you are in a humid area, use a dryer on medium heat. Never use hot water on delicate fabrics like silk or wool; it causes shrinkage. For heavily soiled items, pre-treat with a paste of detergent and water before washing. Always wear gloves to protect your hands. After washing, inspect each item for remaining stains or damages. This step is crucial because customers will not buy dirty or smelly clothes. A clean, fresh-smelling item sells faster and at a higher price. Remember, your reputation depends on the quality you deliver.

2. Removing Stains, Odors, and Common Damages

Even after washing, some stains and odors may remain. Common stains on okrika include oil, ink, rust, and mildew. For oil stains, apply a small amount of dishwashing liquid directly to the stain, rub gently with your fingers, and let it sit for 10 minutes before washing. For ink stains, dab with rubbing alcohol or nail polish remover on a cotton ball, but test on a hidden area first. For rust stains, squeeze lemon juice on the spot and sprinkle salt, then leave in sunlight for 30 minutes before rinsing. Mildew appears as black spots and smells musty. Soak the item in a mixture of one part white vinegar to four parts water for one hour, then wash normally. For odors that persist, soak in a solution of baking soda (half a cup per gallon of water) overnight. For damages like small holes or tears, you can patch them. Use an iron-on patch or a piece of similar fabric with fabric glue. For missing buttons, replace them with matching ones from your sewing kit. Zippers that stick can be rubbed with a graphite pencil or candle wax. If a zipper is broken, you can replace it or sew the opening shut and sell as a pullover. Always check pockets for tissues or money before washing. After treating, wash again to remove any chemical residue. Selling damaged items as-is will hurt your business. Fix what you can, and price accordingly. A little effort turns a rejected item into a bestseller.

3. Basic Repairs: Sewing Buttons, Fixing Zippers, and Patching

Basic repairs can save you money and increase the value of your okrika. You need a small sewing kit: needles, thread (black, white, and common colors), scissors, thimble, and a seam ripper. To sew a button, thread your needle with a double strand and knot the end. Position the button over the mark, and push the needle from the back of the fabric through one hole. Place a toothpick or matchstick on top of the button to create a shank (space for fabric thickness). Sew through the holes in a crisscross pattern four to six times. Remove the toothpick, bring the needle to the back, and wrap the thread around the stitches under the button a few times to secure. Knot and cut. For zippers, if the slider is stuck, rub it with soap or wax. If the zipper pull breaks, you can attach a paperclip or a small key ring as a temporary pull. If the zipper teeth are broken, you can replace the zipper by removing the old one with a seam ripper and sewing a new one. For patching holes, cut a patch from similar fabric, slightly larger than the hole. Pin it inside the garment, and sew around the edges with a small running stitch or use fabric glue for a no-sew option. For tears on seams, turn the item inside out and sew the seam closed with a backstitch. Always match thread color to the fabric. Practice on old rags first. These skills take minutes but can turn a $1 item into a $5 item. Offer free repairs on items you sell to build customer trust.

4. Ironing and Folding for a Neat Appearance

Ironing and folding make okrika look new and professional. Start with a clean iron and a ironing board or a table covered with a thick blanket. Set the iron temperature according to the fabric: low for silk, rayon, and synthetics; medium for wool; high for cotton and linen. Always test on a small hidden area first. For shirts, iron the collar first, then cuffs, then sleeves, then the body. For trousers, iron the waistband, then pockets, then legs, creating a sharp crease if desired. Use steam for stubborn wrinkles, but be careful with dark colors as steam can leave water spots. For delicate items, place a thin cotton cloth over the fabric before ironing. After ironing, fold immediately while the item is still warm to set the fold. For folding, use a clean flat surface. For shirts, button them up, fold sleeves back, and fold in half or thirds. For jeans, fold in half lengthwise, then fold in half again. For dresses, fold in half and then in thirds. You can also hang items on hangers to save time. Use clip hangers for skirts and trousers. For a neat display, group similar items together. Ironing and folding not only make items look better but also save space in your shop. Customers appreciate neatness and are willing to pay more. A well-ironed shirt can sell for double the price of a wrinkled one. Invest in a good iron and a spray bottle for water. Always unplug the iron when not in use.

5. Display Techniques: Hanging, Folding, and Mannequin Use

How you display your okrika affects sales. Start by organizing your shop or stall. Use hanging racks for dresses, shirts, and jackets. Arrange by color, size, or style. For example, group all white shirts together, then blues, then reds. This makes it easy for customers to find what they want. Use hangers that are sturdy and not broken. For folded items like jeans, t-shirts, and sweaters, use shelves or tables. Fold them neatly and stack by size. Place the most attractive items at eye level. Use mannequins or dress forms to show complete outfits. Dress a mannequin with a nice dress, add a scarf, and place it near the entrance to attract attention. If you don't have a mannequin, use a hanger on a hook or a wall. Rotate your display every week to keep it fresh. Use good lighting, preferably white LED, to show true colors. Avoid direct sunlight which can fade fabrics. Keep the area clean and dust-free. Price tags should be clearly visible. You can use small tags or stickers. For shoes, line them up on a shoe rack. For accessories, use a small basket or hooks. Create a comfortable atmosphere with a mirror so customers can try items. A well-organized shop encourages customers to browse longer and buy more. Remember, presentation is key in thrift business. Even a simple stall can look like a boutique with a little effort.

6. Packaging Items for Sale or Delivery

Packaging protects your items and creates a good impression. For in-person sales, use simple clear plastic bags or paper bags with your shop name. For online sales or delivery, you need sturdy packaging. Use poly mailers or cardboard boxes. For clothes, fold them neatly and place in a clear poly bag to keep them clean. If you are shipping multiple items, use a box with padding like newspaper or bubble wrap. Always include a thank-you note or a small gift like a button or a sticker. For delicate items, wrap in tissue paper. Label the package with the customer's address and your return address. Use a permanent marker. For delivery within your city, you can use a motorbike courier. For national delivery, use a bus service or a courier company like G4S or Speedaf. Always get a tracking number. For international shipping, check customs regulations. Package items tightly to prevent shifting. Use tape generously. For shoes, stuff with paper to keep shape. For jewelry, use small ziplock bags. Offer gift wrapping for a small fee. Good packaging reduces damages and returns. It also makes customers feel valued. A well-packaged item is more likely to be shared on social media, bringing you more customers. Keep your packaging costs low by buying in bulk. Reuse boxes and bags when possible. Always include care instructions if needed.

<h2>Practical Reference: Cleaning, Repairing, and Presenting Okrika</h2>

<p>In the second-hand clothing trade across Africa known as <strong>okrika</strong> in Nigeria, <strong>mtumba</strong> in Kenya and Tanzania, <strong>salaula</strong> in Zambia, and <strong>kaunjika</strong> in Malawi the margin is not made at the bale. It is made in the back room. Two traders can buy from the same supplier at the same price, yet one sells a shirt for KES 250 and the other for KES 1,200. The difference is <strong>finishing</strong>: how the item is washed, repaired, pressed, displayed, and packaged. This Practical Reference consolidates the six lessons in this module into one working document you can pin to your stall wall.</p>

<p>Treat every item as inventory, not as laundry. Each step below has a cost (water, detergent, thread, electricity, labour minutes) and a return (higher price, faster turnover, repeat customers, online resale eligibility). Track both.</p>

<h3>Stage 12: Washing and Sanitizing Safely</h3>
<p>Second-hand garments arrive compressed, dusty, and often treated with preservatives. Wash before you price buyers inspect with their noses as much as their eyes.</p>

<p><strong>Sorting rules:</strong> separate whites, lights, darks, and reds. Separate heavyweight denim from light cotton and synthetics. Never mix a new dark indigo item with pale cottons, even after one wash indigo bleeds for three to five cycles.</p>

<p><strong>Washing method:</strong> for bulk cotton, use a 40 C wash with a standard detergent. For delicates, wool, and anything with beading or lace, hand-wash in cool water with a mild liquid detergent. Add half a cup of white vinegar to the rinse cycle it neutralises alkaline detergent residue, softens fabric, and kills a wide range of odour-causing bacteria.</p>

<p><strong>Sanitizing:</strong> the WHO recommends laundering at 60 C with detergent for items that may carry pathogens, or using a laundry disinfectant approved for fabric. In hot African climates, line-drying in direct sunlight for at least three hours provides additional UV disinfection. Do not rely on sunlight alone for heavily soiled items.</p>

<h3>Stage 3: Removing Stains, Odours, and Common Damages</h3>
<p><strong>Stains.</strong> Treat immediately and treat cold first heat sets protein stains (blood, milk, sweat). Use a dab-and-lift motion from the outside of the stain inward so you do not spread the ring. Common treatments:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Grease/oil:</strong> dishwashing liquid worked in with a soft brush, then wash.</li>
<li><strong>Blood, sweat, deodorant:</strong> cold water plus a paste of bicarbonate of soda; soak 30 minutes.</li>
<li><strong>Ink:</strong> isopropyl alcohol on a cotton bud, blot test on a hidden seam first.</li>
<li><strong>Rust:</strong> lemon juice plus salt, sun for 20 minutes, then rinse thoroughly.</li>
<li><strong>Mould/mildew:</strong> vinegar soak, then a hot wash. If the smell persists after two cycles, downgrade the item.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Odours.</strong> Persistent smell usually means bacteria in the fibres, not on them. Soak in a solution of one cup white vinegar per 5 litres of cold water for 30 minutes, then wash normally. For smoke, hang the item outdoors in moving air for 2448 hours before washing. Do not mask odour with perfume buyers smell it, and it signals poor processing.</p>

<p><strong>Common damages to look for during sorting:</strong> pulled seams, missing buttons, broken or stuck zippers, thinning at collars and cuffs, hem drop, and pinholes. Grade each item A (resale-ready), B (repairable), or C (parts/fabric). Grade C items still earn money as cleaning rags, patch fabric, or bulk export.</p>

<h3>Stage 4: Basic Repairs</h3>
<p>You do not need a tailoring certificate to double an item's price. You need a needle, matching thread, a thimble, spare buttons, and a small zip assortment. Keep a <strong>repair kit</strong> at the stall: white, black, navy, and grey thread; assorted buttons; 5 zippers in common lengths; fabric scraps; fabric scissors; seam ripper; safety pins.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Buttons:</strong> use a shank (wrap thread under the button 34 times) so the button sits above thick fabric and does not pull the placket.</li>
<li><strong>Zippers:</strong> test the slider first sometimes only the slider is worn. If teeth are damaged, replace the whole zip. Pin, baste, then machine or hand-stitch; never skip the basting step.</li>
<li><strong>Patching:</strong> iron-on patches are fast but lift in washing. Stitch around the perimeter for durability. For visible mending on denim, a decorative patch can be a selling point, not a flaw.</li>
<li><strong>Seams:</strong> backstitch at both ends to lock. Match thread weight to fabric weight heavy thread on light cotton tears the cloth.</li>
</ul>

<div style="border:2px solid #b00020;background:#fdecec;padding:14px;margin:18px 0;border-radius:6px;">
<strong style="color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING Chemical and electrical hazards</strong>
<ul style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">
<li><strong>Never mix bleach with vinegar, ammonia, or any acid cleaner.</strong> This releases chlorine gas, which can cause severe lung injury in a confined back room. Mix in ventilated outdoor space only.</li>
<li><strong>Never mix bleach and hydrogen peroxide</strong> the reaction is violent and produces oxygen gas plus heat.</li>
<li>Wear gloves when handling bleach, rust removers, or disinfectants. Store all chemicals in original labelled containers, away from children and food.</li>
<li>Irons and steamers: unplug before leaving the stall. Never leave a hot iron face-down on fabric. Keep water away from electrical sockets a common cause of shocks in open-air markets.</li>
<li>Do not use unlabelled or repackaged solvents. If you cannot read the label, do not use it on garments that will touch skin.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h3>Stage 56: Ironing, Folding, Display, and Packaging</h3>
<p><strong>Ironing.</strong> Press on the reverse for dark and embellished fabrics to avoid shine. Use steam for cotton and linen; use low heat for synthetics; never iron directly onto prints, sequins, or reflective tape. A well-pressed shirt on a hanger reads as "new" to a buyer's eye.</p>

<p><strong>Folding.</strong> Fold to a consistent size uniform stacks look professional and make counting stock fast. Fold shirts with the collar visible; fold trousers along the crease. Use a simple cardboard template to keep sizes even.</p>

<p><strong>Display.</strong> Three tiers: <em>hero items</em> on mannequins or hangers at eye level (highest margin), <em>core stock</em> on tables and rails at waist level, <em>value bundles</em> in bins at floor level. Rotate hero items weekly a mannequin that never changes becomes invisible. Face labels and sizes outward. Group by category, then by colour.</p>

<p><strong>Packaging.</strong> For in-person sales, a clean paper bag or branded reusable tote raises perceived value and gives free advertising. For online and delivery sales (WhatsApp, Jumia, Kilimall, Instagram), use a clear poly bag inside a courier envelope, include a printed size/condition note, and photograph the item before sealing to protect against disputes. Include a small care card it reduces returns and builds repeat buyers.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Sort before you wash</strong> colour, fabric weight, and condition grade (A/B/C).</li>
<li><strong>Wash at the right temperature</strong> 60 C for children's wear and hygiene-critical items, cold for wool and delicates.</li>
<li><strong>Add vinegar to the rinse</strong> deodorises, softens, and removes detergent residue.</li>
<li><strong>Treat stains cold and from the outside in</strong> heat sets protein stains permanently.</li>
<li><strong>Run the quality gate</strong> smell, seam pull, stain check in daylight, zip and button test, size label.</li>
<li><strong>Repair before you price</strong> a 3-minute button fix can add 2050% to the sale price.</li>
<li><strong>Press and fold to a uniform standard</strong> consistent presentation signals a professional business.</li>
<li><strong>Package with a care note</strong> protects against disputes and earns repeat customers.</li>
</ol>
