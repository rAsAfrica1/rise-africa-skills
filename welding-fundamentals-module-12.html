1. Project Planning and Material List

Before you strike an arc, you need a plan. For a small fabrication project like a steel gate, a simple table, or a charcoal stove, start by sketching the item with dimensions. Use a pencil and paperâno fancy software needed. For example, a standard gate might be 1.2 meters wide and 2 meters high. Break the project into parts: frame, bars, hinges, and latch. List each piece with its length, width, and thickness. For a gate frame, you might need 2 pieces of 40x40mm square tubing at 2m, 2 pieces at 1.2m, and 10 bars of 12mm round bar at 1.8m. Calculate total material: for square tubing, add up all lengths (2+2+1.2+1.2=6.4m) and add 10% for waste, so buy 7m. For round bar, 10 x 1.8m = 18m, buy 20m. Also list consumables: welding rods (3.2mm E6013 for general work), grinding discs, cutting discs, and paint. Check your stockâdo you have enough? If not, visit your local hardware or steel supplier. In many African markets, you can buy steel by the length or full 6m bars. Ask for a discount when buying in bulk. Also plan your time: cutting (1 hour), fit-up (1 hour), welding (2 hours), finishing (1 hour). Total about 5 hours. Write this down. A good plan saves you from running out of material halfway or wasting money on extra. Always measure twice and cut once. If youâre building for a customer, confirm the design and get a deposit for materials. This prevents loss. Remember, a clear material list is your roadmap to a successful project.

2. Cutting and Fit-Up for a Project

Cutting and fit-up are where accuracy begins. For cutting steel, you can use a hacksaw for small pieces, but for efficiency, use an angle grinder with a cutting disc (1mm thick for clean cuts) or a chop saw. Always wear safety glasses, gloves, and ear protection. Mark your cuts with a soapstone or marker and a try square. For a 40x40mm square tube, measure from the end and draw a line all around using a square. Clamp the tube securely before cutting. When using an angle grinder, let the tool do the workâdonât force it. For round bars, a hacksaw works fine, but a grinder is faster. After cutting, remove burrs with a grinding disc. Now fit-up: arrange the pieces on a flat surface, like a welding table or concrete floor. Use magnetic squares or clamps to hold 90-degree corners. Check diagonalsâfor a rectangular frame, measure corner to corner; both diagonals must be equal. Tack weld at corners, then recheck. For a gate, ensure the frame is square before welding fully. If youâre welding thin material, use a lower amperage (e.g., 70-90 amps for 2mm steel) to avoid burn-through. For thicker steel (5mm+), use 100-130 amps. Tack welds should be smallâabout 10mm long. After tacking, adjust if needed. Fit-up gaps should be minimal; if thereâs a gap, fill it with a small piece of metal or adjust your cut. A good fit-up means less welding and a stronger joint. Take your timeârushing here leads to a twisted project. Use a tape measure and square constantly. In Africa, where materials can be uneven, check for straightness. If a tube is bent, straighten it with a hammer or replace it. Fit-up is the foundation; get it right.

3. Welding a Small Fabrication Project

Now you weld. For a small project like a gate, start with tack welds at all joints. Use a 3.2mm E6013 electrode at 90-110 amps for 3mm steel. Hold the electrode at a 70-80 degree angle, dragging it along the joint. For a butt joint, use a slight back-and-forth motion to fill the gap. For T-joints, aim the arc at the corner where the two pieces meet. Keep a consistent travel speedâtoo fast gives a thin, weak weld; too slow burns through. Listen to the sound: a steady crackling like frying bacon means good settings. If it sounds like a loud hum, increase amps. Weld in short runs, about 50-75mm, then stop and let it cool slightly to prevent distortion. For a gate frame, weld the corners first, then the bars. Use a chipping hammer to remove slag after each weld, then brush with a wire brush. Inspect your welds: they should be smooth, with even ripples, and no undercut or holes. If you see holes (porosity), grind out and re-weld. For round bars, weld both sides if possible. If youâre building a charcoal stove, weld the firebox seams completely to prevent leaks. Practice on scrap first. In many African workshops, youâll use a stick welder (SMAW) because itâs affordable and works off generator or mains. If you have a MIG welder, use 0.8mm wire and 75/25 argon-CO2 gas, but stick is more common. Remember to weld in a well-ventilated areaâfumes are dangerous. After welding, let the project cool naturally. Donât quench in water unless you need to prevent warping on thin metal. A good weld is strong and neat; itâs your signature. Take pride in each bead.

4. Finishing and Cleanup

Finishing makes your project look professional and last longer. After welding, remove all slag, spatter, and sharp edges. Use a chipping hammer and wire brush first, then a grinding disc (4.5 inch, 60 grit) to smooth welds. For a gate or table, grind welds flush if the design calls for it, but donât over-grindâyouâll weaken the joint. Next, clean the metal with a rag and degreaser (or acetone) to remove oil and dirt. Then apply a primer. In Africa, red oxide primer is common and affordable. Brush or spray a thin, even coat. Let it dry for 2-4 hours. Then apply topcoat: enamel paint or hammered finish. For outdoor projects, use a rust-inhibiting paint. Two coats are better than one. If youâre building a charcoal stove, use high-temperature paint (black heat-resistant) on the firebox. For a gate, you might add decorative elements like scrolls or paint it black with gold accents. After painting, check for missed spots. Clean your workspace: sweep up metal shavings, put tools away, and dispose of waste properly. Sharpen your chipping hammer and replace worn grinding discs. A clean shop is a safe shop. Finally, inspect the finished project: open and close the gate, check for wobbles, ensure hinges move freely. If youâre delivering to a customer, wrap it in a blanket or cardboard to prevent scratches during transport. A good finish turns a rough fabrication into a marketable product. It shows you care about quality, which builds your reputation.

5. Portfolio and Work Sample Development

Your portfolio is your proof of skill. As a welder, you need to show what you can do. Start by photographing every project you completeâeven practice pieces. 6. Certification, Job Search, and Career Pathways

Certification proves your skills to employers and customers. In many African countries, you can get tested by a national vocational training authority, like NITA in Kenya, or similar bodies in Nigeria, South Africa, or Ghana. Look for a local welding test center. The test usually involves welding a butt joint, T-joint, and a pipe joint (if youâre going for pipe welding). Youâll be graded on bead appearance, penetration, and defects. Passing gives you a certificateâoften a Level 1, 2, or 3. Level 3 is the highest and can lead to better pay. The cost varies: in Kenya, NITA Grade 3 test might cost around 5,000 KES. Save up for it. While certified, you can also get experience through apprenticeships. Ask a local workshop if you can work as a helper. After 1-2 years, you can apply for jobs. Where to look: construction sites, welding shops, factories, and mines. In Africa, the mining and construction sectors are booming. For example, in South Africa, welders are needed in the platinum mines. In Nigeria, oil and gas companies hire pipe welders. You can also start your own businessâmake and sell gates, windows, beds, and stoves. Advertise on social media, WhatsApp groups, and local notice boards. Set fair prices: calculate material cost + labor (e.g., 30% of material cost) + profit. For a gate, if materials cost 10,000 KES, charge 15,000 KES. Reinvest in better tools: a MIG welder, a plasma cutter, or a generator. Career pathways: you can become a welding inspector, a welding instructor, or a fabrication business owner. Some welders move to offshore rigs or overseas jobs. Keep learning: take advanced courses in TIG, MIG, or pipe welding. Your career is in your handsâweld, learn, and grow.

2. Cutting and Fit-Up

Cutting accuracy determines fit-up, and fit-up determines weld quality. Use a square, a chalk line, and a cutting jig wherever possible. For thin sections, a 115 mm angle grinder with a 1 mm cut-off disc is fast and cheap; for heavy sections, use a chop saw or oxy-fuel cutting. Always deburr and remove mill scale and rust at least 25 mm back from the joint.

Fit-up rules that save rework:

  • Check diagonals before tacking â equal diagonals mean a square frame.
  • Tack in the order: corners first, then mid-spans, to control distortion.
  • Leave a root gap of 1.5â2.5 mm on butt joints for full penetration on thicker material.
  • Clamp or use a strong-back to hold alignment while tacking.
