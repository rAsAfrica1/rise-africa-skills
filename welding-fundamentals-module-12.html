WELDING FUNDAMENTALS · MODULE 12 OF 12

Cutting, Grinding and Finishing

The work that comes before and after the weld. Cut it right, clean it right, finish it right — or the best weld in the world will not save the job.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  • Choose between the common cutting methods for a given job
  • Cut safely with angle grinder, hacksaw, and oxy-fuel torch
  • Select grinding wheels and discs correctly, and inspect them before use
  • Prepare a joint edge accurately by grinding
  • Finish a completed weld — slag removal, brushing, dressing
  • Cost a job that includes cutting, welding and finishing
Lesson 12.1

Cutting methods

Welding starts with cutting. The quality of your cuts decides how much grinding you will do, and how well the joint fits.

The four methods you will meet

MethodWhat it cutsSpeedAccuracyCost of equipment
Angle grinder with cutting discSteel, stainless, some alloysFast on thin, slower on thickGood with practiceLow
HacksawThin stock, tube, small sectionsSlowVery good on straight cutsVery low
Oxy-fuel torchThick steel (over ~5mm)Fast on thickModerate, needs cleanupModerate (torch + cylinders)
Chop saw / abrasive sawAny steel up to its capacityFast, repeatableVery good for straight cutsModerate

In most small workshops, the angle grinder and the chop saw do almost all the cutting. Oxy-fuel is for thick plate or where there is no electricity.

Cutting with an angle grinder

Common on any farm or small workshop. A cutting disc on a grinder will cut any steel up to about 10mm, and more with effort.

Safety before anything

  • Check the disc — no chips, cracks or damage. A disc that breaks at speed throws fragments
  • Check the grinder's guard is in place — it stops fragments
  • Eye protection always — face shield AND safety glasses, because the disc can throw particles
  • Hearing protection — grinders are loud
  • Leather gloves — the cut edges are hot and sharp
  • No loose clothing or gloves that could catch
  • Ventilation or mask if grinding coated metal — galvanising and paint produce dust

Cutting technique

  1. Mark the cut line clearly
  2. Support the workpiece so it cannot shift
  3. Start the grinder away from the work, then bring it into the cut gently
  4. Do not force the disc — let the abrasive work
  5. Keep the grinder moving along the cut, not stuck in one place
  6. Do not bend or twist the disc — this is the main cause of disc breakage
  7. When cutting flat plate, cut from the far edge toward you
  8. When cutting a structural section, cut from the side where the disc is least likely to jam

The kickback hazard

If the disc binds in the cut — usually because the work shifted, or the disc twisted — the grinder can kick back violently toward the user. This is the commonest cause of serious grinder injuries.

Support the work. Do not let it shift. Hold the grinder firmly with both hands. Stand to the side, not directly behind the wheel. Never remove the guard.

Hacksaw cutting

Slow, but accurate and free. Suitable for thin tube, small sections and finishing cuts where a grinder would be too rough.

  • Blade selection — 18 TPI (teeth per inch) for thick sections, 24 TPI for general steel, 32 TPI for thin tube and sheet. Finer blades on thin work cut less violently and are less likely to snag
  • Tension — the blade should be tight in the frame, but not so tight it snaps
  • Technique — long, slow strokes with light pressure. Let the teeth cut. Pushing hard bends the blade
  • On long cuts, the blade walks off line. Rotate the work, or cut from a corner, to keep the line true

Oxy-fuel cutting

Fast and clean on thick steel. Works without electricity.

  • Preheat the cut edge with the heating flame to red heat
  • Squeeze the cutting lever and move steadily along the cut line
  • Speed — fast enough that the cut sparks are landing beyond the plate, slow enough that the cut goes all the way through
  • Standing to the side of the cut, not in line with it, so the sparks do not spray you
  • Eye protection — appropriate shade for oxy-fuel cutting, not the same shade as arc welding
  • Never use oxy-fuel for cutting inside a closed vessel — module 1 covered this. Never cut anything that has held fuel

The cutting torch is not a welding torch

A welding torch has a smaller tip and lacks the cutting oxygen lever. Do not try to cut with a welding torch — you will simply melt the surface without cutting through, and waste gas.

Cutting torches are used for cutting. Welding torches are used for welding. The two are different tools.

Lesson 12.2

Grinding — wheels, discs and safety

Grinding does three jobs: preparing an edge for welding, cleaning up a completed weld, and cutting (with a cutting disc).

Disc types

DiscPurposeNotes
Grinding discRemoving metal, cleaning weld toes, dressing the surfaceThick, made for the flat of the disc to be applied to the surface
Cutting discCutting through metalThin, made for the edge of the disc to be applied. Must not be used for grinding
Flap discFinishing, blending, light material removalAbrasive flaps on a backing. Produces a smooth finish
Wire cup brushRemoving rust, paint, light scale; cleaning between passesTwisted wire for aggressive cleaning, crimped for light
Grinding stone (mounted point)Grinding inside holes, dressing welds in tight spotsVarious shapes for various profiles

Never use a cutting disc for grinding

Cutting discs are thin and are designed to take force on their edge only. Used flat against a surface, they shatter. The fragments travel at high speed and are lethal.

Never use a grinding disc for cutting. It is too thick to cut efficiently and its reinforcement is not designed for edge loading.

Wheel inspection — the routine

Before every use:

  1. Check the disc for cracks, chips or damage. Look at both faces and the edge
  2. Ring test on large grinding wheels — tap the wheel gently and listen. A clear ring means sound; a dull thud means cracked
  3. Check the expiry date. Bonded abrasives have a shelf life. Old discs may fail on start-up
  4. Check the mounting. Blotters (the paper washers) present, flanges clean, nut tight
  5. Check the guard is in place and correctly adjusted
  6. Run the grinder briefly before applying load. Let it spin up to speed and listen for vibration

Grinding safely

  • Face shield AND safety glasses. The face shield stops large fragments; the glasses stop dust and small particles. Both are needed
  • Leather gloves — the sparks burn, and the cut edges are sharp
  • No synthetic clothing — same as welding
  • Hearing protection
  • Respiratory protection if grinding coated metal — galvanising, paint, lead. The dust is toxic
  • Fire extinguisher nearby — sparks travel and can start fires just as welding does
  • Ventilation or outdoor work — grinding dust is fine and stays airborne
  • Do not grind near flammables
  • Do not lay the grinder down while still running
Lesson 12.3

Preparing an edge by grinding

Module 6 covered the geometry of a bevel. This lesson covers cutting it accurately.

Freehand grinding

A beginner can produce a reasonable bevel freehand, particularly if the material is not critical.

  1. Mark the bevel line on the plate edge with a scriber or marker
  2. Set up a straight reference — a straightedge clamped alongside the plate, or a piece of sacrificial plate to gauge the angle against
  3. Hold the grinder at the correct angle and work along the edge in steady passes
  4. Check regularly with a bevel gauge or against a template
  5. Work slowly — freehand grinding removes metal quickly and a slip can ruin the edge

Grinding with a guide

For accuracy, a guide is worth making. Two common approaches:

  • An angled block — a piece of steel cut to the correct angle and clamped alongside the plate edge. The grinder disc is rested against the block and moved along it, producing a consistent bevel
  • A bevel guide on the grinder — some grinders accept an attachment that sets the disc angle automatically. Not common on cheap machines, but useful on more expensive ones

Cleaning the joint edge

Whether you grind a bevel or not, the joint edges need cleaning to bright metal.

  • Grind or wire brush 25mm back from the joint on both faces
  • Remove mill scale at the joint — it interferes with the arc
  • Do not try to remove mill scale across the whole plate. Only the joint area matters
  • Degrease if oil is present — clean with non-chlorinated solvent before grinding

Grinding mistakes that ruin a joint

  • Grinding a concave surface — the disc removes too much in the middle, leaving the plate edge dished. Check with a straightedge
  • Overheating the plate — heavy grinding heats the metal and can harden a small area, which then acts as a stress concentration
  • Leaving grinding marks — deep score marks along the plate become stress raisers under load
  • Grinding thin material fast — thin sheet disappears quickly. Light passes, check often
Lesson 12.4

Finishing the weld

The weld is complete. The job is not. Finishing turns a raw weld into a finished piece of work.

Slag removal

On every stick weld and every flux-cored weld, the slag must be removed after each pass and after the final weld.

  1. Let the weld cool. Hot slag is easier to chip but the weld metal is still soft, and a chipping hammer can damage the surface. Wait until the weld glows no more
  2. Chip along the length, using the pointed end of the chipping hammer on the slag and the flat end at the weld edge
  3. Wire brush off the residues — chipping leaves fine particles that must be removed before the next pass or before finishing
  4. Inspect. Look for slag still attached — dark patches on the weld indicate remaining slag

Dressing the weld

Dressing means grinding the weld to change its shape or finish. Done for three reasons:

  • To remove defects — undercut, overlap, spatter, arc strikes
  • To smooth the appearance — a dressed weld looks more finished than a raw one
  • To prepare for coating or machining — the surface must be smooth for paint, plating or further work

Dressing is not always appropriate

Dressing a weld removes metal. If the weld was already at the minimum size, dressing it can reduce it below specification. On structural work, dressing is often prohibited without engineering approval.

Dress to remove defects, smooth appearance, or meet a specific requirement. Do not dress a weld just because it looks neat — check whether the size still meets the requirement.

Removing spatter

  • Anti-spatter spray applied before welding stops spatter sticking. Cheaper than removal time
  • Chip off after cooling — cold spatter comes off cleanly with a chipping hammer
  • Grinding removes stubborn spatter but damages the surface
  • Wire brush for light residue

Removing arc strikes

Arc strikes are small hardened spots where the arc touched the metal outside the weld. They are stress concentrations and, on structural work, defects.

  • Grind them out completely — the metal around them is hardened and must be removed
  • Check the surface for the next strike — if you often strike outside the weld, adjust your technique

Final finish

FinishHow
As-weldedSlag chipped, spatter removed, wire brushed. Left unfiled. Common for farm and workshop work
Dressed smoothGround flush with the parent metal. Used where appearance or fitting matters
PolishedAfter dressing, polished with progressively finer abrasives. For visible or food-contact work
PaintedCleaned, primed, painted. The finish most farm work receives
GalvanisedSent for hot-dip galvanising. The most durable outdoor finish, and expensive. Weld dressing must be complete before galvanising
Lesson 12.5

Estimating a job

Almost every welding job you do for money will need to be priced before you start. Pricing too low is how small workshops fail.

What a welding job actually costs

CostHow to work it out
MaterialSteel, consumables, gas. Measure or estimate, then add waste
Cutting timeRoughly 1 minute per cut with a grinder on thin stock, more for thick
Preparation timeCleaning, fitting, tacking. Often longer than the welding itself
Welding timeDepends on joint type, position, process. Stick is slow, MIG faster
Finishing timeChipping, brushing, grinding, dressing. Add time for every visible weld
OverheadsElectricity, machine wear, disc wear, PPE replacement. Real costs
Your labourValue your own time at the same rate you would pay an employee
ContingencySomething always takes longer. Add 15–25%

A worked example

A simple gate frame: 4 pieces of square tube, 4 mitred corners, 2 diagonal braces.

  • Material: 6 metres of 25×25 tube — price from supplier
  • Cutting: 8 mitres, 2 brace cuts — roughly 30 minutes
  • Preparation: clean 16 joint edges, fit up square, tack — 45 minutes
  • Welding: 16 corner welds, 4 brace welds. Say 3mm fillets, stick. 30 minutes
  • Finishing: chip, brush, grind the visible welds. 45 minutes
  • Overheads: discs, electricity, electrodes — estimate as a percentage
  • Labour: 2.5 hours total × your hourly rate
  • Contingency: add 20%

For a gate, the price is typically material + labour + overheads + profit margin. On a small job that is often a few hundred percent of the material cost. Do not price at material cost plus a bit — the labour is the real cost.

Do not underprice to win work

The commonest failure of small welding businesses is quoting too low and then doing the work anyway. The customer is happy, you lose money, and you have established a price that is below what future jobs need to be.

Price honestly, hold the price, and be prepared to lose jobs you cannot do at a profit. It is the only way to stay in business.

What this course has not done

Every module has said it. Together:

  • It has not certified you to weld
  • It has not taught you structural welding, pressure welding or coded work
  • It has not taught you to weld high-carbon, alloy, spring, tool or cast iron
  • It has not taught you out-of-position welding as a skill — only described what changes
  • It has not taught you to size a joint for load — that is design
  • It has not given you the hours of supervised practice that turn knowledge into skill

Where to go next

If you want to weld as a trade

  1. Find a workshop and a mentor. Offer to help for free, for a few weeks, in exchange for watching and being taught
  2. Practise deliberately. Modules 7 and 11 give you the framework
  3. Get coded when you are ready. Coded welding is a formal test against a procedure, done at a recognised test centre. It is what allows you to weld on structural and pressure work
  4. Consider the Construction Trades blueprints on the sister courses — gate making, welding in depth, and starting a construction company

If you want to weld for yourself

  1. Buy a stick welder and learn the basics on scrap (module 3)
  2. Practise until you can produce a sound bead in the flat position (module 7)
  3. Learn to cut and prepare joints properly (modules 6 and 12)
  4. Learn to recognise defects (module 11)
  5. Accept that some jobs are for professionals, and call one when you need to

The habit that matters most

Look at what you have done. After every weld, examine the bead, cut a test piece if it matters, and think about what you would change next time.

The welder who does that for a year will be better than the welder who simply does more welding without reflection. Skill comes from practice, but improvement comes from reflection on practice. This is the single most useful thing the whole course can tell you.

Capstone assignment — Welding Fundamentals

Produce a comprehensive welding document for a specific small fabrication you intend to make. This is the final assignment of the course. It brings together everything you have learned.

  1. The item and its purpose. Describe what you are making and what it must do. Reference the load it will carry and the environment it will be in
  2. The material and joint plan. State the material (and how you identified it), the joint type for each connection, and the reason for each choice. Reference modules 6 and 8
  3. The process and consumables plan. State which welding process you will use, which electrode or wire, and why. Reference modules 3 and 5
  4. The preparation plan. Step-by-step: cutting, cleaning, bevels, fit-up, tacks. Reference modules 6 and 12
  5. The distortion plan. State where the pulls will go, and what sequence you will use to control them. Reference module 10
  6. The safety plan. List the hazards specific to this job (fume, fire, height, confined space, coating) and what you will do about each. Reference module 1
  7. The defect watch list. State which defects are most likely for this job and this process, and what you will check for after welding. Reference module 11
  8. The finishing plan. Slag removal, dressing, coating. State what finish is appropriate. Reference module 12
  9. The cost. Material, consumables, time, your labour, contingency. State your price if you were selling it. Reference module 12
  10. One thing you will not do, and why. State one aspect of this job — or a related job — that is outside your competence, and what you would do about it (find a professional, redesign, refuse the work)

Points 9 and 10 carry the most marks. Point 9 tests whether you can cost a job properly. Point 10 tests whether you know where your competence ends — the thing that separates a safe welder from a dangerous one.

Module 12 knowledge check

1. Why must a cutting disc never be used for grinding?

  1. It cuts too slowly
  2. Cutting discs are thin and designed for edge loading only — used flat, they shatter at high speed, throwing lethal fragments
  3. It wastes the disc
  4. It voids the warranty
Show answer

b. Cutting discs have reinforcement and thickness designed for cutting along their edge. Applied flat to a surface, they crack and shatter. The fragments travel at high speed and cause serious injury. Never use one for grinding.

2. What causes angle grinder kickback?

  1. Too much power
  2. The disc binds in the cut — usually because the work shifted, or the disc was twisted — and the grinder is thrown back toward the user
  3. Wrong disc type
  4. Lack of lubrication
Show answer

b. Kickback is the commonest cause of serious grinder injuries. Prevent it by supporting the work securely, holding the grinder with both hands, standing to the side, and never twisting the disc in the cut. Never remove the guard.

3. Why is grinding dust from galvanised steel dangerous?

  1. It is flammable
  2. It contains zinc, which causes metal fume fever when inhaled, and can cause longer-term health effects
  3. It stains clothing
  4. It corrodes the grinder
Show answer

b. Grinding galvanising produces zinc dust that has the same health effects as the fume from welding it. Wear a dust mask, work in ventilated space, and do not breathe the dust. The same applies to grinding lead paint and other coatings.

4. Why must an arc strike outside the weld be removed?

  1. It looks untidy
  2. It is a small hardened spot that acts as a stress concentration, which under load can start a crack
  3. It interferes with painting
  4. It violates welding codes
Show answer

b. The rapid heat and quench of an arc strike hardens the metal around it. Under load, that hardened spot becomes a crack initiation point. Arc strikes on structural work are treated as defects and must be ground out.

5. Why is underpricing welding jobs the commonest failure of small workshops?

  1. Because customers always want discounts
  2. Because the labour cost is often far greater than the material cost, and pricing based on material plus a small margin leaves the labour unpaid
  3. Because welding is a low-value trade
  4. Because competitors always undercut
Show answer

b. On small fabrication jobs, the labour — cutting, preparation, welding, finishing — is usually many times the material cost. Quoting at material cost plus a margin means working for nothing. Price honestly, hold the price, and be prepared to lose jobs you cannot do at a profit.
