1. Types of Aluminum Profiles for Windows and Doors

In aluminum window and door fabrication, profiles are the extruded shapes that form the frame, sash, and other components. The most common types you'll encounter are casement, sliding, and fixed profiles. Casement windows use profiles with a rebate for glass and a sash that opens outward. Sliding windows and doors use profiles with tracks and rollers. Fixed windows use simpler frame profiles. In Africa, popular series include the 40mm, 50mm, and 60mm casement series, and the 70mm, 80mm, and 90mm sliding series. These numbers refer to the width of the profile in millimeters. For example, a 50mm casement profile is 50mm wide. You'll also find specialized profiles like mullions (vertical dividers), transoms (horizontal dividers), and sills (bottom frame). When starting out, choose a common series like 50mm casement or 80mm sliding because accessories (handles, hinges, rollers) are widely available. In Nigeria, Kenya, and South Africa, local extruders produce these standard profiles. Always ask for the profile catalog from your supplier to see the cross-sections and dimensions. A typical casement window uses outer frame profiles, sash profiles, mullion, and glazing beads. Sliding windows use top and bottom tracks, side jambs, interlocking profiles, and rollers. Fixed windows use frame profiles and beads. Understanding these types helps you order the right materials and avoid waste. Start by measuring your opening and calculating the number of profiles needed based on the window design. Most fabricators use a cutting list to optimize material usage. Remember that profiles are sold in standard lengths, usually 6 meters (about 20 feet). So plan your cuts to minimize offcuts. A good practice is to draw the window to scale and list each piece with its length and quantity.

2. Understanding Alloy Grades and Tempers

Aluminum alloy grades and tempers determine the strength, corrosion resistance, and workability of your profiles. The most common alloy for architectural aluminum is 6063, with temper T5 or T6. Alloy 6063 is magnesium-silicon based, offering good extrudability and moderate strength. Temper T5 means the profile is cooled from the extrusion press and artificially aged to increase strength. T6 is solution heat-treated and artificially aged, giving higher strength. In Africa, you'll often see profiles marked with these grades. For windows and doors, 6063-T5 is standard. Some cheaper profiles use alloy 6061 or recycled aluminum with inconsistent properties. Alloy 6061 is stronger but harder to extrude into complex shapes, so it's less common for windows. Tempers range from soft (O) to hard (T6). Soft tempers are easy to bend but dent easily. Hard tempers are strong but may crack if bent. For coastal areas with salt air, choose at least T5 with a good anodized finish. For high-wind areas, consider T6 for extra strength. How do you verify? Ask for the mill certificate from the extruder. It states the alloy and temper. If buying from a local dealer, request a sample and check for a stamp on the profile. Some profiles have inkjet markings like '6063-T5'. If not, you can test hardness with a simple file test: a harder temper resists filing. But the best is to buy from reputable suppliers. In Nigeria, companies like Tower Aluminium and G. Z. Industries produce certified profiles. In Kenya, check with Steel and Allied. In South Africa, look for Wispeco or Hulamin. Using the wrong alloy or temper can lead to windows that sag, crack, or corrode. So always specify 6063-T5 or T6 for architectural use. And avoid unknown alloys from informal markets.

3. Wall Thickness and Its Impact on Strength

Wall thickness is the thickness of the aluminum metal in the profile's walls. It's measured in millimeters (mm) and is critical for strength and durability. In Africa, common wall thicknesses for windows and doors range from 1.0mm to 2.0mm. Cheaper profiles often have 1.0mm or less, while quality profiles have 1.4mm to 2.0mm. For example, a standard 50mm casement profile might have a wall thickness of 1.4mm. A heavy-duty sliding door profile might be 2.0mm. Thicker walls mean greater resistance to bending, wind load, and impact. They also allow for better screw holding, which is important for hinges and handles. In coastal or high-wind areas, always use at least 1.6mm. For large sliding doors, use 2.0mm. How do you measure wall thickness? Use a digital caliper or a micrometer. Measure at several points along the profile because it can vary. If you don't have a caliper, you can sometimes see the thickness marked on the profile or in the catalog. But don't trust markings alone; verify with a tool. A common trick is to weigh a 6-meter length. Thicker walls make the profile heavier. For example, a 6m length of 50mm casement profile with 1.4mm wall might weigh around 3.5kg, while one with 1.0mm wall might weigh 2.5kg. So weight is a good indicator. But beware: some manufacturers add extra fins or change design to compensate. Always rely on actual measurement. In Nigeria, many fabricators prefer 1.5mm for windows and 2.0mm for doors. In Kenya, similar. In South Africa, SANS 10400 specifies minimum thicknesses for different applications. For example, for windows in coastal areas, minimum 1.6mm. So check local building codes. Using too thin profiles can lead to windows that twist, doors that drag, and failures in strong winds. It's better to spend a little more on thicker profiles than to replace a failed installation.

4. Surface Finishes: Mill, Anodized, and Powder Coated

Surface finish protects aluminum from corrosion and gives it color and texture. The three main finishes are mill, anodized, and powder coated. Mill finish is the raw aluminum surface after extrusion. It has a natural silvery shine but no protective coating. It will oxidize over time, turning dull and chalky, especially in coastal or polluted areas. Mill finish is cheapest but only suitable for indoor use or temporary structures. Anodized finish is created by an electrochemical process that thickens the natural oxide layer. It's durable, corrosion-resistant, and can be clear or colored (e.g., bronze, black, gold). Anodizing thickness is measured in microns (Âµm). For architectural use, minimum 10Âµm, but 15-20Âµm is better for outdoor. In Africa, anodized finishes are popular for their metallic look. However, anodized can fade in strong UV and may show scratches. Powder coating is a dry powder applied electrostatically and baked to form a tough, colored film. It offers a wide range of colors (RAL palette) and is more resistant to chipping and fading than anodizing. Thickness is typically 60-80Âµm. Powder coating is ideal for harsh environments. In Nigeria, powder coating is common for windows and doors, with colors like white, cream, and brown. In Kenya, both anodized and powder coated are used. In South Africa, powder coating is preferred for coastal areas. When choosing, consider the environment: for coastal or industrial areas, powder coating or high-thickness anodizing (20Âµm+). For inland residential, anodized (15Âµm) or powder coating (60Âµm) works. How to check quality? For anodized, look for even color and no streaks. For powder coated, check for smooth, uniform coverage and no bubbles. You can also test adhesion by cutting a cross-hatch and applying tape; powder coating should not peel. Always ask for a warranty: good finishes last 10-20 years. Cheap finishes may fail in 2-3 years. So invest in quality finish to avoid repainting or replacement.

5. Identifying Quality Aluminum vs. Substandard

Not all aluminum profiles are created equal. Substandard profiles can ruin your window or door installation. Here's how to spot the difference. First, check the wall thickness with a caliper. Quality profiles meet or exceed the specified thickness, usually 1.4mm for windows and 2.0mm for doors. Substandard ones may be 1.0mm or less. Second, look at the surface finish. Quality anodized or powder coated profiles have even color, no scratches, and no bubbles. Substandard finishes may be thin, patchy, or easily scratched. Third, examine the profile's shape and tolerances. Quality profiles are straight, with sharp corners and consistent dimensions. Substandard ones may be twisted, warped, or have rough edges. Fourth, check the alloy and temper. Ask for mill certificates. If unavailable, look for markings like '6063-T5'. Substandard profiles might use recycled aluminum with unknown properties. Fifth, test the weight. A 6-meter length of quality profile is heavier than a substandard one of the same series. For example, a 50mm casement profile with 1.4mm wall weighs about 3.5kg, while a 1.0mm wall weighs about 2.5kg. Sixth, check the screw holding. Drive a screw into the profile; quality aluminum holds firmly, while substandard may strip. Seventh, look for internal defects. Cut a small section and inspect for porosity or inclusions. Quality profiles are dense and uniform. Eighth, consider the source. Buy from reputable manufacturers or authorized dealers. In Nigeria, brands like Tower Aluminium, G. Z. Industries, and First Aluminium are reliable. In Kenya, check with Steel and Allied or Devki. In South Africa, Wispeco and Hulamin are trusted. Ninth, compare prices. If a deal seems too good, it's likely substandard. Tenth, ask for a warranty. Quality suppliers offer warranties on finish and strength. By following these steps, you can avoid costly failures. Remember, your reputation depends on the quality of your installations. So always inspect materials before you buy.

6. Sourcing Aluminum Locally and Importing

Sourcing aluminum profiles in Africa involves choosing between local suppliers and imports. Local sourcing has advantages: shorter lead times, lower shipping costs, and easier communication. Many African countries have local extruders. In Nigeria, major producers include Tower Aluminium, G. Z. Industries, and First Aluminium. They offer standard profiles and finishes. In Kenya, Steel and Allied and Devki Steel produce aluminum profiles. In South Africa, Wispeco and Hulamin are leading manufacturers. In Egypt, Alumil and other companies serve North Africa. Local profiles are often cheaper and readily available in standard lengths (6m). However, the range of profiles and finishes may be limited, and quality can vary. Always inspect before buying. Importing from China, Turkey, or Europe can give you access to a wider range of profiles, specialized designs, and consistent quality. But importing comes with challenges: shipping costs, customs duties, longer lead times (4-8 weeks), and minimum order quantities (often 1-2 tons). You also need to handle clearing and transport. For small fabricators, importing may not be cost-effective unless you order in bulk. If you import, work with a reputable exporter and specify alloy 6063-T5, wall thickness, and finish. Request samples before placing a large order. Check that profiles meet local standards, such as SANS in South Africa or NIS in Nigeria. For pricing, local profiles might cost $3-$5 per kg, while imported ones might be $2.5-$4 per kg plus shipping and duties. But consider total landed cost. Also, factor in exchange rates and inflation. In some countries, import restrictions or high tariffs make local sourcing more attractive. For beginners, start with local suppliers to avoid complexity. As you grow, you can explore imports for specialized projects. Always build relationships with suppliers and negotiate prices. Join fabricator associations to get recommendations. And keep records of your purchases for warranty claims. Whether local or imported, the key is to ensure quality and reliability.

Practical Reference: Aluminum Profiles and Materials for Windows & Doors

This lesson is the working reference you will return to on site, in the workshop, and at the supplier's counter. It condenses the six preceding lessons into decision-ready formats: profile families, alloy and temper selection, wall-thickness rules of thumb, finish comparisons, quality tests, and sourcing routes that make sense for African fabricators. Treat it as a bench manual, not a textbook. Every table can be printed, laminated, and pinned above the cutting saw.

For an African window and door business, material decisions drive three things simultaneously: structural safety (wind load, glass weight, corrosion), cost per finished unit (profile weight, finish, scrap rate), and market positioning (local vs. imported, warranty credibility). Getting the material specification right is the single highest-leverage cost and quality decision in the entire production chain â more than machinery, more than labour.

3. Wall Thickness â The Strength Rule You Cannot Negotiate

Wall thickness is the single most falsified specification in the African aluminum trade. A profile sold as "1.4 mm" often measures 1.05â1.15 mm on a micrometer. This matters because bending stiffness scales roughly with the cube of the wall thickness: dropping from 1.4 mm to 1.1 mm can cut stiffness by around 50%, which shows up as sagging sliding doors, rattling sashes, and failed wind-load performance.

Practical minimums for African conditions:

  • Fixed glazing frames: 1.2 mm minimum
  • Casement sashes and frames: 1.4 mm minimum
  • Sliding window tracks: 1.6 mm minimum
  • Sliding door tracks and leaves: 1.8â2.0 mm minimum
  • Coastal or high-wind zones: add 0.2â0.4 mm to each figure

Use a digital micrometer or ultrasonic thickness gauge on every new supplier batch. Measure at three points on each profile: the thin web, the corner, and the track base. If the supplier objects to you measuring, change supplier.

5. Identifying Quality Aluminum vs. Substandard

Counterfeit and substandard profiles are the biggest hidden cost in African fabrication. They fail early, damage your reputation, and cost you the warranty call-out. Run these checks on every new batch:

  • Micrometer test: measure wall thickness at three points. Reject if below 95% of the stated value.
  • Certificate check: request the mill test certificate (MTC) with alloy, temper, and heat number.
  • Surface scratch test: anodized surfaces should resist a coin scratch; powder coating should not flake.
  • Weight check: weigh a 1 m sample. Substandard profiles are 10â25% lighter than specification.
  • Straightness: lay a 3 m length on a flat surface; deviation should be under 3 mm.
  • Weld quality at corners: cut a corner and inspect; poor welds show porosity and weak penetration.
  • Colour consistency: compare batch to batch under daylight, not workshop lighting.

Aluminum cutting and welding generate fine metal dust, sparks, and fumes. In African workshops with limited ventilation this is a serious health and fire risk. Always: (1) wear safety glasses and a P2/N95 respirator when cutting or grinding; (2) use local exhaust ventilation or work outdoors; (3) keep a dry powder or COâ extinguisher within 3 m of any cutting or welding station â never use water on an aluminum dust fire; (4) store aluminum swarf in a closed metal bin away from ignition sources; (5) wear cut-resistant gloves when handling sharp extruded edges; (6) never weld or cut near solvent, paint, or powder-coating booths. Aluminum dust is combustible and can explode if finely dispersed and ignited.

6. Sourcing Locally and Importing â A Business Decision

Most African fabricators use a hybrid model: import the main frame and sash profiles in bulk, and buy beads, accessories, and hardware locally. This balances landed cost against lead time and working capital.

Importing (typically from China, Turkey, UAE, or South Africa): lower unit cost at volume, wider system choice, but 6â12 week lead times, container-size minimums (often 20â25 tonnes), customs and clearing costs, and currency risk. Best for a stable, high-volume product line.

Local sourcing: shorter lead times, smaller order quantities, easier returns, and support for local industry. But prices are higher per kilogram, system choice is narrower, and quality control depends on the local supplier â verify with the same tests above.

Practical rule: if your monthly consumption of a given profile exceeds roughly 1.5 tonnes, importing directly is usually cheaper even after clearing. Below that, buy locally and keep your cash for labour, marketing, and installation capacity.
