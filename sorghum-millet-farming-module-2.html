Module 2

Varieties, Land & the Full Stand

Variety choice, land preparation and the discipline of thinning. This is where the yield gap is closed — or opened — before a single grain forms.

Duration: 1 month Delivery: 5 written lessons + knowledge check + capstone

Lessons in Module 2

1. Reading Variety Descriptions — Three Columns That Matter 2. Land Preparation for Small Seed 3. Sowing — Depth, Rate and Station Spacing 4. Thinning — The Single Most Skipped Step 5. Rotation and Intercropping
Lesson 1

Reading Variety Descriptions — Three Columns That Matter

~11 min
In this lesson
  • Read a variety description using three decision columns: maturity, use, Striga resistance
  • Reject varieties that do not match your rainfall zone
  • Identify malting-grade and feed-grade varieties

A variety catalogue is not a menu. It is a set of three-column fact sheets, and only three columns matter for a commercial decision:

  1. Maturity. 90–110 days (short), 110–130 days (medium), 130–150 days (long). Your rainfall zone decides this. Short-season for the Sahel and marginal zones; medium or long for higher rainfall belts.
  2. Use class. Food-grade white (low-tannin), food-grade brown (tannin, bird-resistant), malting-grade white (very low tannin, uniform grain), feed-grade (any high-yielding grain).
  3. Striga resistance. Some varieties are resistant, some tolerant, some susceptible. In Striga-infested fields, a susceptible variety will halve the yield. Get the resistance rating from the release document.

Two further columns are useful but not decisive on their own: bird resistance (tannin grain), and lodging resistance (short, stiff stems). Both are secondary to the three above.

In practice, the released improved varieties in most African countries fall into a manageable set: ICRISAT-derived sorghum lines (with prefix ICSV, ICSR, or a national code), pearl millet lines (ICMV, ICMH), and finger millet lines (IE, P-224, and national releases). Ask your extension officer or national research institute for the current release list — it changes slowly but it does change.

Lesson 2

Land Preparation for Small Seed

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Prepare a seedbed that will accept 2–3 cm sowing of very small seed
  • Decide between flat planting and tied ridges
  • Understand when to use station-placed manure

Sorghum and millet seed is small — 25,000 to 40,000 seeds per kilogram for sorghum, and up to 200,000 per kilogram for pearl millet. It cannot push through clods. Preparation is therefore not about ploughing depth but about getting a fine, level seedbed.

Ploughing and harrowing

One pass with an ox-plough or hand hoe at the start of the rains to break the surface, then a harrow or rake to break clods. In conservation-agriculture systems, a rip-line or planting basin is used instead and the soil is not inverted.

Tied ridges — the storm-harvesting technique

In areas with intense, short storms and long dry spells between them, tied ridges capture rainwater in place and stop it running off the field. Ridges are made 60–75 cm apart, tied every 1–2 m with small cross-bunds. The ridges are made before the rains and the ties are broken in very heavy rain to prevent waterlogging. This single technique can lift yield on dryland sorghum by 20–40% in the right conditions.

Manure — placed, not broadcast

Broadcast manure is largely wasted because the roots of a sorghum plant are concentrated in a narrow column beneath the station. The correct move is to place a handful of well-rotted manure in each planting station, cover it with a little soil, and sow on top. This concentrates the resource where the roots will go.

Lesson 3

Sowing — Depth, Rate and Station Spacing

~11 min
In this lesson
  • Sow sorghum and millet at the correct depth and rate
  • Space correctly for the target population
  • Decide between dibbling, drilling and broadcasting

Depth

2–3 cm. Deeper and the seedling runs out of energy before it reaches the surface. Shallower and it dries out. The test is whether you can still see the seed when you scratch the soil away — if you cannot, it is too deep.

Rate

Sorghum: 4–8 kg/ha if thinned to final stand. Pearl millet: 3–5 kg/ha. Finger millet: 8–12 kg/ha. The rate assumes you will over-sow and thin. If you are using precision planting with a planter calibrated to the target stand, half those rates.

Spacing

Sorghum: 75 cm between rows × 25 cm between stations, one plant per station after thinning. This gives about 53,000 plants per hectare. In drier zones, wider spacing (75 × 40 cm) is used. Pearl millet: 90 × 30 cm in the Sahel, tighter in higher-rainfall zones. Finger millet: 30 × 10 cm or broadcast and thinned.

Method

Dibbling (making a hole with a stick and dropping 3–5 seeds) is the most common and gives the most control. Drilling (opening a furrow) is faster but requires more thinning. Broadcasting is fastest but yields are lower unless the field is very clean and very level.

Lesson 4

Thinning — The Single Most Skipped Step

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Explain why thinning at the right stage gives the biggest free yield gain
  • Time the thinning correctly
  • Do it without damaging surviving plants

Between 10 and 20 days after emergence, most smallholder sorghum fields carry 3–5 plants per station where there should be one. Those extra plants are not a bonus. They compete for water, light and nutrients and cut the yield of the plant that should have been the crop.

Thinning — removing the extra plants by hand, keeping the strongest — takes a morning's work and typically lifts yield by 15–30%. There is no cheaper yield gain available anywhere.

When

When plants are 10–15 cm tall — tall enough to judge the strongest, small enough that the roots of survivors are not damaged. Thinning too early risks losing all plants to a pest; thinning too late means the damage to the survivor's roots is done.

How

Pull or cut the extra plants, keeping the healthiest. Do not pull hard if the soil is dry; a small hoe or knife works better. If in doubt, cut rather than pull — a partially disturbed survivor is worse than a cut extra plant.

The arithmetic

At 75 × 25 cm spacing with 4 plants per station, the field carries about 212,000 plants/ha. Thinned to 1 plant per station, it carries 53,000. The 4-plant field yields about 1.5 t/ha; the thinned field yields about 2.5 t/ha. Same seed, same rain, same fertiliser. Thinning is the difference.

Lesson 5

Rotation and Intercropping

~10 min
In this lesson
  • Choose a legume rotation that pays for itself
  • Design a sorghum/cowpea intercrop for dryland conditions
  • Understand why continuous sorghum degrades soil and invites Striga

Continuous cereal on the same plot degrades soil organic matter, builds up Striga seed bank, and increases pest pressure — particularly stem borer and millet head miner. Two strategies break the cycle.

Rotation with a legume

The best rotation partners for sorghum and millet are cowpea, groundnut, pigeonpea and green gram. Cowpea is the most widely adapted. A sorghum–cowpea rotation alternated each season fixes 40–80 kg/ha of nitrogen (cowpea is a modest fixer), disrupts Striga by forcing it to attach to a non-host, and diversifies household income. Groundnut pays better in some zones but requires calcium and is more demanding.

Intercropping

The traditional Sahelian intercrop is sorghum–cowpea, with cowpea planted between the sorghum rows. The cowpea fixes nitrogen, covers the ground (reducing erosion and weed competition), and provides a second food crop. Yield of sorghum in intercrop drops by 10–20% but the total system value is higher. Pigeonpea is a longer-term intercrop that adds a second harvest and improves soil structure over several years.

A simple plan for a 2-hectare farm in a Striga belt: 1 ha sorghum in rotation, 0.5 ha cowpea, 0.5 ha millet. Next season, swap the sorghum and cowpea plots. Ten seasons of records will show you which crop is carrying the farm.

Knowledge check — Module 2

1. What sowing depth is correct for sorghum?

Correct: 2–3 cm. Small seed cannot emerge from deeper.

2. What spacing is recommended for sorghum in normal rainfall zones?

Correct: 75 × 25 cm. About 53,000 plants/ha after thinning to one plant per station.

3. When should thinning be done?

Correct: 10–15 cm. Late enough to judge the strongest, early enough to avoid root damage to survivors.

4. What is the main advantage of tied ridges?

Correct: they capture rainwater. Gains of 20–40% yield in the right conditions.

5. Why rotate sorghum with cowpea?

Correct: nitrogen and Striga disruption. Cowpea fixes 40–80 kg/ha N and forces Striga to attach to a non-host.

Module 2 capstone

Prepare a variety and planting plan for the coming season. Include: (1) the variety name and its maturity, use class and Striga resistance rating; (2) the target plant population and the spacing you will use; (3) your thinning date in the calendar; (4) whether you will use tied ridges and why; (5) your rotation legume and where it will sit in the rotation.

Write it on one page. Photograph the seed packet. File both.
