1. Building and Maintaining a Forge Fire

To build a forge fire, you need three things: fuel, air, and a firepot. In Africa, charcoal is the most common fuel. Use lump charcoal, not briquettesâbriquettes contain binders that clog your fire. A good forge fire starts with a bed of ash. Clear out clinkers (fused ash and impurities) from your last fire; they block airflow. Place a handful of dry kindling (small sticks or paper) in the bottom of your firepot, then add a layer of charcoal around it, leaving a hollow in the center. Light the kindling from the top or side. Once flames catch, slowly add more charcoal around the edges, not on top. Turn on your air supplyâa hand-cranked blower, bellows, or electric fan. Start with a gentle airflow. Too much air blows heat away and burns fuel fast; too little and the fire smolders. You want a steady, low roar. As the fire grows, push charcoal inward to form a mound. The hottest zone is just above the air inlet (the tuyere). Thatâs where youâll place your metal. Maintain the fire by adding charcoal every 10â15 minutes, always around the edges, not directly on the hot spot. Use a rake or poker to keep the fire compact. Watch for clinkersâthey look like gray, rocky lumps. Remove them with a poker or spoon before they block air. A well-maintained fire should reach welding heat (around 1,200Â°C to 1,300Â°C) in 15â20 minutes. If your fire dies down, donât panic. Clear the ash, add fresh charcoal, and give it air. Never use water to control a forge fireâit cracks the firepot and creates steam. Instead, control heat by adjusting airflow and fuel. In Africa, many smiths use a simple clay-lined forge made from termite mound soil or river clay. That works fine. The key is a deep firepot (at least 15 cm deep) and a consistent air supply. Practice building and tending a fire until you can keep it steady for an hour without it going out or overheating. That skill is the foundation of every blade youâll make.

2. Controlling Heat: Color and Temperature

Steel changes color as it heats. Learning those colors tells you the temperature without a thermometer. In a dimly lit forge, you can judge heat by the glow. Hereâs a practical guide for common high-carbon steels (like 1080 or 5160):

- Dark red (around 550Â°C): Too cold to forge. The steel wonât move; hitting it can crack it.
- Cherry red (700â800Â°C): Forging starts here. You can shape it, but itâs stiff. Good for straightening.
- Orange (900â950Â°C): Ideal forging temperature. The steel moves easily under the hammer. This is your working range.
- Yellow (1,000â1,100Â°C): Hotter, but risky. Grain grows, and you might burn the steel. Use only for quick bends.
- White (1,200Â°C+): Welding heat. Too hot for forging; it sparks and burns.

In Africa, sunlight washes out colors. Work in a shaded area or build a three-sided forge hood to block light. At night, colors are easier to see. Use a magnet to check temperature: steel becomes non-magnetic at around 770Â°C (cherry red). Thatâs a useful reference. For forging, aim for orange. If you see sparks shooting off the steel, itâs too hotâpull it out and let it cool slightly. To control heat, adjust your air supply and how deep you bury the steel in the fire. The hottest spot is near the tuyere. For a slower heat, place the steel higher in the fire. For a fast heat, bury it near the air inlet. Always rotate the steel as it heats so it warms evenly. Donât leave it in one spotâthat causes hot spots and uneven forging. Quenching is also about heat control. For most simple blades, heat to non-magnetic (cherry red), then quench in warm oil (around 50â60Â°C). Vegetable oil works. Water is too fast and can crack the blade. After quenching, temper by heating to 200â250Â°C (straw yellow) for one hour. That relieves stress. Practice reading colors with a piece of scrap steel. Heat it, watch the colors change, and note how it moves under the hammer at each stage. Within a few sessions, youâll know the right temperature by eye.

3. Basic Hammer Techniques: Grip, Stance, and Strikes

A blacksmithâs hammer is not a sledgehammer. For most blade work, use a 1â1.5 kg (2â3 lb) cross-peen or ball-peen hammer. The handle should be wood (hickory or local hardwood) and fit your hand. Grip the handle near the endânot tight, but firm. Your thumb wraps around, and your index finger points along the handle. That gives control and power. Donât choke up unless you need delicate taps. Stand with your feet shoulder-width apart, your anvil at waist height. Your hammer hand is on the same side as your dominant foot (right-handed: right foot back). Your tong hand holds the steel on the anvil. Keep your elbow slightly bent, not locked. Your body rotates from the hips, not just the arm. Thatâs where power comes from. There are three basic strikes:

1. **Drawing strike**: Used to stretch and taper. Raise the hammer to about ear height, then bring it down at a slight angle, pulling the steel toward you as you hit. The hammer face hits flat, then you drag it slightly. This elongates the metal.
2. **Upsetting strike**: Used to thicken. Hit straight down with a heavy blow, no drag. The metal squashes lengthwise.
3. **Fuller strike**: Used with a top fuller (a rounded tool) to create shoulders or grooves. Strike directly on the fuller.

For all strikes, let the hammer do the work. Donât force it. Use a loose grip and let gravity and momentum help. Aim for the anvil face, not the steelâyour hammer should land flat. If you hit at an angle, you leave hammer marks and waste energy. Practice on cold steel or a piece of lead first. Strike 50 times, rest, then 50 more. In Africa, many smiths work sitting on a low stool with a small anvil. Thatâs fine for light work, but for blades, standing gives better control. Keep your anvil clean and free of scale. Brush it off with a wire brush. A clean anvil means clean hammer blows. After a week of daily practice, your accuracy and stamina will improve. The goal is to hit the same spot 10 times in a row without looking. Thatâs when youâre ready to forge a blade.

4. Using Tongs and Holding Tools Safely

Tongs are your hands in the forge. Without them, you canât hold hot steel. Buy or make a few basic tongs: flat-jaw tongs for flat stock, bolt tongs for round stock, and wolf-jaw tongs for square or irregular shapes. In Africa, you can make tongs from old car leaf springs or rebar. Forge them to fit your work. The jaws should grip the steel securely but not so tight that you canât release it quickly. The reins (handles) should be long enough to keep your hands away from the heatâat least 30 cm. Before you use tongs, check the rivet or hinge. It should move freely but not be loose. A loose rivet can pop open and drop hot steel. When you pick up hot steel, close the jaws around it and squeeze the reins. The steel should not wobble. If it does, adjust the jaws or use a different tong. Always hold the tongs with your palm facing up, thumb on top. That gives you better control and keeps your knuckles away from the anvil. When you strike, keep the tong hand steady. The steel should rest flat on the anvil. If you need to turn the steel, rotate your wrist, not your whole arm. Never hold the steel with your bare hand near the hot end. Even a brief touch causes a bad burn. Wear leather gloves on your tong hand, but not on your hammer handâgloves reduce your grip and feel. Keep a bucket of water or sand nearby for emergencies. If you drop hot steel, donât try to catch it. Step back and let it fall. Then pick it up with tongs after it cools. Store tongs on a rack near the forge, not on the floor where you can trip. Inspect them daily for cracks or bent jaws. A broken tong can send hot steel flying. Practice picking up and moving a piece of cold steel with your tongs until itâs automatic. Then do it with hot steel, but start with a low heat (red) so you have time to react. Safety is a habit, not a rule.

5. Introduction to Anvil Tools: Hardy, Pritchel, and Horn

Your anvil is more than a flat surface. It has three main features youâll use every day: the hardy hole, the pritchel hole, and the horn. The hardy hole is a square hole near the heel (the back) of the anvil. It holds hardy toolsâcutters, fullers, and bottom swages. To use a hardy cutter, place it in the hole with the cutting edge up. Heat your steel to orange, place it on the cutter, and strike it with your hammer. The cutter shears the metal. For a clean cut, strike firmly and donât twist. After cutting, the steel may stick to the cutter. Tap it loose with a hammer. The pritchel hole is a round hole near the hardy hole. Itâs for punching holes. Heat your steel to yellow (around 1,000Â°C), place it over the pritchel hole, and drive a punch through it with your hammer. The hole gives the punch somewhere to go without hitting the anvil face. Punch from one side until you feel the punch break through, then flip the steel and punch from the other side. That prevents a ragged hole. The horn is the pointed end of the anvil. Itâs for bending and shaping curves. To make a curve, place your steel on the horn and strike it with glancing blows. The hornâs taper lets you make different radius bends. For a tight curve, use the tip; for a gentle curve, use the base. You can also use the horn to spread metal or to true up a ring. In Africa, many anvils are just a piece of railroad track or a heavy block. Thatâs fine. You can make a hardy hole by drilling and filing a square hole in a block, or you can hold a cutter in a vise. The pritchel hole can be a hole drilled in a stump. The horn can be a rounded piece of pipe or a car axle. The principle is the same: support the steel and use the tool. Keep your anvil tools clean and free of burrs. A burr on a hardy cutter can tear the steel. Dress them with a file. Always wear safety glasses when using hardy toolsâchips can fly. Practice on scrap steel until you can cut, punch, and bend accurately. These three tools will save you hours of work.

6. Practice Project: Forging a Simple Spike

Now youâll forge a simple spike. This project uses all the skills from this module: fire management, heat control, hammer technique, tongs, and anvil tools. Youâll need a piece of mild steel or old rebar, about 20 cm long and 1 cm thick. Start by building a clean, hot fire. Heat the steel to orange (900â950Â°C). Hold it with flat-jaw tongs. Place it on the anvil and begin drawing out one end. Use the drawing strike: hit at a slight angle and pull the steel toward you. Work from the center toward the end. Rotate the steel 90 degrees every few blows to keep it square. After 5â6 heats, you should have a taper about 5 cm long, ending in a point. Donât rushâif the steel cools below cherry red, put it back in the fire. For the point, use lighter blows and turn the steel frequently. Once the point is formed, itâs time to make the head. Heat the other end to orange. Hold the spike vertically on the anvil face, point down. Strike the top end with a heavy, straight blow. That upsets the metal, making a head. You may need to do this in two or three heats. If the head starts to bend, straighten it on the anvil face. Now you have a spike. If you want a hole in the head (for hanging), use the pritchel hole. Heat the head to yellow, place it over the pritchel hole, and punch a hole with a 6 mm punch. Flip and punch from the other side. Finally, use the horn to straighten any curves. Hold the spike by the point and sight down the shank. If itâs bent, place it on the horn and strike with light blows until straight. Quench the spike in oil or water to cool it. For mild steel, water is fine. Dry it and wire-brush off scale. You now have a functional spike. Use it as a tent stake, a poker, or a gift. The more spikes you make, the better your hammer control. Make ten of them. Each one will be faster and cleaner. Thatâs how you build skill.

Practical Reference: Forge Fundamentals for African Bladesmiths

This practical reference consolidates the core competencies required to operate a small-scale forge safely and profitably. In the African contextâwhere bladesmithing supports livelihoods from the Sahel to Southern Africaâmastery of fire control, hammer discipline, and tool handling directly determines product quality, fuel efficiency, and workshop safety. Whether you are forging agricultural tools, knives for local markets, or decorative ironwork for urban buyers, the fundamentals below are your business foundation.

Forge work in Africa often relies on locally sourced materials: charcoal from sustainably managed woodlands, clay from riverbanks, and recycled scrap steel from vehicles and machinery. This resourcefulness is a strength, but it demands disciplined technique. Poor fire management wastes fuelâa significant cost in rural areas where charcoal prices have risen sharply. Poor hammer control wastes steel and time. Poor safety practices cost lives and livelihoods.

Fire Control: The Heart of the Forge

Your forge fire is not a campfire. It is a controlled chemical reactor. The goal is to reach and maintain the correct temperature for the steel you are working, without oxidizing the surface or burning the carbon out of the metal. In African workshops, charcoal is the most common fuel, though coal and gas forges are increasingly used in urban areas.

The fire has three zones: the oxidizing zone (where excess air burns hot and clean), the reducing zone (where oxygen is limited and carbon monoxide protects the steel), and the neutral zone (the sweet spot where steel heats without scaling). You want your workpiece in the neutral or reducing zoneâtypically the upper part of the fire pot, just above the tuyere.

Fuel choice matters. Charcoal burns hot and clean but fast; you must feed it regularly. Coal burns longer and can be coked, but it produces sulfur smoke that must be managed. In many African cities, coal is expensive or unavailable, making charcoal the practical choice. Buy in bulk during the dry season when prices are lower, and store it dryâwet charcoal wastes energy driving off moisture.

For business purposes, consistent heat control means consistent product quality. A customer buying a hoe or knife expects it to hold an edge and not break. If you overheat steel, you burn carbon and create weak grain structure. If you forge too cold, you create micro-cracks. Both lead to returns, refunds, and lost reputation. Train your eye, but also consider investing in a simple thermocouple if your market demands precisionâmany South African and Kenyan suppliers now stock affordable pyrometers.

Hammer Technique: Grip, Stance, and Strikes

The hammer is your primary shaping tool. A bad grip wastes energy and causes injury. Hold the hammer near the end of the handle for maximum leverage, but not so far that you lose control. Your grip should be firm but relaxedâthink of holding a bird: tight enough to keep it from flying away, loose enough not to crush it.

Stance: feet shoulder-width apart, hammer-side foot slightly forward. Your anvil should be at knuckle height when you stand naturally. This reduces back strain and improves accuracy. In African workshops where anvils are often improvised from railway rail or truck axles, adjust your stance to the anvil heightânot the other way around.

Strikes: there are three basic strikes. The heavy blow uses shoulder and back muscles to move metal fast. The controlled blow uses wrist and forearm for precision. The tap is a light correction or planishing strike. Beginners should practice on mild steel or clay before working expensive blade steel.

Efficiency matters for business. A smith who can draw out a knife blank in 20 minutes instead of 40 can produce twice the inventory. Practice your hammer control dailyâeven 15 minutes of deliberate practice on scrap steel improves speed and reduces wasted fuel.

Using Tongs and Holding Tools Safely

Tongs are your hands in the fire. Poor tong technique leads to dropped workpieces, burns, and wasted material. Choose tongs that fit your stock sizeâa loose fit means the workpiece shifts during hammering. For flat stock, use flat-jaw tongs; for round stock, use V-jaw or bolt tongs.

Keep tongs cool. If the jaws heat up, quench them in water. Never leave tongs in the fireâthey will lose their temper and bend. When holding a workpiece, grip firmly but allow slight movement so you can feel the metal's position. Your off-hand controls the workpiece; your hammer hand controls the shape.

In African workshops, tongs are often made from scrap leaf springs or rebar. This is fine if the steel is properly forged and tempered. A good pair of tongs lasts years and is a worthwhile investment. If you sell tools, tongs are a high-margin productâevery smith needs multiple pairs.

Anvil Tools: Hardy, Pritchel, and Horn

Your anvil is not just a flat surface. The hardy hole, pritchel hole, and horn are essential for efficient work. The hardy hole (square hole) holds hardy toolsâcutters, fullers, and bending forks. The pritchel hole (round hole) is for punching holes in hot metal; the punch pushes through and the slug falls out the bottom. The horn (conical end) is for bending curves, forming rings, and drawing out small sections.

In African workshops, anvils are often improvised. A section of railway rail makes a serviceable anvil, but it lacks a hardy hole. You can weld a square tube to the side to create one, or use a separate stake anvil for hardy tools. The investment in a proper anvilâeven a small oneâpays off in productivity. Practice Project: Forging a Simple Spike

The spike is the ideal beginner project. It teaches drawing out, tapering, and pointingâcore skills for bladesmithing. Use 10â12mm mild steel round bar. Heat the end to orange, then hammer it to a square taper. Work on the horn to round the taper, then finish on the flat face. Cut to length with a hot cut or hardy cutter. The spike can be used for tent pegs, garden stakes, or as a market product. In many African markets, forged spikes sell well to construction workers and farmers.

For business: calculate your material cost, fuel cost, and time. If a spike takes 10 minutes and sells for 50 KES, and your fuel costs 20 KES, your labor earns 30 KESâabout $0.25. That is not sustainable. Scale up: make 20 spikes in a batch, use the fire efficiently, and sell wholesale to hardware stores. Quick Reference Checklist

  • Fire: Build a clean fire with adequate air; keep workpiece in neutral/reducing zone.
  • Heat: Judge color in shade; never forge below cherry red or above yellow without purpose.
  • Hammer: Grip near handle end; stance balanced; use heavy, controlled, and tap strikes deliberately.
  • Tongs: Fit tongs to stock; keep jaws cool; never leave tongs in fire.
  • Anvil: Use hardy for cutting, pritchel for punching, horn for bending.
  • Safety: Gloves, glasses, apron, ventilation, water bucket, clear zone.
  • Fuel: Buy charcoal in bulk during dry season; store dry.
  • Business: Track time, fuel, and material per product; batch production for efficiency.
