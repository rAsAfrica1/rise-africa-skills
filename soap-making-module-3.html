Lessons

1. Measuring and Mixing Lye Solution

Lye, also called sodium hydroxide (NaOH), is the chemical that turns oils into soap. You must handle it with respect. Always wear rubber gloves, safety goggles, and a long-sleeved shirt. Work in a well-ventilated areaideally outdoors or near an open window. Never use aluminium containers; lye reacts with aluminium and produces dangerous hydrogen gas. Use heat-resistant glass, stainless steel, or heavy plastic (PP #5).

First, weigh your lye and water separately using a digital kitchen scale accurate to 1 gram. A typical recipe for a 1 kg batch of soap might call for 130 g lye and 300 g water. Always add lye to water, never water to lye. Pouring water onto lye can cause a violent boil-up and splatter. Stir gently with a stainless steel spoon until the lye is fully dissolved. The mixture will get very hotup to 80C or more. Set it aside to cool to about 40-45C before mixing with oils. You can speed cooling by placing the container in a basin of cold water.

In Africa, water quality varies. Use distilled or rain water if possible. Tap water may contain minerals that interfere with soap. If you only have tap water, boil it and let it cool to reduce some impurities. Measure carefully: too little lye leaves unsaponified oil (soft, greasy soap), too much lye makes harsh, lye-heavy soap that can burn skin. Follow your recipe exactly.

Always keep a bottle of white vinegar nearby. If lye splashes on your skin, flush with plenty of cool water, then wash with vinegar to neutralise. Never store lye solution in a closed container while hotpressure can build up. Label your containers clearly. Keep children and animals away. After use, clean all tools with vinegar first, then soap and water.

2. Preparing and Heating Oils

Cold process soap uses a blend of solid and liquid oils. Common solid oils in Africa include palm oil, coconut oil, shea butter, and cocoa butter. Liquid oils include olive oil, sunflower oil, and groundnut oil. A simple beginner recipe: 500 g palm oil, 300 g coconut oil, 200 g olive oil. Weigh each oil accurately on your scale. If you use shea butter, it often comes in chunksgrate or chop it finely so it melts evenly.

Put all oils into a stainless steel pot. Heat gently on a low stove or charcoal stove, stirring until everything is melted. Do not overheat. Once melted, remove from heat and let it cool to about 40-45C. If you don't have a thermometer, test by touch: the pot should feel warm but not hotlike bathwater. If it's too hot, let it sit. If it's too cold, the solid oils will start to harden. You want a clear, liquid oil mixture.

In many African markets, palm oil is red and unrefined. That's fine for soapit adds a nice orange colour and gentle cleansing. Coconut oil makes big bubbles but can be drying if used above 30% of total oils. Shea butter adds creamy lather and skin-conditioning properties. If you cannot find one oil, substitute with another from the same category (solid for solid, liquid for liquid), but keep the total weight the same. Adjusting the recipe changes the soap's qualities.

Before mixing with lye, ensure your oils are at the right temperature. If oils are too hot, they can accelerate trace and cause discoloration. If too cold, they may solidify when lye is added. Use a thermometer if you have one; if not, aim for the same warmth as your lye solution. Both should feel comfortably warm to the touch. Now you are ready to combine.

3. Achieving Emulsion and Trace

Emulsion is when oil and lye water mix into a stable, creamy blend. Trace is when the mixture thickens enough that a drizzle leaves a visible trail on the surface. To get there, pour your lye solution slowly into the oils while stirring gently with a stainless steel spoon or whisk. Stir for 2-3 minutes by handthis is called 'cooking' the mixture. You'll see it change from oily and separate to a uniform, opaque liquid. That's emulsion.

Now use an immersion blender (stick blender) to speed up trace. Pulse it in short bursts5 seconds on, 5 seconds offwhile stirring by hand between pulses. Keep the blender head fully submerged to avoid splashing and introducing air bubbles. After 1-2 minutes, you'll notice the mixture thickening. To test for trace, lift your spoon and let some batter drip back into the pot. If the drip leaves a faint line that stays visible for a second or two, you have a light trace. If the line is clear and holds, that's medium trace. For most recipes, you want light to medium trace before pouring.

In hot African climates, trace can happen very fast. If your mixture becomes too thick (like pudding), you've reached heavy trace. You can still pour, but you must work quickly. To slow trace, use cooler temperatures (around 35-40C) and avoid over-blending. Some essential oils, like clove or cinnamon, also accelerate trace. If you want to add colour or fragrance, do it at light trace and stir gently by handnot with the blender.

If your mixture separates after blending, don't panic. Keep blending in short bursts until it comes together. If it stays separated, your temperatures may be too far apart. Warm the oils slightly and try again. Once you have a stable emulsion and trace, you're ready to pour into molds.

4. Pouring into Molds

Choose your mold based on what you have. Silicone molds are easiestsoap pops out cleanly. Wooden molds lined with baking paper work well. Plastic containers (like ice cream tubs) can be used but may need flexible liners. Avoid metal molds unless they are stainless steel; other metals react with lye. For a 1 kg batch, a mold about 20 cm x 10 cm x 7 cm is a good size.

Pour the soap batter slowly and steadily from a low height to reduce air bubbles. If you want a swirl design, pour a small amount of coloured batter, then alternate with plain batter. Use a spoon or skewer to swirl gentlydon't overmix. For plain soap, just pour it all in. Tap the mold gently on the counter a few times to release trapped air. Smooth the top with a spatula or spoon.

In Africa, many soap makers use wooden boxes lined with banana leaves or plastic bags. Banana leaves give a natural look but can leave creases. Plastic bags are cheap and effectivejust make sure they are clean and free of holes. If you use a cardboard box, line it with baking paper or a plastic bag to prevent sticking. The mold should be deep enough to hold the batter; leave about 1 cm of space at the top for expansion during gel phase.

After pouring, cover the mold with a lid or plastic wrap. This helps keep heat in for gel phase (next lesson). If you are not insulating, you can leave it uncovered, but the soap may not gel fully. Place the mold in a safe, level spot where it won't be disturbed. Do not move it for at least 24 hours. Clean your tools immediatelysoap batter hardens quickly. Scrape excess batter into a separate container; you can use it to make small test bars. Now your soap needs to rest and saponify.

5. Insulating and Gel Phase

Gel phase is when the soap heats up and becomes translucent, like jelly. It happens because saponification generates heat. A full gel phase makes soap harder, shinier, and more uniform in colour. Some soap makers prefer no gel for a creamier look, but for beginners, gelling is easier and faster. To encourage gel, insulate your mold. Wrap it in a thick towel or blanket, or place it in a cardboard box with a lid. In cold weather, you can add a hot water bottle next to the moldnot directly on top.

In hot African climates, gel phase can happen quickly and may even overheat. If the soap gets too hot, it can crack or develop a cavern (a hollow pocket). To prevent overheating, check your soap after 1 hour. If the mold feels very hot to the touch, remove some insulation. You can also place the mold on a wire rack to allow air circulation. A mild gel is fine; a runaway gel is not. If you see cracks, don't worrythey can be trimmed later.

The gel phase usually lasts 1-2 hours. You'll notice the centre of the soap becomes darker and more translucent, spreading outward. Once the whole loaf looks uniform, the gel is complete. Then let it cool slowly. Do not unmold while it's still warmit will be soft and sticky. Leave it insulated for 24 hours, then unwrap and check. If the soap is still warm, wait another 12 hours.

In very hot weather, you might skip insulation entirely. Just cover the mold with a light cloth. The soap will still saponify, but may not gel. That's okayit will just take longer to harden. The key is to avoid drastic temperature changes. A steady, warm environment (25-30C) is ideal. After 24-48 hours, your soap should be firm enough to unmold. If it's still soft, wait another day. Patience here prevents ruined batches.

6. Unmolding and Cutting

After 24-48 hours, your soap should be firm enough to unmold. Test by pressing gently with your fingerif it leaves no indent, it's ready. If it's still soft, wait another 12-24 hours. To unmold, turn the mold upside down and press on the bottom. For silicone molds, peel the sides away first. For wooden or plastic molds, you may need to tap firmly. If the soap sticks, place the mold in the freezer for 30 minutesthe soap will shrink slightly and release. Never use a knife to pry it out; you'll damage the mold and the soap.

Once unmolded, you'll see your loaf. If you used a liner, peel it off. If the soap has rough edges or cracks, you can trim them with a knife. Now cut the loaf into bars. A standard bar is about 2.5 cm thick and 7-8 cm wide. Use a sharp, straight knife or a soap cutter. For even bars, mark the loaf with a ruler first. Cut with a gentle sawing motiondon't press too hard, or the soap may crack. A wire cutter (like a cheese slicer) works well if you have one.

In Africa, many soap makers use a machete or a long kitchen knife. That's finejust keep it clean and sharp. Cut on a clean surface, like a plastic cutting board. Wear gloves if the soap is still slightly soft, as it may contain active lye. After cutting, place the bars in a single layer on a drying rack or cardboard. They need to cure for 4-6 weeks in a cool, dry, well-ventilated place. Turn them once a week so they dry evenly. Curing makes the soap milder and harder.

If you want to sell, stamp or label your bars after curing. Store them in a dry place away from direct sunlight. Properly cured soap lasts for years. Remember: the soap is still caustic when first unmoldedwash your hands after handling. After curing, it's safe to use. Congratulationsyou've made cold process soap from scratch!

<h2>Practical Reference: Cold Process Soap Making Basics</h2>

<p>In the African artisanal soap sector, cold process (CP) soap making is the most commercially viable entry point for small and medium enterprises (SMEs). Unlike hot process, CP requires no external heat after the initial oil melting stage, making it accessible to producers with limited energy infrastructure. The process relies on the exothermic reaction between fatty acids in oils and sodium hydroxide (lye), producing soap and glycerin. For African agribusinesses, CP soap offers a high-margin product that can be differentiated through local ingredientsshea butter, baobab oil, moringa, coconut oil, palm kernel oil, and essential oils from lemongrass, eucalyptus, or tea tree. This practical reference distils the six core lessons into actionable, business-oriented guidance, with an emphasis on consistency, safety, and scalability.</p>

<h3>Lesson 1: Measuring and Mixing Lye Solution</h3>
<p>Lye (sodium hydroxide, NaOH) is the most dangerous input in soap making and the most critical for saponification. In Africa, lye is widely available as drain cleaner or caustic soda flakes, but purity varies. Always source food-grade or cosmetic-grade NaOH with 99% purity. Use a digital scale accurate to 0.1 g. The standard lye concentration for CP soap is 3033% (i.e., 3033 g NaOH per 100 g water), though this can be adjusted for hard water or high-shea recipes.</p>
<p>Mixing rule: always add lye to water, never water to lye. The reaction is exothermic and can reach 8090C. Stir until fully dissolved, then cool to 4045C before combining with oils. In hot African climates, place the container in a water bath to accelerate cooling. Never use aluminium containerslye corrodes them and releases hydrogen gas.</p>

<h3>Lesson 2: Preparing and Heating Oils</h3>
<p>Oil selection determines the soap's hardness, cleansing power, conditioning, lather, and shelf life. For African producers, the most cost-effective base is a blend of palm oil or palm kernel oil (hardness, lather), coconut oil (cleansing, lather), and shea butter or baobab oil (conditioning). A typical balanced recipe: 30% coconut, 30% palm, 25% olive, 15% shea. This yields a hard bar with good lather and mild conditioning.</p>
<p>Melt solid oils (coconut, palm, shea) in a stainless steel pot over low heat. Once melted, add liquid oils (olive, baobab, sunflower). Target temperature: 4045C. If oils are too hot, they accelerate trace and can cause overheating; if too cold, they may cause false trace or uneven saponification. In tropical climates, ambient temperatures often exceed 30C, so minimal heating is required.</p>

<h3>Lesson 3: Achieving Emulsion and Trace</h3>
<p>Emulsion occurs when lye solution and oils are mixed to a uniform, opaque consistencyno separate oil or water layers. Trace is the point where the mixture thickens enough that a drizzle leaves a visible trail on the surface. Light trace is ideal for swirling; medium trace for layered soaps; heavy trace for textured tops. Over-blending leads to "soap on a stick"a seized batch that is difficult to pour.</p>
<p>Use a stick blender in short pulses (510 seconds) to reach emulsion, then stir by hand to control trace. Add fragrance or essential oils at light trace, and colourants just before pouring. In hot climates, work quickly; trace accelerates with heat. If the mixture separates, stick blend again briefly.</p>

<h3>Lesson 4: Pouring into Molds</h3>
<p>Pour at light-to-medium trace into silicone, wooden, or PVC molds. Silicone molds are preferred for ease of unmolding; wooden molds require freezer paper or silicone liners. Tap the mold on the counter to release air bubbles. For layered designs, pour one layer, let it set slightly, then pour the next. In Africa, locally fabricated wooden molds are cost-effective and can be lined with plastic sheets.</p>

<h3>Lesson 5: Insulating and Gel Phase</h3>
<p>After pouring, cover the mold with a lid or cardboard and wrap in towels or a blanket. Insulation traps the exothermic heat, encouraging gel phasea translucent, jelly-like state where saponification completes rapidly. Gel phase produces a harder, more uniform bar with richer colours. However, in hot African climates, over-insulation can cause overheating, cracking, or "alien brains" (cavernous texture). If ambient temperature exceeds 30C, insulate lightly or not at all. Un-gelled soap is fine but may be softer and require longer cure.</p>

<h3>Lesson 6: Unmolding and Cutting</h3>
<p>Unmold after 2472 hours, when the soap is firm and cool. If it sticks, place the mold in the freezer for 3060 minutes. Cut with a stainless steel blade, wire cutter, or miter box for uniform bars. Wear glovesfresh soap is still alkaline. Cure the cut bars on a rack in a cool, dry, ventilated area for 46 weeks. Cure reduces water content, increases hardness, and mellows the pH. In humid African regions, use a dehumidifier or fan to prevent sweating.</p>
