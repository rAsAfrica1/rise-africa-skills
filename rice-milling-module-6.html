1. Sifting and Grading Equipment

Sifting and grading equipment separates rice by size and removes impurities. In Africa, common tools include rotary sifters, vibrating screens, and manual sieves. A rotary sifter has a rotating drum with perforated screens; it spins at 200-300 RPM, separating broken rice from head rice. For small mills, a vibrating screen with 2-3 decks is affordable. The top deck might have 4 mm holes to remove large stones, the middle deck 2 mm holes to separate head rice, and the bottom deck 1 mm holes for small brokens. Capacity ranges from 500 kg/hour to 2 tons/hour. Locally fabricated screens from Uganda or Nigeria cost $500-$2000. Operating tips: adjust screen angle to 10-15 degrees for optimal flow; clean screens every 4 hours to prevent clogging; replace worn screens when holes enlarge by 10%. Safety: lock out power before maintenance. In rural areas, women often use hand sieves with 2 mm mesh to grade rice for local markets. This manual method handles 20-50 kg/hour but is labor-intensive. For better efficiency, a pedal-operated sifter can double output. Regular maintenance includes checking belt tension and lubricating bearings weekly. Remember, proper sifting reduces broken rice in final product, increasing market value by 10-20%.

2. Length and Thickness Grading

Length and thickness grading separates rice grains into uniform sizes. This is crucial because buyers pay more for consistent grain length. The main equipment is a length grader, often a disk or indent cylinder separator. An indent cylinder has small pockets that lift shorter grains as it rotates; longer grains stay in the cylinder and exit separately. Typical cylinder speed is 30-50 RPM. For thickness grading, a thickness grader uses slotted screens: thin grains fall through slots, while thick grains pass over. In Africa, local artisans in Tanzania and Nigeria make simple length graders using wire mesh with 6 mm and 8 mm slots. A motorized unit costs $1,500-$3,000 and processes 1-2 tons/hour. Manual length grading using a slotted sieve can achieve 100 kg/hour. Adjustments: set indent cylinder pockets to match desired length (e.g., 7 mm for long grain). For thickness, slot width should be 1.8-2.0 mm for standard rice. Maintenance: clean pockets daily with a brush; check for worn slots every 100 hours. Calibration: test with a sample of known grain sizes. Safety: wear gloves when handling screens. In West Africa, length grading is often done after destoning to ensure uniform product for export. Proper grading reduces broken rice during milling and improves cooking quality. Aim for 90% uniformity in head rice length.

3. Destoners and Gravity Separators

Destoners and gravity separators remove stones and other heavy impurities from rice. A destoner uses a vibrating deck with a porous surface and upward airflow. Stones, being heavier, stay on the deck and move to a separate outlet, while lighter rice floats and exits at the top. Typical deck angle is 5-10 degrees, vibration frequency 300-500 strokes per minute, and air velocity 10-15 m/s. Capacity ranges from 1 to 5 tons/hour. In Africa, common brands include Satake and local copies from Kenya. A small destoner costs $2,000-$5,000. Gravity separators (also called specific gravity separators) separate by density, removing immature grains, stones, and other debris. They use a similar principle but with a fluidized bed. For small mills, a combined destoner-gravity separator is efficient. Operating tips: adjust air flow based on rice moisture; too much air blows rice away, too little leaves stones. Clean the deck every 8 hours. Maintenance: check rubber mounts for cracks, lubricate bearings monthly. Safety: ensure proper grounding to avoid static shocks. In Nigeria, destoners are essential for rice destined for high-end markets. Without destoning, stones can damage milling equipment and cause customer complaints. A well-tuned destoner can remove 99% of stones larger than 2 mm. Test by mixing a known number of stones in rice and checking removal rate.

4. Color Sorters and Their Operation

Color sorters remove discolored grains, such as yellow, black, or chalky rice, using optical sensors. Modern color sorters use CCD cameras and air jets to eject defective grains. They process 2-10 tons/hour. In Africa, color sorters are increasingly common in medium to large mills. A basic unit costs $10,000-$30,000. Operation: rice is fed into a chute, passing in front of cameras. When a defect is detected, a solenoid valve blasts a short pulse of air (0.1-0.2 seconds) to knock the grain out. Sensitivity is adjustable; too high sensitivity wastes good rice, too low misses defects. Calibration: use a sample with known defects to set sensitivity. Maintenance: clean cameras and lenses daily; check air filters weekly; replace nozzles annually. Power requirement: 3-5 kW. In Uganda, millers use color sorters to meet export standards for East African markets. For small mills, renting a mobile color sorter service is an option. Safety: never put hands near air jets; they can cause injury. Training: operators need 1-2 days to learn. Proper color sorting can increase rice grade by one level, raising price by 5-10%. Test performance by running 1 kg sample and counting defects before and after.

5. Broken Rice Separation and Management

Broken rice separation removes broken grains from head rice. This is done using a plan sifter or a length grader with small holes. Typically, a plan sifter has multiple layers of screens; the top screen retains head rice (over 6 mm), while brokens fall through. Broken rice is classified as large brokens (4-6 mm) and small brokens (less than 4 mm). In Africa, broken rice is often sold at 30-50% lower price than head rice. However, it can be used for porridge, animal feed, or brewing. Management: aim to minimize breakage during milling by proper adjustment of milling machines. Separation efficiency should be 95% or higher. Equipment: a vibratory separator with 5 mm and 3 mm screens. Capacity: 1-3 tons/hour. Cost: $1,000-$3,000. Operation: feed rice evenly; adjust vibration amplitude to 5-8 mm. Maintenance: replace screens when holes enlarge; clean screens every shift. In Senegal, broken rice is often blended with head rice for local consumption. For export, brokens must be below 5%. To reduce brokens, ensure paddy is dried to 14% moisture before milling. Test separation by weighing 100 g sample and sorting manually. Proper broken rice management adds value by finding alternative markets.

6. Final Product Classification

Final product classification grades milled rice into standard categories based on length, wholeness, and color. Common grades in Africa include: Grade 1 (head rice, 90% or more whole grains, no discoloration), Grade 2 (70-89% whole grains, minor discoloration), and Grade 3 (50-69% whole grains, some brokens). Export standards often require Grade 1. Classification is done manually or with automated graders. In small mills, workers use a series of sieves and visual inspection. A 1 kg sample is taken, and grains are sorted into whole, large broken, small broken, and defects. Percentages are calculated. For consistency, use a standardized sample divider. In Nigeria, the Standards Organisation of Nigeria (SON) sets grade limits. Packaging: Grade 1 rice is packed in 5 kg, 10 kg, or 50 kg bags with labels showing grade. Price difference: Grade 1 can fetch 20-30% more than Grade 3. To improve grade, invest in color sorting and length grading. Record keeping: maintain a log of grades per batch for traceability. Training: workers need to recognize defects like paddy, red rice, and chalky grains. A simple classification kit includes sieves, a magnifying glass, and a color chart. Regular calibration with standard samples ensures accuracy. Final classification is the last step before marketing; it determines the price and customer satisfaction.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Rice Grading and Separation in African Rice Mills

Grading and separation is where a rice mill converts paddy into a saleable, price-differentiated product. In West and East AfricaNigeria, Senegal, Mali, Tanzania, Uganda, Kenyamilling margins are thin, and buyers (urban traders, institutional caterers, supermarket chains, exporters) pay a premium for consistent grain length, low broken content, and clean colour. This lesson is a shop-floor reference: the equipment you will meet, the settings that matter, the data you must record, and the business logic behind each separation step.

Treat grading as a commercial function, not a technical afterthought. A 2% improvement in head rice recovery on a 4 t/hr mill running 20 hours/day is roughly 1.6 t of additional head rice per dayat USD 600/t farmgate-equivalent that is nearly USD 1,000/day in recovered value. That is the entire justification for investing in destoners, length graders, and colour sorters.

Sifting and Grading Equipment: What to Buy First

For a small-to-medium African mill (14 t/hr), the minimum grading train is: vibrating sifter (removes straw, dust, immature grains) destoner (removes stones that damage rubber rollers and break teeth) length grader (separates broken from head rice). Colour sorters are the next capex step, typically justified above 3 t/hr or when selling into supermarket/export channels.

Length and Thickness Grading: The Money Step

Length grading uses indented cylinders or disc separators. Head rice (75% of full length) is the premium fraction. Broken rice (50% length) sells at 4060% of head rice price in most African markets. The cylinder pocket size must match the target graintypically 4.05.0 mm for long-grain African varieties (e.g., NERICA, FARO). Thickness grading (slotted screens) removes thin, immature grains that lower bulk density and cooking quality.

Operating discipline: check cylinder rotation speed weekly (too fast = poor separation, too slow = throughput loss). Replace worn pockets every 23 seasons. Record broken-rice yield every shift.

Destoners and Gravity Separators

Destoners exploit density difference between rice (~1.35 g/cm) and stones (~2.6 g/cm). A fluidised bed with controlled air flow lifts rice while stones sink and are discharged separately. Gravity separators do the same job with a vibrating inclined deck and can also separate immature, chalky, or insect-damaged grain. In African conditions, feed rate and air pressure must be adjusted for high-moisture paddy (1416% MC) common in wet-season harvests.

Colour Sorters and Their Operation

Colour sorters use CCD cameras and pneumatic ejectors to remove discoloured grains (yellow, chalky, black, red-streaked). Modern units handle 1.56 t/hr. Critical settings: sensitivity threshold, ejector delay, air pressure (68 bar), and background calibration. In dusty West African conditions, daily cleaning of lenses and air filters is non-negotiabledust causes false rejects and wastes good rice.

Broken Rice Separation and Management

Broken rice is not waste. Options: (1) sell as local-market broken rice (common in Senegal, Mali, Nigeria); (2) re-mill to flour for local food processors; (3) blend into lower-grade consumer packs; (4) sell to feed mills. Track broken rice percentage dailyif it exceeds 1520%, investigate milling pressure, cylinder wear, or paddy moisture.

Final Product Classification

African markets typically recognise three commercial grades: Grade 1 (head rice 90%, broken 5%) for export/supermarket; Grade 2 (head 7085%, broken 1015%) for urban wholesale; Grade 3 (head 5065%) for local/institutional markets. Grade specifications must be agreed with buyers in writingverbal "grade 1" means different things in Lagos, Dakar, and Dar es Salaam.

SAFETY WARNING Dust and Rotating Machinery

Rice milling generates fine, combustible dust. In African mills with poor ventilation, dust explosions and respiratory disease (byssinosis-like symptoms) are real risks. Always: (1) run local exhaust ventilation on sifters and destoners; (2) wear N95 masks and ear protection near cylinders and pneumatic ejectors; (3) lock out/tag out before clearing blockages in indented cylinders or destoner decks; (4) never bypass guards on rotating shaftsentanglement is the leading cause of serious injury in rice mills. Colour sorters use compressed air at 68 barnever point air lines at skin.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Check paddy moisture (target 1314%) before millinghigh moisture increases broken rice.
  • Verify sifter screen condition and clean daily; replace torn screens immediately.
  • Test destoner air pressure and feed rate at start of each shift; record stone discharge weight.
  • Inspect indented cylinder pockets weekly; replace worn cylinders every 23 seasons.
  • Calibrate colour sorter sensitivity against a reference sample; clean lenses and filters daily.
  • Record head rice, broken rice, and reject percentages per shift in a logbook.
  • Sample and seal each output grade for buyer verification; label with date, shift, grade.
  • Review grade price differentials monthly against local market and adjust grade targets.

Business takeaway: Grading is the difference between selling a commodity and selling a specification. In African rice markets, buyers pay for consistency. Invest first in destoning and length gradingthey protect your milling equipment and unlock the head-rice premium. Colour sorting is the next step when you target supermarkets or export. Record everything; your logbook is your pricing power.
