1. Choosing Fresh Meat: Signs of Quality in Local Markets

In hot African markets, freshness is everything. Start by looking at the meat's colour: fresh beef should be a deep, healthy red, lamb a darker red, and pork a pale pink. If it's brown or grey, it's old. Next, press the meat with your finger. It should feel firm and spring back quickly. If it stays dented, bacteria are already breaking it down. Smell is your best tool: fresh meat has a mild, clean smell. Any sour or ammonia-like odour means walk away. Also check the fat: it should be white or creamy yellow, not dull or slimy.

When buying from a butcher or market stall, ask when the animal was slaughtered. Ideally, you want meat that has been chilled for 24-48 hours. In hot climates, meat left at room temperature for more than 2 hours is risky. Look for a clean stall: the butcher's hands, knives, and cutting board should be washed. Avoid meat that is sitting in the sun or near flies. If you can, bring a small cooler box with ice packs to transport your purchase home quickly.

For curing, you need meat that is fresh but not too fresh. Rigor mortis makes meat tough, so let it rest for at least 24 hours after slaughter. If you're buying from a local farmer, ask them to hang the carcass in a cool, ventilated place for a day. For biltong, choose lean cuts like silverside or topside. For sausages, you need meat with about 20-30% fat, so ask for shoulder or belly. Always inspect the lymph nodes (in the neck and armpits) â they should be small and pale, not swollen or dark.

Finally, trust your senses. If something feels off, don't buy it. A good piece of meat is worth the extra cost. In many African markets, you can negotiate, but never compromise on quality. Buy from a butcher you know and who has a good reputation. Remember, curing cannot fix bad meat â it only preserves good meat. So start with the best you can find.

2. Basic Butchery: Breaking Down a Carcass for Curing

Breaking down a carcass is a skill that saves money and gives you control. You'll need a sharp butcher's knife, a boning knife, a meat saw, and a clean table. If you're working with a whole sheep or goat, start by removing the head and feet. Then split the carcass down the spine using a saw. You'll get two halves. Lay each half on its side. First, remove the forequarter (front leg and shoulder) by cutting between the 5th and 6th rib. Then remove the hindquarter (back leg and rump) by cutting between the last lumbar vertebra and the pelvis. Now you have four main sections.

For curing, you want to separate the muscles. On the hindquarter, you'll find the topside, silverside, and thick flank. These are perfect for biltong. Cut along the seams between muscles â don't just saw through bone. Use your boning knife to follow the natural lines. The forequarter gives you the shoulder, which is good for sausages and droÃ«wors because it has more fat and connective tissue. The ribcage and belly can be used for bacon or pancetta. Save the bones for stock.

Work in a cool place, ideally below 15Â°C. If it's hot, work early in the morning or in an air-conditioned room. Keep your knives sharp â a dull knife is dangerous. Clean your knife and board with hot soapy water between different parts to avoid cross-contamination. Wear a clean apron and wash your hands often. If you're not experienced, watch a local butcher or take a short course. In many African communities, older butchers are happy to teach.

After breaking down, sort the meat into three piles: prime cuts for biltong, fatty cuts for sausages, and trim for mince or stock. Weigh each pile and label it with the date. If you're not curing immediately, refrigerate at 0-4Â°C or freeze. Remember, the goal is to maximise usable meat and minimise waste. A well-broken carcass can yield 70-75% meat, 20% bones, and 5% fat and trim. Practice makes perfect.

3. Cutting Meat for Biltong, DroÃ«wors, and Sausages

Different cured products need different cuts. For biltong, you want lean meat with no fat or sinew. The best cuts are topside, silverside, and eye of round. Cut the meat into strips about 2-3 cm thick, 5 cm wide, and as long as you like. Cut with the grain for chewier biltong, or against the grain for more tender biltong. Use a sharp knife and cut on a clean board. If the meat is partly frozen (about 30 minutes in the freezer), it's easier to cut evenly.

For droÃ«wors, you need a mix of lean meat and fat. Use beef or game meat with about 20% fat. Cut the meat into cubes, then mince it coarsely. You can also cut by hand into small pieces if you don't have a mincer. The fat should be cut into small cubes and mixed in. For sausages, the same rule applies: 70-80% lean meat, 20-30% fat. Pork shoulder or beef chuck are ideal. Cut the meat into cubes that fit your mincer. Keep everything cold â put the meat in the fridge for 30 minutes before mincing.

For all products, remove any sinew, gristle, and blood clots. These will make the final product tough and can cause spoilage. Use a sharp boning knife to trim. Work on a clean surface. If you're making sausages, you'll need casings. Natural casings (sheep or hog) are best. Soak them in warm water for 30 minutes before use. For droÃ«wors, you can use thinner sheep casings. For biltong, no casing is needed.

Cutting meat is easier if you have the right tools: a good knife, a sturdy cutting board, and a meat grinder. In many African markets, you can buy a manual grinder for a reasonable price. Always cut on a board, not directly on a table. After cutting, mix your spices and cure according to your recipe. For biltong, you'll typically use salt, brown sugar, coriander, and vinegar. For droÃ«wors, add cloves, nutmeg, and pepper. For sausages, you might add garlic, paprika, and sage. Let the meat marinate for 12-24 hours in the fridge before drying or stuffing.

4. Trimming and Sorting: Fat, Connective Tissue, and Sinew

Trimming is the difference between good and great charcuterie. Fat, connective tissue, and sinew all affect texture, drying time, and shelf life. Fat adds flavour and moisture, but too much fat can go rancid. Connective tissue (collagen) breaks down during cooking, but in dry-cured products it stays tough. Sinew (elastin) never breaks down â it must be removed. So, start by sorting: separate hard fat (suet) from soft fat (back fat). Hard fat is good for sausages, soft fat for pÃ¢tÃ©s. Remove all sinew and silver skin (the thin, pearly membrane).

Use a sharp boning knife. Lay the meat flat and slide the knife under the sinew, angling it slightly upward. Pull the sinew with your other hand as you cut. For silver skin, make a small cut and then use the knife to lift and scrape it off. Work slowly. If you're trimming a large piece, cut it into smaller sections first. Keep your knife sharp â a dull knife will tear the meat. After trimming, you should have three piles: lean meat, fat, and trim (sinew, gristle, blood clots). The trim can be used for stock or pet food, but never for curing.

For biltong, you want almost no fat. Any fat left on will turn yellow and rancid during drying. So trim aggressively. For droÃ«wors and sausages, you need some fat, but it must be clean and fresh. Cut fat into small cubes (5 mm) and keep it cold. For salami, you want distinct fat cubes. For liver pÃ¢tÃ©, you want soft fat. Always taste your fat â if it's rancid, throw it away. In hot climates, fat goes off quickly, so work fast and keep everything refrigerated.

After trimming, sort your meat by quality. Use the best lean cuts for biltong. Use medium cuts for sausages. Use trimmings for mince or stock. Weigh and label each batch. If you're not using it immediately, vacuum-seal or wrap tightly and freeze. Remember, trimming takes time, but it pays off. A well-trimmed piece of meat dries evenly, tastes better, and lasts longer. In African markets, you can often buy untrimmed meat cheaper â do the trimming yourself and save money.

5. Temperature Control: Keeping Meat Safe in Hot Climates

In hot climates, temperature control is a matter of life and death â or at least, of good meat versus spoiled meat. Bacteria multiply fastest between 5Â°C and 60Â°C. In many African countries, daytime temperatures can reach 35Â°C or more. So, you must keep meat below 5Â°C or above 60Â°C. For curing, you need cold temperatures: 0-4Â°C for refrigeration, and 10-15Â°C for drying. If you don't have a refrigerator, you can use a cooler box with ice packs, a evaporative cooler (zeer pot), or a root cellar. In some areas, you can dig a hole and keep meat in a sealed container underground where it's cooler.

When you buy meat, transport it quickly. Use a cooler box with ice. If you're buying a whole carcass, hang it in a cool, ventilated place. In hot weather, you have about 2 hours before bacteria start to grow. So, plan your day: buy meat in the morning, get it home, and either refrigerate or start curing immediately. If you're curing with salt, you can keep meat at slightly higher temperatures because salt inhibits bacteria. But for fresh meat, cold is key.

For drying biltong and droÃ«wors, you need a dry, cool place with good airflow. A biltong box with a fan and a light bulb for warmth works well. The ideal temperature is 20-25Â°C with low humidity. In humid coastal areas, you may need a dehumidifier or a fan. If you're using a traditional drying method (hanging in a breezy, shaded area), bring the meat in at night to avoid dew. Never dry meat in direct sunlight â it will cook on the outside and spoil on the inside.

Monitor temperature with a thermometer. Keep a log. If meat ever goes above 10Â°C for more than 2 hours during curing, throw it away. Don't risk food poisoning. In hot climates, you also need to control insects. Use mesh screens or netting. Keep your work area clean. Wash your hands and tools with hot water and soap. If you have a refrigerator, keep it at 2-4Â°C. If you don't, consider investing in a solar-powered fridge or a gas fridge. Many African entrepreneurs have started meat businesses with just a cooler box and a reliable source of ice. It's possible, but you must be vigilant.

6. Waste Reduction: Using Every Part Profitably

In meat curing, waste is money lost. Every part of the animal can be used. After breaking down a carcass, you'll have bones, fat, trim, and organs. Bones can be boiled for stock, which you can sell or use in soups. Fat can be rendered into tallow or lard, which is great for cooking and for making soap. Trim (sinew, gristle) can be cooked and fed to dogs or used as bait. Organs like liver, heart, and kidneys can be made into pÃ¢tÃ©, liverwurst, or droÃ«wors. Even the blood can be used for blood sausage (like morcilla).

For biltong and droÃ«wors, you'll have small pieces that are too small to dry. Don't throw them away. Mince them and make sausages or meatballs. Or cure them as snapsticks. The fat you trim off can be cut into small pieces and added to sausages for flavour. If you have a lot of fat, render it: cut into small pieces, heat slowly in a pot until liquid fat separates. Strain and store in jars. Rendered fat lasts for months without refrigeration. In many African markets, rendered fat is sold for cooking.

Bones can be sold to restaurants for soup or to individuals for broth. You can also make bone meal by drying and grinding bones â it's a good fertiliser. Hooves and horns can be sold for crafts. Hides can be tanned and sold as leather. Even the stomach and intestines can be cleaned and used as casings for sausages. This is traditional in many African cultures. So, learn to clean them: soak in salt water, scrape, and rinse. It's labour-intensive but saves money.

To reduce waste, plan your cutting. Use the whole animal. If you're buying from a butcher, ask for the offcuts and organs â they are often cheap. Make a business from waste: sell stock, rendered fat, and pÃ¢tÃ©. In a vocational training, you can teach learners to make a profit from every part. Keep records of what you use and what you sell. Even small amounts add up. For example, from a 50 kg carcass, you can get 35 kg meat, 10 kg bones, 3 kg fat, and 2 kg organs. If you sell the bones for $1/kg, that's $10 extra. Over a year, that's significant. So, think like a business owner: nothing is waste until you decide it is.

Practical Reference: Meat Selection, Cutting & Preparation for Curing Businesses

This practical reference consolidates the six lessons in this module into a working document you can print, laminate, and keep in your cutting room. It is written for small and medium meat-processing enterprises across Africa â from biltong producers in Gauteng and Windhoek to drowors makers in Nairobi and sausage kitchens in Lagos and Accra. The goal is simple: buy better, cut smarter, waste less, and sell more profitably.

In hot climates, the margin between profit and loss is often measured in degrees Celsius and minutes. A carcass bought well but handled badly loses value faster than a carcass bought poorly but handled expertly. Treat this reference as your daily operating standard.

1. Choosing Fresh Meat: Signs of Quality in Local Markets

Whether you buy from an abattoir, a municipal market, or a livestock auction, apply the same sensory checklist. Fresh red meat should be firm to the touch, spring back when pressed, and have a clean, slightly metallic smell â never sour or ammonia-like. Beef fat should be creamy white to pale yellow; yellow fat in older animals is normal but excessive yellowing can indicate age or poor feed. Lamb and goat fat is whiter and firmer. Pork fat should be bright white.

Colour is a guide, not a verdict. Beef that is dark purple may be from an older animal or a stressed one (dark, firm, dry â DFD meat), which cures poorly because its high pH reduces water-holding and salt uptake. Pale, soft, exudative (PSE) pork loses moisture rapidly and is unsuitable for dry-cured products. Ask your supplier about pre-slaughter handling; stress management on the farm and in transport directly affects your yield.

In informal markets, insist on seeing the carcass stamp or veterinary health certificate where available. In many African countries, meat inspection is regulated by national authorities â for example, the Kenya Meat Commission, the South African Meat Industry Company (SAMIC), or Nigeria's Federal Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Development. Where formal inspection is weak, rely on your senses and build long-term relationships with trusted suppliers who allow you to inspect before purchase.

2. Basic Butchery: Breaking Down a Carcass for Curing

Curing uses specific muscles and cuts. Learn the primal breakdown for beef, pork, lamb, and goat, then map each primal to its curing destiny. For beef: the hindquarter yields silverside, topside, and rump â ideal for biltong and drowors. The forequarter gives chuck, brisket, and shin â good for sausages, boerewors, and slow-cured pastrami-style products. The loin gives striploin and fillet â premium fresh cuts, less often cured whole.

For pork: the leg (ham) is the classic curing primal; the shoulder (Boston butt) makes excellent coppa and sausage; the belly becomes bacon and pancetta; the loin becomes loin bacon or Canadian bacon.

Work on a clean, stable surface at or below 10 Â°C if possible. Use a sharp boning knife and a saw for bone. Keep your knives honed; a dull knife crushes tissue and creates jagged surfaces that harbour bacteria. Separate primal cuts immediately into clean food-grade containers and move them to cold storage. Time is temperature: every minute above 10 Â°C accelerates spoilage and reduces your curing yield.

3. Cutting Meat for Biltong, Drowors, and Sausages

Biltong is cut with the grain into strips 2â3 cm thick, 5â8 cm wide, and 20â30 cm long, from silverside, topside, or rump. Cut while the meat is partially frozen (about â2 Â°C) for clean, uniform strips. Remove all visible fat and connective tissue â fat rancidifies during drying and shortens shelf life. Drowors (also spelled drowors, a Southern African dried sausage similar to droÃ«wors) uses lean beef or game mixed with fat in a 70:30 lean-to-fat ratio, stuffed into natural casings and air-dried.

For sausages, keep the meat and fat cold (0â4 Â°C) throughout grinding and mixing. Fat smearing is the enemy: warm fat smears inside the grinder and creates a pasty texture. Grind fat and lean separately, then combine. Use a 4â6 mm plate for fresh boerewors, 8â10 mm for coarse drowors. Salt at 1.8â2.2% of total weight; too little salt and your product spoils, too much and it becomes unpalatable.

Consistency is your brand. Weigh ingredients, record batch numbers, and train your team to cut to the same specification every time. A customer who buys your biltong in March and again in September should taste the same product.

4. Trimming and Sorting: Fat, Connective Tissue, and Sinew

Trimming is not waste removal â it is value sorting. Separate trim into four streams: (1) premium lean trim for biltong and drowors; (2) fat for sausage, rendering, or soap-making; (3) connective tissue and sinew for stock, bone broth, or pet food; (4) bones for stock, gelatine, or bone meal.

Hard fat (kidney, pelvic, and channel fat) is unsuitable for dry curing but excellent for rendering. Soft, intramuscular fat (marbling) improves sausage juiciness. Sinew and silver skin must be removed from biltong cuts because they become tough and chewy after drying. Connective tissue rich in collagen â shanks, cheeks, oxtail â can be slow-cooked into high-value ready meals or sold to restaurants.

Keep trim cold and use within 24â48 hours, or freeze immediately at â18 Â°C for later use. Label every container with date, cut, and intended use.
