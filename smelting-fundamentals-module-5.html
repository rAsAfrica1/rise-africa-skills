1. Raw Material Characterization

Before you charge any material into a furnace, you must know exactly what you are dealing with. Raw material characterization means testing your ores, fluxes, and fuels to understand their chemical and physical properties. In Africa, common smelting materials include iron ore from Nigeria or South Africa, copper concentrates from Zambia or DRC, and locally sourced limestone and dolomite. Start by taking a representative sampleabout 5 kg from different parts of your stockpileand send it to a lab for X-ray fluorescence (XRF) analysis. This tells you the percentage of iron, silica, alumina, sulfur, and other elements. For a typical iron ore, you want Fe above 60%, silica below 8%, and alumina below 3% for efficient smelting. If your ore has high sulfur (above 0.1%), you may need to roast it first. Physical tests matter too: measure moisture content by drying a 1 kg sample at 105C for 24 hours. High moisture wastes heat and can cause explosions. Also check particle sizelumps between 10 mm and 30 mm work best in a blast furnace; fines below 5 mm must be sintered or pelletized. Use a simple sieve set (5 mm, 10 mm, 20 mm) to check size distribution. Hardness and porosity affect reduction speed; a drop test from 2 meters onto a steel plate shows if the ore crumbles. Record all results in a logbook. Without characterization, you are guessing, and guessing wastes fuel, flux, and time. A small lab kit with a moisture balance, sieve set, and portable XRF (if affordable) can be set up for under $5,000. Many African smelters fail because they skip this step. Do it right, and your furnace will run smoother.

2. Weighing and Blending of Charge

Once you know your raw materials, you must weigh and blend them in the correct proportions. The charge is the mixture of ore, flux, and fuel (coke or charcoal) that goes into the furnace. A typical iron blast furnace charge might be 1,600 kg of ore, 400 kg of coke, and 200 kg of limestone per batch. But these numbers depend on your ore analysis. Use a simple formula: for every 100 kg of ore, add flux based on the ratio of silica to lime you need. If your ore has 10% silica, you need about 20 kg of limestone (which is 95% CaCO3) to neutralize it. Weighing must be accurate to within 1%. A platform scale with 0.5 kg resolution is adequate for small operations. For larger ones, use a weigh hopper with load cells. Blending means mixing the materials thoroughly before charging. In Africa, many small foundries use manual mixing on a concrete pad with shovelsturn the pile three times. But for consistent results, a rotary drum mixer or a cement mixer works better. Mix for at least 5 minutes. The goal is a homogeneous blend so that every scoop has the same ratio. If you skip blending, you get hot spots and cold spots in the furnace, leading to poor metal quality and high fuel consumption. Also, consider preheating or drying the charge if moisture is above 5%. Store blended charge in a covered bin to prevent rain from changing moisture. Keep a batch record: date, ore source, weights, and mixer time. This traceability helps you troubleshoot when something goes wrong. Remember, a good blend is the foundation of a good smelt.

3. Fluxes and Their Role in Smelting

Fluxes are materials added to the charge to remove impurities and make the slag flow easily. The most common fluxes are limestone (calcium carbonate), dolomite (calcium magnesium carbonate), and silica sand. In African smelting, limestone is often mined locallyfor example, in Ghana, Nigeria, and Kenya. The main job of flux is to combine with unwanted oxides like silica (SiO2) and alumina (Al2O3) to form a slag that melts at a lower temperature than the ore. Without flux, these impurities would thicken the slag, block the furnace, and contaminate the metal. The key concept is basicity: the ratio of basic oxides (CaO, MgO) to acidic oxides (SiO2, Al2O3). For iron smelting, you want a basicity ratio (CaO/SiO2) between 1.0 and 1.2. If your ore is acidic (high silica), add more limestone. If your ore is basic (high lime), add silica sand. You calculate the amount of flux using the formula: Flux (kg) = (Ore kg %SiO2 1.2) / %CaO in flux. For example, 1,000 kg of ore with 10% SiO2 needs 120 kg of pure CaO. If limestone is 95% CaO, you need 126 kg. Flux also affects slag viscosity. A good slag flows like honey at 1,3001,400C. If it is too thick, add fluorspar (CaF2) in small amounts (12% of charge) but avoid excess as it attacks refractory. Dolomite is useful when you need magnesium oxide to protect the furnace lining. Always test your flux by mixing a small batch and melting it in a crucible to see how it flows. Store fluxes drymoisture makes them sticky and hard to weigh. In Africa, many smelters use agricultural limestone, which is cheap but may have high silica; check the analysis. Proper fluxing saves energy and improves metal recovery.

4. Charging Systems and Equipment

Charging is the process of feeding the prepared charge into the furnace. The equipment you choose depends on furnace type and scale. For small African operations, a manual charging system with a wheelbarrow and a charging chute is common. But for consistent production, you need a mechanical system. The most basic is a skip hoist: a bucket on rails that lifts charge to the furnace top and dumps it into a receiving hopper. A skip hoist can handle 5002,000 kg per trip and costs around $10,000$30,000. For larger furnaces, a conveyor belt with a weigh feeder is better. The belt carries charge continuously and can be controlled to match feed rate. In a blast furnace, you also need a double bell or a bell-less top (rotating chute) to distribute charge evenly and seal the furnace gas. A double bell system uses two cones that open alternately to prevent gas escape. A bell-less top uses a rotating chute to place material in specific patternsthis gives better control of burden distribution. For small cupolas, a simple charging door with a manual chute works. Key equipment includes: a charging hopper (to store blended charge), a weigh hopper (to measure each batch), and a charging car or skip. You also need a stockhouse with bins for ore, coke, and flux, each with a weigh feeder. In Africa, many smelters use second-hand equipment from China or India. Ensure your charging system is reliable and easy to maintain. Motors, gearboxes, and belts must be protected from dust and heat. Install limit switches and emergency stops. A well-designed charging system reduces labor, improves safety, and ensures steady furnace operation. Never overload the furnacecharge in small, frequent batches rather than one large dump.

5. Burden Distribution in Blast Furnace

Burden distribution refers to how the solid charge (ore, coke, flux) is arranged inside the blast furnace. It is critical because it affects gas flow, heat transfer, and chemical reactions. In a blast furnace, the charge descends while hot gases rise. If the burden is not distributed properly, gas will take the path of least resistanceusually along the wallsleaving the center cold and inefficient. The ideal distribution has more coke in the center and more ore near the walls. Why? Coke is larger and more permeable, so it allows gas to flow up the center. Ore is finer and less permeable, so it should be near the walls where it can be heated by the descending charge and rising gas. To achieve this, you control the charging pattern. In a bell-less top, you can set the chute to rotate and tilt to different angles. Common patterns are: center coke charging (coke to center, ore to periphery), and mixed charging (alternating layers). For a double bell, you can adjust the stockline (the level of charge) and the size of the bells. A higher stockline gives a flatter profile; a lower one gives a deeper center. You also control the ore-to-coke ratio in each batch. In Africa, many small blast furnaces use manual charging, which makes uniform distribution hard. But you can still improve by charging in small piles around the circumference. Use a template to guide where you dump each skip. Monitor the furnace by checking the temperature of the top gas and the color of the slag. If the center is too cold, add more coke to the center. If the walls are overheating, add more ore near the walls. Burden distribution is a dynamic skilladjust as conditions change. Proper distribution can cut coke consumption by 1020% and increase production.

6. Feed Rate Control and Automation

Feed rate control means regulating how fast charge enters the furnace to match the smelting rate. If you feed too fast, the furnace floods, gas flow is blocked, and you get poor reduction. If you feed too slow, the furnace cools down and production drops. The correct feed rate depends on furnace size, blast volume, and charge composition. For a small blast furnace producing 5 tonnes per day, you might feed 200 kg of charge every 10 minutes. For a larger one (50 tonnes per day), feed 1,000 kg every 5 minutes. The key is to keep the stockline steadythe charge level should stay within 0.5 meters of the target. You can measure stockline with a mechanical probe or a radar sensor. Manual control uses a timer and a weigh hopper: the operator opens the hopper gate for a set time, then closes it. But this is inconsistent. Automation uses a programmable logic controller (PLC) to control the feed rate based on signals from the stockline sensor and gas analysis. A simple automated system includes: a weigh feeder on the conveyor, a variable frequency drive (VFD) on the motor, and a PLC that adjusts belt speed to maintain the desired feed rate. For African smelters, cost-effective automation can be built with an Arduino or Raspberry Pi, a load cell, and a motor driver. You can also automate the charging sequence: when the stockline drops, the PLC starts the skip hoist, weighs the charge, and dumps it. Automation reduces labor, improves safety, and gives consistent results. But you must maintain sensors and calibrate weigh scales weekly. Start with manual control, then upgrade step by step. Even a simple timer-based system is better than random feeding. Always keep a log of feed rate, stockline, and furnace conditions to spot trends.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Charge Preparation and Feeding in African Smelting Operations

In African smelting operations from ferrochrome plants in South Africa's Bushveld Complex to copper smelters in Zambia's Copperbelt and emerging iron and steel mini-mills in Nigeria, Kenya, and Ethiopia the cost of charge preparation typically represents 4060% of total conversion cost. Yet in many small and medium-sized African operations, charge preparation remains the least instrumented and least documented part of the process. This practical reference consolidates the operational knowledge needed to run charge preparation and feeding as a controlled, profitable activity rather than an artisanal routine.

The core business logic is simple: every rand, kwacha, naira, or birr spent on consistent raw material characterization, accurate weighing, disciplined blending, and controlled feeding returns multiples in furnace stability, energy efficiency, and metal recovery. Conversely, variability in charge chemistry and feed rate is the single largest driver of furnace instability, electrode consumption, refractory wear, and off-spec product all of which destroy margin.

1. Raw Material Characterization in Practice

Characterization is not a laboratory luxury; it is the foundation of every downstream decision. For African smelters, the most critical parameters are:

  • Chemical assay (XRF or wet chemistry) of primary elements and penalty elements (S, P, As, Pb, Zn for ferrochrome; As, Sb, Bi for copper).
  • Size distribution lump, sinter, fines, and briquette fractions. In blast furnace burdens, the 640 mm window is typically optimal for permeability.
  • Moisture content critical in tropical and subtropical African climates where stockpiles can gain 28% moisture seasonally.
  • Reducibility and softening-melting behaviour especially for DRI and lump ore blends.
  • Bulk density and angle of repose determines chute design and stockpile volumes.

In South African ferrochrome practice, ore from the UG2 and LG6 seams is routinely characterized for Cr/Fe ratio, SiO, MgO, and AlO because these directly govern slag viscosity and furnace power draw. Zambian copper concentrates are characterized for Cu, Fe, S, and silica flux demand to hit target matte grades. In West African iron ore smelting, alumina and phosphorus are the dominant penalty elements.

2. Weighing and Blending The Accuracy Dividend

In African operations, batch weighing accuracy of 1% on major raw materials and 2% on fluxes is achievable with modern belt scales and load-cell hoppers. The business case is direct: a 1% deviation in coke rate in a ferrochrome furnace translates to roughly 0.51.5% change in specific energy consumption, which at 3.54.5 MWh/t translates to significant cost per tonne.

Blending must account for:

  • Segregation during stockpiling (fines migrate to the base).
  • Moisture variability weigh dry where possible, or apply moisture compensation.
  • Flux reactivity burnt lime must be protected from hydration.
  • Binder and reductant distribution in briquetted or sintered feed.

3. Fluxes and Their Role

Flux selection is a slag-engineering decision. In African ferrochrome smelting, quartzite and dolomite are added to achieve a target SiO/MgO ratio that keeps slag fluid at 16001700 C. In copper smelting, silica flux controls FeO/SiO ratio and matte grade. In iron blast furnaces, limestone and dolomite control basicity (CaO/SiO) and sulphur removal.

4. Charging Systems and Equipment

African smelters typically use one of four charging configurations:

  1. Skip hoist common in blast furnaces; robust, high capacity, but sensitive to material size degradation.
  2. Belt conveyor used in submerged arc furnaces and DRI plants; requires accurate belt scales and tramp metal detection.
  3. Vibratory feeder suited to fines and briquettes; excellent for controlled feed rate.
  4. Chute / gravity low capital, but poor control and high segregation risk.

Equipment selection must weigh capital cost against the cost of variability. In practice, a well-instrumented belt conveyor with belt scales and a vibratory feeder for fines typically delivers the best balance for African mid-scale operations.

5. Burden Distribution in Blast Furnace

Burden distribution governs gas flow, heat exchange, and furnace permeability. Key levers:

  • Charging sequence ore/coke layering (e.g., OO/CC, O/C/O).
  • Stockline level controls ore/coke ratio radially.
  • Chute angle and rotation for bell-less top systems.
  • Burden profile central coke column vs. wall coke.

In African blast furnace practice, where burden quality can vary significantly due to local ore and lump availability, a "centre coke" profile is often used to maintain a permeable central gas channel and protect the refractory lining.

6. Feed Rate Control and Automation

Automation is no longer a luxury in African smelting it is a competitive necessity. A modern charge preparation control system typically includes:

  • PLC-based batching with recipe management.
  • Belt scale feedback for real-time feed rate.
  • Moisture compensation from online moisture meters.
  • Furnace power / electrode position feedback to adjust feed rate.
  • Historical trending and alarm management.

For small and medium African operations, a phased approach works best: start with accurate weighing and manual recipes, then add PLC batching, then integrate furnace feedback. Each phase should show a measurable cost or recovery benefit before proceeding.

SAFETY WARNING Charge Preparation and Feeding

Charge preparation and feeding involve severe hazards: rotating equipment (belts, skips, vibratory feeders), falling loads, dust explosions (especially with fine coal, coke breeze, and sulphide concentrates), and molten metal splash at the furnace. In African operations, the most common serious incidents are entanglement in unguarded conveyor drives, falls from charging platforms, and dust explosions in enclosed transfer points.

  • Enforce lock-out/tag-out (LOTO) on all charging equipment before maintenance.
  • Install and maintain guards on all rotating parts no exceptions.
  • Control dust at transfer points with water sprays or extraction; avoid dry sweeping of sulphide fines.
  • Prohibit entry into bins, hoppers, and silos without confined-space permits and atmospheric testing.
  • Provide and enforce PPE: hard hats, safety boots, high-visibility clothing, dust masks, and face shields near furnaces.
  • Conduct daily pre-start inspections of belts, scales, and feeders; log defects and act on them before production starts.

Compliance with the Mine Health and Safety Act (South Africa), the Occupational Health and Safety Act (Zambia, Nigeria), and equivalent national regulations is mandatory not optional.

7. Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Characterize every incoming batch assay, size, moisture and record it against the supplier and stockpile.
  2. Verify scale calibration weekly and after any mechanical intervention; log accuracy against a certified test weight.
  3. Apply moisture compensation to weighments in wet seasons; never assume dry tonnes.
  4. Confirm flux quality and reactivity before charging; reject hydrated or contaminated lime.
  5. Check burden profile and stockline at least once per shift; adjust charging sequence if permeability drops.
  6. Monitor feed rate against furnace power and electrode position; investigate deviations >5% immediately.
  7. Audit dust control and guarding at all transfer points and drives daily.
  8. Review charge preparation KPIs weekly weighing accuracy, blend consistency, flux usage, feed rate stability and act on trends.
