WELDING FUNDAMENTALS · MODULE 10 OF 12

Distortion and How to Control It

Every weld shrinks as it cools, and it pulls. This module is about predicting where the pull will go and arranging the job so it goes somewhere harmless.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  • Explain why welding distorts metal and why it cannot be eliminated
  • Recognise the three common forms of distortion
  • Apply the five standard techniques to control distortion
  • Work out where distortion will occur and plan for it
  • Straighten distorted work using heat and mechanical force
  • State when the fix is worse than the distortion
Lesson 10.1

Why metal distorts

Metal expands when heated and contracts when cooled. Module 2 explained it. In a weld, this simple fact produces a specific problem.

The shrinking weld

You heat a small area of metal to melting. It expands. You add filler metal, which is also at melting point. It expands. Then everything cools, and the whole assembly — parent metal plus weld plus heated zone beside it — contracts.

But it does not contract back to where it started. The parent metal is larger than the weld area, so it does not get as hot. The weld gets hotter and shrinks more. The result is that the joint pulls together as it cools.

This is not a defect. It is the natural behaviour of metal under welding heat. Every welded joint pulls. The only question is how much, and where the pull goes.

What controls how much

FactorEffect
Heat inputMore heat = more expansion = more shrink. High current, slow travel, thick sections all increase distortion
Length of weldLonger welds shrink more in total, but a long weld made in small pieces shrinks less per piece
Number of passesEach pass shrinks. Multiple passes multiply the total shrink
RestraintIf the work is clamped or fixed, it cannot move, so stress builds instead of distortion
MaterialThicker plate resists distortion better than thin sheet
Joint typeButt joints distort more than fillets, because the whole plate moves. Fillet joints (tee, lap, corner) distort locally
Lesson 10.2

The three forms of distortion

Transverse shrinkage

The joint pulls together across its width. The finished piece is shorter than the pieces you started with.

  • Sign: the overall length of the assembly is smaller than the sum of the parts plus the gap
  • Magnitude: typically 1–2mm per metre of weld for a single-pass butt weld on 6mm plate. More for thicker plate or multiple passes
  • Where it matters: when a specific dimension matters — a frame that must fit into a space, a bracket with a hole spacing that has to match

Angular distortion

The weld pulls the plates up or down at an angle, closing or opening the joint. This is the most visible form, and the one beginners notice first.

The classic case: a single-V butt weld on one side. As the weld cools, it pulls the two plates toward each other on the welded face. The plates that were flat become slightly bent. The same happens on a tee joint — the base plate lifts on both sides of the upright.

Longitudinal bowing

A weld running along one edge of a plate bows the whole plate toward the weld. This is because the shrinking weld is on that edge only, and it pulls the plate into a curve.

  • Sign: a long, curved deflection of the plate, greatest at the ends if the ends are free
  • Typical case: a long weld along the top edge of a plate, or a stiffener welded to one face
  • Fix: usually needs heat straightening or mechanical force — this is one of the harder distortions to correct
Lesson 10.3

Five techniques to control distortion

You cannot stop distortion — it is inherent. What you can do is arrange the work and the sequence so the pull goes somewhere that does not matter, or so the pulls cancel each other.

1. Weld on both sides, alternately

A single-V butt weld on one face pulls one way. If you can access both faces, alternate: make a pass on the front, then a pass on the back. The pulls cancel.

For a long butt joint you cannot access both sides of, staggering the sequence along the length has a similar effect — weld a short run at one end, then a short run at the other, then back. The overall pull is distributed and each run's local pull is balanced by the next.

2. Balance the sequence

  • Backstep welding: instead of welding a joint from one end to the other, weld in short runs in the opposite direction. Run 1 goes from point B toward A. Run 2 starts at C and goes toward B. Each run is behind the previous one, and each shrink pulls back into cold metal rather than forward into hot metal
  • Skip welding: weld short sections, leaving gaps, then fill the gaps after. This spreads the heat and lets each section cool before the next
  • Symmetric welding: on a symmetric part, weld in a pattern that keeps the heat evenly distributed

3. Pre-set

For an angular distortion you know is coming, set the work up with the opposite angle before welding, so the weld's pull brings it to flat.

For a butt weld on plate that will pull the edges up, set the plates up with a slight peak in the middle — say 1–2° — and let the weld close the gap. This requires knowing roughly how much distortion to expect, which comes from experience and from cutting test pieces.

4. Clamp and fix

If the work cannot move, it cannot distort — but the stress builds instead. Clamping is appropriate where the finished shape matters more than the stress — a frame that must be square, a flange that must fit its mate.

Restraint is not free

Heavily restrained welds crack more often, because the stress has nowhere to go. The distortion becomes a locked-in stress in the joint. That stress adds to whatever load the joint later carries.

Clamping is a tool, not a solution. Use it where the shape matters and accept that the joint now carries more stress than it would have.

5. Control the heat

The single biggest lever on distortion is how much heat you put in. Techniques to reduce it:

  • Lower current, faster travel. Heat input is proportional to current and voltage, and inversely proportional to travel speed. All three affect how much heat the work absorbs
  • Shorter welds at a time. A short run shrinks less than a long run, even if the total heat is the same
  • Fewer passes. Each pass shrinks. A single-pass weld distorts less than a multi-pass weld of the same size
  • Cool between passes — but not by quenching. Let the work cool naturally, and start the next pass when the work is warm but not hot
  • Smaller electrodes. Less metal, less heat
Lesson 10.4

Planning for distortion

The best control of distortion is planning it before the first tack. This is where experience really shows — an experienced welder looks at a job and can see where the pull will go.

The questions to ask before tacking

  1. Where does the weld pull? Toward the weld. Toward the side the weld is on
  2. What can move, and what cannot? The parts that can move will. The parts fixed by clamps or by geometry will build stress instead
  3. Does the finished shape matter? If a frame must be square, you must weld in a way that keeps it square. If a bracket only has to hold something, some distortion is acceptable
  4. Can I weld on both sides? If yes, alternating will nearly eliminate the pull. If no, the pull is committed and you plan around it
  5. Can I pre-set? If you know which way the pull will go and roughly how much, pre-set against it

Worked example: a rectangular frame from angle iron

Four pieces of angle iron cut to make a square frame. The welds are at the four corners, on the outside of the angle.

Where does the pull go? Each weld pulls the two pieces toward each other at that corner. The frame wants to become a smaller square.

  • If you weld all four corners in sequence: by the time you weld the last corner, the frame has already pulled in, and the last corner has to close a gap that has opened
  • If you tack all four corners first, check the diagonals, then weld in a balanced pattern: weld opposite corners first, then the other two. The pulls balance
  • If the frame must be exactly square: clamp it to a flat surface, or weld it oversize and trim after

The predictable mistake

A beginner welds corners in the order they come to hand. The frame ends up out of square, with the last corner forced together with a big clamp. The welds look fine, but the frame will not fit what it was made for.

Ten minutes spent checking diagonals between tacks and welding in a balanced order prevents this entirely.

Lesson 10.5

Straightening distorted work

Sometimes distortion cannot be prevented — the job is too complex, or you only notice after. Straightening is then the job.

Three ways

Mechanical force

Bend it back with a press, a lever, or a hammer. Works on thin material and mild steel. Does not work on thick plate or heavily restrained sections. Leaves residual stress.

Local heat and quench

Heat a small spot with a torch until it is red, then quench with water. The quench shrinks the hot spot faster than the surrounding metal, pulling the plate toward the shrunk spot. Multiple spots, in a pattern, can pull a plate back to flat.

Quench-shrinking on structural work is dangerous

Quenching hardens the metal and adds stress. On mild steel of modest thickness this is acceptable for cosmetic straightening — a bent panel, a small bracket. On anything structural, or on higher-carbon steel, quenching produces cracks.

This is a technique to use on non-critical work only. For anything else, mechanical straightening or accepting the distortion is safer.

Heat without quench

Heat a spot or line, let it cool naturally, and the shrink pulls the surrounding metal. Slower, safer, and preferred on structural work. The amount of pull per pass is smaller, so multiple cycles may be needed.

When to accept the distortion

  • If the part is not critical. A shelf bracket that is slightly out of square still holds a shelf
  • If straightening would take longer than remaking the part. Time spent on a borderline job is time not spent on the next one
  • If straightening risks further damage. A distorted thin-wall tube may crack when bent back. A distorted casting may break

The professional view on distortion

An experienced welder looks at a distorted job and asks: does this actually matter? A bench frame with 5mm of bow over 2 metres works perfectly well. A trailer axle mount with 5mm of bow may not.

Reworking a part that did not need it is wasteful. Accepting a part that does need rework is a failure. The skill is telling the difference, and that comes from knowing what the part has to do.

Lesson 10.6

Distortion and the practical weld

Putting the module together into a workable approach.

The five-step plan

  1. Before cutting, work out where the pulls will go. Sketch the joint and mark the pull direction on each weld
  2. Choose the sequence that balances the pulls. Weld opposite sides alternately, or weld from the middle out, or backstep
  3. Tack and check. Tack enough to hold, check the alignment, adjust if needed. Do not tack everything and then find the shape has moved
  4. Control heat as you go. Reasonable current, reasonable travel, reasonable electrode size. Do not use a bigger electrode than the joint needs
  5. Re-check after welding. If the shape matters, measure. If not, look and decide

Everyday rules

SituationWhat to do
Long butt joint, one side access onlyBackstep or skip weld
Long butt joint, both sides accessibleWeld first side, then opposite side, alternating
Corner joints on a frameTack all four, check diagonals, weld opposite corners first
Tee joint on a long plateWeld both sides of the upright alternately, or backstep along the joint
Thin sheetLow current, fast travel, accept some distortion, planish (hammer flat) afterward
Any joint where the finished dimension mattersClamp, or pre-set
Any joint where cracking is a concernDo not over-restrain. Let the joint move a little

Distortion is not a beginner's problem to solve

Every welded assembly distorts. Even a coded welder working to a procedure produces distortion — the procedure is written to control it, not eliminate it.

What a beginner can do is understand the causes and apply the standard techniques. What takes years to develop is the judgement of how much distortion a specific job will produce, which comes from doing the work and seeing the results.

Practical assignment — Module 10

Do the practical work if you have access to a machine and supervision. Otherwise complete the observation and planning parts.

  1. Find three welded assemblies that show visible distortion. Photograph each and label which form it is: transverse, angular or longitudinal. Describe the evidence you see
  2. Find one assembly where distortion was controlled. Describe what you think the welder did to control it — both sides, backstep, pre-set, clamping
  3. Sketch the distortion pull pattern for a rectangular frame made of four pieces of angle iron with welds at all four corners. Mark the direction each weld pulls, and describe the total effect
  4. If you have machine access: weld a 200mm butt joint on 6mm plate with a single pass. Measure the length before and after. Report the total shrinkage. Then cut and check the angular distortion
  5. Plan a distortion-controlled sequence for a real joint you are about to make. Write it as a numbered list of steps, referencing the five techniques in Lesson 10.3
  6. Your distortion policy. For the work you actually do — farm repairs, small fabrications, whatever it is — state which forms of distortion matter and which do not, and why

Points 2 and 6 carry the most marks. Point 2 assesses whether you can read the evidence of control in someone else's work. Point 6 tests whether you can distinguish between distortion that matters and distortion that does not.

Module 10 knowledge check

1. Why does welding inevitably cause distortion?

  1. Because the arc is too hot
  2. Because metal expands when heated and contracts when cooled — the weld shrinks as it cools, and it pulls the surrounding metal with it
  3. Because the filler metal is different from the parent
  4. Because of the electricity passing through
Show answer

b. Every weld shrinks as it cools. This is a physical property of metal under welding heat, not a defect. The question is not whether distortion will occur, but where it will go and how much.

2. What is angular distortion?

  1. The joint pulls together in length
  2. The weld pulls the plates up or down at an angle, closing or opening the joint
  3. The plate bows along its length
  4. The weld curves away from the joint
Show answer

b. Angular distortion is the most visible form. A single-V butt weld on one face pulls the plates toward the weld side, bending them. A tee joint lifts the base plate on both sides of the upright.

3. What is backstep welding and why is it used?

  1. Welding backwards in position
  2. Welding in short runs in the reverse direction, so each run pulls into cold metal rather than hot metal — distributing the distortion and reducing its total effect
  3. Welding only the back of a joint
  4. Welding with a shorter arc
Show answer

b. Backstep welding is one of the standard distortion-control techniques. Each run pulls into cold metal that resists movement, rather than into hot metal that is still expanding. The overall pull is smaller and more evenly distributed.

4. Why is heavy clamping not free when controlling distortion?

  1. Clamps are expensive
  2. Restraint prevents movement, but the pull then becomes locked-in stress in the joint — and restrained joints crack more often
  3. Clamping makes the weld cool too fast
  4. Clamping is always the correct solution
Show answer

b. Distortion is the visible result of the pull. If the pull cannot move the metal, it becomes internal stress. High restraint is one of the conditions that promotes cracking, so clamping trades visible distortion for hidden stress — worth doing where the shape matters, but not a free solution.

5. When is it acceptable to leave distorted work as it is?

  1. Never — always straighten
  2. When the part is not critical, when straightening would take longer than remaking, and when straightening risks further damage
  3. Only on expensive parts
  4. Only when the customer agrees
Show answer

b. A bench frame with 5mm of bow over 2 metres works fine. A trailer axle mount with the same bow may not. The judgement of which is which separates a competent welder from a fussy one, and it comes from knowing what the part has to do.
