1. Types of Joints: Butt, Lap, T, Corner, Edge

When you join two pieces of metal, the way you arrange them before welding is called the joint type. The five basic joints are butt, lap, T, corner, and edge. Each has a specific use and requires different preparation. For a butt joint, you place two plates edge to edge in the same plane. This is the most common joint for pipes and plates. You need to bevel the edges for thick metal (over 6 mm) to ensure full penetration. A root gap of 1.53 mm is typical. In Africa, many workshops use oxy-fuel cutting to bevel edges, then grind them clean.

A lap joint overlaps one plate on another. It's strong and easy to align, often used for sheets and structural work. You weld along the edge of the top plate. For a T-joint, one plate stands perpendicular to another, forming a 'T'. This is common in frames and beams. You can weld from one or both sides. A corner joint joins two plates at an angle, like a box corner. You can weld inside, outside, or both. An edge joint places two plates side by side and welds along the edges; it's used for thin sheets and not for high stress.

In practice, you must clean all joints to bare metal before welding. Remove rust, paint, and oil with a grinder or wire brush. For a butt joint on 10 mm plate, use a 60 included angle bevel, a 2 mm root gap, and a 3 mm root face. For a T-joint on 8 mm plate, a 45 bevel on the vertical plate works well. Always tack weld first to hold alignment. In rural Africa, where power can be unreliable, a generator welder (e.g., 5 kW) is common. Use 3.2 mm electrodes at 90120 amps for these joints. Remember: joint design controls strength. A poorly fitted joint will fail, no matter how good your welding.

2. Welding Positions: Flat, Horizontal, Vertical, Overhead

The position you weld in changes everything: gravity pulls molten metal down, so you must adjust your technique. The four standard positions are flat (1G), horizontal (2G), vertical (3G), and overhead (4G). Flat is easiest: you weld on top of a plate, and gravity helps the metal flow into the joint. Use higher current and a drag angle (electrode tilted 515 away from direction of travel). For 3.2 mm electrode, set 110140 amps. This is where you learn.

Horizontal position: you weld on a vertical surface but the weld axis is horizontal. The molten metal tends to sag. Use a slight backhand angle (electrode tilted 010 toward the weld) and lower current, about 90110 amps for 3.2 mm. Keep a short arc and move steadily. Vertical: you weld up or down a vertical plate. Vertical-up gives better penetration but is slower; vertical-down is faster but prone to lack of fusion. For vertical-up, use 80100 amps, a slight weave, and pause at the sides. Overhead: you weld from below, fighting gravity. Use the lowest current, 7090 amps for 3.2 mm, and a very short arc. Keep the puddle small and move quickly.

In Africa, many welders work on pipelines, water tanks, and gates in all positions. Practice each position on scrap plates. A good exercise: take two 10 mm plates, tack them in a T, and run beads in all four positions. Check your penetration by cutting and etching with acid (or just break it). Remember: position affects heat input. Overhead and vertical need less heat to avoid dripping. Always wear a leather apron and sleevessparks fly downward in overhead. If you're welding a water tank in a village, you might have to do vertical and overhead welds on the inside. That's where skill matters.

3. Visual Inspection of Welds

Visual inspection is the first and most important check of any weld. Before you weld, inspect the joint: are the edges clean, the gap correct, and the parts aligned? After welding, look for defects with your naked eye and a magnifying glass (10x). You need good lightuse a torch or daylight. Check for uniform bead size, smooth ripples, and proper tie-in at the toes. A good weld has a slight crown (12 mm high) and no undercut. Undercut is a groove melted into the base metal along the weld edge; it weakens the joint. Measure it with a welding gauge if you have one.

Look for cracks, porosity (small holes), slag inclusions (dark spots), and incomplete fusion. Cracks can be hairline; clean the surface with a wire brush and look again. Porosity appears as tiny pits or holes on the surface. Slag inclusions look like dark lines or spots. Incomplete fusion shows as a gap between weld and base metal. Also check for spattersmall metal balls stuck to the surface. Spatter is not a defect but indicates wrong current or arc length. For critical welds, use a fillet weld gauge to measure leg length. A 6 mm fillet weld should have 6 mm legs.

In Africa, many workshops lack expensive testing equipment, so visual inspection is your main tool. Train your eye: weld a test piece, then break it in a vice. Look at the fracture surfaceif you see shiny metal, it's fused; if you see dark, rough areas, it's not. Keep a log of your inspections. For structural work, codes like AWS D1.1 require 100% visual inspection. You can make a simple checklist: no cracks, no undercut over 0.8 mm, porosity less than 3 mm, and proper size. Always inspect before, during, and after welding. If you see a defect, grind it out and reweld. Never paint over a bad weld.

4. Common Weld Defects and Causes

Every welder makes defects; the key is to know why and how to fix them. The most common defects are porosity, slag inclusion, lack of fusion, undercut, overlap, and cracks. Porosity is gas trapped in the weld, appearing as small holes. Causes: damp electrodes, dirty base metal, too long an arc, or wrong polarity. Fix: use dry electrodes (store in a heated quiver at 100150C), clean metal, and shorten arc. Slag inclusion is slag trapped in the weld, often from poor cleaning between passes. Use a chipping hammer and wire brush after each pass.

Lack of fusion: the weld metal doesn't fuse to the base metal or previous pass. Causes: low current, fast travel, wrong electrode angle, or too large a root gap. Increase current by 1020 amps, slow down, and direct the arc at the joint. Undercut: a groove melted into the base metal at the weld toe. Causes: too high current, long arc, or wrong angle. Reduce current, shorten arc, and use a slight backhand angle. Overlap: weld metal rolls over the base metal without fusing. Causes: low current, fast travel, or wrong angle. Increase current and slow down. Cracks: can be hot (during solidification) or cold (after cooling). Causes: high sulfur in steel, rapid cooling, or high restraint. Preheat thick steel to 100150C and use low-hydrogen electrodes like E7018.

In Africa, humidity is a common cause of porosityelectrodes absorb moisture. Always keep electrodes in a sealed container with silica gel. For field work, use a portable oven (12V or gas). Also, rusty plates are common; grind to shiny metal. If you see a defect, grind it out completely (to sound metal) and reweld. Never weld over a defect. For critical jobs like bridges or pressure vessels, use a qualified welding procedure. Remember: 80% of defects come from poor preparation and wrong current. Check your machine settings daily with a test bead.

5. Introduction to Non-Destructive Testing

Non-destructive testing (NDT) finds defects without damaging the weld. The main methods are visual (VT), dye penetrant (PT), magnetic particle (MT), ultrasonic (UT), and radiographic (RT). In Africa, VT is everywhere; PT and MT are affordable for workshops; UT and RT are expensive and usually only for critical projects like pipelines or pressure vessels. Dye penetrant works on clean, smooth surfaces. You spray a red penetrant, wait 10 minutes, wipe it off, then spray a white developer. Red lines show cracks or porosity. It costs about $20 for a kit and works on stainless and aluminum. Magnetic particle testing uses a magnetic field and iron powder. It only works on ferromagnetic metals (steel). You magnetize the part, spray iron powder, and defects show as powder clusters. A yoke kit costs $200500.

Ultrasonic testing sends high-frequency sound waves into the weld. A transducer picks up echoes from defects. It requires training and a calibrated machine ($2000+). Radiographic testing uses X-rays or gamma rays to produce a film image. It's the most reliable but needs a license and radiation safety. In many African countries, RT is done by specialized companies. For a small workshop, start with VT and PT. PT can find surface cracks that VT misses. MT is better for subsurface defects near the surface. Always clean the weld before PT or MToil and paint will block penetration.

If you can't afford NDT, use destructive testing on test pieces: bend tests, nick-break tests, or macro-etch. For a bend test, cut a strip across the weld, bend it in a vice; if it cracks, the weld is bad. Macro-etch: polish a cross-section, apply acid (e.g., 10% nitric acid in alcohol), and look at the fusion line. In Africa, many welders use the 'hammer and chisel' test: chisel along the weld toe; if it lifts, lack of fusion. But for code work, you need certified NDT. Consider partnering with a local NDT company for critical jobs. Always document results.

6. Weld Quality Standards and Codes

Welding codes are rules that ensure safety and quality. The most common international codes are AWS D1.1 (structural steel), ASME Section IX (pressure vessels), and ISO 9606 (welder qualification). In Africa, many countries adopt these or have their own, like SANS 10162 in South Africa. A code tells you: joint design, electrode type, preheat, current range, and acceptance criteria. For example, AWS D1.1 says for a 10 mm fillet weld, you must use a minimum leg size of 5 mm, and undercut cannot exceed 0.8 mm. It also requires welders to be qualified by testing.

To qualify a welder, you weld a test coupon according to a Welding Procedure Specification (WPS). The WPS lists all variables: process (SMAW), electrode (E7018), current (110140 A), position (3G), and joint design. Then the coupon is tested by visual, bend, and sometimes RT. If it passes, you get a certificate for that position and thickness. In Africa, many welders learn on the job without certification. But for jobs like building a bridge or a fuel tank, clients demand certified welders. You can get certified through organizations like the African Welding Federation or local training centers. It costs $100500.

In your workshop, create a simple quality plan: use a checklist for each job. Check base metal (clean, correct grade), electrode (dry, correct type), machine settings, and welding technique. After welding, inspect visually. For critical welds, hire an NDT inspector. Keep records: date, welder name, WPS number, and inspection results. If a defect is found, repair it and re-inspect. Remember: codes are not just paperworkthey prevent failures that kill people. A poorly welded trailer hitch can cause a fatal accident. Follow the code, even if it slows you down. In Africa, where infrastructure is growing, quality welding builds trust and gets you better-paying jobs.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Weld Joints, Positions & Inspection for African Fabrication Businesses

This reference module is built for welders, workshop owners, and technical trainers operating in African fabrication markets from informal metalwork clusters in Kariobangi and Suame Magazine to structured welding shops supplying construction, agri-processing, and mining supply chains. The goal is simple: reduce rework, win contracts that require certified quality, and protect your team from injury. Every technique below has a direct commercial consequence a rejected joint means lost material, lost labour, and a damaged reputation with a buyer who may not return.

1. The Five Basic Joint Types

Every weld you will ever make is a variation of five configurations. Knowing which one a drawing calls for and preparing it correctly is the difference between a joint that passes inspection and one that fails under load.

Table 1: Joint types, typical applications, and preparation notes for African fabrication contexts
Joint Type Description Typical Use Preparation Note
Butt Two members aligned in the same plane, edges joined Structural beams, water tanks, gate frames, trailer chassis Square edge for thin plate; single-V or double-V groove for >6 mm plate
Lap One member overlaps another, welded along the edge Roof sheeting, tank patches, low-cost repairs Ensure minimum overlap of 3 plate thickness; clean mill scale
Tee (T) One member meets another at 90, forming a T shape Bracket stiffeners, machine frames, burglar bars Fillet weld both sides where possible; watch for lamellar tearing
Corner Two members meet at an angle (usually 90) Box sections, truck bodies, storage bins Full penetration preferred for load-bearing corners
Edge Edges of two parallel or angled members joined Sheet metal enclosures, thin-gauge ducting Requires careful heat control to avoid burn-through

Source: Adapted from AWS A3.0M/A3.0:2020, Standard Welding Terms and Definitions, American Welding Society.

2. Welding Positions: Flat, Horizontal, Vertical, Overhead

Position determines difficulty, electrode angle, amperage, and travel speed. In the field, most African welders are paid for flat work but asked to deliver vertical and overhead joints on site often without additional training. Understanding position codes (1G, 2G, 3G, 4G for groove welds; 1F4F for fillets) is essential if you intend to bid for structural or pressure work.

3. Visual Inspection of Welds

Visual inspection (VT) is the cheapest and most powerful quality tool available to any African workshop. Before you invest in dye penetrant or ultrasonic equipment, train your eye and your team to catch defects at the bench. A weld that fails visual inspection never reaches the customer saving transport costs, site callbacks, and reputational damage.

Key visual checks: bead uniformity, leg length and throat thickness on fillets, undercut at toes, overlap, surface porosity, cracks, spatter, and arc strikes. Use a weld gauge, a magnifying glass, and a good light source. Record findings against the drawing or WPS (Welding Procedure Specification).

Table 2: Common weld defects, visual indicators, root causes, and corrective actions
Defect Visual Indicator Root Cause Corrective Action
Porosity Surface pinholes or gas pockets Damp electrodes, dirty base metal, insufficient gas (MIG/TIG), long arc Bake electrodes, clean joint, check gas flow, shorten arc
Undercut Groove melted into base metal at weld toe Excessive current, long arc, wrong electrode angle Reduce amperage, tighten arc, adjust travel angle
Slag Inclusion Dark lines or pockets within the bead Poor interpass cleaning, low current, wrong polarity Chipping hammer and wire brush between passes; verify polarity
Lack of Penetration Incomplete fusion at root, visible gap Low current, too-fast travel, wrong joint prep Increase current, slow travel, open root gap correctly
Cracking Visible linear fracture, often at crater or HAZ High restraint, hydrogen, fast cooling, high carbon steel Preheat, use low-hydrogen electrodes, control cooling
Spatter Metal droplets on base plate High current, long arc, wrong gas mix Adjust parameters, use anti-spatter, clean after weld

Source: Adapted from AWS B1.10M/B1.10:2016, Guide for the Nondestructive Examination of Welds, and ISO 6520-1:2007, Classification of geometric imperfections in metallic materials Welding.

4. Introduction to Non-Destructive Testing (NDT)

NDT allows you to verify weld integrity without destroying the workpiece. For most African fabrication businesses, the practical entry points are:

  • Visual Testing (VT): Always first. Low cost, high value.
  • Dye Penetrant Testing (PT): Detects surface-breaking defects on non-porous materials. Cheap and portable.
  • Magnetic Particle Testing (MT): Detects surface and near-surface defects in ferromagnetic steels. Requires a yoke and contrast paint.
  • Ultrasonic Testing (UT): Detects internal defects. Requires trained technicians and calibrated equipment often outsourced.
  • Radiographic Testing (RT): X-ray or gamma-ray imaging. High cost, regulatory oversight, typically for pressure vessels and critical structures.

For most workshops, investing in VT training for supervisors and partnering with a local NDT firm for PT/MT on high-value jobs is the most commercially sensible path. Certification schemes such as ISO 9712 or ASNT Level I/II provide recognised credentials that unlock higher-tier contracts.

5. Weld Quality Standards and Codes

Knowing the code your customer expects is a business differentiator. Common references in African markets include:

  • AWS D1.1/D1.1M Structural Steel Welding Code (widely used in mining and construction).
  • ISO 3834 Quality requirements for fusion welding of metallic materials.
  • ASME BPVC Section IX Welding, Brazing, and Fusing Qualifications (pressure equipment).
  • EN 1090 Execution of steel structures (increasingly referenced in North and West Africa).
  • ISO 9606-1 Qualification testing of welders Fusion welding.

Aligning your workshop procedures with ISO 3834 or AWS D1.1 does not require expensive certification immediately but documenting your WPS, welder qualifications, and inspection records positions you to bid for larger contracts and export-oriented supply chains.

SAFETY WARNING Arc Flash, Fumes & Fire

Welding without proper PPE causes permanent injury. In many African workshops, welders work without auto-darkening helmets, fume extraction, or fire watchers. This is unacceptable and illegal under most national OSH regulations (e.g., Kenya OSHA 2007, South Africa OHS Act 85 of 1993, Nigeria Factories Act).

  • Eyes: Always use a properly shaded helmet (Shade 1013 for arc welding). Never look at an arc with naked eyes.
  • Fumes: Weld in ventilated areas or use local exhaust ventilation. Manganese and hexavalent chromium fumes cause neurological and respiratory damage.
  • Fire: Clear 10 m of combustible material. Keep a fire extinguisher and sand bucket within reach. Never weld near fuel drums, paint, or thatch.
  • Electrical: Inspect cables daily. Use RCDs/earth leakage protection. Never weld in wet conditions without insulation.
  • Burns & UV: Wear leather apron, gloves, and closed shoes. Protect bystanders with screens.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Confirm joint type and position against the drawing or WPS before striking an arc.
  2. Clean base metal remove rust, oil, paint, and mill scale at least 25 mm from the joint.
  3. Set amperage and polarity for the electrode/process and position (flat > horizontal > vertical > overhead).
  4. Use correct electrode angle 90 to the joint, with slight drag or push as specified.
  5. Check interpass cleaning chip slag and brush before each pass.
  6. Inspect visually after each pass look for undercut, porosity, cracks, and incomplete fusion.
  7. Record results welder ID, date, WPS number, and any defects found.
  8. Wear full PPE helmet, gloves, apron, respirator, and safety boots. No exceptions.
