WELDING FUNDAMENTALS · MODULE 5 OF 12

Consumables

Electrodes, MIG wire, shielding gas. What each is, what each does, and why storage in a tropical climate matters more than most beginners realise.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  • Explain what an electrode is and what each part of it does
  • Identify the main electrode coating types and say what each is for
  • Explain why moisture in electrodes causes hydrogen cracking — and how to prevent it
  • Store electrodes correctly in a humid climate
  • Choose the right MIG wire and shielding gas for a job
  • Buy consumables without being sold the wrong thing
Lesson 5.1

What an electrode actually is

A stick electrode — the "stick" — is a consumable metal rod with a coating applied around it. It does three jobs at once, and understanding which is which is the foundation of the whole module.

The three parts

  1. The core wire — a mild steel or alloy wire of a specific diameter. This melts in the arc and becomes the filler metal
  2. The flux coating — a mixture of minerals, compounds and often metal powders applied around the core. It produces shielding gas, forms slag, stabilises the arc, and can add alloying elements to the weld
  3. The contact end — the bare section at the end where the holder grips. No coating, so the current can pass

What the coating does — four jobs

FunctionHow
Produces shielding gasThe coating decomposes in the heat of the arc and releases CO₂ and other gases that push air away from the molten pool
Forms slagMolten minerals float on top of the pool, protecting it as it solidifies and slowing the cooling slightly
Stabilises the arcCertain compounds make the arc easier to strike and maintain, and reduce spatter
Alloying and cleaningThe coating can contain metal powders that add to the weld, and deoxidising agents that clean the pool

Common diameter sizes

Standard diameters, from thin to thick: 1.6mm, 2.0mm, 2.5mm, 3.2mm, 4.0mm, 5.0mm. In practice the beginner will mostly meet 2.5mm and 3.2mm.

  • Thinner electrodes — lower current, less heat, better for thin steel and for out-of-position work. But slower deposition and more electrode changes
  • Thicker electrodes — higher current, more metal laid per minute, better for thick steel in the flat position. But they need more amps, which means a bigger machine

The current range for each diameter is printed on the electrode packet, or available from the supplier. Going outside it — too hot or too cold — causes defects covered in module 11.

Lesson 5.2

Coating types

The coating is what most distinguishes one electrode from another. There are four main types in common use, and each has a different character.

The four to know

TypeCommon nameCharacterBest for
RutileGeneral purposeEasy to strike, smooth arc, low spatter, neat bead. Slag is easy to chip. Absorbs moisture if not stored dryGeneral work, thin sheet, beginners. The default for most farm repairs
Basic / low-hydrogenLime or basicStronger, tougher weld, more resistant to cracking. Requires better technique to run, cleaner joint and dry storage. Slag is harder to chipStructural work, thick sections, any job where the weld carries significant load
CellulosicDeep penetrationThin coating, fierce arc, deep penetration, fast freezing. Lots of spatter. Strong smellPipe welding and root passes, vertical down work
Iron-oxide / rutile-heavyHigh-speedVery smooth arc, high deposition, neat finish, but low penetrationHigh-speed flat-position production work where penetration is not critical

What this course will not do: give you a classification code

Electrode classification systems differ by country — the AWS A5.1 system (E6013, E7018 and so on) is common internationally, while some regions use ISO or BS classifications. What each code means is well-documented and your supplier will have the information.

What matters for this course is the character: rutile for general work, basic/low-hydrogen for anything load-bearing, cellulosic for pipe and root passes. The code is a label, the character is what you actually choose on.

Rutile versus basic — the honest trade-off

Almost every farm repair can be done with rutile. It is easy to run, forgiving of technique, and produces a reasonable bead. Its weaknesses: lower strength than basic, and it can absorb moisture that causes hydrogen cracking.

Basic electrodes produce stronger, tougher welds and are less sensitive to hydrogen if properly stored. They are harder to run and demand better preparation — a basic electrode will not forgive a dirty joint the way a rutile one might.

The rule that most workshops use: rutile for general work and repairs, basic for anything where the weld carries a load that matters — trailer drawbars, structural brackets, hoist points, anything above people.

Lesson 5.3

Moisture: the hidden cause of most weld failures

This is the most important lesson in the module and the one most consistently ignored.

What moisture does to an electrode

An electrode's coating is hygroscopic — it absorbs water from the air. In a humid climate, a box of rutile electrodes left open in a workshop will pick up moisture within hours. The moisture then breaks down in the arc and produces hydrogen, which dissolves into the molten weld.

Module 2 covered what happens next: as the weld cools, the hydrogen becomes trapped and migrates into the hardened heat affected zone, where it causes cracking. The crack can appear hours or days later, long after the joint has been inspected, paid for and put into service.

The delay is the whole problem

Hydrogen cracking gives no warning. The job looks finished, the customer is happy, and a week later the joint fails. This is why commercial welders keep electrodes in heated ovens, and why a welder who has never had a joint fail this way may simply have been lucky.

When moisture matters most

  • You are using basic/low-hydrogen electrodes. These exist specifically to reduce hydrogen, and their advantage is destroyed by poor storage
  • The steel is thick. Thick sections cool faster, which is exactly the condition that promotes hydrogen cracking
  • The joint is highly restrained. A welded frame that cannot move as it cools builds more stress around the trapped hydrogen
  • The work is critical. Drawbars, load-bearing brackets, lifting points — anything where failure has consequences

Prevention

  1. Buy electrodes in sealed packets from a supplier who turns stock over. A dusty box on a shelf that has been there two years has already absorbed everything it is going to
  2. Keep them sealed until use. Open a packet only when you are going to weld, and only as many as you will use
  3. Store opened packets in an airtight container with a desiccant. A sealed bucket with a sachet of silica gel is a real solution. It costs almost nothing and prevents most of the problem
  4. Re-dry electrodes that may have absorbed moisture. Electrodes can be dried in an oven at the temperature the manufacturer specifies — usually 100–150°C for rutile, higher for basic. Ask the supplier for the correct re-drying temperature before doing this; too hot ruins the coating
  5. Never re-dry an electrode more than the manufacturer recommends. Repeated drying cycles degrade the coating
  6. Discard electrodes with visibly damaged coating — cracked, chipped, or missing in patches. An electrode with a damaged coating cannot shield the pool properly

In a humid climate, this is not a nitpick

A rutile electrode left out overnight in a humid workshop is not immediately ruined. A basic electrode left out for a week may already be. In the tropics, buy small quantities often rather than a large quantity that sits on a shelf through the wet season.

Lesson 5.4

MIG wire

A MIG wire is the filler — it is fed continuously through the torch and melts into the pool. It is not coated. Shielding comes from the gas (or from a flux core, in flux-cored wire).

Wire types

TypeWhat it isBest for
Solid mild steel wireThe standard MIG filler. Uncoated, copper-coated for corrosion resistance and conductivityMild steel. The default for MIG work on steel in a workshop
Flux-cored wire (gas-shielded)Tubular wire filled with flux. Used with shielding gas like solid wire, but the flux adds arc stability and penetrationHeavier steel, out-of-position work. Better penetration than solid wire
Flux-cored wire (self-shielded)Tubular wire with a flux that generates shielding gas, so no cylinder is needed. Runs on reverse polarityOutdoor work where wind would defeat MIG gas shielding. The farm-friendly option
Stainless steel wireFor welding stainless steelFood, dairy, chemical equipment
Aluminium wireFor aluminiumAluminium work, but requires the correct machine and technique

Wire diameter

Standard diameters: 0.6mm, 0.8mm, 0.9mm, 1.0mm, 1.2mm. Diameter determines the current range you can run:

  • 0.6mm — very thin sheet, low current. Hard to feed reliably
  • 0.8mm — the general-purpose diameter for thin sheet and light fabrication
  • 1.0mm — heavier work, more deposition, needs more current
  • 1.2mm — heavy fabrication and thick steel

Wire that is too thin for the current you want to run will melt back up the liner and jam. Wire too thick for the current runs cold, spatters heavily, and produces poor fusion.

Spool sizes and the economics

SpoolConvenienceCost per kg
Small hobby spool (~100mm, 0.5kg)Easy to swap, fits any machine, ideal for occasional useMuch higher
Standard spool (200mm, 5kg)Standard on most small machinesModerate
Large spool (300mm, 15kg)Best value, but needs a bigger machineLowest

If you use MIG regularly, the small spools become a false economy quickly. If you use it occasionally, they are convenient and last a long time without rusting.

Storage of MIG wire

Solid MIG wire is copper-coated for corrosion resistance, but the coating is thin. In a humid climate, a spool left off the machine will rust — and rust on wire ruins wire feed and contaminates the weld.

  • Keep spools sealed until use. The plastic bag they come in is not just packaging
  • Avoid opening a spool if you will not use it within a few weeks. Buy the size that suits your use rate
  • Store in a dry place. Not next to an open workshop door in the wet season
  • Discard rusted wire. It cannot be saved
Lesson 5.5

Shielding gases

Every gas-shielded process relies on a stream of gas from a cylinder to exclude air from the pool. Choosing the right gas affects arc stability, penetration, spatter and cost.

The gases you will meet

GasWhat it doesTypical use
CO₂Active gas. Cheap. Produces deep penetration but harsher arc and more spatterMild steel MIG. The budget option for production work
Argon + CO₂ mix (usually 75/25 or 80/20)Argon gives a smoother arc; CO₂ adds penetration and stability. Best of bothMild steel MIG. The standard choice for general fabrication
ArgonInert. Very stable arc, no reaction with the metalTIG for steel, stainless and aluminium. Not used for MIG on steel — too cold
Argon + helium mixHotter arc than pure argon, better penetrationTIG on thick aluminium

Cylinder sizes and cost

Cylinders come in various sizes, and in most countries you hire rather than buy the cylinder itself, paying for the gas each refill. Typical sizes:

  • Small cylinder (~1m³) — enough for a few hours' work. Cheap to refill but expensive per litre of gas
  • Medium (~5m³) — reasonable balance for a small workshop
  • Large (~20–50m³) — cheapest per litre, but the cylinder is heavy and awkward, and needs regular use to justify

The supplier will usually want a deposit or a hire agreement, and refills are only available from them. Find out the arrangement before you buy a MIG set.

Flow rates

Typical shielding gas flow rates for MIG and TIG are in the range of 8–15 litres per minute, with 10–12 L/min a common starting point. Flow that is too low leaves the pool under-shielded; too high causes turbulence that actually draws air into the shielded area.

Flow is set by the regulator on the cylinder. Buy a proper regulator, not a cheap flowmeter that reads wrong. Ask your supplier what they recommend for your setup.

Shielding gas displaces oxygen

Module 1 warned about this and it applies at every use: argon, CO₂ and their mixes are heavier than air and accumulate at low level in enclosed spaces. In a pit, a tank, a small closed room or a lowered car body, they can displace the oxygen with no warning and no smell.

Never weld in a confined space on the strength of this course. That work requires atmospheric testing, ventilation and a permit — see module 1.

Lesson 5.6

Buying and storing consumables

What to buy

For a beginning stick welder, the sensible initial stock is:

  • A small packet of 2.5mm rutile electrodes — for thin sheet and general light work
  • A small packet of 3.2mm rutile electrodes — for the majority of farm work
  • A small packet of 3.2mm basic electrodes — for anything that carries a real load

Buy small quantities. In a humid climate, larger packets mean more of them sit around absorbing moisture. Restocking is a small hassle compared with failing a joint.

What to look for

CheckWhy
Packet intact and sealedOpened packets absorb moisture. Buy from a supplier who sells stock quickly
Manufacturing date printedElectrodes do not have an indefinite shelf life
Coating is intactLook at the electrodes through any window in the packet. Cracks, chips, or missing patches mean the coating is damaged
No rust on the bare contact endRust on the contact end means the electrode has been damp
Supplier knows the classificationIf they cannot tell you what the code means, they cannot help you choose

Storing at home

  • A metal or plastic box with a tight-fitting lid. Not a plastic bag tied shut — that traps moisture in, not out
  • Add a sachet of silica gel desiccant. Recharge it periodically by drying it in an oven
  • Keep the box off the floor. Damp rises
  • Never store electrodes in the same cupboard as acid, paint, or anything that gives off fumes. The coating absorbs them and the weld suffers
  • If you are going to store electrodes for months, buy sealed packets and only open what you need

The rule that saves more welds than any other

Buy small, use soon, store sealed. An electrode is not like a bag of cement that just needs to be dry — it is a chemical product whose performance depends on the coating being in the condition the manufacturer intended. In a hot, humid climate, that condition does not last.

Practical assignment — Module 5

This is a research and setup assignment. No welding is required.

  1. Visit a supplier. Photograph three different electrode packets available in your area. For each, write down: the classification code on the packet, the diameter, the current range printed, and the price
  2. Ask the supplier which is rutile and which is basic. Write down exactly what they say. If they do not know, write that down too — it tells you what kind of supplier this is
  3. Your initial stock decision. State which diameters and which types you will buy, and why. Reference the character table in Lesson 5.2
  4. Your storage plan. Photograph the box or container you will keep electrodes in. Describe where it will sit — off the floor, away from fumes, in the driest place you have
  5. Your re-drying source. Identify a person or business — an oven, a bakery, a laboratory — who could dry electrodes at the correct temperature if you needed to. Write down the temperature you would use and where you got that figure from
  6. If considering MIG: name the nearest supplier of shielding gas, the cylinder sizes they stock, deposit or hire cost, and refill cost
  7. One thing you will not do: state one behaviour from this module that you will specifically avoid, and why

Points 1, 3 and 5 carry the most marks. Point 1 grounds the work in what is actually available to you. Point 3 tests whether you can choose. Point 5 tests whether you know that a good intention without a specific source is not a plan.

Module 5 knowledge check

1. What are the three parts of a stick electrode, and what does each do?

  1. Handle, shaft, tip
  2. Core wire (filler), flux coating (shielding, slag, arc stability), bare contact end (current path)
  3. Metal, plastic, ceramic
  4. Outer shield, inner core, base
Show answer

b. The core wire becomes the filler metal. The flux coating does four jobs: produces shielding gas, forms slag, stabilises the arc and can add alloying elements. The bare contact end is where the holder grips and current passes.

2. Why does moisture in electrodes cause weld failure?

  1. It makes the electrode burn faster
  2. The moisture breaks down in the arc and produces hydrogen, which dissolves into the weld and causes delayed cracking in the hardened zone beside the weld
  3. It makes the electrode too heavy
  4. It shortens the arc
Show answer

b. Hydrogen from moisture dissolves into the molten weld, becomes trapped as the weld cools, and migrates into the hardened heat affected zone. Cracking can appear hours or days later — after the job is finished, inspected and put into service.

3. Which electrode type is the sensible default for most farm repair work, and why?

  1. Cellulosic — deepest penetration
  2. Basic — strongest weld
  3. Rutile — easiest to run, forgiving of technique, produces a reasonable bead
  4. Iron-oxide — smoothest finish
Show answer

c. Rutile is the standard for general repair work because it strikes easily, runs smoothly, and tolerates imperfect joint preparation. Basic electrodes are used for anything where the weld carries a real structural load; cellulosic for pipe and root passes.

4. Why is a self-shielded flux-cored wire often the best choice for outdoor MIG-type work?

  1. It is cheaper than solid wire
  2. It generates its own shielding gas from the flux, so wind cannot blow the shielding away
  3. It does not need a wire feeder
  4. It works on any machine without polarity change
Show answer

b. Self-shielded flux-cored wire produces its own shielding at the arc, so it is wind-tolerant. This is exactly the property that stick has, and the reason both are suited to farm and outdoor work. It requires a polarity change on the machine.

5. What is the guiding rule for storing electrodes in a humid climate?

  1. Buy in bulk for a lower price
  2. Keep them in their sealed packet, buy small quantities often, and store in an airtight container with desiccant — do not let them sit through the wet season
  3. Leave them in the workshop to acclimatise
  4. Wet them slightly before use for a smoother arc
Show answer

b. Buy small, use soon, store sealed. Electrode coatings are hygroscopic and absorb moisture from the air. In a hot, humid climate, an opened packet left on a shelf will absorb enough moisture to risk hydrogen cracking — while the electrodes look perfectly normal.
