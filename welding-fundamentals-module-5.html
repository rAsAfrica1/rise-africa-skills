1. GTAW Equipment and Setup

Gas Tungsten Arc Welding (GTAW), also called TIG welding, uses a non-consumable tungsten electrode to create an arc. The arc melts the base metal, and you add filler rod separately if needed. The main equipment includes a welding machine (constant current type), a TIG torch with a tungsten electrode, a shielding gas cylinder (usually argon), a gas regulator and flowmeter, a foot pedal or amperage control, and cables. In Africa, you can find affordable TIG machines from brands like Riland, Jasic, or even modified stick welders with TIG kits. For basic steel and stainless steel up to 6mm thick, a 200-amp inverter machine is sufficient. You'll need a gas flow rate of about 10-15 litres per minute (LPM) for most jobs. Set up your machine: connect the ground clamp to your workbench or workpiece, plug the torch cable into the negative terminal (for DCEN, which is standard for steel), and connect the gas hose from the regulator to the torch. The tungsten electrode goes into the torch collet, and you tighten the collet body. The electrode should stick out about 3-6mm from the ceramic cup. Choose a cup size based on access: a #7 (11mm) cup is a good all-rounder. Before welding, open the gas valve on the cylinder, set the flowmeter to 12 LPM, and check for leaks with soapy water. Turn on the machine, set it to DCEN (electrode negative), and set your amperage. For 3mm steel, start with 80-100 amps. Practice striking an arc on a scrap piece: with the foot pedal, press down slowly until the arc starts, then maintain. Remember, GTAW requires clean metalgrind off rust, paint, and oil. In many African workshops, you'll use a generator or mains power; ensure stable voltage. A stable 220V supply is ideal. Keep your work area dry and well-ventilated.

2. Tungsten Electrode Preparation

The tungsten electrode is the heart of GTAW. It carries the arc and must be prepared correctly to get a stable, focused arc. There are several types: pure tungsten (green) for aluminum on AC, 2% thoriated (red) for steel on DC, and 2% ceriated (grey) or lanthanated (gold) for general use. Thoriated tungsten is common but slightly radioactive; ceriated or lanthanated are safer and work well on both AC and DC. In Africa, 2% lanthanated (gold) is a good choice because it handles both steel and aluminum and is readily available from welding suppliers. To prepare the electrode, you need a dedicated grinding wheelnever use the same wheel for steel, as contamination ruins the tungsten. Grind the electrode lengthwise (parallel to its axis), not radially. This creates a sharp point that directs the arc. For DCEN on steel, grind a sharp taper about 2.5 times the electrode diameter. For example, a 2.4mm (3/32 inch) electrode should have a taper about 6mm long. For AC on aluminum, you can ball the tip slightly by welding on a scrap piece, but a slightly truncated point also works. Always wear safety glasses and a dust mask when grinding thoriated tungsten. After grinding, insert the electrode into the torch collet so it sticks out about 3-6mm from the cup. If you're welding at low amps (under 50A), you can have a sharper point; at high amps, a slightly blunter tip lasts longer. Check the tip often: if it gets contaminated (touches the weld pool or filler), stop, remove it, and regrind. A contaminated electrode causes an erratic arc and poor weld quality. Keep several pre-ground electrodes on hand. Store them in a clean tube. In a busy workshop, you might grind 5-10 electrodes at a time. Remember, tungsten is expensive, so grind only when needed. A properly prepared electrode makes welding easier and gives you clean, strong welds.

3. Shielding Gas and Torch Angle

GTAW relies on shielding gas to protect the molten weld pool from air. The most common gas is argon (100% Ar) for both steel and aluminum. For thicker stainless steel (over 6mm), you can add 2-5% hydrogen to argon for faster welding, but pure argon is fine for most jobs. In Africa, argon cylinders are available from gas suppliers like Afrox, BOC, or local distributors. You'll need a regulator and flowmeter. Set the flow rate between 10 and 15 litres per minute (LPM). Too low, and you get porosity; too high, and you get turbulence that draws in air. For outside work, increase to 15-20 LPM to counter wind, but better to use a windbreak. The torch angle is critical. For flat welding, hold the torch perpendicular to the work (90 degrees) or tilted back 10-15 degrees in the direction of travel. This angle lets you see the puddle and directs the arc. For butt welds, a slight push angle (tilted forward) helps. For fillet welds, angle the torch about 45 degrees between the two plates. Keep the arc length short: about 2-3mm. A long arc spreads the heat and reduces penetration. The tungsten should be close to the puddle but not touching. Watch the puddle: it should be shiny and calm. If it looks turbulent or has black specks, you have contamination or poor gas coverage. Move the torch steadily, and dip the filler rod into the front edge of the puddle. For left-handed welders, mirror the technique. Practice on scrap: run beads without filler to get used to the arc and gas flow. Then add filler. In a typical African workshop, you might weld mild steel, stainless steel, or aluminum. Each needs slightly different settings, but the gas flow and torch angle principles remain the same. Always check your gas flow before welding. A simple flow test: turn on the gas and feel the flow at the cup with your hand (carefully). If it feels weak, check for leaks.

4. Foot Pedal and Amperage Control

The foot pedal (also called a foot control or amperage control) is what makes GTAW so precise. It lets you adjust the welding current while welding, which is essential for controlling heat input. On a TIG machine, the foot pedal is connected to the machine and controls the amperage from a minimum to a maximum set on the machine. For example, you might set the machine to 150 amps max, but the pedal lets you go from 0 to 150 amps smoothly. This is crucial for thin materials: you can start with low amps, increase as the puddle forms, and back off at the end to avoid burn-through. In Africa, many affordable TIG machines come with a simple on/off foot pedal or a hand amperage control. If you don't have a foot pedal, you can use a torch with a thumb wheel, but foot pedal is better for beginners. To use the foot pedal: place it on a stable, dry surface. Sit or stand comfortably. Press the pedal slowly to start the arc. As you weld, use your foot to increase or decrease amperage. For example, when welding 1.5mm steel, you might start at 30 amps, then increase to 60 amps as you move. When you reach the end, gradually release the pedal to taper off the current and fill the crater. This prevents cracking. Practice on scrap: draw a line, then weld along it, varying the pedal. You'll learn to hear the arca steady hum means good amperage. If it crackles, you're too low or too high. For aluminum, you need more amps to start because it conducts heat away quickly. Set the machine to AC and use the pedal to control. Remember, the foot pedal is not just for starting and stopping; it's your heat control. In a production setting, you might use a remote amperage control on the torch, but for learning, the foot pedal is best. Keep the pedal clean and dry. If it gets wet, you could get a shock. Always check the cable for damage. A good foot pedal can last for years if treated well.

5. Filler Rod Addition Techniques

Adding filler rod in GTAW is a skill that takes practice. Unlike stick welding, the filler is added separately, and you control the rate. The filler rod should match the base metal: for mild steel, use ER70S-6; for stainless steel, use ER308L or ER316L; for aluminum, use ER4043 or ER5356. In Africa, these rods are available from welding suppliers, often in 1.6mm, 2.4mm, or 3.2mm diameters. Choose a rod diameter about half the thickness of the base metal; for 3mm steel, use 1.6mm or 2.4mm rod. Hold the filler rod in your non-dominant hand like a pencil, with about 100-150mm sticking out beyond your fingers. Rest your hand on the workbench or use a support to keep it steady. The rod should enter the puddle at a shallow angle, about 15-20 degrees to the workpiece. Dip the rod into the front edge of the puddle, then withdraw it slightly. Don't pull it out of the gas shieldkeep the tip within the shielding gas to prevent oxidation. The motion is a rhythmic dip and withdraw: dip, move, dip, move. For each dip, add a small amount of filler. The puddle should rise slightly. If you add too much, the weld becomes lumpy; too little, and you get undercut. Coordinate with the foot pedal: as you dip, you might increase amperage slightly to ensure fusion. For a butt joint with a gap, you might need to add more filler to fill the gap. For a fillet weld, direct the filler into the corner. Practice on scrap: run a bead without filler, then add filler. Start with a simple lap joint. Watch the puddle: it should be shiny and fluid. If the rod sticks to the tungsten, you touched the tungsten or the puddle froze. Stop, clean the tungsten, and restart. Keep the rod cleanwipe it with a cloth. In a typical African workshop, you might weld a gate or a trailer. Use a comfortable rhythm. Speed is not as important as consistency. A good weld has evenly spaced ripples. Remember, the filler rod also helps cool the puddle, so you can use it to control heat. With practice, you'll develop a steady hand and good timing.

6. GTAW Safety and Applications

GTAW safety is similar to other welding processes but with some specific hazards. The arc produces intense ultraviolet (UV) radiation that can burn your eyes and skin. Always wear a welding helmet with a shade 10-13 lens, depending on amperage. For low amps (under 50A), shade 10 is fine; for high amps (over 150A), use shade 12 or 13. Wear flame-resistant clothing, leather gloves, and a leather apron. Never weld with bare skin exposed. The UV can cause 'arc eye' (painful eye inflammation) and skin burns. Also, the high-frequency (HF) start on some TIG machines can interfere with pacemakers; consult a doctor if you have one. Shielding gas (argon) is an asphyxiant: it displaces oxygen. Weld in a well-ventilated area. If welding in a confined space, use forced ventilation and a gas monitor. Argon is heavier than air, so it can pool in low areas. In African workshops, many are open-air, which is good, but if you weld inside, open doors and windows. Never weld on containers that held fuel or chemicals without proper cleaning. GTAW is used for many applications: welding stainless steel in food and dairy industries (tanks, pipes), aluminum in automotive and aerospace, and thin-gauge metals in art and furniture. In Africa, common applications include repairing aluminum radiators, fabricating stainless steel kitchen equipment, and building bicycle frames. GTAW gives clean, precise welds, but it is slower than stick or MIG. It's ideal for thin materials (0.5mm to 6mm) and for metals that need high quality. For thicker steel, stick or MIG is more economical. To start, practice on mild steel. Then try stainless and aluminum. Always clean your metal before welding. Keep your work area tidy. Have a fire extinguisher nearby. Never weld near flammable materials. Use a welding screen to protect others from UV. Remember, safety is your responsibility. A safe welder is a productive welder.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: GTAW in African Metalwork Enterprises

Gas Tungsten Arc Welding (GTAW), commonly called TIG (Tungsten Inert Gas) welding, is the precision instrument of the welding trade. In African agribusiness and light manufacturing from stainless steel dairy tanks in Kenya to aluminium irrigation fittings in Senegal, from food-grade processing equipment in Nigeria to repair of milking machine components in South Africa GTAW delivers the clean, contamination-free welds that clients pay premium prices for. This Practical Reference lesson consolidates the essential knowledge from the six preceding lessons into a single job-floor companion. It is written for the welder-entrepreneur: the person who must not only strike an arc but also quote a job, control consumable costs, and deliver a weld that passes inspection the first time.

GTAW is slower than SMAW or GMAW, and its consumables (tungsten electrodes, shielding gas, filler rods) are more expensive per metre of weld. The business case for GTAW therefore rests on three pillars: (1) it welds materials that other processes cannot aluminium, stainless steel, titanium, magnesium; (2) it produces X-ray-quality welds on thin sections where other processes burn through; and (3) it commands higher labour rates because fewer welders master it. In the African context, where imported stainless steel and aluminium fabrications are common in agro-processing, GTAW competence is a direct route to higher-margin contracts.

SAFETY WARNING GTAW Hazards

GTAW emits intense ultraviolet (UV) radiation far stronger than SMAW or GMAW. Never weld without a proper welding helmet fitted with a minimum shade 1013 filter lens (shade 1214 for high-amperage work). UV exposure can cause arc eye (photokeratitis) within minutes and skin burns. In addition, GTAW produces ozone and nitrogen oxides ensure adequate ventilation or fume extraction, especially when welding stainless steel (hexavalent chromium risk) and aluminium. Argon is an asphyxiant: in confined spaces it displaces oxygen; never weld in a tank, pit, or sealed container without forced ventilation and a trained spotter. In African workshops where welding is often done in open-air bays, wind can also blow away shielding gas erect a simple windbreak, but never use a makeshift enclosure that traps argon. Always wear dry leather gloves, flame-resistant clothing, and safety boots. Check your helmet lens for cracks before every shift.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Material identification: Confirm the base metal grade (e.g. 304 vs 316 stainless, 6061 vs 5083 aluminium) before selecting filler rod and gas. If uncertain, perform a spark test or request the mill certificate.
  2. Tungsten preparation: Grind the tungsten longitudinally (not radially) on a dedicated grinding wheel. For DCEN, taper to a point; for AC, ball the tip slightly. Never use a wheel contaminated with other metals.
  3. Gas flow check: Set argon flow at 812 L/min (higher for aluminium). Purge the line for 35 seconds before striking the arc. Check for leaks at the torch body and regulator with soapy water.
  4. Torch angle and travel: Hold the torch 1015 from vertical, pushing forward. Keep the arc length short (13 mm) to maintain gas coverage and heat concentration.
  5. Amperage control: Use the foot pedal (or thumb control) to ramp up and down. For thin sheet, start at the low end and increase; for thick sections, preheat if necessary. Never exceed the electrode's current rating.
  6. Filler addition: Add filler rod to the leading edge of the weld pool, not into the arc. Withdraw the rod slightly after each dip to avoid contaminating the tungsten. Keep the hot end of the rod inside the gas shield.
  7. Post-weld inspection: Check for undercut, porosity, incomplete penetration, and discolouration on stainless (heat tint). Clean the weld with a stainless wire brush dedicated to stainless only.
  8. Cost tracking: Log gas cylinder changes, tungsten usage, and filler rod consumption per job. This data feeds directly into accurate quotations and profit margin analysis.

Business-First Notes for African Enterprises

GTAW equipment is a capital investment. A basic AC/DC inverter machine with torch, regulator, and foot pedal costs between USD 800 and USD 2,500 in most African markets, depending on brand and import route. To justify this, target customers who require certified welds: dairy processors (stainless steel tanks and piping), breweries (sanitary tubing), irrigation companies (aluminium and stainless fittings), and food packaging firms (hygienic fabrication). These sectors pay 3060% more per weld than general fabrication. Additionally, GTAW repair work on imported machinery where a replacement part may take weeks to arrive commands emergency rates. Keep a stock of common filler rods (ER308L, ER4043, ER70S-2) and at least two spare ceramic cups and collet bodies; downtime waiting for consumables is lost revenue.

Training is the other investment. A welder who can produce consistent, X-ray-quality GTAW welds is a business asset. Consider sending your best SMAW welder on a short GTAW course at a local technical college or polytechnic, or bring in a certified welding inspector for on-site training. The return on that investment is measured in premium contracts won and rework avoided.
