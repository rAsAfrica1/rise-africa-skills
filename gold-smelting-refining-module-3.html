1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Gold is a soft, dense, yellow metal that has been valued for thousands of years. In Africa, gold has been mined and traded for centuries, from the ancient kingdoms of Ghana and Mali to modern artisanal mining in Tanzania, Ghana, and South Africa. Understanding gold and its alloys is the foundation of smelting and refining. Pure gold, known as 24 karat (24K), is too soft for most jewellery and industrial uses. It is mixed with other metals to make alloys that are harder, change colour, or lower the melting point. For example, adding copper makes gold reddish and harder, while adding silver makes it greenish-yellow. Adding palladium or nickel makes it white. The purity of gold is measured in karats or fineness. 24K is 99.9% pure, 18K is 75% gold, 14K is 58.3%, and 10K is 41.7%. In Africa, most artisanal gold is smelted into dorÃ© bars, which are a mix of gold and silver, often 70-90% gold. Knowing the composition of your gold is critical because it affects how you smelt, refine, and sell it. If you smelt gold with too much copper, you may create a brittle bar that cracks. If you refine gold without removing silver, you cannot reach high purity. This module will teach you the properties of gold and common alloys, how to identify them, and why this knowledge matters for your business. You will learn to test gold, calculate alloy mixes, and avoid costly mistakes. Whether you are a small-scale miner, a jeweller, or a refiner, this knowledge will help you get the best price and produce quality products. In Africa, where gold is a major source of income, understanding alloys can mean the difference between profit and loss. Letâs get started.

2. Tools and Materials

To work with gold alloys, you need specific tools and materials. First, a digital scale accurate to 0.01 grams. This is essential for weighing gold and alloy metals precisely. A set of jewellerâs pliers, wire cutters, and a rolling mill if you plan to make sheets or wires. For melting, you need a crucible made of clay-graphite or silicon carbide. These can withstand high temperatures up to 1200Â°C. A torch or furnace that can reach at least 1100Â°C. For small-scale, a propane torch with a firebrick forge works. For larger, a gas furnace. You also need moulds to pour molten gold into bars or ingots. Steel or cast iron moulds are common; preheat them to avoid cracking. Flux is critical: borax or a commercial flux like 20 Mule Team Borax. Flux helps remove oxides and impurities. For testing, you need a touchstone (a black stone like slate), a set of test needles (gold alloys of known karat), and nitric acid for acid testing. Alternatively, a gold testing kit with electronic tester. Safety gear: safety glasses, heat-resistant gloves, apron, and a respirator for fumes. For alloying, you need pure metals: copper, silver, zinc, palladium, or nickel. These should be in small granules or sheets for easy weighing. A calculator for alloy math. A notebook to record your mixes and results. In Africa, you can find many of these tools locally: crucibles from pottery suppliers, borax from supermarkets, and scales from mobile phone accessory shops. For acid testing, you may need to order online or from jewellery supply stores in cities like Nairobi, Accra, or Johannesburg. Always buy from reputable sources to ensure quality. Remember, cheap tools can fail at high temperatures and cause accidents. Invest in good quality crucibles and torches. Also, have a fire extinguisher and a bucket of sand nearby. A first aid kit is a must. Keep your workspace clean and organised. Label all metals and chemicals clearly. Never store nitric acid near other chemicals. With the right tools, you can safely and effectively work with gold alloys.

3. Step by Step Method

Step 1: Determine the gold content of your starting material. If you have dorÃ© bars or scrap jewellery, test it. Use a touchstone and acid test or an electronic tester. Record the karat or percentage. For example, if you have 10 grams of 18K gold, it contains 7.5 grams of pure gold (10 x 0.75). Step 2: Decide the alloy you want to make. Common alloys: 22K (91.7% gold, 8.3% copper/silver), 18K (75% gold, 12.5% copper, 12.5% silver), 14K (58.3% gold, 41.7% copper/silver), and 9K (37.5% gold, 62.5% copper/silver). For rose gold, use more copper; for green gold, more silver. Step 3: Calculate the amount of alloy metals needed. Use the formula: (Target karat / 24) x total weight = weight of pure gold. Then subtract pure gold from total weight to get alloy weight. For example, to make 20 grams of 18K from pure gold: pure gold needed = (18/24) x 20 = 15 grams. Alloy metals = 5 grams. Split alloy as desired (e.g., 2.5g copper, 2.5g silver). Step 4: Melt the pure gold first in a preheated crucible. Use a torch or furnace. When molten, add the alloy metals. Stir gently with a graphite rod. Add a pinch of borax to clean. Step 5: Pour into a preheated mould. Let it cool slowly. Quench in water after it solidifies. Step 6: Test the final alloy. Use acid test or electronic tester to confirm karat. If off, remelt and adjust. Step 7: Clean the bar. Pickle in a warm acid solution (e.g., 10% sulfuric acid) to remove borax and oxides. Rinse and dry. Record your results. This method works for any alloy. Always work in a well-ventilated area. Practice with small amounts first. In Africa, many smelters use this method with local materials. For example, in Ghana, smelters often use charcoal furnaces and clay crucibles. The key is precision in weighing and temperature control. Too hot and you lose gold as fumes; too cold and the alloy won't mix. Aim for 1100-1200Â°C for gold alloys. Use a pyrometer if available. If not, watch for the gold to become liquid and shiny like water. With practice, you will master the steps.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Not testing the purity of your starting gold. If you assume your gold is pure when it's not, your alloy will be off. Fix: Always test with acid or electronic tester before alloying. Mistake 2: Using dirty or oxidised metals. Copper and silver can have oxides that affect the alloy. Fix: Clean metals with sandpaper or pickle before use. Mistake 3: Adding alloy metals to molten gold too quickly. This can cause splattering or incomplete mixing. Fix: Preheat alloy metals and add slowly. Mistake 4: Overheating the melt. Gold boils at 2856Â°C, but alloy metals like zinc boil at 907Â°C and can fume off, changing the alloy. Fix: Keep temperature just above melting point (around 1100Â°C). Use a reducing flame to prevent oxidation. Mistake 5: Not using flux. Without flux, oxides and impurities remain, making the alloy brittle. Fix: Add borax or commercial flux before pouring. Mistake 6: Pouring into a cold mould. This causes the gold to cool too fast and crack. Fix: Preheat mould to 200-300Â°C. Mistake 7: Not stirring the melt. This leads to uneven alloy. Fix: Stir gently with a graphite rod for 10-15 seconds. Mistake 8: Quenching too soon. If you quench before the bar solidifies, it can warp. Fix: Wait until the bar loses its red glow, then quench. Mistake 9: Using the wrong acid for testing. Nitric acid alone won't test gold above 14K; you need aqua regia or a testing kit. Fix: Use proper acid test solutions. Mistake 10: Ignoring safety. Fumes from alloying can be toxic (e.g., zinc fumes). Fix: Always wear a respirator and work outdoors or with ventilation. In Africa, many smelters face these mistakes due to lack of training. For example, in Tanzania, some smelters add copper without flux, resulting in brittle bars that break when dropped. By avoiding these mistakes, you save time, money, and gold. Always keep a notebook and learn from each melt. If something goes wrong, remelt and adjust. Practice makes perfect.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety is non-negotiable when working with gold and alloys. First, protect your eyes. Wear safety glasses or a face shield. Molten gold can splatter. Second, wear heat-resistant gloves and a leather apron. Never wear synthetic clothing; it melts and sticks to skin. Third, work in a well-ventilated area. Fumes from flux, zinc, and other metals are toxic. Use a fume hood or work outdoors. If indoors, use a fan to blow fumes away from you. Fourth, keep a fire extinguisher and a bucket of sand nearby. Never use water on a metal fire. Fifth, store acids safely. Nitric acid and sulfuric acid are corrosive. Keep them in labelled, tightly closed containers away from metals and bases. Sixth, never eat or drink in the workspace. Wash hands thoroughly after handling metals and chemicals. Seventh, use a respirator when melting alloys with zinc or nickel. These metals produce harmful fumes. Eighth, preheat crucibles and moulds gradually to avoid thermal shock. Ninth, never leave a torch or furnace unattended. Tenth, have a first aid kit and know how to treat burns. For chemical burns, rinse with plenty of water. For thermal burns, cool with water and cover. Best practices: Always weigh and record everything. Use clean tools. Keep your workspace tidy. Label all alloys and chemicals. Test your gold before and after alloying. Calibrate your scale regularly. Replace worn crucibles. Use a pyrometer to monitor temperature. Train others before letting them work. In Africa, many accidents happen due to lack of safety gear and training. For example, in artisanal mining areas, smelters often work without gloves or glasses. This leads to burns and eye injuries. By following these safety rules, you protect yourself and your business. Remember, a safe smelter is a productive smelter. Also, dispose of waste properly. Pickle solutions contain acids and metals; neutralise with baking soda before disposal. Never pour down the drain. Follow local regulations. Your health and the environment matter. Make safety a habit, not an afterthought.

6. Practical Exercise

In this exercise, you will make a small 18K gold alloy from pure gold. You will need: 5 grams of pure gold (24K), 1.25 grams of copper, 1.25 grams of silver, a crucible, a torch, borax, a mould, a scale, and safety gear. Step 1: Weigh the pure gold and alloy metals accurately. Use a 0.01g scale. Step 2: Preheat your crucible and mould. Step 3: Melt the pure gold in the crucible using a torch. It will turn bright orange and shiny. Step 4: Add the copper and silver. Stir gently with a graphite rod. Step 5: Add a pinch of borax. Step 6: Pour into the preheated mould. Step 7: Let it cool until it loses its red glow, then quench in water. Step 8: Weigh the final bar. It should weigh 7.5 grams (5g gold + 2.5g alloy). If not, you lost some metal. Step 9: Test the karat. Use an acid test or electronic tester. It should be 18K (75% gold). Step 10: Record your results: weights, temperature, observations. If the test shows lower than 18K, you may have lost gold or added too much alloy. Remelt and adjust. For example, if you got 17K, add a little more pure gold. If you got 19K, add a little more alloy. Repeat until you get 18K. This exercise teaches precision and patience. In Africa, you can do this with locally available materials. For example, in Ghana, you can buy pure gold from licensed dealers and copper from scrap wires. Silver can be obtained from old cutlery. Borax is sold in supermarkets as a cleaning agent. Always work in a well-ventilated area. After the exercise, clean your tools and store your alloy safely. This 18K gold can be used for jewellery or sold. Remember, practice makes perfect. Try different alloys: 22K, 14K, and 9K. Adjust the copper and silver ratios to see colour changes. For rose gold, use 4 parts copper to 1 part silver. For green gold, use 3 parts silver to 1 part copper. Document everything. This will build your skills and confidence. Congratulations, you have completed Module 3.

Practical Reference: Understanding Gold and Gold Alloys

This practical reference consolidates the core knowledge you need to handle, test, and value gold and gold alloys in an African gold-processing business. Whether you are buying scrap from artisanal miners in Obuasi, refining dorÃ© bars in Nairobi, or selling finished jewellery in Johannesburg, your margin depends on understanding what you are actually holding. Gold is rarely pure in the field. It arrives as dorÃ© (an alloy of gold and silver, often with traces of copper and iron), as scrap jewellery (alloyed with copper, silver, zinc, or nickel), or as recycled electronics (alloyed with palladium, copper, and silver). Knowing the alloy composition tells you the true gold content, the refining cost, the recovery yield, and the price you can safely offer.

This lesson is a working reference. Keep it beside your scale, your assay kit, and your furnace. It covers the tools and materials, the step-by-step method for identifying and testing alloys, common mistakes and their fixes, safety and best practice, and a practical exercise you can run with your team. It is written for the African context: limited formal assay labs, variable power supply, informal supply chains, and the need to move fast without losing money.

Why Alloy Knowledge Drives Profit

Gold is traded on purity. The London Bullion Market Association (LBMA) defines "good delivery" gold as a minimum of 995.0 parts per thousand (99.5% pure) for large bars. In African markets, however, most transactions happen at lower purities: 22K (91.6%), 18K (75.0%), 14K (58.5%), and 9K (37.5%). A kilogram of 18K scrap contains only 750 grams of gold. If you buy it at the price of pure gold, you lose 25% on the spot. That is the single most common way small and medium African gold businesses go bankrupt.

The alloy also determines your refining route. High-silver dorÃ© (gold-silver alloys above roughly 20% silver) responds well to aqua regia or the Miller process. High-copper alloys need more acid and longer reaction times. Alloys containing nickel or palladium require different chemistry and can contaminate your electrolyte if you use electrorefining. Understanding the alloy before you commit capital is not academic â it is the difference between a profitable lot and a loss.

Step-by-Step Method: Identifying and Testing Gold Alloys

Follow this sequence every time you receive a new lot. It takes 10â20 minutes and prevents expensive mistakes.

  1. Visual inspection. Look for hallmarks (375, 585, 750, 916, 999), colour, and wear patterns. Plated items show a different colour at worn edges.
  2. Magnet test. A strong magnet should not attract gold or its common alloys. If it sticks, you have iron or steel contamination.
  3. Weigh the item. Record the weight in grams to two decimal places. This is your baseline for valuation.
  4. Touchstone streak test. Rub the item on the black slate. Apply the corresponding karat acid. If the streak dissolves, the karat is lower than the acid. If it remains, the karat is equal or higher.
  5. Acid test (aqua regia or karat acid). For dorÃ© or unknown alloys, place a small filing in a test tube, add aqua regia, and observe. Gold dissolves to a yellow solution. Add a drop of stannous chloride: a purple colour confirms gold.
  6. XRF confirmation. If available, scan the item. The XRF gives you the exact percentage of gold, silver, copper, zinc, and any other metals. This is the gold standard for valuation.
  7. Calculate gold content. Multiply the weight by the purity percentage. Example: 100 g of 18K scrap = 100 Ã 0.750 = 75 g of pure gold.
  8. Value the lot. Multiply the pure gold content by the current spot price (less refining cost and your margin). Always quote in local currency and USD to avoid exchange-rate surprises.

For dorÃ© bars from artisanal miners, the composition can vary widely. A typical East African dorÃ© might be 70â85% gold, 10â25% silver, and 1â5% copper. A West African dorÃ© from a different geological setting might be 85â92% gold with higher silver. Never assume. Test every batch.

Aqua regia (a mixture of hydrochloric and nitric acid) and karat testing acids are highly corrosive. They produce toxic fumes (nitrogen oxides, chlorine) that can cause severe lung damage. In many African workshops, ventilation is poor and power for fume hoods is unreliable. Never test gold in a closed room, near food, or without personal protective equipment.

  • Always wear nitrile gloves, chemical splash goggles, and a respirator with acid-gas cartridges.
  • Work outdoors or in a well-ventilated area with a fan pulling fumes away from you.
  • Never add water to acid â always add acid to water, slowly.
  • Keep a bucket of clean water and a box of sodium bicarbonate nearby to neutralise spills.
  • Store acids in labelled, tightly closed HDPE containers, away from children and heat.
  • If acid contacts skin, flush with plenty of water for 15 minutes and seek medical help.

Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Buying on visual appearance alone. Plated brass and gold-filled items look like gold. Fix: always test with acid or XRF before paying.

Mistake 2: Assuming all dorÃ© is 90%+ gold. Some artisanal dorÃ© is 70% or lower. Fix: assay every batch; use a small fire assay or XRF.

Mistake 3: Confusing karat with fineness. 18K is 75% gold, not 18/24 by weight of the whole item if other metals are present in solder. Fix: test the whole item, not just the main body.

Mistake 4: Ignoring refining cost. A 9K lot may contain only 37.5% gold, and refining cost per gram can exceed the margin. Fix: calculate net recoverable value before buying.

Mistake 5: Using dirty or expired test acids. Old acid gives false results. Fix: date your acids and replace every 6â12 months.

Mistake 6: Not calibrating the scale. A 0.1 g error on a 100 g lot is 0.1% â small, but on a 5 kg lot it is 5 g of gold. Fix: calibrate weekly with a certified weight.

Safety and Best Practice

Safety is not optional. In African gold businesses, the most common injuries are acid burns, fume inhalation, and eye damage. Best practice includes: written standard operating procedures (SOPs) for every test; a first-aid kit with eyewash; fire extinguisher (dry chemical); no eating, drinking, or smoking in the testing area; and training for every new staff member before they touch acid. Keep a log of every test: date, item, weight, result, and the name of the tester. This protects you legally and commercially.
