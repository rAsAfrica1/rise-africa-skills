1. Making Corn and Flour Tortillas from Scratch

To make 12 corn tortillas, you need 250g of fine maize flour (like Maseca or locally milled white maize), 300ml warm water, and 1/2 teaspoon salt. In a large bowl, mix the flour and salt. Add water gradually, stirring with your hand until a soft dough forms. It should be like playdoughânot sticky, not crumbly. If too dry, add water a tablespoon at a time; if too wet, add flour. Knead for 2 minutes. Cover with a damp cloth and rest for 15 minutes. This hydrates the flour and makes rolling easier.

For flour tortillas, use 300g all-purpose flour, 1 teaspoon baking powder, 1/2 teaspoon salt, 50g lard or vegetable oil, and 180ml warm water. Mix dry ingredients, rub in the fat until it looks like breadcrumbs, then add water. Knead for 5 minutes until smooth. Rest for 30 minutes. Divide into 12 balls.

To press corn tortillas, use a tortilla press lined with plastic. Place a ball between the plastic sheets and press firmly. If you don't have a press, use a heavy pot or rolling pin between two plates. For flour tortillas, roll each ball into a 15cm circle on a floured surface.

Heat a dry cast-iron pan or heavy skillet over medium-high heat. Cook corn tortillas for 30 seconds per sideâthey should puff slightly and have brown spots. Flour tortillas need about 45 seconds per side until golden. Stack cooked tortillas in a clean cloth to keep warm and soft. In Africa, you can use a flat jiko or charcoal stove with a flat metal sheet. Practice until you can make 12 tortillas in 10 minutes. Fresh tortillas cost a fraction of store-bought and taste far better.

2. Preparing Traditional Taco Fillings: Carnitas, Barbacoa, and Pollo

For carnitas (slow-cooked pork), use 1kg pork shoulder cut into 5cm chunks. In a heavy pot, combine pork with 2 cups water, 1 sliced onion, 4 garlic cloves, 1 tablespoon salt, 1 teaspoon cumin, and 1 orange cut in half (squeeze juice and add the halves). Bring to a boil, then simmer uncovered for 1.5 hours until liquid evaporates and pork fries in its own fat. Turn pieces until browned. Shred with two forks. In Africa, use a charcoal stove or gas cookerâkeep the heat low. This serves 12 tacos.

For barbacoa (beef), use 1kg beef chuck or brisket. Rub with 2 tablespoons chipotle paste (or 2 dried chipotle soaked and blended), 1 tablespoon vinegar, 1 tablespoon oregano, 4 garlic cloves minced, and 1 tablespoon salt. Wrap tightly in banana leaves or foil. Bake at 160Â°C for 3 hours, or simmer in a covered pot with 1 cup water for 2.5 hours until shreddable. Shred and mix with 2 tablespoons of the cooking liquid. Banana leaves add authentic flavour and are common in West and Central Africa.

For pollo (chicken), use 1kg boneless chicken thighs. Marinate in 2 tablespoons achiote paste (or 1 tablespoon paprika + 1 teaspoon turmeric), 2 tablespoons lime juice, 3 garlic cloves, 1 teaspoon salt, and 1 teaspoon black pepper for 30 minutes. Grill or pan-fry over medium heat for 6â8 minutes per side until cooked through. Rest 5 minutes, then slice into strips. For a smoky flavour, grill over charcoal.

All fillings can be made ahead and kept warm in a cooler or insulated container. Portion into 100g servings per taco. Always taste and adjust salt. In Africa, use local spices like suya pepper or berbere for a regional twist.

3. Salsas and Toppings: Pico de Gallo, Guacamole, and Salsa Verde

Pico de Gallo: Dice 4 ripe tomatoes, 1 small red onion, and 2 fresh jalapeÃ±os (remove seeds for less heat). Chop a large bunch of fresh coriander (about 30g). Mix with juice of 2 limes and 1 teaspoon salt. Let sit 15 minutes for flavours to blend. Makes about 2 cups. In Africa, use local tomatoes and onionsâthey are often sweeter and more flavourful. If jalapeÃ±os are unavailable, use 1 teaspoon of chilli powder or 2 small local hot peppers, minced.

Guacamole: Mash 3 ripe avocados with a fork. Add juice of 1 lime, 1/2 teaspoon salt, 1 small tomato diced, 1/4 red onion finely chopped, and 1 tablespoon chopped coriander. Mix gentlyâleave some chunks. To prevent browning, press plastic wrap directly onto the surface and refrigerate. Best made fresh. If avocados are expensive, mix with 1/4 cup mashed cooked green plantain for volumeâstill creamy and delicious.

Salsa Verde: Boil 500g tomatillos (or green tomatoes) with 2 jalapeÃ±os and 1/2 onion for 10 minutes. Drain, then blend with 1 garlic clove, 1/2 cup coriander, 1 teaspoon salt, and juice of 1 lime. For a roasted version, char the vegetables on a dry pan before blending. Makes 2 cups. In Africa, green tomatoes are common and cheap. You can also use unripe mango for a tangy twist.

Toppings: Shredded cabbage, sliced radishes, crumbled queso fresco (or feta), pickled red onions (slice onion, cover with lime juice and salt, rest 30 minutes), and crema (mix 1 cup sour cream with 2 tablespoons lime juice and 1/2 teaspoon salt). Always keep cold toppings in a cooler with ice packs. For takeaway, pack salsas in small 50ml containers. Label with heat levels: mild, medium, hot.

4. Assembling and Wrapping Tacos for Take Away

Warm your tortillas on a dry pan for 20 seconds per sideâthis makes them pliable and prevents tearing. Hold a tortilla in your non-dominant hand. Place 100g of hot filling (carnitas, barbacoa, or pollo) down the centre, leaving 2cm at each end. Add 1 tablespoon of salsa or guacamole, then 1 tablespoon of cold toppings like cabbage and radish. Do not overfillâtoo much filling makes wrapping impossible. For takeaway, use two tortillas stacked for strength, especially for saucy fillings.

To wrap for takeaway, fold one side of the tortilla over the filling, then fold the opposite side. Finally, roll from the bottom up, tucking the ends in as you go. This creates a sealed packet. For a more secure wrap, use aluminium foil: place the taco on a 30cm square of foil, fold the foil over the taco, then roll tightly, twisting the ends. This keeps the taco warm and prevents leaks. Alternatively, use wax paper or banana leaves for an eco-friendly African touch.

For multiple tacos, assemble them on a clean surface and wrap each individually. Do not stack unwrapped tacosâthey will stick and tear. If you are selling from a stall, pre-wrap 10 tacos at a time and keep them in an insulated cooler or hot box at 60Â°C. For delivery, pack wrapped tacos in a cardboard box with a paper towel to absorb moisture. Include a small bag with extra salsa and lime wedges.

Practice your speed: a skilled assembler can wrap 20 tacos in 5 minutes. Set up a production line: one person warms tortillas, one fills, one wraps. In Africa, use locally available materials like clean newspaper (food-safe) or reused plastic containers. Always wash hands and wear gloves. Keep hot fillings above 60Â°C and cold toppings below 5Â°C.

5. Taco Presentation and Packaging

Presentation sells tacos. For a street food stall, use a clean, colourful tableclothâbright African prints work well. Arrange tacos on a wooden board or a woven basket lined with banana leaves. Garnish with lime wedges, fresh coriander sprigs, and sliced radishes. Use small bowls for salsas with serving spoons. A chalkboard menu with prices in local currency and simple drawings of tacos attracts customers. Keep the stall tidy and wipe spills immediately.

Packaging must keep tacos hot, fresh, and easy to eat. For individual takeaway, wrap each taco in aluminium foil, then place in a paper bag. For a premium look, use a cardboard taco holder (a simple folded card) that keeps the taco upright. In Africa, reusable plastic containers with lids are commonâthey are cheap and stackable. Add a sticker with your brand name and phone number. For events, use compostable plates made from palm leaves or bagasseâthese are available in many African cities.

Label your packaging with the filling type (e.g., 'Carnitas', 'Pollo') and a 'best before' time (2 hours after assembly). Include a small packet of extra salsa and a napkin. For delivery, use an insulated bag. Charge a small deposit for reusable containers to encourage returns. For catering, present tacos on a large platter with separate bowls of toppings so guests can assemble their ownâthis reduces labour and looks generous.

Keep costs low: buy foil, paper bags, and containers in bulk from local suppliers. A typical taco costs 30â50% less to package than a burger. Train staff to wrap neatly and quickly. A well-presented taco can sell for 2â3 times the cost of ingredients. Always ask for feedback and adjust your packaging to suit local preferences.

6. Scaling Up Taco Production for Events

To serve 100 tacos at an event, you need: 2kg of filling (carnitas, barbacoa, or pollo), 200 tortillas (make 100 corn and 100 flour for variety), 4 cups of salsa, 4 cups of guacamole, 2 cups of pickled onions, and 2 cups of shredded cabbage. Prep all ingredients the day before. Cook fillings in large batchesâuse a 20-litre pot on a gas burner. Keep hot in insulated coolers (pre-heated with hot water) at 60Â°C. Cold items in coolers with ice packs.

Set up a production line with 4 stations: 1) Tortilla warming (one person using two pans), 2) Filling (one person portioning 100g), 3) Toppings (one person adding salsa and cold items), 4) Wrapping and packing (two people). This line can produce 100 tacos in 30 minutes. Use colour-coded cutting boards to prevent cross-contamination. Wear gloves and change them every 30 minutes. Assign a runner to restock ingredients.

For large events, pre-wrap tacos in foil and keep them in a hot box (a thick polystyrene box with a hot water bottle wrapped in a towel). Do not hold hot tacos for more than 2 hours. For a festival, offer a 'taco combo': 2 tacos, a drink, and a small salsa for a set price. Use a cash register or mobile money (M-Pesa, MTN Mobile Money) for speed. Track sales with a simple tally sheet.

Scaling requires practice. Do a dry run for 20 tacos and time each step. Adjust quantities: if you sell out of one filling, you lose sales. Always prepare 10% extra. Train a team of 3â5 people. Pay them fairly and feed them. After the event, review what sold best and what was left over. In Africa, events like weddings, church gatherings, and music festivals are great opportunities. Start small, then grow.
