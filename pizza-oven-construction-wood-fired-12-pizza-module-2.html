1. Choosing Between Brick, Castable Refractory, and Kit Ovens

When building a wood-fired oven for 12-inch pizzas, you have three main options: traditional brick, castable refractory, or a pre-made kit. Each has trade-offs in cost, skill, and performance. In Africa, where materials and labour vary, your choice depends on local availability and your budget.

Brick ovens use firebricks (refractory bricks) held together with refractory mortar. They are durable, repairable, and give authentic results. A 12-inch pizza needs a floor about 24 inches in diameter, so you'll need roughly 60-80 firebricks for the dome and floor. Firebricks cost $1-$3 each in most African cities, but in rural areas, you may pay double. You'll also need a strong stand and insulation. Brick ovens take 2-3 days to build and require some masonry skill.

Castable refractory is a pre-mixed concrete-like material that you shape over a sand mould. It's faster to build and creates a seamless dome that heats evenly. A 50 kg bag costs $30-$60 and is enough for a 24-inch oven. However, castable is heavy, prone to cracking if not cured slowly, and hard to repair. It's best if you have limited bricklaying experience but can follow mixing instructions precisely.

Kit ovens come as pre-cast sections or metal boxes with refractory linings. They are the easiest and fastest to installâoften in a dayâbut cost $200-$800, plus shipping. In Africa, import duties can double the price. Kits are ideal if you need a portable or commercial oven and have the budget. For a 12-inch pizza, a kit ensures consistent size and insulation.

Consider your long-term goals. If you plan to sell pizzas, a brick oven is economical and can be repaired. If you need to start quickly, a kit saves time. Castable is a middle ground. Check local suppliers: in Kenya, firebricks are common in Nairobi; in Nigeria, castable is available in Lagos. Always prioritize insulation, no matter which type you choose.

2. Calculating Dome Height and Floor Area for 12-Inch Pizzas

A 12-inch pizza needs a floor area that allows easy rotation and even baking. The oven floor should be at least 24 inches in diameterâthat gives 2 inches of clearance around the pizza. For a dome oven, the internal floor is circular, so a 24-inch diameter gives a radius of 12 inches. The dome height should be about 60-70% of the floor diameter. For a 24-inch floor, aim for a dome height of 14-16 inches. This height creates the right convection current: hot air rolls over the dome and down onto the pizza, cooking the top quickly.

To calculate floor area, use the formula for a circle: Area = Ï Ã rÂ². For a 24-inch diameter, radius = 12 inches, so area = 3.14 Ã 144 = 452 square inches. That's enough for one 12-inch pizza at a time, with room to turn it. If you want to bake two pizzas simultaneously, you need a floor at least 36 inches in diameter (radius 18 inches, area 1017 square inches). But for a 12-inch pizza business, a 24-inch oven is efficient and uses less fuel.

The dome shape matters. A perfect hemisphere (height = radius) is too tall; heat escapes. A low dome (height = 50% of radius) causes the fire to choke. The ideal is a segment of a sphere where the height is about 1/3 of the diameter. For a 24-inch oven, height = 8 inches? No, that's too low. Actually, for a 24-inch diameter, a common height is 12-14 inches. Let's be precise: many builders use a height of 60% of the diameter, so 24 Ã 0.6 = 14.4 inches. That's a good target.

You'll need to mark the floor. Use a tape measure and chalk. Draw a circle on your insulating base. For the dome, create a wooden template: a half-circle with radius equal to your dome height (e.g., 14 inches) and a base width equal to your floor diameter (24 inches). This template guides brick placement. Check the height with a straight edge and level. Too high and the oven won't heat the floor; too low and the fire starves. Test with a small fire before finishing.

In Africa, where fuel can be expensive, a properly sized oven saves wood. A 24-inch oven uses about 2-3 kg of wood to reach 450Â°C, while a larger one uses more. So stick to the 24-inch floor for 12-inch pizzas. Always measure twice and cut once.

3. Selecting Refractory Bricks and Mortars

Refractory bricks, also called firebricks, are made from clay and other materials that withstand high heat without cracking. For a pizza oven, you need low-duty firebricks that can handle 1200Â°C. In Africa, common brands include Kyanite, Mullite, and local clay bricks from companies like Corobrik in South Africa or Tanzania's Kigali Bricks. Look for bricks with a density of 1.8-2.2 g/cmÂ³ and a rating of at least 1200Â°C. Avoid ordinary red clay bricksâthey spall and crack at pizza temperatures.

When buying, inspect each brick. It should be uniform, free of large cracks, and ring slightly when tapped. If you can, buy from a masonry supplier, not a general hardware store. In Nairobi, check Kenbro Industries; in Lagos, try Cappa & D'Alberto. A 12-inch pizza oven needs about 60-80 firebricks: 40-50 for the dome, 20-30 for the floor. The floor bricks should be laid flat, tight together, and level. You can use a mix of full and cut bricks for the dome.

Mortar is critical. Use refractory mortar (fireclay mortar) that matches the brick's heat rating. A 25 kg bag costs $15-$30 and covers about 50 bricks. Mix with water to a peanut butter consistency. Do not use ordinary cement mortarâit will crumble. For the dome, you can also use a castable refractory as mortar, but it's more expensive. Some builders use a mix of fireclay and sand (1:1) with a little cement, but this is weaker.

In rural areas, you might find local clay that works. Test it: make a small brick, fire it, and see if it cracks. If it survives, mix it with sand and use as mortar. But for reliability, buy commercial refractory mortar. Always wear gloves and a mask when mixingârefractory materials contain silica.

After laying, cure the oven slowly. Light a small fire and gradually increase over 3-5 days. This drives out moisture and prevents cracks. A well-built brick oven with good mortar lasts 20+ years. So invest in quality materials. If you can't find firebricks, consider castable refractory instead, but bricks are repairable and traditional.

4. Insulation Materials: Ceramic Fiber, Perlite, and Vermiculite

Insulation keeps heat in the oven and makes it safe to touch. Without it, you waste wood and risk burns. For a 12-inch pizza oven, you need insulation under the floor, around the dome, and possibly over the top. Three common materials are ceramic fiber, perlite, and vermiculite. Each has pros and cons for African builders.

Ceramic fiber blanket is the best insulator. It comes in rolls, 1-2 inches thick, and handles 1260Â°C. A 1-inch thick, 25-foot roll costs $30-$50. You wrap it around the dome and place a layer under the floor. It's lightweight and easy to cut with a knife. However, it can irritate skin and lungs, so wear gloves and a mask. In Africa, it's available in industrial areasâcheck suppliers in Johannesburg, Nairobi, or Lagos. If you can't find it, perlite or vermiculite are alternatives.

Perlite and vermiculite are lightweight, expanded minerals that you mix with cement or clay to form an insulating concrete. Perlite is cheaper and more common in agriculture. A 100-liter bag costs $10-$20. Mix 5 parts perlite to 1 part cement (by volume) with water to make a slurry. Pour it under the floor and over the dome. This mix has an insulation value of about R-0.5 per inch, so you need 3-4 inches for good results. Vermiculite is similar but slightly more expensive and absorbs more water. Both are easy to find in garden centers or building suppliers.

For the floor, lay a 2-inch layer of perlite concrete on your stand, then a layer of firebricks on top. For the dome, build a wooden or wire mesh form, pack the perlite concrete around it, and let it cure. You can also use loose perlite or vermiculite in a cavity, but it settles over time. A common African method is to fill the space between the dome and an outer shell with loose perlite. That works well if the outer shell is sealed.

Always add a final layer of cement render or a metal roof to protect insulation from rain. In humid areas, perlite can absorb moisture and lose efficiency. So keep it dry. A well-insulated 24-inch oven stays hot for 4-6 hours and uses 30% less wood. That saves money and time.

6. Reading and Interpreting Oven Plans

Oven plans are drawings that show dimensions, materials, and assembly steps. For a 12-inch pizza oven, you'll find free plans online, but you must read them correctly. Start with the title block: it gives the scale (e.g., 1:10) and units (inches or mm). Always measure the drawing with a ruler and multiply by the scale to get real sizes. For example, if a line is 2.4 inches on a 1:10 plan, the real length is 24 inches.

Next, look at the three views: front, side, and top. The front view shows the opening and dome height. The side view shows the depth and insulation layers. The top view shows the floor diameter and chimney position. Cross-sections (cutaway views) reveal internal details like the firebrick floor, perlite layer, and dome thickness. Learn the symbols: a zigzag line means insulation, a dotted line means hidden edge, and a triangle with a number means a material callout (e.g., 1 = firebrick).

Dimensions are critical. Check the floor diameter: it should be at least 24 inches. The opening height should be about 63% of the dome heightâfor a 14-inch dome, the opening is 8-9 inches. The chimney should be at the front of the dome, above the opening, with a 4-inch diameter. If the plan shows a different size, scale it. For a 12-inch pizza, you can adapt a 24-inch plan; don't use a 36-inch plan because it wastes fuel.

Read the notes section. It lists mortar type, curing schedule, and safety warnings. For example, "Cure slowly over 5 days" or "Use refractory mortar only." If a plan is in metric, convert: 1 inch = 25.4 mm. If you don't understand a symbol, look it up online or ask a builder. Many plans come with a bill of materialsâcompare it to your own list.

Finally, make a full-size template on cardboard or plywood. Cut out the dome profile and floor circle. Place it on your base to check fit. This catches errors before you cut bricks. In Africa, where materials are costly, this step saves money. Also, take photos of the plan on your phone for reference. If you can't read plans, watch YouTube tutorials that walk through them. Practice with a simple plan first. With patience, you'll master it.
