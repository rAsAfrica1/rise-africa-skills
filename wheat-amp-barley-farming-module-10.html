Lessons

1. Finding Buyers: Millers, Breweries, and Feed Companies

Your wheat and barley harvest is valuable, but only if you can sell it. Start by identifying the big buyers in your area. Millers turn wheat into flour for bread and pasta. Breweries need barley for beer. Feed companies buy both grains for animal feed. In Kenya, major millers include Unga Group, Pembe Flour Mills, and Mombasa Maize Millers. Breweries like East African Breweries Limited (EABL) buy barley. Feed companies like Unga Farm Care and Sigma Feeds also purchase grains.

To find these buyers, ask your local agricultural extension officer. Visit the nearest market and ask traders who they sell to. Use your mobile phone to search online for 'wheat millers in [your country]' or 'barley buyers near me'. Many companies have websites with contact details. You can also attend agricultural trade shows or farmer field days where buyers look for suppliers.

When you contact a buyer, be ready to answer questions: How many tonnes do you have? What is the moisture content? Is it clean? What variety? For wheat, millers prefer hard red or soft white with moisture below 13%. Barley for brewing should have low protein (9-12%) and specific germination rates. Feed companies are less picky but still want clean, dry grain.

Start small. If you have 5 tonnes, a small miller or feed company might buy directly. For larger volumes, you may need to join a farmer cooperative that aggregates grain to meet buyer demand. Cooperatives like the Kenya Farmers Association or local cereal growers associations can connect you to bigger markets. Always get a written agreement or at least a receipt when you deliver. Building a relationship with one reliable buyer is better than chasing many unreliable ones.

2. Negotiating Prices and Contracts

Negotiating is not about fighting; it is about finding a price that works for both you and the buyer. Before you talk to any buyer, know your cost of production. If it cost you KES 30,000 to produce one tonne of wheat, you cannot sell below that and stay in business. Add a profit marginat least 20%. So your minimum price should be KES 36,000 per tonne. Check current market prices: in Kenya, wheat prices range from KES 35,000 to KES 45,000 per tonne, while barley for brewing can fetch KES 40,000 to KES 50,000 per tonne. Use these as your guide.

When negotiating, start with a price slightly above your target. If you want KES 40,000, ask for KES 43,000. Be ready to explain why your grain is worth it: high quality, low moisture, cleaned, and delivered on time. Listen to the buyer's offer and counter politely. Never accept the first offer without discussing. If the buyer says they can only pay KES 38,000, ask if they can meet you at KES 40,000 or if they can buy a larger volume at a lower price. Volume discounts are common.

Contracts are important for large sales. A simple contract should include: quantity (e.g., 10 tonnes), price per tonne, quality specifications (moisture, foreign matter), delivery date, payment terms (cash on delivery or within 30 days), and penalties for late delivery or poor quality. Get it in writing, signed by both parties. If you cannot read English, ask a trusted person to explain it. Keep a copy.

For small sales at local markets, contracts may not be practical, but always get a receipt. For brewery contracts, you may need to meet specific standards and may be paid after testing. Be patient but follow up on payments. If a buyer delays payment, call them politely and remind them of the agreed terms. Good negotiation and clear contracts protect your profit and build trust.

3. Selling at Local Markets vs. Bulk Buyers

You have two main options for selling your wheat and barley: local markets or bulk buyers. Each has advantages and disadvantages. Local markets include village markets, town markets, and small traders. You can sell in small quantities, often for cash immediately. Prices may be higher per kilogram because consumers buy in small amounts. For example, a tin of wheat (about 2 kg) might sell for KES 100, which is KES 50 per kg, while bulk buyers pay KES 40 per kg. But selling at local markets takes time and effort. You have to transport your grain to the market, pay market fees, and spend the day selling. You might only sell 50 kg per day. If you have 2 tonnes, that is 40 days of selling.

Bulk buyers, such as millers, breweries, and feed companies, buy large quantities at once. They pay less per kg but you sell everything in one day. You save time and transport costs. For example, a miller might buy 10 tonnes at KES 40,000 per tonne (KES 40 per kg). You get KES 400,000 in one transaction. However, bulk buyers often require minimum quantities, like 5 tonnes or more. They may also have strict quality standards and may delay payment by 30 days or more.

Which is better? It depends on your volume and your need for cash. If you have less than 1 tonne, local markets are better. If you have more than 5 tonnes, bulk buyers are usually more profitable because you save on transport and time. You can also do both: sell a portion at local markets for immediate cash and the rest to a bulk buyer. For example, sell 500 kg at local market for KES 25,000 and 9.5 tonnes to a miller for KES 380,000. Always calculate your net profit after all coststransport, market fees, and labour. Sometimes the higher price at local markets is eaten up by these costs. Bulk selling is often more efficient for large harvests.

4. Using Mobile Phones for Market Information

Your mobile phone is a powerful tool for getting better prices. You can use it to find current market prices, connect with buyers, and even receive payments. Start by registering for agricultural market information services. In Kenya, you can dial *283# to access M-Farm, which gives prices for various crops in different markets. In Nigeria, use the FarmCrowdy app or call the Nigeria Agricultural Quarantine Service. In Tanzania, dial *152*00# for the Agricultural Market Information System (AMIS). These services send SMS with prices for wheat, barley, and other crops.

You can also join WhatsApp and Telegram groups for farmers in your area. Search for groups like 'Wheat Farmers Kenya' or 'Barley Growers Tanzania'. In these groups, farmers share prices, buyers, and warnings about scams. Post a message: 'I have 5 tonnes of wheat, moisture 12%, clean. Looking for buyer.' Buyers in the group may contact you directly. Be careful: never share bank details or send money to someone you do not know. Meet in person or use a trusted escrow service.

Use your phone to call buyers directly. Save numbers of millers, breweries, and feed companies. When you call, be polite and brief: 'Hello, my name is [Name] from [Location]. I have 10 tonnes of barley for sale. What is your price per tonne?' Write down their offers and compare. You can also use mobile money like M-Pesa, Airtel Money, or MTN Mobile Money to receive payments quickly and safely. This avoids carrying large amounts of cash.

Finally, use your phone to take photos of your grain and send them via WhatsApp to buyers. A clear photo of clean, dry grain can convince a buyer to offer a better price. Keep records of all calls and messages. Mobile phones reduce the power of middlemen who used to exploit farmers by hiding market information. With a phone, you can know the real price and negotiate better.

5. Transport and Logistics: From Farm to Buyer

Getting your grain from the farm to the buyer is a critical step. Poor transport can reduce your profit or even spoil your grain. First, ensure your grain is properly dried and cleaned before transport. Moist grain can heat up and mold during transit. For wheat, moisture should be below 13%; for barley, below 12.5%. Use a moisture meter (available at agro-dealers for about KES 5,000) to check. Clean grain by winnowing or using a cleaning machine to remove stones, chaff, and dirt. Buyers pay less for dirty grain.

Next, choose your transport method. For small quantities (up to 1 tonne), you can use a pickup truck, a motorcycle, or even a bicycle. For larger quantities, hire a lorry or a truck. In Kenya, a 10-tonne lorry costs about KES 30,000 to KES 50,000 per trip, depending on distance. Always negotiate the transport price before loading. Ask other farmers for recommended transporters. Avoid transporters who demand payment upfront; pay half before and half after delivery.

Packaging matters. Use clean, strong sacks (e.g., 50 kg or 100 kg polypropylene bags). Do not use sacks that previously held fertiliser or chemicalsthey can contaminate your grain. Stack sacks on a clean tarpaulin in the truck. Cover with a tarpaulin to protect from rain and dust. If you are transporting to a brewery, they may require specific packaging, like 50 kg bags with your name and farm details.

Timing is important. Deliver on the agreed date. Late delivery can lead to penalties or rejection. Call the buyer when you are one hour away. Get a delivery note signed by the buyer or their representative. Keep a copy. If you are delivering to a miller, they will weigh your grain and test moisture. Be present during weighing to ensure accuracy. After delivery, follow up on payment. If you used a transporter, ensure they are paid and get a receipt. Good logistics build your reputation as a reliable supplier.

6. Record Keeping for Profit and Tax

Keeping records is not just for big companies. Every farmer who wants to make a profit must keep records. Records help you know if you are making money or losing it. They also help you calculate taxes and access loans. Start with a simple notebook or a record book from an agro-dealer. You can also use a mobile app like FarmDrive, DigiFarm, or Excel on a computer. Write down every income and expense related to your wheat and barley farming.

For each sale, record: date, buyer name, quantity sold (in kg or tonnes), price per unit, total amount, and payment method (cash, M-Pesa, cheque). For each expense, record: date, item (seed, fertiliser, labour, transport), quantity, cost, and seller. At the end of the season, add up all income and all expenses. Subtract expenses from income to get your profit. For example, if you earned KES 400,000 from selling 10 tonnes of wheat and spent KES 250,000 on production and transport, your profit is KES 150,000.

Records also help with tax. In many African countries, farmers with income above a certain threshold must pay tax. In Kenya, for example, if your annual farming income exceeds KES 1.2 million, you may need to pay tax. But even if you are below the threshold, records prove your income if you want a loan from a bank or SACCO. Banks like Equity, KCB, and Co-operative Bank require records to assess your creditworthiness. Without records, you cannot get formal credit.

Keep your records safe. Use a lockable box or a digital backup (e.g., email the photos of receipts to yourself). At the end of each year, review your records to see which crops were most profitable. Maybe barley gave you a better return than wheat. Use that information to plan next season. Good record keeping turns farming from a gamble into a business. It also helps you negotiate better prices because you know your exact costs. Start todayeven a simple notebook is enough.

<h3>Finding Buyers: Millers, Breweries, and Feed Companies</h3>
<p>Your buyer determines your price, your quality standard, and your payment timeline. In Africa, wheat and barley have three main industrial buyers:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Millers</strong> buy wheat for flour. Require test weight 76 kg/hl, moisture 12.5%, protein 1113%. Payment typically 1430 days after delivery.</li>
<li><strong>Breweries</strong> buy malting barley. Require germination 95%, protein 10.512.5%, moisture 12%. Usually contract-based with pre-agreed volumes and prices. Examples: Nigerian Breweries, Kenya Breweries (EABL), SABMiller/AB InBev subsidiaries.</li>
<li><strong>Feed companies</strong> buy feed-grade wheat and barley. Lower quality thresholds, lower price, but easier to sell and faster payment. Examples: animal feed mills in South Africa, Kenya, Nigeria.</li>
</ul>
<p>Before planting, contact at least two buyers per category. Ask for their quality specification sheet and current buying price. This gives you negotiating power and prevents post-harvest surprises.</p>

<h3>Negotiating Prices and Contracts</h3>
<p>Never accept the first price. Know your cost of production per tonne before you negotiate. Calculate: seed + fertilizer + chemicals + labour + transport + storage + interest. Add a 2030% margin. That is your floor price.</p>
<p>Key negotiation levers:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Volume</strong> larger volumes attract better prices. Aggregate with neighbours if you farm small plots.</li>
<li><strong>Quality</strong> higher protein, lower moisture, and clean grain command premiums.</li>
<li><strong>Timing</strong> prices are lowest at harvest when everyone sells. If you can store safely for 23 months, you may capture 1540% higher prices.</li>
<li><strong>Payment terms</strong> cash on delivery is worth 510% more than 30-day payment. Factor this into your price.</li>
</ul>
<p>Always get contracts in writing. Include: quantity, quality spec, price (or price formula), delivery date, payment date, and penalty clauses for both sides.</p>

<h3>Using Mobile Phones for Market Information</h3>
<p>Mobile phones have transformed African grain marketing. Use these tools:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>SMS price alerts</strong> platforms like Esoko (Ghana, Kenya, Nigeria), M-Farm (Kenya), and Zambia's ZAMACE send daily commodity prices via SMS.</li>
<li><strong>WhatsApp groups</strong> join local farmer cooperatives and trader groups. Prices are shared in real time.</li>
<li><strong>USSD platforms</strong> dial codes for market information without internet. Example: Kenya's *283# for agricultural services.</li>
<li><strong>Mobile money</strong> M-Pesa, Airtel Money, MTN MoMo for receiving payment and paying transport. Reduces cash risk.</li>
<li><strong>Digital scales and moisture meters</strong> some cooperatives offer mobile testing. Know your quality before you sell.</li>
</ul>
<p>Check prices from at least three sources before accepting any offer. Prices vary by region, quality, and season.</p>

<h3>Transport and Logistics: From Farm to Buyer</h3>
<p>Transport can eat 1025% of your revenue if poorly planned. Key rules:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Weigh before loading</strong> use a certified weighbridge. Never rely on the buyer's scale alone.</li>
<li><strong>Use tarpaulins</strong> protect grain from rain and moisture during transport.</li>
<li><strong>Book return loads</strong> if the truck returns empty, negotiate a lower rate by finding another farmer who needs delivery.</li>
<li><strong>Group transport</strong> cooperatives can negotiate 2030% lower rates by pooling volume.</li>
<li><strong>Get delivery receipts</strong> signed and dated, with weight, quality, and vehicle registration.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Record Keeping for Profit and Tax</h3>
<p>You cannot know your profit without records. Keep these five records:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Input log</strong> every seed, fertilizer, and chemical purchase with date and cost.</li>
<li><strong>Labour log</strong> who worked, how many days, how much paid.</li>
<li><strong>Harvest log</strong> tonnes harvested per field, moisture at harvest.</li>
<li><strong>Sales log</strong> buyer name, date, tonnes sold, price per tonne, total received, payment date.</li>
<li><strong>Expense receipts</strong> transport, storage, bags, levies. Keep every receipt.</li>
</ol>
<p>For tax: in most African countries, smallholder farmers are exempt from income tax below a threshold, but you may need to register for VAT if turnover exceeds the threshold (e.g., KES 5 million in Kenya, NGN 25 million in Nigeria). Check your country's revenue authority website.</p>

<strong style="color:#c0392b;"> SAFETY WARNING GRAIN STORAGE AND TRANSPORT</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#333;">Never store or transport grain with moisture above 13%. Wet grain grows <em>Aspergillus flavus</em>, which produces aflatoxin a poison that can cause liver cancer and is rejected by all buyers. If grain heats up, smells musty, or shows mould, do not sell it for human or animal consumption. Report to your extension officer. Also, never enter a sealed grain silo or bin without proper ventilation and a safety harness grain suffocation kills silently and quickly.</p>
