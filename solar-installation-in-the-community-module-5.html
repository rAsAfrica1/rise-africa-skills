Lessons

1. Preparing Mounting Structures

Before you touch a panel, you need a solid mounting structure. In most African community solar projects, we use either galvanised steel angle iron (like 40mm x 40mm x 4mm) or treated timber (50mm x 100mm). Steel is better for longevity, especially in coastal or humid areas. You will need a hacksaw or angle grinder with a metal cutting disc, a power drill with cobalt bits, a spanner set, and a tape measure. Start by measuring the roof or ground area where the panels will go. Panels are typically 1.6m x 1m for a 250W-400W module. Plan for at least 10cm gap between panels for airflow and thermal expansion. Cut your steel to size. For a typical 4-panel array, you need two long rails (called stringers) that span the width of the panels. Each rail should be at least 4.2m long if panels are mounted in portrait. Drill holes in the rails where they will bolt to the roof mounts or ground posts. Use 10mm bolts with spring washers and nuts. If using timber, pre-drill to avoid splitting, and treat all cut ends with wood preservative or used engine oil. For ground mounts, dig holes 60cm deep for concrete footings. Mix concrete 1:3:5 (cement:sand:aggregate) and let it cure for at least 48 hours before loading. Always wear safety glasses and gloves. Deburr all cut metal edges with a file. Check that your structure is level using a spirit level before tightening all bolts. A wobbly structure will crack panels or cause micro-cracks that kill output. In dusty areas, consider raising the structure at least 50cm off the ground to reduce dust accumulation and allow cleaning. For pole mounts, use a 100mm diameter steel pole set in a 80cm deep concrete base. Weld or bolt a top plate to the pole to attach the panel frame. Never use mild steel without painting it with red oxide primer and two coats of enamel. Rust is the number one killer of mounts in Africa. Finally, lay out all parts and count your bolts, nuts, and washers before starting assembly. Missing one bolt means a trip to town.

2. Installing Roof Mounts and Flashing

Roof mounts are the most common in African villages, but they must be done right or you will have leaks and a dangerous installation. First, identify your roof type: corrugated iron sheets (most common), clay tiles, or concrete. For corrugated iron, use L-foot brackets with a rubber gasket and a 8mm x 100mm coach screw. The L-foot must sit on the crest (the high part) of the corrugation, never in the valley. Drill a 8mm pilot hole through the crest and into the roof purlin (the wooden or steel beam under the sheet). Apply a bead of polyurethane sealant (like Sikaflex) around the hole before inserting the screw. Tighten until the gasket just compresses do not over-tighten or you will crush the gasket and leak. For each mount, use two screws diagonally opposite. For tile roofs, you need to lift the tile, fix a galvanised strap to the rafter, then notch the tile to fit over the strap. Replace the tile and seal the notch with roof cement. For concrete roofs, use expansion anchors (M10 x 100mm) with a chemical anchor if the concrete is weak. Drill a 10mm hole, clean dust, inject epoxy, insert anchor, wait 30 minutes, then tighten. Flashing is critical. After installing each L-foot, cover the base with a piece of lead or aluminium flashing (150mm x 150mm) bedded in sealant. Nail or screw the flashing to the roof sheet above the mount, and seal all edges. In heavy rain areas, add a second layer of sealant after 24 hours. Never use silicone sealant on metal roofs it fails in UV. Use polyurethane or butyl tape. Check for leaks by pouring water on the roof after installation. If you see drips inside, re-seal. Also, ensure all mounts are in line: use a string line from the first to the last mount. A crooked array looks bad and stresses panels. Finally, bond all metal mounts to a ground rod with 6mm copper wire to prevent lightning damage. In Africa, lightning is a real risk. A proper roof mount should last 20 years. Take your time.

3. Ground Mount and Pole Mount Installation

Ground mounts are ideal where roofs are weak, shaded, or not owned by the user. They also allow easy cleaning and better cooling. Start by marking out your array footprint with pegs and string. For a typical 4-panel ground mount, you need 4 concrete footings, each 40cm x 40cm x 60cm deep. Dig the holes, then set 100mm x 100mm galvanised steel posts or 100mm diameter steel pipes in the concrete. Use a spirit level to ensure each post is perfectly vertical. Brace them with temporary wooden stakes until concrete cures (48 hours minimum, 7 days for full strength). The posts should extend at least 1m above ground. Weld or bolt horizontal rails (40mm x 40mm angle iron) to the posts. The rails should be spaced to match the panel mounting holes usually 1m apart for portrait panels. For pole mounts, use a single 150mm diameter steel pole set in a 80cm deep, 40cm diameter concrete base. The pole must be perfectly plumb. Weld a top plate (200mm x 200mm x 6mm steel) to the pole. Bolt an adjustable frame to the top plate so you can change tilt seasonally. In sandy or soft soil, increase footing size by 50%. In rocky ground, use a rock anchor with epoxy. Always paint all steel with red oxide primer and two coats of enamel. For earthing, drive a 1.5m copper-clad ground rod near the array and connect all metal parts with 6mm copper wire. For cable management, run cables through PVC conduit buried 30cm deep, or use UV-resistant cable ties to secure cables to the structure. Leave a drip loop where cables enter conduit. In windy areas, add diagonal bracing to the posts. A ground mount should withstand 100km/h winds. Test by pushing hard on the frame it should not move. If it does, add more bracing or deeper footings. Finally, keep grass and weeds away from the base to prevent corrosion and fire risk.

4. Panel Placement and Orientation

Where you place and how you orient your panels determines how much power you get. In Africa, we are in the northern or southern hemisphere. If you are north of the equator (e.g., Nigeria, Kenya, Ethiopia), face panels true south. If south of the equator (e.g., South Africa, Zambia, Zimbabwe), face true north. Never face east or west unless you have a special reason you lose 20-30% of daily energy. Tilt angle: the rule of thumb is to set tilt equal to your latitude. For Nairobi (1S), tilt 5-10 for good year-round performance. For Harare (17S), tilt 15-20. For Cape Town (34S), tilt 30-35. In the rainy season, increase tilt by 10 to help shed water and dirt. In dusty areas, a steeper tilt (30+) lets rain clean the panels. Use a compass or a smartphone app to find true north/south. Remember magnetic declination in Africa it can be 10-20 off. Check a declination map online or use a GPS. For best results, use a solar pathfinder or a simple shadow stick: at solar noon (when sun is highest), the shadow of a vertical stick should point exactly north (if you are south of equator) or south (if north). Mark that line. Panels must not be shaded. Even a small shadow from a pole, tree, or building can cut output by 50% or more. Walk around the array at 9am, noon, and 3pm to check for shadows. Trim trees or move the array. Leave at least 2m clearance from any obstacle. For multiple rows, space them so that at winter solstice noon, the shadow of the front row does not touch the back row. A simple rule: row spacing = panel height x 2.5. For a 1m high panel, space rows 2.5m apart. If you cannot avoid shading, use micro-inverters or power optimisers, but these are expensive. Better to place panels well. Finally, angle panels slightly (5) even if you want flat mounting to allow water runoff and dust shedding. Never mount panels perfectly flat water pools and dust sticks.

5. Securing Panels and Torque Specifications

Once your panels are in place, you must secure them properly. Loose panels vibrate, crack, and can fly off in a storm. Use the correct clamps: mid-clamps between panels and end-clamps at the ends. Most panels have a 35mm or 40mm frame height. Clamps must match. Stainless steel clamps are best they do not rust. Aluminium clamps are okay but can corrode in coastal areas. Never use mild steel clamps. The bolts that hold clamps to the rails are usually M8 (8mm) stainless steel. The torque specification is critical. Too loose and the panel moves; too tight and you crush the frame or crack the glass. For M8 bolts, the recommended torque is 15-20 Nm (Newton-meters). For M6 bolts, 8-10 Nm. For M10, 30-40 Nm. You need a torque wrench. If you don't have one, tighten until the spring washer is flat, then a quarter turn more. But a torque wrench is cheap insurance. Always use a flat washer and a spring washer under the nut. Apply a small amount of anti-seize compound to stainless bolts to prevent galling (cold welding). Tighten in a diagonal pattern, like you would a car wheel, to avoid stressing the frame. Start with the centre clamps, then the ends. After tightening, check that the panel is not distorted look along the frame edge. If it bows, loosen and re-tighten. For ground mounts, also check the bolts holding the rails to the posts. Those should be M10 or M12, torqued to 40-50 Nm. After installation, re-torque all bolts after one week, then every six months. Vibration and thermal cycling loosen bolts. In dusty areas, clean bolts before torquing. Use a paint marker to mark tightened bolts so you can see if any move. For added security, use a thread-locking compound like Loctite 243 on all bolts. Never use ordinary nuts always use nyloc nuts or two nuts jammed together. If you are in a high-theft area, use security bolts (Torx with pin) or weld the nuts to the bolts after tightening. But remember, you may need to remove panels for repair. A good rule: secure panels so they cannot be lifted by hand, but can be removed with tools. Finally, check that all clamps are fully seated on the panel frame not on the glass. Clamping on glass voids warranty and breaks panels.

6. Inspecting Mounting Integrity

After installation, and then every six months, you must inspect the mounting system. A loose mount can cause micro-cracks in panels, reduce output, and create a safety hazard. Start with a visual inspection from the ground. Look for: sagging rails, rust streaks, bent brackets, missing bolts, and loose clamps. Use binoculars if the roof is high. Then climb up safely use a ladder rated for your weight, and tie it off. Wear a harness if working at height. Check every bolt with a torque wrench. Set it to the specified torque (e.g., 18 Nm for M8) and try to turn the bolt. If it moves, it was loose. Re-torque and mark it. Check all clamps: they should be tight against the frame with no gap. Push the panel gently it should not move. If it moves, find the loose clamp and tighten. Check for corrosion: white powder on aluminium, red rust on steel. Clean with a wire brush and apply zinc-rich paint or cold galvanising spray. Check the roof penetrations for leaks. Look for water stains inside the roof. Re-seal any suspect flashing with polyurethane sealant. Check the earthing: the copper wire should be firmly connected to the ground rod and to the mount. Measure resistance with a multimeter it should be less than 1 ohm. Check cable management: cables should not rub against sharp edges or be pinched. Replace any UV-damaged cable ties. In dusty areas, clean the panels and mounts with a soft brush and water. Do not use a pressure washer it can force water into connectors. After cleaning, inspect for cracks in the glass or frame. If you find a cracked panel, replace it immediately it can cause a fire or shock hazard. For ground mounts, check the concrete footings for cracks. If cracks are wider than 2mm, consult an engineer. Check for soil erosion around footings backfill with compacted gravel. In coastal areas, inspect every three months due to salt corrosion. Keep a logbook: date, inspector, findings, actions. This helps you track recurring issues. Finally, after any storm or high wind (over 60km/h), do an emergency inspection. Look for shifted panels, bent mounts, and loose bolts. Do not wait six months. A well-maintained mount lasts 25 years. A neglected one fails in 3.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Solar Panel Mounting and Installation

This practical reference consolidates the six lessons in this module into a single field-ready guide for solar technicians, EPC teams, and agribusiness operators working across sub-Saharan Africa. Mounting is where most solar installations fail not inverters, not panels. Poor mounting causes panel cracks, roof leaks, corrosion, theft, and lost yield. In African conditions high UV, dust, harmattan winds, monsoon downpours, coastal salt, and temperature swings mounting quality determines whether a system lasts 5 years or 25. This lesson gives you the torque values, spacing, orientation data, and inspection routines you need to install and commission mounting systems that survive the continent.

1. Preparing Mounting Structures

Before any panel goes up, the structure must be designed for local wind and load conditions. In most African markets, use galvanised steel or anodised aluminium rails rated for a minimum 120 km/h wind speed; coastal installations (Mombasa, Lagos, Accra, Dar es Salaam) require hot-dip galvanised steel or 316 stainless fasteners to resist salt corrosion. Check roof trusses are sound many rural roofs are timber with limited load capacity. For ground mounts, concrete footings must extend below the local frost-free depth (rare in Africa) but more critically below the erosion line and above the seasonal water table. Use M10 or M12 bolts as standard; pre-drill timber to prevent splitting.

2. Installing Roof Mounts and Flashing

Roof mounts fall into three families: penetrating (lag bolts into rafters), non-penetrating (ballasted, for flat roofs), and hybrid. Every penetration must be flashed: apply a bead of UV-stable polyurethane sealant under the flashing, then over the fastener head. Never rely on silicone alone it fails within 23 years under African UV. On corrugated iron roofs common across East and Southern Africa, use EPDM-backed brackets and lead or rubber flashings. On thatch, avoid penetrations entirely; use ground or pole mounts instead.

3. Ground Mount and Pole Mount Installation

Ground mounts suit farms, schools, and health centres with available land. Set posts in concrete (1:3:5 mix), minimum 600 mm deep and 300 mm diameter per post for a 4-panel array. Space rows to avoid shading: a rule of thumb is row spacing = panel height 2.5 for latitudes near the equator, 3 for southern Africa. Pole mounts are ideal for boreholes, cattle dips, and telecoms use schedule 40 steel pipe, guy wires in windy zones, and a swivel head for seasonal tilt adjustment.

4. Panel Placement and Orientation

In the Northern Hemisphere (most of West, Central, and North Africa), face panels true south. In the Southern Hemisphere (Southern and East Africa below ~5S), face true north. Near the equator (within 5), tilt 510 for self-cleaning. Tilt should approximate latitude 10 for optimal annual yield. Avoid shading from trees, water tanks, and future buildings even partial shade on one cell can cut string output by 3050%.

5. Securing Panels and Torque Specifications

Torque is the single most overlooked step. Under-torqued bolts loosen and panels fly off in storms; over-torqued bolts crack frames and strip threads. Always use a calibrated torque wrench. The table below gives standard values; always defer to the panel manufacturer's datasheet if it differs.

SAFETY WARNING Working at Height & Electrical Risk

Falls are the leading cause of death in solar installation. In Africa, many installations happen on steep iron-sheet roofs with no fall arrest. Never work on a roof without a harness anchored to a certified point, or without a spotter. Never install panels during rain, high wind (>40 km/h), or when the array is energised. DC strings can carry 6001000 V always isolate and lock out before mounting work. Dehydration and heat stroke are real risks; schedule work before 11am and after 3pm, and carry at least 3 litres of water per person per day.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Wind & load check: Confirm structure rated 120 km/h; coastal sites use 316 stainless or hot-dip galvanised.
  2. Roof integrity: Verify rafters can carry load; pre-drill timber; flash every penetration with UV-stable sealant.
  3. Orientation: True north (Southern Hemisphere) or true south (Northern Hemisphere); tilt = latitude 10; 510 near equator.
  4. Shading survey: Walk the site at 9am, noon, and 3pm; no shade on any cell during peak hours.
  5. Torque: Use a calibrated torque wrench; M8 = 1620 Nm, M10 = 3540 Nm, M12 = 6070 Nm (verify against panel datasheet).
  6. Clearance: Minimum 300 mm panel-to-ground and 100 mm panel-to-roof for airflow and cleaning.
  7. Grounding: Bond all rails and frames; test continuity <1 before energising.
  8. Document: Photograph every clamp, take torque readings, log inspection date this becomes your O&M sales asset.
