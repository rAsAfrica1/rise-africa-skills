1. Preparing Fruits and Vegetables for Drying

Start with fresh, ripe but firm produce. Wash everything in clean water. For fruits like mango, pineapple, banana, and papaya, peel and remove seeds or pits. Cut into uniform slices or pieces, about 5-8 mm thick. Uniform size ensures even drying. To prevent browning in apples, bananas, and potatoes, soak cut pieces in a solution of 1 teaspoon lemon juice or 1 tablespoon vinegar per litre of water for 5 minutes. Drain well.

Blanching is important for vegetables like carrots, green beans, and pumpkin. Bring a large pot of water to a rolling boil. Drop vegetables in for 2-4 minutes, then immediately transfer to cold water for the same time. This stops enzyme action that causes spoilage and helps keep colour and vitamins. Pat dry with a clean cloth.

For leafy greens like spinach or kale, wash, remove tough stems, and tear into small pieces. No blanching needed. For tomatoes, slice 1 cm thick and remove seeds to speed drying. For onions and garlic, peel and slice thinly. Spread everything in a single layer on clean drying trays or mats. Do not overlap. You can dry mixed vegetables together, but strong-smelling items like onion and garlic should be dried separately to avoid flavour transfer.

Work quickly and cleanly. Use clean hands, clean knives, and clean surfaces. In hot African climates, fruit flies and dust are a problem. Cover trays with a light cloth or mosquito net during preparation. Prepare only as much as you can dry in one day. If you have a solar dryer, load it immediately. If sun drying, start early in the morning so produce gets maximum sun. Never dry produce that is already mouldy or rotten. A good rule: 1 kg of fresh vegetables yields about 100-150 g of dried product. For fruits, 1 kg fresh gives 150-200 g dried. Plan your quantities accordingly.

2. Building a Simple Solar Dryer with Local Materials

A solar dryer speeds up drying, protects from rain, dust, and animals, and gives better quality. You can build one using cheap local materials. You need: a wooden or metal frame, a clear plastic sheet or glass for the top, black paint or black plastic for the base, and mesh trays. A simple box dryer works well.

Build a wooden box about 1 metre long, 60 cm wide, and 30 cm deep. The bottom should be black â paint it black or line it with black plastic. This absorbs heat. Cut a small hole (5 cm diameter) at the bottom front for air intake, and another at the top back for hot air exit. Cover the top with clear plastic sheet or glass, slanted towards the sun. Inside, place 2-3 mesh trays (mosquito net or wire mesh) stacked with 10 cm gaps. The trays hold the food.

For a cheaper version, use a wooden frame covered with clear plastic on all sides, like a small greenhouse. Put a black plastic sheet on the ground inside. This works well in hot, sunny areas. Another option: a solar tent dryer â a wooden A-frame covered with clear plastic, with vents at bottom and top. The key is: black base absorbs heat, clear cover traps heat, vents allow moist air to escape.

Place the dryer in full sun, facing the direction of the midday sun. In most of Africa, that means facing north in the southern hemisphere and south in the northern hemisphere. Angle the top so it gets maximum sun. Load food in a single layer on trays. The temperature inside should reach 50-70Â°C. That is hot enough to dry food in 1-3 days, faster than open sun. Check the dryer daily. If it rains, close the vents and bring the dryer under shelter. Clean trays after each use. With good care, a simple solar dryer lasts 2-3 years.

3. Sun Drying Fish and Meat Safely

Drying fish and meat preserves them for months, but you must do it safely to avoid food poisoning. Start with very fresh fish or meat. Fish should be gutted, scaled, and washed in clean water. For small fish like sardines or tilapia, you can dry them whole after gutting. For larger fish, split them open and remove the backbone, or cut into strips 2-3 cm thick. Meat should be cut into thin strips, 1-2 cm thick, along the grain. Remove fat and sinew â fat goes rancid.

Salt is your best friend. Make a strong brine: 1 cup of salt per 4 litres of water. Soak fish for 30 minutes to 1 hour. For meat, rub dry salt generously and let it sit for 1 hour, then rinse. Salt kills bacteria and keeps flies away. After salting, you can also dip fish in a weak vinegar solution (1 part vinegar to 4 parts water) for 1 minute to further protect against insects.

Sun drying works best in hot, dry, windy weather. Hang fish or meat on clean lines or spread on raised mesh racks. Do not dry directly on the ground â that invites dirt and animals. Cover with a light mosquito net to keep flies off. Turn pieces every few hours. Drying time: small fish 1-2 days, large fish 3-5 days, meat strips 2-4 days. In humid weather, use a solar dryer instead. If you see maggots, mould, or a bad smell, throw the batch away. Do not eat it.

To test if fish or meat is dry: it should be hard, leathery, and spring back when bent. No moist spots. For fish, the flesh should be dry all the way to the bone. For meat, it should snap when bent. Once dry, store in airtight containers in a cool, dark place. Properly dried and stored fish or meat keeps for 3-6 months. Always cook dried fish or meat thoroughly before eating.

4. Drying Herbs and Spices for Market

Herbs and spices fetch good prices when dried and packaged well. Common ones in Africa include mint, basil, rosemary, thyme, parsley, coriander, ginger, garlic, chili, and turmeric. Harvest herbs in the morning after dew has dried but before the sun is too hot. This preserves essential oils that give flavour. For leafy herbs, cut whole stems. For spices like ginger and turmeric, dig up roots, wash, and slice thinly. Garlic and chili can be dried whole or sliced.

Drying herbs is different from drying fruits. You want to dry them quickly but at lower temperatures to keep colour and aroma. Do not dry herbs in direct hot sun â that bleaches them and loses flavour. Instead, dry them in the shade with good air flow. Tie stems in small bundles and hang them upside down in a dry, dark, well-ventilated room. Or spread on mesh trays in a solar dryer with the vents open to keep temperature below 40Â°C. For roots and spices, sun drying or solar drying at 50-60Â°C is fine.

Drying time: leafy herbs take 2-4 days in shade. Roots like ginger and turmeric take 3-5 days in sun. Chili takes 4-7 days. Garlic takes 3-5 days. Test dryness: leaves should crumble easily when rubbed. Roots should snap, not bend. Once dry, remove stems and large pieces. For leafy herbs, crush or powder by rubbing between your hands or using a clean mortar and pestle. For spices, grind in a clean mill or pound. Work quickly to avoid absorbing moisture.

Packaging is key for market. Use clean, dry glass jars or airtight plastic containers. Label with the herb name and date. Store in a cool, dark place. For selling, small packets of 50g or 100g sell well in local markets and to restaurants. Dried herbs and spices keep their flavour for 6-12 months if stored properly. You can earn good income from a small plot of herbs. One square metre of mint can produce 200-300 g of dried leaves per harvest. With proper drying and packaging, you can sell to neighbours, shops, and even hotels.

5. Testing for Dryness and Preventing Mold

If you package food before it is fully dry, mould will grow and ruin everything. Mould produces poisons that can make people sick. So testing for dryness is critical. There are simple tests for different foods. For fruits and vegetables: cut a piece in half. There should be no moist spots inside. The texture should be leathery or brittle. For leafy vegetables, they should crumble easily. For fish and meat: bend a piece. It should snap, not bend like rubber. For herbs: leaves should crumble when rubbed. For roots and spices: they should be hard and snap when broken.

Another test: put a piece in a clean, dry jar with a lid. Wait 10 minutes. If moisture forms on the inside of the jar, it is not dry enough. Return it to the dryer for more hours. A more accurate method is the salt test: put a small amount of dried food in a jar with a teaspoon of dry salt. Shake and wait 15 minutes. If the salt clumps, there is still moisture. If it stays loose, the food is dry.

Mould prevention starts before drying. Use clean equipment. Dry food quickly and evenly. Do not dry in humid or rainy weather. In humid areas, use a solar dryer or add a small fire under a drying rack (smoke also helps preserve). After drying, cool the food completely before packaging. Any warmth creates condensation. Package in airtight containers â glass jars, sealed plastic bags, or metal tins. Keep in a cool, dark, dry place. Never store dried food near fresh produce or liquids.

Check stored dried food every 2 weeks. Look for mould (fuzzy green, white, or black spots), off-smells, or insects. If you find mould, throw the whole batch away â do not just cut off the mouldy part. Mould spreads invisible threads. If you find insects, you can kill them by putting the food in the freezer for 48 hours, or heating in an oven at 60Â°C for 30 minutes. Then repackage. With proper drying and storage, your dried food lasts 6-12 months or more.

6. Packaging and Storing Dried Products

Good packaging protects dried food from moisture, air, light, insects, and dirt. It also makes your product look professional for selling. The best containers are airtight: glass jars with rubber seals, metal tins with tight lids, thick plastic containers with snap-on lids, or vacuum-sealed bags. For market, small clear plastic bags or jars show the product nicely. Always use food-grade materials. Never use containers that held chemicals, oil, or soap.

Before packaging, make sure food is completely cool. Warm food creates steam inside the container, leading to mould. Fill containers to the top to reduce air space. For plastic bags, squeeze out as much air as possible before sealing. You can seal with a hot iron or a candle flame (carefully). For glass jars, fill to within 1 cm of the top and close tightly. Label each package with the product name, date of drying, and best-before date (usually 6-12 months later).

Store in a cool, dark, dry place. A cupboard away from the stove and sunlight is good. Heat and light destroy vitamins and flavour. Keep containers off the floor to avoid dampness and insects. If you live in a very humid area, add a small packet of silica gel (drying agent) or a few grains of dry rice to each container to absorb moisture. Check stored food every 2-4 weeks. Look for clumping, mould, or insects. If you see any, remove the affected container and check others.

For selling, presentation matters. Use clean, attractive labels. Small packets of 50g, 100g, or 250g are popular. You can sell to neighbours, local shops, restaurants, and schools. Dried fruits and vegetables can be sold as healthy snacks. Dried herbs and spices are always in demand. With good packaging, you can store your harvest for months and sell when prices are higher. This turns food preservation into income. Remember: the goal is to keep food safe, tasty, and sellable for as long as possible.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Sun Drying and Solar Drying Techniques

Drying is the oldest and most accessible form of food preservation in Africa. It removes moisture that bacteria, moulds and yeasts need to multiply, and it reduces weight and volume so produce can be stored longer and transported cheaper. For small and growing agribusinesses, drying converts gluts into storable inventory, reduces post-harvest losses (often 20â40% for fruits and vegetables in sub-Saharan Africa) and creates value-added products for local and export markets.

This practical reference consolidates the six lessons in this module: preparing fruits and vegetables, building a simple solar dryer, sun drying fish and meat safely, drying herbs and spices for market, testing for dryness and preventing mould, and packaging and storing dried products. Treat it as a shop-floor manual: use the checklists, tables and diagrams when training staff, setting batch records or designing a small drying enterprise.

How drying works â and why solar drying beats open sun

Sun drying relies on direct solar radiation and natural air movement. Solar drying uses a structure (direct, indirect or mixed-mode) that traps heat, increases air temperature and improves airflow, so drying is faster, more uniform and better protected from dust, rain, insects, rodents and animals. Faster drying means less spoilage, better colour and vitamin retention, and a product that commands a higher price.

Building a simple solar dryer with local materials

A cabinet or tent dryer can be built with timber, mud brick, bamboo, plywood, clear polythene or glass, black paint or charcoal for the absorber, and mosquito netting for trays. A basic indirect dryer needs: a collector (glazed box with black absorber), a drying chamber with 3â5 perforated trays, and a chimney or vent. Cost in many African markets is USD 30â150 for a small unit, and payback is often one or two harvest seasons when drying high-value crops such as mango, tomato, moringa or fish.

Design rules that make the difference: face the collector towards the equator (north in southern Africa, south in northern Africa); tilt it at roughly your latitude plus 10Â°; keep the chimney at least 1 m above the chamber; use a black absorber; and allow 0.1â0.2 mÂ² of collector per kg of fresh product. Always include a rain cover and insect mesh on all openings.

Never eat or sell dried food that is mouldy, musty, slimy or discoloured. Some moulds produce aflatoxins and other mycotoxins that are heat-stable and can cause liver cancer and stunting in children. Discard the whole batch â do not just cut off the mouldy part. Do not dry fish or meat on the ground, on bare soil, or where animals, rodents and dust can reach it. Use raised racks with insect netting. If rain threatens, move products into a ventilated shelter or finish in a solar dryer. Keep raw meat and fish separate from ready-to-eat dried products to avoid cross-contamination.

Sun drying fish and meat safely

Fish and meat are high-risk products. Salt, smoke and acid (lime, lemon) are your allies: they lower water activity and slow spoilage before drying is complete. For fish, gut within 2 hours of catch, wash in clean water, brine for 10â20 minutes (or dry-salt for 12â24 hours), then dry on raised racks. For meat, cure with salt and spices for 12â24 hours under refrigeration if available, then dry. Target 15â20% moisture for fish and 10â15% for meat. In humid coastal areas, combine solar drying with light smoking or use a solar dryer with a small firebox for 1â2 hours at the start.

Drying herbs and spices for market

Herbs and spices lose aroma and colour if dried too hot or in direct sun. Dry them in shade or in a solar dryer at 35â45 Â°C. Keep leaves whole until sale, then grind to order. Package in opaque, airtight containers to protect volatile oils and colour. High-value examples for African markets include moringa leaf powder, hibiscus (zobo/roselle), ginger, turmeric, black pepper, cloves, cinnamon and chili. Moringa leaf powder can retail at USD 8â20 per kg in urban and export markets when quality is consistent.

Testing for dryness

Three practical tests: (1) Touch test â leathery, pliable but not sticky for fruits; brittle and snapping for vegetables and herbs. (2) Cut test â no visible moisture when a piece is cut. (3) Weight test â record fresh weight and dry until weight stabilises over two consecutive readings. For commercial batches, use a moisture meter or the oven method (dry a sample at 105 Â°C for 24 hours and calculate moisture loss).

Packaging and storing dried products

Dried products are hygroscopic â they absorb moisture from the air. Cool them completely before packing, then use moisture-proof, airtight packaging: laminated foil pouches, PET jars with sealed lids, or vacuum bags. Label with product name, batch number, drying date, best-before date and weight. Store in a cool, dark, dry room below 25 Â°C and below 60% relative humidity, off the floor on pallets. For long storage, add oxygen absorbers or desiccant sachets (food-grade) and consider freezing for 48 hours before storage to kill insect eggs. Fumigation should only be done by licensed operators using approved methods.

For market access, invest in consistency: same slice thickness, same colour, same moisture, clean packaging and a simple brand. Buyers pay for reliability. A well-run solar drying unit can supply supermarkets, hotels, schools, NGOs and export aggregators, and can dry on contract for farmer groups â a strong business model in areas where fresh produce prices collapse at harvest.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Harvest at correct maturity; wash, sort and remove damaged pieces.
  • Prepare uniformly: peel, slice to even thickness, blanch or pretreat as required.
  • Use a solar dryer or raised racks with insect netting â never dry on the ground.
  • Monitor temperature and airflow; keep herbs and spices at 35â45 Â°C.
  • Brine or cure fish and meat before drying; dry quickly and protect from rain.
  • Test for dryness (touch, cut, weight) and confirm safe moisture before packing.
  • Cool completely, then pack in airtight, moisture-proof, labelled containers.
  • Store cool, dark and dry; keep batch records and discard any mouldy batch.
