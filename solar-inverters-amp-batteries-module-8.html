1. Inverter Not Turning On: Diagnosing Battery and Fuse Issues

If your inverter won't turn on, start with the battery. Use a multimeter set to DC volts. Touch the red probe to the battery positive and black to negative. A healthy 12V battery should read 12.6V or higher when resting. If it reads below 11.8V, the battery is too low to start the inverter. Charge it with a separate charger or solar panels. If the battery is completely dead (under 10V), it may be damaged and need replacement.

Next, check the fuse or circuit breaker between the battery and inverter. Many inverters have a built-in fuse or an external fuse holder on the positive cable. Use your multimeter on continuity mode (or ohms) to test the fuse. Disconnect the fuse from the circuit first. If the meter beeps or shows near 0 ohms, the fuse is good. If it shows no reading or OL, the fuse is blown. Replace it with the same amperage rating â never use a higher one. Common sizes are 100A, 150A, or 200A for 12V inverters.

Also inspect the battery cables and terminals. Loose or corroded connections cause voltage drop. Clean terminals with a wire brush and tighten them firmly. A loose connection can read 12.6V at the battery but only 10V at the inverter. Check the inverter's on/off switch and remote switch. Some inverters have a small reset button; press it. If the inverter still won't turn on, test the DC input at the inverter terminals. If you have good voltage there but no power, the inverter itself may be faulty.

In Africa, frequent power cuts and dusty conditions make battery maintenance critical. Keep batteries in a ventilated, shaded place. Check water levels in flooded lead-acid batteries monthly â top up with distilled water only. If you use lithium batteries, they have a BMS that may shut off. Press the reset button on the BMS or disconnect and reconnect the battery. Always wear safety glasses when working with batteries.

2. Battery Not Charging: Panel, Controller, or Wiring Faults

When your battery is not charging, first check the solar panels. Use a multimeter to measure the open-circuit voltage (Voc) of each panel. Disconnect the panel from the controller and measure across the positive and negative terminals. A 12V panel should read around 18-22V in full sun. If it reads 0V, the panel is faulty or shaded. Check for shadows from trees, buildings, or dust. Clean panels with water and a soft cloth â dust can cut output by 30% or more.

Next, test the charge controller. Measure the voltage at the controller's solar input terminals. It should match the panel voltage. Then measure the battery output terminals. If the controller is working, the battery voltage should be slightly higher than the battery's resting voltage when charging. If the controller shows no output, it may be faulty. Check the controller's settings: for a 12V battery, the bulk charge voltage should be around 14.4V, float at 13.6V. If settings are wrong, adjust them. Many controllers have a display or LEDs; a red light often means a fault.

Wiring faults are common. Check all connections between panels, controller, and battery. Loose or corroded wires cause resistance. Use a multimeter to measure continuity along each cable. A good cable should read near 0 ohms. If it reads high resistance, replace the cable. Also check for reverse polarity â if you connected positive to negative by mistake, the controller may be damaged. Always use fuses on the positive cable from the panel to the controller.

In Africa, heat can reduce charging efficiency. Keep the controller in a cool, shaded place. If your battery is old (over 4-5 years for lead-acid), it may not hold charge. Test with a load tester or simply check if it drops below 12V quickly under load. If so, replace the battery. For lithium batteries, check the BMS â it may have shut off charging due to overvoltage or temperature. Reset it if needed.

3. Appliances Not Working: Load Sizing and Inverter Limits

If your appliances don't work even when the inverter is on, the problem is likely load sizing. Every appliance has a power rating in watts (W). Add up the watts of all appliances you want to run at the same time. For example, a 100W TV, 60W fan, and 10W phone charger total 170W. Your inverter must handle at least that much continuous power. But motors and compressors (fridges, freezers, pumps) have a surge wattage 3-5 times their running watts. A 200W fridge may surge to 600-1000W for a few seconds. Your inverter must handle that surge.

Check your inverter's continuous and surge ratings. A typical 1000W inverter can handle 2000W surge for a few seconds. If you overload it, it will shut down or blow a fuse. Start with small loads first: turn on the inverter, then plug in one appliance at a time. If an appliance trips the inverter, it's too big. Use a clamp meter to measure actual current draw. For AC appliances, measure amps and multiply by 230V (or 120V) to get watts. For DC appliances, multiply amps by 12V or 24V.

Also check the appliance itself. Plug it into a known working outlet (grid or generator) to see if it works. If it doesn't, the appliance is faulty. If it works on grid but not inverter, the inverter may be undersized or the battery voltage may be too low. A low battery causes the inverter to shut off under load. Measure battery voltage while the appliance is running. If it drops below 11V for a 12V system, the battery is too weak or discharged.

In Africa, many people use second-hand appliances with unknown power ratings. Use a plug-in power meter (kilowatt meter) to measure actual consumption. Also, avoid using extension cords that are too thin â they cause voltage drop. Use thick cables for high-power appliances. If you need to run a fridge, consider a dedicated inverter just for it. Always match your inverter to your load, not the other way around.

4. Frequent Shutdowns: Overload, Overheat, and Low Voltage

Frequent shutdowns are usually caused by three things: overload, overheat, or low battery voltage. Start by checking the load. If your inverter shuts down when you turn on a specific appliance, that appliance is overloading it. For example, a 1500W inverter cannot run a 2000W kettle. Add up all running appliances. If total watts exceed 80% of the inverter's continuous rating, you are overloading it. Reduce the load by turning off some appliances. Remember surge watts: a 1000W inverter may handle 2000W surge, but only for a few seconds. If the surge lasts longer, it shuts down.

Overheating is common in hot African climates. Inverters need ventilation. Keep them out of direct sunlight and in a shaded, open area. Do not stack items on top of the inverter. Check the cooling fan â if it's not spinning, the inverter will overheat. Clean dust from vents with a brush or compressed air. If the inverter feels too hot to touch (above 60Â°C), it will shut down to protect itself. Improve airflow with a small fan if needed. Some inverters have a temperature fault LED; check the manual.

Low battery voltage is the most common cause of shutdowns under load. When the battery is weak or discharged, its voltage drops when a load is applied. The inverter has a low-voltage cutoff, typically 10.5V for 12V systems. If the voltage drops below that, it shuts off. Measure battery voltage while the inverter is running and loaded. If it drops below 11V, the battery is too small, too old, or not fully charged. Charge the battery fully and test again. If it still drops, replace the battery with a larger capacity (more Ah) or a lithium battery.

Also check for loose connections. A loose battery cable can cause voltage drop under load. Tighten all connections. Use a multimeter to measure voltage at the inverter terminals while running. If it's more than 0.5V lower than at the battery, your cables are too thin or too long. Upgrade to thicker cables (e.g., 25mmÂ² or 35mmÂ² for 12V systems). In Africa, many inverters fail due to poor installation. Always follow the manual's cable size recommendations.

5. Using a Multimeter to Test Voltage and Continuity

A multimeter is your most important troubleshooting tool. You can buy a basic digital multimeter for $10-$20. It measures voltage (V), current (A), resistance (ohms), and continuity. For solar work, you need DC voltage up to 600V and AC voltage up to 600V. Set the dial to the correct setting. For a 12V battery, use DC volts (V with a straight line). For household AC, use AC volts (V with a wavy line).

To test voltage, plug the black probe into the COM port and the red probe into the VÎ©mA port. Touch the black probe to the negative terminal and the red probe to the positive. The display shows the voltage. For a 12V battery, a full charge is 12.6V. For a 24V system, it's 25.2V. For solar panels, measure open-circuit voltage: a 12V panel should read 18-22V in sun. For AC outlets, insert probes into the socket holes (be careful!). You should read 220-240V in most African countries.

To test continuity, turn the dial to the continuity symbol (a diode or sound wave). Touch the probes together â you should hear a beep. Then touch the probes to the two ends of a wire or fuse. If it beeps, the circuit is complete (good). If no beep, there is a break (bad). Always disconnect power before testing continuity. For fuses, remove them from the circuit. For wires, disconnect at least one end. Continuity testing helps find broken wires, blown fuses, and faulty switches.

To test current, you need to connect the multimeter in series with the circuit. This is more advanced. For most troubleshooting, voltage and continuity are enough. Always start with the highest range and work down. Never measure resistance on a live circuit. Keep your multimeter dry and clean. In Africa, dust and humidity can damage it. Store it in a plastic bag. Practice on a known good battery and wire to build confidence.

6. Emergency Power Backup: What to Do During Outages

During a power outage, your solar inverter should switch to battery power automatically if it's a hybrid or off-grid inverter. But if it doesn't, first check the battery voltage. If it's below 11V for a 12V system, the inverter may not start. You can manually start a generator to charge the battery if you have one. If not, prioritize critical loads: lights, phone charging, and a small fan. Turn off all non-essential appliances to save battery.

If your inverter has a bypass switch, you can switch to grid or generator when power returns. But during an outage, keep the inverter on. If the battery is very low, turn off the inverter and wait for solar charging. In the morning, solar panels will recharge the battery. If you have a lithium battery, it may have a reserve capacity; check the state of charge (SOC) on the BMS display. Do not drain lead-acid batteries below 50% regularly â it shortens their life.

For extended outages, conserve energy. Use LED lights (5-10W) instead of incandescent (60W). Charge phones during the day when solar is available. Use a 12V DC fan instead of a 230V AC fan â it uses less power. If you have a fridge, keep it closed and consider turning it off at night. A typical 12V 100Ah battery can run a 50W load for about 20 hours, but only if fully charged and not drained below 50%. So realistically, 10 hours.

Plan ahead: keep a spare fuse, a multimeter, and a headlamp. Label your circuit breakers. Know how to reset your inverter. If you have a generator, test it monthly. In Africa, outages can last hours or days. A well-maintained solar system with a good battery bank is your best backup. If you frequently run out of power, add more battery capacity or more solar panels. Consider a lithium battery for deeper discharge and longer life. Always have a backup plan: a small petrol generator or a second battery bank.

Practical Reference: Troubleshooting Solar Inverters & Batteries in African Agribusiness

In off-grid and hybrid solar systems powering irrigation pumps, cold rooms, poultry brooders, and agro-processing equipment across Africa, downtime is lost revenue. A failed inverter on a fish farm aeration system or a dairy cooling unit can destroy a day's output. This Practical Reference gives technicians, farm managers, and solar entrepreneurs a structured fault-finding method â plus the measurements, limits, and emergency actions that keep agribusiness running.

Work through the five fault categories below in order. Each maps to a lesson in this module and to a real-world failure pattern common in African installations: undersized battery banks, corroded connections in humid coastal farms, dusty panels in Sahelian conditions, and overloaded inverters running pumps with high surge currents.

1. Inverter Not Turning On: Battery and Fuse First

Ninety percent of "dead inverter" calls in African installations trace to the DC side, not the inverter itself. Start at the battery terminals with a multimeter on DC volts. Next, test the fuse or DC breaker between battery and inverter. With the inverter switched off, set the multimeter to continuity (or resistance). A good fuse reads near 0 Î©; a blown one reads open (OL). In humid coastal farms (Mombasa, Lagos, Dar es Salaam), corroded fuse holders and loose lugs cause intermittent no-starts. 2. Battery Not Charging: Panel, Controller, or Wiring

If the battery voltage is flat every morning, the charging path is broken. In order: (a) clean the panels â dust in Sahelian and savanna conditions can cut output 15â30%; (b) measure array open-circuit voltage (Voc) with the controller disconnected; (c) measure controller output to battery; (d) inspect MC4 connectors and cable glands for water ingress.

A common African fault: undersized DC cable over long runs from a borehole-mounted array. Voltage drop above 3% starves the controller. Check the controller's LED or app status â most MPPT units report "PV low" or "night mode" when the array is disconnected.

3. Appliances Not Working: Load Sizing and Inverter Limits

An inverter that runs a fridge but trips on a borehole pump is not faulty â it is undersized for the surge. Induction motors (pumps, compressors, hammer mills) draw 3â7Ã running current at start. A 1 kW pump can demand 5 kW for a second. Check the inverter's continuous and surge ratings against the appliance nameplate.

Also verify the load is within the inverter's AC output waveform and voltage. Modified sine wave inverters cause motors to run hot and timers to drift. For agribusiness, pure sine wave is non-negotiable.

4. Frequent Shutdowns: Overload, Overheat, Low Voltage

Three root causes dominate:

  • Overload: total running watts exceed inverter continuous rating. Add a clamp meter reading at the AC output.
  • Overheat: dusty fans, blocked vents, or direct sun on the inverter in 35â45 Â°C ambient. Relocate to shade and clean monthly.
  • Low voltage: battery sag under load. Measure DC volts while the load runs â a bank that reads 48 V at rest but 42 V under a 2 kW load has high internal resistance or undersized cables.

5. Using a Multimeter to Test Voltage and Continuity

A basic digital multimeter (US$15â40) is the most valuable tool in an African solar kit. Procedure:

  1. DC volts: red probe to positive, black to negative. Measure battery, array Voc, and controller output.
  2. AC volts: switch to AC volts; measure inverter output at a socket or terminal block.
  3. Continuity: power OFF. Touch probes together to confirm 0 Î©, then test fuses, breakers, and cable ends.
  4. Current (optional): use a clamp meter for DC/AC amps â safer than breaking the circuit.

6. Emergency Power Backup: What to Do During Outages

For agribusiness, outage response is a business continuity plan, not a repair job. Priorities: protect cold chain, keep water/aeration running, and preserve breeding stock.

  • Cold rooms: keep doors closed; a loaded cold room holds temperature 4â6 hours. Shift to backup inverter or generator only for critical compressors.
  • Poultry / fish: battery backup for brooders and aerators is sized in hours, not minutes. A 5 kWh LiFePOâ bank can run a 300 W aerator for ~16 hours.
  • Water pumping: schedule irrigation to daylight hours so the array charges while pumping.
  • Generators: never back-feed a solar inverter without a proper transfer switch â this is a lethal and code-violating practice.
