1. Designing Farrowing and Nursery Units

A farrowing unit is where sows give birth and nurse piglets. It must be dry, warm, and safe for both mother and piglets. In Africa, many farmers use simple pens made from locally available materials like timber, bamboo, or concrete blocks. The floor should be smooth but not slippery, with a slope of 1-2% towards a drain. Each farrowing pen should be at least 2.5 meters by 2 meters, with a creep area for piglets that is heated to 30-32Â°C for the first week. Use a heat lamp or a wooden box with a lid and a 100-watt bulb. The sow needs a resting area that is cool, around 18-20Â°C, so provide shade and ventilation. A farrowing crate can reduce piglet crushing, but if not available, use a rail around the pen walls 20-25 cm from the floor to give piglets an escape zone. The nursery unit is for weaned piglets, typically from 4 to 10 weeks old. Keep them in groups of 10-15, with 0.3-0.4 square meters per piglet. The floor should be easy to clean, such as concrete with a slight slope to a urine drain. Provide a warm sleeping area at 26-28Â°C and a cooler dunging area. Use solid dividers between pens to prevent drafts and disease spread. Ensure there is a footbath at the entrance and hand-washing station. Natural lighting is good, but avoid direct sunlight that can overheat. In hot climates, orient the building east-west to reduce sun exposure on the long sides. Use local materials like mud bricks, thatch, or corrugated iron, but ensure they are predator-proof and secure. Always include a small storage area for feed and tools within the unit to reduce movement in and out. Clean and disinfect between batches to break disease cycles.

2. Constructing Grow-Finish Barns

Grow-finish barns house pigs from about 10 weeks old until market weight, usually 90-110 kg. These pigs need more space: 0.8-1.0 square meters per pig in a fully slatted system, or 1.2-1.5 square meters in a bedded system. In Africa, many use open-sided barns with deep litter or concrete floors. The floor should be concrete, at least 10 cm thick, with a 2% slope to a drain. For deep litter, use dry sawdust, wood shavings, or chopped straw, and add fresh material weekly. The walls can be low (1.2 meters) with wire mesh above for ventilation. The roof should be high (3-4 meters at the eaves) and insulated with thatch or reflective material to reduce heat. In hot climates, consider a ridge vent and open sides. In cooler highland areas, use curtains that can be rolled down. Provide 15-20 cm of trough space per pig for feeding, and one water nipple for every 10-15 pigs. Group pigs by size to reduce fighting. Stocking density is critical: overcrowding leads to poor growth, tail biting, and disease. A common mistake is to keep too many pigs in a small space. For a 20-pig pen, you need at least 24 square meters. Build a loading ramp at one end for easy transport to market. Use local materials like eucalyptus poles, bamboo, and mud bricks to cut costs, but ensure the structure is strong enough to withstand wind and rain. Always include a concrete apron around the barn to prevent mud and rodents. Clean the barn daily, removing wet bedding and manure. Disinfect weekly with a 2% caustic soda solution or commercial disinfectant. Keep records of feed intake and weight gain to monitor performance.

3. Ventilation and Temperature Control Systems

Pigs are sensitive to heat. Unlike humans, they cannot sweat effectively, so they need help to stay cool. The ideal temperature for a grow-finish pig is 18-22Â°C, for a lactating sow 18-20Â°C, and for piglets 30-32Â°C in the first week. In hot African climates, temperatures often exceed 35Â°C, causing heat stress. Signs include panting, reduced feed intake, and poor growth. To control temperature, use natural ventilation: open-sided buildings with high roofs, ridge vents, and large openings on the prevailing wind side. For every 10 pigs, provide at least 0.5 square meters of inlet area and 0.3 square meters of outlet area. If natural ventilation is not enough, use fans. A 50-cm diameter fan can move 10,000 cubic meters of air per hour, enough for 50-60 grow-finish pigs. In very hot areas, use evaporative cooling pads or foggers. A simple fogger system uses nozzles that spray fine water droplets; this can lower temperature by 5-10Â°C. For piglets, provide a heated creep area with a heat lamp or a wooden box with a 100-watt bulb. In cold highland areas, use curtains, deep bedding, and maybe a small stove, but ensure carbon monoxide does not build up. Always monitor temperature with a simple thermometer at pig level. Humidity should be 50-70%; too high causes respiratory problems, too low causes dust. Ventilation also removes ammonia and moisture. Ammonia levels should be below 20 ppm; if your eyes water when you enter, it's too high. Use a simple ammonia detector or your nose. Adjust ventilation by opening or closing curtains, vents, or doors. In deep litter systems, ventilation is critical to keep litter dry. Stir litter weekly and add fresh material. During the rainy season, ensure drainage to avoid flooding. In all cases, provide shade and plenty of cool drinking water.

4. Waste Management and Manure Handling

A pig produces about 4-6 kg of manure per day, which is a valuable resource but also a potential pollutant. In Africa, many farmers use pig manure to fertilize crops, but it must be handled properly to avoid disease and flies. The first step is to collect manure daily. In concrete-floor barns, scrape or flush manure into a drain that leads to a collection pit. In deep litter systems, remove wet litter and add fresh bedding. The collection pit should be concrete, covered, and located away from the barn to reduce odors and flies. For liquid manure, use a biogas digester. A simple plastic bag digester or fixed-dome digester can produce biogas for cooking and lighting. For a 10-pig farm, a 6-cubic-meter digester can produce enough gas for 2-3 hours of cooking per day. The effluent from the digester is a rich fertilizer. If you don't have a digester, compost the manure. Mix manure with crop residues like maize stalks or rice straw, and turn the pile every week. After 4-6 weeks, it becomes compost. For solid manure, you can also dry it and sell it as organic fertilizer. Never dump manure into rivers or streams; it causes water pollution and spreads diseases. Also, avoid spreading fresh manure on fields; it can burn plants and spread parasites. Store manure in a covered area to prevent rain from washing nutrients away. Use gloves and boots when handling manure, and wash hands after. Keep the manure storage area away from the pig barn (at least 30 meters) to reduce flies and disease transmission. In intensive systems, consider a lagoon for liquid waste, but ensure it is lined to prevent groundwater contamination. Regularly check for leaks and repair. Good waste management also reduces odors, which improves neighbor relations and pig health.

5. Biosecurity Infrastructure and Layout

Biosecurity means keeping diseases out of your farm. The layout of your pig housing is your first line of defense. Start with a perimeter fence at least 1.5 meters high to keep out people and animals. The entrance should have a gate with a lock and a sign saying 'No Entry Without Permission'. Provide a footbath at the entrance of each building: a shallow concrete basin filled with disinfectant (e.g., 2% caustic soda or commercial virucide). Change the disinfectant daily. Also, have a hand-washing station with soap and water. If possible, have a changing room where workers and visitors put on farm-specific boots and coveralls. The layout should follow a flow: from clean to dirty. That means the feed storage and nursery should be at the entrance, and the grow-finish and sick pens at the back. Never move from sick pens to healthy pens without changing clothes and boots. Keep different age groups in separate buildings at least 10 meters apart. This prevents disease spread between batches. Use solid walls between pens to prevent nose-to-nose contact. For new pigs, have a quarantine pen at least 50 meters from the main herd. Quarantine for 30 days and monitor for signs of disease. Keep feed in closed bins to prevent rodents and birds. Control flies with screens and traps. Dispose of dead pigs immediately by burning or deep burial (at least 1 meter deep) away from water sources. Keep records of all visitors and their contact with pigs. In African contexts, many farms are small and mixed with other livestock; keep pigs separate from poultry and cattle if possible. If you have a vehicle, park it outside the fence. Always wash and disinfect boots and equipment after use. A simple biosecurity plan can save you from devastating diseases like African Swine Fever, for which there is no vaccine.

6. Equipment for Feeding and Watering

Pigs need constant access to clean water and feed. For watering, the simplest method is a nipple drinker connected to a tank. Each nipple serves 10-15 pigs. Nipples should be placed at shoulder height of the pigs: for weaners at 30 cm, for growers at 45 cm, and for finishers at 60 cm. Check water flow daily; a pig drinks 5-10 liters per day, more in hot weather. Use a water tank of at least 100 liters for 10 pigs, and clean it weekly. For feeding, you can use troughs or self-feeders. Troughs are cheaper but require more labor. A concrete trough 30 cm wide and 15 cm deep can feed 10 pigs; allow 20-30 cm of trough space per pig. Feed twice daily, and remove leftovers to prevent spoilage. Self-feeders save labor and reduce waste. A simple wooden or metal hopper with a trough at the bottom can hold 50 kg of feed. Adjust the opening so feed flows freely but doesn't spill. For piglets, use creep feeders with small openings. Always provide feed on a clean surface; never feed on the floor where manure is. In Africa, you can make feeders from local materials like clay pots, wooden planks, or PVC pipes. For example, cut a PVC pipe lengthwise and attach it to a wooden frame. For water, you can use a plastic drum with nipples. Ensure all equipment is easy to clean and disinfect. Remove feed leftovers daily to prevent mold. Store feed in a dry, cool place, off the floor on pallets, to prevent aflatoxin. Use a simple scale to weigh feed for accurate rationing. Keep records of feed intake per pen to detect health issues early. Clean water and proper feeding equipment are the backbone of good pig performance.
