Poultry Farming

A complete 6-month program running broilers and layers side by side — brooding discipline, vaccination, feed maths and staggered batches that turn 100 chicks into a poultry business.

⏱️ 6 Months (8–12 hrs/week)📊 Beginner → Business-ready🌍 Hybrid: Online + Offline🎓 Certificate on completion

Course Overview

Why 6 months? Six months lets you run poultry's two money machines side by side: FOUR complete broiler cycles (each just 6 weeks from day-old chick to sale) while a batch of layer pullets grows toward point-of-lay at 18–20 weeks. Broilers teach you speed and cash flow; layers teach you steady daily income. By graduation you have real sales, real records, and a bankable plan.

Structure: 6 monthly modules → lessons → practical assignments → a 10-question quiz each month → final business project.

How to study (hybrid): Online — modules on your phone, sessions in the Study Diary, questions to the rAs AI assistant. Offline — save this page (Print → Save as PDF); the practicals happen in the brooder and at the market.

⚠️ Read first: the three rules that protect your investment

  • Newcastle disease can kill an entire unvaccinated flock in days. Vaccination on schedule (Month 3) is not optional anywhere in Africa. Sudden mass deaths are notifiable — report, never rush sick birds to market.
  • The first 7 days decide the whole cycle. A chilled, thirsty day-old chick never recovers the lost growth. Brooding discipline is where poultry profit is made or lost.
  • Feed is 65–70% of your cost. Buy the right ration for the right age, from 3-quote-checked suppliers, and never let feeders run empty.

Lesson 1.2 — Don't ignore the indigenous chicken

  • Village/indigenous chickens ("road runners", Kuroilers/Sasso improved types) grow slower but sell at a 30–100% price premium per kg in most African markets — consumers pay for the taste.
  • Low-input model: scavenging + small supplement, natural brooding — slower money, tiny feed bill, strong disease tolerance (but still vaccinate for Newcastle!).
  • Improved dual-purpose (Kuroiler, Sasso, Boschveld): nearly village-tough with double the growth and eggs — the smart semi-intensive middle path.

Lesson 1.3 — The key production numbers

  • Broiler targets: day-old 40–45 g → week 6 at 1.8–2.5 kg; FCR 1.6–1.9; survival 95%+; total feed ~3.5–4 kg per bird to 6 weeks.
  • Layer targets: first egg 18–20 weeks; peak lay 90%+ at 25–30 weeks; ~280–320 eggs per hen per year; feed 110–120 g/hen/day in lay.
  • Temperature: chicks need 32–34°C in week 1, dropping ~3°C per week until feathered (~21°C); adult birds suffer above 32°C — heat stress kills more African broilers than cold ever will.
  • Water: birds drink roughly 1.6–2× the weight of the feed they eat — 100 broilers at week 5 empty 25–35 L a day.

Lesson 1.4 — Planning your unit

  • Starter scale that teaches without sinking you: 100 broilers per cycle, or 100 layer pullets — one person, part-time, real economics.
  • Market check BEFORE chicks: who buys 100 dressed birds or 7 trays of eggs a day within your reach? Get names and prices in the record book first.
  • Chick source: order from a reputable hatchery/agent 2–3 weeks ahead; vaccinated parent stock and Marek's-vaccinated chicks are worth the small premium.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 1): (1) Survey your market: 3 chicken buyers and 3 egg buyers with prices and volumes, in writing. (2) Visit one broiler and one layer unit; record their feed brands, losses and selling prices. (3) Choose your path (broilers first is the default) and book a hatchery quote for 100 chicks.

MONTH 2 — Housing & Equipment ▾

Lesson 2.1 — The deep-litter house (Africa's workhorse)

  • Stocking density: finish broilers at 8–10 birds/m² (hot climates use the lower figure); layers on floor at 5–7 birds/m². 100 broilers therefore need a 10–12 m² house.
  • Orientation east–west; low side walls (0.5–1 m) topped with wire mesh for cross-ventilation; roof overhangs ≥0.5 m against sun and rain.
  • Litter: 5–10 cm of dry wood shavings/chopped straw over a sealed (ideally concrete) floor — litter must stay DRY; wet litter breeds coccidiosis and ammonia.
  • Curtains (sacks/plastic) on the mesh: closed for chicks and cold nights, opened for heat — your free climate control.

Lesson 2.2 — The brooder: a house within the house

  • A brooder ring (cardboard/mats, ~60 cm high) keeps chicks near heat, feed and water — round, so no corners to pile up and smother in.
  • Heat sources: infrared lamp, charcoal stove (jiko) with a guard, or gas brooder — sized so chicks can move toward and away from warmth.
  • Read the chicks, not the thermometer: huddled under the heat = cold; pressed to the ring edge, panting = hot; spread evenly, chirping contentedly = perfect.
  • Space per 100 chicks: start ~2 m² inside the ring, widening every few days.

Lesson 2.3 — Feeders, drinkers and layer extras

  • Height rule: feeder and drinker lips level with the birds' backs — raises with the flock weekly; it halves feed spillage.

Lesson 2.4 — Biosecurity built into the building

  • Footbath at the single door; house sited away from other poultry; wild-bird-proof mesh (Newcastle and worse arrive on wild wings).
  • All-in, all-out: one age group per house; after each batch — litter out, wash, disinfect, REST 1–2 weeks. Mixed ages are a disease bridge.
  • Rodent control around the feed store: rats eat feed by the bag and carry disease between houses.

MONTH 3 — Brooding, Vaccination & the First Broiler Cycle ▾

Lesson 3.1 — Chick arrival day (hour by hour)

  • Brooder pre-heated 24 hours to 32–34°C; drinkers filled with clean water 2–3 hours ahead (room-temperature, not icy).
  • First drink before first feed: dip a few beaks gently to teach the flock; stress-pack/glucose + vitamins in the first water helps travel-stressed chicks.
  • Feed on trays/paper 1–2 hours after water; count the chicks, remove any dead-on-arrival for the supplier claim, and log everything.
  • Crop check that evening: a soft, full crop on 95% of chicks = eating and drinking established; empty crops = fix access NOW.

Lesson 3.2 — The vaccination program (the non-negotiable)

Age (typical)VaccineRoute
Day old (hatchery)Marek's (layers especially)Hatchery injection
Day 7–10Newcastle (e.g. Lasota/ND) + IBEye-drop or drinking water
Day 12–14Gumboro (IBD)Drinking water
Day 18–21Gumboro repeatDrinking water
Day 21–28Newcastle repeatEye-drop/water
Layers ongoingND boosters ~every 2–3 months; fowl pox, others per local vetVarious
  • Confirm the exact local schedule with your vet/agro-dealer — disease pressure differs by area.
  • Water-vaccination rules: withhold water 1–2 hours first, use clean unchlorinated water (a little milk powder protects the vaccine), and size the water so it's drunk within 1–2 hours.
  • Cold chain: vaccines live in the fridge/cool box until the minute of use — a warm vaccine is expensive water.

Lesson 3.3 — Weeks 1–6 broiler routine

  • Week 1: 24 h light, heat discipline, paper/tray feeding, stir litter daily. Target ~4× hatch weight by day 7 (160–180 g).
  • Weeks 2–3: widen the ring away, swap to tube feeders and bell drinkers, start lowering temperature, switch starter→grower per the bag schedule.
  • Weeks 4–6: heat stress watch (open curtains, cool water, feed in cool hours), grower→finisher, weigh a 10-bird sample weekly and log average weight.
  • Daily always: fresh water, feed topped, dead removed and recorded, litter dry, birds' sound (sneezing? gurgling?) noted.

Lesson 3.4 — Growing layer pullets alongside

  • Same brooding, then a different goal: FRAME, not fat — chick mash to 8 weeks, grower mash 8–18 weeks, restricted to keep pullets lean and on the strain's weight curve.
  • Light discipline: never increase day-length during rearing (it triggers early, tiny eggs); increase light only from 17–18 weeks toward 16 h in lay.
  • Move pullets to the layer house by 16–17 weeks so they settle before first eggs.

(2) Run the complete vaccination schedule with your local vet's dates — file the empty vaccine vials with dates as your records. (3) Weigh 10 birds weekly and plot the growth curve against the 6-week target.

Lesson 4.1 — The ration ladder

  • The classic fatal error: feeding growing pullets LAYER mash — its high calcium damages young kidneys. Calcium arrives only at point of lay.

Lesson 4.2 — Feed maths you must own

  • Broiler cycle: 100 birds × ~3.8 kg = ~380 kg ≈ 7.6 × 50 kg bags per cycle. Bag prices × 7.6 = your main cost, known before the chicks arrive.
  • Layers: 100 hens × 115 g = 11.5 kg/day ≈ one 50 kg bag every 4.3 days ≈ 85 bags/year.
  • Feed-to-egg maths: a hen eats ~42 kg/year and lays ~300 eggs → ~140 g feed per egg. If 140 g of feed costs more than one egg sells for, fix price or feed before expanding.

Lesson 4.3 — Cutting feed cost without cutting growth

  • Buy the best, not the cheapest: a $1-cheaper bag that adds 0.2 to FCR costs you more than it saved — judge feed by the weigh-scale, not the price tag.
  • Home mixing (broiler grower guide): ~60% maize meal, ~25% soybean meal/cake, ~10% bran, + broiler premix at the stated rate, salt, DCP — only with a proper premix and accurate scale (see rAs Animal Feed Making).
  • Layers love greens: hanging vegetables, duckweed and kitchen-garden extras cut boredom, deepen yolk colour and trim feed by 5–10% — supplements, never substitutes.
  • Stop the thieves: rats, wild birds, spillage and over-full feeders quietly eat 5–15% of many farms' feed — fill feeders ⅔ full only.

Lesson 4.4 — Water: the forgotten feed

  • A 10% drop in water intake shows as a growth/egg drop within 48 hours — check drinkers morning AND afternoon.
  • Hot-day protocol: extra drinkers, cool shaded water, feed in early morning and evening; heat-stressed birds panting with wings out need airflow and cool water immediately.
✅ Iron-Clad Price Check: your feed supplier decision is a 7.6-bags-per-cycle (broilers) or 85-bags-per-year (layers) contract — 3 supplier quotes, delivered price, every quarter.

  • The one-hour rule: a snick heard or blood seen in droppings today is treated today — poultry diseases move at flock speed, not animal speed.

Lesson 5.2 — Managing hens in lay

  • Point of lay: first small eggs at 18–20 weeks; peak (90%+) at 25–30 weeks; a good flock still lays 70–75% at 60 weeks.
  • Light: 16 hours total (natural + lamp on a timer) — never decreasing in lay, or production crashes.
  • Collect eggs 2–3× daily into clean trays, pointed end DOWN; cool dark storage; dirty eggs dry-cleaned (washing removes the protective bloom).
  • Egg-drop detective list (work in order): water failure → feed change/shortage → light decrease → disease → stress event (predator, noise, new people) → hidden nests/egg-eating.

Lesson 5.3 — Behaviour problems and fixes

  • Pecking/cannibalism: caused by crowding, heat, boredom, light too bright, protein shortage — fix causes, hang greens, darken nests; remove victims to heal.
  • Floor eggs: more nest boxes, darker nests, collect often, block corners; train pullets with ceramic/decoy eggs at point of lay.
  • Broodiness (hens sitting): normal in dual-purpose breeds — either give her fertile eggs to hatch (free chicks) or break it with a bright airy pen for 3–4 days.

Lesson 5.4 — Records that run the unit

  • Broiler cycle card: chicks in, daily deaths, weekly weights, feed bags, sale weights and prices → FCR and margin per cycle.
  • Layer daily sheet: eggs collected, feed given, deaths, notes → hen-day % (eggs ÷ hens alive × 100) tracked on one graph that tells the whole story.

MONTH 6 — Selling, Economics & Your Business Plan ▾

Lesson 6.1 — Selling broilers

  • Channels: live at farm gate (lowest work), live to traders (volume, lower price), dressed to butcheries/restaurants/braai stands (highest margin — needs slaughter hygiene and often licensing), and pre-orders from month-end salary buyers.
  • Sell the WHOLE batch inside 7–10 days of target weight: every extra day is ~65 g of feed per bird for little gain — the classic profit leak.
  • Dressed yield ~70–75% of live weight; price both ways and sell whichever pays more per bird.

Lesson 6.2 — Selling eggs

  • Ladder of margins: farm gate < tuckshops/vendors < offices & salons (weekly standing orders) < bakeries/restaurants (volume contracts).
  • Grade by size, sell in clean trays, keep a delivery day — reliability, not price, wins standing orders.
  • Spent hens (after 72–80 weeks) sell as stew birds — price them into your plan; they're a whole extra payday.

Lesson 6.4 — Scaling without breaking

  • Scale in proven steps: 100 → 200 → 500 broilers per cycle, adding a second house (all-in all-out preserved) before adding numbers.
  • Staggered broiler batches (new chicks every 2–3 weeks across houses) turn lump sums into near-weekly income — the real unlock for shop and restaurant contracts.
  • Add the value chain as you grow: home slaughter/dressing (licensed), manure sales, and eventually your own feed mixing.

Lesson 6.5 — Final project: your bankable business plan

  • Assemble from six months of real cycle cards and daily sheets: chosen model (broilers, layers or both), housing plan, vaccination calendar, feed strategy with quotes, your actual FCR/hen-day results, buyer list with prices, risk plan (Newcastle response, feed price spikes, heat waves), and a 3-year scaling ladder.
  • Submit with photos and records for your rAs certificate — the same file is your loan, grant or partnership application.

📖 Glossary

  • Broiler — fast meat chicken (6–7 week cycle); layer — egg hen; pullet — young female before lay.
  • Point of lay — 18–20 weeks, first eggs.
  • Hen-day % — eggs ÷ live hens × 100.
  • FCR — kg feed per kg live weight.
  • Brooding — the heated first weeks of a chick's life.
  • Crop — the food pouch at the chick's chest; full = eating.
  • All-in all-out — one age per house with full cleanout between.
  • Coccidiostat — feed additive suppressing coccidiosis.
  • Bloom — the egg's natural protective coating (don't wash off).
  • Spent hen — layer at end of lay, sold as stew bird.
