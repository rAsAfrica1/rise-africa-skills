1. Common Diseases of Guinea Fowl and Quail in Africa (Newcastle, Coccidiosis, etc.)

Newcastle disease is the number one killer of guinea fowl and quail in Africa. It spreads through droppings, nasal discharge, and contaminated feed or water. Symptoms include gasping, twisting of the neck, paralysis, and sudden death. There is no cure. Vaccinate chicks at day 7 with the NDV vaccine (I-2 or La Sota strain) via eye drop, then repeat at 4 weeks and every 3 months. Buy vaccines from a reputable agro-vet shop and keep them cold in a flask with ice.

Coccidiosis is caused by a protozoan parasite that attacks the gut. It thrives in wet, dirty litter. Birds become weak, have bloody or mucoid droppings, and stop eating. Treat with amprolium (e.g., Amprol 20% powder) at 1 gram per litre of drinking water for 5 days. Prevent it by keeping litter dry, using raised feeders, and adding coccidiostat to feed for the first 8 weeks.

Fowl pox shows as scabs on the comb, wattle, and face. It spreads by mosquitoes. Vaccinate at 8 weeks with fowl pox vaccine. Treat wounds with iodine and keep birds comfortable. Marekâs disease causes paralysis and tumours; vaccinate day-old chicks at the hatchery. Quail are also prone to ulcerative enteritis (quail disease), causing sudden death and white droppings. Use tetracycline in water for 5 days and improve hygiene.

Respiratory diseases like infectious bronchitis and CRD (chronic respiratory disease) cause coughing, sneezing, and swollen eyes. Use Tylosin or Oxytetracycline in water for 3â5 days. Always consult a vet for proper diagnosis. Remember, most diseases enter through dirty boots, equipment, or new birds. Quarantine new stock for 2 weeks and always buy from certified hatcheries.

In Africa, heat stress and poor nutrition weaken birds, making them vulnerable. Provide shade, fresh water, and a balanced feed with 20â24% protein for guinea fowl and 24â28% for quail. Keep records of deaths and symptoms to spot patterns early.

2. Recognizing Sick Birds: Symptoms and Immediate Actions

A healthy guinea fowl or quail is alert, moves quickly, has bright eyes, and clean feathers. A sick bird separates from the flock, sits with drooping wings, and may have ruffled feathers. Check the flock at least twice dailyâearly morning and eveningâwhen birds are calm. Look for these signs: sneezing, coughing, gasping, or a swollen head. Droppings should be firm and greyish-green with a white cap; bloody, watery, or yellow droppings mean trouble.

Other symptoms include loss of appetite, weight loss, pale comb or wattles, and reduced egg production. In quail, a common sign is a sudden drop in egg laying followed by death. If you see a bird with a twisted neck or paralysis, suspect Newcastle disease. If birds have scabs on the face, itâs likely fowl pox. Bloody droppings point to coccidiosis. Take a photo or note the symptoms.

Immediate actions: Isolate the sick bird in a separate cage or pen, away from the flock. Wear gloves and wash hands after handling. Do not move sick birds to other farms. Give fresh water with electrolytes or glucose (1 teaspoon sugar in 1 litre water) to prevent dehydration. If you suspect a bacterial infection, start a broad-spectrum antibiotic like Oxytetracycline (1g per litre) while waiting for vet advice. Never use antibiotics without a vetâs guidance if you plan to sell eggs or meat.

Call your local vet or agro-vet shop immediately. Describe the symptoms and ask for a diagnosis. If the bird dies, bury it deep (1 metre) or burn itânever eat it. Disinfect the isolation area with a 1% bleach solution. Check the rest of the flock for similar signs. Keep a daily log of sick and dead birds. Early action saves the flock and your money.

3. Using Local Herbs and Remedies for Minor Ailments

Many African farmers use local herbs to treat minor ailments because they are cheap and available. For minor wounds, clean with warm salt water (1 teaspoon salt in 1 litre) and apply aloe vera gel. Aloe has antibacterial properties. For diarrhoea, give a tea made from guava leaves: boil 10 fresh leaves in 1 litre of water, cool, and offer as drinking water for 2 days. Guava leaves contain tannins that soothe the gut.

For respiratory issues like mild coughing, use eucalyptus leaves. Boil a handful in 2 litres of water, let the steam fill a small room or box where the birds are, but ensure ventilation. Alternatively, add crushed garlic (2 cloves per litre) to drinking water. Garlic has natural antimicrobial and immune-boosting effects. For external parasites like lice and mites, dust birds with wood ash or diatomaceous earth. Rub ash into feathers, avoiding eyes and beak.

For coccidiosis prevention, some farmers use papaya seeds: dry and grind 5 seeds, mix with 1 kg feed, twice a week. Papaya seeds contain carpaine, which may reduce parasites. For stress or heat, offer fresh moringa leavesâthey are rich in vitamins and minerals. You can also add 1 tablespoon of honey per litre of water for energy.

Important: Herbs are for minor ailments only. They are not a substitute for vaccines or vet care. If a bird does not improve within 48 hours, call a vet. Never use herbs on birds you plan to sell within a week, as residues may remain. Always test on a small group first. Document what works for your farm. Combine herbs with good hygiene and proper nutrition for best results.

4. Biosecurity on a Small Farm: Keeping Disease Out

Biosecurity means keeping germs away from your birds. Start with a footbath at the entrance of your pen. Use a plastic basin with 2% bleach or 1% formalin solution. Every person must dip their feet before entering. Keep a pair of boots and overalls only for the poultry house. Do not wear them outside. Visitors should not enter the pen unless necessary; if they do, they must wear clean boots and overalls.

Quarantine all new birds for at least 14 days in a separate cage 10 metres away from the main flock. Watch for any signs of illness. Test for Newcastle and other diseases if possible. Never mix new birds with the flock immediately. Keep different age groups separateâchicks, growers, and adultsâbecause older birds can carry diseases without showing symptoms.

Clean and disinfect feeders and drinkers daily with soap and water, then rinse with a bleach solution (1 tablespoon per 5 litres). Remove wet litter every day. Store feed in sealed containers away from rodents. Control rats and wild birdsâthey carry diseases. Use wire mesh to cover windows and doors. Keep the area around the pen free of bushes and stagnant water to reduce mosquitoes.

Limit traffic: only one person should care for the birds. If you must visit other farms, change clothes and shower before entering your pen. After an outbreak, cull all sick birds, burn or bury carcasses deep, and disinfect the entire pen with 2% caustic soda. Wait 2 weeks before restocking. Vaccinate on schedule. Biosecurity is cheaper than treating disease.

5. Parasite Control: Deworming and External Parasites

Internal parasites (worms) are common in guinea fowl and quail, especially when they forage on the ground. Signs include weight loss, pale comb, diarrhoea, and reduced egg production. Deworm every 3 months with Levamisole (e.g., 1 ml per litre of water for 1 day) or Albendazole (1 ml per 2 litres for 3 days). For quail, use half the dose. Always follow the withdrawal period: 7 days for meat, 14 days for eggs. Give dewormer in the morning when birds are thirsty.

External parasites include lice, mites, fleas, and ticks. They cause itching, feather loss, and anaemia. Check under wings and around the vent weekly. For mites, dust birds with permethrin powder (follow label) or use a spray. For lice, use a 1% ivermectin solution applied on the skin at the back of the neck. Treat all birds in the flock at the same time. Clean the pen thoroughly: remove old litter, spray with 2% bleach, and let it dry before adding fresh litter.

Prevent parasites by rotating grazing areas if free-range. Keep litter dry. Add wood ash to dust bathsâbirds will dust themselves to kill mites. For quail in cages, clean trays daily. Use diatomaceous earth in nesting boxes. If you see tapeworms (white, flat segments in droppings), use Praziquantel at 10 mg per kg body weight. Consult a vet for proper dosing.

Never use the same dewormer repeatedly; rotate between Levamisole and Albendazole to prevent resistance. Record deworming dates. After deworming, give vitamins (e.g., Vita-Stress) in water for 3 days to reduce stress. Healthy birds are less susceptible to parasites.

6. Working with Local Vet Services and Agro-Vet Shops

A good relationship with your local vet and agro-vet shop is essential. Find the nearest vet office or animal health assistant. Ask for their phone number and emergency hours. Many districts have a veterinary officer who can visit your farm for a small fee. If you cannot find one, contact the nearest agricultural extension officer. They can connect you to vet services.

At the agro-vet shop, buy only certified vaccines and drugs. Check expiry dates. For vaccines, ensure they are stored in a cold chainâbring a cooler with ice packs. Buy from shops that keep vaccines in a refrigerator, not on a shelf. Ask for a receipt and keep it. For antibiotics, buy only what you need for the current treatment. Never buy unlabelled drugs or those in unsealed containers.

When you call a vet, describe symptoms clearly: how many birds are sick, what you see (droppings, breathing, behaviour), and when it started. If possible, take a short video. The vet may ask you to bring a dead bird for post-mortem. This costs little and gives a precise diagnosis. Follow the vetâs prescription exactlyâdo not change doses.

For routine services, schedule vaccinations and deworming with your vet. Some vets offer training on biosecurity. Attend farmer field days organised by agro-vet shops. They often demonstrate proper vaccine handling. Build a network: share contacts with other farmers. If you suspect a notifiable disease like Newcastle or Avian Influenza, report to the vet immediately. Early reporting can save your communityâs flocks. Keep a farm health record book and update it after every vet visit.

Practical Reference: Health, Disease Prevention and Biosecurity for Guinea Fowl & Quail



In African smallholder and semi-commercial poultry systems, guinea fowl and quail are increasingly kept as income-generating enterprises. Guinea fowl are hardy, semi-wild birds valued for meat, eggs and tick control, while quail are kept for rapid meat and egg production. However, both species are vulnerable to infectious and parasitic diseases that can wipe out a flock within days if not managed properly. This practical reference gives you a business-first approach to health management: preventing losses, protecting your investment, and knowing when to act.



Disease prevention is more profitable than treatment. A single Newcastle disease outbreak can kill 50â90% of an unvaccinated flock. Coccidiosis can reduce growth rates and egg production by 20â40%. Parasites silently drain feed conversion efficiency. Every kwacha, naira or shilling spent on biosecurity, vaccination and deworming returns multiples in saved birds and stable market supply.





1. Common Diseases of Guinea Fowl and Quail in Africa


Guinea fowl and quail share many diseases with chickens, but their management differs. Guinea keets are highly susceptible to Newcastle disease and coccidiosis in the first 4â6 weeks. Quail, especially Japanese quail, are susceptible to Newcastle, coccidiosis, ulcerative enteritis, and mycoplasmosis. In Africa, the most economically damaging diseases are Newcastle disease, coccidiosis, fowl pox, and bacterial infections such as fowl cholera and pullorum.

Early recognition is critical. A sick bird is a business warning. Learn to spot the signs during your daily walk-through. Guinea fowl are masters at hiding illness until it is advanced, so observe behaviour, droppings, and feed/water intake.

Newcastle disease and avian influenza can infect humans in rare cases. Always wear gloves when handling sick or dead birds, wash hands with soap, and bury or burn carcasses deep away from water sources. Report unusual mortality to your local veterinary officer immediately.


3. Using Local Herbs and Remedies for Minor Ailments


Across Africa, farmers use local plants for minor poultry health issues. While these can support flock health, they are not substitutes for vaccines or vet-prescribed drugs. Use them as complementary care for mild conditions, and always confirm with your vet.



  • Aloe vera: Soothing for minor wounds and skin infections. Apply gel topically.

  • Neem (Azadirachta indica): Leaves and seed oil repel external parasites. Crush leaves and add to nesting material.

  • Garlic and ginger: Natural antimicrobials. Add crushed to drinking water during stress periods (1 clove per litre).

  • Black pepper and chili: Used in some regions to stimulate appetite and respiratory clearing. Use sparingly.

  • Moringa leaves: Nutritional supplement, supports immune function. Dry and add to feed at 2â5%.


Business note: Herbal remedies reduce input costs but do not replace vaccination. A flock vaccinated against Newcastle and dewormed regularly will always outperform a flock relying only on herbs.



4. Biosecurity on a Small Farm: Keeping Disease Out


Biosecurity is your cheapest insurance. Implement these measures consistently:



  • Fence the compound: Prevent wild birds, rodents and stray poultry from entering.

  • Footbath at entrance: Use disinfectant (e.g., 2% sodium hypochlorite) and refresh daily.

  • Quarantine new birds: 14â21 days in a separate pen before mixing.

  • All-in/all-out: Where possible, keep age groups separate.

  • Clean feed and water: Store feed in sealed containers, use clean drinkers, avoid spillage.

  • Visitor control: Limit visitors, provide boots and overalls, no visits to other poultry farms same day.

  • Dead bird disposal: Burn or deep bury immediately; never feed carcasses to other animals.



5. Parasite Control: Deworming and External Parasites


Internal parasites (roundworms, tapeworms, coccidia) and external parasites (mites, lice, ticks) reduce growth, egg production and feed efficiency. A regular programme is essential.



  • Deworming: Every 6â8 weeks for guinea fowl; every 4â6 weeks for quail in intensive systems. Use vet-approved dewormers (e.g., piperazine, levamisole, albendazole). Follow withdrawal periods before selling meat or eggs.

  • Coccidiosis control: Use coccidiostats in feed or water during high-risk periods (rainy season, after stress).

  • External parasites: Dust baths with wood ash and sand; neem leaf powder; occasional use of approved acaricides. Check under wings and around vent weekly.

  • Record keeping: Note dates, products and doses. This helps you track costs and effectiveness.



6. Working with Local Vet Services and Agro-Vet Shops


Your vet and agro-vet shop are business partners, not just emergency contacts. Build relationships before a crisis.



  • Identify your nearest vet: Government veterinary office, private clinic, or NGO-supported livestock programme.

  • Buy from licensed agro-vet shops: Avoid counterfeit vaccines and drugs. Check expiry dates and storage conditions (cold chain for vaccines).

  • Ask for training: Many agro-vet shops offer free advice on vaccination schedules and deworming.

  • Keep records: Share mortality and treatment records with your vet for better diagnosis.

  • Participate in farmer groups: Bulk buying of vaccines and drugs reduces costs.



Quick Reference Checklist



  1. Vaccinate guinea fowl and quail against Newcastle disease at the recommended schedule (e.g., day 7, day 28, then every 3â4 months).

  2. Deworm every 6â8 weeks (guinea fowl) or 4â6 weeks (quail) using vet-approved products.

  3. Check birds daily for symptoms: ruffled feathers, bloody droppings, twisted neck, sudden deaths.

  4. Isolate sick birds immediately in a separate, well-ventilated pen.

  5. Maintain a footbath with fresh disinfectant at the entrance to each pen.

  6. Quarantine all new birds for at least 14 days before mixing with the flock.

  7. Keep feed and water clean; store feed in sealed, rodent-proof containers.

  8. Dispose of dead birds by burning or deep burial; never sell or consume sick birds.

  9. Record all treatments, vaccinations and mortalities in a farm notebook.

  10. Establish contact with a local vet and licensed agro-vet shop before problems arise.
