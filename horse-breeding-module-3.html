1. Conformation Evaluation and Scoring

Conformation is the physical structure of the horse. Good conformation means the horse can move efficiently, stay sound, and pass those traits to foals. You don't need a judge's eye to start. Learn to score 10 body parts from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent). Stand the horse on flat ground, ideally concrete or hard dirt. View from the side, front, and rear. For each part, compare to the ideal for the breed. In Africa, local breeds like the Barb, Dongola, and Boerperd have adapted traitsdon't penalize them for not looking like a Thoroughbred.

Start with the head and neck. A medium-sized head with wide forehead and large nostrils is good. Neck should be long and muscular, tying cleanly into the shoulder. Shoulder slope: 45 to 50 degrees is ideal for stride. A steep shoulder (over 55 degrees) gives a choppy gait. Measure with a protractor or smartphone app like Horse Conformation. Back should be short and strongno more than one-third of body length. Long backs weaken under saddle and predispose to kissing spine.

Hindquarters: croup should be level or slightly sloping. A steep croup (over 25 degrees) reduces impulsion. Look for a long, muscular hip. Legs: front legs should be straight when viewed from front; knees should not be offset. From the side, a line from elbow to fetlock should be vertical. Hind legs: hocks should be low and clean. Avoid sickle hocks (curved) or cow hocks (pointing inward). Hooves: large, round, with deep heels. Measure hoof angleshould match shoulder angle (45-50 degrees).

Score each part: 1=severe fault, 2=moderate fault, 3=average, 4=good, 5=excellent. Add scores. A total of 40+ out of 50 is breeding quality. Below 30, do not breed. Take photos from all four sides and use a free app like EquiScore to track. In Africa, heat and rough terrain demand strong hooves and correct legs. Cull any horse with navicular syndrome or ringbone. Remember: conformation is heritable. A mare with crooked legs will likely pass that to her foal. Always evaluate at least three generations if possible.

2. Performance and Athletic Ability Assessment

Performance testing shows what a horse can actually do, not just how it looks. For breeding stock, you want to see natural ability, stamina, and recovery. In Africa, where many horses work on farms or in sport, test under local conditions. Use a simple 5-part assessment: speed, stamina, agility, jumping, and recovery. You don't need a racetrack. A measured 200-meter stretch of safe ground works.

Speed: Time a 200m sprint from a standing start. Use a stopwatch or phone app. For a light horse, under 14 seconds is good; under 12 seconds is excellent. Repeat three times with 5-minute rests. Record best time. Stamina: Trot or canter 2 kilometers. Time it and check heart rate immediately after. Use a stethoscope or a heart rate monitor like Polar H10. A fit horse recovers to under 60 beats per minute (bpm) within 10 minutes. If it stays above 80 bpm after 15 minutes, that's poor.

Agility: Set up a simple obstacle course: 4 poles on the ground, a narrow gap, and a turn. Walk and trot through. Score 1-5 for calmness and accuracy. Knock a pole? Minus 1. Refuse? Minus 2. Jumping: For jumping breeds, free-jump a small fence (60-80 cm). Watch style: front legs tucked, bascule (round back), and landing. A horse that jumps flat and hangs legs is less desirable. Score 1-5.

Recovery: After the 2km test, measure heart rate at 1, 5, and 10 minutes. A drop of 40 bpm in first minute is excellent. Also check respiration: should be under 20 breaths per minute after 10 minutes. In Africa, heat stress matters. Test in early morning or evening. Avoid midday. If a horse pants heavily or sweats excessively, it may not suit your climate.

Keep records for each horse. Compare to breed averages. For example, a Boerperd should trot 2km in under 12 minutes. A Barb horse should sprint 200m in under 13 seconds. If a horse is 20% slower than breed average, do not breed. Athletic ability is 30-40% heritable. Pair performance with conformation scores. A horse with excellent conformation but poor stamina is a poor choice. Always test at least three times on different days to get a true picture.

3. Temperament and Behavior Evaluation

Temperament is how a horse reacts to things. It is highly heritableup to 50%. A nervous, aggressive, or unpredictable horse will pass that to foals. In Africa, where horses often work with cattle, children, or in busy markets, calm temperament is critical. You can evaluate temperament in 30 minutes using a simple scoring system. Do this before buying or breeding.

First, observe the horse in its stall or paddock. Note ears (pinned back = aggression or pain), tail swishing, and pawing. A relaxed horse has soft eyes, ears forward or sideways, and a low head. Approach slowly. Score 1-5 on approach: 5 = walks to you calmly, 1 = runs away or charges. Next, halter and lead. Walk 20 meters, turn, walk back. Score 1-5 for responsiveness: 5 = follows willingly with slack rope, 1 = pulls, rears, or drags.

Then do a 'startle test'. Have a helper drop a feed bucket or open an umbrella 3 meters away. Score reaction: 5 = looks, then relaxes within 5 seconds; 3 = jumps but recovers in 30 seconds; 1 = bolts, sweats, or takes more than 2 minutes to calm. Repeat with a plastic bag tied to a stick waved gently. Never punish fearjust observe.

Next, handle feet. Pick up each hoof. Score 1-5: 5 = lifts willingly, holds for 10 seconds; 1 = kicks, strikes, or pulls away violently. Finally, test with other horses. Turn the horse out with a calm companion. Score 1-5 for social behavior: 5 = eats peacefully, 1 = attacks or is attacked constantly.

Add scores. Total 25 points. 20-25 = excellent breeding temperament. 15-19 = acceptable with training. Below 15 = do not breed. In Africa, avoid breeding horses that are 'hot' or 'spooky'they cause accidents. Also consider local work: a horse that panics around cattle or donkeys is a liability. Test at least twice on different days. Use a simple notebook to record scores. If you can, ask a vet or experienced handler to score with you. Remember: temperament is not fixed by training alone. A foal from a calm mare and calm stallion will likely be calm. Always prioritize temperament over speed or looks for a family horse or workhorse.

4. Veterinary Pre-Purchase Examination for Breeding

Before you buy or breed a horse, you need a vet check. This is not optional. A pre-purchase exam (PPE) for breeding costs $100-$300 in most African countries. It can save you years of heartbreak and money. The exam has 5 stages. Do all of them. Use a licensed veterinarian with horse experience. Ask for a written report.

Stage 1: General health. Vet checks eyes, teeth, heart, lungs, and temperature. Normal temp is 37.5-38.5C. Heart rate 28-40 beats per minute. Respiratory rate 8-16. Check for lumps, wounds, or swelling. Look at eyes for cataracts or uveitis (common in Appaloosas). Teeth: check for sharp points or missing teethbad teeth mean poor condition.

Stage 2: Lameness exam. Vet watches horse walk and trot on hard ground and soft ground. Then flex each leg (joint flexion test) and trot again. Any lameness score of 2 or more (on 0-5 scale) is a red flag. For breeding, mild arthritis may be acceptable if the horse is not ridden, but severe lameness means do not breed. Also check hooves for cracks, abscesses, or laminitis.

Stage 3: Reproductive exam. For mares: rectal palpation and ultrasound to check ovaries, uterus, and cervix. Look for cysts, tumors, or fluid. For stallions: check testicles (size, consistency, symmetry), penis, and semen quality. Semen analysis: volume, concentration, motility, and morphology. Normal stallion: 2-5 billion sperm per ejaculate, >60% motile, >70% normal. If below, do not breed.

Stage 4: Blood tests. Test for Equine Infectious Anemia (EIA/Coggins), Equine Viral Arteritis (EVA), and strangles. In Africa, also test for piroplasmosis (babesia) and African horse sickness if in endemic areas. These diseases can cause abortion or death. Negative results are required.

Stage 5: Genetic testing. For breeds prone to inherited diseases, test DNA. Example: HYPP in Quarter Horses, SCID in Arabians, and DSLD in many breeds. Use a lab like UC Davis or local vet school. Cost $50-$150 per test. If positive, do not breed.

Always get a second opinion if unsure. Never buy a horse without a PPE. Even if you are breeding your own mare, have her checked before covering. A mare with a uterine infection will not conceive. A stallion with poor semen will waste your time. Keep all records. In Africa, many vets are not horse specialistsfind one who is. Ask for references. The exam takes 1-2 hours. Do it before you pay any money.

5. Evaluating Fertility History in Mares and Stallions

Fertility history tells you if a horse can produce foals. For mares, ask: how many foals has she had? How many times was she covered? How many live foals? A good mare has a 70-80% conception rate per cycle. If she has been covered 3 times and no foal, that's a problem. For stallions, ask: how many mares has he covered? What is his foaling rate? A good stallion gets 60-70% of mares in foal per cycle. Below 50% is poor.

For mares, check age. Mares are most fertile from 4 to 12 years. After 15, fertility drops. After 20, it drops sharply. But a 20-year-old mare with a foal every year is still valuable. Check her reproductive tract: vet does ultrasound. Look for fluid in uterus, cysts, or scar tissue. Also check for endometritis (infection). A simple uterine swab and culture costs $50. If positive, treat with antibiotics and lavage. Wait one cycle before breeding.

For stallions, check testicle size. Normal: 8-12 cm long, 5-7 cm wide. Small testicles mean low sperm. Also check for testicular degeneration (common in older stallions). Semen analysis: collect with a dummy mount or artificial vagina. Normal: volume 50-100 ml, concentration 100-300 million sperm/ml, motility >60%, morphology >70% normal. If motility is below 40%, do not breed. If you cannot collect, check libido: does he mount and ejaculate? A stallion that refuses to breed may have pain or low testosterone.

In Africa, heat and nutrition affect fertility. Mares in poor body condition (body condition score under 4 on 1-9 scale) often do not cycle. Feed them well. Stallions in hot climates may have lower sperm quality in summer. Provide shade and water. Also, check for diseases: EVA, EIA, and babesia reduce fertility. Test all breeding stock.

Keep records. For each mare, write: date covered, stallion, result (pregnant/not), foaling date. For each stallion, write: mares covered, pregnancies, foals born. After 3 years, you will see patterns. If a stallion has 20 mares and only 5 pregnancies, cull him. If a mare has 3 covers and no pregnancy, have her vet-checked. Do not breed a mare with a history of abortionshe may have an infection. In Africa, many breeders use natural cover. That's fine, but hand-breeding with a twitch and hobbles reduces injury. Always have a vet on call. Fertility is the bottom line. A beautiful horse that cannot produce foals is useless for breeding.

6. Economic Considerations in Breeding Stock Selection

Breeding horses costs money. You must decide if the foal will be worth more than the costs. In Africa, a good foal can sell for $500-$5,000 depending on breed and training. But costs add up. Feed, vet, farrier, and labor for one mare per year: $300-$800. Stud fee: $100-$1,000. So you need a foal worth at least $500 to break even. If you breed for meat or work, the math is different. Know your market.

First, calculate your costs. For one mare: hay or pasture ($100-$300/year), grain ($100-$200), vet and vaccines ($50-$100), farrier ($60-$120), deworming ($30), and breeding fee ($100-$500). Total: $440-$1,250. Add stallion upkeep if you own him: another $500-$1,000/year. So a foal must sell for at least $1,000 to be profitable. In many parts of Africa, a weanling sells for $300-$800. That means you lose money unless you add value through training or sell to a niche market (sport, export).

Second, consider the mare's value. A proven broodmare with 5 foals is worth more than a maiden mare. But an old mare (over 18) may have complications. Vet costs for a difficult birth (dystocia) can be $200-$500. If the mare dies, you lose her and the foal. Insurance is rare in Africa. So choose a mare with a history of easy foaling. Also, consider the stallion. A popular stallion with proven offspring commands higher stud fees but gives you a foal that sells faster. A cheap stallion may produce a foal no one wants.

Third, think about the market. Who will buy your foal? If you breed for racing, you need speed and conformation. If for farm work, you need strength and calm temperament. If for meat, you need fast growth. In Africa, the biggest markets are: local work horses ($200-$600), sport horses ($1,000-$5,000), and export to Europe or Middle East ($5,000+). Export requires health certificates, quarantine, and transportcostly. So most small breeders sell locally. Research prices at your local auction or online (e.g., Equine.com or local Facebook groups).

Fourth, opportunity cost. Money spent on breeding could be used for other livestock (cattle, goats) or crops. A cow may give more return per dollar. So only breed if you love horses or have a clear market. Keep records: income from foals, expenses. After 3 years, if you are not profitable, change your strategy. Maybe use artificial insemination (AI) to access better stallions without owning one. AI costs $200-$500 per dose, but can improve quality. Or lease a stallion. Or focus on a rare breed that sells for a premium. In Africa, the Boerperd and Nooitgedacht are sought after. Do your homework. Never breed just because you like a horse. Breed because the numbers work.

<h2>Practical Reference: Selecting Breeding Stock in African Horse Operations</h2>

<p>In African horse breeding, the difference between a profitable stud and a costly hobby often comes down to one decision: which animals you breed. Whether you are raising Boerperd, Nooitgedacht, Thoroughbred, Arab, or Warmblood stock in South Africa, Kenya, Namibia, Zimbabwe, or Botswana, your breeding stock is a capital asset. Every foal carries the genetics, the reputation, and the cost structure of the animals you select today. This reference module gives you a business-first framework for evaluating breeding candidates using conformation scoring, performance data, temperament assessment, veterinary screening, fertility history, and economics.</p>

<p>Use this as a working document. Print the tables. Carry the checklist to auctions, farm visits, and pre-purchase examinations. In most African markets, a single poor selection decision can cost more than a year of feed so the discipline of structured evaluation pays for itself immediately.</p>

<h3>Why Selection Discipline Matters in African Markets</h3>

<p>African horse breeding operates under conditions that reward careful selection:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Feed and veterinary costs are rising faster than sale prices</strong> in many regions, so every mare must justify her keep.</li>
<li><strong>Buyer markets are quality-sensitive.</strong> Sport horse buyers, endurance riders, and commercial farmers increasingly demand documented performance, soundness, and temperament.</li>
<li><strong>Reproductive efficiency is the profit engine.</strong> A mare that produces a live, marketable foal every 12 months is worth multiples of a mare that produces one every 24 months.</li>
<li><strong>Transport and export costs are high.</strong> A poorly chosen animal is expensive to move, insure, and maintain.</li>
<li><strong>Reputation compounds.</strong> In tight-knit breeding communities, one unsound or difficult sire can damage a stud's name for years.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Conformation Evaluation and Scoring</h3>

<p>Conformation scoring is the first filter. It predicts soundness, athletic potential, and longevity. Score each animal on a 110 scale across key traits, then weight them for your discipline. For endurance and farm work, weight legs and feet heavily. For dressage and show jumping, weight shoulder angle, back, and neck. For draft and trekking, weight bone and hoof quality.</p>

<h3>Performance and Athletic Ability Assessment</h3>

<p>Performance data separates proven producers from hopeful ones. In Africa, useful performance records include:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Endurance:</strong> completion rates, average speeds, recovery times (Arab, Boerperd, Nooitgedacht).</li>
<li><strong>Racing:</strong> race records, earnings, speed figures (Thoroughbred).</li>
<li><strong>Sport:</strong> show jumping clears, dressage scores, eventing results (Warmblood, Thoroughbred crosses).</li>
<li><strong>Work:</strong> draught capacity, trekking stamina, herding performance (Boerperd, Basotho pony).</li>
</ul>

<p>Where official records are thin, use structured field tests: a 40 km ride with heart-rate recovery, a loading test, or a controlled canter under saddle. Document everything buyers pay for evidence.</p>

<h3>Temperament and Behavior Evaluation</h3>

<p>Temperament is heritable and economically decisive. A nervous mare passes stress to foals, complicates handling, and increases injury risk. Score temperament using a simple 15 scale across five situations: approach in paddock, haltering, leading, grooming, and unfamiliar object. Reject animals scoring 12 in two or more categories unless you have a specific management plan.</p>

<h3>Veterinary Pre-Purchase Examination for Breeding</h3>

<p>Never buy breeding stock without a veterinary examination by a veterinarian independent of the seller. The exam should cover eyes, heart, lungs, limbs, hooves, teeth, and reproductive organs. For mares, include a rectal and ultrasound examination of the uterus and ovaries. For stallions, include testicular assessment and, where possible, a semen evaluation.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #b00020; background:#fdecec; padding:14px 16px; margin:18px 0; border-radius:6px;">
<strong style="color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING Contagious Equine Metritis (CEM) and Equine Viral Arteritis (EVA)</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">CEM and EVA are reportable reproductive diseases that can devastate a stud. In South Africa, the <em>Animal Diseases Act, 1984 (Act No. 35 of 1984)</em> and the <em>Animal Improvement Act, 1998 (Act No. 62 of 1998)</em> regulate movement and breeding of equines. Never introduce a new mare or stallion to your herd without a negative CEM swab (clitoral and uterine) and EVA blood test, plus a documented quarantine period of at least 2130 days. Buying an untested animal to save money is the fastest route to a barren herd and a closed stud.</p>
</div>

<h3>Evaluating Fertility History in Mares and Stallions</h3>

<p>Fertility history is the single best predictor of future reproductive performance. Ask for breeding records covering at least three seasons. For mares: number of covers per conception, foaling dates, foal survival, and any history of retained placenta, dystocia, or endometritis. For stallions: number of mares covered, conception rate, foals registered, and any history of low libido or sperm abnormalities.</p>

<h3>Economic Considerations in Breeding Stock Selection</h3>

<p>Breeding is a business. Every selection decision must be tested against a simple return-on-investment model. Consider purchase price, transport, insurance, feed, veterinary care, farrier, training, and stud fees. Then estimate realistic sale values for foals and any performance income. In most African markets, a mare must produce a live foal every 1214 months to be profitable.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ol>
<li><strong>Conformation:</strong> Score all seven traits; reject any animal scoring below 5 on legs or hooves.</li>
<li><strong>Performance:</strong> Obtain official or documented records for at least two seasons.</li>
<li><strong>Temperament:</strong> Test in five handling situations; reject consistently reactive animals.</li>
<li><strong>Veterinary:</strong> Insist on independent pre-purchase exam including reproductive ultrasound.</li>
<li><strong>Disease:</strong> Require negative CEM and EVA tests plus 2130 day quarantine.</li>
<li><strong>Fertility:</strong> Review three seasons of breeding records; check foaling intervals and conception rates.</li>
<li><strong>Economics:</strong> Build a full cost model; confirm break-even foal price is below local market.</li>
<li><strong>Paperwork:</strong> Verify registration, pedigree, and ownership documents before payment.</li>
</ol>

<p style="font-size:13px; color:#444;"><strong>Business note:</strong> In African breeding operations, the highest-return strategy is usually to breed fewer, better animals. A small herd of high-fertility, well-conformed, disease-tested mares will out-earn a large herd of unproven stock every season. Select ruthlessly, document everything, and let your records not sentiment drive your breeding decisions.</p>
