1. Understanding Yam Nutrient Requirements

Yam is a heavy feeder. A crop of 10,000 plants (about 1 hectare) removes roughly 100 kg nitrogen (N), 30 kg phosphorus (P2O5), and 120 kg potassium (K2O) from the soil. That means if you harvest 20 tonnes of tubers, you are taking away those nutrients. You must replace them or the next crop will be small and sickly. Yam also needs calcium, magnesium, sulfur, and micronutrients like zinc and boron, though in smaller amounts.

The first 3 months after planting are critical. During this time, the vine grows rapidly and the tuber begins to form. If nutrients are missing now, you cannot make up for it later. Nitrogen is needed for leaf and vine growth. Phosphorus helps root development and tuber initiation. Potassium is the most important for tuber size, starch content, and storage quality. A shortage of potassium shows as yellowing between the veins of older leaves and small tubers.

In Africa, most yam is grown on soils that are sandy or loamy and low in organic matter. These soils hold few nutrients. After 2-3 years of continuous yam, yields drop sharply. You can see this in the leaves: pale green or yellow, and vines that stop growing. The best way to know what your soil needs is a soil test. Contact your local agricultural extension officer or a nearby university soil lab. A simple test costs little and tells you exactly which nutrients are low and what fertiliser to buy.

Without a soil test, a general recommendation for yam in West Africa is to apply 400-600 kg per hectare of a compound fertiliser like NPK 15-15-15 at planting, then top-dress with 100-150 kg per hectare of urea or NPK 20-10-10 about 8-10 weeks later. But these are only starting points. Your soil may need more or less. Also, remember that yam responds well to organic matter. Even if you use inorganic fertiliser, adding compost or manure will improve soil structure and water holding capacity, making fertiliser work better.

Finally, yam does not like competition. Weeds take up nutrients faster than yam. Keep the field clean, especially in the first 3 months. Mulching with dry grass or crop residues also adds organic matter slowly and suppresses weeds. A healthy yam crop starts with knowing what the plant needs and what your soil can supply.

2. Organic Matter and Compost Application

Organic matter is the backbone of soil fertility. In Africa, many yam farmers cannot afford enough inorganic fertiliser, so compost and manure are essential. Good compost does three things: it feeds the soil life, it holds water and nutrients, and it improves soil structure so yam roots and tubers can grow straight and big. A soil with 2-3% organic matter will produce far better yam than a sandy soil with less than 1%.

To make compost, you need a mix of 'browns' (dry leaves, straw, sawdust) and 'greens' (fresh grass, kitchen waste, animal manure). A simple pile: alternate 15 cm layers of browns and greens, keep it as moist as a wrung-out sponge, and turn it every 2 weeks. In 6-8 weeks, you get dark, crumbly compost that smells like forest soil. For 1 hectare of yam, you need about 5-10 tonnes of compost. That sounds like a lot, but you can start small. Apply 2-3 handfuls (about 200-300 grams) per planting hole. That is 2-3 tonnes per hectare if you have 10,000 hills. Every bit helps.

Animal manure is also excellent. Poultry manure is rich in nitrogen, but fresh manure can burn plants. Let it dry or compost it for at least 4 weeks before use. Cow dung is milder and adds lots of organic matter. Apply 1-2 kg per hill (10-20 tonnes per hectare) if you have it. Spread it in the planting hole and mix with soil before planting the setts.

You can also grow cover crops like mucuna (velvet bean) or pigeon pea and plough them into the soil before planting yam. These 'green manures' add nitrogen and organic matter. Mucuna can produce over 100 kg of nitrogen per hectare in its biomass. Plant it at the start of the rains, let it grow for 8-10 weeks, then slash and incorporate it 2-3 weeks before planting yam.

Do not burn your crop residues. Burning destroys organic matter and soil life. Instead, chop residues and spread them as mulch. Mulch keeps the soil cool, reduces evaporation, and slowly releases nutrients. In dry areas, mulch can increase yam yield by 20-30%. Remember, building organic matter takes time. It may take 3-4 seasons of consistent compost and manure application to see big changes. But once your soil is rich, it will give you good yam with less fertiliser.

3. Inorganic Fertilizer Types and Application Rates

Inorganic fertilisers are concentrated nutrient salts. They are fast-acting and easy to apply, but they can be expensive and if used wrongly, they can damage your soil. The most common types for yam in Africa are NPK compounds, urea, and muriate of potash (MOP). NPK 15-15-15 means 15% nitrogen, 15% phosphorus (as P2O5), and 15% potassium (as K2O). Other common grades are NPK 20-10-10 (more nitrogen) and NPK 12-12-17 (more potassium for tuber filling).

How much to apply? A standard recommendation for yam is 400-600 kg per hectare of NPK 15-15-15 at planting. That is about 40-60 grams per hill if you have 10,000 hills per hectare. Use a bottle cap or a small tin to measure. A full bottle cap of NPK 15-15-15 weighs about 15-20 grams, so you need 2-3 caps per hill. Place the fertiliser in a ring around the sett, about 10-15 cm away from the seed yam, and cover with soil. Never let fertiliser touch the sett directly; it will burn the young roots.

About 8-10 weeks after planting, when the vines are actively growing, top-dress with nitrogen. Use urea (46% N) at 100-150 kg per hectare, or NPK 20-10-10 at 150-200 kg per hectare. That is about 10-15 grams of urea per hill (half a bottle cap) or 15-20 grams of NPK 20-10-10. Apply in a band 15-20 cm from the plant base and cover lightly. If leaves are pale yellow, you need more nitrogen. If they are dark green and vines are too leafy, skip the top-dress.

For tuber bulking (from about 3 months onward), potassium is key. If you can find NPK 12-12-17 or potassium sulphate (SOP), apply 100-150 kg per hectare. Muriate of potash (MOP, 60% K2O) can also be used at 100-150 kg per hectare. Potassium helps tubers grow large and store well. Do not apply potassium too late; stop by 4 months after planting.

Always read the label on the fertiliser bag. Store fertiliser in a dry place, off the ground, and away from children and animals. Do not mix different fertilisers unless instructed. If you can afford only one bag, buy NPK 15-15-15 and use it at planting. That gives the young plant a balanced start. For best results, combine inorganic fertiliser with compost or manure. The organic matter helps hold the nutrients in the soil so they are not washed away by rain.

4. Timing and Methods of Fertilizer Application

When and how you apply fertiliser is as important as what you apply. Yam needs nutrients at three key stages: at planting (for root and sprout development), during rapid vine growth (8-12 weeks after planting), and during tuber bulking (3-5 months after planting). Missing these windows means wasted fertiliser and poor yields.

At planting, apply your base fertiliser. This is usually NPK 15-15-15 or a similar compound. The method is simple: dig your planting hole, place the seed yam, then put the fertiliser in a ring around the sett, about 10-15 cm away. Cover with soil. Do not put fertiliser directly under the sett or touching it. If you are using manure or compost, mix it thoroughly with the soil in the hole before planting. For a 1-hectare field with 10,000 hills, you need 400-600 kg of NPK. That is 40-60 grams per hill. Use a small tin or bottle cap to measure. One level bottle cap (from a 1-litre bottle) holds about 15-20 grams.

The first top-dressing comes when vines are growing fast, usually 8-10 weeks after planting. At this time, apply urea or NPK 20-10-10. The best method is banding: make a shallow furrow 15-20 cm from the base of the plant, sprinkle the fertiliser along the furrow, and cover with soil. Do not broadcast on the surface; nitrogen can be lost to the air. If the soil is dry, apply just before rain or irrigate lightly after application. For urea, 100-150 kg per hectare is typical. That is 10-15 grams per hill.

The second top-dressing, if needed, is at tuber bulking, around 3-4 months after planting. Use a potassium-rich fertiliser like NPK 12-12-17 or muriate of potash. Apply in a band between the rows, 20-25 cm from the plants, and cover. This helps tubers fill out and improves storage quality. Do not apply nitrogen at this stage; it will encourage leaf growth instead of tuber growth.

Always apply fertiliser to moist soil. If the soil is dry, wait for rain or water the field first. Fertiliser on dry soil can burn roots and is not taken up. Also, avoid applying on waterlogged soil; nutrients can be lost through leaching or denitrification. Keep a simple record: date, type of fertiliser, amount per hill, and method. This helps you learn what works on your farm. After harvest, note the yield. Over 2-3 seasons, you will know exactly what your yam needs.

5. Soil pH Management for Yam

Soil pH is a measure of how acidic or alkaline your soil is. It runs from 0 to 14. A pH of 7 is neutral. Below 7 is acidic, above 7 is alkaline. Yam grows best in soils with a pH between 5.5 and 6.5. In this range, nutrients like phosphorus, potassium, calcium, and magnesium are most available to the plant. If the pH is too low (below 5.0) or too high (above 7.5), yam cannot take up nutrients properly, even if you apply fertiliser.

Most soils in the humid and sub-humid tropics of Africa are naturally acidic. Continuous farming with urea or ammonium sulphate fertilisers makes them even more acidic. You can test your soil pH with a simple kit from an agricultural supply store. It costs little and gives you a number. Or send a soil sample to a lab. To take a sample, dig 15-20 cm deep in 10-15 spots across your field, mix the soil in a clean bucket, and take about 500 grams to the lab or extension office.

If your soil pH is below 5.5, you need to add lime. Agricultural lime (calcium carbonate) or dolomite (calcium magnesium carbonate) raises pH. The amount depends on how acidic your soil is and its texture. A general rule: to raise pH by 0.5 units on a sandy soil, apply 1-2 tonnes of lime per hectare. On clay soil, you may need 3-4 tonnes. Apply lime 2-3 months before planting yam. Spread it evenly over the field and plough or hoe it into the top 15 cm. Lime reacts slowly, so do not expect immediate results. It also adds calcium and magnesium, which yam needs.

If your soil pH is above 7.5 (rare in most yam areas, but possible in dry savannah), you can lower it by adding elemental sulphur or gypsum. Elemental sulphur is applied at 200-500 kg per hectare, but it takes several months to work. Organic matter like compost also helps buffer pH, bringing it closer to neutral over time. In most cases, adding compost is the safest way to improve pH without overdoing it.

Do not guess. A pH test is cheap and prevents wasting money on fertiliser that your crop cannot use. If you cannot afford a test, look at your weeds. If you see lots of sorrel (Oxalis) or bracken fern, your soil is likely acidic. If you see thistles or nettles, it may be more neutral. But these are only clues. For yam, aim for pH 5.5-6.5 and you will get the best response from your fertiliser and organic matter.

6. Foliar Feeding and Micronutrients

Foliar feeding means spraying liquid fertiliser directly onto the leaves. Yam leaves can absorb nutrients quickly, so foliar sprays are a fast way to correct deficiencies. They are not a replacement for soil fertiliser, but they can give a boost during critical growth stages or when the soil is too dry or too wet for roots to take up nutrients. Foliar feeding is especially useful for micronutrients, which yam needs in very small amounts.

Micronutrients include zinc, boron, manganese, iron, copper, and molybdenum. A shortage of zinc causes small leaves and shortened internodes (the space between leaves). Boron deficiency leads to poor tuber formation and hollow tubers. Manganese deficiency shows as yellowing between veins on young leaves. In most African soils, zinc and boron are the most common deficiencies. You can buy micronutrient mixes at agro-dealers. Look for a product that contains chelated micronutrients, which are more stable. Mix according to the label, usually 1-2 grams per litre of water. Spray in the early morning or late evening when it is cool and leaves are not wet from rain. Cover both sides of the leaves.

For a general foliar boost, you can use urea (nitrogen) or potassium nitrate. A common recipe: dissolve 20-30 grams of urea in 10 litres of water (about 2-3 tablespoons per litre) and spray on the leaves. This gives a quick nitrogen boost if vines are pale. For potassium, use potassium nitrate or potassium sulphate at 10-20 grams per 10 litres. Do not mix urea with lime or calcium sprays; they can react and burn leaves. Always test on a few plants first. If leaves show burn (brown spots) within 24 hours, dilute the spray next time.

Foliar feeding is most effective during rapid vine growth (8-12 weeks after planting) and during tuber bulking (3-5 months). Spray every 2-3 weeks, up to 3-4 times per season. Do not spray in the hot midday sun; droplets can act like lenses and burn leaves. Also, do not spray if rain is expected within 6 hours; it will wash off. Use a knapsack sprayer with a fine nozzle. Clean the sprayer thoroughly after use, especially if you previously used herbicides.

Remember, foliar feeding is a supplement, not a cure-all. If your soil is severely lacking in nutrients, you must fix the soil first with compost, manure, and inorganic fertiliser. Use foliar sprays as a quick fix for visible deficiencies or to give your yam a boost during critical stages. Keep notes on what you spray, when, and how the plants respond. Over time, you will know exactly what your yam needs.

<h2>Practical Reference: Soil Fertility and Nutrient Management for Yam</h2>

<p>Yam (<em>Dioscorea</em> spp.) is one of West Africa's most commercially valuable tuber crops, but it is also one of the most nutrient-demanding. A mature yam crop producing 2530 t/ha removes roughly 100150 kg N, 3050 kg PO, and 120180 kg KO per hectare from the soil each season (IITA, 2020). Unlike cassava, yam does not tolerate depleted soils well; yields collapse quickly when fertility is mismanaged. For the commercial yam farmer, soil fertility is not an input cost it is the single largest determinant of marketable tuber grade, storability, and profit per hectare.</p>

<p>This Practical Reference consolidates the six lessons in this module into an operational guide you can take to the field. It covers nutrient requirements, organic and inorganic inputs, application timing and method, pH management, and foliar correction, with Africa-specific figures and business framing.</p>

<h3>1. What a Yam Crop Actually Needs</h3>

<p>Yam's nutrient demand is dominated by potassium, followed by nitrogen and phosphorus. Potassium drives tuber bulking, dry matter accumulation, and storage quality the traits buyers pay for. Nitrogen supports vine and leaf development during the first 1014 weeks; excess N after tuber initiation diverts energy to foliage and produces watery, poorly storing tubers. Phosphorus is needed in smaller quantities but is critical for root initiation and tuber set.</p>

<p>Secondary and micronutrients matter more than most farmers assume. Calcium and magnesium influence tuber skin quality and shelf life; zinc and boron deficiencies cause malformed, cracked, or "corky" tubers that are downgraded at market. Sandy and heavily weathered soils common across the yam belt from Cte d'Ivoire through Ghana, Togo, Benin, and Nigeria into Cameroon are typically deficient in both.</p>

<h3>2. Organic Matter and Compost</h3>

<p>Organic matter is the foundation of yam fertility on African soils. It improves water-holding capacity, buffers pH, supplies micronutrients, and feeds the soil biology that makes inorganic fertilizers work. On degraded soils, applying NPK without organic matter often produces a low fertilizer use efficiency you pay for nutrients the plant cannot access.</p>

<p>Well-decomposed cattle or poultry manure at 510 t/ha, or quality compost at 812 t/ha, applied and incorporated 34 weeks before planting, is the standard recommendation across the yam belt. Poultry manure is richer in N and P; cattle manure is better for building long-term soil structure. Combine both where available.</p>

<h3>3. Inorganic Fertilizer Types and Rates</h3>

<p>For commercial yam, balanced compound fertilizers give the most predictable results. The most widely available formulations in West Africa are NPK 15-15-15, NPK 20-10-10, and NPK 12-12-17 + 2MgO (often marketed for root crops). Straight fertilizers urea (46% N), single superphosphate (18% PO), and muriate of potash (60% KO) allow precision but require more management.</p>

<p>Base your rate on target yield and soil test where possible. A practical rule for 2530 t/ha on moderately fertile soil is 400600 kg/ha of compound fertilizer split across the season, supplemented with potassium during bulking. On poor soils, increase organic inputs rather than simply raising NPK.</p>

<h3>4. Timing and Methods of Application</h3>

<p>Yam responds strongly to split application. A single heavy dose at planting is leached or fixed before the plant can use it. The principle is simple: feed nitrogen early, potassium late.</p>

<p><strong>Method matters as much as rate.</strong> Broadcasting fertilizer on the soil surface in a yam field wastes money rain washes it away and weeds capture it. Place fertilizer in a ring around the base of each mound, 1015 cm from the plant, then cover lightly with soil. On ridges, band along the ridge shoulder. For large commercial operations, a knapsack or tractor-mounted band applicator improves speed and accuracy.</p>

<p>On sandy soils, split into three or four smaller applications to reduce leaching. On heavier loams, two or three applications are sufficient. Irrigated yam can take more frequent, smaller doses; rainfed yam should be timed to follow the onset of steady rains.</p>

<h3>5. Soil pH Management</h3>

<p>Yam performs best between pH 5.5 and 6.5. Below pH 5.0, phosphorus becomes locked up, aluminium toxicity rises, and tuber development is stunted. Above pH 7.0, zinc and boron become less available and tuber skin quality declines.</p>

<p>Most yam soils in West Africa are naturally acidic. If your soil tests below pH 5.5, apply agricultural lime at 12 t/ha, incorporated 48 weeks before planting. On very acidic soils, split the lime across two seasons. Wood ash is a practical local alternative at 23 t/ha, but its effect is smaller and less predictable than lime.</p>

<p>Do not over-lime. Raising pH above 6.8 in pursuit of "good" soil can trigger micronutrient deficiencies and reduce yam storability. Test before you amend.</p>

<h3>6. Foliar Feeding and Micronutrients</h3>

<p>Foliar feeding is a corrective tool, not a replacement for soil fertility. Use it when you see deficiency symptoms or during critical growth windows when root uptake is limited.</p>

<p>Common deficiencies and responses:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Zinc:</strong> interveinal chlorosis on young leaves, shortened internodes. Spray 0.5% zinc sulphate solution.</li>
<li><strong>Boron:</strong> cracked, corky, or hollow tubers. Spray 0.20.3% borax solution do not exceed, boron toxicity is easy.</li>
<li><strong>Magnesium:</strong> yellowing between veins on older leaves. Spray 2% magnesium sulphate (Epsom salt).</li>
<li><strong>Iron:</strong> pale new leaves on high-pH soils. Spray 0.5% ferrous sulphate with a spreader.</li>
</ul>

<p>Spray early morning or late evening to avoid leaf burn. Add a few drops of mild soap as a spreader if commercial adjuvants are unavailable. Two or three sprays at 1014 day intervals are usually enough to correct a deficiency.</p>

<h3>Business Note</h3>
<p>On a 1-hectare commercial yam plot targeting 25 t/ha, a well-managed fertility program costing roughly USD 400700 in inputs can raise marketable yield by 3050% compared with unfertilized plots. At prevailing West African farmgate prices, that is often a 23x return on the fertility investment. The difference between a profitable yam enterprise and a marginal one is rarely land it is soil management discipline.</p>
