1. Preparing for Foaling: Facilities and Supplies

Preparing for foaling starts weeks before the mare's due date. A clean, quiet, and safe foaling environment is critical. In Africa, many breeders use a large, well-ventilated box stall (minimum 4m x 4m) with solid walls and deep, clean bedding such as straw or wood shavings. Avoid sandy floors to prevent contamination. If a stall is not available, a small, grassy paddock with good fencing and no hazards is acceptable, but you must monitor closely. The mare should be moved into the foaling area 2-4 weeks before her due date to allow her to adapt and build immunity to local pathogens.

Gather all supplies in advance and store them in a clean, waterproof container. Essentials include: disposable gloves (at least 10 pairs), lubricant (obstetric gel or clean vegetable oil), tail wrap or clean cloth, iodine or chlorhexidine solution for navel dipping, scissors, clean towels, a bucket, mild soap, and a thermometer. Also have a foal resuscitator (or a small oxygen tank if available), a stomach tube, and a bottle with a nipple for feeding colostrum. Keep your veterinarian's phone number and a transport plan for emergencies.

Ensure the mare is up to date on vaccinations (especially tetanus) and deworming. Consult a vet about any needed boosters 4-6 weeks before foaling. Monitor the mare's udder development and milk changes. As foaling approaches, the udder will enlarge and may drip milk; this is a sign to watch closely. Check the vulva for relaxation and elongation. A mare may foal rapidly, so be prepared to attend. If possible, install a foaling alarm or camera to alert you when the mare lies down and goes into labor.

Finally, create a written plan. Know who will assist, how to reach the vet, and how to handle a dystocia. Practice handling the supplies and know how to use them. Clean the stall daily and keep it dry. A well-prepared foaling kit and environment can mean the difference between life and death for the foal and mare.

2. Stages of Labor and Normal Foaling

Labor in mares occurs in three stages. Stage 1 (preparatory) lasts 1-4 hours. The mare becomes restless, paces, looks at her flank, sweats, and may urinate frequently. The cervix dilates, and the foal rotates into position. You may see mild contractions. Keep disturbance to a minimum; watch from a distance. This stage ends when the water breaks (allantoic fluid).

Stage 2 (expulsion) is fast, usually 15-30 minutes. The mare lies down and strains strongly. The foal should be delivered in a diving position: one front hoof, then the other, followed by the nose, all presented together. If you see a red bag (placenta) covering the foal's nose, tear it immediately to allow breathing. Normally, the foal's front feet appear first, then the head, shoulders, and hindquarters. The mare may rest between contractions. Do not pull unless instructed by a vet. Once the foal is out, clear its nose and mouth if needed. The umbilical cord usually breaks on its own; if not, wait a few minutes, then break it by twisting and pulling gently, leaving about 2-3 cm, and dip the navel in iodine.

Stage 3 (passing of the placenta) occurs within 1-3 hours after foaling. The placenta should be expelled completely. Save it for the vet to examine; a retained placenta (more than 3 hours) is an emergency. The mare may have mild contractions. Provide her with fresh water and hay. Monitor her for signs of colic or hemorrhage. Normal foaling is quick and explosive; if stage 2 lasts more than 30 minutes or the mare is straining without progress, call for help immediately. In Africa, where vet access may be limited, knowing normal progression is crucial to recognize problems early.

3. Dystocia Recognition and Assistance

Dystocia means difficult birth. It is an emergency. Recognize it if: the mare strains for more than 20-30 minutes with no progress, you see a red bag (premature placental separation), abnormal presentation (e.g., only one leg, head back, breech), or the foal is stuck. Call your veterinarian immediately. If no vet is available, you may need to assist, but only if you are trained. Wash your hands and the mare's vulva with soap and water. Use plenty of lubricant. If the foal's head is back, try to push the foal back slightly and bring the head forward. If one leg is back, find it and pull it forward. Always pull during contractions, and pull downwards and towards the mare's hocks. Never pull with excessive force; use steady traction. If you cannot correct the position in 10-15 minutes, seek help.

Common dystocias include: head-back (foal's head turned back), one or both front legs back, breech (hindquarters first), and hip lock (foal's hips too wide). For a breech, you may need to push the foal forward and rotate it. For hip lock, rotate the foal 45 degrees while pulling. If the mare is exhausted, a cesarean section may be needed. In rural Africa, plan ahead: identify a vet who can do surgery, and have transport ready. Do not waste time; foal survival drops rapidly after 30 minutes of dystocia.

After assisting, check the mare for tears and the foal for injuries. Give the mare antibiotics if advised. Ensure the foal receives colostrum within 2 hours. If the mare rejects the foal, restrain her and help it nurse. Dystocia is stressful; monitor both for signs of infection or shock. Prevention includes proper nutrition and regular vet checks during pregnancy.

4. Immediate Postpartum Care of Mare and Foal

Immediately after foaling, ensure the foal is breathing. Clear any membranes from its nose and mouth. If it is not breathing, rub it vigorously with a towel and tickle its nostrils with a straw. If needed, give artificial respiration by compressing the chest. The umbilical cord should be dipped in iodine or chlorhexidine solution within 30 minutes to prevent infection. Do not cut the cord unless it is bleeding; instead, dip the entire stump. Allow the mare to lick the foal; this stimulates circulation and bonding.

The foal should stand within 1 hour and nurse within 2 hours. Colostrum (first milk) is rich in antibodies and must be consumed within 6 hours for maximum absorption. If the foal does not nurse, milk the mare and feed the colostrum via bottle or stomach tube. Ensure the mare has a clean, warm, draft-free area. Offer her warm water with electrolytes and good-quality hay. Check her for signs of colic, hemorrhage, or retained placenta. Monitor her temperature (normal 37.5-38.5C) and heart rate (28-40 beats per minute).

For the foal, monitor breathing rate (20-40 breaths per minute) and heart rate (80-120 beats per minute). Check for meconium (first manure) within 12 hours. If the foal has not passed meconium, consult a vet. Keep the environment quiet and clean. Handle the foal gently to avoid stress. Ensure the mare is producing milk; if not, consult a vet. In the first 24 hours, watch for signs of failure of passive transfer: weakness, poor suckle, or swollen joints. Early intervention is key. In Africa, where resources may be limited, simple hygiene and monitoring can save lives.

5. Neonatal Examination and Passive Transfer

A thorough neonatal examination should be done within 12-24 hours of birth. Check the foal's overall health: alertness, suckle reflex, and ability to stand. Examine the eyes for ulcers or discharge, the mouth for cleft palate, and the umbilicus for swelling or infection. Palpate the limbs for fractures or deformities. Listen to the heart and lungs for murmurs or abnormal sounds. Check the anus and genitalia for abnormalities. Take the foal's temperature (normal 37.5-38.5C). If any abnormalities are found, call a vet.

Passive transfer refers to the absorption of antibodies from colostrum. The foal's gut can absorb large molecules for only about 6-12 hours after birth. After that, the gut closes. To check if the foal received enough antibodies, a blood test for IgG (immunoglobulin G) can be done at 12-24 hours. A level of at least 800 mg/dL is adequate; 400-800 is partial failure; below 400 is failure. If failure occurs, the foal needs a plasma transfusion. In Africa, where testing may be limited, watch for signs of failure: weakness, lethargy, not nursing, or recurrent infections. If you suspect failure, consult a vet immediately.

Ensure the foal nurses well. A mare may produce colostrum for up to 24 hours, but quality drops after 6 hours. If the mare has poor colostrum, you can use frozen colostrum from another mare or a commercial substitute. If the foal cannot nurse, tube feeding may be necessary. Monitor the foal's weight; it should gain 0.5-1 kg per day. Keep the foal warm and dry. In hot African climates, ensure shade and ventilation. Simple records of birth weight, IgG results, and daily observations help track health.

6. Common Neonatal Problems and Emergencies

Common neonatal problems include: failure of passive transfer (FPT), sepsis, neonatal maladjustment syndrome (dummy foal), meconium impaction, and contracted tendons. FPT occurs when the foal does not absorb enough antibodies. Signs: weakness, poor suckle, and infections. Treatment: plasma transfusion. Sepsis is a blood infection; signs: fever, depression, swollen joints, and diarrhea. It requires immediate antibiotics and supportive care. Dummy foal syndrome: the foal is confused, wanders, and cannot nurse. It may need oxygen therapy and sedation. Meconium impaction: the foal strains to defecate and has a distended abdomen. Treat with enemas (warm soapy water) and consult a vet if not resolved. Contracted tendons: the foal's legs are bent; often resolves with exercise and splints, but severe cases need vet care.

Other emergencies: colic, diarrhea, and umbilical infections. Colic in foals can be mild or severe; watch for rolling, kicking at belly, and not nursing. Diarrhea can cause dehydration; give electrolytes and consult a vet. Umbilical infections (omphalitis) cause swelling, heat, and discharge; treat with antibiotics and antiseptic flushing. Also watch for neonatal isoerythrolysis (NI), where the mare's antibodies attack the foal's red blood cells; signs: jaundice, weakness, and dark urine. This is rare but serious; treat with blood transfusion.

In Africa, where veterinary services may be far, prepare a emergency kit: antibiotics, anti-inflammatory drugs, electrolytes, enema supplies, and a stomach tube. Learn to recognize early signs. Keep the foal's environment clean to prevent infections. If you suspect an emergency, call a vet immediately. Many neonatal problems are treatable if caught early. Regular monitoring during the first week is crucial. Record all observations and act quickly.

7. Practical Reference


Practical Reference: Parturition and Neonatal Care in African Horse Breeding



Foaling is the single highest-risk event in any equine breeding operation. In Africa, where veterinary reach is uneven, transport distances are long, and many studs operate with lean teams, the difference between a live foal and a dead one or between a mare that breeds again and one that does not usually comes down to preparation, observation, and speed of response. This practical reference consolidates the essentials from the module into working tools you can print, laminate, and post in the foaling barn. It is written for the commercial breeder: every recommendation is framed around protecting the value of the mare and foal, reducing losses, and keeping the operation profitable.



Across Southern Africa, East Africa, and West Africa, Thoroughbred, Warmblood, Arab, and indigenous working-horse breeders face the same biological clock, but very different logistics. A stud in the Western Cape may have a vet 20 minutes away; a farm in the Kenyan highlands, the Zambian plateau, or the Ethiopian highlands may be 36 hours from the nearest equine practitioner. The protocols below are designed to work in both contexts, with clear decision points for when to act alone and when to call for help.





Facilities and Supplies: The African Reality



You do not need a climate-controlled foaling unit to foal mares safely. You need a clean, dry, well-lit, draught-free space that can be observed without disturbing the mare, plus a stocked kit that does not depend on same-day delivery from a city pharmacy. On most African studs, three constraints dominate: electricity reliability, water quality, and road access. Plan around all three.



The foaling box should be a minimum of 4 m 4 m (larger for Warmbloods and draft crosses), with solid walls to shoulder height, rubber matting or deep clean bedding, non-slip flooring, and a soft, non-slip surface for the newborn foal. Remove all hay nets, buckets, and protruding fittings. If the mare is on a large paddock, a smaller "foaling paddock" of 20 m 20 m with safe fencing is often preferable in warm climates, provided you can observe her from a distance without disturbing her. In areas with predators (jackals, hyenas, leopards), foaling boxes or predator-proof paddocks are non-negotiable.



Lighting is critical. Most mares foal at night, and you must be able to see the foal's position without fumbling. Install a dim red or amber light in the box, or use a headtorch with a red filter. A mains-powered or solar-charged LED floodlight on a nearby pole, angled away from the mare, also works. Keep a rechargeable torch and spare batteries in the kit.

Stages of Labour: What Normal Looks Like



Stage one lasts 14 hours. The mare is restless, may pace, sweat, look at her flank, urinate frequently, and show waxing or dripping milk. The cervix relaxes and the foal rotates into position. Do not disturb her excessively. Stage two is the actual delivery and should take 1530 minutes from the rupture of the water bag to the foal being fully expelled. If the mare has been straining hard for more than 2030 minutes without progress, or if the foal is not delivered within 30 minutes of the water breaking, call for help immediately this is dystocia until proven otherwise.



Stage three is the passing of the placenta, which should occur within 3 hours of foaling. Retained placenta beyond 3 hours is a veterinary emergency because of the risk of metritis, laminitis, and laminitis-related founder. In Africa, where a vet may be hours away, the practical rule is: if the placenta is not out within 3 hours, phone the vet and begin monitoring the mare's temperature and demeanour. Do not pull on the placenta unless instructed by a vet.



Dystocia: Recognition and First Response



Dystocia difficult birth occurs in roughly 510% of foalings, and the most common cause is malposition of the foal. The classic abnormal presentations are: one or both forelegs retained, head turned back, breech (hind legs first with tail presented), or a foal coming upside down. Any of these requires immediate veterinary help. Your role before the vet arrives is to keep the mare safe, keep the foal alive if possible, and avoid making things worse.




SAFETY WARNING RED BOX


Do not attempt to correct a dystocia by pulling on the foal with ropes, chains, or a vehicle unless you are a trained veterinary professional with the correct equipment. Excessive traction causes uterine tears, cervical damage, and fatal haemorrhage in the mare, and broken ribs or spinal injury in the foal. In Africa, where a vet may be hours away, the safest action is to call, keep the mare calm and confined, and prepare for transport if the vet advises it.


Never give oxytocin without veterinary direction. It can cause uterine rupture if the foal is not correctly positioned.




Immediate Postpartum Care



Once the foal is out, the priorities are: clear the airways, ensure the foal is breathing, dip the navel, and let the mare and foal bond. If the foal is not breathing, clear the nostrils and mouth, rub vigorously with a towel, and if necessary, perform artificial respiration by compressing the chest. Do not hang the foal upside down this is an outdated practice that does not help and risks injury.



The mare should be examined for tears, and the placenta should be kept for veterinary inspection. A normal placenta is a uniform red-purple colour; any abnormality (thickened areas, pale patches, or a torn edge) should be shown to the vet. The mare should be monitored for colic, excessive bleeding, or a retained placenta.



Neonatal Examination and Passive Transfer



The foal should stand within 1 hour, suckle within 2 hours, and pass meconium within 1224 hours. Passive transfer of antibodies from colostrum is the single most important determinant of neonatal survival. In Africa, where many studs are far from laboratories, a stall-side IgG test (e.g., a commercial ELISA or a turbidimetric test) is worth the cost. If IgG is below 8 g/L at 1224 hours, the foal needs a plasma transfusion or oral colostrum supplement, ideally within 24 hours. Delayed passive transfer is a major cause of neonatal sepsis, joint ill, and pneumonia.

Common Neonatal Problems and Emergencies



The most common neonatal emergencies in African studs are: neonatal sepsis (often from poor navel hygiene or delayed colostrum), meconium impaction, neonatal maladjustment syndrome (dummy foal), and diarrhoea. Each has a narrow window for successful treatment. The business case for early intervention is simple: a foal that survives to weaning is worth many times the cost of a vet call, while a dead foal represents a full year of feed, labour, and opportunity cost.



Neonatal sepsis presents as a foal that is dull, not nursing, has a swollen navel, or has swollen joints. It is a veterinary emergency. Meconium impaction presents as a foal straining to defecate, rolling, or showing colic signs within the first 24 hours. A vet may need to administer an enema or, in severe cases, surgery. Neonatal maladjustment syndrome (dummy foal) presents as a foal that is disoriented, does not suckle, and may wander or press its head against walls. It requires veterinary support and, in some cases, plasma or medication.



Quick Reference Checklist




  • Foaling box clean, dry, well-lit (red/amber light), draught-free, and predator-proof where relevant.

  • Foaling kit stocked, checked, and accessible gloves, iodine, towels, thermometer, records, vet number.

  • Mare monitored nightly from 320 days; waxing, dripping milk, or restlessness triggers close watch.

  • Stage two: call vet if no foal within 30 minutes of water breaking or if malpresentation suspected.

  • Navel dipped in iodine/chlorhexidine within 15 minutes of birth; repeat at 1224 hours if advised.

  • Foal stands by 1 hour, suckles by 2 hours, passes meconium by 24 hours record all times.

  • IgG tested at 1224 hours; if below 8 g/L, plasma transfusion or colostrum supplement arranged.

  • Placenta passed within 3 hours; retained placenta = vet call, no pulling.

Bottom line for the breeder: The foaling process rewards preparation and punishes delay. In the African context, where veterinary support is often distant, the breeder's job is to know the normal timeline cold, keep a stocked kit, and call for help at the first deviation. Every hour saved in recognising dystocia or delayed passive transfer is an hour of value protected. Put this reference on the barn wall, train your team on it, and review it before every foaling season.
