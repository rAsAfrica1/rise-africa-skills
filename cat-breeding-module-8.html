8. Cat Behaviour and Environmental Enrichment

Understanding cat behaviour is the key to a calm, productive cattery. Here is how to design for it.

Module 8 of 12 · Cat Breeding for Profit

Why behaviour matters in a breeding cattery

Cats are not small dogs. They have specific behavioural needs that, if unmet, lead to stress, illness, and reproductive failure. Stress in cats shows as:

  • Reduced fertility
  • Failed matings
  • Poor milk production
  • Rejection of kittens
  • Aggression between cats
  • Toileting outside the box
  • Over-grooming and hair loss
  • Illness (especially FLUTD and URI)

Understanding cat behaviour and designing the cattery around it prevents these problems.

Territory and social structure

Cats are not strictly solitary. In the wild, related females live in groups with shared territory. Males hold larger territories.

Social groupingNaturally works?
Related females + kittensYes - natural family group
Unrelated femalesSometimes, with careful introductions
Adult males togetherUsually no - territorial conflict
Male + femalesYes if the male is calm and space is sufficient
Kittens togetherYes - littermates form strong bonds

Best cattery grouping: related females with kittens, stud male separate, group of spayed females together.

The core territory

Every cat has a "core territory" - a safe area they know and defend. In a cattery, this is the cat''s own pen or space.

  • Each cat needs their own feeding spot, water, sleeping area, and litter box.
  • Even in group housing, provide multiple resources.
  • Every cat needs somewhere to retreat - a covered bed, a box, a high shelf.
  • Core territory is not shared with strangers without gradual introduction.

Resource distribution

Cats need resources distributed so no cat monopolises:

ResourceRule
Litter boxes1 per cat + 1 spare, in different locations
Food bowls1 per cat, spread apart (not in a cluster)
Water bowlsMultiple, in different locations (cats often dislike water near food)
Scratching postsAt least 1 per cat, spread around
Beds1 per cat + extra, at different heights
Hiding spots1 per cat, at different locations
Play areasMultiple, away from resting areas

Vertical space

Cats are semi-arboreal - they naturally use vertical space for safety, hunting, and territory. In a cattery, vertical space multiplies the effective area without increasing floor size.

  • Cat trees: 1 per 2-3 cats, at different heights.
  • Shelves: Multiple at different heights, stable.
  • Ramps and platforms: For older cats or kittens.
  • High perches: Every cat should have a high vantage point.
  • Ceiling-level platforms: For maximum security.

A cattery with good vertical space can house twice as many cats comfortably.

Scent and marking

Cats communicate through scent. Scratching, rubbing, and urine marking are normal:

  • Scratching: Leaves visual and scent marks. Provide sisal posts.
  • Rubbing (bunting): Face rubbing on objects and people. Positive sign.
  • Urine marking: Territorial, especially in unneutered males. Reduce by neutering or housing separately.
  • Claw marking: Scratching furniture or posts, often after stress.

Do not try to eliminate scent - provide appropriate outlets instead.

Multi-cat household tips

  • Introduce slowly. 2-4 weeks of scent swapping and gradual meetings.
  • Multiple resources. Never force cats to share.
  • Vertical space so cats can avoid each other.
  • Separate feeding stations - 1.5+ m apart.
  • Multiple litter boxes - 1 per cat + 1 spare.
  • Watch for stress signals - hiding, over-grooming, aggression.
  • Do not force friendships. Some cats never bond.
  • Neuter where possible. Reduces aggression and marking.

Working with a veterinary behaviourist

If problems persist, a veterinary behaviourist (not just a trainer) can help. They address:

  • Underlying medical causes
  • Environmental changes
  • Behaviour modification programs
  • Medication when needed (last resort)

Cost: $100-500 per consultation. Worth it for persistent problems in valuable breeding cats.

The welfare rule: Every cat in the cattery must be able to express natural behaviour - hunt (play), scratch, climb, hide, and choose when to interact. Welfare is not a luxury; it is the foundation of a productive cattery.
