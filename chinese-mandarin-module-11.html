1. Office Vocabulary and Job Titles

In this lesson, you will learn essential Chinese words for the office and common job titles. Start with the basics: (b ngngsh) means office, (zhuzi) is desk, (di nno) is computer, and (di nhu ) is telephone. For job titles, (jngl) is manager, (lobn) is boss, (msh) is secretary, and (tngsh) is colleague. Practice saying these aloud daily. In many African countries, Chinese companies operate in sectors like construction, mining, and retail. Knowing these terms helps you communicate with Chinese supervisors or coworkers. For example, if you work at a Chinese-owned factory in Nigeria or Kenya, you might need to say: (w sh gngrn) I am a worker. Or (t sh jngl) He is the manager.

To ask someone's job, say: (n zu shnme gngzu?) What work do you do? A common reply is: (w sh sj) I am a driver. Other useful titles: (ku ij) accountant, (gngchngsh) engineer, (xioshuyun) salesperson. In African markets, these roles are increasingly in demand. Practice with a partner: point to objects and name them in Chinese. Use flashcards with pictures. Write each word 10 times. Listen to Chinese songs or news to hear pronunciation. Remember tones: m () means mother, but m () means horse. So (jngl) has a high tone then a falling-rising tone. Mispronouncing tones can change meaning. For example, (wn) means ask, but (wn) means kiss. So be careful!

In a real office setting, you might hear: (qng b wnji n gi w) Please give me the document. Or (huy z i sn din) The meeting is at 3 o'clock. Practice these phrases. Create a mini-dialogue: A: (n ho, n sh xn li de ma?) Hello, are you new? B: (sh de, w sh xn li de ku ij.) Yes, I am the new accountant. A: (hunyng!) Welcome! Repeat this until fluent. Use apps like HelloChinese or Pleco to review. Set a goal: learn 10 new words per day. By the end of the week, you'll have 70 words. That's enough to have basic conversations at work.

2. School Subjects and Classroom Phrases

In this lesson, you will learn Chinese words for school subjects and common classroom phrases. Start with subjects: (shxu) mathematics, (kxu) science, (yngy) English, (lsh) history, (dl) geography, (ynyu) music, (ty) physical education. In many African schools, Chinese is now offered as an elective. Knowing these terms helps you discuss your timetable. For example: (w xngqy yu shxu k) I have math class on Monday. To ask about someone's favorite subject: (n zu xhun shnme km?) What is your favorite subject? Reply: (w zu xhun kxu) I like science best.

Classroom phrases are essential. (losh) means teacher, (xusheng) means student, (ji osh) means classroom, (sh) means book, (b) means pen. To ask a question: (w ky wn y g wnt ma?) May I ask a question? To answer: (w zhd o) I know, or (w b zhd o) I don't know. When you don't understand: (qng z i shu y bi n) Please say it again. Or (w tng b dng) I don't understand. These are vital for African students learning in Chinese-medium schools or attending Confucius Institute classes.

Practice with a friend: one plays teacher, the other student. Teacher says: (dki sh, fn d o d w y) Open your books, turn to page 5. Student responds: (ho de, losh) Okay, teacher. Use numbers: (d y) first, (d r) second, etc. For homework: (jntin de zuy sh xi h nz) Today's homework is to write Chinese characters. Write each character 10 times. Use grid paper. In Africa, resources may be limited, but you can use a stick in the sand or a chalkboard. Practice pronunciation with YouTube videos. Remember tones: (sh) is high level, (sh) is falling. So (shxu) has a falling then rising tone. Mispronouncing can confuse. For example, (wn) means ask, but (wn) means kiss. So be careful!

Create a study schedule: Monday: math, Tuesday: science, etc. Say each day in Chinese: (xngqy), (xngq'r), etc. By the end of this lesson, you should be able to talk about your school day. Set a goal: learn 5 subjects and 10 classroom phrases per day. In one week, you'll know 35 subjects and 70 phrases. That's a solid foundation.

3. Talking About Your Daily Routine

In this lesson, you will learn to describe your daily routine in Chinese. Start with the time: (zoshang) morning, (zhngw) noon, (xi w) afternoon, (wnshang) evening. Common verbs: (qchung) get up, (shuy) brush teeth, (xlin) wash face, (ch zof n) eat breakfast, (q sh ngbn) go to work, (q sh ngxu) go to school, (ch wf n) eat lunch, (hu ji) go home, (shuji o) sleep. To say what time you do something: (w zoshang li din qchung) I get up at 6 a.m. Use (din) for o'clock. For half past: (b n). So (li din b n) 6:30.

In many African countries, daily routines vary. A farmer might wake at 5 a.m., while an office worker wakes at 6 a.m. Practice describing your own routine. Example: (w zoshang w din qchung, rnhu w q nngchng.) I get up at 5 a.m., then I go to the farm. Or (w q din ch zof n, b din q sh ngbn.) I eat breakfast at 7, go to work at 8. To ask about someone's routine: (n mitin j din qchung?) What time do you get up every day? Reply: (w mitin li din qchung.) I get up at 6 every day.

Use time words: (xin) first, (rnhu) then, (jizhe) next, (zuhu) finally. For example: (w xin shuy, rnhu ch zof n, jizhe q sh ngbn, zuhu hu ji.) First I brush my teeth, then eat breakfast, next go to work, finally go home. Practice with a partner: ask and answer about daily routines. Use a clock to practice telling time. Remember: (sh ngw) means morning (before noon), (xi w) means afternoon. So (sh ngw b din) 8 a.m., (xi w sn din) 3 p.m. In Chinese, time is expressed from largest to smallest: (nin) year, (yu) month, (r) day, /, (din) hour, (fn) minute. So 2025315830 (2025 nin 3 yu 15 r sh ngw 8 din 30 fn) March 15, 2025, 8:30 a.m.

Create a daily schedule in Chinese. Write it down and say it aloud. For example: 6:00 , 6:30 , 7:00 , 12:00 , 17:00 , 22:00 . Then practice with a friend: ask and answer. By the end of this lesson, you should be able to talk about your entire day. Set a goal: learn 10 routine verbs and 5 time expressions per day. In one week, you'll master 70 words and 35 phrases. That's enough to describe your day confidently.

4. Making Phone Calls and Leaving Messages

In this lesson, you will learn how to make phone calls and leave messages in Chinese. Start with basic phone vocabulary: (di nhu ) telephone, (shuj) mobile phone, (d di nhu ) make a call, (ji di nhu ) answer the phone, (gu di nhu ) hang up. To ask for someone: (wi, n ho, qngwn Zhng xinsheng z i ma?) Hello, may I speak to Mr. Zhang? If you are the caller, say: (w sh L Hu) This is Li Hua. If the person is not available: (t b z i) He is not here. Or (t zhngz i kihu) He is in a meeting. To leave a message: (w ky li g yn ma?) May I leave a message? Then say: (qng g osu t gi w hu di nhu ) Please tell him to call me back. Your number: ... (w de di nhu h om sh...) My phone number is...

In Africa, phone calls are crucial for business. Many people use WhatsApp or WeChat for voice calls. Practice common phrases: (xnh o b ho) bad signal, (w tng b qngchu) I can't hear clearly. To ask someone to repeat: (qng z i shu y bi n) Please say again. If you need to end the call: (dubuq, w xin gu le) Sorry, I have to hang up now. Or (wmen xi c z i lio) Let's talk again next time. When leaving a voicemail, speak slowly and clearly: 123456789(n ho, w sh Wng Fng. qng gi w hu di nhu , h om sh 123456789.) Hello, this is Wang Fang. Please call me back at 123456789.

Role-play with a partner. One calls the other, but the other is not available. Practice leaving a message. Use a script: A: B: A: B: A: B: (A: Hello. B: Hello, may I speak to Manager Li? A: He's not here. Who's calling? B: I'm his client, my name is John. Please tell him tomorrow's meeting is changed to 3 p.m. A: Okay, I'll tell him. B: Thanks!) Repeat until fluent. Also practice saying your phone number in Chinese. Numbers: 0 (lng), 1 (y), 2 (r), 3 (sn), 4 (s), 5 (w), 6 (li), 7 (q), 8 (b), 9 (ji). So 123456789 is y r sn s w li q b ji. Practice until you can say it quickly. In business, clear communication is key. By the end of this lesson, you should be able to make a simple call, leave a message, and understand basic responses. Set a goal: practice 5 phone dialogues per day. In one week, you'll be confident on the phone.

5. Writing a Simple Email in Chinese

In this lesson, you will learn to write a simple email in Chinese. Start with the structure: (zht) subject, (chnghu) salutation, (zhngwn) body, (jiwi) closing, (qinmng) signature. Common salutations: / (znjng de xinsheng/nsh) Dear Sir/Madam, or (qn' i de) Dear (for friends). For business, use . After the salutation, add a colon: . Then start the body. Common opening: (nn ho!) Hello! Then state your purpose: ... (w xi yuji n sh wile...) I am writing to... For example: (w xi yuji n sh wile xnwn guny gngzu de shqing.) I am writing to inquire about a job. Or (w xi yuji n sh wile qurn huy shjin.) I am writing to confirm the meeting time.

In the body, be clear and concise. Use simple sentences. For a job application: (w xing shnqng zhge zhwi.) I want to apply for this position. (w yu y nin gngzu jngy n.) I have one year of work experience. (w de jinl z i fji n l.) My resume is in the attachment. For a meeting request: (qngwn nn mngtin xi w sn din yu kng ma?) Are you free tomorrow at 3 p.m.? (rgu fngbi n, qng huf w.) If convenient, please reply. Closing: (xixie!) Thanks! Or (qd i nn de huf.) Looking forward to your reply. Then sign off: (czh jngl) Sincerely, or (zh ho) Best wishes. Then your name: (L Mng).

Practice writing a simple email. Use a template: (Subject: Job Application). / 123456789 (Subject: Job Application. Dear Sir/Madam: Hello! My name is Zhang Hua. I am writing to apply for the salesperson position. I have one year of sales experience. My phone number is 123456789. Thanks! Sincerely, Zhang Hua.) Write this in Chinese characters or pinyin. In Africa, email is common for business. Use Gmail or Outlook. Practice typing Chinese using a pinyin keyboard. Set your phone or computer to Chinese input. Type the email and send it to a friend for practice. Remember: Chinese emails often use formal language. Avoid slang. Keep sentences short. Use (nn) instead of (n) for respect. By the end of this lesson, you should be able to write a basic email in Chinese. Set a goal: write one email per day. In one week, you'll be comfortable with the format.

6. Role-Play: A Job Interview

In this lesson, you will practice a job interview in Chinese. Start with common interview questions: (qng jish o yxi n zj.) Please introduce yourself. (n wishnme xing shnqng zhge zhwi?) Why do you want to apply for this position? (n yu shnme gngzu jngy n?) What work experience do you have? (n de yudin h qudin sh shnme?) What are your strengths and weaknesses? (n qw ng de xnshu sh dusho?) What is your expected salary? Practice answering these. For example: 25(w ji o Wng L. w jnnin 25 su. w by y Nilub D xu. w yu ling nin xioshu jngy n.) My name is Wang Li. I am 25 years old. I graduated from the University of Nairobi. I have two years of sales experience.

In an interview, greet the interviewer: (nn ho!) Hello! Then shake hands if appropriate. In African contexts, a handshake is common. Listen carefully to the questions. If you don't understand, say: (dubuq, qng z i shu y bi n.) Sorry, please say again. To ask about the job: (zhge zhwi de zhy o zhz sh shnme?) What are the main responsibilities of this position? (gngs tgng pixn ma?) Does the company provide training? At the end, you can ask: (w shnme shhou nng zhd o jigu?) When will I know the result? Thank the interviewer: (xixie nn gi w zh c jhu.) Thank you for this opportunity.

Role-play with a partner. One plays interviewer, the other candidate. Use a script: Interviewer: (qng jn. n ho, qng zu.) Come in. Hello, please sit. Candidate: (nn ho, xixie.) Hello, thank you. Interviewer: (qng jish o yxi n zj.) Candidate: 30(w ji o L Mng, jnnin 30 su. w yu w nin ku ij jngy n. w z i Lgs gngzu gu.) My name is Li Ming, I am 30. I have five years of accounting experience. I worked in Lagos. Interviewer: (n wishnme li wmen gngs?) Why do you want to come to our company? Candidate: (ynwi gu gngs z i Zhnggu h Fizhu du yu yw, w xing yng w de jnng bngzh gngs fzhn.) Because your company has business in both China and Africa, I want to use my skills to help the company grow. Practice this dialogue until fluent. Record yourself and listen. Improve pronunciation. By the end of this lesson, you should be able to handle a basic job interview in Chinese. Set a goal: practice one full interview per day. In one week, you'll be confident.

Practical Reference: Chinese for Work and School in African Agribusiness

This practical reference consolidates the seven lessons in the Work and School module into a single, field-ready toolkit. It is written for African agribusiness professionals exporters, cooperative managers, extension officers, procurement staff, and students who need Mandarin to coordinate with Chinese buyers, suppliers, investors, training providers, and academic partners. The focus is on usable language for real transactions: negotiating a maize or cocoa contract, confirming a shipment of solar irrigation pumps, enrolling in a short course, or handling a call from a Guangzhou trading office.

Chinese is a tonal language. Getting the tone wrong changes meaning, and in business that can change a price, a quantity, or a delivery date. The reference below therefore pairs vocabulary with tone marks and practical phrases you can rehearse before a call or a meeting. Where relevant, examples use African agricultural contexts: cocoa from Ghana and Cte d'Ivoire, coffee from Ethiopia and Uganda, cashew from Tanzania and Nigeria, tea from Kenya and Rwanda, and horticulture from Kenya, Ethiopia, and South Africa.

1. Office Vocabulary and Job Titles

3. Talking About Your Daily Routine

Daily-routine language is useful for scheduling calls across time zones and for explaining your availability. Africa and China often differ by five to eight hours, so confirming times in both zones prevents missed meetings.

Key time expressions: (zoshang, morning), (sh ngw, before noon), (zhngw, noon), (xi w, afternoon), (wnshang, evening), (mitin, every day), (yushhou, sometimes), (xin, first), (rnhu, then), (zuhu, finally).

Model: (W mitin zoshang q din q cngk jinch kfi du. Rnhu ji din kihu. Xi w gi kh d di nhu .) "Every morning at seven I go to the warehouse to check the coffee beans. Then at nine I have a meeting. In the afternoon I call clients."

For scheduling with Chinese partners, use: ? (Nmen n bin j din?) "What time is it on your side?" This avoids confusion during harvest-season coordination.

5. Writing a Simple Email in Chinese

Chinese business emails follow a predictable structure: greeting, self-introduction, purpose, action request, closing. Keep sentences short. Use (nn ho) for a respectful greeting to a senior counterpart, and (n ho) for peers.

Template:

Safety Warning: Contract, Payment, and Quality Verification

Never confirm a price, quantity, or delivery date in Chinese unless you have verified it in writing and, where possible, with a qualified interpreter. Tone and character errors can change figures and units. In agribusiness, always insist on:

  • Written confirmation of grade, moisture, and aflatoxin limits before shipment.
  • Payment terms in writing, with bank details verified through a second channel to prevent fraud.
  • Independent inspection (for example, SGS or Bureau Veritas) for cocoa, cashew, coffee, and horticulture consignments.
  • Compliance with destination-market rules, including Codex Alimentarius standards and EU or Chinese import requirements.
