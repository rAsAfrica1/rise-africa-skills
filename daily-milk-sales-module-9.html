1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Calf rearing is the foundation of your dairy herd. A healthy calf today becomes a high-milk cow tomorrow. In Africa, many farmers lose calves to preventable diseases like scours (diarrhoea) and pneumonia. Each calf lost is a loss of future income. If you raise 10 calves successfully, you could have 10 new milking cows in two years, doubling your daily milk sales. Good calf rearing also means better growth: a well-fed calf can weigh 70-80 kg at 3 months and 200 kg at 6 months. That means earlier first calving at 24-30 months instead of 36-40 months, saving you feed costs and bringing milk sooner. In this module, you will learn practical steps to rear calves with limited resources. We focus on the first 12 weeks, the most critical period. You will learn how to feed colostrum, manage milk feeding, provide clean water and starter feed, and keep housing dry and clean. We also cover common mistakes like overfeeding milk or using dirty buckets. By the end, you will be able to set up a simple calf pen using local materials like poles, thatch, and wire mesh. You will also know how to spot sickness early and treat it. Remember, each calf you raise well is money in your pocket. Let's get started.

2. Tools and Materials

To rear calves successfully, you need basic tools and materials. Most can be found locally or made by a village carpenter. First, a calf pen: build a dry, well-ventilated shelter. Use wooden poles for posts (2-3 meters tall), thatch or iron sheets for roofing, and wire mesh or wooden slats for walls. The floor should be slightly sloped for drainage, covered with dry bedding like straw or wood shavings. Each calf needs at least 2 square meters of space. Second, feeding equipment: a clean plastic bucket (10-20 litres) for milk, a smaller bucket (5 litres) for water, and a calf bottle with a teat for young calves. You can also use a nipple bucket. Third, for feeding: fresh cow colostrum (first milk) within 2 hours of birth, then whole milk or milk replacer. If using milk replacer, mix 125g powder per litre of warm water (40C). Fourth, for health: a thermometer, iodine for navel dipping, and a first-aid kit with antibiotics (consult vet), electrolytes for scours, and dewormer. Fifth, for record keeping: a notebook and pen to record birth date, feeding amounts, and health events. Sixth, for cleaning: a stiff brush, soap, and disinfectant. Also, have a calf jacket or old blanket for cold nights. Finally, a simple scale or weigh tape to monitor growth. All these items cost less than $100 and will save you many times that in calf survival.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps for successful calf rearing from birth to 12 weeks. Step 1: Immediately after birth, ensure the calf breathes. Clear mucus from nose and mouth. Dip navel in iodine to prevent infection. Step 2: Feed colostrum within 2 hours. Give 2 litres for a small calf, 3 litres for a large one. Colostrum is thick and yellow; if the dam cannot suckle, milk her by hand and feed the calf with a clean bottle. Feed colostrum for the first 3 days, 10% of body weight daily (e.g., 3 litres for a 30 kg calf). Step 3: From day 4 to week 2, feed whole milk at 10% of body weight per day, split into 2-3 feeds. For a 35 kg calf, that's 3.5 litres daily. Always feed at body temperature (38C). Step 4: From week 3, introduce calf starter (dry feed) and fresh clean water. Offer a handful of starter after each milk feed. Increase gradually to 1 kg per day by week 8. Step 5: From week 5, reduce milk slowly. By week 8, feed only 1-2 litres per day. By week 12, wean completely if the calf eats 1.5 kg starter daily. Step 6: Provide hay or fresh grass from week 2. Step 7: Keep the pen clean: remove wet bedding daily, disinfect weekly. Step 8: Watch for sickness: scours (loose dung), pneumonia (coughing), or dullness. Treat scours with electrolytes and consult a vet. Step 9: Record everything: birth weight, daily milk, starter intake, and health events. Step 10: Weigh monthly to check growth. A healthy calf should gain 400-600g per day. If not, adjust feeding.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Many farmers lose calves due to avoidable mistakes. Here are common ones and how to fix them. Mistake 1: Not feeding colostrum soon enough. Colostrum must be given within 2 hours; after 6 hours, the calf cannot absorb antibodies. Fix: Always have a clean bottle ready and milk the dam immediately after birth. Mistake 2: Overfeeding milk. Giving too much milk causes scours. Fix: Stick to 10% of body weight per day, split into 2-3 feeds. For a 40 kg calf, that's 4 litres total, not 6. Mistake 3: Using dirty buckets. Bacteria cause scours. Fix: Wash buckets with hot water and soap after each feed, then rinse with disinfectant. Mistake 4: Feeding cold milk. Cold milk shocks the calf's stomach. Fix: Warm milk to 38C (test with your wrist; it should feel neither hot nor cold). Mistake 5: Poor housing. Damp, drafty pens cause pneumonia. Fix: Keep bedding dry, ensure ventilation but no direct draft. Mistake 6: Weaning too early. Calves weaned before 8 weeks often stunt. Fix: Wean at 8-12 weeks only when eating 1.5 kg starter daily. Mistake 7: Ignoring sickness. Early signs like loose dung or coughing need quick action. Fix: Check calves daily; treat scours with electrolytes and call vet for pneumonia. Mistake 8: Not recording. Without records, you cannot spot problems. Fix: Keep a simple notebook. Mistake 9: Mixing calves of different ages. Older calves bully younger ones and spread disease. Fix: Group calves by age in separate pens. Mistake 10: Using antibiotics without vet advice. This leads to resistance. Fix: Consult a vet before treating. By avoiding these mistakes, you can achieve over 90% calf survival.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Calf rearing involves safety for both you and the calf. Follow these best practices. Personal safety: Always wash your hands with soap after handling calves, especially before eating. Wear boots and gloves when cleaning pens to avoid infections like leptospirosis. When lifting a calf, bend your knees, not your back. Calf safety: Handle calves gently. Never shout or hit them; this causes stress and poor growth. When moving a calf, use a halter or guide it with your arm around its chest. Ensure the pen has no sharp edges or wires that can cut. Best practices for health: Vaccinate calves according to vet advice (e.g., for anthrax, blackleg). Deworm at 4 and 8 weeks. Keep a footbath at the pen entrance to prevent disease spread. For feeding: Use clean, warm water for mixing milk replacer. Never feed spoiled milk or milk from a cow treated with antibiotics (withdrawal period). For housing: Ensure good drainage to prevent muddy, wet floors. Provide shade in hot weather and windbreaks in cold. For records: Record all treatments, vaccinations, and feed changes. For emergencies: Have a vet's phone number. If a calf has severe scours, isolate it and give electrolytes. If pneumonia, call vet immediately. For biosecurity: Limit visitors to the calf pen. Do not bring in new calves without quarantine. For environmental safety: Dispose of dead calves properly (deep burial or incineration) to prevent disease spread. By following these practices, you protect your investment and your health.

6. Practical Exercise

Now put your knowledge into action with this practical exercise. You will rear a calf from birth to 12 weeks, recording all steps. If you don't have a calf, work with a neighbour or practice with a model. Step 1: Prepare a calf pen. Use local materials: poles, thatch, wire mesh. Ensure it is dry, clean, and has 2 square meters per calf. Step 2: Gather tools: bucket, bottle, thermometer, iodine, notebook. Step 3: At birth, dip navel in iodine, weigh the calf (use a scale or weigh tape). Record birth weight and date. Step 4: Feed 2-3 litres of colostrum within 2 hours. Record time and amount. Step 5: For the first 3 days, feed colostrum 3 times daily. Then switch to whole milk at 10% body weight. For a 35 kg calf, feed 3.5 litres daily, split into 2 feeds. Step 6: From week 1, offer clean water and a handful of starter after each milk feed. Increase starter gradually. Step 7: Weigh the calf weekly and record. Calculate average daily gain. Target: 400-600g per day. Step 8: Observe health daily. Check for scours, coughing, or dullness. If scours, give electrolytes. If not better in 24 hours, call vet. Step 9: At week 8, if calf eats 1.5 kg starter daily, reduce milk to 1 litre per day. At week 12, stop milk completely. Step 10: At week 12, weigh again. A healthy calf should weigh 70-90 kg. Write a one-page report: what went well, what challenges, and what you would change. Share with your group. This exercise builds real skills for your dairy business.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Calf Rearing for Profitable Daily Milk Sales

Calf rearing is the single most important investment a dairy enterprise makes in its future. Every litre of milk sold today funds the replacement heifer that will produce tomorrow. In East and Southern Africa, where smallholder dairy systems supply over 80% of marketed milk, calf mortality rates on farms without structured rearing programmes commonly reach 2030%. Each lost calf represents not just an animal, but two to three years of lost future income, plus the sunk cost of the dam's gestation and lactation. This practical reference consolidates the tools, methods, benchmarks, and safety practices needed to rear calves successfully under African smallholder and emerging commercial conditions.

SAFETY WARNING: Colostrum and Disease Control

Colostrum must be collected from a healthy dam with no signs of mastitis, Johnes disease, or brucellosis. Always use clean, disinfected containers. If using pooled colostrum, only pool from dams of the same herd with known health status. Never feed colostrum from a dam with a retained placenta or fever. Brucellosis and tuberculosis can be transmitted through raw colostrum and milk pasteurise if disease status is unknown. Always wash hands and equipment with soap and clean water before and after handling calves. Wear protective gloves when assisting with difficult births. In case of scours (diarrhoea), isolate the calf immediately and consult a veterinarian do not share feeding equipment between sick and healthy calves.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Colostrum within 2 hours: Feed 10% of body weight (34 L for a 35 kg calf) within the first 2 hours of birth, and another 10% within 68 hours.
  2. Clean, dry, draught-free housing: Individual calf pens or hutches with dry bedding changed daily. Minimum 1.5 m per calf.
  3. Consistent milk feeding: 10% of body weight per day, split into 2 feeds, at body temperature (38C). Use a nipple bottle for first 2 weeks.
  4. Fresh water always available: Calves need water from day 1 for rumen development. Change at least twice daily.
  5. Starter feed from week 1: Offer small handfuls of high-quality calf starter daily. Target 1 kg/day by week 10 before weaning.
  6. Weigh and record weekly: Use a weigh tape or scale. Target 400500 g/day gain. Record all weights in a calf register.
  7. Health monitoring: Check eyes, ears, nose, faeces, and navel daily. Treat scours and pneumonia immediately.
  8. Disinfect equipment and pens: Between calves, and weekly during rearing. Use approved disinfectants and allow to dry.
