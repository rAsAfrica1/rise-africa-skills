Lessons

1. Overview of Electronics Assembly

Electronics assembly is the process of putting together electronic components to create working devices. In Africa, this skill is in high demand for repairing radios, phones, solar charge controllers, and inverters. You don't need a factory to start; many successful technicians began with a simple workbench at home. This module will teach you the basics using tools and materials you can find locally.

A typical assembly job starts with a circuit board (PCB). You will learn to identify components like resistors, capacitors, and integrated circuits. For example, a common resistor might have color bands that tell you its resistance is 220 ohms. You will place these components on the board and solder them in place. In many African cities, you can buy a basic soldering iron for 2,000 to 5,000 Naira (or equivalent) from electronics markets like Alaba in Lagos or Kariakoo in Dar es Salaam.

Safety is critical. Always work in a well-ventilated area and wear safety glasses. You will also learn about Electrostatic Discharge (ESD), which can destroy sensitive chips. Simple precautions like wearing a wrist strap and working on a grounded mat can save you money and time. In this module, you will practice through-hole soldering, which is the foundation for more advanced surface-mount work.

By the end of this module, you will be able to assemble a simple circuit, such as an LED flasher or a small power supply. You will also learn to read schematics and use hand tools like pliers, cutters, and screwdrivers. These skills are the first step toward becoming a professional electronics assembler or starting your own repair business. Remember, practice is key. Set aside at least 30 minutes each day to work on your soldering technique.

2. Safety and ESD Precautions

Electronics assembly can be dangerous if you ignore safety rules. The two biggest risks are electric shock and damage to components from Electrostatic Discharge (ESD). In Africa, where power surges are common, always unplug your soldering iron and test equipment when not in use. Never work on live circuits. A 220-240V mains supply can kill you. Use a multimeter to check for voltage before touching any wires.

ESD is a silent killer of electronic parts. Static electricity builds up on your body, especially in dry climates like the Sahel. A small spark you can't feel can destroy a microcontroller. To prevent ESD, wear a wrist strap connected to a grounded mat. If you can't afford one, touch a grounded metal object (like a water pipe) before handling sensitive components. Store chips in anti-static foam or pink bubble bags. Never wear synthetic clothing like polyester when working; cotton is safer.

Soldering irons get very hotup to 400C. Always place the iron in its stand when not in use. Keep a damp sponge or brass wool nearby to clean the tip. Never flick solder; it can fly into your eyes. Wear safety glasses. Work in a well-ventilated area to avoid breathing flux fumes. If you feel dizzy, stop and get fresh air. Keep a fire extinguisher or a bucket of sand nearby for electrical fires. Never use water on an electrical fire.

Organize your workspace. Keep drinks away from your bench. Use a smoke absorber or a small fan to blow fumes away from your face. In many African workshops, a simple 12V computer fan powered by a small solar panel works well. Teach yourself to double-check connections before powering up. A short circuit can burn components and start a fire. By following these precautions, you protect yourself, your tools, and your customers' equipment.

3. Hand Tools and Their Uses

To assemble electronics, you need a basic set of hand tools. You can buy these from local markets or online. Start with a soldering iron (30-60W), solder wire (0.8mm rosin-core), a pair of flush cutters, needle-nose pliers, a screwdriver set, and a multimeter. A good soldering iron costs around 3,000-8,000 Naira. Don't buy the cheapest; a reliable iron maintains temperature and has replaceable tips.

Flush cutters are for trimming component leads after soldering. Use them only for cutting soft metals like copper; never cut steel or they will dull. Needle-nose pliers help you bend component leads and hold small parts. They also help you reach into tight spaces. Screwdrivers come in flathead and Phillips; get a set with various sizes. A multimeter measures voltage, current, and resistance. A basic digital multimeter costs about 5,000 Naira and is essential for troubleshooting.

Other useful tools include a desoldering pump (for removing solder), tweezers (for placing small parts), and a helping hands tool with magnifying glass. In Africa, you can improvise: a wooden clothespin can hold wires, and a heavy metal nut can act as a heat sink. Keep your tools clean and dry. Wipe them with a cloth after use. Store them in a toolbox to prevent rust, especially in humid coastal areas.

Learn to use each tool properly. For example, when using flush cutters, wear safety glasses to protect your eyes from flying leads. When using pliers, don't squeeze too hard or you may damage component leads. Practice picking up a resistor with tweezers and placing it on a board. The more you practice, the faster and more accurate you become. Remember, good tools don't make you a good assemblerpractice does. But poor tools will slow you down and cause mistakes.

4. Reading Schematics and Assembly Drawings

A schematic is a map of an electronic circuit. It uses symbols to represent components and lines to show connections. Learning to read schematics is like learning a new language. Start with basic symbols: a resistor is a zigzag line, a capacitor is two parallel lines, and a diode is a triangle with a line. In Africa, many repair manuals use schematics, so this skill is vital. You can find free schematic symbols charts online or in old textbooks.

An assembly drawing shows where components go on a printed circuit board (PCB). It often includes a silkscreen layer with component outlines and reference designators like R1, C2, or U3. The schematic tells you how things connect electrically, while the assembly drawing tells you where to physically place them. For example, a schematic might show a resistor connected between a chip pin and ground, but the assembly drawing shows its exact location on the board.

To read a schematic, follow the lines from the power source to ground. Identify key components: resistors limit current, capacitors store energy, transistors amplify signals, and ICs perform complex functions. Look for labels like +5V, GND, and signal names. In many African projects, you'll see 12V or 24V systems for solar. Pay attention to polarity: electrolytic capacitors have a positive and negative lead; diodes have a band indicating the cathode.

Practice by taking a simple circuit, like an LED with a resistor, and drawing its schematic. Then compare it to a real board. Use a multimeter to trace connections. If you get stuck, ask a local technician or join an online forum. Many African electronics communities share knowledge on WhatsApp groups. With time, you'll be able to look at a schematic and visualize the physical assembly. This skill will save you hours of troubleshooting.

5. Introduction to Soldering

Soldering is the process of joining metal parts with a filler metal called solder. In electronics, we use soft solder made of tin and lead (or lead-free for health reasons). The soldering iron heats the joint, and the solder melts and flows to create an electrical and mechanical connection. A good solder joint is shiny, smooth, and concave. A bad joint looks dull, blobby, or cracked.

Start by preparing your iron. Set the temperature to about 350C for leaded solder or 380C for lead-free. Clean the tip with a damp sponge or brass wool, then apply a small amount of solder to tin the tip. This helps heat transfer. For through-hole soldering, insert the component lead through the hole, bend it slightly to hold it, then touch the iron to both the pad and the lead for 1-2 seconds. Feed solder into the joint, not the iron. Remove solder first, then the iron. The whole process should take 2-4 seconds.

Common mistakes include using too much solder (causing bridges), not enough heat (cold joints), or moving the component before the solder cools. In Africa, power fluctuations can affect iron temperature; use a voltage stabilizer if possible. If you don't have one, unplug the iron when not in use to prevent overheating. Practice on a scrap board first. You can buy practice kits online or salvage old circuit boards from broken electronics.

Flux is your friend. It cleans the metal and helps solder flow. Most solder wire has flux inside (rosin core). If you need more, apply flux paste with a toothpick. After soldering, clean the board with isopropyl alcohol and a brush to remove flux residue. This prevents corrosion and makes inspection easier. Remember, soldering is a skill that improves with practice. Set a goal to solder 50 joints a day until they all look perfect.

6. Basic Through-Hole Soldering Practice

Now it's time to practice through-hole soldering. You will need a practice board, some resistors, LEDs, and a soldering iron. If you don't have a practice board, you can use a piece of perfboard (stripboard) from a local electronics shop. Start by inserting a resistor into two adjacent holes. Bend the leads to keep it in place. Flip the board over and solder the two leads. Aim for a shiny cone-shaped joint. Trim the excess leads with flush cutters.

Repeat this with 20 resistors. Then move on to LEDs. LEDs have polarity: the longer lead is positive (anode), and the shorter lead is negative (cathode). There is also a flat side on the plastic lens indicating the cathode. Solder them correctly. If you reverse an LED, it won't light up. Practice soldering 10 LEDs. Next, try a capacitor. Electrolytic capacitors also have polarity; the stripe with minus signs indicates the negative lead. Solder a few.

As you practice, pay attention to heat. If a joint takes more than 3 seconds, your iron may be too cold or the tip is dirty. Clean and re-tin. If solder balls up, you need more flux or higher heat. If it bridges two pads, use a desoldering pump or solder wick to remove the excess. In Africa, many technicians use a simple bulb desoldering pump that costs about 1,500 Naira. Keep one handy.

After soldering, inspect your work with a magnifying glass. Look for cracks, cold joints, and bridges. Use a multimeter to check continuity between connected points. Test your board with a battery and a resistor to light an LED. If it works, congratulations! You've just assembled your first circuit. If not, troubleshoot: check for solder bridges, reversed components, or poor joints. With practice, you'll be able to solder a 40-pin IC in minutes. Remember, consistency is key. Set aside time each day to practice, and soon you'll be ready for more advanced projects.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Electronics Assembly in African Workshops

This practical reference is the working companion to the seven lessons in Introduction to Electronics Assembly. It is written for the technician, apprentice, or small-business owner who needs to assemble, repair, or prototype electronic boards and sell that capability as a service. In many African markets the money is not in manufacturing components it is in assembly, repair, customisation, and after-sales support. A technician who can reliably solder a through-hole board, read a schematic, and protect a circuit from static damage can charge for repairs, build small-run products (LED drivers, sensor nodes, power supplies, school kits), and win contracts from NGOs, farms, clinics, and telecom installers.

This reference assumes a bench, a soldering iron, hand tools, and a multimeter. It does not assume mains power stability, air conditioning, or a full ESD-controlled room. The techniques below are adapted to real conditions: dusty workshops, shared benches, generator power, and components bought from open-air markets where authenticity cannot be guaranteed.

Why these standards matter to an African business

Buyers of electronic goods schools, clinics, security firms, solar installers, and agro-processors increasingly demand reliability. A cold joint that fails after two weeks costs you a return trip, a replacement, and your reputation. Learning to judge a joint against a published standard (IPC-A-610, NASA-STD-8739.3) gives you a defence when a client questions quality, and it gives your apprentice a clear target to aim at. SAFETY WARNING LEAD AND FUMES

Leaded solder (Sn63/Pb37) contains lead. Lead accumulates in the body and causes neurological and reproductive harm. Never eat, drink, or smoke at the bench. Wash hands with soap after every session, especially before eating. Keep solder away from children. Solder flux fumes irritate the airways work near an open window or use a small extractor fan; do not breathe directly over the joint. If you are pregnant or breastfeeding, avoid leaded solder entirely and use lead-free alloy with extraction.

In workshops without ventilation, the risk is higher. A simple 12 V computer fan with a carbon filter, mounted 20 cm from the work, costs less than a rework job and protects your lungs for years.

Step-by-step: a reliable through-hole joint

  1. Prepare the iron. Set the tip to the temperature in Table 1. Wipe the tip on brass wool and tin it immediately with a thin film of solder. A black, oxidised tip transfers heat poorly and will damage pads.
  2. Insert the component. Bend the leads to match the hole spacing. For resistors and diodes, bend with long-nose pliers so the body sits flat. For ICs, insert the notch or dot toward pin 1 check the schematic and the assembly drawing before soldering.
  3. Heat the joint, not the solder. Touch the tip to both the pad and the lead at the same time. Count one second. This is the single most important habit in soldering.
  4. Feed solder into the joint. Apply solder to the heated pad/lead, not to the iron tip. The solder should melt on contact and flow into a concave fillet. Two to three seconds is enough for leaded solder.
  5. Remove solder, then iron. Pull the solder wire away first, then lift the iron. This prevents a spike or icicle.
  6. Inspect. The joint should be shiny (leaded) or slightly dull (lead-free), tent-shaped, and wet both pad and lead. A ball, a dull grey blob, or a joint that looks like a small volcano is a cold joint reheat and add a touch of fresh solder.
  7. Trim the lead. Cut flush with side cutters, leaving about 1 mm above the joint. Do not cut so short that you stress the pad.
  8. Clean. Remove flux residue with isopropyl alcohol and a brush. Flux left on the board attracts dust and moisture, and in humid coastal workshops it can cause leakage currents.

Adapting to African operating conditions

Power. If your iron runs from mains and the supply fluctuates, use a small UPS or a 12 V/24 V soldering iron with a battery. A temperature-controlled station fed by unstable voltage will not hold its setpoint, and joints will vary. Test with a multimeter before blaming your technique.

Humidity and dust. Coastal and rainy-season humidity accelerates corrosion on pads and leads. Store components in sealed bags with silica gel. Clean boards after assembly. Dust on a hot iron tip causes oxidation keep the brass wool covered.

Component sourcing. Market components may be recycled, re-marked, or out of tolerance. Buy from a supplier who allows returns, test a sample with a multimeter before committing to a batch, and keep a small stock of known-good parts for critical jobs.

ESD. In dry highland climates (Nairobi, Addis Ababa, Johannesburg winter), static builds fast on synthetic clothing and plastic chairs. Wear a wrist strap bonded to a common ground point, work on an anti-static mat, and never slide boards across a plastic table. CMOS ICs, MOSFETs, and microcontroller boards are the most vulnerable.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Iron tip tinned, clean, and at the correct temperature for the alloy (Table 1).
  • Wrist strap worn and bonded to ground; anti-static mat in place.
  • Component orientation checked against schematic and assembly drawing before soldering.
  • Heat pad and lead together for one second before feeding solder.
  • Joint is concave, shiny (leaded) or slightly dull (lead-free), and wets both surfaces.
  • Leads trimmed to about 1 mm; no solder bridges between adjacent pads.
  • Flux residue cleaned with isopropyl alcohol; board inspected under magnification.
  • Hands washed with soap after the session; no food or drink at the bench.
