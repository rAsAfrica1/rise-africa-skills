1. Pre-Slaughter Planning and Live Bird Handling

Successful slaughter starts long before the knife touches the bird. In African broiler production, the difference between a profitable processing day and a loss-making one is usually decided in the 24 hours before slaughter. This lesson covers the practical planning, feed withdrawal, catching, crating, transport and lairage management that protect carcass quality and your margins. Start with a slaughter plan. Whether you are processing 50 birds for a weekend market or 5,000 for a supermarket contract, write down the date, the number of birds, the target live weight, the customer specifications, the staff required, the water and electricity needs, and the cold chain plan. In most African settings the biggest constraint is cold chain. If you cannot chill carcasses to below 4 degrees Celsius within a reasonable time, you must reduce batch size or invest in ice, a chill tank or a blast chiller before you scale up. Never plan a slaughter day that your chilling capacity cannot handle. Feed withdrawal is the next critical step. Withdraw feed 8 to 12 hours before slaughter. This empties the digestive tract, reduces the risk of carcass contamination from gut contents during evisceration, and saves feed. Do not withdraw water. Birds must have free access to clean water right up to catching. Water withdrawal causes dehydration, shrinks carcass yield and increases stress. If you are in a hot climate, consider adding electrolytes or vitamin C to the drinking water during the withdrawal period to reduce heat stress. A common mistake is withdrawing feed too early, sometimes 18 to 24 hours, which causes weight loss and poor carcass finish. Stick to 8 to 12 hours. Catching must be done calmly and in low light. Bright light excites birds and causes wing flapping, bruising and broken bones. Use blue or red dim lights if possible. Catch birds by the legs, never by the wings or neck. Two people should work together, one catching and one crating. In small-scale African operations, catching is often done at night or early morning when temperatures are lower and birds are calm. This reduces heat stress and bruising. Avoid chasing birds around the house. Corner them gently using a catching pen or a simple hurdle. Crates must be clean, disinfected and well ventilated. Overcrowding in crates is a major cause of bruising, suffocation and death on arrival. As a rule, allow enough space for each bird to sit comfortably without piling. In hot weather, reduce the number of birds per crate by 20 to 30 percent. Never stack crates so high that airflow is blocked. Transport should be done in the coolest part of the day, ideally early morning or evening. Avoid the hottest hours between 11am and 4pm. The vehicle should have open sides for ventilation but should protect birds from direct sun and rain. Drive smoothly. Sudden braking and sharp corners cause birds to pile up and bruise. The journey should be as short as possible. If you must travel long distances, plan rest stops in shade and check the birds. Mortality during transport is a direct financial loss. On arrival at the processing point, birds need lairage, a resting period in a shaded, ventilated area. Lairage allows birds to recover from transport stress and helps empty the gut further. A rest period of 1 to 2 hours is ideal. Do not feed during lairage. Provide water if the rest period is longer than 2 hours. If birds are slaughtered immediately after arrival, the meat may be pale, soft and exudative, especially in heat-stressed birds. Lairage also gives you time to inspect birds for abnormalities, injuries or disease. Any bird that is sick, dead on arrival or severely bruised should be removed and handled separately. Do not process dead birds for human consumption. Record everything. Keep a simple log of the number of birds caught, the number that died during transport, the number condemned at ante-mortem inspection, and the total live weight. This data helps you calculate shrink, mortality and yield, which are the key performance indicators of your processing operation. In Africa, many small processors lose 5 to 10 percent of birds between farm and slaughter. With good handling, you can reduce this to 1 to 2 percent. That difference is pure profit. Finally, train your team. Catching and handling are skills. A well-trained team works faster, causes less stress and produces better carcasses. Hold a short briefing before every slaughter day. Remind everyone of the plan, the roles and the welfare rules. Good pre-slaughter management is not just about animal welfare. It is about business. Every bruise, every death, every stressed bird costs you money. Handle birds well and your carcass quality, your yield and your reputation will improve.

2. Humane Slaughter, Bleeding and Scalding

The slaughter process itself is short but it determines the safety, appearance and shelf life of the final product. This lesson covers humane slaughter, effective bleeding, scalding and the early steps that set the stage for good carcass quality. In Africa, many small and medium processors use manual methods. That is acceptable if the methods are humane, hygienic and consistent. The goal is to kill the bird quickly, bleed it thoroughly and prepare the carcass for plucking without damaging the skin. Humane slaughter means the bird loses consciousness and dies without unnecessary pain, fear or distress. The most common method in small-scale operations is neck cutting, also called sticking. The bird is restrained, usually hung upside down in a cone or shackle, and a sharp knife is used to cut the jugular vein and carotid artery on one or both sides of the neck. The cut should be deep enough to sever the blood vessels but should not cut the windpipe or the spinal cord completely. A bird whose spinal cord is severed may still show reflex movements, which can be alarming but does not indicate consciousness. However, a proper neck cut that leaves the brain intact allows the bird to bleed out while unconscious. The knife must be sharp, clean and used only for slaughter. Dull knives cause pain and poor bleeding. Use a sharpening steel regularly and replace knives when they are worn. In larger operations, electrical stunning is used before neck cutting. Stunning makes the bird unconscious so it feels no pain during the cut. A simple stunner can be made or purchased. The key parameters are voltage, current and duration. Too little stunning does not render the bird unconscious. Too much causes broken bones and blood spots in the meat. If you use stunning, follow the manufacturer instructions and train your staff. Whether you stun or not, the neck cut must be done within a few seconds. Bleeding must be complete. Incomplete bleeding leaves blood in the muscles, which causes dark, unattractive meat and shortens shelf life. Hang the bird so the head is down and allow 2 to 3 minutes for bleeding. Do not rush this step. During bleeding, the bird should be in a clean, well-drained area. Blood should flow into a container or drain, not onto the floor where it can contaminate other carcasses. After bleeding, the bird is ready for scalding. Scalding loosens the feathers so they can be plucked easily. The water temperature and the time in the water are critical. If the water is too hot or the bird stays too long, the skin cooks and tears. If the water is too cool or the time is too short, feathers are hard to remove and you damage the skin trying to pull them out. For broilers, the ideal scalding temperature is 58 to 62 degrees Celsius. The time is 60 to 90 seconds for soft scalding, which is the most common method for broilers. Soft scalding preserves the skin, which is important for appearance and for customers who eat the skin. Hard scalding at 65 to 70 degrees Celsius for 30 to 60 seconds removes feathers more easily but damages the skin and gives a cooked appearance. Use soft scalding for broilers. In Africa, many processors use a simple pot over a fire or a gas burner. This works if you control the temperature. Use a thermometer. Do not guess. Stir the water and the birds to ensure even heat distribution. If you are processing many birds, the water temperature will drop as you add birds. Reheat as needed. Change the water regularly. Scalding water becomes contaminated with feathers, dirt and bacteria. Change it after every 20 to 30 birds or when it becomes visibly dirty. Clean water is essential for clean carcasses. After scalding, pluck immediately. The feathers come off more easily while the bird is still warm. Manual plucking is common in small operations. Use your hands or a simple rubber finger plucker. Work quickly and gently. Do not tear the skin. For larger volumes, a drum plucker or a motorized plucker saves time and labor. If you use a mechanical plucker, do not overload it. Overloading causes bruising and broken bones. After plucking, singe off any remaining feathers and fine hairs. This is usually done with a gas torch or a flame. Be careful not to burn the skin. A light pass is enough. Singeing also helps kill some surface bacteria and improves the appearance of the carcass. The next step is washing. Wash the carcass thoroughly with clean, cold water. This removes blood, feathers, dirt and bacteria. Do not use warm water, which encourages bacterial growth. If you have chlorinated water, use it at a concentration of 50 to 100 parts per million. If not, clean water is better than no water. After washing, the carcass is ready for evisceration. But before that, check the carcass for defects. Look for bruises, cuts, broken bones, skin tears and signs of disease. Any carcass with a serious defect should be set aside for condemnation or trimming. Do not send defective carcasses to customers. Your reputation depends on consistent quality. Record your slaughter data. How many birds did you slaughter? How many were condemned? What was the average live weight and the average carcass weight? This data helps you calculate dressing percentage, which is the carcass weight divided by the live weight, multiplied by 100. A good dressing percentage for broilers is 70 to 75 percent. If your dressing percentage is low, check your bleeding, scalding and plucking. Poor bleeding and skin damage reduce yield. In summary, humane slaughter, complete bleeding and correct scalding are the foundation of good carcass quality. They are not complicated, but they require attention to detail, clean equipment and trained staff. Get these steps right and the rest of the processing line becomes much easier.

3. Evisceration, Chilling and Carcass Safety

Evisceration is the most critical step for food safety. This is where the gut is removed, and if it is done carelessly, feces and bacteria can contaminate the carcass. In Africa, where cold chain and clean water can be challenging, careful evisceration and rapid chilling are essential to produce safe, marketable chicken. Start with clean hands and clean tools. Every worker should wash their hands before starting and after touching anything dirty. Use soap and clean water. If you have gloves, wear them. Change gloves if they become torn or dirty. Knives, scissors and evisceration spoons must be clean and sanitized. Use a separate knife for opening the abdomen and for cutting the vent. Do not use the same knife for cutting the neck and opening the gut. Cross-contamination is the biggest risk. The evisceration process begins with opening the abdomen. Make a small cut just below the vent, being careful not to cut into the intestines. Then cut around the vent, freeing it from the skin. Pull the vent and the intestines out gently. Do not pull hard, which can tear the gut and spill feces. Use your hand or a evisceration spoon to scoop out the internal organs. The heart, liver and gizzard are edible and should be saved if your market wants them. The lungs and kidneys are embedded in the carcass and are usually removed with a scraper or by hand. The head and feet are removed at this stage or earlier, depending on your market. In many African markets, customers want the head and feet. In others, they want them removed. Know your market. After evisceration, inspect the carcass and the organs. Look for signs of disease: enlarged liver, spots on the liver, fluid in the abdomen, swollen joints, or a bad smell. Any carcass with signs of disease must be condemned. Do not sell it. Do not eat it. Dispose of it safely by burning or burying deep. Do not throw it in the river or on the ground. This spreads disease and creates a public health risk. The most important step after evisceration is washing. Wash the carcass inside and out with clean, cold water. Use a spray or a gentle stream. Do not use a high-pressure spray, which can spread bacteria. If you have chlorinated water, use it at 50 to 100 parts per million. If not, use the cleanest water you have. Change the water frequently. Do not wash carcasses in a tub of standing water where bacteria accumulate. Use running water or change the water after every few carcasses. After washing, the carcass must be chilled quickly. This is the single most important step for shelf life and safety. Bacteria multiply rapidly at warm temperatures. In the tropics, a carcass left at 30 degrees Celsius can double its bacterial load every 20 minutes. You must bring the carcass temperature down to below 4 degrees Celsius as quickly as possible. The best method is a chill tank with ice water. Use clean water and ice. The temperature should be 0 to 4 degrees Celsius. Add the carcasses and stir gently. The chilling time depends on the size of the carcass and the amount of ice. For a 1.5 kilogram carcass, it takes about 30 to 60 minutes to chill. Check the temperature with a probe thermometer. Do not guess. If you do not have a chill tank, use a refrigerator or a cold room. Do not stack carcasses too high. Air must circulate. If you have no electricity, use ice in insulated boxes. This is common in many African markets. It works if you use enough ice and keep the box closed. Do not open the box frequently. The cold chain must be maintained from chilling to sale. If the carcass warms up, bacteria grow and shelf life drops. In hot climates, sell chilled chicken within 24 to 48 hours. If you freeze it, freeze it quickly and keep it frozen. Do not refreeze thawed chicken. Hygiene in the processing area is critical. The floor should be concrete or another washable surface. It should slope to a drain. Blood and water should not pool. Clean and disinfect the area before and after processing. Use a disinfectant like bleach or a commercial sanitizer. Wash crates, tables, knives and aprons. Do not let dogs, cats or rodents into the processing area. They carry bacteria and parasites. Workers should wear clean clothes and boots. Hair should be covered. No eating, drinking or smoking in the processing area. These rules are simple but they save lives. In Africa, poultry is a major source of foodborne illness. Most of it comes from poor hygiene during processing and sale. By following these steps, you can produce safe chicken that your customers trust. Trust builds repeat business. Repeat business builds profit. Finally, keep records. Record the number of birds processed, the number condemned, the weight of the carcasses, the chilling time and temperature, and the final yield. This data helps you identify problems and improve. If you see a high condemnation rate, check your pre-slaughter handling and your evisceration technique. If you see a short shelf life, check your chilling and your hygiene. Good records turn a good processor into a great one.

4. Carcass Quality, Grading and Defects

Carcass quality determines the price you get and the customers you can serve. A well-processed, attractive carcass sells faster and for more money than a bruised, torn or poorly bled one. This lesson covers the main quality parameters, common defects and how to grade and present your product for African markets. The first quality parameter is appearance. The skin should be clean, intact and evenly colored. In most African markets, customers prefer a pale yellow or white skin. The color comes from the feed, especially maize and pigments. If you want a yellow skin, feed maize or add natural pigments like marigold or paprika. If you want a white skin, feed wheat or sorghum and avoid pigments. The skin should not be torn, bruised or covered in feathers. Any remaining feathers or pin feathers reduce quality. Singeing and careful plucking prevent this. The second parameter is bleeding. A well-bled carcass has a pale pink to white color. A poorly bled carcass has a dark red or purple color, especially around the neck and wings. Poor bleeding is caused by a bad neck cut, insufficient bleeding time or stunning problems. Dark meat is less attractive and has a shorter shelf life. The third parameter is fleshing. A well-fleshed carcass has a rounded breast, plump thighs and a good layer of fat. A poorly fleshed carcass looks bony and thin. Fleshing is determined by genetics, nutrition and age at slaughter. Broilers should be slaughtered at 1.8 to 2.5 kilograms live weight, depending on the market. If you slaughter too early, the carcass is small and poorly fleshed. If you slaughter too late, the carcass may be too fat and the feed conversion ratio worsens. The fourth parameter is uniformity. Customers want consistent size and weight. If you sell a box of chicken with mixed sizes, the customer may complain. Sort carcasses by weight. Common weight classes are 0.8 to 1.0 kilogram, 1.0 to 1.2 kilogram, 1.2 to 1.5 kilogram and 1.5 kilogram plus. Label the boxes clearly. Uniformity builds trust. The fifth parameter is absence of defects. Common defects include bruises, broken bones, skin tears, missing parts, and contamination. Bruises are caused by rough handling during catching, transport or processing. They appear as red, purple or green patches on the skin or in the meat. Bruised parts must be trimmed and discarded. Broken bones are caused by rough handling or overcrowding in crates. They are usually found in the wings and legs. Skin tears are caused by poor plucking, over-scalding or careless evisceration. Missing parts include broken wings, torn skin or missing tail. Contamination includes feces, bile or dirt on the carcass. Bile is especially problematic because it is green and bitter. If the gall bladder breaks during evisceration, wash the area immediately with plenty of water. If the contamination is severe, condemn the carcass. Other defects include airsacculitis, which is caused by respiratory disease, and ascites, which is fluid in the abdomen. These are signs of disease or poor management. Condemn affected carcasses. Do not sell them. Grading is the process of sorting carcasses into quality categories. A simple grading system for African markets is Grade A, Grade B and Grade C. Grade A carcasses are well fleshed, well bled, free of defects, and uniform in size. They are sold to supermarkets, hotels and restaurants. Grade B carcasses have minor defects like small bruises or slight skin tears. They are sold to local markets, butcheries or for further processing. Grade C carcasses have major defects or are undersized. They are used for processing into sausages, nuggets or soups, or sold at a discount. Condemned carcasses are not sold. They are disposed of safely. Grading should be done after chilling, when the carcass is firm and easy to handle. Use a clean, well-lit table. Weigh each carcass and record the weight. Inspect the skin, the breast, the thighs and the abdominal cavity. Look for defects. Sort into the appropriate grade. Package the graded carcasses in clean, labeled bags or boxes. For fresh chicken, use a breathable bag or a tray with a film cover. For frozen chicken, use a freezer bag or a vacuum-sealed bag. Label with the grade, weight, date and your farm name. Traceability is important. If a customer complains, you can trace the problem back to the batch. In Africa, many customers buy live birds or freshly slaughtered chicken from open markets. If you sell in these markets, you need to differentiate your product. Use clean packaging, consistent size and good appearance. Tell your customers about your hygiene practices. Show them the cold chain. Offer a guarantee. These are competitive advantages. Finally, train your staff to recognize defects and grade correctly. A poorly trained grader may pass a defective carcass or downgrade a good one. Both cost you money. Use pictures or samples to train. Hold regular quality meetings. Review your defect rates and your grading accuracy. Set targets. For example, aim for 90 percent Grade A, 8 percent Grade B and 2 percent Grade C. If your Grade A percentage is low, find out why. Is it poor handling, poor bleeding, poor plucking or poor chilling? Fix the root cause. Good carcass quality is not an accident. It is the result of good management, good training and attention to detail. It is also a business strategy. High-quality carcasses command higher prices, attract better customers and reduce waste. In the competitive African poultry market, quality is your best marketing tool.

5. Value Addition, Packaging and Marketing

Slaughtering and processing is not the end of the value chain. It is the beginning of the opportunity. Value addition, smart packaging and effective marketing can turn a basic chicken business into a profitable brand. This lesson covers practical ways to add value, package and sell your broiler products in African markets. Start with the whole carcass. A whole chilled or frozen chicken is the most common product. It is simple to produce and easy to sell. But it is also a commodity. Many producers sell the same product, so the price is low and competition is high. To stand out, you need to add value. The simplest value addition is cutting. Cut the carcass into portions: breast, thighs, drumsticks, wings, and backs. Portions sell for more per kilogram than whole chickens. They also appeal to different customers. A restaurant may want breast fillets. A family may want a mixed pack. A street vendor may want wings and drumsticks. Cutting requires a clean table, a sharp knife, a scale and packaging. Train your staff to cut consistently. Use a standard cutting guide. Weigh each portion and pack it. Label the pack with the cut, weight, date and your brand. Another value addition is deboning. Deboned breast meat, also called fillet, sells for a premium. Deboning takes skill and time, but the returns are high. You can sell fillets to hotels, restaurants and supermarkets. You can also use the bones for soup or stock. Deboning reduces waste and increases profit. Further processing is another option. You can make sausages, nuggets, burgers, meatballs, or marinated chicken. These products have a longer shelf life, especially if frozen, and they appeal to urban customers who want convenience. They also allow you to use lower-value cuts and trimmings. To make processed products, you need equipment like a mincer, a mixer, a stuffer and a freezer. You also need to follow food safety rules. If you are not ready for full processing, start with simple marinated chicken. Marinate chicken pieces in spices, herbs, oil and vinegar. Package and freeze. Sell as ready-to-cook. This is popular in cities where people are busy. Packaging is critical. Poor packaging ruins a good product. Use food-grade materials. For fresh chicken, use clean plastic bags or trays with film. For frozen chicken, use freezer bags or vacuum-sealed bags. Vacuum sealing is best because it prevents freezer burn and extends shelf life. Label every package. Include the product name, weight, date of processing, best-before date, storage instructions and your contact details. A label builds trust. It also helps you trace problems. If you sell to supermarkets, they will require proper labeling and barcodes. Invest in a simple label printer or pre-printed labels. Branding is more than a label. It is your reputation. Choose a name that is easy to remember and reflects your values. Use a logo and consistent colors. Tell your story. Are you a family farm? Do you use no antibiotics? Do you deliver fresh every morning? These messages attract customers. In Africa, trust is everything. Customers buy from people they know and trust. Use social media to build trust. Post photos of your farm, your processing area and your products. Show your hygiene practices. Respond to questions. Use WhatsApp to take orders and communicate with customers. Many African consumers use WhatsApp as their main business tool. Use it well. Pricing is a key decision. Calculate your cost of production per kilogram. Include feed, chicks, labor, processing, packaging, transport and overheads. Add a profit margin. Check the market price. If your cost is higher than the market price, you need to reduce costs or add value. Do not compete on price alone. Compete on quality, consistency and service. Offer different products at different price points. A whole chicken for budget buyers. Portions for families. Fillets for restaurants. Marinated products for busy professionals. This way you capture more of the market. Distribution is the next challenge. How will you get your product to customers? If you sell at the farm gate, you need a visible location and a clean sales area. If you sell at markets, you need a stall, a freezer or ice, and a good display. If you sell to supermarkets, you need a reliable supply, consistent quality and proper invoicing. If you deliver, you need a cold box or a refrigerated vehicle. In many African cities, motorcycle delivery with an insulated box is a practical and low-cost option. Build relationships with your customers. Ask for feedback. What do they like? What do they want? Do they want smaller packs? Do they want gizzards and livers? Do they want free-range chicken? Use their feedback to improve. Keep records of sales. Which products sell best? Which customers buy the most? What is your profit per product? This data helps you focus on the most profitable activities. Finally, think about growth. Can you process more birds? Can you add a second product line? Can you supply a school, a hospital or a hotel? Can you train other farmers to supply you? Growth requires planning, investment and discipline. But it is possible. Many successful African poultry businesses started with a few birds and a small processing table. They grew because they focused on quality, hygiene, customer service and value addition. You can do the same. Remember, the goal is not just to slaughter chickens. The goal is to build a profitable, sustainable business that provides safe, affordable protein to your community. Value addition, packaging and marketing are the tools that turn a commodity into a brand. Use them well and your business will grow.

<h2>Practical Reference: Post-Slaughter Records, Yields and Compliance for African Broiler Enterprises</h2>

<p>Module 9 closes the loop between what happens on the killing floor and what happens in your bank account. A broiler enterprise that slaughters 500 birds a week without recording live weight, carcass weight, condemnation causes and cold-chain temperatures is flying blind: it cannot price accurately, cannot prove food safety to a supermarket buyer, cannot claim losses from a feed supplier, and cannot improve yield. This lesson gives you a working reference sheet the numbers, tables, temperature limits and record formats you can print, laminate and pin to the wall of the processing room. Everything here is designed for the realities of African small and medium broiler operations: batch sizes from 100 to 5,000 birds, mixed manual and semi-mechanical processing, unreliable grid power, and buyers ranging from open-market traders to formal processors and quick-service restaurants.</p>

<h3>1. Why Records Are a Business Tool, Not Paperwork</h3>

<p>Three commercial outcomes depend directly on post-slaughter data:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Pricing and margin control.</strong> If you sell by live weight but your buyer pays on dressed weight, the dressing percentage is the single most important number in your profit calculation. A 2-point swing in dressing percentage on 1,000 birds of 2.0 kg live weight is 40 kg of saleable meat real money at any African market price.</li>
<li><strong>Buyer compliance.</strong> Supermarket chains, hotels, schools and export-oriented processors increasingly demand documented cold-chain records, condemnation rates and water-use or hygiene evidence. A simple daily log satisfies most audit checklists.</li>
<li><strong>Loss diagnosis.</strong> Condemnation causes (bruising, faecal contamination, ascites, cellulitis, overscalding) point to specific failures in catching, feed formulation, ventilation or scalding temperature. Without cause codes you cannot fix the root problem.</li>
</ul>

<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0;font-weight:bold;color:#b71c1c;font-size:16px;"> SAFETY WARNING FOOD SAFETY AND CHEMICAL HAZARDS</p>
<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0;color:#7f0000;">Broiler processing involves live birds, blood, gut contents and cold water a high-risk combination for <strong>Salmonella, Campylobacter, E. coli and Listeria</strong>. Follow these rules without exception:</p>
<ul style="margin:0;color:#7f0000;">
<li><strong>Never</strong> allow carcasses to sit above 4 C for more than 2 hours. Bacteria double roughly every 20 minutes in the danger zone (560 C).</li>
<li><strong>Never</strong> use the same knife, table or crate for live-bird handling and eviscerated carcasses without cleaning and disinfection between steps.</li>
<li><strong>Never</strong> taste or sell a carcass that smells off, is slimy, or shows green/yellow discolouration condemn it and record the cause.</li>
<li><strong>Never</strong> mix scalding water, detergents and chlorine in unlabelled containers. Store all chemicals away from the processing room, in original labelled containers, out of reach of children and untrained staff.</li>
<li><strong>Always</strong> wear clean aprons, boots and gloves; wash hands with soap after handling live birds, viscera or waste; cover cuts and sores with waterproof dressings.</li>
<li><strong>Always</strong> use potable water for rinsing and ice. If you chlorinate, keep free chlorine at 0.20.5 mg/L and test it over-chlorination is a chemical hazard and taints meat.</li>
<li>Sick workers must not handle carcasses. Report diarrhoea, vomiting or open wounds to the supervisor immediately.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h3>5. Building Your Daily Processing Record Sheet</h3>

<p>A one-page form per batch is enough. Capture at minimum:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Batch ID, date, house/flock, age at slaughter, number of birds.</strong></li>
<li><strong>Live weight:</strong> weigh a sample of 2030 birds (or all birds if small batch) and record average and total.</li>
<li><strong>Post-slaughter weights:</strong> total dressed weight after chilling and draining; calculate dressing percentage = (dressed weight live weight) 100.</li>
<li><strong>Condemnation tally by cause:</strong> bruising, faecal contamination, ascites, cellulitis, overscald, broken bones, other.</li>
<li><strong>Temperature log:</strong> scald water, chiller air, carcass core at 0 h, 1 h, 2 h; dispatch temperature.</li>
<li><strong>Yield summary:</strong> kg saleable carcass, kg giblets, kg condemned, kg drip loss.</li>
<li><strong>Buyer and price:</strong> who bought, at what price per kg, payment status.</li>
<li><strong>Corrective actions:</strong> what you changed if a limit was breached (e.g. re-calibrated scalder, slowed line, replaced ice).</li>
</ol>

<p>Review the sheet weekly. Three numbers drive improvement: dressing percentage, total condemnation rate, and average carcass temperature at dispatch. If dressing percentage falls below 68% or condemnation rises above 3%, investigate catching, feed withdrawal, scalding and evisceration technique in that order those are the usual culprits in African small-scale operations.</p>

<h3>6. Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul style="list-style:none;padding-left:0;">
<li> <strong>Feed withdrawal:</strong> 812 hours before slaughter, water always available.</li>
<li> <strong>Scald water:</strong> 5862 C, checked with a calibrated thermometer every 30 minutes.</li>
<li> <strong>Chilling:</strong> carcass core 4 C within 2 hours; chiller air 04 C.</li>
<li> <strong>Weigh:</strong> sample live weight and total dressed weight; calculate dressing %.</li>
<li> <strong>Condemnation tally:</strong> every condemned carcass coded by cause.</li>
<li> <strong>Hygiene:</strong> potable water for rinse and ice; knives and tables disinfected between live-bird and carcass work.</li>
<li> <strong>Dispatch:</strong> record temperature at loading and at buyer receipt; keep cold chain unbroken.</li>
<li> <strong>Weekly review:</strong> dressing %, condemnation %, dispatch temperature act on any downward or upward trend.</li>
</ul>
