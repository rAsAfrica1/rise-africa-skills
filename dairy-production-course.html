Dairy Production

A complete 5-month program — breed and feeding decisions, milking hygiene that protects both cow and customer, mastitis and reproduction management, and turning raw milk into pasteurised milk, yoghurt and simple cheese for a market that buys every single day of the year.

Course Overview

Why dairy? Milk is one of the very few farm products with genuine daily demand — customers buy it every day, all year, unlike most crops that sell in a single harvest window. A dairy enterprise built on correct feeding, hygiene and reproduction management produces a steady daily income that smooths out the feast-and-famine cash flow most farms struggle with.

Structure: 5 monthly modules → lessons → practical assignments → a 10-question quiz each module → final business project.

How to study (hybrid): Online — modules on your phone, sessions in the Study Diary, questions to the rAs AI assistant. Offline — save this page (Print → Save as PDF); the practicals happen at the milking parlour, twice a day, every day.

⚠️ Read first: the three rules that protect your investment

  • Milk hygiene is not optional — it is the product. Dirty hands, dirty equipment or a sick cow can put pathogens straight into milk that a family will drink that same day. Every step from udder to container must be clean, every single time.
  • Mastitis caught early is a treatable infection; mastitis caught late is a ruined quarter or a dead cow. Check every cow, every milking, for the early warning signs — clots, heat, swelling, off-colour milk — before reaching for antibiotics.
  • A cow that isn't getting back in calf isn't producing milk next year. Reproduction management is not a side topic in dairy — a missed heat cycle or an undiagnosed reproductive problem directly costs future income, not just this season's.
MONTH 1 — Breeds, Housing & Feeding ▾

Match the breed to your feeding capacity, not just to yield potential on paper — a high-yield exotic cow underfed produces less, and suffers more, than a well-fed crossbred.

Lesson 1.2 — Housing and cow comfort

  • Provide shade, a dry resting area, and protection from rain and cold wind — a stressed, uncomfortable cow diverts energy away from milk production toward simply coping.
  • Non-slip flooring in the milking and walking areas prevents leg and hip injuries that can end a cow's productive life early.
  • Clean bedding regularly — cows that lie in wet, dirty bedding carry mastitis-causing bacteria straight to the udder at the next milking.

Lesson 1.3 — Feeding for milk production

  • A lactating dairy cow needs forage (grass, hay, silage) as the bulk of the diet, plus a concentrate ration to supply the extra energy and protein that milk production demands beyond what forage alone provides.
  • Feed concentrate in proportion to milk yield — roughly 0.3–0.4 kg concentrate per litre of milk produced above a maintenance baseline is a common smallholder rule of thumb, adjusted to your specific concentrate's energy/protein content.
  • Clean, unlimited water access is non-negotiable — a lactating cow can drink 60–100+ litres per day, and water shortage drops milk yield faster and more directly than almost any other single factor.

Lesson 1.4 — Pasture and forage management

  • Rotational grazing (moving cows between paddocks) rests grass and improves pasture yield and quality compared with set-stocking one area continuously.
  • Conserve surplus forage as hay (dry season) or silage (fermented, higher moisture) during periods of pasture abundance — this is what carries milk production through the dry season instead of it collapsing.
  • Legume forages (Lucerne/alfalfa, Desmodium, clover) boost protein intake and improve pasture nitrogen — worth establishing alongside grass pasture where land allows.

MONTH 2 — Milking Hygiene & Milk Quality ▾

Lesson 2.1 — The correct milking routine

  • Wash hands and udder with clean water before milking; dry with a clean, single-use cloth or paper towel — a shared dirty cloth between cows spreads infection.
  • Foremilk-strip the first few streams from each teat into a separate cup before milking begins — this is how early mastitis (clots, watery milk, discoloration) is caught before it contaminates the whole milking.
  • Milk quickly, completely and gently — incomplete milking leaves milk in the udder that increases infection risk and reduces the udder's signal to produce more milk next time.

Lesson 2.2 — Equipment hygiene

  • Wash all milk-contact equipment (buckets, containers, milking machine parts if used) with hot water and detergent after every single milking, and allow to air-dry completely — residual moisture supports bacterial growth.
  • Use food-grade containers only — metal containers that have rusted or plastic containers that have absorbed odours from other uses should be replaced, not "made do with."
  • Cool milk to below 4°C (using a cold water bath or refrigeration) as fast as possible after milking — bacteria multiply rapidly in warm milk, and this single step protects both quality and food safety more than almost any other.

Lesson 2.3 — Recognising and preventing mastitis

  • Mastitis is udder inflammation, usually from bacterial infection, and is the single most common and costly dairy disease worldwide — signs include swelling, heat, clots in milk, watery/discoloured milk, and a cow reluctant to let the affected quarter be touched.
  • The California Mastitis Test (a simple paddle test with a reagent solution) can detect subclinical mastitis (infection present but no visible signs) before it becomes visible clinical disease — worth learning if reagent is accessible.
  • Prevention beats treatment: consistent milking hygiene, dry, clean bedding, teat dipping with disinfectant after milking, and prompt treatment of any teat injury all reduce mastitis incidence dramatically.

Lesson 2.4 — Milk composition and quality testing

  • Milk quality is judged on fat %, protein %, somatic cell count (an indicator of udder health/mastitis), and bacterial count — buyers and processors increasingly pay a quality premium for milk that scores well on these measures.
  • A simple lactometer (measuring milk density) can detect water adulteration — a basic quality check worth doing on milk you buy from others as much as milk you sell.
  • Keep milk records per cow (yield, any mastitis events, treatment) — this data identifies your best and worst-performing animals for future breeding and culling decisions.

MONTH 3 — Health, Reproduction & Calf Rearing ▾

Lesson 3.1 — Vaccination and general herd health

  • Follow the vaccination schedule recommended by your local veterinary service for your region's common diseases (commonly foot-and-mouth disease, lumpy skin disease, anthrax, blackleg, and brucellosis where present) — schedules vary significantly by country and disease pressure.
  • Deworm on a regular schedule and monitor body condition — internal parasites reduce milk yield and body condition even without obvious visible illness.
  • Keep a herd health calendar (vaccinations, deworming, breeding dates) — this prevents both missed treatments and unnecessary duplicate ones.

Lesson 3.2 — The reproductive cycle and breeding

  • Cows cycle roughly every 21 days when not pregnant; heat signs include restlessness, mounting other cows or standing to be mounted, and mucus discharge — accurate heat detection is the foundation of getting cows back in calf on schedule.
  • Breeding options: natural service (a bull) or artificial insemination (AI) — AI gives access to superior genetics without keeping a bull, but requires accurate heat detection timing to succeed.
  • Gestation is approximately 9 months (283 days) — record every breeding date to predict calving and plan the dry period accurately.

Lesson 3.3 — The dry period and calving

  • Dry off (stop milking) a cow roughly 60 days before expected calving — this rest period lets the udder regenerate tissue and rebuild reserves for the next lactation; skipping it reduces the following lactation's yield.
  • Watch closely in the final weeks for signs of approaching calving (udder filling, relaxation of pelvic ligaments, restlessness) and ensure a clean, quiet calving area is ready.
  • Most calvings proceed naturally without assistance; know the signs of a difficult birth (prolonged straining without progress, abnormal calf position) that require prompt veterinary help.

Lesson 3.4 — Colostrum and calf rearing

  • Colostrum (the first milk after calving) is essential — it must be fed to the calf within the first 6 hours of life, as the calf's ability to absorb its protective antibodies drops sharply after that window closes.
  • Feed calves milk or a quality milk replacer through weaning (typically 8–12 weeks), introducing calf starter feed and clean water early to develop rumen function for an earlier, cheaper transition off milk.
  • Keep calf housing clean, dry and draft-free — scours (diarrhoea) from poor hygiene is the leading cause of calf mortality on smallholder dairies.

MONTH 4 — Processing: Pasteurised Milk, Yoghurt & Simple Cheese ▾

Lesson 4.1 — Why process milk

  • Raw milk has a short shelf life and a lower price ceiling than processed products — pasteurised milk, yoghurt and simple cheeses all extend shelf life and access higher-value markets than raw milk sale alone.
  • Processing also smooths supply gluts: milk produced faster than it can be sold fresh can be converted into a shelf-stable product rather than wasted.
  • Start simple (pasteurised milk, then yoghurt) before attempting cheese, which needs more equipment, skill and consistent hygiene control.

Lesson 4.2 — Pasteurisation

  • Batch (vat) pasteurisation heats milk to 63°C and holds for 30 minutes, then cools rapidly — this kills disease-causing bacteria while remaining achievable with simple pots and a thermometer at small scale.
  • Accurate temperature control matters: too low or too short fails to kill pathogens; too high or too long damages flavour and some nutrients unnecessarily.
  • Cool pasteurised milk rapidly to below 4°C and package in clean, sealed containers immediately — pasteurisation only protects milk that stays clean afterward.

Lesson 4.3 — Making yoghurt

  • Heat milk to about 85°C briefly (denatures proteins for a thicker set), cool to about 43–45°C, add a live yoghurt culture (a starter culture or a spoon of live natural yoghurt), and hold at that temperature for 4–8 hours until set.
  • Keep the incubation temperature steady — a flask, insulated box, or simple warm spot works at small scale; temperature swings during incubation cause inconsistent set and flavour.
  • Refrigerate promptly once set to slow further fermentation and extend shelf life; strain for a thicker Greek-style yoghurt if desired.

Lesson 4.4 — Simple fresh cheese

  • Fresh cheeses (paneer-style or simple soft cheese) are made by heating milk and adding an acid (lemon juice or vinegar) or rennet to curdle it, then straining the curds from the whey through cloth.
  • Press the curds lightly for a firmer texture if desired, and salt lightly both for flavour and as a mild preservative.
  • Fresh cheese has a shorter shelf life than aged cheese and should be refrigerated and sold/used within days — it is a good entry product before attempting aged cheese varieties that need more specialised equipment and much longer skill development.

MODULE 5 — Marketing & the Dairy Business ▾

Lesson 5.1 — Costing dairy production

  • Cost per litre = feed cost + labour + veterinary/health costs + a share of housing/equipment costs, divided by total litres produced — many smallholders undercount feed cost, especially the value of their own grown forage.
  • Compare cost per litre against your selling price across different channels (raw milk sale, pasteurised, yoghurt, cheese) — processing usually improves margin but adds cost and labour that must be counted honestly.
  • Track cost per litre by season — dry season feed costs typically rise sharply, and pricing that doesn't reflect this squeezes margin exactly when cash flow is tightest.

Lesson 5.2 — Marketing channels

  • Direct sale to neighbours/community (highest price, smallest volume, needs daily reliability), sale to a local dairy cooperative or processor (steady volume, lower price per litre but guaranteed off-take), and value-added product sale (yoghurt, cheese — highest margin, most work).
  • Milk cooperatives, where accessible, provide bulk collection, cooling infrastructure and a stable buyer relationship that an individual smallholder often cannot access alone — worth joining even at a lower per-litre price for the reliability and shared infrastructure.
  • Building a repeat customer base for value-added products depends on consistent quality and hygiene — one bad batch of yoghurt can cost a customer relationship permanently.

Lesson 5.3 — Building herd value over time

  • Keep breeding and yield records to identify your best-performing cows for future breeding, and cull consistently underperforming or chronically mastitis-prone animals rather than carrying them indefinitely.
  • Retained, well-raised heifer calves from good-yielding cows are how a herd grows in both size and genetic quality without buying in new animals every time.
  • A documented breeding and health record also raises the resale value of animals — buyers pay more for a cow with a known, verifiable production history.

Lesson 5.4 — Final project: your bankable business plan

  • Assemble: breed/herd plan, housing and feeding plan with seasonal forage strategy, milking hygiene and mastitis prevention protocol, breeding and calving calendar, your chosen product mix (raw/pasteurised/yoghurt/cheese) with per-litre costing, marketing channels and target customers, and a 3-season herd growth plan.
  • Submit with your milk and health records for your rAs certificate — dairy plans that document hygiene protocol and per-animal records are exactly what cooperatives, processors and lenders look for.

📖 Glossary

  • Mastitis — udder inflammation, usually bacterial, the most costly common dairy disease.
  • Foremilk stripping — expressing the first streams of milk into a separate cup to check for mastitis before milking.
  • Colostrum — the first milk after calving, essential for calf immunity if fed within 6 hours.
  • Dry period — the roughly 60-day rest before calving when a cow is not milked.
  • Somatic cell count — a milk quality measure indicating udder health/infection level.
  • Pasteurisation — heating milk to a controlled temperature and time to kill pathogens.
  • Lactometer — a tool measuring milk density, used to detect water adulteration.
  • Rennet — an enzyme used to curdle milk for cheesemaking.
