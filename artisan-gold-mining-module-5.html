1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Hard rock mining is the process of extracting gold from solid rock, rather than from loose soil or river gravel. In many parts of Africa, gold is locked inside quartz veins or sulphide-rich rock. This type of mining is more difficult and expensive than alluvial mining, but it can be very rewarding because the gold is often concentrated in specific veins. For example, a rich quartz vein can contain 5 to 30 grams of gold per tonne of rock, while alluvial gravel might only have 0.1 to 0.5 grams per cubic metre. Hard rock mining allows you to target these high-grade zones.

Why does it matter for African artisans? Many easy alluvial deposits are becoming exhausted. As surface gravels run out, miners must follow the gold into the bedrock. Hard rock mining also creates more stable, long-term jobs. A well-run hard rock operation can support a team of 10 to 20 people for years, rather than a few months. In countries like Tanzania, Ghana, South Africa, and Zimbabwe, small-scale hard rock mining is a major source of income for rural communities.

However, hard rock mining requires different skills. You need to break rock, support underground tunnels, ventilate the workings, and process the ore. It is dangerous if done carelessly. This module will teach you the basics: which tools to use, how to drill and blast, how to avoid common mistakes, and how to stay safe. You will also learn a practical exercise to test your understanding.

By the end of this module, you will be able to identify a gold-bearing vein, set up a small hard rock mine, extract ore efficiently, and process it to recover gold. Remember: hard rock mining is a team effort. Always work with at least one other person, and never take shortcuts with safety. The gold is not worth your life.

2. Tools and Materials

To mine hard rock, you need the right tools. The basic kit includes: a heavy-duty pickaxe (2-3 kg head), a sledgehammer (4-6 kg), steel wedges (at least 3, 20-30 cm long), a crowbar (1.5 m), and a shovel. For drilling, you need a hand drill (also called a jumper) a 1.5-2 m steel rod with a hardened, chisel-shaped bit. You will also need a striker (a smaller hammer to hit the drill) and a scraper (a spoon-shaped rod to clean the hole). If you can afford it, a petrol-powered rock drill (like a Cobra or Pionjar) will speed up work by 10 times, but many artisans start with hand tools.

For blasting, you need explosives. In most African countries, you must buy explosives legally from a licensed dealer. Common types are ammonium nitrate/fuel oil (ANFO) or dynamite. You will also need safety fuse, detonators (blasting caps), and a crimper. Never make homemade explosives they are illegal and extremely dangerous. If you cannot access legal explosives, you can use non-explosive methods like expansive grout (e.g., Dexpan) or heating rock with fire and then quenching with water to crack it.

For support, you need timber poles (10-15 cm diameter) or steel arches, and wooden planks for lagging. For ventilation, a hand-cranked fan or a flexible duct with a small blower helps. For lighting, use rechargeable headlamps (at least 100 lumens) never use open flames underground because of gas risk.

For processing ore, you need a hammer mill or stamp mill to crush rock, a sluice box or pan for concentration, and mercury or borax for amalgamation. Mercury is toxic use it only with gloves and in a ventilated area. Borax is safer for small-scale smelting. Also get a sieve (0.5 mm) and a gold pan. Always wear PPE: hard hat, steel-toe boots, safety glasses, gloves, and dust mask. In Africa, many supplies are available in mining towns like Geita (Tanzania), Obuasi (Ghana), or Kadoma (Zimbabwe).

3. Step by Step Method

Step 1: Find the vein. Look for quartz outcrops, especially those with iron staining (reddish-brown) or visible gold. Follow the vein by digging trenches along the surface. Once you find a promising spot, clear the area and mark a tunnel entrance about 1.5 m wide and 1.8 m high.

Step 2: Drill and blast. Using a hand drill, make holes 30-60 cm deep at a slight angle. For a typical round, drill 6 to 10 holes: one central cut hole, four corner holes, and two lifters (bottom holes). Clean each hole with a scraper. Insert a primer (detonator with a stick of dynamite) and fill the rest with ANFO. Tamp it gently with clay. Connect the safety fuse make it long enough to give you at least 2 minutes to escape. Light the fuse and retreat to a safe distance (at least 50 m). Wait 30 minutes before returning.

Step 3: Remove broken rock. After blasting, wait for smoke and dust to clear. Use a crowbar to check for loose rocks on the roof and walls scale them down. Then shovel the broken ore into wheelbarrows or buckets and transport it to the surface. Sort the ore: keep quartz and sulphide-rich pieces, discard waste rock.

Step 4: Support the tunnel. After each blast, install timber supports every 1-1.5 m. Use 10-15 cm diameter poles as posts and caps, and planks as lagging. If the ground is very loose, use steel arches or rock bolts. Never leave unsupported ground more than 2 m from the face.

Step 5: Ventilate and drain. Use a hand fan or flexible duct to push fresh air to the face. If water collects, dig a drainage ditch along one side and pump it out with a hand pump or bucket.

Step 6: Process the ore. Crush the ore to sand size (less than 2 mm) using a hammer mill or stamp mill. Then concentrate the gold using a sluice box or pan. For fine gold, use mercury amalgamation or borax smelting. Finally, melt the gold into a button or bar. Record your recovery a good day's work might yield 1-5 grams from 100 kg of ore.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Not supporting the roof. Many accidents happen because miners dig too far without timber. Fix: Install supports every 1-1.5 m. If you see cracks or hear popping sounds, get out and support immediately.

Mistake 2: Using too much explosive. Overcharging shatters the rock into dust and damages the tunnel. Fix: Use the right amount typically 0.5-1 kg of ANFO per cubic metre of rock. Start with less and adjust.

Mistake 3: Poor ventilation. Underground air can become low in oxygen or filled with blasting fumes. Fix: Always ventilate for at least 30 minutes after blasting. Use a fan or duct. Never enter if you feel dizzy.

Mistake 4: Ignoring water. Water can flood your tunnel and weaken walls. Fix: Dig a drainage ditch and keep a hand pump. If water is heavy, drill a drainage hole ahead of the face.

Mistake 5: Losing gold in processing. Many miners crush ore too coarse, so gold remains locked in rock. Fix: Crush to less than 2 mm. Also, use a correct sluice angle (about 10-15 degrees) and proper water flow. Check tailings for gold if you see any, re-run them.

Mistake 6: Using mercury without safety. Mercury is highly toxic. Fix: Use gloves, work outdoors, and never heat mercury indoors. Better: use borax smelting instead.

Mistake 7: Working alone. Hard rock mining is dangerous. Fix: Always work with at least one partner. Have someone outside who knows your plan and can call for help.

Mistake 8: Not testing the ore. Some miners dig for months without checking grade. Fix: Take a 5 kg sample every 2 m of tunnel and pan it. If you get less than 0.5 g per tonne, move to a new spot. This saves time and money.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety in hard rock mining is not optional. Follow these rules to stay alive. First, never enter an unsupported tunnel. Before starting work, check the roof and walls for loose rock. Use a crowbar to scale down any loose pieces. If you hear cracking or popping, evacuate immediately.

Second, always wear personal protective equipment (PPE): hard hat, steel-toe boots, safety glasses, gloves, dust mask, and headlamp. In dusty conditions, wear a respirator. Hearing protection is needed when using rock drills.

Third, handle explosives legally and carefully. Store explosives in a locked, dry box away from living areas. Never smoke or use open flames near explosives. Use only safety fuse and detonators never improvise. After blasting, wait at least 30 minutes for fumes to clear before returning.

Fourth, ventilate. Underground air can contain carbon monoxide, nitrogen oxides, and silica dust. Use a hand-cranked fan or flexible duct to push fresh air to the face. If you feel dizzy, headache, or short of breath, get to the surface immediately.

Fifth, manage water. Flooding can trap you. Always have a drainage ditch and a hand pump. If water is rising fast, abandon the tunnel.

Sixth, work in teams. Never work alone underground. Have a buddy system, and tell someone on the surface where you are going and when you will return. Use a rope or communication system.

Seventh, practice good hygiene. Wash hands before eating. Mercury and lead dust can be absorbed. Do not eat or smoke underground.

Eighth, have an emergency plan. Know the nearest clinic or hospital. Keep a first aid kit with bandages, antiseptic, and painkillers. For serious injuries, have a stretcher and a way to call for help.

Best practice also includes regular training. Attend safety workshops offered by organizations like the Artisanal Gold Council or local mining departments. Keep your tunnel clean and organized. Store tools safely. And remember: no gold is worth your life. If a tunnel feels unsafe, leave it.

6. Practical Exercise

This exercise will help you practice the key skills of hard rock mining. You will need: a hand drill, sledgehammer, wedges, a pickaxe, a shovel, a bucket, a gold pan, and a safe outdoor area with a rock outcrop (or a concrete block to simulate rock). Work with a partner.

Exercise 1: Drill a hole. Mark a spot on the rock. Hold the drill at a 70-degree angle. Strike the drill with the sledgehammer, rotating it a quarter turn after each hit. Drill to 30 cm depth. This should take about 15-20 minutes in medium-hard rock. Measure the hole and check that it is straight.

Exercise 2: Break rock with wedges. If you cannot use explosives, drill three holes in a line, 15 cm apart. Insert steel wedges into each hole. Hit them evenly with the sledgehammer until the rock cracks. This simulates a blast. Collect the broken pieces.

Exercise 3: Support a tunnel. Using timber poles, build a support frame 1.5 m wide and 1.8 m high. Use two posts, one cap, and lagging planks. Test its strength by pushing on it it should not wobble.

Exercise 4: Process ore. Crush a 5 kg sample of the broken rock to sand size using a hammer or stamp mill. Pan the crushed material in a bucket of water. Swirl gently, letting lighter material wash away. Look for gold it will be at the bottom of the pan. Weigh any gold you find (use a jeweler's scale). Calculate grade in grams per tonne.

Exercise 5: Safety check. Before starting, inspect your area for loose rocks. Put on all PPE. Practice scaling down a loose rock with a crowbar. Then, simulate an emergency: shout 'fire' and evacuate to a safe distance within 30 seconds.

After the exercise, write a short report: what you did, how long it took, how much gold you recovered (if any), and what you would do differently. This will build your confidence and skill. Remember, practice makes perfect. Repeat this exercise until you can drill, break, support, and process efficiently.

<h3>Grade, Recovery and Revenue What the Numbers Mean</h3>

<p>Understanding the relationship between grade, recovery and revenue is the single most important business skill in hard rock mining. A miner who moves 20 tonnes of 3 g/t ore at 70% recovery recovers about 42 grams of gold. At a gold price of USD 1,900/oz (roughly USD 61/g), that is about USD 2,560 in gross revenue before labour, energy, reagents, and transport. If the same miner processes 20 tonnes of 8 g/t ore at 85% recovery, the revenue jumps to about USD 8,300. Grade and recovery are not abstract they are the difference between a marginal operation and a profitable one.</p>

<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#7a0000;">Never enter an unsupported tunnel or shaft deeper than 5 metres without timber or steel sets. Hard rock does not mean stable rock. In many African hard rock belts, sheared and weathered zones collapse without warning. Additionally, underground workings can accumulate methane, carbon monoxide from blasting, and low-oxygen air. Always ventilate before entry, never use open flame for lighting, and never work alone underground. Illegal or unregulated pits are the leading cause of mining fatalities in African ASM official estimates attribute hundreds of deaths annually to tunnel collapse in countries such as Zimbabwe, Tanzania, and Ghana.</p>

<h3>Step-by-Step Practical Method</h3>

<p>The following method is the field-standard sequence for a small hard rock operation. Each step assumes you have legal mineral rights or a registered claim.</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Geological assessment.</strong> Map the reef strike and dip. Collect grab samples every 23 metres along the reef and submit for assay. Do not mine blind.</li>
<li><strong>Site preparation.</strong> Clear the portal area, establish drainage, and set up a secure ore pad. Locate your tailings pond downslope and away from watercourses.</li>
<li><strong>Drilling and blasting.</strong> Drill holes at 0.81.2 m spacing, charge with permitted explosives, and blast at the end of shift. Never return to the face until fumes have cleared.</li>
<li><strong>Mucking and haulage.</strong> Load broken ore into wheelbarrows or cocopans and haul to surface. Sort waste rock from ore at the face to reduce transport costs.</li>
<li><strong>Crushing and milling.</strong> Reduce ore to &lt;10 mm with a jaw crusher, then mill to 75150 microns in a ball mill or stamp mill. Liberation size determines recovery.</li>
<li><strong>Gravity concentration.</strong> Run milled pulp over a sluice, shaking table, or centrifuge to capture free gold. This is the cheapest recovery step.</li>
<li><strong>Chemical recovery (CIL/CIP).</strong> For fine gold locked in sulphides, use a licensed carbon-in-leach or carbon-in-pulp circuit. This requires trained operators and environmental controls.</li>
<li><strong>Smelting and sale.</strong> Smelt dor bars or sell concentrate to a licensed buyer. Record every gram traceability is now required by most formal buyers.</li>
</ol>

<h3>Common Mistakes and Fixes</h3>

<p><strong>Mistake 1: Mining without assay data.</strong> Fix: assay a minimum of 10 samples before committing to a face. A USD 20 assay can save USD 2,000 in wasted labour.</p>
<p><strong>Mistake 2: Over-crushing or under-crushing.</strong> Fix: sieve your milled product weekly. If more than 20% is coarser than 150 microns, your recovery will suffer.</p>
<p><strong>Mistake 3: Poor ventilation.</strong> Fix: install a forced-air fan and flexible ducting. Test air quality before every shift.</p>
<p><strong>Mistake 4: No tailings management.</strong> Fix: build a lined or clay-sealed tailings pond. Untreated tailings contaminate groundwater and trigger regulatory shutdowns.</p>
<p><strong>Mistake 5: Selling unprocessed ore.</strong> Fix: process on-site where possible. Selling raw ore transfers most of the value to the buyer.</p>
<p><strong>Mistake 6: Ignoring mercury and cyanide rules.</strong> Fix: use licensed reagents only, and never burn amalgam in the open. Mercury vapour is a permanent health hazard.</p>

<h3>Safety and Best Practice</h3>

<p>Safety is not a cost it is a licence to operate. Formal buyers, banks, and regulators increasingly require documented safety and environmental compliance. The following practices are non-negotiable:</p>

<ul>
<li>Support every tunnel with timber, steel, or rock bolts. Inspect supports daily.</li>
<li>Ventilate all underground workings. Test for oxygen, CO, and methane before entry.</li>
<li>Use personal protective equipment: helmet, boots, gloves, eye protection, and dust mask.</li>
<li>Store explosives in a locked, licensed magazine. Never store detonators with explosives.</li>
<li>Manage water: pump shafts dry, and never discharge untreated process water into rivers.</li>
<li>Train every worker on emergency evacuation and first aid. Keep a stretcher and first-aid kit on site.</li>
<li>Keep records: daily production, assay results, safety incidents, and reagent use.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Practical Exercise</h3>

<p><strong>Exercise: Grade-to-Revenue Calculation.</strong> You have blasted 15 tonnes of ore from a 0.8 m wide quartz reef. Assay returns 5.2 g/t Au. Your ball mill recovers 78% of gold. Gold price is USD 1,900/oz.</p>
<ol>
<li>Calculate total grams of gold in the ore.</li>
<li>Calculate recovered grams after 78% recovery.</li>
<li>Convert recovered grams to troy ounces (1 oz = 31.1035 g).</li>
<li>Calculate gross revenue in USD.</li>
<li>If your all-in cost is USD 55/tonne, calculate your profit or loss.</li>
</ol>
<p><em>Answer guide:</em> 15 t 5.2 g/t = 78 g Au. 78 0.78 = 60.84 g recovered. 60.84 31.1035 = 1.956 oz. 1.956 1,900 = USD 3,716. Cost = 15 55 = USD 825. Profit = USD 2,891.</p>
