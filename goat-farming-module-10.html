1. Selecting Forage Species for Goats

When choosing forage for goats, think about what grows well in your area, what goats like to eat, and what gives the most feed per hectare. Goats are browsers, not grazers like cattle. They prefer leaves, twigs, and shrubs over plain grass. But they also eat high-quality grasses and legumes. In Africa, common forage species for goats include Napier grass, Rhodes grass, Guinea grass, and legumes like Lucerne, Desmodium, and Lablab. These do well in many regions. For dry areas, consider drought-tolerant species like Buffel grass, Siratro, and Leucaena. Leucaena is a legume tree that goats love, and it provides protein-rich leaves. But be careful: too much Leucaena can cause toxicity because of mimosine. Limit it to 30% of the diet.

Before planting, test your soil. Goats need forage with at least 12-16% protein for good growth and milk production. Legumes like Lucerne and Desmodium have 18-22% protein. Grasses like Napier have 8-12%. Mix grasses and legumes to balance nutrition. Also, consider your climate. In high-rainfall areas (over 800 mm per year), you can grow Napier, Rhodes, and legumes. In drier areas (400-800 mm), use Buffel, Guinea, and drought-resistant legumes like Stylosanthes. For very dry areas (under 400 mm), plant fodder trees like Leucaena, Gliricidia, and Acacia. These deep-rooted trees survive dry spells.

Goats also need variety. A pasture with only one species can lead to nutrient gaps and health problems. Aim for at least three species: one grass, one legume, and one browse plant. For example, plant Napier grass, Desmodium, and Leucaena. This gives energy, protein, and minerals. Also, consider seasonality. Some species grow in the wet season, others in the dry. For dry-season feed, plant fodder trees and conserve hay or silage.

Finally, think about establishment costs and management. Improved species like Napier and Lucerne give higher yields but need more fertiliser, weeding, and water. Local species like Rhodes grass are hardier but lower yielding. As a beginner, start with a mix of improved and local species. Buy certified seeds from reputable agro-dealers to avoid weeds and low germination. Plant at the right time: at the start of the rains. With good species selection, you can feed your goats well all year and reduce feed costs.

2. Pasture Establishment and Renovation

Starting a new pasture or improving an old one takes planning and work. First, clear the land. Remove bushes, stumps, and old grass. You can use a hoe, machete, or tractor. For small plots, hand clearing is fine. For larger areas, hire a tractor to plough and harrow. Ploughing breaks the soil, and harrowing levels it. Aim for a fine seedbed. If the soil is acidic (pH below 5.5), apply agricultural lime at 2-4 tonnes per hectare. Do this 2-3 months before planting. Also, add manure or compost: 5-10 tonnes per hectare. This improves soil fertility and water holding.

Planting depends on the species. For grasses like Napier, plant stem cuttings or splits. Dig holes 30 cm apart in rows 60-90 cm apart. Place one cutting per hole, cover with soil, and press firmly. For Rhodes grass, broadcast seeds at 10-15 kg per hectare, then rake lightly. For legumes like Desmodium, sow seeds at 2-5 kg per hectare. Mix seeds with sand or sawdust for even spreading. Plant at the start of the rains when soil is moist. If you plant in dry soil, seeds may not germinate. After planting, water if possible, but rain is best.

Weed control is critical in the first 3 months. Weeds compete for light, water, and nutrients. Weed by hand or use a hoe. Do not let weeds go to seed. If you use herbicides, choose selective ones that kill broadleaf weeds but not your grass. For example, 2,4-D kills broadleaf weeds in grass pastures. Always read the label and follow safety instructions. After the pasture establishes, usually 3-6 months, you can start light grazing. Do not overgraze young plants; let them grow strong roots.

Renovating an old pasture means improving it without starting over. First, test the soil. If fertility is low, add manure or fertiliser. If the pasture is compacted, aerate it with a fork or a tractor-drawn aerator. If there are bare patches, oversow with grass and legume seeds. If weeds are a problem, control them first. For brush, cut and stump-treat with herbicide. After renovation, rest the pasture for 4-8 weeks before grazing. This allows new growth to establish. With good establishment, your pasture can last 5-10 years, giving you reliable feed for your goats.

3. Grazing Management and Rotation

Grazing management is about giving goats the right amount of forage at the right time without damaging the pasture. Goats are browsers, so they will eat leaves, twigs, and weeds. But they also graze grasses. If you let them roam freely, they will overgraze the best plants, leaving weeds and bare soil. Overgrazing weakens plants, reduces yield, and causes soil erosion. To avoid this, use rotational grazing. Divide your pasture into paddocks or camps. A simple system is 4-8 paddocks. Move goats from one paddock to the next every 3-7 days. This gives each paddock 3-6 weeks of rest. Rest allows plants to regrow and store energy in roots.

How many goats per hectare? It depends on rainfall and pasture quality. In high-rainfall areas (over 800 mm), you can stock 5-10 goats per hectare. In drier areas (400-800 mm), stock 2-5 goats per hectare. In very dry areas (under 400 mm), 1-2 goats per hectare. These are rough guides. Monitor your pasture: if grass is grazed to below 5 cm, you have too many goats. If there is plenty of tall grass, you can add more. Also, consider supplementary feeding. In the dry season, when pasture is scarce, give hay, silage, or crop residues. This reduces pressure on pasture.

Rotational grazing has many benefits. It breaks the life cycle of internal parasites. Goats pick up worms from larvae on grass. If they graze a paddock and then move, the larvae die before the goats return. This reduces deworming costs. It also improves soil health. When goats trample old grass and manure, it adds organic matter. But avoid grazing when soil is waterlogged; hooves damage wet soil. Also, provide shade and water in each paddock. Goats need shade during hot days. Use trees or simple shelters.

For a beginner, start with a simple rotation. Make a plan: draw your paddocks, decide grazing days, and stick to it. Keep records: note when you move goats, how long they graze, and pasture condition. Adjust as needed. If you see signs of overgrazing (bare patches, weeds, erosion), reduce stocking or increase rest periods. With good grazing management, your pasture will stay productive for years, and your goats will be healthier.

4. Weed and Brush Control with Goats

Goats are excellent weed and brush controllers. They eat many plants that other livestock avoid, including thorny bushes, broadleaf weeds, and invasive shrubs. This makes them useful for clearing land and maintaining pastures. But you must manage them carefully. Goats will eat almost anything, including toxic plants. So, first identify the weeds and brush in your area. Common problem plants in Africa include Lantana camara, blackjack, pigweed, thorn apple, and Acacia species. Some are toxic: Lantana causes liver damage, and thorn apple (Datura) is poisonous. Do not let goats eat these. If you have toxic plants, remove them by hand or use herbicide before grazing.

For controlled grazing, use a paddock system. Put goats in a paddock with dense weeds or brush. They will browse the leaves and twigs. Stocking rate for weed control is higher than for normal grazing: 20-30 goats per hectare for 1-2 weeks. This heavy stocking forces them to eat weeds. But do not leave them too long, or they will damage desirable plants. After they clear the paddock, move them. Then, improve the pasture by planting desirable species. This prevents weeds from returning.

Goats prefer browsing to grazing. They will eat leaves from bushes and trees up to 1.5 metres high. For taller brush, you may need to cut it first or use a ladder? No, goats can stand on hind legs to reach higher. But for very tall trees, cut lower branches to make them accessible. Also, goats can control weeds in crops. For example, in a maize field, goats can graze weeds between rows without eating maize, if trained. But be careful with pesticides; do not graze goats on sprayed fields for at least 2 weeks.

Using goats for weed control reduces the need for herbicides, which saves money and protects the environment. It also provides free feed. However, it requires planning. Rotate goats to prevent overgrazing and parasite build-up. Provide mineral supplements, especially salt, because goats on brush may lack minerals. Also, monitor goats for signs of poisoning: drooling, staggering, diarrhoea. If you see these, remove goats from the area and call a vet. With proper management, goats can turn weeds into meat and milk.

5. Soil Fertility and Testing

Soil fertility is the ability of soil to provide nutrients for plant growth. For goats, fertile soil means more and better forage. The key nutrients are nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K), plus micronutrients. In Africa, many soils are deficient in N and P. You can improve fertility with manure, compost, and fertilisers. But first, test your soil. Soil testing tells you what nutrients are missing and what pH you have. It saves money by preventing unnecessary fertiliser. In many African countries, soil testing services are available through agricultural extension offices or private labs. A basic test costs $10-30. Take samples correctly: use a clean hoe or auger to collect soil from 15-20 cm depth. Take 10-15 samples from different spots in your pasture. Mix them in a clean bucket, then take about 500 g for testing.

Soil pH is important. Goats need forage with good nutrient uptake. Most forage species grow best at pH 6.0-7.0. If your soil is acidic (pH below 5.5), apply lime. Lime raises pH and adds calcium. Apply 2-4 tonnes per hectare, depending on acidity. If soil is alkaline (pH above 7.5), add organic matter like manure or compost. Also, consider the type of soil: sandy soils drain fast and lose nutrients, so add compost. Clay soils hold nutrients but can be waterlogged; add organic matter to improve structure.

After testing, apply fertiliser based on results. For nitrogen, use urea or ammonium sulphate. For phosphorus, use triple superphosphate (TSP) or rock phosphate. For potassium, use muriate of potash. But organic sources are better for long-term fertility. Manure from your goats is excellent. A goat produces about 1 tonne of manure per year. Compost it with crop residues and spread 5-10 tonnes per hectare. This adds nutrients and organic matter. Also, plant legumes like Desmodium and Lucerne. They fix nitrogen from the air, adding 50-200 kg N per hectare per year.

Regular soil testing every 2-3 years helps you track fertility. Keep records of yields and fertiliser applications. If you cannot afford a lab test, use a simple soil test kit. These kits give rough estimates of pH and nutrients. Also, observe your pasture: yellow leaves mean nitrogen deficiency; purple leaves mean phosphorus deficiency. With good soil fertility, your pasture will produce more forage, and your goats will be healthier.

6. Hay and Silage Production

Hay and silage are conserved forage for dry-season feeding. Hay is dried grass or legumes, while silage is fermented, wet forage. Both allow you to store surplus feed when pasture is abundant and use it when feed is scarce. Making hay is simple but needs sunny weather. Cut grass or legumes when they are at the right stage: for grasses, before they flower (early heading); for legumes, at early flowering. This gives the best balance of yield and nutrition. Cut with a machete or sickle, or use a mower for large areas. Let the cut forage dry in the sun for 1-3 days. Turn it daily with a rake or fork to speed drying. Aim for 15-20% moisture. If you bend a stem and it snaps, it is dry enough. If it is still flexible, dry more. Then, store in a dry, well-ventilated shed. Stack bales or loose hay on pallets to keep off the ground. Cover with tarpaulin to protect from rain. Good hay keeps for 6-12 months.

Silage is made from wet forage, usually grasses like maize, Napier, or sorghum. Cut when the plant has high sugar and moisture (about 65-75% moisture). Chop into 2-5 cm pieces. Pack tightly into a silo, pit, or plastic bags. The key is to exclude air. Air causes mould and spoilage. Tread the silage to remove air, then seal with plastic and soil. Fermentation happens in 2-3 weeks. Good silage smells like vinegar, not rotten. It can be stored for 6-12 months. For small-scale farmers, use plastic bags or drums. Fill, compress, and tie tightly. Store in a cool, dark place.

Hay and silage are especially important in dry seasons when pasture is dry and low in protein. Feed hay at 2-3% of goat body weight per day. For a 30 kg goat, that is 0.6-0.9 kg of hay. Supplement with concentrates if needed. Silage is more digestible and palatable. Introduce it gradually to avoid digestive upset. Also, ensure clean water is available. Do not feed mouldy hay or silage; it can cause disease.

To make hay and silage, you need equipment: machete, rake, fork, plastic sheets, and storage. For silage, a chopper helps. Start small: make hay from a small plot and test. With practice, you can conserve enough feed to keep your goats productive all year. This reduces costs and improves profits.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Pasture and Forage Management for Goat Enterprises

This practical reference consolidates the operational knowledge a goat farmer needs to plan, establish, manage, and conserve forage in African production systems. It is written for commercial and semi-commercial producers who measure success in kilograms of meat or litres of milk per hectare, not in hectares alone. Every recommendation below is framed around cost of production, animal performance, and return on land and labour.

Goats are browsers by nature. They select leaves, twigs, pods, and herbaceous plants before they graze grass. That single behavioural fact drives every decision in this module: species selection, establishment method, grazing layout, weed control strategy, soil management, and conservation. A pasture programme that ignores the browser instinct will underperform a well-designed browse-grass system by a wide margin.

1. Selecting Forage Species for Goats

Species selection is the highest-leverage decision in the module because it determines yield, persistence, drought tolerance, and feed quality for the next five to ten years. In African conditions, the most reliable forage base for goats combines a grass component for bulk energy and a browse or legume component for protein. Pure grass pastures rarely sustain high milk yield or fast growth without expensive concentrate supplementation.

Recommended grass species include Rhodes grass (Chloris gayana), Napier grass (Pennisetum purpureum), Guinea grass (Megathyrsus maximus), buffel grass (Cenchrus ciliaris) for drier areas, and kikuyu (Cenchrus clandestinus) in cooler highland zones. Legumes and browse species that perform well across the continent include leucaena (Leucaena leucocephala), desmodium (Desmodium intortum), lablab (Lablab purpureus), sweet potato vines, mulberry (Morus alba), and calliandra (Calliandra calothyrsus).

Business rule of thumb: budget at least 60% of the forage area to a persistent grass base and up to 40% to legumes and browse. This ratio balances energy supply with protein and reduces reliance on purchased feed. On a 5-hectare goat unit targeting 30 does, this typically translates to 3 ha of improved grass and 2 ha of mixed legume-browse.

2. Pasture Establishment and Renovation

Establishment failure is the most common cause of wasted forage investment in Africa. The main causes are poor seedbed preparation, low soil pH, wrong planting time, and uncontrolled grazing during the establishment phase. Land preparation should begin at least four to six weeks before the first rains. Plough, harrow, and remove perennial weeds. Apply lime and phosphate based on a soil test, not guesswork.

Plant at the onset of reliable rains. Grass seed rates vary: Napier is planted from stems or splits at 12 t/ha of planting material; Rhodes grass seed at 815 kg/ha; Guinea grass at 510 kg/ha. Legumes are usually planted in rows or broadcast at 25 kg/ha. Cover seed lightly, roll the seedbed, and control weeds in the first eight weeks. Do not graze new pastures until the grass has rooted firmly and reached 2530 cm usually 34 months after planting in warm, moist conditions.

Renovation is cheaper than replanting when the pasture is degraded but not dead. Options include overseeding with legumes, applying fertiliser, controlling weeds, and resting paddocks. A practical renovation sequence: soil test, correct pH and phosphorus, reduce grazing pressure for one season, overseed with a legume such as desmodium or lablab, then resume rotational grazing.

3. Grazing Management and Rotation

Continuous grazing destroys goat pastures faster than any other management error. Goats graze selectively, and continuous stocking allows them to repeatedly graze preferred species until those species disappear. Rotational grazing is the core tool for persistence, parasite control, and yield.

A practical rotational system for African goat farms uses 612 paddocks with a rest period of 3045 days in the growing season and longer in the dry season. Stocking rates depend on rainfall and soil, but a working range for improved pasture is 515 goats per hectare in medium-rainfall zones and 25 goats per hectare in dry zones. Adjust by observation: if grass is grazed below 810 cm, reduce stock or extend rest.

4. Weed and Brush Control with Goats

Goats are effective biological control agents for many weeds and woody invaders. They browse thorny brush, reduce shrub density, and convert low-value biomass into meat and milk. This is a genuine business advantage: a goat enterprise can be paid to clear land while producing income.

Target species include Lantana camara, Acacia regrowth, Prosopis seedlings, Chromolaena odorata, and various thorny shrubs. Goats will not control all weeds, and some are toxic. Lantana is toxic when consumed in large quantities, and many Senecio and Crotalaria species cause liver damage. Introduce goats to unfamiliar weeds gradually and monitor intake.

SAFETY WARNING: Never introduce goats to unfamiliar weeds or browse without a toxicity check. Lantana camara, Senecio species, Crotalaria species, and some Acacia pods can cause liver damage, photosensitisation, or sudden death. Introduce new forage gradually over 710 days, provide clean water and mineral licks, and remove animals immediately if you see drooling, staggering, jaundice, or sudden collapse. Consult a veterinarian or extension officer before using goats for large-scale weed control on land with unknown plant species.

5. Soil Fertility and Testing

Soil fertility is the foundation of pasture yield, and it is the most neglected input on African goat farms. A soil test costs a fraction of the lost production from a deficient pasture. Test at least every two to three years, and always before establishing a new pasture.

Key parameters: pH (target 5.56.5 for most tropical grasses and legumes), phosphorus (critical for root and legume development), potassium, organic matter, and cation exchange capacity. In much of sub-Saharan Africa, phosphorus and nitrogen are the primary limiting nutrients. Legumes fix nitrogen biologically, but they need adequate phosphorus and pH to establish.

Manure from the goat enterprise is a valuable input. A 30-goat unit can produce several tonnes of manure per year, which should be composted and returned to pasture. This closes the nutrient loop and reduces fertiliser cost. Rotational grazing also improves nutrient distribution compared with continuous grazing.

6. Hay and Silage Production

Dry-season feed is the biggest constraint on goat productivity in most of Africa. Hay and silage bridge the gap and stabilise milk and meat output through the year. Hay is easier and cheaper for smallholders; silage is better for large operations and for wet-season surplus.

For hay, cut grass at the optimum stage before it flowers for maximum protein, or at early flowering for maximum yield. Dry in the sun to 1520% moisture, turn regularly, and bale or store under cover. Poorly dried hay moulds and causes respiratory disease and abortion in pregnant does.

For silage, chop forage to 23 cm, wilt to 3035% dry matter, compact thoroughly, and seal airtight. Napier, maize, sorghum, and sweet potato vines all make good silage. Add molasses or a lactic acid bacteria inoculant where sugar content is low. Feed silage within six months for best quality.

Business perspective: each tonne of well-made hay or silage replaces purchased concentrate worth roughly USD 150300 in many African markets. A 5-hectare farm with 2 hectares reserved for conservation can produce 1020 tonnes of hay per year, enough to carry breeding stock through a 34 month dry season.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Test soil before planting; correct pH and phosphorus first.
  • Choose a grasslegumebrowse mix matched to rainfall and enterprise goal.
  • Prepare a fine, weed-free seedbed and plant at the onset of rains.
  • Do not graze new pasture until it is firmly rooted and 2530 cm tall.
  • Use 612 paddocks with 3045 day rest in the growing season.
  • Keep residual grass height above 810 cm to protect regrowth.
  • Introduce goats to new weeds gradually; check for toxicity first.
  • Make hay or silage from surplus forage for dry-season feeding.
