1. Mixing cassava flour with wheat flour for bread

Cassava flour alone makes a dense, crumbly loaf because it has no gluten. To get a bread that rises and holds together, you must blend it with wheat flour. The most common ratio in African bakeries is 70% wheat flour to 30% cassava flour. For a standard loaf, use 350g wheat flour and 150g cassava flour. If you want a more cassava-forward taste, you can push to 50/50, but expect a heavier loaf. Always weigh your flours with a kitchen scaleâcup measures vary too much.

Before mixing, sift both flours together twice with 1 teaspoon of salt and 1 tablespoon of sugar. Sifting removes lumps and distributes the cassava evenly. In a large bowl, whisk 1 sachet (7g) of instant dry yeast into the flour mix. Make a well in the centre. Pour in 250ml of warm water (about 38â40Â°C) and 2 tablespoons of vegetable oil. Mix with a wooden spoon until a shaggy dough forms.

Turn the dough onto a lightly floured surface (use wheat flour for dusting, not cassavaâcassava burns easily). Knead for 10â12 minutes until smooth and elastic. Cassava flour absorbs more water than wheat, so if the dough feels stiff, add water 1 tablespoon at a time. If it sticks, add wheat flour 1 tablespoon at a time. The dough should be soft but not sticky.

Let the dough rest for 10 minutes before shaping. This rest allows the cassava particles to hydrate fully. In hot African climates, work quickly to avoid over-fermentation. If you are in a humid area, reduce water by 20ml. Always use fresh cassava flourâold flour smells sour and ruins the bread. Store cassava flour in an airtight container away from moisture.

2. Getting the right water temperature for yeast activation

Yeast is a living organism. If the water is too cold, it wakes up slowly; if too hot, it dies. The ideal temperature for activating dry yeast is between 35Â°C and 40Â°C. In many African kitchens, you do not have a thermometer. Use the wrist test: drip a few drops of water on the inside of your wrist. It should feel comfortably warm, neither hot nor cold. If it feels hot, let it cool for 2 minutes. If it feels cold, warm it slightly.

Measure 100ml of the total water (250ml) and stir in 1 teaspoon of sugar and the 7g yeast. Let it sit for 5â10 minutes. You should see a creamy foam on topâthat means the yeast is active. If no foam appears, your yeast is dead; throw it away and start with fresh yeast. Never use water above 45Â°Câit kills yeast instantly. In harmattan season, water from the tap can be too cold; warm it slightly on the stove.

Once the yeast is foamy, add the remaining 150ml of water at the same warm temperature. Mix with the flour as usual. If you are using active dry yeast (not instant), you must always proof it first in warm water with sugar. Instant yeast can be mixed directly with dry flour, but proofing it first gives you a head start and confirms it is alive.

In very hot climates (over 35Â°C ambient), use water at 30â35Â°C to avoid over-proofing the dough later. In cold weather, use water at 40â42Â°C. Always err on the side of slightly cooler waterâit slows fermentation but does not kill the yeast. Remember, the dough will also warm up as you knead. So start with water that feels just warm to the touch.

3. Kneading techniques for cassava dough

Cassava dough is more fragile than pure wheat dough because the cassava flour dilutes gluten. You must knead gently but thoroughly. Start by pressing the dough down with the heel of your hand, then fold it in half towards you. Give it a quarter turn and repeat. This is the classic push-fold-turn method. Do this for 10 minutes. The dough will feel sticky at firstâresist adding too much flour. Instead, oil your hands lightly with vegetable oil to prevent sticking.

After 5 minutes, the dough should become smoother. If it tears easily, it needs more kneading. Cassava dough will never become as elastic as pure wheat dough, but it should hold together when you stretch a small piece. If it breaks immediately, knead for another 3â4 minutes. Do not over-kneadâcassava can make the dough tough. Stop when the surface is smooth and slightly tacky.

In African homes, many bakers knead on a wooden board or a clean table. Avoid metal surfacesâthey can react with the cassava. If you are kneading on a mat, make sure it is food-safe and clean. A common mistake is to add too much wheat flour during kneading. This makes the loaf dry. Use oil instead. Another mistake is kneading for too short a timeâthe bread will crumble. Ten minutes is a good target.

To test if the dough is ready, press it with a finger. It should spring back slowly. If it springs back quickly, it needs more kneading. If it leaves a deep indent, it is over-kneaded. Once ready, shape into a ball and place in a lightly oiled bowl. Cover with a clean cloth or plastic wrap. Let it rest for 10 minutes before the first proof. This short rest makes the dough easier to handle.

4. First and second proofing of cassava bread dough

Proofing (also called rising) is when yeast produces gas that makes the dough rise. After kneading, place the dough in a lightly oiled bowl, cover with a damp cloth or plastic wrap, and leave it in a warm place. The ideal temperature is 25â28Â°C. In many African kitchens, the warmest spot is near a sunny window or inside a closed cupboard. Avoid drafty areas. The first proof takes 1 to 1.5 hours. The dough should double in size. If your kitchen is cold, it may take 2 hours. Do not rush it.

To check if the first proof is done, press two fingers into the dough. If the indent stays, it is ready. If it springs back, it needs more time. If it collapses, it is over-proofed. Over-proofed dough smells like alcohol and becomes sticky. If that happens, punch it down and reshape, then let it rise again for 30 minutes. After the first proof, punch down the dough to release the gas. Knead it lightly for 30 seconds.

Now shape the dough into a ball and place it back in the bowl for the second proof. This second proof is shorterâ30 to 45 minutes. The dough should rise again, but not necessarily double. It should look puffy. If you are baking in a hot climate, the second proof may take only 20 minutes. In cooler areas, 60 minutes. Always cover the dough to prevent a dry crust from forming.

A common mistake is to skip the second proof. Without it, the bread will be dense. Another mistake is to proof in a too-hot place (above 35Â°C). That makes the yeast produce too much gas and then collapse. For cassava bread, the second proof is crucial because the cassava flour weakens the gluten structure. A gentle, slow rise gives the best texture. After the second proof, the dough is ready for shaping.

5. Shaping loaves by hand without a loaf pan

You do not need a loaf pan to make beautiful cassava bread. After the second proof, turn the dough onto a lightly floured surface. Do not punch it downâyou want to keep the air. Gently press it into a rough rectangle, about 2cm thick. Fold the top third down and the bottom third up, like a letter. Then fold the left and right sides in. This builds tension. Now shape into a round ball (boule) or an oval (batard). For a round loaf, cup your hands around the dough and drag it towards you on the counter. The friction will tighten the surface.

For an oval loaf, roll the dough gently with your palms to elongate it. Pinch the seam closed. Place the shaped loaf on a baking tray lined with parchment paper or a lightly oiled banana leaf. If you have a banneton (proofing basket), dust it with wheat flour and place the dough seam-side up. If not, use a clean bowl lined with a floured cloth. Let the shaped loaf rest for 20â30 minutes while you preheat the oven to 200Â°C.

In African villages, many bakers use a large clay pot or a metal pot with a lid to bake bread. If you are using a pot, preheat it empty for 10 minutes, then carefully place the shaped dough inside. The lid traps steam, giving a good crust. For a free-form loaf on a tray, slash the top with a sharp knife before baking. This allows steam to escape and prevents cracking.

Handle the dough gently at this stage. Cassava dough is more delicate than wheat dough. If it deflates, you have been too rough. Work quickly and confidently. If the dough sticks, dust with a little wheat flour. Never use cassava flour for dustingâit burns. After shaping, you can also brush the top with water or oil to help browning. Then bake immediately.

6. Scoring and preparing dough for baking

Scoring is cutting the top of the dough with a sharp blade before baking. It lets steam escape and helps the bread expand without tearing. For cassava bread, score with a clean razor blade or a very sharp knife. Make cuts about 1cm deep and 5cm long. You can do a single slash down the middle, a cross, or three parallel slashes. Do not saw back and forthâuse one quick, decisive motion. If the blade drags, dip it in water or oil first.

Before scoring, make sure the oven is fully preheated to 200Â°C. If you are using a clay pot or Dutch oven, preheat it for 20 minutes. For a baking tray, place an empty metal pan on the bottom rack and pour 1 cup of hot water into it when you put the bread in. This creates steam, which gives a crispy crust. Cassava bread benefits from steam because it has less gluten.

Just before baking, you can brush the loaf with a little water or oil. Water gives a shiny crust; oil gives a softer crust. If you want a soft crust, cover the loaf with a clean cloth after baking. If you want a crusty loaf, leave it uncovered. You can also sprinkle sesame seeds or oats on top after brushing with water. Do not add sugar on topâit burns.

Place the scored loaf in the oven. Bake for 30â35 minutes. The bread is done when it sounds hollow when tapped on the bottom. If the top browns too fast, cover with foil. After baking, cool on a wire rack for at least 20 minutes before slicing. Cutting hot bread makes it gummy. In African humidity, store the cooled bread in a paper bag or a clean clothânot plastic, which makes it soggy. Eat within 2 days or freeze for up to 1 month.

Practical Reference: Cassava Bread Dough Fundamentals

This practical reference supports the RISE AFRICA module Cassava Bread Dough Fundamentals. It is written for bakers, small-scale processors, and bakery enterprises in Africa who want to produce consistent cassava-wheat composite bread with locally available ingredients and equipment. The focus is on commercial viability: repeatable dough performance, reduced ingredient waste, and products that meet customer expectations for taste, texture, and shelf life.

Cassava bread in Africa is typically a composite bread, meaning cassava flour is blended with wheat flour. High-quality cassava flour (HQCF) is the preferred cassava input because it is refined, dried, and milled to a particle size closer to wheat flour. This improves dough handling and crumb structure. The practical challenge is that cassava flour has no gluten, so it cannot form the elastic network that wheat flour provides. The role of cassava flour is to extend wheat flour, reduce cost, and add local flavor and nutrition. The role of wheat flour is to provide gluten for gas retention and structure. A workable range for many bakeries is 10â30% cassava flour replacement of wheat flour, depending on the wheat flour strength and the desired product quality.

Mixing Cassava Flour with Wheat Flour

Start with a tested ratio. For commercial bread, a common starting point is 20% cassava flour and 80% wheat flour. If your wheat flour is strong (12â13% protein), you can push cassava to 25â30%. If it is weaker, stay at 10â15%. Always blend dry ingredients first: cassava flour, wheat flour, salt, sugar, and yeast (if using dry yeast). This prevents clumping and ensures even distribution of yeast and salt. Add fat (vegetable oil, butter, or margarine) after the dry blend is uniform. Fat improves crumb softness and extends shelf life, which matters for retail and street vendors.

Water absorption is the most important mixing variable. Cassava flour absorbs more water than wheat flour, but it also releases water during baking differently. A practical starting point is 60â65% water based on total flour weight. If dough feels stiff and dry, add water in 1% increments. If it feels sticky and slack, add wheat flour in small amounts. Do not add cassava flour to fix stickiness, because it will not develop gluten and may make the dough more fragile.

Getting the Right Water Temperature for Yeast Activation

Yeast is a living organism. If the water is too cold, fermentation slows and proofing takes too long. If the water is too hot, yeast cells die and the dough will not rise. The safe range for most commercial dry yeast is 35â43Â°C. In many African bakeries without thermometers, this is described as lukewarm water: comfortable to the wrist, not steaming. If you can hold your finger in the water for 10 seconds without discomfort, it is likely in the safe range. For fresh yeast, use slightly cooler water, around 30â35Â°C.

In hot climates, water from a plastic tank can reach 40Â°C or more in the afternoon. Always check before mixing. If water is too hot, add cool water or let it stand. If water is too cold, mix with warm water. Sugar in the dough feeds yeast, but salt slows it down. Keep salt away from direct contact with yeast during the initial mix. For composite dough, slightly warmer water (within the safe range) helps compensate for the heavier cassava flour and improves initial rise.

Cassava contains naturally occurring cyanogenic compounds. Improperly processed cassava flour can retain dangerous levels of cyanide. Only use high-quality cassava flour (HQCF) from reputable processors who follow proper grating, pressing, fermentation, and drying steps. Never use raw, unprocessed cassava flour for bread. If flour smells bitter or has a strong off-odor, do not use it. Symptoms of cyanide poisoning include dizziness, headache, nausea, and difficulty breathing. Seek medical help immediately if these occur after handling or eating cassava products.

Kneading Techniques for Cassava Dough

Cassava dough is more fragile than pure wheat dough. The goal of kneading is to develop the wheat gluten network without tearing the cassava-starch matrix. Use a moderate kneading speed and time. For hand kneading, use the push-fold-turn method: push the dough away with the heel of your hand, fold it back over itself, rotate 90 degrees, and repeat. Knead for 8â12 minutes for 20% cassava dough. For 30% cassava, reduce to 6â10 minutes to avoid overworking. The dough should be smooth, slightly tacky, and able to stretch without breaking immediately. A windowpane test can help: stretch a small piece thin enough to see light through it. If it tears easily, knead a little more. If it is already smooth and elastic, stop.

First and Second Proofing

First proofing (bulk fermentation) allows flavor development and gas production. Place dough in a lightly oiled bowl, cover with a clean cloth or plastic, and leave at 28â32Â°C for 45â90 minutes. In hot African climates, avoid direct sunlight and very warm rooms. The dough should roughly double in size. Punch down gently to release large gas pockets. Divide and pre-shape, then rest for 10â15 minutes before final shaping. Second proofing (final proof) happens after shaping. Place loaves on a tray or in a proofing box at 30â35Â°C for 30â60 minutes. The dough should rise to about 1.5 times its original size. Do not over-proof, because cassava dough can collapse and produce a flat, dense loaf.

Shaping Loaves by Hand Without a Loaf Pan

Hand shaping is practical for small bakeries and street vendors. Lightly flour your work surface with wheat flour, not cassava flour. Flatten the dough into a rectangle, fold the sides toward the center, then roll from the top to form a tight cylinder. Seal the seam with the edge of your hand. For round loaves, shape into a ball by pulling the dough toward you on the counter, creating surface tension. Place shaped loaves on a baking tray lined with parchment or lightly oiled. Keep space between loaves for expansion. If you do not have a tray, use a clean, oiled metal sheet or a flat clay baking surface.

Scoring and Preparing Dough for Baking

Scoring controls where the bread expands during baking. Use a sharp blade or knife to make quick, shallow cuts (about 1 cm deep) at a 30â45Â° angle. For round loaves, score a cross or square. For long loaves, make 3â4 diagonal cuts. Score just before baking, after the second proof. Preheat your oven to 180â200Â°C. If using a charcoal or wood-fired oven, ensure it is evenly heated. Steam helps crust development: place a small pan of water in the oven or spray the oven walls with water before closing. Bake until the crust is golden brown and the bottom sounds hollow when tapped, usually 25â40 minutes depending on loaf size.

Quick Reference Checklist

  2. Blend cassava and wheat flour dry before adding water, yeast, salt, and fat.
  3. Start with 20% cassava flour and adjust based on dough feel and bread volume.
  4. Use lukewarm water at 35â43Â°C for dry yeast; never use hot water.

  6. First proof until doubled, then gently punch down and rest before shaping.
