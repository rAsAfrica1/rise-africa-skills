Lessons

1. Simple Past Tense

In Swahili, the simple past tense is formed by inserting the infix -li- between the subject prefix and the verb root. For example, 'I ate' is nilikula (ni- I, -li- past, -kula eat). 'You ate' is ulikula. 'He/she ate' is alikula. The subject prefixes are: ni- (I), u- (you singular), a- (he/she), tu- (we), m- (you plural), wa- (they). So 'we ate' is tulikula, 'you all ate' is mlikula, and 'they ate' is walikula. To make the negative past, change -li- to -ku- and add the negative prefix ha- before the subject prefix, except for 'I' which becomes si-. For example, 'I did not eat' is sikula (si- negative I, -ku- past negative, -kula eat). 'You did not eat' is hukula. 'He did not eat' is hakula. Practice with common verbs: kunywa (to drink) -> nilinywa (I drank), kusoma (to read) -> nilisoma (I read), kuandika (to write) -> niliandika (I wrote). Use these in daily African contexts: 'Nilikula ugali na sukuma wiki jana' (I ate ugali and collard greens yesterday). 'Alisoma kitabu cha Kiswahili' (He read a Swahili book). 'Tulinywa chai asubuhi' (We drank tea in the morning). To ask questions, use the same structure but with a rising intonation or question word. For example, 'Ulikula?' (Did you eat?) or 'Ulifanya nini jana?' (What did you do yesterday?). Remember that time expressions like jana (yesterday), juzi (day before yesterday), and asubuhi (morning) often appear with past tense. Practice by saying five things you did yesterday using -li-. For instance, 'Niliamka saa mbili asubuhi' (I woke up at 8 AM), 'Nilikunywa chai' (I drank tea), 'Nilifanya kazi shambani' (I worked in the farm), 'Nilipika wali' (I cooked rice), 'Nililala saa nne usiku' (I slept at 10 PM). This tense is essential for telling stories and reporting events. In African markets, you might hear 'Nilinunua mahindi kwa shilingi elfu tano' (I bought maize for 5000 shillings). Keep practicing with verbs you use daily.

2. Talking About Past Events

When talking about past events in Swahili, you need to combine the past tense with specific time markers and details. Start by identifying the event and when it happened. Use jana (yesterday), juzi (day before yesterday), wiki iliyopita (last week), mwezi uliopita (last month), or mwaka uliopita (last year). For example, 'Jana nilikwenda sokoni' (Yesterday I went to the market). To add more information, use the past tense consistently. 'Nilinunua nyanya, vitunguu, na pilipili' (I bought tomatoes, onions, and peppers). 'Nilikutana na rafiki yangu' (I met my friend). 'Tulizungumza kwa saa moja' (We talked for one hour). To describe a series of past events, use the past tense for each verb. 'Nilifika nyumbani, nilipumzika, kisha nilipika chakula' (I arrived home, rested, then cooked food). For negative past events, use the -ku- infix with negative prefixes. 'Sikuenda kazini jana kwa sababu nilikuwa mgonjwa' (I did not go to work yesterday because I was sick). 'Hakufika kwenye mkutano' (He did not arrive at the meeting). To ask about past events, use question words like lini (when), nini (what), wapi (where), and nani (who). 'Ulifanya nini jana?' (What did you do yesterday?). 'Ulikwenda wapi?' (Where did you go?). 'Nani alikuja?' (Who came?). In African contexts, you might talk about past events like weddings, funerals, harvests, or business transactions. For example, 'Mwaka jana tulivuna mahindi mengi' (Last year we harvested a lot of maize). 'Nilifungua duka langu mwaka 2020' (I opened my shop in 2020). 'Alisafiri kwenda Nairobi wiki iliyopita' (He traveled to Nairobi last week). Practice by telling a short story about your day yesterday, using at least five past tense verbs. For instance: 'Jana niliamka saa nne asubuhi. Nilioga na nilikunywa chai. Kisha nilienda shambani. Nililima na nilipanda mboga. Mchana nilikula na nilipumzika. Jioni nilisoma kitabu. Usiku nililala.' This builds fluency and confidence in narrating past events.

3. Simple Future Tense

The simple future tense in Swahili is formed by inserting the infix -ta- between the subject prefix and the verb root. For example, 'I will eat' is nitakula (ni- I, -ta- future, -kula eat). 'You will eat' is utakula. 'He/she will eat' is atakula. 'We will eat' is tutakula. 'You all will eat' is mtakula. 'They will eat' is watakula. To make the negative future, change -ta- to -ta- but add the negative prefix ha- before the subject prefix, except for 'I' which becomes si-. For example, 'I will not eat' is sitakula. 'You will not eat' is hutakula. 'He will not eat' is hatakula. 'We will not eat' is hatutakula. 'You all will not eat' is hamtakula. 'They will not eat' is hawatakula. Practice with common verbs: kunywa (to drink) -> nitakunywa (I will drink), kusoma (to read) -> nitasoma (I will read), kuandika (to write) -> nitaandika (I will write). Use these in daily African contexts: 'Nitakula ugali na samaki kesho' (I will eat ugali and fish tomorrow). 'Atasoma kitabu cha Kiswahili kesho' (He will read a Swahili book tomorrow). 'Tutakunywa chai asubuhi' (We will drink tea in the morning). To ask questions, use the same structure but with a rising intonation or question word. For example, 'Utakula?' (Will you eat?) or 'Utafanya nini kesho?' (What will you do tomorrow?). Remember that time expressions like kesho (tomorrow), kesho kutwa (day after tomorrow), wiki ijayo (next week), mwezi ujao (next month), and mwaka ujao (next year) often appear with future tense. Practice by saying five things you will do tomorrow using -ta-. For instance, 'Nitaamka saa mbili asubuhi' (I will wake up at 8 AM), 'Nitakunywa chai' (I will drink tea), 'Nitafanya kazi shambani' (I will work in the farm), 'Nitapika wali' (I will cook rice), 'Nitalala saa nne usiku' (I will sleep at 10 PM). This tense is essential for making plans and promises. In African markets, you might hear 'Nitanunua mahindi kwa shilingi elfu tano' (I will buy maize for 5000 shillings). Keep practicing with verbs you use daily.

4. Making Predictions and Plans

In Swahili, you use the future tense -ta- to make predictions and plans. Predictions are about what you think will happen, while plans are about what you intend to do. For predictions, you can use words like labda (maybe), pengine (perhaps), or bila shaka (without doubt). For example, 'Labda mvua itanyesha kesho' (Maybe it will rain tomorrow). 'Bila shaka timu yetu itashinda' (Without doubt our team will win). 'Pengine bei zitapanda' (Perhaps prices will rise). For plans, you often use the future tense with time expressions. 'Kesho nitakwenda kazini' (Tomorrow I will go to work). 'Wiki ijayo tutaanza mradi mpya' (Next week we will start a new project). 'Mwaka ujao nitasafiri kwenda Uganda' (Next year I will travel to Uganda). To express intention, you can also use the verb 'kusudi' (to intend) or 'kupanga' (to plan). 'Nakusudia kufungua duka' (I intend to open a shop). 'Tunapanga kujenga nyumba' (We plan to build a house). In African business contexts, making plans is crucial. For example, a farmer might say, 'Nitapanda mahindi mwezi ujao' (I will plant maize next month). A trader might say, 'Nitaagiza bidhaa kutoka Dubai' (I will order goods from Dubai). A student might say, 'Nitasoma kwa bidii ili nifaulu mtihani' (I will study hard so that I pass the exam). To ask about plans, use 'Una mpango gani?' (What is your plan?) or 'Utakwenda wapi?' (Where will you go?). To make negative plans, use the negative future: 'Sitakwenda sokoni kesho' (I will not go to the market tomorrow). 'Hawatakuja kwenye mkutano' (They will not come to the meeting). Practice by writing down three predictions and three plans for the coming week. For example: 'Nitarudi nyumbani mapema' (I will return home early), 'Nitakutana na mteja wangu' (I will meet my client), 'Nitahudhuria mkutano wa vijana' (I will attend a youth meeting). Predictions: 'Bei ya mafuta itapanda' (Fuel prices will rise), 'Timu ya Taifa itashinda' (The national team will win), 'Mvua itanyesha mwezi ujao' (It will rain next month). Use these structures in daily conversations to express your future intentions and expectations.

5. Time Expressions: Yesterday, Tomorrow

Time expressions are essential for using past and future tenses correctly. In Swahili, the most common time expressions are jana (yesterday) for past and kesho (tomorrow) for future. Other past time expressions include juzi (day before yesterday), wiki iliyopita (last week), mwezi uliopita (last month), mwaka uliopita (last year), asubuhi (morning), mchana (afternoon), jioni (evening), and usiku (night). For future, we have kesho kutwa (day after tomorrow), wiki ijayo (next week), mwezi ujao (next month), mwaka ujao (next year), and also asubuhi, mchana, jioni, usiku but with future context. When using these expressions, the tense of the verb must match. For past, use -li-: 'Jana nilikwenda sokoni' (Yesterday I went to the market). For future, use -ta-: 'Kesho nitakwenda sokoni' (Tomorrow I will go to the market). Note that some time expressions like asubuhi, mchana, jioni, usiku can be used with both past and future depending on the tense. For example, 'Asubuhi nilikunywa chai' (In the morning I drank tea) vs 'Asubuhi nitakunywa chai' (In the morning I will drink tea). To be more specific, you can combine: 'Jana asubuhi' (Yesterday morning), 'Kesho jioni' (Tomorrow evening). Also, 'saa' is used for clock time: 'saa mbili asubuhi' (8 AM), 'saa nne usiku' (10 PM). In African contexts, people often use these expressions in daily conversations. For example, a farmer might say, 'Jana nililima shamba, kesho nitapanda mbegu' (Yesterday I plowed the field, tomorrow I will plant seeds). A trader might say, 'Wiki iliyopita niliuza bidhaa nyingi, wiki ijayo nitaagiza zaidi' (Last week I sold many goods, next week I will order more). Practice by making a timetable of your activities for yesterday and tomorrow using these time expressions. For example: 'Jana asubuhi niliamka saa mbili. Nilikunywa chai. Nilikwenda shambani. Mchana nilikula. Jioni nilipumzika. Usiku nililala. Kesho asubuhi nitaamka saa mbili. Nitakunywa chai. Nitakwenda shambani. Mchana nitakula. Jioni nitapumzika. Usiku nitalala.' This helps you internalize the difference between past and future tenses with time expressions.

6. Narrating a Story

Narrating a story in Swahili requires a mix of past tense for events that happened and sometimes future tense for what will happen next. A good story has a beginning, middle, and end. Start with a time marker like 'Jana' (Yesterday) or 'Mwaka jana' (Last year). Then use past tense verbs to describe events. For example: 'Jana nilikwenda sokoni. Nilikutana na rafiki yangu. Tulizungumza kwa muda mrefu. Kisha tulienda kunywa chai. Tulikunywa chai na kula mandazi. Baada ya hapo, tulienda nyumbani. Nilipumzika kidogo. Usiku nilisoma kitabu. Nililala saa nne usiku.' To make your story interesting, add details like where, when, who, and why. Use connectors like kisha (then), baada ya hapo (after that), kwa hiyo (therefore), lakini (but). For example: 'Nilikwenda sokoni kununua mboga. Lakini sikuwa na pesa za kutosha. Kwa hiyo nilinunua mboga kidogo. Kisha nilirudi nyumbani.' To talk about future events in a story, use future tense. For example, at the end of a story you might say: 'Kesho nitakwenda tena sokoni. Nitanunua mboga zaidi. Nitakutana na rafiki yangu tena.' In African oral storytelling, you often start with 'Hadithi, hadithi!' and the audience responds 'Hadithi njoo!' Then you tell the story. For example: 'Hadithi, hadithi! Hadithi njoo! Zamani za kale, palikuwa na mkulima mmoja. Alikuwa na shamba dogo. Siku moja, aliamka asubuhi na mapema. Alikwenda shambani. Alilima na kupanda mbegu. Mvua ilinyesha. Mimea ilikua. Mkulima alivuna mazao mengi. Aliuza sokoni na akapata pesa nyingi. Kwa hiyo, alijenga nyumba mpya. Kesho yake, alikwenda kuwasaidia majirani. Walifurahi sana.' Practice by telling a short story about your day, your work, or a memorable event. Use at least ten past tense verbs and one future tense verb. This will build your confidence in narrating stories. Remember, storytelling is a key part of African culture, so practice with friends and family.

<h2>Practical Reference: Swahili Past and Future Tenses for African Agribusiness</h2>

<p>In East African agribusiness, Swahili is the working language of farm gate negotiations, cooperative meetings, transport logistics, and market-day transactions. Whether you are a procurement officer in Arusha, a field agronomist in Kisumu, or a trader moving maize from Mbeya to Dar es Salaam, your ability to describe what <em>already happened</em> and what <em>will happen</em> determines how well you close deals, manage risk, and coordinate supply chains. This Practical Reference consolidates the six lessons in this module Simple Past Tense, Talking About Past Events, Simple Future Tense, Making Predictions and Plans, Time Expressions (Yesterday, Tomorrow), and Narrating a Story into one business-first desk guide. It is designed to be printed, pinned in a store, or saved on a phone for quick consultation before a meeting, a farm visit, or a call with a buyer.</p>

<h3>Why Tense Accuracy Matters Commercially</h3>

<p>Swahili tense markers are compact but carry heavy commercial weight. A single infix distinguishes "I paid" from "I will pay." In a maize contract worth millions of shillings, that distinction is the difference between a completed obligation and a default. The past tense marker <strong>-li-</strong> and the future marker <strong>-ta-</strong> sit between the subject prefix and the verb root, and they are non-negotiable in formal and semi-formal business speech. Mastering them allows you to:</p>

<ul>
<li>Confirm delivery, payment, and quality inspection timelines without ambiguity.</li>
<li>Forecast harvest volumes, input requirements, and transport schedules.</li>
<li>Narrate a supply-chain incident (a delayed truck, a rejected batch) in a way that supports insurance or dispute resolution.</li>
<li>Build trust with smallholder farmers, cooperative leaders, and off-takers who expect clear commitments.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Narrating a Story: Structuring an Agribusiness Incident Report</h3>

<p>Story narration in Swahili uses a chain of past-tense verbs, often linked with <strong>nika-</strong> (and then I) or <strong>tuka-</strong> (and then we). This structure is ideal for incident reports, insurance claims, and post-mortem reviews. A strong agribusiness narrative follows a clear sequence:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Setting:</strong> Jana asubuhi, nilifika shambani. (Yesterday morning, I arrived at the farm.)</li>
<li><strong>Observation:</strong> Nilikuta wafanyakazi wakikagua mahindi. (I found the workers inspecting the maize.)</li>
<li><strong>Action:</strong> Tulianza kuvuna na kupima unyevu. (We began harvesting and measuring moisture.)</li>
<li><strong>Problem:</strong> Gari la usafirishaji lilichelewa kwa saa mbili. (The transport truck was delayed by two hours.)</li>
<li><strong>Resolution:</strong> Tulipiga simu kwa meneja, na akatuma gari lingine. (We called the manager, and he sent another truck.)</li>
<li><strong>Outcome:</strong> Hatimaye, tulifanikiwa kusafirisha mzigo jioni. (Finally, we succeeded in transporting the load in the evening.)</li>
</ol>

<p>For future plans, the same logic applies with <strong>-ta-</strong>: <em>Kesho tutaanza kukagua, kisha tutapakia, na mwishowe tutasafirisha.</em> (Tomorrow we will begin inspecting, then we will load, and finally we will transport.) This parallel structure helps teams align on sequence and accountability.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul style="line-height:1.8;">
<li><strong>Identify the tense before you speak:</strong> Past = <strong>-li-</strong>; Future = <strong>-ta-</strong>. Insert the marker between the subject prefix and the verb root.</li>
<li><strong>Match time expressions to tense:</strong> <em>Jana, juzi, wiki iliyopita</em> pair with <strong>-li-</strong>; <em>kesho, kesho kutwa, wiki ijayo</em> pair with <strong>-ta-</strong>.</li>
<li><strong>Use the -li- chain for reports:</strong> Link sequential past actions with <strong>nika-</strong> or <strong>tuka-</strong> to narrate incidents clearly.</li>
<li><strong>Confirm financial verbs twice:</strong> Repeat <em>nililipa</em> (I paid) or <em>nitalipa</em> (I will pay) back to the counterparty before ending a call.</li>
<li><strong>Anchor predictions with evidence:</strong> When forecasting rain or yield, combine <strong>-ta-</strong> with a source, e.g., <em>Kwa mujibu wa metorolojia, mvua itanyesha.</em></li>
<li><strong>Write dates alongside tense:</strong> In contracts, add the calendar date next to <em>kesho</em> or <em>wiki ijayo</em> to prevent ambiguity.</li>
<li><strong>Practise the six core verbs:</strong> <em>kulipa</em> (pay), <em>kupima</em> (measure), <em>kusafirisha</em> (transport), <em>kununua</em> (buy), <em>kuuza</em> (sell), <em>kukagua</em> (inspect) in both past and future.</li>
<li><strong>Review after every negotiation:</strong> Note which tense forms caused confusion and drill them before the next meeting.</li>
</ul>
