Lessons

1. Drawing Out the Blade from Bar Stock

Start with a piece of high-carbon steel bar stock, like 5160 spring steel or 1084, about 25mm wide and 6mm thick. Cut a length of 150mm using a hacksaw or angle grinder. This will become your blade. Heat the steel in your forge until it's a bright orange-yellow, around 1100-1200C. If you don't have a forge, build a simple one with a hole in the ground, charcoal, and a hairdryer for air. Once hot, place the steel on your anvil and strike it with a 2-3kg hammer. You want to draw out the steelmake it longer and thinner. Work from the center outward, using the flat face of the hammer. Strike evenly on both sides to keep the blade straight. If the steel cools below a dull red, reheat it. Don't hammer cold steel; it will crack. For a camp knife, draw the blade out to about 200mm long and 3mm thick at the spine. Use the horn of the anvil or a hardy tool to help stretch the steel. Remember to keep the width consistent; you can use a pair of calipers to check thickness. This step takes timeexpect to reheat the steel 5-10 times. Take breaks to avoid fatigue. In Africa, many smiths use old car leaf springs as source steel; they are tough and hold an edge well. Always wear safety glasses and gloves. Work in a well-ventilated area.

2. Forging the Bevels and Edge

Now that the blade is drawn out, it's time to form the bevels. The bevel is the sloping part that leads to the cutting edge. Heat the blade to a bright orange again. Place it on the anvil with the edge facing up. Using a hammer with a slightly rounded face, strike at about a 10-15 degree angle to create the bevel. Start with light blows to establish the bevel line, then increase force. Work from the base of the blade (near the handle) towards the tip. Flip the blade and do the same on the other side. You want the bevels to meet in the middle, creating a thin edge. Check your progress by looking at the blade from the edge; you should see a symmetrical triangle. If the edge gets too thin, it might fold over (called a cold shut). If that happens, grind it out later. For a camp knife, aim for a bevel height of about 15-20mm. Use a cross-peen hammer for faster material movement if you have one. Keep the blade hot; if it turns red, reheat. After forging the bevels, the edge might be wavy. That's normal; you'll straighten it later. This step requires practice to get even bevels. Take your time and don't rush. In many African workshops, smiths use a simple hammer and anvil to produce beautiful knives. Remember to anneal the blade if it becomes too hard to work; heat it to non-magnetic and let it cool slowly in ash or vermiculite.

3. Creating the Tang and Shoulders

The tang is the part of the blade that extends into the handle. The shoulders are the transition between the blade and the tang. To create the tang, you need to draw out a section of the steel at the base of the blade. Heat the base of the blade to orange. Using a hammer, strike the steel to reduce its thickness and width, but keep it long enough to go through your handle material. For a camp knife, the tang should be about 100mm long and 15mm wide, tapering slightly towards the end. You can use a hot cut or chisel to define the shoulders. The shoulders should be square and even; they will sit against the handle material. Work on both sides to keep the tang centered. If you have a guillotine tool or a spring fuller, use it to neck down the steel. Otherwise, use the edge of your anvil. Once the tang is formed, check that the blade and tang are in line. If not, heat and adjust. The tang should be thick enough to be strong but not too thick to make the handle heavy. Aim for about 4-5mm thick. After forging, let the blade cool slowly. You can now use a file to clean up the shoulders and tang. This step is crucial for a comfortable and durable handle. Many African blades have a stick tang, which is simple and effective. Practice on scrap steel first if you're new.

4. Straightening and Normalizing the Blade

After forging, the blade may be bent or warped. Straightening is done while the steel is hot. Heat the blade to a dull red (about 700-800C) and place it on the anvil. Using a hammer, gently tap the high spots to straighten. You can also use a wooden mallet to avoid marring the steel. Check straightness by sighting down the blade or using a straightedge. Work quickly before the steel cools. Once straight, it's time to normalize. Normalizing relieves stresses and refines the grain. Heat the blade to non-magnetic (about 750-800C) and then let it cool in still air. Do this three times. This cycle makes the steel softer and easier to file and grind. Don't quench it; just let it cool naturally. If you have a magnet, test: when the steel becomes non-magnetic, it's at the right temperature. After normalizing, the blade will have a uniform gray color. This step is often skipped by beginners, but it's important for a tough blade. In Africa, some smiths use a charcoal forge and judge temperature by color. Practice seeing the shades of red. Once normalized, the blade is ready for refining. Always handle hot steel with tongs and wear protective gear.

5. Refining the Profile with Files and Grinders

Now that the blade is normalized, you can refine its shape. Use a file to clean up the profile, remove hammer marks, and set the final edge. A 10-inch bastard file is good for this. Clamp the blade in a vise with the edge up. File from the ricasso (the flat area above the handle) towards the tip, maintaining the bevel angle. Use a file guide if you have one to keep angles consistent. For faster material removal, use an angle grinder with a flap disc or a belt grinder. But be careful not to overheat the steel; dip it in water frequently. You want a smooth transition from the bevel to the spine. The edge should be about 0.5-1mm thick before heat treating; any thinner and it might crack. Check symmetry by looking at the blade from the top. Use a sharpie to mark high spots and file them down. For the handle, shape the tang and shoulders so they are smooth. You can also drill holes in the tang for pins if you plan to use scales. In Africa, many smiths use simple files and stones to finish blades. Take your time; this step determines the final look and performance. After filing, the blade should be ready for heat treatment. Remember to clean the file often with a wire brush.

6. Practice Project: Forging a Simple Camp Knife

Now put it all together: forge a simple camp knife. Start with a 150mm piece of 5160 steel, 25mm wide and 6mm thick. Follow the steps: draw out the blade to 200mm, forge bevels, create a tang, straighten, normalize, and refine. For a camp knife, aim for a blade length of 150mm (6 inches) and a handle of 120mm. The spine should be about 3mm thick, with a full flat grind. After forging and refining, heat treat the blade. Heat to non-magnetic (about 800C), quench in warm oil (canola or motor oil). Temper in an oven at 200C for one hour, twice. This gives a hard edge and tough spine. Then finish with sandpaper up to 400 grit. Attach wooden scales using epoxy and brass pins. Shape the handle with a file and sand smooth. Sharpen on a stone. Test the knife by cutting rope and carving wood. If the edge chips, temper at a higher temperature (220C). If it rolls, temper lower. This project teaches you the entire process. In Africa, you can sell such knives locally or use them for farming and camping. Practice makes perfect; make at least three knives to build skill. Always prioritize safety: wear gloves, glasses, and work in a ventilated area.

<h2>Practical Reference: Forging a Basic Blade</h2>

<p>This practical reference supports the module <strong>Forging a Basic Blade</strong> in the RISE AFRICA Bladesmithing course. It is written for working smiths, workshop owners, and apprentices who intend to turn blade-making into a reliable income stream not just a hobby. In the African context, bladesmithing sits at the intersection of heritage craft and real market demand: agricultural tools, kitchen knives, hunting knives, and custom orders from urban buyers. The lessons in this module Drawing Out the Blade from Bar Stock, Forging the Bevels and Edge, Creating the Tang and Shoulders, Straightening and Normalizing the Blade, Refining the Profile with Files and Grinders, and the Practice Project: Forging a Simple Camp Knife build on one another. This reference condenses the practical numbers, temperatures, and checks you need at the forge so you can produce consistent, sellable blades.</p>

<h3>Why This Matters Commercially</h3>
<p>A bladesmith who can forge a clean, straight, well-profiled camp knife from a length of spring steel or recycled leaf spring has a product with strong margins. In many African markets, a hand-forged camp knife or bush knife sells for 310 times the cost of the raw steel, and repeat customers come from farmers, butchers, safari operators, and households. The difference between a blade that sells and one that does not is usually not the steel it is the forging discipline: correct drawing-out, controlled bevels, a straight blade, and a properly formed tang and shoulders. This reference gives you the numbers to hit those targets consistently.</p>

<h3 style="color:#b71c1c;margin-top:0;"> SAFETY WARNING Read Before Forging</h3>
<p style="margin-bottom:8px;"><strong>Fire and burn risk:</strong> Forging involves open flames, hot metal above 1000 C, and sparks. Always wear leather gloves, eye protection, and non-synthetic clothing (cotton or leather; synthetics melt onto skin). Keep a water bucket and fire extinguisher within reach. Never forge in a confined space without ventilation carbon monoxide from coal and gas forges is lethal.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom:8px;"><strong>Grinding and filing hazards:</strong> Metal dust and sparks from angle grinders can ignite nearby materials and damage eyes. Wear a face shield, not just safety glasses. Use water dips to keep blades cool and reduce dust.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom:8px;"><strong>Quenching:</strong> Quenching hot steel in oil can cause flare-ups. Use a metal quench tank with a lid, and never quench near flammable liquids or materials. Keep the quench area clean and dry.</p>
<p style="margin-bottom:0;"><strong>Ergonomics and fatigue:</strong> Long forging sessions cause strain injuries. Take breaks, stretch, and keep your anvil at the correct height (knuckle height when standing). In many African workshops, heat stress is a real risk work in shaded, ventilated areas and hydrate frequently.</p>

<h3>Step-by-Step Practical Checks</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Drawing out:</strong> Start with bar stock at bright orange. Draw the blade section down evenly, checking thickness with calipers or a gauge. Aim for a consistent spine thickness and a slightly thicker edge. Avoid over-thinning you need material for the bevels.</li>
<li><strong>Bevels and edge:</strong> Set the bevels with angled hammer blows, working from the spine toward the edge. Keep the edge at roughly 1.52 mm thick before heat treatment. Use a file guide or a simple jig to keep the plunge lines even if you are producing multiple blades.</li>
<li><strong>Tang and shoulders:</strong> Draw the tang down to your desired thickness (typically 46 mm). Form the shoulders with a shoulder tool or the anvil edge so they are square and clean. A well-formed shoulder seats the handle securely and looks professional this is a selling point.</li>
<li><strong>Straightening:</strong> After forging, check the blade for bends and twists. Straighten at dull red to orange with light, controlled blows. Do not force cold steel it will crack. Use a straightening jig or the anvil face for reference.</li>
<li><strong>Normalizing:</strong> Heat the blade to non-magnetic (test with a magnet), then air cool to room temperature. Repeat 23 times. This refines grain structure, reduces stress, and makes the blade easier to file and grind. Skipping normalizing is a common cause of warped or cracked blades.</li>
<li><strong>Refining profile:</strong> After normalizing, use files and angle grinders to refine the profile, bevels, and plunge lines. Keep the blade cool with water dips. Work slowly grinding too fast can overheat the thin edge and ruin the heat treatment later.</li>
<li><strong>Practice project camp knife:</strong> Apply all steps to a simple camp knife: 46 inch blade, 45 inch handle, full tang or hidden tang. Focus on symmetry, a straight spine, and a comfortable handle shape. This is your sellable product make it clean and consistent.</li>
</ol>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li> Steel selected and inspected for cracks or damage (especially recycled leaf spring).</li>
<li> Forge at bright orange to yellow for drawing out; reheat before colour drops.</li>
<li> Bevels set with edge left 1.52 mm thick before heat treatment.</li>
<li> Tang drawn to 46 mm; shoulders square and clean.</li>
<li> Blade straightened at dull red to orange; no cold bending.</li>
<li> Normalized 23 times to non-magnetic, then air cooled.</li>
<li> Profile refined with files/grinders, keeping blade cool with water dips.</li>
<li> Safety gear worn: leather gloves, eye/face protection, ventilation, fire safety.</li>
</ul>
