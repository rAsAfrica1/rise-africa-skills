1. Fiber Cement Sheet Installation

Fiber cement sheets are heavy, brittle, and long-lasting. In Africa, they are common in high-rainfall areas because they resist rot and insects. To install, you need at least two people. Start by checking your roof structure: purlins should be at 600 mm to 750 mm centres for 6 mm sheets, and 900 mm for 8 mm sheets. Use timber or steel purlins treated against termites. Always wear gloves, dust mask, and eye protection when cuttingfiber cement dust is harmful.

Lay sheets from the bottom edge (eaves) upward, working across the roof. Overlap side laps by one corrugation and end laps by 150 mm. Pre-drill holes 2 mm larger than the nail shank to allow for expansion. Use galvanised or stainless steel screws with rubber washers, driven into the crest of the corrugation, not the valley. Drive screws just until the washer compressesover-tightening cracks the sheet. For a 3 m sheet, use at least 5 fasteners per purlin. On steep roofs, use safety harnesses and roof ladders.

Cut fiber cement with a diamond blade or fiber cement shear. Avoid abrasive discs that create dust. For ridges, use matching fiber cement ridge caps, fixed with screws and sealed with neutral-cure silicone. At eaves, install a 50 mm overhang and a drip edge to direct water into the gutter. In coastal areas, use stainless steel fasteners to avoid corrosion. After installation, wash the roof to remove dust and check for leaks with a water hose. Maintenance: inspect annually for cracked sheets and loose fasteners. Replace cracked sheets immediately to prevent water damage.

2. Asphalt Sheet Shingle Installation

Asphalt shingles are popular for pitched roofs in Africa because they are lightweight, easy to cut, and relatively cheap. They come in strips or individual tabs. You need a clean, dry, smooth deckusually plywood or OSB at least 12 mm thick, nailed at 150 mm centres. Cover the deck with a waterproof underlayment (felt or synthetic) overlapping 100 mm horizontally and 150 mm vertically. In hot climates, choose light-coloured shingles to reflect heat.

Start at the eaves with a starter stripcut the tabs off a shingle and nail it along the edge, overlapping the drip edge by 10 mm. Nail with 25 mm galvanised roofing nails, 6 per shingle, placed 25 mm above the tab cuts and 12 mm from edges. Drive nails straight and flushnot into the shingle. Work upward in rows, offsetting each row by half a tab so joints don't align. In high-wind areas, use six nails per shingle and apply a dab of asphalt cement under each tab. For valleys, use a 900 mm wide metal valley flashing or woven method. On ridges, use pre-formed ridge caps or cut shingles and bend them over, nailing both sides.

In hot African climates, shingles can soften; avoid walking on them during the hottest part of the day. Clean cut edges with a sharp utility knife on a scrap board. Keep shingles in the shade before installationthey tear easily when hot. After installation, press down all tabs and check for exposed nails. Seal any nail heads with asphalt cement. Inspect after storms for lifted tabs and re-seal. Expect 1520 years life with proper ventilation.

3. Plastic and Composite Sheet Installation

Plastic and composite sheetssuch as polycarbonate, PVC, and fibreglass-reinforced polyesterare lightweight, translucent, and rust-proof. They are ideal for verandas, carports, and skylights. Most come in corrugated or trapezoidal profiles. They expand and contract with heat, so you must allow for movement. Use purlins at 600900 mm centres, depending on sheet thickness. Never use them near open flamesthey melt and drip.

Install from the leeward side (away from prevailing wind) to avoid wind-driven rain. Overlap side laps by one corrugation and end laps by 100150 mm. Pre-drill holes 34 mm larger than the screw diameter. Use screws with neoprene or EPDM washers and plastic caps. Drive screws into the crest, not the valley. Do not over-tightenthe sheet must be able to move. For long sheets, use a expansion gap of 5 mm per 3 m length at the end. Cut with a fine-tooth saw, jigsaw, or angle grinder with a metal cutting discbut keep speed low to avoid melting. Wear goggles and gloves.

For ridges, use matching plastic ridge caps fixed with screws and sealed with silicone. At eaves, allow a 5075 mm overhang and install a foam closure strip to keep out insects and dust. In dusty areas, clean sheets with mild soap and waternever solvents. For composite sheets with a gel coating, avoid walking on them when hot. Fasten with screws that have a low-profile head. Check for cracks around fasteners after a few months. Replace any sheet that shows crazing or yellowing. These sheets last 1015 years in African sun if UV-stabilised.

4. Adhesives and Fasteners for Non-Metal Sheets

Choosing the right fasteners and adhesives is critical for non-metal roofing sheets. For fiber cement, use galvanised or stainless steel screws with rubber washersnever plain steel, which rusts and stains. Screw length should penetrate the purlin by at least 25 mm. For asphalt shingles, use 2530 mm galvanised roofing nails with 10 mm heads. In coastal areas, use stainless steel or hot-dip galvanised. For plastic sheets, use screws with neoprene washers and plastic caps; metal washers can crack the sheet. Always pre-drill to avoid cracking.

Adhesives are used for sealing laps, ridges, and flashings. For fiber cement, use neutral-cure silicone or polyurethane sealant. For asphalt shingles, use asphalt roofing cement (black mastic) or modified bitumen tape. For plastic and composite, use silicone or MS polymer adhesivenot solvent-based adhesives that attack plastic. Apply adhesive in a continuous bead, not dots. Clean surfaces with a dry cloth before applying. In hot climates, choose high-temperature adhesives that won't soften.

Fastener spacing: for fiber cement, 5 screws per sheet per purlin; for asphalt, 6 nails per shingle; for plastic, 46 screws per square metre. Always drive fasteners perpendicular to the sheet. Use a screw gun with adjustable torque to avoid over-driving. For ridges and edges, use longer screws. Keep a supply of spare washers and caps. Inspect fasteners annuallytighten loose ones and replace rusted ones. In termite-prone areas, use steel or treated timber purlins. Never mix metalsgalvanic corrosion will destroy the roof. Store fasteners in a dry place to prevent rust.

5. Edge Details and Ridge Caps

Edge details and ridge caps protect the most vulnerable parts of the roof: the eaves, gables, and ridges. At the eaves, install a drip edge (metal flashing) under the underlayment and over the fascia board. It should extend 50 mm over the gutter. For fiber cement, use a fiber cement barge board or metal flashing. For asphalt shingles, the drip edge goes under the starter strip. For plastic sheets, use a foam closure strip between the sheet and the purlin to block wind and insects. Secure with screws every 300 mm.

Ridge caps cover the peak where two roof planes meet. For fiber cement, use matching fiber cement ridge pieces, bedded in silicone and screwed at 300 mm centres. For asphalt shingles, cut shingles into three tabs and bend them over the ridge, overlapping each by 50 mm, nailing both sides. For plastic, use pre-formed ridge caps with matching profile, fixed with screws and sealed with silicone. In all cases, the ridge cap must overlap the sheets by at least 100 mm on each side. For hips (where ridges meet at an angle), use hip caps or cut shingles to fit.

Gable edges (rake) need a barge board or metal flashing to prevent wind uplift. Fix with screws at 200 mm centres. For all edge details, use corrosion-resistant materials: aluminium, galvanised steel, or stainless steel. In coastal areas, avoid galvaniseduse aluminium or stainless. Seal all joints with neutral-cure silicone. Check edges after storms; re-seal if lifted. Proper edge details stop water from creeping under sheets and extend roof life by years.

6. Ventilation and Moisture Control

Without ventilation, non-metal roofs trap heat and moisture, causing condensation, mould, and premature failure. In Africa, the sun heats the roof, and at night the temperature drops, causing condensation on the underside. To prevent this, you need continuous airflow from eaves to ridge. Install soffit vents at the eaves (at least 1/150 of the roof area) and ridge vents at the peak. For every 1 m of roof, provide 1000 mm of vent area. Use insect mesh to keep out bugs. In hot climates, a reflective foil under the sheets can reduce heat gain.

For fiber cement and plastic sheets, leave a 2550 mm gap between the sheet and any insulation. For asphalt shingles, ensure the attic space is ventilateduse gable vents or turbine vents. In humid coastal areas, use a dehumidifier or ensure cross-ventilation. If you have a cathedral ceiling (no attic), use a ventilated ridge and soffit vents. Never block vents with insulation. For flat roofs (less than 10 slope), use a vapour barrier under the sheets and a ventilated air gap above the deck.

Moisture control also means proper flashing around chimneys, pipes, and walls. Use lead or aluminium flashing, sealed with silicone. Ensure gutters are clean and drain away from the building. In thatched or shingle roofs, use a breathable underlayment. Check for leaks after heavy rain. If you see mould, improve ventilation and clean with a mild bleach solution. For plastic sheets, condensation can dripinstall a anti-condensation felt or fleece on the underside. Regular inspection: check vents, flashings, and gutters twice a year. Good ventilation extends roof life and keeps the building comfortable.

<h2>Practical Reference: Installing Non-Metal Roofing Sheets in African Conditions</h2>

<p>Non-metal roofing sheets fibre cement, asphalt shingles, and plastic/composite profiles are increasingly specified across African markets because they resist corrosion in humid coastal zones, tolerate the intense UV of the Sahel and East African highlands, and can be transported and installed without the heavy plant needed for metal or tile systems. For the contractor, distributor, or developer, the commercial logic is straightforward: lower logistics cost, faster roof closure, and a product that can be sold into the fast-growing mid-income housing, school, clinic, and agro-processing segments.</p>

<p>But the margin on these jobs is destroyed by callbacks leaks at laps, blown-off sheets, cracked fibre cement, and condensation staining the ceiling below. This practical reference consolidates the installation rules that experienced African roofing crews apply on site, with the fasteners, adhesives, edge details, and ventilation provisions that keep the roof watertight and the warranty intact.</p>

<h2>1. Fibre Cement Sheet Installation</h2>
<p>Fibre cement is the workhorse for schools, clinics, and agro-processing sheds because it is non-combustible, resistant to termites and rot, and holds colour under UV. The critical installation rules are minimum pitch, correct lap, and pre-drilled holes.</p>

<p>Fibre cement sheets should be lifted, not dragged, and stacked flat on site. Pre-drill holes 2 mm larger than the shank diameter to allow thermal movement. On corrugated profiles, fix through the crest at every second corrugation, and never over-tighten the sheet will crack at the fixing point under thermal cycling, a common failure in hot inland climates where surface temperatures exceed 60 C.</p>

<h2>2. Asphalt Sheet Shingle Installation</h2>
<p>Asphalt shingles are gaining traction in urban African housing because they are light, easy to cut, and available in a range of colours. They require a continuous deck (plywood or OSB, minimum 15 mm) and a self-adhered or mechanically fixed underlay.</p>

<p>Start at the eave with a starter strip, offset each course by half a tab, and nail 150 mm above the exposure line. In coastal or high-wind zones (e.g. Mombasa, Dar es Salaam, Cape Town), use six nails per shingle and apply a bead of bituminous adhesive along the tab. On pitches below 18, double the underlay and seal all laps.</p>

<h2>3. Plastic and Composite Sheet Installation</h2>
<p>PVC, polycarbonate, and glass-reinforced composite sheets are popular for verandas, carports, and light industrial roofs. They are light and impact-resistant but expand significantly with heat up to 6 mm per metre for PVC on a 40 C swing.</p>

<p>Use slotted or oversize holes, and fix with screws fitted with EPDM or neoprene washers. Do not walk directly on the sheet; use crawl boards. Where sheets abut metal or fibre cement, allow a 10 mm gap and seal with a neutral-cure silicone or butyl tape compatible with the plastic solvent-based sealants will craze polycarbonate.</p>

<h2>5. Edge Details and Ridge Caps</h2>
<p>Edges and ridges are where 80% of leaks begin. At the eave, the sheet should overhang the fascia by 4050 mm and discharge into a gutter sized for the local rainfall intensity in West African monsoon zones, allow 1.52.0 litres per second per 100 m of roof. Fit a eaves closure or bird comb to stop wind-driven rain and pests.</p>

<p>At the ridge, use a purpose-made ridge cap bedded in butyl tape or neutral-cure silicone over a ridge batten. Lap ridge caps in the direction away from prevailing wind, and fix every 300 mm. At gable ends, use barge boards and a verge closure to prevent wind uplift a critical detail in cyclone-exposed regions such as Mozambique and Madagascar.</p>

<h2>6. Ventilation and Moisture Control</h2>
<p>Non-metal roofs are not immune to condensation. In humid tropical and highland climates, warm moist air rising from the building meets a cooler roof underside and condenses, staining ceilings and corroding fixings. Provide a balanced ventilation path: continuous soffit vents at the eave (minimum 25 mm clear opening per metre) and ridge or high-level vents at the ridge, giving a 1:300 to 1:150 net ventilation ratio to roof plan area.</p>

<p>Where a ceiling is fitted, lay a breather membrane (vapour-permeable) directly over the rafters or battens, with laps of 100 mm and taped joints. Do not use a low-permeability plastic sheet as underlay it traps moisture and defeats the ventilation strategy.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #c00;background:#fff0f0;padding:12px;margin:16px 0;">
<strong style="color:#c00;"> SAFETY WARNING</strong>
<p style="margin:6px 0 0 0;">Working at height on non-metal roofs is high-risk. Fibre cement sheets become brittle with age and can fail without warning; plastic sheets become slippery when wet or dusty. Always use edge protection, guardrails, or a fall-arrest harness anchored to a structural member never to the roofing sheet. Do not walk on unsupported spans or on sheets above 3 m without crawl boards. In hot climates, schedule work before 10:00 or after 15:00 to reduce heat stress, and ensure adequate drinking water on site. Follow national OSH regulations (e.g. Kenya OSHA 2007, South Africa OHS Act 85 of 1993, Nigeria Factories Act).</p>
</div>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>
<ol>
<li>Verify sheet pitch is above the minimum for the product (fibre cement 15, shingles 12, plastic 10).</li>
<li>Confirm battens are straight, treated, and spaced per manufacturer (typically 600900 mm).</li>
<li>Install underlay with 100 mm laps, taped, running horizontally and discharging over the eave.</li>
<li>Pre-drill fibre cement and use slotted/oversize holes for plastic to allow thermal movement.</li>
<li>Fix through crests on corrugated profiles; use EPDM or neoprene washers; do not over-tighten.</li>
<li>Maintain side and end laps per Table 1; stagger end laps to avoid continuous joints.</li>
<li>Fit eaves closures, ridge caps, and verge closures with compatible sealant; lap away from prevailing wind.</li>
<li>Provide continuous soffit and ridge ventilation at 1:300 to 1:150 of roof plan area; use a breather membrane.</li>
</ol>
