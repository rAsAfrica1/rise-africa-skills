Lessons

1. Splitting the carcass along the spine with a saw

After the pig has been bled, scalded, and scraped, and the internal organs removed, you are left with a whole carcass. To make handling easier and to begin primary cutting, you must split the carcass along the spine into two halves. This is done using a meat saw, also called a butcher's saw. A clean, sharp saw is essential. You will also need a sturdy table or hanging rail at about waist height. If you don't have a rail, lay the carcass on a clean, washed table. Position the carcass on its back or side, depending on your setup. Start at the tail end and saw through the pelvic bone, then continue up along the spine. Use long, steady strokes, letting the saw do the work. Do not force it; forcing can cause the saw to jump and injure you. As you cut, you will feel the resistance of the backbone. Keep the saw aligned with the centre of the spine. If you have a helper, one person can hold the carcass steady while the other saws. For a typical market-weight pig of 80100 kg, splitting takes about 510 minutes with a good saw. Once you reach the neck, the two halves will separate. If you plan to remove the head, do that before splitting or after, but many butchers remove the head first to reduce weight. After splitting, you have two sides (halves). Wipe the cut surfaces with a clean cloth to remove bone dust. Bone dust can spoil the meat's appearance and taste, so wipe it away. Now the halves are ready for further cutting into quarters or primals. Always work on a clean surface and wash your hands and tools with soap and hot water before and after. In many African settings, this step is done outdoors on a wooden table or a clean tarp. Ensure the area is free of dust and flies. If you are not processing immediately, move the halves to a cool room or refrigerator.

2. Halving and quartering for easier handling

Once the carcass is split into two sides, each side is still heavy and awkward to manage. Halving and quartering make the meat easier to handle, chill, and cut into primal cuts. A side from a 90 kg pig weighs about 45 kg. That is too heavy for one person to lift comfortably. You can further divide each side into two quarters: a forequarter (front) and a hindquarter (rear). The split between fore and hind is usually made between the 5th and 6th rib, or just behind the shoulder. To quarter, lay a side on a clean table. Using a sharp knife or a saw, cut through the ribs and spine at the chosen point. For a forequarter, you will cut through the shoulder area. For a hindquarter, you cut through the loin and belly. The exact cut point can vary, but a common method is to cut at the last rib. This gives a forequarter with the shoulder, belly, and some loin, and a hindquarter with the ham, loin, and belly. After cutting, you will have four pieces: two forequarters and two hindquarters. Each quarter weighs roughly 2025 kg, which one person can lift. Quartering also helps with chilling because smaller pieces cool faster. In hot African climates, rapid chilling is critical to prevent spoilage. If you don't have a chiller, quartering allows you to salt or smoke pieces more evenly. Use a clean saw or knife, and wipe the cut surfaces. Keep your tools sharp; a dull knife tears meat and creates a poor appearance. Always cut on a stable surface. If you are selling at a market, quartering allows you to display and price different sections. For example, a hindquarter with the ham may fetch a higher price per kg than a forequarter. Record the weight of each quarter if you are tracking yields. After quartering, move the pieces to a cool place or continue cutting into primal cuts.

3. Identifying primal cuts: shoulder, loin, belly, and ham

After quartering, you will break each quarter into primal cuts. The four main primal cuts of a pig are the shoulder, loin, belly, and ham. Knowing these cuts helps you price and sell meat according to local demand. Start with the shoulder. The shoulder is the front leg and adjacent area. It includes the picnic shoulder (lower part) and the Boston butt (upper part). Shoulder meat is well-marbled, flavourful, and often used for stews, mince, or roasting. In many African markets, shoulder is sold as bone-in or boneless chunks. Next, the loin. The loin runs along the back from the shoulder to the ham. It includes the rib end, centre loin, and sirloin. The loin is the source of chops, roasts, and back bacon. It is lean and tender. To identify the loin, look for the eye muscle along the spine. The belly is the underside of the pig, from the shoulder to the ham. It is fatty and used for bacon, pork belly, and lard. In African cooking, belly is often cut into strips for grilling or frying. Finally, the ham is the rear leg. It is the largest primal and is used for fresh roasts, curing, or making ham. The ham includes the leg and rump. To separate these cuts, you will use a knife and saw. For example, to remove the ham from the loin, cut through the spine at the hip joint. To separate the shoulder from the loin, cut between the 4th and 5th rib. Each primal can be further processed. For a 90 kg pig, typical yields are: shoulder about 20%, loin about 20%, belly about 15%, ham about 25%, and other parts (head, feet, offal) about 20%. Knowing these percentages helps you estimate how much meat you will have for sale. Always cut on a clean surface and keep your knives sharp. Label each primal if you are storing them.

4. Removing the head and preparing it for local dishes

The head of the pig is often removed before or after splitting the carcass. Removing the head reduces the weight of the carcass and provides valuable meat for local dishes. To remove the head, you will need a sharp knife and a meat saw. First, position the carcass on its back or side. Locate the joint between the skull and the first neck vertebra (atlas joint). Cut through the skin and flesh around the neck, then use the saw to cut through the bone at the joint. Twist the head to separate it. For a 90 kg pig, the head weighs about 57 kg. After removal, you can further process the head. Many African cultures use pig head for dishes like head cheese, stews, or soups. The cheeks, tongue, and brain are considered delicacies. To prepare, first singe any remaining hair with a blowtorch or over an open flame. Then wash the head thoroughly with warm water and scrub with a brush. You can split the head in half using a saw or cleaver to access the brain and tongue. Remove the tongue and set aside. The brain can be scooped out and used for dishes like scrambled eggs with brain or fried brain. The cheeks are often cured or cooked slowly. In West Africa, pig head is used in pepper soup; in Southern Africa, it may be boiled and served with pap. Ensure you clean the head well to remove any dirt or blood. Soak it in salt water for 30 minutes if desired. Then cook according to your recipe. If you are not using the head immediately, refrigerate or freeze it. The head should be chilled quickly to prevent spoilage. Always wash your hands and tools after handling raw meat. Properly prepared, the head adds value and reduces waste.

5. Chilling the carcass to firm up the meat

Chilling is a critical step after slaughter and cutting. It firms up the meat, making it easier to cut and improving its texture and shelf life. In hot African climates, meat can spoil within hours if not chilled. Ideally, the carcass should be chilled to below 4C as quickly as possible. If you have a walk-in chiller or refrigerator, place the halves or quarters on clean trays or hooks. Ensure there is space between pieces for air circulation. Do not stack meat; stacking slows cooling and can cause spoilage. If you don't have refrigeration, you can use alternative methods. One method is to hang the carcass in a cool, well-ventilated shed and use a fan to circulate air. You can also pack the meat in ice in a clean cooler box. For a 90 kg pig, you will need about 2030 kg of ice. Place a layer of ice at the bottom of the cooler, then a layer of meat, then more ice, and so on. Keep the cooler in the shade. Another method is to use a evaporative cooler (zeer pot) for smaller pieces. Chilling the carcass for 1224 hours is ideal. This allows rigor mortis to resolve, making the meat more tender. After chilling, the meat will be firm and easier to saw into chops or cuts. If you are selling fresh meat, chilling also improves appearance. Never leave meat at room temperature for more than 2 hours. Bacteria multiply rapidly above 4C. Use a thermometer to check the temperature if possible. After chilling, you can proceed to weigh and package the meat. Remember to maintain hygiene: wash hands, clean surfaces, and keep insects away. In many African markets, meat is sold fresh, but chilling is still important for quality.

6. Weighing and recording yields for pricing

Weighing and recording yields is essential for pricing your pork accurately and for managing your business. After chilling, weigh each primal cut and any other parts (head, feet, offal). Use a reliable scale, preferably a digital hanging scale or platform scale. For a 90 kg live pig, the dressed carcass weight (after removing head, organs, and blood) is typically about 7075% of live weight, so around 6368 kg. After splitting and quartering, you will have various cuts. Weigh each cut separately. For example, a hindquarter might weigh 1518 kg, a forequarter 1215 kg, loin 810 kg, belly 68 kg, and shoulder 1012 kg. Record these weights in a notebook or a simple spreadsheet. Also record the weight of the head, feet, and offal. This data helps you calculate your yield percentage. For example, if you sell the loin for 50 units per kg, you can calculate expected revenue. Pricing should cover your costs: feed, labour, slaughter, and overheads. In many African markets, pork is sold per kg, but sometimes per piece. Knowing the weight of each cut allows you to set a fair price. For example, if the total cost of raising the pig was 500 units, and you have 65 kg of saleable meat, your cost per kg is about 7.7 units. You then add your profit margin. Record sales as well, so you know which cuts sell best. This helps you plan future pig production. Always keep records for at least a year. Use a simple book or a mobile app. If you are selling at a market, a small notebook is fine. Accurate records also help you compare yields from different pigs or feeds. Over time, you can improve efficiency. Finally, ensure your scale is calibrated and clean. Weigh meat on a clean tray or paper. After weighing, package and label the meat with weight and price. This builds trust with customers.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Splitting the Carcass and Primary Cutting

In an African pig-processing enterprise, the way you split and break down a carcass determines your yield, your product range, and ultimately your margin. A clean, well-executed primary cut gives you consistent primal cuts that buyers trust, reduces bone dust and bruising, and lets you price each part of the animal according to local demand from the head that becomes a prized roadside delicacy to the ham that commands a premium in urban butcheries. This module walks you through the practical steps: splitting along the spine, halving and quartering, identifying the four primal cuts, removing and preparing the head, chilling for firmness, and recording yields for pricing. The business logic is simple: every kilogram you recover and correctly classify is money in the bank.

1. Setting Up Before You Cut

Primary cutting should begin as soon as possible after bleeding, scalding, and evisceration, while the carcass is still warm warm meat cuts more cleanly and the spine is easier to split. You need a firm, clean cutting table or gambrel hoist, a bone saw (manual or electric), a sharp breaking knife, a steeling rod, a meat hook, a clean food-grade crate, and a calibrated scale. In many African abattoirs and small butchers, a manual bone saw and a well-honed knife are perfectly adequate; what matters is hygiene and consistency, not expensive equipment.

Work on a surface that is at least waist height. Hang the carcass by the Achilles tendons on a gambrel so it is stable. Have a bucket of potable water and a clean cloth for wiping the saw between cuts. Keep a record sheet and pen within reach you will weigh and note each primal as you go.

2. Splitting the Carcass Along the Spine

The first major operation is splitting the carcass lengthwise down the spinal column into two sides (halves). Start at the pelvic end: locate the aitch bone and the sacrum, and make a guiding cut with the knife to expose the vertebrae. Then take the bone saw and cut steadily down the spine, following the natural line of the backbone. Keep the saw vertical and let it do the work; forcing it creates bone dust and ragged edges that spoil appearance and shorten shelf life.

As you approach the neck, slow down and finish with the knife to avoid cutting into the shoulder muscle. The aim is a symmetrical split equal left and right sides because unequal halves are harder to quarter and price fairly. A good split opens the spinal canal cleanly; a poor one leaves bone fragments that must be trimmed away, which is lost weight and lost money.

3. Halving and Quartering for Easier Handling

Once split, each side is a half-carcass. Halving makes the load manageable for one or two people and lets you work methodically through the primals. To quarter, cut each half between the 10th and 11th rib (or at the last rib, depending on your market's convention) into a forequarter (shoulder, neck, head) and a hindquarter (loin, belly, ham). In small African butcheries, quartering is often done to match demand: forequarters for stewing and minced products, hindquarters for grilling and curing.

Handle quarters with a hook through the aitch bone or by gripping the shank. Never drag meat across the floor or a dirty surface. If you are processing several pigs, quarter one side at a time and keep the rest covered and cool.

4. Identifying the Primal Cuts

The four primals shoulder, loin, belly, and ham are the backbone of your product mix. Learn their boundaries by feel and by landmark bones, not by guesswork.

Beyond the four primals, you recover the head, hocks, trotters, tail, and offal. In many African markets these are not "waste" they are high-demand, high-margin items. The head in particular is a cultural staple in dishes such as isitshwala accompaniments, head cheese, and braised head stews across Southern and East Africa.

5. Removing the Head and Preparing It for Local Dishes

Remove the head early, ideally before splitting or immediately after, using a knife and saw at the atlas joint (between the skull and the first neck vertebra). A clean cut at the joint preserves the cheek meat and tongue, which are the most valuable parts. Singe any remaining hair over an open flame or with a blowtorch, then scrape and wash thoroughly with potable water. Split the skull if your market wants brain and cheek separate, or leave whole for traditional preparation.

In African cuisine, the head is often boiled, then the meat is picked and fried or stewed with onions, peppers, and spices. Tongue may be boiled and sliced; brains are sometimes fried. Prepare the head promptly or chill it it is highly perishable. Because of its strong local demand, the head should be weighed and priced separately, not lumped into the carcass weight.

6. Chilling the Carcass to Firm Up the Meat

After splitting and primary cutting, chill the meat rapidly to below 7 C (ideally 04 C) within a few hours. Cold firms the muscle, makes further cutting and deboning easier, improves slicing quality, and slows bacterial growth. In settings without reliable grid power, use a well-insulated chill room, an evaporative cooler, or a chest freezer with ice packs, and monitor temperature with a simple thermometer. Never leave warm carcasses stacked; space them for airflow. Chill primals separately and cover them to prevent drying and cross-contamination.

7. Weighing and Recording Yields for Pricing

Weigh every primal, the head, and the offal. Record live weight, hot carcass weight, and each cut weight. This gives you your dressing percentage and cut yield the numbers you need to price profitably. If you do not know your yield, you cannot know your true cost per kilogram.

Use your yield data to set prices. If your total cost (pig purchase + feed + labour + transport + processing + chilling) is known, divide by the saleable kilograms to get your break-even per kilogram, then add your target margin. Price premium cuts (loin, ham) higher and move volume cuts (shoulder, belly, head, offal) at competitive prices to clear stock quickly. Keep a simple ledger or a phone spreadsheet; consistency in recording is what turns a butcher into a business.

SAFETY WARNING Bone Saw and Knife Hazards

Bone saws and breaking knives cause serious cuts. Always cut away from your body, never toward your hand. Wear a cut-resistant glove on the non-cutting hand and non-slip boots. Keep the saw blade sharp and the work surface dry. Never use a saw with a cracked handle or a loose blade. When splitting the spine, ensure the carcass is securely hung a falling half-carcass can crush a foot or leg. Keep children and untrained workers away from the cutting area. Wash hands and tools with potable water and disinfectant between carcasses to prevent cross-contamination.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Set up a clean, stable cutting station with a sharp bone saw, breaking knife, steel, hook, and calibrated scale.
  • Split the warm carcass lengthwise down the spine, keeping the saw vertical and the cut symmetrical.
  • Halve and then quarter at the last rib; handle quarters with a hook, never drag meat on the floor.
  • Identify and separate the four primals shoulder, loin, belly, ham by bone landmarks.
  • Remove the head at the atlas joint, singe, scrape, wash, and prepare it for local dishes; weigh separately.
  • Chill all meat to below 7 C (ideally 04 C) quickly; cover and space for airflow.
  • Weigh every primal, the head, and offal; record live weight, carcass weight, and cut yields.
  • Calculate break-even per kilogram and set prices by cut, using your yield record to protect margin.
