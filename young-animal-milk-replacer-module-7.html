1. Why Kid and Lamb Milk Replacers Differ from Calf Replacers

Kid and lamb milk replacers are not interchangeable with calf milk replacer, and understanding the biological reasons prevents some of the most expensive mistakes in small ruminant rearing. Goat kids and lambs are born with a functional esophageal groove reflex that directs milk straight into the abomasum, bypassing the rumen. This reflex is triggered by suckling and by the temperature and composition of the liquid. If you feed cold or incorrectly mixed replacer, the groove may not close properly, and milk enters the rumen where it ferments and causes bloat, acidosis, and often death within 24 to 48 hours. Calf replacers are formulated for a different digestive physiology and often contain higher levels of lactose and different protein sources. Feeding calf replacer to kids or lambs is a common mistake in East Africa, particularly in Kenya and Uganda where dairy calf replacer is widely available but small ruminant replacer is not. The correct product should contain 20 to 24 percent crude protein, 18 to 25 percent fat, and less than 0.1 percent fiber. Denkavit, a Dutch company with distribution across Africa, produces specific lamb and kid milk replacers under their Denkamilk range. In South Africa, suppliers such as Afgri and Meadow Feeds stock lamb replacer at approximately ZAR 45 to 60 per kilogram in 2024. In Kenya, imported kid replacer through distributors in Nakuru and Nairobi costs KES 800 to 1,200 per kilogram, while local repackaged products may cost KES 500 to 700 per kilogram but vary widely in quality. In Nigeria, small ruminant milk replacer is scarce, and farmers in Oyo and Kaduna states often use evaporated milk or cow milk powder, which is not ideal but can work if fortified correctly. FAO's 2019 manual on small ruminant rearing notes that orphaned or rejected kids and lambs should receive colostrum within the first 6 hours of life, ideally 50 milliliters per kilogram of body weight in the first 6 hours, then 50 milliliters per kilogram every 6 hours for the first 24 hours. Only after 24 to 36 hours should milk replacer be introduced. The transition must be gradual: day one of replacer feeding should be 25 percent replacer and 75 percent maternal milk or colostrum if available, moving to full replacer by day three. Temperature is critical. Feed at 38 to 39 degrees Celsius, never above 40 or below 35. A simple thermometer costs KES 200 in Nairobi and prevents scalding of the abomasum or failure of the esophageal groove reflex. Mixing rates vary by brand but typically 150 to 200 grams of powder per liter of clean water at 45 to 50 degrees Celsius for mixing, then cooled to 38 to 39 degrees for feeding. Never mix with hot water above 55 degrees as this denatures proteins and destroys added vitamins. Weigh the powder, do not scoop by volume, because density varies and underfeeding or overfeeding causes scours or poor growth. A kid or lamb should receive 10 to 15 percent of body weight in reconstituted milk per day, split into 3 to 4 feedings for the first two weeks, then 2 to 3 feedings. For a 4 kilogram newborn lamb, that is 400 to 600 milliliters per day, or roughly 100 to 150 milliliters per feeding. By week four, increase to 20 percent of body weight, so an 8 kilogram lamb receives 1.6 liters per day. Teagasc in Ireland recommends feeding lambs at 20 percent of body weight from week two onward when using automatic feeders, but manual feeding should stay at 15 to 18 percent to avoid overfeeding. Overfeeding is the most common cause of nutritional scours in hand-reared lambs and kids. If scours occur, reduce volume by 25 percent for 24 hours and feed an electrolyte solution such as Diakur or Lectade at 50 milliliters per kilogram per day, divided into 4 feedings. Do not feed milk and electrolytes together; alternate them. Cleanliness is non-negotiable. All feeding equipment must be washed with hot water and detergent after every feeding and sanitized with a chlorine solution at 200 parts per million. In Africa, where water may be scarce, this is often skipped, leading to bacterial scours and high mortality. Royal Canin's veterinary nutrition guidelines for young animals emphasize that bacterial contamination of milk replacer is a leading cause of neonatal mortality in hand-reared animals worldwide. Store powder in a cool, dry place, use within 30 days of opening, and never use moldy or caked powder. Finally, introduce solid feed from day 7 to 10. A creep feed of 18 to 20 percent crude protein, with 2 to 3 millimeter pellets, should be available free choice. This stimulates rumen development and allows weaning at 6 to 8 weeks when the lamb or kid consumes 200 grams of creep feed per day. The National Academies Press (NAP) 2007 Nutrient Requirements of Small Ruminants states that early rumen development is accelerated by offering palatable solid feed from the first week, and that milk replacer alone cannot support normal rumen papillae growth after week three. Ignoring this leads to stunted rumens and poor post-weaning growth, a problem seen frequently in Kenyan and Nigerian smallholder flocks.

2. Mixing, Temperature, and Feeding Equipment for Kids and Lambs

Correct mixing and feeding technique determines whether your milk replacer investment produces healthy kids and lambs or a pile of dead animals. The first principle is water quality. Use clean, fresh water that is safe for human consumption. In many parts of Africa, borehole water may have high mineral content or bacterial contamination. If you would not drink it, do not mix milk replacer with it. Boil water if necessary, then cool it to 45 to 50 degrees Celsius before adding powder. This temperature dissolves powder fully without denaturing proteins. Stir with a clean whisk or fork for 30 to 60 seconds until no lumps remain. Lumps can cause abomasal blockages and are a common cause of death in hand-reared lambs. Let the mixture stand for 2 to 3 minutes, then check temperature with a thermometer. It must be 38 to 39 degrees Celsius at feeding. If it is above 40, cool it by placing the container in cold water. If below 35, reheat gently, but never in a microwave because hot spots can scald. A digital thermometer costs KES 300 to 500 in Nairobi or NGN 2,000 to 3,500 in Lagos, and it is the single most important tool after the milk replacer itself. Mixing rates are specific to each product. Denkavit lamb milk replacer, for example, is mixed at 150 grams per liter for standard feeding, or 200 grams per liter for intensive feeding programs. MaxCare in New Zealand recommends 160 grams per liter for their lamb replacer. Always read the label. If the label says 150 grams per liter and you use 200 grams, you will cause nutritional scours. If you use 100 grams, the lamb will be hungry and stunted. Weigh the powder on a kitchen scale accurate to 1 gram. Volume scoops are unreliable because powder density changes with humidity, which is high in coastal Kenya and Nigeria. For a 4 kilogram lamb, day one feeding should be 50 milliliters per kilogram, so 200 milliliters total, divided into 4 feedings of 50 milliliters. Increase by 10 milliliters per feeding per day until day 7, when the lamb should receive 150 milliliters per feeding, 4 times daily, totaling 600 milliliters. By day 14, a 6 kilogram lamb should receive 200 milliliters per feeding, 3 times daily, totaling 600 milliliters, which is 10 percent of body weight. From day 21, increase to 15 percent of body weight, and from day 28 to weaning, 18 to 20 percent. Teagasc recommends that lambs on automatic feeders receive 20 to 25 percent of body weight, but manual feeders should not exceed 20 percent because of the risk of overfeeding and scours. Feeding equipment must be dedicated to young stock and cleaned thoroughly. Bottles with lamb or kid teats are best for small numbers. Teats should be soft and have a small hole so the animal sucks rather than gulps. Gulping leads to milk entering the rumen and causing bloat. For larger flocks, bucket feeders with multiple teats are available. These must be cleaned daily. A 5 liter bucket feeder with 6 teats costs approximately KES 3,500 in Kenya or ZAR 450 in South Africa. Automatic feeders, such as those from Denkavit or Forster-Technik, cost ZAR 15,000 to 30,000 and are only economical for flocks above 50 lambs. In Nigeria, where equipment is scarce, farmers often use clean plastic bottles with rubber teats from calf feeding kits, which works if the hole is small enough. Cleaning protocol: after each feeding, rinse all equipment with lukewarm water to remove milk residue. Then wash with hot water and dish detergent. Then sanitize with a chlorine solution made by adding 20 milliliters of household bleach (3.5 percent sodium hypochlorite) to 1 liter of water, giving approximately 200 parts per million chlorine. Soak for 2 minutes, then rinse with clean water and air dry. Do not stack wet equipment. In areas with limited water, use a three-bucket system: wash, rinse, sanitize. Reuse the wash water for the first rinse of the next batch, but never reuse sanitizer. Royal Canin's veterinary guidelines state that bacterial contamination of feeding equipment is a leading cause of neonatal diarrhea in hand-reared animals, and that chlorine sanitization reduces bacterial load by over 99 percent when done correctly. Common mistakes in Africa include mixing milk replacer with cold water and feeding it cold, which causes abomasal stasis and bloat; using too little powder to save money, which causes starvation and stunted growth; feeding from a shared bucket without cleaning between feedings, which spreads disease; and leaving mixed milk replacer standing for hours in the heat, which allows bacteria to multiply rapidly. Mixed milk replacer should be fed within 30 minutes of mixing. If you must store it, refrigerate at 4 degrees Celsius for no more than 12 hours, then reheat to 38 to 39 degrees Celsius. Never leave it at ambient temperature for more than 1 hour. In hot African climates, bacterial growth is rapid. A study by the University of Nairobi in 2018 found that milk replacer left at 28 degrees Celsius for 4 hours had bacterial counts exceeding 10 million colony forming units per milliliter, which is enough to cause severe scours. Finally, feed at consistent times. Kids and lambs are creatures of habit. Feed at 6 am, 12 pm, 6 pm, and 10 pm for the first week, then drop the 10 pm feeding. By week three, feed at 7 am, 1 pm, and 7 pm. Sudden changes in schedule or volume cause stress and scours. If you must change the schedule, do so gradually over 3 days. The same applies to changing milk replacer brands: mix the old and new 50:50 for 3 days before switching completely. This prevents digestive upset and keeps growth on track.

3. Feeding Programs from Birth to Weaning for Kids and Lambs

A structured feeding program from birth to weaning is essential for healthy growth and cost control. The program has four phases: colostrum, transition, full milk replacer, and weaning. Each phase has specific volumes, frequencies, and targets. Phase 1: Colostrum, day 0 to 2. The kid or lamb must receive colostrum within the first 6 hours of life. If the dam is alive and willing, allow natural suckling. If not, hand-feed colostrum at 50 milliliters per kilogram of body weight in the first 6 hours, then 50 milliliters per kilogram every 6 hours for the first 24 hours. For a 4 kilogram lamb, that is 200 milliliters per feeding, 4 times in 24 hours. Colostrum can be from the dam, from another freshly kidded or lambed female, or from a frozen colostrum bank. Cow colostrum can be used if small ruminant colostrum is unavailable, but it must be from a Johne's disease-free herd and fed at the same volume. Do not feed milk replacer during this phase. FAO's 2019 manual on small ruminant rearing states that failure to receive adequate colostrum in the first 6 hours is the single largest risk factor for neonatal mortality in hand-reared kids and lambs. Phase 2: Transition, day 3 to 7. Introduce milk replacer gradually. Day 3: 25 percent replacer, 75 percent colostrum or maternal milk. Day 4: 50:50. Day 5: 75 percent replacer, 25 percent milk. Day 6: 100 percent replacer. Feed 4 times daily. Volume: 50 milliliters per kilogram per day on day 3, increasing to 80 milliliters per kilogram per day by day 7. For a 4 kilogram lamb, that is 200 milliliters on day 3, rising to 320 milliliters on day 7, divided into 4 feedings. Always feed at 38 to 39 degrees Celsius. Phase 3: Full milk replacer, week 2 to 6. This is the main growth phase. Feed 3 to 4 times daily. Volume: 10 percent of body weight in week 2, 12 percent in week 3, 15 percent in week 4, 18 percent in week 5, and 20 percent in week 6. For a lamb growing from 6 kilograms in week 2 to 12 kilograms in week 6, that means 600 milliliters per day in week 2, rising to 2.4 liters per day in week 6. Split into 3 feedings from week 3 onward. Teagasc recommends that lambs on manual feeding should not exceed 20 percent of body weight to avoid nutritional scours. If you are feeding a high-fat replacer (25 percent fat), you can feed at the lower end of the range. If feeding a lower-fat replacer (18 percent fat), feed at the higher end. Weigh the lamb weekly to adjust volumes. A hanging scale costs KES 2,500 in Kenya or NGN 15,000 in Nigeria. Without weighing, you will either underfeed or overfeed. Phase 4: Weaning, week 6 to 8. Weaning should begin when the lamb or kid consumes 200 grams of creep feed per day for 3 consecutive days. This usually happens at 6 to 8 weeks of age. Start by reducing milk replacer volume by 25 percent in the first week of weaning, then 50 percent in the second week, then stop. Do not stop abruptly. Abrupt weaning causes stress, weight loss, and sometimes death. During weaning, provide unlimited creep feed and fresh water. Creep feed should be 18 to 20 percent crude protein, with 2 to 3 millimeter pellets. In Kenya, a 50 kilogram bag of lamb creep feed costs KES 3,000 to 3,800. In Nigeria, it costs NGN 25,000 to 35,000. In South Africa, it costs ZAR 450 to 600. If commercial creep feed is unavailable, mix your own: 40 percent maize meal, 30 percent soybean meal, 20 percent wheat bran, 8 percent molasses, 1 percent mineral premix, and 1 percent salt. This mix should be 18 percent protein. The National Academies Press (NAP) 2007 Nutrient Requirements of Small Ruminants states that lambs and kids should be consuming 200 grams of solid feed per day before weaning to ensure rumen development is sufficient to support growth on forage and concentrate alone. Common mistakes in Africa include weaning too early because milk replacer is expensive, which leads to stunted growth and high mortality; weaning too late because the farmer is afraid to stop, which increases costs and delays rumen development; and failing to provide creep feed from week one, which means the rumen never develops properly. A study by the International Livestock Research Institute (ILRI) in 2016 found that Kenyan farmers who weaned lambs at 8 weeks with adequate creep feed had 30 percent lower mortality and 25 percent higher weights at 6 months compared to farmers who weaned at 4 weeks without creep feed. Cost analysis: For a 4 kilogram lamb raised to 12 kilograms at 8 weeks, total milk replacer consumption is approximately 12 to 15 kilograms of powder. At KES 800 per kilogram, that is KES 9,600 to 12,000 per lamb. In South Africa, at ZAR 50 per kilogram, it is ZAR 600 to 750. In Nigeria, at NGN 8,000 per kilogram for imported product, it is NGN 96,000 to 120,000. This is expensive, which is why milk replacer is best used for orphaned, rejected, or triplet lambs, not for all lambs. For healthy singles and twins with good dams, natural rearing is cheaper and often better. Use milk replacer strategically, not universally. Finally, keep records. Record daily volume fed, weekly weight, any scours or illness, and feed costs. Without records, you cannot know if your program is working. A simple notebook costs KES 100 and will save you thousands by identifying problems early.

4. Health, Disease, and Troubleshooting in Replacer-Fed Kids and Lambs

Health problems in replacer-fed kids and lambs are common but largely preventable. The three main categories are nutritional scours, infectious scours, and bloat. Each has different causes and treatments. Nutritional scours occur when the feeding program is wrong: too much volume, too high concentration, inconsistent feeding times, or cold milk. Symptoms include loose, pale yellow or white feces, often with a sour smell, and the animal remains alert and suckles strongly. Treatment: reduce milk replacer volume by 25 percent for 24 hours, feed electrolytes at 50 milliliters per kilogram per day divided into 4 feedings, and maintain warmth. Do not feed milk and electrolytes together; alternate them every 2 hours. If scours persist beyond 24 hours, consult a veterinarian. Teagasc recommends using an electrolyte product such as Diakur or Lectade, which contains sodium, potassium, chloride, and glucose. In Kenya, Diakur costs KES 1,200 per 5-liter pack. In Nigeria, Lectade costs NGN 8,000 per 5-liter pack. Infectious scours are caused by bacteria such as E. coli, Salmonella, or Cryptosporidium, or by viruses such as rotavirus. Symptoms include watery, foul-smelling feces, often with blood or mucus, depression, refusal to suckle, and fever above 40 degrees Celsius. Treatment: isolate the animal immediately, provide electrolytes, and consult a veterinarian for antibiotics or antiparasitics. Prevention is through colostrum, cleanliness, and vaccination of the dam. In South Africa, a rotavirus vaccine for sheep costs ZAR 250 per dose and is given to the dam 6 weeks before lambing. In Kenya, it is less available but can be imported through veterinary suppliers in Nairobi. Bloat occurs when milk enters the rumen instead of the abomasum. This happens when the esophageal groove reflex fails, usually because milk is too cold (below 35 degrees Celsius), too hot (above 42 degrees Celsius), or fed too fast. Symptoms include a distended left abdomen, discomfort, kicking at the belly, and rapid breathing. Treatment: massage the abdomen, keep the animal upright, and administer 10 milliliters of vegetable oil orally to break down foam. If severe, a veterinarian may need to pass a stomach tube. Prevention: feed at 38 to 39 degrees Celsius, use teats with small holes, and feed at consistent times. Other health issues include navel ill (omphalitis), which is prevented by dipping the navel in iodine at birth; pneumonia, which is prevented by good ventilation and dry bedding; and coccidiosis, which is prevented by clean bedding and coccidiostat in feed from week 3. Royal Canin's veterinary guidelines emphasize that hygiene, colostrum, and correct feeding temperature prevent over 80 percent of health problems in hand-reared young animals. Common mistakes in Africa include treating scours by withholding all milk, which causes starvation and death; using human diarrhea medicine, which is ineffective and can be toxic; and waiting too long to call a veterinarian. If an animal is depressed, not suckling, or has a fever, call a veterinarian within 12 hours. In Kenya, veterinary services cost KES 2,000 to 5,000 per visit. In Nigeria, NGN 15,000 to 30,000. In South Africa, ZAR 500 to 1,000. The cost of losing a lamb is higher than the cost of a vet visit. Vaccination and deworming: kids and lambs should receive a clostridial vaccine at 4 weeks and again at 8 weeks. In South Africa, a 50-dose vial of Pulpy Kidney vaccine costs ZAR 300. In Kenya, it costs KES 2,500. Deworming should start at 6 weeks with a product such as albendazole at 5 milligrams per kilogram. In Nigeria, albendazole costs NGN 2,000 per 100 milliliters. Do not deworm before 6 weeks unless advised by a veterinarian, as it can be toxic to young animals. Finally, monitor growth. A healthy replacer-fed lamb should gain 150 to 250 grams per day. A kid should gain 100 to 200 grams per day. If growth is below 100 grams per day, review the feeding program, check for disease, and weigh the powder accurately. A study by Teagasc in 2020 found that lambs fed milk replacer at 20 percent of body weight gained 220 grams per day, while those fed at 12 percent gained only 140 grams per day. The difference was entirely due to feeding rate. In Africa, where growth targets are often lower, aim for at least 150 grams per day for lambs and 120 grams per day for kids. If you cannot achieve this, the problem is usually underfeeding, incorrect mixing, or disease. Address these systematically, and your animals will thrive.

5. Costs, Suppliers, and Practical Economics for African Smallholders

Milk replacer feeding is expensive, and African smallholders must understand the economics before committing. This lesson provides real costs from Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa, compares milk replacer to natural rearing, and offers strategies to reduce costs without compromising animal health. Cost of milk replacer powder: In Kenya, imported kid and lamb milk replacer from Denkavit or similar European brands costs KES 800 to 1,200 per kilogram through distributors in Nairobi, Nakuru, and Eldoret. Local repackaged products cost KES 500 to 700 per kilogram but vary in quality; some are simply calf replacer repackaged, which is unsuitable. In Nigeria, imported lamb milk replacer costs NGN 8,000 to 12,000 per kilogram in Lagos and Abuja. Local products are rare and often unreliable. In South Africa, lamb milk replacer from Afgri, Meadow Feeds, or Denkavit costs ZAR 45 to 60 per kilogram. In all three countries, buying in 20-kilogram bags reduces the cost by 10 to 15 percent compared to 1-kilogram packs. Cost of feeding one lamb from birth to weaning: A 4-kilogram lamb raised to 12 kilograms at 8 weeks consumes approximately 12 to 15 kilograms of milk replacer powder. In Kenya, at KES 800 per kilogram, that is KES 9,600 to 12,000. In Nigeria, at NGN 8,000 per kilogram, that is NGN 96,000 to 120,000. In South Africa, at ZAR 50 per kilogram, that is ZAR 600 to 750. Add creep feed: 10 to 15 kilograms per lamb, costing KES 600 to 900 in Kenya, NGN 5,000 to 7,500 in Nigeria, and ZAR 90 to 135 in South Africa. Add veterinary costs, equipment, and labor. Total cost per lamb: KES 11,000 to 14,000 in Kenya, NGN 110,000 to 140,000 in Nigeria, and ZAR 800 to 1,000 in South Africa. Market value of a 12-kilogram lamb: In Kenya, a 12-kilogram lamb sells for KES 8,000 to 12,000. In Nigeria, it sells for NGN 60,000 to 90,000. In South Africa, it sells for ZAR 1,200 to 1,800. This means that in Kenya and Nigeria, milk replacer feeding is often break-even or a loss unless the lamb is sold as a breeding animal or at a premium. In South Africa, it is profitable because of higher market prices and lower feed costs. This is why milk replacer should be used strategically: for orphaned lambs, rejected lambs, triplet lambs, or when the dam dies. It should not be used for all lambs. Natural rearing costs almost nothing beyond the dam's feed. A study by ILRI in 2016 found that Kenyan farmers who used milk replacer for all lambs lost money, while those who used it only for orphans and triplets made a small profit. Cost-reduction strategies: First, use colostrum from a bank. Collect colostrum from dams that produce excess, freeze it in 500-milliliter bottles, and use it for orphans. This reduces the amount of milk replacer needed in the first 2 days. Second, use a lower-cost local product if it meets specifications. Test the product by mixing 150 grams in 1 liter of water at 45 degrees Celsius. It should dissolve completely with no sediment. If it does not, it is likely low quality. Third, buy in bulk with other farmers. A 20-kilogram bag costs 10 to 15 percent less per kilogram than 1-kilogram packs. Fourth, wean at 6 weeks instead of 8 weeks if the lamb is consuming 200 grams of creep feed per day. This saves 2 weeks of milk replacer, about 3 to 4 kilograms of powder, saving KES 2,400 to 4,800 in Kenya or NGN 24,000 to 48,000 in Nigeria. Fifth, use a bucket feeder instead of bottles for groups of 5 or more lambs. A 5-liter bucket feeder with 6 teats costs KES 3,500 in Kenya or ZAR 450 in South Africa, and saves labor. Sixth, mix your own creep feed instead of buying commercial. A mix of 40 percent maize meal, 30 percent soybean meal, 20 percent wheat bran, 8 percent molasses, 1 percent mineral premix, and 1 percent salt costs KES 2,000 to 2,500 per 50 kilograms in Kenya, compared to KES 3,000 to 3,800 for commercial creep feed. In Nigeria, it costs NGN 20,000 to 25,000 per 50 kilograms, compared to NGN 25,000 to 35,000 for commercial. Suppliers: In Kenya, milk replacer is available from Cooper K-Brands, Unga Farm Care, and veterinary suppliers in Nairobi. In Nigeria, it is available from Grand Cereals, Animal Care Konsult, and importers in Lagos. In South Africa, it is available from Afgri, Meadow Feeds, and Denkavit distributors. MaxCare in New Zealand exports to some African countries through agents; contact them directly for bulk orders. FAO and ILRI provide training materials and sometimes subsidize milk replacer for development projects. Check with local extension officers. Common mistakes: buying calf replacer instead of lamb or kid replacer; buying the cheapest product without checking protein and fat content; using too little powder to save money, which causes starvation; feeding for too long, which increases costs without benefit; and failing to record costs, which means the farmer does not know if the enterprise is profitable. Keep records of all purchases, feedings, weights, and sales. A simple notebook and a calculator are enough. Finally, consider the opportunity cost. If a farmer spends KES 12,000 on milk replacer for one lamb, that money could buy 2 bags of maize or pay school fees. Milk replacer is only justified if the lamb would otherwise die or if the lamb is a valuable breeding animal. For healthy singles and twins with good dams, natural rearing is better and cheaper. Use milk replacer as a tool for specific problems, not as a routine. This is the key economic lesson for African smallholders.
