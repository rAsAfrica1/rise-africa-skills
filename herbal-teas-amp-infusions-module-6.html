1. Understanding Potential Allergies and Contraindications

Before you sell or even share your herbal teas, you must understand that natural does not always mean safe for everyone. Some herbs can cause allergic reactions or interact badly with medications or health conditions. For example, chamomile is related to ragweed, so people with ragweed allergies may react to chamomile tea with sneezing, itchy eyes, or even a rash. Similarly, hibiscus tea can lower blood pressure, so someone already on blood pressure medication could feel dizzy or faint. Pregnant women should avoid herbs like parsley, sage, and rosemary in large amounts because they can stimulate the uterus. People with kidney disease should avoid nettle and dandelion, which affect fluid balance.

Your job is to know these risks and warn your customers. Start by keeping a simple reference chart of the herbs you use and their known contraindications. For each herb, note who should avoid it: pregnant or breastfeeding women, children under 12, people with high or low blood pressure, diabetics, or those on blood thinners. For example, ginger is generally safe but in high doses can thin the blood; people on warfarin should limit it. Liquorice root can raise blood pressure and drop potassium levels, so it's unsafe for people with hypertension or heart disease.

When a customer buys your tea, ask if they have any health conditions or take medication. If they do, recommend a different blend or suggest they consult a doctor. Always include a warning label on your packaging: "Consult your healthcare provider before use if pregnant, nursing, or taking medication." For online sales, add a disclaimer on your website.

In Africa, where many people use both traditional herbs and modern medicine, interactions are common. For instance, someone taking antiretroviral therapy (ART) for HIV should avoid St. John's wort, which reduces the effectiveness of those drugs. Moringa, though nutritious, can interfere with thyroid medication. Be honest and cautious. If you are unsure about an herb's safety, do not use it. Your reputation depends on keeping customers safe.

Finally, test a small amount of any new tea on yourself before selling it. Wait 24 hours to see if you have any reaction. Keep a notebook of any customer complaints or side effects. This helps you improve and shows you care about safety.

2. Hygiene Practices for Handling and Processing Herbs

Cleanliness is the foundation of safe herbal products. Dirt, sweat, and germs can turn a healthy tea into a source of illness. You must treat your workspace like a small food factory. Start with yourself: wash your hands with soap and clean water for at least 20 seconds before touching any herbs. Scrub under your nails. If you have a cut, cover it with a waterproof bandage and wear gloves. Wear clean clothes and tie back long hair. Do not handle herbs if you have diarrhoea, vomiting, or a fever.

Your tools and surfaces must be just as clean. Use stainless steel or food-grade plastic bowls, spoons, and trays. Wooden tools can absorb moisture and harbour bacteria, so avoid them or scrub them thoroughly and dry in the sun. After each use, wash all tools with hot water and dish soap, then rinse with clean water. Once a week, sanitise with a bleach solution: mix 1 tablespoon of household bleach in 1 litre of water. Soak tools for 2 minutes, then rinse and air-dry. Do not use the same cloth for wiping surfaces and drying dishes.

Water quality matters. Use clean, safe water for washing herbs and for any processing. If your tap water is not safe to drink, boil it and let it cool before use. When rinsing fresh herbs, use a large basin and change the water often. For dried herbs, do not wash them after dryingâmoisture invites mould. Instead, clean them before drying by gently shaking off dirt or wiping with a damp cloth.

Your drying and packaging area should be free of pests. Keep floors swept, cover bins, and store herbs in sealed containers. Do not allow animals or children in the processing area. If you have a cough or cold, wear a mask.

Finally, train anyone who helps you. Show them these steps and check their work. A simple rule: if it looks dirty, it is dirty. Clean as you go. Good hygiene not only prevents sickness but also makes your tea taste better and last longer. Customers notice clean packaging and trust you more.

3. Avoiding Contamination During Drying and Packaging

Drying and packaging are the two steps where contamination most often happens. If you dry herbs on the ground or package them with dirty hands, all your earlier hygiene work is wasted. Start with drying. Never dry herbs directly on the ground, on newspapers, or on bare wood. Use clean drying screens, trays, or hanging bundles in a well-ventilated, shaded area. The ideal temperature for drying most herbs is between 35Â°C and 45Â°C. In hot African climates, you can use a solar dryerâa simple box with a glass top and vents. If drying outdoors, bring herbs in before evening dew or rain.

Turn herbs gently every day to ensure even drying. They are ready when leaves crumble easily and stems snap, not bend. This usually takes 3 to 7 days depending on the herb and weather. If you dry in an oven, set it to the lowest temperature (50Â°C) and leave the door slightly open. Never use a microwaveâit dries unevenly and can cause fires.

Once dry, move to packaging. Your packaging area must be clean and dry. Wash your hands again. Use food-grade packaging: sealed glass jars, zip-lock pouches, or vacuum-sealed bags. Avoid reusing old containers that held chemicals or spices, as residues can contaminate. Plastic bags must be food-grade and free of odours.

Weigh and fill packages quickly to minimise exposure to air and moisture. If you are packaging loose tea, use a clean scoop that is only for that purpose. Do not breathe or cough over open containers. Seal each package immediately. For long shelf life, add a small oxygen absorber or silica gel packet (clearly marked "do not eat") to absorb moisture.

Label each package with the date and batch number. Store in a cool, dark, dry place away from strong-smelling items like onions or kerosene. Inspect packages weekly for signs of moisture, mould, or insects. If you see clumping or a musty smell, discard the batch. Contamination can also come from pests: keep dried herbs in airtight containers and use traps or natural repellents like neem leaves.

Remember, a single mouldy batch can ruin your reputation. Always err on the side of caution.

4. Labeling Requirements: Ingredients, Warnings, and Expiry Dates

A good label protects your customer and your business. It tells people exactly what they are buying and how to use it safely. In most African countries, food and herbal product labeling is regulated, but even if you sell informally, you should follow these basic rules.

First, list all ingredients. Use common names that everyone understands. For example, write "hibiscus" not "Hibiscus sabdariffa." List ingredients in order of weight, from most to least. If you use a blend, name each herb. If you add flavouring like orange peel or ginger, list that too. If your tea contains caffeine (e.g., from green tea or yerba mate), state the amount per serving.

Second, include warnings. Every package should have: "Consult a healthcare provider before use if pregnant, nursing, taking medication, or have a medical condition." If your tea contains a known allergen (e.g., chamomile for ragweed allergy), state it clearly: "Contains chamomile. People with ragweed allergies should avoid." If an herb has a maximum daily dose, write it. For example, "Do not exceed 3 cups per day." For herbs like liquorice, add: "Not for people with high blood pressure."

Third, show the expiry date. Herbal teas lose potency and flavour over time. Dried herbs generally last 12 to 18 months if stored properly. Write "Best before:" followed by month and year (e.g., "Best before: Dec 2026"). Also include a batch number so you can trace it if there is a problem.

Fourth, give clear brewing instructions. For example: "Use 1 teaspoon per cup of boiling water. Steep 5 minutes. Strain and drink." This prevents overuse.

Finally, include your contact details: business name, phone number, and physical address. This builds trust and allows customers to reach you with questions or complaints.

You can print labels at a local print shop or write neatly with a permanent marker on sticker paper. Keep a copy of every label you use in a record book. If you change ingredients, update the label immediately. A clear, honest label is your best marketing tool.

5. Safe Dosage Guidelines for Common Herbs

More is not better when it comes to herbal teas. Too much of a good herb can cause side effects. This lesson gives you safe dosage guidelines for common herbs used in African herbal teas. Always start with the lowest dose and adjust. These guidelines are for healthy adults. Children, pregnant women, and people with health conditions need professional advice.

**Hibiscus**: 1 to 2 teaspoons of dried petals per cup of boiling water. Drink up to 3 cups daily. More than that can drop blood pressure too low.

**Ginger**: 1 to 2 slices of fresh root or Â½ teaspoon of dried powder per cup. Up to 3 cups daily. High doses (more than 4 grams per day) can cause heartburn or thin the blood.

**Chamomile**: 1 tablespoon of dried flowers per cup. Up to 3 cups daily. Avoid if allergic to ragweed.

**Peppermint**: 1 to 2 teaspoons of dried leaves per cup. Up to 4 cups daily. Avoid if you have acid reflux; it can worsen heartburn.

**Liquorice root**: Â½ to 1 teaspoon per cup. Do not drink more than 1 cup daily for more than 4 weeks. Can raise blood pressure and lower potassium.

**Moringa**: 1 teaspoon of dried leaf powder per cup. Up to 2 cups daily. High doses can cause stomach upset and may interfere with thyroid medication.

**Rooibos**: 1 to 2 teaspoons per cup. Up to 4 cups daily. Generally safe, but may cause headaches in some people.

**Nettle**: 1 to 2 teaspoons dried leaf per cup. Up to 3 cups daily. Avoid if on blood thinners or with kidney disease.

**Lemongrass**: 1 to 2 stalks or 1 teaspoon dried per cup. Up to 3 cups daily. May cause dizziness in large amounts.

**Fennel**: 1 teaspoon crushed seeds per cup. Up to 3 cups daily. Avoid in high doses if pregnant.

Always use a measuring spoon, not a random spoon from your kitchen. Write the dosage on your label. If a customer wants to drink more, tell them to consult a doctor. Keep a dosage chart at your stall or shop. Remember, these are general guidelines; individual needs vary. When in doubt, use less.

Finally, teach your customers to steep, not boil, delicate herbs like chamomile and peppermint. Boiling can make them bitter and more concentrated. Safe dosage is a sign of professionalism.

6. Record-Keeping for Safety and Traceability

Record-keeping may sound like paperwork, but it is your safety net. If a customer gets sick, you need to know exactly which batch they bought, what herbs were in it, and when it was made. Without records, you cannot fix the problem or prove your innocence. Good records also help you improve quality and comply with regulations.

Start with a simple notebook or a spreadsheet on your phone. For every batch of tea you make, record: the date, the batch number (e.g., 2025-03-15-HIB), the herbs used and their quantities, the source of each herb (your own garden, a local farmer, a market), the drying method and duration, the packaging date, and the expiry date. Also note who made it and any deviations from your normal process.

Keep records of your suppliers. For each herb you buy, write down the farmer's name, location, and contact. If a supplier's herb is contaminated, you can trace it back and warn others. Ask suppliers if they use pesticides and when they last sprayed. If possible, buy from organic-certified sources.

You also need customer records for traceability. When you sell, note the date, batch number, and customer contact (if they agree). If a problem arises, you can call them. For online sales, keep order records.

Store your records in a safe, dry place. Keep them for at least 2 years. If you use a phone, back up to the cloud or email yourself a copy.

Use your records to spot patterns. If a batch spoils quickly, check your drying recordsâmaybe it was humid that week. If a customer complains of a reaction, check the batch's ingredients.

Finally, keep a log of any customer complaints or side effects. Write down what happened, what you did, and how you resolved it. This shows you take safety seriously.

In many African countries, food safety inspectors may ask for records. Having them ready can save you from fines or closure. More importantly, records help you build a reputation for quality and trust. A simple notebook can be the difference between a small mistake and a ruined business.

Practical Reference: Health and Safety Standards for Herbal Products

This practical reference lesson consolidates the six core lessons in this module into a single, business-ready resource. It is written for African herbal tea and infusion enterprises â from smallholder cooperatives in Kenya and Uganda to urban processing units in Nigeria, Ghana, and South Africa â that need to meet health, safety, and regulatory expectations while remaining commercially viable. Every standard discussed here has a direct business payoff: fewer recalls, stronger buyer confidence, access to formal retail and export markets, and protection against legal liability.

Herbal products are regulated as food, medicine, or both, depending on the jurisdiction. In most African markets, herbal teas sold as beverages fall under food safety law, while products making therapeutic claims may be classified as complementary medicines. This distinction affects labeling, dosage guidance, and record-keeping obligations. The practical standards below apply regardless of classification and are drawn from World Health Organization (WHO) guidance, Codex Alimentarius, and national food safety authorities.

1. Allergies and Contraindications: Managing Risk Before It Reaches the Customer

Herbal teas are not inherently risk-free. Common African herbal ingredients â rooibos, hibiscus, moringa, lemongrass, ginger, and Sutherlandia â can trigger allergic reactions or interact with medication. Hibiscus, for example, may lower blood pressure and interact with antihypertensives. Moringa leaves contain compounds that may affect thyroid function when consumed in very large quantities. Sutherlandia (cancer bush) is contraindicated in pregnancy and for people on antiretroviral therapy.

For a business, the practical response is threefold: (a) screen your ingredient list against known allergen and contraindication databases; (b) print clear warnings on every package; and (c) train staff to answer customer questions honestly rather than making medical claims. Never market a product as a cure. In most African jurisdictions, therapeutic claims trigger medicine registration requirements that a small tea business is not equipped to meet.

2. Hygiene, Drying, and Packaging: The Contamination Control Chain

Contamination in herbal products usually enters at one of four points: dirty hands and tools during harvest and handling; unsafe water used for washing; inadequate drying that allows mould and aflatoxin development; and poor packaging that permits moisture, pests, or chemical migration. Each point is controllable with low-cost practices.

Handling and processing: workers must wash hands with soap and potable water, wear clean outer garments, and avoid handling herbs when ill. Tools and drying racks should be food-grade or stainless steel, cleaned between batches. Water used for washing must meet drinking-water standards â in many rural African settings this means boiling, filtering, or using a verified borehole source.

Drying: target a moisture content below 12% for dried leaf and flower material. Sun-drying is acceptable in most of Africa but must be raised off the ground, covered with mesh to exclude insects and dust, and brought in before evening dew. Solar dryers give more consistent results and reduce contamination. Never dry herbs near livestock, fuel, or chemicals.

Packaging: use food-grade, moisture-barrier materials â laminated foil, metallised polyester, or high-density polyethylene. Seal immediately after filling. Label each unit with batch number and date. Store finished goods off the floor, away from walls, and away from strong-smelling substances such as spices or detergents.

Improperly dried or stored herbs â especially in humid tropical and coastal regions â can develop aflatoxins, which are carcinogenic and can cause acute liver damage. Aflatoxin contamination is invisible, odourless, and not destroyed by boiling. If dried herbs show mould, mustiness, or discolouration, discard the entire batch. Never blend suspect material into a saleable product. Test finished batches where a laboratory service is available, and keep drying and storage areas below 12% moisture and below 65% relative humidity.

3. Labeling Requirements: Ingredients, Warnings, and Expiry Dates

Labeling is both a legal obligation and a marketing asset. A compliant label signals professionalism to buyers, retailers, and export partners. At minimum, every herbal tea package sold in African markets should carry the following information in the language(s) required by the national regulator:

  • Product name and net weight
  • Full ingredient list in descending order of weight
  • Allergen and contraindication warnings (see Table 1)
  • Batch or lot number
  • Manufacture date and expiry or best-before date
  • Storage instructions
  • Name and physical address of the manufacturer or packer
  • Country of origin
  • Preparation instructions (e.g., 1 teaspoon per cup, steep 5 minutes)

Expiry dates for dried herbal teas typically range from 12 to 24 months from packing, depending on moisture content and packaging. Set your shelf life based on real stability data, not guesswork. If you cannot test, use a conservative 12-month date and state "best before" rather than "expiry."

5. Record-Keeping for Safety and Traceability

Traceability is the backbone of food safety compliance and export readiness. If a batch is recalled, you must be able to identify which raw material lots went into it, who processed it, and where it was sold. Keep the following records for a minimum of two years (longer if required by your national regulator):

  • Supplier records: name, contact, lot number, date received, quantity
  • Processing records: date, batch code, operator, drying conditions, moisture readings
  • Packaging records: packaging lot, quantity packed, date, operator
  • Distribution records: buyer name, quantity, date, batch codes supplied
  • Complaint and incident records: date, nature, batch, action taken
  • Cleaning and pest-control logs

Paper records are acceptable, but a simple spreadsheet or mobile app reduces errors and speeds up traceability. Whichever system you use, back it up. A traceability exercise that takes more than four hours to complete is a sign your records need improvement.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Ingredient safety screen done? Every herb checked against allergen and contraindication lists.
  2. Hands, tools, and surfaces clean? Soap, potable water, clean garments, no ill workers handling product.
  3. Moisture below 12%? Drying verified before packaging; no mould, mustiness, or discolouration.
  4. Food-grade packaging sealed? Moisture-barrier material, sealed immediately, batch-coded.
  5. Label complete? Ingredients, warnings, batch number, dates, storage, manufacturer address.
  6. Dosage guidance printed? Clear preparation instructions and maximum daily use stated.
  7. Records up to date? Supplier, processing, packaging, and distribution logs current and backed up.
  8. Recall plan ready? You can trace any batch from raw material to customer within four hours.
