1. Preparing the Opening: Cleaning and Repairing

Before you install any aluminum window or door, the opening must be clean, square, and structurally sound. Start by removing all debris: old mortar, wood chunks, nails, and dust. Use a wire brush and a hammer to chip away loose material. For concrete or block walls, check for cracks wider than 2mm. Fill these with a non-shrink repair mortar, such as SikaRep or a 1:3 cement-sand mix with a bonding agent. Let it cure for at least 24 hours. If the opening is out of square by more than 5mm, you must correct it. Measure diagonals: they should be equal within 3mm. If not, grind or chip high spots, or apply a leveling render. For brick walls, replace any broken bricks. For wood frame openings, check for rot; replace soft wood with treated timber. Ensure the sill is sloped outward at least 5 degrees to shed water. In hot climates, this prevents water pooling that can lead to mold. Also, check that the opening is the correct size: the frame should fit with a 10-15mm gap all around for expansion and sealing. If the gap is too small, the frame may buckle in heat; if too large, sealing becomes difficult. Clean the surfaces where sealant will bond: they must be free of oil, dust, and loose particles. Use a vacuum or a damp cloth. Finally, prime porous surfaces like concrete and brick with a suitable primer to improve adhesion. This preparation step is criticalpoor preparation leads to leaks, drafts, and callbacks. Spend the time now to save hours later.

2. Setting Frames Level and Plumb

Setting frames level and plumb is the most important step for smooth operation and weathertightness. You need a spirit level (at least 600mm long), a plumb bob or laser level, and packers (plastic shims). Start by placing the frame into the opening. Use wooden wedges or plastic packers at the corners and at the midpoint of each side. Do not use stones or wood scraps that can rot. Check level on the sill: place the level on the bottom frame. Adjust packers until the bubble is centered. Then check plumb on each side: hold the level vertically against the jamb. Adjust packers until plumb. Check the head (top) for level. Now check the diagonals: measure from top-left corner to bottom-right, then top-right to bottom-left. They must be equal within 2mm. If not, adjust packers until they match. This ensures the frame is square. For doors, also check that the threshold is level and that the frame is not twisted. Use a string line across the face of the frame to check for twist. In hot climates, aluminum expands; leave a 5mm gap at the top and sides for expansion. Do not over-pack: packers should be snug but not forcing the frame out of shape. Once level and plumb, secure the frame temporarily with screws or nails through the packers into the wall. Re-check all measurements. A common mistake is to fix the frame before checking diagonals. Always check and re-check. If the frame is not level and plumb, the windows will not close properly, and water will leak. Take your timethis step sets the foundation for the entire installation.

3. Fixing Frames to Block, Brick, and Concrete Walls

The method of fixing depends on the wall material. For block and brick walls, use masonry screws or expansion anchors. Drill holes through the frame into the wall using a hammer drill and a bit slightly smaller than the screw. For concrete, use concrete anchors or wedge bolts. Space fixings at maximum 600mm apart, and within 150mm of each corner. For windows, fix through the sides and top; for doors, also fix through the bottom (threshold) if it is a fixed frame. Do not fix through the drainage holes or the weatherstripping. Use stainless steel screws to avoid rust. Pre-drill the aluminum frame to prevent cracking. For brick, avoid fixing into mortar joints; fix into the brick itself. For block, if the block is hollow, use toggle bolts or chemical anchors. In hot climates, use plastic isolation tape between the aluminum frame and steel or concrete to prevent galvanic corrosion. After fixing, check that the frame is still level and plumb. If you overtighten, you can distort the frame. Tighten screws snugly, then check operation of the window or door. If it binds, loosen and adjust. For large openings, fix the mullions (vertical dividers) securely. Always follow the manufacturer's instructions for fixing positions. A common error is to fix only at the corners, causing the frame to bow. Fix at regular intervals. After fixing, cut off any protruding packers flush with the frame. Then proceed to sealing. Remember: the fixings hold the frame in place, but the sealant provides the weather seal. Both are essential.

4. Sealing Gaps with Expanding Foam and Sealant

Sealing is what keeps water, air, and insects out. Use low-expansion polyurethane foam for gaps wider than 10mm. Shake the can well, then inject foam into the gap, filling about 50% because it expands. Do not overfill, or it will bow the frame. Let it cure for at least 1 hour (depending on humidity). Trim excess foam with a knife after it is fully cured. For gaps smaller than 10mm, use a high-quality silicone or polyurethane sealant. Clean the surfaces first: they must be dry, dust-free, and oil-free. Use a backing rod (foam rod) for deep gaps to control sealant depth and save material. Apply sealant with a caulking gun at a 45-degree angle, pushing the sealant ahead of the nozzle. Tool the sealant with a spatula or your finger dipped in soapy water to smooth it and ensure good adhesion. In hot climates, choose a sealant that can withstand high temperatures and UV exposure, such as neutral-cure silicone or polyurethane. Do not use acrylic sealant outdoors. Seal both inside and outside. On the outside, ensure a continuous seal around the entire frame. Pay special attention to the sill: seal the ends and the back of the sill to prevent water ingress. For doors, seal the threshold and sides. After sealing, check for gaps by shining a light from inside at night. If you see light, re-seal. Allow sealant to cure for 24 hours before exposing to water. Proper sealing prevents drafts, water damage, and mold, which are common problems in African climates with heavy rains and intense sun.

5. Installing Windows and Doors in Hot Climates

Hot climates present unique challenges: extreme heat, intense UV, and heavy rains. Aluminum expands significantly in heatabout 1mm per meter for a 50C temperature change. So, always leave expansion gaps: 5mm at the top and sides for windows, and 10mm for large doors. Never fix frames rigidly at both ends; allow one end to slide. Use thermal breaks if possible: these are plastic separators between the inner and outer aluminum, reducing heat transfer. For windows, consider low-E glass or tinted glass to reduce solar gain. Install windows with the drainage holes facing outward and unobstructed. In hot climates, rain can be torrential; ensure the sill has a slope of at least 5 degrees and a drip groove. Use wide overhangs or awnings if possible to protect the sealant from direct sun and rain. Sealant degrades faster under UV; choose a UV-resistant sealant and inspect annually. For doors, install with a raised threshold to prevent water ingress during heavy rain. Use weatherstripping that can withstand high temperatures, such as silicone or EPDM. Avoid PVC weatherstripping, which can become brittle. In dusty areas, use brush seals in addition to rubber seals. During installation, work in the cooler parts of the day to avoid heat stress and to prevent sealant from skinning too fast. If the frame is in direct sun, cool it with water before applying sealant. After installation, check that windows and doors operate freely; heat expansion may cause binding if gaps are insufficient. Regular maintenance: lubricate hinges and locks with a silicone spray every 6 months. Following these practices ensures your installation lasts in harsh African conditions.

6. Final Adjustments and Testing Operation

After installation and sealing, you must test and adjust. Open and close each window and door several times. They should operate smoothly with one hand. If they stick, check the hinges and rollers. For sliding windows, adjust the rollers using a screwdriver: turn the adjustment screw clockwise to raise the sash, counterclockwise to lower. For casement windows, adjust the friction stays or hinges. For doors, adjust the hinges: loosen the screws slightly, tap the hinge to align, then retighten. Check the lock: it should engage easily without forcing. If the lock is misaligned, adjust the strike plate or the frame. Check the drainage: pour a cup of water into the window track; it should drain out the weep holes quickly. If not, clear the holes with a wire. Check the seal: close the window and run your hand around the edges to feel for drafts. At night, shine a light from inside and look for gaps outside. If you find gaps, re-seal. For doors, check the threshold seal: it should touch the floor evenly. Adjust if needed. Also check that all screws are tight and that packers are trimmed. Clean the glass and frames with a soft cloth and mild soap. Do not use abrasive cleaners. Finally, demonstrate operation to the client: show them how to open, close, lock, and clean. Provide a maintenance schedule: lubricate moving parts every 6 months, check sealant annually, and clean drainage holes. A well-installed and adjusted window or door will last for decades and keep your client happy.

<h2>Practical Reference: Installing Aluminium Windows &amp; Doors on Site</h2>

<p>This practical reference is written for installers, site supervisors and small-to-medium aluminium fabrication businesses operating across Africa. Getting installation right the first time protects your margin, your reputation and your warranty obligations. Poorly installed aluminium frames leak, rattle, corrode and fail and in most African markets, word of mouth travels faster than any advertising budget. Treat this module as your on-site playbook: clean, level, fix, seal, test in that order, every time.</p>

<p>Aluminium is the dominant window and door material in African construction because it resists termites, does not warp in humidity, and tolerates the high UV and thermal cycling common from Lagos to Nairobi to Lusaka. But aluminium is unforgiving of bad workmanship. A frame that is 3 mm out of plumb will drag on its seals within a year. A gap left unsealed invites dust, insects and monsoon rain. This lesson gives you the field-tested sequence, the tolerances, and the checkpoints that separate a professional installation from a callback.</p>

<h3>1. Preparing the Opening: Cleaning and Repairing</h3>
<p>Before any frame arrives on site, the opening must be structurally sound, clean and dimensionally correct. Remove loose mortar, dust, oil, and any previous sealant residue. Repair spalled concrete, cracked blockwork and honeycombed mortar joints with a non-shrink repair mortar. In coastal markets such as Mombasa, Dar es Salaam or Durban, treat exposed steel reinforcement with a rust inhibitor before patching salt-laden air accelerates corrosion dramatically.</p>
<p>Check the opening dimensions against the fabrication drawings. As a rule of thumb, allow a 1015 mm gap on each side of the frame for packing, fixing and sealing. Confirm the opening is square by measuring both diagonals; a difference greater than 6 mm over a 1.5 m opening should be corrected before installation. Verify the sill is level and drains outward ponding water at the sill is the single most common cause of water ingress in tropical installations.</p>

<h3>2. Setting Frames Level and Plumb</h3>
<p>Set the frame into the opening on temporary packers (plastic shims are preferred over timber, which swells and rots). Use a spirit level and a plumb bob or laser level. Target tolerances: level and plumb within 2 mm per metre, and no more than 3 mm total deviation across the frame height. Check that the frame is not twisted by measuring corner-to-corner diagonals they must match within 3 mm.</p>
<p>Packers should be placed at fixing points, not randomly. For a typical window, that means at each corner, at the midpoint of the head and sill, and at intermediate points no more than 600 mm apart. Do not over-pack; the frame should sit without stress. Once set, re-check level, plumb and square before fixing.</p>

<p>Fixings should never be driven through the drainage channels or glazing rebates. Use the pre-drilled fixing points supplied by the fabricator. In brick or block, drill at low speed to avoid cracking, and always blow dust out of the hole before inserting the plug dust is the number one cause of loose fixings in African site conditions.</p>

<h3>4. Sealing Gaps with Expanding Foam and Sealant</h3>
<p>Expanding polyurethane foam provides thermal and acoustic insulation and structural stability, but it is not a waterproofing layer. Apply foam only after the frame is fixed and checked. Use a low-expansion foam for window frames to avoid bowing the frame high-expansion foam can push a frame out of plumb by several millimetres.</p>
<p>Once the foam has cured (typically 12 hours in African heat, longer in humid coastal conditions), trim it flush and apply a high-quality neutral-cure silicone or polyurethane sealant to the exterior joint. In hot climates, choose a sealant rated for high UV and movement a low-quality acrylic will crack within a single dry season. The exterior seal must be a continuous, tooled bead; the interior can be finished with a decorative trim or sealant.</p>

<strong style="color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING Expanding Foam and Sealant</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Expanding polyurethane foam contains isocyanates and is flammable during application. Always wear nitrile gloves, safety glasses and a respirator (P2/P3) when spraying. Ensure the work area is ventilated never apply foam in an enclosed room without airflow. Keep away from open flames, cigarettes and hot work. Uncured foam cannot be removed from skin with solvent; use a dedicated foam remover. Sealant solvents can cause eye irritation flush immediately with clean water and seek medical attention if irritation persists. On multi-storey sites, always use fall arrest equipment when working at height.</p>
</div>

<h3>5. Installing Windows and Doors in Hot Climates</h3>
<p>African installations face two enemies: heat and dust. In hot climates, aluminium expands and contracts significantly. Allow for thermal movement by not over-tightening fixings and by using sealants with adequate movement capability. Install frames during the cooler part of the day where possible foam and sealant cure best between 5C and 35C.</p>
<p>For doors, ensure the threshold drains outward and that the bottom rail has weep holes. In dusty regions such as the Sahel, specify brush seals or double gaskets on sliding windows and doors to keep out fine dust. For windows facing the sun, consider low-E or reflective glazing to reduce solar heat gain this is a strong selling point for your clients and reduces their cooling costs.</p>

<h3>6. Final Adjustments and Testing Operation</h3>
<p>After sealing, operate every window and door through its full range. Check that:</p>
<ul>
<li>Handles, locks and hinges engage smoothly without force.</li>
<li>Sliding sashes run freely and do not bind.</li>
<li>Seals compress evenly against the frame.</li>
<li>Drainage weep holes are clear and water drains outward.</li>
<li>No gaps remain between frame and wall.</li>
</ul>
<p>Adjust rollers, hinges and strikers as needed. Test with a water hose on the exterior to confirm no leaks this is a simple but powerful quality check that prevents costly callbacks. Document the test with photos for your client and for your warranty records.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li>Opening cleaned, repaired and dimensionally checked against drawings.</li>
<li>Diagonal measurements within 6 mm; sill level and draining outward.</li>
<li>Frame level and plumb within 2 mm per metre; diagonals matched within 3 mm.</li>
<li>Packers placed at fixing points, not randomly; frame not stressed.</li>
<li>Correct anchors used for substrate; fixings at 600 mm max spacing.</li>
<li>Low-expansion foam applied after fixing; trimmed flush when cured.</li>
<li>UV-rated sealant applied as continuous exterior bead; interior finished.</li>
<li>All windows and doors operated, adjusted and water-tested before handover.</li>
</ul>
