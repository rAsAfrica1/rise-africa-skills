Lessons

1. The Problem With Continuous Farrowing

If you keep a boar running with your sows all year, you get piglets born every week. That sounds good until you try to sell them. Say you have 10 sows. With continuous farrowing, each sow gives birth about twice a year, so you get roughly 20 litters spread across 52 weeks. That's a litter every 2.6 weeks, or about 2-3 piglets weaned per week if each litter has 8 piglets. You end up with a trickle of pigs of all different ages and sizes. Buyers want 20 or 50 pigs of the same size at one time. You can't fill that order. You also waste time: every week you're moving a few pigs, mixing feeds for different age groups, and trying to find a buyer for 3 pigs. Transport costs eat your profit because you're making many small trips. Health suffers too: piglets of different ages mix and pass diseases. And your records become a messyou can't tell which sow is performing. The worst part is cash flow: you get small amounts of money irregularly, so you can't plan investments or pay bills. Continuous farrowing is the default for many smallholders, but it's a trap. It keeps you busy but poor. The solution is to group your sows so they give birth at the same time. That's batch farrowing, and it changes everything.

2. What Buyers Actually Want

Put yourself in the buyer's shoes. A hotel in Nairobi needs 30 pigs of about 60 kg each every Friday. A butcher in Kampala wants 50 carcasses of uniform size twice a week. A trader from Lagos buys 100 weaners every month to fatten. They all want consistency: same weight, same age, same health status, delivered on a fixed schedule. If you show up with 5 pigs today, 8 next week, and none the week after, they will drop you. Buyers also want to plan their own business. They need to know that on the 15th of every month, you will have 40 pigs ready. That reliability lets them sign contracts with their customers. They also care about biosecurity: mixing pigs from different sources or ages increases disease risk. Uniform batches are safer. And they want value: if you can deliver 50 pigs at once, you can negotiate a better price per kg because you save them the trouble of sourcing from many farmers. In Africa, the demand for pork is growing fastin cities like Accra, Johannesburg, and Kampala, pork consumption is rising. But the supply is fragmented. The farmer who can supply consistent batches will capture the market. So don't think about what you can produce; think about what the buyer needs. Then design your farm to deliver exactly that. Batch farrowing is the only way to meet those demands.

3. The Batch Farrowing Solution

Batch farrowing means you group your sows so that they give birth within a short windowusually one weekand then you repeat that cycle at fixed intervals. For example, with 20 sows, you might breed 10 sows in week 1, and the other 10 in week 4. Then each group farrows together, and you have two batches per cycle. But to get daily sales, you need more batches. The most common system for daily sales is the 3-week batch system with 7 groups. Here's how it works: you divide your sows into 7 groups. Each group is bred one week apart. Since gestation is 16 weeks (114 days), each group farrows 16 weeks after breeding. But because you breed one group per week, you get a batch of piglets born every week. However, that's weekly, not daily. To get daily sales, you need to spread the sales of each batch across the days of the week. That's done by having multiple batches at different stages. Actually, the 7-batch system gives you one batch farrowing per week. If each batch has, say, 10 sows, and each sow weans 8 piglets, you get 80 piglets per week. You can sell those 80 pigs over 7 daysabout 11-12 pigs per day. That's daily sales. The key is that all pigs in a batch are the same age, so they are uniform. You also plan breeding, feeding, and health care by batch, which saves labor. And you can synchronize weaning and selling. Batch farrowing turns a chaotic operation into a predictable factory.

4. The 3-Week Cycle Explained

The 3-week cycle is the heartbeat of batch farrowing. It comes from the pig's reproductive cycle: sows come into heat every 21 days. If you breed sows on a fixed 3-week schedule, you can group them easily. But for daily sales, we use a 7-batch system with a 3-week cycle? Here's the math: You have 7 groups of sows. Each group is bred 3 weeks apart. Gestation is 16 weeks, so each group farrows 16 weeks after breeding. Lactation is 4 weeks, so piglets are weaned at 4 weeks. Then sows are rebred. The cycle for each group is: breed, gestate 16 weeks, farrow, lactate 4 weeks, wean, and then rebreed. That's 20 weeks from breeding to weaning. But with 7 groups bred 3 weeks apart, you get a batch farrowing every 3 weeks? No, that would be every 3 weeks, not daily. Wait, the 7-batch system with 3-week intervals gives you a batch farrowing every 3 weeks. That's not daily. To get daily sales, you need a batch farrowing every week. So you need 7 batches with 1-week intervals. But the 3-week cycle refers to the sow's estrus cycle. In a 7-batch system with 1-week intervals, you breed one batch per week. Each batch returns to heat every 3 weeks, so you need to manage breeding accordingly. The 3-week cycle is the biological rhythm. You can use it to synchronize sows: breed them on the same day, and they will farrow on the same day 16 weeks later. So the 3-week cycle is a tool for grouping. For daily sales, you want 7 batches farrowing one week apart. That gives you a weekly batch, which you can sell daily. So the 3-week cycle helps you plan breeding groups, but the sales rhythm is weekly. This lesson explains that distinction.

5. Seven Batches in Motion

Imagine you have 70 sows. You divide them into 7 groups of 10 sows each. You breed Group 1 in Week 1. Group 2 in Week 2. Group 3 in Week 3, and so on up to Group 7 in Week 7. Then you repeat the breeding every 7 weeks? No, you breed each group every 20 weeks (16 weeks gestation + 4 weeks lactation). But because you have 7 groups, you can breed one group per week in a rotating schedule. Let's track a full cycle. Week 1: Breed Group 1. Week 2: Breed Group 2. ... Week 7: Breed Group 7. Week 8: No breeding? Actually, after weaning, sows come into heat about 4-7 days later. So if Group 1 farrows in Week 17 (16 weeks after breeding), they lactate for 4 weeks, wean in Week 21, and can be rebred in Week 22. So the cycle length is 21 weeks from breeding to rebreeding. With 7 groups, you can breed one group every 3 weeks? That would be 7 groups * 3 weeks = 21 weeks. Yes! So the correct system is: 7 groups, bred 3 weeks apart. That means you breed Group 1 in Week 1, Group 2 in Week 4, Group 3 in Week 7, Group 4 in Week 10, Group 5 in Week 13, Group 6 in Week 16, Group 7 in Week 19. Then Group 1 is rebred in Week 22 (21 weeks after Week 1). So you have a breeding event every 3 weeks. But farrowing also happens every 3 weeks: Group 1 farrows in Week 17 (16 weeks after Week 1), Group 2 farrows in Week 20, etc. So you get a batch farrowing every 3 weeks. That's not daily sales. To get daily sales, you need a batch farrowing every week. So the 7-batch system with 3-week intervals gives you a batch every 3 weeks. That's 7 batches per 21 weeks, but the sales would be lumpy. For daily sales, you need to sell the pigs from each batch over the 3-week period? Actually, if you have a batch of 80 pigs every 3 weeks, you can sell about 4 pigs per day. That could work. But the lesson title says 'Seven Batches in Motion'so we explain that with 7 batches, you always have pigs at different stages: some breeding, some gestating, some lactating, some growing, some finishing. You can plan daily sales by selling from the oldest batch each day. For example, if each batch has 80 pigs, and you sell them over 21 days, that's about 4 pigs per day. So daily sales are possible even with 3-week batch intervals. The key is to have multiple batches so you never run out.

6. Why This System Works Anywhere

Batch farrowing works in any climate, any country, any scale. Whether you are in a hot, dry area of northern Nigeria or a cool, highland region of Ethiopia, pigs reproduce the same way. The 3-week cycle is universal. The 7-batch system uses simple math: 7 groups, 3-week intervals, 21-week cycle. You don't need expensive equipment. You need a calendar, a breeding board, and discipline. In Africa, many smallholders have 5 to 20 sows. You can adapt the system to your size. With 7 sows, you can have 7 batches of 1 sow each. That's small, but you still get a batch every 3 weeks. With 70 sows, you get batches of 10 sows. The system scales. It also works with local breeds or exotic breeds. Local breeds may have smaller litters, but you can adjust batch size. The key is grouping. You also don't need artificial insemination; natural mating works if you manage the boar. The system reduces disease because you can clean and disinfect between batches. It saves labor because you do tasks by batch, not pig by pig. It improves cash flow because you know when pigs will be ready. And it opens markets because you can promise consistent supply. In Africa, where demand for pork is rising, the farmer who can deliver daily will win. This system has been used in Europe, Asia, and the Americas. It works in Uganda, Kenya, South Africa, and Ghana. It works anywhere you can keep a calendar. So don't think you need a big farm. Start with what you have, group your sows, and follow the schedule. You will see the difference in your bank account.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Why Daily Sales Require Batch Production

This practical reference consolidates the core operating logic of a batch farrowing system for small and medium pig enterprises in Africa. If you are selling pigs every week to local butcheries, roadside roast pork joints, hotels, or live-animal traders you cannot afford the unpredictable supply that continuous farrowing creates. This module explains, in business terms, why batch production is the engine behind consistent daily sales, and how to run it.

The Problem With Continuous Farrowing

Continuous farrowing means sows give birth throughout the year without synchronisation. On paper, this sounds like steady supply. In practice, it creates a wave pattern: some weeks you have more pigs than buyers want, other weeks you have none. When supply is high, buyers push prices down. When you have no pigs, you lose the customer. Meanwhile, labour, feed, and veterinary costs are spread unevenly, making it impossible to plan cash flow or negotiate contracts.

What Buyers Actually Want

Butcheries, roast pork vendors, hotels, and institutional buyers need reliable weekly volumes. They want to know: how many pigs, what weight, which day of the week, every week. This predictability is worth more to them than the lowest price. A buyer who can plan their menu, staff, and cash flow will pay a premium for consistency and will stay loyal. That is the business case for batch production.

The Batch Farrowing Solution

Batch farrowing groups sows into cohorts that give birth in the same week. Instead of managing individual sows, you manage batches. All sows in a batch are served at the same time, farrow together, and their piglets are weaned together. This synchronises every stage breeding, gestation, farrowing, weaning, finishing and produces a predictable flow of pigs to market.

The 3-Week Cycle Explained

The most practical cycle for small African farms is the 3-week batch cycle. Sows are grouped so that a new batch farrows every 3 weeks. Because the gestation period is approximately 114 days (16 weeks and 2 days), a sow served in week 1 farrows in week 17. By managing seven batches in rotation, you can maintain weekly market supply from a 3-week farrowing rhythm the finishing stage smooths the output into weekly deliveries.

Seven Batches in Motion

With seven batches, each at a different stage of production, your farm operates like a conveyor belt. While batch 1 is breeding, batch 2 is gestating, batch 3 is farrowing, batch 4 is lactating, batches 57 are growing and finishing. Every week, some pigs reach market weight. Every week, you deliver. This is how a small farm achieves the consistency that large commercial operations enjoy.

Why This System Works Anywhere

Batch farrowing does not require imported genetics, expensive housing, or high-tech equipment. It requires discipline, record-keeping, and a breeding calendar. It works in Uganda, Nigeria, Kenya, South Africa, and across the continent because it is a management system, not a technology. It turns a small herd into a reliable business.

SAFETY WARNING: Biosecurity and Batch Mixing

Never mix pigs from different batches in the same pen or transport vehicle. Batch farrowing creates age-uniform groups, but mixing breaks the biosecurity barrier and spreads disease rapidly especially African Swine Fever (ASF), which has no vaccine. Always clean and disinfect between batches, enforce a 24-hour downtime for visitors, and quarantine new animals for at least 30 days before introduction. A single breach can wipe out an entire batch and destabilise your supply contract.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Group sows into batches of 35 based on body condition and parity for even farrowing.
  • Serve all sows in a batch within the same week use boar contact or AI on a fixed schedule.
  • Keep a breeding calendar on the wall or phone mark serve, farrow, wean, and market dates.
  • Maintain 7 batches in rotation to achieve weekly market supply from a 3-week farrowing rhythm.
  • Record every batch: number served, number farrowed, piglets born alive, weaned, and sold.
  • Clean and disinfect pens between batches never mix age groups, especially with ASF risk.
  • Confirm buyer volumes before each batch farrows sell forward, not backward.
  • Review performance quarterly cull repeat offenders, adjust batch size, renegotiate prices.
