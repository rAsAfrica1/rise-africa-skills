1. Queen Biology and Mating

The queen is the mother of every bee in the colony. She is the only female with fully developed ovaries, and she can lay up to 2,000 eggs per day during peak season. A good queen keeps the colony productive and calm. In Africa, local races like Apis mellifera scutellata are well adapted, but they can be aggressive. Understanding her biology helps you manage swarming and improve honey yields.

A queen develops from a fertilised egg. When workers feed a larva royal jelly for its entire larval stage, it becomes a queen. She emerges 16 days after the egg is laid, compared to 21 days for workers. Virgin queens take orientation flights, then go on mating flights 57 days after emergence. They mate in the air with 1020 drones, often 15 km from the hive. Mating happens once in her life, usually within a week. She stores sperm in a spermatheca and uses it to fertilise eggs for years.

After mating, the queen returns to the hive and begins laying eggs 23 days later. She can live 13 years, but her egg-laying peaks in the first year. In Africa, queens often mate year-round if forage and weather allow. However, during dry seasons or dearth, mating may be poor. A queen that fails to mate properly becomes a drone layer, laying only unfertilised eggs that become drones. This ruins the colony.

To check queen quality, look at the brood pattern. A good queen lays in a solid, compact pattern with few gaps. You can also mark her to track her age. Replace queens every 12 years to maintain colony strength. In African conditions, requeening before the rainy season helps colonies build up for honey flow. Always handle queens gently; they are delicate and can be injured.

2. Methods of Queen Rearing

There are several ways to raise queens, but for African beekeepers, the Doolittle grafting method and the Miller method are most practical. You need a strong queenright colony as a cell builder, and a starter colony that is queenless. The cell builder should have plenty of young nurse bees, pollen, and nectar. You also need a queenless starter to accept grafted larvae. In Africa, use local materials like wooden bars and beeswax cups.

The Doolittle method: Transfer 1-day-old larvae from a chosen breeder queen into artificial queen cups. Use a grafting tool to lift the larva with royal jelly and place it in the cup. Then attach cups to a frame and put them in the starter colony. After 24 hours, move the accepted cells to the cell builder. The bees will draw out queen cells. Ten days after grafting, the cells are ready to be placed in mating nucs. This method gives you control over genetics and produces many queens from one breeder.

The Miller method: Cut a comb with eggs and young larvae into strips. Attach the strips to top bars in a frame. The bees will build queen cells along the cut edges. Once the cells are sealed, cut them out and place them in nucs. This method is simpler and requires no grafting tool, but you get fewer queens and less control over which larvae become queens.

Another method is the Jenter system, a plastic kit that cages the queen to lay eggs in specific cells. It is expensive but easy for beginners. In Africa, you can also use natural queen cells from swarming colonies, but you risk disease and poor quality. Always choose breeder queens from colonies that are productive, gentle, and disease-resistant. Feed the cell builder with sugar syrup (1:1) during dearth to keep them working.

3. Grafting Larvae

Grafting is the skill of moving a tiny larva from a worker cell into an artificial queen cup. It sounds delicate, but with practice you can do it. You need a grafting tool a thin, flexible stick with a curved tip, often made of plastic or stainless steel. A headlamp or bright sunlight helps you see the tiny larvae. Work quickly but gently; larvae dry out fast.

First, prepare your queen cups. These are small wax or plastic cups attached to a bar. Prime them with a drop of royal jelly or diluted honey. Then, go to your breeder colony and find a frame with young larvae, ideally 1 day old. They look like tiny white C-shapes floating in royal jelly. Choose larvae from eggs you know the age of if you see eggs, the larvae next to them are 12 days old. Do not graft larvae older than 2 days; they may not become good queens.

Slide the grafting tool under the larva and lift it with the royal jelly. Do not flip it or squeeze it. Place the larva in the queen cup, and gently push it to the bottom. One larva per cup. After grafting 2030 cups, place the frame in a queenless starter colony. The bees will accept the grafts if they are well fed and have no queen. Check after 24 hours; accepted cells will have bees feeding the larva. Remove any rejected cups.

Keep the starter colony strong and feed them sugar syrup. After 2448 hours, move the accepted cells to a cell builder colony. Grafting is best done during a honey flow or when you feed. In Africa, avoid grafting during very hot, dry afternoons. Practice with drone larvae first to build your skill. With time, you can achieve 80% acceptance.

4. Mating Nucs and Queen Banks

A mating nuc is a small hive used for a virgin queen to mate and start laying. It holds 23 frames of bees, brood, and food. You can make nucs from wood, or use divided boxes. In Africa, a common design is a three-frame nuc box. Each nuc gets a sealed queen cell or a virgin queen, plus a cup of bees. Shake young bees from a brood frame into the nuc they are more likely to accept the queen. Close the entrance with grass for a day to help them settle.

Place nucs in a shady spot, away from strong winds. Provide sugar syrup and pollen if forage is scarce. After 1014 days, check if the queen is laying. A mated queen will have a shiny, extended abdomen and you will see eggs or young larvae. If she fails to mate, combine the nuc with another or give a new cell. In Africa, ants and small hive beetles can invade nucs, so keep them off the ground and use ant guards.

A queen bank is a colony that holds many mated queens temporarily. You can bank queens in a queenless colony with frames of young bees. Each queen is caged individually with attendant workers. The bank keeps queens alive for weeks until you need them. Feed the bank regularly. This is useful when you have more queens than nucs, or when you want to introduce queens later.

To introduce a queen from a bank, remove her cage and place it between frames. Workers will feed her through the screen. After 23 days, if bees are not biting the cage, release her. In Africa, queen banks help you multiply colonies quickly. Always keep records of queen age and source.

5. Marking and Introducing Queens

Marking a queen helps you know her age and whether she has been replaced. Use a queen marking kit: a small cage, a marker pen with non-toxic paint, and a steady hand. The international colour code changes each year ending in 1 or 6 (white), 2 or 7 (yellow), 3 or 8 (red), 4 or 9 (green), 5 or 0 (blue). In Africa, you can use model paint or nail polish, but make sure it dries quickly and is non-toxic.

To mark, catch the queen by her wings or use a queen catcher. Hold her gently against a surface. Put a small dot of paint on her thorax (the middle part). Do not get paint on her eyes, antennae, or wings. Let it dry for a minute, then release her into the colony. Marked queens are easier to spot during inspections. Check the mark to know if she is still the same queen.

Introducing a new queen to a colony is risky; bees may kill her. Use a queen introduction cage. Place the queen in the cage with a few attendant workers. Put the cage between two frames of brood. The bees will get used to her pheromones. After 23 days, open the cage exit (often a candy plug) so workers can release her. Do not release her directly; let the bees do it. If the colony is queenless and aggressive, first remove all queen cells.

In Africa, introduce queens in the evening when bees are calm. Feed the colony sugar syrup to reduce aggression. Check after 3 days to see if she is laying. If the bees ball her, they may kill her. Always have a backup queen. Marking and introducing are skills that improve with practice.

6. Splitting Colonies for Increase

Splitting a colony is the simplest way to multiply your hives. You take a strong colony and divide it into two or more. Do this during a honey flow or when there is plenty of forage, so both splits can build up. In Africa, the best time is after the first rains when flowers are abundant. You need a queenright parent colony with at least 810 frames of bees and brood.

Method 1: Even split. Move the parent hive a few metres away. Place a new empty hive on the original spot. Put half the frames of brood, bees, and food in each hive. Make sure one hive has the old queen. The queenless half will raise a new queen from young larvae. If you want to control genetics, introduce a mated queen or a sealed queen cell after 24 hours. Both hives will need feeding with sugar syrup (1:1) to help them draw comb and raise brood.

Method 2: Walk-away split. This is easier. Take 34 frames of brood and bees from a strong colony and put them in a new hive. Move the new hive to a different location at least 3 km away to prevent bees from returning. The parent colony gets a new queen cell or is left to raise its own. The new hive will also raise a queen. This method is less stressful but requires moving hives.

After splitting, check both colonies after 7 days. The queenless one should have queen cells. After 30 days, check for eggs. Feed regularly. In Africa, splits help you recover from colony losses and increase honey production. Never split weak colonies; they will fail. Always keep notes on which hive has which queen.

<h2>Practical Reference: Queen Rearing and Colony Multiplication</h2>

<p>Queen rearing is the engine room of any commercial beekeeping operation. In Africa, where demand for honey, beeswax, propolis, and pollination services is rising faster than the supply of quality colonies, the ability to raise your own queens and multiply colonies is the difference between a hobby and a business. A single well-managed mother colony can produce 1530 daughter colonies per season, each worth US$60150 in East and Southern Africa, depending on region and market. This lesson consolidates the practical reference material you need on the farm: queen biology, rearing methods, grafting technique, mating nucs, queen banks, marking and introduction, and colony splitting all framed around the economics of an African apiary.</p>

<h3>1. Queen Biology and Mating What Drives Colony Economics</h3>

<p>The queen is the only reproductive female in the colony. She develops from a fertilised egg laid in a queen cell, fed royal jelly throughout larval life. Development takes about 16 days from egg to emergence (queen), versus 21 days for workers and 24 days for drones. This 5-day advantage is critical: it is why queen cells must be handled on a strict schedule.</p>

<p>Mating occurs 514 days after emergence. The virgin queen takes 13 orientation flights, then 13 mating flights, usually 15 km from the hive, mating with 1020 drones in flight. She stores sperm in her spermatheca for life typically 24 years, though commercial queens are usually replaced after 12 years. In African contexts, local <em>Apis mellifera scutellata</em>, <em>A. m. adansonii</em>, <em>A. m. monticola</em>, and <em>A. m. capensis</em> show different swarming and absconding tendencies; <em>scutellata</em> in particular is highly defensive and swarms frequently, so queen replacement must be planned, not reactive.</p>

<p><strong>Business implication:</strong> A queen that lays 1,5002,000 eggs/day supports a colony producing 2040 kg honey/year in good forage. A failing queen can cut that by 6080%. Replacement queens from a reputable breeder cost US$1540 in Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda, and South Africa but raising your own drops that to under US$35 per queen once you have equipment and skills.</p>

<h3>2. Methods of Queen Rearing Choose by Scale and Budget</h3>

<p>Four practical methods dominate African apiaries:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Natural queen cells (swarm cells):</strong> Free, but uncontrolled genetics and timing. Suitable only for colony increase, not commercial queen sales.</li>
<li><strong>Emergency cells:</strong> Queenless colony raises cells from young larvae. Quick but unpredictable quality.</li>
<li><strong>Miller method:</strong> A frame with a zig-zag strip of young larvae is placed in a queenless starter. Low equipment cost, good for beginners.</li>
<li><strong>Doolittle grafting:</strong> Larvae transferred into artificial cups. Highest output (3060 cells per batch), standard for commercial queen producers.</li>
<li><strong>Jenter / Cupkit systems:</strong> Queen lays directly into cups no grafting skill required. Higher equipment cost (US$80200 per kit) but excellent for semi-commercial operations.</li>
</ul>

<h3>3. Grafting Larvae The Skill That Sets Your Output</h3>

<p>Grafting is transferring 1224 hour old larvae (the youngest, smallest, C-shaped larvae floating in royal jelly) from a breeder frame into artificial queen cups. Success depends on:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Lighting:</strong> Work under indirect daylight or a headlamp direct sun dries larvae.</li>
<li><strong>Temperature:</strong> Keep the breeder frame warm (3034C). In hot African conditions, work in the early morning or under shade.</li>
<li><strong>Tool:</strong> A Chinese grafting tool or a fine paintbrush. Under 30 seconds per larva; a full frame of 30 cells in 1520 minutes.</li>
<li><strong>Starter colony:</strong> Queenless, well-fed, crowded with young nurse bees. Grafts accepted at 7090% in good conditions, 3050% in poor ones.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Business implication:</strong> If you graft 30 cells weekly for 10 weeks and achieve 70% acceptance, you produce ~210 queens per season. At US$10 farm-gate value per mated queen, that is US$2,100 before honey and wax. This is why grafting is the highest-return skill in queen rearing.</p>

<h3>4. Mating Nucs and Queen Banks</h3>

<p>A mating nuc is a small colony (25 frames, or a mini-nuc) that houses a virgin queen until she mates and starts laying. Common types in Africa: 3-frame wooden nucs, 5-frame Langstroth splits, and polystyrene mini-mating boxes (Apidea-type). Key management:</p>

<ul>
<li>Stock each nuc with 12 frames of sealed brood, 1 frame of honey/pollen, and 1 cup of bees (about 250400 g).</li>
<li>Introduce the queen cell 24 hours after nuc formation.</li>
<li>Check for eggs 1421 days after emergence. A laying queen means success.</li>
<li>Re-use nucs by re-queening with a fresh cell every 34 weeks.</li>
</ul>

<p>A <strong>queen bank</strong> holds mated queens temporarily (up to 23 weeks) before sale or introduction. Use a strong queenless colony with a queen excluder and banking frames. Keep queens separated in cages with attendant workers. Temperature 2530C and 5070% relative humidity are ideal critical in hot, dry African zones.</p>

<h3>5. Marking and Introducing Queens</h3>

<p>Mark queens with a numbered disc or colour code (international system: white, yellow, red, green, blue for years ending 1/6, 2/7, 3/8, 4/9, 5/0). Marking helps you track age, origin, and performance.</p>

<p>Introduction methods, in order of reliability:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Push-in cage</strong> over emerging brood highest acceptance.</li>
<li><strong>Queen cage with candy plug</strong> standard, 7085% acceptance.</li>
<li><strong>Direct release</strong> only with young, mated queens in queenless colonies.</li>
<li><strong>Newspaper method</strong> for combining colonies.</li>
</ol>

<p>Never introduce a queen into a colony with a laying queen, queen cells, or a virgin queen she will be killed.</p>

<h3>6. Splitting Colonies for Increase</h3>

<p>Colony splitting is the simplest multiplication method. Standard splits:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Even split:</strong> Divide brood, bees, and food equally; both halves get a queen cell or mated queen.</li>
<li><strong>Walk-away split:</strong> Queenless half raises its own queen from eggs. Slower but no queen purchase.</li>
<li><strong>Nuc split:</strong> Take 23 frames from a strong colony into a nuc box; introduce a mated queen.</li>
<li><strong>Demaree / swarm control split:</strong> Used to prevent swarming while increasing colony numbers.</li>
</ul>

<p>Split only strong colonies (8+ frames of bees, 4+ of brood) and only when forage is available. In Africa, the best splitting windows are the start of the rains and the main nectar flow timing varies by region (e.g., MarchMay in East Africa, SeptemberNovember in Southern Africa).</p>

<p style="margin:0;color:#8b1a1a;font-weight:bold;"> SAFETY WARNING African Bee Defensiveness</p>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0;color:#5a1a1a;">African honey bee subspecies (especially <em>Apis mellifera scutellata</em>) are significantly more defensive than European races. Always wear a full bee suit, gloves, and closed boots when working with queenless starter colonies, mating nucs, or during splitting. Queenless colonies are the most aggressive. Work in early morning or late evening, use smoke sparingly but correctly, and never open hives near livestock, homes, or public paths. If you are allergic to bee stings, do not perform queen rearing without a trained partner and an adrenaline auto-injector on site. In areas with Africanised bee populations, maintain a 50100 m buffer from dwellings and notify neighbours before splitting.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Select breeder stock</strong> from colonies with high honey yield, low defensiveness, and resistance to pests (varroa, small hive beetle, wax moth).</li>
<li><strong>Prepare starter colony</strong> 24 h before grafting: queenless, crowded with young bees, fed 1:1 sugar syrup.</li>
<li><strong>Graft larvae</strong> 1224 h old, in shade, under 30 seconds per larva, into primed cups with a drop of royal jelly.</li>
<li><strong>Transfer ripe cells</strong> (day 1014) to mating nucs; never shake or chill cells.</li>
<li><strong>Check mating success</strong> 1421 days after emergence; look for eggs and a rounded, laying queen.</li>
<li><strong>Mark and record</strong> every queen (colour code + number) and track performance per colony.</li>
<li><strong>Introduce queens</strong> using push-in cage or candy-release cage; never into a queenright colony.</li>
<li><strong>Split only strong colonies</strong> during nectar flow; feed splits until established.</li>
<li><strong>Maintain hygiene</strong>: sterilise tools between hives to prevent disease spread (AFB, EFB, Nosema).</li>
<li><strong>Plan for season</strong>: 10-week grafting cycle can yield 150250 queens; align sales with planting and pollination seasons.</li>
