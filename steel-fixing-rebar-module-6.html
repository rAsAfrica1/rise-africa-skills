1. Module 6 Lesson 1: Reading Bar Bending Schedules and Setting Out on African Sites

Welcome to Module 6, the stage where steel fixing moves from general site labour into controlled, paid, professional work. In this first lesson we focus on the document that separates a gang that guesses from a gang that gets paid on time: the bar bending schedule. On many sites in Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana, Tanzania, Uganda and South Africa, the schedule is the single most important piece of paper after the drawing itself. It tells you the bar mark, the diameter, the shape code, the number of bars, the cutting length, the bending dimensions and often the total weight. If you cannot read it, you cannot price your work, you cannot order steel correctly, and you cannot prove to the main contractor that you delivered what was specified. Start by understanding the header information. The project name, drawing number, revision, member reference and date must match the drawing you are holding. A schedule from revision A used on a revision C drawing is a costly mistake. Check the member: is it a pad footing, a ground beam, a column, a suspended slab, a staircase or a retaining wall? Each member has different fixing rules, cover requirements and lap positions. Next, read the bar mark. Bar marks are usually letters or numbers such as B1, C4 or S12. They link the schedule to the drawing. Never cut steel from memory. Mark every bundle with the bar mark using a paint pen or metal tag. In busy yards in Lagos or Nairobi, mixed bundles are the number one cause of wrong bars in the wrong place. Then confirm the shape code. Shape codes describe bends: straight bars, L-bars, U-bars, cranked bars, links and stirrups. A shape code 00 is straight, 11 is an L, 21 is a U, 51 is a closed link. If the schedule says shape code 51 with a 135 degree hook, you must bend the hook, not leave a 90 degree corner. The engineer will reject it and you will cut it out at your own cost. Now move to cutting length. The cutting length is the total length of bar needed before bending, including hooks and bends but excluding laps unless stated. Learn the basic deductions for bends. For a 90 degree bend, the deduction depends on the bar diameter and the bend radius. A common site rule for small bars is to deduct two times the diameter for each 90 degree bend, but always follow the engineer's schedule. For 135 degree hooks in links, the hook length is usually ten times the diameter or as specified. Do not guess. If the schedule is unclear, raise a technical query the same day. A written answer protects you. Setting out comes next. Setting out means transferring the drawing dimensions to the ground or to the formwork. You need a baseline, a tape, a plumb bob, string lines, marking paint and a good spirit level. On a pad footing, mark the centre lines, then the bar positions at the specified spacing. Spacing is usually given as 150, 200 or 250 millimetres centre to centre. Count the bars: number equals length divided by spacing plus one. If the footing is 2000 millimetres wide and spacing is 200, you need eleven bars. Getting this wrong means either a weak footing or wasted steel. Both cost money. In Africa, steel is often delivered in 12 metre lengths. Plan your cutting to minimise waste. A good steel fixer can save five to ten percent of steel by nesting cuts. That saving can be your profit margin. Keep a cutting list, a offcut rack and a waste bin. Sell scrap legally and record it. Finally, remember cover. Cover is the distance from the outside of the concrete to the nearest bar. It protects steel from rust and fire. In foundations cast against earth, cover may be 50 to 75 millimetres. In columns and beams exposed to weather, cover may be 40 millimetres. In slabs, 20 to 25 millimetres. Use cover blocks, not stones or wood. Stones crush, wood rots, and both leave voids. Buy or make proper cover blocks with the right strength. At the end of this lesson, you should be able to take a bar bending schedule, confirm it against the drawing, mark your steel, plan your cuts and set out a simple member. That is the foundation of profitable steel fixing.

2. Module 6 Lesson 2: Cutting, Bending and Fabricating Rebar Safely and Accurately

In this lesson we move into the steel yard, where raw bars become finished reinforcement. This is where accuracy, safety and speed decide whether your gang makes money or loses it. Start with the tools. You need a bar cutting machine, a bar bending machine, a measuring tape, a steel square, a chalk line, a paint pen, a hammer, a punch, a grinder with a cutting disc, and personal protective equipment. PPE means safety boots, gloves, eye protection, ear protection and a dust mask when grinding. In many African countries, site safety rules are getting stricter, and a gang without PPE will be sent off site. That is lost income. Before cutting, check the bar diameter. Use a caliper or a marked gauge. Do not trust the colour or the label alone. Deformed bars are usually marked with the manufacturer, the size and the grade. Common grades are 250, 460 and 500 megapascals. Grade 500 is stronger, so you must not over-bend it or heat it. Never weld rebar unless the engineer approves it in writing. Welding changes the metallurgy and can make the bar brittle. Now plan the cut. Lay out the bars on trestles at waist height. Measure from the same end every time. Mark with a paint pen, not chalk, because chalk rubs off. Use a stop block on the cutting machine so every cut is the same length. For links and stirrups, cut a batch, then bend a batch. Batch work is faster and more consistent. When bending, set the machine stops to the correct dimensions. For a closed link, the bending sequence matters. Bend one end, then the other, then the two sides, then close the hook. If you bend in the wrong order, the link will not fit around the main bars. For cranked bars, mark the crank position and the crank height. A common crank is one in ten or one in twelve. The crank must sit in the right place so the bar sits in the middle of the concrete. For L-bars and U-bars, check the internal dimensions, not the external. The schedule usually gives internal dimensions. If you bend to external dimensions, the bar will be too short. Accuracy tolerance on site is usually plus or minus 25 millimetres for cutting length and plus or minus 5 millimetres for bending dimensions. Keep a steel square and a tape at the bending machine. Check the first bar of every batch. If the first bar is wrong, the whole batch is wrong. Safety in the yard is serious. Cutting machines can amputate fingers. Keep hands away from the blade. Use a push stick for short offcuts. Bending machines can crush hands. Never reach into the machine while it is running. Grinders throw sparks and metal fragments. Use a face shield, not just goggles. Keep a fire extinguisher nearby, especially in dry season when grass and rubbish can catch fire. Stack bars safely. Do not stack bars too high. Use timber bearers. Keep walkways clear. In hot climates, dehydration is a real risk. Provide clean drinking water and shade. Schedule heavy work for early morning. In malaria areas, drain standing water and use repellent. Now think about quality. Rust is normal on rebar, but loose scale, pitting and section loss are not. Reject bars that are badly corroded. Do not use bent or twisted bars without straightening them properly. Straightening must be done with a machine or a controlled method, not by hammering on the ground. Keep bars clean. Oil, paint, mud and concrete splatter reduce bond. If bars are contaminated, wipe them before fixing. Finally, record your work. Keep a fabrication log: date, bar mark, diameter, number cut, number bent, and the name of the operator. This log helps you claim payment and trace defects. A professional steel fixing business runs on records. At the end of this lesson, you should be able to set up a safe yard, cut and bend bars to schedule, check your own work, and keep records that protect your money.

3. Module 6 Lesson 3: Fixing Foundations, Columns and Ground Beams in African Ground Conditions

This lesson covers the first structural elements you will fix on most projects: foundations, columns and ground beams. African ground conditions vary widely. You may work in soft clay in the Niger Delta, black cotton soil in Kenya, laterite in Ghana, sand in the Sahel, or rock in Ethiopia. Each soil type affects how you handle cover, excavation and support. Start with pad foundations. A pad footing usually has a bottom mat of bars in two directions. The bars are placed on cover blocks, usually 50 to 75 millimetres above the blinding. The longer bars often go in the bottom layer, but always follow the drawing. Tie the intersections with binding wire, usually 1.6 millimetre black annealed wire. Use a tie that is tight but not so tight that it lifts the bar off the cover block. A common pattern is a cross tie or a figure of eight. For a 200 millimetre spacing, tie every second intersection, then go back and tie the rest. This saves wire and time. Next, place the starter bars for the column. Starter bars must be in the correct position and at the correct projection. Use a setting out template or a timber frame to hold them. If starters are out of position, the column bars will not line up, and the engineer will ask for a repair. Repairs cost money and time. For column starters, the lap length matters. Lap length depends on bar diameter, concrete grade and whether the bar is in tension or compression. A common lap for 16 millimetre bars in grade 30 concrete is 40 times the diameter, which is 640 millimetres. But always use the engineer's lap schedule. Do not guess. Now move to ground beams. Ground beams sit on the ground or on pile caps. They carry walls and transfer loads. Fix the bottom bars first, then the links, then the top bars. Links must be vertical and at the correct spacing. The first link should be 50 millimetres from the face of the support. Keep the links square. Use a spacing stick, which is a piece of timber marked with the link positions. This is faster and more accurate than measuring every link. For columns, the vertical bars are usually fixed first, then the links. Column links must have 135 degree hooks, and the hooks should alternate around the column. The hook must engage the vertical bar. Do not leave hooks open. For a rectangular column, the link is usually a closed shape with two hooks. For a circular column, use spiral links or circular links as specified. Cover is critical in columns. Use cover blocks on all four faces. In coastal areas, cover may be increased because of salt. In areas with sulphate in the soil, use sulphate-resisting cement and increased cover. Always check the drawing. In hot climates, concrete can dry too fast. Fixing must be complete before the pour. Do not leave bars exposed for weeks. If there is a delay, cover the bars or apply a protective coating if specified. In rainy season, water can fill excavations. Pump out water before fixing. Mud on bars must be washed off. In some areas, termites and rodents can damage formwork and cover blocks. Use durable materials. Now think about coordination. On a busy site, the steel fixer must work with the carpenter, the electrician and the plumber. Sleeves and pipes must not clash with bars. If a pipe must pass through a beam, the engineer must approve the position and any strengthening. Never cut a bar without approval. Cutting a main bar can cause a collapse. Finally, check your work before the pour. Walk the area with the drawing. Check bar size, number, spacing, laps, cover and ties. Take photographs. A photo record is proof of quality. If the engineer asks for a change, get it in writing. At the end of this lesson, you should be able to fix a pad footing, a ground beam and a column correctly, with the right cover, laps and ties, in common African ground conditions.

4. Module 6 Lesson 4: Fixing Slabs, Beams and Staircases with Speed and Quality

This lesson moves to horizontal and inclined elements: slabs, beams and staircases. These are the elements where steel fixing gangs often make the most money, because the work is repetitive and can be organised into a production line. But they are also the elements where mistakes are most visible and most expensive. Start with suspended slabs. A slab has a bottom mat and usually a top mat. The bottom mat carries tension in the middle of the span. The top mat carries tension over supports. The bars are placed in two directions. The main bars run the short way, and the distribution bars run the long way, unless the drawing says otherwise. Spacing is usually 150, 200 or 250 millimetres. Use a spacing stick or a marked tape to set out the first bar, then use a gauge to keep the rest parallel. Tie the intersections with binding wire. On large slabs, use a tie pattern that saves wire: tie every third intersection in a diagonal pattern, then tie the edges and around openings. For top mat support, use chair bars or bar chairs. Chairs keep the top mat at the correct height. A common chair is a piece of bar bent into a Z or a triangle. The chair height equals the slab thickness minus the top cover minus the bar diameter. If chairs are not used, the top mat will be pushed down during the pour, and the slab will crack. This is a common defect in African construction. Do not allow it. For beams, the sequence is bottom bars, then links, then top bars. Links must be vertical and at the correct spacing. The first link is 50 millimetres from the face of the support. The last link is also 50 millimetres from the face. At supports, the top bars must extend into the support and lap with the column bars or the adjacent beam bars. Lap lengths must follow the schedule. For long beams, bars may be lap spliced. Stagger the laps so they are not all in the same section. A common rule is to stagger laps by at least 1.3 times the lap length. For deep beams, use side bars and links as specified. For staircases, the geometry is more complex. A staircase has a waist slab, steps and sometimes a landing. The main bars run along the slope. The distribution bars run across the slope. The bars must be bent to follow the slope. Use a template or a string line to set the slope. The lap between the staircase bars and the landing bars must be correct. The cover must be maintained on the sloping soffit. Use cover blocks tied to the bars so they do not fall off during the pour. Openings in slabs, such as for stairs, lifts, pipes and ducts, need trimmer bars. Trimmer bars are extra bars around the opening. They must be fixed as per the drawing. Never cut a main bar to make an opening. If an opening is not on the drawing, get approval before cutting. Now think about productivity. A good slab gang can fix 1 to 2 tonnes of steel per day, depending on the complexity. To achieve this, organise the work: one group cuts and bends, one group carries, one group fixes. Use a crane or a hoist for heavy bundles. Do not carry bars up ladders by hand. In many African cities, labour is available, but trained steel fixers are scarce. Train your helpers to recognise bar marks and spacing. Pay by output, not just by day, where possible. But never sacrifice quality for speed. A rejected slab costs more than a slow slab. Finally, protect your work. After fixing, keep the area clean. Do not walk on the top mat without a walkway. Do not throw tools or materials onto the bars. Before the pour, check cover, laps, ties, chairs and trimmer bars. Take photos. At the end of this lesson, you should be able to fix a slab, a beam and a staircase correctly, with the right sequence, spacing, laps and chairs, and with the productivity that makes a steel fixing business profitable.

5. Module 6 Lesson 5: Quality Control, Measurement, Payment and Running a Steel Fixing Business

This final lesson brings everything together. You can fix steel well, but if you cannot measure it, claim it and get paid, you do not have a business. In this lesson we cover quality control, measurement, payment and the practical steps to run a steel fixing enterprise in Africa. Start with quality control. Quality control means checking your own work before someone else checks it. Use a simple checklist for every element. Check bar diameter, bar mark, number of bars, spacing, laps, cover, ties, chairs, hooks and cleanliness. Use a tape, a cover meter if available, and a visual check. Keep a site diary. Record the date, the element, the gang size, the weather, the work done and any instructions received. If the engineer gives a verbal instruction, write it down and ask for a signature. A diary is evidence in a dispute. Next, measurement. Steel fixing is usually measured by weight, not by length. The weight of a bar is calculated from its diameter. A common formula is weight in kilograms per metre equals diameter squared divided by 162. For example, a 12 millimetre bar weighs 144 divided by 162, which is 0.888 kilograms per metre. A 16 millimetre bar weighs 256 divided by 162, which is 1.58 kilograms per metre. A 20 millimetre bar weighs 400 divided by 162, which is 2.47 kilograms per metre. A 25 millimetre bar weighs 625 divided by 162, which is 3.86 kilograms per metre. A 32 millimetre bar weighs 1024 divided by 162, which is 6.32 kilograms per metre. Use these figures to estimate and to claim. But remember that the actual weight may vary slightly. For payment, most contracts use the bar bending schedule weight, not the actual cut weight. Some contracts pay for laps, some do not. Some pay for chairs and spacers, some do not. Read the contract. If there is no contract, agree in writing before you start. A simple agreement should state the rate per tonne, the scope, the payment terms, the retention, the programme and who provides what. In many African countries, payment delays are common. Protect yourself. Ask for a mobilisation payment. Submit your valuation on time. Keep copies of delivery notes, fabrication logs and site instructions. If you are a subcontractor, know the main contractor's payment cycle. Do not finance the main contractor for months. If payment is late, stop work after giving notice, as allowed by the contract. Now think about costing. Your cost includes labour, tools, consumables, transport, safety equipment, insurance, taxes and overhead. Binding wire, cutting discs, cover blocks and paint pens are consumables. They can be 5 to 10 percent of your cost. Do not forget them. Labour is usually the biggest cost. Pay your workers fairly and on time. A trained gang is more productive than a cheap gang. Invest in training. Teach your helpers to read a schedule and to use a tape. A helper who can set out is worth more than a helper who only carries. Next, business development. Register your business. Get a tax identification number. Open a bank account. Keep records. Use a simple book or a phone app to record income and expenses. Separate business money from personal money. Build relationships with engineers, contractors and suppliers. Deliver on time. Answer your phone. Keep your word. In Africa, reputation travels fast. A good steel fixer is recommended. A bad one is blacklisted. Finally, plan for growth. Start with small jobs: residential slabs, fences, water tanks, small shops. Then move to commercial and industrial work. Buy your own cutting and bending machines when you can. Hire them out when you are not using them. Train apprentices. Document your methods. Aim to move from a labour-only gang to a labour-and-material subcontractor. That is where the real money is. At the end of this module, you should be able to fix steel to a professional standard, measure your work, claim your money, and run a steel fixing business that survives and grows in the African construction market.

<h3>3. Site Tolerances and Checks</h3>

<p>Clients and engineers reject work for small errors that could have been caught early. The following checks should be done before every pour. They are based on the general tolerance and inspection principles in FAO/ILO/UNIDO construction guidance and typical national building code requirements.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Cover:</strong> Use spacers/chairs at the specified spacing. Check cover at columns, beams, slabs and foundations. Typical cover: 2540 mm for internal work, 4050 mm for foundations and external work, and more in aggressive soils or coastal areas.</li>
<li><strong>Bar spacing:</strong> Check against the drawing at the start, middle and end of each element. Spacing errors are common at corners and openings.</li>
<li><strong>Lap lengths:</strong> Confirm lap length and stagger laps where required. Do not cluster laps in one section unless the drawing allows it.</li>
<li><strong>Links/stirrups:</strong> Check spacing, hook direction and that links are tight and correctly positioned.</li>
<li><strong>Cleanliness:</strong> Remove mud, oil, loose rust and debris before pouring. In African ground conditions, mud and termite soil on bars is common clean it off.</li>
<li><strong>Stability:</strong> Ensure the cage is braced and will not move during pouring. Vibration can shift unbraced bars.</li>
</ul>

<div style="border:2px solid #c0392b; background:#fdecea; padding:14px 16px; margin:18px 0; border-radius:6px;">
<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0; font-weight:bold; color:#c0392b; font-size:16px;"> SAFETY WARNING HEALTH, CHEMICALS AND PHYSICAL HAZARDS</p>
<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0; color:#222;">Steel fixing exposes workers to serious health and safety hazards. Rebar is heavy, sharp and can cause deep puncture wounds, cuts and tetanus. Bending and cutting machines can crush fingers and hands. Dust from cutting and grinding can damage lungs. Some sites use chemical release agents, form oils and epoxy coatings that irritate skin and eyes.</p>
<ul style="margin:0; padding-left:20px; color:#222;">
<li>Wear gloves, safety boots, eye protection and a dust mask when cutting or grinding.</li>
<li>Keep tetanus vaccination up to date puncture wounds from rebar are a real risk.</li>
<li>Never stand in line with a bar under tension or being cut; bars can whip and strike.</li>
<li>Isolate and guard bending machines; only trained operators should use them.</li>
<li>Store chemicals (release agents, epoxy, solvents) away from food and water, and follow the supplier's safety data sheet.</li>
<li>On hot sites, manage heat stress: shade, water, rest breaks and electrolytes. Heat stroke kills.</li>
<li>Report all cuts, puncture wounds and eye injuries immediately and get medical attention.</li>
</ul>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#222; font-size:13px;">Sources: WHO occupational health guidance and ILO construction safety and health standards.</p>
</div>

<h3>4. Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ol>
<li><strong>Drawings and schedule:</strong> Confirm you have the latest revision and a bar bending schedule before cutting any steel.</li>
<li><strong>Setting out:</strong> Check grid lines, levels and column positions against the drawing before fixing starts.</li>
<li><strong>Bar identification:</strong> Sort and label bars by diameter and mark to avoid mixing sizes on site.</li>
<li><strong>Cutting list:</strong> Plan cuts to minimise offcuts; keep a reuse bin for short lengths.</li>
<li><strong>Cover and spacers:</strong> Fix spacers/chairs at the specified spacing and check cover before pouring.</li>
<li><strong>Laps and hooks:</strong> Verify lap lengths, stagger and hook dimensions against the drawing.</li>
<li><strong>Stability and cleanliness:</strong> Brace cages, remove mud and debris, and protect bars from contamination.</li>
<li><strong>Record and measure:</strong> Photograph completed work, record measurements and get sign-off for payment.</li>
</ol>

<h3>5. Running the Business Payment and Pricing Notes</h3>

<p>In most African markets steel fixing is paid either by weight fixed (per kg or per tonne), by element (per column, per square metre of slab), or by day rate. Each has risks. Weight-based payment rewards speed but can punish you if you are slow. Element-based payment rewards efficiency but requires accurate estimating. Day rate is safe but caps your income.</p>

<p>Whichever model you use, protect yourself with documentation: a signed measurement sheet, dated photographs, delivery notes for steel, and a simple invoice showing quantity, rate and total. Where possible, agree a mobilisation payment and stage payments. Cash flow kills small contractors faster than bad workmanship.</p>

<p>Finally, build a reputation for clean, accurate work. On African sites, engineers and main contractors re-hire fixers who make their inspections easy. That reputation is worth more than any single job.</p>
