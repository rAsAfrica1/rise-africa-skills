1. Soil Testing and Analysis

Soil testing is the first step to growing healthy citrus. Without it, you are guessing what your trees need. In Africa, many soils are old and weathered, often lacking nitrogen, phosphorus, and organic matter. A soil test tells you exactly what is missing and what is too much. You can get a soil test done at a government agricultural office, a university lab, or a private company. In Kenya, for example, the Kenya Agricultural and Livestock Research Organisation (KALRO) offers soil testing services. In Nigeria, you can contact the Soil Science Society of Nigeria or local agricultural development programmes. The cost is usually between $10 and $30 per sample.

To take a good sample, use a clean bucket and a soil auger or a spade. Walk in a zigzag pattern across your orchard. Collect soil from 15 to 20 spots at a depth of 0 to 20 cm, avoiding areas near trees, fences, or where you have applied fertilizer recently. Mix all the soil in the bucket, then take about 500 grams (one pound) of the mixed soil and put it in a clean bag. Label it with your name, farm, and the date. Send it to the lab.

The lab will test for soil pH, organic matter, nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), potassium (K), calcium, magnesium, and micronutrients like zinc and boron. For citrus, the ideal pH is between 5.5 and 6.5. If your pH is below 5.0, the soil is too acidic; you may need to add lime. If it is above 7.0, it is too alkaline; you may need to add gypsum or elemental sulfur. The lab report will give you recommendations for fertilizer. For example, if your soil test shows low phosphorus (below 10 ppm), you might need to apply 200 kg of triple superphosphate per hectare. Always follow the lab's advice, but also consider your budget and local availability of fertilizers.

Soil testing should be done every two to three years. If you are planting a new orchard, test before planting so you can correct problems early. Keep records of your test results and what you applied. This helps you track changes and make better decisions. Remember, a soil test is an investment, not an expense. It saves you money by preventing over-fertilization and poor yields.

2. Fertilizer Types and Application Methods

Citrus trees need regular feeding to produce good fruit. Fertilizers come in many forms: inorganic (mineral) and organic (manure, compost). In Africa, common inorganic fertilizers include urea (46% N), ammonium nitrate (34% N), triple superphosphate (46% P2O5), and muriate of potash (60% K2O). These are sold in 50 kg bags. Organic fertilizers like poultry manure, cattle manure, and compost are also valuable. They add nutrients and improve soil structure.

The amount of fertilizer you apply depends on the age of the tree and the soil test results. For a young tree (1-3 years), apply 100-200 grams of nitrogen per year, split into two or three applications. For a mature fruiting tree (5+ years), apply 500-1000 grams of nitrogen per year, plus phosphorus and potassium. A common recommendation in Kenya is to apply 500 g of NPK 17:17:17 per mature tree per year, split into two doses. But always adjust based on your soil test.

Application methods matter. Broadcasting fertilizer on the surface is easy but less effective because some nutrients are lost to the air or runoff. For citrus, the best method is to apply fertilizer in a ring around the tree, at the drip line (the edge of the canopy). Dig a shallow trench 5-10 cm deep, spread the fertilizer evenly, then cover with soil. This puts nutrients where the roots can reach them. For young trees, apply in a circle 30-60 cm from the trunk. Never put fertilizer directly against the trunk; it can burn the bark.

Timing is also important. Apply fertilizer at the start of the rainy season, when roots are actively growing. In East Africa, this is usually March-April and September-October. Avoid applying during heavy rains, as nutrients can wash away. For organic manure, apply 10-20 kg per mature tree per year, spread under the canopy. Manure should be well-rotted to avoid burning roots and to kill weed seeds. You can also use foliar sprays for quick correction of deficiencies, but they are not a substitute for soil application. Always read the label and use protective gear when handling fertilizers.

3. Micronutrient Deficiencies and Corrections

Citrus trees need small amounts of micronutrients like zinc, boron, iron, manganese, and copper. In African soils, zinc and boron deficiencies are very common, especially in sandy or highly weathered soils. A deficiency can reduce fruit yield and quality. You can identify deficiencies by looking at the leaves. Zinc deficiency causes small, narrow leaves with yellow patches between green veins (mottled). New leaves are most affected. Boron deficiency causes thick, brittle leaves, and fruit may be lumpy or have gum pockets. Iron deficiency shows as yellow leaves with green veins, starting on new leaves. Manganese deficiency looks similar but affects older leaves first.

To correct zinc deficiency, spray the leaves with zinc sulfate. Mix 5 grams of zinc sulfate per liter of water (about 0.5%) and spray the canopy until it drips. Do this 2-3 times per year, especially during flushing. You can also apply zinc sulfate to the soil at 50-100 grams per mature tree. For boron deficiency, spray with borax or boric acid at 1-2 grams per liter. Be careful: too much boron is toxic. Do not exceed 2 grams per liter. For iron deficiency, spray with ferrous sulfate at 5 grams per liter. In alkaline soils, iron is less available, so foliar sprays are better than soil application. For manganese, spray with manganese sulfate at 2-5 grams per liter.

Before spraying, confirm the deficiency with a leaf tissue test. Collect leaves from the middle of the canopy, 4-6 months old, from non-fruiting branches. Send them to a lab. The lab will tell you the exact nutrient levels. For example, zinc should be 25-100 ppm in dry leaf tissue. If it is below 25 ppm, you need to act. In many African countries, micronutrient fertilizers are available as pre-mixed foliar sprays. For example, in South Africa, products like 'Zinc Plus' or 'Boron Plus' are common. In Kenya, you can find 'Masterfol' or 'Agro-Zinc'. Always follow the label instructions.

Prevention is better than cure. Keep soil pH in the right range (5.5-6.5) because micronutrients are most available at that pH. Add organic matter regularly; it helps chelate micronutrients and makes them available to roots. Avoid over-liming, which can cause iron and manganese deficiencies. If you see symptoms, act quickly. A timely spray can save your crop.

4. Organic Matter and Composting

Organic matter is the backbone of healthy soil. It improves soil structure, holds water, and feeds beneficial microbes. In Africa, many soils are low in organic matter (below 1%). Citrus trees grow best when organic matter is 2-3% or higher. You can increase organic matter by adding compost, manure, or mulch. Compost is made from plant residues, animal manure, and kitchen waste. It is cheap and easy to make on the farm.

To make compost, choose a shaded area near your orchard. Build a pile 1.5 meters wide and 1 meter high. Use alternating layers: 15 cm of dry materials (maize stalks, straw, dry leaves) and 10 cm of green materials (grass clippings, kitchen waste, fresh manure). Add a handful of wood ash or a little soil to each layer. Keep the pile as moist as a wrung-out sponge. Turn the pile every two weeks to add air. In 2-3 months, the compost will be dark, crumbly, and smell earthy. You can also use a compost pit or a bin. A pile of 1 cubic meter will produce about 300-500 kg of compost.

Apply compost under the canopy of each tree, 10-20 kg per mature tree per year. Spread it evenly from the trunk to the drip line, but keep it 10 cm away from the trunk to prevent rot. You can also use compost as a mulch, which suppresses weeds and conserves moisture. In dry areas, mulch is very important. Use dry grass, straw, or banana leaves as mulch, 5-10 cm thick. Do not use fresh manure as mulch; it can burn roots and attract flies.

Another way to add organic matter is to grow cover crops and green manures (see next lesson). You can also apply vermicompost (worm castings) or compost tea. Compost tea is made by soaking compost in water for 24-48 hours, then straining and spraying on leaves or soil. It adds beneficial microbes. In Kenya, some farmers use 'bokashi' compost, which is fermented with effective microorganisms. Whatever method you choose, remember that organic matter is a long-term investment. It takes time to build, but the benefits last for years.

5. Cover Crops and Green Manures

Cover crops are plants grown to cover the soil, not for harvest. Green manures are cover crops that are ploughed into the soil to add nutrients. For citrus orchards, cover crops are very useful. They reduce erosion, suppress weeds, improve soil structure, and add organic matter. In Africa, good cover crops include cowpea, lablab, pigeon pea, sunhemp, and mucuna. These are legumes that fix nitrogen from the air. Grasses like sorghum, millet, or rye can also be used, but they do not fix nitrogen.

To plant a cover crop, clear the weeds and broadcast the seed at the start of the rainy season. For cowpea, use 20-30 kg of seed per hectare. For lablab, 25-30 kg per hectare. Rake the soil lightly to cover the seed. The cover crop will grow between the trees. When it flowers or before it sets seed, cut it down and leave it on the surface as mulch, or plough it into the soil. If you plough it in, wait 2-3 weeks before planting or fertilizing to allow decomposition. Green manure crops can add 50-100 kg of nitrogen per hectare. That is like applying 100-200 kg of urea.

Cover crops also help with pest management. Some, like marigold, repel nematodes. Others, like cowpea, attract beneficial insects. In citrus, nematodes are a big problem. Marigold (Tagetes spp.) can be planted as a cover crop to reduce nematode populations. You can also use cover crops to improve soil aeration. Deep-rooted crops like pigeon pea break up hardpan. After harvest, their roots decay and create channels for water and air.

One caution: do not let cover crops compete with young citrus trees for water and nutrients. Keep a clear area of at least 1 meter around the trunk. For mature trees, competition is less of a problem. In dry areas, cover crops may use too much water. In that case, use mulch instead. You can also grow cover crops only during the rainy season and then terminate them. If you use a legume cover crop, you may need to inoculate the seed with rhizobium bacteria before planting. This ensures good nitrogen fixation. Inoculants are available from agricultural suppliers. Always choose cover crops that are adapted to your region and rainfall pattern.

6. Fertigation Principles

Fertigation is the application of fertilizers through the irrigation system. It is common in commercial citrus production, especially where drip irrigation is used. Fertigation saves labour, reduces fertilizer waste, and delivers nutrients directly to the root zone. In Africa, fertigation is used in South Africa, Morocco, and Egypt, and increasingly in Kenya and Nigeria. To use fertigation, you need a drip irrigation system, a fertilizer tank, and soluble fertilizers. Common soluble fertilizers include potassium nitrate, calcium nitrate, and monoammonium phosphate (MAP).

The first principle is to choose the right fertilizers. Not all fertilizers are soluble. Urea is highly soluble, but triple superphosphate is not. For fertigation, use fertilizers that dissolve completely and do not clog emitters. You also need to consider compatibility. For example, calcium nitrate and phosphate fertilizers should not be mixed in the same tank because they form a precipitate (calcium phosphate) that clogs drippers. Instead, inject them separately or at different times. A good practice is to inject calcium nitrate first, then flush the lines, then inject phosphate.

The second principle is to calculate the right concentration. Too much fertilizer can burn roots; too little is ineffective. A common rule is to apply 100-200 ppm of nitrogen in the irrigation water. For a mature citrus tree, you might apply 500-1000 grams of nitrogen per year, split into weekly or biweekly doses during the growing season. For example, if you irrigate 1000 liters per tree per week, and you want 150 ppm N, you need 150 grams of N per 1000 liters. If using calcium nitrate (15.5% N), you need about 970 grams of calcium nitrate per 1000 liters. Always check the label for nutrient content.

The third principle is timing. Fertigate when the tree needs nutrients most: during flowering, fruit set, and fruit enlargement. In the rainy season, reduce fertigation because rain provides some nutrients and can leach them away. In the dry season, fertigation is very effective. Always start with a short irrigation cycle to wet the soil, then inject fertilizer, then flush with clean water for 10-15 minutes to prevent clogging. Monitor your system regularly. Check emitters for clogging and clean filters. Use a pressure gauge to ensure proper pressure. If you are new to fertigation, start with a small area and test. You can also use a simple venturi injector, which is cheap and works with low pressure. With practice, fertigation can increase yields by 20-30% compared to broadcasting.

<h2>Practical Reference: Soil &amp; Nutrient Management for Citrus in Africa</h2>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the six lessons in this module into field-ready guidance for citrus growers, farm managers and agribusiness operators across Africa. It is written for commercial decision-making: every recommendation is framed around yield response, input cost, and return on investment. Citrus in Africa whether sweet orange, mandarin, lemon or lime is a long-term perennial investment. A tree that is well-nourished from planting produces marketable fruit for 2030 years; a tree that is mismanaged in its first five years never fully recovers its yield potential. Soil and nutrient management is therefore not an agronomic afterthought but a core business function.</p>

<p>African citrus soils vary enormously: deep weathered loams in the Eastern Cape and Mpumalanga, sandy coastal soils in Kenya and Tanzania, heavy vertisols in Sudan and parts of Nigeria, and volcanic-influenced soils in the East African highlands. What unites them is that most are acidic, low in organic matter, and depleted in one or more micronutrients particularly zinc, boron and manganese. Fertilizer is typically the second-largest input cost after labour. Getting it right is the single highest-leverage agronomic decision a citrus business makes.</p>

<h3>1. Soil Testing and Analysis the foundation of every fertilizer budget</h3>

<p>Never buy fertilizer before you have a soil test. A composite soil sample taken from 2030 points per management block, at 030 cm depth under the canopy edge, costs the equivalent of a few bags of fertilizer but can save 2040% of your annual input bill. In Africa, accredited laboratories include the Agricultural Research Council (ARC) in South Africa, the Kenya Agricultural and Livestock Research Organisation (KALRO), and the Soil Science laboratories at Makerere, Sokoine and Ahmadu Bello universities. Turnaround is typically 24 weeks.</p>

<p>Interpret results against local calibrations, not generic global tables. For citrus, target soil pH (water) of 5.56.5; below 5.0, aluminium toxicity stunts roots and phosphorus becomes unavailable. Above 7.0, iron, zinc and manganese lock up. Leaf analysis is the more sensitive tool for micronutrients and should be done annually in MarchApril (autumn flush) on 46 month-old leaves from non-fruiting terminals.</p>

<h3>2. Fertilizer Types and Application Methods</h3>

<p>Citrus responds to nitrogen more than any other nutrient, but nitrogen without adequate potassium produces large, low-juice, poorly coloured fruit. Split nitrogen into 34 applications across the growing season to match root activity and reduce leaching critical on sandy soils across coastal Kenya, Tanzania and Mozambique.</p>

<p>Application methods matter as much as the product. Broadcasting is cheap but inefficient on slopes and under drip. Band placement under the canopy edge where the feeder roots are concentrates nutrients where uptake occurs. Fertigation through drip delivers nutrients in small, frequent doses directly to the root zone, achieving 2030% higher nutrient use efficiency than broadcasting. For a commercial orchard, the choice is a business decision: broadcasting has low capital cost but higher recurring fertilizer cost; fertigation requires investment in a dosing system but reduces fertilizer spend per tonne of fruit over the life of the orchard.</p>

<h3>3. Micronutrient Deficiencies and Corrections</h3>

<p>Micronutrient deficiencies are the most common cause of unmarketable citrus in Africa and the most commonly misdiagnosed. Zinc deficiency produces "little leaf" and mottled interveinal chlorosis; boron deficiency causes corky veins, lumpy fruit and gum pockets; manganese deficiency gives a dull, pale interveinal pattern with green veins; iron deficiency (common on calcareous soils in North and West Africa) produces a striking yellow leaf with a green "herringbone" vein pattern. Correction is by foliar spray soil application of micronutrients is usually ineffective because they are fixed in the soil.</p>

<h3>4. Organic Matter and Composting</h3>

<p>Most African citrus soils sit below 1% organic matter. Raising it to 23% improves water-holding capacity, cation exchange, and micronutrient availability and reduces fertilizer requirement over time. On-farm composting using citrus pulp, cattle or poultry manure, and crop residues is the lowest-cost route. Apply 1020 kg of mature compost per tree per year, spread under the canopy and lightly incorporated or mulched. Compost must be fully mature (C:N below 20:1, no ammonia smell) before use, otherwise it will rob nitrogen from the tree.</p>

<h3>5. Cover Crops and Green Manures</h3>

<p>In the inter-row, legume cover crops such as cowpea, lablab, vetch and jack bean fix 40120 kg N/ha/year and suppress weeds. Non-legumes such as rye, oats and buckwheat scavenge residual nitrogen and reduce leaching. In African orchards, cover crops also reduce soil temperature, conserve moisture in dryland production, and provide habitat for beneficial insects. Mow and leave residues as mulch; do not plough deeply, as citrus feeder roots are shallow.</p>

<h3>6. Fertigation Principles</h3>

<p>Fertigation is the highest-precision tool in citrus nutrition. Key rules: use only fully soluble fertilizers (potassium nitrate, calcium nitrate, urea, MKP); maintain the solution EC between 1.0 and 2.0 mS/cm for mature trees; inject in the middle third of the irrigation cycle and flush with clear water afterwards to protect emitters; and never mix calcium with phosphate or sulphate fertilizers in the same tank they precipitate and block drippers. On sandy soils, daily or alternate-day fertigation with 3050% of the seasonal N and K budget is now standard practice in South Africa, Morocco and increasingly in Kenya and Tanzania.</p>

<p style="margin:0;color:#b71c1c;font-weight:bold;font-size:1.05em;"> SAFETY WARNING Fertilizer handling and storage</p>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#7f0000;">Calcium nitrate and ammonium nitrate are oxidising agents and can cause fire or explosion if contaminated with fuel, oil or organic matter, or if stored in bulk in a hot, closed store. Store fertilizers in a dry, ventilated, lockable store away from fuel, pesticides and animal feed. Never smoke or weld near fertilizer. Wear gloves, eye protection and a dust mask when handling powders. Boron products are toxic if ingested keep away from children and animals, and never exceed the recommended foliar rate, as boron toxicity (leaf tip burn, dieback) is far harder to correct than deficiency. Dispose of fertilizer bags and rinse water responsibly; do not allow runoff into streams or drinking water sources.</p>

<li>Take a composite soil sample from each management block every 1224 months, plus annual leaf analysis in autumn.</li>
<li>Maintain soil pH at 5.56.5; lime on the basis of test results, not habit.</li>
<li>Split nitrogen into 34 applications across the season to match root activity and reduce leaching losses.</li>
<li>Match potassium to crop load under-supply causes small, low-juice fruit; over-supply blocks magnesium.</li>
<li>Spray micronutrients (Zn, B, Mn) to the leaf; do not rely on soil application for correction.</li>
<li>Apply 1020 kg mature compost per tree per year and maintain a legume cover crop in the inter-row.</li>
<li>If fertigating, keep solution EC 1.02.0 mS/cm, never mix calcium with phosphate or sulphate, and flush lines after each injection.</li>
<li>Keep written records of every application, cost and yield response this is the only way to know what actually pays on your farm.</li>
</ul>
