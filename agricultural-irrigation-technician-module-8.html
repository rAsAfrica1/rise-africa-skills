1. Lesson 1: Introduction to Irrigation Systems in African Agriculture

Welcome to Module 8 of the Agricultural Irrigation Technician training. This lesson introduces you to the fundamental concepts of irrigation systems as they apply to smallholder and commercial farms across Africa. Irrigation is the artificial application of water to soil to supply moisture essential for plant growth. In Africa, where rainfall is often erratic and climate change intensifies droughts, irrigation is not a luxury but a necessity for food security and income generation. As a technician, your role is to design, install, maintain, and repair irrigation systems that are appropriate for local conditions. You must understand the water needs of different crops, the soil types in your region, and the available water sources such as rivers, boreholes, wells, and dams. The first step in any irrigation project is assessing the water source. You need to measure the flow rate, usually in litres per second or cubic metres per hour, and check water quality for salinity and contaminants. Next, evaluate the soil. Sandy soils drain quickly and need frequent, small applications. Clay soils hold water but drain slowly, so you must avoid waterlogging. Loamy soils are ideal. You also need to know the crop water requirement, which depends on the crop type, growth stage, and local evapotranspiration rates. In Africa, common irrigated crops include maize, rice, vegetables, sugarcane, and cotton. Each has different water needs. For example, rice requires standing water, while tomatoes need consistent moisture but not waterlogging. Irrigation systems are broadly classified into surface, sprinkler, and drip irrigation. Surface irrigation includes furrow, basin, and border methods. It is cheap but inefficient, often losing 40 to 60 percent of water to runoff and evaporation. Sprinkler irrigation mimics rainfall and is suitable for cereals and pastures. Drip irrigation delivers water directly to the root zone and can achieve 90 percent efficiency, making it ideal for water-scarce areas and high-value crops. As a technician, you must also consider energy sources. Many African farms lack reliable electricity, so solar-powered pumps are increasingly popular. Diesel and petrol pumps are common but costly to run. Manual pumps like treadle pumps are suitable for very small plots. Your business as an irrigation technician depends on solving real problems. Farmers will pay for systems that increase yields, reduce labour, and save water. You must be able to calculate the cost of a system versus the expected increase in income. For example, a drip kit for a quarter-acre vegetable plot might cost 200 to 500 US dollars, but it can double yields and allow year-round production. That is a strong return on investment. You also need to understand water rights and regulations in your country. Some areas require permits for boreholes or river abstraction. Always advise farmers to comply with local laws. Finally, remember that irrigation is not just about pipes and pumps. It is about managing water as a resource. You must train farmers on scheduling, maintenance, and monitoring. A well-designed system that is poorly managed will fail. In this module, you will learn how to select, install, and maintain different irrigation systems. You will also learn how to troubleshoot common problems such as clogged emitters, leaky pipes, and pump failures. By the end of this module, you should be able to work as a competent irrigation technician who can serve farmers in your community. Let us begin with the components of a typical irrigation system. Every system has a water source, a pump or gravity feed, a mainline, sub-mains, laterals, and emitters or sprinklers. You also need valves, filters, pressure gauges, and fittings. For drip systems, filters are critical because emitters are easily clogged by sand, silt, and organic matter. For sprinklers, you need proper spacing and pressure to ensure uniform coverage. As you go through the lessons, keep in mind the African context: limited capital, unreliable power, diverse soils, and a need for low-cost, durable solutions. Your success will be measured by the farmers who increase their harvests and incomes because of your work. So pay attention to details, practice calculations, and always think about the practical challenges on the ground. This lesson has given you an overview. In the next lesson, we will dive into water sources and pumping systems.

2. Lesson 2: Water Sources and Pumping Systems for African Farms

In this lesson, we focus on water sources and pumping systems, which are the heart of any irrigation setup. Africa has diverse water sources: rivers, streams, lakes, springs, boreholes, wells, and rainwater harvesting. Your first task is to assess the reliability and quantity of the source. A river may flow year-round or seasonally. A borehole may yield enough for a small drip system but not for sprinklers. You must measure the yield. For a borehole, conduct a pump test: pump water for several hours and record the drawdown and recovery rate. For a river, measure the cross-sectional area and velocity to calculate flow. Never design a system that demands more water than the source can sustainably provide. Next, consider water quality. Test for salinity, pH, and specific ions like sodium and chloride. High salinity can damage crops and clog emitters. In coastal areas, saltwater intrusion is a risk. Also test for iron and manganese, which can cause staining and clogging. If water is dirty, you need filtration or settling ponds. Now, pumping systems. The choice of pump depends on the water source, the total head (the height the water must be lifted plus friction losses), and the required flow rate. There are two main types: surface pumps and submersible pumps. Surface pumps sit above the water and use suction. They can lift water up to about 7 metres at sea level, less at higher altitudes. Submersible pumps are placed inside the water, usually in a borehole or well, and can lift water from great depths. They are more efficient and reliable for deep sources. In Africa, solar-powered submersible pumps are transforming irrigation. A typical solar pump system includes solar panels, a controller, a pump, and a storage tank. The advantage is zero fuel cost and low maintenance. The disadvantage is high initial cost and variable output depending on sunlight. You must size the solar array correctly. For example, to pump 10,000 litres per day from a 20-metre depth, you might need 500 to 800 watts of panels, depending on location. Diesel and petrol pumps are still common. They are cheaper to buy but expensive to run. A diesel pump might consume 1 to 2 litres of fuel per hour, costing 1 to 2 US dollars. Over a season, that adds up. You should advise farmers to compare lifecycle costs. Often, solar pays back in 1 to 2 years. Manual pumps like treadle pumps are suitable for very small plots, up to 0.2 hectares. They are cheap, easy to maintain, and do not require fuel. However, they are labour-intensive. For community schemes, you might consider windmills, but they are less common now. When installing a pump, ensure proper priming for surface pumps. Protect the pump from sand and debris with a screen or filter. Use the correct pipe diameter to minimise friction loss. A common mistake is using a pipe that is too small, which increases pressure loss and reduces flow. You also need a pressure tank or a storage tank to buffer demand. Storage tanks are essential for solar systems because they store water for cloudy periods. Size the tank for at least one day of water requirement. For a 1-hectare vegetable farm needing 30,000 litres per day, a 30,000-litre tank is ideal. But many farmers start with smaller tanks and expand. You must also consider the energy source for the pump. If grid electricity is available, use it. But in rural Africa, grid power is often unreliable. So hybrid systems with solar and grid or generator backup are practical. Now, let us talk about installation. For a borehole, you need a casing, a screen, and a gravel pack. The submersible pump is lowered on a riser pipe with a safety rope. The electrical cable must be waterproof and secured. The control box should be in a dry, shaded place. For a river or stream, you need a intake structure to prevent debris from entering. A simple gravel and sand filter bed can work. You also need a settling tank. Always install a foot valve to keep the suction line primed. For solar pumps, mount panels facing the equator (north in the southern hemisphere, south in the northern hemisphere) at an angle equal to your latitude. Use a tracking system if affordable, but fixed mounts are fine. Keep panels clean; dust reduces output by up to 20 percent. Maintenance is critical. Check pump impellers for wear. Clean filters. Inspect wiring for corrosion. In Africa, rodents and insects can damage cables and pipes. Use conduits and mesh. Train farmers to check the system daily. A simple logbook of pump hours, fuel use, and water output helps detect problems early. Finally, consider the cost. A solar pump system for a 1-hectare farm might cost 1,500 to 3,000 US dollars. A diesel system might cost 500 to 1,000 US dollars but with high running costs. A treadle pump costs 50 to 150 US dollars. You must help farmers choose based on their budget, water source, and crop value. High-value crops like vegetables can justify solar. Low-value staples may need cheaper options. In the next lesson, we will cover distribution systems: pipes, valves, and emitters.

3. Lesson 3: Designing and Installing Distribution Systems

Now that you understand water sources and pumps, we move to distribution systems. These are the pipes, fittings, valves, and emitters that deliver water from the pump to the plants. A well-designed distribution system ensures uniform water application, minimises losses, and is easy to maintain. The first step is to map the field. Draw a sketch showing the water source, pump location, and the layout of crops. Decide on the irrigation method: surface, sprinkler, or drip. For each method, the distribution design differs. For surface irrigation, you need to grade the field and create furrows or basins. Water flows by gravity. You must ensure the slope is correct: too steep causes erosion, too flat causes waterlogging. For sprinkler systems, you need to calculate the pipe sizes and sprinkler spacing. The goal is to apply water at a rate that the soil can absorb, without runoff. For drip systems, you need to design a network of mainlines, sub-mains, and laterals with emitters. Let us start with pipes. In Africa, common pipe materials are PVC, HDPE (polyethylene), and galvanised iron. PVC is rigid, cheap, and easy to glue, but it can crack if exposed to sunlight or impact. HDPE is flexible, UV-resistant, and comes in coils, making it ideal for buried or surface lines. Galvanised iron is strong but expensive and corrodes. For most smallholder systems, HDPE is the best choice. You must size pipes correctly. If pipes are too small, friction loss reduces pressure and flow. If too large, you waste money. Use the Darcy-Weisbach equation or online calculators. A simple rule: for a flow of 1 litre per second, a 25 mm pipe is good for up to 30 metres; a 32 mm pipe for up to 60 metres. But always check. Friction loss also depends on fittings. Each elbow, tee, or valve adds resistance. Keep the system as straight as possible. Now, valves. You need a main shut-off valve at the pump. Then, zone valves to control different sections. For drip systems, you need a pressure regulator and a filter. A sand filter removes organic matter; a screen filter removes fine particles. A disc filter is a good compromise. Install the filter after the pump and before the mainline. Flush valves at the end of each lateral are essential for cleaning. For sprinklers, you need a pressure gauge to ensure the correct operating pressure. Most sprinklers work at 1.5 to 3 bar. If pressure is too low, coverage is uneven. If too high, misting occurs and water is lost to wind. You can use a booster pump if needed. Now, emitters. For drip irrigation, there are inline emitters (built into the pipe) and online emitters (inserted into the pipe). Inline emitters are cheaper and easier to install. They come in different flow rates: 1, 2, 4, 8 litres per hour. Choose based on soil type. Sandy soil needs higher flow (4-8 L/h) to spread water laterally. Clay soil needs lower flow (1-2 L/h) to avoid runoff. Emitter spacing depends on crop. For tomatoes, 30 cm spacing is common. For maize, 60 cm. You must also consider the number of emitters per plant. One emitter per plant is typical for vegetables. For trees, use 2 to 4 emitters per tree. When installing drip laterals, lay them along the crop rows. Keep them on the surface or bury them 5-10 cm deep. Buried lines last longer but are harder to inspect. Surface lines are easy to check for leaks and clogging. Use stakes or clips to hold them in place. At the end of each lateral, install a flush valve. At the start, connect to the sub-main with a barbed connector and a grommet. For sprinkler systems, you need to space sprinklers according to their wetted diameter. For example, a sprinkler with a 12-metre diameter should be spaced 12 metres apart in a square pattern. But in windy areas, reduce spacing to 60 percent of diameter. Use risers to raise sprinklers above the crop. For tall crops like maize, use tall risers. For low crops, short risers are fine. Now, installation tips. Always flush the mainline and sub-mains before connecting laterals. This removes debris that could clog emitters. Use a filter during installation. When gluing PVC, use primer and cement, and hold for 30 seconds. For HDPE, use compression fittings or electrofusion. For drip, use a hole punch to insert online emitters. Do not use a nail or knife; it causes leaks. Test the system in sections. Start with the mainline, then sub-mains, then laterals. Check for leaks and pressure. Adjust the pressure regulator to the recommended setting. For sprinklers, check coverage with cups or cans. Place them in a grid and run the system for 15 minutes. Measure the water in each can. If variation is more than 20 percent, adjust spacing or pressure. Maintenance is ongoing. Flush laterals weekly. Clean filters daily or as needed. Check for clogged emitters by looking for dry spots. If clogged, remove and clean or replace. For sprinklers, check nozzles for wear and clean them. In Africa, rodents chew drip lines. Use bait stations or physical barriers. Also, train farmers to avoid stepping on pipes. Provide a simple map of the system so they know where valves and filters are. Finally, consider the cost. A drip system for 0.5 hectares might cost 300 to 800 US dollars. A sprinkler system for 1 hectare might cost 500 to 1,500 US dollars. Surface irrigation is cheapest but labour-intensive. Your design should match the farmer's budget and skills. In the next lesson, we will cover maintenance and troubleshooting.

4. Lesson 4: Maintenance and Troubleshooting of Irrigation Systems

A well-maintained irrigation system lasts for years and saves money. Poor maintenance leads to breakdowns, water waste, and crop failure. As a technician, you must teach farmers how to care for their systems and how to fix common problems. This lesson covers routine maintenance, troubleshooting, and repair techniques. Start with a maintenance schedule. Daily tasks: check the pump for unusual noise or vibration, inspect for leaks, clean the pump intake screen, and check the filter. For solar pumps, check that panels are clean and free of shade. For diesel pumps, check fuel, oil, and air filters. Weekly tasks: flush drip laterals, clean filters thoroughly, check emitter flow rates, and inspect sprinkler nozzles. Monthly tasks: check pipe connections, test valves, and inspect the pump impeller for wear. Seasonal tasks: before the dry season, do a full system check. After the season, drain pipes to prevent damage from freezing (rare in Africa but possible in highlands) and store pumps in a dry place. Now, common problems and solutions. Problem 1: Low pressure or flow. Causes: clogged filter, leak in mainline, worn pump impeller, or undersized pipes. Solution: Clean or replace filter. Find and repair leaks. Check pump impeller and replace if worn. If pipes are undersized, you may need to replace them or reduce the number of emitters per zone. Problem 2: Clogged emitters. Causes: sand, silt, algae, or chemical precipitates. Solution: Flush laterals. If that fails, remove emitters and soak in a solution of chlorine (1 part bleach to 10 parts water) for 30 minutes. For severe clogging, replace emitters. To prevent, install a good filter and flush regularly. Problem 3: Uneven water distribution. Causes: pressure differences along the line, clogged emitters, or wrong emitter spacing. Solution: Install a pressure regulator. Use pressure-compensating emitters, which maintain flow regardless of pressure. Check for clogged emitters and clean. Adjust spacing if needed. Problem 4: Pump not starting. Causes: no power, tripped breaker, faulty switch, or burnt motor. Solution: Check power source. Reset breaker. Test switch with a multimeter. If motor is burnt, replace or rewind. For solar pumps, check controller and panel connections. Problem 5: Leaking pipes. Causes: damaged pipe, loose fittings, or high pressure. Solution: Cut out damaged section and replace with a coupling. Tighten fittings. Install a pressure relief valve if pressure is too high. Problem 6: Sprinkler not rotating. Causes: worn bearings, debris in nozzle, or low pressure. Solution: Clean nozzle. Replace bearings. Increase pressure if below recommended. Problem 7: Algae growth in drip lines. Causes: sunlight and nutrients in water. Solution: Bury lines or use opaque pipes. Treat with chlorine. Problem 8: Rodent damage. Causes: rodents chewing drip lines. Solution: Use thicker pipes, set traps, or use repellents. Now, tools and spare parts. Every technician should carry a basic toolkit: pipe cutter, hole punch, screwdrivers, pliers, multimeter, pressure gauge, and Teflon tape. Spare parts: filters, emitters, connectors, valves, and a spare pump if possible. In Africa, sourcing spare parts can be difficult. So, standardise on common sizes and brands. Train farmers to keep a small stock of spares. Troubleshooting steps: First, identify the symptom. Is it low pressure, no water, or uneven coverage? Second, isolate the problem. Check the pump, then mainline, then sub-main, then laterals. Third, test components. Use a pressure gauge to check pressure at different points. Fourth, repair or replace faulty parts. Fifth, test the system again. Always keep a logbook. Record date, problem, action taken, and cost. This helps identify recurring issues. Preventive maintenance is cheaper than repairs. For example, cleaning a filter costs nothing but prevents clogged emitters that cost money to replace. Training farmers is key. Teach them to do daily checks. Show them how to flush laterals. Explain why they should not remove filters. Many farmers bypass filters because they think it slows water. But that leads to clogging. You must explain the long-term cost. Also, teach them to protect the system from animals and children. Fence the pump area. Bury pipes where possible. Use lockable valves. Now, consider the business aspect. As a technician, you can offer maintenance contracts. For a monthly fee, you visit the farm, check the system, clean filters, and fix minor issues. This provides steady income and keeps farmers loyal. You can also sell spare parts. Many farmers will pay for convenience. In Africa, trust is important. If you respond quickly to breakdowns, you will get more clients. Keep a mobile phone and be reachable. Finally, document your work. Take photos before and after repairs. Write a simple report. This helps you learn and shows professionalism. In the next lesson, we will cover business skills and customer service for irrigation technicians.

5. Lesson 5: Business Skills and Customer Service for Irrigation Technicians

You have learned the technical skills. Now, you must learn how to turn those skills into a successful business. As an agricultural irrigation technician in Africa, you can work as an independent contractor, for an NGO, for a government agency, or for a private company. But the greatest opportunity is self-employment, serving smallholder and commercial farmers in your area. This lesson covers business planning, pricing, marketing, customer service, and record-keeping. First, define your services. You can offer: system design, installation, maintenance, repair, training, and supply of parts. You can specialise in drip, sprinkler, or solar pumping. Start with what you know best. For example, if you are good at drip irrigation for vegetables, focus on that. Then expand. Next, understand your market. Who are the farmers in your area? What crops do they grow? What is their budget? Talk to them. Visit farms. Attend farmer meetings. Identify their pain points: water scarcity, high fuel costs, labour shortages. Your service solves these problems. Now, pricing. You must cover your costs and make a profit. Calculate your costs: transport, tools, labour, and materials. Add a markup of 20 to 50 percent. For installation, you can charge a fixed fee per hectare or per system. For example, installing a drip system for 0.5 hectares might take 2 days and cost 100 to 200 US dollars in labour. For maintenance, charge a monthly retainer. For a small farm, 20 to 50 US dollars per month. For repairs, charge an hourly rate plus parts. A common mistake is undercharging. If you charge too little, you cannot sustain your business. Farmers respect professionals who charge fairly. But be flexible. Offer payment plans. Many farmers have seasonal income. You can accept payment after harvest. But get a written agreement. Now, marketing. In rural Africa, word of mouth is powerful. Do good work and ask satisfied customers to refer you. Create a simple business card or flyer. Use WhatsApp to share photos of your work. Join farmer groups on social media. Partner with agro-dealers, seed companies, and NGOs. They can refer clients to you. Give free demonstrations. Set up a small drip system at a market or farm show. Let farmers see it working. Offer a free consultation. This builds trust. Customer service is critical. Respond to calls quickly. If you cannot come immediately, tell the farmer when you will come. Keep your promises. Arrive on time. Dress neatly. Speak respectfully. Listen to the farmer's concerns. Do not talk down to them. Explain technical concepts in simple language. Show them how to use the system. After installation, follow up after one week. Ask if everything is working. This small gesture creates loyalty. Handle complaints professionally. If something goes wrong, fix it at no extra cost if it is your fault. If it is the farmer's fault, explain politely and offer a solution. Never argue. Your reputation is your biggest asset. Record-keeping is essential. Keep a log of all jobs: date, client, service, parts used, cost, and payment. Keep receipts for materials. Track your income and expenses. This helps you know if you are making a profit. It also helps you get loans or grants. Use a simple notebook or a smartphone app. Many African countries have mobile money services like M-Pesa. Use them to receive payments. It is safe and convenient. Now, legal and regulatory issues. Register your business with the local authorities. Get a tax identification number. Some countries require technicians to be certified. Check with your ministry of agriculture. Insurance is also important. If you damage a farmer's property, you could be liable. Consider liability insurance if available. Now, growth strategies. As you gain experience, you can hire assistants. Train them well. You can also expand to sell irrigation kits. Buy in bulk and sell at a profit. You can offer financing options. Partner with microfinance institutions. Many farmers cannot afford a full system upfront. You can arrange a loan for them. You can also offer a lease-to-own model. Another opportunity is training. NGOs and government agencies often hire technicians to train farmers. You can charge a daily rate. You can also create a demonstration farm. This shows your skills and attracts clients. Finally, think about the future. Climate change is making irrigation more important. Governments and donors are investing in irrigation. Your skills are in demand. Keep learning. Attend workshops. Read technical manuals. Join professional associations. The more you know, the more you can charge. Remember, your business is not just about pipes and pumps. It is about helping farmers grow more food, earn more income, and improve their lives. When you do that, you succeed. This concludes Module 8. You now have the knowledge to start or improve your career as an agricultural irrigation technician. Practice your skills, be honest, and always put the farmer first. Good luck.

6. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Field Data, Calculations and Compliance for Irrigation Technicians in Africa

This practical reference lesson supports the rise AFRICA Agricultural Irrigation Technician course. It is the module you keep in the truck, on the phone, or taped inside the pump shed. Everything here is designed to help a working technician quote faster, install correctly, diagnose faults without guesswork, and protect the customer's investment. In African smallholder and commercial settings, margins are thin and water is often the single largest input cost. A technician who can produce reliable numbers on the spot wins repeat business, referrals, and service contracts. This lesson therefore combines field calculations, real reference data from FAO and other official sources, and a business-first mindset: every measurement you take should either reduce risk, reduce cost, or increase yield for the client.

1. Core Field Calculations Every Technician Must Master

Before quoting a job, you need four numbers: the crop water requirement, the available flow rate, the total dynamic head, and the system application rate. Get these wrong and the customer either drowns the crop, burns the pump, or pays for a system that cannot keep up in the dry season.

Crop water requirement. Reference evapotranspiration (ETo) varies hugely across Africa. The FAO Irrigation and Drainage Paper 56 remains the standard method for calculating crop water needs using the Penman-Monteith equation. For a quick field estimate, multiply ETo by the crop coefficient (Kc) for the growth stage. In the Sahel, peak ETo can exceed 7 mm/day; in the highlands of Ethiopia or Kenya it may be 3â4 mm/day. A drip-irrigated tomato crop at mid-season (Kc â 1.15) in a 6 mm/day zone needs roughly 6.9 mm/day, which is about 69 mÂ³ per hectare per day.

Flow rate. Measure, never assume. Use a bucket-and-stopwatch test for small systems or an inline flow meter for larger ones. Flow is volume divided by time. If a 20-litre bucket fills in 12 seconds, flow is 20 Ã· 12 = 1.67 L/s, or about 6 mÂ³/hour. Always test at the actual operating pressure, not at the pump outlet with no back-pressure.

Total dynamic head (TDH). TDH = static lift + friction losses + pressure required at the emitter. Friction losses in pipes are typically 10â20% of static head for well-designed systems, but can exceed 40% with undersized or fouled pipes. Undersizing pipe to save money is the most common cause of poor performance we see in the field.

Application rate. Match the system's precipitation rate to the soil's infiltration rate. If you apply water faster than the soil can absorb it, you get runoff, erosion, and wasted pumping energy. Sandy soils may take 20â30 mm/hour; heavy clay may take only 4â8 mm/hour.

3. Business-First Application: Turning Data into Quotes and Contracts

Data is not just technical; it is commercial. When you can show a farmer that a correctly sized drip system will use 40% less water than furrow irrigation for the same tomato yield, you are not selling pipe â you are selling profit. Use the tables above to build a simple one-page quotation that includes:

  • Water demand estimate in mÂ³/day and mÂ³/season, based on Table 1.
  • Pump sizing with TDH calculation and a 10â15% safety margin.
  • Pipe sizing with friction loss shown explicitly so the client sees why you did not undersize.
  • Filtration specification matched to the water source: sand media for river water, screen or disc filters for boreholes with low solids.
  • Service schedule with a monthly maintenance visit priced as a contract, not a one-off call-out.
  • Payback estimate comparing water, energy and labour savings against the system cost.

In our experience across East and West Africa, technicians who present a written quotation with real numbers close roughly twice as many jobs as those who quote verbally. The numbers also protect you: if a client later claims the system underperforms, you can point to the design assumptions, the measured flow test, and the signed handover checklist.

â  SAFETY WARNING â WATER, CHEMICALS AND ELECTRICAL RISK

Irrigation work combines water, electricity, and often agricultural chemicals. Never open a pump control panel, float switch or solenoid valve while the circuit is energised â 230 V and 400 V supplies are lethal, and wet ground increases shock risk. When chemigation (injecting fertiliser or pesticide through the irrigation system) is used, always fit a double check valve and an air gap to prevent backflow into the water source; contaminated water can poison a household or community well. Wear nitrile gloves, eye protection and a respirator when handling or mixing agrochemicals, and never eat, drink or smoke during chemigation work. Follow the product label and the WHO Recommended Classification of Pesticides by Hazard. If a chemical contacts skin or eyes, rinse with clean water for at least 15 minutes and seek medical help immediately. Lock out and tag out pumps before maintenance, and keep children and livestock away from open trenches, wet valve boxes and pump pits.

4. Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Measure before you quote. Record source flow (L/s), static water level, and the client's peak water demand in mÂ³/day.
  2. Confirm soil and crop. Note soil texture, infiltration rate from Table 2, and the crop's peak Kc stage.
  3. Calculate TDH. Static lift + friction loss + emitter pressure. Add 10â15% safety margin.
  4. Size pipe and pump together. Never undersize pipe to save cost; friction loss will destroy performance.
  5. Specify filtration correctly. Sand media for surface water; screen/disc for clean boreholes; clean filters on every service visit.
  6. Test the installed system. Measure emitter discharge at the start, middle and end of at least three laterals; record pressure at the filter and at the furthest lateral.
  7. Hand over with documentation. Provide a signed commissioning sheet, a simple operating guide in the client's language, and a maintenance schedule.
  8. Protect against backflow and electrical hazards. Fit double check valve and air gap for chemigation; lock out pumps before service.
