1. Roasted Maize: Methods and Seasoning

Roasted maize is one of the cheapest and most profitable street foods you can sell. A single cob costs you between 50 and 100 naira (or 1-2 shillings) from a farm gate. You sell it for 300-500 naira. That is a 400% markup. The key is consistent roasting and the right seasoning.

Start with fresh maize. Yellow or white varieties both work, but white maize is firmer and holds up better on the grill. Soak the cobs in water for 10 minutes before roasting. This steams the kernels from inside and stops them from drying out. You need a charcoal grill or a metal drum cut in half. Light your charcoal until it is grey and glowing, not flaming. Flames burn the outside before the inside cooks.

Place the cobs directly on the grill grate. Turn every 2 minutes using long tongs. Total roasting time is 12-15 minutes for a medium cob. You know it is done when the kernels are soft and slightly blackened in spots. Do not overcook or the kernels become hard and chewy.

Seasoning is where you make your money. The classic street mix is: 2 tablespoons of ground dried pepper, 1 tablespoon of salt, 1 teaspoon of ground ginger, and a pinch of ground cloves. Mix with 3 tablespoons of vegetable oil to make a paste. Brush this on the hot cobs right after they come off the grill. The heat opens the kernels and the paste soaks in. Some vendors also offer a squeeze of lime or a sprinkle of suya spice for an extra 50 naira.

For service, wrap the cob in newspaper or a small paper bag. Sell plain for 300 naira, or seasoned for 400 naira. Keep a small cooler with extra seasoning so customers can add more. You can also sell roasted maize kernels off the cob in a cup for 200 naira. This uses broken cobs that you cannot sell whole. Nothing goes to waste.

2. Plantain: Ripe, Unripe, Fried and Grilled

Plantain is the most versatile street food in Africa. You can sell it at three stages: unripe (green), semi-ripe (yellow with black spots), and fully ripe (black). Each stage has a different texture and price. Green plantain is hard and starchy. It is used for chips or boiled. Yellow plantain is sweet and soft. It is best for frying or grilling. Black plantain is very sweet and mushy. It is used for dodo (fried ripe plantain) or mashed.

Buy plantains by the bunch. A bunch of 10-12 fingers costs between 1,500 and 3,000 naira depending on the season. In peak season (July to September), prices drop. Buy green and let them ripen in a cool, dark place. They take 3-5 days to turn yellow and 5-7 days to turn black. Never store them in the sun or they rot.

For fried plantain (dodo), use yellow or black plantain. Peel and cut into 1cm thick slices. Heat vegetable oil to 180C. Fry for 2-3 minutes per side until golden brown. Drain on a wire rack, not paper, so they stay crispy. Season with salt immediately. Sell in a small paper cone for 200 naira. A single plantain yields about 8 slices. Your cost per cone is 50 naira.

For grilled plantain, use yellow plantain. Peel and cut lengthwise into 2cm thick planks. Brush with oil and grill on charcoal for 4 minutes per side. The outside should be charred and the inside soft. Serve with groundnut sauce or pepper sauce. Grilled plantain sells for 300 naira per skewer of 3 pieces.

For unripe plantain chips, peel and slice very thin (2mm) using a mandoline or sharp knife. Soak in salted water for 5 minutes, then pat dry. Fry at 160C for 3-4 minutes until light golden. These chips are crunchy and sell in a small bag for 200 naira. You can also make plantain flour by drying and grinding unripe plantain. That is a longer process but gives you a product with a 6-month shelf life.

3. Roasted Sweet Potato and Yam

Roasted sweet potato and yam are the heavy hitters of roadside vegetables. They fill the stomach and cost little to produce. A medium sweet potato costs 100 naira. You sell it roasted for 300 naira. A tuber of yam costs 1,500 naira and yields 8-10 slices. Each slice sells for 200 naira. That is 2,000 naira from one tuber.

Choose sweet potatoes that are firm and have no soft spots. Orange-fleshed varieties are sweeter and more nutritious. White-fleshed ones are drier and better for roasting because they hold their shape. Wash them well but do not peel. The skin protects the flesh and adds flavour. Prick each potato 3-4 times with a fork so steam can escape. If you do not, they burst.

For yam, peel the rough brown skin with a knife. Cut into 2cm thick rounds or 5cm long fingers. Soak in salted water for 10 minutes to remove excess starch. Pat dry with a clean cloth. This helps them roast evenly.

Roast on a charcoal grill over medium heat. Sweet potatoes take 20-30 minutes, turning every 5 minutes. Yam takes 15-20 minutes. You know they are done when a knife slides in easily. For a faster method, pre-boil the yam for 5 minutes before grilling. This cuts roasting time in half.

Seasoning is simple: salt, ground pepper, and a little oil. For sweet potato, you can also offer a sprinkle of cinnamon or honey for an extra 50 naira. For yam, serve with pepper sauce or groundnut sauce. Some vendors sell roasted yam with palm oil and salt as a dip.

To increase sales, sell them in combos. A plate of roasted sweet potato and yam with sauce sells for 500 naira. You can also cube leftover roasted yam and fry it into yam chips the next day. Never throw away unsold roasted yam. It can be mashed and mixed with egg to make yam balls, which you fry and sell for 100 naira each.

4. Grilled Peppers and Onions

Grilled peppers and onions are the secret weapon of every successful street food vendor. They are cheap, they add colour and flavour, and they make your other dishes look premium. A single red bell pepper costs 100 naira. An onion costs 50 naira. You can grill both and sell them as a side for 200 naira per portion. Your cost is 50 naira.

Choose firm peppers: red, yellow, or green bell peppers, plus hot peppers like scotch bonnet or habanero. For onions, use red or white onions. They should be firm and heavy for their size. Soft onions are old and will turn mushy on the grill.

Wash and dry the peppers and onions. Cut peppers in half and remove the seeds and white pith. Cut onions into 1cm thick rounds. Do not separate the rings or they will fall through the grill. Brush everything with a little vegetable oil. This stops them from sticking and helps them char.

Grill over medium charcoal heat. Peppers take 3-4 minutes per side. Onions take 2-3 minutes per side. You want char marks but not black ash. The peppers should soften and the onions should become translucent. Do not overcook or they lose their sweetness.

After grilling, season immediately with salt and a squeeze of lime. For a smoky flavour, add a pinch of smoked paprika. You can serve them whole, chopped, or in a small bowl. Many customers like them chopped and mixed with a little oil and vinegar as a relish.

To increase profit, make a grilled pepper and onion sauce. Blend grilled peppers, onions, one tomato, and a hot pepper with a little oil and salt. Simmer for 5 minutes. This sauce sells for 500 naira per bottle. It goes with roasted maize, plantain, yam, and even rice. You can also sell grilled peppers and onions on skewers. Three pieces per skewer for 150 naira. This is a popular add-on for customers buying roasted maize. Always keep a batch grilling so the smell draws people in.

5. Vegetable Skewers

Vegetable skewers are the most profitable item in this module. They cost almost nothing to make and sell for 200-300 naira each. You need bamboo skewers, which cost 500 naira for 100 pieces. That is 5 naira per skewer. Your vegetables cost another 50 naira per skewer. You sell for 250 naira. That is a 400% profit.

Choose vegetables that hold their shape on the grill: bell peppers, onions, cherry tomatoes, courgettes (zucchini), mushrooms, and firm tofu or paneer. You can also use baby corn, broccoli, and pineapple for sweetness. Cut everything into 2cm chunks. Keep pieces roughly the same size so they cook evenly.

Soak bamboo skewers in water for 30 minutes before using. This stops them from burning. Thread the vegetables onto the skewers, alternating colours. A good pattern is pepper, onion, tomato, courgette, mushroom. Leave a small gap between pieces so heat can circulate. Do not pack them tightly or the inside will not cook.

Make a marinade: 3 tablespoons of vegetable oil, 1 tablespoon of soy sauce, 1 teaspoon of ground ginger, 1 teaspoon of ground garlic, and 1 teaspoon of pepper. Mix well. Brush the skewers generously with this marinade. Let them sit for 10 minutes.

Grill over medium-high charcoal for 8-10 minutes, turning every 2 minutes. Brush with more marinade each time you turn. The vegetables should be tender and slightly charred. Do not overcook or they become limp. For a smoky flavour, add a pinch of smoked paprika to the marinade.

Serve hot. You can offer a dipping sauce: groundnut sauce, pepper sauce, or a simple mix of lime juice and salt. Sell three skewers for 700 naira or one for 250 naira. To reduce waste, use any leftover vegetables the next day in a stir-fry or soup. You can also pre-assemble skewers and keep them in a cooler with ice packs. They last 2 days. This is perfect for busy days when you cannot prep on site.

6. Street Salads and Sides

Street salads and sides are your low-cost, high-margin add-ons. They use the same vegetables you already have for skewers and grills. A basic salad costs you 100 naira in ingredients. You sell it for 300 naira. That is 200 naira profit per bowl. If you sell 20 bowls a day, that is 4,000 naira extra profit.

The classic African street salad is cabbage, carrot, onion, and tomato. Shred half a small cabbage (cost: 150 naira). Grate two carrots (cost: 100 naira). Slice one onion (cost: 50 naira). Dice two tomatoes (cost: 100 naira). Mix everything in a large bowl. This makes 4 portions. Your cost per portion is 100 naira. Add a boiled egg or a spoon of baked beans for an extra 100 naira. Sell the base salad for 300 naira, or with egg for 400 naira.

Dressing is key. The standard street dressing is: 3 tablespoons of vegetable oil, 1 tablespoon of vinegar, 1 teaspoon of salt, 1 teaspoon of sugar, and a pinch of ground pepper. Shake it in a bottle. Pour over the salad just before serving. Do not dress the salad in advance or it becomes soggy. For a creamy version, mix 2 tablespoons of mayonnaise with 1 tablespoon of ketchup and a squeeze of lime. This is popular with children.

Other sides you can sell: coleslaw (shredded cabbage and carrot with mayonnaise), beetroot salad (boiled and diced beetroot with onion and oil), and potato salad (boiled potatoes, eggs, and mayonnaise). Each sells for 300-400 naira.

To keep costs low, buy vegetables in bulk from a local market. A whole cabbage costs 500 naira and makes 8 portions. A bag of carrots (1kg) costs 800 naira and makes 10 portions. Store everything in a cool, dark place. Wash all vegetables in salted water for 10 minutes before cutting. This removes dirt and insects. Never sell salad that has been sitting out for more than 4 hours. If you have leftover salad at the end of the day, eat it yourself or give it to a neighbour. Do not sell it the next day.

<h2>Practical Reference: Grilled Maize, Plantain and Roadside Vegetables</h2>

<p>This practical reference is the working manual for your roadside grill. It consolidates the methods, seasoning ratios, holding times, cost drivers and safety rules that determine whether your maize, plantain, sweet potato, yam, pepper and vegetable skewer business makes money or quietly loses it. Read it as an operations document, not a recipe book. Every number here is something you can put on a chalkboard, a price card or a training sheet for a new assistant.</p>

<p>Across West, East and Southern Africa, the grilled maize and plantain trade is one of the lowest-barrier food businesses available: capital is small, equipment is locally fabricated, and demand peaks reliably around commuter times, school closing and evening leisure. The margin, however, is thin and is destroyed by three things inconsistent seasoning, poor holding practice, and unsafe water or handling. This module addresses all three.</p>

<h3>Grilled Maize: Method and Seasoning</h3>
<p>Roasted maize (called <em>mas grill</em>, <em>agbado</em>, <em>roasted corn</em> or <em>mielie</em> depending on the market) is your volume anchor. Buy in bulk from a wholesaler or directly from farmers at harvest to cut unit cost by 2040%. Choose cobs with tight, green husks and plump kernels; reject any with black mould at the tip.</p>
<p>Two service formats sell well: <strong>husk-on roasting</strong>, which steams and keeps the cob juicy, and <strong>husk-off direct grilling</strong>, which gives the char marks customers associate with street flavour. Many vendors do both husk-on for the lunch crowd who eat on the go, husk-off for the evening crowd who want the smoky finish.</p>
<p>Seasoning is where margin is won. A standard batch rub for 20 cobs: 4 tablespoons chilli powder, 2 tablespoons salt, 1 tablespoon ground ginger, 1 tablespoon garlic powder, 3 tablespoons vegetable oil, juice of 2 limes. Brush after the first turn, not before, or the sugar in the rub burns before the kernels cook.</p>

<h3>Plantain: Ripe, Unripe, Fried and Grilled</h3>
<p>Plantain is the highest-margin item on most roadside grills because the raw material is cheap, holds well, and the same finger can be sold two ways. <strong>Ripe plantain</strong> (yellow with black spots) is sweet, caramelises fast and suits grilling over medium coals; it sells to customers wanting a snack. <strong>Unripe plantain</strong> (green) is firm, starchy and absorbs seasoning; it is grilled longer, often with a chilli-salt rub, and pairs with grilled fish or pepper sauce.</p>
<p>Frying and grilling are not competitors they are a menu pair. Fried ripe plantain (<em>dodo</em>, <em>kekefia</em>, <em>alloco</em>) can be pre-fried at 160C, held, and finished on the grill to order, which cuts customer wait time and lets you sell more per hour. Pre-frying also lets you buy plantain when it is cheap and extend shelf life by a day under refrigeration.</p>
<p>Key control point: plantain burns quickly because of its sugar content. Grill ripe plantain over medium coals, turning every 90 seconds. Unripe plantain can take direct high heat for the first two minutes, then move to the medium zone.</p>

<h3>Roasted Sweet Potato and Yam</h3>
<p>Sweet potato and yam are your filling, higher-ticket items. Both are roasted whole in their skins or in thick slices over medium coals. Whole roasting takes 2540 minutes depending on size; sliced roasting takes 812 minutes per side. The skin protects the flesh and reduces moisture loss, which matters when you are holding product for two hours.</p>
<p>Buy yam tubers that are firm and free of soft spots; a single rotten tuber can spoil a whole batch. Sweet potato should be cured (kept 510 days in a dry, ventilated place) before roasting curing converts starch to sugar and improves flavour and shelf life.</p>
<p>Serving format: split the roasted potato or yam, add a spoon of pepper sauce, groundnut sauce or a sprinkle of suya spice, and wrap in paper. This raises perceived value and lets you charge a premium over a plain roasted root.</p>

<h3>Grilled Peppers and Onions</h3>
<p>Grilled peppers and onions are the flavour engine of the whole operation. They go into pepper sauce, into skewers, and are sold as a side. Use the warm zone of the grill never the hot zone because peppers and onions release water and will steam rather than char if the heat is too high.</p>
<p>Whole bell peppers, scotch bonnets and onions can be grilled until the skin blisters, then peeled and blended for sauce. This is cheaper than buying pre-made sauce and gives you a signature flavour. Hold grilled peppers and onions in a covered container for no more than four hours; after that, refrigerate or discard.</p>

<h3>Vegetable Skewers</h3>
<p>Vegetable skewers (<em>kebab</em>, <em>suya-style veg</em>, <em>mishkaki</em>) are the fastest-growing item in urban street food because they serve vegetarians, health-conscious customers and office workers who want a light lunch. Build them from what is cheap and in season: onion quarters, bell pepper squares, courgette or cucumber rounds, tomato halves, mushroom, and cubes of firm tofu or paneer if your market supports it.</p>
<p>Skewer economics: a skewer with 68 pieces costs very little in raw material but sells at a multiple because of the preparation and grilling labour. Standardise your skewer same number of pieces, same size so customers trust the value and your assistant can assemble quickly.</p>
<p>Marinade for 30 skewers: 6 tablespoons oil, 3 tablespoons soy sauce, 2 tablespoons vinegar, 1 tablespoon chilli flakes, 1 tablespoon minced garlic, 1 teaspoon salt. Marinate 2030 minutes, not longer, or the vegetables go soft.</p>

<h3>Street Salads and Sides</h3>
<p>Salads and cold sides are a high-risk, high-reward category. They carry the highest food-safety risk because they are not cooked after handling, and they carry the best margin because customers pay for freshness. Keep the range small and rotate fast: a cabbage-carrot-onion slaw, a tomato-onion-cucumber salad, and a bean or groundnut side.</p>
<p>Wash all salad vegetables in clean, treated water. If you cannot guarantee safe tap water, use bottled or boiled-and-cooled water with a small amount of food-grade sanitising solution, following the manufacturer's dilution. Cut salads fresh in small batches never hold a large bowl from morning to evening.</p>

<div style="border:3px solid #b30000;background:#fff2f2;padding:16px;margin:20px 0;border-radius:8px;">
<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0;font-weight:bold;color:#b30000;font-size:18px;"> SAFETY WARNING READ BEFORE EVERY SERVICE</p>
<p style="margin:0;">Unsafe water and cross-contamination are the two biggest causes of foodborne illness at roadside grills. <strong>Never</strong> use untreated water to wash salad vegetables or to rinse utensils. <strong>Never</strong> store raw meat, fish or poultry above or beside ready-to-eat salads, grilled vegetables or cooked plantain. Use separate chopping boards and knives for raw and cooked foods, or wash them with soap and hot water between uses. Keep a covered container of clean water and soap at the stall and wash hands after handling money, raw food, waste or the toilet. If you or an assistant has diarrhoea, vomiting, fever or an open wound on the hand, do not handle food this is a legal and ethical requirement in most African markets and protects your business reputation.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Buy maize, plantain, yam and sweet potato in bulk at harvest or from a wholesaler; record unit cost per cob, finger and kilogram every week.</li>
<li>Set up the grill in three zones hot for searing, medium for finishing, warm for holding and keep peppers and onions off the hot zone.</li>
<li>Season after the first turn, not before, to prevent burning and to use less rub per cob.</li>
<li>Pre-fry ripe plantain at 160C and finish on the grill to order; this cuts wait time and increases hourly sales.</li>
<li>Hold hot food at 60C or above and cold food at 5C or below; discard anything held beyond the times in Table 2.</li>
<li>Wash all salad vegetables in treated water and cut in small batches throughout the day.</li>
<li>Keep raw and cooked foods separate, wash hands and utensils often, and exclude sick staff from food handling.</li>
<li>Price every item against a written cost card, and review prices weekly against market input costs.</li>
</ol>

Standardise your seasoning, your skewer size and your holding times. Train one assistant to run the grill to the same standard you do. Then your business can open a second grill, and a third, without your flavour or your reputation changing.</p>
