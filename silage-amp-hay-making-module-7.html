1. Chopping and Wilting Forage for Silage

To make good silage, you must chop and wilt the forage correctly. Start by cutting your grass, maize, or sorghum when it is mature but still greenâusually when the grain is at the dough stage for maize. Let it wilt in the field for 4 to 24 hours, depending on the weather. In hot, dry conditions, 4-6 hours is enough; in cool, humid weather, up to 24 hours. The goal is to reduce moisture to 60-70%. To test, squeeze a handful of chopped forage: it should feel like a wrung-out sponge, with no water dripping. If it drips, wilt longer; if it crumbles, it's too dry.

Chop the forage into pieces 1-2 cm long. You can use a hand chaff cutter, a forage chopper, or even a sharp machete on a wooden block. Short pieces pack tighter, exclude more air, and ferment faster. For maize, chop whole plants including stalks and leaves. For grasses, chop after wilting. If you use a tractor-mounted chopper, set it to a fine cut. For small-scale, a simple hand chopper costs about $50-100 and can process 500 kg per hour.

After chopping, test moisture again. If too wet, spread the chop in thin layers in the sun for 30-60 minutes, turning often. If too dry, add water sparingly while mixing. Aim for 65% moistureâthis is ideal for lactic acid bacteria to grow and produce acid that preserves the silage. Avoid chopping too fine (less than 1 cm) because it can cause compaction problems and poor fermentation. Also, avoid contamination with soil, which introduces harmful bacteria. Work quickly; chopped forage should be ensiled within 24 hours to prevent spoilage.

In Africa, where rain can be unpredictable, wilting under a shed or turning frequently helps. For maize silage, you can chop and wilt simultaneously if using a precision chopper. Remember, proper chopping and wilting are the foundation of good silage. Take your time, and your animals will thank you with better milk and meat production.

2. Packing Silage Tightly to Remove Air

Packing silage tightly is the most important step to ensure good fermentation. The goal is to remove as much air (oxygen) as possible because lactic acid bacteria only grow without oxygen. If air remains, harmful bacteria and molds grow, causing spoilage and heating. After chopping and wilting, fill your silo or pit in layers of 15-20 cm. Pack each layer thoroughly before adding the next. On small farms, use your feet, a heavy roller, or a drum filled with water to press down. On larger farms, a tractor can drive over the silage, but be careful not to push too much at once.

For a bunker silo, spread the forage evenly and pack continuously. The best method is to pack in thin layers (10-15 cm) and drive over them slowly with a heavy tractor. The weight of the tractor compacts the silage, squeezing out air. If you don't have a tractor, use a heavy roller or a group of people trampling. Pack along the walls and corners especially well, as these areas are prone to air pockets. The silage should feel firm and springy, not loose. A good test: after packing, push a metal rod into the silage; it should be difficult to push in more than 30 cm.

Packing must be done quickly. The entire filling and packing process should take no more than 2-3 days for a bunker silo. If filling is slow, the silage on top may spoil. For bag silage, pack by pressing the bag as you fill, or use a bagging machine. For drum silage, press down with a wooden plunger every 10 cm. In all cases, the silage should be denseâaim for a density of at least 700 kg per cubic meter (fresh weight). This means a 1 mÂ³ box should hold about 700 kg of packed silage.

After packing, seal immediately. Any delay allows air back in. Remember, the tighter you pack, the less air remains, and the better the fermentation. This step alone can make the difference between sweet-smelling silage and a rotten, moldy mess. Invest time and effort in packing; it pays off in feed quality.

3. Using Plastic Sheets and Tires for Sealing

After packing, you must seal the silage airtight to keep oxygen out. The most common method on small and medium farms is to cover the silage with a plastic sheet and weigh it down with tires. Use a thick, UV-resistant plastic sheet, at least 0.15 mm (150 microns) thick. Black silage plastic is best because it blocks sunlight and reduces heating. You can also use two layers: a thin inner sheet and a thicker outer sheet. Make sure the sheet is large enough to cover the entire silage and overlap the walls by at least 1 meter.

Before covering, smooth the top of the silage to remove any sharp stalks that could puncture the plastic. Then lay the sheet over the silage, starting from one end and pushing out air bubbles as you go. Pull the sheet tight so it touches the silage surface. Overlap the edges over the bunker walls or pit edges. Now, weigh down the sheet with tires. Use old car or tractor tires, cut in half if needed. Place them close together, touching each other, covering the entire surface. On a bunker silo, also place tires along the edges and on the side walls if possible. For a pit silo, cover the walls with plastic and then put tires on top.

Tires are cheap and effective, but you need many. For a 10 m x 5 m bunker, you might need 100-150 tires. Alternatively, use sandbags, soil, or heavy stones, but tires are easier to handle. Check the seal daily for the first week. If you see holes or tears, patch them immediately with silage tape. Also, make sure water cannot pool on the plastic; if it does, pump or drain it off. After sealing, the silage will ferment and produce gas, which may cause the plastic to balloon. That's normal; just release the gas by making a small slit and resealing with tape.

In Africa, where plastic can be expensive, share costs with neighbors or reuse sheets from year to year if they are still intact. Proper sealing prevents mold, reduces dry matter losses, and ensures good fermentation. Without a good seal, your hard work in chopping and packing is wasted. So, take time to seal well, and check regularly.

4. Natural Additives: Molasses, Salt, and Lactic Acid Bacteria

Sometimes forage is too wet, too dry, or low in sugars, making it hard for lactic acid bacteria to ferment properly. In such cases, you can use natural additives to improve silage quality. The three most common are molasses, salt, and lactic acid bacteria (LAB) inoculants. Molasses is a cheap source of sugars that feed LAB. Use it at 2-5% of fresh forage weight. For example, for 100 kg of chopped forage, dissolve 2-5 kg of molasses in 10-20 liters of water and mix thoroughly. This is especially useful for grasses cut at maturity or when wilting was excessive. In Africa, molasses is available from sugar factories; you can also use honey or fruit waste, but molasses is best.

Salt (sodium chloride) can also help, but use it sparingly. Add 0.5-1% salt (0.5-1 kg per 100 kg forage). Salt inhibits harmful bacteria and improves palatability, but too much can reduce fermentation. Mix salt with the forage as you fill the silo. Another option is to use a commercial LAB inoculant. These are dried bacteria that you mix with water and spray onto the forage. Follow the instructions on the package; typically, 1 gram of inoculant treats 1 ton of silage. LAB inoculants are more expensive but effective, especially for legumes or wet forages. They speed up fermentation and reduce dry matter losses.

When using additives, mix them evenly. For molasses, dilute in water and sprinkle over each layer as you pack. For salt, sprinkle dry and mix. For LAB, use a sprayer. Always wear gloves and avoid inhaling powders. In Africa, you can make your own LAB inoculant by fermenting rice water or milk with sugar, but commercial products are more reliable. Additives are not a substitute for good managementâchopping, wilting, and packing are still key. But when conditions are challenging, they can save your silage. Test small batches first to see what works best for your forage and climate.

5. Building and Filling a Bunker Silo Step by Step

A bunker silo is a simple, low-cost structure for making silage. It consists of two parallel walls with a concrete or soil floor. To build one, choose a well-drained site close to where you feed animals. The size depends on herd size: for 10 cows eating 20 kg silage per day for 6 months, you need about 36 tons of silage. A bunker 10 m long, 3 m wide, and 1.5 m deep holds about 45 tons if well packed. Walls can be made of concrete, timber, or even packed soil. Concrete is best but expensive; timber or soil are cheaper. Ensure walls are smooth and airtight. The floor should slope slightly (1-2%) to drain any seepage.

Step 1: Harvest and chop forage as described. Step 2: Fill the bunker in layers of 15-20 cm. Step 3: Pack each layer thoroughly with a tractor or heavy roller. Step 4: Continue filling until the silage reaches 0.5-1 m above the walls (if walls are 1.5 m high, fill to 2-2.5 m). This extra height helps with compaction and drainage. Step 5: Shape the top into a dome to shed water. Step 6: Cover with a plastic sheet and weigh down with tires. Step 7: Check daily for the first week and patch holes.

Filling should be done quickly, ideally in 2-3 days. If you fill slowly, the silage on top may spoil. Work in a team: one person chopping, one packing, one spreading. For small farms, you can fill a bunker manually, but it's hard work. Use a tractor if available. After sealing, fermentation takes 3-6 weeks. Open the silo from one end, removing only what you need each day. Keep the face tight and re-cover with plastic after feeding. A well-built and well-managed bunker silo can last for years. In Africa, many farmers use bunker silos successfully for maize and grass silage. With careful construction and filling, you can store enough feed to get your animals through the dry season.

6. Making Silage in Plastic Bags and Drums

For small-scale farmers, plastic bags and drums are excellent alternatives to bunker silos. They are cheap, portable, and require no construction. You can use heavy-duty plastic bags (like feed bags or silage bags) or 200-liter drums. For bags, choose thick, UV-resistant plastic, at least 0.1 mm thick. For drums, any clean metal or plastic drum works, but ensure it has a tight-fitting lid. First, chop and wilt your forage as usual. Then, fill the bag or drum in layers of 10-15 cm, packing each layer firmly by hand, with a wooden plunger, or by stomping. The goal is to remove as much air as possible.

For bags, fill to the top, then squeeze out air by twisting the top or pressing down. Tie the bag tightly with rope or tape. For extra security, double-bag: put the filled bag inside another bag. For drums, fill to the brim, press down hard, and seal with the lid. If the lid is not airtight, place a plastic sheet over the top before closing. You can also use a plastic liner inside the drum. After sealing, store in a cool, shaded place, away from rodents and direct sun. Bags can be stacked, but keep them off the ground on wooden pallets to prevent moisture damage.

Fermentation takes 3-4 weeks. Once opened, use the silage within 3-5 days, especially in hot weather. For bags, open one end and take what you need, then reseal tightly. For drums, open the lid, remove silage, and reseal. If you see mold, discard that part. Bags and drums are ideal for 1-2 cows or goats. A 200-liter drum holds about 150 kg of silage, enough for a cow for 7-10 days. You can make many bags or drums at once. In Africa, this method is popular because it requires little capital and can be done by women and youth. Just remember: pack tight, seal well, and keep cool. With practice, you can produce high-quality silage in bags and drums.

1. Chopping and Wilting: Setting the Fermentation Stage

Chop length controls both compaction and the rate at which plant sugars become available to lactic acid bacteria (LAB). For maize and sorghum, target a theoretical chop length of 10â20 mm; for grasses and legumes, 20â30 mm. Longer particles trap air pockets and slow fermentation; too-fine material (<8 mm) can cause rumen acidosis and increases effluent loss in wet crops.

Wilting is the cheapest quality lever you have. Fresh maize at 70â75% moisture ferments slowly and loses nutrients as effluent. Wilt to 60â70% moisture (30â40% dry matter) for bunker silos, and 55â65% moisture for bagged silage. A simple field test: squeeze a chopped handful â if liquid runs freely, wilt longer; if it holds shape without dripping, you are close. In humid coastal West Africa, wilting may take only 4â8 hours; in the highlands of Kenya or Ethiopia, 12â24 hours is common.

2. Packing Tightly: The Air Is the Enemy

Every kilogram of oxygen left in the silage mass feeds yeasts and moulds that burn sugars and protein. Target a fresh-weight density of at least 700 kg/mÂ³ for maize and 600 kg/mÂ³ for grass silage. Achieve this by:

  • Filling in thin layers of 15â20 cm.
  • Running a heavy tractor or, on smallholder farms, rolling with a water-filled drum or repeatedly treading with a group of workers.
  • Never stopping mid-fill for more than a few hours â delays restart the aerobic phase.
  • Filling the bunker in a wedge or progressive ramp, not a flat pile.

3. Sealing with Plastic Sheets and Tires

Use a double layer of UV-stabilised silage plastic (0.08â0.15 mm per layer). Overlap sheets by at least 1.5 m and weight the entire surface â edges included â with tires cut in half, sandbags, or gravel-filled bags. A common error is weighting only the centre: the shoulders are where air enters first. Immediately after sealing, inspect daily for the first two weeks and patch holes with silage tape. A single 5 cm tear can spoil a 2-tonne wedge.

4. Natural Additives: Molasses, Salt, and LAB

Additives are not mandatory but pay back quickly when forage is low in water-soluble carbohydrates (WSC).

  • Molasses: 2â4% of fresh weight (20â40 kg per tonne). Dilute 1:3 with water and apply in layers. Increases lactic acid production and palatability.
  • Salt: 0.5â1% of fresh weight (5â10 kg per tonne). Helps restrict undesirable bacteria and improves palatability; excess causes water retention and diarrhoea.
  • Lactic acid bacteria inoculant: 1 g of commercial inoculant per tonne of fresh forage, dissolved in 5 L of non-chlorinated water. Fastest, most reliable option, especially for legumes and wet forages.

6. Silage in Plastic Bags and Drums

For smallholders with 1â5 cows, bags and drums are the lowest-capital entry point. Use 0.08â0.15 mm UV-treated bags, fill to 20â25 kg, expel air by twisting and compressing, then tie tightly and store under shade. Drums (200 L) should be lined with a bag, filled in layers, packed with a wooden rammer, and sealed with a lid plus plastic. Fermentation completes in 3â4 weeks. Keep bags off bare ground and away from rodents; a single rat hole can spoil a whole bag.
