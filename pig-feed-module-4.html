Module 4

🐷 Finishing Pig Nutrition (25-120 kg)

Where most of the feed is eaten and most of the money is made or lost. Covers energy density through the finishing phases, cutting protein safely as lean growth slows, how dietary fat becomes carcass fat, trimming mineral and vitamin specifications without damaging the pig, choosing additives that actually earn their place, and the final weeks before sale.

What you will be able to do after this module

  • Set the correct energy density for each finishing weight band
  • Step SID lysine down correctly across the finishing weight bands
  • Explain how dietary fatty acids are deposited in carcass fat
  • Step calcium and phosphorus down correctly across the finishing weight bands
  • Rank finishing additives by published evidence rather than by sales claim
  • Calculate the marginal feed cost of the last ten kilograms of liveweight

The six lessons

1. Energy Requirements for Finishing 2. Protein Reduction and Lean Gain 3. Fat Quality and Composition 4. Mineral and Vitamin in Finishing Diets 5. Feed Additives for Finishing 6. Pre-Marketing Feeding Strategies
Lesson 4.1~11 min

Energy Requirements for Finishing

In this lesson
  • Set the correct energy density for each finishing weight band
  • Explain why maintenance cost rises and efficiency falls as pigs get heavier
  • Calculate whether adding dietary fat pays at your own oil and feed prices

Finishing is where the feed bill is decided. A pig eats more between 25 and 120 kg than in the whole of its earlier life, so a small error in energy specification repeated over 250 kg of feed is a large error in money.

The published energy densities fall steadily as the pig grows:

  1. Early finisher, 27 to 60 kg: 3,200 to 3,350 kcal ME/kg
  2. Mid finisher, 60 to 100 kg: 3,150 to 3,300 kcal ME/kg
  3. Late finisher, 100 to 130 kg: 3,100 to 3,250 kcal ME/kg

On a net energy basis, finisher diets are published at roughly 2.46 to 2.73 Mcal NE per kg. The reason density falls is capacity. A 100 kg pig can physically consume a large volume of feed, so you no longer need to pay for concentration, and cheaper, bulkier ingredients such as maize bran, cassava meal or brewers' grain become viable within the 4 to 6 percent crude fibre ceiling for this class.

Now the uncomfortable side. Maintenance energy is expressed as 100 multiplied by bodyweight in kilograms raised to the power 0.75, and the standards give two published results: about 1,495 kcal net energy per day for a 50 kg pig, and about 2,371 kcal for a 100 kg pig. The heavier pig burns roughly 876 kcal a day more simply existing. At the same time, net energy needed for each kilogram of gain rises with bodyweight, because a heavier pig deposits proportionally more fat and fat is energetically expensive to lay down. Both effects push in the same direction: every kilogram of gain costs more feed than the one before it. This is why finishing pigs slowly is expensive twice over, and why the last lesson of this module deals with when to stop.

Energy sources in an African finisher ration usually come down to cereals, cereal by-products, root crops and added fat or oil. The published relationship for fat is precise and useful: adding 1 percent dietary fat improves feed conversion by about 0.04 points. That gives you a direct way to test whether oil pays.

Worked example. A producer in Mbarara, Uganda, finishes 180 pigs a year from 30 to 100 kg. His ration is maize, maize bran, soybean meal and a premix, which his supplier estimates at 3,120 kcal ME/kg, just below the 3,150 to 3,300 window for the mid finisher phase. His measured feed conversion over the whole finishing period is 3.4. He is considering 2 percent added cooking-oil residue from a local processor.

  • Expected feed conversion improvement: 2 x 0.04 = 0.08 points, so 3.4 becomes 3.32
  • Feed to take a pig from 30 to 100 kg at 3.40: 70 kg gain x 3.40 = 238 kg
  • At 3.32: 70 x 3.32 = 232.4 kg. Saving 5.6 kg of feed per pig
  • Base feed cost, illustrative: 0.41 USD/kg. Value of feed saved: 5.6 x 0.41 = 2.30 USD per pig
  • Oil used: 2 percent of 232.4 kg = 4.65 kg per pig. At an illustrative recovered-oil price of 0.85 USD/kg that is 3.95 USD, but it displaces 4.65 kg of base ration worth 1.91 USD, so the net extra cost is 3.95 - 1.91 = 2.04 USD per pig
  • Net benefit: 2.30 - 2.04 = 0.26 USD per pig, or 47 USD a year across 180 pigs

Marginal, but positive, and it becomes clearly positive if the denser diet also lifts daily gain enough to shorten the batch. Note two cautions before he commits. First, oil quality: rancid or repeatedly heated oil damages vitamins and palatability. Second, and more important commercially, the fatty acid composition of added fat ends up in the carcass, which lesson 3 deals with in detail. Cheap unsaturated oil in the final weeks can cost you more in carcass quality than it saves in feed. Use your own prices and check both sides.

Lesson 4.2~12 min

Protein Reduction and Lean Gain

In this lesson
  • Step SID lysine down correctly across the finishing weight bands
  • Apply the protein to energy ratio appropriate to your herd's lean growth potential
  • Calculate the saving from moving from one finisher diet to three

A pig's capacity to deposit lean tissue falls as it approaches market weight, while its appetite keeps rising. That divergence is the whole story of finisher formulation. Feed a heavy pig the protein specification of a young one and you pay for amino acids it cannot use. Cut protein too early and you shift gain from muscle to fat and lose carcass value.

The published SID lysine schedule steps down through six phases:

  1. 27 to 40 kg: 1.05 percent, energy 3,350 kcal ME/kg, roughly 2 to 3 weeks
  2. 40 to 60 kg: 0.95 percent, energy 3,300, 2 to 3 weeks
  3. 60 to 80 kg: 0.85 percent, energy 3,250, 2 to 3 weeks
  4. 80 to 100 kg: 0.75 percent, energy 3,200, 2 to 3 weeks
  5. 100 to 120 kg: 0.70 percent, energy 3,150, 2 to 3 weeks
  6. 120 to 130 kg: 0.65 percent, energy 3,100, 1 to 2 weeks

Grouped more simply, the bands are 1.00 to 1.10 percent for 27 to 60 kg, 0.80 to 0.95 percent for 60 to 100 kg, and 0.70 to 0.85 percent for 100 to 130 kg. Amino acid ratios hold steady through finishing: methionine at 28 percent of lysine, methionine plus cysteine 56 to 58 percent, threonine 62 to 64 percent, tryptophan 18 to 19 percent.

The protein to energy ratio is where genetics enter. Published guidance separates herds by lean gain potential:

  • High lean potential: early finisher ratio 3.4 to 3.6, mid 3.1 to 3.3, late 2.8 to 3.0
  • Medium lean potential: early 3.2 to 3.4, mid 2.9 to 3.1, late 2.6 to 2.8
  • Low lean potential: early 3.0 to 3.2, mid 2.7 to 2.9, late 2.4 to 2.6

This matters enormously in Africa, where herds range from imported high-lean crosses to local and heritage pigs with genuinely lower lean deposition. Feeding a high-lean specification to a low-lean pig wastes protein; feeding a low-lean specification to modern genetics leaves growth on the table. Module 11 lesson 2 deals with matching genetics to systems; here the point is simply that the ratio table has three rows and you must know which one you are in.

Two practical cautions. First, higher lysine relative to energy increases lean deposition and reduces backfat, while lower lysine relative to energy increases fat deposition. That is a lever you can pull deliberately, but only if you know how your buyer pays. Second, cutting crude protein while holding lysine with synthetic amino acids saves money and reduces heat load, but only until a non-supplemented amino acid becomes limiting, as Module 3 lesson 3 explains.

Worked example. A producer in Ziguinchor, Senegal, finishes 150 pigs a year from 30 to 105 kg on a single ration at 0.95 percent SID lysine. He compares that with three phases.

Single ration, illustrative cost 0.45 USD/kg. Total feed at a measured feed conversion of 3.3 over 75 kg of gain: 247.5 kg per pig. Cost: 247.5 x 0.45 = 111.38 USD per pig.

Three-phase programme, using his own weight bands and the same total feed:

  • Phase 1, 30 to 55 kg, 25 kg gain at FCR 2.8 = 70 kg of feed at 1.05 percent lysine, illustrative cost 0.49 USD/kg = 34.30 USD
  • Phase 2, 55 to 80 kg, 25 kg gain at FCR 3.3 = 82.5 kg at 0.85 percent lysine, cost 0.44 USD/kg = 36.30 USD
  • Phase 3, 80 to 105 kg, 25 kg gain at FCR 3.8 = 95 kg at 0.75 percent lysine, cost 0.41 USD/kg = 38.95 USD
  • Total feed: 247.5 kg. Total cost: 34.30 + 36.30 + 38.95 = 109.55 USD per pig

The saving is 1.83 USD per pig, or 275 USD a year across 150 pigs, for the same weight of feed. Modest, but it comes with a second effect that is larger and harder to price: the early phase now carries adequate lysine for a 30 to 55 kg pig, which the flat 0.95 percent diet did not, so lean gain in the phase where it is cheapest improves. He should weigh a sample in each band, as Module 3 lesson 6 describes, and confirm on his own farm rather than trusting the arithmetic alone.

Lesson 4.3~12 min

Fat Quality and Composition

In this lesson
  • Explain how dietary fatty acids are deposited in carcass fat
  • Identify which local ingredients produce soft fat and at what inclusion levels
  • Measure backfat and interpret it against published carcass targets

A pig deposits the fat it eats, largely unchanged. That single fact explains why two pigs of identical weight and breed can produce carcasses of very different commercial value. Where a ruminant hydrogenates dietary fat in the rumen before it reaches the tissues, the pig has no such filter. Feed it soft, highly unsaturated fat and it lays down soft, highly unsaturated body fat.

Soft fat is a commercial problem, not a health one. It will not firm up in a chiller. It makes bacon and cured products difficult, it separates and looks oily on a butcher's counter, and it shortens shelf life because unsaturated fat oxidises faster. The published guidance links high inclusion of unsaturated fat sources, distillers grains above about 15 percent and high-linoleic oils among them, to soft pork fat, and to problems including pale, soft, exudative pork and degraded bacon quality.

The management response is a timing rule rather than a ban. Unsaturated fat sources can be used through the earlier finishing phases where they are cheap, then limited in the final four to six weeks, shifting toward saturated fats such as tallow or palm oil, or simply reducing the distillers grains and oil-cake residues. Because fat turnover in the body takes time, the composition of what a pig eats in its last weeks disproportionately determines the fat that reaches the carcass. Module 11 lesson 3 works through this as a premium market strategy; here the concern is the basic carcass value every producer is paid on.

African ingredient watch list. The following are common, useful, and worth limiting late:

  • Distillers grains and brewers' spent grain, especially where fat has not been extracted
  • Rice bran, which carries a high level of unsaturated oil unless it is defatted
  • Sunflower cake, groundnut cake and soybean full-fat, all rich in unsaturated fatty acids
  • Recovered or waste cooking oil, which is usually highly unsaturated and may also be oxidised

Against these, palm oil and animal tallow are more saturated and firm the carcass, though they carry their own cost and availability issues.

The second half of carcass management is lean versus fat, which you control through the lysine to energy relationship covered in lesson 2. Higher SID lysine relative to energy increases lean deposition and reduces backfat. Lower lysine relative to energy does the reverse. The published modern market target for backfat at the tenth rib is 0.60 to 0.75 inches, which is 15 to 19 mm, for gilt and barrow carcasses.

Measure it. On a live pig you can estimate backfat by feel along the loin and over the last rib, and with practice a producer becomes reasonably consistent. At slaughter, ask the butcher to let you measure fat depth at the tenth rib with a ruler and record it against the pig's diet and finishing weight. Three or four measurements per batch, recorded over a year, will tell you more about your feeding programme than any brochure.

Worked example. A producer in Kisumu, Kenya, sells to a butcher who has begun complaining that his pork fat is soft and oily on the counter. His finisher ration carries 18 percent brewers' spent grain, undefatted, plus 3 percent recovered cooking oil.

His plan for the final five weeks before sale:

  1. Cut brewers' spent grain from 18 percent to 6 percent, replacing it with maize and maize bran
  2. Remove the recovered cooking oil entirely, replacing the energy with maize
  3. Hold SID lysine at the published 0.70 to 0.85 percent band for this weight so lean deposition is not sacrificed

Cost effect, illustrative and to be replaced with his own prices: brewers' grain costs 0.22 USD/kg against maize at 0.38, so replacing 12 percent of the ration raises cost by 0.12 x (0.38 - 0.22) = 0.019 USD/kg. Removing 3 percent cooking oil at 0.85 and replacing with maize at 0.38 saves 0.03 x (0.85 - 0.38) = 0.014 USD/kg. Net increase 0.005 USD/kg. Over five weeks at 3.0 kg a day that is 105 kg of feed, so 0.53 USD per pig.

Against 0.53 USD per pig he keeps a butcher who buys 6 pigs a month. The arithmetic barely needs finishing. Fat quality is rarely a large cost problem; it is a large market access problem.

Mineral and Vitamin in Finishing Diets

In this lesson
  • Step calcium and phosphorus down correctly across the finishing weight bands
  • State the vitamin specifications for finishing pigs and when to raise them
  • Calculate the premix saving available in late finishing without harming the pig

Minerals and vitamins are a small share of finisher feed cost, but the specification falls sharply with weight and most small producers never adjust it. Feeding a weaner premix to a 110 kg pig is not dangerous, it is simply money handed over for nothing. Equally, cutting the premix out altogether to save that money is one of the most reliably expensive decisions in pig farming.

The published calcium and phosphorus schedule for finishing:

  1. 27 to 60 kg: total calcium 0.65 percent, STTD phosphorus 0.32 percent, calcium to STTD phosphorus ratio about 2.0 to 1
  2. 60 to 100 kg: calcium 0.55 percent, STTD phosphorus 0.28 percent, ratio about 1.96 to 1
  3. 100 to 130 kg: calcium 0.46 percent, STTD phosphorus 0.26 percent, ratio about 1.77 to 1

Note that these ratios are expressed against digestible rather than total phosphorus, which is why they look wider than the 1:1 to 1.5:1 rule quoted against total phosphorus for growers in Module 3 lesson 4. Read carefully which basis a table is using before you act on it. The underlying principle is unchanged: excess calcium impairs phosphorus absorption, and calcium above 1.0 percent also depresses zinc absorption.

Trace minerals for finishing pigs: iron 40 to 80 mg/kg, since mature digestive systems absorb it efficiently; copper 5 to 6 ppm as the nutritional baseline; zinc 80 to 100 ppm; manganese 2 to 4 mg/kg; selenium 0.15 to 0.30 mg/kg; iodine 0.14 to 0.20 mg/kg.

Vitamins for finishing pigs, per kilogram of diet:

  • Vitamin A: 1,300 to 1,600 IU, the lowest of any class
  • Vitamin D3: 150 to 220 IU
  • Vitamin E: 11 to 22 IU, with the stress level of 44 IU and above applying during heat, transport, mixing or disease challenge
  • Vitamin K: 0.50 mg
  • Choline: 0.30 to 0.50 g

Compare those with the weaner figures of 2,000 to 2,200 IU vitamin A and 30 to 44 IU vitamin E and you can see the size of the reduction available. A finisher premix is a genuinely different product from a nursery premix.

Phytase belongs in this lesson as much as in the grower one. Because plant phosphorus is 70 to 80 percent unavailable, and because finishing pigs eat the largest tonnage on the farm, phytase at 500 to 2,500 FTU per kg releasing 0.10 to 0.20 percentage points of available phosphorus produces its largest absolute saving here.

Worked example. A producer in Chipata, Zambia, finishes 220 pigs a year, each eating about 230 kg of finisher feed, so roughly 50 tonnes. He currently uses one premix across all finishing diets, a grower specification at 3 kg per tonne costing an illustrative 4.80 USD/kg.

  • Current premix cost: 3 x 4.80 = 14.40 USD per tonne, so 720 USD a year
  • He splits into two premixes: the grower specification for 27 to 60 kg, and a late finisher premix at 2.5 kg per tonne with vitamin A at 1,400 IU/kg of feed and vitamin E at 16 IU/kg, costing an illustrative 4.10 USD/kg, so 10.25 USD per tonne
  • Roughly 30 percent of his finisher tonnage is in the 27 to 60 kg band and 70 percent above it: 15 tonnes at 14.40 = 216 USD, plus 35 tonnes at 10.25 = 359 USD. Total 575 USD
  • Saving: 720 - 575 = 145 USD a year

He then adds phytase at 1,000 FTU/kg across all 50 tonnes. Taking a conservative release of 0.12 percentage points, that is 1.2 kg of available phosphorus per tonne, replacing 1.2 / 0.18 = 6.7 kg of dicalcium phosphate at an illustrative 0.95 USD/kg, saving 6.37 USD per tonne against a phytase cost of 2.60, so 3.77 USD per tonne net, or 189 USD a year.

Combined saving 334 USD, with calcium re-balanced after removing the phosphate. He keeps the stress-level vitamin E option on the shelf for hot months and for the week around mixing and transport, because that is when 44 IU/kg earns its keep.

Lesson 4.5~12 min

Feed Additives for Finishing

In this lesson
  • Rank finishing additives by published evidence rather than by sales claim
  • Calculate the cost per tonne and the required response for any additive
  • Apply the copper and mycotoxin binder decisions correctly in finishing diets

By the finishing stage most of the gut-health additives that matter in the nursery have done their work. What remains are a smaller set of tools that either protect the feed, improve digestion of the ingredients you can afford, or manage a specific risk. The discipline is the same in every case: get a written price, convert it to cost per tonne, and calculate what response is needed to break even.

The published hierarchy of evidence for alternatives to antibiotic growth promoters places probiotics, organic acids and phytogenic essential oils in the most extensively studied group; enzymes, immunostimulants and bacteriophages in the moderate evidence group; and antimicrobial peptides and short-chain fatty acid salts in the emerging group with limited data. That hierarchy should determine what you try first. Module 10 lesson 2 works through the ranking and buying order in detail for producers going antibiotic-free.

What is worth considering in a finishing diet:

  1. Phytase, at 500 to 2,500 FTU/kg, releasing 0.10 to 0.20 percentage points of available phosphorus. The most reliable return of any additive in plant-based diets.
  2. Non-starch polysaccharide enzymes, where the ration carries real fibre substrate such as brewers' grain, rice bran or wheat offal. Published enzyme response overall is 2 to 5 percent feed conversion improvement, ingredient-dependent.
  3. Propionic acid at 0.5 to 1.0 percent, which gives modest pH reduction and inhibits mould growth in feed. In humid African conditions the mould inhibition alone often justifies it in stored feed.
  4. Mycotoxin binders such as activated charcoal, bentonite or montmorillonite clays and yeast cell wall extract, at a published cost of 5 to 20 USD per tonne of premix, giving a 30 to 50 percent reduction in toxin bioavailability that varies by toxin and binder. Binders are a mitigation, not a licence to buy bad grain.
  5. Essential oil blends of carvacrol, cinnamaldehyde and thymol at 0.05 to 0.15 percent, with the strongest published responses in young pigs but documented residual benefits carrying into the fattening period.

Copper deserves a clear warning. The nutritional requirement is only 5 to 6 ppm. The pharmacological, antimicrobial range is 125 to 250 ppm, and chronic toxicity risk begins above 250 ppm. That is a narrow margin, mixing on a small farm is rarely accurate to within a few percent, and copper accumulates in the liver. Module 9 lesson 3 covers the doses and legal status; the finishing-specific point is that pharmacological copper is a nursery tool, not a finisher tool, and that finisher specifications sit at 5 to 6 ppm.

Worked example. A producer in Bukoba, Tanzania, finishes 200 pigs a year on 46 tonnes of feed. His stored maize showed visible mould in two of last year's batches and he is deciding on a binder plus a mould inhibitor.

  • Mycotoxin binder at the published range, taking 12 USD per tonne of feed: 46 x 12 = 552 USD a year
  • Propionic acid at 0.75 percent of the diet, illustrative price 1.90 USD/kg: 7.5 kg per tonne x 1.90 = 14.25 USD per tonne, so 656 USD a year
  • Combined: 1,208 USD a year, which is 6.04 USD per pig

That is a heavy cost, and it should push him toward prevention first. Drying grain to 15 percent moisture or below, storing below 15 degrees Celsius where possible, keeping stores dry and testing high-risk batches before purchase are all cheaper than treating contaminated feed. If he uses the binder only on batches that test positive, and the acid only during the humid season, his cost falls by more than half while covering the actual risk. Additives applied by season and by test result, rather than year-round by habit, is the difference between a managed cost and a leak.

Set a break-even test for every product. At 6.04 USD per pig, the binder and acid together need to save roughly 2.6 kg of liveweight per pig at an illustrative 2.30 USD/kg, or prevent about one death in every 7 pigs at a 45 USD weaner value plus feed already invested. Write the break-even down before you buy, then measure against it.

Lesson 4.6~12 min

Pre-Marketing Feeding Strategies

In this lesson
  • Calculate the marginal feed cost of the last ten kilograms of liveweight
  • Set a final-phase ration that protects carcass quality and food safety
  • Decide an optimum sale weight from your own buyer's payment method

The last four to six weeks decide three things at once: how much feed you spend, what the carcass looks like, and whether the pork is safe to sell. All three come together in a single decision about when to stop feeding and sell.

Start with the marginal arithmetic, because it is the part most producers never do. Feed conversion worsens as pigs get heavier. If a pig converts at 3.5 in the 90 to 100 kg band and 4.0 in the 100 to 110 kg band, then:

  • Feed for the 90 to 100 kg band: 10 kg x 3.5 = 35 kg
  • Feed for the 100 to 110 kg band: 10 kg x 4.0 = 40 kg
  • At an illustrative 0.42 USD/kg, that is 14.70 USD and 16.80 USD respectively
  • Marginal revenue for the extra 10 kg at an illustrative 2.30 USD/kg liveweight: 23.00 USD
  • Marginal profit on the last 10 kg: 23.00 - 16.80 = 6.20 USD, before pen cost and the risk of holding the pig longer

So on those numbers it still pays, but the margin is shrinking with every band and will eventually go negative. Do this calculation with your own feed conversion figures from Module 3 lesson 6 and your own buyer's price, and repeat it whenever feed or pig prices move. Note too that the answer depends heavily on how you are paid. A buyer paying flat liveweight rewards weight. A buyer paying on carcass weight and grade may penalise the extra backfat that comes with heavier pigs, which flips the answer.

The published specification for the final phases:

  1. 100 to 120 kg: SID lysine 0.70 percent, energy 3,150 kcal ME/kg, protein to energy ratio about 2.2
  2. 120 to 130 kg: SID lysine 0.65 percent, energy 3,100 kcal ME/kg, ratio about 2.1
  3. Calcium 0.46 percent and STTD phosphorus 0.26 percent for the 100 to 130 kg band
  4. Vitamin A 1,300 to 1,600 IU/kg, vitamin E 11 to 22 IU/kg with the 44 IU/kg stress level applying around transport

Three final-phase rules matter more than the numbers.

First, fat quality. Limit unsaturated fat sources in the last four to six weeks and shift toward more saturated sources, as lesson 3 sets out. This is the window in which fat composition actually changes.

Second, food safety and withdrawal. Any medicated feed or in-feed product with a withdrawal period must be removed in time, and the removal must be recorded. Module 10 lesson 5 covers the records and withdrawal discipline buyers audit. Mycotoxin limits also matter here: the aflatoxin limit for finishing pigs over about 45 kg is 200 parts per billion, against 20 ppb for breeding animals and young pigs, and deoxynivalenol is capped at 5 ppm for all ages.

Third, transport and handling. Vitamin E at the stress level of 44 IU/kg and above is published for exactly this kind of challenge, and pigs should have water available up to loading. The reference standards do not publish a fasting duration before transport, so follow your abattoir's instruction rather than a number you cannot verify.

Worked example. A producer in Mzuzu, Malawi, sells to two buyers. Buyer one pays a flat 2.25 USD per kg liveweight at the farm gate. Buyer two pays 3.40 USD per kg of carcass but deducts for carcasses with excessive fat.

  • Pig at 100 kg liveweight, dressing at roughly 72 percent: 72 kg carcass
  • Buyer one: 100 x 2.25 = 225.00 USD
  • Buyer two: 72 x 3.40 = 244.80 USD, provided the carcass grades
  • Holding to 110 kg costs 40 kg of feed at 0.42 = 16.80 USD, and adds 10 kg liveweight or about 7.2 kg of carcass
  • Buyer one at 110 kg: 247.50, a gain of 22.50 against 16.80 of feed, so plus 5.70
  • Buyer two at 110 kg: 79.2 x 3.40 = 269.28, a gain of 24.48 against 16.80, so plus 7.68, but only if the fatter carcass still grades

His decision is not simply to feed longer. It is to feed longer while holding lysine at the published band and cutting unsaturated fat, so that the extra 10 kg arrives as acceptable carcass rather than as a deduction. Substitute your own prices, dressing percentage and grading rules, and write the answer down before the next batch reaches 90 kg.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. Why does the published energy density of finisher diets fall as pigs get heavier?

Gut capacity rises with size, so a heavier pig can meet its energy needs from a bulkier, cheaper diet within the fibre ceiling.

2. Roughly how much maintenance net energy does a 100 kg pig require per day?

The published figure is about 2,371 kcal net energy per day for maintenance at 100 kg, against about 1,495 kcal at 50 kg.

3. Why does each kilogram of gain cost more feed as a pig gets heavier?

Both maintenance and the energy cost per unit of gain rise with bodyweight, which is why efficiency declines through the finishing period.

4. Adding 2 percent fat to a finisher diet should improve feed conversion by approximately how much?

At about 0.04 FCR points per 1 percent added fat, 2 percent gives roughly 0.08 points of improvement.

5. Beyond price, what is the main risk of adding cheap unsaturated oil in the final finishing weeks?

Dietary fatty acids are deposited largely unchanged, so unsaturated fat sources late in finishing produce soft pork fat and downgrade carcass quality.

6. What is the published SID lysine specification for a pig in the 100 to 120 kg phase?

The six-phase table places 100 to 120 kg pigs at 0.70 percent SID lysine with energy at 3,150 kcal ME/kg.

7. A herd with low lean growth potential should be fed which early finisher protein to energy ratio?

The published table gives 3.0 to 3.2 for low lean potential in early finishing, against 3.4 to 3.6 for high lean potential herds.

8. What is the carcass effect of lowering lysine relative to energy?

Lower lysine relative to energy shifts gain from lean tissue toward fat, increasing backfat. Higher lysine relative to energy does the opposite.

9. Why does a single finisher ration fed from 30 to 105 kg waste money?

Requirements fall steadily with weight, so a flat specification is simultaneously too rich at the top of the range and often too lean at the bottom.

10. Which amino acid ratio applies to methionine in finishing diets?

Methionine is published at 28 percent of lysine in finishing diets, with methionine plus cysteine together at 56 to 58 percent.

11. Why does a pig's carcass fat reflect its diet more closely than a cow's does?

Ruminants modify dietary fatty acids in the rumen. The pig deposits them largely unchanged, so diet composition carries straight into the carcass.

12. What is the published backfat target at the tenth rib for modern market carcasses?

The target is 0.60 to 0.75 inches, which is 15 to 19 mm, for gilt and barrow carcasses.

13. Above roughly what inclusion level are distillers grains linked to soft pork fat?

The standards flag distillers grains above about 15 percent, along with high-linoleic oils, as producing soft pork fat.

14. When does limiting unsaturated fat sources have the greatest effect on carcass fat quality?

Body fat turns over slowly, so the diet fed in the final weeks disproportionately determines the composition of the fat in the carcass.

15. Which pair of fat sources is more likely to firm carcass fat?

Palm oil and animal tallow are more saturated, which is why the published guidance suggests shifting toward them late in finishing.

16. What is the published calcium requirement for a pig in the 100 to 130 kg band?

Calcium falls from 0.65 percent at 27 to 60 kg to 0.46 percent at 100 to 130 kg as skeletal growth slows.

17. Why do finisher calcium to phosphorus ratios look wider than the grower rule of 1:1 to 1.5:1?

The finisher table uses calcium to STTD phosphorus, a digestible basis, so the numbers are not directly comparable with a total phosphorus ratio.

18. What is the standard vitamin E level for finishing pigs, and when should it be raised?

The finisher standard is 11 to 22 IU/kg, with the published stress level of 44 IU/kg and above applying during heat, transport, mixing or disease challenge.

19. Why does phytase produce its largest absolute saving in finishing rather than in nursery diets?

The per-tonne saving is similar, but finishing pigs eat far more tonnes, so the same rate of saving multiplies into the largest total.

20. What is the published iron requirement for finishing pigs?

Finishers need only 40 to 80 mg/kg because mature digestive systems absorb iron efficiently, unlike suckling piglets.

21. Which group of alternatives to antibiotic growth promoters is described as the most extensively studied?

The published hierarchy places probiotics, organic acids and phytogenics in the most extensively studied group, with enzymes and immunostimulants next.

22. What reduction in mycotoxin bioavailability do binders typically achieve?

Binders deliver a 30 to 50 percent reduction that varies by toxin and binder, which is why they are a mitigation rather than a cure for contaminated grain.

23. What is the copper level at which chronic toxicity risk begins?

The pharmacological range is 125 to 250 ppm and toxicity risk begins above 250 ppm, a narrow margin for on-farm mixing accuracy.

24. Why is propionic acid particularly useful in humid African feed storage?

Its main practical value at 0.5 to 1.0 percent in these conditions is mould inhibition, which protects the feed you have already paid for.

25. What is the correct first response to a mycotoxin risk in stored maize?

Prevention is listed as the primary strategy and is far cheaper than treating contaminated feed. Binders are for mitigation once contamination has occurred.

26. Why does the profit on each additional 10 kg of liveweight shrink as a pig gets heavier?

Efficiency declines with bodyweight, so the marginal feed cost of each extra kilogram rises while the price per kilogram usually stays the same.

27. A pig converts at 4.0 in the 100 to 110 kg band. How much feed does that 10 kg of gain require?

10 kg of gain at a feed conversion of 4.0 requires 40 kg of feed, which at typical prices is the largest feed cost of any 10 kg band.

28. How does the buyer's payment method change the optimum sale weight?

Flat liveweight payment rewards weight without regard to composition, while grade-based payment can deduct for excessive fat, reversing the decision.

29. What is the deoxynivalenol limit for swine of all ages?

DON is capped at 5 ppm for swine of every age, and voluntary feed intake already declines at 1 to 3 ppm.

30. What should guide fasting duration before transport to the abattoir?

The reference standards do not publish a fasting duration, so follow your abattoir's requirement rather than applying an unverified number.

Module 4 capstone

Build a Finishing Phase Plan and Marketing Budget for your own herd. Step 1: weigh a sample of pigs at three points across your finishing period and establish your actual average daily gain and feed conversion in each weight band. Step 2: write out every finisher ration you use with local ingredient prices, calculate cost per kilogram, and compare the SID lysine, energy, calcium and phosphorus of each against the published phase table in this module. Step 3: design a phase feeding programme with at least three finisher diets, stating the weight range, the specification and the feed budget in kilograms per pig for each, and calculate the total feed cost per pig against your current programme. Step 4: visit or telephone your buyers and record exactly how they pay, whether by liveweight, by carcass weight, by grade or by fat measurement, and what weight range they prefer. Step 5: using your own gain and feed conversion figures, calculate the marginal feed cost of the last ten kilograms of liveweight and compare it with the marginal revenue at your buyer's price, then state your optimum sale weight. Step 6: write a one-page finishing plan covering ration by phase, additive decisions with their cost per pig, the final six-week fat quality strategy, and your target sale weight and date for the next batch.
