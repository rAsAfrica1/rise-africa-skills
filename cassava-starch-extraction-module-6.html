1. Using Screens and Sieves for Separation

Screening is the step where you separate the starch milk from the fiber and other particles. After you grind or grate the cassava roots, you get a mixture of starch, water, fiber, and sand. You need to pass this mixture through a screen or sieve to catch the fiber and let the starch milk flow through. The screen has holes that are small enough to block fiber but big enough to let starch granules pass. A common size is 100 to 200 mesh (about 0.15 to 0.075 mm). In Africa, many small processors use a simple nylon cloth or a fine wire mesh stretched over a wooden frame. You can also buy a stainless steel sieve from a hardware store.

To do the screening, you pour the grated cassava mixed with water onto the screen. Use your hands or a paddle to move the pulp around so the starch can wash through. Keep adding water to help the starch pass. The fiber stays on top of the screen. You then squeeze or press the fiber to remove as much starch as possible. The liquid that passes through is called starch milk. It still contains fine fiber and sand, so you may need to screen it again with a finer screen. A second screening with a 200-mesh screen will remove more impurities.

For best results, screen in two stages: first with a coarse screen (like 60 mesh) to remove large fiber, then with a fine screen (like 200 mesh) to remove fine fiber and sand. You can build a simple screening table by mounting a screen at a slight angle so the starch milk flows off while the fiber stays on top. Always use clean water. If the water is dirty, it will contaminate your starch. After screening, you should have a smooth, milky liquid. If it feels gritty, there is still sand. If it looks fibrous, screen again. This step is critical because fiber left in the starch will make the final product poor quality and lower its price.

2. Types of Screens: Rotary, Vibrating, Static

There are three main types of screens used in cassava starch extraction: rotary, vibrating, and static. Each has advantages and disadvantages for small-scale African processors. A static screen is the simplest and cheapest. It is just a frame with a screen cloth set at an angle. You pour the cassava mash onto the top and let gravity pull the starch milk through while the fiber slides down. Static screens are easy to build with local materials like wood and nylon mesh. They work well for small batches, but they clog quickly and require frequent cleaning. You can improve them by adding a water spray bar to wash the fiber as it moves down.

A vibrating screen uses a motor with an off-center weight to shake the screen. This shaking helps the starch pass through faster and keeps the screen from clogging. Vibrating screens can handle more cassava per hour than static screens. In Africa, you can adapt a vibrating screen from a locally made sieving machine used for garri or maize. The motor can be a small electric motor (0.5 to 1 HP) or a diesel engine. The screen is usually mounted on springs or rubber mounts. The vibration separates the starch milk from the fiber efficiently. However, vibrating screens need more maintenance and power.

A rotary screen is a cylinder made of screen material that rotates slowly. The cassava mash is fed into one end, and as it tumbles, the starch milk passes through the screen while the fiber moves to the other end. Rotary screens are good for continuous processing and are less likely to clog because the tumbling action keeps the screen clear. They are more expensive and complex to build, but you can make a small one using a metal drum with screen panels. Rotary screens are common in medium-scale plants in Nigeria and Ghana. For a beginner, start with a static screen. As your production grows, consider a vibrating or rotary screen. Always match the screen type to your budget and production volume.

3. Washing and Dewatering Screens

After you extract the starch milk, you need to wash it to remove remaining impurities and then dewater it to remove excess water. Washing and dewatering screens are designed for these tasks. A washing screen is similar to a screening screen but with a water spray system. You pass the starch milk over the screen while spraying clean water. The water washes the starch through the screen, leaving behind fine fiber and sand. The diluted starch milk is then collected. Washing screens are often used after the initial screening to improve starch purity. You can build a simple washing screen by placing a fine screen (200 mesh) over a basin and using a watering can to spray water as you pour the starch milk.

Dewatering screens are used to remove water from the starch milk after washing. The starch milk is very dilute, often containing only 5-10% starch. Dewatering concentrates it. A dewatering screen is usually a fine screen that allows water to drain while retaining the starch. You can use a vacuum or just gravity. In small operations, you can pour the starch milk into a cloth bag and let it drain. The bag acts as a dewatering screen. For larger operations, a vibrating dewatering screen works well. The vibration helps water pass through quickly. After dewatering, the starch is a thick paste with about 40-50% moisture. This paste is then dried.

It is important to use clean water for washing. If you use dirty water, you will introduce new impurities. Also, the water you use for washing should be soft if possible. Hard water can affect starch quality. After washing, you can test the starch milk by letting it settle. If the water on top is clear, the starch is clean. If it is cloudy, there is still fine fiber. In that case, repeat the washing step. Dewatering screens should be cleaned regularly to prevent clogging. A clogged screen will slow down your production and may cause starch to be lost with the water. Always check the screen for holes or tears, as these will let fiber through.

4. Fiber Removal Techniques

Fiber removal is the key to getting pure starch. Cassava roots contain about 20-25% fiber on a dry basis. If you don't remove it, your starch will be brownish and low quality. The first step in fiber removal is proper grinding. If you grind the cassava too coarsely, the fiber will be in large pieces and easy to remove by screening. If you grind too finely, the fiber breaks into small pieces that pass through your screens. So, aim for a medium grind. A hammer mill with a screen size of 1-2 mm is good. After grinding, you mix the mash with water to form a slurry. The ratio is about 1 part cassava to 3-4 parts water. This helps the starch granules separate from the fiber.

The main fiber removal technique is screening, as covered earlier. But you can improve fiber removal by using a combination of techniques. One is the use of a centrifugal sieve or a paddle washer. A paddle washer is a tank with rotating paddles that wash the fiber while the starch passes through a screen. This is common in medium-scale plants. Another technique is to use a series of screens with decreasing mesh sizes. Start with a 40-mesh screen to remove coarse fiber, then 80-mesh, then 120-mesh, and finally 200-mesh. Each screen catches finer fiber. You can set up a simple screening cascade using buckets and screens.

After screening, you can also use a hydrocyclone to remove fine fiber. A hydrocyclone is a cone-shaped device that uses centrifugal force to separate particles by density. Starch is denser than fiber, so it settles to the bottom while fiber goes out the top. Hydrocyclones are expensive but very effective. For small-scale processors, you can use a settling tank. Let the starch milk sit for 4-6 hours. The starch will settle to the bottom, and you can pour off the water with floating fiber. Then add fresh water, stir, and let it settle again. Repeat 2-3 times. This is called the settling method. It is slow but requires no special equipment. Always check for fiber by rubbing a small amount of starch between your fingers. If it feels smooth, it's good. If it feels gritty or stringy, remove more fiber.

5. Recovering Residual Starch from Fiber

After you remove the fiber, it still contains a lot of starch. In fact, fresh cassava fiber can hold 20-30% of the total starch if you don't recover it. Recovering this residual starch increases your yield and income. The simplest way is to rewash the fiber. Take the fiber you collected from your screens and put it in a clean container. Add water at a ratio of 1 part fiber to 2 parts water. Stir vigorously for 5-10 minutes. This washes the starch off the fiber. Then pour the mixture through a fine screen (200 mesh) to separate the fiber from the starch milk. The starch milk that passes through can be added to your main batch. You can repeat this washing 2-3 times until the water coming out is clear. Each washing recovers more starch.

Another method is to use a screw press or a hydraulic press. You put the fiber in a cloth bag and press it. The pressure squeezes out the starch milk. The liquid is collected and screened. A simple manual press can be made with a car jack and a wooden frame. You can also use a mangle (wringer) like the ones used for laundry. For larger operations, a continuous screw press is used. The fiber is fed into the press, and as it moves through, the starch milk is squeezed out. The pressed fiber comes out drier and with less starch. The recovered starch milk is then screened and added to the main process.

A third method is to soak the fiber in water overnight. The starch dissolves or suspends in the water, and then you can separate it by screening. This is called steeping. After soaking, stir and screen. The fiber can be used as animal feed. The recovered starch milk should be processed the same way as your main starch milk. It is important to recover as much starch as possible because it directly increases your profit. For every 100 kg of fresh cassava roots, you can get about 20-25 kg of starch if you recover well. Without recovery, you might only get 15-18 kg. So, spending time on fiber recovery pays off. Always keep your recovery water clean and use a fine screen to avoid losing starch.

6. Maintenance and Cleaning of Screening Equipment

Your screening equipment will not work well if you don't maintain and clean it. After each use, you must clean all screens, sieves, and the frames. Starch dries and hardens like glue, blocking the holes. If you leave it, the next batch will be slow and inefficient. Start by rinsing with clean water. Use a soft brush to scrub the screen. Never use a wire brush on fine screens because it will damage the mesh. For nylon or cloth screens, wash with mild soap and water, then rinse well. For stainless steel screens, you can use a pressure washer on low setting. After washing, let the screens dry completely before storing. Store them flat or hanging, not folded, to prevent creases and tears.

Check your screens regularly for holes, tears, or worn spots. A small hole will let fiber through and contaminate your starch. You can patch small holes with a piece of the same screen material and a strong adhesive or by sewing with nylon thread. For larger tears, replace the screen. Keep a spare screen on hand. Also check the frames and supports for cracks or rust. Wooden frames can rot if left wet. Paint them with a waterproof paint or use treated wood. Metal frames should be painted or galvanized to prevent rust. Lubricate moving parts of vibrating screens, such as bearings and motors, according to the manufacturer's instructions. If you use a rotary screen, check the bearings and drive belt regularly.

Establish a cleaning schedule. After every production day, clean all equipment. Once a week, do a deeper clean: disassemble screens if possible, soak in a mild bleach solution (1 tablespoon per 5 liters of water) for 10 minutes to kill bacteria, then rinse thoroughly. This prevents mold and sour odors. Before starting a new batch, inspect the screens for cleanliness and damage. Keep a logbook to record when you cleaned and when you replaced screens. This helps you track maintenance. Also, train everyone who uses the equipment on proper cleaning. A clean screen means better starch quality and faster production. Remember, maintenance takes time, but it saves money by preventing downtime and starch loss. If a screen is clogged, you lose starch and time. If it's torn, you lose quality. So, take care of your screens, and they will take care of your business.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Screening and Fiber Separation in Cassava Starch Extraction

In cassava starch processing, screening and fiber separation sit at the heart of your recovery rate and therefore your margin. Every kilogram of starch you fail to separate from fiber is a kilogram you paid to grow, harvest, transport, wash, and grate, only to throw away as waste. For small and medium-scale processors across Africa from Nigeria's cassava belt to Ghana's Volta Region, Tanzania's coastal zones, and Uganda's Lake Victoria basin the difference between a profitable operation and a struggling one often comes down to how well you manage this single unit operation.

This practical reference consolidates the key knowledge from the module: screen selection, washing and dewatering, fiber removal techniques, residual starch recovery, and maintenance. It is written for the operator, the mill owner, and the entrepreneur scaling from 1 tonne/day to 20 tonnes/day of fresh roots.

Why Screening and Fiber Separation Drive Profit

Fresh cassava roots contain roughly 2030% starch, 6070% moisture, and 25% fiber (FAO, 2013). After grating or rasping, the resulting pulp is a slurry of free starch granules, bound starch, fiber, and water. A well-tuned screening system can recover 8592% of available starch; a poorly maintained one may drop to 6070%. At a market price of USD 400600 per tonne of dry starch, that gap translates to tens of thousands of dollars annually for even a modest operation.

Types of Screens: Matching Equipment to Your Scale

Three screen types dominate African cassava starch operations. Choosing the wrong one for your throughput is one of the most common and most expensive mistakes.

Rotary screens (trommels) are the workhorse for small to medium processors (110 t/day). A rotating cylindrical drum with perforated plates or mesh separates coarse fiber from starch slurry. They are robust, low-energy, and easy to fabricate locally. Their main limitation is lower capacity per unit area and a tendency to blind if not cleaned regularly.

Vibrating screens handle higher throughput (550 t/day) and give sharper separation. A vibrating motor drives the screen deck, keeping apertures clear and moving fiber to the discharge. They cost more, need a stable power supply, and require more maintenance but for a growing business, they are the upgrade path.

Static screens (side-hill or curved screens) have no moving parts. Slurry flows over a curved wedge-wire or mesh surface; starch passes through, fiber slides off. They are cheap, need almost no power, and are excellent for pre-screening or as a first stage before vibrating screens. Their weakness is lower recovery per pass and sensitivity to feed consistency.

Washing and Dewatering Screens

Washing screens serve two purposes: they rinse residual starch from fiber, and they dewater the slurry before drying. In practice, a counter-current washing arrangement where fresh water enters at the last stage and flows backward against the fiber can cut water use by 4060% while improving starch recovery.

For dewatering, a vibrating screen with a fine mesh (200325 mesh) can bring slurry from 58% solids to 2535% solids, reducing the load on your centrifuge or drying beds. In off-grid settings, simple gravity dewatering over a static screen followed by solar drying is a viable low-cost route.

Key operating parameters: feed consistency (keep slurry at 812% solids for optimal screening), screen aperture (match to your fiber size), and spray water pressure (24 bar is typical).

Fiber Removal Techniques

Fiber removal is not a single step it is a cascade. The most effective African processors use a three-stage approach:

  1. Coarse removal on a rotary or static screen immediately after grating. This removes the bulk of large fiber and prevents downstream blinding.
  2. Fine removal on a vibrating screen (100200 mesh) to capture smaller fiber particles.
  3. Polishing on a fine static or vibrating screen (200325 mesh) before dewatering.

Between stages, re-slurrying the fiber with fresh water (counter-current washing) releases bound starch. A simple rule: each additional washing stage can recover 37% more starch, up to a practical limit of 45 stages.

SAFETY WARNING HYDROGEN CYANIDE (HCN) AND MECHANICAL HAZARDS

Fresh cassava roots contain cyanogenic glucosides that release hydrogen cyanide (HCN) during grating and screening. Never work in an enclosed space without ventilation. Wear gloves, eye protection, and a dust mask. Do not allow workers to consume raw pulp or slurry. Keep all screen guards in place rotary and vibrating screens cause severe hand injuries. Lock out power before any cleaning or maintenance. In high-cyanide varieties (e.g., some bitter cultivars common in West Africa), ensure adequate washing and drying to reduce HCN to safe levels (below 10 mg/kg dry weight, per Codex Alimentarius).

Recovering Residual Starch from Fiber

Fiber leaving your final screen still contains 1530% starch on a dry basis. Recovering it is often the single highest-return improvement a processor can make. Techniques include:

  • Counter-current washing: pass fiber through 24 washing stages with fresh water flowing opposite to fiber movement.
  • Re-screening: re-slurry fiber and pass through a finer screen (200325 mesh).
  • Mechanical pressing: a screw press or hydraulic press extracts starch-laden water from fiber. Common in medium-scale Ghanaian and Nigerian mills.
  • Enzymatic or fermentation-assisted release: emerging low-cost methods using cellulase or controlled fermentation, still mostly at pilot scale in Africa.

Realistic recovery from fiber: 515% additional starch yield, depending on equipment and diligence. For a 5 t/day mill, that can mean an extra 50150 kg of starch per day worth USD 2090 daily at current prices.

Maintenance and Cleaning of Screening Equipment

Screen performance degrades silently. A blinded screen, a torn mesh, or a worn bearing can cut recovery by 1020% before anyone notices. Build these habits into your daily routine:

  • Daily: rinse screens with clean water after each shift; inspect mesh for tears, holes, or blinding.
  • Weekly: check vibration motor mounts, belt tension, and bearing temperature; tighten bolts.
  • Monthly: replace worn mesh, inspect rotary drum perforations, lubricate bearings, and calibrate feed consistency.
  • Annually: full overhaul replace bearings, re-balance vibrating motors, and re-level the screen frame.

Keep a simple logbook: date, screen type, mesh size, hours run, issue found, action taken. This data will tell you when to replace rather than repair and it is essential for food safety audits and buyer due diligence.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Match screen type to throughput: rotary for 110 t/day, vibrating for 550 t/day, static for pre-screening.
  2. Use a three-stage cascade: coarse fine polishing.
  3. Keep feed slurry at 812% solids for optimal screening.
  4. Install counter-current washing to cut water use and boost recovery.
  5. Recover residual starch from fiber target 515% extra yield.
  6. Inspect and rinse screens daily; replace torn mesh immediately.
  7. Log all maintenance and screen changes for audits and planning.
  8. Never bypass guards or clean screens while powered lock out first.
