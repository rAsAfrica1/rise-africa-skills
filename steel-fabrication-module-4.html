Lessons

1. Oxy-Fuel Cutting

Oxy-fuel cutting uses a flame from burning fuel gas (acetylene, propane, or LPG) mixed with oxygen to heat steel to about 1,100C, then a stream of pure oxygen burns the iron. It cuts mild steel from 3 mm to 300 mm thick. In Africa, LPG is common because acetylene is expensive and unstable in heat. You need a cutting torch, oxygen and fuel cylinders, regulators, hoses, and a spark lighter. Set oxygen pressure at 24 bar and fuel at 0.51 bar for 10 mm plate. Light the torch, adjust to a neutral flame (blue cone), preheat the edge until it glows orange, then press the oxygen lever and move steadily at 300500 mm/min. Keep the tip 35 mm above the plate. For clean cuts, use a tip size matching thickness: a #1 tip for 36 mm, #2 for 612 mm, #3 for 1225 mm. In dusty or windy sites, build a simple windbreak from corrugated sheets. Common mistakes: too much preheat (melts edges), too fast (incomplete cut), too slow (dross). Always wear goggles, gloves, and leather apron. In rural workshops, many use a 'cutting rig' made from a modified car alternator and a homemade torch; but safety first: never use petrol or improvised fuel. Oxy-fuel is ideal for thick sections, rusty steel, and demolition work where plasma is too expensive. Practice on scrap 10 mm plate: draw straight lines, circles, and bevels. After cutting, chip off dross with a chipping hammer and grind the edge if needed. Remember: oxygen is the cutting gas, fuel only preheats. Store cylinders upright, away from sun, and never oil fittings. With practice, you can achieve a cut quality good enough for welding without extra grinding.

2. Plasma Cutting

Plasma cutting uses an electric arc through a constricted gas (air, nitrogen, or argon) to create a plasma jet at 20,000C that melts and blows away metal. It cuts mild steel, stainless, aluminium, and copper from 0.5 mm to 50 mm (handheld) or 150 mm (mechanised). In Africa, small inverter plasma cutters (e.g., 40A, 60A) running on 220V single-phase are popular. For a 40A machine, cut up to 10 mm mild steel at 400 mm/min; for 60A, up to 20 mm. You need a clean, dry air supply at 46 bar (use a filter-regulator). Connect the work clamp to bare metal. Set current: 30A for 3 mm, 40A for 6 mm, 60A for 12 mm. Hold the torch perpendicular, drag the tip on the plate (or use a standoff guide). Start the arc, then move steadily. Too fast leaves dross; too slow melts a wide kerf. For stainless, use nitrogen to avoid oxidation. In dusty environments, clean the torch tip daily. Common problems: worn consumables (electrode, nozzle) cause poor cut; moisture in air causes double-arcing. Replace consumables after 12 hours of cutting. Plasma is faster than oxy-fuel on thin plate (<20 mm) and cuts any conductive metal. It produces a narrow kerf (12 mm) and less heat distortion. Safety: wear shaded goggles (shade #58), keep away from flammables, and ensure good ventilation for fumes. Many workshops in Lagos or Nairobi use a 60A plasma cutter on a homemade CNC table for repeat parts. For field work, a generator of at least 7 kVA is needed. Practice on 6 mm plate: cut straight lines, then circles. Keep a spare set of consumables. With practice, plasma gives clean, weld-ready edges.

3. Shearing and Guillotine Cutting

Shearing cuts straight lines in sheet metal using a guillotine shear or a bench shear. A guillotine has a fixed lower blade and a moving upper blade that forces the metal down, creating a clean cut without chips. In Africa, manual foot-operated shears (up to 1.5 mm) and hydraulic shears (up to 6 mm) are common in fabrication shops. For a 3 mm mild steel sheet, a 2-metre manual shear costs about $800$1,500. Set the back gauge to the desired width. Align the sheet against the side guide and hold it firmly. Step on the foot pedal or press the hydraulic button. The blade cuts from one side to the other. Keep hands clear of the blade. For thicker plate (612 mm), use a hydraulic shear with a hold-down to prevent slipping. Shearing produces a slightly deformed edge (rollover) but is fast and accurate for straight cuts. You can shear up to 3 mm on a manual machine; for 6 mm, you need a motorised shear. Common mistakes: cutting without clamping (sheet moves), dull blades (leaves burrs), and exceeding capacity (breaks machine). Always wear gloves. In many African workshops, a shear is used to cut blanks for doors, gates, and roofing sheets. For short cuts, a hand shear or nibbler works. Shearing is not for curves or thick plate. After shearing, deburr edges with a file or grinder. For production, set up a stop to repeat cuts. Maintain blades: sharpen or replace when they nick. A well-adjusted shear can cut 1,000 sheets before sharpening. Safety: never put fingers under the blade; use a hold-down. In rural areas, a simple lever shear mounted on a wooden bench can cut 1 mm sheet. Practice on 1 mm galvanised sheet: cut 100 mm strips. Check squareness with a try square. Shearing is the fastest way to cut straight edges in thin sheet.

4. Sawing and Abrasive Cutting

Sawing cuts metal with a toothed blade; abrasive cutting uses a high-speed disc that grinds through metal. For steel fabrication, common tools are hacksaws, portable band saws, chop saws (abrasive), and cold saws. A hacksaw with a 24 TPI blade cuts 25 mm round bar in about 2 minutes by hand. Use light pressure and let the blade cool. For faster work, a portable band saw (e.g., 1,000 W) cuts 100 mm pipe in 30 seconds. In Africa, a 355 mm abrasive chop saw (2,200 W) is common; it uses a 355 x 3 x 25.4 mm abrasive disc. For 50 mm square tube, a chop saw takes 1015 seconds. Always wear a face shield, hearing protection, and gloves. Clamp the work securely. Let the saw reach full speed before cutting. Do not force the disc; let it cut. Abrasive discs wear down; replace when diameter drops below 250 mm. Cold saws (with coolant) cut cleaner and cooler but are expensive. For stainless, use a dedicated stainless blade to avoid contamination. In many workshops, a 'cut-off saw' with a petrol engine is used for on-site cutting of rebar and pipe. For accurate cuts, use a mitre guide. Common mistakes: using a worn disc (breaks), cutting without clamping (kickback), and wrong blade for material. For aluminium, use a blade with fewer teeth (1014 TPI) and paraffin as lubricant. For thin sheet, use a fine-tooth blade (32 TPI). After cutting, deburr with a file. For large sections, a horizontal band saw with a coolant pump is ideal. In rural areas, a bow saw with a tungsten carbide blade can cut 50 mm steel. Always check the blade tension and alignment. Practice on 25 mm square tube: cut 10 pieces to length. Measure with a tape. Sawing and abrasive cutting are versatile but slower than shearing or plasma. Keep a spare disc or blade.

5. Laser and Waterjet Cutting

Laser cutting uses a focused beam of light to melt or vaporise metal; waterjet uses a high-pressure stream of water mixed with abrasive particles to erode metal. Both are computer-controlled (CNC) for high precision. In Africa, laser cutting is found in industrial hubs like Johannesburg, Lagos, and Nairobi; waterjet is rarer due to cost. A 1 kW fibre laser cuts 3 mm mild steel at 5 m/min, 6 mm at 2 m/min. It gives a kerf of 0.2 mm and a heat-affected zone of 0.1 mm. For stainless, use nitrogen assist gas. Waterjet cuts up to 100 mm steel with a 0.8 mm kerf, no heat distortion. A 4,000 bar pump with 0.3 mm abrasive (garnet) cuts 20 mm steel at 200 mm/min. Both need CAD files (DXF). For a small shop, outsourcing to a laser cutter costs about $2$5 per metre for 3 mm steel. You cannot do laser or waterjet by hand; they are for repeat parts, intricate shapes, and thin sheet. In Africa, many fabricators send DXF files to a service centre. If you invest, a 1.5 kW laser with a 1.5 x 3 m bed costs $50,000$100,000. Waterjet starts at $80,000. For most workshops, it is better to use plasma or shearing for simple cuts. However, for architectural panels, signage, and machine parts, laser gives a clean edge needing no finishing. Safety: laser requires enclosure (Class 1), waterjet needs guarding against high-pressure stream. In dusty environments, maintain clean optics and water filters. Practice by designing a simple bracket in Inkscape or LibreCAD, then sending to a service. Learn to nest parts to reduce waste. Laser and waterjet are not for thick, rusty steel; they need clean, flat material. For a beginner, focus on plasma and oxy-fuel first, then outsource laser for precision jobs.

6. Cut Quality and Edge Preparation

Cut quality determines how much grinding and welding you need. Key terms: kerf (width of cut), dross (resolidified metal on bottom edge), drag (lines angled back), and heat-affected zone (HAZ). For oxy-fuel, a good cut has sharp top edges, minimal dross, and a drag of less than 10% of thickness. For plasma, dross should be easily chipped. For shearing, edges are clean but may have rollover. After any cutting, you must prepare the edge for welding: remove dross, slag, and rust. Use a chipping hammer, then a 115 mm angle grinder with a 4.5 inch grinding disc. For a 6 mm plate, grind a 30 bevel on one edge for a V-groove weld. For a butt weld, leave a 2 mm root gap. Clean 25 mm back from the edge with a wire brush. For stainless, use a dedicated stainless brush to avoid contamination. In Africa, many welders skip edge prep, causing porosity and weak welds. Always check squareness with a try square and straightness with a straight edge. For critical joints, use a file to remove 0.5 mm of HAZ. If you cut with oxy-fuel, the edge may have a hard layer; grind it off. For plasma, the edge is usually weldable after light grinding. For laser, edges are often ready to weld. For waterjet, edges are smooth but may have abrasive residue; wash with water. Measure cut quality: use a vernier caliper to check kerf width (should be 12 mm for plasma, 0.2 mm for laser). Check for dross by visual inspection. If dross is heavy, adjust speed, gas pressure, or consumables. For sheared edges, deburr with a file. Always wear gloves and goggles. In a workshop, set a standard: no dross, no rust, no paint within 50 mm of the weld. Practice on 10 mm plate: cut with oxy-fuel, then grind a bevel. Weld it and bend test. Good edge prep saves time and ensures strong welds. Remember: cutting is only half the job; preparation is the other half.

<h2>Practical Reference: Cutting Processes for Steel Fabrication</h2>

<p>In African steel fabrication shops from the industrial clusters of Nairobi's Athi River and Lagos's Ikeja to the growing workshops of Accra, Lusaka, and Dar es Salaam cutting is where material cost, labour time, and downstream weld quality are decided. A fabricator who cuts accurately on the first pass saves grinding hours, reduces scrap, and can quote faster on tenders for gates, roofing trusses, water tanks, truck bodies, and structural frames. This practical reference consolidates the six cutting methods covered in this module into a single decision-and-action guide you can pin above the cutting bay.</p>

<p>The core business logic is simple: <strong>the cheapest cut is the one that needs no rework</strong>. Every method below has a cost-per-metre profile driven by consumables, power reliability, gas availability, and operator skill. In most African markets, oxy-fuel and abrasive sawing dominate because consumables are locally available and tolerate unstable power. Plasma and laser demand stable electricity and imported consumables, so they are best reserved for high-value, high-tolerance work where the margin justifies the input cost.</p>

Oxy-acetylene cutting produces flames above 3,100C and can cause flashback into the torch or hoses. Always fit <strong>flashback arrestors</strong> on both fuel and oxygen lines, check hoses for cracks before every shift, and never use oil or grease on oxygen fittings this causes spontaneous ignition. Keep a <strong>dry powder or CO extinguisher</strong> within 3 metres of every cutting station, and never cut near flammable materials, thinners, or LPG cylinders.

<h3>Cut Quality and Edge Preparation</h3>

<p>Cut quality determines how much grinding and weld prep follows. The key parameters are <strong>squareness, dross, kerf width, and HAZ</strong>. A square cut with minimal dross can go straight to welding; a rough cut with heavy dross costs labour and consumables.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Squareness:</strong> Check with a try square. Plasma and laser give the best squareness on thin-to-mid sections; oxy-fuel tends to round the top edge and can drag on thick plate.</li>
<li><strong>Dross:</strong> Low-speed plasma and oxy-fuel produce dross on the underside. Increase speed slightly or adjust gas pressure to reduce it. Dross must be chipped or ground before welding.</li>
<li><strong>Kerf compensation:</strong> For CNC cutting, program the kerf offset so the finished part matches the drawing. A 2 mm kerf on a 100 mm part is a 2% error if ignored.</li>
<li><strong>Edge preparation:</strong> For welds requiring full penetration, cut a bevel (3037.5) using a bevel torch, grinder, or bevelling machine. Oxy-fuel bevel cuts are common on thick plate; plasma bevel heads give cleaner results on stainless.</li>
<li><strong>HAZ removal:</strong> On high-carbon or alloy steels, the HAZ can harden and crack. Grind back 12 mm or preheat before cutting to control cooling rate.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ol>
<li><strong>Confirm material and thickness</strong> before choosing the method never guess.</li>
<li><strong>Check gas pressures and consumable condition</strong> (tips, electrodes, nozzles, blades) at the start of every shift.</li>
<li><strong>Set the correct speed and current</strong> for the thickness; test on a scrap piece first.</li>
<li><strong>Wear correct PPE:</strong> shade 810 helmet, gloves, apron, safety boots, respirator for coated steel.</li>
<li><strong>Verify squareness and kerf</strong> on the first cut before running the batch.</li>
<li><strong>Remove dross and grind edges</strong> before welding or painting.</li>
<li><strong>Log consumable usage</strong> so you can cost the job accurately and reorder in time.</li>
<li><strong>Keep the cutting area clear</strong> of flammables and ensure ventilation is working.</li>
</ol>
