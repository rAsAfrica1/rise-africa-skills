1. Comparing sorghum and millet to maize: climate resilience and market demand

Maize is the most common staple crop in Africa, but it is risky in dry areas. Maize needs 500-800 mm of rain and fails if there is a dry spell during flowering. Sorghum and millet are different. They can survive on 300-500 mm of rain and tolerate temperatures above 35C. In the Sahel, farmers who plant maize often lose everything when rains fail, while sorghum and millet still give 50-70% of a normal harvest. That difference is money in your pocket.

Market demand is also shifting. Breweries like Nigerian Breweries, Guinness Ghana, and SABMiller (now AB InBev) use sorghum and millet to make beer because they are cheaper than imported barley. Flour mills mix sorghum and millet flour with wheat for biscuits, baby food, and porridge. Open-air markets sell these grains daily for food. In 2023, a tonne of sorghum sold for about $250 in Nigeria, while maize sold for $200but sorghum often costs less to grow. Millet, though lower in yield, fetches higher prices ($300$350 per tonne) because it is used in health foods and traditional drinks like kunu.

Climate resilience means you harvest even in bad years. Market demand means you have buyers. Together, they make sorghum and millet a smart business choice. You spend less on irrigation and pesticides, and you can sell to breweries, mills, and local traders. Start by checking which crops your local market wants. Talk to a nearby brewery or flour mill to see if they buy sorghum or millet. Then plan your season around that demand.

2. Identifying local buyers: breweries, flour mills, and open-air markets

Before you plant, find your buyer. In most African countries, three main buyers exist: breweries, flour mills, and open-air markets. Breweries are the biggest. In Nigeria, Nigerian Breweries buys over 100,000 tonnes of sorghum each year for its malt drinks and beer. In Ghana, Guinness Ghana uses sorghum and millet. In Kenya, East African Breweries uses sorghum for low-cost beer. These companies often contract farmers in groups and provide seeds and training. To sell to them, you need to meet quality standards: clean grain, less than 14% moisture, and no stones. They also prefer large volumesat least 5 tonnes.

Flour mills are another buyer. Companies like Honeywell Flour Mills in Nigeria and Pembe Flour Mills in Tanzania buy sorghum and millet to blend with wheat flour. They want white, bold grains with low tannin. You can sell as little as 1 tonne, but you must dry your grain properly. Open-air markets are the easiest entry point. Traders buy in 100 kg bags. In Burkina Faso, a bag of millet sells for $40$50. In Uganda, sorghum sells for $30$40 per bag. You can sell directly to market women or through a cooperative.

To find buyers, visit your local market and ask traders what they need. Call the procurement office of a nearby brewery or flour mill. Join a farmer cooperativethey often have existing contracts. Start small: plant 1 hectare, sell to the open market, then scale up to a brewery contract. Always agree on price and quantity before harvest to avoid losses.

3. Calculating potential profit per hectare for sorghum vs. millet

Lets do the math for 1 hectare in a savannah zone. For sorghum: you need 10 kg of seed ($10), 50 kg of NPK fertilizer ($30), and labour for land prep, planting, weeding, and harvest ($120). Total cost: $160. A good sorghum yield is 1.5 tonnes per hectare. At $250 per tonne, revenue is $375. Profit = $375 - $160 = $215. For millet: seed costs $8, fertilizer $20, labour $100. Total cost: $128. Yield is lower1 tonne per hectare. At $300 per tonne, revenue is $300. Profit = $300 - $128 = $172. So sorghum gives higher profit per hectare in this example, but millet costs less to grow and is more drought-tolerant.

In drier areas, sorghum yield may drop to 1 tonne, giving profit of $90, while millet stays at 0.8 tonnes, giving profit of $112. So millet wins in very dry zones. Also, if you sell to a brewery, you may get a premium: $280 per tonne for sorghum, raising profit to $260. If you process millet into flour, you can earn $500 per tonne, but you need equipment. For a beginner, start with sorghum if you have moderate rain (400-600 mm) and access to a brewery. Choose millet if you are in the Sahel (300-400 mm) and sell to local markets. Always keep records of your costs and yields to know your true profit.

4. Understanding drought-tolerant crop advantages in Sahel and savannah zones

The Sahel is a belt of dry land south of the Sahara, stretching from Senegal to Sudan. Rainfall is 200-600 mm per year, often in one short season. Savannah zones have 600-1000 mm but with dry spells. In both, drought kills maize. Sorghum and millet have special features. Their roots go deepup to 2 metresto find water. Their leaves have a waxy coating that reduces water loss. They can stop growing during drought and resume when rain returns. This is called drought escape.

In the Sahel, millet is the most reliable crop. Varieties like SOSAT-C88 and ICMV IS 89305 mature in 75-90 days, escaping late-season drought. Sorghum varieties like Sariaso 14 and CSM 63E mature in 90-110 days and tolerate heat. In savannah zones, you can grow both, but sorghum yields more if rain is good. The advantage is not just survivalit is stable income. A farmer in Niger with millet harvests at least 500 kg per hectare even in a bad year, while maize gives zero. That 500 kg can feed a family and sell for $150.

Drought tolerance also means less irrigation. You save money on pumps and fuel. You also reduce risk of crop failure, so you can plan your finances. In 2022, a drought hit the Sahel, and maize farmers lost 80% of their crop, but sorghum and millet farmers lost only 20-30%. That is why development agencies like ICRISAT and AGRA promote these crops. For your business, choose drought-tolerant varieties from your national research institute. Plant early with the first rains. Use za pits or half-moons to catch water. These simple methods can double your yield in dry zones.

5. Case study: How a smallholder in Nigeria doubled income with millet

Aisha is a smallholder in Katsina State, northern Nigeria. She used to grow maize on 2 hectares. In 2020, drought hit, and she harvested only 200 kg of maizebarely enough to feed her family. She had no money for school fees. In 2021, an extension officer from the Sasakawa Africa Association introduced her to millet. He gave her 5 kg of improved millet seed (variety SOSAT-C88) and taught her to plant in rows with 75 cm spacing, apply 20 kg of NPK per hectare, and use za pits to capture water.

Aisha planted 1 hectare of millet and 1 hectare of maize. She spent $50 on millet (seed, fertilizer, labour) and $80 on maize. The rains were poor again. Her maize yielded 300 kg, which she sold for $90a loss. Her millet yielded 1.2 tonnes. She sold 800 kg to a local trader for $240 and kept 400 kg for food. Her profit from millet was $240 - $50 = $190. From maize, she lost $10. Total income from millet was more than double her previous years total income.

In 2022, she planted 2 hectares of millet and no maize. She joined a cooperative that sold to a flour mill in Kano. The mill paid $320 per tonne. She harvested 2.5 tonnes and earned $800. After costs of $120, her profit was $680. She bought a goat and paid school fees. Her advice: Millet is not a poor mans crop. It is a business. Use improved seed, plant on time, and find a buyer before you harvest. Aisha now trains other women in her village.

6. Setting your first season production goal and budget

Your first season should be small and focused. Aim to plant 0.5 to 1 hectare of sorghum or millet. A realistic goal is to harvest 1 tonne of sorghum or 0.8 tonnes of millet. That gives you $250$300 in revenue if you sell to a local market, or more if you get a brewery contract. Write this goal down: I will plant 1 hectare of sorghum and harvest 1.5 tonnes by [date].

Now make a budget. For 1 hectare of sorghum in a savannah zone: seed (10 kg improved variety) = $10; fertilizer (50 kg NPK) = $30; labour (land prep, planting, weeding, bird scaring, harvest) = $120; transport to market = $20; total = $180. For millet: seed (5 kg) = $8; fertilizer (25 kg) = $15; labour = $100; transport = $15; total = $138. If you cannot afford fertilizer, use compost or manure. You can also reduce labour by using family labour or a cooperative work group.

To fund your budget, save from other income or take a small loan from a village savings group. Many NGOs and government programmes give free or subsidised seed. Check with your local agricultural extension office. Keep a record book: write down every cost and every sale. After harvest, compare your actual profit to your goal. If you made a profit, increase to 2 hectares next season. If you lost money, find out whymaybe poor seed, late planting, or low price. Adjust and try again. Farming is a business; treat it like one.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Sorghum & Millet as a Business in Africa

This Practical Reference lesson is your working tool. It pulls together the numbers, buyers, and decision rules you need to treat sorghum and millet as a business not just a subsistence crop. Keep it open when you plan your season, negotiate with a buyer, or sit down with a lender. Everything here is drawn from official and peer-reviewed sources, with Africa-specific context and a business-first lens.

1. Why sorghum and millet beat maize in the market

Maize is the dominant staple in much of Africa, but it is also the most climate-fragile of the three cereals. Sorghum and pearl millet are C4 cereals with deeper root systems, waxy leaf cuticles, and the ability to suspend growth under stress and resume after rain traits that make them far more reliable in the Sahel and savannah zones. That reliability translates directly into business value: lower yield variance means more predictable revenue and easier access to credit.

On the demand side, sorghum and millet are no longer "poor people's food." Breweries (notably in Nigeria, Kenya, and Zimbabwe) use sorghum and millet as adjuncts in clear beer and traditional beverages. Flour mills blend them into composite flours for porridge, biscuits, and baby food. Open-air markets and urban supermarkets sell them as wholegrain health foods. This diversified demand base industrial, institutional, and retail reduces your price risk compared with maize, which is heavily influenced by government policy and import competition.

2. Identifying local buyers and what they pay for

Your buyer determines your variety, your quality standard, and your price. In most African markets, four buyer types dominate:

  • Breweries (e.g., Nigerian Breweries, Guinness Nigeria, Delta Beverages in Zimbabwe): buy white sorghum and pearl millet in bulk, typically 20100 tonnes per contract, with strict moisture (<12%) and aflatoxin specs. Prices are often set in advance, giving you price certainty.
  • Flour mills and food processors: buy sorghum and millet for composite flours, porridge, and snack products. They value consistent grain size, low foreign matter, and traceability.
  • Open-air and urban markets: buy smaller volumes, pay cash, and accept a wider quality range. Margins are lower but entry is easy.
  • Institutions (schools, hospitals, WFP/relief programs): buy in bulk, often through tenders. Requirements are documented and payment is reliable but slower.

Before you plant, visit at least two buyers and ask: What variety? What moisture? What aflatoxin limit? What price per tonne? What delivery point? Write the answers down. This is your production contract in draft form.

4. Drought-tolerant advantages in the Sahel and savannah

The Sahel receives 300600 mm of rainfall, often in a single erratic season. The savannah zones of Nigeria, Ghana, Burkina Faso, and Sudan have 6001,000 mm but with dry spells. In both zones, sorghum and millet outperform maize because:

  • They germinate and establish with less soil moisture.
  • They flower earlier and can escape terminal drought.
  • Their deep roots access sub-soil moisture that maize cannot reach.
  • They tolerate high temperatures (3540C) during grain filling.

For a business, this means you can plant in marginal fields that would fail with maize, and you can plant later if the rains are delayed a critical flexibility when you are juggling labour and cash flow.

5. Case study: How a smallholder in Nigeria doubled income with millet

Aisha, a 2-hectare farmer in Katsina State, Nigeria, grew maize for years. In 2021, a late-onset rainy season cut her maize yield to 0.8 t/ha, and she sold at 180 USD/t a gross margin of about 30 USD/ha after costs. In 2022, she switched 1 hectare to improved pearl millet (variety LCIC-MV1) and kept 1 hectare in maize. She sold 1.3 t of millet to a local flour mill at 310 USD/t, and 1.5 t of maize at 200 USD/t. Her millet hectare returned a gross margin of about 300 USD, versus 120 USD for maize. She used the extra cash to buy a second-hand thresher, which cut her post-harvest labour by 40%. By 2023, she had doubled her total farm income and signed a forward contract with the mill for 3 tonnes.

Lesson: The switch was not about yield it was about price, cost, and reliability. Aisha did not need more land; she needed a better crop-buyer match.

SAFETY WARNING AFLATOXIN AND GRAIN STORAGE

Sorghum and millet can accumulate aflatoxins toxic compounds produced by moulds if grain is not dried to below 1213% moisture and stored in a dry, ventilated place. Aflatoxin-contaminated grain is rejected by breweries and mills and is a serious health hazard. Never sell or consume mouldy, musty, or discoloured grain. Dry on a clean tarpaulin (not on bare ground), test moisture with a cheap meter before storage, and use hermetic bags or clean, dry drums. If you suspect contamination, contact your extension officer or the national food safety authority do not mix it with clean grain.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Choose your buyer first. Identify at least two local buyers (brewery, mill, market) and confirm variety, moisture, aflatoxin limit, price, and delivery point before planting.
  2. Pick the right crop for your zone. Pearl millet for the driest Sahel fields; sorghum for savannah zones with 400600 mm; keep maize only on your best-watered land.
  3. Use certified, drought-tolerant seed. Improved varieties (e.g., LCIC-MV1, SAMSORG, CSM series) give 3050% higher yields than local landraces under stress.
  4. Budget before you plant. Use Table 2 as a template; know your break-even yield and price.
  5. Dry and store properly. Target <12% moisture; use hermetic bags; never store on bare ground.
  6. Keep simple records. Track seed, fertilizer, labour, yield, and sale price per hectare this is what lenders and contract buyers want to see.
  7. Start small and scale. Plant 0.51 ha in your first season, prove the margin, then expand with a forward contract.
  8. Join a farmer group or cooperative. Group sales meet brewery and mill volume thresholds and improve your bargaining power.
