1. Accurate Measurement for Pipe Runs in Bathrooms and Kitchens

In bathroom and kitchen plumbing, a wrong measurement means wasted pipe, extra joints, and leaks. Start by understanding what you are measuring: pipe runs go from fitting to fitting, not wall to wall. For example, if you are connecting a sink P-trap to a wall drain, measure from the center of the trap outlet to the center of the drain pipe. Use a steel tape measure, not a cloth tape, because cloth stretches. Write down each measurement in millimeters. In many African markets, PVC pipes come in 3-meter or 6-meter lengths, so plan cuts to minimize waste. For a typical bathroom sink, the horizontal run from the wall to the P-trap might be 400 mm. Always measure twice: once at the actual location, and once after checking that the pipe will sit straight without forcing. Use a pencil or marker to mark the pipe where it will enter each fitting. Remember that fittings add length: a 90-degree elbow adds about 30-40 mm to the run. If you forget that, your pipe will be too short. For kitchen sinks, the run from the cabinet floor to the sink basket might be 600 mm vertical. Measure from the floor flange to the bottom of the basket strainer. Account for the slope: drain pipes need a fall of about 1 cm per 30 cm of horizontal run. So if your horizontal run is 900 mm, the end should be 30 mm lower than the start. Use a spirit level to check slope. In Africa, many homes have concrete walls, so you cannot easily move pipes inside walls. Measure from the outside of the wall to the fixture. Always add 10-20 mm extra on each end for insertion into fittings, then cut to final length after dry-fitting. Dry-fit all pipes and fittings before gluing. If a pipe is too long, you can cut more; if too short, you must start over. Keep a notebook with a sketch of the run, showing each measurement and fitting. This saves time and money.

2. Using a Hacksaw or Pipe Cutter for Clean Cuts on PVC

PVC pipe must be cut square, meaning the cut face is at exactly 90 degrees to the pipe length. A crooked cut causes leaks because the pipe will not seat fully in the fitting. You have two main tools: a hacksaw or a pipe cutter. A hacksaw is cheap and available everywhere in Africa. Use a 24-tooth per inch blade for PVC; too many teeth clog, too few chip the plastic. Secure the pipe in a vise or clamp it to a workbench. If you don't have a vise, use a G-clamp to hold it against a sturdy table. Mark the cut line all the way around the pipe with a pencil or marker. Then use a square (or a piece of paper folded to 90 degrees) to draw a line around the pipe. Start the cut with light strokes to make a groove, then use full strokes. Do not force the saw; let the teeth do the work. For a clean cut, rotate the pipe slightly after every few strokes. A pipe cutter (also called a plastic pipe cutter) works faster. It has a ratcheting mechanism and a sharp blade. Place the pipe in the cutter, tighten the handle until the blade touches the pipe, then rotate the cutter around the pipe while tightening slightly after each turn. Do not over-tighten or you will crack the pipe. The cutter makes a square cut with minimal burrs. For large diameters (50 mm and above), use a hacksaw or a mitre box. A mitre box helps keep the saw straight. After cutting, check squareness by standing the pipe on a flat surface; it should not wobble. If it wobbles, recut. Always wear safety glasses and gloves. PVC chips are sharp. In many African workshops, a hacksaw with a new blade costs less than a pipe cutter, but a pipe cutter saves time on many cuts. For a simple bathroom job, a hacksaw is fine. For production work, invest in a good pipe cutter. Remember: a clean cut saves you 10 minutes of deburring and prevents leaks.

3. Deburring and Chamfering Pipe Ends for Smooth Joints

After cutting PVC pipe, the cut edge has burrs: small ridges of plastic that stick out. If you don't remove them, the pipe will not slide fully into the fitting, and the solvent cement will not seal properly. Deburring means removing those burrs. Chamfering means making a slight bevel (angle) on the outside edge. Both are quick and essential. Use a deburring tool, a pocket knife, or a file. A deburring tool has a curved blade that shaves the inside and outside edges. If you don't have one, use a sharp knife to scrape the inside and outside edges at a 45-degree angle. Then use a file or sandpaper (80-120 grit) to smooth the edge. For chamfering, you want a bevel of about 1-2 mm on the outside edge. This helps the pipe slide into the fitting without pushing the cement out. A simple way: hold the pipe at a 45-degree angle and rub it on a piece of sandpaper or a file a few times. Do not over-chamfer; you don't want to reduce the wall thickness. After deburring and chamfering, wipe the pipe end with a clean rag to remove dust and plastic shavings. Then dry-fit the pipe into the fitting. It should go in easily but not loosely. If it is too tight, chamfer more. If too loose, the pipe may be out of round; you may need to cut a new end. In Africa, many plumbers skip deburring, but that leads to leaks. A leak in a wall means breaking the wall to fix it. Take 30 seconds to deburr and chamfer every pipe end. Use a rag to clean the inside of the fitting too. Solvent cement needs clean surfaces to melt and fuse the plastic. Any dust or oil weakens the joint. Keep a small deburring tool in your pocket. It costs little and saves hours.

4. Marking Insertion Depth for Solvent-Weld Joints

Solvent-weld joints (also called glue joints) work by melting the PVC surfaces and fusing them together. For a strong joint, the pipe must be inserted to the full depth of the fitting socket. If you insert too little, the joint is weak; too much, and the pipe bottoms out and may crack. So you must mark the insertion depth on the pipe. First, measure the depth of the fitting socket. For example, a 20 mm (1/2 inch) PVC elbow typically has a socket depth of about 15-20 mm. A 50 mm (2 inch) fitting has a depth of about 30-40 mm. Use a tape measure or a small ruler. Then, using a pencil or marker, draw a line around the pipe at that distance from the end. For example, if the socket depth is 20 mm, mark a line 20 mm from the cut end. When you insert the pipe into the fitting, you should see that line just at the edge of the fitting. This ensures full insertion. Some plumbers mark the pipe with a wrap of masking tape or a permanent marker. Do not use a grease pencil because it leaves residue. After marking, dry-fit the pipe and check that the mark aligns with the fitting rim. Then remove the pipe and apply solvent cement. Apply cement to both the pipe end (up to the mark) and the inside of the fitting. Push the pipe into the fitting with a quarter-turn twist. Hold for 30 seconds. The mark should disappear into the fitting. If the mark is still visible, you did not push hard enough. If the pipe bottoms out before the mark, the socket is shallower than you thought; recut and remark. In Africa, many leaks come from under-inserted pipes. A simple pencil mark prevents that. Keep a marker in your tool bag. Also, mark the pipe for orientation: draw a line along the pipe and fitting to align them. This helps when you have multiple joints. Always work quickly; solvent cement sets in seconds.

5. Avoiding Common Cutting Mistakes That Cause Leaks

Many PVC leaks are not caused by bad glue but by bad cuts. Here are the most common mistakes and how to avoid them. First, cutting at an angle. If your cut is not square, the pipe will not seat fully in the fitting. The cement will only touch part of the surface, leaving a gap. Always use a square or mitre box. Check squareness by standing the pipe on a flat surface. Second, not deburring. Burrs push the pipe out of the fitting or create a channel for water to leak. Always deburr and chamfer. Third, using the wrong blade. A hacksaw blade with too many teeth clogs and overheats the PVC, causing rough edges. Use 24 TPI. A dull blade smears the plastic. Change blades often. Fourth, cutting without measuring twice. If you cut too short, you waste pipe and may need a coupling. Always dry-fit before cutting. Fifth, not accounting for fitting depth. If you cut a pipe to the exact distance between two fittings, it will be too short because each fitting adds depth. Add the socket depth (e.g., 20 mm) at each end. Sixth, cutting on a dirty surface. PVC shavings and dirt get inside the pipe and contaminate the joint. Cut over a clean cloth or bucket. Seventh, using a wood saw. Wood saws have teeth that tear PVC. Use a hacksaw or pipe cutter. Eighth, cutting without clamping. If the pipe moves, the cut is crooked. Clamp it securely. Ninth, not marking the cut line all the way around. A partial mark leads to a crooked cut. Tenth, cutting pipe that is cold. In cold weather, PVC is brittle and cracks. Warm it slightly with your hands or leave it in the sun. In Africa, many of these mistakes happen because of rushing. Take your time. A clean, square, deburred cut is the foundation of a leak-free joint. Replace your blade when it starts to snag. Keep a file handy. And always test your joints with water before closing walls.

6. Practice Project: Cutting and Preparing a Simple Pipe Assembly

Now you will practice everything from this module by making a simple pipe assembly: a 90-degree elbow connecting two straight pipes. This is the most common joint in bathroom and kitchen plumbing. You will need: a 1-meter length of 20 mm PVC pipe, two 20 mm 90-degree elbows, a hacksaw or pipe cutter, a deburring tool or knife, sandpaper, a pencil, a tape measure, and PVC solvent cement. First, plan your assembly. Let's say you want a pipe that goes 300 mm up, then turns 90 degrees, then goes 200 mm horizontally. Measure and mark the first pipe: cut a piece 300 mm long. Remember to add insertion depth: each elbow socket is 20 mm deep, so cut the pipe 300 mm + 20 mm = 320 mm. Mark the cut line all the way around. Cut with a hacksaw, keeping the cut square. Deburr and chamfer both ends. Mark the insertion depth (20 mm) on each end. Repeat for the second pipe: 200 mm + 20 mm = 220 mm. Cut, deburr, chamfer, and mark. Now dry-fit the assembly: insert the first pipe into one elbow, then the second pipe into the other end of that elbow. Check that the marks align with the fitting rims. The assembly should look like an L shape. If any pipe is too long, recut. If too short, start over. Once dry-fit is correct, disassemble. Clean all surfaces with a rag. Apply solvent cement to the pipe end and inside the fitting. Push together with a quarter turn. Hold for 30 seconds. Wipe off excess cement. Repeat for the other joint. Let it cure for at least 1 hour before handling, and 24 hours before pressure testing. Test by blocking one end and filling with water. Check for leaks. If you see a leak, cut out the joint and redo it. This project teaches you measurement, cutting, deburring, marking, and gluing. Practice it three times. Each time, try to make the cuts cleaner and the joints stronger. In Africa, this simple assembly is used in almost every sink and shower installation. Master it, and you can build any pipe run.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Measuring, Cutting, and Preparing PVC Pipes

In African plumbing and construction markets from Nairobi's Gikomba hardware corridor to Lagos's Ladipo pipe traders the difference between a profitable job and a callback nightmare usually comes down to preparation. A solvent-welded PVC joint that leaks after handover costs you the pipe, the fittings, the labour, and often the client. This reference lesson consolidates the field knowledge you need to measure, cut, and prepare PVC pipes correctly the first time, so your business builds a reputation for work that holds.

PVC (polyvinyl chloride) is the dominant pressure and drainage pipe material across most African markets because it is cheap, chemically resistant, easy to work, and widely stocked. But it demands discipline: PVC does not tolerate sloppy cuts or rushed joints. This module gives you the numbers, the tools, and the checks that protect your margin.

Why Measurement Accuracy Drives Profit

Every millimetre of over-cut pipe is a fitting you cannot reuse, and every under-cut pipe is a joint that will not seat. In a bathroom or kitchen run, the most common failure is not the glue it is the measurement. Pipe runs must account for fittings, wall thickness, and the insertion depth that each fitting consumes. A 90 elbow does not add zero length; it consumes a specific insertion depth at each end.

A practical rule used by experienced installers in Kenya and South Africa: measure the centreline run, subtract the fitting allowances, and cut 23 mm long, then trim to fit. This "cut long, trim to fit" approach is faster than re-cutting and protects your material budget.

Cutting Tools: Choosing the Right Method

On site, the two practical options are a fine-tooth hacksaw (2432 TPI) or a rotary pipe cutter designed for plastic. A hacksaw is cheap and universal; a rotary cutter is faster and produces a squarer cut with less burr. For high-volume production work say, prefabricating bathroom pipe sets for a housing project a rotary cutter pays for itself in a week through reduced rework.

SAFETY WARNING PVC CUTTING AND SOLVENT CEMENT

PVC dust and solvent cement vapours are hazardous. Always cut in a well-ventilated area, wear safety glasses and a dust mask, and never use solvent cement in a confined space without forced ventilation. Solvent cement is flammable keep it away from open flames, sparks, and hot work. Wear nitrile gloves; PVC solvent cement can cause skin irritation and is harmful if inhaled. In African markets where workspace ventilation is often limited, take the cut outside or open both ends of the room before applying cement.

Deburring and Chamfering: The Step Most Installers Skip

After cutting, the pipe end has a burr on the inside and a sharp edge on the outside. If you do not remove these, three things happen: the burr scrapes cement off the fitting wall, the sharp edge pushes cement ahead of the pipe and creates a dry joint, and the pipe does not seat to full insertion depth. The result is a leak often not immediately, but after the first pressure test or thermal cycle.

Deburr with a deburring tool, a pocket knife, or a file. Then chamfer the outer edge at 1015 using a chamfering tool or a file. The chamfer should be about one-third of the wall thickness. This is not cosmetic it is what allows the pipe to slide into the fitting without wiping away the cement.

Marking Insertion Depth

Before applying cement, dry-fit the joint and mark the insertion depth on the pipe with a pencil or marker. This mark tells you whether the pipe has seated fully. If the mark is still visible after assembly, the joint is not fully inserted and will leak. In high-volume work, a simple depth gauge or a pre-marked jig speeds this up dramatically.

Common Cutting Mistakes That Cause Leaks

From field experience across African plumbing contractors, these are the mistakes that generate callbacks:

  • Cutting at an angle: A bevelled cut reduces contact area and creates a stress point. Always use a mitre box or a square to guide the cut.
  • Not deburring: Burrs scrape cement and prevent full insertion.
  • Forgetting the chamfer: Without it, the pipe pushes cement out of the joint.
  • Measuring without fitting allowance: The pipe ends up short and the joint fails under pressure.
  • Using a dull blade: It cracks the pipe and leaves a rough edge.
  • Cutting in cold weather without warming: PVC becomes brittle below 5C; warm the pipe slightly before cutting.
  • Not marking insertion depth: You cannot verify full seating.

Practice Project: Cutting and Preparing a Simple Pipe Assembly

Build a simple L-shaped assembly: two lengths of 25 mm PVC pipe joined by a 90 elbow. Measure the centreline run, subtract the elbow insertion allowance (19 mm each end), cut 2 mm long, deburr, chamfer, mark insertion depth, dry-fit, then solvent-weld. Pressure-test at 1.5 working pressure for 15 minutes. If it holds, you have the core skill for every bathroom and kitchen run.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Measure centreline run and subtract fitting insertion allowances.
  2. Cut 23 mm long, then trim to fit never cut short.
  3. Use a fine-tooth hacksaw (2432 TPI) or rotary pipe cutter.
  4. Deburr inside and outside edges immediately after cutting.
  5. Chamfer the outer edge at 1015 (about one-third wall thickness).
  6. Dry-fit and mark insertion depth on the pipe.
  7. Clean both surfaces with PVC cleaner before cementing.
  8. Apply cement to both surfaces, insert fully, and hold for 30 seconds.
