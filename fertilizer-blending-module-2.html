1. Nitrogen Sources: Urea, Ammonium Nitrate, etc.

Nitrogen is the nutrient that makes crops green and leafy. The most common nitrogen fertilizer used in Africa is urea, which contains 46% nitrogen. Urea is a white, prilled or granular solid that dissolves easily in water. It is popular because it is cheap and has high nitrogen content. However, urea has a problem: when it sits on the soil surface, it can turn into ammonia gas and escape into the air, especially in hot weather. To prevent this, you must incorporate urea into the soil or apply it just before rain. Ammonium nitrate (34% nitrogen) is another source, but it is more expensive and can be dangerous to store because it is an oxidizer. In many African countries, ammonium nitrate is regulated due to security concerns. Other nitrogen sources include ammonium sulfate (21% nitrogen and 24% sulfur), which is good for acid-loving crops and provides sulfur, and calcium ammonium nitrate (CAN, 27% nitrogen), which is less acidic than ammonium sulfate. When blending, you must consider the nitrogen source's compatibility with other materials. For example, urea can react with superphosphate to create a sticky mess. Also, urea has a low melting point and can absorb moisture, so keep it dry. In Africa, urea is often blended with phosphate and potash to make NPK blends. For a 50 kg bag of 20-10-10, you would need about 22 kg of urea, 22 kg of DAP, and 8 kg of muriate of potash, plus filler. Always wear gloves and a dust mask when handling these materials, as they can irritate skin and lungs. Store urea in a cool, dry place away from direct sunlight. If you are blending on a small scale, use a shovel and a concrete mixer or a rotating drum. Always test your blend for uniformity by taking samples from different parts of the pile.

2. Phosphorus Sources: DAP, MAP, TSP

Phosphorus is essential for root development, flowering, and seed formation. The main phosphorus fertilizers used in African blending are DAP (diammonium phosphate), MAP (monoammonium phosphate), and TSP (triple superphosphate). DAP contains 18% nitrogen and 46% phosphate (P2O5). It is the most widely used phosphorus source in Africa because it has high nutrient content and good physical properties. DAP granules are hard and uniform, making them easy to blend. MAP contains 11% nitrogen and 52% phosphate. It is slightly more concentrated in phosphorus and produces a more acidic reaction in the soil, which can be good for alkaline soils. TSP contains 0 to 46% phosphate (no nitrogen) and is often used when you want phosphorus without extra nitrogen. TSP is made by treating rock phosphate with phosphoric acid. It is available in granular form, but it can be dusty and may cake if stored in humid conditions. When blending, phosphorus sources are generally compatible with potassium sources but can react with calcium nitrate or ammonium sulfate. For example, mixing TSP with ammonium sulfate can cause the blend to become sticky due to the formation of gypsum. In Africa, rock phosphate is also used directly in some areas, but it is slow-release and better for acidic soils. To blend, you need to calculate the amount of each material based on the desired N-P-K ratio. For a 50 kg bag of 10-20-10, you would need about 11 kg of DAP (to supply 5 kg N and 10 kg P2O5), 8 kg of urea (to supply additional 4 kg N), and 8 kg of muriate of potash (to supply 5 kg K2O), plus filler. Always use a scale to weigh accurately. Store phosphorus fertilizers in a dry place, as they can absorb moisture and become difficult to handle. If you are using TSP, consider adding a conditioner like kaolin clay to prevent caking.

3. Potassium Sources: Muriate of Potash, Sulfate of Potash

Potassium is vital for water regulation, disease resistance, and fruit quality. The two main potassium fertilizers are muriate of potash (MOP, potassium chloride, 60% K2O) and sulfate of potash (SOP, potassium sulfate, 50% K2O). MOP is the most common and cheapest potassium source. It is a white or reddish crystalline material that dissolves readily. However, MOP contains chloride, which can harm chloride-sensitive crops like tobacco, potatoes, and some fruits. In those cases, SOP is preferred. SOP also provides sulfur, which is beneficial for oilseeds and legumes. SOP is more expensive and less available in some African countries. When blending, both MOP and SOP are compatible with most nitrogen and phosphorus sources, but they can react with ammonium nitrate to form hygroscopic compounds that absorb moisture. Therefore, if you blend MOP with ammonium nitrate, you should add a conditioner like diatomaceous earth or use the blend quickly. Potassium sources are also prone to caking during storage, especially in humid climates. To prevent caking, keep them in sealed bags and store in a dry place. For blending, you need to know the potassium content of your material. For example, to make a 50 kg bag of 15-15-15, you need about 12.5 kg of MOP (to supply 7.5 kg K2O), along with 16 kg of DAP, 6 kg of urea, and filler. Always weigh accurately. In Africa, MOP is often imported from Canada, Russia, or Israel, while SOP may come from Europe or China. Check the label for nutrient content and impurities. If you are blending on a small scale, use a clean concrete mixer or a rotating drum. Wear a dust mask because potassium dust can irritate your throat. After blending, test the uniformity by taking samples from different parts of the batch. Store the final blend in a cool, dry place, and use within a few months to avoid nutrient loss.

4. Secondary Nutrients and Micronutrients

Secondary nutrientsâcalcium, magnesium, and sulfurâare needed in smaller amounts than nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, but they are still essential for healthy crops. Micronutrientsâzinc, boron, iron, manganese, copper, molybdenum, and chlorineâare needed in very tiny amounts but can limit yields if missing. In African soils, sulfur deficiency is common, especially in areas with low organic matter and high rainfall. You can add sulfur through ammonium sulfate, single superphosphate (which contains 12% sulfur), or elemental sulfur. Magnesium can be supplied through dolomitic lime or magnesium sulfate (Epsom salt). Calcium is often added through lime or gypsum. For micronutrients, you can use zinc sulfate (contains 35% zinc), borax (contains 11% boron), and iron sulfate (contains 20% iron). These are usually applied in small quantities, often as a coating on the fertilizer granules or blended in as a fine powder. When blending, micronutrients must be distributed evenly because even a small overdose can be toxic. For example, boron should not exceed 0.5% of the blend. To incorporate micronutrients, first mix them with a small amount of filler or a carrier like sand or clay, then add to the main blend. In Africa, many soils are deficient in zinc, especially in the savanna regions. A common practice is to blend 1-2 kg of zinc sulfate per 50 kg bag of NPK. However, zinc can react with phosphate to form insoluble compounds, so it is best to coat the granules rather than blend them intimately. Similarly, boron can react with calcium. Always check compatibility. For secondary nutrients, you can use gypsum (calcium sulfate) as a filler that also provides calcium and sulfur. Dolomite (calcium magnesium carbonate) can be used as a filler and liming agent. Store micronutrients separately in a dry place, as they can absorb moisture and cake. Wear gloves and a mask when handling fine powders. Test your blend for uniformity by sampling.

5. Fillers, Conditioners, and Coatings

Fillers are materials added to a fertilizer blend to make up the weight and achieve the desired nutrient concentration. They are usually inert materials like sand, clay, limestone, or dolomite. Conditioners are added to improve the physical properties of the blend, such as reducing caking, improving flowability, and preventing dust. Coatings are thin layers applied to granules to control nutrient release, reduce dust, or prevent moisture absorption. Common fillers in Africa include river sand, kaolin clay, and limestone. Sand is cheap but adds no nutrients and can be abrasive. Kaolin clay is a good filler because it also acts as a conditioner, absorbing moisture and preventing caking. Limestone (calcium carbonate) and dolomite (calcium magnesium carbonate) are fillers that also provide calcium and magnesium, and they help neutralize soil acidity. Conditioners include diatomaceous earth, talc, and oils. Diatomaceous earth is a fine powder that absorbs moisture and prevents caking. Talc is also a good anti-caking agent. Vegetable oils or mineral oils can be sprayed onto granules to reduce dust and improve adhesion of coatings. Coatings include polymer coatings, sulfur coatings, and wax coatings. Polymer coatings are used for controlled-release fertilizers, which are more expensive but reduce nutrient loss. Sulfur coatings are used on urea to slow nitrogen release. Wax coatings are used to prevent moisture absorption during storage. When blending, add fillers and conditioners after mixing the main nutrients. For a 50 kg bag, you might add 1-2 kg of conditioner. Mix thoroughly to ensure even distribution. If you are coating, use a rotating drum and spray the coating material evenly. In Africa, many small-scale blenders use simple concrete mixers. Always wear a dust mask when handling fine powders like diatomaceous earth or clay. Store fillers and conditioners in a dry place. Test the blend for caking by leaving a sample in a humid place for a few days. If it cakes, increase the conditioner. Remember that fillers can affect the nutrient analysis, so calculate carefully.

6. Material Compatibility and Segregation

Not all fertilizer materials can be blended together. Some combinations react chemically, causing caking, nutrient loss, or even fire and explosion hazards. For example, ammonium nitrate should never be blended with urea, because the mixture becomes highly hygroscopic and can form a flammable compound. Ammonium nitrate is also incompatible with powdered metals, acids, and organic materials. Urea is incompatible with superphosphate, because the acidic superphosphate reacts with urea to release ammonia gas and water, creating a sticky mess. Urea is also incompatible with ammonium sulfate, as the mixture can become damp and cake. DAP and MAP are generally compatible with most materials, but they can react with calcium nitrate and ammonium sulfate to form gypsum, which causes caking. Potassium chloride (MOP) is incompatible with ammonium nitrate, as the mixture absorbs moisture rapidly. Potassium sulfate (SOP) is more compatible but still can cake with ammonium nitrate. To avoid problems, follow these rules: First, segregate materials that are incompatible. Store them in separate, clearly labeled bins. Second, when blending, add the least compatible materials last and mix quickly. Third, use conditioners like kaolin clay or diatomaceous earth to reduce caking. Fourth, avoid blending materials with different particle sizes, as they will segregate during handling. For example, if you blend fine urea with coarse DAP, the urea will settle to the bottom. To prevent segregation, use materials with similar particle size (2-4 mm) and mix thoroughly. In Africa, many blenders use a rotating drum mixer, which gives good uniformity. After blending, test for segregation by taking samples from top, middle, and bottom of the pile. If nutrients vary by more than 10%, remix. Always wear personal protective equipment: gloves, dust mask, and goggles. Keep a fire extinguisher nearby when handling ammonium nitrate. Store all materials in a cool, dry, well-ventilated area away from direct sunlight and ignition sources.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Raw Materials for Fertilizer Blending

This practical reference consolidates the essential facts a blending plant manager, procurement officer, or agribusiness entrepreneur in Africa needs when selecting, storing, and combining raw materials. Fertilizer blending is a margin business: profitability depends on getting the right nutrient sources at the right price, keeping them dry and free-flowing, and avoiding incompatible mixes that cake, segregate, or damage crops. Whether you are running a 5 t/hour batch blender in Nakuru, a 20 t/hour bulk blending unit in Kaduna, or a toll-blending operation serving cooperatives in Lilongwe, the physical and chemical properties of your raw materials determine your product quality, your storage losses, and your reputation.

Africa consumes roughly 3â4 million tonnes of fertilizer nutrients annually, but consumption per hectare remains among the lowest globally. The Abuja Declaration target of 50 kg/ha is still unmet in most countries. This gap is a commercial opportunity: blending allows you to tailor NPK ratios to specific crops and soils, differentiate from commodity importers, and capture value closer to the farmer. But blending only works if you understand the raw materials.

Nitrogen Sources

Nitrogen is the nutrient most limiting to cereal yields across African smallholder systems. The three dominant sources in blending are urea (46-0-0), ammonium nitrate (AN, 34-0-0), and calcium ammonium nitrate (CAN, 27% N). Urea is the cheapest per unit N and the most widely traded in Africa, but it is hygroscopic, prone to caking, and loses N through volatilization if surface-applied. AN is agronomically excellent and less volatile, but it is a security-sensitive oxidizer, regulated in many countries, and can become dangerous if contaminated with organic matter or combustible materials. CAN is the safest high-N option and is popular in South Africa and Kenya, but its lower analysis raises freight cost per unit N.

For blending, urea's critical weakness is chemical incompatibility with acidic phosphorus sources. When urea contacts superphosphate or TSP in the presence of moisture, it hydrolyses and releases ammonia; the resulting alkaline micro-environment can also cause reversion of water-soluble phosphate to less available forms. In practice, ureaâTSP blends should be avoided or made only with a conditioning agent and immediate use.

Phosphorus Sources

DAP (18-46-0) is the workhorse P source in African blends because it is granulated, relatively stable, and supplies both N and P. MAP (11-52-0) is preferred in acidic soils because it is slightly acidic and less likely to raise pH. TSP (0-46-0) is almost pure P and is used where no additional N is wanted, but it is acidic and reacts with urea. Rock phosphate is cheap but only effective in acid soils and is not suitable for bulk blending because of its fine, dusty nature.

Potassium Sources

Muriate of Potash (MOP, KCl, 0-0-60) is the dominant K source globally and in Africa because it is cheap and highly soluble. Its main drawback is chloride sensitivity in crops such as tobacco, some vegetables, and certain fruit trees. Sulfate of Potash (SOP, KâSOâ, 0-0-50) avoids chloride and supplies sulfur, commanding a premium price. For high-value horticulture in Kenya, Ethiopia, and South Africa, SOP-based blends are a real margin opportunity.

Secondary Nutrients and Micronutrients

African soils are increasingly deficient in sulfur, zinc, boron, and sometimes magnesium. Elemental sulfur, ammonium sulfate, kieserite, and langbeinite are common secondary nutrient carriers. Zinc sulfate, boric acid, and sodium molybdate are typical micronutrient sources. These are added at very low rates (0.1â2%), which makes uniform distribution difficult in simple blenders. Micronutrients are best added through a pre-blend or coating rather than direct dosing into a ribbon blender.

Fillers, Conditioners, and Coatings

Fillers such as sand, limestone, dolomite, and kaolin bring a blend up to the target analysis and weight. Conditioners (anti-caking agents) include kaolin, diatomaceous earth, and proprietary amines. Coatingsâoil, wax, or polymerâreduce dust, slow moisture uptake, and can carry micronutrients. In humid tropical Africa, a good coating and moisture barrier can be the difference between a free-flowing product and a bag of rock.

Material Compatibility and Segregation

Segregation occurs when particles differ in size, shape, or density. Urea prills (2â4 mm) and fine DAP granules can separate during conveying and bagging, causing nutrient variability in the bag. The practical rule: match particle size distributions within Â±10% and avoid excessive handling. Compatibility is a chemical issue: urea + TSP, AN + TSP, and urea + superphosphate are all problematic. Always run a small jar test before committing a production batch.

  • Ammonium nitrate is a strong oxidizer. Never store it near diesel, oil, sulfur, organic matter, or combustible dust. Contamination has caused catastrophic explosions (e.g., Beirut 2020, West, Texas 2013).
  • Do not blend AN with TSP, SSP, or any acidic phosphate in the presence of moistureâit can generate heat and toxic gases.
  • Use dedicated, grounded, non-sparking equipment. Avoid copper, zinc, and galvanized surfaces.
  • Keep urea and AN in separate, dry, ventilated stores. Control humidity below 50% where possible.
  • Train all staff on emergency response; post local fire and civil defence contacts visibly.
  • Comply with national explosives and fertilizer regulationsâAN is licensed in Kenya, Nigeria, South Africa, and most African states.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Match particle sizes of all blend components to within Â±10% to prevent segregation during conveying and bagging.
  2. Run a jar test for every new formulation: mix small quantities, add moisture, and observe heat, caking, or ammonia smell.
  3. Never blend urea with TSP or single superphosphate unless you use a proven conditioner and sell immediately.
  4. Keep AN isolated from acids, organic matter, and combustibles; follow national licensing rules.
  5. Control moisture: store raw materials on pallets, off concrete floors, with ventilation and humidity monitoring.
  6. Use coatings and conditioners in humid regions to prevent caking and dust.
  7. Verify nutrient analysis of finished bags by sampling and, where possible, laboratory testing.
  8. Calculate nutrient cost per kg, not just price per tonne, before choosing between MOP and SOP or urea and CAN.
  9. Train staff on compatibility charts and keep them posted at the blending line.
