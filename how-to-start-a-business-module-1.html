1. Step 1 — Find a Business Idea That Fits You

Every business that succeeds started with an idea that matched three things: what you can actually do, what you can actually afford, and what the market will actually pay for. Miss any one of those three and the business dies. This module shows you how to find an idea where all three line up.

Module 1 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~50 minutes reading · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 1.1 — The three questions that filter every idea

Before you spend a single dollar, ask three questions about any business idea you are considering. If you cannot answer yes to all three, the idea is not ready. Most failed small businesses fail because the founder skipped one of these three.

Question 1 — Can you actually do this? Not "could you learn it someday." Can you do it today, or can you learn it within 30 days? If the answer requires a 3-year apprenticeship, this is not a business you can start now. It might be a career change — but it is not a small business you launch next month.

Question 2 — Can you afford to start and survive? Not "can you afford the startup cost." Can you afford to start AND fund yourself for 6 months without any business income? Because most businesses take 3–6 months to break even. If you have $200 saved and the business needs $500 plus 6 months of living expenses, you cannot afford it yet.

Question 3 — Will anyone actually pay for this? Not "would this be nice to have." Will 20 specific people, whose names you can write down, pay real money for this within 30 days? If you cannot name 20 potential buyers, the market does not exist yet.

The 3-question test: Can you do it? Can you afford it? Will anyone pay? If the answer to any one of them is "no, not yet," stop. Fix that gap first. Then come back to this course.

Notice the discipline this creates. Most aspiring entrepreneurs fall in love with an idea before checking if they can actually pull it off. The three questions break that pattern. They force you to confront the three ways every business dies: founder cannot deliver, founder runs out of money, market does not exist.

Take a piece of paper. Write your current business idea at the top. Below it, write the three questions and answer each one honestly. Circle any "no." That circle is your first homework.

Practical exercise 1.1: Write your top business idea. Answer the three questions in writing — not in your head. Be brutally honest. If you have more than one idea, do this for the strongest three. Keep this paper — you will use it in later lessons.

Lesson 1.2 — Skills audit — what can you already do?

Your skills are your cheapest capital. Every skill you already have is money you do not have to spend on training, staff, or consultants. Before you look outside yourself for the business idea, look inside.

Skills come in three layers, and most people only count the first.

Technical skills — what you can do with your hands. Cooking, welding, farming, driving, sewing, coding, cutting hair, baking, brewing, fixing engines, building, painting, writing, drawing. These are the obvious ones. List every technical skill you have, even ones you consider "just a hobby."

People skills — how you work with others. Can you sell? Can you teach? Can you lead a team? Can you stay calm in a crisis? Can you make strangers trust you quickly? Some of the highest-earning small businesses in Africa are pure people-skill plays — consulting, sales, coaching, community work. Do not overlook these.

Resource skills — what you can access. Land, water, transport, a spare room, a workshop, an uncle who owns a shop, a cousin who is a nurse, a friend in customs. These are resources most people forget to count. They are often the difference between starting and not starting.

LayerExamplesBusiness ideas this unlocks
TechnicalWelding, cooking, drivingMetal fabrication shop, catering, transport
PeopleSelling, teaching, listeningConsulting, training, sales agency, coaching
ResourcesLand, a room, a networkFarming, small factory, retail, import

The best business for you is usually where your skills overlap with the market's needs. If you have three technical skills, two people skills, and one resource, you have six starting points. Most people think they have none because they only count the technical layer.

Practical exercise 1.2: Take an hour. Write down every skill you have in all three layers. Aim for at least 20 items. Do not filter — write everything, then circle the top 5. Those 5 are the raw material for your business idea.

Lesson 1.3 — Capital reality — what can you afford to lose?

The number that matters is not how much money you have. It is how much you can afford to lose completely and still eat next month. That is your startup budget. Everything else is not available.

Three common mistakes here:

Mistake 1 — counting money you need for rent. If you have $500 saved and $400 goes to rent this month, you do not have $500 of capital. You have $100 of capital and a rent bill.

Mistake 2 — counting borrowed money as capital. A loan is not capital — it is an obligation. A loan you cannot repay from business income in 6 months is not funding, it is a trap. Only count borrowed money if you already know how the business will produce the repayment.

Mistake 3 — ignoring the survival runway. You need enough to start the business plus enough to live on while the business gets going. If startup costs $200 and you need $150/month to live, then over 6 months you need $200 + $900 = $1,100 before you start. Not $200.

Capital availableWhat you can realistically start
Under $100Trading, reselling, services (no equipment), mobile work, agent work
$100 – $500Small production, market stall, kiosk, tools-based services
$500 – $2,000Small workshop, salon, small farm, food processing, transport (motorbike)
$2,000 – $10,000Proper premises, small factory, delivery vehicle, multiple staff
$10,000+Manufacturing, import, wholesale, fleet, multiple locations

Be honest with yourself. If your capital is $300 and the business you want needs $5,000, you have three choices. You save for 2 years and start later. You start a smaller version that fits your capital now. You find investors or partners. Every one of those is legitimate. What is not legitimate is pretending your $300 is going to make a $5,000 business happen.

Never use rent money, school fees, or medical savings as startup capital. Not for any business, ever. If those are the only funds you have, your startup budget is $0, and you should focus on saving or on services that need no capital (freelancing, agent work, selling on commission).
Practical exercise 1.3: Write two numbers. Number one is the money you can afford to lose completely. Number two is your monthly survival need. Multiply number two by 6 (or by 3 if you have other income). Add that to number one. That total is your real startup budget. Write it down. That is your ceiling for now.

Lesson 1.4 — Market check — is anyone already selling this?

There are two failure modes here, and beginners fall into both. The first is choosing a market that is already so crowded there is no room. The second is choosing a market so empty that no demand exists.

The truth is somewhere in between. Competition is not a bad sign — it is proof that demand exists. What matters is whether you can differentiate, and whether the market is growing or shrinking.

Here is the actual test. Walk around your target area for one full day. Count how many businesses offer what you want to offer. Note their prices, their hours, their location, and how busy they are. Talk to at least five of them — most will happily tell you about the trade.

Then ask three questions:

  • Is the market growing? Are there more customers than five years ago, or fewer? Are new businesses opening, or old ones closing?
  • What do customers complain about? Every existing business has a weakness. Slow service, high prices, poor quality, inconvenient hours, unfriendly staff. That is your opening.
  • Can you serve a specific slice better? Not "better than everyone in every way" — that is impossible. But "faster for the lunch rush" or "cheaper for bulk buyers" or "politer for elderly customers." One clear advantage is enough to start.
Rule of thumb: If you find zero competitors in a market that should have them, your idea is probably not viable. If you find a dozen, you can still enter — but only with a specific advantage that is obvious to customers within 30 seconds of meeting you.

One more thing. Ask existing operators whether they would want to sell the business. Sometimes the fastest way to enter a market is to buy an existing operation from a tired owner. A failing business with a good location and existing customers is often a better buy than starting from scratch.

Practical exercise 1.4: Spend one full day in the market for your idea. Count competitors. Talk to five. Write down the single most common complaint you hear. That complaint is your competitive angle. Put it in one sentence.

Lesson 1.5 — The 20-idea test — scoring your options

Now that you have skills, capital, and market intelligence, generate a long list of possible ideas — at least 20. Do not judge them yet. Write them all.

Once you have 20, score each one from 1 to 5 on five criteria:

CriterionScore 1 (bad)Score 5 (great)
Skill fitWould take years to learnCan start today with existing skills
Capital fitNeeds 10× your budgetFits your budget with room to spare
Market demandHard to find buyersCustomers already asking for it
Margin potentialThin, volume-only businessStrong margins, repeat customers
Your energy for itYou would dread doing it dailyYou would happily do it for years

Add the five scores. The highest total wins. If two ideas tie, pick the one you can start this week.

Notice two things. First, "your energy for it" is not optional. A business you dread will not survive month 3, no matter how profitable the numbers look. Second, the scoring forces you to confront ideas that fail on skill or capital — before you spend money on them.

Most people who do this exercise are surprised by which idea wins. The fancy idea they were excited about comes third. The boring service business they had dismissed comes first, because it fits all five criteria.

Practical exercise 1.5: Write at least 20 ideas. Score every one on the five criteria. Rank them. Circle the top three. Do not skip the "energy" score — be honest about which businesses you would actually enjoy running for three years.

Lesson 1.6 — Narrowing to one idea and committing for 90 days

You have three top ideas. Now you must pick one and commit to it for 90 days. Not 90 days of "trying" — 90 days of doing this business and only this business. No side projects, no half-hearted experiments.

Why 90 days? Because most businesses need at least that long to reach the first honest signal: either customers are paying and it is growing, or they are not and it is not. Less than 90 days and you cannot tell. More than 90 days and you have sunk cost into an idea that could have been tested faster.

During the 90 days, keep a simple weekly log with four numbers:

  • Money in — every dollar the business earned
  • Money out — every dollar spent
  • Customers — the number of distinct paying customers
  • Hours worked — the time you put in

At the end of week 12, read the log honestly. If customers are up and revenue is up, keep going. If both are flat after 90 days of real effort, you have three options: pivot the offer, change the market, or stop and start the next idea. All three are good decisions. Only one is bad: continuing to sink time and money into a business that is not working because you are afraid to admit it.

Commitment rule: For 90 days, you do not start another business. Not one. If a new idea comes to you — and it will — write it in a notebook and close the notebook. You will look at it on day 91.

This rule is not cruel. It is protecting you from the most common cause of failed small businesses: the founder who tries three things at once and finishes none. One idea, 90 days, full focus. That is the whole discipline.

Write out your choice on a single sheet of paper. Date it. Put it somewhere you will see every day — your wallet, your bathroom mirror, your phone lock screen. That sheet of paper is your contract with yourself. If you break it, you will know.

Practical exercise 1.6: Write one sentence: "For the next 90 days, I am committed to [idea] and I will not start another business until [date]." Sign it. Date it. Put it somewhere visible.

Module 1 quiz

Answer these five questions. You need 4 out of 5 to unlock module 2.

  1. Name the three questions that filter every business idea. Why must all three be yes?
  2. What is the difference between a technical skill, a people skill, and a resource skill? Give one example of each from your own life.
