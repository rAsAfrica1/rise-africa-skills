1. Properties of Vine Charcoal

Vine charcoal is made by burning grapevines, usually the prunings from vineyards. In Africa, you can find it in art supply shops in cities like Cape Town, Nairobi, or Lagos, or you can make your own if you have access to grapevines. It is a soft, brittle charcoal that produces a range of greys, from light silver to deep charcoal black. Because it is so soft, it breaks easily, so handle it gently. A single stick is usually about 10-15 cm long and 5-10 mm thick. It is lightweight and has a slightly rough texture.

When you draw with vine charcoal, it glides smoothly over paper, leaving a fine, dusty line. It is excellent for quick sketches and for covering large areas quickly. The marks are easily erased with a kneaded eraser or a chamois cloth, which makes it perfect for blocking in shapes and values. However, this also means that vine charcoal smudges easily, so you need to fix your drawings to prevent them from rubbing off. It is not as dark as compressed charcoal or charcoal pencils, but you can build up layers to get darker tones.

Vine charcoal is often used in life drawing and landscape sketching because it allows you to work fast and make corrections. It is also great for creating soft gradients and atmospheric effects. In Africa, where art supplies can be expensive, vine charcoal is a cost-effective option because you can make it yourself from grapevine prunings. To make your own, cut grapevines into sticks, bundle them tightly in a metal container, and burn them in a low-oxygen fire until they are completely black. Let them cool, and they are ready to use. This is a sustainable way to produce art materials from agricultural waste.

One key property of vine charcoal is that it does not bind well to paper. It sits on the surface, so it can be lifted off with a kneaded eraser. This makes it ideal for underdrawings and for techniques like subtractive drawing, where you cover the paper with charcoal and then erase to create highlights. However, for a permanent drawing, you must use a fixative. Overall, vine charcoal is a versatile medium that is perfect for beginners because it is forgiving and easy to control.

2. Vine Charcoal vs. Willow Charcoal

Vine charcoal and willow charcoal are both natural charcoals made from burnt wood, but they have different properties. Vine charcoal is made from grapevines, while willow charcoal is made from willow branches. Both are soft and produce a range of greys, but vine charcoal is generally harder and produces a slightly lighter, cooler grey. Willow charcoal is softer and darker, and it smudges more easily. Vine charcoal holds its shape better and is less likely to break in your hand, but it still breaks easily. Willow charcoal is even more brittle and often comes in thinner sticks.

In terms of mark-making, vine charcoal gives a more controlled line, while willow charcoal is more powdery and creates broader, softer strokes. Vine charcoal is better for detailed work and for building up layers without smudging too much. Willow charcoal is great for very dark, expressive marks and for covering large areas quickly. Both are easily erased, but willow charcoal erases even more completely, leaving almost no residue. Vine charcoal leaves a slight stain, which can be useful for creating a base tone.

In Africa, willow charcoal is less common because willow trees are not as widespread as grapevines. Grapevines are grown in countries like South Africa, Egypt, Morocco, and Algeria. So vine charcoal is more readily available or can be made locally. Willow charcoal is often imported and more expensive. If you are on a budget, vine charcoal is the better choice. However, if you need very dark blacks, you might want to combine vine charcoal with compressed charcoal or use willow charcoal for the darkest areas.

When choosing between them, consider your subject. For a landscape with soft, atmospheric effects, vine charcoal works well because it allows for subtle transitions. For a dramatic portrait with strong contrasts, willow charcoal might be better for the deep shadows. But you can use both in the same drawing. Start with vine charcoal for the initial sketch and then use willow charcoal to deepen the darks. Remember that both need fixing. In a hot, dry climate, fixative is essential because the charcoal dust can blow away. So, experiment with both and see which one suits your style.

3. Techniques for Vine Charcoal

Vine charcoal is a versatile medium, and there are several techniques you can use to get the best results. First, always work on textured paper, like cartridge paper or charcoal paper. Smooth paper will not hold the charcoal well. Use a medium-grit sandpaper block to sharpen your vine charcoal to a point for detailed lines. You can also use the side of the stick for broad strokes. To create a uniform tone, rub the charcoal into the paper with a chamois cloth or a paper stump. This is called blending. For lighter areas, lift off charcoal with a kneaded eraser. You can also use a brush to sweep away excess dust.

One key technique is layering. Start with light pressure and build up layers gradually. Vine charcoal is transparent, so you can see the paper through it. This allows you to create depth. If you press too hard, you will fill the tooth of the paper and it will be hard to add more layers. So, use light strokes and build up. For darker areas, apply more pressure or use multiple layers. You can also use cross-hatching: draw parallel lines in one direction, then cross them with lines in another direction. This creates texture and value.

Another technique is subtractive drawing. Cover your paper with a layer of vine charcoal, then use a kneaded eraser to erase out highlights. This is great for drawing clouds, water, or bright objects. You can also use a chamois to wipe out large areas. For fine details, use a charcoal pencil or a piece of vine charcoal sharpened to a point. Remember to work from light to dark. Start with your lightest values and gradually add darker ones. Vine charcoal is forgiving, so you can erase mistakes easily. But be careful not to overwork the paper, as it can become muddy.

In Africa, where natural light is often bright, you can use vine charcoal to capture strong contrasts. Practice on newsprint or cheap paper to save money. Set up a still life with local objects like calabashes, woven baskets, or fruit. Use a lamp or window light to create shadows. Experiment with different strokes: hatching, cross-hatching, scumbling (light circular strokes), and stippling (dots). Keep your charcoal sticks clean by wiping them with a cloth. Store them in a rigid box to prevent breakage. With practice, you will develop control and confidence.

4. Layering with Vine Charcoal

Layering is the key to creating rich, dimensional drawings with vine charcoal. Because vine charcoal is soft and transparent, you can build up many layers to achieve a wide range of values. Start with a light sketch using very light pressure. Use the side of the charcoal to cover large areas. Then, use a chamois or paper towel to blend the first layer into a smooth, even tone. This becomes your base. Let it be light grey. Now, add a second layer, focusing on the mid-tones. Use slightly more pressure but still keep it light. Blend again, but leave some areas unblended for texture.

For the darkest areas, you can use a combination of vine charcoal and compressed charcoal. Compressed charcoal is darker and harder to erase, so use it sparingly. Apply it over the vine charcoal in the shadow areas. You can also use a craft knife to scrape charcoal dust onto the paper and then rub it in. This creates a very dark, velvety black. To create highlights, use a kneaded eraser to lift out charcoal. You can shape the eraser into a point for fine lines. Another technique is to use white charcoal or white pastel for the brightest highlights, but only if you are working on toned paper.

Layering works best when you allow each layer to settle. Do not blow on the paper; instead, tap it gently to remove excess dust. If you blow, you might spit and ruin the drawing. Work from light to dark, and always keep your hand off the paper by using a mahl stick or a piece of clean paper under your hand. In Africa, humidity can affect how charcoal adheres. In coastal areas, the paper might absorb moisture and make the charcoal smudge more. So, work in a dry environment if possible, or use a fixative between layers to set them.

Practice layering with a simple exercise: draw a sphere. Start with a light circle. Add a mid-tone layer on one side. Then add a dark layer in the shadow. Blend each layer. Finally, erase a highlight. You will see how the layers create a three-dimensional effect. Apply this to landscapes: layer the sky with light greys, the distant hills with mid-greys, and the foreground trees with dark greys. Remember, patience is important. Rushing will result in a flat drawing. Take your time and enjoy the process.

5. Fixing Vine Charcoal Drawings

Vine charcoal drawings are fragile because the charcoal sits on the surface of the paper. If you touch them, they smudge. If you store them loosely, they rub off. So, you must fix them. Fixative is a liquid that you spray over the drawing to bind the charcoal to the paper. You can buy fixative at art supply stores, but it can be expensive. In Africa, you can make your own fixative using skim milk or hairspray. Skim milk is a traditional fixative: mix one part skim milk with two parts water, put it in a spray bottle, and spray a light coat over the drawing. Let it dry. Hairspray works too, but choose an unscented, cheap brand. Test on a scrap first to make sure it does not yellow the paper.

When using fixative, work in a well-ventilated area, preferably outdoors. Hold the spray can or bottle about 30 cm from the drawing. Spray in light, even coats. Do not soak the paper. One or two light coats are enough. If you spray too much, the paper will buckle and the charcoal might bleed. Let it dry completely between coats. Fixative will darken the charcoal slightly, so your drawing will become a bit darker and less dusty. It also makes the charcoal more resistant to smudging. However, it does not make it completely permanent. You can still smudge it with heavy rubbing.

If you cannot find fixative or milk, you can store your drawings between sheets of glassine paper or tracing paper. Do not use newspaper because the ink will transfer. You can also roll the drawing loosely and store it in a tube. But for a portfolio, fixative is best. In humid climates, fixative also protects against moisture. If you plan to frame the drawing, use a mat to keep the glass away from the surface. Otherwise, the charcoal might stick to the glass. Always sign your drawing with a charcoal pencil after fixing, not before, because the fixative might blur the signature.

For a professional finish, you can apply a second fixative after framing. But be careful: some fixatives are flammable. Keep away from fire. In Africa, where power cuts are common, avoid electric sprayers. Manual spray bottles are fine. Practice fixing on a test piece to get the distance and amount right. Remember, a well-fixed drawing will last for years. So, do not skip this step. It is the difference between a sketch and a finished artwork.

6. Practice Project: Vine Charcoal Landscape

Now it is time to put everything together. For this project, you will draw a landscape using vine charcoal. Choose a scene you know well: a baobab tree in a field, a village market, or a mountain range. Work from a photograph or from life. You will need: vine charcoal sticks (a few thicknesses), a kneaded eraser, a chamois cloth, a blending stump, a sheet of A3 charcoal paper (or cartridge paper), a drawing board, masking tape, and fixative. Set up your workspace with good lighting. Tape your paper to the board at the corners.

Start with a light sketch. Use the side of a vine charcoal stick to block in the main shapes: the horizon line, the position of the sun or moon, the large masses of trees or buildings. Do not worry about details. Keep it light. Use a chamois to blend this first layer into a soft grey base. This is your sky and distant land. Now, add mid-tones. Use the tip of the charcoal to draw the outlines of hills, trees, and foreground elements. Use hatching to create texture. For the sky, use light, diagonal strokes. For the ground, use horizontal strokes. Blend some areas but leave others rough for contrast.

Next, add the darkest values. Use compressed charcoal or a darker vine charcoal for shadows: the dark side of trees, deep shadows in the foreground, and any dark accents. Be careful not to overdo it. Remember, vine charcoal is transparent, so you can always add more. Use your kneaded eraser to lift out highlights: the sun, reflections on water, or light hitting a rock. Shape the eraser to a point for fine lines. You can also use a brush to sweep away charcoal dust. Step back from your drawing often to see it from a distance. This helps you judge values.

Finally, fix your drawing. Take it outdoors or to a well-ventilated area. Spray two light coats of fixative, letting it dry between coats. If you made your own milk fixative, test it first. Once fixed, your landscape is complete. Sign it and date it. This project will teach you how to manage layers, control values, and use vine charcoal effectively. You can repeat this with different scenes: a desert, a coastal view, or a cityscape. Each time, focus on one new technique. With practice, you will become proficient. Show your work to others for feedback. Remember, every artist starts somewhere. Keep drawing.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Vine Charcoal in African Charcoal Enterprises

Vine charcoal is the artisan's charcoal light, clean, and prized for sketching, fine detail, and controlled tonal work. For African charcoal businesses, it represents a high-margin, low-volume product line that sits comfortably alongside bulk lump charcoal and briquettes. This Practical Reference consolidates the technical properties, comparative performance, layering methods, fixing techniques, and a field-ready practice project, with a business lens throughout.

Vine charcoal is traditionally made from grapevine cuttings, but in Africa it is more commonly produced from similar thin, fast-growing woody vines and twigs including wild grape (Lannea spp.), passion fruit vine, and selected Combretum twigs. The defining characteristic is the thin, cylindrical, low-density stick that burns to a soft, friable, uniformly black charcoal with minimal ash and low resin content.

1. Properties of Vine Charcoal

Vine charcoal is the softest of the traditional drawing charcoals. It produces a pale-to-mid grey-black mark that lifts easily with a kneaded eraser, making it ideal for underdrawing and expressive gesture work. Because it is made from thin, low-density feedstock, it lacks the hard, shiny inclusions found in compressed charcoal and leaves almost no residue on the paper surface.

2. Vine Charcoal vs. Willow Charcoal

Willow charcoal is the traditional European benchmark. African producers often ask whether willow can be grown locally it can, in cooler highland zones such as Kenya's Aberdares, Tanzania's Southern Highlands, and Ethiopia's highlands but vine charcoal from local feedstock is usually cheaper and faster to produce. 3. Techniques for Vine Charcoal

Vine charcoal rewards speed and confidence. Because it is soft, it responds to pressure and to the side of the stick. Core techniques:

  • Side-stroke shading: snap a 1015 cm stick and drag the flat side across the paper for broad, even tone.
  • Tip work: sharpen on fine sandpaper for thin lines and detail.
  • Smudging: use a chamois, paper stump, or finger to blend. Great for atmospheric skies and African savannah haze.
  • Lifting: kneaded eraser pulls highlights from mid-tones essential for clouds and dust.
  • Reduction: work dark-to-light, removing charcoal to build form.

4. Layering with Vine Charcoal

Layering is where vine charcoal outperforms compressed charcoal. Because each layer is friable, it can be partially lifted before the next is applied, producing depth without muddiness. A practical workflow:

  1. Layer 1 Gesture: light side-strokes to establish composition.
  2. Layer 2 Shadow mass: build mid-tones with a snapped stick.
  3. Layer 3 Blend: smudge with chamois to unify.
  4. Layer 4 Detail: sharpen the tip for foreground texture (grass, bark, rock).
  5. Layer 5 Highlights: lift with kneaded eraser for light and atmosphere.

For African landscape work baobab silhouettes, acacia thorn, red-earth roads layering in three to five passes gives the tonal depth collectors expect.

5. Fixing Vine Charcoal Drawings

Vine charcoal never fully "binds" to paper. Without a fixative, drawings smudge in transit a real problem for African artists shipping to galleries in Europe and North America. Options:

  • Workable fixative: spray in thin coats, 30 cm from the surface, in a ventilated area.
  • Final fixative: matte or satin, applied in 23 light passes.
  • Hairspray (low-cost): common in local markets, but yellowing and inconsistent use only for practice work.
  • Glassine interleaving: place between stacked drawings during transport.
  • Matting and framing: the most reliable protection for high-value pieces.
SAFETY WARNING Charcoal Dust and Fixative Vapours

Vine charcoal dust is a respiratory irritant. Prolonged inhalation in poorly ventilated workshops can cause chronic bronchitis and, in documented cases, pneumoconiosis among charcoal workers. Always:

  • Wear an N95 or FFP2 mask when sanding, sharpening, or packing charcoal.
  • Work in cross-ventilated spaces; use extraction fans in production rooms.
  • Apply spray fixatives outdoors or in a spray booth never in a closed studio or near open flames.
  • Wash hands and forearms after handling; avoid eating or smoking in work areas.
  • Store charcoal away from moisture, fuels, and ignition sources.

Source: ILO, Encyclopaedia of Occupational Health and Safety Charcoal (4th ed., 2012); WHO, Air Quality Guidelines (2021).

6. Practice Project: Vine Charcoal Landscape

Objective: produce a finished A3 vine charcoal landscape of an African scene for example, a baobab at sunset over the Limpopo, Mount Kilimanjaro at dawn, or a Sahel acacia grove using at least four layers and one lifting pass.

Materials: A3 cartridge or Ingres paper (160220 gsm), 68 vine charcoal sticks of mixed thickness, kneaded eraser, chamois, sandpaper block, matte fixative, N95 mask.

Steps:

  1. Sketch the horizon and main tree mass in light side-strokes (5 minutes).
  2. Block in the sky with broad side-strokes; smudge with chamois.
  3. Build the land mass in mid-tone; leave the light source (sun) as paper white.
  4. Sharpen a stick and add foreground detail grass, rocks, bark.
  5. Lift cloud highlights and rim-light on the tree with a kneaded eraser.
  6. Deepen the darkest accents (tree trunk, shadow under canopy).
  7. Fix in 23 light passes outdoors; allow 15 minutes between coats.
  8. Sign, date, and interleave with glassine for storage.

Business extension: photograph the finished piece in natural light, list it on a print-on-demand platform (e.g., local Art Kenya, African Artists' Foundation marketplace), and offer signed gicle prints. A single vine charcoal original can generate a print run of 2550 units a strong margin for a small studio.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Source vine feedstock from agricultural residue (vineyards, passion fruit farms) to cut input cost.
  • Carbonise at 350450 C in a retort or drum kiln for uniform, low-ash charcoal.
  • Grade sticks by diameter (35 mm, 69 mm, 1015 mm) for distinct SKUs.
  • Test erasability and mark density on every production batch before packing.
  • Pack in moisture-barrier sleeves with desiccant for export.
  • Provide a bilingual (English/French or English/Swahili) usage card in each pack.
  • Enforce dust masks, ventilation, and no-smoking rules in production areas.
  • Track export HS code 4402 and price against willow charcoal competitors quarterly.
