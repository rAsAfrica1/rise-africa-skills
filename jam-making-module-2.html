JAM MAKING · MODULE 2 OF 12

Fruit Sourcing and Selection

Fruit quality decides the jam before you light the stove. Where to buy, what to look for, how to use seconds and imperfect fruit, and how to build a year-round supply from a seasonal harvest.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  • Judge fruit ripeness by feel, smell, and appearance
  • Identify the fruits that make the best jam and why
  • Buy direct from farmers and negotiate a fair price at harvest
  • Use seconds and imperfect fruit without losing quality
  • Preserve fruit for year-round production
  • Cost your fruit supply honestly per jar
Lesson 2.1

Understanding fruit quality and ripeness

Jam is made from fruit, sugar, acid, and time. If the fruit is wrong, no amount of sugar or technique will save it. This is the lesson that decides whether your jam tastes like a memory or like medicine.

The four stages of fruit

StageFeelSmellBest for
Under-ripeHard, no giveNothing or greenNothing — the pectin is high but the flavour is not there. Use only as a small percentage blended with ripe fruit.
Just-ripeFirm, slight giveLight, clean fruit scentJam that needs to hold shape (chunks, whole-fruit, marmalade)
Fully ripeSoft but not mushyStrong, sweet fruit scentMost jam. Maximum flavour, still holds texture. This is the sweet spot.
Over-ripeVery soft, almost liquidFermenty, slightly alcoholicFast-cook jam, sauce, or fruit butter. High sugar, low pectin. Cook quickly.

The most common mistake

Beginners pick fruit at maximum ripeness for eating, because that is what tastes best raw. That is exactly wrong for jam. Fully ripe for eating means low pectin and high water — your jam will not set without added pectin, and the flavour will be flat after long cooking.

Pick for jam when the fruit is just-turning-ripe to fully-ripe, but before it softens past its peak. A handful of under-ripe fruit mixed in (10–15%) brings natural pectin without hurting flavour.

The fruits that make the best jam

Not every fruit makes good jam. The best jam fruits share three qualities: natural acidity, natural pectin, and strong flavour that survives cooking with sugar.

Jam fruits by natural pectin and acidity. Excellent fruits set without help; good fruits need added pectin; tricky fruits need both pectin and lemon juice.

The African context — your fruit is often better than you think

Many of the fruit varieties grown across Africa are naturally higher in pectin and flavour than the commercial varieties sold in Europe. Wild guava, indigenous plums, marula, baobab, tamarind, and sour tropical fruits often set beautifully without any added pectin at all. If your grandmother's jam set without pectin, that is why.

Experiment with local varieties. They may be your competitive advantage.

Lesson 2.2

Sourcing fruit — direct from farmers

The single biggest cost in your jam is fruit. Buying at the right source, at the right time, changes your margin by 30% to 70% per jar. This is where a jam business is won or lost on the economics.

The four sources, ranked by cost

SourceCostQualityReliability
Direct from farmer at harvestLowestVariable — you must inspectSeasonal, requires relationships
Farmers' market at closeLowOften good — traders discount end-of-day stockWeekly, requires you to be there
Wholesale produce marketMediumConsistentReliable, but you compete with everyone else
Supermarket / retailHighestGoodAlways available, kills your margin

The farmer relationship that changes everything

Find three farmers within driving distance who grow the fruit you want to make jam from. Visit them at harvest. Explain what you do. Offer to buy fruit that is not perfect for the fresh market — seconds, fruit with blemishes, fruit that is over-ripe, fruit that will not sell on the stall that day.

That fruit is currently waste or animal feed to the farmer. To you it is your raw material at a fraction of retail price. Both sides win. Two or three of these relationships will supply your entire year.

The economics in one table

Take mango as an example. One 250ml jar of mango jam needs about 500g of peeled fruit — around 700g of whole mango with skin and stone.

SourcePrice per kgCost per jarJars per $10
Direct from farmer at harvest$0.30$0.21~47
Farmers' market close-of-day$0.50$0.35~28
Wholesale market$0.90$0.63~16
Supermarket$2.00$1.40~7

Same jar. Same recipe. The difference between buying from a farmer and buying from a supermarket is $1.19 per jar. On 100 jars a month, that is $119 a month — over $1,400 a year — for the same work.

What to ask a farmer before you buy

  • "What variety is this, and does it set?" Some varieties have more pectin than others. Farmers often know.
  • "What is your price per kilogram for seconds?" The seconds price is usually 30–60% less than the first-grade price.
  • "Can you hold fruit for me between harvest and my collection?" Do they have a cool store or shade?
  • "If I buy 50kg a month, will you hold the price for one season?" Lock in the price before harvest, when farmers are nervous about selling.
  • "Can I see the tree, and can I taste this year's fruit?" Anyone who refuses is hiding something.

What to refuse, always

  • Fruit with mould on any part. Mould produces toxins that spread through the whole batch. Never cut off the mouldy bit and use the rest.
  • Fermented fruit. If it smells of alcohol or vinegar, it is already spoiling. Your jam will taste sour or vinegary.
  • Fruit from a tree treated with insecticide within the last week. Ask. Wash thoroughly if in doubt.
  • Fruit with an unknown spray history. You are making a food product. Traceability matters.
Lesson 2.3

Using seconds and imperfect fruit

This is the single biggest lever on your jam economics, and most beginners never touch it.

"Seconds" are fruit that are perfectly edible but will not sell at first-grade price. Common reasons: cosmetic blemish, slight over-ripeness, odd shape, small size, uneven colour. Supermarkets reject them. Farmers have no market for them. You buy them at half price or less.

Sorting seconds: what to keep, what to discard, and how to process them fast.

The three rules for seconds

  1. Reject anything with mould, fermentation, or insect damage inside the flesh. Cosmetic blemish on the skin is fine. Damage to the flesh is not.
  2. Process them the same day. Seconds are closer to spoiling than first-grade fruit. If you cannot process within 24 hours, freeze them or cook them into a base immediately.
  3. Adjust the recipe if the fruit is very ripe. Very ripe fruit has less pectin, so you will need more lemon juice or added pectin. Recipes assume just-ripe fruit.

What you can and cannot use

ConditionUse?Notes
Slight skin blemishYesCut off the blemish when prepping, use the rest
Odd shape / small sizeYesNobody sees the fruit, only the jam
Slightly over-ripeYesAdd extra lemon juice. Cook faster.
Very ripe / mushyYes, with pectinAdd commercial pectin — natural pectin is gone
Skin discoloured with mouldNoMould produces invisible toxins. Do not use.
Smells of alcohol or vinegarNoAlready fermenting. Spoils the whole batch.
Insect hole in the skin, flesh undamagedCut and useCut a generous margin around the hole, use the rest
Insect damage to the fleshNoFlesh is compromised. Discard.
Froze and thawedYesPerfectly fine for jam. Adjust for extra water.

The 60% rule

If your seconds cost 40% of first-grade fruit and 80% of the fruit is usable, your effective cost per usable kilogram is 40/0.8 = 50% of first-grade — half price, with no quality loss in the final jam. This is how small jam businesses compete on price without competing on margin.

Lesson 2.4

Building a year-round fruit supply from a seasonal harvest

Fruit is seasonal. Jam sells all year. The gap between the two is where most jam businesses stumble — either they only produce for a few months, or they buy expensive out-of-season fruit, or they cannot meet demand when a buyer wants 200 jars in November.

The answer is preservation. Buy in season, preserve the fruit, use it later.

Four ways to preserve fruit for year-round jam production

MethodHow long it lastsQuality when usedBest for
FreezingUp to 12 months at −18°CVery good — flavour close to freshMost fruit, especially soft fruits
Pulp and bottle (pasteurised pulp)6–12 months in cool darkGoodFruit that is easy to pulp (mango, guava, papaya)
Drying12+ monthsFair — texture changesFruit for fruit butters, not for chunky jam
Bottled in syrup12+ monthsGood — fruit stays firmFruits you want as whole or halved pieces in jam

Freezing the harvest — the simplest method

  1. Buy at harvest when fruit is at its cheapest. A 50kg load at $0.30/kg is $15. In season, that is 50kg of usable fruit.
  2. Prepare the same day. Wash, peel, stone, cut into jam-sized pieces.
  3. Pack in flat bags or containers, 500g or 1kg per pack. Flat packs freeze and thaw faster and stack better.
  4. Remove air and seal. Squeeze the air out of the bag or use a vacuum sealer if you have one.
  5. Label with the date and fruit type. You will not remember what is in the plain bag in three months.
  6. Freeze at −18°C or below. The colder, the longer the quality holds.

What freezing does to fruit for jam

Freezing bursts the cell walls of the fruit, which means frozen fruit releases its juices faster and softens more on cooking. That is actually an advantage for jam — the fruit breaks down quickly and the jam sets evenly. Many commercial jam makers freeze fruit deliberately for this reason.

Adjust for extra liquid: frozen fruit carries a bit more water than fresh, so add slightly more pectin or cook slightly longer.

Working out how much to freeze

Do this before the season ends, not after:

  1. Estimate your monthly jar sales. Say 100 jars a month on average.
  2. Convert to fruit. 100 jars × 500g usable fruit = 50kg usable fruit a month.
  3. Convert to whole fruit. Mango needs about 40% more (skin and stone), so 70kg of whole fruit per month.
  4. Multiply by the number of months until the next harvest. If mango is available 3 months and you need to cover the other 9, you need 70kg × 9 = 630kg frozen.
  5. Add 20% buffer. Spoilage, growth in sales, unexpected orders. Final figure: ~750kg.

That is the number you buy at harvest. Yes, it is a lot. Yes, it costs money up front. Yes, it is the difference between a seasonal jam project and a jam business.

If you do not have a freezer

A chest freezer pays for itself within one season of jam-making. It is the single most important piece of equipment after your pot. If you truly cannot afford one yet, use the pulp-and-bottle method instead — bottle pasteurised fruit pulp in sterilised jars and store in a cool dark place. It works, it just takes more time on the front end.

Lesson 2.5

Costing your fruit supply honestly

Here is how to build the fruit cost figure that goes into your jar costing from Module 1.

The full calculation, step by step

  1. Purchase price of whole fruit. What you actually paid, including transport to your kitchen.
  2. Yield percentage. How much usable fruit you get after peeling, stoning, trimming. This varies by fruit:
    • Mango: 60–65% usable
    • Guava: 85–90% usable
    • Strawberry: 95% usable (just hull and wash)
    • Plum: 90% usable
    • Pineapple: 50–55% usable
  3. Divide purchase price by yield. That is your cost per kilogram of usable fruit.
  4. Multiply by the fruit weight per jar. Typically 500g of usable fruit per 250ml jar.
  5. That is your fruit cost per jar.

Example — mango jam

StepFigure
Whole mango purchase price$0.30/kg
Yield after peel and stone62%
Cost per kg usable fruit$0.30 / 0.62 = $0.48/kg
Fruit per 250ml jar500g = 0.5kg
Fruit cost per jar$0.24

Add 10% for fruit that spoils or is discarded in preparation. Final fruit cost: $0.26 per jar. That is the number that goes into your jar costing in Module 1.

The reason to do this every batch

Fruit prices change. Yields change with variety, season, and how good you are at trimming. If you assume the fruit cost instead of calculating it, your margin will slowly erode and you will not know why. Five minutes with a calculator every batch tells you the truth.

Lesson 2.6

The fruit sourcing plan

Bring the whole module together.

What a working fruit supply looks like

  1. Three farmer relationships — for your main fruit. Visit at harvest. Agree a seconds price.
  2. A chest freezer — at least 200 litres if you are doing this seriously. Bigger is better.
  3. A preserving rhythm — every harvest, buy enough to last until the next harvest. Freeze it. Label it.
  4. A cost per jar figure — calculated, not assumed. Updated every time fruit prices change.
  5. A backup source — a wholesale market for when a farmer has a bad year, or when demand spikes.

The mistake that costs the most

Buying fruit retail

Buying jam fruit from a supermarket or a retail stall is the single most expensive mistake a jam maker makes. It costs you 4–6 times what the same fruit costs from a farmer. Over a year, that difference pays for the freezer, all your jars, and your labels — and it is money you simply throw away.

If you cannot get to a farmer yet, buy from a wholesale produce market instead. Never retail.

Practical assignment — Module 2

Research assignment. You do not need to buy fruit yet.

  1. Identify your main fruit. Which fruit will your first three jam products be based on? State the fruit, the variety if known, and why you chose it (local availability, customer demand, pectin content).
  2. Find three farmers. Visit or phone three farmers within driving distance who grow that fruit. Ask about seconds, price, and how much they can supply per month. Write down names and phone numbers.
  3. Yield test. Buy 1kg of the fruit. Peel, stone, trim, and weigh what is left. That is your actual yield percentage. Compare to the table in Lesson 2.5.
  4. Cost per jar. Calculate your fruit cost per 250ml jar using your local prices and your actual yield. State the figure.
  5. Freezer plan. Do you have a freezer that can hold the fruit you would need for a year? If not, what is your alternative — pulp and bottle? State it.
  6. Fruit calendar. List the twelve months of the year and mark, for your main fruit, which months are harvest, which are usable from storage, and which are the lean months. This tells you when you need to preserve.

Points 2, 3 and 6 carry the most marks. Point 2 builds the relationships that make the business work. Point 3 turns guesswork into real numbers. Point 6 tells you when your jam kitchen is busy and when it needs frozen stock.

Module 2 knowledge check

1. What is the ideal ripeness stage for jam fruit?

  1. Under-ripe, for maximum pectin
  2. Fully ripe for eating — the sweetest stage
  3. Just turning to fully ripe, but not yet past its peak
  4. Over-ripe, because the flavour is strongest
Show answer

c. Fully ripe for eating means too much water and too little pectin for jam. Just-ripe gives the best balance of flavour, texture and pectin. A small percentage of under-ripe fruit (10–15%) can be added for extra pectin.

2. Why is buying fruit from a supermarket the most expensive mistake a jam maker can make?

  1. The fruit is worse
  2. You pay 4–6× what the same fruit costs from a farmer, which destroys your margin on every jar
  3. There is less fruit available
  4. You have to carry it further
Show answer

b. Retail fruit is priced for the home cook buying 1kg, not for a business buying 50kg. The difference over a year pays for a freezer, your jars, and your labels — money you throw away.

3. Which of these is safe to use for jam?

  1. Fruit with a slightly mouldy skin
  2. Fruit that smells of alcohol
  3. Fruit with a cosmetic blemish on the skin but sound flesh
  4. Fruit with fermented flesh
Show answer

c. Cosmetic blemishes are fine — nobody sees the fruit, only the jam. Mould produces invisible toxins that spread through the whole batch. Fermentation has already changed the fruit and will make the jam sour.

4. What is the correct way to preserve fruit for year-round jam production?

  1. Leave it out at room temperature in an open basket
  2. Freeze it, or bottle pasteurised pulp, or dry it, or preserve in syrup
  3. Boil it briefly and store in oil
  4. Do not preserve it — only make jam in season
Show answer

b. Freezing is the simplest and highest-quality method for most fruit. Pulping and bottling works if you have no freezer. All four methods allow you to buy cheap at harvest and produce jam year-round.

5. Why must you calculate fruit cost per jar using yield percentage, not just the purchase price?

  1. Because fruit shrinks when it cooks
  2. Because the usable fruit after peeling and stoning is only 50–90% of what you bought, so the real cost is higher than the shelf price suggests
  3. Because you need to account for sugar
  4. Because the farmer's price varies
Show answer

b. Mango yields around 62% usable; pineapple around 50%. If you assume the whole price, you under-cost your jar and set prices that lose money. Divide the purchase price by the yield to get the true cost per kilogram of usable fruit.
