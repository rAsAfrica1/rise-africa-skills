WELDING FUNDAMENTALS · MODULE 6 OF 12

Metal Identification and Preparation

What you are welding, what is on it, and how you clean it. The three questions that decide whether the weld succeeds before you strike an arc.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  • Identify mild steel and explain why it is the default for beginners
  • Recognise the higher-carbon and alloy steels that crack when welded without procedure
  • Identify common coatings and platings, and know which ones are dangerous to weld
  • Clean a joint correctly and know how far back the cleaning must go
  • Bevel, gap and fit up a joint so the arc can reach the root
  • Use the three-question check before starting any weld
Lesson 6.1

Why identify the metal

Module 2 explained that the heat affected zone can harden and crack in steels with higher carbon content. Module 5 explained that moisture introduces hydrogen that causes delayed cracking. Both of those are affected by what you are welding, and by what is on it.

Most welding problems on a farm or small workshop come from one of three identification failures:

  1. Welding a steel that should have been welded with a procedure — spring steel, tool steel, high-tensile components. These crack because the HAZ hardens as it cools
  2. Welding through a coating without removing it — galvanising, paint, oil. These produce fume that harms you and contamination that harms the weld
  3. Welding something that used to contain fuel or pressure — the case that kills. Module 1 covered it in full

All three are avoided by asking three questions before you start. The questions are the content of the rest of this module.

The three-question check

  1. What is this metal? Mild steel is safe. Anything else — find out what it is before welding
  2. What is on it? Any coating, plating, paint or residue must be identified and removed
  3. What has it contained? Any vessel, pipe or tank that has held fuel, solvent or an unknown substance must not be welded (module 1)

Ten seconds of asking. Every hour of welding that follows depends on the answer.

Lesson 6.2

Mild steel — the default

Mild steel — also called low-carbon steel or structural steel — contains typically less than 0.25% carbon. It is the material the whole of this course is written around, and the material you should weld for at least your first year of practice.

Why mild steel welds well

  • Its HAZ does not readily harden. Low carbon content means the metal does not form the hard, brittle structures that higher-carbon steels form on rapid cooling. The HAZ of a mild steel weld is generally softer and more ductile
  • It tolerates a range of heat input without cracking — you can be somewhat imprecise about current, travel speed and cooling rate and still produce a sound weld
  • It is widely available. Angle iron, flat bar, tube, sheet — most of what you buy from a steel merchant in Africa is mild steel, and most of what is in a workshop is mild steel
  • It is cheap. Relative to alloy steels, mild steel costs far less, which means practice is affordable

How to recognise it

There is no field test that identifies steel with certainty. What you can do:

  • Ask the supplier. New steel from a merchant is usually mild steel, and the receipt or product label will say. Keep the label
  • Ask the previous owner. Second-hand steel is the difficult case. Knowing what it was used for is often enough
  • Test a spark on a grinder. Mild steel sparks are typically short, bushy, yellow-white, with few forks. Higher-carbon steel produces brighter, more explosive sparks with many branching forks. This is a rough guide, not a test — but with practice it distinguishes obvious cases
  • Break a test piece. Mild steel bends before it breaks, and shows a torn, fibrous fracture. Higher-carbon steel snaps with a cleaner break

What to do when you cannot identify the steel

Assume the worst, unless the consequences of being wrong are trivial.

For low-stakes work — a bench bracket, a shelf support — you can weld unknown scrap and accept that it might fail. For anything that carries a load above people, holds a vehicle, or matters structurally, if you cannot say what it is, do not weld it. Find a piece you can identify, or have it identified.

Lesson 6.3

The steels that bite

Higher-carbon and alloy steels can be welded — but they require procedures this course does not teach, and welding them without those procedures produces joints that crack.

The categories to be suspicious of

CategoryWhere you meet itWhy it cracks
Medium and high-carbon steelSome tools, springs, cutting edges, higher-strength componentsCarbon content makes the HAZ harden on cooling, then brittle
Spring steelVehicle leaf springs, coil springs, some tool handlesSpecifically alloyed and heat-treated for resilience; welding destroys both
Tool steelDrill bits, chisels, punches, cutting toolsVery high carbon and alloying content — cracks almost always
High-tensile / high-strength bolts and studsSome vehicle and machinery fasteners marked 8.8, 10.9 or 12.9Higher carbon and alloying; welding destroys the strength class
Vehicle suspension and steering componentsTrack rods, tie rods, steering armsOften alloy steel heat-treated for toughness. A repaired suspension component is a lethal hazard
Implement and machinery partsPlough tines, cultivator points, harvester partsOften hardened or alloy steel. Depends on the specific part
Cast ironEngine blocks, older housings, some machineryNot steel at all — different process required. Ordinary welding cracks it

The list of things you must not weld

This course will not teach you to weld any of the following. Do not attempt them on the strength of this course:

  • Vehicle suspension or steering components
  • Lifting points, slings, hooks, chains
  • Trailer drawbars, couplings or hitch components
  • Any part of a pressure vessel, cylinder or gas line
  • Any container that has held fuel, oil, solvent or an unknown substance
  • Tool steel, spring steel, or components marked as high-tensile
  • Anything whose failure would drop a load on someone

Some of those can be welded, with the right procedure, by a qualified welder. They must not be welded on the strength of a written course and unsupervised practice.

What professional welding of those steels requires

For information, so you know what the boundary is:

  • A welding procedure specification (WPS) written for that exact material and joint
  • Preheating the work to a specified temperature before welding, so the cooling rate is slower
  • Controlled heat input within set limits
  • Low-hydrogen consumables, correctly stored
  • Interpass temperature control between passes
  • Sometimes post-weld heat treatment to relieve stress and soften the HAZ
  • Testing — often non-destructive, sometimes destructive on a procedure qualification piece

That is a professional job, done by a trained welder against a documented procedure. It is not something to improvise.

Lesson 6.4

Coatings and platings

Every coating on metal does two bad things when welded through: it produces fume that damages you, and it contaminates the weld. Some coatings produce fume that kills.

The list, with what each does

CoatingWhere you meet itWhat happens when you weld
Mill scale (blue-black oxide on new steel)New hot-rolled steelProduces inconsistent arc and porosity. Not dangerous but should be removed at the joint
RustOld steelHolds moisture → hydrogen. Causes porosity. Must be removed at the joint
Paint / primerAny painted steelBurns into fume. Old paint may contain lead. Some primers contain zinc, producing metal fume fever
Oil, grease, cutting fluidMachined or stored partsBurns into fume; introduces carbon contamination
Galvanising (zinc)Fencing, gates, roofing, poles, water tanksProduces zinc oxide fume — metal fume fever (module 1). Must be ground off before welding
Cadmium platingSome bright-plated older fasteners, certain toolsCadmium fume is acutely lethal. Do not weld cadmium-plated metal
Chrome platingDecorative and hard-chrome surfacesProduces hexavalent chromium — a carcinogen
Chlorinated solvent residueBrake cleaners, degreasers, some cleaning productsUV from the arc converts them to phosgene — a gas that severely damages lungs with a delay of hours. Do not use chlorinated solvents anywhere near welding
Lead paintOlder painted structuresLead fume. Serious long-term poisoning risk

How to remove coatings

  • Grind back to bright metal on both sides of the joint, over a wider area than the weld itself. For galvanising, remove all of it in the weld zone plus at least 25mm each side
  • Wire brush after grinding to remove loose particles
  • Degrease before grinding where oil is present, so the grinder does not smear oil into the surface
  • Clean MIG wire and filler too, where they might be oily
  • If the coating is cadmium or chrome, or you do not know what it is — do not weld it. That is the whole answer

Grinding galvanising is itself a hazard

Grinding galvanised steel produces zinc dust and particles. Wear a dust mask, work in ventilated space, and do not breathe the dust. The same is true of grinding lead paint — mask, ventilation, and no eating or smoking until you have washed your hands.

Lesson 6.5

Preparation — cleaning, bevels, fit-up

Preparation is the part of welding that looks like nothing and decides everything. A weld made on a properly prepared joint with mediocre technique usually works. A weld made on a poorly prepared joint with excellent technique often fails.

Cleaning — how far back

The rule: clean at least 25mm back from the joint on both sides, and further for thick or highly restrained work. Clean means bright metal — no rust, no scale, no paint, no oil, no moisture.

Why so wide? Because heat conducts. The pool is at the joint, but the metal just outside the pool is hot enough that contamination there can vaporise and enter the arc's shielding.

Bevels

For plate thicker than about 5–6mm, a square edge cannot be fused all the way through by a single arc. The arc has to reach the root, and for thick material the arc needs a bevel to reach into.

Thin plate is welded square. Thick plate is bevelled so the arc can penetrate to the bottom. Standard single-V bevels use a total included angle somewhere in the range of 60–75° for a stick weld, sometimes less for MIG. The root face — the small vertical land at the bottom of the bevel — is typically a few millimetres.

Fit-up — the three dimensions

DimensionWhat it isHow much
Root gapThe gap between the two edges at the bottom of the jointUsually 2–3mm. Too small and the arc cannot get in. Too large and you burn through or fill it with excess filler
Root faceThe flat land at the bottom of a bevelTypically 1.5–3mm. Too little and you burn through. Too much and penetration fails
AlignmentHow well the two pieces line upOffset causes stress concentration. Misalignment is a defect. Get it right before tacking

Tacking

Tacks are short welds that hold the fit-up until you weld the joint. Get them right:

  • Use the same consumable and process as the main weld
  • Place tacks on the side that will be welded, at intervals along the joint. Typical spacing is 4–6 times the material thickness
  • Make them big enough to hold, small enough that they can be fully consumed by the main weld. A tack that remains partially un-melted into the weld is a stress point
  • On thick material, preheat the tack area. A tack on cold thick steel cools fast and can crack

A cracked tack is not a tack

If a tack cracks — which you can see immediately, or hear as a small crack as it cools — do not leave it. Grind it out and re-tack, with preheat if thick material. A cracked tack will propagate into the main weld and cause a failure.

Practical assignment — Module 6

This is an identification and preparation assignment. No welding is required.

  1. Photograph five different pieces of metal from your workshop, farm or yard. For each, write down: what you believe it is (mild steel, cast iron, unknown, etc.), how you arrived at that belief, and whether you would weld it
  2. Find one piece you cannot identify. Photograph it, describe it, and state what you would do if you had to join it — weld, do not weld, or find out first
  3. Find one coated piece — galvanised, painted, chrome-plated, or unknown. Describe the coating as best you can, and state what process you would use to remove it if you were going to weld it. If the coating is chrome or cadmium, do not weld it — say so
  4. Draw a single-V bevel on paper, labelled with the included angle you intend to use, the root gap, and the root face. State your source for those values — supplier, textbook, or this course
  5. Practical preparation: take one scrap piece of steel, clean it to bright metal at the joint area, and photograph it before and after. State how far back you cleaned
  6. Write out the three-question check in your own words, and explain in one sentence why each question matters

Points 1, 2 and 6 carry the most marks. Points 1 and 2 test whether you can honestly assess what you have. Point 6 tests whether you understand the reasoning, not just the checklist.

Module 6 knowledge check

1. Why is mild steel the sensible default for beginners?

  1. It is the strongest steel
  2. It contains typically less than 0.25% carbon, so its heat affected zone does not readily harden, and it tolerates a range of heat input without cracking
  3. It does not need cleaning
  4. It is available in every country
Show answer

b. Low carbon means the HAZ stays relatively soft and ductile, and the weld tolerates imprecision in current, travel speed and cooling rate. This is exactly what a beginner needs — a material that forgives the mistakes you will make while learning.

2. What should you do with a piece of unknown steel that will carry a structural load?

  1. Weld it with a rutile electrode — rutile is forgiving
  2. Find out what it is, or use a piece you can identify instead. Do not weld unknown steel for structural work
  3. Weld it with a small test weld first
  4. Use higher current to be safe
Show answer

b. Unknown steel may be a grade that cracks readily when welded without procedure. Higher current makes that worse, not better. For anything carrying a real load, identification is not optional.

3. Why is cadmium-plated steel specifically dangerous to weld?

  1. It produces a strong smell
  2. Cadmium fume is acutely lethal — it causes severe lung damage and has killed welders
  3. The weld will be weak
  4. It is magnetic and interferes with the arc
Show answer

b. Cadmium fume poisoning can initially resemble metal fume fever, which is exactly what makes it dangerous — people assume they will recover by morning. It has killed welders. Do not weld cadmium-plated metal, and do not grind it without respiratory protection.

4. Why must the joint be cleaned back at least 25mm from the weld on both sides?

  1. To make the arc easier to strike
  2. Because heat conducts — the metal outside the pool gets hot enough that contamination there can vaporise and enter the arc's shielding
  3. So the weld looks neater
  4. To reduce spatter
Show answer

b. The visible pool is at the joint, but the surrounding metal gets very hot during welding. Any contamination there — oil, paint, rust — can vaporise into the shielded zone and contaminate the weld. Clean wider than the weld itself.

5. What are the three dimensions that define a fit-up on a bevelled joint?

  1. Length, width, height
  2. Root gap, root face, alignment
  3. Bevel angle, plate thickness, electrode size
  4. Current, voltage, travel speed
Show answer

b. The root gap is the space between the edges, the root face is the flat land at the bottom of the bevel, and alignment is how well the pieces line up. Get all three right before tacking.
