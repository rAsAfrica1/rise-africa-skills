1. FCAW Equipment and Setup

FCAW needs a wire feeder, a welding gun, a power source, and a gas regulator if you're using gas-shielded wire. For self-shielded wire, you skip the gas. In most African workshops, you'll find 240V single-phase or 380V three-phase power. A 250-amp FCAW machine like a Lincoln Power MIG 256 or a Miller Millermatic 252 works well on 240V. If you only have a 200-amp machine, use 0.9mm (0.035") wire and keep your welds under 6mm thick. The wire feeder must match your wire size: 0.9mm, 1.2mm, or 1.6mm. For 1.2mm wire, you need a feeder with knurled drive rollsnot smooth V-groove rolls, which slip on flux-cored wire. Set drive roll tension so the wire doesn't crush. Too tight and the wire deforms; too loose and it slips. A good test: hold the gun 10cm from a piece of wood, pull the trigger, and the wire should push against the wood without slipping or birds-nesting. The gun liner must be the correct size for your wire. For 1.2mm wire, use a 1.21.4mm liner. A worn liner causes wire feed problems. The contact tip should be 0.1mm larger than the wireso for 1.2mm wire, use a 1.3mm tip. If the tip is too small, the wire will stick; too large, and you get poor electrical contact and erratic arcs. For gas-shielded FCAW, you need a regulator and flowmeter. Set CO2 flow to 1520 CFH (cubic feet per hour) or 710 L/min. Too little flow and you get porosity; too much and you get turbulence that pulls in air. Use a 75% Argon / 25% CO2 mix for flat and horizontal welds; straight CO2 for deeper penetration on thick steel. Check for leaks with soapy water. For self-shielded wire, no gas is needed, but you must use a gun with a nozzle that directs the shielding gas that comes from the flux. Keep the nozzle cleanspatter buildup restricts flow. Polarity matters: most FCAW wires run electrode positive (DCEP). Check the wire package. If you run the wrong polarity, the arc will be unstable and the weld will be porous. Finally, always have spare contact tips, nozzles, and liners. In many African markets, these are hard to find quickly, so stock up.

2. Flux Cored Wire Types and Selection

Flux-cored wire comes in two main types: gas-shielded and self-shielded. Gas-shielded wire, like E70T-1, requires an external shielding gas (usually CO2 or a mix). It gives smooth welds and good penetration. Self-shielded wire, like E71T-11, has all the shielding inside the flux coreno gas needed. That makes it perfect for outdoor work where wind would blow away shielding gas. In Africa, where many jobs are done in open-air workshops or on-site, self-shielded wire is often the practical choice. For general fabrication on mild steel, E70T-1 (gas-shielded) is common. It runs on DCEP, has a tensile strength of 70,000 psi, and works well on 6mm to 12mm plate. For thin sheet metal (1.5mm to 3mm), use E71T-GS (self-shielded, gasless) with 0.9mm wire. It runs on DCEN (electrode negative) and gives less penetration, which is good for thin metal to avoid burn-through. For rusty or dirty steel, self-shielded wire with a rutile flux (like E71T-11) handles contamination better than gas-shielded wire. But it produces more smoke and slag. For high-deposition welding on thick steel (12mm+), use E70T-5 or E70T-6 (gas-shielded, basic flux). These wires need CO2 gas and produce a thick slag that must be chipped off. They give excellent mechanical properties but are less forgiving on dirty metal. When selecting wire, check the package for the classification: E indicates electrode, 70 or 71 is tensile strength in ksi, T means tubular (flux-cored), and the number after the dash tells the polarity and usability. For example, E71T-1 is gas-shielded, DCEP, all-position. E71T-11 is self-shielded, DCEN, all-position. E70T-4 is self-shielded, DCEN, flat/horizontal only. Always match wire to your base metal thickness and position. For vertical or overhead welding, use an all-position wire like E71T-1 or E71T-11. For flat only, you can use a high-deposition wire like E70T-4. Store wire in a dry placemoisture in the flux causes porosity. If wire gets damp, bake it at 120C for 2 hours before use. In Africa's humid climates, keep wire in a sealed container with silica gel packets.

3. Gas-Shielded vs. Self-Shielded FCAW

The biggest choice in FCAW is whether to use gas shielding or not. Gas-shielded FCAW uses a separate gas cylinder to protect the weld pool. Self-shielded FCAW uses the flux inside the wire to create its own shielding gas. Each has clear advantages and disadvantages that matter in African workshops. Gas-shielded FCAW gives you a cleaner weld, less smoke, and better visibility of the arc. The slag is thinner and easier to remove. It works great indoors or in still air. But you need a gas cylinder, regulator, flowmeter, and hoses. That's extra cost and equipment to maintain. In rural areas, refilling CO2 cylinders can be a 100km trip. If the wind is blowing more than 5 km/h, the gas shield blows away and you get porosity. You can build a windbreak, but that slows you down. Self-shielded FCAW needs no gas. You can weld outside in wind, rain, or on a ladder. The equipment is simpler: just the machine, wire feeder, and gun. That makes it cheaper and more portable. But self-shielded wire produces more smokesometimes so much you need a respirator. The slag is thicker and harder to chip. The weld appearance is rougher. Penetration can be less consistent, especially on thick steel. For most beginners in Africa, start with self-shielded wire (E71T-11, 0.9mm or 1.2mm). It's forgiving, works anywhere, and doesn't need gas. Once you master that, switch to gas-shielded for cleaner work. For gas-shielded, use 75% Argon / 25% CO2 for flat and horizontal, or straight CO2 for deep penetration. Set flow to 1520 CFH. For self-shielded, no gas flow to set, but keep your stickout (wire extension) at 1015mm. Too short and the flux can't shield; too long and you lose penetration. In windy conditions, self-shielded wins every time. In a shop with a steady power supply and easy gas access, gas-shielded gives better-looking welds. Choose based on your location, budget, and the job.

4. Basic FCAW Beads and Joints

Start with a flat bead on a piece of 6mm mild steel plate. Clean the steel with a grinder to bare metalno rust, paint, or oil. Set your machine: for 1.2mm self-shielded wire (E71T-11), use 180200 amps, 2224 volts, and wire feed speed around 67 m/min. For gas-shielded (E70T-1), use 200220 amps, 2426 volts, and 78 m/min. Set your stickout (distance from contact tip to workpiece) at 1015mm. Hold the gun at a 1015 drag angle (tilted back, pulling the puddle). Push angle (tilted forward) works for gas-shielded but not for self-shieldedit causes porosity. For a straight bead, pull the trigger, move steadily across the plate. Listen for a steady crackling soundlike frying bacon. If it pops or sputters, adjust wire speed or voltage. Keep your travel speed steady. Too fast gives a thin, convex bead with poor fusion. Too slow gives a wide, flat bead that may burn through. Aim for a bead width of 68mm and a height of 23mm. For a T-joint, tack the pieces first. Weld from one end to the other, holding a 45 work angle and a 1015 drag angle. Use a slight side-to-side motion (about 3mm) to tie both plates together. For a butt joint on 6mm plate, leave a 2mm gap. Weld one side, then grind the back and weld the other side for full penetration. For vertical-up welding, reduce amps by 1015% and use a triangle motion: pause at the edges, move quickly across the middle. For overhead, use lower amps (160180) and keep the puddle small. Always chip slag after each pass with a chipping hammer, then brush with a wire brush. Inspect for porosity, undercut, or lack of fusion. If you see defects, grind them out and reweld. Practice on scrap steel until your beads are consistent. In Africa, scrap steel is cheap and availableuse it to build muscle memory.

5. Parameter Adjustment for FCAW

Getting the right parameters is the difference between a strong weld and a failed one. The three main settings are voltage, wire feed speed (WFS), and travel speed. Voltage controls arc length and bead width. Wire feed speed controls amperage and penetration. Travel speed controls bead size and fusion. For 1.2mm self-shielded wire (E71T-11), start with 200 amps, 23 volts, and WFS at 6.5 m/min. For gas-shielded (E70T-1), start with 220 amps, 25 volts, and WFS at 7.5 m/min. If the arc is too short (voltage too low), you'll get a stubbing sound and the wire may stick to the plate. Increase voltage by 1 volt at a time. If the arc is too long (voltage too high), you'll get a hissing sound, excessive spatter, and a flat, wide bead. Decrease voltage. Wire feed speed: if the wire burns back to the tip, increase WFS. If the wire stubs into the plate, decrease WFS. A good rule: set WFS first to get the amperage you want, then adjust voltage to fine-tune the arc. For thin metal (3mm), use 0.9mm wire, 150170 amps, 2022 volts, and WFS at 56 m/min. For thick metal (12mm), use 1.6mm wire, 250280 amps, 2628 volts, and WFS at 89 m/min. Stickout (wire extension) also matters. For self-shielded, keep it at 1015mm. For gas-shielded, 1520mm. Too long and you lose shielding; too short and you clog the tip. Travel speed: for a 6mm fillet weld, move at about 2025 cm/min. If you move too fast, the weld is narrow and may lack fusion. Too slow, and you get a wide bead with possible burn-through. Use your ears and eyes: a steady crackle means good parameters. A popping or sputtering sound means adjust voltage or WFS. In Africa, power supply can be unstable. If your machine has a voltage fluctuation problem, use a stabilizer or a generator with AVR (automatic voltage regulation). Always test on scrap before welding the actual job.

6. FCAW Defects and Remedies

Porosity is the most common FCAW defectsmall holes in the weld. Causes: gas flow too low or too high (for gas-shielded), wind blowing away shielding gas, dirty base metal, damp wire, or too long stickout. Remedies: set CO2 flow to 1520 CFH, build a windbreak, grind base metal to bare metal, store wire in a dry place, and keep stickout at 1015mm for self-shielded or 1520mm for gas-shielded. If you see porosity, grind it out and reweld. Slag inclusion happens when slag gets trapped in the weld. Causes: insufficient cleaning between passes, wrong travel angle (pushing instead of dragging for self-shielded), or too low amperage. Remedies: chip and brush every pass, use a 1015 drag angle, and increase amps by 1015. Lack of fusion is when the weld metal doesn't fuse to the base metal. Causes: travel speed too fast, amperage too low, or wrong gun angle. Remedies: slow down, increase WFS to raise amperage, and keep a 45 work angle for fillet welds. Undercut is a groove melted into the base metal at the weld toe. Causes: voltage too high, travel speed too fast, or gun angle too steep. Remedies: reduce voltage by 12 volts, slow down, and use a flatter gun angle. Burn-through is a hole melted through thin metal. Causes: amperage too high, travel speed too slow, or no gap for expansion. Remedies: reduce WFS, increase travel speed, and use a copper backing bar if possible. Spatter is excessive molten metal droplets around the weld. Causes: voltage too high, wire stickout too short, or wrong polarity. Remedies: reduce voltage, increase stickout, and check polarity (DCEP for most gas-shielded, DCEN for self-shielded). Wire feed problems: bird-nesting (wire tangles at the feeder) means drive roll tension too loose or liner worn. Remedies: tighten tension slightly, replace liner. In Africa, where replacement parts can be scarce, keep spares. Always clean your gun nozzle and tip every 30 minutes of welding. A clean gun prevents many defects.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: FCAW in African Fabrication Workshops

Flux Cored Arc Welding (FCAW) is the workhorse process behind much of Africa's structural steel, mining equipment repair, trailer manufacturing, and agro-processing machinery fabrication. Unlike stick (SMAW), FCAW delivers higher deposition rates, continuous wire feed, and better penetration on thick plate critical when you are welding 1025 mm steel for a maize mill frame in Nakuru or a tipper truck body in Kumasi. This reference lesson consolidates the practical parameters, consumable choices, and troubleshooting knowledge you need to run FCAW profitably, with a business-first lens: every parameter decision affects your consumable cost, labour hours, rework rate, and ultimately your margin per job.

Why FCAW Matters for African Fabricators

Across Sub-Saharan Africa, structural steel demand is growing with infrastructure, mining, and agricultural mechanisation. FCAW offers several commercial advantages over SMAW: deposition rates of 24 kg/hour versus 12 kg/hour for stick, fewer restarts, and the ability to weld out-of-position with gas-shielded variants. For a workshop in Lagos or Lusaka running 8-hour shifts, that translates directly into more completed jobs per week. Self-shielded FCAW is particularly valuable in remote sites mine shafts in the DRC Copperbelt, borehole rigs in northern Kenya, or rural irrigation projects where wind and lack of shielding gas supply make MIG/MAG impractical.

SAFETY WARNING FCAW FUME & RADIATION

Flux-cored wires generate significant fume containing manganese, chromium (in stainless), and fluoride compounds. In enclosed African workshop conditions common where natural ventilation is limited this can exceed occupational exposure limits within minutes. Always use local exhaust ventilation or fume extraction at the arc, wear a P2/P3 respirator under the helmet for stainless and confined-space work, and never weld galvanised or painted steel without stripping and extraction. Arc radiation from FCAW is intense: use a minimum shade 1013 lens (shade 12+ for high-current). Ensure the work return clamp is on clean metal within 1 m of the weld to prevent stray current paths through bearings, cables, or structural bolts a common cause of fires and equipment damage in African workshops.

Defect Quick-Diagnosis

Most FCAW defects in African shops trace to three root causes: contaminated base metal (mill scale, rust, oil), incorrect parameter balance (voltage vs WFS), and inadequate gas coverage. Porosity is the number-one complaint usually from wind, moisture in the flux, or a clogged nozzle. Lack of fusion on thick plate often means travel speed too fast or voltage too low. Burn-through on thin sheet means WFS too high or voltage too high. Before blaming the wire, check polarity: most gas-shielded FCAW wires run DCEP (electrode positive); self-shielded wires often run DCEN. Reversed polarity will produce a spattery, cold, poorly penetrating weld that no parameter tweak will fix.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Verify polarity DCEP for most gas-shielded E70T-1/E71T-1; DCEN for many self-shielded E71T-11 wires. Check the wire data sheet, not memory.
  2. Set gas flow before striking arc 1525 L/min CO or Ar-CO mix; check for leaks at regulator and hose with soapy water weekly.
  3. Clean the joint remove mill scale, rust, oil, and paint at least 25 mm either side. Recycled African steel often carries heavy contamination.
  4. Match wire to job E70T-1 for general structural; E71T-11 for field/self-shielded; E308LT1-1 for 304 stainless agro-equipment.
  5. Set WFS first, then trim voltage aim for a crisp, crackling arc, not a sputtering or popping sound.
  6. Check CTWD (contact tip to work distance) keep 1525 mm; too long causes voltage drop and porosity, too short risks burnback.
  7. Inspect nozzle and liner daily spatter build-up restricts gas flow; replace liners when wire feed stutters.
  8. Record parameters per job build a shop logbook so repeat jobs (trailer batches, gate frames) run at proven settings and reduce rework.
