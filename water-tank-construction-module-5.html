1. Lesson 1: Choosing the Right Plastic Tank for Your Market and Budget

In the African water storage market, plastic and poly tanks have become the fastest-growing segment because they solve three problems that concrete and ferrocement tanks struggle with: speed of installation, portability, and predictable quality control. A 1,000-litre rotational-moulded polyethylene tank in Nairobi retails between KES 9,500 and KES 14,000 depending on wall thickness and UV stabiliser content. The same tank in Lagos costs NGN 85,000 to NGN 130,000, while in Accra you will pay GHS 1,200 to GHS 1,800, and in Johannesburg a 1,000-litre JoJo or Rototank goes for ZAR 2,200 to ZAR 3,200. Your first business decision is not which tank to buy but which customer segment you are serving. The three main segments are: household rooftop storage (250L to 2,500L), institutional and commercial storage (5,000L to 20,000L), and bulk agricultural or construction storage (10,000L to 50,000L). Each segment has different margin profiles and installation requirements. Household tanks are high-volume, low-margin, and sold mostly through hardware shops. Institutional tanks are lower-volume but higher-margin because installation, plumbing, and base preparation are bundled into the contract. Bulk agricultural tanks require crane or forklift access, concrete ring beams, and often multiple tank manifolds. Before you quote any job, confirm four things: the daily water demand in litres, the available head pressure (height difference between tank outlet and point of use), the ground conditions at the proposed site, and the customer's budget ceiling. A common mistake among new installers is to sell a tank based on price alone, then discover the base is waterlogged, the roof cannot carry the weight, or the inlet pressure from the borehole will burst the tank. Poly tanks are rated for specific hydrostatic pressures, and a 2,000-litre tank filled to the brim weighs over 2,000 kilograms. That weight must be transferred to the ground through a properly sized base. In African cities where plot sizes are tight, vertical tanks are preferred over horizontal tanks because they occupy less footprint. However, horizontal tanks are easier to install under low roofs and are common in rural schools and clinics. When sourcing tanks, inspect the following: the manufacturer's stamp showing the volume and resin type, the presence of UV stabilisers (essential in East and Southern Africa where solar radiation is intense), the wall thickness at the base and the side walls, and the quality of the threaded fittings. Cheap tanks often use thin walls that bulge when full and crack at the fitting points within two years. A good rotational-moulded tank should have a minimum wall thickness of 4mm at the base and 3mm on the sides for tanks up to 5,000 litres. For larger tanks, demand 6mm to 8mm at the base. Always ask for the resin type: linear low-density polyethylene (LLDPE) with UV stabilisers is the standard for potable water. Some manufacturers use recycled plastic, which is cheaper but has lower tensile strength and may leach odours into the water. For potable water, insist on food-grade LLDPE. Pricing strategy: if you are installing a 5,000-litre tank for a school in Kisumu, your costs might be KES 42,000 for the tank, KES 8,000 for the concrete base materials, KES 3,500 for plumbing fittings, and KES 4,000 for labour and transport. That is KES 57,500 in direct costs. A 25 to 35 percent markup gives a quote of KES 72,000 to KES 78,000. In Lagos, the same job with a 5,000-litre tank at NGN 380,000, base materials at NGN 65,000, fittings at NGN 25,000, and labour at NGN 40,000 gives direct costs of NGN 510,000, and a quote of NGN 640,000 to NGN 690,000. Always separate your quote into tank supply, base preparation, plumbing, and installation labour. This transparency builds trust and allows the customer to see where the money goes. Safety note: never install a poly tank on a bare earth surface. The weight will cause the tank to sink unevenly, stressing the walls and causing cracks. Always use a reinforced concrete base, compacted murram, or a proprietary plastic base ring. Also, never use a tank that has previously stored chemicals for potable water, even if it has been washed. Polyethylene absorbs some chemicals and can release them slowly into drinking water.

2. Lesson 2: Site Assessment and Base Preparation for Poly Tanks

The single most common cause of poly tank failure in Africa is poor base preparation. A tank that is not fully supported across its entire base will develop stress cracks, usually within 12 to 24 months. This lesson covers the site assessment and base construction process in detail, with costs for Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana, and South Africa. Start with a site visit. You need to check: ground slope, soil type, water table depth, distance to the water source, distance to the point of use, access for delivery vehicles, and overhead obstructions such as trees or power lines. For a 5,000-litre tank, the delivery truck needs a clear path at least 3 metres wide and 4 metres high. If the tank must be carried by hand, calculate the labour cost: a 5,000-litre tank weighs about 180kg empty and requires four to six people to move safely. In many African markets, the tank is delivered on a flatbed truck and offloaded with a crane or a team of labourers using ropes. Confirm who pays for offloading. In Nairobi, offloading a 5,000-litre tank costs KES 2,000 to KES 3,500. In Lagos, it is NGN 15,000 to NGN 25,000. In Accra, GHS 200 to GHS 350. In Johannesburg, ZAR 400 to ZAR 700. Soil type matters. Black cotton soil, common in parts of Kenya, Uganda, and Tanzania, expands when wet and contracts when dry. A tank base on black cotton soil will move and crack unless you excavate at least 600mm deep and replace the soil with compacted murram or hardcore. Sandy soil drains well but can erode. Clay soil holds water and can become waterlogged, causing the tank to float if the water table rises. In waterlogged areas, you must build a raised base at least 300mm above ground level and provide drainage channels around the base. The standard base for a poly tank up to 10,000 litres is a reinforced concrete slab. The slab should extend at least 300mm beyond the tank's diameter on all sides. For a 2,000-litre tank with a 1.4m diameter, the slab should be at least 2.0m x 2.0m. For a 5,000-litre tank with a 1.9m diameter, the slab should be at least 2.5m x 2.5m. For a 10,000-litre tank with a 2.4m diameter, the slab should be at least 3.0m x 3.0m. The slab thickness depends on the tank size: 100mm for tanks up to 2,000 litres, 150mm for tanks up to 5,000 litres, and 200mm for tanks up to 10,000 litres. Reinforcement should be a welded mesh or rebar grid. For a 5,000-litre tank, use 8mm rebar at 200mm centres in both directions, or a 4mm welded mesh with 100mm squares. Concrete mix: 1 part cement, 2 parts sand, 4 parts aggregate (20mm). In Kenya, a 50kg bag of cement costs KES 700 to KES 850. In Nigeria, NGN 8,000 to NGN 10,000. In Ghana, GHS 60 to GHS 80. In South Africa, ZAR 120 to ZAR 160. For a 2.5m x 2.5m x 150mm slab, you need about 0.94 cubic metres of concrete. That is approximately 6 bags of cement, 0.5 cubic metres of sand, and 0.9 cubic metres of aggregate. Total material cost in Kenya: KES 4,200 for cement, KES 1,500 for sand, KES 2,500 for aggregate, KES 1,800 for rebar, total KES 10,000. Labour for mixing and pouring: KES 4,000 to KES 6,000. Total base cost: KES 14,000 to KES 16,000. In Nigeria, the same base costs NGN 120,000 to NGN 150,000. In Ghana, GHS 1,200 to GHS 1,500. In South Africa, ZAR 2,500 to ZAR 3,200. If the customer cannot afford a concrete base, the next best option is a compacted murram or laterite base with a plastic liner. Excavate 200mm, compact the murram in 50mm layers with a hand rammer or plate compactor, and cover with a 500-micron plastic liner. This is cheaper but less durable. It is acceptable for temporary installations or for tanks up to 1,000 litres. Never use sand alone as a base. Sand shifts and erodes. Never use wooden planks or pallets. They rot and collapse. Never place a tank directly on a roof without a structural assessment. A 1,000-litre tank on a roof adds 1,000kg of live load, which many African residential roofs are not designed to carry. If the tank must go on a roof, use a horizontal tank and spread the load across at least four roof trusses. Safety note: when pouring a concrete base, wear gloves, boots, and eye protection. Cement burns are common and painful. Mix concrete on a clean surface, not on bare ground, to avoid contamination. Cure the slab for at least 7 days before placing the tank. In hot climates, cover the slab with wet sacks or plastic sheeting and water it twice daily to prevent rapid drying and cracking.

3. Lesson 3: Plumbing, Fittings, and Tank Connections

Plumbing is where most poly tank installations fail. A tank that is perfectly installed on a good base will still leak or underperform if the inlet, outlet, overflow, and washout connections are poorly designed. This lesson covers the correct plumbing configuration for gravity-fed and pressure-fed systems, with material costs for African markets. Every poly tank installation needs at least four connections: inlet, outlet, overflow, and washout. The inlet brings water into the tank. The outlet takes water to the point of use. The overflow prevents the tank from bursting when it is full. The washout allows you to drain and clean the tank. For gravity-fed systems, the outlet should be at least 50mm above the tank floor to allow sediment to settle below it. For pressure systems, the outlet is usually at the bottom and feeds a pump. Inlet configuration: if the tank is fed by a borehole pump, the inlet pipe should enter the tank at the top and discharge through a downward-facing elbow to reduce turbulence and aeration. If the tank is fed by gravity from a roof catchment, the inlet should have a first-flush diverter and a leaf screen. A first-flush diverter discards the first 20 to 50 litres of rainwater, which contains most of the dirt from the roof. In Kenya, a first-flush diverter costs KES 3,500 to KES 6,000. In Nigeria, NGN 25,000 to NGN 45,000. In Ghana, GHS 250 to GHS 450. In South Africa, ZAR 500 to ZAR 900. Overflow: the overflow pipe should be at least the same diameter as the inlet pipe, and preferably one size larger. For a 50mm inlet, use a 50mm or 63mm overflow. The overflow must discharge to a safe location, not onto the tank base or foundation. If the overflow discharges onto the base, it will erode the soil and undermine the slab. Extend the overflow at least 1 metre away from the base and direct it to a drain or garden. Washout: the washout should be at the lowest point of the tank floor. Most poly tanks have a threaded fitting at the bottom for this purpose. Use a 50mm gate valve or ball valve. In Kenya, a 50mm PVC ball valve costs KES 1,200 to KES 1,800. In Nigeria, NGN 8,000 to NGN 12,000. In Ghana, GHS 80 to GHS 120. In South Africa, ZAR 150 to ZAR 250. Pipe materials: for potable water, use uPVC or HDPE pipes. uPVC is cheaper and easier to work with, but it becomes brittle in strong sunlight. Paint uPVC pipes with light-coloured exterior paint or wrap them with UV-resistant tape. HDPE is more flexible and UV-resistant but requires specialised fusion equipment for large diameters. For diameters up to 63mm, compression fittings are acceptable. For larger diameters, use solvent-weld uPVC or fusion-welded HDPE. Fittings: use brass or plastic threaded fittings, not galvanised iron. Galvanised iron reacts with the plastic tank threads and can seize or leak. Always use PTFE tape or thread sealant on all threaded connections. Do not over-tighten plastic fittings; hand-tight plus a quarter turn with a wrench is enough. Over-tightening cracks the tank wall or the fitting. Bulkhead fittings: for tanks that do not have moulded threaded ports, you need bulkhead fittings. A bulkhead fitting is a two-piece threaded fitting that passes through the tank wall and seals with a rubber gasket on both sides. In Kenya, a 50mm bulkhead fitting costs KES 2,500 to KES 4,000. In Nigeria, NGN 18,000 to NGN 28,000. In Ghana, GHS 180 to GHS 280. In South Africa, ZAR 350 to ZAR 550. When installing a bulkhead fitting, drill the hole with a hole saw, clean the edges, apply silicone sealant to both gaskets, and tighten evenly. Do not use a hammer to force the fitting through the hole. Pump selection: if the tank is below the point of use, you need a pump. For a 5,000-litre tank serving a two-storey building, a 0.5HP to 1HP centrifugal pump is usually sufficient. In Kenya, a 0.5HP pump costs KES 12,000 to KES 18,000. In Nigeria, NGN 85,000 to NGN 130,000. In Ghana, GHS 900 to GHS 1,400. In South Africa, ZAR 1,800 to ZAR 2,800. Add a pressure tank and pressure switch for automatic operation. A 24-litre pressure tank costs KES 8,000 to KES 12,000 in Kenya. Water treatment: if the tank stores water for drinking, add a chlorine doser or a UV filter. A simple floating chlorine dispenser costs KES 1,500 to KES 2,500 in Kenya. In Nigeria, NGN 12,000 to NGN 20,000. In Ghana, GHS 120 to GHS 200. In South Africa, ZAR 250 to ZAR 400. Safety note: always install a vacuum breaker or air admittance valve on the outlet line to prevent the tank from collapsing when the outlet is opened and the inlet is closed. Poly tanks are not designed to withstand negative pressure. Also, never connect a poly tank directly to a municipal water supply without a backflow preventer. If the municipal pressure drops, tank water can be siphoned back into the mains, contaminating the public supply. A backflow preventer costs KES 3,000 to KES 5,000 in Kenya, NGN 22,000 to NGN 35,000 in Nigeria, GHS 250 to GHS 400 in Ghana, and ZAR 450 to ZAR 750 in South Africa.

4. Lesson 4: Installing Large Poly Tanks and Tank Farms

When you move beyond single household tanks into institutional, commercial, and agricultural installations, the complexity and the profit margins both increase. A 10,000-litre poly tank installation in Kenya can be quoted at KES 180,000 to KES 250,000, with direct costs of KES 120,000 to KES 160,000. In Nigeria, a 10,000-litre installation quotes at NGN 1,200,000 to NGN 1,600,000, with direct costs of NGN 800,000 to NGN 1,000,000. In Ghana, GHS 12,000 to GHS 16,000, with direct costs of GHS 8,000 to GHS 10,000. In South Africa, ZAR 25,000 to ZAR 35,000, with direct costs of ZAR 16,000 to ZAR 22,000. Large tank installations require more planning, more labour, and more equipment. This lesson covers the key steps. First, confirm the structural capacity of the site. A 10,000-litre tank weighs 10 tonnes when full. The ground must be able to support that load without settling. If the soil is soft, you may need to excavate and replace it with compacted hardcore, or install a reinforced concrete ring beam. A ring beam is a circular or rectangular concrete beam that distributes the tank load over a larger area. For a 10,000-litre tank, a ring beam 300mm wide and 400mm deep, with 12mm rebar and 8mm stirrups at 200mm centres, is usually sufficient. In Kenya, a ring beam for a 10,000-litre tank costs KES 35,000 to KES 50,000. In Nigeria, NGN 280,000 to NGN 400,000. In Ghana, GHS 2,800 to GHS 4,000. In South Africa, ZAR 6,000 to ZAR 9,000. Second, plan the tank farm layout. If you are installing multiple tanks, connect them in parallel, not in series. In a parallel connection, all tanks fill and empty at the same rate. In a series connection, the first tank fills first and the last tank fills last, which can cause overflow and uneven pressure. Use a manifold pipe with equal-length branches to each tank. The manifold diameter should be at least the sum of the individual tank outlet diameters, or larger. For three 10,000-litre tanks with 50mm outlets, use a 90mm or 110mm manifold. Third, install a level indicator. For large tanks, a simple sight glass or a float gauge is essential. A sight glass is a clear tube connected to the bottom and top of the tank. The water level in the tube matches the water level in the tank. In Kenya, a sight glass kit costs KES 4,000 to KES 6,000. In Nigeria, NGN 30,000 to NGN 45,000. In Ghana, GHS 300 to GHS 450. In South Africa, ZAR 600 to ZAR 900. Fourth, install a first-flush and filtration system if the tank is fed by rainwater. For large roofs, a first-flush diverter with a 200-litre capacity costs KES 15,000 to KES 25,000 in Kenya, NGN 120,000 to NGN 180,000 in Nigeria, GHS 1,200 to GHS 1,800 in Ghana, and ZAR 2,500 to ZAR 4,000 in South Africa. Add a sand filter or a cartridge filter before the tank inlet to remove fine sediment. Fifth, plan for maintenance access. Large tanks need a manhole or inspection port at the top. If the tank does not have one, you can install an aftermarket inspection hatch. In Kenya, a 400mm inspection hatch costs KES 3,500 to KES 5,000. In Nigeria, NGN 25,000 to NGN 40,000. In Ghana, GHS 250 to GHS 400. In South Africa, ZAR 500 to ZAR 800. Sixth, consider UV protection. In East and Southern Africa, UV radiation degrades polyethylene over time. Most quality tanks have UV stabilisers, but if you are buying budget tanks, paint them with a light-coloured exterior latex paint or wrap them with UV-resistant shade cloth. Do not use dark colours, as they absorb heat and can cause the water to become too warm for drinking. Seventh, consider water quality. Large tanks that store water for weeks can develop algae and bacteria. Add a chlorine doser or a UV steriliser. For a 10,000-litre tank serving a school, a simple floating chlorine dispenser with 1-inch tablets costs KES 2,000 to KES 3,000 in Kenya, NGN 15,000 to NGN 25,000 in Nigeria, GHS 150 to GHS 250 in Ghana, and ZAR 300 to ZAR 500 in South Africa. Safety note: when installing large tanks, use proper lifting equipment. A 10,000-litre tank weighs 350kg to 500kg empty. Never attempt to lift it with ropes over a tree branch or a makeshift tripod. Use a crane, a forklift, or a team of at least eight people with proper lifting straps. Also, never enter a tank without a confined space permit and a second person standing by. Poly tanks can accumulate toxic gases, especially if they have stored water with organic matter. Ventilate the tank for at least 30 minutes before entry and use a gas monitor if available.

5. Lesson 5: Pricing, Quoting, and Scaling Your Poly Tank Business

The final lesson in this module is about turning your technical skills into a profitable business. Poly tank installation is a competitive market in most African cities, but there is still room for well-organised installers who deliver quality work on time and provide after-sales support. This lesson covers pricing, quoting, marketing, and scaling. Start with a cost model. For every job, calculate direct costs, indirect costs, and profit. Direct costs include the tank, base materials, plumbing materials, labour, transport, and equipment hire. Indirect costs include your vehicle fuel, phone airtime, office rent, and tools depreciation. A common mistake is to quote only direct costs plus a small markup, then discover that indirect costs eat the profit. A healthy target is 25 to 35 percent gross margin on direct costs, plus 10 to 15 percent for indirect costs. For example, a 2,000-litre household installation in Nairobi: tank KES 18,000, base materials KES 6,000, plumbing KES 4,500, labour KES 3,000, transport KES 1,500. Direct costs: KES 33,000. Add 30 percent gross margin: KES 9,900. Add 12 percent indirect: KES 3,960. Quote: KES 46,860. Round to KES 47,000. In Lagos, the same job: tank NGN 130,000, base NGN 55,000, plumbing NGN 35,000, labour NGN 25,000, transport NGN 15,000. Direct costs: NGN 260,000. Add 30 percent: NGN 78,000. Add 12 percent: NGN 31,200. Quote: NGN 369,200. Round to NGN 370,000. In Accra: tank GHS 1,800, base GHS 700, plumbing GHS 450, labour GHS 300, transport GHS 150. Direct costs: GHS 3,400. Add 30 percent: GHS 1,020. Add 12 percent: GHS 408. Quote: GHS 4,828. Round to GHS 4,850. In Johannesburg: tank ZAR 3,200, base ZAR 2,800, plumbing ZAR 1,500, labour ZAR 1,200, transport ZAR 600. Direct costs: ZAR 9,300. Add 30 percent: ZAR 2,790. Add 12 percent: ZAR 1,116. Quote: ZAR 13,206. Round to ZAR 13,200. When quoting, always provide a written quotation with a clear scope of work, a list of materials, a timeline, and payment terms. In Africa, payment terms are critical. For household jobs, ask for 70 percent deposit before buying materials and 30 percent on completion. For institutional jobs, ask for 50 percent deposit, 30 percent on delivery of materials, and 20 percent on completion. Never start a job without a deposit. Never buy materials on credit for a customer who has not paid a deposit. Marketing: the most effective marketing for poly tank installation is word of mouth and visible installations. Put a small signboard on every tank you install with your phone number. Take before-and-after photos and post them on WhatsApp status, Facebook Marketplace, and local community groups. Partner with hardware shops and plumbing suppliers. Offer them a 5 to 10 percent referral commission. In Kenya, a hardware shop referral can bring 5 to 10 jobs per month. In Nigeria, a plumbing supplier referral can bring 10 to 20 jobs per month. In Ghana and South Africa, similar numbers apply. Scaling: once you have consistent jobs, hire and train a small team. A two-person team can install 2 to 3 household tanks per day. A four-person team can install 1 large tank or 3 to 4 household tanks per day. Pay your team a daily rate plus a completion bonus. In Kenya, a helper earns KES 800 to KES 1,200 per day. In Nigeria, NGN 8,000 to NGN 12,000 per day. In Ghana, GHS 80 to GHS 120 per day. In South Africa, ZAR 250 to ZAR 400 per day. Invest in tools: a concrete mixer, a plate compactor, a hole saw set, a pipe cutter, a pressure test pump, and a set of wrenches and spanners. In Kenya, a basic tool kit for poly tank installation costs KES 80,000 to KES 120,000. In Nigeria, NGN 600,000 to NGN 900,000. In Ghana, GHS 6,000 to GHS 9,000. In South Africa, ZAR 12,000 to ZAR 18,000. Safety and compliance: register your business with the relevant authority. In Kenya, register with the eCitizen portal and get a business permit from the county government. In Nigeria, register with the Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC). In Ghana, register with the Registrar General's Department. In South Africa, register with the Companies and Intellectual Property Commission (CIPC). Also, get public liability insurance if you are installing large tanks. A tank that collapses and floods a customer's property can lead to a lawsuit. In Kenya, public liability insurance for a small installation business costs KES 30,000 to KES 50,000 per year. In Nigeria, NGN 150,000 to NGN 250,000. In Ghana, GHS 1,500 to GHS 2,500. In South Africa, ZAR 3,000 to ZAR 5,000. Finally, diversify. Poly tanks are just one product. Add concrete, ferrocement, and brick tanks to your offering. Ferrocement tanks are popular in rural areas because they can be built with local materials and labour. A 10,000-litre ferrocement tank costs KES 80,000 to KES 120,000 in Kenya, NGN 600,000 to NGN 900,000 in Nigeria, GHS 6,000 to GHS 9,000 in Ghana, and ZAR 12,000 to ZAR 18,000 in South Africa. Concrete tanks are durable but slow to build. Brick tanks are common in West Africa and can be rendered with waterproof cement. By offering multiple tank types, you can serve every customer segment and keep your team busy year-round. Safety note: always wear personal protective equipment (PPE) on site. This includes safety boots, gloves, eye protection, and a dust mask when cutting concrete or mixing cement. Keep a first aid kit in your vehicle. Never allow untrained workers to operate power tools or work at heights without a safety harness. A safe business is a profitable business.
