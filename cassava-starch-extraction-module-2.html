1. Determining Optimal Harvest Time

Cassava roots can be harvested from 6 to 24 months after planting, depending on variety and purpose. For starch extraction, you want maximum starch content, which usually peaks between 9 and 12 months for most African varieties. Harvest too early (before 8 months) and roots are small with low starch; too late (after 18 months) and starch turns to sugar, roots become woody, and post-harvest deterioration speeds up. So the sweet spot is 912 months for starch.

How do you know it's time? First, check the leaves: when lower leaves turn yellow and drop, and the plant stops growing new leaves, it's a sign. Second, look at the roots: dig up one or two sample plants. Mature roots have a thick, dark brown skin that is easy to peel, and the flesh is white and firm. If the skin is thin and pale, they are still young. Third, do a simple starch test: grate a small piece of root, squeeze the juice onto a clear glass, and let it settle. If a thick white layer of starch forms at the bottom within 30 minutes, starch content is high.

In Africa, many farmers harvest at 12 months for maximum yield, but if you want early cash, you can harvest at 89 months. However, for starch extraction, waiting until 1012 months gives you more starch per root. Also consider the dry season: harvesting in dry weather reduces root rot and makes peeling easier. If you harvest in the rainy season, roots absorb water, starch gets diluted, and roots rot faster.

Tools for checking: a machete or hoe to dig, a knife to cut the root, a clear glass or jar for the starch test, and a scale to weigh sample roots. Record the date you planted and the date you harvest. Keep notes on starch yield per root. Over time, you'll learn the best time for your variety and soil.

Remember: once roots mature, they don't store well in the ground. If you leave them too long, they become fibrous and lose starch. So plan your harvest when you have labour and a processing facility ready.

2. Harvesting Techniques for Cassava Roots

Harvesting cassava is hard work, but with the right technique you can avoid damaging roots and reduce losses. The goal is to lift the roots out of the soil without breaking or bruising them, because damaged roots rot quickly and lose starch.

Start by cutting the stems about 3040 cm above the ground using a sharp machete or cutlass. This gives you a handle to pull the plant. Then, use a strong hoe or a digging fork to loosen the soil around the roots. Insert the tool about 30 cm away from the stem and push down, then lever the soil up. Do not dig directly into the root clusteryou will cut roots. Once the soil is loose, grab the stem and pull firmly. If the plant resists, loosen more soil. The whole root cluster should come out. If some roots break off, dig them out separately.

For large fields, you can use a tractor-drawn cassava lifter, but most smallholders in Africa harvest by hand. A team of 23 people can harvest about 200300 kg of fresh roots per day. Work in the cool hours of the morning or late afternoon to avoid heat stress. Wear gloves to protect your hands from the rough stems and soil.

After lifting, shake off excess soil gently. Do not bang roots against each other or drop them. Cut the roots from the stem with a sharp knife, leaving about 23 cm of the root attached to the stem. Then, trim off any damaged or rotten parts. Place roots in a shaded area on a clean tarpaulin or mat. Do not leave them in the sunsunlight causes greening and increases deterioration.

If you are harvesting for starch, process within 2448 hours. The sooner, the better. If you must wait, keep roots in a cool, shaded, ventilated place. Never pile roots in a heapthey heat up and rot. Spread them in a single layer. Use baskets or crates for transport to avoid bruising. With careful harvesting, you can reduce post-harvest losses from 30% to under 10%.

3. Sorting and Grading Cassava Roots

Sorting and grading are simple steps that make a big difference in starch quality and price. After harvesting, spread your roots on a clean surface under shade. Look at each root and separate them into three groups: Grade A, Grade B, and rejects.

Grade A roots are fresh, firm, and undamaged. They have no cuts, bruises, or soft spots. The skin is intact and the root is straight or slightly curved. These roots give the highest starch yield and are best for processing. Grade B roots have minor damagesmall cuts, slight bruising, or irregular shapebut are still firm and not rotting. They can be processed quickly or sold for fresh consumption. Rejects are roots that are soft, rotten, infested by insects, or have a bad smell. These must be discarded immediately because they will spoil the whole batch and reduce starch quality.

How to sort: work on a table or mat. Use a sharp knife to trim off any damaged parts. If a root is partially rotten, cut away the rotten part and check the rest. If more than 20% is rotten, discard the whole root. For starch extraction, you want clean, healthy roots. Even a small amount of rot can introduce bacteria that ferment the starch and cause off-flavours.

Grading also considers size. Large, thick roots (more than 5 cm diameter) are easier to peel and grate. Small, thin roots are more work and have less starch. You can sell Grade A roots to starch factories or process them yourself. Grade B can be used for home consumption or local markets. Rejects can be composted or fed to animals after proper treatment (but never use rotten roots for starch).

Sorting should be done within 12 hours of harvest. Keep the groups separate in different baskets or piles. Label them if you are selling. A simple grading system like this helps you get a better price and ensures your starch is clean. In many African markets, ungraded roots fetch 2030% less than graded roots. So take the time to sort.

4. Transportation and Storage of Fresh Roots

Fresh cassava roots are perishable. They start to deteriorate within 2448 hours after harvest. So transportation and storage must be quick and careful. The goal is to keep roots cool, shaded, and ventilated to slow down spoilage.

For transportation, use clean baskets, crates, or sacks. Never use airtight plastic bagsthey trap heat and moisture, causing rot. If you use sacks, make sure they are woven and breathable. Load roots gently; do not throw or drop them. Stack baskets no more than three high to avoid crushing the bottom layer. If you are transporting by truck or pickup, cover the load with a tarpaulin to protect from sun and rain, but leave the sides open for air. Travel during the cooler parts of the day (early morning or evening). The maximum transport time should be 68 hours. If you must travel longer, keep roots in a cool, shaded place overnight.

For storage, fresh roots can be stored for a short time using simple methods. The most common in Africa is to spread roots in a thin layer (one or two roots deep) on a raised platform or on the floor of a well-ventilated shed. Keep them out of direct sunlight. Sprinkle water lightly on the floor to keep humidity high (8090%) but not wet. This can keep roots fresh for 35 days. Another method is to store roots in trenches: dig a shallow trench 30 cm deep in a shaded area, line it with leaves, place roots in a single layer, cover with leaves and soil. This can extend storage to 12 weeks. However, for starch extraction, you should process within 48 hours. Longer storage increases starch loss and off-flavours.

Do not store roots in piles. Piles generate heat and rot. Also, never store roots near chemicals or fuelthey absorb odours. If you see signs of mould or soft spots, remove those roots immediately. For commercial starch production, plan your harvest and processing so that roots go from farm to grater within 24 hours. That is the best way to preserve starch quality.

5. Post-Harvest Physiological Deterioration

Post-harvest physiological deterioration (PPD) is the natural process that makes fresh cassava roots go bad within 13 days after harvest. It starts as blue-black streaks inside the root, then the root becomes soft, brown, and rotten. PPD is not caused by insects or microbes initiallyit is a wound response by the root itself. When you cut or damage the root during harvest, enzymes react with oxygen and cause discoloration. This reduces starch quality and yield.

Why does PPD matter for starch extraction? Because deteriorated roots have lower starch content, the starch is darker, and it may have off-flavours. A root with 25% starch can drop to 15% within 48 hours. That means you lose 40% of your starch. So preventing PPD is critical.

How to slow PPD: First, minimize damage during harvest. Use careful digging and handling. Second, keep roots cool. PPD speeds up in heat. Store roots in shade or in a cool room. Third, process quickly. The best way is to grate roots within 24 hours. If you cannot process immediately, you can use simple treatments. For example, dipping roots in a solution of 1% sodium metabisulfite (food-grade) for 5 minutes can delay PPD for up to 5 days. But this chemical may not be available everywhere. Another method is to store roots in moist sawdust or leaves, which reduces oxygen and slows the reaction. Some farmers in Nigeria and Ghana store roots in trenches with moist soil, which keeps them fresh for up to 2 weeks.

However, for starch extraction, the safest approach is to process within 24 hours. If you must wait, keep roots in a single layer in a well-ventilated, shaded area. Do not pile them. Check for signs of PPD: cut a root openif you see blue-black streaks, it has started. Use those roots first. If roots are already soft and smelly, discard them. Remember, PPD is irreversible. Once it starts, you cannot stop it. So speed is your best tool.

6. Pre-Processing Steps: Washing and Peeling

Before you can extract starch, you must wash and peel the cassava roots. These steps remove dirt, sand, and the outer skin, which contains bitter compounds and reduces starch purity. Clean roots give you white, high-quality starch. Dirty roots give you brown, gritty starch that sells for less.

Washing: Start by soaking roots in a large basin or drum of clean water for 1015 minutes. This softens the dirt. Then, scrub each root with a stiff brush or a piece of rough cloth. Use a brush with plastic bristlesnot metal, which can scratch the root. For large quantities, you can build a simple washing trough: a slanted wooden or concrete trough with water flowing through it. Put roots at the top, and as they roll down, they rub against each other and the sides, removing dirt. Rinse with clean water. Change the water oftenif it gets muddy, you are just spreading dirt. For 100 kg of roots, you need about 200 litres of clean water. After washing, roots should look clean and the skin should be visible.

Peeling: You can peel cassava with a sharp knife or a traditional peeler. The skin has two layers: a thin outer layer (brown) and a thicker inner layer (pinkish or white). For starch, you should remove both layers because the inner layer contains cyanogenic glucosides (bitter compounds) that can affect starch quality. Use a knife to cut off the ends, then slit the skin lengthwise and peel it off. Work on a clean surface. For 100 kg of roots, peeling takes about 23 hours with 2 people. Wear gloves if your hands are sensitive. The peels can be composted or fed to animals after drying (but not fresh, as they contain cyanide).

After peeling, cut the roots into small pieces (about 23 cm) to make grating easier. Then, wash the peeled roots again in clean water to remove any remaining dirt or starch. Now they are ready for grating. Remember: wash and peel within 24 hours of harvest to avoid PPD. If you peel too early, the roots darken. So time your processing: harvest, wash, peel, grateall in one day for best starch yield.

<h2>Practical Reference: Cassava Harvesting and Post-Harvest Handling</h2>

<p>For cassava starch processors across Africa, the gap between a profitable season and a loss-making one is often decided in the 48 hours between lifting roots from the soil and feeding them into the grater. Cassava roots contain roughly 6070% moisture and begin to deteriorate within hours of harvest. Unlike grains or tubers such as yams, cassava has no dormancy period after maturity it starts consuming its own starch reserves immediately. Every hour of delay translates into lost starch yield, higher processing costs, and lower-grade final product. This practical reference consolidates the decision points that determine whether your roots reach the factory gate in optimal condition: when to harvest, how to lift and handle roots, how to sort and grade, how to transport and store, how to manage post-harvest physiological deterioration (PPD), and how to prepare roots for washing and peeling. It is written for smallholder farmers, aggregators, and small-to-medium starch enterprises operating in African conditions where labour, transport, and market timing are the binding constraints.</p>

<h3>Determining Optimal Harvest Time</h3>

<p>Cassava roots reach peak starch content between 9 and 18 months after planting, depending on variety, rainfall pattern, and soil type. Early-maturing varieties (e.g., TME 419, TMS 30572) can be harvested at 912 months; late-maturing varieties may need 1524 months. The business decision is not simply "when is starch highest" but "when does marginal starch gain justify the cost of leaving the crop in the ground?" After 18 months, starch content typically declines as roots lignify and become fibrous, while the risk of root rot increases. In practice, harvest timing should be driven by three signals:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Root maturity indicators:</strong> Brown, dry outer skin; easy detachment from the root neck; a creamy-white, firm internal flesh with no visible vascular streaking.</li>
<li><strong>Starch content testing:</strong> A simple specific gravity test (weight in air vs. weight in water) or a rapid starch iodine test can indicate starch percentage. Roots with specific gravity above 1.12 generally indicate good starch content.</li>
<li><strong>Market and factory scheduling:</strong> If you supply a starch factory, harvest to match their intake schedule. Delivering 5 tonnes on Monday when they can only process 3 tonnes by Wednesday means 2 tonnes will deteriorate before processing.</li>
</ul>

<p>In rain-fed systems across Nigeria, Ghana, and Tanzania, the dry season (NovemberMarch) is preferred for harvest because roots have lower moisture content, PPD progression is slower, and roads are passable. However, harvesting in peak dry season can harden soils and increase root breakage during lifting.</p>

<h3>Harvesting Techniques for Cassava Roots</h3>

<p>Mechanical damage during harvesting is the single largest controllable cause of rapid PPD. Bruised, cut, or snapped roots deteriorate 35 times faster than intact roots. The standard manual technique involves:</p>

<ol>
<li>Cutting the stem 2030 cm above ground 12 weeks before harvest to allow the root to "cure" slightly and reduce moisture.</li>
<li>Loosening the soil around the root with a fork or hoe, then lifting the root by the stalk base never pulling by the root tip.</li>
<li>Shaking off excess soil gently; do not beat roots against the ground or each other.</li>
<li>Placing roots in shade immediately direct sun exposure for more than 2 hours accelerates PPD.</li>
</ol>

<p>For larger operations, tractor-mounted cassava lifters or modified potato diggers can reduce labour by 6070% and lower root damage if operated at correct depth and speed. The key is to set the blade depth to 2025 cm so the root is lifted rather than cut.</p>

<h3>Transportation and Storage of Fresh Roots</h3>

<p>Fresh cassava roots are perishable. The goal of transport and storage is to minimise time, heat, and physical damage. Best practices for African conditions include:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Harvest to processing window:</strong> Target less than 24 hours; never exceed 48 hours without shade and ventilation.</li>
<li><strong>Transport:</strong> Use open-sided trucks or trailers with ventilation. Never pile roots more than 60 cm deep. Line the bed with leaves or jute sacks to cushion impact.</li>
<li><strong>Storage:</strong> Fresh roots cannot be stored long-term without processing. For short-term (1224 hrs), spread in a shaded, well-ventilated shed in a single layer. Sprinkling water can reduce temperature but increases rot risk if drainage is poor.</li>
<li><strong>Alternative:</strong> If processing is delayed, roots can be peeled, washed, and stored in clean water for up to 24 hours but this is a pre-processing step, not a storage solution.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Post-Harvest Physiological Deterioration (PPD)</h3>

<p>PPD is the blue-black vascular streaking that appears in cassava roots within 2472 hours of harvest. It is a wound response, not a microbial infection, though secondary infections follow. PPD is triggered by mechanical damage, heat, and water loss. Once PPD advances beyond the vascular ring, starch extraction becomes inefficient and the final starch may be discoloured. Management strategies include:</p>

<ul>
<li>Minimising root damage during harvest and transport.</li>
<li>Keeping roots cool and shaded.</li>
<li>Processing within 24 hours.</li>
<li>Using PPD-tolerant varieties where available (e.g., some TMS varieties).</li>
<li>Applying a post-harvest dip in clean water or a dilute sodium hypochlorite solution (food-grade) to slow microbial growth but this does not stop PPD itself.</li>
</ul>

<div style="background:#fdecea; border-left:6px solid #c0392b; padding:14px 18px; margin:18px 0; font-family:Arial, sans-serif; font-size:14px; color:#5a1a1a;">
<strong style="color:#c0392b; font-size:15px;"> SAFETY WARNING</strong><br>
<strong>Never consume or process cassava roots that show signs of rot, mould, or severe PPD without proper testing.</strong> Damaged roots can accumulate cyanogenic glycosides (linamarin and lotaustralin), which release hydrogen cyanide during processing. While normal starch extraction (soaking, grating, pressing, drying) reduces cyanide to safe levels, roots with advanced decay may have unpredictable toxin levels. Always discard mouldy or foul-smelling roots. When in doubt, test with a picrate paper kit (available from IITA or local agricultural extension offices) before processing for food use. For industrial starch, ensure your process includes adequate soaking and pressing steps to reduce cyanide below 10 ppm.
</div>

<h3>Pre-Processing Steps: Washing and Peeling</h3>

<p>Washing and peeling are the bridge between harvest and starch extraction. Poor washing introduces sand and grit that damage graters and contaminate starch. Poor peeling leaves the cortex (brown outer layer) and the inner rind, which contain higher cyanide levels and lower starch quality.</p>

<p><strong>Washing:</strong> Use a three-stage wash system pre-soak, scrub, rinse. A simple rotary washer or a concrete washing bay with running water works well. Change water frequently; dirty water re-contaminates roots. For small operations, a 200-litre drum with a brush and clean water is sufficient.</p>

<p><strong>Peeling:</strong> Manual peeling with knives removes the outer skin and inner rind. Aim for 1015% peel loss by weight. Mechanical peelers (abrasion or knife type) can reduce labour but increase root damage. After peeling, roots should be washed again and processed immediately peeled roots deteriorate faster than unpeeled roots.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul>
<li> Confirm root maturity: brown skin, easy detachment, firm white flesh.</li>
<li> Harvest in cool hours (early morning or late afternoon) to reduce heat stress.</li>
<li> Lift roots with a fork never pull by the root tip.</li>
<li> Shake off soil gently; place roots in shade within 30 minutes.</li>
<li> Sort into Grade A, B, C, and Reject before transport.</li>
<li> Transport in ventilated trucks; pile depth 60 cm; cushion with leaves or sacks.</li>
<li> Process within 24 hours; never exceed 48 hours without shade and ventilation.</li>
<li> Wash in clean water, peel to remove skin and rind, wash again, and process immediately.</li>
</ul>
