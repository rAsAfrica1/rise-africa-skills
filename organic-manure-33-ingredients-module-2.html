1. Sourcing and Selecting the 33 Ingredients Locally

Welcome to Module 2 of Organic Manure with 33 Ingredients. In this first lesson we focus on the foundation of your entire production business: sourcing and selecting the 33 ingredients locally. If you get this step right, everything else becomes easier. If you get it wrong, you will waste money, time and labour. The goal is to produce a consistent, high-quality organic manure that farmers will buy again and again. The 33 ingredients are grouped into five families: bulky carbon sources, nitrogen-rich greens, mineral and ash sources, microbial activators, and binders or conditioners. Your job is to identify at least three local suppliers for each family so you are never dependent on one source. Start with a simple notebook or phone spreadsheet. List every ingredient, its local name, where it is found, the best season to collect it, and the current price per kilogram or per bag. This becomes your sourcing map. In most African countries you can find rice husks, maize cobs, groundnut shells, coffee husks, sawdust, sugarcane bagasse, banana pseudostems, and dry grass. These are your carbon sources. They are usually cheap or free but you must negotiate transport. Nitrogen-rich greens include tithonia, moringa leaves, comfrey, pawpaw leaves, kitchen waste, poultry litter, goat and cattle dung, fish waste, and urine. Mineral and ash sources include wood ash, charcoal dust, bone meal, eggshells, rock phosphate where available, limestone, gypsum, and termite mound soil. Microbial activators include effective microorganisms, forest topsoil, compost from a trusted neighbour, molasses, yeast, and fermented plant juices. Binders and conditioners include clay, bentonite, cassava starch, and water. Now think like a business person. Your target is to produce one tonne of finished manure per week within three months. That means you need roughly 600 to 700 kilograms of dry carbon material, 200 to 250 kilograms of nitrogen material, 50 to 80 kilograms of mineral material, 20 to 30 litres of liquid activators, and enough water to keep moisture at 50 to 60 percent. Write these quantities on a wall chart. Visit markets, mills, abattoirs, poultry farms, and municipal waste collection points. Offer to remove waste for free or at a small fee. Many millers pay to dispose of rice husks and coffee husks. You can turn their disposal cost into your raw material. Always test materials before committing. Avoid sawdust from treated timber, avoid plastic, glass, metal, and battery waste, and avoid manure from animals recently treated with long-acting antibiotics. Smell is a guide: fresh manure is fine, but rancid or chemically smelling material should be rejected. For minerals, ask local agro-dealers for rock phosphate, gypsum and lime. For bone meal, work with butchers and boil, dry and crush bones safely. For ash, collect only from clean wood fires, never from burnt plastics or painted wood. For microbial activators, you can start with a simple local brew: 1 kilogram of sugar or 1 litre of molasses, 1 kilogram of fresh forest soil, 10 litres of water, and 1 litre of fresh manure. Ferment for seven days in a shaded drum. This becomes your starter. Now build a sourcing calendar. In the rainy season, green matter is abundant but drying is hard. In the dry season, carbon materials are dry and easy to store but greens are scarce. Plan to collect and dry carbon materials in the dry season and store them under a tarpaulin. Collect greens in the rainy season and compost them immediately or dry them in thin layers. Buy minerals in bulk once or twice a year when prices are low. Negotiate transport early. A single trip with a hired pickup or tractor trailer can move two to three tonnes of raw materials. Share transport with other compost makers to reduce cost. Keep records of every purchase: date, supplier, quantity, price, moisture condition, and any problems. After three months you will know your true cost per tonne. Most beginners underprice their manure because they forget transport, labour, water and shrinkage. Your selling price must cover all costs plus 30 to 50 percent profit. Finally, build relationships. Pay suppliers on time. Give them a small gift at harvest or Eid or Christmas. Offer to help them manage their waste. A reliable supplier is more valuable than a cheap one. In the next lesson we will look at how to prepare, dry, crush and store each ingredient so that your final 33-ingredient manure is consistent and sellable.

2. Preparing, Drying, Crushing and Storing the Ingredients

In this second lesson we move from sourcing to preparation. Raw ingredients are not ready to mix. They must be dried, crushed, screened and stored correctly. Poor preparation is the number one reason why organic manure fails in the market. Farmers complain about lumps, stones, bad smell, uneven texture, and low performance. All of these are preparation problems. Let us go through the process step by step. First, drying. Carbon materials like rice husks, maize cobs, groundnut shells, coffee husks and sawdust must be dried to below 15 percent moisture. Spread them on a concrete slab, black plastic sheet or tarpaulin in thin layers of 5 to 10 centimetres. Turn them twice a day. In strong sun, most materials dry in two to four days. If you have a lot, build a simple solar dryer with a transparent roof and vents. Never dry on bare ground because you will pick up soil, stones and moisture. For greens like tithonia, moringa, comfrey and pawpaw leaves, you can either compost them fresh or dry them for storage. If you dry them, shade-dry for two days then sun-dry for one day. This preserves nitrogen. Poultry litter and animal dung should be dried or composted immediately. Fresh dung attracts flies and loses nitrogen. Spread it thin and turn daily. Second, crushing and grinding. Many ingredients must be reduced in size so they mix evenly and break down faster. Maize cobs, bones, eggshells, charcoal and dry stems need crushing. You can use a hammer mill, a mortar and pestle, or a simple wooden mallet on a concrete floor. For a small business, hire a hammer mill once a week. For a larger business, buy a diesel or electric hammer mill. The target particle size is 2 to 5 millimetres for most materials. Fine powders like ash, bone meal and clay should pass through a 2 millimetre sieve. Third, screening. Build a simple screen with wooden frame and wire mesh. Use 5 millimetre mesh for the first screen and 2 millimetre mesh for the final screen. Screening removes stones, plastic, glass, unbroken cobs and oversized pieces. It also gives your final product a uniform look that customers trust. Fourth, measuring and batching. The 33 ingredients must be mixed in the right proportions. A practical recipe for one tonne of finished manure is: 600 kilograms carbon materials, 250 kilograms nitrogen materials, 60 kilograms mineral and ash materials, 30 kilograms microbial activator carrier, 40 kilograms binder and conditioner, and 20 kilograms water or liquid activator. Within each family, vary the ingredients so you use all 33 over a production cycle. For example, use rice husks, maize cobs, coffee husks and sawdust together as carbon. Use tithonia, moringa, poultry litter, goat dung and fish waste together as nitrogen. Use wood ash, bone meal, eggshells, charcoal dust and termite soil together as minerals. Use molasses, yeast, forest soil and fermented plant juice together as activators. Use clay, cassava starch and bentonite together as binders. This is how you honestly claim 33 ingredients. Fifth, storage. Store dry carbon materials under a tarpaulin or in a dry shed on pallets. Keep them off the floor. Store minerals in sealed bags or drums because ash and lime absorb moisture. Store microbial activators in shaded, cool conditions. Never store fresh manure and dried materials together. Label every bag and drum with the ingredient name, date and moisture condition. Use a simple first-in, first-out system. Sixth, quality control. Before mixing, test each batch. Squeeze test for moisture: if water drips, it is too wet; if it crumbles, it is too dry. Smell test: good materials smell earthy, not rotten or chemical. Colour test: carbon materials should be brown or grey, not black from burning. Mineral materials should be dry and free-flowing. Keep a sample of each batch in a small jar for three months. If a customer complains, you can check the sample. Seventh, labour and costing. Preparation is labour-intensive. For one tonne per week you need two to three workers for drying, turning, crushing and screening. Pay them fairly and train them well. A trained worker produces less waste and better quality. Record labour hours per tonne. If your labour cost is more than 20 percent of your selling price, improve your tools. A hammer mill, a good screen and a concrete drying floor will pay for themselves in one season. Finally, safety. Wear dust masks when crushing bones, ash and charcoal. Wear gloves when handling fresh manure. Keep children away from the work area. Store tools safely. In the next lesson we will mix the 33 ingredients and manage the composting process so that the final manure is mature, safe and full of beneficial microbes.

3. Mixing the 33 Ingredients and Managing Composting

Now we come to the heart of Module 2: mixing the 33 ingredients and managing the composting process. This is where your raw materials become a living product. The goal is to produce mature, stable, pathogen-free organic manure with a carbon-to-nitrogen ratio between 20 and 30 to 1, moisture at 50 to 60 percent, and temperature that rises to 55 to 65 degrees Celsius then falls back to ambient. Let us start with the mixing area. Choose a shaded, flat, well-drained spot near your water source. Build a concrete slab or use a thick tarpaulin. Never mix on bare ground because you lose nutrients and pick up soil. You will need a shovel, a fork, a watering can, a hose, a thermometer, and a tarpaulin for covering. Now the mixing sequence. First, lay down the carbon materials in a layer of about 20 centimetres. Second, add nitrogen materials in a layer of 10 centimetres. Third, sprinkle mineral and ash materials thinly. Fourth, spray the microbial activator liquid evenly. Fifth, sprinkle the binder and conditioner. Repeat these layers until you have a windrow or pile about 1.5 metres high and 2 metres wide. The length can be any size. For one tonne, a pile of 2 metres by 2 metres by 1.5 metres is enough. Turn the pile with a shovel or fork until colours are uniform. Break all lumps. Add water gradually while turning. The squeeze test is your guide: when you squeeze a handful, a few drops should appear between your fingers but not stream out. If it streams, add dry carbon. If it crumbles, add water. Now manage the compost. The first three days are the heating phase. Temperature should rise to 55 to 65 degrees Celsius. This kills weed seeds and pathogens. If temperature goes above 70 degrees, turn the pile to cool it. If it does not heat up after three days, your pile is too dry, too wet, or lacking nitrogen. Add fresh green material or urine and turn. Turn the pile every three days for the first two weeks. Then turn every five days for the next two weeks. Then turn once a week for two more weeks. In total, composting takes six to eight weeks in warm African conditions. In cooler highlands it may take ten to twelve weeks. Keep the pile covered with a tarpaulin during heavy rain to prevent waterlogging. In dry weather, water the pile every two days. Watch for signs of maturity. Mature compost is dark brown or black, smells earthy like forest soil, is cool to the touch, and has no recognizable original materials. A simple test: put a handful in a jar of water, shake, and let it settle. Mature compost floats and the water stays clear. Immature compost sinks and the water turns dark and smelly. Another test: plant bean or maize seeds in a small pot of the compost. If they germinate and grow well, the compost is safe. If they yellow and die, it is immature or too salty. Now let us talk about the 33 ingredients in practice. You do not need all 33 in every single batch. You need to use all 33 across your production cycle. For example, batch one may use rice husks, maize cobs, coffee husks, tithonia, poultry litter, wood ash, bone meal, molasses and clay. Batch two may use groundnut shells, sawdust, banana pseudostems, moringa, goat dung, fish waste, eggshells, charcoal dust, yeast and cassava starch. Over three or four batches, every ingredient is used. This keeps your product consistent and your sourcing flexible. Record every batch. Write the date, ingredients, quantities, moisture, temperature, turning dates, and final yield. After three batches you will know your best recipe. Now consider additives. Some trainers add rock phosphate, gypsum, lime, and beneficial microbes. Use them carefully. Lime raises pH. Do not add lime and ammonium-rich materials at the same time because you lose nitrogen. Add lime at the start if your materials are acidic, or at the end if your soil is acidic. Rock phosphate works best in compost because organic acids release phosphorus. Gypsum adds calcium and sulphur. Keep records of every additive. Finally, think about business. Your compost is not just waste management. It is a product. Package it in 50 kilogram bags or 25 kilogram bags. Label it with your brand, the 33 ingredients, the batch number, the production date, and the application rates for maize, vegetables, bananas, coffee and tea. Sell to farmers, nurseries, landscapers, and municipal parks. Offer a money-back guarantee if the manure does not perform. In the next lesson we will focus on quality testing, packaging, branding and pricing so you can sell at a premium.

4. Quality Testing, Packaging, Branding and Pricing

In this fourth lesson we focus on turning your organic manure into a premium product that customers trust and pay for. Quality testing, packaging, branding and pricing are not optional extras. They are the difference between selling a few bags to neighbours and building a real business. Let us start with quality testing. You do not need a laboratory for every batch. You need simple field tests and occasional laboratory tests. Field tests include the squeeze test for moisture, the smell test for maturity, the jar test for stability, and the seed germination test for safety. Do these tests on every batch. Record the results. For laboratory tests, send one sample per month or per ten batches to a government soil laboratory, a university, or a private lab. Ask for pH, organic matter, total nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium, moisture, and heavy metals if available. In most African countries, a basic test costs between 20 and 80 US dollars. This is a business expense, not a waste. It gives you data for your label and confidence for your customers. Now packaging. Choose bags that protect the product from moisture and sunlight. Woven polypropylene bags with a polyethylene inner liner are good. For premium markets, use printed bags with your brand. Start with 50 kilogram bags for field crops and 25 kilogram bags for nurseries and home gardens. Also consider 5 kilogram and 10 kilogram bags for urban customers. Fill bags to a consistent weight. Use a simple scale. Seal bags with a sewing machine or a heat sealer. Store bags on pallets in a dry, shaded place. Never store bags directly on the floor or against a wall. Now branding. Your brand is your promise. Choose a name that is easy to remember and easy to pronounce in local languages. Register the name if possible. Design a simple label with: brand name, product name, net weight, the 33 ingredients, batch number, production date, expiry date, application rates, storage instructions, and your phone number. Use pictures of healthy maize, vegetables or bananas. Use local language and English or French. Keep the design clean and honest. Do not claim to cure every problem. Claim what you can prove: improves soil organic matter, increases water holding capacity, supplies balanced nutrients, and supports beneficial microbes. Now pricing. Many organic manure producers underprice because they do not calculate their true cost. Let us calculate. For one tonne of finished manure, your costs may include: raw materials 20 to 40 dollars, transport 10 to 30 dollars, labour 30 to 60 dollars, water and energy 5 to 15 dollars, packaging 10 to 20 dollars, testing and labelling 5 to 10 dollars, and overheads 10 to 20 dollars. Total cost may be 90 to 195 dollars per tonne. Add 30 to 50 percent profit. So your selling price should be 120 to 290 dollars per tonne, or 6 to 15 dollars per 50 kilogram bag. In many African markets, a 50 kilogram bag of good organic manure sells for 8 to 20 dollars. Check your local market. Do not compete on price alone. Compete on quality, consistency and service. Offer delivery within 20 kilometres. Offer free application advice. Offer a discount for bulk orders of 20 bags or more. Offer a loyalty card for repeat customers. Now distribution. Sell directly to farmers at your site, at markets, at agricultural shows, and through agro-dealers. Give agro-dealers a 15 to 25 percent margin. Train them so they can explain your product. Work with extension officers, farmer groups, cooperatives and NGOs. They can buy in bulk and distribute to members. Use radio, WhatsApp, Facebook and local meetings to market your product. Show before-and-after photos. Invite farmers to a demonstration plot. Let them see the difference. Now customer service. Keep a customer register with name, phone, location, crop, and quantity bought. Follow up after two weeks and after harvest. Ask about results. Solve problems quickly. If a customer is not satisfied, replace the product or refund the money. A satisfied customer brings five more. An unsatisfied customer tells twenty people. Finally, record keeping. Keep a simple cash book, a stock book, a production book and a customer book. Know your cost per bag, your selling price, your profit, and your best-selling size. After six months you will know which products and which customers are most profitable. In the next lesson we will look at scaling up, training workers, and building partnerships for long-term success.

5. Scaling Up, Training Workers and Building Partnerships

In this final lesson of Module 2 we focus on growth. You have learned to source, prepare, mix, compost, test, package, brand and price your 33-ingredient organic manure. Now you must scale up without losing quality. Scaling up means producing more, selling more, and earning more while keeping your standards high. Let us start with production planning. Decide your target: 1 tonne per week, 5 tonnes per week, or 20 tonnes per week. For 1 tonne per week you need a small shed, a concrete slab, a screen, a hammer mill, a scale, and two to three workers. For 5 tonnes per week you need a larger shed, a mechanical turner or a tractor with a front loader, a bigger hammer mill, a bagging machine, and eight to twelve workers. For 20 tonnes per week you need a factory-style setup with conveyor belts, mixers, dryers, and a laboratory. Do not jump too fast. Grow step by step. Every time you double production, check your quality. If quality drops, stop and fix it. Now training workers. Your workers are your most important asset. Train them in safety, quality, record keeping and customer care. Use a simple training manual with pictures. Train in local language. Show them how to do the squeeze test, the smell test, the jar test and the seed test. Teach them to record every batch. Teach them to keep the work area clean. Teach them to treat customers with respect. Pay wages on time. Give bonuses for quality and productivity. Provide gloves, masks, boots and drinking water. A trained, motivated worker produces better manure and wastes less material. Now systems and standard operating procedures. Write down every step: receiving raw materials, drying, crushing, screening, mixing, composting, turning, testing, packaging, labelling, storage, and dispatch. Put these steps on a wall chart. Use checklists for every batch. Assign one person to be quality controller. This person checks every batch before it is bagged. This simple system prevents most mistakes. Now partnerships. You cannot do everything alone. Partner with farmers, farmer groups, cooperatives, agro-dealers, transport companies, input suppliers, NGOs, government extension services, schools, churches, mosques and community leaders. Farmers can supply raw materials and buy your manure. Cooperatives can buy in bulk. Agro-dealers can sell your product. Transport companies can move raw materials and finished goods. NGOs and government can provide training, grants and market linkages. Schools and religious groups can host demonstrations and help with marketing. Build a simple partnership agreement. Write down what each partner gives and what each partner gets. Keep your promises. Now finance and growth. You need money to scale up. Start with your own savings and reinvest your profits. Then consider a small loan from a bank, a microfinance institution, a savings group, or a government youth or women fund. Prepare a simple business plan with your production cost, selling price, profit, and repayment plan. Keep records. Lenders trust records. Also consider grants from NGOs and development agencies. Many support organic agriculture, climate-smart farming and youth employment. Now marketing at scale. Build a brand that customers trust. Use radio, WhatsApp, Facebook, TikTok and local markets. Attend agricultural shows. Organise field days. Offer free samples to influential farmers. Work with extension officers. Publish your laboratory results. Show before-and-after photos. Build a customer database. Send reminders before planting season. Offer credit to trusted customers. Offer training on how to use organic manure with other good practices. Now risk management. Every business has risks. Raw material prices may rise. Rain may spoil your compost. Pests may invade your store. Customers may delay payment. Workers may leave. Plan for these risks. Keep three months of raw materials in stock. Cover your compost. Store bags on pallets. Keep a cash reserve. Train more than one person for every key job. Insure your business if possible. Keep good records. Now social and environmental responsibility. Your business should improve soil, water, air and community health. Do not cut down forests for raw materials. Use crop residues, animal waste and municipal organic waste. Do not use toxic chemicals. Do not exploit workers. Pay fair wages. Support local farmers. Teach children about composting. Your business can be a model for your community. Finally, your action plan. In the next 30 days, identify your 33 ingredients and three suppliers for each. In 60 days, build your drying floor, screen and mixing area. In 90 days, produce your first tonne and test it. In 120 days, package, label and sell your first 20 bags. In 180 days, produce five tonnes per week and train five workers. In 365 days, build partnerships, register your brand, and apply for a small loan or grant. Review your plan every month. Adjust as needed. Remember, organic manure with 33 ingredients is not just a product. It is a business that builds soil, creates jobs, and strengthens communities. Thank you for completing Module 2. In Module 3 we will look at application rates for different crops and how to combine organic manure with other sustainable practices.

<h3>1. Local sourcing map: what to collect, where, and when</h3>

<p>Africa's advantage is biomass density. A single market in Kampala, Nairobi, Accra or Lilongwe can supply more vegetable waste in one day than a small composting yard can process in a week. The constraint is not supply. It is logistics, moisture and contamination.</p>

<p>Build a sourcing map with five columns: ingredient, source, distance (km), typical moisture, and landed cost per kg. Update it every season. Prices for crop residues move with the harvest calendar; when maize is harvested in the Rift Valley or the Guinea savannah, cobs and stover are nearly free but transport is the real cost. Off-season, the same material may be priced as animal feed.</p>

<h3>2. Testing each ingredient at intake</h3>

<p>You do not need a laboratory to run the first gate. Train every worker on four field tests: squeeze, smell, sight and float. Record results on a simple intake sheet. A batch rejected at intake costs you transport only. A batch rejected after mixing costs you the whole heap.</p>

<p><strong>Squeeze test (moisture).</strong> Take a fistful. If water drips, moisture is above 60%. If it holds shape but no water appears, it is around 4050%. If it crumbles, it is below 25% and ready for storage. Target below 15% for dry storage.</p>

<p><strong>Smell test.</strong> Earthy and sweet means active decomposition. Sharp ammonia means too much nitrogen or too much poultry litter. Sour or rotten means anaerobic conditions and a failed batch. Petrol, chemical or burning smells mean contamination. Reject.</p>

<p><strong>Sight test.</strong> Look for glass, metal, plastic, battery casings, pesticide containers, veterinary needles and faecal matter from unknown sources. A 10-minute visual inspection per tonne prevents a 3-month loss.</p>

<p><strong>Float test (for ash and charcoal).</strong> Drop a spoonful in water. Clean wood ash sinks slowly and clouds the water. Ash with sand, grit or plastic floats or leaves residue. Reject contaminated ash.</p>

<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0;font-weight:bold;color:#8a1f16;font-size:17px;"> SAFETY WARNING read before handling any manure, ash or poultry litter</p>
<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0;color:#5c1710;">Manure and poultry litter can carry <em>Salmonella</em>, <em>E. coli</em> O157:H7, <em>Campylobacter</em>, <em>Cryptosporidium</em> and <em>Ascaris</em> eggs. These cause serious diarrhoeal disease, especially in children under five and immunocompromised workers. WHO and FAO classify untreated manure as a high-risk material for food crops.</p>
<ul style="margin:0 0 8px 0;color:#5c1710;">
<li>Wear gloves and closed shoes. Never handle fresh manure barefoot or with open wounds.</li>
<li>Wash hands with soap for at least 20 seconds after any contact, and before eating, drinking or smoking.</li>
<li>Never apply fresh (untreated) manure to vegetables eaten raw. Compost at 5565C for at least 15 days, turning at least five times, or follow WHO/FAO treatment guidance.</li>
<li>Do not use manure from animals recently treated with antibiotics or anthelmintics without a withholding period. Residues harm soil biology and can enter the food chain.</li>
<li>Keep children and livestock out of the composting yard. Cover heaps to prevent runoff into wells, streams or vegetable plots.</li>
<li>Do not burn plastic, batteries, tyres or treated timber in the yard. Ash from these sources contains heavy metals and dioxins and must never enter your manure.</li>
</ul>

<h3>3. Business-first decisions this week</h3>

<p>Three decisions separate a profitable yard from a busy one. First, standardise your intake sheet and refuse contaminated loads at the gate. Second, negotiate standing collection agreements with two markets and one abattoir so your raw material cost is predictable. Third, cost every ingredient per kilogram before it enters the heap, not after the bag is sealed.</p>

<p>Use the checklist below at every intake. Print it, laminate it, and keep it on the wall of the store.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Source verified.</strong> Ingredient traced to a known farm, market, mill or abattoir. No municipal mixed waste.</li>
<li><strong>Moisture checked.</strong> Squeeze test done. Dry material below 15%; fresh material scheduled for immediate composting.</li>
<li><strong>Contaminants screened.</strong> No plastic, glass, metal, battery ash, pesticide containers or veterinary waste.</li>
<li><strong>Smell and colour acceptable.</strong> Earthy, not ammonia-sharp, sour or chemical.</li>
<li><strong>Safety confirmed.</strong> No manure from animals under antibiotic or dewormer treatment. Withholding period respected.</li>
<li><strong>Cost recorded.</strong> Landed cost per kg written on the intake sheet before the material is stored.</li>
<li><strong>Storage correct.</strong> Raised pallets, dry floor, labelled sacks, away from walls and livestock.</li>
<li><strong>Batch traceable.</strong> Date, supplier, weight and intake decision recorded for every load.</li>
</ol>
