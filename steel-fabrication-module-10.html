Lessons

1. Beam and Column Fabrication

Beams and columns are the backbone of any steel structure. In Africa, we often use universal beams (UB) and universal columns (UC) imported or locally rolled. Your job is to cut, drill, and weld them to exact lengths and shapes. Start by studying the fabrication drawings. Mark cut lengths with a chalk line or soapstone, allowing for shrinkageabout 1.5 mm per metre for welding. Use a band saw or oxy-fuel torch to cut. For clean cuts, a plasma cutter works well on thinner sections. After cutting, grind the ends square. For columns, you may need to weld base plates and cap plates. Base plates are usually 2025 mm thick, with holes for anchor bolts. Position the plate, check squareness with a try square, and tack weld. Then weld all around with a 3 mm root gap. Use E7018 electrodes for strength. For beams, you might need to cope the ends to fit into connections. A beam-to-column connection often requires notching the beam flanges. Use a template to mark the cope, then cut with a torch and grind smooth. Always preheat thick sections (over 25 mm) to 100150C before welding to prevent cracking. In many African workshops, you'll use a 350A inverter welder. Set it to 130150A for 3.2 mm electrodes. After welding, clean slag and inspect for undercut or porosity. Finally, mark the member with its piece mark using a paint pen or metal stamp. This ensures correct assembly on site. Remember, a beam cut 5 mm short is scrap. Measure twice, cut once.

2. Truss and Girder Fabrication

Trusses and girders carry heavy loads over long spans. In Africa, they are common in warehouses, churches, and bridges. A truss is made of chords (top and bottom) and web members (diagonals and verticals). Girders are deep beams, often welded from plates. Start with a jiga flat steel table or concrete slab where you can assemble the truss. Mark the node points using a tape measure and chalk. Cut all members to length, allowing for gusset plates. Gusset plates are typically 812 mm thick. For a 12 m span truss, use angles like 75x75x6 mm for chords and 50x50x5 mm for webs. Assemble the truss on the jig, clamp members, and check diagonals with a tape measurethey must be equal. Tack weld all joints. Then weld fully using a sequence to avoid distortion: weld opposite sides alternately. Use E6013 electrodes for general work, or E7018 for high strength. For girders, you may need to weld web to flanges with a continuous fillet weld. Use a 4 mm electrode at 160180A. To prevent twisting, weld in a back-step pattern. After welding, check straightness with a string line. If the truss is warped, apply heat with a torch and correct with a hydraulic jack. Finally, drill holes for connectionsusually 18 mm for M16 bolts. Paint with red oxide primer. In rural areas, you might use a generator-powered welder. Always wear a welding helmet and gloves. A well-made truss can last 50 years if protected from rust.

3. Connection Design and Detailing

Connections transfer forces between members. They are designed by engineers but fabricated by you. Common connections: bolted, welded, and riveted (rare today). For bolted connections, you need to drill holes accurately. Use a magnetic drill or a pillar drill. Hole diameter is usually 2 mm larger than bolt diameter. For M20 bolts, drill 22 mm holes. Mark hole positions from the drawing using a template. Clamp members together and drill through both at oncethis ensures alignment. Deburr holes with a grinder. For welded connections, prepare the edges: bevel 30 for full penetration welds. Use a 3 mm root gap and tack weld. Then weld in multiple passes. For a moment connection (beam to column), you might weld the beam flanges directly to the column and bolt the web. This requires a backing plate. In Africa, we often use simple shear connections: a fin plate welded to the column and bolted to the beam web. Fin plates are 10 mm thick, with 34 bolts. Always check bolt torque: for M20 grade 8.8, torque to 400 Nm. Use a torque wrench. If not available, use the turn-of-nut method: snug tight, then turn 1/3 turn. For welded connections, inspect with a magnifying glass for cracks. Remember, a connection that fails can collapse the whole structure. So, follow the drawing exactly. If something doesn't fit, don't force itreport to the engineer.

4. Steel Decking and Joists

Steel decking and joists form floors and roofs. In Africa, they are used in multi-storey buildings and industrial sheds. Steel decking is a corrugated sheet, usually 0.91.2 mm thick, galvanised. It sits on beams and is filled with concrete. Joists are open-web steel bars (like a mini truss) or rolled sections. For decking, start by cutting sheets to length with a nibbler or angle grinder. Overlap sheets by one corrugation and fasten with self-drilling screws (1214 x 20 mm) every 300 mm. At the ends, use shear studswelded through the deck to the beam. Use a stud welding gun. Studs are typically 19 mm diameter, 100 mm long. After welding, bend the stud 30 to testif it doesn't break, it's good. For joists, they are often prefabricated. You just need to cut and weld end plates. A common joist is the SJI (Steel Joist Institute) type. For a 6 m span, you might use a 200 mm deep joist. Place joists at 1.2 m centres. Weld or bolt them to the supporting beams. Use a spacer to keep them parallel. After installation, decking is laid and concrete poured. In hot African climates, ensure decking is not walked on before concreteit can buckle. Use temporary props every 2 m. Finally, check that all screws are tight and studs are welded. A good decking job saves concrete and speeds construction.

5. Erection and Field Assembly

Erection is where the structure goes up. In Africa, this is often done with a mobile crane or a chain hoist. Before erection, ensure all members are marked and delivered to site in order. Check the foundation boltsthey must be within 3 mm of the drawing. Use a template to set them. Start by erecting the first bay: two columns and a beam. Use a crane to lift columns, then bolt to base plates. Plumb the column with a spirit level and adjust with shims. Tighten anchor bolts to 200 Nm. Then lift the beam and connect. Use drift pins to align holes. Once the first bay is stable, add temporary guying (cables) to keep it plumb. Continue with adjacent bays. For trusses, assemble on the ground first, then lift as a unit. Use a spreader bar to prevent damage. Bolting: use impact wrenches for speed, but final torque with a torque wrench. For welded connections on site, use E7018 electrodes and preheat if cold. In remote areas, you might use a generator and a 300A welder. Safety: never stand under a suspended load. Use tag lines to control members. After erection, check alignment with a total station or plumb bob. Tighten all bolts after final alignment. Finally, apply touch-up paint to welds and scratches. A well-erected structure can be completed 30% faster than one with poor planning.

6. Case Studies of Structural Projects

Let's look at real African projects. Case 1: A warehouse in Nairobi, Kenya. Span 24 m, trusses at 6 m centres. Fabricated with locally available 100x100x10 angles. Welded with E6013. Erection took 3 weeks with a 20-ton crane. Total steel weight: 45 tonnes. Cost: $2,500 per tonne. Case 2: A pedestrian bridge in Kampala, Uganda. Main girders: welded plate girders, 12 m long, 800 mm deep. Flanges 300x20 mm, web 800x10 mm. Welded with E7018, preheated to 120C. Bolted connections on site. Challenge: transport to site was difficult, so they assembled in two halves and bolted mid-span. Case 3: A multi-storey office in Lagos, Nigeria. Used steel decking on beams. Decking 1.2 mm thick, studs 19 mm. Concrete 150 mm thick. Saved 20% on concrete compared to traditional slab. Case 4: A church in Accra, Ghana. Trusses 18 m span, made from recycled steel pipes. Welded with a generator welder. Despite limited resources, the structure stood strong. Lessons: Always use jigs for repetitive work. Pre-drill holes to avoid site problems. Protect steel from rust with primer and paint. In Africa, logistics and power supply are big challengesplan for backup generators. Also, train your team on safety. Many accidents happen from falls or falling loads. Use harnesses and hard hats. Finally, document everything: photos, measurements, and material certificates. This helps in future projects and for client approval.

<h2>Practical Reference: Fabrication of Structural Steel in African Markets</h2>

<p>Structural steel fabrication is one of the highest-value segments of Africa's metals and construction value chain. From the Lekki Deep Sea Port and Dangote Refinery in Nigeria to the Standard Gauge Railway stations in Kenya, the Noor Ouarzazate solar complex in Morocco, and the massive housing programmes in Egypt and South Africa, structural steel frames underpin nearly every large project on the continent. For fabricators, the commercial opportunity is not simply cutting and welding steel it is delivering certified, dimensionally accurate, erection-ready assemblies that keep main contractors on schedule and avoid costly site rework.</p>

<p>This Practical Reference module consolidates the six lessons of the Structural Steel Fabrication module into a working document. It is written for shop owners, foremen, estimators, and welders who need practical guidance on beam and column fabrication, truss and girder work, connection detailing, decking, erection, and the commercial lessons from African projects.</p>

<h3>1. Beam and Column Fabrication: From Mill Order to Marked Member</h3>

<p>Beams and columns are the backbone of any steel frame. In the African context, most hot-rolled sections arrive from South Africa (ArcelorMittal), Turkey, India, or China through ports such as Durban, Mombasa, Tema, Lagos/Apapa, and Alexandria. Fabricators must therefore plan for long lead times, currency exposure, and customs clearance. A beam fabrication workflow typically runs: receipt inspection straightening cutting to length coping and notching hole drilling or punching fit-up of stiffeners and end plates welding dimensional check surface preparation marking and dispatch.</p>

<p>For African fabricators, two commercial realities dominate. First, section availability is limited you often design around what is in stock or on the next vessel, not what the ideal drawing specifies. Second, shipping damage and mill tolerances must be checked on arrival, because returning material is expensive and slow. A documented receipt inspection protects both quality and your claim position.</p>

<h3>2. Truss and Girder Fabrication</h3>

<p>Trusses and plate girders are used for long-span roofs, industrial sheds, warehouses, and bridges. In Africa, these are common in mining infrastructure (DRC copper belt, Zambian copper, Ghanaian gold), agricultural processing plants, airport terminals, and sports stadia. Fabrication is more demanding than simple beam work because of cumulative dimensional tolerance, camber control, and the need for trial assembly on large spans.</p>

<p>Key commercial factors for African fabricators:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Jig-based assembly</strong> reduces rework and improves repeatability for repetitive trusses such as those used in warehouse roof systems.</li>
<li><strong>Camber</strong> must be set during fabrication, not corrected on site. Incorrect camber is one of the most common causes of rejected girders.</li>
<li><strong>Transport limits</strong> road regulations in Kenya, Nigeria, South Africa, and Egypt cap load widths and lengths. Many trusses must be spliced for transport and bolted on site.</li>
<li><strong>Galvanising and painting</strong> coastal and humid climates (Lagos, Mombasa, Durban, Dar es Salaam) demand hot-dip galvanising or high-build epoxy systems. This adds cost and lead time.</li>
</ul>

<h3>3. Connection Design and Detailing</h3>

<p>Connections are where most structural steel failures and most site disputes originate. Bolted connections dominate African site practice because welding on site is difficult to control, especially in remote locations. The fabricator's job is to translate engineer's design intent into shop drawings that can be built and erected efficiently.</p>

<p>Practical rules that save African fabricators money:</p>
<ul>
<li>Standardise bolt sizes and grades across a project typically M16, M20, M24 in grade 8.8.</li>
<li>Use shear end-plate and fin-plate connections wherever possible; they are faster to fabricate and erect.</li>
<li>Detail for wrench access a connection that cannot be tightened on site is a defect.</li>
<li>Allow for hole tolerances; oversized or slotted holes may be required for erection tolerance.</li>
<li>Specify surface treatment for contact surfaces when friction grip connections are required.</li>
</ul>

<h3>4. Steel Decking and Joists</h3>

<p>Steel decking and open-web joists are increasingly used in African commercial buildings, shopping malls, and multi-storey car parks because they reduce concrete volume, speed up construction, and lower foundation loads. Composite decking with shear studs is common in South Africa, Kenya, Nigeria, and Egypt.</p>

<p>Fabrication considerations include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Deck sheets are usually imported and must be ordered early; local roll-forming capacity exists in South Africa, Egypt, and Nigeria.</li>
<li>Joists are often fabricated locally from angle and rod a good entry-level product line for small fabricators.</li>
<li>Shear stud welding requires certified operators and daily pull tests.</li>
<li>Decking layout must consider propping requirements during concrete pouring.</li>
</ul>

<h3>5. Erection and Field Assembly</h3>

<p>Erection is where fabrication quality is proven. In Africa, erection is often constrained by crane availability, access roads, and weather (rainy seasons in West and Central Africa, wind in coastal areas). Good fabricators plan erection sequences with the main contractor before fabrication begins.</p>

<p>Practical erection planning points:</p>
<ul>
<li>Mark every member with a unique piece mark matching the erection drawing.</li>
<li>Supply a bolt kit per connection, not loose bolts.</li>
<li>Provide shim packs and packers for base plates.</li>
<li>Plan for temporary bracing and guy wires.</li>
<li>Confirm crane capacity and radius before lifting long girders.</li>
</ul>

<h3>6. Case Studies of African Structural Projects</h3>

<p>Three instructive African cases:</p>

<p><strong>Lekki Deep Sea Port, Nigeria.</strong> Large-scale structural steel for container handling and warehousing was fabricated partly locally and partly imported. The lesson: local fabrication capacity can capture significant scope when quality systems and delivery reliability are demonstrated.</p>

<p><strong>Noor Ouarzazate Solar Complex, Morocco.</strong> Structural steel for parabolic trough and tower assemblies required very tight tolerances and corrosion protection in desert conditions. The lesson: high-specification projects reward fabricators with certified welding and QA systems.</p>

<p><strong>Standard Gauge Railway, Kenya.</strong> Station structures and maintenance depots used structural steel fabricated in Kenya and China. The lesson: local content requirements create real opportunities for fabricators who can meet international standards.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #b00020; background:#fdecec; padding:12px; margin:20px 0;">
<strong style="color:#b00020;"> SAFETY WARNING CRITICAL</strong>
<p>Structural steel fabrication and erection involve fatal risks. In African shops and sites, the most common causes of serious injury and death are: falls from height during erection, crane and rigging failures, crushing during member handling, welding fume exposure in poorly ventilated shops, and electric shock from arc welding in wet conditions. Every fabricator must enforce: certified rigging and crane operators, daily inspection of slings and shackles, fall arrest systems above 2 metres, fume extraction or forced ventilation in welding bays, and correct PPE including welding helmets, gloves, and flame-retardant clothing. Do not allow untrained workers to weld structural connections. Non-compliance is not only dangerous it will disqualify you from major project tenders.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Confirm section availability and delivery lead time before quoting.</li>
<li>Perform documented receipt inspection of all incoming steel.</li>
<li>Verify welder qualifications to AWS D1.1 or equivalent before production.</li>
<li>Use jigs for repetitive trusses, girders, and columns.</li>
<li>Drill matched parts together to guarantee hole alignment.</li>
<li>Apply correct surface preparation and coating for the project environment.</li>
<li>Mark every member with a unique piece mark matching erection drawings.</li>
<li>Plan erection sequence, crane capacity, and temporary bracing with the main contractor before dispatch.</li>
</ol>
