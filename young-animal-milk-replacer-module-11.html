1. Lesson 1: Diagnosing Scours and Dehydration in Milk-Fed Young Stock

Scours is the number one killer of milk-fed young stock on African smallholdings, and the difference between a calf that lives and one that dies is usually how fast you recognise dehydration and act. This lesson gives you a practical diagnostic routine for piglets, calves, goat kids, lambs, puppies and kittens. Start with the skin tent test. Pinch the skin on the neck or shoulder blade and release. In a well hydrated calf the skin snaps back in under 2 seconds. If it stays tented for 2 to 6 seconds the animal is 5 to 8 percent dehydrated. If it stays tented longer than 6 seconds, or the eyes are sunken into the sockets, dehydration is 8 to 12 percent and the animal needs oral fluids within the hour. In puppies and kittens the skin tent is even faster to read because the skin is thinner; a tent lasting 3 seconds in a 1 kg puppy is serious. Next check the gums. Normal gums are pink and moist and refill in under 2 seconds after you press them with a finger. Pale, white or blue gums mean poor circulation and shock. Cold extremities, ears and feet that feel cold to the touch, mean the animal is close to collapse. For calves, a simple rule from Teagasc Ireland is that a scouring calf under 10 days old should be assumed to have rotavirus, coronavirus, E. coli or cryptosporidium until proven otherwise, and the treatment is the same: fluids, warmth and continued feeding. Do not starve a scouring calf. Research from Teagasc and from university extension services in the United States consistently shows that calves kept on milk during scours recover faster than calves whose milk is withdrawn, because the gut lining needs nutrients to repair. The old practice of taking milk away for 24 to 48 hours is wrong and increases death rate. Instead, feed the normal daily volume, split into smaller feeds. A 40 kg calf on 10 percent of body weight needs 4 litres per day. During scours, give that 4 litres as four feeds of 1 litre rather than two feeds of 2 litres. Mix the milk replacer slightly weaker for two days, at 110 grams of powder per litre instead of the normal 125 to 150 grams per litre, but never below 100 grams per litre because you then deliver too little energy. For oral rehydration, use a proper electrolyte solution, not homemade salt water. A standard recipe used across East Africa is 1 litre of clean warm water, 20 grams of glucose or 40 grams of ordinary sugar, 3.5 grams of salt and 2.5 grams of baking soda. Mix fresh each time and give it at 37 to 39 degrees Celsius, the same temperature as milk. Give a calf 2 litres of electrolyte between milk feeds, not instead of milk. For goat kids and lambs, give 200 to 300 millilitres of electrolyte three times a day. For piglets, dehydration is often secondary to chilling; warm the piglet first under a heat lamp at 30 to 32 degrees Celsius, then give 20 to 30 millilitres of electrolyte by stomach tube or bottle every 4 hours. Puppies and kittens with diarrhoea from milk replacer intolerance need 10 to 15 millilitres of electrolyte per kilogram body weight every 4 to 6 hours, and a switch to a lower lactose formula. Common mistakes in Africa include using dirty borehole water for mixing, mixing electrolyte and milk in the same bottle, and continuing to feed spoiled or curdled milk. If the milk replacer smells sour or has lumps after mixing, throw it away. In Kenya a 25 kg bag of calf milk replacer costs roughly 8,000 to 12,000 KES, and in Nigeria a 20 kg bag costs 45,000 to 70,000 NGN, so farmers try to stretch powder by using less than recommended. That is a false economy: under-mixed milk causes malnutrition and scours. Weigh the powder with a kitchen scale, not a cup, because a cup of powder can vary by 30 percent depending on how it is scooped. If the animal has bloody scours, a fever above 40 degrees Celsius, or has not improved after 24 hours of electrolyte and milk, call a veterinarian. Blood in the stool of a calf under 3 weeks old often means E. coli or salmonella and needs antibiotics, which only a vet should prescribe. Finally, record everything: time of feeding, volume, temperature, stool consistency and the animal's weight. A simple notebook kept by the calf pen will show you patterns, such as scours appearing every time the mixing water is too hot or the feeder is not cleaned. Clean all feeding equipment with hot water and detergent after every feed, and let it dry in the sun. Sunlight is a free disinfectant in most of Africa, and it works.

2. Lesson 2: Fixing Refusal to Drink and Poor Intake

When a calf, kid, lamb, piglet, puppy or kitten refuses milk, the cause is almost always one of six things: wrong temperature, wrong concentration, wrong smell or taste, illness, stress, or a bad feeding technique. This lesson walks through each cause with numbers so you can fix the problem the same day. Temperature is the most common culprit. Young animals are extremely sensitive to milk temperature. The correct range is 37 to 39 degrees Celsius for calves, goat kids and lambs, and 35 to 38 degrees Celsius for piglets, puppies and kittens. Milk below 30 degrees Celsius causes gut stasis and refusal; milk above 42 degrees Celsius can scald the mouth and oesophagus and cause ulcers. Use a thermometer, not your finger. A cheap digital thermometer costs 300 to 500 KES in Kenya or 2,000 to 3,500 NGN in Nigeria and pays for itself in one saved calf. If you have no thermometer, test by dripping a few drops on the inside of your wrist; it should feel neither hot nor cold, like body-temperature water. Concentration errors are the second cause. Standard calf milk replacer is mixed at 125 to 150 grams of powder per litre of water, which gives about 12.5 to 15 percent solids. If a farmer uses one cup of powder per 2 litres because powder is expensive, the calf gets weak milk, drinks slowly, and may refuse because the taste is watery. If the powder is over-concentrated, for example 200 grams per litre, the calf gets osmotic diarrhoea and then refuses because it feels ill. Always follow the bag. For goat kids and lambs, a common mixing rate is 150 to 200 grams per litre for the first week, then 200 grams per litre. For piglets, sow milk replacer is usually mixed at 100 to 150 grams per litre. For puppies and kittens, commercial bitch or queen milk replacers such as Royal Canin Babydog or Babycat milk are mixed at 1 scoop per 30 millilitres of water, roughly 150 grams per litre, and must be fed at 35 to 37 degrees Celsius. Smell and taste problems come from stale powder, dirty water, or a sudden change of brand. Milk replacer powder absorbs moisture and rancid fats quickly in humid African conditions. Once a bag is opened, use it within 4 weeks in dry conditions or 2 weeks in humid coastal conditions. Store it in an airtight container in a cool dark place, never in the sun and never on a concrete floor where it draws damp. If you must change brands, transition over 3 days by mixing 75 percent old with 25 percent new, then 50:50, then 25:75, then all new. Illness is the fourth cause. A calf with pneumonia or a navel infection will refuse milk before it shows other signs. Check the navel for swelling or pus, check breathing rate (normal calf is 20 to 40 breaths per minute), and check temperature (normal calf is 38.0 to 39.5 degrees Celsius). A kid or lamb with a temperature above 40.5 degrees Celsius is sick. A puppy or kitten that was drinking well and suddenly refuses may have hypothermia; warm it first to 37 degrees Celsius using a hot water bottle wrapped in a towel, then offer milk. Stress is the fifth cause. Newly arrived animals, animals that have been transported, dehorned, castrated or vaccinated may refuse for 12 to 24 hours. Do not force feed during this window; offer small amounts every 2 hours and keep the animal warm and quiet. The sixth cause is technique. Calves fed from a bucket must learn to drink; if the bucket is too high or the nipple is too hard, they refuse. Teagasc recommends teat buckets for calves under 2 weeks because the teat encourages natural sucking and saliva production. For goat kids and lambs, a bottle with a lamb teat works well. For piglets, use a shallow pan or a commercial piglet milk bar; piglets cannot suck a calf teat. For puppies and kittens, use a pet nursing bottle with a small hole; if the hole is too big the puppy aspirates milk into the lungs and gets pneumonia. Test the hole by filling the bottle with water and turning it upside down; it should drip about one drop per second, not pour. If an animal still refuses after you have corrected temperature, concentration, freshness and technique, try these steps in order. First, offer warm milk by syringe into the side of the mouth, 10 to 20 millilitres at a time for a small animal, letting the animal swallow between squirts. Never squirt into the throat; that causes choking. Second, try a different feeding time; some animals prefer dusk or early morning. Third, check for mouth ulcers or a sore tongue, which are common in calves with BVD or in kittens with calicivirus. Fourth, if refusal lasts more than 24 hours in a young animal, treat it as an emergency and call a vet. A calf that refuses milk for 24 hours can lose 5 percent of body weight and become dehydrated. Practical Africa-specific tips: in hot lowland areas, mix milk with cooler water and feed early morning and evening to avoid heat stress. In cold highland areas such as Kenya's Rift Valley or Ethiopia's highlands, warm the water to 40 degrees Celsius before mixing because cold water drops the mix temperature fast. In all cases, keep a feeding chart on the wall showing each animal's target volume as a percentage of body weight: calves 10 percent, goat kids and lambs 12 to 15 percent, piglets 20 to 25 percent in the first week, puppies and kittens 15 to 20 percent. A 10 kg goat kid therefore needs 1.2 to 1.5 litres per day, split into 3 or 4 feeds. A 5 kg lamb needs 600 to 750 millilitres per day. A 1 kg puppy needs 150 to 200 millilitres per day, split into 4 to 6 feeds. Write these numbers down and check them every week as the animal grows.

3. Lesson 3: Bloat, Abdominal Pain and Feeding-Related Digestive Upsets

Bloat and abdominal pain in milk-fed young stock are emergencies. A calf with bloat can die in 2 to 4 hours if the rumen or abomasum fills with gas and presses on the heart and lungs. This lesson explains how to recognise, prevent and treat the common digestive upsets linked to milk replacer and supplement feeding. Start with the difference between abomasal bloat and ruminal bloat. In calves under 3 weeks old, the abomasum is the working stomach and the rumen is small. Abomasal bloat happens when milk ferments in the abomasum, often because of overfeeding, cold milk, dirty equipment, or a milk replacer with too much starch or too fine a particle size. The calf's left side may look normal but the calf is dull, grinds its teeth, kicks at its belly, and breathes fast. Ruminal bloat is more common in calves over 3 weeks that are eating starter feed and hay. The left side behind the ribs balloons out and feels tight like a drum when you tap it. In goat kids and lambs, bloat is usually ruminal and follows sudden access to lush green forage or too much concentrate. In piglets, abdominal pain often shows as squealing, huddling, and a tucked-up belly; the cause is usually overfeeding or a cold creep area. In puppies and kittens, abdominal pain after milk replacer is usually from overfeeding, too rapid feeding, or lactose intolerance. The first action for any bloated animal is to stop all milk feeding for 4 to 6 hours, but keep offering warm water or electrolyte. For a calf with ruminal bloat, walk it slowly for 10 to 15 minutes to encourage gas to move, then massage the left flank with a firm circular motion for 5 minutes. If the calf is not improved in 30 minutes, call a vet; a stomach tube or trocar may be needed. Never try to pass a stomach tube yourself unless you have been trained, because you can put the tube into the trachea and kill the calf. For abomasal bloat, do not massage hard; instead keep the calf warm and call a vet immediately. Prevention is where you win. Feed calves at the same times each day, with no more than 2 litres per feed for a calf under 3 weeks, and no more than 2.5 litres per feed after that. Feed at 38 to 39 degrees Celsius every time. Mix milk replacer at the correct rate, usually 125 to 150 grams per litre, and mix it thoroughly with a whisk for at least 30 seconds so there are no lumps. Lumps sit in the abomasum and ferment. Use a milk replacer with a consistent particle size; Denkavit and other European manufacturers publish particle size data, and a coarse, uniform powder is less likely to cause abomasal bloat than a very fine dusty powder. In Africa, many farmers buy repacked milk replacer from local agrovets in Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, Nigeria and South Africa. Repacking can introduce moisture and bacteria. Buy sealed branded bags where possible, or if you buy from a bulk drum, use it within 2 weeks and keep the drum sealed. For goat kids and lambs, introduce concentrates gradually over 7 to 10 days, starting at 20 grams per day and increasing by 20 grams every 2 days until you reach 200 to 300 grams per day by 8 weeks. Never let a kid or lamb eat more than 300 grams of concentrate in one day before 10 weeks of age. For piglets, creep feed should be offered from day 7, and milk replacer should be fed in small amounts, 100 to 200 millilitres per piglet per day, split into 4 feeds, because piglets overeat and then scour. For puppies and kittens, feed small volumes every 3 to 4 hours for the first 2 weeks, then every 4 to 6 hours. A puppy's stomach holds about 20 to 30 millilitres per kilogram body weight per feed, so a 1 kg puppy should get no more than 25 millilitres per feed. If you feed more, the puppy will vomit and may aspirate. Common mistakes in Africa include feeding cold milk left over from the morning, using a bucket that was rinsed but not scrubbed, and mixing milk replacer with maize porridge or other starch to stretch the powder. Adding starch to milk replacer is a major cause of abomasal bloat and diarrhoea because young animals cannot digest starch well. If you want to reduce cost, feed less milk replacer and introduce a good quality calf starter or creep feed earlier, but do not adulterate the milk. Another common mistake is feeding once a day. Calves need at least two feeds per day, and young calves under 2 weeks do better on three or four feeds. A 40 kg calf on 4 litres per day should get 1 litre four times a day in the first week, then 2 litres twice a day from week 3. If you work away from the farm, use a teat bucket that allows the calf to drink slowly, or arrange for a family member to do the midday feed. Finally, watch for the early signs of pain: teeth grinding, kicking at the belly, lying with the head turned to the flank, and refusing to stand. If you see these signs, act within the hour. A simple pain and bloat kit on the farm should include a digital thermometer, a stethoscope, a bottle of simethicone or dimethicone anti-foaming agent if available, and the phone number of your vet. In Kenya, a vet call-out costs 1,500 to 3,000 KES; in Nigeria, 10,000 to 25,000 NGN. Preventing one bloat case pays for a year of careful feeding.

4. Lesson 4: Respiratory Problems and Poor Growth in Milk-Fed Young Stock

Respiratory disease and poor growth are the two quiet thieves of milk-fed young stock. A calf with pneumonia may still drink, so farmers miss it until the calf is stunted or dead. This lesson shows you how to spot respiratory problems early and how to fix the feeding and housing factors that cause poor growth. Start with the calf respiratory scoring system used by Teagasc and many university extension services. Score five things: temperature, cough, nasal discharge, eye or ear position, and breathing effort. Normal calf temperature is 38.0 to 39.5 degrees Celsius. A temperature above 39.5 degrees Celsius with no other signs may be early pneumonia. A single spontaneous cough is 1 point, repeated cough is 2 points, and coughing after exercise is 3 points. Clear nasal discharge is 1 point, cloudy or thick discharge is 2 points, and heavy pus-like discharge is 3 points. Normal ears are alert and forward; droopy ears or ears held back are 2 points. Normal eyes are bright; sunken eyes or discharge are 2 points. Normal breathing is 20 to 40 breaths per minute in a calf; 40 to 60 is 1 point, over 60 is 2 points, and breathing with the head extended or elbows out is 3 points. Add the scores. A total of 4 or more means the calf needs treatment now. In goat kids and lambs, normal breathing is 20 to 40 breaths per minute; in piglets, 30 to 50; in puppies and kittens, 15 to 40. Any young animal breathing with its mouth open, or with a blue tinge to the gums, is in crisis. The main causes of respiratory disease in milk-fed young stock are poor ventilation, high ammonia from dirty bedding, cold draughts, overcrowding, and mixing animals of different ages. In African smallholder sheds, the most common problem is a closed shed with no air movement. Ammonia from urine burns the airways. You can smell ammonia at 10 parts per million, and it becomes harmful to calves above 20 parts per million. If your eyes sting when you walk into the shed, the ammonia is too high. Fix it by opening the ridge of the roof, adding a vent, or simply removing the solid wall on the leeward side. Calves need 10 to 15 cubic metres of air space each. A 2 by 3 metre pen with a 2.5 metre high roof gives 15 cubic metres, enough for one calf but not two. Do not keep calves in the same air space as adult cattle; adults carry respiratory viruses that kill calves. For goat kids and lambs, the same rules apply: dry bedding, no draught at floor level, and 1 to 1.5 square metres per animal. For piglets, the creep area must be 30 to 32 degrees Celsius in the first week and dry; wet piglets get pneumonia and scours. For puppies and kittens, keep the box at 29 to 32 degrees Celsius in the first week, 26 to 29 degrees Celsius in the second, and 23 to 26 degrees Celsius after that, with no draughts. Poor growth is usually a feeding problem, not a disease problem. Weigh your animals every week. A healthy calf should gain 400 to 700 grams per day in the first 8 weeks. A goat kid should gain 100 to 200 grams per day. A lamb should gain 150 to 250 grams per day. A piglet should gain 200 to 300 grams per day. A puppy should gain 5 to 10 percent of its body weight per day in the first 2 weeks. If growth is below these numbers, check four things. First, is the milk replacer mixed at the right concentration? Use a scale. A 25 kg bag of calf milk replacer at 125 grams per litre makes 200 litres of milk. If you are getting 250 litres from a bag, you are underfeeding. Second, is the daily volume correct? Calves need 10 percent of body weight per day, goat kids and lambs 12 to 15 percent, piglets 20 to 25 percent, puppies and kittens 15 to 20 percent. A 35 kg calf needs 3.5 litres per day. If it is getting 2 litres, it will grow slowly. Third, is the milk replacer good quality? Look at the label. A good calf milk replacer has 20 to 28 percent protein, 15 to 20 percent fat, and skim milk or whey protein as the main source. Cheap powders with vegetable protein and high starch cause poor growth. In Kenya, good calf milk replacer costs 8,000 to 12,000 KES per 25 kg; in Nigeria, 45,000 to 70,000 NGN per 20 kg; in South Africa, 900 to 1,400 ZAR per 20 kg. If the price is much lower, the quality is likely poor. Fourth, is the animal eating starter feed? Calves should start nibbling calf starter at 3 to 5 days old and be eating 1 to 2 kg per day by 8 weeks. Goat kids and lambs should eat 200 to 300 grams per day by 8 weeks. Piglets should eat 300 to 500 grams per day by 4 weeks. Puppies and kittens should be on solid food by 3 to 4 weeks. If they are not eating starter, they will not grow well after weaning. Common mistakes in Africa include keeping milk replacer in a hot store where fats go rancid, feeding milk replacer that is past its use-by date, and using the same bucket for milk and for starter feed without washing. Rancid fat smells like old cooking oil; if you smell it, throw the bag away. Another mistake is weaning too early. Do not wean a calf before it is eating 1 kg of starter per day and is at least 8 weeks old. Do not wean a goat kid or lamb before 8 to 10 weeks. Do not wean a puppy or kitten before 6 to 8 weeks. Early weaning causes a growth check that costs more than the milk you saved. Finally, keep records. A simple chart on the shed wall with weekly weights, daily milk volume, and any coughing or droopy ears will let you see problems before they become disasters. If you see two or more animals with respiratory scores of 4 or more in one week, treat the whole group and review your ventilation immediately.

5. Lesson 5: Preventing and Managing Equipment, Hygiene and Milk Quality Problems

Most problems in milk-fed young stock come down to hygiene and milk quality. A clean feeder and correctly mixed milk prevent more disease than any medicine. This lesson gives you a practical hygiene routine for African smallholdings, with numbers and locally available materials. Start with water quality. Milk replacer is mostly water, so the water must be clean and safe. Use water that is fit for humans to drink. If you would not drink it, do not feed it to a calf. In many rural areas, borehole or river water carries E. coli, salmonella and cryptosporidium. If you cannot test the water, boil it and cool it to 40 degrees Celsius before mixing, or treat it with household bleach at 2 drops per litre, wait 30 minutes, then use it. Do not use chlorinated water directly with milk replacer if the chlorine smell is strong, because it can reduce intake; let it stand for 30 minutes first. Next, mixing hygiene. Wash your hands before mixing. Use a clean bucket that is used only for milk. Rinse with cold water first to remove milk residue, then wash with hot water and detergent, then rinse with clean water, then disinfect with a food-safe disinfectant or with boiling water. Let the bucket dry upside down in the sun. Sunlight kills many bacteria, and drying prevents biofilm. Do not use the same bucket for milk and for cleaning the shed. Do not use a cloth to dry the bucket; let it air dry. For feeding equipment, scrub teats and bottles with a bottle brush after every feed. Teats have small holes where milk collects and bacteria grow. Soak teats in a disinfectant solution for 5 minutes, then rinse and air dry. Replace rubber teats every 4 to 6 weeks because they crack and harbour bacteria. In Kenya, a calf teat costs 150 to 300 KES; in Nigeria, 1,000 to 2,000 NGN. Replacing them is cheaper than treating scours. For automatic feeders, clean the mixing bowl and delivery tube daily. For piglet milk bars, dismantle and clean every day; piglet milk residue ferments quickly in warm weather. For puppy and kitten bottles, boil the bottle and teat for 5 minutes before first use, then wash in hot soapy water after each feed. Milk quality is the next issue. Always check the bag before opening. Look for the manufacturing date and the use-by date. Do not use powder past its date. Once opened, store the powder in an airtight container in a cool, dark, dry place. In humid coastal areas such as Mombasa, Lagos or Durban, use opened powder within 2 weeks. In dry highland areas such as Nairobi, Addis Ababa or Harare, use within 4 weeks. Never store powder in the sun, in a hot car, or on a damp floor. If the powder is lumpy, smells sour, or has changed colour, throw it away. Do not try to use it by mixing it with fresh powder. Mix only what you need for one feed. Do not mix a day's supply in the morning and leave it standing. Bacteria double every 20 minutes in warm milk. If you must mix ahead, cool it to below 4 degrees Celsius and use within 12 hours, but on most African smallholdings without reliable refrigeration, mix fresh for each feed. Feed at 37 to 39 degrees Celsius for calves, goat kids and lambs, and 35 to 38 degrees Celsius for piglets, puppies and kittens. Use a thermometer. If milk is left over after a feed, throw it away. Do not keep it for the next feed. Do not add fresh milk to old milk. Common mistakes in Africa include using one bucket for milk and for water from the river, using a dirty cloth to wipe teats, mixing milk with hot water straight from the kettle without checking temperature, and buying repacked milk replacer from open drums at the market. Repacked powder from open drums is often contaminated with bacteria and moisture. If you must buy from a bulk drum, ask to see the original bag and the date, and buy from a supplier who sells quickly. Local suppliers in Kenya include agrovets in Nakuru, Eldoret and Kiambu; in Nigeria, feed stores in Ibadan, Kaduna and Abuja; in South Africa, cooperatives in the Free State and KwaZulu-Natal. Ask for the manufacturer's name and batch number. Good brands such as Denkavit, MaxCare NZ and Royal Canin print batch numbers and dates on the bag. If there is no date, do not buy. Record keeping is part of hygiene. Write down the date you open each bag, the batch number, and the date you finish it. If a problem appears, you can trace it to a batch. Also record cleaning times. A simple chart on the wall with columns for morning feed, midday feed, evening feed, and cleaning done will keep everyone honest. If you have workers, train them once a month on the routine. Show them how to use the thermometer, how to measure powder with a scale, and how to scrub teats. Pay them a small bonus for clean pens and healthy animals; it is cheaper than losing calves. Finally, remember that prevention is cheaper than treatment. A 25 kg bag of good calf milk replacer costs 8,000 to 12,000 KES in Kenya, 45,000 to 70,000 NGN in Nigeria, and 900 to 1,400 ZAR in South Africa. A vet visit and a course of antibiotics for one calf with pneumonia costs 2,000 to 5,000 KES in Kenya or 15,000 to 30,000 NGN in Nigeria, plus the lost growth. Clean water, clean buckets, correct mixing and correct temperature cost almost nothing extra and prevent most problems. Make hygiene a habit, not a reaction to disease.
