1. Shona Greetings and Business Introductions for African Professionals

Welcome to Module 3 of the Shona Language course. This lesson focuses on greetings and introductions in professional African settings. Shona is spoken by over 10 million people, primarily in Zimbabwe, but also in Mozambique, Zambia, and South Africa. As Africa's business landscape grows, knowing how to greet and introduce yourself in Shona can open doors in mining, agriculture, tourism, and cross-border trade. In this lesson, you will learn formal and informal greetings, how to introduce yourself and your company, and cultural nuances that build trust. Let us start with basic greetings. The most common greeting is 'Mhoro' (singular) or 'Mhoroi' (plural/respectful) meaning hello. For morning, say 'Mangwanani' (good morning). Afternoon: 'Masikati' (good afternoon). Evening: 'Manheru' (good evening). When meeting someone for the first time in a business context, use 'Mhoroi' with a handshake. In Zimbabwe, a soft clap followed by a handshake is common for elders or in rural areas. For business, a firm handshake with eye contact is acceptable in urban settings. Next, introductions. To ask someone's name: 'Zita rako ndiani?' (informal) or 'Zita renyu ndiani?' (formal). To respond: 'Zita rangu ndi...' (My name is...). For example, 'Zita rangu ndiTendai.' To ask where someone is from: 'Unobva kupi?' (informal) or 'Munobva kupi?' (formal). Respond: 'Ndinobva kuHarare' (I come from Harare). To ask about work: 'Unoita basa rei?' (What work do you do?) or 'Munoita basa rei?' (formal). Respond: 'Ndinoshanda ku...' (I work at...). For example, 'Ndinoshanda kuEconet' (I work at Econet). In business introductions, it is polite to ask about the other person's family and health before discussing business. 'Mhuri yako iri sei?' (How is your family?) or 'Muri sei?' (How are you?). Respond: 'Ndiripo' (I am fine) or 'Tiripo' (We are fine). If someone says 'Wakadini?' (How are you? informal), respond 'Ndiripo.' For formal: 'Makadini?' (How are you?) respond 'Ndiripo.' Now, let us practice a full business introduction. You meet a potential partner in Harare. You say: 'Mhoroi. Zita rangu ndiJohn. Ndinobva kuSouth Africa. Ndinoshanda kuABC Mining. Makadini?' They respond: 'Ndiripo. Zita rangu ndiTendai. Ndinobva kuBulawayo. Ndinoshanda kuZimplats. Makadini?' You: 'Ndiripo. Ndinofara kusangana nemi.' (I am happy to meet you.) They: 'Ndinofarawo.' (I am also happy.) Notice the use of 'mi' for formal you. In Shona, respect is shown by using plural forms for elders or superiors. For example, 'Mhoroi' instead of 'Mhoro', 'Makadini' instead of 'Wakadini', 'Munoita' instead of 'Unoita'. In business, always use formal forms until invited to use informal. Another key phrase: 'Ndapota' means please. 'Ndatenda' or 'Ndinotenda' means thank you. 'Mazvita' is also thank you (more informal). 'Fambai zvakanaka' means goodbye (to someone leaving). 'Sara zvakanaka' means goodbye (to someone staying). In a meeting, you might say 'Ndatenda nekusangana neni' (Thank you for meeting me). To ask for a business card: 'Ndipeiwo kadhi renyu?' (Please give me your card). To exchange contacts: 'Ndipeiwo nhamba yenyu yefoni' (Please give me your phone number). Cultural note: In Zimbabwe, it is common to ask about someone's clan or totem (mutupo) as a way to connect. For example, 'Mutupo wenyu ndiani?' (What is your totem?) This is more common in rural areas but can be used in business to build rapport. Also, Shona people often use honorifics like 'Baba' (father) for older men, 'Amai' (mother) for older women, 'Sekuru' (grandfather), 'Mbuya' (grandmother). In business, you might address a senior man as 'Baba' and a senior woman as 'Amai' to show respect. For example, 'Mangwanani, Baba. Makadini?' Now, let us look at a typical business meeting opening. The host might say: 'Tinofara kuti mauya' (We are happy that you have come). You respond: 'Ndatenda nekundida' (Thank you for loving me) or simply 'Ndatenda.' Then the host may offer tea: 'Munoda tii here?' (Do you want tea?) You respond: 'Hongu, ndinoda' (Yes, I want) or 'Aiwa, ndatenda' (No, thank you). During the meeting, use 'Ndapota' when asking for something. To agree: 'Ndinobvuma' (I agree). To disagree politely: 'Ndinoona zvakasiyana' (I see it differently). To ask a question: 'Ndipeiwo mukana wekubvunza' (Give me a chance to ask). At the end, say 'Ndatenda nekusangana neni. Fambai zvakanaka.' (Thank you for meeting me. Goodbye.) Practice these phrases with a colleague. Remember, in African business, relationships are paramount. Greetings are not just formalities; they are the foundation of trust. By using Shona greetings, you show respect for local culture and increase your chances of successful partnerships. In the next lesson, we will cover numbers and money, essential for negotiations. Before we finish, here are key vocabulary words: Mhoro (hello), Mangwanani (good morning), Masikati (good afternoon), Manheru (good evening), Zita (name), Basa (work), Mhuri (family), Ndatenda (thank you), Ndapota (please), Hongu (yes), Aiwa (no), Fambai zvakanaka (goodbye). Practice saying these aloud. Also, note that Shona has different dialects: Zezuru, Karanga, Manyika, Ndau, Korekore. The standard used in business is Zezuru, based on the Harare dialect. However, you may hear variations. For example, 'Mhoro' in Zezuru, 'Mhoro' in Karanga, but 'Mhoro' is widely understood. In Manyika, 'Mhoro' becomes 'Mhoro' as well. So you are safe with standard Shona. Now, let us do a role-play. Imagine you are at a trade fair in Bulawayo. You approach a booth. You: 'Mhoroi. Makadini?' They: 'Ndiripo. Makadiniwo?' You: 'Ndiripo. Zita rangu ndi... Ndinobva ku... Ndinoshanda ku...' They: 'Zita rangu ndi... Ndinobva ku... Ndinoshanda ku...' You: 'Ndinofara kusangana nemi.' They: 'Ndinofarawo.' You: 'Ndipeiwo kadhi renyu.' They: 'Heino.' (Here it is.) You: 'Ndatenda. Fambai zvakanaka.' They: 'Sara zvakanaka.' This simple exchange can lead to a business relationship. Remember to smile and maintain a relaxed posture. Shona culture values politeness and humility. Avoid being too direct or aggressive in initial meetings. Use indirect language when negotiating. For example, instead of saying 'Ndoda mari yakawanda' (I want a lot of money), say 'Tinogona here kuwana mutengo uri nani?' (Can we get a better price?) This shows respect. Also, learn to say 'Ndine urombo' (I am sorry) if you make a mistake. And 'Ndokumbirawo' (Please) is more polite than 'Ndapota' in some contexts. In conclusion, mastering Shona greetings and introductions is your first step to building successful business relationships in Zimbabwe and beyond. Practice daily, listen to native speakers, and don't be afraid to make mistakes. The effort is highly appreciated. In the next lesson, we will learn numbers and currency, which are crucial for pricing and negotiation. Until then, fambai zvakanaka.

2. Numbers, Money, and Negotiation in Shona for African Business

In this lesson, we dive into numbers, money, and negotiation phrases in Shona. Whether you are buying goods at Mbare Musika in Harare, negotiating a mining contract in Mutare, or setting up a joint venture in Gaborone, Botswana, where Shona is also spoken, numbers are essential. Let us start with basic numbers from one to ten. One: poshi or imwe. Two: piri or mbiri. Three: tatu or nhatu. Four: ina or china. Five: shanu. Six: tanhatu. Seven: nomwe. Eight: sere. Nine: pfumbamwe. Ten: gumi. For eleven to nineteen, combine gumi ne (ten and) with the unit. Eleven: gumi neposhi. Twelve: gumi nepiri. Thirteen: gumi netatu. Fourteen: gumi neina. Fifteen: gumi neshanu. Sixteen: gumi netanhatu. Seventeen: gumi nenomwe. Eighteen: gumi nesere. Nineteen: gumi nepfumbamwe. Twenty: makumi maviri. Thirty: makumi matatu. Forty: makumi mana. Fifty: makumi mashanu. Sixty: makumi matanhatu. Seventy: makumi manomwe. Eighty: makumi masere. Ninety: makumi mapfumbamwe. One hundred: zana. Two hundred: mazana maviri. One thousand: churu or chiuru. Two thousand: zviuru zviviri. One million: miriyoni. Now, money. The currency in Zimbabwe is the Zimbabwean dollar (ZWL), but US dollars are widely used. In business, you will often hear 'dhora' for dollar. 'Madhora' for dollars. For example, 'Madhora makumi mashanu' means fifty dollars. To ask the price: 'Imarii?' (How much?) or 'Mutengo wacho wakadii?' (What is the price?) To respond: 'Iri madhora makumi maviri' (It is twenty dollars). To negotiate, you can say: 'Ndipeiwo mutengo uri nani' (Give me a better price). Or 'Tinogona here kudzikisa mutengo?' (Can we reduce the price?) The seller might say: 'Kwete, mutengo wacho wakaderera' (No, the price is already low). You: 'Ndapota, ndipeiwo discount' (Please give me a discount). Or 'Ndipeiwowo vhiri' (Give me a little something extra). In Shona, 'vhiri' means a small extra. In business negotiations, it is common to bargain. Start with a lower offer. For example, if the price is 100 dollars, you might say: 'Ndinopa makumi masere' (I give eighty). The seller: 'Aiwa, makumi mapfumbamwe' (No, ninety). You: 'Makumi masere neshanu' (Eighty-five). Seller: 'Zvakanaka' (Okay). This is expected in informal markets, but in formal business, prices may be fixed. However, for bulk orders, you can still negotiate. Key phrases for negotiation: 'Tinogona kubvumirana' (We can agree). 'Ndine chivimbo' (I am confident). 'Ngatitaurirane' (Let us talk). 'Ndoda kuziva zvakawanda' (I want to know more). 'Ndipeiwo nguva yekufunga' (Give me time to think). 'Handisi kukurumidza' (I am not in a hurry). 'Tinoda chibvumirano chakanaka' (We want a good agreement). In African business, relationship building is key. Before negotiating, ask about the other person's family and health. Use the greetings from Lesson 1. Then, you can say: 'Ndauya kuzotaura nezvebhizimisi' (I have come to talk about business). To discuss payment terms: 'Munoda kubhadhara sei?' (How do you want to pay?) 'Ndapota, bhadhara neUS dollars' (Please pay in US dollars). 'Tinogona kubhadhara nechikwereti here?' (Can we pay on credit?) 'Ndinoda kubhadhara muchikamu' (I want to pay in installments). To talk about quantities: 'Ndinoda zviuru zvishanu' (I want five thousand). 'Ndipeiwo mazana maviri' (Give me two hundred). 'Ndine mari shoma' (I have little money). 'Mari yangu haisviki' (My money is not enough). In Zimbabwe, mobile money is popular. 'EcoCash' is a mobile money service. You can say: 'Ndinobhadhara neEcoCash' (I will pay with EcoCash). 'Ndipeiwo nhamba yako yeEcoCash' (Give me your EcoCash number). For bank transfers: 'Ndinobhadhara nebank' (I will pay by bank). 'Ndipeiwo account number' (Give me the account number). Now, let us practice a negotiation dialogue. You are buying 100 kg of maize from a farmer in Chinhoyi. You: 'Mhoroi. Makadini?' Farmer: 'Ndiripo. Makadiniwo?' You: 'Ndiripo. Ndinoda kutonga chibage.' (I want to buy maize.) Farmer: 'Chibage changu chakanaka. Imarii? Iri madhora makumi maviri pakirogiramu.' (My maize is good. How much? It is twenty dollars per kilogram.) You: 'Aiwa, inodhura. Ndinopa madhora gumi nemashanu.' (No, it is expensive. I give fifteen dollars.) Farmer: 'Kwete, madhora gumi nemasere.' (No, eighteen dollars.) You: 'Gumi nematanhatu.' (Sixteen.) Farmer: 'Zvakanaka, gumi nematanhatu.' (Okay, sixteen.) You: 'Ndatenda. Ndichabhadhara neEcoCash.' (Thank you. I will pay with EcoCash.) Farmer: 'Ndipeiwo nhamba yako.' (Give me your number.) You: 'Heino. Ndatenda.' (Here it is. Thank you.) This dialogue shows how to negotiate. Notice the use of 'Aiwa' for no, 'Zvakanaka' for okay. Also, 'inodhura' means it is expensive. 'Ndinopa' means I give. In formal business, you might use more polite forms: 'Ndingakwanisa here kuwana mutengo uri nani?' (Could I get a better price?) 'Ndingakumbirawo discount here?' (May I ask for a discount?) 'Tinogona kubvumirana pamutengo uyu here?' (Can we agree on this price?) For contracts, you might say: 'Ndoda kusaina chibvumirano' (I want to sign an agreement). 'Ndipeiwo kopi yechibvumirano' (Give me a copy of the agreement). 'Ndapota, verenga zviri muchibvumirano' (Please read what is in the agreement). 'Ndinoda kuziva miganhu' (I want to know the terms). 'Ndinoda kuziva nguva yekubhadhara' (I want to know the payment period). In African business, trust is built over time. Do not rush. Use numbers accurately. If you make a mistake, say 'Ndine urombo, ndakanganisa' (I am sorry, I made a mistake). Also, learn to count money: 'Mari iyi inonzi madhora makumi maviri' (This money is called twenty dollars). 'Ndipeiwo change' (Give me change). 'Mari yangu yapera' (My money is finished). In Zimbabwe, the informal sector is huge. You will often negotiate at markets. Use these phrases: 'Ndipeiwo mutengo wekupedzisira' (Give me your final price). 'Handina mari yakawanda' (I do not have a lot of money). 'Ndipeiwo mari yekufambisa' (Give me transport money). 'Ndinoda kutenga zvakawanda' (I want to buy in bulk). For bulk, you can ask: 'Ndipeiwo discount yekutenga zvakawanda' (Give me a bulk discount). In business meetings, you might present numbers: 'Tine vanhu makumi mashanu' (We have fifty people). 'Tinogadzira zviuru gumi' (We produce ten thousand). 'Tinoda madhora zviuru zviviri' (We need two thousand dollars). 'Mari yedu inosvika pamiriyoni' (Our money reaches a million). Practice these numbers daily. Write them down. Listen to Shona news on radio or YouTube to hear numbers in context. Also, learn the days of the week and months for scheduling. Days: Muvhuro (Monday), Chipiri (Tuesday), Chitatu (Wednesday), China (Thursday), Chishanu (Friday), Mugovera (Saturday), Svondo (Sunday). Months: Ndira (January), Kukadzi (February), Kurume (March), Kubvumbi (April), Chivabvu (May), Chikumi (June), Chikunguru (July), Nyamavhuvhu (August), Gunyana (September), Gumiguru (October), Mbudzi (November), Zvita (December). To schedule a meeting: 'Ngatisangane neMuvhuro' (Let us meet on Monday). 'Nguva yakanaka ndeye2 o'clock' (A good time is 2 o'clock). 'Ndichakusvika' (I will arrive). 'Ndine musangano' (I have a meeting). In conclusion, numbers and money are the backbone of business. Mastering them in Shona will give you a competitive edge in African markets. Practice with a friend, use flashcards, and immerse yourself. In the next lesson, we will cover common business vocabulary and phrases for meetings, emails, and phone calls. Until then, fambai zvakanaka.

3. Essential Shona Business Vocabulary and Phrases for Meetings and Communication

In this lesson, we focus on essential Shona business vocabulary and phrases for meetings, emails, and phone calls. Africa's business environment is diverse, and knowing how to communicate effectively in Shona can set you apart. Whether you are in Harare, Bulawayo, or Johannesburg, these phrases will help you navigate professional interactions. Let us start with meeting vocabulary. A meeting is 'musangano'. To have a meeting: 'kuva nemusangano'. To attend: 'kupinda musangano'. To schedule: 'kuronga musangano'. Agenda: 'zvinyorwa zvekusangana' or 'ajenda'. Minutes: 'maminitsi'. Chairman: 'sachigaro'. Secretary: 'munyori'. To start a meeting: 'Ngatitange musangano' (Let us start the meeting). To welcome: 'Tinokugamuchirai' (We welcome you). To introduce: 'Ndinoda kukuzivisai...' (I want to introduce you to...). To present: 'Ndichakuratidzai...' (I will show you...). To discuss: 'Ngatitaure nezve...' (Let us talk about...). To agree: 'Tinobvumirana' (We agree). To disagree: 'Hatibvumirani' (We do not agree). To postpone: 'Ngatiisei musangano kune imwe nguva' (Let us postpone the meeting to another time). To close: 'Ngatipedze musangano' (Let us end the meeting). Key phrases for phone calls. To answer: 'Hallo' or 'Mhoro'. To ask who is speaking: 'Ndiani ari kutaura?' (Who is speaking?). To respond: 'Ndini...' (It is me...). To ask for someone: 'Ndinoda kutaura na...' (I want to speak to...). To leave a message: 'Ndipeiwo meseji' (Give me a message). To say call back: 'Ndichakufonera' (I will call you). To ask for a number: 'Ndipeiwo nhamba yako' (Give me your number). To say the line is bad: 'Foni yakaipa' (The phone is bad). To say you cannot hear: 'Handisi kunzwa' (I am not hearing). To end: 'Ndatenda, ndichakufonera' (Thank you, I will call you). For emails, Shona is less common in formal business emails, but you can use Shona greetings and closings. Start with 'Mhoroi' or 'Mangwanani'. Then 'Ndinovimba makadini?' (I hope you are well?). Body: 'Ndiri kunyora kuzokumbira...' (I am writing to request...). 'Ndapota, ndiudzei...' (Please tell me...). 'Ndatenda nekupindura' (Thank you for responding). Close with 'Ndatenda' and your name. In business meetings, you may need to express opinions. 'Ndinofunga kuti...' (I think that...). 'Ndinoda kuwedzera...' (I want to add...). 'Ndinopikisa' (I object). 'Ndinotsigira' (I support). 'Ndinoda kuziva zvakawanda' (I want to know more). 'Ndipeiwo mukana' (Give me a chance). 'Ndapota, dzokororai' (Please repeat). 'Handina kunzwisisa' (I did not understand). 'Ndiudzei zvakare' (Tell me again). For presentations: 'Ndichakuratidzai mashandisirwo...' (I will show you how to use...). 'Heino mufananidzo' (Here is an example). 'Mubvunzo?' (Any questions?). 'Ndatenda nekunzwa' (Thank you for listening). Common business terms: Bhizimisi (business), Kambani (company), Chitoro (shop), Musika (market), Mari (money), Purofiti (profit), Kurasikirwa (loss), Mutero (tax), Chikwereti (loan), Bhangi (bank), Inishuwarenzi (insurance), Kontiraka (contract), Chibvumirano (agreement), Mubhadharo (payment), Muripo (fee), Mutengo (price), Discount (discount), Invoice (invoice), Receipt (risiti), Order (oda), Delivery (kuendesa), Transport (zvifambiso), Warehouse (imba yekuchengetera), Employee (mushandi), Employer (musikidzi), Manager (maneja), Director (director), Shareholder (mugovani), Partnership (kudyidzana), Investment (kudyara), Profit margin (mari inowanikwa), Cash flow (kuyerera kwemari), Budget (bhajeti), Forecast (fungidziro), Strategy (zvirongwa), Goal (chinangwa), Target (tarisiro), Performance (kuita), Productivity (kubudirira), Quality (hunhu), Customer (mutengi), Supplier (mutengesi), Competitor (mukwikwidzi), Market share (chikamu chemusika), Brand (chiratidzo), Marketing (kushambadzira), Advertising (kushambadza), Sales (kutengesa), Revenue (mari inopinda), Expenses (mari inobuda), Asset (pfuma), Liability (chikwereti), Equity (mari yakaenzana). Now, let us practice a meeting dialogue. You are chairing a meeting in Harare. You: 'Mangwanani, vana vechikwata. Ngatitange musangano. Tinoda kukurukura nezvebhajeti regore rinouya. Ndapota, munyori, verenga maminitsi ekupedzisira.' Secretary: 'Ndatenda. Maminitsi anoti...' You: 'Ndatenda. Ndinoda kuzwa pfungwa dzenyu. Baba Tendai, munofungei?' Tendai: 'Ndinofunga kuti tinoda kuwedzera mari yekushambadzira.' You: 'Zvakanaka. Amai Chipo, imi?' Chipo: 'Ndinopikisa. Tinofanira kudzikisa mari yekufambisa.' You: 'Ndinonzwa. Ngatitaure nezveizvi. Ndipeiwo mukana wekupfupisa. Tinoda kusvika pachibvumirano. Tinobvumirana here kuti tipe 10% kushambadzira uye 5% kufambisa?' All: 'Tinobvumirana.' You: 'Zvakanaka. Ngatipedze musangano. Ndatenda nekuuya.' This shows how to facilitate. Use polite forms. In phone calls, practice: You: 'Hallo. Ndini John. Ndinoda kutaura naTendai.' Receiver: 'Ndapota, mirira.' Tendai: 'Hallo, ndini Tendai.' You: 'Mhoro, Tendai. Makadini? Ndiri John kubva kuABC Mining. Ndinoda kusangana nemi nezvechibvumirano.' Tendai: 'Ndiripo. Zvakanaka. Ngatisangane neChipiri.' You: 'Nguva ipi?' Tendai: '2 o'clock.' You: 'Zvakanaka. Ndatenda. Ndichakufonera.' Tendai: 'Fambai zvakanaka.' For emails, write: 'Mhoroi Tendai, Ndinovimba makadini. Ndiri kunyora kuzokumbira musangano wekukurukura nezvemutengo wekutenga. Ndapota, ndiudzei kana Muvhuro wakanaka. Ndatenda, John.' This is simple but effective. In African business, building relationships is key. Use these phrases to show respect and professionalism. Also, learn to say 'Ndine urombo' (I am sorry) if you make a mistake. 'Ndokumbirawo' (Please) is more polite. 'Ndatenda' (Thank you) is essential. 'Fambai zvakanaka' (Goodbye) when leaving. 'Sara zvakanaka' (Goodbye) when staying. Practice these phrases daily. Role-play with a colleague. Listen to Shona business news on radio. The more you practice, the more confident you will become. In the next lesson, we will cover cultural etiquette and negotiation strategies in African business contexts. Until then, fambai zvakanaka.

4. Cultural Etiquette and Negotiation Strategies in Shona-Speaking African Business

Welcome to Lesson 4 of Module 3. In this lesson, we explore cultural etiquette and negotiation strategies in Shona-speaking African business environments. Understanding culture is as important as language. In Zimbabwe, Mozambique, Zambia, and South Africa, Shona speakers have specific customs that affect business interactions. Let us start with etiquette. When meeting someone, a handshake is common. For elders, you may clap softly before shaking. In rural areas, men may bow slightly. Women may curtsy or kneel. In urban business, a firm handshake with eye contact is fine. However, avoid prolonged eye contact with elders, as it may be seen as disrespectful. Use titles: 'Baba' for older men, 'Amai' for older women, 'Sekuru' for grandfather, 'Mbuya' for grandmother. For professionals, use 'Mr', 'Mrs', 'Dr' with the surname. In Shona, you can say 'VaMoyo' for Mr Moyo. 'Va' is a respectful prefix. For example, 'VaMoyo, makadini?' (Mr Moyo, how are you?). When entering a room, greet everyone before sitting. Do not start business immediately. Ask about family, health, and the journey. 'Mafamba sei?' (How was your journey?). 'Mhuri yako iri sei?' (How is your family?). This builds rapport. In meetings, the most senior person speaks first. Do not interrupt. Wait for your turn. Use indirect language. For example, instead of saying 'Ndoda kuti musaine izvozvi' (I want you to sign now), say 'Tinogona here kusaina mangwana?' (Can we sign tomorrow?). This shows respect. When receiving a business card, take it with both hands or right hand. Read it before putting it away. Do not write on it. When giving a card, present it with right hand. In Shona culture, the right hand is used for giving and receiving. The left hand is considered unclean. So always use your right hand or both hands. When eating, if invited to a meal, wait to be told where to sit. Wash hands before eating. Eat with your right hand if no utensils. Do not start eating until the eldest has started. Compliment the food: 'Kudya kwakanaka' (The food is good). After eating, say 'Ndatenda' (Thank you). In negotiations, patience is key. Shona culture values long-term relationships over quick deals. Do not rush. Use silence as a tool. If you are unsure, say 'Ndichafunga' (I will think about it). Do not say 'no' directly. Instead, say 'Ndichaona' (I will see) or 'Zvinogona kunetsa' (It might be difficult). This softens the refusal. When making an offer, start lower than your target. Be prepared to compromise. Use 'Ngatitaurirane' (Let us talk). 'Tinogona kubvumirana' (We can agree). 'Ndipeiwo mutengo wakanaka' (Give me a good price). 'Ndapota, wedzerawo' (Please add a little). In African business, trust is built through personal connections. You may be invited to a home or a beer garden. Accept if possible. Bring a small gift: sugar, tea, or something from your country. Do not bring alcohol unless you know they drink. In Zimbabwe, many people are Christian, so avoid business on Sundays. Also, respect fasting days if you are invited to a meal

<p>Poultry and livestock value chains are among the fastest-growing agribusiness segments in Shona-speaking Africa. Zimbabwe's smallholder poultry sector alone produces an estimated 6070 percent of national poultry meat and eggs, according to FAO and ILRI value chain studies. For agribusiness professionals, the ability to negotiate feed, day-old chicks, vaccines, and finished birds in Shona is not a courtesy it is a commercial capability. The tables below provide real reference figures you can use in budgeting, quoting, and field negotiations.</p>

<strong style="color:#b00020;font-size:16px;"> SAFETY WARNING ANIMAL HEALTH, VACCINES, AND CHEMICAL INPUTS</strong>
<p style="color:#7a0015;margin-top:10px;">Vaccines, antibiotics, and agrochemicals used in poultry and livestock production are health-critical inputs. Never purchase vaccines without confirming the cold chain from supplier to farm. Do not use antibiotics without a veterinary prescription, and always observe the withdrawal period before selling meat, eggs, or milk for human consumption. Store all chemicals and veterinary medicines in a locked cabinet away from children, food, and animal feed. In Shona, instruct staff clearly: <em>Chengetedza mushonga kure nevana uye kure nechikafu.</em> Misuse contributes to antimicrobial resistance (AMR), which WHO identifies as one of the top ten global public health threats. If you suspect a notifiable disease such as Newcastle disease or foot-and-mouth disease, contact your local veterinary officer immediately do not attempt to treat and sell affected animals.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul>
<li><strong>Confirm supplier credentials:</strong> Ask for the supplier's registration and veterinary product licence. In Shona: <em>Mune gwaro here rekunyoresa?</em></li>
<li><strong>Verify weights on delivery:</strong> Use a calibrated scale and record live weight or bag weight. <em>Ndiyere zvakare.</em></li>
<li><strong>Check cold chain:</strong> Touch-test vaccine boxes and ask for temperature logs. Reject any vaccine that is warm or has been exposed to sunlight.</li>
<li><strong>Read the label dosage:</strong> Confirm dosage in Shona and English before administration. <em>Chiyero ndechei?</em></li>
<li><strong>Record the withdrawal period:</strong> Write the date treatment ends and the earliest safe sale date. <em>Nguva yekumira mushonga inopera rinhi?</em></li>
<li><strong>Negotiate in writing:</strong> Confirm price, quantity, delivery date, and payment terms on paper or WhatsApp. <em>Nyora pasi kuti tione.</em></li>
<li><strong>Inspect biosecurity:</strong> Ensure footbaths, clean water, and visitor control are in place before receiving new stock.</li>
<li><strong>Report disease early:</strong> Contact veterinary services if mortality exceeds normal levels. <em>Ndizivise vet.</em></li>
</ul>
