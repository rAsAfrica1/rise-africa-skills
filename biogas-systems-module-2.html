1. The Four Stages of Anaerobic Digestion: Hydrolysis, Acidogenesis, Acetogenesis, Methanogenesis

Anaerobic digestion happens in four sequential stages, each carried out by different groups of bacteria. Understanding these stages helps you diagnose problems when your digester underperforms. In hydrolysis, complex organic matter (carbohydrates, proteins, fats) is broken down into simpler soluble molecules like sugars, amino acids, and fatty acids. This is the slowest step for tough materials like crop residues or straw. For example, maize stover may take weeks to hydrolyse, while cow dung hydrolyses in days. In acidogenesis, fermentative bacteria convert these simple molecules into volatile fatty acids (VFAs), alcohols, hydrogen, and carbon dioxide. This stage is fast and can cause acid buildup if the next stage lags. In acetogenesis, syntrophic bacteria convert VFAs (like propionate and butyrate) into acetic acid, hydrogen, and CO2. This step is sensitive to hydrogen partial pressure; if hydrogen accumulates, acetogenesis stops. Finally, methanogenesis uses two main pathways: acetoclastic methanogens convert acetic acid to methane and CO2, while hydrogenotrophic methanogens combine hydrogen and CO2 to form methane. Methanogens are slow-growing and sensitive to pH, temperature, and toxins. In a typical African digester using cattle manure, the entire process takes 20â40 days. If hydrolysis is slow, you can pre-treat feedstock by chopping or soaking. If acidogenesis outpaces methanogenesis, you get acidification. Keep all four stages balanced by maintaining stable temperature (30â37Â°C for mesophilic), pH 6.8â7.4, and a consistent feed rate. Monitor VFA/alkalinity ratio weekly; if it exceeds 0.4, reduce feeding. Remember: the four stages are a chainâweak links break the whole system.

2. Key Microbial Groups: Fermentative Bacteria, Syntrophs, Methanogens

Three main groups of microorganisms drive anaerobic digestion: fermentative bacteria, syntrophs, and methanogens. Each has different roles, growth rates, and sensitivities. Fermentative bacteria are the workhorses. They break down sugars, amino acids, and fats into VFAs, alcohols, hydrogen, and CO2. They grow fastâdoubling in 30 minutes to a few hoursâand tolerate a wide pH range (5.0â8.0). In a digester fed with cassava peels, fermentative bacteria quickly produce acids, which can drop pH if not buffered. Syntrophs are a special group that work in pairs with methanogens. They convert propionate and butyrate into acetate, hydrogen, and formate. Syntrophs are slow growers (doubling in 2â6 days) and are inhibited by high hydrogen levels. They depend on methanogens to consume hydrogen and keep its partial pressure low. If methanogens are stressed (e.g., by ammonia or temperature shock), hydrogen builds up, syntrophs stop, and VFAs accumulate. Methanogens are archaea, not bacteria. They are the slowest growers (doubling in 3â10 days) and the most sensitive. Acetoclastic methanogens (e.g., Methanosarcina) convert acetate to methane; hydrogenotrophic methanogens (e.g., Methanobacterium) use hydrogen and CO2. Methanogens require pH 6.8â7.4, temperatures 30â37Â°C (mesophilic) or 50â55Â°C (thermophilic), and trace metals like nickel and cobalt. In African digesters, ammonia from poultry litter can inhibit methanogens at total ammonia nitrogen above 3,000 mg/L. To keep all groups healthy, feed consistently, avoid sudden temperature changes, and add trace element supplements if needed. A balanced community means stable biogas production.

3. Factors Affecting Microbial Activity: Temperature, pH, Alkalinity

Three key factors control microbial activity in your digester: temperature, pH, and alkalinity. Get these right, and biogas production is steady. Temperature: Most African digesters run in the mesophilic range (30â37Â°C). Methanogens are very sensitive to fluctuationsâeven 2Â°C change can reduce biogas by 10â20%. In hot climates, digesters may overheat above 40Â°C, so shade them or use underground insulation. In cooler highlands, you may need a greenhouse or compost jacket to maintain 35Â°C. Never let temperature swing more than 1Â°C per day. pH: Optimal pH is 6.8â7.4. Below 6.6, acid-producing bacteria thrive while methanogens slow, causing acidification. Above 7.8, ammonia toxicity increases. Measure pH daily with a calibrated digital meter or pH strips. If pH drops, add lime or sodium bicarbonateâbut first check alkalinity. Alkalinity: This is the buffering capacity, mainly bicarbonate. It neutralises acids and keeps pH stable. For stable operation, alkalinity should be 2,000â5,000 mg/L as CaCO3. If alkalinity is below 1,000 mg/L, the digester is vulnerable to acid shock. You can raise alkalinity by adding agricultural lime (calcium carbonate) or wood ash. In a typical 6 mÂ³ fixed-dome digester in Kenya, adding 500 g of lime when pH drops to 6.7 can restore balance. Monitor the VFA/alkalinity ratio: if it exceeds 0.4, reduce feeding and add buffer. Remember, pH is a symptom; alkalinity is the cause. Test both weekly. Keep recordsâa simple notebook works. If you maintain temperature, pH, and alkalinity within range, your microbes will reward you with consistent methane.

4. Monitoring Microbial Health: VFA/Alkalinity Ratio, FOS/TAC

To keep your digester healthy, you need to monitor microbial activity before problems become visible. The two most practical tests are the VFA/alkalinity ratio and FOS/TAC. VFA/alkalinity ratio: This is the concentration of volatile fatty acids (VFAs) divided by total alkalinity, both measured in mg/L as CaCO3. A healthy digester has a ratio between 0.1 and 0.4. If it rises above 0.4, acid-producing bacteria are outpacing methanogensâearly warning of acidification. If it drops below 0.1, the digester may be underfed. You can measure VFAs with a simple titration kit (e.g., Hach or Hanna) or send samples to a lab. Alkalinity is measured by titrating with sulfuric acid to pH 4.3. For a small farm in Uganda, a weekly test using a $50 titration kit is enough. FOS/TAC: This is a German standard (FOS = volatile organic acids, TAC = total alkalinity). It is essentially the same as VFA/alkalinity but expressed as a ratio of acetic acid equivalent to CaCO3. Target FOS/TAC: 0.2â0.4 for stable operation. Above 0.5 indicates overloading; below 0.2 suggests underfeeding. Many African biogas programmes now use handheld FOS/TAC meters (e.g., from Bioprocess Control) that give results in 10 minutes. If you cannot afford a meter, use the simple titration method: add 0.1N sulfuric acid to 20 mL of digestate until pH 5.0, then continue to pH 4.3. The volume used between pH 5.0 and 4.3 correlates with VFAs. Record results weekly. If ratio is high, stop feeding for 2â3 days, mix the digester, and add lime or sodium bicarbonate. If ratio is low, increase feeding gradually. Consistent monitoring prevents crashes and keeps biogas flowing.

5. Troubleshooting Microbial Issues: Acidification, Ammonia Inhibition

Two common microbial problems in African biogas digesters are acidification and ammonia inhibition. Learn to spot them early and act fast. Acidification: This happens when acid-producing bacteria grow faster than methanogens, causing VFAs to accumulate and pH to drop below 6.6. Symptoms: sharp drop in biogas production, sour smell, pH below 6.6, VFA/alkalinity ratio above 0.5. Causes: overfeeding (especially with sugary or starchy waste like fruit peels or molasses), sudden temperature drop, or lack of buffering. Fix: Stop feeding immediately. Mix the digester gently to distribute alkalinity. Add lime (calcium carbonate) or sodium bicarbonate: for a 6 mÂ³ digester, add 1â2 kg of lime dissolved in water. Check pH daily. Resume feeding only when pH is above 6.8 and VFA/alkalinity ratio is below 0.4. To prevent, feed consistently and avoid shock loads. Ammonia inhibition: This occurs when total ammonia nitrogen (TAN) exceeds 3,000â4,000 mg/L, especially from poultry litter, pig manure, or urine. Symptoms: biogas production drops, pH rises above 7.8, and you smell ammonia. Methanogens are inhibited, but acidogens may still produce VFAs. Fix: Dilute the digester contents with water (add 20â30% fresh water), stop feeding high-nitrogen feedstock, and add a carbon-rich material like maize stover or sawdust to adjust the C/N ratio. You can also add zeolite or clay minerals to adsorb ammonia. In severe cases, you may need to empty part of the digester and refill with fresh manure and water. Prevention: Balance your feedstockâmix poultry litter with cattle manure or crop residues to keep C/N ratio between 20 and 30. Monitor TAN monthly if using high-nitrogen waste. Keep records. Early action saves your digester.

6. Lab Exercise: Measuring Biogas Potential (BMP Test)

A Biochemical Methane Potential (BMP) test measures how much methane a feedstock can produce. It helps you choose the best mix for your digester. Lab Exercise: Biochemical Methane Potential (BMP) Test

The BMP test measures how much methane a feedstock can produce per gram of volatile solids (VS). It is the standard tool for feedstock selection, co-digestion design, and bankable feasibility studies. A simple field-adapted protocol:

  1. Collect representative feedstock samples (e.g., cattle manure, food waste, market waste, poultry litter).
  2. Determine total solids (TS) and volatile solids (VS) by drying and ashing.
  3. Prepare 500 mL reactors with inoculum (digester sludge) and substrate at a VS ratio of 1:1 to 2:1.
