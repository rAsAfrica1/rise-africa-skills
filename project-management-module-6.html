Lessons

1. Quality Planning and Standards

Quality planning means deciding in advance what 'good' looks like for your project, and how you will check it. On an African construction site, for example, you might specify that concrete must reach a strength of 25 MPa after 28 days, or that a maize storage shed must keep grain below 13% moisture. These are measurable standards. Without them, quality is just opinion. Start by listing every deliverablea report, a borehole, a training sessionand for each one write one or two acceptance criteria. Use the SMART rule: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound. For a solar installation, a criterion could be 'System must provide 5 kWh per day for 95% of days in the first year.' Next, identify the standards you must follow. In Africa, this often means national bureau of standards (like KEBS in Kenya or SON in Nigeria), donor requirements (World Bank, AfDB), or industry codes (SANS in South Africa). For a small business, you might simply adopt ISO 9001 principles: document what you do, do what you document, prove it. Write a quality plana one-page table listing deliverable, standard, how you will check, who checks, and when. For example: 'Borehole yield: at least 1,000 litres/hour; check by pump test; checked by hydrogeologist; at completion.' Share this plan with your team and client. In many African projects, quality problems come from unclear expectationsa client expects tiles, you install terrazzo. A written plan prevents that. Finally, budget for quality. If you plan to test every batch of blocks, you need a testing machine or a lab fee. If you plan to inspect every bag of cement, you need a storekeeper's time. Quality planning is not paperwork; it is the cheapest way to avoid rework, which can eat 15-20% of a project budget. Do it before you break ground.

2. Cost of Quality

The cost of quality is not just the money you spend on testing. It is the total cost of making sure things are done rightand the cost of when they are not. There are four categories: prevention, appraisal, internal failure, and external failure. Prevention costs are what you spend to stop defects before they happen. Examples: training a mason on correct mortar mix, buying a concrete mixer instead of hand-mixing, writing clear specifications. Appraisal costs are what you spend to check quality: lab tests for soil compaction, a supervisors time inspecting rebar spacing, hiring a surveyor to verify levels. Internal failure costs are defects caught before the client sees them: re-doing a wall that is out of plumb, re-pouring a slab because the mix was wrong, re-training a worker who installed a solar panel at the wrong angle. External failure costs are the worst: defects the client finds. In Africa, this could be a building that cracks after handover, forcing you to repair at your own cost, plus reputational damage that loses you future contracts. Real numbers: a 2022 study in Kenya found that construction rework averages 5-10% of contract value. For a 10 million shilling project, that is 500,000 to 1 million shillings wasted. Prevention is cheaper. Spending 1% of budget on training and mock-ups can cut rework by half. For a small business, track these costs simply. Keep a notebook: 'Prevention: 50,000 for training. Appraisal: 30,000 for tests. Internal failure: 200,000 rework. External failure: 0.' After each project, review. If internal failure is high, invest more in prevention. If external failure appears, your appraisal missed something. The goal is not zero costthat is impossiblebut the lowest total. Often, the cheapest project is the one that spends a little more upfront on quality planning and checks.

3. Quality Assurance vs. Quality Control

People often use these terms interchangeably, but they are different. Quality assurance (QA) is about the process. Quality control (QC) is about the product. QA is proactive: you set up systems so mistakes are less likely. QC is reactive: you inspect the output and catch mistakes. In an African agribusiness project, QA might include writing a standard operating procedure for sorting tomatoes: 'Only red, firm tomatoes with no bruises go in Grade A.' You train workers on that procedure. QC is the actual inspection: you check a sample of 100 tomatoes and count how many are bruised. If more than 5 are bruised, you stop and fix the sorting line. Another example: a road project. QA: you specify that gravel must be compacted in 150mm layers, and you buy a roller with a working vibrator. QC: you test the compaction density every 200 metres using a sand replacement test. If it fails, you re-compact. QA prevents defects; QC detects them. In practice, you need both. QA without QC means you assume the process works, but you never verify. QC without QA means you are constantly firefighting, fixing defects after they happen. For a small project, QA can be as simple as a checklist for each task: 'Before pouring concrete: check formwork is oiled, rebar is tied, mix ratio is 1:2:4.' QC is the supervisor signing off after checking. A good rule: spend 70% of your quality effort on QA (training, procedures, good tools) and 30% on QC (inspections, tests). Many African projects do the oppositethey inspect heavily but never fix the process, so the same defects repeat. Track defects by type. If 60% of your defects are 'wrong mix ratio', that is a QA problemretrain the mixer operator. If 60% are 'cracked corners', that is a QC problemimprove handling. Use the data to decide.

4. Tools for Quality Control (Pareto, Fishbone, Control Charts)

Three simple tools can solve most quality problems. First, the Pareto chart. It is a bar graph that ranks problems from most to least frequent. The rule: 80% of defects come from 20% of causes. Example: on a poultry project, you record 100 egg cracks in a month. Causes: rough handling (60), poor nesting boxes (25), calcium deficiency (10), other (5). The Pareto chart shows rough handling is the biggest bar. Fix that firsttrain handlers, provide glovesand you cut cracks by 60%. Second, the fishbone diagram (also called Ishikawa). It helps you find root causes. Draw a horizontal line (the problem: 'late delivery of maize flour'). Add four to six diagonal bones: People, Methods, Machines, Materials, Measurement, Environment. For each, ask 'why?' until you reach a root cause. Example: Peopleuntrained drivers. Methodsno delivery schedule. Machinesone truck breaks down. Materialspackaging delays. Measurementno GPS tracking. Environmentrainy season roads. You then pick the most likely root cause and test it. Third, control charts. These track a process over time to see if it is stable. You plot a key measuree.g., moisture content of dried cassavaon a chart with an average line and upper/lower control limits (usually 3 standard deviations). If points stay within limits, the process is in control. If a point goes outside, or if seven points in a row trend up or down, investigate. For a small business, you can use a simple Excel sheet. Record daily moisture readings. Calculate average and limits. Plot. This tells you when to adjust the dryer. These three tools cost nothing but time. Use Pareto to prioritise, Fishbone to diagnose, and Control Charts to monitor. Together they turn quality from guesswork into a system.

5. Continuous Improvement in Projects

Continuous improvement means always looking for small ways to do betternot waiting for a big crisis. The Japanese call it kaizen. In an African context, it is about using what you have to make things a little faster, cheaper, or better each week. Start with a simple routine: every Friday, spend 30 minutes with your team. Ask three questions: What went well? What went wrong? What will we change next week? Write down one action. For example, a tailoring project: 'We wasted 2 metres of cloth because we cut patterns wrong. Next week, we will draw patterns on paper first.' That is continuous improvement. You can also use the PDCA cycle: Plan, Do, Check, Act. Plan a small change (e.g., move the cutting table closer to the sewing machines). Do it for one week. Check the result (time per garment dropped from 45 to 40 minutes). Actmake it permanent or adjust. Another tool is the '5 Whys'. When a problem occurs, ask 'why?' five times. Example: 'The client rejected the furniture.' Why? 'The drawers stick.' Why? 'The wood swelled.' Why? 'We used unseasoned timber.' Why? 'We bought from the cheapest supplier.' Why? 'We didn't specify moisture content.' Root cause: no specification. Fix: add moisture content to purchase order. Continuous improvement works best when everyone can suggest ideas. Give a small rewardeven a sodafor the best improvement each month. In many African projects, workers see problems daily but no one asks them. Ask. Also, measure before and after. If you change a process, record the old time or cost and the new one. A 5% improvement per month compounds to 80% over a year. Do not aim for perfection; aim for 1% better every week. That is how you beat competitors who stay the same.

6. Validating Deliverables and Acceptance Criteria

Validation means proving that what you delivered actually meets the agreed criteria. It is the final gate before the client accepts and pays. Without validation, you risk disputes and non-payment. Start with acceptance criteria written in the contract or quality plan. They must be testable. 'Good quality' is not testable. 'Compressive strength of 20 MPa at 28 days' is testable. For each deliverable, define: the test method, the sample size, who does it, and the pass/fail threshold. Example: a water pump installation. Criteria: flow rate at least 10 litres per minute, no leaks for 24 hours, pump runs on solar for 6 hours continuously. Test: bucket and stopwatch, visual inspection, timer. Who: clients engineer and your foreman. Pass: all three met. If any fail, you fix and retest. In Africa, many projects fail validation because the client was not involved. Invite the client to witness tests. Get signatures. Take photos and videos. For a training deliverable, criteria might be: 80% of participants pass a post-test, and 90% attend all sessions. Validate by marking tests and checking attendance sheets. For a software project, criteria: all 20 features work as described in the user manual, and no critical bugs after 48 hours of use. Validate by a user acceptance test (UAT) with real users. Always document. Create a simple acceptance certificate: project name, deliverable, test results, date, signatures. If the client refuses to sign, ask why. Often it is a missing criterion. Fix it or negotiate a change order. Do not hand over without validation. A handshake is not enough. In many African small businesses, the biggest loss is doing work that is not accepted. Validation protects you. It also builds trustclients see you care about results, not just finishing.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Quality Management in African Projects

In African project environmentswhether you are managing a road upgrade in Ghana, a solar mini-grid rollout in Kenya, or a cocoa processing line in Cte dIvoirequality is rarely just a technical checkbox. It is a commercial lever. Poor quality inflates rework costs, delays handover, damages client trust, and can disqualify your firm from future tenders. This practical reference consolidates the core quality management lessons into a working guide for project managers operating in African markets.

Quality Planning and Standards

Quality planning starts with defining what "acceptable" means for the client, the regulator, and the end user. In many African projects, three standards layers apply simultaneously:

  • International standards ISO 9001:2015 for quality management systems, ISO 21502:2020 for project management guidance, and sector-specific codes such as IEC 61215 for solar PV modules.
  • National standards e.g., Ghana Standards Authority (GSA), Kenya Bureau of Standards (KEBS), South African Bureau of Standards (SABS), Nigerian Industrial Standards (NIS).
  • Client and donor specifications AfDB, World Bank, DFID/FCDO, and private developers often impose stricter acceptance criteria than local law requires.

Best practice: build a Quality Management Plan (QMP) that maps each deliverable to its governing standard, the verification method, the responsible person, and the evidence required. This plan should be signed off during project initiation, not after the first defect appears.

Cost of Quality

Cost of Quality (CoQ) is the total cost of ensuring quality plus the cost of failing to achieve it. For African project managers, CoQ is a boardroom argument: prevention is cheaper than failure. Tools for Quality Control

Three tools deliver disproportionate value in African project settings where data is scarce and site conditions are variable:

  • Pareto Analysis Identify the 20% of defect causes responsible for 80% of rework. On a Kenyan road project, cracked culverts and poor compaction typically account for the majority of defects. Fix those first.
  • Fishbone (Ishikawa) Diagram Map causes across Man, Machine, Material, Method, Measurement, and Environment. In agribusiness, "Material" (seed quality) and "Environment" (rainfall, storage humidity) are frequent root causes.
  • Control Charts Track process stability over time. Use X-bar and R charts for concrete strength, moisture content in cocoa, or voltage output from solar arrays. A process in control is predictable; one out of control requires intervention.

Continuous Improvement in Projects

Continuous improvement in African projects should be lightweight and iterative. Adopt:

  • PDCA cycles (Plan-Do-Check-Act) at weekly site meetings.
  • Lessons-learned registers that feed the next project phasenot just the next project.
  • Kaizen events focused on one bottleneck (e.g., reducing cement wastage by 15% on a housing project).
  • Digital capture using mobile tools (Kobo Toolbox, ODK) for real-time defect logging where paper systems fail.

Validating Deliverables and Acceptance Criteria

Validation is not the same as verification. Verification confirms the deliverable meets specifications; validation confirms the client accepts it for its intended use. In African projects, acceptance criteria must be:

  • Written not verbal agreements at site meetings.
  • Measurable "smooth road surface" is not a criterion; "IRI 2.5 m/km" is.
  • Signed off by the client's authorized representative, with date and evidence attached.
  • Aligned to donor requirements AfDB and World Bank projects require specific handover documentation.
SAFETY WARNING Quality Failures Can Kill

In construction, energy, and agri-processing projects, quality failures are not just commercial lossesthey are safety hazards. A substandard solar installation can cause electrocution or fire. Poorly compacted road base leads to accidents. Contaminated food batches cause illness. Never sign off a deliverable that fails a safety-critical acceptance criterion, regardless of schedule pressure or client requests. Document the refusal in writing and escalate to the project sponsor. In many African jurisdictions, the project manager carries personal legal liability under occupational health and safety legislation (e.g., Kenya OSHA 2007, South Africa OHS Act 85 of 1993, Nigeria Factories Act).

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Quality Management Plan signed off before execution begins.
  2. Every deliverable mapped to a governing standard (ISO, national, or donor).
  3. Prevention budget allocatednot just inspection budget.
  4. QA and QC roles assigned to different people where possible.
  5. At least one control chart active for a critical process parameter.
  6. Pareto analysis updated monthly on defect data.
  7. Acceptance criteria written, measurable, and signed by client representative.
  8. Safety-critical criteria flagged and never waived under schedule pressure.
