1. Estrus Detection and Synchronization

Estrus, or heat, is when a sow or gilt is ready to mate. Missing it wastes feed, time, and money. In Africa, where many farmers keep local breeds, heat signs can be subtle, so you must check twice dailyearly morning and late eveningfor 15 minutes each. Key signs: swollen, reddened vulva; mucus discharge; restlessness; grunting; and the standing reflex. To test the standing reflex, apply pressure on the sow's back near the tail. If she stands still and cocks her ears, she is in standing heatthe best time to mate. Gilts (young females) cycle every 1824 days, sows every 21 days. Heat lasts 23 days. For synchronization, you can use a boar to stimulate heat in groups of gilts. If you want to batch-farrow, use hormones like PG600 (400800 IU) injected into gilts, but this requires a vet. Natural methods: mix new boars into the group, or move females to a new pen. In Africa, heat stress reduces conception, so provide shade and cool water. Record each heat date on a calendar or notebook. If a sow fails to show heat for 30 days, check for cysts or poor nutrition. Proper detection means you breed at the right time, saving money on feed and avoiding empty sows.

2. Mating Management and Artificial Insemination

Mating can be natural (boar to sow) or artificial insemination (AI). Natural mating: bring the sow to the boar's pen when she is in standing heat. A young boar can serve 23 times a week; a mature boar up to 6 times. Allow 1520 minutes per service. If the boar is too heavy, use a mating crate. In Africa, many farmers use local boars, but they may be related, causing inbreeding. Rotate boars every 1218 months. For AI, you need a trained technician, a semen catheter, and a dose of semen (80100 ml with 35 billion sperm). AI is cheaper if you have many sows, as one boar's semen can serve 2030 sows. Thaw frozen semen at 37C for 15 seconds. Insert the catheter into the cervix, then slowly deposit semen over 5 minutes. Do not push air. After mating, keep the sow calm for 10 minutes. Best time to mate: 1224 hours after standing heat begins. Mate twice, 1224 hours apart, to increase litter size. Record the date and boar used. If using AI, order semen from a reputable lab. In hot climates, semen quality drops, so keep it cool. Proper mating management gives 90% conception and 1012 piglets per litter.

3. Pregnancy Diagnosis and Farrowing Preparation

After mating, check for pregnancy. A sow that conceives will not show heat again in 21 days. That's the simplest test: watch for return to heat. If she doesn't, she's likely pregnant. For early detection, use an ultrasound scanner at 30 days. In Africa, where scanners are scarce, you can use a boar to test: if the sow refuses to stand for him, she's probably pregnant. Another sign: after 30 days, the vulva shrinks and becomes pale. By 60 days, the belly drops and teats enlarge. Prepare for farrowing (birth) 7 days before expected date. Clean and disinfect the farrowing pen. Use a broad-spectrum disinfectant like Virkon. Provide a farrowing crate or a corner with a rail to prevent crushing. Bed with dry straw or wood shavings. Reduce sow feed to 2 kg per day to prevent constipation. Wash the sow with warm water and mild soap, especially the udder and rear. Check for mastitis. Have on hand: iodine for navel, towels, gloves, and a heat lamp for piglets. In Africa, use a charcoal stove or lantern for warmth if electricity is unreliable. Keep the pen at 3032C for newborn piglets. Record the expected farrowing date (114 days after mating). If the sow shows no signs by day 118, call a vet. Good preparation saves piglets and keeps the sow healthy.

4. Farrowing Management and Assistance

Farrowing (birth) lasts 38 hours. Signs: restlessness, nesting, milk let-down, and a drop in body temperature. Be present or check every 30 minutes. Most piglets are born every 1520 minutes. If a sow strains for more than 30 minutes without a piglet, assist. Wash hands, use gloves and lubricant. Gently insert a clean hand into the birth canal to check for a stuck piglet. Pull only with contractions. After each piglet: clear the mouth and nose with a towel, dry the body, tie the umbilical cord 2 cm from the belly with a clean thread, and cut with sterilized scissors. Dip the navel in iodine. Put the piglet under a heat lamp (3032C) or in a warm box with straw. Ensure each piglet suckles within 30 minutes to get colostrum. If a sow has more than 12 piglets, rotate them: let the smaller ones nurse first. In Africa, keep the pen quiet and dark. If the sow is aggressive, use a farrowing crate. After farrowing, give the sow warm water and 1 kg of feed. Check for retained placenta: if not passed within 4 hours, call a vet. Record number born alive, stillborn, and mummified. Good assistance reduces piglet deaths from crushing and starvation.

5. Lactation and Sow Care

A lactating sow produces 510 liters of milk per day. She needs high-energy feed: 2 kg for maintenance plus 0.5 kg per piglet. So a sow with 10 piglets gets 7 kg per day. Feed a lactation diet with 1618% protein and plenty of clean watersows drink 2030 liters daily. In Africa, use locally available feed like maize, soybean meal, and fish meal. Add minerals and vitamins. Keep the farrowing pen clean and dry. Check udders daily for mastitis: hot, hard, or painful quarters. If infected, strip the quarter and call a vet. Piglets need iron: inject 200 mg iron dextran at 3 days old. Also clip needle teeth at 3 days to prevent udder damage. Castrate males at 710 days. Provide a creep feed area for piglets from 10 days. Keep the sow cool: shade, fans, or wet the floor. Heat stress reduces milk. Wean piglets at 2128 days. After weaning, reduce sow feed to 2 kg for 3 days to dry up milk. Then flush her with 34 kg for 7 days to bring her back into heat. Record weaning weight and sow condition. Good sow care means more piglets weaned and a shorter time to next litter.

6. Reproductive Efficiency Metrics

To know if your breeding program is working, track these numbers: conception rate, farrowing rate, litter size, and weaning rate. Conception rate = (number of sows pregnant / number mated) x 100. Aim for 8590%. Farrowing rate = (number of sows farrowing / number mated) x 100. Aim for 8085%. Litter size: born alive per litter. Good sows give 1012. Weaning rate = (piglets weaned / piglets born alive) x 100. Aim for 90%. Also track weaning-to-service interval: days from weaning to next mating. Should be 57 days. If longer, check nutrition or health. Farrowing interval: days between litters. Target 145150 days (114 days gestation + 2128 days lactation + 57 days to rebreed). In Africa, record everything in a notebook or phone app. Calculate feed cost per piglet weaned. If a sow weans 10 piglets and eats 500 kg feed per year, and feed costs $0.30/kg, then feed cost per piglet = $150/10 = $15. If piglet sells for $30, profit is $15. Use these metrics to cull poor sows: any sow with less than 8 piglets born alive for two litters should be culled. Replace with gilts from high-performing litters. Good records help you make money, not just keep pigs.

<h2>Practical Reference: Breeding and Reproduction in Pig Farming</h2>

<p>In African pig production, reproductive efficiency determines whether a farm is profitable or merely surviving. A sow that weans fewer than 18 piglets per year consumes feed, labour, and housing without returning a profit. This practical reference distils the essential field procedures for estrus detection, mating and artificial insemination (AI), pregnancy diagnosis, farrowing management, lactation care, and the key metrics that tell you whether your breeding herd is working. It is written for farm managers and extension officers who need to act, not just understand.</p>

<p>Across sub-Saharan Africa, smallholder and medium-scale piggeries typically achieve 1.82.2 litters per sow per year, with pre-weaning mortality often exceeding 25%. Commercial benchmarks are 2.32.4 litters and under 12% mortality. Closing that gap is the single fastest route to higher margins, because every additional piglet weaned carries almost no extra feed cost beyond the sow's maintenance.</p>

<h2>1. Estrus Detection and Synchronization</h2>

<p>Estrus (heat) lasts 4872 hours in gilts and 4060 hours in sows, with ovulation occurring about two-thirds of the way through. The standing reflex the sow freezing when pressure is applied to her back is the single most reliable indicator. Detect it twice daily, morning and evening, using a boar in a nose-to-nose fence line. A teaser boar 1015 metres away, moved closer, triggers the reflex within minutes.</p>

<p>Physical signs to score: swollen and reddened vulva, mucus discharge, restlessness, mounting pen-mates, reduced appetite, and erect ears. Record every observation. In African conditions where boars may be shared between farms, biosecurity during estrus checks is critical use disposable boots and wash hands between units.</p>

<p><strong>Synchronization</strong> is used to batch farrowings so that labour, farrowing crates, and piglet processing are concentrated. Common protocols:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>PG600 (PMSG/hCG):</strong> for gilts and weaned sows that are not cycling. Administer 1 dose (5 mL) intramuscularly.</li>
<li><strong>Altrenogest (Regumate):</strong> feed 1520 mg/sow/day for 1418 days, then withdraw; estrus follows in 46 days. Useful for gilts.</li>
<li><strong>Prostaglandin (cloprostenol):</strong> for aborting or inducing farrowing, not for estrus sync in cycling sows.</li>
</ul>

<p>In many African markets, PG600 and Regumate are expensive or unavailable. The cheapest synchronization tool is the boar: daily fence-line exposure of 1520 minutes after weaning will bring 8090% of sows into estrus within 7 days.</p>

<h2>2. Mating Management and Artificial Insemination</h2>

<p>Mating should be a planned event, not an accident. The golden rule is <strong>double service</strong>: serve the sow twice, 1224 hours apart, timed to the standing reflex. For gilts, serve on the first day of standing and again 12 hours later; for sows, serve 12 and 24 hours after standing begins. This covers the ovulation window and lifts conception rate above 90%.</p>

<p>Natural mating ratios: one boar to 2025 sows for hand mating, or 1:15 for pen mating. Boars must be rested maximum two services per week for young boars, three for mature boars. Overworked boars produce poor-quality semen and lose libido.</p>

<p><strong>Artificial insemination</strong> is increasingly viable in Africa where semen from superior genetics can be transported chilled (1518C) for up to 48 hours. The technique:</p>

<ol>
<li>Confirm standing reflex with boar contact.</li>
<li>Clean the vulva with a disposable wipe; never use disinfectant inside.</li>
<li>Insert the catheter at a 45 upward angle, then level out and rotate clockwise.</li>
<li>Attach the semen bottle and allow gravity flow do not squeeze.</li>
<li>Maintain boar contact during insemination; 35 minutes per dose.</li>
<li>Leave the sow undisturbed for 10 minutes after.</li>
</ol>

<strong style="color:#c0392b;"> SAFETY WARNING Boar Handling</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Boars are the most dangerous animal on a pig farm. Never enter a boar pen alone, never turn your back, and always use a solid board or hurdle. In African heat, boars can become agitated rapidly. Keep a clear escape route. Children and untrained workers must never handle boars. For AI, always secure the boar behind a sturdy fence before working with the sow.</p>

<h2>3. Pregnancy Diagnosis and Farrowing Preparation</h2>

<p>Early pregnancy diagnosis saves money: a sow that is not pregnant should be re-served or culled immediately, not fed for 114 days. Methods available in Africa:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Ultrasound (day 2128):</strong> most reliable; detects fluid and embryonic vesicles. Portable units now cost US$300600.</li>
<li><strong>Doppler / A-mode (day 30+):</strong> detects heartbeat or fluid; cheaper but less accurate early.</li>
<li><strong>Not-in-estrus observation (day 1824):</strong> free but only 7080% accurate.</li>
<li><strong>Vaginal biopsy / hormone tests:</strong> laboratory-based, rarely practical on-farm.</li>
</ul>

<p>Once pregnancy is confirmed, manage the sow in three phases: <strong>early gestation (day 030)</strong> 2.02.2 kg feed/day, avoid stress; <strong>mid gestation (day 3190)</strong> 2.22.5 kg/day, maintain body condition score 3; <strong>late gestation (day 91114)</strong> increase to 3.03.5 kg/day to support piglet growth and mammary development.</p>

<p>Farrowing preparation begins at day 110: wash and disinfect the crate, move the sow in, reduce feed to 2 kg/day, and provide clean bedding. Have on hand: iodine, clean towels, a heat lamp or heat box (3235C for piglets), and a farrowing record sheet.</p>

<h2>4. Farrowing Management and Assistance</h2>

<p>Farrowing takes 38 hours; piglets are born at 1520 minute intervals. Intervene only if the sow strains for more than 30 minutes without a piglet, or if a piglet is stuck. Wash hands, use lubricant, and gently assist never pull with force. After each piglet: clear the mouth and nose, dry with a towel, dip the navel in iodine, and place under the heat lamp.</p>

<p>Watch for <strong>stillbirths</strong> (common after prolonged farrowing) and <strong>mummified piglets</strong> (indicate infection during gestation). Retained placentas beyond 4 hours require veterinary attention. Provide the sow with 23 litres of warm water with electrolytes immediately after farrowing.</p>

<h2>5. Lactation and Sow Care</h2>

<p>A lactating sow produces 58 litres of milk per day and must be fed 57 kg/day of a 1618% protein diet, split into 23 meals. Water intake is 2030 litres/day a sow that cannot drink will not milk. Keep the farrowing pen at 1820C for the sow and 3034C for piglets; this temperature split is achieved with a heat lamp or creep box.</p>

<p>Monitor for <strong>mastitis-metritis-agalactia (MMA)</strong>: hot, hard udder, fever, and piglets that are hungry and restless. Treat with veterinary antibiotics and anti-inflammatories. Body condition score the sow at weaning target BCS 3 (ribs not visible but easily felt).</p>

<h2>6. Reproductive Efficiency Metrics</h2>

<p>Track these numbers monthly. They are the business dashboard of your breeding herd:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Farrowing rate:</strong> sows farrowed sows served 100. Target &gt;85%.</li>
<li><strong>Litters per sow per year (LSY):</strong> 365 (gestation + lactation + weaning-to-service). Target 2.3+.</li>
<li><strong>Piglets weaned per sow per year (PWSY):</strong> the ultimate profitability metric. Target 2226.</li>
<li><strong>Non-productive days (NPD):</strong> days a sow is neither pregnant nor lactating. Target &lt;40.</li>
<li><strong>Pre-weaning mortality:</strong> piglets dead before weaning born alive 100. Target &lt;12%.</li>
<li><strong>Culling rate:</strong> 3040% annually is normal; cull for repeat breeding, poor litter size, lameness, or age.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>
<ul>
<li> Check estrus twice daily with a boar; record standing reflex time.</li>
<li> Double-serve every sow 1224 hours apart; rest boars between services.</li>
<li> Confirm pregnancy by ultrasound at day 2128; re-serve or cull empty sows immediately.</li>
<li> Move sow to a clean, disinfected farrowing crate at day 110.</li>
<li> Attend farrowing; dry piglets, dip navels in iodine, provide heat at 3235C.</li>
<li> Feed lactating sows 57 kg/day and ensure 2030 L clean water daily.</li>
<li> Record all reproductive events; calculate LSY, PWSY, and NPD monthly.</li>
<li> Never handle a boar alone; always use a board and a clear escape route.</li>
</ul>
