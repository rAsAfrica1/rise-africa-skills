1. Estrus Detection and Synchronization

Estrus, or heat, is when a sow or gilt is ready to mate. Missing it wastes feed, time, and money. In Africa, where many farmers keep local breeds, heat signs can be subtle, so you must check twice dailyâearly morning and late eveningâfor 15 minutes each. Key signs: swollen, reddened vulva; mucus discharge; restlessness; grunting; and the standing reflex. To test the standing reflex, apply pressure on the sow's back near the tail. If she stands still and cocks her ears, she is in standing heatâthe best time to mate. Gilts (young females) cycle every 18â24 days, sows every 21 days. Heat lasts 2â3 days. For synchronization, you can use a boar to stimulate heat in groups of gilts. If you want to batch-farrow, use hormones like PG600 (400â800 IU) injected into gilts, but this requires a vet. Natural methods: mix new boars into the group, or move females to a new pen. In Africa, heat stress reduces conception, so provide shade and cool water. Record each heat date on a calendar or notebook. If a sow fails to show heat for 30 days, check for cysts or poor nutrition. Proper detection means you breed at the right time, saving money on feed and avoiding empty sows.

2. Mating Management and Artificial Insemination

Mating can be natural (boar to sow) or artificial insemination (AI). Natural mating: bring the sow to the boar's pen when she is in standing heat. A young boar can serve 2â3 times a week; a mature boar up to 6 times. Allow 15â20 minutes per service. If the boar is too heavy, use a mating crate. In Africa, many farmers use local boars, but they may be related, causing inbreeding. Rotate boars every 12â18 months. For AI, you need a trained technician, a semen catheter, and a dose of semen (80â100 ml with 3â5 billion sperm). AI is cheaper if you have many sows, as one boar's semen can serve 20â30 sows. Thaw frozen semen at 37Â°C for 15 seconds. Insert the catheter into the cervix, then slowly deposit semen over 5 minutes. Do not push air. After mating, keep the sow calm for 10 minutes. Best time to mate: 12â24 hours after standing heat begins. Mate twice, 12â24 hours apart, to increase litter size. Record the date and boar used. If using AI, order semen from a reputable lab. In hot climates, semen quality drops, so keep it cool. Proper mating management gives 90% conception and 10â12 piglets per litter.

3. Pregnancy Diagnosis and Farrowing Preparation

After mating, check for pregnancy. A sow that conceives will not show heat again in 21 days. That's the simplest test: watch for return to heat. If she doesn't, she's likely pregnant. For early detection, use an ultrasound scanner at 30 days. In Africa, where scanners are scarce, you can use a boar to test: if the sow refuses to stand for him, she's probably pregnant. Another sign: after 30 days, the vulva shrinks and becomes pale. By 60 days, the belly drops and teats enlarge. Prepare for farrowing (birth) 7 days before expected date. Clean and disinfect the farrowing pen. Use a broad-spectrum disinfectant like Virkon. Provide a farrowing crate or a corner with a rail to prevent crushing. Bed with dry straw or wood shavings. Reduce sow feed to 2 kg per day to prevent constipation. Wash the sow with warm water and mild soap, especially the udder and rear. Check for mastitis. Have on hand: iodine for navel, towels, gloves, and a heat lamp for piglets. In Africa, use a charcoal stove or lantern for warmth if electricity is unreliable. Keep the pen at 30â32Â°C for newborn piglets. Record the expected farrowing date (114 days after mating). If the sow shows no signs by day 118, call a vet. Good preparation saves piglets and keeps the sow healthy.

4. Farrowing Management and Assistance

Farrowing (birth) lasts 3â8 hours. Signs: restlessness, nesting, milk let-down, and a drop in body temperature. Be present or check every 30 minutes. Most piglets are born every 15â20 minutes. If a sow strains for more than 30 minutes without a piglet, assist. Wash hands, use gloves and lubricant. Gently insert a clean hand into the birth canal to check for a stuck piglet. Pull only with contractions. After each piglet: clear the mouth and nose with a towel, dry the body, tie the umbilical cord 2 cm from the belly with a clean thread, and cut with sterilized scissors. Dip the navel in iodine. Put the piglet under a heat lamp (30â32Â°C) or in a warm box with straw. Ensure each piglet suckles within 30 minutes to get colostrum. If a sow has more than 12 piglets, rotate them: let the smaller ones nurse first. In Africa, keep the pen quiet and dark. If the sow is aggressive, use a farrowing crate. After farrowing, give the sow warm water and 1 kg of feed. Check for retained placenta: if not passed within 4 hours, call a vet. Record number born alive, stillborn, and mummified. Good assistance reduces piglet deaths from crushing and starvation.

5. Lactation and Sow Care

A lactating sow produces 5â10 liters of milk per day. She needs high-energy feed: 2 kg for maintenance plus 0.5 kg per piglet. So a sow with 10 piglets gets 7 kg per day. Feed a lactation diet with 16â18% protein and plenty of clean waterâsows drink 20â30 liters daily. In Africa, use locally available feed like maize, soybean meal, and fish meal. Add minerals and vitamins. Keep the farrowing pen clean and dry. Check udders daily for mastitis: hot, hard, or painful quarters. If infected, strip the quarter and call a vet. Piglets need iron: inject 200 mg iron dextran at 3 days old. Also clip needle teeth at 3 days to prevent udder damage. Castrate males at 7â10 days. Provide a creep feed area for piglets from 10 days. Keep the sow cool: shade, fans, or wet the floor. Heat stress reduces milk. Wean piglets at 21â28 days. After weaning, reduce sow feed to 2 kg for 3 days to dry up milk. Then flush her with 3â4 kg for 7 days to bring her back into heat. Record weaning weight and sow condition. Good sow care means more piglets weaned and a shorter time to next litter.

6. Reproductive Efficiency Metrics

To know if your breeding program is working, track these numbers: conception rate, farrowing rate, litter size, and weaning rate. Conception rate = (number of sows pregnant / number mated) x 100. Aim for 85â90%. Farrowing rate = (number of sows farrowing / number mated) x 100. Aim for 80â85%. Litter size: born alive per litter. Good sows give 10â12. Weaning rate = (piglets weaned / piglets born alive) x 100. Aim for 90%. Also track weaning-to-service interval: days from weaning to next mating. Should be 5â7 days. If longer, check nutrition or health. Farrowing interval: days between litters. Target 145â150 days (114 days gestation + 21â28 days lactation + 5â7 days to rebreed). In Africa, record everything in a notebook or phone app. Calculate feed cost per piglet weaned. If a sow weans 10 piglets and eats 500 kg feed per year, and feed costs $0.30/kg, then feed cost per piglet = $150/10 = $15. If piglet sells for $30, profit is $15. Use these metrics to cull poor sows: any sow with less than 8 piglets born alive for two litters should be culled. Replace with gilts from high-performing litters. Good records help you make money, not just keep pigs.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Breeding and Reproduction in Pig Farming

In African pig production, reproductive efficiency determines whether a farm is profitable or merely surviving. A sow that weans fewer than 18 piglets per year consumes feed, labour, and housing without returning a profit. This practical reference distils the essential field procedures for estrus detection, mating and artificial insemination (AI), pregnancy diagnosis, farrowing management, lactation care, and the key metrics that tell you whether your breeding herd is working. It is written for farm managers and extension officers who need to act, not just understand.

Across sub-Saharan Africa, smallholder and medium-scale piggeries typically achieve 1.8â2.2 litters per sow per year, with pre-weaning mortality often exceeding 25%. Commercial benchmarks are 2.3â2.4 litters and under 12% mortality. Closing that gap is the single fastest route to higher margins, because every additional piglet weaned carries almost no extra feed cost beyond the sow's maintenance.

1. Estrus Detection and Synchronization

Estrus (heat) lasts 48â72 hours in gilts and 40â60 hours in sows, with ovulation occurring about two-thirds of the way through. The standing reflex â the sow freezing when pressure is applied to her back â is the single most reliable indicator. Detect it twice daily, morning and evening, using a boar in a nose-to-nose fence line. A teaser boar 10â15 metres away, moved closer, triggers the reflex within minutes.

Physical signs to score: swollen and reddened vulva, mucus discharge, restlessness, mounting pen-mates, reduced appetite, and erect ears. Record every observation. In African conditions where boars may be shared between farms, biosecurity during estrus checks is critical â use disposable boots and wash hands between units.

Synchronization is used to batch farrowings so that labour, farrowing crates, and piglet processing are concentrated. Common protocols:

  • PG600 (PMSG/hCG): for gilts and weaned sows that are not cycling. Administer 1 dose (5 mL) intramuscularly.
  • Altrenogest (Regumate): feed 15â20 mg/sow/day for 14â18 days, then withdraw; estrus follows in 4â6 days. Useful for gilts.
  • Prostaglandin (cloprostenol): for aborting or inducing farrowing, not for estrus sync in cycling sows.

In many African markets, PG600 and Regumate are expensive or unavailable. The cheapest synchronization tool is the boar: daily fence-line exposure of 15â20 minutes after weaning will bring 80â90% of sows into estrus within 7 days.

2. Mating Management and Artificial Insemination

Mating should be a planned event, not an accident. The golden rule is double service: serve the sow twice, 12â24 hours apart, timed to the standing reflex. For gilts, serve on the first day of standing and again 12 hours later; for sows, serve 12 and 24 hours after standing begins. This covers the ovulation window and lifts conception rate above 90%.

  4. Attach the semen bottle and allow gravity flow â do not squeeze.

4. Farrowing Management and Assistance

Farrowing takes 3â8 hours; piglets are born at 15â20 minute intervals. Intervene only if the sow strains for more than 30 minutes without a piglet, or if a piglet is stuck.
