General Agriculture

A complete 6-month program covering the fundamentals every farmer needs before specialising: soil science, crop rotation and intercropping, integrated pest management, irrigation basics, farm record-keeping, and a full farm business plan — the foundation course for every other rAs agriculture programme.

Course Overview

Why start here? Every specialised rAs farming course (maize, tomato, pig, rice, and more) assumes the fundamentals covered here: knowing your soil, planning a rotation, managing pests without wrecking your soil biology, using water wisely, and running the farm as a business with real numbers. Many smallholders skip straight to a crop or livestock course and then struggle with problems that trace back to gaps covered in this one.

Structure: 6 monthly modules → lessons → practical assignments → a 10-question quiz each module → final business project.

How to study (hybrid): Online — modules on your phone, sessions in the Study Diary, questions to the rAs AI assistant. Offline — save this page (Print → Save as PDF); the practicals happen on your own soil, in your own field.

⚠️ Read first: the three rules that protect your investment

  • Know your soil before you plant anything. Fertiliser, variety choice and irrigation decisions all depend on soil type and fertility — guessing wastes money and can damage soil that takes years to rebuild.
  • Never grow the same crop family on the same land season after season. Continuous mono-cropping builds up pests, diseases and depletes specific nutrients — rotation is the single cheapest form of pest and disease control available to any farmer.
  • Chemical inputs (fertiliser, pesticide, herbicide) are powerful tools that can also cause real harm if misused. Follow label rates and pre-harvest intervals exactly, protect yourself with the recommended gear, and store all chemicals locked away from children and food.
MONTH 1 — Understanding Your Soil ▾

Lesson 1.1 — Soil texture and structure

  • Soil texture (the mix of sand, silt and clay) determines how water and nutrients move through it: sandy soils drain fast but hold few nutrients; clay soils hold water and nutrients well but drain slowly and compact easily.
  • The simple hand "ribbon test" (moisten a small ball of soil and try to squeeze it into a ribbon between thumb and finger) gives a rough texture estimate anywhere, without a lab: sandy soil won't ribbon at all, clay ribbons long and smooth.
  • Soil structure — how particles clump into crumbs or compact into hard blocks — is heavily affected by management: organic matter, tillage timing and avoiding working wet soil all protect good structure.

Lesson 1.2 — Soil pH and fertility testing

  • Soil pH controls nutrient availability — most crops prefer roughly pH 6.0–7.0; outside this range, nutrients can be present in the soil but chemically locked away from plant roots.
  • A basic soil test (from an agricultural extension lab where accessible) reports pH plus major nutrient levels (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium) — worth doing once per field before major investment, and again every few years.
  • Where lab testing isn't accessible, simple pH test kits/strips and observing indicator weeds (certain weeds favour acidic or alkaline soils) give a useful rough picture.

Lesson 1.3 — Organic matter and soil life

  • Organic matter (decomposed plant/animal material) is the single biggest lever most smallholders can pull to improve poor soil — it improves water retention, nutrient supply, structure and feeds soil microbial life.
  • Compost, well-rotted manure, and crop residues returned to the field all build organic matter over time — burning crop residues destroys this resource and should generally be avoided.
  • Earthworm activity, visible soil crumb structure and a "earthy" (not sour or chemical) smell are simple signs of healthy, biologically active soil.

Lesson 1.4 — Erosion and soil conservation

  • Water erosion (rills, gullies, sheet erosion washing topsoil away) is one of the most damaging and hardest-to-reverse problems in farming — topsoil, once lost, takes centuries to rebuild naturally.
  • Contour ploughing, terracing on steep land, cover crops, and maintaining ground cover (mulch, crop residue, grass strips) all slow water movement and protect topsoil.
  • Wind erosion matters in dry, exposed areas — windbreaks (hedges, tree lines) and maintaining ground cover reduce topsoil loss to wind as well as water.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 1): (1) Run the ribbon test on soil from 3 spots in your field and record the estimated texture for each. (2) Get or estimate your soil pH and record the result. (3) Identify visible erosion risk on your land and note one conservation measure you could apply.

MONTH 2 — Crop Planning: Rotation & Intercropping ▾

Lesson 2.1 — Why rotate crops

  • Continuous mono-cropping (the same crop, same land, every season) builds up crop-specific pests and diseases in the soil and depletes the specific nutrients that crop uses most.
  • Rotation breaks pest and disease cycles by removing their preferred host for a season, and spreads nutrient demand across different crop types rather than exhausting the same ones repeatedly.
  • A basic 3–4 year rotation plan (e.g. legume → heavy feeder like maize → light feeder like a root crop → back to legume) is a practical starting template that suits most smallholder mixed farms.

Lesson 2.2 — Crop families and rotation groups

Family groupExamplesRotation role
LegumesBeans, groundnuts, soybean, cowpeasFix nitrogen — plant before a heavy feeder
Grasses/cerealsMaize, sorghum, millet, wheat, riceHeavy nitrogen feeders — benefit from following legumes
BrassicasCabbage, kale, rape, broccoliShare pests/diseases — avoid repeating or following other brassicas
NightshadesTomato, potato, pepper, eggplantShare soil-borne diseases (blight, wilt) — rotate away for 2–3 seasons
Root/tuber cropsCassava, sweet potato, onion, carrotGenerally lighter feeders — good rotation partner after cereals

Lesson 2.3 — Intercropping

  • Intercropping (growing two or more crops together in the same field at the same time) can improve total land productivity, suppress weeds through ground cover, and reduce pest pressure through diversity.
  • Classic combinations work because they complement each other: maize-bean intercropping (bean fixes nitrogen the maize uses, and climbs the maize stalk), or a tall crop with a shade-tolerant low crop underneath.
  • Avoid intercropping two crops that compete heavily for the same resource at the same time (two heavy feeders, two crops needing full sun at the same height) — poor combinations reduce yield of both.

Lesson 2.4 — Cover crops and green manure

  • Cover crops (fast-growing crops planted to cover bare soil between main crop seasons, not necessarily for harvest) protect soil from erosion and suppress weeds during fallow periods.
  • Green manure crops are cover crops deliberately grown then dug into the soil while still green — this returns organic matter and, if legume, fixed nitrogen directly into the soil.
  • Even a short 6–8 week cover crop between main seasons meaningfully improves the following crop's performance compared with leaving land bare.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 2): (1) Map your last 2 seasons' crops by field and identify any repeated mono-cropping. (2) Design a 3–4 year rotation plan for one field using the family-group table. (3) Plan one intercrop or cover-crop trial for your next season.

MONTH 3 — Integrated Pest & Disease Management ▾

Lesson 3.1 — What is IPM

  • Integrated Pest Management (IPM) combines multiple tactics — prevention, monitoring, biological control, cultural practice and chemical control only as a last resort — rather than relying on spraying alone.
  • IPM protects natural predators (spiders, ladybirds, parasitic wasps, birds) that do free pest control — blanket spraying kills these along with the target pest and often makes the next outbreak worse.
  • The core IPM question before any spray decision: is the pest population actually at a level that threatens yield (the "economic threshold"), or is it a minor presence better left to natural control?

Lesson 3.2 — Cultural and prevention practices

  • Rotation, resistant varieties, correct spacing (for airflow, reducing fungal disease), clean seed, field sanitation (removing crop residue that harbours pests/disease between seasons), and correct planting timing all reduce pest and disease pressure before any spray is needed.
  • Scouting regularly (walking the field and checking a set number of plants per area, weekly) catches problems early when cheaper, less invasive control still works.
  • Trap crops (a preferred host planted to draw pests away from the main crop) and companion planting (certain plant combinations that repel or confuse pests) are low-cost preventive tools worth testing on a small scale.

Lesson 3.3 — Biological and low-toxicity control

  • Biological control uses natural enemies (predatory insects, parasitic wasps, beneficial fungi/bacteria) either by conserving those already present or introducing them where locally approved.
  • Botanical and low-toxicity options (neem extract, soap sprays for soft-bodied insects, diatomaceous earth for crawling pests) offer a middle ground between doing nothing and reaching for a synthetic pesticide.
  • These options generally have shorter pre-harvest intervals and lower risk to beneficial insects and the farmer applying them, but still require correct identification of the pest to be effective.

Lesson 3.4 — Safe and correct chemical use (when needed)

  • Correctly identify the pest or disease before choosing a product — the wrong product wastes money and can worsen the problem by killing beneficial insects while leaving the actual pest untouched.
  • Always follow label rate, timing and pre-harvest interval exactly — using more than the label rate does not work better and increases residue risk and cost; using less can promote pest resistance.
  • Wear the recommended protective equipment, avoid spraying in wind or before rain, and store all chemicals locked away from children, food and water sources.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 3): (1) Scout one field weekly for 3 weeks and record pest/disease presence per plant sampled. (2) Identify one cultural practice (rotation, spacing, sanitation) you can improve on your farm. (3) If a chemical application is needed, record product, rate, date and pre-harvest interval correctly.

MONTH 4 — Water & Irrigation Basics ▾

Lesson 4.1 — Understanding crop water needs

  • Crop water demand varies by growth stage — most crops are most drought-sensitive during flowering and early fruit/grain fill, not during early vegetative growth or late maturity.
  • Signs of water stress (wilting during the heat of the day but recovering by evening is normal; wilting that persists into morning signals real deficit) help you judge irrigation timing without instruments.
  • Overwatering is as damaging as underwatering — waterlogged roots suffocate and rot, and nutrients (especially nitrogen) leach away in excess water.

Lesson 4.2 — Irrigation methods compared

MethodWater efficiencyBest suited to
Flood/furrowLow–moderateRice, level fields, cheap to set up
SprinklerModerateField crops, pasture, larger areas
DripHigh (90%+)Vegetables, orchards, high-value crops; higher upfront cost
Bucket/watering canVariable, labour-intensiveSmall nursery/garden scale, no capital needed

Lesson 4.3 — Water sources and harvesting

  • Assess your available water source (borehole, river, dam, rainwater harvesting) for reliability across the whole growing season, not just at planting time — many crop failures trace back to a water source that looked adequate early on but failed mid-season.
  • Rainwater harvesting (roof catchment into tanks, or small farm dams/pans capturing runoff) is a low-cost way to extend water availability into dry spells, worth planning even on rainfed farms.
  • Mulching dramatically reduces evaporation from soil surface — on many crops, mulch alone can cut irrigation frequency needed by a third or more.

Lesson 4.4 — Basic irrigation scheduling

  • Water deeply and less frequently rather than shallow and often — deep watering encourages deep root growth, which makes the crop more resilient to short dry spells.
  • Irrigate early morning or late afternoon to reduce evaporation loss and disease risk from wet foliage sitting overnight; avoid midday irrigation in hot climates.
  • Keep a simple irrigation log (date, duration, method) alongside rainfall — this is the data that lets you fine-tune scheduling season over season instead of repeating guesswork.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 4): (1) Assess your water source reliability across a full season and identify any gap risk. (2) Compare two irrigation methods for your main crop on cost and water efficiency. (3) Start an irrigation log for 4 weeks alongside rainfall records.

MONTH 5 — Farm Tools, Mechanization & Labour ▾

Lesson 5.1 — Matching tools to your scale

  • Hand tools (hoe, machete, planting stick) suit very small plots and low capital; animal traction (ox-plough) suits medium plots where draft animals are affordable and available; tractors/mechanisation suit larger scale where the investment can be justified by area farmed.
  • Hiring mechanisation services (a tractor/plough owner who works multiple farms) is often more cost-effective than owning equipment for a smallholder — compare hire cost per hectare against ownership cost including maintenance and idle time.
  • Match the tool to the task correctly — using undersized equipment for the job takes longer and often does the job poorly (shallow tillage, uneven planting depth).

Lesson 5.2 — Tool maintenance

  • Clean and dry tools after use, sharpen blades regularly, and oil moving metal parts — a well-maintained hand tool lasts years longer and works far more efficiently than a neglected one.
  • For animal traction equipment, check yokes and harnesses for wear that could injure the animal, and maintain plough shares (sharpening or replacing) for effective, fuel/labour-efficient tillage.
  • Store tools under cover, off damp ground — rust and rot from poor storage is a completely avoidable cost.

Lesson 5.3 — Planning and managing labour

  • Map your farm's labour calendar — land prep, planting, weeding, harvesting all have peak labour windows that don't move; running short-handed at a peak window (e.g. weeding at the critical stage) costs far more in lost yield than the wage saved.
  • Where hired labour is used, agree tasks, rates and timing clearly in advance, and keep simple records — this avoids disputes and helps you cost your true labour expense accurately at season end.
  • Consider labour-sharing arrangements with neighbouring farmers (rotating work groups) for peak tasks like harvest — a traditional practice that reduces the cost of short, intense labour peaks.

Lesson 5.4 — Post-harvest handling basics

  • Post-harvest losses (spoilage, pest damage, poor drying, rough handling) commonly destroy 10–30% of a smallholder's crop value after all the work of growing it — this is often the cheapest yield gain available, because it needs better practice, not more inputs.
  • Basic principles apply across most crops: harvest at correct maturity, handle gently to avoid bruising/cracking, dry to safe storage moisture, and store off the ground in a dry, pest-proof, ventilated space.
  • Grade produce before sale (removing damaged, diseased or undersized items) — uniform, clean produce consistently earns a better price than an ungraded mixed lot.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 5): (1) List your farm's tools and identify any needing maintenance or replacement. (2) Build a labour calendar marking peak-demand windows for your main crops. (3) Estimate your post-harvest loss rate on your last harvest and identify one improvement.

MODULE 6 — Farm Records, Marketing & the Business Plan ▾

Lesson 6.1 — Why keep farm records

  • Most smallholder farms carry accurate knowledge only in the farmer's memory — records turn that knowledge into something that can be checked, improved, and shown to a lender, buyer or partner.
  • The minimum useful set: an input log (what you bought, cost, date), a field log (what was planted where, when), a harvest log (yield by field), and a sales log (what sold, to whom, at what price).
  • Records reveal patterns invisible in a single season — which field consistently underperforms, which input actually paid for itself, which buyer pays reliably and which doesn't.

Lesson 6.2 — Basic farm costing

  • Cost of production = all inputs + labour (including your own, valued fairly) + a share of equipment/land costs — many farmers only count cash spent and miss their own unpaid labour, which distorts whether a crop is actually profitable.
  • Compare cost of production per unit (per kg, per bag) against market price to know your real margin — a crop can have high total revenue and still be a loss-maker per unit if costs are high.
  • Track cost trends season to season — rising input costs without matching yield or price gains is an early warning sign worth acting on before it becomes a crisis.

Lesson 6.3 — Marketing and selling well

  • Understand your market channels: farm-gate sale to traders (lowest price, least effort), local market sale (better price, more time/transport), and direct-to-buyer or contract arrangements (best price, requires reliability and sometimes volume).
  • Timing matters — prices are typically lowest at harvest peak when everyone sells at once; storage (where feasible and the crop stores well) to sell later in the season can capture a real premium.
  • Group marketing with neighbouring farmers (pooling produce to meet buyer volume requirements, sharing transport cost) often accesses better prices and buyers than any one smallholder could reach alone.

Lesson 6.4 — Final project: your farm business plan

  • Assemble: soil assessment and rotation plan, IPM approach for your main crops/livestock, water source and irrigation plan, tools/mechanisation and labour plan, a full cost-of-production worksheet for your main enterprise, your marketing channel plan, and a simple 3-season farm growth path.
  • Submit with your Month 1–5 records and photos for your rAs General Agriculture certificate — this document becomes the foundation you carry into any specialised rAs course you take next.

📖 Glossary

  • Soil texture — the proportion of sand, silt and clay in soil, affecting drainage and nutrient retention.
  • Rotation — changing which crop family is grown on a field each season to break pest/disease cycles and balance nutrient use.
  • Intercropping — growing two or more crops together in the same field at the same time.
  • IPM (Integrated Pest Management) — combining prevention, monitoring, biological and cultural control, with chemicals as a last resort.
  • Economic threshold — the pest population level at which control action is actually justified by yield risk.
  • Pre-harvest interval — the required waiting time between a chemical application and safe harvest.
  • Cost of production — total input, labour and overhead cost to produce a crop, used to judge true profitability per unit.
  • Green manure — a cover crop dug into the soil while still green to add organic matter and nutrients.
