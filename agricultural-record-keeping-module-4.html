Lessons

1. Types of Farm Inventory

Farm inventory is everything you own or use on the farm that has value. It falls into two main groups: consumables and durable assets. Consumables are items you use up each season, like seeds, fertilizer, chemicals, feed, and fuel. Durable assets last more than one year, like tractors, ploughs, water pumps, buildings, and livestock. You need to record both types separately because they are handled differently in your records.

Start by walking around your farm and listing every item you can see. For consumables, note the quantity and unit. For example: 5 bags of maize seed (each 25 kg), 10 bags of NPK fertilizer (each 50 kg), 2 litres of glyphosate, 3 bales of hay. For durable assets, note the item, its age, and its condition. For example: 1 water pump, 3 years old, working; 2 ox ploughs, 5 years old, one needs repair.

Livestock is a special type of inventory. You should record animals by type, age, and sex. For example: 10 cows (8 female, 2 male), 5 goats (all female), 20 chickens. Update this record whenever animals are born, sold, or die. In many African farms, livestock is the biggest asset, so accurate counts matter for loans and insurance.

Also include stores and supplies: veterinary drugs, fencing wire, ropes, jerrycans, and spare parts. Even small tools like hoes and machetes are assets. Keep a simple notebook or use a smartphone app like Farm Records or Excel. Write the date, item, quantity, and value. Review your inventory list at the start and end of every season. This helps you know what you have, what you need to buy, and what has been lost or stolen. Without this record, you cannot plan or apply for credit.

2. Physical Count and Valuation Methods

A physical count means actually counting every item in your store or on your farm. Do not guess. For consumables, count bags, bottles, or units. For example, count the exact number of fertilizer bags on the pallet. For livestock, walk through the herd and count each animal. For durable assets, list each tractor, pump, or building. Do this count at least twice a year: before planting and after harvest. Use a tally sheet or a notebook. Write the date, item name, and quantity. If you have many small items, count in groups of ten.

After counting, you must value the inventory. There are three common methods. First, cost method: record what you paid for the item. If you bought a bag of fertilizer for 30,000 shillings, its value is 30,000. Second, market value: record what it would sell for today. If fertilizer prices have risen to 35,000, use that. Third, net realizable value: for items you plan to sell, estimate the selling price minus any selling costs. For example, if a goat sells for 50,000 but transport to market costs 5,000, net value is 45,000.

For durable assets, depreciation reduces value over time. You will learn more about depreciation in Lesson 3. For now, know that a tractor bought for 2 million shillings five years ago is not worth 2 million today. You must estimate its current value based on age and condition. A simple way is to ask a local mechanic or check similar used tractors for sale.

Always keep a written record of your count and valuation. Include the method you used. For example: '10 bags NPK, cost method, 30,000 each, total 300,000.' This record helps you calculate the total value of your farm inventory. Banks and cooperatives often ask for this when you apply for a loan. Update your valuation every season because prices change.

3. Depreciation of Farm Assets

Depreciation is the loss in value of an asset over time. A tractor, plough, or building does not last forever. Each year it becomes worth less because of wear and tear, age, or new technology. You must record depreciation to know the true value of your farm assets. This is important for taxes, loans, and deciding when to replace an asset.

The simplest method is straight-line depreciation. You take the original cost, subtract the salvage value (what it will be worth at the end of its life), and divide by the number of years it will last. For example, a water pump costs 500,000 shillings. You expect it to last 5 years and then sell for 50,000. Depreciation per year = (500,000 - 50,000) / 5 = 90,000 shillings per year. After one year, the pump's book value is 500,000 - 90,000 = 410,000. After two years, 320,000, and so on.

For assets like vehicles or tractors, you might use the reducing balance method. This applies a fixed percentage to the remaining value each year. For example, a tractor costs 2,000,000 and you depreciate at 20% per year. Year 1: 2,000,000 x 20% = 400,000 depreciation, book value 1,600,000. Year 2: 1,600,000 x 20% = 320,000 depreciation, book value 1,280,000. This method gives higher depreciation in early years.

You do not need to depreciate consumables like seeds or fertilizer. They are used up in one season. Only durable assets that last more than one year are depreciated. Keep a depreciation schedule for each asset. Write the asset name, purchase date, cost, salvage value, useful life, and annual depreciation. Update it each year. If you sell an asset, record the sale price and compare it to the book value. If you sell for more than book value, you made a gain; if less, a loss. This record helps you manage your farm finances better.

4. Tracking Tools and Equipment

Tools and equipment are the backbone of your farm. You need to know where each item is, who is using it, and its condition. Start by giving each item a unique number or tag. For example, write 'HOE-001' on a hoe with a permanent marker or paint. For larger equipment like a tractor, use a metal tag with a number. Then create a logbook. In the logbook, write the item number, name, date purchased, cost, and current location. For example: 'PUMP-003, water pump, bought 10/05/2022, 500,000 shillings, stored in shed B.'

Every time a tool is borrowed or used, record it. Use a simple checkout sheet. Columns: date, item number, name of user, date returned, condition on return. For example: '15/06/2024, HOE-001, John, returned 16/06/2024, good condition.' This stops tools from disappearing. In many African farms, tools go missing because there is no tracking. A checkout sheet takes two minutes to fill and saves money.

For equipment like tractors or pumps, keep a maintenance log. Write the date, hours used, and any repairs. For example: 'Tractor TR-001, 20/06/2024, 1200 hours, changed oil and filter, cost 80,000.' This helps you predict when repairs are needed and avoid breakdowns during planting season. Also record fuel consumption. If a tractor uses 10 litres per hour, you can estimate fuel costs for a day's work.

At the end of each month, do a quick inventory of all tools and equipment. Compare what you have with your logbook. If something is missing, investigate immediately. For expensive equipment, consider insurance. Keep all receipts for purchases and repairs. Use a smartphone app like Equipment Tracker or a simple Excel sheet. If you have no computer, use a hardcover notebook. The goal is to always know what you own, where it is, and its condition. This record also helps when you want to sell or replace equipment.

5. Seed, Fertilizer, and Chemical Inventory

Seeds, fertilizers, and chemicals are expensive and sensitive. You must store them properly and track them carefully. Start with a store that is dry, cool, and locked. Keep seeds in airtight containers or bags off the floor. Fertilizers should be stacked on pallets to avoid moisture. Chemicals must be stored separately from seeds and feed, and away from children. Use a shelf with labels.

Create an inventory card for each product. Write the product name, supplier, date received, quantity, unit size, and expiry date. For example: 'Maize seed SC-403, bought 01/03/2024, 10 bags of 25 kg, expiry 12/2024.' When you use a bag, record the date and quantity used. For example: '05/04/2024, used 2 bags of maize seed for field A.' After each use, update the remaining balance. This is called a perpetual inventory system. It tells you exactly how much you have left without counting every time.

For fertilizers, note the type (e.g., NPK 23-21-0, urea, DAP) and application rate. If you apply 100 kg per hectare, you can calculate how many bags you need for your field. For example, a 2-hectare field needs 200 kg, which is 4 bags of 50 kg. Record the date of application and the field. This helps you compare yields and costs.

Chemicals like pesticides and herbicides are dangerous. Record the name, active ingredient, and expiry date. Never use expired chemicals. Keep a record of where and when you sprayed. For example: '10/05/2024, glyphosate, field B, 2 litres.' Also record the name of the person who applied it. After each season, do a physical count of all remaining stock. Compare with your records. If there is a difference, investigate. Maybe there was a spill, theft, or recording error. Adjust your records accordingly. This keeps your inventory accurate and helps you plan purchases for the next season.

6. Inventory Turnover Analysis

Inventory turnover tells you how quickly you use or sell your inventory in a given period. It is a key measure of farm efficiency. If you have too much inventory sitting idle, your money is tied up. If you have too little, you may run out during critical times. The formula is: Inventory Turnover = Cost of Goods Sold / Average Inventory. For a farm, 'cost of goods sold' is the total value of consumables used in a season. 'Average inventory' is the value at the start plus the value at the end, divided by two.

Let's use an example. Suppose you start the season with 200,000 shillings of fertilizer and seed. You buy another 300,000 during the season. At the end, you have 100,000 left. Your cost of goods sold is 200,000 + 300,000 - 100,000 = 400,000. Average inventory = (200,000 + 100,000) / 2 = 150,000. Turnover = 400,000 / 150,000 = 2.67 times per season. This means you used and replaced your inventory about 2.67 times. For a seasonal farm, a turnover of 2 to 4 is good. If it is less than 1, you are holding too much stock. If it is more than 6, you might be understocking and risking shortages.

You can also calculate turnover for individual items. For example, if you used 40 bags of fertilizer in a season and average stock was 10 bags, turnover = 4. That is good. If you used 5 bags and average stock was 20 bags, turnover = 0.25, meaning you have a lot of dead stock. You should reduce future purchases.

Use turnover analysis to make decisions. High turnover for seeds means you need to reorder more often. Low turnover for chemicals means you should buy smaller quantities. Record your turnover each season in a notebook or spreadsheet. Compare across seasons. If turnover drops, find out why. Maybe you overbought or had a poor harvest. This analysis helps you avoid waste and improve cash flow. It is a simple but powerful tool for any African farm.

Practical Reference: Inventory and Asset Records for African Farm Enterprises

Inventory and asset records are the backbone of any commercially viable farm. Without them, you cannot know your true cost of production, your available working capital, or whether your business is actually making a profit. In the African context â where farms range from 0.5-hectare smallholdings to 500-hectare commercial estates, and where input costs are often financed through seasonal credit â record keeping is not an administrative luxury. It is a survival tool.

This practical reference consolidates the six lessons in this module into a working guide you can apply immediately. It covers the types of farm inventory, how to count and value them, how to depreciate assets, how to track tools and equipment, how to manage seed/fertilizer/chemical stocks, and how to analyse inventory turnover to free up cash.

1. Types of Farm Inventory

Farm inventory falls into four broad categories. Consumable inputs include seed, fertilizer, agrochemicals, feed, fuel, and veterinary supplies. Produce inventory covers harvested crops, livestock ready for sale, and stored grain. Spare parts and maintenance supplies keep machinery running. Livestock and biological assets â breeding stock, dairy cattle, poultry layers â are living inventory that grow, reproduce, and depreciate.

Each category requires a different record format. Inputs are tracked by quantity and expiry. Produce is tracked by weight, grade, and storage location. Livestock is tracked individually or by herd unit with age, health, and reproductive status.

2. Physical Count and Valuation Methods

A physical count must be conducted at least quarterly, and always at financial year-end. Count in the presence of two people, record on a signed count sheet, and reconcile against the bin card or stock ledger the same day.

Valuation methods commonly used in African farm accounting include:

  • FIFO (First In, First Out): Best for fertilizer and chemicals where expiry matters.
  • Weighted Average Cost: Practical for grain and feed stored in bulk.
  • Net Realisable Value (NRV): Used for produce near harvest or livestock ready for market.
  • Standard Cost: Used by cooperatives and outgrower schemes for uniform pricing.

3. Depreciation of Farm Assets

Depreciation spreads the cost of a long-term asset over its useful life. For African farms, common assets include tractors, ploughs, boreholes, irrigation pumps, and storage sheds. The straight-line method is simplest and most widely accepted by lenders.

4. Tracking Tools and Equipment

Every tool and piece of equipment should have a unique asset tag, a location, and a responsible person. A simple asset register should capture:

  • Asset ID and description
  • Date of purchase and supplier
  • Purchase cost and funding source (own funds, loan, grant)
  • Useful life and depreciation rate
  • Location and custodian
  • Maintenance history and next service date
  • Disposal date and proceeds

In many African contexts, tools are shared among farmer groups or cooperatives. The asset register must therefore record group ownership and the rules for use, to avoid disputes.

5. Seed, Fertilizer, and Chemical Inventory

These are the highest-risk inventory items because they are perishable, seasonal, and often financed by credit. Key controls:

  • Store in a dry, ventilated, lockable store away from livestock and living quarters.
  • Use FIFO to move older stock first.
  • Record batch numbers and expiry dates on every bag.
  • Reconcile stock weekly during planting season.
  • Keep a separate register for restricted chemicals and observe national regulations.

Follow the label and national pesticide regulations at all times.
