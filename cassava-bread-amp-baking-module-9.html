Lessons

1. Identifying your best local customers

Start by walking around your neighbourhood for one week. Note where people gather: bus parks, markets, schools, churches, mosques, construction sites, and offices. Talk to at least 20 people. Ask them: 'If fresh cassava bread was available near you every morning, would you buy it? How much would you pay?' Write down their answers. You will quickly see who is most interested. Typically, your best customers are workers who eat breakfast away from home, school children who buy snacks, and families who buy bread for tea. Also consider small shop owners and kiosk operators who can resell your bread. They buy in bulk but expect a discount.

Next, look at your competition. Who else sells bread in your area? What type of bread? At what price? If there is a big bakery selling wheat bread for 500 shillings per loaf, you can sell cassava bread for 400 shillings and highlight that it is locally made and gluten-free. If there are no bread sellers, you have an open market. But you must create demand by giving free samples.

Create a simple customer list. Use a notebook or your phone. Write names, phone numbers, and what they like. For example: 'Mama Grace buys 2 loaves every Saturday prefers soft crust.' 'John bus driver buys 1 loaf daily at 6am.' This list becomes your gold. You can call or WhatsApp them when you have fresh bread.

Focus on people who buy daily or weekly. A customer who buys one loaf every day is better than one who buys 10 loaves once a year. Build relationships. Greet them by name. Remember their preferences. Small touches like extra slice or a smile will keep them coming back.

Finally, test different locations. Set up a small table at three different spots on different days. Count how many loaves you sell at each. After two weeks, you will know exactly where your best customers are. Then concentrate your selling efforts there.

2. Packaging bread for retail and wholesale

Packaging protects your bread from dust, flies, and handling. It also makes your bread look professional. For retail (selling to individual customers), use clear plastic bags or paper bags. Clear plastic bags are cheap and show the bread. You can buy 100 bags for about 2,000 shillings (or equivalent). Paper bags are more eco-friendly but cost a bit more. Print or write your phone number and 'Fresh Cassava Bread' on the bag with a permanent marker. This is free advertising.

For wholesale (selling to shops and kiosks), you need stronger packaging. Use larger plastic bags that hold 6 or 12 loaves. Tie them securely. Label each bag with the number of loaves, the date, and your price. Wholesale buyers want to know exactly what they are getting. You can also use cardboard boxes if you have many loaves. Ask local supermarkets for empty boxes. They often give them for free.

Consider using a simple sticker or stamp with your brand name. If you cannot afford printing, use a marker to write 'Cassava Bread [Your Phone Number]' on each bag. This helps customers find you again. For wholesale, agree on a consistent packaging style so shop owners recognise your product.

Always package bread when it is completely cool. If you bag warm bread, condensation will form and make the bread soggy and mouldy quickly. Let loaves cool on a rack for at least 1 hour before packing. For retail, you can also offer sliced bread in a bag. Many customers prefer sliced for convenience. Use a clean knife and cut evenly. Charge the same price or slightly more for slicing.

Keep packaging costs low. Buy in bulk from suppliers who sell plastic bags and paper bags. Share costs with other bakers if possible. Remember, good packaging reduces complaints and increases sales. A neat package tells customers you care about quality.

3. Selling at markets, schools, and bus parks

Markets, schools, and bus parks are gold mines for bread sellers. Each location has different peak times. At markets, the busiest days are usually market days (e.g., every 5 days) and weekends. Arrive early, by 6am, to catch vendors who want breakfast. Set up a small table or tray with your bread. Wear clean clothes and a smile. Price your bread competitively: if others sell at 500, sell at 450 for the first week to attract customers. Offer a free slice to taste. Once they taste it, many will buy.

At schools, target break time and closing time. Talk to the school administration first. Some schools allow vendors outside the gate. Others may ask for a small fee. Be respectful and follow rules. Sell small loaves or half-loaves for children's pocket money. A half-loaf at 200 shillings is easier for a child to afford than a full loaf at 400. You can also sell to teachers and staff. They often buy in bulk for their homes. Give them a small discount for 5 or more loaves.

At bus parks, drivers and travellers are always in a hurry. Sell bread that is already packaged and ready to grab. Keep change ready. Move between buses and call out 'Fresh cassava bread! 400 shillings!' But do not block passengers. Be polite. Bus drivers often buy multiple loaves for their journey. If you can, make a deal with one or two drivers to buy from you daily. They can also resell to passengers.

Always carry a notebook to record sales. Note which location sells best and at what time. After a month, you will know where to focus. Also, watch the weather. On rainy days, markets may be slow. Have a backup plan, like delivering to regular customers. Keep your bread covered when not selling to protect from dust and sun. A clean white cloth works well.

4. Using WhatsApp and Facebook to take orders

WhatsApp and Facebook are powerful free tools to sell bread. Start by creating a WhatsApp Business account. It is free. Set your profile picture to a photo of your bread. Write a status like 'Fresh cassava bread order now 400 shillings per loaf'. Add your phone number and location. Share your WhatsApp status every morning with a photo of the day's bread. People who see it will message you to order.

Create a Facebook page for your bakery. Name it something like 'Grace's Cassava Bread'. Post photos of your bread, your baking process, and happy customers. Join local Facebook groups for your area (e.g., 'Nairobi Buy and Sell' or 'Kampala Market'). Post in these groups once or twice a week. Do not spam. Offer a special: 'Order 5 loaves, get 1 free' for first-time customers. Use Facebook Messenger to chat with customers and take orders.

For orders, ask customers to send a message with: name, number of loaves, and pickup or delivery time. Confirm the order and give a total price. If you offer delivery, charge a small fee (e.g., 100 shillings within 2 km). For payment, use mobile money like M-Pesa, MTN Mobile Money, or Airtel Money. Give your number and ask them to send the money before delivery. This avoids losses.

Keep a record of all orders in a notebook or a simple spreadsheet on your phone. After delivery, send a thank-you message. Ask them to refer friends. You can also create a broadcast list for regular customers. Send them a message when you have fresh bread. But do not over-message. Once a day is enough.

Remember to reply quickly. If someone messages and you take hours to respond, they may buy elsewhere. Set aside time each morning and evening to check messages. Use auto-reply if you are busy. A simple 'Thank you for your order. I will confirm shortly' works. With consistent effort, WhatsApp and Facebook can bring you more customers than a physical stall.

5. Working with small shops and kiosks

Small shops and kiosks are great for wholesale. They have regular customers who buy bread daily. Approach shop owners politely. Go when they are not busy, usually mid-morning or early afternoon. Introduce yourself and your cassava bread. Offer a free sample loaf. Let them taste it. Explain that you can supply them daily or every other day at a wholesale price. For example, if you sell retail at 400 shillings, offer wholesale at 300 shillings per loaf. They can sell at 400 and make 100 shillings profit per loaf.

Start with a small trial. Give them 6 loaves on credit for the first delivery. Agree that they pay you after selling. If they sell quickly, they will order more. If not, you can adjust. Always provide fresh bread. Stale bread will not sell and will damage your reputation. Deliver at the same time each day so the shop owner can plan.

Make a simple agreement. Write down: price per loaf, delivery days, payment terms (cash on delivery or after sale), and return policy for unsold bread. Some shops will not accept returns. So start with small quantities. If a shop cannot sell all, you can offer to take back unsold loaves and give them fresh ones next time. But only if they are still fresh. This builds trust.

Keep a record of each shop: name, owner, phone number, order size, payment status. Follow up on payments. If a shop owes you money, politely remind them. Do not let debts grow. For reliable shops, you can offer a small discount for bulk orders (e.g., 20 loaves or more). You can also provide a simple display basket or tray with your bakery name. This helps customers identify your bread.

Visit your shops regularly. Ask how sales are going. Adjust quantities if needed. If a shop is not selling well, find out why. Maybe the location is bad or the price is too high. Work together to solve the problem. Good relationships with shop owners can give you steady income every day.

6. Handling customer complaints and feedback

Complaints are a gift. They tell you what to fix. When a customer complains, listen carefully. Do not argue. Say 'Thank you for telling me. I am sorry about that.' Ask questions to understand: Was the bread stale? Too hard? Too small? Did it arrive late? Write down the complaint. Then act quickly. If the bread was bad, replace it for free or give a refund. This may cost you a little now, but it keeps the customer for life. A customer who complains and is treated well becomes your most loyal supporter.

Common complaints include: bread is dry, bread is too dense, bread moulds quickly, or delivery is late. For dryness, check your baking time and storage. Maybe you need to wrap bread better. For density, adjust your cassava flour ratio or add more water. For mould, ensure bread is completely cool before packaging and sell within 2 days. For late delivery, wake up earlier or use a bicycle to deliver faster. Always aim to solve the root cause, not just the single complaint.

Ask for feedback regularly. After a customer buys, ask 'How was the bread?' If they say it was good, ask 'What could make it better?' You can also give a small slip of paper with your phone number and say 'Please tell me if you have any issues.' This shows you care. Positive feedback is also useful. Ask happy customers to give you a testimonial or to refer friends. You can post these on your WhatsApp status or Facebook page.

Keep a complaint log. Write the date, customer name, complaint, and what you did. Review it weekly. If you see the same complaint many times, change your process. For example, if many say the bread is too small, increase the size slightly or lower the price. If many say it is too expensive, consider reducing costs or offering a smaller loaf at a lower price.

Never ignore a complaint. Even if you think the customer is wrong, treat them with respect. Word of mouth travels fast in a community. One unhappy customer can tell ten others. But one well-handled complaint can turn a critic into a promoter. Always thank them for their feedback and follow up to ensure they are satisfied.

<h2>1. Identifying Your Best Local Customers</h2>
<p>Not every buyer is worth the same effort. In most African communities, cassava bread sells best to four groups: <strong>school pupils and canteens</strong> (predictable daily demand), <strong>bus park workers and travellers</strong> (impulse buyers, cash-ready), <strong>low-income households</strong> (volume buyers, price-sensitive), and <strong>small shop owners</strong> (repeat bulk buyers). Walk your area for one week and note who buys bread, when, and how much. Your best customer is the one who buys the same quantity at the same time every week not the one who buys once and disappears.</p>

<h2>2. Packaging Bread for Retail and Wholesale</h2>
<p>Cassava bread is fragile and dries out fast. Packaging must protect it, look clean, and carry your name. For <strong>retail</strong> (15 loaves), use food-grade clear polythene bags with a printed or handwritten label: bakery name, phone number, date baked. For <strong>wholesale</strong> (10+ loaves), use sturdy cartons or reusable crates lined with clean paper this prevents crushing during transport and keeps loaves saleable for shops.</p>
<p>Avoid reusing cement or fertiliser sacks. Customers notice, and it damages trust. If you can afford it, invest in a simple rubber stamp or printed sticker with your WhatsApp number this alone increases repeat orders in most communities.</p>

<h2>3. Selling at Markets, Schools, and Bus Parks</h2>
<p>Each location has its own rhythm. At <strong>markets</strong>, you need a visible stall, a clean table, and a price that beats your neighbour. At <strong>schools</strong>, the gate opens and closes on a strict timetable arrive 30 minutes early, and agree a fixed weekly order with the canteen manager. At <strong>bus parks</strong>, speed matters: travellers buy in seconds, so pre-bag loaves and keep change ready. Do not chase every customer serve the ones who come back.</p>

<h2>4. Using WhatsApp and Facebook to Take Orders</h2>
<p>WhatsApp is the most powerful free sales tool in African communities. Create a <strong>WhatsApp Business</strong> profile with your bakery name, location, opening hours, and a catalogue of your bread sizes and prices. Post a fresh photo of your bread every morning to your status. Ask satisfied customers to refer you to one friend. On Facebook, join local buy-and-sell groups and community pages, and post once or twice a week not every hour, or you will be muted.</p>
<p>Always confirm orders with three details: <strong>quantity, pickup/delivery time, and payment method</strong>. Keep a simple order book (or a notebook) so you never forget a customer.</p>

<h2>5. Working with Small Shops and Kiosks</h2>
<p>Shops and kiosks give you steady volume without you standing at the stall all day. Start with a small trial: give 10 loaves on consignment, agree a price, and return in two days to collect payment and unsold bread. If the shop sells out, increase the order. Always put the agreement in writing even a WhatsApp message counts. Offer a small margin to the shopkeeper (1020%) so they are motivated to sell your bread over a competitor's.</p>

<h2>6. Handling Customer Complaints and Feedback</h2>
<p>Complaints are free market research. If a customer says the bread is dry, stale, or too small, do not argue thank them, replace the product if reasonable, and note the issue. Common complaints in cassava bread are: <em>too crumbly</em>, <em>sour taste</em>, <em>poor packaging</em>, and <em>late delivery</em>. Each one points to a fix in your baking or logistics. Keep a complaint log for one month and you will see a pattern. Fix the top pattern first.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #c0392b;background:#fdecea;padding:14px;border-radius:8px;margin:18px 0;">
<strong style="color:#c0392b;"> SAFETY WARNING Food Handling and Sales</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Never sell cassava bread that smells sour, shows mould, or was baked more than 48 hours ago without proper storage. Cassava products can spoil quickly in heat and humidity, and poorly processed cassava flour can carry residual cyanogenic compounds. Always use <strong>properly processed, well-dried cassava flour</strong> from a trusted supplier, wash hands before handling bread, cover all bread on display, and keep raw and baked products separate. If a customer reports illness after eating your bread, stop sales immediately, keep a sample, and report to your local food safety authority.</p>
