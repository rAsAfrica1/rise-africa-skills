1. Determining Harvest Maturity Indices

Knowing when to harvest cassava is critical. Harvest too early and roots are small, watery, and low in starch. Wait too long and roots become woody, fibrous, and may rot in the ground. Most cassava varieties mature between 9 and 18 months after planting, depending on the variety and growing conditions. For example, early-maturing varieties like TMS 30572 can be ready in 912 months, while late varieties like local bitter types may take 1518 months. A good rule is to check maturity when leaves start turning yellow and fall, but that is not reliable alone. The best index is root size and starch content. Dig up a few sample plants from different parts of the field. Look at the roots: mature roots are firm, have a thick skin, and when you cut them, the flesh is white or cream-colored and feels heavy. You can also do a simple starch test: cut a root and press the cut surface; if it feels sticky and milky, starch is high. Another method is the 'crack test': bend a root; if it snaps cleanly, it's mature; if it bends without breaking, it's immature. In Africa, farmers often use the 'calendar method' based on planting date, but this can be off by months. Combine calendar with root sampling. For processing into gari or fufu, harvest when roots are 1012 months for best starch yield. For fresh market, harvest slightly earlier when roots are tender. Always harvest during the dry season when possible, as wet soil makes harvesting harder and roots rot faster. If you must harvest in the rainy season, do it quickly and process immediately. Remember, maturity indices are not just about time; they are about the plant's physical signs and your market need. Train yourself to check roots every month after 8 months. A simple tool like a soil probe or a machete can help you sample without destroying the whole plant. In the end, experience is the best teacher, but these guidelines will get you started.

2. Manual and Mechanical Harvesting Methods

Harvesting cassava is labor-intensive. Manual harvesting is the most common method in Africa, using simple tools like hoes, machetes, and digging sticks. The process: first, cut the stems about 3040 cm above the ground, leaving a stake for pulling. Then, loosen the soil around the roots with a hoe or fork, being careful not to cut the roots. Finally, pull the stem upward to lift the roots out. If the soil is hard, you may need to dig around each plant. A skilled worker can harvest about 200300 plants per day, which yields roughly 12 tons of fresh roots. This method is cheap but slow and can damage roots if not careful. Mechanical harvesting uses tractors with specialized cassava lifters or potato diggers. These machines work best on large, flat fields with sandy or loamy soil. They can harvest 1 hectare in 24 hours, equivalent to 1020 manual laborers. However, machines are expensive to buy or hire, and they can bruise roots, leading to faster spoilage. In Africa, mechanical harvesting is rare except on commercial farms in Nigeria, Ghana, and Tanzania. For smallholders, a compromise is the use of animal-drawn plows to loosen soil before manual pulling. Another option is the 'ridge and furrow' method: plant on ridges, then harvest by pulling the ridge apart. When harvesting, always start from the edge of the field to avoid compacting soil. After lifting, shake off excess soil but do not wash roots in the field; that invites rot. Leave roots in small heaps under shade for no more than a few hours before transport. If you use a machine, adjust the blade depth to avoid cutting roots. Check for root damage and separate damaged roots for immediate processing. In all methods, timing matters: harvest when soil is moist but not waterlogged. Dry, hard soil makes manual digging difficult; wet soil makes roots heavy and muddy. Plan your harvest for the early morning or late afternoon to avoid heat stress on roots. Whether manual or mechanical, the goal is to get roots out of the ground with minimal damage and move them quickly to processing or market.

3. Harvesting Tools and Equipment

Having the right tools makes cassava harvesting easier and reduces root damage. The basic manual tools are: a sharp machete or cutlass for cutting stems, a hoe (like the West African 'daba' or 'kpolokpo') for loosening soil, a digging fork or spade for lifting roots, and a digging stick for sandy soils. A machete should be 4560 cm long with a wooden handle; keep it sharp with a file. A hoe with a broad blade (1520 cm wide) works well for shallow digging. Forks with 34 tines are good for lifting without cutting. In some areas, farmers use a 'cassava lifter'a long metal bar with a curved endto pry roots up. For mechanical harvesting, you need a tractor (at least 5070 horsepower) with a cassava lifter or a modified potato digger. These implements have a blade that goes under the roots and a chain or conveyor that separates soil. They cost $3,000$8,000, but you can hire one for about $50$100 per hectare. Other useful equipment: gloves to protect hands from thorny stems and rough roots, boots for muddy fields, and a wheelbarrow or cart to move roots to the collection point. For post-harvest, you need baskets or crates for transport, and a shade structure or tarpaulin to protect roots from sun. A simple root chipper or grater is needed if you process immediately. In Africa, many farmers share tools through cooperatives. For example, in Nigeria, the 'Cassava Tool Kit' includes a machete, hoe, fork, and gloves, sold for around 15,000 naira. Maintenance is key: clean tools after use, remove soil, and sharpen blades regularly. A dull machete requires more force and can slip, causing injury. Store tools in a dry place. For mechanical harvesters, check oil, belts, and blades before use. Always follow safety: wear goggles when using power tools, and never put hands near moving parts. Remember, good tools are an investment; they save time and reduce losses. A farmer with a sharp machete and a sturdy hoe can harvest 0.5 hectare in a day with family labor. Without them, the same work takes twice as long and roots get damaged.

4. Field Handling and Transportation of Fresh Roots

Cassava roots begin to spoil within 2448 hours after harvest. Proper field handling and quick transport are essential to reduce losses. As soon as roots are lifted, remove excess soil by shaking or brushingdo not wash them. Washing removes the protective skin and speeds up rot. Place roots in small, shallow heaps (no more than 30 cm high) under shade, such as a tree or a temporary thatch shelter. Never leave roots in direct sun; the heat causes 'vascular streaking' (brown lines inside) and rapid deterioration. If you cannot transport immediately, keep roots in a cool, ventilated area. For transport, use baskets, plastic crates, or woven bags. Avoid throwing roots into a pile; they bruise easily. Bruised roots rot first. Load them gently. If using a truck or pickup, line the bed with leaves or a tarpaulin to cushion the roots. Do not overload; roots at the bottom will be crushed. Cover the load with a light cloth or tarpaulin to protect from sun and rain, but leave some ventilation. Transport time should be as short as possibleideally less than 6 hours to the processing site or market. In many African countries, farmers use bicycles, motorcycles, or donkey carts for short distances. For longer distances, hire a truck. If you are selling fresh roots, harvest in the early morning and transport to market by noon. If you are processing, harvest and process the same day. For storage, fresh roots can be kept for 23 days in a cool, humid place, but beyond that, they become unfit. A simple technique is to store roots in pits lined with moist sawdust or sand, but this only works for a few days. Another method is to dip roots in a fungicide solution (like benomyl) but this is not common for smallholders. The best practice is to harvest only what you can handle in a day. If you have a large field, harvest in batches. Remember, every hour of delay reduces quality and price. In Nigeria, studies show that up to 30% of cassava roots are lost due to poor handling and transport. By following these steps, you can cut that loss to less than 10%.

5. Post-Harvest Physiological Deterioration and Control

Post-harvest physiological deterioration (PPD) is the main reason fresh cassava roots spoil quickly. Within 2472 hours of harvest, roots develop blue-black streaks, become soft, and emit a bad smell. This is caused by enzymes that react with oxygen when the root is wounded during harvest. PPD is not a disease; it's a natural process. However, it can be controlled. The first step is to minimize wounding. Harvest carefully, avoid cuts and bruises. Do not drop roots or pile them high. Second, keep roots cool and humid. High temperatures (above 30C) and low humidity speed up PPD. In Africa, shade and ventilation are key. You can build a simple 'root store' with a thatched roof and open sides, where roots are spread in thin layers on raised platforms. Third, use chemical treatments. Dipping roots in a solution of 0.1% sodium hypochlorite (bleach) or 0.5% calcium chloride can delay PPD by 23 days. Some farmers use a wax coating, but it's expensive. Fourth, process quickly. If you cannot sell fresh, grate or chip roots within 24 hours. Processing into gari, fufu, or chips stops PPD. Fifth, use improved varieties. Some cassava varieties have slower PPD, like TMS 91/02324. Ask your extension officer for these. Sixth, storage techniques: you can store roots in polyethylene bags with small holes, which maintains humidity. Or use the 'pit storage' method: dig a pit 1 m deep, line with straw, place roots, cover with straw and soil. This can keep roots fresh for 46 weeks, but it's laborious. In practice, most smallholders harvest daily and process immediately. For those selling fresh, harvest in the morning, transport in the cool of the day, and sell by evening. If you must store overnight, keep roots in a cool room or under a wet cloth. Never store roots in a closed plastic bag without ventilation; they will rot. By combining careful harvesting, shade, and quick processing, you can reduce PPD losses from 3050% to under 10%. This means more food and more income for your family.

6. Sorting, Grading, and Packaging for Fresh Market

Sorting and grading fresh cassava roots add value and fetch higher prices. After harvest, remove any roots that are damaged, bruised, or show signs of rot. These should be processed immediately or discarded. Next, grade roots by size and shape. For fresh market, buyers prefer uniform, straight roots that are 2030 cm long and 510 cm in diameter. Roots that are too small or misshapen are sold for processing. In many African markets, cassava is sold in bundles of 510 roots, tied with twine or rubber bands. Grading into three classes is common: Grade A (large, straight, no blemishes), Grade B (medium, slight curves), Grade C (small or damaged, for processing). This helps you set prices: Grade A may fetch 2030% more than Grade C. Packaging is important for transport and presentation. Use clean, ventilated plastic crates (like those for tomatoes) or woven baskets. Avoid sacks that trap heat and moisture. If using baskets, line with leaves or paper to prevent bruising. For long-distance transport, pack roots in layers with leaves between them. Do not wash roots before packaging; wash only if selling immediately and keep them dry. Label your packages with your farm name and harvest date. This builds trust. For export, fresh cassava is rarely traded due to PPD, but frozen or processed cassava is. For local markets, sell within 24 hours. If you are part of a cooperative, you can pool your roots for better bargaining. In Nigeria, the 'Cassava Market Queens' often buy graded roots at premium prices. Remember, presentation matters: clean, uniform roots attract buyers. Also, consider packaging for specific uses: for fufu, larger roots are preferred; for garri, any size works. Always keep records of your harvest, grades, and sales. This helps you plan for next season. By sorting and grading, you reduce waste, increase income, and build a reputation as a reliable supplier. Start simple: a table, a knife, and crates. You can upgrade later.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Harvesting and Post-Harvest Handling of Cassava in Africa

Cassava (Manihot esculenta Crantz) is Africa's most important root crop by volume, with Nigeria alone producing over 60 million tonnes annually (FAOSTAT, 2022). Yet post-harvest losses of 2040% are common across sub-Saharan Africa, eroding farmer margins and limiting the growth of processing enterprises. This practical reference distils the key decisions that determine whether a cassava harvest becomes profit or waste. It is written for farm managers, extension officers, aggregators, and small-to-medium processors who need actionable guidance, not theory.

Every decision from maturity assessment to packaging directly affects root quality, starch content, cyanogenic potential, and shelf life. Getting these right is the difference between a 15% margin and a 40% margin for a processing business.

Determining Harvest Maturity

Cassava roots reach physiological maturity 918 months after planting, depending on variety. Sweet varieties mature earlier (912 months); bitter, high-starch industrial varieties typically need 1218 months. Harvesting too early reduces starch yield and increases cyanogenic glucosides; harvesting too late causes lignification (woody roots) and in-ground deterioration.

Practical maturity indices for African conditions:

  • Leaf senescence: Lower leaves yellow and drop; canopy thins by 5070%.
  • Root skin texture: Skin becomes rough and brown; easy to scrape with thumbnail.
  • Root diameter: 48 cm for most varieties; roots snap cleanly with a sharp "crack."
  • Starch content: 2532% fresh weight for industrial varieties (test with a simple specific gravity method or iodine test).
  • Variety calendar: Track planting date; do not rely on visual cues alone.

Harvesting Methods and Tools

Manual harvesting dominates across Africa, using a simple hoe, machete, or digging stick. The process involves cutting the stem 2030 cm above ground, loosening soil around the root, and pulling the root cluster upward. Mechanical harvesters (tractor-mounted or walk-behind) exist but are rare in smallholder systems due to cost and soil conditions.

Key tools and their business implications:

  • Machete/cutlass: Low cost, but slow (0.20.3 ha/day per worker).
  • Digging hoe: Standard tool; requires skill to avoid root damage.
  • Mechanical harvester: 12 ha/day, but capital cost of $8,000$25,000; viable for commercial estates or service providers.
  • Root lifter: Tractor attachment that lifts roots without severing; reduces bruising.

For a processing business, harvest scheduling is critical. Roots must reach the factory within 2448 hours of harvest to avoid post-harvest physiological deterioration (PPD). Contract farming arrangements with staggered planting dates allow continuous supply.

Post-Harvest Physiological Deterioration (PPD)

PPD is the single greatest threat to fresh cassava quality. Within 2472 hours of harvest, roots develop vascular streaking, discolouration, and off-flavours. PPD is triggered by wound-induced oxidative stress and is accelerated by high temperature and physical damage.

Control strategies:

  • Minimise wounding: Careful digging and handling; avoid dropping roots.
  • Cool storage: Shade, ventilated crates, or evaporative cooling; target 2530C and high humidity.
  • Chemical treatment: Dipping in 0.1% sodium hypochlorite or ascorbic acid solution can extend shelf life by 24 days.
  • Waxing or coating: Edible coatings (e.g., cassava starch-based) reduce moisture loss and oxidation.
  • Rapid processing: The most reliable control process within 24 hours.
SAFETY WARNING Cyanide Risk in Cassava Handling

Bitter cassava varieties contain cyanogenic glucosides (linamarin and lotaustralin) that release hydrogen cyanide (HCN) when tissues are damaged. Fresh roots can contain 20400 mg HCN/kg. Never eat raw or inadequately processed bitter cassava. Processing (soaking, fermentation, drying, roasting) reduces cyanide to safe levels (<10 mg/kg). Workers handling large volumes of fresh roots should ensure adequate ventilation, wear gloves, and avoid inhaling dust from dried cassava. Children and malnourished individuals are especially vulnerable to konzo, an irreversible paralytic disease linked to high-cyanide cassava consumption. Always test new varieties or process batches before consumption or sale.

Sorting, Grading, and Packaging

Grading directly affects price. Fresh market buyers in urban Africa pay premiums of 2050% for uniform, undamaged roots. Sorting should remove:

  • Roots with PPD symptoms (vascular streaking, soft spots)
  • Mechanically damaged or broken roots
  • Roots with insect damage (e.g., cassava mealybug, white grubs)
  • Over-sized or lignified roots

Packaging options: woven polypropylene sacks (cheap, poor ventilation), ventilated plastic crates (better, reusable), or modified atmosphere packaging (for premium urban markets). For processors, packaging is less critical than rapid transport in ventilated trucks.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Confirm maturity using at least two indicators (calendar + leaf senescence + root test).
  2. Harvest in the cool of the day (early morning or late afternoon) to reduce heat stress on roots.
  3. Minimise wounding use proper tools, avoid dropping or throwing roots.
  4. Keep roots shaded from field to collection point; never leave in direct sun.
  5. Transport within 24 hours to processing or market; use ventilated vehicles.
  6. Sort and grade immediately on arrival; remove PPD-affected roots.
  7. Process rapidly within 24 hours for best starch and food quality.
  8. Record batch data (harvest date, variety, field, weight) for traceability and buyer confidence.
