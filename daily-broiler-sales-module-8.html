1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Broiler chickens grow fast, but they are also fragile. A disease outbreak can wipe out your entire batch in days, turning your investment into a loss. In Africa, common killers include Newcastle disease, Gumboro (infectious bursal disease), coccidiosis, and chronic respiratory disease. These spread quickly through droppings, air, equipment, and even your clothes. Biosecurity is the set of simple, cheap actions you take every day to keep germs out and stop them from spreading if they get in. It is not about expensive drugs; it is about discipline. For example, a farmer in Kenya who lets visitors walk into the pen without boots can bring in Newcastle virus on their shoes. One week later, 80% of the birds may be dead. But a farmer who uses a footbath, keeps visitors out, and cleans drinkers daily can keep mortality under 5%. Healthy birds eat well, gain weight, and sell at top price. Sick birds eat less, grow slowly, and may be rejected by buyers. Your profit margin in broiler production is thinoften only 10-15% per bird. One disease outbreak can erase profits for three batches. Biosecurity also protects your family and community from diseases like avian flu that can pass to humans. And it protects your reputation: buyers return to farms with consistent, healthy birds. The core rules are: keep a closed flock (no mixing with other poultry), clean and disinfect regularly, control traffic, and act fast when you see signs of illness. This module will give you the tools, steps, and habits to make biosecurity a normal part of your daily routine. You do not need a veterinary degreejust consistency and attention to detail.

2. Tools and Materials

To practice good biosecurity, you need a few basic tools and materials. Most are cheap and available in local agro-vet shops or markets. First, footbaths: use a shallow basin or a wooden box lined with plastic. Fill it with disinfectant solution. Common disinfectants include glutaraldehyde, quaternary ammonium, or bleach (sodium hypochlorite). Bleach is cheapest but loses strength in sunlight and dirtchange it daily. A 0.5% bleach solution (5 ml bleach per 1 liter of water) works for footbaths. Second, boots: dedicated rubber boots that stay in the poultry house. Never wear them outside. Third, overalls or a farm coat: keep them inside the pen area. Fourth, hand washing: soap, water, and a nail brush. Fifth, cleaning tools: a stiff broom, a scraper, a high-pressure sprayer (or a knapsack sprayer), and scrubbing brushes. Sixth, disinfectants for surfaces: Virkon, glutaraldehyde, or a bleach solution (1% for surfaces). Seventh, rodent control: traps, bait stations, and wire mesh to block holes. Eighth, a foot-operated hand sanitizer station at the entrance. Ninth, a record book to track cleaning schedules, vaccinations, and any disease signs. Tenth, vaccines: keep a small cooler or fridge dedicated to vaccines (not food). Common vaccines in Africa: Newcastle (HB1, Lasota), Gumboro (intermediate), and fowl pox. Eleventh, a thermometer to check vaccine storage temperature (2-8C). Twelfth, a spray bottle for spot disinfection. You also need a separate area for sick birds (hospital pen) with its own tools. Finally, a boot dip at the entrance of each pen, and a wheel dip for vehicles if you have a driveway. All these items cost less than the price of 10 birds, but they can save your whole batch. Buy them before you stock your next chicks.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps daily to keep your broilers healthy. Step 1: Start with clean shoes and clothes. Before entering the poultry area, change into farm boots and overalls. Step 2: Use the footbath. Step into the footbath basin and scrub your boots on the brush provided. The disinfectant must cover the soles. Step 3: Wash hands with soap and water for at least 20 seconds. Step 4: Enter the pen. Walk calmly; avoid stressing the birds. Step 5: Check the birds first. Look for signs of illness: dull eyes, ruffled feathers, coughing, sneezing, watery droppings, or birds sitting apart. Count dead birds. Remove any dead birds immediately and dispose of them by burning or deep burial. Step 6: Check feed and water. Ensure feed is fresh and not moldy. Clean drinkers and refill with clean water. Add vitamins or electrolytes if birds are stressed. Step 7: Clean feeders and drinkers. Scrub them with a brush and disinfectant at least once daily. Step 8: Remove wet litter. Wet litter breeds coccidiosis and bacteria. Add fresh wood shavings or rice husks. Step 9: Check ventilation. Ammonia smell means poor air qualityopen vents or curtains. Step 10: Record everything. Note mortality, feed intake, water intake, and any symptoms. Step 11: After working, clean tools and store them inside the pen area. Step 12: Before leaving, wash hands again and change back into street clothes. Step 13: Weekly, do a deep clean: remove all litter, scrub walls and floors with detergent, then disinfect. Step 14: Control visitors. Allow only essential visitors. They must wear farm boots and overalls, and walk through the footbath. Step 15: Vaccinate on schedule. Typical schedule: Newcastle at day 7 (HB1), day 21 (Lasota), Gumboro at day 14 and 21. Use clean water for oral vaccines. Step 16: Quarantine new birds for 2 weeks before mixing. Step 17: Set traps for rodents and repair holes. Step 18: If you see sick birds, isolate them immediately in a hospital pen and call a vet. Do not sell sick birds. These steps take 30-45 minutes daily but prevent disasters.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Many farmers lose birds because of avoidable mistakes. Mistake 1: Not using a footbath. Fix: Place a footbath at every entrance. Use a lid to keep sunlight and rain out. Change disinfectant daily. Mistake 2: Wearing the same boots inside and outside. Fix: Keep dedicated boots inside the pen. Never wear them to the market or to visit other farms. Mistake 3: Allowing visitors without protection. Fix: Have spare boots and overalls for visitors. If they refuse, do not let them in. Mistake 4: Overcrowding. Fix: Broilers need space. Provide 10 birds per square meter (0.1 m per bird) for heavy breeds. Too many birds stress them and spread disease fast. Mistake 5: Wet litter. Fix: Stir litter daily and add fresh shavings. Fix leaking drinkers immediately. Mistake 6: Dirty drinkers. Fix: Wash drinkers daily with soap and disinfectant. Biofilm forms quickly and harbors germs. Mistake 7: Skipping vaccinations. Fix: Set phone reminders. Buy vaccines from reputable agro-vets and keep them cold. Mistake 8: Mixing age groups. Fix: Use separate pens for different batches. Never mix chicks with older birds. Mistake 9: Ignoring early signs. Fix: Act immediately. Isolate sick birds, increase vitamins, and consult a vet. Do not wait. Mistake 10: Using the wrong disinfectant. Fix: Use the correct dilution. Bleach for footbaths (0.5%), glutaraldehyde for surfaces (1%). Read labels. Mistake 11: Not cleaning feed storage. Fix: Keep feed in sealed containers off the floor. Clean up spills to avoid rodents. Mistake 12: Selling sick birds. Fix: Never sell sick birdsthey spread disease and ruin your reputation. Bury or burn them. Mistake 13: No record keeping. Fix: Keep a simple notebook. Track mortality, feed, and treatments. This helps you spot problems early. Mistake 14: Poor personal hygiene. Fix: Wash hands before and after handling birds. Change clothes daily. Mistake 15: Ignoring wild birds. Fix: Cover openings with wire mesh. Wild birds carry diseases. Fix these mistakes and your flock will thrive.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Biosecurity protects your birds, but it also protects you and your workers. Always wear protective clothing: boots, overalls, and gloves when handling sick birds or cleaning. Wash hands thoroughly with soap after any contact with birds or their droppings. Some poultry diseases, like avian influenza and salmonellosis, can infect humans. If you have flu-like symptoms after handling sick birds, see a doctor and mention your work. When using disinfectants, follow safety rules. Bleach and glutaraldehyde can burn skin and eyes. Wear gloves and goggles. Mix in a well-ventilated area. Never mix bleach with ammoniait produces toxic gas. Store chemicals away from feed and out of reach of children. Vaccines must be stored correctly: keep them between 2-8C. Do not freeze. Use a cooler with ice packs when transporting. Use clean, cool water for oral vaccines (no chlorine). Vaccinate in the cool part of the day. Handle birds gently to avoid stress. Dispose of dead birds properly: burn or bury deep (at least 1 meter) away from water sources. Never throw dead birds in the bush or river. This spreads disease. Keep a first-aid kit in the farm: soap, antiseptic, bandages, and eye wash. Train all workers on these practices. Post signs: 'No entry without boots' and 'Wash hands before entering.' Maintain a visitor log. Limit visits to essential people. Keep a closed flock: do not bring in birds from unknown sources. If you must, quarantine for 2 weeks. Keep the farm clean: remove weeds, standing water, and old equipment. Control rodents and insects. Use bait stations and fly traps. Best practice also means planning: have a vet's phone number, know the nearest diagnostic lab, and keep emergency funds for medicines. Finally, review your biosecurity plan monthly. Check what worked and what didn't. Adjust as needed. Healthy birds mean better profits and a safer farm for everyone.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it's time to put biosecurity into action. This exercise will help you set up a simple, effective system on your farm. You will need: a notebook, a pen, a footbath basin, disinfectant (bleach or glutaraldehyde), a scrubbing brush, rubber boots, overalls, soap, and a spray bottle. Step 1: Draw a map of your poultry area. Mark all entry pointsdoors, windows, gates. Identify where you will place footbaths. You need one at each entrance. Step 2: Set up your footbath. Mix 0.5% bleach solution (5 ml bleach per 1 liter of water). Fill the basin so it covers the soles of your boots. Place a brush next to it. Step 3: Create a hand-washing station. Put a jerrycan with a tap, soap, and a nail brush near the entrance. Step 4: Prepare dedicated clothing. Hang overalls and boots inside the pen area. Make a rule: no one enters without them. Step 5: Do a daily check. For one week, follow the step-by-step method from Lesson 3. Record mortality, feed intake, water intake, and any symptoms in your notebook. Step 6: Clean and disinfect. On day 3 and day 7, do a deep clean: remove litter, scrub walls and floors with detergent, rinse, then spray with 1% bleach or glutaraldehyde. Let dry before adding new litter. Step 7: Check your vaccine schedule. Write down the dates for Newcastle and Gumboro vaccines. If you have chicks, vaccinate on time. Step 8: Control visitors. For one week, allow no visitors except essential workers. If someone must enter, they must wear farm boots and overalls and walk through the footbath. Step 9: Set up a hospital pen. Choose a small, separate area for sick birds. Keep separate feeders and drinkers. Step 10: Review your records. At the end of the week, calculate mortality rate: (number dead / number started) x 100. A good target is under 5% for broilers. If higher, find the cause. Step 11: Make a plan. Based on what you learned, write three improvements for next week. For example: 'Change footbath daily' or 'Fix leaking drinker.' Step 12: Share with a neighbor. Teach one other farmer these steps. Biosecurity works best when everyone in the area practices it. By the end of this exercise, you will have a working biosecurity system. Keep it up dailyconsistency is the key to healthy birds and good profits.

<h2>Practical Reference: Health and Biosecurity for Daily Broiler Sales (Batch Production System)</h2>

<p>In a batch production system, biosecurity is not a cost centre it is a revenue protection tool. Every disease outbreak you prevent is a batch you sell at full weight, on schedule, at the market price you planned for. For African broiler producers running daily sales cycles, the difference between a profitable batch and a loss-making batch is usually decided in the first 14 days, and almost always by hygiene, foot traffic, and water quality. This practical reference gives you the tools, numbers, and checklists to run a defensible biosecurity programme on a small-to-medium farm.</p>

<h3>Why the zone model matters for daily sales</h3>
<p>If your broiler house is on the same path as your gate, your feed store, and your market crates, you are running a single-zone farm. A single-zone farm has no biosecurity it has hope. The three-zone model above costs almost nothing to implement: a footbath, a pair of dedicated boots, a hand-wash station, and a rule that nobody crosses from dirty to clean without changing. In practice, this one change reduces the introduction of <em>Newcastle disease</em> and <em>infectious bursal disease</em> (Gumboro) the two diseases that most often wipe out a batch in West and East Africa more than any vaccine alone.</p>

<strong style="color:#b91c1c;"> SAFETY WARNING DO NOT SKIP</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#7a1a1a;">Never enter the broiler house after visiting a live bird market, another poultry farm, or a slaughter point without a full change of clothing and footwear. Avian influenza and Newcastle disease can be carried on clothing, hair, and vehicle tyres. If you or your workers have been to a market, shower, change into farm-dedicated clothes, and disinfect boots before entering. Also: disinfectants must be used at the correct dilution a too-strong solution burns skin and corrodes equipment, a too-weak solution does nothing. Always follow the label and keep a dedicated measuring jug.</p>

<h3>Step-by-step practical method (daily routine)</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Before entering:</strong> Park vehicles outside Zone 1. Change into farm boots and overalls at the Zone 2 boundary.</li>
<li><strong>Footbath:</strong> Step into the footbath for at least 10 seconds. Keep it clean replace solution every 23 days or when visibly dirty.</li>
<li><strong>Hand wash:</strong> Wash hands with soap and water, then apply sanitiser.</li>
<li><strong>Inspect the house:</strong> Check water lines, feeders, litter, and ventilation. Record temperature and mortality.</li>
<li><strong>Remove dead birds:</strong> Use dedicated gloves and a sealed container. Never leave carcasses in or near the house.</li>
<li><strong>Clean drinkers and feeders:</strong> Scrub with a brush and disinfectant. Rinse with clean water before refilling.</li>
<li><strong>After work:</strong> Clean boots and tools, then leave them in Zone 2. Shower or change before going to the market or home.</li>
</ol>

<h3>Common mistakes and fixes</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Mistake:</strong> Reusing footbath solution for a week. <strong>Fix:</strong> Replace every 23 days; keep a lid on it to stop rain dilution.</li>
<li><strong>Mistake:</strong> Letting buyers walk into the house to inspect birds. <strong>Fix:</strong> Show birds at the door in a crate; never let buyers cross into Zone 3.</li>
<li><strong>Mistake:</strong> Mixing new birds with older birds. <strong>Fix:</strong> Practise all-in/all-out; clean and rest the house for 714 days between batches.</li>
<li><strong>Mistake:</strong> Using the same crates for market and farm. <strong>Fix:</strong> Keep market crates in Zone 1; never bring them into Zone 3.</li>
<li><strong>Mistake:</strong> Ignoring water quality. <strong>Fix:</strong> Test water at least once per batch; chlorinate if needed.</li>
</ul>
