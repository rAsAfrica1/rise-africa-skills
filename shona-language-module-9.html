1. Types of Weather

In Shona, weather is called mamiriro ekunze. The most common weather word is kunze, which means outside, but when talking about weather you use mamiriro ekunze. For example, 'Mamiriro ekunze akanaka' means 'The weather is good.' To ask about the weather, say 'Mamiriro ekunze akadii?' meaning 'How is the weather?'

Let's learn the basic weather types. Mvura means rain. When it rains, you say 'Kuri kunaya' or 'Mvura iri kunaya.' If it's sunny, say 'Kune zuva' or 'Zuva riri kubuda.' For cloudy weather, use 'Kune makore' or 'Makore akazara.' Wind is mhepo, so 'Kune mhepo' means 'It's windy.' When it's cold, say 'Kuri kutonhora' or 'Kunotonhora.' For hot weather, say 'Kuri kupisa' or 'Kunopisa.'

Other useful terms: chando means cold (as in winter cold), while kutonhora is the feeling of cold. Denga means sky. Bhanzi is a light drizzle. Mvura yemvura is heavy rain. Chimvuramabwe is hail. Mheni is lightning, and kutinhira is thunder. If there's a storm, say 'Kune dutu.' Fog is mhute, and mist is kapfungaira. Humidity is unyoro.

Practice by describing today's weather: 'Nhasi kuri kunaya uye kuri kutonhora' means 'Today it is raining and cold.' 'Nhasi kune zuva uye kuri kupisa' means 'Today it is sunny and hot.' You can also combine: 'Kune makore asi kuri kupisa' means 'It is cloudy but hot.'

Remember that Shona uses the verb ku- at the beginning for many weather expressions, like kuri kunaya (it is raining) or kuri kupisa (it is hot). The word 'kuri' means 'it is' and is used for weather. So when you want to say what the weather is doing, start with 'Kuri...' and then the verb. For example, 'Kuri kunaya' (It is raining), 'Kuri kupisa' (It is hot), 'Kuri kutonhora' (It is cold). This is the simplest way to talk about weather in Shona.

2. Seasons in Zimbabwe

Zimbabwe has four main seasons, although they are not as distinct as in Europe or North America. The seasons are: chirimo (spring), chirimo chikuru (summer), matsutso (autumn), and chando (winter). However, in Shona, the seasons are often described by the weather patterns rather than fixed dates.

The rainy season is called kunaya kwemvura or nguva yemvura. It usually runs from November to March. During this time, it rains heavily, especially from December to February. The Shona say 'Mvura inonaya zvikuru' meaning 'It rains a lot.' This is the farming season when crops like maize (chibage) and groundnuts (nzungu) are planted. The rain brings life to the land.

The dry season is called nguva yakaoma or chirimo. It goes from April to October. Within this, there is a cool dry season from May to August, called chando. During chando, it can be very cold, especially in the mornings and evenings. In Harare, temperatures can drop to around 5Â°C (41Â°F) at night. People wear warm clothes like majuzi (jerseys) and makoti (coats). The Shona say 'Kuri kutonhora zvikuru' meaning 'It is very cold.'

From September to October, it gets very hot, called kunopisa. Temperatures can reach 35Â°C (95Â°F) or more. This is before the rains come. The Shona say 'Kuri kupisa zvikuru' meaning 'It is very hot.' People stay in the shade and drink lots of water.

Autumn (matsutso) is a short period around April-May when leaves fall and the weather starts to cool. Spring (chirimo) is around August-September when it starts to warm up but before the rains.

In rural areas, these seasons guide farming activities. Planting happens with the first rains (mvura yekutanga), and harvest (kukohwa) happens at the end of the rainy season. Knowing the seasons helps you plan daily activities. For example, you wouldn't plan an outdoor party during the heavy rains without a backup plan. So learn to say: 'Muna Zvita, mvura inonaya zvikuru' (In December, it rains a lot) and 'Muna Chikumi, kuri kutonhora' (In June, it is cold).

3. Describing Today's Weather

To describe today's weather in Shona, you need to know how to say 'today' (nhasi) and use the correct verb forms. Start by asking 'Mamiriro ekunze akadii nhasi?' meaning 'How is the weather today?' The answer will often start with 'Nhasi...' (Today...).

If it's sunny, say 'Nhasi kune zuva' or 'Nhasi zuva riri kubuda.' If it's very sunny and hot, add 'zvikuru' (very): 'Nhasi kune zuva zvikuru uye kuri kupisa.' If it's cloudy, say 'Nhasi kune makore.' If it's partly cloudy, you can say 'Kune makore mashoma' (There are few clouds). If it's raining, say 'Nhasi kuri kunaya.' For heavy rain, 'Mvura iri kunaya zvikuru.' For light rain, 'Kuri kunaya zvishoma.' If it's windy, 'Nhasi kune mhepo.' If it's cold, 'Nhasi kuri kutonhora.' If it's hot, 'Nhasi kuri kupisa.'

You can combine weather conditions. For example: 'Nhasi kune zuva asi kune mhepo' means 'Today it is sunny but windy.' 'Nhasi kuri kunaya uye kuri kutonhora' means 'Today it is raining and cold.' 'Nhasi kune makore uye kuri kupisa' means 'Today it is cloudy and hot.'

To ask about temperature, you can say 'Kuri kupisa here?' (Is it hot?) or 'Kuri kutonhora here?' (Is it cold?). To answer, 'Hongu, kuri kupisa' (Yes, it is hot) or 'Kwete, kuri kutonhora' (No, it is cold).

Also, you can describe the weather by time of day. In the morning (mangwanani), you might say 'Mangwanani kuri kutonhora' (In the morning it is cold). In the afternoon (masikati), 'Masikati kuri kupisa' (In the afternoon it is hot). In the evening (manheru), 'Manheru kuri kunaya' (In the evening it is raining).

Practice by looking outside and describing what you see. For example, if you see dark clouds, say 'Kune makore matema' (There are dark clouds). If you feel a cool breeze, say 'Kune mhepo inotonhora' (There is a cold wind). The more you practice, the more natural it becomes. Remember to use 'kuri' for ongoing weather conditions. So today, when you wake up, try to say the weather in Shona: 'Nhasi kuri kunaya' or 'Nhasi kune zuva.'

4. Weather Forecasts

Weather forecasts in Shona are called 'fungidziro yemamiriro ekunze' or simply 'mamiriro ekunze anotevera.' To talk about tomorrow's weather, use 'mangwana' (tomorrow). For example, 'Mangwana kuchanaya' means 'Tomorrow it will rain.' The verb 'kuchanaya' is the future tense of 'kunaya' (to rain). Similarly, 'Mangwana kuchapisa' means 'Tomorrow it will be hot,' and 'Mangwana kuchatonhora' means 'Tomorrow it will be cold.'

To ask about the forecast, say 'Mamiriro ekunze mangwana akadii?' meaning 'What is the weather forecast for tomorrow?' Or 'Kuchanaya here mangwana?' meaning 'Will it rain tomorrow?' You can answer: 'Hongu, kuchanaya' (Yes, it will rain) or 'Kwete, hakuzonayi' (No, it will not rain). Note that 'hakuzonayi' is the negative future form.

Weather forecasts are important for planning. In Zimbabwe, the Meteorological Services Department (MSD) provides forecasts on radio, TV, and online. They use Shona to broadcast: 'Mamiriro ekunze' is the weather segment. You might hear: 'Kubva mangwana kusvika Muvhuro, mvura inotarisirwa munzvimbo zhinji' meaning 'From tomorrow to Monday, rain is expected in many areas.'

Other useful phrases: 'Mvura inonaya masikati' (Rain will fall in the afternoon). 'Kune mukana wemvura' (There is a chance of rain). 'Kuri kunaya kwakanyanya' (There is heavy rain expected). 'Mhepo inovhuvhuta' (Wind will blow). 'Tembiricha ichakwira' (Temperature will rise). 'Tembiricha ichadzika' (Temperature will drop).

To talk about the extended forecast, use 'vhiki rinouya' (next week) or 'mwedzi unouya' (next month). For example, 'Vhiki rinouya, mvura inotarisirwa' (Next week, rain is expected).

When listening to a forecast, pay attention to words like 'kunaya' (rain), 'kupisa' (heat), 'kutonhora' (cold), 'mhepo' (wind), and 'makore' (clouds). Also, learn the days of the week in Shona: Muvhuro (Monday), Chipiri (Tuesday), Chitatu (Wednesday), China (Thursday), Chishanu (Friday), Mugovera (Saturday), Svondo (Sunday). So you can say 'NeChishanu kuchanaya' (On Friday it will rain). Practice by making your own forecast: 'Mangwana kuchapisa uye kune zuva' (Tomorrow it will be hot and sunny).

5. Activities for Different Weather

Different weather calls for different activities. In Shona, you can talk about what you do in various weather conditions. When it's sunny and hot (kune zuva uye kuri kupisa), people often go to the river to swim (kunogeza murwizi) or stay in the shade (kugara mumumvuri). They drink cold water (mvura inotonhora) and eat ice cream (aisikirimu) if available. Children play outside (vanotamba panze). You might say: 'Kana kuri kupisa, ndinoda kunwa mvura inotonhora' (When it's hot, I like to drink cold water).

When it's raining (kuri kunaya), people stay indoors (vanogara mumba). They might read books (kuverenga mabhuku) or watch TV (kuona TV). Farmers are happy because rain helps crops grow (mvura inobatsira zvirimwa kukura). But too much rain can cause flooding (mafashamo). You might say: 'Kana kuri kunaya, ndinogara mumba' (When it's raining, I stay indoors).

When it's cold (kuri kutonhora), people wear warm clothes (vanopfeka mbatya dzinodziya) like juzi (jersey) and koti (coat). They drink hot tea (tii inopisa) or coffee (kofi inopisa). They sit by the fire (vanogara pamoto). In rural areas, families gather around the fire to tell stories (nyaya). You might say: 'Kana kuri kutonhora, ndinopfeka juzi' (When it's cold, I wear a jersey).

When it's windy (kune mhepo), people might fly kites (vanobhururutsa kayiti) if it's not too strong. But strong winds can be dangerous, so people stay indoors. You might say: 'Kana kune mhepo, ndinovhara musuwo' (When it's windy, I close the door).

In Zimbabwe, seasonal activities are common. During the rainy season (nguva yemvura), farming activities like planting (kudyara) and weeding (kusakura) take place. During the dry season (nguva yakaoma), harvesting (kukohwa) happens. In winter (chando), people might go to the Eastern Highlands for cold weather activities, but most stay warm at home. In summer (chirimo), people enjoy outdoor gatherings and braais (barbecues).

To ask someone what they do in a certain weather, say: 'Unoitei kana kuri kunaya?' (What do you do when it's raining?) The answer could be: 'Ndinoverenga bhuku' (I read a book). Practice by describing your own activities for different weather.

6. Extreme Weather Expressions

Extreme weather happens in Zimbabwe, and it's important to know how to talk about it in Shona. For heavy rain and storms, use 'dutu' (storm) and 'mvura yemvura' (heavy rain). You might hear: 'Kune dutu guru' (There is a big storm). 'Mvura iri kunaya zvikuru' (It is raining heavily). 'Mheni iri kupenya' (Lightning is flashing). 'Kutinhira kuri kuitika' (Thunder is happening). To warn someone, say: 'Chenjera, kune mheni!' (Be careful, there is lightning!).

For floods, the word is 'mafashamo.' A flood warning might be: 'Mafashamo ari kuuya' (Floods are coming). 'Rwizi rwazara' (The river is full). 'Usayambuke rwizi' (Don't cross the river). In 2000, Cyclone Eline caused severe floods in Zimbabwe, and people still talk about it: 'Mafashamo aEline akanga akaipa' (The Eline floods were bad).

For drought, use 'kusanaya kwemvura' (drought) or 'chirimo chakaoma' (dry season). You might say: 'Kusanaya kwemvura kwakonzera nzara' (The drought caused famine). 'Mvura haina kunaya kwemwedzi mitatu' (It hasn't rained for three months).

For extreme heat, say 'kupisa kwakanyanya' (extreme heat). 'Kuri kupisa zvikuru' (It is very hot). 'Tembiricha iri pa40 degrees' (The temperature is at 40 degrees). People say: 'Kupisa uku kunouraya' (This heat is killing).

For extreme cold, say 'chando chakanyanya' (extreme cold). 'Kuri kutonhora zvikuru' (It is very cold). 'Chando chiri kuuraya' (The cold is killing). In 2020, Zimbabwe experienced very cold temperatures, and people said: 'Chando chakanga chakaipa' (The cold was bad).

Other extreme weather: 'chimvuramabwe' (hail) can destroy crops. 'Mhepo inoparadza' (destructive wind). 'Dutu remhepo' (windstorm). To express fear or surprise, say: 'Mwari wangu!' (My God!) or 'Hoo!' (Oh!).

To ask about extreme weather, say: 'Mamiriro ekunze akaipa here?' (Is the weather bad?) or 'Kune njodzi here?' (Is there danger?). Always listen to warnings from the Meteorological Services Department. In Shona, they might say: 'Chenjerai mafashamo' (Beware of floods). Practice these expressions so you can understand and respond to weather emergencies.

4. Activities by Weather Type

Match your labour and machinery plan to the forecast. The table below is a quick operational guide used by extension officers.

  • Rainy days: Move to indoor work â grading, packaging, record-keeping, equipment maintenance. Do not spray (wash-off and drift). Avoid heavy vehicle traffic on wet soils to prevent compaction.
  • Hot dry days: Irrigate before 9am or after 4pm. Move livestock to shade. Schedule chemical application early. Watch for heat exhaustion in workers.
  • Windy days: Suspend spraying and aerial operations. Secure greenhouse covers and tarpaulins. Postpone burning.
  • Cold days (chando): Protect nursery seedlings, cover frost-sensitive crops, increase livestock feed for thermoregulation.
  • Overcast days: Good for transplanting (reduced transplant shock) but monitor for fungal disease.
â  SAFETY WARNING â Extreme Weather

During lightning (mheni), hail (chimvuramabwe), or flood conditions, stop all field operations immediately. Lightning kills livestock and farm workers in Zimbabwe every rainy season â do not shelter under isolated trees, do not handle metal implements in the open, and do not attempt to cross flooded rivers or low-level bridges. Hail can cause traumatic injury; move people and animals to solid shelter. During drought (kusanaya), enforce water rationing and monitor borehole levels daily. Never enter a grain silo or confined storage during or after storms without proper ventilation checks. Report extreme weather events to the Meteorological Services Department and your local AGRITEX officer for record-keeping and insurance claims.
