Onion Processing

8 lessons · 30 quiz questions · Fruit Vegetables

1 Onion varieties

Onions (Allium cepa) split into three groups based on day length. Choosing the wrong type for your latitude means bulbs never form.

Long-day: Need 14–16 hours daylight. Grown in Europe, northern US. Varieties: Yellow Globe, Red Baron.

Short-day: Need 10–12 hours. Grown in Africa, southern US. Varieties: Texas Grano, Red Creole.

Intermediate: 12–14 hours. Middle latitudes.

For processing, choose high-dry-matter varieties (Yellow Globe, Red Creole). Higher dry matter = better dehydration yield and longer storage.

2 Harvesting and curing

Onion harvest timing affects storage life.

  1. Harvest when tops fall over and start to brown
  2. Pull and leave in the field 2–3 days to dry (if weather permits)
  3. Move to a curing shed with good airflow
  4. Cure for 3–4 weeks at 25–30°C with 65–70% humidity
  5. Trim tops to 2 cm and roots
  6. Grade by size (small, medium, large, jumbo)

Well-cured onions store 6–9 months at 0–4°C, 65–70% humidity.

⚠ Poorly cured onions rot fast and smell awful. Cure properly or process immediately.

3 Peeling and grading

Manual peeling is slow (5 kg/hour). Mechanical peelers (abrasive or steam) do 300+ kg/hour.

Manual peeling: Cut both ends, then peel off outer skin. Keep 1–2 inner layers of skin.

Steam peeling: High-pressure steam 30 seconds, then cold water bath. Skin slips off.

Abrasive peeling: Rotating drum with abrasive walls. Fast but higher loss.

Grade peeled onions by size. Small (20–40 mm) for dehydration; large for slicing and pickling.

4 Dehydrated onion flakes

Dehydrated onion is a bulk commodity sold to food manufacturers for soups, ready meals and sauces.

  1. Peel and wash
  2. Slice 3 mm thick (mandolin or mechanical)
  3. Blanch 60 seconds (preserves colour, prevents browning)
  4. Cool in cold water, drain
  5. Dehydrate at 60°C for 8–10 hours
  6. Target moisture: 5–6%
  7. Rub into flakes or leave slices
  8. Pack in foil-lined bags

Yield: 100 kg fresh → 12–15 kg dried.

Bulk price: $6–$12 per kg to food manufacturers.

5 Onion powder

Onion powder sells for 2× the price of flakes and is used in seasoning blends, snack coatings and spice mixes.

  1. Dehydrate onion slices as above
  2. Mill in a hammer mill
  3. Sieve through 250 micron
  4. Package in foil bags or dark glass jars

Yield: 100 kg fresh → 10–13 kg powder.

Retail: $15–$30 per kg. Bulk to manufacturers: $10–$18 per kg.

6 Onion paste and puree

Onion paste is a popular retail product in Africa and India — used as a cooking base.

  1. Peel and dice onions
  2. Blanch 3 minutes (removes harshness)
  3. Blend into smooth paste
  4. Add salt (1.5%), citric acid (0.3%) and 0.1% potassium sorbate
  5. Heat to 90°C
  6. Hot-fill into jars

Yield: 100 kg fresh → 90 kg paste.

Retail: $4–$8 per 500 g jar.

7 Pickled onions

Pickled onions are a traditional product in Europe and the Middle East.

  1. Use small onions (20–30 mm) — pearl onions are ideal
  2. Peel and blanch 1 minute
  3. Pack into jars
  4. Cover with brine: 500 ml vinegar + 250 ml water + 100 g sugar + 30 g salt + pickling spices
  5. Seal and store 2 weeks before eating

Retail: $6–$12 per jar.

8 Storage and sprout control

Onions are one of the longest-storing vegetables when handled right.

  • Storage temp: 0–4°C at 65–70% humidity for 6–9 months
  • Do not store at: 5–15°C (below 4°C and above 15°C prevent sprouting; the middle range causes it)
  • Ventilation: Essential — 1 m³ airflow per tonne
  • Do not store with: potatoes, apples (ethylene causes sprouting)

Dried onion: 12 months in sealed foil bags, cool and dry.

💰 Onion flakes and powder are the highest-margin products — $6–$30 per kg vs $0.30–$0.60 for fresh onions.
