Module 1

The Mushroom Business and What the House Must Do

Oyster and button mushrooms grow in a narrow band of temperature, humidity and CO₂. A mushroom house is a machine for holding that band stable through a day/night swing. Everything the house does is in service of that.

Lesson 1.1

What makes a mushroom house different

Mushroom growing is a controlled-environment agriculture problem, closer to a hatchery than to a vegetable garden. The mushroom itself — the fruiting body you harvest and sell — is the reproductive organ of a fungus. The fungus lives in the substrate (the block of sawdust, straw, or compost), and it fruits only when the environment around it hits a specific narrow band of conditions.

A mushroom house must hold four parameters stable, 24 hours a day, for the 4–6 weeks of the grow cycle:

  1. Temperature. Within ±2 °C of the target for the species. Oyster mushrooms want 18–24 °C. Button mushrooms want 16–18 °C. Above or below, the fungus stops fruiting.
  2. Relative humidity. 85–95%. Below 85% and the substrate dries; the fruiting bodies wither. Above 95% and condensation forms, encouraging contamination.
  3. CO₂ concentration. Below 800 ppm during fruiting. Above 1,000 ppm and the fruiting bodies produce long stems and small caps — unsaleable. This is the parameter that surprises most first-time growers.
  4. Fresh air exchange. Introduced without killing the humidity and temperature. This is the hard engineering problem in a mushroom house.
The rule: a mushroom house is a set of parameters held steady. Every design decision — wall thickness, ceiling insulation, air inlet, humidifier, air conditioner — exists to serve those four parameters.

Oyster vs button — two different houses

For most small African growers, oyster mushrooms are the correct species to start with. Oyster species fruit on pasteurised straw, do not need composted manure, tolerate wider temperature swings, and finish a cycle in 4–6 weeks. Button mushrooms are a second-stage crop, requiring more capital, more skill, and a tighter temperature regime.

This course assumes oyster mushrooms as the primary species. Button mushroom adaptations are covered in Module 11.

Lesson 1.3

Why thermal mass beats insulation

Most buildings are insulated to keep heat in or out. A mushroom house needs something different: thermal mass. A heavy brick wall absorbs heat during the day and releases it slowly at night, holding the internal temperature stable across the 24-hour cycle. A light, well-insulated shed swings rapidly — hot at midday, cool at night — and the oyster mushrooms stall.

This is why a brick house is preferred over a lightweight shed for mushroom growing. The brick is not insulating; it is a thermal battery. Mass stabilises temperature in a way that foam panels cannot.

How much mass is enough?

For a small mushroom house, a 230 mm brick wall (single brick thickness) provides adequate mass. For high-altitude or very hot climates, a 350 mm cavity wall is better. Combined with an insulated ceiling (to stop heat leaking upward), this gives the stable interior the fungus needs.

What the house cannot do alone

Thermal mass holds temperature. It does not create it. In cold climates you need a heater; in hot climates you need cool air from an air conditioner or evaporative cooler. The house is the passive part of the system. Active equipment handles the rest.

Lesson 1.4

What a mushroom house must have

The seven essential features

  1. Masonry walls for thermal mass. 230 mm minimum brick, or 200 mm concrete block filled with grout.
  2. Insulated ceiling. 50–100 mm of foam or insulated panel to stop heat loss and condensation drip.
  3. Controllable air inlet and outlet. Small enough to prevent fast temperature swings, large enough to bring CO₂ down. Best practice: an inlet low on one side and an outlet high on the other, with adjustable dampers.
  4. Humidity source. Misting nozzles, a fogger, or a wet floor if humidity is naturally high. The system must be controllable, not on all day.
  5. Cooling and heating. Sized for your climate. Often an air conditioner or evaporative cooler for cooling and, at altitude, a small heater for winter nights.
  6. Shelving. Steel or concrete-frame shelves for grow bags. Two to three tiers typically. Modular so it can be reconfigured.
  7. Clean/dirty routes. A separate pasteurisation room or area, with a one-way flow: substrate comes in dirty, is pasteurised, goes into the grow room clean.

What is not essential

  • Windows. Mushrooms need darkness during spawn run; light only during fruiting. Windows reduce control.
  • Lights on all day. Fruiting mushrooms need 8–12 hours of dim light per day. That is enough to work by.
  • A polished floor. Cleanable, yes. Polished, no.
  • A separate spawn room. You can buy spawn in instead of making it (Module 8).

Knowledge check — Module 1

1. Why is a mushroom house different from a vegetable store or drying shed?

Correct: narrow environmental control. Temperature, humidity, CO₂ and fresh air must all be held in a specific band.

2. What species should a new grower start with?

Correct: oyster. Easier substrate, wider tolerance, shorter cycle, lower capital.

3. Why is thermal mass more important than insulation in a mushroom house?

Correct: mass stabilises temperature. A brick wall absorbs heat in the day and releases it at night, holding the interior steady.

4. What is the target CO₂ during fruiting?

Correct: under 800 ppm. Higher CO₂ produces long-stemmed, small-capped mushrooms that do not sell.

5. Which feature is NOT essential in a mushroom house?

Correct: windows on all sides. Windows reduce control. Mushrooms need darkness during spawn run and only dim light during fruiting.

Practical assignment — Module 1

Write a one-page grow-house decision for your own site.

  1. Species chosen — oyster, button, or other.
  2. Climate at your site — average daily high and low across the year.
  3. Target internal temperature and humidity for the species, and the maximum daily swing you must accommodate.
  4. Passive features — wall material and thickness, ceiling insulation.
  5. Active features — cooling method, heating method, humidity source, fresh-air inlet and outlet.
  6. Room footprint and shelving plan (number of tiers, total grow bag capacity).
  7. Pasteurisation room location relative to the grow room (in the clean/dirty route).
  8. One paragraph (150 words) naming the biggest environmental risk at your site and how you will reduce it.

Marks are weighted toward points 5 and 8. Environmental control is the whole business; the paragraph is where you decide what could kill it.
