5. Cooking the Porridge Correctly

Cooking is where good ingredients become good food — or where they get ruined. This module teaches you to cook over firewood, charcoal or a three-stone fire, with no electricity and no refrigeration. Every technique here has been used in African villages for generations. Every recipe has been calculated for a specific number of servings.

Module 5 of 12 · 6 lessons · ~55 minutes · off-grid cooking methods · batch tables from 1 serving to 5,000 servings · video demonstrations · quiz at the end
🌍 Off-Grid Design Principles

No electricity. Every method works on firewood, charcoal, or three-stone fire.

No refrigeration. Every recipe assumes cooked porridge will be eaten within 2 hours or safely stored without cold.

Limited water. Water ratios are calibrated for both safe cooking and minimal waste.

Minimal utensils. One pot, one spoon, one bowl. Nothing fancy.

Dry mix stores 4–6 months at ambient temperature in sealed containers. This is the product that survives the off-grid reality.

Lesson 5.1 — The right water ratio: 1 part flour to 5 parts water for young children

Get the water ratio wrong and you have either a lumpy porridge a child refuses, or a thin gruel that fills the stomach without feeding the body. This lesson fixes that forever.

The core ratio table

Child's ageRatio (flour : water)ConsistencyCalories per 100 ml
6–8 months1 : 6Thin, drips off spoon~35
9–11 months1 : 5Soft, ribbons off spoon~42
12–24 months1 : 4Thick, holds shape~55
25–59 months1 : 3.5Very thick, spoon stands up~65
Adults / adults recovering1 : 3Very thick, like stiff oats~75

Notice: the older the child, the less water. This is intentional. A 6-month-old's gut cannot handle thick food yet — but by age 2 the same child needs far more calories per mouthful. The ratio shifts to pack more nutrition into every spoonful.

The 1:5 rule for young children. If you remember only one number from this module, remember this: 1 part flour to 5 parts water for children 9–11 months — the most vulnerable feeding stage. Every meal: measure, don't guess.

How to measure without a measuring cup

Most households in the target regions do not have measuring cups. Use what is at hand:

  • 1 part = anything consistent: a tin can, a metal cup, a specific spoon, a small gourd
  • 5 parts = 5 of the same container filled with water
  • Once you set your "1 part" tool, always use that tool. Consistency matters more than the exact millilitres.

Example: A 300 ml tomato tin can. 1 tin of dry mix + 5 tins of water = one batch of porridge for a 9–11 month old. That is 300 g dry mix cooked in 1,500 ml water — roughly 6 servings.

The three water mistakes

  1. Adding too much water — the child fills up and stops before getting enough calories. This is the #1 reason home porridge fails to rehabilitate malnourished children.
  2. Adding too little water — the porridge is too thick to swallow safely, especially for children under 2. Risk of choking.
  3. Adding water after cooking — thinning a thick porridge with cold water introduces bacteria and dilutes the nutrition. Adjust before cooking, not after.

Lesson 5.2 — Cooking method without electricity: cold-water paste first, then simmer on firewood or charcoal

The single most important cooking technique in this course. Do it wrong and you get lumps — the number one reason children refuse porridge. Do it right and you get smooth, safe, digestible food every time.

The cold-water paste method (also called "slurry method")

  1. Measure the dry flour first. Do not eyeball it. A 50 g serving needs 50 g — not 60, not 40.
  2. Put flour in a bowl. Add a small amount of COLD water (about 2 parts water to 1 part flour).
  3. Stir vigorously with a spoon or whisk until you have a smooth paste with no lumps. This is the critical step — 30 seconds of stirring here saves 10 minutes of fighting lumps later.
  4. Bring the remaining water to a rolling boil in your pot, on your firewood, charcoal or three-stone fire.
  5. Slowly pour the cold paste into the boiling water while stirring constantly. Do not pour it all in at once. Do not stop stirring.
  6. Reduce the fire. Simmer for 5–7 minutes, stirring frequently. The porridge will thicken and become glossy.
  7. Remove from fire. Cool to safe eating temperature (test by dropping a small amount on your inner wrist — it should feel warm, not hot).
  8. Serve immediately. Do not let it sit for more than 30 minutes before feeding.

Why cold-water paste beats adding flour to boiling water

MethodResultWhy
Cold-water paste then add to boiling water✅ Smooth, no lumpsFlour hydrates gradually before hitting heat
Add dry flour to boiling water❌ Lumpy, unevenOuter layer cooks instantly, sealing inner flour
Add flour to cold water, then heat whole mixture⚠️ Slow and can burn bottomWorks but takes longer and needs constant stirring

Cooking on firewood, charcoal or three-stone fire

All three work. Here are the rules that matter for each:

Firewood

  • Use dry hardwood — it burns longer and hotter with less smoke
  • Build a small, focused fire. A big fire wastes wood and makes the pot too hot at the bottom
  • Bank the coals to one side for a "simmer zone" — move the pot away from the flames to control heat

Charcoal

  • Best for controlling heat — no flames to fight
  • Wait until the coals are glowing red with a grey ash surface before cooking
  • Spread coals evenly under the pot for even heat. Do not pile them

Three-stone fire

  • The traditional method — three stones support the pot over open fire
  • Wind is your enemy. Build in a sheltered spot or use a windbreak
  • Stir more frequently — heat distribution is uneven compared to gas or charcoal
Tested everywhere. The cold-water paste method works on any heat source — gas, charcoal, firewood, electric hotplate, even a solar cooker. It is the method, not the fire, that stops lumps.

Lesson 5.3 — Consistency by age: from thin for 6-month-olds to thick for 3-year-olds

The right consistency is not aesthetic — it is medical. A 6-month-old's swallowing reflex is different from a 3-year-old's. Feed the wrong consistency and either the food is refused or the child is at risk of choking.

The age-by-age guide

AgeConsistencyTestFeedings per dayAmount per feeding
6–8 monthsThin, drips off spoonSpoon tilted — a small drip falls2–3 + breastmilk2–4 tablespoons
9–11 monthsSoft, ribbons off spoonSpoon dipped — porridge hangs then slowly drips3–4 + breastmilk4–6 tablespoons
12–24 monthsThick, holds shape on spoonSpoon upside down — porridge does not fall4–5150–200 ml per feeding
25–59 monthsVery thick, stickySpoon stands upright in the porridge4–5250–350 ml per feeding
AdultsStiff porridge consistencySpoon stands, porridge can be shaped3 meals300–400 ml per meal

Notice the pattern: as the child grows, both the consistency thickens and the number of feedings increases. Adults often find this surprising — but a young child's stomach is small, so they need many small meals. An adult can eat three larger meals.

Why thick porridge is better for older children

A 2-year-old child needs about 1,000–1,400 calories per day. If you feed them thin porridge (35 calories per 100 ml), they would need to drink 3–4 litres — physically impossible. If you feed them thick porridge (55–65 calories per 100 ml), they need less than 2 litres across 4–5 feedings. That is achievable.

This single fact — thick porridge for older children — is the difference between a child who gains weight and one who stays malnourished despite being fed "plenty of porridge."

The infant exception — breastmilk still matters

For children under 12 months, breastmilk remains the primary food. This porridge is a supplement, not a replacement. The WHO recommends exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months and continued breastfeeding alongside solid foods until at least 2 years. If a mother stops breastfeeding to give only porridge, the child will become more malnourished, not less.

Adjusting consistency as the child recovers

As a malnourished child gains weight, their appetite increases and their stomach can hold more. Here is the recovery progression:

  • Weeks 1–2: Feed small amounts of thin porridge (1:6 ratio), 6–8 times daily. The child's gut is damaged and cannot handle more.
  • Weeks 3–4: Slowly thicken to 1:5 and increase portions. Appetite will return as the gut heals.
  • Months 2–3: Move to age-appropriate consistency and standard feeding frequency.
  • Beyond: The child should be eating family food with porridge as a supplement, not the main food.

Lesson 5.4 — Safe storage without refrigeration: 2-hour rule, fermentation, and dry mix longevity

This is the lesson designed for the reality you described — no electricity, no fridge. Everything here has been proven in rural Africa, South Asia and humanitarian emergencies.

The 2-hour rule — the most important food-safety rule

Cooked porridge is a perfect breeding ground for bacteria. Warm, moist, full of nutrients. At room temperature (20–35°C), bacteria double every 20 minutes.

After cooking, porridge is safe for only 2 hours at room temperature. After that, harmful bacteria have multiplied to dangerous levels. If a child eats it, they will likely get diarrhoea — which makes malnutrition worse, not better. Discard anything left after 2 hours.

Rules for cooked porridge without a fridge:

  • Cook only what will be eaten within 2 hours. Do not cook a large pot for the day unless you have a way to keep it hot or cold.
  • Keep hot porridge hot. If you must hold it, keep it above 60°C — over low heat, on a hot stone, or wrapped in a thick cloth inside an insulated container.
  • Do not reheat more than once. Once it cools and rewarms, the bacterial window opens again.
  • Never add fresh porridge to old porridge. This "topping up" practice is one of the most common causes of child diarrhoea in rural communities. Always start a fresh batch.

Fermentation — the traditional preservation method

Fermentation is Africa's answer to refrigeration. Long before fridges, African households fermented maize, cassava, milk and vegetables to preserve them. The same technique works for fortified porridge.

How fermented porridge works

Fermentation uses beneficial bacteria (lactic acid bacteria) to acidify the food, which:

  • Prevents harmful bacteria from growing
  • Extends safe eating window to 24–48 hours without refrigeration
  • Improves digestibility — the bacteria pre-digest some of the starch and protein
  • Increases absorption of iron and zinc by reducing phytates
  • Adds a slightly sour taste many children prefer

The fermentation method

  1. Mix dry flour with water at 1:4 ratio (thicker than usual) — but do NOT cook it yet.
  2. Cover the bowl with a clean cloth or lid.
  3. Leave at room temperature for 12–24 hours. In hot climates (30°C+), 8–12 hours is enough. In cool climates (below 20°C), 24–36 hours.
  4. The mixture will bubble slightly and smell slightly sour when ready. This is correct.
  5. Cook as normal (cold-water paste method, Lesson 5.2).
  6. Eat within 24 hours — the fermentation extends the safe window significantly.
Fermentation is a learned skill. The first few attempts may over-ferment (too sour) or under-ferment (no benefit). Adjust time based on your climate and taste. In Zimbabwe, Malawi and Zambia, fermented maize porridge is a traditional food called mangisi or chikondamoyo. Your grandmother likely knows this technique — ask her.

The dry mix — your real long-shelf-life asset

Here is the key insight: the dry fortified flour stores 4–6 months with no refrigeration. The cooked porridge lasts 2 hours. The dry mix lasts 4–6 months.

This means the correct model for off-grid families is:

  • Store the dry mix in sealed containers in a cool, dark, dry place
  • Cook only what is needed for the next meal
  • Never cook more than 2 hours' worth unless you plan to ferment it

This is why the dry mix is what you produce and sell. It is the product that survives the off-grid reality.

Dry mix storage rules (no refrigeration)

ContainerShelf lifeNotes
Open paper bag2–3 weeksNot recommended for extended storage
Sealed plastic bag (heavy)2–3 monthsWorks if kept cool and dark
Airtight plastic container4–6 monthsBest home option
Sealed metal tin6–9 monthsBest for long-term storage — keeps out light, moisture and pests
Vacuum-sealed bag9–12 monthsRequires equipment — best for commercial

Storage location rules

  • Coolest room in the house — usually the innermost room away from sun-facing walls
  • Elevated off the ground — on a shelf or wooden pallet, never directly on concrete or earth
  • Away from strong-smelling items — soap, fuel, onions, spices will permeate the flour
  • Sealed at all times — open containers invite insects, moisture and rodents
  • Rotate stock — use oldest first, label every container with date
🌍 Off-Grid Storage Cheat Sheet
  • Cooked porridge: 2 hours safe, no exceptions
  • Fermented porridge base: 12–24 hours (before cooking) + 2 hours after cooking
  • Dry mix in sealed container: 4–6 months at room temperature
  • Dry mix in sealed metal tin: 6–9 months
  • Vacuum-sealed dry mix: up to 12 months
  • Never: add new porridge to old, reheat more than once, or store cooked porridge overnight without fermentation

Lesson 5.5 — Feeding frequency: how many times a day, and how much per feed

The right food fed the wrong number of times does nothing. This lesson gives you the schedule that actually rehabilitates children.

The recommended feeding schedule

AgeFeedings per dayAmount per feedingTotal daily porridge
6–8 months2–3 (plus breastmilk)30–60 ml~150 ml
9–11 months3–4 (plus breastmilk)60–100 ml~350 ml
12–24 months4–5150–200 ml~750 ml
25–59 months4–5250–350 ml~1,200 ml

Notice the total daily porridge for a 2-year-old: about 750 ml — that is about 3 large cups. Spread across 4–5 feedings, that is easy for a child to eat. If you try to feed it in 2 meals, they cannot finish it and you waste food.

Timing across the day

For older children (2 years+), the ideal schedule:

6:30–7:00 AMBreakfast porridge

4:00 PMAfternoon porridge

For younger children (under 12 months), the schedule is tied to breastmilk feedings — porridge is offered between breastmilk meals, 2–4 times a day.

Don't force-feed, but don't give up

A malnourished child often has no appetite. This is a symptom, not stubbornness. Here is how to handle it:

  • Small, frequent feeds. A child who refuses 200 ml might eat 50 ml four times.
  • Feed slowly. Give 5 minutes of calm feeding, then pause, then continue.
  • Sit with the child. Feeding is social. Sing, talk, gently encourage.
  • Never force. Forcing food causes the child to associate eating with distress and makes things worse.
  • Persist for days. Appetite returns as the gut heals. If a child refuses for 3 days in a row, take them to a clinic — infection may be present.

Signs you are feeding correctly

Success is measurable. Look for these within 2–4 weeks:

  • Weight gain — the single best indicator. Weigh the child weekly. Even 100 g per week is progress.
  • Improved energy — the child plays more, cries less, is easier to comfort
  • Better mood — smiles, responds to you, makes eye contact
  • Return of appetite — the child asks for food instead of refusing it
  • Less illness — fewer episodes of diarrhoea or infection
  • Improved appearance — skin smoother, hair less brittle, eyes brighter
Weigh weekly. Without weekly weight tracking, you cannot tell whether the porridge is working. Buy a simple hanging scale (fishing scale with a sling) for $5–$10. It will pay for itself many times over — it tells you when to adjust, when to celebrate, and when to seek help.

Reality check — how much one child actually needs

To treat a moderately malnourished child back to health, plan on:

  • Duration: 8–12 weeks of daily feeding
  • Daily need: 4–5 servings of 50 g dry mix = 200–250 g dry mix per day
  • Total needed: 12–18 kg of dry mix over the full treatment
  • Cost to produce: ~$0.12 per serving × 4 servings/day × 90 days = $43 to fully rehabilitate one malnourished child

That is the number to remember when you talk to funders, clinics or NGOs. Forty-three dollars in raw materials can bring one malnourished child back to health. That is the entire premise of this business and this course.
