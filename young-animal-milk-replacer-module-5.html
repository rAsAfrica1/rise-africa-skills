1. Why Piglets Need Milk Replacer and Creep Feed: Biology, Economics and African Context

Piglets are born with very small energy reserves. A newborn piglet has roughly 1 to 2 percent body fat and about 30 grams of glycogen in the liver, which is enough for only 12 to 24 hours of fasting. If the sow is sick, has mastitis, has more than 14 piglets, or is a first-litter gilt with low milk output, piglets will starve within a day. This is why piglet milk replacer and creep feed are not luxuries but life-saving tools. In Africa, smallholder pig systems in Kenya, Uganda, Nigeria, South Africa and Zimbabwe often have sows that farrow 10 to 16 piglets, and mortality before weaning can reach 20 to 40 percent without intervention. FAO estimates that pre-weaning mortality in smallholder pig units in East Africa averages 25 percent, with starvation and crushing as the top causes. A good milk replacer and creep feed programme can cut that to below 10 percent. The biology is simple. A piglet weighs about 1.2 to 1.6 kg at birth and should double birth weight by day 7 and reach 8 to 9 kg by day 28. Sow milk provides about 1.0 to 1.3 kg of milk per piglet per day in a good sow, but this peaks at day 18 to 21 and then declines. By day 21, a litter of 12 piglets needs more energy and protein than the sow can supply. Creep feed fills that gap. Teagasc in Ireland recommends starting creep feed at day 7 to 10, offering 50 to 100 grams per piglet per day initially, increasing to 300 to 500 grams per piglet per day by day 28. Milk replacer is used when the sow has failed, when litters are large, or when piglets are orphaned. Denkavit, a Dutch company with distributors in Africa, produces piglet milk replacers that are mixed at 125 to 150 grams of powder per litre of water at 40 to 45 degrees Celsius. The same product can be fed as a liquid or as a gruel. Cost in Africa varies. In Kenya, a 25 kg bag of imported piglet milk replacer costs between 12,000 and 18,000 KES (about 90 to 140 USD). Local alternatives using skim milk powder, whey powder, soy protein concentrate, vegetable oil and vitamin-mineral premix can be made for 6,000 to 9,000 KES per 25 kg, but quality control is critical. In Nigeria, a 20 kg bag of piglet creep feed costs 18,000 to 25,000 NGN. In South Africa, a 25 kg bag of creep feed costs 450 to 650 ZAR. The most common mistakes are: feeding cold milk replacer (below 35 degrees Celsius causes gut stasis), mixing too thick (above 200 grams per litre causes diarrhoea), using dirty water (E. coli and rotavirus), feeding with a bottle and teat that is too large (piglets inhale milk into the lungs), and leaving wet feed in the trough for more than 2 hours (mould and bacterial growth). A piglet that does not get colostrum in the first 6 hours has a 50 percent chance of dying. If the sow is unable to nurse, colostrum must be hand-milked from another sow or replaced with a colostrum supplement. Royal Canin and other veterinary brands make colostrum replacers for piglets, but in Africa, fresh cow or goat colostrum can be used if heated to 40 degrees Celsius and fed at 20 to 30 ml per piglet every 2 hours for the first 6 hours. After that, milk replacer at 125 grams per litre is fed at 200 to 300 ml per piglet per day in 6 to 8 feeds. By day 7, introduce creep feed. By day 21, piglets should be eating 150 to 250 grams of creep feed per day and drinking 300 to 500 ml of milk replacer. By day 28, weaning can occur if piglets weigh at least 7 kg and are eating 400 grams of creep feed per day. This module will teach you how to mix, feed, clean and monitor piglets on milk replacer and creep feed, with practical numbers for African farms.

2. Mixing and Feeding Piglet Milk Replacer: Temperatures, Volumes and Hygiene

Mixing piglet milk replacer correctly is the difference between a thriving litter and a dead one. The first rule is water quality. Use clean, fresh water that has been boiled and cooled to 40 to 45 degrees Celsius. Do not use chlorinated water directly from a tap if chlorine is above 1 ppm, because it kills beneficial bacteria and can reduce intake. If you must use tap water, let it stand for 24 hours. The second rule is concentration. Most commercial piglet milk replacers, including Denkavit, MaxCare (NZ) and local brands from South Africa and Kenya, are mixed at 125 to 150 grams of powder per litre of water. That means for 10 litres, you weigh 1,250 to 1,500 grams. A common mistake is using a cup or scoop instead of a scale. A level tablespoon of powder weighs about 8 to 10 grams, so 15 tablespoons is roughly 125 to 150 grams. But volume varies with moisture and compaction. Always use a digital scale if possible. A 5 kg bag of milk replacer at 125 grams per litre makes 40 litres of milk. The third rule is temperature. Feed at 38 to 40 degrees Celsius. If the milk is below 35 degrees Celsius, piglets will drink less and may get gut stasis. If it is above 45 degrees Celsius, it can scald the mouth and oesophagus and destroy vitamins and probiotics. Use a thermometer. In Africa, where ambient temperatures can be 30 to 35 degrees Celsius, milk cools slowly, but in the early morning it can drop quickly. Feed within 15 minutes of mixing. Do not mix a large batch for the whole day. Bacteria double every 20 minutes at 30 degrees Celsius. The fourth rule is feeding volume. A newborn piglet needs 200 to 300 ml of milk replacer per day in the first week, divided into 6 to 8 feeds. That is 30 to 50 ml per feed. In the second week, increase to 400 to 500 ml per day in 5 to 6 feeds. In the third week, 600 to 800 ml per day in 4 to 5 feeds. In the fourth week, 800 to 1,000 ml per day in 3 to 4 feeds, plus creep feed. As a rule of thumb, feed 10 to 15 percent of body weight per day as liquid milk. A 2 kg piglet at day 7 gets 200 to 300 ml. A 5 kg piglet at day 21 gets 500 to 750 ml. A 7 kg piglet at day 28 gets 700 to 1,000 ml. The fifth rule is hygiene. All feeding equipment must be washed with hot water and detergent after every feed, then rinsed and dried. Do not use the same bucket for milk and for cleaning. Do not feed milk that has been standing for more than 2 hours. If you use a nipple bar or a trough, clean it twice daily. Piglets are messy. They will step in the trough and defecate. A dirty trough causes diarrhoea. The sixth rule is feeding method. For very young piglets (day 1 to 7), use a bottle with a soft teat or a shallow pan. For day 7 to 28, use a trough with a lip that prevents spillage. Some farmers use a nipple bar with 10 to 20 nipples, which is excellent for large litters but requires daily cleaning. MaxCare in New Zealand recommends feeding orphan piglets every 2 hours for the first 24 hours, then every 3 hours for the next 3 days, then every 4 hours. In Africa, where labour is available, this is possible. If not, feed at least 6 times per day. The seventh rule is transition. When moving from milk replacer to creep feed, do not stop milk suddenly. Reduce milk replacer by 20 percent per day over 5 days while increasing creep feed. By day 28, piglets should be on dry feed and water only. The eighth rule is monitoring. Weigh a sample of piglets every 3 days. If average daily gain is below 150 grams per day, something is wrong. Check temperature, concentration, hygiene and health. Diarrhoea is the most common problem. If more than 10 percent of piglets have diarrhoea, reduce milk concentration to 100 grams per litre for 2 days, increase feeding frequency, and add an electrolyte solution. Common electrolyte recipes: 3.5 grams sodium chloride, 2.5 grams sodium bicarbonate, 1.5 grams potassium chloride, 20 grams glucose per litre of water. Give 50 to 100 ml per piglet per day in addition to milk. If diarrhoea persists, consult a veterinarian. Do not use antibiotics without prescription. Cost in Africa: a 25 kg bag of milk replacer at 12,000 KES makes 200 litres at 125 grams per litre. That is 60 KES per litre. Feeding 10 piglets for 21 days at 500 ml per day each requires 105 litres, costing 6,300 KES. That is 630 KES per piglet. If those piglets survive and reach 8 kg, they are worth 4,000 to 6,000 KES each. The economics are clear.

3. Creep Feed Formulation and Feeding: From Day 7 to Weaning

Creep feed is a dry, highly digestible diet offered to piglets while they are still nursing. Its purpose is to teach the piglet to eat solid food, to supply nutrients that sow milk cannot provide after day 18, and to prepare the gut for weaning. Teagasc recommends starting creep feed at day 7 to 10. In Africa, where sow milk yield may be lower due to heat stress and poor sow nutrition, start at day 5 to 7. The ideal creep feed is 18 to 20 percent crude protein, 1.2 to 1.4 percent lysine, 3 to 5 percent crude fibre, and 14.5 to 15.5 MJ digestible energy per kg. It should contain cooked cereals (maize, rice, wheat), soybean meal, fish meal, skim milk powder, whey powder, vegetable oil, and a vitamin-mineral premix. Many African farmers make their own creep feed. A typical recipe from university extension in Nigeria and Kenya: 40 kg maize, 20 kg soybean meal, 10 kg fish meal, 10 kg skim milk powder, 10 kg whey powder, 5 kg vegetable oil, 3 kg dicalcium phosphate, 1 kg limestone, 0.5 kg salt, 0.5 kg vitamin-mineral premix. This gives about 19 percent protein. Mix well. Do not use raw soybeans; they contain trypsin inhibitors that cause diarrhoea. Use toasted or extruded soybeans. Do not use cottonseed meal; gossypol is toxic to piglets. Do not use high levels of wheat bran; it reduces energy density. Commercial creep feeds are available from companies like Denkavit, MaxCare, and local feed mills in South Africa (e.g., Meadow Feeds, Epol) and Kenya (e.g., Unga, Pembe). A 25 kg bag costs 3,500 to 5,000 KES in Kenya, 15,000 to 22,000 NGN in Nigeria, and 400 to 600 ZAR in South Africa. Feeding method: offer small amounts fresh 3 to 4 times per day. Start with 50 grams per piglet per day at day 7. Increase to 100 grams at day 14, 200 grams at day 21, and 400 grams at day 28. Do not leave stale feed in the trough. Piglets will not eat feed that smells musty or has been contaminated with urine or faeces. Use a shallow, clean trough that piglets can easily reach. Place it in a clean, dry area away from the sow's dunging area. Some farmers use a creep box or a covered creep area to protect feed from the sow. In Africa, where heat and humidity are high, feed can go mouldy within 24 hours. Offer only what piglets will eat in 2 hours. Weigh the feed offered and the feed left over to calculate intake. A good target is 200 to 300 grams of creep feed per piglet per day by day 21. If piglets are not eating creep feed by day 14, check the following: is the feed fresh? Is it too coarse or too fine? Is the trough in a comfortable place? Are piglets healthy? Are they getting too much milk replacer? Sometimes reducing milk replacer by 20 percent encourages creep feed intake. Water is critical. Piglets need fresh, clean water at all times. A lactating sow drinks 20 to 30 litres per day, but piglets need 0.5 to 1 litre per day by day 21. If water is not available, creep feed intake drops. Use a low-pressure nipple drinker or a shallow bowl. Change water at least twice daily. By day 28, piglets should be eating 400 to 500 grams of creep feed per day and weigh 7 to 9 kg. At this point, weaning can occur. Teagasc recommends weaning at 28 days if piglets are eating 400 grams per day. In Africa, many farmers wean at 35 to 42 days because piglets are lighter. If you wean at 42 days, continue creep feed until day 56. After weaning, switch to a weaner diet with 18 percent protein and 1.1 percent lysine. Do not change feed suddenly. Mix the creep feed with the weaner diet for 5 days. Common mistakes with creep feed: offering too much at once (waste and mould), using dirty troughs (diarrhoea), not providing water (dehydration), using low-quality ingredients (poor growth), and stopping creep feed too early (growth check). A well-managed creep feeding programme can increase weaning weight by 1 to 2 kg, which translates to 10 to 20 days earlier marketing. In Kenya, a piglet that weighs 8 kg at weaning sells for 4,000 to 6,000 KES. A piglet that weighs 6 kg sells for 2,500 to 3,500 KES. The extra 1,500 to 2,500 KES per piglet is profit.

4. Managing Orphaned and Large Litters: Practical Protocols for African Farms

Orphaned piglets are those whose mother has died, has no milk, or has rejected them. Large litters are those with more than 14 piglets, where the sow has only 12 to 14 functional teats. In both cases, milk replacer is essential. The first 6 hours are critical. A newborn piglet needs 200 to 250 ml of colostrum per kg of body weight in the first 24 hours. That means a 1.5 kg piglet needs 300 to 375 ml of colostrum. If the sow is dead, you must find colostrum from another sow that farrowed within the last 24 hours. If none is available, use a commercial colostrum replacer (e.g., Royal Canin colostrum for piglets, or Denkavit colostrum). In Africa, fresh cow or goat colostrum can be used. Heat it to 40 degrees Celsius and feed 20 to 30 ml per piglet every 2 hours for the first 6 hours, then every 3 hours for the next 18 hours. Do not feed cow colostrum that has been frozen and thawed more than once. Do not feed milk from a cow that has mastitis. After 24 hours, switch to piglet milk replacer at 125 to 150 grams per litre. Feed 6 to 8 times per day for the first week. Use a bottle with a soft teat. If piglets are weak, use a stomach tube. A stomach tube is a soft rubber tube passed down the oesophagus into the stomach. It must be done by a trained person. The tube should be lubricated with clean water or vegetable oil. Measure the tube from the mouth to the last rib. Mark it. Insert gently. If the piglet coughs or struggles, stop. Feed 20 to 30 ml per kg body weight per feed. For a 1.5 kg piglet, that is 30 to 45 ml. Do not feed more than 50 ml per kg per feed. For large litters, the best strategy is split nursing. Divide the litter into two groups. Group A nurses from 6 am to 12 pm and 6 pm to 12 am. Group B nurses from 12 pm to 6 pm and 12 am to 6 am. The sow is with one group while the other group is in a warm, clean box with a heat lamp or hot water bottle. Feed the group away from the sow with milk replacer at 100 to 125 grams per litre, 4 to 6 times per day. This reduces competition and ensures all piglets get milk. Another strategy is cross-fostering. Move the smallest piglets to a sow that farrowed within the last 3 days and has fewer than 10 piglets. Move the largest piglets to a sow that farrowed 7 to 10 days ago. Never mix piglets from different farms without quarantine. In Africa, African Swine Fever is a major threat. Any new piglet must be isolated for 30 days. For orphaned piglets, create a warm, dry nest. The temperature should be 30 to 32 degrees Celsius for the first week, 28 to 30 degrees Celsius for the second week, and 25 to 28 degrees Celsius for the third week. Use a heat lamp (250 watts) or a hot water bottle wrapped in a towel. Do not use open fires; smoke damages lungs. Provide clean bedding (straw, wood shavings, or shredded paper). Change bedding daily. Weigh piglets every 3 days. A healthy orphan piglet should gain 150 to 250 grams per day. If weight gain is below 100 grams per day, increase milk replacer concentration to 150 grams per litre and feed more frequently. If diarrhoea occurs, reduce concentration to 100 grams per litre for 2 days and give electrolytes. Common mistakes with orphans: feeding cold milk (causes hypothermia and gut stasis), overfeeding (causes diarrhoea and bloating), underfeeding (causes starvation), using dirty equipment (causes infection), and not providing enough warmth (piglets cannot digest milk if cold). Cost in Africa: feeding 10 orphan piglets for 28 days requires about 140 litres of milk replacer at 125 grams per litre, which is 17.5 kg of powder. At 12,000 KES per 25 kg, that is 8,400 KES. Plus creep feed at 400 grams per piglet per day for the last 14 days: 10 piglets x 0.4 kg x 14 days = 56 kg, costing 8,000 to 11,000 KES. Total cost 16,400 to 19,400 KES. If 10 piglets survive and sell at 5,000 KES each, revenue is 50,000 KES. Profit is 30,000 to 33,000 KES. The investment is worth it.

5. Troubleshooting, Costing and Scaling Up: Making Piglet Milk Replacer Work on Your Farm

Even with good protocols, problems occur. This lesson covers troubleshooting, costing, and scaling up. The most common problem is diarrhoea. In piglets under 7 days, diarrhoea is usually caused by E. coli, rotavirus, or overfeeding. In piglets over 7 days, it is usually caused by coccidiosis, feed changes, or dirty water. If more than 10 percent of piglets have diarrhoea, do the following: reduce milk replacer concentration to 100 grams per litre for 2 days. Increase feeding frequency to 8 times per day. Give 50 to 100 ml of electrolyte solution per piglet per day. Clean and disinfect all equipment with hot water and a disinfectant (e.g., Virkon S at 1 percent). Remove wet bedding. If diarrhoea persists for more than 3 days, consult a veterinarian. Do not use antibiotics without a prescription. The second problem is poor growth. If average daily gain is below 150 grams per day, check the following: is the milk replacer fresh? Is it stored in a cool, dry place? Is it mixed at the correct concentration? Is the water clean? Is the temperature correct? Are piglets warm enough? Are they getting enough milk? A 2 kg piglet needs 200 to 300 ml per day. A 5 kg piglet needs 500 to 750 ml per day. If you are feeding less, increase. The third problem is refusal to eat creep feed. This is common if piglets are getting too much milk replacer. Reduce milk replacer by 20 percent for 3 days. Offer fresh creep feed 4 times per day. Add a small amount of milk replacer powder on top of the creep feed to encourage eating. The fourth problem is mortality. If piglets are dying, do a post-mortem. Look for signs of starvation (empty stomach, no fat), crushing (bruising, broken ribs), or infection (enlarged liver, fluid in lungs). If starvation, increase milk replacer. If crushing, improve the farrowing pen. If infection, consult a veterinarian. Costing: In Kenya, a 25 kg bag of piglet milk replacer costs 12,000 to 18,000 KES. A 25 kg bag of creep feed costs 3,500 to 5,000 KES. A 25 kg bag of weaner feed costs 3,000 to 4,500 KES. For a litter of 10 piglets, from day 1 to day 28, you need about 140 litres of milk replacer (17.5 kg powder) and 56 kg of creep feed. Total cost: 8,400 KES for milk replacer + 8,000 to 11,000 KES for creep feed = 16,400 to 19,400 KES. If you sell 10 piglets at 5,000 KES each, revenue is 50,000 KES. Profit is 30,600 to 33,600 KES. In Nigeria, a 20 kg bag of milk replacer costs 15,000 to 22,000 NGN. A 20 kg bag of creep feed costs 12,000 to 18,000 NGN. For 10 piglets, you need 14 kg of milk replacer (10,500 to 15,400 NGN) and 56 kg of creep feed (33,600 to 50,400 NGN). Total cost: 44,100 to 65,800 NGN. If you sell 10 piglets at 15,000 NGN each, revenue is 150,000 NGN. Profit is 84,200 to 105,900 NGN. In South Africa, a 25 kg bag of milk replacer costs 800 to 1,200 ZAR. A 25 kg bag of creep feed costs 400 to 600 ZAR. For 10 piglets, you need 17.5 kg of milk replacer (560 to 840 ZAR) and 56 kg of creep feed (896 to 1,344 ZAR). Total cost: 1,456 to 2,184 ZAR. If you sell 10 piglets at 600 ZAR each, revenue is 6,000 ZAR. Profit is 3,816 to 4,544 ZAR. Scaling up: If you have 10 sows, you will have about 100 piglets at a time. You will need a dedicated milk replacer mixing area, a refrigerator for storing colostrum, a digital scale, a thermometer, and a clean water supply. You will need to train workers on hygiene and feeding. You will need to keep records of feed intake, weight gain, and mortality. A simple record book is enough. Write down the date, the number of piglets, the amount of milk replacer mixed, the amount of creep feed offered, and any deaths. Review the records weekly. If mortality is above 10 percent, find the cause. If growth is below 150 grams per day, find the cause. Common mistakes when scaling up: buying cheap milk replacer that is low in protein and energy (check the label: it should have 20 to 24 percent protein and 15 to 20 percent fat), storing milk replacer in a hot room (above 25 degrees Celsius causes spoilage), mixing with dirty water, feeding cold milk, and not cleaning troughs. A good milk replacer should contain skim milk powder, whey powder, vegetable oil, and a vitamin-mineral premix. It should not contain high levels of starch or fibre. Denkavit, MaxCare, and Royal Canin are reliable brands. Local brands from South Africa (e.g., Meadow Feeds) and Kenya (e.g., Unga) are also good. If you make your own, use a recipe from a university extension service. The National Academies Press (NAP) publishes nutrient requirements for swine, which is the gold standard. FAO publishes practical guides for smallholder pig production. Teagasc publishes research on piglet feeding. Use these sources. With good management, piglet milk replacer and creep feed can reduce mortality from 30 percent to below 10 percent and increase weaning weight by 1 to 2 kg. That is the difference between a profitable pig farm and a struggling one.
