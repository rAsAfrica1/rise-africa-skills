🐠 Aquaculture Feeding Business and Economics

The arithmetic of making money from fish. Measure feed efficiency properly, build a cost per kilogram of fish produced, work out what water quality is costing you in lost revenue, compare systems on the numbers, price your fish deliberately and put a figure on what growth would require.

What you will be able to do after this module

  • Measure feed conversion correctly, including mortality and feed waste
  • Build a complete cost per kilogram of fish produced from your own figures
  • Express water quality limits as a ceiling on standing biomass and therefore on revenue
  • Compare pond, tank, raceway and recirculating systems on density, FCR, waste and break-even price
  • Set a selling price from your own cost per kilogram rather than from rumour
  • Calculate the working capital a larger cycle actually requires before committing to it

Lesson 8.1~11 min

Feed Conversion Efficiency

In this lesson
  • Measure feed conversion correctly, including mortality and feed waste
  • Convert a biological FCR into an economic feed cost per kilogram of gain
  • Identify which part of a cycle is wasting feed by calculating FCR phase by phase

Feed conversion is where the money is decided, and most farms measure it once at the end of a cycle, if at all. That is too late to change anything. This lesson is about measuring it properly and converting the number into cash.

The formula is total feed used in kilograms divided by weight gain in kilograms. One thousand kilograms of feed producing six hundred kilograms of gain is an FCR of 1.67. Three details decide whether your number is honest.

First, gain means final biomass minus stocked biomass, not final biomass. If you stock 1,000 fingerlings at 20 g, that is 20 kg going in, and it must be subtracted.

Second, mortality is included whether you like it or not. Fish that died ate feed. If survival is below 90 percent, your FCR looks worse than the biology, and that is not a bookkeeping error, it is the true cost of the loss.

Third, feed waste is inside the number. Documented waste by system: extensive ponds 15-25 percent, semi-intensive ponds 10-15 percent, intensive ponds 5-10 percent, static tanks 5-8 percent, raceways and recirculating systems 2-5 percent. Floating pellets waste 15-25 percent against 5-10 percent for sinking pellets, because floating feed drifts and is taken by birds once the fish are satisfied, though it does let you see when to stop.

What you should be achieving at good temperature. African catfish fingerlings of 10-50 g at 25-28 C run 1.2-1.4 and grow-out 1.4-1.7. Nile tilapia fingerlings of 20-50 g at 27-30 C run 1.3-1.5, juveniles of 50-200 g 1.5-1.8 and grow-out of 200-500 g 1.6-2.0. Channel catfish run 1.4-1.6 as fingerlings and 1.8-2.2 at grow-out. Carp run 1.5-1.8 and 2.0-2.5. Below 20 C, tilapia collapse to 3.0-5.0 and channel catfish to 3.5-6.0.

Measure it in phases rather than once. Weigh a sample of at least thirty fish every two weeks, record total feed given between weighings, and calculate FCR for that fortnight. Fish are most efficient when small: tilapia at 1.3-1.5 as fingerlings against 1.6-2.0 near market size. Seeing a fortnight where FCR jumps tells you exactly when something went wrong, and it is almost always a water quality event, a temperature drop or an overfeeding week.

Now the money. Multiply feed cost per kilogram by FCR and you get feed cost per kilogram of fish gained.

  • Feed at USD 0.70/kg, FCR 2.0: USD 1.40 of feed per kilogram of fish.
  • Same feed, FCR 1.6: USD 1.12.
  • Home-mixed feed at USD 0.44/kg, FCR 1.7: USD 0.75.

Against a smallholder break-even fish price of USD 0.80-1.50/kg, the first line is already close to unviable before you have paid for a single fingerling.

Protein retention explains the physical limit. Only 28-34 percent of feed protein is retained in fish tissue; the other 66-72 percent leaves as ammonia and faeces. That is why overfeeding protein does not produce more fish, it produces more ammonia, which then makes FCR worse. It is also why improving protein quality and digestibility improves FCR more reliably than raising protein level.

Worked example, Uganda. A farmer at Mukono stocks 2,000 tilapia fingerlings at 25 g, so 50 kg. He harvests 1,700 fish at 320 g, so 544 kg. Gain is 544 minus 50 = 494 kg. He used 940 kg of feed, so FCR is 940 / 494 = 1.90. Survival is 1,700 / 2,000 = 85 percent, which is what has pushed the number to the top of the tilapia band. At USD 0.70/kg his feed cost per kilogram of fish gained is 1.90 x 0.70 = USD 1.33. If he lifts survival to 95 percent by grading and by fixing the dawn oxygen problem, harvest becomes 1,900 fish at 320 g, so 608 kg, gain 558 kg, and with the same feed FCR falls to 1.68 and feed cost per kilogram of gain to USD 1.18. Nothing was bought.

Lesson 8.2~12 min

Production Economics

In this lesson
  • Build a complete cost per kilogram of fish produced from your own figures
  • Calculate gross margin per pond per cycle
  • Test how far your farm can absorb a feed price rise before it stops paying

A fish farm has one number that matters more than any other: what it costs you to produce one kilogram of fish. Everything else is commentary.

Build it from six lines.

  1. Fingerlings. Number stocked multiplied by price each.
  2. Feed. Kilograms used multiplied by price per kilogram. This is 50-70 percent of production cost in aquaculture, and 60-70 percent of revenue on a small-scale pond in Uganda, Egypt or Vietnam.
  3. Labour. Hours multiplied by a real local wage, including your own time.
  4. Energy and water. Pumping, aeration, transport.
  5. Pond preparation, liming, fertiliser, treatments.
  6. Depreciation. Nets, liners, tanks, mill, divided by their expected years of life.

Divide the total by kilograms harvested and you have your cost per kilogram.

Worked example, Nigeria. A farmer in Ogun State runs one 400 square metre earthen pond of African catfish for a six-month cycle.

  • Fingerlings: 3,000 at USD 0.06 = USD 180.
  • Feed: harvest is 2,550 fish at 450 g, so 1,148 kg. Stocked biomass at 15 g is 45 kg, so gain is 1,103 kg. At FCR 1.6 that is 1,765 kg of feed. At USD 0.55/kg home-mixed, USD 971.
  • Labour: 2 hours a day for 180 days at USD 1.20 an hour = USD 432.
  • Energy and water: USD 90.
  • Pond preparation and treatments: USD 60.
  • Depreciation on nets, buckets and a small mill: USD 80 for the cycle.

Total cost: 180 + 971 + 432 + 90 + 60 + 80 = USD 1,813. Divided by 1,148 kg, that is USD 1.58 per kilogram produced.

Feed is 971 / 1,813 = 53.6 percent of cost, at the low end of the 50-70 percent band precisely because he mixes his own.

Now revenue and margin. If he sells at USD 2.20/kg farm gate, revenue is 1,148 x 2.20 = USD 2,526. Gross margin is 2,526 minus 1,813 = USD 713 for the pond for the cycle, or USD 0.62 per kilogram. Two cycles a year on that pond is roughly USD 1,426.

That single figure, margin per pond per cycle, is the unit you should think in. It tells you what a second pond would be worth, what a week of neglect costs, and whether the enterprise is worth your labour at all.

Now the stress test, which is the part most farms never do. Feed is over half the cost, so a feed price rise hits harder than anything else.

  • Feed price up 15 percent: feed cost 971 x 1.15 = USD 1,117, total cost USD 1,959, cost per kilogram USD 1.71, margin USD 567.
  • Feed price up 30 percent: feed cost USD 1,262, total cost USD 2,104, cost per kilogram USD 1.83, margin USD 422.
  • Feed price up 30 percent and the fish price falls 10 percent to USD 1.98: revenue USD 2,273, margin USD 169. The pond is now barely paying for the farmer's own labour, which is already counted at USD 432.

The historical evidence says this is not hypothetical. In the 2008 feed price crisis, prices rose 30-40 percent. Vietnamese catfish farmers could absorb only about a 15 percent rise before margins disappeared; Thai tilapia farmers could absorb about 31 percent. Many small producers exited and the industry consolidated. Break-even fish prices by farm type are USD 0.80-1.50/kg for a small-scale pond, USD 1.20-2.00 for a commercial raceway and USD 4.00-8.00 for an intensive tank or recirculating system, and the reference data judges the small pond viable up to a 10-15 percent feed price rise and marginal beyond 20 percent.

So write down your own absorption number. Take your margin, divide by your feed cost, and express it as a percentage. In the example above, USD 713 of margin against USD 971 of feed means the farm can absorb a 73 percent feed price rise before margin reaches zero, which is comfortable, and that comfort comes almost entirely from mixing his own feed at USD 0.55 instead of buying at USD 0.75. Module 12 covers what to do about price risk once you know your exposure.

Water Quality and Productivity

In this lesson
  • Express water quality limits as a ceiling on standing biomass and therefore on revenue
  • Cost the FCR penalties caused by low oxygen and high ammonia
  • Decide whether aeration buys more kilograms than it costs

Water quality is usually taught as a fish health subject. It is really a revenue subject, because oxygen and ammonia set a hard ceiling on how many kilograms of fish a pond can carry, and kilograms are what you sell. Module 9 covers the management detail; this lesson is about what those limits cost.

Start with the ceiling. Documented stocking by system: extensive ponds carry 1-5 kg per hectare of standing crop supported by natural productivity, semi-intensive 5-50 kg/ha, intensive 50-150 kg/ha with aeration required, static tanks 100-200 kg per cubic metre, raceways 50-150 kg/m3 and recirculating systems 200-500 kg/m3 and above. What moves a pond from one band to the next is not feed, it is the ability to keep oxygen up and ammonia down.

Now the penalties, which are the part with a price on them.

  1. Dissolved oxygen below 4 mg/L: FCR worsens 15-30 percent. This is the largest single penalty in the whole data set.
  2. Dissolved oxygen below 5 mg/L for tilapia grow-out: reduced feed intake and FCR worse by 15-30 percent.
  3. Ammonia above 0.1 mg/L: FCR worse by 5-10 percent, and for Nile tilapia at 0.1 mg/L growth falls 25-30 percent with reduced feed intake and fin erosion. At 0.05 mg/L, growth is already down 10-15 percent. At 0.02 mg/L there is measurable immune depression and 2-5 percent slower growth.
  4. Stocking above about 100 tilapia per cubic metre: FCR worse by 10-15 percent from stress and feeding competition.

Safe thresholds to work to: ammonia below 0.05 mg/L for Nile tilapia and African catfish and below 0.1 mg/L for channel catfish and carp; nitrite below 0.5 mg/L for tilapia and carp and below 1 mg/L for channel catfish; dissolved oxygen above 5 mg/L for grow-out and above 6 mg/L for fry. African catfish is the exception that makes it popular: it breathes air and survives down to 1.0 mg/L, though it grows poorly there and prefers above 4 mg/L.

Put a price on it. Take the Nigerian pond from Lesson 2: 1,765 kg of feed at USD 0.55, so USD 971, producing 1,103 kg of gain at FCR 1.6.

  • If dawn oxygen regularly drops below 4 mg/L and FCR worsens 20 percent to 1.92, the same 1,103 kg of gain now needs 2,118 kg of feed, costing USD 1,165. That is USD 194 lost from a USD 713 margin, 27 percent of the profit, from one water quality fault.
  • If ammonia also sits above 0.1 mg/L, add another 5-10 percent, so roughly USD 60-120 more.
  • Add reduced feed intake and the fish simply do not reach 450 g in six months, so you either feed longer or sell smaller.

Against that, the cost of fixing it. Reducing feeding 20-30 percent at dawn and after sunset, when oxygen is lowest, is free. Removing dead fish and uneaten feed is free. Cutting the ration 20-30 percent whenever ammonia exceeds 0.1 mg/L or oxygen falls below 5 mg/L is free and actually saves feed. Adding salt at 1-2 g/L to protect against nitrite toxicity costs almost nothing and halves nitrite toxicity by competing for gill chloride channels. Water exchange of 10-20 percent weekly for a semi-intensive pond costs pumping fuel only.

The formulation side is also a water quality lever. Every 5 percent reduction in dietary protein cuts ammonia output 15-20 percent, so a farm running a 35 percent protein feed on grow-out tilapia that only needs 25-32 percent is paying twice, once for the protein and once for the ammonia it creates. Improving digestibility above 80 percent cuts faecal nitrogen. Reducing feed waste cuts sediment ammonia.

Worked example, Kenya. A farmer at Kirinyaga has a 600 square metre pond that repeatedly loses fish before dawn. He shifts his 06:00 feed to 09:00, splits the daily ration across four feeds instead of two, drops the ration by 25 percent on any morning the water smells or looks dark, and adds a weekly 15 percent water exchange. His FCR moves from 2.1 to 1.75 over one cycle. On 900 kg of gain that is 1,890 kg of feed instead of 2,268 kg, saving 378 kg at USD 0.55, or USD 208, from decisions that cost nothing but attention.

System Selection and Economics

In this lesson
  • Compare pond, tank, raceway and recirculating systems on density, FCR, waste and break-even price
  • Match a system to the capital and the market you actually have
  • Judge whether feeding equipment pays back on your volume

The system you farm in sets your costs before you buy a single fingerling. Choosing it on the numbers rather than on ambition is the difference between a farm and a lesson.

The comparison, from the reference data.

  1. Extensive pond: 1-5 kg per hectare, manual broadcast once or twice daily, low labour, 15-25 percent feed waste, FCR 2.5-3.5. Feed is a supplement to natural pond productivity, supported by fertilisation at an N:P ratio of 3:1.
  2. Semi-intensive pond: 5-50 kg/ha, hand-fed two or three times daily to satiation, moderate labour, 10-15 percent waste, FCR 1.8-2.2. Weekly water exchange of 10-20 percent. This is the typical smallholder position.
  3. Intensive pond: 50-150 kg/ha, hand-fed three or four times daily plus a spreader, high labour, 5-10 percent waste, FCR 1.5-1.8. Aeration required and 25-40 percent water exchange two or three times a week.
  4. Static tank: 100-200 kg per cubic metre, hand-fed two to four times daily, high daily labour, 5-8 percent waste, FCR 1.4-1.7, with 10-30 percent daily water exchange.
  5. Recirculating system: 200-500 kg/m3 and above, automatic or demand feeding, low labour but daily monitoring, 2-5 percent waste, FCR 1.2-1.5. The biofilter is unforgiving of overfeeding.
  6. Raceway: 50-150 kg/m3, automatic demand feeding, low labour, 2-5 percent waste, FCR 1.3-1.6, but it needs constant river or spring water.

Read that list alongside break-even fish prices and the picture completes itself. A small-scale pond breaks even at USD 0.80-1.50/kg with feed at 60-70 percent of revenue and can absorb only a 10-15 percent feed price rise. A commercial raceway breaks even at USD 1.20-2.00/kg with feed at 50-60 percent and absorbs 25-30 percent. An intensive tank or recirculating system breaks even at USD 4.00-8.00/kg with feed at 40-50 percent and can absorb over 40 percent, because it shifts cost onto a premium market.

That last line is the one that catches people. A recirculating system has the best FCR and the least waste on the list, and it is still the wrong choice for most African smallholders, because it only works if you can reliably sell fish at four to eight dollars a kilogram. If your market pays USD 2.20, the technology cannot save you.

So the honest decision sequence is: find out what your market pays, work back to the break-even band that price supports, and choose the system that sits inside it. Then spend on moving up one step within that system rather than jumping bands.

Within a system, the improvement with the clearest arithmetic is feeding equipment. Demand feeders in field trials improved FCR from 1.5 to 1.3, a 13 percent gain, raised growth rate substantially, improved survival by 8-10 percent through reduced overfeeding, shortened the production cycle by about four weeks from eighteen to fourteen, and raised yield by around 30 percent. Equipment costs USD 500-2,000 per feeder. Feed savings of 10-15 percent equate to USD 0.03-0.10 per kilogram of feed, so break-even comes at 5,000-20,000 kg of feed consumed, which is one to two years on a small pond. The return is rated 1:1 to 3:1 for intensive farms and marginal for extensive ponds under 5 kg/ha, which is exactly what you would expect: equipment pays where volume is high.

Sinking versus floating pellets is a smaller but free decision. Sinking pellets waste 5-10 percent and cost USD 0.20-0.50/kg less to make because they need no lipid or binder premium for buoyancy. Floating pellets waste 15-25 percent but let you watch the fish and stop when they stop. In a tank or raceway, prefer sinking. In a pond where you cannot see the fish, the observation value of floating feed may be worth the waste, particularly while you are still learning your ration.

Worked example, Tanzania. A farmer at Morogoro has two semi-intensive ponds using 3,500 kg of feed a year at FCR 2.0. A demand feeder at USD 700 would take him toward 1.75, saving about 440 kg of feed a year, worth USD 242 at USD 0.55/kg, so payback lands just under three years, slower than the quoted one to two because his volume is low. He instead spends USD 120 on a second-hand aerator hire arrangement and splitting his feeding into four daily rations, capturing much of the same FCR gain for a sixth of the money. Equipment is a volume decision. Module 12 Lesson 3 works through on-farm milling equipment on the same basis.

Marketing and Market Positioning

In this lesson
  • Set a selling price from your own cost per kilogram rather than from rumour
  • Choose harvest size and form to move up the price band your market offers
  • Use feed decisions to deliver the consistency buyers actually pay for

Most small fish farms sell whatever comes out of the net to whoever turns up. That is not a market position, it is an accident, and it is usually the difference between a thin margin and a good one.

Start from your own number. Lesson 2 gave a cost of USD 1.58 per kilogram produced on a home-mixed feed. Any price below that loses money regardless of what the neighbours are charging. Any price above it earns margin, and the size of the gap is your position. Write your cost per kilogram on a card and carry it to every negotiation, because a buyer who offers a price below your cost is offering you work, not business.

Then understand the bands. Break-even fish prices are USD 0.80-1.50/kg for small-scale ponds, USD 1.20-2.00 for commercial raceways and USD 4.00-8.00 for intensive tank systems. Those bands exist because different buyers pay different prices for what is nominally the same fish. Moving between them is a marketing decision, not a production one.

Four levers you actually control.

  1. Size at harvest. Tilapia reach 500 g in six to eight months at 25-30 C, and African catfish grow-out spans 50-500 g at FCR 1.4-1.7. Larger fish usually command a higher price per kilogram, but the last stage of growth is also the least efficient: tilapia FCR runs 1.3-1.5 as fingerlings and 1.6-2.0 from 200-500 g. So growing to a bigger size costs more per kilogram of gain, and it only pays if the price step is real. Ask your buyers for their price at 300 g, 400 g and 500 g before you decide, and compare the price difference with the extra feed cost per kilogram of the last growth phase.
  2. Timing. Harvesting when everyone else harvests puts you into the worst price of the year. Because tilapia below 20 C run FCR 3.0-5.0 and channel catfish 3.5-6.0, growing through a cold season to hit a high-price window is usually a bad trade; adjusting stocking dates so the growth happens in warm water and the sale falls in the scarce season is a good one.
  3. Form. Fresh whole fish, iced, gutted, smoked or dried are different products with different prices and different shelf lives. Smoking or drying converts a perishable harvest into something you can hold for a better price, at the cost of processing labour and weight loss. Cost the labour honestly at your local wage before assuming it pays.
  4. Consistency. This is where feed reaches the market. Buyers pay for a uniform size grade and predictable supply. Grading, even ration delivery and stable water quality produce that uniformity, and the same practices reduce the 10-15 percent FCR penalty that comes from feeding competition at high density.

One further product deserves naming. Fingerlings sell at USD 0.03-0.10 each and a broodstock unit producing 100,000 fry represents USD 3,000-10,000 of value against a feed cost of USD 0.001-0.005 per larva, which the reference data rates at a 300-1,000 percent return. If there is unmet fingerling demand near you, it is frequently the highest-margin product a small farm can make. Module 11 covers that enterprise in full.

Worked example, Ghana. A farmer at Kpong produces 1,100 kg of tilapia a cycle at a cost of USD 1.55/kg, so USD 1,705. Selling everything at the pond gate to one trader at USD 2.00 gives USD 2,200 and a margin of USD 495. He changes three things. He grades at harvest and sells the top 400 kg of 400 g fish directly to two restaurants at USD 2.70, giving USD 1,080. He sells 500 kg to the same trader at USD 2.00, USD 1,000. He smokes the remaining 200 kg of smaller fish, losing weight in processing but selling the output for USD 380 after USD 40 of labour and fuel. Total revenue is USD 2,460 against USD 1,745 of cost including the processing, so margin is USD 715, up from USD 495. He produced exactly the same fish. Marketing added USD 220, a 44 percent margin increase, for the cost of a grading net and two conversations.

The discipline is simple: know your cost, know what each buyer pays, and stop treating your harvest as one undifferentiated pile.

Scaling and Growth Strategy

In this lesson
  • Calculate the working capital a larger cycle actually requires before committing to it
  • Rank growth options by return per dollar invested rather than by size
  • Set the sequence of investments that lowers feed cost before volume is increased

Growth on a fish farm is bought with working capital, and working capital is mostly feed. Understanding that one sentence prevents most of the failures that follow a decision to expand.

Start with the cash requirement. Take the Nigerian pond from Lesson 2: 1,765 kg of feed at USD 0.55 is USD 971, plus USD 180 of fingerlings, spent across a six-month cycle before a single fish is sold. Doubling to two ponds doubles that to roughly USD 2,300 of cash that must be in hand and cannot come back until harvest. A farmer with USD 700 of savings and a margin of USD 713 per cycle cannot fund a second pond from one cycle's profit; it takes two, or it takes credit, and credit priced above the margin destroys the reason for expanding.

So the first rule of scaling is: lower your cost per kilogram before you raise your volume, because every improvement is then multiplied by the larger volume, and because the cheaper feed reduces the working capital the expansion needs.

The documented sequence, with investment, payback and feed cost reduction.

  1. Buy premixes and hand-mix feed on the farm. No cash investment. Immediate payback. 15-20 percent feed cost reduction.
  2. Join or form a cooperative for bulk ingredient buying. Organisational cost only. Immediate payback. 10-15 percent reduction.
  3. Ferment plant ingredients locally. USD 50-100 for containers and starter. Two to three months. A further 5-10 percent.
  4. Establish a BSFL colony or algae culture. USD 200-500. Three to six months. A further 10-15 percent.
  5. Buy a small pellet mill of 2-3 kW. USD 500-1,500. Six to twelve months. A further 5-10 percent.

Run that on the example farm. Feed cost of USD 971 a cycle, two cycles a year, so USD 1,942. Steps one and two together, at 25-35 percent, save USD 485-680 a year for no equipment. Add fermentation for USD 80 and save perhaps another USD 150. That is USD 635-830 of extra annual margin without a new pond, without extra fingerlings, without extra risk, and without a single extra kilogram of fish to sell.

Compare that with the alternative use of the money. A second pond at USD 713 of margin per cycle, two cycles, is USD 1,426 a year, but it requires roughly USD 1,150 of working capital per cycle, more labour, and it carries full production risk. The efficiency route returns most of that gain for under USD 100 and no risk. Do it first. Then expand.

The second rule is to rank options by return per dollar rather than by scale. Amino acid supplementation costs USD 0.02-0.05/kg of feed and returns 2 to 5 to 1. Phytase costs USD 0.03-0.10/kg and returns 1.5 to 3 to 1. Probiotics cost USD 0.02-0.10/kg and return 1 to 2 to 1. Immunostimulants at USD 0.05-0.20/kg return 0.5 to 1 to 1, which means they mostly do not pay unless disease is your binding constraint. Fish oil in grow-out feed and specialty amino acids such as taurine are rated poor value for smallholders outright. Work down that list in order and stop where the return falls below what the money would earn elsewhere on your farm.

The third rule is that scale changes your water quality problem before it changes your income. Moving a pond from semi-intensive at 5-50 kg/ha to intensive at 50-150 kg/ha requires aeration and 25-40 percent water exchange two or three times a week, and if you increase stocking without those, the 15-30 percent FCR penalty from low oxygen will take back more than the extra fish are worth. Density above about 100 tilapia per cubic metre costs 10-15 percent of FCR by itself. Scale the water management first, then the fish.

Worked example, Zambia. A farmer at Chisamba with three ponds plans to build a fourth for USD 900. Instead he spends USD 80 on fermentation drums, joins four neighbours to buy soya and premix by the tonne, and starts a BSFL unit for USD 300. His feed cost falls from USD 0.62 to USD 0.44/kg. Across 5,500 kg of feed a year that is USD 990 saved, more than the fourth pond would have earned in its first year, for USD 380 spent, and his working capital requirement per cycle has fallen rather than risen. He builds the fourth pond the following year out of the savings, funded rather than borrowed.

Recirculating systems waste 2-5 percent; extensive ponds waste 15-25 percent, all of it inside your FCR.

The rest leaves as ammonia and faeces, which is why overfeeding protein worsens water quality rather than growth.

Phase-by-phase FCR shows you which fortnight went wrong while you can still act on it.

5. Raising survival from 85 to 95 percent with the same feed:

Fish that died still ate; converting those losses into harvest is a free FCR improvement.

It is the dominant cost, and 60-70 percent of revenue on a small-scale pond.

It is the largest single water quality penalty, larger than ammonia or stocking density.

It has the best FCR and least waste on the list, but the price it needs is above most local markets.

Any price below your cost per kilogram loses money no matter what anyone else is doing.

Every efficiency gain is multiplied by the larger volume and it reduces the working capital an expansion needs.

Module 8 capstone

Produce a one-page economic model of a single pond or tank over one cycle, using only your own numbers, and then test it. Record: number of fingerlings stocked and their cost, average stocking weight, total feed used and its cost per kilogram, the number and weight of fish harvested, labour hours and their value, energy and water costs, and any pond preparation or treatment cost. From these calculate survival rate, total weight gain, biological FCR, feed cost per kilogram of fish gained, total cost per kilogram of fish produced, revenue at your actual selling price, and gross margin for the pond for the cycle. Then run three sensitivity tests on the same page. First, what happens to margin if the feed price rises 15 percent and then 30 percent, and identify the point at which the pond stops making money. Second, what happens if FCR moves to the good end of the band for your species and what that is worth in cash. Third, what happens if you sell at a 10 percent higher price by changing size, timing or form. Write the three answers in one sentence each and decide which of the three levers you will pull first this cycle.
