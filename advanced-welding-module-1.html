1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Advanced welding is not just about joining metal; it is about creating strong, safe, and lasting structures that can withstand the demands of African industry, construction, and repair work. Whether you are welding a trailer chassis in Nairobi, repairing a water tank in Lagos, or building a gate in Accra, the quality of your weld determines safety and durability. Basic welding gets two pieces stuck together. Advanced welding ensures the joint can carry loads, resist vibration, and survive years of exposure to sun, rain, and dust. In many African markets, skilled welders who can work with different metals and positions earn 2-3 times more than general welders. For example, a certified welder on a pipeline project in Tanzania can earn $30-$50 per day, while an unskilled welder might earn $5-$10. This module takes you beyond simple flat-position stick welding. You will learn to select the right process for the job, prepare metal properly, control heat, and troubleshoot defects. You will also learn safety practices that prevent injury and death. Welding is dangerous: electric shock, fumes, burns, and eye damage are real risks. But with the right techniques and gear, you can work safely and productively. By the end of this module, you will be able to make high-quality welds in various positions, identify and fix common defects, and apply best practices on any job site. This knowledge is your ticket to better pay and more respect in the trade. Lets get started.

2. Tools and Materials

To do advanced welding, you need more than a basic arc welder and a chipping hammer. Start with a reliable welding machine. For stick welding (SMAW), a 200-amp inverter welder works well on 220V single-phase, common in many African workshops. For MIG welding, a 200-amp MIG with gas capability is ideal. You will also need an angle grinder with 115mm or 230mm discs for cutting and grinding. A wire brush, chipping hammer, and slag hammer are essential for cleaning welds. For measuring and marking, get a steel tape, combination square, and soapstone or marker. Clamps (C-clamps, magnetic clamps) hold parts in place. Personal protective equipment (PPE) is non-negotiable: auto-darkening welding helmet with at least shade 10-13, leather gloves, flame-resistant jacket, safety boots, and a respirator for fumes. For materials, mild steel is the most common and easiest to weld. You will also encounter stainless steel (needs different electrodes and lower heat) and aluminum (requires AC TIG or spool gun MIG). Electrodes: for mild steel, use E6013 for general work and E7018 for high-strength joints. For stainless, use 308L or 316L rods. Filler wire for MIG: ER70S-6 for mild steel. Shielding gas: for MIG, use 75% argon/25% CO2 mix for steel; for TIG, pure argon. Always store electrodes in a dry place; moisture causes porosity. In Africa, you can find these tools at hardware stores in major cities or order online. Invest in quality; cheap tools cost more in downtime and bad welds.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps for a strong advanced weld. First, prepare the metal. Clean it with a grinder to bare shiny metal, removing rust, paint, and oil. Bevel edges for thick metal (over 6mm) to ensure penetration. Set your machine: for 3.2mm E6013 electrode on mild steel, set around 90-120 amps. For 4.0mm, use 120-160 amps. For MIG on 6mm steel, set voltage to 20-22V and wire speed to 4-5 m/min. Clamp your work securely. Second, tack weld the pieces at both ends and middle to prevent distortion. Third, start welding. For stick, strike the arc like a match, then maintain a short arc (arc length about equal to electrode diameter). Use a drag angle of 10-15 degrees. For a flat butt weld, move in a straight line or slight weave. For vertical up, use a triangle or Z weave, pausing at edges. For overhead, use lower amperage and faster travel. Fourth, control heat input. If the metal gets too hot (turns blue or grey), stop and let it cool. Overheating causes warping and weakens the weld. Fifth, after welding, clean the slag with a chipping hammer and wire brush. Inspect for defects: cracks, porosity, undercut. If you find a defect, grind it out and re-weld. Finally, let the weld cool naturally; do not quench with water unless specified, as rapid cooling can cause cracking. Practice each position on scrap metal before doing a real job. Remember, consistency in travel speed, angle, and arc length is key.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Even experienced welders make mistakes. Here are common ones and how to fix them. 1) Porosity: small holes in the weld caused by moisture, oil, or too long an arc. Fix: clean metal, use dry electrodes, and shorten arc. 2) Undercut: a groove melted into the base metal along the weld toe. Caused by too high amperage or wrong angle. Fix: reduce amps, adjust angle to 10-15 degrees, and use a slight weave. 3) Slag inclusion: trapped slag inside the weld. Happens when you don't clean between passes or use wrong polarity. Fix: clean thoroughly, use correct polarity (electrode positive for E6013). 4) Spatter: small metal droplets stuck to the workpiece. Caused by too high amperage or long arc. Fix: reduce amps, shorten arc, use anti-spatter spray. 5) Warping: metal bends due to uneven heating. Fix: clamp securely, use tack welds, alternate welding sides, and allow cooling. 6) Incomplete penetration: weld doesn't reach the root. Fix: increase root gap, higher amps, slower travel. 7) Cracking: often from rapid cooling or high carbon steel. Fix: preheat thick steel, use low-hydrogen electrodes like E7018, and cool slowly. 8) Wrong electrode angle: leads to uneven beads. Fix: maintain 10-15 degree drag angle. Always inspect your work with a flashlight and magnifying glass. If you find a defect, grind it out completely and re-weld. Never try to cover up a defect with another pass; it will fail. Practice on scrap until you can consistently produce clean welds.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Welding safety is not optional; it saves lives. In Africa, many workshops lack proper ventilation, so you must protect yourself. Always wear an auto-darkening helmet with correct shade: shade 10 for low amps, 13 for high amps. Never look at the arc without protection; it causes arc eye (painful burns to the cornea). Wear leather gloves, a flame-resistant long-sleeve jacket, and safety boots. Use a respirator when welding galvanized or painted metal, as fumes are toxic. Ensure good ventilation: open doors and windows, use a fan to blow fumes away, but not directly on the weld (it disrupts shielding gas). Keep a fire extinguisher nearby and a bucket of sand or water. Never weld near flammable materials. Check your cables for damage; exposed wires cause electric shock. Ground the work clamp securely. Work on a dry surface, not wet ground. When welding overhead, wear a cap and ear plugs to keep sparks out. Keep your work area clean and organized. Store gas cylinders upright and secured. Never weld a closed container; it can explode. Best practices: plan your weld, use the right process for the metal, and don't rush. Take breaks to avoid fatigue. Stay hydrated. If you feel dizzy, stop and get fresh air. Finally, get trained in first aid for burns and electric shock. Your safety is your responsibility.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it's time to practice. This exercise will help you apply what you've learned. You will need: mild steel plates (100mm x 100mm x 6mm), a stick welder (200A), E6013 electrodes (3.2mm), angle grinder, wire brush, chipping hammer, clamps, and PPE. Task 1: Butt weld in flat position. Clean two plates, bevel edges if needed, tack weld at both ends. Set amperage to 100-120A. Weld a straight bead along the joint. Clean slag, inspect for porosity and undercut. Task 2: T-joint in horizontal position. Place one plate vertical on another, tack weld. Weld along the joint using a slight weave. Use 110-130A. Task 3: Vertical up weld. Set two plates vertically with a gap, tack. Weld from bottom to top using a triangle weave, pausing at edges. Use 90-110A. Task 4: Overhead weld. Position plates overhead (secure safely). Use 80-100A, short arc, fast travel. After each task, grind out any defects and re-weld. Record your settings and observations. Aim for consistent bead width and penetration. If you don't have a welder, visit a local workshop and ask to practice under supervision. Alternatively, use a welding simulator app. After completing, compare your welds to the examples in the module. Take photos and share with your instructor or peers for feedback. Repeat until you can produce clean, strong welds in all positions. This practice is the foundation for advanced certification.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Advanced Welding for African Fabrication Businesses

This practical reference is the working companion to Beyond the Basics in the Advanced Welding Techniques course. It is written for the African fabricator, workshop owner, or technical trainer who needs repeatable results on the bench and on site whether you are welding a maize sheller frame in Kumasi, a water tank in Nairobi, a truck body in Lagos, or a gate in Gaborone. The focus is on decisions that protect your margin: correct process selection, sound consumable choices, controlled distortion, and safe practice that keeps your team working and your insurance valid.

Advanced welding is not about exotic machines. It is about controlling five variables: process, current, travel speed, joint preparation, and heat input. When those are controlled, you reduce rework, cut consumable waste, and win repeat contracts. In many African markets, the difference between a profitable fabrication shop and one that struggles is not the price of steel it is the cost of repair welding, rejected work, and downtime.

Why this matters in the African context

Across the continent, fabrication demand is rising with infrastructure, agro-processing, transport, and mining. At the same time, power supply can be unstable, consumables may be imported at high cost, and skilled welders are in short supply. A business-first approach means standardising on processes you can power reliably (for example, inverter-based SMAW or FCAW where generator support is available), training your team on one or two procedures done well, and documenting settings so results do not depend on one person's memory.

Process selection at a glance

Use the table below to match the job to the process. In most African workshops, SMAW (stick) and FCAW (flux-cored) dominate structural and repair work because they tolerate wind, rust, and less-than-perfect fit-up. GMAW (MIG/MAG) is faster for production but needs clean metal and shielding gas. TIG is for precision, thin sections, and aluminium or stainless where appearance and code compliance matter.

SAFETY WARNING FUME, ARC FLASH AND ELECTRIC SHOCK

Welding fume contains metal oxides and gases that can cause lung damage, metal fume fever, and in the case of stainless steel, hexavalent chromium. Arc flash can cause permanent eye damage ("arc eye") within seconds. Wet conditions, damaged cables, and poor earthing cause electrocution. Never weld without appropriate fume extraction or forced ventilation, a proper welding helmet with correct shade, dry gloves, and an inspected earth clamp. In confined spaces such as tanks, silos, and vehicle bodies, use a fume extractor and a trained spotter. Do not weld on containers that have held fuel or chemicals without certified cleaning and gas testing. This is a legal requirement under most national occupational safety laws and is enforced by inspectors in Kenya, Nigeria, South Africa, Ghana, and elsewhere.

Step-by-step practical method

  1. Plan the joint and the sequence. Decide weld order to balance heat and control distortion. On long frames, use back-step or skip welding.
  2. Prepare the metal. Remove rust, mill scale, oil, paint, and moisture at least 25 mm from the joint. Poor preparation is the number one cause of porosity and lack of fusion.
  3. Set up the machine. Confirm polarity, current range, and cable condition. For E7018, use DCEP and a dry electrode (keep low-hydrogen electrodes in a heated quiver or sealed container).
  4. Tack and check alignment. Use short tacks at the ends and midpoints. Check square, level, and dimensions before full welding.
  5. Run the root pass. Keep a consistent arc length and travel speed. For open roots, use a keyhole technique or backing strip depending on the procedure.
  6. Clean between passes. Chip slag, wire brush, and inspect. Trapped slag causes defects that fail inspection and cost you the contract.
  7. Fill and cap. Control interpass temperature. For thick sections, avoid overheating the base metal, which can reduce toughness.
  8. Inspect and document. Visual inspection, then dye penetrant or ultrasonic where required. Record settings and welder ID for traceability.

Common mistakes and fixes

  • Porosity: Usually moisture, oil, or paint. Fix by cleaning and using dry electrodes.
  • Undercut: Current too high or travel too fast. Reduce current or slow down; adjust angle.
  • Lack of fusion: Travel too fast, current too low, or wrong angle. Increase heat input and direct the arc at the joint root.
  • Distortion: Poor sequence or too much heat. Use clamps, tacks, and balanced welding.
  • Slag inclusions: Skipping cleaning between passes. Chip and brush every pass.

Business-first notes for African workshops

Track your consumable cost per metre of weld. On a typical structural job, electrode and gas costs can be 1020% of the contract value; rework can erase the entire margin. Standardise on two or three processes, train apprentices on those, and keep a logbook of settings for repeat jobs. Where power is unreliable, invest in a generator sized for your largest machine, and keep spare cables, clamps, and helmets. Finally, price for inspection and documentation clients who pay for quality expect records.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Confirm material type, thickness, and joint design before starting.
  • Clean 25 mm either side of the joint no rust, oil, paint, or moisture.
  • Check polarity, current, and cable/earth condition.
  • Use dry low-hydrogen electrodes for structural work; store them correctly.
  • Tack, check alignment, and plan weld sequence to control distortion.
  • Clean slag between every pass; inspect before capping.
  • Wear correct PPE: helmet shade, gloves, apron, and fume control.
  • Record settings and welder ID for traceability and repeat jobs.
