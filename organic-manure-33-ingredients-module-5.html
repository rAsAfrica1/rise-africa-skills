1. Sourcing and Preparing the 33 Ingredients

Welcome to Module 5 of the Organic Manure with 33 Ingredients training. This module is the practical heart of the course. By the end you will know exactly where to find each ingredient, how to prepare it, and how to turn a pile of farm waste into a product that customers pay for. We keep the focus on Africa because that is where you operate. The ingredients are local, cheap, and often free. The challenge is not availability. The challenge is consistency, quality, and turning this into a business that pays school fees, rent, and salaries. Let us start with the mindset. You are not making compost for fun. You are manufacturing a product. Every decision must answer one question: will this make my manure better, faster, or cheaper to produce? If the answer is no, do not do it. The 33 ingredients fall into six families. First, carbon sources: maize stover, rice husks, sawdust, dry grass, sugarcane bagasse, and groundnut shells. Second, nitrogen sources: poultry litter, cattle dung, goat droppings, rabbit manure, fresh green weeds, kitchen vegetable waste, and urine. Third, mineral and ash sources: wood ash, charcoal dust, bone meal, eggshells, and rock phosphate where available. Fourth, microbial boosters: forest topsoil, termite mound soil, mature compost, and effective microorganisms from fermented rice water. Fifth, moisture and structure agents: water, clay soil, and river sand. Sixth, fortifiers: molasses, milk, and crushed neem leaves or tobacco for pest control. Count them and you get thirty-three. The exact list can flex with your region, but the families must all be present. A pile missing carbon will smell. A pile missing nitrogen will not heat. A pile missing minerals will produce weak crops. A pile missing microbes will take months instead of weeks. Now let us talk sourcing. In most African towns you can secure all thirty-three ingredients within a ten kilometre radius. Start with a waste audit. Walk your neighbourhood and list every organic stream. Markets produce tonnes of rotten tomatoes, banana peels, and cabbage leaves every week. Poultry farms produce litter that they often pay to remove. Sawmills produce sawdust they burn or dump. Abattoirs produce bone and blood. Schools produce food waste. Churches and mosques produce grass cuttings. Your job is to intercept these streams before they become trash. Build relationships, not transactions. Visit the poultry farmer and offer to clean the shed for free in exchange for the litter. Visit the market chairman and offer to cart away waste every Tuesday. Visit the carpenter and offer to buy sawdust by the bag. These relationships cost you time, not money. In six months you will have a reliable supply chain that competitors cannot copy. Next, preparation. Each ingredient needs a different treatment. Carbon sources must be chopped or shredded to increase surface area. A maize stover stalk that is two metres long will take a year to break down. The same stalk chopped into five centimetre pieces will break down in six weeks. Use a machete, a chaff cutter, or a simple hammer mill. If you cannot afford a machine, organise a chopping day with neighbours and pay them in manure. Rice husks are already small but they are slow to decompose. Soak them in urine or manure tea for three days before adding. Sawdust is the slowest of all. Use it sparingly, no more than ten percent of the pile, and always mix it with fresh green material. Nitrogen sources must be used fresh. Poultry litter loses nitrogen within forty eight hours if left in the sun. Collect it, bag it, and use it within three days. Cattle dung can be stored but cover it with a tarpaulin to prevent rain leaching. Urine is liquid gold. Collect it in jerrycans from households, schools, and urinals. Dilute one part urine to three parts water before adding to the pile. Mineral sources need grinding. Eggshells and bones must be crushed to powder. A mortar and pestle works for small volumes. For larger volumes, use a hammer mill or a car wheel and a flat rock. Wood ash must be sieved to remove charcoal chunks and nails. Charcoal dust is excellent but must be moistened before adding to avoid dust clouds. Microbial boosters are the secret weapon. Forest topsoil from under a mature tree contains thousands of beneficial fungi and bacteria. Collect the top five centimetres, not deeper. Termite mound soil is rich in calcium and microbes. Break it into small pieces. Mature compost from a previous batch is your starter culture. Always keep a bucket of finished manure to inoculate the next pile. Effective microorganisms can be made at home. Wash one cup of rice, keep the water, and let it sit covered for three days. Add one litre of milk and let it ferment for seven days. The resulting liquid smells like yoghurt and is full of lactic acid bacteria and yeast. Use one cup per hundred kilograms of pile. Moisture and structure agents are simple. Water must be clean, not salty or chlorinated. If your only water is chlorinated, let it sit in an open drum for twenty four hours before use. Clay soil helps bind the pile and retain nutrients. River sand improves drainage in heavy clay piles. Fortifiers are optional but increase quality. Molasses feeds microbes. Milk adds protein. Crushed neem leaves repel ants and termites. Now let us talk ratios. The ideal pile has a carbon to nitrogen ratio of thirty to one. In practice, you do not need a laboratory. Use the feel method. Mix three parts dry brown material to one part fresh green material by volume. If the pile is too dry, add water. If it smells like ammonia, add more carbon. If it smells like rotten eggs, add more carbon and turn the pile. If it does not heat up within three days, add more nitrogen and moisture. The pile should be as wet as a squeezed sponge. When you squeeze a handful, one or two drops of water should appear. Not zero, not ten. One or two. Build the pile in layers. Start with a ten centimetre base of coarse carbon like maize stover or sugarcane bagasse. This allows air to enter from below. Then add a five centimetre layer of nitrogen material. Then a thin layer of mineral and ash. Then a sprinkle of microbial booster. Then a thin layer of clay or sand. Repeat until the pile is one and a half metres high. Water each layer as you build. Do not compact the pile. Air is essential. Insert a few vertical sticks or bamboo poles and pull them out after three days to create air channels. Cover the pile with a tarpaulin or banana leaves to retain moisture and heat. Turn the pile every seven days for the first three weeks, then every ten days until maturity. Maturity takes four to eight weeks depending on climate. In hot dry areas it is faster. In cold highlands it is slower. You know the manure is ready when it is dark brown, crumbly, smells like forest soil, and the temperature has dropped to ambient. At this point, screen it through a five millimetre sieve. Large pieces go back into the next pile. The fine product is bagged in fifty kilogram sacks. Label the sacks with your brand, the date, and the ingredients. Store in a dry place away from direct sunlight. Now let us talk business. Your cost per bag should be less than one third of the selling price. In most African markets, a fifty kilogram bag of organic manure sells for between five and fifteen US dollars. Your costs are labour, transport, bags, and water. If you source ingredients for free and use family labour, your cost can be as low as one dollar per bag. That is a gross margin of eighty percent or more. But do not compete on price alone. Compete on quality and consistency. Farmers will pay more for manure that is fully mature, finely sieved, and free of weeds and stones. Offer a money back guarantee. If a farmer does not see results, refund the bag. This builds trust and generates word of mouth. Sell in bulk to commercial farms, greenhouses, and landscaping companies. Sell in small bags to urban gardeners and flower shops. Offer training to farmers who buy ten bags or more. This adds value and creates loyalty. Keep records. Every bag that leaves your yard must be recorded in a notebook. Date, quantity, customer name, and payment. At the end of the month, calculate your revenue, costs, and profit. Reinvest at least thirty percent into equipment, bags, and marketing. In the next four lessons we will go deeper into each family of ingredients, quality control, marketing, and scaling. For now, your assignment is simple. Walk your neighbourhood and list every source of the thirty-three ingredients within ten kilometres. Then build one pile using the layering method described here. Take photos and share them with your trainer. Remember, this is not theory. This is a business. Every pile you build is money in the bank. Every relationship you build is a supply chain. Every bag you sell is a step toward financial freedom. Let us move to Lesson Two.

2. Building the Pile: Layering, Moisture, and Heat Management

In Lesson One we identified the thirty-three ingredients and prepared them for use. Now we build the pile. This is where science meets sweat. A poorly built pile will take six months and produce weak manure. A well built pile will finish in four weeks and produce a product that farmers fight to buy. The difference is in the details. Let us start with site selection. Choose a level area close to your water source and close to your ingredient storage. The site must be well drained. If water pools around the pile, nutrients leach away and the pile becomes anaerobic. Anaerobic piles smell like rotten eggs and attract flies. If your only site is sloped, dig a shallow trench on the uphill side to divert rainwater. The site should be shaded in the afternoon but receive morning sun. Full shade slows decomposition. Full sun dries the pile too fast. A tree that casts dappled shade is ideal. If you have no tree, build a simple shade structure using poles and palm fronds. The pile itself should be one and a half metres wide, one and a half metres high, and as long as you need. A pile that is too small will not heat up. A pile that is too large will not get enough air in the centre. For a commercial operation, build multiple piles of this size rather than one giant pile. Now let us talk layering. The order of layers matters. Start with a ten centimetre base of coarse carbon material. Maize stover, sugarcane bagasse, or chopped branches work well. This base allows air to flow upward from the ground. If you skip this step, the bottom of the pile will become compacted and anaerobic. Next, add a five centimetre layer of nitrogen material. Poultry litter, cattle dung, or fresh green weeds. Spread it evenly. Do not dump it in one corner. Next, add a two centimetre layer of mineral and ash. Wood ash, charcoal dust, crushed eggshells, and bone meal. These provide calcium, potassium, and phosphorus. Next, sprinkle a microbial booster. Forest topsoil, termite mound soil, or mature compost. A handful per square metre is enough. Next, add a thin layer of clay soil or river sand. This binds the nutrients and prevents them from washing away. Now water the layer. Use a watering can or a hose with a fine spray. The goal is to moisten, not to soak. Then repeat the layers. Carbon, nitrogen, minerals, microbes, clay, water. Carbon, nitrogen, minerals, microbes, clay, water. Continue until the pile reaches one and a half metres. The top layer should be carbon material. This acts as a lid and reduces odour. Now let us talk moisture. Moisture is the most common mistake. Too dry and the microbes go dormant. Too wet and the pile becomes anaerobic. The ideal moisture is fifty to sixty percent. You cannot measure this without a meter, but you can use the squeeze test. Take a handful of material from the middle of the pile. Squeeze it hard. If no water comes out, the pile is too dry. Add water and turn. If water drips out freely, the pile is too wet. Add dry carbon material and turn. If one or two drops appear, the moisture is perfect. Check moisture every three days. In hot dry weather, you may need to water every two days. In rainy weather, cover the pile with a tarpaulin. Rain leaches nitrogen and cools the pile. Now let us talk heat. A well built pile will heat up within twenty four to forty eight hours. The centre should reach sixty to seventy degrees Celsius. You can measure this with a compost thermometer or a long metal rod. Push the rod into the centre, leave it for ten minutes, and pull it out. If it is too hot to touch, the pile is working. If it is warm but not hot, the pile needs more nitrogen or moisture. If it is cold, the pile is dead. Turn it and add fresh green material and water. Heat is a sign that microbes are breaking down organic matter. But too much heat kills the microbes. If the pile exceeds seventy five degrees, turn it immediately. Turning releases heat and brings cooler material from the outside into the centre. Turn the pile every seven days for the first three weeks. Use a fork or a shovel. Move the outside material to the centre and the centre material to the outside. This ensures even decomposition. After three weeks, turn every ten days. After six weeks, turn once a week. The pile is ready when it stops heating up and the temperature drops to ambient. Now let us talk aeration. Microbes need oxygen. Without oxygen, they produce methane and hydrogen sulphide, which smell bad and kill beneficial organisms. To aerate the pile, insert vertical poles or bamboo sticks before you build the pile. Pull them out after three days. This creates chimneys that allow air to enter. You can also build the pile on a base of branches or stones. This allows air to enter from below. If the pile smells bad, turn it immediately. Bad smell is a sign of anaerobic conditions. Add coarse carbon material like maize stover or sawdust to create air pockets. Now let us talk troubleshooting. Problem one: the pile is not heating up. Causes: too dry, too wet, not enough nitrogen, or too small. Solution: turn the pile, add water or dry material, add fresh green material, and rebuild if necessary. Problem two: the pile smells like ammonia. Cause: too much nitrogen. Solution: add carbon material like sawdust or maize stover and turn. Problem three: the pile smells like rotten eggs. Cause: too wet and anaerobic. Solution: turn the pile, add coarse carbon, and reduce watering. Problem four: the pile is attracting flies. Cause: exposed food waste or meat. Solution: bury food waste in the centre of the pile and cover with carbon material. Problem five: the pile is attracting rodents. Cause: food waste or bones. Solution: grind bones finely and bury them deep. Do not add meat or dairy unless you have a sealed system. Problem six: the pile is not breaking down. Cause: pieces are too large. Solution: chop or shred material before adding. Problem seven: the pile is too hot. Cause: too much nitrogen or too large a pile. Solution: turn the pile and add carbon material. Now let us talk timing. In the tropics, a well managed pile finishes in four to six weeks. In the subtropics, six to eight weeks. In highland areas, eight to twelve weeks. You can speed up decomposition by adding effective microorganisms, molasses, and urine. You can also use a tarp to trap heat. But do not rush. Immature manure can burn plants and introduce weeds. Test maturity by planting a few bean seeds in a small pot of the manure. If they sprout and grow green, the manure is ready. If they turn yellow and die, the manure is still immature. Another test is the bag test. Put a handful of manure in a sealed plastic bag. Leave it for three days. Open the bag and smell. If it smells like forest soil, it is ready. If it smells sour or like ammonia, it is not ready. Now let us talk equipment. You do not need expensive machinery. A fork, a shovel, a watering can, a wheelbarrow, and a tarpaulin are enough to start. As you grow, invest in a chaff cutter, a hammer mill, a compost thermometer, and a sieve. These tools pay for themselves within one season. A chaff cutter costs between one hundred and three hundred US dollars. It reduces labour by seventy percent. A hammer mill costs between five hundred and two thousand US dollars. It grinds bones, eggshells, and charcoal. A compost thermometer costs twenty to fifty US dollars. It takes the guesswork out of heat management. A sieve costs ten to thirty US dollars. It ensures a fine, uniform product. Now let us talk labour. A single person can manage ten piles of one and a half cubic metres. Each pile produces about five hundred kilograms of finished manure. That is five tonnes per cycle. With four cycles per year, that is twenty tonnes. At ten US dollars per fifty kilogram bag, that is four thousand US dollars per year. With two workers, you can double that. Pay workers fairly. A happy worker is a productive worker. Offer bonuses for quality and quantity. Train them in every step so they can manage the operation when you are away. Now let us talk records. Keep a daily log. Date, pile number, temperature, moisture, action taken. This helps you identify patterns and improve. Keep a financial log. Income, expenses, and profit. Keep a customer log. Names, contact details, and purchase history. These records are the difference between a hobby and a business. In the next lesson we will cover quality control and testing. For now, your assignment is to build one pile using the layering method. Record the temperature every day for two weeks. Take photos. Share your results with your trainer. Remember, the pile is not just a pile. It is a factory. Treat it with respect and it will reward you. Let us move to Lesson Three.

3. Quality Control, Testing, and Certification

In Lesson Two we built the pile and managed heat and moisture. Now we focus on quality. Quality is what separates a bag of manure that sells for five dollars from a bag that sells for fifteen dollars. Quality is what makes farmers come back season after season. Quality is what allows you to sell to commercial farms, greenhouses, and export markets. In this lesson you will learn how to test your manure, how to fix quality problems, and how to certify your product for premium markets. Let us start with the definition of quality. Good organic manure has five characteristics. First, it is fully mature. Immature manure contains organic acids that burn plants. Second, it is finely sieved. Large pieces break down slowly and can contain weed seeds. Third, it is free of contaminants. Plastic, glass, metal, and stones must be removed. Fourth, it has a balanced nutrient profile. Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium, and micronutrients must be present in plant available forms. Fifth, it is free of pathogens. E. coli, salmonella, and parasitic eggs must be destroyed by proper heating. Now let us talk testing. You do not need a laboratory to test most of these characteristics. You can do it on your farm with simple tools. Test one: maturity. Plant ten bean seeds in a pot of your manure. Plant ten more in a pot of regular soil. Water both pots equally. If the manure beans sprout and grow as well as or better than the soil beans, the manure is mature. If they turn yellow, curl, or die, the manure is immature. This test takes seven to ten days. Test two: texture. Take a handful of manure and rub it between your fingers. It should feel crumbly and soft, like forest soil. If it feels sticky or greasy, it is too wet. If it feels dry and dusty, it is too dry. Test three: smell. Good manure smells like earth after rain. Bad manure smells like ammonia, rotten eggs, or vinegar. Ammonia means too much nitrogen. Rotten eggs means anaerobic conditions. Vinegar means incomplete decomposition. Test four: colour. Good manure is dark brown or black. Light brown or green manure is immature. Test five: temperature. Good manure is at ambient temperature. If it is still warm, it is still decomposing. Test six: weed seeds. Put a handful of manure in a shallow tray and keep it moist for two weeks. Count the number of weed seedlings that sprout. If more than five sprout, your pile did not get hot enough to kill weed seeds. Test seven: pathogens. You cannot test for pathogens at home, but you can reduce risk by ensuring the pile reaches sixty five degrees Celsius for at least three days. Turn the pile so that all material passes through the hot centre. Do not add fresh manure to a mature pile. Test eight: nutrient content. You can send a sample to a government laboratory or a private lab. In most African countries, a basic soil and manure test costs between twenty and one hundred US dollars. The report will tell you the percentage of nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, organic matter, and pH. This is essential if you want to sell to commercial farms. Test nine: pH. Use a simple pH meter or pH strips. Good manure has a pH between six and eight. If it is too acidic, add wood ash. If it is too alkaline, add more carbon material and water. Test ten: moisture. Use the squeeze test. One or two drops is ideal. Now let us talk quality problems and solutions. Problem one: immature manure. Cause: pile was turned too early or did not heat up. Solution: rebuild the pile, add fresh green material and water, and let it heat for another three weeks. Problem two: high ammonia smell. Cause: too much nitrogen or too wet. Solution: add carbon material, turn, and reduce watering. Problem three: low nutrient content. Cause: too much carbon or leaching. Solution: add poultry litter, urine, or bone meal. Cover the pile to prevent rain leaching. Problem four: weed seeds. Cause: pile did not reach sixty five degrees. Solution: turn the pile so all material passes through the centre. Add more nitrogen to increase heat. Problem five: plastic and glass contamination. Cause: poor sorting at source. Solution: train your workers to sort ingredients before adding. Use a sieve with five millimetre holes. Problem six: pathogens. Cause: fresh manure added to mature pile or pile did not heat enough. Solution: never add fresh manure to mature pile. Ensure the pile reaches sixty five degrees for three days. Problem seven: heavy metals. Cause: industrial waste or contaminated water. Solution: source ingredients from trusted suppliers. Test your water. Avoid sawdust from treated timber. Now let us talk certification. Certification is a formal recognition that your product meets a certain standard. In Africa, there are several certification bodies. The African Organic Network, the East African Organic Products Standard, and national bureaus of standards. Certification allows you to use an organic label, which commands a premium price. The process has four steps. Step one: application. Contact the certification body and submit an application. Pay the application fee, which ranges from one hundred to five hundred US dollars. Step two: inspection. An inspector visits your farm or factory. They check your records, your ingredients, your production process, and your storage. They take samples for laboratory testing. Step three: review. The certification body reviews the inspector report and the lab results. If everything meets the standard, they issue a certificate. Step four: renewal. Certification is valid for one year. You must renew annually and pay a renewal fee. Certification is not for everyone. It is expensive and time consuming. But if you want to sell to supermarkets, export markets, or large commercial farms, it is essential. Start with a simpler quality assurance system. Write a one page quality policy. Train your workers. Keep records. Label your bags with the production date, the ingredients, and your contact details. Offer a money back guarantee. This builds trust without the cost of formal certification. Now let us talk packaging. Packaging is part of quality. A cheap bag makes your manure look cheap. Invest in strong, printed bags. A fifty kilogram polypropylene bag with your logo and contact details costs between fifty cents and two US dollars. A five kilogram bag for urban gardeners costs between ten and thirty cents. Label each bag with the following information: product name, net weight, ingredients, nutrient analysis if available, production date, expiry date, storage instructions, and your contact details. Add a QR code that links to a video showing how to use the manure. This adds value and reduces customer complaints. Now let us talk storage. Store your manure in a dry, shaded place. Direct sunlight degrades nutrients. Rain leaches nitrogen. Stack bags on pallets to prevent moisture from the ground. Keep bags away from walls to allow air circulation. Do not store manure with chemicals, fuel, or pesticides. Manure absorbs odours and contaminants. Use first in, first out. Sell older bags before newer ones. Now let us talk customer feedback. Quality is defined by the customer, not by you. Ask your customers for feedback. What did they like? What did they not like? Did they see results? How can you improve? Keep a feedback log. Use the feedback to improve your product and your service. If a customer complains, replace the bag free of charge. This costs you a little but builds loyalty. Now let us talk pricing. Price your manure based on quality, not on cost. If your manure is fully mature, finely sieved, and certified, you can charge a premium. Research your competitors. What do they charge? What quality do they offer? Position your product as the best, not the cheapest. Offer volume discounts to commercial farms. Offer small bags to urban gardeners. Offer training to farmers who buy in bulk. This creates multiple revenue streams. Now let us talk records. Keep a quality control log. Date, batch number, test results, action taken. Keep a customer complaint log. Date, customer name, complaint, action taken. Keep a certification log. Date, inspector name, findings, certificate number. These records are essential for certification and for continuous improvement. In the next lesson we will cover marketing and sales. For now, your assignment is to test your current pile using the ten tests described here. Record the results. Identify one quality problem and fix it. Share your results with your trainer. Remember, quality is not an accident. It is a habit. Build the habit and the business will grow. Let us move to Lesson Four.

4. Marketing, Sales, and Distribution

In Lesson Three we focused on quality. Now we focus on selling. You can produce the best manure in Africa, but if no one buys it, you have a hobby, not a business. Marketing is the bridge between your pile and your customer. In this lesson you will learn how to identify your customers, how to reach them, how to price your product, and how to distribute it efficiently. Let us start with customer identification. There are five main customer segments for organic manure in Africa. Segment one: smallholder farmers. They farm one to five hectares. They grow maize, beans, cassava, vegetables, and bananas. They are price sensitive but loyal if they see results. They buy in small quantities, usually ten to fifty kilogram bags. Segment two: commercial farmers. They farm ten to one thousand hectares. They grow cash crops like tobacco, cotton, coffee, tea, and horticulture. They buy in bulk, often by the tonne. They demand consistent quality and reliable supply. Segment three: urban gardeners. They grow vegetables and flowers in backyards, schools, churches, and community gardens. They buy small bags, five to twenty kilograms. They are willing to pay a premium for convenience and quality. Segment four: landscaping companies. They install and maintain gardens for hotels, offices, and wealthy homeowners. They buy in bulk and demand fine, weed free manure. Segment five: institutions. Schools, prisons, hospitals, and municipal parks. They buy through tenders. They demand certification and formal invoicing. Now let us talk market research. Before you produce, find out what your customers want. Visit ten farmers in your area. Ask them four questions. What crops do you grow? What fertiliser do you use? How much do you pay? What problems do you have with your current fertiliser? Listen carefully. Their answers will tell you what to produce and how to price it. If they complain that chemical fertiliser is too expensive, position your manure as a cheaper alternative. If they complain that chemical fertiliser degrades soil, position your manure as a soil builder. If they complain that manure is full of weeds, position your product as weed free. Now let us talk branding. A brand is a promise. It tells the customer what to expect. Choose a name that is easy to remember and easy to pronounce. Use a local language word if possible. For example, Mavuno Bora means good harvest in Swahili. Nyumbani means home in Swahili. Use a logo that is simple and recognisable. A green leaf, a sprouting seed, or a smiling farmer. Use colours that stand out. Green, yellow, and brown are good for organic products. Print your brand on every bag. Print your contact details. Print a simple instruction on how to use the manure. Now let us talk pricing. There are three pricing strategies. Strategy one: cost plus. Calculate your cost per bag and add a profit margin. If your cost is two dollars and you want a fifty percent margin, sell for four dollars. This is simple but ignores what customers are willing to pay. Strategy two: competitor based. Find out what your competitors charge and set your price slightly lower or slightly higher depending on quality. If your manure is better, charge more. If it is similar, charge the same. Strategy three: value based. Set your price based on the value your manure delivers. If your manure increases a farmer yield by twenty percent, and that yield is worth one hundred dollars, you can charge twenty dollars. This is the most profitable strategy but requires evidence. Use a combination of all three. Start with cost plus to ensure you cover your costs. Then adjust based on competitors and value. Offer discounts for bulk purchases. Offer a loyalty programme. Buy ten bags, get one free. Offer a referral programme. Refer a friend, get ten percent off your next purchase. Now let us talk promotion. Promotion is how you tell customers about your product. There are six low cost promotion methods. Method one: field days. Invite farmers to your production site. Show them your piles. Show them your sieving process. Give them a free sample. Let them see the results on a demonstration plot. Method two: farmer groups. Join existing farmer groups. Offer to give a talk on organic farming. Bring samples. Collect contact details. Follow up with a phone call. Method three: radio. Local radio stations are cheap and reach thousands of farmers. A thirty second advert costs between five and fifty US dollars. A thirty minute talk show costs between twenty and two hundred US dollars. Method four: social media. WhatsApp, Facebook, and TikTok are free. Post photos of your piles, your customers, and your results. Create a WhatsApp group for your customers. Share tips and answer questions. Method five: demonstrations. Plant two plots. One with chemical fertiliser, one with your manure. Invite farmers to see the difference. This is the most convincing promotion method. Method six: partnerships. Partner with agro dealers, extension officers, and NGOs. They already have relationships with farmers. Offer them a commission for every bag they sell. Now let us talk sales. Sales is the process of turning a prospect into a customer. There are five steps. Step one: approach. Greet the customer. Introduce yourself and your product. Be friendly and respectful. Step two: needs identification. Ask questions. What do you grow? What problems do you have? What fertiliser do you use? Listen more than you talk. Step three: presentation. Show how your manure solves their problem. Use evidence. Show photos. Share testimonials. Give a sample. Step four: objection handling. Customers will have objections. It is too expensive. I have never used it. I do not trust it. Listen to the objection. Acknowledge it. Respond with evidence. Offer a guarantee. Step five: close. Ask for the sale. Would you like to buy one bag or two? Would you like delivery? Would you like to pay now or on delivery? Do not be afraid to ask. Now let us talk distribution. Distribution is how you get your product to the customer. There are four channels. Channel one: direct sales. You sell directly to farmers from your production site. This gives you the highest margin but limits your reach. Channel two: agro dealers. You sell to agro dealers at a wholesale price. They sell to farmers at a retail price. This expands your reach but reduces your margin. Channel three: cooperatives. You sell to cooperatives that buy in bulk and distribute to their members. This gives you large orders but requires credit terms. Channel four: online. You sell through WhatsApp, Facebook Marketplace, or a simple website. This reaches urban customers but requires delivery

<h3>Why 33 Ingredients? The Business Case</h3>
<p>Single-source manure (cattle only, poultry only) is a commodity. A 33-ingredient blend is a branded product. In Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana, and South Africa, blended organic fertilisers retail at 24 the price of raw manure per kilogram because they carry a nutrient analysis, a certification mark, and a consistent NPK guarantee. The 33-ingredient approach spreads nutrient risk (carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium, sulphur, and micronutrients) and lets you target specific crops maize, tomato, tea, banana, vegetables with a tailored blend.</p>

<p style="margin:0; color:#7b241c;"><strong> SAFETY WARNING Pathogens and Chemical Contamination</strong></p>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#7b241c;">Fresh manure and compost can carry <em>E. coli</em>, <em>Salmonella</em>, and <em>Listeria</em>. WHO guidelines require that compost used on food crops reach <strong>5565 C for at least 3 consecutive days</strong> (or 55 C for 15 days in turned piles) to achieve pathogen reduction. Never apply fresh manure to leafy vegetables within 90 days of harvest. Wear gloves and dust masks when turning piles. If you add urine or ash, avoid contact with eyes and open wounds. Store finished compost away from livestock and children. Test for heavy metals (lead, cadmium) if you source from urban waste WHO limits are 0.3 mg/kg Cd and 100 mg/kg Pb for agricultural soil amendments.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Source verification:</strong> Confirm all 33 ingredients are free of plastics, glass, and chemical residues. Reject any batch with visible contamination.</li>
<li><strong>C:N ratio:</strong> Target 2530:1 overall. Use Table 1 to calculate. Add carbon (stover, sawdust) if the pile smells of ammonia.</li>
<li><strong>Moisture:</strong> Squeeze test a few drops should appear. If it drips, add dry material. If it crumbles, add water.</li>
<li><strong>Temperature:</strong> Turn when the pile reaches 5565 C. If it exceeds 70 C, turn immediately to prevent nitrogen loss and fire risk.</li>
<li><strong>Turning schedule:</strong> Turn on days 3, 7, 14, 21, and 30. Finished compost is dark, crumbly, and smells earthy not sour.</li>
<li><strong>Testing:</strong> Send a composite sample to a certified lab for NPK, pH, moisture, and heavy metals before bagging.</li>
<li><strong>Certification:</strong> Apply for organic certification (e.g., Kenya Organic Agriculture Network, Nigerian Organic Agriculture Network) before using the organic label.</li>
<li><strong>Pricing:</strong> Use Table 2 to benchmark. Price at 34 raw manure if certified; 2 if uncertified but branded.</li>
</ol>
