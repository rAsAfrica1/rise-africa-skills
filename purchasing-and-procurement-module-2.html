1. Understanding the African Procurement Landscape

Welcome to Module 2 of Purchasing and Procurement. This lesson sets the foundation for everything that follows by examining the unique characteristics of procurement across the African continent. Africa is not a single market. It is 54 countries with different currencies, languages, legal systems, infrastructure realities, and business cultures. A procurement strategy that works in Nairobi may fail in Lagos, and a supplier relationship that thrives in Accra may struggle in Kinshasa. Your first task as a procurement professional is to stop thinking of Africa as one place and start mapping the specific operating environment for each category you buy. Start with the basics. Identify the countries where your organisation operates or sources from. For each country, document the currency and its volatility, the inflation rate, the import duty structure, the VAT regime, and the typical payment terms suppliers expect. In many African markets, cash flow is tight and suppliers often prefer advance payment or payment on delivery. This affects your working capital planning. If you are buying from a small or medium enterprise in Kampala or Dakar, asking for 60-day terms may be unrealistic. Instead, consider partial advance payments, mobile money transfers, or letters of credit where banks are reliable. Next, understand the informal sector. In many African economies, a large share of trade happens outside formal registered businesses. This does not mean it is illegal or low quality. It means you need different due diligence. Visit the supplier. Check their physical premises. Ask for references from other buyers. Look for consistent quality over time. Formal registration documents are useful but not sufficient. Build a supplier scorecard that includes reliability, quality, price competitiveness, and responsiveness. Weight these factors according to your risk appetite. Infrastructure matters more than many textbooks admit. Road conditions, port congestion, customs clearance times, and power reliability all affect total cost of ownership. A cheaper supplier in a landlocked country may become more expensive once you add transport, demurrage, and inventory buffer costs. Always calculate landed cost, not just unit price. Landed cost includes the purchase price, freight, insurance, customs duties, port charges, inland transport, and the cost of holding extra stock to cover delays. In Africa, the difference between unit price and landed cost can be 30 to 50 percent or more. Currency risk is another constant. If you buy in US dollars or euros but sell in local currency, exchange rate movements can wipe out your margin. Mitigate this by negotiating local currency contracts where possible, using forward contracts with your bank, or building a currency adjustment clause into long-term agreements. Some buyers maintain a natural hedge by matching currency of revenue and currency of procurement. If you sell in naira, try to buy in naira. If that is impossible, price your products with a buffer that reflects expected depreciation. Regulatory and compliance frameworks vary widely. The African Continental Free Trade Area, or AfCFTA, is gradually reducing tariffs and simplifying rules of origin, but implementation is uneven. You still need to know the local import licence requirements, standards certifications, and tax registration rules. Work with a reputable customs broker in each country. A good broker saves more money than their fee. Also understand local content requirements. Many governments now insist that a percentage of goods or services be sourced locally. This can be an opportunity to develop local suppliers, but it requires patience and investment in supplier development. Payment systems are evolving fast. Mobile money is dominant in East Africa, bank transfers are common in Southern Africa, and cash still matters in parts of Central and West Africa. Choose payment methods that are secure, traceable, and acceptable to your supplier. Avoid cash payments above small thresholds because they create audit and fraud risks. Use electronic transfers where possible and keep clear records. Finally, build relationships. In many African business cultures, trust is built through personal contact, not just email. Visit suppliers. Attend trade fairs. Join industry associations. A strong relationship can help you resolve disputes, get priority during shortages, and negotiate better terms over time. This does not mean you abandon formal contracts. It means you combine legal rigour with relational intelligence. In summary, procurement in Africa requires local knowledge, flexible payment thinking, landed cost discipline, currency awareness, regulatory diligence, and relationship building. Master these and you will outperform buyers who treat the continent as a single, simple market.

2. Supplier Identification and Due Diligence in African Markets

This lesson focuses on finding the right suppliers and verifying that they can deliver. In Africa, supplier identification is part detective work, part networking, and part structured research. You cannot rely only on online directories. Many capable suppliers have a limited digital presence. Your goal is to build a pipeline of qualified suppliers for each critical category. Start with a clear specification. Before you look for suppliers, define what you need in measurable terms. Quantity, quality standards, delivery frequency, packaging, payment terms, and after-sales support. A vague specification invites vague quotations and later disputes. Write a simple request for information, or RFI, and send it to potential suppliers. The RFI should ask about their company registration, years in business, key customers, production capacity, quality certifications, and financial stability. Keep it short. African suppliers are busy and often understaffed in sales. A two-page RFI gets better responses than a ten-page questionnaire. Where do you find suppliers? Use multiple channels. Trade associations and chambers of commerce are excellent starting points. In Kenya, the Kenya Association of Manufacturers. In Nigeria, the Manufacturers Association of Nigeria. In South Africa, the Steel and Engineering Industries Federation. These bodies can provide member lists and introductions. Industry trade fairs are also valuable. The Lagos International Trade Fair, the Kenya Trade Expo, the Ghana Trade Fair, and the Africa Food Show attract suppliers from across regions. Attend with a colleague. Collect business cards. Take photos of products. Ask about lead times and minimum order quantities. Another channel is referrals from peer companies. Ask other procurement managers in your industry who they buy from and who they avoid. This informal intelligence is often more reliable than any database. You can also use online platforms such as Africa Business Pages, Exporters India, and Alibaba for imported goods, but verify every supplier before committing. Once you have a long list, move to due diligence. Due diligence means verifying that the supplier is real, capable, and ethical. Start with legal registration. Ask for a certificate of incorporation, tax registration, and any sector-specific licences. In many countries you can verify these documents online through the company registry or revenue authority. If online verification is not available, ask your local lawyer or accountant to confirm. Do not accept photocopies without checking the original. Next, check physical presence. Visit the factory, warehouse, or office. Look for signs of real operations. Are there workers? Is there stock? Is the equipment maintained? A supplier who cannot show you their premises is a risk. If travel is impossible, hire a local inspection agent. Several firms in Africa offer supplier verification services for a modest fee. Financial health matters. Ask for audited financial statements for the last two years. Many small suppliers will not have audited accounts, so ask for bank statements, tax clearance certificates, and references from their bank. A supplier with chronic cash flow problems may accept your order but fail to deliver. You can also check credit reference bureaus where they exist, such as in Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa. Quality capability is next. Ask for samples. Test them against your specification. Visit their quality control process. Do they have written procedures? Do they calibrate their measuring equipment? Do they keep batch records? If they cannot answer basic quality questions, they are not ready for your business. For food, pharmaceutical, or construction purchases, insist on relevant certifications such as ISO 9001, HACCP, or local standards bureau approval. Ethical and compliance checks are increasingly important. Ask about labour practices, environmental compliance, and anti-bribery policies. Check whether the supplier appears on any sanctions lists or debarment lists. In some African countries, public procurement debarment lists are published. For private buyers, you can use international databases and local media searches. If a supplier has been involved in fraud or corruption, avoid them. The reputational risk is not worth the price saving. Payment terms and currency should be discussed early. Ask what currency they invoice in, what payment methods they accept, and what their lead times are. Be clear about your own payment process. Many disputes arise because the buyer assumes 30-day terms and the supplier expects cash on delivery. Put everything in writing. A simple purchase order with clear terms and conditions is better than a verbal agreement. Finally, rank your suppliers. Use a weighted scorecard. For example, quality 30 percent, price 25 percent, delivery 20 percent, financial stability 15 percent, and compliance 10 percent. Score each supplier from 1 to 5. This gives you an objective shortlist. Do not choose on price alone. The cheapest supplier often becomes the most expensive when you add rework, delays, and emergency air freight. In Africa, reliability is worth a premium. Build a supplier database with all this information. Update it regularly. Review your top suppliers every six months. Remove underperformers and add new candidates. This discipline turns supplier identification from a one-off task into a continuous capability. Remember, your suppliers are an extension of your business. Choose them carefully, verify them thoroughly, and treat them as partners in delivering value to your customers.

3. Negotiation and Contracting with African Suppliers

Negotiation is where procurement creates measurable value. In Africa, negotiation is both an economic exchange and a relationship-building process. This lesson gives you a practical framework for preparing, conducting, and closing negotiations with African suppliers, and for turning the agreement into a clear contract. Preparation is 80 percent of success. Before you meet a supplier, know your numbers. Calculate your target price, your walk-away price, and your best alternative to a negotiated agreement, or BATNA. Your BATNA is what you will do if this supplier says no. If you have two other qualified suppliers, your BATNA is strong. If you have none, your BATNA is weak and you should not negotiate yet. Gather market intelligence. What are competitors paying? What are raw material prices doing? What is the exchange rate trend? What are import duties and transport costs? Use this data to justify your position. Also understand the supplier's situation. Are they hungry for orders? Do they have idle capacity? Are they trying to enter your market? A supplier with empty machines is more flexible on price than one running at full capacity. When you meet, start with relationship building. In many African cultures, business conversations begin with greetings, family, and general news. Do not rush to the price. Ask about their business, their challenges, and their plans. This is not wasted time. It builds trust and gives you information. Then present your needs clearly. Explain your specification, volumes, delivery schedule, and payment terms. Be honest about your constraints. If you cannot pay upfront, say so early. Suppliers respect clarity more than surprises. Use a structured agenda. Cover price, quality, delivery, payment, warranty, and after-sales support. Do not let the discussion jump randomly. Take notes. Confirm understanding by summarising what you heard. This avoids misunderstandings later. On price, use a range rather than a single number. For example, say that your budget for this volume is between X and Y, and you need the supplier to help you reach the lower end. Ask open questions. What can they do to reduce cost? Can they change packaging? Can they deliver in larger batches? Can they source raw materials locally? Often the supplier can find savings you did not see. Be prepared to trade. Negotiation is not just about price. You can offer longer contracts, faster payment, larger volumes, or help with logistics in exchange for better unit price. For example, if you pay within seven days instead of thirty, ask for a 2 percent discount. If you commit to a twelve-month volume, ask for a fixed price or a price cap. These trades create value for both sides. Watch for non-price issues. Lead time, minimum order quantity, warranty period, and return policy can matter more than a small price difference. A supplier who delivers on time every time is worth more than one who is 5 percent cheaper but always late. Calculate total cost of ownership for each offer. Include freight, duties, inventory carrying cost, and quality inspection. Sometimes the higher-priced supplier wins on total cost. Be aware of cultural differences. In some African countries, direct confrontation is uncomfortable. Use indirect language. Say that the price is challenging rather than that it is unacceptable. Offer face-saving ways for the supplier to adjust. In other countries, negotiation is more direct and assertive. Read the room. Ask your local colleagues for advice. When you reach agreement, document it immediately. A verbal deal is not enough. Write a clear contract or purchase order. Include the specification, quantity, unit price, currency, payment terms, delivery date, delivery location, Incoterms, quality standards, inspection rights, warranty, dispute resolution, and termination clauses. If the contract is in a language other than English, ensure you have a certified translation. Use simple language. Long legalistic contracts that no one understands create disputes. If the value is significant, have a local lawyer review the contract. Pay attention to governing law and dispute resolution. Which country's law applies? Where will disputes be heard? International arbitration can be expensive and slow. Consider mediation first. Many African countries have arbitration centres, such as the Lagos Regional Centre for International Commercial Arbitration or the Nairobi Centre for International Arbitration. Choose a neutral venue if both parties are in different countries. Currency clauses matter. If you pay in local currency but the supplier imports raw materials in dollars, they bear exchange risk. They may price that risk into the contract. You can agree to share the risk. For example, if the exchange rate moves more than 5 percent, the price adjusts by half the movement. This is fair and keeps the relationship stable. Payment terms should be realistic. In Africa, cash is king. If you can pay faster, you often get a better price. If you need credit, expect to pay for it. Offer a letter of credit or bank guarantee if the supplier is worried about non-payment. This reduces their risk and may lower the price. Finally, manage the contract after signing. Hold a kick-off meeting. Agree on communication channels and escalation points. Monitor delivery against the schedule. Inspect goods on arrival. Pay on time. If there is a problem, raise it early and professionally. A good contract is the beginning of a relationship, not the end of a negotiation. By preparing well, trading creatively, and documenting clearly, you will build supplier relationships that deliver value year after year.

4. Managing Supplier Performance and Relationships

Once a contract is signed, the real work begins. Supplier performance management is how you ensure that the promises made during negotiation are kept during delivery. In Africa, where supply chains can be disrupted by weather, customs delays, currency shortages, and political events, active management is essential. This lesson covers how to measure, review, and improve supplier performance, and how to build relationships that survive challenges. Start with clear key performance indicators, or KPIs. The KPIs should flow directly from your contract. Common KPIs include on-time delivery, order fill rate, quality defect rate, response time to queries, and invoice accuracy. Define each KPI precisely. For example, on-time delivery means delivery at the agreed location within the agreed window. If the window is plus or minus two days, say so. Ambiguity leads to arguments. Set targets. A reasonable target for on-time delivery is 95 percent. For quality, a defect rate below 2 percent is often acceptable, but it depends on your industry. Share these targets with the supplier at the kick-off meeting. Make sure they understand how you will measure and when you will review. Collect data continuously. Use your goods receiving records, inspection reports, and accounts payable system. Do not rely on memory or anecdotes. If you have an enterprise resource planning system, set up supplier scorecards. If not, use a simple spreadsheet. The key is consistency. Review performance monthly or quarterly, depending on volume. A monthly review is good for high-volume or critical suppliers. A quarterly review is fine for others. Invite the supplier to the review. Show them the data. Discuss what went well and what did not. Ask for their perspective. Sometimes a late delivery is caused by your own late payment or unclear specification. Be fair. The goal is improvement, not blame. Use a structured format. Start with safety and compliance. Then quality. Then delivery. Then cost. Then relationship and responsiveness. Give a score for each category. Agree on corrective actions for any red flags. Write them down. Set deadlines. Follow up. If a supplier repeatedly fails, escalate. First, a phone call or meeting with the account manager. If no improvement, a formal letter to senior management. If still no improvement, consider suspending new orders or terminating the contract. Document every step. In some African jurisdictions, you need evidence of poor performance before you can legally terminate. Keep records. Relationship management is the softer side but equally important. In Africa, personal relationships often determine how quickly problems get solved. A supplier who trusts you will prioritise your order when raw materials are scarce. A supplier who feels disrespected may deprioritise you. So invest in the relationship. Visit the supplier regularly. Meet their team. Understand their pressures. Share your own challenges. Be transparent about your forecasts. If you expect lower volumes next quarter, tell them early so they can plan. If you expect higher volumes, give them time to prepare. Do not surprise them. Celebrate successes. A simple thank-you note or a public recognition at a supplier day goes a long way. Some companies hold annual supplier awards. This motivates suppliers and strengthens loyalty. It also signals to other suppliers that good performance is noticed. Manage risk actively. Identify single-source suppliers and develop backup options. For critical items, keep safety stock. For imported goods, understand the customs process and have a broker ready. For currency risk, monitor exchange rates and adjust orders if needed. For political risk, have contingency plans. If a country is heading into elections, expect delays. Order earlier. If a port is congested, consider alternative routes. Build a supplier risk register. List each risk, its likelihood, its impact, and your mitigation plan. Review it quarterly. Train your suppliers. Many African suppliers, especially small ones, lack formal training in quality management, export documentation, or financial planning. You can help them improve. Offer workshops on your quality standards, your packaging requirements, or your invoicing process. This reduces errors and builds goodwill. Some buyers provide advance payment or equipment loans to help suppliers upgrade. This is a strategic investment. It locks in capacity and loyalty. Measure the total cost of your supplier relationships, not just price. Include the cost of quality inspections, expedited freight, inventory holding, and management time. A supplier with a slightly higher price but fewer problems may be cheaper overall. Use total cost of ownership in your reviews. Finally, keep learning. The African business environment changes quickly. New regulations, new competitors, new technologies. Attend industry events. Read local business press. Talk to peers. Update your supplier strategy every year. A supplier that was excellent two years ago may have new management or new problems. A supplier that was weak may have improved. Do not assume. Verify. In summary, managing supplier performance requires clear KPIs, regular reviews, fair treatment, active risk management, and investment in the relationship. Do this well and your suppliers become a competitive advantage. Do it poorly and they become a source of constant firefighting. Choose the disciplined path.

5. Ethics, Compliance, and Sustainable Procurement in Africa

Procurement professionals in Africa face complex ethical and compliance challenges. Corruption, fraud, environmental damage, and labour abuses exist in every region. Your job is not to pretend these problems do not exist. Your job is to manage them. This lesson covers the practical steps you can take to buy ethically, comply with laws, and promote sustainability without losing commercial focus. Start with your own code of conduct. Every procurement professional should have a written code that covers conflicts of interest, gifts and hospitality, confidentiality, and fair dealing. The code should be signed by all staff. It should be reviewed annually. It should be enforced consistently. If a senior manager violates the code, they must face the same consequences as a junior buyer. Otherwise the code is meaningless. Conflicts of interest are common. A buyer may have a relative who owns a supplier. A manager may sit on the board of a company that bids for work. These situations are not automatically corrupt, but they must be disclosed and managed. Require annual declarations. If a conflict exists, remove the person from the decision. Document the decision. Transparency protects everyone. Gifts and hospitality are a grey area in many African cultures. Refusing a small gift can cause offence. Accepting a large gift can compromise your judgement. Set a clear threshold. For example, gifts under 20 US dollars may be accepted but must be recorded. Gifts above that must be declined or shared with the team. Cash gifts are never acceptable. Meals during a business meeting are usually fine. Expensive entertainment is not. When in doubt, ask your compliance officer. Bribery and facilitation payments are illegal in most countries and under international laws such as the UK Bribery Act and the US Foreign Corrupt Practices Act. Even if local customs tolerate small payments to speed up customs clearance, your company policy should prohibit them. Instead, plan ahead. Ensure documentation is complete. Use reputable brokers. Build buffer time into your schedule. If a customs officer demands a bribe, escalate to your broker or to the customs authority. Do not pay. Fraud is another risk. Common schemes include fake invoices, duplicate payments, phantom suppliers, and kickbacks. Prevent fraud with segregation of duties. The person who orders goods should not be the person who approves payment. The person who receives goods should not be the person who reconciles invoices. Use bank transfers, not cash. Reconcile supplier statements monthly. Run duplicate payment checks. Train your team to spot red flags, such as a supplier with a post office box as their only address, or an invoice that does not match the purchase order. Environmental sustainability is increasingly important. Buyers in Africa are under pressure from customers, investors, and regulators to reduce waste, carbon emissions, and resource use. Start with your own operations. Reduce packaging. Choose recycled materials. Consolidate shipments to reduce transport emissions. Buy energy-efficient equipment. Ask suppliers about their environmental practices. Do they treat waste water? Do they recycle? Do they use sustainable raw materials? For some categories, such as timber, fish, or agricultural products, sustainability certification matters. Look for certifications such as Forest Stewardship Council, Marine Stewardship Council, or Rainforest Alliance. If certification is not available, ask for evidence of responsible practices. Social sustainability is equally important. Ensure that suppliers do not use child labour, forced labour, or unsafe working conditions. This is not just a legal requirement. It is a reputational risk. A scandal in your supply chain can destroy customer trust. Visit supplier sites. Talk to workers. Check age records. Look for safety equipment. Ask about working hours and wages. If you find problems, do not simply cut the supplier. Work with them to improve. Cutting them may push workers into worse jobs. Give them a timeline and support. If they refuse to improve, then terminate. Compliance with local laws is non-negotiable. Register for VAT. Pay customs duties. Obtain import licences. Follow local content rules. File taxes on time. Keep records for the required period. In many African countries, tax authorities are becoming more digital and more aggressive. A compliance failure can result in fines, seizure of goods, or criminal charges. Use a local accountant or tax advisor if you are not sure. Data privacy is a growing issue. If you collect personal data from suppliers or customers, you must protect it. South Africa has the Protection of Personal Information Act. Kenya has the Data Protection Act. Nigeria has the Nigeria Data Protection Regulation. These laws require you to secure data, get consent, and report breaches. Train your team. Do not share supplier bank details over unsecured email. Use encryption. Verify bank changes by phone. Finally, build a culture of ethics. Lead by example. Reward ethical behaviour. Punish unethical behaviour. Encourage whistleblowing. Provide a confidential channel for reports. Protect whistleblowers from retaliation. Discuss ethics in team meetings. Include ethics in supplier evaluations. Ask suppliers about their own codes of conduct. Share your code with them. Expect them to follow it. In summary, ethical and sustainable procurement in Africa is practical, not theoretical. It means clear codes, conflict disclosure, gift rules, anti-fraud controls, environmental action, social responsibility, legal compliance, and a culture of integrity. It requires effort, but it protects your organisation, your suppliers, and your reputation. It also creates long-term value by reducing risk and building trust. Make it part of every sourcing decision, not an afterthought.

<h2>Practical Reference: Procurement Documentation, Inspection, and Traceability in African Markets</h2>

<p>This practical reference lesson supports the module on <strong>Supplier Identification and Due Diligence in African Markets</strong>. It is written for procurement officers, agribusiness managers, and buying teams who must convert supplier promises into verifiable transactions. In African markets, where formal and informal supply chains often overlap, the quality of your procurement documentation determines whether you can enforce a contract, claim insurance, satisfy a buyer, or defend your business in a dispute. This lesson provides field-ready templates, inspection checklists, and traceability benchmarks drawn from FAO, ILRI, WHO, and university extension guidance.</p>

<p>The commercial logic is simple. Every undocumented delivery is a hidden liability. Every missing inspection record is a future argument. Every unverified batch number is a recall waiting to happen. Buyers in the EU, Middle East, and within Africa's growing modern retail sector increasingly require proof of origin, proof of safety, and proof of ethical sourcing. Procurement teams that build documentation discipline into daily operations reduce rejection rates, shorten payment cycles, and win repeat contracts.</p>

<h3>Why Documentation Discipline Pays</h3>

<p>African agribusiness procurement faces three recurring risks: identity risk (is the supplier who they claim to be?), quality risk (does the product meet specification?), and traceability risk (can you prove where the product came from?). Each risk has a documentation solution. A supplier registration file with verified tax identification and certificate of incorporation reduces fraud. A goods receipt note (GRN) signed only after inspection protects you from paying for substandard goods. A batch register with farm or plot identifiers allows you to isolate a problem and avoid a full recall.</p>

<p>Data from the FAO and World Bank consistently show that post-harvest losses and quality rejections in Sub-Saharan Africa are driven less by production failures than by weak handling, storage, and documentation along the chain. Procurement officers sit at the point where these losses can be prevented or locked in. The tables below give you real benchmarks to build into supplier scorecards and inspection protocols.</p>

<h3>Building the Inspection Habit</h3>

<p>Inspection is not a one-off event. It is a repeatable procedure that every receiving officer must follow. The most effective procurement teams in African agribusiness use a three-tier inspection model:</p>

<p><strong>Tier 1 Visual and documentary check at the gate.</strong> Confirm the supplier, vehicle, driver, and batch number match the purchase order. Check the delivery note, health certificate, and any required permits. If documents are missing, the vehicle waits. No exceptions.</p>

<p><strong>Tier 2 Physical quality check at the receiving bay.</strong> Sample according to a written plan. For grains, take a composite sample and measure moisture. For fresh produce, inspect for bruising, decay, and pest damage. For dairy, test temperature and antibiotic residues. Record results on the GRN or a separate inspection sheet.</p>

<p><strong>Tier 3 Laboratory or third-party verification for high-risk lots.</strong> Aflatoxin, pesticide residues, and veterinary drug residues require laboratory testing. Use accredited laboratories or rapid test kits approved by the national regulator. Do not rely on supplier-provided certificates alone for high-risk products.</p>

<p>ILRI's work on smallholder dairy chains in Kenya and Ethiopia shows that simple, consistent testing at collection centres temperature, acidity, and antibiotic residue dramatically reduces rejection at processing. The same principle applies across commodities: consistent, documented inspection at the point of receipt is cheaper than recall, rejection, or reputational damage later.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul style="font-family:Arial, sans-serif;font-size:14px;line-height:1.6;">
<li><strong>Supplier file complete:</strong> Registration certificate, tax identification number, bank details, and relevant health or safety certificates verified and filed before the first purchase order.</li>
<li><strong>Purchase order specific:</strong> Product description, grade, quantity, unit price, delivery date, packaging, and inspection terms stated in writing and acknowledged by the supplier.</li>
<li><strong>Delivery documents checked at gate:</strong> Delivery note, batch number, health certificate, and any import or phytosanitary permits match the purchase order.</li>
<li><strong>Inspection recorded before signing:</strong> Moisture, temperature, visual quality, and any required rapid test results written on the GRN or inspection sheet and signed by the receiving officer.</li>
<li><strong>Batch traceability captured:</strong> Farm or plot ID, harvest or production date, batch/lot number, and transport conditions recorded in the traceability register.</li>
<li><strong>Non-conforming goods quarantined:</strong> Rejected lots physically segregated, labelled, and documented; supplier notified in writing within 24 hours.</li>
<li><strong>Payment tied to inspection:</strong> Invoice matched to PO and GRN; payment released only after inspection approval and any corrective action agreed.</li>
<li><strong>Records archived:</strong> Supplier file, transaction file, and traceability file retained for at least five to seven years, or longer if required by the destination market.</li>
</ul>
