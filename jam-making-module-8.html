JAM MAKING · MODULE 8 OF 12

Packaging, Labelling and Branding

The jar, the label, the brand. What customers see before they taste. The layer between the jam in your kitchen and a product on a shelf.

By the end of this module you will be able to

  • Choose jars by size, shape, and quality — and know the cost trade-offs
  • Understand what your local labelling regulations require
  • Design a label that sells and complies
  • Build a brand identity that customers remember
  • Calculate packaging cost per jar and factor it into pricing
  • Create a launch-ready package for your first product
Lesson 8.1

Choosing your jars

The jar is the most visible part of your product after the jam itself. It also carries most of the packaging cost. Getting the jar right changes both the margin and the impression the customer takes away.

Jar size

SizeBest forWholesale appealRetail appeal
120ml / 4 ozSampler, gift sets, hotel portionsModerate — small unit price, high per-kgGood for gifting, low
200ml / 7 ozIndividual retail, single-person householdsGoodGood — best seller for premium jam
250ml / 8 ozStandard retailVery goodVery good — the industry standard
370ml / 12 ozFamily retail, best value per kgGoodGood
500ml / 16 ozFamily size, cateringLow — sits on shelf too longNiche

Start with 250ml. It is the standard for premium jam everywhere, the customer knows what to do with it, and it is the size most shops and hotels prefer to stock.

Jar material and quality

TypeCostQualityNotes
New clear glass jars with new lidsHighestBestThe standard for any serious producer. Food-safe, sterile by design, consistent dimensions
Reused commercial jars (from your own kitchen use)FreeNot for saleFine for personal jam, not for sale. Lids cannot be relied on for a second seal
Cheap imported jarsLowVariableCheck the seal quality. Some come with lids that fail more often.
Recycled/secondhand jarsFreeNot for saleCannot verify sterility, seal, or integrity. Not for commercial use
Fancy hexagonal or decorative jarsHigherPremiumJustify a higher retail price. Popular for gift markets.

Never sell in reused jars

Reused commercial jars from someone else's kitchen or from a recycling bin carry unknown history. The lid seal is compromised. The jar may have microscopic cracks. The glass may not be food-grade for long-term acidic storage. For anything you sell, use new jars with new lids.

The lid matters more than the jar

The jar is glass — it lasts forever. The lid is metal, plastic, and rubber — the seal is what decides shelf life.

  • Twist-off metal lid with plastisol lining — the industry standard. Best vacuum, best seal. Buy from suppliers who sell to food businesses, not general markets.
  • Bail-wire with rubber ring — traditional, visually appealing, good seal. Rubber ring must be replaced every batch.
  • Screw cap with heat-shrink seal — modern commercial look. Higher cost, requires more equipment.

Buy your lids and jars together from the same supplier. Lids that do not match the jar threads produce weak seals.

Lesson 8.2

Cost of packaging — the full picture

Packaging is often 25–40% of the total cost of a jar of jam. Getting the numbers right is where profit lives or dies.

Itemised cost per jar

ItemTypical cost (250ml jar)Bulk discount?
Glass jar$0.15–0.40Yes, at 100+ units
Lid (metal, gasket)$0.08–0.20Yes
Label (printed)$0.03–0.15Yes, at 500+ units
Shrink band (optional, tamper-evident)$0.02–0.05Yes
Cardboard outer box$0.05–0.15Only for wholesale
Total packaging per jar$0.33–0.95

Where to buy

  • Commercial packaging suppliers — best prices, MOQ (minimum order quantity) usually 100–1,000 units. Ask for a sample before ordering.
  • Wholesale markets — variable quality. Inspect the seal before buying in bulk. Good for small initial runs.
  • Online (Alibaba, local marketplaces) — cheapest if you can afford the MOQ. Watch for shipping and import duties.
  • Print shops for labels — local digital print is cheapest for runs under 500 labels. For 1,000+ labels, professional label printers are better.

The MOQ trap

Minimum order quantities look like savings until you have 2,000 jars and only need 200. Buy the minimum that gets you a reasonable price, not the volume that ties up your cash. Restocking is easy; unsold stock is expensive.

Factoring into your price

From Module 1, the true cost of a jar includes packaging. If your packaging is $0.60 and your ingredients and labour are $2.05, your true cost is $2.65. Multiply by 2.5–3× for retail = $6.60–$7.95 per jar.

If your packaging costs are higher, your retail price has to be higher. This is why choosing the right jar matters so much. A $0.20 saving per jar on the jar itself, at 500 jars a month, is $100 a month — over $1,200 a year.

Lesson 8.3

What must appear on the label

Labelling is regulated. Selling food without the required information is a legal offence in most countries and can result in fines, confiscation, or closure.

This module gives the categories. Your local standards authority gives the exact wording

The list below is what most countries require in some form. The exact format, language, and font size varies by jurisdiction. Before you print labels for sale, check with your national standards authority or your local public health office.

The universal requirements

ElementWhy
Product nameWhat the product is. "Strawberry Jam", "Mango Preserve", etc. If the fruit is not the main ingredient, you cannot call it that fruit's jam.
Ingredients listIn descending order by weight. Fruit, sugar, lemon juice, pectin.
Net weightIn grams or kilograms. The weight of the jam only, not including the jar.
Best-before dateWhen the product is at its best. Usually 12–24 months from date of manufacture for jam.
Batch numberTraceability. You need this to identify which batch a jar came from.
Producer name and addressWho made it. Full postal address in most jurisdictions.
Country of originWhere it was made.
Storage instructions"Store in a cool dark place. Refrigerate after opening."
Allergen warningsIf any of the top 8 allergens are present. Uncommon in jam, but check — some pectins contain traces.

Additional requirements in some places

  • Nutrition information — increasingly required, especially for export
  • Barcode — required by many retailers
  • Recycling information — varies
  • Sugar content or percentage of fruit — some countries require this
  • Country-specific language requirements

The one you can never miss

If you only get one thing right on your label, get the best-before date and batch number right. Food safety inspectors look for these first. A jar without them can be confiscated on the spot.

Lesson 8.4

Designing a label that sells

A label has to do two jobs: comply with the law, and convince the customer to buy. Most beginner labels do neither well — they list the requirements in tiny grey text and hope for the best.

What good labels do

  1. Communicate the fruit clearly — a customer scanning a shelf should know in half a second what the jam is
  2. Look professional — clean layout, consistent typography, no clutter
  3. Feel like the price point — a $7 jar needs a label that looks like $7, not $3
  4. Meet the legal requirements — without dominating the design
  5. Create a memorable brand — customer should recognise your jam on a second visit

The label layout

A simple layout. Brand at top, product name prominent, fruit identity obvious, legal information grouped at the bottom.

Colour and typography

  • Colours that match the fruit — red for strawberry, yellow-orange for mango, deep purple for blackcurrant. This is intuitive and it works.
  • Two fonts maximum — one for the brand and product name (can be decorative), one for the legal text (must be plain and readable).
  • Contrast is king — dark text on light background, or light on dark. Never grey on grey.
  • Size matters — the legal text must be at least 1.5mm tall. Too small and it fails inspection.

Where to get labels printed

MethodCost for 200 labelsBest for
Home printer + label paper$5–15Testing, family use, first 50 jars
Local digital print shop$30–80Small commercial runs, 100–500 labels
Online label printer$50–150Larger runs, waterproof and fade-resistant
Professional offset print$150–4001,000+ labels, best price per label

Do not use inkjet-printed labels for a commercial product

Inkjet ink runs and fades when it gets damp — and jam jars sit in humid fridges. Use laser printing on waterproof label paper, or proper commercial label printing. A label that has run after one week in the fridge is worse than no label at all.

Lesson 8.5

Building a brand identity

Your brand is not your logo. It is what customers remember about your product after they have finished the jar. Three things create it:

  1. A name — short, memorable, easy to spell, ideally your own or a place name
  2. A visual signature — colours, fonts, and a shape or symbol that stays the same on every product
  3. Consistent quality — the jam is the brand. A great label on a bad jar destroys the brand faster than no label at all.

Choosing a brand name

Good options, in rough order of strength:

  • Your own name — "Mary's Jam", "Tendai Homemade" — personal, trustworthy, easy to build
  • A place name — "Nyanga Kitchen", "Kariba Preserves" — regional identity
  • A story name — something that tells the customer about how the jam is made
  • A descriptive name — "Real Fruit Co.", "Slow Cooked Jams"

Weak names: long, hard to spell, generic ("Delicious Jams"), or that mean nothing ("ZT Enterprises").

Check the name before you commit

Before you print 500 labels, check that the name is not already in use in your country by another food business. A quick search on your national business registry website, or a search on Facebook and Instagram for the same name in your town, will tell you. If it is taken, choose another. Do not fight a trademark you did not know about.

Building a visual signature

Three consistent elements, used on every product:

  1. A signature colour — the gold on this course, for example, is a signature. It appears on the course pages, and the course certificates, and the welcome screens. Customers recognise it.
  2. A consistent font — the brand name should always look the same. On the label, on the website, on the WhatsApp catalog.
  3. A shape or symbol — a small illustration, a seal, a pattern. Something simple the eye can identify from across a room.

The brand in every interaction

Your brand is not just the label. It is:

  • How you answer WhatsApp messages from customers
  • How you package jars for delivery
  • Whether you deliver on time and in good condition
  • What you say when a customer has a complaint
  • How you follow up after a purchase

A small food business with consistent, friendly, reliable communication beats a large one that treats customers as transactions. This is your advantage.

Lesson 8.6

Your launch package

Everything you need to launch the first product. This is the checklist.

Before you print or buy in bulk

  1. Choose your first three products. Start with three, not ten. Most sales come from a handful of flavours.
  2. Choose your jar size and source. Standardise on one jar size and one lid type. Consistency matters.
  3. Decide your brand name. Check it is available. Register it if your country requires it.
  4. Design your label layout. One layout, three colour variations (one per product).
  5. Check labelling compliance. Take a draft label to your local public health office. Ask if it meets requirements.
  6. Print 100 labels as a test run. Not 500. Use them, refine, then do a real print run.
  7. Photograph your products properly. Good product photos are what sell through WhatsApp and Instagram. One clean image per flavour, on a plain background, with natural light.

Cost of a launch

ItemCost
100 jars with lids (250ml)$30–50
100 labels (digital print)$25–60
Fruit for first 100 jars$20–40
Sugar, pectin, lemon$10–20
Print materials, samples, misc$20–40
Total launch cost$105–210

At 100 jars and a $6 retail price, that is $600 gross revenue from the first production run — a payback of 3–5× on the initial investment.

The launch mistake

Beginners often launch with 10 flavours, 3 jar sizes, and no consistent label design. This dilutes effort, multiplies packaging cost, and makes the brand unrecognisable. Launch with three products, one jar, one label layout, one brand. Add flavours after the first three are proven.

Practical assignment — Module 8

Design your launch package. You do not need to print yet.

  1. Choose your brand name. State it, and write one sentence on why. Check it is not already in use by another food business in your country.
  2. Choose your first three products. Name them and give one reason each.
  3. Design your label. Draw it on paper or design it in a free tool like Canva. Include all the required legal elements from Lesson 8.3.
  4. Photograph the label mockup stuck on an actual jar (paper cut to size is fine). This is what your final product will look like.
  5. Cost your packaging. Get real quotes for jars, lids, and labels at 100, 250, and 500-unit quantities. State the price per jar at each quantity.
  6. Update your retail price based on the new packaging cost. State the new true cost and the new retail price.
  7. Photograph three jars of your proposed products (real jam, or mockups with paper labels) for use in your WhatsApp catalog and social media.

Points 1, 3 and 5 carry the most marks. Point 1 tests whether you can commit to a brand. Point 3 tests whether you can design a compliant label. Point 5 tests whether you can negotiate and compare real quotes.

Module 8 knowledge check

1. What is the standard retail jar size for premium jam?

  1. 100ml
  2. 250ml
  3. 1L
  4. Whatever is cheapest
Show answer

b. 250ml is the industry standard. It is what customers expect, what shops and hotels prefer to stock, and it supports a good retail price without sitting on the shelf too long. Start there.

2. Why can you not sell jam in reused commercial jars?

  1. They are too heavy
  2. The lid seal is compromised after first use, the jar's history is unknown, and it cannot be relied on for a second shelf life
  3. They are the wrong shape
  4. They contain chemicals
Show answer

b. Reused jars carry unknown history and the lid gasket cannot be relied on for a second seal. For anything you sell, use new jars with new lids — the integrity of the seal is the shelf life.

3. Which label element is most critical for food safety compliance?

  1. Your photo
  2. Best-before date and batch number
  3. Your WhatsApp number
  4. A decorative border
Show answer

b. Best-before date and batch number are the first things a food safety inspector checks. Without them a jar can be confiscated on the spot. Never print labels without these two.

4. Why should you avoid inkjet-printed labels for a commercial product?

  1. They are too expensive
  2. Inkjet ink runs and fades when damp — and jam jars sit in humid fridges
  3. Inkjet labels are not allowed by law
  4. Inkjet printers are slow
Show answer

b. Inkjet ink is water-soluble. On a jam jar in a fridge, it will run within a week. Use laser printing on waterproof label paper, or proper commercial label printing.

5. What is the correct launch strategy for a new jam business?

  1. Launch with 10 flavours, 5 jar sizes, and a full product range
  2. Launch with three products, one jar size, one label layout, one brand — then expand
  3. Launch with one product only
  4. Launch with a wholesale contract before making anything
Show answer

b. Three products, one jar, one label layout. This concentrates effort, keeps packaging simple, and builds a recognisable brand. Add flavours after the first three are proven to sell.
