1. Module 12 Lesson 1: Setting Up Your Shop for Daily Profit

Welcome to Module 12. This lesson focuses on the practical steps to set up a small shop or market stall that makes a profit every single day. Whether you sell tomatoes in Kumasi, airtime in Lagos, or second-hand clothes in Nairobi, the basics are the same. You need a location, a product, a price, and a system. Let us start with location. In African markets, location is not just about foot traffic. It is about who walks past, when they walk past, and what they are already buying. A spot near a busy matatu stage or a mosque exit on Friday can be worth more than a bigger stall in a quiet lane. Spend three days watching a spot before you rent it. Count people, note what they carry, and ask nearby traders what sells best. If you sell perishable goods like vegetables, you need shade and quick access to water. If you sell phone accessories, you need security and a place to charge your own phone. Write down your daily costs: rent, transport, ice, polythene bags, and any fees to the local council. If your rent is 2,000 shillings a day and you sell 5,000 shillings of goods with a 30 percent margin, you make 1,500 shillings before other costs. That is not profit yet. You must subtract transport, packaging, and your own food. Many traders fail because they count gross sales as profit. Do not do that. Keep a small notebook or use a simple phone app. Record every sale and every expense. At the end of the day, count your cash and your stock. If you started with 10,000 shillings in cash and stock and ended with 11,200, your real profit is 1,200. That number tells you if the day was good. Now think about your product mix. A shop that sells only one item is fragile. If you sell only eggs, a price drop or a disease outbreak can kill your business. Add complementary items: bread, milk, salt, or airtime. These bring people in and increase the average sale. But do not add too many items. Too much stock ties up your money and can expire. A good rule is to have three to five fast-moving items that make up 70 percent of your sales, and a few slow items that give you higher margins. Pricing is next. Many small shops in Africa use cost-plus pricing: you buy for 100, sell for 130. That is fine, but you must also watch what competitors charge. If the shop next door sells sugar at 120, you cannot sell at 150 unless you offer something extra, like credit or home delivery. Learn to calculate your margin. If you buy a crate of soda for 12,000 and sell each bottle for 500, and there are 24 bottles, your revenue is 12,000. That is zero profit. You need to buy at a lower price or sell at a higher price. Always negotiate with your supplier. Buy in bulk when you can, but only for items that do not expire quickly. For perishables, buy small and often. Now let us talk about display. In a market, your stall is your advertisement. Arrange goods so that colors pop and prices are visible. Put the cheapest items at the front to attract attention, and the higher-margin items at eye level. Use clean containers and avoid clutter. If you sell food, keep it covered. Customers in Africa are very sensitive to hygiene. A clean stall can charge slightly more than a dirty one. Security is also critical. Markets attract thieves. Do not keep all your cash in one pocket. Use a locked box and take money to a mobile money agent or bank regularly. If you can, work with a neighbor to watch each other's stalls when you need a break. Finally, build relationships. In African trading, trust is currency. Greet people, remember their names, and give small credit to reliable customers. But keep a written record of who owes what. A simple exercise book works. At the end of this lesson, write a one-page plan for your shop: location, three main products, your daily cost, your target profit, and one way you will keep records. Bring that plan to the next lesson. Remember, a shop is not just a place to sit. It is a system that turns goods into cash and cash into more goods. If you control that system, you control your profit.

2. Module 12 Lesson 2: Buying Smart from Suppliers and Farmers

In this lesson we look at the buying side of your small shop or market business. Many traders focus only on selling, but your profit is made when you buy. If you buy badly, no amount of selling will save you. In Africa, your suppliers might be farmers, wholesalers, importers, or even other traders. Each has different prices, credit terms, and reliability. Your job is to find the best combination for your business. Start with farmers if you sell fresh produce. Visit the farm or the local collection point. Talk to the farmer about volume, quality, and timing. If you buy tomatoes from a farmer in a village, you might pay half the price of the market. But you must add transport, spoilage, and your time. Do the math. If you buy a crate for 5,000 and transport costs 1,000, your cost is 6,000. If you can sell it for 9,000, your gross profit is 3,000. But if 20 percent of the tomatoes spoil, you only sell 7,200 worth. Your profit drops to 1,200. That is still okay, but you must plan for spoilage. For dry goods like rice, beans, or flour, wholesalers are usually better. Find the biggest wholesaler in your area. Ask about price breaks. For example, a bag of rice might cost 50,000 if you buy one, but 45,000 if you buy ten. If you cannot afford ten, team up with two or three other traders and buy together. This is called a buying group. It is common in West and East Africa. You pool your money, buy in bulk, and share the goods. You get a lower price and the wholesaler gets a bigger order. Everyone wins. But you must have a clear agreement. Write down how much each person pays and how much they get. Do not rely on memory. Now consider credit. Many suppliers will give you goods on credit if they trust you. This is a huge advantage because you can sell first and pay later. But credit is a trap if you are not disciplined. Only take credit for items you know you can sell quickly. If you take credit for 20 bags of maize and it takes two months to sell, you may be forced to sell at a low price to pay the supplier. That kills your margin. A good rule is to use credit only for fast-moving items that sell within one week. Always pay on time. Your reputation is your biggest asset. If you pay late, the supplier will stop giving you credit or charge you more. Next, learn to grade and sort. When you buy from farmers, you often get a mix of good and bad. Sort the good ones for your best customers and sell the rest at a discount. Do not throw away anything that can be sold. Even bruised tomatoes can be sold as tomato sauce or to a restaurant. This increases your overall revenue. You should also negotiate. In many African markets, the first price is not the final price. But do not negotiate just for the sake of it. Know the market price before you start. If you know that a bag of onions sells for 30,000 in the main market, you can confidently offer 25,000 to a farmer. If he says no, you can walk away. But if you do not know the price, you might pay too much or offend the supplier. Always be polite. A good relationship with a supplier can get you better prices, first pick of new stock, and even a small loan when you are in trouble. Keep a supplier notebook. Write down names, phone numbers, what they sell, their prices, and their credit terms. Update it every week. If one supplier fails you, you have a backup. Also, watch for seasonal changes. In Africa, prices for maize, tomatoes, and other crops drop during harvest and rise during the dry season. If you can store goods safely, you can buy low and sell high. But storage is not free. You need a dry, secure place. You may need to treat grains against weevils. Calculate the cost of storage and add it to your buying price. If storage costs more than the price increase, do not store. Finally, think about transport. The cost of moving goods from the supplier to your shop can eat your profit. If you buy from a distant market, you may need to hire a pickup or use a boda boda. Always negotiate the transport price before you load. Sometimes it is cheaper to buy from a closer supplier even if the price is slightly higher. Do a total cost comparison. At the end of this lesson, make a list of your top three suppliers. For each one, write their price, their credit terms, and how long they take to deliver. Then write one action you will take to improve your buying, such as joining a buying group or negotiating a better price. Remember, a smart buyer is a profitable seller.

3. Module 12 Lesson 3: Pricing, Discounts, and Customer Credit

Pricing is one of the hardest parts of running a small shop or market stall. Set your price too high and customers walk away. Set it too low and you work for nothing. In this lesson, we will cover how to set prices, when to give discounts, and how to manage customer credit without losing money. Let us start with the basics. Your price must cover three things: the cost of the goods, your operating expenses, and a profit for you. Many traders forget the second part. If you sell a bottle of water for 50 and you bought it for 40, you think you made 10. But if your rent, transport, and packaging cost you 5 per bottle, your real profit is only 5. So you need to know your break-even point. Break-even is the number of items you must sell to cover all your costs. If your daily costs are 3,000 shillings and you make 10 shillings profit per item, you must sell 300 items to break even. Anything above that is profit. Now, how do you set the actual price? Look at your competitors. In a market, prices are usually similar. If you charge much more, you must offer something extra: better quality, credit, delivery, or a friendly smile. If you charge much less, you may start a price war that hurts everyone. A common strategy in African markets is to have a standard price for regular customers and a slightly higher price for tourists or one-time buyers. But be careful. If people find out you charge different prices, they may lose trust. It is better to have one fair price and give a small discount for bulk purchases. Discounts are a powerful tool. Use them to move slow stock, reward loyal customers, and attract new buyers. For example, if you sell rice by the cup, you can offer a discount if someone buys a full bag. If you sell clothes, you can offer a small discount for cash instead of credit. But never give a discount that takes you below your cost. That is not a discount; that is a loss. A good rule is to set your discount from your profit, not from your cost. If you make 200 profit on an item, you can afford to give a 50 discount and still make 150. But if you only make 50 profit, a 50 discount means you work for free. Now let us talk about customer credit. In many African communities, credit is a normal part of business. A customer takes goods today and pays tomorrow or at the end of the month. This can build loyalty and increase sales. But it can also destroy your business if you are not careful. The first rule of credit is to know your customer. Do not give credit to strangers. Give it to people who have bought from you before and paid on time. The second rule is to set a limit. If a customer usually buys 1,000 shillings of goods, do not give them 10,000 in credit. Start small. The third rule is to write it down. Use a credit book or a phone app. Write the date, the customer's name, what they took, and how much they owe. Ask the customer to sign or initial. If they cannot write, make a mark and have a witness. The fourth rule is to follow up. Do not wait until the debt is huge. If a customer is one day late, send a friendly reminder. If they are one week late, visit them. If they are one month late, stop giving credit and ask for payment. Be firm but respectful. You can say, 'I need the money to buy more stock. Can you pay half today and half next week?' Most people will pay if you give them a chance. But some will not. If a customer refuses to pay, you must decide if the debt is worth the relationship. Sometimes it is better to write off a small debt and never give that person credit again. Do not let one bad customer ruin your business. You can also use technology. Mobile money is now common in many African countries. You can ask customers to pay via M-Pesa, Airtel Money, or MTN Mobile Money. This reduces the risk of carrying cash and makes it easier to track payments. If you have a smartphone, you can use a simple credit app or even a spreadsheet. But do not rely on technology alone. A paper backup is always good. Finally, think about your own credit. If you buy from suppliers on credit, you must manage that credit carefully. Do not use customer credit to pay supplier credit. That is a dangerous cycle. Instead, use your profit to pay suppliers. If you are short, talk to your supplier before the due date. Ask for a few more days. Most suppliers will understand if you communicate early. At the end of this lesson, review your prices. Are they covering all your costs? Are you giving discounts that make sense? Are you tracking customer credit? Write down one change you will make this week. For example, you might start a credit book or set a maximum credit limit of 2,000 shillings per customer. Remember, pricing and credit are not just about money. They are about trust, discipline, and respect. If you master them, your shop will grow.

4. Module 12 Lesson 4: Managing Stock, Records, and Daily Cash Flow

A small shop or market stall lives or dies by its stock and cash flow. If you run out of stock, you lose sales. If you have too much stock, your money is tied up and goods may expire. If you do not track cash, you will not know if you are making a profit. This lesson gives you practical tools to manage stock, keep simple records, and control daily cash flow. Let us start with stock. The goal is to have enough stock to meet demand without overbuying. This is called stock management. You can do it with a simple notebook or a phone. Every time you buy goods, write down the date, the item, the quantity, and the cost. Every time you sell, write down the date, the item, the quantity, and the price. At the end of the day, count what is left. This tells you what is selling fast and what is sitting on the shelf. For example, if you bought 50 loaves of bread and sold 45, you know bread is a fast mover. If you bought 20 tins of sardines and sold 2, you know sardines are slow. You can then decide to buy more bread and less sardines. A good rule is to keep a minimum stock level for fast movers. If you sell 10 loaves a day, never let your stock go below 15. That gives you a buffer if the supplier is late. For slow movers, buy small quantities. Do not tie up your money in items that do not sell. You should also watch for expiry dates. In Africa, many shops sell food, drinks, and medicine. If you sell expired goods, you can lose customers or even face legal trouble. Check dates every week. Put older items at the front and newer items at the back. This is called FIFO: first in, first out. If something is about to expire, sell it at a discount or bundle it with a fast-moving item. For example, if your milk expires in two days, offer a special: buy two packets of tea and get milk at half price. Now let us talk about records. Many small traders keep everything in their head. That works until you grow. Then you forget who owes you, how much you paid for an item, or whether you made a profit last month. You need three simple records: a sales book, an expense book, and a stock book. The sales book records every sale. The expense book records every payment: rent, transport, airtime, food, and supplier payments. The stock book records what you buy and what you have left. You can use one exercise book with three sections, or three separate books. If you have a smartphone, you can use a free app like a simple spreadsheet or a note-taking app. But do not overcomplicate it. The best record is the one you actually use. At the end of each day, spend five minutes writing down your sales and expenses. At the end of the week, add them up. Your profit is total sales minus total expenses. If you are not making a profit, look at your expenses. Can you reduce transport? Can you negotiate a lower rent? Can you buy in bulk to get a lower price? Now, cash flow. Cash flow is the movement of money in and out of your business. You can be profitable on paper but still run out of cash. For example, if you sell 10,000 shillings of goods on credit, you have a profit but no cash to buy more stock. That is why you must manage credit carefully, as we learned in the last lesson. You should also separate your business money from your personal money. This is one of the biggest mistakes small traders make. If you take money from the shop to pay for your child's school fees, you may not have enough to restock. Pay yourself a small salary instead. Decide on a fixed amount, say 500 shillings a day, and take only that. Any extra profit stays in the business for growth. If you need more, take a loan from the business and write it down. Pay it back. Another cash flow tool is the daily cash count. At the end of each day, count all the cash in your box. Subtract the cash you started with. That is your cash increase. Compare it to your sales record. If they do not match, you may have a leak. Maybe you forgot to record a sale, or maybe someone stole from you. Find the problem and fix it. Finally, plan for slow days. In many African markets, sales are high on weekends and low on Mondays. Or high at the end of the month when people get paid, and low in the middle. Know your pattern. On slow days, do not buy a lot of stock. Use the time to clean, arrange, and talk to customers. On busy days, make sure you have enough stock and enough change. At the end of this lesson, set up your three books if you do not have them. Then do a stock count. Write down what you have and what it is worth. This is your starting point. From now on, update it every day. Remember, a shop without records is like a car without a dashboard. You do not know how fast you are going or how much fuel you have. With good records, you can steer your business to profit.

5. Module 12 Lesson 5: Growing Your Shop and Beating Common Market Risks

You have learned how to set up, buy, price, and manage your shop. Now it is time to grow. Growth does not always mean opening a second shop. It can mean selling more to existing customers, adding new products, or finding new markets. But growth also brings risks. In this final lesson, we will look at practical ways to grow and how to protect your business from common dangers in African markets. Let us start with growth. The easiest way to grow is to increase sales to your current customers. You can do this by upselling. If a customer buys bread, ask if they need milk or sugar. If they buy a phone charger, ask if they need a cable. This is not pushy if you do it with a smile. It is helpful. You can also offer a loyalty card. For example, buy ten items and get one free. This keeps customers coming back. Another way to grow is to add a new product line. But do not add everything at once. Test one new item for two weeks. If it sells well, keep it. If not, drop it. You can also sell to other businesses. If you sell vegetables, talk to local restaurants. If you sell stationery, talk to schools. Bulk sales to businesses can be more stable than selling to individual customers. You can also grow by opening a second location. But be careful. A second shop doubles your rent, your staff, and your problems. Only open a second shop if your first shop is running smoothly without you being there every minute. You need a trusted person to manage it. And you need enough cash to cover three months of expenses for both shops. Many traders expand too fast and collapse. Do not be one of them. Now let us talk about risks. The first risk is theft. This can be from customers, employees, or even yourself. To reduce theft, keep cash in a locked box. Do not count money in front of customers. Install a small camera if you can afford it. If you have employees, pay them fairly and treat them well. A happy worker is less likely to steal. Also, do random stock counts. If you find a shortage, talk to your staff. Do not accuse without proof. The second risk is fire. Many markets in Africa have fires that destroy everything. To reduce fire risk, do not use candles or open flames near your stock. Keep a bucket of water or sand nearby. If you can, buy a small fire extinguisher. It is cheaper than losing your whole shop. The third risk is flooding. If your shop is in a low area, raise your goods on pallets or shelves. Keep important documents and cash in a waterproof bag. The fourth risk is illness. If you or your family get sick, you may not be able to open the shop. Build a small emergency fund. Even 1,000 shillings a week can help. You can also train a family member to run the shop for a day or two. The fifth risk is competition. A new shop may open next door and sell cheaper. Do not panic. Compete on service, not just price. Greet customers by name. Offer credit to reliable people. Keep your shop clean. Be open when you say you will be open. These small things build loyalty. The sixth risk is price changes. If the price of your main product doubles, you may lose customers if you raise your price. Try to negotiate with your supplier. Buy in bulk before the price goes up. Or switch to a cheaper alternative. For example, if cooking oil is expensive, you can sell palm oil or another local oil. The seventh risk is debt. If too many customers owe you money, you cannot restock. Keep credit small and short. The eighth risk is overstocking. Do not buy a lot of a new product just because it is cheap. Test it first. The ninth risk is personal spending. It is easy to take money from the shop for personal needs. Pay yourself a fixed salary and stick to it. The tenth risk is lack of records. Without records, you cannot see problems coming. Keep your books updated. Now, let us talk about opportunities. In many African countries, there are government programs and NGOs that support small businesses. They offer training, loans, or grants. Ask at your local council or chamber of commerce. There are also mobile money loans. But be careful. Only borrow if you have a clear plan to repay. Do not borrow for consumption. Borrow for stock or equipment that will increase your profit. Another opportunity is technology. You can use WhatsApp to take orders. You can use Facebook Marketplace to reach more customers. You can use mobile money to receive payments. You can even use a simple accounting app. Do not be afraid of technology. Start small. Learn one tool at a time. Finally, think about your exit. You will not run this shop forever. You may want to retire or move to another business. Build value in your shop so you can sell it one day. Keep your records clean. Build a good reputation. Train your staff. A shop with good records and loyal customers is worth more than one without. At the end of this lesson, write a one-page growth plan. What is one new product you will test? What is one risk you will reduce this month? What is one way you will use technology? Then take action. Remember, growing a small shop is not about luck. It is about discipline, learning, and treating people well. You have the tools. Now go and use them.

<h4 style="color:#b71c1c;margin-top:0;"> SAFETY WARNING: Food, Chemicals, and Money Handling</h4>
<p style="color:#333;margin-bottom:8px;">If your shop sells food, drinks, or agricultural chemicals, records alone will not protect your customers or your business. Follow these rules:</p>
<ul style="color:#333;margin:0;padding-left:20px;">
<li><strong>Never store chemicals, pesticides, or fuel near food, drinks, or cooking oil.</strong> Keep them on a separate shelf or in a locked cabinet. WHO and FAO advise strict separation of pesticides from food to prevent poisoning (WHO/FAO, 2016).</li>
<li><strong>Check expiry dates every week.</strong> Remove and record expired stock. Selling expired food or medicine is illegal in most African countries and can kill customers.</li>
<li><strong>Wash hands before handling food and after handling money.</strong> Money carries bacteria. Use clean water and soap where possible.</li>
<li><strong>Do not use the same container for chemicals and food.</strong> Never repackage pesticides into drink bottles. This causes child poisoning deaths across Africa every year.</li>
<li><strong>Keep a first aid kit and emergency numbers visible.</strong> For suspected poisoning, contact your local health facility immediately. Do not wait.</li>
<li><strong>Record any spoilage or damage immediately.</strong> Hidden spoilage becomes a hidden loss. Write it in the stock card the same day.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h3>How to Use These Records Together</h3>
<p>Records only work when they connect. The stock card connects to the cash sheet through sales and purchases. The supplier ledger connects to the cash sheet through payments. The debtor list connects to the cash sheet through credit sales and collections.</p>

<p>Every evening, do these five steps:</p>
<ol>
<li>Count the cash in your box. Write it on line 10 of the daily cash sheet.</li>
<li>Add up your sales book, credit collections, and cash payments. Fill lines 1 to 8.</li>
<li>Calculate expected cash on line 9. Compare with line 10. Write the difference on line 11.</li>
<li>Update every stock card for products sold or received that day.</li>
<li>If the difference is not zero, investigate before you sleep. Check credit given, money taken for home, and stock that left the shop without payment.</li>
</ol>

<p>Once a week, review your records. Which products sell fastest? Which customers owe you money? Which suppliers give the best price? Which products are close to expiry? This weekly review is where records turn into profit.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li> Count opening cash and stock every morning before you sell anything.</li>
<li> Write every sale in the sales book, even small credit sales to friends and family.</li>
<li> Record every purchase on the stock card the same day it arrives.</li>
<li> Keep one stock card per product and update it daily.</li>
<li> Fill the daily cash sheet every evening and calculate the difference.</li>
<li> Investigate any cash difference immediately, not the next week.</li>
<li> Review debtors weekly and follow up on overdue credit politely but firmly.</li>
<li> Keep records for at least 12 months and store them safely from water, fire, and theft.</li>
</ul>
