Lesson 1

Stem Rust and Yellow Rust — the Field Killers

In this lesson
  • Identify stem rust and yellow rust
  • Understand the Ug99 threat
  • Use resistant varieties and timely sprays

Stem rust (Puccinia graminis)

Reddish-brown pustules on stems and leaf sheaths. It ruptures the stem, causing lodging and shrivelled grain. The Ug99 race, first identified in Uganda in 1999, has spread across East Africa and the Middle East and is virulent against most older resistance genes.

Yellow (stripe) rust (Puccinia striiformis)

Yellow pustules in stripes along the leaf veins. Worse in cool, humid conditions. Can reduce yield 40–70% in susceptible varieties.

Leaf rust (Puccinia triticina)

Orange-brown pustules on leaves. Less devastating than stem rust but still reduces yield 20–40%.

Control

  1. Resistant varieties. The single most important control. Ask your research institute for the current recommended varieties with documented resistance.
  2. Early planting. Escape the peak rust period.
  3. Fungicide programme. Triazole or strobilurin fungicides at first symptom, repeated every 14 days. Usually 2–3 sprays.
  4. Rogueing. Remove infected plants early.
The rule: rust is prevented, not cured. Once it is on the flag leaf, spraying rarely saves the yield. Scout weekly and spray at first symptom.
Lesson 2

Septoria and Leaf Blotch

In this lesson
  • Identify septoria leaf blotch
  • Understand its weather triggers
  • Time fungicide sprays

Septoria tritici blotch

Yellow-brown lesions with small black dots (pycnidia) on leaves. Starts on lower leaves and moves upward. Worse in cool, wet weather. Can reduce yield 30–50%.

Septoria nodorum (glume blotch)

Brown lesions on leaves, glumes and nodes. Worse in warm, wet weather. Reduces grain weight and quality.

Control

  1. Resistant varieties. Available in most national programmes.
  2. Rotation and residue management. Plough in or remove wheat residue — septoria survives on stubble.
  3. Fungicide programme. Triazole fungicides (epoxiconazole, propiconazole) or strobilurin mixtures at first symptom, repeated every 14–21 days. Usually 2–3 sprays.
  4. Avoid overhead irrigation in cool wet weather. Wets leaves and spreads spores.
Lesson 3

Fusarium Head Blight

In this lesson
  • Recognise fusarium head blight
  • Understand the mycotoxin risk
  • Prevent with variety, rotation and timing

Symptoms

Bleached spikelets on the head, sometimes with pink or orange fungal growth. Kernels are shrivelled and chalky — often called "tombstone kernels".

Mycotoxin risk

Fusarium produces deoxynivalenol (DON) and other mycotoxins. Regulatory limits for DON in wheat flour range from 750 ppb (EU) to 1,000 ppb (USA). Flour mills test and reject loads above the limit.

Control

  1. Resistant varieties. Partial resistance is available in most national programmes.
  2. Rotation. Avoid wheat after maize — maize residue hosts Fusarium. Rotate with a legume or a break crop.
  3. Residue management. Plough in or remove maize and wheat residue.
  4. Avoid overhead irrigation at flowering. Wets the head and triggers infection.
  5. Fungicide at flowering. Some fungicides (metconazole, prothioconazole) reduce fusarium infection if applied at early flowering.
  6. Test before sale. If you have any suspicion, send a sample to a lab for DON testing.
Lesson 4

Smuts, Bunts and Seed-Borne Disease

In this lesson
  • Identify loose smut, covered smut and bunt
  • Prevent with seed treatment
  • Avoid planting infected seed

Loose smut (Ustilago tritici for wheat; U. nuda for barley)

The entire head is replaced by a black, dusty spore mass. The spores blow away leaving a bare rachis. Seed-borne — the fungus infects the embryo of the seed.

Covered smut (Ustilago hordei for barley)

The head is replaced by a black spore mass but the membrane remains, so the head looks intact. Seed-borne and soil-borne.

Bunt (Tilletia spp.)

The grain is replaced by a black, smelly spore mass. The head looks normal but the kernels are "bunt balls". Grain smells of fish — buyers reject it immediately.

Control

  • Certified clean seed. The most important control.
  • Seed treatment. Carboxin, triadimenol, or similar systemic fungicide. Follow label rate.
  • Rotation. Break the disease cycle.
  • Resistant varieties. Some varieties carry resistance.
Lesson 5

Scouting and the Spray Programme

In this lesson
  • Set a weekly scouting routine
  • Decide when spraying is justified
  • Respect pre-harvest intervals

Weekly scouting (20 min per hectare)

  1. Walk a W pattern
  2. At 10 points, check 5 plants: upper leaves (rust, septoria), stems (stem rust), heads (fusarium at flowering)
  3. Record observations with dates
  4. Photograph anything uncertain

Spray decisions

  • Rust: spray immediately at first symptom. Triazole or strobilurin.
  • Septoria: spray if lesions are on >10% of the leaf area.
  • Fusarium: spray at early flowering if weather is wet and warm — metconazole or prothioconazole.

Pre-harvest intervals

Wheat and barley PHIs are 21–35 days for most fungicides. Respect them — residues are tested by flour mills and breweries.

Knowledge check — Module 4

1. What is the deadliest rust in Africa?

Correct: stem rust Ug99. Virulent against most older resistance genes.

2. What weather triggers septoria?

Correct: cool and wet. Septoria thrives in cool humid conditions.

3. What is the biggest risk from fusarium head blight?

Correct: mycotoxins. Mills test and reject loads above limits.

4. What prevents smuts and bunts?

Correct: certified seed + seed treatment. Prevention starts before planting.

5. When do you spray for fusarium head blight?

Correct: early flowering. Metconazole or prothioconazole.
