Lessons

1. Butt Joints

A butt joint is when you place two pieces of aluminium edge to edge, like two planks meeting on a table. This is the most common joint for welding flat sheets, pipes, or extrusions. For aluminium, you must prepare the edges carefully. Use a stainless steel wire brush dedicated to aluminium onlynever use it on steel. Clean the edges and 25mm back from the edge on both sides. Wipe with acetone or methylated spirits to remove oil. For 3mm to 6mm thick aluminium, a square edge is fine. For thicker than 6mm, bevel the edges to 60 included angle (30 each side) using a grinder or file. Leave a root gap of 1.5mm to 2mm for full penetration on 3mm to 6mm plate. If you have no gap, you risk lack of fusion. Clamp the pieces flat and aligned. Tack weld at both ends and every 150mm. Use a push angle of 10 to 15 with your TIG torch. For MIG, use a 10 drag angle. Set your machine: for 3mm aluminium, TIG at 120-150 amps AC, MIG at 130-160 amps with 1.2mm wire. Use pure argon gas at 10-12 litres per minute. Weld steadily, watching the molten pool wet out on both sides. If the pool looks like a bright mirror and flows, you are good. If it balls up, you need more heat or better cleaning. After welding, wire brush the weld while still warm to remove smut. In Africa, where power can be unstable, use a generator with stable output or an inverter welder that tolerates voltage drops. Practice on scrap pieces first.

2. Tee and Lap Joints

A tee joint is when one piece stands upright on another, forming a T shape. A lap joint is when two pieces overlap like a book cover. Both are common in aluminium fabricationthink window frames, truck bodies, and water tanks. For a tee joint, the vertical piece sits on the flat piece. You need to weld on both sides for strength. Clean both surfaces with a stainless steel brush and acetone. For 3mm aluminium, set your TIG at 130-160 amps AC. Use a 2.4mm tungsten electrode with a balled tip for AC. For MIG, use 1.2mm wire at 140-170 amps. Use a push angle for TIG and a slight drag for MIG. Keep your arc shortabout 2mm to 3mm. Watch the edges of the vertical piece; they melt faster than the flat piece. Direct more heat to the flat piece. For lap joints, the overlap should be at least 3 times the thickness of the thinner piece. For 3mm sheets, overlap 9mm. Weld along the edge of the top piece. Use a slight back-and-forth motion to wash the pool onto both pieces. If you are welding thin sheets (1.5mm to 2mm), use a heat sinka piece of copper or aluminium backingto prevent burn-through. In many African workshops, you can use a heavy steel plate as a heat sink. Clamp your work securely. Tack weld every 100mm to 150mm. For tee joints, tack both sides. After welding, check for undercut at the toes of the weld. If undercut is more than 0.5mm, reduce your travel speed or increase wire feed. Clean the weld with a stainless brush. Remember, aluminium conducts heat away fast, so you may need to increase amperage by 10% compared to steel of the same thickness.

3. Corner and Edge Joints

Corner joints meet at 90 degrees, like the corner of a box. Edge joints meet along their thin edges, like two coins standing on edge. These are tricky because aluminium melts quickly at edges. For a corner joint, you can weld from the outside (a V-groove) or inside (a fillet). For 3mm aluminium, outside corner welds need a root gap of 0mm to 1mm and a bevel if thicker than 6mm. Set your TIG at 100-130 amps AC for 3mm. Use a 1.6mm or 2.4mm tungsten. For MIG, use 1.0mm or 1.2mm wire at 120-150 amps. Use a push angle of 5 to 10. The key is to move fastedges overheat and collapse. For inside corner welds, treat it like a tee joint but with less access. Use a short arc and watch for the pool to wet both walls. For edge joints, you are welding two thin edges together. This is common for thin sheet (1mm to 2mm) in ductwork or panels. You need a backing platecopper or aluminiumto support the molten pool. Use low amperage: 60-90 amps AC for TIG on 1.5mm. Use a 1.6mm tungsten and 1.6mm filler rod. Move quickly with a slight weave. If you burn through, stop, let it cool, and start again. In Africa, where thin aluminium sheets are common in roofing and signage, practice on scrap. Use a foot pedal if you have one to control heat. For corner joints, tack weld every 50mm to keep alignment. After welding, grind the corner smooth if needed for appearance. Always wear a respirator when grinding aluminiumthe dust is harmful.

4. Flat, Horizontal, Vertical and Overhead

Welding positions change how gravity affects your molten pool. Flat (1G) is easiest: you weld on top of a horizontal surface. The pool sits still. Use your normal settings. For 3mm aluminium, TIG at 120-150 amps, MIG at 130-160 amps. Horizontal (2G) is on a vertical surface but the weld axis is horizontal. The pool wants to sag downward. Reduce amperage by 10% and increase travel speed. Use a slight upward push angle for TIG. Vertical (3G) can be uphill or downhill. Uphill gives better penetration but is harder. For 3mm, use 100-130 amps TIG, 110-140 amps MIG. Use a triangular motion: pause at the sides, move quickly across the centre. Downhill is faster but prone to lack of fusion. For beginners, start with downhill on thin material. Overhead (4G) is welding above your head. This is the hardest. You must fight gravity. Use lower amperageabout 20% less than flat. For 3mm, TIG at 90-120 amps, MIG at 100-130 amps. Use a very short arc and fast travel. Wear a leather cape and sleeves to protect from sparks. In Africa, many jobs are in the field: repairing truck beds (horizontal), water tanks (vertical), or structural frames (overhead). Practice each position on scrap. For vertical and overhead, use smaller diameter filler rods (1.6mm or 2.4mm) for better control. Keep your tungsten sharp for TIG. For MIG, use a slightly longer stick-out (12mm to 15mm) to see the pool. Always clamp your work. If you are welding overhead, make sure no flammable materials are below. Use a fire blanket. Remember, aluminium freezes fast, so you must move quickly but steadily.

5. Out-of-Position Welding on Aluminium

Out-of-position welding means any position other than flat: horizontal, vertical, or overhead. Aluminium is harder because it conducts heat away fast and has a low melting point. You need to adjust your technique. First, cleaning is critical. Use a stainless steel brush and acetone. For out-of-position, use a slightly smaller tungsten for TIG (1.6mm or 2.4mm) and a smaller filler rod (1.6mm or 2.4mm). For MIG, use a 1.0mm or 1.2mm wire. Set your machine: for vertical uphill on 3mm, TIG at 100-130 amps AC, MIG at 110-140 amps. For overhead, reduce by another 10-20%. Use a push angle for TIG (5 to 10) and a drag angle for MIG (5 to 10). Keep your arc very short1.5mm to 2mm. For vertical uphill, use a weave: move up, pause at each side, then quickly across. This lets the pool cool slightly. For overhead, use a stringer bead (no weave) and move fast. If the pool starts to sag, increase travel speed or reduce amperage. Use a heat sink if possibleclamp a copper or aluminium bar behind the joint. In Africa, you can use a piece of heavy aluminium as a backing. For TIG, use a foot pedal to control heat moment by moment. For MIG, you can use a pulsed MIG setting if available. Practice on scrap in each position. Start with horizontal, then vertical downhill, then vertical uphill, then overhead. Always wear proper PPE: leather gloves, apron, and a welding helmet with a clear lens. Keep your work area clear of flammable materials. After welding, inspect for undercut, porosity, and lack of fusion. If you see defects, grind out and re-weld. Out-of-position welding takes practice, but with patience, you can master it.

6. Multi-Pass Welds

Multi-pass welding means making several passes to fill a joint, usually on thick aluminium (6mm and above). Each pass builds on the previous one. For a butt joint on 10mm aluminium, you might need 3 to 4 passes. First, prepare a V-groove: bevel each edge to 30 (60 included) and leave a root face of 1.5mm to 2mm. Clean with a stainless brush and acetone. For the root pass, use TIG at 150-180 amps AC with a 3.2mm tungsten and 3.2mm filler rod. Or MIG at 180-220 amps with 1.2mm wire. Use a root gap of 2mm to 3mm. Weld the root pass, ensuring full penetration. Let it cool, then wire brush. For the second pass (hot pass), increase amperage by 10% and use a slight weave to fill the groove. For the third and fourth passes (fill and cap), use a weave motion, pausing at the sides to fuse into the base metal. Keep the interpass temperature below 120Cif it gets too hot, wait. In Africa, where large aluminium structures like truck frames or boat hulls are common, multi-pass is essential. Use a temperature stick or touch the metal with a gloved handif it's too hot to touch, let it cool. For each pass, clean with a stainless brush. Watch for slag inclusionsaluminium doesn't have slag like steel, but oxides can form. Use a push angle for TIG and a drag for MIG. For the cap pass, make it slightly convex and uniform. After welding, inspect with a magnifying glass for cracks or porosity. If you find defects, grind out and re-weld. Multi-pass welds are strong but take time. Practice on scrap thick plates. Use a foot pedal for TIG to control heat. For MIG, use a spray transfer mode if possible. Remember, aluminium conducts heat, so preheating to 100C can help on thick sections. But never exceed 150C or you risk losing strength.

<h2>Practical Reference: Joint Types and Positions for Aluminium Welding</h2>

<p>In aluminium fabrication across Africa from truck body builders in Nairobi and Lagos to window-frame workshops in Accra and irrigation-pipe fabricators in the Nile Delta the single biggest driver of rework, rejected work, and lost margin is getting the <strong>joint type</strong> and <strong>welding position</strong> wrong before the arc even starts. This practical reference module consolidates the six lessons in this unit butt joints, tee and lap joints, corner and edge joints, flat/horizontal/vertical/overhead positions, out-of-position welding on aluminium, and multi-pass welds into a single shop-floor resource you can print, laminate, and pin above the bench.</p>

<p>Aluminium behaves differently from steel in ways that directly affect joint design and position selection. Its thermal conductivity is roughly <strong>46 times that of steel</strong>, its melting point (~660 C) is about half that of steel, and its oxide layer (AlO) melts at ~2,072 C far above the base metal. That combination means heat runs away from the weld pool fast, oxide must be mechanically or chemically removed before welding, and once a joint is positioned badly, the welder fights gravity, distortion, and cold lapping all at once. For an African SME running a 35 person fabrication cell, the cost of one failed aluminium joint is not just the filler wire it is the customer relationship, the transport back to site, and the reputation in a market where referrals drive 6080% of new work.</p>

<h3>Joint Type Selection at a Glance</h3>

<p>The five fundamental joint types butt, tee, lap, corner, and edge each have a different load path, heat-sink behaviour, and distortion signature in aluminium. Choosing the wrong one is the most common cause of premature cracking in trailers, water tanks, and structural frames.</p>

<h3>Position Selection and Its Effect on Productivity</h3>

<p>Position is not just a welder comfort issue it changes deposition rate, defect risk, and the amount of grinding and rework. In a typical African SME aluminium cell, a welder working flat can deposit 35 kg of filler per shift; the same welder working overhead on the same joint may deposit less than 1 kg, with double the defect rate. That is a direct labour-cost multiplier.</p>

<h3>Out-of-Position Welding on Aluminium</h3>

<p>Aluminium is especially unforgiving out of position because its low melting point and high thermal conductivity mean the pool freezes almost as soon as the arc moves away but it also means the pool can collapse suddenly if travel speed drops. For African fabricators working on truck tippers, fuel tankers, and rooftop solar mounting frames, out-of-position work is unavoidable. The practical rules are:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Use pulsed GMAW (MIG) wherever possible.</strong> Pulsed transfer gives a controlled droplet detachment that resists gravity, and is far more forgiving than short-circuit transfer in vertical and overhead positions.</li>
<li><strong>Reduce wire feed speed by 1020%</strong> and increase travel speed. This keeps the pool small and fast-freezing.</li>
<li><strong>Preheat thick sections (10 mm) to 80120 C.</strong> This is counter-intuitive for overhead work, but it prevents lack of fusion at the root while allowing you to use lower current.</li>
<li><strong>For 3F and 4F fillets, use a slight weave (max 2 wire diameter)</strong> and pause briefly at each toe. This wets the toes and prevents undercut.</li>
<li><strong>Never weld vertical-down on aluminium thicker than 6 mm.</strong> Penetration is unreliable and lack of fusion is almost guaranteed.</li>
</ul>

<div style="background:#ffebee;border-left:6px solid #c62828;padding:12px 16px;margin:18px 0;">
<strong style="color:#b71c1c;"> SAFETY WARNING ALUMINIUM WELDING HAZARDS</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#212121;">Aluminium welding produces <strong>ozone, nitrogen oxides, and ultrafine metal fume</strong> that are more hazardous than steel fume. In enclosed African workshop conditions common in metal-fabrication clusters in Nairobi's Gikomba, Lagos's Ladipo, or Johannesburg's Germiston fume can accumulate within minutes. <strong>Always use local exhaust ventilation (LEV) at the arc, or a supplied-air respirator (P3 filter minimum).</strong> Aluminium is also highly reflective to UV arc eye can occur from reflected radiation off bright surfaces. Wear a welding helmet with shade 1013 for GMAW, and use flame-resistant cotton or leather sleeves. Never weld aluminium near chlorinated solvents (degreasers) the UV arc breaks them down into phosgene gas. Keep a dry-powder or CO extinguisher within 3 m; aluminium fines and chips are combustible.</p>
</div>

<h3>Multi-Pass Welds: When and How</h3>

<p>Single-pass welds are the goal for thin aluminium (6 mm). Above 6 mm, and especially for butt joints in structural sections, multi-pass welding is required to fill the groove without exceeding the heat input that would cause distortion or hot cracking. Multi-pass also gives the welder a chance to inspect each pass critical when the joint will carry dynamic load (trailer chassis, crane booms, pressure vessels).</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ol style="line-height:1.7;">
<li><strong>Confirm joint type against load path.</strong> Butt for tension, tee for shear/compression, lap for thin sheet and repair, corner for box sections, edge for flanged thin-gauge.</li>
<li><strong>Clean oxide and hydrocarbons within 30 minutes of welding.</strong> Stainless-steel wire brush (dedicated to aluminium only) + acetone or isopropyl alcohol. Never use a brush that has touched steel.</li>
<li><strong>Select position deliberately.</strong> If the joint can be rotated to flat using a positioner or a second pair of hands, do it flat welding is 24 more productive and far less defect-prone.</li>
<li><strong>Set machine for the position, not the plate.</strong> Reduce voltage 12 V and WFS 1020% for vertical and overhead compared to flat.</li>
<li><strong>Control interpass temperature.</strong> 120 C max for 6xxx alloys use a contact thermometer or temperature crayon. Exceeding this causes hot cracking and loss of T6 properties.</li>
<li><strong>Inspect each pass before the next.</strong> Look for lack of fusion at sidewalls, porosity, and undercut. Grind out defects before re-welding never weld over them.</li>
<li><strong>Manage fume and UV.</strong> LEV at the arc, shade 1013 helmet, flame-resistant clothing. In enclosed shops, forced ventilation is non-negotiable.</li>
<li><strong>Document the weld.</strong> For structural or customer-critical joints, record welder ID, filler batch, parameters, and date. This is your defence if a failure claim arises and increasingly a tender requirement for NGO, government, and mining contracts across Africa.</li>
</ol>
