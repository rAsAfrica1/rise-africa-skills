Lessons

1. Cutting chops, roasts, and steaks from the loin

The loin runs from the shoulder to the ham along the back. It is the most valuable cut because it yields chops, roasts, and steaks. First, remove the loin from the carcass by cutting along the backbone with a meat saw or cleaver. Then trim off excess fat, leaving about 1 cm (half an inch) of fat for flavor. Remove the tenderloin (the long, thin muscle underneath the backbone) and set it aside for steaks or roasts.

For chops, cut the loin into slices 2.5 to 3 cm (1 to 1.25 inches) thick. Use a sharp knife or saw. Each chop should have a bit of fat and maybe a piece of bone. For roasts, cut larger sections, about 15 to 20 cm (6 to 8 inches) long. These can be bone-in or boneless. For steaks, cut thinner slices, about 1.5 to 2 cm (0.6 to 0.8 inches) thick, from the tenderloin or from boneless loin.

In African markets, chops are often sold fresh for grilling or frying. Roasts are popular for Sunday meals or celebrations. Steaks are quick to cook and can be sold at a premium. Price accordingly: chops might sell for 10-15% more than roasts, and steaks for 20-30% more. Always keep your knives sharp and your cutting surface clean. A sharp knife makes cleaner cuts and reduces waste.

After cutting, trim any silver skin or gristle. These are tough and reduce value. Use a boning knife to remove them. Package chops and steaks in trays with plastic wrap for display. Roasts can be tied with butcher's twine to hold their shape. Label each cut with its name and weight. In many African countries, customers prefer to see the cut before buying, so display them neatly.

Remember, the loin can also be cured into back bacon or smoked. But for fresh retail, focus on chops, roasts, and steaks. Cut to consistent sizes so customers know what they are getting. Keep everything cold (below 4Â°C) until sale. This ensures safety and quality.

2. Preparing shoulder for stewing and braising

The shoulder is a tough but flavorful cut. It comes from the front leg and upper chest. It contains a lot of connective tissue, which makes it perfect for slow cooking like stewing and braising. To prepare, first separate the shoulder from the carcass by cutting through the joint. Then remove the skin and excess fat, leaving some fat for moisture. You can keep the bone in or debone it. Bone-in shoulder is often cheaper and adds flavor.

Cut the shoulder into cubes for stewing. Aim for 3 to 4 cm (1.2 to 1.6 inches) pieces. Remove any large pieces of gristle or sinew. For braising, you can cut larger chunks, about 5 to 6 cm (2 to 2.5 inches). In African markets, shoulder is often sold as bone-in pieces for stew or as boneless cubes. Stewing meat is a staple in many dishes, like Nigerian goat meat stew or South African potjiekos, but with pork it works well too.

Packaging: For stewing cubes, pack in 500g or 1kg portions in plastic bags or containers. Label as 'stewing pork' or 'shoulder cubes'. For braising, you can sell larger pieces or whole shoulder roasts. Many customers prefer to buy shoulder on the bone for soups. Price shoulder lower than loin cuts because it requires more cooking. In local markets, shoulder might sell for 20-30% less than chops.

To add value, you can marinate or season the cubes before packaging. But if selling fresh, keep it simple. Always keep the meat cold. If you have a grinder, you can also mince shoulder for sausages or ground pork, which is popular for burgers and meatballs. But for this lesson, focus on stewing and braising cuts.

When cutting, use a heavy cleaver for bone-in pieces and a boning knife for deboning. Work on a sturdy cutting board. Remember to wash your hands and tools often. In many African communities, customers buy meat daily, so freshness is key. Cut to order if possible. This reduces waste and ensures customer satisfaction.

3. Making belly cuts for bacon and ribs

The belly is the underside of the pig, from the chest to the hind legs. It is fatty and flavorful. It yields two main retail cuts: bacon and ribs. To start, remove the belly from the carcass by cutting along the bottom of the ribs. You can keep the skin on or remove it. For bacon, you will cure and smoke the belly. For ribs, you will cut the rib section from the belly.

First, separate the ribs from the belly. The ribs are the bones along the side. Use a saw or cleaver to cut through the ribs, leaving some meat on them. You can cut them into individual ribs or leave them as a rack. In African markets, ribs are often sold fresh for grilling or stewing. They are popular for braais (barbecues) in South Africa or suya in Nigeria. Cut ribs into manageable pieces, about 10-15 cm (4-6 inches) long.

For bacon, the belly is cured with salt, sugar, and nitrates (if available) for several days, then smoked or dried. If you don't have curing salts, you can make fresh pork belly for roasting. Cut the belly into rectangular slabs, about 20-30 cm (8-12 inches) long. Score the skin if present. In many African countries, bacon is a niche product, but growing in urban areas. You can sell fresh belly as 'pork belly' for roasting or frying.

Packaging: Wrap bacon in plastic or vacuum seal if possible. Label with curing date and weight. For ribs, package in trays with plastic wrap. Price bacon higher than fresh belly because of processing. Ribs are usually priced similar to shoulder. In local markets, you might sell more fresh belly than bacon. So assess demand.

Always keep the belly cold during processing. Use a sharp knife to trim excess fat. Save trimmings for sausage or lard. Remember, the belly is about 15% of the carcass weight. So from a 100 kg pig, you get about 15 kg of belly. Plan your cuts accordingly.

4. Trimming and portioning ham for roasting or curing

The ham is the hind leg of the pig. It is a large, lean cut that can be sold fresh for roasting or cured into ham. First, remove the ham from the carcass by cutting through the hip joint. Use a meat saw to cut through the bone. Then trim off excess fat, leaving a thin layer for flavor. Remove the skin if you plan to cure, but leave it on for fresh roasting to keep moisture.

For fresh ham roasts, you can cut the whole ham into smaller roasts, about 1-2 kg each. Or you can sell the whole ham. In African markets, whole hams are often sold for celebrations. Cut portions to suit local demand: some customers want small roasts for family meals, others want large for parties. Use a sharp knife to make clean cuts. For curing, you will need to brine or dry-cure the ham. A simple brine: 1 kg salt, 500 g sugar, 100 g curing salt (if available), in 10 liters water. Soak ham for 1 day per kg. Then smoke or air-dry.

If you don't have curing facilities, focus on fresh ham. Cut into roasts or steaks. Ham steaks are thick slices, about 2 cm (0.8 inches). They can be grilled or fried. Roasts are larger, 1-2 kg. In many African countries, fresh ham is cheaper than loin but more expensive than shoulder. Price accordingly.

Packaging: For fresh roasts, wrap in plastic and label. For cured ham, vacuum seal if possible. Display in a cool area. Always keep ham below 4Â°C. When cutting, be careful of the bone. A cleaver helps. Remove the aitchbone (pelvic bone) if you want boneless roasts. This takes practice, but it increases value.

Remember, the ham is about 20-25% of carcass weight. So a 100 kg pig yields 20-25 kg of ham. Plan your cuts to maximize profit. If you have a smoker, cured ham can fetch a premium. But start with fresh if you are new.

5. Packaging cuts for display in a butcher shop or market

Packaging is crucial for selling meat. It protects the meat, keeps it clean, and makes it attractive to customers. In African markets, many butchers sell meat on open tables or hanging. But for retail cuts, proper packaging can increase sales. Use food-grade plastic wrap, trays, and bags. For chops and steaks, place on styrofoam trays and wrap with cling film. Label with cut name, weight, and price. For roasts, wrap in butcher paper or plastic bags. For stewing cubes, pack in clear plastic bags so customers can see the meat.

If you have a vacuum sealer, use it for longer shelf life. Vacuum-sealed meat can last up to 2 weeks in the fridge, compared to 3-5 days for wrapped meat. In many African countries, vacuum sealing is rare but can be a selling point. If not, use good quality cling film and change it daily. Keep displays refrigerated or on ice. In hot climates, meat spoils quickly. So use cool boxes or refrigerated display cases if possible.

Labeling: Write the cut name, weight, and date. Include cooking suggestions if helpful. For example, 'Pork Chops - Perfect for Grilling'. This adds value. Price tags should be clear. In local markets, customers often bargain, so set prices with some margin. But be fair.

Display: Arrange cuts neatly. Group similar cuts together. Put premium cuts at eye level. Use lighting to make meat look red and fresh. But avoid direct sunlight. Keep the display clean and tidy. Rotate stock so older meat is sold first. Train your staff to handle meat hygienically. Wash hands, wear gloves, and clean surfaces regularly.

In many African communities, trust is key. Customers buy from butchers they know. So consistent quality and honest weights build repeat business. Packaging that looks professional can set you apart. Even simple improvements like using a clean tray and plastic wrap can boost sales.

6. Pricing cuts based on local demand and competition

Pricing is both an art and a science. You need to cover costs, make a profit, and stay competitive. Start by calculating your cost per cut. For a whole pig, you pay a certain price. Then you have processing costs: labour, packaging, electricity, rent. Divide total costs by the number of cuts to get a base cost. Then add a markup. In African markets, markups vary. For premium cuts like loin, you might mark up 30-50%. For shoulder, 20-30%. For belly, 25-40%.

But you must consider local demand. If everyone wants chops, you can charge more. If there is a glut of shoulder, lower the price. Check competitors' prices. Visit other butcher shops or market stalls. See what they charge for similar cuts. If you are the only butcher, you have more flexibility. But if there are many, you must be competitive. In many African cities, pork is not as popular as beef or chicken, so pricing must be attractive.

Seasonality matters. During holidays like Christmas or Eid, demand for roasts and hams goes up. You can increase prices by 10-20%. During Ramadan, pork sales may drop in Muslim areas. So adjust. Also consider the cut's yield. A bone-in roast has less meat per kg than boneless. So price bone-in lower per kg. For example, if boneless loin is 1000 KES/kg, bone-in might be 800 KES/kg.

Promotions: Offer discounts for bulk purchases. Sell 'value packs' with a mix of cuts. This moves slower-selling items. Use social media or WhatsApp to announce specials. In many African markets, word of mouth is powerful. So give good value and customers will return.

Record keeping: Keep a simple notebook of costs and sales. Track which cuts sell best and at what price. Adjust weekly. Don't be afraid to experiment. Start with a price, see how customers react, and change if needed. The goal is to sell all cuts at a profit, not just the premium ones.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Butchering into Retail Cuts for Local Markets

In most African pig value chains, the loin, shoulder, belly, and ham leave the carcass as a single "half-carcass" and lose 20â35% of their potential retail value. This module shows you how to convert that half-carcass into chops, roasts, steaks, stewing cubes, bacon, ribs, and portioned ham sold at prices set by your local market, not by the abattoir's wholesale rate. The objective is simple: every kilogram you cut and package should earn more than the kilogram you sold whole. Work through the cuts below, then use the pricing tables to build a margin sheet for your own shop or stall.

1. Cutting Chops, Roasts, and Steaks from the Loin

The loin is the highest-value primal on the carcass and the easiest to ruin with inconsistent cutting. Remove the loin from the shoulder at the 4th/5th rib and from the ham at the last lumbar vertebra. Trim the fat cover to 6â8 mm â thicker than that and chops look greasy on display; thinner and they dry out on the grill.

Cut bone-in chops at 2.5â3 cm. This is the thickness local grillers and butcheries ask for because it cooks evenly over charcoal. Sell the rib end (nearest the shoulder) as chops because it is well marbled. Cut the centre loin into chops for the top of the display tray and reserve the short loin for boneless steaks at 2 cm, or for a tied loin roast at 1.5â2 kg. In most African urban markets, bone-in chops outsell boneless steaks by two to one, so do not over-trim into steaks. Keep the tenderloin whole for a premium steak or butterfly it for a small grill portion.

2. Preparing Shoulder for Stewing and Braising

The shoulder carries more connective tissue than the loin and rewards slow cooking. Separate the picnic (lower, near the leg) from the Boston butt (upper, near the back). The Boston butt is the better braising roast: bone it out, roll and tie at 1.5â2 kg, and sell as a "slow-roast shoulder." The picnic is tougher â cut it into 3 cm stewing cubes, or bone it and cut 2 cm braising steaks for the pot.

For stewing cubes, keep the cube size uniform at 3 cm and remove all silver skin and gristle, because customers judge stewing meat by how clean it looks in the tray. Trim fat to no more than 10% of cube weight. In markets where customers buy by the bowl rather than the kilogram, pre-weigh 500 g and 1 kg packs so the stall moves faster at peak hours.

3. Making Belly Cuts for Bacon and Ribs

The belly runs from the 4th rib to the ham. Square the slab, remove the teat line and any bruised edges, and decide early whether the belly goes to fresh ribs or to bacon. Spare ribs are cut from the leaner, meatier section; cut them into 3â4 rib racks or single ribs for the grill trade. The rest of the belly becomes bacon slab.

For bacon, keep the slab rectangular and 3â4 cm thick. If you cure, use a controlled brine or dry cure and hold at 2â4Â°C; if you sell fresh belly, cut 1 cm strips for the braai and label it as "fresh pork belly," not bacon, to stay within local food labelling rules. Ribs and belly are the cuts that build repeat customers at a butcher shop because they are affordable and cook fast.

4. Trimming and Portioning Ham for Roasting or Curing

The ham is the largest single primal and the one where portioning decisions most affect your margin. Remove the hock at the hock joint and set it aside for soup bones or smoked hock. Skin the ham and trim the fat cover to 8â10 mm. For roasting, bone out the leg, roll and tie at 2â3 kg, or cut fresh ham roasts at 1.5 kg. For curing, keep the leg whole or split into two â a whole dry-cured ham takes weeks and ties up working capital, so many small butchers cure in halves or quarters.

Cut ham steaks at 1.5â2 cm from the leaner cushion. Save the shank and trimmings for mince or sausage, and keep a clean bone pile for stock. If you sell to restaurants, portion ham roasts to their spec rather than to a generic weight â a 50 kg restaurant order at a fixed portion size is more profitable than 50 kg of mixed cuts.

5. Packaging Cuts for Display in a Butcher Shop or Market

Packaging is where African butchers lose margin to spoilage and shrink. Use food-grade trays with absorbent pads for chops, steaks, and ribs so purge does not pool and discolour the meat. Vacuum-pack roasts, bacon slabs, and cured hams â vacuum packing typically extends chilled shelf life from 3â5 days to 2â3 weeks at 0â4Â°C. For open-market stalls without reliable power, sell smaller packs, use ice chests, and turn stock daily.

Label every pack with cut name, weight, pack date, and price. A legible price per kilogram on the pack reduces haggling time and lets one attendant serve more customers per hour. Group the display by cooking method â "for the grill," "for the pot," "for the oven" â rather than by anatomy; customers buy solutions, not primals.

6. Pricing Cuts Based on Local Demand and Competition

Price from your cost, not from your competitor's price alone. Start with the live or dressed carcass cost, add slaughter, chilling, transport, packaging, and labour, then allocate that total cost across cuts by weight. The loin and ham must carry the carcass; the belly and shoulder cover volume. If your chops are priced below the true cost of the loin, no amount of belly sales will save the business.
