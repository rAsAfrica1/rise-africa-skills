1. Site Selection and Preparation

Choosing the right site is the first step to a successful biogas plant. You need a spot that is at least 10 meters from your house and any water well to avoid contamination and fire risk. The site should be sunny most of the day because the digester needs heat to produce gas efficiently. In Africa, a sunny spot with a slight slope helps with drainage. Avoid low-lying areas that flood during rains. Also, keep the plant close to where you will use the gasâlike your kitchenâto reduce piping costs. If you have animals, place the plant near the cattle shed so you can easily load dung into the inlet.

Once you pick the site, clear the area of grass, rocks, and roots. Level the ground using a shovel, hoe, and a spirit level. For a fixed-dome plant, you will dig a pit. Mark a circle on the ground using a rope and peg. The diameter should be at least 1.2 times the planned digester diameter to allow working space. For a 6 cubic meter plant, the pit might be 2.5 meters deep and 2 meters wide. Dig the pit with a pickaxe and shovel. Keep the walls straight and the bottom flat. If the soil is loose, reinforce the walls with wooden planks to prevent collapse.

For a flexible bag digester, you need a flat, compacted area. Remove any sharp stones that could puncture the bag. Build a low wall of bricks or sandbags around the perimeter to protect the bag from wind and animals. Also, dig a shallow trench for the inlet and outlet pipes. Make sure the ground is level so the bag sits evenly.

Gather your tools before you start: shovels, pickaxes, wheelbarrows, measuring tape, spirit level, ropes, pegs, and a water hose. You will also need materials like bricks, cement, sand, gravel, and PVC pipes. Arrange for waterâabout 200 liters per cubic meter of digester volumeâto mix concrete and slurry. Finally, check local regulations. In some countries, you need a permit for underground structures. Talk to your local agriculture extension officer for guidance.

2. Materials for Digester Construction: Concrete, Steel, Geomembrane

The materials you choose determine how long your biogas plant will last. The three main options are concrete, steel, and geomembrane. Each has pros and cons, and the best choice depends on your budget, local availability, and skills.

Concrete is the most common for fixed-dome digesters in Africa. It is strong, durable, and can last over 20 years if built well. You need cement, sand, gravel, and water. A typical mix for digester walls is 1 part cement to 2 parts sand to 3 parts gravel (1:2:3). For a 6 cubic meter plant, you might need 20 bags of cement (50 kg each), 2 cubic meters of sand, and 3 cubic meters of gravel. Concrete must be cured for at least 7 days by keeping it wet. Concrete is heavy and requires skilled labor, but it resists corrosion and is easy to clean.

Steel is used for prefabricated digesters or as reinforcement in concrete. Steel tanks are lighter and faster to install, but they rust unless coated with epoxy or galvanized. In coastal areas, rust is a big problem. You can use steel drums for small-scale digesters. A 200-liter drum can make a simple batch digester. For larger plants, steel plates are welded together. You need a welder and angle grinder. Steel is expensive and requires maintenance every few years.

Geomembrane, also called HDPE or PVC liner, is used for flexible bag digesters. It is cheap, lightweight, and easy to install. A 1 mm thick geomembrane sheet costs about $5 per square meter. For a 6 cubic meter bag, you need about 12 square meters. Geomembrane is UV-resistant and flexible, but it can be punctured by sharp objects or rodents. You must lay it on a smooth surface and protect it with sand or old carpet. It has a lifespan of 5 to 10 years. In Africa, geomembrane is popular because it is affordable and can be repaired with patches.

Other materials include bricks for inlet and outlet chambers, PVC pipes for gas and slurry, and valves. Always buy from reputable suppliers and check for cracks or defects. If you use local materials like clay bricks, make sure they are well-fired. Remember, quality materials mean fewer repairs and more gas.

3. Constructing Fixed-Dome and Flexible Bag Digesters

Building a fixed-dome digester is a step-by-step process. First, dig the pit as described in Lesson 1. The pit has two parts: the digester (where slurry sits) and the gas storage dome (above the slurry). For a 6 cubic meter plant, the digester is usually a cylinder 2 meters deep and 2 meters in diameter. The dome is a hemisphere on top. Start by laying a concrete floor at the bottom of the pit. Mix concrete 1:3:5 (cement:sand:gravel) and pour a 10 cm thick slab. Let it cure for 3 days.

Next, build the walls. Use bricks or concrete blocks, laying them with a 1:4 cement-sand mortar. Leave a gap for the inlet pipe (where you feed dung) and the outlet pipe (where slurry exits). The inlet should be at a 45-degree angle so waste flows in easily. The outlet is a vertical pipe that leads to a slurry pit. After the walls reach ground level, build the dome. You can use a metal or wooden formwork shaped like a dome. Place it on top of the walls and pour concrete 1:2:3 over it. The dome must be airtight. After curing for 7 days, remove the formwork. Plaster the inside of the dome with a waterproof cement mix to prevent gas leaks. Test for leaks by filling with water and checking for bubbles.

For a flexible bag digester, construction is simpler. You need a long plastic bag (geomembrane) typically 0.5 to 1 mm thick. The bag is laid horizontally in a trench or on a flat surface. One end is connected to the inlet pipe, the other to the gas outlet. The bag is filled with slurry (cow dung and water mixed 1:1). As bacteria digest the slurry, gas collects in the top of the bag. The bag inflates like a balloon. You must anchor the bag with ropes or sandbags to keep it from moving. The inlet and outlet are sealed with clamps or glue. A flexible bag plant can be built in one day with basic tools. However, the bag is vulnerable to damage, so you need to protect it from sun and animals. In Africa, many farmers use this type because it is cheap and easy to repair.

4. Installing Piping, Valves, and Fittings

Piping and valves are the veins of your biogas plant. They carry gas to your stove and slurry to your garden. Use PVC pipes for gas lines because they are cheap and easy to work with. For gas, use 1/2 inch or 3/4 inch diameter pipes. For slurry, use 3 or 4 inch pipes to avoid clogging. You also need valves to control gas flow, a pressure gauge, and a water trap to remove moisture.

Start by laying the gas pipe from the digester to the point of use. Keep the pipe slope slightly downward toward a water trap. This trap collects condensation and prevents water from blocking the gas. Install a main gas valve right after the digester. Then run the pipe to your kitchen. Use elbows and tees as needed. Every joint must be sealed with PVC glue or Teflon tape. Leaks are dangerousâthey waste gas and can cause explosions. After installation, test for leaks by pressurizing the system with air and brushing soapy water on joints. Bubbles mean a leak.

For the slurry outlet, connect a pipe from the digester to a slurry pit or directly to your garden. The pipe should have a gentle slope so slurry flows by gravity. Install a valve to control flow. If you use a flexible bag, the inlet and outlet are often built into the bag. Clamp them securely with metal clamps or hose clamps.

Valves: You need a gas valve at the stove and a main valve at the digester. Use brass or plastic ball valves. They last longer than gate valves. Also install a pressure gauge (manometer) to monitor gas pressure. A simple U-tube manometer made from clear plastic tubing and water works well. It shows pressure in centimeters of water. Normal pressure is 10-20 cm. If pressure is too high, you may need a pressure relief valve to prevent damage.

Fittings: Use elbows, tees, reducers, and adapters to connect pipes. For gas lines, use compression fittings or solvent weld. For slurry, use rubber seals or cement. Always use pipe clamps to secure pipes to walls. Keep pipes away from heat and sunlight. In Africa, UV light degrades PVC, so paint exposed pipes or bury them. Finally, label valves for easy operation. A well-installed piping system ensures years of trouble-free gas supply.

5. Safety Measures During Construction

Biogas construction can be dangerous if you ignore safety. The main risks are cave-ins, falls, gas leaks, and infections. Always wear personal protective equipment (PPE): hard hat, gloves, boots, dust mask, and safety glasses. When digging a deep pit, shoring is essential. Use wooden planks or steel sheets to support the walls. Never dig aloneâhave a buddy on the surface with a rope. If the pit is deeper than 1.5 meters, test for toxic gases like hydrogen sulfide (H2S) before entering. Use a canary or a gas detector. H2S smells like rotten eggs and can kill in seconds.

Concrete work involves heavy lifting and chemical burns. Cement is causticâwear gloves and long sleeves. Mix concrete on a hard surface, not on bare ground. When working with steel, use gloves and eye protection. Welding produces fumes and sparks; weld in a well-ventilated area and wear a welding mask. For geomembrane, handle with care to avoid cuts. Use sharp knives safely.

Gas safety is critical. Biogas is flammable and can explode if mixed with air. Never smoke or use open flames near the digester. When testing for leaks, use soapy water, not a match. If you smell gas, ventilate the area and find the leak. Install a pressure relief valve to prevent over-pressurization. Keep a fire extinguisher nearby.

Biological hazards: Slurry contains bacteria. Wear gloves and wash hands with soap after contact. If you get slurry in your eyes, rinse with clean water for 15 minutes. Avoid breathing dust from dried dungâit can cause lung diseases.

Electrical safety: If you use pumps or lights, ensure proper grounding. Use waterproof cables. In Africa, many accidents happen from poor electrical work. Hire a qualified electrician.

Finally, have a first aid kit on site. Include bandages, antiseptic, and eye wash. Train your workers in basic first aid. Work in teams and take breaks to avoid fatigue. Remember, a safe construction site is a productive one.

6. Commissioning a New Biogas Plant

Commissioning is the process of starting up your biogas plant and getting it to produce gas. After construction, you must test for leaks and then fill the digester with slurry. First, do a leak test. For a fixed-dome plant, fill the digester with water up to the top. Mark the water level. Wait 24 hours. If the level drops, there is a leak. Fix it by patching with cement or sealant. For a flexible bag, inflate it with air and check for holes using soapy water. Repair with geomembrane patches.

Next, prepare the inoculum. You need active bacteria to start digestion. The best source is fresh cow dung from a working biogas plant, or from a cattle shed. Mix cow dung with water in a 1:1 ratio to make slurry. For a 6 cubic meter plant, you need about 3 cubic meters of slurry. Pour it into the inlet. Fill the digester to about 80% capacity. Do not fill completelyâleave space for gas.

Then, seal the inlet and outlet. For a fixed-dome, ensure the dome is airtight. For a flexible bag, clamp the inlet and outlet pipes. The bacteria will start working within a few days. You may see bubbles in the water trap. Initially, the gas produced may not burn because it contains too much carbon dioxide. This is normal. Flare off the first few days of gas. After 1-2 weeks, the gas should burn with a blue flame. Test by opening the gas valve and lighting a stove. If the flame is yellow or sputters, the gas quality is poor. Wait a few more days.

Monitor the plant daily. Check for leaks, pressure, and temperature. The ideal temperature is 30-40Â°C. In cold weather, gas production drops. You can insulate the digester with straw or compost. Keep a record of gas production and slurry output. Add fresh dung dailyâabout 10 kg for a 6 cubic meter plant. Remove excess slurry from the outlet.

Commissioning is complete when the gas burns steadily and pressure is stable. This may take 2-4 weeks. Be patient. Once running, your plant will provide clean cooking gas and rich fertilizer for years.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Biogas Plant Construction and Installation

This practical reference consolidates the field-tested essentials for building and commissioning biogas systems in African agribusiness settings. Whether you are a commercial farmer, an agro-processor, an EPC contractor, or a cooperative manager, the decisions you make during construction determine the plant's gas yield, lifespan, and return on investment. The guidance below draws on standards and field manuals used across East, West, and Southern Africa, with a business-first lens: every technical choice is framed around cost, reliability, and payback.

Site Selection and Preparation

Site selection is the single most consequential decision in the construction phase. A poorly sited plant will suffer from water ingress, unstable foundations, or inefficient feedstock logistics that erode margins. Key criteria include:

  • Proximity to feedstock: Keep the digester within 50â100 m of the animal housing or agro-processing waste stream. Every extra metre of slurry transport adds labour cost and reduces net energy gain.
  • Distance from water table: The base of the digester should sit at least 1 m above the highest seasonal water table. In flood-prone areas, raise the entire structure on a plinth.
  • Solar orientation: In cooler highland regions (e.g., Kenya's Rift Valley, Ethiopia's highlands), orient the plant so the dome receives maximum sunlight to maintain mesophilic temperatures (20â40 Â°C).
  • Access for construction and servicing: Allow vehicle access for concrete delivery, pipe replacement, and future desludging.
  • Distance from dwellings: Maintain at least 10 m from kitchens and living areas to manage odour and safety.

Site preparation involves clearing vegetation, levelling, and excavating the pit with a 5â10 cm over-dig to accommodate the foundation slab. In rocky or expansive clay soils, bring in 15â20 cm of compacted hardcore or murram. A simple percolation test â digging a 30 cm hole and filling it with water â tells you whether the subsoil drains adequately or whether you need a waterproof liner.

Constructing Fixed-Dome and Flexible Bag Digesters

Fixed-dome construction proceeds in stages: excavation, foundation slab, digester walls (brick or concrete), dome formwork, and gas-tight plastering. Critical quality gates include:

  1. Foundation: 10 cm compacted hardcore + 10 cm C20 concrete slab.
  2. Walls: 15 cm masonry with continuous vertical rebar at corners.
  3. Dome: cast over a removable earth or steel form; cure 14 days minimum.
  4. Plaster: three coats â scratch, brown, and a final waterproof coat (1:3 cement-sand with waterproofing admixture).
  5. Gas-tightness test: pressurise to 50 mbar and hold for 24 hours; pressure drop must not exceed 5%.

Flexible bag digesters are simpler: a trench is lined with sand, the HDPE or PVC bag is laid, inlet and outlet pipes are connected, and the bag is partially buried to insulate and protect from UV. The gas outlet is fitted with a valve and a condensate trap. Installation can be completed by two trained technicians in a single day.

Installing Piping, Valves, and Fittings

Gas piping must be gas-tight, UV-resistant, and sized to minimise pressure drop. In African installations, 20â25 mm HDPE or galvanised steel is standard for mainlines; 15 mm for branch lines. Key practices:

  • Slope all horizontal runs back toward a condensate trap at 1â2%.
  • Install a main gas valve at the digester outlet and a secondary valve at each appliance.
  • Use compression fittings rather than threaded joints where possible; wrap threaded joints with PTFE tape.
  • Pressure-test the entire line at 50 mbar for 30 minutes before commissioning.

Biogas contains 55â70% methane (flammable) and 30â45% carbon dioxide, with trace hydrogen sulphide (toxic). Never enter a digester or gas storage chamber without forced ventilation, a calibrated multi-gas monitor, a safety harness, and a standby attendant. Hydrogen sulphide at concentrations above 100 ppm causes immediate olfactory fatigue and can be fatal within minutes. No open flames, smoking, or unrated electrical equipment within 5 m of the digester. In 2021, two fatalities were recorded in Uganda from workers entering a partially emptied fixed-dome digester without PPE. Follow national occupational safety regulations and the ILO Safety and Health in Agriculture Convention (No. 184).

Safety Measures During Construction

Beyond confined-space entry, construction-phase hazards include trench collapse, cement burns, and falls from dome formwork. Practical controls:

  • Shore trenches deeper than 1.2 m with timber or steel sheets.
  • Provide nitrile gloves, goggles, and dust masks for cement work.
  • Erect guardrails around the excavation perimeter and at the dome edge.
  • Keep a first-aid kit and a charged mobile phone on site; brief all workers in the local language.
  • Prohibit lone working; maintain a buddy system.

Commissioning a New Biogas Plant

Commissioning is the business handover point. A structured checklist ensures the plant produces saleable gas and digestate from day one:

  1. Leak test: Pressurise the gas system to 50 mbar; hold 24 hours; accept â¤5% drop.
  2. First charge: Fill the digester to 80% with fresh cattle slurry (1:1 manure-to-water) or equivalent. Avoid antibiotics or disinfectants in feedstock.
  3. Seeding: Add 10â20% inoculum from an operating digester if available to accelerate startup.
  4. Flare test: After 5â10 days, attempt ignition at the burner. A stable blue flame confirms methane production.
  5. Calibrate appliances: Adjust air-to-gas ratio on stoves for a blue flame; check biogas lamp mantles.
  6. Training: Train the operator on daily feeding, condensate draining, and monthly pressure checks.
  7. Documentation: Record as-built drawings, material batch numbers, and warranty terms.
  8. Handover: Sign a commissioning certificate with the client; schedule a 30-day follow-up visit.
