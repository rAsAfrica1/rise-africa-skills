1. Customs Brokerage in Africa: The Strategic Role of the Licensed Broker

Customs brokerage is the professional service of preparing and submitting documentation, calculating duties and taxes, and facilitating the clearance of goods through customs authorities on behalf of importers and exporters. In Africa, where cross-border trade is growing rapidly under the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA), the customs broker sits at the center of the supply chain. A licensed broker is not merely a form filler. The broker is a risk manager, a compliance officer, a tariff specialist, and often the importer's most important advisor on cross-border movement of goods. This lesson establishes the strategic foundation of the profession and explains why skilled brokerage is a competitive advantage for African businesses. The first practical concept is the legal mandate. In most African jurisdictions, customs brokerage is a regulated activity. Countries such as Kenya, Nigeria, South Africa, Ghana, Tanzania, and Uganda require brokers to be licensed by the national revenue authority or customs agency. In Kenya, the Kenya Revenue Authority licenses customs agents under the East African Community Customs Management Act. In Nigeria, the Nigeria Customs Service licenses agents and clearing companies. In South Africa, SARS Customs licenses clearing agents under the Customs and Excise Act. The license is not a formality. It carries obligations: accurate declarations, record keeping, payment of duties, and personal liability for false statements. A broker who under-declares value or misclassifies goods can face fines, suspension, or criminal prosecution. The second concept is the broker's value proposition. Importers often think they can save money by clearing goods themselves. In practice, the cost of a mistake is far higher than the broker's fee. A wrong tariff code can trigger penalties, delayed release, demurrage, and storage charges. A missing certificate of origin can forfeit preferential duty rates under AfCFTA or regional economic communities such as COMESA, SADC, or ECOWAS. A broker who knows the local customs regime can reduce landed cost, accelerate clearance, and protect the importer from compliance risk. The third concept is the customs process itself. Although procedures differ by country, the core steps are similar. The importer or broker submits a customs declaration, usually electronically through systems such as Kenya's iCMS, Nigeria's NICIS, Ghana's ICUMS, or South Africa's SARS eFiling. The declaration includes the consignee, consignor, invoice value, freight and insurance, country of origin, tariff code, quantity, and description of goods. Customs then assesses duty and taxes, may inspect the goods, and releases them once payment is made and compliance is verified. The broker coordinates each step with shipping lines, airlines, ports, terminals, and transport providers. The fourth concept is the Africa-specific operating environment. African customs administrations vary widely in digital maturity, staffing, and infrastructure. Some ports, such as Mombasa, Durban, Lagos, Tema, and Djibouti, handle high volumes and have relatively advanced electronic systems. Others rely on manual processes and face congestion, power outages, and connectivity problems. A successful broker must be adaptable. You may need to file online in the morning and physically follow up at the port in the afternoon. You must build relationships with customs officers, terminal operators, shipping agents, and freight forwarders. Relationships are not corruption; they are professional networks that help you resolve legitimate problems quickly. The fifth concept is ethics and professional standards. Customs brokerage is a trust business. Importers rely on you to tell them the truth about duties, delays, and risks. If you collude in undervaluation, smuggling, or false origin claims, you expose your client and yourself to severe consequences. African revenue authorities are increasingly using data analytics, scanner technology, and post-clearance audits to detect fraud. A broker with a clean record can obtain faster release and preferential treatment. A broker with a bad record will face inspections on every shipment. The sixth concept is the business model. Brokers earn fees per declaration, per container, or on a retainer basis. Some also earn commissions from shipping lines or transport providers, but this creates conflicts of interest and should be disclosed. The most sustainable model is a transparent service fee plus value-added services such as tariff consulting, compliance audits, warehousing, and last-mile delivery. As African trade expands, brokers who specialize in specific sectors, such as agriculture, pharmaceuticals, machinery, or e-commerce, can command higher fees and build stronger client relationships. The seventh concept is technology. Customs brokerage is becoming digital. Electronic single windows, risk management systems, and blockchain-based traceability are spreading across Africa. Brokers must learn to use these tools. They must also protect client data. A broker who can integrate with a client's ERP system and provide real-time shipment visibility will win business over a broker who still relies on paper and phone calls. The eighth concept is the AfCFTA opportunity. The AfCFTA aims to create a single market for goods and services across Africa. It will reduce tariffs on most goods and simplify rules of origin. For brokers, this means new demand for origin advice, cumulation strategies, and cross-border documentation. It also means more competition from brokers in other African countries. The brokers who thrive will be those who understand both local customs procedures and the continental trade rules. In summary, customs brokerage in Africa is a strategic profession. It requires legal knowledge, operational skill, ethical discipline, and business acumen. A licensed broker who masters these elements becomes indispensable to importers and exporters. The remaining lessons in this module will build on this foundation with practical guidance on classification, valuation, documentation, and clearance management.

2. Tariff Classification and Valuation: The Core Technical Skills

Tariff classification and customs valuation are the two most important technical skills in customs brokerage. Classification determines the tariff code, which determines the duty rate, the need for permits, and eligibility for preferential treatment. Valuation determines the customs value, which is the base on which duties and taxes are calculated. Errors in either area can cost your client money, cause delays, and expose you to penalties. This lesson provides practical, Africa-focused guidance on both disciplines. Tariff classification begins with the Harmonized System (HS), a global nomenclature maintained by the World Customs Organization. Most African countries use the HS at the six-digit level and add national subdivisions at the eight, ten, or twelve-digit level. For example, Kenya uses the East African Community Common External Tariff, which extends the HS to ten digits. Nigeria uses the ECOWAS Common External Tariff. South Africa uses the Southern African Customs Union tariff book. The first step in classification is to read the product description carefully. You need to know what the item is, what it is made of, how it functions, and how it is presented. A plastic chair and a metal chair may fall under different headings. A smartphone and a tablet may also differ. The second step is to consult the General Interpretative Rules (GIR). These six rules govern classification when the tariff schedule is ambiguous. GIR 1 says classification is determined by the terms of the headings and any relative section or chapter notes. GIR 2 covers incomplete or unassembled articles. GIR 3 covers goods that appear to fall under multiple headings, with the most specific description prevailing. GIR 4 covers goods that cannot be classified otherwise, using the heading for goods most akin. GIR 5 covers cases and packing materials. GIR 6 applies the rules at the subheading level. The third step is to use the explanatory notes and the customs rulings database. The World Customs Organization publishes explanatory notes that interpret the HS. Many African revenue authorities also publish national rulings. If you are unsure, you can request a binding ruling from customs before importing. This is a smart risk management step for high-value or recurring shipments. The fourth step is to document your reasoning. If customs challenges your classification, you need to show why you chose that code. Keep product literature, technical specifications, photographs, and laboratory reports where relevant. The fifth step is to monitor changes. The HS is updated every five years, and national tariffs change more frequently. A code that was correct last year may be wrong this year. Valuation is equally critical. The WTO Customs Valuation Agreement, which most African countries follow, sets out six methods. Method 1 is transaction value, the price actually paid or payable for the goods. This is the preferred method. It includes the cost of goods, plus commissions and brokerage (except buying commissions), plus cost of containers, plus packing, plus assists (materials, tools, dies provided by the buyer), plus royalties and license fees related to the goods, plus proceeds of resale accruing to the seller, plus transport, insurance, and related costs up to the port of importation if not already included. Method 2 is transaction value of identical goods. Method 3 is transaction value of similar goods. Method 4 is deductive value, based on resale price in the importing country minus costs. Method 5 is computed value, based on production cost plus profit. Method 6 is fallback, using reasonable means consistent with the agreement. In practice, most African customs administrations apply Method 1. The most common valuation disputes involve related-party transactions, low-value invoicing, and under-declaration. If the buyer and seller are related, customs may examine whether the relationship influenced the price. If the invoice value is unusually low compared to identical goods, customs may reject it and use Method 2 or 3. Brokers must advise clients to keep commercial invoices, contracts, purchase orders, payment records, and shipping documents. These documents support the declared value. A practical Africa example: an importer in Ghana buys used clothing from Europe. The invoice shows a low price per bale. Customs may suspect undervaluation and use a reference price or database value. The broker should be ready to provide evidence of the actual transaction, such as bank transfers, supplier contracts, and market analysis. Another example: a Nigerian manufacturer imports machinery from China. The invoice includes the machine price but not the installation and training fees. Customs may add those fees to the customs value if they are part of the sale. The broker should review the contract carefully and declare all relevant costs. Duties and taxes vary by country. In Kenya, imports are subject to import duty, value added tax (VAT), excise duty on certain goods, and the Railway Development Levy. In Nigeria, there is import duty, VAT, surcharge, and levies on specific goods. In South Africa, there is customs duty, VAT, and ad valorem duties on certain items. The broker must calculate all applicable charges accurately. Under AfCFTA, goods that meet the rules of origin can enter with reduced or zero duty. The broker must verify the origin criteria and obtain a valid certificate of origin. Without it, the importer pays the full rate. This is a major opportunity for brokers who understand the rules. To build classification and valuation expertise, follow these practical steps. First, study the HS and your national tariff book regularly. Second, attend training offered by the revenue authority, the World Customs Organization, or professional bodies. Third, build a personal library of rulings and explanatory notes. Fourth, use classification software where available. Fifth, create a checklist for every declaration that covers description, material, function, origin, value, and supporting documents. Sixth, review your declarations after clearance to identify errors and improve. Seventh, never guess. If you are unsure, ask customs for a ruling or consult a specialist. The cost of a wrong code or value is always higher than the cost of getting it right. By mastering classification and valuation, you protect your clients, your license, and your reputation.

3. Documentation, Declarations, and Digital Systems in African Customs

Documentation is the backbone of customs brokerage. A clearance can be delayed or denied because of a missing signature, an inconsistent name, or an expired certificate. In Africa, where customs systems are increasingly digital but still paper-intensive in many places, the broker must manage documents with precision. This lesson covers the key documents, the declaration process, and the digital systems used across the continent. The core documents for an import declaration typically include the commercial invoice, the packing list, the bill of lading or airway bill, the certificate of origin, the import declaration form, and any permits or licenses required for restricted goods. The commercial invoice must show the seller and buyer, the description of goods, the quantity, the unit price, the total value, the currency, the terms of sale (Incoterms), and the country of origin. The packing list must show how the goods are packed, the number of packages, weights, and dimensions. The bill of lading is the transport contract and the document of title. The certificate of origin proves where the goods were produced and is essential for preferential tariff treatment. The import declaration form is the official customs document that summarizes the shipment and calculates duties. Permits may be required for pharmaceuticals, food, chemicals, telecommunications equipment, and other regulated items. In addition to these, some countries require a tax identification number, a business registration certificate, an insurance certificate, and a bank payment confirmation. The broker should prepare a document checklist for each shipment and verify every item before submission. Common errors include mismatched names, wrong weights, missing signatures, expired certificates, and incorrect currency codes. These errors cause delays, inspections, and penalties. The declaration process begins with data entry. In most African countries, the broker enters the declaration into an electronic system. Kenya uses the Integrated Customs Management System (iCMS). Nigeria uses the Nigeria Integrated Customs Information System (NICIS). Ghana uses the Integrated Customs Management System (ICUMS). Tanzania uses the Tanzania Customs Integrated System (TANCIS). Uganda uses the Uganda Electronic Single Window. South Africa uses SARS eFiling and the Customs and Excise Act system. These systems validate the data, calculate duties, and route the declaration for risk assessment. After submission, customs may select the shipment for physical inspection, scanning, or documentary review. The broker must coordinate with the terminal, the shipping line, and the transporter to present the goods for inspection. If the inspection is clean, customs releases the goods. If there are issues, the broker must respond quickly with additional documents or explanations. Payment of duties and taxes is usually required before release. The broker must ensure the client has funds available and that payment is made through the correct channel. Some countries allow deferred payment for approved importers. The broker should know the options and advise the client accordingly. Digital systems have transformed customs brokerage in Africa, but they also create new challenges. System downtime, network failures, and user errors can delay clearance. The broker must be proficient in the system, keep login credentials secure, and have a backup plan for manual processing. Data accuracy is critical because electronic systems automatically flag inconsistencies. The broker should also use the system's reporting features to track shipments, monitor duty payments, and identify bottlenecks. The single window concept is spreading across Africa. A single window allows traders to submit documents to multiple government agencies through one portal. This reduces duplication and speeds up clearance. Countries such as Ghana, Kenya, Rwanda, and Senegal have implemented single windows. The broker should learn how to use these platforms and integrate them into the clearance workflow. Another digital trend is the use of scanners and non-intrusive inspection. Scanners allow customs to see inside containers without opening them. If a scanner image is unclear or suspicious, customs may still require a physical inspection. The broker should ensure that packing is orderly and that the cargo matches the declaration. Blockchain and traceability platforms are also emerging, especially for agricultural exports and minerals. These platforms can provide tamper-proof records of origin and movement. Brokers who understand them can offer new services to clients. For exports, the documentation requirements differ. The broker may need an export declaration, a certificate of origin, a phytosanitary certificate for plants, a health certificate for animals, and a quality certificate for certain products. African exports often face strict standards in destination markets, such as the European Union's food safety rules. The broker should work with the exporter to ensure compliance before shipment. A practical example: a Kenyan exporter of fresh flowers to the Netherlands needs a phytosanitary certificate, a certificate of origin, and an airway bill. The broker must coordinate with the Kenya Plant Health Inspectorate Service and the airline. Any delay can cause the flowers to perish. Another example: a Nigerian exporter of cocoa beans needs a quality certificate from the Nigerian Export Promotion Council and a certificate of origin. The broker must ensure the documents are ready before the vessel sails. Record keeping is a legal requirement. Most African customs laws require brokers and importers to keep records for a minimum period, often five years. Records include declarations, invoices, bills of lading, payment receipts, and correspondence. The broker should maintain both electronic and physical copies. Good record keeping helps during post-clearance audits and supports dispute resolution. To succeed in documentation and digital systems, follow these practices. First, create standard operating procedures for each document type. Second, use checklists and double-check every entry. Third, train your team on the electronic systems and keep them updated on changes. Fourth, build relationships with customs help desks and terminal staff. Fifth, monitor system announcements for downtime and new requirements. Sixth, back up all data securely. Seventh, review every clearance after completion to identify lessons learned. Documentation is not glamorous, but it is where brokers win or lose. A broker who submits clean, complete, and accurate declarations will earn the trust of clients and the respect of customs. A broker who cuts corners will face delays, penalties, and lost business. In the African context, where supply chains are complex and infrastructure is uneven, documentation discipline is a competitive advantage.

4. Managing Clearance Operations: Ports, Transport, and Stakeholders

Clearance operations are the physical and logistical side of customs brokerage. Even with perfect documentation, goods will not move unless the broker coordinates with ports, terminals, shipping lines, transporters, and warehouses. In Africa, this coordination is often the hardest part of the job. Ports are congested, infrastructure is strained, and multiple agencies are involved. This lesson provides practical guidance on managing clearance operations effectively. The first step is pre-arrival planning. The broker should receive the shipping documents before the vessel or aircraft arrives. This allows time to review the documents, identify missing items, and prepare the declaration. Pre-arrival clearance is common in countries such as Kenya, Ghana, and Nigeria, where customs allows submission before arrival. The broker should also confirm the expected arrival date, the terminal, and the storage arrangements. The second step is arrival and manifest reconciliation. When the vessel arrives, the shipping line submits a manifest to customs. The broker must ensure that the shipment appears on the manifest and that the details match the bill of lading. Discrepancies can cause delays. The broker should also check for any holds, such as unpaid freight, demurrage, or customs flags. The third step is declaration submission and assessment. The broker submits the declaration through the electronic system. Customs assesses the duty and taxes and may select the shipment for inspection. The broker should monitor the system for the assessment and respond quickly to any queries. The fourth step is inspection coordination. If customs selects the shipment for inspection, the broker must arrange for the container or cargo to be moved to the inspection area. This requires coordination with the terminal, the transporter, and the customs officer. The broker should be present or represented at the inspection to answer questions and provide documents. The fifth step is duty payment and release. Once the assessment is final, the broker arranges payment. After payment, customs issues a release order. The broker then presents the release order to the terminal to obtain the goods. The sixth step is transport and delivery. The broker arranges transport from the port to the importer's premises or warehouse. This may involve trucks, rail, or inland container depots. The broker should ensure the transporter is licensed, insured, and reliable. The seventh step is post-clearance follow-up. The broker should confirm delivery, collect signed proof of delivery, and archive all documents. If there are any disputes or audits, the records will be essential. Stakeholder management is central to clearance operations. The key stakeholders include customs officers, terminal operators, shipping lines, freight forwarders, transporters, warehouse operators, and government agencies such as standards bureaus, health authorities, and agriculture departments. Each stakeholder has its own priorities and procedures. The broker must communicate clearly, follow up persistently, and resolve conflicts professionally. In many African ports, personal relationships matter. A broker who is known and respected can get faster responses and better information. This does not mean paying bribes. It means building a reputation for honesty, competence, and reliability. Corruption is a real challenge in some African customs environments. The broker must resist pressure to pay unofficial fees. Instead, the broker should use official channels, escalate issues to supervisors, and document everything. A clean reputation is a long-term asset. Port congestion is a major operational risk. In ports such as Lagos, Mombasa, Durban, and Dar es Salaam, congestion can cause weeks of delay. The broker should monitor port conditions, book transport early, and advise clients on realistic timelines. The broker can also use inland container depots and bonded warehouses to reduce port dwell time. Demurrage and storage charges are costly. The broker should track free days and ensure that containers are moved before charges accrue. If delays are caused by customs or the terminal, the broker should document the cause and seek waivers where possible. Security is another concern. Cargo theft and pilferage occur in some ports and along transport corridors. The broker should advise clients on insurance, use reputable transporters, and consider tracking devices for high-value goods. The broker should also comply with security regulations, such as the International Ship and Port Facility Security Code. Technology can improve clearance operations. Port community systems, vehicle tracking, and electronic gate passes are available in some African ports. The broker should adopt these tools where possible. A broker who can provide real-time updates to clients will stand out. A practical example: a broker in Mombasa handles a container of electronics from China. The vessel arrives on Monday. The broker has already submitted the declaration and paid the duty. On Tuesday, customs selects the container for scanning. The broker coordinates with the terminal to move the container to the scanner. The scan is clean, and customs releases the container on Wednesday. The broker arranges a truck to deliver the container to Nairobi. The truck arrives on Thursday. The broker confirms delivery and sends the proof of delivery to the client. This is a smooth operation, but it requires planning, coordination, and follow-up. Another example: a broker in Lagos handles a shipment of machinery. The vessel arrives, but the terminal is congested. The container is not available for inspection for five days. The broker communicates with the client, explains the delay, and negotiates with the terminal to prioritize the container. The broker also applies for a waiver of demurrage because the delay was caused by the terminal. The container is eventually released, and the client appreciates the broker's proactive communication. To manage clearance operations effectively, the broker should develop standard operating procedures, maintain a contact list of stakeholders, use a shipment tracking system, and train staff on port procedures. The broker should also conduct post-clearance reviews to identify delays and improve processes. Clearance operations are where the broker's operational skills are tested. A broker who can navigate African ports, manage stakeholders, and solve problems will build a strong reputation and a profitable business. A broker who is disorganized or reactive will lose clients to competitors. The key is preparation, communication, and persistence.

5. Compliance, Risk Management, and Building a Customs Brokerage Business

Compliance and risk management are essential for a sustainable customs brokerage business. In Africa, customs authorities are increasing enforcement, using data analytics, and conducting post-clearance audits. A broker who ignores compliance will eventually face penalties, suspension, or loss of license. This lesson covers the compliance framework, risk management strategies, and practical steps for building and growing a brokerage business. The compliance framework includes laws, regulations, and procedures. The broker must know the customs act of each country where they operate, the tariff schedule, the valuation rules, the origin rules, and the licensing requirements. The broker must also know the laws of other agencies, such as standards, health, agriculture, and security. Compliance is not a one-time task. It requires continuous learning and adaptation. Risk management begins with identifying risks. The main risks in customs brokerage include classification errors, valuation errors, origin fraud, missing documents, late payment, inspection delays, demurrage, cargo theft, and legal liability. The broker should assess each risk, decide how to mitigate it, and monitor it. For classification risk, the broker should use binding rulings, keep product information, and train staff. For valuation risk, the broker should verify invoices, review related-party transactions, and keep payment records. For origin risk, the broker should verify certificates of origin and understand the rules of origin. For document risk, the broker should use checklists and double-check entries. For payment risk, the broker should confirm funds before submission and use secure payment channels. For inspection risk, the broker should prepare cargo and documents in advance. For demurrage risk, the broker should track free days and move containers quickly. For theft risk, the broker should advise clients on insurance and use secure transport. For legal risk, the broker should maintain professional indemnity insurance and follow ethical standards. Post-clearance audits are a growing trend in Africa. Customs authorities select declarations for audit after release. The broker must be able to produce all supporting documents and explain the declaration. If errors are found, the broker may need to pay additional duties and penalties. Good record keeping is the best defense. The broker should keep records for at least five years, organized by client and shipment. Compliance also includes anti-money laundering and counter-terrorism financing rules. Brokers handle payments and trade documents, so they must know their clients and report suspicious transactions. Many African countries have financial intelligence units that monitor trade-based money laundering. The broker should implement a know-your-customer process and keep records of client identities and beneficial owners. Building a customs brokerage business requires more than technical skills. It requires business planning, marketing, pricing, staffing, and financial management. The first step is to define your target market. Do you serve small importers, large manufacturers, or specific sectors such as agriculture, pharmaceuticals, or e-commerce? A niche focus can help you build expertise and reputation. The second step is to set up the legal structure. You need a registered business, a tax identification number, and a customs broker license. You may also need professional memberships, such as a customs brokers association. The third step is to develop your service offerings. Core services include declaration preparation, duty calculation, clearance coordination, and document management. Value-added services include tariff consulting, origin advice, compliance audits, warehousing, transport, and trade finance facilitation. The fourth step is to price your services. Pricing can be per declaration, per container, or on a retainer. You should cover your costs, including staff, software, office, transport, and insurance, and earn a reasonable profit. Be transparent with clients about fees and avoid hidden charges. The fifth step is to build a team. You need licensed brokers, declaration officers, documentation clerks, and operations coordinators. Invest in training and retain talent. The sixth step is to market your business. Use referrals, industry associations, trade fairs, and digital channels. Build a professional website and maintain an active presence on LinkedIn and other platforms. Share your expertise through articles and seminars. The seventh step is to manage finances. Customs brokerage involves cash flow for duty payments. You may need a credit line or client deposits. Keep accurate books and comply with tax laws. The eighth step is to manage client relationships. Communicate proactively, provide regular updates, and solve problems quickly. A satisfied client will refer others. The ninth step is to manage risk. Use contracts, insurance, and clear terms of service. Limit your liability where possible and disclose risks to clients. The tenth step is to plan for growth. Consider expanding to new countries, new sectors, or new services. The AfCFTA creates opportunities for brokers who can operate across borders. You may need to partner with brokers in other countries or open branches. A practical example: a broker in Ghana starts with small importers of consumer goods. She builds a reputation for fast clearance and honest advice. She then specializes in pharmaceuticals, which require permits and cold chain handling. She invests in training and equipment. She partners with a broker in Nigeria to handle cross-border shipments. She uses a cloud-based system to track shipments and communicate with clients. Her business grows from one person to ten staff in five years. Another example: a broker in South Africa focuses on automotive parts. He learns the tariff codes, the rules of origin, and the logistics of the automotive supply chain. He offers just-in-time clearance and warehousing. He becomes the preferred broker for several manufacturers. He also provides compliance training to his clients. His business is stable and profitable. To succeed in the long term, the broker must be ethical, professional, and customer-focused. Compliance is not a burden; it is a competitive advantage. A broker who is known for integrity will attract better clients and earn the trust of customs. Risk management is not about avoiding all risks; it is about understanding and controlling them. Building a business takes time, but with a clear strategy and disciplined execution, a customs brokerage can thrive in Africa's growing trade environment. The key takeaways from this module are: understand the strategic role of the broker, master classification and valuation, manage documentation and digital systems, coordinate clearance operations, and build a compliant and profitable business. These lessons provide the foundation. Continuous learning and practical experience will turn them into expertise.

<p style="margin:0;font-family:Arial,sans-serif;font-size:14px;color:#8b1a12"><strong>SAFETY WARNING Agricultural and veterinary consignments:</strong> Never clear live animals, veterinary vaccines, crop protection chemicals, or food consignments without verifying the import permit, the sanitary/phytosanitary (SPS) certificate, and the cold-chain or storage conditions required by the national competent authority. Mis-declaring a pesticide or veterinary product can cause human and animal poisoning, residue violations that close export markets, and criminal liability under national plant and animal health laws. If a certificate is missing or expired, stop the clearance and notify the importer and authority in writing. Do not release goods on a "pending" undertaking for these categories.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Classify first, quote second.</strong> Confirm the 6-digit HS line and the national 8/10-digit tariff code before calculating duty. Use the WCO GRI sequence and document your reasoning.</li>
<li><strong>Build the CIF value correctly.</strong> Add freight, insurance, assists, and dutiable royalties; exclude post-import transport and buying commissions.</li>
<li><strong>Check the permit matrix.</strong> Match the HS code to the permit, standard, or PVoC certificate required by the destination authority and the relevant regional body.</li>
<li><strong>Verify origin and preference.</strong> Confirm the rules of origin and the certificate (e.g., AfCFTA, COMESA, ECOWAS, SADC) before claiming a preferential rate.</li>
<li><strong>Reconcile documents.</strong> Invoice, packing list, bill of lading, and declaration must agree on quantity, description, and value. Discrepancies trigger inspection.</li>
<li><strong>File pre-arrival.</strong> Lodge the declaration and pay estimated duty in advance to qualify for fast-track release and avoid demurrage.</li>
<li><strong>Keep the audit file.</strong> Retain classification rationale, valuation workings, permits, and correspondence for the statutory period (commonly 5 years).</li>
<li><strong>Escalate, don't guess.</strong> For ambiguous classification or valuation, request a binding ruling or written advance decision from the revenue authority.</li>
</ul>
