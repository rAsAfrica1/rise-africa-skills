Lessons

1. India as the Pharmacy of the World

India supplies over 20% of the world's generic medicines by volume, making it the largest provider of generic drugs globally. For African importers, this means access to affordable, high-quality pharmaceuticals that are often 80-90% cheaper than branded equivalents. Key export hubs include Mumbai, Ahmedabad, Hyderabad, and Chennai. The Indian pharmaceutical market is valued at over $50 billion, with exports exceeding $25 billion annually. Major destinations include Nigeria, Kenya, South Africa, Tanzania, and Uganda.

To start importing, you need to identify reliable suppliers. Begin by searching online directories like IndiaMART, TradeIndia, and ExportersIndia. Look for manufacturers with WHO-GMP certification, which ensures good manufacturing practices. For example, a box of 100 tablets of paracetamol (500mg) from a WHO-GMP Indian manufacturer might cost $1.50-$2.50 FOB, while the same in a local African pharmacy sells for $5-$10. Your profit margin can be 30-50% after shipping and duties.

Shipping is typically by air or sea. Air freight from Mumbai to Lagos takes 3-5 days and costs $4-$8 per kg. Sea freight takes 25-35 days and costs $1.50-$3 per kg. For smaller orders (under 500kg), air is more practical. You'll need an import license from your country's drug regulatory authority, such as NAFDAC in Nigeria or PPB in Kenya. Always request a proforma invoice, and verify the supplier's credentials through the Indian embassy or trade council.

Payment is usually via Letter of Credit (LC) or Telegraphic Transfer (TT). For first orders, start small: $2,000-$5,000. Use a freight forwarder experienced in pharmaceuticals. Ensure the supplier provides a Certificate of Pharmaceutical Product (COPP) and a WHO-GMP certificate. With proper planning, you can build a profitable business supplying essential medicines to hospitals, pharmacies, and NGOs across Africa.

2. WHO-GMP Certified Manufacturers

WHO-GMP (World Health Organization Good Manufacturing Practices) certification is your gold standard for selecting pharmaceutical suppliers. It means the manufacturer meets strict quality, safety, and efficacy standards. In India, over 1,500 companies hold WHO-GMP certificates, but only about 200 are truly export-ready for regulated markets. For African importers, WHO-GMP ensures the product is safe for human consumption and accepted by your local drug regulator.

How to verify a WHO-GMP certificate? Ask the supplier for a copy. Check the certificate number on the WHO website or the Indian Central Drugs Standard Control Organization (CDSCO) portal. The certificate should be valid for 3 years and list the manufacturing site. Be wary of suppliers who hesitate to share certificates or offer only ISO 9001 (which is not pharmaceutical-specific). A genuine WHO-GMP manufacturer will also have a drug license and a manufacturing license from a state drug controller.

Real examples: Sun Pharma, Cipla, Dr. Reddy's, Aurobindo Pharma, and Lupin are top-tier WHO-GMP companies. But they often have minimum order quantities (MOQs) of $10,000-$50,000. For smaller importers, mid-sized companies like Ajanta Pharma, Alkem Laboratories, or Intas Pharmaceuticals may be more flexible. You can find them on IndiaMART by filtering for 'WHO-GMP certified'. Expect to pay $0.02-$0.10 per tablet for common antibiotics like amoxicillin, and $0.05-$0.20 for antihypertensives like amlodipine.

When negotiating, ask for a sample before bulk order. Test the sample through a local lab for dissolution and potency. A typical lab test costs $50-$150. Always sign a contract specifying quality standards, packaging, and delivery terms. Remember, WHO-GMP is not just a paperit's your assurance that the medicine will work and won't harm patients. This protects your business and your reputation.

3. Using IndiaMART and Pharmaceutical B2B Platforms

IndiaMART is India's largest B2B marketplace with over 7 million suppliers. For pharmaceutical sourcing, it's a powerful tool if used correctly. Start by creating a free buyer account. Search for 'pharmaceutical tablets', 'WHO-GMP medicines', or specific products like 'paracetamol 500mg'. Use filters: 'WHO-GMP certified', 'Manufacturer', and 'Exporters'. You'll get hundreds of results. Don't contact allshortlist 10-15 with complete profiles, including photos of certificates and factory.

When you send an inquiry, be specific: state your country, required quantity, and target price. Example: 'Need 10,000 boxes of amoxicillin 500mg, 10x10 blisters, WHO-GMP, CIF Lagos. Please send FOB price and COPP.' Suppliers typically respond within 24-48 hours. Expect 5-10 quotes. Compare prices, MOQs, and payment terms. A typical quote might be $0.80 per box FOB Mumbai. Use this to negotiate. Be cautious of suppliers who ask for advance payment via Western Union or personal bank accountsthese are red flags.

Other platforms: TradeIndia, ExportersIndia, and Pharmexcil (Pharmaceutical Export Promotion Council of India). Pharmexcil is a government body that lists verified exporters. You can also attend trade shows like CPhI India or iPHEX to meet suppliers face-to-face. For African importers, the Indian embassy in your country often has a trade section that can verify suppliers. Always ask for a video call to see the factory and warehouse. This builds trust and reduces fraud risk.

After selecting a supplier, request a proforma invoice. It should include product details, quantity, unit price, total value, payment terms, and delivery time. Use this to apply for your import permit. Start with a small trial order to test quality and logistics. Once satisfied, scale up. IndiaMART is free, but paid memberships ($200-$500/year) give better visibility and response rates. For a serious importer, it's worth the investment.

4. Understanding Certificate of Pharmaceutical Product

The Certificate of Pharmaceutical Product (COPP) is a document issued by the drug regulatory authority of the exporting country. For Indian exports, it's issued by the Central Drugs Standard Control Organization (CDSCO) or state drug controllers. The COPP confirms that the product is approved for sale in India and meets quality standards. It is required by many African drug regulators, such as NAFDAC (Nigeria), PPB (Kenya), and SAHPRA (South Africa), for product registration.

The COPP contains key information: product name, dosage form, strength, manufacturer's name and address, and the issuing authority. It also states whether the product is manufactured under WHO-GMP conditions. The certificate is typically valid for 2 years. Without a COPP, your shipment may be seized at customs or rejected by the port health authority. So always request a COPP from your supplier before shipping. A genuine COPP will have a unique certificate number that you can verify with the issuing authority.

How to get a COPP? The manufacturer applies to CDSCO with details of the product and manufacturing site. It takes 2-4 weeks and costs around $100-$300 per product. Some suppliers include COPP in the price; others charge extra. When you receive the COPP, check that the product name and strength match your order exactly. Also ensure the manufacturing site is the same as on the WHO-GMP certificate. Discrepancies can cause delays.

For African importers, the COPP is often part of the product registration dossier. You'll need to submit it to your local regulator along with samples, labels, and a free sale certificate. Work with a local agent who knows the registration process. In Nigeria, NAFDAC requires a COPP for each product. In Kenya, PPB requires it for registration. The process can take 3-6 months. Start early. Keep copies of all COPPsthey are valuable for future orders. If a supplier cannot provide a COPP, do not buy from them. It's a sign the product may not be approved or safe.

5. The Asian Common Technical Dossier

The Asian Common Technical Dossier (ACTD) is a standardized format for submitting pharmaceutical product information to regulatory authorities in ASEAN countries and beyond. For African importers, understanding ACTD helps you evaluate suppliers and navigate your own registration process. The ACTD has four parts: Part I (Administrative and Product Information), Part II (Quality), Part III (Safety), and Part IV (Efficacy). It's like a comprehensive file that proves a medicine is safe, effective, and of high quality.

Part I includes the COPP, manufacturing license, and product labels. Part II covers quality data: composition, manufacturing process, specifications, and stability studies. Part III includes non-clinical safety data, and Part IV has clinical trial results. For generic medicines, Parts III and IV are often replaced by bioequivalence studies. A full ACTD can be 500-1000 pages. Your supplier should provide the ACTD in English. If they can't, they may not be export-ready.

Why does ACTD matter to you? Many African regulators, such as NAFDAC and PPB, accept or require ACTD format for product registration. It streamlines the review process. When you request a COPP, also ask for the ACTD. A supplier with a complete ACTD is serious about exports. Use it to verify claims: for example, the stability data should show the product is stable for at least 24 months under tropical conditions. The bioequivalence study should show the generic is equivalent to the original brand.

How to use ACTD practically? When you shortlist a supplier, ask for the ACTD table of contents. If they hesitate, move on. For your registration, you'll submit the ACTD to your local authority. You may need to hire a consultant to compile it. In Nigeria, a consultant might charge $1,000-$3,000 per product. In Kenya, $800-$2,000. It's an investment, but it opens doors to selling in regulated markets. Keep the ACTD updatedit's valid for 5 years. Always cross-check the ACTD with the COPP and WHO-GMP certificate for consistency.

6. What You Must Never Buy or Sell

In the pharmaceutical import business, some products are illegal, dangerous, or unethical. Never buy or sell: narcotics, psychotropic substances, or controlled drugs without a special license. These include tramadol, codeine-based syrups, and benzodiazepines like diazepam. In many African countries, tramadol abuse is a crisis, and importing it without authorization can lead to arrest and imprisonment. Even if a supplier offers a high profit margin, walk away.

Counterfeit or substandard medicines are another absolute no. If the price is too good to be true, it probably is. Fake antimalarials, antibiotics, and antiretrovirals kill people. Always verify the supplier's WHO-GMP and COPP. Never buy from street vendors or unlicensed online sellers. Also avoid expired drugs, even if offered at a discount. Expired medicines can be toxic or ineffective. Check expiry dates on every box. As a rule, ensure at least 2 years of shelf life remaining at the time of shipment.

Unapproved or banned drugs: Some drugs are banned in India but still manufactured for export. For example, ranitidine was recalled in 2020 due to NDMA contamination. Always check the FDA or WHO recall lists. Also avoid drugs that are not approved in your own country. Selling unapproved drugs can lead to confiscation and legal action. You must have a valid import license and product registration for every medicine you sell.

Finally, never sell samples or freebies as commercial stock. And do not repackage or relabel medicines without authorization. This is illegal and unsafe. Your reputation is your biggest asset. Build a business based on quality, transparency, and compliance. Work only with WHO-GMP suppliers, demand COPP and ACTD, and keep proper records. If you suspect a supplier is offering something illegal, report them to the Indian embassy or your local drug regulator. Protecting patients is more important than quick profits.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Sourcing Human Pharmaceuticals from India A Working Toolkit for African Importers

This Practical Reference consolidates the operational knowledge you need before you place a single purchase order for human pharmaceuticals from India. It is written for African importers, distributors, pharmacy chains, hospital procurement officers, and NGO buyers who need to move from I want to import to I have a compliant, bankable shipment. Every section below is designed to be used at your desk: print the tables, keep the checklist visible, and treat the safety box as non-negotiable.

India supplies a large share of the generic medicines consumed across Africa, and the reason is not only price. It is the depth of its regulatory and manufacturing ecosystem. But that depth also means the market contains both world-class WHO-GMP plants and unregulated traders. Your job as an importer is to separate them before money leaves your account.

RED SAFETY WARNING Never Buy or Sell These

  • Narcotics and controlled substances (e.g., morphine, pethidine, tramadol without a valid controlled-substance licence).
  • Substandard or falsified medicines including products with no batch number, no expiry date, or unverifiable CoA.
  • Unregistered products in your destination country, even if they are WHO-GMP certified in India.
  • Sample or gift shipments that bypass customs and regulatory registration this is trafficking, not trade.
  • Products from manufacturers with suspended or withdrawn WHO-GMP status always re-check the CDSCO and WHO lists before payment.
  • Cold-chain products (insulin, vaccines, biologics) without validated cold-chain documentation and insurance.

Consequence: seizure, blacklisting, criminal liability, and permanent loss of your import licence. When in doubt, do not ship.

Quick Reference Checklist (68 Points)

  1. Verify the manufacturer on the CDSCO website and the WHO prequalification list. Match the exact plant address on the WHO-GMP certificate.
  2. Request the full dossier: CoPP, WHO-GMP, Site Master File, ACTD modules, and batch-specific CoA before paying any deposit.
  3. Confirm your destination regulators requirements (NAFDAC, PPB, TMDA, NDA, FDA Ghana) and budget 312 months for registration.
  4. Use only traceable payment channels bank transfer against a proforma invoice. Avoid cash, crypto, or personal accounts.
  5. Inspect before shipment: batch records, packaging, labelling, and a third-party pre-shipment QC report where possible.
  6. Check Incoterms and insurance CIF or CIP with marine cargo insurance covering the full consignment value.
  7. Plan customs and clearing: Form M (Nigeria), PVoC/SONCAP where applicable, and a licensed clearing agent in your country.
  8. Keep an audit file for every shipment: invoices, CoA, CoPP, GMP certificate, bill of lading, and customs entry. Regulators will ask.
