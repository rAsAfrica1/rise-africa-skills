1. Upholstery Leather Production

Upholstery leather is thick, durable, and made for furniture, car seats, and bags. Start with hides from cattle or goats. For furniture, use full-grain hides at least 1.21.5 mm thick. After tanning (chrome or vegetable), you need to soften and finish the leather. For chrome-tanned upholstery, drum dye the leather to the desired colour, then fatliquor with 810% oil to keep it supple. Dry by hanging or vacuum drying to 1214% moisture. Then staking (mechanical softening) is criticalrun the leather through a staking machine 23 times to break fibres and make it pliable. For a natural look, apply a light coat of clear lacquer or wax. For a matte finish, use a duller. In Africa, many workshops use vegetable-tanned goat leather for traditional chairs and poufs. Vegetable tanning takes longer (weeks) but gives a firm, earthy feel. After tanning, apply a mixture of neatsfoot oil and beeswax (50:50) to condition. For upholstery, thickness matters: 1.52 mm for heavy-use sofas, 1.01.2 mm for decorative cushions. You can also emboss patterns using heated brass platescommon in Moroccan leatherwork. Test your finish by folding the leather; it should not crack. Store in a cool, dry place away from direct sun. Market your upholstery leather to local furniture makersa single hide (23 m) can cover a small armchair. Price: $30$60 per hide depending on quality. With practice, you can produce 1015 hides per week in a small workshop.

2. Garment and Glove Leather

Garment and glove leather must be soft, lightweight, and flexible. Use sheep, goat, or lamb skinsthey are thin (0.50.8 mm) and have a fine grain. After tanning (usually chrome), the leather is shaved to uniform thickness on a splitting machine. Then it is dyed in a drum with acid dyes at 4050C for 12 hours. Rinse and fatliquor with 1012% sulfated oil to keep it soft. Dry by hanging or toggle drying (stretched on frames) to prevent shrinkage. For glove leather, you need extreme softnessrun through a staking machine 34 times, then mill in a drum with wooden balls for 23 hours. This breaks the fibres without damaging the grain. For garment leather, a light spray finish of polyurethane or acrylic gives water resistance. In Africa, many tanneries in Nigeria and Ethiopia produce goat glove leather for local markets. A key test: fold the leather like a gloveit should feel like cloth. For gloves, you need 0.40.6 mm thickness; for jackets, 0.70.9 mm. Dye penetration must be completecut a cross-section to check. Common defects are veiny grain or scars; select clean skins. After finishing, measure per square foot. A goat skin yields 46 sq ft. Price: $2$4 per sq ft for garment, $3$5 for glove. You can sell to tailors, glove makers, or export to Europe. Keep leather away from moisture; store flat with tissue paper between layers. For a small workshop, a drum, a splitting machine, and a staking machine are essential. Start with 20 skins per week.

3. Shoe Upper Leather

Shoe upper leather needs strength, breathability, and a good finish. Use cattle hide (full-grain or corrected-grain) at 1.21.4 mm thickness. After tanning (chrome or vegetable), the leather is sammed (moistened) and set out. For shoe uppers, you want a tight grain. Defects like scratches are sanded and buffed to create corrected grain, then embossed with a plate to mimic full grain. Dye in a drum with anionic dyes at 60C for 1 hour. Fatliquor with 68% oiltoo much oil weakens the leather. Dry by vacuum or hanging to 14% moisture. Then stake and buff. For a shiny finish, apply a base coat of polyurethane, then a top coat of nitrocellulose lacquer. For matte, use a wax finish. In Africa, Ethiopia and Nigeria have tanneries making shoe upper leather for local shoemakers. Test quality by flexing: 100,000 flexes without cracking is ideal. Also check water resistancedrop water on the surface; it should bead for at least 30 seconds. For safety shoes, use 1.51.8 mm thickness. For dress shoes, 1.01.2 mm. Cut patterns to minimise wastea hide of 20 sq ft makes 23 pairs of shoes. Price: $3$6 per sq ft. Sell to shoe factories or artisan cobblers. Store in rolls, not folded, to avoid creases. With a drum, splitting machine, and spray booth, you can produce 50100 sq ft per day.

4. Belting and Strap Leather

Belting and strap leather must be strong, stretch-resistant, and thick. Use cattle hide (bull or ox) at 35 mm thickness. Vegetable tanning is preferred because it gives a firm, dense leather that holds shape. After tanning, the hide is split to the required thickness, then shaved. For belts, you need 34 mm; for heavy machinery belting, 56 mm. The leather is then wetted and stretched on a frame to remove wrinkles. Drying takes 23 days in shade. Do not use heatit makes leather brittle. After drying, apply a mixture of tallow and cod oil (70:30) to lubricate fibres. Then compress the leather with a roller or hammer to increase density. For straps, cut strips 25 cm wide, bevel edges, and burnish with a slicker and water. Dye with oil-based dye for deep penetration. In Africa, many artisans make belts from vegetable-tanned goat or cow leather. For machinery belting, you need to join ends with a lap splice and rivets or stitching. Test tensile strength: a 2 cm wide strap should hold at least 200 kg without breaking. For horse harness, use 45 mm thickness. Price: $5$10 per sq ft for belting, $2$4 per sq ft for belt strips. Sell to farmers, factories, or leather crafters. Store flat, away from moisture. A small workshop can produce 1020 belts per day with a splitting machine, strap cutter, and burnisher.

5. Bookbinding and Craft Leather

Bookbinding and craft leather is thin, flexible, and takes tooling well. Use goat, calf, or sheep skin at 0.50.8 mm thickness. Vegetable-tanned leather is best because it accepts dyes, gold tooling, and embossing. After tanning, the leather is split to uniform thickness, then dyed with spirit or water-based dyes. For bookbinding, you want a smooth grainskive edges thin for turning in. Apply a paste of wheat starch and water to the flesh side to stiffen it slightly. Dry under pressure between boards to keep flat. For craft leather (wallets, purses), you can use chrome-tanned leather for softness, but vegetable-tanned is preferred for tooling. To tool, case the leather (dampen with sponge), then stamp with heated brass tools. In Africa, Moroccan and Nigerian craftsmen are famous for leather bookbinding and poufs. For a book cover, cut leather 1 cm larger than the book board. Glue with PVA, then turn edges and press. For decoration, use gold leaf with heated tools. Test flexibility: fold the leather 180it should not crack. Price: $4$8 per sq ft for bookbinding leather, $2$5 for craft. Sell to bookbinders, craft shops, or tourists. Store in a cool, dark place. With a skiving machine, press, and tooling set, you can produce 2030 book covers per week.

6. Specialty Leathers

Specialty leathers are made for specific functions: waterproof, fire-resistant, or decorative. Examples include patent leather (high-gloss), nubuck (brushed grain), suede (flesh side), and exotic leathers (ostrich, crocodile). For patent leather, start with a corrected-grain hide, apply a base coat of polyurethane, then a top coat of acrylic lacquer, and cure under UV light. The finish must be mirror-like. For nubuck, buff the grain side with fine sandpaper (400 grit) to raise a velvet nap. For suede, use the flesh sidesplit the hide, then buff. In Africa, ostrich leather from South Africa is a luxury export. Ostrich skin has quill follicles that create a unique bumpy texture. Tan with chrome, then dye and finish with a light wax. Crocodile leather from Nile crocodiles is also prized. For these, you must comply with CITES regulations. Other specialty leathers include: fire-resistant leather (treated with ammonium phosphate), used for welding aprons; and waterproof leather (silicone or wax treatment). For a small workshop, focus on nubuck and suedethey are easier. Use a buffing machine with 180400 grit. Dye with a spray gun for even colour. Test waterproofing by dropping waterit should roll off. Price: patent $8$15 per sq ft, nubuck $5$8, ostrich $20$40. Sell to fashion designers, shoe makers, or export. Store specialty leathers in acid-free paper. With a spray booth and buffing machine, you can produce 50 sq ft per day.

7. Practical Reference


Practical Reference: Leather Types and Applications



This practical reference module is designed for African leather entrepreneurs, tanners, and artisans who need to match leather type to end-use market. Whether you are a small tannery in Ethiopia supplying shoe uppers, a workshop in Nigeria crafting upholstery for hospitality clients, or a cooperative in Kenya producing garment leather for fashion export, the commercial success of your operation depends on correctly identifying which leather type suits which application and producing to specification.



Africa produces approximately 1215% of the world's hides and skins by volume, yet captures less than 5% of the global finished leather value. The gap is largely due to insufficient downstream processing and poor matching of leather type to application. This lesson provides the technical and commercial reference data you need to close that gap in your own enterprise.

SAFETY WARNING: Chemical Hazards in Leather Processing


Critical safety information for all leather workers:



  • Chromium salts (basic chromium sulphate): Used in 85% of global leather production. Causes skin sensitisation, respiratory irritation, and is a known carcinogen when inhaled as dust. Always use in ventilated areas with appropriate PPE (nitrile gloves, respirator, goggles).

  • Formaldehyde and aldehydes: Used in tanning and finishing. Toxic by inhalation and skin contact. Ensure exhaust ventilation and never work in enclosed spaces without air monitoring.

  • Phenolic compounds and biocides: Common in preservation and soaking. Can cause chemical burns and systemic toxicity. Store separately from food and water sources.

  • Hydrogen sulphide (HS): Generated during liming and deliming. Colourless, highly toxic gas with a rotten-egg smell. At high concentrations, olfactory fatigue occurs you cannot smell it, but it can be fatal within minutes. Never enter pits or tanks without gas monitoring and confined-space procedures.

  • Acids and alkalis: Sulphuric acid, formic acid, sodium hydroxide. Always add acid to water, never water to acid. Have eyewash stations and safety showers accessible.


African context: Many small and medium tanneries in Africa operate with inadequate ventilation, limited PPE, and insufficient worker training. The ILO estimates that leather processing is among the top ten hazardous industries globally. If you are a tannery owner or manager, invest in ventilation, PPE, and worker training before scaling production. Your workers' health is not negotiable and a single serious incident can close your business.




Quick Reference Checklist




  1. Identify the end-use application first upholstery, garment, shoe upper, belting, bookbinding, or specialty before selecting raw materials or processing route. The application dictates thickness, tannage, and finish.

  2. Match raw material to leather type: cattle hide for upholstery and shoe upper; sheep and goat for garment and glove; vegetable-tanned cattle for belting; exotic skins for specialty. Do not substitute without testing.

  3. Verify thickness specifications with a calibrated gauge at multiple points across each hide. Buyers reject shipments for thickness variation exceeding 0.2 mm.

  4. Confirm tannage chemistry: chrome for most upholstery and shoe upper; vegetable for belting, bookbinding, and craft; combination tannages for specialty applications.

  5. Test physical properties before shipping: tensile strength, tear strength, flex resistance, colour fastness to light and rubbing, and shrinkage temperature. Keep test records for every batch.

  6. Document traceability: record hide/skin origin, date of processing, chemical inputs, and batch number. EU and US buyers increasingly require full traceability for compliance and sustainability reporting.

  7. Price against export benchmarks (Table 2) and factor in freight, export documentation, and compliance costs. Do not compete on price alone compete on consistent quality and reliable delivery.

  8. Invest in worker safety and environmental compliance before scaling. Inadequate effluent treatment and unsafe working conditions are the two fastest ways to lose export contracts and face regulatory shutdown.
