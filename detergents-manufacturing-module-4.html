Lessons

1. Balancing surfactant and builder ratios

In detergent formulation, the surfactant does the cleaning by lifting dirt and oil from surfaces, while the builder softens water and helps the surfactant work better. In Africa, water is often hard, meaning it contains calcium and magnesium ions that react with soap and reduce cleaning power. To balance them, start with a simple ratio: for a basic powder detergent, use 15-20% surfactant (like linear alkylbenzene sulfonate, LAS) and 20-30% builder (like soda ash or zeolite). If your water is very hard (above 300 ppm as CaCO3), increase builder to 30-40% and surfactant to 20-25%. Test by washing soiled cloth samples in your local water. If you see scum or poor cleaning, add more builder. If foam is too low, add more surfactant. For liquid detergents, use 5-10% surfactant and 5-15% builder (like sodium citrate). Always measure with a scale accurate to 0.1 g. A simple field test: mix 1 liter of water with 0.5 g of your detergent, shake, and observe foam height. Compare with a commercial brand. Adjust until your formula matches or beats it. Remember, too much builder can leave white residue on clothes, so balance is key. Use locally available materials: palm oil-based soap, soda ash from Lake Magadi, or zeolite from natural deposits. Keep records of each trial: date, water hardness, ratio, and result. This helps you refine your formula for your market.

2. Liquid vs powder detergent formulations

Liquid and powder detergents differ in ingredients and production. Powders are made by dry mixing or spray drying. For a simple powder: mix 30% soda ash, 20% LAS powder, 20% sodium sulfate, 10% sodium silicate, and 20% zeolite. Blend in a ribbon mixer for 10 minutes. Powders are cheap, easy to package, and stable in heat. But they can cake in humid climates like coastal West Africa. Add 1-2% anticaking agent like magnesium oxide. Liquids are made by dissolving ingredients in water. A basic liquid: 10% LAS, 5% sodium citrate, 2% coconut diethanolamide (foam booster), 0.1% preservative, and water to 100%. Heat water to 40Â°C, add LAS slowly while stirring, then add other ingredients. Adjust pH to 7-9 with citric acid or soda ash. Liquids clean better in cold water and are easy to use, but they spoil faster and need preservatives. In Africa, liquid detergents are popular for hand washing and dishwashing. Powders are preferred for machine washing and bulk sales. Consider your market: if customers have running water and wash by hand, liquid is convenient. If they wash in buckets with hard water, powder with high builder works better. Start with small batches: 5 kg powder or 10 L liquid. Use a digital scale, a bucket, and a wooden stirrer. Test both types with local soils and water. Choose the one that gives best cleaning at lowest cost.

3. Concentrated and compact detergents

Concentrated detergents use less water and packaging, saving money and reducing waste. They contain higher active ingredients: 30-40% surfactant and 40-50% builder in powders, or 20-30% surfactant in liquids. Compact powders are made by increasing LAS and zeolite, and reducing sodium sulfate filler. For example, a regular powder might have 15% LAS and 30% sodium sulfate; a concentrated version has 30% LAS and no sulfate. This means you use half the dose per wash. To make a concentrated powder: mix 35% LAS, 30% zeolite, 20% soda ash, 10% sodium silicate, and 5% sodium citrate. Blend well. Test dosage: use 20 g per 5 L water instead of 40 g. For liquid concentrate: 25% LAS, 10% sodium citrate, 5% coconut diethanolamide, 1% preservative, and water to 100%. Adjust viscosity with 1-2% sodium chloride. Concentrates save transport costs, which is crucial in landlocked countries like Uganda or Rwanda. But they must dissolve well; if not, users may see clumps. Add 2% sodium carbonate to help dissolution. Also, concentrates can be harsh on skin, so add 1-2% glycerin as a skin softener. Package in small sachets (10-20 g) for single use, which is popular in informal settlements. Always label with clear dosage instructions: one sachet per bucket. Test stability: store at 40Â°C for 4 weeks; if it cakes or separates, adjust formula. Concentrates are a growing market in Africa due to cost savings and environmental benefits.

4. pH control and buffering

pH measures how acidic or alkaline a detergent is. Most detergents work best at pH 9-11 for cleaning oily dirt, but too high can damage fabrics and skin. For liquid detergents, aim for pH 8-9; for powders, pH 10-11 when dissolved. To control pH, use buffers: chemicals that resist pH changes. Common buffers: sodium carbonate (soda ash) and sodium bicarbonate (baking soda) for alkaline; citric acid for acidic. For a liquid detergent, add 2-3% sodium citrate to buffer at pH 8.5. For powder, 20-30% soda ash naturally buffers at pH 10.5. Always measure pH with a digital pH meter or pH paper. Calibrate the meter with pH 7 and 10 solutions. If pH is too high, add citric acid slowly (0.1% at a time) until target. If too low, add soda ash. In hard water, pH may drop because minerals react with detergent. So test your detergent in local water: mix 1 g in 100 mL water, stir, and measure pH. If it drops below 8, increase buffer. Also, pH affects preservatives: some work only at pH below 8. For liquid detergents, use preservatives like methylisothiazolinone that work at pH 8-9. Store your product in a cool place; high heat can change pH. Keep a log of pH for each batch. This ensures consistent quality and prevents complaints about skin irritation or poor cleaning.

5. Viscosity and rheology modifiers

Viscosity is how thick a liquid detergent is. Thick liquids are popular because they feel concentrated and pour easily without splashing. To thicken, use rheology modifiers: sodium chloride (table salt), xanthan gum, or carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC). For a simple liquid detergent, start with 1-2% sodium chloride. Add it slowly while stirring; too much can thin the product. Test by pouring: if it flows like water, add more salt. For a gel-like detergent, use 0.5-1% xanthan gum. Dissolve xanthan in water first, then add other ingredients. CMC at 1-2% also thickens and helps suspend dirt. In Africa, xanthan gum may be expensive; a cheaper alternative is cassava starch: cook 1% starch in water, cool, and add to detergent. But starch can spoil, so add preservative. For powders, viscosity is not an issue, but particle size affects flow: use a 0.5-1 mm mesh sieve to ensure uniform granules. For liquids, also consider temperature: viscosity drops when hot. Test at 25Â°C and 40Â°C. If it becomes too thin at 40Â°C, increase thickener. Use a simple viscometer or a flow cup: measure time for 100 mL to flow through a 4 mm hole. Water takes 5 seconds; a good detergent takes 20-30 seconds. Adjust until you reach that. Always add thickeners after all other ingredients are dissolved. Stir gently to avoid air bubbles. Record the amount used for each batch.

6. Stability and compatibility testing

Stability testing checks if your detergent stays good over time. Compatibility testing checks if ingredients mix without separating or reacting. For a new formula, do a simple test: mix a small batch and store samples in three conditions: room temperature (25Â°C), warm (40Â°C), and cool (10Â°C). Check after 1 day, 1 week, and 4 weeks. Look for separation, sedimentation, color change, or smell. For liquids, if layers form, you need an emulsifier like cocamidopropyl betaine (1-2%). For powders, if it cakes, add 1% magnesium oxide or silica. Also test compatibility with packaging: fill a sample in your intended bottle or sachet and observe for 4 weeks. Some plastics react with surfactants, causing leaks or swelling. In Africa, high humidity can cause powder clumping; store in sealed containers with desiccant. For liquids, test freeze-thaw: freeze overnight, then thaw; if it separates, add 5% propylene glycol as antifreeze. Also test with local water: mix 1 g in 100 mL of your water source; if scum forms, increase builder. Keep a control sample of a commercial detergent for comparison. Document everything: date, batch number, observations. If a batch fails, adjust one ingredient at a time. For example, if pH drops, add more buffer. Stability testing takes time but prevents costly recalls. Start with small batches and scale up only after passing 4-week tests.

Practical Reference: Formulation Principles for Detergent Manufacturing in Africa

This practical reference consolidates the core formulation principles covered in this module: balancing surfactant and builder ratios, choosing between liquid and powder formats, developing concentrated and compact products, controlling pH and buffering, managing viscosity and rheology, and running stability and compatibility tests. It is written for African detergent manufacturers, SME formulators, and technical managers who need to move from laboratory trials to consistent, saleable production. Each section links formulation decisions to cost, raw material availability, equipment constraints, and market realities across the continent.

1. Balancing Surfactant and Builder Ratios

The surfactant-to-builder ratio is the single most important formulation decision. Surfactants (LAS, SLES, AOS, soap) do the cleaning; builders (sodium carbonate, zeolites, sodium citrate, STPP) soften water, buffer pH, and prevent soil redeposition. In African markets, water hardness varies enormouslyâfrom soft rainwater in parts of Central Africa to very hard borehole water in the Sahel and Southern Africa. A formulation that works in Nairobi may fail in Dakar if builder levels are too low.

For powder detergents, a surfactant-to-builder ratio of 1:1 to 1:3 is typical. For liquids, the ratio shifts to 3:1 up to 6:1 because liquid builders are limited (citrate, zeolite slurries) and high electrolyte levels destabilise surfactants. Start with a 1:2 ratio for powder and 4:1 for liquid, then adjust based on water hardness in your target market.

2. Liquid vs Powder Detergent Formulations

Format choice drives everything: raw materials, equipment, packaging, shelf life, and consumer perception. In Africa, powder dominates the mass market because it is cheaper to produce, easier to package in sachets, and has a longer shelf life in hot, humid conditions. Liquids are growing in urban markets where consumers value convenience and cold-water washing.

Powder formulations require spray towers or agglomeration equipment, which are capital-intensive. Small African manufacturers often use dry blendingâmixing powdered surfactants (e.g., sodium LAS powder) with builders and fillers. This limits surfactant loading to about 15â20% but keeps investment low. Liquid formulations need mixing tanks, homogenisers, and preservatives. They are easier to start producing but cost more per wash because of water content and packaging.

Key formulation differences: powders can use high levels of sodium carbonate and zeolite; liquids cannot tolerate high electrolyte without phase separation. Powders need anti-caking agents (e.g., sodium sulphate, magnesium silicate); liquids need hydrotropes (e.g., sodium xylene sulphonate, urea) and preservatives (e.g., MIT, benzisothiazolinone).

3. Concentrated and Compact Detergents

Concentrated detergents deliver the same cleaning with less product, reducing packaging, transport, and storage costs. In Africa, where distribution costs are high and shelf space limited, compaction is a strong business case. A compact powder at 40â50 g/wash replaces 100â120 g/wash of conventional powder.

Formulation principles for compaction: increase surfactant active matter (to 20â30%), use high-performance builders (zeolite A, layered silicates), add polymers (polycarboxylates) to inhibit crystal growth and improve soil suspension, and control particle size distribution (300â800 Âµm) to avoid segregation. For liquids, use hydrotropes and rheology modifiers to keep high surfactant concentrations stable.

4. pH Control and Buffering

Detergent pH affects cleaning, fabric care, skin safety, and stability. Hand-wash powders typically target pH 9.5â10.5 in use; machine powders pH 9â10; liquid hand dishwash pH 7â8; and liquid laundry pH 7.5â9. In Africa, where hand washing dominates, high pH can cause skin irritation, so buffering systems must be chosen carefully.

Common buffers: sodium carbonate/bicarbonate (pH 9â10.5), sodium citrate/citric acid (pH 3â6), and monoethanolamine (pH 9â11 in liquids). For powders, sodium carbonate is the workhorseâcheap, available, and effective. For liquids, citrate buffers are preferred because they are mild and compatible with enzymes. Always measure pH at use dilution, not concentrate, because dilution changes ionic strength and pH.

Concentrated sodium hydroxide (caustic soda) and sodium carbonate solutions are strongly alkaline and can cause severe skin burns and permanent eye damage. In African production environments where PPE may be limited, always wear chemical splash goggles, nitrile gloves, and a face shield when handling caustic solutions. Never add water to concentrated causticâalways add caustic to water slowly, with stirring, to control heat release. Ensure adequate ventilation and keep acid and alkali storage separated. Provide eyewash stations and train all staff on emergency procedures. Do not allow untrained workers to handle concentrated alkalis.

5. Viscosity and Rheology Modifiers

Viscosity affects pourability, consumer perception, and stability. In liquid detergents, thickening is achieved with sodium chloride (for surfactant-structured liquids), xanthan gum, carbomers, or cellulose derivatives (HEC, HPMC). In Africa, sodium chloride is the cheapest and most available thickener for surfactant-based liquids, but it only works within a narrow concentration windowâtoo much causes phase separation.

For structured liquids (e.g., those containing suspended particles or enzymes), xanthan gum or modified cellulose at 0.2â0.8% provides yield stress and shear-thinning behaviour. For compact liquids, high surfactant levels naturally increase viscosity; hydrotropes (sodium xylene sulphonate, sodium cumene sulphonate) are used to control it. Always measure viscosity at 25Â°C with a Brookfield viscometer (spindle 2 or 3, 20 rpm) and record the shear rate.

6. Stability and Compatibility Testing

Stability testing is non-negotiable before commercial launch. In Africa, products may be stored at 35â45Â°C for months, so accelerated testing is essential. Standard tests include:

  • Accelerated heat test: Store samples at 45Â°C for 4â12 weeks. Check for phase separation, colour change, viscosity drift, and pH drift.
  • Freeze-thaw cycling: 3â5 cycles between â5Â°C and 25Â°C to simulate transport in cold-chain or highland regions.
  • Compatibility testing: Mix surfactant, builder, polymer, enzyme, and perfume in the final ratio. Observe for precipitation, cloudiness, or gel formation over 72 hours.
  • Packaging compatibility: Store product in the intended packaging (HDPE, PET, sachet film) at 45Â°C for 4 weeks. Check for swelling, leakage, or migration.
  • Performance test: Wash standard soiled swatches (e.g., EMPA 116, EMPA 117) at the intended dilution and temperature. Measure reflectance before and after.

Document every batch. In African markets, where regulatory enforcement varies, your own stability data is your best defence against costly recalls and reputational damage.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Water hardness first: Test the water in your target market before setting builder levels. Hard water needs more builder, not more surfactant.
  2. Ratio rule of thumb: Powder 1:2 surfactant:builder; liquid 4:1. Adjust up or down based on cleaning performance and cost.
  3. Format follows market: Powder for mass market and sachets; liquid for urban convenience. Don't assumeâask your customers.
  4. Compact means concentrated: Increase surfactant and polymer, reduce filler. Target 40â50 g/wash for powder.
  5. pH at use dilution: Measure pH after dilution, not in concentrate. Target 9.5â10.5 for hand-wash powder, 7.5â9 for liquid laundry.
  6. Thicken with care: Sodium chloride is cheap but narrow. Use xanthan gum for structured liquids. Always measure viscosity at 25Â°C.

  8. Document everything: Batch records, raw material certificates, and test results protect your business and your customers.
