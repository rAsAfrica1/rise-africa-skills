1. Air Drying: Controlling Humidity and Temperature

After shaping your tiles or ceramics, they must dry slowly and evenly to avoid cracks. In Africa, humidity varies widely: coastal regions like Mombasa or Lagos can be 80-90% relative humidity, while inland areas like Harare or Kano may drop to 20-30%. Your goal is to dry pieces at a rate of about 1-2% moisture loss per day. Start by covering fresh pieces with a plastic sheet or damp cloth for the first 24-48 hours. This equalizes moisture. Then, gradually uncover them over 3-5 days. If drying too fast, you'll see warping or surface cracks. If too slow, pieces stay soft and may grow mold.

Monitor temperature and humidity with a simple hygrometer (available at agricultural supply stores) and a thermometer. Ideal drying conditions: 20-30C and 50-70% relative humidity. In dry heat, mist the air around pieces with a spray bottle twice daily. In humid conditions, use a fan to circulate air, but not directly on the pieces. Place pieces on wooden boards or wire racks so air reaches all sides. Rotate them every 12 hours.

For tiles, stack them with spacers (thin sticks or coins) between each tile to allow airflow. Keep stacks no higher than 10 tiles. Dry in a shaded, well-ventilated area away from direct sun and wind. A drying cabinet can be made from a wooden box with a 40-watt bulb for gentle heat and a small vent. Test dryness by pressing a piece to your cheek: if it feels cool, it's still damp. When bone dry, it will feel room temperature and sound hollow when tapped. This usually takes 7-14 days depending on thickness and climate. Never rush drying; it's the cheapest way to prevent losses.

2. Building a Simple Kiln with Local Materials

You can build a kiln that reaches 1000-1100C using mud bricks, a metal drum, or even termite mound clay. For a small updraft kiln, gather 200-300 fired clay bricks (or mud bricks mixed with straw), a metal grate, and a metal sheet for a door. Dig a pit 60 cm deep and 80 cm wide. Line the bottom with bricks, leaving a 15 cm channel for air. Build walls up to 1 meter high, leaving a 30x30 cm opening for the firebox at the base and a 20x20 cm flue at the top. Use clay mortar (mix sieved clay, sand, and water to peanut butter consistency) to join bricks. Let the structure dry for 3 days before firing.

For a drum kiln, cut a 200-liter steel drum in half vertically. Line the inside with 5 cm of clay-sand mixture (3 parts clay, 1 part sand). Cut a 20 cm hole near the bottom for a firebox and a 10 cm hole at the top for a chimney. Place a metal grate 10 cm above the bottom. The drum acts as the kiln chamber. Insulate the outside with sand or ash to reduce heat loss. This kiln can reach 1000C if you use dry hardwood or charcoal and force air with a hair dryer or bellows.

Key tips: Build on level ground away from flammable materials. Ensure the firebox is large enough to hold enough fuel for 4-6 hours. Use a metal door with a peephole to check cones. Test-fire with no pots to cure the clay and find leaks. Repair cracks with fresh clay mortar. In rural areas, a simple trench kiln (a 1.5 m long, 50 cm wide trench lined with bricks) works well for bisque firing. Always have a lid (metal sheet) to control air. With careful construction, your kiln will last for many firings.

3. Loading and Firing a Bisque Kiln

Bisque firing hardens your greenware so it can be glazed without breaking. Load the kiln when pieces are bone dry. Arrange them on shelves or stacked with kiln furniture (clay stilts or sea shells). Leave 2-3 cm gaps between pieces for heat circulation. Place larger pieces on the bottom, smaller ones on top. Never stack glazed pieces in a bisque firing. Use a kiln shelf (a fired clay slab) to separate layers. If you don't have shelves, place pieces on a bed of sand or alumina powder to prevent sticking.

Start the fire slowly. For the first hour, keep the kiln at 100-200C to drive off remaining moisture. If you see steam, slow down. Increase to 400C over the next 2 hours. Then ramp to 600C over 2 hours. Finally, go to 900-1000C over 2-3 hours. Total firing time: 6-8 hours. Use dry wood or charcoal. Stoke the fire every 15-20 minutes, adding small amounts to avoid temperature drops. Watch the color: at 500C, the kiln interior glows dull red; at 900C, bright orange.

After reaching temperature, stop adding fuel and let the kiln cool naturally. Do not open the door until the kiln is below 200C (usually 8-12 hours). Opening early causes cracking. Once cool, unload. Bisque-fired pieces are porous and ready for glazing. If pieces are still black (from carbon), they need more oxygen during firing. If they are warped, you fired too fast. Record your times and temperatures for next time. A successful bisque firing makes a clear ringing sound when tapped.

4. Understanding Pyrometric Cones and Temperature

Pyrometric cones are small ceramic pyramids that bend at specific temperatures. They are your most reliable tool for judging heat work in a kiln, especially without a digital controller. Cones are numbered: cone 022 bends at 600C, cone 06 at 999C, cone 04 at 1060C, cone 1 at 1130C, and cone 10 at 1300C. For bisque firing, you typically use cone 06 to cone 04. Place three cones in the kiln: a guide cone (one number lower than target), a target cone, and a guard cone (one number higher). Put them on a clay patty near a peephole so you can see them.

To use cones, make a small clay coil and press the cone into it at a 90 angle, leaning slightly. The cone should bend when the kiln reaches its rated temperature. Watch through the peephole. When the target cone bends to touch its base, shut off the fuel. If the guide cone bends but target doesn't, you need more heat. If the guard cone bends, you've overfired. Cones measure both temperature and time (heat work), so they are more accurate than a thermometer.

In Africa, you can buy cones from ceramic suppliers or make your own by mixing feldspar, clay, and silica in specific ratios. But buying is easier. Store cones in a dry place. Use a cone holder (a small clay stand) to keep them upright. For kilns without peepholes, place cones on the top shelf and check after cooling. A bent cone tells you the firing was successful. Always keep a log of cone numbers and bending behavior. This helps you adjust your firing schedule for different clay bodies.

5. Firing Schedules for Different Clay Bodies

Different clays need different firing schedules. Earthenware (red or white) matures at 950-1100C (cone 06-04). Stoneware matures at 1200-1300C (cone 4-10). Porcelain needs 1300-1400C (cone 10-14). In Africa, most local clays are earthenware, so focus on 1000C. For earthenware bisque, fire slowly to 600C (2 hours), then fast to 1000C (2 hours), hold for 15 minutes, then cool. For stoneware, extend the 600-1000C ramp to 3 hours, then go to 1250C over 2 hours, hold 20 minutes.

If you mix clays, test small bars first. Make a schedule: rate of temperature rise, target temperature, and hold time. A typical schedule: 100C/hour to 200C, 150C/hour to 600C, 200C/hour to 1000C, hold 15 minutes. For thick pieces (over 2 cm), slow down to 50C/hour up to 600C to avoid cracking. For thin tiles, you can go faster. Always preheat the kiln to 100C for 1 hour to remove moisture.

Use cones to verify. If your clay bloats or warps, you fired too high or too fast. If it's underfired, it will be weak and absorb water. Adjust your schedule based on results. Keep a notebook: date, clay type, firing schedule, cone result, and observations. In humid climates, add an extra 30 minutes at 100C to drive off moisture. In cold weather, insulate the kiln better. With practice, you'll develop schedules for your specific clay and kiln.

6. Safety and Maintenance of Kilns

Kiln safety is critical. Always wear safety glasses, heat-resistant gloves, and a dust mask when handling kiln materials. Never fire a kiln indoors without proper ventilation; carbon monoxide and other gases are deadly. Place your kiln outdoors or in a shed with a chimney and open vents. Keep a fire extinguisher (dry powder) nearby. Do not leave a firing kiln unattended for more than 30 minutes. Have a clear area of 2 meters around the kiln, free of flammable materials like wood, paper, or petrol.

Before each firing, inspect the kiln for cracks, loose bricks, or damaged metal parts. Repair cracks with clay mortar. Check the firebox and grate for ash buildup; clean them. Ensure the door seals well; use clay or sand to fill gaps. During firing, monitor the chimney for excessive smoke (indicating incomplete combustion). If you smell gas or see sparks, stop firing immediately. After firing, let the kiln cool completely before cleaning. Remove ash and debris. Check cones and shelves for damage.

Monthly maintenance: tighten any metal bands, replace worn firebricks, and re-coat the interior with clay slip if needed. Keep a log of maintenance and repairs. For drum kilns, check for rust and repaint with high-temperature paint. For mud kilns, re-plaster cracks after every 5 firings. Store kiln furniture (shelves, stilts) in a dry place. Train all users on safety procedures. In case of burns, cool with water and seek medical help. Never use water on a hot kiln; it can cause steam explosions. With proper care, your kiln will last for years and fire safely.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Drying, Bisque Firing & Kiln Operation for African Ceramics Enterprises

In African ceramics enterprises from Nkwerre and Ilorin in Nigeria to Thimi in Nepal-style traditions adapted across the continent, and from Ethiopian pottery cooperatives to Kenyan and South African studio potteries the difference between a profitable kiln load and a wasted one is rarely the clay. It is moisture control, firing discipline, and kiln maintenance. This reference module gives you the numbers, schedules, and checks that protect your margin on every firing.

Business case first: a failed bisque firing can destroy 2060% of a load. At a modest 100-piece load selling at $3$8 per piece, that is $60$480 lost per firing often more than the monthly fuel budget. Controlled drying and correct bisque schedules convert that loss into sellable inventory.

1. Air Drying: Controlling Humidity and Temperature

Greenware must be bone-dry before bisque firing. Any residual moisture turns to steam at 100 C and can explode the piece or crack the kiln wall. In humid African coastal zones (Lagos, Mombasa, Accra) ambient relative humidity can sit above 80% for weeks drying that would take 3 days in Kano can take 10 in Calabar.

Operating rules that protect your load:

  • Dry in the shade, never direct sun fast surface drying causes differential shrinkage and cracking.
  • Cover with damp cloth or plastic for the first 2448 hours in dry climates to equalise moisture.
  • Use a cheap hygrometer (US$515) and a thermometer. Record daily. Data beats guesswork.
  • Weigh a test piece daily. When it stops losing weight, it is dry. This is the most reliable test available to a small workshop.
  • Warm, moving air (fan on low, 3040 C) cuts drying time 3050% without cracking risk.

2. Building a Simple Kiln with Local Materials

Imported electric kilns cost US$800$3,000 landed in most African markets. A well-built updraft kiln from fired bricks, mud mortar, and a metal grate can be built for US$80$300 in materials and fired on charcoal, wood, or waste oil. Many Nigerian, Ghanaian, and Kenyan potteries run profitable businesses on exactly this model.

Core components:

  1. Firebox 4060 cm deep, with a cast-iron or steel grate for ash drop.
  2. Ware chamber 60100 cm internal diameter, walls 2025 cm thick.
  3. Insulation layer sawdust/clay/ash mix (1:1:1) packed outside the fired brick.
  4. Chimney 1.52.5 m tall, cross-section 1/8 of ware chamber area, for reliable draught.
  5. Shelves fired clay slabs or kiln shelves on clay props.

Build on a dry, level concrete or packed-earth base. Cure the mud mortar for 714 days before first firing, then do a slow "seasoning" fire to 200 C over 46 hours to drive off residual moisture.

3. Loading and Firing a Bisque Kiln

Bisque firing (typically 9501,000 C) hardens greenware so it can be glazed without dissolving. Loading discipline directly affects fuel cost and success rate.

  • Leave 12 cm gaps between pieces for airflow. Never stack pieces touching.
  • Place larger, thicker pieces lower; smaller, thinner pieces higher.
  • Use stilts and wadding so glazed bases (for single-fire) don't stick.
  • Keep the spy hole clear to observe cones.
  • Load when the kiln is cold. Never load hot.

4. Understanding Pyrometric Cones and Temperature

Pyrometric cones measure heat work time + temperature not just temperature. A kiln that sits at 1,000 C for 2 hours has done more heat work than one that spikes to 1,000 C and drops. This is why cone numbers, not thermocouple readings, are the potter's true endpoint.

5. Firing Schedules for Different Clay Bodies

Match schedule to body. A generic schedule wastes fuel or fails pieces.

  • Red earthenware (low-fire): 60 C/hr to 120 C, hold 1 hr; 100 C/hr to 600 C; 150 C/hr to 950 C; end on cone 08.
  • Buff stoneware (mid-fire): 80 C/hr to 120 C, hold 1 hr; 120 C/hr to 600 C; 150 C/hr to 1,000 C; end on cone 06.
  • High-fire stoneware: 60 C/hr to 120 C, hold 12 hr; 100 C/hr to 600 C; 120 C/hr to 1,200 C; end on cone 6.
  • Always: slow 60120 C/hr through the 500600 C quartz inversion zone, or you will crack thick ware.
SAFETY WARNING KILN OPERATION
  • Carbon monoxide: never fire a wood/charcoal kiln indoors or in a closed shed. CO is odourless and kills. Fire outdoors or with forced cross-ventilation.
  • Burns: kiln surfaces reach 300 C+. Wear leather gloves, closed shoes, and cotton (not synthetic) clothing. Keep a water bucket and burn kit within 3 m.
  • Fire risk: keep 3 m clearance from thatch, plastic, fuel drums, and dry vegetation. Never leave an active firing unattended for more than 15 minutes.
  • Lead glazes: never fire leaded glazes in a kiln also used for food-ware unless you have separate kilns. Lead vapour contaminates the chamber.
  • Children and animals: fence the kiln area. Firing is an adult-only task.

6. Safety and Maintenance of Kilns

Maintenance is cheaper than replacement. A kiln that loses 20% of its heat through cracks can double your fuel bill within a month.

  • After every 10 firings, inspect walls for cracks. Patch with fresh mud mortar while cold.
  • Clean the grate and ash pit before every firing blocked draught equals uneven temperature.
  • Replace warped shelves immediately; they cause wobbly loads and cracked ware.
  • Keep a firing log: date, fuel used, cones reached, load count, defects. This is your business intelligence.
  • Cover the kiln with a simple roof (corrugated sheet) to stop rain damage during the rainy season.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Test piece weight stable for 2 days before firing.
  2. Kiln cold, clean grate, ash pit emptied.
  3. Ware loaded with 12 cm gaps, spy hole clear.
  4. Cones placed at top, middle, and bottom of chamber.
  5. Slow ramp through 500600 C (quartz inversion).
  6. Firing log updated with fuel, time, cones, defects.
  7. Kiln cooled naturally to below 100 C before unloading.
  8. Walls inspected and patched after cooling.
