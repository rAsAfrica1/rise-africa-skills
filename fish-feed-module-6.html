Module 6

🐠 Aquafeed Ingredients and Sourcing

Know every ingredient you can buy or grow: what protein it carries, how much of it a fish can actually digest, what it costs at your gate, and where it stops working. This module turns a market list into a set of usable feed inputs with prices, inclusion limits and quality tests attached.

Lesson 6.1~11 min

Fish Meal and Fish Oil

In this lesson
  • State the protein, amino acid and phosphorus value that makes fish meal expensive but hard to replace
  • Decide which classes of fish on your farm justify fish meal and which do not
  • Test artisanal fish meal for the adulteration and quality problems common at local markets

Fish meal is the benchmark every other protein is measured against, and for good reason. At 60-65 percent crude protein it carries 2.2-2.4 percent lysine and 0.9-1.1 percent methionine, and fish digest 90-95 percent of that protein. It also brings 1.2-1.5 percent total phosphorus at roughly 85 percent availability, no phytate at all, and vitamin B12, which is produced by bacteria and is essentially absent from plant meals. A feed built only on soya and cereals is a real B12 deficiency risk unless it is supplemented.

The problem is price. Fish meal at 65 percent protein ran USD 500-700 per tonne in 2005 and is estimated at USD 1,400-2,000 per tonne in 2026, or USD 1.50-3.00/kg at farm level. Fish oil has risen further, from USD 600-800 per tonne to an estimated USD 2,200-3,500. Both are tied to wild pelagic catch, so both are volatile and neither is going to get cheaper in a predictable way.

That pushes you to a simple decision rule: spend fish meal and fish oil where the fish cannot manage without them, and substitute everywhere else.

  1. Fry and larvae. Tilapia fry need 50-56 percent crude protein and African catfish fry 45-50 percent. Reaching that density with plant meals at 44-48 percent protein is arithmetically difficult and digestibility is worse at exactly the stage when digestibility matters most. Fish meal earns its price here.
  2. Broodstock. Fish oil supplies preformed EPA and DHA, which drive spawning and larval quality, and vegetable oils contain none. This is the one place the reference data recommends buying fish oil outright.
  3. Fingerling diets. A modest 10-15 percent inclusion balances the amino acid profile of a mostly plant formula cheaply.
  4. Grow-out. Here it is largely optional. Tilapia grow-out feeds run well on soybean meal at 40-50 percent of the diet with rice bran, and channel catfish trials show 92-98 percent of fish meal performance from a fully plant-based diet when amino acids are balanced.

Africa has an advantage the global price table does not show. Artisanal fish meal from local landing sites, made from dagaa, mukene, omena or fish processing offcuts, is quoted at USD 50-150 per tonne in Nigeria, Tanzania and Uganda, against USD 300-450 for imported soybean meal. Where you can buy it, small fish meal is the cheapest quality protein on the continent.

It comes with quality risks that you must test for, and none of the tests need equipment.

  • Sand and salt adulteration. Stir a level tablespoon into a clear glass of water, let it settle, and look for a gritty layer at the bottom that is a different colour from the meal. Meal that tastes strongly of salt has been bulked out with it.
  • Moisture. Squeeze a handful. It should fall apart, not hold a shape. Damp meal at a landing site will mould before you have used it.
  • Rancidity. Fish meal is high in unsaturated fat. A sharp, paint-like smell rather than a clean fishy one means the oil has oxidised, and oxidised fat destroys vitamin E and depresses intake.
  • Thiaminase. Feeds containing more than about 5 percent raw fish material carry a thiamine destruction risk, showing as loss of appetite and neurological signs. Properly dried and processed meal is not a concern; wet or barely dried trash fish is.

Worked example, Uganda. A farmer at Jinja compares two ways to reach 32 percent crude protein in a 100 kg batch. Option A uses 20 kg of imported fish meal at 65 percent protein, USD 2.20/kg, so USD 44 for 13 protein points. Option B uses 25 kg of mukene meal at 55 percent protein bought at the landing site for USD 0.55/kg, so USD 13.75 for 13.75 protein points. Option B is 69 percent cheaper for the same protein contribution, and the extra 5 kg of inclusion is easily absorbed by cutting maize bran. The whole gain sits in local sourcing, not in formulation cleverness.

Plant-Based Protein Sources

In this lesson
  • Compare the common oilseed cakes on protein, lysine, methionine, digestibility and price
  • Blend two plant proteins so their amino acid weaknesses cancel out
  • Reduce anti-nutritional factors with heat and simple on-farm fermentation

Plant protein is what actually fills a smallholder feed bag. The question is never whether to use it but which ones, in what proportions, and what has to be done to them first.

The reference figures, per kilogram of ingredient, for protein, lysine, methionine, digestibility in fish and 2026 price:

  1. Soybean meal: 44-48 percent protein, 1.6-1.8 percent lysine, 0.4-0.5 percent methionine, 75-85 percent digestible, USD 0.30-0.50/kg.
  2. Cottonseed meal: 40-45 percent protein, 1.4-1.6 lysine, 0.4-0.5 methionine, 65-75 percent digestible, USD 0.35-0.50/kg.
  3. Canola meal: 36-40 percent protein, 1.2-1.4 lysine, 0.5-0.6 methionine, 70-80 percent digestible, USD 0.40-0.60/kg.
  4. Sunflower meal: 38-42 percent protein, 1.2-1.4 lysine, 0.5-0.6 methionine, 70-80 percent digestible, USD 0.40-0.60/kg.
  5. Rapeseed meal: 38-42 percent protein, 1.4-1.6 lysine, 0.5-0.6 methionine, 68-78 percent digestible, USD 0.35-0.55/kg.
  6. Pea meal: 20-24 percent protein, 0.9-1.1 lysine, 0.2-0.3 methionine, 75-85 percent digestible, USD 0.35-0.50/kg.
  7. Chickpea meal: 22-26 percent protein, 1.0-1.2 lysine, 0.2-0.3 methionine, 72-80 percent digestible, USD 0.40-0.60/kg.

Read the lysine and methionine columns and the strategy becomes obvious. Soybean meal is lysine-rich and methionine-poor. Canola and rapeseed are the opposite way round: less lysine, slightly more methionine. Blending them covers both. The documented blend is 60 percent soybean meal, 30 percent canola and 10 percent fish meal, which gives a balanced essential amino acid profile at 20-25 percent lower cost than a half soy, half fish meal formula, with DL-methionine added at 0.2-0.3 percent to finish the job. Pulses such as pea and chickpea, produced widely in Ethiopia and across the Sahel, work at 20-30 percent inclusion alongside 35-40 percent soy and 15-20 percent maize, though their indigestible oligosaccharides carry a 5-10 percent FCR penalty that fermentation reduces.

The anti-nutritional factors are the price of using plants. Soybean meal carries phytate, which binds 60-80 percent of its phosphorus and also ties up zinc, iron and copper; trypsin inhibitors, which block protein digestion; and oligosaccharides. Three fixes, in order of practicality for a small farm.

Heat treatment. Steam or extrusion inactivates trypsin inhibitors and improves soybean meal digestibility by 30-40 percent, at a cost of USD 0.05-0.15/kg for extrusion, and it reduces the supplemental amino acids needed by about 10 percent. Most commercial soybean meal from an oil mill has already been toasted; raw full-fat soya beans have not, and must be cooked before use.

Fermentation. Mix soybean meal with water at two parts meal to one part water, add whey or plain yoghurt as a Lactobacillus source, seal the container and leave it 48-72 hours at ambient temperature. This cuts phytate 30-50 percent and raises methionine bioavailability 10-15 percent, at a cost of USD 0.05-0.08/kg. Fermented meal keeps two to three weeks; sun-dry it on a tarp if you want longer.

Phytase enzyme. Covered in Lesson 5, at 500-1,000 FTU/kg it recovers 30-40 percent of the bound phosphorus.

One warning specific to cottonseed cake, which is cheap and abundant across West and East Africa: it is a good buy at moderate inclusion, but it must come from a mill that heat-treats, and it should not be the sole protein in a fry or fingerling diet where its lower 65-75 percent digestibility hurts most.

Worked example, Nigeria. A farmer near Kano needs 40 kg of protein concentrate for a 100 kg batch. Using 40 kg of soybean meal at USD 0.42/kg costs USD 16.80 and supplies about 18.4 kg of protein with 0.68 kg lysine and 0.18 kg methionine. Switching to 25 kg soya plus 15 kg cottonseed cake at USD 0.38/kg costs USD 10.50 plus USD 5.70, so USD 16.20, and supplies 11.5 plus 6.4 kg of protein, roughly the same total. The saving is small; the real reason to blend is supply security when one cake is short at the mill.

Alternative Proteins (Insect, Algae)

In this lesson
  • Evaluate black soldier fly larvae meal against fish meal on protein, digestibility and cost
  • Set realistic inclusion levels for insect meal, microalgae and Moringa leaf meal
  • Cost a small on-farm BSFL production unit against buying protein

Three alternative proteins are within reach of an African smallholder: black soldier fly larvae meal, microalgae, and Moringa leaf meal. They are not equally useful, and the difference is mostly cost.

Black soldier fly larvae meal is the strongest of the three. It carries 40-45 percent crude protein on a dry basis with 25-35 percent lipid, and fish digest 85-90 percent of that protein, close to fish meal and clearly better than soybean meal. It contains no phytate. Its one amino acid weakness is methionine, which needs supplementing above about 0.3 percent of the diet when BSFL meal is doing most of the protein work. Tilapia fingerlings on 50 percent BSFL inclusion perform at 90-100 percent of a fish meal control, and channel catfish at 95-98 percent. Chitin in the exoskeleton reduces mineral bioavailability slightly, in the range of 2-5 percent. It is quoted at USD 1.00-1.50/kg, cost-competitive with soya once you account for its much higher protein density and lower ammonia output.

The practical formula is 30-50 percent BSFL meal plus plant proteins plus 1-2 percent fish meal. Full replacement works in trials but is not yet realistic at smallholder scale.

Producing it yourself is the interesting part. The process is: collect a feedstock of about half plant waste, vegetable peelings, brewery spent grain or fish processing scraps, and half bran or manure substrate. Larvae take two to three weeks to reach pre-pupal stage. Harvest the pre-pupae, sun-dry to under 10 percent moisture, which takes three to five days in tropical conditions, and grind. Ferment the dried meal 1:1 with soybean meal using a Lactobacillus starter for 48-72 hours if you want the extra 10-15 percent digestibility gain. All-in cost including fermentation is USD 0.10-0.25/kg of BSFL meal. Starting a colony costs about USD 20-50 for pupae. The roadmap in the reference data puts a BSFL colony or algae culture at USD 200-500 of investment, three to six months to break even, and 10-15 percent off the feed bill on top of earlier savings.

Microalgae are a different proposition. Spirulina is 60-75 percent protein, the densest of any feed ingredient, and Chlorella 45-60 percent. But cell walls limit digestibility to 60-75 percent for spirulina and 70-85 percent for chlorella, and the price is USD 5-20/kg for spirulina and USD 3-15/kg for chlorella, five to twenty times soybean meal. At 10-20 percent inclusion, growth is 90-100 percent of control, so you are paying a great deal for parity. The honest uses are narrow: 3-5 percent in broodstock conditioning feed for immune support and pigmentation, costing USD 0.05-0.20/kg of feed, and 10-15 percent in fingerling diets on a disease-prone farm at USD 0.10-0.40/kg of feed, where the return comes from reduced mortality rather than growth. Full replacement of fish meal by algae is not economic.

Moringa leaf meal is the cheapest of the three and the most underused. It is 18-25 percent crude protein with a good amino acid balance, adequate in lysine, methionine and arginine per unit of protein and comparable to soybean meal on that basis. Digestibility is 70-75 percent because it is fibrous, so keep it under 10 percent of the feed's protein. A 10 percent inclusion contributes 1-2.5 percent crude protein to the diet. If you dry it yourself it costs nothing but labour; bought, USD 0.10-0.20/kg. Trials report fish fed 10 percent Moringa leaf meal growing similarly to a full soy control, and it brings polyphenols with immune value.

Worked example, Kenya. A farmer in Kiambu sets up a BSFL unit for USD 300 using shaded crates and market waste. She produces 15 kg of dried BSFL meal a month at a running cost of USD 0.20/kg, so USD 3. Fifteen kg of BSFL meal at 42 percent protein delivers 6.3 kg of protein. Buying the same protein as soybean meal at 46 percent would need 13.7 kg at USD 0.42/kg, or USD 5.75. The direct monthly saving is small at USD 2.75, but the meal also replaces about 4 kg of fish meal a month, worth roughly USD 9, and she has removed her exposure to fish meal price swings. Payback on the USD 300 lands inside the three to six month band the reference data expects.

Carbohydrate and Energy Sources

In this lesson
  • Set maximum carbohydrate and fibre levels for each species class you farm
  • Explain why cooking starch changes its value more than any other processing step
  • Choose between grain, bran and oil to add energy at least cost

Energy ingredients are the cheap half of a feed bag. Maize, rice bran and wheat cost USD 0.20-0.40/kg against USD 0.30-0.60/kg for the oilseed cakes, and getting the energy fraction right is what lets you use less protein without losing growth. Get it wrong and you either waste protein as fuel or you fill fish with material they cannot digest.

Start with how much carbohydrate each fish can take.

  1. Carnivorous fish, meaning catfish fry: maximum 10 percent crude fibre and under 15 percent soluble carbohydrate. Gelatinised starch digests at 50-70 percent, raw starch at only 20-40 percent. Carbohydrate here is mainly a binder.
  2. Omnivorous fish, meaning tilapia fingerlings: up to 10-15 percent crude fibre and 20-30 percent soluble carbohydrate. Gelatinised starch digests at 70-85 percent, raw at 40-60 percent.
  3. Herbivorous fish, meaning carp juveniles: up to 15-20 percent fibre and 30-40 percent soluble carbohydrate, with gelatinised starch at 85-95 percent digestibility.

The practical glucose limits are under 200 g/kg of feed for catfish fingerlings and under 300 g/kg for tilapia and carp.

Now the single most valuable processing fact in this whole course. Starch digestibility improves by 50-100 percent when it is gelatinised by heat and pressure. Raw maize starch is 30-40 percent digestible; steam-extruded maize is 75-85 percent. That is the same maize, the same price, and more than twice the useful energy. It is also why raw home-mixed feed runs 10-20 percent worse on FCR than processed feed. If you cannot extrude, you can still cook: boiling or steaming the starch fraction before mixing captures a large part of the gain, and it doubles as your binder, which Module 7 covers in detail.

The main energy ingredients you will actually see:

  • Maize, ground: USD 180-250 per tonne in 2026, roughly 8-9 percent protein. The default energy grain. Keep it under 20 percent of the feed because of fumonisin risk in poor harvest seasons, and remember tilapia tolerate under 5 ppb aflatoxin.
  • Rice bran: USD 120-180 per tonne, and USD 100-150 in sub-Saharan markets. It is 11-13 percent protein and 15-18 percent lipid, so it is really an energy ingredient with a protein bonus. Fibre is 8-10 percent, so use 5-10 percent inclusion for energy. Its lipid oxidises fast: use within two to three weeks, or buy heat-stabilised bran which lasts two to three months for an extra USD 0.02-0.05/kg.
  • Wheat: USD 200-280 per tonne. Better binding than maize, usually more expensive.
  • Maize bran and millet bran: USD 150-200 per tonne locally, cheaper than grain but more fibre.
  • Copra or coconut cake: 20-24 percent protein, 12-15 percent lipid, USD 0.20-0.35/kg, but 8-12 percent fibre and only 60-70 percent digestible, so keep it under 15 percent.
  • Brewer's yeast: 35-45 percent protein, 8-12 percent lipid, rich in B vitamins, USD 0.30-0.60/kg, usable at 10-20 percent. It spoils in two to three weeks wet, so it must be dried.
  • Oil: soybean oil at USD 800-1,200 per tonne. Each additional 1 percent of lipid in place of carbohydrate adds about 30-50 kcal/kg of digestible energy.

Worked example, Tanzania. A farmer at Iringa is formulating a 100 kg tilapia grow-out batch and needs to lift digestible energy by about 150 kcal/kg. Option A is 4 kg more oil, since 4 percentage points of lipid at 30-50 kcal each gives 120-200 kcal/kg; at USD 1.00/kg for oil that is USD 4. Option B is more maize, which is cheaper per kilogram but displaces protein ingredients and, if it is raw, delivers only 30-40 percent of its starch energy. He takes the oil, cooks his maize fraction anyway, and holds maize at 18 percent for mycotoxin safety.

The rule to carry forward: energy is cheap, but only if it is digestible. Uncooked starch is not a bargain.

Vitamin and Mineral Premix

In this lesson
  • Justify premix as the highest-return small purchase in a feed budget
  • Set phosphorus supplementation correctly once phytase is included
  • Avoid the trace mineral over-supplementation that poisons pond water

Premix is the smallest line in a feed budget and the one that decides whether the rest of the budget works. Mineral premix runs USD 0.03-0.05/kg of feed and vitamin premix another USD 0.03-0.05/kg. Add salt at 2-3 percent, which costs almost nothing and supplies chloride, and the whole mandatory supplementation tier is USD 0.10-0.15/kg of feed. On a 100 kg batch that is USD 10-15. Skipping it to save that money is the most expensive decision a small fish farmer makes.

What the vitamin premix must carry, per kilogram of feed: vitamin A 8,000-20,000 IU, vitamin D3 1,000-2,000 IU, vitamin E 100-200 mg, vitamin K 5-10 mg, vitamin C 100-200 mg, thiamine 10 mg, riboflavin 10 mg, niacin 50-100 mg, pantothenic acid 30-50 mg, B12 0.01 mg, folic acid 5-10 mg, biotin 0.5-1.0 mg and choline 1,000-2,000 mg. Two of these deserve attention. Vitamin D3 must be dietary because fish cannot make it from sunlight at all, unlike land animals. Vitamin C has no body store and degrades fastest of anything in the bag, losing 50-90 percent in two to four weeks unstabilised, so insist on ascorbyl phosphate or ascorbyl palmitate for USD 0.02-0.05/kg extra.

The minerals divide into the one that costs money and the ones that cause trouble.

Phosphorus is the expensive one. Requirements are 0.6-0.8 percent for tilapia, 0.6-0.7 for African catfish, 0.5-0.7 for channel catfish and carp. Calcium is 0.8-1.2 percent for tilapia, with an optimal Ca:P ratio of 1.5-2.0:1, wider than in mammals because fish also absorb calcium from the water. In hard water above 100 mg/L calcium, dietary calcium is often already adequate; phosphorus is not, because natural waters are phosphorus-poor.

The difficulty is availability. Fish meal has 1.2-1.5 percent total phosphorus at about 85 percent available. Soybean meal has 0.6-0.7 percent at only about 30 percent available. Rice bran looks rich at 1.5-2.0 percent but only 20-30 percent is available. Phytate locks the rest. A plant-based diet therefore usually needs 0.3-0.5 percent supplemental inorganic phosphorus as monobasic or dibasic phosphate.

Phytase changes that arithmetic. At 500-1,000 FTU/kg and a cost of USD 0.03-0.10/kg of feed, it breaks 40-50 percent of the phytic acid and recovers roughly 30-40 percent of the bound phosphorus, which cuts the supplemental phosphorus you must buy by 30-50 percent. The reference data rates its return at 1.5 to 3 to 1. It also releases zinc, iron and copper that phytate had tied up, so the trace mineral premix works harder. Fermentation does a similar job at similar cost and can be done with no purchased enzyme at all.

Now the trouble. Trace mineral requirements are small: iron 30-80 mg/kg, zinc 30-50, copper 3-10, manganese 10-13, selenium 0.1-0.5, iodine 0.8-2.4 for tilapia. Over-supplying them is not harmless, because what the fish does not absorb ends up in the water. Copper above 0.1 mg/L in pond water kills algae and zooplankton, which collapses the green water your pond productivity depends on, and immunosuppresses the fish. Zinc above 0.3 mg/L in accumulating systems impairs nitrification. Selenium is toxic in feed above 1 mg/kg, so the gap between the 0.1-0.5 requirement and toxicity is narrow. Iodine above 10 mg/kg is goitrogenic, and vitamin A above 200,000 IU/kg is toxic. The right approach is minimum supplementation plus phytase to unlock what the ingredients already contain, and weekly water monitoring in high-density systems.

Worked example, Ghana. A farmer near Ho mixes 500 kg of tilapia feed a month. Full Tier 1 supplementation at USD 0.12/kg costs USD 60. He adds phytase at USD 0.06/kg, USD 30, which lets him cut dicalcium phosphate by 0.4 percentage points of the mix, saving 2 kg of phosphate a month at roughly USD 1.20/kg, so USD 2.40, plus better growth and less phosphorus going into the pond. On phosphate cost alone phytase does not pay; on growth, phosphorus retention and reduced eutrophication it does, which is why the documented return is expressed as a range rather than a single figure. Buy premix as an unbroken sealed pack, store it cool and dark, and use it within its date.

Local Ingredient Integration

In this lesson
  • Build a feed from ingredients available within a day's travel of your farm
  • Apply quality tests to local ingredients before they go into a batch
  • Sequence the investments that cut feed cost, from free to USD 1,500

Everything in this module comes together in one question: what can you actually get, at what price, this month? A formula built on ingredients that are not in your market is a piece of paper.

The sub-Saharan picture from the reference data. Local artisanal fish meal runs USD 50-150 per tonne. Imported soybean meal is USD 350-450. Maize or millet bran is USD 150-200 locally. Rice bran is USD 100-150. Commercial pelleted feed costs USD 0.35-0.55/kg at the mill gate and USD 0.60-0.80/kg retail. Against that, a documented low-cost farm mix of 5-10 percent rice bran, 30-35 percent soya, 15-20 percent groundnut cake, 15-20 percent maize and 5-10 percent local fish by-product comes to USD 0.25-0.40/kg. That is roughly half of retail feed, and the gap is where a small farm's profit lives.

But the saving is only real if the ingredients are sound. Four checks, all free.

  1. Moisture. Every local ingredient should be dry enough to crack or crumble, not bend or clump. A handful squeezed should fall apart. Wet bran and wet cake mould in days in a warm store, and mouldy ingredients bring the mycotoxins that tilapia are least able to tolerate.
  2. Smell. Clean and slightly sweet or nutty is right. Musty means fungus. Sharp or paint-like means rancid fat, common in rice bran and fish meal.
  3. Sight. Reject anything with visible mould, insect damage, caking, or foreign matter. Sand and ground shell are common adulterants in cake and fish meal.
  4. Consistency of supply. Ask the supplier what happens in the three lean months. An ingredient you cannot get in October should not be more than a small share of a formula you feed all year.

The substitution discipline matters as much as the tests. If your soya supply fails, you cannot simply put in the same weight of maize bran, because you would drop from 46 percent protein to about 12 percent. Every substitution has to be recalculated on protein, which is what Module 7 teaches. Hold three rules: never substitute an energy ingredient for a protein ingredient without recalculating, never exceed the inclusion ceilings from Lessons 3 and 4, and never let a single new ingredient go into more than a trial batch before you have watched fish eat it for a week.

The investment sequence, from the reference roadmap, is the useful part of this lesson because it tells you what to do first.

  1. Buy premixes and hand-mix feed on the farm. Investment: none beyond containers. Payback: immediate. Feed cost reduction: 15-20 percent.
  2. Ferment plant ingredients locally. Investment: USD 50-100 for containers and starter. Payback: two to three months. Additional reduction: 5-10 percent.
  3. Establish a BSFL colony or algae culture. Investment: USD 200-500. Payback: three to six months. Additional reduction: 10-15 percent.
  4. Buy a small pellet mill of 2-3 kW. Investment: USD 500-1,500. Payback: six to twelve months. Additional reduction: 5-10 percent.
  5. Join or form a farmer cooperative for bulk ingredient buying. Investment: organisational, minimal cash. Payback: immediate. Reduction: 10-15 percent.

Notice that the two steps with no cash cost, hand-mixing and buying together, deliver 25-35 percent between them. Module 12 covers cooperative buying and price risk as a business subject.

Worked example, Malawi. A farmer near Zomba uses 3,000 kg of retail feed a year at USD 0.70/kg, so USD 2,100. He moves to hand-mixing with bought premix and reaches USD 0.52/kg, saving USD 540 in the first year with no equipment. He then adds fermentation for USD 80 of buckets and starter and reaches USD 0.48/kg, saving a further USD 120 a year, so the buckets pay back in eight months. He joins four neighbours to buy soya and premix by the tonne and reaches USD 0.42/kg. Total annual feed spend falls from USD 2,100 to USD 1,260, a saving of USD 840, against USD 80 of equipment. No new pond, no new fish, no extra risk.

Write your ingredient list on the store wall with this month's price beside each item, and update it every quarter. Feed cost is not decided by the formula. It is decided by what you paid for the things in it.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. Fish meal protein digestibility in fish is about:

Fish meal digests at 90-95 percent, against 75-85 percent for soybean meal.

2. The vitamin most at risk in an all-plant fish feed is:

B12 is bacterial in origin and found mainly in fish meal and animal proteins, so plant-only diets must supplement it.

3. Fish oil is clearly worth buying for:

Vegetable oils carry no preformed EPA or DHA, and those fatty acids drive spawning and larval quality.

4. A sharp, paint-like smell in fish meal indicates:

Rancid fish meal destroys vitamin E and depresses feed intake.

5. Twenty kg of imported fish meal at 65 percent protein and USD 2.20/kg versus 25 kg of local mukene meal at 55 percent protein and USD 0.55/kg is:

USD 44 against USD 13.75 for essentially the same protein contribution to the batch.

6. Soybean meal's weakest essential amino acid is:

Soybean meal has 1.6-1.8 percent lysine but only 0.4-0.5 percent methionine, the lowest of the common plant meals.

7. A 60 percent soy, 30 percent canola, 10 percent fish meal blend costs about:

The blend balances amino acids while cutting the expensive fish meal share, saving 20-25 percent.

8. Fermenting plant meal for 48-72 hours reduces phytate by:

Lactobacillus fermentation cuts phytate 30-50 percent and raises methionine bioavailability 10-15 percent.

9. Heat treatment of soybean meal improves its digestibility by roughly:

Heat inactivates trypsin inhibitors, and the gain also cuts supplemental amino acid needs by about 10 percent.

10. Pulses such as pea and chickpea carry an FCR penalty of about:

Indigestible oligosaccharides cause a 5-10 percent penalty, which fermentation reduces.

11. BSFL meal protein digestibility in fish is about:

It sits close to fish meal at 90-95 percent and clearly above soybean meal at 75-85 percent.

12. The amino acid that must be supplemented when BSFL meal carries most of the protein is:

BSFL meal is slightly low in methionine and needs supplementation above about 0.3 percent of the diet.

13. Moringa leaf meal should be limited to:

It is fibrous, at 70-75 percent digestibility, so beyond about 10 percent of feed protein it dilutes the diet.

14. Spirulina's main practical role in smallholder aquaculture is:

At USD 5-20/kg it can only justify small inclusions for immune support and pigmentation.

15. Black soldier fly larvae reach harvestable pre-pupal stage in:

Two to three weeks of culture, then three to five days of sun-drying to under 10 percent moisture.

16. Raw maize starch digestibility in fish is about:

Cooking or extrusion lifts it to 75-85 percent, which is why raw home mixes give poor FCR.

17. The maximum soluble carbohydrate for a tilapia fingerling diet is about:

Omnivores handle 20-30 percent; catfish fry are limited to under 15 percent.

18. Rice bran must be used within two to three weeks because:

Heat-stabilised bran lasts two to three months for an extra USD 0.02-0.05/kg.

19. Adding one percentage point of lipid in place of carbohydrate adds roughly:

This is the working figure for adjusting digestible energy with oil.

20. Maize is usually held under 20 percent of a fish feed because of:

Fumonisin in maize argues for limiting inclusion, and tilapia tolerate under 5 ppb aflatoxin B1.

21. Phosphorus availability from soybean meal is about:

Phytate binds most of it; fish meal phosphorus is about 85 percent available by comparison.

22. Phytase at 500-1,000 FTU/kg reduces supplemental phosphorus needs by:

It breaks 40-50 percent of phytic acid and recovers 30-40 percent of the bound phosphorus.

23. Copper becomes dangerous in pond water above:

Above 0.1 mg/L it kills algae and zooplankton, collapsing pond productivity.

24. The optimal calcium to phosphorus ratio in fish feed is:

Wider than in mammals because fish also take calcium from the water.

25. The total mandatory vitamin and mineral supplementation cost is about:

Ten to fifteen US cents per kilogram of feed, the highest-return small spend in the budget.

26. A documented low-cost sub-Saharan farm mix costs about:

Roughly half the retail price of pelleted feed, using rice bran, soya, groundnut cake, maize and local fish by-product.

27. The first step in the cost-reduction roadmap, with no cash investment, is:

It delivers 15-20 percent with immediate payback and no equipment.

28. Substituting maize bran for soybean meal at the same weight would:

Every substitution must be recalculated on protein, which is what feed formulation arithmetic is for.

29. A pellet mill of 2-3 kW costs and pays back in:

It adds 5-10 percent of feed cost reduction on top of the earlier, cheaper steps.

30. The correct test for whether a local ingredient is dry enough is:

Ingredients that hold a shape are too wet and will mould in a warm store.

Module 6 capstone

Build a priced ingredient inventory for your own farm and use it to cost one tonne of feed. Visit or phone at least six suppliers within reach: a fish landing site or artisanal fish meal maker, an oil mill for soya or cottonseed or groundnut cake, a rice or maize mill for bran, a brewery or distillery, an agrovet or feed shop for premix and phytase, and one BSFL or Moringa producer if there is one. For each ingredient record the price per kilogram delivered to your farm, the minimum quantity you must buy, how often it is available through the year, and its moisture and smell on the day you inspect it. Add the crude protein, lysine, methionine and digestibility figures from this module to each line. Then take the largest quantity of feed you would mix in one batch, price that batch three ways: entirely from bought commercial pellet, from a home mix using your cheapest local ingredients, and from a home mix that also includes premix, phytase and supplemental lysine and methionine. Write down the cost per kilogram of all three and note which ingredients drive the difference. Keep the inventory as a living page and update the prices every quarter, because ingredient prices, not feed formulas, are what move your cost of production.
