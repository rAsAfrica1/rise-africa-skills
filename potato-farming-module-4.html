1. Determining Optimal Planting Dates

In Africa, potato planting dates depend on rainfall, temperature, and altitude. Potatoes grow best when soil temperature is between 15C and 20C. If you plant when it's too hot (above 30C), tubers may not form. If too cold (below 10C), growth stops. In highland areas like Kenya's Nyandarua or Ethiopia's Holeta, the main planting season is when the long rains begin"usually March to April. A second season can be planted in September to October with short rains. In lowland areas with irrigation, you can plant year-round, but avoid the hottest months. For rain-fed farming, plant 2-3 weeks after the first consistent rains, when soil is moist but not waterlogged. Waterlogged soil causes seed pieces to rot. A simple test: squeeze a handful of soil"if it holds together but breaks apart when you poke it, it's ready. If it drips water, wait. Also consider frost: potatoes are sensitive to frost. In areas like the South African Highveld, plant after the last frost. In West Africa's Jos Plateau, plant at the start of the rainy season (April-May). To be sure, ask local extension officers or experienced farmers for the best planting window in your area. Keep a farm diary: note planting date, rainfall, and yield. After 2-3 seasons, you'll know your optimal dates. Planting at the right time can increase yields by 30-50%. Don't guess"use a simple rain gauge and soil thermometer. These cost little and save your crop.

2. Planting Depth and Spacing Guidelines

Plant seed potatoes at a depth of 8-10 cm from the top of the seed piece to the soil surface. If you plant too shallow (less than 5 cm), tubers may turn green (sunburn) and become bitter. If too deep (more than 15 cm), sprouts take longer to emerge and may rot. In sandy soils, plant deeper (10-12 cm) because soil dries out faster. In clay soils, plant shallower (6-8 cm) to avoid waterlogging. Spacing: for large seed pieces (whole small tubers or cut pieces with 2-3 eyes), space plants 25-30 cm apart within the row. For smaller seed pieces, 20-25 cm. Between rows, keep 70-90 cm apart. This allows air movement, reduces disease, and makes hilling easier. If you plant too close, you get many small tubers; too far, you waste land. A common mistake is planting in a grid. Instead, use ridges or furrows. In Africa, many farmers use the "two-eye" method: cut large tubers into pieces with at least two eyes, then plant. But cutting increases rot risk"if you cut, let pieces dry for 1-2 days in shade before planting. For whole small tubers (30-40 g), plant directly. A hectare needs about 2,000-2,500 kg of seed potatoes if spaced 30x75 cm. That's roughly 20-25 bags of 100 kg each. Always plant with the cut side down or the eyes facing up. Cover with soil and press gently. Mark rows with sticks or string to keep spacing even. After planting, the soil should be level or slightly mounded. Don't leave seed pieces exposed"birds and rodents will eat them.

3. Mechanical vs. Manual Planting Methods

Manual planting is the most common method in Africa. It uses hand hoes, machetes, or dibbers (pointed sticks). Steps: clear the field, dig a furrow 10 cm deep with a hoe, place seed pieces 25-30 cm apart, cover with soil. A person can plant about 0.2-0.3 hectares per day. Manual planting is cheap, but slow and labor-intensive. It's good for small plots (less than 1 hectare) and steep slopes where machines can't go. Mechanical planting uses tractor-drawn planters. These machines open a furrow, drop seed pieces at correct spacing, and cover them in one pass. A two-row planter can plant 1-2 hectares per day. But they are expensive to buy or hire (US$50-100 per hectare). They also require well-prepared, stone-free soil. In Africa, mechanical planting is used on large commercial farms in South Africa, Kenya, and Egypt. For smallholders, a middle option is a hand-pushed planter (e.g., a jab planter). It's faster than a hoe and costs less than a tractor. If you use a mechanical planter, calibrate it first: check spacing and depth on a small area. Also, ensure seed pieces are uniform in size"otherwise, the machine may clog. For manual planting, organize labor into teams: one person digs, one drops, one covers. This speeds up work. Whichever method, plant in straight rows to allow later hilling and spraying. After planting, clean the tools to prevent disease spread. If you hire a tractor, book early"everyone plants at the same time.

4. Hilling and Ridging After Planting

Hilling (also called ridging or earthing up) means pulling soil around the base of potato plants. Do it when plants are 15-20 cm tall"about 3-4 weeks after planting. Hilling covers the stems, encouraging more tubers to form and preventing them from turning green. It also improves drainage and weed control. Use a hoe or a tractor-drawn ridger. For manual hilling, work along each row: pull soil from between rows to form a ridge 15-20 cm high around the plants. Be careful not to bury the leaves completely"leave the top 10 cm of the plant exposed. In Africa, many farmers hill twice: first when plants are 15 cm, second 2-3 weeks later. This is especially important in areas with heavy rain, as it prevents waterlogging. If you plant in flat beds, hilling is a must. If you already planted on ridges, you may only need to repair them. Hilling also helps control weeds"bury small weeds. But don't hill when soil is too wet; it compacts the soil and spreads disease. Wait until soil crumbles. For large farms, a tractor with a disc ridger can hill 1 hectare in 1-2 hours. For small farms, a team of 5 people can hill 0.5 hectare in a day. After hilling, the field should look like a series of parallel ridges. This also makes harvesting easier"you can dig along the ridges. If you see potato tubers pushing out of the soil, hill immediately. Exposed tubers turn green and are unsellable. Hilling is one of the simplest ways to increase yield by 10-20%.

5. Use of Pre-Emergence Herbicides

Pre-emergence herbicides kill weeds before they sprout. Apply them right after planting, before potato plants emerge. They work by forming a chemical barrier in the top 2-5 cm of soil. Common products for potatoes include metribuzin (e.g., Sencor), pendimethalin (e.g., Prowl), and linuron. In Africa, metribuzin is widely available. Use the correct dose: for metribuzin 70% WP, apply 0.5-1.0 kg per hectare in 200-400 liters of water. For pendimethalin 33% EC, use 3-4 liters per hectare. Always read the label"overdosing harms potatoes. Apply with a knapsack sprayer or tractor sprayer. Calibrate your sprayer first: spray water on a 10 m x 10 m area, measure how much water you used, then adjust. Spray evenly over the soil surface. Do not spray if rain is expected within 6 hours"rain washes the herbicide away. Also, do not spray on wet soil or when it's windy. After spraying, avoid disturbing the soil"hilling later will break the barrier. That's why many farmers apply pre-emergence herbicides after the final hilling, but before potatoes emerge. If weeds already emerged, pre-emergence herbicides won't work. You must hand-weed first. Pre-emergence herbicides save labor: one spray can replace 2-3 hand-weedings. But they can damage potatoes if used incorrectly. Never use a herbicide not labeled for potatoes. Also, rotate herbicides to prevent resistant weeds. For organic farmers, use mulch (e.g., dry grass) instead. Always wear protective clothing: gloves, mask, and long sleeves. Store herbicides away from food and children.

6. Irrigation Setup for Newly Planted Potatoes

Newly planted potatoes need consistent moisture, but not waterlogging. For the first 2 weeks after planting, keep soil moist but not soaked. If you have irrigation, apply 20-25 mm of water per week. In sandy soil, water more often (every 3-4 days). In clay soil, less often (every 7 days). The best irrigation methods for potatoes are drip and sprinkler. Drip irrigation saves water and reduces disease, but it's expensive (US$1,000-2,000 per hectare). Sprinklers are cheaper and common in Africa. For small plots, use watering cans or buckets. A simple setup: a 200-liter drum with a tap and a hose. For larger farms, use a pump and pipes. If you use furrow irrigation, make sure water flows slowly to avoid washing away soil. After planting, irrigate immediately if soil is dry. But if you applied pre-emergence herbicide, wait 24 hours before irrigating to let the chemical settle. Check soil moisture by digging 10 cm deep"if it's dry, irrigate. Too much water causes seed pieces to rot. Too little causes slow sprouting. Once plants emerge, reduce watering slightly to encourage deep roots. In Africa, many farmers rely on rain. If rain is irregular, supplement with irrigation. A rain gauge helps: if you get less than 25 mm per week, irrigate. For drip irrigation, place emitters 20 cm from plant stems. For sprinklers, run them early morning or evening to reduce evaporation. Always monitor for puddles"if water stands for more than 2 hours, reduce the flow. Proper irrigation can increase yields by 20-30%.

<h2>Practical Reference: Planting Logistics, Inputs and Field Records for African Potato Enterprises</h2>

<p>This practical reference lesson consolidates the field-level decisions that determine whether a potato enterprise makes money or loses it during the first six weeks after planting. In African potato systems from the highlands of Ethiopia, Kenya, Uganda and Rwanda to the cooler zones of Nigeria, Cameroon, South Africa and Zimbabwe the difference between a profitable crop and a failed one is rarely the variety alone. It is the accuracy of planting dates, seed rate calculations, input costing, irrigation scheduling and record keeping. Module 4 has already covered the technical mechanics of planting. This lesson converts those mechanics into business decisions: what to buy, when to plant, how much to budget, and what to write down so that next season is better than this one.</p>

<h3 style="color:#c62828; margin-top:0;"> Safety Warning Chemical Handling and Food Safety</h3>
<p style="color:#333;">Pre-emergence herbicides (e.g. metribuzin, linuron, pendimethalin) and fungicides used in potato production are hazardous. Always read the label and follow the manufacturer's instructions. Wear protective clothing gloves, boots, long sleeves, eye protection and a mask when mixing and applying. Never eat, drink or smoke during application. Store chemicals in a locked, ventilated store away from food, feed and children. Observe the pre-harvest interval (PHI) strictly; potatoes harvested before the stated PHI may contain illegal residues and are unsafe for sale or consumption. Dispose of empty containers through approved collection schemes never reuse them for water or food. In case of poisoning, contact a qualified medical professional immediately and provide the product label. These precautions align with WHO recommendations on pesticide safety and FAO's Code of Conduct on Pesticide Management.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Confirm seed quality:</strong> Use certified or quality-declared seed, sprouted and free from disease. Budget 2.02.5 t/ha for rainfed highland systems.</li>
<li><strong>Lock in planting date:</strong> Plant when soil moisture is adequate and the 10-day forecast shows no prolonged dry spell. In East African highlands, target the onset of the long rains (MarchApril) or short rains (SeptemberOctober) depending on region.</li>
<li><strong>Calibrate spacing and depth:</strong> Standard 75 30 cm, planted 1015 cm deep. Adjust for soil type and seed size.</li>
<li><strong>Apply basal fertiliser at planting:</strong> Place DAP or equivalent in the furrow, separated from the seed by a thin layer of soil to avoid burn.</li>
<li><strong>Apply pre-emergence herbicide within 48 hours of planting:</strong> Ensure adequate soil moisture for activation. Never apply after emergence without checking crop tolerance.</li>
<li><strong>Set up irrigation before emergence:</strong> Keep the ridge moist but not waterlogged. Over-irrigation causes seed rot and leaching of nutrients.</li>
<li><strong>Record every input and cost:</strong> Track seed, fertiliser, labour, chemicals, fuel and transport in a simple notebook or mobile app. Calculate gross margin per hectare at harvest.</li>
<li><strong>Review and adjust:</strong> Compare actual yields and costs against budget at the end of the season. Use the data to negotiate better input prices and choose more profitable markets next season.</li>
</ul>
