Rabbit Farming

A complete 6-month program — from empty hutch to litters sold. Breeding at 31-day speed, forage feeding that halves costs, and the health discipline that keeps the fastest livestock alive.

⏱️ 6 Months (6–10 hrs/week)📊 Beginner → Business-ready🌍 Hybrid: Online + Offline🎓 Certificate on completion

Course Overview

Why 6 months? Rabbits are livestock at fast-forward: a doe bred in Month 2 kindles after just 31 days, and her first litter reaches slaughter weight (2.5–3 kg) about 3 months later — so this program takes you from empty hutch to your first litters SOLD, with the second generation already growing. No farm animal turns feed into meat and cash faster on less land.

Structure: 6 monthly modules → lessons → practical assignments → a 10-question quiz each month → final business project.

How to study (hybrid): Online — modules on your phone, sessions in the Study Diary, questions to the rAs AI assistant. Offline — save this page (Print → Save as PDF); the practicals happen at the hutches.

⚠️ Read first: the three rules that protect your investment

  • Heat kills rabbits before disease does. Above 30°C rabbits stop breeding and start dying — shade, ventilation and cool water are life support, not luxury. Never place hutches in afternoon sun.
  • Sudden feed changes kill rabbits. Their gut flora is fragile: introduce any new feed over 7–10 days. A rabbit that stops eating for 24 hours is an emergency, not a wait-and-see.
  • Never spend before the Iron-Clad Price Check: 3 quotes for breeding stock, wire mesh and pellets — startup savings of 30–50% live in those phone calls.
MONTH 1 — Rabbit Science, Breeds & Planning ▾

Lesson 1.1 — Why rabbits are the micro-livestock champion

  • One doe can produce 30–40 kits a year (5–7 litters of 6–10) — over 60 kg of meat from an animal you can hold in one hand.
  • FCR of 3–4 on balanced feeding, largely from forage humans can't eat; a backyard fits a 10-doe unit.
  • Rabbit meat is white, lean (lower fat and cholesterol than chicken), and increasingly stocked by health-conscious urban butcheries and restaurants — a growing niche with thin competition.
  • Quiet, odour-light (managed well) and legal in towns where pigs and cattle are not.

Lesson 1.2 — The key numbers

Lesson 1.3 — Breeds that pay

  • New Zealand White: the commercial meat standard — fast growth, big litters, calm, white pelt.
  • Californian: close second; excellent carcass; often crossed with NZW.
  • Chinchilla & Flemish Giant: Flemish adds frame in crosses (pure Flemish eat too much for their meat); Chinchilla hardy dual-purpose.
  • Local/crossbred rabbits: heat-hardier and cheaper — ideal foundation does, upgraded by an NZW/Californian buck.
  • Starter formula: 5–10 crossbred/local does + 1 pure NZW or Californian buck from an unrelated source.

Lesson 1.4 — Judging and sexing stock

  • Buy young (3–5 months), bright-eyed, dry-nosed animals with clean ears (no crusts = no ear mange), glossy coats, firm droppings, and no wet chins (teeth problems).
  • Sexing: press gently either side of the vent — a slit (doe) vs a protruding round tip (buck); practise at the seller's, not after paying.
  • Check hocks (foot undersides): raw sore hocks reveal bad wire floors and future lameness.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 1): (1) Visit a rabbitry and a butchery/restaurant that sells rabbit; record breeds, prices per rabbit live and per kg dressed. (2) Practise sexing and health-checking 5 rabbits. (3) Choose your breed plan and write the 10-doe target layout on paper.

MONTH 2 — Housing & Setting Up the Rabbitry ▾

Lesson 2.1 — Hutch design that works in heat

  • Space: 0.6–0.9 m² per adult (e.g. 80×90 cm, 45–50 cm high); a doe with litter needs the larger size plus her nest box.
  • Raise hutches 80–100 cm on legs (with rat guards) — droppings fall away, snakes and rats are frustrated, and your back survives.
  • Floors: 1.5×1.5 cm to 1.9×1.9 cm wire mesh lets droppings through but supports feet; add a solid resting board per cage to prevent sore hocks.
  • Site under permanent shade (trees, roof) with open-mesh sides for cross-breeze; solid roof against rain and sun; NEVER in afternoon-sun walls.

Lesson 2.2 — The nest box

  • Simple wooden box ~30×40×25 cm with a 15 cm lip, filled with straw/grass — placed in the doe's hutch on day 27–28 of pregnancy.
  • The doe pulls her own fur to line it; kits are born blind and naked and MUST have this insulation — no nest box = dead litters.

Lesson 2.3 — Feeders, drinkers, and the barn routine

  • Feed hoppers or heavy clay bowls (rabbits flip light bowls); hay racks keep forage off the floor.
  • Water: nipple bottles or heavy bowls, cleaned daily — a lactating doe drinks up to 1 L/day; no water = no eating = crisis.
  • Hygiene rhythm: droppings cleared from under hutches weekly (straight to the compost/garden — rabbit manure is the only manure gentle enough to use FRESH), cages brushed weekly, full disinfection between litters.

Lesson 2.4 — Alternative systems

  • Rabbit colonies (floor pens): natural and cheap but worm/coccidia pressure and uncontrolled breeding make them harder — hutches first, colony experiments later.
  • Underground burrow systems: traditional, cool in heat, but disease control and record-keeping suffer; not for commercial goals.
✅ Iron-Clad Price Check: wire mesh, timber and breeding stock — 3 quotes each. Mesh is your biggest capital item; offcut and second-hand mesh can halve it.

MONTH 3 — Feeding: Pellets, Forage & the Fragile Gut ▾

Lesson 3.1 — What rabbits must get

  • Fibre first: rabbits are fibre engines — long-stem roughage (hay/dry grass) must ALWAYS be available; low fibre = fatal gut shutdown and fur-chewing.
  • Protein: growers and lactating does need 16–18% CP; dry adults 12–14%.
  • Commercial rabbit pellets deliver the balance: growers ~100–120 g/day plus hay; lactating does ad lib (up to 250–350 g/day feeding 8 kits).

Lesson 3.2 — The forage economy (cutting the feed bill 40–60%)

  • Proven free feeds: sweet-potato vines, blackjack, wandering jew, wild sunflower, banana leaves, maize leaf strips, mulberry, pigeon pea, hay of any clean grass.
  • Wilt fresh greens 12–24 hours before feeding — wet lush greens cause deadly bloat/diarrhoea, especially in young stock.
  • NEVER feed: potato leaves/green potatoes, tomato leaves, avocado, raw beans, mouldy anything, lawn clippings from sprayed lawns.
  • Practical mix that works: morning pellets/homemade mash + midday hay always in the rack + evening wilted forage.

Lesson 3.3 — The 7-day change rule and gut health

  • Any new feed enters at 10–20% and rises over 7–10 days — the gut flora must adapt or the rabbit scours.
  • Night droppings (cecotropes — soft clusters rabbits re-eat): completely normal and essential nutrition; only daytime wet diarrhoea is disease.
  • Danger signs to act on TODAY: not eating, hunched sitting, grinding teeth (pain), no droppings, watery scour — hay + water + warmth and a vet call.

Lesson 3.4 — Water and heat management

  • Heat protocol above 30°C: frozen water bottles laid in hutches, wet sacks on the roof, feed at dawn/dusk, breeding paused (bucks go temporarily sterile in heat waves).
  • Watch ears: rabbits dump heat through them — ears hot and rabbit stretched flat panting = emergency cooling now.

(2) Run the 7-day introduction of one new forage on two rabbits and log droppings. (3) Cost a month of feeding for 6 adults: pellets-only vs pellets+forage — the saving is your wage.

MONTH 4 — Breeding, Kindling & Raising Kits ▾

Lesson 4.1 — Mating done right

  • ALWAYS take the doe to the buck's cage — does defend their territory and injure visiting bucks.
  • Ready doe: restless, chin-rubbing, vulva swollen and reddish-purple. Successful mating ends with the buck falling off sideways (often with a grunt) — comic but conclusive.
  • Two matings 8–12 hours apart lift conception; record doe, buck and date — kindling is 31 days later.
  • Pregnancy check day 12–14 by gentle palpation (grape-sized lumps in the lower belly) — or simply re-present to the buck: a pregnant doe usually refuses loudly.

Lesson 4.2 — Kindling and the nest weeks

  • Nest box in at day 27–28; kindling usually at night, 10–30 minutes, no help needed — DO NOT disturb during birth.
  • Day-after check (rub hands in the nest hay first to mask scent): count kits, remove any dead, confirm round milk-full bellies. Does nurse only 1–2× daily for 5 minutes — an absent doe is normal, shrivelled kits are not.
  • Cold or stray kits are rewarmed in your hands/box and returned — does accept them back; fostering between does that kindled within 2–3 days also works.
  • Eyes open day 10–12; kits leave the nest ~day 18; remove the box at 3 weeks.

Lesson 4.3 — Weaning to slaughter weight

  • Wean at 4–6 weeks (move the DOE if possible, less stress); group weaners by size in grower cages, 5–6 per cage, splitting sexes by 10–12 weeks (they can breed shockingly young).
  • Weaning is the danger window for coccidiosis and enteritis: dry cages, gradual feed, hay always — losses here decide profit.
  • Growth target: weaner 600–800 g → 2.5–3 kg by 12–16 weeks on pellets + forage.
  • Rebreed the doe 10–14 days after kindling (moderate system) for the 5–7 litters/year rhythm — condition-check her first; a thin doe gets a rest cycle.

Lesson 4.4 — The breeding calendar and records

  • One card per doe: matings, kindled/born alive, weaned, weights — after 3 litters her card tells you keep or cull (target: 7+ weaned per litter).
  • Replace the buck line every 2 years or run two unrelated bucks — inbreeding shrinks litters fast in rabbits.
  • Staggered matings (2 does per week) give continuous weekly output instead of gluts.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 4): (1) Mate two does (doe→buck, double service) and log the dates; palpate at day 12–14. (2) Run the full kindling protocol with nest-box timing and day-after check. (3) Open doe cards for every female.

MONTH 5 — Health & Daily Management ▾

Lesson 5.1 — The disease map

ProblemSignsPrevention / action
CoccidiosisDiarrhoea, pot belly, deaths around weaningDry wire floors, clean water, weaning care; treat with sulpha/amprolium per label
Ear mange (canker)Brown crusts inside ears, head-shakingIvermectin-type per label + oil-soften crusts; treat all in-contact rabbits
Skin mangeScaly nose/paws/edges, fur lossSame family treatment; disinfect cages
Snuffles (pasteurella)Persistent sneezing, white nasal discharge, matted forepawsVentilation, no ammonia; chronic cases culled — it spreads and rarely cures
Sore hocksRaw ulcers on hind feetRest boards, correct mesh, heavier rabbits watched
Bloat / GI stasisSwollen belly, no droppings, not eatingEmergency: hay+water only, gentle massage, vet; caused by feed errors
MastitisHot, hard, bluish teats in nursing doesClean nest boxes; vet antibiotics fast; strip milk gently

Lesson 5.2 — The daily and weekly rhythm

  • Daily 10 minutes: water, feed, hay, every rabbit LOOKED at eating (the non-eater is your emergency), droppings normal, heat check.
  • Weekly: hocks and ears inspected, nails as needed, cages brushed, manure cleared, records updated.
  • Monthly: weigh a grower sample; deep-clean one block of cages on rotation.

Lesson 5.3 — Handling without injury

  • NEVER lift by the ears. Grip the scruff with one hand, support the full hindquarters with the other — a kicking rabbit can break its own spine.
  • Carry against your body, head tucked under your arm; calm rabbits handle easier every time they're handled calmly.

Lesson 5.4 — Biosecurity for the rabbitry

  • Quarantine every incoming rabbit 2 weeks; wash hands between sick and healthy animals; visitors admire from outside the shed.
  • Rats steal kits and feed and carry disease — guards on legs, sealed feed bins.
  • Where RHD (rabbit haemorrhagic disease) occurs, ask your vet about vaccination — sudden multiple adult deaths warrant immediate vet contact.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 5): (1) Handle and inspect every rabbit correctly: ears, hocks, teeth, coat — logged. (2) Set up the sealed feed bin and rat guards. (3) Write the emergency card: GI-stasis steps and your vet's number, pinned in the shed.

MONTH 6 — Slaughter, Marketing & Your Business Plan ▾

Lesson 6.1 — From hutch to market

  • Sell live (easiest — buyers slaughter), or dressed where licensed: humane slaughter, clean skinning, chilled same day; a 2.8 kg rabbit dresses to ~1.4–1.5 kg.
  • Channels: health-conscious households (WhatsApp/estate groups), restaurants and lodges (the premium contract market), butcheries, other farmers (breeding stock — your best young trios of 1 buck + 2 does sell for 2–3× meat price).
  • The pitch that sells rabbit: leaner than chicken, white meat, farm-fresh — sample cooked tastings convert more buyers than any advert.

Lesson 6.2 — By-products: the hidden margins

  • Manure: sellable fresh by the bag to gardeners — a 10-doe unit yields a steady side income.
  • Pelts: dried/tanned skins feed craft markets (see rAs Leather Tanning); urine is bottled by some farms as organic pesticide/fertiliser input.

Lesson 6.3 — The worked economics (5-doe starter, steady state)

  • The levers: forage share of the diet (feed cost), kits weaned per doe (nest and weaning care), and channel (restaurant/breeding-stock prices vs farm-gate). Scale to 10–20 does doubles-to-quadruples this on the same daily labour.

Lesson 6.4 — Scaling and the production line

  • Steady output needs staggered breeding: 2 does mated every week beats all does at once.
  • Growth ladder: 5 does → 10 → 20 with a grower shed; then contracts, then a small licensed slaughter/dressing setup.
  • Replacement discipline: every quarter, the doe cards nominate the culls and the best young does step up.

Lesson 6.5 — Final project: your bankable business plan

  • From six months of doe cards and money records: unit description, breed plan, housing built/costed, feeding strategy (pellet/forage split with quotes), health calendar, staggered breeding calendar, buyer list with real prices, risk plan (heat, feed changes, disease), 3-year growth ladder.
  • Submit with photos and records for your rAs certificate — the same file works for youth funds, banks and partners.
