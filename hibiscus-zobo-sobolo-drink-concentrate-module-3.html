1. Hot water extraction: temperature and time parameters

Hot water extraction is the fastest way to pull colour, flavour, and natural acids from dried hibiscus calyces. In Africa, most zobo makers use a simple pot on a stove or fire. The key is to control temperature and time so you get a strong concentrate without bitterness. Start with clean, dried hibiscus petalsâabout 100 grams (roughly 2 large handfuls) for every 1 litre of water. Bring the water to a rolling boil (100Â°C), then turn off the heat and add the petals. Do not boil the petals themselves; boiling them for more than 2 minutes extracts harsh tannins and makes the drink bitter. Instead, let them steep in the hot water for 20 to 30 minutes. Stir once after 5 minutes. For a stronger concentrate, you can use a ratio of 150 grams per litre and steep for 40 minutes, but never exceed 45 minutes or the flavour turns woody. If you are using a gas cooker, keep the lid on to trap steam and heat. If you are using a charcoal stove, move the pot off the direct flame after the initial boil. After steeping, the liquid should be deep red and slightly viscous. Strain immediately to stop extraction. This method yields about 800 ml of concentrate from 1 litre of water because some liquid is absorbed by the petals. You can reuse the steeped petals once more with half the water for a second, weaker batchâuseful for mixing with the first. Always use a stainless steel, enamel, or clay pot; aluminium reacts with the acids and gives a metallic taste. For food safety, cool the concentrate quickly by placing the pot in a basin of cold water, then refrigerate within 2 hours.

2. Cold brew extraction for delicate flavor profiles

Cold brew extraction is slower but produces a smoother, less acidic hibiscus concentrate with a brighter, fruitier taste. This method is ideal for makers who want a premium product or who live in hot climates where boiling water is expensive or inconvenient. Use the same dried hibiscus petalsâ100 grams per 1 litre of clean water. The water should be at room temperature (20â25Â°C) or slightly cool (15â20Â°C). Place the petals in a clean glass jar or food-grade plastic bucket with a lid. Pour the water over them, stir well, then cover. Let it sit in a cool, dark place for 12 to 24 hours. In hot weather (above 30Â°C), do not exceed 12 hours or the liquid may ferment. In cooler weather (below 20Â°C), you can go up to 24 hours. Stir every 4 to 6 hours to improve extraction. After the steeping time, the liquid will be a deep ruby red but less cloudy than hot extraction. Strain through a fine mesh or cloth. Cold brew typically yields a concentrate that is about 20â30% less strong in colour than hot extraction, but it has a cleaner, more delicate flavour. To increase strength, use 150 grams of petals per litre and steep for 24 hours. You can also combine cold brew with a small amount of hot extraction (10% of total volume) to boost colour without bitterness. Store the cold brew concentrate in sterilised bottles in the refrigerator. It keeps for up to 2 weeks, while hot extract keeps for about 1 week. For a shelf-stable product, you must pasteurise or add preservatives. Cold brew is excellent for mixing with sparkling water, ginger, or pineapple juice because its flavour is less dominant.

3. Ratio of hibiscus to water for concentrate strength

The ratio of hibiscus to water determines how strong your concentrate is, and that affects cost, taste, and how much you dilute it later. A standard starting point for a drink concentrate is 100 grams of dried hibiscus petals per 1 litre of water. This gives a medium-strength concentrate that you dilute 1 part concentrate to 3 parts water or juice for serving. If you want a stronger concentrateâfor example, to sell as a syrup or to reduce transport weightâuse 200 grams per 1 litre. That is a 1:5 ratio by weight (200g to 1000g water). The maximum practical ratio is 250 grams per 1 litre; beyond that, the petals absorb too much water and you lose yield. For a lighter, more economical concentrate, use 50 grams per 1 litre, but then you must dilute only 1 part concentrate to 1 part water. Always weigh your petals with a simple kitchen scale (available in most African markets for a few dollars). If you donât have a scale, use a consistent measure: 1 packed cup of dried petals weighs about 40â50 grams, so 2 cups per litre gives roughly 100 grams. The type of hibiscus matters: deep red, thick calyces from Nigeria or Sudan are stronger than pale, thin ones from other regions. Adjust by 10â20% based on colour after steeping. For a commercial concentrate, aim for a Brix (sugar content) of 15â20% if you add sugar, but the hibiscus ratio is independent. Test your ratio by diluting a small sample: 1 tablespoon of concentrate in 100 ml of water should taste pleasantly tart, not bitter or watery. Record every batch: weight of petals, volume of water, steeping time, and final yield. This helps you repeat a winning formula. Remember that hot extraction yields about 80% of the water volume, while cold brew yields about 90%, so adjust your starting water accordingly.

4. Effect of pH and water hardness on extraction

The pH (acidity) of your water and its hardness (mineral content) directly change how much colour and flavour you get from hibiscus. Hibiscus contains natural acids, so it prefers slightly acidic water. If your water is alkaline (pH above 7.5), the red colour turns dull brown or purple, and extraction slows down. In many parts of Africa, tap water and well water are alkaline due to limestone or chalk. You can test with cheap pH strips (available at pharmacies or agricultural shops). If your water is alkaline, add a small amount of lemon juice or vinegarâabout 1 teaspoon per litreâto bring the pH down to 6.0â6.5. That small acid boost can increase colour extraction by 30% and make the flavour sharper. Conversely, if your water is already acidic (pH below 5.5), like rainwater in some regions, extraction is fast but the taste may be too sour; add a pinch of baking soda to raise pH to 6.0. Water hardness is about dissolved calcium and magnesium. Hard water (common in boreholes and wells) binds to hibiscus pigments and reduces yield. You can soften hard water by boiling it first and letting it cool, then decanting off the sediment, or by adding a pinch of citric acid (1 gram per 5 litres). Soft water (rainwater, distilled) extracts more colour but can taste flat; add a tiny amount of mineral salt (a pinch of Epsom salt per 10 litres) to improve mouthfeel. For consistent results, always use the same water source. If you must switch, test a small batch first. In coastal areas, salty water is a problemânever use it, as salt ruins the flavour. For a simple field test: put 1 teaspoon of dried hibiscus in 100 ml of your water, heat to 80Â°C for 10 minutes, and compare the colour to a sample made with bottled water. Adjust with lemon or baking soda until the colour matches.

5. Filtration methods: mesh, cloth, and press

After steeping, you must separate the liquid from the spent petals. The method you choose affects clarity, yield, and speed. The simplest tool is a fine mesh strainer (1â2 mm holes), common in every African kitchen. Pour the steeped mixture through the mesh into a clean pot. This removes large pieces but lets fine sediment through, giving a cloudy concentrate. For clearer concentrate, line the mesh with a clean cotton cloth (muslin or a new, washed handkerchief). The cloth traps fine particles. You can also use a nylon stockingâcheap and effective. For best results, use a two-stage filtration: first mesh, then cloth. A cloth alone can clog quickly; squeeze it gently to recover more liquid. Do not wring too hard or you extract bitter tannins from the petals. If you have a fruit press or a simple potato ricer, you can press the spent petals to get an extra 10â15% yield. After pressing, strain the pressed liquid through cloth again. For a commercial operation, a wine press or a hydraulic press is ideal, but for home-scale, a clean brick wrapped in cloth and pressed down works. Always work with clean hands and sanitised tools. After filtration, let the concentrate sit for 1â2 hours; sediment will settle at the bottom. Carefully pour off the clear liquid, leaving the sediment behind. This step, called decanting, gives a much clearer product without expensive filters. You can also use a coffee filter for small batches, but it is slow. For large volumes, a bucket with a spigot and a cloth liner works well. Never use aluminium or iron pots for filtration because they react with acids. Use plastic, glass, stainless steel, or enamel. After filtration, sterilise your storage bottles by boiling them for 10 minutes or washing with bleach solution and rinsing well. Store the concentrate in the refrigerator.

6. Practical: preparing a simple hibiscus infusion

Now letâs put everything together to make a simple hibiscus infusion that you can drink or use as a base for concentrate. You will need: 100 grams dried hibiscus petals (about 2 packed cups), 1 litre clean water, a pot, a stove or fire, a spoon, a fine mesh strainer, a clean cloth, a 1-litre bottle, and optionally 1 teaspoon lemon juice or 1 tablespoon sugar. First, check your water. If it is hard or alkaline, add the lemon juice to the water before heating. Bring the water to a rolling boil in a stainless steel or enamel pot. Once boiling, turn off the heat. Add the hibiscus petals and stir gently. Cover the pot with a lid and let it steep for 25 minutes. Do not boil after adding petals. After 25 minutes, the liquid should be deep red. Taste a small spoonfulâit should be tart and fruity, not bitter. If it is bitter, you steeped too long or used too much petal. If it is weak, steep for another 5 minutes. Now strain through a mesh strainer into a second pot. Then strain again through a cloth-lined strainer to remove fine sediment. Squeeze the cloth gently to get extra liquid. You should have about 800â850 ml of infusion. While still warm, you can stir in sugar (50â100 grams) if you want a sweet concentrate. For a drink, dilute 1 part infusion with 3 parts water or soda water. For a concentrate, reduce the infusion by simmering gently for 10 minutesâthis evaporates water and makes it stronger. But do not boil hard. Let the concentrate cool to room temperature, then pour into a clean bottle. Label with date. Store in the refrigerator for up to 1 week. For a longer shelf life, pasteurise by heating to 80Â°C for 10 minutes, then bottle hot. This simple infusion is the foundation for zobo, sobolo, and other hibiscus drinks. Practice this recipe three times, changing one thing each time (time, ratio, or filtration) to learn how each affects the final product.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Preparing a Simple Hibiscus Infusion

This practical reference is the working heart of the Basic Extraction Techniques module. It converts theory into a repeatable bench process you can run in a home kitchen, a shared community kitchen, or a small commercial processing room. The objective is simple: produce a clean, strong hibiscus (zobo/sobolo/bissap) infusion that becomes the base for a drink concentrate. In African agribusiness, the difference between a product that sells and one that spoils on the shelf is usually not the recipe â it is consistency, hygiene, and control of temperature, time, and ratio.

Hibiscus concentrate is a high-value product because it is light, easy to transport, and can be diluted at the point of sale. A single 500 ml bottle of concentrate can serve 10â20 drinks depending on the dilution ratio. That makes it attractive for street vendors, supermarkets, hotels, and export buyers. But concentrate also concentrates mistakes. If your extraction is too weak, you pay to transport water. If it is too bitter or too sour, customers reject it. If it is contaminated, you lose the batch and possibly your reputation.

This lesson gives you a practical method, two data tables for decision-making, a process diagram, safety guidance, and a quick reference checklist. It is written for African conditions: variable water quality, hot climates, and limited refrigeration. Where possible, use locally available equipment and food-grade materials.

Step-by-Step Practical Method

Step 1 â Weigh the dried calyces. Use a digital scale accurate to 1 g. For a 5-litre batch of concentrate, weigh 400â500 g of clean, dry hibiscus calyces. Remove any stones, stems, or mouldy pieces. Mouldy calyces will ruin the taste and may produce harmful mycotoxins. Store calyces in a dry, dark container away from moisture.

Step 2 â Heat the water. Use clean, potable water. If you are unsure of water quality, boil and cool it first, or use filtered/bottled water. Heat to 80â90Â°C. Do not use rolling boiling water for the main steep unless you are deliberately making a bitter extract for blending. Boiling water extracts more colour but also more tannins and pectin, which can make the concentrate cloudy and harsh.

Step 3 â Steep. Add the calyces to the hot water. Stir gently. Cover the pot to retain heat and volatile aromatics. Steep for 15â30 minutes. For a lighter, more floral profile, use 15 minutes at 80Â°C. For a deeper, more robust concentrate, use 25â30 minutes at 90Â°C. Stir once halfway through. Do not over-stir, as this introduces oxygen and can dull the colour.

Step 4 â Filter. Strain through a clean, food-grade cloth (muslin or cheesecloth) or a fine stainless steel mesh. For commercial batches, a small manual press or a basket press improves yield. Squeeze the spent calyces gently to recover retained liquid, but avoid excessive pressure that pushes fine particles through. For a clearer concentrate, filter twice: first through coarse mesh, then through fine cloth.

Step 5 â Cool and bottle. Cool the infusion rapidly. In hot climates, place the pot in a basin of cool water or use a clean stainless steel chiller. Once below 40Â°C, transfer to clean, food-grade bottles or jerrycans. Fill to the top to minimise headspace oxygen. Label with batch number, date, and ratio. Store in a cool, dark place. For extended shelf life, refrigerate or pasteurise according to your local food safety regulations.

  • Do not use boiling water in plastic containers. Hot liquid can leach chemicals and deform containers. Use stainless steel, enamel, or food-grade glass.
  • Never bottle hot concentrate. Cool below 40Â°C before bottling to prevent burns and condensation that promotes mould.

  • If producing for sale, follow your national food safety regulations (e.g., NAFDAC in Nigeria, FDA Ghana, KEBS in Kenya). Unregulated production can lead to fines, recalls, or closure.
