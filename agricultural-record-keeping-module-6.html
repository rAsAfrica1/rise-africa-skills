1. Organic Certification Record Requirements

Organic certification opens premium markets in Africa, from EU exporters to local supermarkets. To get certified, you must prove you follow organic rules for at least 3 years before your first harvest. That means keeping detailed records from day one. Start with a field map showing every plot, and note the last date any prohibited substance was used. For each field, record all inputs: compost, manure, botanical sprays. Write down the source, amount, and date. For example, '10 kg chicken manure from Kuku Farm, applied to Plot A on 15 March 2025.' You also need a seed log: variety, supplier, and whether it is organic or treated. If treated, it cannot be used. Keep a harvest log: date, field, quantity, and how it was stored. Your records must be traceable from seed to sale. Use a simple notebook or a spreadsheet on your phone. Many certifiers like Ecocert or CERES require you to keep records for at least 5 years. They will check your records against your actual practices during an annual inspection. If you sell to a cooperative, they may need copies. Also keep receipts for any purchased organic inputs. A common mistake is mixing organic and conventional produce in storage. Record separate lot numbers and storage locations. Train your workers to write in the log immediately after each activity. A waterproof notebook and a pen tied to a post in the field works well. At the end of each season, review your records with your certifier's checklist. Missing records can delay certification by a year. So start simple, be consistent, and treat your record book as your proof of organic integrity.

2. Good Agricultural Practices (GAP) Records

Good Agricultural Practices (GAP) certification is required by many buyers, especially for export fruits and vegetables. GAP covers food safety, worker health, and environmental care. To pass a GAP audit, you need records for every step. First, a farm map with all fields, storage, and packing areas. Then, a water test record: test your irrigation water for E. coli at least once a year. Keep the lab report. Next, a fertilizer log: date, field, type, rate, and method. For example, 'NPK 17-17-17, 50 kg per hectare, broadcast on Plot 3, 10 June 2025.' You also need a pesticide log (see Lesson 4). Worker training records: who was trained, when, and on what topic (e.g., safe pesticide handling). A hygiene log for toilets and handwashing stations: check daily and record. Harvest records: date, field, harvest crew, and quantity. Also keep a cleaning log for harvest tools and crates. For post-harvest, record storage temperature and humidity if you have a cold room. Traceability is key: each harvest lot must link back to the field and inputs. Use a simple coding system: farm initials, field number, date. For example, 'KFM-F3-20250610' for Kilimo Farm Field 3 on 10 June 2025. Keep all records for at least 2 years. During an audit, the inspector will pick a lot number and ask you to show all records for that lot. If you cannot, you fail. So store records in one place, like a ring binder with plastic sleeves. Use a checklist from your buyer or from GlobalG.A.P. to self-audit before the real inspection. A well-kept record system is your best tool for passing GAP.

3. Traceability and Lot Numbers

Traceability means you can follow a product from the field to the customer, and back. It is required by law in many countries and by buyers. The key tool is a lot number. A lot number is a unique code for a batch of produce. For example, 'TOM-20250615-01' means tomatoes harvested on 15 June 2025, first lot of the day. You must assign a lot number at harvest. Write it on the harvest crate, on the delivery note, and in your record book. Then, every time you move or process that lot, record the lot number. If you mix lots, you lose traceability. So keep lots separate. For each lot, record: field name, harvest date, harvest crew, inputs used (fertilizer, pesticide) with dates, and quantity. Also record storage location and any treatments (e.g., washing, waxing). When you sell, record the buyer, date, quantity, and lot number. If a customer complains, you can trace back to the field and see if there was a problem. For example, if a buyer finds pesticide residue, you check your pesticide log for that lot. If you used a banned chemical, you know which field and when. That helps you fix the problem and prevent it. Use a simple numbering system: farm code (2 letters), field number (1-2 digits), date (YYMMDD), and sequence (01, 02). Write it with a permanent marker on crates. For bulk bins, use a tag. Keep a master log of all lot numbers. For small farms, a notebook works. For larger farms, use a spreadsheet or a free app like Farmbrite. Train your workers to never mix lots. During an audit, the inspector will pick a lot number and ask for all records. If you can show them in 5 minutes, you pass. If you search for hours, you fail. So make lot numbers a habit.

4. Pesticide Application Logs

Pesticide logs are the most important record for food safety. Buyers and auditors will check them first. You must record every pesticide application, even if it is organic. For each application, write: date, time, field, crop, pest, product name, active ingredient, rate (e.g., 500 ml per hectare), amount used, water volume, applicator name, and weather conditions (wind, temperature). Also record the pre-harvest interval (PHI) the number of days between spraying and harvest. For example, if the label says 7 days, and you spray on 1 June, you cannot harvest before 8 June. Write that in the log. After spraying, record the re-entry interval (REI) when workers can go back in the field. Use a dedicated notebook or a form. Keep it with the pesticides in a locked store. Never use a banned pesticide. Check with your local agricultural office for a list of approved products. In Kenya, for example, the Pest Control Products Board (PCPB) lists approved pesticides. In Nigeria, use NAQS. For organic certification, only use products allowed by your certifier. Record the name of the person who mixed and applied. Also record the calibration of your sprayer: date, nozzle type, and output. After application, record the disposal of empty containers: triple-rinsed and punctured, then taken to a collection point. Keep all receipts for pesticide purchases. During an audit, the inspector will compare your log with your harvest records. If you sprayed and harvested too soon, you fail. So always double-check the PHI. A simple rule: write it down immediately after spraying, before you forget. Use a waterproof pen. If you make a mistake, cross it out, write the correct entry, and initial. Never erase. A clean, honest log builds trust and keeps your market access.

5. Animal Health and Treatment Records

If you keep livestock, animal health records are essential for certification and for selling to processors or exporters. You need to record every treatment, vaccination, and health event. Start with individual animal identification: ear tags, brands, or names. For each animal, keep a record card or a page in a logbook. Write: species, breed, sex, date of birth or purchase, and source. For treatments, record: date, animal ID, diagnosis or reason, product name, active ingredient, dose, route (injection, oral, topical), and withdrawal period. The withdrawal period is the time after treatment before you can sell milk, meat, or eggs. For example, if you treat a cow with oxytetracycline, the milk withdrawal might be 3 days and meat withdrawal 28 days. Write those dates clearly. Also record the person who administered the treatment. For vaccinations, record: vaccine name, batch number, expiry date, date given, and next due date. Keep a herd health calendar. For example, in East Africa, you might vaccinate cattle against East Coast Fever (ECF) at 3 months, and against Foot and Mouth Disease (FMD) every 6 months. Record every visit from a vet or animal health assistant: date, name, reason, and advice given. If an animal dies, record the date, cause (if known), and disposal method. For milk, keep a daily milk record: animal ID, litres, and any abnormalities (e.g., mastitis). If you sell milk, you need to show that treated cows are not milked during the withdrawal period. Use a red tag or separate the animal. During an audit, the inspector will pick an animal and ask for its full health record. If you cannot show it, you fail. So keep records in a sturdy folder, one page per animal. Use a simple table. Update it every time you treat. This protects your animals, your customers, and your certification.

6. Preparing for Audits and Inspections

An audit is a check that your records match your practices. It can be for organic, GAP, or a buyer's own standard. To prepare, start 3 months before. Get the checklist from your certifier or buyer. Go through each item and check if you have the record. For example, if the checklist asks for 'water test report', do you have one from the last 12 months? If not, get it done. Organize your records in one place. Use a ring binder with sections: farm map, input logs, harvest logs, pesticide logs, training records, and receipts. Label each section. For digital records, keep a folder on your phone or computer, and back it up. Make sure your records are up to date. If you missed an entry, write it in now, but mark it as 'late entry' and explain why. Honesty is better than gaps. Do a self-audit: pick a lot number and try to trace it from field to sale. Can you find all records in 10 minutes? If not, improve your filing. Train your workers on what to say. The inspector may ask a worker: 'What do you do after spraying?' The worker should say: 'I record it in the log and respect the PHI.' Practice with them. On audit day, be polite and calm. Have your records ready. The inspector will walk your farm. They will look at storage, toilets, and worker safety. They may take samples. If they find a non-compliance, they will give you time to fix it. Usually, you get 30 days. Write a corrective action plan and send it. After the audit, keep a copy of the report. If you pass, you get a certificate. Display it. If you fail, learn from it and try again. Many certifiers offer a pre-audit for a fee. That is a good investment. Remember, an audit is not a test; it is a way to prove you are doing the right thing. Good records make it easy.

<h2>Practical Reference: Compliance and Certification Records</h2>

<p>Compliance records are not paperwork for its own sake. They are the commercial infrastructure that lets an African farm or agribusiness sell into premium markets organic, GlobalG.A.P., fair trade, supermarket supply chains, and export channels. Buyers, certifiers, and regulators do not inspect your farm on trust; they inspect your records. If it is not written down, it did not happen. This practical reference consolidates the record-keeping requirements across the six lessons in this module into one working document you can adapt to your operation, whether you manage 2 hectares of export vegetables in Kenya or 200 head of cattle in Botswana.</p>

<h3>Why compliance records are a business asset, not a cost</h3>

<p>Across Africa, the premium paid for certified produce is real: organic export vegetables, GlobalG.A.P.-certified fruit, and traceable livestock products consistently attract higher farmgate prices and more reliable buyers than uncertified equivalents. But certification is conditional on demonstrable records. A missing pesticide log or an untraceable lot number can fail an audit, cost you a contract, and lock you out of a market for a full season. The records below are therefore treated as commercial documents the evidence base for your price premium.</p>

<p>The six lessons in this module map to distinct record sets. Each certification standard (organic, GlobalG.A.P., fair trade) draws on the same underlying evidence but weights it differently. Organic certification focuses on input provenance and prohibited substance avoidance; GAP focuses on food safety and worker hygiene; traceability underpins both. Animal health records are central to livestock certification and export health certificates.</p>

<h3>Pesticide application logs: the audit's first target</h3>

<p>Pesticide logs are the single most scrutinised record in almost every audit, because they directly connect to food safety, worker safety, and organic compliance. A log entry must be made on the day of application, not reconstructed later. Auditors cross-check spray dates against harvest dates to verify pre-harvest intervals (PHIs), and against the approved input list to confirm no prohibited substances were used. For organic operations, any synthetic pesticide application can trigger decertification and a three-year conversion period a commercially devastating outcome.</p>

<strong style="color:#c62828;"> SAFETY WARNING Pesticide Handling and Record Integrity</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Never sign or backdate a pesticide log for an application you did not personally perform or supervise. Falsified records are a criminal offence in most African jurisdictions and result in immediate certification withdrawal, contract termination, and potential prosecution. Store all pesticides in a locked, ventilated store away from food, feed, and water sources; keep the store key log and inventory in the same record file as application logs. Operators must wear the PPE specified on the label, and re-entry intervals must be observed. If a spill or accidental exposure occurs, record it in the same log and report to your certifier and relevant authority.</p>
</div>

<h3>Traceability and lot numbering: the commercial backbone</h3>

<p>Traceability is what allows a buyer to identify the exact field and harvest date of any produce they receive. For African exporters, this is non-negotiable: a single food safety incident in a European or Middle Eastern market can trigger a recall that requires tracing product back to source within hours. A simple lot numbering system for example, <code>FARM-FIELD-DATE-SEQ</code> (e.g., <code>KIL-B3-20250314-01</code>) is sufficient if applied consistently. Every harvest crate, dispatch note, and invoice should carry the lot number, and the lot number should be recorded in the field log at harvest.</p>

<h3>Animal health and treatment records</h3>

<p>For livestock operations, animal health records serve both certification and export health certificate purposes. Each animal or group must be identifiable (ear tag, brand, or RFID), and every treatment must be recorded with the drug name, dose, route, date, and withdrawal period. Auditors and veterinary authorities check that no animal was slaughtered or milked within the withdrawal period. In many African markets, livestock export to the Middle East and North Africa requires documented veterinary treatment histories and official health certificates without records, the animal cannot be sold into these channels.</p>

<h3>Preparing for audits and inspections</h3>

<p>Audit preparation is a year-round discipline, not a last-minute scramble. The most effective approach is a monthly internal self-inspection using the same checklist the external auditor will use. Keep a dedicated audit file containing: current certificate, previous audit report, corrective action evidence, input purchase receipts, calibration records, training certificates, and a map of fields and storage facilities. Two weeks before the audit, walk the farm with the checklist, verify that all logs are current and legible, and confirm that workers can explain the record system in their own words. Auditors frequently interview field staff, not just management.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul>
<li><strong>Daily:</strong> Complete pesticide, fertiliser, and animal treatment logs on the day of activity never retrospectively.</li>
<li><strong>Weekly:</strong> Verify that all harvest lots are assigned a unique lot number and that dispatch notes match field records.</li>
<li><strong>Monthly:</strong> Conduct an internal self-inspection against your certification standard's checklist and file the signed report.</li>
<li><strong>Quarterly:</strong> Review input purchase receipts against the approved input list; remove any prohibited products from store.</li>
<li><strong>Annually:</strong> Reconcile all records against the certification retention period and archive expired files securely.</li>
<li><strong>Always:</strong> Ensure every record is dated, signed, legible, and stored where it can be retrieved within 30 minutes during an audit.</li>
<li><strong>Before audit:</strong> Brief all field staff on the record system and confirm they can locate and explain the logs they maintain.</li>
<li><strong>After audit:</strong> File the audit report and begin corrective actions immediately; document evidence of completion with dates and photos.</li>
</ul>
