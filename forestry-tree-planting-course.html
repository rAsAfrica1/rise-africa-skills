AGRICULTURE · FORESTRY

Forestry & Tree Planting

A complete 5-month program — raising timber, fruit and medicinal tree seedlings, planting and establishing woodlots that survive their first dry season, managing growth for timber, poles, fuelwood and fodder, and building a nursery or woodlot business with a genuine multi-year income plan.

Course Overview

Why forestry? Wood — for poles, timber, fuelwood and charcoal — is one of the few products whose demand only grows as populations grow, while natural forest supply keeps shrinking. A well-managed woodlot planted today is a savings account that compounds: a tree that costs almost nothing to plant becomes a pole worth real money in 5–8 years and continues producing (through coppicing) for decades after that.

Structure: 5 monthly modules → lessons → practical assignments → a 10-question quiz each module → final business project.

How to study (hybrid): Online — modules on your phone, sessions in the Study Diary, questions to the rAs AI assistant. Offline — save this page (Print → Save as PDF); the practicals happen in the nursery and on the planting site.

⚠️ Read first: the three rules that protect your investment

  • The first dry season kills more trees than anything else. A newly planted tree's roots have not yet reached deep, reliable moisture — survival in year one depends almost entirely on planting timing, mulching and follow-up watering, not on species choice.
  • Match species to purpose and to your land, not just to what's available. A fast-growing exotic may outcompete local vegetation and water resources; a slow native hardwood may be the better long-term timber investment. Know what you are planting and why before you plant a thousand of them.
  • Get land tenure and planting rights clear before you invest years of work. Tree crops are a long-term claim on land — disputes over who owns a mature woodlot are common where planting happened on unclear or communal land without agreement.
MONTH 1 — Species Selection & Purpose ▾

Lesson 1.1 — Why plant trees commercially

  • Markets exist for poles (construction, fencing), sawn timber, fuelwood and charcoal, fodder (for livestock), fruit, and increasingly for carbon/agroforestry payments in some regions.
  • Trees also protect the farm itself: windbreaks reduce crop damage, boundary trees mark and protect land, and nitrogen-fixing species improve soil in surrounding fields — a woodlot is rarely "just" a woodlot.
  • The main challenge is patience: most timber species take 5–15 years to full maturity, so tree planting works best alongside shorter-term income, not as a first-year cash crop.

Lesson 1.3 — Native vs exotic species

  • Fast-growing exotics (eucalyptus, some pines) often out-yield native species dramatically but can deplete local water tables and outcompete native vegetation if planted at scale near water sources or farmland.
  • Native species are typically slower but better adapted to local pests, rainfall patterns and soils, and often carry cultural, fodder or medicinal value that exotics don't.
  • A mixed approach — exotics for fast cash-crop poles on a defined block, natives for boundaries, windbreaks and long-term timber — balances income speed against ecological risk.

Lesson 1.4 — Land, tenure and planning

  • Confirm land tenure and, where the land is communal or leased, get planting rights agreed and recorded in writing before investing labour — a mature woodlot is a valuable, contestable asset.
  • Map the planting site: soil type, slope, distance to water, proximity to farmland or homes (fast-growing roots can damage nearby structures or compete with crops if planted too close).
  • Set a realistic scale for year one — better to establish and properly maintain a smaller area than to overplant and lose most seedlings to neglect in the first dry season.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 1): (1) Identify your primary purpose (poles, timber, fodder, fruit, windbreak) and pick 2–3 candidate species with reasoning. (2) Map your planting site including water source and distances to structures/farmland. (3) Confirm and document land tenure/planting rights in writing.

MONTH 2 — The Nursery ▾

Lesson 2.1 — Seed collection and treatment

  • Collect seed from healthy, mature, well-formed mother trees — form and vigour are partly heritable, so poor mother trees produce poor offspring on average.
  • Many tree seeds have hard coats requiring pre-treatment: nicking, soaking in hot water, or scarification (light sanding) to allow water uptake and germination — check the specific requirement for your species.
  • Store seed cool, dry and pest-free if not sown immediately; most tree seed loses viability faster than crop seed, so use fresh seed where possible.

Lesson 2.2 — Sowing and potting

  • Sow pre-treated seed into a seedbed or directly into polybags/root trainers filled with a light mix of topsoil, sand and compost — most tree species need good drainage to avoid damping-off (fungal seedling collapse).
  • Root trainers (deep, narrow containers) encourage straight taproot development and reduce transplant shock compared with shallow bags — worth the extra cost for timber species where root form affects tree stability.
  • Thin overcrowded seedbeds early — competition for light produces weak, leggy seedlings that transplant poorly.

Lesson 2.3 — Nursery care and hardening off

  • Water consistently and provide partial shade for the first weeks, gradually increasing sun exposure as seedlings establish — sudden full sun on tender seedlings causes scorch and stunting.
  • "Harden off" seedlings in the 2–3 weeks before planting by reducing watering frequency and shade — this toughens them for the shock of transplanting into open, unshaded field conditions.
  • Grade seedlings by size before planting and plant like with like — mixing vigorous and weak seedlings in the same block means the weak ones get shaded out and die.

Lesson 2.4 — Vegetative propagation alternatives

  • Some species (Gliricidia, some willows, certain fodder shrubs) establish readily from cuttings or truncheons (large stem cuttings planted directly) — faster than seed and guarantees the parent's characteristics.
  • For species where seedling supply is limited or seed is hard to source, wildling collection (carefully lifting naturally regenerated seedlings from the wild) is a traditional low-cost option, done sustainably and with permission where required.
  • Grafting/budding (see the dedicated Grafting, Budding & Orchards course) applies to fruit and nut trees within a forestry programme just as it does to standalone orchards.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 2): (1) Collect and correctly pre-treat seed from a confirmed healthy mother tree of your chosen species. (2) Establish 200 seedlings in polybags or root trainers and record germination rate. (3) Begin hardening off your most advanced batch 2–3 weeks before your planned planting date.

MONTH 3 — Planting & Establishment ▾

Lesson 3.1 — Timing and spacing

  • Plant at the start of the rainy season so roots have maximum moisture to establish before any dry spell — planting into dry soil "to get ahead" almost always backfires.
  • Spacing depends on final purpose: dense spacing (1×1 to 2×2 m) for fast pole/fuelwood woodlots harvested young and often thinned; wider spacing (4×4 m or more) for long-term timber or fruit trees that need full canopy development.
  • Line planting on contour reduces erosion on sloping land and makes later maintenance and harvesting far more manageable than scattered random planting.

Lesson 3.2 — Planting technique

  • Dig a hole roughly twice the width of the root ball/container, loosen the surrounding soil, and plant at the same depth the seedling was growing in its bag — check for and straighten any circling roots first, as circling roots can eventually strangle the tree.
  • Firm the soil around the roots to remove air pockets, water in immediately after planting even if rain is expected, and mulch around (not touching) the stem to conserve moisture and suppress weeds.
  • Add a small amount of compost or basal fertiliser at planting for species that respond well to it, but avoid excess fresh manure directly against roots, which can burn them.

Lesson 3.3 — Protecting young trees

  • Livestock and wild browsing animals are one of the biggest causes of woodlot failure — fence, use thorn-branch barriers, or use individual tree guards until trees are tall enough to survive browsing.
  • Weed competition in the first 1–2 years slows growth dramatically — maintain a weed-free ring around each young tree through hand weeding or mulch.
  • Fire is a major risk in dry-season grassland areas — maintain firebreaks around young plantings and coordinate with neighbours on controlled burning practices where relevant.

Lesson 3.4 — First-season monitoring and replacement

  • Check survival at 6 and 12 weeks after planting; expect some losses (5–20% depending on species and conditions) and replant gaps promptly with reserve seedlings from the same nursery batch.
  • Record survival rate by species and planting date — this data is what tells you which species and techniques actually work on your specific site, more reliably than general advice.
  • Water newly planted trees through any dry spell in the first season if at all possible — the investment in a few weeks of carrying water is small compared with losing an established planting.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 3): (1) Plant your first block at correct spacing and depth, on contour if sloping. (2) Set up protection (fencing, guards or barriers) before planting is complete, not after damage occurs. (3) Check survival at 6 weeks and replant any gaps.

MONTH 4 — Growth Management, Thinning & Coppicing ▾

Lesson 4.1 — Pruning for form and value

  • Formative pruning removes competing leaders and low side branches early to encourage a single straight stem — straight, knot-free timber commands a far higher price than crooked or heavily branched wood.
  • Prune in the dry season where possible to reduce disease entry through fresh cuts, and never remove more than about a third of the live canopy at once.
  • For pole production specifically, side-pruning lower branches as the tree grows produces the long, clean pole length that buyers want.

Lesson 4.2 — Thinning dense plantings

  • Densely planted woodlots need thinning as trees grow — removing weaker, crowded or malformed trees so the remaining trees get enough light, water and nutrients to reach full size.
  • Thinned material is not waste — it typically becomes the first harvest of small poles, stakes or fuelwood, generating income years before the main crop matures.
  • A typical schedule thins by roughly a third to a half of stems at each thinning, done in 2–3 rounds over the woodlot's life rather than one drastic cut.

Lesson 4.3 — Coppicing and pollarding

  • Coppicing means cutting a tree near ground level and letting it regrow multiple new stems from the stump — many species (eucalyptus, Gliricidia, willow) regrow vigorously and can be harvested this way repeatedly for decades without replanting.
  • Pollarding cuts the tree higher up (above browsing height) and is useful where livestock would otherwise eat new coppice regrowth at ground level.
  • Coppice cycles are typically 3–7 years depending on species and target pole size — this turns a woodlot into a renewable, repeatable harvest rather than a one-time crop.

Lesson 4.4 — Pest, disease and fire management

  • Monitor for common pests (borers, termites on stressed trees, defoliating caterpillars) and diseases (root rot in poorly drained sites, canker) — healthy, unstressed trees resist most pest pressure far better than drought- or waterlogging-stressed ones.
  • Termites are a particular risk to young trees and dead wood in many African regions — remove dead/dying trees promptly rather than leaving termite-attractant material standing.
  • Maintain firebreaks and coordinate fire management with neighbours every dry season — a single uncontrolled fire can destroy years of growth in an afternoon.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 4): (1) Formatively prune your Month 3 planting, removing competing leaders. (2) Identify which blocks need thinning and mark trees for removal. (3) If your species coppices well, test-coppice a small section and record regrowth after 8 weeks.

MODULE 5 — Harvest, Markets & the Woodlot Business ▾

Lesson 5.1 — Harvesting for value

  • Match harvest timing and method to product: poles are cut at target diameter regardless of full maturity; sawn timber is generally left to reach larger diameter and full maturity for maximum board yield.
  • Fell trees carefully with correct directional cutting to avoid damage to standing trees and to make extraction easier — poor felling technique wastes usable length and risks injury.
  • Season/dry poles and timber properly before sale where the market demands it — green (unseasoned) wood is prone to warping, cracking and insect attack, and typically sells for less.

Lesson 5.2 — Markets and value addition

  • Sell standing (buyer fells and removes), as cut poles/logs at the roadside, or processed (sawn, treated, or as charcoal) — each step up the value chain earns more per tree but requires more equipment, skill and time.
  • Treating poles (creosote or other approved preservatives) against rot and termites can significantly extend service life and price for construction poles — worth learning if pole sales are a major income stream.
  • Fuelwood and charcoal markets are usually local and price-sensitive; construction pole and timber markets often pay significantly more per tree but require larger scale or partnership with a buyer/miller.

Lesson 5.3 — Legal and sustainability considerations

  • Check national regulations on tree felling permits, especially for native species or larger-scale harvesting — many countries require a permit even on privately planted woodlots.
  • Practice sustainable harvest scheduling (staggered blocks or coppice rotation) rather than clear-felling everything at once — this keeps income flowing every year instead of one large payout followed by years of nothing.
  • Where a carbon credit, agroforestry payment or reforestation incentive scheme is accessible in your area, document your planting (species, area, dates, survival) from day one — retrofitting records later is far harder.

Lesson 5.4 — Final project: your bankable business plan

  • Assemble: species and purpose plan, nursery/seedling source, planting site map and tenure documentation, spacing and thinning/coppice schedule, protection and fire management plan, target markets and expected price per product type, and a staggered multi-year harvest and income plan (not a single-year payout).
  • Submit with survival-rate records and photos for your rAs certificate — woodlot plans that show staggered, sustainable harvest scheduling are exactly what land-use financiers and reforestation programmes look for.

📖 Glossary

  • Coppicing — cutting a tree near ground level to regrow multiple new stems from the stump.
  • Pollarding — cutting a tree higher up, above browsing height, to regrow new stems out of livestock reach.
  • Thinning — removing weaker/crowded trees so remaining trees have room to reach full size.
  • Truncheon — a large stem cutting planted directly to establish a new tree.
  • Wildling — a naturally regenerated seedling collected from the wild for planting.
  • Root trainer — a deep, narrow nursery container that encourages straight taproot growth.
  • Hardening off — toughening nursery seedlings before transplant by reducing water and shade.
  • Firebreak — a cleared strip maintained to stop the spread of fire into a planted area.
