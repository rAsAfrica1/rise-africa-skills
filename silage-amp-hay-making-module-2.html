1. Selecting a Well-Drained Site for Hay and Silage

Choosing the right site is the first step to making good hay and silage. You need a place that stays dry. If water collects, your hay will rot and your silage will smell bad. Look for land that slopes gentlyâabout 2 to 5 percent. That means it drops 2 to 5 meters over 100 meters. A gentle slope lets rainwater run off, but not so fast that it washes away soil.

Avoid low spots where water stands after rain. Also avoid areas near rivers that flood. If you must use flat land, you can dig shallow ditches around the area to carry water away. Test the drainage by digging a hole 30 cm deep and filling it with water. If the water drains within 2 hours, the soil is well-drained. If it takes more than 4 hours, find another spot.

Check the soil type. Sandy or loamy soil drains better than clay. If your soil is heavy clay, you can add organic matter like compost or manure to improve drainage. Also, make sure the site gets plenty of sunâat least 6 hours a day. Sun helps dry hay and keeps silage from getting too wet.

Think about access. You need to bring in tools, tractors, and animals. A site near a road or path saves time and energy. But keep it away from busy roads where dust and exhaust can contaminate your fodder. Finally, consider wind. A site with some breeze helps dry hay faster, but strong winds can blow away loose hay. A natural windbreak like trees on one side can help.

In Africa, many farmers use the same site year after year. If you do, rotate crops or let the land rest to avoid depleting nutrients. Also, keep the site free from trash, rocks, and old stumps. A clean, level, well-drained site will give you better quality hay and silage with less spoilage.

2. Clearing and Leveling the Land with Hand Tools

Before you plant fodder or build storage, you must clear and level the land. This work can be done with simple hand tools: a machete (panga), hoe, rake, shovel, and a wheelbarrow. Start by cutting tall grass, bushes, and small trees. Use a machete or slasher for thick vegetation. For bigger trees, use an axe or saw, but leave the roots if you canâthey help hold soil. If you must remove stumps, dig around them and cut roots with a hoe or axe.

After cutting, remove all debris: rocks, sticks, and roots. Pile them in a corner or use them for fencing or compost. Next, level the ground. Use a hoe to break up large clods of soil. Then use a rake to smooth the surface. For bigger areas, you can drag a heavy log or a wooden plank behind you to flatten bumps. If the land slopes, create small terraces or contour ridges to slow water runoff. You can mark contours using a simple A-frame level made from three wooden poles tied in an A shape with a string and stone as a plumb bob.

Leveling is important because uneven ground makes it hard to cut hay evenly and can cause water to pool. It also makes it easier to build a shed or pit. If you are preparing a seedbed for fodder crops like maize or sorghum, till the soil to a depth of 15â20 cm. Use a hoe or fork. Break up lumps and remove weeds. A smooth, fine seedbed helps seeds germinate and roots grow.

Work in the dry season when the soil is not too wet or too dry. Wet soil sticks to tools and compacts; dry soil is hard to dig. Wear gloves and boots for safety. Take breaks and drink water. After clearing, let the land rest for a week or two before planting or building. This allows weeds to sprout, which you can then remove. A well-cleared and leveled site saves you time and money later.

3. Building a Simple Storage Shed Using Local Materials

A storage shed protects your hay and silage from rain, sun, and pests. You can build one using local materials: wooden poles, bamboo, thatch grass, and mud. Choose a site that is well-drained and close to your fodder plot. The shed should be at least 3 meters wide and 4 meters long to store enough feed for a few cows. Make it tall enough to stand insideâabout 2.5 meters high at the center.

Start by setting corner posts. Use strong poles like eucalyptus or acacia, about 10 cm in diameter. Dig holes 50 cm deep and set the posts. Pack soil and stones around them. Add middle posts along the sides for extra support. Connect the posts with horizontal poles using nails or rope. For the walls, you can use bamboo splits, woven sticks, or mud plaster. Mud walls keep the shed cool but need a roof to protect them from rain. If you use mud, mix it with straw to prevent cracking.

The roof is the most important part. Use a thatch of dry grass or reeds, or corrugated iron sheets if you can afford them. Thatch should be at least 15 cm thick and layered like shingles so water runs off. Make the roof slope at least 30 degrees. Extend the roof over the walls by 30 cm to keep rain out. You can also use plastic tarps as a temporary roof.

Inside the shed, raise the floor with a layer of dry stones or wooden pallets to keep hay off the ground. This prevents moisture from seeping up. Leave space between the walls and the hay for air to circulate. Good ventilation prevents mold. If you store silage, keep it in airtight bags or a pit, not loose in the shed.

Check the shed regularly for leaks, holes, and termites. Repair thatch or mud as needed. A simple shed like this can last 2â3 years with maintenance. It will keep your feed dry and nutritious, saving you money on bought feed.

4. Constructing a Silage Pit or Bunker with Mud and Poles

A silage pit or bunker is a hole or walled structure where you pack chopped green fodder and let it ferment. You can build one using mud and poles. Choose a site that is well-drained and close to where you will feed the animals. The pit should be about 1.5 to 2 meters deep, 2 meters wide, and 3 meters long. This size holds enough silage for 2â3 cows for several months.

Dig the pit in dry soil. If the soil is loose, line the walls with poles or bamboo to prevent collapse. Use strong poles (5â10 cm diameter) driven into the ground every 50 cm along the walls. Weave smaller sticks or bamboo between them to make a basket-like wall. Then plaster the walls with mud mixed with chopped straw. This makes them airtight. The floor should be slightly sloped to one corner so any excess liquid can drain out. You can put a layer of stones at the bottom for drainage.

For a bunker, build walls above ground using poles and mud. Make the walls 1 meter high and 30 cm thick. Leave an opening on one side for loading and unloading. The walls should be smooth inside so silage packs tightly. You can also use old oil drums or plastic-lined pits if you have them.

When filling the pit, chop fodder into 2â3 cm pieces. Pack it in layers of 15 cm, trampling each layer to remove air. Add salt or molasses if you have them to improve fermentation. Cover the top with a plastic sheet or banana leaves, then a layer of mud or soil to seal it. Weight it down with stones. The silage will be ready in 4â6 weeks. Open only what you need and reseal quickly.

Check the pit for cracks and repair them. A well-built pit keeps silage fresh for up to 6 months. This method is cheap and uses materials you already have.

5. Sourcing Clean Water for Irrigation and Processing

Clean water is essential for growing fodder, making silage, and cleaning equipment. You need a reliable source that is free from chemicals, animal waste, and harmful bacteria. In many parts of Africa, water sources include rivers, streams, wells, boreholes, and rainwater harvesting. Test your water before using it. If it looks dirty, smells bad, or has algae, it may not be safe. You can send a sample to a local agricultural office for testing, or use a simple test kit for pH and bacteria.

For irrigation, you need enough water to keep fodder crops growing, especially in the dry season. A drip irrigation system using buckets or pipes can save water. You can also use a treadle pump or a small solar pump to lift water from a well or river. If you use river water, dig a small infiltration pit near the bank to filter out dirt. Line it with gravel and sand. Let the water settle before use.

For processing silage, water is used to clean the pit and equipment. Always use clean water to avoid introducing mold or bacteria. If you use water from a pond, boil it or add a small amount of chlorine (1 teaspoon per 20 liters) and let it sit for 30 minutes before use. For drinking water for animals, provide clean water separatelyâdirty water can make them sick.

Rainwater harvesting is a good option. Build a simple gutter system on your shed roof and direct water into a tank or drum. A 200-liter drum can collect water from a small roof. Keep the tank covered to prevent mosquitoes and debris. If you have a well, protect it with a concrete apron and a cover to keep out animals and dirt.

Always store water in clean containers. Use a bucket with a lid to scoop water, not your hands. Test your water source every few months, especially after heavy rains. Clean water leads to healthier fodder and better silage.

6. Fencing to Protect Your Fodder from Livestock and Wildlife

Fencing keeps your fodder safe from stray livestock, wild animals, and even people. Without a fence, your hard work can be eaten or trampled in one day. Choose a fence that suits your budget and the animals you want to keep out. For small plots, a simple live fence of thorny bushes like sisal, acacia, or euphorbia works well. Plant them 50 cm apart and weave them together as they grow. This takes time but is cheap and long-lasting.

For quicker protection, use wooden posts and wire. Set posts every 2â3 meters. Use durable wood like eucalyptus or cedar. Dig holes 40 cm deep and pack them with stones. String two or three strands of barbed wire or smooth wire between posts. For goats and sheep, use woven wire or chain-link. For cattle, a single electric wire can work if you have a power source, but it needs maintenance. You can also use old fishing nets or used grain sacks as a temporary barrier.

If wildlife like elephants or antelopes are a problem, you may need a stronger fence. Elephants can push over trees, so use thick poles and strong wire. In some areas, beehive fences (hives hung on posts) deter elephants. Check with local wildlife authorities for advice.

Make a gate that you can close securely. Keep the fence line clear of grass and bushes so you can see holes and repair them. Walk your fence every week. Fix broken wires, replace rotten posts, and fill gaps. If you have a live fence, trim it regularly to keep it thick.

Fencing also helps with rotation. You can divide your fodder plot into paddocks and let animals graze one section while others regrow. This improves yield. Remember, a good fence is an investment. It protects your feed and saves you money on buying extra fodder.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Choosing and Preparing the Right Site for Silage & Hay Making

For smallholder and emerging commercial fodder enterprises across Africa, the site you choose and how you prepare it determines whether your silage ferments cleanly and your hay dries without spoiling. Poor siting causes waterlogging, contamination, rodent damage, and up to 30â40% dry matter losses â losses that translate directly into lost income. This practical reference consolidates the essential field decisions behind each lesson in this module: drainage, clearing and levelling, storage, pit construction, water sourcing, and fencing. Treat it as your pre-construction and pre-harvest site checklist before you invest labour or capital.

1. Selecting a Well-Drained Site

Silage and hay both fail when water sits where it should not. A waterlogged pit develops butyric acid fermentation, producing unpalatable, mouldy feed. A poorly drained hay field keeps swaths damp, encouraging fungal growth and spontaneous heating. Choose gently sloping land (1â3% gradient) with sandy loam or loam soils that drain freely. Avoid black cotton soils (vertisols) that swell and crack, and avoid valley bottoms where water collects. In high-rainfall zones such as the Kenyan highlands or Ghana's forest belt, position pits on the upper slope and divert runoff with a cut-off drain at least 3 m uphill.

2. Clearing and Levelling with Hand Tools

Most African fodder enterprises start with hand tools: slashers, hoes, machetes, rakes, and a line level. Clear all vegetation, roots, and stones from the working area â roots puncture pit liners and harbour pests. Level the pit or bunker floor to within 2 cm using a spirit level and string line, because an uneven floor traps effluent and creates anaerobic pockets. Compact the subsoil with a hand rammer in 10 cm layers. For shed floors, raise the pad 15â20 cm above surrounding ground and crown it slightly so water runs off.

3. Building a Simple Storage Shed Using Local Materials

Hay and dry silage bales must stay dry. A simple shed of poles, thatch, or corrugated iron on a raised, well-drained pad protects against rain and reduces spoilage. Use treated eucalyptus or mukau poles for uprights, set 60 cm into the ground and braced diagonally. Space uprights 2â3 m apart. A roof pitch of 25â30Â° sheds water effectively; thatch needs annual replacement, while iron sheets last longer but conduct heat â insulate with a ceiling of poles and grass if storing hay. Keep bales off the floor on wooden pallets or a 10 cm layer of dry stones to prevent moisture wicking.

4. Constructing a Silage Pit or Bunker with Mud and Poles

In many parts of Africa, mud-and-pole bunkers are the most cost-effective silage structures. Excavate a rectangular pit 1.5â2 m deep, or build above-ground walls of rammed earth reinforced with vertical poles at 1 m centres. Line the walls and floor with 500-gauge polythene sheeting to exclude air and prevent seepage. Fill quickly, compact in 15 cm layers with a hand rammer or by treading, and seal with weighted polythene. A well-built mud-and-pole bunker holds 5â15 tonnes of silage â enough for a 5â10 head dairy unit through a dry season.

5. Sourcing Clean Water for Irrigation and Processing

Fodder quality depends on water quality. Irrigation with contaminated water introduces pathogens and heavy metals into the feed chain. Test water for salinity (EC < 1.5 dS/m for most fodder crops), faecal coliforms, and pH (6.0â8.0). Rainwater harvesting from shed roofs into a lined tank or ferrocement jar is the cheapest clean source. Where boreholes or rivers are used, filter and, if necessary, chlorinate for processing water. Drip or furrow irrigation is more water-efficient than flooding, saving 30â50% of water in dryland fodder production.

6. Fencing to Protect Fodder from Livestock and Wildlife

Unfenced fodder plots are an open invitation to crop raiding by livestock and wildlife. A perimeter fence of live posts (gliricidia, moringa, or acacia) with barbed wire or chain-link protects your investment. In areas with elephants or buffalo, use beehive fences or chilli-grease rope fences â low-cost, community-tested deterrents. Fence height of 1.2â1.5 m suits cattle and goats; 2 m is needed where wildlife pressure is high. Maintain a 1 m clear strip along the fence line to prevent fire and pest harbourage.

Silage effluent is highly acidic and can cause severe skin burns and eye damage. Never enter a freshly opened pit or bunker without ventilation â gases such as nitrogen dioxide and carbon dioxide can be lethal. Always work in pairs, wear gloves and eye protection when handling effluent, and keep children and livestock away from open pits. Dispose of effluent in a lined soakaway, not into watercourses.
