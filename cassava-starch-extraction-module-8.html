1. Mechanical Dewatering: Centrifuges and Presses

After you extract starch from cassava, you have a thin slurryabout 95% water. You must remove most of that water before drying. Mechanical dewatering does this fast and cheaply. The two main tools are the centrifuge and the screw press.

A centrifuge spins the slurry at high speed. The starch, being heavier, settles to the outside, while water stays in the middle. You can use a simple basket centrifugea perforated drum lined with filter cloth. Pour in slurry, spin for 510 minutes at 1,5003,000 RPM. The water flies out, leaving a moist starch cake with about 4050% moisture. For small-scale, a manual or motorized basket centrifuge costs $300$800. In Nigeria, many processors use locally fabricated stainless steel centrifuges.

A screw press (also called a dewatering press) works continuously. Slurry enters one end, and a rotating screw pushes it against a cone or plate. Water squeezes out through screens, and dry cake exits the other end. A good screw press can reduce moisture to 5060%. These are common in Ghana and Tanzania for cassava starch. You can build a small screw press with a 2 HP motor and stainless steel parts for about $500.

If you lack machinery, you can use a hydraulic press or even a manual jack press. Fill filter cloth bags with slurry, stack them, and press for 3060 minutes. This gets moisture down to 5565%. Its labour-intensive but works for 50100 kg batches.

Key tips: Always use clean water and stainless steel or plastic equipment to avoid darkening. Add a little salt (0.1%) to help water release. After dewatering, break the cake into small crumblesthis speeds up drying. Never let the cake sit wet for more than 12 hours, or it will ferment and smell. For best results, aim for 5060% moisture before drying. This cuts drying time by half compared to drying slurry directly.

2. Solar Drying of Cassava Starch

Solar drying is the cheapest way to dry cassava starch in Africa. You use the suns heat and moving air to evaporate water. But open sun drying on mats or concrete is risky: dust, rain, insects, and animals can contaminate your starch. A simple solar dryer solves this.

Build a solar cabinet dryer. Make a wooden or metal box 2 m long, 1 m wide, and 0.5 m deep. Cover the top with clear polythene or glass. Paint the inside black. Add vents at the bottom and top for airflow. Inside, place trays of starch crumbles 23 cm thick. The sun heats the air, which rises and exits, carrying moisture. Temperature inside reaches 4060C. Drying takes 12 days in bright sunshine.

A more efficient design is the solar tunnel dryer. Its a long hoop structure covered with UV-resistant plastic. A small fan (12V, 10W) at one end pushes air through. You can dry 50100 kg of starch per batch in 812 hours. Cost: $200$500. In Kenya, such dryers are used for cassava and other crops.

For best results: Spread starch no thicker than 2 cm. Stir every 2 hours with a clean wooden rake. Start drying early (8 AM) to catch maximum sun. Bring trays inside at night to avoid dew. If humidity is high, add a small charcoal stove or electric heater at the air inletbut keep it outside the drying chamber to avoid smoke.

Solar drying works best in dry seasons. In rainy months, you need backup. A hybrid solar-gas or solar-electric dryer costs more but ensures production. Always use food-grade plastic or stainless steel trays. Never dry on bare ground or on tarpaulin that may leach chemicals. With a good solar dryer, you can reach 1214% moisture in 12 sunny days, ready for milling or packaging.

3. Hot Air Drying Systems

When you need to dry starch fast or during rainy season, hot air drying is the answer. You burn fuel (gas, diesel, firewood, or biomass) to heat air, then blow it over the starch. The hot air picks up moisture and exits. This works 24/7 and gives consistent results.

A simple hot air dryer is a cabinet with trays and a heat source. You can use a gas burner (LPG) or a diesel furnace. A fan (0.52 HP) blows air through a heat exchanger, then into the drying chamber. Temperature should be 5070C. Higher temperatures can gelatinize starch, making it sticky and hard to dry. Airflow must be strong enough to move moisture outabout 510 m per minute per 100 kg of wet starch.

For small-scale, a rotary dryer is better. Its a long cylinder that turns slowly. Hot air flows through, and starch tumbles, exposing new surfaces. You can dry 200500 kg per hour. These are common in medium-scale cassava processing in Nigeria and Thailand. A used rotary dryer might cost $2,000$5,000.

If you have a biomass furnace, you can burn cassava peels, rice husks, or wood chips. This cuts fuel costs. But never let smoke touch the starchuse a heat exchanger. In Tanzania, some processors use a furnace with a stainless steel pipe to heat air, keeping smoke separate.

Key controls: Measure temperature with a thermometer. Keep it below 70C. Check moisture every 30 minutes. Stop when starch reaches 1214% moisture. Over-drying wastes fuel and makes starch dusty. Under-drying causes mould. Clean the dryer dailystarch dust can explode if it builds up near a flame. With good hot air drying, you can process year-round and meet large orders.

4. Monitoring Moisture Content

You cannot guess when starch is dry. Too wet (above 14% moisture) and it will mould in storage. Too dry (below 10%) and you lose weight and money. You must measure moisture accurately.

The best tool is a moisture meter. A handheld grain moisture meter works for starch. Cost: $50$200. You fill a small cup with starch, press a button, and read the percentage. Calibrate it monthly with a known sample. If you cant afford a meter, use the salt test. Put 10 g of starch in a dry bottle with 10 g of dry salt. Shake for 1 minute. If salt sticks to the sides, starch is too wet (above 14%). If salt stays loose, its dry enough.

Another method: the squeeze test. Take a handful of starch and squeeze. If it holds shape and feels damp, its above 15%. If it crumbles and feels dry, its around 1214%. This is not precise but works in the field.

For accurate results, use the oven method. Weigh 10 g of starch in a metal dish. Dry in an oven at 130C for 1 hour. Cool in a desiccator and weigh again. Moisture % = (initial weight final weight) / initial weight 100. This is the standard lab method.

During drying, test every 3060 minutes. Record the weight loss. For a 100 kg batch, you know you need to remove about 85 kg of water to go from 85% moisture (slurry) to 12% moisture (dry starch). But after dewatering, you start at 50% moisture, so you need to remove about 38 kg of water per 100 kg. Track your progress.

Store dried starch in clean, dry sacks. Keep a sample from each batch and test it weekly for the first month. If moisture rises, your storage is damp. Move to a dry room. Always aim for 1213% moisture for long-term storage.

5. Preventing Contamination During Drying

Drying is when your starch is most vulnerable. Dust, insects, rodents, and dirty hands can ruin a whole batch. Contaminated starch fails food safety tests, sells for less, or gets rejected. You must keep it clean.

First, location. Never dry starch near animal pens, toilets, or garbage. Build your dryer at least 20 m from these. If using open sun, dry on raised tables covered with food-grade mesh. Never dry on bare ground, tarpaulin, or concrete that has been used for chemicals. Use stainless steel or food-grade plastic trays.

Second, protect from insects and birds. Cover solar dryers with fine mesh (0.5 mm) at air inlets and outlets. For open drying, use a mesh tent or a simple frame with mosquito netting. Fruit flies and beetles love starch. A single fly can lay eggs that hatch into maggots. If you see small holes or webbing, discard that batch.

Third, human hygiene. Workers must wash hands with soap before touching starch. Wear clean aprons and hairnets. No eating, smoking, or spitting near the dryer. Sick workers should stay away. Use clean toolswooden rakes and shovels dedicated to starch only. Never use the same shovel for manure or waste.

Fourth, control dust. Starch dust floats and settles on everything. Keep the drying area swept. But dont use a broom that sheds bristles. Use a damp cloth or a vacuum. If dust builds up in a hot air dryer, it can explode. Clean the dryer after every batch.

Fifth, water quality. If you rewet starch (e.g., to adjust moisture), use only clean, potable water. Never use river water or well water that might contain bacteria. After drying, cool starch completely before packing. Pack in new, clean polypropylene sacks. Store on pallets, not on the floor. With these steps, your starch will be clean, safe, and ready for market.

6. Energy Efficiency in Drying

Drying uses a lot of energywhether sun, gas, or electricity. In Africa, energy is expensive or unreliable. Saving energy means more profit. Heres how.

First, dewater mechanically as much as possible. Removing 1 kg of water by pressing costs about 10 times less than removing it by drying. So invest in a good press or centrifuge. Aim for 50% moisture before drying. This cuts drying energy by half.

Second, insulate your dryer. For hot air dryers, wrap the chamber with fibreglass or rockwool insulation. A well-insulated dryer loses less heat. You can save 2030% on fuel. Seal all gaps. Use a door that closes tightly. For solar dryers, add a black absorber plate and double glazing to trap more heat.

Third, reuse heat. Exhaust air from a hot air dryer is still warm (4050C) and humid. Use a heat exchanger to preheat incoming air. This recovers 3050% of heat. Simple systems use a pipe-in-pipe exchanger. In Kenya, some processors use this on cassava dryers.

Fourth, control airflow. Too much air wastes heat; too little air keeps moisture in. Use a variable speed fan or a damper. Test: if exhaust air feels very humid, increase airflow. If it feels dry and hot, reduce airflow. Aim for 510 m/min per 100 kg wet starch.

Fifth, use renewable energy. Solar is free after installation. For hot air, consider biogas from cassava waste. A small biogas digester (25 m) can produce enough gas to run a dryer for 23 hours per day. In Nigeria, some processors use biogas from peels. Also, burn agricultural residues (rice husks, maize cobs) in a gasifier to produce heat. This is cheaper than diesel.

Sixth, schedule drying during sunny hours. If you have both solar and backup, use solar first. Only run the hot air dryer when needed. Keep records of fuel use per batch. Aim to reduce by 10% each month. With these practices, you can cut drying costs by 4060%.

<h2>Practical Reference: Dewatering and Drying of Cassava Starch</h2>

<p>Dewatering and drying are the two most expensive steps in cassava starch processing after grating. For a small-to-medium enterprise (SME) in Nigeria, Ghana, Tanzania or Uganda, they determine whether you can sell into the formal market where moisture content must be below 13% and contamination must be controlled or only into informal local trade at lower prices. This lesson is a practical reference: the numbers, the equipment choices, the monitoring routines, and the energy decisions that protect your margin.</p>

<p>Think of it as a business problem first. Every kilogram of water you remove mechanically is a kilogram you do not pay to evaporate with heat. A well-run press or decanter centrifuge can cut the thermal drying load by 6075%, which directly reduces fuel or electricity cost per tonne of starch. The goal of this module is to help you choose the right combination of mechanical dewatering and drying for your scale, and to run it consistently.</p>

<h3>1. Mechanical Dewatering: Centrifuges and Presses</h3>
<p>Mechanical dewatering removes free water before thermal drying. The two common options in African SME settings are:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hydraulic or screw presses</strong> lower capital cost, batch operation, suitable for 0.53 t/day. Cake moisture typically 5565%.</li>
<li><strong>Decanter centrifuges</strong> continuous, higher throughput (550 t/day), better cake consistency, but higher capital and maintenance cost. Cake moisture 5060%.</li>
</ul>
<p>Business rule of thumb: if your drying fuel cost exceeds 15% of your selling price per tonne, invest in better mechanical dewatering before adding drying capacity. A decanter that drops cake moisture by 8 percentage points can pay back in 1224 months at medium scale.</p>

<h3>2. Solar Drying of Cassava Starch</h3>
<p>Solar drying is the lowest-cost option where land and sunshine are available, but it is slow and weather-dependent. Open sun drying on mats or concrete is common but risks contamination from dust, animals and rain. Improved options include:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Solar cabinet dryers</strong> enclosed, with a glazed collector and chimney; 25 faster than open sun; protects from dust and rain.</li>
<li><strong>Greenhouse-type tunnel dryers</strong> larger capacity, 100500 kg per batch, good for cooperatives.</li>
<li><strong>Hybrid solar-biomass dryers</strong> solar by day, biomass backup at night or in cloudy weather; essential for consistent supply contracts.</li>
</ul>
<p>Solar drying works best when starch is first dewatered to a cake and broken into small granules (25 mm) to increase surface area. Drying time: 13 days in open sun; 824 hours in a good cabinet dryer.</p>

<h3>3. Hot Air Drying Systems</h3>
<p>For industrial buyers and export, hot air drying gives consistent moisture and throughput. Common systems:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Flash dryers</strong> very fast (seconds), for fine starch; high capital, needs skilled operation.</li>
<li><strong>Fluidised bed dryers</strong> uniform drying, 1030 minutes, good for medium scale.</li>
<li><strong>Tray/oven dryers</strong> batch, simple, 412 hours; suitable for 0.55 t/day.</li>
<li><strong>Rotary dryers</strong> continuous, 550 t/day, but risk of over-drying and case-hardening if not controlled.</li>
</ul>
<p>Fuel choice matters: diesel and LPG are expensive in most African markets; biomass (rice husk, maize cob, cassava peels) or grid electricity (where reliable) is usually cheaper. A well-insulated tray dryer using biomass can dry starch at 3050% lower cost per tonne than diesel.</p>

<h3>4. Monitoring Moisture Content</h3>
<p>Moisture is the single most important quality parameter. Buyers reject starch above 13%; below 10% you are wasting energy. Practical monitoring:</p>
<ul>
<li>Use a calibrated moisture meter (grain or starch type) check every batch.</li>
<li>Do a simple oven test weekly: weigh 10 g, dry at 105C for 3 hours, reweigh. Difference = moisture.</li>
<li>Record batch number, drying time, temperature and final moisture in a logbook.</li>
<li>Adjust drying time based on ambient humidity rainy season needs longer drying or hybrid backup.</li>
</ul>

<strong style="color:#b03a2e"> SAFETY WARNING Drying and Dust</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#5c1f18">Cassava starch dust is combustible. In hot air dryers, fine starch particles can form an explosive dust cloud if ignition sources are present. Never use open flames near dryers, ensure proper earthing of metal equipment, keep dust extraction and cleaning routines, and never enter a dryer or silo without lockout and ventilation. Also protect workers from hot surfaces and rotating parts: presses and centrifuges must have guards and emergency stops. In solar drying, ensure structures are stable and food-grade surfaces are used never dry starch on bare ground or on surfaces treated with pesticides.</p>

<h3>5. Preventing Contamination During Drying</h3>
<p>Contamination destroys export value. Key controls:</p>
<ul>
<li>Use food-grade stainless steel or food-grade plastic surfaces; avoid galvanised or painted metal in contact with wet starch.</li>
<li>Cover open-air dryers with mesh or netting to keep out insects, birds and rodents.</li>
<li>Locate dryers away from roads, livestock and waste areas.</li>
<li>Filter hot air intake; never use exhaust from engines directly on starch.</li>
<li>Clean dryers between batches; wet starch residues ferment and attract pests.</li>
<li>Store dried starch in clean, dry, sealed bags on pallets, not on the floor.</li>
</ul>

<h3>6. Energy Efficiency in Drying</h3>
<p>Energy is the largest variable cost in drying. Practical efficiency measures:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Dewater first:</strong> every 1% reduction in cake moisture cuts drying energy by roughly 35%.</li>
<li><strong>Insulate dryers</strong> lagging and sealed doors reduce heat loss by 1530%.</li>
<li><strong>Recirculate air</strong> where safe; use heat exchangers to recover exhaust heat.</li>
<li><strong>Match batch size to dryer capacity</strong> overloading lengthens drying and wastes fuel.</li>
<li><strong>Use biomass or solar hybrid</strong> instead of diesel where supply is reliable.</li>
<li><strong>Maintain equipment</strong> dirty burners, leaking ducts and worn belts waste 1020% of energy.</li>
<li><strong>Schedule drying in daylight</strong> for solar; avoid night drying unless using waste heat.</li>
</ul>
