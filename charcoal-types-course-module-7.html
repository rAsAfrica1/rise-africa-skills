1. Types of Charcoal Paper

Charcoal paper comes in three main types: laid, wove, and textured. Laid paper has parallel lines from the manufacturing process, giving a ribbed surface that grabs charcoal dust and creates a broken, linear texture. Wove paper is smoother and uniform, good for detailed work but holds less charcoal. Textured paper, often called 'tooth,' has a pronounced grain that catches charcoal particles, allowing for rich darks and expressive marks. In Africa, common brands include Canson Mi-Teintes (available in Nairobi, Lagos, and Johannesburg art shops), Fabriano Tiziano, and local alternatives like Kenyan-made 'Kraft' paper from Nairobi's Kamukunji market. For beginners, a pad of 130gsm (grams per square meter) charcoal paper is ideal. Lighter papers (90gsm) buckle when you apply wet media or heavy pressure. Heavier papers (160gsm and above) withstand erasing and blending. If you cannot find dedicated charcoal paper, try brown kraft paper (80-120gsm) from a packaging supplierit has enough tooth for charcoal and is very cheap. Newsprint is another option but it is too smooth and tears easily; use it only for quick sketches. Always check the paper's surface: run your finger across it. If it feels like fine sandpaper, it will hold charcoal well. If it feels like glass, it will smudge and frustrate you. In West Africa, 'Sahara' brand charcoal paper is popular in Accra and Dakar. In East Africa, 'Canson' and 'Strathmore' are found in Kampala and Dar es Salaam. For a low-cost start, buy a roll of 'sugar paper' (also called kraft paper) from a stationery shop in your local market. Cut it to A3 size (297 x 420 mm) and tape it to a wooden board. This gives you a large, affordable surface to practice on. Remember: the paper you choose affects how your charcoal behaves. Soft charcoal on rough paper gives bold, grainy strokes; hard charcoal on smooth paper gives sharp, thin lines. Experiment with what you can find locally before ordering expensive imports.

2. Texture and Tooth

Texture and tooth are the two properties that determine how charcoal interacts with paper. Tooth is the microscopic roughness of the paper surfacethe peaks and valleys that catch charcoal particles. Texture is the visible pattern, like lines or bumps. A paper with high tooth holds more charcoal, allowing you to build deep blacks and soft gradients. A paper with low tooth (smooth) lets charcoal sit on the surface, making it easy to smudge but hard to layer. To test tooth, draw a single stroke with a soft charcoal stick (e.g., 6B or willow charcoal) across the paper. On high-tooth paper, the stroke will look broken and grainy. On low-tooth paper, it will be solid and dark. For African learners, common high-tooth papers include Canson Mi-Teintes (textured side), Fabriano Tiziano, and local 'rough' watercolor paper (300gsm). Low-tooth papers include Bristol board and smooth newsprint. The best paper for charcoal has medium toothenough to hold layers but smooth enough for detail. You can create your own texture by sanding smooth paper with 120-grit sandpaper. Rub the sandpaper lightly over the surface in one direction, then the other, to create a cross-hatch tooth. This is a cheap way to turn cheap paper into charcoal-friendly paper. Another method: apply a coat of clear gesso or a mix of acrylic medium and fine sand (from a hardware store) to any paper. Let it dry, then draw. In Nigeria, artists often use 'Sahara' charcoal paper which has a natural tooth similar to Canson. In Kenya, 'Kraft' paper from Kamukunji has a rough side and a smooth sidealways use the rough side for charcoal. When you blend charcoal with a tortillon or your finger, high-tooth paper will hold the blended tone, while smooth paper will just push the charcoal around. For a practice exercise, take three papers: newsprint (smooth), kraft (medium), and watercolor paper (rough). Draw the same simple shapea mango or a calabashon each. Notice how the charcoal behaves differently. This will teach you to choose the right paper for the effect you want.

3. Toned Paper for Charcoal

Toned paper is any paper that is not whiteusually grey, tan, blue, or ochre. Charcoal works beautifully on toned paper because you can use both black charcoal for darks and white chalk or white charcoal pencil for highlights. The paper's mid-tone acts as your middle value, saving time and creating a cohesive look. In Africa, you can find toned paper in art shops (Canson Mi-Teintes in 50+ colors) or make your own. To make toned paper cheaply, mix acrylic paint or gouache with water to a light wash, and brush it over a sheet of strong paper (e.g., 160gsm cartridge paper). Let it dry flat. Popular tones for African subjects: warm ochre (like the soil in Ghana), cool grey (like the sky in the Sahel), and terracotta (like the walls in Mali). For a portrait of a person with dark skin, a mid-grey or warm brown toned paper helps you render skin tones more realistically. You can also use brown kraft paper as a ready-made toned surfaceit is already a warm tan. On toned paper, start with your darkest darks using compressed charcoal or a 6B stick. Then add white highlights with a white charcoal pencil (e.g., General's White Charcoal) or a piece of white chalk. The paper's tone becomes your mid-tone. This three-value system (dark, mid, light) is fast and effective. A common mistake is to cover the whole paper with charcoal, losing the tone. Instead, leave areas of the paper untouched to show the mid-tone. For a practice project, take a sheet of grey Canson paper (A4 size) and draw a simple still life: a banana, a tin cup, and a folded cloth. Use only black charcoal and white chalk. See how the grey paper unifies the drawing. In East Africa, 'Canson Mi-Teintes' in 'Felt Grey' or 'Tobacco' is popular. In West Africa, 'Sahara' toned paper in 'Sand' is common. If you cannot afford toned paper, buy a large sheet of brown kraft paper from a packaging shop and cut it to size. It costs a fraction of art paper and works well for charcoal studies.

4. Preparing Surfaces for Charcoal

Preparing your surface before drawing with charcoal is crucial for good results. First, choose a paper with enough tooth (see Lesson 2). If the paper is too smooth, you can roughen it with sandpaper (120-grit) or apply a coat of gesso mixed with fine sand. Second, tape your paper to a rigid boarda piece of plywood or hardboard from a local carpenter works well. Use masking tape or brown paper tape. Tape all four edges to prevent curling. This also gives you a clean border. Third, if you plan to use wet media (like a charcoal wash with water), you must stretch the paper. Soak the paper in water for 5 minutes, lay it flat on a board, and tape the edges tightly. As it dries, it shrinks and becomes taut. This prevents buckling. For dry charcoal only, stretching is not necessary, but taping is still recommended. Fourth, consider applying a fixative before drawing? Nofixative is used after drawing to prevent smudging. But you can apply a workable fixative between layers to build up darks without smudging previous layers. In Africa, local fixatives include cheap hairspray (unscented) or diluted PVA glue (1 part glue to 5 parts water) sprayed lightly. Test on a scrap first. Fifth, for a tinted ground, mix acrylic paint or gouache with water and brush it on. Let it dry completely before drawing. A common recipe: 1 part acrylic paint, 2 parts water. Apply with a wide brush in one direction, then dry. This gives a colored ground that shows through your charcoal. Sixth, if you are using charcoal powder, you can create a base tone by rubbing powder into the paper with a cloth. This is called a 'toned ground' and can speed up your work. Finally, always keep a scrap piece of the same paper nearby to test your charcoal strokes and values before committing to your final drawing. In Nigeria, artists often prepare kraft paper by sanding it lightly and wiping off dust. In Kenya, some use a mixture of cassava starch and water as a size to seal the paperbut this can make it too smooth, so test first. Proper preparation saves you from frustration and wasted materials.

5. Testing Charcoal on Different Papers

Testing charcoal on different papers is the fastest way to learn which paper suits your style. You need at least four paper types: newsprint (smooth), cartridge paper (medium), kraft paper (rough), and watercolor paper (very rough). Also gather your charcoal: willow charcoal (soft, thin), compressed charcoal (hard, dark), and white charcoal pencil. For each paper, do the same set of tests. First, draw a single vertical line with willow charcoal, pressing lightly. Then draw another line pressing hard. Compare how dark each line is and how much texture shows. Second, make a gradient: start with heavy pressure at the top and gradually lift your hand as you move down. See how smoothly you can transition from dark to light. Third, try blending: use your finger or a tortillon to smudge a patch of charcoal. Note how easily it blends and whether it leaves a smooth or grainy finish. Fourth, test erasing: use a kneaded eraser to lift charcoal from a dark patch. High-tooth paper will leave a ghost image; smooth paper will erase almost completely. Fifth, test layering: draw a dark area, then try to add a lighter layer on top. On rough paper, layers build up; on smooth paper, they just mix. Record your results in a notebookwrite the paper name and a few words about each test. In Africa, you can find these papers at local markets: newsprint from a newspaper printer, cartridge paper from a stationery shop, kraft paper from a packaging supplier, and watercolor paper from an art shop (or use the back of a calendar). For a cheap alternative to watercolor paper, use the rough side of a cement bag (washed and dried). After testing, you will know which paper to use for which effect. For example, if you want a detailed portrait, use cartridge paper. If you want a bold, expressive landscape, use kraft or watercolor paper. This knowledge will save you money and improve your drawings immediately. Do this test once a month with new papers you find.

6. Practice Project: Paper Comparison Study

For this project, you will create a single drawing that compares three different papers side by side. This will solidify your understanding of how paper affects charcoal. You need: three sheets of paper of the same size (A5 or 150 x 210 mm) but different texturese.g., smooth cartridge paper, medium kraft paper, and rough watercolor paper. Also, your charcoal set (willow, compressed, white pencil), a kneaded eraser, and masking tape. Tape each sheet to a separate board or to one large board with gaps between them. Choose a simple subject that has light, mid-tone, and dark areasa ripe mango, a clay pot, or a folded headscarf. Draw the same subject on each paper using the same sequence: first, outline with willow charcoal. Second, block in the darkest shadows with compressed charcoal. Third, blend lightly with your finger. Fourth, add highlights with white charcoal pencil (if using toned paper) or erase out lights with a kneaded eraser (if using white paper). Fifth, refine edges and details. Work on all three simultaneously, spending about 20 minutes on each. This forces you to notice differences. After finishing, step back and compare. On smooth paper, your drawing will look sharp but may lack depth. On rough paper, it will look grainy and expressive but may lack detail. On medium paper, you get a balance. Write a short note on each drawing: 'Smooth: easy to smudge, hard to layer,' 'Rough: great texture, difficult for fine lines,' etc. Then, choose which paper you prefer for this subject and explain why in your notebook. For an African context, use locally available papers: a page from a cheap exercise book (smooth), a piece of brown kraft from the market (medium), and a sheet of rough watercolor paper or a washed cement bag (rough). If you cannot find white charcoal, use white chalk or even a piece of white soap (test first). This project takes about 2 hours. Repeat it with a different subject next week. Over time, you will develop a personal preference and a mental library of which paper to use for which effect. This is how professional artists in Lagos, Nairobi, and Accra make their choicesthrough repeated testing.

<h2>Practical Reference: Charcoal Papers and Surfaces</h2>

<p>In the African charcoal economy, paper and surface selection is not a small detail. It determines how much charcoal you waste, how fast you can produce finished work, what you can sell, and what price you can command. For artists, trainers, and small studios from Nairobi to Lagos to Accra, understanding charcoal papers and surfaces is a business decision. The right surface reduces rework, protects your margins, and opens access to higher-value commissions and export markets.</p>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the key technical points from the module: paper types, texture and tooth, toned papers, surface preparation, and testing. It is written for African charcoal producers, art entrepreneurs, and trainers who need reliable, field-tested guidance that can be applied in a studio, a workshop, or a market stall.</p>

<h3>Why Surface Choice Drives Profit</h3>
<p>Charcoal is a dry medium. It sits on the surface rather than soaking in like ink or watercolour. That means the paper's tooth the microscopic peaks and valleys in the sheet controls how much charcoal is captured, how easily it blends, and how much dust is lost. For a working artist in Africa, this has direct commercial consequences:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Waste reduction:</strong> A paper with the wrong tooth forces repeated passes, consuming more charcoal sticks or powder.</li>
<li><strong>Speed:</strong> Toned paper lets you work from mid-tone outward, cutting drawing time by up to 30% on portrait commissions.</li>
<li><strong>Durability:</strong> Correctly prepared surfaces resist smudging during transport to markets, galleries, or export packing.</li>
<li><strong>Price positioning:</strong> Toned and textured papers photograph better for online sales, supporting higher asking prices.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Texture and Tooth: What to Look For</h3>
<p>Tooth is the physical grain of the paper. It is created during manufacture by the fibre blend, the pressing method, and any surface sizing. For charcoal, three practical tests matter:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Grip test:</strong> Drag a compressed charcoal stick lightly across the sheet. A good tooth holds a broken, textured line without skipping.</li>
<li><strong>Blend test:</strong> Use a torchon or finger to smear a small patch. Smooth papers blend into flat grey; rough papers retain streaks and grain.</li>
<li><strong>Lift test:</strong> Try to erase or lift charcoal with a kneaded eraser. Papers with weak sizing stain permanently, which ruins commission work.</li>
</ol>
<p>In African markets, "tooth" is often confused with "weight." They are different. A 200gsm paper can have high tooth; a 300gsm paper can be smooth. Always test by feel and mark, not by label alone.</p>

<h3>Toned Paper for Charcoal</h3>
<p>Toned paper grey, tan, ochre, or warm brown is a commercial advantage. It provides a mid-value base so the artist only needs to add darks and lights. This reduces production time and creates a finished look that photographs well under natural African light. Common toned options include Canson Mi-Teintes, Strathmore Toned, and locally cut kraft or sugar paper.</p>
<p>For business use, toned paper works best for:</p>
<ul>
<li>Portrait commissions with tight deadlines</li>
<li>Figure studies for gallery consignment</li>
<li>Workshop demonstrations where speed impresses students</li>
<li>Social media content toned works show contrast clearly on phone screens</li>
</ul>

<h3>Preparing Surfaces: Field Practice</h3>
<p>Preparation is where African studios can gain a real edge. Imported prepared papers are expensive and often delayed at ports. Local preparation using gesso, pumice, or even cassava starch sizing (used traditionally in some West African paper-making) can produce workable surfaces at a fraction of the cost. The key is consistency: prepare in batches, label each sheet with the date and mix ratio, and test before committing to a commission.</p>
<p>Recommended workflow:</p>
<ol>
<li>Select your base paper by weight and fibre.</li>
<li>Wipe with a dry cloth to remove dust.</li>
<li>Apply preparation in thin, even coats. Let dry fully between coats.</li>
<li>Sand lightly between coats if a smoother tooth is needed.</li>
<li>Test a small corner with your chosen charcoal before starting the final work.</li>
</ol>

<h3>Testing Charcoal on Different Papers</h3>
<p>Never buy in bulk without testing. A simple A5 swatch test takes 20 minutes and can save thousands of shillings or naira. Test each paper with:</p>
<ul>
<li>One vine charcoal stick</li>
<li>One compressed charcoal stick</li>
<li>One charcoal pencil</li>
<li>One kneaded eraser and one hard eraser</li>
<li>One blending tool (torchon, paper stump, or finger)</li>
</ul>
<p>Record results in a simple notebook: grip, blend, lift, dust, and final appearance. Over time, this becomes your personal surface library a business asset that lets you quote confidently and deliver consistently.</p>
