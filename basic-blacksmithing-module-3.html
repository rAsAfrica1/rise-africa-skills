1. The Anvil: Parts and Proper Use

The anvil is the heart of your forge. In Africa, you can find new anvils from suppliers in Nairobi, Lagos, or Johannesburg, but many smiths use a heavy piece of railway line or a solid block of scrap steel. A good anvil weighs between 50 kg and 150 kg. It must sit on a sturdy basea thick wooden stump buried in the ground works well. The main parts are the face (the flat top), the horn (the pointed end), the heel (the square end), the step (a lower flat area near the horn), and the hardy hole (a square hole for tools) and pritchel hole (a round hole for punching).

When you work, the anvil should be at knuckle height when you stand straight. This saves your back and gives you full swing. Secure the anvil so it does not bounce. You can chain it to the stump or use metal straps. The face must be smooth but not polisheda slight texture helps grip the hot metal. Never hammer on the face when it is cold, and never use the anvil as a welding table without protecting it.

Use the horn for bending curves. The step is for cutting with a chisel, so you don't damage the main face. The hardy hole holds tools like the hardy cutter. The pritchel hole is for punching holesthe punch goes through the metal and into the hole. Keep the face clean; wipe off scale and rust with a wire brush. A light oil rub once a week prevents rust in humid climates. If the edges are chipped, grind them smooth with an angle grinder, but keep a small radius on the edges for drawing out metal without cutting it.

A common mistake is to hammer directly on the anvil without a workpiece. That dents the face and can chip it. Always have hot metal between your hammer and the anvil. Also, never strike the anvil with a hardened hammerit can shatter. With proper care, a good anvil will last for generations.

2. Hammers: Types, Weights, and Handling

As a blacksmith, your hammer is your main tool. You need at least two: a heavy hammer for drawing out metal (around 1.5 to 2 kg or 34 lb) and a lighter one for finishing and detail work (around 0.5 to 1 kg or 12 lb). In Africa, you can buy blacksmith hammers from industrial suppliers or make your own from car axles or leaf springs. The head should be hardened but not brittle. The handle should be hickory or local hardwood like mukwa or irokostraight-grained and free of knots. Fit the handle through the eye and wedge it tight with a metal wedge and a wooden wedge at right angles. The handle length should be about 3035 cm, and the end should be slightly flared so it doesn't slip out of your hand.

Grip the hammer near the end of the handle, not close to the head. This gives you more leverage and power. Your thumb should wrap around the handle, not rest on top. When you swing, use your whole arm, not just your wrist. Let the hammer do the workraise it and let it fall with gravity. For heavy drawing out, use the flat face. For rounding, use the ball peen. For texturing, you can use a cross peen or a straight peen. Always strike the metal flat; if you strike at an angle, you will leave marks and waste energy.

Keep your hammer face smooth and slightly domed. If it gets mushroomed, grind off the mushroomed edges immediatelythey can break off and injure you. Never use a hammer with a loose head. Check the wedges regularly and tighten them if needed. Store hammers hanging on a rack or lying flat on a shelf, not on the floor where they can get damaged or cause a trip hazard. A well-maintained hammer will give you years of service.

3. Tongs: Selecting and Using the Right Pair

Tongs are your hands in the forge. They hold hot metal so you can hammer, twist, or cut it. You need several pairs because different jobs require different jaws. The most common types are flat-jaw tongs for holding flat bar, box-jaw tongs for square or round stock, and pick-up tongs for small pieces. In Africa, you can buy tongs from blacksmith suppliers or make your own from mild steel. A good pair of tongs is made from 1012 mm round or square bar, with jaws that fit the stock you are working with. The reins (handles) should be long enough to keep your hands away from the heatat least 40 cm. The joint should be a rivet or a bolt that allows smooth opening and closing.

When selecting tongs, match the jaw shape to the workpiece. For flat bar, use flat jaws that grip the sides. For round bar, use box jaws or V-jaws that hold the curve. The jaws should close completely without gaps. If they don't, heat them and adjust with a hammer. The reins should be comfortable to hold; you can curve them slightly outward for better grip. Always hold the tongs with the workpiece centered in the jaws, not at the tip, to avoid dropping it.

Never use tongs to hold cold metal for heavy hammeringthey can slip. For heavy work, use a tong with a longer handle and a tighter grip. Keep the joint lubricated with a drop of oil. If the jaws get too hot, cool them in waterbut don't quench hot tongs in water if they are made of high-carbon steel, as they may crack. Instead, let them air cool. Store tongs hanging on a rack or lying flat. A good pair of tongs will last for many years if you don't use them as a hammer or pry bar.

4. Hardies, Swages, and Fullers

Hardies, swages, and fullers are tools that fit into the hardy hole or are held by hand. The hardy is a chisel-like tool that fits in the anvil's hardy hole. It is used for cutting hot metal. You place the hot metal on the hardy and strike it with a hammer. The hardy should be made of high-carbon steel and hardened. It should fit snugly in the hole but be easy to remove. Keep the cutting edge sharp with a file or grinder. Never cut cold metal with a hardyit will chip. Also, never leave a hardy in the anvil when not in use; it can trip you or get damaged.

Swages are used to shape metal into curves or rounds. A top swage is held by a handle and struck with a hammer; a bottom swage fits in the hardy hole. Together they form a round or oval shape. For example, to make a round bar from square stock, you use a swage with a half-round groove. Swages come in different sizes. In Africa, you can make your own swages by grinding grooves into a block of steel. Fullers are similar but have a rounded, convex face. They are used to draw out metal quickly or to make grooves. A top fuller is held by a handle; a bottom fuller fits in the hardy hole. You strike the top fuller to push metal into the bottom fuller, thinning and spreading it.

When using these tools, always work with hot metalat least a bright orange heat. Strike squarely and evenly. Keep the tools clean and free of scale. Store them in a rack or box, not on the floor. A hardy that is too loose can bounce out and injure you, so check the fit regularly. If it's too loose, you can shim it with a piece of sheet metal. With practice, these tools will speed up your work and improve the quality of your forgings.

5. Measuring and Marking Tools

Accurate measuring and marking are essential for making consistent products. You need a steel rule or tape measure (at least 30 cm), a try square, a scriber or a soapstone marker, and a center punch. In Africa, you can find these at hardware stores or market stalls. A steel rule is best for precise work; a tape measure is fine for longer lengths. The try square checks 90-degree angles and marks lines across bars. A scriber is a sharp-pointed steel rod for scratching lines on metal. Soapstone is a natural stone that leaves a white mark on hot or cold metalit's cheap and easy to find. A center punch is a pointed tool you strike with a hammer to make a small dent, which guides a drill bit.

Before you cut or bend, mark your workpiece clearly. Use soapstone on hot metalit won't burn off like paint. For cold metal, use a scriber or a permanent marker. Measure twice, mark once. When marking, hold the rule or square firmly against the metal. For a square line, place the try square against the edge and scribe along the blade. For a center line, measure from both edges and mark the midpoint. Use a center punch to mark hole locations. Strike it once with a hammer to make a dent. This prevents the drill from wandering.

Keep your measuring tools clean and dry. Store them in a box or pouch, not loose in a toolbox where they can get bent or rusty. A steel rule can get damaged if used as a scraper or pry bar. Check your try square regularly for squarenessif it's out, your work will be out. A simple test is to mark a line, flip the square, and mark again; if the lines don't match, the square is off. Replace or adjust it. Good measuring habits save time and material.

6. Tool Maintenance and Storage

Your tools are an investment. Proper maintenance and storage will keep them working safely and last for years. At the end of each day, wipe down all tools with an oily rag to prevent rust. In humid African climates, rust can appear overnight. Use any light oilengine oil, coconut oil, or even used cooking oil works in a pinch. For the anvil, wire-brush the face and oil it. For hammers and tongs, check for cracks, loose handles, or mushroomed heads. Grind off any mushrooming immediately. For hardies and swages, check the edges and grind them sharp if needed. Keep the hardy hole clean of scale and debris.

Store tools in a dry place. A wooden box or a rack on the wall is ideal. Hang tongs and hammers on pegs so they don't touch the floor. Keep the anvil on its stump, not on the ground where it can rust. If you have a dirt floor, place a wooden pallet or a concrete slab under your work area to reduce moisture. Never store tools in a damp corner or near a forge that is not in usecondensation can form. For long-term storage, coat tools with grease or wrap them in oiled cloth.

Schedule a weekly maintenance check. Tighten hammer wedges, oil tong joints, and check the anvil for chips. Replace worn handles. A loose hammer head is dangerousfix it before you use it again. Keep a file and a grinder handy for touch-ups. Teach yourself to inspect tools before each use: look for cracks, loose parts, or damage. If a tool is unsafe, repair it or discard it. Don't take chances. A well-maintained toolkit is the mark of a professional blacksmith.

<h2>Practical Reference: Blacksmithing Tools and Equipment</h2>

<p>In a working African forge whether you are setting up under a mango tree in Mbale, a roadside shed in Kumasi, or a rented bay in Gweru your income depends on how fast and how accurately you can turn scrap steel into hoes, pangas, door hinges, and ox-cart fittings. That speed and accuracy come from six tool families working together: the anvil, the hammer, the tongs, the hardie/swage/fuller set, measuring and marking tools, and a disciplined maintenance routine. This reference lesson gives you the practical numbers, dimensions, and habits that keep a forge productive and profitable.</p>

<p>Treat every tool as a capital asset. A blacksmith who buys one good 50 kg anvil and three properly weighted hammers will out-earn a smith who owns five broken anvils and a bucket of mystery hammers. Buy for the work you actually sell, not for the work you imagine.</p>

<h3>The Anvil: Parts and Proper Use</h3>

<p>The anvil is your workbench, your third hand, and your measuring reference. A London-pattern anvil has a <strong>face</strong> (hardened top), a <strong>horn</strong> (rounded cone for bending and drawing), a <strong>step</strong> (soft, unhardened shoulder for cutting), a <strong>heel</strong> (rear of the face), a <strong>hardy hole</strong> (square, takes bottom tools), a <strong>pritchel hole</strong> (round, for punching clearance), a <strong>waist</strong> (the neck), and a <strong>base</strong>.</p>

<p>In African markets, the most common anvils are: (1) imported London-pattern anvils from China or India, 25100 kg; (2) local "rail anvils" made from a section of railway line; (3) improvised anvils from truck axles or excavator pins. All can earn money but only the first two give you a true flat face, a hardy hole, and rebound. Test rebound by dropping a ball bearing or hammer head from 30 cm: a good anvil returns the hammer 6080% of the drop height. A dead anvil (no rebound) wastes your arm and slows production.</p>

<p>Mount the anvil so the face is at knuckle height (roughly 6575 cm for most adults) when you stand relaxed. Secure it with chain, timber wedges, or a steel strap over the feet a bouncing anvil is dangerous and inefficient. Work hot steel on the face, never on the step (which is soft), and never hammer cold steel on the face you will chip the edge.</p>

<h3>Hammers: Types, Weights, and Handling</h3>

<p>You need at least three hammers: a <strong>cross-peen</strong> for drawing out, a <strong>ball-peen</strong> for riveting and texturing, and a <strong>straight-peen</strong> or <strong>rounding hammer</strong> for general forging. Head weights for African production work typically run 0.5 kg (light finishing), 1.01.5 kg (general forging), and 2.54 kg (heavy drawing with a striker).</p>

<p>Handle length matters more than head weight for control. A 1 kg head wants a 3035 cm handle; a 3 kg head wants 7590 cm for two-handed use. Fit handles with a wooden or steel wedge, and never use a hammer with a loose head it is the single most common forge injury.</p>

<h3>Tongs: Selecting and Using the Right Pair</h3>

<p>Tongs are your fingers. A smith with the wrong tongs drops hot steel, loses time, and risks burns. Build a set of at least four pairs: <strong>flat-jaw</strong> for bar stock, <strong>bolt tongs</strong> for round stock, <strong>pick-up tongs</strong> for small pieces, and <strong>hollow-bit tongs</strong> for holding a punched head. In many African forges, tongs are forged on-site from leaf-spring steel (5160 or 9260) this is cheaper and gives you exactly the jaw you need.</p>

<p>The rule of thumb: the tongs should grip the stock at the <em>jaw</em>, not the <em>rein</em>. The reins should be long enough to keep your hand 2530 cm from the hot end, and the jaws should close parallel on the stock. If you must squeeze hard to hold the work, the tongs are wrong reforge or swap them.</p>

<h3>Hardies, Swages, and Fullers</h3>

<p>Bottom tools sit in the hardy hole and top tools are struck with a hammer. The <strong>hardie</strong> (hot cut) cuts hot steel never cold, or you will mushroom the edge. <strong>Swages</strong> (top and bottom pairs) shape round or hexagonal sections. <strong>Fullers</strong> (top and bottom) draw out and neck down steel. In a typical African forge, you will make most of these yourself from truck leaf springs, which are tough, cheap, and locally available.</p>

<p>Match the tool shank to the hardy hole (usually 25 mm or 1 inch square). A loose tool that rocks in the hole will damage the anvil and produce inaccurate work. If your anvil has no hardy hole, weld a short length of square tube to a heavy steel plate and use it as a portable bottom-tool holder.</p>

<h3>Measuring and Marking Tools</h3>

<p>You cannot sell what you cannot measure. Keep a steel rule (300 mm), a pair of dividers, a centre punch, a scriber, and a set of callipers within reach. For production runs say 50 hoe blades a week make a <strong>template</strong> from 3 mm mild steel plate, then use it to mark every blade. Templates cut marking time by 6070% and reduce rejects, which directly increases profit per unit.</p>

<p>Forge temperature is also a measurement. Learn the colour chart: dull red (~600 C) for annealing, cherry red (~800 C) for forging mild steel, orange-yellow (~1000 C) for welding, and bright yellow-white (~1200 C) for wrought iron. A magnet is a cheap, reliable test: steel becomes non-magnetic at its critical temperature (~770 C), which is the correct point to quench many carbon steels.</p>

<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#7a1a15;">Hot steel at forging temperature (8001200 C) causes instant, deep burns. Always wear leather gloves on the tong hand, safety glasses or a face shield when cutting or punching, and ear protection when striking. Keep a bucket of water and a dry-sand bucket within 2 m of the anvil. Never quench in water that contains oil or unknown chemicals it can flash. In dusty African forges, keep the coal or charcoal stock at least 3 m from the fire to prevent run-away ignition. Do not forge galvanised steel indoors: the zinc fumes cause metal-fume fever. Work in a well-ventilated area, ideally with a chimney or open wall.</p>
</div>

<h3>Tool Maintenance and Storage</h3>

<p>Maintenance is a profit activity, not a chore. A hammer with a chipped face leaves marks on every product; a tong with worn jaws drops work; a rusty anvil face drags on the steel. Set aside 30 minutes every Friday afternoon for tool care this is cheaper than replacing tools and it protects your reputation.</p>

<p>Daily habits: brush the anvil face with a wire brush, wipe hammers with an oily rag, and hang tongs on a rail so they do not sit in dust. Weekly: dress hammer faces with a smooth file or belt sander to remove chips and mushrooming; check handle wedges; oil all metal surfaces with used engine oil or palm oil (both work). Monthly: re-dress the hardie edge, check the anvil for loose mounting, and sharpen centre punches and scribers.</p>

<p>Storage in humid African climates needs care. A simple tool rack made from a length of 25 mm pipe or a wooden rail, mounted at chest height, keeps tools off the ground and out of termite reach. Cover the anvil with a piece of canvas or an old sack not plastic, which traps condensation. Store files separately; they dull each other when rattled together.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li>Anvil face at knuckle height (6575 cm) and firmly mounted no bounce.</li>
<li>Three hammers minimum: 0.5 kg, 1.01.5 kg, 2.54 kg, all with tight heads.</li>
<li>Four tong pairs: flat-jaw, bolt, pick-up, hollow-bit jaws parallel on stock.</li>
<li>Hardie, swage, and fuller shanks fit the hardy hole with no rocking.</li>
<li>Steel rule, dividers, centre punch, and a 3 mm plate template for repeat jobs.</li>
<li>Safety gear within reach: gloves, eye shield, ear muffs, water and sand buckets.</li>
<li>Weekly 30-minute maintenance slot: dress faces, oil metal, check wedges.</li>
<li>Tools stored off the ground on a rail; anvil covered with canvas, not plastic.</li>
</ul>
