1. Aggregate Planning and Master Production Scheduling

Aggregate planning is how you decide, over the next 3 to 12 months, how much you will produce and how many people you will need. For a small African bakery, it might mean planning to make 5,000 loaves per week in December but only 3,000 in June. You start by forecasting demand using simple tools like a 3-month moving average. If your sales were 2,000, 2,500, and 3,000 loaves in the last three months, your forecast for next month is (2,000+2,500+3,000)/3 = 2,500 loaves. Then you compare that to your capacity. If one oven can bake 500 loaves per day and you work 6 days a week, you can make 3,000 loaves weekly. If demand is 3,500, you need overtime, a second oven, or to outsource.

Master Production Scheduling (MPS) breaks the aggregate plan into specific products and weeks. Instead of 'bread', you plan 'white loaf 700g', 'brown loaf 700g', and 'rolls'. For example, in week 1 you schedule 1,200 white loaves, 800 brown loaves, and 1,000 rolls. The MPS must respect capacity and inventory. If you have 200 white loaves left from last week and want 1,000 in stock at the end of week 1, you need to produce 800 + (1,000 - 200) = 1,600? Wait, thats wrong. Let's recalc: Desired ending inventory = 1,000. Beginning inventory = 200. Demand = 1,200. Production needed = Demand + Desired ending inventory - Beginning inventory = 1,200 + 1,000 - 200 = 2,000 loaves. So you schedule 2,000 white loaves.

In Africa, aggregate planning must account for rainy seasons, harvests, and holidays. A tomato sauce factory in Nigeria knows that demand spikes during Ramadan and Christmas. They start building inventory two months early. Use a simple spreadsheet with columns for month, forecast, production, inventory, and workforce. Calculate inventory each month as previous inventory + production - forecast. Aim to keep inventory between 10% and 20% of monthly sales. If inventory goes negative, increase production or reduce sales. This is called a chase strategy: match production to demand by hiring and laying off. A level strategy keeps production steady and lets inventory rise and fall. For small businesses, a hybrid works: keep a core workforce and use overtime during peaks. Always update your plan monthly as actual sales come in.

2. Material Requirements Planning (MRP)

MRP is a calculator that tells you exactly what materials to order, how much, and when, based on your production schedule. Imagine you make wooden chairs. Each chair needs 4 legs, 1 seat, 8 screws, and 2 meters of varnish. You have an order for 100 chairs in week 5. MRP works backwards from that due date. It uses three inputs: the Master Production Schedule (what you will make), the Bill of Materials (what goes into each product), and inventory records (what you already have).

Lets run MRP for the chair legs. You need 100 chairs in week 5. Each chair needs 4 legs, so you need 400 legs in week 5. You check inventory: you have 50 legs. So net requirement = 400 - 50 = 350 legs. You also have a safety stock of 20 legs, so you need 370 legs. Your supplier takes 2 weeks to deliver. So you must place the order in week 3 (week 5 minus 2 weeks lead time). If you order in batches of 500 (because of supplier discounts), you order 500 legs in week 3, leaving 130 legs in inventory after week 5 (500 - 370 = 130).

For the seat, you need 100 seats in week 5. You have 10 in stock. Net requirement = 90. Lead time is 1 week, so order 90 seats in week 4. For screws, you need 800 screws in week 5. You have 200 in stock. Net requirement = 600. Lead time is 1 day, so order 600 screws in week 5. For varnish, you need 200 meters. You have 50 meters. Net requirement = 150 meters. Lead time is 3 days, so order 150 meters in week 5.

In Africa, MRP helps you avoid both stockouts and excess cash tied up in inventory. Use a simple spreadsheet: list all components, their lead times, current stock, and safety stock. For each week, calculate gross requirements (from MPS), then net requirements = gross - on hand + safety stock. Then offset by lead time to get order release dates. Always add a buffer for unreliable suppliers. If your supplier in China takes 6 weeks but sometimes 8, use 8 weeks in your MRP. Review your MRP weekly. If a supplier fails, you can quickly see which orders are at risk. Many African manufacturers use free tools like Excel or Google Sheets for MRP. Start simple: one product, five components. Once you master that, expand.

3. Capacity Planning and Management

Capacity planning is about matching your ability to produce with the demand you expect. If you run a maize mill in Kenya, your capacity might be 10 tonnes per day. If demand is 12 tonnes, you have a capacity gap. You can close it by adding a shift, buying a bigger mill, or outsourcing. If demand is 8 tonnes, you have excess capacity and are wasting money on idle machines and labour.

First, measure your capacity. For a machine, capacity = (hours available) (units per hour) (efficiency). If your mill runs 8 hours a day at 1.5 tonnes per hour, but efficiency is 80% due to breakdowns, actual capacity = 8 1.5 0.8 = 9.6 tonnes per day. For labour, capacity = number of workers hours per day productivity. If 5 workers each process 0.2 tonnes per hour, capacity = 5 8 0.2 = 8 tonnes per day. Your bottleneck is the lower of the two: 8 tonnes. So you can only produce 8 tonnes daily, not 9.6.

Next, forecast demand. Use historical sales and adjust for trends. If you sold 150 tonnes last month and expect 10% growth, forecast 165 tonnes. Over 20 working days, thats 8.25 tonnes per day. Your capacity is 8 tonnes, so you have a small gap. You can close it by reducing downtime (improve efficiency to 85% gives 8.5 tonnes) or adding 30 minutes of overtime (8.5 hours 5 0.2 = 8.5 tonnes).

Capacity management strategies include: lead strategy (build capacity ahead of demand), lag strategy (wait until demand is consistently high), and match strategy (add capacity in small increments). For African businesses, a match strategy is often best because cash is tight. Use a capacity planning table: months in rows, demand and capacity in columns. Calculate utilisation = demand / capacity. If utilisation is above 90%, you risk delays. If below 70%, you are losing money. Aim for 80-85%. If you have seasonal demand, like a tomato processor during harvest, you can build inventory during low season and draw it down during peak. That way you keep capacity level. Always involve your team: machine operators know real capacity better than any formula.

4. Lean Production and Just-in-Time (JIT)

Lean production is about removing waste. Waste is anything that does not add value for the customer. The eight wastes are: defects, overproduction, waiting, non-utilised talent, transportation, inventory, motion, and extra processing. In an African garment factory, overproduction means making 1,000 shirts when you only have orders for 600. That ties up cash in fabric and finished goods. Waiting means workers standing idle because the sewing machine broke down. Inventory waste is having 3 months of thread in stock when you only need 1 week.

Just-in-Time (JIT) means you produce only what the customer wants, when they want it, and in the amount they want. Instead of pushing products through the factory, you pull them based on actual demand. A kanban card is a simple signal. In a car parts factory, when a worker uses the last of 10 bolts from a bin, they send a kanban card to the store to bring 10 more. The store then orders from the supplier. This keeps inventory low. For JIT to work, you need reliable suppliers, consistent quality, and flexible workers. In Africa, JIT can be challenging because suppliers may be far or unreliable. So you adapt: use a 'JIT with buffer' approach. Keep a small safety stock of critical items, but aim for 3-5 days of inventory instead of 30 days.

A practical JIT tool is the 5S system: Sort, Set in order, Shine, Standardise, Sustain. Sort means remove unnecessary items from the work area. Set in order means put tools where they are needed. Shine means clean. Standardise means create rules. Sustain means keep it up. A bakery in Ghana used 5S to reduce flour waste by 15% in one month. Another tool is value stream mapping. Draw every step from raw material to customer, and mark which steps add value. You will find that 80% of time is waiting or moving. Eliminate those steps. Start small: pick one product line, map it, and remove one waste per week. Train your team to spot waste and suggest improvements. Lean is not about cutting jobs; it is about making work easier and more profitable.

5. Theory of Constraints and Drum-Buffer-Rope

The Theory of Constraints (TOC) says that every system has one bottleneck that limits its output. If you improve anything else, you wont increase total production. The bottleneck is called the drum because it sets the beat for the whole factory. In a furniture workshop, the bottleneck might be the drying oven. If the oven can only dry 20 chairs per day, the whole workshop can only produce 20 chairs per day, no matter how fast the carpenters work.

Drum-Buffer-Rope (DBR) is a scheduling method for TOC. The drum is the bottleneck schedule. You plan production around the bottlenecks capacity. The buffer is a time buffer of inventory before the bottleneck to ensure it never starves. The rope is the release of materials to the start of the line, tied to the bottlenecks pace. For example, if the oven dries 20 chairs per day and takes 2 days to dry a batch, you need to have 40 chairs ready to enter the oven at all times. Thats your buffer. The rope means you only release new wood to the cutting station when the oven has finished a batch. So you dont flood the workshop with work-in-progress.

How to implement DBR: First, identify your bottleneck. Look for the step with the longest queue or the most overtime. Second, exploit the bottleneck: make sure it never stops. Give it its own maintenance, lunch relief, and quality checks. Third, subordinate everything else to the bottleneck. Non-bottleneck machines should work at the bottlenecks pace, not faster. Fourth, elevate the bottleneck: if you still need more capacity, invest in the bottleneck (e.g., a second oven). Fifth, repeat. In an African context, a maize mills bottleneck might be the sifting machine. You can add a buffer of 2 hours of unsifted maize before it. The rope is to only bring maize from the storage when the sifter is ready. This reduces waiting and inventory. Use a simple whiteboard to track the bottlenecks hourly output. If it drops, find out why immediately. Train your team to respect the bottleneck: never let it idle. The goal is to increase throughput, not to keep everyone busy. Measure throughput, inventory, and operating expense. TOC is powerful because it focuses your limited resources on the one thing that matters most.

6. Case Study: Lean Manufacturing at Toyota

Toyota is the birthplace of lean manufacturing. After World War II, Toyota faced a small market and limited resources. They could not afford mass production like Ford. So they created the Toyota Production System (TPS), which focuses on eliminating waste and respecting people. The two pillars of TPS are Just-in-Time and Jidoka (automation with a human touch). Jidoka means that when a problem occurs, the machine stops, and a worker fixes it immediately. This prevents defective products from moving forward.

One famous Toyota tool is the Andon cord. Any worker on the assembly line can pull a cord to stop the line if they see a problem. In many factories, stopping the line is a sin. At Toyota, it is expected. The line stops, the team leader comes, and they solve the root cause. This builds quality into the process. Another tool is the kanban system. Toyota uses cards to signal when to replenish parts. Each card specifies the part number, quantity, and location. When a bin is empty, the card goes back to the previous process to request more. This creates a pull system.

Toyota also uses standardised work. Every task has a defined sequence, takt time (the rate of customer demand), and standard inventory. Takt time = available work time per shift / customer demand per shift. If demand is 400 cars per day and you work 480 minutes, takt time = 480/400 = 1.2 minutes per car. Workers are trained to follow the standard, but they are also encouraged to improve it. This is called kaizen, continuous improvement. Small teams meet regularly to suggest changes.

What can African businesses learn from Toyota? First, you dont need expensive technology. Toyota started with simple tools like andon cords and kanban cards. Second, respect for people is central. Workers are not machines; they are problem solvers. Third, go to the gemba (the actual place where work happens). Managers should spend time on the floor, not in offices. Fourth, solve problems at the root cause using the 5 Whys. Ask 'why' five times to get to the real issue. A Nigerian bottling company applied Toyotas 5S and reduced changeover time from 45 minutes to 15 minutes. A Kenyan textile firm used kanban to cut work-in-progress by 40%. Start small: pick one production line, train your team on 5S and standard work, and celebrate small wins. Lean is a journey, not a destination.

<h2>Practical Reference: Production Planning &amp; Scheduling in African Agribusiness</h2>

<p>Production planning and scheduling is where agribusiness strategy meets the shop floor or, in the African context, the farm gate, the collection centre, the processing plant and the cold chain. This Practical Reference lesson consolidates the tools, benchmarks and rules of thumb you need to move from aggregate plans to daily schedules that actually hold. It is written for managers in milling, beverages, edible oils, dairy, horticulture, poultry and agro-processing who must plan under real constraints: seasonal raw material supply, unreliable grid power, long inland transport corridors and thin working capital.</p>

<p>We treat six planning layers in sequence: aggregate planning and master production scheduling (MPS), material requirements planning (MRP), capacity planning, lean/JIT, Theory of Constraints (drum-buffer-rope), and the Toyota case as a transferable benchmark. Each layer answers a different question <em>how much, what and when, can we, should we, and where is the bottleneck</em>.</p>

<h3>1. Aggregate Planning and Master Production Scheduling</h3>
<p>Aggregate planning sets output, workforce and inventory at the family level over a 618 month horizon. In African agribusiness the binding constraint is usually <strong>raw material availability windows</strong>, not demand. Maize arrives in a 610 week harvest window in Zambia and Tanzania; cashew in Cte d'Ivoire and Tanzania peaks over 812 weeks; milk supply in Kenya follows bimodal rainfall. Your aggregate plan must therefore choose between chase, level and hybrid strategies against a supply calendar, not just a demand calendar.</p>

<p>The MPS then disaggregates families into end items (e.g., 1 kg, 2 kg and 5 kg maize meal packs) with specific due dates, typically weekly over 812 weeks. The MPS is the contract between sales and operations: it is the schedule that MRP explodes and capacity planning validates. Keep a frozen zone of 24 weeks and a firm planned order discipline so that packaging suppliers and shift rosters are not disturbed weekly.</p>

<h3>2. Material Requirements Planning (MRP)</h3>
<p>MRP converts the MPS into time-phased requirements for raw materials, packaging and components using the bill of materials (BOM), inventory records and lead times. The core arithmetic is simple and unforgiving: <strong>Net requirement = Gross requirement On-hand Scheduled receipts + Safety stock</strong>, then offset by lead time to get the planned order release.</p>

<p>Three African realities break textbook MRP: (1) <strong>lead-time variability</strong> a container of PET preforms from Asia may take 3570 days through Mombasa or Durban; (2) <strong>supplier reliability</strong> local packaging and spare-part suppliers may deliver at 7085% on-time; (3) <strong>yield and moisture variation</strong> in raw materials, which changes the effective BOM. Address these with safety stock sized on variability (not a flat 10%), dual sourcing, and periodic BOM yield reviews after each harvest.</p>

<h3>3. Capacity Planning and Management</h3>
<p>Rough-cut capacity planning (RCCP) tests the MPS against key resources mill throughput, filling line speed, boiler capacity, cold room volume, truck fleet. Capacity requirements planning (CRP) then tests the MRP at work-centre level. In African plants, capacity constraints are frequently <strong>utilities and infrastructure</strong> rather than machines: grid outages, water availability, refrigeration, and road access during rains. Build a capacity model that includes power availability (e.g., 85% grid uptime plus generator hours) and treat it as a real constraint, not an assumption.</p>

<p>Levers, in order of preference: (1) smooth the load by shifting MPS dates within the frozen-zone rules; (2) add temporary capacity (overtime, extra shift, toll manufacturing); (3) subcontract; (4) build pre-season inventory; (5) ration supply to priority customers. Always quantify the cost per lever before choosing.</p>

<h3>4. Lean Production and Just-in-Time (JIT)</h3>
<p>JIT means producing to demand, in small batches, with minimal buffers powerful but fragile where supplier reliability and transport are weak. In Africa, adopt <strong>selective JIT</strong>: apply it to items with short, reliable local lead times (fresh milk, local packaging, sugar) and keep strategic buffers on imported or unreliable items. The waste categories of lean overproduction, waiting, transport, over-processing, inventory, motion, defects map directly onto agro-processing losses: moisture shrink, spillage, rework, downtime waiting for spares, and trucks queuing at weighbridges.</p>

<strong style="color:#b00020"> SAFETY &amp; QUALITY WARNING</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0">Do not apply JIT thinking to food safety, aflatoxin control, or cold-chain integrity. Reducing safety stock of vitamin premix, mycotoxin binders, pasteurisation holding time, or cold-room buffer capacity to "cut inventory" can cause regulatory failure, product recall, or consumer harm. In aflatoxin-prone maize and groundnut chains, and in dairy and meat cold chains, buffers exist for <em>safety</em>, not waste. Any JIT or inventory-reduction initiative must be signed off by QA and must not breach HACCP, ISO 22000, KEBS, SABS, SON or equivalent national food safety standards.</p>
</div>

<h3>5. Theory of Constraints and Drum-Buffer-Rope</h3>
<p>Goldratt's Theory of Constraints (TOC) says throughput is set by the system's bottleneck. In agribusiness the bottleneck shifts by season: the dryer or the mill during harvest, the filler during peak demand, the cold room in hot months, the weighbridge during intake. Drum-Buffer-Rope (DBR) operationalises TOC:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Drum</strong> the bottleneck schedule that sets the pace for the whole plant.</li>
<li><strong>Buffer</strong> time or material protection before the bottleneck so it never starves. Size it to absorb real variability, not to look tidy.</li>
<li><strong>Rope</strong> the release signal that prevents upstream work centres from overproducing and choking the bottleneck.</li>
</ul>
<p>Practical steps: identify the constraint; exploit it (no idle time, no bad quality reaching it); subordinate everything else to it; elevate it (add capacity); then repeat, because the constraint moves. In many African mills the constraint is not the machine but <strong>raw material intake quality and moisture</strong> investing in drying and grading often yields more throughput than a new mill.</p>

<h3>6. Case Study: Lean Manufacturing at Toyota</h3>
<p>Toyota's Toyota Production System (TPS) is the reference model for lean: JIT, jidoka (stop and fix), kaizen, heijunka (levelling), standardised work and pull systems via kanban. The transferable lessons for African agribusiness are not the tools but the <strong>management system</strong>: go-and-see at the point of value creation, solve root causes rather than firefight, level the schedule to remove peaks, and give frontline teams authority to stop the line on a defect.</p>
<p>Adaptation examples: a Kenyan dairy applying heijunka to level milk intake across the week; a Tanzanian edible-oil plant using kanban between crushing and refining; a Zambian miller using jidoka principles at the packing line to stop on weight deviations. The warning is equally important: TPS was built on stable demand, reliable suppliers and disciplined maintenance. Where those are absent, sequence the work stabilise supply and maintenance first, then lean.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Plan to the supply calendar, not just demand.</strong> Map harvest windows and rainfall before locking the aggregate plan.</li>
<li><strong>Freeze the MPS for 24 weeks</strong> and manage changes through a formal exception process.</li>
<li><strong>Size safety stock on variability</strong> (lead-time and yield), not a flat percentage; review after each harvest.</li>
<li><strong>Validate every MPS with RCCP</strong> including power, water, cold storage and transport, not only machines.</li>
<li><strong>Find the real bottleneck</strong> each season and run DBR: drum schedule, buffer before it, rope upstream.</li>
<li><strong>Apply selective JIT</strong> short, reliable local items only; never on food-safety-critical materials.</li>
<li><strong>Track OEE, on-time-in-full (OTIF), schedule adherence and inventory days</strong> weekly; act on the worst.</li>
<li><strong>Escalate root causes, not symptoms</strong> use a simple A3 or 5-why at the point of the problem.</li>
</ol>
