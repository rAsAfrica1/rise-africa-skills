1. Spacing recommendations for sorghum: 75cm x 25cm and variations

Spacing is one of the most important decisions you make at planting. For sorghum, the standard recommendation in many African conditions is 75 cm between rows and 25 cm between plants within the row. This gives you about 53,000 plants per hectare. That density works well for tall, open-pollinated varieties and for many improved varieties under rain-fed conditions. If you plant too close, plants compete for water and nutrients, and you get tall, thin stalks with small heads. If you plant too wide, you waste land and get more weeds.

But one spacing does not fit all situations. In drier areas with less than 500 mm annual rainfall, widen the spacing to 75 cm x 30 cm or even 90 cm x 30 cm. This reduces plant population to about 44,000 or 37,000 plants per hectare, giving each plant more soil moisture. In higher rainfall areas or with short, early-maturing varieties, you can tighten to 75 cm x 20 cm (about 66,000 plants/ha) or 60 cm x 25 cm (about 66,000 plants/ha). The key is to match plant population to available water and soil fertility.

When planting, use a rope and pegs to mark rows. A simple string with knots every 75 cm helps you keep rows straight. For within-row spacing, you can use a stick cut to 25 cm. Drop 2-3 seeds per hole and later thin to one plant. If you use a planter, calibrate it to drop the right number of seeds. Always plant on the contour or across the slope if your land is not flat, to reduce erosion.

Remember that sorghum tillers (produces side shoots) when spaced wider. That can be an advantage in good seasons, but in poor seasons it wastes resources. For maximum yield, aim for the recommended spacing for your variety and rainfall zone. Check with your local extension officer for the best spacing in your area. With correct spacing, you give each plant enough room to grow strong and produce a heavy head.

2. Spacing recommendations for millet: 75cm x 20cm and variations

Millet, especially pearl millet, is often planted at 75 cm between rows and 20 cm between plants within the row. This gives about 66,000 plants per hectare. Millet is more drought-tolerant than sorghum, but it still responds well to proper spacing. If you plant too densely, the plants become weak and produce small heads. If you plant too sparsely, you allow weeds to take over and you harvest less grain per hectare.

In the dry Sahel and similar areas with less than 400 mm rainfall, widen the spacing to 90 cm x 30 cm or 100 cm x 30 cm. This reduces plant population to about 37,000 or 33,000 plants per hectare. Wider spacing means each plant has more water and nutrients, which is critical in dry years. In areas with more reliable rainfall (600 mm or more), you can use 75 cm x 15 cm (about 88,000 plants/ha) or 60 cm x 20 cm (about 83,000 plants/ha) for short varieties. For tall, traditional varieties, stick to 75 cm x 20 cm or wider.

Millet is often broadcast-planted in traditional systems, but row planting gives better yields because you can weed and thin easily. Use a hoe or a stick to make shallow furrows 75 cm apart. Drop seeds every 20 cm, then cover lightly with soil. If you plant in hills, put 5-8 seeds per hill and later thin to 2-3 plants per hill. That is equivalent to about 20 cm spacing if hills are 40-50 cm apart within the row.

Spacing also affects light interception and airflow, which reduce disease. In humid areas, wider rows help prevent fungal diseases. Always consider your variety, rainfall, and soil fertility. A good rule: the drier the area, the wider the spacing. With correct spacing, millet can yield 1-2 tonnes per hectare in good conditions, compared to less than 0.5 tonnes when crowded or too sparse.

3. Seed rate calculation per hectare for both crops

Getting the seed rate right saves money and ensures good plant population. Seed rate depends on spacing, seed size, germination percentage, and how many seeds you drop per hole. For sorghum, the 1000-grain weight is about 25-35 grams. If you want 53,000 plants per hectare (75 cm x 25 cm) and you drop 3 seeds per hole, you need about 159,000 seeds per hectare. At 30 g per 1000 seeds, that is 4.77 kg of pure live seed. But seed lots often have 80-90% germination, so divide by 0.85 to get about 5.6 kg per hectare. In practice, farmers use 5-10 kg/ha for sorghum, depending on method.

For millet, the 1000-grain weight is much smaller, about 5-10 grams for pearl millet. At 66,000 plants per hectare (75 cm x 20 cm) and 3 seeds per hole, you need 198,000 seeds. At 8 g per 1000 seeds, that is 1.58 kg of pure live seed. With 85% germination, you need about 1.86 kg/ha. But millet is often planted at higher rates because seeds are small and some are lost to birds or poor placement. A common recommendation is 3-5 kg/ha for row planting, and up to 10 kg/ha if broadcasting.

To calculate your own seed rate: (Desired plant population number of seeds per hole) (germination fraction 1000-grain weight in grams) 1000. For example, for sorghum: (53,000 3) (0.85 30) 1000 = 6.2 kg/ha. Always test germination before planting: count 100 seeds, plant them in a pot or wet cloth, and see how many sprout. If germination is 70%, you need more seed. Also consider seed treatment, which adds a little weight but not much. Buy certified seed when possible, as it has better germination and purity. With correct seed rate, you avoid wasting seed and get the stand you need for a good harvest.

4. Planting depth and seed treatment against termites

Planting depth is critical for germination. For sorghum and millet, plant seeds 2-5 cm deep. In sandy soils, plant deeper (4-5 cm) because the surface dries out quickly. In clay soils, plant shallower (2-3 cm) because heavy soil can prevent emergence if seeds are too deep. A good rule: plant at a depth where the soil is moist. If you plant too shallow, seeds may dry out or be eaten by birds. If too deep, the seedling uses all its energy to reach the surface and may die before emerging. Use a hoe or stick to make a furrow of the right depth, drop seeds, and cover with loose soil. Firm the soil gently with your foot to ensure good seed-soil contact.

Termites are a major problem for sorghum and millet in many parts of Africa, especially in dry areas. They attack seeds and young seedlings, causing gaps in the field. To protect against termites, treat seeds before planting. You can use a seed dressing such as imidacloprid or thiamethoxam, following the label instructions. For organic options, mix wood ash with seeds (one part ash to three parts seed) or coat seeds with a slurry of cow dung and water, then dry. Some farmers use neem seed powder or extract. Another method is to plant with a pinch of sand mixed with wood ash in each hole.

Also, cultural practices help. Plant when the soil is moist but not waterlogged, as termites are less active in wet soil. Keep the field clean of crop residues that harbor termites. Destroy termite mounds near the field. If you have a severe termite problem, consider using a soil insecticide at planting, such as chlorpyrifos, but use it carefully and follow safety precautions. Always wear gloves and a mask when handling treated seeds. Treated seeds should not be eaten or fed to livestock. With proper depth and seed treatment, you can get a uniform stand and protect your crop from termites.

5. Thinning and gap filling after emergence

Thinning and gap filling are simple but powerful practices to maximize yield. After your sorghum or millet emerges, you will often have too many plants per hill because you dropped multiple seeds. If you leave them, they compete and all become weak. Thinning means removing the extra plants so that only the strongest one (or two for millet hills) remains. Do this when plants are 10-15 cm tall, usually 2-3 weeks after planting. For sorghum, thin to one plant per station. For millet, thin to two plants per station if you planted in hills, or one plant if you planted in rows. Use your fingers or a small hoe to carefully pull out the weaker plants, taking care not to disturb the roots of the one you keep. Thinning also reduces competition for water and nutrients, and it allows better airflow, reducing disease.

Gap filling means replanting where seeds did not germinate or where seedlings died. Gaps reduce yield because you have fewer plants than planned. Check your field 1-2 weeks after emergence. Where you find a gap of 30 cm or more, replant with the same variety. Use pre-germinated seeds for faster establishment: soak seeds overnight, then plant them in the gap. Water the replanted spot if possible. If you have extra seedlings from a nursery or from thinned plants, you can transplant them carefully, but sorghum and millet do not transplant as well as some crops. Replanting is usually better. Do gap filling early, within 2 weeks of emergence, so the new plants catch up with the rest.

Thinning and gap filling together ensure you have the right plant population. A uniform stand uses sunlight, water, and nutrients efficiently. It also makes weeding and harvesting easier. If you skip thinning, you may get many small heads instead of fewer large ones. If you skip gap filling, you lose yield from empty spaces. So take the time to walk your field and do these tasks. They are low-cost and can increase your yield by 20-30% or more. Remember, a good stand is the foundation of a good harvest.

6. Using zai pits or planting basins in dry areas

In dry areas with less than 500 mm rainfall, zai pits (also called planting basins or tassa) are a traditional but powerful technique to capture water and improve soil fertility. A zai pit is a small, shallow basin dug into the soil, about 20-30 cm in diameter and 10-15 cm deep. You dig these pits before the rains, usually during the dry season. Space them 75-80 cm apart in rows that are also 75-80 cm apart. That gives about 15,000-20,000 pits per hectare. In each pit, you place a handful of manure or compost, then plant 3-5 seeds of sorghum or millet after the first rains. The pit collects rainwater and keeps it around the roots, so plants survive dry spells.

Zai pits are especially good for degraded, crusted soils where water runs off. The pits break the crust and allow water to infiltrate. The organic matter in the pit feeds the plants and improves soil structure over time. In the Sahel, farmers who use zai pits with manure have increased yields from almost nothing to 500-1000 kg/ha even in dry years. You can also add a small amount of wood ash or termite soil to the pit to deter termites and add nutrients. After planting, you may need to protect the pits from animals with thorn branches until plants are established.

Maintenance: Each year, you can reuse the same pits, adding fresh manure. Over time, the soil in and around the pits becomes more fertile. If you have a plow, you can make ridges and plant on the ridge, but zai pits are better for dry, flat land. They are labor-intensive to dig, but the payoff is worth it. You can also use a hoe to make planting basins in a grid. For millet, you can plant in the same pits. In very dry areas, combine zai pits with mulching to reduce evaporation. With zai pits, you turn a dry, barren field into a productive one. It is a climate-smart technique that has been proven across Africa.

7. Practical Reference

<section>
<h2>Practical Reference: Planting Techniques for Maximum Yield in Sorghum &amp; Millet</h2>

<p>Getting planting right is the single cheapest yield lever a smallholder or emerging commercial grain farmer has. Unlike fertiliser, hybrid seed or mechanisation, correct spacing, seed rate, depth and establishment cost almost nothing extra yet they routinely separate a 0.8 t/ha sorghum crop from a 2.5 t/ha crop on the same rainfall. For aggregators, contract-farming schemes and grain traders working with outgrowers, standardising these parameters across a farmer base is also the fastest way to reduce variability in delivered volumes and quality. This lesson gives the field-ready numbers for sorghum (<em>Sorghum bicolor</em>) and pearl millet (<em>Pennisetum glaucum</em>), plus the dryland establishment techniques zai pits and planting basins that de-risk early season moisture stress across the Sahel, East African ASALs and southern African drylands.</p>

<h3>1. Spacing Recommendations</h3>

<h4>Sorghum</h4>
<p>The regional standard for medium-to-tall sorghum in rainfed systems is <strong>75 cm between rows 25 cm between plants</strong>, giving approximately 53,000 plants per hectare. This is the spacing promoted across ICRISAT and national extension services in the semi-arid tropics because it balances light interception, air movement (reducing foliar disease) and machine/hand access for weeding.</p>
<p>Variations matter by agro-ecology and end-use:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Dwarf/short hybrids under high input:</strong> tighten to 60 cm 20 cm (~83,000 plants/ha) to exploit yield response to fertiliser.</li>
<li><strong>Tall, late-maturing landraces in low-rainfall areas:</strong> widen to 90 cm 30 cm (~37,000 plants/ha) to reduce competition for stored soil moisture.</li>
<li><strong>Intercropping with cowpea or groundnut:</strong> use 90 cm 25 cm and place the legume in the inter-row to preserve sorghum plant population.</li>
<li><strong>Mechanised row-crop (e.g. Botswana, Zambia, Zimbabwe):</strong> match 75 cm rows to standard 75 cm planter/row-crop tractor wheel spacing for herbicide and cultivator compatibility.</li>
</ul>

<h4>Millet (Pearl Millet)</h4>
<p>The standard recommendation is <strong>75 cm between rows 20 cm between plants</strong>, giving approximately 66,000 plants per hectare. Pearl millet tillers heavily, so it compensates well for gaps but excessive density in low-rainfall years causes terminal drought stress and sterile tillers.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Low rainfall (&lt;400 mm):</strong> widen to 90 cm 20 cm (~55,000 plants/ha) or 75 cm 30 cm to conserve moisture.</li>
<li><strong>Higher rainfall / high-input hybrids:</strong> 60 cm 15 cm (~110,000 plants/ha) is used in some Indian and West African hybrid systems.</li>
<li><strong>Finger millet (where grown):</strong> typically 30 cm 10 cm on ridges or 22.5 cm rows in East Africa a different geometry from pearl millet.</li>
</ul>

<h3>3. Planting Depth and Seed Treatment Against Termites</h3>
<p>Depth is a moisture-management decision. Too shallow and the seed dries out or is taken by birds; too deep and the seedling exhausts its reserves before emergence.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Sorghum:</strong> 35 cm on sandy soils, 2.54 cm on heavier soils. In dry sowing, place at 56 cm to reach moisture.</li>
<li><strong>Pearl millet:</strong> 23 cm on light soils, up to 4 cm in very dry conditions. Millet has weaker coleoptile vigour than sorghum do not bury it deep.</li>
<li>Always sow into moist soil; if dry-sowing, use a firm, moist seedbed and consider a light compaction after planting to improve capillary contact.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Termite protection.</strong> Termites (<em>Macrotermes</em>, <em>Odontotermes</em>, <em>Microtermes</em> spp.) are the number-one establishment pest for sorghum and millet in dryland Africa, killing germinating seed and young seedlings. Practical, legal options include:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Seed dressing:</strong> registered neonicotinoid or fipronil-based seed treatments (e.g. imidacloprid or thiamethoxam formulations approved for sorghum/millet in-country). Follow label rate exactly typically 35 ml formulation per kg seed.</li>
<li><strong>Botanical/low-cost options:</strong> wood ash or neem (<em>Azadirachta indica</em>) seed powder dusted in the planting hole; effectiveness is partial and best used where synthetic dressings are unavailable or unaffordable.</li>
<li><strong>Cultural control:</strong> destroy old sorghum/millet stubble, termite mounds and crop residues near the field before planting; sow early with the first reliable rains so seedlings establish before termite activity peaks.</li>
<li><strong>Resistant/tolerant varieties:</strong> select improved varieties with documented termite tolerance where available from national programs.</li>
</ul>

<h3>4. Thinning and Gap Filling After Emergence</h3>
<p>Over-sowing is deliberate insurance against poor emergence but it must be corrected or plants compete for water and nutrients. Thinning is one of the highest-return, lowest-cost operations in dryland cereal production.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Timing:</strong> thin at 1015 cm plant height (roughly 23 weeks after emergence), when plants are large enough to handle but before competition sets in.</li>
<li><strong>Sorghum:</strong> thin to one plant per station at 25 cm (or 20 cm for dwarf hybrids).</li>
<li><strong>Millet:</strong> thin to one plant per station at 20 cm; where tillering is strong, two plants per station can be left in higher-rainfall areas.</li>
<li><strong>Gap filling:</strong> replant gaps larger than 30 cm within 710 days of first emergence using the same variety and pre-germinated or soaked seed. After this window, transplanting or leaving the gap is usually better than late replanting, which produces immature, unmarketable grain.</li>
<li><strong>Use thinnings:</strong> in millet, thinned seedlings can be transplanted into gaps if soil is moist a cheap way to recover plant population.</li>
</ul>

<h3>5. Zai Pits and Planting Basins in Dry Areas</h3>
<p>In the Sahel and similar low, erratic-rainfall zones, conventional ploughing loses water and exposes soil to crusting. <strong>Zai pits</strong> (also called tassa in Niger) and larger <strong>planting basins</strong> are water-harvesting micro-catchments that concentrate runoff, organic matter and seed in one spot.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Zai dimensions:</strong> typically 2030 cm diameter, 1015 cm deep, spaced 80 cm 80 cm (about 15,000 pits/ha). Larger basins of 3040 cm diameter are used for sorghum in higher-rainfall Sahelian zones.</li>
<li><strong>Construction:</strong> dig during the dry season. Place 12 handfuls of manure or compost in each pit and mix with soil. The pit captures runoff from the surrounding crusted surface.</li>
<li><strong>Planting:</strong> sow 35 seeds per pit at the first effective rain, then thin to 23 plants per pit. This gives roughly 30,00045,000 plants/ha comparable to conventional spacing but with far better moisture per plant.</li>
<li><strong>Yield evidence:</strong> zai systems have been documented to raise sorghum and millet yields by 30100% in Sahelian conditions compared with flat planting, especially in dry years, and to rehabilitate degraded land.</li>
<li><strong>Labour:</strong> zai is labour-intensive (roughly 100150 person-hours/ha to dig), so it suits smallholder plots, rehabilitation of hardpan/degraded land, and where labour is available in the dry season. For larger areas, half-moon or contour bund basins are more scalable.</li>
</ul>
