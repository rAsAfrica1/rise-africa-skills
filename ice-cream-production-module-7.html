Lessons

1. Principles of Dynamic Freezing in Continuous Freezers

In continuous freezers, ice cream mix is frozen dynamically as it is pumped through a barrel while a dasher scrapes the frozen mix off the walls. This rapid freezing creates small ice crystals, giving a smooth texture. The mix enters at about 4C and exits at -6C to -8C, with about 50% of the water frozen. The dasher turns at 100-200 RPM, incorporating air to control overrun. A typical continuous freezer can produce 100-500 liters per hour, depending on model. In Africa, where ambient temperatures can exceed 35C, pre-cooling the mix to 4C before freezing is crucial to reduce strain on the freezer. Use a glycol chiller or cold room to store mix. The freezer barrel is cooled by refrigerant (e.g., R404A) to -25C to -30C. The mix is pumped under pressure (2-4 bar) and exits through a draw valve. Overrun is controlled by adjusting the air inlet valve and the mix pump speed. For small-scale production, a continuous freezer like the Carpigiani 191 can produce 30 liters per hour. Always monitor the draw temperature with a thermometer; if it's too warm, the ice cream will be soft and melt quickly. If too cold, it may be crumbly and hard to scoop. Regular cleaning of the dasher and barrel prevents buildup that can cause inconsistent freezing. In hot climates, consider insulating the freezer and using a generator to avoid power outages that can ruin a batch.

2. Batch Freezing for Artisanal Production

Batch freezers freeze one batch of mix at a time, typically 5-20 liters, making them ideal for small-scale artisanal ice cream makers in Africa. The mix is poured into a cylindrical bowl, and a dasher scrapes the frozen mix from the walls while incorporating air. Batch freezing takes 20-40 minutes, depending on the mix composition and desired overrun. For a 10-liter batch, you might get 15-20 liters of finished ice cream if you aim for 50% overrun. The key is to start with a cold mix (4C) and pre-freeze the bowl if possible. In hot climates, work in an air-conditioned room or early morning to reduce melting. Use a thermometer to check the draw temperature; ideal is -6C to -8C. If the batch takes too long, the ice cream may be icy; if too fast, it may be soft. The dasher speed is usually fixed, but you can control overrun by the amount of mix and the time churning. For artisanal production, you can add inclusions like nuts or fruit in the last 2 minutes of freezing. After freezing, transfer to a pre-chilled container and harden in a freezer at -18C for at least 2 hours. Batch freezers are affordable (e.g., a 10-liter model costs $1,500-$3,000) and easy to maintain. Clean the bowl and dasher immediately after use to prevent dairy buildup. In areas with unreliable electricity, use a generator or freeze during cooler hours. Batch freezing gives you flexibility to experiment with local flavors like mango, baobab, or hibiscus.

3. Overrun: Definition, Measurement, and Control

Overrun is the amount of air incorporated into ice cream, expressed as a percentage of the volume of the mix. For example, if 1 liter of mix becomes 1.5 liters of ice cream, overrun is 50%. Overrun affects texture, cost, and scoopability. Too little overrun (less than 30%) makes ice cream dense and heavy; too much (over 100%) makes it fluffy and airy, melting quickly. Commercial ice cream typically has 50-100% overrun. To measure overrun, weigh a fixed volume of mix (e.g., 1 liter) and the same volume of finished ice cream. Use the formula: Overrun % = (Weight of mix - Weight of ice cream) / Weight of ice cream 100. For accuracy, use a digital scale and a 1-liter container. In Africa, where ingredients can be costly, controlling overrun helps maximize profit. To increase overrun, increase dasher speed, reduce mix pump speed, or open the air inlet valve more. To decrease overrun, do the opposite. In batch freezers, overrun is controlled by the amount of mix and churning time; more mix and longer churning can increase overrun. For continuous freezers, adjust the air valve and pump settings. Keep records of overrun for each batch to maintain consistency. High overrun is not always better; for premium ice cream, aim for 30-50% overrun for a rich, creamy texture. In hot climates, lower overrun (30-40%) helps the ice cream resist melting. Always taste and adjust: if it feels too airy, reduce overrun next time.

4. Effect of Draw Temperature on Texture and Overrun

Draw temperature is the temperature of the ice cream as it exits the freezer. It directly affects texture, overrun, and scoopability. Ideal draw temperature is -6C to -8C for batch freezers and -5C to -7C for continuous freezers. If the draw temperature is too warm (above -5C), the ice cream will be soft, melt quickly, and may have poor overrun because air escapes easily. If too cold (below -9C), it becomes hard, crumbly, and difficult to scoop, and overrun may be low because the mix freezes too fast, trapping less air. In hot African climates, aim for the colder end of the range to prevent rapid melting. To control draw temperature, adjust the refrigerant temperature or the mix flow rate. In continuous freezers, the draw temperature is set by the refrigerant pressure and the speed of the mix pump. In batch freezers, it depends on the bowl temperature and churning time. Use a probe thermometer to check the temperature of the ice cream as it comes out. If it's too warm, continue churning for a few more minutes; if too cold, add a little warm mix or reduce churning time. Overrun is also affected: warmer draw temperatures tend to give higher overrun because air is easier to incorporate, but the texture suffers. Colder draw temperatures give lower overrun but a denser, creamier product. Find the balance for your recipe and market. Record draw temperature for each batch and adjust as needed. In power outages, draw temperature can rise; have a backup plan.

5. Incorporating Inclusions During Freezing

Inclusions are solid pieces like nuts, chocolate chips, fruit, or cookies added to ice cream. They add texture, flavor, and visual appeal. The key is to add them at the right time and in the right way to avoid damage or clumping. In batch freezers, add inclusions in the last 2 minutes of freezing, after the ice cream has reached about -5C. If added too early, they can be crushed by the dasher or make the ice cream hard to churn. For continuous freezers, use a fruit feeder or ingredient feeder that drops inclusions into the stream as the ice cream exits the barrel. The inclusions should be cold (refrigerated or frozen) to prevent melting the ice cream. Chop nuts and fruits into small, uniform pieces (about 0.5-1 cm) so they distribute evenly and don't clog the feeder. For chocolate chips, use chips that are designed for ice cream (they resist melting). In Africa, popular inclusions include roasted groundnuts, cashews, dried mango, coconut flakes, and crushed biscuits. Add inclusions at a rate of 10-20% of the total volume; too many can make the ice cream difficult to scoop and may reduce overrun. Mix inclusions gently by hand if you don't have a feeder. After adding, churn for only 30 seconds to distribute. Then transfer to containers and harden. Always taste a sample to ensure even distribution. If inclusions are too hard, they can damage the dasher; if too soft, they may disintegrate. Experiment with local ingredients to create unique flavors.

6. Troubleshooting Freezing Issues: Hardness, Iciness, and Air Pockets

Common freezing issues include hardness, iciness, and air pockets. Hardness: If ice cream is too hard to scoop, it may have been frozen too long or at too low a temperature. Check draw temperature; it should be -6C to -8C. If it's below -9C, adjust freezer settings. Also, high overrun can make it hard when frozen solid; reduce overrun. In batch freezers, don't over-churn. Iciness: Large ice crystals form when freezing is slow or when the mix is warm. Ensure mix is pre-cooled to 4C and freeze quickly. Use a stabilizer like gelatin or guar gum (0.1-0.3%) to reduce ice crystal growth. In hot climates, work in a cool room. If ice cream is icy, it may also be due to low overrun; increase overrun to mask iciness. Air pockets: These occur when air is not properly incorporated or when the ice cream is not packed tightly. In batch freezers, air pockets can form if the dasher is not scraping properly or if the mix is too viscous. In continuous freezers, check the air valve and pump settings. After freezing, pack ice cream firmly into containers, pressing out air pockets. Use a spatula to press down. If air pockets persist, check for leaks in the freezer or worn dasher blades. For all issues, keep records of settings, temperatures, and results. Adjust one variable at a time. In Africa, power fluctuations can cause inconsistent freezing; use a voltage stabilizer. Regular maintenance of freezers (cleaning, lubricating) prevents many issues. Train staff to recognize signs early.

<h2>Practical Reference: Freezing and Overrun for African Ice Cream Businesses</h2>

<p>Freezing is where your recipe, your money, and your reputation meet. A mix that costs good money can be ruined in minutes by poor freezing producing icy, hard, airy, or inconsistent product that customers reject and retailers return. For African ice cream businesses working with unreliable power, high ambient temperatures, and costly imported ingredients, mastering freezing and overrun is not a technical nicety. It is a direct lever on margin, shelf life, and brand trust.</p>

<p>This Practical Reference consolidates the six lessons in this module into a working guide. Use it on the production floor, in staff training, and when troubleshooting customer complaints. All temperatures are in C. Where local conditions (ambient heat, voltage fluctuation, water hardness) differ from temperate-market assumptions, notes are provided.</p>

<h3>1. Dynamic Freezing in Continuous Freezers</h3>
<p>Continuous freezers (also called continuous scraped-surface freezers) freeze mix under pressure while a dasher scrapes ice crystals from the cylinder wall. The result is rapid nucleation, many small crystals, and controlled air incorporation. Key operating variables are mix flow rate, dasher speed, refrigerant temperature, back pressure, and draw temperature.</p>
<p>For African producers running continuous lines, voltage stability is the single biggest hidden variable. A 10% voltage drop slows the compressor, raises draw temperature, and increases overrun unpredictably. Install a voltage stabiliser or AVR on the freezer circuit and log draw temperature every 15 minutes. If you cannot hold draw temperature within 1 C, your overrun and texture will drift batch to batch.</p>

<h3>2. Batch Freezing for Artisanal Production</h3>
<p>Batch freezers suit small African producers making premium or local-flavour lines (baobab, hibiscus, tamarind, coconut, moringa, groundnut). They freeze one load at a time in a horizontal cylinder with a dasher and scraper blades. Cycle time is typically 1530 minutes depending on mix, ambient temperature, and machine load.</p>
<p>Batch freezing is more forgiving on power interruptions than continuous freezing but less consistent. To manage this, standardise: same mix batch size, same mix temperature (4 C), same dasher speed, same ambient (use a fan if room exceeds 30 C), and same draw point. Record each batch's cycle time. A sudden increase in cycle time signals refrigerant loss, worn blades, or mix formulation drift.</p>

<h3>3. Overrun: Definition, Measurement, and Control</h3>
<p>Overrun is the percentage increase in volume of finished ice cream compared to the unfrozen mix volume, caused by incorporated air. It is the single most important commercial variable in ice cream: it determines portion yield, texture, meltdown, and perceived value.</p>
<p><strong>Formula:</strong> Overrun % = [(Weight of mix Weight of same volume of ice cream) Weight of ice cream] 100.</p>
<p>Practical floor method: fill a fixed-volume cup (e.g., 500 ml) with mix and weigh it. Then fill the same cup with frozen ice cream from the draw point and weigh it. Apply the formula. Repeat three times per batch.</p>

<h3>4. Effect of Draw Temperature on Texture and Overrun</h3>
<p>Draw temperature is the temperature of ice cream as it leaves the freezer. It controls ice crystal size, air cell size, and how easily the product can be packaged and hardened.</p>

<p>In African conditions, aim for the colder end of your target range (6 to 7 C) if your hardening tunnel or blast freezer is weak or intermittent. Colder draw gives you a buffer against ambient heat and slow hardening.</p>

<h3>5. Incorporating Inclusions During Freezing</h3>
<p>Inclusions fruit pieces, nuts, chocolate, biscuit, local ingredients like dried mango, coconut flakes, roasted groundnut must be added at the correct point. Add too early and they are crushed by the dasher; add too late and they are not evenly distributed.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Continuous freezers:</strong> use a fruit feeder or injection pump set to 815% of total volume. Calibrate weekly.</li>
<li><strong>Batch freezers:</strong> add inclusions in the last 12 minutes of the cycle, after overrun has developed, at low dasher speed.</li>
<li><strong>Moisture control:</strong> fruit must be drained, sugared, or partially dried. Wet fruit introduces free water that becomes ice crystals and can also carry spoilage organisms.</li>
<li><strong>Size:</strong> pieces 48 mm for retail tubs; smaller for soft-serve and cups.</li>
</ul>

<strong style="color:#c0392b;"> SAFETY WARNING Ammonia, refrigerant, and cold burns</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Ice cream freezers use refrigerants (often ammonia in larger plants, or HFCs in small units). Ammonia is toxic and flammable in concentration; HFCs displace oxygen in enclosed spaces. Never open a refrigerant circuit yourself. Only certified technicians may service the system. Always wear insulated gloves when handling cold metal parts, dry ice, or product straight from the freezer cold burns occur within seconds at 20 C and below. In African plants with unreliable ventilation, ensure the freezer room has cross-ventilation and a refrigerant leak alarm if ammonia is used. Keep a written emergency procedure and train all staff on it. Also: never block freezer ventilation grilles, and never bypass voltage protection devices electrical fires are a real risk in plants with unstable grid supply.</p>
</div>

<h3>6. Troubleshooting Freezing Issues</h3>
<p><strong>Hardness (product too hard):</strong> usually low overrun, low mix solids (fat, MSNF, sugar), draw temperature too low, or over-hardening. Check overrun first, then mix formulation, then hardening time and temperature.</p>
<p><strong>Iciness (coarse crystals):</strong> slow freezing, warm draw temperature, temperature fluctuations during storage, or low stabiliser. In African markets, the most common cause is a weak or intermittent cold chain product partially melts and refreezes, growing crystals. Fix the cold chain before changing the recipe.</p>
<p><strong>Air pockets (large voids):</strong> poorly dispersed air, overrun too high, dasher speed too low, or inclusions added too early. Reduce overrun, increase dasher speed, and check the air metering system.</p>
<p><strong>Sandiness (lactose crystallisation):</strong> too much MSNF, slow freezing, or warm storage. Reduce milk powder, draw colder, and store below 18 C.</p>
<p><strong>Shrinkage (product pulls away from tub):</strong> typically caused by temperature fluctuation and air cell collapse. Stabilise storage temperature.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Verify mix temperature is 4 C or below before freezing.</li>
<li>Record draw temperature every 15 minutes (continuous) or every batch (batch freezer); target 5 to 7 C.</li>
<li>Measure overrun at least once per batch using the weight-of-fixed-volume method.</li>
<li>Confirm inclusions are drained, correctly sized (48 mm), and added at the right stage.</li>
<li>Check voltage stabiliser is functioning; log any grid interruptions.</li>
<li>Inspect dasher blades weekly for wear; replace when scraping is uneven.</li>
<li>Move product to hardening within 15 minutes of draw; harden to 18 C or below as fast as possible.</li>
<li>Record every batch: mix ID, draw temp, overrun, cycle time, and any deviation. Review weekly.</li>
</ol>
