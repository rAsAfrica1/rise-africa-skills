1. Surface Preparation in Tropical Climates

Welcome to Module 6, Lesson 1. In this lesson we focus on the single most important factor that determines whether a paint job lasts two years or ten: surface preparation. Across Africa, from the humid coastal cities of Lagos, Accra, Mombasa and Dar es Salaam to the hot dry interiors of the Sahel, the condition of the substrate decides your reputation and your repeat business. A painter who skips preparation is not a painter; he is a gambler. Your clients in Africa are increasingly educated about finishes, and they will judge you by how the job looks after one rainy season, not on the day you collect your final payment. So treat preparation as the product you sell, not the chore before the product. Start every job with a proper assessment. Walk the site with the client and note the substrate type: cement render, sand cement block, plasterboard, timber, metal, or previously painted surfaces. Note the age of the building, the presence of rising damp, salt efflorescence, mould, algae, cracks, and loose or flaking paint. In coastal West and East Africa, salt-laden air accelerates corrosion on metal and causes paint to chalk and blister. In inland areas with heavy seasonal rains, water ingress around windows, parapets and roof edges is the number one cause of paint failure. In dusty northern regions, fine dust settles into porous surfaces and prevents adhesion unless removed. Your assessment should be written down, photographed, and priced. Never give a verbal quote without a written scope. For new cement render, the critical issue is alkalinity and moisture. Fresh cement is highly alkaline and can burn or saponify oil-based paints. It must cure for at least four to six weeks in dry weather, longer in the humid south. Test moisture with a simple moisture meter or the plastic sheet method: tape a square of clear polythene to the wall, leave it overnight, and check for condensation. If moisture is present, do not paint. Treat the surface with an alkali-resisting primer or a dilute acid wash followed by thorough rinsing, then allow to dry. Fill hairline cracks with a flexible filler, not brittle plaster, because thermal movement in African climates is significant. For previously painted surfaces, the rule is simple: remove anything loose. Scrape, wire-brush, and sand back to a sound edge. Feather the edges so the repair disappears under the new coating. Wash down with a mild detergent solution to remove dust, grease, mould and salt. In coastal areas, use a fungicidal wash to kill mould spores, then rinse and dry. Do not paint over mould; it will grow through the film. For metal surfaces, remove rust to bare metal using wire brushes, sandpaper or a grinder, then apply a rust-inhibiting primer within hours, before flash rust forms. In humid conditions, this window is short, so plan your work in dry morning hours. For timber, sand with the grain, fill nail holes with wood filler, and prime knots with a shellac-based knotting solution to stop resin bleeding. In many African markets, local hardwoods such as mahogany, iroko and teak are common; they are oily, so use a suitable primer and avoid water-based products directly on bare oily timber. Preparation also includes protection. Mask windows, doors, floors and furniture with polythene and masking tape. In dusty environments, dampen floors before sweeping to control dust. Set up your work area so that you can move efficiently and leave the site clean each day. Clients remember cleanliness as much as colour. Finally, document your preparation. Take before, during and after photographs. This protects you if a client later claims the paint failed. It also becomes powerful marketing material for your business. In Africa, word of mouth is the strongest advertising, and a portfolio of well-prepared, long-lasting jobs will win you contracts in schools, hospitals, hotels, churches, mosques and private homes. Remember, preparation is not a cost to cut when a client pushes for a lower price. Explain the consequences clearly: a cheaper job that fails in one season costs more to repair than a properly prepared job done once. Offer a written guarantee on your preparation and application. That confidence closes sales. In the next lesson we will look at choosing the right paint systems for African conditions. Before you move on, complete this practical task: visit a local building, assess its surface condition, and write a one-page preparation plan with materials, labour time and price. This is exactly what you will do on real jobs.

2. Selecting Paint Systems for African Conditions

Welcome to Lesson 2 of Module 6. Now that you understand surface preparation, we turn to choosing the right paint system. A paint system is not just a colour; it is a combination of primer, undercoat and topcoat designed for a specific substrate and environment. In Africa, the range of climates is enormous, and a system that works in cool highland Nairobi may fail in hot, humid Mombasa or in the dry, dusty conditions of Kano. Your job as a professional is to match the system to the situation, and to explain your choices to the client in plain language. Start with the substrate. For interior cement and plaster walls in homes, schools and offices, a good system is an alkali-resisting primer followed by one or two coats of a quality acrylic emulsion. Acrylic emulsions are breathable, low odour, quick drying and easy to recoat. In humid coastal areas, choose a washable or anti-mould emulsion for kitchens, bathrooms and clinics. For exteriors, you need a system that resists UV, rain and algae. Exterior acrylic or acrylic-copolymer paints are the workhorse of African exteriors. They adhere well to cement, resist chalking and can be cleaned. In very hot, sunny regions, light colours reflect heat and reduce thermal stress on the wall; dark colours absorb heat and can cause premature failure, especially on south and west facing walls. Explain this to clients who want a black or dark blue exterior in a hot climate. For metal roofs, gutters, gates and window frames, use a zinc-rich or red oxide primer followed by a gloss or satin enamel, or a direct-to-metal acrylic for light-duty work. In coastal areas, specify a system with good salt resistance and recoat according to the manufacturer's data sheet. For timber doors, windows and fascia, use an aluminium wood primer or a suitable solvent-based primer, then an undercoat and two topcoats of gloss or satin. In high-rainfall areas, pay special attention to end grain and joints, which absorb water. For floors, use two-pack epoxy or polyurethane coatings in garages, workshops, hospitals and commercial kitchens. These are more expensive but far more durable than single-pack floor paints. For roofs, consider reflective or heat-reflective coatings, which are increasingly popular in Africa because they reduce indoor temperatures and air-conditioning costs. When selecting brands, do not simply choose the cheapest. In many African markets there are genuine international brands, regional manufacturers and local products of varying quality. Read the technical data sheet. Check the volume solids, drying time, recoat time, coverage and recommended film thickness. A paint with low volume solids may need more coats, which erases the apparent saving. Buy from authorised dealers to avoid counterfeit or expired products, which are a real problem in some markets. Check batch numbers and dates. Store paint correctly: away from direct sun, in a cool dry place, with lids tightly closed. In hot climates, paint can skin over or thicken in the tin. Stir thoroughly before use and never thin unless the data sheet allows it. Over-thinning is one of the most common causes of poor coverage and early failure. Colour choice is a business decision as well as an aesthetic one. In Africa, colours carry cultural meaning and personal pride. Work with the client to select colours that suit the architecture, the light and the function of the space. Use colour cards and, where possible, apply sample patches on the actual wall. A colour that looks good in a shop under fluorescent light can look completely different in strong tropical sunlight. Sample patches also help the client commit, which reduces disputes later. Consider the sheen level. Matt finishes hide imperfections but are harder to clean. Silk, satin and semi-gloss are easier to clean and are better for high-traffic areas, corridors, hospitals and schools. Gloss is durable and washable but shows surface defects. Match sheen to function. Also consider drying and recoat times in your scheduling. In humid conditions, water-based paints may take longer to dry. In hot dry conditions, they may dry too fast, causing lap marks. Work in manageable sections and maintain a wet edge. For large commercial jobs, calculate quantities carefully. Measure the area, subtract windows and doors, and use the coverage figure on the data sheet. Add a contingency of ten to fifteen percent. Under-ordering causes delays and colour mismatch between batches; over-ordering ties up your cash. Finally, build relationships with suppliers. Reliable suppliers give you technical support, credit terms and consistent stock. In many African cities, the painting contractor who knows his suppliers well can win contracts because he can guarantee delivery on time. Your ability to specify and supply the right system, not just labour, is what moves you from a handyman to a professional decorating business. 3. Application Techniques and Quality Control

Welcome to Lesson 3. Good preparation and the right paint system will still fail if application is poor. In this lesson we cover practical application techniques for African conditions and the quality control that protects your reputation and your profit. Before you start, check the weather. Do not paint exteriors when rain is imminent, when the wall is hot from direct sun, or when strong wind will blow dust and spray onto wet paint. In many parts of Africa, the best exterior painting hours are early morning and late afternoon. In humid coastal areas, avoid painting when relative humidity is very high, because water-based paints may not cure properly. In dusty areas, dampen the ground and avoid working in windy conditions. Always follow the manufacturer's instructions on thinning, pot life, induction time for two-pack products, and recoat windows. For brush work, use quality brushes sized to the job. A two-inch to three-inch brush for cutting in and a wider brush for large flat areas. Load the brush about one third of the bristle length and apply in a systematic pattern: first cut in the edges, then fill the middle. Use long, even strokes and finish with light, upward strokes to lay off the paint and avoid brush marks. For rollers, choose the correct sleeve: short pile for smooth surfaces, medium pile for light texture, long pile for rough render or brick. Use a roller frame with a good bearing and a pole for ceilings and high walls. Keep a wet edge. Paint in sections no wider than you can complete before the edge dries. In hot, dry conditions, this may mean smaller sections and faster work. For spray application, you need proper training and protective equipment. Spraying is fast and gives a uniform finish, especially on large commercial jobs, but it requires masking, ventilation and careful control to avoid overspray on cars, windows and neighbouring property. Use the correct tip size and pressure for the product. Keep the gun perpendicular to the surface and maintain a consistent distance. In Africa, spraying is increasingly common on estates, hotels and schools, so learning this skill increases your earning power. Quality control starts with the first coat. Check for missed areas, runs, sags, drips and dry spray. Correct defects while the paint is still workable. Between coats, inspect the surface under good light. Use a torch or work light at a low angle to reveal imperfections. Sand lightly between coats where the data sheet recommends it, and dust off thoroughly. Do not apply the next coat until the previous one is properly dry. Recoat too early and you trap solvent or water, causing blistering and poor adhesion. Recoat too late and you may need to sand for intercoat adhesion. Keep a daily log of products used, batch numbers, weather conditions, areas painted and any issues. This log is your evidence if a dispute arises. It also helps you estimate future jobs more accurately. Protect your work. Use dust sheets, masking tape and polythene. In occupied buildings, work room by room and clean as you go. In hospitals, schools and offices, schedule work to minimise disruption. Many African clients will award repeat contracts based on how little mess you made, not just how good the paint looks. Safety is part of quality. Use ladders and scaffolding correctly. In many African markets, scaffolding is often improvised; do not take risks. Use harnesses for high work, and ensure ladders are on firm, level ground. Keep paints and solvents away from children and open flames. Provide gloves, eye protection and dust masks. For solvent-based products, ensure good ventilation. For two-pack products, understand the hardener ratio and pot life; mixing errors cause failures. Dispose of waste responsibly. Do not pour paint or solvents into drains or on the ground. In many countries, environmental regulations are tightening, and responsible disposal is part of being a professional. Train your team. A painter who has been taught properly will produce consistent work and waste less material. Hold a short toolbox talk each morning: what is being painted, what product, what technique, what safety issue. This five-minute habit improves quality and reduces accidents. Finally, measure your productivity. Record how many square metres you and your team complete per day for different surfaces and products. This data lets you price accurately and win profitable contracts. In the next lesson we will cover pricing, quotations and running a profitable painting business in Africa.

4. Pricing, Quotations and Business Management

Welcome to Lesson 4. Many skilled painters in Africa fail not because they cannot paint, but because they cannot price or manage a business. This lesson gives you a practical framework for pricing, quoting and running a profitable decorating enterprise. Start with your costs. Divide them into direct costs and overheads. Direct costs include labour, paint, primers, fillers, sandpaper, masking tape, brushes, rollers, thinners, fuel and transport. Overheads include rent for storage, tools replacement, insurance, phone and data, marketing, vehicle maintenance, and your own salary. If you do not include overheads, you are not running a business; you are subsidising clients. Calculate your labour rate carefully. Decide a fair daily rate for yourself and your team, based on skill and local market conditions. Then estimate labour time from your productivity records. For example, if you know you can prepare and paint twenty square metres of interior wall per day with two coats, you can calculate labour days for a given area. Add a contingency of ten to fifteen percent for unexpected repairs, difficult access or weather delays. Measure the job properly. Do not guess. Use a laser measure or a tape. Measure wall areas, ceilings, doors, windows, trim and metalwork separately, because they have different labour and paint requirements. Count the number of coats required. A common mistake is to quote for one coat and then discover the client expects two or three. Write down the scope clearly: surfaces included, preparation level, products and brands, number of coats, colours, and exclusions. Your quotation should be professional. Use your business name, logo if you have one, contact details, date, quotation number, client name and site address, detailed scope, price, payment terms, validity period and signature line. In many African markets, clients respect a printed or neatly typed quotation far more than a verbal estimate. It also protects you legally. Payment terms are critical. Ask for a deposit to cover materials, commonly thirty to fifty percent. Do not buy materials from your own pocket for a large job unless you have a signed agreement and a deposit. Progress payments can be tied to milestones: preparation complete, first coat complete, final coat complete. Retain a small percentage until the client signs off. This encourages prompt final payment. Be clear about variations. If the client asks for extra work or changes colour after work has started, issue a written variation with a price before doing the work. Verbal variations are the source of most disputes. In Africa, where relationships matter, it can feel awkward to insist on paperwork, but a polite written confirmation protects the relationship by preventing misunderstandings. Manage your cash flow. Painting businesses often fail because they are owed money. Keep a simple record of invoices, payments and outstanding balances. Follow up politely but firmly. Offer mobile money, bank transfer and cash options, and issue receipts. Keep business money separate from personal money. Even a simple notebook or a mobile app can transform your control. Build a client base. In Africa, referrals are gold. Ask satisfied clients for a testimonial and for permission to photograph the work. Use WhatsApp, Facebook, Instagram and local radio or community groups to showcase before and after photos. Partner with architects, quantity surveyors, estate agents, property managers, hotels, schools and churches. These partners can give you steady work if you deliver on time and on budget. Train and retain good staff. Pay fairly and on time. Provide basic safety equipment and tools. A loyal team produces better work and reduces supervision costs. Consider apprenticeships; many young people in African cities need skills, and you need reliable hands. A structured apprenticeship with clear expectations benefits both sides. Keep records for tax and compliance. Depending on your country, you may need to register your business, obtain a tax identification number, and comply with local regulations. Do not ignore this. Formal businesses can bid for larger contracts, open bank accounts, and access credit. Finally, think like a business owner, not just a painter. Your product is a finished, durable, beautiful space. Your promise is reliability. Your profit comes from accurate pricing, controlled costs and repeat clients. 5. Customer Care, Site Management and Business Growth

Welcome to Lesson 5, the final lesson of Module 6. Here we bring everything together: how you manage the client, the site and the growth of your painting and decorating business in an African context. Customer care begins before you lift a brush. When you visit a site, arrive on time, dress neatly, and listen more than you talk. Ask about the client's budget, timeline, colour preferences and concerns. Explain what you will do, how long it will take, and what you need from them, such as access to water and electricity, parking, and a place to store materials. Put everything in writing. A client who feels respected and informed is far less likely to dispute the final bill. During the job, communicate regularly. Send a short message or call at the end of each day or week to report progress. If there is a delay due to rain, humidity or a supply issue, tell the client immediately and offer a revised plan. Never hide problems. In many African cultures, personal relationships and trust are central to business. A client who trusts you will recommend you to family, friends, colleagues and neighbours. That is your most powerful marketing. Site management is the difference between a professional operation and chaos. Plan the sequence of work: preparation first, then priming, then undercoats, then topcoats, then touch-ups and cleaning. Keep materials organised and secure. Paint theft and tool theft are real risks on some sites, so lock up what you can and account for what you use. Keep the site clean. Remove rubbish daily, protect floors and furniture, and leave the site tidy each evening. In occupied buildings, be considerate. Work around the client's routine, keep noise to reasonable hours, and protect children and pets from wet paint and fumes. Health and safety on site protects your team and your business. Provide gloves, masks, eye protection and safe ladders or scaffolding. Keep a basic first aid kit. Know the emergency numbers. Avoid lone working at height. In hot climates, ensure workers drink water and take breaks. A tired or dehydrated painter makes mistakes and has accidents. Quality sign-off is a formal step. Walk the site with the client. Use a checklist: all surfaces covered, correct number of coats, no runs or missed areas, edges clean, floors and fittings protected and clean, and colours match the approved sample. Ask the client to sign a completion certificate. This triggers the final payment and closes the job professionally. If there are minor defects, fix them promptly. A quick, no-argument return visit builds enormous goodwill. Now, growth. The African construction and renovation market is expanding, and demand for quality decorating is rising in residential estates, commercial buildings, hotels, schools, hospitals and places of worship. To grow, you must move from selling labour to selling solutions. Offer additional services: colour consultation, wallpaper installation, decorative finishes such as stucco, textured coatings, epoxy floors, waterproofing and roof coatings. These higher-value services increase your margin and differentiate you from competitors who only offer basic painting. Invest in your brand. A simple logo, a clean vehicle, neat uniforms and professional business cards or flyers make a strong impression. In the digital age, a WhatsApp business profile, a Facebook page and a Google Business listing with photos and reviews can bring in leads. Encourage satisfied clients to leave reviews and refer you. Consider specialising. Some painters do well focusing on hospitals and clinics, where hygiene and washable finishes matter. Others focus on luxury homes, hotels or heritage buildings. Specialisation lets you charge more and become known as the expert. Manage your money wisely. Save a portion of every payment for tools, vehicle maintenance, taxes and slow periods. Avoid taking on too many jobs at once if it means rushing and damaging your reputation. It is better to complete fewer jobs excellently than many jobs poorly. Build a team you can trust. Train apprentices, delegate supervision, and create systems so the business can run without you being on every site. This is how a painter becomes a business owner. Finally, give back. Train young people, support community projects, and maintain ethical standards. In Africa, reputation is built over years and lost in a day. Honesty, punctuality, quality and fair pricing will carry you further than any shortcut. As you complete this module, remember the chain: preparation, correct paint system, skilled application, accurate pricing, excellent customer care and disciplined business management. Master this chain and you will not just paint walls; you will build a respected, profitable decorating business that creates jobs and improves the spaces where people live, work, learn and worship. Thank you for completing Module 6. Apply these lessons on your next job and review your results.

6. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Paint Calculations, Coverage Rates and Site Data for African Painters

Every profitable painting job in Africa starts with accurate numbers. Under-estimate paint quantities and you lose money on return trips; over-estimate and you tie up working capital in stock that sits in a hot container. This practical reference gives you the calculation tools, coverage data and site benchmarks you need to quote and deliver painting and decorating work confidently across tropical and semi-arid African conditions. It is written for the working contractor: the person who must convert a site visit into a priced, profitable job.

Why Coverage Data Drives Profit

Paint manufacturers publish theoretical spread rates on the tin, but actual coverage on site is almost always lower. Rough cement plaster, porous blockwork, high ambient temperatures and wind all increase absorption and evaporation. In many African markets, the gap between theoretical and practical coverage can be 1530%. If you quote from the tin alone, you absorb that gap as a loss. Always build a site-specific coverage factor into your calculation.

Worked Example: A Three-Room Clinic Repaint

You are asked to repaint the interior of a rural clinic: three rooms, each with 40 m of wall, 2 m of window and 1.9 m of door. Total gross wall = 120 m. Deduct openings: 3 (2.0 + 1.9) = 11.7 m. Net = 108.3 m. Using practical emulsion coverage of 8 m/L over two coats, you need 108.3 8 = 13.5 L per coat, so 27 L total. Add 10% waste and touch-up = 30 L. At a 20 L drum price, you buy one drum plus two 5 L tins. Add primer at 7 m/L = 15.5 L, so one 20 L pail covers it with margin. This is the difference between a profitable quote and a guess.

SAFETY WARNING CHEMICAL AND HEALTH RISK

Paints, thinners, primers and strippers contain volatile organic compounds (VOCs), solvents, lead pigments (in some older or imported stock) and isocyanates. In hot, poorly ventilated African interiors, vapour concentrations can rise rapidly. Follow these rules:

  • Wear a respirator with organic vapour cartridges when spraying or using solvent-based products.
  • Never smoke, eat or drink on site solvents are flammable and toxic.
  • Store paint away from direct sun and sparks; African container temperatures can exceed 50C.
  • Do not burn empty paint containers or use them for food or water a common and dangerous practice.
  • Keep pregnant workers away from solvent-based products and lead paint removal.
  • Wash skin contact immediately with soap and water; seek medical help for eye splashes.

Reference: WHO Chemical Safety in the Workplace and ILO safety guidance on construction chemicals.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Measure gross wall area and deduct all openings (doors, windows, vents) before ordering paint.
  • Confirm substrate type (smooth, rough, porous, previously painted) and adjust coverage rate accordingly.
  • Always include a primer/sealer coat on new plaster it saves topcoat and improves adhesion.
  • Add 10% waste allowance for touch-up, spillage and colour matching on large jobs.
  • Check paint batch numbers match across drums to avoid shade differences in African sunlight.
  • Schedule painting for cooler hours (early morning) to reduce flash drying and roller marks.
  • Record actual coverage on completion it becomes your pricing benchmark for the next job.
  • Store and transport paint upright, shaded and sealed; heat and vibration degrade emulsion.
