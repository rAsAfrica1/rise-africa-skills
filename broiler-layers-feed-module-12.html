Lesson 12.1~12 min

Knowing Your True Cost of Production Per Bird and Per Egg

In this lesson
  • List every cost that belongs in a cost-of-production calculation
  • Calculate cost per bird sold and cost per dozen eggs from your own records
  • Separate fixed from variable costs to make better pricing decisions

Most small poultry producers do not know what a bird costs them to produce. They know the feed price and the chick price and they assume the rest is small. It is not, and the missing costs are exactly the ones that determine whether the business survives a bad month.

Build the list properly. For a broiler batch the costs are: day-old chicks; every batch of feed used, at what it actually cost you and not at what you hoped; vaccines and medication; litter or bedding; brooding fuel or electricity; water and power; hired labour and your own labour valued at what you could earn doing something else; transport of inputs in and birds out; packaging and marketing; a depreciation charge on housing, feeders, drinkers and equipment; interest on any borrowed money; and mortality, which is not a separate cost but is captured by dividing total cost across the birds you actually sold rather than the birds you started.

That last point is where most calculations go wrong. Divide by birds sold, never by birds placed.

Work a full example for 500 broilers.

  1. Chicks: 500 x $0.80 = $400
  2. Feed: 500 birds x 3.8 kg = 1,900 kg at $0.55 = $1,045
  3. Vaccines and medication: $45
  4. Litter: 15 bags of shavings at $2.00 = $30
  5. Brooding fuel: $35
  6. Water and power: $18
  7. Labour: 42 days x 1.5 hours x $0.90/hour = $57
  8. Transport: $40
  9. Depreciation: house and equipment worth $2,400 over 10 years is $240 a year, across 6 batches = $40
  10. Miscellaneous and marketing: $25

Total cost = $1,735. If mortality is 6%, you sell 470 birds. Cost per bird sold = $1,735 / 470 = $3.69. At an average sale price of $4.60 the margin is $0.91 a bird, or $428 for the batch.

Notice what happens if you had used the lazy calculation of chicks plus feed only: $1,445 / 500 = $2.89 a bird, and you would believe your margin was $1.71. You would be overstating profit by nearly 90%, and you would price too cheaply in a competitive negotiation.

Now the layer version. Take 200 layers over a 52-week laying year. Feed at 110 g a day is 200 x 0.11 x 365 = 8,030 kg at $0.50 = $4,015. Point-of-lay pullets at $6.00 each is $1,200, depreciated across the laying year less a cull value of $2.50 each, so ($6.00 - $2.50) x 200 = $700 charged to the year. Add medication $80, labour $400, litter and egg trays $180, transport $220, depreciation $150, power and water $60. Total = $5,805.

If each hen lays 250 saleable eggs, that is 50,000 eggs, or 4,167 dozen. Cost per dozen = $5,805 / 4,167 = $1.39, or $0.116 per egg. Now you know the number below which selling loses money, and you can check it against the published benchmark: Hy-Line Brown standards give 1.23-1.28 kg of feed per dozen eggs at peak, rising to 1.55-1.61 kg per dozen across a full 20-100 week cycle. If your own feed use per dozen is far above that, your problem is production or wastage, not price.

Separate fixed from variable costs, because they behave differently. Variable costs - feed, chicks, medication, litter - rise with every bird. Fixed costs - depreciation, a permanent worker's wage, land - do not. This matters for two decisions. First, in a price slump, you should keep producing as long as price covers variable cost, because every sale above variable cost contributes something to fixed costs you must pay anyway. Second, adding birds to an underused house spreads fixed costs over more units and lowers cost per bird, which is the real financial argument for scaling.

A Zambian example: a Kabwe producer selling broilers at $4.20 believed she was making about $1.30 a bird. Costing properly, including her own labour and a depreciation charge, showed $3.85 of cost and a real margin of $0.35. She raised her price to $4.60 with her two regular buyers, explaining the cost structure, and lost neither of them.

Cash Flow Planning Across a Production Cycle

In this lesson
  • Map money out and money in week by week across a batch
  • Identify the deepest cash trough and size the working capital needed
  • Structure sales and purchases to smooth cash without cutting quality

Profit and cash are different things, and businesses die of cash problems while still profitable on paper. In poultry the mismatch is severe: a broiler batch takes all your money in the first three weeks and returns none of it until week six. A layer flock takes money for five months before the first egg is sold.

Map it. Take a sheet of paper, write the weeks across the top and the cost items down the side, and fill in when money actually leaves your hand and when it actually arrives.

A 500-broiler cycle looks roughly like this. Week 0: chicks $400 plus brooding fuel and litter $65, and the first feed purchase. Weeks 1-2: starter feed, perhaps 300 kg at $0.60 = $180, plus vaccines $45. Weeks 3-4: grower feed, about 700 kg at $0.55 = $385. Weeks 5-6: finisher feed, about 900 kg at $0.52 = $468, plus transport and marketing $65. Money in arrives only in week 6 and possibly week 7 if you sell over several days.

Add the outflows: $645 by end of week 2, $1,030 by end of week 4, $1,563 by end of week 6. That last figure, minus anything you collected as deposits, is your cash trough. You need $1,563 of working capital available to run this batch, on top of the housing you already own. If you have $900, you cannot run 500 birds - you can run about 290, and attempting 500 will leave you unable to buy finisher feed at exactly the moment the birds are eating most.

That failure mode is worth naming because it is extremely common: running out of money in week five, feeding a cheap or reduced ration during the period of highest intake, and losing far more in weight and feed conversion than the shortfall was worth. It is better to place fewer birds and feed them properly.

Layers are harder. A pullet bought at point of lay at $6.00 produces nothing for a week or two and reaches peak only after several more. If you rear from day-old, you are feeding for about 17 weeks before an egg appears - the Lohmann Brown-Classic rearing target is 1.42 kg body weight at 17 weeks - and every one of those weeks is cash out. For 200 pullets reared from day-old, expect several months of feed cost with zero revenue. Most small producers should buy point-of-lay pullets rather than rear, precisely because rearing consumes cash for months.

Smoothing techniques that do not damage the flock:

  1. Stagger batches on separate sites or in separate houses where biosecurity allows, so one batch is selling while another is starting. Note the tension with all-in all-out from Module 10 - staggering must be by site or fully separated house, never by mixing ages in one airspace.
  2. Take deposits. Regular buyers will often pay 30-50% in advance for a guaranteed batch, which shifts your trough dramatically.
  3. Buy feed ingredients at harvest when grain is cheapest, if and only if you have the storage discipline from Module 10 Lesson 3 to hold it without spoilage.
  4. Sell in tranches rather than one day, if your buyers allow, to bring some cash forward.
  5. Keep a written reserve equal to at least one batch's feed bill. This is the difference between a business that survives a disease event and one that does not.

One warning about trade credit. Feed on credit at a merchant's terms is often extremely expensive when you annualise it. If a merchant charges 5% more for 30-day credit than for cash, that is roughly 80% a year. Paying cash from a reserve, even a reserve you built slowly, is usually far cheaper than habitual credit.

A Kenyan example: a Thika broiler producer repeatedly ran short in week five and bought cheap unbranded finisher feed. His birds reached only 1.6 kg instead of 2.0 kg. After mapping his cash on paper he cut placement from 600 to 450 birds for two cycles, built a reserve from the improved sale weights, and returned to 600 birds in the fourth cycle with the full feed budget in hand.

Records That Actually Change Decisions

In this lesson
  • Identify the minimum daily records worth the time to keep
  • Calculate feed conversion, mortality and production percentage from raw records
  • Use record trends to catch problems before they become losses

Most farm record keeping fails because it collects data nobody uses. The test for any record is simple: what decision will this change? If the answer is none, stop collecting it. What follows is the minimum set that earns its keep.

Daily, take five minutes and write down: date, number of birds alive, deaths and culls with a note on cause, feed given in kilograms, water consumed if you can measure it, eggs collected if laying, and one line on anything unusual - weather, a visitor, a medication, a new feed batch.

Weekly, add: average body weight from ten randomly caught birds, cumulative feed used, and cumulative mortality percentage.

That is all. It fits on one A4 sheet per week.

Now the calculations that turn those numbers into decisions.

Feed conversion ratio is total feed eaten divided by total live weight produced. If 470 broilers average 2.05 kg and ate 1,900 kg of feed, live weight produced is 470 x 2.05 = 963.5 kg, so FCR is 1,900 / 963.5 = 1.97. That is poor for a modern broiler and tells you to look at feed quality, wastage, disease or house temperature. Always calculate on birds sold, not birds placed, and if you want to be strict, include the feed eaten by birds that died.

Mortality percentage is deaths divided by birds placed. Fifteen deaths from 500 is 3.0%. Track it cumulatively by week, because the shape matters more than the total. Deaths concentrated in week one point to chick quality, brooding temperature or dehydration. A spike at weeks three to four suggests coccidiosis or necrotic enteritis. A steady trickle late suggests ascites, leg problems or heat.

Layer production percentage is eggs collected divided by hens present, times 100. If 190 hens produce 152 eggs, that is 80.0%. Compare against the standard for the flock's age. Feed per dozen is cumulative feed divided by dozens produced - benchmark against Hy-Line Brown at 1.23-1.28 kg per dozen at peak and 1.55-1.61 kg across a 20-100 week cycle.

The European Production Efficiency Factor is worth knowing because buyers and lenders recognise it: EPEF = (liveability % x average live weight in kg) / (age in days x FCR) x 100. For our example flock, liveability 94%, weight 2.05 kg, age 42 days, FCR 1.97: (94 x 2.05) / (42 x 1.97) x 100 = 192.7 / 82.74 x 100 = 232.9. Higher is better. Tracking one number across batches shows whether you are genuinely improving.

The real value is in trends and comparisons. A single batch's FCR means little. Six batches on one page, with a note against each of what was different, is a management tool. It lets you answer questions like whether supplier B's maize actually performs worse, whether the cheaper premix cost you more than it saved, and whether the batch you brooded in the cold month really did worse.

Set trigger levels in advance. Decide now what will make you act: water intake down more than 20% from yesterday; mortality above 0.5% in a single day; weekly weight more than 10% below target; feed intake flat or falling in a growing flock. Write these on the record sheet. A trigger you decided in advance beats a judgement made while worried.

Keep it simple enough to survive. A cheap exercise book by the house door, filled in daily, beats a spreadsheet that is updated for two weeks and then abandoned. If you use a phone, take a photograph of the page - that is a backup and a timestamp, and it is what you show a lender.

A Ghanaian example: a producer near Tamale kept records for six batches and noticed his FCR was consistently worse in batches brooded during the harmattan. The record showed feed intake dropping and mortality rising in week one. He added a simple curtain and improved the brooder heat source for cold-season batches, and his cold-season FCR came into line with the rest of the year.

Deciding When to Mill and Mix Your Own Feed

In this lesson
  • Compare home-mixed and commercial feed on a true total-cost basis
  • Calculate the break-even volume that justifies buying a mixer
  • Judge whether you have the skill and discipline home mixing requires

Home mixing is the most common growth step in African poultry, and the most commonly regretted. Done well it cuts feed cost meaningfully and gives you control over quality. Done badly it produces uneven, contaminated or unbalanced feed that costs more than the commercial bag you were trying to avoid.

Start with an honest comparison. Commercial feed costs what the bag costs, delivered. Home-mixed feed costs the ingredients, plus milling, plus your labour, plus transport of ingredients, plus storage losses, plus the cost of mistakes. Most people compare only the first item and conclude home mixing saves 20%. Add the rest and the saving is usually smaller, though often still real.

Work it for a broiler grower ration on a one-tonne batch.

Ingredients: maize 620 kg at $0.32 = $198.40; soybean meal 300 kg at $0.62 = $186.00; limestone 12 kg at $0.10 = $1.20; dicalcium phosphate 18 kg at $0.90 = $16.20; salt 3 kg at $0.35 = $1.05; premix 2.5 kg at $4.00 = $10.00; DL-methionine 1.5 kg at $5.50 = $8.25; lysine 1.5 kg at $3.20 = $4.80; vegetable oil 25 kg at $1.30 = $32.50; phytase 0.1 kg at $12.00 = $1.20. Ingredient subtotal is about $459.60 per tonne, or $0.46/kg.

Now add the rest. Milling at a commercial hammer mill, $12 per tonne. Transport of ingredients, $15 per tonne. Labour, four hours at $0.90 = $3.60. Storage and handling losses at 2% = about $9.20. Total is roughly $499.40 per tonne, or $0.50/kg.

Compare against commercial grower at, say, $0.58/kg. The saving is $0.08/kg, or $80 per tonne - about 14%. On 1,000 broilers eating 3,800 kg that is $304 per batch. Real money.

But check the conditions under which that saving survives.

  1. You must be able to buy quality ingredients consistently. If you cannot get soybean meal reliably, you will substitute and your formulation will drift.
  2. You must weigh, not scoop, and mix properly, following Module 10 Lesson 5. A badly mixed tonne can cost more than the saving on ten tonnes.
  3. You must have dry, rodent-proof storage for several tonnes of ingredients, which is more space than the finished feed you currently buy.
  4. You must have the cash to buy ingredients in bulk, which increases your working capital requirement even as it lowers your cost per kilogram.
  5. You must accept the quality risk. A commercial mill takes responsibility for mycotoxins, amino acid balance and uniformity. When you mix, that responsibility is yours.

Break-even on a mixer. Suppose a small horizontal mixer and motor costs $1,400 installed, plus $200 for a platform scale. Total $1,600. If home mixing saves $80 per tonne and you use 25 tonnes a year, you save $2,000 a year, so payback is under a year. If you use only 6 tonnes a year, you save $480 a year and payback is over three years - and in that case you should keep using a hire mill or keep buying commercial feed.

A sensible middle path many producers overlook is the concentrate route. You buy a commercial protein-vitamin-mineral concentrate and mix it with your own maize. You capture most of the saving on the largest ingredient, the mill retains responsibility for the difficult micro-ingredient part, and your mixing task is far simpler because you are blending two or three components rather than ten. For a producer between about 5 and 20 tonnes a year, this is often the best answer.

A Nigerian example: an Ibadan producer bought a mixer to feed 8,000 birds a year, then discovered soybean meal supply in her town was unreliable and she was substituting groundnut cake at varying quality. Her flock uniformity worsened and FCR rose from 1.72 to 1.91, which more than erased the ingredient saving. She switched to a commercial concentrate plus her own maize, kept the mixer for that simpler job, and recovered her performance within two batches.

Financing Growth: Savings Groups, Loans and Contract Arrangements

In this lesson
  • Compare the true annual cost of the finance options available to you
  • Assess whether a contract growing arrangement suits your situation
  • Structure borrowing so repayment matches when cash actually arrives

Growth needs money. The question is not whether to use finance but which kind, at what real cost, and repaid on what schedule. Poultry has a specific danger: cycles are short, margins per bird are thin, and a single disease event can wipe out a batch. Debt that assumes everything goes right will break you the first time it does not.

Line up the options and compare them on annual cost, not headline rate.

  1. Retained earnings. Growing from your own profit is the cheapest and slowest option. Its real cost is the opportunity you did not take. For most small producers, funding the first two or three growth steps from profit is the right answer, because it also builds the record a lender will later want to see.
  2. Savings and lending groups. Village savings and loan associations, chamas, susu and cooperatives typically lend at rates that sound high per month but come with flexibility, no collateral and social enforcement. Always convert the monthly rate: 3% a month is not 36% a year but roughly 43% compounded. That is expensive money and suits short, clearly profitable uses - buying a feed batch at harvest prices, for example - not long-term assets.
  3. Microfinance institutions. Rates vary widely. Read the schedule and calculate the total you will repay, not the advertised rate. Ask specifically about fees, insurance charges and whether interest is charged on the original amount or the declining balance. Flat-rate interest on the original amount roughly doubles the effective rate compared with declining balance.
  4. Bank loans. Cheaper if you can access them, but they usually demand collateral, a track record and formal records. This is where the record keeping from Lesson 3 and the costing from Lesson 1 turn into money.
  5. Supplier and buyer credit. Feed on credit is expensive as shown in Lesson 2. Buyer advances are usually cheaper and safer, because a buyer paying in advance is buying certainty rather than lending money.
  6. Equipment leasing and asset finance. Sometimes available for larger equipment; compare against the total repayment on a loan.

Structure matters as much as rate. A loan with monthly repayments starting immediately is badly matched to a broiler cycle where cash arrives every six weeks, and disastrously matched to layers where nothing arrives for months. Ask for a repayment schedule that matches your cash cycle, with a grace period covering the production gap. Many lenders will agree if you show them the cash flow map from Lesson 2. Bring the map to the meeting.

Borrow for assets that generate income, not for operating shortfalls. Borrowing to buy a second house or a mixer that will earn a return is sound. Borrowing to cover feed because the last batch went badly is how farms enter a spiral, because you now carry the loss plus interest into the next cycle.

Contract growing deserves careful thought. In a typical arrangement an integrator supplies chicks, feed and veterinary support, you supply housing, labour and management, and you are paid a fee per bird or per kilogram produced. The advantages are large: no working capital tied up in feed, no market risk, a guaranteed buyer, and technical support. The disadvantages are equally real: thin margins per bird, loss of independence, exposure to the integrator's decisions, penalties for performance below target, and the risk of the contract simply not being renewed after you have built housing to their specification.

Run the numbers before signing. If a contract pays $0.45 per bird and you can place 4,000 birds five times a year, that is $9,000 a year of gross fee. Subtract labour, housing depreciation, electricity, litter and water, say $3,600, leaving $5,400. Compare that against your independent margin - if you make $0.91 a bird independently but can only finance 500 birds a cycle, six cycles gives 3,000 birds and $2,730. The contract wins on scale. But if you can finance 2,000 birds a cycle independently, six cycles gives $10,920 and independence wins. The deciding variable is how much working capital you have, which is exactly why Lesson 2 comes before this one.

A Kenyan example: a Kiambu producer joined a savings group, borrowed at a monthly rate to buy 30 tonnes of maize at harvest, stored it properly using the methods in Module 10, and repaid over four months from three broiler cycles. The loan was expensive on an annual basis but the holding period was short and the harvest-price discount exceeded the interest. She refused the same group's offer to fund a new house on the same terms, and saved for it instead.

Scaling in Steps Without Breaking What Already Works

In this lesson
  • Identify the binding constraint that currently limits your farm
  • Plan a growth step that removes one constraint at a time
  • Recognise the management thresholds where systems must change

Farms rarely fail from growing too slowly. They fail from growing faster than their weakest system can carry. The discipline of scaling is finding the one thing that actually limits you, fixing that, and running long enough at the new size to be sure it holds before moving again.

Find the binding constraint. At any moment one thing limits your output, and money spent anywhere else is wasted. Ask in order: is it market - can you sell more than you produce? Is it capital - could you place more birds if you had the cash? Is it housing - are you at capacity? Is it management - are mortality and FCR already drifting because you cannot supervise what you have? Is it labour, water, or feed supply?

The test is simple. If you doubled your birds tomorrow, what would break first? That is your constraint. If the answer is that you could not sell them, buying a second house is the wrong move and finding buyers is the right one.

A useful sequence for most small producers looks like this:

  1. Fill the house you have. Under-stocking existing housing is the cheapest growth available, because your fixed costs are already paid. Spreading fixed costs over more birds directly lowers cost per bird, as Lesson 1 showed.
  2. Improve performance before adding volume. Moving FCR from 1.95 to 1.75 on your current flock is often worth more than adding 20% more birds, and costs nothing but attention. On 1,000 birds at 2.0 kg, that improvement saves 1,000 x 2.0 x 0.20 = 400 kg of feed, worth $220 at $0.55/kg, every cycle.
  3. Secure the market. Get written or clearly agreed commitments for the extra volume before you produce it.
  4. Build working capital. Reach the point where you can fund a full cycle at the new size without credit, using the trough figure from Lesson 2.
  5. Then add housing.

Watch for management thresholds - the points where the way you run the farm must change, not just get bigger.

The first threshold is when you can no longer see every bird yourself. Below a few hundred birds you notice a sick one by walking through. Above that you need records and trigger levels, because you will not spot it by eye.

The second is when you employ someone. Now you need written routines, defined responsibilities and a way to verify work you did not watch. Most farms that fail at this point fail because the owner assumed the worker would do things the way the owner did, without being told or checked.

The third is when you have more than one house or site. Now biosecurity discipline from Module 10 becomes formal - order of work from young to old, separate boots, no equipment sharing between houses.

The fourth is when feed volume justifies milling, covered in Lesson 4.

The fifth is when you sell to institutional buyers rather than individuals. Now you need invoices, consistent grading, reliable delivery and often formal registration.

Grow in steps you can reverse. A step that commits you to fixed costs you cannot cover in a bad month is a bet, not a plan. Adding 200 birds to an existing house is reversible; borrowing $8,000 for a new house on a contract that could end is not.

Protect what works while you grow. The most common scaling failure is that the original 500-bird operation, which was profitable and well-run, deteriorates while attention goes to the new 1,500 birds. Before adding, write down what currently makes the existing operation work - the daily walk-through, the feed supplier relationship, the Saturday delivery - and decide explicitly who will do each of those things after you grow.

Set a review point. Decide in advance that after two full cycles at the new size you will sit down with the records from Lesson 3 and compare cost per bird, FCR, mortality and margin against the previous size. If they got worse, stop and fix before growing again. Writing that review date down before you expand is what turns growth into a managed process rather than momentum.

A Tanzanian example: a producer near Mbeya went from 400 to 1,500 broilers in one step after a good year, adding two houses and hiring a worker. Mortality rose from 4% to 11% across all three houses because he could no longer do the daily walk-through and had not trained anyone else to do it. He cut back to 900 birds, wrote a one-page daily routine for his worker, checked it for three cycles, and only then returned to 1,500 - this time with mortality holding at 5%.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. Cost per bird should be calculated by dividing total cost by what?

Dividing by birds sold automatically accounts for mortality. Dividing by birds placed understates true cost per saleable bird by the full mortality percentage.

2. 500 broilers cost $1,735 in total with 6% mortality. What is the cost per bird sold?

6% mortality means 470 birds sold. $1,735 / 470 = $3.69 per bird sold, against $3.47 if you wrongly divided by the 500 placed.

3. Which of these is a fixed cost?

Depreciation on housing does not change with bird numbers, making it fixed. Feed, chicks and medication all rise with each additional bird and are variable.

4. In a price slump, when should you keep producing?

Fixed costs are incurred whether or not you produce. Any price above variable cost makes a contribution toward them, so producing at a small loss on total cost can be better than stopping.

5. Your layer flock uses 2.1 kg of feed per dozen eggs. What does that suggest?

Hy-Line Brown standards are 1.23-1.28 kg per dozen at peak and 1.55-1.61 kg across a full cycle. Well above that indicates poor production, feed wastage, rodents or theft rather than a pricing issue.

6. Why can a profitable poultry farm still fail?

In a broiler cycle almost all spending happens before any revenue arrives. A business can be profitable on paper across the batch and still be unable to buy week-five feed.

7. What is the most common consequence of underestimating working capital?

Running short in week five, when intake is highest, forces a cheap or reduced ration and costs far more in lost weight and feed conversion than the shortfall was worth. Placing fewer birds and feeding properly is better.

8. A merchant charges 5% more for 30-day credit than for cash. Roughly what annual rate is that?

Roughly twelve 30-day periods a year at 5% each compounds to about 80% annually. Habitual feed credit is one of the most expensive forms of finance a small farm can use.

9. How can batches be staggered without breaking biosecurity?

Staggering smooths cash flow, but mixing ages in one airspace destroys all-in all-out and keeps disease circulating. Separate sites or genuinely separated houses preserve both benefits.

10. Why is buying point-of-lay pullets often better than rearing for a small producer?

Rearing from day-old means roughly 17 weeks of feed cost before the first egg. For a cash-constrained producer, buying at point of lay converts a long cash drain into a single known payment.

11. What is the test for whether a record is worth keeping?

Records that change no decision are wasted effort and cause people to abandon record keeping entirely. Keep the minimum set that actually drives action.

12. 470 broilers average 2.05 kg and ate 1,900 kg of feed. What is the FCR?

Live weight produced is 470 x 2.05 = 963.5 kg. FCR = 1,900 / 963.5 = 1.97, which is poor for a modern broiler and warrants investigation of feed, wastage, disease or temperature.

13. Mortality concentrated in week one most likely points to what?

The shape of the mortality curve is diagnostic. Week-one deaths point to chick source, brooding conditions or water access; a week three to four spike suggests coccidiosis or necrotic enteritis.

14. A flock has 94% liveability, 2.05 kg at 42 days and FCR 1.97. What is its EPEF?

(94 x 2.05) / (42 x 1.97) x 100 = 192.7 / 82.74 x 100 = 232.9. Tracking this one figure across batches shows whether performance is genuinely improving.

15. Why should trigger levels be decided in advance?

Deciding in advance what will make you act - a 20% water drop, 0.5% daily mortality - removes hesitation and hindsight bias at exactly the moment when acting quickly matters most.

16. What do most producers leave out when comparing home-mixed to commercial feed?

Comparing ingredient cost alone against a delivered commercial bag overstates the saving. Adding milling, transport, labour and storage losses moved the worked example from $0.46 to $0.50 per kilogram.

17. A $1,600 mixer saves $80 per tonne. At 6 tonnes a year, what is the payback period?

6 tonnes x $80 = $480 saved per year. $1,600 / $480 = about 3.3 years. At that volume, hiring a mill or buying commercial feed is the better decision.

18. What is the main advantage of the concentrate route?

Buying a protein-vitamin-mineral concentrate and adding your own maize captures most of the saving on the largest ingredient while leaving the technically hard part to a commercial mill, with a far simpler mixing job.

19. How does home mixing affect working capital?

Buying ingredients in bulk is what lowers cost per kilogram, but it also means more money tied up in stock at any moment and more storage space than the finished feed you previously bought.

20. In the Ibadan example, why did home mixing fail initially?

Inconsistent ingredient supply forced substitutions of varying quality. FCR rose from 1.72 to 1.91, more than erasing the ingredient saving - which is why reliable supply is a precondition for home mixing.

21. A savings group charges 3% a month. What is the approximate annual cost?

Compounding 3% monthly gives roughly 43% a year, not the 36% a simple multiplication suggests. Always convert monthly rates before comparing finance options.

22. Why does flat-rate interest matter?

Flat interest is charged on the full original amount throughout the term even as you repay it, so the effective rate is roughly double a declining-balance loan at the same headline rate.

23. What should you bring to a lender meeting?

The cash flow map shows the lender when money actually arrives and justifies a repayment schedule with a grace period. The costing sheet proves you know your business.

24. A contract pays $0.45 per bird for 4,000 birds five times a year, with $3,600 of costs. What is the net?

4,000 x 5 x $0.45 = $9,000 gross fee, minus $3,600 of labour, depreciation, litter, power and water, leaves $5,400 net for the year.

25. What is the main risk of building housing to an integrator's specification?

Purpose-built housing has little alternative use, and a non-renewed contract leaves you with the asset, possibly the debt on it, and no guaranteed buyer. This risk must be weighed against the working-capital relief a contract provides.

26. What is the binding constraint on a farm?

At any moment one factor caps output. Investing anywhere else adds cost without adding output, so identifying the true constraint before spending is the core scaling discipline.

27. Improving FCR from 1.95 to 1.75 on 1,000 birds of 2.0 kg saves how much feed?

1,000 birds x 2.0 kg x 0.20 FCR difference = 400 kg of feed saved, worth $220 at $0.55/kg every single cycle, for no capital outlay.

28. What is the cheapest form of growth available to most producers?

Fixed costs on existing housing are already incurred. Adding birds to under-stocked housing spreads those costs over more units and lowers cost per bird immediately.

29. What changes when you first employ someone?

Most farms that stumble at this threshold do so because the owner assumed a worker would do things the owner's way without being told or checked. Written routines and verification replace direct observation.

30. Why set a review point before expanding?

Deciding in advance to review after two full cycles turns growth into a managed process. Without it, momentum carries a farm past the point where performance has already deteriorated.

Module 12 capstone

Produce a complete business file for your own farm covering one full production cycle and a costed growth plan. Part 1: build a cost-of-production sheet from your actual records for your most recent batch, listing every input in USD - chicks or pullets, each feed batch, medication and vaccines, litter, labour including your own time valued at a local wage, transport, water and power, and a depreciation charge on housing and equipment - and calculate cost per bird sold and cost per kilogram or per dozen. Part 2: build a week-by-week cash flow for that same cycle showing money out and money in, identify the deepest cash trough and the week it occurred, and state how much working capital you must hold to run the next cycle without borrowing. Part 3: price a single specific growth step you actually want to take - more birds, a mixer, a delivery motorcycle, a second house - showing the capital cost, the extra working capital it demands, the additional monthly margin it should generate, and the payback period. Part 4: write half a page stating the three risks that would break the plan and what you would do about each. Use only your own farm and local market figures.
