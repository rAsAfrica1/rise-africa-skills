1. Why curing is critical: keeping blocks wet for 714 days

Curing is the process of keeping cement blocks moist after they are made so the cement can harden properly. When you mix cement with water, a chemical reaction called hydration starts. This reaction only continues if there is enough water. If blocks dry out too fast, the cement particles never fully bind together, and the blocks stay weak and crumbly. In hot African climates, blocks can lose moisture within hours if left in the sun. That is why curing is not optionalit is the difference between blocks that last for decades and blocks that crack within months.

A common mistake is to think that once blocks are hard to the touch, they are done. In reality, blocks gain most of their strength during the first 7 to 14 days. During this time, you must keep them wet. The minimum curing period is 7 days, but for strong load-bearing blocks, aim for 14 days. In very hot or windy conditions, extend to 21 days. The longer you cure (up to 28 days), the stronger the blocks become. After 28 days, the cement has reached about 99% of its potential strength.

How do you know if blocks are curing properly? They should remain damp and dark in colour. If they turn light grey and feel dry, they are not getting enough water. You can test by pressing a finger on the blockif it leaves a mark, it is still moist. If it feels hard and dry, you need to water more often. Remember, curing is not about drying; it is about keeping wet. Many builders in Africa lose money because they skip curing and then have to rebuild walls that fail.

To cure blocks, you can stack them in a shaded area and water them twice a day. Or you can cover them with plastic sheets or wet grass. The key is to prevent evaporation. In dry regions like the Sahel, you might need to water three times a day. Always use clean waterdirty water can introduce salts that weaken the cement. And never cure blocks in direct sun; move them to shade or cover them. Curing costs almost nothing but pays off with strong, durable blocks that can support heavy roofs and resist rain and wind.

Start curing as soon as blocks are firm enough to handle, usually 24 hours after casting. Then keep them wet for at least 7 days. If you are selling blocks, tell your customers how long you cured themit builds trust. Strong blocks mean safe houses, happy customers, and a good reputation for your business.

2. Building a curing area with shade and water access

A good curing area is essential for making strong blocks. It should be shaded, well-ventilated, and close to a water source. In many parts of Africa, the sun is intense, so direct sunlight can dry blocks too quickly. Build your curing area under a tree, a simple roof made of palm fronds, or a corrugated iron sheet supported by poles. The roof should be at least 2.5 meters high to allow air circulation. If you use a tree, make sure it does not drop leaves that could stain the blocks. A simple shade net (available at hardware stores) also works well and blocks up to 70% of sunlight.

The floor of the curing area should be level and well-drained. If the ground is uneven, blocks may crack as they dry. You can spread a layer of sand or gravel to prevent water from pooling. Avoid concrete floors if possible, because they can reflect heat. A dirt floor that is kept damp is ideal. If you live in a rainy area, raise the blocks on wooden pallets or stones to keep them off the wet ground. This also allows air to circulate underneath.

Water access is critical. You need a reliable source within 10 meters of the curing area. A tap, a drum with a tap, or a small water tank works well. If you have to carry water from far, you will likely skip watering. So invest in a hose or a watering can. A 200-litre drum can be filled once and used for several waterings. In dry seasons, you may need to water twice a day, so having water nearby saves time and energy. You can also set up a simple drip system using a bucket with small holes, but that requires more maintenance.

Organize your blocks in stacks no higher than 1.5 meters. Leave gaps between blocks for air to flow, but not so much that they dry out. A common pattern is to stack them in a crisscross pattern with spaces of about 2-3 cm. If you stack them too tightly, the ones inside may not get enough water. If you stack them too loosely, they dry too fast. Also, keep different batches separate so you can track curing times. Label each stack with the date you made them.

Finally, protect your curing area from animals and children. A simple fence of sticks or wire mesh can keep goats and chickens away. If you have a small operation, you can cure blocks on a raised platform under a roof. The goal is to create a microclimate that stays humid and cool. With a good curing area, you will see fewer cracks and stronger blocks, which means more profit and fewer complaints.

3. Watering schedules: morning and evening routines

Watering your blocks on a consistent schedule is the most important thing you can do during curing. In most African climates, you need to water twice a day: once in the early morning (before 9 AM) and once in the late evening (after 5 PM). Avoid watering during the hottest part of the day (11 AM to 3 PM) because the water evaporates quickly and can even shock the blocks. Morning watering prepares the blocks for the heat, and evening watering replaces moisture lost during the day. If you live in a very hot, dry area, add a midday watering, but only if the blocks are in deep shade.

How much water? You want to keep the blocks damp, not soaked. A light spray from a watering can or hose is enough. The blocks should look dark and feel cool. If water runs off, you are using too much. A good rule: for 100 blocks, you need about 20 litres per watering. So a 200-litre drum will last about 5 waterings. In the rainy season, you may not need to water at all, but check the blocksif they are dry, water them. In the dry season, you might need 30 litres per 100 blocks. Always water the top of the stack first, then the sides. Use a fine rose on the watering can to avoid washing away the cement.

Set a routine. Many successful block makers water at 6 AM and 6 PM. If you work away from home, ask a family member or hire a helper to water in the afternoon. Missing even one watering can cause surface cracks. You can also use a simple timer with a drip irrigation system, but that is expensive. A cheaper method is to cover blocks with wet burlap sacks or banana leaves after watering. This slows evaporation and reduces the need for frequent watering. In very dry areas, you can dig a shallow trench around the stack and fill it with water, so moisture seeps up.

Keep a record. Write the date you started curing and the times you watered. This helps you spot patternsfor example, if blocks are drying too fast, you might need to water more or move them to deeper shade. After 7 days, you can reduce watering to once a day for the next 7 days, but keep them damp. After 14 days, you can stop watering and let them dry slowly in the shade for another 7 days before use. Never let blocks dry in direct sun; that causes rapid shrinkage and cracks.

Remember, curing is not a one-time event. It is a daily commitment. The blocks that get consistent watering will be up to 50% stronger than those that are neglected. So set an alarm, make it a habit, and your blocks will reward you with durability and strength.

4. Covering blocks with plastic or grass to retain moisture

Covering blocks is a simple and cheap way to keep them moist during curing. In hot, dry climates, uncovered blocks can lose moisture within an hour. A cover slows evaporation and reduces how often you need to water. You can use plastic sheets, wet grass, banana leaves, burlap sacks, or even old tarpaulins. The best cover depends on what you have available. Plastic sheeting is very effective because it traps moisture, but it must be removed for a few hours each day to let air in, otherwise mould can grow. Wet grass or leaves are breathable and also provide shade.

If you use plastic, choose clear or black polyethylene sheets. Clear plastic lets sunlight through, which can heat the blocks and cause condensationthat is good for moisture but can also overheat. Black plastic blocks sunlight and keeps the stack cooler. Lay the plastic directly over the stack, but leave the sides open for air. You can weigh down the edges with stones or bricks. Remove the plastic in the morning for 1-2 hours to allow fresh air, then water and cover again. In very hot weather, you can leave the plastic on all day if you water underneath. But check for mouldif you see white or green patches, remove the cover and let the blocks dry for a day, then resume curing.

Wet grass or banana leaves are traditional and effective. After watering, spread a 5-10 cm layer of wet grass over the stack. The grass shades the blocks and releases moisture as it dries. You will need to replace the grass every 2-3 days as it dries out. This method is labour-intensive but uses free materials. In many African villages, this is the preferred method. You can also use palm fronds, which are large and provide good shade. Just make sure the leaves are not poisonous or acidicsome leaves can leach chemicals that affect cement. Stick to grass, banana, or palm.

Burlap sacks (jute bags) are excellent. They hold water well and allow air circulation. Soak the sacks in water, then drape them over the blocks. Re-wet the sacks every time you water. They are durable and can last for many curing cycles. You can often get them free from coffee or grain traders. If you use sacks, stack them in a way that covers the top and sides, but leave the bottom open for drainage.

Combining methods works best. For example, use a shade roof, then cover blocks with wet grass, and water twice a day. Or use plastic at night and grass during the day. The goal is to keep the blocks damp and cool. A simple test: lift the cover and touch a block. If it is dry, you need more cover or more water. With good covering, you can reduce watering to once a day in moderate weather. This saves water and time, and your blocks will be stronger.

5. Testing block strength: simple drop and scratch tests

You do not need a laboratory to check if your blocks are strong. Two simple teststhe drop test and the scratch testcan tell you a lot. Do these tests after curing, usually at 7 days and again at 14 days. They are not perfect, but they help you catch problems early. If blocks fail, you can adjust your mix or curing and avoid using weak blocks in construction.

The drop test: Take a cured block and drop it flat onto a hard surface (concrete or stone) from a height of about 1 meter. A strong block will either stay intact or break into a few large pieces. A weak block will shatter into many small pieces or crumble. You can also drop it on its cornera strong block may chip but not break completely. Do this test on 3-4 blocks from different parts of the stack. If more than one breaks easily, your blocks are weak. Note: this test destroys the block, so use blocks you can spare. In some areas, you can drop from waist height (about 1 meter). If the block breaks into more than 5 pieces, it is too weak for load-bearing walls.

The scratch test: Use a nail or a sharp piece of metal to scratch the surface of a block. A well-cured block will resist scratchingyou will need to press hard to make a mark. A weak block will scratch easily, and you may see powder or crumbs. You can also try to break off a corner with your fingers. If it snaps off easily, the block is weak. Another version: scratch a line and then try to rub it away with your thumb. If it rubs away, the surface is not hard. For a more precise test, use a coin: drag the edge across the block. A strong block will leave a faint mark; a weak block will gouge deeply. Do this on the side and top of the block.

What do the results mean? If blocks fail either test, possible causes: not enough cement (use a 1:6 ratio for strong blocks, not 1:8), too much sand, dirty water, poor curing, or blocks dried too fast. To fix, increase cement content, cure for longer (14-21 days), and ensure consistent watering. Also, check your sandif it has too much clay or silt, it weakens the block. You can test sand by putting it in a jar with water: good sand settles with clear water; dirty sand leaves cloudy water.

Remember, these tests are for your own quality control. If you are selling blocks, do not sell blocks that fail. Instead, use them for non-load-bearing walls or as fill. Keep records of test results for each batch. Over time, you will learn what works. A block that passes both tests is likely strong enough for a single-story house. For two-story buildings, you need stronger blocksconsider adding more cement or using a block-making machine that compacts better.

6. Avoiding common curing mistakes that weaken blocks

Many block makers lose money because of simple curing mistakes. The most common is skipping curing altogether. Some people think that because the block is hard, it is cured. But hardness is not strength. Without curing, blocks can be 50% weaker. Another mistake is curing in direct sunlight. The sun dries the surface too fast, causing cracks and preventing the cement from binding. Always cure in shade. If you have no shade, build a simple roof or cover with grass.

Watering too little or too much is another issue. Too little water means the cement reaction stops, and blocks remain weak. Too much water can wash away cement or cause the blocks to swell and crack. Water should be applied gently, not with a high-pressure hose. Also, watering at the wrong timelike middaycan cause thermal shock. Stick to morning and evening. If you cannot water twice a day, cover the blocks with wet burlap or plastic to reduce evaporation.

Using dirty water is a hidden mistake. Water with oil, soap, or high salt content can weaken cement. Use clean waterrainwater, well water, or tap water. If you must use river water, let it settle and filter it. Another mistake: stacking blocks too high. If you stack more than 1.5 meters, the bottom blocks bear too much weight and can crack. Also, do not stack blocks directly on the ground; use a pallet or stones to allow air circulation and prevent moisture from the soil from making them too wet.

Not covering blocks is a mistake in hot climates. Without a cover, you will need to water more often, and you may still lose moisture. Use plastic, grass, or burlap. But do not leave plastic on all the time without ventilationmould can grow and weaken the surface. Remove the cover for a few hours each day. Also, do not cure blocks on a concrete floor that gets hot; the heat can dry them from below. Use a sand bed or wooden pallets.

Finally, do not rush the curing period. Seven days is the minimum; 14 days is better. If you sell blocks before they are cured, customers will complain, and your reputation will suffer. Plan your production so you have enough blocks curing while you make new ones. Keep records of when each batch was made and when it will be ready. Train your workers on these points. A little discipline in curing will give you strong, durable blocks that can withstand rain, wind, and heavy loads. Remember, curing is the cheapest insurance for your block business.

<h2>Practical Reference: Curing and Drying Blocks for Strength</h2>

<p>In the cement and blocks trade, your reputation is built on one thing: whether your blocks hold up. A block that crumbles under load, cracks in the wall, or fails a customer's inspection is not just a lost sale. It is a lost contract, a lost referral, and a damaged name in a market where word travels fast. The difference between a block that earns repeat business and one that gets rejected often comes down to curing the controlled process of keeping freshly moulded blocks moist so the cement can fully hydrate and gain strength.</p>

<p>Curing is where many small producers cut corners. It is easy to demould a block, stack it, and move on to the next batch. But skipping proper curing can reduce block strength by 3050%, according to research from the <a href="https://www.ihs.ac.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Institute for Housing and Urban Studies</a> and the <a href="https://www.unhabitat.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">UN-Habitat</a> in their joint work on low-cost building materials. In African markets where blocks are sold on trust and tested by hand, that strength gap is the difference between a business that grows and one that gets a bad name.</p>

<p>This lesson gives you a practical, business-focused reference for curing and drying blocks. It covers the science, the site setup, the daily routine, and the tests you can run yourself to confirm quality before your blocks leave the yard.</p>

<h3>Why Curing Is Critical: Keeping Blocks Wet for 714 Days</h3>

<p>Cement does not dry to gain strength. It cures through a chemical reaction called hydration. When cement particles mix with water, they form crystals that bind the sand and aggregate together. This reaction needs two things: time and moisture. If the block dries out too early, hydration stops, and the block never reaches its full potential strength.</p>

<p>Research from the <a href="https://www.ihs.ac.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Institute for Housing and Urban Studies</a> shows that concrete blocks cured for 7 days reach about 70% of their design strength, while those cured for 14 days reach roughly 90%. Blocks cured for 28 days reach full strength. In practice, most small producers in Africa cannot wait 28 days, so a 714 day curing period is a realistic and effective compromise.</p>

<p>The key point: keep blocks wet, not soaked. They should be damp, not dripping. Water should be applied gently so it does not erode the block surface or wash out cement.</p>

<h3>Building a Curing Area with Shade and Water Access</h3>

<p>Your curing area is a business asset. A simple, well-designed curing yard reduces water waste, prevents block damage, and speeds up your production cycle. Here is what you need:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Shade:</strong> Direct sun dries blocks too fast. Build a simple roof from corrugated iron, palm fronds, or shade netting. In hot climates, shade can reduce water loss by 4060%.</li>
<li><strong>Level ground:</strong> Blocks should sit on a flat, hard surface concrete slab, wooden pallets, or compacted gravel. Uneven ground causes warping and cracking.</li>
<li><strong>Water access:</strong> A tap, hose, or watering can within easy reach. If water is scarce, a small tank or drum with a tap works well.</li>
<li><strong>Drainage:</strong> Water should drain away, not pool around the blocks. Standing water softens blocks and breeds mosquitoes.</li>
<li><strong>Airflow:</strong> Good ventilation prevents mould and allows even drying. Do not pack blocks too tightly.</li>
</ul>

<p>In many African markets, a curing area can be built for under $50 using local materials. The return on that investment is immediate: fewer rejected blocks, stronger products, and happier customers.</p>

<h3>Watering Schedules: Morning and Evening Routines</h3>

<p>Consistency is everything. Blocks should be watered twice a day early morning and late evening during the curing period. This mimics the natural dew cycle and keeps blocks damp without soaking them.</p>

<h3>Covering Blocks with Plastic or Grass to Retain Moisture</h3>

<p>In hot, dry climates common across the Sahel, East Africa, and Southern Africa water evaporates quickly. Covering blocks is a simple, low-cost way to retain moisture and reduce watering frequency.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Plastic sheeting:</strong> Cheap, effective, and reusable. Lay it loosely over stacks. Do not seal tightly some airflow prevents mould.</li>
<li><strong>Grass or leaves:</strong> A traditional method used across Africa. Wet grass or banana leaves placed over blocks keep humidity high. Replace every 23 days to prevent rot.</li>
<li><strong>Wet burlap or sacks:</strong> Excellent for small batches. Soak sacks in water, wring out, and drape over blocks.</li>
<li><strong>Sand or sawdust:</strong> In very dry areas, a thin layer of wet sand over blocks works well. It slowly releases moisture.</li>
</ul>

<p>Covering can cut water use by up to 50%, according to field trials by <a href="https://www.ihs.ac.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">IHS</a>. For a producer making 500 blocks a day, that is a significant saving in water and labour.</p>

<h3>Testing Block Strength: Simple Drop and Scratch Tests</h3>

<p>You do not need a laboratory to check if your blocks are strong. Two simple tests the drop test and the scratch test give you a reliable field assessment.</p>

<p><strong>Drop Test:</strong> Lift a cured block to chest height (about 1.2 metres) and drop it onto a hard, flat surface. A well-cured block should either remain intact or break into large pieces. If it shatters into small fragments, the curing was insufficient or the mix was too weak.</p>

<p><strong>Scratch Test:</strong> Use a nail or key to scratch the surface of a cured block. A properly cured block will resist scratching and leave only a faint mark. If the nail digs in easily and leaves a deep groove, the block is too soft.</p>

<h3>Avoiding Common Curing Mistakes That Weaken Blocks</h3>

<p>Most weak blocks are not the result of bad materials. They are the result of bad habits. Here are the most common mistakes and how to avoid them:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Starting curing too late:</strong> Blocks should be watered within 12 hours of demoulding. Delays cause surface drying and cracking.</li>
<li><strong>Overwatering:</strong> Soaking blocks can wash out cement and weaken the mix. Water gently think "damp," not "drenched."</li>
<li><strong>Uneven watering:</strong> Blocks at the edge of a stack dry faster. Rotate stacks and water all sides.</li>
<li><strong>Direct sunlight:</strong> Sun causes rapid drying and surface cracks. Always cure under shade.</li>
<li><strong>Stacking too early:</strong> Blocks should not be stacked high until they have cured for at least 34 days. Early stacking causes deformation.</li>
<li><strong>Ignoring weather:</strong> In rainy season, cover blocks to prevent overwatering. In dry season, water more frequently.</li>
<li><strong>Skipping the final air-dry:</strong> After curing, blocks need 12 weeks of air drying before use. This allows excess moisture to escape and strength to stabilise.</li>
</ol>

<li> <strong>Water twice daily</strong> morning and evening for 714 days.</li>
<li> <strong>Keep blocks damp, not soaked.</strong> Gentle watering only.</li>
<li> <strong>Cure under shade</strong> to prevent rapid drying and cracks.</li>
<li> <strong>Cover with plastic, grass, or burlap</strong> to retain moisture.</li>
<li> <strong>Do not stack high</strong> until day 34 of curing.</li>
<li> <strong>Test with drop and scratch tests</strong> before selling or using.</li>
<li> <strong>Air-dry 12 weeks</strong> after curing before final use.</li>
</ul>
