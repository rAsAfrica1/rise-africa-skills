Lessons

1. Calculating Cost of Production per Hectare

To know if you are making a profit, you must first know your cost of production per hectare. This is the total of all expenses divided by the number of hectares you farm. For wheat in Kenyas Rift Valley, a typical hectare might need: 100 kg of certified seed (KES 8,000), 200 kg of DAP fertiliser (KES 12,000), 150 kg of urea top dressing (KES 7,500), and 2 litres of herbicide (KES 3,000). Labour for land prep, planting, weeding, and harvesting might cost KES 25,000. If you also pay for tractor hire (KES 10,000) and transport (KES 5,000), your total cost per hectare is KES 70,500. If you harvest 3 tonnes per hectare and sell at KES 35,000 per tonne, your revenue is KES 105,000, giving a profit of KES 34,500 per hectare. In Ethiopia, costs may be lower but yields may also be lower; always use local prices. Write down every expense, even small ones like twine or fuel. Use a notebook or a simple phone app like Excel or Google Sheets. At the end of the season, divide total costs by hectares to get your cost per hectare. Compare this to your selling price. If your cost per hectare is higher than your revenue, you are losing money. You can then look for ways to reduce costs (e.g., use manure instead of some fertiliser) or increase yields. This calculation is the foundation of all farm business decisions.

2. Budgeting for Inputs, Labor, and Equipment

A budget is a plan for how you will spend money before the season starts. For a 2-hectare wheat farm in Nigeria, you might budget: seeds (200 kg at NGN 1,500/kg = NGN 300,000), fertiliser (400 kg NPK at NGN 800/kg = NGN 320,000), and herbicides (4 litres at NGN 3,000/litre = NGN 12,000). Labour: land clearing (NGN 40,000), planting (NGN 30,000), weeding (NGN 40,000), harvesting (NGN 50,000). Equipment: tractor hire for ploughing (NGN 60,000) and threshing (NGN 30,000). Total budget = NGN 882,000. You need to know where this money will come from: savings, a loan, or family. If you expect to sell 6 tonnes at NGN 200,000/tonne = NGN 1,200,000, your expected profit is NGN 318,000. But budgets must be realistic. Include a 10% contingency for unexpected costs like fuel price increases or extra weeding after heavy rains. Write your budget on paper or use a free app like Farm Budget or a simple spreadsheet. Review it monthly. If you overspend on one item, cut back on another. For equipment, consider renting instead of buying if you only need it once a year. For labour, you can reduce costs by using family labour or community work groups. A good budget helps you avoid running out of money mid-season and ensures you can pay for harvest and transport.

3. Accessing Loans and Grants for Small Farms

Many small farms need extra money for inputs. In Africa, you can get loans from microfinance institutions (MFIs), village savings and loan associations (VSLAs), or agricultural banks. For example, in Tanzania, a farmer can join a VSLA, save TZS 10,000 per week, and after 6 months borrow TZS 500,000 at low interest. Banks like Equity Bank in Kenya offer Kilimo Biashara loans for inputs, requiring a group guarantee or collateral. Grants are also available: the African Development Banks Feed Africa program, or NGOs like One Acre Fund which provides inputs on credit and takes repayment after harvest. To qualify, you often need: a national ID, a farm record, and a group of other farmers. Before taking a loan, calculate if the extra money will increase your profit enough to repay it. For example, if you borrow KES 50,000 at 10% interest to buy fertiliser, and it increases your yield from 2 to 3 tonnes per hectare, the extra 1 tonne sold at KES 35,000 gives KES 35,000, which covers the loan and interest. Avoid loans with very high interest (above 20% per year) unless you are sure of a good harvest. Always keep a copy of the loan agreement and repayment schedule. If you cannot repay, talk to the lender early. Grants do not need repayment, but they are competitive. Apply through local agricultural offices or NGOs. Keep records of how you used the money.

4. Record Keeping: Simple Ledgers and Apps

Record keeping means writing down every money transaction and farm activity. Without records, you cannot know your profit or make good decisions. Start with a simple notebook. Divide it into two sections: income and expenses. For each expense, write the date, what you bought, and the amount. For example: 5 May 2025 2 bags DAP KES 6,000. For income: 20 Sept 2025 sold 3 tonnes wheat KES 105,000. At the end of the month, add up each column. You can also use a phone app like Farm Records or AgriTracker (free on Android). These apps let you take photos of receipts and automatically calculate totals. If you have a smartphone, use Google Sheets to make a simple table. For livestock or equipment, keep a separate page for each asset: when you bought it, cost, and repairs. Record labour too: who worked, how many days, and how much you paid. This helps you calculate cost per hectare. At the end of the season, your records show if you made a profit. They also help when applying for a loan: lenders want to see your records. Keep records for at least 5 years. Store them in a safe, dry place. If you use a notebook, keep it in a plastic bag to protect from rain. Share records with your family so everyone knows the farms finances.

5. Managing Risk: Weather, Pests, and Price Fluctuations

Farming is risky. Weather can fail, pests can attack, and prices can drop. To manage weather risk, plant drought-tolerant wheat varieties like Danda or Kenya King if your area has short rains. Use early maturing varieties (e.g., 90 days) to escape drought. If you have irrigation, you can reduce risk. For pests, scout your field weekly. For aphids, use neem extract or recommended pesticides. For rust, plant resistant varieties and apply fungicide if needed. Crop rotation with legumes (e.g., beans) breaks pest cycles. Price fluctuations: wheat prices often drop at harvest when everyone sells. To avoid low prices, store your grain in a hermetic bag (e.g., GrainPro) and sell later when prices rise. In Ethiopia, prices can double from harvest to lean season. You can also join a cooperative that negotiates better prices. Another risk is input price increases. Buy inputs early or in bulk with other farmers. For financial risk, keep a small savings fund for emergencies. Consider crop insurance: in Kenya, Kilimo Salama offers weather-index insurance. In Nigeria, NAIC provides insurance. The premium is small (e.g., 5% of expected yield value). If you have a loan, insurance can protect you. Always have a backup plan: if wheat fails, do you have another crop or off-farm income? Diversify: grow a small plot of vegetables or keep chickens. These steps reduce the chance of total loss and help you sleep better.

6. Planning for Profit and Reinvestment

Profit is what is left after all costs are paid. To plan for profit, set a target. For example, if you farm 2 hectares of barley in Morocco, aim for a profit of MAD 10,000 per hectare. Work backwards: if barley sells at MAD 3,000 per tonne, you need 4 tonnes per hectare to get MAD 12,000 revenue. If your cost per hectare is MAD 8,000, profit is MAD 4,000. To reach MAD 10,000, you need either higher yield (5 tonnes) or lower costs. Once you make a profit, reinvest some of it. Reinvestment means using part of the profit to improve your farm. For example, buy a water pump (MAD 5,000) to irrigate and increase yields. Or buy a better seed variety. Or build a small storage shed to reduce post-harvest losses. A good rule: reinvest at least 30% of profit each season. Keep 20% as savings for emergencies, and use the rest for family needs. Track your reinvestments: after 3 years, you might have a pump, a shed, and more land. This grows your farm business. Also, plan for the next season before the current one ends. Order inputs early, prepare land, and secure labour. Write a simple business plan: what you will grow, how much, costs, expected income, and profit. Review it with a mentor or extension officer. Remember, profit is not just money in your pocket; it is the fuel for your farms future.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Farm Business and Financial Management for Wheat & Barley in Africa

Wheat and barley are among the most capital-intensive cereals a smallholder or emerging farmer can grow in Africa. Unlike maize or sorghum, they demand purchased seed of a specific variety, basal and top-dress fertiliser, reliable irrigation or well-timed rainfed planting, and often mechanised land preparation. That means the business of growing wheat or barley is decided before the first seed goes into the soil in the budget, the loan application, the ledger, and the risk plan. This Practical Reference consolidates the seven lessons in this module into one working document you can print, laminate, and keep in the farm office or the back of the pickup.

1. Calculating Cost of Production per Hectare

Cost of production (CoP) per hectare is the single most important number on a wheat or barley farm. It tells you the break-even price and yield, and it is the number a lender will ask for first. Build it in four blocks:

  • Land preparation: ploughing, harrowing, levelling. In Kenya's Narok and Uasin Gishu wheat belts, tractor hire typically runs KES 8,00012,000 per hectare per pass.
  • Inputs: certified seed (150200 kg/ha for wheat), fertiliser (DAP at planting, CAN/Urea top-dress), herbicides, and fungicide for rust control.
  • Labour: planting, weeding, bird-scaring, harvesting, threshing, bagging. Peak labour is the biggest hidden cost.
  • Overheads: land rent, depreciation, interest, insurance, transport to market.

Divide total cost by expected yield to get cost per tonne. If your CoP is US$280/t and the market price is US$250/t, you are losing money before you even harvest fix the budget, not the field, first.

2. Budgeting for Inputs, Labour, and Equipment

A crop budget is a forward-looking plan, not a historical record. Use a simple three-column format: Item | Quantity | Cost. Budget at prevailing market prices plus 10% buffer for fertiliser and fuel, because these swing sharply with global markets and exchange rates.

Separate cash costs (seed, fertiliser, hired labour) from non-cash costs (family labour, own tractor depreciation). Many small farms look profitable on paper only because family labour is unpaid. Price family labour at the local casual wage rate so your budget reflects reality.

3. Accessing Loans and Grants for Small Farms

Wheat and barley are attractive to lenders because they have structured markets, but small farms often lack collateral. Practical routes in Africa include:

  • Warehouse receipt financing store grain in a certified warehouse and borrow against the receipt (widely used in Kenya, Tanzania, Zambia, Ghana).
  • Value chain / contract farming credit millers and seed companies advance inputs against a delivery contract.
  • Group lending and SACCOs joint liability replaces individual collateral.
  • Government and donor grant windows e.g., Kenya's Agricultural Finance Corporation, Nigeria's Anchor Borrowers' Programme, AGRA-supported input credit schemes.

Before applying, prepare: a one-page enterprise budget, a simple cash-flow forecast, proof of land use, and a repayment plan tied to harvest month. Lenders reject vague plans, not small farms.

4. Record Keeping: Simple Ledgers and Apps

Records turn farming from a gamble into a business. At minimum keep four books:

  1. Cash book every shilling/cedi/naira in and out, dated.
  2. Input ledger what you bought, from whom, at what price.
  3. Labour book who worked, how many days, how much paid.
  4. Harvest and sales book bags harvested, bags sold, price per bag.

Free or low-cost mobile tools used across Africa include FarmDrive (Kenya), Apollo Agriculture (Kenya/Zambia), Farmerline (Ghana), and simple spreadsheets on a smartphone. A paper exercise book with ruled columns works perfectly if used daily.

5. Managing Risk: Weather, Pests, and Price Fluctuations

Wheat and barley face three dominant risks in Africa: drought and heat stress at flowering, stem rust and yellow rust, and price collapse at harvest when everyone sells at once. Mitigation tools that actually work on small farms:

  • Plant rust-resistant varieties (e.g., Kenya's KALRO-released varieties such as Kenya Robin and Kenya Tai).
  • Stagger planting dates by 23 weeks to spread weather risk.
  • Use index-based crop insurance (e.g., ACRE Africa, Pula) where available.
  • Store grain and sell in the off-season when prices rise 2040%.
  • Diversify: never put more than 60% of the farm into one crop.

SAFETY & COMPLIANCE WARNING

Do not apply for a loan or input credit without a written repayment plan tied to your expected harvest. Agrochemicals used in wheat and barley (herbicides, fungicides, insecticides) must be handled with gloves, boots, and a face mask, stored in a locked, labelled cabinet away from food and children, and applied strictly according to the label. Never spray within the pre-harvest interval stated on the label. In many African countries, exporting grain with pesticide residues above Codex limits will cause the entire consignment to be rejected and the loss is yours, not the buyer's.

6. Planning for Profit and Reinvestment

Profit is not what is left after harvest it is what you deliberately allocate. A simple reinvestment rule used by successful emerging farmers: 50% of net profit to reinvestment (seed, equipment, irrigation, storage), 30% to family drawings, 20% to a reserve fund for the next season's inputs. Without a reserve, one bad season wipes out the next.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Calculate cost of production per hectare before planting know your break-even price and yield.
  2. Build a written budget with a 10% price buffer on fertiliser and fuel.
  3. Keep a daily cash book and a labour book no exceptions.
  4. Match any loan repayment date to your harvest and sale month.
  5. Plant at least one rust-resistant variety and stagger planting dates.
  6. Store grain safely and sell in the off-season where storage is available.
  7. Allocate net profit: 50% reinvestment, 30% family, 20% reserve.
  8. Review your records monthly and adjust the budget before the next season.
