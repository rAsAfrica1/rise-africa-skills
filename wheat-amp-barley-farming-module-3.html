1. Understanding Wheat Classes: Bread, Biscuit, and Durum

Wheat is not just wheat. There are three main classes you need to know: bread wheat, biscuit wheat, and durum wheat. Each has different uses and growing requirements. Bread wheat is the most common. It has high protein and strong gluten, making it perfect for yeast breads. In Africa, bread wheat is grown in Ethiopia, Kenya, and South Africa. Varieties like 'Kakaba' in Ethiopia or 'Kenya Nyangumi' are popular. Bread wheat needs 400-600 mm of rainfall or irrigation. It takes 120-150 days to mature. Biscuit wheat, also called soft wheat, has weaker gluten. It is used for cakes, biscuits, and pastries. It yields well in cooler, wetter areas. In Kenya, 'Kenya Fahari' is a biscuit wheat. It matures in 110-130 days. Durum wheat is the hardest. It has very high protein and is used for pasta, couscous, and semolina. Durum thrives in hot, dry conditions. It is grown in Ethiopia, Morocco, and Algeria. 'Yerer' and 'Ude' are durum varieties in Ethiopia. Durum needs 300-500 mm of rainfall and 100-120 days to mature. When choosing a class, think about your market. If you sell to a bread bakery, grow bread wheat. If you supply a pasta factory, grow durum. If you sell to a biscuit company, grow biscuit wheat. Also check your local climate. Durum does best in dry heat. Bread wheat is more adaptable. Biscuit wheat needs cool, moist conditions. Always test a small plot before planting a whole field. Talk to other farmers and extension officers. They can tell you which class works best in your area. Remember, seed of the wrong class will not get a good price. So choose carefully from the start.

2. Barley Types: Two-Row, Six-Row, and Malting Barley

Barley comes in three main types: two-row, six-row, and malting barley. Two-row barley has two rows of seeds on each spike. The seeds are large and uniform. It is often used for food and feed. Two-row barley is popular in Ethiopia and Kenya. It yields well in highlands. Varieties like 'HB-42' and 'Sabini' are two-row. It matures in 90-120 days. Six-row barley has six rows of seeds. The seeds are smaller and less uniform. It is mainly used for animal feed. Six-row barley is grown in Egypt and Morocco. It tolerates poorer soils. Varieties like 'Giza 123' and 'ACSAD 176' are six-row. It matures in 80-110 days. Malting barley is a special type. It must have low protein and high starch. It is used to make beer and malt. Malting barley fetches a higher price. But it needs specific conditions. It requires cool, moist weather during grain filling. It also needs uniform maturity. In Africa, malting barley is grown in Ethiopia, Kenya, and South Africa. Varieties like 'Grace' and 'Maltings' are used. Malting barley must meet strict quality standards. The grains must be plump and even. They must have low nitrogen. If you grow malting barley, you must dry it properly. You also need a contract with a maltster. Without a buyer, malting barley is risky. For most smallholder farmers, two-row or six-row barley for feed or food is safer. They are easier to grow and have a ready local market. Choose based on your soil, rainfall, and market. If you have irrigation and a contract, try malting barley. If not, stick to feed barley. Always buy certified seed to ensure the type is true.

3. Drought-Tolerant and Disease-Resistant Varieties for Africa

Africa has unpredictable rainfall. Drought can wipe out your crop. That is why you need drought-tolerant varieties. These varieties have deep roots and can survive dry spells. They also mature early. For wheat, look for varieties like 'Danda'a' in Ethiopia. It matures in 85-95 days and yields well with little water. 'Kingbird' is another drought-tolerant wheat in Kenya. It can produce 2-3 tonnes per hectare with only 300 mm of rain. For barley, 'Mari' and 'Shage' in Ethiopia are drought-tolerant. They mature in 80-90 days. 'Giza 123' in Egypt is also drought-tolerant. Disease is another big problem. Rusts, smuts, and bunts can destroy your crop. Disease-resistant varieties are your best defense. For wheat, look for resistance to stem rust, yellow rust, and leaf rust. In Ethiopia, 'Kakaba' is resistant to stem rust. 'Hidase' is resistant to yellow rust. In Kenya, 'Kenya Tai' is resistant to both. For barley, look for resistance to barley yellow dwarf virus and net blotch. 'HB-42' is resistant to net blotch. 'Sabini' is resistant to yellow dwarf. When you buy seed, ask for the resistance profile. The seed label or the extension officer can tell you. Also, rotate crops. Do not grow wheat after wheat. Use a legume like faba bean or lentil in between. This breaks disease cycles. Plant on time. Early planting helps avoid rust. Do not over-fertilize with nitrogen. Too much nitrogen makes plants soft and prone to disease. Use certified seed. It is clean and disease-free. If you save your own seed, treat it before planting. You can also plant mixtures of varieties. This spreads risk. If one variety fails, others may survive. Always check with your local research station. They test varieties for your area. They can recommend the best ones for drought and disease.

4. Where to Buy Certified Seed Locally

Certified seed is clean, high-quality seed. It has been inspected and tested. It will give you better yields than saved seed. But where can you buy it? In most African countries, certified seed is sold by licensed agro-dealers. These are shops that sell farm inputs. They are often found in district towns. In Ethiopia, you can buy from the Ethiopian Seed Enterprise (ESE) or from cooperatives. In Kenya, the Kenya Seed Company and Freshco Seeds sell certified wheat and barley. In Tanzania, the Tanzania Seed Company (TANSEED) is a good source. In Nigeria, the National Agricultural Seeds Council (NASC) licenses dealers. You can also buy from research stations. For example, the Ethiopian Institute of Agricultural Research (EIAR) sells seed of new varieties. The Kenya Agricultural and Livestock Research Organization (KALRO) also sells seed. Another option is farmer cooperatives. Many cooperatives buy certified seed in bulk and sell to members at a discount. Ask your local extension officer. They know the nearest certified seed dealer. When buying, check the label. It should show the variety name, lot number, germination percentage, and purity. It should also have a certification tag. Do not buy seed from open sacks or without a label. That seed may be fake or poor quality. Also check the date. Seed older than one year may have low germination. Buy from a reputable dealer. Ask other farmers where they buy. Avoid buying from roadside vendors. They often sell uncertified seed. If you cannot find certified seed, you can buy from a trusted farmer who grows certified seed. But you must test it first. Remember, certified seed costs more, but it pays off. It can increase your yield by 20-30%. So invest in good seed.

5. Testing Seed Germination Before Planting

Before you plant, you must test your seed. Even certified seed can have low germination if it was stored badly. A simple germination test saves you money and time. Here is how to do it. First, collect a sample. Take seeds from different parts of the bag. Mix them together. Then count out 100 seeds. You can use 50 or 20 if you have few seeds. But 100 is easy to calculate. Next, get a clean cloth or paper towel. Moisten it with water. It should be damp, not dripping. Spread the seeds evenly on the cloth. Cover them with another moist cloth. Roll it up. Put the roll in a plastic bag. Keep it in a warm place, around 20-25C. Do not put it in direct sunlight. Check the seeds every day. Keep the cloth moist. After 7 days, count how many seeds have sprouted. A seed is sprouted if the root is at least 1 cm long. The number of sprouted seeds is your germination percentage. For example, if 85 out of 100 seeds sprout, germination is 85%. For wheat and barley, good germination is 85% or higher. If it is 70-85%, you can still plant, but use more seed. If it is below 70%, do not plant it. Buy new seed. You can also do a float test. Put seeds in a glass of water. Good seeds sink. Dead seeds float. Remove the floaters. But this test is not as accurate as the germination test. Another method is the rag-doll test. Use a piece of old cloth. Same steps. Always test before planting. If you store seed, test it again before the next season. Store seed in a cool, dry place. Use airtight containers. Add ash or neem leaves to keep insects away. Never store seed in a damp place. It will rot or germinate too early. With a simple test, you can be sure your seed will grow.

6. Seed Treatment: Protecting Against Smuts and Bunts

Smuts and bunts are fungal diseases. They attack wheat and barley. They can reduce your yield by 20-50%. The good news is you can prevent them with seed treatment. Seed treatment is coating the seed with a fungicide before planting. This kills the fungus on the seed or in the soil. Common smuts are loose smut, covered smut, and flag smut. Bunts include common bunt and dwarf bunt. These diseases survive in the seed or soil. If you plant untreated seed, the fungus grows with the plant. It produces black spores instead of grain. To treat seed, you need a fungicide. Common ones are carboxin, thiram, and tebuconazole. You can buy them from agro-dealers. They come as powders or liquids. For example, Vitavax and Apron are popular brands. Always read the label. Use the recommended dose. For wheat, a common dose is 2-3 grams of fungicide per kilogram of seed. For barley, use 2 grams per kilogram. You can treat seed in a bucket or a drum. Put the seed in the bucket. Add the fungicide. Mix well with a stick or gloved hands. Make sure every seed is coated. Wear gloves and a mask. Do not eat or drink while treating. After treatment, let the seed dry in the shade. Do not dry in direct sun. Plant within a few days. If you store treated seed, keep it away from food and feed. It is poisonous. Also, do not use treated seed for animal feed or human consumption. Another method is hot water treatment. Soak seed in hot water at 52-54C for 10 minutes. Then cool and dry. This kills loose smut. But it is tricky. You need a thermometer. If water is too hot, it kills the seed. If too cold, it does not kill the fungus. So chemical treatment is safer. Always buy certified seed. It is often already treated. But check the label. If not, treat it yourself. Treating seed is cheap. It costs about $1-2 per 10 kg. It protects your crop. So do not skip this step.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Seed Selection, Testing, and Treatment for Wheat & Barley in Africa

Seed is the single highest-leverage input in a cereal enterprise. It typically represents 1525% of variable costs but determines 6080% of yield potential. For African wheat and barley growers whether in the Ethiopian highlands, the Kenyan Rift Valley, the Egyptian Delta, or the irrigated schemes of Sudan, Zambia, and Zimbabwe the practical decisions made before planting are where profit is won or lost. This reference module distils the technical guidance from the seven lessons in this unit into a field-ready toolkit: how to match variety to market, how to verify seed quality, and how to protect germination and establishment against smuts and bunts.

Because African cereal markets are segmented bread flour, biscuit flour, pasta semolina, and malting barley variety choice must be driven by the buyer's specification, not by habit or by whatever is cheapest at the agro-dealer. The business logic is straightforward: a variety that fails the miller's specification is a feed-grade crop sold at a 3050% discount, regardless of tonnage.

1. Matching Variety to End-Use Market

Wheat in Africa is traded in three main commercial classes. Bread wheat (hard red or hard white) requires high protein (1114%) and strong gluten for leavened bread. Biscuit/soft wheat needs low protein (810%) and weak gluten so dough spreads rather than springs. Durum wheat is a distinct tetraploid species (Triticum turgidum subsp. durum) with very hard, vitreous grain used for pasta and couscous a major crop in North Africa (Egypt, Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia) and increasingly in Ethiopia and Sudan.

Barley is similarly segmented. Two-row barley produces plumper, more uniform grain and is preferred for malting. Six-row barley yields more tillers and is more widely adapted, often used for feed and some malting. Malting barley must meet strict specifications: protein 1012%, low grain nitrogen, high germinative energy (>95%), and specific grain size (>85% above 2.5 mm). Ethiopian and Kenyan highland barley is increasingly contracted by breweries (e.g., Heineken's local sourcing programmes in Ethiopia, Rwanda, and Nigeria), giving smallholders a premium market but only if variety and quality specs are met.

2. Drought-Tolerant and Disease-Resistant Varieties for Africa

African cereal production is dominated by rainfed systems with erratic rainfall. Variety selection must therefore prioritise drought tolerance and resistance to the diseases that cause the greatest losses: stem rust (notably the Ug99 lineage), yellow rust, leaf rust, septoria, and Fusarium head blight in wheat; and net blotch, scald, and barley yellow dwarf virus in barley.

CIMMYT and ICARDA have released drought-tolerant wheat lines through the Wheat Breeding for Africa programme, including varieties such as Danda'a, Kakaba, Kingbird, and Wane, which are widely grown in Ethiopia, Kenya, and Sudan. For durum, ICARDA lines such as Om Rabia and Nasr are used in North Africa. In barley, ICARDA's Gobe, Bahati, and Shage are popular in Ethiopia for both food and malting. Growers should always check the national variety list (e.g., Ethiopia's Ministry of Agriculture crop variety register, Kenya's KALRO released varieties list) for the most current recommendations.

3. Where to Buy Certified Seed Locally

Counterfeit and recycled seed is a major cause of yield loss across Africa. Certified seed carries a blue or official label with lot number, germination percentage, purity, and expiry date. In practice, growers should buy from:

  • National seed agencies: Ethiopia Seed Enterprise (ESE), Kenya Seed Company, Tanzania's ASA, Zambia's SeedCo, Zimbabwe's Seed Co International.
  • Licensed agro-dealers registered with the national seed trade association (e.g., AFSTA members).
  • Cooperatives and farmer unions with formal seed multiplication contracts (common in Ethiopia, Rwanda, and Malawi).
  • Breeder or foundation seed units of national research institutes (EIAR, KALRO, ARC, INRAN) for the newest releases.

Always request the seed lot certificate and check the germination percentage printed on the label. Avoid open sacks sold at markets without labels these are the primary vector for smut and bunt introduction.

4. Testing Seed Germination Before Planting

Even certified seed can lose vigour through poor storage. A simple germination test costs nothing but a paper towel and seven days. Count 100 seeds at random, place them on moist paper towel or cotton cloth, roll, keep moist and warm (2025C), and count normal seedlings after 7 days. Target: >85% germination for wheat and barley. Below 80%, increase seeding rate or reject the lot. Below 70%, do not plant the economics do not work.

5. Seed Treatment: Protecting Against Smuts and Bunts

Smuts and bunts loose smut, covered smut, common bunt, and dwarf bunt are seed- and soil-borne fungal diseases that can destroy 2050% of a cereal crop. They are largely preventable with correct seed treatment. In Africa, the most common active ingredients registered for cereal seed treatment include tebuconazole, fludioxonil, carboxin, thiram, and metalaxyl (the latter mainly for downy mildew and Pythium).

Treatment must be applied evenly either by the seed supplier (preferred) or on-farm using a rotary drum or shovel mixing on a clean tarpaulin. Wear gloves, a mask, and long sleeves. Never treat seed intended for food or feed. Store treated seed separately and label clearly.

SAFETY WARNING SEED TREATMENT CHEMICALS

Seed treatment chemicals are highly toxic if ingested, inhaled, or absorbed through skin. Always:

  • Wear chemical-resistant gloves, a dust mask, goggles, and long sleeves.
  • Never eat, drink, or smoke during treatment.
  • Never use treated seed for food, feed, or oil extraction it is poisonous.
  • Keep treated seed locked away from children and livestock.
  • Wash hands and equipment thoroughly after use; dispose of containers at approved collection points.
  • Follow the label rate exactly overdosing damages germination; underdosing fails to control smuts.

Reference: FAO/WHO International Code of Conduct on Pesticide Management; national pesticide registration authorities.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Confirm the buyer's specification before choosing a class (bread, biscuit, durum) or barley type (2-row, 6-row, malting).
  2. Match variety to rainfall and disease pressure select drought-tolerant, rust-resistant lines from the current national variety list.
  3. Buy only certified seed from licensed dealers, cooperatives, or national seed agencies check the blue label and lot number.
  4. Run a 7-day germination test on a 100-seed sample; reject lots below 80% germination.
  5. Adjust seeding rate using the formula: base rate (germination % 100).
  6. Treat seed against smuts and bunts with a registered fungicide or buy pre-treated seed.
  7. Store treated seed separately, clearly labelled, away from food, feed, children, and livestock.
  8. Keep records of variety, lot number, germination %, treatment product, and date essential for traceability and buyer contracts.
