Lessons

1. Selection and Preparation of Stem Cuttings

Selecting the right stem cuttings is the first step to a healthy cassava crop. Choose stems from mature plants that are 8 to 12 months old, free from disease, and at least 2.5 cm thick. Avoid stems that are too young (soft, green) or too old (woody, dry). A good stem has prominent nodes (buds) and is firm. Cut stems into pieces 20 to 30 cm long, each with at least 5 to 7 nodes. Use a sharp, clean machete or pruning shears to make clean cuts; jagged cuts invite rot.

After cutting, treat the cuttings to prevent disease. Soak them in a fungicide solution (e.g., 1% benomyl) for 5 minutes, or use wood ash as a natural alternative. In dry areas, dip the cut ends in melted wax to reduce moisture loss. Let the cuttings dry in a shaded, well-ventilated area for 1 to 2 days before planting. This allows the cut surfaces to heal and reduces the risk of rot.

Proper storage is crucial if you cannot plant immediately. Store cuttings upright in a cool, shaded place, with the basal ends (the end that was closer to the ground) buried in moist sand or soil. Never store them in plastic bags or in direct sunlight. They can be stored for up to 2 weeks this way.

When planting, ensure the basal end is planted downwards. The buds on the stem will sprout into new shoots. If you plant upside down, the shoots will struggle to emerge. Always plant fresh cuttings; old or shriveled cuttings have low sprouting rates. With good selection and preparation, you can achieve over 90% sprouting. Remember, healthy cuttings mean a healthy crop.

2. Planting Methods: Horizontal, Vertical, and Slanting

Cassava can be planted in three main ways: horizontal, vertical, and slanting. Each method suits different soil and climate conditions. Horizontal planting: Lay the cutting flat in a furrow 5-10 cm deep, then cover with soil. This method is good for dry areas because the cutting is fully covered, reducing moisture loss. However, it can lead to rot in waterlogged soils. Space cuttings 50-75 cm apart in rows.

Vertical planting: Push the cutting upright into the soil, leaving only 2-3 nodes above the surface. This is ideal for heavy soils and areas with good rainfall. It allows roots to develop evenly and is easy to mechanize. Plant at a depth of 10-15 cm. Vertical planting can result in deeper roots and better drought tolerance.

Slanting: Insert the cutting at a 45-degree angle, with 2-3 nodes above ground. This is a compromise between horizontal and vertical. It is widely used in Africa because it works in many soil types. It reduces rot because part of the cutting is above soil, and it allows for easy weeding.

Whichever method you choose, ensure the buds point upwards. Plant at the start of the rainy season when soil is moist. Use a spacing of 1 m x 1 m for sole cassava, or 1 m x 0.5 m for intercropping. After planting, firm the soil around the cutting to remove air pockets. Water if no rain within 2 days. With proper planting, sprouts appear in 1-2 weeks. Choose the method that fits your soil and rainfall for best results.

3. Spacing and Plant Density Recommendations

Correct spacing is key to high cassava yields. The general recommendation for sole cassava is 1 m between rows and 1 m between plants within the row. This gives a plant density of 10,000 plants per hectare. However, this can vary with variety and soil fertility. For branching varieties, use 1 m x 1 m; for non-branching, you can use 1 m x 0.8 m.

In intercropping systems, increase spacing to reduce competition. For example, when intercropping with maize, use 1 m x 0.5 m for cassava, and plant maize in between. This gives cassava 20,000 plants per hectare, but maize will compete for nutrients. Adjust based on your goal: if you want more cassava, give it more space.

Plant density affects root size and yield. Higher density (e.g., 12,000-15,000 plants/ha) can increase total yield but may reduce individual root size. Lower density (8,000-10,000 plants/ha) gives larger roots but lower total yield. For fresh market, larger roots are preferred; for processing, total yield matters more.

Measure your field and mark planting spots with pegs and ropes. Use a hoe or dibber to make holes at the correct spacing. Plant in straight rows to ease weeding and harvesting. If you plant too close, plants compete for light, water, and nutrients, leading to stunted growth and poor yields. If too far, you waste land. Always follow recommended spacing for your variety and cropping system. Consult your local extension officer for specific advice.

4. Intercropping Systems with Cassava

Intercropping cassava with other crops is common in Africa because it maximizes land use and provides income before cassava matures. Cassava is slow-growing initially, so you can plant short-season crops between the rows. Good companion crops include maize, beans, groundnuts, cowpeas, and vegetables like okra and pepper.

When intercropping, plant cassava first at the recommended spacing (e.g., 1 m x 1 m). Then plant the companion crop in the spaces between. For maize, plant 2 seeds per hole, 50 cm from cassava. For legumes like beans, plant 2-3 seeds per hole, 30 cm from cassava. This reduces competition.

Timing is important: plant the companion crop at the same time as cassava or shortly after, so it matures before cassava canopy closes. Maize and beans mature in 3-4 months, while cassava takes 9-12 months. This way, you harvest the intercrop before cassava needs all the space.

Intercropping has benefits: it suppresses weeds, reduces soil erosion, and improves soil fertility if legumes are used. However, avoid planting tall crops that shade cassava, like sorghum or tall maize varieties. Also, do not intercrop with crops that share pests and diseases, such as tomato (which can attract whiteflies).

Use a crop rotation plan to avoid depleting soil. After harvesting the intercrop, leave cassava to grow alone. This system can increase total farm income by 30-50% compared to sole cassava. Always consider market demand for the intercrop and your labor availability.

5. Weed Management: Manual, Mechanical, and Chemical

Weeds are a major threat to cassava, especially in the first 3 months when the crop is small. Weeds compete for water, nutrients, and light, reducing yields by up to 50%. Effective weed management combines manual, mechanical, and chemical methods.

Manual weeding: Use a hoe or hand to remove weeds. This is the most common method in Africa. Weed at 3-4 weeks after planting, then again at 8-12 weeks. After that, the cassava canopy shades out most weeds. For small farms, manual weeding is practical and cheap. But it is labor-intensive: one person can weed about 0.1 hectare per day.

Mechanical weeding: Use a tractor-mounted cultivator or a hand-pushed weeder. This is faster for large farms. However, be careful not to damage cassava roots. Mechanical weeding is best before the roots start to swell (first 2 months).

Chemical weeding: Use herbicides like glyphosate or atrazine. Apply pre-emergence herbicides (e.g., pendimethalin) right after planting to prevent weed seeds from germinating. For post-emergence, use selective herbicides that kill weeds but not cassava. Always follow label instructions and use protective gear. Chemical weeding is effective but can be costly and harmful to the environment if misused.

Integrated approach: Combine methods. For example, apply pre-emergence herbicide, then do one manual weeding at 8 weeks. Mulching also helps suppress weeds. Keep your field clean to ensure good cassava growth. Regular monitoring is key: remove weeds before they set seed.

6. Mulching and Soil Moisture Conservation

Mulching is covering the soil surface with organic or inorganic materials to conserve moisture, suppress weeds, and improve soil health. For cassava, mulching is especially beneficial in dry areas or during dry spells.

Use organic mulches such as dry grass, straw, leaves, or crop residues. Spread a layer 5-10 cm thick around the cassava plants, leaving a small gap around the stem to prevent rot. Apply mulch after planting, when sprouts are about 10-15 cm tall. Good sources include maize stover, rice straw, and dry banana leaves. Avoid mulching with fresh, green material as it may attract termites.

Mulching conserves soil moisture by reducing evaporation. It also keeps soil temperature stable, which helps root development. Weeds are suppressed because they lack light. As mulch decomposes, it adds organic matter, improving soil fertility and structure. This can increase cassava yields by 20-30%.

In addition to mulching, other soil moisture conservation practices include: planting on ridges or mounds to capture rainwater, using cover crops like mucuna or cowpea, and adding manure or compost to improve water-holding capacity. Terracing or contour ploughing on slopes reduces runoff.

During dry season, mulch heavily to keep soil moist. If you use plastic mulch, it is effective but costly and not biodegradable; remove it after harvest. For most African smallholders, organic mulching is the best option. Combine mulching with proper spacing and weeding for maximum benefit. Monitor soil moisture by digging a small hole; if dry below 5 cm, consider irrigation if available. Mulching is a simple, low-cost way to protect your cassava crop.

<h2>Practical Reference: Planting and Crop Management for Cassava</h2>

<p>Cassava is one of Africa's most strategic crops. It tolerates poor soils, survives erratic rainfall, and produces a storable root that feeds households and factories alike. But the gap between average African yields of 812 t/ha and achievable yields of 2540 t/ha is not genetics alone. It is management. This Practical Reference consolidates the six lessons in this module into a single field-ready document you can print, laminate, and carry to the farm. Every recommendation is framed for commercial viability: cost, labour, and return on investment.</p>

<p>Whether you are planting 0.5 ha for household food security or 50 ha to supply a starch processor, the decisions you make in the first six weeks after land preparation determine 70% of your final yield. Get the planting material right, get the geometry right, and keep the crop clean until canopy closure. After that, cassava largely manages itself.</p>

<h3>1. Selection and Preparation of Stem Cuttings</h3>
<p>The cutting is your seed. A poor cutting cannot be rescued by good agronomy. Select stems from 818 month old plants that are free of Cassava Mosaic Disease (CMD) and Cassava Brown Streak Disease (CBSD). Stems should be 23 cm thick, with healthy brown-green bark, and at least 57 viable buds. Avoid stems from plants that have produced very few roots you will propagate low yielders.</p>
<p>Cut into 2030 cm lengths with a clean, sharp machete or secateurs. A clean cut heals faster and resists rot. Treat cuttings by dipping in a fungicide-insecticide slurry (e.g., imidacloprid + mancozeb) if you are in a whitefly pressure zone this protects against CMD vectors during the critical first month. Store cuttings in a shaded, ventilated stack for no more than 35 days before planting. Longer storage reduces sprouting vigour by up to 40%.</p>

<h3>2. Planting Methods: Horizontal, Vertical, and Slanting</h3>
<p>Your choice of orientation is a risk-management decision, not a tradition. Horizontal planting buries the whole cutting, giving more buds contact with soil and higher sprouting excellent on light, well-drained soils. But in heavy clay or waterlogged fields, horizontal cuttings rot. Vertical planting buries two-thirds of the cutting with buds pointing up; it is the safest choice in dry zones and sandy soils because the buried portion taps deeper moisture. Slanting at 45 is the most widely used across Africa: it balances sprouting, root development, and rot resistance.</p>

<h3>3. Spacing and Plant Density</h3>
<p>Spacing controls the trade-off between root size and root number. Dense planting gives more roots per hectare but smaller individual roots fine for processing (starch, gari) where uniformity matters less. Wider spacing gives larger roots for fresh market and higher starch content per root. On a business basis, calculate expected revenue per hectare against your target market.</p>

<h3>4. Intercropping Systems</h3>
<p>Cassava is slow to canopy 34 months. That gap is expensive empty land. Intercropping with short-cycle legumes (cowpea, groundnut, soybean) or maize fixes nitrogen, suppresses weeds, and generates early cash flow before the cassava harvest. The classic African systems are cassava + maize + cowpea, or cassava + groundnut. Plant the companion crop first or simultaneously in the same row or in alternating rows. Keep cassava rows 1 m apart and place intercepts between them. Avoid shading crops like tall maize varieties that compete for light.</p>

<h3>5. Weed Management</h3>
<p>Weeds are the single biggest yield thief in cassava. The critical period is the first 34 months before canopy closure. After that, cassava's own canopy suppresses most weeds. Manual weeding (hoe or hand) is the default for smallholders: 23 rounds, ideally at 34 weeks, 810 weeks, and 1214 weeks. Mechanical weeding with a tractor-mounted cultivator is viable above 5 ha but risks root damage if too deep. Chemical control: pre-emergence herbicides such as pendimethalin or S-metolachlor applied within 48 hours of planting, followed by one post-emergence selective herbicide (e.g., fluazifop-p-butyl for grasses) if needed. Always rotate herbicide modes of action to prevent resistance.</p>

<h3>6. Mulching and Soil Moisture Conservation</h3>
<p>Cassava is drought-tolerant, but moisture stress in the first 8 weeks reduces sprouting and early root bulking. Mulch with crop residues (maize stover, rice straw, grass cuttings) at 23 t/ha, spread 510 cm thick around the base of plants but not touching the stem. Mulch reduces evaporation by 3050%, suppresses weeds, and adds organic matter. On sloping land, combine mulch with contour ridges or vetiver grass strips to reduce runoff. In dry savanna zones, mulching alone can lift yields by 1525% with no additional fertiliser.</p>
