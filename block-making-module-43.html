Machine Maintenance

8 lessons · 30 quiz questions · Business

1 Daily maintenance checklist

Machine downtime stops production. Daily maintenance prevents 80% of breakdowns.

Daily checks (before starting):

  1. Grease all lubrication points (refer to machine manual for locations)
  2. Clean the mould — remove any dried concrete
  3. Check bolts and nuts — tighten if loose
  4. Check hydraulic oil level (if hydraulic machine)
  5. Check electrical connections — no loose wires, no exposed cables
  6. Test emergency stop
  7. Run machine empty for 1 minute — listen for unusual noise
  8. Clean the mixer and conveyor if using

Time: 15–20 minutes. Do it every morning before first production. Record on a daily maintenance sheet.

💡 15 minutes of daily care prevents 3 days of downtime. Best ROI in the business.

2 Weekly checks

Deeper inspection every week. Catches developing problems before they become failures.

Weekly tasks:

  • Check vibrator motor mounts — loose mounts cause vibration problems
  • Inspect belts and pulleys — look for cracks, fraying, wear
  • Check hydraulic oil quality — dark or gritty means change needed
  • Check oil filters and air filters
  • Inspect electrical panel — dust buildup causes shorts
  • Test all limit switches and safety interlocks
  • Check mould wear — dimensions and surface condition
  • Inspect hydraulic hoses for leaks or cracks
  • Clean the whole machine thoroughly

Time: 60–90 minutes. Do it on a slower day (e.g. Saturday morning if you produce on other days).

3 Lubrication points

Friction destroys machines. Lubrication is not optional.

Common lubrication points on block machines:

  • Guide rails and slides — daily grease
  • Hydraulic cylinders — oil level daily, seals checked weekly
  • Bearings on vibrator motor — grease monthly (sealed bearings don't need regular greasing)
  • Chain drives — oil weekly
  • Gearbox — oil level weekly, change yearly
  • Pivot points on lever arms — grease weekly

Grease types:

  • Lithium-based grease — general purpose
  • Moly grease — high-load applications
  • Graphite grease — high-temperature applications

Use the grease type specified in your machine manual. Wrong grease causes more damage than no grease.

Cost: $30–$80 per month for grease. Trivial compared to a $5,000 repair.

4 Replacing worn moulds

Mould wear affects block quality. Replace before it hurts sales.

Signs of mould wear:

  • Blocks are getting out of tolerance (>2 mm variance)
  • Sharp edges becoming rounded
  • Surface scratches showing on blocks
  • Cracks appearing in blocks after demoulding
  • Worn corners causing chipped blocks
  • Loose bolts or fittings on the mould

Mould lifespan: 10,000–30,000 blocks for standard steel moulds. Fibreglass moulds: 1,000–3,000.

Cost of new mould: $300–$2,500 depending on type and cavity count.

Keep a spare mould on hand. A worn mould stops production until a replacement arrives — often 2–4 weeks from overseas.

⚠ Worn moulds don't just hurt quality — they eventually break, forcing unplanned downtime. Replace at 80% of expected lifespan.

5 Motor and pump care

Motors and pumps are the most expensive parts. Take care of them.

Vibrator motor:

  • Check mounting bolts weekly — vibration loosens them
  • Clean cooling fins monthly — dust causes overheating
  • Check electrical connections — loose terminals cause overheating
  • Listen for unusual noise — bearing failure is common
  • Replace bearings yearly on high-use machines

Hydraulic pump:

  • Check oil level daily
  • Check oil colour weekly — dark = needs changing
  • Change oil every 2,000 hours or 6 months (whichever first)
  • Change filters every 500 hours
  • Check for leaks weekly
  • Listen for cavitation noise — indicates air in the system

Spare parts to keep on hand: 1 spare vibrator motor, 1 set bearings, hydraulic filter set, belts.

6 Spare parts stock

Waiting for parts costs production. Stock the essentials.

Essential spares to keep:

  • 1 spare mould (most critical)
  • 1 spare vibrator motor
  • 1 set of bearings for vibrator
  • Hydraulic filters (4–6 units)
  • Air filters (2–4 units)
  • Drive belts (2 sets)
  • Hydraulic oil (20 L)
  • Grease cartridges (5 units)
  • Bolts and nuts (assorted sizes)
  • Electrical fuses and contactors

Cost: $500–$2,000 initial stock. Pays for itself the first time a breakdown is avoided.

Track spares usage in a log — reorder when half stock is used, not when empty.

7 When to call an engineer

Some problems are DIY; others need a professional. Know the difference.

DIY jobs:

  • Changing filters, belts, grease
  • Tightening bolts
  • Cleaning and lubricating
  • Replacing simple electrical components (fuses, switches)
  • Adjusting simple settings

Call an engineer:

  • Hydraulic system problems (pump failure, pressure loss)
  • Motor rewinding needed
  • Control panel / PLC issues
  • Major mechanical damage
  • Welding or major fabrication
  • Any problem you can't identify in 30 minutes

Finding an engineer: Local welding/engineering workshop, machine supplier's service team, or trade network. Have at least 2 engineers on speed dial.

Budget $500–$1,500 per year for external engineer services.

8 Vibration equipment

Vibration is critical for block quality. Special maintenance needed.

Vibrator maintenance:

  • Bolts: Check daily — vibration loosens everything
  • Mounting: Rubber mounts absorb vibration and wear out. Replace every 6–12 months.
  • Bearings: The most common failure point. Listen for squealing. Replace at first sign.
  • Amplitude: Check vibration amplitude monthly — declining indicates wear
  • Balancing: Vibrators are balanced at factory. If it starts wobbling, the shaft may be bent.

Signs of vibrator failure:

  • Unusual noise (squealing, knocking)
  • Reduced vibration amplitude
  • Blocks poorly compacted (honeycomb, soft)
  • Overheating of vibrator body
  • Electrical draw higher than normal

Spare vibrator motor: $200–$800. Keep one on hand — no vibration = no production.

💰 Maintenance costs 5-10% of machine cost per year. Ignoring it costs 30%+ in downtime and premature replacement. Do the math — maintenance wins every time.
