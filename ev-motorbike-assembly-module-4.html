1. Motor Mounting Bracket Installation

The motor mounting bracket is the backbone of your EV motorbike's drivetrain. It must hold the motor firmly, align with the rear wheel sprocket, and withstand constant vibration on rough African roads. Start by identifying the correct bracket for your motor type"most hub motors need a swingarm bracket, while mid-drive motors require a custom frame plate. Use 4mm to 6mm thick steel; aluminium bends too easily under load. If you don't have a pre-made bracket, cut one from a steel plate using an angle grinder. Measure the motor's bolt hole spacing (commonly 100mm or 120mm) and drill matching holes with a drill press or hand drill. Deburr all holes with a file.

Before welding or bolting the bracket to the frame, mock-fit the motor. The motor shaft must be parallel to the rear axle, and the sprockets must line up within 1mm. Use a straight edge or string line to check alignment. If the bracket is welded, tack-weld first, check alignment again, then complete the weld. For bolted brackets, use M10 or M12 grade 8.8 bolts with nyloc nuts and washers. Tighten to 40-50 Nm for M10 and 70-80 Nm for M12. Add a second bracket or gusset if the motor weighs more than 10kg to prevent flex.

In Africa, dust and water are constant enemies. After installing the bracket, paint it with anti-rust primer and a top coat. Check that the bracket does not interfere with the chain or belt. Spin the motor by hand"it should turn freely without rubbing. If you hear scraping, adjust the bracket. Finally, ride the bike slowly for 100 metres and re-check all bolts. Vibration will settle the bracket, so re-torque after the first ride. A well-installed bracket lasts for years; a poor one cracks and destroys your motor.

2. Aligning and Torquing Motor Bolts

Motor bolts are the only thing keeping your motor from spinning off the bracket. Loose bolts cause vibration, stripped threads, and eventually a broken motor casing. You need a torque wrench"borrow one if you don't own it. For M8 bolts, use 20-25 Nm; for M10, 40-50 Nm; for M12, 70-80 Nm. Always use new nyloc nuts or thread-locking compound like Loctite 243. Never reuse old nuts that have lost their nylon insert.

Alignment starts before you tighten anything. Insert all bolts finger-tight. Check that the motor shaft is perpendicular to the bracket face. Use a square or a machined block to verify. If the motor tilts, shim it with stainless steel washers"never use soft metal or rubber. For hub motors, the axle flats must sit fully in the dropout slots. If the axle is too short, do not force it; get the correct axle length. For mid-drive motors, ensure the motor sprocket and rear sprocket are in line. A misalignment of more than 1mm will wear out the chain in a few hundred kilometres.

Tighten bolts in a star pattern, like you would a cylinder head. First pass: 50% of final torque. Second pass: 100%. This prevents warping the bracket. After torquing, mark each bolt and nut with a paint pen so you can see if they move. Ride the bike for 10km, then re-check torque. Most bolts will need a small extra turn. Check again after 100km and then every 500km. In dusty conditions, bolts can loosen faster. Carry a spare set of bolts and a small torque wrench on long trips. If a bolt strips, drill it out and use a helicoil insert"do not just use a bigger bolt.

3. Controller Placement and Heat Sink Setup

The controller is the brain of your EV motorbike. It must be mounted where it gets airflow but is protected from water, mud, and direct sun. The best place is under the seat or on the frame downtube, facing forward so air hits the heat sink fins. Never mount it upside down"water will pool inside. Use rubber grommets or silicone pads between the controller and frame to reduce vibration. Vibration kills controllers faster than heat.

Most controllers have an aluminium heat sink. If yours is small, add a larger heat sink using thermal paste and M4 screws. Clean both surfaces with alcohol first. Apply a thin, even layer of thermal paste"too much is as bad as too little. For African heat, consider adding a 12V computer fan blowing across the fins. Wire the fan to the controller's 12V output or a separate switch. Keep the fan intake clean; dust clogs it quickly. If you ride in mud, mount the controller higher, above the rear wheel splash zone.

Cable entry points must face down or sideways, never up. Use dielectric grease on all connectors to keep water out. After mounting, check that the controller does not touch any moving parts. Leave at least 20mm clearance from the tyre and chain. Test the temperature after a 15-minute ride: if the controller is too hot to touch (above 60C), improve airflow or reduce current. A cool controller lasts longer and delivers more power. In the rainy season, cover the controller with a breathable cover"not plastic, which traps moisture. Check mounting bolts every month; a loose controller can crack its circuit board.

4. Wiring Motor Phase Cables

Motor phase cables carry heavy current between the controller and motor. They are usually three thick wires"yellow, green, and blue"but colour codes vary. What matters is that each controller phase matches the correct motor phase. If you mix them up, the motor will run rough, overheat, or not turn at all. Start by identifying the motor's phase wires. Use a multimeter on continuity: touch two motor phase wires together and spin the wheel"it should feel stiff. That confirms they are paired. Label them A, B, C with tape.

Strip 10mm of insulation from each cable. Use a proper crimping tool"not pliers"to attach ring terminals or bullet connectors. Solder the crimp for extra strength if you have a soldering iron. Cover the joint with heat shrink tubing, then wrap with self-amalgamating tape for waterproofing. Route the cables away from sharp edges and hot parts. Use cable ties every 150mm. Do not run phase cables alongside the throttle or hall sensor wires"they cause interference. If you must cross, cross at 90 degrees.

Connect the phase cables to the controller. The order matters: if the motor runs backwards, swap any two phase cables (e.g., yellow and green). If it stutters, you have a wrong combination"try different pairings until it runs smooth. Never disconnect phase cables while the controller is powered; it can blow the MOSFETs. After wiring, check resistance between each phase and the motor casing"it should be infinite. Any reading means a short. Test the bike on a stand first. If the motor gets hot quickly, recheck phase order. Use 10 AWG or 8 AWG silicone wire for motors above 1000W. In Africa, buy extra cable"cheap wire overheats and melts.

5. Connecting Controller to Battery and Motor

This is the high-voltage step. Before you connect anything, turn off the battery and remove the fuse. Use a multimeter to confirm zero volts at the battery terminals. Connect the main battery positive (red) to the controller's B+ terminal, and negative (black) to B-. Use ring terminals crimped and soldered. Tighten with a spanner"do not overtighten, or you will crack the controller's plastic housing. For 48V systems, use 10 AWG wire; for 72V, use 8 AWG. Add an inline fuse (40A for 1000W, 60A for 2000W) on the positive cable within 200mm of the battery.

Next, connect the motor phase cables as described in the previous lesson. Then connect the hall sensor plug (usually a 5-pin connector). If your motor has no hall sensors, ignore this. Connect the throttle: three wires"red (5V), black (ground), and green or white (signal). Never connect the throttle to battery voltage; it will burn out. Connect the brake cut-off switch if your controller supports it. This cuts power when you brake, saving energy and improving safety.

Before powering up, double-check every connection. Look for stray wire strands touching other terminals. Turn on the battery. The controller may beep or show an LED. If nothing happens, check the fuse and the ignition wire (often a thin red wire that needs to be connected to battery positive). If the motor spins when you push the throttle, you are good. If it spins immediately without throttle, your throttle is wired wrong or the controller is faulty. Test all lights and the brake cut-off. After a 5-minute test ride, check all connections for heat. Warm connectors mean loose or undersized wiring. Re-torque battery terminals after the first ride.

6. Initial Controller Parameter Setup

Your controller comes with factory settings that may not match your motor, battery, or riding conditions. You need a programming cable or a Bluetooth module and a smartphone app (like Kelly or Sabvoton). If you don't have one, borrow from a local EV shop. First, set the battery voltage. For a 48V lithium battery, set low-voltage cut-off at 42V and high-voltage cut-off at 58V. For lead-acid, set low cut-off at 44V. Never set low cut-off below 40V for 48V systems, or you will damage the battery.

Next, set the rated motor current. This is the continuous current the controller can send. For a 1000W motor at 48V, that's about 20A. Set the peak current to 1.5 times rated (30A) for 10 seconds. Too high and you overheat the motor; too low and you lose power. Set the phase current to 2.5 times rated (50A). If your controller has a "motor type" setting, choose "hall" or "sensorless" as appropriate. For hub motors, set the pole pairs (usually 23 or 46). Count the magnets in your motor and divide by 2.

Set the throttle response to "linear" or "smooth" for beginners. Set the max speed to match your gearing"start at 40km/h and increase gradually. Enable regenerative braking if your controller supports it, but set it to low (10-20%) first. Test the bike on a stand. Check that the motor does not stutter at low speed. If it does, adjust the hall sensor angle or reduce phase current. Save the settings to the controller. After a 10km ride, check motor and controller temperatures. If either is too hot, reduce current by 10%. Write down your final settings and keep them for future reference. In Africa, dusty conditions mean you should set a lower current to extend motor life.

Practical Reference: Motor Mounting, Alignment, and Controller Integration for E-Motorbike Assembly

In African e-mobility assembly, the difference between a bike that runs 80,000 km and one that fails at 8,000 km is rarely the motor itself. It is the mounting geometry, bolt torque discipline, controller thermal management, and phase wiring integrity. This practical reference consolidates the critical tolerances, real-world failure data, and assembly checks that determine whether your e-motorbike fleet becomes a revenue asset or a warranty liability. It is written for assembly technicians, workshop supervisors, and small-scale manufacturers operating in Kenya, Rwanda, Nigeria, Uganda, and similar markets where spare parts logistics are long and customer tolerance for downtime is short.

SAFETY WARNING HIGH-VOLTAGE AND THERMAL HAZARDS

E-motorbike battery packs typically operate at 48V, 60V, or 72V DC. At these voltages, direct contact can cause serious injury or death. Always disconnect the battery before working on motor, controller, or phase cables. Use insulated tools rated for 1000V. Never work alone. Controller heat sinks can exceed 80C under load allow 15 minutes of cooling before handling. Phase cables carry high current; loose connections can arc and cause burns or fire. In workshops without RCD protection, install a residual current device before any high-voltage work. Keep a Class C fire extinguisher within 3 metres of the assembly bay. These are not optional precautions; they are the minimum standard for professional e-mobility assembly in any African market.

Quick Reference Checklist: Motor Mounting and Controller Integration

  1. Verify bracket alignment: Use a straight edge across the swingarm and motor mount. Lateral offset must be 0.5 mm. If not, shim or re-machine the bracket before proceeding.
  2. Torque motor bolts in a cross pattern: M8 grade 8.8 bolts to 1822 Nm. Use a calibrated torque wrench. Apply thread locker (medium strength) and mark bolts with torque paint for visual inspection.
  3. Check phase cable routing: Cables must not chafe against frame, swingarm, or sharp edges. Use conduit or spiral wrap. Maintain a minimum 10 mm clearance from moving parts.
  4. Test phase cable continuity: Measure resistance between controller terminals and motor terminals. Each phase must be < 0.5 . Check for shorts between phases and to ground.
  5. Mount controller with airflow: Heat sink fins must be vertical and unobstructed. Minimum 20 mm clearance on all sides. In dusty environments, add a removable dust filter.
  6. Apply thermal interface material: Between controller base and heat sink, use a thin, even layer of thermal paste. Do not use excessive amounts it insulates rather than conducts.
  7. Verify battery voltage match: Controller rated voltage must match battery nominal voltage within 2 V. Check BMS communication and pre-charge circuit before first power-up.
  8. Run a 10-minute load test: After assembly, run the motor under load for 10 minutes. Check controller temperature (must stay below 75C), listen for unusual noise, and re-check bolt torque after cooling.
