1. Climate and Soil Requirements for Cassava

Cassava grows best in warm, humid climates. It needs at least 8 months of warm weather with temperatures between 25C and 29C. It cannot tolerate frost. Rainfall should be between 1000 mm and 1500 mm per year, but cassava can survive with as little as 500 mm because it is drought-tolerant. However, for good yields, consistent moisture is important. In Africa, cassava is grown from sea level up to 2000 meters, but lower altitudes with plenty of sun are best.

Soil is critical. Cassava prefers well-drained sandy loam or loamy soils. It does not like waterlogged areas; roots will rot. The ideal soil pH is between 5.5 and 6.5, but cassava can grow in soils with pH as low as 4.5. It tolerates poor soils better than many crops, but for high yields, you need fertile soil. Avoid heavy clay soils because they hold water and make it hard for roots to expand.

Before planting, check your soil. Dig a small pit 30 cm deep. If the soil is sticky and clumps, it has too much clay. If it is gritty and falls apart, it is sandy. You want something in between. Also, look for signs of previous crops. If maize or beans grew well there, cassava will too. Avoid land that floods during heavy rains. Choose a site with a gentle slope so water drains away.

In Africa, cassava is often grown on smallholder farms without irrigation. So choose a site that gets reliable rainfall. If you are in a drier region, plant at the beginning of the rainy season. Also, cassava needs full sun. Do not plant under trees or in shaded areas. Clear the land of tall weeds and bushes that compete for light and nutrients.

Finally, consider access. You need to be able to transport harvest to market or processing site. A field near a road or path saves time and labor. Also, keep away from areas with known disease problems, like cassava mosaic virus. Rotate with other crops if possible. By selecting the right site, you set the foundation for a healthy cassava crop.

2. Land Clearing and Stumping Techniques

Land clearing is the first step to prepare your field. You need to remove bushes, small trees, and weeds. Use a machete or slasher to cut down vegetation. For thicker bushes, use an axe or a brush cutter. If you have a tractor, a rotary slasher can clear large areas quickly. But most smallholder farmers in Africa do this manually. Clear the land at least one month before planting so the cut vegetation can dry and decompose.

After clearing, you must stump the land. Stumping means removing tree stumps and large roots. This is hard work but necessary because stumps interfere with tillage and can harbor pests. Use a hoe, axe, or mattock to dig around the stump. Cut the roots and pull the stump out. For very large stumps, you may need a winch or a tractor. In some areas, farmers burn stumps, but burning destroys soil organic matter. Instead, pile stumps and burn them away from the field, or use them for firewood.

Stumping also includes removing all roots larger than your thumb. If you leave them, they will rot and create air pockets that dry out the soil. Also, they make it hard to make ridges. After stumping, collect all debris and burn or compost it. Do not leave piles of trash in the field because they attract termites and rodents.

If you are clearing new land from forest, you must be careful. Slash-and-burn is common, but it can damage the soil. If you must burn, do it when the vegetation is dry, and keep the fire controlled. After burning, spread the ash evenly. Ash adds potassium and raises pH. But do not burn too hot; it kills beneficial soil organisms. In many parts of Africa, farmers practice slash-and-mulch, where they cut vegetation and leave it on the soil as mulch. This conserves moisture and adds organic matter.

Finally, after clearing and stumping, level the land. Fill any holes and remove rocks. A level field makes planting and harvesting easier. If the land is on a slope, do not clear all vegetation; leave some strips to prevent erosion. Use contour lines to guide your clearing. By doing a thorough job, you reduce weeds and make subsequent operations smoother.

3. Tillage Methods: Plowing, Harrowing, and Ridging

Tillage is the preparation of soil for planting. It loosens the soil, controls weeds, and creates a good seedbed. For cassava, tillage is important because the roots need loose soil to grow big. There are three main steps: plowing, harrowing, and ridging. You can do these with hand tools or with a tractor. Choose based on your land size and budget.

Plowing is the first step. It breaks up the soil to a depth of 20-30 cm. If you use a tractor, a disc plow is common. For manual plowing, use a hoe or a hand plow. Plow when the soil is moist but not too wet. If you squeeze a handful of soil and it forms a ball that doesn't crumble, it's too wet. Plowing wet soil creates clods that are hard to break. Plow along the contour if your field is on a slope. This reduces erosion. After plowing, let the soil rest for a week or two to allow weeds to die and soil to settle.

Harrowing comes next. It breaks up clods and levels the soil. A disc harrow or a spike-tooth harrow is used with a tractor. Manually, you can use a rake or a hoe to break clods. Harrowing makes the soil fine and smooth. It also incorporates any organic matter you added. Do harrowing when the soil is dry enough to crumble. If you are planting on flat land, harrowing is enough. But for cassava, ridging is often better.

Ridging means making raised beds or ridges. Ridges improve drainage and make harvesting easier. They also give the cassava roots more room to expand. Ridges are usually 30-40 cm high and 60-100 cm apart. You can make ridges with a hoe, or use a tractor with a ridger. In Africa, many farmers make ridges by hand. It is labor-intensive but effective. Make ridges along the contour to slow water runoff. If you are in a flat area, you can make ridges in straight lines.

After ridging, you are ready to plant. But remember, excessive tillage can damage soil structure. If your soil is sandy, you may not need to plow deeply. If it is clay, you need more tillage. Also, consider minimum tillage: just make planting holes or small ridges. This saves labor and preserves soil organic matter. For cassava, deep tillage is beneficial because roots grow deep. But do not over-till. Test your soil and adjust. With proper tillage, your cassava will have a good start.

4. Soil Testing and Fertility Assessment

Soil testing tells you what nutrients your soil has and what it lacks. Cassava needs nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K). It also needs calcium and magnesium. A soil test is the best way to know how much fertilizer to apply. You can send soil samples to a laboratory. In many African countries, agricultural extension offices offer soil testing services for a small fee. If you cannot afford a lab test, you can do simple field tests.

To take a soil sample, use a clean hoe or auger. Collect soil from 10-15 spots in your field. Dig 15-20 cm deep. Mix all samples in a clean bucket. Take about 500 grams of the mixed soil and put it in a labeled bag. Label with your name, field location, and date. Send it to the lab. The lab will report pH, organic matter, and nutrient levels. It will also give fertilizer recommendations.

If you cannot test, observe your soil and plants. If cassava leaves are yellow, it may need nitrogen. If roots are small and poor, it may need potassium. If plants are stunted, it may need phosphorus. Also, look at weeds. If you see many weeds like striga, your soil may be poor. You can also do a simple pH test with a kit. These kits are available at agro-dealers. They cost a few dollars. Follow the instructions carefully.

Fertility assessment also includes checking organic matter. Soil that is dark and crumbly has good organic matter. It holds water and nutrients. If your soil is pale and hard, it needs organic matter. Add compost or manure. Cassava responds well to organic matter. You can also grow cover crops like mucuna or cowpea before cassava. These fix nitrogen and add biomass.

Based on test results, you can apply fertilizer. Common fertilizers for cassava are NPK 15-15-15 or 12-12-17. Apply at planting or shortly after. In Africa, many farmers cannot afford fertilizer, so they use manure or compost. A good practice is to apply 5-10 tons of manure per hectare. If you use mineral fertilizer, apply 200-400 kg per hectare. Split the application: half at planting, half two months later. Always follow local recommendations. Soil testing saves money and increases yields.

5. Erosion Control and Conservation Practices

Erosion is a major problem in cassava fields, especially on slopes. When it rains, water runs off and carries away topsoil. This reduces fertility and yields. You need to control erosion from the start. The best way is to keep the soil covered and slow down water flow. There are several practical methods you can use.

First, plant on contours. Contour lines follow the shape of the land. If you plow and plant along these lines, water slows down and soaks in. You can mark contours with a simple A-frame level. Make an A-frame with two sticks and a crossbar. Hang a stone from the top. When the stone hangs straight, the two legs are level. Use this to mark contour lines across your field. Then plow and ridge along these lines. This is called contour plowing.

Second, use cover crops or mulch. After clearing, plant cover crops like mucuna, cowpea, or groundnut. These cover the soil and protect it from raindrops. They also add organic matter. If you cannot plant cover crops, leave crop residues on the surface as mulch. Mulch reduces erosion and conserves moisture. In dry areas, mulch is very important. Do not burn residues; spread them.

Third, build physical barriers. You can make contour ridges or terraces. On gentle slopes (2-5%), contour ridges are enough. On steeper slopes (5-10%), you need terraces. Terraces are steps cut into the slope. They are labor-intensive but effective. Another option is to plant grass strips. Plant vetiver grass or napier grass in strips across the slope. These strips slow water and trap soil. They also provide fodder for livestock.

Fourth, maintain soil cover. Avoid leaving soil bare. After harvesting cassava, plant a cover crop immediately. Also, practice crop rotation. Rotate cassava with cereals or legumes. This improves soil structure and reduces pests. Keep some trees on the farm. Trees like Faidherbia albida drop leaves that add organic matter. They also shade the soil.

Finally, manage water flow. Dig small ditches or cut-off drains at the top of your field to divert runoff. Make sure they are gentle so water does not erode the ditch itself. If you have a gully, fill it with stones or plant trees. In Africa, many farmers use stone bunds. These are lines of stones placed along contours. They slow water and trap soil. By combining these practices, you can reduce erosion and maintain soil fertility for years.

6. Layout and Plot Design for Cassava Fields

A good layout makes your work easier and increases yields. Before planting, plan how you will arrange your cassava field. Consider access, spacing, and intercropping. Cassava is often intercropped with maize, beans, or vegetables. If you intercrop, you need wider spacing. For pure cassava, the recommended spacing is 1 m by 1 m. This gives about 10,000 plants per hectare. If you intercrop, use 1 m by 1.5 m or 1.5 m by 1.5 m.

First, divide your field into plots. Each plot should be rectangular and easy to manage. Leave paths between plots for walking and transporting harvest. Paths should be 1-2 m wide. If you use a tractor, leave wider paths. Also, leave a border around the field. Borders can be planted with grass or legumes to reduce weed invasion. Mark the corners of each plot with stakes. Use a rope to keep lines straight.

Second, decide on ridge orientation. Ridges should run along the contour if the land slopes. If the land is flat, run ridges north-south. This allows even sunlight on both sides of the ridge. For cassava, ridges are usually 1 m apart. Plant cuttings on top of the ridge. If you plant on flat land, make planting holes. But ridges are better for drainage and root growth.

Third, plan for intercropping. If you intercrop with maize, plant maize first. Maize grows faster and provides shade for young cassava. But do not plant maize too close to cassava. Keep at least 50 cm between maize and cassava rows. If you intercrop with legumes like cowpea, plant them between cassava rows. Legumes fix nitrogen and suppress weeds. They also provide extra income. But avoid intercropping with crops that compete heavily for nutrients, like sweet potato.

Fourth, consider your harvesting and processing needs. If you plan to process cassava into garri or flour, you need a processing area. Locate it near the field to reduce transport. Also, leave space for drying. If you sell fresh roots, you need access for trucks. Plan your layout so that you can easily move harvested roots out. Also, think about future expansion. Leave some land fallow or for rotation.

Finally, record your layout. Draw a simple map of your field. Mark plots, paths, and intercropped areas. This helps you remember what you planted where. It also helps with crop rotation. By designing your field well, you save time, reduce labor, and improve cassava yields.

<h2>Practical Reference: Site Selection and Land Preparation for Cassava</h2>

<p>Cassava (<em>Manihot esculenta</em> Crantz) is one of Africa's most strategic crops: it tolerates poor soils, survives erratic rainfall, and produces a starchy root with strong demand in food, feed, starch, and industrial markets. But tolerance is not the same as profitability. A farmer or agribusiness that plants cassava on waterlogged, compacted, or depleted land will harvest 812 t/ha where 2540 t/ha was achievable a loss that compounds across every hectare and every season. This lesson is a business-first reference for selecting sites and preparing land so that your cassava enterprise starts with a defensible yield baseline.</p>

<h3>Climate and Soil Requirements</h3>

<p>Cassava is grown across sub-Saharan Africa from sea level to about 2,000 m, but commercial productivity is concentrated where the following conditions hold:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Rainfall:</strong> 6001,500 mm per annum, with at least 500 mm during the growing cycle. Cassava is drought-tolerant because of its deep, fibrous root system, but prolonged moisture stress in the first 3 months after planting reduces stand establishment and final root bulking.</li>
<li><strong>Temperature:</strong> Optimal growth at 2529C. Growth slows below 16C; frost is lethal.</li>
<li><strong>Photoperiod:</strong> Cassava is a short-day plant; long days favour leaf and stem growth over root bulking. Most African production zones (latitude 015N/S) are well suited.</li>
<li><strong>Soils:</strong> Well-drained sandy loams to loams, pH 5.56.5 (tolerates 4.57.5), depth >60 cm, moderate organic matter. Cassava tolerates low fertility but yields respond strongly to improved nutrition.</li>
<li><strong>Avoid:</strong> Waterlogged valley bottoms, heavy clays that waterlog in the rainy season, shallow soils over rock, and slopes >15% without conservation structures.</li>
</ul>

<p>From a business perspective, site selection is a logistics decision as much as an agronomic one. Cassava roots are bulky (250400 kg/m) and perishable within 2472 hours of harvest. A field more than 3050 km from a processing facility, or more than 2 hours from a starch or gari market, will lose margin to transport and spoilage. Select sites with all-weather feeder road access, or plan to process on-site.</p>

<h3>Land Clearing and Stumping</h3>

<p>Clearing method determines your first-year cost, your soil carbon, and your weed pressure. Options:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Manual/slash-and-carry (low capital, high labour):</strong> Cut vegetation, remove stumps by hand or with a mattock. Suited to &lt;2 ha and where machinery access is poor. Preserves topsoil but slow (1525 person-days/ha).</li>
<li><strong>Semi-mechanised:</strong> Bulldozer or excavator for stumping, followed by manual clearing of debris. Fast but expensive; bull-dozing removes topsoil and can create a hardpan. Restrict to 12 passes and windrow debris rather than burning.</li>
<li><strong>Chemical + slashing (conservation-friendly):</strong> Slash tall vegetation, apply approved herbicide to stumps and regrowth, then plant. Lower soil disturbance and lower erosion risk on slopes. Requires careful, licensed application.</li>
</ul>

<p>Always stump fully. Cassava is harvested 918 months after planting; stumps and roots left in the field will resprout, compete, and damage harvesting equipment. Where land has been fallow for &gt;5 years, expect high weed seed banks budget for 23 weedings in the first 4 months.</p>

<h3>Tillage Methods: Plowing, Harrowing, and Ridging</h3>

<p>Tillage in cassava is about root-zone preparation, not seedbed fineness. Cassava roots need loose, deep, well-aerated soil to bulk. Recommended sequence:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Plowing (primary tillage):</strong> 2030 cm depth using disc or mouldboard plough, or hand hoe on small plots. Break the plough pan if present. On slopes &gt;8%, plough across the contour.</li>
<li><strong>Harrowing (secondary tillage):</strong> 12 passes to break clods and incorporate residues. Do not over-till pulverised soil crusts and erodes.</li>
<li><strong>Ridging:</strong> Ridges or mounds 2540 cm high, spaced 80100 cm apart for sole cassava, or 100120 cm for intercropping. Ridging improves drainage, root penetration, and harvesting ease. On flat, well-drained land, flat planting is acceptable and cheaper.</li>
</ol>

<p>Minimum tillage (spot planting on slashed plots) reduces cost and erosion but may lower yields on compacted soils. Choose based on soil condition, slope, and machinery availability not habit.</p>

<h3>Soil Testing and Fertility Assessment</h3>

<p>Cassava removes roughly 35 kg N, 12 kg PO, and 47 kg KO per tonne of fresh roots. A 25 t/ha crop therefore exports substantial potassium. Soil testing converts guesswork into a fertiliser budget.</p>

<p>Sample at 020 cm depth, 1520 sub-samples per 25 ha in a zig-zag pattern, mixed and quartered to ~500 g. Send to a national soil laboratory or a certified private lab. Test for pH, organic carbon, total N, available P (Bray-1 or Olsen), exchangeable K, Ca, Mg, and texture.</p>

<h3>Erosion Control and Conservation Practices</h3>

<p>Cassava's slow early canopy (first 3 months) leaves soil exposed. On slopes &gt;5%, combine:</p>

<ul>
<li>Contour plowing and planting</li>
<li>Grass strips (e.g. vetiver, <em>Paspalum notatum</em>) every 1520 m</li>
<li>Mulching with crop residues</li>
<li>Cover crops or intercrops (cowpea, groundnut, maize) during establishment</li>
<li>Drainage channels on flat, high-rainfall sites to prevent waterlogging</li>
</ul>

<h3>Layout and Plot Design</h3>

<p>Design fields for machinery, harvest logistics, and future rotation. Standard layout for commercial cassava:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Plot size:</strong> 0.52 ha blocks, separated by 23 m access paths for harvest trucks and sprayers.</li>
<li><strong>Plant spacing:</strong> 1 m 0.8 m (12,500 plants/ha) for root yield; 1 m 1 m (10,000 plants/ha) for starch varieties; 1 m 0.5 m (20,000 plants/ha) for fodder or high-density seed systems.</li>
<li><strong>Orientation:</strong> Rows across the slope (contour) to slow runoff; on flat land, orient rows to prevailing wind for ventilation.</li>
<li><strong>Buffer zones:</strong> 510 m from waterways and 3 m from boundaries to reduce herbicide drift and erosion.</li>
<li><strong>Rotation plan:</strong> Cassavalegumecereal or cassavafallow to break pest cycles and restore K.</li>
</ul>

<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Herbicides and stump-killing chemicals are toxic. Always wear gloves, boots, long sleeves, eye protection, and a respirator when mixing or applying. <h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Confirm rainfall 600 mm/yr and no prolonged dry spell &gt;3 months in the first 6 months after planting.</li>
<li>Verify slope 15%; if steeper, install contour strips and drainage before planting.</li>
<li>Dig a 60 cm test pit: confirm soil depth, texture, and absence of a hardpan or water table.</li>
<li>Take a composite soil sample (020 cm) and test pH, P, K, and organic carbon before fertiliser purchase.</li>
<li>Clear and stump fully; windrow debris rather than burning to protect soil carbon.</li>
<li>Plow across the contour, harrow lightly, and ridge only where drainage or root bulking requires it.</li>
<li>Map access paths, buffer zones, and processing/market distance before finalising plot layout.</li>
<li>Budget land preparation at 1525 person-days/ha (manual) or USD 250600/ha (mechanised) before planting.</li>
</ol>
