1. Brooder Setup and Temperature Requirements

Ostrich chicks are fragile in their first weeks. A good brooder mimics the warmth and protection of a mother ostrich. Start with a clean, dry, draft-free room or pen. Cover the floor with 5â10 cm of clean sand or wood shavings â never use slippery surfaces that can cause leg splay. Provide a heat source: a 250-watt infrared heat lamp or a brooder stove. For the first week, keep the temperature at 32â35Â°C at chick level. Reduce by 2â3Â°C each week until you reach 21â24Â°C by week 4. In hot African climates, you may need to turn off heat during the day and only use it at night. Always monitor chick behaviour: if they huddle under the lamp, they are cold; if they spread to the corners, they are too hot. Aim for even distribution. Use a thermometer at chick height, not on the wall. Provide a 'creep' area with a lower temperature so chicks can self-regulate. Good ventilation is critical â ammonia from droppings can damage their lungs. But avoid direct draughts. A simple setup: a 3m x 3m pen for 20 chicks, with a central heat source and a surround of cardboard or wire mesh to prevent chicks from wandering too far. Clean and disinfect the brooder before chicks arrive. Use a safe disinfectant like Virkon or bleach solution (1:10), then rinse and dry. Place feed and water on flat trays or shallow lids â chicks are curious and will peck at anything. Never use newspaper as bedding; it causes leg injuries. For the first 3 days, provide continuous light so chicks can find feed and water. After that, reduce to 12 hours of light. Always have a backup heat source (gas or paraffin lamp) in case of power cuts. In rural Africa, many farmers use a charcoal stove in a metal drum with a lid â but this risks carbon monoxide poisoning. If you use charcoal, pipe fumes outside. Better to use solar-powered heat lamps or a well-insulated brooder box. Check chicks every 2 hours for the first week. A healthy chick is alert, active, and has bright eyes. A cold chick will chirp loudly and shiver. A hot chick will pant and lie flat. Adjust immediately. Keep records of temperature and chick behaviour daily. This simple habit saves lives.

2. Feeding and Watering Chicks

Ostrich chicks grow fast â up to 30 cm per month. They need high-quality feed from day one. For the first 3 days, offer a starter crumble with 20â22% protein. You can buy commercial ostrich starter or mix your own: 50% maize meal, 20% soybean meal, 15% fish meal, 10% alfalfa meal, 5% minerals and vitamins. Always add a vitamin-mineral premix. A simple rule: feed 50â70 grams per chick per day in week 1, increasing to 150 grams by week 4. Split into 4â5 small meals daily â chicks have small crops and cannot eat much at once. Use shallow, clean feed trays (not deep bowls) so chicks can see the feed. Scatter some feed on a clean board to encourage pecking. Chicks learn by copying â put a few older chicks or a hen with them if possible. Fresh, clean water must be available at all times. Use shallow waterers with marbles or stones in the base to prevent drowning â chicks can drown in just 2 cm of water. Change water 3â4 times daily. Add electrolytes and vitamins to water for the first 3 days to reduce stress. Never feed mouldy or stale feed â it causes fatal digestive problems. Ostrich chicks also need grit to digest feed. From week 2, offer fine granite grit (2â3 mm) in a separate tray. They will eat what they need. Green feed is important: from week 1, offer chopped lucerne, spinach, or cabbage. This provides fibre and vitamins. But never feed them too much green â it can cause diarrhoea. A good ratio: 80% starter crumble, 20% greens by weight. In Africa, many farmers use local ingredients like moringa leaves, amaranth, and sweet potato vines â these are excellent. But avoid toxic plants like nightshade, castor oil, and bracken fern. Feed at the same times daily to establish a routine. Watch for chicks that are not eating â they may be sick. Isolate them and offer a wet mash (feed mixed with water) to stimulate appetite. Clean feed trays daily to prevent mould and bacteria. Store feed in a cool, dry place, off the floor, in sealed containers. Rats and mice carry disease â keep them out. A 20-chick unit will consume about 1 kg of feed in week 1, 5 kg in week 2, and 15 kg by week 4. Plan your purchases accordingly. Water consumption is roughly 2â3 times feed intake. On hot days, chicks drink more â never let them run dry.

3. Health Monitoring and Common Chick Ailments

Ostrich chicks are susceptible to several diseases and management problems. The most common is 'starve-out' â chicks that fail to start eating. This happens when they are cold, stressed, or cannot find feed. Prevent it by ensuring correct brooder temperature, providing bright light for 3 days, and scattering feed on flat trays. Another major issue is 'yolk sac infection' â the unabsorbed yolk becomes infected. Signs: swollen abdomen, lethargy, and death within 5 days. This is caused by dirty brooder conditions or chilling. Always disinfect the brooder and keep bedding dry. 'Leg problems' are common: splayed legs, twisted legs, and curled toes. Causes include slippery floors, vitamin deficiencies (especially riboflavin and manganese), and rapid growth. Use sand or shavings, not newspaper. Add a vitamin-mineral premix. If a chick has splayed legs, you can hobble it with a soft bandage for 3â5 days. 'Respiratory infections' cause sneezing, nasal discharge, and rattling breath. They are usually caused by ammonia from wet litter or draughts. Improve ventilation and clean litter daily. 'Coccidiosis' is a deadly intestinal parasite. Signs: bloody droppings, ruffled feathers, and weight loss. Treat with amprolium or sulphonamides in water. Prevent by keeping feed and water off the floor and cleaning regularly. 'Worms' â roundworms and tapeworms â can stunt growth. Deworm at 6 weeks with fenbendazole or levamisole. 'Newcastle disease' and 'avian influenza' are viral and often fatal. Vaccinate chicks according to your local veterinary schedule â typically at 7 days for Newcastle. In Africa, many countries have mandatory vaccination. Always consult a vet. 'Pasting' â droppings stuck to the vent â is common in young chicks. Gently clean with warm water and a soft cloth. This prevents blockage. Check chicks at least 3 times daily. Look for: dull eyes, drooping wings, sitting apart, not eating, abnormal droppings. Isolate sick chicks immediately in a warm, quiet pen. Keep a health record: date, symptoms, treatment, outcome. This helps you spot patterns. Use a simple notebook or a phone app. Never use antibiotics without a vet's advice â misuse creates resistance. For wounds, clean with iodine and apply an antibiotic ointment. Keep a first-aid kit: iodine, vet wrap, scissors, thermometer, electrolytes, and a syringe. In hot climates, heat stress can kill chicks â provide shade and cool water. In cold, damp weather, pneumonia is common â keep bedding dry and increase heat. A healthy chick is active, has bright eyes, clean vent, and eats well. Trust your observation â you know your birds best.

4. Exercise and Socialization

Ostrich chicks need exercise to develop strong legs and bones. From day 3, allow them to walk short distances inside the brooder. By week 2, give them access to a small outdoor run on warm, sunny days â but only if the temperature is above 20Â°C and there is no wind. Start with 15 minutes, then increase gradually to 2 hours by week 4. The run should be secure with wire mesh and a solid floor or grass. Never let chicks out in wet or cold weather â they chill easily. Exercise prevents leg weakness and improves digestion. It also reduces stress and pecking. Socialization is equally important. Ostrich chicks are flock birds â they learn from each other. Keep chicks in groups of 10â20. Never isolate a single chick unless it is sick. If you have to isolate, place it in a pen next to the others so it can see and hear them. Mix chicks of similar size â bigger chicks will bully smaller ones. If bullying occurs, provide more space, more feeders, and hiding spots. You can also put a few older, calm chicks with the younger ones to teach them to eat and drink. In Africa, many farmers use a 'foster mother' â a gentle hen or even a goat â but this is risky. Better to rely on the flock. Human interaction also matters: spend 10â15 minutes daily walking among the chicks. Talk softly. This reduces fear and makes handling easier later. Handle chicks gently â support the body and legs. Never lift by the neck or wings. From week 3, introduce them to new objects: a plastic ball, a piece of cloth, or a small box. This builds curiosity and reduces neophobia (fear of new things). Rotate toys weekly. Provide perches â low branches 10â15 cm off the ground â for roosting. This strengthens legs and provides entertainment. At night, chicks should huddle together for warmth. If they are spread out, it is too hot; if they pile up, it is too cold. Adjust heat accordingly. Socialization also means exposure to different sounds: radio, vehicles, people. Play a radio softly during the day. This prevents panic later. In free-range systems, chicks learn from the mother. If you have a broody hen, let her raise the chicks â she will teach them to forage and avoid danger. But ostrich chicks are not chicken chicks â they have different needs. Still, a good hen can be a great teacher. Always supervise. By week 6, chicks should be well-socialized, active, and ready for larger groups. Introduce them to older chicks gradually â separate by a wire fence for 3 days, then mix. Watch for fighting. Provide plenty of space and feed. A well-exercised, socialized chick grows into a calm, productive adult.

5. Growth Monitoring and Weight Records

You cannot manage what you do not measure. Ostrich chicks grow rapidly â a healthy chick weighs about 0.8â1 kg at day 1, 2 kg at week 4, 5 kg at week 8, and 15 kg at week 16. Weigh your chicks weekly using a digital scale (accurate to 0.1 kg for young chicks, 0.5 kg for older). Use a small basket or a cloth sling to hold the chick. Weigh at the same time each week â preferably early morning before feeding. Record the weight in a notebook or a simple spreadsheet. Also record feed intake and any health issues. A simple growth chart helps you spot problems early. If a chick is not gaining weight, it may be sick, stressed, or not eating enough. Compare each chick to the group average. If one is 20% below average, isolate and investigate. Common causes: worms, coccidiosis, or bullying. Weighing also helps you adjust feed rations. A chick gaining 150â200 grams per week is on track. If gaining less, increase protein or feed quantity. If gaining too fast, reduce energy feed to prevent leg problems. Keep a permanent record for each chick â use ear tags or leg bands for identification. In Africa, many farmers use coloured zip ties on the leg (loose enough to allow growth). Replace every 2 weeks. Record: date, weight, feed consumed, health notes. At the end of each month, calculate average daily gain (ADG). For ostrich chicks, ADG should be 20â30 grams per day in weeks 1â4, then 50â80 grams per day in weeks 5â12. If ADG drops, check for disease, parasites, or feed quality. Also measure height â from ground to top of head when standing. Height correlates with leg strength. A chick that is tall but thin may need more protein. A short, fat chick may need more exercise. Take photos monthly to see progress. Share records with your vet or extension officer â they can help interpret. Use a simple phone app like 'Livestock Manager' or 'Farm Records' to store data. If you have no phone, a notebook works. At the end of the grow-out period (usually 16â20 weeks), you will have a clear picture of which chicks are ready for the next stage. Cull or sell the slow growers â they cost more to raise. Keep the best for breeding. Remember: weighing is stressful for chicks. Handle gently, and always return them to a warm, quiet pen. Do not weigh sick chicks. Weigh in the cool of the morning. Clean the scale between groups to prevent disease spread. Good records lead to better decisions and higher profits.

6. Transition to Outdoor Pens

By week 8â12, ostrich chicks are ready to move from the brooder to outdoor pens. But this transition must be gradual. Sudden changes cause stress, disease, and even death. Start by allowing supervised outdoor access for 1â2 hours daily in week 6, increasing to full days by week 8. Only move them permanently when they are fully feathered and the weather is mild â not during cold rains or extreme heat. The outdoor pen must be secure. Use 1.5â2 m high wire mesh fencing, buried 30 cm into the ground to prevent digging predators. Ostrich chicks are curious and can squeeze through small gaps. Check for holes daily. Provide a shelter â a simple three-sided shed with a roof â facing away from prevailing winds. The floor should be dry, with sand or gravel for drainage. Inside, provide perches and a dust bath area. Chicks need shade â plant trees or use shade cloth. In hot African climates, ensure plenty of water points. The pen should have a mix of grass and bare ground. Rotate pens if possible to prevent parasite build-up. Before moving, vaccinate and deworm according to schedule. Treat for external parasites like mites and lice with a dusting powder. Introduce chicks to the new pen in the morning on a sunny day. Put their familiar feeders and waterers in the shelter. Keep them locked inside the shelter for the first 2 days, then open the door to the run. Stay with them for the first hour to watch for bullying or escape attempts. Provide extra feed and water at multiple points to reduce competition. Expect some fighting as they establish pecking order â this is normal. But if blood is drawn, isolate the aggressor. At night, lock them in the shelter to protect from predators. After 2 weeks, they can be allowed to roost outside if the weather is warm. Continue weighing weekly. A smooth transition means chicks continue to gain weight. If they lose weight or get sick, move them back to the brooder for a few days. Common problems: coccidiosis from contaminated soil, worms, and respiratory infections from dust. Monitor droppings daily. Keep a footbath with disinfectant at the pen entrance. Limit visitors. In free-range systems, chicks may be moved to a larger field at 12 weeks. But always provide shelter and supplementary feed. Ostrich chicks are not fully hardy until 6 months. So even in outdoor pens, provide heat lamps during cold nights for the first month. A good transition sets the stage for healthy grow-out and eventual breeding or meat production. Take your time â rushing causes losses.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Chick Rearing and Grow-Out for Ostrich Farming

Ostrich farming in Africa is a high-value agribusiness. Chicks are the most fragile and expensive stage of production; mortality in the first 8 weeks can exceed 30% without disciplined brooding, feeding, and health routines. This Practical Reference condenses the operational standards used by commercial ostrich producers in South Africa, Namibia, Botswana, Kenya, and Nigeria into checklists, tables, and a visual brooder diagram. Use it as a daily field guide for your farm team.

1. Brooder Setup and Temperature Requirements

Chicks cannot regulate body temperature for the first 10â14 days. A brooder must provide a warm zone (32â35Â°C in week 1) and a cooler zone (24â27Â°C) so chicks self-regulate. Reduce temperature by roughly 2â3Â°C per week until 21â24Â°C at week 5. In hot African climates (e.g., northern Namibia, Botswana, Kenya's Rift Valley), reduce lamp hours during the day and monitor chick behaviour rather than relying only on thermometers. Chicks that huddle tightly under the lamp are cold; those spread to the edges and pant are too hot.

Use dry, dust-free litter (wood shavings or chopped straw), 5â7 cm deep. Avoid smooth concrete floors, which cause splayed legs. Provide 0.5 mÂ² per chick in the brooder, expanding to 1.5 mÂ² by week 4. Solid, draught-proof guards 40â50 cm high prevent chilling and keep chicks away from power cables.

2. Feeding and Watering Chicks

Offer clean, lukewarm water within 2 hours of arrival. Add electrolytes and glucose for the first 24â48 hours to reduce transport stress. Use shallow drinkers with marbles or pebbles to prevent drowning. Feed a high-quality ostrich chick starter crumble (18â20% protein, 2,600â2,800 kcal/kg ME) from day 1. Provide insoluble granite grit from week 2 to aid digestion. Feed small amounts 4â5 times daily for the first 2 weeks, then move to 3 feeds daily. Never feed layer mash or poultry broiler feed â the mineral and protein profiles cause leg deformities and poor growth.

Average feed intake: 30â50 g/chick/day in week 1, rising to 250â350 g/day by week 8. Always provide fresh water; an ostrich chick can drink 2â3 times more water than feed by weight in hot weather.

3. Health Monitoring and Common Chick Ailments

Inspect every chick daily for drooping wings, closed eyes, pasted vents, lameness, or abnormal droppings. Common ailments include:

  • Yolk sac infection (omphalitis): navel redness or swelling; usually from dirty hatchery conditions. Treat with vet-prescribed antibiotics and isolate.
  • Coccidiosis: bloody or watery droppings, huddling. Treat with amprolium or toltrazuril under veterinary direction.
  • Leg deformities (splayed leg, twisted tibia): caused by slippery floors, vitamin/mineral deficiency, or excess protein. Correct litter and supplement vitamin D3, calcium, and phosphorus.
  • Impacted gizzard: from eating litter, sand, or long grass. Provide grit and monitor intake.
  • Newcastle disease and avian influenza: notifiable in most African countries. Vaccinate according to national schedules and report suspected outbreaks immediately.

Keep a daily log of mortality, feed intake, water intake, and temperature. Any sudden spike in mortality requires immediate veterinary investigation.

Heat lamps are a leading cause of barn fires in African ostrich operations. Secure all lamps with chains, not clamps; keep them at least 45 cm from litter and never directly above dry straw. Install smoke detectors and a fire extinguisher in every brooder house. On biosecurity: restrict visitor access, disinfect boots and hands at entry, and never mix chicks from different hatches or suppliers without a 14-day quarantine. Newcastle disease and avian influenza can wipe out an entire flock and trigger national trade bans â report suspicious deaths to your veterinary authority within 24 hours.

4. Exercise and Socialization

From day 3, allow chicks short supervised exercise periods on dry, clean ground or a covered veranda. Exercise strengthens legs and prevents splayed leg. By week 3, provide access to a small outdoor run with shade and windbreaks. Ostrich chicks are highly social; grouping 15â25 chicks of similar age reduces stress. Avoid mixing age groups â older chicks bully and injure younger ones.
