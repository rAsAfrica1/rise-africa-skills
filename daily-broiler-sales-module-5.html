1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Broiler chickens are sensitive to their environment. In Africa, where temperatures can soar above 35C and sudden rains can flood open pens, proper housing is not optionalit is the difference between profit and loss. Good housing protects birds from predators, theft, and disease, and it gives you control over temperature, ventilation, and hygiene. Without it, your batch will grow slowly, get sick, and die. With it, you can raise healthy birds that reach 2.5 kg in 6 weeks.

Why does housing matter for daily sales? Because you need a consistent supply of uniform birds. If some birds are stunted from cold or heat stress, you cannot sell them at the same price. A well-designed house keeps all birds comfortable, so they grow evenly. It also makes your work easier: you can feed and water efficiently, spot sick birds quickly, and clean without hassle.

Consider the climate. In hot regions like northern Nigeria or Senegal, you need open-sided houses with wire mesh for maximum airflow. In cooler highlands like Kenya or Ethiopia, you need solid walls with curtains to trap heat. Rainy areas require raised floors and good drainage. Your house must suit your local weather.

Start small. A 3m x 4m house can hold 100 broilers (10 birds per square meter). Use local materials: wooden poles, bamboo, mud bricks, or burnt bricks. Roof with iron sheets or thatch. The key is to keep it dry, ventilated, and secure. Remember, housing is an investment. A simple but well-built house costs less than losing a batch to disease or theft. In the next lessons, you will learn exactly how to build one.

2. Tools and Materials

To build a broiler house for 100 birds, you need basic tools and materials. Most can be sourced locally. For tools: a hoe, machete (panga), hammer, saw, measuring tape, spade, wheelbarrow, and a shovel. If you can, borrow or rent a power drill for screws. For materials: wooden poles (10-15 cm diameter) for posts, bamboo or timber for rafters, iron sheets or thatch for roofing, wire mesh (1 cm holes) for sides, cement and sand for floor, and nails or screws. You will also need plastic sheeting or gunny bags for curtains in cold weather.

For the floor, a mixture of cement, sand, and gravel (1:2:4 ratio) is ideal. If cement is expensive, use compacted murram or clay, but cover it with dry wood shavings. For walls, you can use mud bricks, burnt bricks, or even woven sticks plastered with mud. Ensure the wire mesh covers all openings to keep out wild birds and rodents.

For feeders and drinkers, you need 10 tube feeders (capacity 10 kg each) and 10 drinkers (capacity 5 liters each) for 100 birds. These can be made from local materials: feeders from plastic basins with a rim, drinkers from jerrycans with a trough. But commercial plastic ones are cheap and easy to clean.

Lighting: If you want birds to eat at night, use a solar lamp or a 40-watt bulb per 20 square meters. For heating in cold weather, use charcoal stoves (jiko) or infrared bulbs, but ensure ventilation to avoid carbon monoxide. Always have a thermometer and hygrometer to monitor temperature and humidity.

Safety gear: gloves, boots, and a nose mask when mixing cement or cleaning. Keep a first aid kit handy. Total cost for a 3m x 4m house using local materials: about $200-$300. That is a small price for a structure that will serve you for many batches.

3. Step by Step Method

Step 1: Choose the site. Pick a spot that is slightly raised, well-drained, and away from noise and other animals. The house should face east-west to minimize direct sunlight on the long sides. Clear the area of grass and bushes.

Step 2: Mark the floor plan. For 100 birds, measure 3m x 4m. Use pegs and string to mark corners. Dig holes for posts every 1.5m along the perimeter. Posts should be 2.5m long, with 0.5m buried in the ground. Use treated wood or char the ends to prevent termites.

Step 3: Build the floor. If using concrete, mix cement, sand, and gravel (1:2:4) and pour a 5cm thick slab. Let it cure for 3 days. If using earth, compact the soil and cover with 10cm of dry wood shavings. The floor should slope slightly to one side for drainage.

Step 4: Erect the frame. Connect posts with horizontal timbers at 1m and 2m heights. This gives a wall height of 2m. For the roof, add rafters every 1m, sloping to one side or both sides. Cover with iron sheets or thatch. Leave a 30cm overhang to keep rain out.

Step 5: Cover the walls. Use wire mesh (1cm holes) to cover all sides from the floor to the roof, leaving a door (0.8m x 1.8m) on one side. If you need solid walls, build them up to 1m high, then wire mesh above for ventilation. For cold areas, add curtains that can be rolled up or down.

Step 6: Install equipment. Hang feeders and drinkers from the ceiling or place on stands to prevent contamination. Provide one feeder and one drinker per 10 birds. Spread wood shavings 5cm deep as litter. Place a thermometer at bird level.

Step 7: Test and adjust. Before bringing chicks, check for drafts, leaks, and predators. Run the heating (if needed) to reach 32-34C for day-old chicks. Ensure ventilation without strong drafts. Your house is now ready for batch production.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Overcrowding. Many farmers put 200 birds in a 3m x 4m house. This causes stress, pecking, and disease. Fix: Stock at 10 birds per square meter. For 100 birds, use 3m x 4m. If you want more birds, build a bigger house.

Mistake 2: Poor ventilation. Closing all sides to keep birds warm leads to ammonia buildup and respiratory disease. Fix: Always have wire mesh openings on at least two sides. In cold weather, use curtains but leave a gap at the top for air. Ammonia smell should not sting your eyes.

Mistake 3: Wet litter. Water spills and rain entering the house create wet litter, which causes coccidiosis and foot problems. Fix: Raise drinkers on stands, fix leaks, and add fresh shavings when wet. Ensure the floor drains well.

Mistake 4: Using the wrong wire mesh. Chicken wire with large holes lets in wild birds and rodents. Fix: Use 1cm hexagonal wire mesh. It costs a bit more but saves you from disease.

Mistake 5: No predator protection. Snakes, hawks, and thieves can wipe out your flock. Fix: Bury wire mesh 30cm into the ground around the perimeter. Cover windows with strong mesh. Lock the door at night.

Mistake 6: Ignoring temperature. Chicks need 32-34C in week 1, reducing by 3C each week. Without a thermometer, you guess. Fix: Buy a cheap thermometer. Watch chick behavior: if they huddle, it's too cold; if they pant and spread out, it's too hot.

Mistake 7: Building with inadequate materials. Using weak poles or leaking roof leads to collapse or wet birds. Fix: Use treated poles and secure roofing. Spend a little more now to avoid losses later.

Mistake 8: No footbath. Disease enters on your shoes. Fix: Place a footbath with disinfectant at the entrance. Change it daily. These fixes are simple but make a huge difference.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety first for you and your birds. When building, wear gloves, boots, and a dust mask. Cement dust and wood shavings can harm your lungs. Use sharp tools carefully. Keep children away from the construction site. If using electricity, ensure wires are insulated and away from water.

Biosecurity is best practice. Limit visitors. Always wear clean boots and clothes when entering the house. Use a footbath with disinfectant (e.g., 2% sodium hypochlorite) at the door. Wash hands before and after handling birds. Never bring live birds from other farms into your house. If you must, quarantine them for 2 weeks.

Daily routine: Check birds early morning and evening. Look for sick birds (droopy, ruffled feathers, diarrhea). Remove dead birds immediately and bury or burn them. Clean drinkers and feeders daily with clean water and soap. Replace wet litter. Record feed and water intakea sudden drop means trouble.

Ventilation management: In hot weather, open all vents. In cold, close curtains but leave a small opening. Use a thermometer to monitor. Avoid drafts directly on chicks. In humid areas, use fans or increase airflow to prevent respiratory issues.

Litter management: Keep litter dry and loose. Turn it weekly. Remove caked litter. After each batch, remove all litter, clean and disinfect the house, and let it dry for 3 days before bringing new chicks. This breaks disease cycles.

Emergency plan: Have a contact for a vet. Keep a basic medicine kit: antibiotics, vitamins, and disinfectants. In case of power outage, have a backup heat source (charcoal stove) but ensure ventilation. Train your workers on safety and biosecurity. A safe house is a productive house.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it's time to apply what you've learned. This exercise will guide you to plan and build a broiler house for 100 birds. You will need a notebook, measuring tape, and a pencil. If you already have a house, use this to assess and improve it.

Task 1: Site selection. Walk around your compound and identify three possible sites. For each, note: is it raised? Does it drain well? Is it away from noise and other animals? Is it secure? Choose the best site and explain why in your notebook.

Task 2: Design your house. On paper, draw a 3m x 4m floor plan. Mark the door, wire mesh walls, and roof slope. Decide on materials: will you use wooden poles or bricks? Iron sheets or thatch? Write down the quantities you need. For example, for a 3m x 4m house: 12 posts (2.5m long), 20 rafters, 6 iron sheets, 12m of wire mesh (1m high), 1 door, 10 feeders, 10 drinkers, 2 bags of cement, and 1 truck of sand.

Task 3: Cost estimate. Visit a local hardware store or ask around for prices. Calculate the total cost. Compare with your budget. If too high, consider using cheaper local materials like bamboo or mud bricks. Write down your final budget.

Task 4: Build a model. If you cannot build full-size immediately, make a small model (1:10 scale) using sticks, cardboard, and wire. This helps you visualize the structure and spot problems.

Task 5: Set up the house. Once built, test it. Place a thermometer inside. Check for drafts by standing inside and feeling air movement. Ensure predators cannot enter. Add 5cm of wood shavings. Install feeders and drinkers. Simulate a batch by placing stones or balls to represent birds. Check if you can move easily for feeding and cleaning.

Task 6: Write a report. In your notebook, describe your house, the materials used, the cost, and any challenges. Take a photo if possible. Share with your trainer or fellow learners for feedback. This exercise ensures you are ready to house your first batch of broilers.

<h2>Practical Reference: Housing for Batch Production</h2>

<p>In a batch production system for broilers, housing is not just shelter it is the primary tool that controls your feed conversion ratio, mortality rate, and ultimately your profit per batch. Across Africa, from the highlands of Kenya to the coastal plains of Ghana, broiler producers who treat housing as an engineered system rather than a casual structure consistently achieve 1.51.8 kg feed conversion ratios and under 5% mortality. Those who do not often lose 1525% of their flock to preventable causes. This reference lesson gives you the practical specifications, tables, and checklists to build or upgrade housing that supports a 68 week batch cycle, whether you are raising 100 birds for a local market or 5,000 for urban supply chains.</p>

<strong style="color:#c0392b; font-size:16px;"> SAFETY WARNING Ammonia and Heat Stress</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#333;">Never enter a closed broiler house without first opening vents and waiting at least 10 minutes. High ammonia levels (above 25 ppm) can cause respiratory failure in both birds and humans. If your eyes water or you cough on entry, ammonia is too high increase ventilation immediately and add fresh litter. During heat waves (above 35C), birds can die within 30 minutes. Install a thermometer at bird level, provide cool drinking water, and never overcrowd. In case of power failure for fans, have a manual ventilation plan (open all side curtains) and act within 5 minutes.</p>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>
<ul style="font-family:Arial, sans-serif; font-size:15px; line-height:1.8;">
<li><strong>Site:</strong> Well-drained, elevated ground; at least 30 m from other poultry houses and 100 m from roads.</li>
<li><strong>Orientation:</strong> Eastwest to minimise direct sunlight on long walls; open sides facing prevailing breeze.</li>
<li><strong>Roof:</strong> 1.2 m overhang, 2530 pitch, reflective or insulated material to reduce heat load.</li>
<li><strong>Ventilation:</strong> Open sides with wire mesh (1.52 m high); ridge vents or open ridge for hot air escape.</li>
<li><strong>Floor:</strong> Raised 1520 cm, concrete or compacted earth, with 58 cm fresh litter (wood shavings, rice hulls).</li>
<li><strong>Biosecurity:</strong> Footbath at entrance, dedicated boots and clothing, no visitors inside the house.</li>
<li><strong>Equipment:</strong> 45 tube feeders and 810 drinkers per 100 birds; adjust heights as birds grow.</li>
<li><strong>Records:</strong> Daily temperature, mortality, feed and water intake logged for every batch.</li>
</ul>
