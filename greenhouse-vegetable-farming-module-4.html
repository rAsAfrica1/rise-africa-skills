1. Lesson 1: Selecting and Preparing Your Greenhouse Site in Africa

Welcome to Module 4 of Greenhouse Vegetable Farming. In this first lesson we focus on one of the most important business decisions you will make: where to put your greenhouse and how to prepare the ground beneath it. A greenhouse is a capital investment. In many African countries a small 8 metre by 15 metre structure can cost between 1,500 and 6,000 US dollars depending on whether you use timber, galvanised steel, or locally fabricated frames. If you place it badly you will lose that money through poor ventilation, flooding, or weak market access. So treat site selection as a financial decision, not just a farming decision. Start with water. Vegetables such as tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers, and leafy greens need reliable water every day. A greenhouse of 120 square metres in a warm climate can use 300 to 600 litres per day during peak season. Before you build, confirm that you have a borehole, a well, a river, or a reliable municipal supply within 50 metres. If you must pump water, calculate the cost of fuel or electricity. Solar pumps are increasingly popular across Africa because they reduce monthly running costs. A solar pump for a small greenhouse may cost 400 to 900 US dollars but can pay for itself within two or three seasons by removing diesel expenses. Next, look at drainage. Greenhouses concentrate rainwater from the roof. If the surrounding ground slopes toward the structure, water will enter and destroy your beds. Choose land that slopes away gently, or dig a shallow drainage channel around the perimeter. In flood-prone areas such as parts of Mozambique, Nigeria, and Kenya, raise the greenhouse floor with gravel and soil to at least 15 centimetres above the surrounding ground. This simple step prevents root diseases caused by waterlogging. Sunlight matters too. Vegetables need six to eight hours of direct light daily. Avoid sites shaded by tall trees, buildings, or hills. In East Africa, orient the greenhouse east to west so that the long side faces the sun. This reduces overheating during the middle of the day. In West Africa, where afternoon heat is intense, a north-south orientation with shade netting on the western side can lower internal temperatures by 3 to 5 degrees Celsius. Wind is another factor. Strong winds can tear polythene covers and damage frames. Plant a windbreak of fast-growing trees such as Grevillea or Casuarina about 10 metres from the greenhouse. Do not plant trees too close because roots may damage the foundation and branches may shade the structure. Now consider market access. A greenhouse is not a hobby. You must sell what you grow. Choose a site within 30 kilometres of a market, hotel, school, or restaurant that buys vegetables regularly. Good roads reduce transport costs and post-harvest losses. If you are in a remote area, consider forming a cooperative with other farmers to share transport. Some of the most profitable greenhouse farmers in Kenya and Uganda are those who supply supermarkets and hotels with consistent quality. They chose sites near tarmac roads. Soil preparation is the next step. Even though you will grow in beds or bags, the soil beneath the greenhouse affects drainage and temperature. Remove all weeds and roots before construction. Level the ground. If the soil is heavy clay, mix in sand, compost, and rice husks to improve drainage. If it is sandy, add well-rotted manure and compost to improve water retention. A good growing medium for greenhouse vegetables in Africa is a mixture of topsoil, compost, and sand in a ratio of 3:2:1. You can also use coco peat, rice husks, or sawdust, but make sure sawdust is well decomposed to avoid nitrogen lock-up. Fumigation or solarisation can reduce soil pests. Solarisation means covering moist soil with clear plastic for four to six weeks during the hottest part of the year. The heat kills nematodes, fungi, and weed seeds. This is a low-cost, chemical-free method suitable for organic production. Finally, plan your layout before you build. Leave a central path of at least 60 centimetres so you can move harvests and inputs. Beds should be 1 metre wide and no more than 10 metres long for easy access. Include a small nursery area, a water tank, and a storage space for tools and fertiliser. A well-planned greenhouse saves labour and increases yields. In summary, site selection and preparation determine whether your greenhouse becomes a profitable business or an expensive shade house. Check water, drainage, sunlight, wind, market access, and soil. Spend time on this step. It is far cheaper to move a planned greenhouse than to move a built one. In the next lesson we will look at climate control and ventilation, which keep your crops healthy inside the structure you have chosen.

2. Lesson 2: Climate Control and Ventilation for African Greenhouses

In this lesson we tackle climate control. A greenhouse creates a protected environment, but without proper ventilation and temperature management it can become an oven. In many parts of Africa, midday temperatures inside a poorly ventilated greenhouse can reach 45 degrees Celsius or more, which causes flower drop in tomatoes and peppers, leaf scorching in leafy greens, and reduced pollination. Your goal is to keep the internal temperature between 18 and 28 degrees Celsius for most warm-season vegetables, and between 15 and 24 degrees Celsius for cool-season crops such as lettuce and spinach. The first tool for climate control is ventilation. There are two types: roof vents and side vents. Roof vents allow hot air to escape because hot air rises. Side vents allow cooler air to enter. For effective passive ventilation, the total vent area should be at least 15 to 20 percent of the floor area. For example, a 120 square metre greenhouse needs about 18 to 24 square metres of vent opening. Many low-cost greenhouses in Africa use roll-up sides. These are polythene curtains that you roll up in the morning and roll down in the evening. They are cheap and effective, but they require daily labour. If you cannot be present every day, consider installing automatic vent openers. These use wax cylinders or solar-powered motors and cost between 50 and 200 US dollars per vent. They pay for themselves by preventing heat stress on days when you are away. The second tool is shading. Shade netting reduces incoming solar radiation and lowers temperature. In West Africa, use 30 to 50 percent shade netting during the hottest months. In East and Southern Africa, 20 to 30 percent is often enough. Shade netting can be placed on top of the greenhouse or on the western side where afternoon sun is strongest. Do not over-shade because plants need light for photosynthesis. A good rule is to observe your plants. If leaves are small and stems are thin, they may need more light. If leaves are scorched and flowers drop, they need more shade. The third tool is air circulation. Even with vents, hot spots can develop. Horizontal airflow fans move air across the crop and reduce humidity-related diseases such as powdery mildew and botrytis. A small solar-powered fan can run during the day. Alternatively, open vents on opposite sides to create cross-ventilation. In humid regions such as coastal Kenya, Uganda, and Nigeria, airflow is critical to prevent fungal diseases. The fourth tool is humidity management. Most greenhouse vegetables grow best at 60 to 80 percent relative humidity. High humidity encourages disease. Low humidity causes wilting and flower drop. You can manage humidity by watering in the morning rather than the evening, by using drip irrigation instead of overhead sprinklers, and by opening vents to release moist air. A simple hygrometer costs less than 10 US dollars and helps you monitor humidity. The fifth tool is temperature monitoring. Buy a maximum-minimum thermometer. It records the highest and lowest temperatures since you last reset it. Check it every morning. If nighttime temperatures are too low, you may need to close vents earlier in the evening. If daytime temperatures are too high, increase ventilation or shade. In highland areas such as Ethiopia, Kenya, and Rwanda, nighttime temperatures can drop below 10 degrees Celsius. This slows growth and can damage sensitive crops. In these areas, close vents in the late afternoon to trap warmth. Some farmers use thermal curtains or double-layer polythene to reduce heat loss. In lowland hot areas such as Senegal, Mali, and northern Nigeria, the challenge is extreme heat. Here you may need evaporative cooling. This works by passing air through wet pads. The evaporation cools the air. A simple fan-and-pad system can lower internal temperature by 5 to 10 degrees Celsius. It uses water and electricity, so calculate the running cost. For small greenhouses, a misting system that sprays fine water droplets can also cool the air. Be careful not to over-wet the foliage because this encourages disease. Another important factor is the greenhouse cover itself. Polythene film with anti-drip and anti-UV properties lasts longer and reduces condensation dripping on plants. In Africa, UV radiation is strong. Cheap polythene may become brittle and tear within one year. Invest in quality film that lasts three to five years. The extra cost is usually recovered through lower replacement labour and better crop protection. Finally, remember that climate control is not just about equipment. It is about daily management. Open vents early in the morning. Close them before temperatures drop in the evening. Adjust shade nets according to the season. Monitor your crops for signs of stress. Keep a simple record of temperature, humidity, and actions taken. This record helps you learn and improve. A well-ventilated greenhouse in Africa can produce tomatoes, peppers, and cucumbers year-round, even when open-field farmers struggle with heat, rain, or pests. But a poorly ventilated greenhouse becomes a disease chamber. The difference is management. In the next lesson we will discuss irrigation and fertigation, which work together with climate control to produce healthy, high-yielding crops.

3. Lesson 3: Irrigation and Fertigation Systems for Greenhouse Vegetables

Water and nutrients are the engine of your greenhouse business. In this lesson we cover irrigation and fertigation. Fertigation means applying fertiliser through the irrigation system. It is one of the most efficient ways to feed greenhouse vegetables because it delivers nutrients directly to the root zone in small, regular doses. This saves water, saves fertiliser, and reduces labour. Start with water quality. Not all water is suitable for irrigation. Test your water for salinity, pH, and harmful elements such as sodium and chloride. In coastal areas of Africa, wells may be salty. High salinity reduces yields and can kill young seedlings. A simple electrical conductivity meter costs 20 to 50 US dollars and measures salinity. Most vegetables grow well when electrical conductivity is below 2.0 decisiemens per metre. If your water is salty, you may need to blend it with rainwater or use a reverse osmosis system, which is expensive. It is better to find a better water source before you invest in a greenhouse. Water pH should be between 5.5 and 7.0. If it is too high or too low, nutrients become unavailable to plants. You can adjust pH with food-grade acids or bases, but this requires careful measurement. Next, choose an irrigation system. The most common systems for greenhouse vegetables in Africa are drip irrigation, micro-sprinklers, and hand watering. Hand watering is cheap but labour-intensive and often uneven. It also wets the foliage, which encourages disease. Drip irrigation is the best choice for most greenhouse vegetables. It delivers water directly to the root zone, saves 30 to 50 percent of water compared to overhead irrigation, and reduces weeds and disease. A drip kit for a 120 square metre greenhouse costs between 100 and 300 US dollars depending on quality. Look for pressure-compensating drippers because they deliver the same amount of water even if the terrain is uneven. You will also need a filter to prevent clogging. Clean the filter weekly. If you use river or pond water, use a sand filter and a screen filter. Fertigation equipment can be simple or advanced. The simplest method is a venturi injector. It uses the flow of water to suck fertiliser solution into the irrigation line. A venturi injector costs 30 to 80 US dollars. A more precise method is a dosing pump, which costs 200 to 800 US dollars. For small greenhouses, a venturi is sufficient. For larger operations, a dosing pump saves labour and improves accuracy. You will also need a tank for mixing fertiliser. Use a plastic tank of 100 to 200 litres. Mark it with volume lines so you can measure accurately. Always add fertiliser to water, not water to fertiliser. Stir well. Use a filter after the tank to catch undissolved particles. Now let us discuss nutrient management. Greenhouse vegetables need balanced nutrition. The three main nutrients are nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium. Nitrogen promotes leafy growth. Phosphorus supports roots and flowers. Potassium improves fruit quality and disease resistance. You also need calcium, magnesium, and micronutrients such as iron, zinc, and boron. Many African soils are deficient in these. A soil test before planting is essential. It costs 20 to 100 US dollars depending on the laboratory. It tells you what nutrients your soil has and what you need to add. For greenhouse production, you will likely use both soil amendments and fertigation. Before planting, incorporate well-rotted manure or compost at 5 to 10 kilograms per square metre. Add rock phosphate or bone meal for phosphorus. Add wood ash or potassium sulphate for potassium. If your soil is acidic, add agricultural lime. If it is alkaline, add gypsum or elemental sulphur. During the growing season, apply fertiliser through the drip system once or twice per week. A common schedule for tomatoes is 100 to 150 parts per million of nitrogen and potassium, and 30 to 50 parts per million of phosphorus. You can buy pre-mixed greenhouse fertilisers or mix your own using calcium nitrate, potassium nitrate, monoammonium phosphate, and magnesium sulphate. Mixing your own is cheaper but requires careful calculation. Many agricultural supply shops in Africa now sell ready-to-use fertigation blends. Use them according to the label. Do not over-fertilise. Excess fertiliser burns roots, pollutes groundwater, and wastes money. Monitor your plants. Dark green leaves with strong stems indicate good nitrogen. Yellow lower leaves may indicate nitrogen deficiency. Purple leaves may indicate phosphorus deficiency. Brown leaf edges may indicate potassium deficiency. Keep a notebook and adjust your fertigation based on what you see. Water scheduling depends on crop stage, weather, and soil type. Seedlings need small amounts of water frequently. Mature fruiting plants need more water. In hot weather, water twice per day, morning and late afternoon. In cool weather, once per day may be enough. Use a tensiometer or a simple moisture meter to check soil moisture. Do not guess. Overwatering causes root rot and leaches nutrients. Underwatering causes wilting and flower drop. Finally, maintain your system. Flush drip lines monthly to remove salt buildup. Clean filters weekly. Check emitters for clogging. Repair leaks immediately. A well-maintained irrigation system can last five to ten years. A neglected system fails within one or two seasons. In summary, irrigation and fertigation are the heart of greenhouse vegetable production. Invest in drip irrigation, test your water and soil, apply nutrients carefully, and monitor your crop. This approach will give you healthier plants, higher yields, and better profits. In the next lesson we will cover pest and disease management, which protects the investment you have made in climate control and irrigation.

4. Lesson 4: Integrated Pest and Disease Management in Greenhouses

Pests and diseases can destroy a greenhouse crop within days. In this lesson we cover integrated pest management, often called IPM. IPM combines cultural, biological, and chemical methods to keep pests and diseases below damaging levels. It is especially important in greenhouses because the enclosed environment can allow pests and diseases to multiply quickly. The first principle of IPM is prevention. A clean greenhouse is a healthy greenhouse. Before planting, remove all crop residues from the previous season. Pests such as whiteflies, thrips, and aphids can survive on old leaves and stems. Burn or compost residues far from the greenhouse. Clean tools, boots, and gloves. Disinfect pruning shears with alcohol or bleach solution between plants. Use a footbath at the entrance to prevent soil-borne diseases from entering. The second principle is exclusion. Keep pests out. Use insect-proof netting on vents and doors. A mesh size of 40 to 50 mesh is fine enough to keep out whiteflies and aphids while allowing air movement. Check nets for tears and repair them immediately. Avoid opening the greenhouse during peak pest flight times, usually early morning and late afternoon. Do not allow visitors to walk through your crop without clean footwear. The third principle is monitoring. Walk through your greenhouse at least twice per week. Look at the underside of leaves, growing tips, and flowers. Use yellow sticky traps to monitor flying insects. Hang 10 to 20 traps per 100 square metres. Check them weekly. If you see many pests on traps, take action before the population explodes. Keep a record of pest numbers and locations. This helps you target your response. The fourth principle is cultural control. Healthy plants resist pests better. Provide balanced nutrition and proper watering. Avoid excessive nitrogen because it produces soft, succulent growth that attracts aphids and whiteflies. Remove weeds around the greenhouse because they harbour pests. Prune lower leaves of tomatoes and peppers to improve airflow and reduce disease. Stake plants properly to keep fruit off the ground. Rotate crops. Do not grow tomatoes in the same greenhouse season after season because soil-borne diseases such as bacterial wilt and fusarium build up. Rotate with crops from different families, such as leafy greens or beans. The fifth principle is biological control. This means using living organisms to control pests. For example, you can release predatory mites to control spider mites, or parasitic wasps to control whiteflies. These beneficial insects are available from commercial suppliers in Kenya, South Africa, and Egypt. They are more expensive than chemicals but safer and more sustainable. You can also encourage natural enemies by planting flowering plants such as marigolds or basil around the greenhouse. These provide nectar and pollen for beneficial insects. The sixth principle is chemical control. Use pesticides only as a last resort. When you must spray, choose the right product. Use selective pesticides that target the pest without killing beneficial insects. Rotate chemicals with different modes of action to prevent resistance. Always follow the label. Wear protective clothing, including gloves, mask, and long sleeves. Do not spray during the hottest part of the day. Do not spray just before harvest. Observe the pre-harvest interval, which is the time between spraying and harvesting. This is usually 3 to 14 days depending on the product. Never use banned pesticides. Many African countries have strict regulations, and export markets reject produce with illegal residues. Now let us look at common pests. Whiteflies are small white insects that suck sap and transmit viruses. Control them with yellow sticky traps, insect-proof nets, and neem oil. Neem oil is a natural pesticide that is safe for beneficial insects when used correctly. Aphids are small green or black insects that cluster on new growth. Wash them off with a strong water spray or use insecticidal soap. Thrips are tiny insects that cause silvery streaks on leaves and flowers. Use blue sticky traps and spinosad, a biological pesticide. Spider mites are tiny red or brown pests that cause yellow stippling. They thrive in hot, dry conditions. Increase humidity and use predatory mites or neem oil. Common diseases include powdery mildew, which appears as white powder on leaves. It thrives in humid conditions with poor airflow. Improve ventilation and use potassium bicarbonate or sulphur-based fungicides. Botrytis, also called grey mould, causes fuzzy grey growth on stems and fruit. It thrives in cool, humid conditions. Remove infected parts and improve airflow. Bacterial wilt causes sudden wilting and yellowing. It is soil-borne and has no cure. Remove infected plants and solarise soil before replanting. Fusarium wilt causes yellowing and wilting from the bottom up. Use resistant varieties and rotate crops. Viral diseases such as tomato yellow leaf curl virus are transmitted by whiteflies. There is no cure. Control whiteflies and remove infected plants immediately. Finally, keep records. Write down what pest or disease you saw, when you saw it, what you did, and what happened. This record helps you predict problems in future seasons. It also helps you calculate the cost of control measures. IPM is not about eliminating all pests. It is about keeping them below the level that causes economic damage. A few aphids do not require spraying. A few whiteflies do not require spraying. But a sudden outbreak does. By combining prevention, monitoring, cultural control, biological control, and careful chemical use, you can protect your crop and your profit. In the next lesson we will cover harvesting, post-harvest handling, and marketing, which turn your healthy crop into money.

5. Lesson 5: Harvesting, Post-Harvest Handling, and Marketing Greenhouse Vegetables

You have invested in a greenhouse, managed the climate, irrigated and fertilised, and controlled pests. Now you must harvest and sell your crop profitably. In this lesson we cover harvesting, post-harvest handling, and marketing. These steps determine whether you make money or lose it. Harvesting at the right time is critical. Vegetables have different maturity stages. Tomatoes for fresh market are harvested when they are pink or light red, not fully red, because they continue to ripen after harvest. Tomatoes for processing are harvested fully red. Peppers are harvested when they reach full size and colour, either green or red depending on the market. Cucumbers are harvested young and tender, usually 15 to 20 centimetres long. Leafy greens such as lettuce and spinach are harvested when leaves are young and tender, before they become bitter. Use clean, sharp tools. For tomatoes and peppers, use pruning shears or scissors. For leafy greens, use a sharp knife. Do not pull fruit or leaves because this damages the plant and reduces future yields. Harvest in the cool of the morning. This is when produce is crisp and has the highest sugar content. Avoid harvesting in the heat of the day because produce wilts quickly. Place harvested produce in clean crates or baskets. Do not pile produce too high because the weight causes bruising. Do not use dirty containers. Dirty containers carry bacteria that cause rot. Post-harvest handling is where many African farmers lose money. Vegetables are living products. They continue to respire, which produces heat and moisture. If you do not manage this, they spoil. The first step is to remove field heat. Move harvested produce to a cool, shaded area immediately. If you have a cold room, set it to 10 to 12 degrees Celsius for tomatoes and peppers, and 0 to 4 degrees Celsius for leafy greens. If you do not have a cold room, use a simple evaporative cooler. This is a box with wet sand or charcoal on the sides. As water evaporates, it cools the air inside. Evaporative coolers can reduce temperature by 5 to 10 degrees Celsius and are cheap to build. The second step is sorting and grading. Remove damaged, diseased, or overripe produce. Grade by size, colour, and quality. Uniform produce sells for a higher price. Buyers such as supermarkets and hotels want consistent quality. The third step is packaging. Use clean, ventilated crates or boxes. Do not use plastic bags for tomatoes and peppers because they trap moisture and cause rot. For leafy greens, use perforated plastic liners to maintain humidity without causing condensation. Label your packages with your farm name, the product, and the harvest date. This builds trust with buyers. The fourth step is transport. Use a vehicle with a covered bed to protect produce from sun and rain. Do not overload. Stack crates carefully so air can circulate. If you are transporting long distances, consider using a refrigerated truck or a vehicle with a cooling system. For short distances, transport early in the morning or late in the evening when temperatures are lower. Now let us discuss marketing. Your greenhouse is a business. You need customers. There are several market channels in Africa. The first is direct sales to consumers. You can sell at farm gates, farmers markets, or through community supported agriculture schemes. Direct sales give you the highest price but require time and effort. The second is sales to retailers. Small shops, greengrocers, and supermarkets buy in bulk. Supermarkets offer stable prices but require consistent quality and volume. They may also delay payment by 30 to 60 days, which affects your cash flow. The third is sales to hotels, restaurants, and schools. These buyers want fresh, high-quality produce and are often willing to pay a premium. Build relationships with chefs and procurement officers. The fourth is sales to processors. Tomato paste, dried vegetables, and juices are examples. Processors pay less but buy large volumes. The fifth is export. This is the most demanding channel. Export markets require strict quality standards, traceability, and certification. It is not for beginners. Before you plant, identify your market. Talk to potential buyers. Ask what they want, how much they need, and when they need it. Then plan your production to meet that demand. Do not plant first and look for buyers later. That is a recipe for loss. Pricing is another key decision. Calculate your cost of production. Include seeds, fertiliser, water, labour, pest control, packaging, transport, and depreciation on your greenhouse. Add a profit margin. Research market prices. Do not undercut other farmers because this destroys the market for everyone. Compete on quality and reliability, not just price. Keep records. Record every sale, every expense, and every harvest. This helps you calculate profit and loss. It also helps you get loans from banks or microfinance institutions. Many African farmers fail to get credit because they do not have records. Finally, think about value addition. Can you wash, sort, and package your produce to get a better price? Can you make tomato sauce, dried peppers, or pickled cucumbers? Value addition increases your income and reduces waste. In summary, harvesting, post-harvest handling, and marketing are as important as production. Harvest at the right time, handle produce gently, keep it cool, grade and package it well, and sell to the right market. This is how you turn a good crop into a profitable business. Congratulations on completing Module 4. You now have the knowledge to manage a greenhouse vegetable farm from site selection to market. Apply these lessons carefully, keep records, and continue learning. Your greenhouse can provide healthy food, create jobs, and generate income for years to come.

Greenhouse workers are at elevated risk of pesticide exposure because of enclosed spaces with limited air exchange. WHO estimates that pesticide poisoning causes approximately 20,000 deaths annually worldwide, with the majority in low- and middle-income countries, including many in Africa. Observe the following without exception:

  • Pre-Harvest Interval (PHI): Never harvest before the PHI stated on the label. For most greenhouse-approved insecticides in Kenya and Nigeria, PHI ranges from 3 to 14 days. Record the spray date and product on the greenhouse wall.
  • Personal Protective Equipment (PPE): Wear nitrile gloves, goggles, a respirator (N95 or better), long sleeves, and rubber boots during mixing and application. Never re-use pesticide containers for drinking water or food storage.
  • Re-Entry Interval (REI): Do not enter the greenhouse for at least 24 hours after spraying, or as specified on the label. Ventilate fully before re-entry.
  • Nutrient safety: Store fertilisers away from food, feed, and children. Calcium nitrate and potassium nitrate are oxidisers â keep away from fuels and open flames.
  • Water safety: If using wastewater or surface water for irrigation, test for E. coli and heavy metals before applying to edible crops. WHO guidelines for safe use of wastewater in agriculture set a limit of 10Â³â10â´ E. coli per 100 mL for restricted irrigation and <10Â³ for unrestricted irrigation of vegetables eaten raw.
  • First aid: Keep a written emergency plan with local clinic contacts, and a stocked first-aid kit at the greenhouse. If poisoning is suspected, call the nearest poison control centre immediately.

  5. Fertigation schedule review (weekly): Adjust the nutrient recipe based on crop stage â vegetative, flowering, or fruiting. Fruiting crops need more potassium and calcium; leafy greens need more nitrogen. Never mix calcium fertilisers with phosphate or sulphate fertilisers in the same stock tank.
  6. Harvest and post-harvest (daily during harvest season): Harvest in the early morning when temperatures are low. Cool produce within 2 hours of harvest. Grade into A, B, and C categories. Use clean, disinfected crates â never reuse soiled containers from previous crops.
  7. Record-keeping (daily): Log all inputs (fertiliser, pesticide, water, labour), outputs (kg harvested, kg sold, price), and observations (pest pressure, weather, equipment faults). Accurate records are the foundation of bank credit, contract negotiation, and certification (GlobalG.A.P., KenyaGAP).
  8. Weekly business review (every Friday): Calculate cost per kg, revenue per mÂ², and gross margin. Compare against the benchmarks in Table 2. If cost per kg exceeds 70% of your farm-gate price, investigate labour, fertiliser, or transport costs immediately.
