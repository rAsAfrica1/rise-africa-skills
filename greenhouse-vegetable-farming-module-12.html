1. Module 12 Lesson 1: Greenhouse Site Selection and Preparation in Africa

Welcome to Module 12 of Greenhouse Vegetable Farming. In this first lesson we focus on the foundation of every profitable greenhouse operation: choosing and preparing the right site. Many African farmers lose money not because they lack skill but because they put a good greenhouse in a bad location. This lesson will help you avoid that mistake. Start by understanding your market. Before you even look at land, decide who will buy your tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers, or leafy greens. Are you selling to a nearby city hotel, a school, a local market, or a supermarket chain? Your market determines your transport distance, your volume, and your price. A greenhouse near a tarmac road or a trading center saves you money on every kilogram you sell. If you are far from buyers, your profit disappears in fuel and spoilage. Next, assess water. A greenhouse vegetable crop needs reliable irrigation. In most of Africa, rain is seasonal and unreliable. You need a water source that lasts through the dry season. This could be a borehole, a river, a dam, a well, or a municipal supply. Test the water for salt and chemicals. High salinity burns roots and reduces yield. If you use river water, check upstream for pollution. Calculate your daily water need. A 8 by 15 meter greenhouse with tomatoes can use 200 to 400 liters per day in hot weather. Make sure your source can supply that for at least six months without failing. Then look at the land itself. The site should be gently sloping, not flat and not steep. A gentle slope of 1 to 2 percent allows drainage and prevents flooding. Avoid low-lying areas where water collects. Avoid rocky ground that makes anchoring difficult. Avoid old cattle kraals or rubbish dumps because of disease and soil contamination. The soil should be loamy, deep, and well drained. If the soil is poor, you can grow in bags or raised beds, but good soil saves money. Check the soil pH. Most vegetables prefer pH 6.0 to 6.8. You can test with a simple kit or send a sample to a lab. Also check for nematodes and soil-borne diseases. If the land grew tomatoes or peppers recently, choose another spot or plan to fumigate or use grafted seedlings. Sunlight is critical. Choose a site that receives full sun for at least six hours per day. Avoid shade from tall trees, buildings, or hills. In hot regions, afternoon shade can help, but too much shade reduces growth and increases disease. Wind is another factor. Strong winds tear polythene and damage plants. A site with a natural windbreak such as a hedge or a row of trees is ideal. If not, plant a windbreak before you build. Do not plant tall trees too close because roots compete for water and nutrients. Now consider security. Greenhouses contain valuable inputs: drip lines, polythene, pumps, and crops. Theft is a real risk in many areas. Choose a site near your home or a trusted neighbor. Fence the area. If possible, keep a dog or hire a watchman. Insurance is rare in African smallholder farming, so prevention is your best policy. Access is next. You need a road or path that allows a pickup truck or motorcycle to reach the greenhouse, especially during harvest. If the road becomes impassable in the rainy season, your produce will rot. Also consider electricity. A small solar panel can run a pump or fan, but if you have grid power, it helps for pumping and lighting. Do not rely on a single source. Plan for a backup. Now let us talk about preparation. Clear the site of bushes, stumps, and stones. Level the ground if needed. Mark the corners of the greenhouse using pegs and string. The standard orientation is east to west for maximum light in the morning and evening, but in very hot areas, north to south can reduce midday heat. Check with local extension officers for your region. Dig holes for the corner posts and intermediate posts. Use treated timber, galvanized steel, or concrete. In termite-prone areas, treat wood with used engine oil or use metal. The posts should go at least 60 centimeters into the ground. Space them according to your design, usually 2 to 3 meters apart. Build a strong frame. The frame must withstand wind and rain. Use diagonal bracing at the corners. For the roof, use a single or double slope depending on your design. Cover with UV-treated polythene of 150 to 200 microns. In Africa, UV treatment is essential because the sun is harsh. Cheap polythene will crack within six months. Invest in quality. Install insect netting on the sides. This keeps out whiteflies, aphids, and other pests. Netting also reduces wind. Use a mesh size of 40 to 50 mesh for small insects. For the door, use a double door system to prevent pests from entering when you go in. Now prepare the soil inside. Add well-rotted manure or compost. A good rate is 20 to 30 kilograms per square meter. Mix it into the top 20 centimeters. If you use raised beds, make them 1 meter wide and 20 centimeters high. Add a layer of mulch to conserve water and suppress weeds. Install your irrigation system. Drip irrigation is best because it saves water and reduces disease. Lay the drip lines along the beds. Connect to a filter and a tank. Test the system for leaks. If you use a watering can, plan for daily labor. Drip is more efficient and allows you to fertigate, which means adding fertilizer through the water. Before planting, solarize the soil if you have pest problems. Cover the moist soil with clear plastic for four to six weeks during the hottest season. This kills many soil-borne pests and weeds. Alternatively, use a fumigant if registered in your country, but follow safety rules. Finally, keep records. Write down the site history, soil test results, water tests, and costs. This helps you improve and also helps if you apply for a loan. A well-chosen and well-prepared site is the first step to a profitable greenhouse business. 2. Module 12 Lesson 2: Crop Selection and Planting Schedules for African Greenhouses

In this second lesson of Module 12, we focus on what to grow and when to grow it. Greenhouse vegetable farming is a business, so crop selection must be driven by market demand, price, and your ability to manage the crop. Many African greenhouse farmers grow tomatoes because everyone grows tomatoes, then they struggle to sell. Others grow high-value crops like colored bell peppers, cherry tomatoes, lettuce, and herbs for hotels and supermarkets. The key is to match your crop to your market and your skills. Start with a market survey. Visit local markets, hotels, restaurants, and supermarkets. Ask what vegetables they buy, in what quantities, and at what prices. Ask about quality standards. For example, supermarkets may want uniform size, no blemishes, and specific packaging. Hotels may want fresh herbs like basil, mint, and coriander. Export markets are demanding and often require certifications, so start local. Also check what your neighbors are growing. If everyone has tomatoes in the same month, prices will be low. Plan to harvest when supply is low. In many African countries, tomato prices are high during the rainy season because field tomatoes rot, but greenhouse tomatoes can be produced. However, greenhouse production costs are higher, so you need a premium price. Choose crops with a high value-to-weight ratio. Leafy greens like lettuce, spinach, and kale are heavy and perishable, so they are best sold locally. Tomatoes, peppers, and cucumbers are heavier but have a longer shelf life. Herbs like basil, parsley, and mint are light and high value, perfect for hotels. Strawberries are high value but require more skill and care. Now consider your greenhouse type. A simple polythene tunnel with insect netting is good for tomatoes, peppers, and cucumbers. A shade house is better for leafy greens in hot areas. A fully controlled greenhouse with cooling and heating is expensive and rare in Africa. Most farmers use naturally ventilated greenhouses. Choose crops that suit your structure. For example, tall indeterminate tomatoes need vertical space and trellising. Determinate tomatoes are bushier and easier. Cucumbers need trellising too. Peppers are bushy and can be grown in beds or bags. Lettuce can be grown in beds or hydroponically. Next, think about crop duration and cash flow. You want a mix of short and long cycle crops. Lettuce is ready in 30 to 45 days. Spinach in 30 to 40 days. Radish in 25 to 30 days. These give quick returns. Tomatoes take 60 to 90 days to first harvest and can produce for 6 to 9 months if indeterminate. Peppers take 70 to 90 days and produce for several months. Cucumbers take 50 to 70 days and produce for 2 to 3 months. If you only grow tomatoes, you wait months for income. A good strategy is to have one main crop like tomatoes or peppers and a quick crop like lettuce or herbs in a small section. This spreads risk and improves cash flow. Now let us talk about planting schedules. In Africa, seasons vary. In East Africa, there are two rainy seasons: long rains from March to May and short rains from October to December. In West Africa, the rainy season is from May to October in the south and shorter in the north. In Southern Africa, rains come from November to March. Greenhouses allow you to grow year-round, but you must manage temperature and humidity. In hot dry seasons, you need cooling and more water. In cold seasons, you need to protect from cold, especially at night. For tomatoes, the ideal temperature is 20 to 27 degrees Celsius during the day and 15 to 18 at night. Above 30 degrees, fruit set fails. Below 10 degrees, growth stops. So in very hot areas, plant tomatoes at the beginning of the cool season or use shade nets. In cool highland areas, plant when temperatures are moderate. Peppers like similar temperatures but can tolerate a bit more heat. Cucumbers like warmer temperatures, 22 to 28 degrees. Lettuce likes cooler temperatures, 15 to 22 degrees. In hot areas, grow lettuce in the coolest months or use shade. Herbs like basil like warmth, 20 to 30 degrees. Now plan your planting calendar. Work backwards from your target market. If you want to sell tomatoes for Christmas, you need to plant about 3 to 4 months before. If you want lettuce for a hotel every week, you need to plant a small batch every week. This is called succession planting. It ensures continuous supply and steady income. Keep a simple chart on your wall. Mark planting dates, expected harvest dates, and actual dates. This helps you learn and improve. Seed selection is critical. Buy certified seed from reputable suppliers. Avoid saving seed from hybrid varieties because they will not perform the same. For open-pollinated varieties, you can save seed, but select the best plants. Choose varieties that are disease-resistant. For tomatoes, look for resistance to tomato yellow leaf curl virus, bacterial wilt, and fusarium wilt. In Africa, these diseases are common. For peppers, look for resistance to bacterial spot and viruses. For cucumbers, look for resistance to powdery mildew and downy mildew. Ask your extension officer or seed company for recommendations for your area. Now consider seedlings. You can raise your own seedlings in a nursery or buy from a trusted nursery. Raising your own saves money but requires care. Use clean trays and a sterile growing medium. Water gently. Harden off seedlings before transplanting by reducing water and exposing them to more sun. Transplant when seedlings have 4 to 6 true leaves, usually 3 to 4 weeks after sowing. Transplant in the evening or on a cloudy day to reduce shock. Water immediately after transplanting. Spacing depends on the crop. Tomatoes: 50 to 60 centimeters between plants and 60 to 80 centimeters between rows. Peppers: 40 to 50 centimeters between plants and 50 to 60 centimeters between rows. Cucumbers: 30 to 40 centimeters between plants and 1 meter between rows if trellised. Lettuce: 20 to 30 centimeters between plants and 20 to 30 centimeters between rows. Do not overcrowd. Good airflow reduces disease. After planting, apply mulch. Mulch conserves water, suppresses weeds, and keeps fruit clean. Use dry grass, straw, or plastic mulch. Plastic mulch is more expensive but lasts longer and warms the soil. In hot areas, use white or silver plastic to reflect heat. In cool areas, use black plastic to warm the soil. Fertilization is next. Greenhouse crops need more fertilizer because they are grown intensively. Do a soil test to know what is missing. Generally, you need nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium. You also need calcium, magnesium, and micronutrients. Use a balanced fertilizer at planting, then switch to a high-potassium fertilizer during fruiting. Fertigate with soluble fertilizers through the drip system. Do not over-fertilize. Too much nitrogen gives big leaves but few fruits. Too much salt burns roots. Follow the label. Organic farmers can use compost, manure, and approved organic fertilizers. Now think about crop rotation. Do not grow the same crop in the same soil season after season. Pests and diseases build up. Rotate with a different family. For example, after tomatoes, grow lettuce or carrots. After peppers, grow beans or peas. Legumes add nitrogen to the soil. If you grow in bags or containers, you can replace the growing medium each season. Finally, keep records of every crop: variety, planting date, fertilizer, pesticides, yield, and price. This data helps you decide what to grow next season. In the next lesson, we will cover irrigation and fertigation management for greenhouse vegetables.

3. Module 12 Lesson 3: Irrigation and Fertigation Management in African Greenhouses

Welcome to Lesson 3 of Module 12. Water and fertilizer are the two biggest input costs in greenhouse vegetable farming after labor. If you manage them well, you increase yield and profit. If you manage them poorly, you waste money and harm the environment. This lesson gives you practical steps for irrigation and fertigation in African greenhouses. First, understand water quality. Not all water is safe for vegetables. Test your water for salinity, pH, and harmful elements like sodium, chloride, and boron. High salinity reduces water uptake and causes leaf burn. If your water is salty, you may need to leach the soil with extra water or switch to a more salt-tolerant crop. The ideal pH for irrigation water is 5.5 to 7.0. If it is too high or too low, adjust with acids or bases. You can buy test kits or send samples to a lab. In many African cities, labs at agricultural research stations offer this service. Next, choose an irrigation system. The best for greenhouses is drip irrigation. It saves water, reduces disease, and allows precise fertigation. A drip system consists of a water source, pump, filter, main line, sub-main, and drip lines. You also need a tank for mixing fertilizer. For small greenhouses, you can use a gravity system with a raised tank. For larger ones, use a pump. Always install a filter. Sand filters remove particles. Screen filters remove smaller particles. If you use river water, use both. Drip lines come in different diameters and emitter spacings. For tomatoes and peppers, use emitters every 30 centimeters. For lettuce, every 20 centimeters. The flow rate is usually 2 to 4 liters per hour per emitter. Calculate your system capacity. For a 8 by 15 meter greenhouse with 10 beds of 15 meters, you might have 10 drip lines of 15 meters each. If emitters are every 30 centimeters, that is 50 emitters per line, 500 emitters total. At 2 liters per hour, that is 1000 liters per hour. Your pump and tank must supply that. Now plan your irrigation schedule. The goal is to keep the root zone moist but not waterlogged. Overwatering wastes water, leaches nutrients, and causes root diseases. Underwatering stresses plants and reduces yield. The amount of water needed depends on crop stage, temperature, humidity, and wind. A simple method is to use a tensiometer or a moisture meter. These devices tell you when to water. If you cannot afford them, use the feel method. Dig 10 centimeters into the soil. If it feels dry, water. If it feels moist, wait. For most greenhouse vegetables, you water once or twice a day in hot weather and every other day in cool weather. Early morning is the best time. Avoid late evening watering because leaves stay wet and disease spreads. Drip irrigation avoids wet leaves, but the soil surface stays wet. Mulch helps. Now let us talk about fertigation. Fertigation is applying fertilizer through the irrigation system. It saves labor and delivers nutrients directly to the roots. Use only soluble fertilizers. Common ones are calcium nitrate, potassium nitrate, monoammonium phosphate, and magnesium sulfate. You can also buy pre-mixed greenhouse fertilizers. Do not use granular fertilizers in drip systems because they clog emitters. Mix fertilizers in a tank. Follow the label. A general schedule for tomatoes: week 1 to 3 after transplanting, use a high-nitrogen fertilizer. Week 4 to 6, use a balanced fertilizer. Week 7 onward, use a high-potassium fertilizer. Always add calcium and magnesium. Calcium prevents blossom end rot. Magnesium prevents yellow leaves. Do not mix calcium with phosphate or sulfate fertilizers in the same tank because they form precipitates that clog emitters. Apply them on different days or use two tanks. The electrical conductivity of the nutrient solution should be 1.5 to 2.5 millisiemens per centimeter for tomatoes. For lettuce, 1.0 to 1.5. For peppers, 1.5 to 2.0. Measure with an EC meter. If EC is too high, reduce fertilizer. If too low, increase. Also check pH. The ideal pH of the nutrient solution is 5.8 to 6.2. Adjust with nitric acid or potassium hydroxide. Now consider the frequency of fertigation. You can fertigate every time you water, but at a lower concentration, or once or twice a week at a higher concentration. The first method is better because it provides steady nutrition. However, it requires a good injector or a tank mix. For small farmers, a simple tank with a valve works. Fill the tank with water and fertilizer. Open the valve to let it flow into the drip lines. After the tank is empty, flush the lines with clear water for 10 to 15 minutes. This prevents salt buildup. Flushing is critical. Once a week, flush the drip lines with clear water. Once a month, remove the end caps and flush each line. If emitters clog, soak them in vinegar or a commercial cleaner. In hard water areas, use acid to dissolve calcium carbonate. Now let us discuss water conservation. In many African regions, water is scarce. Use mulch to reduce evaporation. Use shade nets to reduce temperature and water loss. Fix leaks immediately. Harvest rainwater from the greenhouse roof. A 8 by 15 meter roof can collect thousands of liters per year. Store in a tank or a lined pond. Use a first-flush diverter to keep out dirt. If you use a borehole, install a solar pump to reduce electricity costs. Solar pumps are now affordable and reliable in Africa. They also work during power cuts. Now consider the labor aspect. Irrigation and fertigation can be done by one person if the system is well designed. But you must train that person. Write down the schedule. Use a simple logbook. Record the date, time, amount of water, amount of fertilizer, and any problems. This helps you track costs and improve. If you have multiple greenhouses, use a manifold to direct water to one section at a time. This reduces the pump size and cost. Now let us talk about common mistakes. Mistake one: overwatering. This causes root rot, yellow leaves, and fruit cracking. Mistake two: underwatering. This causes wilting, small fruits, and blossom drop. Mistake three: mixing incompatible fertilizers. This clogs emitters. Mistake four: not flushing lines. This causes salt buildup and poor flow. Mistake five: using dirty water without a filter. This clogs emitters. Mistake six: ignoring EC and pH. This causes nutrient deficiencies or toxicities. Mistake seven: applying fertilizer to dry soil. This burns roots. Always water first, then fertigate, then water again. Now let us talk about monitoring. Walk through your greenhouse every day. Look at the plants. Yellow leaves can mean nitrogen deficiency. Purple leaves can mean phosphorus deficiency. Brown leaf edges can mean potassium deficiency. Blossom end rot on tomatoes is calcium deficiency, often caused by irregular watering. If you see these signs, adjust your fertigation. Use a leaf color chart if available. Take soil samples every season. Keep records. Finally, think about the business. Water and fertilizer are costs. If you waste them, you lose profit. If you manage them well, you can produce more with less. A good irrigation and fertigation plan can increase yield by 20 to 50 percent. It also improves quality, which means higher prices. 4. Module 12 Lesson 4: Pest and Disease Management in African Greenhouses

In this fourth lesson of Module 12, we tackle one of the biggest challenges in greenhouse vegetable farming: pests and diseases. A greenhouse protects plants from rain, wind, and some pests, but it also creates a warm, humid environment where diseases can spread quickly. If you do not manage pests and diseases well, you can lose your entire crop. This lesson gives you practical, Africa-focused strategies. First, understand the principle of integrated pest management, or IPM. IPM means using a combination of methods: cultural, physical, biological, and chemical. Do not rely on chemicals alone. Chemicals are expensive, can harm human health, and pests develop resistance. Start with prevention. Prevention begins with sanitation. Keep your greenhouse clean. Remove weeds, crop residues, and fallen fruits. These harbor pests and diseases. Do not pile up rubbish near the greenhouse. Clean your tools and boots before entering. Use a footbath with disinfectant at the door. If you smoke, do not smoke near the plants because tobacco can carry viruses. Use clean seed and seedlings. Buy certified seed. If you raise your own seedlings, use sterile trays and media. Do not reuse old soil without sterilizing it. Solarize soil or use steam. Next, manage the environment. Ventilation is key. Open vents during the day to reduce humidity. In hot areas, use shade nets to reduce temperature. In cool areas, close vents at night to keep warmth. Avoid overhead watering. Use drip irrigation. Space plants properly. Do not overcrowd. Prune lower leaves of tomatoes and peppers to improve airflow. Remove diseased leaves immediately and put them in a bag, not on the ground. Now let us talk about common pests. Whiteflies are a major pest in African greenhouses. They suck sap and transmit viruses like tomato yellow leaf curl virus. Control them with yellow sticky traps. Use insect netting on vents and doors. Spray with neem oil or insecticidal soap. If you use chemicals, rotate between different modes of action. Do not spray the same chemical repeatedly. Aphids are another common pest. They also transmit viruses. Control with ladybirds, lacewings, and parasitic wasps. You can buy these beneficial insects from suppliers in Kenya, South Africa, and other countries. If not, use neem oil or soap. Thrips cause silvery streaks on leaves and damage flowers. Use blue sticky traps. Spray with spinosad or neem. Spider mites cause yellow stippling and fine webs. They thrive in hot, dry conditions. Increase humidity. Spray with neem oil or sulfur. Tuta absoluta is a devastating pest of tomatoes in Africa. The larvae mine leaves and bore into fruits. Use pheromone traps to monitor. Remove infested leaves. Spray with Bacillus thuringiensis or spinosad. Rotate chemicals. Do not compost infested plant material. Now let us talk about diseases. Fungal diseases are common in greenhouses because of humidity. Early blight and late blight on tomatoes cause brown spots and fruit rot. Prevent by improving airflow, reducing leaf wetness, and using resistant varieties. If you see symptoms, remove affected leaves and spray with copper-based fungicide or a biofungicide. Powdery mildew appears as white powder on leaves. It thrives in warm, dry conditions with high humidity at night. Spray with potassium bicarbonate, sulfur, or neem oil. Downy mildew causes yellow patches on the upper leaf surface and fuzzy growth underneath. It thrives in cool, wet conditions. Improve ventilation and use copper sprays. Bacterial wilt is a soil-borne disease that causes sudden wilting. There is no chemical cure. Remove and destroy infected plants. Solarize soil. Rotate crops. Use grafted seedlings if available. Viral diseases like tomato yellow leaf curl virus and cucumber mosaic virus have no cure. Prevent them by controlling whiteflies and aphids. Use resistant varieties. Remove infected plants immediately. Do not save seed from infected plants. Now let us talk about root diseases. Damping off affects seedlings. It causes stems to rot at the soil line. Prevent by using sterile media, clean trays, and good airflow. Do not overwater. Pythium and Phytophthora cause root rot in older plants. Improve drainage. Avoid overwatering. Use raised beds. Apply Trichoderma, a beneficial fungus, to the soil. Now let us talk about chemical control. If you must use chemicals, follow these rules. First, identify the pest or disease correctly. Do not guess. Second, choose the right chemical. Read the label. Third, wear protective clothing: gloves, mask, goggles, and long sleeves. Fourth, mix only the recommended dose. More is not better. Fifth, spray in the evening or early morning when bees are not active. Sixth, observe the pre-harvest interval. This is the time between spraying and harvest. Do not harvest before that time. Seventh, store chemicals safely, away from children and food. Eighth, dispose of empty containers properly. Do not reuse them for water or food. Ninth, rotate chemicals with different modes of action to prevent resistance. Tenth, keep records of every spray: date, chemical, dose, and reason. Now let us talk about biological control. Africa has many natural enemies. You can encourage them by planting flowering plants near the greenhouse, such as marigolds and sunflowers. You can also buy beneficial insects. For example, Encarsia formosa controls whiteflies. Phytoseiulus persimilis controls spider mites. Bacillus thuringiensis controls caterpillars. Trichoderma controls soil-borne fungi. These products are available in many African countries. They are safe for humans and the environment. Now let us talk about physical control. Yellow sticky traps catch whiteflies and aphids. Blue sticky traps catch thrips. Pheromone traps catch Tuta absoluta and other moths. Insect netting keeps pests out. Use 40 to 50 mesh. Double doors prevent pests from entering. UV-absorbing plastic can reduce some insects. Mulch can reduce thrips and aphids. Now let us talk about cultural control. Crop rotation breaks pest cycles. Do not grow the same crop in the same soil season after season. Rotate with a different family. For example, after tomatoes, grow lettuce or carrots. After peppers, grow beans or peas. Sanitation removes pest habitats. Pruning improves airflow. Resistant varieties reduce disease. Grafting tomatoes onto resistant rootstocks controls bacterial wilt and nematodes. Now let us talk about monitoring. Walk through your greenhouse at least twice a week. Look at the underside of leaves. Look at flowers and fruits. Use a hand lens. Check sticky traps. Write down what you see. If you see a few pests, act early. Do not wait until they are everywhere. Early action is cheaper and more effective. Now let us talk about the business side. Pests and diseases cost money. You lose yield, you spend on chemicals, and you spend time. A good IPM program reduces costs and increases profit. It also produces safer food for consumers. Many supermarkets and hotels now demand safe food. They may ask about your pesticide use. Keep records. If you can, get certified organic or GlobalG.A.P. These certifications open premium markets. Finally, train your workers. They are your eyes and hands. Teach them to recognize pests and diseases. Teach them to sanitize. Teach them to spray safely. Give them protective clothing. Reward them for good work. In the next lesson, we will cover harvesting, post-harvest handling, and marketing for greenhouse vegetables.

5. Module 12 Lesson 5: Harvesting, Post-Harvest Handling, and Marketing Greenhouse Vegetables

Welcome to the final lesson of Module 12. You have selected a site, prepared the greenhouse, chosen crops, managed water and fertilizer, and controlled pests and diseases. Now comes the payoff: harvesting, post-harvest handling, and marketing. This is where many African greenhouse farmers lose money. They grow good crops but harvest at the wrong time, handle them poorly, or fail to find a good market. This lesson will help you get the best price for your produce. First, understand maturity indices. Each crop has a right time to harvest. Tomatoes for fresh market are harvested when they are mature green to pink, depending on the distance to market. If you sell locally, you can harvest when they are red and ripe. If you transport far, harvest at the breaker stage, when the fruit is starting to turn color. This reduces damage. Cherry tomatoes are harvested when fully red or yellow. Peppers are harvested when they are green or colored, depending on the market. Green bell peppers are immature but firm. Red, yellow, and orange peppers are mature and sweeter. Cucumbers are harvested when they are young and firm, before seeds harden. Lettuce is harvested when the head is firm but before it bolts. Spinach and kale are harvested leaf by leaf or as whole plants. Herbs are harvested before flowering for best flavor. Harvest at the right time of day. Early morning is best because the crop is cool and crisp. Avoid harvesting in the heat of the day. Use clean, sharp tools. Do not pull fruits or tear leaves. This damages the plant and the produce. Use clean crates or baskets. Do not throw produce into a pile. Do not step on it. Now let us talk about post-harvest handling. The goal is to keep the produce cool, clean, and undamaged. After harvest, move the produce to a shaded area immediately. Do not leave it in the sun. Sun causes wilting, sunburn, and weight loss. If you have a cool room, use it. If not, use a shaded shed with good airflow. You can also use evaporative cooling. This is a simple technology where you place wet sacks or pads over a frame and blow air through them. It can reduce temperature by 5 to 10 degrees Celsius. This is very useful in hot African climates. Wash produce only if necessary. Some markets want washed produce. Others prefer unwashed. If you wash, use clean water. Add a small amount of chlorine if allowed. Do not soak produce. Dry it gently. Sort and grade your produce. Remove damaged, diseased, or undersized items. Grade by size, color, and quality. Uniform produce sells for a higher price. Pack in clean, appropriate containers. For tomatoes, use shallow crates so they do not crush. For leafy greens, use ventilated crates. For herbs, use small bunches or bags. Label your produce with your farm name and date. This builds trust. Now let us talk about storage. Most greenhouse vegetables are perishable. Do not store them for long. Sell as soon as possible. If you must store, keep them cool and humid. Tomatoes store best at 12 to 15 degrees Celsius. Peppers at 7 to 10 degrees. Cucumbers at 10 to 12 degrees. Lettuce at 0 to 4 degrees. Herbs at 0 to 4 degrees. Do not store tomatoes with ethylene-producing fruits like bananas or apples. Ethylene speeds ripening and spoilage. Now let us talk about transport. Use a vehicle that protects produce from sun, rain, and dust. Do not overload. Do not stack heavy crates on soft produce. Use a tarpaulin if needed. Plan your route. Avoid rough roads. If you sell to a distant market, consider a refrigerated truck. These are expensive but can be shared with other farmers. In many African cities, there are transport cooperatives. Join one. Now let us talk about marketing. Marketing starts before you plant. You need to know who will buy your produce. There are several market channels. First, local markets. These are the most common. You sell directly to consumers or to market vendors. Prices are often low but you get cash immediately. <p style="margin:0;font-family:Arial,sans-serif;font-size:15px;color:#b71c1c;"><strong> SAFETY WARNING PESTICIDES AND FOOD SAFETY</strong></p>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;font-family:Arial,sans-serif;font-size:14px;color:#333;">Greenhouse workers and consumers are at risk from misuse of agrochemicals. Always follow the label, wear full personal protective equipment (PPE), observe pre-harvest intervals (PHI) strictly, and never use WHO Class Ia or Ib pesticides on greenhouse vegetables. Store chemicals in a locked, ventilated area away from food and water sources. In case of poisoning, contact your local poison control centre or health facility immediately. Reference: WHO Recommended Classification of Pesticides by Hazard (2020); FAO/WHO International Code of Conduct on Pesticide Management (2014).</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist Greenhouse Vegetable Operations</h3>
<ol style="font-family:Arial,sans-serif;font-size:14px;line-height:1.7;">
<li><strong>Site and structure:</strong> Confirm water source within 100 m, slope under 2%, and all-weather road access before construction.</li>
<li><strong>Crop plan:</strong> Match variety to market demand and local disease pressure; stagger planting every 23 weeks for continuous supply.</li>
<li><strong>Irrigation:</strong> Test water EC and pH monthly; maintain drip system pressure at 1.01.5 bar and flush lines weekly.</li>
<li><strong>Fertigation:</strong> Apply nutrients based on soil/leaf analysis, not calendar; target EC 2.03.5 mS/cm for fruiting crops.</li>
<li><strong>IPM:</strong> Scout twice weekly; use yellow/blue sticky traps; introduce beneficials (e.g., <em>Encarsia formosa</em>) before chemical intervention.</li>
<li><strong>Harvest hygiene:</strong> Use clean, sanitised crates; harvest in early morning; pre-cool to 1012C within 2 hours where possible.</li>
<li><strong>Record keeping:</strong> Log all inputs, labour, yields and sales weekly this is the basis for credit access and enterprise improvement.</li>
<li><strong>Market linkage:</strong> Secure at least one forward contract or aggregation agreement before the season begins to reduce price risk.</li>
</ol>
