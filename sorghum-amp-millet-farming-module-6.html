Lessons

1. Rainwater harvesting techniques: tied ridges, contour bunds, and dams

In dryland Africa, every drop of rain counts. Tied ridges are a simple technique: after ploughing, you make ridges 20-30 cm high and 60-90 cm apart, then tie them with cross-ridges every 2-3 meters to form basins. These basins catch rainwater and hold it long enough to soak into the soil. This can increase yields by 30-50% in dry years. Contour bunds are earth banks built along the contour of a slope, spaced 10-20 meters apart. They slow runoff and let water infiltrate. For small dams, you can dig a farm pond 2-3 meters deep, lined with clay or plastic, to store water for supplemental irrigation. In areas with 500-800 mm annual rainfall, a 10x10 meter pond can collect enough water for 1-2 hectares of sorghum during a dry spell. Always dig ponds at the lowest point of your field and keep them fenced from livestock. Maintenance is key: repair ridges and bunds after each season, and desilt ponds before the rains. These methods are labour-intensive but use local materials and can be done with hand tools like hoes and shovels.

2. Mulching to conserve soil moisture

Mulching is covering the soil with crop residues, grass, or leaves to reduce evaporation and keep the soil cool. In sorghum and millet fields, you can use previous season's stalks, groundnut haulms, or even dry grass. Spread a layer 5-10 cm thick between plants and along rows. This can reduce water loss by up to 70% and lower soil temperature by 5-10C, which is critical for root growth. Mulching also suppresses weeds and adds organic matter as it decomposes. Do not use mulch from diseased plants. In dry areas, apply mulch right after the first rains when soil is moist. If you have limited residues, prioritise mulching around the base of each plant. You can also use living mulches like cowpea or lablab, which cover the ground and fix nitrogen. Avoid mulching in very wet conditions as it can harbour pests. A simple test: dig 15 cm into mulched and unmulched soil after a week without rainmulched soil will still be dark and moist. Mulching is cheap, effective, and can be done by any farmer with a hoe and a pile of crop residues.

3. Supplemental irrigation with buckets, drip kits, or treadle pumps

When rains fail, supplemental irrigation can save your crop. For small plots, a bucket and a cup are enough: pour 1-2 litres of water around each plant every 3-4 days during flowering and grain filling. A drip kit is more efficient: a 20-litre bucket with drip lines can irrigate 50-100 plants, delivering water directly to roots with minimal waste. Treadle pumps are foot-operated and can lift water from shallow wells or rivers to irrigate up to 0.5 hectares. They cost $50-$150 and are easy to maintain. Use irrigation only as a supplementdo not overwater, as sorghum and millet are drought-tolerant but can rot in waterlogged soil. Irrigate early morning or evening to reduce evaporation. If using a treadle pump, direct water into furrows between rows. For bucket irrigation, make a small basin around each plant to hold water. Prioritise irrigation during the booting and flowering stages, when water stress causes the greatest yield loss. A single supplemental irrigation of 20-30 mm at flowering can increase yields by 20-30%. Always check soil moisture by squeezing a handfulif it crumbles, it's too dry; if it holds together, it's fine.

4. Recognizing drought stress stages in sorghum and millet

Drought stress shows in stages. Early stress: leaves roll inward and look greyish-green, especially in the afternoon. This is a sign the plant is conserving water. If you see this, act immediately. Moderate stress: leaves turn yellow at the tips, and lower leaves dry out. Growth slows, and the plant looks stunted. Severe stress: leaves become brown and crispy, stems may crack, and the plant wilts permanently. At this stage, yield loss is 50% or more. Critical stages: for sorghum, the booting stage (when the head is forming inside the stem) and flowering are most sensitive. For millet, flowering and grain filling are critical. If drought hits at these stages, you can lose everything. Check your field daily during dry spells. Dig 10 cm into the soilif it's dry, you need to act. Also look for 'leaf firing'brown edges on leaveswhich means the plant is pulling water from leaves to survive. Early detection lets you apply mulch, irrigate, or thin plants before it's too late. Keep a simple record of rainfall and stress symptoms to plan for next season.

5. Emergency measures: reducing plant population during drought

When drought hits and you cannot irrigate, thinning plants can save the crop. Sorghum and millet are naturally tillering, but under severe stress, fewer plants mean each remaining plant gets more water. If you planted 30,000 plants per hectare (spacing 30 cm x 30 cm), during a severe drought you can thin to 15,000 plants per hectare (spacing 60 cm x 30 cm). Do this when plants are 15-20 cm tall, before they compete too much. Pull out the weakest plants, leaving the healthiest. For millet, thin to 10-15 cm between plants. This reduces competition for water and nutrients. Another emergency measure is to remove lower leaves of sorghum to reduce transpirationbut do not remove more than 2-3 leaves per plant. You can also stop all weeding and let weeds die naturally, as they compete for water. If the drought continues, consider harvesting the crop for fodder rather than grain. Thinning is painful but can mean the difference between a total loss and a half harvest. After thinning, apply mulch to conserve the remaining moisture. Record what you did and how the crop responded for future planning.

6. Choosing the most drought-tolerant varieties as backup

Not all sorghum and millet are equal in drought tolerance. For sorghum, choose varieties like 'Macia', 'Seredo', 'Kari Mtama 1', or local landraces such as 'Mugudhi' in Tanzania. These mature in 90-120 days and can survive 3-4 weeks without rain. For millet, 'Okashana 1' (Namibia), 'Kombi' (Zimbabwe), and 'Pennisetum glaucum' landraces like 'Babala' are excellent. Always plant at least two varieties: one early-maturing (75-90 days) and one medium (90-120 days) to spread risk. Early varieties escape drought by finishing before the dry spell, while medium varieties yield more if rains are good. Get certified seed from local agrodealers or research stations. If you cannot afford hybrid seed, save seed from your best drought-tolerant plants. Test new varieties on a small plot first. Also consider 'stay-green' varieties that keep leaves green longer under stress. Plant early with the first rains to maximise moisture. In dry areas, choose varieties with good tillering and deep roots. Ask your extension officer for the best varieties for your area. Having the right variety is your cheapest insurance against drought.

<h2>Practical Reference: Water Management and Drought Survival for Sorghum &amp; Millet</h2>

<p>In the semi-arid belt that runs from Senegal to Sudan and down through the Horn, sorghum and millet are not just crops they are the backbone of household food security and a growing source of income for aggregators, millers, and feed producers. But water is the single biggest constraint on yield. A farmer who loses a sorghum crop to a two-week dry spell in the booting stage does not just lose food; they lose the cash they had planned for school fees, inputs, and loan repayment. This reference module gives agripreneurs and extension agents the practical tools to keep water in the soil, recognise drought stress early, and make decisions that protect both the crop and the business.</p>

<h3>1. Rainwater Harvesting: Tied Ridges, Contour Bunds, and Dams</h3>

<p>In the drylands of West and East Africa, 7080% of rainfall can be lost to runoff and evaporation if the soil surface is left bare. The cheapest way to capture that water is to slow it down and hold it where the roots can reach it.</p>

<p><strong>Tied ridges</strong> are the workhorse of dryland sorghum and millet. A farmer ploughs ridges 75100 cm apart, then ties them across the furrow at intervals of 14 m with a small soil bund. Rain that falls in a 20 mm storm is trapped between the ties instead of running off, giving the crop 23 days of extra moisture. In the Sahel, tied ridging has increased sorghum yields by 2040% compared with flat planting in dry years. The labour cost is real roughly 2030 person-hours per hectare but the return is a crop that survives a dry spell that would otherwise wipe it out.</p>

<p><strong>Contour bunds</strong> (also called contour ridges or stone bunds) work on slopes above 2%. Bunds are laid along the contour at 1530 m intervals, with a small trench on the uphill side. They slow runoff, trap sediment, and build soil depth over time. In Burkina Faso and Niger, stone bunds have restored degraded land to the point where sorghum and millet can be grown again. For a business, this means a more reliable supply of grain from a defined catchment area useful when you are contracting farmers for a mill or a feed plant.</p>

<p><strong>Small dams and farm ponds</strong> are the next step up. A well-sited earth dam or lined pond can capture 5005,000 m of runoff from a 520 hectare catchment. That water can be used for supplemental irrigation of a 0.52 hectare sorghum or millet plot, or for livestock, or for a small vegetable enterprise that carries the household through the dry season. The economics are simple: a 1,000 m pond costing USD 8001,500 can support a 0.5 ha drip-irrigated plot that produces 1.52 tonnes of sorghum in a year when rainfed fields fail. In a market where sorghum sells for USD 200350 per tonne, that is a 24x return in a single dry year.</p>

<h3>2. Mulching to Conserve Soil Moisture</h3>

<p>Every kilogram of dry mulch left on the soil surface can save 12 litres of water that would otherwise evaporate. In the Sahel, where evaporation can exceed 2,000 mm per year, mulching is not optional it is a business decision. A farmer who mulches with 24 tonnes of crop residue per hectare can reduce evaporation by 3050% and increase sorghum grain yield by 1525% in a dry year.</p>

<p>What to use: sorghum and millet stover, maize stover, cowpea haulms, groundnut shells, or dry grass. The best mulch is a mix of cereal and legume residues, because the legume adds nitrogen as it breaks down. Apply after the first weeding, when the crop is 1525 cm tall, and keep a 510 cm gap around the plant stem to avoid termite damage and disease.</p>

<p>For a business, mulching has a second benefit: it reduces the need for supplemental irrigation, which lowers the cost of production. A farmer who mulches may only need 23 supplemental waterings instead of 56. At USD 0.501.00 per m of water pumped, that is a direct saving of USD 3060 per hectare per season money that can be reinvested in seed or fertiliser.</p>

<h3>3. Supplemental Irrigation: Buckets, Drip Kits, and Treadle Pumps</h3>

<p>Supplemental irrigation is not about replacing rain it is about bridging the 1020 day dry spells that hit sorghum and millet at flowering and grain filling. Even 2030 mm of water applied at the right time can double grain yield in a drought year.</p>

<p><strong>Buckets</strong> are the entry point. A farmer with a 20-litre bucket and a 50 m walk to a well can apply 23 litres per plant every 34 days during a dry spell. This is labour-intensive but requires almost no capital. For a 0.25 ha plot with 4,000 plants, that is 812 m per irrigation achievable with 400600 bucket trips.</p>

<p><strong>Drip kits</strong> are the next step. A 500 m kit costs USD 50150 and can be gravity-fed from a 200-litre drum or a small tank. Drip irrigation uses 3050% less water than bucket application and reduces labour by 70%. For a farmer selling sorghum to a miller or a feed company, a drip kit on 0.5 ha can produce 11.5 tonnes of grain in a dry year enough to honour a contract.</p>

<p><strong>Treadle pumps</strong> are the workhorse of smallholder irrigation in East and Southern Africa. A treadle pump costs USD 80200 and can lift 13 m per hour from a shallow well or a river. It can irrigate 0.51 hectare of sorghum or millet with 23 supplemental waterings. The payback period is typically one season if the crop is sold at market price.</p>

<h3>5. Emergency Measures: Reducing Plant Population During Drought</h3>

<p>When a drought hits and there is no water for supplemental irrigation, thinning is a last-resort survival strategy. The logic is simple: fewer plants mean more water per plant. A sorghum field with 40,000 plants per hectare will run out of water faster than one with 20,000 plants.</p>

<p>Practical steps:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Thin to 5060% of normal population</strong> at the vegetative stage if a dry spell is forecast. For sorghum, that means reducing from 40,000 to 20,00024,000 plants per hectare.</li>
<li><strong>Remove the weakest plants first</strong> the ones that are already wilting or stunted. Leave the most vigorous plants, even if they are not evenly spaced.</li>
<li><strong>Do not thin after flowering.</strong> At that stage, the plant has already invested in grain, and thinning will not help.</li>
<li><strong>Use the thinned plants for livestock feed.</strong> This recovers some of the lost value and supports the household's livestock enterprise.</li>
</ul>

<p>Thinning can reduce yield loss from 50% to 2030% in a severe drought, but it is not a substitute for water. It is a damage-control measure. For a business, the decision to thin should be made early waiting until the crop is visibly dying is too late.</p>

<h3>6. Choosing the Most Drought-Tolerant Varieties as Backup</h3>

<p>The cheapest drought insurance is a drought-tolerant variety. In the Sahel and the Horn, varieties such as <em>Sorghum bicolor</em> 'Macia', 'Sariaso', and 'CSM 335' for sorghum, and 'Okashana 1', 'Sosat C88', and 'PMV 1' for millet have been bred for early maturity (7590 days), stay-green traits, and tolerance to mid-season drought.</p>

<p>For a business, the choice of variety is a supply-chain decision. A miller who contracts farmers for sorghum needs a variety with consistent grain quality and a predictable harvest date. A drought-tolerant variety that matures in 85 days instead of 110 days reduces the risk of a failed crop and allows the miller to plan procurement with more confidence.</p>

<h4 style="color: #c0392b; margin-top: 0;"> SAFETY WARNING: Water Handling and Pump Use</h4>
<p style="margin-bottom: 0;">When using treadle pumps, drip kits, or buckets for supplemental irrigation, observe the following safety rules: (1) Never drink untreated water from farm ponds or dams treat with chlorine or boil before drinking. (2) Treadle pumps can cause back strain and foot injuries wear closed shoes and take breaks every 30 minutes. (3) Drip kits and buckets can become breeding grounds for mosquitoes empty and dry containers weekly. (4) Do not use untreated human or animal waste in water used for irrigation of crops that will be eaten raw. (5) If using a petrol or diesel pump, ensure proper ventilation and never refuel a hot engine. (6) Store all water containers away from children and livestock.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Before the rains:</strong> Repair tied ridges and contour bunds. Clear drainage channels. Check that your pond or dam is leak-free.</li>
<li><strong>At planting:</strong> Use a drought-tolerant variety suited to your rainfall zone. Plant at the first effective rain (20 mm or more).</li>
<li><strong>After emergence:</strong> Apply 24 tonnes/ha of mulch between rows. Keep mulch 510 cm away from stems.</li>
<li><strong>At 2030 days:</strong> Thin to the recommended population. If a dry spell is forecast, thin to 5060% of normal.</li>
<li><strong>At flowering:</strong> This is the critical stage. If you have water, apply 2030 mm. If you do not, prepare to thin or accept yield loss.</li>
<li><strong>During drought:</strong> Monitor soil moisture at 15 cm depth. If it is dry for 7 days, act irrigate, thin, or harvest for fodder.</li>
<li><strong>After harvest:</strong> Keep 1020% of stover for mulch. Store grain in hermetic bags or metal silos to protect against pests.</li>
<li><strong>For business:</strong> Contract farmers on drought-tolerant varieties. Offer a drip kit or treadle pump as part of an input package. Pay a premium for consistent quality.</li>
</ol>
