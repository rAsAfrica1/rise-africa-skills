1. Understanding the Small Shop and Market Trading Business

Welcome to Module 1 of Small Shop and Market Trading. This first lesson sets the foundation for everything that follows. Whether you are already running a small shop in Nairobi, a market stall in Lagos, a table in Accra, or a mobile trading business in Kampala, the principles in this module will help you see your business with fresh eyes. Small shop and market trading is one of the most common and most important forms of enterprise across Africa. It employs millions of people, feeds families, funds school fees, and builds communities. Yet many traders operate without a clear understanding of what their business really is, how money moves through it, and what separates those who grow from those who stay stuck. This lesson will change that. We will define the business, explore its role in African economies, identify the different types of small shops and market traders, and help you locate yourself within that picture. By the end, you should be able to describe your own business in clear, practical terms and understand why this matters for your profit. Let us begin with a simple question: what is a small shop and market trading business? At its core, it is any business that buys goods and sells them to customers, usually in small quantities, from a fixed location like a shop or stall, or from a mobile setup like a bicycle, cart, or head pan. The key activities are buying, storing, displaying, pricing, selling, and managing cash. The key assets are your stock, your location, your relationships, and your reputation. The key challenge is making sure that the money you collect is more than the money you spend, after accounting for everything. Many traders think their business is just about selling. But selling is only one part. If you buy a carton of soap for 10,000 shillings and sell it for 12,000, you might feel you have made 2,000 profit. But if you paid 500 for transport, 300 for a market fee, and gave 200 to a helper, your real profit is 1,000. And if one bar of soap was stolen or damaged, your profit drops further. This is why understanding the full picture matters. In Africa, small shops and market trading take many forms. There is the kiosk or container shop selling airtime, sweets, and household items. There is the market stall selling fresh produce, fish, or meat. There is the clothing and fabric trader. There is the hardware shop, the pharmacy, the grain seller, the mobile phone accessory vendor, and the cooked food seller. Some traders operate from a single table. Others have a small room. Some move from market to market on different days. Some sell online through WhatsApp and deliver by boda boda. Each type has its own rhythm, its own risks, and its own opportunities. But they all share the same basic money logic: buy low, sell higher, control costs, and keep cash moving. Why is this business so important in Africa? First, it provides livelihoods where formal jobs are scarce. Second, it brings goods and services close to people, often in areas that large supermarkets and chain stores do not reach. Third, it is a training ground for entrepreneurship. Many successful business owners in Africa started with a small shop or market table. Fourth, it keeps money circulating in local communities. When you buy from a local farmer and sell to local customers, you are part of a chain that supports many families. Understanding this bigger picture can give you pride in your work and help you see your business as a real enterprise, not just a way to survive. Now let us look at the different roles you play as a small shop or market trader. You are a buyer. You must choose what to stock, where to buy, and how much to pay. You are a seller. You must attract customers, set prices, and close sales. You are a cash manager. You must collect money, keep records, and decide what to reinvest. You are a storekeeper. You must protect your stock from theft, damage, and expiry. You are a marketer. You must let people know what you have and why they should buy from you. You are a problem solver. You must handle difficult customers, supply shortages, price changes, and competition. And you are a planner. You must think about tomorrow, next week, and next season. Many traders focus only on selling and neglect the other roles. That is like trying to cook with only one ingredient. The more roles you handle well, the stronger your business becomes. Let us talk about the money cycle in a small shop or market trading business. Money starts as cash in your hand or mobile money account. You use it to buy stock. That stock sits in your shop or stall. Customers buy the stock and give you cash or mobile money. You now have more cash than before, if you priced correctly. You use some of that cash to restock, some to pay expenses, some to pay yourself, and some to save or invest. The cycle repeats. The faster the cycle, the more profit you can make in a month. If your money is stuck in slow-moving stock, the cycle slows down. If customers owe you money, the cycle slows down. If you spend all your cash on personal needs, the cycle stops. Understanding this cycle helps you make better decisions. For example, if you sell tomatoes and they spoil quickly, you need a fast cycle. If you sell hardware, your cycle may be slower but your margins may be higher. You must match your money cycle to the type of goods you sell. Another important concept is the difference between profit and cash. Profit is what is left after all costs. Cash is the money you actually have available. A trader can be profitable on paper but have no cash because customers are buying on credit or because money is tied up in stock. In Africa, where credit is common and cash flow can be tight, this distinction is critical. You must always know how much cash you have, how much you owe, and how much customers owe you. Without this knowledge, you may make sales but still fail to pay your suppliers or your rent. Let us also consider the customer. In small shop and market trading, customers are not just people who give you money. They are your partners in business. They decide whether your business grows or dies. They talk to their friends and neighbours. They come back if you treat them well. They disappear if you cheat them, ignore them, or run out of what they need. In many African markets, relationships matter as much as prices. A customer may pay a little more at a shop where the owner greets them warmly, remembers their name, and offers small credit when they are short. Building customer loyalty is cheaper than finding new customers. So from day one, treat every customer as someone you want to see again. Now let us think about location. In this business, location is not just a place. It is a strategic asset. A shop near a busy bus stage, a market stall near the entrance, a table near a school gate, or a mobile cart that follows events and crowds can all be good locations. But location must match your goods and your customers. Selling cold drinks near a construction site may work well. Selling school uniforms near a school may work well. Selling expensive cosmetics in a poor neighbourhood may not. You must study foot traffic, customer needs, competition, and security. A good location can double your sales. A bad location can drain your money through rent and slow stock movement. Do not choose a location only because it is cheap. Choose it because it puts you close to the people who will buy from you. Another key idea is stock selection. What you sell determines how much profit you make and how fast your money moves. Some goods are fast-moving but low-margin, like sugar, salt, and airtime. Some are slow-moving but high-margin, like shoes, bags, and electronics. Some are perishable, like milk, bread, and vegetables. Some are seasonal, like umbrellas, school supplies, and festive items. A smart trader balances these categories. You need fast-moving goods to bring in daily cash. You need high-margin goods to build profit. You need seasonal goods to capture special opportunities. And you need to avoid too much of any one thing, because if it does not sell, your money is trapped. In Africa, supply chains can be unpredictable. Prices change. Transport breaks down. Goods run out. A good trader always has a backup supplier and always asks about price changes before buying in bulk. Let us talk about competition. In many African markets, competition is fierce. Several traders may sell the same goods in the same area. How do you survive? You survive by being different in ways that matter to customers. Maybe you open earlier and close later. Maybe you offer credit to trusted customers. Maybe you package goods in smaller, affordable quantities. Maybe you deliver. Maybe you keep your shop cleaner and more organised. Maybe you specialise in a product that others do not stock. Maybe you build a reputation for fair prices and honest measures. Competition is not just about price. It is about value, convenience, trust, and relationships. If you compete only on price, you may win customers but lose profit. If you compete on value, you can win both. Now let us discuss the mindset of a successful small shop or market trader. First, you must see yourself as a business owner, not just a seller. That means keeping records, planning, and investing. Second, you must be disciplined. You must separate business money from personal money. You must resist the temptation to eat your stock or spend your capital. Third, you must be patient. Growth takes time. You may start small, but with consistent effort, you can expand. Fourth, you must be adaptable. Markets change. Customers change. Prices change. You must be willing to change your products, your location, or your methods. Fifth, you must be honest. Cheating customers may bring quick money, but it destroys your reputation and your future. In small communities, word travels fast. A good name is one of your most valuable assets. Let us also address the role of record keeping. Many traders avoid records because they think it is too much work or because they fear seeing the truth. But records are your business eyes. Without them, you are guessing. With them, you can see what sells, what does not, what you spend, what you earn, and where you can improve. You do not need a computer or an accountant. A simple exercise book can work. Write down daily sales, daily expenses, stock purchases, and money taken for personal use. At the end of the week, review your numbers. At the end of the month, calculate your profit. This simple habit can transform your business. It helps you stop leaks, spot opportunities, and make better decisions. Finally, let us think about the future. Small shop and market trading can be a stepping stone to something bigger. Many large businesses in Africa started as small market stalls. With discipline, savings, and smart reinvestment, you can grow from a table to a shop, from one shop to two, from selling one product to selling many, from local sales to online sales, from a sole trader to an employer. The skills you learn in this business, buying, selling, negotiating, managing cash, handling customers, and solving problems, are the same skills that build empires. So take this business seriously. It is not a small thing. It is a real enterprise with real potential. In this lesson, we have defined the small shop and market trading business, explored its importance in Africa, identified different types, explained the money cycle, and introduced key ideas like profit versus cash, customer relationships, location, stock selection, competition, mindset, record keeping, and future growth. In the next lesson, we will look at how to choose the right products to sell and how to find reliable suppliers. But before you move on, take a moment to write down three things: what type of small shop or market trading business you run or want to run, what your biggest challenge is right now, and what you hope to learn from this module. Keep these notes. They will guide your learning and help you apply what you discover. Remember, every big business starts small. What matters is that you start smart, stay disciplined, and keep learning. Your shop or stall is not just a place to sell. It is a classroom, a laboratory, and a launchpad. Use it well.

2. Choosing Products and Finding Reliable Suppliers

In the previous lesson, we looked at the big picture of small shop and market trading. Now we get practical. The products you choose and the suppliers you buy from will determine whether your business makes money or loses it. This lesson will help you select the right products for your market and build strong relationships with suppliers. In Africa, where supply chains can be unpredictable and competition is fierce, these skills are essential. Let us start with product selection. What should you sell? The answer depends on several factors: your customers, your location, your capital, your skills, and your goals. There is no single best product. What works in one market may fail in another. But there are principles you can follow. First, know your customers. Who are they? What do they need? What can they afford? What do they buy regularly? What do they complain about not finding? If you sell in a residential area, your customers may need daily household items like soap, salt, cooking oil, matches, and bread. If you sell near a bus stage, they may need snacks, drinks, airtime, and newspapers. If you sell near a school, they may need exercise books, pens, and uniforms. If you sell in a market, they may need fresh produce, meat, fish, or spices. The more you understand your customers, the better you can choose products that sell. Second, consider demand and frequency. Some products are bought every day, like milk, bread, and vegetables. These bring in regular cash. Some are bought occasionally, like shoes, bags, and kitchenware. These may have higher margins but slower turnover. A good strategy is to combine fast-moving and slow-moving products. The fast movers keep cash flowing. The slow movers build profit. But be careful not to tie up too much money in slow movers. If a product sits on your shelf for months, it is not an asset. It is a trap. Third, think about perishability. Fresh produce, dairy, and cooked food spoil quickly. They require fast sales, good storage, and careful handling. If you cannot sell them quickly, you may lose money. Non-perishable goods like soap, salt, and canned food last longer and are easier to manage. If you are new to trading, start with non-perishables and add perishables as you gain experience. Fourth, consider your capital. Some products require a large upfront investment, like electronics, hardware, or bulk grain. Others require very little, like sweets, airtime, or single cigarettes. Start with what you can afford. It is better to sell a small amount of a product that sells fast than to sell a large amount of a product that sits. As your capital grows, you can expand your range. Fifth, think about competition. If ten traders around you are selling the same thing, you will struggle to stand out. Look for gaps. Maybe no one is selling fresh fruit. Maybe no one is selling phone chargers. Maybe no one is selling affordable school bags. You do not have to be completely unique. You just need a reason for customers to choose you. That reason could be a product others do not have, a better price, better quality, or better service. Sixth, consider seasonality. Some products sell more at certain times of the year. Umbrellas and raincoats sell during rainy season. School supplies sell before school opens. Festive items sell during holidays. Agricultural inputs sell during planting season. If you plan ahead, you can stock these products before demand peaks and make good profits. But you must also know when demand will fall, so you do not get stuck with unsold stock. Seventh, test before you invest heavily. If you are not sure whether a product will sell, buy a small quantity first. See how customers respond. If it sells well, buy more. If it does not, try something else. This is called testing the market. It reduces your risk and helps you learn what works. Many traders lose money because they buy large quantities of a new product without testing. Do not make that mistake. Now let us talk about suppliers. Your supplier is your business partner. A good supplier helps you succeed. A bad supplier causes you stress, losses, and missed sales. How do you find good suppliers? First, look around your local market. Many suppliers are wholesalers or distributors who sell to traders. Ask other traders where they buy. Visit the wholesale market. Compare prices, quality, and reliability. Do not just go with the first supplier you find. Take time to compare. Second, consider going directly to producers. If you sell fresh produce, you can buy from farmers. If you sell crafts, you can buy from artisans. If you sell processed food, you can buy from small factories. Buying directly can reduce costs and give you fresher or more unique products. But it may require more effort and travel. Third, use technology. In many African countries, you can find suppliers through online platforms, WhatsApp groups, and social media. Some suppliers deliver. Some allow you to order and pay by mobile money. This can save time and transport costs. But be careful. Not all online suppliers are reliable. Check reviews, ask for references, and start with small orders. Fourth, attend trade fairs and exhibitions. These events bring together many suppliers in one place. You can see products, compare prices, and meet suppliers face to face. They are also good places to learn about new products and trends. Now let us discuss how to evaluate a supplier. There are several factors to consider. Price is important, but it is not the only factor. A supplier with the lowest price may have poor quality, unreliable delivery, or bad customer service. Look at the total cost, including transport, delays, and losses. Quality is critical. If you sell poor-quality goods, customers will not come back. Always check the quality of what you buy. If possible, inspect the goods before paying. Reliability matters. Can the supplier deliver on time? Do they have stock when you need it? Do they communicate clearly? A reliable supplier helps you avoid stockouts and keep customers happy. Credit terms are also important. Some suppliers allow you to buy now and pay later. This can help your cash flow, especially if you are waiting for sales. But do not abuse credit. Pay on time to maintain a good relationship. Finally, consider the supplier's reputation. Ask other traders about them. A supplier with a good reputation is likely to treat you fairly. A supplier with a bad reputation may cheat you or disappear. Once you find good suppliers, build strong relationships with them. How? Pay on time. Communicate clearly. Give them feedback on what sells and what does not. Order consistently. Do not switch suppliers for small price differences. Loyalty can earn you better prices, priority during shortages, and credit when you need it. Treat your supplier as a partner, not an adversary. Negotiation is a key skill in supplier relationships. Many traders accept the first price they are given. But in most African markets, prices are negotiable. How do you negotiate well? First, know the market price. Before you negotiate, find out what others are paying. This gives you a reference point. Second, buy in larger quantities if you can. Suppliers often give discounts for bulk purchases. But do not buy more than you can sell. Third, build a relationship before you ask for a discount. Suppliers are more likely to help loyal customers. Fourth, be polite but firm. Do not insult the supplier or threaten to leave. Explain why you need a better price. For example, you can say that you are a regular customer and that a lower price will help you sell more, which means you will buy more from them. Fifth, be willing to walk away. If the price is too high, you can politely say you will think about it and check other suppliers. Sometimes this leads to a better offer. But do not walk away if the price is fair. Remember, negotiation is about finding a deal that works for both sides. Now let us talk about managing your stock. Once you have products and suppliers, you need to manage your inventory. Poor stock management is one of the biggest causes of failure in small shops and market trading. There are two main problems: overstocking and understocking. Overstocking means you have too much of a product. Your money is tied up. The product may expire, get damaged, or go out of fashion. Understocking means you have too little. Customers leave empty-handed. You lose sales and may lose customers. How do you find the right balance? First, track your sales. Know how much of each product you sell per day or per week. This helps you predict how much you need. Second, set reorder levels. This is the point at which you must order more stock. For example, if you sell ten loaves of bread a day and it takes two days for delivery, you should reorder when you have twenty loaves left. Third, keep records. Write down what you buy, what you sell, and what you have left. This helps you spot patterns and avoid mistakes. Fourth, review your stock regularly. At least once a week, check your shelves. Identify slow-moving items. Consider discounting them to free up cash. Identify fast-moving items. Make sure you never run out. Fifth, protect your stock. Theft, damage, and expiry are silent killers. Keep your shop secure. Store goods properly. Rotate stock so older items are sold first. If you sell perishables, check them daily and remove spoiled items immediately. Let us also discuss pricing. The price you set affects your sales and your profit. If you price too high, customers will go elsewhere. If you price too low, you may sell a lot but make little profit. How do you set the right price? First, know your costs. Add up the cost of the product, transport, packaging, and any other direct costs. This is your break-even point. You must sell above this to make a profit. Second, know your market. What are other traders charging? What are customers willing to pay? Third, consider your target profit margin. A common approach is to mark up your cost by a certain percentage. For example, if you buy an item for 1,000 shillings and want a 20 percent profit, you sell it for 1,200. But this is not a fixed rule. Some products have higher margins, some lower. Fourth, think about value. If you offer better quality, better service, or more convenience, you can charge a little more. Customers will pay for value. Fifth, be willing to adjust. Prices are not permanent. If a product is not selling, you may need to lower the price. If demand is high, you may be able to raise it slightly. But do not change prices too often. Customers like consistency. Finally, let us talk about building a product strategy. A product strategy is a plan for what you sell and why. It includes your product mix, your pricing, your suppliers, and your target customers. A good product strategy helps you stand out and grow. To build one, ask yourself: What is my main product category? What are my secondary products? Who are my customers? What do they need that I can provide? How do I compare to competitors? What is my unique advantage? How will I keep my shelves stocked? How will I handle slow-moving items? How will I introduce new products? Write down your answers. Review them regularly. Adjust as needed. In this lesson, we have covered product selection, supplier evaluation, negotiation, stock management, pricing, and product strategy. These are the building blocks of a successful trading business. In the next lesson, we will focus on money management: how to track cash, calculate profit, and separate business and personal finances. But before you move on, take action. Look at your current products. Which ones sell fast? Which ones sit? Who are your suppliers? Are they reliable? What is one change you can make this week to improve your product mix or supplier relationships? Write it down and do it. Small improvements lead to big results.

3. Money Management for Small Traders

Money is the lifeblood of your small shop or market trading business. If you manage it well, your business grows. If you manage it poorly, your business struggles, no matter how hard you work. This lesson is about money management. We will cover how to track cash, calculate profit, separate business and personal money, manage credit, and build savings. These skills are especially important in Africa, where cash flow can be unpredictable and where many traders mix business and personal funds without realising the damage it causes. Let us start with the basics: tracking cash. Do you know how much money you have right now? Not just in your pocket, but in your mobile money account, in your cash box, and with customers who owe you. Do you know how much you owe suppliers? Do you know how much you spent yesterday and how much you earned? Many traders cannot answer these questions. They operate on guesswork. That is dangerous. You cannot manage what you do not measure. Tracking cash means writing down every shilling, cedi, naira, or rand that comes in and goes out. It does not have to be complicated. A simple notebook can work. Create columns for date, description, money in, money out, and balance. Every time you sell something, write it down. Every time you buy stock, pay rent, or take money for personal use, write it down. At the end of the day, count your cash and compare it to your records. If there is a difference, investigate. Maybe you forgot to record a sale. Maybe you gave change incorrectly. Maybe money was stolen. Finding the difference helps you fix problems early. Why is tracking cash so important? First, it tells you whether you are making a profit or a loss. Second, it helps you plan. If you know how much cash you have, you can decide whether to restock, save, or pay a bill. Third, it reduces theft and waste. When people know you keep records, they are less likely to steal. Fourth, it helps you qualify for loans or credit. Lenders want to see records. Fifth, it gives you peace of mind. You know where you stand. Now let us talk about profit. Profit is what is left after you subtract all your costs from your sales. There are two types of profit: gross profit and net profit. Gross profit is sales minus the cost of the goods you sold. For example, if you bought a bag of rice for 50,000 and sold it for 60,000, your gross profit is 10,000. Net profit is gross profit minus all your other expenses, like rent, transport, electricity, airtime, and wages. If your expenses for that period were 4,000, your net profit is 6,000. Net profit is the true measure of your business success. Many traders confuse sales with profit. They see a lot of money coming in and think they are doing well. But if their expenses are high, they may be losing money. For example, a trader may sell 100,000 worth of goods in a day but spend 95,000 on stock, transport, and rent. The net profit is only 5,000. If the trader spends that 5,000 on personal items, the business has no money to restock. This is why calculating profit is essential. How do you calculate profit accurately? First, record all sales. Second, record the cost of all goods sold. This is not the cost of all goods you bought, but the cost of the goods you actually sold. If you bought 100 items and sold 60, the cost of goods sold is the cost of those 60 items. Third, record all expenses. This includes rent, transport, packaging, airtime, electricity, water, wages, and any other business costs. Fourth, subtract cost of goods sold and expenses from sales. The result is your net profit. Do this weekly or monthly. Use a simple format. Write it down. Review it. If your profit is low, look for ways to increase sales or reduce costs. If your profit is high, consider reinvesting some of it to grow. Now let us discuss the separation of business and personal money. This is one of the biggest challenges for small traders in Africa. Many traders use the same pocket, the same mobile money account, or the same cash box for both business and personal money. This makes it impossible to know whether the business is profitable. It also leads to a common problem: the business funds personal life, and personal life drains the business. How do you separate business and personal money? First, open a separate bank account or mobile money account for your business. If you cannot open a bank account, use a separate wallet or cash box. Second, pay yourself a salary. Decide on a fixed amount to take from the business each week or month. This is your personal income. Do not take more. If you need more, you must find a way to increase business profit, not just take more cash. Third, record all personal withdrawals as business expenses. This helps you see how much the business is supporting you. Fourth, keep business records separate from personal records. Do not mix receipts or notes. Fifth, be disciplined. It is tempting to take money from the till for personal needs. But every time you do, you reduce your ability to restock and grow. Think of the business as a separate person. You would not steal from a friend. Do not steal from your business. Let us talk about managing credit. In many African markets, credit is a normal part of business. Customers buy on credit and pay later. Suppliers give you goods on credit and expect payment later. Credit can help you sell more and manage cash flow. But it can also destroy your business if not managed carefully. There are two sides to credit: credit you give and credit you receive. Let us start with credit you give to customers. Many traders give credit to build loyalty. But not all customers are trustworthy. Some pay late. Some never pay. How do you protect yourself? First, have a clear credit policy. Decide who qualifies for credit and how much. Do not give credit to strangers or to people who have not paid before. Second, keep a credit book. Write down the customer name, date, amount, and items taken. Ask the customer to sign or confirm. Third, set a payment date. Tell the customer when you expect payment. Follow up politely but firmly. Fourth, set a limit. Do not let a customer owe you more than you can afford to lose. Fifth, stop giving credit to customers who do not pay. It is better to lose a bad customer than to lose your business. Now let us talk about credit you receive from suppliers. This can be a powerful tool. If a supplier gives you goods on credit, you can sell them and pay later. This improves your cash flow. But you must manage it carefully. First, only take credit you can repay. Do not overorder just because you are not paying immediately. Second, keep track of what you owe. Write down the supplier name, amount, and due date. Third, pay on time. Late payment damages your relationship and may lead to higher prices or no credit in the future. Fourth, communicate. If you cannot pay on time, talk to the supplier before the due date. Explain the situation and ask for more time. Most suppliers appreciate honesty. Fifth, do not take credit from too many suppliers at once. It can become overwhelming. Now let us discuss savings. Savings are the foundation of growth. Without savings, you cannot take advantage of opportunities, handle emergencies, or expand your business. Many traders save informally, through savings groups, known as chamas, susus, or stokvels. These are excellent. They provide discipline, support, and access to lump sums. But you should also save within your business. How? First, set a savings goal. Decide how much you want to save each week or month. Even a small amount helps. Second, pay yourself first. When you make a sale, set aside a portion for savings before you spend on anything else. Third, keep savings separate. Do not mix savings with your daily cash. Put it in a bank, mobile money savings account, or a locked box. Fourth, use savings for investment, not consumption. Use it to buy more stock, improve your shop, or buy equipment. Fifth, review your savings regularly. Celebrate your progress. Increase your savings as your profit grows. Let us also talk about budgeting. A budget is a plan for how you will spend your money. It helps you avoid impulse purchases and ensure you have enough for important expenses. How do you create a budget? First, estimate your income. How much do you expect to sell this week or month? Be realistic. Second, list your fixed expenses. These are costs that do not change much, like rent, airtime, and transport. Third, list your variable expenses. These are costs that change, like stock purchases and packaging. Fourth, subtract expenses from income. The result is your expected profit. Fifth, decide how to use that profit. <h2>Practical Reference: Daily Shop Operations, Stock Records and Safe Handling</h2>

<p>This practical reference lesson supports the module <strong>Small Shop and Market Trading</strong>. It is written for shopkeepers, market traders, kiosk owners and small agri-input retailers across Africa who want to reduce losses, keep customers, and make better buying decisions. The lesson focuses on three business-critical routines: daily stock control, simple record keeping, and safe handling of food and agricultural chemicals. Each routine is practical, low-cost, and can be started with a notebook, a pen, and a mobile phone.</p>

<p>In small retail, profit is often lost quietly: stock is stolen or expires, cash is spent without being recorded, and customers leave because shelves are empty or products are unsafe. Research from FAO and ILRI shows that post-harvest and retail losses in African food systems remain high, often 2040% for perishable products, and that better handling and record keeping can significantly reduce these losses. For a small shop, a 10% reduction in spoilage can be the difference between a struggling business and a profitable one.</p>

<h3>1. Daily Stock Control: The Core of Shop Profit</h3>

<p>Stock control means knowing what you have, what is selling, what is not selling, and what is missing. In many small shops, the owner keeps stock "in the head", but this fails as soon as the business grows or when more than one person serves customers. A simple stock card or notebook page for each product is enough to start.</p>

<p>Use the <strong>opening stock + purchases closing stock = sales</strong> formula. If the calculated sales do not match the cash received, investigate immediately. This is how you detect theft, recording errors, or spoilage. For perishable goods such as tomatoes, milk, bread, and fresh vegetables, count stock twice daily: morning and evening. For dry goods such as rice, sugar, soap, and cooking oil, count once daily or every two days.</p>

<h3>2. Simple Record Keeping That Works</h3>

<p>Record keeping is not accounting for its own sake. It is a business tool. Three records are enough for most small shops:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Daily sales sheet:</strong> date, items sold, quantity, price, total cash received.</li>
<li><strong>Stock card:</strong> product name, opening stock, purchases, sales, closing stock, losses.</li>
<li><strong>Cash book:</strong> money in, money out, reason, balance.</li>
</ul>

<p>At the end of each day, spend 1015 minutes filling these records. At the end of each week, calculate your gross profit: sales minus cost of goods sold. At the end of each month, review which products made the most profit per unit and per shelf space. This is how you decide what to buy more of and what to drop.</p>

<h4 style="color:#b71c1c;margin-top:0"> SAFETY WARNING: Food, Chemicals and Health</h4>
<p style="color:#b71c1c;margin-bottom:8px"><strong>Never store agricultural chemicals, pesticides, or veterinary medicines near food, drinks, cooking oil, or animal feed.</strong> Keep them in a locked cabinet or separate room, clearly labelled, and out of reach of children and customers. Always read the label before selling or advising on any chemical product.</p>
<p style="color:#b71c1c;margin-bottom:8px">For food handling: wash hands with soap before touching food, keep raw and cooked food separate, cover food to protect it from flies and dust, and use clean water. Do not sell expired, swollen, or damaged canned goods. Do not sell meat or milk that has been unrefrigerated for long periods.</p>
<p style="color:#b71c1c;margin-bottom:0">If a customer or worker shows signs of poisoning (vomiting, dizziness, difficulty breathing) after contact with a chemical, seek medical help immediately and contact your local health facility or poison control centre. In Kenya, the Kenya Poisons and Toxicology Centre can be reached through the Ministry of Health emergency line. In Nigeria, contact the Nigeria Centre for Disease Control (NCDC) emergency line.</p>
</div>

<h3>3. Safe Handling of Food and Agricultural Inputs</h3>

<p>Many small shops sell both food and agricultural inputs. This creates a serious health risk if products are not separated. Pesticides, herbicides, and veterinary drugs can contaminate food through spills, fumes, or shared storage. WHO and FAO guidelines are clear: agricultural chemicals must be stored separately from food, and traders must be able to read and explain label instructions.</p>

<p>For food products, follow the <strong>FIFO rule</strong> First In, First Out. Place older stock in front and new stock behind. Check expiry dates every morning. For fresh produce, use clean crates, avoid stacking heavy items on soft items, and keep produce out of direct sun. For meat and dairy, if you do not have refrigeration, buy only what you can sell within a few hours and source from inspected suppliers.</p>

<p>For agricultural inputs, keep a separate sales record showing the name of the buyer, the product, the quantity, and the date. This helps you track any complaints and protects you if a customer misuses a product. Never decant pesticides into unlabelled bottles, and never sell a chemical to a customer who cannot read the label without explaining the safe use in the local language.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ul>
<li>Count opening stock every morning and closing stock every evening.</li>
<li>Record every sale, even small ones, in the daily sales sheet.</li>
<li>Check expiry dates on all packaged foods and chemicals daily.</li>
<li>Apply FIFO: old stock in front, new stock behind.</li>
<li>Keep food and agricultural chemicals in separate, clearly labelled areas.</li>
<li>Wash hands and clean surfaces before handling food.</li>
<li>Review weekly profit and identify your top three and bottom three products.</li>
<li>Lock away cash, high-value stock, and all chemicals at closing time.</li>
</ul>
