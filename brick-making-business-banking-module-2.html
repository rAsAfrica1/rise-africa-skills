2. Materials and Mixing

Cement, sand, water. Three simple inputs that determine 80% of your quality and 90% of your cost. This module shows you how to test each one, how to mix them correctly, and the mistakes that ruin batches.

Module 2 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~50 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 2.1 — Cement — types, freshness, quality test

Cement typeBest forNotes
OPC (Ordinary Portland)General blocksStandard, widely available
PPC (Portland Pozzolana)Humid climatesCheaper, more durable in moisture
Rapid-hardeningFast productionHigher cost, faster cure
Sulphate-resistantCoastal, soil-sulphate areasEssential in some regions

Freshness matters more than brand. Cement sitting 6+ months in a warehouse absorbs moisture and weakens.

Signs of stale cement:

  • Lumps in the bag (fresh cement flows freely)
  • Hard crust on top of the bag
  • Yellowish tint instead of grey-green
  • Weak smell (fresh cement has a distinct earthy odour)

The ball test: Take a handful, add a little water, mould into a ball. Leave 24 hours. Good cement forms a ball that is hard and does not crumble when squeezed. If it stays soft or cracks, it is stale.

Freshness rule: Buy from high-turnover suppliers. Never buy cement more than 3 months past its packaging date.
Practical exercise 2.1: Buy 1 bag from each of 3 suppliers. Run the ball test on all three. Compare price, freshness, quality. That is your cement supplier for the next 12 months.

Lesson 2.2 — Sand — types and sourcing

Sand typeBest forNotes
River sandAll brick makingRounded grains, easy to work
Pit sandStrong blocksAngular grains, sharper edges
Sea sandNot recommendedSalt causes efflorescence and long-term weakness
Manufactured sandModern blocksCrushed stone, uniform grain

Quality checks: rub a handful — should not leave dirt or clay on your palms. Well-graded (mix of coarse and fine). No salty taste. No visible plant matter or earth smell.

Sourcing: sand pits, river deposits, quarry operations. Transport cost usually dominates. Source within 10 km of your yard.

Practical exercise 2.2: Get 3 quotes for sand delivered to your yard. Ask if washed or unwashed. Test a small sample of each. Choose quality first, price second.

Lesson 2.3 — Water — quality and quantity

Water requirements:

  • Clean — no visible dirt or plant matter
  • Not salty — sea or brackish water weakens cement
  • Not acidic — very low pH disrupts curing
  • Not soapy — detergent breaks cement bonds

Quantity per 100 blocks: ~65 litres for mixing, plus 400–600 litres for curing over 7 days.

Production levelDaily water (mixing + curing)
200 blocks/day~1,000 litres
500 blocks/day~2,500 litres
1,000 blocks/day~5,000 litres

Plan for storage: a 5,000-litre tank or a small dam. Water shortage stops production entirely.

Water rule: Build water storage before you buy more moulds. Moulds are useless without water.
Practical exercise 2.3: Measure your water source output (litres per hour). Calculate your daily requirement. Plan storage for at least one full day of production.

Lesson 2.4 — Mix ratios

Standard for cement blocks: 1 part cement to 6 parts sand by volume.

RatioStrengthBest forCement per 100 blocks
1 : 4Very highStructural, load-bearing3.5 bags
1 : 5HighStandard walls2.8 bags
1 : 6StandardGeneral purpose2.4 bags
1 : 7EconomyNon-load-bearing2.0 bags
1 : 8WeakNot recommended1.8 bags

Water-to-cement ratio: 0.4–0.5 litres of water per kg of cement. The mix should hold its shape when squeezed but not drip.

Never go below 1:6 for customer-facing blocks. The 3–4% saved on cement per block is not worth the reputation damage when the blocks fail. If you want to save, economise on sand sourcing — not on cement.
Practical exercise 2.4: Make 20 blocks at 1:5, 1:6 and 1:7. Cure for 28 days. Drop each from waist height onto concrete. Learn what quality feels like by hand.

Lesson 2.5 — Mixing methods

MethodOutput per hourConsistencyCost
Hand mixing (shovels)100–200 blocksVariableFree
Small drum mixer400–600 blocksConsistent$500–$1,200
Pan mixer800–1,500 blocksVery consistent$1,500–$4,000
Batching plant5,000+ blocksExcellent$20,000+

Hand mixing technique:

  1. Level, clean hard surface
  2. Spread sand 15 cm thick
  3. Spread cement evenly on top
  4. Turn the pile over twice with shovels until colour is uniform
  5. Form a well in the centre, add water gradually from the edges
  6. Target consistency: forms a ball when squeezed, no drips
Mixing signal: Uniform colour means uniform mix. If you see grey or tan patches, keep mixing.
Practical exercise 2.5: Time yourself mixing 20 blocks' worth by hand. Multiply by your daily target. If hand mixing takes 3× longer than expected, plan for a mixer.

Lesson 2.6 — Common material mistakes

MistakeConsequencePrevention
Stale cementWeak, crumbling blocksBall test, high-turnover supplier
Dirty or salty sandWeak blocks, efflorescenceWash, avoid sea sand
Wrong mix ratioWeak or over-expensive blocksStandard 1:6, measure by volume
Too much waterWeak, cracked blocks0.4–0.5 w/c ratio
Inconsistent mixingVariable quality in one batchMix until uniform colour
Reusing set mixWeak, lumpy blocksDiscard partial set mix, do not add water
Never add water to a partially set mix. Adding water does not revive curing cement — it produces weak blocks that crumble. Throw it away and start fresh.
