1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Metal basketry is the craft of weaving, bending, and joining metal wires, rods, and strips into containers that look like traditional baskets but are far stronger. In Africa, baskets have always been essential for carrying, storing, and selling goods. By making them from metal, you create products that last for decades, resist insects, and can be used for heavy loads or even cooking. This skill lets you turn cheap fencing wire or scrap into high-value items for homes, hotels, and markets.

Why does this matter for your business? Tourists and urban customers pay well for unique, handmade metal baskets. A simple wire basket that takes two hours to make can sell for 5,000 to 15,000 Naira (or equivalent) depending on size and finish. You can also make custom orders for restaurants, gift shops, and export markets. Unlike wooden baskets, metal baskets can be painted, galvanised, or left rustic. They are also fire-resistant and waterproof, making them ideal for outdoor use.

You do not need expensive machines. A pair of pliers, a hammer, a hacksaw, and a few jigs are enough to start. Many successful metal basket makers in Kenya, Ghana, and South Africa began by recycling scrap wire from construction sites. The key is to learn the basic weaves and then develop your own style. This module will teach you the exact steps to make a round, sturdy basket from galvanised wire.

By the end of this module, you will be able to select the right wire, build a simple jig, weave a basket base and sides, and finish it safely. You will also learn how to avoid common mistakes that ruin baskets and how to price your work. Metal basketry is a low-cost, high-demand skill that can create jobs for you and your community. Let's get started.

2. Tools and Materials

To make metal baskets, you need a few basic tools. First, a pair of heavy-duty lineman's pliers for cutting and twisting wire. Second, a needle-nose pliers for tight spaces. Third, a ball-peen hammer for flattening and shaping. Fourth, a hacksaw or bolt cutters for thicker rods (6mm and above). Fifth, a measuring tape and a marker. Sixth, a jig: a simple wooden board with nails or bolts arranged in a circle to hold the wire while you weave. For a 30cm basket, hammer 12 nails in a 30cm circle. Seventh, safety glasses and leather gloves.

Materials: The best wire for beginners is galvanised steel wire, 2mm to 3mm thick (also called 12 to 14 gauge). It is cheap, rust-resistant, and easy to bend. You can buy it in 10kg coils from hardware stores or scrap metal dealers. For the basket rim, use 4mm to 5mm wire or thin metal rods. For the base, use 3mm wire. You will also need tie wire (1.5mm) to bind the frame. Avoid aluminium wire for structural parts because it bends too easily; use it only for decoration. If you want a copper or brass look, use thin copper wire for wrapping.

Other useful items: a permanent marker, a file for sharp ends, and a can of spray paint or clear lacquer for finishing. For production, make several jigs in different sizes (20cm, 30cm, 40cm). You can also use a welding machine if you have one, but it is not necessary. Most African metal basket makers use only hand tools and a jig. Keep your wire clean and dry to prevent rust. Store coils off the ground. With these tools and materials, you are ready to start.

3. Step by Step Method

Step 1: Make the base ring. Cut a 1.2m length of 4mm wire. Bend it into a circle using your jig or a cylindrical object like a paint bucket. Overlap the ends by 5cm and wrap tightly with 1.5mm tie wire. You now have a 30cm ring.

Step 2: Add spokes. Cut 12 pieces of 3mm wire, each 40cm long. These are your spokes. Place the base ring on your jig. Lay each spoke across the ring so that 10cm sticks out inside and 30cm outside. Space them evenly like a wheel. Tie each spoke to the ring with tie wire, wrapping 3-4 turns. Make sure all spokes are tight and straight.

Step 3: Weave the base. Start at the centre. Take a long piece of 2mm wire (about 3m) and tie it to one spoke near the centre. Weave over and under each spoke in a circular pattern, going around and around. Keep the weave tight; push each row against the previous one. After 5-6 rounds, you have a solid base. Cut and tuck the end under a spoke.

Step 4: Bend up the sides. Once the base is 15cm wide, bend all the outside spokes upward at 90 degrees. Use your pliers to make a sharp bend at the base ring. Now your spokes become the vertical ribs of the basket.

Step 5: Weave the sides. Continue weaving with 2mm wire, going over and under each vertical spoke. Every 5cm, tap the weave down with your hammer to keep it tight. To make the basket stronger, you can weave two wires at once (double weave). Stop when the basket is 20cm tall.

Step 6: Make the top rim. Cut a 1.2m length of 4mm wire and bend it into a circle that fits the top of the basket. Tie it to each vertical spoke with tie wire. Cut off excess spoke ends, leaving 1cm. Bend these ends over the rim and hammer flat.

Step 7: Finish. File any sharp ends. Paint with rust-resistant spray paint or clear lacquer. Let dry. Your basket is ready.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Loose weave. If you do not push each row tight against the previous one, the basket will be floppy and weak. Fix: After every 2-3 rounds, use your pliers to push the weave down. Keep tension on the wire as you weave.

Mistake 2: Uneven spokes. If the spokes are not spaced evenly, the basket will be lopsided. Fix: Use a jig with pre-marked positions. Count the spokes and adjust before tying. For a 12-spoke basket, each spoke should be 30 degrees apart.

Mistake 3: Sharp ends. Cutting wire leaves sharp points that can cut hands. Fix: Always file or hammer the ends flat. Bend the last 1cm of any cut wire into a small hook and tuck it under a weave.

Mistake 4: Rust. Galvanised wire can rust if scratched or left in the rain. Fix: Paint the basket with a rust-resistant primer and then a topcoat. Store baskets indoors.

Mistake 5: Using too-thin wire for the rim. A 2mm rim will bend under load. Fix: Use 4mm or 5mm wire for the top and bottom rims. If you only have thin wire, double it up by twisting two strands together.

Mistake 6: Weaving too fast. Speed causes gaps and crossed wires. Fix: Work at a steady pace. Check every few rounds that the weave is flat and tight. If you see a mistake, undo it immediately; fixing later is harder.

Mistake 7: Not wearing gloves. Wire cuts and punctures are common. Fix: Always wear leather gloves and safety glasses. Keep a first-aid kit nearby.

Mistake 8: Skipping the jig. Freehand weaving is slow and inconsistent. Fix: Build a simple jig from a wooden board and nails. It takes 15 minutes and saves hours.

By avoiding these mistakes, your baskets will be strong, safe, and sellable.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety first. Metal wire can cause serious cuts, eye injuries, and puncture wounds. Always wear leather gloves when handling wire, especially when cutting or bending. Wear safety glasses when cutting or hammering; wire fragments can fly. Keep your work area clean and well-lit. Do not leave wire scraps on the floor; they can trip you or others. Use a vice or clamp to hold wire when cutting with a hacksaw. Never cut toward your body.

Tools safety: Use the right tool for the job. Lineman's pliers for cutting and twisting, not for hammering. A hacksaw for thick rods, not for thin wire (it will snag). Check your hammer handle for cracks. Keep tools sharp and oiled. When using a jig, make sure nails are hammered in straight and do not protrude underneath. If you use a welding machine, wear a welding mask and gloves, and work in a ventilated area.

Material safety: Galvanised wire can release toxic fumes if welded or heated above 200C. Do not burn off the zinc coating. If you need to weld, grind off the galvanised layer first and work outdoors. Avoid breathing metal dust when filing or sanding; wear a dust mask. Wash your hands after handling wire, especially before eating.

Best practices: Plan your basket before you start. Measure twice, cut once. Keep your wire coils organised by gauge. Label your jigs with the size. Work on a sturdy table at waist height to avoid back strain. Take breaks every 30 minutes to stretch your hands and wrists. When finishing, use spray paint in a well-ventilated area or outdoors. Store finished baskets in a dry place. Teach others these safety rules. A safe workshop is a productive workshop. Remember, no basket is worth an injury.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it's your turn. In this exercise, you will make a simple round metal basket, 20cm wide and 15cm tall, using the skills from this module. You will need: 3mm galvanised wire (about 5m), 2mm galvanised wire (about 10m), 1.5mm tie wire (2m), 4mm wire for rims (2.4m), lineman's pliers, needle-nose pliers, hammer, hacksaw, file, measuring tape, marker, gloves, safety glasses, and a jig (12 nails in a 20cm circle).

Step 1: Make the base ring. Cut 80cm of 4mm wire. Bend into a 20cm circle. Overlap ends by 5cm and wrap with tie wire. Step 2: Cut 12 spokes of 3mm wire, each 30cm long. Tie them to the base ring evenly, leaving 5cm inside and 25cm outside. Step 3: Weave the base with 2mm wire. Start at the centre and weave over-under each spoke, going around until the base is 10cm wide. Keep tight. Step 4: Bend the spokes up at 90 degrees. Step 5: Weave the sides with 2mm wire until the basket is 15cm tall. Tap down every 5cm. Step 6: Make the top rim from 80cm of 4mm wire. Tie it to the spokes. Trim and flatten spoke ends. Step 7: File sharp points. Paint if desired.

Time: This should take 1.5 to 2 hours for your first basket. Do not rush. Check your work: The basket should stand flat, the weave should be tight, and there should be no sharp edges. If you make a mistake, undo and redo. Practice makes perfect. Once you master this 20cm basket, try a 30cm or 40cm version by scaling up the jig and wire lengths. Take a photo of your finished basket and share it with your trainer. Congratulations, you are now a metal basket maker!

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Baskets and Basketry in Metal

This practical reference brings together the working knowledge you need to design, price, produce and sell metal baskets in an African market. Basketry forms round market baskets, shallow winnowing trays, deep storage baskets, tapered fruit bowls, wall planters and decorative centrepieces have carried value in African households for centuries. Reinterpreting them in mild steel, stainless steel and recycled wire lets a small workshop turn familiar cultural shapes into durable, higher-margin products for hotels, lodges, gift shops, exporters and urban home buyers.

Treat this module as a business tool, not just a craft lesson. Every technique below is tied to cost, production time, finish quality and what a customer will actually pay for. Read it alongside the earlier lessons on tools, step-by-step method, mistakes and safety.

Production Sequence at a Glance

  1. Design and template. Draw the basket full size on a board or metal sheet. Mark warp positions every 2540 mm.
  2. Cut and form. Cut warp rods to length, bend the base ring, weld or bind the base.
  3. Set the warps. Weld or hook warp rods to the base ring at equal spacing. Keep them vertical and even.
  4. Weave the weft. Work from base upward, one wire over one warp and under the next. Keep tension even.
  5. Form the rim. Fold warp tips over a rim band and weld, or bind with a heavier wire.
  6. Add handles or fittings. Weld handles, hooks or wall brackets before finishing.
  7. Clean and finish. Grind sharp points, degrease, prime and topcoat or powder coat.
  8. Inspect and pack. Check for sharp edges, loose wires and coating runs before dispatch.

SAFETY WARNING READ BEFORE YOU START

Metal basketry involves sharp cut wire, hot sparks and welding fumes. Always wear safety glasses, leather gloves and a welding helmet or face shield. Weld only in a well-ventilated area or use local exhaust extraction galvanised wire releases toxic zinc oxide fumes when heated, so never weld or grind it without extraction or a respirator rated for metal fumes. Keep a fire extinguisher and a bucket of sand within reach, and never leave hot work unattended. Secure long wire ends so they cannot whip back and strike your face or eyes.

Quality Checks Buyers Notice

  • Even spacing. Uneven gaps read as "home-made" and reduce price.
  • No sharp ends. Run a gloved hand inside and around the rim. File or weld every cut end.
  • Flat base. The basket must sit without rocking on a flat surface.
  • Consistent finish. No runs, bare patches or rust spots. Recoat any exposed steel.
  • Straight handles. Symmetrical handles signal care and command a higher price.
  • Weight and balance. Heavy, unstable baskets are hard to sell to hotels and exporters.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Template drawn and warp positions marked before cutting.
  • Wire gauge and basket size match the agreed specification.
  • Warp rods evenly spaced and vertical; base ring flat.
  • Weft tension even; no loose or crossed wires.
  • Rim band fitted and all cut ends ground smooth.
  • Handles or fittings welded square and tested for strength.
  • Degreased, primed and topcoated or powder coated.
  • Costing sheet completed and price set before the batch is sold.
