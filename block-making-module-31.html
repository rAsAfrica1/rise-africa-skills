Site Selection and Yard Layout

8 lessons · 30 quiz questions · Business

1 Choosing a yard location

Where you put your yard determines your costs, your logistics, and your growth ceiling. This decision matters more than any other.

Key criteria:

  • Near raw materials: Close to cement suppliers, sand pits, stone quarries. Every kilometre adds delivery cost.
  • Near customers: Builders, hardware stores, construction sites. Most blocks sell within 30 miles.
  • Road access: Trucks must enter and turn easily. Muddy tracks cost you rainy-season orders.
  • Space: 300 m² for 500 blocks/day, 1,000 m² for 5,000 blocks/day, 2,000+ m² for 10,000+ blocks/day.
  • Level ground: Sloped sites need expensive levelling. Curing areas must be flat.

A site 30 minutes from your cement supplier is 30 minutes cheaper per delivery. Over 250 working days per year, that's real money.

💡 Rent first for 12 months. Buy once you've proven the business. Renting is cheap flexibility; buying is a 20-year commitment.

2 Space needed for stock

Blocks need space — both green (curing) and finished (stock). Underestimate and you block your own production.

Space per 1,000 blocks:

  • Production pallets: 1,000 blocks on pallets = 60 m² (pallets are 1.2 × 0.8 m)
  • Curing area (7 days): needs 7,000 blocks worth of space = 420 m²
  • Finished stock (30 days): 30,000 blocks = 1,800 m²
  • Loading area: 100 m²

Total for 5,000 blocks/day: about 2,500 m² (including circulation).

In practice, blocks go out fast enough that you rarely store 30 days' worth. But plan for the worst case (slow month) — no space = stop production.

3 Water supply access

Block making uses water — for mixing, cleaning, and curing. A 5,000 blocks/day yard uses about 3,000 litres/day.

Water sources:

  • Mains water: Reliable but costly in urban areas. £1.50–£4 per 1,000 L.
  • Borehole: Best value long-term. Cost: $3,000–$15,000 to drill + pump.
  • Rainwater harvesting: Free but seasonal. Store in tanks.
  • Recycled water: Capture curing overspray and washout water. Reuse for mixing.

Store water in tanks (5,000–20,000 L) so you're never stopped by a supply interruption. Cover tanks to prevent evaporation and contamination.

⚠ If you're on mains water, confirm the pipe size can support your flow needs. Upgrading later is expensive.

4 Road access for delivery trucks

Trucks must move in and out without blocking each other. Design the yard as one-way flow if possible.

Road requirements:

  • Minimum road width: 6 m for two-way traffic, 4 m for one-way
  • Surface: Concrete or compacted gravel — dirt roads become mud in rain
  • Turning circle: 12 m minimum for large trucks
  • Loading bay: Flat, level, near the finished stock
  • Weight rating: Heavy loads of blocks need reinforced road base

Confirm your landlord's road is public (not shared private road) — otherwise you may be blocked by neighbours.

5 Curing area layout

Curing is where new blocks sit for 7 days. Design for productivity:

Layout rules:

  • Next to the production line: Curing area should be directly off the machine — avoid carrying wet blocks
  • Covered roof or shade net: Direct sun and rain ruin curing
  • Pallet flow: Pallets move in one direction (straight rows) — no zigzag
  • Spray access: Water hoses reach every pallet without walking over blocks
  • Row width: 1.5 m for pallet stacking and access
  • Stacking: 8–10 blocks high per pallet

For 5,000 blocks/day: 7 days of curing × 5,000 blocks = 35,000 blocks in the curing area at any time. That's 2,100 m² of pallet space.

6 Storage for cement and sand

Raw materials need protection from weather. Storage design:

Cement store:

  • Enclosed shed, dry, above ground
  • Pallets off the floor (100 mm gap)
  • Ventilated (not sealed — cement cures from humidity)
  • Capacity: 2–4 weeks of stock
  • FIFO rule — first in, first out
  • Size: 6–10 m² for 5–10 tonnes

Sand and aggregate bays:

  • Separate bays for sand, stone, and any additives
  • Concrete or stone base (not dirt — dirt contaminates)
  • Concrete dividing walls between bays
  • Covered or partially covered (rain changes moisture content)
  • Easily accessible by loader or shovel
  • Size: 4 bays × 30 m² for 5,000 blocks/day
💡 Cement stored over 6 weeks loses 10-20% strength. Rotate stock weekly, use oldest first.

7 Fencing and security

Block yards hold valuable stock and equipment. Security is not optional.

Layers of security:

  • Perimeter fence: 2.4 m high, chain-link or concrete wall. Cheap deterrent.
  • Gate: Locked after hours, controlled entry during work.
  • Lighting: Floodlights at gate, storage, and machine areas.
  • Storage lock: Cement, tools, small equipment in a locked shed.
  • CCTV: 4-camera system ($300–$1,000) records all entries. Even fake cameras deter theft.
  • Guard: Night guard for high-value yards ($150–$400/month).

Insurance requires documented security. Without it, theft is uninsured.

8 Setting up day one

Your first day of production. Here's the checklist:

  1. Machine installed, levelled, bolted, tested
  2. Mixer wired and tested
  3. Cement, sand, stone delivered and stored
  4. Pallets stacked and accessible
  5. Water tank full, hose connected
  6. Curing area cleared and covered
  7. Batch log, quality log, safety sheets on the wall
  8. PPE for all workers (gloves, boots, masks)
  9. Emergency stop tested
  10. First batch mixed, blocks cast, curing started

Do this with your full crew so everyone knows the layout. Walk the yard daily for the first week — find problems before they find you.

💰 Yard layout determines your efficiency for years. Spend a full week planning before installing any equipment. Cheap to change on paper; expensive to change in concrete.
