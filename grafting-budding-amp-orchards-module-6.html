Lessons

1. The whip-and-tongue graft: step-by-step with local fruit trees

The whip-and-tongue graft is the best choice for beginners because it gives strong, natural-looking unions on young, flexible wood. Use it when your scion (the variety you want) and rootstock (the base tree) are both pencil-thick (about 610 mm diameter) and dormant. In Africa, this works well for mango, avocado, citrus, and guava. Start in early spring before buds break, or during the cool dry season if you can irrigate.

First, collect scion wood from a healthy, productive tree of the variety you want. Choose last season's growth that is firm and green-brown, not soft. Cut 2030 cm lengths with at least 3 buds. Keep them wrapped in damp cloth in a cooler until you graft. For rootstock, use a seedling of the same species that is at least 30 cm tall and 610 mm thick at the point where you will graft.

Make the cuts: Hold the rootstock firmly. With a sharp, clean grafting knife, make a smooth, 45 cm long diagonal cut on one side of the rootstock, starting about 2 cm below the top. The cut should be flat and straight. Then make a similar cut on the scion, but slightly shorter (34 cm). Now the 'tongue': on the rootstock cut, start about one-third from the top and slice down into the wood, making a 12 cm deep slit. Do the same on the scion, but slice upward. The tongues should interlock.

Slide the two pieces together so the tongues lock and the cambium layers (the green layer just under the bark) match on at least one side. If the scion and rootstock are different sizes, align one edge perfectly. Wrap tightly with grafting tape or plastic strips, covering the entire cut and the scion's top cut. Seal with wax or a plastic bag to prevent drying. In 23 weeks, check for new growth. Remove any shoots from the rootstock below the graft. After 23 months, cut the tape carefully. Your new tree will grow true to the scion variety.

2. The cleft graft: reviving old trees or top-working

Cleft grafting is the go-to method for changing the variety of an established tree (top-working) or reviving an old tree with a damaged trunk. It works when the rootstock is much larger than the scionoften 515 cm in diameter. In Africa, this is common for improving mango, citrus, and avocado trees that produce poor fruit. Do this in late winter or early spring while the tree is still dormant but sap is beginning to flow.

First, cut off the rootstock branch or trunk squarely with a pruning saw, leaving a clean, flat surface. If the branch is large, you may need to graft on several branches around the tree to balance growth. Use a sharp knife to smooth the cut. Then, with a sturdy grafting chisel or a wide screwdriver, split the stump down the middle to a depth of 58 cm. If the wood is hard, tap the chisel with a mallet. The split should be centered.

Prepare scions: Choose dormant wood of the desired variety, 12 cm thick and 1015 cm long with 23 buds. Make a long, sloping cut on each side of the base of the scion, about 45 cm long, tapering to a point. The cuts should be flat and clean. Insert one scion into each side of the split, with the cambium layers (the green layer under the bark) aligned with the cambium of the rootstock. The scion's sloping cut should face outward. If the rootstock is very large, you can place two scions, one on each side.

Remove the chisel. The split will close and hold the scions tightly. Wrap the entire graft union and the cut surface with grafting tape or plastic strips, and seal all exposed cuts with wax or tree wound paint. Cover the scion tips with wax too. In a few weeks, buds should start growing. Remove any shoots from the rootstock below the graft. After one season, select the strongest shoot and remove the others. This method can turn an old, unproductive tree into a valuable one in 23 years.

3. The side graft: when and how to use it

The side graft is useful when the rootstock is too large for a whip-and-tongue but you don't want to cut off the entire top (as in cleft grafting). It is ideal for adding a new variety to an existing tree (top-working) without killing the main trunk, or for grafting onto a rootstock that is slightly larger than the scion. In Africa, this technique works well for citrus, mango, and avocado, especially when you want to change only part of a tree or when the rootstock is 25 cm in diameter.

Choose a smooth area on the side of the rootstock, about 1520 cm above the ground or on a main branch. Make a shallow, downward-sloping cut into the bark and wood, about 34 cm long, starting at a 30-degree angle. The cut should go just deep enough to expose the cambium (the green layer). Then, on the scion (same diameter as the cut depth, or slightly smaller), make a matching cut: a long, sloping cut on one side, about 34 cm long, and a short cut on the opposite side at the base to form a wedge. The scion should have 23 buds.

Insert the scion into the cut on the rootstock so the cambium layers match on at least one side. The scion will stick out at an angle. Wrap the union tightly with grafting tape or plastic strips, covering the cut and the scion base. Seal with wax. Do not cut off the rootstock top immediately; leave it to draw sap and keep the tree alive. After 34 weeks, check for growth from the scion. If it grows, gradually cut back the rootstock top over several months, a little at a time, to force the scion to take over. Remove any shoots from the rootstock below the graft. This method is slower than cleft grafting but less risky because the tree continues to grow while the graft establishes.

4. Making clean cuts and matching cambium layers

The success of any graft depends on two things: clean cuts and matching cambium layers. The cambium is the thin, green layer just under the bark. When you cut, you expose it. For the graft to heal, the cambium of the scion and rootstock must touch and grow together. If they don't, the graft fails.

First, tools: You need a sharp grafting knife. A dull knife crushes cells and makes a rough cut that won't heal. Sharpen your knife before each grafting session. Use a sharpening stone and oil. A good test: the knife should shave hair off your arm. Also, clean your tools with alcohol or bleach solution between trees to prevent disease spread.

Making the cut: For whip-and-tongue, the cuts should be smooth, flat, and long enough to expose plenty of cambium. Make the cut in one continuous motion, not sawing back and forth. For cleft and side grafts, the cuts must be flat and straight. If you make a mistake, cut againdon't try to patch it. Always cut on a firm surface. Hold the wood securely. Practice on pruned branches before grafting your valuable trees.

Matching cambium: After inserting the scion, look at the union. You want the green layer of the scion to line up with the green layer of the rootstock. If the scion is smaller, align one side perfectly; the other side will have a gap. That's okay as long as one side matches. For cleft grafts, place the scion so the cambium touches the cambium of the rootstock on the outer edge. For side grafts, the same. Use a knife tip to adjust if needed. Once aligned, don't move the pieces. Wrap tightly to hold them in place. Remember: no cambium contact, no graft. With practice, you'll make clean cuts and match layers quickly.

5. Wrapping and sealing grafts with tape and wax

After you've inserted the scion and matched the cambium, you must wrap and seal the graft to hold it tight and prevent drying. Without wrapping, the union will dry out and fail. You can use grafting tape, plastic strips, or even cut plastic bags. Wax or tree wound paint seals the exposed cuts.

Wrapping: Start below the graft union and wrap upward, overlapping each turn by half. Pull the tape snugly but not so tight that it cuts into the bark. The tape should cover the entire cut and the scion base. For whip-and-tongue, also wrap the scion's top cut if it's exposed. For cleft and side grafts, wrap the union and the cut surfaces. Use a rubber band or twine if tape is not available, but plastic tape is best because it stretches and seals. In Africa, many farmers use strips of inner tube or plastic bags cut into 1 cm wide strips. Whatever you use, make sure it's clean.

Sealing: After wrapping, seal all exposed cuts with wax or tree wound paint. This includes the top of the scion (if not covered by tape), the cut surfaces on the rootstock, and any gaps. You can buy grafting wax or make your own by melting 1 part beeswax, 1 part rosin, and 1 part paraffin. Apply warm wax with a brush. Alternatively, use melted candle wax. The seal prevents water loss and keeps out disease.

Aftercare: Check the graft every week. If tape loosens, rewrap. If you see new growth from the scion, great. If the scion dries out, it failedtry again. After 23 months, the union should heal. Then carefully cut the tape on one side to avoid girdling the tree. Remove tape completely after 46 months. For wax, it will weather away. Always remove any shoots growing from the rootstock below the graft, as they compete with the scion. With proper wrapping and sealing, your graft has a much higher chance of success.

6. Hands-on: Practicing three grafts on rootstocks

Now it's time to practice. You'll need rootstocks and scion wood. For practice, use fast-growing local species like citrus (rough lemon or sour orange), mango, or guava. You can also practice on pruned branches of any tree. Get at least 10 rootstocks and 20 scions so you can make mistakes. Work in a shaded area to prevent drying.

Gather your tools: sharp grafting knife, pruning shears, grafting tape, wax, and a sharpening stone. Clean your tools with alcohol. Set up a table. Label your scions with the variety name and date. Practice each graft type at least three times.

First, whip-and-tongue: Choose rootstock and scion of equal thickness (610 mm). Make the cuts as described in Lesson 1. Remember to interlock the tongues. Wrap tightly. Do this three times. Second, cleft graft: Use a larger rootstock (25 cm diameter). Cut it off, split it, and insert two scions. Wrap and seal. Do this three times. Third, side graft: Use a rootstock 25 cm diameter. Make the side cut, insert scion, wrap. Do this three times.

After each graft, check your work: Are the cambium layers matching? Is the wrap tight? Is the wax sealed? If not, redo. Keep a notebook: record the date, graft type, and any problems. After 23 weeks, check for growth. Count how many took. With practice, your success rate will improve. In Africa, many farmers learn by doing. Don't be afraid to faileach failure teaches you. Share your results with neighbors. Grafting is a skill that can increase your fruit production and income.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Step-by-Step Grafting Techniques for Beginners

Grafting is one of the fastest ways for African agribusinesses to upgrade fruit quality, shorten time-to-first-harvest, and multiply proven genetics without buying new trees. A farmer who masters three core grafts whip-and-tongue, cleft, and side can convert a nursery into a revenue stream, revive moribund orchards, and supply certified planting material to neighbours at a premium. This reference pulls the six lessons of this module into one field-ready guide: clean cuts, matched cambium, correct wrapping, and disciplined aftercare.

Why grafting pays in African orchards

Mango, avocado, citrus, and macadamia seedlings from seed are genetically variable and can take 712 years to fruit. Grafted trees, by contrast, begin bearing in 24 years and carry the exact traits of the mother tree fruit size, taste, disease tolerance, and market timing. For a commercial grower, that difference is cash flow. A nursery selling 5,000 grafted avocado seedlings at $1.50 each turns $7,500 from a modest shade structure; the same number of seedlings sold as unimproved stock might fetch $0.40 each. Grafting is therefore both an orchard tool and a business model.

Rootstocks also solve local problems. In Kenya, avocado growers use Persea americana seedling rootstocks selected for Phytophthora tolerance; in South Africa, citrus growers rely on rough lemon or Troyer citrange for soil adaptability. Choosing the right rootstock is a business decision as much as a horticultural one.

The three grafts at a glance

Lesson 1: The whip-and-tongue graft

The whip-and-tongue is the workhorse for nursery production of mango, avocado, citrus, and apple. It works best when scion and rootstock are the same diameter (12 cm) and are dormant or semi-dormant. Make a smooth 35 cm diagonal cut on both, then a small downward tongue on each so they interlock. Match the cambium layers on at least one side ideally both. Wrap tightly and seal. Success rates of 8095% are common when the cuts are clean and the union is kept moist.

Business tip: A small nursery in Uganda can graft 200 avocado seedlings in a day with two trained workers. At a 90% take rate, that is 180 saleable trees a $270 revenue day at $1.50 per tree.

Lesson 2: The cleft graft

Cleft grafting is the go-to method for top-working old or neglected trees, and for rootstocks that are much thicker than the scion. Cut the rootstock or branch cleanly, split it with a cleft knife or chisel, and wedge one or two tapered scions into the split so the cambium of scion and stock touch. Because the stock is large, it supplies strong sap flow; this is why cleft grafts can revive a mature mango or citrus tree in a single season. The trade-off is that the large wound needs proper sealing to prevent disease.

Business tip: Top-working an old orchard is cheaper than replanting. A grower in Tanzania can convert a 20-tree mango orchard to a premium variety for the cost of scion wood and labour, then sell improved fruit within 23 years.

Lesson 3: The side graft

Side grafting is used when the rootstock is too thick for whip-and-tongue but you want to avoid the large wound of a cleft. A slanting cut is made into the side of the stock, and a similarly cut scion is inserted so cambium contacts cambium. It is common in citrus, mango, and avocado, and is useful for adding a second variety to an existing tree or for repairing a damaged trunk. Side grafts are slower to callus than whip grafts, so hygiene and moisture retention matter more.

Lesson 4: Clean cuts and matching cambium

The cambium is the thin green layer between bark and wood. Every graft succeeds or fails on whether scion and stock cambium touch and stay in contact. Use a sharp, clean grafting knife a dull blade crushes cells and invites rot. Cut in one smooth motion, never saw. Sterilise blades between trees with 70% alcohol or a 10% bleach solution to prevent disease spread. Align at least one side of the cambium; on thin stems, align both.

Lesson 5: Wrapping and sealing

After the union is made, wrap it tightly with grafting tape, parafilm, or clean polythene strips to hold cambium in contact and prevent moisture loss. Seal all exposed cut surfaces with grafting wax or a thin layer of paraffin to stop water loss and fungal entry. In hot African climates, shade the grafted plant for 714 days and keep humidity high. Remove wrapping once the union has callused usually 36 weeks to avoid girdling.

SAFETY WARNING SHARP TOOLS AND CHEMICALS

Grafting knives are razor-sharp and cause deep cuts. Always cut away from your body, never toward your hand, and wear a cut-resistant glove on the holding hand. Keep a first-aid kit with sterile gauze and antiseptic at the grafting bench. When sterilising blades, use 70% alcohol or 10% bleach in a labelled container away from children; never mix bleach with other chemicals. Store grafting wax and paraffin away from open flame. If you feel dizzy or cut yourself deeply, stop work and seek medical help immediately.

Lesson 6: Hands-on practice

Practice on cheap, abundant rootstock before working on valuable trees. In most African settings, rough lemon, local mango seedlings, or wild avocado seedlings make good practice stock. Aim for three grafts per session, then inspect after 2 weeks for callus and after 6 weeks for bud break. Keep a simple record: date, method, variety, take/fail, and notes. Over one season, that record becomes your nursery's most valuable asset it tells you which method works best for your climate, species, and skill level.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Sharpen and sterilise your knife before every tree; use 70% alcohol or 10% bleach.
  2. Match cambium on at least one side both sides on thin stems.
  3. Cut in one smooth stroke; never saw or crush the wood.
  4. Work fast exposed cuts dry out in minutes under African sun.
  5. Wrap tightly with grafting tape or parafilm; seal all exposed surfaces with wax.
  6. Shade and humidify for 714 days; keep soil moist but not waterlogged.
  7. Remove wrapping at 36 weeks once callus forms, to prevent girdling.
  8. Record every graft date, method, variety, result to build your nursery's data.
