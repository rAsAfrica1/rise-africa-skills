1. Hands-on Substrate Preparation

Start by gathering materials for 50 kg of substrate. You need 40 kg of dry chopped cereal straw (wheat, rice, or barley), 5 kg of cotton seed hulls if available, 2 kg of gypsum, and 1 kg of lime. Also prepare a clean 200-litre drum for pasteurisation, a large tarpaulin, and a water source. Chop straw to 35 cm pieces. In a wheelbarrow, mix dry straw, gypsum, and lime thoroughly. Add water gradually while turning until the mixture holds together when squeezed but no water drips outabout 6570% moisture. This takes roughly 60 litres of water. Heap the wet mixture under a tarpaulin for 24 hours to absorb moisture evenly.

Next, pasteurise the substrate to kill competitors. Fill the drum halfway with water and heat to 80C. Place the soaked substrate into a porous sack or wire basket and submerge it in the hot water. Maintain 7080C for 60 minutes. Do not boil, as this can break down nutrients. Alternatively, use a simple steam pasteurisation: place substrate on a raised platform inside a sealed drum with a small water layer at the bottom, heat until steam escapes for 30 minutes. After pasteurisation, drain the substrate on a clean, disinfected surface (e.g., plastic sheet wiped with 70% alcohol). Let it cool to below 30C (test with a clean handshould feel barely warm).

Now check moisture again. If too dry, add a little clean water; if too wet, spread and air-dry for 30 minutes. The final substrate should be dark, earthy-smelling, and not slimy. Pack it into clean plastic bags or trays. For a small grow room, fill 10 bags of 5 kg each. Work in a shaded, dust-free area. Avoid touching the substrate with bare hands after pasteurisationuse clean gloves. This process takes about 4 hours from start to finish. With practice, you can prepare 50 kg in one morning. Remember: contamination comes from dirty tools, hands, or water. Always sanitise everything.

2. Inoculation Practice

Inoculation is mixing mushroom spawn into cooled substrate. Work in a clean, still-air room. Wear clean clothes, wash hands with soap, and put on disposable gloves. Wipe all surfaces with 70% alcohol. You need mushroom spawn (e.g., oyster or button) at 510% of substrate weight. For 5 kg of substrate, use 250500 g of spawn. Break spawn into small piecesabout the size of a thumbusing clean hands or a sterile spoon. Do not crush it.

Open your substrate bag or tray. Sprinkle spawn evenly over the surface, then mix gently but thoroughly so spawn is distributed throughout the substrate. Do not overmixjust 34 turns. For bag culture, you can layer: one-third substrate, one-third spawn, repeat, ending with substrate. Close the bag with a clean rubber band or tie, leaving a small filter for air (e.g., a cotton plug). For trays, cover with a clean plastic sheet with small holes. Label each bag with date and spawn type.

After inoculation, place bags in a dark, warm room at 2528C and 8085% humidity. Do not open bags for 1014 days. You will see white mycelium spreading. If you see green, black, or pink patches, remove that bag immediatelyit is contaminated. For the first 3 days, check temperature daily; if too hot, open a window or use a fan indirectly. After 10 days, the substrate should be fully white. This is called 'spawn run'. Now move bags to the fruiting room with light and lower temperature (1824C). Cut small slits in bags for mushrooms to emerge. Mist twice daily. In 57 days, pins (tiny mushrooms) appear. Harvest when caps are fully open but before spores drop. This hands-on practice is the heart of mushroom farmingdo it carefully, and you will succeed.

3. Setting Up a Small Grow Room

Choose a room that is easy to clean, has no direct sunlight, and can be closed off. A spare bedroom, shed, or corner of a garage works. Size: for 20 bags (5 kg each), you need about 2 m x 2 m floor space with shelves. You will need: plastic sheeting to cover walls and floor, wooden or metal shelves (34 tiers), a humidifier or hand sprayer, a thermometer, a hygrometer (measures humidity), a small fan, and a light source (fluorescent tube or LED). Also a cleanable table for inoculation.

First, clean the room thoroughly. Remove all dust and debris. Wipe walls and floor with a disinfectant (1 part bleach to 10 parts water). Cover walls and floor with plastic sheeting for easy cleaning. Install shelves leaving 40 cm between tiers. Place a thermometer and hygrometer at mid-height. For humidity, you can use a simple humidifier (ultrasonic) or manually mist 23 times daily. Aim for 8085% during spawn run and 8590% during fruiting. For temperature, oyster mushrooms prefer 1824C; button mushrooms 1620C. In hot African climates, you may need a small air conditioner or evaporative cooler. In cooler areas, a heater may be needed. Use a fan on low speed to circulate air, but avoid direct wind on bags.

Light: mushrooms need indirect light for fruiting. A 20-watt fluorescent tube 1.5 m above shelves for 12 hours daily is enough. Do not use direct sunlight. Keep the room dark during spawn run (first 1014 days). After opening bags, provide light. Also maintain hygiene: no shoes inside, wear clean lab coat or apron, wash hands before entering. Keep a spray bottle of 70% alcohol to sanitise tools. Monitor daily: check temperature, humidity, and any signs of contamination. A small notebook to record data helps. Total setup cost in many African cities: plastic sheeting $10, shelves $30, humidifier $25, thermometer/hygrometer $10, light $15, fan $15about $105. You can start with 10 bags and expand. This room can produce 35 kg of mushrooms per week within a month.

4. Harvesting and Packaging Simulation

Harvest mushrooms when they are fully grown but before spores release. For oyster mushrooms, the caps should be flat or slightly upturned, edges smooth. For button mushrooms, harvest when caps are closed but gills not visible. Do not wait until they turn brown or slimy. Harvest in the morning when cool. Wash hands and use a clean, sharp knife or scissors. Twist the mushroom gently at the base and cut, leaving a small stub. Do not pull, as this damages the substrate. Place mushrooms in a clean basket or traynot plastic bags, which cause sweating. Harvest all mature mushrooms at once. For a 5 kg bag, you might get 11.5 kg in the first flush. After harvest, mist the substrate and wait for a second flush in 710 days. You can get 34 flushes.

After harvesting, clean mushrooms. Remove any substrate bits with a soft brush or damp cloth. Do not wash with watermushrooms absorb water and spoil faster. Sort by size and quality: Grade A (uniform, unblemished), Grade B (slightly uneven), Grade C (for processing). For packaging, use food-grade plastic containers, paper bags, or cling film. In many African markets, 250 g or 500 g punnets are popular. Weigh accurately. For 250 g, use a small scale. Pack mushrooms stem down, caps up, in a single layer. For oyster mushrooms, you can bundle 34 together. Label with harvest date and weight. Store at 4C (refrigerator) for up to 7 days. Without refrigeration, sell within 24 hours. For transport, use a cool box with ice packs. Simulate a market sale: set a price. In Kenya, oyster mushrooms retail at KES 400600 per kg; in Nigeria, NGN 2,0003,000 per kg. Practice packaging 10 punnets of 250 g each. This simulation teaches you speed and hygiene. Remember: bruised mushrooms lose value. Handle gently.

5. Troubleshooting Real Cases

Case 1: Green mould on substrate. This is Trichoderma, a common competitor. Cause: insufficient pasteurisation, dirty tools, or too high humidity. Solution: remove and bury contaminated bags far from grow room. Increase pasteurisation time to 90 minutes. Clean room with bleach solution. In future, add 1% lime to substrate. Case 2: Mushrooms are long and thin with small caps. Cause: too much carbon dioxide, not enough fresh air. Solution: increase ventilationopen door for 10 minutes twice daily or use fan on low. Also reduce humidity to 85%. Case 3: No mushrooms after 3 weeks. Cause: spawn run not complete, temperature too low, or spawn too old. Solution: check for white mycelium; if present, wait another week. If not, re-inoculate with fresh spawn. Ensure temperature is 1824C. Case 4: Mushrooms have brown spots. Cause: bacterial blotch from too much water on caps. Solution: water only the substrate, not mushrooms. Improve air circulation. Case 5: Substrate smells sour. Cause: anaerobic bacteria from over-wet substrate. Solution: discard; next time, drain substrate well after pasteurisation. Case 6: Mushrooms are small and dry. Cause: low humidity or insufficient water. Solution: mist more often, aim for 8590% humidity. Case 7: Insects (flies, mites). Cause: open doors, dirty environment. Solution: use fine mesh on windows, keep room clean, use sticky traps. Case 8: Mushrooms taste bitter. Cause: harvesting too late or substrate with too much gypsum. Solution: harvest earlier, reduce gypsum to 1 kg per 50 kg. For each case, record what you did. Share with other farmers. In Africa, common problems are heat and contamination. Simple fixes often work: shade, ventilation, and hygiene. Practice diagnosing by looking at symptoms. If unsure, ask an experienced grower or extension officer.

6. Field Trip to a Commercial Farm

Arrange a visit to a commercial mushroom farm near you. Look for farms that grow oyster, button, or shiitake mushrooms. Call ahead and ask for a tour. Bring a notebook, pen, and camera (if allowed). Wear clean clothes and shoes that can be disinfected. On arrival, observe the layout: where is the substrate preparation area? How do they pasteurise? What materials do they use? Ask about their production scale: how many bags per week? What is their yield per bag? For example, a commercial farm might produce 100 kg per week from 500 bags. Note their spawn sourcedo they make their own or buy? Ask about costs: substrate materials, labour, electricity, water. In Kenya, a 5 kg bag might cost KES 150 to produce and sell for KES 400. In Nigeria, similar ratios.

Observe hygiene practices: do workers wear gloves, masks, and clean coats? How do they enter the grow room? Is there a footbath? How do they control temperature and humidity? Look for equipment: autoclave, humidifiers, air conditioners, shelving. Ask about problems: what are their biggest challenges? Common answers: contamination, heat, market access, and transport. Ask how they solve them. For example, they might use solar-powered fans or evaporative cooling. Ask about harvesting and packaging: how do they grade? What packaging do they use? Where do they sellsupermarkets, restaurants, hotels, direct? What price do they get? Note their record-keeping: do they track daily temperature, humidity, yields? Ask if they keep a logbook.

After the tour, sit down and write your observations. Compare with your own small grow room. What can you improve? Maybe better ventilation, a cleaner inoculation area, or a simple record system. Ask if you can volunteer for a day to learn hands-on. Many farmers are willing to share if you show respect. Also ask about their market linkageswho buys their mushrooms? Can they connect you? Finally, calculate the economics: if you produce 5 kg per week and sell at KES 500 per kg, that is KES 2,500 per week. After costs, profit might be KES 1,500. This field trip is your best teacher. Take photos, ask questions, and network. When you return, implement at least three new ideas in your own farm.

<h2>Practical Reference: Mushroom Farming Workshop &amp; Farm Visit</h2>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the hands-on skills you will practise during the workshop and farm visit. It is written for African smallholder and peri-urban growers who want to move from subsistence to a market-oriented mushroom enterprise. The focus is on doing things correctly the first time, reducing contamination losses, and protecting your health and your investment.</p>

<p>Mushroom farming rewards discipline. In Africa, the most common causes of failure are contaminated substrate, poor hygiene during inoculation, unstable fruiting conditions, and delayed harvesting. Each of these can be managed with simple, low-cost practices. Use this reference alongside the practical stations: substrate preparation, inoculation, grow-room setup, harvesting and packaging simulation, troubleshooting, and the commercial farm visit.</p>

<h3>1. Hands-On Substrate Preparation</h3>
<p>Substrate is your biggest cost and your biggest contamination risk. In most African contexts, pasteurised or sterilised straw (wheat, rice, or barley), cottonseed hulls, maize cobs, or sawdust are used. Oyster mushrooms grow well on pasteurised straw; shiitake and milky mushrooms prefer supplemented sawdust.</p>

<p><strong>Practical steps:</strong></p>
<ol>
<li>Chop substrate to 25 cm pieces to increase surface area.</li>
<li>Hydrate to 6570% moisture. Test: squeeze a handfula few drops should appear, not a stream.</li>
<li>Pasteurise by hot-water immersion at 6080C for 6090 minutes, or use lime (calcium hydroxide) soak for 1218 hours (cold pasteurisation).</li>
<li>Drain and cool to below 30C before inoculation. Warm substrate kills spawn.</li>
<li>Mix in 25% gypsum and, if available, 510% wheat bran for supplemented substrates (higher contamination risk; use only if you can sterilise).</li>
</ol>

<h3>2. Inoculation Practice</h3>
<p>Inoculation is the moment of highest contamination risk. Work in a clean, still-air space. In low-resource settings, a simple still-air box (plastic tote with arm holes) or a cleaned bathroom with doors and windows closed works. Flame-sterilise tools between bags.</p>

<p><strong>Key practices:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Use 510% spawn by wet weight. More spawn = faster colonisation = less contamination.</li>
<li>Layer or mix spawn evenly through the substrate. For bag cultivation, layer spawn every 510 cm.</li>
<li>Seal bags with a breathable filter patch or loosely tie with clean cotton wool in the neck.</li>
<li>Label with date, substrate type, and spawn strain.</li>
<li>Incubate in darkness at 2228C. Oyster mushrooms colonise in 1421 days.</li>
</ul>

<div style="border: 3px solid #c00; background: #ffe6e6; padding: 15px; margin: 20px 0;">
<p style="color: #c00; font-weight: bold; font-size: 1.1em;"> SAFETY WARNING: Never use agricultural pesticides, fungicides, or unapproved chemicals inside the grow room or on substrate. These are toxic to humans, leave residues in mushrooms, and will destroy your market access. If you suspect contamination, remove and bury or compost the affected bags away from the grow area. Always wear a dust mask when handling dry substrate and gloves when mixing lime. Lime (calcium hydroxide) causes severe skin and eye burnswear goggles and gloves, and keep it away from children and livestock.</p>
</div>

<h3>3. Setting Up a Small Grow Room</h3>
<p>A grow room can be a converted shed, a mud-brick room, or a simple polythene tunnel. The critical factors are temperature, humidity, fresh air, and light. Oyster mushrooms fruit at 1824C with 8595% relative humidity and 12 hours of indirect light per day.</p>

<p><strong>Low-cost setup tips for African conditions:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Use a double-layer roof with thatch or shade net to buffer midday heat.</li>
<li>Hang wet jute sacks or use a sand-floor humidifier to raise humidity without electricity.</li>
<li>Ventilate at dawn and dusk to avoid CO build-up (CO above 800 ppm causes long stems and small caps).</li>
<li>Use a solar-powered fan if grid electricity is unreliable.</li>
<li>Keep the floor clean and sloped for drainage. Never let water pool.</li>
</ul>

<h3>4. Harvesting and Packaging Simulation</h3>
<p>Harvest timing determines shelf life and price. Oyster mushrooms are harvested when the cap margins are still slightly curled downward, before they flatten or release spores. Harvest in the cool of the morning. Twist and pull the whole cluster; do not cut individual mushrooms, as this leaves stumps that rot.</p>

<p><strong>Post-harvest handling:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Remove field heat quickly. In hot climates, harvest before 9 AM and move to a shaded, ventilated area.</li>
<li>Pack in perforated polyethylene bags (0.51% ventilation holes) or paper bags. Avoid sealed plasticmushrooms sweat and rot.</li>
<li>Store at 48C if refrigeration is available. Without refrigeration, sell within 24 hours.</li>
<li>Weigh and label for consistency. Buyers pay for predictable quality and weight.</li>
<li>Clean mushrooms with a soft brush or damp cloth. Never soak in water.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Business tip:</strong> In many African markets, value addition (drying, powdering, or pickling) extends shelf life and increases margins. Dried oyster mushrooms can be sold at 35 times the fresh price per kg in urban markets and export niches.</p>

<h3>5. Troubleshooting Real Cases</h3>
<p>During the workshop, you will encounter these common problems. Learn to diagnose quickly.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Green mould (Trichoderma):</strong> Caused by incomplete pasteurisation or dirty inoculation. Remove affected bags immediately. Increase pasteurisation time and improve hygiene.</li>
<li><strong>Long stems, small caps:</strong> High CO or low light. Increase ventilation and provide indirect light.</li>
<li><strong>Dry, cracked caps:</strong> Low humidity. Increase misting or add wet sacks.</li>
<li><strong>Yellow or brown patches on substrate:</strong> Bacterial contamination from wet substrate. Reduce moisture and improve drainage.</li>
<li><strong>No fruiting after 25 days:</strong> Check temperature and humidity. Some strains need a cold shock (drop temperature by 58C for 24 hours).</li>
<li><strong>Mushrooms with white fuzzy feet:</strong> Normal for oyster mushrooms; indicates high humidity. Increase fresh air slightly.</li>
</ul>

<li>Their biggest losses and how they prevent them.</li>
<li>Which customers buy the most (restaurants, hotels, supermarkets, local markets).</li>
<li>How they manage labour and record-keeping.</li>
</ul>
