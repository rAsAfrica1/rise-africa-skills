Lessons

1. Principles of Annealing: Stress Relief

When glass cools unevenly, internal stresses build up that can cause it to crack or shatter unexpectedly. Annealing is the controlled cooling process that relieves these stresses. In a small workshop in Nairobi or Lagos, you might make glass beads, fused glass art, or even simple window panes. After shaping the hot glass, you must cool it slowly through a specific temperature range. For soda-lime glass, the annealing point is around 540C. At this temperature, the glass is still slightly plastic and can relieve stress in about 15 minutes. Then you cool it slowly to the strain point, about 510C, where stress is frozen in if cooled too fast. The key is to keep the glass at the annealing point long enough for all parts to reach the same temperature, then cool at a controlled rate, usually 1-2C per minute for thick pieces. A simple annealing oven, called a lehr, can be made from firebricks and a PID controller. Without annealing, glass may look fine but break from a slight bump or temperature change. In Africa, where daily temperature swings can be large, proper annealing is critical for durable products. Always test your annealed glass by checking for stress with a polariscope, which shows stress patterns as colored bands. If you see bright colors, you need to adjust your cooling schedule. Remember, annealing is not just about cooling; it's about giving the glass time to relax internally.

2. Annealing Lehr Design and Operation

A lehr is a specialized oven for annealing glass. For small-scale operations in Africa, you can build a simple lehr using locally available materials. The body can be made from a 200-litre steel drum lined with ceramic fiber insulation. Heating elements from old electric stoves or kanthal wire can be used, controlled by a PID temperature controller with a thermocouple. The lehr should have a ramp-up zone, a soaking zone, and a slow cooling zone. For a batch lehr, you load glass, heat to 540C, hold for 30 minutes, then cool at 1C per minute to 400C, then faster to room temperature. For continuous lehrs, like those used in larger workshops, glass moves on a conveyor through zones. In Africa, many artisans use a programmable kiln for annealing. The key is even temperature distribution. Use a fan to circulate air if possible. Place thermocouples at different points to monitor. A common mistake is opening the lehr door too early, causing thermal shock. Wait until the glass is below 100C. You can test the lehr by annealing a test piece and checking stress with a polariscope. If stress is high, adjust the cooling rate. Also, ensure the lehr is level and free from drafts. In hot climates, the lehr may need extra insulation to maintain temperature stability. Regular maintenance includes checking thermocouples and heating elements. With a well-designed lehr, you can produce consistently strong glass products.

3. Tempering: Thermal and Chemical

Tempering makes glass stronger by creating compressive stress on its surface. Thermal tempering involves heating glass to about 620-680C, then rapidly cooling it with air jets. This causes the surface to cool faster than the interior, putting the surface into compression and the interior into tension. The result is glass that is 4-5 times stronger than annealed glass. In Africa, thermal tempering is used for car windows, shower doors, and building facades. However, it requires precise control. You need a tempering furnace with rollers and air blowers. The glass must be cut and edged before tempering because any cutting after will cause it to shatter. Chemical tempering, also called ion exchange, is done by immersing glass in a molten salt bath, usually potassium nitrate, at about 400-450C. Smaller sodium ions in the glass are replaced by larger potassium ions, creating surface compression. This method is used for thin glass, like smartphone screens. In Africa, chemical tempering is less common due to the cost of salts and equipment, but it can be done in small tanks. Safety is critical: molten salt can cause severe burns. Always wear protective gear. Both methods require strict quality control. Tempered glass breaks into small, blunt pieces, which is a safety feature. You can test tempered glass by hitting it with a hammer; it should shatter into tiny fragments. If it breaks into large shards, it's not properly tempered.

4. Tempering Furnace Operation

Operating a tempering furnace requires attention to detail. First, preheat the furnace to the required temperature, typically 650C for 6mm glass. Load the glass on ceramic rollers, ensuring it is clean and free of dust. Any dirt will cause defects. The glass moves through the furnace on rollers, heated by electric elements or gas burners. The heating time depends on thickness: for 6mm glass, about 2-3 minutes per mm, so 12-18 minutes. The glass must reach a uniform temperature. Then it quickly moves to the quenching section, where air jets at 0.5-1.5 bar cool it rapidly. The air pressure and nozzle design determine the cooling rate. After quenching, the glass is checked for flatness and stress. A polariscope can reveal stress patterns. In Africa, many small tempering setups use modified pizza ovens or custom-built furnaces. It's crucial to have a backup power source, as power cuts can ruin the glass and damage the furnace. Also, maintain the rollers; they must be smooth and parallel to avoid marking the glass. Regular calibration of thermocouples is essential. If the furnace overheats, glass may warp or break. If underheated, tempering will be incomplete. Always follow the manufacturer's guidelines. For safety, wear heat-resistant gloves and face shield. After tempering, allow the glass to cool to room temperature before handling. Test a sample from each batch by breaking it; it should fragment into small pieces. If not, adjust the furnace settings. Record all parameters for consistency.

5. Fragmentation Testing and Standards

Fragmentation testing is how you verify that glass is properly tempered. The standard test involves breaking a sample and counting the number of fragments in a 50mm x 50mm square. According to ISO 12543 and EN 12150, tempered glass must break into at least 40 fragments in that area for 4mm glass, and more for thicker glass. The fragments should be small, blunt, and relatively uniform. To perform the test, take a tempered glass sample, place it flat on a surface, and strike it with a sharp tool at a point about 20mm from the edge. The glass should shatter into tiny pieces. Collect the fragments and count them in the central area. If the count is low or if there are large shards, the tempering is inadequate. In Africa, many workshops use this simple test to ensure quality. You can also use a polariscope to see stress patterns; properly tempered glass shows a uniform stress pattern. For chemical tempered glass, the test is similar, but the fragment count may differ. Standards also require that tempered glass not have any bubbles, scratches, or edge defects that could cause spontaneous breakage. Spontaneous breakage can occur due to nickel sulfide inclusions, which expand over time. To reduce this risk, use high-quality glass and proper tempering. Always document your test results. If your glass fails, review your furnace operation, cooling rate, and glass quality. Consistency is key to meeting standards and satisfying customers.

6. Quality Control of Annealed and Tempered Glass

Quality control (QC) ensures that every piece of glass meets the required standards. For annealed glass, QC focuses on stress levels and optical distortion. Use a polariscope to check for stress; a properly annealed piece shows minimal color bands. Also, inspect for bubbles, stones, and scratches. For tempered glass, QC includes checking flatness, fragmentation, and surface compression. Measure surface compression with a surface stress meter, which should read at least 69 MPa (10,000 psi) for thermally tempered glass. In Africa, you can use a simple polariscope and a fragmentation test as primary QC tools. Establish a sampling plan: test at least one piece per batch. Keep records of furnace temperature, cooling rate, and test results. Calibrate your equipment regularly. Train workers to identify defects. Common defects in tempered glass include warp, roller marks, and edge damage. Warp can be caused by uneven heating or cooling; adjust the furnace. Roller marks come from dirty rollers; clean them. Edge damage from poor handling; train workers to handle with care. For annealed glass, slow cooling is critical; if you see stress, re-anneal. Also, consider the end use: glass for a tabletop may need different QC than glass for a building. Always follow customer specifications and national standards. In Africa, organizations like the Kenya Bureau of Standards provide guidelines. By implementing strict QC, you reduce waste, increase customer trust, and build a reputation for quality.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Annealing and Tempering in African Glass Processing

Annealing and tempering are the two heat-treatment pillars of any profitable glass operation. In Africa's growing construction, automotive, and solar glass markets, getting these processes right determines whether a fabricator can supply safety glass that meets building codes, wins tender contracts, and avoids costly breakage in transit. This practical reference distils the operating parameters, testing regimes, and quality controls you need on the factory floor.

Why Heat Treatment Drives Business Value

Raw float glass emerging from a tin bath carries internal stresses that make it brittle and prone to spontaneous cracking. Annealing relieves those stresses so the glass can be cut, drilled, and processed without shattering. Tempering goes further: it locks the surface into high compression, so the glass fails into small blunt fragments rather than dangerous shards. Tempered glass commands a 24 price premium over annealed glass in most African markets, and it is mandatory for balustrades, shopfronts, vehicle glazing, and solar panels under national building standards.

Annealing Lehr Design and Operation

An annealing lehr is essentially a long tunnel furnace with a carefully controlled temperature gradient. Glass enters at around 550600C (the annealing point), is held in the soaking zone until internal stresses relax, then cools at a controlled rate so the surface and core contract together without generating residual stress. Exit temperature must be below 60C to prevent thermal shock during handling.

For African fabricators, the most common lehr types are electric resistance-heated roller hearths (common in Nigeria, Kenya, and South Africa) and gas-fired lehrs where natural gas is cheap and reliable. Roller alignment, zone insulation, and thermocouple calibration are the three maintenance items that most often cause quality failures. A 1C drift in the soaking zone can shift residual stress enough to cause edge cracking during cutting.

Tempering: Thermal and Chemical

Thermal tempering heats glass to 620700C (above the softening point but below melting), then quenches it rapidly with high-pressure air jets. The surface cools faster than the core, creating permanent compressive stress at the surface (typically 70100 MPa) balanced by tensile stress in the core. This is what makes tempered glass 45 stronger than annealed glass and gives it the characteristic fragmentation pattern.

Chemical tempering (ion exchange) immerses glass in a molten potassium nitrate bath at 400450C. Potassium ions replace smaller sodium ions in the surface, creating compressive stress. This is used for thin glass (under 3 mm), smartphone screens, and specialised applications. In Africa, chemical tempering is less common due to the cost of molten salt baths and safety requirements, but it is growing in the electronics and solar cover glass sectors.

Tempering Furnace Operation

A tempering furnace has three stages: loading, heating, and quenching. Roller hearth furnaces dominate in Africa because they handle large architectural panels. Key operating variables are:

  • Heating rate: 815C/s for 46 mm glass; slower for thicker glass to avoid distortion.
  • Soak time: 3090 seconds per mm of thickness at peak temperature.
  • Quench pressure: 200800 Pa depending on thickness; too low gives weak temper, too high causes bow or warp.
  • Roller speed: matched to glass thickness to prevent roller wave (optical distortion).
  • Ambient conditions: in hot African climates, inlet air temperature and humidity affect quench efficiency pre-cooling may be needed.
SAFETY WARNING HIGH TEMPERATURE AND FRAGMENTATION HAZARD

Tempering furnaces operate at 620700C and quenching systems use high-pressure air. Never open furnace doors during operation. Always wear heat-resistant gloves, face shields, and aprons when handling hot glass. Tempered glass can shatter explosively if edge damage or nickel sulphide inclusions are present always inspect edges before tempering and store tempered glass away from impact. In African workshops with limited ventilation, ensure fume extraction is working; chemical tempering salt baths release toxic vapours. First-aid: cool burns with clean water for 20 minutes and seek medical attention immediately.

Fragmentation Testing and Standards

Fragmentation testing verifies that tempered glass breaks into small, blunt particles rather than sharp shards. The standard test involves striking a 100 mm 100 mm sample at a point 10 mm from the edge with a hardened steel punch, then counting particles in a 50 mm 50 mm square. EN 12150-1 requires at least 40 particles per square; ASTM C1048 requires a minimum of 40 particles in the 50 mm square and no particle larger than 3 mm in any direction. For architectural safety glass in most African countries, compliance with EN 12150 or ASTM C1048 is required for tender acceptance.

In practice, African fabricators should:

  • Test at least one sample per production shift or per 500 m.
  • Keep a photographic record of fragmentation patterns for client audits.
  • Calibrate the punch and measure particle size with a calibrated grid.
  • Investigate any sample with fewer than 40 particles it usually indicates insufficient quench pressure or furnace temperature drift.

Quality Control of Annealed and Tempered Glass

Quality control in African glass processing must balance international standards with local realities: variable power supply, high ambient temperatures, and limited access to calibration services. A practical QC regime includes:

  • Annealed glass: polariscope inspection for residual stress, edge chip inspection, and dimensional checks.
  • Tempered glass: fragmentation test, surface stress measurement (using a surface stress meter or GASP polarimeter), bow and warp measurement, and optical distortion checks.
  • Documentation: batch records linking furnace parameters to test results essential for winning and retaining large contracts.
  • Nickel sulphide risk: for critical applications (balustrades, high-rise), specify heat-soak testing (EN 14179) to reduce spontaneous breakage risk.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Verify furnace soaking zone temperature is within 5C of setpoint before loading.
  2. Check glass thickness and adjust roller speed and quench pressure accordingly.
  3. Inspect all glass edges for chips or cracks before tempering reject damaged edges.
  4. Record heating rate, soak time, and quench pressure for every batch.
  5. Perform fragmentation test at least once per shift and photograph results.
  6. Measure bow/warp on a flat surface reject if exceeds 0.3% of length.
  7. Calibrate thermocouples and pressure gauges monthly; log calibration dates.
  8. Store tempered glass on padded A-frames, never flat on bare concrete.
