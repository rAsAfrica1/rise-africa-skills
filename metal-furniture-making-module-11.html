1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Pricing your metal furniture correctly is the difference between a business that grows and one that slowly dies. Many welders in Africa are great with a grinder and a welding machine but struggle to make a profit because they guess prices or copy what others charge. This module will teach you how to calculate costs, set a fair price, and handle commissions so you get paid what you deserve.

When you set a price, you must cover three things: materials, labour, and overhead. Materials include steel, paint, hinges, and consumables like welding rods and grinding discs. Labour is your time and skillnever give it away for free. Overhead includes rent for your workshop, electricity, transport to buy materials, and wear on your tools.

In many African markets, customers will bargain hard. If you don't know your true cost, you might agree to a price that leaves you with nothing after expenses. For example, a simple steel bed frame might use 20kg of square tubing at 2,500 Naira per kg (50,000 Naira), plus paint and consumables (5,000 Naira), plus 8 hours of labour at 1,500 Naira per hour (12,000 Naira). Your cost is 67,000 Naira. If you sell for 70,000 Naira, you only make 3,000 Naira profittoo little. A fair price would be 100,000 Naira or more.

Commissions are when a customer asks you to make a custom piece. You must agree on a deposit, usually 50% upfront, to buy materials. The balance is paid on delivery. This protects you from customers who disappear.

This module will give you practical tools: a simple cost sheet, a pricing formula, and scripts for negotiating. By the end, you will be able to price any metal furniture item confidently and handle commissions without losing money. Remember, pricing is not just about moneyit's about respecting your craft and building a sustainable business that can support your family.

2. Tools and Materials

To price and manage commissions effectively, you need a few simple tools. You don't need a computer or expensive software. A notebook, a pen, and a basic calculator will do. But if you have a smartphone, you can use a free spreadsheet app like Google Sheets to track costs and prices. A tape measure is essential to calculate material lengths accurately. A weighing scale helps if you buy steel by weight. A folder or envelope to keep receipts for materials and transport is also important.

For materials, you need to know the current prices of your common inputs: square tubing, round pipe, flat bar, angle iron, welding rods, grinding discs, paint, primer, hinges, and locks. Prices change often, so update your list every month. Visit your local steel supplier and ask for a price list. In many African cities, steel is sold per kilogram or per length. For example, a 6-meter length of 25mm square tubing might cost 4,500 Naira in Lagos, but 5,000 Naira in Kano due to transport. Always buy from a supplier who gives you a receipt and a fair price.

You also need to know your labour rate. Decide how much you want to earn per hour. A beginner might charge 1,000 Naira per hour, while a skilled welder with a good reputation can charge 3,000 Naira or more. Your overhead costs include workshop rent, electricity, and tool maintenance. Calculate your monthly overhead and divide it by the number of hours you work per month to get an overhead rate per hour. For example, if rent is 30,000 Naira per month and you work 100 hours, that's 300 Naira per hour.

Finally, keep a price book. This is a notebook where you write down the cost and selling price of every item you make: beds, gates, chairs, tables. After a few months, you will have a quick reference so you don't have to recalculate from scratch. This book also helps you see which items are profitable and which are not. With these tools, you can price with confidence and avoid the common mistake of underpricing.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps to price any metal furniture item. First, calculate material cost. List every material: steel, paint, hinges, screws, welding rods, grinding discs, and gas. Add up the cost. For example, a simple metal chair might use 5kg of square tubing at 2,500 Naira per kg = 12,500 Naira, plus 1kg of welding rods at 1,200 Naira, plus 2 grinding discs at 500 Naira each = 1,000 Naira, plus paint and primer 2,000 Naira. Total material cost = 16,700 Naira.

Second, calculate labour cost. Estimate how many hours the job will take. If it takes 4 hours and your labour rate is 1,500 Naira per hour, labour cost = 6,000 Naira. Third, add overhead. If your overhead rate is 300 Naira per hour, for 4 hours that's 1,200 Naira. Fourth, add up all costs: 16,700 + 6,000 + 1,200 = 23,900 Naira. This is your break-even cost. You must charge more than this to make a profit.

Fifth, decide your profit margin. A common practice is to add 30% to 50% profit on top of cost. For 30% profit, multiply cost by 1.3: 23,900 x 1.3 = 31,070 Naira. Round up to 32,000 Naira. For 50% profit, multiply by 1.5: 23,900 x 1.5 = 35,850 Naira, round to 36,000 Naira. Your final price should be within this range, depending on market conditions and your reputation.

Sixth, for commissions, always ask for a 50% deposit before starting. Use the deposit to buy materials. Write a simple agreement: describe the item, dimensions, colour, delivery date, total price, deposit amount, and balance due. Both you and the customer sign. Give the customer a copy. This prevents misunderstandings. On delivery, collect the balance before unloading. If the customer refuses to pay, you keep the item and can sell it to someone else. This method works for any metal furniture, from beds to gates. Practice it until it becomes second nature.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Forgetting to include overhead. Many welders only add materials and labour, then wonder why they have no money for rent or electricity. Fix: Always add overhead per hour. Calculate your monthly overhead and divide by your working hours. Add that to every job.

Mistake 2: Undercharging for labour. Beginners often charge too little because they are afraid to lose customers. Fix: Know your worth. If you are skilled, charge accordingly. Start at 1,000 Naira per hour and increase as you gain experience and reputation.

Mistake 3: Not accounting for material waste. Steel cuts leave offcuts, and mistakes happen. Add 10% to your material cost for waste. For example, if materials cost 16,700 Naira, add 1,670 Naira, making it 18,370 Naira.

Mistake 4: Giving discounts too easily. Customers will ask for a lower price. If you drop your price without reducing costs, you eat the loss. Fix: Offer value instead of discounts. For example, offer free delivery or a free coat of paint instead of reducing the price.

Mistake 5: Not taking a deposit for commissions. Without a deposit, a customer can cancel and leave you with expensive, custom-cut materials. Fix: Always take at least 50% deposit. If the customer refuses, walk away. It's not worth the risk.

Mistake 6: Not keeping records. Without records, you don't know if you made a profit or loss. Fix: Keep a notebook or use a simple spreadsheet. Record every sale, material purchase, and expense. Review it weekly.

Mistake 7: Copying competitors' prices without knowing their costs. A competitor might be selling at a loss to attract customers. Fix: Calculate your own costs and set your own price. Compete on quality and service, not just price.

Mistake 8: Forgetting to include transport. If you deliver, add transport cost to the price. Fix: Estimate fuel or fare and add it. For example, if delivery costs 2,000 Naira, add it to the final price.

By avoiding these mistakes, you will build a profitable and respected metal furniture business.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Pricing and commissions are not just about moneythey also involve safety and professional best practices. When you agree on a commission, you must consider the safety of the final product. For example, a metal bed frame must be strong enough to support weight without collapsing. If you cut corners on materials to lower the price, you risk injuring your customer and destroying your reputation. Always use the correct thickness of steel and proper welding techniques. Never sacrifice safety for a lower price.

Best practice 1: Always have a written agreement. Even a simple handwritten note signed by both parties is better than a verbal deal. Include the item description, dimensions, materials, colour, delivery date, total price, deposit paid, and balance due. This protects both you and the customer.

Best practice 2: Take photos of the finished item before delivery. This serves as proof of quality and can be used for your portfolio. If a customer later claims the item was defective, you have evidence.

Best practice 3: Communicate clearly. If you are running late, inform the customer early. If the price of materials increases after you quoted, discuss it with the customer before proceeding. Honesty builds trust.

Best practice 4: Keep your workshop safe. Wear protective gear: welding helmet, gloves, apron, and safety boots. Keep a fire extinguisher nearby. A safe workshop is a productive workshop.

Best practice 5: Invest in your skills. Attend workshops or watch online tutorials to improve your welding and business skills. The better your quality, the more you can charge.

Best practice 6: Save for taxes and emergencies. In many African countries, small businesses must pay taxes or licenses. Set aside a small percentage of each sale for taxes and unexpected expenses.

Best practice 7: Treat customers with respect. Even if a customer is difficult, remain calm and professional. Word of mouth is powerfula happy customer will refer others, while an angry customer will warn others away.

By following these safety and best practices, you will not only make more money but also build a lasting business that contributes to your community.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it's time to practice. This exercise will help you apply everything you learned. You will price a simple metal stool and handle a commission scenario. Grab your notebook, pen, and calculator.

Part 1: Cost calculation. Imagine you are making a metal stool. It uses 3kg of square tubing at 2,500 Naira per kg. It needs 0.5kg of welding rods at 1,200 Naira per kg. It uses 1 grinding disc at 500 Naira. Paint and primer cost 1,000 Naira. Labour takes 2 hours at 1,500 Naira per hour. Overhead rate is 300 Naira per hour. Calculate: (a) material cost, (b) labour cost, (c) overhead cost, (d) total cost. Add 10% for waste to material cost. Then add 40% profit to total cost to get your selling price. Round to the nearest 500 Naira.

Part 2: Commission agreement. A customer wants a custom metal gate. You estimate materials at 45,000 Naira, labour 8 hours at 1,500 Naira per hour, overhead 8 hours at 300 Naira per hour. Add 10% waste on materials and 50% profit on total cost. Calculate the total price. Then write a simple agreement with: description (metal gate, 3m wide, 2m high, black paint), total price, deposit (50%), balance due on delivery, and delivery date (2 weeks from today). Sign and date it.

Part 3: Negotiation role-play. Ask a friend to act as a customer who says, "That's too expensive. Can you reduce the price?" Practice responding without lowering your price. Instead, offer value: free delivery, a free can of paint for touch-ups, or a longer warranty. Write down your response.

Part 4: Record keeping. Create a simple table in your notebook with columns: Date, Item, Materials Cost, Labour Cost, Overhead Cost, Total Cost, Selling Price, Profit. Fill it in for the stool and the gate. Review your profit. If profit is less than 20% of selling price, adjust your pricing.

Part 5: Reflection. Write a short paragraph on what you learned. Did you forget any costs? How did the negotiation feel? What will you do differently next time? This exercise will build your confidence and ensure you never underprice your work again.

<h2>Practical Reference: Pricing and Commissions for Metal Furniture Making</h2>

<p>This practical reference is your go-to sheet when you are standing in your workshop in Kumasi, Kano, Nairobi, or Gaborone with a finished metal gate, a set of school desks, or a batch of welded chairs, and you need to quote a price that keeps you in business. Pricing is not guesswork. It is a business decision that determines whether you can pay for gas, electrodes, grinding discs, transport, and still have money left to feed your family and grow your workshop. Commissions the cut a sales agent, showroom, or middleman takes for bringing you work must be built into your price before you quote, not deducted after.</p>

<p>In African metal fabrication markets, the most common failure is not poor welding. It is poor pricing. A welder who quotes GHS 400 for a gate that costs GHS 380 to produce is not running a business; he is running a charity with sparks. This lesson gives you the numbers, the formulas, and the discipline to price with confidence.</p>

<h3>Why This Matters for Your Workshop</h3>

<p>Metal furniture and fabrication work in Africa is often sold in one of three ways: direct to a customer who walks into your workshop, through a commission agent who sources contracts (schools, churches, NGOs, hotels), or through a showroom or hardware shop that displays your product for a markup. Each channel has a different cost structure. If you do not separate these, you will find that your most "successful" months the ones with the biggest contracts leave you with the emptiest pockets. Pricing and commissions are the difference between a workshop that survives five years and one that closes after the first big job.</p>

<h3>Step-by-Step Pricing and Commission Method</h3>

<ol>
<li><strong>List every material</strong> used on the job, with quantity and current market price. Walk to the steel dealer and confirm the price today, not last month.</li>
<li><strong>Count labour hours honestly.</strong> If a gate takes two days, write two days. Do not pretend it took one to look fast.</li>
<li><strong>Add overhead per job.</strong> Divide your monthly rent, electricity, and consumables by the number of jobs you expect in the month, then add that figure to each quote.</li>
<li><strong>Add transport and installation.</strong> Many fabricators lose money here because they forget the pickup or the site welding.</li>
<li><strong>Decide your commission rate</strong> based on the channel table above, and add it as a line item never deduct it from your profit.</li>
<li><strong>Add profit margin</strong> of at least 1525% on total cost. This is your growth, your tool replacement, and your emergency fund.</li>
<li><strong>Write the quote on paper</strong> with a validity date (e.g. 14 days) because steel prices move.</li>
<li><strong>Get a deposit</strong> of 5070% before buying materials. This protects you from clients who disappear.</li>
</ol>
