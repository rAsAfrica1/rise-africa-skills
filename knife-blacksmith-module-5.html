1. Sourcing Steel and Materials in African Markets

Welcome to Module 5, the heart of your knife blacksmithing business. In this first lesson we focus on the single most important input you will ever need: steel. Without reliable steel, you cannot make reliable knives, and without reliable knives you cannot build a reputation or a profitable workshop. Across Africa, the steel supply chain is both a challenge and an opportunity. The blacksmith who learns to source smartly will always have work while others wait for scraps. Let us begin with the reality of local markets. In most African cities and towns, you will find three main sources of steel: scrap yards, informal metal traders, and industrial suppliers. Scrap yards are the most accessible for a beginner. Old car springs, leaf springs, coil springs, and axles are excellent high-carbon steel. Truck leaf springs are especially prized because they are thick, tough, and already shaped in long flat bars. When you visit a scrap yard, bring a small file and a magnet. A file will tell you if the steel is hard enough to skate across rather than bite. A magnet will confirm it is ferrous. Learn to identify spring steel by its shape and by the way it rings when tapped. Avoid galvanised steel, painted steel, and anything that looks like it came from a chemical plant. Those can release toxic fumes when heated. For a small workshop, a single truck leaf spring can yield ten to twenty knife blanks depending on the size of your blades. That is a huge return on a small investment. Your second source is the informal metal trader. These are the men and women who sell used metal along roadsides or in open-air markets. They often have connections to garages, farms, and construction sites. Build a relationship with two or three of them. Tell them exactly what you need: flat bars of high-carbon steel, at least five millimetres thick, and at least five centimetres wide. Offer to pay a small deposit for them to set aside good pieces for you. In many African markets, trust and consistency matter more than price. If you show up every week and pay fairly, you will get the best pieces before anyone else. Your third source is the industrial supplier. These are companies that sell new steel: spring steel, tool steel, and bearing steel. They are more expensive but they offer consistency. If you are making high-end knives for export or for wealthy local customers, consistency is worth the extra cost. Ask for 5160, 1080, or 1075 steel. These are common spring and tool steels that forge and heat-treat well. In South Africa, Kenya, Nigeria, and Egypt you can find industrial steel suppliers in the major cities. If you are far from a city, you can sometimes order by phone or through a transport company. Always ask for a material certificate if available. That certificate tells you the exact chemical composition, which helps you plan your heat treatment. Now let us talk about other materials. You will need handle material, pins, epoxy, and abrasives. For handles, Africa offers some of the most beautiful hardwoods in the world: ebony, African blackwood, mahogany, olive wood, and wild olive. You can often get offcuts from furniture makers for free or very cheap. Also consider bone, horn, and antler from local livestock. Cow horn is tough, polishes beautifully, and is abundant in cattle-raising regions. For pins, you can use brass rod, copper rod, or even mild steel rod. Brass is traditional and easy to peen. You can buy brass rod from welding supply shops or online. For epoxy, a two-part slow-curing epoxy is best. Avoid quick-set epoxy because it becomes brittle. For abrasives, you need sandpaper in grits from 80 up to 2000, and ideally a belt grinder with belts in 36, 60, 120, 220, 400, and 800 grit. If you cannot afford a belt grinder, a good file set and lots of sandpaper will still get you there. Let us talk about business. Your sourcing strategy directly affects your profit margin. If you pay too much for steel, you cannot compete. If you pay too little, you may get bad steel that ruins your knives. The sweet spot is to build a small stockpile of known-good steel. Buy in bulk when you find a good batch. Store it dry, oiled, and labelled. Keep a notebook with the source, date, and type of each piece. When you make a knife, write down which steel you used and how it performed. Over time, you will know exactly which scrap yards and traders to trust. You will also know which steel heat-treats well in your forge. That knowledge is worth more than any single knife sale. Finally, think about transport and logistics. In many African countries, moving heavy steel is expensive. If you buy a hundred kilograms of leaf spring, you need a way to get it home. A motorcycle with a trailer, a pickup truck, or a shared taxi with a roof rack can work. Plan your purchases around trips you already make. Combine a steel run with a market visit or a delivery. If you are in a rural area, consider partnering with other blacksmiths to buy in bulk and share the transport cost. That is how you turn a solo workshop into a small cooperative that can negotiate better prices. In summary, your steel sourcing plan should include at least two scrap yards, one informal trader, and one industrial supplier. Test every new steel before you use it. Keep records. Build relationships. And always remember that the knife you sell tomorrow depends on the steel you source today. In the next lesson we will take that steel and turn it into a blade through forging and stock removal.

2. Forging and Stock Removal Techniques for African Workshops

Now that you have your steel, it is time to shape it. In this lesson we cover both forging and stock removal. You do not need a massive power hammer or a factory. You need a forge, an anvil, a hammer, and patience. Many successful African blacksmiths work with a simple charcoal forge made from a car wheel rim or a metal bucket lined with clay. That is enough to make world-class knives. Let us start with the forge. A good forge must reach welding heat, which is around 1300 degrees Celsius for most high-carbon steels. Charcoal is the most common fuel in Africa. It burns hot and is widely available. You can also use coal, coke, or even dry hardwood in a pinch. Your forge needs an air source. A hand-cranked blower, a hair dryer, or a small electric fan can work. The key is to control the air so you do not burn your steel. Too much air and you oxidise the steel, creating scale and losing carbon. Too little air and you never reach the right temperature. Learn to watch the colour of the steel. A dull red is around 600 degrees. A cherry red is around 800. An orange is around 1000. A bright yellow is around 1200. A white sparkle is around 1300 and above. For forging, you want orange to bright yellow. For heat treatment, you want cherry red to orange depending on the steel. Now let us talk about forging. Start with a piece of leaf spring. Cut it to length with a hacksaw or an angle grinder. Heat one end in the forge until it is orange. Place it on the anvil and use your hammer to draw it out. Drawing out means making the steel longer and thinner. You do this by striking with the face of the hammer and moving the steel along the anvil. Keep your hammer blows consistent. Do not hit too hard when the steel is thin, or you will create cold shuts, which are folds that do not weld and become cracks. For a knife, you usually want to forge the blade profile first. That means drawing out the tip, shaping the spine, and forming the tang. The tang is the part that goes into the handle. A full tang is strongest and most popular for working knives. A hidden tang is lighter and common on daggers and hunting knives. For most African markets, a full tang knife with a sturdy handle is the best seller. It is strong, easy to repair, and looks valuable. After forging, you need to normalise the steel. Normalising means heating it to critical temperature and letting it cool in still air. This relieves stress and refines the grain. Do this two or three times. Then you can move to stock removal. Stock removal means cutting and grinding the blade shape from a flat bar without forging. Many beginners start with stock removal because it is simpler. You can use an angle grinder, a hacksaw, and a file. Cut the profile with a grinder, then refine it with a file. Be careful not to overheat the steel. If the steel turns blue or purple, you have softened it. That is fine before heat treatment, but it means you need to re-normalise. For bevels, you can file them by hand or use a belt grinder. A belt grinder is faster but expensive. A good file and a lot of patience will still produce a beautiful knife. The key is to keep your angles consistent. Mark your centre line with a scribe or a marker. File from the spine down to the edge on both sides. Check your progress often. You want the edge to be thin but not too thin before heat treatment. Leave about half a millimetre of thickness at the edge. That prevents cracking during quenching. Now let us talk about heat treatment. This is where the magic happens. Heat treatment makes the knife hard, tough, and able to hold an edge. The process has three steps: austenitising, quenching, and tempering. Austenitising means heating the steel until it becomes non-magnetic. Use a magnet to test. When the magnet no longer sticks, you are at the right temperature. For most high-carbon steels, that is around 800 degrees Celsius. Hold it there for a minute or two to let the heat soak through. Then quench. Quenching means cooling the steel very quickly. You can use oil, water, or brine. Oil is safest for most knife steels. Warm oil is better than cold oil because it cools slightly slower and reduces cracking. Vegetable oil can work, but commercial quenching oil is better. Water is faster and can crack thin blades. Brine is even faster and is used for some simple steels. For leaf spring steel, warm oil is a good choice. After quenching, the blade is very hard but also very brittle. You must temper it. Tempering means reheating the blade to a lower temperature, usually between 180 and 220 degrees Celsius, and holding it there for one to two hours. You can use a kitchen oven, a toaster oven, or even a charcoal fire with careful watching. The tempering temperature determines the final hardness. Lower temperature means harder and more brittle. Higher temperature means softer and tougher. For a working knife, aim for 200 degrees Celsius for one hour, then let it cool in air. You can do two cycles for better results. Now let us talk about business. Your forging and stock removal methods affect your production speed and your costs. If you forge, you save steel because you can stretch a small piece into a large blade. If you do stock removal, you waste more steel but you save time. The best approach is a hybrid: forge the basic profile, then use stock removal to refine the bevels and the edge. That gives you the best of both worlds. You also need to think about your product line. Do not try to make every kind of knife. Pick three or four models that sell well in your area. A camp knife, a skinning knife, a chef knife, and a small utility knife are a good start. Make them consistently. Use the same steel, the same heat treatment, and the same handle shape. That way you can produce faster and your customers know what they are getting. In rural areas, a heavy chopping knife or a machete may be more popular than a chef knife. In cities, a stylish chef knife or a folding knife may sell better. Talk to your customers. Ask them what they need. Then make that. Finally, keep your workspace safe. Forging involves fire, hot metal, and sharp tools. Wear leather gloves, safety glasses, and an apron. Keep a bucket of water nearby. Do not quench in water if you are using oil because oil and water do not mix and can cause a fire. Keep your forge area clear of flammable materials. And always remember that a sharp knife is safer than a dull one because it does not slip. In the next lesson we will talk about handles and finishing, which is where your knife becomes a piece of art.

3. Handle Making and Finishing for Market Appeal

A knife is only as good as its handle. A beautiful blade with a bad handle will not sell. A simple blade with a comfortable, durable handle will sell every time. In this lesson we focus on handle making and finishing. This is where you can differentiate your product in the African market. Let us start with handle materials. As mentioned earlier, Africa has incredible hardwoods. Ebony is black and dense. African blackwood is even denser and polishes like glass. Mahogany is reddish-brown and easy to work. Olive wood has beautiful grain and is common in North and East Africa. Wild olive is tough and takes a fine finish. You can also use bone, horn, and antler. Cow horn is abundant and can be heated and pressed flat. Bone from cattle is strong and can be dyed or left natural. Antler from kudu, impala, or other game is prized for traditional knives. For synthetic materials, you can use micarta, G10, or even recycled plastic. Micarta is made from layers of cloth or paper soaked in resin. You can make it yourself with old denim and epoxy. That is a great way to use waste and create a unique product. G10 is similar but uses fibreglass. It is very strong but requires good dust protection when grinding. For most African workshops, natural materials are the best choice because they are cheap, locally available, and tell a story. A knife with a handle made from a fallen tree in the customer's village is more valuable than a plastic handle from a factory. Now let us talk about handle construction. There are two main types: full tang and hidden tang. A full tang handle has the steel tang running the entire length of the handle. You attach two scales, one on each side, and pin them together. This is the strongest and most common design for working knives. A hidden tang handle has a narrower tang that goes into a hole drilled in a block of wood or horn. This is lighter and allows for more sculpted shapes. For a beginner, full tang is easier because you can see what you are doing. For a professional, hidden tang allows for more elegant designs. Let us go through the full tang process. First, make sure your tang is flat and clean. Remove any scale or rust with sandpaper or a grinder. Then trace the tang onto your handle material. Cut two scales slightly larger than the tang. Mix your epoxy. Slow-curing epoxy is best. Apply epoxy to the tang and to the inside of both scales. Place the scales on the tang and clamp them tightly. You can use C-clamps, spring clamps, or even a vice. Let it cure for at least 24 hours. After curing, you shape the handle. Use a file, a rasp, or a belt grinder. Start by shaping the front of the handle, called the bolster area. Then shape the sides and the back. You want a handle that fits your hand comfortably. It should not be too thick or too thin. A good test is to hold the knife and make a cutting motion. If your hand cramps or slips, the handle is wrong. For the pins, you can use brass, copper, or mild steel. Drill holes through the tang and the scales before you glue. Then insert the pins and peen them over. Peening means hammering the ends of the pin to mushroom them out. This locks the scales in place. You can also use Corby bolts or Loveless bolts, which are screws that look like pins. They are stronger but more expensive. After shaping, you sand the handle. Start with 80 grit and work up to 2000 grit. Sand with the grain, not against it. For a glossy finish, use a buffing wheel with white compound. For a matte finish, stop at 400 grit and apply a coat of oil or wax. For a waterproof finish, use polyurethane or boiled linseed oil. Boiled linseed oil is traditional and easy to apply. Rub it in with a cloth, let it dry, and repeat three or four times. Now let us talk about finishing the blade. After heat treatment, the blade will have scale and discolouration. You need to clean it up. Use sandpaper or a belt grinder to remove the scale. Start with 120 grit and work up to 800 or 1000 grit. For a mirror polish, go up to 2000 grit and then buff. For a satin finish, stop at 400 grit and use a Scotch-Brite pad. For a forged finish, leave some of the hammer marks and scale. That is popular in rustic and traditional knives. You can also etch the blade with ferric chloride to bring out the hamon, which is the visible line between the hard edge and the softer spine. Ferric chloride is available from electronics suppliers. Mix it with water and dip the clean blade for a few minutes. Then neutralise with baking soda and water. Now let us talk about sheaths. A knife without a sheath is incomplete. In Africa, leather is the most common sheath material. You can buy vegetable-tanned leather from local tanners or from online suppliers. You can also use rawhide, which is cheaper but less durable. For a simple sheath, cut two pieces of leather slightly larger than the knife. Sew them together with a saddle stitch. Use a stitching awl and waxed thread. You can also rivet the sheath with copper rivets. For a more advanced sheath, wet-form the leather around the knife. That means soaking the leather in water, wrapping it around the knife, and letting it dry. That gives a perfect fit. You can also make sheaths from wood, bone, or even plastic. For the African market, a leather sheath with a belt loop is the standard. Now let us talk about business. Your handle and finish determine your price point. A rough knife with a simple handle sells for a low price. A polished knife with a beautiful hardwood handle and a leather sheath sells for a much higher price. The difference in material cost is small, but the difference in labour is significant. You need to decide where you want to compete. If you are in a rural area, you may need to make affordable working knives. If you are in a city or selling to tourists, you can make high-end collectibles. The key is to be consistent. If you sell a knife as high-end, it must be perfect. No gaps in the handle, no scratches on the blade, no loose pins. If you sell a knife as a working tool, it must be strong and comfortable. It does not need to be pretty, but it must work. Finally, think about branding. A simple stamp or etch with your name or logo adds value. You can make a stamp from a piece of steel and hit it with a hammer while the blade is hot. Or you can use electro-chemical etching with a stencil. A brand tells the customer that you stand behind your work. It also helps you build a reputation. In the next lesson we will talk about pricing, selling, and building a customer base in Africa.

4. Pricing, Selling, and Building a Customer Base in Africa

You have made a knife. Now you need to sell it. This lesson is about the business of blacksmithing in Africa. We will cover pricing, sales channels, customer relationships, and growth. Let us start with pricing. Many African blacksmiths undercharge. They look at the cost of materials and add a small amount for labour. That is a recipe for poverty. You must price for profit. Your price should cover materials, labour, overhead, and a profit margin. Let us break it down. Materials include steel, handle material, epoxy, pins, sandpaper, and sheath leather. Labour includes the time you spend forging, grinding, heat treating, and finishing. Overhead includes rent, electricity, fuel, tools, and transport. Profit is what you keep to reinvest and to live on. A simple formula is: price equals materials plus labour plus overhead plus profit. For example, if a knife takes four hours of labour and you want to earn the equivalent of ten dollars per hour, that is forty dollars. If materials cost five dollars and overhead is five dollars, your total cost is fifty dollars. Add a twenty percent profit margin, and your price is sixty dollars. That may sound high, but it is fair. If you sell for thirty dollars, you are losing money. You are paying yourself nothing. You cannot grow. You cannot buy better tools. You cannot support your family. So learn to charge what you are worth. Now, you may be thinking that your local market cannot afford sixty dollars for a knife. That may be true. But you have other options. You can make different products for different markets. A simple working knife with a wooden handle and a basic sheath might sell for fifteen dollars. A high-end knife with a polished blade and an ebony handle might sell for one hundred dollars. You need both. The cheap knife pays the bills. The expensive knife builds your reputation. You can also sell to tourists, expats, and online customers. Tourists often pay more for authentic handmade goods. Expats and international customers can buy through social media and shipping. Let us talk about sales channels. The first channel is direct sales. You can sell from your workshop, at local markets, or at craft fairs. Direct sales are great because you meet your customers and get feedback. You can also do custom orders. A customer asks for a specific knife, you make it, and they pay a deposit. Custom orders are profitable because there is no unsold inventory. The second channel is retail. You can sell your knives to shops, outfitters, and hardware stores. They will buy at wholesale, which is usually fifty percent of retail. That means if you sell a knife to a shop for thirty dollars, they will sell it for sixty. That is fine because they handle the marketing and the customer service. You just need to make sure your wholesale price still covers your costs and profit. The third channel is online. You can use Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, and local e-commerce platforms. Take good photos of your knives. Use natural light. Show the knife from different angles. Include a description of the steel, the handle material, and the dimensions. Be honest about the flaws. Customers appreciate honesty. You can also use online marketplaces like Etsy or Amazon Handmade, but shipping from Africa can be expensive and slow. Still, for high-end knives, international customers will pay for shipping. The fourth channel is word of mouth. This is the most powerful channel in Africa. If you make a good knife, people will talk. If you make a bad knife, people will talk even more. So always do your best work. Treat every customer with respect. Deliver on time. If you make a mistake, fix it. A happy customer will tell ten people. An unhappy customer will tell a hundred. Now let us talk about customer relationships. In Africa, business is personal. You do not just sell a knife. You build a relationship. Learn your customer's name. Ask about their work. If they are a farmer, ask about their crops. If they are a hunter, ask about their last hunt. Remember what they like. Send them a message when you have a new knife that matches their interests. Offer to sharpen their knife for free after a year. That keeps you in contact and shows that you care. You can also create a customer list. Write down names, phone numbers, and what they bought. Use that list to send updates about new products or special offers. Now let us talk about growth. How do you go from a one-person workshop to a small business? First, systemise your production. Make the same models over and over. Create templates for your blades. Use jigs for your grinding. That way you can train an apprentice to do the repetitive work while you focus on the skilled tasks. Second, invest in tools. A belt grinder, a drill press, and a good forge will pay for themselves many times over. Buy them one at a time as your profits allow. Third, build a team. Hire one apprentice. Teach them your methods. Pay them fairly. As your business grows, hire more. Fourth, diversify your products. You can make knives, but you can also make machetes, axes, garden tools, and custom hardware. That spreads your risk. If knife sales are slow, you still have income. Fifth, protect your reputation. Never sell a knife you would not use yourself. Never lie about your materials. Never miss a deadline without communicating. Your reputation is your most valuable asset. Finally, think about the future. Africa is a growing market. More people are buying quality tools. More tourists are visiting. More people are shopping online. The blacksmith who adapts to these trends will thrive. The blacksmith who refuses to change will disappear. So keep learning. Keep improving. Keep selling. In the next lesson we will talk about scaling up, apprenticeships, and building a legacy.

5. Scaling Up, Apprenticeships, and Building a Legacy

You have learned to source steel, forge a blade, make a handle, and sell your knives. Now it is time to think bigger. This final lesson is about scaling up, training apprentices, and building a legacy that lasts beyond your own hands. Let us start with scaling up. Scaling up does not mean becoming a factory. It means increasing your output without sacrificing quality. There are three ways to do that: better tools, better systems, and better people. Better tools are the easiest. A belt grinder can replace hours of hand filing. A power hammer can replace days of forging. A heat-treating oven can replace guesswork with precision. But tools cost money. You need to reinvest your profits. Set aside a percentage of every sale for tool upgrades. Even ten percent adds up. Make a list of the tools you need most. For most African blacksmiths, the first big purchase should be a belt grinder. The second should be a drill press. The third should be a proper quenching tank and a tempering oven. Better systems are about how you work. Create a workflow. For example, Monday is forging day. Tuesday is grinding day. Wednesday is heat treatment day. Thursday is handle day. Friday is finishing and sheath day. Saturday is sales and delivery day. Sunday is rest. That rhythm keeps you productive and prevents burnout. You can also create checklists for each knife. A checklist ensures you do not forget a step. It also makes it easier to train someone else. Better people means hiring and training apprentices. This is the most important part of scaling up in Africa. There is high youth unemployment in many countries. Young people are looking for skills. If you can offer a good apprenticeship, you will have a line of candidates. But you must do it right. Let us talk about apprenticeships. A good apprenticeship has four parts: selection, training, payment, and progression. Selection means choosing the right person. Look for someone who is patient, honest, and willing to learn. Do not just pick a family member. Pick someone who shows up on time and follows instructions. Training means teaching them step by step. Start with simple tasks: cleaning the workshop, sorting steel, cutting blanks. Then move to grinding, then forging, then heat treatment, then handle making. Do not rush. A good apprentice needs at least one year to become productive. Payment means paying them fairly. In many African countries, apprentices are unpaid or paid very little. That is exploitation. Pay them a living wage. If you cannot afford a living wage, you cannot afford an apprentice. You can start with a small salary and increase it as they become more skilled. You can also offer a percentage of the profit on knives they make. That motivates them to work hard. Progression means giving them a path. After one year, they become a junior smith. After three years, they become a senior smith. After five years, they can start their own workshop or become a partner in yours. That gives them hope and loyalty. Now let us talk about building a legacy. A legacy is what you leave behind. It is not just money. It is knowledge, reputation, and community. To build a legacy, you need to document your methods. Write down your recipes for heat treatment. Draw your knife designs. Record your sources for steel and handle material. That way, when you are gone, your knowledge does not disappear. You can also teach workshops. Invite young people to your shop for a weekend class. Teach them the basics. Some will become competitors, but most will become allies. A rising tide lifts all boats. You can also form a blacksmith association. In many African countries, blacksmiths work alone. They do not share information or negotiate together. If you form an association, you can buy steel in bulk, share tools, and lobby for better policies. You can also create a brand that outlives you. A brand is a promise. If your brand stands for quality, honesty, and fair prices, people will buy from you even after you retire. You can pass the brand to your children or to your best apprentice. Finally, think about the African context. Africa has a rich history of blacksmithing. In many cultures, the blacksmith is a respected figure. The blacksmith makes the tools that farmers use, the weapons that hunters carry, and the knives that families use every day. You are part of that tradition. But you are also part of the future. You can use modern tools and modern marketing. You can sell to the world. You can create jobs. You can build wealth. You can make beautiful things that last for generations. So take this training and use it. Start where you are. Use what you have. Do what you can. And never stop learning. Thank you for completing Module 5. Now go make something sharp.

Practical Reference: Steel Identification, Forge Fuels, and Workshop Economics for African Knife Blacksmiths

This practical reference lesson consolidates the technical and commercial knowledge a working African blacksmith needs to source steel reliably, choose and manage forge fuel, and keep the workshop profitable. It is written for smiths operating in both urban informal markets and rural settings, where supply chains are unpredictable and cash flow is tight. The focus is on decisions that protect your margin: what steel to buy, what fuel to burn, and what to charge.

1. Identifying Usable Steel in African Markets

Most African knife smiths work with recycled steel: vehicle leaf springs (5160 / 65G spring steel), coil springs, ball bearings (52100), files (W1 / 1095), and machete blanks. The problem is that scrap yards and jua kali markets rarely label material. A smith who cannot identify steel wastes fuel and time on unhardenable or brittle stock. The practical tests below require only a grinder, a magnet, and a hammer.

Charcoal and coal forges produce carbon monoxide (CO), a colourless, odourless gas that kills without warning. Never forge inside a closed room, and never sleep near a lit forge. WHO guidelines set indoor CO exposure limits far below what an unventilated forge produces. Always work in a well-ventilated area or outdoors with the fire downwind of your face. Wear leather gloves and eye protection when grinding or quenching; hot scale and oil splashes cause serious burns. Quenching oils can ignite â keep a lid and a fire blanket within reach. If you feel dizzy, headachy, or nauseous, leave the area immediately and get fresh air.

  3. Cut and normalize spring steel before forging to relieve stress and prevent cracks.
  4. Measure fuel use per session so you know your true hourly forging cost.
