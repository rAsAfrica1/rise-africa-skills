Lessons

1. Identifying Potential Founding Members

Start by listing farmers you already know who sell the same crop or product. For example, if you grow maize in Nakuru, Kenya, target at least 15 farmers within 10 km. Look for people who already work together informallymaybe they share transport to market or borrow tools. These are your best founding members because trust exists. Use a simple notebook to record names, phone numbers, farm size, and main crop. Aim for a mix: 5 large farmers (2+ hectares), 8 medium (0.52 ha), and 2 small (under 0.5 ha). This balance prevents the group from being dominated by one person. Also include at least 3 women and 2 youth under 35cooperatives with diverse leadership access more grants and training. Avoid people with a history of defaulting on loans or conflicts in other groups. Ask around at the local market or input shop. Once you have a list of 2530 potential members, invite them to a informal meeting at a central place, like a church hall or under a mango tree. Provide tea and mandazi. Explain that you are forming a cooperative to buy inputs in bulk and sell together. Ask each person to bring a friend who farms. After the meeting, follow up with phone calls within 3 days. Keep a simple sign-up sheet with name, ID number, phone, and signature. Do not collect money yetjust gauge interest. If at least 20 people sign up, you have a strong founding team. If fewer, expand your search to nearby villages or a different crop. Remember: founding members must be willing to attend monthly meetings and contribute 5001000 KES or equivalent for initial registration fees.

2. Roles and Responsibilities of a Steering Committee

A steering committee is a temporary team of 57 people who organize the cooperative until formal elections. Their main job is to register the cooperative, open a bank account, and draft bylaws. Assign clear roles: Chairperson (leads meetings, represents the group), Secretary (keeps minutes, handles correspondence), Treasurer (collects fees, keeps receipts), and three members who help with mobilization and logistics. For example, in a 30-member maize cooperative in Tanzania, the chairperson should be a respected farmer with at least 5 years of experience. The secretary must be literate in English or the local official language to fill government forms. The treasurer must be trustworthysomeone who has handled money before, like a shopkeeper or church treasurer. Each role has specific tasks: Chairperson calls meetings at least twice a month, Secretary records attendance and decisions, Treasurer collects 2,000 TZS per member as registration fee and deposits it in a bank within 48 hours. The committee also drafts a simple constitution: name, objectives, membership rules, meeting schedule, and dispute resolution steps. They must consult all founding members before finalizing. The steering committee serves for 36 months, then organizes a general assembly to elect permanent leaders. During this time, they report progress at every meeting. If a committee member misses three meetings without excuse, the group can replace them. Keep all records in a locked box or a plastic folder. After registration, the committee hands over all documents to the elected board. This prevents confusion and builds trust.

3. Recruiting Members and Building Trust

Recruiting is not just about numbersit's about building trust. Start with a clear incentive: tell farmers they will save 2030% on fertilizer if they buy together. For example, a cooperative in Uganda bought NPK fertilizer at 80,000 UGX per 50 kg bag instead of 110,000 UGX retail. That saves 30,000 UGX per bag. Share this math at every meeting. Use a simple recruitment script: 'We are forming a cooperative to buy inputs and sell maize together. You pay 5,000 UGX registration and attend monthly meetings. In return, you get cheaper inputs and a better market price.' Recruit in personvisit farms, attend market days, and speak at church or mosque gatherings. Bring a sign-up sheet and a receipt book. Collect the registration fee on the spot and give a receipt. Trust grows when people see money handled transparently. Open a bank account with two signatories (chairperson and treasurer) and show the bank slip at the next meeting. Post a written list of all members and fees paid on a wall or tree. Hold a 'trust-building' event: a free training on climate-smart farming or a group visit to a successful cooperative. For every 10 new members, assign a 'buddy' from the founding team to answer questions. Address rumors immediatelyif someone says the treasurer stole money, call a meeting and show the bank statements. After three months, aim for 50100 members. Keep a waiting list if you exceed 100. Never pressure anyone to join. Trust is built slowly: consistent meetings, honest money handling, and visible benefits like a bulk purchase of seeds at 15% discount.

4. Facilitating Initial Organizing Meetings

Your first organizing meeting should happen within two weeks of identifying founding members. Choose a neutral, accessible place: a community hall, school classroom, or a member's compound. Set the time for after harvest or on a market day when people are already in town. Send reminders by SMS or WhatsApp two days before. At the meeting, start with a prayer or a short icebreaker. Have a sign-in sheet with name, phone, and signature. The chairperson opens with a clear agenda written on a flipchart or blackboard: 1) Welcome and purpose, 2) Explain cooperative benefits, 3) Discuss registration fees, 4) Form steering committee, 5) Set next meeting date. Keep the meeting to 90 minutes maximum. Use simple languageno jargon. For example, say 'we will buy fertilizer together to get a lower price' instead of 'bulk procurement synergies.' Encourage questions. If someone disagrees, write their concern on the board and discuss it. Make decisions by show of hands, but record any strong objections. At the end, summarize action items: who will open a bank account, who will draft bylaws, who will collect fees. Assign a timekeeper to keep you on track. After the meeting, share minutes via WhatsApp or read them at the next meeting. For the second meeting, invite a local cooperative officer or NGO worker to explain registration steps. Provide tea and a small snack if possibleit increases attendance. Document everything in a notebook. If attendance drops below 15, call each absent member to ask why. Adjust the meeting time or location if needed. Consistency is key: hold meetings on the same day each month, like the first Saturday.

5. Conflict Resolution During Early Stages

Conflicts are normal in any group. In early cooperative stages, common fights are about money, leadership, or unequal work. For example, a treasurer might be accused of spending 50,000 KES without receipts. Or two members might argue over who gets to be chairperson. Handle conflict quickly and openly. First, listen to both sides separately. Then bring them together in a neutral place with a mediatorsomeone respected who is not in the steering committee. Use a simple method: each person states their complaint without interruption, then they suggest a solution. Write down the agreement and have both sign. If it's about money, demand receipts and bank statements. If the treasurer cannot account for funds, ask them to repay within 7 days or step down. For leadership fights, remind everyone that the steering committee is temporaryelections will happen in 36 months. If two people want the same role, hold a secret ballot at the next general meeting. For personal conflicts, separate the people into different subcommittees. Set clear rules in your bylaws: no insults, no violence, no stealing. If someone breaks a rule, the committee can suspend them for one meeting. For serious issues like theft, report to local authorities. Always keep a written record of the conflict and resolution. After resolving, hold a short 'peace meeting' with tea to rebuild trust. Train two members as peacemakers using free online guides from Search for Common Ground or local NGOs. Prevention is better: rotate roles, keep all money in a bank, and share minutes. If conflicts persist, invite a professional mediator from the district cooperative office.

6. Developing a Shared Vision and Mission Statement

A vision is where you want to be in 510 years. A mission is what you do every day to get there. Both must be written by the members, not copied from a book. Hold a special 2-hour workshop with all founding members. Start by asking: 'What do we want our cooperative to look like in 5 years?' Write every answer on a flipchart. Common answers: 'We sell 100 tons of maize per year,' 'Every member has a bank account,' 'We own a warehouse,' 'Our children go to school.' Group similar ideas. Then vote on the top three. Turn them into a vision statement, e.g., 'To be a leading maize cooperative in Nakuru County, with 200 members earning 50% more income by 2030.' For the mission, ask: 'What do we do every day?' Answers might be: 'We buy inputs in bulk,' 'We train members on farming,' 'We sell together.' Combine into one sentence: 'Our mission is to reduce farming costs and increase market access for smallholder maize farmers through collective buying, training, and fair selling.' Write both statements in the local language and English. Paint them on a signboard at the meeting place. Read them at every meeting. Review them every year. Make sure they are realisticif you have 30 members, don't say you'll have 10,000. Use numbers: 'increase income by 30%,' 'reach 100 members.' Share the vision with new recruits. It builds pride and direction. If members disagree, facilitate a discussion until everyone can say 'I agree' or 'I can live with it.' Never let one person write the vision alone. The process of writing together is as important as the final words.

<h1>Practical Reference: Building the Founding Team</h1>
<p><strong>Module context:</strong> Agricultural Cooperative Formation RISE AFRICA Skills Business-first track</p>

<p>This practical reference consolidates the six lessons in the <em>Building the Founding Team</em> module into a single working document. It is written for promoters, extension officers, agribusiness advisors, and lead farmers who are moving from an idea ("we should form a cooperative") to a legally registered, functioning agricultural cooperative with a competent founding team. The emphasis is on decisions, documents, and deadlines the things that determine whether the cooperative trades, aggregates, and negotiates contracts, or dissolves within two seasons.</p>

<h2>1. Why the Founding Team Decides the Cooperative's Fate</h2>
<p>In agricultural cooperatives across Africa, the founding team performs three functions that no later committee can easily repair: it defines the membership boundary (who is in, who is out), it allocates initial roles and liabilities, and it sets the first norms of trust and accountability. Research on cooperative development consistently shows that weak governance at formation unclear membership, dominant founders, absent records is the strongest predictor of later failure. The practical implication is simple: spend disproportionate time on the founding team before registration, not after.</p>

<h2>2. Identifying Potential Founding Members</h2>
<p>Start with a commercial logic, not a social one. Ask: what volume, quality, and frequency of produce can we aggregate, and who controls that produce? Founding members should be drawn from farmers who share the same value chain, the same agro-ecological zone, and roughly the same market window. Mixed value chains at formation (e.g., maize plus dairy plus poultry) create governance noise before the cooperative has any trading record.</p>
<p>Use a simple screening matrix. Score each candidate on four dimensions: production capacity, willingness to contribute capital or in-kind, reliability in previous group activity, and proximity to the aggregation point. Aim for a founding group of 1530 members for most smallholder cooperatives large enough to meet registration thresholds in most jurisdictions, small enough to hold a genuine conversation.</p>

<h2>3. Roles and Responsibilities of a Steering Committee</h2>
<p>The steering committee is a temporary, pre-registration body. Its job is to prepare the cooperative for registration and hand over to an elected board. Keep it small: five to seven people, with clearly separated functions. Do not allow one person to hold the chair, treasurer, and secretary roles simultaneously this is the single most common governance defect in emerging cooperatives.</p>

<h2>4. Recruiting Members and Building Trust</h2>
<p>Trust in a new cooperative is built through three visible behaviours: money is accounted for publicly, decisions are minuted, and promises are kept on schedule. Recruit in concentric circles start with the founding group, then invite neighbours who meet the screening criteria, then open to the wider community once the constitution is drafted. Each new member should receive a written membership agreement stating share value, delivery obligations, and exit rules.</p>
<p>Practical trust-building actions: publish a one-page financial summary at every meeting; rotate who counts the money; record every contribution in a receipt book with carbon copies; and hold meetings at the same time and place each month. In many African contexts, mobile money records (M-Pesa, MTN MoMo, Airtel Money) provide a low-cost audit trail that members can verify independently.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #b00020;background:#fdecea;padding:12px 16px;margin:18px 0;border-radius:6px;">
<strong style="color:#b00020;"> Safety and integrity warning:</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;">Never collect share capital or membership fees in cash without issuing a numbered, signed receipt and recording the entry in a cash book the same day. Unrecorded cash is the leading cause of founding-team disputes and, in many jurisdictions, constitutes a criminal offence. Where possible, open a cooperative bank or mobile-money account in the name of the group not in the name of any individual before collecting any money. Do not allow a single founder to hold both the treasurer role and sole access to funds.</p>
</div>

<h2>5. Facilitating Initial Organizing Meetings</h2>
<p>Hold at least four organizing meetings before registration. Each should have a written agenda, a timekeeper, and minutes circulated within 48 hours. A workable sequence:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Meeting 1 Purpose and scope:</strong> agree the value chain, geographic boundary, and target market. Output: a one-page concept note.</li>
<li><strong>Meeting 2 Membership and shares:</strong> agree share value, minimum delivery, and exit rules. Output: draft membership agreement.</li>
<li><strong>Meeting 3 Governance:</strong> agree the constitution outline, steering committee roles, and election date. Output: draft constitution.</li>
<li><strong>Meeting 4 Vision, mission, and registration:</strong> adopt the vision and mission, approve the constitution, and mandate the steering committee to file registration documents.</li>
</ol>
<p>Facilitation tip: use a "parking lot" flipchart for issues that cannot be resolved in the meeting. This prevents one dispute from consuming the entire agenda and gives the chair a structured way to defer without dismissing.</p>

<h2>6. Conflict Resolution During Early Stages</h2>
<p>Early conflicts typically fall into four categories: contested leadership, disputed money, unequal contribution, and value-chain disagreement. Address them with a three-step protocol:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Name the issue in writing.</strong> The secretary records the dispute and the facts as presented by both sides.</li>
<li><strong>Apply the agreed rule.</strong> If the constitution or membership agreement covers the issue, apply it. If not, the steering committee proposes a rule and the general meeting adopts it.</li>
<li><strong>Escalate only if necessary.</strong> Use a neutral mediator (extension officer, district cooperative officer, or respected local leader) before involving formal legal processes.</li>
</ol>
<p>Document every resolution in the minutes. A cooperative that resolves its first three conflicts in writing has effectively built its dispute-resolution system for the next decade.</p>

<h2>7. Developing a Shared Vision and Mission Statement</h2>
<p>The vision states what the cooperative intends to become; the mission states what it does, for whom, and how. Both should be short enough to be recited at the start of every meeting. Test them against three questions: does it name the value chain? does it name the beneficiary? does it imply a measurable outcome?</p>
<p><strong>Weak:</strong> "To improve the lives of farmers."<br>
<strong>Stronger:</strong> "To aggregate and market quality maize for our members in [district], achieving a stable price premium of at least 10% above the local spot market by [year]."</p>
<p>Adopt the vision and mission by a formal vote at Meeting 4, record them in the constitution, and display them at the aggregation point. They become the reference point for every later decision about membership, investment, and contracts.</p>

<ul>

<li> Steering committee of 57 members with separated chair, secretary, and treasurer roles.</li>
<li> Membership register, cash book, and receipt book opened before any money is collected.</li>
<li> Group bank or mobile-money account opened in the cooperative's name, not an individual's.</li>
<li> At least four organizing meetings held, each with agenda and minutes circulated within 48 hours.</li>
<li> Draft constitution and membership agreement reviewed and approved by the general meeting.</li>
