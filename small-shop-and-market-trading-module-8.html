1. Module 8 Lesson 1: Setting Up Your Shop for Profit in African Markets

Welcome to Module 8, where we move from planning to practical action. In this first lesson, we focus on setting up your small shop or market stall so that it is positioned for profit from day one. Whether you are selling in a busy urban market in Lagos, a roadside stall in Kampala, a container shop in Nairobi, or a table in a rural weekly market in Malawi, the principles are the same: location, layout, stock, and record keeping decide whether you eat profit or eat capital. Start with location. In African markets, foot traffic is king. A spot near the entrance, near a transport stage, near a mosque or church, or near a popular food seller can double your daily sales. If you sell perishable goods like tomatoes, onions, or fish, you need shade and quick access to water. If you sell airtime, mobile money, or phone accessories, you need security and a visible counter. Before you pay rent, stand at the spot for three days and count people passing between 7am and 6pm. Count how many stop, how many buy, and what they buy. This simple count will tell you more than any landlord promise. Next, layout. A cluttered stall hides your best products. Arrange your highest-margin items at eye level. Put cheap impulse items like sweets, biscuits, or sachet water near the front where children and hurried buyers can grab them. Keep a clear walking path so customers can enter and exit without squeezing. Use vertical space: hang bags, belts, or phone cases on the wall or on a simple wooden frame. In many African markets, a small wooden pallet or a plastic crate turned upside down makes a strong display table. Paint your stall in bright, clean colours that match your main product. A blue stall for a fish seller, a green stall for vegetables, a yellow stall for snacks. Colour attracts attention and helps customers remember you. Stock is your engine. Do not copy what everyone else is selling unless you can sell it cheaper or better. Instead, find a gap. If three people sell the same rice, you can sell rice plus a small cooking oil sachet plus a matchbox as a meal bundle. If everyone sells cold drinks, you can sell cold drinks plus charged phones or a place to sit. Start with a narrow range of fast-moving products. Ten to fifteen items is enough for a new shop. Track which item sells every day and which item sits for a week. Cut the slow movers and reinvest in the fast movers. In Africa, capital is often small and turnover is more important than big margins. A 10 percent profit that repeats five times a week beats a 50 percent profit that happens once a month. Pricing must be simple and fair. Walk around your market and note the price of your main competitors. Then decide: will you be the cheapest, the most convenient, or the most trusted? You cannot be all three at once. If you are cheapest, you must buy in bulk and control waste. If you are most convenient, you must open early and close late, and accept mobile money. If you are most trusted, you must never sell expired goods, never cheat on weight, and always give a receipt when asked. Many African customers will pay a small premium for a seller who is honest and consistent. That reputation is your real shop. Record keeping starts on day one. Use a simple exercise book. Write the date, opening cash, money received, money paid out, and closing cash. Write every debt given and every debt paid. At the end of the day, count your cash and compare it with your book. If the numbers do not match, find out why before you sleep. This habit separates a trader from a business owner. You do not need a computer or an accountant. You need a pen, a book, and honesty with yourself. Security is also part of setup. In many African markets, theft and fire are real risks. Keep daily cash low by banking or using mobile money. Do not display all your stock at once; keep backup stock hidden and locked. If you sell flammable goods like kerosene or cooking gas, keep a bucket of sand or water nearby and never store fuel inside a sleeping room. Talk to your neighbours and agree on a simple watch system. A market is a community, and community eyes protect everyone. Finally, think about your customer. In Africa, shopping is social. Greet every customer. Learn names. Ask about their family. Offer a small extra like a free pepper or a plastic bag. These small touches turn a one-time buyer into a weekly buyer. A weekly buyer is worth more than a one-time big sale. Your goal in the first month is not to get rich. Your goal is to learn what sells, when it sells, and who buys it. By the end of this lesson, you should be able to choose a location, arrange your stall, select your first stock, set simple prices, and keep a daily record. Do these five things well, and you will have a shop that survives the first year. Most small shops in Africa fail not because of bad luck but because of no records, no focus, and no customer relationship. You now have the tools to avoid that. In the next lesson, we will look at buying and negotiating with suppliers so your cost price stays low and your profit stays healthy.

2. Module 8 Lesson 2: Buying, Negotiating and Managing Suppliers

In this lesson, we focus on the money you spend, because every shilling, naira, cedi, or rand you save when buying is a shilling, naira, cedi, or rand added to your profit. Many small traders in Africa focus only on selling and ignore buying. That is a mistake. Your supplier is your silent partner. If your supplier is expensive, slow, or dishonest, your shop will struggle no matter how hard you sell. Start by knowing your true cost. When you buy a crate of tomatoes, do not just count the price of the crate. Add transport, market fees, loading, and the cost of spoiled tomatoes. If you buy a bale of second-hand clothes, add transport, sorting time, and the clothes you cannot sell. This is your landed cost. Only when you know your landed cost can you set a selling price that gives real profit. Many traders think they are making 30 percent when they are actually making 5 percent after costs. Write your landed cost in your book for every product. Next, find more than one supplier. In most African markets, there is a main wholesale market, a rural collection point, and sometimes a direct farmer or manufacturer. For example, in Accra you might buy from Makola, from a farm near Dodowa, and from a factory in Tema. In Nairobi you might buy from Wakulima, from a farm in Kiambu, and from an importer in Industrial Area. Having three sources gives you power. When one raises prices, you can shift. When one runs out, you can still stock. When one gives credit, you can compare terms. Never depend on a single supplier for your best-selling product. Negotiation is a skill, not a fight. In African trading culture, relationship comes before price. Greet your supplier. Ask about business and family. Buy small amounts first and pay on time. After two or three good purchases, you have earned the right to ask for a better price. When you negotiate, do it privately, not in front of other buyers. Be specific: I will buy ten crates every Monday if you give me this price. Suppliers respect volume and consistency more than begging. If you cannot get a lower price, ask for a better term: free delivery, extra units, or seven days to pay. A free delivery can be worth more than a small discount. Credit is a powerful tool but also a trap. Many African traders grow because a supplier gives them stock on credit and they pay after selling. This is called trust-based trade and it is common in markets from Kano to Kumasi to Dar es Salaam. Use credit to grow, not to survive. Only take credit for goods you are confident you can sell within the agreed time. Always pay on the agreed day, even if you must borrow from another source. Your reputation for paying on time is worth more than the credit itself. If you delay, you will lose the supplier and the word will spread. Keep a simple supplier book. For each supplier, write the name, phone number, product, price, credit terms, and payment date. Note the quality: are the tomatoes fresh or soft? Are the phone chargers original or fake? Are the bags strong or weak? Over time, you will see which supplier gives the best value, not just the lowest price. Cheap goods that fail or spoil are more expensive than slightly costly goods that sell well. Quality control is part of buying. In many African markets, fake and expired goods are a serious problem. Check expiry dates on food, drinks, and medicine. Check seals on cosmetics and phone accessories. Check weight on rice, sugar, and flour. If you find a bad batch, return it immediately and ask for a replacement or refund. Do not pass bad goods to your customers. One bad sale can destroy years of trust. Also, think about storage. If you buy in bulk to get a lower price, you need a dry, safe place to keep stock. Moisture ruins flour, sugar, and maize. Heat ruins chocolate and some cosmetics. Rats and insects ruin grains and clothes. Use airtight containers, pallets, and regular cleaning. A small investment in storage saves a large amount of stock. Transport is another cost you can control. In many African cities, transport is expensive and unpredictable. If you buy from a distant market, group your purchases with other traders and hire one vehicle. If you buy from a nearby farm, use a bicycle, motorbike, or pickup and agree on a fixed rate. Avoid buying small amounts daily if transport costs eat your margin. Buy in bulk for fast movers and in small amounts for slow movers. This is called matching your buying to your selling speed. Finally, build a win-win relationship. A good supplier will warn you when prices are about to rise, when a new product is coming, or when a competitor is cheating. A good supplier will sometimes give you extra stock when you are short. To earn this, be a good buyer. Pay on time. Do not argue over small amounts. Do not switch suppliers for a tiny discount. Bring new business when you can. In African markets, trust is built over months and years, and it is the cheapest form of capital you will ever get. By the end of this lesson, you should know your landed cost, have at least two suppliers for your main product, understand how to negotiate price and terms, and keep a supplier book. Do these things and your cost price will fall, your profit will rise, and your shop will become stronger than the shops around you that buy blindly. In the next lesson, we will look at selling, customer service, and how to turn a market stall into a trusted brand.

3. Module 8 Lesson 3: Selling, Customer Service and Building Trust

Selling is not just handing over goods and taking money. In African markets, selling is a relationship. The trader who greets, remembers, and helps will always beat the trader who sits silently behind a pile of goods. In this lesson, we learn how to sell more, serve better, and build a name that customers trust. Start with the first five seconds. When a customer approaches, stop what you are doing, look at them, and greet them in a friendly voice. In many African cultures, the greeting is not optional; it is the door to the sale. Use the local language if you can. A simple good morning, welcome, or how are you today opens the conversation. Ask what they are looking for. Do not push. Listen. If they say they are just looking, let them look and stay available. Many sales happen after the customer feels comfortable, not after the first question. Next, know your products. You must be able to explain what each item does, how to use it, and why it is good value. If you sell phone chargers, know which phones they fit. If you sell seeds, know the planting season and the expected yield. If you sell cooking oil, know the difference between refined and unrefined. A customer who trusts your knowledge will come back and will bring friends. Ignorance loses sales. If you do not know an answer, say you will find out and then actually find out. Honesty builds trust faster than pretending. Price clearly. In many African markets, prices are negotiated, but not every customer wants to bargain. Some want a fixed, fair price. You can do both. Put a small sign with your price for common items. For others, quote a fair price and be willing to move a little for bulk or for a loyal customer. Never start with a very high price hoping to cheat a stranger. Word travels fast in a market. If you are known as a cheater, customers will avoid you even if your goods are good. A fair price with a small profit repeated many times is better than one big cheating profit that kills your name. Offer choices. Customers like to compare. If you sell rice, offer small, medium, and large packs. If you sell soap, offer single bars and multipacks. If you sell drinks, offer cold and room temperature. Choices make the customer feel in control and increase the chance they buy something. But do not overwhelm. Three choices per product is enough. Too many choices confuse and slow the sale. Handle complaints well. In any business, something will go wrong. A customer will find a torn bag, a dead phone charger, or a wrong measurement. How you handle it decides whether you keep that customer. Listen without arguing. Apologise sincerely. Fix the problem quickly, even if it costs you a little. A customer whose complaint is solved becomes more loyal than a customer who never had a problem. Never blame the customer in public. Take them aside, solve it, and protect their dignity. That is how you build a reputation in an African market. Use small extras. A free plastic bag, a free matchbox, a free sweet for a child, or a small discount for a regular buyer costs very little but creates a strong memory. In many African markets, these small gifts are called dash, kitu kidogo, or small-small. They are not bribes; they are relationship builders. Use them wisely and honestly. Do not promise what you cannot give. Keep your word. If you say you will have a product tomorrow, have it. If you say you will deliver, deliver. If you say the price is fixed, keep it fixed for that customer. Broken promises spread faster than good service. A trader who keeps small promises will be trusted with bigger business. Use mobile money and simple technology. In many African countries, customers now prefer to pay with mobile money, cards, or bank transfer. If you can accept these, you remove a barrier. A customer who has no cash but has mobile money can still buy. Keep a simple record of every mobile money payment. Do not mix business money with personal money. If you can, print a small receipt or send a confirmation message. This makes you look professional and helps with disputes. Open on time and stay open. Customers learn your hours. If you open at 7am, be there at 7am. If you close at 8pm, do not close at 6pm because you are tired. Consistency is a form of advertising. A customer who finds you closed twice will stop coming. If you must close, put a sign with your return time and a phone number. Better still, have a trusted family member or assistant cover for you. Dress and behave professionally. You do not need expensive clothes, but you must be clean and neat. Keep your stall clean. Sweep daily. Remove rubbish. Arrange goods neatly. A clean shop signals that you care about quality. Speak politely. Do not shout, insult, or gossip with other traders while a customer waits. In many African markets, the trader who is calm and respectful attracts the best customers. Ask for feedback. At the end of a sale, ask a simple question: was everything okay? Is there anything else you need? What would you like me to stock? Customers will tell you what to sell next. They will also tell you what you are doing wrong. Listen without defending. Then act. A shop that changes based on customer feedback will always stay relevant. Build a simple customer list. If you can, write the names and phone numbers of your best customers. Send them a message when a new stock arrives or when you have a special price. Do not spam. One or two messages a month is enough. This turns a market stall into a small brand. In Africa, where trust and relationships drive trade, a trader with a customer list has a real advantage. Finally, think long term. A customer today may be a customer for ten years. Their children may become customers. Their friends may become customers. Treat every person as if they will return, because many will. The goal is not to win one sale but to win a lifetime of sales. By the end of this lesson, you should be able to greet, listen, explain, price fairly, handle complaints, and follow up. Do these things and your stall will not just sell; it will become a trusted name in your market. In the next lesson, we will look at money management, savings, and how to grow from a small stall to a bigger business.

4. Module 8 Lesson 4: Money Management, Savings and Growth

Many small shops in Africa make sales every day but never grow. The reason is simple: money comes in and money goes out with no plan. In this lesson, we learn how to manage cash, save, and grow from a small stall into a stronger business. The first rule is separate business money from personal money. This is the hardest rule for many traders, but it is the most important. If you take money from the till to buy food, pay school fees, or help a relative, your business will stay small. It is not wrong to use profit for personal needs, but you must decide how much and when. A simple method is to pay yourself a small fixed amount each week, called a salary. Everything else stays in the business. Write this salary in your book. If the business cannot pay your salary, it is not yet a business; it is a survival activity. Your goal is to make it a business. The second rule is know your numbers. Every day, write opening cash, sales, expenses, and closing cash. Every week, add them up. Every month, look at the totals. You need to know three numbers: gross profit, net profit, and cash on hand. Gross profit is sales minus the cost of goods sold. Net profit is gross profit minus all expenses like rent, transport, airtime, and your salary. Cash on hand is the money you actually have. Many traders confuse profit with cash. You can have profit but no cash if customers owe you or if you bought too much stock. You can have cash but no profit if you are selling at a loss. Know both. The third rule is control debt. In African markets, giving credit is common. It helps loyal customers and builds relationships. But too much credit kills businesses. Set a clear credit limit. For example, never let one customer owe more than the profit you make from them in a month. Write every debt in a book with the name, amount, date, and promise date. Follow up politely but firmly. If a customer keeps delaying, stop giving credit until they pay. Do not be ashamed to ask for your money. It is your capital. Also control what you owe. Supplier credit is useful, but only if you can pay on time. Never take new credit to pay old credit unless you are sure of a big sale. That is how traders fall into a debt trap. The fourth rule is save something every day. Even 100 shillings, 50 naira, or 10 rand a day adds up. Save in a separate place: a bank account, a mobile money savings wallet, a sacco, a village savings group, or a trusted table banking group. In many African communities, table banking and chama groups are excellent because they combine savings with small loans. Join one if it is honest and well managed. The goal is to build a reserve for emergencies and opportunities. An emergency is sickness, theft, fire, or a sudden price rise. An opportunity is a cheap bulk purchase, a new product, or a bigger stall. Without savings, you will miss both. The fifth rule is reinvest. As your business grows, put part of your profit back into stock, tools, and training. Buy a better display shelf. Buy a small freezer. Buy a weighing scale. Buy a phone for mobile money. Attend a short course on bookkeeping or customer service. These investments increase your capacity to sell and reduce your costs. Do not spend all profit on personal things. A trader who reinvests will overtake a trader who only consumes. The sixth rule is manage stock carefully. Too much stock ties up cash and risks spoilage or theft. Too little stock means empty shelves and lost sales. Find your balance. For fast movers, keep enough for three to seven days. For slow movers, keep enough for one to two weeks. For perishables, buy daily or every two days. Use the first-in, first-out method: sell the oldest stock first. Check expiry dates weekly. Mark down items that are close to expiry and sell them quickly. A small loss on old stock is better than a total loss. The seventh rule is plan for risk. In Africa, small businesses face many risks: theft, fire, flooding, sickness, currency changes, and sudden market closures. You cannot prevent everything, but you can reduce the damage. Keep cash low. Use mobile money. Lock stock. Insure if possible. Have a backup supplier. Have a backup plan for transport. Keep a small emergency fund. Talk to other traders and learn how they handle risk. A trader who plans for trouble survives longer than one who assumes everything will be fine. The eighth rule is set goals. A goal is a target with a date. For example: I will save enough to buy a second display table by December. I will increase daily sales by 20 percent in three months. I will open a second stall in the next market by next year. Write your goals in your book. Review them every month. Adjust them if needed. Goals turn a daily struggle into a journey with a destination. The ninth rule is separate your business identity. If possible, register your business, open a business bank account, and get a mobile money merchant number. This makes it easier to save, borrow, and grow. It also separates your personal name from your business name. In many African countries, registration is now simple and cheap. Do it when you can. It opens doors to loans, grants, and bigger customers. The tenth rule is learn continuously. Read small business books. Listen to radio programs on business. Join a traders association. Talk to successful traders and ask how they did it. Do not copy their every move, but learn their principles. Business is a skill, and skills improve with practice and study. By the end of this lesson, you should be able to separate business and personal money, track profit and cash, control debt, save daily, reinvest, manage stock, plan for risk, set goals, and keep learning. These habits will not make you rich overnight, but they will make you stable. Stability is the foundation of growth. In the next lesson, we will look at expanding your shop, adding new products, and handling the challenges of growth in African markets.

5. Module 8 Lesson 5: Expanding Your Shop and Handling Growth

You have set up your shop, learned to buy well, sell well, and manage money. Now it is time to think about growth. Growth in a small African shop does not always mean a big building or many employees. It can mean more stock, more customers, a second stall, a delivery service, or a new product line. In this lesson, we learn how to grow safely and how to handle the challenges that come with growth. The first step is to know when you are ready. Do not expand just because you are bored or because a neighbour is expanding. Expand when you have three things: steady profit, reliable supply, and a system that works without you standing there every minute. If you still cannot take one day off without sales collapsing, you are not ready to open a second stall. First, build a simple system. Write down how you open, how you serve, how you record sales, and how you close. Train a family member or trusted assistant to follow the same steps. This is called a standard operating procedure, even if it is just one page in a notebook. A business that depends only on you cannot grow. The second step is to add products carefully. Look at what your customers ask for. If many ask for bread and you do not sell it, add bread. If many ask for phone charging, add a charging station. If many ask for delivery, start small deliveries. Add one product at a time. Test it for two weeks. If it sells, keep it. If it does not, stop it. Do not add ten new products at once. That ties up cash and confuses customers. The third step is to add a second location or a second stall. In many African markets, a second stall in the same market can work if the first stall is well managed. A second stall in a different market is riskier because you cannot watch both. If you open a second stall, put a trusted person in charge and visit often. Give them clear rules on pricing, credit, and record keeping. Check their books daily at first, then weekly. Do not assume they are honest just because they are family. Trust but verify. The fourth step is to consider delivery and online selling. In many African cities, customers now order by WhatsApp, Facebook, or Instagram. You can take orders by phone and deliver by motorbike or bicycle. Start with your existing customers. Tell them you now deliver. Set a small delivery fee or a minimum order. Use mobile money for payment. This expands your market beyond your stall. It also builds a customer list that you own. The fifth step is to formalise your business. As you grow, you will need a business bank account, a tax identification number, and possibly a licence. This may feel like a burden, but it opens doors. Formal businesses can get loans, supply bigger customers, and protect their name. In many African countries, there are simplified tax regimes for small businesses. Ask your local revenue office or a business association for help. Do not avoid tax; plan for it. The sixth step is to build a team. At first, you may hire one assistant. Hire slowly and fire quickly if needed. Look for honesty, punctuality, and willingness to learn. Train them well. Pay them fairly and on time. Give them clear duties and clear limits. Do not let them give credit or change prices without your approval. A good team multiplies your capacity. A bad team destroys your business. The seventh step is to manage cash flow during growth. Growth eats cash. When you add stock, open a stall, or hire staff, money goes out before it comes back. Keep a cash buffer of at least one month of expenses. Do not use all your savings for expansion. Keep some for emergencies. If you borrow, borrow for a specific purpose and have a clear plan to repay. Avoid borrowing for personal consumption. The eighth step is to protect your brand. As you grow, your name becomes valuable. Register your business name if possible. Use consistent colours, signs, and packaging. Treat every customer well, whether they buy one item or many. A strong brand makes it easier to open new stalls and to sell new products. In African markets, a trusted name is worth more than a big shop. The ninth step is to handle competition. When you grow, others will copy you. Some will cut prices. Some will spread rumours. Do not panic. Compete on service, quality, and trust, not on price alone. Keep your costs low so you can survive a price war. Keep your customers close so they do not leave. Keep improving so you stay ahead. The tenth step is to plan for succession and exit. Every business owner will one day stop. Train someone to take over. Write down your systems. Keep your records clean. If you want to sell the business later, clean records and a strong brand will get a better price. If you want to pass it to your children, train them early. Do not let your business die with you. Growth also brings new risks. More stock means more theft. More staff means more mistakes. More customers means more complaints. More money means more people asking for loans. Prepare for these. Keep tight records. Use mobile money. Separate business and personal accounts. Set clear rules and follow them. Do not let success make you careless. Finally, remember why you started. A small shop is not just a way to make money. It is a service to your community. It provides goods, jobs, and hope. When you grow, you create more jobs and more value. Do it honestly, do it patiently, and do it with your customers in mind. By the end of this lesson, you should be able to decide when to expand, add products, open a second stall, start delivery, formalise your business, build a team, manage cash flow, protect your brand, handle competition, and plan for the future. These are the skills that turn a market stall into a lasting business. You have now completed Module 8. Go back to your shop, apply one lesson at a time, and watch your business grow. Remember: small steps, done daily, build big things.

6. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Stock Records, Pricing and Safe Handling for Small Shops and Market Traders

This Practical Reference lesson supports Module 8 by giving you a single, printable-style guide you can use every day in your shop or market stall. It focuses on three things that decide whether a small trading business survives and grows: knowing your stock, pricing for real profit, and handling food and chemicals safely. The numbers and methods here come from official sources, but they are written for the realities of African markets: cash sales, small margins, irregular supply, family labour, and customers who buy in small quantities.

Use this lesson as a reference. You do not need to memorise it. Print it, keep it near your till, and check it when you are buying stock, setting prices, or storing goods.

1. Why stock records are a profit tool, not paperwork

Many small traders lose money without realising it. The loss is not always theft. It is often spoilage, over-buying, under-pricing, or forgetting what was paid for goods. A simple stock record helps you answer four questions: What did I buy? What did it cost? What did I sell? What is left?

You do not need a computer. A hardcover exercise book with columns works. Record the date, item, quantity bought, unit cost, total cost, quantity sold, selling price, and balance. At the end of each day, count cash and compare it with sales recorded. At the end of each week, count remaining stock. This is called a stock count. Even a rough count is better than none.

FAO and ILRI extension materials consistently show that small agribusinesses that keep basic records make better buying decisions and reduce losses. The record does not need to be perfect. It needs to be honest and regular.

2. Pricing for real profit

Profit is not the same as cash in your hand. If you buy a crate of tomatoes for 10,000 and sell all of them for 12,000, you have 2,000 gross profit. But you may have paid transport, market fees, a helper, and lost some tomatoes to spoilage. Your real profit is smaller. If you spent 1,500 on those costs, your real profit is only 500. That is a 5% net margin, which is thin.

A practical pricing rule for small shops and market traders is:

Selling price = unit cost + transport and fees per unit + spoilage allowance + labour + target profit

Spoilage allowance matters most for fresh produce, bread, milk, and cooked food. If one in ten items spoils, your spoilage allowance is at least 10% of unit cost. If you ignore it, you are selling at a loss without knowing.

For packaged goods with a printed price, you may not be able to change the price much. In that case, negotiate a better buying price, buy in larger volume when cash allows, or choose products with better margins. Do not compete only on price. Compete on reliability, cleanliness, and trust.

SAFETY WARNING: Food, chemicals and medicines

Never store pesticides, cleaning chemicals, or veterinary medicines next to food, drinks, or animal feed. Even closed containers can leak or be mistaken for food or drink. Keep them in a separate, locked or clearly marked place. Never repurpose food or drink bottles for chemicals. Never sell food that has been in contact with chemicals. If a customer becomes ill after buying food from you, stop selling that item immediately, keep a sample if possible, and seek guidance from your local health or veterinary authority. Wash hands with soap and clean water before handling food, after using the toilet, and after handling animals or chemicals. Keep raw and cooked food separate. Cook food thoroughly. Keep food at safe temperatures. Use safe water and raw materials. These are the WHO Five Keys to Safer Food.

Source: WHO Five Keys to Safer Food; FAO/WHO Codex Alimentarius.

5. Quick Reference Checklist

  • Record every purchase and sale the same day. Do not wait until evening if you are tired. Write it when it happens.
  • Know your unit cost. Include transport, market fees, and spoilage allowance before you set a selling price.
  • Count stock weekly. Compare physical stock with your record. Investigate any difference.
  • Separate food from chemicals and medicines. Use a locked or clearly marked cabinet. Label everything.
  • Check expiry dates weekly. Rotate stock so older items sell first. Remove expired goods immediately.
  • Keep the shop clean and dry. Sweep daily, keep goods off the floor, and control pests without contaminating food.
  • Wash hands and keep utensils clean. Follow the WHO Five Keys to Safer Food at all times.
  • Review your margins monthly. If a product is not making real profit, change the price, change the supplier, or stop selling it.
