Lessons

1. Mechanical Recycling: Shredding, Sorting, and Pelletizing

Mechanical recycling is the most common way to turn plastic waste into new products. It starts with shredding. You feed sorted plastic into a shredder or granulator. A small machine for a workshop costs about $2,000 to $5,000. It cuts plastic into flakes the size of your fingernail. In Kenya, Mr. Green Africa uses shredders to process 5 tons of plastic per day. After shredding, you must sort by plastic type. Use a simple float-sink tank: fill it with water, add salt to increase density. PP and PE float; PET and PVC sink. You can also use a near-infrared (NIR) handheld scanner, which costs around $1,500. For small businesses, manual sorting by resin codes (1-7) is cheaper but slower. Next, wash the flakes to remove dirt, labels, and glue. A washing line with a friction washer and a drying centrifuge costs $10,000 to $20,000. Finally, pelletizing: you melt the clean flakes in an extruder at 200-260C. The molten plastic is pushed through a die and cut into small pellets. A basic pelletizer costs $8,000 to $15,000. In Nigeria, Wecyclers collects 500 tons of PET bottles monthly and sells pellets to bottlers. Pelletizing increases the value of plastic waste by 3 to 5 times. For example, a kilo of mixed plastic flakes sells for $0.20, but pellets sell for $0.60 to $1.00. Start with PET and HDPE because they have ready markets. You need a steady supply of clean, sorted plastic. Partner with hotels, schools, and estates to collect. Train your workers on safety: wear gloves, masks, and ear protection. Mechanical recycling is low-tech, profitable, and creates jobs. With $15,000, you can set up a small line processing 1 ton per day.

2. Chemical Recycling: Advanced Techniques and Limitations

Chemical recycling breaks plastic down into its original building blocks (monomers) or into fuel-like oils. It handles mixed and dirty plastics that mechanical recycling cannot. There are three main methods: pyrolysis, gasification, and depolymerization. Pyrolysis heats plastic to 400-600C without oxygen. It produces pyrolysis oil, gas, and char. A small pyrolysis plant (10 tons per day) costs $100,000 to $300,000. In South Africa, companies like CSIR are testing pyrolysis on agricultural plastic waste. The oil can be sold as industrial fuel for $0.40 to $0.60 per liter. Gasification heats plastic to 700-1200C with limited oxygen, producing syngas (carbon monoxide and hydrogen). Syngas can generate electricity or make diesel. Depolymerization uses chemicals or heat to turn PET back into its original monomers (ethylene glycol and terephthalic acid). This is done at 200-250C with catalysts. The monomers can be re-polymerized into virgin-quality PET. However, chemical recycling has big limitations. It is energy-intensive: pyrolysis uses 2-4 kWh per kg of plastic. It requires large scale to be profitableusually above 50 tons per day. The technology is complex and needs skilled operators. In Africa, high capital costs and unreliable electricity are major barriers. Also, mixed plastics produce inconsistent oil quality. Environmental concerns include air pollution from emissions and toxic ash. For a small business, chemical recycling is rarely viable. Instead, focus on mechanical recycling. If you have access to funding and partners, consider a pilot pyrolysis unit for hard-to-recycle plastics like multilayer films. But always check local regulations: in many African countries, pyrolysis is classified as waste incineration and requires permits. Start small with a 500 kg per day batch reactor, which costs $20,000 to $50,000. Sell oil to local industries. But be prepared for maintenance and safety challenges. Chemical recycling is not a silver bullet; it is a complement to mechanical recycling.

3. Single-Stream vs. Multi-Stream Recycling Systems

Single-stream recycling means all recyclablespaper, plastic, glass, metalare put in one bin. Multi-stream means you separate them at the source: paper in one bin, plastics in another, glass in a third. In Africa, most recycling is multi-stream because it is cheaper and creates jobs. For example, in Cairo, Egypt, the Zabbaleen (informal waste collectors) sort waste door-to-door into 8 different streams. This yields clean materials and high recycling rates (over 80% for some materials). Single-stream is common in Europe and North America, where automated MRFs do the sorting. But single-stream requires expensive equipment and can lead to contamination. In Africa, single-stream is rare because of high capital costs and low labor costs. Multi-stream has advantages: less contamination, higher quality recyclables, and more local employment. A multi-stream system needs bins, labels, and training. You can start with 3 streams: plastics, paper, and metals. Use color-coded bins: blue for plastics, green for paper, yellow for metals. In Ghana, the NGO Waste Africa runs a multi-stream program in schools, collecting 2 tons per week. Single-stream can work in high-income areas with a reliable MRF. But for most African cities, multi-stream is better. It costs less to set up: a set of 3 bins costs $30 per household, while a single-stream truck and MRF cost millions. Multi-stream also reduces water and energy use because materials are cleaner. However, multi-stream requires public participation. You must educate people to rinse containers and remove caps. In Kenya, TakaTaka Solutions uses a multi-stream system, processing 20 tons per day and employing 300 people. They sell clean PET, HDPE, and paper to local recyclers. If you start a collection business, begin with multi-stream. Partner with estates, offices, and schools. Provide bins and weekly pickups. Charge a small fee ($5 per household per month). You will earn more from selling clean materials. Single-stream is not practical for small-scale African recyclers.

4. The Role of MRFs (Materials Recovery Facilities)

A Materials Recovery Facility (MRF) is a plant where mixed recyclables are sorted into clean streams for sale. MRFs can be manual, semi-automated, or fully automated. In Africa, most MRFs are manual or semi-automated because labor is cheap and capital is scarce. A small manual MRF costs $20,000 to $50,000. It needs a concrete floor, a conveyor belt, sorting tables, and balers. A baler costs $5,000 to $15,000 and compresses materials into blocks for transport. In South Africa, the PET Recycling Company (PETCO) supports MRFs that process 1,000 tons per month. In Nigeria, Wecyclers operates a MRF in Lagos that handles 500 tons per month. The MRF process: trucks bring mixed recyclables. Workers sort on a conveyor, pulling out plastics, paper, glass, and metals. Magnets remove steel; eddy currents remove aluminum. Plastics are further sorted by type (PET, HDPE, PP) using manual inspection or NIR scanners. Paper is separated into cardboard, office paper, and newsprint. Glass is sorted by color. Each stream is baled and sold. A MRF can increase the value of waste by 10 times. For example, mixed waste sells for $10 per ton, but sorted PET sells for $200 per ton. However, MRFs need steady input. You must secure contracts with municipalities, estates, and businesses. You also need a market for your sorted materials. In many African countries, local plastic recyclers will buy PET and HDPE. Paper goes to paper mills. Glass is often crushed and used for construction. Metals are sold to scrap dealers. To start a MRF, begin small: a manual sorting line with 10 workers can process 5 tons per day. Cost: $30,000 for equipment and setup. Train workers on safety and quality. Keep records of input and output. Partner with local government for waste supply. MRFs are the backbone of recycling in Africa. They create jobs, reduce landfill waste, and supply clean feedstock to manufacturers.

5. Innovations in Recycling: AI Sorting and Robotics

AI sorting and robotics are changing recycling in Africa, but adoption is still early. AI sorting uses cameras and machine learning to identify materials on a conveyor belt. A camera takes images, and software classifies each item by type, color, and shape. Then a robotic arm or air jet pushes it into the correct bin. This can sort 3 to 5 tons per hour with 95% accuracy. A small AI sorting unit costs $50,000 to $150,000. In South Africa, the CSIR has piloted AI sorting for plastic bottles. In Kenya, a startup called Mr. Green Africa uses basic AI to sort PET by color. Robotics for recycling includes delta robots and pick-and-place arms. They cost $30,000 to $80,000 per arm. They are best for high-value sorting, like separating clear PET from colored PET. In Africa, high cost and unreliable electricity are barriers. But you can start with low-cost innovations: smartphone apps that use AI to identify plastic resin codes. For example, the app Plastic Scanner uses your phone camera to tell you if a plastic is PET, HDPE, or PP. It is free. You can also use cheap sensors: a near-infrared (NIR) sensor connected to an Arduino board can detect plastic types. A DIY NIR sorter costs $500 to $1,000. In Ghana, a group built a NIR sorter that separates PET from HDPE at 200 kg per hour. For a small business, AI sorting is not necessary yet. But you can prepare by keeping your sorting manual but organized. Use color-coded bins and train workers. As your volume grows, consider a used AI sorter from Europe or China. Prices are falling. By 2025, a basic AI sorter may cost $20,000. The future of recycling in Africa will combine cheap labor with smart tools. AI will not replace workers; it will help them sort faster and earn more. Start by learning about AI sorting through free online courses. Then pilot a small unit if you have funding.

6. Lifecycle Analysis of Recycled Materials

Lifecycle analysis (LCA) measures the environmental impact of a product from cradle to grave. For recycled materials, LCA compares recycling to making new products from virgin materials. The results show big savings. For example, recycling 1 ton of PET plastic saves 1.5 tons of CO2 compared to making virgin PET. Recycling 1 ton of aluminum saves 9 tons of CO2. Recycling 1 ton of paper saves 3 tons of CO2. These numbers matter for African businesses because they can sell carbon credits or attract green funding. To do a simple LCA, you list all inputs and outputs for each stage: collection, sorting, washing, pelletizing, and transport. Then you calculate energy use, water use, and emissions. You can use free tools like the OpenLCA software or the EPAs WARM model. For a small recycler, focus on three key metrics: energy per kg of recycled material, water per kg, and CO2 saved per kg. For example, mechanical recycling of PET uses 0.5 kWh per kg, while virgin PET uses 2.5 kWh per kg. Recycling HDPE uses 0.3 kWh per kg vs. 1.8 kWh for virgin. These savings are real. In Nigeria, a recycler that processes 1 ton of PET per day saves 1.5 tons of CO2 dailythat is 547 tons per year. You can sell this as a carbon offset for $5 to $15 per ton. That is $2,700 to $8,200 extra income per year. LCA also shows where you can improve. For example, washing uses a lot of water. You can recycle wash water or use dry cleaning methods. Transport is another big impact. Locate your facility near waste sources and buyers. In South Africa, PETCO uses LCA to show that recycled PET bottles have 70% lower carbon footprint than virgin bottles. Use LCA to market your product. Tell customers: This pellet saved 1.5 kg of CO2. That builds trust and allows you to charge a premium. Start with a simple spreadsheet. Track your energy and water bills, and calculate your savings. Then share your results with buyers and funders.

<h3>Mechanical Recycling: The Backbone of African Recycling Business</h3>

<p>Mechanical recycling shredding, sorting, washing, and pelletizing is the most commercially accessible route for African operators because it requires moderate capital, is modular, and serves local manufacturers of pipes, crates, fibres, and packaging. The critical variables are contamination rate, moisture, and polymer consistency. Each 1% increase in contamination can reduce bale value by 38%, and every additional wash step adds water, energy, and effluent costs that small operators often underestimate.</p>

<p>Shredding reduces particle size for downstream separation and densification. Wet shredding suits heavily soiled agricultural film; dry shredding suits rigid HDPE and PP. Sorting is where most value is created or destroyed: manual pre-sort on a conveyor removes large contaminants, while near-infrared (NIR) or AI-assisted optical sorters separate PET, HDPE, PP, and mixed polyolefins. Pelletizing converts flake into a saleable resin, but requires consistent melt flow index (MFI) and low moisture typically below 0.5% to avoid voids and weak products.</p>

<h3>Chemical Recycling: Advanced Techniques and Limitations</h3>

<p>Chemical recycling pyrolysis, gasification, solvolysis, and depolymerization can process mixed, multilayer, and contaminated plastics that mechanical recycling rejects. In Africa, pyrolysis of waste plastics into fuel oil has attracted interest, particularly for agricultural film and mixed packaging. However, the economics are fragile: energy input is high, output quality varies, and off-take markets for pyrolysis oil are limited outside export or industrial fuel blending. Solvolysis and depolymerization are capital-intensive and best suited to PET and polyurethane streams where virgin resin prices are high. For most African agribusinesses, chemical recycling is a complementary, not primary, strategy.</p>

<h3>Single-Stream vs. Multi-Stream Systems</h3>

<p>Single-stream collection increases participation and lowers collection cost per tonne, but raises contamination and requires more sorting technology. Multi-stream collection produces cleaner bales and higher prices but raises collection logistics and public education costs. In African cities with informal collection networks, a hybrid model often works best: single-stream for households, multi-stream for commercial and agricultural generators who can segregate at source.</p>

<h3>The Role of MRFs in African Value Chains</h3>

<p>Materials Recovery Facilities (MRFs) are the pivot between collection and manufacturing. A well-run MRF stabilises bale quality, provides traceability, and enables contracts with off-takers. In Africa, MRFs range from low-tech manual sorting sheds serving local plastic recyclers to semi-automated facilities with trommels, magnets, eddy current separators, and optical sorters. The business case depends on throughput, bale purity, and proximity to end markets. A 510 tonne/day MRF can be viable where transport costs are high and local off-takers exist.</p>

<h3>Innovations: AI Sorting and Robotics</h3>

<p>AI-enabled optical sorters use NIR and visible-spectrum cameras with machine-learning classifiers to identify polymers, colours, and shapes at high speed. Robotic pickers then remove targeted items. These systems improve purity and reduce labour, but require stable power, skilled maintenance, and capital. For African operators, the practical entry point is often a single AI optical sorter paired with manual pre-sort, rather than a fully robotic line.</p>

<h3>Lifecycle Analysis of Recycled Materials</h3>

<p>Lifecycle analysis (LCA) quantifies environmental impact from collection to end-of-life. Recycled PET typically reduces greenhouse gas emissions by 4070% compared with virgin PET, but the benefit shrinks when collection distances are long, washing is energy-intensive, or contamination causes high rejection. African LCA studies must account for informal collection, transport over poor roads, and grid emissions factors that are often coal- or diesel-heavy.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li>Confirm off-taker specification (polymer type, colour, moisture, contamination) before buying equipment.</li>
<li>Measure contamination rate at the tipping floor weekly; target below 10% for single-stream, below 5% for multi-stream.</li>
<li>Size the MRF to local collection volume, not to equipment vendor capacity claims.</li>
<li>Install magnetic separation before optical sorting to protect downstream sensors.</li>
<li>Track energy and water use per tonne of output; these determine margin more than labour cost.</li>
<li>Secure a written offtake agreement before scaling pelletizing capacity.</li>
<li>Train all operators on lock-out/tag-out and machine guarding; document drills quarterly.</li>
<li>Run a simple mass-balance audit monthly: input tonnes vs. saleable bales vs. residue.</li>
</ul>
