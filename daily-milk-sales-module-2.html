1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Welcome to Module 2. In Module 1, you learned why selling milk daily can change your life. Now we get to the heart of the business: the dairy herd formula. This formula is simple: your daily milk sales depend on three numbers: how many cows you milk, how much milk each cow gives, and how many days you sell. But the real magic is in the fourth number: your cost per litre. If you know these numbers, you can predict your profit and fix problems before they eat your money. In Africa, many small dairy farmers fail because they guess. They guess how much milk they get, guess how much feed costs, and guess why some cows give less. Guessing leads to losses. The dairy herd formula replaces guessing with counting. For example, if you have 5 cows giving 8 litres each, that's 40 litres a day. If you sell at 50 KES per litre, that's 2,000 KES daily. But if your feed, labour, and health costs are 1,500 KES, your profit is only 500 KES. Now imagine you improve feed and each cow gives 12 litres. You now have 60 litres, earning 3,000 KES, and profit jumps to 1,500 KES. That's the power of the formula. It also helps you decide when to cull a cow, when to buy feed in bulk, and when to hire help. This module will teach you the tools, the step-by-step method, common mistakes, safety, and give you a practical exercise. By the end, you will be able to calculate your own herd's daily milk sales and profit with confidence. Remember: what gets measured gets managed. Let's start measuring.

2. Tools and Materials

To use the dairy herd formula, you don't need expensive equipment. You need a few simple tools that cost little but save you big money. First, a record book. Any exercise book will do, but a hardcover one lasts longer. You will record daily milk yield per cow, feed given, and any health issues. Second, a measuring jug or a calibrated bucket. You must measure milk accurately, not by eye. A 10-litre plastic bucket with litre markings is perfect. Third, a weighing scale for feed. A simple hanging scale or a bathroom scale works. You need to know exactly how much feed each cow gets. Fourth, a chalkboard or whiteboard for your shed. Write today's milk total and feed cost so everyone sees it. Fifth, a mobile phone. Use it for a calculator, a calendar, and to take photos of records. You can also use free apps like 'Milk Manager' or 'Farm Records' if you have internet. Sixth, a notebook for individual cow cards. Each cow gets a page: her name or number, calving date, daily milk, and breeding dates. Seventh, a simple first aid kit for udder care: clean cloths, iodine, and teat dip. Eighth, a feed storage container with a lid to keep feed dry and safe from rats. Ninth, a water bucket or trough with measurements so you know water intake. Tenth, a pen and permanent marker to label cow cards. All these tools together cost less than 2,000 KES if you buy locally. In Africa, you can find them at agrovet shops or markets. The most important tool is discipline: use them every day, at the same time. Without records, the formula is just numbers on paper. With records, it becomes your roadmap to profit.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps every day to apply the dairy herd formula. Step 1: Identify each cow. Give every milking cow a number or name. Write it on a card. Step 2: Measure milk per cow. At each milking (morning and evening), use your calibrated bucket. Record the litres for each cow separately. If you milk twice, add both. Step 3: Total daily milk. Add all cows' litres. Example: Cow 1 gives 10L, Cow 2 gives 8L, Cow 3 gives 12L. Total = 30 litres. Step 4: Calculate average per cow. Divide total by number of cows. 30 3 = 10 litres per cow. Step 5: Record feed. Weigh the feed you give each cow. If you feed concentrates, note the kilograms. Also note forage (hay, silage, grass) in bundles or kg. Step 6: Calculate feed cost. Multiply kg of feed by price per kg. Example: 2 kg concentrate at 40 KES/kg = 80 KES per cow. For 3 cows, 240 KES. Step 7: Add other daily costs. Labour, water, electricity, and health care. Divide monthly costs by 30 to get daily. Example: labour 300 KES/day, water 50 KES/day, health 20 KES/day. Total other = 370 KES. Step 8: Total daily cost. Feed cost + other costs. 240 + 370 = 610 KES. Step 9: Calculate revenue. Total milk litres selling price. 30L 50 KES = 1,500 KES. Step 10: Calculate profit. Revenue total cost. 1,500 610 = 890 KES profit per day. Step 11: Calculate cost per litre. Total cost total litres. 610 30 = 20.33 KES per litre. Step 12: Compare to selling price. If selling price is 50 KES, your margin is 29.67 KES per litre. Step 13: Set targets. If you want 1,200 KES profit daily, you need to either increase litres or reduce cost per litre. Step 14: Review weekly. Look for cows giving less than average. Check feed quality. Step 15: Adjust. If a cow gives 5L but eats 2kg feed, she may be losing money. Consider culling or treating. Repeat daily. This method turns your herd into a business you control.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Not measuring milk per cow. Many farmers just look at the total bucket. Fix: Use individual cow records. You cannot manage what you don't measure. Mistake 2: Guessing feed amounts. If you feed by handfuls, you overfeed some and underfeed others. Fix: Weigh feed with a scale. Use a standard measure like a 2kg tin. Mistake 3: Ignoring cost per litre. You might sell milk at 50 KES but spend 45 KES to produce it. Fix: Calculate cost per litre weekly. If it's above 70% of selling price, find savings. Mistake 4: Feeding low-quality forage. In dry season, poor grass gives less milk. Fix: Invest in hay or silage. One bale of hay can add 2 litres per cow per day. Mistake 5: Not culling unproductive cows. A cow that gives 3 litres but eats like a 15-litre cow is a parasite. Fix: Use the formula: if a cow's daily feed cost exceeds her milk value for 3 months, sell her. Mistake 6: Water shortage. Cows need 60-100 litres of water daily. If water is limited, milk drops. Fix: Provide clean water always. A cow giving 20 litres needs 80 litres of water. Mistake 7: Poor record keeping. Records on loose papers get lost. Fix: Use a single notebook or a phone app. Back up with photos. Mistake 8: Selling milk on credit. You lose cash flow and can't buy feed. Fix: Sell for cash or mobile money. If you must give credit, limit to trusted customers and keep a ledger. Mistake 9: Ignoring cow health. Mastitis, ticks, and worms reduce milk. Fix: Dip cows weekly, deworm every 3 months, and check udders daily. Mistake 10: Overstocking. Too many cows on small land leads to feed shortage. Fix: Keep only as many cows as your land and budget can feed well. Each mistake has a fix. Start with the biggest one in your herd today.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety in dairy farming protects you, your workers, and your cows. First, personal safety. Always wear clean overalls and gumboots when handling cows. Cows can kick, especially during milking. Never stand directly behind a cow. Use a kick bar or tie her legs gently if she is nervous. Wash your hands with soap before and after milking. Second, milk safety. Clean the udder with a clean cloth and warm water before milking. Use a separate cloth for each cow to prevent mastitis spread. After milking, dip teats in iodine solution. Filter milk immediately using a clean cloth or filter. Cool milk to 4C within 2 hours if you sell to a processor. If you sell raw, keep it in a clean container and sell within 4 hours. Third, feed safety. Never feed mouldy hay or silage. Mould causes aflatoxin, which passes into milk and harms people. Store feed on pallets, off the floor, in a dry place. Keep rats out. Fourth, chemical safety. Store veterinary drugs, pesticides, and dips away from feed and milk. Use gloves when handling. Follow withdrawal periods: do not sell milk from a cow treated with antibiotics for at least 3 days after last dose. Fifth, animal welfare. Provide shade, clean bedding, and space. Cows that are stressed give less milk. Handle them calmly. Sixth, record safety. Keep records in a safe, dry place. Take photos as backup. Seventh, financial safety. Keep business money separate from household money. Pay yourself a salary. Save 10% of profit for emergencies. Eighth, best practice: milk at the same times daily, 12 hours apart. Keep a regular routine. Train your workers on these practices. Post a simple checklist on the shed wall. Review safety monthly. A safe farm is a profitable farm.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it's time to practice the dairy herd formula with your own herd or a sample herd. If you don't have cows yet, use the example below. Follow these steps and write your answers in your record book. Step 1: Choose 3 cows. If you have cows, pick three. If not, use: Cow A gives 12L, Cow B gives 8L, Cow C gives 5L daily. Step 2: Calculate total daily milk. 12+8+5 = 25 litres. Step 3: Calculate average per cow. 25 3 = 8.33 litres. Step 4: Record feed per cow. Assume each cow gets 2kg of dairy meal at 45 KES/kg, and 10kg of forage at 5 KES/kg. Feed cost per cow = (245) + (105) = 90 + 50 = 140 KES. For 3 cows, total feed = 420 KES. Step 5: Add other daily costs. Labour 200 KES, water 30 KES, health 20 KES, transport 50 KES. Total other = 300 KES. Step 6: Total daily cost = 420 + 300 = 720 KES. Step 7: Calculate revenue. Sell at 55 KES/litre. 25L 55 = 1,375 KES. Step 8: Calculate profit = 1,375 720 = 655 KES. Step 9: Calculate cost per litre = 720 25 = 28.8 KES. Step 10: Margin per litre = 55 28.8 = 26.2 KES. Step 11: Identify the least productive cow. Cow C gives 5L. Her feed cost is 140 KES. Her milk value = 555 = 275 KES. She still makes a profit of 135 KES, but less than others. Step 12: What if you improve feed for Cow C to 3kg dairy meal? Feed cost becomes (345)+50 = 185 KES. If milk increases to 8L, revenue = 440 KES, profit = 255 KES. Better. Step 13: Set a target. If you want 1,000 KES profit daily, how many litres at 55 KES with cost per litre 28.8? Profit per litre = 26.2. Need 1000 26.2 = 38.2 litres. You need 13 more litres. Step 14: Write your action plan. Example: add 1kg feed to Cow C, check water, and cull if no improvement in 30 days. Step 15: Repeat this exercise weekly with real numbers. Bring your results to the next session.

<h2>Practical Reference: The Dairy Herd Formula</h2>

<p>This practical reference sheet is the working companion to the <strong>Daily Milk Sales: Dairy Herd System</strong> course. It pulls together the numbers, tables, tools and habits that turn a herd of cows into a predictable daily milk business. Use it at the farm gate, in the milk shed, and when you review your week's sales. The goal is simple: know how many litres you can sell tomorrow, and know what it costs you to produce them.</p>

<p>In most African dairy systems from Kiambu and Nakuru in Kenya to Wakiso in Uganda, Bahati in Tanzania, and the smallholder clusters around Harare and Lusaka the difference between a profitable dairy enterprise and a break-even one is not the breed of cow alone. It is the <em>system</em>: consistent feeding, clean water, controlled breeding, reliable record-keeping, and honest cost accounting. The Dairy Herd Formula gives you a single framework to manage all of these.</p>

<ul style="margin:6px 0 0 18px;font-family:Arial,Helvetica,sans-serif;font-size:14px;color:#3d1a17;">
<li><strong>Never sell milk from a cow treated with antibiotics</strong> until the full withdrawal period stated on the drug label has passed. Residues harm consumers and can shut you out of formal markets.</li>
<li><strong>Cool milk to below 4 C within 2 hours</strong> of milking, or deliver to the collection point immediately. Warm milk is a bacterial breeding ground and spoils within hours in tropical heat.</li>
<li><strong>Wash hands and udders</strong> with clean water before milking. Use a separate clean cloth per cow where possible to avoid spreading mastitis.</li>
<li><strong>Do not use untreated river water</strong> to dilute or rinse milk. Always use potable water contamination is the fastest route to rejected deliveries.</li>
<li><strong>Store and handle pesticides, acaricides and veterinary drugs</strong> away from the milk room. Never decant chemicals into milk or feed containers.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul style="font-family:Arial,Helvetica,sans-serif;font-size:14px;line-height:1.7;">
<li> Count and record the number of <strong>cows actually in milk</strong> today not the total herd.</li>
<li> Record the <strong>morning and evening yield</strong> per cow, or at least the herd total.</li>
<li> Subtract <strong>home use and calf milk</strong> before calculating saleable litres.</li>
<li> Confirm your <strong>farm-gate or cooperative price per litre</strong> for the current week.</li>
<li> Track <strong>feed cost per litre</strong> if it exceeds 60% of the milk price, review the ration.</li>
<li> Check <strong>water availability</strong>: a milking cow needs 60100 litres of clean water per day.</li>
<li> Verify <strong>withdrawal periods</strong> for any animal under treatment before the milk goes to market.</li>
<li> Update the <strong>daily margin</strong> (income minus feed, labour and health costs) at least weekly.</li>
<li> Review <strong>lactation days</strong> and plan breeding so calving is spread to keep milk flowing year-round.</li>
</ul>
