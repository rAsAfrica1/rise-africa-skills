1. Duct System Layout and Components

Before you cut any metal, you must understand how duct systems work. A duct system moves air from a fan or air handler to rooms and back. The main parts are: the plenum (the big box on top of the air handler), trunk ducts (main lines), branch ducts (smaller lines to each room), and diffusers or grilles (where air enters or leaves). In Africa, many buildings use galvanised steel ducts because they resist rust in humid areas. You'll also see flexible ducts for short runs, but they're not for long distances.

Start by measuring the building. For a small house, you need about 1 CFM (cubic foot per minute) per square foot of floor area. A 3m x 4m room (12 mÂ²) needs roughly 130 CFM. Use a ductulator (a sliding ruler) or a phone app to size ducts. For a 130 CFM branch, a 150mm round duct or a 200x100mm rectangular duct works. Keep air speed below 5 m/s in branches to avoid noise.

Draw the layout on paper. Mark the air handler position, then run trunk lines in corridors or above ceilings. Avoid crossing electrical cables or water pipes. Use 45-degree offsets instead of 90-degree turns where possible to reduce resistance. Each branch should have a damper (a metal flap) to balance airflow.

Common components: takeoffs (where branches leave the trunk), elbows (90Â° or 45Â°), reducers (to change size), and end caps. You'll need access doors for cleaning. In dusty African environments, install filters at the return grille. Always leave at least 100mm clearance around ducts for insulation and servicing.

Finally, check local building codes. In many African countries, ducts must be fire-rated if passing through walls. Use fire dampers that close automatically when heat melts a fusible link. Plan your layout to minimise duct length â shorter runs mean less material and lower fan power.

2. Fabricating Rectangular Ducts

Rectangular ducts are the most common in African commercial buildings because they fit easily above ceilings. You'll make them from galvanised steel sheets, usually 0.6mm to 1.0mm thick. For ducts up to 300mm wide, use 0.6mm; for wider ducts, use 0.8mm or 1.0mm to prevent buckling. You need: tin snips (straight and aviation), a hand seamer, a mallet, a rivet gun, a drill, and a bending brake (or a homemade wooden brake).

First, calculate the flat pattern. For a duct 400mm x 200mm, the perimeter is 2*(400+200) = 1200mm. Add 30mm for the lock seam (the folded joint). So you need a sheet 1230mm wide. The length is the duct length plus 20mm for end flanges. Mark these dimensions with a scriber and try square. Cut with tin snips â for long cuts, use a nibbler or electric shear.

Bend the sheet on a brake. Make 90Â° bends at the corners. For the lock seam, fold 10mm on one edge and 20mm on the other, then interlock and hammer flat. Alternatively, use pop rivets every 50mm â faster but less airtight. For larger ducts, add cross-braces (angle iron) every 1.2m to stop oil-canning (banging noise).

Join duct sections with drive slips (S-cleats) and Pittsburgh seams. A Pittsburgh seam is a folded edge that locks into the next section. You can buy pre-formed cleats or make them from the same sheet. Apply duct sealant (mastic) to all joints before assembly. In Africa, use a butyl-based sealant that withstands high temperatures.

Safety: wear gloves â cut edges are razor-sharp. Support long sheets on trestles. After assembly, check for square by measuring diagonals. If they differ by more than 3mm, adjust before riveting. Finally, label each section (e.g., 'Trunk 1') to ease installation on site.

3. Fabricating Round Ducts

Round ducts are stronger and more efficient than rectangular ones â they use less metal and have lower friction. In Africa, you'll see them in workshops, restaurants, and smaller homes. You make them from galvanised steel coils, usually 0.5mm to 0.8mm thick. The key tool is a roll former (a machine that curves the sheet into a cylinder), but you can also use a hand roller or a pipe and mallet for small jobs.

To make a round duct, first calculate the circumference. For a 200mm diameter duct, circumference = Ï Ã 200 = 628mm. Add 10mm for the grooved seam. Cut a sheet 638mm wide and as long as needed (e.g., 1.2m). If you don't have a roll former, clamp the sheet over a length of pipe and hammer it gradually into a curve. Work from one end to the other, using a mallet â don't hit too hard or you'll stretch the metal.

Form the grooved seam: on one edge, bend a 5mm hem (fold back on itself). On the other edge, bend a 10mm flange. Interlock them and close the seam with a hand seamer or by hammering over a stake. For airtightness, run a bead of silicone sealant inside the seam. Alternatively, use pop rivets every 75mm â quick but not as smooth.

For elbows, you can buy pressed bends or make segmented elbows. A 90Â° elbow from five segments: each segment is a trapezoid. Cut them, roll each, then rivet together. For a 200mm duct, each segment is about 100mm long. Use a template to mark the angles. In dusty areas, round ducts are easier to clean because there are no corners.

Join round ducts with slip joints (one end crimped to fit inside the next). Use a crimping tool to make 10mm crimps. Secure with self-tapping screws or a pop rivet. Always support round ducts every 1.5m with hangers. Test for leaks by blocking one end and blowing smoke (or using a wet hand) at joints â feel for air.

4. Duct Insulation and Lining

In hot African climates, duct insulation is critical to stop condensation and save energy. Uninsulated ducts in a 35Â°C roof space can lose up to 30% of cooling. You'll use either external wrap or internal lining. External wrap is cheaper and easier: use fibreglass or rockwool blanket with a foil facing. For a 400x200mm duct, wrap 50mm thick insulation around it, foil side out. Secure with adhesive pins or tie wire every 300mm. Overlap joints by 50mm and tape with aluminium foil tape.

Internal lining is used for acoustic control (to reduce fan noise). Use closed-cell foam or fibreglass duct liner, 25mm thick. Cut it to fit inside the duct, leaving no gaps. Glue it with contact adhesive (follow safety instructions â use in a ventilated area). The liner must not block the duct area â for a 400x200 duct, the internal dimensions become 350x150 after lining. This reduces airflow, so you may need a larger duct.

For condensation control, install a vapour barrier on the outside of the insulation. In humid coastal areas like Mombasa or Lagos, use a sealed foil facing and tape all seams. If the duct is in a conditioned space, you can use 25mm insulation; if in an unconditioned roof, use 50mm. Never compress insulation â it loses R-value.

In Africa, many installers use locally available materials: sisal fibre, sawdust, or even newspaper as makeshift insulation â but these are fire hazards and attract pests. Stick to proper fibreglass or rockwool. For outdoor ducts, add a weatherproof cladding (aluminium sheet or UV-resistant paint).

Safety: wear a dust mask and gloves when handling insulation. Fibreglass particles irritate skin and lungs. After installation, check that insulation is not blocking dampers or access doors. Finally, label the duct with insulation thickness and date for maintenance records.

5. Duct Hangers and Supports

Ducts must be supported so they don't sag, vibrate, or fall. In Africa, you'll often use threaded rods, angle iron, and channel strut. The spacing depends on duct size: for rectangular ducts up to 600mm wide, support every 2.5m; for larger ducts, every 1.5m. Round ducts: every 1.5m for up to 300mm diameter, every 1.2m for larger. Always support near elbows, dampers, and takeoffs.

For rectangular ducts, use a trapeze hanger: two threaded rods (8mm or 10mm diameter) hanging from the ceiling, with a piece of angle iron (25x25x3mm) or channel across the bottom. The duct sits on the angle. Add a top angle to clamp the duct if it's wide (over 600mm). Use spring washers and nuts to prevent loosening. For round ducts, use a U-shaped hanger made from 25mm x 3mm flat bar or a pipe clamp. Wrap the hanger around the duct and bolt to the threaded rod.

In ceilings, attach hangers to structural beams or concrete slabs. Use expansion bolts (M8 or M10) for concrete. For wooden beams, use lag screws. Never hang from electrical conduits or water pipes. If you must span a long distance, use a trapeze with two rods per side to prevent twisting. For outdoor ducts, use galvanised hardware to resist rust.

Vibration isolation: if the duct is near a fan, install rubber-in-shear mounts or spring isolators. This stops noise from travelling through the building. In hospitals or studios, use neoprene pads under hangers.

Check hanger tension: the duct should not sag more than 10mm between supports. After installation, push down on the duct â it should feel firm. If it moves, tighten nuts or add more hangers. Finally, ensure hangers don't crush insulation â use oversized clamps or saddle supports. In dusty environments, avoid using wire or rope that can fray.

6. Testing Duct Systems for Leaks

Leaky ducts waste money and reduce comfort. In Africa, where energy costs are high, a leak test is essential. The standard test is the pressure test: seal all openings (diffusers, grilles) with plastic and tape. Connect a duct leakage tester (or a calibrated fan) to one opening. Pressurise the duct to 250 Pa (about 1 inch water gauge) for rectangular ducts, or 500 Pa for round. Measure the airflow needed to maintain that pressure â that's your leakage rate.

For small systems, you can do a smoke test. Light a smoke pencil or incense stick near joints while the fan is running. Watch for smoke being sucked in or blown out. For a more precise check, use a soap bubble solution on joints â bubbles will form at leaks. This is cheap and effective for site work.

Acceptable leakage: for a well-built system, leakage should be less than 5% of total airflow. If you don't know total airflow, use this rule: for every 10 mÂ² of duct surface, leakage should be under 1 litre/second at 250 Pa. If it's higher, find and seal leaks. Common leak points: seams, joints, damper shafts, and access doors. Use mastic sealant (brush-on or caulk) or foil tape. For large gaps, use butyl tape or fibreglass mesh with mastic.

After sealing, retest. If leakage is still high, check for missing gaskets on access doors or unsealed drive slips. In dusty environments, leaks also let in dust, so sealing improves indoor air quality. For critical spaces (e.g., operating theatres), use a certified leak tester and document results.

Safety: never pressurise a duct above 1000 Pa â it can burst. Wear eye protection when using smoke or sealants. Finally, record the test date, pressure, and leakage rate on a label attached to the duct. This helps future maintenance.

Practical Reference: Sheet Metal Ductwork Fabrication

This practical reference consolidates the core field knowledge you need to fabricate, install, and commission sheet metal ductwork on African projects â from a single workshop in Kano to a multi-storey HVAC fit-out in Nairobi. It is written business-first: every technique below is framed around what it costs, what it saves, and what it wins you on tender and on site. Whether you are quoting a restaurant kitchen extract, a hospital theatre ventilation system, or a cold-store distribution duct, the principles are the same. Master them once and you can price work faster, reduce waste, and avoid the rework that destroys margin.

In most African markets, ductwork is fabricated from galvanised mild steel (GMS) sheet in thicknesses from 0.5 mm to 1.2 mm, with heavier gauges for large spans or high-velocity systems. Aluminium and stainless steel are used for corrosive or hygiene-critical environments â dairies, food processing, chemical plants, and coastal installations where salt air attacks galvanising. The choice of material, gauge, and jointing method is a commercial decision as much as an engineering one, and it drives your material cost, labour hours, and transport weight.

Round Duct Fabrication â The Spiral Advantage

Round ductwork is increasingly preferred on African projects because spiral machine fabrication is faster, uses less material per metre of airflow, and leaks less than rectangular. A spiral tube requires no longitudinal seam on the main body â the seam is a continuous lock formed by the machine. This means fewer leak paths and lower labour per metre. For long runs in car parks, warehouses, and industrial extract, spiral duct wins on both cost and performance.

The trade-off is tooling. A spiral machine is a significant capital item, and many small African workshops do not have one. If you are fabricating round ducts without a spiral former, you are likely doing longitudinal seam work on rolled sheet, which is slower and more skill-dependent. If your market has enough volume, the payback on a spiral line can be under two years â but only if you have consistent order flow. Otherwise, partner with a specialist spiral fabricator and focus your own workshop on rectangular work, fittings, and installation.

Insulation, Lining, and Supports

Duct insulation is specified for thermal performance, condensation control, and acoustic attenuation. In hot African climates, the dominant risk is condensation on cold supply ducts running through unconditioned spaces â plant rooms, ceiling voids, and service shafts. Closed-cell elastomeric insulation is the standard for chilled water and cold air, while fibreglass blanket with foil facing is common for thermal and acoustic wrap on larger ducts.

Internal lining is used for acoustic attenuation, particularly on AHU discharge and in occupied spaces. It must be faced and sealed to prevent fibre erosion into the airstream â a health issue in hospitals and food facilities. If you are lining ducts, budget for the additional labour and the inspection time, because a poorly bonded lining will delaminate and become a callback.

Hangers and supports are the silent margin killer. Under-specifying hanger spacing means sagging ducts, failed inspections, and rework. Over-specifying means buying more threaded rod, angle iron, and anchors than the job needs. The practical rule for rectangular ductwork is support at intervals not exceeding 2.4 m for horizontal runs, with additional support at each change of direction and within 600 mm of a joint. For round spiral duct, spacing can often extend to 3 m depending on diameter and gauge.

Sheet metal edges are razor-sharp. Always wear cut-resistant gloves when handling, forming, and installing ducts. Never reach into a guillotine, press brake, or roll former while it is energised â isolate and lock out before any maintenance or clearing of jams. When working at height on duct installation, use full body harness and certified anchor points; falls remain the leading cause of fatality on construction sites across Africa. Ensure adequate ventilation when applying solvent-based mastics and adhesives, and keep a fire extinguisher within reach during hot works such as welding and brazing. Verify that all lifting equipment, slings, and shackles carry valid load test certificates before use.

Testing for Leaks â Protecting Your Retention

Leakage testing is where your fabrication quality is verified. The test method depends on the specified leakage class and the size of the system. For small systems, a pressure test with a calibrated fan and manometer is sufficient. For larger systems, section-by-section testing is more practical and protects you from a single failure bringing down the whole installation.

Before testing, walk the duct run and check every joint, damper, access door, and flexible connection. Flexible ducts are a common leak source â they should be fully extended, not compressed, and secured with clamps or ties as specified. Test at the specified pressure, record the leakage rate, and compare against the class limit. If you fail, do not simply add more sealant and retest â find the leak, fix it properly, and document the repair.

For African projects where third-party commissioning agents are involved, keep a test log with date, section, pressure, measured leakage, and pass/fail. This log is your evidence when retention is released. It also becomes a marketing asset â a fabricator who can show clean test records wins the next tender.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Confirm gauge and pressure class against the specification before cutting any sheet â verify against SMACNA or DW/144 tables.
  • Check material certificates for galvanised coating mass (typically 275 g/mÂ² for GMS) and sheet thickness tolerance.
  • Lay out fittings first to maximise sheet yield and reduce offcut waste â fittings consume more material per kilogram than straight duct.
  • Set hanger spacing at maximum 2.4 m for rectangular duct, with supports at direction changes and within 600 mm of joints.
  • Seal to the specified leakage class â mastic on transverse joints for Class B, gasket plus mastic for Class C and above.
  • Protect duct ends and openings during transport and storage to prevent damage and contamination.
  • Test section by section and record results in a signed log for retention release and future tenders.
  • Brief the installation team on handling, lifting, and joint protection â most leaks are caused on site, not in the workshop.
