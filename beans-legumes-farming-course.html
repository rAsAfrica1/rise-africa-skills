Beans & Legumes Farming

A complete 6-month program across beans, cowpea, soya, pigeon pea and bambara — inoculation and nodule checks, the flowering battle, and the hermetic storage war against weevils that turns harvest grain into lean-season money.

⏱️ 6 Months (crop + storage)📊 Beginner → Business-ready🌍 Hybrid: Online + Offline🎓 Certificate on completion

Course Overview

Why 6 months? Most grain legumes mature in 60–120 days, so six months covers a full crop plus the storage and marketing that determine your actual income. This course teaches beans, cowpeas, soya, pigeon pea and bambara as one family with one logic — because they share the same biology (nitrogen fixation), the same enemies (pod borers, aphids, storage weevils) and the same profit rule: legumes make money at storage and value addition, not at harvest.

Structure: 6 monthly modules → lessons → practical assignments → a 10-question quiz each module → final business project.

How to study (hybrid): Online — modules on your phone, sessions in the Study Diary, questions to the rAs AI assistant. Offline — save this page (Print → Save as PDF); the practicals happen in the field and the grain store.

⚠️ Read first: the three rules that protect your investment

  • Storage weevils (bruchids) can destroy a bean crop inside the store within 3–6 months. They arrive with the crop from the field, hidden as eggs on the pods. Month 5 is where your harvest is saved or lost.
  • Never treat grain with chemicals not registered for STORED FOOD. Farmers have poisoned families using cotton or field pesticides on beans. Use only registered grain protectants at label rates, or hermetic (airtight) storage — which needs no chemicals at all and is the better answer.
  • Raw and undercooked beans and soya are toxic (lectins/trypsin inhibitors). Beans must be boiled hard; soya must be properly heat-treated before human or livestock consumption. Never sell "quick-cook" undercooked products.
MONTH 1 — Legume Science, Crop Choice & Planning ▾

Lesson 1.1 — Why legumes belong on every farm

  • They feed people: 20–25% protein in beans and cowpeas, 35–40% in soya — the cheapest protein an African household can grow.
  • They feed the soil: through rhizobium bacteria in root nodules, a good legume crop can fix 30–150 kg of nitrogen per hectare, much of which benefits the following cereal. Rotating maize with legumes measurably cuts your fertiliser bill.
  • They feed cash flow: short cycles (60–90 days for beans and cowpea) mean a legume crop can fit between main-season crops or fill a short rains window.
  • They store: dry grain legumes keep for a year or more when protected — turning harvest gluts into lean-season prices.

Lesson 1.2 — Choosing your legume

  • The beginner rule: match the crop to your rainfall FIRST, market second. Growing common beans in a hot dry sandy area because they fetch the best price is how farmers lose seasons.

Lesson 1.3 — Growth habits and their implications

  • Determinate (bush) types: stop growing at flowering, mature together, easy single harvest — best for market production and mechanised threshing.
  • Indeterminate (climbing/semi-climbing) types: keep growing and podding, need staking or intercropping support, but yield substantially more per square metre — excellent for small plots with labour available.
  • Climbing beans on stakes or intercropped with maize can double the yield per unit area versus bush beans — a serious option for land-scarce farmers.

Lesson 1.4 — Rotation and intercropping

  • Rotate: legume → cereal → legume. Avoid legume-after-legume, which builds root rots, wilts and nematodes.
  • Intercropping with maize is the classic African system: plant the legume 1–2 weeks after maize, in the inter-rows, so it doesn't compete during maize's critical early growth. You gain a second harvest, ground cover and nitrogen.
  • Pigeon pea intercropped in maize is especially powerful — it grows slowly while maize matures, then uses the residual season after harvest.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 1): (1) Match crop to your rainfall and season length; write three reasons. (2) Survey 3 buyers for the legume you chose — grain price now vs 6 months ago. (3) Check rotation history and plan your intercrop layout if using one.

MONTH 2 — Seed, Inoculation & Planting ▾

Lesson 2.1 — Seed selection

  • Legume seed is fragile: the two halves (cotyledons) split easily and cracked seed doesn't germinate. Handle gently, never pour from height, and never plant seed that rattles broken in the bag.
  • Select plump, uniform, undamaged seed of one variety, free of weevil holes (a tiny round hole means a weevil emerged — that seed is dead).
  • Run a 100-seed germination test 10 days before planting; below 80%, increase seed rate proportionally or find better seed.
  • Certified seed matters most for soya (variety-specific inoculant response) and for disease-prone bean areas (anthracnose, halo blight and CBB are seed-borne).

Lesson 2.2 — Rhizobium inoculation

  • Inoculant is a powder of live rhizobium bacteria that lets the crop fix its own nitrogen. Soybean in particular needs the specific strain — without inoculant, soya on new land is often pale, yellow and low-yielding.
  • Method: moisten seed lightly (sugar water or gum arabic solution), mix in the inoculant in SHADE, and plant within a few hours. Direct sunlight and heat kill the bacteria.
  • Never mix inoculant with fungicide-treated seed unless the products are stated compatible — the fungicide kills the bacteria.
  • Check nodulation at flowering: dig a plant, cut a nodule — pink/red inside means it's fixing nitrogen; white or green means it isn't.

Lesson 2.3 — Land preparation and fertility

  • Fine, firm, well-drained seedbed. Legumes hate waterlogging — root rot follows standing water within days.
  • Fertility: legumes need PHOSPHORUS, not nitrogen. Apply a phosphate-rich basal at roughly 100–200 kg/ha; heavy nitrogen actually SUPPRESSES nodulation (the plant stops fixing when free nitrogen is available).
  • Lime where soil is acidic (pH below 5.5) — beans in particular perform poorly on acid soils and nodulation fails.
  • Compost/manure at 5–10 t/ha where available improves moisture holding and nodulation.

MONTH 3 — Weeding, Water & Crop Management ▾

Lesson 3.1 — The weeding window

  • Legumes must be weed-free for the first 4–5 weeks. Weed 1 at 2 weeks, weed 2 at 4 weeks — after flowering starts, weed only by hand-pulling and avoid disturbing roots.
  • Shallow weeding only: legume roots and their nodules sit near the surface, and deep hoeing tears both.
  • Weed competition losses run 30–50% when the early window is missed.

Lesson 3.2 — Water and the critical stages

  • Total need: 300–500 mm for short-season legumes. The critical windows are FLOWERING and POD FILLING — stress at flowering causes flower abortion (the plant simply drops its flowers), and stress at pod fill gives small shrivelled seed.
  • Legumes are shallow-rooted (except pigeon pea, which is deep-rooted and genuinely drought-hardy); a two-week dry spell at flowering can halve yield.
  • Waterlogging at any stage causes root rot — ridge or drain heavy soils.

Lesson 3.3 — Staking climbing types

  • Stake climbing beans at 2–3 weeks, before the tendrils search: 2–2.5 m stakes per plant, or a string trellis between posts, or plant alongside maize which acts as the living stake.
  • Staked climbers yield far more and stay cleaner — pods off the ground means less disease and less loss.

Lesson 3.4 — Reading the crop

  • Pale yellow overall growth: check nodules first (fixation failure), then consider soil acidity or waterlogging — reaching for nitrogen fertiliser is usually the wrong reflex.
  • Flower drop: normal to some extent (plants set more flowers than they can fill), but heavy drop points to heat, drought or insect damage.
  • Leaf spots and yellowing from the bottom up: disease — see Month 4.
🛠️ Practical assignments (Month 3): (1) Complete both weedings within 5 weeks and log dates. (2) Stake or trellis a climbing block if growing one; compare its pod count later against any unstaked plants. (3) Log rainfall/irrigation against the flowering and pod-fill windows.

MONTH 4 — Pests & Diseases ▾

Lesson 4.1 — Field pests

  • The timing rule for legumes: the crop's most vulnerable moment is FLOWERING to early pod set. Scout twice weekly through that window; damage there is irreversible.

Lesson 4.2 — Diseases

  • Anthracnose: dark sunken lesions on pods and stems, especially in cool wet weather. Seed-borne — so clean seed, rotation, and avoiding working the crop when wet are the controls.
  • Angular/halo leaf spots and common bacterial blight: angular brown spots or water-soaked patches with yellow halos. Seed-borne and splash-spread — clean seed, rotation, and mulch to stop splash.
  • Rust: reddish-brown pustules on leaves; resistant varieties and, where economic, protectant fungicides.
  • Root rots and fusarium wilt: yellowing, wilting, rotted root systems — caused by waterlogging and short rotations. Improve drainage, rotate 2–3 years, add organic matter.
  • Viruses (bean common mosaic etc.): mottled distorted leaves, stunting — spread by aphids and seed; use resistant varieties and clean seed, rogue infected plants.

Lesson 4.3 — Low-cost protection

  • Cultural first: clean certified seed, rotation, timely planting, correct population, and removing crop residues that host pests.
  • Botanical options widely used by smallholders: neem extract, soap sprays, chilli-garlic mixtures — most effective against aphids and young larvae, applied weekly during flowering.
  • Chemicals last, registered only, on thresholds, at label dose, rotated between groups, with pre-harvest intervals respected — especially important for fresh green beans and cowpea leaves eaten as relish.

Lesson 4.4 — The bruchid clock starts in the field

  • Storage weevils (bruchids) lay eggs on drying pods IN THE FIELD. The infestation you fight in the store began before harvest.
  • Therefore: harvest promptly at maturity, don't leave dry pods standing for weeks, and dry and treat/seal grain immediately (Module 5).

MODULE 5 — Harvest, Threshing & Beating the Weevil ▾

Lesson 5.1 — Harvest timing

  • Dry grain harvest: when 80–90% of pods are dry and rattling, leaves have yellowed and dropped. Beans shatter (pods split and throw seed) if left too long — cowpea and soya especially.
  • Harvest in the morning while pods are slightly damp with dew to reduce shattering losses; or cut whole plants and stack to finish drying.
  • Green/fresh harvest: green beans picked every 2–3 days at pencil thickness before seeds bulge; cowpea leaves picked young for relish (a separate income stream from the same field).
  • Don't harvest late "to be safe" — every extra dry week in the field adds weevil eggs, shattering and quality loss.

Lesson 5.2 — Drying and threshing

  • Dry pods to about 12–13% moisture — the bite test: seed cracks sharply between the teeth rather than denting.
  • Dry on tarpaulins, mats or racks — NEVER on bare soil (dirt, stones, moisture and contamination).
  • Thresh gently: beating stacked plants with sticks on a tarpaulin is standard, but excessive force cracks and splits seed, and cracked seed is what weevils enter first and what buyers downgrade.
  • Winnow to clean out chaff and dust, then hand-sort out broken, discoloured, insect-holed and mouldy seed.

Lesson 5.3 — The bruchid war (the single most valuable lesson)

  • Bruchids (bean/cowpea weevils) lay eggs on grain; larvae eat the inside of the seed and emerge as adults through neat round holes. Populations multiply every 3–4 weeks — a clean-looking bag can be 40–70% destroyed in 4–6 months.
  • Hermetic (airtight) storage is the best answer: sealed metal drums, or triple-layer hermetic bags. The insects consume the oxygen inside and die — no chemicals, no residues, and grain keeps for a year or more. This technology has transformed smallholder legume storage across Africa.
  • Traditional/low-cost supports: mixing grain with dry ash or sand (creating a physical barrier that abrades and blocks the insects), thorough sun-drying before sealing, and storing in cool dark conditions.
  • Chemical protectants: only products REGISTERED FOR STORED GRAIN, at exact label dose. Never use field, cotton or livestock chemicals on grain — this has killed people.
  • Whatever the method: start with clean, sorted, fully dry grain and seal it within days of threshing. Weevils don't invade a sealed drum; they arrive inside it with the grain.

Lesson 5.4 — Seed selection for next season

  • During the growing season, peg the healthiest, highest-podding, disease-free plants; harvest and store their seed separately in a sealed container.
  • Legume seed keeps its viability 1–2 years if stored dry, cool and insect-free — but germination falls fast in hot humid conditions, so test before planting.

MODULE 6 — Value Addition, Marketing & Business Plan ▾

Lesson 6.1 — Selling on the curve

  • Legume prices are lowest at harvest and typically climb 40–100% into the lean season. Hermetic storage converts that curve directly into profit — the whole reason Module 5 exists.
  • Sell in tranches: a portion at harvest for cash flow, the rest across the rising months.
  • Grading pays: cleaned, uniform, single-variety, insect-free grain in clear bags earns a visible premium over mixed field-run — buyers can see quality instantly in legumes.

Lesson 6.2 — Value addition options

  • Fresh green beans and cowpea leaves: harvested repeatedly, sold daily to households and vendors — the fastest cash from the same field.
  • Cleaned graded packs: 1 kg and 2 kg clear packs for supermarkets and shops — small effort, real margin.
  • Soya products: soya milk, tofu-style products, roasted soya snacks, and full-fat soya meal for livestock — but soya MUST be properly heat-treated (boiling/roasting) to destroy trypsin inhibitors before human or animal consumption.
  • Bean flour and precooked/quick-cook beans: flours for porridges and instant products save households cooking fuel and command premiums; requires proper processing and food-safety compliance.
  • Seed sales: clean, well-stored, high-germination legume seed sells at a premium to neighbours at planting time.

📖 Glossary

  • Rhizobium / inoculant — nitrogen-fixing bacteria and the powder that supplies them.
  • Nodule — root swelling where fixation happens; pink inside when active.
  • Determinate / indeterminate — bush vs climbing growth habit.
  • Shattering — dry pods splitting and throwing seed before harvest.
  • Bruchid — bean/cowpea storage weevil.
  • Hermetic storage — airtight storage that suffocates storage insects.
  • Anthracnose — seed-borne fungal disease with sunken pod lesions.
  • Bean fly — stem maggot killing seedlings at the base.
  • Trypsin inhibitor — anti-nutrient in raw soya destroyed by heat.
  • Pod fill — the seed-swelling stage; a critical moisture window.
