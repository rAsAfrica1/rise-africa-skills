Lessons

1. Primary Treatment: Sedimentation, Filtration, and Coagulation

Primary treatment removes the bulk of dirt, sand, and organic matter from your raw water before it hits expensive filters. Start with sedimentation: pump raw water into a settling tank or large drum and let it sit for 24 hours. Heavy particles sink. In many African settings, you can use a simple 1000-litre tank with a drain valve at the bottom to flush out the sludge. If your water is cloudy (turbidity above 10 NTU), add coagulation chemicals. Aluminium sulphate (alum) is cheap and widely available. Dose 1030 mg per litre of waterroughly 1030 grams per 1000 litres. Dissolve the alum in a small bucket of clean water first, then stir it into the tank for 2 minutes, then stir slowly for 15 minutes. This makes tiny particles clump into flocs that settle faster. After 12 hours of settling, drain the clear water from the top into a filtration unit.

Filtration comes next. A rapid sand filter is a tank with layers of gravel (bottom), coarse sand, and fine sand (top). Water flows down through the sand, trapping remaining particles. You must backwash the filter every 12 days by pushing clean water upward to flush out trapped dirt. For small-scale bottling (5002000 litres per day), a simple pressure sand filter with a 50-micron cartridge after it works well. Replace cartridges when pressure drops by 0.5 bar.

If you cannot afford alum, use moringa seeds: crush 12 seeds per litre, mix with water, and add to the tank. The natural proteins act as a coagulant. This is common in rural Kenya and Nigeria. Always test your treated water with a turbidity meter (or a simple Secchi disk) to ensure it is below 5 NTU before moving to fine filtration. Never skip sedimentationit saves money on filters and chemicals downstream.

2. Reverse Osmosis and Nanofiltration Systems

Reverse osmosis (RO) forces water through a semi-permeable membrane that rejects 9599% of dissolved salts, heavy metals, and bacteria. For bottled water, RO is the workhorse for treating brackish or high-TDS water. A typical small RO system for 500 litres per hour uses a 4-inch membrane, a high-pressure pump (1015 bar), and pre-filters (5-micron sediment and carbon block). You must pretreat water with antiscalant (25 mg/L) to prevent scaling, especially if your water is hard. In Africa, many boreholes have TDS above 1000 mg/L; RO brings it down to 50150 mg/L, which is ideal for drinking water.

Nanofiltration (NF) is similar but uses a looser membrane. It rejects 5090% of salts, including most hardness (calcium and magnesium) but lets some beneficial minerals through. NF operates at lower pressure (510 bar) and wastes less water (1020% reject vs. 3050% for RO). If your source water has moderate TDS (300800 mg/L) and you want to keep some taste, NF is a good choice. However, RO is more common because it guarantees consistent low TDS, which helps with blending later.

Key maintenance: replace pre-filters every 13 months, clean membranes every 36 months with a caustic/acid wash, and replace membranes every 23 years. Always install a flow meter and pressure gauges. A sudden drop in permeate flow means fouling. In many African countries, power fluctuations can damage pumpsuse a voltage stabiliser. Also, the reject water (concentrate) can be reused for cleaning floors or irrigation, but not for drinking. Test your product water with a TDS meter daily; aim for 1050 mg/L for RO permeate. If TDS is higher, check membrane integrity.

3. Distillation and Deionization Processes

Distillation removes nearly all impurities by boiling water into steam and condensing it back to liquid. It kills microbes and leaves behind salts, heavy metals, and most organics. For small-scale bottling, a simple solar still or electric distiller can produce 520 litres per day. But distillation is energy-intensive: boiling 1 litre of water takes about 0.7 kWh of electricity. In Africa, where grid power may be unreliable or expensive, distillation is usually reserved for high-value products (e.g., laboratory water or specialty bottled water). If you use a solar still, you need 23 square metres of glass or plastic cover per litre per day. The condensate is very pure (TDS < 5 mg/L) but flat-tasting and slightly acidic (pH 6.57.0). You must remineralise it before bottling.

Deionization (DI) uses ion-exchange resins to remove dissolved ions. Water passes through a cation resin (releases H+ ions) and an anion resin (releases OH- ions), producing ultra-pure water with TDS < 1 mg/L. DI is not a disinfectantit does not kill bacteriaso you must pre-treat with UV or ozone. DI resins need regeneration with hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide, which is dangerous and requires proper training. For most African bottling startups, DI is overkill. Instead, use RO followed by a mixed-bed DI cartridge if you need very low TDS for a specific product (e.g., water for baby formula).

A practical alternative: if your water is already low in TDS (< 200 mg/L), you can skip distillation and DI entirely. Use RO only when TDS is high. For distilled or DI water, add a mineral blend (see Lesson 6) to bring TDS to 50150 mg/L and pH to 7.07.5. Always test with a conductivity meter. Store distilled water in stainless steel or glassnot plasticbecause pure water can leach chemicals from some plastics.

4. Ozonation and UV Light Disinfection

Disinfection is the final barrier against bacteria, viruses, and parasites. Ozone (O3) is a powerful gas that kills microbes 3,000 times faster than chlorine. In bottled water plants, ozone is generated on-site from air or oxygen and injected into water via a venturi or bubble diffuser. Dose 0.20.5 mg/L for 410 minutes contact time. Ozone also removes colour, taste, and odour. After treatment, ozone decays to oxygen within 2030 minutes, leaving no chemical residue. However, ozone is toxic to breatheinstall an ozone destruct unit and ventilate the room. In Africa, small ozone generators (510 g/h) cost $200$500 and run on 220V. They are common in Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa. Test residual ozone with a DPD kit; aim for 0.10.2 mg/L after 10 minutes.

UV light (254 nm) is a physical disinfectant. Water flows past a UV lamp inside a quartz sleeve. The UV dose must be at least 40 mJ/cm2 to inactivate bacteria and viruses. For a flow rate of 500 L/h, you need a 2530 W UV lamp. Change the lamp every 9,000 hours (about 12 months). Clean the quartz sleeve weekly with a soft cloth and vinegar to remove scalescale blocks UV light. UV does not leave residual, so it is best used after ozone or with a small chlorine dose (0.20.5 mg/L) to protect against recontamination in bottles. In many African cities, UV units are sold as water purifiers for homes; for bottling, buy a commercial stainless-steel UV reactor with a UV intensity monitor. Always pre-filter water to < 5 microns before UVparticles can shield microbes. Combine ozone and UV for a robust, chemical-free disinfection train.

5. Activated Carbon Filtration for Taste and Odor Removal

Activated carbon (AC) removes chlorine, pesticides, industrial solvents, and the earthy-musty compounds (geosmin and 2-MIB) that make water taste bad. It works by adsorption: contaminants stick to the carbons huge internal surface area (one gram has 5001500 m2). For bottled water, use granular activated carbon (GAC) in a pressure vessel or carbon block cartridges. A typical GAC filter for 1000 L/h has a bed depth of 6090 cm and an empty bed contact time (EBCT) of 1015 minutes. That means for 1000 L/h, you need about 170250 litres of GAC. Carbon block cartridges (510 micron) are simpler for small-scale: a 10-inch cartridge treats 5,00010,000 litres before replacement. Always put a 5-micron sediment filter before the carbon to prevent clogging.

In Africa, coconut-shell carbon is preferred because it is hard, renewable, and has more micropores than coal-based carbon. It is produced in Ghana, Nigeria, and Tanzania. You can also make your own carbon from coconut shells or rice husks by burning them at 600800C with limited air, then activating with steam. But for consistent quality, buy food-grade certified carbon. Test performance by measuring chlorine residual (should be zero after carbon) and taste. If water tastes flat or has a slight sulphur smell, the carbon may be exhaustedreplace it. Carbon does not remove bacteria; in fact, bacteria can grow on carbon if water sits stagnant. So always disinfect after carbon (ozone or UV). Backwash GAC filters every 23 days with clean water to remove trapped particles. Replace GAC every 612 months depending on usage. For a 500 L/h plant, budget $50$100 per year for carbon replacement.

6. Mineral Adjustment and Blending for Consistent Taste

After RO or distillation, water is too pureit tastes flat and can be aggressive to pipes and bottles. You must add minerals to reach a target TDS of 50150 mg/L and a pH of 7.07.5. The most common blend uses calcium chloride (CaCl2), magnesium sulphate (MgSO4), and sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3). A simple recipe for 1000 litres of RO water: dissolve 50 g calcium chloride, 30 g magnesium sulphate, and 20 g sodium bicarbonate in 10 litres of RO water, then dose this concentrate into the main tank while stirring. This gives roughly 50 mg/L calcium, 15 mg/L magnesium, and 30 mg/L bicarbonate. Adjust to tastesome markets prefer a sweeter profile (more magnesium) or a crisper profile (more calcium).

For consistent taste, you must measure TDS and pH every batch. Use a calibrated TDS meter and pH meter. If TDS is below 50 mg/L, add more mineral concentrate; if above 150 mg/L, dilute with more RO water. In Africa, many bottlers use commercial mineral blends (e.g., Water Mineral Additive from South Africa or Kenya) that come with dosing instructions. These cost about $10$20 per kilogram and treat 10,00020,000 litres. Always mix minerals in a clean, food-grade tank. Never add minerals directly to bottlesmix in a batch tank with a mechanical stirrer for 10 minutes.

Blending is also used to mix high-TDS water with low-TDS water to achieve a target. For example, if you have 500 litres of RO water (TDS 10) and 500 litres of spring water (TDS 300), blending gives 1000 litres at TDS 155. This is common in Kenya and Uganda where spring water is abundant. But blending does not remove contaminantsonly mix with water that already meets safety standards. After mineral adjustment, run the water through a 1-micron filter and then UV or ozone before bottling. Record every batch: date, TDS, pH, and mineral amounts. This traceability is required by many African food safety authorities (e.g., KEBS, NAFDAC, SABS).

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Water Treatment & Purification for Bottled Water in Africa

This practical reference consolidates the six core lessons of this module into one operating document. It is written for the plant manager, quality lead, or founder running a bottled water line in Africa, where the constraints are rarely technical theory they are power reliability, spare-part lead times, inconsistent raw water, and the cost of every kWh and every gram of chemical. Use this page as your bench reference, your training handout, and your audit checklist.

The guiding principle throughout: treat only what your raw water and your market require. Over-treatment destroys margin. Under-treatment destroys your brand. Your job is to find the narrow, defensible band between the two and to document it.

1. Primary Treatment: Sedimentation, Filtration, Coagulation

Primary treatment is your first line of defence and the cheapest water you will ever treat. In Africa, raw water from boreholes is often high in iron and manganese; surface sources carry turbidity that spikes with the rains. Coagulation (alum or a polyaluminium chloride) destabilises colloidal particles; flocculation gently agitates them into larger flocs; sedimentation lets them settle; filtration polishes. Dose jar tests weekly, not monthly, because raw water quality shifts with the season.

2. Reverse Osmosis and Nanofiltration

RO removes 9599% of dissolved solids, which is excellent for brackish boreholes but also strips the minerals that give water its taste. NF sits between RO and softening, retaining some divalent ions. Practical African realities: RO membranes are imported, so keep a spare set; pre-treatment must be excellent or you will foul membranes within weeks; and recovery rates of 5075% mean you must plan for concentrate disposal a regulatory and community-relations issue, not just a technical one.

3. Distillation and Deionization

Distillation produces very pure water but is energy-hungry rarely economical where grid power is unstable or diesel is the backup. Deionization (DI) using mixed-bed resins produces ultra-pure water but resins must be regenerated with acid and caustic, which is hazardous and costly. In most African bottled water plants, RO plus carbon plus ozone/UV is the pragmatic choice; distillation and DI are reserved for niche products (e.g., laboratory-grade or specialised beverages).

4. Ozonation and UV Light Disinfection

Ozone is a powerful oxidant and leaves a residual that protects water in the bottle; UV is a physical barrier with no residual. Best practice is a combination: ozone for bulk disinfection and bottle-rinse water, UV as a final polish before filling. UV lamps degrade with hours of use log the hours and replace on schedule, not when someone remembers. Ozone must be dosed and monitored carefully: too little and you fail microbiology; too much and you create bromate (a regulated disinfection by-product) and off-tastes.

5. Activated Carbon Filtration for Taste and Odour Removal

Activated carbon (granular or block) removes chlorine, chloramines, organics, and the earthy/musty compounds (geosmin, 2-MIB) that ruin taste. It is also where biofilms love to grow, so it must be upstream of your final disinfection never after. Replace carbon on a schedule based on throughput (litres treated) and differential pressure, not on appearance. In hot African climates, biological activity in carbon beds accelerates; shorten replacement intervals accordingly.

6. Mineral Adjustment and Blending for Consistent Taste

After RO, water can taste "flat." Blending a controlled fraction of raw or partially treated water back in or dosing a mineral concentrate (calcium, magnesium, potassium, bicarbonate) restores mouthfeel and consistency. This is where your brand lives. Target a fixed total dissolved solids (TDS) band, e.g., 80150 mg/L for a mainstream still water, and verify every batch. Consistency across seasons and sources is what separates a brand from a commodity.

SAFETY WARNING CHLORINE, OZONE, AND CHEMICAL HANDLING

Chlorine gas and concentrated hypochlorite cause severe respiratory and eye injury. Ozone is a toxic gas never operate an ozone generator in an unventilated room, and install an ozone destruct unit on vent lines. Alum, acids, and caustic soda used in coagulation and DI regeneration cause chemical burns. Always: (1) store chemicals in a locked, ventilated, bunded area away from food and fuel; (2) provide and enforce PPE goggles, gloves, apron, and respirator where gas is possible; (3) install gas-leak detection and emergency eyewash/shower within 10 seconds' reach; (4) train every operator on the safety data sheet (SDS) before first use; (5) never mix chlorine with acid or ammonia toxic gas results. In many African jurisdictions, occupational safety regulators and the national standards body will inspect; treat this as a licence-to-operate issue, not paperwork.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Know your raw water. Test source water weekly for turbidity, iron, manganese, TDS, pH, and microbiology; adjust dosing to the season.
  2. Log everything. Record coagulant dose, filter differential pressure, carbon throughput, RO recovery, UV lamp hours, and ozone residual at every shift.
  3. Protect the membranes. Verify pre-treatment (SDI < 5, chlorine < 0.1 mg/L) before RO; keep a spare membrane set on site.
  4. Carbon before disinfection, never after. Replace on schedule by litres treated, not by look.
  5. Dose ozone with a target, not a guess. Monitor bromate if bromide is present; always install an ozone destruct unit.
  6. Verify UV dose. Check lamp intensity and replace lamps by hours; clean quartz sleeves weekly.
  7. Lock your taste profile. Blend or dose minerals to a fixed TDS/pH band and verify every batch before release.
  8. Safety first. PPE, ventilation, gas detection, eyewash, SDS training, and locked chemical storage are non-negotiable and often legally required.
