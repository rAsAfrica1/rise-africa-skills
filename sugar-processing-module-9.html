1. Affination and Melting of Raw Sugar

Raw sugar from a mill is covered in a film of molasses. Affination removes this film by mixing the raw sugar with a heavy syrup (about 70-75 Brix) in a magma mixer. The mixture is then spun in centrifugals to separate the syrup from the sugar crystals. In Africa, many refineries use batch centrifugals for this step. The syrup, called affination syrup, is recycled or sold as a byproduct. After affination, the sugar is melted with hot water or sweet water to form a liquor of 60-65 Brix. This melting step is done in a melter equipped with steam coils and agitation. The liquor is then screened to remove debris like bagasse, sand, and metal pieces. In small-scale operations in countries like Kenya, Nigeria, and South Africa, a simple tank with a stirrer and steam injection can be used. The melted liquor is then pumped to clarification. Key parameters: affination syrup Brix should be 70-75, temperature around 60-70Â°C. Melting water temperature should be 80-90Â°C. The purity of the melted liquor should be at least 98%. This step is crucial because it reduces color and ash, making subsequent refining easier. Without proper affination, the final white sugar will have high color and may fail quality standards.

2. Clarification and Decolorization: Carbonatation and Phosphatation

After melting, the raw liquor still contains impurities like gums, waxes, and colorants. Clarification removes these. Two main methods are used: carbonatation and phosphatation. In carbonatation, lime (calcium hydroxide) is added to the liquor to raise pH to about 8.5-9.0. Then carbon dioxide gas is bubbled through, forming calcium carbonate precipitate. This precipitate traps impurities and color. The mixture is then filtered. Carbonatation is common in large refineries in South Africa and Egypt. In phosphatation, phosphoric acid is added, followed by lime to form calcium phosphate precipitate. This method is simpler and used in smaller plants. The pH is adjusted to about 7.5-8.0. After precipitation, a flocculant is added to help settle the precipitate. The clear liquor is then decolorized using activated carbon or bone char. In Africa, bone char is used in some refineries, but activated carbon is more common due to availability. The decolorization step reduces color from about 1000 ICUMSA to less than 100. Temperature during clarification is kept at 70-80Â°C. Proper control of pH and chemical dosage is critical. Over-liming can cause scaling, while under-liming leaves impurities. This step ensures the final sugar is bright and pure.

3. Filtration and Ion Exchange Resins

Filtration removes the precipitate formed during clarification. Common equipment includes plate-and-frame filter presses, rotary vacuum filters, and pressure leaf filters. In African refineries, plate-and-frame presses are often used because they are robust and easy to maintain. Filter aids like diatomaceous earth (DE) are used to improve filtration. The filtered liquor should be clear, with turbidity less than 5 NTU. After filtration, the liquor may still contain dissolved color and ions. Ion exchange resins are used for further decolorization and demineralization. Strong base anion resins remove color bodies, while cation resins remove metal ions like calcium and magnesium. In a typical setup, the liquor passes through a series of columns: first a cation resin (to remove cations), then an anion resin (to remove anions and color). The resins are regenerated with sodium chloride (for anion) and hydrochloric acid (for cation). In small-scale operations in Africa, ion exchange may be skipped if the sugar is for local markets with less strict color standards. However, for export-quality white sugar, ion exchange is essential. The resin beds must be backwashed regularly to prevent fouling. The flow rate should be controlled to ensure proper contact time. This step can reduce color to below 20 ICUMSA and ash to less than 0.01%.

4. Crystallization in Refining: White Sugar Production

Crystallization is the heart of white sugar production. The clarified and decolorized liquor is concentrated in evaporators to about 65-70 Brix, then further boiled in vacuum pans to form crystals. In refining, the process is done in multiple stages: first, a high-purity liquor is boiled to produce white sugar crystals. The vacuum pan is operated under vacuum to lower the boiling temperature to about 60-70Â°C, preventing color formation. Seed crystals are added to start crystallization. The pan is then fed with more liquor until the crystals reach the desired size (typically 0.4-0.6 mm). The mixture, called massecuite, is then dropped into crystallizers for cooling and further crystal growth. In Africa, many refineries use batch vacuum pans. The boiling time can be 2-4 hours. The purity of the mother liquor drops as sugar crystallizes. The massecuite is then sent to centrifugals. The first crop of crystals is the white sugar. The mother liquor (run-off) is reboiled to produce lower-grade sugars, which are recycled back to affination or sold as brown sugar. Key control points: vacuum level (about 600-650 mmHg), temperature, and supersaturation (1.1-1.3). Proper seeding and boiling ensure uniform crystal size and good yield.

5. Centrifugation and Drying of Refined Sugar

After crystallization, the massecuite is separated into crystals and mother liquor using centrifugals. In refining, high-speed batch centrifugals are used for white sugar. The centrifugal basket has a fine screen (0.06-0.08 mm openings) that retains crystals while allowing syrup to pass. The cycle includes loading, spinning, washing, and discharging. Washing is done with hot water (about 80-90Â°C) to remove the film of mother liquor from the crystals. The wash water is then spun off. The spinning time is typically 3-5 minutes. The sugar is then discharged and sent to dryers. Drying reduces moisture from about 1-2% to less than 0.05%. Common dryers are rotary drum dryers and fluidized bed dryers. In African refineries, rotary dryers are common. Hot air at 60-80Â°C is passed through the dryer. After drying, the sugar is cooled to below 30Â°C and then screened to remove lumps and fines. The final product is stored in silos or bags. Proper centrifugation and drying are critical for sugar quality: moisture above 0.05% can cause caking, and residual syrup can cause color development. The centrifugals must be well maintained to avoid screen damage, which can lead to sugar loss.

6. Quality Parameters for Refined Sugar

Refined sugar must meet strict quality standards. Key parameters include: color (ICUMSA), moisture, ash, invert sugar, and particle size. For white sugar, ICUMSA color should be less than 45 (often 20-30 for premium grade). Moisture should be below 0.05%. Ash content should be less than 0.01%. Invert sugar should be less than 0.04% to prevent deterioration. Particle size distribution: typically 0.3-0.8 mm, with uniform size for even packaging. In Africa, standards vary by country. For example, Kenya Bureau of Standards (KEBS) requires ICUMSA color max 100 for white sugar, while South Africa (SABS) requires max 50. Export to the EU requires ICUMSA less than 45. Other tests include: turbidity, insoluble matter, and heavy metals (lead, arsenic) which must be below 1 ppm. Microbiological tests for yeast, mould, and bacteria are also done. To achieve these, every step from affination to drying must be controlled. Regular sampling and testing in a laboratory are essential. Small refineries can use simple tests like a refractometer for Brix and a colorimeter for ICUMSA. Maintaining quality ensures market access and premium prices. Training staff on hygiene and equipment cleaning is also vital to prevent contamination.

Business-First Notes for African Refineries

Refining economics in Africa are dominated by three cost drivers: energy (boilers, evaporators, dryers), chemicals (lime, phosphoric acid, resins, filter aids), and sucrose recovery (molasses loss, undetermined losses). A 0.5% improvement in recovery on a 500 t/day refinery is worth roughly 900 tonnes of sugar per year â at USD 400â500/tonne, that is USD 360,000â450,000 in additional revenue. Small process discipline gains translate directly into cash.

Export markets matter. EU and UK buyers require ICUMSA 45 with full traceability, SOâ limits, and often sustainability certification (Bonsucro, Fairtrade, or Rainforest Alliance). Regional markets (ECOWAS, COMESA, SADC) accept ICUMSA 100â150 and are less demanding on documentation, but price is lower. A refinery that can switch grades on the same line without long changeover times has a strategic advantage.

Power reliability is a recurring constraint. Where grid supply is unstable, invest in a standby generator sized for the centrifugal and drying sections â a stoppage during crystallization can ruin an entire strike and cost hours of rework.
