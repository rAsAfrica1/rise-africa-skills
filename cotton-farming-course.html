Agriculture · 6-Month Program

Cotton Farming

Africa's most important cash crop — outgrower discipline, scouting and economic thresholds, three-round quality picking, lint protection from field to gin, and navigating scheme credit without the debt spiral.

Course Overview

Cotton is Africa's most important non-food cash crop: grown across the Sahel and savanna, it is the primary cash income for millions of smallholder families — school fees, medical bills, farm inputs — delivered through one of the continent's most organised buyer networks. It is also the most pest-demanding crop in this college, and the difference between a 700 kg/ha farmer and a 2 t/ha farmer is almost entirely scouting discipline and threshold-based spray decisions. This course teaches cotton as a systems crop: outgrower or scheme discipline first (read the rules before planting), seed and stand establishment, fertiliser split for boll-not-straw, then the pest management centrepiece — economic thresholds, W-pattern weekly scouting, and knowing the difference between a spray that saves bolls and one that kills natural enemies. Cotton disease and weed management follow: three weedings, boll rot prevention on ridges, virus resistance through early planting, and stalk destruction as mandatory community pest hygiene. Picking discipline — only open dry white bolls, multiple rounds, first picking kept separate, no wet storage — is where all the agronomic work is either captured or lost. The business module covers gin price, quality premiums, scheme credit and the debt-spiral discipline, and the growth path to seed-grower niche or group aggregation for better prices.

What You Will Learn

  • Understand cotton's outgrower model: scheme rules, input credit, prescribed variety, and compliance discipline
  • Establish certified treated seed at correct spacing, depth and furrow planting into first effective rains
  • Split fertiliser: P at planting in the furrow, N in two applications at thinning and flower bud
  • Master scouting: W-pattern weekly, economic thresholds, only spray when justified — the yield gap between farmers
  • Manage boll rot, leaf curl virus, angular leaf spot and root rot with variety and drainage, not chemistry
  • Weed three times on the right calendar; return stalks and rotate legumes for K and pest breaks
  • Pick only open dry clean bolls, three rounds, first separate, no wet storage, deliver promptly
  • Read the gin price before planting, manage scheme credit with records, and grow for quality premiums

Lesson 1.1 — Cotton's place in African smallholder farming

Cotton is Africa's most important non-food cash crop: grown across the Sahel, savanna and east African drylands, it is the primary cash income for millions of smallholder families who depend on its ginned lint price to pay school fees, medical bills and farm inputs. Organised buyer networks — ginners, outgrower schemes and government purchasing agencies — exist almost everywhere cotton grows, giving it one of the continent's most reliable (if price-volatile) market structures. The crop is also labour-intensive at picking, making it a community crop where family and hired hands work together at peak.

  • Season: 150–200 days — a long-season commitment matched to the main rains.
  • Yields: African smallholder average ~600–800 kg seed cotton/ha; well-managed plots reach 1.5–2.5 t/ha.
  • Products: lint (the white fibre, ~38–42% of seed cotton), cottonseed (crushed for oil and cake), and stalks (fuel and mulch).
  • Markets: cotton is bought as seed cotton by ginneries — no farm processing required to sell; gin price is the benchmark.

Lesson 1.3 — The outgrower model

Most African cotton is grown under outgrower or contract farming schemes: the ginner or an intermediary supplies seed, inputs (sometimes on credit) and a guaranteed purchase price; the farmer delivers seed cotton at collection points. This model provides market certainty but also discipline — variety, planting date and pesticide use are often prescribed. Know your scheme's rules before planting; non-compliance often means loss of input credit and price guarantee.

Month 2: Seed, Spacing, Fertility & the Early Stand
Locked

Lesson 2.1 — Seed and planting

Cotton seed is large (relatively) and delicate: use only certified, delinted, fungicide-treated seed from your scheme or agro-dealer — untreated seed germinates poorly and carries soil-borne diseases. Planting rules:

  • Timing: first effective rains (enough soil moisture to 30 cm); in most zones, a 1–2 week window exists — don't rush (cold soil = poor germination) and don't delay (late = late harvest).
  • Spacing: rows 90–100 cm, stations 25–30 cm, 2 seeds/station, thinned to 1; population ~33,000–45,000/ha.
  • Depth: 2–3 cm — cotton emerges from its food reserves, so depth is critical.
  • Furrow planting: many schemes require furrows to collect rainwater at the row base.

Lesson 2.3 — Early stand and thinning

Cotton emerges in 5–10 days in warm moist soil. Thin to one plant per station at 2–3 weeks — crowded plants produce vegetative competition and small bolls. Gap-fill within 2 weeks using a transplant or overseed from the same batch. Thinned seedlings can be transplanted on a rainy day (cotton takes transplant reasonably well at seedling stage). Scout for cutworms at emergence — circular cut seedlings at ground level are the signature; evening hand collection works on small plots.

Month 3: Pest Scouting, Thresholds & the Spray Decision
Locked

Lesson 3.1 — Cotton's pest list: the most demanding crop in this college

Cotton attracts more pest species than any other crop in this curriculum. Scouting is not optional — it is the heart of cotton farming. The critical mental model: economic thresholds exist for every pest; chemical treatment is justified only when pest numbers cross the threshold. Below-threshold treatment wastes money, kills natural enemies, and breeds resistance. Above-threshold treatment saves bolls and income. The difference between a 700 kg and a 2 t farmer is often entirely scouting discipline.

Lesson 3.3 — Scouting: the core skill

Weekly scouting from thinning to boll opening: walk a W-pattern through the field, inspect 10–20 plants per stop, count larvae, adults, eggs, boll entry holes, and estimate percent damage. Record on a simple sheet. Your scheme's threshold table tells you when to spray. No spray before the threshold; no delay after it. Scouting is the cheapest pest control in the budget.

Month 4: Weeds, Diseases & Crop Health
Locked

Lesson 4.1 — Weeds: the early war

Cotton's long season and slow early growth make it highly weed-sensitive in the first 6 weeks. Two or three weedings, starting at thinning (2–3 weeks), are the standard programme:

  1. First weeding at thinning (2–3 weeks): hand-hoe or inter-row cultivation; remove all weeds before they set seed or compete seriously; thin to one plant per station.
  2. Second weeding at 6–8 weeks: at first flower bud appearance; critical — late weeds at this stage directly reduce boll set by competing for light and nutrients.
  3. Third weeding / last cultivation at 10–12 weeks: final clean-up; also a chance to ridge up soil at the base of plants for support and water concentration.

Lesson 4.2 — Cotton diseases

  • Boll rot (Colletotrichum, Alternaria, bacterial): Open bolls infected by rain splash or soil contact; late planting, poor drainage and dense canopy are risk factors. Grow on ridges, drain fields, harvest promptly when bolls open.
  • Cotton leaf curl virus (CLCuV): transmitted by whitefly; cupped, curled, small leaves; stunted plant; no cure. Resistant varieties where available; early planting to miss peak whitefly; control whitefly vigorously where endemic.
  • Angular leaf spot (Xanthomonas): angular water-soaked lesions, wet season; clean seed, rotation, resistant varieties.
  • Root rot (Pythium, Phytophthora) at emergence: waterlogged soils kill seedlings; improve drainage, plant on ridges, use treated seed.

Lesson 4.3 — Crop nutrition through the season

Cotton is a heavy feeder: a 1 t/ha yield removes ~40–60 kg N, 20–30 kg P₂O₅, 60–100 kg K₂O from the soil. Where the crop is grown continuously on the same field, potassium depletion and organic matter loss happen quietly and compound. Return cotton stalks (chopped) to the field after harvest to partially replace K and organic matter; add manure on alternate seasons; and rotate with legumes (cowpea, groundnut) to rebuild N and break pest cycles.

Month 5: Boll Quality, Picking & Post-Picking Handling
Locked

Lesson 5.1 — Boll development and quality

Cotton lint quality is determined in the field, not the gin. Three quality enemies:

  • Staining from insect honeydew (aphid, whitefly), rain splash on open bolls, and early picking of unripe bolls — all downgrade lint colour.
  • Short fibre from water stress at boll fill, early-season pests, and poor variety choice.
  • High trash content from picking leaves, twigs and dirt into the bag — a direct gin-price cut.

Lesson 5.2 — Picking: the discipline

Pick only fully open, dry, white-fluffy bolls. Never pick:

  • Green or half-open bolls (immature, staining)
  • Wet or dew-covered bolls (mould in the bag)
  • Bolls with leaves, twigs or sand mixed in (trash cut at gin)

Multiple picking rounds: first picking at 50–60% boll opening, second and third at 2–3 week intervals. Never mix first and later pickings (later pickings are more weathered and lower quality); sell separately where the scheme permits. Pick into clean cotton bags or baskets — never off the ground, never into vegetable sacks or fertiliser bags (chemical staining).

Lesson 5.3 — Post-picking handling

  • Sun-dry picked cotton same day on clean tarpaulins if any moisture — never store wet seed cotton in closed bags (heat and fermentation destroy fibre quality within 24 hours).
  • Store in clean open-weave cotton bags (not woven plastic) in a dry, ventilated space — temperature and moisture are the enemies.
  • Deliver to collection point promptly — same day or next day where possible; the gin price is based on quality at delivery.

Lesson 5.4 — Stalk destruction after harvest

Destroy cotton stalks promptly after final picking — chop, pull and either return (partial K/organic matter) or burn (pink bollworm prevention where schemes require). Late-standing stalks are the overwintering hotel for pink bollworm and other pests, seeding next season's problem. Many schemes prescribe a mandatory destruction date with penalties for non-compliance — know your scheme rule.

Month 6: Gin Price, Budget & the Scheme-Credit Discipline
Locked

Lesson 6.1 — Cotton income: the gin price and quality premium

The gin price (seed cotton price) is set by the ginner, scheme or government price board. In many countries, it is announced before planting season — use it to assess the budget's viability before committing labour and inputs. Quality premiums (for clean, unstained, full-boll cotton) exist in some schemes and are worth knowing. Where no premium is paid for quality, it still pays you in volume: clean cotton picks faster, stores better and wastes less at delivery.

Lesson 6.3 — Scheme credit and the debt spiral to avoid

Most African cotton schemes supply inputs on credit deducted at delivery. This is convenient but creates a risk: crop failure or poor yield means debt carried into next season. Avoid the spiral: grow only what you can manage well; don't over-commit on area when family labour is uncertain; insist on records of input credit received and deducted (your receipt is your audit); and keep 10–20% of your cotton separate as the emergency float for next season's cash needs.

Lesson 6.4 — Records and the growth path

Per plot per season: variety and scheme; planting date; stand count at thinning; weeding dates; scout records (week, pest, count, threshold decision, spray or not); fertiliser applications with dates; picking round dates and weights per round; delivery date and gin price received; credit deducted; net income. Report card: total yield (kg/ha), scouting rate (weeks scouted/weeks growing), and net income after debt. Growth path: year one — one hectare managed to scheme standard with scouting discipline; year two — potentially expand area OR invest in own hand-pump or irrigation for a food crop alongside cotton; year three — seed grower for the scheme (clean high-yield seed at premium) or group aggregation for a better gin price.

  1. Scheme choice & climate case: ginnery/scheme selected, gin price, prescribed variety, input package terms, climate and soil suitability, collection point distance.
  2. Establishment plan: seed source and treatment status, land prep (furrows at 90–100 cm), planting date tied to first effective rains, spacing, depth, furrow P placement, thinning date and N split schedule.
  3. Scouting protocol: your scouting record sheet, weekly W-pattern plan, pest list with scheme thresholds, approved products with their prices, and your spray-decision rules (spray if and only if threshold crossed).
  4. Disease & weed calendar: three weedings with dates, boll rot and virus prevention strategies (drainage, early planting, variety), stalk-destruction plan with committed date.
  5. Picking & quality plan: first-picking trigger (50–60% boll opening), three-round calendar, quality rules (open, dry, clean, no mixing of rounds), post-picking drying and storage protocol, delivery target (same or next day).
  6. Budget & credit discipline: full budget with gin price scenario (at forecast price), labour breakdown (picking as the biggest cost), credit deduction estimate, net income target, and receipt-keeping plan.
  7. Risk page & year-3 vision: three risks (crop failure/debt, pest resistance, poor gin price) with mitigations, and the growth path — one managed hectare, then possibly seed-grower niche or group aggregation.

Submission standard: A plan you could hand to a savings group, a bank officer, or a family investor without embarrassment. Real local prices, real named buyers, honest risks.

📚 Glossary

Seed cotton
Unginned cotton as harvested — lint fibres still attached to the seed; sold to ginneries.
Lint
The white fibre separated from the seed by the ginnery — the internationally traded commodity (~38–42% of seed cotton weight).
Ginnery
A processing facility that separates lint from seed, bales the lint, and crushes seed for oil and cake.
Gin price
Price per kg of seed cotton paid by the ginnery at collection; set by scheme, market or government board.
Outgrower scheme
A contract arrangement: ginner supplies seed, inputs (on credit) and a purchase price; farmer delivers seed cotton at harvest.
Economic threshold
The pest population level at which the yield loss (if no spray) exceeds the cost of the spray — the spray trigger.
W-pattern scouting
Walking a W-shape through the field, inspecting 10–20 plants per stop — a representative, low-bias sampling method.
Boll
The fruit of the cotton plant; matures from green to brown-and-splitting, revealing the white lint fibre.
Square (flower bud)
The pre-blossom bud stage of cotton — damaged by spiny bollworm; counts of squares vs dropped squares are a field-health metric.
American bollworm (Helicoverpa)
The primary boll pest: larva bores into bolls, leaving entry hole and frass; controlled at the economic threshold.
Pink bollworm
Internal boll pest; recognised by rosette flower or crossed lint; controlled primarily by stalk destruction post-harvest.
Cotton leaf curl virus (CLCuV)
Whitefly-transmitted virus causing cupped, curled, small leaves and stunting; no cure; managed by early planting and whitefly control.
Boll rot
Rain-splash or soil-contact fungal/bacterial infection of open bolls — prevented by ridges, drainage and prompt harvest.
Delinted seed
Seed from which the short fuzzy fibres (linters) have been removed — required for uniform germination and seed-drill planting.
Treated seed
Seed coated with fungicide (and sometimes insecticide) to protect against soil-borne pathogens and early insect attack.
Stalk destruction
Mandatory post-harvest removal or destruction of cotton stalks to eliminate pink bollworm overwintering habitat.
Picking rounds
Sequential harvests every 2–3 weeks to capture bolls as they open — typically 3 rounds per season.
Scheme credit
Inputs supplied on credit by the ginner, deducted from the farm-gate payment at delivery.
