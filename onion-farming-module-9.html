1. Determining Harvest Maturity

Knowing when to harvest onions is critical for storage life and market quality. Onions are ready when the tops (leaves) begin to yellow, weaken, and fall over naturally. This usually happens 90 to 120 days after transplanting, depending on variety and climate. In Africa, where many farmers grow short-day varieties like Red Creole or Bombay Red, maturity can be as early as 80 days in hot lowlands. Do not wait until all leaves are brownif the necks are still thick and green, the bulbs are not mature and will not store well.

A simple field test: dig up a few bulbs and check the neck. A mature onion has a thin, papery neck that is soft and dry. If the neck is thick and juicy, wait a few more days. Also, the skin should be tight and papery, and the bulb should feel firm. Another sign: the outer scales become dry and rustle when you rub them. In sandy soils, bulbs may push themselves partly out of the ground, making them easy to see.

Timing also depends on the weather. If rain is coming, harvest early to avoid rot. In dry areas like northern Nigeria or Senegal, let the tops dry completely in the field if no rain is expected. But in humid regions like Uganda or Cameroon, harvest as soon as 50% of the tops have fallen to prevent fungal diseases. Use a calendar and walk your field every 2-3 days as harvest approaches.

Do not harvest when the soil is wet; wet soil clings to bulbs and increases rot. If you must harvest after rain, lift bulbs and let them dry in a shaded, ventilated area before curing. Remember: over-mature onions may split or sprout in storage, while immature ones shrivel. Aim for 70-80% of tops fallen and dry. For seed production, wait until seed heads turn brown, but for bulb harvest, follow the leaf signs. A good rule: when you can pinch the neck and it feels like paper, it's time.

2. Harvesting Techniques: Lifting, Topping, and Windrowing

Harvesting onions by hand is common in Africa, but proper technique prevents bruising and extends shelf life. Start by loosening the soil around the bulbs with a fork or hoe, then lift them gently by the neck or under the bulb. Never pull by the leavesthey may tear, opening the bulb to rot. In large fields, you can use a plough or tractor-drawn lifter, but set it shallow to avoid cutting bulbs. Work in dry conditions, preferably in the morning after dew has dried.

After lifting, leave the onions in the field to dry for 2-3 days. This is called windrowing. Arrange them in rows with the tops of one row covering the bulbs of the next, so the bulbs are shaded from direct sun but still get air. In hot, dry areas like Egypt or Sudan, windrow for 3-5 days; in humid areas, only 1-2 days, then move to a shed. If rain threatens, cover windrows with tarps or move onions under shelter. The goal is to dry the outer skin and neck, not to cook the bulbs.

Topping (cutting the leaves) is done after drying. Leave about 2-3 cm of neck attached to the bulb. Cutting too close invites rot; leaving too much neck slows curing. Use sharp shears or a knife, and discard any bulbs with thick necks or signs of disease. In some regions, farmers braid the tops and hang them, but for bulk storage, topping is better. If you plan to store in sacks, topping is essential to reduce bulk and prevent sprouting.

Windrowing also helps synchronise maturity. After 2-3 days, the necks should be completely dry and the outer skin papery. If you see new roots emerging, the onions are re-growingmove them to a dry, ventilated place immediately. Remember: every bruise is an entry point for rot. Handle bulbs like eggs. Use baskets or crates, not sacks that crush. In Kenya, farmers often use wooden crates for transport to avoid damage. After windrowing, gather onions in the cool of the evening to reduce heat stress.

3. Curing Onions for Storage

Curing is the most important step for long-term onion storage. It dries the neck and outer scales, sealing the bulb against rot and sprouting. In Africa, where humidity can be high, curing must be done properly or you lose your harvest within weeks. There are two methods: field curing and artificial curing. Field curing works in dry areas with low rainfall. After windrowing, leave onions in the field for 2-3 weeks, turning them every few days. But if rain is likely, move them to a shaded, well-ventilated shed.

For artificial curing, spread onions in a single layer on racks or shelves in a dry, airy room. Aim for 25-30C and relative humidity below 70%. Good airflow is keyuse fans if available, or open windows and doors. In West Africa, some farmers use raised platforms under a thatched roof. Curing takes 2-4 weeks. The necks should be completely dry and papery, and the outer scales should rustle. When you cut a bulb, the neck should be tight and no moisture should appear.

During curing, check for rotten bulbs daily. Remove any soft, smelly, or sprouting onions immediatelythey release ethylene gas that speeds up spoilage of others. Also, protect from rain and direct sun. In humid regions like the Congo Basin, curing may take longer; consider using a solar dryer or a well-ventilated barn. A simple test: after 2 weeks, squeeze the neck of a few bulbs. If it's still green or damp, cure longer. If it's papery and dry, curing is done.

Once cured, onions can be stored for 3-6 months in cool, dry conditions. Without curing, they may last only 2-4 weeks. Curing also improves flavour and appearance. Do not wash onions before curingmoisture encourages rot. If onions are muddy, let them dry, then brush off soil gently. After curing, move to storage. Remember: curing is not drying in the sun until they bake; it's a slow, ventilated drying process. In Ethiopia, farmers store cured onions in ventilated sacks in a dark room. Always keep cured onions off the floor on pallets to prevent moisture uptake.

4. Grading and Sorting for Market

Grading and sorting onions increases your income because buyers pay more for uniform, high-quality bulbs. After curing, spread onions on a clean surface and remove any that are soft, bruised, diseased, or sprouting. Also remove bulbs with thick necks, doubles (two bulbs fused), or sunburn. These can be sold separately for processing or home use, but not for premium market. In Africa, many traders reject mixed sizes, so grading by size is essential.

Use simple tools: a wooden or plastic sizing ring, or just your hands. Common grades: large (over 6 cm diameter), medium (4-6 cm), and small (under 4 cm). In Kenya, large onions fetch up to 30% more than small ones. You can also grade by colourred, yellow, or whiteand by shape (round, flat). For export, stricter standards apply, but for local markets, consistent size and cleanliness matter most. Remove loose skins and trim necks to 1-2 cm.

Sorting also means separating onions for different uses. Firm, well-cured bulbs go for fresh market. Slightly blemished ones can be sold for processing (soups, sauces). Rotten ones must be destroyed or buried, not left in the field. In Nigeria, some farmers grade into three categories: Grade A (export), Grade B (urban supermarkets), Grade C (local markets). This increases overall returns.

Do grading in a shaded area to avoid sunscald. Use clean crates or baskets. Never mix grades in one containerbuyers will pay the lowest price. Also, grade before storage if you plan to sell later, because re-grading after storage causes extra handling damage. A good practice: grade at harvest, then store in labelled crates. For small-scale farmers, grading by size can be done with a simple mesh screen. Remember: uniform onions in a bag look professional and attract repeat buyers. In Tanzania, women's groups often do collective grading to meet larger orders. Always wear gloves to protect bulbs from fingernail cuts.

5. Packaging and Storage Conditions

Proper packaging and storage keep onions fresh and reduce losses. For storage, onions need cool temperatures (0-5C is ideal, but in Africa, 10-15C is more practical), low humidity (65-70%), and dark, ventilated conditions. In many African homes, a well-ventilated shed or room works. Avoid storing near fruits like bananas or tomatoesthey release ethylene that makes onions sprout. Also, keep away from strong-smelling chemicals.

Packaging options: for short-term storage (1-2 months), use mesh bags or netted sacks that allow airflow. For longer storage, use ventilated crates or wooden boxes. Never use plastic bagsthey trap moisture and cause rot. In West Africa, farmers often use jute sacks, but these absorb moisture; if used, keep them in a dry room. For transport to market, use rigid crates to prevent bruising. If you must use sacks, fill only halfway and do not stack heavy loads.

Stacking is important. Place crates on pallets 10-15 cm off the floor to allow air circulation and prevent moisture from the ground. Leave space between stacks for air movement. Do not stack more than 4-5 crates high, or the bottom ones will be crushed. Inspect stored onions every 2 weeks. Remove any sprouting or rotting bulbs immediately. A good storage room has vents near the floor and ceiling for natural convection.

For small quantities, you can hang braided onions in a cool, dry place. Braiding works best with long-necked varieties. For larger quantities, consider a simple evaporative cooling chamber (zero-energy cool chamber) made from bricks and sandit can keep onions 5-10C cooler than outside. In Kenya, such chambers reduce losses by 30%. Always label your storage with the date and variety. If you sell later when prices are high, proper storage pays off. Remember: onions are living bulbs; they respire. Good airflow is non-negotiable. In humid areas, use a dehumidifier if possible, or store only for short periods.

6. Reducing Post-Harvest Losses

Post-harvest losses in onions can reach 30-50% in Africa, but simple steps can cut them to under 10%. The main causes are bruising, rot, sprouting, and weight loss. Start by harvesting at the right maturity, then handle gently. Every bruise becomes a rot entry point. Use padded baskets, avoid throwing onions, and never walk on them. In Nigeria, farmers who switched from sacks to plastic crates reduced losses by 25%.

Curing is your best defence. Proper curing reduces rot by up to 70%. After curing, store in cool, dry, ventilated conditions. Control temperature and humidity: if you see condensation on walls, increase ventilation. In humid regions, use charcoal or silica gel in storage rooms to absorb moisture. Also, remove diseased bulbs immediatelyone rotten onion can spoil a whole crate. Check stored onions weekly and discard any soft or sprouting ones.

Sprouting is a major loss. To prevent it, store at low temperatures (below 10C) or use sprout inhibitors like maleic hydrazide (approved in some countries). For organic farmers, keep storage dark and cool. Also, avoid excessive nitrogen fertiliser during growthit makes bulbs sprout early. Harvest when 50-70% of tops fall; late harvest increases sprouting. In Ethiopia, farmers who harvest on time and cure well store onions for 5 months with less than 5% loss.

Transport losses are also high. Use crates, not sacks, and load carefully. Avoid long journeys in open trucks under hot sun. If you must transport far, do it at night or early morning. For market, sell in small lots to avoid repeated handling. Finally, keep records: note how much you harvest, store, and sell. This helps you identify where losses happen. In Ghana, a simple loss log helped farmers reduce waste by 15% in one season. Share tips with neighbourscollective action improves everyone's storage. Remember: a little care after harvest doubles your profit.

<h2>Practical Reference: Harvesting and Post-Harvest Handling of Onions in Africa</h2>

<p>Onion farming in Africa is a high-value business, but the money is not made in the field alone. It is made in the careful transition from a mature bulb to a market-ready sack. Across major production zones the Nile Delta, the Ethiopian Rift Valley, the Sahel, the Kenyan highlands, and South Africa's Western Cape post-harvest losses of 2050% are common. For a commercial grower, that is not an agricultural statistic; it is lost revenue, lost loan repayment capacity, and lost market credibility. This practical reference covers the six core post-harvest lessons: determining harvest maturity, harvesting techniques, curing, grading and sorting, packaging and storage, and reducing losses.</p>

<h3>1. Determining Harvest Maturity</h3>
<p>Harvest too early and bulbs are thin, poorly cured, and prone to rot. Harvest too late and bulbs split, sunburn, or re-sprout. The reliable indicators are:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Top fall:</strong> 5070% of the tops have fallen over naturally for storage onions; 100% for immediate-sale crops.</li>
<li><strong>Neck softness:</strong> The neck tissue just above the bulb feels soft and pinches easily.</li>
<li><strong>Skin colour:</strong> Outer scales are dry, papery, and show the variety's true colour.</li>
<li><strong>Days after transplanting:</strong> 90150 days depending on variety and altitude.</li>
<li><strong>Bulb firmness:</strong> Firm, with no visible new root growth at the base.</li>
</ul>
<p>In African markets, timing also responds to price. If prices are high and the crop is 90% mature, a strategic early harvest for short-distance sale can be more profitable than waiting for full storage maturity. But for storage onions destined for months of holding, wait for full maturity.</p>

<h3>2. Harvesting Techniques: Lifting, Topping, and Windrowing</h3>
<p>Harvesting is a sequence, not a single action. Done poorly, it creates wounds that become entry points for <em>Aspergillus niger</em> (black mould), <em>Botrytis</em>, and bacterial soft rot.</p>
<p><strong>Lifting:</strong> Use a fork, hoe, or mechanical lifter to undercut the bulb at 510 cm depth. Never pull by the tops this breaks the neck and invites rot. In sandy soils of Senegal and Niger, hand lifting with a hoe is common; in the mechanised farms of South Africa and Egypt, tractor-mounted lifters are standard.</p>
<p><strong>Topping:</strong> Cut the roots and tops to 23 cm above the bulb. Topping is done either immediately after lifting (for short-cured crops) or after field drying (for storage crops). Use sharp, clean shears or knives. Do not cut into the bulb itself.</p>
<p><strong>Windrowing:</strong> After lifting, leave bulbs in the field in rows (windrows) for 37 days to dry. In humid coastal West Africa, windrowing is risky; move to a covered curing shed instead. In dry Sahelian and North African conditions, full field windrowing is ideal.</p>

<h3>3. Curing Onions for Storage</h3>
<p>Curing is the single most important step for storage life. It dries the neck, seals the bulb, and forms 23 layers of dry outer scales. In dry African climates (Egypt, Sudan, Niger, northern Nigeria), field curing for 23 weeks works well. In humid zones (Ghana, Uganda, coastal Kenya), move onions to a well-ventilated shed with fans or natural cross-ventilation.</p>
<p>Do not rush curing. A properly cured onion has a completely dry neck, rustling outer skins, and no green tissue. Poorly cured onions develop neck rot within weeks.</p>

<h3>4. Grading and Sorting for Market</h3>
<p>Grading is where price is captured. Buyers from local traders to supermarket chains and exporters pay premiums for uniform size, clean skins, and consistent quality. Sorting removes defective bulbs; grading separates them by size and quality class.</p>

<h3>5. Packaging and Storage Conditions</h3>
<p>Packaging must allow airflow. Polypropylene sacks with mesh, net bags, or ventilated crates are appropriate. Never use sealed plastic bags or tightly woven sacks they trap moisture and cause rot. For long storage, stack on pallets with 2030 cm gaps between stacks for air circulation.</p>

<h3>6. Reducing Post-Harvest Losses</h3>
<p>Losses occur at every step. The main causes in African onion value chains are:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Mechanical damage</strong> during lifting, topping, and transport (3040% of losses).</li>
<li><strong>Sunburn and sunscald</strong> from leaving bulbs exposed too long.</li>
<li><strong>Neck rot and black mould</strong> from poor curing and humid storage.</li>
<li><strong>Rodent and insect damage</strong> in poorly constructed stores.</li>
<li><strong>Weight loss and sprouting</strong> in warm, humid conditions.</li>
<li><strong>Market rejection</strong> due to poor grading and dirty bulbs.</li>
</ul>
<p>Practical loss-reduction actions: harvest at correct maturity, lift carefully, cure thoroughly, grade before storage, use ventilated packaging, keep stores clean and dry, and sell in a planned sequence rather than dumping all at once.</p>

<strong style="color:#c0392b;"> SAFETY WARNING Onion Harvest and Storage Hazards</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0;color:#7b241c;">Onion harvesting and storage can cause serious injury. Always wear gloves when lifting with forks or hoes to prevent cuts and puncture wounds. Topping knives and shears must be sharp and used away from the body. In closed stores, ammonia and carbon dioxide from rotting bulbs can accumulate never enter a sealed onion store without ventilation. Pesticide-treated onions must be clearly labelled and never sold for food use before the pre-harvest interval has passed. Follow national pesticide regulations and keep all agrochemicals locked away from children and food.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Confirm 5070% top fall and soft necks before harvesting storage onions.</li>
<li>Lift bulbs with a fork or lifter never pull by the tops.</li>
<li>Top to 23 cm above the bulb; do not cut into the bulb.</li>
<li>Windrow 37 days in dry conditions; cure indoors in humid zones.</li>
<li>Cure until necks are fully dry and outer scales papery (24 weeks).</li>
<li>Grade by size and remove all damaged, diseased, or thick-necked bulbs.</li>
<li>Pack in ventilated mesh or net bags; never sealed plastic.</li>
<li>Store cool, dry, and dark; inspect every 23 weeks and remove rots immediately.</li>
</ol>
