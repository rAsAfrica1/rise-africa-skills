1. Compressors: Types and Selection

A compressor is the heart of any refrigeration system. It pumps refrigerant gas from the evaporator to the condenser, raising its pressure and temperature. In Africa, you will mostly see three types: hermetic, semi-hermetic, and open-drive. Hermetic compressors are sealed and cheap, common in domestic fridges and small cold rooms. Semi-hermetic can be repaired and are used in commercial cold rooms, like those storing fish in Mombasa. Open-drive compressors are belt-driven and found in older or very large systems.

When selecting a compressor, match it to the cooling load. For a 10 mÂ³ cold room at 0Â°C, you need about 1.5 to 2 kW of cooling capacity. A 1 HP semi-hermetic compressor (like a Bitzer or Copeland) can handle that. Always check the refrigerant type: R134a for medium temp, R404A for low temp. In Africa, R22 is still common but being phased out due to ozone damageâuse R407C or R410A as alternatives.

Tools you need: manifold gauge set, clamp meter, and a thermometer. To test a compressor, measure suction and discharge pressures. Normal suction for a 0Â°C room is around 2-3 bar, discharge 12-15 bar. If suction is too low, you may have a restriction or low refrigerant. If discharge is too high, check for air in the system or a dirty condenser.

Installation tips: always mount the compressor on rubber pads to reduce vibration. Keep it level. Ensure proper ventilationâat least 30 cm clearance around it. In hot African climates, oversize the compressor by 10-20% to handle high ambient temperatures. Regular maintenance: check oil level every 3 months, change oil annually if mineral, every 2 years if POE. Listen for unusual noisesâknocking means internal wear.

Finally, never start a compressor without checking that the suction service valve is open. A closed valve will destroy the compressor in minutes. Always use a qualified technician for electrical connections, as wrong wiring can burn the motor.

2. Condensers: Air-Cooled and Water-Cooled

The condenser removes heat from the refrigerant gas, turning it back into a liquid. In Africa, air-cooled condensers are most common because water is scarce and expensive. They use fans to blow air over finned tubes. Water-cooled condensers use water from a cooling tower or municipal supply, and are more efficient but need constant water and treatment.

For a small cold room in Nairobi, an air-cooled condenser with a 1/3 HP fan motor is typical. It should be placed outside or in a well-ventilated area, never in a closed room. Ambient temperature matters: if it's 35Â°C outside, the condenser must be sized to reject heat at that temperature. A rule of thumb: condenser capacity should be 1.2 to 1.5 times the evaporator capacity. So for a 2 kW evaporator, use a 2.5-3 kW condenser.

Water-cooled condensers are found in large dairies or breweries. They use shell-and-tube design. You need a water pump and a cooling tower. In Lagos, a 10 kW system might use 1.5 mÂ³/h of water. But water quality is criticalâhard water scales the tubes, reducing efficiency. Install a water softener or use treated water. Clean the tubes every 6 months with a brush and descaling solution.

Maintenance for air-cooled: clean the fins every month with a soft brush and compressed air. Dust and cottonwood block airflow. Check fan blades for cracks. Lubricate fan bearings every 3 months. For water-cooled: check water flow with a flow meter, clean strainers weekly, and inspect for leaks. In dusty regions like the Sahel, consider a filter on the air intake.

A common mistake is undersizing the condenser. If the condenser is too small, discharge pressure rises, compressor works harder, and electricity bill shoots up. Always calculate heat rejection: total heat = cooling load + compressor power. For a 2 kW cooling load with a 1 kW compressor, condenser must reject 3 kW. Use that to select the model.

3. Evaporators and Unit Coolers

The evaporator absorbs heat from the cold room, cooling the air. It's usually a finned coil with a fan, called a unit cooler. In Africa, you'll see wall-mounted, ceiling-mounted, and floor-standing units. For a 20 mÂ³ cold room at 2Â°C for vegetables, a ceiling-mounted unit cooler with 2 fans and a 1.5 kW capacity works well. For frozen storage at -20Â°C, you need a low-temperature evaporator with a larger surface area and defrost heaters.

Selection depends on temperature difference (TD) between the room and refrigerant. A typical TD is 8-10Â°C. So for a 0Â°C room, refrigerant evaporates at -8 to -10Â°C. If TD is too high, humidity drops and products dry out. If too low, you need a bigger, more expensive evaporator. Calculate cooling load first: for 1,000 kg of tomatoes, you need about 1.5 kW to cool from 30Â°C to 10Â°C in 24 hours. Add 20% for door openings and lights.

Installation: mount the unit so air blows across the room, not directly on products. Keep at least 30 cm clearance from walls and ceiling. Slope the drain pan toward the drain lineâcondensate must flow out. In humid areas like Kampala, insulate the drain line to prevent sweating. Use a trap on the drain to stop warm air entering.

Defrost is critical. For low-temp rooms, use electric heaters or hot gas defrost. Set defrost cycles every 4-6 hours for 15-20 minutes. Check defrost termination thermostatâit should cut off at 10Â°C. If not, ice builds up and blocks airflow. Clean the evaporator fins every month with a vacuum or soft brush. Check fan motors for smooth operation. Lubricate bearings every 6 months.

Common problems: ice on the coil means defrost failed or door left open. Low airflow means dirty fins or fan failure. High superheat means undercharge or expansion valve issue. Always use a thermometer to check air-on and air-off temperatures: air-off should be 5-8Â°C colder than air-on for medium temp. If not, investigate.

4. Expansion Valves and Controls

The expansion valve controls how much liquid refrigerant enters the evaporator. It also drops pressure, causing the refrigerant to boil and absorb heat. In Africa, you'll see thermostatic expansion valves (TXV), capillary tubes, and electronic expansion valves (EEV). TXVs are most common in commercial cold rooms. Capillary tubes are in small fridgesâthey are cheap but fixed. EEVs are precise but need a controller.

A TXV has a bulb, diaphragm, and spring. The bulb senses evaporator outlet temperature and pressure, adjusting the valve opening. Superheat is the difference between actual and boiling temperature. For a cold room, set superheat to 5-8Â°C. Too low, liquid floods the compressor. Too high, evaporator starves and room won't cool. To adjust, turn the superheat screw clockwise to increase superheat. Use a manifold gauge and a thermometer on the suction line.

Selecting a TXV: match its capacity to the evaporator. For a 2 kW evaporator with R404A, choose a TXV rated 2-3 kW. Always check the orifice sizeâit must match the refrigerant and capacity. In hot climates, use a balanced port TXV to handle varying condensing pressures. Install the bulb on a horizontal suction line, clean and insulated, at the 4 o'clock or 8 o'clock position. Never mount it on the bottomâoil can pool.

Other controls: low-pressure switch cuts out compressor if suction drops too low (e.g., 0.5 bar). High-pressure switch cuts out if discharge exceeds 25 bar. Defrost timer controls defrost cycles. Solenoid valve stops refrigerant flow when room reaches setpoint. Thermostat cycles the compressor. All these must be wired correctly. In Nigeria, power fluctuations are commonâuse a voltage stabilizer or surge protector.

Maintenance: check TXV for frost or iceâit may be stuck. Clean or replace if needed. Test pressure switches annually with a gauge. Set thermostat to desired temperature, e.g., 3Â°C for milk. Never bypass a safety switch. If a valve fails, replace with same model. Keep spare TXV bulbs and orifices. In dusty areas, cover controls with a plastic box to prevent dust ingress.

5. Piping and Pumps

Piping connects all components. In Africa, copper tubing is standard for refrigerant lines. Use soft copper for small systems, hard copper for larger. Size matters: suction lines must be large enough to avoid pressure drop, but not so large that oil doesn't return. For a 2 kW system, use 1/2 inch suction line, 1/4 inch liquid line. Discharge line is usually 3/8 inch. Always insulate suction lines with closed-cell elastomeric insulation, at least 13 mm thick. In hot climates, use 19 mm to prevent condensation.

Brazing is the method for joining copper. Use silfos (15% silver) for copper-to-copper, and flux for copper-to-brass. Purge with nitrogen while brazing to prevent oxidation inside the pipe. After brazing, pressure test with dry nitrogen to 20 bar for 24 hours. If pressure drops, leak test with soap bubbles. Never use oxygenâit's dangerous. For flares, use a flaring tool and torque wrench: 1/4 inch flare needs 15-20 Nm, 1/2 inch needs 50-60 Nm.

Pumps are used for water-cooled condensers, chilled water systems, and drain lines. A centrifugal pump is common. For a 10 kW condenser, you need about 1.5 mÂ³/h at 10 m head. Select a pump with 20% extra capacity. Install a strainer on the suction side to protect the impeller. Prime the pump before startingâdry running destroys seals. Check for leaks at the mechanical seal; replace if dripping.

Pipe supports: use clamps every 1.5 m for horizontal runs, every 2 m for vertical. Avoid sharp bendsâuse long-radius elbows. Slope suction lines toward the compressor at 1 cm per meter for oil return. Install a P-trap at the evaporator to collect oil. In dusty areas, seal pipe penetrations with silicone to keep insects out.

Maintenance: check for oil stains on pipesâsign of leak. Tighten fittings gently. Insulation gets brittleâreplace every 3-5 years. For pumps, check motor current with a clamp meter; if higher than nameplate, investigate. Lubricate pump bearings every 3 months. In areas with power cuts, install a non-return valve to prevent water hammer. Always keep a pipe cutter, deburring tool, and torch kit on hand.

6. Equipment Maintenance Schedules

A maintenance schedule keeps your cold room running and saves money. In Africa, where power is expensive and parts are imported, preventive maintenance is critical. Create a checklist for daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly, and annual tasks. Assign a technician and keep a logbook. For a small cold room, daily checks take 10 minutes; monthly service takes 2 hours.

Daily: check room temperature and compare to setpoint (e.g., 2Â°C for vegetables). Listen for unusual noises. Check for water leaks or ice buildup. Ensure doors close tightlyâadjust hinges if needed. Look at compressor oil level through sight glass; it should be between 1/4 and 3/4. Record suction and discharge pressures: normal suction 2-3 bar, discharge 12-15 bar for medium temp. If outside, investigate.

Weekly: clean condenser fins with a soft brush. Check fan blades for cracks. Clean drain pan and check drain line flow. Test defrost cycle manuallyâit should terminate within 20 minutes. Check door gaskets with a dollar bill test: if it slips out easily, replace gasket. Lubricate door hinges. Inspect electrical connections for tightnessâuse a screwdriver, not fingers.

Monthly: check compressor oil colorâif dark, change oil. Test high and low pressure switches. Clean or replace air filters if present. Measure compressor current with a clamp meter; compare to nameplate. Check belt tension on open-drive compressorsâdeflection should be 1 cm. Clean evaporator fins with vacuum. Check insulation on suction lines for gaps. Test thermostat calibration with an ice bath.

Quarterly: change oil if mineral (every 6 months for POE). Replace filter drier. Check all electrical terminals for corrosionâclean with contact cleaner. Test defrost heaters with a multimeter. Inspect pipe insulation for damage. Lubricate fan and pump bearings. Check water quality for water-cooled systems; clean strainers.

Annually: perform a full leak test with nitrogen and soap bubbles. Replace all gaskets and O-rings. Flush water-cooled condenser tubes with descaling solution. Overhaul compressor if hours exceed 8,000. Replace contactors and relays if pitted. Update logbook and plan next year's budget. In dusty regions, shorten intervals by half. Always keep spare parts: filters, gaskets, fan motors, and a manifold gauge set.

Why the Equipment Set Must Be Designed Together

Most cold store failures in Africa are not component failures; they are mismatch failures. A compressor selected for a 35 Â°C condensing temperature will run at 48â52 Â°C condensing temperature in a Kano or Juba summer, losing 15â25% capacity and drawing 20â30% more power. A condenser sized for European ambient conditions will not reject enough heat, so discharge pressure climbs, the expansion valve overfeeds, the evaporator floods, and liquid returns to the compressor. The result is a system that "works" but costs 40% more to run and fails two years early.

The correct approach is to size the whole loop for the local design ambient, not the catalogue default. In West and Sahelian Africa, use 40â43 Â°C dry-bulb design ambient for air-cooled condensers. In East and Southern Africa highveld, 32â35 Â°C is realistic. Condensers: Air-Cooled and Water-Cooled

Air-cooled condensers are the default in most African cold stores because water is scarce or expensive. The trade-off is that at 40 Â°C ambient, condensing pressure is high, power draw is high, and capacity is reduced. Mitigations that pay back within 12â24 months: shade the condenser from direct sun, ensure at least 1.5Ã fan diameter of free air on all sides, clean fins monthly in dusty areas, and use EC fans with variable speed. Adiabatic pre-cooling (spraying a fine mist of water on the incoming air) can cut condensing temperature by 8â12 K on the hottest days, but only where water quality and supply allow.

Water-cooled condensers (shell-and-tube or plate) with a cooling tower give lower condensing temperatures and better efficiency, but they demand water treatment, blowdown control, and a tower that does not become a legionella or algae risk. In coastal cities (Mombasa, Dar es Salaam, Lagos, Accra), salt-laden air corrodes air-cooled fins quickly; specify epoxy-coated or copper-fin coils, or use water-cooled with a closed-circuit tower.

Evaporators and Unit Coolers

Evaporator selection is about the temperature difference (TD) between room air and refrigerant. A small TD (6â8 K) gives high humidity and low weight loss â essential for fresh produce, meat, and dairy. A large TD (10â12 K) gives faster pull-down and lower capital cost â acceptable for frozen goods and ice. In African cold stores, the most common mistake is oversizing the evaporator for pull-down and then running it at low load, causing short cycling and poor humidity control. Match the unit cooler to the steady-state load, not the pull-down load.

Defrost strategy matters. In humid coastal and highland areas, electric defrost every 4â6 hours is common; in dry inland areas, off-cycle or hot-gas defrost may suffice. Always slope the drain pan and heat-trace the drain line â a blocked drain in a 90% RH environment becomes an ice block within days.

Expansion Valves and Controls

Thermostatic expansion valves (TXVs) remain the standard for small and medium systems. Electronic expansion valves (EEVs) cost more but hold superheat more accurately across wide load and ambient swings â a real advantage in Africa where ambient can swing 20 K between morning and afternoon. Set superheat at 5â8 K at the evaporator outlet; below 4 K risks liquid return, above 10 K starves the evaporator and wastes capacity.

Controls to insist on: a high-pressure cut-out, a low-pressure cut-out, an oil-pressure safety, a phase-failure/voltage relay (critical in Africa), and a defrost timer or demand defrost controller. A simple GSM or Wi-Fi alarm module that texts the manager on high temperature or high pressure costs little and has saved more produce than any single piece of equipment.

Piping and Pumps

Correct piping is invisible when it works and catastrophic when it does not. Key rules: size suction lines for a pressure drop of no more than 1 K (about 0.5â1.5% of absolute pressure); slope suction lines toward the compressor at least 1:200; fit a P-trap at the base of every suction riser; insulate all suction lines with closed-cell elastomeric insulation (25 mm minimum in hot plant rooms); and support lines so vibration does not fatigue joints. Liquid lines need a filter-drier, a sight glass with moisture indicator, and a solenoid valve at the receiver.

Pumps appear in two places: condenser water pumps and, in some designs, secondary refrigerant (glycol) pumps for blast freezers. Choose pumps with a 10â15% head margin, install a strainer on the suction, and keep a spare mechanical seal and coupling on site. In areas with frequent power cuts, fit a soft starter or VFD to avoid water hammer on restart.

â  SAFETY WARNING â REFRIGERANT AND ELECTRICAL HAZARDS

Refrigerant release in a closed plant room can displace oxygen and cause rapid asphyxiation; ammonia (R717) is toxic and flammable at high concentrations, and hydrocarbon refrigerants (R290, R600a) are highly flammable. Never enter a plant room after a suspected leak without forced ventilation, a calibrated gas detector, and a buddy outside. Never braze, weld, or open a system without recovering refrigerant to a cylinder â venting to atmosphere is illegal under the Montreal Protocol and Kigali Amendment and dangerous. Isolate and lock off electrical supplies before working on compressors, pumps, or controls; capacitor banks hold lethal charge after shutdown. In Africa, where grid voltage can swing from 180 V to 260 V, use a lockable isolator and verify dead with a tester before touching conductors. Only trained, certified refrigeration technicians should work on these systems.

Equipment Maintenance Schedules

Preventive maintenance is the highest-return activity in a cold store. A 30-minute weekly check prevents the 3-day breakdown that spoils 20 tonnes of produce. Build the schedule below into your operations manual and log every action with date, technician, and reading.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Daily: Record suction and discharge pressures, room temperature, and compressor running hours; check for unusual noise or oil level.
  2. Weekly: Clean condenser fins (dusty African sites need this weekly, not monthly); check fan blades and belts; inspect drain lines for ice or blockage.
  3. Weekly: Verify defrost cycles are completing and terminating on temperature, not just time.
  4. Monthly: Check superheat and subcooling; clean or replace filter-driers; test all safety cut-outs and the voltage/phase relay.
  5. Monthly: Inspect electrical terminals for heat discolouration and tighten to torque; check insulation resistance of compressor motor.
  6. Quarterly: Oil analysis on screw and large reciprocating compressors; leak-test the whole circuit with an electronic detector; clean cooling tower and dose water treatment.
  7. Annually: Full system performance test against design; replace worn seals, gaskets, and contactors; re-calibrate sensors and controllers; review refrigerant charge and top up only after finding and fixing the leak.
  8. Always: Keep a spare parts kit on site â filter-driers, contactors, fan motors, TXV, oil, and a refrigerant cylinder â because in many African cities, a 2-day wait for a spare is a 2-day loss of stock.
