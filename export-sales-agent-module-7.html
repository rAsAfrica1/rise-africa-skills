1. Understanding Export Sales in the African Context

Export sales in Africa is not simply a mirror of export sales in Europe or Asia. The African market is a mosaic of 54 countries, each with its own currency, regulatory framework, logistics reality, and consumer behaviour. An export sales agent working in this environment must be part trader, part diplomat, part logistics coordinator, and part risk manager. This lesson sets the foundation for everything that follows in Module 7. We begin by defining what export sales actually means in practical terms. Export sales is the process of selling goods or services produced in one country to buyers located in another country. The seller may be a manufacturer, a trading company, or an intermediary. The buyer may be a wholesaler, a retailer, a government agency, or an industrial end user. In Africa, the most common export flows include agricultural commodities such as cocoa, coffee, cashew, and sesame; minerals and metals; manufactured goods such as processed foods, cosmetics, and building materials; and services such as logistics, consulting, and digital solutions. The export sales agent sits at the centre of these flows. Your job is to identify buyers, build trust, negotiate terms, coordinate documentation, and ensure that goods or services reach the buyer in the condition and timeframe promised. That sounds straightforward, but the African context adds layers of complexity. First, consider the regulatory environment. Every country has its own import rules, tariff schedules, and standards requirements. A product that sells freely in Kenya may require a certificate of conformity in Nigeria or an import licence in Algeria. Regional economic communities such as ECOWAS, SADC, COMESA, and the East African Community have harmonised some rules, but implementation varies. An effective export sales agent builds a working knowledge of the target market rules before quoting a price. Second, consider logistics. Africa has some of the highest freight and insurance costs in the world. Port congestion, poor road networks, border delays, and limited cold chain capacity all affect delivery. A shipment from Mombasa to Kampala may take longer and cost more than a shipment from Shanghai to Mombasa. The export sales agent must therefore price logistics accurately and communicate realistic lead times to buyers. Third, consider payment risk. Currency volatility, foreign exchange shortages, and weak banking infrastructure in some markets mean that getting paid is not automatic. Agents must understand instruments such as letters of credit, documentary collections, advance payment, and open account terms. They must also know how to assess buyer creditworthiness using trade references, bank checks, and credit insurance. Fourth, consider cultural and relationship factors. In many African markets, business is personal. Buyers want to know who you are, who you represent, and whether you can be trusted. Long-term relationships often matter more than the lowest price. An export sales agent who understands this will invest time in face-to-face meetings, trade fairs, and local representation. Now let us look at the profile of a successful export sales agent in Africa. You need commercial awareness to understand pricing, margins, and competitive positioning. You need technical knowledge of your product and its applications. You need operational discipline to manage documentation, shipping schedules, and payments. You need cross-cultural communication skills to work with buyers, agents, and officials from different backgrounds. And you need resilience, because export deals rarely close on the first attempt. A practical example illustrates the point. A Kenyan exporter of macadamia nuts wants to sell to a buyer in Germany. The export sales agent must first confirm that the product meets EU pesticide residue limits and food safety standards. Then the agent must identify a buyer, perhaps through a trade fair such as Anuga or Fruit Logistica. After initial interest, the agent sends samples, negotiates price and Incoterms, and agrees on payment terms. The agent then coordinates with the freight forwarder, prepares the commercial invoice, packing list, certificate of origin, phytosanitary certificate, and bill of lading. Finally, the agent monitors payment and handles any quality disputes. Each step requires attention to detail and proactive communication. In Africa, the export sales agent also plays a development role. By connecting local producers to international markets, you help create jobs, generate foreign exchange, and transfer knowledge. This is not just a commercial activity; it is a contribution to economic growth. But do not let that noble purpose obscure the need for commercial rigour. Export sales is a business, and it must be profitable to be sustainable. Throughout this module, we will cover the full export sales cycle: market research and buyer identification, pricing and Incoterms, documentation and compliance, negotiation and relationship management, and finally risk management and payment collection. Each lesson builds on the previous one. By the end of Module 7, you should be able to take a product or service and develop a practical export sales plan for at least one African market. Before we move on, take a moment to reflect on your own context. What products or services are you selling? Which African countries are your natural targets? What are the biggest obstacles you face? Write these down. They will guide your learning throughout the module. Remember that export sales in Africa rewards preparation, patience, and persistence. There are no shortcuts, but there is enormous opportunity for those who do the work.

2. Market Research and Buyer Identification Across Africa

The first operational step in export sales is finding the right market and the right buyer. Too many exporters waste time and money chasing markets that are not viable for their product. This lesson gives you a practical framework for market research and buyer identification in Africa. We start with market selection. Not every African country is a good fit for every product. You need to assess market size, growth rate, import dependence, tariff and non-tariff barriers, competition, and ease of doing business. A simple scoring model can help. List potential countries and score each on a scale of one to five for factors such as GDP per capita, import volume for your product category, distance and freight cost, existing trade agreements, and currency stability. The countries with the highest total scores deserve deeper investigation. Data sources for this exercise include ITC Trade Map, UN Comtrade, national statistics offices, central banks, and industry associations. Trade Map is particularly useful because it shows import values, quantities, and growth rates by product and partner country. If you cannot afford the subscription, use the free version or ask your national export promotion agency for help. Once you have a shortlist of countries, you need to understand the competitive landscape. Who are the existing suppliers? What are their prices, quality levels, and delivery terms? Are there local producers who benefit from protection? What do buyers complain about? Trade fairs, industry magazines, and online B2B platforms can provide clues. You can also commission a small market study from a local consultant, but be clear about your objectives and budget. Now let us turn to buyer identification. There are several categories of buyers you might target. Importers and distributors buy in bulk and resell to wholesalers or retailers. They are often the best entry point for new exporters because they know the local market and can handle customs clearance. Wholesalers buy in large volumes and sell to retailers. Retail chains, such as supermarkets, may buy directly from exporters if volumes are large enough. Industrial buyers purchase raw materials or components for manufacturing. Government agencies buy through tenders, which can be lucrative but slow and bureaucratic. Finally, online marketplaces and B2B platforms allow you to reach smaller buyers directly. To find these buyers, use a mix of channels. Trade directories such as Kompass, Europages, and Africa Business Pages list importers by country and sector. Embassies and trade missions can provide lists of potential buyers and arrange introductions. Industry associations often publish member directories. Trade fairs are excellent for face-to-face meetings; some of the most important for African exporters include the Canton Fair in China, SIAL in Paris, Gulfood in Dubai, and Intra-African Trade Fair. LinkedIn and other professional networks can help you identify procurement managers and category buyers. When you have a list of potential buyers, you need to qualify them. Not every buyer is worth your time. Ask yourself: Does this buyer import products similar to mine? What volumes do they handle? Do they have a good reputation for payment? Are they looking for new suppliers? A simple qualification checklist can save you months of wasted effort. You can also use trade references. Ask the buyer for names of current suppliers and contact them. Most suppliers will share their experience if approached professionally. Credit reports from agencies such as Dun and Bradstreet or COFACE can provide financial background, though coverage in Africa may be limited. Once you have qualified buyers, you need to make contact. The first approach should be professional and concise. Introduce your company, your product, and your unique selling proposition. Attach a product catalogue or specification sheet. Do not send a generic mass email; personalise every message. If possible, follow up with a phone call or a meeting at a trade fair. In many African markets, a local agent or representative can open doors more effectively than direct contact from abroad. Consider appointing a commission agent in the target country. A good agent knows the buyers, speaks the language, and can handle local logistics. But choose carefully. Ask for references, agree on clear commission terms, and monitor performance. Now let us consider a practical example. A Ghanaian producer of shea butter wants to export to the United States. The export sales agent first uses Trade Map to confirm that US imports of shea butter have been growing. Then the agent identifies potential buyers through the American Shea Butter Institute, trade fairs such as Natural Products Expo West, and LinkedIn. After qualifying five buyers, the agent sends samples and price quotes. Two buyers respond with interest. The agent negotiates terms and eventually secures a trial order. This process takes six to nine months, but it is realistic. In Africa, you must also be aware of informal cross-border trade. A large share of trade within Africa happens through informal channels, especially in West and Central Africa. While this may not be your target, understanding it helps you appreciate the competitive dynamics. For formal export sales, you need to work with registered buyers and proper documentation. Another important consideration is regional integration. The African Continental Free Trade Area, or AfCFTA, aims to create a single market for goods and services. As implementation progresses, tariffs on many intra-African trade flows will fall, making it easier to sell across borders. An export sales agent should monitor AfCFTA developments and identify opportunities in neighbouring countries. To summarise, market research and buyer identification require a systematic approach. Select markets using data and scoring models. Understand the competitive landscape. Identify buyers through multiple channels. Qualify them rigorously. Make contact professionally. Consider local agents. And stay informed about regional trade agreements. This foundation will determine whether your export sales efforts succeed or fail. In the next lesson, we will look at pricing and Incoterms, which are the commercial heart of any export deal.

3. Pricing, Incoterms, and Quotations for African Exports

Pricing an export product is not the same as pricing for the domestic market. You must account for additional costs, risks, and currency movements. This lesson explains how to build an export price, how to use Incoterms correctly, and how to prepare a professional quotation. We begin with cost components. The base cost of your product includes raw materials, labour, overheads, and profit margin. To this you add export-specific costs: packaging for export, inland transport to the port or airport, customs clearance at origin, terminal handling charges, freight, insurance, customs clearance at destination, import duties, and inland transport to the buyer. You may also incur costs for certificates, inspections, and documentation. A common mistake is to quote a price without including all these costs, only to discover later that your margin has disappeared. To avoid this, build a detailed cost sheet for each shipment. List every cost item and estimate it as accurately as possible. Use historical data if available, or get quotes from freight forwarders and customs agents. Then add a contingency of five to ten percent for unexpected charges. Currency is another critical factor. Most international trade is denominated in US dollars or euros. If your costs are in local currency and your selling price is in dollars, exchange rate movements can wipe out your profit. You can mitigate this risk by quoting in a stable currency, using forward contracts, or adjusting prices periodically. Some African currencies are volatile, so monitor rates closely and build a buffer into your price. Now let us discuss Incoterms. Incoterms are standard trade terms published by the International Chamber of Commerce. They define who is responsible for costs, risks, and tasks such as transport, insurance, and customs clearance. The most common Incoterms for African exports are EXW, FOB, CFR, CIF, and DAP. EXW means ex works. The buyer collects the goods from your premises and bears all costs and risks from that point. This is the simplest term for the seller but may not be attractive to buyers. FOB means free on board. You deliver the goods to the port of shipment and load them onto the vessel. The buyer pays freight and insurance. FOB is widely used for sea freight and is often the preferred term for African exporters because it gives the buyer control over shipping. CFR means cost and freight. You pay the freight to the destination port, but risk transfers to the buyer when the goods are on board. CIF means cost, insurance, and freight. You pay freight and insurance to the destination port. CIF is common in commodity trades. DAP means delivered at place. You deliver the goods to a named destination, bearing all costs and risks except import duties and customs clearance. DAP is useful when the buyer wants a door-to-door service. Choosing the right Incoterm depends on your capabilities and the buyer's preferences. If you have strong logistics partners, CIF or DAP can be a competitive advantage. If you are new to export, FOB or EXW may be safer because they limit your responsibility. Always state the Incoterm clearly in your quotation and contract, including the named place. For example, FOB Mombasa or CIF Rotterdam. Now let us look at how to prepare a quotation. A professional export quotation includes several elements. Start with your company details and the buyer's details. Add a reference number and date. Describe the product clearly, including specifications, packaging, and quantity. State the unit price and total price, and specify the currency. State the Incoterm and the named place. Specify the payment terms, such as 30 percent advance payment and 70 percent against copy of bill of lading. State the delivery time or shipment schedule. Mention the validity period of the quotation, for example 30 days. Include any additional conditions, such as inspection requirements or force majeure. Finally, provide your bank details and contact information. A well-prepared quotation reduces misunderstandings and speeds up the sales process. It also protects you legally if a dispute arises. In Africa, buyers often negotiate hard on price. You need to know your bottom line and be prepared to walk away if the price is not viable. But do not compete only on price. Differentiate on quality, reliability, packaging, or after-sales service. Many African buyers value suppliers who deliver on time and communicate clearly more than they value the lowest price. Let us consider a practical example. A South African exporter of processed fruit juices wants to sell to a buyer in Zambia. The exporter calculates the cost of production, packaging, inland transport to Johannesburg, customs clearance, and freight to Lusaka. The total cost per carton is 20 US dollars. The exporter adds a 15 percent margin, giving a selling price of 23 US dollars per carton. The exporter quotes CIF Lusaka, including insurance and freight. Payment terms are 50 percent advance and 50 percent on delivery. The buyer accepts the quotation, and the exporter arranges shipment through a freight forwarder. Because the exporter included all costs, the deal is profitable. If the exporter had quoted FOB Johannesburg without understanding the buyer's preference for door delivery, the deal might have gone to a competitor. Another important aspect of pricing is volume discounts. Buyers often ask for lower prices for larger orders. You can offer tiered pricing, but make sure that your margin remains positive at every tier. Calculate your break-even point and do not go below it. You can also use price escalation clauses in long-term contracts to cover increases in raw material or freight costs. Finally, be aware of anti-dumping duties and countervailing measures. Some countries impose extra duties if they believe imports are priced below fair market value. This is more common in large markets such as the EU, US, and South Africa. Check the rules before you quote. In summary, export pricing requires a detailed cost sheet, careful currency management, correct use of Incoterms, and a professional quotation. Do not guess; calculate. Do not promise what you cannot deliver; be realistic. And always protect your margin. In the next lesson, we will cover export documentation and compliance, which are essential for smooth customs clearance and payment.

4. Export Documentation and Compliance in African Trade

Export documentation is the paperwork that accompanies your goods across borders. It may seem tedious, but it is critical. Missing or incorrect documents can delay shipments, trigger fines, or even cause the buyer to reject the goods. This lesson covers the main documents you need for African exports and how to ensure compliance. We start with the commercial invoice. This is the primary document that describes the transaction. It includes the seller's and buyer's details, the date, the invoice number, a description of the goods, the quantity, the unit price, the total value, the currency, the Incoterm, and the payment terms. The commercial invoice is used by customs to assess duties and by banks to process payments. It must be accurate and consistent with other documents. Next is the packing list. This document lists the contents of each package, including the number of units, the weight, and the dimensions. It helps customs officers and the buyer verify that the shipment is complete. The packing list should match the commercial invoice exactly. The bill of lading is a transport document issued by the shipping line or freight forwarder. It serves as a receipt for the goods, a contract of carriage, and a document of title. The buyer needs the bill of lading to claim the goods at the destination port. There are different types: original bill of lading, telex release, and sea waybill. Make sure you understand which type your buyer requires. For air freight, the equivalent document is the air waybill. The certificate of origin proves where the goods were produced. It is often required to claim preferential tariff treatment under trade agreements such as AfCFTA, ECOWAS, or SADC. The certificate must be issued by an authorised body, such as a chamber of commerce or customs authority. Incorrect origin claims can lead to penalties. Phytosanitary certificates are required for plant and plant products. They confirm that the goods are free from pests and diseases. Veterinary certificates are required for animal products. Health certificates are required for food and pharmaceuticals. These certificates are issued by the relevant government authority in the exporting country. A certificate of conformity is required by some countries, such as Nigeria and Algeria, to confirm that products meet local standards. It is usually issued by an inspection company such as SGS or Bureau Veritas. Other documents may include insurance certificates, inspection reports, fumigation certificates, and export licences. The specific requirements depend on the product and the destination country. Now let us discuss compliance. Compliance means following the laws and regulations of both the exporting and importing countries. This includes customs regulations, sanitary and phytosanitary rules, technical standards, and trade sanctions. Non-compliance can result in seizure of goods, fines, or blacklisting. To ensure compliance, start by researching the import requirements of your target market. The buyer may not always know the rules, so you must verify them yourself. Use official sources such as the destination country's customs website, standards bureau, and embassy. The International Trade Centre's Market Access Map is a useful tool. You can also hire a customs broker or compliance consultant in the destination country. Another key compliance issue is classification. Every product has a Harmonized System code, which determines the tariff rate and any special requirements. If you classify your product incorrectly, you may pay the wrong duty or face penalties. Work with a customs expert to confirm the correct HS code. Valuation is also important. Customs authorities assess the value of your goods to calculate duties. If you undervalue the goods, you risk fines and seizure. If you overvalue them, you may pay unnecessary duties. Always declare the true transaction value. In Africa, some countries have foreign exchange controls that affect how you get paid. For example, you may be required to repatriate export earnings within a certain period. Understand these rules before you sign a contract. You may also need to register with the central bank or export promotion agency. Let us consider a practical example. A Nigerian exporter of sesame seeds wants to sell to China. The exporter needs a commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading, certificate of origin, phytosanitary certificate, and quality certificate. The buyer in China requires the documents to be legalised by the Nigerian embassy in Beijing or the Chinese embassy in Abuja. The exporter must also comply with Chinese import standards for pesticides and aflatoxin levels. If any document is missing or incorrect, the shipment will be held at the port, incurring demurrage and storage costs. To avoid these problems, the exporter prepares a document checklist and verifies each item before shipment. The exporter also works with a freight forwarder who specialises in China trade. Another example is a Kenyan exporter of cut flowers to the Netherlands. The flowers must meet EU quality and phytosanitary standards. The exporter needs a phytosanitary certificate, a certificate of origin, and an air waybill. Because flowers are perishable, timing is critical. The exporter must ensure that documents are ready before the flowers are cut. Any delay can destroy the shipment. In both examples, the export sales agent plays a key role in coordinating documentation. You do not need to be an expert in every document, but you must know what is required and who is responsible for each item. Create a standard operating procedure for documentation. Use a checklist for each shipment. Keep digital and physical copies of all documents for at least five years. This will help you resolve disputes and comply with audits. Finally, consider the impact of digitalisation. Many African countries are moving towards electronic customs systems, such as the Single Window. These systems allow you to submit documents online and track clearance. Examples include Kenya's Tradenet, Ghana's ICUMS, and Nigeria's NICIS. Using these systems can reduce delays and costs. However, they require you to be computer literate and to have reliable internet access. In summary, export documentation and compliance are not optional. They are the backbone of international trade. Master the main documents, understand the rules of your target market, classify your product correctly, and use digital tools where available. This will protect your business and build trust with buyers. In the next lesson, we will cover negotiation and relationship management, which are essential for closing deals and keeping customers.

5. Negotiation, Relationship Management, and Risk Mitigation

The final lesson in Module 7 brings together the commercial and human aspects of export sales. You can have the best product and the most competitive price, but if you cannot negotiate effectively and manage relationships, you will not succeed. This lesson covers negotiation strategies, relationship management, and risk mitigation for African export sales. We begin with negotiation. Export negotiations are different from domestic negotiations because of distance, culture, and legal systems. You may never meet the buyer face to face. You may communicate in a second language. You may have different expectations about time, contracts, and trust. To negotiate well, you need to prepare. Know your bottom line: the lowest price and the most generous terms you can accept. Know your walk-away point. Know the buyer's needs and pressures. What are their deadlines? What quality standards must they meet? What are their competitors doing? The more you know, the stronger your position. During the negotiation, focus on value, not just price. Explain how your product reduces the buyer's costs, improves their quality, or helps them win customers. Use evidence: test results, certifications, customer references. Be willing to trade concessions. If the buyer asks for a lower price, ask for a larger volume, faster payment, or a longer contract. Never give something for nothing. Also, be aware of cultural differences. In many African cultures, relationship building comes before business. Do not rush to the contract. Spend time on introductions, meals, and small talk. In some Asian cultures, indirect communication is common. Learn to read between the lines. In European and North American cultures, directness is valued. Adapt your style to the buyer. Now let us discuss relationship management. Once you have a buyer, your job is to keep them. This is easier and cheaper than finding new buyers. Good relationship management means regular communication, reliable delivery, and quick problem-solving. Send updates on production and shipment. Respond to emails and calls within 24 hours. If there is a delay, tell the buyer before they ask. If there is a quality issue, propose a solution immediately. Visit your buyers when possible. Attend trade fairs where they exhibit. Invite them to your factory or farm. Personal contact builds trust. You should also manage your buyer's expectations. Do not promise what you cannot deliver. Under-promise and over-deliver. If you say you will ship in four weeks, ship in three. If you say the product meets a certain specification, make sure it does. Reliability is your best marketing tool. In Africa, word of mouth is powerful. A satisfied buyer will recommend you to others. An unsatisfied buyer will warn others. Protect your reputation. Now let us turn to risk mitigation. Export sales involves several risks: payment risk, delivery risk, quality risk, currency risk, and political risk. You need to manage each one. Payment risk is the most common. The buyer may refuse to pay, delay payment, or become insolvent. To reduce payment risk, use secure payment terms. Advance payment is safest for you but may be unacceptable to the buyer. Letters of credit are safer for both parties, but they are expensive and require strict document compliance. Documentary collections are cheaper but riskier. Open account terms are the riskiest. You can also use credit insurance or export factoring. Credit insurance covers you if the buyer defaults. Export factoring sells your receivables to a bank at a discount. Both have costs, but they can be worth it for large orders. Delivery risk means the goods may be lost or damaged in transit. Use reputable freight forwarders and insure your shipments. Specify the Incoterm clearly so that responsibility is unambiguous. Quality risk means the goods may not meet the buyer's specifications. Use pre-shipment inspections and quality certificates. Keep samples of each batch. If a dispute arises, you can prove that the goods met the agreed standard. Currency risk means exchange rate movements may reduce your profit. Quote in a stable currency, use forward contracts, or adjust prices regularly. Political risk means the destination country may experience instability, expropriation, or changes in import rules. Monitor the news and diversify your markets. Do not rely on a single buyer or a single country. Let us consider a practical example. A Tanzanian exporter of coffee wants to sell to a buyer in Japan. The buyer asks for open account terms with 60 days credit. The exporter is nervous about payment risk. After negotiation, they agree on a letter of credit confirmed by a Japanese bank. The exporter also buys credit insurance for the portion not covered by the letter of credit. The coffee is shipped in vacuum-sealed bags to protect quality. The exporter uses a reputable freight forwarder and insures the shipment. The buyer receives the coffee on time and is satisfied. The exporter has mitigated payment, delivery, and quality risks. Another example is a Senegalese exporter of fish products to Spain. The EU has strict hygiene and traceability rules. The exporter invests in a cold chain and works with a laboratory for testing. The exporter also hires a Spanish-speaking sales agent to manage the buyer relationship. The agent visits the buyer regularly and handles any issues. This investment pays off with repeat orders. In both examples, risk mitigation is not a one-time activity. It is ongoing. You must review your risks regularly and adjust your strategies. You should also have a contingency plan. What will you do if the buyer does not pay? What if the shipment is delayed? What if the currency crashes? Having a plan reduces stress and improves decision-making. Finally, remember that export sales is a long-term game. Do not expect instant success. Build your reputation, your network, and your skills. Learn from every deal, whether it succeeds or fails. Keep records of what worked and what did not. Share your knowledge with your team. Over time, you will become a trusted export sales agent in your market. This brings us to the end of Module 7. You have learned about the African export context, market research and buyer identification, pricing and Incoterms, documentation and compliance, and negotiation, relationship management, and risk mitigation. These are the core skills of an export sales agent. Practice them, refine them, and apply them to your own business. The opportunities in African export trade are growing. With the right approach, you can capture them.

SAFETY WARNING Aflatoxin and Pesticide Residue Compliance

Groundnuts, maize, cottonseed, and tree nuts exported from Africa are routinely rejected at EU and Gulf ports due to aflatoxin contamination exceeding 4 g/kg (total aflatoxins) for direct human consumption, or 10 g/kg for processed products. Pesticide residue limits (MRLs) in the EU are frequently lower than those registered for use in African producing countries. Never ship without a pre-shipment laboratory test. Ensure storage humidity is below 10% moisture content for grains and that approved drying and sorting protocols are documented. Rejected consignments result in total loss, re-export costs, and potential delisting of the supplier. Verify current MRLs via the EU Pesticides Database and Codex Alimentarius before every shipment.

Quick Reference Checklist for Export Sales Agents

  1. Verify buyer creditworthiness before committing supply request bank references, use Letters of Credit (L/C) for first transactions, or credit insurance (e.g., ATI, African Trade Insurance Agency).
  2. Confirm regulatory access for the destination market: tariff lines, quotas, SPS requirements, and any import licence obligations. Use ITC Market Access Map and AfCFTA Rules of Origin.
  3. Lock Incoterms in writing FOB, CIF, or CFR and specify the named port. Ambiguity is the leading cause of margin erosion.
  4. Arrange pre-shipment inspection and laboratory testing (aflatoxin, pesticide residue, microbiology) at least 7 working days before loading.
  5. Assemble the document pack: commercial invoice, packing list, certificate of origin, phytosanitary/health certificate, bill of lading, and inspection certificate.
  6. Confirm payment terms and currency hedge or price in USD/EUR where possible; note local currency convertibility risk.
  7. Plan logistics with buffer African border crossings and port dwell times can add 515 days. Book freight early and insure cargo.
  8. Document the relationship maintain a CRM record of buyer specifications, past shipments, and complaint history for repeat sales.
