1. Testing Your Soil: Simple pH and Nutrient Checks

Before you plant wheat or barley, you need to know if your soil is sweet or sour. Most cereals grow best at a soil pH between 6.0 and 7.5. You can test pH cheaply using a handheld pH meter (cost about $20) or pH test strips from a farm supply shop. Dig a small hole 15 cm deep, take a handful of moist soil, and press it against the meter probe or strip. Wait one minute for the reading. If pH is below 6.0, your soil is too acidic; add agricultural lime (calcium carbonate) at 2 to 4 tonnes per hectare. If above 7.5, add gypsum or elemental sulphur at 500 kg per hectare. Always test three spots per hectare and average the results.

For nutrients, the most important for wheat and barley are nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K). A simple jar test shows soil texture: fill a clear jar one-third with soil, add water and a pinch of salt, shake hard, then let it settle. Sand settles in 1 minute, silt in 2 hours, clay in 24 hours. Sandy soil needs more manure; clay soil holds nutrients but drains poorly. You can also send a soil sample to a local agricultural extension office. They will give you exact fertilizer recommendations. In Kenya, for example, a typical wheat field needs 50 kg of DAP (diammonium phosphate) per acre at planting.

Organic matter is the best indicator of fertile soil. Take a spade of soil and look for earthworms. If you see 5 to 10 worms per spade, your soil is healthy. If none, add compost or manure. A simple test for nitrogen: dig a hole, smell the soil. A sweet, earthy smell means good nitrogen; a sour or chemical smell means poor. For phosphorus, look for purple tints on young leavesâthat signals deficiency. Potassium deficiency shows as yellow or brown edges on older leaves.

Record your results in a notebook: date, pH, texture, worm count. Test every season before planting. Correcting pH takes 3 to 6 months, so test early. With these simple checks, you will avoid wasting seed and fertilizer on land that cannot support wheat or barley.

2. Clearing and Leveling Land with Hand Tools and Oxen

Start clearing your plot at least two months before planting. For small plots (under 1 acre), use a machete (panga) to cut tall grass and bushes. Remove all roots and stumps; they harbour pests and break your plough. For medium plots (1-5 acres), use an ox-drawn plough. A single ox can pull a light plough, but a pair of oxen is better. Train oxen for two weeks using a yoke and a simple rein. Work early morning or late afternoon when it is cool. Clear brush in strips, piling debris for burning or composting.

After clearing, level the land. Uneven land causes water to pool, drowning young wheat. Use a hand hoe (jembe) to break large clods. Then drag a wooden plank or a metal harrow behind oxen to smooth the surface. For hand tools only, use a rake and a heavy log pulled by two people. The goal is a fine tilth: soil particles the size of maize grains. Test by walking across the fieldâyour footprints should be shallow and even. If you sink or stumble, keep leveling.

For oxen leveling, make a simple drag: a 2-meter wooden plank with a chain attached to the yoke. Drive the oxen in straight lines across the field, then diagonally. This takes 3 to 4 passes. In steep areas (slope over 5%), make contour ridges instead of flat beds. Use an A-frame level (two sticks tied in an A shape with a string and stone) to mark contour lines. Dig trenches along these lines to slow water runoff.

Remove all stones larger than a fist. Stones damage ploughs and interfere with seed placement. Pile them at field edges for future use in building. After leveling, let the soil rest for two weeks before planting. This allows weed seeds to sprout; then you can remove them with a light harrow. Proper clearing and leveling saves you hours of work later and gives your wheat or barley a uniform seedbed for even germination.

3. Building Soil Fertility with Manure and Compost

Wheat and barley are heavy feeders. A hectare of wheat removes about 100 kg of nitrogen, 40 kg of phosphorus, and 60 kg of potassium from the soil. Without adding fertility, your yields will drop every year. The cheapest way to build fertility is farmyard manure (cattle, goat, or poultry). Fresh manure burns roots; always compost it first. Pile manure in a heap, cover with leaves or plastic, and turn it every two weeks. It is ready in 6 to 8 weeks when it is dark, crumbly, and smells earthy. Apply 10 to 15 tonnes per hectare (about 4 to 6 tonnes per acre) before ploughing. Spread it evenly and incorporate it into the top 15 cm of soil.

Compost is made from crop residues, kitchen waste, and animal bedding. Build a compost pile 1.5 m wide and 1 m high. Alternate layers of green material (fresh grass, vegetable peels) and brown material (dry leaves, straw, maize stalks). Add a handful of wood ash or a bucket of old manure to speed up decomposition. Keep the pile as moist as a squeezed sponge. Turn it every week. In 4 to 6 weeks, you will have compost. Apply 5 to 10 tonnes per hectare. Compost improves soil structure, holds water, and feeds beneficial microbes.

For a quick boost, plant green manures like cowpeas, lablab, or sunhemp before cereals. Grow them for 6 to 8 weeks, then plough them into the soil while still green. These legumes fix nitrogen from the airâup to 100 kg per hectare. You can also use wood ash from cooking fires; it is rich in potassium and lime. Spread 1 to 2 tonnes per hectare on acidic soils.

Do not burn crop residues. Instead, chop them and mix with manure. If you keep livestock, collect urineâit is a powerful nitrogen source. Dilute one part urine with four parts water and apply to the soil. Remember: manure and compost work slowly. Apply them 4 to 6 weeks before planting. Test your soil every year to see if fertility is improving. With consistent organic matter, your wheat and barley will grow stronger and resist drought better.

4. Choosing the Best Plot: Drainage, Sunlight, and Slope

Wheat and barley hate wet feet. Choose a plot that drains well after rain. Dig a hole 30 cm deep and fill it with water. If the water drains away within 24 hours, drainage is good. If it stays for 2 days or more, find another plot or make raised beds. Avoid low-lying areas where water collects, and avoid heavy clay soils unless you add lots of compost. Sandy loam is idealâit holds moisture but drains excess. Test by squeezing a handful of moist soil: if it forms a ball that crumbles when you poke it, it is loam. If it stays sticky, it is clay.

Sunlight is critical. Wheat and barley need at least 6 hours of direct sunlight per day. Watch your plot from morning to evening. If trees or buildings shade it for more than 2 hours in the middle of the day, choose another spot. In Africa, north-facing slopes get more sun in the southern hemisphere, but the difference is small. The main thing is to avoid shade. Clear any overhanging branches. If you must plant near trees, keep a distance of 10 meters from the trunk.

Slope matters for erosion and water. A gentle slope of 1 to 3 percent is bestâit allows water to drain without washing soil away. If your plot is steeper than 5 percent, make contour ridges or terraces. Use an A-frame level to mark contour lines every 10 meters. Plant across the slope, not up and down. On flat land (0 percent slope), water may pool; make shallow furrows every 5 meters to guide water away. Avoid hilltops where wind dries the soil, and avoid valley bottoms where frost collects.

Also consider access. You need to carry manure, seeds, and tools. A plot near your home or a road saves labour. Check for previous crops: if the plot grew wheat or barley last year, choose another plot to avoid diseases like take-all. If you must replant, rotate with legumes or fallow for one season. Finally, look for signs of good soil: dark colour, earthworms, and healthy weeds like blackjack. With the right plot, your work will be half done.

5. Making Ridges and Beds for Small-Scale Planting

On small plots, ridges and beds make planting easier and improve drainage. For wheat and barley, you can plant on flat beds, raised beds, or ridges. Flat beds are simplest: after leveling, mark rows 20 to 30 cm apart. Use a hand hoe to make shallow furrows 5 cm deep. Drop seeds every 2 to 3 cm, then cover with soil. This works well in dry areas where you want to conserve moisture. But if your soil is heavy clay or drains poorly, raised beds are better. Make beds 1 meter wide and 10 to 20 cm high. Leave 30 cm paths between beds for walking and weeding. Build beds with a hoe, pulling soil from the paths. This raises the root zone above waterlogged soil.

Ridges are best for very wet areas or for oxen ploughing. A ridge is a long mound of soil 30 to 40 cm high and 60 cm wide at the base. Make ridges 75 cm apart (from centre to centre). Plant two rows of wheat or barley on top of each ridge, 15 cm apart. Ridges warm up faster in spring and drain excess water. To make ridges by hand, use a hoe to pull soil from both sides. For oxen, use a ridging plough. In East Africa, farmers often make ridges with a hand hoe called a jembe. Work when soil is moist but not wetâif it sticks to your hoe, wait a day.

For small-scale planting, you can also use a dibble stick (a pointed stick) to make planting holes. This is quick for sowing seeds in dry soil. Space holes 20 cm apart in rows 30 cm apart. Drop 3 to 5 seeds per hole, then cover. This method saves seed and is good for intercropping with legumes. After planting, mulch with dry grass or crop residues to conserve water and suppress weeds. Mulch 2 to 3 cm thick, but keep it 5 cm away from the stems to avoid rot.

Maintain your ridges and beds each season. Repair eroded edges, add compost, and reshape. Well-made beds last for years. They also make harvesting easierâyou can pull plants by hand. Remember: wheat and barley do not like to be buried too deep. Plant seeds no deeper than 5 cm. With proper ridges and beds, your small plot can produce as much as a larger flat field.

6. Water Access: Boreholes, Rivers, and Irrigation Basics

Wheat and barley need 400 to 500 mm of water during the growing season. In many parts of Africa, rainfall is unreliable. You need a plan for water. First, find your water source. A river or stream is cheapest, but you must pump or carry water. A borehole is reliable but expensiveâdrilling costs $3,000 to $10,000. A shallow well (hand-dug) can work if the water table is less than 10 meters deep. Test the water: if it is salty (taste it), it will harm your crop. Also check if it is cleanâmuddy water clogs drip lines.

For small plots (under 1 acre), you can irrigate with a watering can or bucket. Carry water from a river or well. Apply 20 liters per square meter per week if no rain. That is 200 buckets for a 10 m by 10 m plot. Hard work, but doable. Better: use a treadle pump. It costs $50 to $150 and can lift water from 7 meters deep. Two people can pump 1,000 liters per hour. Connect it to a hose and water your beds. For larger plots, a petrol or diesel pump (2 to 5 horsepower) can irrigate 1 to 2 acres. They cost $200 to $500 and use 1 liter of fuel per hour.

Drip irrigation saves water. You can make a simple drip system with plastic bottles. Poke small holes in a 2-liter bottle, bury it next to plants, and fill it with water. The water drips slowly to the roots. For a whole field, buy drip tape (costs about $100 per 100 meters). Lay it along rows, and connect to a tank or pump. Drip uses 50% less water than flooding. But you must filter the water to prevent clogging.

If you have a river, you can use a gravity system. Place a tank uphill, run a pipe downhill to your field. Even a 2-meter drop gives enough pressure for drip. For flooding, make basins around beds and fill them. But flooding wastes water and can cause erosion. Always water early morning or evening to reduce evaporation. Mulch heavily. Check soil moisture by digging 10 cmâif dry, irrigate. Do not overwater; soggy soil causes root diseases. With careful water management, you can grow wheat and barley even in dry areas.

In African smallholder and emerging commercial wheatâbarley systems, land selection and land preparation are the first profit-determining decisions. 1. Testing Your Soil: Simple pH and Nutrient Checks



Wheat and barley perform best between pH 6.0 and 7.5 (in water). Barley tolerates slightly more alkalinity and salinity than wheat; wheat is slightly more tolerant of acidity. Below pH 5.2, aluminium toxicity stunts roots and yields collapse. Above pH 8.0, zinc and phosphorus lock up.



You do not need a laboratory for a first diagnosis. Use these field methods:




  • Vinegar test (for alkalinity): Place a spoonful of dry soil in a cup, add white vinegar. Fizzing = likely calcareous/alkaline soil (pH >7.5).

  • Bicarbonate test (for acidity): Mix soil with distilled water, add a pinch of baking soda. Fizzing = acidic soil (pH <5.5).

  • Indicator strips or a portable pH meter: Mix 1 part soil : 2 parts distilled water, stir, wait 10 minutes, dip strip. Cheap, repeatable, and good enough for management decisions.

  • Organic matter eye-check: Dark, crumbly, earthworm-rich soil = good. Pale, compacted, crusting soil = low organic matter, needs manure or compost.



For nutrient status, the reliable route is a laboratory soil test through your national agricultural research institute or a certified private lab. Sample at 0â20 cm depth, take 15â20 sub-samples per 2â5 ha in a "W" pattern, mix in a clean bucket, and send about 500 g. Request: pH, organic carbon, total N, available P (Olsen or Bray), exchangeable K, and CEC. This single test typically costs the equivalent of 20â60 kg of wheat grain and can save you 10 times that in misapplied fertiliser.

3. Building Soil Fertility with Manure and Compost



Africa's wheat yields average around 2 t/ha versus 5â8 t/ha in well-managed systems. The single largest gap is soil organic matter. Well-rotted cattle manure supplies nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and micronutrients, improves water-holding capacity, and buffers pH.




  • Rate: 5â10 t/ha of well-rotted manure every 2â3 years; 10â20 t/ha on degraded soils.

  • Timing: Apply and incorporate 4â6 weeks before planting to avoid nitrogen tie-up and burn risk.

  • Compost: Layer 3 parts dry material (straw, maize stover) : 1 part green material (weeds, kitchen waste) : thin layers of manure and ash. Turn every 2 weeks; ready in 6â10 weeks when dark, crumbly, and odourless.

  • Business rule: One tonne of good compost or manure is worth roughly the same as 20â30 kg of urea plus 10 kg of DAP in nutrient value â but also builds long-term soil health that fertiliser alone cannot.



4. Choosing the Best Plot: Drainage, Sunlight, and Slope



Before you commit to a plot, walk it and score it:




  • Drainage: Dig a 50 cm Ã 50 cm hole, fill with water, and see how fast it drains. If water is still standing after 24 hours, the plot is unsuitable for wheat/barley without drainage works.

  • Sunlight: Wheat and barley need 6+ hours of direct sun. Avoid plots shaded by trees, buildings, or hills, especially on the eastern side where morning sun dries dew and reduces rust and Septoria pressure.

  • Slope: 0.5â2% is ideal. 2â5% needs contour farming. Above 5% needs terraces or permanent grass strips.

  • Previous crop: Best after legumes (beans, peas, groundnut) or fallow. Avoid following wheat on wheat â disease and nematode pressure build up.

  • Access: Within 1â2 km of a road or market. Transport costs eat margins on remote plots.



5. Making Ridges and Beds for Small-Scale Planting



Ridges and raised beds are essential where drainage is marginal or where irrigation is by furrow. Standard practice:




  • Ridge spacing: 60â75 cm between ridge centres for wheat; 45â60 cm for barley.

  • Ridge height: 15â25 cm above furrow bottom.

  • Bed width: 1.0â1.2 m on flat, drained land; 0.8â1.0 m on heavier soils.

  • Seed placement: 2â3 cm deep on ridges, 3â5 cm on flat beds. Deeper planting on light soils, shallower on heavy soils.

  • Furrow use: Furrows double as irrigation channels and drainage. Keep them 10â15 cm deep and continuous to the field edge.

Wheat and barley need 400â650 mm of water per season. In rainfed systems, the critical period is stem elongation to grain fill. If you have irrigation, plan for 4â6 applications of 40â60 mm each.
