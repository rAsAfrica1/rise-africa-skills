1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Metal casting is one of the oldest and most useful trades in the world. In Africa, foundries have been shaping metal for centuriesfrom the bronze casters of Benin to the iron smelters of the Haya people in Tanzania. Today, the demand for cast metal parts is everywhere: water pumps, grinding mills, car parts, cooking stoves, farm tools, and construction fittings. If you can melt metal and pour it into a mould, you can make money solving real problems in your community.

Starting a foundry does not require a huge factory. Many successful African foundries began in a backyard with a simple charcoal furnace, a few crucibles, and locally sourced scrap metal. The key is to learn the basics: how to build a furnace that reaches the right temperature, how to prepare sand moulds that don't crack, and how to pour safely. You also need to understand the business side: what parts are in demand, how to price your work, and how to find customers.

In this module, you will learn the foundations of the foundry business. We will cover the tools you need, the step-by-step process of making a casting, common mistakes and how to fix them, safety practices that keep you alive and healthy, and a practical exercise to get your hands dirty. By the end, you will have the knowledge to start small and grow. Remember, every big foundry started with a single pour. Your first casting might be a simple hoe blade or a cooking pot handle. That's fine. What matters is that you start, learn from each attempt, and keep improving. The market for quality cast products in Africa is growing, and with the right skills, you can be part of it.

2. Tools and Materials

To start a small foundry, you need a few essential tools and materials. First, the furnace: you can build a simple charcoal or coke furnace using a 200-litre steel drum lined with refractory clay (a mix of clay, sand, and water). For melting aluminium, a charcoal furnace works well; for iron, you need coke and a forced air supply from a blower or vacuum cleaner. Next, crucibles: clay-graphite crucibles are best for holding molten metal. A #6 or #8 crucible can handle 3-6 kg of aluminium. You'll also need tongs to lift the hot crucible, a skimmer to remove slag, and a pouring shank or ring.

For moulding, you need casting sand. Good sand is fine, slightly damp, and holds shape when squeezed. You can mix silica sand with bentonite clay (about 5-8% by weight) and water. A simple moulding box (flask) made of wood or metal, with a bottom board and a top board, is essential. You'll also need a rammer to pack the sand, a strike-off bar to level it, and a sprue pin to make the pouring hole. For patterns (the shape you want to cast), you can use wood, metal, or even existing parts. A simple pattern like a gear or a pulley can be made from hardwood.

Other tools: a wire brush for cleaning castings, a hacksaw for cutting gates, and a file for finishing. For safety, you need leather gloves, a face shield, a leather apron, and boots. Never pour metal without eye and hand protection. Materials you'll need regularly: scrap metal (aluminium from old pots, car parts; iron from old engine blocks), charcoal or coke, and sand. You can source scrap from local mechanics, junkyards, or households. Keep a stock of sand and clay. With these tools and materials, you're ready to set up a basic foundry. Start small, and upgrade as you earn.

3. Step by Step Method

Casting a metal part follows a clear sequence. First, make your pattern. This is a replica of the part you want, slightly larger to allow for shrinkage (about 1% for aluminium, 2% for iron). You can carve it from wood or use an existing part. Next, prepare the mould. Place the pattern in the drag (bottom half of the flask) and fill with sand, ramming it firmly. Flip the drag, place the cope (top half) and the sprue pin, then ram sand in the cope. Remove the sprue pin and pattern, creating a cavity. Cut a pouring basin and vents. Now you have a mould ready for metal.

Melt your metal. Light the furnace and add charcoal or coke. Once hot, place the crucible with scrap metal inside. Use a blower to increase heat. For aluminium, you need about 660C; for iron, 1200-1400C. Watch the metal until it's fully liquid and shiny. Remove slag with a skimmer. Then, pour the molten metal into the mould through the sprue. Pour steadily and continuouslydon't stop halfway. Fill the mould completely; you'll see metal rise in the vents. Let it cool. Small parts cool in 10-15 minutes; larger ones may take an hour.

After cooling, break the sand mould and remove the casting. Cut off the sprue and gates with a hacksaw. Grind or file the rough edges. Inspect for defects like holes or cracks. If defective, you can remelt and try again. Finally, finish the part: sand it, paint it, or machine it as needed. Practice this process with simple shapes like a flat bar or a ring. Each step builds skill. Remember, the quality of your mould determines the quality of your casting. Take time to ram sand properly and ensure the pattern is clean. With practice, you'll cast complex parts efficiently.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Even experienced foundry workers make mistakes. Here are common ones and how to fix them. First, mould collapse: if the sand is too dry or not rammed hard enough, the mould walls can fall in when you pour. Fix: mix sand with the right amount of water (it should hold shape when squeezed) and ram it firmly in layers. Second, misrun: when metal cools too quickly and doesn't fill the mould. This happens if the metal isn't hot enough or the mould is too cold. Fix: heat metal until it's fully liquid and preheat the mould slightly (by pouring a little metal and letting it cool, or using a torch). Third, porosity: small holes in the casting due to gas trapped in the metal. This can come from wet sand, dirty scrap, or too much slag. Fix: use dry sand, clean scrap, and skim slag before pouring. Also, add vents to let gas escape.

Fourth, shrinkage: a sunken area where metal contracts as it cools. Fix: add a riser (a reservoir of molten metal) that feeds the casting as it shrinks. Fifth, rough surface: if the sand is too coarse or the pattern is rough, the casting will be rough. Fix: use finer sand and smooth your pattern. Sixth, cold shuts: when two streams of metal meet but don't fuse. This happens if the metal is too cold or pouring is too slow. Fix: pour quickly and continuously with hot metal. Seventh, sand inclusion: sand particles stuck in the casting. Fix: ensure the mould is clean and the sprue is properly designed to avoid turbulence.

Finally, safety mistakes: not wearing protective gear, pouring on wet ground (causes steam explosion), or using wet tools. Always wear leather gloves, face shield, and apron. Pour on dry sand or concrete. Keep a bucket of dry sand to smother fires. If you make a mistake, don't give up. Remelt the metal and try again. Keep a notebook to record what went wrong and what you changed. That's how you improve.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Foundry work is dangerous if you ignore safety. Molten metal can cause severe burns, and fumes can harm your lungs. Always wear proper protective equipment: a face shield (not just goggles), leather gloves, a leather apron, and steel-toed boots. Never wear synthetic clothingit melts and sticks to skin. Work in a well-ventilated area, preferably outdoors or with a chimney to draw fumes away. When melting scrap, avoid painted, galvanised, or oily metalthese release toxic fumes. Use only clean scrap.

Before you start, check your equipment. Make sure the crucible is not cracked. Preheat it gradually to avoid thermal shock. Keep the furnace on a stable, non-flammable surface like concrete or bare earth. Clear the area of anything flammable. Have a fire extinguisher or a bucket of dry sand nearby. When pouring, stand to the side, not directly in front of the mould. Pour steadily and avoid splashing. Never pour on wet groundsteam can explode and throw molten metal. After pouring, let the mould cool completely before opening. Use tongs and gloves to handle hot castings.

Best practices: work with a partner when possible. Have a first-aid kit ready for burns. Keep water away from molten metala drop of water can cause an explosion. Store sand and clay in a dry place. Clean your tools after each use. Maintain your furnace: replace lining when worn. Learn to read the metal: when it's ready to pour, it looks like liquid silver and moves easily. Don't rush. Take breaks to avoid fatigue. Remember, safety is not just for youit's for everyone around you. A safe foundry is a productive foundry.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it's time to put your knowledge into practice. For this exercise, you will cast a simple aluminium keychain or a small flat bar. This will teach you the full process without needing expensive materials. You will need: a small charcoal furnace (or a metal bucket with holes and a blower), a #4 crucible, aluminium scrap (old pots, cans), casting sand, a small wooden pattern (a flat rectangle 5 cm x 2 cm x 1 cm), a two-part flask (or two wooden boxes), and safety gear.

Step 1: Make your pattern from wood. Sand it smooth. Step 2: Prepare the mould. Place the pattern in the drag, fill with sand, and ram it. Flip, place the cope, and ram again. Remove the pattern and cut a sprue hole. Step 3: Melt aluminium in the crucible. Use charcoal and a blower. It should take about 15-20 minutes. Step 4: Pour the molten aluminium into the mould. Be steady. Step 5: Let it cool for 15 minutes. Step 6: Break the mould, remove the casting, and cut off the sprue. File the edges smooth. Congratulationsyou've made your first casting!

Evaluate your work: Does it have a smooth surface? Are there holes or rough spots? If yes, note what you might change next time (e.g., ram sand harder, use hotter metal). Repeat the exercise to improve. Once you can cast a simple shape reliably, try a more complex pattern like a small gear or a pulley. This exercise is the foundation of your foundry business. With each casting, you build skill and confidence. Soon, you can start selling your products. Keep a record of your pours, including metal temperature, sand mix, and results. That data will help you perfect your craft.

<h3>Inputs, Costs, and Local Sourcing</h3>
<p>In most African markets, your biggest variable cost is metal. Aluminium scrap from old pots, window frames, and vehicle parts is widely available. Brass and bronze come from taps, fittings, and plumbing waste. Cast iron is harder to source but can be recovered from old stoves, engine blocks, and machine parts. Prices vary by city and season. Always buy by weight, not by piece, and always inspect for contamination before you pay.</p>

<h3>Process Parameters and Quality Control</h3>
<p>Quality is not a final inspection; it is a sequence of controlled steps. Record your melt temperature, pouring temperature, mould moisture, and cooling time for every job. Even a simple notebook builds your business data. When a customer complains, you can trace the cause instead of guessing. In sand casting, the most common defects are porosity, shrinkage, cold shuts, and sand inclusion. Each has a process cause and a process fix.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Weigh everything:</strong> Record scrap in, metal out, and fuel used per job. If you do not weigh, you cannot price.</li>
<li><strong>Sort scrap before melting:</strong> Keep aluminium, brass, and iron separate. Contamination costs more than sorting.</li>
<li><strong>Dry moulds and tools:</strong> Moisture causes explosions and porosity. Warm moulds before pouring.</li>
<li><strong>Control pour temperature:</strong> Too cold causes cold shuts; too hot causes gas and shrinkage. Use a pyrometer or trained eye.</li>
<li><strong>Inspect first article:</strong> Check dimensions and defects before running the full batch.</li>
<li><strong>Price with margin:</strong> Include metal, fuel, labour, PPE, transport, and 2030% profit. Never quote from memory.</li>
<li><strong>Keep a job book:</strong> Date, customer, alloy, weight, defects, delivery. This is your business memory.</li>
<li><strong>Reinvest monthly:</strong> Allocate a fixed percentage to tools, training, and safety equipment.</li>
</ul>
