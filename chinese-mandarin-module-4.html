1. Family Members: Immediate and Extended

In Mandarin, family terms are very specific. Start with immediate family: (b ba) father, (mma) mother, (gge) older brother, (ddi) younger brother, (jijie) older sister, (mimei) younger sister. For 'I have' use (w yu). Example: (W yu y g gge) I have one older brother. Note: no plural marker needed after numbers.

Extended family: (yye) paternal grandfather, (ninai) paternal grandmother, (w igng) maternal grandfather, (w ip) maternal grandmother. On your father's side, (shshu) younger uncle, (bbo) older uncle, (ggu) aunt. On mother's side, (jijiu) uncle, (ym) aunt. Cousins: (tngg) older male cousin (paternal), (bioji) older female cousin (maternal).

In many African cultures, extended family is central. Practice by listing your own family: ... (W ji yu...) My family has... For example, (W ji yu li ku rn) My family has six people. Use (ku) as measure word for family members. Then say: (b ba, mma, ling g ddi h w).

When greeting, ask (N ji yu j ku rn?) How many people in your family? Answer: (W ji yu w ku rn). To introduce, say (Zh sh w mma) This is my mother. For 'and', use (h). Practice with a partner: point to photos and say ... (This is...).

Remember: Chinese doesn't change nouns for singular/plural. can mean 'older brother' or 'older brothers' depending on context. Use numbers or (men) for plural: (ggemen) older brothers. But is not used after specific numbers. So say (ling g gge) two older brothers. Practice daily by naming your family members in Mandarin.

2. Describing People: Height, Hair, Eyes

To describe height, use (go) tall and (i) short. For 'very', add (hn): (T hn go) He is very tall. To say 'not tall', use (b go). For 'a bit', use (yudinr): (T yudinr i) She is a bit short. For hair: (tufa). Colors: (hi) black, (bi) white, (hu) grey. Length: (chng) long, (dun) short. Style: (zh) straight, (jun) curly. Example: (T yu y tu chng zh hi f ) She has long straight black hair. Note: (y tu) is measure word for hair.

For eyes: (ynjing). Size: (d ) big, (xio) small. Color: (zngs) brown, (lns) blue, (ls) green. Shape: (yun) round, (x) narrow. Example: (T yu d ynjing) He has big eyes. To say 'with', use (yu) or (zhngzhe) for facial features: (T zhngzhe zngs ynjing) She has brown eyes.

Other features: (lin) face: (yun lin) round face, (chng lin) long face. (bzi) nose: (go bzi) high nose, (bin bzi) flat nose. (zuba) mouth: (d zuba) big mouth, (xio zuba) small mouth. Skin: (pf): (hi) dark, (bi) light, (hung) yellow. Example: (T pf hn hi) His skin is very dark.

In Africa, people have diverse features. Practice describing yourself: (W hn go, w yu hi tufa h zngs ynjing). Describe a friend: (W de pngyou yudinr i, t yu junf ). Note: (junf ) means curly hair. Use before adjectives: , , , . To ask: (T go ma?) Is he tall? Answer: (T hn go) or (T b go).

Remember: adjectives can be verbs in Chinese. So (T go) is not common; use (T hn go). Practice by describing family photos: (Zh sh w b ba, t hn go, yu hi tufa).

3. Using (de) for Possession

The particle (de) is used to show possession, like 's in English. Structure: Possessor + + Possessed. Example: (w de sh) my book. (n de shuj) your phone. (t de ch) his car. For family: (w de mma) my mother. Note: For close family, is often omitted: (w mma) is also correct. But for clarity, use .

To ask 'whose?', use (shu de): (Zh sh shu de sh?) Whose book is this? Answer: (Zh sh w de sh). For plural: (wmen de) our, (nmen de) your (plural), (tmen de) their. Example: (wmen de losh) our teacher.

can also be used to nominalize, meaning 'the one that is...'. Example: (hngs de) the red one. (d de) the big one. (w de) mine. In a sentence: (W xhuan d de) I like the big one. To say 'my family's', use (w ji de): (w ji de gu) my family's dog.

In African contexts, you might talk about community: (wmen cn de) our village's. (Fizhu de) Africa's. Example: (Fizhu de wnhu ) Africa's culture. Practice by making sentences: (Zh sh w de pngyou). (N sh t de fngzi).

Be careful: is not used for adjectives directly before nouns. Say (ho rn) good person, not . But when adjective is used as noun, use : (ho de) the good one. Also, for relationships, is optional: (w b ba) is fine. But for non-family, always use : (w de tngxu) my classmate. Practice with a partner: point to objects and say ...? (Zh sh shu de...?) and answer ... (Zh sh... de).

4. Measure Words for People and Objects

In Chinese, you must use measure words ( li ngc) between a number and a noun. For people, the common measure word is (g). Example: (y g rn) one person. (sn g pngyou) three friends. For family members, (ku) is used for number of people in a family: (w ku rn) five people (in a family). But is also acceptable.

For objects, different measure words apply. (bn) for books: (ling bn sh) two books. (zhng) for flat objects like paper, tables, beds: (y zhng zh) one piece of paper. (b) for tools with handles: (y b do) one knife. (li ng) for vehicles: (y li ng ch) one car. (zh) for animals: (y zh gu) one dog. (ji n) for clothes: (y ji n yfu) one piece of clothing.

For family photos, you might say: (zh zhng zh opi n) this photo. (n zhng zh opi n) that photo. For hair, (tu) as in (y tu chng f ) a head of long hair. For eyes, (shung) for pairs: (y shung ynjing) a pair of eyes. For shoes, (shung): (y shung xi) a pair of shoes.

In Africa, you might talk about livestock: (y tu ni) one cow. (y zh j) one chicken. (y tio y) one fish. For crops: (y k sh) one tree. (y l m) one grain of rice. Practice by counting items around you: (W yu sn g ddi). (W ji yu w ku rn). (Zhuzi sh ng yu ling bn sh) There are two books on the table.

Remember: measure words are required. Never say ; say . For 'this' and 'that', use (zh) and (n ) + measure word: (zh g), (n g), (zh bn), (n bn). Practice with a partner: ask ...? (N yu j g...?) How many ... do you have? Answer with number + measure word + noun.

5. Talking About Hobbies and Interests

To talk about hobbies, use (xhuan) to like. Structure: + activity. Example: (W xhuan k n sh) I like reading. (W xhuan tng ynyu) I like listening to music. (W xhuan yndng) I like sports. For 'very much', add : ... (W hn xhuan...). To ask: (N xhuan shnme?) What do you like?

Common hobbies: (ch ngg) singing, (ti ow) dancing, (hu hu ) drawing, (t zqi) playing football, (d lnqi) playing basketball, (pob) running, (yuyng) swimming, (zuf n) cooking, (pizh o) taking photos. In Africa, football is popular: (W xhuan t zqi). Also (dg) drumming, (ti ow) dancing.

To say 'I also like', use (y): (W y xhuan ch ngg). To say 'I don't like', use (b xhuan): (W b xhuan pob). For 'a little', use (yudinr): (W yudinr xhuan hu hu ). To ask about frequency: ...(N jngchng... ma?) Do you often...? Answer: ... (W jngchng...) I often... or ... (W b jngchng...).

To talk about hobbies with family: (W b ba xhuan xi q) My dad likes playing chess. (W mma xhuan zuf n). (W ddi xhuan d yux) My younger brother likes playing games. Practice by making sentences about each family member.

In African context, you might say: (W xhuan d Fizhug) I like playing African drums. (W xhuan tng Fizhu ynyu). To express interest in learning: ... (W xing xu...) I want to learn... Example: (W xing xu Zhngwn). Practice with a partner: ask and answer about hobbies. Use (ynwi) because: (W xhuan pob, ynwi hn ji nkng) I like running because it's healthy.

6. Describing Your Family Photo

When describing a family photo, start with ... (Zh sh...) This is... and ... (N sh...) That is... For example: (Zh sh w de qunjif) This is my family photo. (qunjif) means family portrait. Then point to people: (Zh sh w b ba). (Zh sh w mma). (Zh sh w gge). Use for possession: (w de b ba) is also correct.

Describe each person: (W b ba hn go, yu hi tufa). (W mma yudinr i, yu chng tufa). (W gge yu d ynjing). Use measure words: (W b ba chun y ji n bi chnshn) My dad wears a white shirt. (W mma chun y tio hng qnzi) My mom wears a red dress. Note: (chun) for clothes, (tio) for dresses/pants.

Talk about location in the photo: (zubin) left, (yubin) right, (zhngjin) middle, (qinmi n) front, (humi n) back. Example: (W z i zhngjin) I am in the middle. (W ddi z i zubin). (W mimei z i yubin). Use (z i) for location.

Talk about hobbies in the photo: (Wmen z i gngyun) We are in the park. (W b ba xhuan pizh o). (W mma xhuan ti ow). (W xhuan t zqi). In Africa, photos might be at a wedding, church, or village gathering. Example: (Zh sh z i wmen de cnzhung) This is in our village.

Practice by taking a real photo and describing it in Mandarin. Start with ... Then describe each person: height, hair, eyes, clothes, location. Use (h) to connect: (W b ba h w mma). End with (Wmen hn goxng) We are very happy. Record yourself and listen. Repeat until fluent.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Family and Descriptions in Mandarin for African Agribusiness

In agribusiness, relationships are capital. Whether you are negotiating a supply contract with a Chinese buyer, onboarding a Mandarin-speaking technical partner, or introducing your cooperative to a visiting delegation, the ability to describe people and family structures in Mandarin builds trust fast. This practical reference module equips you with the vocabulary, grammar patterns, and cultural context to talk about family, describe colleagues and counterparts, and navigate the social dimensions of Chinese business culture all grounded in African agribusiness realities.

Mandarin family terminology is precise. Unlike English, which uses broad terms like "aunt" or "cousin," Mandarin distinguishes maternal and paternal relatives, older and younger siblings, and in-laws with specific words. For an agribusiness professional, this precision matters: when a Chinese partner mentions their bio g (older male cousin via mother's side), you know exactly where that person sits in the family hierarchy and hierarchy often maps to decision-making authority in family-run agribusiness enterprises.

Describing People: Height, Hair, and Eyes

When you describe a person in Mandarin, the structure follows a predictable pattern: subject + (hn, "very") + adjective. For example, (t hn go) "He is tall." Note that is often a neutral connector rather than an intensifier; without it, the sentence can sound comparative.

For agribusiness contexts, describing people accurately helps in logistics: identifying a driver at a pickup point, describing a new staff member to a Chinese partner, or writing a bio for a trade delegation. Key vocabulary includes:

Using (de) for Possession

The particle (de) is the Mandarin equivalent of the English possessive apostrophe-s. The pattern is: Possessor + + Possessed. For example:

  • (w de gngs) my company
  • (wmen de nngchng) our farm
  • (Zhnggu de kh) China's clients / Chinese clients
  • (hzush de chngyun) the cooperative's members

When the relationship is close (family, close colleagues), is often omitted: (w mma) "my mother" is more natural than . In business, however, keeping is standard and respectful: (wmen de hzu hub n) "our business partner."

Common error: English speakers sometimes place after the possessed noun. Remember: the possessor comes first. (the company's farm) is correct; (farm's company) is wrong.

Talking About Hobbies and Interests

Hobbies are a relationship-building topic in Chinese business culture. After the formal negotiation, a dinner conversation about interests can open doors. The pattern is:

  • + [activity] I like + [activity]
  • + [topic] + I am interested in + [topic]
  • + [activity] My hobby is + [activity]

Useful agribusiness-adjacent hobbies: (zhng c i vegetable gardening), (yng fng beekeeping), (di o y fishing), (p shn hiking), (h ch drinking tea), (k n nngy xnwn reading agricultural news).

Cultural note: In Chinese business settings, shared meals often include discussion of food origins. Knowing how to describe your family's farming history (wmen ji zhng kfi our family grows coffee) creates immediate rapport.

Describing Your Family Photo

A common task in Chinese language exams and business introductions is describing a family photo. The structure is: location + (yu, "have") + number + measure word + person + descriptive clause. Example:

Translation: "There are five people in this photo. On the left is my father; he is tall and wears glasses. On the right is my mother; she has long hair. In the middle is my younger sister; she loves growing flowers. Our family farm is in Kenya; we grow tea."

This structure works for business introductions too: describing your team photo, your cooperative members, or your production site. The key is to move from general (number of people) to specific (positions, descriptions, relationships).

Safety Warning: Cultural and Privacy Considerations

In Chinese business culture, asking about family is common and polite but there are boundaries. Do not ask about a person's marital status, children, or age unless they volunteer it first. Since 2016, China's revised Family Planning Law allows two children, but family size remains a sensitive topic for some. In African contexts, similar sensitivities exist around polygamous family structures and child mortality. When describing your own family photo in a business setting, share only what you are comfortable with. Never photograph or share images of colleagues' family members without explicit consent this violates privacy norms under both Chinese law (PIPL, 2021) and African data protection frameworks (e.g., Kenya's Data Protection Act 2019, Nigeria's NDPR 2019).

Source: Personal Information Protection Law of the People's Republic of China (PIPL), effective November 1, 2021; Kenya Data Protection Act No. 24 of 2019.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Family terms: Know the difference between / (paternal) and / (maternal).
  • Descriptions: Use + adjective for neutral statements; avoid (old) as a compliment.
  • Possession: Possessor + + possessed. Omit only with close family.
  • Measure words: for polite people, for cattle, for poultry, for bags, for tons.
  • Hobbies: Use + activity or + topic + .
  • Photo description: Location + + number + measure word + person + description.
  • Business etiquette: Let Chinese counterparts initiate family topics; reciprocate only at their comfort level.
  • Data privacy: Never share family photos or personal details without consent PIPL and African data laws apply.
