1. Hands-On Pad Making (Disposable)

To make disposable pads, you need absorbent core materials, a leak-proof backsheet, and a soft topsheet. In Africa, common core materials are fluff pulp (from wood pulp) or SAP (super absorbent polymer) mixed with tissue. You can buy fluff pulp from suppliers in Nairobi, Lagos, or Johannesburg. For a small workshop, start with 100 pads per batch. You will need: a pad-making machine (manual or semi-automatic), a heat sealer, a cutting die, and raw materials. If you cannot afford a machine, you can make pads manually using a sewing machine and heat-sealable wrappers.

Step 1: Cut the topsheet (non-woven fabric) to 8 cm x 23 cm. Cut the backsheet (polyethylene film) to the same size. Cut the absorbent core (fluff pulp + SAP) to 7 cm x 20 cm. Step 2: Place the core on the backsheet, then cover with topsheet. Step 3: Seal the edges using a heat sealer at 150Â°C for 2 seconds. Step 4: Attach wings (if desired) by cutting side flaps from the topsheet and sealing. Step 5: Fold and wrap in individual wrappers. For 100 pads, you need about 1 kg of fluff pulp, 0.2 kg of SAP, 2 mÂ² of non-woven, and 2 mÂ² of polyethylene. Cost per pad in Kenya is roughly KES 8â12 (USD 0.06â0.09) if you buy in bulk. Sell at KES 20â30.

Safety: Wear a dust mask when handling SAP and fluff pulp. Work in a well-ventilated area. Keep heat sealer away from flammable materials. Quality check: Test absorbency by pouring 10 ml of water on a pad; it should absorb in under 10 seconds without leaking. If it leaks, add more SAP or increase core thickness. Common mistakes: too little SAP (leaks), uneven sealing (pad opens), and using dirty hands (contamination). Train your team on hygiene: wash hands, wear gloves, keep workspace clean. With practice, two people can make 200 pads per day. Start small, sell to local schools and clinics, and reinvest profits to buy a semi-automatic machine (cost: USD 2,000â5,000).

2. Hands-On Pad Making (Reusable)

Reusable pads are made from layers of absorbent fabric (cotton, bamboo, or flannel) and a waterproof layer (PUL â polyurethane laminate, or recycled plastic). They are washed and reused for 1â2 years. To make one reusable pad, you need: 3 layers of cotton (each 10 cm x 25 cm), 1 layer of PUL (same size), a sewing machine, thread, and snaps or velcro. In Africa, cotton fabric costs about KES 300 per metre; one metre makes 4 pads. PUL costs KES 800 per metre; one metre makes 10 pads. So material cost per pad is roughly KES 150â200 (USD 1.10â1.50). You can sell for KES 300â500.

Step 1: Cut fabric. For the absorbent core, use 3 layers of cotton. For the back, use 1 layer of PUL. Step 2: Stack the cotton layers and sew them together with a zigzag stitch around the edges. Step 3: Place the PUL behind the cotton stack, with the shiny side facing out. Sew around the edges, leaving a small opening to turn inside out. Step 4: Turn the pad inside out so the PUL is on the outside. Sew the opening closed. Step 5: Add wings by cutting two rectangles of cotton (5 cm x 10 cm) and sewing them to the sides. Attach snaps or velcro to the wings. Step 6: Wash the pad in hot water before first use to shrink and sanitise.

Quality check: Pour 10 ml of water on the pad; it should absorb within 5 seconds and not leak through the PUL. If it leaks, add another cotton layer. Common mistakes: using fabric softener (reduces absorbency), not pre-washing, and poor stitching (pads fall apart after 5 washes). For a workshop, you can train 5 women to make 20 pads per day each. Start with a sewing machine (USD 100â200) and local fabric. Sell to NGOs, schools, and markets. Reusable pads are popular because they are cheap long-term and eco-friendly. Keep a record of each pad's production date and fabric type for quality control.

3. Testing Pad Performance

Testing pad performance ensures your product works and meets customer needs. You need: a graduated cylinder, a stopwatch, a scale, and a testing table. For absorbency, pour 10 ml of water (or synthetic blood: 0.9% saline) onto the centre of the pad. Start the stopwatch. Record the time it takes for the liquid to disappear from the surface (should be under 10 seconds for disposable, under 5 for reusable). Then press a blotting paper on the pad with 1 kg weight for 1 minute. If the blotting paper is wet, the pad leaks under pressure. Repeat 3 times per pad; test 5 pads per batch.

For leakage, place the pad on a tilted surface (30 degrees) and pour 10 ml of liquid at the top. See if liquid runs off the sides. For disposable pads, check the backsheet for wetness. For reusable, check the PUL layer. For odour control, add 1 ml of vinegar to the liquid and smell after 30 minutes. A good pad should not smell strongly. For comfort, test thickness: a disposable pad should be 2â3 mm thick; reusable 3â5 mm. If too thick, customers won't buy.

Record all results in a notebook: date, batch number, absorbency time, leakage (yes/no), and odour rating (1â5). If a batch fails, adjust the formula: add more SAP for disposable, add more cotton layers for reusable. Common issues: slow absorbency (too little SAP or cotton), leakage (poor sealing or insufficient layers), and odour (no antibacterial treatment). For antibacterial treatment, add 1% silver nitrate or use bamboo fabric (naturally antibacterial). Test after every 100 pads. Share results with your team and improve. In Kenya, the Kenya Bureau of Standards (KEBS) requires absorbency under 10 seconds and no leakage for certification. Use their standards as your target.

4. Developing a Menstrual Health Campaign

A menstrual health campaign educates your community and sells your pads. Start by identifying your audience: school girls (age 10â19), women in markets, or refugees. In Africa, many girls miss school during periods because they lack pads. A campaign can change that. First, set a goal: e.g., 'Reach 500 girls in 3 months with pads and education.' Second, partner with local schools, churches, mosques, and NGOs. Third, create simple materials: posters in Swahili, English, or local language; a 10-minute talk; and a demo of pad use.

Your talk should cover: what menstruation is (normal, not dirty), how to use a pad (disposable: change every 4â6 hours; reusable: wash with soap and dry in sun), and where to buy pads. Use a cloth model of a vagina (made from fabric) to show how to place a pad. Address myths: 'You cannot cook during periods' â false. 'You cannot bathe' â false. Keep it positive. For a campaign, train 5 peer educators (older girls or women) to lead sessions. Give each a script and a pad sample. Hold sessions at schools during lunch break. Distribute free pads to the first 50 girls, then sell at a subsidised price (KES 10 for disposable, KES 100 for reusable).

Budget: For 500 girls, you need 500 pads (KES 5,000), 100 posters (KES 2,000), transport (KES 3,000), and refreshments (KES 2,000). Total: KES 12,000 (USD 90). Seek funding from local businesses, county government, or crowdfunding. Track your impact: count how many girls attend, how many buy pads, and how many report improved school attendance. Use a simple form: name, age, school, did you use the pad? After 3 months, if 80% of girls say they now use pads, your campaign works. Document success stories and share on social media to attract more support. Remember: confidentiality is key. Never ask a girl to publicly say she is menstruating. Use anonymous feedback boxes.

5. Business Plan Development

A business plan helps you get funding and manage your pad business. Write it in 5 sections: 1) Business description, 2) Market analysis, 3) Operations, 4) Marketing, 5) Financials. For a pad-making business in Africa, start with a simple one-page plan. Business description: 'We make and sell affordable sanitary pads to girls and women in [your town].' Market analysis: How many girls in your area? In a town of 10,000, about 2,500 are menstruating. If you capture 10%, that's 250 customers per month. Competitors: imported pads cost KES 100â150 per pack; your pads cost KES 20â30. You have an advantage.

Operations: Where will you make pads? A room with clean water, electricity, and a table. Equipment: sewing machine (USD 150), heat sealer (USD 100), raw materials (USD 200 per month). Labour: 2 workers at KES 500 per day. Production: 200 pads per day. Marketing: Use radio, WhatsApp, and school visits. Offer a free pad to first-time buyers. Financials: Start-up capital: KES 50,000 (USD 380). Monthly costs: materials KES 20,000, labour KES 20,000, rent KES 5,000, transport KES 3,000. Total: KES 48,000. Monthly revenue: 200 pads/day x 20 days x KES 25 = KES 100,000. Profit: KES 52,000 per month. Break-even in 1 month. But be realistic: start with 50 pads/day. Then profit is KES 25,000 â 48,000 = loss. So you need to sell at least 100 pads/day to break even. Adjust by raising price to KES 30 or reducing costs.

Include a cash flow statement for 6 months. Show how you will repay a loan. Add a risk section: competition, raw material price increase, and stigma. Mitigate by building a brand and educating customers. Keep records of every sale and expense. Use a notebook or a free app like Wave. After 6 months, review and update your plan. A good plan is 2â3 pages. Present it to a bank, NGO, or investor. In Kenya, you can get a loan from Youth Enterprise Fund at 8% interest. Use the plan to show you are serious.

6. Final Project Presentation and Evaluation

Your final project is to make a batch of pads, test them, and present your business plan. You will be evaluated on: product quality (40%), business plan (30%), presentation (20%), and teamwork (10%). Start by preparing 10 disposable pads and 10 reusable pads. Test each batch using the methods from Lesson 3. Record absorbency time, leakage, and odour. Make a simple poster showing your results. For the business plan, use the template from Lesson 5. Fill in your local numbers: cost per pad, selling price, monthly profit. Practice your presentation in front of friends. Time yourself: 5 minutes for product demo, 5 minutes for business plan, 5 minutes for questions.

On presentation day, set up a table with your pads, testing tools, and poster. Show how you make a pad in 2 minutes. Then explain your test results: 'Our disposable pad absorbs 10 ml in 8 seconds, no leakage.' Then present your business plan: 'We need KES 50,000 to start. We will sell 100 pads per day at KES 25. Monthly profit is KES 27,000.' Answer questions from the evaluator. Be honest about challenges: 'Our first batch leaked because we used too little SAP. We fixed it by adding 0.5 g more per pad.'

Evaluation rubric: Product quality â pads must absorb under 10 seconds, no leakage, and be comfortable. Business plan â must include costs, revenue, profit, and break-even. Presentation â clear voice, eye contact, and visual aids. Teamwork â everyone contributes. After evaluation, you will get a score. If you pass (70% or above), you receive a certificate from rise AFRICA skills. If you fail, you can retake the module. Use feedback to improve. For example, if your business plan lacked a cash flow, add one. If your pads leaked, adjust the formula. Keep your best pads as samples for future customers. Celebrate your success â you are now a pad maker and entrepreneur. Share your skills with others: train 2 friends for free. This multiplies your impact and builds your reputation.

â  SAFETY WARNING â Hygiene, Chemicals & Waste
  • Never use unapproved SAP, industrial-grade pulp, or recycled film in a product that contacts mucosal tissue. Source food/medical-grade or sanitary-grade materials only, with certificates of analysis (CoA).
  • Production space must be dust-controlled, dry, and pest-free. Workers must wear gloves, hairnets, and masks. Hand-washing stations and clean uniforms are non-negotiable.
  • Adhesives and films can release VOCs â ensure ventilation. Do not store SAP near moisture; it will clump and lose absorbency.
  • Disposable pad waste is a real environmental issue. Plan a disposal or take-back message in your Menstrual Health Campaign; consider a reusable line to reduce waste and diversify revenue.
  • Do not make medical claims (e.g., "prevents infection") without regulatory clearance. Stick to hygiene and comfort claims you can substantiate.
