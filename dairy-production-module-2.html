1. Nutrient requirements of dairy cattle

Dairy cows need six key nutrients: water, carbohydrates, proteins, fats, minerals, and vitamins. The amount required depends on body weight, milk production, and stage of lactation. For example, a 450 kg cow producing 20 litres of milk per day needs about 16-18 kg of dry matter (DM) intake, 12-14% crude protein in the total diet, and 1.7 Mcal/kg of energy (TDN). In early lactation, energy demand is highest; a cow may lose weight because she cannot eat enough to meet her needs. This is normal but should be managed.

To calculate nutrient needs, use the cow's body weight and milk yield. A simple rule: for every litre of milk, a cow needs about 0.3 kg of concentrate (e.g., dairy meal) plus good quality forage. For a 20-litre cow, that's 6 kg of concentrate. But this varies with forage quality. Poor quality straw has low energy and protein, so more concentrate is needed. Always test your forage or use local extension advice.

Minerals like calcium and phosphorus are critical. A cow needs about 0.6% calcium and 0.4% phosphorus in her total diet. In many African soils, these are deficient, so provide mineral licks. Vitamins A, D, and E are also important; green forage provides these. If cows are dry or on poor pasture, supplement.

Water is the most critical nutrient. A cow drinks 3-5 litres of water per litre of milk, plus more in hot weather. For a 20-litre cow, that's 60-100 litres per day. Never restrict water. Adjust feeding based on body condition score (BCS): aim for BCS 3 on a 1-5 scale. If cows are too thin, increase energy; if too fat, reduce. Monitor milk yield and adjust. Remember, overfeeding protein is wasteful and costly, while underfeeding reduces milk and fertility.

2. Feedstuffs: forages, concentrates, and by-products

Forages are the backbone of dairy diets. They include grasses (Napier, Rhodes, Kikuyu), legumes (alfalfa, desmodium, lablab), and crop residues (maize stover, rice straw). Good quality forage is green, leafy, and cut before flowering. Napier grass can yield 20-30 tonnes DM per hectare per year if fertilized. Legumes add protein: alfalfa hay has 15-20% crude protein, while maize stover has only 4-6%. In Africa, farmers often use cut-and-carry systems with Napier and desmodium. Conserve excess forage as hay or silage. Hay is dried to 15-20% moisture; silage is fermented at 65-70% moisture. Maize silage is high energy (70% TDN) and palatable.

Concentrates are energy or protein feeds. Energy concentrates include maize grain, sorghum, molasses, and wheat bran. Maize grain has about 80% TDN and 9% protein. Protein concentrates include soybean meal (44-48% protein), cottonseed cake (36-40%), and sunflower cake (28-30%). These are expensive, so use them strategically. A typical dairy meal in East Africa contains 16% protein and is fed at 1 kg per 2.5 litres of milk. Mix your own: 50 kg maize, 20 kg wheat bran, 25 kg soybean meal, 3 kg mineral premix, and 2 kg salt. This gives about 16% protein.

By-products are cheap alternatives. Brewers' grains (20-25% protein), molasses (high energy but laxative), and citrus pulp are common. In Kenya, farmers use sweet potato vines, banana pseudostems, and poultry litter (only for dry cows, not lactating). Always introduce new feeds gradually to avoid digestive upsets. Avoid mouldy or spoiled feeds. Store concentrates in dry, ventilated places. For smallholders, planting fodder trees like Leucaena and Calliandra provides free protein. Calliandra has 20-25% protein and can be fed at 2-3 kg per day. Combine forages and concentrates to balance the ration and reduce costs.

3. Formulating balanced rations for different production stages

A balanced ration meets the cow's needs for energy, protein, minerals, and vitamins without excess or deficiency. Start by grouping cows by production stage: dry cows, fresh cows (first 3 weeks after calving), early lactation (0-100 days), mid lactation (100-200 days), and late lactation (200+ days). Dry cows need 12-14 kg DM, 12% protein, and low energy to avoid fatness. Feed mostly good quality forage plus 2 kg concentrate. Fresh cows need 16-18 kg DM, 18% protein, and high energy to support peak milk. Feed 4-6 kg concentrate, divided into 2-3 meals. Early lactation cows need 20-24 kg DM, 18-19% protein, and 1.7 Mcal/kg energy. A 25-litre cow may need 8-10 kg concentrate.

Use the Pearson Square method to balance protein. Suppose you have maize (9% protein) and soybean meal (44% protein) and want a 16% mix. Draw a square: put 16 in the centre, 9 on left top, 44 on left bottom. Subtract diagonally: 44-16=28 parts maize, 16-9=7 parts soybean meal. So 28 kg maize + 7 kg soybean meal = 35 kg mix with 16% protein. Scale up as needed. Add 2% minerals and 1% salt.

For energy, ensure forage is high quality. If milk yield drops, increase concentrate by 0.5 kg per litre of milk above 15 litres. But don't exceed 2 kg per 100 kg body weight per meal. For dry cows, feed low-energy forage like straw and limit concentrate. For heifers, feed to reach 350-400 kg at first calving. Always provide fresh water and mineral licks. Monitor body condition: dry cows should be BCS 3.0-3.5, early lactation 2.5-3.0. Adjust ration if BCS changes. In Africa, use locally available feeds: maize silage, Napier, and Calliandra. Test your ration by weighing milk and checking manure for undigested grain. If manure is loose, reduce protein; if firm and dry, increase energy.

4. Feeding management and practices

Feeding management ensures cows eat the right amount at the right time. Feed at least twice a day, preferably after milking. Cows are creatures of habit; keep a consistent schedule. Provide 60-70 cm of trough space per cow so all can eat. For a 20-cow herd, that's 12-14 metres of trough. Clean troughs daily to prevent mould and disease. Feed forage first, then concentrate. This encourages chewing and saliva production, which buffers the rumen. Never feed more than 2 kg of concentrate per meal; split into 3 meals if needed. In hot climates, feed early morning and evening when it's cooler.

Use the 'challenge feeding' method: give a base amount of concentrate and increase by 0.5 kg every 2-3 days until milk yield stops increasing. Then maintain. This helps identify the cow's true potential. For example, start with 4 kg and increase to 6 kg if milk rises. If milk doesn't increase, drop back. This avoids overfeeding. Also, group cows by production: high yielders get more concentrate. In small herds, use a simple neck strap or chalk mark to identify groups.

Water management is key: provide clean water ad libitum. A lactating cow drinks 60-100 litres daily. Use a trough with a float valve or fill manually 3 times a day. In dry areas, harvest rainwater. Minerals: offer a mineral lick (e.g., Maclik) free choice. Salt blocks are not enough; they lack trace minerals. Weigh feed accurately; use a kitchen scale or a calibrated bucket. Don't guess. Record feed intake and milk yield daily. If a cow goes off feed, check for illness. Introduce new feeds over 7-10 days. Avoid sudden changes. For grazing systems, rotate pastures to prevent overgrazing. In zero-grazing, cut and carry fresh forage twice daily. Always provide shade and ventilation. Stress reduces intake. Handle cows gently. These practices increase milk yield by 10-20% without extra feed cost.

5. Water requirements and quality

Water is the most important nutrient for dairy cattle. A lactating cow needs 3-5 litres of water per litre of milk produced. So a cow giving 20 litres needs 60-100 litres per day. In hot weather (above 30Â°C), needs increase by 20-50%. Dry cows need 30-50 litres. Heifers need 20-30 litres. If water is restricted, milk yield drops immediately. Cows can survive days without feed but not without water. Always provide water ad libitum. A cow will drink 10-20 litres per minute if thirsty, so ensure troughs can refill quickly.

Water quality affects intake and health. Clean, fresh water is essential. Test water for total dissolved solids (TDS), nitrates, and bacteria. TDS should be below 1000 mg/L; above 3000 mg/L is unsafe. Nitrates above 100 mg/L can cause poisoning. In many African regions, borehole water may have high salts or fluoride. If water tastes salty, cows drink less. Use rainwater or treated water if available. Avoid stagnant water; it breeds mosquitoes and carries disease. Clean troughs weekly with a brush and disinfectant. Do not let algae grow. In zero-grazing units, place troughs in shade to keep water cool.

Water sources: boreholes, rivers, dams, rainwater harvesting, and piped water. For smallholders, a 200-litre drum with a tap can serve 2-3 cows. But refill daily. In dry seasons, water scarcity is common. Harvest rainwater from roofs into tanks. A 10,000-litre tank can supply 5 cows for a month. If using river water, filter or let settle, then chlorinate if possible. For communal grazing, ensure cows have access to water at least twice daily. Never let cows walk more than 2 km to water; it wastes energy. In hot climates, add electrolytes (e.g., 1 tablespoon salt + 1 tablespoon baking soda per 20 litres) during heat stress. Monitor water intake: if a cow drinks less than expected, check for illness or poor quality. Provide water before milking to reduce stress. Remember, water is cheap; milk is not. Invest in water infrastructure.

6. Common nutritional disorders and their prevention

Nutritional disorders reduce milk yield and can kill cows. The most common in Africa are bloat, acidosis, milk fever, ketosis, and mineral deficiencies. Bloat occurs when cows eat lush legumes (alfalfa, clover) or young grass. Gas builds up in the rumen. Prevent by feeding dry forage before grazing legumes, and avoid sudden access. If bloat occurs, drench with 100 ml vegetable oil or walk the cow. For severe cases, use a trocar. Acidosis happens when too much concentrate (grain) is fed. The rumen becomes acidic, causing diarrhoea, off-feed, and laminitis. Prevent by limiting concentrate to 2 kg per meal, feeding forage first, and adding 1% sodium bicarbonate to the ration. Introduce grains gradually over 1 week.

Milk fever (hypocalcaemia) affects high-yielding cows after calving. Signs: muscle tremors, staggering, lying down. Prevent by feeding a low-calcium diet (e.g., grass hay) for 2 weeks before calving, then a high-calcium diet after. If signs appear, call a vet for calcium injection. Ketosis (acetonemia) occurs in early lactation when energy demand exceeds intake. Signs: weight loss, reduced milk, sweet-smelling breath. Prevent by feeding high-energy rations (maize silage, molasses) and avoiding fat cows at calving. Treat with propylene glycol (300 ml daily) or glucose.

Mineral deficiencies are widespread. In East Africa, soils lack phosphorus, copper, and selenium. Signs: poor fertility, weak bones, and white muscle disease. Provide a mineral lick with 8% phosphorus, plus copper and selenium. In zinc-deficient areas, supplement with zinc sulphate. Vitamin A deficiency causes night blindness and poor fertility; feed green forage or inject vitamin A. Urea poisoning occurs when urea is mixed incorrectly. Never feed more than 1% urea in the total diet, and mix well. Signs: salivation, tremors, death. Treat with 5 litres of vinegar in water. Prevention: always provide clean water, balanced rations, and gradual changes. Keep records of feed and health. Work with a vet or extension officer. Early detection saves cows and money.

1. How Nutrients Become Milk: The Digestion Pathway

Dairy cattle are ruminants. Their four-chambered stomach allows them to convert fibrous foragesâcrop residues, pasture, and conserved roughageâinto milk. The key biological insight for African dairy businesses is that the rumen is a fermentation vat: microbes break down fibre into volatile fatty acids (VFAs), which supply most of the cow's energy. When we feed concentrates, we are feeding the microbes as much as the cow. Any sudden change in diet disrupts this microbial ecosystem, reducing milk yield and butterfat, and can cause acidosis.

3. Formulating Rations: A Business Approach

Ration formulation is a cost-minimisation exercise. Start with the cheapest available forage to meet fibre needs. Then add concentrates to fill the energy and protein gap. In East Africa, a common profitable strategy is: ad libitum Napier or maize silage + 1 kg concentrate per 2.5 L milk above 5 L + mineral supplement. This simple rule avoids overfeeding and protects margins.

For a 450 kg cow producing 20 L/day, a practical ration might include: 12 kg fresh Napier (or 8 kg silage), 4 kg maize grain, 1.5 kg cottonseed cake, 200 g molasses, and 100 g mineral mix. This provides roughly 15 kg DM, 16% CP, and 10.5 MJ ME/kg DMâenough for maintenance plus 18â20 L. Adjust based on body condition and milk response.
