1. Overview of Cassava Processing Steps

Cassava roots start spoiling within 24 to 48 hours after harvest. To turn them into safe, storable food, you must process them quickly. The main steps are: peeling, washing, chipping or grating, dewatering, sieving, drying, and sometimes milling or frying. Each step removes water or toxins and changes the texture for the next stage. For example, fresh roots contain about 60-70% water. After processing into gari or flour, moisture drops to 10-12%. That is why drying is critical.

In Africa, most processing is done by hand or with small machines. A typical day for a family might start with harvesting 200 kg of roots. They peel by hand with knives, wash in basins or drums, then grate using a motorized grater (often 5-10 HP). The grated pulp is pressed in a hydraulic jack or screw press to remove excess water. Then it is sieved to break lumps and finally dried on a concrete floor, solar dryer, or mechanical dryer. The whole process can take 1-3 days depending on weather and equipment.

Different end products need different steps. For gari, after pressing you ferment for 1-3 days, then sieve, fry in a hot pan with palm oil, and sieve again. For cassava flour, you skip fermentation, press, dry, and mill. For chips, you slice roots into pieces and dry them directly. Always use fresh roots; discard any that are soft, moldy, or smell bad. Also, never eat raw cassavaâit contains cyanide that must be removed by soaking, fermenting, or drying. Proper processing makes cassava safe and profitable. Start with small batches to learn the timing, then scale up as you master each step.

2. Peeling, Washing, and Chipping

Peeling is the first hands-on step. Use a sharp knife with a short blade, like a kitchen knife or machete. Hold the root firmly and cut off both ends, then slit the skin lengthwise and peel it away. The outer brown skin and the inner pinkish layer must be removed because they contain most of the cyanide. A skilled person can peel 50-80 kg per hour. For large-scale, you can buy a motorized peeler (cost: $500-$2000) that rubs roots against rough surfaces, but hand peeling is still common in Africa.

After peeling, wash the roots in clean water. Use a large basin, drum, or concrete tank. Change the water oftenâdirty water spreads bacteria. For 100 kg of roots, use about 200 liters of water. Scrub with your hands or a soft brush to remove dirt and remaining skin. If you plan to make chips, you can skip washing and just peel, but washing improves quality.

Chipping means cutting roots into small pieces, usually 2-5 cm thick. Use a sharp knife or a mechanical chipper. Chips dry faster than whole roots. Spread chips in a thin layer (5-10 cm deep) on a drying rack or clean concrete. In sunny weather, 100 kg of fresh chips will dry to about 30-35 kg in 2-3 days. Turn them twice a day. If it rains, cover them or move them under a shelter. Chips are ready when they snap easily and are brittle. Store dried chips in jute bags in a dry room. They can be milled into flour or sold to processors. Always wear gloves when peeling to avoid skin irritation from cassava juice.

3. Grating and Pulping Techniques

Grating turns peeled roots into a fine pulp. This breaks cell walls and releases enzymes that help remove cyanide during fermentation. The traditional method uses a hand grater (a piece of tin with punched holes nailed to a board). It is slowâonly 10-15 kg per hourâand hard on the hands. Better is a motorized grater. In many African markets, you can buy a locally made grater for $300-$800. It has a rotating drum with sharp teeth, powered by a 5-10 HP diesel or electric motor. It can grate 500-1000 kg per hour.

To grate, feed roots into the hopper while the machine runs. Mix in a little water to prevent clogging. The pulp comes out as a wet mash. For gari, you want a fine, uniform pulp. For other products like fufu, a coarser grate is okay. After grating, you can add a starter culture (like a spoonful of old fermented pulp) to speed up fermentation and improve flavor. Let the pulp sit in a clean container for 1-3 days. It will ferment, sour, and release water. This fermentation reduces cyanide by 80-95%.

If you don't have a machine, you can use a pulping method: soak peeled roots in water for 3-4 days until soft, then mash them with a pestle and mortar or squeeze them by hand. This is labor-intensive but works for small quantities. Always keep the grating area clean. Wash the machine after each use to prevent mold and bacteria. Wear a nose mask if the pulp smells strong. After grating, move quickly to pressingâdon't let the pulp sit too long before pressing, or it may spoil.

4. Dewatering and Pressing Methods

After grating or fermenting, the pulp contains 60-70% water. You must remove most of it to get a dry, stable product. Pressing does two things: it squeezes out water and helps compact the pulp into a cake. The traditional method is to put the pulp in a woven bag (like a polypropylene sack) and place heavy stones on top. This works but is slowâit can take 12-24 hours to press 50 kg. A better method is a screw press or hydraulic jack press. A simple screw press costs $200-$500 and can press 100 kg in 30-60 minutes. A hydraulic jack press (using a 10-20 ton jack) costs $500-$1500 and is faster.

To press, fill a clean sack with fermented pulp, tie it tightly, and place it in the press. Apply pressure gradually. Water will run out. Collect the waterâit can be used for animal feed or fertilizer after treatment. Press until the cake is firm and no more water drips. For gari, you want the cake to be about 40-50% moisture. For flour, press harder to get 30-40% moisture. If you press too hard, you may lose starch in the water, so don't overdo it.

After pressing, the cake is ready for sieving or drying. If you don't have a press, you can use a dewatering machine (also called a presser) that combines grinding and pressing. In some areas, you can rent a press from a local cooperative for $5-$10 per batch. Always press on a clean surface, like a concrete floor or a plastic sheet, to avoid contamination. Wear gloves and wash hands after handling the wet cake. Proper pressing saves drying time and fuel.

5. Sieving and Particle Size Reduction

Sieving breaks up the pressed cake into small, uniform particles. This is important for even drying and for the final texture of your product. For gari, you sieve after pressing and before frying. For flour, you sieve after drying and before milling. The traditional sieve is a woven basket or a mesh screen (like a mosquito net) stretched over a wooden frame. A standard sieve has holes of 2-5 mm. You can buy a set of sieves for $20-$50.

To sieve, rub the pressed cake through the sieve with your hands or a wooden paddle. The fine particles fall through; the lumps stay on top and are rubbed again. For 100 kg of wet cake, sieving takes 1-2 hours by hand. A motorized sieve (vibrating screen) costs $500-$1500 and can sieve 500 kg per hour. It is worth it if you process more than 500 kg per day. After sieving, you get a fluffy, uniform mash. For gari, you then fry this mash in a hot pan with a little palm oil. For flour, you spread the sieved mash on a drying tray.

Particle size matters. For gari, you want a medium-coarse grain (like semolina). For fufu flour, you want a fine powder (like wheat flour). You can adjust by using different sieve sizes. Always sieve in a clean, dust-free area. Cover the sieved product with a clean cloth to keep off flies and dust. If you are making chips, you skip sievingâchips are already in pieces. After sieving, move quickly to drying or frying to prevent spoilage. Clean your sieves after each use with water and a brush, then dry them in the sun.

6. Drying Methods: Sun, Solar, and Mechanical

Drying is the most critical step to preserve cassava products. Fresh processed cassava has 40-60% moisture. You must bring it down to 10-12% for safe storage. There are three main methods: sun drying, solar drying, and mechanical drying. Each has pros and cons.

Sun drying is the cheapest. Spread the product (chips, sieved mash, or grated pulp) on a clean concrete floor, black plastic sheet, or woven mat. Use a thin layer (2-5 cm). In full sun (30-35Â°C), it takes 2-3 days for chips, 1-2 days for gari mash. Turn the product every 2-3 hours with a rake or wooden paddle. Protect from rain and animals. Sun drying is free but slow and risky in rainy season. It also exposes food to dust and insects.

Solar drying uses a solar dryerâa box with a glass or plastic top and ventilation holes. The temperature inside can reach 50-70Â°C, so drying is 2-3 times faster than sun. A small solar dryer (1-2 mÂ²) costs $100-$300 and can dry 20-50 kg per batch. It protects from rain, dust, and animals. You can build one with local materials: wooden frame, clear polythene sheet, and black painted interior. Mechanical drying uses a fuel or electric dryer. A small mechanical dryer costs $500-$2000 and can dry 100-500 kg per batch in 4-8 hours. It is best for large-scale or rainy season. But it needs fuel (diesel, gas, or electricity), which adds cost.

Whichever method, test for dryness: a dried chip should snap, not bend. Dried gari should be crispy. Store dried products in airtight containers or jute bags in a dry, cool room. Never store while still warmâlet it cool first. Proper drying stops mold and keeps your cassava safe for months.

Raw cassava contains cyanogenic glycosides (linamarin and lotaustralin). Improper processing â especially insufficient soaking, fermentation, or drying â can leave dangerous residual cyanide. Never consume or sell cassava products that have not been adequately processed. Symptoms of cyanide poisoning include headache, dizziness, nausea, and in severe cases, respiratory failure. Additionally, mechanical graters, presses, and chippers cause severe injuries if operated without guards. Always use push-sticks, never hands, to feed machines. Ensure adequate ventilation when drying mechanically â cyanide gas can accumulate in enclosed drying rooms. Train all staff on emergency shutdown procedures and keep a first-aid kit on site.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Root freshness: Process within 24â48 hours of harvest; reject roots with soft spots, mould, or offensive odour.
  • Peeling quality: Remove all outer skin and cortex; aim for 85â90% peel removal with minimal flesh loss.
  • Washing efficiency: Use at least 3:1 water-to-root ratio; final rinse water must be clear; remove all sand and grit.
  • Chip uniformity: Cut chips 2â5 cm thick for even drying; avoid mashed or crushed pieces that invite mould.
  • Grating consistency: Achieve 0.5â2 mm particle size; check for uncooked chunks that reduce product quality.
  • Dewatering target: Reduce moisture to 45â50%; cake should be firm with no free water when squeezed.
  • Drying endpoint: Dry to <12% moisture; chips should snap cleanly, not bend; test with moisture meter if available.
  • Hygiene and safety: Wash equipment between batches; use machine guards; never touch moving parts; store dried product in airtight, food-grade containers.
