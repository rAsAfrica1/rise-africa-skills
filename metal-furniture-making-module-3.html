1. Introduction and Why It Matters

When you build metal furniture, the steel you choose decides everything: how strong it is, how much it weighs, how easy it is to weld, and how much it costs. In Africa, we often work with square or rectangular tubes and angle iron because they are strong, readily available, and affordable. But picking the wrong size or thickness can make a chair that wobbles, a table that sags, or a frame that cracks after a few months. This lesson will show you why tube and angle selection is the foundation of good metal furniture.

Tube is hollow and comes in square, rectangular, or round shapes. Angle iron is L-shaped. Both are measured by their outside dimensions and wall thickness. For example, a square tube might be 25mm x 25mm with a 1.5mm wall. A common angle iron is 25mm x 25mm x 3mm. The thickness (gauge) matters more than the outside size for strength. A 25mm tube with 2mm wall is much stronger than the same size with 1mm wall. In many African markets, you'll find tubes in 6-meter lengths, and you can buy them cut to size. Knowing what's available locally saves time and money.

Why does this matter? Because furniture must hold weight without bending. A dining chair needs to support at least 150kg. A workbench might need to hold 300kg. If you use thin tube, it will dent or buckle. If you use very thick tube, it becomes too heavy and expensive. The goal is to match the steel to the job. For example, a simple stool can use 20mm square tube with 1.2mm wall. A heavy-duty table might need 40mm x 40mm tube with 2mm wall. Angle iron is great for frames that need flat surfaces to bolt onto, like a bed frame.

In this module, you'll learn how to identify different types of tube and angle, what tools you need to cut and join them, and how to choose the right size for common furniture projects. We'll also cover common mistakes like using too thin a wall or mixing sizes, and safety practices to avoid injuries. By the end, you'll be able to walk into a steel shop and confidently select the right material for your next piece of furniture.

2. Tools and Materials

To work with tube and angle iron, you need a few basic tools. First, a measuring tape (5m is fine) and a steel square or try square for marking accurate 90-degree cuts. A scriber or permanent marker is used to mark the steel. For cutting, you can use a hacksaw with a 24-tooth blade for thin walls, or an angle grinder with a cutting disc for faster work. A metal file is needed to deburr edges after cutting. For welding, you'll need a welding machine (stick or MIG), electrodes, a welding mask, gloves, and a chipping hammer. A bench vise is very helpful to hold pieces while cutting or welding. Finally, a wire brush for cleaning welds.

Materials: You'll need tube or angle iron, of course. Common sizes in Africa: square tube 20x20mm, 25x25mm, 30x30mm, 40x40mm, with wall thickness from 1.2mm to 2mm. Rectangular tube like 20x40mm or 25x50mm is also common. Angle iron typically comes in 20x20x3mm, 25x25x3mm, 30x30x3mm, and 40x40x4mm. You can buy these in 6-meter lengths from steel merchants. Also, you'll need welding rods (e.g., 2.5mm or 3.2mm E6013), cutting discs, grinding discs, and maybe primer and paint for finishing.

When buying, check for rust, straightness, and wall thickness. A quick way to check thickness: use a caliper or compare with a known sample. Thin walls (1mm) are easy to cut but can burn through when welding. Thicker walls (2mm and above) are stronger but require more welding skill. For beginners, 1.5mm to 2mm wall is a good compromise. Also, consider the weight: a 6m length of 25x25x1.5mm tube weighs about 6.5kg, while 40x40x2mm weighs about 14kg. Heavier means more expensive but stronger.

Always buy a little extra material (10% more) for mistakes and cutting waste. Store your steel indoors or under cover to prevent rust. If you're using recycled steel, clean it well with a wire brush and check for cracks. With these tools and materials, you're ready to start cutting and joining.

3. Step by Step Method

Step 1: Plan your project. Decide what furniture you're making (e.g., a stool, table, or bed). Draw a simple sketch with dimensions. For a stool, you might need four legs of 45cm each, and four crossbars. Calculate the total length of tube needed. For example, a stool with 25x25mm tube: legs (4 x 45cm = 180cm), top frame (2 x 30cm + 2 x 30cm = 120cm), and lower crossbars (2 x 30cm + 2 x 30cm = 120cm). Total = 420cm, so buy a 6m length (600cm) to have extra.

Step 2: Select your tube or angle. For a stool, 25x25x1.5mm square tube is good. For a heavy table, use 40x40x2mm. If using angle iron, choose 25x25x3mm for light frames, 40x40x4mm for heavy. Make sure the material is straight and free of heavy rust. If rusty, clean with a wire brush or sandpaper.

Step 3: Measure and mark. Use a measuring tape and try square to mark cut lines. For square tube, mark all around using a square to ensure a straight cut. For angle iron, mark on both flanges. Use a scriber or marker. Double-check measurements before cutting.

Step 4: Cut the steel. Clamp the piece in a vise. Use a hacksaw or angle grinder to cut along the mark. For hacksaw, use slow, steady strokes. For grinder, wear safety glasses and face shield. After cutting, use a file to remove burrs. Check that cut ends are square; if not, file them flat.

Step 5: Fit and weld. Lay out the pieces on a flat surface (e.g., a welding table or concrete floor). Use clamps or magnets to hold them in place. Check for squareness by measuring diagonals. Tack weld first (small welds at corners), then check again. If square, weld all joints fully. For thin tube (1.5mm), use low amperage (e.g., 60-70A) and 2.5mm electrodes to avoid burn-through. For thicker, increase amperage. Clean welds with a wire brush.

Step 6: Finish. Grind welds smooth if desired. Apply primer and paint to prevent rust. Let dry. Your furniture is ready.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Using too thin a wall. Many beginners buy 1mm wall tube because it's cheaper and lighter. But thin walls dent easily and burn through when welding. Fix: For any furniture that bears weight, use at least 1.5mm wall. For heavy-duty, use 2mm. If you already bought thin tube, reduce welding amperage and use short welds, or reinforce with angle iron.

Mistake 2: Mixing different sizes or thicknesses. Using 25mm tube for legs and 20mm for crossbars can cause alignment problems and weak joints. Fix: Stick to one size for the main frame. If you must mix, ensure the smaller tube fits inside the larger for telescoping joints, or use connector plates.

Mistake 3: Not cutting square. If cuts are not at 90 degrees, the frame will be twisted. Fix: Always use a try square to mark and check cuts. After cutting, file the end flat and check with a square. If a cut is off, recut or file to correct.

Mistake 4: Welding too hot on thin tube. This burns holes. Fix: Practice on scrap pieces. Set your welder to the lowest setting that still strikes an arc. Use 2.5mm electrodes and move quickly. If you burn a hole, fill it by welding a small patch or using a copper backing plate.

Mistake 5: Not cleaning before welding. Rust, paint, or oil causes porous, weak welds. Fix: Clean the joint area with a wire brush or grinder until shiny metal is exposed. Wipe with acetone if available.

Mistake 6: Ignoring rust prevention. Bare steel rusts quickly, especially in humid African climates. Fix: Always apply primer and paint, or use galvanized tube (but note that galvanized fumes are toxicâweld in a well-ventilated area and wear a respirator).

Mistake 7: Overloading furniture. Just because it's metal doesn't mean it's indestructible. A stool made from 20mm tube may not hold 200kg. Fix: Calculate the load. For a chair, use at least 25mm tube with 1.5mm wall. For a table, 40mm tube with 2mm wall. When in doubt, go thicker.

By avoiding these mistakes, your furniture will be strong, safe, and long-lasting.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety is not optional when working with metal. You'll be cutting, grinding, and welding, which produce sparks, sharp edges, and toxic fumes. Always wear personal protective equipment (PPE): safety glasses or goggles, a face shield for grinding, leather gloves, a welding mask, and steel-toe boots. Wear long sleeves and trousers made of cotton or leatherâno synthetic clothing that can melt. Tie back long hair and avoid loose clothing.

Ventilation: Welding produces harmful fumes, especially with galvanized steel (zinc fumes cause metal fume fever). Work outdoors or in a well-ventilated area. If indoors, use a fan to blow fumes away from your breathing zone. Never weld in a closed room.

Fire safety: Keep a fire extinguisher or a bucket of sand nearby. Sparks can ignite flammable materials. Clear your work area of paper, rags, and solvents. Don't weld near fuel or gas cylinders.

Tool safety: Before using an angle grinder, check the disc for cracks and ensure the guard is in place. Never remove the guard. Use two hands and let the tool reach full speed before cutting. For hacksaws, ensure the blade is tensioned and teeth point forward. Clamp work securelyânever hold with one hand while cutting.

Electrical safety: Check welding cables for damage. Don't weld in wet conditions. Use a residual current device (RCD) if possible. Ground the welder properly.

Best practices: Plan your cuts to minimize waste. Store steel indoors to prevent rust. Keep your work area tidy. When lifting heavy steel, bend your knees, not your back. Use a trolley or ask for help. After welding, let the metal cool before touching. Chip slag with a chipping hammer and brush welds. Finally, always double-check your measurements and fit before weldingâmeasure twice, cut once. Following these practices will keep you safe and your work professional.

6. Practical Exercise

Now it's time to apply what you've learned. In this exercise, you will build a simple but sturdy stool using square tube. This project will test your ability to select the right tube, cut accurately, and weld clean joints. Follow the steps carefully.

Materials needed: 2.5 meters of 25mm x 25mm x 1.5mm square tube. You can buy a 6m length and cut it, or ask the supplier to cut it into: 4 pieces of 45cm (legs), 4 pieces of 30cm (top frame), and 4 pieces of 25cm (lower crossbars). Total length: 4x45 + 4x30 + 4x25 = 180 + 120 + 100 = 400cm, so 2.5m is not enoughâactually 4m needed. Wait, recalc: 4 legs of 45cm = 180cm; top frame: two long pieces of 30cm and two short pieces of 30cm? For a square top, all four sides are equal if the stool is square. Let's make a square stool: top frame sides = 30cm each, so 4 pieces of 30cm = 120cm; lower crossbars: also 4 pieces of 30cm? That would be 120cm. Total = 180+120+120 = 420cm. So you need 4.2m. Buy a 6m length to have extra. So materials: 6m of 25x25x1.5mm square tube. Tools: measuring tape, try square, scriber, hacksaw or angle grinder, file, welding machine with 2.5mm electrodes, clamps, welding mask, gloves, wire brush, primer and paint.

Step 1: Cut all pieces. Measure and mark: 4 legs at 45cm, 8 pieces at 30cm (4 for top frame, 4 for lower crossbars). Cut with hacksaw or grinder. File ends flat.

Step 2: Build the top frame. Lay two 30cm pieces parallel, and two more 30cm pieces perpendicular to form a square. Ensure corners are 90 degrees using a try square. Tack weld corners. Check diagonalsâthey should be equal. If not, adjust and re-tack. Then weld all corners fully.

Step 3: Attach legs. Place the top frame on a flat surface. Position each leg at a corner, flush with the outside of the frame. Use a square to ensure legs are perpendicular to the frame. Tack weld. Check that all legs are vertical and the stool doesn't wobble. Adjust if needed. Weld legs fully.

Step 4: Add lower crossbars. Measure 15cm up from the bottom of each leg. Mark all legs. Cut four 30cm pieces for crossbars (or use remaining 30cm pieces). Weld them between legs at the 15cm mark, forming a square. Ensure they are level. Weld fully.

Step 5: Clean and finish. Grind welds smooth if desired. Brush with wire brush. Apply primer and paint. Let dry.

Step 6: Test. Sit on the stool. It should support your weight without wobbling. If it wobbles, check leg lengths and welds. Congratulationsâyou've built your first metal stool!

Metal furniture in Africa is a cash business. Customers pay for durability, and fabricators who over-spec waste money on steel; those who under-spec lose money on warranty claims, rework, and reputation. This lesson treats tube and angle selection as a profitability decision, not just a technical one.

Understanding the Two Main Families

Square and rectangular hollow sections (tubes) are the backbone of modern metal furniture: chair frames, table legs, bed frames, shelving uprights. They are strong in bending, easy to weld, and give a clean, modern finish that customers associate with quality.

Angle sections (equal and unequal angles) are L-shaped and are used for frames, corner brackets, base rails, and reinforcement. They are cheaper per metre than equivalent tubes and are excellent where you need a flat face for bolting, drilling, or fixing timber and board surfaces.

In most African markets, tubes and angles are sold by length (commonly 6 m) and priced per length or per kilogram. Knowing both the standard sizes and the mass per metre lets you quote jobs accurately and control waste.

Galvanised or painted tube and angle can release toxic zinc fumes when welded or cut with a grinder. Always work in a well-ventilated area or outdoors, wear a P2/P3 respirator, and never weld galvanised steel without removing the coating first. Keep a fire extinguisher within reach â sparks from cutting metal furniture frames are a leading cause of workshop fires in African cities. Wear eye protection, gloves, and steel-toe boots at all times.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Confirm the load: Identify whether the furniture is light (home), medium (office), or heavy (school, restaurant, industrial) before choosing a section.
  2. Match section to span: Longer unsupported spans need deeper or thicker sections; short spans can use lighter tube.
  3. Check wall thickness: 1.6 mm is minimum for furniture; 2.0â3.0 mm for heavy-duty and commercial use.
  4. Calculate steel weight: Use kg/m from Table 1 to estimate material cost before quoting the customer.
  5. Buy standard 6 m lengths: Plan cuts to minimise offcuts; offcuts are lost profit.
  6. Inspect for defects: Reject tubes with dents, rust-through, or uneven wall thickness â they fail at the weld.
  7. Use angle for flat faces: Choose angle where you need to bolt, drill, or fix timber and board.
  8. Record your choice: Keep a job sheet of section sizes used per product so you can repeat profitable builds.
