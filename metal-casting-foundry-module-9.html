Lessons

1. Introduction and Why It Matters

After you pour molten metal into a mould and it cools, what you pull out is a rough casting. It has sand stuck to it, rough edges, maybe some extra metal where the gates were, and dimensions that are close but not exact. Finishing and machining turn that rough casting into a usable product that fits, works, and lasts. In Africa, where many metal parts are made in small workshops, this step is often the difference between a part that works and one that fails. For example, a pump housing cast in a local foundry must have its bore machined to within 0.1 mm so the bearing fits tightly. If not, the pump will vibrate and leak. Similarly, a grinding mill hammer needs a smooth finish to avoid cracking. Finishing includes cleaning, grinding, sanding, and sometimes polishing. Machining involves cutting metal with lathes, milling machines, or drills to achieve precise dimensions and smooth surfaces. In many African countries, replacement parts for tractors, water pumps, and food processing equipment are expensive or unavailable. Local foundries can cast these parts, but they must be finished properly to work. This module teaches you how to take a raw casting and turn it into a finished part using hand tools, power tools, and basic machine tools. You will learn to remove sand, cut off gates and risers, grind smooth, drill holes, and even turn on a lathe. The skills are practical and can be done in a small workshop with limited electricity. By the end, you will be able to take a casting from your foundry and make it ready for use. This matters because it adds value to your product, reduces waste, and creates opportunities to sell to industries that need reliable parts. Whether you are making spare parts for a village or supplying a factory, finishing and machining are essential steps to get paid.

2. Tools and Materials

To finish and machine castings, you need a set of basic tools and materials. Start with safety gear: leather gloves, safety glasses, a dust mask (for sand and metal dust), and ear protection when using power tools. For cleaning sand off castings, you need a wire brush (hand or wheel), a chipping hammer, and a file. A stiff bristle brush helps for loose sand. For cutting off gates and risers, you can use a hacksaw with a 24-tooth blade for small parts, or an angle grinder with a cutting disc for larger ones. A cold chisel and hammer also work for brittle cast iron. For grinding and smoothing, an angle grinder with a grinding disc (for rough work) and a flap disc (for finishing) is essential. If you have electricity, a bench grinder is useful for sharpening tools and deburring. For machining, a basic lathe is the most versatile tool. A small 1-meter bed lathe with a 3-jaw chuck can turn diameters up to 200 mm. You also need a drill press or hand drill with HSS drill bits (high-speed steel) for drilling holes. For measuring, a vernier caliper (0-150 mm) and a micrometer (0-25 mm) are critical. A steel rule and try square help with layout. For materials, you need cutting oil (or used engine oil) for drilling and turning, emery cloth in grits 80, 120, 220 for polishing, and possibly a surface plate and marking blue for precision layout. In Africa, you can find many of these tools in local markets or from hardware suppliers. If you cannot afford a lathe, you can often rent time at a local workshop or use a friend's machine. Start with hand tools and add power tools as your work grows. Always keep tools sharp and clean; a dull file or grinder disc wastes time and gives poor results.

3. Step by Step Method

Follow these steps to finish and machine a typical casting, such as a pulley or a flange. First, remove the sand. Use a wire brush and chipping hammer to knock off loose sand and scale. For stubborn sand, soak the casting in water for an hour, then brush again. Second, cut off gates and risers. Use a hacksaw or angle grinder to cut them close to the casting, leaving about 2 mm for grinding. For cast iron, score a line with a chisel and hit it with a hammer to break off large gates. Third, grind the stubs flush. Use an angle grinder with a grinding disc. Hold the grinder at a 15-degree angle and move it back and forth. Do not overheat the metal; if it turns blue, cool it with water. Fourth, file and sand. Use a coarse file (bastard cut) to remove grinder marks, then emery cloth (80 grit, then 120) to smooth. For a polished finish, use 220 grit and then buff with a cloth wheel and polishing compound. Fifth, layout and mark. Use a steel rule, try square, and marking blue (or a felt-tip marker) to mark where holes or cuts go. Center punch the marks. Sixth, drill holes. Use a drill press or hand drill with the correct size HSS bit. Start with a pilot hole (e.g., 5 mm) then drill to final size. Use cutting oil and clear chips often. Seventh, machine on a lathe if needed. For a pulley, chuck the casting and face the side, then turn the outer diameter to size. Use a sharp HSS tool bit, set speed to about 300 rpm for cast iron, and feed slowly. Measure with a caliper as you go. Eighth, deburr and final check. Remove sharp edges with a file or countersink. Check dimensions against your drawing. If within tolerance (e.g., 0.2 mm), your part is done. Clean off oil and metal chips with a rag. This method works for most small castings and can be done in a basic workshop.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Even experienced foundry workers make mistakes in finishing and machining. Here are common ones and how to fix them. Mistake 1: Not removing all sand before machining. Sand is abrasive and will dull your cutting tools quickly. Fix: Clean the casting thoroughly with a wire brush and inspect for embedded sand. If sand is baked on, use a grinder to remove it. Mistake 2: Cutting off gates too close to the casting. This can cause a crack or gouge. Fix: Leave 2-3 mm and grind down. If you already cut too close, weld the gouge (if weldable) or grind a smooth radius. Mistake 3: Overheating the metal while grinding. This can cause warping or cracking, especially in cast iron. Fix: Grind in short bursts, dip the part in water to cool, and do not hold the grinder in one spot. Mistake 4: Drilling without a pilot hole. This causes the drill bit to wander and break. Fix: Always center punch and drill a small pilot hole first (5 mm for a 10 mm hole). Mistake 5: Using the wrong speed on the lathe. Too fast for cast iron (above 500 rpm) will burn the tool and give a poor finish. Fix: Use 200-400 rpm for cast iron, 400-600 for aluminium, and use cutting oil. Mistake 6: Not measuring often enough. You may remove too much metal. Fix: Measure with a caliper after every pass. Leave 0.5 mm for final finishing. Mistake 7: Ignoring safety. Metal dust and sparks can hurt you. Fix: Always wear safety glasses, gloves, and a dust mask. Keep the work area clean. Mistake 8: Using a dull file or grinding disc. This makes the work harder and less accurate. Fix: Replace dull tools. A sharp file cuts faster and smoother. Mistake 9: Not deburring. Sharp edges can cut hands and cause parts to fit poorly. Fix: Always run a file or countersink along edges. Mistake 10: Skipping the final check. You may deliver a part that is out of tolerance. Fix: Use a caliper and compare to your drawing. If it is wrong, you may need to re-cast or weld and re-machine. Learn from each mistake and keep a notebook of what went wrong and how you fixed it.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety is not optional in finishing and machining. Metal dust, sparks, sharp edges, and moving machinery can cause serious injury. Always wear personal protective equipment (PPE): safety glasses (not just sunglasses), a dust mask (N95 or better) when grinding or sanding, leather gloves when handling rough castings, and earplugs or earmuffs when using power tools. Wear tight-fitting clothes and tie back long hair. Never wear loose sleeves or jewellery near a lathe or drill press; they can get caught and pull you in. Before you start, check your tools. Make sure grinder discs are not cracked, drill bits are sharp, and the lathe chuck is tight. Use a guard on the grinder and never remove it. When using a lathe, never leave the key in the chuck. Always remove it before turning on the machine. Keep your hands away from rotating parts. Use a brush, not your fingers, to clear chips. For grinding, hold the work securely. Use a vice or clamp. Never grind on the side of a grinding wheel. Let the tool do the work; do not force it. For drilling, clamp the work to the drill press table. Use cutting oil to keep the bit cool. If a drill bit grabs, release the pressure and slow down. For hand tools, keep files and chisels sharp. A dull tool requires more force and can slip. Store tools safely. Keep your work area clean and well-lit. Metal chips and sand on the floor can cause slips. Best practice includes planning your work. Study the drawing, decide the sequence, and gather all tools before starting. Measure twice, cut once. Keep a tolerance of 0.2 mm for most parts unless specified. If you are unsure, ask an experienced machinist. In Africa, many workshops have limited space and power. Use a generator or solar if needed, but never run power tools from unsafe wiring. Learn first aid for cuts and burns. Keep a first aid kit nearby. Finally, respect the metal. It can be hot after grinding or cutting. Use tongs or gloves. By following these rules, you will work safely and produce quality parts that customers trust.

6. Practical Exercise

In this exercise, you will finish and machine a simple cast iron flange. You need a rough casting of a flange (about 100 mm diameter, 20 mm thick, with a 30 mm center hole cast in), an angle grinder with grinding and cutting discs, a file, emery cloth (80 and 120 grit), a drill press with a 10 mm HSS bit, a vernier caliper, a steel rule, a center punch, a hammer, and safety gear. Step 1: Clean the casting. Use a wire brush and chipping hammer to remove all sand. If there are gates or risers, cut them off with the angle grinder, leaving 2 mm. Step 2: Grind the stubs and rough edges flush. Use the grinding disc. Keep the flange cool by dipping in water. Step 3: File and sand the flat faces. Use a coarse file to remove grinder marks, then emery cloth (80 then 120) to smooth. Step 4: Layout the bolt holes. The flange should have four 10 mm holes on a 70 mm bolt circle (35 mm radius from center). Use a steel rule and caliper to mark the positions. Center punch each mark. Step 5: Drill the holes. Use a drill press with a 10 mm bit. Clamp the flange to the table. Use cutting oil. Drill through, clearing chips often. Step 6: Deburr the holes with a larger drill bit or countersink. Step 7: Check dimensions. The center hole should be 30 mm; measure with caliper. The bolt circle should be 70 mm. The thickness should be 20 mm. If any dimension is off by more than 0.5 mm, note it. Step 8: Clean the flange with a rag. Apply a light oil to prevent rust. Step 9: Write a short report: what went well, what was difficult, and what you would do differently. This exercise gives you practice in cleaning, grinding, layout, drilling, and measuring. It uses only basic tools and can be done in a small workshop. Repeat with other castings to build speed and accuracy. When you can consistently hold 0.2 mm, you are ready for more complex machining like turning on a lathe.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Finishing and Machining Cast Metal Products

Finishing and machining is where a rough casting becomes a sellable product. In African foundries from the informal workshops of Suame Magazine in Kumasi to the growing light-industrial clusters around Nairobi, Lagos, and Johannesburg the difference between a casting that earns a premium price and one that is rejected often comes down to how well it is cleaned, dressed, and machined. This lesson is the working reference you keep at the bench: the tools, the tolerances, the defects, and the numbers that matter when you are quoting a job or delivering to a customer.

Treat this as your shop-floor companion to the earlier lessons. It consolidates the practical data a small or medium foundry needs to finish castings consistently, price them profitably, and avoid the rework that destroys margin. Every table and figure here is drawn from published technical sources you can cite to customers, trainers, or lenders.

Why Finishing Determines Your Price

A raw casting carries the cost of metal, fuel, labour, and mould. Finishing adds value but also adds cost. The business discipline is to know which finishing operations a given product actually needs and which are waste. A grinding operation that removes 2 mm of flash on a decorative bracket is money; the same operation on a machined bearing housing is preparation for a tolerance that the customer will pay for. Your quotation should separate cleaning (removing sand, gates, flash), dressing (cosmetic and dimensional correction), and machining (cutting to tolerance). Customers in construction, agriculture, and automotive aftermarket pay for the third category and merely expect the first two.

Step-by-Step Method

  1. Fettling and shakeout. Remove moulding sand, cores, and loose material. Knock off the bulk of gates and risers with a hammer or cut-off wheel, leaving 23 mm to grind.
  2. Gate and riser removal. Cut with a cut-off wheel or band saw. On cast iron, avoid excessive heat input that can crack the casting; on aluminium, use a softer wheel and lower speed.
  3. Grinding and blending. Grind the stubs flush, blend into the parent surface, and remove flash at the parting line. Do not over-grind removing more than 12 mm can breach the casting skin and expose porosity.
  4. Cleaning. Wire-brush or shot-blast to remove scale, sand, and rust. For aluminium, use a dedicated stainless brush to avoid iron contamination.
  5. Inspection before machining. Check for blowholes, sand inclusions, cold shuts, and cracks. Mark defects and decide: repair, scrap, or downgrade to a non-critical product.
  6. Marking out. Use a surface plate, height gauge, or scribe to mark datums and machining allowances. Confirm the casting has enough stock before cutting.
  7. Machining. Set up on the lathe, mill, or drill. Take a light first cut to establish the skin, then proceed to the drawing tolerance. Use coolant appropriate to the material.
  8. Deburring and final inspection. Break all sharp edges, remove chips, and measure against the drawing. Record results on a simple inspection sheet.
  9. Surface protection. Apply primer, oil, or paint to prevent corrosion during storage and transport especially important in humid coastal markets.

SAFETY WARNING Grinding and Cutting

Angle grinders and cut-off wheels cause severe injuries when used without guards, at wrong RPM, or on the wrong material. Always wear a full face shield, safety glasses, hearing protection, and leather gloves. Never remove the guard. Never use a cut-off wheel for side grinding. Check the wheel for cracks before mounting and run it at no-load for 30 seconds. Keep bystanders clear. In African workshops where ventilation is often poor, also wear a P2/N95 dust mask when grinding cast iron or cleaning sand respirable silica causes silicosis, an irreversible lung disease. Secure the workpiece; a casting that moves can throw the wheel and shatter it.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Confirm the casting is cool and fully shaken out before fettling.
  • Inspect for visible defects (blowholes, cracks, sand) before spending time on machining.
  • Leave 23 mm of stock when cutting gates and risers; never cut flush.
  • Match wheel and tooling to the material cast iron, aluminium, and steel behave differently.
  • Check the drawing and stock allowance before the first machining cut.
  • Measure with a calibrated caliper or micrometer; record results.
  • Break all sharp edges and deburr holes and threads.
  • Apply protective coating before storage or dispatch.
  • Wear full PPE face shield, gloves, hearing protection, dust mask.

Business Takeaway

Finishing and machining is where your foundry earns its reputation and its margin. A disciplined sequence fettle, cut, grind, inspect, machine, protect reduces scrap, shortens lead time, and lets you quote confidently. For African foundries competing with imports, the winning formula is not the cheapest casting but the casting that arrives clean, dimensionally correct, and ready to use. Keep this reference at the bench, train your team on the checklist, and treat every rejected part as a process lesson rather than a loss.
