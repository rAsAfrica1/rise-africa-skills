Lessons

1. History and Evolution of Robotics

Robotics didn't start with shiny humanoids. In Africa, the roots go back to ancient automata and traditional craft. For example, the Egyptian water clock by Ctesibius around 270 BC used mechanical figures. But modern robotics began in the 1950s with Unimate, the first industrial robot, installed at General Motors in 1961. It weighed 2 tons and cost $25,000. In Africa, the first robots arrived in the 1980s in South African gold mines for drilling and in Egyptian textile factories for packaging. Today, countries like Nigeria, Kenya, and Rwanda are building local robotics startups. For instance, in 2020, a team in Nairobi built a robot to sort recyclable plastics using Arduino and servo motors. To understand this evolution, start by tracing key milestones: 1961 Unimate, 1970s Stanford Arm, 1990s mobile robots like Sojourner on Mars, and 2010s collaborative robots (cobots). In your workshop, create a timeline on a wall using cardboard and markers. Mark five events: first industrial robot, first mobile robot, first humanoid (ASIMO in 2000), first African robotics competition (2015 in South Africa), and first locally made robot in your country. Then, disassemble a simple toy robot or a broken DVD player to see how gears and motors work. This hands-on look shows that robotics is not magicit's engineering that evolves step by step. Document your findings in a notebook: note the year, the inventor, and the country. This history lesson gives you context for why robots look and work the way they do today.

2. Types of Robots: Industrial, Mobile, Humanoid

Robots come in three main types, each suited to different jobs. Industrial robots are fixed in place and do repetitive tasks with high precision. In South Africa's automotive plants, KUKA robots weld car frames 24/7, lifting 200 kg payloads with 0.1 mm accuracy. They cost between $20,000 and $100,000. Mobile robots move aroundlike drones, autonomous guided vehicles (AGVs), and rovers. In Kenya, Zipline drones deliver blood to rural clinics, covering 80 km in 30 minutes. AGVs in Moroccan warehouses move pallets using line-following sensors. Humanoid robots look like humans and are the hardest to build. They walk on two legs, use arms with multiple joints, and interact with people. Examples include Hanson Robotics' Sophia and Honda's ASIMO. In Africa, humanoid research is still emerging; a lab at the University of Cape Town built a small walking robot in 2019 using 12 servo motors and an Arduino Mega. To explore these types, build a simple mobile robot first: use a plastic chassis, two DC motors with wheels, an L298N motor driver, and an ultrasonic sensor. Program it to avoid obstacles. Then, convert it into a stationary industrial arm by replacing wheels with a gripper and a base. For humanoid, start with a bipedal walker kit. Compare their degrees of freedom (DOF): industrial 6 DOF, mobile 2-3 DOF, humanoid 20+ DOF. Choose the type based on your task: welding? industrial. Delivery? mobile. Customer service? humanoid. In your community, identify one task that a robot could dolike sorting maize or fetching waterand decide which type fits best. Write a one-page proposal with cost estimates.

3. Key Components of a Robot: Sensors, Actuators, Controllers

Every robot has three core parts: sensors, actuators, and a controller. Sensors are the robot's eyes and ears. Common types include ultrasonic (HC-SR04, $2) for distance, infrared (Sharp GP2Y0A21, $10) for proximity, and temperature (LM35, $1) for heat. In African farms, soil moisture sensors (capacitive, $5) help robots decide when to water. Actuators are the musclesthey create motion. DC motors ($3) spin wheels, servo motors (SG90, $3) rotate arms to precise angles, and stepper motors (NEMA 17, $15) move 3D printer beds. In Ghana, a startup uses 12V DC motors to drive a solar-powered weeding robot. The controller is the brain. Popular choices: Arduino Uno ($25), Raspberry Pi 4 ($35), and ESP32 ($10). Arduino is best for beginners because it's cheap and has many tutorials. To see these parts in action, build a line-following robot. You need: 2 IR sensors, 2 DC motors with wheels, an L298N driver, an Arduino Uno, and a 9V battery. Wire the sensors to analog pins A0 and A1, motors to digital pins 5,6,9,10. Write code: if left sensor sees black, turn left; if right sees black, turn right; else go straight. Test on a black tape line. If the robot veers off, adjust sensor sensitivity with a potentiometer. For a challenge, add an ultrasonic sensor to stop when an obstacle is 10 cm away. Document each component's voltage and current: motors draw 200 mA each, sensors 15 mA, Arduino 50 mA. Total current must not exceed your battery's capacity. This hands-on project teaches you to identify and connect real parts.

4. Robot Applications in Industry and Society

Robots are already working across Africa. In South African mines, automated drills and loaders operate in dangerous tunnels, reducing human exposure to rockfalls. In Nigerian oil refineries, robots inspect pipelines for leaks, saving $1 million per incident. In Kenyan agriculture, a robot called "Shamba" uses a camera and machine learning to identify and pick ripe tomatoes, increasing yield by 20%. In healthcare, Rwandan hospitals use Zipline drones to deliver 150,000 blood units per year, cutting delivery time from 4 hours to 30 minutes. In education, Ghanaian schools use LEGO Mindstorms kits to teach coding. In society, robots assist people with disabilities: a Moroccan startup built a wheelchair with ultrasonic sensors that avoids obstacles and climbs curbs. In waste management, a South African robot sorts plastic, glass, and metal at 2 tons per hour. To apply robotics in your community, follow these steps: 1) Identify a probleme.g., water fetching, crop monitoring, or security. 2) Research existing solutions and costs. 3) Build a prototype with cheap parts. For example, a solar-powered water pump robot can be made with a 12V pump, a soil moisture sensor, and an Arduino. 4) Test it for one week. 5) Calculate return on investment: if the robot costs $200 and saves 10 hours of labor per week at $2/hour, payback is 10 weeks. Document your results. Many African governments now offer grants for agri-robotics. In Kenya, the Kenya Industrial Research and Development Institute (KIRDI) funds prototypes up to $5,000. Write a proposal with a clear problem, solution, budget, and timeline. This lesson shows that robotics is not just for factoriesit solves real African challenges.

5. Safety Standards and Ethical Considerations

Robots can hurt people if not designed safely. In 2015, a worker at a Volkswagen plant in Germany died when a robot crushed him against a metal plate. That's why safety standards exist. ISO 10218-1 and ISO 10218-2 cover industrial robot safety. They require emergency stop buttons, safety fences, and light curtains. In Africa, South Africa adopted SANS 10218, and Nigeria uses NIS 10218. For mobile robots, ISO 13482 covers personal care robots. Ethical issues include job displacement, privacy, and bias. In South Africa, mining robots replaced 5,000 jobs between 2010 and 2020, but created 1,200 new technical roles. In Kenya, drones used for delivery must respect privacyno flying over private homes without consent. To build safe robots, follow these rules: 1) Always include a physical emergency stop that cuts power to motors. 2) Use low voltage (12V or less) for beginner projects. 3) Add bump sensors or ultrasonic sensors to detect humans. 4) Test in an open area away from people. 5) Label all wires and moving parts. For ethical design, ask: Who benefits? Who could be harmed? For example, a robot that sorts job applications might inherit human bias if trained on biased data. In your workshop, practice a safety drill: build a simple robot with a motor, then simulate a failure by disconnecting a sensor. Observe what happens. Then, add a kill switch. Write a one-page safety checklist for your robot, including: battery type, max speed, emergency stop location, and operating zone. Share it with your team. Remember, a safe robot is a useful robot.

6. Lab: Disassembling and Identifying Robot Parts

In this lab, you will take apart a broken robot or electronic toy and identify its components. You need: a broken RC car, toy robot, or old printer; a small Phillips screwdriver; a multimeter; a notebook; and safety goggles. Work on a clean, well-lit table. Start by removing batteries. Then, unscrew the casing. Take photos at each step. Inside, you'll find: 1) DC motorssmall cylindrical motors with two wires. Test with a multimeter: set to continuity, touch probes to wires; if it beeps, the motor is good. 2) Gearsplastic or metal wheels that transfer motion. Count the teeth: a 10-tooth gear driving a 50-tooth gear gives 5:1 reduction, increasing torque. 3) Circuit boardlook for the microcontroller (a black chip), resistors (color bands), capacitors (cylinders), and connectors. 4) Sensorsin an RC car, you might find an infrared receiver or a bump switch. 5) Battery holdernote the voltage (usually 3V to 9V). 6) Wirestrace them from motors to the board. Draw a schematic in your notebook. For example, a typical RC car has: battery (+) switch board motor driver two motors. After disassembly, clean each part with a dry brush. Test motors with a 1.5V AA battery: touch wires to battery terminals; the motor should spin. If not, it's dead. Sort parts into labeled containers: motors, gears, sensors, boards, screws. Now, reassemble the robot in a new way: add a second motor to make a simple car. Or use the board to control an LED. Document what you learned: list three parts and their functions. This lab builds your confidence to repair and innovate. Remember, many great robots start from salvaged parts. Take clear notesyou'll need them for Module 2.

<h2>Practical Reference: Robot Components, Sourcing, and Field Reality in African Agribusiness</h2>

<p>This practical reference is built for the rise AFRICA Robotics learner who will one day specify, buy, install, maintain, or repair robots on a farm, in a warehouse, on a mine site, or in a light manufacturing plant. The goal is business-first: every component discussion ends with a procurement, cost, or uptime implication. Africa's robotics market is small but growing fast, and the winners will be the operators who understand what is inside the machine, what it costs to keep it running, and where spare parts actually come from.</p>

<h3>1. Anatomy of a Working Robot</h3>

<p>A robot is a system, not a gadget. Five subsystems must all function for value to be delivered: the <strong>controller</strong> (the brain), the <strong>sensors</strong> (the senses), the <strong>actuators</strong> (the muscles), the <strong>power system</strong> (the heart), and the <strong>end effector</strong> (the hands). In agribusiness, the end effector is often the most context-specific part a gripper for tomatoes is not a gripper for maize cobs, and neither is a welding torch.</p>

<h3>4. Business-First Component Selection</h3>

<p>When you specify a robot for an African operation, work backwards from the business case. Ask: what is the cost of one hour of downtime? If a tomato-packing line loses USD 400 per hour when stopped, then a USD 900 LiDAR with a 6-week lead time is a liability unless you stock a spare. If a maize field sprayer saves USD 30 per hectare, then a USD 12,000 drone must fly at least 400 hectares to break even on capital alone.</p>

<p>Three practical rules for African deployments:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Buy for the environment, not the datasheet.</strong> Dust, humidity, heat, and voltage fluctuations kill more robots in Africa than any software bug. Specify IP65 or better for outdoor work.</li>
<li><strong>Stock critical spares locally.</strong> A 6-week lead time on a servo motor is a 6-week shutdown. Keep at least one spare of every failure-prone part on site.</li>
<li><strong>Train two people, not one.</strong> The single biggest cause of abandoned robotics projects in Africa is the departure of the one person who knew how to run it.</li>
</ul>

<p style="margin:0;font-family:Arial;font-size:14px;color:#7f1d1d;"><strong> SAFETY WARNING:</strong> Never disassemble a robot especially an industrial arm, mobile platform, or battery-powered unit without first isolating all energy sources. Lithium battery packs can deliver lethal current even when "switched off," and industrial arms can store mechanical energy in springs and counterweights. In the lab, always work in pairs, use insulated tools, wear safety glasses, and follow your institution's lock-out/tag-out (LOTO) procedure. In African field conditions, also guard against unstable power: unplug from mains before opening any enclosure, and never assume a generator-fed circuit is safe.</p>

<h3>5. The Lab: Disassembling and Identifying Robot Parts</h3>

<p>Your lab task is to take apart a low-cost robot (educational kit, toy rover, or retired service unit) and produce a component inventory with photos, part numbers, and replacement costs in your local currency. This is not an academic exercise it is exactly what you will do when a robot fails on a farm or factory floor and you need to source a replacement fast. Document:</p>
<ul>
<li>Controller board model, firmware version, and programming interface.</li>
<li>Every sensor: type, range, interface (IC, UART, analog), and local replacement cost.</li>
<li>Every actuator: motor type, voltage, torque rating, and driver circuit.</li>
<li>Power system: battery chemistry, voltage, capacity, and charging method.</li>
<li>End effector: degrees of freedom, payload, and tool interface.</li>
<li>Wiring harness: connector types and any proprietary plugs (these are the hardest to replace).</li>
</ul>

<h3>6. Quick Reference Checklist</h3>

<ol>
<li><strong>Identify the five subsystems</strong> controller, sensors, actuators, power, end effector before touching anything.</li>
<li><strong>Isolate all energy</strong> mains, battery, compressed air, springs and verify zero state with a meter.</li>
<li><strong>Photograph every step</strong> of disassembly; label every connector and screw bag.</li>
<li><strong>Record part numbers and country of origin</strong> for every component you may need to re-order.</li>
<li><strong>Check local availability</strong> of each part before reassembly; note lead times in weeks.</li>
<li><strong>Verify IP rating and operating temperature</strong> against your actual deployment environment.</li>
<li><strong>Confirm the controller's programming environment</strong> can you actually code it, or does it need a vendor licence?</li>
<li><strong>Write the business case</strong>: cost of robot, cost of downtime, cost of spares, payback period in months.</li>
</ol>
