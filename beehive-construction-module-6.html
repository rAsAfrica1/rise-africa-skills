1. Differences Between Deep, Medium, and Shallow Supers

In beekeeping, supers are the boxes added on top of the brood chamber to store honey. They come in three common depths: deep, medium, and shallow. Deep supers (also called hive bodies) are typically 9 5/8 inches tall and hold 10 frames. Medium supers are 6 5/8 inches tall, and shallow supers are 5 11/16 inches tall. In Africa, where beekeepers often manage smaller, more manageable hives, medium and shallow supers are preferred because they are lighter when full of honey. A deep super full of honey can weigh over 40 kg, while a medium weighs around 25 kg and a shallow about 18 kg. This weight difference is crucial for beekeepers who harvest manually without mechanical lifts.

Medium supers are a good compromise: they hold a decent amount of honey and are still manageable. Shallow supers are the lightest and are often used for comb honey production or for beekeepers with back problems. In terms of frame count, all three can be built to hold 8 or 10 frames. In Africa, 8-frame boxes are common because they are lighter still and use less timber. The choice between deep, medium, and shallow also affects brood management. Many beekeepers use deep supers for the brood chamber and medium or shallow supers for honey. This allows the queen to be excluded from the honey supers using a queen excluder.

When building, the depth determines the height of the box sides. For a medium super, you cut side boards to 6 5/8 inches wide. For a shallow, cut to 5 11/16 inches. The length and width remain the same as a standard Langstroth hive: 19 7/8 inches long and 16 1/4 inches wide for a 10-frame box, or 19 7/8 inches by 13 3/4 inches for an 8-frame box. In Africa, many beekeepers use locally available timber such as cypress, pine, or eucalyptus. Ensure wood is seasoned and free of cracks. Use exterior-grade glue and galvanized nails or screws to withstand weather.

Understanding these differences helps you plan your apiary. If you plan to sell honey in bulk, medium supers may be more efficient. If you want comb honey, shallow supers are ideal. Always standardize your equipment so parts are interchangeable. This saves time and money. In the next lessons, you will learn to cut and assemble medium and shallow supers step by step.

2. Cutting and Assembling Medium Super Boxes

To build a medium super, you need the following materials: seasoned timber (cypress, pine, or eucalyptus) 1 inch thick, exterior wood glue, 1.5-inch galvanized nails or 1.5-inch screws, and a measuring tape, pencil, saw, hammer or drill, and square. The standard dimensions for a 10-frame medium super are: two side boards 19 7/8 inches long, 6 5/8 inches wide; two end boards 16 1/4 inches long, 6 5/8 inches wide. For an 8-frame box, end boards are 13 3/4 inches long. Cut all boards to these dimensions. Use a square to ensure square cuts. Sand rough edges.

Assembly: Apply a thin bead of exterior glue to the ends of the side boards where they meet the end boards. Join one side board to one end board, ensuring the end board sits flush with the side board's edge. Nail or screw through the side board into the end board. Use at least three fasteners per joint. Repeat for the other side. Then attach the remaining end board. Check for squareness by measuring diagonals; they should be equal. If not, adjust before the glue dries. Wipe off excess glue.

For added strength, you can cut a rabbet joint or use finger joints, but butt joints with glue and screws are adequate for most African conditions. Some beekeepers add a metal frame rest (also called a rabbet) on the inside top of the end boards. This is a 3/8-inch deep, 3/4-inch wide groove that holds the frame top bars. You can cut this with a router or chisel. Alternatively, nail a small strip of wood to create the rest. This prevents frames from sliding and maintains bee space.

After assembly, apply two coats of exterior paint or wood preservative that is safe for bees. Do not use creosote or pentachlorophenol. Allow to dry completely before introducing bees. Medium supers are versatile: they can be used for brood or honey. In Africa, many beekeepers use medium supers exclusively for honey to keep weight manageable. Store assembled boxes in a dry place. With proper care, a medium super can last 10 years or more. Building your own saves money and ensures you have equipment when you need it.

3. Cutting and Assembling Shallow Super Boxes

Shallow supers are the lightest option for honey storage. They are ideal for comb honey or for beekeepers who cannot lift heavy boxes. The standard dimensions for a 10-frame shallow super are: side boards 19 7/8 inches long, 5 11/16 inches wide; end boards 16 1/4 inches long, 5 11/16 inches wide. For an 8-frame box, end boards are 13 3/4 inches long. The height is the key difference from medium supers. Cut all boards accurately using a saw and square. Sand edges to prevent splinters.

Assembly follows the same method as medium supers: glue and screw or nail butt joints. Use at least three fasteners per joint. Ensure the box is square by checking diagonals. For shallow supers, because they are shorter, they are less prone to warping, but still use exterior glue and galvanized fasteners. Some beekeepers add a cleat or handle on the sides for easier lifting. A simple wooden handle made from a 1x2 inch strip screwed to the side boards helps. Position handles about 2 inches from the top edge.

Frame rests: Like medium supers, shallow supers need a rabbet or strip to hold frames. The frame rest is typically 3/8 inch deep and 3/4 inch wide. If you don't have a router, nail a 3/4-inch square strip along the inside top of the end boards, leaving a 3/8-inch lip for the frame to rest on. This maintains bee space (3/8 inch) between the top of the frame and the bottom of the next box. Without proper bee space, bees will glue frames together with propolis.

After assembly, paint or treat the wood. Use a bee-safe preservative. Allow to dry. Shallow supers are often used with a queen excluder to prevent the queen from laying eggs in the honey. They are also great for producing cut comb honey. In Africa, shallow supers are popular because they are easy to handle and require less wood. You can build several from the same amount of timber as one deep super. They are also easier to transport to market. Always store empty supers in a dry, ventilated area to prevent mold and pests. With proper construction, shallow supers will serve you well for many harvests.

4. Adding Queen Excluders and Their Placement

A queen excluder is a screen or grid with gaps precisely 4.2 mm (0.163 inches) wide. This allows worker bees to pass through but excludes the larger queen and drones. Queen excluders are placed between the brood chamber (deep super) and the honey supers (medium or shallow). The purpose is to keep the queen from laying eggs in the honey supers, ensuring the honey is clean and free of brood. In Africa, queen excluders are often made of plastic or metal. Plastic excluders are cheaper but can warp in heat; metal ones last longer but cost more. You can also make a wooden frame with wire grid, but ensure gaps are accurate.

Placement: Put the queen excluder directly on top of the brood chamber, then add the honey super on top. Ensure the excluder sits flat and covers the entire opening. If there are gaps, the queen may squeeze through. Some beekeepers place the excluder above a medium super that is used for brood, but the standard is above the brood box. In a two-deep brood chamber setup, place the excluder on top of the upper deep. If you use a single deep brood chamber, place it directly on that.

When adding a queen excluder, give the bees time to accept it. Sometimes workers are reluctant to pass through a new excluder. You can remove it for a few days to let them start using the super, then reinsert it. Or, place the excluder on top of the brood chamber and put the honey super above it. If bees don't move up, check for queenlessness or other issues. In Africa, where honey flows can be intense, a queen excluder helps keep honey supers pure. However, some beekeepers prefer not to use excluders because they can reduce honey production if workers are reluctant to pass. Monitor your hive.

Clean the excluder regularly to remove propolis and wax. Soak in warm water and scrape with a hive tool. Do not use harsh chemicals. Store excluders flat to prevent bending. In areas with small hive beetles, a queen excluder can also help keep beetles out of honey supers. Always ensure the excluder is properly aligned with the box edges. Misalignment can create gaps. With correct placement, you will harvest clean honey and maintain a healthy brood nest.

5. Stacking and Aligning Supers for Stability

Stacking supers correctly is crucial for hive stability, especially in areas with strong winds or wildlife. Start with a level hive stand. The stand should be at least 30 cm off the ground to deter pests like ants and termites. Use concrete blocks, bricks, or treated timber. Ensure the stand is level in all directions. Place the bottom board on the stand, then the brood chamber. Add supers one by one, aligning each box flush with the one below. Any offset creates a ledge where rain can enter and makes the stack unstable.

When stacking, lift with your legs, not your back. Keep the box level as you place it. Ensure the frames inside are pushed together to prevent rolling. In Africa, where hives are often kept in remote areas, stability is key. Use a ratchet strap or rope to secure the stack to the stand, especially during windy seasons. You can also place a heavy stone on the lid. Some beekeepers use a hive strap that goes around the entire hive and hooks to the stand.

Alignment of frames: When adding a super, check that the frames in the lower box are properly spaced. Use a frame spacer or push them together. The bee space between the top of the lower frames and the bottom of the upper frames should be 3/8 inch. If the gap is too large, bees build burr comb; if too small, they propolize. Your supers should have frame rests that maintain this space. If you built your boxes correctly, the rest will ensure proper spacing.

For multiple supers, stack them in the order you want bees to use them. Typically, bees move up, so add new supers on top. If you have a queen excluder, it goes below the first honey super. Keep the stack straight. Inspect the stack after storms. In Africa, honey badgers and monkeys can tip hives. A sturdy stand and strapping help. Also, keep vegetation cleared around the hive to reduce pest harborage. Regularly check the alignment and tightness of straps. A well-stacked hive is easier to inspect and harvest. It also reduces stress on the bees, leading to better honey production.

6. Seasonal Management of Supers

Seasonal management of supers involves adding and removing boxes based on nectar flow and colony strength. In Africa, seasons vary: in East Africa, long rains (March-May) and short rains (Oct-Dec) bring flowers. In Southern Africa, spring and summer are peak. During peak flow, add supers as needed. Start with one honey super when the brood chamber is 70-80% full of bees and brood. Add a second super when the first is 70% full of honey. This gives bees space to store nectar and prevents swarming.

During dearth (dry season), remove empty supers to prevent pests like wax moths and small hive beetles. Leave only the brood chamber and maybe one super if bees are still using it. In hot climates, reduce the hive entrance and ensure ventilation. In cold areas, reduce entrance and consider insulating the hive. Always leave enough honey for the bees to survive. In Africa, many beekeepers harvest twice a year. After harvest, remove supers and store them properly. Clean frames and boxes. Store in a dry, well-ventilated room. You can use paradichlorobenzene crystals (moth balls) to prevent wax moths, but do not use near bees. Alternatively, freeze frames for 24 hours to kill eggs.

Monitor for pests: ants, termites, wax moths, and small hive beetles. Use ant moats or grease bands on stands. Keep the apiary clean. In areas with Africanized bees, manage supers carefully to avoid aggression. Work during cool hours. Always wear protective gear. When adding supers, consider the colony's population. A weak colony cannot defend a large hive. It's better to keep the hive smaller. Also, consider the honey harvest: shallow supers are easier to harvest and spin. Medium supers are versatile. Deep supers are heavy; use them mainly for brood.

Record keeping: Note when you add supers, when they are full, and when you harvest. This helps you plan for next season. In Africa, where resources are limited, proper seasonal management ensures you maximize honey yield while keeping colonies healthy. Adjust your management to local conditions. Talk to experienced beekeepers in your area. With practice, you will know exactly when to add or remove supers.

<h2>Practical Reference: Building Medium and Shallow Supers</h2>

<p>In African beekeeping enterprises, the super is not just a box it is a revenue unit. Every medium or shallow super you build and stack correctly translates directly into more honey harvested, more comb honey sold, and more pollination contracts fulfilled. This practical reference consolidates the construction, assembly, and seasonal management decisions you must make when working with medium and shallow supers in an African context, where timber availability, humidity, pests, and market demand differ sharply from temperate-zone beekeeping.</p>

<p>Deep supers (also called brood boxes or full-depth supers) typically measure around 241 mm (9 inches) in depth. Medium supers measure roughly 168 mm (6 inches), and shallow supers measure about 146 mm (5 inches). The difference is not cosmetic. A medium super holds about 70% of the honey volume of a deep, and a shallow holds about 60%. For African beekeepers selling comb honey, cut comb, or chunk honey, shallow supers are the preferred choice because the comb fits standard retail containers and the lighter weight reduces harvest labour a critical factor when family labour is the primary workforce.</p>

<h3>Cutting and Assembling Medium Super Boxes</h3>

<p>Medium supers are the workhorse of African honey production. They are deep enough to hold a full honey crop but light enough for one person to lift when full typically 1520 kg versus 2530 kg for a deep super. When cutting timber for medium supers, use 20 mm ( inch) boards. In East Africa, <strong>Cupressus lusitanica</strong> (Cypress) and <strong>Pinus patula</strong> are the most readily available and affordable softwoods. In West Africa, <strong>Terminalia superba</strong> (Afara) and <strong>Ceiba pentandra</strong> are common. Avoid timber with visible knots on the box ends, as these become entry points for pests and moisture.</p>

<p>Cut list for a standard 10-frame medium super (Langstroth dimensions):</p>
<ul>
<li>2 end boards: 505 mm 168 mm 20 mm</li>
<li>2 side boards: 425 mm 168 mm 20 mm</li>
<li>Rabbet or frame rest: 10 mm deep, 12 mm wide, cut on the inside top edge of the end boards</li>
</ul>

<p>Assembly: Apply waterproof wood glue (PVA exterior grade or polyurethane) to all joints. Nail or screw through the side boards into the end boards pre-drill to prevent splitting, especially with hardwood. Use galvanised nails or exterior screws. After assembly, check for square by measuring diagonally across the box; both diagonals must be equal. In humid coastal regions (Mombasa, Lagos, Accra), apply two coats of linseed oil or food-safe wood preservative to all external surfaces. Never use creosote or engine oil bees will abscond and honey will be contaminated.</p>

<h3>Cutting and Assembling Shallow Super Boxes</h3>

<p>Shallow supers are your comb honey and cut comb production units. They are also ideal for queen rearing and for hives managed by women and youth groups, where lighter weights improve safety and reduce injury. The construction method is identical to medium supers only the height of the end and side boards changes from 168 mm to 146 mm. Because shallow frames are shorter, you can often get two shallow supers from the same timber that would yield one deep super, improving material efficiency by up to 40%.</p>

<p>When assembling shallow supers, pay extra attention to frame rest alignment. A 2 mm misalignment causes burr comb and cross-comb, which makes harvesting difficult and reduces comb honey grade. Use a jig or template when cutting rabbets. After assembly, stack the boxes and check that frames sit level. In African markets, neat, uniform comb honey commands a 2030% price premium over irregular comb, so precision here is directly linked to profitability.</p>

<h3>Adding Queen Excluders and Their Placement</h3>

<p>The queen excluder is a management tool, not a permanent fixture. Place it directly on top of the brood chamber (deep super) and below the first medium or shallow super. This prevents the queen from laying in honey supers, keeping comb clean for harvest. Use a metal or plastic excluder with 4.2 mm spacing this allows worker bees through but excludes the larger queen.</p>

<p>In African conditions, remove the queen excluder during dearth periods or when the colony is building up after swarming. Bees are reluctant to pass through excluders when nectar flow is weak, and a poorly maintained excluder can cause swarming. Clean excluders with a wire brush every 34 months. In top-bar and transitional hives common in rural Africa, excluders may not fit standard dimensions measure your hive opening before purchase.</p>

<h3>Stacking and Aligning Supers for Stability</h3>

<p>Stacking is where many African beekeepers lose honey not to pests, but to misalignment. When supers are not flush, bees build brace comb in the gaps, rain enters, and the stack becomes unstable. Always stack on a level hive stand at least 40 cm above ground. Place the brood chamber first, then queen excluder, then medium or shallow supers. Use a strap or rope to secure the stack in windy areas and in areas with honey badgers or livestock.</p>

<p>Align boxes by pressing each super against the one below before lowering it. A 3 mm overhang is enough to create a rain channel into the hive. In Tanzania, beekeepers using shallow supers report 15% less honey loss when stacks are strapped and aligned compared to unstrapped stacks (Tanzania Forest Services Agency, 2020).</p>

<h3>Seasonal Management of Supers</h3>

<p>In Africa, the beekeeping calendar is driven by rainfall, not temperature. In bimodal rainfall zones (Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania), you have two honey flows per year. In unimodal zones (Ethiopia, Zambia), one main flow. Your super management must follow this rhythm. During build-up, add medium supers to give the colony space. During flow, add shallow supers for comb honey. After harvest, remove empty supers and store them with moth crystals (paradichlorobenzene) in a dry place never store wet supers, as they attract wax moths and small hive beetles.</p>

<p>Business tip: Keep at least 20% more supers than your active hives. This allows you to add supers instantly when flow starts, capturing peak nectar. Beekeepers who wait to build supers during flow lose 1015% of potential honey yield. In Ethiopia, beekeepers with surplus supers report 25% higher annual income than those without (SNV Ethiopia, 2019).</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li> Timber cut to correct height: 168 mm medium, 146 mm shallow</li>
<li> Rabbit/frame rest cut 10 mm deep, 12 mm wide, level</li>
<li> Boxes square diagonals equal, no gaps over 2 mm</li>
<li> Waterproof glue + galvanised nails/screws used throughout</li>
<li> Food-safe preservative applied (linseed oil), never creosote</li>
<li> Queen excluder placed between brood and honey supers only</li>
<li> Stacks aligned flush and strapped in windy or high-pest areas</li>
<li> Supers stored dry with moth protection during dearth</li>
</ul>
