Lessons

1. Cleaning Aluminium Properly

Aluminium forms a tough oxide layer within minutes of being cleaned. That oxide melts at 2072C while the aluminium beneath melts at 660C. If you weld through the oxide, you get porosity, lack of fusion, and a weak joint. So cleaning is not optionalit is the first weld pass.

Start by degreasing. Use acetone or a dedicated aluminium cleaner. Never use petrol or dieselthey leave oily residues that cause porosity. Wipe with a clean lint-free cloth. For heavy grease, use a water-based alkaline cleaner and rinse with distilled water. In dusty workshops, cover cleaned parts with clean paper.

Next, remove the oxide mechanically. Use a dedicated stainless steel brushnever one that has touched steel or carbon steel. Brush in one direction only, with light pressure. A wire brush on a drill at 20003000 rpm works for large areas, but keep it moving to avoid gouging. For critical joints, follow with a carbide burr or a stainless steel scraper. Do not use sandpaper or grinding discsthey embed particles.

Clean within 30 minutes of welding. If you must wait, wrap the part in clean plastic. Humidity above 70% accelerates oxide regrowth. In coastal areas like Mombasa or Lagos, clean just before striking the arc. After cleaning, handle with clean glovesskin oils contaminate.

For thick sections, preheat to 80120C to drive off moisture. Check with a temperature crayon. If you see black soot while welding, stopit means you are burning through dirt. Clean again. A clean joint gives a bright, shiny weld. A dirty joint gives a grey, sooty weld that will crack.

Remember: clean aluminium welds like a dream; dirty aluminium welds like a nightmare. Spend the time, and your welds will pass inspection.

2. Stainless Brushes and Dedicated Tools

Aluminium contamination is the number one cause of bad welds. Steel particles embedded in aluminium cause black specks, porosity, and cracking. The solution is simple: dedicate tools to aluminium only. Never use the same brush, grinder, or file on steel and aluminium.

Buy a stainless steel wire brush. The bristles must be stainlessnot carbon steel. Carbon steel bristles leave rust particles that contaminate the weld. Look for brushes labelled 'stainless steel' or 'Inox'. They cost a little more but save you rework. Keep one brush for aluminium only. Mark it with red tape or store it in a separate drawer.

For grinders, use dedicated discs. Aluminium clogs standard grinding wheels. Use a silicon carbide disc or a flap disc designed for aluminium. Never use a disc that has touched steelit will embed steel particles. If you must share a grinder, change the disc and clean the spindle with acetone.

Files and burrs: use a coarse single-cut file for aluminium. Clean it with a wire brush after each use. Carbide burrs for aluminium have a different flute design than those for steel. Keep them separate.

In many African workshops, tools are shared. That is a recipe for contamination. Talk to your supervisor. Explain that one contaminated brush can ruin a whole weld. A simple solution: buy a cheap angle grinder for aluminium only. A 500-watt grinder costs less than a rework job.

Store brushes and tools in a clean, dry place. Dust and grit from the workshop floor can contaminate them. Hang them on a wall or keep them in a sealed box. Before each use, check the brush for rust or debris. If in doubt, clean it with acetone and blow it dry.

Remember: a stainless brush costs a few dollars. A failed weld costs a client. Dedicate your tools.

3. Cutting Aluminium

Cutting aluminium is different from cutting steel. Aluminium is softer, gummier, and conducts heat five times faster. If you use the wrong method, you get rough edges, warping, and a lot of wasted material. The goal is a clean, square cut with minimal heat input.

For thin sheet (up to 3 mm), use tin snips or an electric shear. A jigsaw with a fine-tooth blade (1824 TPI) also works. Use a lubricantkerosene or a dedicated aluminium cutting fluidto stop the blade from clogging. Cut slowly. Let the tool do the work. For straight cuts, a circular saw with a carbide-tipped blade (negative rake) is fast. Set the blade depth just past the material. Wear safety glasses and ear protection.

For thicker plate (620 mm), use a band saw or a chop saw with a carbide blade. A plasma cutter is excellent for aluminium if you have one. Set the current according to thickness: 30 amps for 3 mm, 60 amps for 6 mm. Use compressed air or nitrogen as the plasma gas. Keep the torch moving to avoid dross.

For profiles and tubes, a mitre saw with a carbide blade works well. Clamp the work securely. Aluminium can grab and kick back. Never cut freehand. Use a fence or a stop block for repeat cuts.

After cutting, deburr the edges. A file or a deburring tool removes sharp edges and oxide. For weld prep, bevel the edges to 3037.5 degrees for a V-groove. Leave a root face of 12 mm. Clean the cut with a stainless brush and acetone.

In hot African climates, aluminium expands as it heats. Cut slightly oversize (12 mm) and trim after cooling. If you are cutting outside, work in the shade to reduce heat distortion. Always support the material to prevent binding. A clean cut saves time on fit-up.

4. Fit-Up and Tacking

Fit-up is where good welds are made. A gap that is too wide, too narrow, or misaligned will cause defects. For aluminium, fit-up must be precise because the metal moves twice as much as steel when heated. Aim for a root gap of 01 mm for butt joints up to 3 mm thick. For thicker plate, use a 23 mm gap with a backing strip.

Before tacking, clean both mating surfaces with a stainless brush and acetone. Clamp the parts in position. Use a square, a level, or a straight edge to check alignment. For pipes, use a pipe clamp or a chain clamp. Tack welds should be 1015 mm long for thin material, 2030 mm for thick. Space them every 150200 mm. Tack in a sequence that balances shrinkage: start at the centre, then alternate sides.

Use the same filler wire for tacks as for the final weld. For 3 mm aluminium, use 1.6 mm or 2.4 mm filler. Set your welder to the correct amperage: about 1 amp per 0.025 mm of thickness. For 3 mm, that is roughly 120 amps. Use AC for TIG, DCEP for MIG. Keep the torch angle at 1015 degrees push.

Tack welds must be strong enough to hold the part but small enough to be fully remelted during welding. If a tack cracks, grind it out and re-tack. Do not leave a cracked tackit will propagate.

After tacking, check for distortion. If the part has pulled, you may need to adjust with a clamp or a wedge. In many African workshops, you will not have a welding table. Use a flat concrete floor or a heavy steel plate as a reference. Always work on a flat surface.

Remember: a good fit-up means a good weld. A bad fit-up means a repair. Take the time to fit and tack properly.

5. Jigs and Fixtures

A jig holds your work in the correct position while you weld. A fixture is a larger jig that also locates and clamps. For aluminium, jigs are critical because the metal distorts easily. A simple jig can save you hours of straightening.

Start with a flat base. A 10 mm steel plate works well. Weld or bolt stops and clamps to the plate. Use toggle clamps, C-clamps, or wedge clamps. For repeat work, make a dedicated jig. For one-offs, use a combination of clamps and blocks.

Materials for jigs: steel is best because it is strong and heat-resistant. Aluminium jigs can work but they may warp. If you use aluminium, make them thick. Wooden jigs are only for light work and must be kept dry.

Design your jig so that you can access the weld. Leave clearance for the torch and for your hand. For T-joints, use a 90-degree angle jig. For butt joints, use a backing bar to control penetration. A copper backing bar works well because it draws heat away and does not stick to aluminium.

In Africa, many workshops cannot afford expensive jigs. You can make simple jigs from angle iron, channel, and bolts. A few C-clamps and a straight edge can act as a jig. For pipes, use a pipe stand with a rotating clamp. For frames, use a flat table with corner squares.

When using a jig, tack the part first. Then remove the jig if it restricts access. Weld in a sequence that balances distortion. For long seams, use backstep welding: weld from the centre out, or from both ends toward the centre. This reduces pulling.

After welding, let the part cool in the jig. This holds it straight. Do not quench with waterit can cause cracking. Air cool is best.

A good jig is an investment. It saves time, reduces rework, and improves quality. Start simple, then improve.

6. Controlling Distortion Before You Weld

Aluminium expands and contracts more than steel. When you weld, the heat causes the metal to expand, then contract as it cools. This movement causes distortion: bending, twisting, and buckling. You cannot eliminate distortion, but you can control it.

First, minimise heat input. Use the lowest amperage that gives good fusion. Weld fast. Use a small weave. For TIG, use a foot pedal to taper off. For MIG, use a push angle and move quickly. Do not over-weld. A 6 mm fillet does not need a 10 mm leg.

Second, use balanced welding. Weld on both sides of a joint, or alternate sides. For a T-joint, weld one side, then the other. For a butt joint, use a backstep technique: start 50 mm from the end, weld toward the end, then go back and weld the rest. This balances the shrinkage.

Third, use presetting. If you know a part will pull, set it in the opposite direction. For example, if a beam will bend up, clamp it down before welding. For a butt joint, leave a slight gap that closes as it cools.

Fourth, use chill blocks or heat sinks. Copper or aluminium blocks clamped next to the weld draw heat away. This reduces the heat-affected zone and distortion. You can also use wet rags, but be careful not to quench the weld.

Fifth, use strongbacks and stiffeners. Tack a stiffener across the joint before welding. Remove it after cooling. For long seams, use a strongback every 500 mm.

In Africa, where temperatures can be 40C, aluminium expands more. Work in the shade or early morning. Do not weld in direct sun if possible. If you must, use a windbreak to reduce convection.

After welding, let the part cool naturally. Do not force cool with water or air. Check for distortion with a straight edge. If it has moved, you can sometimes straighten with a torch and quench, but that is risky. Better to prevent it.

Remember: control distortion before you strike the arc. Plan your welds, use jigs, and balance your heat. Your work will be straighter and stronger.

<section>
<h2>Practical Reference: Preparation, Fit-Up and Jigs for Aluminium Welding</h2>

<p>In aluminium fabrication, the weld is often the least profitable part of the job. The money is made or lost in preparation. Aluminium oxide melts at roughly 2,075 C while the aluminium beneath it melts at about 660 C. That single fact explains why cleaning, dedicated tooling, accurate cutting, disciplined fit-up and distortion control decide whether a workshop in Nairobi, Lagos, Johannesburg or Accra turns a profit on a gate frame, a truck body, a water tank or a solar mounting structure.</p>

<p>This practical reference condenses the six lessons of this module into shop-floor procedures you can apply today. It is written for the African context: intermittent power, imported consumables, mixed-skilled teams, and clients who judge you on delivery time and appearance as much as on weld strength.</p>

<h3>1. Cleaning Aluminium Properly</h3>

<p>Aluminium arrives from the supplier with a naturally re-forming oxide layer plus mill oils, cutting fluid, marking ink and atmospheric dust. Weld porosity, lack of fusion and erratic arc behaviour usually trace back to this contamination, not to the machine settings.</p>

<p><strong>Two-stage cleaning is the standard:</strong></p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Degrease.</strong> Remove oils and greases with a solvent or an alkaline cleaner. Acetone, isopropyl alcohol or a dedicated aluminium cleaner all work. Wipe in one direction with clean lint-free cloths; never circulate a dirty rag back over a clean area. Allow the solvent to flash off completely before welding.</li>
<li><strong>Remove the oxide.</strong> Mechanically abrade the joint faces and 2550 mm either side with a stainless steel wire brush, a carbide burr or abrasive paper. Brush in one direction only. Do not use grinding discs contaminated with steel, and do not use a brush that has touched carbon steel.</li>
</ol>

<p>Clean immediately before welding. In humid coastal cities such as Mombasa or Lagos, the oxide layer can rebuild noticeably within hours; in dusty inland workshops, airborne contamination settles just as fast. A useful rule: if you can see or feel the difference, clean it again.</p>

<h3>2. Stainless Brushes and Dedicated Tools</h3>

<p>Cross-contamination is the silent killer of aluminium welds. A carbon steel brush deposits iron particles into the aluminium surface; those particles then introduce porosity, inclusions and galvanic corrosion points. The discipline is simple but must be enforced by the workshop owner, not left to habit.</p>

<p><strong>Rules that pay for themselves:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Stainless steel wire brushes only, and dedicated to aluminium. Mark them with a colour code (for example, blue handle tape) so they cannot be borrowed for steel work.</li>
<li>Dedicated cutting discs, grinding discs, burrs and files. Store them separately.</li>
<li>Dedicated gloves and clean rags. A glove used on galvanised steel will contaminate aluminium.</li>
<li>Dedicated work surface or a clean sacrificial plywood/MDF sheet over the bench.</li>
<li>Never use a wire brush on a grinder that has been used on steel; the brush itself carries the contamination.</li>
</ul>

<p>For a small workshop, a practical investment is a set of colour-coded tools kept in a locked cabinet with the aluminium stock. The cost of one contaminated weld rework, lost client confidence, transport back to site is usually far higher than the tool set.</p>

<p>After any thermal cut (plasma, abrasive disc), the cut edge carries oxide and dross. Remove it with a stainless brush or a dedicated aluminium burr before fit-up. A square, deburred edge is the foundation of a consistent root gap.</p>

<h3>4. Fit-Up and Tacking</h3>

<p>Fit-up determines how much filler you need, how much heat you put in, and how much the part moves. In aluminium, where thermal conductivity is roughly five to six times that of steel, poor fit-up is punished quickly: gaps widen as the part heats, and the welder chases distortion around the joint.</p>

<p><strong>Fit-up essentials:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Joint preparation:</strong> Square butt edges for thin material; bevel (typically 60 included) for thicker sections. Remove burrs.</li>
<li><strong>Root gap:</strong> Keep it consistent. For TIG on 36 mm aluminium, a gap of roughly 12 mm is common; for MIG, tighter fit is usually better. Consistency matters more than the exact number.</li>
<li><strong>Tack sequence:</strong> Tack alternately, working from the centre outwards or from both ends towards the centre, depending on the joint. Short tacks (1020 mm) with full penetration.</li>
<li><strong>Tack placement:</strong> Place tacks where they will be consumed by the final weld, or where they can be dressed back if appearance matters.</li>
<li><strong>Check before welding:</strong> Measure diagonals, check squareness, confirm flatness. Once the arc starts, correction becomes expensive.</li>
</ul>

<p>For business owners, the fit-up bench is where labour cost is controlled. A fitter who produces consistent joints reduces welder time, filler consumption and rework. Invest in straight edges, squares, clamps and a flat assembly table before investing in a second welding machine.</p>

<h3>5. Jigs and Fixtures</h3>

<p>A jig holds the part; a fixture holds the part and locates the tool or torch. In aluminium fabrication, both are used to control distortion, repeat accuracy and cycle time. For a business, jigs convert a skilled welder's judgment into a repeatable process that a less experienced team member can follow.</p>

<p><strong>Design principles for aluminium jigs:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Material choice:</strong> Steel jigs are common and robust, but must be insulated or isolated from the aluminium to avoid galvanic contamination and arc strikes. Stainless or aluminium jigs are lighter and avoid contamination but cost more.</li>
<li><strong>Clamping:</strong> Use toggle clamps, wedge clamps or bolted bars. Avoid over-clamping thin sheet; it can dent or cold-work the surface.</li>
<li><strong>Heat management:</strong> Aluminium conducts heat away fast. Jigs can act as heat sinks and increase the amperage needed. Where possible, keep the jig clear of the weld zone or use insulating pads.</li>
<li><strong>Access:</strong> Leave room for the torch, filler rod and the welder's helmet. A jig that blocks access will be abandoned.</li>
<li><strong>Repeatability:</strong> Locate from machined edges or pins, not from raw saw-cut edges.</li>
<li><strong>Economics:</strong> Build jigs for parts you make more than a handful of times. A simple jig that saves 20 minutes per unit pays back within weeks on a batch of 50.</li>
</ul>

<p>In many African workshops, jigs are improvised from angle iron, flat bar and G-clamps. That is fine if the jig is square, clean and repeatable. What is not fine is welding directly on a dirty steel bench with no location that is where rework starts.</p>

<h3>6. Controlling Distortion Before You Weld</h3>

<p>Aluminium shrinks roughly twice as much as steel on cooling. Distortion is therefore not an accident; it is a predictable result of heat input and restraint. The welder's job is to plan for it before striking an arc.</p>

<p><strong>Practical distortion controls:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Balance the heat:</strong> Weld alternately on opposite sides of the joint, or use a back-step sequence.</li>
<li><strong>Minimise heat input:</strong> Use the lowest amperage that gives fusion, travel fast enough to avoid puddling, and avoid unnecessary weaving.</li>
<li><strong>Pre-set or pre-camber:</strong> Set the parts slightly out of position in the opposite direction to expected shrinkage.</li>
<li><strong>Restraint:</strong> Use jigs, strongbacks or tacked-on stiffeners to hold alignment during welding. Remove restraint only after the weld has cooled.</li>
<li><strong>Sequence:</strong> Weld from the centre outwards on long joints, or from both ends towards the centre on restrained joints.</li>
<li><strong>Cooling:</strong> Allow natural cooling. Do not quench with water it can cause cracking and introduces contamination.</li>
<li><strong>Post-weld correction:</strong> Planishing or controlled heat straightening can correct minor distortion, but it adds cost and risk. Prevention is cheaper.</li>
</ul>

<strong style="color:#b00020;">SAFETY WARNING Aluminium welding hazards</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#111;">Aluminium welding produces intense ultraviolet radiation, ozone and metal fume. Always weld with a properly shaded helmet (minimum shade 1013 depending on process and amperage), local exhaust ventilation or forced air movement, and appropriate PPE. Aluminium dust from cutting and grinding is combustible; keep it cleared and do not allow it to accumulate near ignition sources. Never weld on containers or tanks that have held fuel, solvents or unknown liquids without certified cleaning and gas testing. In confined spaces, ensure continuous ventilation and a trained standby person. If you are unsure, stop and get advice no deadline is worth a fatality.</p>
</div>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li>Degrease, then stainless-brush the joint faces and 2550 mm either side; clean immediately before welding.</li>
<li>Use only stainless brushes, discs and files dedicated to aluminium colour-code them and store separately.</li>
<li>Cut square, deburr, and remove oxide/dross from thermal cut edges before fit-up.</li>
<li>Set a consistent root gap and bevel; check squareness and diagonals before tacking.</li>
<li>Tack alternately from the centre outwards; keep tacks short and full-penetration.</li>
<li>Use a square, clean jig with good torch access for any part made more than a few times.</li>
<li>Plan the weld sequence to balance heat; use restraint and pre-set to control shrinkage.</li>
<li>Allow natural cooling; never quench. Inspect and record before releasing the job.</li>
</ol>
