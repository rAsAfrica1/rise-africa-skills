6. Quality Control and Contamination Prevention

One contaminated jar can destroy a whole batch. One bad batch destroys a customer's crop. One bad customer experience ends your reputation in a small industry. This module is about the discipline that keeps your spawn clean and your business trusted.

Module 6 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~50 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 6.1 — Recognising contamination — green, black, orange, bacterial

Learn these four contaminant types by sight. You will see them all within your first few months.

ContaminantColourTextureSpeed
Trichoderma (green mould)Bright to dark greenPowdery, spreadingVery fast — 24-48h from invisible to green
Aspergillus / RhizopusBlack, dark greyFluffy, spore-heavyFast — 2-3 days
Bacteria (Serratia)Orange-red, pinkSlimy, wetMedium
Bacteria (general)Off-white, cream, grey translucentWet, glossy streaksFast
YeastCream-beigeSmooth, pastyMedium, smells yeasty

Signs of contamination you can smell:

  • Sour, vinegary, or alcoholic smell — bacterial
  • Sweet, rotting fruit — yeast or bacterial
  • Nail polish remover (acetone) — specific bacterial contamination
  • Mouldy, musty — fungal contamination

The single most dangerous contaminant is Trichoderma. It spreads faster than any other contaminant, produces billions of spores, and will infect everything in your lab within days if you do not act. If you ever see green in any container, remove it from the lab immediately, take it outside, and dispose of the contents.

Green = gone. No exceptions. Do not open the container inside. Do not try to salvage the grain. Take it outside, open it outside, dispose in a sealed bag, and clean the container outside.
Practical exercise 6.1: Deliberately contaminate one jar (leave it open near a compost pile for 30 minutes). Watch how fast it goes green. This trains your eye for the earliest stages — before it is obvious.

Lesson 6.2 — The five most common contamination sources

Every contamination has a source. If you find the source, you fix the pattern.

1 — Inadequate sterilisation. Undercooked grain, insufficient pressure, too-short cycle. This is the #1 cause. Solution: verify your pressure cooker reaches 15 PSI. Cook the full time. Use a timer.

2 — Contaminated culture. You started with a plate that looked clean but was not. Solution: inspect every plate at 5 days and 10 days. Never use a plate you are not 100% sure about.

3 — Poor sterile technique. A draught, a careless movement, an unsterilised tool. Solution: practise slow, deliberate movements. Never skip alcohol wipe of the still-air box.

4 — Wet grain. Grain that was not dried enough before filling jars. Solution: the squeeze test. If it drips, dry longer.

5 — Dirty room. Dust, draughts, pets, or food in the lab. Solution: dedicate the room. Clean before every session. No food, no visitors.

Every jar contaminateSterilisation or culture problem

Practical exercise 6.2: Every time a jar contaminates, write down: what, when, and what you suspect. After 20 contaminated jars, patterns will be obvious. Fix the pattern, not just the jars.

Lesson 6.3 — Preventing airborne, contact, and substrate contamination

Contamination arrives by three routes. Protect against all three.

Airborne contamination — spores floating in the air. Protection:

  • Work only inside the still-air box or laminar flow hood
  • Keep the room sealed
  • Clean surfaces with 70% alcohol before every session
  • Never sweep or dust within 1 hour of starting work
  • Keep door closed, no draughts, no fans

Contact contamination — touching contaminated surfaces with tools, hands, or containers. Protection:

  • Flame-sterilise all metal tools before use
  • Use fresh gloves for every session
  • Wipe jar exteriors with alcohol before they enter the box
  • Never set tools down on non-sterile surfaces
  • Never touch your face, hair, or phone during transfers

Substrate contamination — contaminants already inside the grain before sterilisation. Protection:

  • Buy fresh grain from reliable sources
  • Soak and drain properly
  • Fully sterilise at 15 PSI for the full time
  • Do not overfill jars — air circulation is part of sterilisation
  • After sterilisation, let jars cool before opening the cooker
The three-layer protection: Even if one layer fails, the other two should catch most contamination. If you find yourself relying on any single layer, you are one mistake away from losing a batch.
Practical exercise 6.3: Before your next session, go through the three lists. Check every item. This is your pre-flight checklist. After 20 sessions, it will be instinct.

Lesson 6.4 — Testing spawn vigour — the shake test

Before you sell spawn, test its vigour. Vigour is the mycelium's health and vitality — how fast it grows and how strong it is. Weak spawn produces weak mushroom crops.

The shake test:

  1. Once your jar is fully colonised (all white), shake it firmly.
  2. The grain should break apart into individual grains, each coated in mycelium.
  3. Wait 24–48 hours.
  4. If the grain re-forms into a solid white block within 2–3 days, the spawn is vigorous.
  5. If the grain does not recover or stays loose, the spawn is weak — do not sell it.

Other vigour signals:

  • Mushroomy smell — strong, clean, pleasant. Weak or off-smell = weak spawn.
  • Mycelium pulling from the glass — a sign of vigour and health.
  • Fast colonisation — from inoculation to full colonisation in under 14 days is ideal. Over 20 days is slow.
  • No yellow liquid at the bottom of the jar. Yellow = mycelium metabolites = stress or age.
Never sell spawn that fails the shake test. A customer will plant it into their substrate, wait weeks, and find the substrate never colonises or gets contaminated. That is the end of that customer. Destroy the batch instead.
Practical exercise 6.4: Run the shake test on every batch before selling. Log the recovery time for each batch. Over time, you will see which grain, which species, and which technique produce the strongest spawn.

Lesson 6.5 — Batch records and traceability

Even small operations need records. When a customer complains that spawn failed, you need to trace back to the batch and understand what happened. Without records, you have no way to know.

What to record for every batch:

FieldExample

Source of cultureAgar plate #14, dated 1 September

The batch number system: [Species code]-[YYYYMMDD]-[Letter]. Example: OSP-20260915-A means Oyster Spawn, made 15 September 2026, first batch of that day.

Why this matters: If Mr. Moyo calls to say his substrate did not colonise, you look at batch OSP-20260915-A. You see it was made from plate #14 — which you can check for issues. You see the shake test passed. You can tell Mr. Moyo with confidence that your spawn was healthy, and together you troubleshoot his substrate. Or you find that batch #14 was borderline, and you make it right. Without records, you cannot do either.

Practical exercise 6.5: Create a batch log — a physical notebook or a spreadsheet. Start using it today. Every batch gets an entry. Even a badly-run batch teaches you something.

Lesson 6.6 — Dealing with a contaminated batch — never sell it

Some batches will fail. It happens to every producer, even experienced ones. What you do with a failed batch separates professionals from amateurs.

The rules:

  1. Never sell contaminated spawn. Not at a discount. Not with a warning. Not "just this once". Not to a friend. Never.
  2. Isolate the batch immediately. If you see contamination in one jar, check all jars. Remove any with signs.
  3. Remove contaminated jars from the lab — take them outside before opening.
  4. Dispose safely. The contents can go on a compost pile far from your lab, or into a sealed bag to landfill. Do not put them back into your production chain.
  5. Clean the container. Wash the jar or bag with bleach solution (1:10). Rinse thoroughly. Only then reuse.
  6. Record the failure. Write down: what contaminated, at what stage, and your best guess at the cause.
  7. Do not blame yourself. Failure is part of the learning curve. Every producer has a contamination rate. Yours will drop from 30% to under 5% within 6 months of careful practice.

What you lose on a failed batch:

Loss per contaminated jar (500 ml)Cost
Grain$0.30
Jar, filter, labour$0.70
Sterilisation fuel$0.30

Direct cost$1.30

You can absorb 30% contamination in month 1 — that is $0.40 per saleable jar. What you cannot absorb is selling contaminated spawn and losing a customer permanently. That customer was worth $500–$5,000 in lifetime value.

The one rule that never breaks: If you are not sure, do not sell it. Your reputation is worth more than any single batch. Destroy the batch, record the lesson, move on.
Practical exercise 6.6: Write down your contamination protocol — the seven steps above, in your own words. Post it in your lab. When you have your first contaminated batch, follow it exactly. Do not improvise under pressure.
