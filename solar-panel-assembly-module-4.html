Lessons

1. Aligning Cells for Series Stringing

Before you solder, you must align the solar cells correctly. Each cell has a front (blue or black, with thin silver lines called fingers) and a back (usually silver or grey, with wider flat areas called busbars). For series stringing, you want the front of one cell to connect to the back of the next. So lay them out in a row, all facing the same way, but with a small gap of about 2-3 mm between them. This gap prevents cracking when the panel heats up.

Use a straight edge or a jig made from a wooden board with nails or small blocks to keep the cells in a perfect line. A simple jig: take a flat board, screw down two thin strips of wood or metal at a distance equal to the cell width plus 2 mm. Slide each cell into the slot. This ensures the busbars line up. If you are working on a table, mark the positions with a pencil or tape.

Clean the busbars on the front of each cell with a soft eraser or isopropyl alcohol and a lint-free cloth. Any dust or oil will weaken the solder joint. Wear cotton gloves to avoid getting fingerprints on the cell surface. Fingerprints can reduce light absorption and cause hot spots.

Once aligned, you are ready to solder. Remember: the front busbars of one cell connect to the back busbars of the next. For a typical 5-inch cell, there are 2 or 3 busbars. You will need tabbing wire (also called ribbon) that is pre-tinned. Cut strips about 1.5 times the cell length. For example, if the cell is 156 mm long, cut 230 mm strips. You will need two strips per cell for a 2-busbar cell, three for a 3-busbar cell.

Keep your work area clean and well-lit. A small fan helps to blow away solder fumes, but avoid strong wind that could move the cells. In many African workshops, a simple wooden jig and a 40-watt soldering iron are all you need. Practice aligning a few cells without soldering first until you can do it quickly and accurately.

2. Soldering Cells in Series to Form Strings

Soldering solar cells is delicate work. You need a soldering iron of 40 to 60 watts with a flat or chisel tip, rosin-core solder (lead-free is safer), and tabbing wire (pre-tinned ribbon). Work on a heat-resistant surface like a ceramic tile or a piece of plywood. Set your iron to about 350C. If your iron has no temperature control, let it heat for 5 minutes and test on a scrap piece of wire.

Start by placing a cell face up (front busbars visible). Take a strip of tabbing wire and lay it across the busbar, leaving about 10 mm overhang on each end. Press the iron onto the wire for 2-3 seconds, then touch the solder to the joint. The solder should flow smoothly and shiny. Do not hold the iron too longmore than 5 seconds can damage the cell. Solder one end, then the other, then the middle. Repeat for each busbar.

Now flip the cell over. The back has wider busbars (usually 2 or 3). Solder another strip of tabbing wire across the back, but this time leave the overhang only on one side (the side that will connect to the next cell). For a series string, the front of cell 1 connects to the back of cell 2. So after soldering both sides of cell 1, take cell 2 and place it face up. Solder the overhanging tabs from the back of cell 1 onto the front busbars of cell 2. The connection must be flat and straight.

Continue this process: cell 2's back connects to cell 3's front, and so on. A typical string has 6 to 12 cells. For a 12-volt panel, you need 36 cells in series (or 60 for a 24V panel). But for practice, make a string of 6 cells. After each connection, gently tug the wire to check the solder joint. If it moves, re-solder.

Use a damp sponge to clean your iron tip often. Keep the tip tinned (coated with solder) to prevent oxidation. In dusty environments, cover your work when not soldering. Practice on broken cells first. A good solder joint is smooth, shiny, and concave. A bad joint looks dull, blobby, or has cracks. Reheat and re-solder if needed.

3. Testing String Voltage and Current Output

After soldering a string, you must test it before going further. Use a digital multimeter (DMM) set to DC volts. For a 6-cell string, each cell produces about 0.5 to 0.6 volts in full sun. So expect 3.0 to 3.6 volts open-circuit (Voc). If you get less, check for reversed cells or bad solder joints. Test each cell individually by touching the probes to the front and back busbars of that cell. A cell that reads 0 volts is dead or has a broken connection.

To measure current, set the DMM to DC amps (10A range). Connect the probes across the string's positive and negative ends. You should get a short-circuit current (Isc) of about 4 to 8 amps for a typical 5-inch cell, depending on the cell's rating. But be careful: do not leave the string shorted for more than a few seconds. The cell will heat up. Use a solar irradiance meter if available, or test at solar noon on a clear day. If irradiance is lower, current will be proportionally lower.

A simple test jig: place the string on a non-conductive board, connect the ends to a multimeter with alligator clips. Take readings every hour to see how output changes with sun angle. For a 6-cell string, if you get 3.2V and 5A, that's about 16 watts. Multiply by the number of strings to estimate total panel power. But remember, in a full panel, cells are connected in series to increase voltage, and strings are connected in parallel to increase current.

If a string fails, re-check each solder joint. Use a magnifying glass to look for cracks. Sometimes a cell is cracked but still works; those cracks can cause hot spots later. Mark any suspect cells and replace them. Keep a logbook of voltage and current for each string. In African workshops, where grid power is unreliable, a good multimeter is essential. A basic model like the DT-830B costs about $10 and is sufficient. Always test before encapsulatingonce the panel is laminated, repairs are impossible.

4. Arranging Strings into a Matrix Layout

A matrix layout means arranging multiple strings in a grid to get the desired voltage and current. For a 12V panel, you need 36 cells. If each string has 6 cells (3.0V), you need 6 strings in series to get 18V (open-circuit). But that's too high; actually, 36 cells in series gives about 21V open-circuit, which is fine for a 12V battery via a charge controller. So for a 12V panel, you connect all 36 cells in seriesone long string. But that's not a matrix; it's a single string. A matrix comes when you have multiple parallel strings to increase current.

For a larger panel, say 100W, you might use 36 cells of 5-inch size (each about 2.5W). But 36 cells in series gives about 90W. To get more power, you use bigger cells or more cells. For a 200W panel, you might use 72 cells in series (24V system). The matrix layout is about physically placing strings side by side. Typically, you arrange strings in rows. For example, 6 strings of 12 cells each: connect each string in series (12 cells = 6V), then connect the 6 strings in series to get 36V. Or connect them in parallel to get higher current.

Physically, lay the strings on a flat surface, like a large table or a clean floor covered with a protective sheet. Leave 5-10 mm gaps between strings for expansion. Align the busbars so you can easily interconnect them with bus ribbons. Use tape to hold strings in place temporarily. For a standard 60-cell panel, the layout is 6 columns by 10 rows. But in small workshops, you might do 4 strings of 9 cells (36 cells). The key is to keep all strings parallel and evenly spaced.

Plan your layout on paper first. Draw the cells and number them. Decide where the positive and negative terminals will be. Usually, you want the terminals at one edge for easy wiring to the junction box. Use a marker to label the ends of each string. In dusty environments, cover the layout with a cloth when not working. A good matrix layout reduces wiring complexity and prevents short circuits.

5. Interconnecting Strings with Bus Ribbons

Once your strings are laid out, you need to connect them together using bus ribbons (wider tabbing wire, usually 5-6 mm wide). Bus ribbons carry higher current than the thin tabbing wire between cells. For series connections, you connect the positive end of one string to the negative end of the next. For parallel connections, you connect all positives together and all negatives together.

Start by cutting bus ribbons to length. For a series connection between two strings, measure the distance between the end of one string and the start of the next, add 20 mm for overlap. Solder one end of the bus ribbon to the last cell's back busbar of string 1, and the other end to the first cell's front busbar of string 2. Use a soldering iron with a wider tip (6 mm) and more solder. The joint must be strong because it carries the full current of the string.

For parallel connections, you need a bus bar (a thick copper strip) or a heavy bus ribbon. Solder all positive ends of the strings to one common bus, and all negative ends to another. Keep the bus bars separated to avoid short circuits. Use heat shrink tubing or electrical tape to insulate exposed bus ribbons. In many African workshops, you can make bus bars from flattened copper pipe or heavy gauge wire.

After interconnecting, test the whole matrix with a multimeter. Check voltage across the main positive and negative terminals. For a 36-cell series matrix, expect about 21V open-circuit. Check current by shorting the terminals briefly. If current is lower than expected, check for bad solder joints or reversed strings. Use a clamp meter to measure current without disconnecting.

Finally, secure the bus ribbons with tape or silicone adhesive to prevent movement. Avoid sharp bends that could break the ribbon. In humid areas, coat the solder joints with silicone or epoxy to prevent corrosion. Remember, a poor interconnection can cause resistance, heat, and fire risk. Take your time and test at each step.

6. Handling and Transporting Completed Strings

Completed strings are fragile. A single crack in a cell can reduce output or cause a hot spot. Always handle strings by the edges, never by the cells themselves. Wear cotton gloves to avoid oils. When lifting a string, support it from underneath with a flat board or a stiff plastic sheet. Never bend or flex the string. Even a slight bend can crack cells.

For transport within your workshop, use a custom carrier. A simple carrier: a wooden board with shallow grooves or foam strips to hold each string separately. Place strings face down (front side down) on soft foam. Do not stack strings directly on top of each other. If you must stack, place a soft foam sheet between each string and limit the stack to 3-4 strings. Use a box with dividers for moving multiple strings.

If you are transporting strings to a different location, secure them in a padded box. Wrap each string in bubble wrap or foam. Mark the box "FRAGILE - SOLAR CELLS". Avoid exposing strings to vibration, extreme heat, or moisture. In hot African climates, do not leave strings in a closed vehicle in the suntemperatures can exceed 60C, which can degrade the encapsulant later. Keep them in the shade.

Before storing, test each string again to ensure no damage occurred during handling. Store in a dry, clean place. If you are not encapsulating immediately, keep strings in a dust-free cabinet. Dust can settle on the cells and reduce efficiency. Cover with a clean cloth.

When moving a string from the layout table to the laminating area, use two people if the string is longer than 1 meter. One person supports each end. Never lift a string by the tabbing wiresthey can tear off. With proper handling, your strings will arrive at the next stage ready for encapsulation. Remember, a cracked cell is a lost cell. Treat each string like a pane of glass.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Stringing and Matrix Layout for Solar Panel Assembly

In an African solar assembly workshop whether in Lagos, Nairobi, Accra, or a rural mini-grid hub the difference between a panel that lasts 20 years and one that fails in 18 months is almost always decided at the stringing and matrix layout stage. This practical reference gives technicians, workshop supervisors, and small-scale manufacturers the hands-on standards, tolerances, and business considerations needed to assemble reliable solar strings and matrices from individual cells.

Stringing is the process of connecting individual solar cells in series to build voltage. Matrix layout is the arrangement of multiple strings into a panel-level array that delivers the target power. Both steps demand precision, consistent materials, and a clear understanding of how electrical output translates into customer value and warranty risk.

Why Stringing Discipline Matters in African Workshops

Most solar panels sold in African markets are imported, but a growing number of local assemblers from Kenya's Nakuru solar cluster to Nigeria's Lagos clean energy startups are building panels from imported cells. The business case is strong: local assembly reduces shipping costs, creates jobs, and allows customization for off-grid and mini-grid customers. But the technical risks are real. A single misaligned cell in a string can reduce panel output by 515%, and poor bus ribbon connections are the leading cause of field failures in locally assembled panels.

Stringing is not just a technical step it is a quality control gate. Every string that leaves the soldering station must be tested for voltage and current before it enters the matrix. Skipping this step to save time is the fastest way to lose a customer, a warranty claim, or a reputation.

Aligning Cells for Series Stringing

Cells must be aligned with consistent spacing typically 24 mm between cells to allow for thermal expansion and encapsulation. Use a stringing jig or alignment template. In low-resource workshops, a simple wooden or acrylic template with cell-sized cutouts works well. Align busbars (the silver strips on the cell surface) so they face the same direction for every cell in the string.

Key alignment checks:

  • All cells face the same direction (front/back orientation).
  • Busbar positions are consistent across the string.
  • Spacing between cells is uniform (24 mm).
  • No cell is cracked, chipped, or has damaged busbars.

Soldering Cells in Series to Form Strings

Use tabbing ribbon (typically 1.52 mm wide, 0.150.2 mm thick, coated with SnPb or lead-free solder). Soldering irons should be temperature-controlled at 350400C for lead-free solder. Apply flux sparingly. Solder one tab at a time, moving from cell to cell in a consistent direction.

Common soldering defects to avoid:

  • Cold joints (dull, lumpy appearance) reheat and reflow.
  • Excess solder bridging between busbars clean and redo.
  • Overheating cells can cause micro-cracks and efficiency loss.
  • Misaligned tabs causes mechanical stress during lamination.

In African workshops where ambient temperatures can exceed 35C, soldering iron recovery time is slower. Use a higher-wattage iron (6080W) and keep the tip clean. Train technicians to solder in short bursts rather than long holds.

Testing String Voltage and Current Output

Every completed string must be tested under standard test conditions (STC: 1000 W/m, 25C cell temperature, AM1.5 spectrum) or as close as possible using a solar simulator or outdoor sunlight at solar noon. For workshop testing, a simple multimeter and a resistive load can verify open-circuit voltage (Voc) and short-circuit current (Isc).

Arranging Strings into a Matrix Layout

A matrix layout arranges multiple strings in parallel or series-parallel to achieve the desired panel voltage and current. For a typical 12V off-grid panel, you might use 36 cells in series (one string) or 2 strings of 18 cells in parallel. For a 24V panel, use 72 cells in series or 2 strings of 36 cells in parallel.

Layout considerations:

  • Keep strings physically separated to avoid shading and thermal hotspots.
  • Route bus ribbons along the back of the panel to the junction box.
  • Maintain symmetry mismatched string lengths cause current imbalance.
  • Leave space for bypass diodes (typically 3 per panel).

Interconnecting Strings with Bus Ribbons

Bus ribbons (also called busbars or interconnect ribbons) carry current from string ends to the junction box. Use wider ribbon (35 mm) for higher-current paths. Solder connections must be mechanically strong and electrically low-resistance. Avoid sharp bends they can crack the ribbon or damage cells.

Best practices:

  • Pre-tin both ribbon and connection point before joining.
  • Use a heat sink or wet sponge to protect cells from heat.
  • Inspect every joint under magnification look for voids and cold solder.
  • Strain-relieve ribbons with tape or adhesive before lamination.
SAFETY WARNING Soldering and Electrical Hazards

Soldering irons operate at 350400C and can cause severe burns. Always use heat-resistant gloves, eye protection, and work in a well-ventilated area solder fumes contain flux vapors that are harmful with prolonged exposure. In African workshops with limited ventilation, use a fume extractor or work outdoors under shade. Never solder near flammable materials (e.g., dry grass, plastic sheeting, or fuel containers). When testing strings, cover panels or disconnect from sunlight to avoid live voltage a 72-cell string can produce over 40V DC, which is dangerous. Use insulated tools and never touch exposed busbars with bare hands. Keep a fire extinguisher (CO or dry powder) within 3 meters of the soldering station.

Handling and Transporting Completed Strings

Completed strings are fragile. Cells can crack under mechanical stress, and solder joints can break if strings are bent or dropped. Handle strings by the edges or with a vacuum pick-up tool. Never stack strings directly on top of each other use foam or paper separators.

For transport within the workshop or to a lamination station:

  • Use rigid trays or cardboard carriers with cell-sized slots.
  • Label each string with its measured Voc and Isc.
  • Avoid exposing strings to direct sunlight for extended periods heat can degrade encapsulant and solder.
  • In dusty or humid environments, cover strings with clean plastic sheeting.

For businesses selling assembled panels, document string test results and include them in the warranty record. This builds customer trust and provides evidence in case of a warranty claim.

Business Considerations for African Assemblers

Stringing and matrix layout directly affect your cost per watt and your warranty liability. A well-trained technician can string 36 cells in 4560 minutes. Poorly soldered strings may pass initial testing but fail in the field after 612 months, leading to costly replacements and reputational damage.

Invest in:

  • Temperature-controlled soldering stations (not cheap fixed-temperature irons).
  • String testing equipment (multimeter, solar simulator or outdoor test jig).
  • Training for technicians on IPC-A-610 or equivalent soldering standards.
  • Quality documentation for every panel traceability from cell batch to finished product.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Verify all cells are uncracked and busbars are intact before stringing.
  2. Use an alignment jig maintain 24 mm spacing between cells.
  3. Solder at 350400C with controlled iron; avoid cold joints and overheating.
  4. Test every string for Voc and Isc before matrix assembly.
  5. Reject any string with >5% deviation from expected voltage.
  6. Route bus ribbons without sharp bends; strain-relieve before lamination.
  7. Handle strings by edges only; use trays for transport.
  8. Document test results for every string and panel for warranty traceability.
