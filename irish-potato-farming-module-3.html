1. Certified Seed Potato Sources and Grades

Certified seed potatoes are your best bet for a healthy crop. In Africa, you can buy certified seed from national agricultural research stations, licensed seed multipliers, or agro-dealers registered with the national seed certification service. For example, in Kenya, the Kenya Plant Health Inspectorate Service (KEPHIS) certifies seed potatoes. In Uganda, the National Agricultural Research Organisation (NARO) and its partners produce certified seed. Always ask for the certification label, which shows the variety, generation, and certifying authority. Grades of seed potatoes are based on size and generation. Common grades are: Grade A (28-35mm), Grade B (35-45mm), and Grade C (45-55mm). Smaller tubers are cheaper but may yield less; larger tubers cost more but can be cut to plant more area. Generation refers to how many times the seed has been multiplied from the original disease-free stock. Generation 1 (G1) is the first multiplication and is most expensive but has the highest health and vigour. G2, G3, and G4 are progressively cheaper but carry higher risk of disease accumulation. For most smallholder farmers, G2 or G3 offers a good balance of cost and performance. Avoid buying seed from open markets or from other farmers unless you are sure it is disease-free. Such seed is often infected with viruses or bacterial wilt, which can wipe out your entire crop. When buying, inspect the tubers: they should be firm, sprouted slightly (eyes just waking up), and free from rot, cracks, or insect damage. Buy from a reputable source and keep the receipt. If you can, visit the seed farm to see how the potatoes are grown. Remember, investing in certified seed may cost more upfront, but it pays off with higher yields and better quality potatoes. Plan ahead: order your seed at least two months before planting to ensure you get the variety and grade you want.

2. Seed Potato Storage and Cutting Techniques

Proper storage of seed potatoes keeps them healthy until planting time. After buying certified seed, store them in a cool, dark, and well-ventilated place. Ideal temperature is 4-8C for long storage, but in most African farms without cold rooms, you can use a dark shed with good airflow. Spread the tubers in thin layers (not more than two tubers deep) on slatted wooden trays or in mesh bags. This prevents sweating and rot. Avoid direct sunlight, which makes tubers green and toxic. Also keep away from rats and other pests. If you store in a sack, open the mouth and turn the sack occasionally. Check regularly and remove any rotten tubers immediately. Cutting seed potatoes is a common practice to increase planting material, but it must be done correctly. Cut large tubers (over 45mm) into pieces, each with at least two healthy sprouts or eyes. Use a clean, sharp knife. Dip the knife in a disinfectant (like diluted bleach or methylated spirits) between cuts to avoid spreading disease. After cutting, allow the pieces to dry in a shaded, ventilated area for 1-2 days so the cut surface suberizes (forms a protective skin). You can dust the cut pieces with wood ash or a recommended fungicide to prevent rot. Do not cut small tubers (under 35mm); plant them whole. Cutting increases the risk of disease, so only cut healthy, certified seed. If you have enough whole seed, plant whole tubersthey perform better. For storage before cutting, keep tubers in sprouting conditions: diffuse light and 10-15C for 2-3 weeks to encourage short, strong sprouts. This is called chitting. Chitting gives the crop a head start. In warm climates, store seed in a cool room or evaporative cooler. Never store potatoes near fruits like bananas or apples; they release ethylene gas that causes premature sprouting and ageing.

3. Disease-Free Seed Selection

Diseases are the biggest threat to potato farming in Africa. Using disease-free seed is the first line of defense. The main seed-borne diseases are bacterial wilt, blackleg, common scab, and viruses like potato leafroll virus and potato virus Y. These can reduce yields by 50-100%. To select disease-free seed, start by buying certified seed from a trusted source. But even certified seed can sometimes be infected, so inspect carefully. Look at the tubers: healthy seed potatoes are firm, with smooth skin and no blemishes. Avoid tubers with sunken dark spots (blackleg), brownish rot (bacterial wilt), or rough, corky patches (scab). Also reject tubers with holes (insect damage) or those that are soft and shriveled. If you cut a tuber and see brown rings or discoloration inside, do not plant itit may have bacterial wilt or brown rot. For viruses, tubers may look normal but still carry the virus. Thats why certification and generation matter: lower generations have less virus. You can also do a simple test: plant a few sample tubers in a pot and observe the plants. If they show mosaic patterns, leaf rolling, or stunting, the seed is infected. Another method is to use tissue culture-derived seed (minitubers) which are virus-free but expensive. For most farmers, the best strategy is to buy G2 or G3 certified seed, rogue out diseased plants in the field, and never save seed from a crop that had disease. Also practice crop rotation: dont plant potatoes in the same field for at least 3 years. This reduces soil-borne diseases. Clean your tools and boots before moving between fields. If you see a diseased plant, remove and burn it immediately. Do not compost it. By being strict about seed health, you protect your investment and ensure a good harvest.

4. Seed Treatment and Sprouting Practices

Seed treatment protects your seed potatoes from soil-borne diseases and pests after planting. Before planting, you can treat whole or cut seed with a fungicide like mancozeb or a bio-fungicide such as Trichoderma. Mix according to label instructions and dip or dust the tubers. For cut seed, allow the cut surface to dry first, then treat. Some farmers use wood ash, which has mild antifungal properties. For insect control, especially against potato tuber moth, you can dust with diatomaceous earth or use a recommended insecticide. Always wear gloves and a mask when handling chemicals. After treatment, plant immediately or store in a cool, dark place for no more than a few days. Sprouting, or chitting, is the practice of encouraging seed potatoes to produce short, strong sprouts before planting. This gives them a head start and can increase yields by 10-20%. To chit, place seed potatoes in a single layer in a shallow tray or box. Keep them in a cool, light place (10-15C) with indirect sunlight. In 2-3 weeks, they will develop sprouts about 1-2 cm long. The sprouts should be green or purple, not white and longwhite sprouts mean the tubers were kept in the dark and are weak. If sprouts are too long, you can rub them off and start again. For cut seed, chitting is not recommended because the pieces may rot. Instead, plant cut pieces directly after suberization. In warm African climates, you can chit in a shaded, ventilated shed. Avoid direct sun, which can burn the sprouts. Some farmers use a sprouting bed: a layer of sand or sawdust where tubers are placed and kept moist. This is called sprouting in beds and is common in Kenya and Ethiopia. Once sprouts are ready, handle tubers carefully to avoid breaking them. Plant them sprout-up, about 5-10 cm deep. With proper treatment and sprouting, your potatoes will emerge faster and stronger.

5. Handling and Transport of Seed Potatoes

Seed potatoes are fragile. Rough handling can bruise them, leading to rot and poor sprouting. When moving seed potatoes, always use clean crates or sacks. Do not throw or drop them. Bruised tubers are entry points for bacteria and fungi. If you must transport them, pack them in layers no more than 30 cm deep to avoid crushing the bottom ones. Use a vehicle with good ventilation. Avoid transporting in the heat of the day; early morning or evening is better. Never transport seed potatoes together with fresh produce like tomatoes or bananas, as ethylene gas from those fruits can cause premature sprouting and ageing. Also keep away from chemicals, fuels, and fertilizers. If you are buying seed from a distant source, arrange for quick transport. The longer they are in transit, the more they deteriorate. Upon arrival, unpack immediately and store in a cool, dark, ventilated place. Inspect for damage during transit: remove any crushed or rotten tubers. If you are transporting cut seed, it is best to plant within 24 hours. If not possible, keep them in a cool, humid place and plant as soon as you can. For large quantities, use a refrigerated truck if available, but that is expensive. A simpler method is to transport at night when temperatures are lower. In many African countries, seed potatoes are moved in open pickup trucks. Cover the load with a breathable tarpaulin to protect from sun and rain, but do not seal it airtight. Also, secure the load to prevent shifting. When loading and unloading, use a ramp or lift, not throwing. Train your workers on gentle handling. Remember, every bruise reduces your yield. Proper handling from farm to field ensures your seed arrives healthy and ready to grow.

6. Calculating Seed Potato Requirements

To avoid buying too much or too little seed, you need to calculate how many seed potatoes you need for your field. The amount depends on the variety, seed size, spacing, and whether you cut the seed. Start by knowing your field size. For example, if you have 1 hectare (10,000 square meters), and you want to plant at a spacing of 75 cm between rows and 30 cm between plants, you need about 44,444 plants per hectare (10,000 / (0.75 x 0.30) = 44,444). Now, if you plant whole tubers of grade B (35-45mm), each tuber weighs about 50-80 grams. Lets say average 60 grams. Then you need 44,444 x 60 grams = 2,666,640 grams, which is about 2.67 tonnes of seed per hectare. If you cut large tubers, you can reduce the seed rate. For example, if you cut grade C (45-55mm) tubers into two pieces, each piece weighs about 30-40 grams. Then you need about 1.5 tonnes per hectare. But cutting increases risk, so many farmers plant whole seed. A common recommendation in East Africa is 2-2.5 tonnes per hectare for whole seed. For small plots, say 0.1 hectare (1000 square meters), you need 200-250 kg of whole seed. To be precise, weigh a sample of your seed: count 100 tubers and weigh them. Then calculate average tuber weight. Multiply by the number of plants needed. Also account for 5-10% extra for losses during handling and planting. If you are buying in bags, know the weight per bag. For example, a bag of 50 kg. So for 0.1 hectare, you need 4-5 bags. Always buy a little more than you need. Store extra properly. If you cut seed, you need fewer tubers, but you must cut correctly. Use this formula: Seed rate (kg/ha) = (Plant population x average tuber weight in grams) / 1000. Then add 10% for safety. This calculation helps you budget and plan. Dont guessmeasure your field and weigh your seed.

7. Practical Reference


Practical Reference: Seed Potato Selection and Handling



Seed potato is the single largest variable cost in a potato enterprise typically 4050% of total production costs in African smallholder and commercial systems. A farmer who pays 30% more for certified seed but gains 40% in marketable yield has made a profitable decision. A farmer who plants cheap, diseased, recycled seed at half the price and loses 60% of the crop has made a ruinous one. This practical reference consolidates the commercial rules of thumb, calculations, and handling standards you need to buy, store, cut, treat, sprout, and transport seed potatoes profitably in African conditions.





Certified Seed Potato Sources and Grades in Africa



Africa's certified seed potato supply is dominated by a handful of institutional and commercial producers. Knowing where to buy is a business decision as much as an agronomic one. The main sources are:




  • National agricultural research systems (NARS): KALRO (Kenya), NARO (Uganda), RICA (Rwanda), EIAR (Ethiopia), and similar bodies produce pre-basic and basic seed, often through tissue culture labs and screenhouses. Prices are subsidised but volumes are limited and allocations are competitive.

  • Commercial seed companies: Firms such as Kisima Farm (Kenya), Molo Potato Growers (Kenya), and private producers in South Africa, Egypt, and Morocco supply certified C1 and C2 seed at market prices. Delivery timelines and minimum order quantities apply.

  • Farmer seed cooperatives: Groups like the Kenya National Potato Farmers Association and similar cooperatives in Rwanda and Uganda bulk-buy certified seed and redistribute. Quality assurance varies insist on inspection certificates.

  • Imported seed: Netherlands, Scotland, and Egypt supply certified seed to North and East Africa. Import costs are high but genetic quality is reliable. Used mainly for variety introduction, not routine planting.



Grade definitions follow the international generation system. Pre-basic is the first field generation from tissue culture. Basic (G1G3) follows. Certified C1 and C2 are the commercial generations. In most African markets, C1 and C2 offer the best balance of cost and yield pre-basic and basic are too expensive for ware production, while recycled ware seed carries unacceptable disease risk.

Seed Potato Storage and Cutting Techniques



Seed potato storage is where many African farmers lose value before planting. The three enemies are light, heat, and moisture. Store seed in a cool, dark, well-ventilated space at 48C if cold storage is available, or in a shaded, ventilated barn at 1015C for short-term storage (24 weeks). Never store in direct sunlight or in airtight sacks tubers will sprout prematurely or rot.



Cutting techniques: Cutting large seed tubers into pieces reduces seed cost per hectare but increases disease entry risk. If you cut:



  • Cut only healthy, disease-free tubers. Discard any with soft rot, blackleg, or visible lesions.

  • Ensure each piece has at least 23 viable eyes.

  • Cut into blocky pieces, not thin slices thin slices desiccate and rot.

  • Disinfect the knife between tubers using 70% alcohol or a 1% bleach solution.

  • Allow cut pieces to suberise (form a protective skin) in a shaded, humid place for 2448 hours before planting.

  • Treat cut surfaces with fungicide (e.g., mancozeb or thiophanate-methyl) to prevent seed-piece decay.



Whole seed vs. cut seed: Whole seed tubers (4060g) are preferred where disease pressure is high, labour is scarce, or mechanised planting is used. Cut seed is common in smallholder systems where seed cost is the binding constraint. The economics depend on seed price: if certified seed costs more than USD 1.00/kg, cutting large tubers can reduce seed cost by 2030%, provided disease management is rigorous.



Disease-Free Seed Selection



Disease-free seed is not a luxury it is the foundation of yield. The main seed-borne diseases in African potato systems are bacterial wilt (Ralstonia solanacearum), blackleg (Pectobacterium spp.), common scab (Streptomyces scabies), potato virus Y (PVY), potato leafroll virus (PLRV), and late blight (Phytophthora infestans).



Selection rules:



  • Buy only from certified sources with inspection certificates. Ask for the certificate if the seller cannot produce one, walk away.

  • Inspect tubers before purchase: reject lots with more than 1% visible disease or damage.

  • Avoid tubers with blackleg symptoms (black, slimy stem end), soft rot (foul smell, mushy tissue), or scab lesions (corky, raised spots).

  • Prefer tubers of uniform size (3555mm) they emerge evenly and simplify planting.

  • For bacterial wilt, insist on seed from fields with no history of the disease. There is no chemical cure.




SAFETY WARNING Bacterial Wilt and Chemical Handling

Bacterial wilt (Ralstonia solanacearum) is untreatable and persists in soil for years. Never plant seed from a field with known bacterial wilt you will contaminate your land permanently. When handling seed treatment chemicals (fungicides, disinfectants), always wear gloves, a mask, and eye protection. Store chemicals away from food, seed, and children. Dispose of chemical containers and cut seed waste responsibly do not feed diseased tubers to livestock or dump them near water sources. In case of skin contact, wash immediately with soap and water and seek medical advice if irritation persists.




Seed Treatment and Sprouting Practices



Seed treatment: Treat seed potatoes before planting to protect against seed-piece decay and soil-borne fungi. Common treatments include:



  • Fungicide dip or dust: Mancozeb (2g/L) or thiophanate-methyl (1g/L) dip for cut seed; dust formulations for whole seed.

  • Hot water treatment: 50C for 30 minutes for seed-borne bacterial diseases but this is risky and should only be done with expert supervision.

  • Biological treatments: Trichoderma spp. and Bacillus subtilis products are increasingly available in Africa and offer disease suppression without chemical residues.



Sprouting (chitting): Sprouting seed before planting accelerates emergence by 714 days and can increase yield by 1020% in short-season environments. To sprout:



  • Place seed tubers in a single layer in a cool, light, well-ventilated room (1015C) 24 weeks before planting.

  • Ensure light reaches the tubers this produces short, green, sturdy sprouts, not long, white, fragile ones.

  • Turn tubers occasionally for even sprouting.

  • Plant when sprouts are 12cm long. Handle carefully to avoid breaking sprouts.



Handling and Transport of Seed Potatoes



Seed potatoes are living tissue. Rough handling causes bruising, which becomes entry points for rot. Transport rules:



  • Use ventilated crates or mesh bags never airtight sacks or plastic bags for long transport.

  • Stack no more than 45 crates high to prevent crushing.

  • Avoid transport in the heat of the day cover loads with a breathable tarpaulin.

  • Minimise drop heights during loading and unloading every 30cm drop increases bruising risk.

  • Transport directly to a shaded, ventilated storage point. Do not leave seed in a parked vehicle.

Calculating Seed Potato Requirements



The formula is straightforward:


Seed requirement (kg/ha) = (Plants per ha Average seed tuber weight in g) 1000



For example, 45,000 plants/ha 50g = 2,250,000g = 2,250 kg/ha. If you are cutting 80g tubers into two 40g pieces, your seed rate drops to 1,800 kg/ha a 20% saving. But if disease pressure is high, the yield loss from cut seed may exceed the seed cost saving. Run the numbers for your farm before deciding.



Business tip: Always order 10% more seed than your calculated requirement to allow for size grading losses, damage, and reject rates. If your calculated requirement is 2,250 kg, order 2,500 kg.



Quick Reference Checklist



  1. Buy certified C1 or C2 seed from a reputable source demand the inspection certificate.

  2. Reject any lot with more than 1% visible disease, damage, or off-types.

  3. Store seed in a cool (415C), dark, ventilated place never in direct sun or airtight bags.

  4. If cutting seed, disinfect knives between tubers and suberise cut pieces for 2448 hours.

  5. Treat seed with fungicide or biological agent before planting.

  6. Sprout seed in light for 24 weeks before planting to gain 714 days in the field.

  7. Transport in ventilated crates, minimise drop heights, and never leave seed in a hot vehicle.

  8. Calculate seed requirement precisely and order 10% extra to cover losses.
