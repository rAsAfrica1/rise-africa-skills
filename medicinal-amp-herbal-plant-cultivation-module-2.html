1. Testing Your Soil: Simple pH and Texture Tests for African Soils

Before you plant any medicinal herb, you need to know what kind of soil you have. Two quick testsâpH and textureâwill tell you most of what you need. For pH, you can use a simple vinegar and baking soda test. Take a handful of soil, put it in a cup, add a few drops of vinegar. If it fizzes, your soil is alkaline (pH above 7). If no fizz, take another sample, add water to make mud, then add baking soda. If it fizzes, your soil is acidic (pH below 7). No fizz either time means your soil is roughly neutral (pH 6.5â7.5), which is ideal for most herbs. For a more precise reading, buy a cheap pH test kit from an agroâdealer; they cost about 5,000â10,000 shillings and give a color chart.

Texture is about how much sand, silt, and clay your soil has. Do a jar test: fill a clear jar oneâthird with soil, top up with water, add a pinch of salt, shake hard for 3 minutes, then let it settle for 24 hours. Sand settles first (bottom layer), then silt, then clay on top. Measure each layer as a percentage. If sand is over 70%, your soil is sandyâit drains fast but loses nutrients. If clay is over 40%, itâs heavy clayâholds water but roots can rot. The best for herbs is loam: roughly 40% sand, 40% silt, 20% clay. In many African soils, youâll find sandy loam or clay loam. Knowing this tells you how to amend: sandy soil needs compost and mulch; clay soil needs sand, compost, and raised beds.

Also check for earthworms. Dig a 30 cm x 30 cm hole, count worms. If you find 10 or more, your soil is alive and fertile. Fewer than 3 means you need to add organic matter. Do these tests before preparing beds. Write down your results. For example, if your pH is 5.5 (acidic) and texture is sandy loam, youâll need to add wood ash or lime to raise pH, plus plenty of compost. If pH is 8.0 (alkaline), add compost, manure, or sulfur. These simple tests cost almost nothing and save you from crop failure.

2. Making Compost from Local Materials: Kitchen Waste, Manure, and Crop Residues

Compost is the cheapest and best fertilizer for herbal plants. 3. Creating Raised Beds and Sunken Beds for Different Climates

The type of bed you build depends on your climate and soil. In areas with heavy rainfall or poor drainage, use raised beds. In dry areas with little rain, use sunken beds. Raised beds are 20â30 cm high, 1 m wide, and any length. They warm up faster in the morning, drain excess water, and make harvesting easier. Build them by digging soil from the paths between beds and piling it on the bed. Mix in compost and sand if your soil is clay. The paths should be 30â40 cm wide. For a 1 m x 3 m bed, you need about 0.6 cubic meters of soil. Line the sides with stones, wood, or grass to prevent erosion. Sunken beds are the opposite: dig 20â30 cm deep, 1 m wide, and any length. The soil you remove is used to make a small ridge around the bed. Sunken beds catch and hold rainwater, keeping roots moist for longer. They also protect plants from hot dry winds. This is perfect for dry regions like the Sahel, Kalahari, or northern Kenya. Fill the sunken bed with a mix of soil, compost, and manure. Plant your herbs on the flat bottom. The ridges should be 10â15 cm high. Test your bed after a heavy rain: if water stands for more than 2 hours, raise it more; if it dries out in a day, sink it. Observe and adjust.

4. Using Mulch to Conserve Water and Suppress Weeds in Dry Regions

5. Natural Fertilizers: Preparing Manure Tea and Liquid Organic Fertilizers

Liquid fertilizers give your herbs a quick boost. Manure tea is the easiest. Fill a sack (burlap or woven polypropylene) with 10 kg of fresh animal manureâcow, goat, chicken, or rabbit. 6. Crop Rotation and Intercropping with Legumes for Soil Health

Crop rotation means changing what you plant in a bed each season. This prevents pests and diseases from building up, and it balances soil nutrients. Different herbs take different nutrients. For example, leafy herbs like basil and mint need lots of nitrogen. Root herbs like ginger and turmeric need potassium and phosphorus. Intercropping is growing two or more crops together. Legumes are perfect companions for herbs. Plant cowpea, groundnut, or lablab between your herb rows. They shade the soil, suppress weeds, and add nitrogen. For example, plant basil with cowpea. The cowpea grows tall and fixes nitrogen; the basil grows bushy and uses that nitrogen.
