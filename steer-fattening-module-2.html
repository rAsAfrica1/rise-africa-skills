1. Criteria for Selecting Steers for Fattening

When selecting steers for fattening, your goal is to buy animals that will gain weight quickly and efficiently. The best candidates are young, intact males (steers) that have been castrated early, typically before 6 months of age. Look for steers with a good appetite and a calm temperament. Age matters: steers between 12 and 24 months old are ideal because they are still growing and will put on muscle and fat rapidly. Avoid very old steers (over 3 years) as they convert feed less efficiently. Weight should be between 250 kg and 400 kg at purchase, depending on breed and target market. In Africa, common fattening breeds include Boran, Sahiwal, and crossbreeds with exotic breeds like Holstein or Angus. These breeds respond well to improved feeding. Health status is critical: only buy steers that are alert, have bright eyes, and no signs of diarrhea or coughing. Check for lameness and abscesses. Also consider the source: steers from known farms with vaccination records are safer than those from open markets where disease risk is higher. Finally, assess body condition: steers with a body condition score (BCS) of 2 to 3 on a 1-5 scale (where 1 is emaciated and 5 is obese) are ideal. They have some fat cover but are not overly fat, so they will gain efficiently. Avoid extremely thin steers (BCS 1) as they may have health issues and require costly recovery. Selecting the right steers at the start saves you money on feed and veterinary bills later.

2. Evaluating Body Condition and Frame Size

Body condition scoring (BCS) is a hands-on method to assess fat cover on a steer. Use a 1-5 scale: 1 = emaciated (bones prominent, no fat), 2 = thin (some fat over ribs, but ribs easily felt), 3 = moderate (ribs felt with slight pressure, fat cover over tailhead), 4 = fat (ribs hard to feel, fat deposits visible), 5 = obese (heavy fat cover, mobility issues). For fattening, aim for steers with BCS 2 to 3. They have enough energy reserves to start gaining but are not too fat, which would reduce feed efficiency. To check BCS, run your hands over the ribs, spine, and tailhead. Feel for the amount of fat. Also assess frame size: steers with a larger frame (taller, longer body) can grow to heavier weights before finishing. Frame score is often estimated by hip height at a given age. For example, a 12-month-old steer with a hip height of 120 cm is considered medium-frame; 130 cm is large-frame. In Africa, many local breeds are smaller-framed, but crossbreeds can be larger. Choose steers with a frame size that matches your market: if you sell to local butcheries, a 350-400 kg finished weight is fine; for premium markets, you may need 450-500 kg, requiring larger-framed steers. Also evaluate muscle thickness: look at the width of the stance, the roundness of the hindquarters, and the thickness of the forearm. A steer with good muscle and moderate fat will yield more meat. Remember, frame size and body condition together determine how long you need to feed and the final weight. Always handle the steers before purchase to evaluate both.

3. Assessing Health and Soundness

A healthy steer is a profitable steer. Before buying, inspect each animal carefully. Start with the eyes: they should be bright, clear, and free of discharge. Cloudy eyes or excessive tearing can indicate pinkeye or respiratory disease. Check the nose for discharge; a runny nose with coughing suggests pneumonia. Listen for breathing: normal breathing is quiet and regular. Rapid or labored breathing is a red flag. Examine the mouth for sores or excessive salivation, which could be foot-and-mouth disease. Look at the coat: it should be smooth and shiny. A dull, rough coat often means internal parasites or poor nutrition. Check the skin for lumps, wounds, or abscesses. Any open wound can become infected and reduce weight gain. Next, assess mobility: have the steer walk on a flat surface. It should move freely without limping. Lameness can be caused by foot rot, abscesses, or injuries, and will reduce feed intake. Inspect the hooves for cracks or swelling. Also check the anus for signs of diarrhea, which could be due to worms or bacterial infections. Finally, look at the overall posture: a healthy steer stands alert with head up. A depressed steer with droopy ears and head down is likely sick. In Africa, common diseases to watch for include East Coast fever (caused by ticks), trypanosomiasis (spread by tsetse flies), and foot-and-mouth disease. If possible, ask for vaccination records. If buying at a market, avoid animals that have been transported long distances and are stressed. Quarantine new steers for at least 14 days before mixing with your herd. During quarantine, observe them daily and treat any health issues immediately. A thorough health check saves you from buying a problem that will cost more than the steer itself.

4. Genetic Considerations and Expected Progeny Differences

Genetics determine how fast a steer grows, how efficiently it converts feed, and the quality of its meat. When selecting steers, you cannot change their genetics, but you can choose animals with superior genetic potential. In Africa, indigenous breeds like Boran, N'Dama, and Ankole have good heat tolerance and disease resistance but slower growth. Crossbreeds with exotic breeds like Angus, Hereford, or Simmental can grow faster and reach heavier weights. If you are buying steers for fattening, look for crossbreeds that combine local hardiness with exotic growth. Expected Progeny Differences (EPDs) are tools used by breeders to predict the genetic merit of an animal as a parent. For fattening steers, you are not breeding, but EPDs can still indicate the steer's own genetic potential for growth. Key EPDs include birth weight (lower is better for calving ease, but not relevant for steers), weaning weight (higher means faster early growth), yearling weight (higher means faster overall growth), and carcass weight (higher means more meat). If you buy from a registered breeder, ask for EPDs. For example, a steer with a yearling weight EPD of +30 kg is expected to be 30 kg heavier at one year than the breed average. However, EPDs are only useful if the breeder has accurate records. In many African markets, such data is scarce. Instead, you can assess genetics by looking at the steer's parents if available, or by choosing steers from herds known for good growth. Also consider hybrid vigor (heterosis): crossbred steers often outperform purebreds in growth and health. For small-scale fatteners, a simple strategy is to buy crossbred steers from reputable farms. Avoid mixing breeds with different mature sizes if you want uniform batches. Remember, genetics set the ceiling for performance; nutrition and management determine how close you get to that ceiling.

5. Purchasing Strategies: Auctions, Direct Sales, and Contracts

Where you buy your steers affects price, health, and convenience. Three common methods in Africa are auctions, direct sales, and contracts. Auctions: Livestock auctions are held in many towns. You can buy steers at competitive prices, but there are risks. Animals may be stressed from transport and mixing, increasing disease risk. You also have limited time to inspect. To succeed at auctions, arrive early, inspect animals before the sale, set a maximum price, and stick to it. Check for auctioneer fees (often 5-10% of the sale price). Direct sales: Buying directly from a farmer or feedlot allows you to inspect the animals in their home environment. You can negotiate price, ask about health and feeding history, and build a relationship. This is often the best option for beginners. Visit the farm, check records, and observe the steers. Agree on a price per kilogram live weight or per head. For example, in Kenya, a 300 kg steer might sell for KES 200-250 per kg live weight (about USD 1.50-2.00). Contracts: Some large feedlots or meat processors offer contract farming. You agree to raise steers to a certain weight and they buy at a predetermined price. This reduces market risk but may lock you into a low price. Contracts often require you to follow specific feeding and health protocols. Read the fine print. For all methods, ensure you get a receipt and health certificate if possible. Also consider transport costs: buying from a distant market may be cheaper but add transport and stress. Always quarantine new arrivals for 14 days. If buying in bulk, try to source from a single herd to reduce disease spread. Finally, timing matters: prices are often lower during dry seasons when farmers sell off animals. Buy then and fatten during the wet season when feed is cheaper.

6. Transport and Handling of Newly Purchased Steers

Transport is a critical period that can make or break your fattening operation. Stress during transport leads to weight loss, disease, and even death. Plan ahead to minimize stress. Before loading, ensure steers are healthy and have been off feed for 6-8 hours (but have access to water). This reduces defecation and urine in the truck. Use a well-ventilated truck with non-slip flooring. Cover the floor with sand or wood shavings for grip. Load steers calmly; shouting and hitting will stress them. Use flags or sticks to guide them. Do not overload: allow about 1.5 to 2 square meters per adult steer. For a 300 kg steer, that's roughly 1.5 m. If traveling long distances (over 4 hours), stop every 4-6 hours to check animals and offer water. In hot African climates, transport during early morning or evening to avoid heat stress. Never transport in the heat of the day. If possible, use a truck with a roof for shade. During the journey, avoid sudden braking and sharp turns. Upon arrival, unload gently into a clean, dry quarantine pen. Provide fresh water and good quality hay. Do not feed concentrates immediately; wait 12-24 hours to avoid digestive upset. Observe for signs of stress: rapid breathing, drooling, or collapse. If any steer shows these, consult a vet. Keep new steers separate from your herd for at least 14 days. During quarantine, monitor temperature and appetite daily. Treat for internal and external parasites as advised by a vet. Vaccinate if not already done. Handle steers quietly and patiently; they learn routines. Use a race or crush for restraint when needed. Remember, good handling reduces bruises and injuries, which lowers meat quality. A calm steer gains weight faster. So invest time in proper transport and handling; it pays off in better health and performance.

<h2>Practical Reference: Selection, Evaluation and Purchase of Steers for Fattening</h2>

<p>In African beef finishing enterprises, profit is decided long before the animal enters the feedlot. The price paid per kilogram of live weight, the growth potential of the animal, its health status on arrival and the cost of getting it to the pen together determine whether a 90120 day fattening cycle returns a margin or a loss. This practical reference consolidates the working criteria used by commercial steer finishers in Southern, East and West Africa: how to score body condition and frame, how to judge soundness and health, how to read Expected Progeny Differences (EPDs) where they exist, how to buy through auctions, direct sales and contracts, and how to transport and handle newly purchased steers so that weight loss and mortality stay low.</p>

<p>Treat every purchase as a business transaction with a defined target: a steer that will gain 0.81.2 kg per day on a ration costing a known amount per kilogram of gain, reaching a target carcass or live weight within a defined feeding window. Selection is simply the process of buying the animals most likely to hit that target.</p>

<h2>Criteria for Selecting Steers for Fattening</h2>

<p>Selection criteria should be written down and applied consistently. The following are the practical, high-impact variables:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Age and dentition:</strong> Steers with two permanent incisors or fewer (approximately 1830 months) generally convert feed most efficiently. Very young steers need longer finishing periods; old steers with a full mouth (six permanent incisors) gain fat rather than muscle and often fail to meet premium specifications.</li>
<li><strong>Weight and frame:</strong> Buy at a weight that allows 90150 days of gain to reach your market weight. A 250320 kg steer is typical for intensive finishing in Africa; heavier entry weights shorten the cycle but reduce compensatory growth.</li>
<li><strong>Body condition:</strong> Target a Body Condition Score (BCS) of 2.53.0 on a 15 scale at purchase. Thin animals (BCS 2) can show compensatory gain, but only if healthy; fat animals (BCS 45) are expensive and inefficient to finish.</li>
<li><strong>Frame size:</strong> Larger-framed steers reach heavier final weights but need more feed and time. Match frame to your market: local butcher markets reward moderate frame and early maturity; export or premium markets may reward larger frame.</li>
<li><strong>Muscling and conformation:</strong> Look for a long, deep body, well-developed hindquarters and a wide stance. Avoid narrow, shallow-bodied animals.</li>
<li><strong>Health and soundness:</strong> Clear eyes, moist muzzle, no lameness, no abscesses, no diarrhoea, no signs of respiratory distress.</li>
<li><strong>Uniformity:</strong> Buy in uniform groups where possible. Uniform pens are easier to feed, market and manage, and reduce competition at the trough.</li>
<li><strong>Origin and traceability:</strong> Where possible, buy from known sources with vaccination and treatment records. This reduces the risk of introducing disease and improves market access.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Evaluating Body Condition and Frame Size</h2>

<p>Body condition scoring and frame assessment are the two fastest field tools for valuing a steer. BCS is assessed by hand and eye over the ribs, spine, hips and tailhead. Frame size is assessed by height at the withers, body length and leg length relative to body depth.</p>

<p>In field practice, run your fingers over the short ribs and the spine. If the ribs are sharp and the spine prominent, the steer is BCS 2 or below. If the ribs are smooth and covered, and the spine is felt only with light pressure, the steer is BCS 3, the ideal starting point for finishing. If the ribs cannot be felt and the tailhead is buried in fat, the steer is BCS 45 and will be expensive to finish.</p>

<h2>Assessing Health and Soundness</h2>

<p>Health assessment at purchase is non-negotiable. A steer that arrives sick will cost you feed, drugs, labour and lost gain. Use a structured check:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Eyes and muzzle:</strong> Bright, clear eyes; moist muzzle; no discharge.</li>
<li><strong>Breathing:</strong> Quiet, regular respiration; no coughing or laboured breathing.</li>
<li><strong>Gait:</strong> Walk the animal. No lameness, no swollen joints, no stiffness.</li>
<li><strong>Skin and coat:</strong> Smooth coat; no lumps, abscesses, wounds or external parasites.</li>
<li><strong>Dung:</strong> Well-formed; no diarrhoea or blood.</li>
<li><strong>Body temperature:</strong> Normal rectal temperature is approximately 38.039.3 C in cattle.</li>
<li><strong>Vaccination and treatment history:</strong> Ask for records of FMD, anthrax, blackleg and brucellosis vaccination where relevant.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Genetic Considerations and Expected Progeny Differences</h2>

<p>In Africa, structured EPDs are available mainly for registered breeds and some commercial programmes (e.g. Boran, Bonsmara, Nguni, Afrikaner, and increasingly for Brahman and composite breeds). Where EPDs are available, prioritise the following:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Weaning weight and yearling weight:</strong> Higher values indicate faster growth, but balance against mature size and feed cost.</li>
<li><strong>Feed efficiency or residual feed intake (RFI):</strong> Where available, low RFI animals eat less for the same gain.</li>
<li><strong>Carcass traits:</strong> Marbling, ribeye area and fat thickness where the market rewards them.</li>
<li><strong>Birth weight:</strong> Important if you breed your own replacements; less relevant for purchased steers.</li>
<li><strong>Adaptation traits:</strong> Tick and heat tolerance, and resistance to internal parasites, are economically important in tropical Africa.</li>
</ul>

<p>Where EPDs are not available, use breed and crossbreed knowledge, visual appraisal and the reputation of the source herd. Crossbred steers (e.g. Boran Friesian, Nguni Charolais) often show hybrid vigour and better growth than pure local breeds, but require better nutrition and health management.</p>

<ol>
<li>Set a maximum purchase price per kg live weight before you enter the market.</li>
<li>Score BCS (target 2.53.0) and assess frame size and muscling on every animal.</li>
<li>Check dentition: prefer steers with two permanent incisors or fewer.</li>
<li>Inspect eyes, muzzle, breathing, gait, skin and dung; reject sick or lame animals.</li>
<li>Ask for vaccination and treatment records; prefer known source herds.</li>
<li>Buy uniform groups to simplify feeding and marketing.</li>
<li>Plan transport: correct loading density, ventilation, water stops and cool hours.</li>
<li>Quarantine and adapt new arrivals for 1421 days before full finishing ration.</li>
