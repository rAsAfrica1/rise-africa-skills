Module 2

Buying Store Cattle

Most of the profit in a fattening batch is decided on the day you buy, before a single kilogram of feed is eaten. This module teaches you which breeds and crosses in the region actually respond to a finishing ration, how to age an animal by its teeth, how to score body condition and frame, how to walk a market and reject the animals that will cost you money, and how to move stock legally and safely so it arrives ready to eat.

What you will be able to do after this module

  • You will be able to name the main breed types available in your region and say which suit a short finishing period.
  • You will be able to age cattle by dentition and explain why age matters for feed conversion.
  • You will be able to score body condition on a 1 to 5 or 1 to 9 scale and judge frame size independently of fat.
  • You will be able to run a structured 10-point inspection on a store animal before bidding.
  • You will be able to spot signs of illness and the warning signs of stolen or undocumented stock.
  • You will be able to plan transport and an arrival protocol that reduces shrink and sickness.

Lesson 2.115 min

Breeds and Crosses: What Actually Finishes Well in the Region

In this lesson
  • Describe the main breed groups available regionally
  • Match breed type to your feed quality and target market
  • Avoid paying a premium for a name rather than performance

Breed is not magic. A well fed indigenous steer will beat a badly fed exotic cross every time. But breed does shift two things that matter to you: the rate at which an animal converts feed into gain, and the frame size it can reach before it starts laying down fat. Understanding the groups helps you predict how an animal will behave on your ration.

The main groups you will meet

  • Indigenous Sanga and Zebu types. Tuli, Mashona and Nguni in southern Africa; Boran and East African Zebu in East Africa; White Fulani, Sokoto Gudali and Red Bororo in West Africa. These animals are hardy, tick and heat tolerant, walk well and forage well. They typically have smaller mature frames, which means they finish at lighter weights and start depositing fat sooner. On a modest ration in a hot climate they are often the most reliable choice, and they generally suit local butcher markets well.
  • Improved tropical beef breeds. Boran selected lines and Tuli selected lines fall here, as do Brahman and Brahman crosses. Brahman influence brings heat and tick tolerance with a larger frame than most local Zebu. Brahman crosses are very common in commercial feeding across the region.
  • Exotic beef breeds and their crosses. Hereford, Angus, Simmental, Charolais, Beefmaster, Bonsmara and Santa Gertrudis. Bonsmara and Beefmaster were bred specifically for African conditions and carry both frame and adaptation. Pure European types generally have the highest growth potential on a good ration but the least tolerance of heat, ticks and poor feed.
  • Dairy and dairy cross males. Friesian and Ayrshire bull calves and crosses. These are often cheap to buy, which is genuinely attractive, but they carry less muscling, dress out at the lower end of the range, and can be discounted by buyers who want a beef carcass. They can work if the price gap is large enough and your buyer does not penalise conformation.

The practical decision rule

Match frame to ration. A large framed animal on a poor ration simply grows slowly and never finishes, and you pay for the extra days. A small framed animal on a rich ration lays down fat quickly and reaches finish early, which is fine if you sell early.

  1. Good concentrate supply, buyer pays for weight and finish: medium to large frame - Brahman crosses, Bonsmara, Simmental crosses, improved Boran.
  2. Modest ration built on crop residues and some bran: medium frame indigenous or indigenous cross. Do not buy big frames you cannot fill.
  3. Hot, tick heavy, limited veterinary access: favour indigenous or Brahman influenced cattle for adaptation, even at some cost in growth rate.

Crossbred vigour

Crosses between an adapted local type and a beef breed - for example a Brahman or Bonsmara cross over a local cow - commonly outperform either parent type for growth under local conditions because of hybrid vigour combined with adaptation. In practice, well grown crossbred steers are often the best value in regional markets, because they perform like an improved animal but are not priced like a pedigree one.

What to avoid paying for

  • A name without evidence. Being told an animal is a Bonsmara cross means nothing without the animal in front of you showing the frame and muscling. Judge the animal, not the label.
  • Colour. Coat colour is not a performance trait. Some markets do pay a colour preference; know your market, but do not confuse preference with productivity.
  • Bulls when you want steers. Entire bulls grow faster but are harder and more dangerous to handle in a small pen, may fight, and in some markets are discounted. For a smallholder pen, steers are usually the safer choice. If you buy bulls, get castration done properly and early by someone competent, and understand that castrating a large animal is a real setback and a welfare and infection risk - discuss it with a vet.

Sex and class

Steers are the standard fattening animal. Cull cows can be fattened profitably where they are cheap and the market accepts manufacturing beef, but they gain more slowly than young steers and their gain is more fat than lean. Heifers finish at lighter weights than steers of the same breed and lay down fat earlier.

Lesson 2.214 min

Age and Dentition: Reading the Mouth

In this lesson
  • Age cattle from the incisor teeth
  • Explain why younger animals convert feed better
  • Use dentition to detect a misrepresented animal

A seller will tell you an animal's age. The animal's mouth will tell you the truth. Dentition is the single most reliable field method for aging cattle, it costs nothing, and every serious buyer in the region uses it.

How the mouth works

Cattle have eight incisor teeth in the front of the lower jaw only. There are no upper incisors - just a hard dental pad. A calf grows eight small, narrow, white milk teeth. As the animal matures, these are replaced from the centre outward by permanent incisors, which are noticeably broader, squarer and slightly more yellow than the milk teeth they replace.

The progression

MouthDescriptionApproximate age
Milk teethAll eight small and narrowUnder about 18 to 24 months

Treat these as approximate ranges, not exact ages. Eruption is earlier in well grown animals on good nutrition and later in animals that have been stunted, and it varies by breed. Grazing on sandy soils wears teeth faster, so a full mouth animal from a sandy area can look older than it is.

How to check a mouth safely

  1. Restrain the animal properly. Use a crush or race. Never put your hand in the mouth of a loose animal.
  2. Stand to the side, not directly in front.
  3. Part the lips at the front of the lower jaw with your thumb and fingers - you do not need to open the jaw at all. The incisors are visible with the lips simply pushed apart.
  4. Count the broad permanent teeth. Look also at wear, gaps and any missing teeth.

Why age matters to you

  • Feed conversion. Younger growing animals convert feed to gain more efficiently, sitting nearer the better end of the roughly 6 to 10 kg dry matter per kg gain range. Older animals sit nearer the poor end and lay down proportionally more fat.
  • Growth remaining. A two tooth steer still has frame growth left, so a good part of his gain is muscle and skeleton. A full mouth animal is mostly done growing, so his gain is mostly fat.
  • Carcass acceptability. Many markets and grading systems discount older carcasses for toughness. Where a grading system exists in your country, dentition is often part of it - find out exactly how your buyer grades.
  • Days to finish. A younger animal in good health usually gives you a longer productive feeding window before conversion collapses.

The sweet spot for fattening

For a 90 to 120 day finishing batch, animals in the two tooth to six tooth range are the usual target: old enough to have frame and to handle a concentrate ration, young enough to still convert well. Very young weaners can be fattened but often need a longer growing phase first and a more carefully balanced protein supply. Very old cull animals can still be profitable when bought cheaply for the manufacturing market, but do not expect good conversion and do not plan a long feeding period on them.

Catching a misrepresentation

If a seller says an animal is 18 months and the mouth shows six broad teeth, either the seller is wrong or the animal is not the one described. Either way, adjust your price or walk away. Equally, an animal claimed to be a well grown two year old that still has all milk teeth and a small frame is likely a stunted animal - it may still fatten, but the ration must fix a growth deficit first, and it will not give you a finished carcass in 90 days.

Check the rest of the mouth too

While you are there, look at the tongue and check the animal can chew normally. Broken or badly worn molars, or pain on chewing, will cap intake no matter how good your ration is, and an animal that cannot eat cannot be fattened.

Lesson 2.316 min

Frame Size and Body Condition Scoring

In this lesson
  • Score body condition using bone cover
  • Judge frame size separately from fatness
  • Combine the two to pick the ideal store animal

Frame and condition are two different things, and confusing them is the most expensive beginner mistake in a cattle market. Frame is the size of the skeleton - it does not change much once the animal is grown. Condition is the fat and muscle covering that skeleton - it changes all the time, and it is what you are paid to add.

Body condition scoring

Condition scoring is done by feeling and looking at how much cover sits over specific bones. Different countries use a 1 to 5 scale or a 1 to 9 scale. Both work; use whichever your local extension service uses so you can talk to people. Here is the 1 to 5 version.

ScoreWhat you see and feel
1 - EmaciatedRibs, hips, pins and backbone sharply visible. No fat anywhere. Animal looks angular and weak.
2 - ThinRibs and hips clearly visible, backbone prominent, but the animal is alert and moving well.
3 - ModerateRibs felt with light pressure but not obvious to the eye. Hips visible but rounded. Smooth overall appearance.
4 - GoodRibs hard to feel. Fat felt over the tailhead and brisket. Body looks blocky and full.
5 - FatBone structure buried. Soft fat pads at tailhead, brisket and flank. Movement may look heavy.

Where to look and feel

  1. Ribs. The clearest indicator. Can you see them from a few metres? Can you feel them with light pressure?
  2. Backbone and loin. Run a hand along the top line. Sharp and knobbly means thin; smooth and level means covered.
  3. Hooks and pins. The hip bones and the bones either side of the tail. Sharply pointed means thin; rounded and covered means conditioned.
  4. Tailhead. The hollows either side of the tail root. Deep hollows mean thin; filled or padded means fat.
  5. Brisket and flank. Fat fills the brisket in front and the flank behind. These fill last and are a good sign of true finish.

The store animal target

For fattening you want an animal in the range of about condition score 2 to 3 - thin but healthy. That is the whole trick. You are paying for frame and buying the right to add the condition yourself.

  • Score 1 is a trap. An emaciated animal may be sick, parasitised or too old, and it will spend your first month recovering rather than gaining. Some very thin animals do show good compensatory growth, but distinguishing a nutritionally thin animal from a diseased one takes experience - if in doubt, do not buy.
  • Score 2 to 3 is your buy zone. Healthy, alert, good frame, clearly under-conditioned, plenty of room to gain.
  • Score 4 to 5 is money already spent. Someone else added that fat and you are paying for it. There is little gain left to capture, and a fat animal converts feed poorly.

Judging frame independently

Look at the animal from the side and from behind, ignoring the flesh:

  • Height at the hip and shoulder. Tall for its age means large frame.
  • Length of body. A long animal from shoulder to pin bone carries more valuable cuts.
  • Width across the hooks and pins. Width of skeleton predicts how much muscle can be carried.
  • Bone in the leg and depth through the chest. Heavy flat bone and a deep chest suggest a frame that will support real weight.

A useful mental test: picture the animal with an extra 120 kg on it. If the frame looks like it could carry that weight and still look proportionate, the frame is big enough. If it would look grotesque, the animal is small framed and will finish early at a light weight.

Combining the two

Your ideal store animal is large frame for its price bracket, low condition, high health. The animal that looks slightly disappointing - big, rangy, thin, but bright eyed and moving well - is usually the one that makes the money. The animal that looks lovely and round is usually the one that makes the seller money.

Beware gut fill

An animal that has just drunk deeply and eaten a full belly of green grass looks heavier and fuller than it is. Judge condition over the bones, not by belly size. If you buy on liveweight, ask about gut fill and when the animals last ate and drank, since gut fill can move recorded liveweight noticeably.

Lesson 2.416 min

The Ten Point Inspection: Judging a Store Animal

In this lesson
  • Run a repeatable inspection in the right order
  • Apply hard rejection rules before bidding
  • Set a walk-away price before the auction starts

In a busy market you have minutes, not hours, and the seller controls the conversation. A fixed inspection order protects you: you check the same things in the same sequence on every animal, so you never forget the important one because you were distracted by a nice colour.

Start at a distance

Before you touch anything, stand back about ten metres and simply watch the animal for a full minute. This is the step most buyers skip and it catches the most problems.

The ten points, in order

  1. Movement. Watch it walk. It should move freely with even weight on all four feet, head carried normally. Any limp, stiffness, dragging toe or reluctance to move is a reject until proven otherwise.
  2. Alertness. Ears up and moving, head responding to sounds and to you. A dull animal standing apart from the group with a dropped head is a sick animal.
  3. Breathing. Watch the flank. Breathing should be quiet and even. Rapid, laboured or noisy breathing, or coughing, suggests respiratory disease - a serious risk in a pen because it spreads.
  4. Eyes and nose. Bright clear eyes. No cloudiness, no ulcer, no thick discharge. Nose moist and clean; a thick yellow or bloody discharge is a reject.
  5. Coat and skin. Coat should be reasonably smooth and shiny for the season. Staring, rough, dull coat suggests parasites or poor nutrition. Look for lumps, wounds, abscesses, mange patches, and check the heavy tick sites - ears, tail switch, under the tail, brisket, udder or scrotum area.
  6. Rear end. Lift the tail area with your eyes, not your hand. Scouring, dirty hindquarters, or blood indicate gut disease or heavy worms. A clean tail is a good sign.
  7. Mouth and teeth. Age by dentition as taught in Lesson 2. Check the animal can chew.
  8. Feet and legs. Look at each hoof for overgrowth, cracks, swelling above the hoof, or heat. Foot problems cap intake because a sore animal will not walk to the trough, and they are slow and costly to fix.
  9. Frame and condition. Apply Lesson 3. Large frame, condition 2 to 3, room to grow.
  10. Temperament. Watch how it behaves in the pen and when approached. A wild, panicky or aggressive animal is dangerous in a small pen, injures itself and others, eats poorly and stresses the whole group. Calm animals gain better.

Hard rejection rules

Write these on a card and keep it in your pocket. No exceptions on market day, when excitement clouds judgement.

  • Any lameness.
  • Coughing, laboured breathing or nasal discharge.
  • Scouring or a soiled, wet hindquarter.
  • Eye ulcer, cloudiness or heavy discharge.
  • Open wound, large abscess or swollen joint.
  • Standing alone, dull, head down, not chewing cud when the rest are.
  • Condition score 1 with no clear feeding explanation.
  • Wild or aggressive temperament.
  • Any animal whose paperwork or ownership you cannot confirm.

Bulls, cryptorchids and old castrations

Check whether an animal is actually a steer. Feel and look at the scrotal area. An incompletely castrated animal, or one with a retained testicle, behaves like a bull - restless, mounting, fighting - and disrupts a small pen. If you suspect an incomplete castration, get a vet to examine the animal, and do not attempt surgery yourself.

Set your price before the bidding

Work backwards from what you can afford to pay:

  1. Estimate the animal's likely sale weight: current weight plus 1.0 to 1.5 kg/day times your planned days on feed. Use the conservative end.
  2. Estimate sale value using the price you were actually quoted by three buyers, not a hoped-for price.
  3. Subtract your full feed, health, water, labour and selling cost.
  4. Subtract the margin you require.
  5. What remains is your maximum purchase price. That is your walk-away line.

Write that number down before the auction starts and do not move it. Auctions are designed to make you move it. There is always another animal next week; there is not always another batch of capital.

Lesson 2.515 min

Avoiding Sick Stock and Stolen Stock

In this lesson
  • Recognise disease risk in a market environment
  • Complete the ownership and permit checks correctly
  • Set up quarantine for new arrivals

Two problems can end a fattening business in a single day: buying disease into your pen, or buying an animal that turns out to belong to someone else. Both are avoidable with discipline, and both are easier to prevent than to fix.

Why markets are high risk for disease

A livestock market mixes animals from many herds, stresses them with transport and handling, and holds them in crowded pens. Stress suppresses immunity, and mixing exposes every animal to pathogens it has never met. Animals that looked fine in the market can break down with respiratory disease in the following one to two weeks. That pattern is well recognised in feedlots worldwide.

Reducing the risk at purchase

  • Buy from fewer sources. Animals from one farm of origin carry far less risk than the same number bought from six different sellers. Where you can, buy direct from a known farmer rather than through a mixed market.
  • Ask what has been done. What vaccinations, when, by whom? What dipping or tick control, and when? Any treatments in the past month? Get names and dates. A seller who cannot answer at all is telling you something.
  • Watch the whole group, not just your animal. If several animals in the pen are coughing or scouring, assume your animal has been exposed even if it looks well today.
  • Prefer animals that have not travelled far or long. Every extra hour in transit adds stress and shrink.

Notifiable and regional disease

Some diseases in the region are controlled by law and may make movement illegal or require permits - foot and mouth disease control zones are the obvious example in southern Africa, and tick borne diseases such as East Coast fever, anaplasmosis, babesiosis and heartwater are major regional risks. Contamination of a batch by lumpy skin disease or by a serious respiratory outbreak is a vet matter, not a home remedy matter. Know your local rules before you buy, and involve your veterinary officer early.

Ownership: do not buy stolen cattle

Cattle theft is common across the region and the penalties for handling stolen stock can fall on the buyer. Being told a good story is not a defence.

  1. Check the brand or mark. Confirm the animal carries the seller's registered brand or ear mark, and that it matches the paperwork.
  2. Check the movement permit or stock clearance certificate. Most countries in the region require a permit or a police clearance to move cattle. Confirm the document is current, matches the number and description of animals, and names the seller.
  3. Confirm the seller's identity. Match the name on the permit to identification. Note the name and contact details of the seller in your record book.
  4. Insist on a receipt. Date, seller name and identity number, number of animals, descriptions and brands, price, and both signatures.
  5. Prefer formal channels. A recognised auction or a sale routed through the local livestock office gives you a paper trail that a roadside deal never will.

The warning signs

  • Pressure to complete quickly, or to buy at night or away from the market.
  • Price clearly below the market for an animal in obvious good condition.
  • A brand that appears altered, blotched or freshly burned over.
  • No permit, or a permit whose description does not match the animal in front of you.
  • A seller who will not give a name, address or receipt.

Any one of these is enough to walk away. Report suspicions to your local livestock or police office - protecting the community from theft also protects your own herd.

Quarantine your arrivals

Even careful buying does not guarantee healthy stock, so build the last line of defence into your yard. Hold every new arrival separate from any existing animals for about two to four weeks, with its own trough, its own water, and ideally handled last in your daily routine so you do not carry infection back to the settled group. Watch every animal every day for coughing, scouring, dullness or not eating. If several animals go off feed or start coughing, call a vet promptly rather than treating blindly - the wrong medicine wastes money, delays the right treatment and can leave residues that block sale.

Lesson 2.615 min

Transport and Arrival

In this lesson
  • Load and transport cattle to minimise shrink and injury
  • Run a structured arrival protocol
  • Start rumen adaptation correctly from day one

Transport is short and cheap compared with feeding, so it is easy to treat it carelessly. That is a mistake. Poor transport costs weight, causes bruising that downgrades carcasses, and triggers the disease that shows up in week two. What you do in the first 72 hours after arrival shapes the whole batch.

Shrink: the weight that disappears

Cattle lose weight during transport and handling. Part is gut fill and water, which comes back within a day or two of eating and drinking normally. Part is real tissue loss, which does not come back for days. Shrink increases with the length of the journey, with heat, with rough handling and with time off feed and water. Two practical consequences:

  • If you buy on liveweight, know where and when the weighing happened. An animal weighed after a long journey weighs less than the same animal weighed off feed at the farm gate.
  • Take your own arrival weight and use it as your batch starting weight. Do not compute gain from the market weight or you will flatter yourself in week one and disappoint yourself later.

Loading and the journey

  1. Load in the cool of the day where you can, early morning or late afternoon.
  2. Use a proper loading ramp at a sensible slope with solid sides. Most transport injuries happen at loading.
  3. Move animals quietly. No sticks, no shouting, no dogs, no twisting tails. Frightened cattle slip, fall and bruise, and bruised meat is trimmed off and paid for by you.
  4. Load at the correct density: tight enough that animals cannot fall and be trampled, loose enough that every animal can stand normally. If an animal goes down, stop and get it up.
  5. Non slip flooring. Sand, gravel or bedding over a bare metal deck.
  6. Do not mix horned and polled animals, or a bull with steers, in the same compartment.
  7. Drive gently. Cornering and braking cause more falls than speed does.
  8. For long journeys, plan rest, feed and water in line with your national animal welfare and transport regulations. Know the rules for your country.
  9. Do not transport an animal that is lame, sick, heavily pregnant or unable to bear weight. That is both a welfare failure and, in most countries, illegal. If an animal is injured in transit, get veterinary attention.

Arrival: the first hours

  1. Unload calmly into a clean, prepared pen with dry footing and shade available.
  2. Water first. Give clean water immediately and in generous supply. Thirsty cattle will drink before they eat, and water needs are substantial and rise with temperature and dry feed intake. Make sure every animal can find the trough - a new arrival that has only known a river or a dam may not recognise a small trough, so let the water run so it makes noise.
  3. Offer good quality roughage, not concentrate. Hay or a familiar roughage for the first day or two. A stressed rumen fed grain immediately is a recipe for acidosis.
  4. Rest. Leave the animals alone for the rest of the day. No weighing, no injections, no dosing. Handling a just-arrived animal adds stress you do not need.

Day two to three: processing

  • Weigh each animal and record the arrival weight against its number.
  • Apply or check ear tags so every animal has an identity.
  • Deworm and apply tick control appropriate to your area.
  • Vaccinate as advised for your district. Which vaccines are needed varies enormously by country and by disease zone. Ask your veterinary officer what your area requires - this is a decision to make with a vet, not from a book.
  • Record every product, dose, date and withdrawal period in the health record.

Starting rumen adaptation

Cattle need rumen adaptation to a high concentrate diet over roughly 14 to 21 days. The rumen microbes that digest grain must build up in number, and the rumen wall must adapt, or the animal risks acidosis - a serious and sometimes fatal condition.

The principle: start on mostly roughage, then step the concentrate up in small increments every few days, holding the level whenever intake wobbles. Never make a large jump. Watch for the warning signs - animals off feed, loose bubbly manure, kicking at the belly, reduced cud chewing. If those appear, pull the concentrate back and, if animals are seriously affected, call a vet.

Watch every animal, every day

For the first two weeks, walk the pen twice daily and check that every single animal comes to feed and is chewing cud when resting. The one that hangs back is the one to look at closely today, not next week.

Knowledge check

Five questions. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. You are buying store cattle to fatten for 100 days. Which animal is the best buy?

You want to pay for frame and add the condition yourself. A fat animal means someone else already captured the gain, lameness caps intake, and a dull emaciated animal is likely sick rather than just underfed.

2. An animal has three pairs of broad permanent incisors. What is its approximate age class?

Three broad pairs is a six tooth mouth, roughly 30 to 36 months. These are approximate ranges, since eruption varies with nutrition and breed.

3. Your cattle arrive after a four hour journey. What should you do first?

Water and roughage first, then rest. Processing waits until day two or three, concentrate must be introduced gradually over 14 to 21 days, and new arrivals should be quarantined separately for about 2 to 4 weeks.

4. Which of these is a legitimate reason to walk away from an otherwise good looking animal at a market?

An altered brand with no movement permit is a clear theft warning sign, and penalties can fall on the buyer. Insist on a permit, matching brand, identity check and a signed receipt.

5. Why does the course recommend taking your own arrival weight rather than using the market weight?

Cattle lose gut fill and some tissue during transport. Using the market weight as your start point flatters early gain figures and makes your batch records unreliable.

Module 2 capstone

Attend one market or farm sale as an observer only, with no money to spend. Run the ten point inspection and dentition check on at least five animals, score them for frame and condition, write down the price you would have paid and why, then compare your figure to what each animal actually sold for.
