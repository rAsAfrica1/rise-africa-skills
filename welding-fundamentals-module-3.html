1. SMAW Principles and Equipment Setup

Shielded Metal Arc Welding (SMAW), often called stick welding, is the most common welding process in African workshops because it is cheap, portable, and works outdoors. You need a welding machine (AC or DC), electrode holder, ground clamp, welding cables, electrodes, and safety gear. In Nigeria, a 200-amp AC machine like the 'Oxford' or 'Lincoln' is common; in Kenya, many use 180-amp DC inverters. Set up your machine on a stable surface, away from moisture. Connect the ground clamp to your metal table or workpiececlean the contact point with a wire brush to get good conductivity. Plug the electrode holder into the positive terminal (DCEP) for most electrodes, but check the electrode packet; some run better on negative (DCEN).

Select your electrode based on the metal thickness. For general work, use E6013 (1/8 inch or 3.2 mm) for thin sheets (1-3 mm) and E7018 (1/8 inch) for thicker plates (6 mm and up). Set your amperage: for 3.2 mm E6013, start at 90-110 amps on AC. If the electrode sticks, increase amps by 10; if it burns through, decrease by 10. Always wear a welding helmet with at least shade 10, leather gloves, and a leather apron. In dusty African conditions, keep your machine covered when not in use and blow out dust weekly.

Before striking an arc, practice your stance: stand comfortably, hold the electrode holder like a pencil, and rest your free hand on a stable surface. Turn on the machine and listen for the hum. Touch the electrode to the metal and quickly lift to create the arc. The arc should sound like frying bacon. If you hear a loud crackle, your amps are too high. If it's silent, amps are too low. Always work on a clean surfacegrind off rust, paint, and oil. In rural areas, use a generator if mains power is unreliable, but ensure it delivers at least 5 kW for a 200-amp machine. Remember: proper setup prevents 80% of welding defects.

2. Electrode Classification and Selection

Electrodes are classified by a code like E6013. The 'E' stands for electrode. The first two digits (60) indicate the minimum tensile strength in thousands of pounds per square inch (psi)60,000 psi. The third digit (1) tells you the welding position: 1 means all positions (flat, horizontal, vertical, overhead), 2 means flat and horizontal only. The fourth digit (3) indicates the coating type and current: 3 means rutile coating, usable on AC or DC. For example, E6013 is a general-purpose electrode for all positions, great for beginners. E7018 has 70,000 psi strength, all-position, and a low-hydrogen coating that requires DC or AC, and must be kept dry.

In Africa, the most available electrodes are E6013 and E7018. E6013 is forgiving, easy to strike, and gives a smooth bead, but it leaves slag that must be chipped off. E7018 produces stronger welds and less slag, but it is more sensitive to moisturestore it in a sealed container with silica gel. If you are welding mild steel up to 6 mm thick, E6013 in 3.2 mm diameter is ideal. For structural work like gates or trailers, use E7018 in 3.2 mm or 4.0 mm.

Electrode size matters: 2.5 mm for thin sheets (1-2 mm), 3.2 mm for 3-6 mm, and 4.0 mm for 6-10 mm. Smaller electrodes need lower amps. For 2.5 mm E6013, set 60-80 amps; for 4.0 mm, set 120-160 amps. Always match the electrode to the metal thickness and joint type. For vertical-up welding, use E6013 or E7018 with a slight weave. For overhead, use lower amps and a short arc.

When buying electrodes in local markets, check for cracks or damaged coatingreject them. Store in a dry place; if damp, bake E7018 at 250C for 2 hours before use. In humid coastal areas like Mombasa or Lagos, keep electrodes in an airtight container with a 40-watt bulb to keep them warm. Never use rusty or wet electrodes; they cause porosity and weak welds. Start with E6013 to build skill, then move to E7018 for critical jobs.

3. Striking and Maintaining an Arc

Striking an arc is the first skill you must master. There are two methods: scratch start and tap start. For scratch start, drag the electrode across the metal like striking a matchquickly, then lift to about 3 mm. For tap start, tap the electrode straight down and lift immediately. Both work, but scratch start is easier for beginners. Use a piece of scrap metal to practice 50 times. Set your machine to 90-110 amps for a 3.2 mm E6013 electrode. Put on your helmet, then strike. You should see a bright arc and hear a steady crackle.

Once the arc is lit, maintain it. Keep the arc length equal to the electrode diameterfor a 3.2 mm electrode, keep a 3.2 mm gap between the electrode tip and the molten pool. If the arc is too long, it sputters, wanders, and causes spatter. If too short, the electrode sticks. Adjust by moving your hand closer or farther. The sound should be like bacon frying. If it sounds like a machine gun, you are too close; if it hisses, too far.

Maintaining the arc requires steady hand movement. As the electrode melts, you must feed it down at the same rate. Use both hands if needed: one to hold the holder, the other to steady your wrist. For long welds, rest your elbow on a table or use a support. In Africa, where power can fluctuate, keep an eye on the arcif it dims, your voltage dropped; stop and wait for stable power.

When you finish a weld, break the arc by quickly lifting the electrode. This leaves a crater. To avoid a crater crack, do a small backstep: reverse your travel direction for 10 mm before lifting. Practice striking and maintaining the arc on a flat plate until you can run a 100 mm bead without sticking. If the electrode sticks, release the holder immediately, let it cool, then twist it free. Never pull the electrode out while the machine is onyou can damage the holder. With 30 minutes of daily practice, you will strike an arc reliably within a week.

4. Basic Bead Running and Travel Speed

A bead is a single pass of weld metal. To run a bead, strike an arc, then move the electrode along the joint at a steady speed. For a 3.2 mm E6013 electrode at 100 amps, your travel speed should be about 150-200 mm per minute. If you move too fast, the bead is thin and narrow, with poor penetration. If too slow, the bead is wide and tall, with excess metal and possible burn-through. The correct speed gives a bead that is about 2-3 times the electrode diameter widefor 3.2 mm, aim for 6-10 mm wide.

Start with a flat plate. Clean it with a grinder or wire brush. Strike your arc at the edge, then move in a straight line. Keep a consistent angle: 10-15 degrees drag (tilted slightly away from the direction of travel). Watch the molten poolit should be a bright, fluid puddle. If the pool looks too liquid, you are moving too slow; if it looks sluggish, too fast. For a neat bead, use a slight side-to-side motion (weaving) about 3 mm wide. But for beginners, a straight drag is easier.

In Africa, many welders use a 'whipping' motion: move forward 10 mm, back 5 mm, forward 10 mm. This helps control heat on thin metal. But for thick plates, a steady drag is better. Practice on 6 mm mild steel. Run 10 beads side by side, each 100 mm long. Let each cool, then chip off slag with a chipping hammer and brush with a wire brush. Inspect: a good bead has fine ripples, even width, and no holes.

Adjust your travel speed based on the weld: for vertical-up, move slower and use a triangle weave; for overhead, move faster and use lower amps. Remember, travel speed affects penetration. Too fast = lack of fusion; too slow = burn-through. A good rule: if the slag is easy to remove and the bead looks like a row of dimes, your speed is right. If slag is stuck, you moved too slow. Practice 2 hours daily for a week, and you will run consistent beads.

5. Arc Length and Angle Control

Arc length is the distance from the electrode tip to the workpiece. For SMAW, the correct arc length is equal to the electrode diameter. For a 3.2 mm electrode, keep a 3.2 mm gap. If the arc is too long, you get spatter, a wide bead, and poor penetration. If too short, the electrode sticks and the arc stutters. To control arc length, listen to the sound: a steady crackle means correct; a popping sound means too long; a silent arc means too short. Practice by holding the electrode at different heights and noting the sound and bead appearance.

Angle control is equally important. The electrode angle has two parts: travel angle and work angle. Travel angle is the tilt along the direction of travel. For flat welding, use a 10-15 degree drag angle (electrode tilted back). For vertical-up, use a 5-10 degree push angle (tilted forward). Work angle is the tilt across the joint. For a T-joint, angle the electrode 45 degrees between the two plates. For a butt joint, keep it perpendicular (90 degrees) to the plates.

In Africa, many welders use a 'drag' technique for flat and horizontal, and a 'push' for vertical-up. But the key is consistency. Use both hands: one to hold the holder, the other to steady your wrist. Rest your elbow on a table or use a clamp to hold the workpiece. For pipe welding, angle changes as you go aroundpractice on a rotating pipe.

To improve, set up a T-joint of 6 mm plates. Weld a 100 mm bead with a 45-degree work angle and 10-degree drag. Then change to 60-degree work angle and see the difference. A good weld has equal leg lengths and a slightly convex bead. If the bead is lop-sided, your work angle is off. If the bead is too wide, your arc length is too long. Always keep the arc tightlong arcs cause porosity. Practice 30 minutes daily with different angles, and you will develop muscle memory.

6. SMAW Safety and Troubleshooting

Safety first: SMAW produces intense UV light, sparks, and fumes. Always wear a welding helmet with shade 10-13, leather gloves, a leather apron, and closed shoes. Never weld in shorts or open sandals. Use a welding screen or curtain to protect others from arc flash. In Africa, many workshops are open-air, so ensure good ventilationfumes from E6013 and E7018 contain manganese and fluorides. If welding indoors, use a fan to blow fumes away, but not directly on the arc. Keep a fire extinguisher or a bucket of sand nearby.

Electrical safety: check cables for cracks, and never weld in wet conditions. Use a dry wooden board to stand on if the floor is damp. Ground the machine properly. If you get a shock, turn off the machine immediately and do not touch the electrode.

Common troubleshooting: If the electrode sticks, increase amps by 10 or change to a smaller electrode. If the arc wanders, your arc length is too long or the electrode coating is damaged. If the bead is porous (holes), your metal is dirty, your electrode is damp, or your arc is too long. Clean the metal and bake E7018 at 250C for 2 hours. If the weld cracks, your amps are too high or you used the wrong electrode for the steel. For mild steel, use E6013 or E7018.

If the machine hums but no arc, check the ground clamp and cable connections. If the machine overheats, let it cool for 15 minutesnever pour water on it. In dusty areas, blow out the machine weekly. If you see slag inclusions (dark spots in the weld), chip them out and re-weld. Always chip slag after each pass.

For overhead welding, use lower amps (80-90 for 3.2 mm) and a short arc. For vertical-up, use 90-100 amps and a triangle weave. Practice on scrap until you can identify problems by sound and sight. Remember: a clean workplace and sharp focus prevent 90% of accidents. Keep a first-aid kit with burn gel and eye wash. Never weld alone; have a helper nearby.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Shielded Metal Arc Welding (SMAW) in African Agribusiness Workshops

In African agribusiness, SMAW (stick welding) is the workhorse process for repairing trailers, grain augers, plough frames, feed mixers, borehole pump brackets, and irrigation pipe supports. It survives dusty conditions, unreliable power, and field repairs where gas-shielded processes fail. This practical reference gives you the numbers, settings, and decisions that keep your workshop productive and profitable.

SAFETY WARNING ARC EYE AND BURNS

Never strike an arc without a proper welding helmet (shade 1013). "Arc eye" (photokeratitis) is extremely painful and can cause permanent damage. In many African workshops, welders work in bright sunlight and underestimate the risk UV radiation from the arc is invisible and harmful even at a distance. Always use:

  • Auto-darkening or fixed-shade helmet (shade 10 minimum for 100 A, shade 1213 for 200 A)
  • Leather gloves, apron, and closed shoes no synthetic clothing
  • Fire extinguisher or sand bucket within 3 m; clear all flammable material (crop residues, fuel, oil rags)
  • Ventilation or fume extraction galvanized and painted metal produce toxic fumes
  • Insulated electrode holder and dry gloves especially on generator-powered sets in wet conditions

Business cost of ignoring safety: One arc eye injury means a welder off work for 23 days, plus medical costs. A fire in a grain store can destroy an entire season's income.

Practical Settings for Common Agribusiness Jobs

Use these starting points and adjust by sound and bead appearance. A stable arc sounds like frying bacon; a popping or sputtering sound means too long an arc or damp electrode.

  • Trailer chassis repair (6 mm steel): E7018, 3.2 mm, DCEP 110130 A, drag angle 1015, stringer beads or slight weave.
  • Irrigation pipe root (3 mm wall): E6010, 2.5 mm, DCEP 7090 A, whip technique, short arc.
  • Plough point hardfacing: E7-UM-60-G, 3.2 mm, DCEP 120140 A, two overlapping passes, no weave.
  • Feed mixer auger flighting: E6013, 3.2 mm, AC 90110 A, 45 work angle, fast travel to avoid burn-through.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Prepare the metal: Grind to bright steel within 25 mm of the joint. Remove paint, rust, oil, and galvanizing. Clean metal = sound weld.
  2. Set the machine: Match polarity and amperage to the electrode. DCEP for E7018/E6010; AC or DCEN for E6013. Check the electrode packet.
  3. Check the ground: Clamp directly to the workpiece on clean metal. A loose or dirty ground causes arc blow, sticking, and inconsistent beads.
  4. Strike the arc: Use a scratch or tap start. Do not strike on the finished weld area strike on a run-on tab or scrap plate.
  5. Maintain arc length: Keep arc length equal to the electrode core diameter (approx. 23 mm for 3.2 mm rod). Listen for a steady crackle.
  6. Control travel speed: Aim for a bead width 23 times the electrode diameter. Too fast = narrow, convex bead; too slow = wide, flat bead with undercut.
  7. Clean each pass: Chip slag, brush, and inspect before the next pass. Slag inclusions are the most common defect in multi-pass welds.
  8. Inspect and record: Check for porosity, undercut, and cracks. Record settings and electrode batch for repeat jobs this is your workshop's quality system.

Business Notes for African Agribusiness Workshops

SMAW is the most cost-effective welding process for rural and peri-urban workshops because it requires only a power source, electrodes, and a helmet. A 200 A inverter welder can run on a 5 kVA generator, making it suitable for field repairs where grid power is unreliable. Electrode storage is critical: E7018 must be kept in a heated quiver or re-baked before use. In humid coastal or tropical conditions, damp electrodes are the number one cause of porosity. Buy electrodes in sealed packets, store them in a dry cabinet with silica gel, and re-bake low-hydrogen rods at 250300C for 12 hours before critical work.

For agribusiness owners, the return on investment in SMAW training is direct: a welder who can run a consistent bead and troubleshoot defects reduces rework, extends equipment life, and can take on paid repair work for neighboring farms. A single well-repaired trailer axle can save a harvest; a hardfaced plough point can double its service life. Invest in good electrodes, a reliable ground clamp, and proper PPE these are not expenses, they are insurance against downtime.
