1. Overview of Lamb and Goat Primal Cuts

When you break down a lamb or goat carcass, you start by dividing it into primal cuts. These are the major sections that you then cut into smaller retail cuts. For both lamb and goat, the main primals are: shoulder, rack (rib section), loin, leg, and breast/forequarter. In Africa, goat is often sold as whole carcasses or in large pieces, but knowing primals helps you maximize value and cut consistently.

A typical lamb carcass weighs 18-25 kg, while a goat carcass (depending on breed and age) ranges from 10-20 kg. The shoulder is the front leg and adjacent meat; it's tough but flavorful, good for stewing or grinding. The rack is the rib section, containing 7-8 ribs; it's tender and can be roasted as a rack or cut into chops. The loin runs along the back, from the last rib to the hip; it's the most tender part, yielding loin chops and tenderloin. The leg is the hind leg, including the sirloin; it's lean and can be roasted, cut into steaks, or deboned for roasts. The breast and foreshank are bony and tough, best for slow cooking or grinding.

In many African markets, butchers often cut goat into smaller pieces for stews, but for higher-end sales, you can separate the rack and loin for grilling. Tools you need: a sharp breaking knife (6-8 inch), a boning knife, a meat saw (for splitting the carcass), a cutting board, and a scale. Always work on a clean surface, and keep your knives sharp.

Start by splitting the carcass lengthwise down the spine using a saw. Then remove the shoulder by cutting between the shoulder blade and the ribs. Next, separate the rack by cutting between the 5th and 6th rib (or according to your market preference). The loin is separated from the leg by cutting between the last lumbar vertebra and the hip bone. The leg is then separated from the sirloin if desired. Practice on a half carcass to get comfortable. In Africa, lamb is popular in North and South Africa, while goat is common across the continent. Knowing these primals allows you to sell specific cuts to restaurants and individuals who want consistent quality.

2. Breaking Down a Lamb Carcass

Breaking down a lamb carcass is a step-by-step process. You'll need a sturdy table, a meat saw, a breaking knife, a boning knife, a sharpening steel, and a meat hook. Start with a whole lamb carcass, typically 18-25 kg, hung or laid on the table. First, split the carcass into two halves by cutting along the spine with the saw. If you have a band saw, that's faster, but a hand saw works.

Lay one half on the table, skin side down. Remove the kidney and excess fat from the loin area. Then, identify the shoulder: it's the front quarter. Cut between the shoulder and the rack (rib section) by following the seam between the shoulder blade and the ribs. Use your knife to separate the shoulder, then set it aside. Next, separate the rack from the loin: count 7 ribs from the shoulder end; cut between the 5th and 6th rib (or after the 7th rib for a longer rack). The rack is now a rectangular block of ribs. Then, separate the loin from the leg: find the hip bone (pelvis) and cut just in front of it, between the last lumbar vertebra and the sacrum. The leg includes the sirloin and hind shank. Finally, trim the breast and foreshank from the rack and shoulder.

Now you have primal cuts: shoulder, rack, loin, leg, breast/foreshank. For each, you can further fabricate. For example, the shoulder can be deboned and cubed for stew, or cut into shoulder chops. The rack can be Frenched (trimming the rib bones clean) and cut into chops. The loin can be cut into loin chops or left as a roast. The leg can be bone-in or boneless, cut into steaks or roasts. In Africa, lamb is often roasted whole for celebrations, but breaking down allows you to sell premium cuts. Always keep your knives sharp and work safely. Chill the meat to 0-4Â°C before cutting for easier handling. Practice on a half carcass first, then move to whole. With experience, you can break down a lamb in under 30 minutes.

3. Cutting Lamb Chops, Racks, and Legs

Lamb chops, racks, and legs are high-value cuts. Let's start with the rack. A rack of lamb is the rib section, typically 7-8 ribs. To prepare a rack, trim the fat cap to about 1/4 inch, then French the bones: scrape the meat and fat off the ends of the rib bones about 1-2 inches down, exposing the bone. Use a boning knife. Then, cut the rack into chops: each chop should be about 1 inch thick, containing one rib bone. You can also cut double chops (2 ribs) for larger portions. For a crown roast, tie two racks together in a circle.

Loin chops come from the loin primal. Remove the tenderloin (a long, thin muscle under the spine) and set aside. Cut the loin into chops about 1 inch thick. You can bone them or leave the bone in. For a kidney chop, leave a piece of kidney attached. Leg of lamb can be sold whole, bone-in or boneless, or cut into steaks. To bone a leg, make a cut along the inside of the leg, following the bone. Use your knife to scrape the meat away from the bone, then remove the bone. You can then tie the leg into a roast or cut into cubes for kebabs. For leg steaks, cut across the grain into 1-inch thick slices.

In Africa, lamb chops are popular for grilling (braai in South Africa). Goat chops are also common in East Africa. Always cut against the grain for tenderness. Use a sharp knife and let the knife do the work. For racks, a meat saw can help cut through bone if needed, but a sharp boning knife can go between ribs. After cutting, trim excess fat and silverskin. Package chops in sets of 4-6 for retail. Store at 0-4Â°C and sell within 3-5 days, or freeze for longer. Practice cutting uniform chops for even cooking. A good butcher can get 14-16 chops from a rack and loin combined.

4. Fabricating Goat Meat for Various Cuisines

Goat meat is lean, flavorful, and widely used in African, Caribbean, and Asian cuisines. Fabricating goat is similar to lamb but with some differences: goat carcasses are smaller (10-20 kg) and the meat is leaner, so cuts may need different handling. In Africa, goat is often cut into bone-in pieces for stews, curries, and grills. Start by breaking down the carcass into primals: shoulder, rack, loin, leg, and breast. But for many African dishes, you'll cut the goat into smaller pieces: 2-3 inch chunks with bone for stews, or larger pieces for grilling.

For West African goat stew (like pepper soup), cut meat into bite-sized pieces, leaving some bone for flavor. For East African nyama choma (grilled meat), cut larger pieces like ribs, leg, and shoulder into 4-6 inch sections. For North African tagine, use shoulder and leg cut into cubes. For Caribbean curry goat, bone-in pieces are preferred. To fabricate, first split the carcass, then separate the primals. For stew meat, take the shoulder and breast, remove excess fat and silverskin, and cut into uniform cubes. For grilling, keep the rack and loin intact or cut into chops. The leg can be left whole for roasting or cut into steaks.

Tools: a cleaver is useful for cutting through bone for stew pieces. A meat saw for splitting. Always keep your knives sharp. Goat meat benefits from marinating to tenderize. In Africa, goat is often sold fresh in local markets. To maximize value, separate the tender cuts (rack, loin) for grilling and the tougher cuts (shoulder, leg) for slow cooking. Practice cutting uniform pieces for even cooking. Remember, goat has less fat, so it can dry out; leave some fat on for moisture. After cutting, package according to customer needs: stew packs, grill packs, or whole leg. Store at 0-4Â°C and use within 3-5 days, or freeze.

5. Preparing Ground Lamb and Goat

Ground lamb and goat are versatile and popular for burgers, kofta, sausages, and meatballs. To make ground meat, you need a meat grinder (manual or electric), a sharp knife, a cutting board, and a bowl. Start with meat that is cold (0-4Â°C) and has a fat content of about 20-30% for lamb, and 15-20% for goat (since goat is leaner, you may need to add fat). Use trimmings from the shoulder, breast, and leg. Remove any silverskin, gristle, and excess connective tissue. Cut meat into 1-inch cubes.

If using a grinder, assemble it with a medium die (4-5 mm holes) for a standard grind. Feed the meat cubes into the grinder, alternating with fat if needed. For a finer grind, use a smaller die. For a coarser grind, use a larger die. If you don't have a grinder, you can chop meat finely with a knife or use a food processor, but pulse to avoid making a paste. After grinding, mix in salt and spices if desired, but for plain ground meat, just mix lightly. Ground lamb is often used for kebabs (kofta) and moussaka. Ground goat is used in curries, tacos, and burgers.

In Africa, ground meat is used in dishes like kefta (North Africa), and in various street foods. To ensure food safety, keep everything clean and cold. Grind in small batches and refrigerate immediately. You can also add binders like breadcrumbs and egg for meatballs. Package ground meat in 500g or 1kg portions, flatten in bags for quick freezing. Label with date and use within 1-2 days if fresh, or freeze for up to 3 months. Always cook ground meat to an internal temperature of 71Â°C (160Â°F) to kill bacteria. Practice by grinding 2 kg of lamb shoulder with 500g of fat. Adjust fat ratio to your taste. With experience, you can create signature blends with herbs and spices.

6. Handling and Cooking Offal from Lamb and Goat

Offal includes organs and edible internal parts: liver, heart, kidneys, tongue, brains, sweetbreads (thymus/pancreas), and intestines (for sausage casings). These are nutritious and valued in many African cuisines. Handle offal with care: it spoils quickly, so keep it ice-cold and cook within 1-2 days. Start by rinsing organs in cold water. For liver, remove any green spots (gallbladder bile) and soak in milk or saltwater for 30 minutes to reduce bitterness. For kidneys, remove the outer membrane and cut out the white core. For heart, trim fat and arteries, then slice. For tongue, blanch in boiling water for 5 minutes, then peel off the skin. For intestines, clean thoroughly by flushing with water and scraping, then soak in salt and vinegar.

Cooking methods: Liver is best fried or grilled quickly (2-3 minutes per side) to avoid toughness. Heart can be stewed or grilled. Kidneys are often grilled or sautÃ©ed. Tongue is usually boiled until tender (2-3 hours), then sliced and served. Brains can be fried or poached. Sweetbreads are soaked, blanched, then sautÃ©ed. In Africa, offal is used in dishes like Nigerian pepper soup (with liver, kidney, heart), South African skilpadjies (liver wrapped in caul fat), and Moroccan tagine. For sausage casings, clean intestines and soak in saltwater, then use for making sausages.

To ensure safety, cook offal to well-done (internal temperature 71Â°C). Offal is highly perishable, so freeze if not used immediately. When selling, label clearly and educate customers on cooking. Offal can be a good source of income if marketed well. Practice by preparing a mixed offal stew: brown onions, add offal, tomatoes, and spices, then simmer until tender. Serve with pap or rice. Always maintain hygiene: use separate cutting boards for offal and other meats. With proper handling, offal can be a delicious and profitable addition to your butchery.

Breaking Down the Carcass: Step-by-Step

Start with a clean, chilled carcass (0â4Â°C). Warm carcasses tear and bruise; chilled carcasses cut cleanly and yield higher-value primal shapes. Use a bone saw for the spine and a breaking knife for muscle separation. Work from the hindquarter forward.

  1. Split the carcass down the spine into two sides. Remove the kidney knob and pelvic fat â render it or sell it as suet; do not leave it on the leg.
  2. Separate the hindquarter from the forequarter at the last lumbar vertebra (between the loin and rack). This is your first major commercial decision: a longer loin means more chops, a shorter loin means a bigger rack.
  3. Break the hindquarter into leg, loin, and flank. The leg is removed by cutting through the hip joint (ball-and-socket). Leave the shank on for a traditional bone-in leg, or remove it for shank cuts.
  4. Break the forequarter into rack, shoulder, breast, and shank. The shoulder is seam-boned: follow the natural muscle lines rather than sawing through bone. This preserves the square-cut shoulder roast that commands premium prices.
  5. Trim and portion according to your order book. French the rib bones on racks for restaurant clients; leave them untrimmed for wholesale.

Fabricating Goat for African and Export Cuisines

Goat is the most widely consumed red meat in Africa, and its fabrication follows lamb logic but with key differences: less fat cover, tighter muscle seams, and stronger bones. For West African markets, cut goat into bone-in stewing pieces (2â4 cm cubes) with the bone left in â this is what customers expect for pepper soup and jollof. For East African nyama choma trade, keep the leg and ribs whole or in large blocks for grilling. For Middle Eastern and export halal markets, fabricate into standardized 1 kg bone-in shoulder and leg bags, and 500 g mince packs.

Goat offal is a profit centre, not a waste stream. Liver, kidneys, heart, tongue, and tripe all have strong local demand. Clean tripe thoroughly with salt and lime, blanch, and sell fresh or frozen. In many markets, goat head and feet (known as mutura ingredients in Kenya or paya in South Asia) command steady prices â do not discard them.

Never handle offal (especially liver, kidneys, and reproductive organs) with bare hands if you have open cuts. Chill carcasses to below 4Â°C within 2 hours of slaughter. Clean and sanitise knives, saws, and tables between species and between offal and muscle cuts to prevent cross-contamination. In pregnant women, Toxoplasma and Brucella can cause miscarriage â pregnant staff should not handle raw offal. Pregnant customers should cook all lamb and goat to well-done (71Â°C internal).

Offal Handling and Cooking Notes

Liver: remove the gallbladder without puncturing it â bile ruins the meat. Soak in salted water or milk for 30 minutes, then slice and grill or fry. Kidneys: split, remove the white core, soak, then braise or grill. Heart: trim, stuff or dice for skewers. Tongue: blanch and peel the outer skin before slicing. Tripe: wash repeatedly with salt and lime, blanch in boiling water, then simmer for 2â3 hours before adding to stews. In African cuisine, offal is often the most flavour-dense and profitable part of the animal â treat it with the same care as your premium chops.
