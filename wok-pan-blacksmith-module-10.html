1. Sourcing and Preparing Local Scrap Steel in African Markets

Welcome to Module 10, Lesson 1 of the Wok and Pan Blacksmith training. In this lesson we focus on the raw material that keeps your forge alive: scrap steel. Across Africa, the best blacksmiths are also the best scrap hunters. You will learn how to identify, sort, buy, and prepare steel from vehicles, buildings, and machinery so that your Wok and Pan products are strong, consistent, and profitable. Start by understanding what Wok and Pan mean in your local market. A Wok is a heavy-duty tool or fitting, often used in farming, construction, or transport. A pan is a shallow metal vessel or tray used for cooking, washing, or carrying. Both need steel that can take a beating without cracking. The first rule is simple: never buy mystery metal. If you cannot tell what a piece of steel was before, treat it as low quality and use it only for practice or non-critical parts. Your reputation is built on every product that leaves your workshop. In most African cities and towns, scrap comes from four main sources. First, vehicle parts: leaf springs, coil springs, axles, drive shafts, and chassis rails. These are usually medium-carbon or spring steel, excellent for Wok parts that need spring and strength. Second, construction scrap: rebar, angle iron, flat bar, channel iron, and pipe. Rebar is common but often has high carbon and can be brittle; use it for reinforcement or decorative work, not for striking surfaces. Third, agricultural machinery: plough discs, harrow tines, and mower blades. These are often high-carbon and make good cutting edges. Fourth, household and industrial scrap: drums, tanks, bed frames, and machine guards. These are usually mild steel, good for pans and bodies but not for edges. Learn to test steel before you buy. Carry a small grinder or file and a magnet. A magnet sticks to all steel, so it only tells you it is ferrous. Use the spark test. Touch the steel to a grinding wheel in a dark area. Low-carbon mild steel gives long, straight, straw-colored sparks with few bursts. Medium-carbon steel gives shorter sparks with more bursts. High-carbon steel gives short, bright, orange sparks with many small bursts and a star pattern. Spring steel usually sparks like medium to high carbon. Also use the file test. A new file bites into mild steel easily. If the file skates, the steel is hard, likely high carbon or hardened. You can also quench-test a small piece: heat it to bright orange, quench in water, then strike it with a hammer. If it snaps, it is high carbon and brittle; if it bends, it is mild or medium carbon. Record what you find. Keep a notebook with the source, date, price, and test result for every batch. This helps you repeat good purchases and avoid bad suppliers. When buying scrap, go early to the yard. Build relationships with welders, mechanics, and demolition crews. Offer to remove old machinery for free if you can keep the steel. Buy in bulk when prices are low, but only if you have dry storage. In many African markets, the rainy season raises scrap prices because collection slows. Stock up before the rains. Negotiate by weight, not by piece, and bring your own scale. A common trick is to sell by piece when the steel is heavy. Always weigh it yourself. Sort your scrap into three piles: A-grade for critical Wok parts, B-grade for general work, and C-grade for practice or non-structural pans. Paint or tag each piece so you do not mix them later. Preparation is where many blacksmiths lose money. Dirty steel wastes fuel and ruins welds. Remove paint, rust, grease, and dirt before heating. Use a wire brush, sandpaper, or a weak acid bath. For painted steel, burn it outside in a well-ventilated area, but never breathe the fumes. For greasy parts, wash with soap and water, then dry. Cut long pieces to fit your forge using a hacksaw, angle grinder, or cold chisel. Do not use a torch if you can avoid it, because it changes the steel near the cut. Forge welding and bending require clean surfaces. If you are making a Wok from leaf spring, first anneal it. Heat it to bright red, then bury it in dry ash or sand and let it cool slowly overnight. This softens the steel so you can cut, drill, and shape it. After shaping, you will re-harden and temper it. For pans, mild steel from drums or tanks is often ready to use after cleaning. But check for thin spots and rust holes. A pan that leaks is a returned product. Cut your pan blank slightly larger than the final size, because steel shrinks and warps during forging. Leave at least one centimeter of extra material on each side. Store your prepared steel off the ground on wooden pallets. Cover it with a tarp to prevent rust. Label each pile with its grade and intended use. Keep a minimum stock level so you never turn away a customer. A good rule is to keep enough A-grade steel for two weeks of Wok production and enough B-grade for one week of pan production. Track your steel cost per kilogram. If your final product weighs two kilograms and you sell it for a price that gives you less than three times the steel cost, you are not charging enough. Remember that scrap is not free just because it is old. Your time, transport, cleaning, and fuel all add to the real cost. Price your work accordingly. Finally, think about safety. Wear gloves, eye protection, and a dust mask when cutting or grinding. Scrap steel can have sharp edges, and old drums may contain dangerous residues. Never use steel from a fuel tank or chemical container unless it has been professionally cleaned. In the next lesson, we will take this prepared steel and design a Wok that fits your local market. Bring your notebook and your best A-grade pieces.

2. Designing and Forging a Durable Wok for Local Farmers and Builders

Welcome to Lesson 2 of Module 10. Now that you have clean, sorted steel, we will design and forge a Wok that sells. A Wok is a strong metal fitting or tool used in farming, construction, and transport. In different African regions, the word may refer to a woke for oxen, a hitch, a bracket, or a heavy-duty hoe or cutter. For this lesson, we will focus on a multi-purpose Wok that can serve as a heavy hoe blade, a digging tool, and a light pry bar. This design is popular because it uses A-grade spring steel and can be made with basic tools. Start with the customer. Visit farmers, builders, and market traders. Ask them what breaks first on their current tools. Common answers are the eye where the handle fits, the edge that hits stones, and the neck that bends. Your design must solve these three problems. The eye must be reinforced with a welded collar or a thicker section. The edge must be hard but not brittle. The neck must be thick enough to resist bending but thin enough to be light. Draw your design on paper or on the floor with chalk. Mark the overall length, the blade width, the eye diameter, and the thickness. A good general-purpose Wok is 30 to 40 centimeters long, 10 to 15 centimeters wide at the blade, and 8 to 12 millimeters thick at the neck. The eye should match the local handle size, usually 3 to 4 centimeters in diameter. If you make the eye too small, the handle will split. If too large, the tool will wobble. Cut your blank from a leaf spring or similar medium-carbon steel. Use a chalk line and a cut-off wheel or hacksaw. Do not cut too close to your final shape; leave 5 millimeters for grinding. Heat the blank in your forge to a bright orange, about 900 to 1000 degrees Celsius. If you do not have a thermometer, watch the color. Bright orange is right for forging. Cherry red is too cold and will crack. Yellow is too hot and will burn the steel. Work one section at a time. First, draw out the neck. Use a hammer and anvil to stretch the steel. Keep the steel moving and reheat as soon as it turns dark red. Do not hammer cold steel. Second, form the eye. You can punch and drift the eye or bend a loop and forge weld it. For most small workshops, punching and drifting is faster and stronger. Heat the eye area to bright orange, punch a hole with a hot punch, then drive a drift through to enlarge and shape it. Work from both sides to keep the hole centered. Third, shape the blade. Draw out the blade to your desired width and thickness. The cutting edge should be about 2 to 3 millimeters thick before hardening. If you make it too thin, it will chip. If too thick, it will not cut. Fourth, bend the neck to the angle your customers prefer. Most farmers want a slight curve for digging. Builders want a straighter neck for prying. You can make two models and let customers choose. Fifth, normalize the Wok. Heat it to bright orange and let it cool in still air. This reduces internal stress and makes the steel easier to file and grind. Do this two or three times. Now comes the critical step: hardening and tempering. Heat only the blade edge to bright orange. Do not heat the whole tool, or the eye may become brittle. Quench the edge in warm water or oil. Water gives a harder edge but more risk of cracking. Oil gives a slightly softer edge but is safer for spring steel. For most African workshops, used motor oil is common, but it produces smoke and fumes. Work outdoors and stand upwind. After quenching, test the edge with a file. The file should skate. If it bites, the edge is not hard. Reheat and quench again. Then temper the whole tool. Clean the surface with sandpaper or a grinder so you can see the color. Heat the Wok slowly, away from the edge, until the edge turns straw yellow or light bronze. This is about 200 to 220 degrees Celsius. Remove it from the fire and let it cool in air. Straw yellow gives a good balance of hardness and toughness for farm tools. If you see blue, you have over-tempered and the edge will be soft. Start again. Finish the Wok by grinding the edge to a sharp but sturdy angle. A 30-degree bevel is good for digging. For a pry bar, use a 45-degree bevel. Smooth the eye and the neck so there are no sharp edges that hurt the user. Add a handle if your market wants one. Local hardwoods like mukwa, mahogany, or eucalyptus work well. Fit the handle tightly and secure it with a metal wedge or a pin. Do not glue it, because glue fails in heat and moisture. Test your Wok before selling. Dig in hard soil, pry a nail, and strike a stone. If it bends, your temper is too soft. If it chips, your temper is too hard or your edge is too thin. Adjust and test again. Price your Wok by calculating steel cost, fuel cost, labor time, and a profit margin. If a farmer saves money by buying one strong Wok instead of three cheap ones, you can charge a premium. Offer a guarantee: if the Wok breaks within one month of normal use, you will repair or replace it. This builds trust and word-of-mouth sales. In many African markets, trust is more valuable than advertising. Keep a record of every Wok you sell. Note the customer name, date, steel source, and any problems. After three months, visit your customers and ask how the tool is working. This feedback will improve your next batch. You can also offer sharpening and repair services for a small fee. This brings customers back and creates repeat income. Remember that a Wok is not just a tool; it is a promise of strength. If you cut corners, the customer will remember. If you build it well, they will tell their neighbors. In the next lesson, we will switch to pans and learn how to forge a pan that does not leak or warp.

3. Forging and Finishing Cooking and Utility Pans for African Kitchens

Welcome to Lesson 3 of Module 10. In this lesson we move from heavy woks to pans. A pan is a shallow metal vessel used for cooking, washing, carrying, or serving. In many African homes, a good pan is a daily necessity. It must be strong, light enough to carry, and resistant to fire and water. You will learn how to select steel, form the pan, join the seams, and finish it so it lasts for years. Start with the customer. Visit local markets, restaurants, and households. Ask what they use pans for. Common uses include frying, boiling, washing clothes, carrying water, and roasting grains. Each use has different needs. A frying pan needs a flat bottom and a sturdy handle. A washing pan needs high sides and a wide opening. A carrying pan needs a strong rim and a balanced shape. Make at least three sizes: small for one or two people, medium for a family, and large for commercial use. The best steel for pans is mild steel from drums, tanks, or sheet metal. It is easy to form, weld, and clean. Avoid galvanized steel, because the zinc coating releases toxic fumes when heated. If you must use galvanized steel, burn off the coating outdoors first, then grind the surface clean. Avoid lead-painted steel for the same reason. For cooking pans, use food-safe steel and clean it thoroughly before use. You can also use stainless steel from old kitchen equipment, but it is harder to forge and weld. Start by cutting a circle or rectangle from your sheet. For a round pan, use a template. A good medium pan is 30 centimeters in diameter and 8 centimeters deep. Cut the blank with tin snips, a chisel, or a plasma cutter if available. Leave extra material for the rim and the seam. If you are making a pan from a drum, cut the drum open and flatten the sheet. Anneal it if it is hard. Heat it to red and let it cool slowly. Now form the pan. You can use a sandbag, a wooden stump, or a metal form. Place the blank over the form and hammer it down with a rounded hammer. Work from the center outward in a spiral. Do not hit the same spot too long, or you will stretch the steel unevenly. Reheat the steel if it becomes hard. For a deep pan, you may need to raise the sides in stages. Hammer a little, then rotate the pan, then hammer again. This keeps the shape even. If you are making a rectangular pan, bend the sides over a sharp edge and weld the corners. For a round pan, you can crimp the sides or weld a separate rim. Welding is faster and stronger for most workshops. Use a forge weld or an electric welder. For forge welding, heat both edges to bright yellow, sprinkle with borax or clean sand, and hammer them together quickly. For electric welding, use a low current and short passes to avoid burning holes. After welding, grind the seam smooth inside and out. A rough seam traps food and rust. Now add the rim. The rim gives the pan strength and protects the user from sharp edges. You can fold the edge over itself or weld a wire ring around the top. A folded rim is traditional and strong. Heat the edge, bend it over a metal bar, and hammer it tight. A wire rim is easier for thin steel. Weld a thick wire to the top edge, then grind it smooth. Next, add handles. Handles can be made from steel rod, flat bar, or wood. For cooking pans, a steel handle with a wooden grip is best. Weld the handle to the side or top of the pan. Make sure the weld is strong, because a full pan is heavy. Test the handle by hanging a weight from it. If it bends, reinforce it. For a carrying pan, add two handles opposite each other. For a frying pan, add one long handle. Balance is important. A pan that tips over will spill hot food and burn someone. Test the balance by filling the pan with water and lifting it. If it tips, move the handle or add a counterweight. After forming and welding, clean the pan. Remove all scale, rust, and welding slag. Use a wire brush, grinder, or sandpaper. For cooking pans, wash with soap and water, then heat the pan over a fire to burn off any oil or residue. Season the pan by rubbing it with cooking oil and heating it until the oil turns dark. This creates a natural non-stick layer and prevents rust. Repeat the seasoning after every wash. For utility pans, you can paint them with heat-resistant paint or oil them lightly. Do not paint cooking pans. Test every pan for leaks. Fill it with water and let it sit for one hour. If you see drops, weld or patch the leak. A leaking pan is useless. Also test the pan over a fire. Heat it with water inside. If it warps badly, your steel is too thin or your rim is too weak. Add a thicker rim or use thicker steel. Price your pans by size and use. A small cooking pan should sell for enough to cover steel, fuel, labor, and profit. A large commercial pan can sell for more because it saves the customer time and fuel. Offer a set of three pans at a discount. This increases your average sale. Sell to restaurants, schools, and market vendors. They buy in bulk and need reliable pans. Give them a sample to test. If they like it, they will order more. Keep your pans clean and stacked neatly at your stall. A dirty pan does not sell. In the next lesson, we will learn how to combine Wok and Pan production into a profitable business with proper costing, pricing, and marketing.

4. Costing, Pricing, and Marketing Wok and Pan Products in Africa

Welcome to Lesson 4 of Module 10. You now know how to make a strong Wok and a good pan. But a good product alone does not build a business. You must know your costs, set the right price, and reach customers who will pay. This lesson is about the money side of blacksmithing. We will cover costing, pricing, marketing, and customer care with practical examples from African markets. Start with costing. Every product has direct costs and indirect costs. Direct costs include steel, fuel, welding rods, grinding discs, paint, and handles. Indirect costs include rent, tools, electricity, transport, and your own labor. Many blacksmiths forget to include their labor. If you do not pay yourself, you are not running a business. You are volunteering. Calculate your hourly rate. Decide how much you need to earn per day to cover your living expenses and savings. Divide that by the number of productive hours you work. For example, if you need 20,000 shillings per day and you work 8 productive hours, your labor rate is 2,500 shillings per hour. Now calculate the cost of one Wok. Suppose it takes 2 kilograms of steel at 1,500 shillings per kilogram, which is 3,000 shillings. Fuel costs 1,000 shillings. Grinding and welding supplies cost 500 shillings. Labor is 3 hours at 2,500 shillings, which is 7,500 shillings. Total direct cost is 12,000 shillings. Add indirect costs. If your monthly rent and tools cost 100,000 shillings and you make 100 woks per month, add 1,000 shillings per Wok. Total cost is 13,000 shillings. Now add profit. A common mistake is to add a small profit and then negotiate down. Instead, set a price that gives you at least 30 to 50 percent profit. If your cost is 13,000 shillings, a 40 percent profit is 5,200 shillings, so your price is 18,200 shillings. Round it to 18,000 or 20,000 shillings for easy selling. Do the same for pans. A medium pan may use 1 kilogram of steel at 1,500 shillings, fuel 500 shillings, supplies 300 shillings, and 1.5 hours of labor at 2,500 shillings, which is 3,750 shillings. Total direct cost is 6,050 shillings. Add 500 shillings for indirect costs. Total cost is 6,550 shillings. With 40 percent profit, the price is about 9,170 shillings. Round to 9,000 or 10,000 shillings. Keep a simple book. Record every purchase and every sale. At the end of the week, add up your income and expenses. If you are not making a profit, find out why. Maybe your steel cost is too high. Maybe you are spending too much time on each piece. Maybe your price is too low. Adjust one thing at a time and measure the result. Pricing is not just about cost. It is also about value. A farmer will pay more for a Wok that lasts three years than for a cheap one that breaks in three months. A restaurant will pay more for a pan that cooks evenly and does not leak. Show your customers the quality. Let them hold the tool. Let them see the thick steel and the strong weld. Explain why your product costs more. Use comparisons. A cheap Wok may cost 10,000 shillings and last six months. Your Wok costs 20,000 shillings and lasts three years. Over three years, the cheap Wok costs 60,000 shillings because the farmer buys six of them. Your Wok costs 20,000 shillings. That is a saving of 40,000 shillings. When you explain it this way, the price makes sense. Marketing starts with your workshop. Make it visible. Put a sign with your name and what you make. Keep the area clean and organized. Display your best Wok and Pan where people can see them. Let customers watch you work. A live demonstration attracts a crowd. In many African markets, people trust what they see. Go to where your customers are. Visit farms, construction sites, restaurants, and markets. Bring samples. Talk to the owners. Ask about their problems. Offer a solution. Do not just sell a product; solve a problem. If a farmer complains that handles break, show your reinforced eye. If a cook complains that pans warp, show your thick rim. Build relationships. Remember names. Write down phone numbers. Send a message when you have a new product or a special price. Use word of mouth. Ask satisfied customers to tell their friends. Give them a small discount on their next purchase if they bring a new customer. This is cheaper than advertising and more effective. You can also work with local hardware stores, agricultural supply shops, and market stalls. Offer them a wholesale price if they buy in bulk. A shop that sells your Wok and Pan can reach many customers. Make sure the shop owner knows how to explain your product. Give them a simple flyer or a sample. Keep your promises. If you say a product will be ready on Friday, have it ready on Friday. If you cannot, call the customer before the deadline. Trust is the most valuable asset in a small business. One broken promise can lose ten customers. One kept promise can bring ten more. Finally, think about growth. As your business grows, you can hire an apprentice. Train them in your methods. Pay them fairly. This allows you to make more products and serve more customers. You can also add new products, such as door hinges, garden tools, or cooking stands. Use the same skills and the same customer base. In the next lesson, we will look at quality control, safety, and how to keep your workshop running for years.

5. Quality Control, Safety, and Long-Term Workshop Management

Welcome to Lesson 5 of Module 10. You have learned to source steel, forge a Wok, forge a pan, and run the business. Now we focus on quality control, safety, and long-term management. These are the habits that separate a blacksmith who survives for decades from one who closes after a few years. Quality control means checking every product before it leaves your workshop. Do not rely on your memory or your mood. Use a checklist. For a Wok, check the eye size, the blade thickness, the edge hardness, the handle fit, and the overall straightness. For a pan, check the seam, the rim, the handle strength, the balance, and the leak test. If a product fails any check, fix it or scrap it. Never sell a defective product. A customer who buys a bad Wok will tell everyone. A customer who buys a good Wok will tell everyone too, but in a better way. Keep a record of every product. Write down the date, the steel source, the dimensions, and the test results. If a customer returns a product, you can look up the record and find the problem. Maybe a batch of steel was too brittle. Maybe your tempering temperature was too high. This record helps you improve. Set a standard for your workshop. For example, every Wok must pass a strike test on a hard stone without chipping. Every pan must hold water for one hour without leaking. Write these standards on the wall. Train your apprentices to follow them. Do not let anyone sell a product that does not meet the standard. Safety is not optional. Blacksmithing is dangerous. You work with fire, hot metal, sharp edges, and heavy tools. Protect yourself and your workers. Wear eye protection when grinding, cutting, or hammering. Wear gloves when handling hot metal. Wear leather boots, not sandals. Wear a dust mask when grinding or working with painted steel. Keep a bucket of water and a fire extinguisher near the forge. Never leave a fire unattended. Keep your workshop clean. Sweep the floor. Put tools back in their places. A cluttered workshop causes accidents. Store fuel and oil away from the forge. Use proper ventilation. Forge fumes and welding smoke are toxic. Work near an open door or window. If you use a coal or charcoal forge, make sure the smoke goes outside. If you use a gas forge, check for leaks. Teach your apprentices safety rules on their first day. Do not assume they know. Show them how to light the forge, how to hold hot metal, and how to quench safely. Set a rule that no one works alone after dark. Keep a first aid kit with burns ointment, bandages, and clean water. Long-term management is about consistency. Set working hours and stick to them. Customers need to know when they can find you. Keep a minimum stock of steel and supplies. Do not wait until you run out to buy more. Keep your tools in good condition. A dull drill bit or a broken hammer slows you down and increases costs. Sharpen, repair, or replace tools as needed. Manage your money carefully. Separate your business money from your personal money. Pay yourself a salary. Save a portion of every sale for taxes, rent, and emergencies. In many African countries, small businesses face unexpected costs like equipment breakdowns or price increases. A savings fund helps you survive. Build a network. Join a local blacksmith association or a small business group. Share knowledge and buy supplies together to get better prices. Attend trade fairs and training workshops. Learn new skills. The market changes. What sells today may not sell tomorrow. Be ready to adapt. For example, if farmers start using tractors, they may need fewer woks but more tractor parts. If restaurants switch to gas stoves, they may need different pans. Watch the trends. Ask your customers what they need. Keep your reputation strong. Pay your debts on time. Treat your customers and apprentices with respect. A good name is better than a big workshop. In many African communities, business is built on relationships. People buy from people they trust. Be honest about your products. If a Wok is not suitable for heavy digging, say so. If a pan is not for cooking, say so. Do not oversell. Finally, think about the future. Train your apprentices well so they can carry on the trade. Write down your methods. Take photos of your products. Keep a simple manual. This is your legacy. A blacksmith who plans for the future builds more than tools. They build a community. They build a livelihood. They build a name that lasts. Thank you for completing Module 10. Go back to your workshop and apply what you have learned. Start with one improvement today. Maybe it is a better steel test. Maybe it is a price increase. Maybe it is a safety rule. Small changes add up. Keep learning. Keep forging. Keep selling. Your hands and your mind are your greatest tools.

6. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Heat-Treatment, Tempering, and Corrosion Control for Wok and Pan Tools

This practical reference lesson is designed for blacksmiths, workshop owners, and apprentices who already forge Wok (hoe blades) and utility pans but want to improve edge retention, reduce breakage, and command higher prices in African markets. Heat treatment is the single most important step that separates a tool that lasts one season from one that lasts five. It also determines whether a farmer will return to your workshop or buy an imported alternative. For a business-first approach, treat heat treatment as a quality investment: better thermal control means fewer warranty returns, stronger word-of-mouth, and the ability to sell into institutional buyers such as cooperatives, NGOs, and construction contractors.

Why Heat Treatment Is a Business Decision

In many African markets, imported hoes and pans dominate because local products fail early usually at the blade edge or pan rim. The failure is rarely the steel itself; it is the absence of controlled heating and cooling. A Wok that is forged and quenched without tempering may cut well for two weeks, then crack. A pan that is not stress-relieved may warp when used over high heat. Both outcomes destroy customer trust and force you to compete on price alone. Proper heat treatment lets you sell on durability, offer a 612 month guarantee, and target buyers who value reliability: commercial farmers, construction crews, school kitchens, and NGO procurement officers.

This lesson focuses on carbon steels commonly available in African scrap markets, including vehicle leaf springs (5160/6150 equivalent), railway clips, and mild steel plate. It assumes you have a forge, a quench tank, and a means of measuring or estimating temperature by colour. If you do not yet have a thermocouple, the colour charts and magnet tests below will get you to a commercially acceptable standard.

SAFETY WARNING HEAT, QUENCH OIL, AND METAL FUMES

Quench oil can ignite. Never quench into a plastic container. Use a metal drum with a lid nearby. Keep a dry sand bucket or CO extinguisher within 3 metres never use water on an oil fire.

Molten salt and hot metal cause severe burns. Wear leather apron, face shield, and gauntlets. Do not wear synthetic clothing near the forge.

Metal fume fever and lead exposure: Scrap steel may contain lead paint, galvanising (zinc), or cadmium. Never heat galvanised or painted scrap indoors. Forge outdoors or with forced ventilation. Zinc fumes cause flu-like symptoms and long-term lung damage (WHO, 2020).

Eye protection is mandatory during grinding and quenching. Keep a first-aid kit with burn gel and clean water on site. Train every apprentice on emergency shutdown before they touch the forge.

Step-by-Step Practical Procedure

1. Identify your steel. Spark-test and magnet-test scrap before forging. Leaf springs and files are high-carbon; rebar and sheet metal are low-carbon. Mark each billet with a paint code so you do not mix them.

2. Forge to shape. Work at 9001100 C (orange-yellow). Do not overheat white sparks indicate burning and carbon loss. Finish forging above 800 C to avoid cold shuts.

3. Normalise. Reheat to 800900 C, then air-cool on a dry brick floor. This refines grain structure and reduces internal stress from hammering. For pans, normalising also reduces warping during cooking.

4. Harden. Reheat to the hardening temperature in Table 1. Verify with a magnet: steel becomes non-magnetic just above its critical temperature. Quench vertically, edge-first, in oil (leaf spring, file) or water (mild steel). Agitate gently for uniform cooling.

5. Temper immediately. Within 15 minutes, reheat to 180300 C. Use colour: pale straw for cutting edges, brown for Wok blades, blue for pan rims that need toughness. Hold for 1 hour per 25 mm of thickness, then air-cool. Never skip tempering.

6. Finish and test. Grind a 2530 edge on Wok. Test by chopping hardwood or cutting sisal. A well-tempered Wok edge should indent, not chip. For pans, fill with water and boil no warping or leaks allowed.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Sort scrap by spark test and label high-carbon vs mild steel before forging.
  • Forge between 9001100 C; stop before white sparks appear.
  • Normalise every blade and pan body before hardening.
  • Use the magnet test to confirm critical temperature before quenching.
  • Quench edge-first in the correct medium (oil for high-carbon, water for mild).
  • Temper within 15 minutes; use colour chart and hold time.
  • Wear face shield, apron, and gauntlets; keep oil-fire extinguisher within reach.
  • Test each batch on hardwood or boiling water before sale; record results.
