1. Glass Cutting Tools and Techniques

To cut glass, you need a few basic tools: a glass cutter (wheel-type or carbide), a straight edge or T-square, a cutting oil (or kerosene), and a workbench covered with a felt or rubber mat. For straight cuts, measure twice with a tape measure and mark with a grease pencil. Apply a drop of oil along the cut line. Hold the cutter like a pen, apply steady pressure, and score from one edge to the other in one smooth stroke. You should hear a light zipping sound. Do not go back over the score. For curves, use a pattern or template and a cutter with a smaller wheel. After scoring, place a thin rod (like a welding rod) under the score line and press down gently on both sides to snap the glass. For thick glass (6mm+), use running pliers to help the break follow the score. In Africa, many workshops use locally made cutters from recycled ball bearings set in a wooden handle. Keep your cutter wheel clean and oiled. Practice on scrap pieces first. Always wear safety glasses and gloves. Score on the smooth side of the glass; the side with the score will be the inside of the break. For laminated glass, score both sides and use a razor blade to cut the plastic interlayer. For tempered glass, you cannot cut itit will shatter. So always cut before tempering. Store cutters in a dry place to prevent rust. A good quality cutter costs about 500-2000 KES (5-20 USD) and lasts for hundreds of cuts if cared for. Replace the wheel when it starts skipping or leaving a wide score. Remember: a clean score and a quick snap give the best edge.

2. Edge Grinding and Chamfering

After cutting, glass edges are sharp and weak. Grinding removes sharpness and prevents chips. You need a wet belt sander or a diamond grinding wheel. For small shops, a handheld angle grinder with a diamond disc (100mm) works, but keep it wet to avoid dust and heat. Start with a coarse grit (80-120) to remove the sharp edge, then move to finer grits (220, 400) for a smooth finish. Always grind with plenty of waterdry grinding creates silica dust that damages lungs. Wear a N95 mask and safety glasses. Chamfering means grinding a beveled edge, usually at 45 degrees. This is common for glass tabletops, shelves, and mirrors. To chamfer, set your grinder at a consistent angle using a jig or guide. Grind a flat bevel about 1-2mm wide. Check with a square or protractor. For a flat polish, you need a series of grinding wheels from coarse to fine, then a polishing wheel with cerium oxide. In Africa, many workshops use a vertical spindle grinder with a diamond wheel. Keep the glass moving to avoid burning the edge. A common mistake is grinding too fast, which causes chipping. Use light pressure and let the tool do the work. After grinding, clean the edge with a damp cloth. For safety, always grind edges before installing glass. A well-ground edge reduces breakage during handling and installation. For laminated glass, grind after cutting but be careful not to delaminate. For tempered glass, edges are already ground before tempering, so you cannot grind after. So plan your grinding before tempering. A basic wet grinder costs around 15,000-30,000 KES (150-300 USD) but pays for itself quickly.

3. Polishing: Mechanical and Flame

Polishing glass edges makes them clear and smooth, like the surface. There are two main methods: mechanical and flame. Mechanical polishing uses a series of abrasive wheels and a polishing compound. After grinding with 400 grit, use a felt or cork wheel charged with cerium oxide or tin oxide slurry. Keep the wheel wet and move the glass back and forth. It takes 2-5 minutes per edge to get a clear polish. For a high-gloss finish, use a final buff with a soft wheel and fine cerium. Mechanical polishing is safe and works on all glass types. Flame polishing uses a hydrogen-oxygen torch or a propane-oxygen torch. The flame is played quickly along the edge. The heat melts the microscopic peaks, giving a clear, glossy finish. But flame polishing can cause thermal shock and break the glass if you move too slowly or use too much heat. It works best on thin glass (3-6mm) and on edges that are already ground smooth. Flame polishing is faster but riskier. In Africa, flame polishing is common in small workshops because it's cheap. But you need a steady hand and good ventilation. Never flame polish tempered glassit will shatter. Also, flame polishing can leave a slight waviness. For a perfect edge, many shops combine both: mechanical polish first, then a quick flame pass. Always wear safety goggles and heat-resistant gloves. Keep a fire extinguisher nearby. For chemical polishing, you can use a mixture of hydrofluoric acid and sulfuric acid, but this is extremely dangerous and not recommended for beginners. Stick to mechanical and flame methods. With practice, you can achieve a professional finish that sells for higher prices.

4. Drilling and Machining Glass

Drilling holes in glass requires special drill bits and technique. Use a diamond core drill bit or a carbide-tipped glass drill bit. For holes up to 12mm, a spear-point bit works. Always use a variable-speed drill on low speed (300-600 RPM). Keep the drill perpendicular to the glass. Use a lubricantwater or keroseneto cool the bit and flush away glass dust. Start the hole by making a small guide notch with a center punch or a small file. Then drill slowly with light pressure. Let the bit cut; do not force it. For large holes (over 20mm), use a diamond hole saw. Clamp the glass to a sacrificial backing board (plywood or MDF) to prevent breakout. Drill from one side until the tip just breaks through, then flip the glass and drill from the other side to meet the hole. This prevents chipping. For machining, you can use a CNC router with diamond bits to cut shapes and edges. But for most African workshops, a simple drill press with a diamond bit is enough. Always wear a dust mask and safety glasses. Keep the work area wet to suppress dust. After drilling, smooth the hole edges with a diamond file or a small grinding stone. For tempered glass, you cannot drill after temperingit will shatter. So drill before tempering. For laminated glass, drill slowly and use a lubricant to avoid delamination. A good diamond drill bit costs 1000-3000 KES (10-30 USD) and can drill 20-50 holes if used correctly. Replace when it stops cutting. Practice on scrap glass first. Remember: patience and lubrication are key.

5. Water Jet Cutting

Water jet cutting uses a high-pressure stream of water mixed with abrasive particles to cut glass. It can cut complex shapes, thick glass (up to 100mm), and laminated or tempered glass without heat damage. The machine directs a jet of water at 30,000-60,000 PSI through a small nozzle (0.2-0.5mm) mixed with garnet abrasive. The glass is placed on a support table and the jet moves along a computer-controlled path. This method is precise (tolerance 0.1mm) and leaves a smooth edge that often needs no further polishing. However, water jet machines are expensive (from 15,000 USD) and require a lot of space and maintenance. In Africa, water jet cutting is available in larger cities like Nairobi, Lagos, and Johannesburg, but many small workshops outsource to these services. If you send glass for water jet cutting, provide a DXF or CAD file of your design. The cost is usually per meter of cut or per hour. For a small shop, you can offer water jet cutting as a service if you have the capital. But for most learners, the key is to know when to use it: for intricate shapes, thick glass, or when heat-sensitive glass (tempered) must be cut. Water jet cutting does not create dust, so it's safer for workers. But the abrasive slurry must be disposed of properly. Always wear ear protection and safety glasses when near the machine. The cutting bed must be kept clean. If you are considering buying a water jet, start with a small entry-level model (e.g., 5 HP) and learn maintenance. Many African entrepreneurs have started successful water jet cutting businesses serving the construction and automotive industries.

6. Laser Cutting and Scribing

Laser cutting uses a focused beam of light to melt or vaporize glass. It is very precise and fast, but it has limitations. CO2 lasers (10.6 micron wavelength) can cut thin glass (up to 6mm) but often leave a rough, frosted edge that needs polishing. For thicker glass, laser cutting is slow and can cause micro-cracks. Laser scribing is different: it uses a low-power laser to create a score line, which is then broken like traditional cutting. This is common for thin glass (0.5-2mm) used in electronics and solar panels. For a small workshop, a laser cutter is a big investment (from 5,000 USD). But you can start with a laser engraver (e.g., 40W CO2) to scribe glass and then break it. This works well for making small decorative pieces or drilling holes. When laser cutting glass, use a honeycomb bed and air assist to blow away debris. Focus the laser on the surface. For scribing, use low power (10-20%) and high speed (100-200 mm/s). After scribing, break the glass along the line with your hands or running pliers. Always wear laser safety glasses that block the specific wavelength. Never leave the laser unattended. In Africa, laser cutting is growing in cities like Cairo, Nairobi, and Accra. Many sign makers and gift shops use lasers to cut glass awards and ornaments. If you don't have a laser, you can outsource to a local laser cutting service. The cost is usually per minute of cutting time. For a beginner, learning to operate a laser cutter requires training and practice. Start with simple shapes and work up. Remember: laser cutting glass is not for every jobsometimes traditional cutting and grinding are cheaper and better.

<p>This practical reference consolidates the core knowledge from the six lessons in this module<strong>Glass Cutting Tools and Techniques</strong>, <strong>Edge Grinding and Chamfering</strong>, <strong>Polishing: Mechanical and Flame</strong>, <strong>Drilling and Machining Glass</strong>, <strong>Water Jet Cutting</strong>, and <strong>Laser Cutting and Scribing</strong>into a single, shop-floor-ready guide. It is written for the African context: small and medium enterprises (SMEs) in Lagos, Nairobi, Accra, Johannesburg, and Cairo that process flat glass for windows, doors, tabletops, shower screens, and architectural glazing. The emphasis is on <strong>business outcomes</strong>: fewer breakages, faster throughput, lower consumable costs, and higher-value finished products that can be sold at premium margins.</p>

<p>Across Africa, glass processing is dominated by manual and semi-automatic techniques. Fully automated CNC lines exist in South Africa, Egypt, and Morocco, but the majority of workshops rely on hand cutters, portable grinders, and simple polishing wheels. This reference helps you get the most from that equipment while knowing when to invest in advanced methods such as water jet and laser.</p>

<h3>1. Glass Cutting: The Foundation of Every Job</h3>
<p>Manual cutting remains the most common method in African workshops. The key variables are <strong>wheel angle</strong> (typically 120140), <strong>cutting pressure</strong> (24 kg), and <strong>lubrication</strong> (light oil or kerosene). A clean score followed by uniform break-out pressure produces a straight, chip-free edge. For laminated glass, score both sides and use a razor blade to separate the interlayer. For thick glass (1019 mm), use a triple-wheel cutter and apply steady, even pressure.</p>

<p><strong>Business tip:</strong> Train one operator per shift as the master cutter. Inconsistent scoring is the single largest cause of breakage and rework in small shops. A consistent cutter can reduce waste by 1525%, directly improving margin on every square metre sold.</p>

<h3>2. Edge Grinding and Chamfering</h3>
<p>Raw cut edges are sharp and prone to chipping. Grinding removes micro-cracks and creates a safe, uniform edge. Use a <strong>diamond-impregnated grinding wheel</strong> (grit 80120) on a portable or bench grinder. Chamfering (a 45 bevel) is required for laminated glass and for edges that will be exposed. For flat polish, follow with finer grits (240, 400, 600).</p>

<p><strong>Africa context:</strong> Many workshops in Kenya and Nigeria use angle grinders with diamond pads because they are affordable and locally available. Keep a water feed or use a wet sponge to control dust and heat. Dry grinding generates silica dusta serious health hazard.</p>

<h3>3. Polishing: Mechanical and Flame</h3>
<p>Mechanical polishing uses <strong>cerium oxide</strong> or <strong>tin oxide</strong> slurry on a felt or polyurethane wheel. It produces a clear, mirror-like edge. Flame polishing uses an <strong>oxy-hydrogen torch</strong> to melt the edge surface; it is fast but risks thermal shock and is only suitable for certain glass types. In South Africa and Egypt, flame polishing is common for decorative glass; in most other African markets, mechanical polishing is more practical because it requires less gas infrastructure.</p>

<h3>4. Drilling and Machining Glass</h3>
<p>Use <strong>diamond core drills</strong> with water cooling. Drill at low speed (500800 rpm) and low feed pressure. For holes larger than 25 mm, use a pilot hole and a hole saw. Never use a standard masonry bitit will shatter the glass. For countersinking, use a diamond countersink bit. Always support the glass on a flat, cushioned surface.</p>

<h3>5. Water Jet Cutting</h3>
<p>Water jet cutting uses a high-pressure stream of water mixed with abrasive (garnet) to cut glass without heat. It is ideal for complex shapes, thick glass (up to 100 mm), and laminated or tempered glass. In Africa, water jet machines are concentrated in South Africa, Egypt, and Morocco. For SMEs, outsourcing water jet cutting to a regional service centre is often more profitable than buying a machine (capital cost: USD 50,000150,000).</p>

<h3>6. Laser Cutting and Scribing</h3>
<p>Laser cutting uses a focused CO laser to melt or vaporise glass. It produces very fine edges but is slow and expensive. Laser scribing creates a controlled crack that is then broken out. This method is used for thin glass (0.53 mm) in electronics and solar panel manufacturing. In Africa, laser glass processing is still rare outside research labs and large solar factories.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Measure twice, cut once:</strong> Verify dimensions and glass type (annealed, laminated, tempered) before scoring.</li>
<li><strong>Use the right wheel:</strong> Match wheel angle and pressure to glass thickness; replace worn wheels immediately.</li>
<li><strong>Lubricate the score:</strong> Light oil or kerosene reduces chipping and extends wheel life.</li>
<li><strong>Break out evenly:</strong> Apply uniform pressure; never force a breakre-score if needed.</li>
<li><strong>Grind wet:</strong> Use water feed or wet sponge to control dust and heat; wear a P2 mask.</li>
<li><strong>Polish in stages:</strong> Start with 80120 grit, then 240, 400, 600, then cerium oxide.</li>
<li><strong>Drill slow and cool:</strong> 500800 rpm with water; support the glass fully.</li>
<li><strong>Know your limits:</strong> Outsource water jet and laser work until volume justifies in-house investment.</li>
</ol>
