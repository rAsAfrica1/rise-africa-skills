3. Step 3 — Get the Money Right

The number one reason small businesses fail in Africa is not lack of customers. It is running out of money. This module teaches the money math that most founders never do — the real cost of starting, the real price to charge, and the cash flow forecast that tells you when you will hit trouble, before you hit it.

Module 3 of 7 · 6 lessons · ~55 minutes · 6 practical exercises · quiz at the end

Lesson 3.1 — How much do you actually need? The honest numbers

Beginners almost always underestimate startup cost. They think of the biggest item — the machine, the stock, the rent — and assume that is the number. In reality, that is usually only 40% of the true startup cost.

True startup cost has five parts. Miss any one and you run out of money before the business gets going.

Cost categoryWhat it includesTypical %
Equipment and toolsMachines, vehicles, tools, furniture, fittings30–40%
Initial stock/materialsRaw materials, inventory for first 30–60 days20–30%
Setup and licensingRegistration, permits, licences, painting, signage5–10%
Working capital cushion2–3 months of operating expenses (rent, salaries, fuel) before income catches up15–25%
Your survival moneyPersonal living costs for 3–6 months10–20%

Most founders calculate only the first two categories. That is why they run out of money in month 2 or 3 — exactly when the business is starting to work, but has not paid back yet.

Here is the correct way to calculate. Take your business idea and list every single cost until the day the business earns more than it spends. Not "startup cost" — full cost. Include:

  • The obvious things — equipment, stock, rent
  • The forgotten things — transport, phone data, packaging, cleaning supplies, casual labour
  • The invisible things — your own time, your own transport to run errands, airtime, thank-you gifts to early customers
  • The delays — how long until you actually open? Add 2–3 weeks because things always take longer than planned
  • The surprises — something always breaks. Add 15% as a buffer for the unexpected.
The 15% rule: Whatever number you calculate for startup cost, add 15% for the things you did not think of. Every single time. Things break, prices rise, deliveries are late. The 15% buffer is not optional.

For example, if you want to open a bakery stall: Equipment (oven, mixer, trays) $600. Stock (flour, sugar, yeast, packaging) $200. Setup (licence, sign, paint) $80. Working capital (2 months rent + utilities + casual labour) $300. Personal survival (3 months food/rent for you) $450. Total before buffer: $1,630. Plus 15% buffer = $1,875. That is the real number. Almost nobody says $1,875 when asked. They say $600 for the oven.

Practical exercise 3.1: List every cost until your business is self-funding. Include all five categories plus the 15% buffer. Write the final number at the top of a page. That is your real startup cost.

Lesson 3.2 — Sources — savings, family, chamas, loans, grants

You now have a real number. The next question is where the money comes from. There are six sources available to most Africans, and each has different costs, risks, and requirements.

SourceCostRiskBest for
Personal savings$0 (opportunity cost only)Lowest — you lose only what you put inStartups under $500; any business with a proven model
Family / friendsOften $0 but always a relationship riskHigh — money and family mix badlySmall top-ups, matching funds, emergencies only
Chama / SACCO / VSLA5–20% per yearMedium — group guaranteeStartups $100–$2,000; members-only
Bank / MFI loan15–40% per year in local currencyHigh — must repay no matter whatBusinesses with existing cash flow; asset purchases
Grants / competitions$0 but competitiveLow but slowSocial enterprises, youth-owned, women-owned
Investors / partnersEquity — give up part of the businessDependsScalable businesses with strong growth potential

Most small businesses should start with savings and chama, and avoid debt until the business has proven cash flow. The reason is simple: a loan must be repaid whether the business succeeds or not. A loan on an unvalidated business is a trap.

The debt rule: Never borrow money for a business that has not yet proven it can pay back the loan. The proof is 3 months of steady cash flow. Before that, only spend money you can afford to lose.

Grants are real in Africa. The Tony Elumelu Foundation, Youth Enterprise Fund (Kenya), SEDCO (Eswatini), NYDA (South Africa), and many government and NGO programmes give real money to young entrepreneurs. They take months to apply, need documentation, and are competitive — but the money is free and non-repayable. If you fit the profile (under 35, women-owned, agricultural, tech), apply to several at once.

Practical exercise 3.2: For your business, list which sources you can access. Include the amount each source could give you, the cost (interest), and the risk. Total the safe sources — savings and chama — as your "safe capital." That is your real ceiling for now.

Lesson 3.3 — Pricing your product or service for real profit

Pricing is the single biggest cause of thin profits in small businesses. Most new entrepreneurs price by looking at competitors and then going slightly lower. That is a race to the bottom. You need to price from cost up, and then check against the market.

Here is the correct sequence.

Step 1 — Calculate your direct cost per unit. Every material and every bit of labour that goes into producing one unit. For a loaf of bread: flour, yeast, salt, sugar, oil, fuel to bake, packaging. Suppose that totals $0.55.

Step 2 — Add the labour cost per unit. If you make 100 loaves per day and your helper costs $5 per day, that is $0.05 per loaf. Add it. Total so far: $0.60.

Step 3 — Add operating overhead allocation. Rent, electricity, transport, phone — all monthly costs divided across expected units. If monthly overhead is $200 and you expect to sell 1,500 loaves, that is $0.13 per loaf. Total: $0.73.

Step 4 — Add your profit margin. Target at least 40% margin on cost, ideally 50–60% for retail. So $0.73 × 1.5 = $1.10. Your price is $1.10 minimum.

Step 5 — Compare to the market. If competitors sell at $1.00, you have a problem. Either you cut your cost, or you differentiate so customers will pay $1.10, or you accept lower margins. Never just slash to $0.90 — that guarantees you lose money on every sale.

The minimum price rule: Never price below your total cost per unit. That is not a business — it is a hobby that pays you nothing. If the market will not pay above your cost, you have the wrong product, the wrong market, or the wrong production method.

For services, the same logic applies with different units. Your unit is your hour, or your job, or your client. Direct cost = materials + your time at a fair hourly rate. Add overhead. Add margin. That is your minimum hourly rate or job price.

A common mistake in services: pricing based on what you think you are worth personally, which is almost always too low. If you would charge yourself $5 per hour, price the market at $15 per hour. The market judges by the result, not by your self-image.

Practical exercise 3.3: For your business, calculate cost per unit through all five steps. Write your minimum profitable price. Compare to competitors. Adjust your offer, your cost, or your positioning if there is a gap.

Lesson 3.4 — Costing — what it costs vs what you charge

Costing is the discipline of knowing, per item or per job, exactly what leaves your pocket. Most small businesses do not cost properly. They sell a lot, and at the end of the month they have less money than they expected — because some product or service was priced below cost.

Here is the simplest cost-tracking method that works without software.

1 — Build a recipe card for each product. Every item you sell should have its own card. On it, list every material and its quantity. For each material, the cost per unit of measure (per kg, per litre, per piece). Multiply through to get total materials cost. Update costs every month or whenever a supplier price changes.

2 — Track your "true take" per sale. This is the price minus materials cost minus any per-sale cost (packaging, delivery, commission, packaging fees, taxes). That number is what you actually earn per sale before overhead.

3 — Know your overhead separately. Rent, staff, electricity, transport, subscriptions. Overhead is a fixed monthly figure. It is covered by the sum of your "true take" across all sales. If the sum does not exceed overhead, you are losing money even though you are selling well.

ItemPriceMaterialsTrue take
Small loaf$1.00$0.55$0.45
Large loaf$1.80$0.90$0.90
Cake slice$2.50$0.80$1.70

Now you can see which products actually pay for your rent. If overhead is $300/month and you sell 500 small loaves + 200 large loaves + 150 cake slices, your true take is $225 + $180 + $255 = $660. After overhead, you have $360. That is your real profit — before your own time.

Review monthly: At the end of each month, recalculate your top 5 products. If any has a "true take" below 20% of price, either raise the price, lower the cost, or stop selling it.
Practical exercise 3.4: Build a recipe card for your 3 best-selling or planned products. Calculate true take per sale. Multiply by expected monthly sales. Subtract overhead. That is your real monthly profit — write it down.

Lesson 3.5 — The 90-day cash flow projection

Cash flow is not profit. A business can be profitable on paper and still fail because the cash is not in the right place at the right time. This is the single biggest killer of small businesses in Africa.

A cash flow projection shows, week by week, how much cash you expect to come in, how much will go out, and what is left. The projection tells you two critical things: when you will run short, and whether you need a short-term loan, more capital, or a change in plan.

Here is how to build a simple 90-day (12-week) cash flow for a small business.

WeekCash inCash outNetRunning balance
1$0$320 (equipment)-$320$680
2$80$120 (stock)-$40$640
3$140$60+$80$720
4$180$80+$100$820
...............
12$420$200+$220$1,580

Assume you start with $1,000 of savings. Week 1 you spend $320 on equipment. That leaves $680. Week 2 you start selling — small numbers. If by week 12 your running balance is higher than the start, your business is funding itself and growing. If the balance dips to zero at any point, you must arrange more money before that week, not after.

Two rules for a realistic cash flow:

  • Underestimate income by 20%. New businesses always earn less than planned at the start. If you plan $500, enter $400.
  • Overestimate costs by 20%. Things cost more than you think. If you plan $300, enter $360.
The negative-week rule: Any week with a negative running balance is a crisis week. Circle it. Decide now how you will cover that gap: more savings, a chama top-up, delay a purchase, or plan a sale push in the week before.
Practical exercise 3.5: Build a 12-week cash flow for your business. Use realistic numbers with the 20% under/over rule. Find your lowest running balance. If it is below 30% of your starting cash, plan how you will top it up before then.

Lesson 3.6 — What to do when you run out of money in month 2

Almost every small business hits a cash crisis in the first 3 months. It is normal. The difference between businesses that survive and those that fail is knowing what to do in that moment.

Here is the sequence, in order of preference. Do them in this order — not the reverse.

1 — Cut everything non-essential, immediately. Not "plan to cut" — cut today. Subscriptions, casual labour you could survive without, extra transport, that small upgrade you were considering. Cut to the bone for 2 weeks. Most cash crises are 15–20% overspend, and cutting 20% right now may be enough.

2 — Push sales harder than usual. A short, sharp sales push. WhatsApp every contact. Do a limited-time offer. Sell to the customers who already bought — they buy again. Every $100 you bring in this week is $100 you do not need to borrow.

3 — Delay payments you can legitimately delay. Supplier credit, rent for a week, casual wages by 3 days — with agreement. Never delay statutory payments (tax, NSSA, medical aid) because those carry legal penalties. But informal delays are often fine if communicated honestly.

4 — Liquidate anything sellable. Second-hand equipment you bought but have not used. Excess stock. Your own personal items you no longer need. This is hard, but it is money that is sitting idle in things you are not using.

5 — Take a short-term loan or chama top-up. Only at this stage. Only enough to bridge to the next revenue cycle. Only if you can see clearly how the next month pays it back. Do not take a long-term loan for a short-term problem.

6 — Bring in a partner with cash. If a trusted person can inject money in exchange for a share of the business, that is a real option. But write down what percentage they own, what rights they have, and what happens if they leave.

Never do these in a cash crisis: borrow from a loan shark (30–100% interest), take customer money you cannot deliver on, use the rent or school fees money, or hide the problem from family or partners. All four turn a temporary problem into a permanent disaster.

The most important thing: tell someone. Silence is what turns a small cash gap into a business-ending crisis. Tell your family. Tell your chama. Tell a mentor. Tell your supplier. Somebody in your network has the answer or the introduction you need. But they cannot help if they do not know.

Practical exercise 3.6: Write down the 3 steps you will take in the first 48 hours of a cash crisis. Sign and date it. Put it with your business records so it is ready when you need it — not a plan made in panic.
