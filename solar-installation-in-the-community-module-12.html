1. Selecting a Community Project Site

Before you install any solar panels, you must choose the right site. A good site gets at least 5 hours of direct sunlight every day, even in the rainy season. Avoid places shaded by tall trees, buildings, or hills. In Africa, north-facing roofs or open ground are best because the sun moves north of the equator. Use a simple tool like a solar pathfinder or a phone app such as Sun Surveyor to check shading. Also, the site must be easy to access for installation and maintenance. If the community centre is on a hill, carrying panels and batteries up is hard work. Choose a flat area near a road.

Next, talk to the community. Hold a meeting with local leaders, womenâs groups, and youth. Ask them who will use the solar system, what they need to power (lights, phone charging, a fridge for vaccines, a water pump), and how they will pay for upkeep. For example, a health post might need 500 watts for lights and a vaccine fridge, while a school might need 1,000 watts for lights and computers. Write down these needs.

Then check the ground or roof. For ground mounts, dig a small test hole to see if the soil is soft or rocky. Rocky soil needs concrete foundations. For roofs, make sure the roof can hold the weight of panels and mounting frames. A typical 300-watt panel weighs about 20 kg. If the roof is old or made of grass, choose a ground mount instead.

Finally, measure the distance from the solar array to where the power will be used. Long distances mean thicker cables and more voltage drop. Keep the distance under 20 metres if possible. Also, plan for security: panels and batteries are valuable. A fence or locked room reduces theft. Document everything with photos and notes. This site selection saves you time, money, and arguments later.

2. Final System Design and Approval

Now you turn the communityâs needs into a real design. Start by listing every appliance and its wattage. For example: 10 LED lights at 5W each = 50W, 1 phone charging station at 20W, 1 vaccine fridge at 100W (but it runs 24 hours, so use 100W Ã 24h = 2,400Wh per day). Add up all daily energy in watt-hours (Wh). Multiply watts by hours used. For a typical small community centre, you might get 3,000Wh per day.

Next, size the solar array. In most of Africa, you get about 4 to 5 peak sun hours per day. Divide daily Wh by 4.5. For 3,000Wh, you need 3,000 Ã· 4.5 = 667W of panels. Round up to 800W, using four 200W panels. Always add 20% extra for cloudy days and dust. So buy 1,000W of panels.

Then size the battery. Use deep-cycle lead-acid or lithium. For 3,000Wh daily use, and you want 2 days of backup (no sun), you need 6,000Wh storage. But you should not discharge lead-acid below 50%, so double that to 12,000Wh. At 12V, that is 1,000Ah. That is big and expensive. So maybe reduce to 1 day backup: 6,000Wh, and at 50% depth, 12,000Wh still. Consider lithium, which can go to 80% depth, so 7,500Wh. A 48V lithium battery of 150Ah gives 7,200Wh. That is more practical.

Choose a charge controller and inverter. Use an MPPT controller sized for the arrayâs short-circuit current plus 25%. For 1,000W at 48V, current is about 21A, so a 30A MPPT works. Inverter must handle the total watts of all appliances running at once. If you have a fridge (100W), lights (50W), and phone charger (20W), total 170W, but surge for fridge motor might be 3x, so get a 500W pure sine wave inverter.

Write this design in a simple report: list of components, prices, and a diagram. Present it to the community committee and get their written approval. Also check local rules: some countries require a licensed electrician to sign off. Get that signature before you buy anything.

3. Installation Day 1: Mounting and Panels

On installation day 1, you will mount the panels. Start early to avoid the hottest sun. Gather tools: spanners (10mm, 13mm), screwdrivers, a drill with masonry bits, a spirit level, a tape measure, a hacksaw, and gloves. You also need mounting rails, brackets, bolts, and concrete mix if doing ground mount.

For a ground mount, mark the positions of the posts. Dig holes 50cm deep and 30cm wide. Mix concrete: 1 part cement, 2 parts sand, 3 parts gravel, and water. Set the posts in concrete, check they are vertical with a spirit level, and let them cure for at least 24 hours. If you are on a roof, use roof hooks or L-brackets. Drill into rafters, not just the roof sheet. Seal every hole with silicone to stop leaks.

Once the rails are fixed, carry the panels up carefully. Two people per panel. Place panels on the rails, leaving a small gap (2-3 cm) between them for airflow. Use the mounting clamps that come with the panels. Tighten bolts firmly but do not overtighten, as you can crack the glass. Check that all panels face the same directionâusually true north in the southern hemisphere, true south in the northern hemisphere. Tilt angle: for Africa, set the tilt equal to your latitude. For example, in Kenya (0Â° latitude), tilt 10Â° for rain runoff. In South Africa (30Â° latitude), tilt 30Â°.

After mounting, test each panel with a multimeter. Set it to DC volts. Put the probes on the panelâs positive and negative terminals. In full sun, a 12V panel should read about 18-20V open circuit. If a panel reads zero, check connections or replace it. Write down the voltage of each panel. This helps later if one fails. Cover panels with a cloth when not working to avoid shocks. At the end of the day, secure all tools and panels. Do not leave panels unbolted overnightâwind can flip them.

4. Installation Day 2: Wiring and Connections

Day 2 is for wiring. You will connect panels in series or parallel to match your controller. For a 48V system, connect four 12V panels in series (positive to negative) to get 48V. For more current, connect strings in parallel. Use solar cable (PV cable) that is UV-resistant and double-insulated. Size the cable: for 20A, use 4mmÂ²; for 30A, use 6mmÂ². Keep cable runs short to reduce voltage drop. Use MC4 connectorsâcrimp them with a proper crimping tool. Never twist wires together.

Run the cables from the array to the charge controller. Use conduit or cable clips to secure them. Drill holes through walls with a slight downward slope so water does not run in. Seal with silicone. Connect the controller to the battery first, then to the panels. This order protects the controller. Use a fuse or circuit breaker on the positive cable between battery and controller. Size the fuse at 1.25 times the controllerâs rated current. For a 30A controller, use a 40A fuse.

Next, connect the inverter to the battery. Use thick cables (at least 10mmÂ² for 500W inverter). Keep the inverter close to the batteryâunder 1.5 metres. Connect positive to positive, negative to negative. Install a DC breaker on the positive cable. Then wire the AC output of the inverter to a distribution board. If you are not a licensed electrician, do not do AC wiring. Get a professional. For DC lights and phone chargers, you can wire them yourself using 2.5mmÂ² cable. Use colour coding: red for positive, black for negative.

After all connections, double-check polarity with a multimeter. A reversed positive and negative can destroy the controller or inverter. Test the battery voltage: a 48V battery should read about 52V when full. Then turn on the controller and check its display. It should show battery voltage and charging current. If it shows zero, check fuses and connections. Finally, tidy all cables with cable ties. Label each cable with tape and a marker: âPV+â, âPV-â, âBattery+â, âBattery-â. This makes future maintenance easy.

5. System Testing and Handover

Testing day is when you prove the system works. Start with a visual check: all panels clean, all connections tight, no loose wires. Use a multimeter to measure open-circuit voltage of the array. For a 48V system, expect around 80-90V open circuit (four 20V panels in series). Then measure short-circuit current with a clamp meter. It should be close to the panelsâ rated current. If a string reads zero, find the fault.

Next, test the battery. Check voltage: 48V lead-acid full is about 50.9V; lithium full is about 54.6V. Turn on the controller and let it charge for 30 minutes. The battery voltage should rise slowly. Then turn on the inverter. Plug in a small load like a phone charger. It should work. Gradually add loads: lights, then fridge. Watch the inverterâs display for overload. If it shuts down, you have too much load. Turn off some appliances.

Test the low-voltage disconnect. With a lead-acid battery, the controller should cut off load at around 42V to protect the battery. You can simulate by draining the battery with a load, but that takes time. Instead, check the controller settings. Set the cut-off voltage correctly (e.g., 42V for 48V lead-acid). For lithium, set to 44V. Also set the reconnect voltage (e.g., 48V).

Now, train the community. Show two or three people how to read the controller, clean panels weekly, and check water levels in lead-acid batteries (if used). Give them a simple logbook to record daily energy produced and any problems. Hand over the user manual, warranty papers, and a list of spare fuses. Get a signed handover form from the community leader. Take photos of the finished installation. Finally, demonstrate how to safely shut down the system: turn off inverter, then controller, then battery breaker. This handover ensures the system lasts for years.

6. Project Evaluation and Celebration

After the system has run for one month, hold an evaluation meeting. Invite everyone who uses the solar system: the health worker, teachers, students, shop owners. Ask three simple questions: What works well? What problems have you seen? What would you change? Write down all answers. For example, maybe the lights are too dim in the evening, or the fridge runs too long and drains the battery. These are normal issues.

Check the logbook. Look at daily energy produced and used. If the battery is often below 50% in the morning, you need more panels or less load. If the inverter shuts down often, you need a bigger inverter. Use a clamp meter to measure current from panels on a sunny day. Compare to the design. If actual current is much lower, clean the panels or check for shading. Also check all connections for corrosion. Tighten any loose bolts. Replace any burnt fuses.

Calculate the money saved. For example, if the community used to spend 2,000 shillings per month on kerosene and phone charging, and now they spend 500 shillings on battery maintenance, they save 1,500 shillings monthly. That is 18,000 shillings per year. Share this number with the community. It shows the projectâs value. Also note any income generated: a phone charging business might earn 200 shillings per day.

Then celebrate. Organise a small event with music, speeches, and food. Invite local leaders and the media. Thank everyone who helped: the installers, the community members who carried panels, the women who cooked. Give certificates to trainees. This celebration builds ownership. People will protect the system because they are proud of it. Finally, plan for the future: set up a maintenance fund. Each user contributes a small monthly fee (e.g., 100 shillings) for replacements. Choose a caretaker and schedule quarterly check-ups. Document lessons learned for your next project. This evaluation and celebration closes the loop and ensures sustainability.

Practical Reference: Community Solar Project Implementation

This practical reference consolidates the field-level knowledge you need to move a community solar project from site selection through evaluation and celebration. It is written for technicians, cooperative managers, and local entrepreneurs who must deliver a working system on time, on budget, and to standard â then keep it running. In rural and peri-urban Africa, the difference between a solar project that lasts ten years and one that fails in eighteen months is rarely the panels. It is the quality of the installation, the clarity of the handover, and the strength of the local service model built around it.

Use this module alongside the six lessons in this course. Each section below maps to a lesson and gives you the numbers, checks, and references you will actually use on site.

1. Selecting a Community Project Site

Site selection determines your energy yield, your installation cost, and your long-term maintenance burden. A site that looks convenient may be shaded at noon; a site that looks perfect may be a two-hour walk from the nearest road. Use the checklist below before you commit.

The single most important measurement is peak sun hours (PSH). Across most of sub-Saharan Africa, PSH ranges from about 4.0 in the humid coastal belt to 6.5 in the Sahel and southern African interior. A system sized for 5.5 PSH will underperform badly if installed at a 4.0 PSH site. Always use local irradiance data, not a national average.

2. Final System Design and Approval

Design is where you lock in cost and performance. For a community project, design must balance three constraints: the load profile, the battery autonomy required, and the budget available. A common mistake is to design for the peak load rather than the daily energy demand. A clinic may have a 5 kW peak but only 12 kWh daily consumption; sizing the array for 5 kW continuous will waste money.

Use this formula for array sizing:

Array size (kWp) = Daily energy demand (kWh) Ã· (PSH Ã system efficiency)

System efficiency accounts for cable losses, inverter losses, temperature derating, and dust. In African conditions, use 0.70â0.75 for a well-designed system, and 0.60â0.65 if the site is dusty or hot. For battery sizing, multiply daily demand by the number of autonomy days required (typically 1â2 for community systems without a generator).

Approval requirements vary by country. In Kenya, a solar installation above a certain capacity must be signed off by an EPRA-licensed electrician. In Nigeria, NEMSA and the Standards Organisation of Nigeria (SON) set equipment and installation standards. Always confirm local requirements before ordering equipment.

3. Installation Day 1: Mounting and Panels

Day 1 is structural. Everything you do today determines whether the array survives the next hailstorm and whether the roof or ground mount stays true for a decade.

For roof mounts, verify the roof structure can carry the load â a typical panel adds 12â15 kg/mÂ² including mounting. For ground mounts, dig foundations below the frost line if applicable, or at least 600 mm in stable soil. In coastal West Africa, use galvanised or aluminium mounting to resist salt corrosion. In the Sahel, leave at least 100 mm clearance under panels for airflow; module temperatures above 65 Â°C reduce output by 10â15%.

Tilt angle: for off-grid systems, tilt at latitude plus 5â10 degrees to favour winter production. For year-round community loads, latitude is usually fine. In equatorial regions, a 10â15 degree tilt is sufficient and helps with rain self-cleaning.

4. Installation Day 2: Wiring and Connections

Day 2 is electrical. Poor wiring causes more system failures than any other factor. Use correct cable sizes, correct polarity, and correct torque on all terminals. A loose DC terminal will arc, melt, and start a fire.

Follow this sequence: array combiner â DC isolator â charge controller â battery â inverter â AC distribution. Never disconnect the battery while the array is connected; the controller can be damaged by open-circuit voltage. Label every cable at both ends. Use ferrules on stranded cable. Test continuity and polarity before energising.

â  SAFETY WARNING â DC ARC AND BATTERY HAZARD

PV arrays generate dangerous DC voltage whenever there is light. Unlike AC, DC arcs do not self-extinguish at zero crossing. Never work on live DC circuits. Isolate the array, verify zero voltage with a rated meter, and lock off before touching terminals. Battery banks can deliver thousands of amps into a short circuit â remove metal jewellery, use insulated tools, and never short a battery terminal to test it. Lead-acid batteries produce explosive hydrogen; ventilate battery rooms. LiFePO4 batteries can enter thermal runaway if overcharged or physically damaged â use a BMS and never bypass it.

5. System Testing and Handover

Testing is not a formality. It is your evidence that the system works and your protection if something fails later. Test in this order: insulation resistance, polarity, open-circuit voltage, short-circuit current, charge controller settings, inverter output, earth continuity, and RCD trip time.

Record every reading in a commissioning sheet. Photograph the installation. Hand over the following to the community: system manual, wiring diagram, commissioning sheet, maintenance schedule, and contact details for service. Train at least two local people â not one â so that knowledge survives if one person leaves.

6. Project Evaluation and Celebration

Evaluation should happen at 30 days and 12 months. At 30 days, check for loose connections, battery state of charge, and user complaints. At 12 months, review energy production against design, maintenance costs, and user satisfaction. Celebration matters: it builds ownership, recognises the technicians, and creates demand for the next project. Invite local officials, the cooperative, and the users. Share the numbers publicly â kWh generated, money saved, services delivered.
