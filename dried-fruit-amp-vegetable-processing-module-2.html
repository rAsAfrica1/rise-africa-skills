1. Choosing the Best Mangoes, Bananas, and Pineapples for Drying

For drying, you need fruits that are ripe but still firm. Overripe fruits are too soft and mushy; underripe fruits lack sweetness and flavour. For mangoes, choose varieties like Kent, Keitt, or local fibrous types that hold their shape. The fruit should give slightly when pressed but not be squishy. Avoid mangoes with black spots or soft patches. For bananas, use firm ripe plantains or dessert bananas like Cavendish. They should be yellow with no green tips, but not brown. Overripe bananas become sticky and dark during drying. For pineapples, select ones with a sweet aroma at the base and golden-yellow skin. The leaves should be green and plump. A ripe pineapple will have a slight give when squeezed. Avoid pineapples with soft spots or a fermented smell.

In Africa, you can find these fruits at local markets or farms. Buy in bulk when in season to reduce cost. For example, mango season in West Africa runs from April to July. During this time, a crate of mangoes can be half the off-season price. Check with local farmers for bulk discounts. Always inspect each fruit individually. Remove any that are bruised or diseased. A single rotten fruit can spoil the whole batch.

Tools you need: a sharp knife, a cutting board, and a clean basket. Wear gloves if you have sensitive skin, as mango sap can cause irritation. Work in a shaded, well-ventilated area. Sort fruits by ripeness so you can process them in batches. For bananas, separate them from the bunch to slow ripening. For pineapples, twist off the crown and store upside down for a day to distribute sugars.

After selecting, wash the fruits thoroughly. Use clean water and a soft brush to remove dirt. For mangoes, you can peel and slice immediately. For bananas, peel just before drying to prevent browning. For pineapples, remove the skin and eyes. Cut into uniform slices or chunks, about 5-8 mm thick. Uniform size ensures even drying. Spread on drying trays without overlapping. Dry at 55-60Ã‚Â°C for 6-12 hours, depending on fruit and thickness. Proper selection and preparation give you high-quality dried products that fetch better prices at market.

2. How to Wash and Sanitize Fruits and Vegetables

Washing removes dirt, insects, and pesticide residues. Sanitizing kills harmful bacteria and mould spores that can spoil your dried products. Start with clean hands and clean tools. Use potable waterÃ¢Â€Â”if you wouldn't drink it, don't use it on produce. For washing, fill a large basin with water. Add 1 tablespoon of salt per litre of water for leafy vegetables to remove dirt and insects. For fruits with firm skins like mangoes and pineapples, use a soft brush to scrub the surface. For bananas, simply rinse under running water; their thick skin protects the flesh.

After washing, sanitize. The cheapest sanitizer is vinegar: mix 1 part white vinegar with 3 parts water. Soak produce for 5-10 minutes. For stronger sanitizing, use food-grade hydrogen peroxide (3% solution) or commercial produce wash. In Africa, you can also use a diluted bleach solution: 1 teaspoon of unscented bleach per 4 litres of water. Soak for 2 minutes, then rinse thoroughly with clean water. Never mix bleach with vinegarÃ¢Â€Â”it creates toxic gas.

For vegetables like tomatoes, peppers, and onions, wash and sanitize the same way. Remove stems and cores before sanitizing to expose more surface. After sanitizing, rinse again with clean water. Pat dry with a clean cloth or paper towel. Moisture on the surface can slow drying and encourage mould. If you don't have a salad spinner, air-dry on a clean rack for 10 minutes.

Sanitizing is especially important if you plan to sell your dried products. Buyers expect clean, safe food. A simple test: after sanitizing, your produce should smell fresh, not like chemicals. If you use bleach, the smell should disappear after rinsing. Always sanitize in small batches so you don't overcrowd the basin. Change the water when it becomes cloudy. For 10 kg of mangoes, you'll need about 20 litres of wash water and 10 litres of sanitizing solution. Set up a three-basin system: wash, sanitize, rinse. This is how commercial processors do it. With practice, you can wash and sanitize 10 kg of fruit in 30 minutes. Clean produce means better drying, longer shelf life, and happier customers.

3. Peeling, Coring, and Slicing Techniques for Even Drying

Even drying starts with even cutting. If slices are different thicknesses, thin ones burn while thick ones stay wet. Aim for 5-8 mm thickness for most fruits and vegetables. Use a sharp knife or a mandoline slicer. A mandoline gives uniform slices quickly, but be careful of your fingers. For mangoes, peel with a vegetable peeler or sharp knife. Cut along both sides of the flat seed to get two cheeks. Score the flesh in a grid and scoop out cubes, or slice into strips. For bananas, peel and slice into 5 mm rounds or lengthwise strips. For pineapples, cut off the top and bottom, slice off the skin, and remove the eyes with a small knife. Cut into rings or chunks.

Coring is key for apples, pears, and peppers. An apple corer removes the seed core in one push. If you don't have one, cut the fruit into quarters and slice out the core. For peppers, cut off the top, remove the seeds and white membranes, then slice into rings or strips. For tomatoes, remove the stem and cut into halves or slices. For onions, peel and slice into rings or dice. Uniform pieces dry at the same rate.

Use a clean cutting boardÃ¢Â€Â”wood or plastic. Sanitize it before and after use. Work in batches. For 1 kg of mangoes, you should get about 700 g of usable flesh after peeling and coring. The rest is waste. Keep waste for compost or animal feed. To prevent browning while cutting, dip slices in lemon water (1 tablespoon lemon juice per litre of water) as you go. This is especially important for apples, bananas, and potatoes.

After cutting, spread slices on drying trays in a single layer. Do not overlap. For sticky fruits like bananas, lightly oil the tray or use a mesh liner. If you don't have commercial trays, use clean window screens or bamboo mats. Ensure good airflow. Pre-heat your dryer to 55-60Ã‚Â°C. Load trays quickly to avoid temperature drops. Drying time varies: mangoes 6-8 hours, bananas 8-10 hours, pineapples 10-12 hours. Check every 2 hours. Proper peeling, coring, and slicing are the difference between a premium product and a rejected batch. Practice your knife skillsÃ¢Â€Â”speed comes with time, but uniformity comes first.

4. Using Lemon Juice or Salt to Prevent Browning

When you cut fruits like apples, bananas, and mangoes, the flesh turns brown. This is called enzymatic browning. It doesn't make the fruit unsafe, but it looks unappetizing and lowers market value. You can prevent it with lemon juice or salt. Lemon juice contains citric acid, which slows the browning reaction. Salt works by denaturing the enzymes. Both are cheap and available in any African market.

For lemon juice: mix 1 tablespoon of fresh or bottled lemon juice per 1 litre of clean water. Soak sliced fruit for 5 minutes. Remove and drain. You can also rub lemon juice directly on the cut surfaces. For 1 kg of apple slices, use about 2 tablespoons of lemon juice. If lemons are expensive, use lime juice or tamarind water. Tamarind is common in West Africa and works well. For salt: make a brine with 1 teaspoon of salt per 1 litre of water. Soak slices for 5-10 minutes. Then rinse with clean water to remove excess salt. Salt can affect flavour, so use it for vegetables like potatoes and eggplant, not for sweet fruits.

Another method is blanching. Dip slices in boiling water for 1-2 minutes, then transfer to ice water. This deactivates the enzymes. Blanching works well for vegetables but can make fruits mushy. For fruits, lemon juice or salt is better. You can also use ascorbic acid (vitamin C) powder, but it's more expensive. In many African markets, you can find "fruit fresh" or citric acid powder. Use 1 teaspoon per litre of water.

Preventing browning is not just about looks. Browned fruit may develop off-flavours and lose vitamin C. It also signals to buyers that the product is old. So always treat your slices before drying. Set up a dipping station next to your cutting board. As you slice, drop pieces into the lemon water. When the bowl is full, let sit for 5 minutes, then transfer to drying trays. For large batches, use a large basin. Change the solution every 2 hours or when it becomes cloudy. With proper treatment, your dried fruits will stay bright and golden. This simple step can increase your selling price by 20-30%.

5. Sorting and Grading Produce for Quality Control

Sorting means removing bad items. Grading means separating good items into quality levels. Both are essential for consistent dried products. Start by sorting out any fruit that is bruised, diseased, insect-damaged, or overripe. A single rotten fruit can spread mould to the whole batch. Remove and compost it. For vegetables, discard any with soft spots, holes, or discoloration. After sorting, grade the remaining produce into three grades: Grade A (premium), Grade B (standard), and Grade C (processing). Grade A fruits are firm, ripe, uniform in size and colour, with no blemishes. These will give the best dried product. Grade B has minor blemishes or slight unevenness but is still good. Grade C is overripe or damaged but can be used for sauces, purÃƒÂ©es, or animal feed.

Grading helps you set prices. Grade A dried mango slices can sell for twice as much as Grade B. Buyers like consistency. If you mix grades, you get a lower average price. So grade before drying. Use simple tools: a clean table, a sorting tray, and a grading chart. Draw pictures of each grade so workers can match. For example, a Grade A dried mango slice is bright orange, pliable, and uniform. Grade B is slightly brown or uneven. Grade C is dark, brittle, or broken.

In Africa, many small processors skip grading. That's a mistake. A buyer in Nairobi or Lagos will pay more for graded products. Start by grading at the raw material stage. Then grade again after drying. Remove any pieces that are burnt, mouldy, or under-dried. Pack each grade separately. Label with grade, date, and weight. For export, you need consistent grading. Even for local markets, grading builds trust. You can charge a premium for Grade A. Use Grade B for local sales or value-added products like fruit leather. Grade C goes to jam or juice. Nothing is wasted.

Train your workers to sort and grade. Use a simple checklist. Check for colour, firmness, size, and defects. Do a final inspection before packaging. With practice, sorting and grading become quick. For 10 kg of fresh mangoes, sorting and grading takes about 20 minutes. It's worth the time. Your reputation depends on quality.

6. Reducing Waste by Using Overripe or Blemished Produce

Many farmers and market vendors throw away overripe or blemished fruits. But these can be turned into high-quality dried products if handled correctly. Overripe fruits are sweeter and have more flavour. Blemished fruits just need the bad parts cut away. By using them, you reduce waste and increase profits. For example, a mango with a small bruise can be trimmed, and the rest dried. A soft banana can be mashed and dried as fruit leather. A wrinkled pineapple can be juiced and the pulp dried.

Start by inspecting each fruit. Cut away any mouldy, rotten, or insect-damaged parts. Use a clean knife and cut at least 1 cm beyond the damaged area. Soak the good pieces in lemon water or salt water to prevent browning. Then slice and dry as usual. Overripe fruits may be softer, so slice them thicker (8-10 mm) to prevent them from falling apart. They may also dry faster because of higher sugar content. Check them more often. For very soft fruits, you can purÃƒÂ©e them and spread on trays to make fruit leather. Add a little lemon juice and sugar if desired. Dry at 55Ã‚Â°C for 8-12 hours until pliable.

Blemished vegetables like tomatoes, peppers, and onions can also be used. Cut away bad spots, then dice or slice. For tomatoes, overripe ones make excellent sun-dried tomatoes. For peppers, remove soft spots and dry the rest. For onions, use the outer layers if they are still firm. Always sanitize after trimming. Never use produce that is mouldy or has a foul smellÃ¢Â€Â”mould produces toxins that can make people sick.

In Africa, post-harvest losses can be 30-50%. By using overripe and blemished produce, you can turn a waste stream into income. Set up a relationship with local market sellers. They often throw away fruits that are too ripe to sell. You can buy them cheaply or get them free. Process them the same day. For example, a crate of overripe mangoes might cost 1/4 of the price of perfect ones. After drying, the difference in quality is minimal if you trim and treat properly. This is a smart business move. It also helps the environment. Start small: collect 5 kg of overripe bananas and make dried banana chips. Sell them at a premium as "natural" or "no waste" products. Customers love a good story.

Practical Reference: Drying Methods, Equipment, and Quality Benchmarks for Mango, Banana, and Pineapple

Across sub-Saharan Africa, small and medium dried-fruit enterprises typically choose between three drying routes: open-sun drying, solar drying (cabinet or tunnel), and electric or gas-powered mechanical dehydration. â ï¸ SAFETY WARNING â FOOD SAFETY AND CHEMICAL HAZARDS

Dried fruits are classified as low-moisture foods but are NOT sterile. Salmonella, Listeria monocytogenes, and mycotoxin-producing moulds (especially Aspergillus flavus producing aflatoxins) can survive and even concentrate during drying. Key hazards:

  • Never dry on bare ground, tarpaulins, or surfaces exposed to animal or human waste. Use raised, food-grade racks.
  • Never use sulphur dioxide or sodium metabisulphite without measuring and recording concentrations. WHO/FAO Codex limits sulphite residues in dried fruit to 2,000 mg/kg (as SOâ) for mango and pineapple, and 1,000 mg/kg for banana. Over-sulphiting causes asthma attacks in sensitive consumers and is a legal violation in the EU and Kenya.
  • Never dry fruit that has visible mould, insect damage, or off-odours. Aflatoxin is heat-stable and cannot be destroyed by drying.
  • Always test final moisture with a calibrated moisture meter. Fruit above 18% moisture will grow mould within weeks.
  • Always store dried fruit in food-grade, airtight containers away from chemicals, fuel, and pesticides.

Reference: WHO/FAO Codex Alimentarius CXS 130-1981; WHO, Five Keys to Safer Food; Kenya Bureau of Standards KS EAS 39:2019.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Match method to market: If your buyer is a supermarket or exporter, choose solar cabinet or mechanical drying â open-sun product will be rejected on colour and microbiology.
  • Calibrate your moisture meter weekly against a laboratory oven test. A 2% error can turn a 12-month shelf life into a 2-month mould problem.
  • Record every batch: date, fresh weight, dry weight, drying method, temperature, time, pre-treatment used, and final moisture. This is your HACCP evidence and your cost-control tool.
  • Calculate your recovery rate: Mango 12â18%, banana 20â30%, pineapple 10â15%. If your recovery is outside these ranges, your slicing thickness or drying time is wrong.
  • Pre-treat consistently: Lemon juice (0.5â1% citric acid) or 0.2% sodium metabisulphite dip for 5â10 minutes prevents browning and extends shelf life.
  • Cool before packing: Dried fruit must reach room temperature before sealing, or condensation will cause mould.
  • Price on quality, not weight: Solar-dried mango at USD 7/kg beats open-sun mango at USD 2.50/kg on every margin metric.
  • Plan for power outages: If you use a mechanical dehydrator, have a backup generator or a solar cabinet as a fallback to avoid losing a batch mid-cycle.
