Lessons

1. Service Level Agreements (SLAs) and KPIs

A Service Level Agreement (SLA) is a contract between you and your client that defines what service you will provide, how fast, and what happens if you fail. In Africa, where internet can be unreliable and power cuts happen, SLAs are critical to manage expectations. For example, if you run a web hosting company in Lagos, your SLA might promise 99.5% uptime (which allows about 3.6 hours of downtime per month). You must also define response times: for critical issues, respond within 1 hour; for minor issues, within 8 hours. Use tools like Google Docs or a simple contract template to write your SLA. Include penalties if you miss targets, like a 10% discount on the monthly fee. Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) measure how well you meet the SLA. Common KPIs include average response time, resolution time, and customer satisfaction score (CSAT). Track these using a spreadsheet or a free tool like Trello or Airtable. For a small IT support business in Nairobi, you might aim for 90% of tickets resolved within 24 hours. Review KPIs monthly with your team and share a simple report with clients. This builds trust and shows professionalism. Remember, an SLA is not just paperworkit's a promise. If you can't meet a target, renegotiate before signing. Always include a clause about 'force majeure' (like natural disasters or government actions) to protect yourself. Start with simple SLAs and adjust as you grow.

2. Customer Support and Help Desk Management

Customer support is the frontline of your tech business. In Africa, many customers prefer WhatsApp or phone calls over email. Set up a WhatsApp Business account with a greeting message and away replies. Use a free help desk tool like Freshdesk (free for up to 2 agents) or Zoho Desk to track tickets. When a customer contacts you, log the issue immediately. Assign a ticket number and priority: High (system down), Medium (slow performance), Low (how-to question). Train your support staff to use a simple script: greet, listen, apologize for inconvenience, ask clarifying questions, and give a timeline. For example, a customer in Accra reporting a payment gateway error should get a response within 15 minutes. If you can't solve it immediately, tell them when you will follow up. Use a knowledge basea Google Doc or Notion pagewith answers to common problems. This reduces repeat questions. Measure your support with metrics: first response time, resolution time, and customer satisfaction (CSAT). After resolving a ticket, send a short survey: 'How did we do? 1-5 stars.' Aim for 4.5+ average. If a customer is angry, stay calm and focus on the solution. Escalate to a manager if needed. For after-hours support, use a rotating on-call schedule. In many African countries, mobile data is cheap, so encourage customers to send screenshots via WhatsApp. Always follow up after 24 hours to confirm the issue is fixed. This personal touch sets you apart from big competitors.

3. Incident Management and Problem Resolution

An incident is any unplanned interruption to your service, like a server crash or a network outage. Problem resolution is finding the root cause to prevent it from happening again. In Africa, common incidents include power failures, fibre cable cuts, and software bugs. You need a clear process. First, detect the incidentuse monitoring tools like UptimeRobot (free) or Pingdom to alert you via SMS or email when your site goes down. Second, classify severity: P1 (critical, all users affected), P2 (major, some users), P3 (minor). For P1, assemble a response team immediately. Communicate with customers: post a status update on your website or WhatsApp group every 30 minutes until resolved. Third, resolve the incidentfix the immediate issue, even if it's a temporary workaround. For example, if your server in Johannesburg fails, switch to a backup server in Cape Town. Fourth, after resolution, conduct a 'post-mortem' within 48 hours. Write a simple report: what happened, why, how you fixed it, and what you'll change to prevent it. Use the '5 Whys' method: ask 'why' five times to get to the root cause. For instance, why did the server fail? Power surge. Why? No surge protector. Why? Not in budget. Then you can justify buying one. Track incidents in a log (Google Sheets) with date, duration, and cause. Review monthly to spot patterns. If the same incident happens twice, it's now a problemassign someone to fix it permanently. Train your team on this process so everyone knows their role. Quick, transparent incident handling builds customer trust.

4. Quality Assurance and Testing

Quality Assurance (QA) means checking your work before the customer sees it. In tech services, a single bug can lose you a client. Testing is how you find bugs. For a small software team in Kigali, start with manual testing: before releasing any update, test every feature on different devices (Android, iPhone, laptop) and browsers (Chrome, Firefox). Use a checklist in Google Sheets or Trello. For websites, test load speed with Google PageSpeed Insightsaim for under 3 seconds on 3G. For apps, test on a slow network (like 2G) to simulate rural areas. Write test cases: 'User can log in with correct password' and 'User sees error with wrong password.' Run these tests every time you change code. Automate repetitive tests using free tools like Selenium or Cypress for web apps, or Appium for mobile. But automation takes time to set up, so start manual. Also, do 'user acceptance testing' (UAT): ask a real customer to try the product and give feedback. Pay them a small fee or give a discount. Track bugs in a tool like Bugzilla or a simple Google Sheet with columns: ID, description, severity, status. Fix critical bugs before release; minor bugs can wait. After release, monitor for errors using tools like Sentry (free tier). Hold a weekly QA meeting to review open bugs. Remember, quality is not just about codeit's about customer experience. A typo in an invoice or a broken link in an email can damage trust. So, proofread everything. Create a simple QA process document and train your team. Consistency is key.

5. Scalability and Capacity Planning

Scalability is your ability to handle more customers or data without crashing. Capacity planning is predicting when you'll need more resources. For a tech business in Africa, growth can be suddena viral post or a new contract can triple your users overnight. Start by measuring your current usage: how many users per day, how much data you store, how many support tickets. Use free tools like Google Analytics for websites, or server monitoring like Netdata. If you run a cloud service, check your CPU and memory usage. For example, if your server is at 80% CPU during peak hours, you're close to capacity. Plan for 2x growth: if you have 1000 users, prepare for 2000. Options: upgrade your server (vertical scaling) or add more servers (horizontal scaling). In Africa, cloud providers like AWS, Azure, and local ones like Africa Data Centres offer pay-as-you-go. But bandwidth can be expensive, so optimize: compress images, use caching (e.g., Redis), and a Content Delivery Network (CDN) like Cloudflare (free tier). For a help desk, scalability means hiring more agents or using chatbots for common questions. Create a capacity plan spreadsheet: list resources (servers, staff, licenses), current usage, and trigger points for action (e.g., 'when CPU >70% for 3 days, upgrade'). Review monthly. Also, test your system under load: use tools like JMeter or Loader.io to simulate 1000 users. If it fails, fix before it happens. Document your architecture so you can scale quickly. And always have a backup planif your main server fails, can you switch to a backup in another region? Scalability is not just about technology; it's about processes and people. Train your team to handle increased volume.

6. Vendor and Supplier Management

As a tech business, you rely on vendors for internet, hosting, software, and hardware. Managing these relationships well saves money and prevents downtime. Start by listing all your vendors: your ISP, cloud provider, domain registrar, payment gateway, and any freelancers. For each, note contract terms, costs, and contact person. In Africa, vendor reliability varies, so always have a backup. For example, if your main ISP in Kampala has frequent outages, sign a secondary contract with a mobile data provider like MTN or Airtel as a fallback. Negotiate contracts: ask for a discount for annual payment, or a service level agreement (SLA) with penalties. For critical vendors, meet quarterly to review performance. Use a simple scorecard: rate them on uptime, response time, and cost. If a vendor consistently underperforms, switch. But switching costs time, so weigh the risk. For software vendors, check if they offer local support or training. For hardware, buy from reputable dealers and keep spare parts (e.g., extra routers, cables). Pay vendors on time to maintain good creditmany African suppliers offer discounts for early payment. Use a tool like Google Sheets to track invoices and renewal dates. Set reminders 30 days before a contract expires. Also, diversify: don't rely on one vendor for everything. For cloud, you might use AWS for compute and Cloudflare for CDN. For payment, have a backup like Paystack or Flutterwave. Build relationships: a good vendor can alert you to issues before they happen. Finally, document everythingemails, contracts, and meeting notes. If a vendor fails, you have evidence to negotiate or exit. Treat vendors as partners, not just suppliers.

<h2>2. Customer Support and Help Desk Management</h2>
<p>African customers increasingly reach businesses through WhatsApp, USSD, and phone calls not just email. Your help desk must meet them where they are. A ticket that arrives via WhatsApp at 21:00 deserves a logged ticket and an automated acknowledgment, even if the human reply comes the next morning.</p>
<p>Practical rules for lean teams:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>One queue, many channels.</strong> Whether it comes via email, WhatsApp, or a web form, every request becomes a ticket with an ID.</li>
<li><strong>Tiered triage.</strong> Tier 1 handles password resets and how-to questions. Tier 2 handles configuration. Tier 3 handles engineering.</li>
<li><strong>Knowledge base first.</strong> A well-maintained FAQ reduces ticket volume by 2040% within a year.</li>
<li><strong>Language coverage.</strong> In markets like Nigeria or Kenya, support in English, Pidgin, Swahili, or French expands reach significantly.</li>
</ul>

<h2>3. Incident Management and Problem Resolution</h2>
<p>An <em>incident</em> is an unplanned interruption. A <em>problem</em> is the underlying cause of one or more incidents. Confusing the two leads to firefighting without ever fixing the root cause.</p>
<p>Use severity levels to drive response:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>P1 (Critical):</strong> Service down for all users. Respond in 15 minutes, resolve in 4 hours.</li>
<li><strong>P2 (High):</strong> Major feature broken or many users affected. Respond in 1 hour, resolve in 8 hours.</li>
<li><strong>P3 (Medium):</strong> Degraded performance or single-user impact. Respond in 4 hours, resolve in 2 days.</li>
<li><strong>P4 (Low):</strong> Cosmetic or enhancement request. Respond in 1 business day.</li>
</ul>
<p>Every P1 and P2 incident requires a blameless post-mortem within five working days. Document what happened, what you learned, and what changed. This is how small African teams build institutional memory without expensive tooling.</p>

<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0; color:#721c24;">If an incident involves a data breach, you have legal obligations under national data protection laws Nigeria's NDPA (2023), Kenya's DPA (2019), South Africa's POPIA, and Ghana's DPA (2012). Failure to disclose can result in fines of up to 2% of annual turnover or millions of naira/shilling/rand. Do not attempt to hide breaches to protect your SLA metrics. Document, disclose, and remediate.</p>

<h2>4. Quality Assurance and Testing</h2>
<p>QA is not a luxury for funded startups. A single payment bug can destroy customer trust faster than any marketing can rebuild it. For African digital services handling mobile money (M-Pesa, Paystack, Flutterwave, MTN MoMo), testing must cover:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Functional testing:</strong> Does the feature do what it should?</li>
<li><strong>Regression testing:</strong> Did the new release break something old?</li>
<li><strong>Performance testing:</strong> Can the system handle month-end traffic spikes?</li>
<li><strong>Offline and low-bandwidth testing:</strong> Does the app behave gracefully on 2G or when the connection drops?</li>
<li><strong>Payment reconciliation testing:</strong> Are failed transactions refunded correctly?</li>
</ul>
<p>Automate what you can, but never skip manual testing on payment flows and authentication. A test script that runs before every deployment is worth more than a thousand-page QA manual.</p>

<h2>6. Vendor and Supplier Management</h2>
<p>No African digital business operates alone. You depend on ISPs, cloud providers, payment gateways, SMS aggregators, and hardware suppliers. Each is a point of failure.</p>
<p>Good vendor management means:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Written contracts with SLAs.</strong> Verbal promises from an ISP account manager mean nothing at 2 a.m.</li>
<li><strong>Redundancy for critical vendors.</strong> Two ISPs, two payment gateways, two SMS providers where budget allows.</li>
<li><strong>Quarterly reviews.</strong> Track vendor performance against their SLA. Escalate early.</li>
<li><strong>Exit plans.</strong> Know how you would leave a vendor before you need to.</li>
<li><strong>Local sourcing where possible.</strong> Local vendors understand local conditions and can respond faster.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>
<ol>
<li>Every client contract includes a written SLA with uptime, response, and resolution targets.</li>
<li>All support channels (email, WhatsApp, phone, web) feed into one ticketing system.</li>
<li>Incidents are classified P1P4 with defined response and resolution times.</li>
<li>Every P1/P2 incident gets a blameless post-mortem within five working days.</li>
<li>Payment and authentication flows are manually tested before every release.</li>
<li>Capacity dashboards are reviewed weekly against the triggers in Table 2.</li>
<li>Critical vendors have written SLAs and at least one backup option.</li>
<li>Data breach response plan is documented and tested annually, aligned with NDPA/DPA/POPIA.</li>
</ol>
