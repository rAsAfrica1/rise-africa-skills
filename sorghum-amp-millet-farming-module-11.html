1. Keeping simple farm records: costs, yields, and sales

You don't need a computer or expensive software to keep good records. A simple notebook and a pen will do. Every time you spend money on your sorghum or millet farm, write it down. Every time you harvest or sell, write it down. This is the foundation of knowing if you are making a profit.

Start with three columns: Date, Description, and Amount. For costs, record things like seeds, fertiliser, labour, ploughing, transport, and bags. For example, if you buy 5 kg of sorghum seed for 2,000 KES, write: '15 March, sorghum seed, 2,000'. For yields, record the bags harvested per plot. If you harvest 10 bags of 50 kg from one acre, write: '10 July, sorghum harvest, 10 bags'. For sales, record the buyer, quantity, and price. If you sell 5 bags at 3,000 KES each, write: '20 July, sold 5 bags to local miller, 15,000'.

At the end of the season, add up all costs in one column and all sales in another. The difference is your profit or loss. Many farmers are surprised to see that after all expenses, their profit is small. But without records, you can't tell which crop or which field is losing money. You also need records to apply for a loan. Banks and microfinance institutions will ask for your records to see if you can repay.

Keep your notebook in a safe, dry place. At the end of each month, review your entries. You can also use a simple exercise book with columns drawn with a ruler. If you have a smartphone, apps like 'Farm Records' or 'AgriTracker' can help, but a notebook is just as good. The key is consistency: record every transaction, no matter how small. Over time, you will see patterns, like which month you spend the most, and you can plan better.

Remember, records are not for anyone elsethey are for you. They help you make decisions: should you plant more sorghum next year? Should you change your fertiliser? Should you negotiate a better price? Without records, you are farming blind.

2. Calculating profit and loss for your sorghum and millet enterprise

Profit is what remains after you subtract all your costs from your total sales. If your costs are higher than your sales, you have a loss. To calculate profit for your sorghum or millet, you need to know your total revenue and total costs for the season.

Total revenue is the money you received from selling your grain, and also from selling fodder, seed, or any other product. For example, if you sold 20 bags of sorghum at 3,000 KES per bag, that's 60,000 KES. If you also sold 5 bags of millet at 4,000 KES, that's 20,000 KES. Total revenue = 80,000 KES.

Total costs include all variable costs: seeds, fertiliser, pesticides, hired labour, transport, and bags. Also include fixed costs like land rent or depreciation on tools. Suppose you spent 10,000 KES on seed, 15,000 KES on fertiliser, 8,000 KES on labour, 2,000 KES on transport, and 5,000 KES on rent. Total costs = 40,000 KES. Profit = 80,000 - 40,000 = 40,000 KES.

But be careful: some costs are hidden. Did you use your own labour? If you worked 100 hours, that labour has value. If you hired someone, you would pay them. So even if you don't pay yourself, you should estimate the cost of your own labour. Also, if you used manure from your own animals, that has value too. For simple records, you can note these as 'own labour' and 'own manure' but not include them in cash costs. However, for true profit, you should include them.

To make better decisions, calculate profit per acre. If you planted 2 acres of sorghum and made 40,000 KES profit, that's 20,000 KES per acre. Compare that to other crops. If millet gives you 25,000 KES per acre, you might shift more land to millet. Also, calculate your break-even point: how many bags must you sell to cover costs? If your total costs are 40,000 KES and you sell at 3,000 KES per bag, you need to sell 14 bags just to break even. Anything above that is profit.

Keep these calculations in your record book. At the end of each season, compare with previous seasons. Are your profits increasing? If not, why? Maybe input costs are rising, or yields are falling. With these numbers, you can make smart choices for next season.

3. Accessing micro-loans and agricultural credit

Micro-loans are small loans, often from microfinance institutions (MFIs), savings groups, or banks, designed for farmers like you. They can help you buy better seeds, fertiliser, or equipment. But you must borrow wisely and repay on time.

First, know your credit options. In many African countries, there are agricultural banks, e.g., the Agricultural Finance Corporation in Kenya, or the Nigeria Agricultural Cooperative and Rural Development Bank. Microfinance institutions like BRAC, FINCA, or local SACCOs (Savings and Credit Cooperatives) also lend to farmers. Savings groups (also called village banks or chamas) are common: you contribute weekly, then borrow from the group at low interest. These are often the easiest to access because they don't require collateral.

To get a loan, you need records. Lenders want to see your farm records, proof of land ownership or rental agreement, and a repayment plan. If you have kept records for at least one season, you can show your yields, sales, and costs. That builds trust. Also, you may need a guarantor or collateral, like livestock or land title. Some loans are group loans, where members guarantee each other.

Before borrowing, calculate how much you need and how you will repay. For example, if you need 20,000 KES for fertiliser and improved seed, and you expect to harvest 20 extra bags worth 60,000 KES, you can repay the loan with interest. But if the rains fail, you might struggle. So only borrow what you can repay even in a bad year. Consider the interest rate: if it's 20% per year, you pay 4,000 KES on a 20,000 KES loan. That's 24,000 KES total. Make sure your expected profit covers that.

Also, look for agricultural credit in kind: some companies give you seed and fertiliser on credit, and you pay after harvest. This can be convenient but often has high hidden interest. Read the terms carefully. And always keep a copy of the loan agreement. Never borrow from informal money lenders who charge very high interest; they can trap you in debt.

Finally, build a good credit history. Repay on time, even if it means selling some assets. A good record with a lender means you can borrow more next season. And remember, loans are not free moneythey are a tool to grow your farm. Use them for productive investments, not for consumption.

4. Insurance options for drought and crop failure

Drought and crop failure can wipe out your entire season's work. Insurance can protect you, but you need to understand what is available and how it works. In many African countries, agricultural insurance is growing, especially index-based insurance.

Index-based insurance pays out when a certain trigger is met, such as rainfall below a certain level or satellite images showing poor vegetation. You don't need to prove individual loss; the payout is based on the index. For example, if the rainfall in your area is less than 200 mm during the growing season, the insurance pays you a set amount per acre. This is simpler and cheaper than traditional insurance, which requires loss assessment. In Kenya, the Kenya Agricultural Insurance Program (KAIP) offers index-based insurance for maize, sorghum, and other crops. In Nigeria, the Nigerian Agricultural Insurance Corporation (NAIC) provides similar products.

How do you get it? Often through cooperatives, banks, or mobile phone platforms. For instance, you can buy insurance when you buy seeds from an agro-dealer. The premium might be 5-10% of the sum insured. If you insure 1 acre of sorghum for 20,000 KES, you might pay 1,500 KES premium. If drought triggers a payout, you receive 20,000 KES. That money helps you replant or buy food.

But insurance is not a substitute for good farming. You still need to plant on time, use drought-tolerant varieties, and practice water conservation. Also, read the policy: what triggers payout? How long does it take? Are there exclusions? Some policies only cover specific risks, like drought, not pests or floods. And you must pay premiums on time.

Is insurance worth it? If you farm in a drought-prone area, yes. A single payout can save you from losing everything. But if you have other risk management strategies, like irrigation or diversified crops, you might not need it. Talk to other farmers who have used insurance. Visit your local agriculture office or microfinance institution to ask about available products. Start small: insure one acre and see how it works. Over time, you can expand.

Remember, insurance is a tool for risk management, not a guarantee of profit. It helps you recover from a bad year so you can farm again next season.

5. Diversifying income: seed production, fodder, and intercropping

Relying only on selling sorghum or millet grain is risky. If prices fall or drought hits, you lose everything. Diversifying your income means producing other products from the same land, spreading your risk and increasing total earnings.

One option is seed production. If you grow a high-quality variety, you can save seed and sell to other farmers. For example, if you grow improved sorghum like 'Gadam' or 'KARI Mtama 1', you can harvest, clean, and treat the seed, then package it in 1 kg or 5 kg bags. Sell at a premium: grain might sell for 60 KES/kg, but certified seed can sell for 150 KES/kg. You need to maintain purity: isolate your seed plot from other sorghum, rogue out off-types, and dry properly. Contact your national seed certification service to get certified, which boosts trust and price.

Another option is fodder. Sorghum and millet stalks and leaves are good livestock feed. After harvesting grain, you can cut and bale the stover, or make silage. Sell to dairy farmers or keep for your own animals. A tonne of sorghum stover can fetch 5,000-10,000 KES. If you have 2 acres, you might get 4 tonnes, earning 20,000-40,000 KES extra. You can also plant dual-purpose varieties that give both grain and fodder.

Intercropping is a third way. Plant sorghum or millet with legumes like cowpeas, groundnuts, or pigeon peas. The legumes fix nitrogen, improving soil, and give you an extra crop. For example, sorghum + cowpea: you harvest sorghum grain and cowpea grain, plus cowpea leaves as vegetables. Or sorghum + pigeon pea: pigeon pea can be sold as dry grain, and the stalks used for fuel. Intercropping reduces pest and disease pressure and makes better use of sunlight and water.

You can also add value: make flour, porridge mixes, or animal feed. Sell directly to consumers or shops. The key is to plan your diversification. Don't try everything at once. Start with one extra enterprise, learn it, then add another. Keep records for each so you know what is profitable. With diversified income, a bad year for one crop doesn't mean a bad year for your farm.

6. Planning for next season based on this season's records

At the end of the season, your record book is full of numbers. Now use them to plan for next season. This is how you turn records into better harvests and profits.

First, review your yields and costs per crop and per plot. Which plot gave the highest yield? Which variety performed best? For example, if your sorghum plot yielded 12 bags per acre while your millet plot yielded 8 bags per acre, and sorghum costs were similar, you might plant more sorghum next year. But also check market prices: if millet sells for twice the price of sorghum, it might still be more profitable. Calculate profit per acre for each crop.

Second, look at your costs. Where did you spend the most? Was it fertiliser, labour, or transport? Can you reduce any cost without hurting yields? For instance, if you spent 10,000 KES on fertiliser but got only a small yield increase, maybe you can use manure instead, or reduce the rate. If labour was high, consider family labour or better tools. Also, check if you had any losses: did you lose grain to pests or during storage? If so, plan to invest in proper storage, like hermetic bags.

Third, set specific goals for next season. For example: 'Increase sorghum yield from 10 to 15 bags per acre by using improved seed and planting on time.' 'Reduce post-harvest losses from 10% to 5% by using PICS bags.' 'Add cowpea intercropping on 1 acre to earn extra 15,000 KES.' Write these goals down and refer to them.

Fourth, plan your inputs. Based on your records, you know how much seed, fertiliser, and labour you need. You can buy early, often at lower prices. If you need a loan, use your records to apply. Also, plan your crop rotation: don't plant sorghum on the same plot every year; rotate with legumes to maintain soil health.

Finally, set aside some money as savings or emergency fund. If you made a profit, put 10-20% aside for next season's inputs or for unexpected expenses. This reduces the need for borrowing. And keep records again next season, so you can compare and improve. Farming is a business; planning based on records is how you grow.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Planning for Next Season Based on This Season's Records

Record keeping is not paperwork for its own sake. For a sorghum or millet enterprise, records are the raw material of every profitable decision you will make next season. This lesson shows you how to turn this season's records costs, yields, sales, rainfall notes, loan balances into a written plan that raises your margin per hectare and reduces the chance that a single drought or price crash wipes out your household income.

In the semi-arid and dry sub-humid zones where sorghum (Sorghum bicolor) and pearl millet (Pennisetum glaucum) dominate the Sahel, northern Nigeria, Ethiopia's lowlands, Tanzania's central plateau, Zimbabwe's communal areas the difference between a farmer who breaks even and one who accumulates capital is usually not rainfall. It is whether the farmer knows, to the bag and the naira, what last season actually cost and earned. Studies across African cereal systems consistently find that gross margins vary by 25 between farmers in the same village, largely explained by input timing, variety choice, and market timing all decisions that depend on records.

Step 1: Close the season's books the four numbers that matter

You do not need a computer. A single exercise book with four columns per enterprise is enough: Date | Item | Quantity | Amount (local currency). At season end, extract four numbers:

  1. Total variable costs (TVC) seed, fertilizer, manure transport, hired labour, ploughing, pesticide, bags, transport to market, loan interest.
  2. Total yield in kg or bags, per plot, converted to kg/ha.
  3. Total sales revenue quantity sold price, plus the value of grain kept for home consumption (imputed at local market price this is real income).
  4. Gross margin = Revenue TVC. This is the number you compare across plots, varieties, and seasons.

Step 2: Benchmark against real African data

Before you decide to expand, check whether your yields and margins are near, below, or above typical benchmarks. The table below uses published averages for rainfed smallholder sorghum and pearl millet. If your yield is below the low figure, the problem is agronomic (variety, spacing, fertility, striga, birds). If your yield is fine but your margin is thin, the problem is economic (input prices, labour timing, market timing).

Step 3: Build the next-season budget from your own numbers

Take your actual TVC from this season and your actual yield. Then ask three questions:

  • Which cost gave the best return? Divide the yield gain from each input by its cost. In sorghum, fertilizer and improved seed usually return 24 their cost where rainfall exceeds 500 mm; in millet in low-rainfall zones, they may not.
  • Which cost can be cut without cutting yield? Often transport, bags, or untimely weeding labour.
  • What is the break-even price? Break-even price = TVC expected yield. If your break-even is above the harvest-time market price, you must either store for a higher price, reduce cost, or shift to a lower-input strategy.

SAFETY WARNING Credit and Input Risk

Never take a loan or use credit for inputs based on an expected yield that you have not achieved in at least one previous season on the same plot. Sorghum and millet yields in dryland Africa can fall 6090% in a drought year. If your loan repayment is due before harvest, or if the loan is secured against next season's harvest, a single failed rainy season can force asset sale or default. Before signing: (1) confirm the interest rate and all fees in writing; (2) confirm the repayment date is after your normal harvest and sale month; (3) check whether the lender is registered with your country's central bank or microfinance regulator; (4) keep loan money separate from household money. Avoid lenders who demand your land title or who charge compound interest. If in doubt, borrow less than you need rather than more than you can repay.

Step 4: Use records to reduce risk and diversify

Your records will show you which single risk hurts most. In most sorghum and millet systems it is drought at flowering, followed by price collapse at harvest. Three record-driven responses:

  • Stagger planting dates across 23 weeks if you have labour records show which window usually escapes the mid-season dry spell.
  • Add a second enterprise that uses the same land or labour: seed production for a registered variety, fodder sales from stover, or intercropping with cowpea or groundnut. Your records will show which one has the best margin per labour-day.
  • Insure what you cannot absorb. Index-based drought insurance (for example, products offered through ACRE Africa, Pula, or national schemes such as Kenya's KCEP and Nigeria's NAIC) pays out on rainfall or vegetation-index triggers, not on farm visits. Record your GPS plot location and planting date you will need them to claim.

Step 5: Write the one-page plan

At the end of every season, write one page containing: plot name and size; variety planted; actual TVC; actual yield; actual average price; gross margin; break-even price; the single biggest cost and the single biggest risk; and three specific changes for next season with the number attached to each. Sign and date it. This page is what you show a lender, a cooperative, or an extension officer and it is what you compare against in twelve months.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Record every cost on the day it is paid seed, labour, fertilizer, transport, interest.
  • Weigh or count yield per plot, not per farm convert to kg/ha.
  • Record every sale price and date, including grain kept for home use.
  • Calculate gross margin = revenue total variable costs, per plot.
  • Calculate break-even price = TVC expected yield before you sell.
  • Compare this season's numbers to the benchmarks in Table 1.
  • Check loan terms in writing; repayment must fall after harvest.
  • Write a one-page next-season plan with three numbered changes and sign it.
