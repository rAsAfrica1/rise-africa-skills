Kerb Stone

8 lessons · 30 quiz questions · Easy difficulty

1 Kerb profiles (bullnose, splayed)

Kerbs are the edging stones between a road and a pavement. They come in four main profiles:

  • Bullnose: Rounded top edge. Safest for pedestrians. Most common in cities.
  • Splayed (bevelled): Angled top face. Drains water away from pavement. Common on highways.
  • Square (flat top): Simple rectangular profile. Cheapest to make. Used for parking and yards.
  • Half-battered: Vertical inner face, sloped outer face. Traditional on older roads.

Standard size: 1 m × 125 mm × 255 mm (length × depth × height). Weight: about 45 kg each.

💡 Bullnose kerbs sell at a 20% premium over square kerbs. Worth the extra mould cost.

2 Kerb mould construction

Kerb moulds are long and heavy. Key features:

  • Length: 1 m standard, some 0.5 m for corners
  • Multi-cavity: 3–6 kerbs per mould to speed production
  • Steel build: Heavy-duty steel, not fibreglass (kerbs need pressure compaction)
  • Bolted sides: Split vertically so the kerb releases cleanly
  • Vibration point: Reinforced base for a vibrating table

Cost: £400–£1,500 per 3-cavity mould. Fibreglass won't last — stick with steel.

Moulds must be perfectly straight. A bent mould gives warped kerbs that won't lie flat on the road.

3 Dense kerb mix

Kerbs face truck tyres and weathering. They need a stronger mix than regular blocks.

Standard kerb mix by weight:

  • Cement: 1 part
  • Sharp sand (0–4 mm): 1.5 parts
  • Crushed stone (6–10 mm): 3 parts
  • Water: earth-damp (dry side for pressed kerbs)

Cement is about 15–18% of total weight. A 50 kg bag makes 2–3 kerbs of 1 m.

Target strength: 40–50 N/mm² at 28 days. Kerbs must survive heavy loads without cracking.

For freeze-thaw climates, add 1% air-entraining admixture. Reduces cracking from water freezing in the concrete.

4 Reinforcement basics

Standard kerbs don't need reinforcement, but heavy-duty kerbs (airport, port, industrial) do.

When to reinforce:

  • Kerb on a busy road (over 5,000 vehicles/day)
  • Kerb with a service duct or drainage channel running under it
  • Kerb at a corner or junction
  • Any kerb over 2 m long

Reinforcement used:

  • Steel bar (rebar): 6–8 mm diameter, 2 bars running lengthwise, 50 mm from bottom and sides
  • Cover: 25–30 mm minimum concrete cover over steel
  • Stirrups: small U-shaped bars every 500 mm

Pre-cut and pre-position rebar before casting. Never place rebar after the mix is poured.

5 Compaction methods

Kerbs need high compaction. Three methods:

Vibrating table: Mould sits on a vibrating table during casting. Vibration runs 8–12 seconds. Best for medium production (100–500 kerbs/day). Cost: $2,000–$8,000.

Hand tamping (small scale): Fill the mould in 3 layers, tamp each with a heavy bar. 20–30 firm taps per layer. Slow but works for <20 kerbs/day.

Hydraulic press: High-pressure press (like a block machine). 5–15 seconds per cycle. Best quality but expensive ($30,000+).

Under-compacted kerbs have honeycomb and break in service. Don't skip compaction.

6 Demoulding long pieces

1 m kerbs are long and fragile when green. Handle with care:

  1. Leave in the mould 24–36 hours (longer than blocks)
  2. Loosen bolts slowly (releasing the two halves evenly, not one side first)
  3. Two people lift the mould halves straight off
  4. Slide the kerb onto a soft pallet (foam or sand bed)
  5. Do NOT lift by the ends — the middle sags
  6. Move to curing area with two people supporting the middle

For multi-cavity moulds, remove one kerb at a time. Don't rush.

⚠ Green kerbs crack if dropped. Train workers carefully. Every broken kerb is £3 lost.

7 Curing kerbs

Same as blocks: 7 days damp, 28 days full strength. Kerbs need extra care:

  • Stack flat: Never stack kerbs on their edges while curing — they crack.
  • Stack height: Maximum 6 layers, with wooden battens between layers to spread weight.
  • Cover with plastic: Protects from sun and rain.
  • Keep damp: Spray 2–3 times a day. Water is your friend.

Strength test: drop a 1 m kerb from 300 mm onto soft ground. A good kerb chips at the edge. A bad kerb snaps.

8 Selling to road contractors

Kerbs sell to a different customer base than blocks:

  • Road contractors: Buy in thousands of units. Payment 30–60 days.
  • Municipalities: Tender-based, competitive. Bulk orders 1,000+ units.
  • Private developers: Small orders (50–500) for estates and compounds.
  • Parking lot builders: Regular steady orders for industrial yards.

Pricing (Africa, 2026):

  • Square kerbs: $6–$10 each
  • Bullnose kerbs: $8–$14 each
  • Splayed kerbs: $7–$12 each

Cost to make: $3–$5 each. Margin: 40–60%.

To win road contracts you need: consistent quality, delivery on schedule, and often a compliance certificate (BS EN 1340 or national equivalent). Start with small private jobs, build reputation, then chase council tenders.

💰 Kerbs are high-value, low-volume. One council contract can be worth more than a year of walk-in block sales.
