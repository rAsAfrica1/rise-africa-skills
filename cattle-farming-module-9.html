1. Low-Stress Handling Techniques

Cattle are prey animals. Their survival instinct makes them fearful of sudden movements, loud noises, and anything that feels like a predator. Low-stress handling means working with this instinct, not against it. The payoff is real: calmer cattle gain weight faster, bruise less, and are less likely to injure you or themselves. A stressed cow can lose 2-3 kg of body weight in a single day of rough handling, and bruises cost you money at the abattoir.

The flight zone is the space around an animal that triggers it to move away when you enter it. For most African cattle, this zone is 3-5 metres. If you walk directly toward a cow's head, she will turn and run. Instead, approach at an angle from the side, and work the edge of the flight zone. To move a cow forward, step into the zone behind her shoulder. To stop her, step back out. Use the point of balance at the shoulder: pressure behind it moves her forward, pressure in front moves her backward.

Cattle have a wide field of vision, nearly 300 degrees, but a blind spot directly behind them. They also see shadows and contrast poorly. A dark puddle on a concrete floor may look like a hole. A flapping plastic bag on a fence can stop a whole herd. Before moving cattle, walk the route and remove anything that flutters, shines, or looks unfamiliar. Use a solid barrier or a person to block the escape route you don't want them to take.

When you must move cattle, do it slowly. Walk, don't run. Speak in low tones or stay quiet. Use flags or sticks as extensions of your arm, not as weapons. Never hit an animal around the head or udder. If one animal panics, stop, let it settle, then continue. In Africa, many farms use donkeys or dogs to move cattle; trained dogs can work well, but untrained dogs cause chaos. If you use dogs, keep them under voice control and never let them bite.

Finally, handle cattle in the cool hours of early morning or late afternoon. Heat stress adds to handling stress. Provide water and shade at the handling facility. A well-designed crush, race, and loading ramp reduces the need for force. We will cover equipment in the next lesson, but remember: good handling is 80% attitude and 20% equipment.

2. Use of Handling Equipment

Good equipment makes handling safer and faster. The basic kit for any cattle farm includes a crush (also called a chute), a head gate, a race, a loading ramp, and a set of hurdles or panels. In many parts of Africa, you can build these from local timber or welded steel. A simple wooden crush can be made from 10 cm x 10 cm hardwood posts set 60-70 cm apart, with a head gate that closes behind the neck. The floor should be concrete or packed gravel that drains well, because cattle slip on smooth, wet surfaces.

The crush should be 70-75 cm wide for mature zebu or Boran cattle, and 60-65 cm for smaller breeds. If it is too wide, the animal turns around; too narrow, and she panics. The head gate must open and close smoothly from the side, not from behind the animal. A good head gate has a neck opening of about 20-25 cm and a bottom bar that prevents the animal from going down. Always check that the gate cannot swing back and hit the operator.

A race is a narrow alley leading to the crush. It should be 60-70 cm wide, with solid sides at least 1.5 m high. Solid sides prevent the animal from seeing distractions and reduce balking. If you cannot afford solid sides, use tightly spaced horizontal rails. The race should curve gently; cattle naturally follow curves better than straight lines. A curved race with a 3-4 m radius works well. At the end of the race, a loading ramp should rise no more than 20 degrees. A steeper ramp frightens cattle and causes slips.

For restraining individual animals, you need a halter and a nose lead. A simple rope halter can be made from 8 mm nylon rope. Place it over the head behind the ears, with the nose loop over the muzzle. Never tie a cow by the horns alone; she can break her neck. For leg restraint, use a hobble or a rope around the hind legs above the hock. For castration or branding, use a crush with a head gate and side access. Always have a second person to help.

Maintain your equipment. Check for sharp edges, broken welds, and loose bolts. Oil hinges and latches regularly. Store ropes and halters out of the sun to prevent rot. A well-maintained crush lasts 10-15 years. If you cannot afford metal, hardwood treated with used engine oil will last 5-7 years. Remember, equipment is only as good as the handler. Even the best crush will not help if you rush and shout.

3. Animal Welfare Principles

Animal welfare is not just about being kind. It is about meeting the basic needs of cattle so they stay healthy, grow well, and produce more. The World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) recognises five freedoms: freedom from hunger and thirst, freedom from discomfort, freedom from pain, injury and disease, freedom from fear and distress, and freedom to express normal behaviour. In practical African cattle farming, these freedoms translate into specific actions.

Freedom from hunger and thirst means cattle must have access to clean water at all times. A mature cow drinks 30-50 litres per day, more in hot weather. A lactating cow can drink up to 80 litres. If water is more than 500 metres away, cattle will not walk to it often enough. Provide troughs or dams within 200 metres of grazing. Feed must be adequate for the season. In the dry season, supplement with hay, silage, or crop residues. A cow needs about 2-2.5% of her body weight in dry matter per day. For a 400 kg cow, that is 8-10 kg of dry feed.

Freedom from discomfort means shade and shelter. Cattle can suffer heat stress above 30C, especially if humidity is high. Provide trees, a simple roof, or a wall for windbreak. In the Sahel, a thatched shade structure reduces heat load significantly. Also, keep bedding dry if cattle are housed. Wet, muddy floors cause foot rot and mastitis.

Freedom from pain, injury and disease means regular health care. Vaccinate against anthrax, blackquarter, and foot-and-mouth disease where available. Deworm every 3-6 months. Treat wounds immediately with antiseptic. If you castrate or dehorn, use pain relief if possible, and do it at a young age. Disbud calves before 8 weeks with a hot iron or caustic paste.

Freedom from fear and distress means low-stress handling as covered earlier. Never shout, hit, or use electric prods except as a last resort. Freedom to express normal behaviour means cattle need space to graze, socialise, and lie down. A cow lies down for 10-12 hours a day. If she cannot, her rumen stops working properly. Provide at least 5 square metres per cow in a pen, and rotate grazing to prevent overgrazing. Good welfare is good business.

4. Transportation of Cattle

Transporting cattle is one of the most stressful events in their lives. Poor transport causes weight loss, bruising, injuries, and even death. In Africa, cattle are moved by truck, pickup, trailer, or on foot. Each method has rules that protect both the animal and your wallet.

Before loading, prepare the truck. The floor must be non-slip. Use sand, wooden cleats, or rubber mats. The sides must be high enough that cattle cannot jump out: at least 1.8 m for adult cattle. The roof should provide shade. Never overload. A standard 7-tonne truck can carry about 20-25 head of 400 kg cattle. For a pickup with a cage, carry no more than 4-5 head. Overloading causes suffocation and broken legs. Also, check the ramp angle: no more than 20 degrees. If the ramp is too steep, build a step or use a loading bank.

During loading, move cattle quietly. Use a race and a flag. Do not use electric prods. If an animal goes down, stop and let her get up. Never drag a downer animal. Once loaded, secure the tailgate. Drive smoothly. Sudden braking throws cattle forward. On bad roads, reduce speed to 20-30 km/h. Avoid potholes. If the journey is longer than 8 hours, stop every 4 hours to check the animals. Provide water at stops, but do not feed heavily before transport; a full rumen increases stress and bloat risk.

In hot weather, transport early morning or at night. The temperature inside a metal truck can reach 40C even if outside is 30C. Ventilation holes should be at least 10% of the floor area. In cold or rainy weather, provide a tarpaulin on the windward side, but leave gaps for air. Never leave cattle standing in a parked truck in the sun. If you must wait, park in shade and open the sides.

For long distances, consider rest stops at quarantine stations or holding grounds. In many countries, you need a movement permit and veterinary health certificate. Check local regulations. On arrival, unload slowly. Let cattle settle in a pen with water and hay. Do not mix with resident cattle for at least 2 weeks to avoid disease spread. Weigh a sample before and after to monitor shrinkage. A well-managed 100 km trip should cause less than 2% weight loss. Poor transport can cause 10% or more. That is money lost.

5. Euthanasia and Emergency Procedures

Sometimes an animal is suffering and cannot be saved. Euthanasia means ending life humanely, without pain or distress. As a cattle farmer, you must know when and how to do it. The decision is never easy, but delaying causes more suffering. Indications include: a broken leg that cannot be set, severe bloat that does not respond to treatment, incurable disease, or a downer animal that cannot stand after 24 hours of proper care.

Before euthanasia, confirm the diagnosis with a veterinarian if possible. If not, use your judgement. The goal is a quick, painless death. The best method for cattle is a captive bolt pistol, followed by bleeding (exsanguination) or pithing. A captive bolt stuns the animal instantly. Place the bolt at the intersection of two lines drawn from the base of the horns to the opposite eye. For hornless cattle, aim at the centre of the forehead, about 2-3 cm above the eyes. The animal should collapse immediately. If she does not, repeat. After stunning, bleed by cutting the throat or sticking the jugular vein. Death occurs within 1-2 minutes.

If you do not have a captive bolt, a firearm is acceptable. Use a .22 calibre or larger, aimed at the same point. Never use a shotgun; it may not penetrate. For young calves, a sharp blow to the head with a heavy hammer can be used, but it must be followed by bleeding. Do not use poison; it is dangerous to humans and scavengers. Do not bury alive. Do not let an animal bleed out without stunning first.

Emergency procedures include bloat treatment and downer cow care. For bloat, if the animal is breathing heavily and the left flank is distended, pass a stomach tube or trocarise. A trocar is a sharp metal tube inserted into the left flank at the point of the triangle formed by the hip, last rib, and loin. Insert it 2-3 cm behind the last rib, 10-15 cm below the spine. Release gas slowly. For a downer cow, roll her every 2 hours, provide deep bedding, and lift with a hip hoist if available. Give calcium if milk fever is suspected.

Always have a euthanasia plan. Keep the captive bolt and ammunition clean and stored safely. Train at least two people on your farm. After euthanasia, dispose of the carcass by deep burial (at least 1.5 m deep) or burning, away from water sources. Notify your veterinarian if anthrax is suspected; do not open the carcass. Humane endings are part of good farming.

6. Stockmanship and Observation

Stockmanship is the art of knowing your cattle. A good stockman can spot a sick cow before she shows obvious signs. This skill saves money and suffering. Observation is not just looking; it is seeing. Spend at least 15-20 minutes each day walking among your herd, quietly, without disturbing them. Watch how they move, eat, drink, and interact. You will learn each animal's normal behaviour. Then, any change stands out.

Start with the head. A healthy cow has bright eyes, alert ears, and a moist nose. A sick cow may have dull eyes, droopy ears, and a dry nose. Check the mouth for drooling or sores. Look at the rumen: the left side of the abdomen should have a visible contraction every 1-2 minutes. You can also listen with a stethoscope or put your ear against the flank. A rumen that is not moving means trouble. Check the manure: it should be well-formed, like a soft pat. Diarrhoea or blood is a red flag. Urine should be clear to slightly yellow. Dark urine can mean kidney problems.

Watch the gait. A cow with foot rot will limp and favour one leg. A cow with laminitis will walk stiffly, like a soldier. A cow with a dislocated hip will drag a leg. Check hooves every month; trim overgrown hooves with hoof nippers. In wet conditions, foot rot is common. Treat with zinc sulphate footbath or antibiotics.

Body condition scoring (BCS) is a simple 1-5 scale. A score of 1 is emaciated, 5 is obese. For beef cattle, aim for 3. For dairy, 3.5 at calving. Check the backbone, ribs, and hip bones. If you can see individual ribs, she is too thin. If you cannot feel the ribs, she is too fat. Weigh or condition score every month. Sudden weight loss means parasites, poor feed, or disease.

Observe behaviour. A cow in heat will stand to be mounted. A cow about to calve will separate from the herd and have a swollen vulva. A cow with mastitis will kick when you touch the udder. A cow with a retained placenta will have a bloody discharge. Write down what you see. Keep a notebook or use a simple phone app. Record dates of vaccination, deworming, and calving. Good records help you spot patterns. Finally, train your workers. Everyone who handles cattle must know these signs. A good stockman is the best insurance you can have.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Cattle Handling and Welfare

This practical reference consolidates the seven lessons in the Cattle Handling and Welfare module into a working field guide for African cattle operations. Whether you run a 20-head feedlot in Nakuru, a 300-head ranch in Botswana, or a mixed crop-livestock enterprise in Ethiopia, the principles are the same: calm animals gain weight, injured animals lose money, and poorly handled cattle produce discounted carcasses. Every technique below is designed to protect your margin while meeting the welfare expectations of buyers, abattoirs, and regulators.

1. Low-Stress Handling Techniques

Cattle are prey animals with a wide panoramic field of vision (roughly 300) and a strong flight zone. Understanding these two facts is the foundation of low-stress handling. In African contexts, where many herds are handled by herders on foot with minimal infrastructure, the flight zone is often larger because animals are less habituated to close human contact.

Core principles:

  • Point of balance: Standing behind the shoulder drives an animal forward; standing in front turns it back. Use this to move cattle without shouting.
  • Pressure and release: Apply gentle pressure (your presence) and release the moment the animal moves correctly. This trains cattle to respond to minimal pressure.
  • Flight zone management: Work at the edge of the flight zone, not inside it. Entering the zone causes flight; retreating calms the animal.
  • No shouting, no sticks to the face: Vocal and physical abuse raises cortisol, reduces weight gain, and increases bruising. A study in Australian feedlots found that cattle handled quietly had significantly lower bruising rates.
  • Work in the animal's language: Use slow, deliberate movements. Cattle read body language; sudden movements trigger flight.

In East African pastoral systems, the traditional practice of moving cattle at dawn and dusk exploits the animals' natural grazing rhythm and reduces heat stress a low-stress technique rooted in local knowledge. Document it, refine it, and train new stockmen in it.

2. Use of Handling Equipment

Well-designed equipment reduces labour, injury, and stress. In many African operations, equipment is minimal a crush, a race, and a head gate are the essentials. Where budgets are tight, prioritise a head gate and a race over a full squeeze chute.

Key equipment and correct use:

  • Crush (chute): Ensure it is the correct size for your breed. Zebu cattle have larger humps and different body dimensions than European breeds. A crush that is too wide allows animals to turn; too narrow causes injury.
  • Head gate: Use a self-catching or manual gate. Never leave an animal caught by the head for extended periods this causes distress and can lead to injury if the animal struggles.
  • Race (alley): Solid-sided races prevent animals from seeing distractions and reduce balking. Curved races exploit the natural circling behaviour of cattle.
  • Nose tongs and halters: Use only when necessary and never as a primary restraint. Nose tongs should be used for short procedures only.
  • Loading ramps: Should be non-slip, with a maximum slope of 20 (preferably 15). Side rails should be solid to prevent animals from seeing over the edge.

In West Africa, the use of locally fabricated metal crushes has improved handling efficiency in peri-urban dairy operations. Where equipment is unavailable, use a head gate made from local timber a practical, low-cost solution documented by FAO in smallholder cattle systems.

Welfare is not a Western luxury; it is a business input. The five freedoms provide a globally recognised framework, and they translate directly into productivity metrics:

In Kenya, the Kenya Bureau of Standards (KEBS) and the Directorate of Veterinary Services are tightening welfare requirements for export-certified slaughterhouses. Welfare compliance is becoming a market access issue, not just an ethical one.

4. Transportation of Cattle

Transport is the most stressful event in a beef animal's life. In Africa, long-distance transport by road is common, often over poor roads and in high ambient temperatures. The financial cost of poor transport is visible in shrink (weight loss), bruising, and dark cutters all of which reduce carcass value.

Best practices for African road transport:

  • Loading density: Allow 1.01.4 m per adult animal for short journeys; increase for long journeys. Overloading causes injury and stress; underloading wastes fuel.
  • Bedding: Use sand or wood shavings to prevent slipping. In wet conditions, ensure adequate drainage.
  • Ventilation: Open-sided trucks are essential in tropical climates. Never transport cattle in closed trucks without forced ventilation.
  • Rest stops: For journeys over 8 hours, provide rest, water, and feed every 12 hours. In East Africa, the standard is often 24 hours for long-haul trips to export abattoirs.
  • Timing: Transport during cooler hours (early morning or night) to reduce heat stress.
  • Handling at loading: Use a loading ramp with solid sides and non-slip flooring. Never use electric prods as a primary driving tool.

In Nigeria and Ghana, the use of open-bed trucks with wooden side rails is common but often overcrowded. A 2021 study in Nigeria found that overcrowding during transport was a major cause of bruising and mortality. The economic loss is estimated at 510% of animal value per trip.

5. Euthanasia and Emergency Procedures

Euthanasia is a necessary part of responsible cattle management for emergency slaughter, severe injury, or disease control. In African contexts, where veterinary services may be distant, stockmen must be trained in humane emergency killing.

Approved methods:

  • Firearm: The most common method in rural Africa. Use a .22 calibre or larger with a captive bolt or free bullet. The target point is the intersection of lines from the base of the horns to the opposite eye. Ensure the animal is dead before disposal check for absence of corneal reflex and respiration.
  • Captive bolt: Where available, a penetrating captive bolt is effective. Follow with bleeding (exsanguination) to ensure death.
  • Emergency slaughter: If an animal is severely injured and cannot be transported, slaughter on site. Bleed thoroughly and dispose of the carcass according to local regulations.

SAFETY WARNING: Firearm Use and Emergency Killing

Firearms must only be used by trained, licensed personnel. Never attempt to kill an animal with a firearm without proper training a poorly placed shot causes suffering and is dangerous. Ensure the area behind the animal is clear of people and property. In many African countries, firearm ownership and use are regulated; check local laws before acquiring or using a firearm for livestock euthanasia. Always have a backup method available. After euthanasia, confirm death before disposal or bleeding. If in doubt, call a veterinarian or veterinary paraprofessional.

6. Stockmanship and Observation

Stockmanship is the art of seeing. A good stockman notices the cow that is lagging, the one with a slight limp, the one not eating. In African cattle systems, where herds may be large and spread out, observation skills are the primary diagnostic tool.

Daily observation checklist:

  • Appetite: Is every animal eating? A cow off feed is the first sign of illness.
  • Rumination: Are animals chewing cud? Absence indicates digestive upset or disease.
  • Gait: Watch for lameness, stiffness, or abnormal posture.
  • Body condition: Score monthly. Sudden weight loss indicates parasites, disease, or feed shortage.
  • Eyes and nose: Discharge indicates respiratory infection.
  • Manure: Consistency and colour. Scours (diarrhoea) are a major killer of calves.
  • Behaviour: Isolation from the herd is a classic sign of illness.

In Botswana, the traditional moraka (cattle post) system relies on herders who know each animal individually. This level of stockmanship is a competitive advantage it allows early intervention and reduces mortality. Commercial operations should train staff to observe and record daily, using a simple notebook or mobile app.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Before handling: Check equipment (crush, race, head gate) for sharp edges, broken parts, and correct size for your breed.
  2. During handling: Work at the edge of the flight zone; use point of balance; no shouting or striking.
  3. During transport: Load at correct density; provide non-slip flooring; transport in cool hours; rest every 68 hours.
  4. Daily observation: Check appetite, rumination, gait, eyes, manure, and behaviour. Record anything abnormal.
  5. Welfare compliance: Ensure water, feed, shade, and space are available at all times. Keep records for buyers and regulators.
  6. Emergency preparedness: Have a euthanasia plan, trained personnel, and a backup method. Know your vet's contact.
  7. Training: Train all stockmen in low-stress handling and observation. Refresher training every 6 months.
  8. Business link: Track bruising, shrink, and mortality. Each 1% reduction in bruising improves carcass value by 0.51%.

Business takeaway: Low-stress handling is not a cost it is an investment. Calm cattle gain more weight, bruise less, and fetch higher prices. In a competitive African market, the operation that handles cattle well will out-earn the one that does not. Train your team, maintain your equipment, and record your results. Welfare is your margin.
