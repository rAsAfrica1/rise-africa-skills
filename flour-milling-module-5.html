1. Roller Mill Components and Operation

A roller mill is the heart of a flour mill. It breaks wheat kernels open and scrapes the endosperm from the bran. The main parts are the feed hopper, feed roll, grinding rolls (usually two pairs), and the gap adjustment. In Africa, many mills use roller mills from Turkey, China, or Europe. The rolls are made of chilled cast iron or steel, and they have a spiral groove pattern called corrugation. The number of grooves per inch (or per centimeter) and the spiral angle determine how the wheat is cut. For first break, you want a coarse corrugation, like 10 to 12 grooves per inch, with a spiral angle of 4 to 6 percent. For later breaks, finer corrugation, like 20 to 28 grooves per inch. The two rolls in a pair turn in opposite directions. One turns faster than the other. The speed ratio is called the differential. For break rolls, a differential of 2.5:1 is common; for reduction rolls, 1.25:1. The gap between the rolls is critical. For first break, set the gap to about 0.5 to 0.6 mm. You measure it with a feeler gauge. If the gap is too wide, the wheat passes through uncut. If too narrow, you get too much bran powder. The feed roll controls the flow of wheat into the grinding zone. It turns slower than the grinding rolls and ensures an even curtain of grain. The whole mill is driven by an electric motor, often 30 to 75 kW per pair of rolls. In many African mills, the rolls are changed every 3 to 6 months, depending on tonnage. A typical 100-tonne-per-day mill might have 8 to 12 roller mills, each with two or four rolls. The operator must check the temperature of the rolls; if they get too hot, the flour quality drops. You can feel the roll temperature by handif it's too hot to touch, stop and check the gap or the feed rate.

2. Plansifters and Purifiers

After the roller mill, the ground material goes to a plansifter. A plansifter is a stack of rectangular sieves that separate particles by size. It shakes back and forth, and the particles fall through different screens. In a typical mill, a plansifter has 8 to 16 sections, each with a different mesh size. The top screens are coarser, like 20 mesh (about 0.8 mm openings), and the bottom screens are finer, like 100 mesh (0.15 mm). The material that stays on top of a screen is called 'overs' and goes to the next break or reduction roll. The material that passes through is 'throughs' and may go to a purifier or to the flour bin. A purifier is a machine that separates pure endosperm from bran and germ using a combination of sieving and air. It has a sloping, vibrating screen and a fan that blows air upward. The lighter bran particles are lifted by the air and carried away, while the heavier endosperm falls through the screen. In Africa, purifiers are often used in semolina mills for pasta, but also in flour mills to get cleaner flour. A common purifier has a capacity of 1 to 2 tonnes per hour. The screen mesh is usually 10 to 14 mesh. The air speed is adjusted so that bran is lifted but endosperm is not. You can test by putting a sample on the screen and watching: if too much endosperm is blown away, reduce the air. The plansifter is driven by a motor with an eccentric weight, creating a gyratory motion. The speed is around 200 to 250 rpm. The sieves need to be cleaned regularly. In many African mills, workers brush the screens every shift to prevent blocking. If the screens are blocked, the sifting efficiency drops, and flour gets mixed with bran. A simple check: take a handful of material from the overs and rub it between your fingers. If you feel fine flour, the screen is passing too much. Adjust the tension or replace the screen.

3. Bran Finishers and Detachers

Bran finishers and detachers are machines that recover flour still attached to bran. After the break rolls, the bran particles often carry small chunks of endosperm. A bran finisher beats the bran to shake off the endosperm. It has a horizontal rotor with beaters that rotate at high speed inside a cylindrical screen. The bran enters one end and is beaten against the screen. The endosperm falls through the screen, and the bran exits the other end. A typical bran finisher has a rotor speed of 800 to 1200 rpm and a screen with 0.5 to 1.0 mm openings. Capacity is about 1 to 3 tonnes per hour. In many African mills, bran finishers are used after the last break roll. The recovered flour is called 'finisher flour' and is usually lower quality, so it might be added to the feed flour or to the main flour depending on the market. A detacher is similar but gentler. It is used after the reduction rolls to break up lumps of flour that form when the rolls are set too close. A detacher has a rotor with pins or blades that turn at 500 to 800 rpm. It doesn't have a screen; it just breaks the lumps. The material then goes to the plansifter. You can tell if a detacher is needed if you see lumps in the flour after the reduction roll. In a typical 100-tonne mill, you might have 2 to 4 bran finishers and 4 to 6 detachers. Maintenance is simple: check the beaters for wear every month. Worn beaters reduce efficiency. Also check the screen for holes. A hole as small as 2 mm will let bran through and contaminate the flour. You can patch small holes with a piece of screen and wire, but it's better to replace the screen. In Africa, where spare parts can be hard to get, many mills keep a spare screen and a set of beaters on hand.

4. Pneumatic Conveying Systems

Pneumatic conveying moves flour, bran, and semolina through pipes using air. In a modern mill, you rarely see bucket elevators for flour; instead, a fan blows air through a pipe, and the material is carried along. There are two main types: positive pressure and negative pressure (vacuum). Positive pressure is common for moving flour from the mill to the packing bin. A blower pushes air at 15 to 25 meters per second through a pipe. The material is fed into the pipe via a rotary valve. The air speed must be high enough to keep particles suspended but not so high that it damages the flour. For flour, a speed of 18 to 22 m/s is typical. The pipe diameter is usually 100 to 150 mm. The system needs a cyclone or a filter receiver at the end to separate the flour from the air. In Africa, many mills use a simple cyclone and a rotary valve to discharge the flour. Negative pressure systems are used for dusty areas, like the intake of wheat or the collection of bran. A fan pulls air through the pipes, and the material is sucked into a cyclone. The advantage is that if there is a leak, air is pulled in, not blown out, so dust stays inside. The disadvantage is that the fan must handle the material, so it wears faster. For a 100-tonne mill, you might have a central vacuum system with a 30 kW fan. The pipes should be as straight as possible; every bend adds resistance. Use long-radius bends, not sharp elbows. Also, keep the pipe size consistent. If you reduce the diameter, the air speed increases and can cause blockages. A common problem in African mills is moisture. If the flour is too damp, it sticks to the pipe walls and causes blockages. Keep the flour moisture below 14%. You can check by squeezing a handful; if it holds shape, it's too wet. Clean the system daily: open the inspection hatches and remove any buildup.

5. Maintenance and Roll Changes

Roller mill rolls wear out. The corrugations become dull, and the gap becomes uneven. In a busy African mill, you might need to change rolls every 3 to 6 months. The first sign is poor extraction: you get less flour and more bran in the break system. You can check by measuring the gap with a feeler gauge. If the gap is more than 0.1 mm wider than the setting, the rolls are worn. Also, look at the flour: if it feels gritty, the rolls are not cutting properly. To change a roll, you need to lock out the power, remove the guards, and disconnect the feed roll. Then you remove the old roll and install a new one. The new roll must be balanced and aligned. Use a dial indicator to check runout; it should be less than 0.05 mm. The gap is set with a feeler gauge and locked. After changing rolls, run the mill empty for 10 minutes to check for vibration. Then feed a small amount of wheat and check the stock. You also need to maintain the rest of the mill. Grease the bearings every 500 hours. Check the belts and chains for tension. Clean the plansifter screens daily. In many African mills, there is no dedicated maintenance team, so the operator does it. Keep a logbook: record the date, the roll number, and the gap setting. This helps you predict when the next change is due. For a 100-tonne mill, a full roll change might take 4 to 6 hours per roll pair. Spare rolls are expensive, so many mills send worn rolls to a specialist for re-corrugation. In Africa, there are companies in South Africa, Kenya, and Nigeria that re-corrugate rolls. The cost is about 30% of a new roll. Always keep at least one spare roll pair on site. And train your staff: a well-trained operator can double the life of a roll by setting the gap correctly and feeding evenly.

6. Automation and Control Systems in Milling

Automation in milling uses sensors, computers, and controllers to run the mill efficiently. In Africa, many small mills still run manually, but larger mills are adopting automation. The most common system is a PLC (Programmable Logic Controller) that controls the roller mills, plansifters, and conveyors. Sensors measure the feed rate, the roll gap, the temperature, and the motor load. For example, a sensor on the feed roll tells the PLC how much wheat is entering. If the flow drops, the PLC speeds up the feed roll. If the motor load gets too high, the PLC opens the gap to prevent a jam. A typical automated mill has a central control room with a computer screen showing the whole flow. The operator can start and stop machines with a mouse. One key automation is the gap control on the roller mills. Instead of turning a handwheel, a motor adjusts the gap based on the load. This keeps the gap constant even as rolls wear. Another is the moisture control. A sensor measures the moisture of the wheat before the first break. If it's too dry, a valve adds water. The target moisture is 15.5 to 16.5% for hard wheat, 14.5 to 15.5% for soft wheat. In Africa, automation can be a challenge because of power fluctuations. Many mills install voltage stabilizers and backup generators. Also, dust and heat can damage electronics. So control panels are often placed in air-conditioned rooms. For a small mill, you don't need full automation. You can start with a simple variable frequency drive (VFD) on the main motors. A VFD lets you adjust the speed of the rolls and the fans, saving energy. It also gives a soft start, reducing wear. A VFD for a 75 kW motor costs about $2,000 to $3,000. Many African millers find that a VFD pays for itself in six months through energy savings. Finally, keep records. Even a simple logbook of run hours, gaps, and temperatures helps you spot problems before they stop production.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Milling Equipment and Machinery

This practical reference is written for the African miller, plant manager, or mill technician who must keep a flour mill running profitably. It covers the core machines found in a modern roller mill: the roller mill itself, plansifters and purifiers, bran finishers and detachers, pneumatic conveying systems, maintenance and roll changes, and automation and control systems. The focus is on decisions that affect throughput, extraction rate, flour quality, energy cost, and downtime the variables that determine whether a milling business in Nairobi, Lagos, Accra, or Johannesburg competes or collapses.

African mills face specific conditions: variable wheat quality from local and imported sources, high energy costs, limited access to OEM spare parts, and skilled labour shortages. The equipment choices and maintenance practices below are therefore presented with a business-first lens. Every hour of unplanned downtime has a direct cost in lost production, and every percentage point of extraction gained is revenue.

1. Roller Mill Components and Operation

A roller mill consists of a pair of chilled cast-iron or steel rolls mounted in a sturdy frame, with a feed mechanism, roll gap adjustment, and a drive system. Break rolls are corrugated and operate at a differential speed (typically 2.5:1), while reduction rolls are smooth and run at a closer differential (1.25:1). The roll gap, feed rate, and differential speed determine the grind and the extraction rate.

In African mills, roll wear is a major cost driver. Local wheat with higher foreign matter accelerates wear. Operators should monitor roll temperature, vibration, and product particle size daily. A well-maintained roll set can last 1824 months; poorly maintained rolls may need re-corrugation in 69 months.

2. Plansifters and Purifiers

Plansifters separate ground stock into size fractions using stacked sieves. Purifiers use air and sieves to separate pure endosperm from bran and germ. Inefficient sifting or purification directly reduces extraction rate and increases bran contamination in flour. In many African mills, sifter sieve clogging from high-moisture wheat is a common cause of reduced capacity.

3. Bran Finishers and Detachers

Bran finishers recover adhering endosperm from bran, improving extraction by 13 percentage points. Detachers break up agglomerated stock after roller milling, improving sifter efficiency. In African mills where wheat is often harder or higher in foreign matter, these machines are critical to protect extraction rate and reduce waste.

Key maintenance points: check beater bars for wear, balance the rotor, and ensure screens are not torn. A torn screen in a bran finisher sends bran into the flour stream, causing quality rejection. Detacher screens should be inspected weekly.

4. Pneumatic Conveying Systems

Pneumatic conveying moves stock between machines using air. It offers flexibility in layout, reduces dust, and enables automation. However, it consumes significant energy often 1020% of mill power. In African mills with unreliable electricity, pneumatic systems should be designed with adequate capacity and spare blowers.

Common problems: duct wear at bends, air leaks, filter blockages, and fan imbalance. Regular inspection of bends and filters prevents unplanned downtime. Energy audits often show that reducing air leaks and optimising fan speed can cut conveying energy by 1525%.

SAFETY WARNING Roller Mill and Pneumatic Systems

Never open a roller mill, plansifter, bran finisher, or pneumatic duct while the machine is running or while air pressure remains in the system. Isolate and lock out electrical power, close and lock air valves, and allow moving parts to come to a complete stop before any inspection or maintenance. In African mills, lockout/tagout (LOTO) procedures are often informal; formal LOTO saves lives and prevents costly equipment damage. Ensure all operators are trained and authorised before entering any machine.

5. Maintenance and Roll Changes

Planned maintenance is the single biggest lever for profitability in African milling. Unplanned downtime costs far more than planned maintenance. A typical roller mill should have a preventive maintenance schedule covering daily, weekly, monthly, and annual tasks.

Roll changes should be planned around production schedules. Re-corrugation or replacement of break rolls is needed when the corrugation profile is worn, typically after 500800 hours of operation depending on wheat quality. Smooth reduction rolls may need re-grinding or replacement after 2,0003,000 hours.

Spare parts management is critical. Mills in landlocked countries or far from ports should keep a minimum stock of critical spares: roll bearings, sieve frames, gaskets, belts, and pneumatic filters. A simple spreadsheet-based spare parts register can prevent costly emergency imports.

6. Automation and Control Systems in Milling

Automation improves consistency, reduces labour cost, and enables remote monitoring. In African mills, automation ranges from basic PLC control of feed rates to full SCADA systems with recipe management. The business case for automation is strongest where labour is scarce or expensive, or where quality consistency is required for premium customers.

Key automation functions: automatic roll gap control, feed rate control, sifter monitoring, pneumatic pressure monitoring, and production reporting. Even simple automation such as automatic feed control on roller mills can increase capacity by 510% and reduce energy per tonne.

Challenges in Africa include unstable power, limited technical support, and high initial cost. A phased approach is recommended: start with feed rate control and production monitoring, then add quality control and remote diagnostics.

Quick Reference Checklist

  • Check roll gap and feed rate daily; adjust for wheat moisture and hardness.
  • Inspect plansifter sieves weekly for blinding, tears, and gasket wear.
  • Verify purifier air flow and clean air channels monthly.
  • Inspect bran finisher and detacher screens and beater bars weekly.
  • Check pneumatic conveying ducts, bends, filters, and air leaks monthly.
  • Follow lockout/tagout (LOTO) before any maintenance; never bypass safety devices.
  • Maintain a critical spare parts register and reorder before stock runs out.
  • Review production and energy data weekly to identify efficiency losses.

Business takeaway: In African flour milling, the machines are only as good as the maintenance and operating discipline behind them. Investing in planned maintenance, operator training, and simple automation delivers higher extraction, lower energy cost per tonne, and fewer costly breakdowns. The mills that treat maintenance as a profit centre not a cost centre are the ones that win contracts and grow.
