1. Boiling water and steeping fresh ginger slices

Start with clean water. If you are on municipal supply, tap water is fine. If you use well or river water, boil it first and let it cool, then boil again for tea. For a strong ginger tea, use 1 litre of water and 30â40 grams of fresh ginger root (about a 5 cm piece). Wash the ginger well, scrape off the skin with a spoon or knife, and slice it thinâabout 2 mm thick. Thin slices release more flavour. You can also grate the ginger, but slices are easier to strain.

Bring the water to a rolling boil in a stainless steel or enamel pot. Do not use aluminiumâit reacts with ginger and gives a metallic taste. Once boiling, add the ginger slices. Reduce heat and simmer, covered, for 10â15 minutes. The longer you simmer, the stronger the tea. For a mild tea, 5 minutes is enough. For a spicy, medicinal brew, go up to 20 minutes. You will see the water turn pale yellow.

Turn off the heat and let the ginger steep for another 5 minutes. This extracts the last of the flavour. Strain through a fine mesh sieve or clean cloth into a teapot or flask. If you want to keep it hot for hours, pour into a vacuum flask. Fresh ginger tea is best drunk within 4 hours. After that, it loses its zing and can ferment.

For a single cup, use 250 ml water and 8â10 grams ginger. Simmer 10 minutes. You can reuse the ginger slices once: after straining, add fresh boiling water and simmer 5 minutes more. The second brew is weaker but still good.

If you are selling, make a big batch: 10 litres water, 400 grams ginger. Simmer 20 minutes. This yields about 40 cups of 250 ml. Keep the pot covered to trap volatile oils. Serve hot. Do not add sugar during boilingâit can burn. Sweeten in the cup.

Always taste before serving. If it is too weak, add more ginger and simmer longer. If too strong, dilute with hot water. Practice with small batches until you know your preferred strength.

2. Making turmeric tea with milk or plant-based alternatives

Turmeric tea, often called golden milk, is made with fresh or dried turmeric, milk, and a pinch of black pepper. Pepper boosts absorption of curcumin, the active compound. For one cup, use 250 ml milk and 5 grams fresh turmeric (or 1 teaspoon turmeric powder). If using fresh, grate or slice thin. If using powder, whisk it into a little cold water first to avoid lumps.

Choose your milk. In many African cities, fresh cow milk is available. You can also use plant milks: coconut milk (canned or fresh), soy milk, or groundnut milk. Coconut milk gives a rich, creamy tea. Soy milk is neutral. Groundnut milk is local and cheapâsoak raw groundnuts overnight, blend with water, strain. For a dairy-free version, use any of these. If using powdered milk, reconstitute first.

In a pot, warm the milk over medium heat. Do not boil hardâmilk scorches easily. Add turmeric, a pinch of black pepper (about 1/8 teaspoon), and optional spices: a small piece of cinnamon, one crushed cardamom pod, or a thin slice of ginger. Stir well. Simmer gently for 5â7 minutes. The milk should turn a bright golden yellow. If it looks pale, add more turmeric.

Taste. It should be earthy, slightly bitter, and creamy. Add honey or sugar to tasteâabout 1 teaspoon per cup. For a vegan version, use honey alternative like date syrup or jaggery. If you used fresh turmeric, strain before drinking. If you used powder, it settles at the bottomâstir before each sip.

For a large batch for market: 4 litres milk, 80 grams fresh turmeric (or 16 teaspoons powder), 2 teaspoons black pepper, 4 cinnamon sticks. Simmer 15 minutes. This serves about 16 cups. Keep hot in a vacuum flask or slow cooker. Stir often. Do not let it boil over.

Turmeric tea can also be made with water instead of milk for a lighter drink. But milk helps the curcumin absorb better. Experiment with ratios. If it is too thick, add hot water. If too thin, simmer longer to reduce. Always label your tea if sellingâsome people are allergic to dairy or nuts.

3. Adding honey, lemon, or local spices for flavour

Plain ginger or turmeric tea is healthy but can taste sharp. Adding honey, lemon, or local spices makes it delicious and increases sales. Add these after brewing, not during boiling, to preserve flavour and nutrients.

Honey: Use raw, unprocessed honey if possible. Add 1â2 teaspoons per cup after the tea has cooled slightly (below 60Â°C). Boiling honey destroys its enzymes and can make it toxic. Stir well. If you are selling, offer honey on the side so customers can sweeten their own. Local honey from baobab, acacia, or miombo flowers adds unique taste.

Lemon: Fresh lemon juice brightens the tea. Use 1 teaspoon per cup. Add after brewing. You can also drop in a slice of lemon with peel for decoration. Lime works too. If lemons are expensive, use tamarind pasteâsoak tamarind in hot water, strain, and add 1 teaspoon per cup for a sour kick. Or use hibiscus (zobo) petals for tartness and red colour.

Local spices: Ginger and turmeric pair well with black pepper, cinnamon, cloves, cardamom, and nutmeg. In West Africa, add a pinch of grains of selim (uda) or African nutmeg. In East Africa, add a small piece of cinnamon bark or a few cloves. In Southern Africa, add a slice of fresh ginger and a stick of cinnamon. Use whole spices, not ground, for better flavour. Crush them lightly before adding. Simmer with the tea for the last 5 minutes.

Other local additions: lemongrass (fever grass) gives a citrusy note. Moringa leaves add nutrition but turn tea greenâuse sparingly. Mint leaves are refreshing. For a chai-style tea, add 2 crushed cardamom pods, 1 clove, and a small piece of cinnamon per cup.

When selling, offer options: plain, honey-lemon, spicy. Label clearly. Keep honey and lemon in separate squeeze bottles. For large batches, add honey and lemon just before serving to avoid fermentation. If you add them early, the tea can spoil within hours. Always taste after addingâadjust to your customers' preference. Too sweet? Add more lemon. Too sour? Add a little honey. Record your best ratios so you can repeat them.

4. Brewing large batches for events and markets

Selling at events or markets requires brewing 10â50 litres at a time. You need large pots, a reliable heat source, and a system. For 20 litres of ginger tea, use 800 grams fresh ginger, 20 litres water, and optional spices. For turmeric tea, use 400 grams fresh turmeric (or 80 teaspoons powder) and 20 litres milk or water. Always make a test batch of 2 litres first to check strength.

Equipment: Use 20â50 litre stainless steel pots. Avoid aluminium. You will need a long wooden spoon, a fine mesh strainer or clean cloth, a funnel, and insulated dispensers (thermos flasks or coolers). For markets, a gas burner or charcoal stove works. If using firewood, keep the fire steady. Bring water to a boil, add ingredients, and simmer. For 20 litres, simmering takes 30â40 minutes. Stir occasionally.

Timing: Start brewing 2 hours before the event. For ginger tea, simmer 20 minutes, then steep 10 minutes. For turmeric tea, simmer 15 minutes. Strain into a clean pot. Keep hot. If you need to transport, pour into vacuum flasks or insulated jugs. Do not use plastic buckets for hot teaâthey leach chemicals and melt.

Serving: Set up a table with cups, lids, stirrers, honey, lemon, and napkins. Use a dispenser with a tap to avoid lifting heavy pots. Price per cup: in many African cities, 200â300 ml sells for 50â100 KES (or equivalent). For events, charge a flat rate per cup or offer a package. Keep a cash box and a helper.

Hygiene: Wash all equipment with soap and hot water before and after. Use clean water. Do not touch the inside of cups. Wear an apron and head covering. If you have leftover tea, cool quickly and refrigerate. Reheat only once. Never mix old tea with new. For large batches, consider adding a little citric acid or lemon juice to preserve colour and taste. Always have extra ingredients on handârunning out mid-event loses sales. Keep a notebook of quantities and costs to calculate profit.

5. Serving hot tea safely at pop-up stalls

Hot tea can burn you and your customers. Safety is critical at pop-up stalls. First, choose a stable table. A folding table with locking legs is best. Place it on flat ground, away from crowds and children. Cover with a clean cloth. Put your pots and dispensers at the back of the table, not the front, so customers cannot knock them over.

Use insulated dispensers with spigots. They keep tea hot (above 60Â°C) and prevent spills. If you use a pot, never fill it more than two-thirds full. Hot liquid expands. Always keep the lid on. When pouring, use a ladle or a spigot. Never pour over a customer's hand. Place cups on the table, then fill. Warn customers: "Very hot." Serve with a napkin or sleeve.

Personal safety: Wear closed shoes, not sandals. Hot tea can splash. Wear an apron and, if possible, heat-resistant gloves when handling pots. Tie back long hair. Keep a bucket of clean water and a first-aid kit nearby. If you burn yourself, cool the burn under running water for 10 minutes, then cover. Do not apply oil or toothpaste.

Stall setup: Have a clear sign with prices. Keep a bin for used cups. Provide a small bin for waste. If you offer honey and lemon, put them in squeeze bottles with labels. Keep a damp cloth for wiping spills. Check the spigot for leaks before opening. Test the temperature by pouring a small amount into a cup and touching the outsideâit should be hot but not scalding.

Customer safety: Do not serve tea that has been sitting out for more than 2 hours. Bacteria grow in warm tea. If you cannot keep it above 60Â°C, reheat it to a rolling boil for 1 minute, then cool to serving temperature. Never serve tea that smells sour or looks cloudy. Use lids on cups for takeaway. For children, serve in a smaller cup and warn the parent. Always have a fire extinguisher or sand bucket if using a gas stove. At the end of the day, wash all equipment with soap and hot water. Store dry. Count your earnings and restock for next time.

6. Packaging dry tea mixes for longer shelf life

Dry tea mixes let you sell beyond your stall. You can package ginger-turmeric tea blend, spiced chai mix, or instant golden milk. The key is to remove all moisture. Any moisture causes mould. Start with fully dry ingredients: dried ginger slices, dried turmeric slices, dried lemon peel, cinnamon bark, cloves, cardamom pods, black pepper, and optional dried milk powder or coconut milk powder. If you use fresh ingredients, dry them first in a solar dryer or oven at low heat (50â60Â°C) until brittle.

Grind the dried spices in a clean blender or mortar. For a tea mix, grind to a coarse powderânot fine dust. A coarse grind brews better and strains easily. For an instant mix, grind to a fine powder and add milk powder. A basic recipe: 100 g dried ginger, 50 g dried turmeric, 10 g black pepper, 20 g cinnamon, 5 g cloves, 5 g cardamom. Mix well. This makes about 190 g. For one cup, use 1â2 teaspoons.

Packaging: Use airtight containers. Options: glass jars with rubber seals, zip-lock pouches, or heat-sealed foil bags. For small-scale, clean dry jam jars work. Fill to the top to reduce air. Add a silica gel packet if available to absorb moisture. Label with ingredients, weight, and date. Include brewing instructions: "Add 1 teaspoon to 250 ml boiling water. Simmer 5 minutes. Add milk and honey if desired."

Shelf life: Properly dried and sealed, the mix lasts 6â12 months in a cool, dark place. Keep away from sunlight and heat. Do not store near strong-smelling foodsâthe spices absorb odours. If you see clumps or smell mustiness, discard. For selling, calculate cost: ingredients plus packaging plus labour. A 100 g jar might cost 200 KES to make and sell for 500 KES. Price competitively.

Marketing: Sell at markets, health shops, and online. Offer samples. Use clear labels with your brand and contact. For export, check local regulations. In many African countries, you need a food handling permit. Start small, test your mix with friends, and adjust spices. Record your best recipe. Package in attractive, clean containers. Dry mixes are lightweight and travel wellâperfect for pop-up stalls and gift sets.

Practical Reference: Brewing Ginger & Turmeric Teas for African Markets

This practical reference consolidates the six lessons in the Brewing Ginger and Turmeric Teas module into a single working document. It is written for African agribusiness operators â from a one-person kitchen in Kumasi to a cooperative bottling line in Nairobi â who need to produce consistent, safe, saleable tea products at scale. Every technique below is framed around three business questions: What does it cost? How long does it keep? What will customers pay?

Ginger (Zingiber officinale) and turmeric (Curcuma longa) are both widely cultivated across West, East and Southern Africa. Nigeria, Cameroon, Ghana, Uganda and Tanzania are significant producers. Turmeric's active compound, curcumin, is fat-soluble and poorly absorbed in plain water â which is exactly why the milk-based preparation described below matters both for health claims and for product differentiation.

1. Boiling Water and Steeping Fresh Ginger Slices

Use 10â15 g of fresh, washed, unpeeled ginger per 1 litre of water for a mild commercial brew, or 20â25 g for a strong "shot-style" base. Bring water to a rolling boil (100 Â°C at sea level; roughly 94â96 Â°C in highland towns such as Addis Ababa or Nairobi). Add sliced ginger, reduce heat, and simmer covered for 10â15 minutes. Covering matters: gingerols and shogaols are volatile, and an open pot loses aroma â and therefore perceived value â to the air.

For a lighter, more refreshing tea, steep off the boil for 20 minutes instead of simmering. Strain through muslin or a fine sieve. The spent slices can be dried and re-milled into a lower-grade tea mix, reducing waste by 15â20%.

2. Turmeric Tea with Milk or Plant-Based Alternatives

Turmeric needs fat and heat to release curcumin. In West Africa, this is traditionally done with cow's milk or groundnut milk; in East Africa, coconut milk is common. The working formula is 1 teaspoon (3 g) turmeric powder + 1 cup (240 ml) milk + pinch of black pepper. The piperine in black pepper can increase curcumin bioavailability by up to 2,000% according to published pharmacokinetic studies (Shoba et al., 1998, Planta Medica).

Plant-based options â soy, coconut, groundnut, tiger nut â all work, but soy milk gives the closest mouthfeel to dairy and the highest protein, which supports a premium price point. Heat gently; do not boil milk hard or it will split.

3. Adding Honey, Lemon, or Local Spices

Add honey and lemon after removing the pot from heat. Boiling destroys honey's enzymes and volatilises lemon oils. Local spice additions that sell well across African markets include:

  • Black pepper â bioavailability booster (essential, not optional)
  • Cloves and cinnamon â warm, familiar, cheap in bulk
  • Lemongrass â abundant in East Africa, adds citrus note without lemon cost
  • Hibiscus (sobolo/zobo) â deep red colour, high vitamin C, strong regional recognition
  • Moringa leaf â nutritional positioning, but bitter above 5% of blend
  • Baobab powder â tangy, high in vitamin C, premium positioning

5. Serving Hot Tea Safely at Pop-Up Stalls

Hot liquid is the single biggest liability at a pop-up. Use insulated urns with locking lids, never open pots on a folding table. Serve in cups with a sleeve or double wall. Position the urn behind the counter, away from the customer side, and never let a customer self-serve from a 50-litre urn.
