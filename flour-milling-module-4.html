1. Overview of the Milling Flow Diagram

A milling flow diagram is simply a map of how wheat becomes flour. It shows each machine, the order they run in, and where the stock (the material being ground) goes after each step. In a typical African mill, whether in Nairobi, Lagos, or Johannesburg, the diagram starts with wheat cleaning, then goes to the break system, then the reduction system, then sifting and purification, and finally to finished flour and by-products. You need to understand this flow because if one machine is set wrong, the whole line suffers.

Draw the diagram on a piece of paper or a whiteboard. Use boxes for machines and arrows for stock movement. For example: Wheat Cleaner First Break Roll Sifter Second Break Roll Sifter Purifier Reduction Rolls Sifter Flour. In a real mill, you might have 4 or 5 break rolls and 8 to 12 reduction rolls. The diagram helps you see where problems start. If your flour is too dark, maybe the break system is over-grinding. If your extraction rate is low, maybe the sifters are overloaded.

In many small to medium African mills, the flow diagram is often ignored, and operators adjust machines by guesswork. That leads to inconsistent flour and high energy costs. Take time to walk the mill with your diagram. Mark each machine with a number or letter. Check that the stock from each break goes to the correct sifter and then to the correct purifier or reduction roll. A simple mistake like sending first break stock to the wrong sifter can ruin your whole day.

Also, note the by-products: bran, germ, and shorts. They have value for animal feed. Your diagram should show where they are separated. In Africa, where feed costs are high, selling bran can add income. So the flow diagram is not just about flour; its about money. Keep a copy of the diagram in the mill office. Update it if you change the flow. Train your workers to read it. That way, everyone knows what happens next and why.

2. Break System: Roller Mills and Corrugations

The break system is the first grinding stage. Its job is to open the wheat kernel and scrape the endosperm away from the bran, not to make fine flour. You do this with roller mills that have corrugated (grooved) rolls. The rolls turn against each other at different speeds. The faster roll pulls the wheat in, and the grooves shear it open. In a typical African mill, you might have 4 or 5 break passages. First break rolls are set wide, maybe 0.5 to 0.8 mm apart, to just crack the wheat. Later breaks (second, third, fourth) are set tighter to scrape more endosperm off the bran.

Corrugations matter. They come in different pitches (number of grooves per inch) and profiles (saw-tooth, sharp-to-sharp, dull-to-dull). For first break, use a coarse pitch, like 10 to 12 grooves per inch, with sharp-to-sharp edges. That bites into the wheat. For later breaks, use finer pitch, like 16 to 20 grooves per inch, and dull-to-dull to scrape without cutting the bran too much. In many small mills, operators don't change corrugations until they are worn flat. Worn rolls mean poor separation, more bran in flour, and higher power use. Check your rolls every 3 to 6 months. If the grooves are rounded, re-corrugate or replace.

Set the roll gap with a feeler gauge. Start with first break at 0.6 mm, second at 0.4 mm, third at 0.2 mm, fourth at 0.1 mm. Adjust based on the stock you see. After first break, you should have large chunks of endosperm and big bran flakes. After fourth break, the bran should be mostly clean, with little endosperm sticking to it. If bran comes out with white patches, your later breaks are too wide. If you see fine powder at first break, your gap is too tight and you are making flour too early, which loads the sifters.

In Africa, wheat can be harder or softer depending on origin. Hard wheat needs more break passages and tighter gaps. Soft wheat needs fewer. Always check the wheat specs before setting rolls. Also, keep the rolls cool. Friction heats them, and hot rolls can smear bran into the endosperm. Use water cooling if available, or run at lower feed rates. Remember: the break system is not a flour maker; it's a separator. Your goal is clean bran and clean endosperm chunks.

3. Reduction System: Smooth Rolls and Roll Chills

After the break system, you have chunks of endosperm (called semolina or middlings) and some flour. The reduction system grinds those chunks into fine flour using smooth rolls. Unlike break rolls, reduction rolls have no grooves. They are smooth, chilled iron rolls that crush and shear the stock. In a typical mill, you have 8 to 12 reduction passages. The first reduction rolls are set wider, and later ones are set tighter. The goal is to reduce particle size gradually, not all at once. If you grind too fine too early, you damage starch and make poor flour.

Roll chills are the hardened surface of the rolls. They are made by chilling the outer layer of cast iron, which makes it very hard and wear-resistant. In Africa, where mills often run 24/7, roll chills can wear out. You know they are worn when the surface feels rough or you see streaks on the stock. Worn rolls give inconsistent grind and heat up faster. Check the roll surface every month with a magnifying glass. If you see pits or grooves, re-grind or replace. A good roll chill lasts 2 to 3 years in a well-maintained mill.

Set reduction roll gaps carefully. First reduction might be 0.3 mm, then 0.2 mm, then 0.1 mm, and final reduction 0.05 mm or less. Use a feeler gauge and check both ends of the roll. The rolls must be parallel, or you will get uneven wear and poor grinding. Also, the speed differential between rolls matters. Typically, the fast roll runs 1.5 to 2.5 times faster than the slow roll. That differential creates a shearing action that grinds the stock. If the differential is too low, you get coarse flour; if too high, you overheat the stock.

Feed the reduction rolls evenly. Use a distributor or feed roll to spread the stock across the full width of the roll. If you feed in one spot, you wear the rolls unevenly and get hot spots. In many African mills, operators run reduction rolls too tight to increase extraction, but that leads to starch damage and dark flour. Better to run slightly wider and rely on sifting and purification to separate the flour. Remember: reduction is about quality, not just quantity. Aim for a smooth, cool grind that gives you bright, white flour.

4. Sifting and Purification

Sifting and purification are the sorting steps. After each break and reduction passage, the ground stock goes to a sifter. A sifter is a machine with stacks of sieves that separate particles by size. In a typical African mill, you use plansifters or rotary sifters. Plansifters have square sieves that shake side to side. They are efficient and take less floor space. Rotary sifters are cylindrical and rotate. Both do the same job: separate flour, semolina, and bran.

The sifter has different mesh sizes. For example, a first break sifter might have a 20 mesh (coarse) on top to catch large bran, then a 40 mesh to catch semolina, and a 100 mesh to catch flour. The tailings (overs) go to the next break or reduction roll. The throughs (unders) go to the next stage. You must check the sieves daily for holes or tears. A small hole can send bran into your flour and ruin the color. In Africa, where sieves are often imported, keep spare screens. Patch small holes with flour paste and cloth, but replace torn screens quickly.

Purification is for the semolina (middlings) from the break system. A purifier is a machine that uses air and sieves to separate pure endosperm from bran and germ. It looks like a long vibrating box with air blowing up through the sieves. The lighter bran and germ float and are sucked away, while the heavier pure semolina falls through. In a small mill, you might not have a purifier, but you can use a simple aspirator or even a fan and sieve setup. The goal is to remove as much bran as possible before reduction. Pure semolina gives whiter flour and less wear on reduction rolls.

Set your sifter and purifier for the stock you are running. If you see flour in the bran stream, your sifter is overloaded or the mesh is too coarse. If you see bran in the flour, the mesh is too fine or the sifter is torn. Check the air settings on the purifier. Too much air sucks good semolina into the bran; too little air leaves bran in the semolina. In Africa, humidity affects sifting. On rainy days, stock may stick to sieves. Reduce feed rate or add a little heat to the sifter. Keep records of sifter performance. A well-tuned sifter and purifier can raise extraction by 2 to 3 percent, which is real money.

5. Material Balance in Milling

Material balance means tracking how much wheat goes in and how much flour, bran, and shorts come out. In a mill, you want to know your extraction rate: the percentage of flour you get from clean wheat. In Africa, a good extraction rate for bread flour is 72 to 75 percent. For biscuit flour, it can be 65 to 70 percent. If you are getting 60 percent, you are losing money. Material balance helps you find where you are losing stock.

Start by weighing your wheat before cleaning. Then weigh the clean wheat after cleaning. The difference is cleaning loss (stones, dust, foreign matter). Typically, cleaning loss is 1 to 2 percent. Then weigh your finished flour and by-products. If you put in 1000 kg of clean wheat, you should get about 750 kg flour, 200 kg bran, and 50 kg shorts. If your numbers don't add up, you have a leak. Common leaks: stock spilled on the floor, dust going to the cyclone, or sifter screens passing too much into the wrong stream.

Do a material balance test every month. Run the mill at steady rate for one hour. Collect and weigh all outputs. Calculate extraction: (flour weight / clean wheat weight) x 100. Also calculate bran yield and shorts yield. Compare with previous months. If extraction drops, check your break and reduction roll gaps. Worn rolls or too-wide gaps leave endosperm in the bran. If bran yield is high, you are not scraping enough endosperm off the bran. If shorts yield is high, your sifters are not separating properly.

In many African mills, material balance is not done because it takes time. But it is the only way to know if you are efficient. Use a simple spreadsheet or notebook. Record wheat in, flour out, bran out, shorts out, and running hours. Also record power consumption. You can calculate kWh per ton of flour. If power per ton goes up, your rolls are dull or your sifters are overloaded. Material balance also helps with costing. If you know your extraction, you can price your flour correctly. Remember: what you don't measure, you can't improve.

6. Optimizing Break and Reduction Performance

Optimizing break and reduction means getting the most flour of the best quality with the least energy and wear. Start with the break system. Check your corrugations. If they are worn, you are not opening the wheat properly. Re-corrugate or replace rolls. Set the gap for each break based on the wheat hardness. For hard wheat, use tighter gaps and more break passages. For soft wheat, use wider gaps and fewer passages. Also, check the roll speed differential. For break rolls, the fast roll should run 2.5 to 3 times faster than the slow roll. That pulls the wheat in and shears it. If the differential is too low, the wheat just rides on the rolls.

In the reduction system, the key is gradual size reduction. Don't try to make fine flour in one pass. Use multiple reduction passages with progressively tighter gaps. Check the roll chills for wear. If they are smooth and shiny, they are good. If they are dull or have pits, re-grind them. Also, check the roll temperature. If rolls are hot to the touch, reduce feed rate or improve cooling. Hot rolls cause starch damage and dark flour. In Africa, where ambient temperatures can be high, you may need to run the mill slower during the hottest part of the day.

Sifting and purification also need optimization. Check sieve mesh sizes. For break sifters, use coarser mesh to let semolina through and keep bran over. For reduction sifters, use finer mesh to get white flour. If you see flour in the bran, your sifter is overloaded. Reduce feed rate or add more sifter area. For purifiers, adjust air volume. Too much air wastes good semolina; too little leaves bran. Use a clear glass window or sample port to see what is happening. In many African mills, purifiers are set once and never touched. That is a mistake. Adjust them weekly based on wheat and humidity.

Finally, train your operators. They should know how to check roll gaps, read sifter performance, and record data. Give them a simple checklist for each shift: check roll temperatures, listen for unusual noises, look at stock color and texture. A good operator can spot problems before they cause downtime. Also, keep a maintenance schedule. Re-corrugate break rolls every 6 to 12 months, re-grind reduction rolls every 12 to 24 months, and replace sifter screens as needed. In Africa, where spare parts can take weeks to arrive, preventive maintenance is critical. Optimizing is not a one-time job; it is a daily habit.

<h2>2. Reduction System: Smooth Rolls and Roll Chills</h2>

<p>Reduction rolls are smooth, chilled-iron (or chilled cast iron) rolls that grind purified middlings into flour. They operate at higher differentials, smaller gaps, and lower flute counts (zero flutes). Roll chill hardness is critical: a chilled-iron roll with a surface hardness of 500550 Brinell resists wear far longer than a standard steel roll, which matters when you are running 24/6 in an African mill where spare parts lead times can be weeks.</p>

<h2>3. Optimising Break and Reduction Performance</h2>

<p>Optimisation in an African milling context is about three things: extraction rate, energy cost, and flour consistency. A 1% improvement in extraction on a 200 t/day mill yields an extra 2 tonnes of flour per day at USD 400/tonne, that is roughly USD 800/day or USD 250,000/year in additional revenue. Here is how to get there.</p>

<p><strong>Break optimisation:</strong> Start with wheat tempering. Target 15.516.5% moisture for hard wheat, 14.515.5% for soft wheat, with a minimum 1218 hour tempering time (24 hours for hard wheat). Under-tempered wheat produces bran that shatters and contaminates flour; over-tempered wheat smears on the rolls. Check your B1 roll gap weekly with a feeler gauge. If B1 extraction drops below 6%, your flutes are dull schedule re-corrugation.</p>

<p><strong>Reduction optimisation:</strong> Monitor C1 gap daily. A 0.05 mm drift over a week is normal wear; a 0.10 mm drift means you need to re-set or re-grind. Check roll temperature if rolls run above 55 C, you are generating excessive heat that denatures gluten and reduces flour quality. Ensure the roll cooling water system is working, especially in hot African climates where ambient temperatures exceed 35 C.</p>

<p><strong>Sifting and purification:</strong> Check sieve mesh integrity weekly. A single torn sieve can send bran into your flour stream and cause a customer complaint or a rejected delivery. Purifier settings air velocity and sieve inclination should be adjusted for each wheat batch, as local wheat varieties differ in density and particle size.</p>

<p><strong>Material balance:</strong> Run a full mill balance test quarterly. Weigh inputs and outputs over a 4-hour run. If your extraction is below 70%, investigate: are your break flutes dull? Are your reduction gaps too wide? Is your tempering moisture correct? A mill balance test is the single most valuable diagnostic you can do, and it costs only a few hours of production time.</p>

<h2>Quick Reference Checklist</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Tempering:</strong> Verify wheat moisture (15.516.5% hard, 14.515.5% soft) and tempering time (1224 h) before milling.</li>
<li><strong>Break flutes:</strong> Inspect B1B3 flutes weekly; re-corrugate when extraction falls below target for two consecutive shifts.</li>
<li><strong>Roll gaps:</strong> Measure B1, B2, C1 and C2 gaps daily with a feeler gauge; log readings and investigate any drift &gt; 0.05 mm/week.</li>
<li><strong>Roll temperature:</strong> Confirm rolls run below 55 C; check cooling water flow and temperature, especially in hot climates.</li>
<li><strong>Sieve integrity:</strong> Inspect plansifter sieves weekly for tears, holes or loose frames; replace immediately.</li>
<li><strong>Extraction rate:</strong> Track daily extraction; investigate any drop below 72% within 24 hours.</li>
<li><strong>Energy:</strong> Monitor kWh/tonne; a sudden rise indicates dull rolls, over-grinding or blocked sifters.</li>
<li><strong>Mill balance:</strong> Run a full material balance test quarterly and document inputs, outputs, and losses.</li>
</ol>
