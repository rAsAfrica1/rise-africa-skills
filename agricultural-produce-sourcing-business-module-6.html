1. Planning Transportation: Mode, Route, and Scheduling

When moving produce from farm to market, you must decide on the mode of transport. In most African contexts, road transport is the most practical. For short distances (under 100 km), a pickup truck or a hired boda boda can work. For longer distances, a 7-tonne lorry is common. Rail is cheaper for bulk but limited in reach. Air is only for high-value perishables like flowers or green beans, and it's expensive. Water transport can be used along rivers or lakes, but it's slow and risky for perishables.

Route planning saves money and time. Use Google Maps or a local map to identify the shortest safe route. Avoid routes with known delays like weighbridges or bad roads. For example, from a farm in Nakuru to Nairobi, the A104 is faster than the B3. Schedule transport to avoid peak traffic hours. If you're moving tomatoes, harvest in the early morning, load by 6 AM, and reach the market by 10 AM to catch the best prices.

Consider backhauling: don't let a truck return empty. If you hire a lorry to take produce to the city, find a load for the return trip, like fertilizer or animal feed. This can cut your transport cost by 30-40%. Always agree on loading and offloading times with the driver. Delays mean spoilage. For perishables, use refrigerated trucks if available, but they cost more. A cheaper option is to use insulated boxes with ice packs.

Keep a transport log: date, vehicle, driver, route, cost, and any delays. This helps you negotiate better rates and avoid unreliable transporters. Remember, proper planning reduces post-harvest losses, which can be as high as 40% in Africa. So invest time in planning.

2. Packaging and Handling to Prevent Damage and Spoilage

Packaging protects produce from bruising, crushing, and spoilage. Use the right material for each crop. For tomatoes, use shallow wooden crates or plastic crates (not sacks) to prevent squashing. For leafy greens, use ventilated plastic crates or baskets. For grains, use woven polypropylene sacks (50 kg or 100 kg). For fruits like mangoes, use single-layer trays with paper liners.

Proper handling starts at harvest. Train workers to handle produce gently. Use clean, sharp knives for harvesting. Do not throw produce into bins. Use padded bins or liners. For example, when harvesting avocados, pick with a pole and catch them in a soft net. After harvest, sort and grade. Remove damaged items immediately. Pack only healthy produce.

Ventilation is key. Perishables like bananas and tomatoes produce ethylene gas, which speeds ripening. Use crates with holes to allow air circulation. Do not stack too high; maximum stack height for tomatoes is 4 crates. For onions, use mesh bags and store in a cool, dry place. For leafy greens, keep them cool and mist them with water.

Label each package with the crop, grade, weight, and harvest date. This helps with inventory and traceability. Use cheap labels or marker pens. For export, follow buyer specifications. For local markets, simple labeling is fine. Remember, good packaging reduces losses by up to 25%. So invest in quality crates and train your team.

3. Warehousing and Storage Best Practices

A warehouse protects your produce from weather, pests, and theft. For a small operation, you can use a clean, dry room with good ventilation. For larger volumes, rent a warehouse near your market. Choose a location with easy access for trucks. The floor should be concrete or pallets to prevent moisture. Walls should be free of cracks. Install rodent-proof screens and use traps.

Temperature and humidity control are critical. For grains, keep humidity below 14% to prevent mold. Use a moisture meter (cost: $20-$50) to check. For perishables like potatoes and onions, store at 4-10C with 85-90% humidity. If you don't have a cold room, use evaporative cooling: place produce in a shaded, ventilated structure with wet sand or charcoal. This can reduce temperature by 5-10C.

Organize your warehouse with FIFO (First In, First Out). Stack older stock in front. Use pallets to keep produce off the floor. Leave aisles for movement. Inspect weekly for pests, mold, or spoilage. Keep a log of in/out movements. For grains, use hermetic storage bags (e.g., Purdue Improved Crop Storage bags) to prevent weevils without chemicals. They cost about $2 each and can store 100 kg.

Security: install locks, and if possible, a watchman. For high-value produce, use a simple alarm. Keep records of who enters and leaves. A well-managed warehouse can reduce losses by 15-20%. So invest in basic storage improvements.

4. Inventory Management and Stock Control

Inventory management ensures you always have enough produce to meet orders without overstocking. Start by keeping a simple stock card for each crop. Record date, quantity in, quantity out, and balance. Use a notebook or a free app like Google Sheets. For example, if you buy 100 kg of tomatoes on Monday and sell 60 kg by Wednesday, your balance is 40 kg. Update daily.

Set reorder levels. If you sell 20 kg of onions per day, and it takes 2 days to get new stock, keep at least 40 kg as safety stock. When stock falls to 50 kg, order more. This prevents stockouts. For perishables, order smaller quantities more frequently. For grains, you can buy in bulk during harvest when prices are low.

Conduct a physical count every week. Compare with your records. If there's a discrepancy, investigate: theft, spoilage, or recording errors. Use FIFO to rotate stock. For perishables, mark dates on packages. Sell older stock first. For grains, use fumigation or hermetic bags to prevent losses.

Use a simple ABC analysis: A items are high-value (e.g., vanilla), B medium (e.g., tomatoes), C low (e.g., maize). Focus more control on A items. For example, count A items daily, B weekly, C monthly. This saves time. Also, track shrinkage: the difference between recorded and actual stock. If shrinkage is above 5%, find the cause. Good inventory management increases profit by reducing waste and improving cash flow.

5. Cold Chain Management for Perishable Produce

A cold chain keeps produce at low temperatures from harvest to sale. For perishables like milk, meat, fish, and leafy vegetables, it's essential. Without it, spoilage can reach 50%. Start at the farm: harvest in the cool of the morning. Use insulated boxes with ice packs for immediate cooling. For example, for green beans, pre-cool to 7C within 2 hours of harvest.

Transport in refrigerated trucks if available. They cost more, but reduce losses. A 7-tonne refrigerated truck costs about $0.50 per km in East Africa. If you can't afford that, use insulated containers with dry ice or gel packs. For short trips, use a cooler box with ice. For milk, use a 20-litre aluminum can with a lid, and deliver within 4 hours.

At the warehouse, use a cold room. Set temperature according to crop: leafy greens at 0-2C, tomatoes at 10-12C, bananas at 13-15C. Use a thermometer to monitor. If power is unreliable, use a backup generator or solar-powered cold room. Solar cold rooms are available in Kenya for about $5,000 for a small unit.

Keep records of temperature. Any break in the cold chain reduces shelf life. For meat, keep frozen at -18C. For fish, use ice and keep at 0C. Train staff on hygiene: wash hands, clean containers. A good cold chain can extend shelf life by 3-5 times. So invest in it for high-value perishables.

6. Coordinating with Transporters and Logistics Providers

You need reliable transporters. Start by identifying local truck owners, boda boda riders, or logistics companies. Ask other farmers or traders for recommendations. Check their reputation: do they deliver on time? Are they careful with produce? Visit their office or yard. For small loads, use apps like Uber Freight or Sendy (in Kenya) to find trucks.

Negotiate a clear contract. Include: pickup and delivery times, route, cost, payment terms, and liability for damage. For example, agree that the transporter pays for any spoilage caused by delays. Get it in writing, even if handwritten. For regular routes, negotiate a monthly rate. For example, a 7-tonne truck from Nakuru to Nairobi might cost $150 per trip. If you commit to 4 trips a month, ask for $130.

Communication is key. Share your schedule in advance. Confirm 24 hours before pickup. Use WhatsApp to send location and updates. Have a backup transporter in case of breakdowns. For perishables, insist on a refrigerated truck or insulated boxes. Inspect the truck before loading: is it clean? Does it have a working tailgate? Is it covered?

Track your shipment. Ask for the driver's phone number. Get updates at checkpoints. If delays occur, inform your buyer. For export, work with freight forwarders who handle customs and documentation. They charge a fee but save time. Build long-term relationships: pay on time, be polite, and give feedback. A good transporter is a business partner.

<h3>1. Planning Transportation: Mode, Route, and Scheduling</h3>
<p>For most African sourcing operations, road transport dominates: it is flexible, reaches farmgate, and can be arranged at short notice. Rail is competitive only on high-volume, longer corridors (e.g., MombasaNairobi, Tazara line), and air freight only for high-value, low-weight produce (French beans, cut flowers, berries) where the price premium justifies the cost.</p>
<p>Route planning must account for border crossings, weighbridges, and seasonal road conditions. A NairobiKampala reefer run can take 18 hours in dry season and 30+ hours in the long rains. Build buffer time into contracts, not into the produce.</p>

<h3>2. Packaging and Handling to Prevent Damage and Spoilage</h3>
<p>Packaging is not a cost centre it is a loss-prevention investment. The single biggest packaging error in African sourcing is overfilling crates or sacks, which crushes the bottom layer. Ventilated plastic crates (returnable) consistently outperform woven sacks for tomatoes, mangoes, and peppers because they allow airflow and stack without crushing.</p>
<p>Handle at every transfer point as if the produce were eggs. Each drop, throw, or careless stack adds bruising that becomes rot within 2448 hours. Train loaders and pay them for zero-damage handling it is cheaper than the loss.</p>

<h3>3. Warehousing and Storage Best Practices</h3>
<p>A warehouse for produce is not a general store. It needs airflow, pest control, temperature control, and clear segregation between commodities that emit ethylene (bananas, ripening fruit) and those sensitive to it (leafy greens, cucumbers). Stack on pallets, never on the floor; leave a 1015 cm gap from walls for air circulation.</p>
<p>Power reliability is the operational Achilles heel across much of Africa. Budget for a backup generator or solar-battery hybrid, and monitor temperature continuously with a data logger. A single 4-hour outage in a chill room can destroy a week's sourcing margin.</p>

<strong style="color:#7f1d1d;"> SAFETY WARNING:</strong>
<span style="color:#7f1d1d;"> Never enter a sealed cold room or reefer container alone. Refrigerant leaks (ammonia, CO) and oxygen depletion can cause rapid unconsciousness. Always use a two-person rule, a working external door release, and a gas monitor where ammonia systems are used. In addition, never stack produce above head height without a spotter, and keep loading bays clear of reversing vehicles struck-by incidents are the leading cause of serious injury at African produce hubs.</span>

<h3>4. Inventory Management and Stock Control</h3>
<p>For perishables, the correct discipline is FEFO First Expired, First Out not FIFO. Every pallet must carry a harvest date and a "sell-by" date. Use a simple stock card or a low-cost app (e.g., a spreadsheet synced to a phone) to track in/out daily. Reconcile physical stock against records at least weekly; shrinkage in produce is normal, but unexplained shrinkage is theft or spoilage you can fix.</p>
<p>Set reorder points based on lead time, not on gut feel. If your NairobiMombasa lane takes 2 days and you sell 40 crates a day, your reorder point is 80 crates plus a safety buffer for delays. Review weekly.</p>

<h3>5. Cold Chain Management for Perishable Produce</h3>
<p>The cold chain is a chain one broken link ruins the whole consignment. The critical control points are: (1) rapid precooling at farmgate or collection centre, (2) uninterrupted refrigerated transport, (3) cold storage at the hub, and (4) temperature-controlled display at market. Use data loggers on every reefer trip and review them with your transporter; this creates accountability and evidence for claims.</p>
<p>Match setpoints to commodity: leafy greens 02C, tomato 1012C (never below 10C or flavour and texture collapse), banana 13C, avocado 57C. Wrong setpoints cause chilling injury that looks like disease but is actually your own doing.</p>

<h3>6. Coordinating with Transporters and Logistics Providers</h3>
<p>Treat transporters as partners, not vendors. Sign a simple service-level agreement covering: pickup window, temperature setpoint, transit time, proof-of-delivery with photos, and a claims process for damage. Pay on time reliable transporters prioritise reliable payers. Where possible, contract dedicated vehicles for your peak season rather than competing on the spot market.</p>
<p>Track two KPIs per transporter: on-time delivery rate and damage/loss rate. Review monthly. A transporter with 95% on-time and 2% loss is worth a premium; one with 70% on-time is costing you more than the rate difference.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ul>
<li> Precool produce within 24 hours of harvest; measure pulp temperature, not air temperature.</li>
<li> Use ventilated, stackable crates; never overfill; never stack above shoulder height without a spotter.</li>
<li> Record harvest date and sell-by date on every pallet; operate FEFO, not FIFO.</li>
<li> Verify reefer setpoint against commodity requirement before every trip; log it.</li>
<li> Place a data logger in every refrigerated consignment and review on arrival.</li>
<li> Confirm backup power for cold storage; test weekly.</li>
<li> Reconcile physical stock to records weekly; investigate any variance above 2%.</li>
<li> Review transporter on-time and damage KPIs monthly; renegotiate or replace below threshold.</li>
</ul>
