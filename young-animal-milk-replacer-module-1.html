1. Why Young Animal Rearing Is a Business, Not a Chore

Young animal rearing is often treated as a side activity on African smallholder farms, but when done with commercial discipline it becomes one of the highest-return enterprises available. A single dairy calf reared to six months can add 80,000 to 150,000 KES in lifetime milk value if it is a heifer, while a well-fed piglet reaching 90 kg in five months can return 25,000 to 40,000 NGN above feed cost in Nigeria. The difference between profit and loss is almost always the first eight weeks of life. This lesson sets the economic frame for the entire module. The core principle is simple: colostrum and milk replacer are not costs, they are capital investments with measurable payback. FAO livestock sector studies repeatedly show that pre-weaning mortality above 15 percent erases the entire margin of a smallholder herd. Teagasc Ireland data from dairy calf-to-beef systems show that calves receiving 6 litres of colostrum in the first 24 hours have a 3.2 times lower risk of mortality and reach slaughter weight 28 days earlier than calves receiving less than 2 litres. That 28-day advantage is worth roughly 12,000 KES per calf in reduced feed and labour. The same biology applies in Kenya, Uganda, and Tanzania. For piglets, NAP (National Academies Press) Nutrient Requirements of Swine states that piglets weaned at 21 days need 200 to 250 g of high-quality creep feed per day by day 28, and that each 100 g of additional weaning weight is worth about 1.5 kg of final carcass weight. At a farmgate price of 1,800 NGN per kg live weight, that is 2,700 NGN per piglet of additional value from a feeding decision made in week three. Goat kids and lambs follow similar economics. A Boer cross kid receiving 300 ml of milk replacer twice daily for 42 days costs roughly 1,200 KES in powder at Kenyan retail prices of 1,100 KES per kg, and reaches 12 kg at weaning instead of 8 kg. The extra 4 kg at a market price of 450 KES per kg live weight returns 1,800 KES, a 50 percent return on the milk replacer spend. Puppies and kittens are a different market but the same logic. Royal Canin breeder guidance and university extension publications from Cornell and Texas A&M show that orphaned puppies fed a commercial bitch milk replacer at 15 to 20 percent of body weight per day in divided feeds have 90 percent survival to weaning, versus 40 percent for puppies fed diluted cow milk. In urban Africa, a healthy puppy sells for 15,000 to 60,000 KES depending on breed, so a 50 percent survival improvement is worth tens of thousands of shillings per litter. The common mistakes that destroy these returns are consistent across species. First, feeding cow milk to non-bovine young. Cow milk has 3.5 percent fat and 3.3 percent protein, while sow milk has 7 percent fat and 5.5 percent protein, and bitch milk has 9 percent fat and 7 percent protein. Diluting or substituting cow milk causes diarrhoea, bloat, and stunted growth. Second, mixing milk replacer with too little powder. Denkavit and MaxCare NZ both specify 125 to 150 g of powder per litre of water for calves, and 200 g per litre for piglets. Farmers who use 100 g per litre are effectively feeding water and then wonder why animals are thin. Third, feeding cold milk. The correct feeding temperature for calves, kids, and lambs is 38 to 39 degrees Celsius, and for piglets 37 to 39 degrees Celsius. Milk below 30 degrees Celsius slows abomasal clotting and causes scour. Fourth, skipping colostrum. No milk replacer on the market replaces colostrum. FAO and Teagasc both state that the first feed must be colostrum within two hours of birth, at 10 percent of body weight for calves and 15 percent for piglets. Fifth, buying cheap unbranded powder. In Nairobi, a 25 kg bag of reputable calf milk replacer costs 27,000 to 32,000 KES, while a cheap bag costs 18,000 KES. The cheap bag often contains 18 percent protein instead of 22 to 24 percent, and vegetable protein instead of dairy protein. The saving of 10,000 KES per bag is lost within two weeks through slower growth and higher veterinary costs. The business model for young animal rearing rests on four numbers: purchase or production cost of the young animal, cost of milk replacer and supplements to weaning, mortality rate, and sale or lifetime value of the survivor. If mortality is above 10 percent, fix that first. If daily gain is below target, fix the powder concentration and temperature. If cost per kg gain is above the market price of live weight, change the replacer or the feeding plan. This module will give you the specific measurements for each species. The next four lessons cover colostrum and transition, milk replacer selection and mixing, supplement feeding and weaning, and record keeping and cost control. Treat each animal as a business unit with a budget, and the enterprise will pay.

2. Colostrum and the First 48 Hours: The Non-Negotiable Foundation

The first 48 hours of a young animal's life determine whether it will be a profitable unit or a loss. Colostrum, the first milk, is not optional and cannot be replaced by any commercial product. It contains immunoglobulins, primarily IgG, that the newborn gut can absorb intact only during the first 24 hours, with absorption dropping sharply after 12 hours. FAO animal production and health guidelines state that calves need 10 percent of body weight in colostrum within the first six hours, and another 5 percent within 12 hours. For a 35 kg calf, that is 3.5 litres in the first feed and 1.75 litres in the second. For piglets, the target is 200 to 250 ml per piglet in the first 24 hours, which is best achieved by split-suckling or bottle feeding if the sow has more than 12 piglets. For goat kids and lambs, the target is 10 to 15 percent of body weight in the first 24 hours, so a 3 kg kid needs 300 to 450 ml, given in four to six small feeds. Puppies and kittens need 10 to 15 percent of body weight in the first 24 hours, which for a 300 g puppy is 30 to 45 ml, fed every two hours. The quality of colostrum matters as much as the quantity. Teagasc Ireland recommends testing colostrum with a Brix refractometer. A reading of 22 percent or higher indicates good quality, 18 to 21 percent is fair, and below 18 percent is poor and should be supplemented with frozen or artificial colostrum. In Africa, many smallholders do not have a refractometer, so the practical rule is to use the first milking from a healthy dam within two hours, and to pool colostrum from several dams if one is weak. Colostrum can be frozen in 500 ml bottles for up to six months and thawed in warm water at 40 degrees Celsius, never in a microwave, which destroys antibodies. The common mistakes in the first 48 hours are severe. First, delaying the first feed. A calf that receives colostrum at 12 hours has absorbed only about 30 percent of the antibodies compared to a calf fed at two hours. Second, feeding too little. A 35 kg calf given 1 litre of colostrum is effectively unvaccinated. Third, feeding colostrum from a dam with mastitis or after a difficult birth with retained placenta. Fourth, using cow colostrum for piglets or puppies. Species-specific antibodies are required. For piglets, sow colostrum is essential; if the sow dies, use frozen sow colostrum or a commercial porcine colostrum replacer such as those from Denkavit or MaxCare NZ. For puppies and kittens, Royal Canin and university extension guidance from Cornell recommend a commercial bitch or queen milk replacer if maternal colostrum is unavailable, but note that these replacers provide nutrition, not passive immunity, so survival is lower. After the first 24 hours, the gut closes and the animal must produce its own antibodies. This is why vaccination and hygiene become critical from day two. The transition from colostrum to milk replacer should be gradual. For calves, feed colostrum for the first three days, then transition to milk replacer over two days by mixing 75 percent colostrum with 25 percent replacer on day four, 50:50 on day five, and 100 percent replacer on day six. For piglets, keep them on the sow for at least 21 days if possible, and offer creep feed from day seven. If the sow is not available, use a piglet milk replacer at 200 g powder per litre of water, fed at 37 to 39 degrees Celsius, at 200 to 250 ml per piglet per day in four feeds. For goat kids and lambs, feed colostrum for two days, then transition to a kid or lamb milk replacer at 150 g per litre, fed at 38 to 39 degrees Celsius, at 10 to 15 percent of body weight per day. For puppies and kittens, feed colostrum or commercial replacer every two hours for the first week, then every three to four hours. The temperature of the environment is also critical. Newborn calves need a dry, draught-free pen at 15 to 20 degrees Celsius. Piglets need a creep area at 30 to 32 degrees Celsius in the first week, dropping to 26 degrees Celsius by week three. Kids and lambs need 20 to 25 degrees Celsius. Puppies and kittens need 29 to 32 degrees Celsius in the first week, 26 to 29 degrees Celsius in weeks two to four. Cold stress increases mortality and reduces colostrum absorption. The cost of getting the first 48 hours right is minimal. A 500 ml bottle of frozen colostrum costs nothing if saved from the dam. A Brix refractometer costs 8,000 to 15,000 KES and pays for itself after preventing one calf death. A 25 kg bag of colostrum replacer costs 35,000 to 45,000 KES and is used only for emergencies. The cost of getting it wrong is a dead animal worth 15,000 to 150,000 KES, plus the feed and labour already invested. The rule is simple: if the young animal has not received adequate colostrum in the first six hours, treat it as a high-risk patient and consult a veterinarian immediately. Do not wait for signs of weakness. By then, it is often too late.

3. Choosing and Mixing Milk Replacers: Species, Powder, and Temperature

Milk replacer is the engine of young animal rearing. Choosing the wrong product or mixing it incorrectly wastes money and kills animals. This lesson gives the specific numbers for each species, based on FAO, Teagasc, Denkavit, MaxCare NZ, NAP, and Royal Canin guidance. Start with species-specific formulation. Calf milk replacer should contain 22 to 24 percent crude protein, 18 to 20 percent fat, and less than 0.1 percent fibre. The protein should be at least 80 percent dairy protein, ideally skim milk or whey. Vegetable proteins such as soy isolate can be used but reduce growth by 10 to 15 percent. Denkavit and MaxCare NZ both specify 125 to 150 g of powder per litre of water for calves. A 40 kg calf needs 4 to 5 litres of mixed milk per day in two feeds, which is 10 to 12.5 percent of body weight. Mix at 40 to 45 degrees Celsius water, then feed at 38 to 39 degrees Celsius. Never mix with hot water above 50 degrees Celsius, which denatures proteins, and never feed below 35 degrees Celsius, which causes abomasal stasis. Piglet milk replacer should contain 24 to 28 percent crude protein, 18 to 22 percent fat, and be based on milk proteins. The mixing rate is 200 g of powder per litre of water, fed at 37 to 39 degrees Celsius. A 2 kg piglet needs 400 to 500 ml per day in four to six feeds, which is 20 to 25 percent of body weight. By week three, reduce to 300 ml per day and introduce creep feed. NAP Nutrient Requirements of Swine states that piglets on milk replacer should gain 200 to 250 g per day in the first three weeks. Goat kid and lamb milk replacer should contain 20 to 24 percent crude protein, 20 to 25 percent fat, and be based on milk proteins. The mixing rate is 150 g per litre of water, fed at 38 to 39 degrees Celsius. A 3 kg kid needs 450 to 600 ml per day in three to four feeds, which is 15 to 20 percent of body weight. By week four, reduce to two feeds and offer creep feed. Puppy and kitten milk replacers are highly specialised. Royal Canin and Cornell University extension guidance specify that bitch milk replacer should contain 30 to 35 percent crude protein, 20 to 25 percent fat, and be mixed at 150 to 200 g per litre. Puppies need 15 to 20 percent of body weight per day in four to six feeds, fed at 37 to 38 degrees Celsius. Kittens need 20 to 25 percent of body weight per day in four to six feeds. Never feed cow milk to puppies or kittens. Cow milk has 3.5 percent fat and 3.3 percent protein, while bitch milk has 9 percent fat and 7 percent protein. Diluting cow milk with water makes it worse. The common mistakes in mixing are consistent. First, using too little powder. A farmer who uses 100 g per litre for calves is feeding 20 percent less nutrition than required, and the calf will lose weight. Second, using too much powder. A farmer who uses 200 g per litre for calves causes osmotic diarrhoea and dehydration. Third, mixing with cold water. Powder does not dissolve properly below 30 degrees Celsius, and lumps can cause choking. Fourth, mixing with hot water above 50 degrees Celsius. This destroys vitamins and denatures proteins. Fifth, mixing in advance. Mixed milk replacer should be fed within 30 minutes. If left at room temperature for more than two hours, bacteria multiply rapidly and cause scour. Sixth, using dirty water. Always use clean, potable water. In Africa, borehole or rainwater is best. If using tap water, let it stand for 30 minutes to release chlorine. The cost of milk replacer varies by country. In Kenya, a 25 kg bag of calf milk replacer costs 27,000 to 32,000 KES from suppliers such as Unga, Bidco, or Denkavit distributors. In Nigeria, a 25 kg bag costs 85,000 to 120,000 NGN from suppliers such as Vital Feed or Premier Feed. In South Africa, a 25 kg bag costs 1,800 to 2,400 ZAR from suppliers such as Meadow Feeds or Epol. Piglet milk replacer is more expensive, at 35,000 to 45,000 KES per 25 kg in Kenya, 120,000 to 160,000 NGN in Nigeria, and 2,500 to 3,200 ZAR in South Africa. Goat and lamb milk replacer is similar to calf replacer. Puppy and kitten milk replacer is sold in small tins, at 2,500 to 4,000 KES per 500 g in Kenya, 8,000 to 12,000 NGN in Nigeria, and 250 to 400 ZAR in South Africa. The cost per animal per day is the key number. A calf on 5 litres per day at 150 g per litre uses 750 g of powder per day, which at 1,200 KES per kg is 900 KES per day. Over 60 days, that is 54,000 KES. A piglet on 400 ml per day at 200 g per litre uses 80 g per day, which at 1,600 KES per kg is 128 KES per day. Over 21 days, that is 2,688 KES. A kid on 500 ml per day at 150 g per litre uses 75 g per day, which at 1,100 KES per kg is 82.5 KES per day. Over 42 days, that is 3,465 KES. These costs must be compared to the value of the animal at weaning. If the cost of milk replacer exceeds 30 percent of the weaning value, the enterprise is not profitable. For a calf worth 150,000 KES at six months, 54,000 KES is 36 percent, which is high but acceptable if mortality is below 5 percent. For a piglet worth 25,000 NGN at 90 kg, 2,688 NGN is 11 percent, which is excellent. For a kid worth 12,000 KES at 12 kg, 3,465 KES is 29 percent, which is acceptable. The final rule is to buy from a reputable supplier and check the label. The label must state the protein and fat percentages, the mixing rate, and the expiry date. If the label is missing or the powder smells rancid, do not buy it. Store powder in a cool, dry place, and use within 30 days of opening. Never use mouldy powder. The health of the young animal depends on the quality of the powder and the accuracy of the mixing. Measure with a scale, not a cup, because powder density varies. A 250 ml cup of calf milk replacer may weigh 100 g or 150 g depending on the brand. Use a digital kitchen scale, which costs 2,000 to 4,000 KES, and measure every feed. This single habit prevents most cases of scour and stunted growth.

4. Supplement Feeding, Weaning, and Transition to Solid Feed

Milk replacer alone does not raise a profitable animal. The transition to solid feed, called weaning, determines whether the animal continues to grow or stalls. This lesson covers supplement feeding, weaning age, and the specific measurements for each species, based on FAO, Teagasc, NAP, and university extension guidance. Start with the principle of rumen or gut development. Calves, goat kids, and lambs are ruminants. Their rumen is undeveloped at birth and must be stimulated by solid feed, especially grain, to grow papillae and absorb volatile fatty acids. Piglets, puppies, and kittens are monogastric. Their digestive systems mature with age and require highly digestible feed. The weaning age differs by species. Calves are typically weaned at 8 to 12 weeks, when they consume at least 1 kg of calf starter per day. Piglets are weaned at 21 to 28 days in commercial systems, but smallholders in Africa often wean at 35 to 42 days to reduce stress. Goat kids and lambs are weaned at 6 to 8 weeks, when they consume 200 to 300 g of creep feed per day. Puppies are weaned at 6 to 8 weeks, and kittens at 8 to 10 weeks. The supplement feeding plan for calves starts at day 3 with a handful of calf starter. Calf starter should contain 18 to 20 percent crude protein, 3 to 5 percent fat, and 15 to 20 percent fibre. Teagasc Ireland recommends feeding 100 g per day at week one, increasing to 500 g per day at week four, and 1 kg per day at week eight. Fresh water must be available at all times from day 3. Hay or straw should be offered from week two to stimulate rumination. The common mistake is feeding too much hay and too little grain. Hay fills the rumen but does not grow papillae. Grain does. A calf that eats 1 kg of starter per day for three consecutive days is ready to wean. For piglets, creep feed should be offered from day 7. Creep feed should contain 20 to 22 percent crude protein, 6 to 8 percent fat, and be highly digestible, with cooked cereals and milk proteins. NAP Nutrient Requirements of Swine states that piglets should consume 200 to 250 g of creep feed per day by day 28. At weaning, piglets should weigh 7 to 9 kg. The common mistake is weaning too early without adequate creep feed. A piglet weaned at 21 days with no creep feed will lose weight for 5 to 7 days and may develop diarrhoea. For goat kids and lambs, creep feed should be offered from day 7. Creep feed should contain 16 to 18 percent crude protein and be based on ground maize, soybean meal, and molasses. Kids and lambs should consume 200 to 300 g per day by week six. Wean at 6 to 8 weeks if they reach 10 to 12 kg. The common mistake is feeding too much milk and too little creep feed. A kid that drinks 1 litre of milk per day will not eat creep feed and will be stunted at weaning. For puppies and kittens, weaning starts at 3 to 4 weeks with a gruel made of milk replacer and puppy or kitten food. Royal Canin and Cornell University extension guidance recommend mixing 1 part food with 3 parts warm milk replacer at 38 degrees Celsius, and offering it four times per day. By week six, puppies should eat dry food soaked in water. By week eight, they should eat dry food only. Kittens follow a similar schedule but wean at 8 to 10 weeks. The common mistake is leaving puppies on milk replacer too long. After week six, milk replacer does not provide enough iron or energy for a growing puppy. The cost of supplement feed is lower than milk replacer but still significant. Calf starter costs 2,500 to 3,500 KES per 50 kg bag in Kenya, 8,000 to 12,000 NGN per 50 kg in Nigeria, and 350 to 450 ZAR per 50 kg in South Africa. Piglet creep feed costs 3,500 to 4,500 KES per 50 kg in Kenya, 12,000 to 16,000 NGN in Nigeria, and 450 to 550 ZAR in South Africa. Kid and lamb creep feed costs 2,800 to 3,800 KES per 50 kg in Kenya, 9,000 to 13,000 NGN in Nigeria, and 380 to 480 ZAR in South Africa. Puppy and kitten food costs 4,000 to 8,000 KES per 10 kg in Kenya, 15,000 to 25,000 NGN in Nigeria, and 500 to 900 ZAR per 10 kg in South Africa. The total cost of rearing to weaning includes milk replacer, supplement feed, veterinary costs, and labour. For a calf, the total is 60,000 to 80,000 KES. For a piglet, 4,000 to 6,000 KES. For a kid, 5,000 to 7,000 KES. For a puppy, 15,000 to 25,000 KES. These costs must be compared to the value of the weaned animal. A weaned calf is worth 40,000 to 60,000 KES, a weaned piglet 8,000 to 12,000 KES, a weaned kid 8,000 to 12,000 KES, and a weaned puppy 20,000 to 60,000 KES. The margin is positive if mortality is below 10 percent and growth is on target. The final rule is to weigh animals weekly. A digital hanging scale costs 5,000 to 10,000 KES and is essential. If a calf is not gaining 700 to 800 g per day, check the milk replacer concentration, the temperature, and the starter intake. If a piglet is not gaining 200 to 250 g per day, check the creep feed and the water. If a kid is not gaining 150 to 200 g per day, check the milk replacer and the creep feed. Weighing turns rearing from guesswork into a business.

5. Record Keeping, Cost Control, and Scaling the Enterprise

Young animal rearing becomes a business only when records are kept and costs are controlled. This lesson covers the specific records needed, the key performance indicators, and the steps to scale from a few animals to a commercial unit, based on FAO, Teagasc, NAP, and university extension guidance. Start with the records. Every animal should have a record card with birth date, dam, birth weight, colostrum intake, milk replacer brand and daily volume, supplement feed intake, weekly weight, vaccinations, treatments, and weaning date. In Africa, a simple exercise book works, but a smartphone app such as Farm Records or Livestock Manager is better because it calculates averages and alerts you to problems. The record card costs nothing but saves thousands of shillings by identifying slow-growing animals early. The key performance indicators for young animal rearing are mortality rate, average daily gain, feed conversion ratio, and cost per kg gain. Mortality rate should be below 5 percent for calves, below 8 percent for piglets, below 10 percent for kids and lambs, and below 10 percent for puppies and kittens. If mortality is above these targets, investigate colostrum, hygiene, temperature, and milk replacer quality in that order. Average daily gain should be 700 to 800 g per day for calves, 200 to 250 g per day for piglets, 150 to 200 g per day for kids and lambs, and 50 to 100 g per day for puppies and kittens. If gain is below target, check the powder concentration and the feeding temperature. Feed conversion ratio should be 1.5 to 2.0 kg of milk replacer powder per kg of gain for calves, 1.2 to 1.5 for piglets, 1.5 to 2.0 for kids and lambs, and 1.0 to 1.5 for puppies and kittens. If the ratio is above target, the animal is wasting feed, usually because of cold stress or disease. Cost per kg gain should be below the market price of live weight. For calves, the cost per kg gain is 600 to 800 KES. For piglets, 400 to 600 KES. For kids, 500 to 700 KES. For puppies, 800 to 1,200 KES. If the cost is above these figures, change the milk replacer brand or reduce waste. The common mistakes in record keeping are failing to record daily feed intake, failing to weigh animals weekly, and failing to calculate cost per animal. A farmer who does not know the cost per animal cannot know if the enterprise is profitable. A farmer who does not weigh animals cannot know if growth is on target. A farmer who does not record mortality cannot know if there is a disease problem. The solution is a simple routine: weigh every animal on the same day each week, record the weight and the feed consumed, and calculate the average daily gain and the cost per kg gain. This takes 30 minutes per week for 20 animals. The cost of the scale and the record book is less than 15,000 KES, which is less than the value of one calf. Scaling the enterprise requires three things: a reliable source of young animals, a reliable source of milk replacer and supplement feed, and a reliable market for the weaned animals. For calves, the source is often the farmer's own dairy herd or a local dairy cooperative. For piglets, the source is a breeding sow or a local pig multiplier. For kids and lambs, the source is a breeding doe or ewe. For puppies and kittens, the source is a breeding bitch or queen. The milk replacer source should be a reputable supplier with consistent quality. In Kenya, Unga, Bidco, and Denkavit distributors are reliable. In Nigeria, Vital Feed and Premier Feed are reliable. In South Africa, Meadow Feeds and Epol are reliable. The market for weaned animals is often local, but can be expanded to urban areas or export markets. A weaned dairy calf can be sold to a dairy farmer for 40,000 to 60,000 KES. A weaned piglet can be sold to a pig fattening unit for 8,000 to 12,000 KES. A weaned kid can be sold to a meat goat producer for 8,000 to 12,000 KES. A weaned puppy can be sold to a pet owner for 20,000 to 60,000 KES. The final step in scaling is to calculate the return on investment. If a calf costs 60,000 KES to rear to weaning and sells for 50,000 KES, the enterprise is losing money. If it costs 40,000 KES and sells for 50,000 KES, the margin is 10,000 KES, or 25 percent. If it costs 30,000 KES and sells for 50,000 KES, the margin is 20,000 KES, or 67 percent. The target is a margin of at least 30 percent. To achieve this, keep mortality below 5 percent, keep average daily gain above target, keep feed conversion ratio below target, and keep cost per kg gain below the market price. The final rule is to treat every animal as a business unit. If an animal is not profitable, identify the reason and fix it. If the reason is disease, improve hygiene and vaccination. If the reason is feed, change the milk replacer or the supplement. If the reason is genetics, change the source of young animals. Young animal rearing is not a charity. It is a business that rewards discipline, measurement, and consistency. The farmers who keep records and control costs are the ones who make money. The farmers who guess are the ones who lose money. This module has given you the specific measurements and the business framework. The next step is to apply them on your farm, one animal at a time.
