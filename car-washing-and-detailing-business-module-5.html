1. Interior Detailing: Vacuuming, Shampooing, and Leather Care

Start by removing all loose items: floor mats, coins, papers, and anything in the glovebox or door pockets. Use a 5-gallon bucket to collect trash. Vacuum thoroughly with a wet/dry shop vac (at least 1000 watts). Use a crevice tool for seat rails, under seats, and between cushions. For carpets, use a stiff brush to loosen dirt before vacuuming. If you have a steam cleaner, use it on stainsâotherwise, mix 1 part laundry detergent with 10 parts water in a spray bottle. Spray lightly, scrub with a soft brush, then wipe with a clean microfiber cloth. For fabric seats, use a foam cleaner (like a 1:10 mix of dish soap and water). Apply, agitate, and blot dry. Avoid soakingâtoo much water causes mildew, especially in humid African climates.

For leather seats, first vacuum and wipe with a damp cloth. Use a pH-neutral leather cleaner (or a mix of 1 part white vinegar to 10 parts water). Apply with a soft sponge, then wipe dry. Follow with a leather conditioner (like shea butter-based products) to prevent cracking from sun damage. In hot, dry regions, condition monthly.

Plastic and vinyl dashboards: clean with a microfiber cloth and a mild all-purpose cleaner (1:10 dilution). Avoid silicone-based dressings that attract dust. Use a water-based dressing for a matte finish. For air vents, use a soft paintbrush or compressed air. Glass: use a 1:1 mix of white vinegar and waterâno streaks. Dry with a clean microfiber cloth.

Finally, use a detailing brush to clean around buttons and crevices. Finish by wiping all surfaces with a clean, damp cloth. Allow everything to dry completely before closing doors. This process takes 1.5 to 3 hours for a standard sedan. Charge accordingly: in many African cities, interior detailing alone can fetch $20â$50.

2. Exterior Detailing: Claying, Polishing, and Waxing

After washing and drying the car, inspect the paint with your handâif it feels rough like sandpaper, it needs claying. Use a clay bar kit (around $15â$25) with a lubricant (quick detailer or soapy water). Work in 2x2 foot sections. Spray lubricant, then rub the clay bar back and forth gently. You'll feel it grab at first, then glide smoothly. Wipe with a microfiber cloth. Fold the clay when it gets dirty. Never drop itâif you do, throw it away. Claying removes embedded contaminants that washing can't.

Next, polishing. Use a dual-action polisher (like a DA polisher, $80â$150) with a foam polishing pad and a fine polish. Apply 3â4 pea-sized drops on the pad. Work at low speed (setting 2â3) to spread, then medium speed (4â5) with overlapping passes. Keep the polisher moving. Don't press hardâlet the machine do the work. Polish removes light swirls and oxidation. For deeper scratches, use a compound first, then polish. Always test on a small area.

After polishing, wipe off residue with a microfiber cloth. Then apply wax. Use a paste wax (like carnauba) or a liquid wax. Apply a thin, even layer with a foam applicator. Let it haze for 5â10 minutes (not in direct sun). Buff off with a clean microfiber cloth. Wax protects paint from UV, rain, and dust. In dusty African conditions, wax monthly. For better durability, use a paint sealant (synthetic polymer) that lasts 3â6 months.

Finally, dress tires and trim. Use a water-based tire dressing for a satin finish. Avoid silicone-based products that sling. For plastic trim, use a trim restorer. This full exterior detail takes 3â5 hours. Charge $50â$150 depending on vehicle size and condition.

3. Paint Correction and Swirl Removal

Swirl marks are fine scratches caused by improper washing, drying, or wiping. They look like spider webs under sunlight. To remove them, you need a machine polisherâdoing it by hand is nearly impossible. Use a dual-action (DA) polisher with a foam cutting pad and a medium-cut compound. Start with a test spot: apply 3â4 dots of compound on the pad, spread at low speed, then work at speed 4â5 with moderate pressure. Move the polisher slowly (1â2 inches per second) in overlapping passesâhorizontal, then vertical. Wipe residue and inspect under a bright light. If swirls remain, repeat. For deeper scratches, use a microfiber cutting pad and a heavier compound.

After compounding, the paint may look hazy. That's normalâit's micro-marring. Now polish with a finishing pad and a fine polish. Use light pressure and speed 3â4. This restores gloss. Wipe with a microfiber cloth. Always use a clean pad for each step. Clean pads on the fly with a pad brush.

Paint correction is time-consuming: a full car can take 6â12 hours. Charge accordinglyâ$100â$300 or more. In Africa, many cars have oxidized paint due to intense sun. For oxidation, use a wool pad and a compound, but be carefulâwool cuts fast. Follow with foam pads.

Safety: wear a mask when compounding (dust), and work in a shaded, well-ventilated area. Keep the polisher moving to avoid burning the paint. If you feel the panel getting hot, stop. After correction, apply a wax or sealant to protect the fresh paint. This is a high-value skillâfew detailers in Africa offer true paint correction. Market it as a premium service.

4. Headlight Restoration and Trim Care

Yellow, cloudy headlights reduce visibility and fail inspections. Restoration is a quick, profitable add-on. Start by cleaning the headlight with soap and water. Tape around the edges with painter's tape to protect paint. Wet sand with 800-grit sandpaper, then 1500, then 2000. Keep the surface wetâuse a spray bottle. Sand in one direction, then switch 90 degrees for the next grit. Wipe dry. The lens will look dullâthat's normal. Now polish with a DA polisher and a plastic polish, or by hand with a microfiber pad and compound. Work until clear. Finally, apply a UV sealant (like a headlight clear coat) or a ceramic coating. Without UV protection, they'll yellow again in 3â6 months. Charge $20â$40 per pair.

Trim care: exterior plastic trim (bumpers, mirrors, door handles) fades to grey. Clean with an all-purpose cleaner and a brush. For restoration, use a heat gun carefullyâwave it over the plastic until it darkens (don't hold in one spot). This brings back oils. Alternatively, use a trim restorer (solution or dye). Apply with a foam applicator. For rubber seals, use a silicone-based conditioner to prevent cracking. In dusty conditions, trim collects dirtâclean monthly.

Interior trim: clean with a damp microfiber cloth. For scratches on piano black or silver trim, use a plastic polish. Avoid abrasive cleaners. For fabric pillars, use a foam cleaner and a soft brush. Always test on a hidden area.

Headlight restoration and trim care are low-cost, high-margin services. A basic kit costs under $30 and can serve 10+ cars. Offer as a package with a wash or interior detail.

5. Engine Bay Cleaning and Degreasing

A clean engine bay makes maintenance easier and impresses customers. But water and electronics don't mix. Start with a cold engineânever clean a hot engine. Cover the alternator, fuse box, and air intake with plastic bags or foil. Remove loose debris with a brush or compressed air. Spray a degreaser (like a 1:1 mix of dish soap and water, or a dedicated engine degreaser) on greasy areas. Let it sit 5â10 minutes. Agitate with a parts brush or a soft brush. For heavy grease, use a steam cleaner if available.

Rinse with a gentle streamânot a pressure washer at close range. Use a hose with a spray nozzle, keeping water away from electrical connectors. Start from the top and work down. Avoid spraying directly into the alternator or fuse box. After rinsing, remove the plastic covers. Start the engine and let it idle for 10â15 minutes to dry everything. The heat evaporates remaining water. Then apply a water-based engine dressing (like a silicone-free protectant) to plastic and rubber parts. Wipe off excess. Don't use oil-based dressingsâthey attract dust and can cause fires.

Safety: wear gloves and eye protection. Work outdoors or in a well-ventilated area. Dispose of degreaser runoff properlyâdon't let it flow into drains. In Africa, many cars have dusty, oily engine bays. Charge $15â$30 for engine cleaning. It's a quick service (30â45 minutes) that adds value. Always check for leaks or damaged hoses while cleaningâyou might spot a problem the owner missed. Offer this as an add-on to a full detail.

6. Ceramic Coating and Paint Protection Application

Ceramic coating is a liquid polymer (SiO2-based) that bonds to paint, providing years of protection and a hydrophobic surface. It's a premium serviceâcharge $200â$800 depending on the coating and vehicle size. Preparation is critical: the paint must be flawless. Wash, clay, and polish first. Any swirls will be locked in. Then wipe the paint with an IPA (isopropyl alcohol) solution (1:1 with water) to remove oils. Work in a shaded, dust-free area.

Apply the coating using a applicator block with a suede cloth. Wrap the suede around the block. Shake the bottle. Apply 3â4 drops on the suede. Work in 2x2 foot sections. Apply in overlapping passesâhorizontal, then vertical. You'll see a rainbow sheen (flashing). Let it sit 1â5 minutes depending on temperature (shorter in heat). Buff off with a clean, plush microfiber towel. Use a separate towel for final wipe. Work panel by panel. Don't let it dry too longâit becomes hard to remove. If it does, use a polish to remove and start over.

After coating, let it cure for 24 hours (no water). Full cure takes 7 days. Avoid washing for 7 days. For maintenance, use a pH-neutral shampoo. Apply a ceramic boost spray every 3â6 months. In dusty Africa, ceramic coating reduces wash frequency and makes cleaning easier. It also protects against UV and bird droppings.

For paint protection film (PPF), it's a clear urethane film applied to high-impact areas (bumper, hood, mirrors). It's more expensive ($500â$2000) and requires skill. Start with ceramic coatingâit's easier and profitable. Always wear gloves. Store coating in a cool place. Offer a warranty (1â5 years) based on the product. 7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Detailing Techniques for African Car Wash & Detailing Businesses

This practical reference consolidates the core technical knowledge a detailing technician needs to deliver professional interior and exterior work in an African context. It is written for business owners and lead technicians who must balance quality, cost, water and power availability, and customer expectations. Each section links technique to revenue, repeat business, and risk management.

Why This Matters for African Detailing Businesses

In most African markets, vehicle owners face harsh conditions: high UV exposure, dusty harmattan or dry-season winds, unpaved roads, and frequent rain in coastal cities. These conditions accelerate paint oxidation, interior fabric soiling, and trim fading. A detailing business that understands these pressures can sell protection packages rather than one-off washes, increasing average ticket size and customer retention.

Water and power reliability vary widely. A business in Nairobi, Lagos, Accra, or Kampala may face intermittent supply, so techniques must be water-efficient and equipment choices must match available power. The practical guidance below assumes a small to mid-sized operation with access to a pressure washer, wet/dry vacuum, dual-action polisher, and basic chemicals.

Interior Detailing: Vacuuming, Shampooing, and Leather Care

Interior work is the highest-margin service in most African detailing shops because labour is the main cost and chemical usage is low. Start with a full dry vacuum: carpets, seats, boot, and crevices. Use a crevice tool and a soft brush attachment for vents and stitching. In dusty regions, vacuum before any liquid touches the surface, otherwise mud forms and stains set.

For fabric shampooing, dilute a pH-neutral upholstery cleaner according to the manufacturer's ratio. Agitate with a soft brush, extract with a wet/dry vacuum, and repeat for heavy soiling. Avoid over-wetting, which causes mildew in humid coastal cities. For leather, use a dedicated pH-balanced leather cleaner, then apply a conditioner with UV inhibitors. Leather in African markets often suffers from cracking due to heat, so conditioning is a sellable protection step, not an optional extra.

Exterior Detailing: Claying, Polishing, and Waxing

After washing and drying, clay the paint to remove bonded contaminants. Use a clay bar or clay mitt with a lubricant spray. Fold and knead the clay frequently; if you drop it, discard it. Claying leaves the surface smooth and ready for polishing. Skipping claying means polishing pads clog and swirl removal becomes inconsistent.

Polishing removes light defects and restores gloss. Use a dual-action polisher with a foam pad and a polish matched to paint hardness. African vehicles often have harder clear coats due to UV exposure, so test a small area first. Follow with a wax or sealant. In high-UV markets, a synthetic sealant or ceramic spray offers longer protection than natural carnauba wax, which can melt in extreme heat.

Paint Correction and Swirl Removal

Paint correction is a multi-stage process: compound for deeper defects, polish for refinement, and a final jewelling step for maximum gloss. Swirl marks are fine circular scratches caused by improper washing and drying. They are removed by abrasion, not by filling. Use a dual-action polisher with a cutting pad and compound, then refine with a polishing pad and finishing polish. Always work in small sections, keep the pad clean, and use a paint depth gauge if available. In Africa, where repainting may be costly, correction preserves original paint and adds resale value.

Headlight Restoration and Trim Care

Oxidised headlights reduce night visibility and fail vehicle inspections in some countries. Wet-sand with 800, 1500, and 3000 grit, then polish and apply a UV-blocking clear coat or sealant. Without UV protection, yellowing returns within months in high-sun regions. For exterior trim, clean with an all-purpose cleaner, then apply a trim restorer or ceramic trim coating. Faded black plastic is common on older vehicles and is a quick, visible improvement that customers notice immediately.

Engine Bay Cleaning and Degreasing

Engine bay cleaning is a high-value add-on but carries risk. Cover sensitive electrical components, alternator, and air intake before spraying. Use a degreaser, agitate with brushes, and rinse with low pressure. Avoid high-pressure water near electrical connectors. After cleaning, apply a plastic and rubber dressing. In dusty environments, engine bays accumulate fine dust that can become abrasive; regular cleaning supports vehicle longevity.

Ceramic Coating and Paint Protection Application

Ceramic coatings form a semi-permanent bond with the clear coat, providing hydrophobic properties, UV resistance, and easier cleaning. Application requires a perfectly prepared surface: wash, decontaminate, clay, polish, and wipe with a panel wipe. Apply in a controlled environment away from dust and direct sun. Cure time varies by product and humidity. In coastal African cities, humidity can affect curing, so follow the manufacturer's guidance. Ceramic coating is a premium service that can double or triple the price of a standard detail.

Always wear nitrile gloves, eye protection, and a respirator when using compounds, ceramic coatings, and degreasers. Ceramic coatings can cause severe skin and eye irritation. Never mix chemicals. Work in a well-ventilated area. Keep a fire extinguisher and first-aid kit on site. In areas with unreliable electricity, use RCD/earth-leakage protection for all polishers and pressure washers. Store chemicals away from direct sunlight and out of reach of children. Follow local regulations for waste water disposal; do not discharge detailing runoff into storm drains or water bodies.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Pre-inspect vehicle and record existing damage with photos before starting.
  2. Vacuum interior dry before applying any liquid to carpets or seats.
  3. Wash from top to bottom, use two-bucket method, and dry with clean microfibre.
  4. Clay the paint after washing and before polishing; discard dropped clay.
  5. Test polish on a small area to match pad and compound to paint hardness.
  6. Apply UV protection to headlights, trim, and leather after cleaning.
  7. Cover electrical components before engine bay cleaning; use low pressure.
  8. For ceramic coating, ensure surface is decontaminated, polished, and panel-wiped before application.
