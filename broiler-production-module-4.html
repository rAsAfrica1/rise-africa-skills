1. Why a Three-Phase Feeding Programme Matters for African Broiler Profit

Every naira, shilling, cedi or rand you spend on broiler feed is an investment that must return more than it costs. In most African broiler operations, feed accounts for 65 to 75 percent of total production cost. That single fact explains why the feeding programme is not a technical detail but the core of your business model. A broiler feeding programme divides the bird's life into phases, each with a different feed formulation matched to the bird's changing nutritional needs. The standard three-phase programme is Starter, Grower and Finisher. Some producers add a Pre-Starter for the first seven to ten days and a Withdrawal feed for the final five days before slaughter, but the three-phase structure remains the backbone of commercial broiler production across the continent. The logic is simple. A day-old chick needs highly digestible protein, high energy and a small particle size because its digestive system is immature. As the bird grows, its maintenance requirement rises but its feed conversion efficiency changes, so the diet must shift. In the finisher phase, the bird is depositing fat and muscle rapidly, and the goal is to maximise weight gain while keeping feed cost per kilogram of live weight as low as possible. If you feed a starter diet all the way to market, you waste money on expensive protein that the mature bird does not need. If you feed a finisher diet too early, you stunt growth and lose uniformity. The business case for phase feeding is straightforward. By matching nutrient density to age, you reduce feed cost per bird, improve feed conversion ratio, shorten the time to market weight and reduce the risk of metabolic disorders. In Africa, where feed raw materials such as maize, soybean meal and fishmeal are often imported or subject to seasonal price shocks, getting the phase transitions right can be the difference between profit and loss. Consider a typical 1,000-bird flock in Nigeria or Kenya. If a farmer uses a single all-purpose feed, the cost per bird might be 15 to 20 percent higher than a well-managed three-phase programme. On a 1,000-bird cycle, that is a significant margin. Multiply that across multiple cycles per year and the impact on farm profitability is enormous. The three-phase programme also supports cash flow planning. Starter feed is the most expensive per kilogram, but the bird eats the least during this phase. Grower feed is intermediate in cost and the bird eats more. Finisher feed is the cheapest per kilogram and the bird eats the most. Understanding this curve helps you budget and buy feed at the right time. Another critical reason for phase feeding is bird health. Starter diets are often medicated or supplemented with vitamins and minerals to support early immunity. Grower diets focus on skeletal development and lean tissue growth. Finisher diets may be medicated with a withdrawal period or formulated without antibiotics to meet market requirements. In many African markets, buyers and consumers are increasingly demanding antibiotic-free chicken, so the finisher phase is your opportunity to comply without compromising performance. The phase programme also interacts with environmental conditions. In hot climates, birds eat less to reduce heat stress, so nutrient density must be higher to compensate. In cold or rainy seasons, energy requirements rise. A flexible feeding programme allows you to adjust formulations and feed quantities based on the season, the breed and the market weight you are targeting. Finally, the feeding programme is the foundation of your record-keeping and decision-making. By tracking feed intake, body weight and feed conversion ratio at each phase, you can identify problems early. If birds are not hitting target weight at the end of the starter phase, you can adjust before the problem becomes expensive. If feed conversion is poor in the grower phase, you can investigate disease, ventilation or feed quality. This data-driven approach is what separates commercial producers from backyard operations. In summary, the three-phase feeding programme is not just a technical recommendation. It is a business strategy that reduces cost, improves efficiency and increases profit. In the African context, where margins are tight and feed prices volatile, mastering the starter, grower and finisher phases is essential for anyone serious about broiler production. The next four lessons will take you through each phase in detail, from formulation and feeding schedules to practical management tips for African conditions.

2. The Starter Phase: Giving Chicks the Best Start from Day One to Day 14

The starter phase covers the first 14 days of a broiler's life, although some producers extend it to 18 or 21 days depending on the breed and target market. This is the most critical period in the entire production cycle. A chick that gets a poor start will never fully recover. It will be smaller, less uniform and more susceptible to disease. In Africa, where day-old chicks are often transported long distances and exposed to heat or cold, the starter phase demands extra attention. The goal of the starter phase is to establish a strong immune system, develop the digestive tract and skeleton, and achieve rapid early growth. The starter diet is the most nutrient-dense feed the bird will receive. It typically contains 22 to 24 percent crude protein and 2,900 to 3,000 kilocalories of metabolisable energy per kilogram. It is also rich in vitamins, minerals, amino acids such as lysine and methionine, and often includes a coccidiostat or probiotic. The particle size is usually crumbles or mini-pellets, which are easier for small chicks to eat than mash or large pellets. In many African markets, starter feed is sold in 25 kg or 50 kg bags, and quality varies widely. As a producer, you must inspect feed for freshness, smell, mould and uniformity. Never buy feed that is damp, clumped or has an off smell. Mouldy feed can cause mycotoxin poisoning, which is a common and costly problem in tropical climates. Store feed in a cool, dry place, off the floor, on pallets, and use it within four to six weeks of manufacture. The feeding schedule for starter phase is straightforward but must be followed precisely. On arrival, give chicks access to clean water with glucose or electrolytes for the first few hours. Then offer starter feed ad libitum, meaning the birds always have feed available. Do not restrict feed during this phase. Chicks have small crops and need to eat frequently. Use shallow feeders or trays for the first three to five days so that even the weakest chicks can reach the feed. After that, switch to standard tube feeders and adjust the height so the feed level is at the level of the bird's back. A common mistake in African broiler farms is overcrowding during the starter phase. Chicks need space to move, eat and drink. Provide at least 10 to 12 chicks per square metre for the first two weeks, and ensure there are enough feeders and drinkers. A good rule is one feeder tray per 50 chicks for the first week, then one tube feeder per 50 to 60 birds. For drinkers, provide one bell drinker per 80 to 100 chicks or use nipple lines with one nipple per 10 to 12 birds. Water quality is just as important as feed quality. Clean the drinkers daily and provide fresh, cool water. In hot African conditions, water consumption can double, so check drinkers at least three times a day. Add vitamins and electrolytes to the water during the first three days and during any period of stress, such as vaccination or transport. The starter phase is also the time for vaccinations. Most African broiler programmes include Newcastle disease and infectious bronchitis vaccines at day 7 to 10, and sometimes Gumboro at day 14. Vaccination causes stress, so avoid handling birds roughly and ensure they have plenty of feed and water before and after. Weigh a sample of birds at day 7 and day 14 to track growth. A typical broiler should weigh around 180 to 200 grams at day 7 and 450 to 500 grams at day 14, depending on the breed. If your birds are below target, check feed quality, water access, temperature and disease. Temperature management is critical. Day-old chicks need a brooding temperature of 32 to 34 degrees Celsius, reduced by about 2 to 3 degrees per week until it reaches 22 to 24 degrees. In Africa, many farms use charcoal stoves, gas brooders or infrared bulbs. Whatever you use, watch the chicks' behaviour. If they huddle under the heat source, they are cold. If they spread out away from it, they are too hot. If they are evenly distributed and active, the temperature is right. Litter management also matters. Use clean, dry litter such as wood shavings, rice husks or chopped straw. Never use wet or mouldy litter. Stir the litter daily to prevent caking and ammonia build-up. Ammonia irritates the respiratory system and predisposes birds to disease. In the starter phase, keep litter depth at 5 to 8 centimetres and top it up as needed. Feed conversion ratio during the starter phase is not the main focus because the bird is small and growing rapidly. However, you should still monitor feed intake. A typical chick eats about 20 to 25 grams of feed per day in the first week and 50 to 60 grams per day in the second week. If intake is lower, investigate immediately. In summary, the starter phase is about giving chicks a strong foundation. Use high-quality starter feed, provide clean water and correct temperature, manage litter and ventilation, vaccinate on time and track growth. In the African context, where environmental stress and disease pressure are high, the starter phase is where you win or lose the flock. A well-managed starter phase leads to a uniform, healthy flock that will convert feed efficiently in the grower and finisher phases.

3. The Grower Phase: Building Frame and Muscle from Day 15 to Day 28

The grower phase runs from about day 15 to day 28, though some programmes extend it to day 35 depending on the target market weight. This is the phase where the bird builds its frame, develops its skeletal structure and starts to lay down muscle. The grower diet is less nutrient-dense than the starter diet but still supports rapid growth. A typical grower feed contains 19 to 21 percent crude protein and 2,800 to 2,900 kilocalories of metabolisable energy per kilogram. The particle size is usually pellets or coarse crumbles. The grower phase is a period of transition. The bird's digestive system is more mature, so it can handle lower-quality ingredients and larger particles. However, the bird is also more active and its maintenance requirement increases. The goal is to achieve steady, efficient growth without excessive fat deposition. In Africa, the grower phase often coincides with the hottest part of the year, so heat stress management becomes critical. The feeding schedule during the grower phase is still ad libitum, but you may need to adjust the timing of feed delivery. In hot climates, birds eat less during the hottest part of the day. Offer feed early in the morning and late in the evening when temperatures are cooler. This encourages intake and reduces heat stress. Some producers also use wet feeding, where feed is mixed with water to form a mash, which can improve intake in hot weather. However, wet feeding requires strict hygiene because wet feed spoils quickly in tropical heat. If you use wet feeding, prepare only enough for one meal and clean the feeders immediately after. Feeders and drinkers must be adjusted as birds grow. Raise tube feeders so the feed level is at the level of the bird's back. This reduces spillage and contamination. Provide enough feeder space: one tube feeder per 30 to 40 birds during the grower phase. For drinkers, provide one bell drinker per 60 to 80 birds or one nipple per 8 to 10 birds. Water consumption increases significantly during the grower phase. A bird may drink 150 to 250 millilitres of water per day, depending on temperature and feed intake. Always provide clean, cool water. In many African farms, water is the limiting factor for growth. If water is scarce or dirty, feed conversion will suffer. Test your water source for salinity and bacterial contamination. If you use borehole water, consider filtering or treating it. The grower phase is also the time for the second round of vaccinations, such as Newcastle disease booster and infectious bursal disease. Follow your local veterinary advice. Some African countries have specific disease challenges, such as Newcastle disease in West Africa or avian influenza in South Africa. Work with a veterinarian to design a vaccination programme that fits your region. Nutritionally, the grower diet should be balanced for amino acids, especially lysine and methionine. These are the building blocks of muscle. If they are deficient, the bird will grow slowly and deposit fat instead of lean meat. In Africa, where soybean meal and synthetic amino acids can be expensive or unavailable, some producers use alternative protein sources such as sunflower cake, groundnut cake or fishmeal. These can work, but they must be properly balanced. For example, groundnut cake is low in lysine and methionine, so it must be supplemented. Fishmeal is high in protein but can be inconsistent in quality and may carry bacteria if not properly processed. If you mix your own feed, use a reputable formulation software or consult a nutritionist. Do not guess. A poorly balanced grower diet will cost you more in the long run through poor growth and higher feed conversion ratio. The grower phase is also when you should start planning for the finisher phase. Monitor body weight weekly. A typical broiler should weigh around 1.0 to 1.2 kilograms at day 21 and 1.8 to 2.0 kilograms at day 28, depending on the breed and sex. If your birds are below target, investigate feed quality, disease, ventilation and stocking density. If they are above target, you may be able to switch to finisher feed earlier, saving money. Uniformity is another key indicator. In a well-managed flock, at least 80 percent of birds should be within 10 percent of the average weight. Poor uniformity often indicates disease, feed competition or inadequate feeder space. In Africa, many producers sell birds at different weights to different markets. Some buyers prefer smaller birds for grilling, while others want larger birds for roasting. The grower phase is your opportunity to sort birds if you are targeting different markets. However, sorting causes stress, so do it carefully and only if the price premium justifies it. Litter management continues to be important. As birds grow, they produce more droppings, so the litter gets wetter. Add fresh litter as needed and stir it regularly. Ammonia levels should not exceed 20 parts per million. If you smell ammonia strongly, you need better ventilation or more frequent litter changes. In summary, the grower phase is about building a strong, uniform flock at a reasonable cost. Use a well-balanced grower diet, provide ample feeder and drinker space, manage heat stress and keep the environment clean. In the African context, where heat and disease are constant challenges, the grower phase is where good management pays off. A flock that performs well in the grower phase will be ready for the finisher phase and will reward you with high feed conversion and good market weight.

4. The Finisher Phase: Maximising Weight and Profit from Day 29 to Market

The finisher phase covers the final period before slaughter, typically from day 29 to day 35 or day 42, depending on the target market weight. In some African markets, birds are sold at 1.8 to 2.2 kilograms live weight, while in others, especially in South Africa and urban Nigeria, the target is 2.5 to 3.0 kilograms. The finisher phase is where you maximise weight gain and minimise feed cost per kilogram of live weight. The finisher diet is the cheapest per kilogram because it contains less protein and more energy. A typical finisher feed contains 17 to 19 percent crude protein and 3,000 to 3,100 kilocalories of metabolisable energy per kilogram. The particle size is usually pellets. The goal is to encourage high feed intake and efficient conversion of feed into meat. In the finisher phase, the bird's growth rate slows slightly, but feed intake increases. The bird is depositing fat and muscle, and the feed conversion ratio tends to worsen. This is normal. Your job is to manage the transition smoothly and avoid any stress that could reduce intake. Feed withdrawal before slaughter is a critical part of the finisher phase. In most African countries, birds are transported to market or slaughterhouses, and feed is withdrawn 8 to 12 hours before slaughter to reduce contamination and improve processing. However, do not withdraw water. Water should be available until the birds are caught. In hot climates, water withdrawal can cause dehydration and weight loss. Plan the withdrawal carefully. If you are selling live birds at the farm gate, you may not need to withdraw feed, but you should still avoid feeding immediately before transport to reduce stress and defecation. The finisher phase is also the time to consider antibiotic withdrawal. If you used medicated feed or water during the grower phase, you must respect the withdrawal period specified on the product label. In many African markets, buyers are increasingly demanding antibiotic-free chicken. If you are targeting that market, you should stop all antibiotics at least 5 to 7 days before slaughter, or use only approved natural alternatives. Always check local regulations. Some countries, such as South Africa and Kenya, have strict rules on antibiotic use in poultry. The finisher phase is not the time to introduce new feed ingredients. Keep the diet consistent. Sudden changes in feed texture, taste or composition can reduce intake and cause digestive upset. If you must change feed, do it gradually over three to four days, mixing the old and new feed. Water quality remains critical. In the finisher phase, birds drink a lot of water. A flock of 1,000 birds may drink 200 to 300 litres per day. Ensure your water system can handle that demand. Clean drinkers daily. If you use nipple lines, check for leaks and blockages. In hot weather, add electrolytes to the water to help birds cope with heat stress. Ventilation is also crucial. As birds grow, they produce more heat and moisture. Without adequate ventilation, ammonia and humidity build up, leading to respiratory disease and poor performance. In open-sided houses common in Africa, use curtains and fans to control airflow. In closed houses, use tunnel ventilation. The goal is to keep the air fresh and the litter dry. Stocking density in the finisher phase should be 8 to 10 birds per square metre, depending on the climate and housing. Overcrowding reduces feed intake, increases stress and leads to poor uniformity. If you are in a hot region, reduce density further. Some African producers use 6 to 8 birds per square metre in the finisher phase to manage heat. Weigh birds weekly during the finisher phase. A typical broiler should weigh around 2.2 to 2.5 kilograms at day 35 and 2.8 to 3.2 kilograms at day 42, depending on the breed and sex. If your birds are not hitting target, check feed quality, water access, disease and ventilation. If they are exceeding target, you may be able to sell earlier and save on feed. The finisher phase is also when you should plan your marketing. In Africa, broiler prices fluctuate. Sometimes there is a glut, and prices fall. Other times, especially during festive seasons such as Christmas, Eid or Easter, prices rise. If you can, time your finisher phase to coincide with high-demand periods. This requires planning from the starter phase. Keep records of feed intake, body weight and mortality. These records help you calculate your cost of production and your break-even price. In summary, the finisher phase is about maximising weight and profit while managing feed withdrawal, antibiotic withdrawal and heat stress. Use a high-energy finisher diet, provide plenty of clean water, maintain good ventilation and monitor growth. In the African context, where markets are dynamic and climate is challenging, the finisher phase is where you convert your hard work into money. A well-managed finisher phase means healthy birds, good weight and a profitable sale.

5. Practical Feed Management, Record Keeping and Troubleshooting for African Broiler Farms

This final lesson brings together the practical skills you need to run a successful three-phase feeding programme in an African broiler operation. It covers feed storage, feed budgeting, record keeping, troubleshooting common problems and adapting the programme to local conditions. Feed storage is one of the most overlooked aspects of broiler production in Africa. Poor storage leads to mould, mycotoxins, insect damage and nutrient loss. Always store feed in a cool, dry, well-ventilated room. Use pallets to keep bags off the floor. Stack bags no more than 10 high to prevent compaction. Keep feed away from walls to allow air circulation. Never store feed with chemicals, pesticides or fertilisers. Use the first-in, first-out principle. Buy only as much feed as you can use within four to six weeks. In humid regions, consider using airtight silos or hermetic bags. If you see mould or smell a musty odour, do not feed it to your birds. Mycotoxins can cause liver damage, poor growth and high mortality. If you suspect mycotoxins, add a mycotoxin binder to the feed and consult a veterinarian. Feed budgeting is essential for cash flow. Calculate the total feed required for each phase based on the number of birds and the expected feed intake. For example, a broiler may eat about 0.5 kilograms of starter feed, 1.5 kilograms of grower feed and 2.0 kilograms of finisher feed to reach 2.5 kilograms live weight. Multiply by the number of birds and the price per kilogram to estimate your total feed cost. Add a 5 to 10 percent buffer for waste and mortality. This budget helps you negotiate with feed suppliers and plan your finances. Record keeping is the backbone of good management. Keep a daily record of feed issued, water consumption, mortality, temperature and any medications. Weigh a sample of birds weekly and record the average weight. Calculate feed conversion ratio by dividing total feed consumed by total weight gain. A good feed conversion ratio for broilers is 1.5 to 1.8. If your ratio is above 2.0, you are losing money. Use records to identify trends. If feed intake drops suddenly, check for disease, heat stress or feed quality issues. If mortality spikes, investigate immediately. In Africa, many producers use paper records, but smartphone apps and simple spreadsheets are increasingly available. Choose a system that works for you and stick to it. Troubleshooting common problems is a key skill. If birds are not eating, check the feed for freshness and particle size. Check water availability and quality. Check temperature and ventilation. Check for disease symptoms such as respiratory distress, diarrhoea or lameness. If birds are eating but not growing, check the feed formulation. It may be deficient in protein, energy or amino acids. If birds are growing but feed conversion is poor, check for feed wastage. Are feeders too low? Are birds spilling feed? Are rodents eating the feed? If birds are dying, get a post-mortem examination. Do not guess. In many African countries, veterinary diagnostic services are available through government offices or private clinics. Use them. Adapting the feeding programme to local conditions is also important. In hot climates, increase nutrient density and offer feed during cooler hours. In cold climates, increase energy content. If local feed ingredients are cheaper, consider mixing your own feed, but only if you have a reliable formulation and a mixer. If you buy commercial feed, choose a reputable brand and check the label for nutrient content and manufacturing date. Consider using local alternatives such as cassava, maize bran, palm kernel cake or insect meal, but only after proper research and balancing. Finally, think about your market. Are you selling live birds, dressed chicken or processed products? Each market has different requirements for weight, uniformity and antibiotic use. Your feeding programme should be designed to meet those requirements. If you are selling to a supermarket or restaurant, you may need to meet specific standards. If you are selling in a local market, you may have more flexibility. In summary, successful broiler feeding in Africa requires more than just buying the right feed. It requires careful storage, budgeting, record keeping, troubleshooting and adaptation. By mastering these practical skills, you can reduce costs, improve performance and increase profit. The three-phase programme is your roadmap, but your management decisions determine your success. Start with good chicks, good feed and good records. Pay attention to detail. Treat every flock as a business. With discipline and consistency, you can build a profitable broiler enterprise that contributes to food security and economic growth in your community.

â  SAFETY WARNING â FEED, WATER AND MEDICATION HANDLING

Improper handling of feed, water additives and veterinary medicines is a direct food-safety and human-health risk. Observe the following at all times:

  • Withdrawal periods are not optional. If you use any medicated feed or administer antibiotics or coccidiostats, respect the full withdrawal period before slaughter. Selling birds during withdrawal leaves drug residues in meat and is illegal in most African markets. Follow the label and your veterinarian's instruction â never guess.
  • Never use human antibiotics as growth promoters. WHO classifies several antibiotics used in poultry as critically important for human medicine. Misuse drives antimicrobial resistance and can disqualify your birds from formal buyers, hotels and export chains.
  • Store feed dry and off the floor. Mouldy feed produces aflatoxins â a serious liver carcinogen. Discard any feed that is caked, musty or discoloured. Do not "dilute" mouldy feed with good feed.
  • Protect water quality. Clean drinkers daily. Do not mix medications in rusty or dirty containers.
  • Wear gloves and a mask when handling medicated premixes or spraying disinfectants.
  • Record every treatment â product, dose, date, withdrawal end date â in your batch record book.

4. Quick Reference Checklist

  • Weigh weekly. Sample 10â20 birds every 7 days and record average body weight against the phase target.
  • Calculate FCR every batch. Total feed in Ã· total live weight out. Compare to the previous batch.
  • Calculate feed cost per kg live weight. This is your real cost driver â track it, not just the price of a bag of feed.
  • Record mortality daily with cause. A spike on any single day is a signal â investigate within 24 hours.
  • Compute EPEF at sale. One number to compare batches, houses and workers.
  • Check withdrawal dates before every sale. Write the date on the pen door.
  • Store feed in a dry, raised, pest-proof store. Use oldest stock first (FIFO).
  • Review feed supplier quality quarterly. Ask for aflatoxin test results and crude protein specification.

5. Turning Numbers into Profit Decisions

Three decisions follow directly from the numbers above:

  1. If FCR is above 2.1: investigate feed wastage (feeder height, spillage), disease, heat stress, or feed quality â in that order. Feed wastage alone can add 0.2â0.3 to FCR on poorly managed farms.
  2. If mortality is above 10%: review brooding management, vaccination schedule and biosecurity. Every 1% of mortality above target costs you roughly 1% of revenue with no reduction in feed cost.
  3. If feed cost per kg live weight is above your market's farm-gate price minus your non-feed costs: you are producing at a loss. Either renegotiate feed supply, improve FCR, or move to a higher-value market segment (live bird, dressed, or branded).

The farms that survive and grow in African broiler markets are not the ones with the cheapest feed â they are the ones that know their numbers and act on them weekly.
