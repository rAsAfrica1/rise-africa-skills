1. Sourcing and Preparing Local Scrap Steel in African Markets

Welcome to Module 7, Lesson 1. In this lesson we focus on the single most important input for any blacksmith business in Africa: your steel. Whether you are working in Kano, Kumasi, Nakuru, or a small town in Limpopo, the quality and cost of your raw material will decide whether you make profit or loss. Most African blacksmiths do not buy new billets from a mill. You buy scrap. That is not a weakness. It is a strength if you learn to source and prepare it properly. Scrap steel is everywhere: abandoned cars, old railway lines, broken ploughs, worn-out truck leaf springs, construction rebar offcuts, discarded water pipes, and even old tools. Your job is to turn that chaos into consistent stock. Start by building relationships with three types of suppliers. First, the local scrap dealer or junkyard owner. Visit weekly, not monthly. Bring a small gift or buy him tea. Ask him to set aside leaf springs, coil springs, and thick plates. Second, the mechanics and welders in your area. They generate offcuts and broken parts daily. Offer to buy by weight or trade a simple repair for scrap. Third, the farmers and transport riders. Old plough shares, harrow discs, and axle shafts are high-carbon steel and perfect for tools. Pay a fair price but never overpay. Your profit margin starts at the scrap pile. Once you have scrap, you must sort it. Not all steel is the same. Mild steel, like rebar and angle iron, is low carbon. It bends easily and cannot be hardened. Use it for gates, stands, and decorative work. High-carbon steel, like leaf springs, coil springs, and old files, can be hardened and tempered. Use it for knives, hoes, axes, chisels, and plough points. Cast iron, like old engine blocks and cooking pots, is brittle. Avoid it for forging unless you know exactly what you are doing. Test your steel before you invest time. The spark test is free and fast. Take a sample to your grinder in a dark corner. Press it lightly. A long yellow-orange spark with few bursts means low carbon. A short red-orange spark with many star bursts means high carbon. The more bursts, the more carbon. The file test is also useful. A sharp file bites into mild steel but slides on high-carbon steel. The bend test: cut a thin strip and bend it in a vice. If it bends without cracking, it is likely mild or medium carbon. If it snaps, it is high carbon or cast iron. Keep a notebook. Record where each piece came from, what test showed, and how it performed in the forge. After six months you will have a personal database worth more than any textbook. Preparation is next. Cut your scrap to manageable lengths with a hacksaw, angle grinder, or chisel. Remove rust, paint, and galvanising. Galvanised steel releases toxic zinc fumes when heated. Never forge it indoors. Soak it in vinegar for 24 hours or grind it clean outdoors with a respirator. Remove bolts, rubber, and plastic. For leaf springs, separate the leaves and grind off the centre bolt head. For coil springs, straighten them by heating to orange and pulling. For rebar, cut into 30 cm lengths for handles or decorative twists. Store your prepared stock by type in labelled bins or under a roof. Moisture causes rust. Rust wastes metal and time. A simple rack with a tarpaulin cover works. Now think like a businessperson. Calculate your cost per kilogram. If you paid 500 naira for 10 kg of leaf spring, that is 50 naira per kg. Add transport, cutting discs, and your time. If a hoe head weighs 1 kg and sells for 3000 naira, your gross margin is huge. But if you waste half the steel in bad forging, your margin disappears. So track your yield. Weigh the finished product. Divide finished weight by starting weight. Aim for 70 percent or better. Improve by using a hot cut instead of grinding, by forging close to shape, and by recycling offcuts into small items like nails, hooks, and rings. Finally, build a buying calendar. In many African markets, scrap prices rise before the rainy season when construction peaks. Buy in the dry season when farmers sell old equipment. Buy in bulk when you have cash. Store three months of stock. That protects you from price shocks and shortages. Remember: your forge is only as good as your steel. Master sourcing, sorting, testing, and preparing, and you will outproduce and outsell competitors who grab any random metal. In the next lesson we will set up your forge and fire for consistent heat.

2. Building and Running an Efficient African Forge

Welcome to Lesson 2. A forge is not just a fire. It is a controlled heat engine. In Africa you have many options: a traditional ground forge with a clay tuyere, a metal drum forge, a brake drum forge, or a simple brick box. The best forge is the one you can build with local materials, run with local fuel, and repair with local skills. Start with the firepot. The firepot is a shallow bowl where the fire sits. It should be 15 to 25 cm deep and 30 to 40 cm wide. If it is too shallow, heat escapes. If it is too deep, your work sits too far from the air blast. You can make a firepot from a car brake drum, a truck wheel rim, or a thick clay bowl. Line it with clay, sand, and ash mixed with water. This protects the metal from burning through. The tuyere is the pipe that brings air into the fire. In traditional forges it is a clay tube. In modern forges it is a steel pipe. Angle the tuyere slightly downward so ash does not clog it. The air source can be a hand bellows, a foot bellows, a vacuum cleaner motor, a hair dryer, or a small centrifugal fan. For a small shop, a 12-volt car heater fan connected to a battery or a solar panel works well. For a larger shop, use a 0.5 horsepower electric blower with a gate valve to control air. Too much air burns steel and wastes fuel. Too little air gives a cold fire. Learn to listen and watch. A good fire has a bright yellow-white centre, a orange middle, and a dark outer edge. The work goes in the yellow-white zone for welding, the orange zone for forging, and the red zone for bending. Fuel choice matters. Charcoal is the most common fuel in Africa. It burns clean, reaches high heat, and is easy to find. But it is expensive in cities and contributes to deforestation. Buy from sustainable sources or make your own charcoal from agricultural waste like maize cobs, rice husks, or coconut shells. Coal is cheaper per kilogram but produces smoke and sulphur. Use coal only outdoors or with a chimney. Do not use coal with galvanised steel. It creates toxic fumes. Wood is a poor fuel for forging because it burns cool and leaves lots of ash. Use wood only for preheating or for a simple rural forge. Whatever fuel you use, keep it dry. Wet fuel smokes, sputters, and wastes heat. Store fuel under a roof on a raised platform. Now let us talk about the workshop layout. You need a clear triangle: forge, anvil, and quench tank. The distance from forge to anvil should be one step, about one metre. That saves time and energy. The quench tank should be within arm's reach of the anvil. A 20-litre bucket of water or oil works. For oil quenching, use old engine oil or vegetable oil. Never use petrol or diesel. They are explosive. Your anvil can be a piece of railway line, a heavy block of steel, or a commercial anvil. Mount it at knuckle height. If it rings too much, put a chain or a magnet under the heel. That deadens the sound and improves rebound. Your hammer rack should hold at least three hammers: a 1 kg cross-peen for drawing, a 2 kg straight-peen for heavy work, and a 0.5 kg ball-peen for details. Keep a wire brush, a hot cut chisel, a cold cut chisel, a punch, and a pair of tongs for every stock size you use. Tongs are not optional. Holding hot steel with pliers or bare hands is how you get burned and how you lose control of the work. Make or buy at least three pairs: flat jaws, bolt jaws, and pick-up jaws. Ventilation is critical. Forge fumes contain carbon monoxide, sulphur dioxide, and metal oxides. In a closed room these can kill you. Build your forge under an open-sided shed. If you must work indoors, install a chimney hood directly above the firepot. The hood should be at least 60 cm above the fire and connected to a pipe that exits the roof. A simple 20-litre drum with the bottom cut out works as a hood. Light your fire properly. Start with paper or dry grass, then small kindling, then charcoal or coal. Do not pour paraffin or petrol on the fire. Use a small amount of candle wax or a firelighter. Once the fire is established, add fuel around the edges, not in the centre. Keep the centre clear for the work. Maintain your fire by removing clinker and ash every hour. Clinker is melted ash that blocks air. Use a poker or a slice bar. Add fresh fuel in a ring. Never let the fire go cold if you are working all day. Bank it with wet ash and restart easily. Finally, think about cost and productivity. A good forge uses 2 to 4 kg of charcoal per hour of heavy work. If charcoal costs 500 naira per kg, that is 1000 to 2000 naira per hour. Your finished product must cover that. So work in batches. Heat multiple pieces at once. Use the same fire for forging, bending, and heat treating. Do not waste heat. Plan your day: morning for heavy forging, afternoon for grinding and finishing, evening for heat treating and packing. A well-run forge is a profit centre. A poorly run forge is a money pit. In the next lesson we will cover hammer technique and basic forging operations that turn scrap into saleable goods.

3. Core Forging Techniques for African Tools and Products

Welcome to Lesson 3. Now that you have steel and fire, you need skill. This lesson covers the core forging operations that every African blacksmith must master: drawing out, upsetting, bending, twisting, punching, cutting, and welding. Each operation has a purpose and a technique. Practice each one until it is automatic. Start with drawing out. Drawing out means making steel longer and thinner. You do this by striking with the cross-peen or the flat face at an angle. Heat the steel to orange-yellow. Place it on the anvil with the section you want to draw hanging over the edge. Strike with the hammer face at a slight angle, pushing the metal away from you. Turn the steel 90 degrees every few blows. This keeps the shape square or round. Do not hit cold steel. It will crack or damage your hammer. Do not hit too hard at first. Let the hammer do the work. A good rhythm is one blow per second. For heavy drawing, use a striker with a sledgehammer. The striker hits where you point with a hand hammer or a punch. This is how you make a hoe blade from a leaf spring. Draw the end to a flat, wide shape. Then bend the eye. Then heat treat. Upsetting is the opposite of drawing out. You make the steel shorter and thicker. This is useful for making a hammer head, a chisel top, or a bolt head. Heat the end of the bar to yellow. Stand it on the anvil face. Strike the top with a heavy hammer. The metal squashes down and spreads out. Turn it often to keep it centred. If it starts to bend, straighten it immediately. Upsetting is hard on the anvil and the hammer. Use a heavy anvil and a heavy hammer. Bending is simple but must be controlled. Heat the section to orange. For a sharp bend, place it over the anvil edge and strike with the hammer. For a smooth curve, use a bending fork or a horn. The horn of the anvil is your friend. It lets you make rings, hooks, and curved blades. For a right angle, bend slightly past 90 degrees because steel springs back. For a circle, bend around a pipe or a mandrel. Twisting is decorative and functional. Heat a square bar to orange. Clamp one end in a vice. Grip the other end with a wrench or tongs. Turn slowly and evenly. The number of twists per inch determines the pattern. For a rope twist, twist one way, then the other. For a basket twist, twist four bars together. Twisting is popular for gate handles, curtain rods, and knife handles. Punching makes holes without drilling. Heat the steel to yellow. Place it over the pritchel hole or a bolster. Use a punch with a tapered square or round shank. Strike the punch with a hammer. Turn the steel over and punch from the other side to finish the hole. This gives a clean hole with no burrs. For a large hole, use a drift. A drift is a tapered tool that expands the hole. This is how you make an axe eye or a hammer eye. Cutting is done with a hot cut or a cold cut. A hot cut is a chisel with a long handle. You place it on the hot steel and strike it with a hammer. A cold cut is a chisel with a short handle. You hold it and strike it. Never cut cold steel with a hot cut. It will chip. Never cut hot steel with a cold cut. It will lose its temper. Forge welding is the most advanced operation. It joins two pieces of steel into one. Heat both pieces to a bright yellow-white, almost sparkling. Sprinkle borax or fine sand on the joint. This prevents oxidation. Bring the pieces together on the anvil. Strike lightly with a hammer to set the weld. Then strike harder to consolidate. Forge welding is used to make chains, rings, and layered blades. It takes practice. Start with mild steel. Then move to high carbon. If the weld fails, clean the surfaces and try again. Now let us apply these techniques to real products. A hoe is the most common tool in African agriculture. Start with a leaf spring 30 cm long, 5 cm wide, and 5 mm thick. Draw out one end to a triangular blade. Bend the other end into a socket or eye. Heat treat the blade: heat to cherry red, quench in oil, then temper in an oven at 200 degrees Celsius for one hour. A knife is another high-demand product. Use a file or a coil spring. Forge the blade to shape. Leave the spine thick for strength. Grind the bevel. Heat treat the same way. Add a wooden handle with rivets or epoxy. A machete or panga is a bestseller in rural areas. Use a leaf spring. Forge a long blade with a slight curve. Add a wooden handle. Balance is critical. The blade should feel light in the hand but heavy when it strikes. A gate or a window frame is a high-value product for urban customers. Use mild steel. Cut, bend, and weld. Add decorative twists. Paint with red oxide primer and then gloss enamel. A charcoal stove or a cooking stand is a simple product with steady demand. Use rebar and angle iron. Cut, bend, and weld. Add a grate made from rebar. A set of tools for a mason or a carpenter is a package deal. Make a chisel, a punch, a bolster, and a trowel. Sell them as a set. That increases your average sale. Remember: quality sells. A tool that breaks in the field destroys your reputation. A tool that lasts for years brings you repeat customers and referrals. So do not rush. Heat properly. Forge properly. Heat treat properly. Finish properly. In the next lesson we will cover heat treatment and finishing, which turn a rough forging into a professional product.

4. Heat Treatment, Finishing, and Quality Control

Welcome to Lesson 4. A forged tool is not finished until it is heat treated and finished. Heat treatment gives steel its hardness, toughness, and edge retention. Finishing gives it corrosion resistance and customer appeal. Quality control protects your brand. Let us start with heat treatment. There are three steps: hardening, tempering, and sometimes annealing or normalising. Hardening means heating the steel to its critical temperature and then quenching it. The critical temperature depends on the carbon content. For high-carbon steel like leaf springs and files, heat to 760 to 820 degrees Celsius. That is cherry red to bright orange in a dim light. Do not guess. Use a magnet. When the steel becomes non-magnetic, it is at the right temperature. Hold it there for one minute per 3 mm of thickness. Then quench. Quenching medium matters. Water is fast and can crack high-carbon steel. Oil is slower and safer. Brine is in between. For most African blacksmiths, used engine oil or vegetable oil is the best choice. Warm the oil to about 50 degrees Celsius before quenching. That improves the quench speed. Move the steel in the oil in a figure-eight pattern. That prevents soft spots. Do not drop it in and leave it. Hold it with tongs until it stops smoking. Then remove it. It should be hard enough that a file slides across it. If the file bites, the quench failed. Reheat and try again. Tempering reduces brittleness. Hardened steel is too brittle for tools. It will chip or snap. Tempering relaxes the internal stresses. Clean the steel with a grinder or sandpaper. Then heat it slowly. You can use an oven, a charcoal fire, or a hot plate. Watch the oxide colours. They appear in this order: pale yellow, straw, brown, purple, blue, dark blue, grey. For a knife or a chisel, aim for straw to brown, about 200 to 250 degrees Celsius. For a hoe or an axe, aim for purple to blue, about 280 to 320 degrees Celsius. For a spring, aim for dark blue, about 320 to 350 degrees Celsius. Hold the temper for one hour. Then cool in air or quench in water. Do not over-temper. Over-tempered steel is soft and will not hold an edge. Do not under-temper. Under-tempered steel is brittle and will break. Annealing is softening steel so you can file, drill, or bend it. Heat to critical temperature. Then bury it in ash, sand, or vermiculite. Let it cool slowly overnight. Normalising is similar but you cool in air. Normalising refines the grain and relieves stress. It is useful before hardening. Now let us talk about finishing. A rough forging has scale, hammer marks, and sharp edges. Customers in Africa want tools that look good and feel good. Start with a grinder or a file. Remove the scale. Smooth the bevels. Round the sharp edges. For a knife, grind the bevel to a thin edge. For a hoe, grind the blade to a sharp edge. For a machete, grind a convex edge for strength. Then sand. Start with 80 grit, then 120, then 220, then 400. For a mirror finish, go to 800 or 1000. Use water or oil as a lubricant to prevent clogging. For a rustic finish, leave some hammer marks. That is popular with tourists and high-end buyers. For a polished finish, spend the time. It increases the price. Next, handle installation. For a knife, use hardwood like ebony, mahogany, or camel thorn. Cut two scales. Drill holes for rivets. Mix epoxy. Clamp the scales to the tang. Let it cure for 24 hours. Then sand the handle to shape. Finish with oil or wax. For an axe or a hoe, use a wooden handle with a wedge. Fit the head to the handle. Cut a slot in the handle top. Insert a wooden or metal wedge. Tap it in. Soak the head in water for an hour before use. That swells the wood and tightens the fit. For a machete, use a full tang handle with rivets. Shape it for comfort. Add a lanyard hole. Now quality control. Every product must pass five checks. First, hardness. Test with a file. The file should slide on the cutting edge but bite on the spine. Second, toughness. Bend the blade slightly. It should spring back. If it stays bent, it is too soft. If it snaps, it is too hard. Third, edge retention. Cut a piece of wood or rope 50 times. The edge should still be sharp. Fourth, handle security. Twist and pull the handle. It should not move. Fifth, finish. Look for cracks, pits, and sharp edges. Fix them before selling. Keep a record of every batch. Note the steel source, the heat treatment temperatures, the quench medium, and the tempering colour. If a customer complains, you can trace the problem. If a batch is good, you can repeat it. Also think about packaging. A simple cardboard sleeve with your name and phone number is enough. For higher-end products, use a wooden box or a leather sheath. A sheath adds value and safety. Make sheaths from local leather. Cut, stitch, and wet-form to the blade. Add a belt loop. Sell the knife with the sheath as a set. Finally, pricing. Your price must cover materials, fuel, labour, and overhead. Then add profit. A common mistake is to price only by comparing with imported tools. Imported tools are often cheap but low quality. Your tools are better. Charge for that. Offer a guarantee. If a tool breaks within one month of normal use, replace it. That builds trust. That builds a brand. 5. Marketing, Sales, and Scaling Your Blacksmith Business

Welcome to Lesson 5. You now have steel, fire, skill, and quality. The final step is to turn that into money. This lesson covers marketing, sales, pricing, and scaling. It is written for the African context, where trust, relationships, and word of mouth matter more than glossy advertising. Start with your target customer. In every African community there are three main markets. First, the rural farmer. He needs hoes, machetes, axes, plough points, and sickles. He buys based on durability and price. He is loyal if you treat him well. Second, the urban tradesman. He needs chisels, hammers, trowels, punches, and bolts. He buys based on precision and finish. He will pay more for quality. Third, the household and tourist market. They need knives, cooking stands, gate hinges, curtain rods, and decorative pieces. They buy based on beauty and story. They will pay a premium for handmade goods. Do not try to serve all three at once. Pick one primary market. Master it. Then expand. Next, build your brand. A brand is not a logo. It is a promise. Your promise is: strong tools, fair prices, honest service. Put your name and phone number on every product. Use a metal stamp or a paint marker. If you cannot write, use a symbol. A lion, a hammer, a star. Register your business if possible. In many African countries, a small business registration is cheap and gives you access to bank loans and government contracts. Even without registration, keep a receipt book. Give every customer a receipt. That builds trust and helps you track sales. Marketing on a budget. You do not need radio or television. You need three things: a visible workshop, a network of referrers, and a simple phone. Make your workshop visible. Paint your sign. Keep the front clean. Display your best products. Put a bench outside where people can sit and watch. Watching a blacksmith work is entertainment. It draws a crowd. That crowd becomes customers. Build a network. Talk to farmers, mechanics, builders, and shopkeepers. Give them a small commission for every customer they send. Give them a free tool or a discount for their own use. They will talk about you. Use your phone. Take photos of your products. Send them on WhatsApp. Post them on Facebook Marketplace or local buy-and-sell groups. Join farming and trade groups. Answer questions. Share tips. Do not just sell. Help. That builds authority. Sales conversations. When a customer comes, do not start with price. Start with questions. What will you use it for? How often? What have you used before? Then recommend the right tool. Show the difference between your tool and a cheap import. Let them hold it. Let them feel the balance. Explain the heat treatment. Explain the guarantee. Then give the price. Do not apologise for the price. Say it with confidence. If they hesitate, offer a smaller version or a payment plan. In many African markets, customers buy on market day. So be ready. Have stock. Have change. Have a helper. Pricing strategy. There are three methods. Cost-plus: add up materials, fuel, labour, and overhead, then add 30 to 50 percent profit. Competitor-based: check what others charge, then charge 10 to 20 percent more if your quality is better. Value-based: charge what the customer saves or earns by using your tool. A farmer who buys a hoe that lasts three seasons instead of one saves money. Charge for that value. Most importantly, do not compete on price alone. There is always someone cheaper. Compete on quality, service, and story. Scaling your business. You cannot scale alone. You need a team. Start with one apprentice. Teach him the basics: fire management, hammer technique, and finishing. Pay him a small wage plus a share of profits. Treat him well. He will become your best worker. Then add a second apprentice. One focuses on forging, the other on finishing. You focus on sales and quality control. Eventually, you can open a second workshop in another town. Put a trusted apprentice in charge. Visit weekly. Share profits. That is how you grow. Also consider product diversification. If you make hoes, add machetes. If you make knives, add leather sheaths. If you make gates, add window frames. Each new product uses the same skills and the same tools. That increases revenue without increasing fixed costs. Consider adding a repair service. Farmers and tradesmen always need repairs. A broken hoe, a loose handle, a dull knife. Charge a small fee. It brings people into your shop. They often buy something new while they are there. Consider training. Other blacksmiths will pay to learn your techniques. Run a one-week workshop. Charge a fee. That is income with no material cost. Consider exporting. African handmade knives, axes, and jewellery are popular in Europe and America. Work with a local exporter or start an online shop. Take good photos. Write clear descriptions. Ship in small batches. It is not easy, but the margins are high. Finally, manage your money. Keep business money separate from personal money. Save 10 percent of every sale. Use that savings for new tools, new stock, or emergencies. Do not buy a motorcycle or a big phone before you have three months of operating expenses saved. Track your sales weekly. Know your best-selling product. Know your most profitable product. Push that one. Cut the ones that do not sell. Pay yourself a salary. Even a small one. That makes your business real. In conclusion, blacksmithing in Africa is not a dying trade. It is a growing trade. As imports become more expensive and unreliable, local production becomes more valuable. You have the skills. You have the materials. You have the market. Now you need the discipline to run it as a business. Treat every customer with respect. Treat every tool as a signature. Treat every day as a chance to improve. That is how you build a blacksmith business that lasts for generations. Thank you for completing Module 7.

Why reference data matters in a blacksmith business

Most African blacksmiths operate in the informal sector, where prices are negotiated and quality is judged by the customer's eye rather than a certificate. That creates an opportunity. If you can show a buyer that your hoe blade meets a known hardness, that your machete handle uses a standard wood, or that your workshop meets basic safety norms, you can charge a premium and win institutional buyers such as cooperatives, NGOs, and agro-dealers. â  SAFETY WARNING â Metalworking fumes, burns, and hearing damage

Forging and quenching release toxic fumes, especially when working galvanised, painted, or oil-coated scrap. Zinc fumes from galvanised steel cause metal fume fever; lead paint and chromium coatings are carcinogenic. Always forge outdoors or under a chimney with cross-ventilation. Wear leather gloves, eye protection, and ear plugs. Quench oil can flash-fire; never add water to a burning oil quench. Keep a dry-powder fire extinguisher or a bucket of sand within 3 metres of the firepot. According to WHO and the International Labour Organization, informal metalworkers face elevated risks of respiratory disease, burns, and hearing loss, yet fewer than 20% in sub-Saharan Africa use any form of respiratory protection. Treat this as a business risk: an injured smith cannot earn.

  2. Sort scrap by spark test before forging. Low-carbon sparks are long and yellow; high-carbon sparks are short and bright. This prevents tool failure.
  3. Pre-heat quench oil to 40â60 Â°C for medium-carbon steels. Cold oil causes warping and cracks.
