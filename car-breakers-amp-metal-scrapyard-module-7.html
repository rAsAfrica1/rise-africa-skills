Lessons

1. Cleaning and testing used parts before sale

Before you sell any used part, you must clean it and test it. A dirty part looks like junk and won't fetch a good price. Start by scraping off heavy grease with a putty knife, then soak the part in a bucket of diesel or kerosene for 30 minutes. Use a wire brush to scrub away rust and dirt. For aluminium parts like alternator housings, use a brass wire brush to avoid scratching. After cleaning, rinse with water and dry with compressed air or a clean rag. For electrical parts like alternators and starters, testing is critical. You need a multimeter (cost: about 5,000-10,000 Naira or 300-600 KES). Set it to DC volts. For an alternator, spin the pulley by hand and check that the rotor turns freely without grinding. Connect the multimeter to the output terminal and spin the pulley with a drill; you should see 12-14 volts. For a starter, connect it to a 12V battery with jumper cables. The starter gear should jump out and spin strongly. If it just clicks or spins slowly, it's no good. For brake calipers, check that the piston moves in and out smoothly using a C-clamp. If it's stuck, it needs a rebuild. For mechanical parts like steering racks or driveshafts, check for excessive play by wiggling joints. Any part that fails testing should be sold as scrap metal, not as a used part. Keep a testing log: write the part name, test result, and date. This helps you price accurately and avoid returns. Remember, a clean, tested part can sell for 3-5 times more than an untested, dirty one. In many African markets, buyers trust you more if you show them the test results. So invest in a multimeter and a battery charger; they pay for themselves quickly.

2. Basic repairs: rebuilding alternators, starters, and brake calipers

Rebuilding alternators, starters, and brake calipers can double or triple your profit. Start with alternators. Most failures are worn brushes, bad bearings, or a faulty voltage regulator. Buy a rebuild kit (brushes, bearings, regulator) for about 3,000-5,000 Naira or 200-400 KES. Disconnect the battery, remove the alternator, and open the casing. Take photos as you go. Replace the brushes if they are less than 5mm long. Press out old bearings with a socket and hammer, then tap in new ones gently. Clean the slip rings with fine sandpaper (400 grit). Solder new brushes in place. Reassemble and test: spin by hand, then with a drill, and check voltage output. For starters, common issues are worn solenoid contacts, bad brushes, and dirty commutator. A starter rebuild kit costs 2,500-4,000 Naira or 150-300 KES. Open the starter, clean the commutator with sandpaper, undercut the mica if needed, and replace brushes and solenoid contacts. Test with a battery: the gear should kick out and spin fast. For brake calipers, the main problem is a stuck piston due to rust. Buy a caliper rebuild kit (new seals and dust boots) for 1,500-3,000 Naira or 100-200 KES. Remove the piston using compressed air (careful: it can fly out). Clean the bore with brake cleaner and a hone. Replace seals, lubricate with brake grease, and reassemble. Test by pumping the piston out with a brake pedal or air. Always use new brake fluid when installing. Rebuilding requires patience and cleanliness. Work on a clean bench, use the right tools (snap ring pliers, torque wrench), and never reuse old seals. Sell rebuilt parts with a 30-day warranty. In many African cities, rebuilt alternators sell for 15,000-25,000 Naira or 1,000-1,500 KES, while starters go for 12,000-20,000 Naira or 800-1,200 KES. Calipers fetch 8,000-15,000 Naira or 500-900 KES. That's good money for a few hours of work.

3. Pricing used parts based on condition and demand

Pricing used parts is not guesswork. You need to consider condition, demand, and the price of new parts. Start by grading each part: A (like new, tested, clean), B (good, minor wear, works), C (usable but needs repair), D (for scrap only). Grade A parts can sell for 50-70% of new price. Grade B for 30-50%. Grade C for 10-25%. Grade D for scrap value by weight. For example, a new alternator for a Toyota Corolla might cost 60,000 Naira or 4,000 KES. A Grade A used one can sell for 30,000-40,000 Naira or 2,000-2,800 KES. A Grade B for 18,000-30,000 Naira or 1,200-2,000 KES. Check online platforms like Jiji, OLX, or Facebook Marketplace to see what others are asking. But don't just copy prices; adjust for your part's condition. Demand matters: parts for popular models like Toyota, Honda, Nissan, and Hyundai sell faster and for higher prices. Rare models may sit for months, so price lower to move them. Also consider urgency: if a mechanic needs a part today, they'll pay more. Offer a small discount for bulk buys (e.g., 10% off for 5+ parts). Keep a record of what you paid for the scrap car and what you've sold from it. Your goal is to recover the car's cost within the first 5-10 parts sold, then everything else is profit. For example, if you bought a scrap Corolla for 150,000 Naira or 10,000 KES, selling the alternator, starter, gearbox, and ECU might already cover that. Don't be afraid to negotiate, but know your bottom line. A good rule: never sell below your cost plus 20% for Grade A and B parts. For Grade C, sell at cost or slightly above to clear space. Use a pricing sheet: list part, grade, new price, your price, and date sold. Review monthly and adjust.

4. Marketing parts to local mechanics and online platforms

Marketing is how you turn parts into cash. Start with local mechanics. Visit at least 10 mechanics within a 5km radius. Bring a notebook with your parts list and prices. Introduce yourself, hand out a simple business card (name, phone, what you sell). Offer a 5-10% commission for any mechanic who brings you a buyer. Mechanics talk to each other, so one good relationship can bring many sales. Also visit auto electricians, panel beaters, and tyre shops. For online platforms, use Jiji, OLX, Facebook Marketplace, and WhatsApp groups. Take clear photos: clean the part, place it on a plain background (like a white cloth), and take photos from multiple angles. Include a ruler or coin for scale. Write a detailed title: e.g., 'Toyota Corolla 2005 Alternator - Tested, 3-Month Warranty'. In the description, state condition, part number, compatible models, and your location. Price competitively but not too low. Respond to messages quickly; buyers often move to the next seller if you delay. Use WhatsApp Business to catalogue your parts and set up auto-replies. Join local buy-and-sell groups on Facebook and WhatsApp. Post daily or every other day, but don't spam. Offer delivery within your city for a small fee (e.g., 1,000 Naira or 100 KES). For distant buyers, use online payment methods like bank transfer or mobile money (M-Pesa, Airtel Money). Build trust by asking for reviews after each sale. A simple 'Thank you, please recommend me' goes a long way. Track which platforms bring the most sales. If Jiji works best, focus there. If mechanics buy more, visit them weekly. Consistency is key: market every day, even when you're busy with dismantling. Remember, a part not marketed is a part not sold.

5. Packaging and shipping parts to distant buyers

When you sell to a distant buyer, packaging and shipping must be done right to avoid damage and returns. Start with cleaning the part thoroughly; no one wants a greasy box. For small parts like alternators, starters, or ECUs, wrap them in bubble wrap or foam. If bubble wrap is expensive, use old newspaper or cardboard. Place the wrapped part in a sturdy cardboard box. Fill empty spaces with more newspaper, foam peanuts, or air pillows so the part doesn't move. For heavy parts like brake calipers or gearboxes, use a wooden crate or a double-walled box. Secure with strong tape (packing tape, not cello tape). Label the box clearly with the buyer's address and your return address. For shipping within your country, use bus companies (e.g., ABC Transport, Chisco in Nigeria; Easy Coach in Kenya) or courier services (G4S, DHL). Bus companies are cheaper for heavy items: a 10kg box might cost 2,000-3,000 Naira or 200-300 KES. Courier services are faster but pricier. For international shipping, use EMS, DHL, or FedEx, but check customs rules. Always insure high-value parts. Get a tracking number and share it with the buyer. Take photos of the packaged part before sealing and after sealing. This protects you if the buyer claims damage. For payment, insist on full payment before shipping. Use bank transfer or mobile money. If the buyer is new, ask for a deposit (50%) and the rest on delivery. For fragile parts like sensors, add 'FRAGILE' stickers. For liquids like brake fluid, don't ship; it's hazardous. After shipping, follow up with the buyer to confirm delivery. If they report damage, ask for photos and file a claim with the courier. Good packaging reduces returns and builds your reputation. A happy distant buyer will order again and refer others.

6. Offering warranties and handling returns professionally

Offering a warranty builds trust and can justify higher prices. But you must handle returns professionally to avoid losing money and reputation. Start with a simple written warranty: 30 days for rebuilt parts, 14 days for tested used parts, and no warranty for 'as-is' parts. Print warranty cards or write on the receipt: part name, date, warranty period, and your contact. Explain what is covered (defects in workmanship or part failure) and what is not (damage from installation error, overheating, or accidents). When a buyer returns a part, stay calm and polite. Ask for the receipt and the part. Inspect it immediately. If it's a valid warranty claim, offer a replacement, repair, or refund. For example, if a rebuilt alternator fails within 30 days, replace it or refund 100%. If the buyer damaged it, explain politely and offer a discount on a new part. Never argue; a bad review spreads fast. Keep a returns log: date, part, reason, action taken. This helps you spot patterns. If you see many returns for a certain part, stop selling it or improve your rebuild process. For refunds, use the same payment method. If you shipped the part, ask the buyer to ship it back; you can split shipping costs. For online sales, clearly state your return policy in the listing. Many platforms like Jiji allow buyers to rate you; a good rating means more sales. Consider offering a 'core charge' for rebuilt parts: you charge a deposit that is refunded when the buyer returns the old core. This ensures you get rebuildable cores. Finally, learn from every return. If a part fails, find out why. Was it a bad rebuild? Did you miss a crack? Improve your testing and rebuilding. A professional returns process turns a negative into a positive: the buyer sees you stand behind your work and becomes a loyal customer.

<h2>Practical Reference: Refurbishing and Selling Used Auto Parts</h2>

<p>In Africas informal and semi-formal automotive sector, used auto parts are not a side business they are the backbone of vehicle maintenance. From Lagos to Nairobi, Accra to Johannesburg, mechanics and fleet operators rely on salvaged alternators, starters, brake calipers, and suspension components because new OEM parts are often 310 times more expensive and slow to import. For the car breaker or scrapyard operator, the difference between selling a dirty, untested part and a cleaned, tested, warrantied part is the difference between a low-margin scrap trade and a repeat-customer parts business.</p>

<p>This Practical Reference module covers the full commercial pathway: cleaning and testing, basic rebuilding, condition-based pricing, marketing to local and online buyers, packaging and shipping, and professional warranty and returns handling. Each section is designed for the African context limited capital, unreliable power, long-distance buyers, and a market that values trust and durability above brand polish.</p>

<h3>1. Cleaning and Testing Used Parts Before Sale</h3>

<p>Presentation drives price. A greasy alternator sitting on a dirt floor will attract scrap offers; the same unit cleaned, tested, and labelled can sell for 24 times more. Start with a three-stage cleaning process:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Dry cleaning:</strong> Remove loose dirt, leaves, and oil with a wire brush and compressed air. In yards without compressed air, use a hand pump or foot pump.</li>
<li><strong>Degreasing:</strong> Apply a water-based degreaser (or diesel in low-resource settings) and scrub with a stiff nylon brush. Avoid soaking electrical components in water.</li>
<li><strong>Rinsing and drying:</strong> Rinse with clean water, then dry immediately with rags and sunlight. Never leave alternators or starters wet moisture causes internal corrosion.</li>
</ul>

<p>Testing is where you build trust. For alternators, use a multimeter to check diode continuity and a bench test to confirm charging output (typically 13.514.5V). For starters, test solenoid engagement and motor spin using a battery and jumper leads. For brake calipers, check piston movement, boot condition, and bleed screw function. Record results on a simple tag attached to each part buyer confidence rises sharply when they see Tested: 14.2V output written by hand.</p>

<h3>2. Basic Repairs: Rebuilding Alternators, Starters, and Brake Calipers</h3>

<p>Rebuilding is where margins expand. A used alternator bought for $15 can sell for $60$120 after a bearing and brush replacement. A starter rebuilt with new solenoid contacts can fetch $80$150. Brake calipers rebuilt with new seals and boots sell for $40$90 each.</p>

<p><strong>Alternators:</strong> Disassemble, test diodes and stator, replace bearings and brushes, clean slip rings, reassemble, and bench-test output. Keep a stock of common bearings (6202, 6203) and brush sets.</p>

<p><strong>Starters:</strong> Replace solenoid contacts and plunger, check armature for burn marks, replace bushings, and test under load. A simple growler test or battery-load test is sufficient for most African workshops.</p>

<p><strong>Brake calipers:</strong> Remove piston, inspect bore for pitting, replace seals and dust boots, lubricate with brake grease, and reassemble. Pressure-test with compressed air or brake fluid.</p>

<div style="background:#ffebee;border-left:6px solid #c62828;padding:14px 18px;margin:18px 0;border-radius:4px;">
<strong style="color:#b71c1c;font-size:1.05em;"> SAFETY WARNING Brake Caliper Rebuilding</strong>
<p style="margin:8px 0 0 0;color:#333;">Brake calipers are safety-critical components. Never sell a rebuilt caliper unless you have pressure-tested it and confirmed zero leaks. A failed caliper can cause brake loss and fatal accidents. Use only OEM-spec seals, never substitute with generic O-rings. Work in a clean area dust and grit inside a caliper will destroy seals and cause failure. If you cannot test properly, sell the caliper as a core for rebuilding by a certified workshop, not as a finished part.</p>
</div>

<h3>4. Marketing Parts to Local Mechanics and Online Platforms</h3>

<p>Local mechanics are your repeat buyers. Build a WhatsApp broadcast list segmented by vehicle brand Toyota, Nissan, Honda, Isuzu, Hyundai. Send weekly stock lists with photos, grades, and prices. Offer a 510% discount for bulk purchases of 5+ parts. Visit the 1015 busiest workshops in your area monthly; bring a sample part and a printed price list.</p>

<p>Online platforms expand your reach beyond your city. In Africa, the most effective channels are:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>WhatsApp Business</strong> catalogue feature, auto-replies, and broadcast lists.</li>
<li><strong>Facebook Marketplace &amp; Groups</strong> join local car parts for sale groups; post with clear photos and prices.</li>
<li><strong>Jiji (Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana, Uganda, Tanzania)</strong> the largest classifieds platform in Africa; free listings with paid boosts.</li>
<li><strong>OLX (South Africa, Kenya, Nigeria)</strong> strong for higher-value parts.</li>
<li><strong>Local e-commerce</strong> e.g., Kilimall (Kenya), Jumia (West Africa) for standardized parts.</li>
</ul>

<p>Always include: part number, vehicle compatibility, grade, test results, warranty, and your location. Respond within 1 hour during business hours speed wins sales.</p>

<h3>5. Packaging and Shipping Parts to Distant Buyers</h3>

<p>Shipping is the biggest trust barrier in African used-parts trade. A buyer in Kampala will not pay upfront for a part from Nairobi unless packaging and delivery are professional. Use the following protocol:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Packaging:</strong> Wrap electrical parts in bubble wrap, then place in a sturdy cardboard box with foam or newspaper filling. Brake calipers should be wrapped in plastic to prevent fluid leakage, then boxed.</li>
<li><strong>Labelling:</strong> Include a printed invoice with part number, grade, test results, warranty terms, and your phone number.</li>
<li><strong>Courier options:</strong> Use established regional couriers G4S, DHL, Sendy (East Africa), Kwik (West Africa), or bus parcel services (e.g., Easy Coach, Modern Coast) for cheaper intercity delivery.</li>
<li><strong>Payment:</strong> Use mobile money (M-Pesa, MTN MoMo, Airtel Money) with a 50% deposit and 50% on delivery for new buyers. For repeat buyers, offer credit terms.</li>
<li><strong>Tracking:</strong> Share the courier tracking number immediately and follow up on delivery day.</li>
</ul>

<h3>6. Offering Warranties and Handling Returns Professionally</h3>

<p>A warranty is your strongest marketing tool. It signals confidence and reduces buyer risk. For Grade A rebuilt parts, offer 36 months; for Grade B tested parts, 1430 days. Write the warranty terms clearly on the invoice: what is covered (manufacturing defects, premature failure), what is not (physical damage, incorrect installation, overheating), and the return process.</p>

<p>Handle returns professionally:</p>
<ul>
<li>Ask the buyer to send photos and a description of the failure.</li>
<li>If the part is under warranty and the failure is covered, replace or refund within 48 hours.</li>
<li>If the failure is due to installation error, explain politely and offer a discounted replacement.</li>
<li>Log every return in a notebook patterns reveal supplier quality issues or installation training needs for your mechanic customers.</li>
</ul>

<p>Returns handled well create loyal customers. In the African used-parts market, reputation travels fast through mechanic networks. One fair warranty claim resolved can bring ten new buyers.</p>

<h3>Quick Reference Checklist</h3>
<ol>
<li><strong>Clean every part</strong> degrease, rinse, dry, and label with test results before listing.</li>
<li><strong>Test electrical parts</strong> alternators at 13.514.5V, starters under load, calipers under pressure.</li>
<li><strong>Grade honestly</strong> A (refurbished), B (tested used), C (as-is/core), D (scrap). Price accordingly.</li>
<li><strong>Track demand</strong> keep a notebook of what mechanics ask for; stock the top 10 fast-moving parts.</li>
<li><strong>Market on WhatsApp + Jiji/OLX</strong> post clear photos, part numbers, grades, warranty, and location.</li>
<li><strong>Package professionally</strong> bubble wrap, sturdy box, invoice inside, tracking number shared.</li>
<li><strong>Offer written warranty</strong> 36 months on rebuilt, 1430 days on tested used; state exclusions clearly.</li>
<li><strong>Resolve returns within 48 hours</strong> log every claim and use data to improve sourcing and testing.</li>
</ol>
