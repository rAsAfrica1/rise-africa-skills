Lessons

1. Selling to Local Shops and Salons

Start by identifying 10 small shops and salons within a 5 km radius of your production site. Walk in during off-peak hours (mid-morning or early afternoon) and ask to speak to the owner. Bring samples: 3 lip balms, 3 body butters, and 3 hair oils. Offer a free full-size product for the owner to try. If they like it, propose a consignment arrangement: you leave 10 units of each product, they pay you after selling. Set a clear price: your wholesale price should be 60% of your retail price. For example, if you retail a lip balm at KES 300, wholesale is KES 180. The shop sells at KES 300 and keeps KES 120. For salons, offer a bulk deal: 20 hair oils for the price of 15. Provide a simple invoice book (cost: KES 100) and record every transaction. Visit each shop weekly to check stock and collect payments. After one month, if a shop sells well (more than 50% of stock), offer them a 5% discount on their next order. If a shop sells nothing, take back the stock and try another. Use WhatsApp to send reminders and confirm orders. Keep a notebook with each shop's name, contact, products taken, and payment status. This system builds trust and repeat business.

2. Packaging for Shipping within Africa

Your products must survive rough handling by buses, boda bodas, and postal services. For lip balms, wrap each tube in a small piece of bubble wrap (or newspaper) and place in a zip-lock bag. For body butters, use a strong plastic jar with a screw lid; wrap the jar in a plastic bag, then bubble wrap, then place in a padded envelope or small cardboard box. For hair oils, use a glass or PET bottle; wrap in bubble wrap and seal in a plastic bag to prevent leakage. Use a double-walled cardboard box for multiple items. Fill empty spaces with shredded paper or old newspaper. Seal with strong packing tape (not cello tape). Label the box with the recipient's name, phone number, and address in waterproof marker. Include a small card with your contact and a thank-you note. For shipping within your country, use local bus companies (e.g., in Kenya, Easy Coach or Guardian) â they charge KES 200-500 for a small box up to 5 kg. For cross-border (e.g., Uganda to Kenya), use bus parcel services or courier companies like G4S or DHL, but expect KES 1,500-3,000 for 5 kg. Always get a tracking number or receipt. Test your packaging: drop a packed box from waist height; if it survives, it's good. Buy packaging materials in bulk from local suppliers to save money.

3. Using Boda Boda and Local Couriers

Boda boda (motorcycle taxis) are the fastest and cheapest way to deliver within a city or town. For a 5 km delivery, expect to pay KES 150-250. For 10 km, KES 300-400. Negotiate the price before the rider leaves. Use a delivery app like Uber Boda (in Kenya), SafeBoda (Uganda), or Bolt (various countries) to book and track. If you use a regular rider, build a relationship: agree on a fixed rate for regular deliveries and give them a small tip during peak seasons. Always package your products securely (see previous lesson) and label clearly. For multiple deliveries in one area, combine them into one trip to save money. For local couriers, consider using Pickup Mtaani (Kenya) or similar agent networks: they charge KES 100-200 per parcel and have pickup points. You drop off the parcel, they deliver to their agent near the customer, and the customer picks it up. This is cheaper but slower (1-2 days). For same-day delivery, use boda boda. Always get a proof of delivery: a photo of the parcel at the customer's door, or a signed slip. Keep a log of all deliveries: date, rider name, destination, cost, and delivery status. If a rider loses or damages a parcel, stop using them. Consider hiring a part-time rider if you have more than 10 deliveries per day.

4. Managing Inventory and Stock Levels

Keep a simple inventory system: a notebook or a free app like Google Sheets. List each product (e.g., 'Lip Balm - Mint', 'Body Butter - Shea', 'Hair Oil - Coconut') and track: quantity produced, quantity sold, quantity in stock, and reorder level. For each product, set a reorder level: when stock drops to that number, produce more. For fast-moving items (e.g., lip balms), reorder when you have 20 left. For slow movers (e.g., hair oils), reorder when you have 10 left. Count your stock physically every week. Compare with your records. If there's a mismatch, investigate: theft, spoilage, or recording error. Store products in a cool, dark place away from direct sunlight. Use shelves or plastic crates. Keep raw materials (shea butter, oils, beeswax) in sealed containers. Label everything with production date and expiry date (typically 12 months for balms, 6 months for butters, 12 months for oils). Use the FIFO method: First In, First Out. Sell older stock first. If you produce in batches, record batch numbers. This helps if a customer complains. For online orders, keep a separate 'reserved' stock so you don't oversell. Review your inventory weekly and adjust production based on sales trends. During rainy season, sales may drop; produce less. During holidays, produce more.

5. Handling Returns and Refunds

Have a clear return policy: accept returns within 7 days of purchase if the product is unopened and in original packaging. For opened products, only accept returns if there is a quality issue (e.g., rancid smell, separation, or skin reaction). In that case, offer a replacement or full refund. Never argue with a customer. If a customer complains, listen, apologize, and solve the problem quickly. For local shop returns, visit the shop within 48 hours, inspect the product, and either replace it or credit the shop. For online orders, ask the customer to send a photo of the problem. If valid, send a replacement via boda boda or courier, and ask them to return the defective item if possible. Keep a returns log: date, customer name, product, reason, action taken. Analyze returns monthly. If a specific product has many returns, check your production process: maybe your oil ratio is wrong, or your packaging is leaking. Fix the root cause. For refunds, use mobile money (M-Pesa, MTN MoMo) for speed. Refund within 3 days. If you sold through a shop, refund the shop and let them refund the customer. Always follow up with the customer after a return to ensure they are satisfied. A well-handled return can turn a dissatisfied customer into a loyal one.

6. Expanding to Online Marketplaces

Start with social media: create a business Instagram, Facebook, and TikTok account. Post photos of your products, show how they are made, and share customer testimonials. Use WhatsApp Business to catalog products and take orders. For wider reach, join local online marketplaces: in Kenya, use Jumia, Kilimall, or Sky.Garden; in Nigeria, Jumia and Konga; in South Africa, Takealot. To sell on Jumia, register as a seller, provide your ID, business permit, and bank details. They charge a commission (typically 10-15%) and a monthly fee. You ship your products to their warehouse or use their drop-ship service. For drop-ship, you keep stock and ship when an order comes. Pack securely and include an invoice. For international marketplaces like Etsy or Amazon, you need a payment gateway (PayPal or Payoneer) and international shipping. Start with local marketplaces first. Optimize your product listings: use clear photos on a white background, write detailed descriptions (ingredients, benefits, size), and set a competitive price. Offer promotions: free delivery for orders above KES 2,000. Respond to customer queries within 24 hours. Use a simple accounting app like Wave or QuickBooks to track online sales. Reinvest 20% of online profits into ads (Facebook or Instagram ads) to grow. Always comply with local regulations for cosmetics sales.

Practical Reference: Distribution and Logistics for African Cosmetics Businesses

Getting your lip balms, body butters, and hair oils from your kitchen or workshop into customers' hands is where most African cosmetics brands either scale or stall. This practical reference gives you the operational playbook: how to sell to local shops and salons, package for African shipping realities, work with boda boda riders and local couriers, manage stock without expensive software, handle returns professionally, and expand onto online marketplaces. Every tactic here is designed for the African context â informal retail, cash economies, poor roads, and mobile money.

1. Selling to Local Shops and Salons

Local shops and salons are your fastest route to repeat revenue. In most African markets, these outlets operate on consignment or low-margin wholesale. Offer a wholesale price 30â40% below your retail price, and start with a small "starter pack" of 10â20 units. Salons are especially valuable because stylists apply your hair oils directly to clients â they become your live demonstration team. Give them a free sample for every 20 units purchased. Use a simple invoice book or mobile money receipt; formal purchase orders are rare. Key terms to agree: unit price, minimum order, delivery frequency, and payment on delivery versus 7-day credit. Cash flow is king â avoid extending credit beyond 7 days to new outlets.

2. Packaging for Shipping within Africa

African shipping routes involve heat, dust, rough roads, and multiple handling points. Your packaging must survive all three. Use double-walled corrugated boxes for bulk orders, and for individual shipments use padded envelopes with bubble wrap for glass containers. Heat is your biggest enemy: body butters and lip balms melt above 35Â°C. Ship in insulated liners or schedule dispatch for early morning. Label every parcel with a waterproof marker â printed labels smudge in humidity. Include a packing slip with your WhatsApp number and a "fragile" sticker in the local language where possible.

3. Using Boda Boda and Local Couriers

Boda boda (motorcycle taxi) riders are the backbone of last-mile delivery in East and Central Africa. In West Africa, the equivalent is the okada or keke. Negotiate a fixed rate per delivery zone rather than per parcel â this encourages riders to batch deliveries. Build a relationship with 2â3 trusted riders rather than using a different one each time; they learn your routes and handle your products carefully. For inter-city, use established couriers like Sendy, G4S, or Easy Coach parcel services. Always get a tracking number and share it with the customer via WhatsApp. Pay riders via mobile money immediately after delivery confirmation â this builds loyalty and reliability.

4. Managing Inventory and Stock Levels

You do not need expensive software. A simple notebook or Google Sheet with columns for date, product, quantity in, quantity out, and balance works for most small brands. Use the "par level" method: decide the minimum stock you need before reordering. For cosmetics with a 12â24 month shelf life, keep no more than 8 weeks of stock on hand to avoid expiry and cash tied up. Track your top 5 best-selling SKUs weekly and your slow movers monthly. When a product has not sold in 60 days, bundle it with a best-seller or discount it. Always keep a "safety stock" of your top 3 products â running out of your best hair oil costs you repeat customers.

5. Handling Returns and Refunds

Returns in African cosmetics are usually due to leakage, melting, wrong item, or allergic reaction. Have a written policy: accept returns within 7 days for unopened products, and within 48 hours for quality issues with photo evidence. For melted body butters, offer a replacement rather than a refund â it preserves the sale and the relationship. Log every return with the reason; if one product keeps coming back, fix the packaging or formula. Refund via the same channel the customer paid (mobile money, cash, or bank transfer) within 3 working days. Never argue publicly on social media â take it to WhatsApp or a call.

â  SAFETY WARNING: Product Liability and Recalls

If a customer reports a skin reaction, burn, or eye injury from your product, stop selling that batch immediately. In many African countries (e.g., Kenya's KEBS, Nigeria's NAFDAC, South Africa's SAHPRA), cosmetics are regulated and a complaint can trigger an investigation. Keep batch numbers on every unit so you can trace and recall. Never dismiss a reaction as "the customer's fault" â document it, refund fully, and reformulate if needed. Selling unsafe cosmetics can lead to fines, business closure, or criminal liability.

6. Expanding to Online Marketplaces

Once you have consistent local sales, expand online. Start with WhatsApp Business catalog â it is free, familiar, and works on low bandwidth. Then list on Jumia (West/East Africa), Kilimall (Kenya), or Takealot (South Africa). These platforms charge 8â20% commission but give you reach and payment processing. For cross-border, use platforms like Afrikrea (now ANKA) which handles international shipping. Keep your online prices 10â15% higher than wholesale to cover commissions and shipping. Always include clear product photos, weight, and ingredients â African online buyers are increasingly ingredient-conscious. Sync your online stock with your notebook inventory to avoid overselling.
