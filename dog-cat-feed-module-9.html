🐕🐈 Therapeutic Diet Formulation

How to build, price and sell diets for animals with a diagnosed health problem, where the veterinarian owns the diagnosis and you own the formulation, the label and the batch record.

Lesson 9.1~12 min

Food Allergy vs Food Intolerance

In this lesson
  • Distinguish an immune-mediated food allergy from a non-immune food intolerance using the pattern of signs
  • Explain why an elimination diet trial is a veterinary diagnostic procedure and not a product you can sell as a cure
  • Calculate the full cost of an eight-week elimination trial for a client so you can quote it honestly

Owners bring you the same story again and again: the dog scratches, the ears smell, the skin under the belly is red, and somebody said it is the food. Sometimes it is. Often it is not. Your job as a producer is to know the difference well enough to make an honest product and to send the animal to a veterinarian when the answer belongs there.

A food allergy is an immune response to a dietary protein. The immune system has learned to treat a normal food protein as a threat, so the reaction does not depend on dose in any simple way. A very small amount of the offending protein can set it off, which is why cross-contamination in your mixer matters. Signs are commonly skin signs, sometimes with gut signs alongside, and they persist all year rather than following a season.

A food intolerance has no immune involvement. It is a digestive or metabolic failure to handle something: a fat load the pancreas cannot cope with, a lactose load the small intestine cannot split, a poorly digestible protein meal that ferments in the colon. Intolerance is dose-related. Half the amount often causes half the trouble, or none. Signs are usually gut signs, loose stool, wind, vomiting, and they follow the meal closely in time.

Why this distinction changes your formulation. If the problem is allergy, you must change the protein source to one the animal has never eaten, and you must exclude even traces of the old one. If the problem is intolerance, you may only need to lower fat, raise digestibility, or change the fibre source without changing the protein at all. Guessing wrong wastes the owner's money and your reputation.

Be honest about what is known. Our verified reference set gives the AAFCO nutrient profiles, digestibility trial data and toxicity thresholds, but it does not contain a ranked table of which proteins most commonly cause allergy in dogs and cats. You will see confident lists in marketing material. Do not repeat percentages you cannot source. Say instead, correctly, that beef, dairy, chicken and wheat are among the proteins most often reported, and that the only reliable way to identify the individual animal's trigger is a strict elimination trial supervised by a veterinarian, followed by a deliberate re-challenge with the suspected ingredient. Without the re-challenge there is no diagnosis, only a coincidence.

Worked cost of a trial. Take a 20 kg neutered adult dog. RER is 70 x 20 to the power 0.75 = 662 kcal per day. A neutered adult multiplier of 1.6 gives 1059 kcal per day. On a dry food at roughly 3800 kcal per kilogram, that is 1059 divided by 3800 = 0.279 kg per day, about 279 g. An eight-week trial is 56 days, so 56 x 0.279 = 15.6 kg of food. At a standard commercial dry food price of USD 1.20 per kilogram, the same eight weeks would cost 15.6 x 1.20 = USD 18.72. A novel-protein limited-ingredient diet at USD 3.00 per kilogram costs 15.6 x 3.00 = USD 46.80. The difference the owner must be told about in advance is USD 28. Add a veterinary consultation and you have the real number for the conversation.

African context. A producer in Kumasi, Ghana who buys rabbit from local smallholders at USD 3.20 per kilogram of dressed carcass has a genuine novel-protein advantage over an importer, because imported venison or duck diets land at three to five times that ingredient cost. The catch is supply continuity: an elimination trial that runs out of the test diet in week five has told you nothing, and the owner has to start over. Before you sell a trial diet, secure eight weeks of the same batch.

Finally, the discipline that protects you. A trial diet is only as clean as the equipment it passes through. Mixing a chicken-based grower in the morning and a rabbit elimination diet in the afternoon in the same unwashed mixer defeats the whole exercise. Run trial diets first after a full clean-down, record the batch, and keep a retained sample.

Ask for the animal's species, age, weight, every food and treat given in the last three months, whether signs are skin, gut or both, whether they come and go with the seasons, and whether a veterinarian has examined the animal. Lesson 9.2~12 min

Limited-Ingredient Diet (LID) Formulation

In this lesson
  • Build a single-protein, single-carbohydrate formulation that still meets the AAFCO adult profile
  • Calculate dry-matter protein, calcium and phosphorus from ingredient inclusion percentages
  • Price a limited-ingredient batch and set a retail price that survives a small production run

A limited-ingredient diet uses one animal protein source and one carbohydrate source, plus fat, fibre and a vitamin-mineral premix. Limited does not mean incomplete. The AAFCO profile applies exactly as it does to any other food, and cutting the ingredient list makes hitting it harder, not easier, because you have removed the accidental overlaps that used to cover your gaps.

Start with the target. For an adult dog on a dry-matter basis the binding minima are crude protein 18.0 percent, crude fat 5.5 percent, calcium 0.5 percent, phosphorus 0.4 percent, with the calcium to phosphorus ratio between 1:1 and 2:1, phosphorus never above 1.6 percent. Lysine must reach 0.63 percent and methionine plus cystine 0.65 percent. For an adult cat the minima are protein 26.0 percent, fat 9.0 percent, calcium 0.6 percent, phosphorus 0.5 percent, plus the three feline non-negotiables: taurine 0.10 percent in an extruded dry food (0.20 percent if canned), arachidonic acid 0.02 percent, and preformed vitamin A at 3332 IU per kilogram, because cats cannot cleave beta-carotene. Cat niacin must reach 60 mg per kilogram and cannot be covered by tryptophan.

Now a worked dog formulation using ingredients an African producer can actually buy. Assume rabbit meal at 55 percent crude protein, cassava flour at 2 percent protein, tallow or palm-based fat, a fibre source and a premix.

  1. Rabbit meal 30 percent, contributing 0.30 x 55 = 16.5 percent protein.
  2. Cassava flour 52 percent, contributing 0.52 x 2 = 1.04 percent protein.
  3. Fat 7 percent, contributing negligible protein but most of your energy flexibility.
  4. Beet pulp 6 percent as the fibre source.
  5. Vitamin-mineral premix plus limestone and dicalcium phosphate 5 percent.

Total crude protein is 16.5 + 1.04 = 17.5 percent. That is below the 18.0 percent minimum, so the formulation fails as written. Raise rabbit meal to 32 percent and drop cassava to 50 percent: 0.32 x 55 = 17.6, plus 0.50 x 2 = 1.0, giving 18.6 percent. Now it clears with a small margin. This is the whole discipline of limited-ingredient work: with only one protein source, every adjustment is visible and you cannot hide behind a second meal.

Cassava is a good choice here on evidence, not fashion. In a six-carbohydrate comparison in extruded dog diets, brewer's rice and cassava flour gave the greatest digestibility of dry matter, organic matter and gross energy, followed by corn and sorghum, with pea and lentil diets lowest. Starch digestibility exceeded 98 percent across all six diets, so the claim that dogs cannot digest properly cooked cereal or root starch is not supported.

The premix is where limited-ingredient diets are won or lost. Zinc alone must reach 80 mg per kilogram for an adult dog and 75 for a cat; a rabbit-and-cassava mix will not get there by accident. Do not attempt a single-protein diet without a purchased, guaranteed premix. And do not top up calcium by eye: for a food that may be fed to large-breed puppies the calcium ceiling is 1.8 percent dry matter, against 2.5 percent for other life stages.

Costing. Suppose rabbit meal costs USD 2.40 per kilogram, cassava flour USD 0.45, fat USD 1.30, beet pulp USD 0.80 and premix USD 6.00. Per 100 kg batch: rabbit 32 x 2.40 = 76.80; cassava 50 x 0.45 = 22.50; fat 7 x 1.30 = 9.10; beet pulp 6 x 0.80 = 4.80; premix and minerals 5 x 6.00 = 30.00. Ingredient cost is USD 143.20 per 100 kg, or USD 1.43 per kilogram. Add milling, energy, packaging and losses at, say, 35 percent and your cost is about USD 1.93 per kilogram. Retailing at USD 3.20 leaves a gross margin near 40 percent, which a low-volume specialty product needs.

African context. A Nairobi producer running rabbit LID found the real constraint was not price but batch continuity, because rabbit supply arrived in 40 kg lots from different farms with different fat content. The fix was to buy and blend three lots into one homogeneous batch before milling, and to run 300 kg at a time so a single elimination trial never spans two batches.

Transition matters too. Move an animal onto a new diet over about fourteen days, increasing the new food by roughly a quarter of the ration every three to four days, so that gut upset is not mistaken for a reaction to the novel protein.

If it fails, adjust the protein inclusion until it clears and note by how much.

Lesson 9.3~12 min

Kidney Disease Diet Formulation

In this lesson
  • State clearly which decisions in a renal diet belong to the veterinarian and which belong to the producer
  • Explain why a therapeutic renal diet usually cannot carry a complete-and-balanced maintenance statement
  • Calculate a sourced omega-3 dose for a dog with kidney disease and check it against the safe upper limit

Read this lesson as a limits lesson. Chronic kidney disease is diagnosed on blood and urine testing, staged by a veterinarian, and managed with a diet that is deliberately built below the normal maintenance profile. You can manufacture such a diet. You cannot decide, from the outside, that an animal needs one.

Why the profile matters here more than anywhere else. The AAFCO adult maintenance minima exist to keep a healthy animal healthy: for a dog, 18.0 percent crude protein and 0.4 percent phosphorus on a dry-matter basis; for a cat, 26.0 percent protein and 0.5 percent phosphorus. A therapeutic renal diet works in part by restricting phosphorus and moderating protein, which means going under those numbers on purpose. A food that sits below the profile is not complete and balanced for adult maintenance, and it must not be labelled as if it were. In most jurisdictions such a product is sold under veterinary direction, or carries a statement that it is intended for intermittent or supplemental feeding, or is manufactured to a veterinarian's specification for a named patient. Establish which of those routes your national feed regulator allows before you make a single bag. Selling an under-profile food as a general maintenance diet is both a regulatory offence and a way to harm a healthy animal.

What our verified reference set does and does not give you. It gives the AAFCO minima and maxima, the dog phosphorus maximum of 1.6 percent dry matter, the energy equations, and a sourced therapeutic omega-3 dose for kidney disease. It does not contain validated target ranges for protein or phosphorus restriction by disease stage. Those staged targets exist in the veterinary literature and in the IRIS staging system, and you should obtain them through the prescribing veterinarian rather than from a course, a competitor's label or a forum. Do not invent a number here. A phosphorus figure that is wrong by a small margin, fed daily for months, is not a small error.

The part you can act on with confidence is the omega-3 dose, because it is sourced with real arithmetic. The kidney-disease dose of combined EPA and DHA is 140 mg per kilogram of body weight to the power 0.75, which is about 66 mg per kilogram of body weight per day. Take a 20 kg dog. 20 to the power 0.75 is 9.46. 9.46 x 140 = 1324 mg of EPA plus DHA per day. Now check it against the safe upper limit, which is 370 mg per kg BW^0.75, that is 9.46 x 370 = 3500 mg per day, or equivalently 280 mg per 100 kcal. The therapeutic dose sits comfortably inside the ceiling. Compare with the osteoarthritis dose of 310 mg per kg^0.75, which for the same dog is 2932 mg and leaves very little margin. Doses stack: an animal on a fish-oil-rich diet plus a fish oil supplement plus a joint product can cross the limit without anybody intending it.

Two cautions on fish oil that are easy to miss. First, excess omega-3 is associated with altered platelet function, gastrointestinal disturbance, effects on wound healing, and supplement-drug interactions, with omega-3 combined with NSAIDs linked to gastric ulceration and blood loss in affected dogs. Second, high-fat fish carry vitamin D, and the AAFCO dog safe upper limit for vitamin D was cut to 3000 IU per kilogram dry matter in 2016, a change that specifically lowered how much high-fat fish such as salmon could safely be used in pet food recipes. The EU legal ceiling is lower still at 2270 IU per kilogram dry matter. Vitamin D is the nutrient you are most likely to poison an animal with by accident, and real-world cases come from premix and formulation error, not from raw ingredients.

Worked cost, African context. A producer in Arusha, Tanzania supplying two veterinary clinics with a vet-specified renal diet runs 150 kg batches. Fish oil at USD 9.00 per litre delivering roughly 300 mg EPA plus DHA per millilitre means the 20 kg dog's 1324 mg daily dose is about 4.4 ml, costing about USD 0.04 per day, or USD 1.20 a month. The oil is not what makes a renal diet expensive; the low-ash protein sources and the custom premix are. Quote on those.

Finally, the sentence to keep saying: a renal diet is prescribed, monitored with repeat blood tests, and adjusted by the veterinarian. Your role is consistent manufacture, accurate labelling, retained samples and batch records, so that when a vet changes the specification you can prove exactly what the animal has been eating.

Lesson 9.4~12 min

Digestive Disorder Diets

In this lesson
  • Select fibre and starch sources using measured digestibility and faecal quality data
  • Apply the tested inclusion limits for rendered by-product meals to protect stool quality
  • Rebuild an existing formulation to raise digestibility without changing the protein source

Digestive complaints are the commonest reason an owner changes food, and they are the easiest therapeutic category for a small producer to serve well, because the levers are ingredient choice and processing rather than clinical staging. Two warnings first. Vomiting, blood in the stool, weight loss, or diarrhoea lasting more than a couple of days is a veterinary case, not a food case. And a dog with diagnosed pancreatitis or exocrine pancreatic insufficiency is managed by a veterinarian, who will specify the fat level; our verified sources give no fat targets for those conditions, so do not publish any.

What the evidence does give you is unusually practical. Start with fibre. In a Beagle trial comparing beet pulp, cellulose and Miscanthus grass, all at 10 percent inclusion, beet pulp gave the highest dry matter digestibility at 81.3 percent against 78.2 percent for Miscanthus and 77.2 percent for cellulose, and the highest organic matter digestibility at 86.1 against 82.1 and 80.8. But beet pulp gave slightly lower crude protein digestibility, 84.5 percent against 87.9 and 87.6, and the dogs on beet pulp had softer stools, scoring 3.15 against 3.68 for cellulose and 3.64 for Miscanthus. So beet pulp is a moderately fermentable fibre that raises overall digestibility and softens stool at 10 percent. If your customer's complaint is loose stool, that trade-off runs the wrong way, and a less fermentable fibre at the same inclusion is the better choice. Note also that the commonly quoted 7.5 percent practical ceiling for beet pulp could not be verified; what is verified is the 10 percent trial data above.

Next, rendered meals. In a Beagle trial of bone protein meal and feather meal, apparent crude protein digestibility held up even at high inclusion, with bone protein meal at 24 percent giving 82.4 percent protein digestibility against 78.0 percent for the control. Digestibility was not the limiting factor. Faecal quality was. The conclusion was that bone protein meal could be included up to 12 percent and feather meal up to 5 percent without negative effects on faecal scores. Those two numbers, 12 and 5, are the ones to write on your formulation sheet. Feather meal in particular is cheap and tempting in African markets, and 5 percent is where it stops being a bargain.

Starch is the third lever. Brewer's rice and cassava flour gave the highest dry matter, organic matter and gross energy digestibility of six carbohydrate sources in extruded dog diets, followed by corn and sorghum, with pea and lentil lowest. Starch digestibility exceeded 98 percent in every diet, so the problem with pulses is not the starch, it is the rest of the seed. If you are building a high-digestibility gut diet, brewer's rice or cassava is the evidence-based choice.

Worked reformulation. Take an existing adult dog diet containing 20 percent field peas, 8 percent feather meal, 10 percent beet pulp and 12 percent bone protein meal, sold to a customer complaining of soft, voluminous stools. Three changes, each defensible:

  1. Replace the 20 percent peas with 20 percent brewer's rice, moving from the lowest-digestibility carbohydrate group to the highest.
  2. Cut feather meal from 8 to 5 percent, the tested faecal-quality limit, and replace the 3 percent with additional rice plus a small increase in a higher-quality protein to hold crude protein above 18.0 percent DM.
  3. Cut beet pulp from 10 to 6 percent, because at 10 percent it measurably softens stool, and hold total fibre with a less fermentable source.

Check the protein arithmetic after every swap. If feather meal ran at 80 percent crude protein, removing 3 percentage points of it removes 0.03 x 80 = 2.4 percentage points of dietary crude protein. Rice at about 8 percent protein returns only 0.03 x 8 = 0.24. So you are 2.16 points short and must recover it, for example by adding about 4 percentage points of a 55 percent protein meal, which returns 0.04 x 55 = 2.2 points.

African context and cost. A Lusaka producer selling a 15 kg bag at USD 24 who makes these swaps might see ingredient cost rise from USD 1.05 to USD 1.22 per kilogram, that is USD 2.55 more per bag. If the change stops one customer in ten from leaving after their first bag, and each retained customer buys twelve bags a year at a USD 6 gross margin, the arithmetic favours the reformulation easily. Sell the improvement as firmer stools and less waste in the yard, which is what the owner actually sees.

Run any change on a small number of animals first, with the owner recording stool score daily for two weeks, and keep those records.

Lesson 9.5~12 min

Obesity Management Diets

In this lesson
  • Calculate a weight-loss energy allowance from RER for a dog and a cat
  • Show why protein density must rise when energy intake falls, using the energy-basis protein minima
  • Convert a calorie target into a daily gram feeding guide at a known food energy density

Obesity is the most common nutritional disorder you will meet, and it is the one where correct arithmetic does most of the work. The whole method is: calculate resting energy requirement, apply a weight-loss factor, convert to grams of your food, and then adjust against body condition every two weeks.

Start with resting energy requirement. RER in kcal per day is 70 x body weight in kilograms to the power 0.75, and this form is valid at any weight. For animals between 2 and 45 kg you can also use the linear approximation RER = (30 x kg) + 70, which is easier on a phone without a power key.

Dogs. Take a 30 kg overweight neutered Labrador. RER = 70 x 30^0.75 = 897 kcal per day. As a neutered adult at maintenance the multiplier is 1.6, giving 1436 kcal per day, and that is roughly what has made him fat. The weight-loss factor is 1.0 x RER, so the target is 897 kcal per day. That is a cut of 1436 minus 897 = 539 kcal, a reduction of 539 divided by 1436 = 37.5 percent. On a dry food at 3800 kcal per kilogram, 897 divided by 3800 = 0.236 kg, so 236 g per day, best split into three or four meals.

Cats. Take a 6 kg overweight neutered cat. RER = 70 x 6^0.75 = 268 kcal per day. Neutered adult maintenance at 1.2 is 322 kcal. The feline weight-loss factor is 0.8 x RER, giving 214 kcal per day. On a dry food at 3800 kcal per kilogram that is 214 divided by 3800 = 0.056 kg, about 56 g per day. On a wet food at about 1000 kcal per kilogram it is 214 g per day, which at 85 g per pouch is about 2.5 pouches. Cats must lose weight slowly and under veterinary supervision, because rapid weight loss in cats carries a real risk of hepatic lipidosis. Never simply halve a cat's food and walk away.

Now the formulation consequence that most producers miss. Nutrient minima are stated on a dry-matter basis, but the animal eats calories. The protein minima can also be read on an energy basis: 45 g of protein per 1000 kcal ME for adult dog maintenance, and 65 g per 1000 kcal for adult cat maintenance. When you cut the Labrador from 1436 to 897 kcal you cut his intake of everything by 37.5 percent, including protein, unless the food is denser in protein per calorie. At 45 g per 1000 kcal he receives 897 divided by 1000 x 45 = 40 g of protein a day, whereas at maintenance he was receiving 65 g. That is why a weight-loss food is built with more protein and less fat per calorie: you are protecting lean muscle while the fat comes off. Fibre earns its place in the same way, adding bulk and slowing the meal without adding energy, and the fibre lesson applies here too, since a moderately fermentable fibre like beet pulp raises digestibility but softens stool at 10 percent inclusion.

Rate and monitoring. Weigh the animal on the same scale every two weeks and record it. If weight is not moving after a month, recheck the household: treats, table food and a second person feeding are the usual cause, not the calculation. Keep a written list of everything the animal eats, including scraps.

One safety item belongs here because weight-loss households give more treats, not fewer. Xylitol causes hypoglycaemia in dogs above 100 mg per kilogram body weight and hepatic injury above 500 mg per kilogram; dogs are the only domestic species in which xylitol toxicosis has been reported. Sugar-free products marketed as diet foods for humans are therefore a specific hazard in exactly the households you are advising. Chocolate is the other one, with mild signs from 20 mg per kilogram of theobromine and seizures at 60 mg per kilogram or more.

African context and the business case. A veterinary practice in Kampala runs a paid weight clinic: the owner pays about USD 15 for enrolment, receives fortnightly weigh-ins, and buys a locally produced reduced-energy diet at USD 2.60 per kilogram. Our 30 kg Labrador eats 236 g a day, so 7.1 kg a month, that is about USD 18.40 a month in food. The producer supplies the clinic at USD 1.80 per kilogram, earning USD 12.80 a month per enrolled dog, with the clinic doing the monitoring and the retail. Twenty enrolled dogs is USD 256 a month of predictable, non-seasonal volume from a single clinic relationship.

Lesson 9.6~12 min

Joint Support and Mobility Diets

In this lesson
  • Calculate a sourced therapeutic EPA+DHA dose for osteoarthritis and compare it with the safe upper limit
  • Explain why weight control is the highest-value intervention in a mobility diet
  • Separate ingredient claims you can support from those your verified sources do not cover

Mobility products sell well because the owner can see the problem: a stiff old dog struggling to stand. That visibility is also the trap, because it makes exaggerated claims profitable in the short term and ruinous in the long term. Build this product on the two things that are actually supported, and be plain about the rest.

The first supported lever is body weight. Every extra kilogram is carried by the same joints, and the weight-loss arithmetic from the previous lesson is the highest-value thing you can do for an arthritic dog. A 30 kg dog reduced to 26 kg is carrying 13 percent less load with every step, and you have not added a single ingredient. Sell the mobility diet with a weighing schedule, not just a bag.

The second supported lever is long-chain omega-3. The therapeutic osteoarthritis dose of combined EPA and DHA is 310 mg per kilogram of body weight to the power 0.75, which is about 147 mg per kilogram of body weight per day. Work it for a 20 kg dog: 20^0.75 = 9.46, and 9.46 x 310 = 2932 mg of EPA plus DHA per day. Now compare that with the safe upper limit of 370 mg per kg^0.75, which for the same dog is 9.46 x 370 = 3500 mg per day, equivalently 280 mg per 100 kcal. The osteoarthritis dose is inside the limit, but only just: it uses 2932 divided by 3500 = 84 percent of the ceiling. That is the single most important number in this lesson. If a dog eats a joint diet formulated at the osteoarthritis dose and the owner also gives a fish oil capsule, you are over the limit.

Compare the other sourced doses: cardiovascular support is 115 mg per kg^0.75, about 1088 mg for that dog, and kidney disease 140 mg per kg^0.75, about 1324 mg. Joint doses are two to three times the others, which is why joint products run out of margin.

Adverse effects of excess are real and specific: supplement-drug interactions, altered platelet function, gastrointestinal disturbance, and detrimental effects on wound healing, with omega-3 combined with NSAIDs linked to gastric ulceration and blood loss in affected dogs. Most arthritic dogs seen by a vet are on an NSAID. That makes the conversation with the veterinarian compulsory, not optional, before you supply a high-omega-3 diet to a treated patient.

There is a second ceiling on fish oil. High-fat fish such as salmon carry vitamin D, and the 2016 cut in the AAFCO safe upper limit to 3000 IU per kilogram dry matter specifically lowered how much high-fat fish could be used in pet food recipes. The EU legal maximum is lower again at 2270 IU per kilogram dry matter. So you cannot simply raise fish inclusion until the omega-3 target is met; you have to check the vitamin D consequence, which usually means using a refined fish oil for the omega-3 and keeping whole fish inclusion moderate.

One more practical point on oxidation. The standard practice of raising vitamin E when polyunsaturated fat rises is real, but the specific ratio is not given in our verified sources, so specify it with your premix supplier rather than guessing. Adult dogs and cats both have a vitamin E minimum of 50 and 40 IU per kilogram respectively, and a high-PUFA diet should sit above the minimum, not at it. Rancid oil is also a shelf-life problem in warm climates: buy oil in quantities you will use within weeks, store it dark and cool, and date every drum.

On glucosamine and chondroitin, be straight. Our verified reference set does not contain dosing or efficacy data for them. You may include them because the market expects them, but do not print a therapeutic claim you cannot support, and do not price the product as if those ingredients are the active part when the omega-3 and the weight control are what you can defend.

African context and cost. A producer in Nairobi supplying a senior mobility diet buys refined fish oil at USD 11 per litre at roughly 300 mg EPA plus DHA per millilitre. The 20 kg dog needs 2932 mg a day, that is 9.8 ml, costing USD 0.108 a day, about USD 3.24 a month, or USD 39 a year per dog. If the dog eats 279 g of food a day, the oil must be present at 9.8 ml in 279 g, which is 3.5 percent of the diet as fish oil alone. That is a large inclusion with real consequences for extrusion, storage stability and cost, and it is why many producers supply the oil as a separate topper rather than trying to build the full therapeutic dose into the kibble. Price both options and let the veterinarian choose.

Knowledge check

Questions from all lessons. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. Which pattern best fits a food intolerance rather than a food allergy?

Intolerance is non-immune and dose-related, so reducing the amount usually reduces the signs. Allergy is immune-mediated and can be triggered by traces.

2. What makes an elimination diet trial a diagnosis rather than a guess?

Improvement alone can be coincidence or seasonal change. Re-challenge under veterinary supervision is what confirms the trigger.

3. A 20 kg neutered adult dog needs roughly how many kcal per day using RER x 1.6?

RER = 70 x 20^0.75 = 662 kcal; 662 x 1.6 = 1059 kcal per day. The 662 figure is RER itself, and 1986 would be a puppy under four months at x3.

4. Why does mixer hygiene matter more for an elimination diet than for a standard grower?

Food allergy is not simply dose-dependent, so carry-over of even small amounts of the previous protein can produce signs and destroy the trial's meaning.

5. Which statement about allergen frequency should you avoid making to customers?

Precise frequency statistics are widely repeated but not supportable from the verified reference set. Stating a specific percentage you cannot source is a claim you cannot defend.

6. A dog LID contains 30 percent rabbit meal at 55 percent CP and 52 percent cassava flour at 2 percent CP. What is the crude protein?

0.30 x 55 = 16.5 and 0.52 x 2 = 1.04, giving 17.54 percent. That is below the 18.0 percent adult minimum, so the formulation must be adjusted.

7. Which carbohydrate sources gave the highest dry matter and energy digestibility in the six-source dog trial?

Brewer's rice and cassava flour were highest, followed by corn and sorghum, with pea and lentil lowest. Starch digestibility still exceeded 98 percent in all six.

8. Why can a limited-ingredient cat diet not rely on beta-carotene for vitamin A?

Cats lack the intestinal dioxygenase needed for beta-carotene cleavage and require preformed vitamin A from animal sources. In a cat food, beta-carotene is a colourant or antioxidant, not a vitamin.

9. What is the calcium ceiling for a food labelled for growth including large-size dogs?

The AAFCO footnote sets 1.8 percent DM for formulas that may be fed to puppies weighing 70 lb or more as mature lean adults. Other life stages allow up to 2.5 percent.

10. Why is a purchased vitamin-mineral premix effectively compulsory in a limited-ingredient diet?

With one protein and one carbohydrate there is no second ingredient to make up a zinc or vitamin shortfall. Adult zinc minima alone are 80 mg/kg for dogs and 75 mg/kg for cats.

11. Why can a therapeutic renal diet usually not be labelled complete and balanced for adult maintenance?

Restriction below the profile is the point of the diet, and a food below the profile does not meet the maintenance claim. Such products are sold under veterinary direction or with an intermittent-feeding statement.

12. What is the daily EPA+DHA kidney-disease dose for a 20 kg dog at 140 mg per kg BW^0.75?

20^0.75 = 9.46, and 9.46 x 140 = 1324 mg per day. 2932 mg is the osteoarthritis dose and 3500 mg is the safe upper limit for this dog.

13. Which nutrient is most likely to be given at toxic levels through a formulation or premix error?

Vitamin D toxicity in dogs and cats has repeatedly arisen from premix and formulation errors rather than raw ingredients, causing multiple commercial recalls.

14. A dog is on a diet containing fish oil, plus a joint supplement, plus an owner-added oil. What is the correct concern?

Doses stack across every source. Excess is linked to altered platelet function, gastrointestinal effects and interactions, including gastric ulceration when combined with NSAIDs.

15. Where should staged protein and phosphorus restriction targets for kidney disease come from?

Staged targets are clinical decisions tied to the animal's blood and urine results. The verified nutrient standards give minima and maxima for healthy animals, not disease-stage targets.

16. In the Beagle fibre trial at 10 percent inclusion, which fibre produced the softest stools?

Beet pulp scored 3.15 against 3.68 for cellulose and 3.64 for Miscanthus. It raises overall digestibility but softens stool at that inclusion.

17. What inclusion limits kept faecal scores acceptable in the poultry by-product trial?

Protein digestibility stayed acceptable at higher levels, but faecal quality set the practical limits at 12 percent bone protein meal and 5 percent feather meal.

18. Removing 3 percentage points of feather meal at 80 percent CP removes how much dietary crude protein?

0.03 x 80 = 2.4 percentage points, which must be recovered from another protein source to stay above the 18.0 percent adult dog minimum.

19. Which claim about starch is supported by the six-carbohydrate dog trial?

Starch digestibility exceeded 98 percent in every diet. The differences between sources were in overall dry matter, organic matter and energy digestibility, not in starch itself.

20. A customer reports blood in the stool and weight loss. What is the correct response?

Blood in the stool and weight loss are clinical signs requiring diagnosis. Selling a diet in place of examination delays treatment and exposes you to serious liability.

21. What is the RER of a 30 kg dog?

RER = 70 x 30^0.75 = 897 kcal per day. 1436 is that dog at neutered maintenance (x1.6) and 2692 would be a puppy under four months (x3).

22. Which weight-loss multiplier applies to cats?

Cats use 0.8 x RER for weight loss while dogs use 1.0 x RER. Feline weight loss must be gradual and supervised because of the risk of hepatic lipidosis.

23. A 214 kcal daily allowance on a wet food at about 1000 kcal per kilogram is roughly how much food?

214 divided by 1000 kcal/kg = 0.214 kg, that is 214 g, roughly 2.5 pouches of 85 g. The 56 g answer applies to a dry food at 3800 kcal/kg.

24. Why must protein density per calorie rise in a weight-loss diet?

Minima can be read as 45 g protein per 1000 kcal for adult dogs and 65 g for adult cats. A 37.5 percent calorie cut is a 37.5 percent protein cut unless the food is reformulated.

25. Why is xylitol a particular hazard in a weight-loss household?

Households trying to lose weight keep sugar-free products. Xylitol toxicosis is reported in dogs only, with hypoglycaemia above 100 mg/kg and hepatic injury above 500 mg/kg.

26. What is the osteoarthritis EPA+DHA dose for a 20 kg dog?

9.46 x 310 = 2932 mg per day. 1088 mg is the cardiovascular dose, 1324 mg the kidney dose, and 3500 mg is the safe upper limit for that dog.

27. Why is the combination of a joint diet, a fish oil capsule and an NSAID a concern?

The joint dose already uses about 84 percent of the ceiling, and excess omega-3 alters platelet function and, with NSAIDs, has been linked to gastric ulceration and blood loss in affected dogs.

28. Which intervention for an arthritic dog is best supported and costs nothing in ingredients?

Weight reduction lowers the load carried by every joint at every step and is the highest-value lever available. Ingredient claims should not displace it.

29. Why can you not simply raise whole fish inclusion until the omega-3 target is met?

The 2016 safe upper limit cut specifically lowered the amount of high-fat fish that could safely be used in recipes. Refined oil plus moderate fish inclusion is the usual answer.

30. What is the honest position on glucosamine in your product literature?

The verified reference set contains no dosing or efficacy data for glucosamine. Including an ingredient is a commercial choice; claiming an effect requires evidence.

Module 9 capstone

Build a costed therapeutic product case for one condition you can actually serve in your own town. Step 1: visit or telephone at least three veterinary clinics within reach and ask each one which two diet-responsive conditions they see most often, what they currently recommend, whether that product is available locally, and what it retails for per kilogram. Write the answers in a table with the clinic name and date. Step 2: pick the single condition with the most demand and the least local supply. Step 3: collect real market prices per kilogram for every ingredient you would need, including a vitamin-mineral premix quotation from a supplier, and write a full formulation on paper with inclusion percentages summing to 100. Step 4: calculate the dry-matter crude protein, crude fat, calcium and phosphorus of your formulation from ingredient table values and compare each against the AAFCO adult minima for the species (dog 18 percent protein, 5.5 percent fat, 0.5 percent calcium, 0.4 percent phosphorus; cat 26 percent protein, 9 percent fat, 0.6 percent calcium, 0.5 percent phosphorus). Step 5: calculate cost per kilogram, set a retail price, and calculate the daily feeding cost for a 20 kg dog or a 4 kg cat. Step 6: write one page addressed to a veterinarian stating what the diet is, what it is not, which claims you will and will not make, and what you would need a vet to confirm before any animal eats it. Finish by naming the parts of the formulation you cannot verify without a laboratory analysis.
