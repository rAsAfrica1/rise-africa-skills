1. Folding Serviettes: Napkin, 1/4 Fold, and 1/8 Fold

Serviettes are the easiest tissue product to start with because the folding is simple and the demand is constant. You need a flat surface, a ruler, a sharp knife or guillotine cutter, and clean hands. Start with a roll of 2-ply or 3-ply tissue paper, usually 33 cm wide. Cut sheets to 33 cm x 33 cm for a standard napkin fold. For a napkin fold, lay the sheet flat, fold it in half diagonally to make a triangle, then fold the two side corners up to the top point, and finally fold the bottom point up to cover the middle. This gives a classic dinner napkin. For a 1/4 fold, cut sheets to 40 cm x 40 cm, fold in half, then in half again, so you have a quarter-size square. This is the most common restaurant serviette. For a 1/8 fold, start with a 40 cm x 40 cm sheet, fold in half three times, ending with a small square about 10 cm x 10 cm. This is used for tea cups or cocktail service. Always crease each fold firmly with your thumb or a clean roller. Stack folded serviettes in dozens (12 pieces) and wrap with a paper band or poly bag. In Africa, many small producers use a hand-cranked embosser to add a pattern before folding. Keep your work area dust-free and wear cotton gloves to avoid oil marks. A team of two can fold 1,000 serviettes per hour once practised. Sell to local hotels, restaurants, and caterers. Price per 100: napkin fold $1.50$2.50, 1/4 fold $1.00$2.00, 1/8 fold $0.80$1.50, depending on paper quality.

2. Making Facial Tissues: Interleaving and Box Packaging

Facial tissues are softer and lighter than serviettes. You need 2-ply or 3-ply tissue paper, usually 20 gsm per ply. Cut sheets to 20 cm x 20 cm. The key step is interleaving: you fold one tissue slightly over the next so that when you pull one out, the next pops up. To interleave, take two tissues, place one on top of the other with a 2 cm offset, then fold the bottom tissue's edge over the top tissue's edge. Stack 100 tissues per box. For a pop-up box, you need a cardboard box with an oval opening. You can buy plain boxes locally or print your own with a simple logo. Fill the box by placing the stack inside, ensuring the first tissue pokes through the opening. For flat boxes, just stack tissues without interleaving. Use a small amount of glue or a paper band to hold the stack. In Africa, many producers use manual cutting machines and a simple wooden jig to keep stacks neat. Wear gloves to prevent smudges. A team of three can produce 500 boxes per day. Cost per box: paper $0.30, box $0.15, labour $0.10. Sell at $1.00$1.50 per box. Target supermarkets, pharmacies, and hotels. Always label with your brand and contact.

3. Producing Kitchen Towels: Perforating and Roll Sizes

Kitchen towels are strong, absorbent rolls. You need 2-ply or 3-ply tissue paper, often 2224 gsm per ply, and a core board tube. Standard roll width is 23 cm or 28 cm. Cut the tissue to the desired width. For a standard roll, use 23 cm width and 60 sheets per roll. Each sheet is about 22 cm long. To perforate, you can use a manual perforating machine or a simple jig with a serrated blade. Perforations should be every 22 cm. Roll the tissue onto a cardboard core (inner diameter 4 cm, outer 5 cm). You can roll by hand or use a small motorised roller. For a 2-ply roll, align two layers and roll together. For embossing, pass the paper through an embosser to increase absorbency. After rolling, cut the roll to the final width if needed. Pack rolls in poly bags of 2, 4, or 6. In Africa, many small producers start with a hand-roller and a foot-operated perforator. Production: one person can roll 100 rolls per day. Cost per roll: paper $0.20, core $0.05, bag $0.02, labour $0.05. Sell at $0.50$0.80 per roll. Target supermarkets, hotels, and local markets. Keep rolls dry and clean.

4. Choosing Packaging: Poly Bags, Boxes, and Labels

Packaging protects your product and sells it. For serviettes, use clear poly bags (20 cm x 30 cm) with a self-adhesive flap. For facial tissues, use a cardboard box (22 cm x 12 cm x 8 cm) with an oval opening. For kitchen towels, use poly bags with a handle or a cardboard wrap. In Africa, poly bags are cheap and available in most markets. Buy in bulk: 1,000 bags cost $10$20. Boxes can be plain or printed. A local printer can make 1,000 boxes for $100$150. Labels must include: product name, quantity, your business name, phone number, and a production date. Use waterproof stickers or print directly on the bag. For a professional look, use a single colour logo. You can also add a barcode if selling to supermarkets. Always seal bags with a heat sealer or a simple impulse sealer ($30$50). For boxes, use a glue gun or double-sided tape. Store packaging in a dry place. Remember: packaging is your silent salesman. A clean, well-labelled pack can double your sales. Test different sizes: small packs for individuals, large packs for hotels. Keep costs low: aim for packaging to be no more than 20% of your selling price.

5. Branding Your Products with Local Languages and Designs

Branding makes your product stand out. Use local languages like Swahili, Hausa, Yoruba, Zulu, or Amharic on your labels. For example, 'Karatasi ya choo' (toilet paper in Swahili) or 'Tishu' (tissue). Add a simple design: a pattern from local fabric like kente, ankara, or mudcloth. You can print these on labels or directly on poly bags. Use a local printer or a digital printer. For low volume, use a stamp or sticker. Include a short slogan in a local language, e.g., 'Soft kama mkono' (soft like a hand). This builds trust and pride. Also, use a logo that reflects your community: a baobab tree, a calabash, or a sun. Keep colours bright but not too many. Choose two main colours. For example, green and yellow. Print your phone number and a WhatsApp line. Many customers order via WhatsApp. Also, add a QR code that links to your social media. In Africa, word-of-mouth is powerful, so make your brand memorable. Test names with local customers. Avoid offensive words. Register your business name if possible. A strong brand can allow you to charge 1020% more. Update your design every year or two to stay fresh.

6. Small-Batch Production for Local Events and Hotels

Small-batch production means making exactly what a customer needs, when they need it. For a wedding, you might need 500 serviettes in a specific colour. For a hotel, 1,000 facial tissues per week. Start by talking to event planners, hotels, and restaurants. Offer samples. Set a minimum order: 100 pieces for serviettes, 50 boxes for facial tissues, 50 rolls for kitchen towels. Price per unit is higher for small batches: add 2030% to cover setup time. For a wedding, you can customise with the couple's names or date. Use a simple printing method like a stamp or a digital printer. For hotels, offer a weekly subscription: deliver every Monday. Keep a simple record book: customer name, order, date, payment. Use a WhatsApp group for orders. Production planning: if you have one folding table, you can make 500 serviettes in 2 hours. For facial tissues, 100 boxes in 3 hours. For kitchen towels, 50 rolls in 4 hours. Work with a team of 23 people. Buy raw materials in bulk to reduce cost. For events, deliver on time and include a free sample. Build relationships: a happy hotel will reorder every month. In Africa, many small producers start with one hotel and grow. Reinvest profits into better equipment. Keep your promises.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Producing Serviettes, Facial Tissues, and Kitchen Towels

This practical reference is designed for small and growing African tissue converters who want to move from buying finished tissue to producing serviettes, facial tissues, and kitchen towels for local markets. It focuses on the decisions that determine whether a small production line makes money: fold types, sheet counts, roll sizes, packaging choices, branding, and batch planning for hotels, events, and local retailers.

Across Africa, demand for tissue products is growing with urbanisation, food service, hospitality, and health awareness. Imported and branded tissue is often expensive, while locally converted products can compete on price, freshness, and cultural relevance. The key is to match production to the market you can actually serve: guesthouses, weddings, restaurants, salons, clinics, and small supermarkets.

1. Serviette Folding: Napkin, 1/4 Fold, and 1/8 Fold

Serviettes are the lowest-barrier tissue product for small producers. They require simple cutting and folding, and they sell in high volumes to restaurants, weddings, funerals, churches, and takeaway outlets. The three common folds are:

  • Napkin fold (1/1): A flat, unfolded or single-fold serviette. Usually 3333 cm or 4040 cm. Best for casual dining and bulk catering.
  • 1/4 fold: Folded twice, giving a finished size around 16.516.5 cm or 2020 cm. This is the most common restaurant serviette in Africa.
  • 1/8 fold: Folded three times, often used for table settings, hotels, and cocktail events. Finished size is smaller and more elegant.

Business tip: 1/4 fold is the volume seller. 1/8 fold earns a higher margin per pack but requires cleaner cutting and better packaging to avoid looking cheap.

2. Making Facial Tissues: Interleaving and Box Packaging

Facial tissues are a higher-value product because they require softness, interleaving, and box packaging. Interleaving means each tissue is folded so that pulling one lifts the next. This is done on a folding machine or, at very small scale, by hand with a consistent Z-fold or C-fold.

Key production decisions:

  • Ply: 2-ply is standard. 3-ply commands a premium but needs better pulp.
  • Sheet size: Common sizes are 2020 cm and 2121 cm. Smaller sheets reduce cost but feel cheap.
  • Box count: 100 sheets per box is the standard retail unit. 50-sheet boxes work for hotels and clinics.
  • Box material: Printed carton board or laminated paper. Local printers can produce boxes in runs of 1,0005,000.

For hotels, offer 50-sheet boxes with the hotel logo. For retail, offer 100-sheet boxes with a strong local brand. The same tissue can serve both markets with different packaging.

3. Producing Kitchen Towels: Perforating and Roll Sizes

Kitchen towels are the most technically demanding of the three products because they require perforation, embossing, and tight rewinding. The perforation allows the user to tear a sheet cleanly. Poor perforation is the number one complaint in local kitchen towel products.

Standard roll sizes in African markets:

  • 2-roll pack: 2 rolls of 6070 sheets. Common in supermarkets.
  • 4-roll pack: 4 rolls of 60 sheets. Best value for families.
  • Jumbo roll: 200+ sheets, sold to restaurants and cleaning companies.

Perforation blades must be sharp and aligned. Dull blades create ragged tears and waste. Embossing improves absorbency and gives a premium look. If you cannot emboss, focus on clean perforation and honest sheet counts.

SAFETY WARNING: Dust, Blades, and Heat

Tissue converting produces fine paper dust that can cause respiratory irritation and, over time, lung damage. Always install dust extraction or wear appropriate N95/FFP2 masks in cutting, folding, and perforating areas. Perforation and cutting blades are extremely sharp: use push sticks, never hands, and lock out machines before blade changes. Embossing and sealing units can reach high temperatures; allow cooling before maintenance. Ensure electrical installations are earthed and protected from paper dust, which is flammable. Keep a fire extinguisher rated for Class A fires near the line. Train every worker on emergency stop locations before they touch the machine.

4. Choosing Packaging: Poly Bags, Boxes, and Labels

Packaging is where small producers win or lose. In many African markets, the package is the product. A clean, well-labelled poly bag outsells a superior tissue in a plain bag.

  • Poly bags: Cheapest option. Use 3040 micron LDPE or biodegradable alternatives where available. Include a header card or printed sticker.
  • Boxes: Best for facial tissues and premium serviettes. Local printers can produce small runs. Include sheet count, ply, and contact details.
  • Labels: Must include product name, sheet count, ply, production date, and business phone number. A WhatsApp number is essential in most African markets.

For hotels and events, offer custom-printed packaging with the clients logo. This is a high-margin service and builds repeat business.

5. Branding with Local Languages and Designs

Branding in local languages builds trust and shelf appeal. Examples: Softi in Nigeria, Safi in Kenya, Tissue ya Mtaa in Tanzania, or Papel Suave in Lusophone markets. Use local proverbs, patterns, or colours that match the region.

Practical branding rules:

  • Use one dominant colour that stands out on a crowded shelf.
  • Put the sheet count and ply in large, readable text.
  • Include a local-language tagline that explains the benefit: softness, strength, or value.
  • Add a QR code or WhatsApp number for reorders.

For hotels, co-branding is powerful: your brand on the back, the hotels logo on the front. This gives the hotel prestige and gives you a locked-in customer.

6. Small-Batch Production for Local Events and Hotels

Small-batch production is the fastest route to cash flow. Instead of competing with imported brands in supermarkets, target weddings, funerals, church conferences, hotels, and restaurants. These buyers need custom packs, reliable delivery, and credit terms.

Batch planning example for a wedding of 300 guests:

  • 300 guests 2 serviettes = 600 serviettes. Produce 700 for safety.
  • If using 1/8 fold, pack in 20s: 35 packs. If using 1/4 fold, pack in 50s: 14 packs.
  • Add 50 facial tissue boxes for the bridal party and VIP tables.
  • Add 20 kitchen towel rolls for the catering team.

Price for the event, not per unit. A single event order can generate more profit than a month of supermarket sales, with no shelf fees and immediate payment.

Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Confirm your parent reel GSM and ply before setting fold or roll specifications.
  2. Keep perforation blades sharp; check every 8 hours of production.
  3. Weigh and count every pack; never guess sheet counts.
  4. Print production date and WhatsApp number on every label.
  5. Offer 1/4 fold as your volume serviette and 1/8 fold as your premium option.
  6. For hotels, always quote custom-printed packaging with their logo.
  7. Keep a minimum of 3 event-ready packs: serviettes, facial tissues, kitchen towels.
  8. Train all staff on dust masks, blade safety, and emergency stops before production.

Brand in local languages, price for events, and never compromise on sheet counts or safety. In the African tissue market, trust and consistency beat size every time.
