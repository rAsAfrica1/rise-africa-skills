1. Introduction and Why It Matters

Metal furniture is everywhere in Africafrom the bustling markets of Lagos to the roadside welders in Nairobi. But what makes a metal chair comfortable? What makes a tabletop durable? The answer lies in how you design and attach seats, tops, and panels. These are the surfaces people touch, sit on, and use daily. If they fail, the whole piece fails. In this module, you will learn to create strong, comfortable, and long-lasting seats, tops, and panels using metal and other materials.

Why does this matter for your business? Because customers judge your work by these surfaces. A wobbly seat or a warped tabletop will lose you repeat sales. In many African markets, metal furniture competes with imported plastic and wooden furniture. Your advantage is customisation and durability. By mastering seats, tops, and panels, you can build chairs that support heavy weights, tables that resist heat and rain, and cabinets that secure goods. You can also use local materials like hardwood, plywood, or even recycled metal sheets to keep costs low.

Think about the environments where your furniture will be used: outdoor cafs in Accra, school desks in Kampala, or workbenches in a Johannesburg workshop. Each demands different properties. Seats need ergonomic curves and ventilation. Tops need flatness and resistance to scratching. Panels need to be rigid and lightweight. You will learn to select the right metal gauge, choose fasteners, and apply finishes that prevent rust. We will focus on practical methods: cutting, bending, drilling, welding, and bolting. You will also learn to avoid common mistakes like uneven legs or sharp edges.

By the end of this module, you will be able to build a complete stool or small table from scratch. You will understand how to measure, cut, join, and finish. You will also learn safety practices to protect your eyes, hands, and lungs. This knowledge is in high demand. Many vocational graduates in Africa start their own workshops with just a few tools. With these skills, you can produce furniture for homes, schools, and businesses. Lets get started.

2. Tools and Materials

To build seats, tops, and panels, you need the right tools and materials. Start with measuring and marking tools: a steel tape measure (5m), a try square, a scriber or permanent marker, and a centre punch. For cutting, you need a hacksaw with 24 or 32 teeth per inch for metal, an angle grinder with cutting and grinding discs, and a metal file. For drilling, a power drill with high-speed steel (HSS) bits (3mm to 10mm) and a step drill for larger holes. For joining, a welding machine (arc or MIG) is ideal, but you can also use bolts, rivets, or self-tapping screws. A hammer, mallet, and clamps are essential for holding pieces. For finishing, you need sandpaper (80 to 220 grit), wire brush, and paint or powder coating.

Materials: The most common metal is mild steel. Use square tubes (20mm x 20mm or 25mm x 25mm) for frames, and flat bars or angle iron for supports. For seats, you can use expanded metal mesh (for ventilation), perforated sheet, or solid sheet (1.5mm to 3mm thick). For tops, use sheet metal (1mm to 2mm) or wooden boards (plywood, MDF) covered with laminate or metal. For panels, consider aluminium sheets (lightweight, rust-resistant) or galvanised steel (for outdoor use). You will also need fasteners: bolts (M6, M8), nuts, washers, rivets, and self-tapping screws. For welding, use electrodes (2.5mm or 3.2mm) and welding wire. Dont forget safety gear: safety glasses, welding helmet, gloves, earplugs, and a respirator.

Where to source: In most African cities, you can find metal suppliers in industrial areas. For example, in Lagos, visit Ladipo Market; in Nairobi, go to Kamukunji; in Accra, try Suame Magazine. Buy standard lengths (6m) and have them cut to size. For small projects, you can buy offcuts at a lower price. Always check for rust, bends, or dents. For wooden tops, visit local timber yards. For fasteners, look for hardware stores in town. Keep a small stock of common sizes to avoid delays.

Budget tips: Start with a basic toolkit: hacksaw, drill, grinder, welder, clamps, and measuring tools. You can buy second-hand tools from other workshops. For materials, use recycled metal from old furniture or car parts. Clean and straighten them before use. Always buy a little extra for mistakes. With these tools and materials, you can build professional-quality furniture.

3. Step by Step Method

Step 1: Design and measure. Decide on the size of your seat, top, or panel. For a stool, a comfortable seat height is 45cm, width 35cm, depth 35cm. For a table, height 75cm, top 60cm x 120cm. Draw a simple sketch with dimensions. Use a tape measure and try square to mark your metal. Remember to account for the thickness of the seat material. For example, if your seat is 2mm thick, your frame should be 2mm smaller.

Step 2: Cut the metal. Use a hacksaw or angle grinder to cut square tubes and sheets. Clamp the metal securely. For straight cuts, use a guide or mark a line with a scriber. After cutting, remove burrs with a file or grinder. For sheet metal, use a jigsaw with a metal blade or a nibbler. Wear gloves and safety glasses.

Step 3: Build the frame. Weld or bolt the frame together. For a stool, make two square frames (top and bottom) and connect them with four legs. Ensure the frame is square by measuring diagonalsthey should be equal. Tack weld first, check for squareness, then complete the welds. If bolting, drill holes and use M8 bolts with washers and nuts. For a table, add cross braces for stability.

Step 4: Attach the seat or top. For a metal seat, cut expanded metal or perforated sheet to size. Weld or rivet it to the frame. For a wooden top, cut plywood to size, drill countersunk holes, and screw it to the frame with self-tapping screws. For a panel, attach sheet metal to a frame using rivets or bolts. Ensure the surface is flat and secure. If using a solid metal top, weld it continuously to prevent water ingress, then grind smooth.

Step 5: Finish and inspect. Grind all welds and sharp edges. Clean with a wire brush. Apply a rust-resistant primer, then paint or powder coat. For wooden parts, sand and varnish. Check for stability: place on a flat floor, ensure no wobble. Test the seat with weight. Make adjustments as needed. Your furniture is now ready for use or sale.

4. Common Mistakes and Fixes

Mistake 1: Uneven legs or wobbling. This happens when legs are cut at different lengths or the frame is not square. Fix: Measure twice, cut once. Use a try square to check angles. After welding, place the piece on a flat surface and adjust by grinding or adding shims. For bolted frames, loosen and re-tighten after squaring.

Mistake 2: Weak welds or joints. Beginners often use too little heat or move too fast, causing poor penetration. Fix: Practice on scrap metal. Set your welder to the correct amperage (e.g., 80-100A for 2.5mm electrode). Clean metal before welding. For bolted joints, use washers and lock nuts to prevent loosening.

Mistake 3: Sharp edges and burrs. These cause injuries and look unprofessional. Fix: Always file or grind edges after cutting. Use a deburring tool or a flap disc on your grinder. Wear gloves when handling.

Mistake 4: Rust and corrosion. Metal furniture left unpainted will rust quickly, especially in humid African climates. Fix: Apply a primer within 4 hours of cleaning. Use a rust-inhibiting primer, then two coats of enamel or powder coating. For outdoor furniture, use galvanised steel or aluminium.

Mistake 5: Warping or bending. This occurs when welding too much on one side, causing heat distortion. Fix: Weld in short segments, alternating sides. Clamp the work to a heat sink (like a thick steel plate) to dissipate heat. For thin sheets, use a backing plate.

Mistake 6: Poor ergonomics. A seat that is too high, too low, or lacks a backrest will be uncomfortable. Fix: Follow standard dimensions: seat height 45cm, depth 35-40cm, backrest height 30-40cm. Add a slight curve to the seat for comfort. Test with different users.

Mistake 7: Using the wrong material thickness. Too thin will bend; too thick is heavy and costly. Fix: For seats, use 1.5-2mm sheet or 20mm square tube frame. For tabletops, use 2mm sheet or 18mm plywood. For panels, use 1mm aluminium.

Mistake 8: Skipping safety gear. This leads to injuries. Fix: Always wear safety glasses, gloves, earplugs, and a respirator when grinding or welding. Keep a fire extinguisher nearby. By avoiding these mistakes, your furniture will be stronger, safer, and more sellable.

5. Safety and Best Practice

Safety is not optional in metalwork. Every year, thousands of workshop injuries occur in Africa due to carelessness. Protect yourself and your workers. Always wear personal protective equipment (PPE): safety glasses or goggles, welding helmet with shade 10 or higher, leather gloves, earplugs or earmuffs, and a respirator mask (N95 or better) when grinding or welding. Wear closed-toe leather boots and avoid loose clothing. Tie back long hair.

Workshop safety: Keep your work area clean and well-lit. Remove flammable materials like rags, paper, and solvents. Store gas cylinders upright and secure. Use a fire extinguisher (dry powder or CO2) and a first aid kit. Ensure good ventilationopen doors and windows, or use a fan to blow fumes away. Never weld or grind near fuel or paint. Use screens to protect others from sparks.

Tool safety: Before using any power tool, inspect cords and plugs for damage. Use the right blade or disc for the material. Never remove guards. When using an angle grinder, hold it with both hands and let the disc reach full speed before touching metal. Do not force the tool. For drilling, clamp your work securely. For welding, check for gas leaks and ensure proper grounding. Turn off and unplug tools when not in use.

Best practices: Plan your work to minimise waste. Measure twice, cut once. Keep a tidy workspace. Use jigs and clamps for repeat cuts. When welding, work in short bursts to avoid overheating. After welding, allow metal to cool before handling. For finishing, apply paint in a well-ventilated area. Dispose of waste metal and chemicals responsibly.

Health: Metal fumes and dust can cause long-term damage. Always wear a respirator when welding galvanised steel (which releases toxic zinc fumes) or grinding. Wash hands before eating or drinking. If you feel dizzy or nauseous, move to fresh air immediately. Stay hydrated.

Emergency: Know where your first aid kit and fire extinguisher are. For burns, cool with water and cover. For cuts, apply pressure and seek medical help. For eye injuries, flush with water and go to a clinic. By following these rules, you protect yourself, your workers, and your business. A safe workshop is a productive workshop.

6. Practical Exercise

For this exercise, you will build a simple metal stool with a wooden seat. This project combines all the skills from this module: measuring, cutting, welding, drilling, and finishing. You will need: 20mm x 20mm square steel tube (2.5m), 18mm plywood (350mm x 350mm), M6 bolts and nuts (8 sets), self-tapping screws (4), paint, and tools (hacksaw, grinder, drill, welder, clamps, tape measure, try square).

Step 1: Cut the metal. Cut four legs at 45cm each. Cut four top rails at 35cm each. Cut four bottom rails at 30cm each. Clean all cuts with a file.
Step 2: Build two square frames. Weld or bolt the top rails into a square (35cm x 35cm). Repeat for bottom rails (30cm x 30cm). Ensure diagonals are equal (49.5cm for top, 42.4cm for bottom).
Step 3: Attach legs. Weld or bolt the legs to the corners of the bottom frame, then to the top frame. Check that the stool is square and level. Add cross braces if needed.
Step 4: Prepare the seat. Cut plywood to 35cm x 35cm. Sand edges. Drill four countersunk holes for screws, positioned to align with the top frame.
Step 5: Attach seat. Place plywood on top frame. Screw down with self-tapping screws. Ensure screws are flush.
Step 6: Finish. Grind all welds. Clean with wire brush. Apply primer and two coats of paint to the metal. Varnish the wooden seat.
Step 7: Inspect. Place on flat floor. Check for wobble. Sit on it to test strength. Adjust if needed.

Variations: For a taller bar stool, increase leg length to 75cm. For a table, make a larger top frame (60cm x 120cm) and use a metal top instead of wood. For a panel, build a rectangular frame and attach aluminium sheet with rivets.

Time: This project takes about 4-6 hours for a beginner. Cost: In most African cities, materials cost about $10-15. You can sell the stool for $25-40.

Safety: Wear PPE throughout. Ask a mentor to check your welds.

Extension: Once you master this stool, try adding a backrest or armrests. Or build a set of four stools for a caf. Take photos of your work and share with rise AFRICA skills for feedback. This exercise proves you can create a functional, sellable product.

<h2>3. Step-by-Step Method: Seats, Tops and Panels</h2>

<p>This is the core production sequence. It assumes you have the tools listed in the <em>Tools and Materials</em> lesson: measuring tape, square, scriber, angle grinder, welding machine, drill, clamps, and a workbench.</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Plan and mark out.</strong> Draw the full-size layout on the workshop floor or a plywood sheet. Mark seat height, top height, and panel positions using a scriber and square. Double-check against Table 1.</li>
<li><strong>Cut the frame tubes.</strong> Measure twice, cut once. Use an angle grinder with a cutting disc. Deburr every cut with a grinding disc sharp edges cut hands and weaken welds.</li>
<li><strong>Build the seat frame.</strong> Weld a rectangular frame from square tube. Check diagonals with a tape if they differ by more than 2 mm, the frame is not square. Clamp before welding.</li>
<li><strong>Build the top frame.</strong> For tables and workbenches, weld a frame that supports the top panel. Add cross-braces every 400500 mm to prevent sagging.</li>
<li><strong>Cut and fit the seat panel.</strong> Cut sheet to size, leaving 12 mm clearance. Clamp to the frame. Drill pilot holes and fix with rivets, bolts, or plug welds. For slatted seats, space slats 1015 mm apart for drainage.</li>
<li><strong>Cut and fit the top panel.</strong> For a solid top, weld or bolt the sheet to the frame. For a removable top, use countersunk bolts. Check for flatness with a straight edge gaps over 2 mm will show.</li>
<li><strong>Fit back and side panels.</strong> Weld or bolt panels to the frame. Keep panels flush and square. For outdoor furniture, leave a 23 mm gap at the bottom for water drainage.</li>
<li><strong>Grind and finish.</strong> Grind all welds smooth. Remove rust and mill scale. Apply primer within 4 hours of grinding to prevent flash rust.</li>
<li><strong>Paint or powder-coat.</strong> Use a zinc-rich primer followed by two topcoats. In coastal areas, use epoxy or polyurethane paint. Allow full curing before delivery.</li>
<li><strong>Inspect and label.</strong> Check all dimensions, welds, sharp edges, and paint. Attach your workshop label or stamp this builds trust and repeat business.</li>
</ol>

<h2>4. Common Mistakes and Fixes</h2>

<p>These are the faults that cost African metal furniture makers the most money. Learn them, and teach them to your team.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Seat height too high or too low.</strong> Fix: always mark 430450 mm from the floor before welding. Test with a real user.</li>
<li><strong>Frame out of square.</strong> Fix: measure diagonals before welding. Clamp to a flat surface. If already welded, cut and re-weld do not force the panel.</li>
<li><strong>Thin sheet warping during welding.</strong> Fix: use lower current, tack welds first, and clamp a heat sink (aluminium or copper bar) behind the sheet.</li>
<li><strong>Rust appearing within weeks.</strong> Fix: remove all mill scale, prime within 4 hours, and use zinc-rich primer. Never paint over rust.</li>
<li><strong>Sharp edges on seats and panels.</strong> Fix: deburr every cut and grind every weld. A 2-minute grind saves a customer injury and a lost contract.</li>
<li><strong>Panels rattling or loose.</strong> Fix: use the correct rivet or bolt size, add a rubber or nylon washer, and check torque.</li>
<li><strong>Top sagging under load.</strong> Fix: add cross-braces every 400500 mm and use 2.0 mm sheet for workbenches.</li>
<li><strong>Poor paint adhesion.</strong> Fix: clean with degreaser, sand with 120-grit, and apply primer within the recommended window.</li>
</ul>

<h2>5. Safety and Best Practice</h2>

<p>Metal furniture making involves welding, grinding, cutting, and painting. Every one of these can injure or kill if done carelessly. In many African workshops, safety equipment is seen as a cost but one injury can cost more than a year of PPE. Treat safety as a business investment.</p>

<div style="border:2px solid #b00020; background:#fff5f5; padding:12px; margin:16px 0;">
<p style="color:#b00020; font-weight:bold; margin-top:0;"> DANGER WELDING AND GRINDING HAZARDS</p>
<p style="margin-bottom:0;">Arc welding produces intense ultraviolet radiation that can permanently damage eyes and skin. Grinding throws hot metal fragments that can cause blindness and burns. <strong>Always wear a welding helmet with the correct shade lens (shade 1013), leather gloves, a leather apron, and safety glasses under the helmet.</strong> Never weld or grind in a closed room without extraction or open ventilation. Keep a fire extinguisher and a bucket of sand within reach. Do not weld near fuel, paint, or solvents. In Africa's heat, take frequent breaks and drink water to avoid heat stress.</p>
</div>

<p><strong>Best practice checklist for every job:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Inspect tools and cables before use replace damaged leads.</li>
<li>Secure workpieces with clamps; never hold by hand.</li>
<li>Wear PPE: helmet, gloves, apron, boots, ear protection, and dust mask when grinding or painting.</li>
<li>Keep the workshop clean and dry; store gas cylinders upright and chained.</li>
<li>Paint and solvents must be stored in a locked, ventilated cabinet away from welding areas.</li>
<li>Train every worker on emergency stop, first aid, and fire response.</li>
</ul>
