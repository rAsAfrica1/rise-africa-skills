1. Lesson 1: Introduction to Township and Village Enterprise Management

Welcome to Module 6 of Township and Village Enterprise Management. This module focuses on the practical management of small-scale enterprises that operate in townships, villages, and peri-urban areas across Africa. Township and village enterprises, often called TVEs, are the backbone of rural and semi-urban economies. They include agro-processing units, welding and fabrication shops, tailoring and textile businesses, poultry and livestock ventures, retail kiosks, transport services, and artisanal manufacturing. In many African countries, these enterprises provide the majority of employment outside the formal public sector. Managing them well is not about copying large corporate models. It is about applying sound business principles in a context of limited infrastructure, informal credit, seasonal demand, and strong community relationships. This first lesson sets the foundation by defining what a TVE is, why it matters, and what management functions are essential for survival and growth. A township or village enterprise is typically owned by an individual, a family, or a small group of partners. It operates with fewer than fifty workers, often fewer than ten. Capital comes from personal savings, family contributions, rotating savings groups known as susu, stokvel, or chama, and sometimes microfinance institutions. The enterprise may be registered with local authorities or remain in the informal sector. Regardless of registration status, it must generate enough revenue to cover costs, pay owners, and reinvest for the future. The management of a TVE involves five core functions: planning, organizing, staffing, directing, and controlling. Planning means deciding what products or services to offer, how much to produce, and what resources are needed. Organizing means arranging tasks, tools, and people so work flows smoothly. Staffing means finding and keeping reliable workers, whether full-time, part-time, or casual. Directing means leading, motivating, and communicating with the team. Controlling means checking performance against plans and correcting mistakes. In an African village context, these functions must be adapted. For example, planning cannot ignore the rainy season, which may block roads and reduce customer traffic. Organizing must account for shared tools and communal workspaces. Staffing must consider family obligations, migration, and the need for flexible hours. Directing must respect local hierarchies and cultural norms. Controlling must use simple records that a owner with basic literacy can maintain. A key challenge for TVE managers is the dual economy. They buy inputs at retail prices but sell in markets where customers have very little cash. This squeezes margins. Successful managers find ways to add value. A maize farmer who simply sells sacks of maize earns little. A farmer who grinds the maize into flour, packages it in one-kilogram bags, and sells to local shops earns more. A tailor who only sews on order earns less than one who also makes ready-to-wear school uniforms and sells them before the school term begins. Value addition is a central theme throughout this module. Another challenge is access to finance. Banks often require collateral that village entrepreneurs do not have. Microfinance helps but comes with high interest rates. Rotating savings groups are cheaper but limited in size. The best strategy is to combine sources: use personal savings for start-up, a savings group for working capital, and a small loan only for a specific growth opportunity with a clear repayment plan. Never borrow more than the business can repay from monthly profits. Record-keeping is another critical area. Many TVEs fail because the owner does not know whether the business is making a profit. Cash in the pocket is not profit. Profit is what remains after deducting all costs, including the owner's own labor and the replacement cost of tools. A simple cash book, a stock card, and a debtors list are enough for most TVEs. These records help the owner make decisions about pricing, purchasing, and hiring. This module will cover five lessons. Lesson 1 introduces the concept and management functions. Lesson 2 covers market analysis and customer focus. Lesson 3 deals with operations, procurement, and quality control. Lesson 4 addresses financial management, record-keeping, and access to finance. Lesson 5 focuses on growth strategies, risk management, and succession planning. Each lesson includes practical exercises that you can apply to your own enterprise or to a case study from your community. By the end of this module, you should be able to assess a TVE, identify its strengths and weaknesses, and create a simple management improvement plan. Remember that management is not a one-time event. It is a daily practice of observing, deciding, acting, and reviewing. In the African context, successful TVE managers are those who combine business discipline with community trust. They pay their debts on time. They treat workers fairly. They deliver quality goods. They adapt to changing seasons and customer needs. They reinvest a portion of profits. They plan for the future. These habits, more than any single technique, determine whether a township or village enterprise survives and thrives.

2. Lesson 2: Market Analysis and Customer Focus for TVEs

In this lesson, we turn to the market. A township or village enterprise does not operate in a vacuum. It serves a specific set of customers with specific needs, incomes, and buying habits. Many TVE owners assume they know their market because they live in the community. But assumptions can be wrong. A tailor may think customers want cheap clothes, when in fact they want durable school uniforms that last a full year. A poultry farmer may think customers want large chickens, when the local market prefers medium-sized birds for family meals. Market analysis is the disciplined process of finding out what customers actually want, what they are willing to pay, and how competitors are serving them. The first step is to define your market area. For a village enterprise, this may be the village itself, nearby villages, a weekly market, a trading center, or a small town. For a township enterprise, it may be a specific neighborhood, a taxi rank, a school zone, or a industrial area. Draw a simple map. Mark where your customers live, where they work, where they shop, and how they move. This map helps you decide where to locate your business, how to distribute your products, and when to be open. The second step is to identify customer segments. Not all customers are the same. In a typical African township or village, you may find several segments. There are daily wage earners who buy small quantities frequently. There are farmers who have cash after harvest and buy in bulk. There are teachers and nurses with regular salaries who can afford slightly higher quality. There are students who buy snacks and airtime. There are passing traders who buy for resale. Each segment has different price sensitivity, quality expectations, and purchasing times. A smart TVE manager serves two or three segments rather than trying to serve everyone. The third step is to understand customer needs and pain points. Talk to people. Ask questions. What do they buy now? Where do they buy it? What do they like about the current supplier? What frustrates them? For example, a village may have no reliable source of fresh bread. Customers travel ten kilometers to town. A small bakery in the village could capture that market if it can produce consistent quality and open early. Another village may have a welder who takes two weeks to make a gate. Customers complain about the delay. A new welder who promises three-day delivery can win business even at a slightly higher price. The fourth step is to analyze competitors. Who else is selling the same product or service? What are their prices, quality, location, and opening hours? Do they offer credit? Do they deliver? A simple competitor table helps. List each competitor and rate them on price, quality, convenience, and trust. Then identify a gap. Maybe all competitors sell only in large bags, but customers want small sachets. Maybe all competitors close at 5 p.m., but workers need to buy after 6 p.m. Maybe no competitor offers home delivery. The gap is your opportunity. The fifth step is to set your value proposition. This is a short statement of why customers should buy from you rather than someone else. Examples include: the freshest produce in the village, the only shop open on Sundays, the tailor who guarantees a perfect fit or your money back, the agro-dealer who gives free advice on planting. Your value proposition must be true and must matter to customers. It is not a slogan. It is a promise you keep every day. Pricing is a critical part of market analysis. Many TVE owners set prices by copying competitors or by adding a random markup. A better method is cost-plus pricing with a market check. First, calculate your unit cost: materials, labor, transport, packaging, and a share of overheads like rent and electricity. Add a profit margin that covers your time and risk. Then compare with competitor prices. If your price is too high, find ways to reduce costs without reducing quality. If your price is too low, you may be leaving money on the table or heading for losses. In many African markets, customers also expect credit. Selling on credit can increase sales, but it can also destroy your business if customers do not pay. Have a clear credit policy. For example, credit only to known customers, only for amounts below a set limit, and only with a written record and a repayment date. Follow up politely but firmly. Do not let one customer's unpaid debt become another customer's problem. Promotion in a TVE context is mostly word of mouth. Satisfied customers tell others. Unsatisfied customers tell even more. You can also use simple, low-cost methods: a painted signboard, a WhatsApp status update, a announcement at church or mosque, a stall at the weekly market, a loyalty card for frequent buyers. Radio is still powerful in many rural areas. Community radio announcements can reach thousands of people for a small fee. Whatever method you choose, be consistent. A promotion that runs for one week and then stops has little effect. Customer service is the heart of market focus. In a village or township, reputation is everything. Greet customers warmly. Listen to complaints without arguing. Fix mistakes quickly. Remember names and preferences. Keep your premises clean. Dress neatly. These small actions build trust. Trust leads to repeat business. Repeat business leads to stable income. A TVE that ignores customer service will lose customers to a competitor who smiles and remembers their name. Finally, market analysis is not a one-time exercise. Markets change. A new road may bring competitors. A new school may create demand for uniforms. A drought may reduce spending. Review your market every three months. Talk to customers. Watch competitors. Adjust your products, prices, and promotions. The best TVE managers are constantly learning from the market. They do not wait for a crisis to change. They change before the crisis arrives. In the next lesson, we will look at operations and procurement, which turn market insights into daily production and delivery.

3. Lesson 3: Operations, Procurement, and Quality Control

Operations management is the engine room of a township or village enterprise. It covers everything from buying raw materials to delivering the final product or service to the customer. In a small African enterprise, operations are often informal and flexible. But without some structure, chaos creeps in. Materials run out. Orders are forgotten. Quality drops. Customers leave. This lesson provides practical tools to organize operations, procure inputs efficiently, and maintain consistent quality. Start with a simple operations flow. Write down the steps from start to finish. For a poultry enterprise, the flow might be: buy day-old chicks, prepare brooder, feed and water, vaccinate, clean pens, sell mature birds, record income and expenses. For a tailoring business: take measurements, buy fabric, cut, sew, finish, press, deliver, collect payment. For a maize mill: receive maize, clean, grind, package, collect payment, maintain machine. Once the flow is clear, identify bottlenecks. A bottleneck is a step that slows down the whole process. In the poultry example, if the brooder is too small, chicks crowd and die. That is a bottleneck. In tailoring, if only one person can cut fabric, orders pile up. In milling, if the engine overheats, grinding stops. Fix bottlenecks first. They give the biggest improvement for the least effort. Procurement is the next focus. Procurement means buying the right inputs at the right price, quality, and time. Many TVE owners buy from the nearest supplier without comparing. This is convenient but often expensive. A better approach is to identify at least three suppliers for each key input. Compare price, quality, distance, and reliability. For example, a baker needs flour, yeast, sugar, and fuel. One supplier may be cheap but far. Another may be close but often out of stock. A third may offer credit. The best choice depends on your cash flow and production schedule. Consider forming a buying group with other TVEs. Several tailors can combine their fabric orders to get a bulk discount. Several poultry farmers can share transport for feed. Several millers can buy spare parts together. Buying groups reduce costs and increase bargaining power. They also build community cooperation. However, they require clear rules: who collects money, who places the order, how costs are shared, what happens if someone fails to pay. Write these rules down. Verbal agreements often fail. Inventory management is closely linked to procurement. Too much stock ties up cash and risks spoilage or theft. Too little stock leads to stockouts and lost sales. Use a simple stock card for each item. Record date, quantity received, quantity used, and balance. Set a reorder level. When stock falls to that level, order more. For perishable goods like tomatoes, milk, or fresh meat, use a first-in-first-out rule. Sell older stock before newer stock. For non-perishables like nails, fabric, or soap, keep a buffer stock for busy periods. In villages, transport delays are common. A buffer of one or two weeks may be wise. Quality control is not a luxury. It is a survival requirement. Customers in African townships and villages are increasingly quality-conscious. They may not use the word quality, but they know when bread is stale, when a dress tears at the seam, when a chicken is tough, or when a hammer breaks after one week. Poor quality destroys reputation faster than low price attracts customers. Define quality standards for your product. For a bakery: bread must be well-baked, not burnt, not doughy, consistent size. For a tailor: seams must be straight, buttons secure, measurements accurate. For a welder: joints must be strong, no sharp edges, paint even. Write these standards down. Show them to workers. Check every batch or every order. Keep a simple quality log. When a defect appears, find the cause and fix it. Do not just apologize to the customer. Apologize, replace or repair, and then change your process so it does not happen again. Training is essential for quality. Many TVE workers learn on the job by watching others. This can pass on bad habits. Invest in short training sessions. A experienced tailor can teach apprentices how to measure properly. A successful poultry farmer can teach others about vaccination schedules. A miller can teach machine maintenance. Training does not have to be expensive. It can be one hour per week. It can be a demonstration. It can be a visit to a better-performing enterprise. The key is to keep learning and to share knowledge. Maintenance is another part of operations. Tools and machines break down. A broken machine means lost production and lost income. Create a maintenance schedule. Daily: clean and check. Weekly: oil and tighten. Monthly: inspect belts, filters, and electrical connections. Yearly: major service. Keep spare parts for critical items. For a maize mill, spare belts and bearings are essential. For a sewing machine, spare needles and bobbins. For a vehicle, spare tyre and fan belt. A small investment in maintenance saves large losses later. Health and safety also matter. A workshop with sharp tools, hot surfaces, or chemicals can injure workers. Injured workers cannot work. Provide basic protective equipment: gloves, boots, goggles, aprons. Keep first aid kit. Ensure good ventilation. Store chemicals safely away from food. Train workers on safe practices. In many African countries, there are laws about workplace safety. Even if enforcement is weak, following good practice protects your people and your business. Finally, document your operations. A simple operations manual, even handwritten, helps new workers learn faster. It also helps you spot inefficiencies. Include your flow chart, supplier list, quality standards, maintenance schedule, and safety rules. Review it every six months. Update it when you change a process. In the next lesson, we will focus on financial management, which turns operational efficiency into profit and cash flow.

4. Lesson 4: Financial Management, Record-Keeping, and Access to Finance

Money is the lifeblood of any enterprise. In a township or village enterprise, money is often scarce, irregular, and mixed with household finances. This lesson teaches you how to separate business money from personal money, keep simple records, calculate profit, manage cash flow, and access finance without falling into debt traps. The first rule is: separate business and personal finances. Open a separate bank account or mobile money wallet for the business. If that is not possible, use a separate cash box and a notebook. Never take money from the business for personal use without recording it as a drawing. Never pay personal expenses directly from the business cash box. When you mix money, you cannot tell whether the business is profitable. You may think you are making money when you are actually eating your capital. The second rule is: keep records. You do not need a computer or accounting degree. You need three simple books. The cash book records every shilling, naira, cedi, or rand that comes in and goes out. Date, description, money in, money out, balance. The stock book records inventory purchases, usage, and balance. The debtors book records who owes you money, how much, when it is due, and when it is paid. If you have employees, keep a wages book. If you have assets like machines, vehicles, or buildings, keep an asset register with purchase date, cost, and condition. These records take ten minutes a day. They are the foundation of every financial decision. The third rule is: calculate profit correctly. Profit is not the cash in your pocket. Profit is total sales minus total costs. Total costs include materials, wages, rent, transport, electricity, water, packaging, and a share of your own time. If you do not pay yourself a salary, estimate what you would pay someone else to do your job. Include that as a cost. Also include depreciation. Depreciation is the wear and tear on your tools and machines. If a sewing machine costs 300,000 and lasts five years, it loses 60,000 per year, or 5,000 per month. That 5,000 is a cost, even if you did not spend cash this month. Without depreciation, you may think you are profitable when you are actually consuming your equipment. The fourth rule is: manage cash flow. Cash flow is the timing of money in and money out. A business can be profitable on paper but fail because it runs out of cash. For example, a poultry farmer sells chickens at the end of the month but must buy feed every week. If cash is tight, the farmer cannot feed the birds, they die, and there is nothing to sell. To manage cash flow, forecast weekly. Write down expected cash in and expected cash out for the next four weeks. If you see a shortfall, act early. Delay non-essential purchases. Ask customers to pay earlier. Ask suppliers for a few days credit. Sell some slow-moving stock at a discount. Do not wait until the cash box is empty. The fifth rule is: price for profit. Many TVE owners set prices by looking at competitors or by guessing. A better method is cost-plus pricing. Calculate your unit cost. Add a profit margin. Check the market. If the market will not accept your price, reduce costs or change the product. Do not simply sell at a loss and hope to make it up in volume. Volume at a loss only makes you poorer faster. The sixth rule is: manage debt carefully. Debt can help a business grow, but it can also destroy it. Before borrowing, ask three questions. What will I use the loan for? How will it increase my income? How will I repay it if sales are lower than expected? Only borrow for productive assets or working capital that directly generates cash. Do not borrow for consumption, weddings, or luxury items. Do not borrow to cover daily losses. If you are already losing money, a loan will only delay the collapse. The seventh rule is: access finance strategically. In Africa, TVEs have several sources of finance. Personal savings are the cheapest and safest. Rotating savings groups, known as susu, stokvel, chama, or tontine, provide small, short-term capital at low or no interest. Microfinance institutions provide larger loans but at higher interest rates. Banks provide the largest loans but require collateral and formal registration. Government programs and NGOs sometimes offer grants or subsidized loans, but they are competitive and often slow. Value chain financing is another option. A buyer may advance inputs to a farmer and deduct repayment when the crop is sold. An equipment supplier may offer hire purchase. A mobile money platform may offer small loans based on transaction history. Choose the source that matches your need. For a small, short-term need, use savings or a savings group. For a larger, long-term investment, consider microfinance or a bank. Always read the terms. Understand the interest rate, repayment schedule, fees, and penalties. If you cannot understand the terms, ask someone you trust who understands finance. Do not sign what you do not understand. The eighth rule is: build a financial safety net. Save a small amount every week, even if it is very small. Keep it in a separate account or a locked box. Use it for emergencies: machine breakdown, illness, theft, or a sudden drop in sales. A safety net prevents you from borrowing at high interest or selling assets at a loss. Aim for at least one month of operating costs. Over time, build to three months. The ninth rule is: review your finances monthly. At the end of each month, calculate your sales, costs, profit, and cash balance. Compare with the previous month and with your plan. Identify what went well and what went wrong. Adjust your prices, purchases, or operations. Share the results with your workers if appropriate. When workers understand the numbers, they become partners in improving performance. Finally, remember that financial management is a habit, not a one-time task. The most successful TVE owners are not necessarily the most educated. They are the most disciplined. They record every transaction. They separate business and personal money. They save for emergencies. They borrow only when they have a clear repayment plan. They review their numbers every month. These habits turn a struggling enterprise into a stable and growing business. In the next lesson, we will look at growth strategies, risk management, and succession planning.

5. Lesson 5: Growth Strategies, Risk Management, and Succession Planning

A township or village enterprise that survives is good. An enterprise that grows is better. Growth creates jobs, increases income, and strengthens the local economy. But growth also brings risk. This lesson covers how to grow deliberately, manage risks, and plan for the future beyond the current owner. Growth starts with a clear vision. Where do you want the business to be in three years? In five years? Write it down. A vision might be: to become the leading supplier of fresh eggs in the district, to open a second tailoring shop in the nearby town, to process and package cassava flour for sale in supermarkets, to train ten young people every year in welding. A vision gives direction. It helps you say no to distractions. It helps you choose which opportunities to pursue. There are several growth strategies for TVEs. Market penetration means selling more of your existing products to your existing customers. You can do this by improving quality, offering credit, extending opening hours, or running promotions. Market development means selling your existing products to new customers or new areas. You can open a new outlet, attend a different market, or sell online through WhatsApp or a simple website. Product development means creating new products for existing customers. A bakery that already sells bread can add cakes, doughnuts, and meat pies. A tailor who makes uniforms can add school bags and aprons. Diversification means adding unrelated products or services. A poultry farmer might add a feed mill. A welder might add a hardware shop. Diversification spreads risk but requires new skills and capital. Choose one or two strategies at a time. Do not try to do everything at once. Growth requires resources. Before expanding, check your capacity. Do you have enough working capital? Do you have reliable suppliers? Do you have trained workers? Do you have a system for quality control? Do you have a market for the extra output? If the answer to any of these is no, fix that first. Growth without capacity leads to poor quality, unhappy customers, and financial loss. A simple growth plan includes: the goal, the actions needed, the resources required, the timeline, and the person responsible. For example, goal: increase egg production from 100 to 200 trays per week within six months. Actions: build a second poultry house, buy 500 more layers, hire one worker, secure a feed supply contract. Resources: 2 million shillings for construction and birds, 500,000 for working capital. Timeline: month 1-2 construction, month 3 purchase birds, month 4-6 ramp up production. Responsible: owner and farm manager. Write this plan. Review it monthly. Adjust as needed. Risk management is the other side of growth. Every enterprise faces risks. Some are internal: theft, machine breakdown, poor quality, worker absence, cash shortage. Some are external: drought, flood, price changes, new competitors, government regulations, disease outbreaks, theft, and civil unrest. Identify your top five risks. For each, assess how likely it is and how much damage it would cause. Then decide how to prevent, reduce, transfer, or accept the risk. Prevention means stopping the risk from happening. For theft, install locks, keep records, separate duties, and do not leave cash overnight. For machine breakdown, do regular maintenance and keep spare parts. For disease, vaccinate animals and follow biosecurity rules. Reduction means limiting the damage if the risk occurs. For drought, use irrigation or drought-resistant seeds. For price changes, grow a mix of crops or products. For flood, build on higher ground and keep drainage clear. Transfer means shifting the risk to someone else. Insurance is the main tool. In many African countries, microinsurance is available for crops, livestock, health, and property. The premiums may seem expensive, but one disaster without insurance can wipe out years of work. Explore index-based insurance for farmers, which pays out when rainfall or satellite data shows a drought, without needing a loss adjuster. Accept means acknowledging the risk and setting aside a contingency fund. Some risks are too small or too unlikely to insure. For those, keep a small reserve. Succession planning is the final topic. Many TVEs are family businesses. When the founder dies or retires, the business often collapses because no one else knows how to run it. Succession planning means preparing someone to take over. It does not mean giving up control today. It means building a system that can survive without you. Start by documenting your processes. Write down how you buy, produce, sell, and keep records. Train your successor. This could be a son, daughter, spouse, nephew, or a trusted employee. Give them increasing responsibility. Let them make decisions while you are still there to guide them. Teach them about suppliers, customers, and finances. Introduce them to key contacts. If you have multiple children, decide who will lead and how others will be compensated. Put it in writing. If possible, register the business formally and write a will. Formal registration makes it easier to transfer ownership. A will prevents disputes. Without a will, family members may fight, and the business may be sold or closed. Even a simple handwritten will, signed by witnesses, is better than nothing. Consider separating ownership from management. The owner may be a family member, but the manager may be a trained employee. This allows the business to continue even if the owner is not active. Pay the manager well and give them a share of profits. Aligned incentives lead to better performance. Finally, think about exit strategies. Not every business should continue forever. If the market disappears, if the owner is tired, if a better opportunity arises, it may be wise to sell or close. Selling requires clean records, a good reputation, and a fair valuation. Closing requires paying debts, selling assets, and informing customers and workers. Plan for these possibilities. Do not let a crisis force a rushed decision. In summary, growth, risk management, and succession planning are not separate topics. They are connected. A growing business attracts more risk. A business that manages risk is more attractive to successors. A business with a successor is more likely to survive and continue growing. The best TVE managers think in years, not days. They build systems, train people, save money, and prepare for change. They understand that the enterprise is bigger than any one person. It is a community asset that can create jobs and wealth for generations. This concludes Module 6. You now have a practical framework for managing a township or village enterprise in Africa. Use it. Adapt it. Share it. And keep learning from your customers, your workers, and your own records.

<h2>Lesson 6: Practical Reference Records, Standards, and Daily Operating Controls for TVEs</h2>

<p>This practical reference lesson is designed to be printed, pinned to the wall of your shop, workshop, poultry house, or processing room, and used every day. It does not replace the earlier lessons; it operationalises them. Township and Village Enterprises (TVEs) in Africa rarely fail because the owner lacks ambition. They fail because the owner lacks a simple, repeatable control system: a record book that is actually filled in, a quality standard that is actually checked, a price that is actually calculated, and a safety rule that is actually followed. This lesson gives you those tools in one place, with real figures you can adapt to your own enterprise.</p>

<p>The module is business-first. Every table, checklist, and diagram below is intended to help you make a faster, more profitable decision not to satisfy a donor or a form. Where the topic touches food safety, animal health, or chemicals, a red warning box is included because getting these wrong can destroy your business, your reputation, or your family's health.</p>

If you skip recording, you cannot check, and if you cannot check, you cannot adjust. Most informal businesses skip the record step and then wonder why cash "disappears".</p>

<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0;color:#b71c1c;font-weight:bold;font-size:15px;"> SAFETY WARNING CHEMICALS, FEED, AND FOOD CONTACT</p>
<p style="margin:0 0 8px 0;color:#7f0000;font-size:13px;">If your TVE handles caustic soda (soap making), agricultural pesticides, veterinary drugs, or any chemical that touches food, feed, or drinking water, you must follow these rules. They are not optional.</p>
<ul style="margin:0;padding-left:20px;color:#7f0000;font-size:13px;">
<li><strong>Never store chemicals near food, feed, or water.</strong> Use a locked, labelled cabinet. WHO and FAO recommend physical separation and locked storage for all hazardous substances (WHO/FAO, <em>International Code of Conduct on Pesticide Management</em>, 2014).</li>
<li><strong>Wear gloves and eye protection</strong> when handling caustic soda, lime, or concentrated acids. Caustic soda causes severe burns and permanent eye damage.</li>
<li><strong>Observe withdrawal periods</strong> for any veterinary drug used in poultry or livestock. Selling meat or eggs before the withdrawal period ends leaves drug residues that can harm consumers and get your business closed.</li>
<li><strong>Label every container.</strong> Never put chemicals in a drink bottle, cooking oil container, or unmarked jerrycan. This is a leading cause of accidental poisoning in African households.</li>
<li><strong>Wash hands and equipment</strong> after handling chemicals and before touching food. Use separate buckets for food and for chemicals.</li>
<li><strong>Keep a first-aid kit</strong> and the number of the nearest health facility posted on the wall.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h3>4. Quick Reference Checklist Print and Pin This</h3>

<p>Run through this checklist at the end of every working day. It takes five minutes. It will save you money, time, and reputation.</p>

<ol style="font-family:Arial,sans-serif;font-size:14px;line-height:1.7;">
<li><strong>Did I record every sale and every expense today?</strong> If not, do it now before you sleep. Memory fades by morning.</li>
<li><strong>Is my cash box balanced?</strong> Count cash, compare to the record book, and note any difference. Investigate any gap immediately.</li>
<li><strong>Did I check the quality of what I sold or produced?</strong> One bad batch of gari, one sick bird, or one torn uniform can lose a customer for life.</li>
<li><strong>Are my chemicals, drugs, and fuels locked and labelled?</strong> Check that no chemical is stored near food or feed.</li>
<li><strong>Did I feed and water my animals correctly today?</strong> Check feed consumption against the target; a drop is an early warning of disease.</li>
<li><strong>Is my equipment clean and safe to start tomorrow?</strong> Clean the mill, the sewing machine, the processing table, and the poultry house.</li>
<li><strong>Did I note one thing to improve tomorrow?</strong> Write it down. Small daily improvements compound into a strong business.</li>
<li><strong>Did I pay or record any debt, loan repayment, or supplier credit?</strong> Unrecorded debt is the most common cause of sudden cash crises in TVEs.</li>
</ol>
