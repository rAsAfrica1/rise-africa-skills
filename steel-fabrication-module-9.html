1. Visual Inspection Techniques

Visual inspection is your first and most important quality check. Before you weld, look at the fit-up. Check that the gap is consistent, usually 2-3 mm for a butt joint. Use a welding gauge to measure the root gap and land. Look for mill scale, rust, oil, or paint on the edgesthese cause porosity and lack of fusion. Clean the joint to bare metal at least 25 mm back from the edge. A simple wire brush and grinder are your main tools.

After welding, inspect the bead. Good welds have a smooth, uniform ripple pattern with no visible undercut, overlap, or cracks. Undercut deeper than 0.5 mm is a defect. Use a magnifying glass (10x) and a bright torch (LED) to check for fine cracks, especially at the weld toes. Run your fingernail along the toeif it catches, there's undercut or overlap. Check for spatter, which can hide cracks. Remove spatter with a chipping hammer and wire brush before final inspection.

In Africa, where heat and dust are common, pay extra attention to moisture. Electrodes exposed to humid air for more than 4 hours must be redried at 250-300C for 2 hours. Visual inspection of electrodes: look for damaged flux, rust on the core wire, or dampness. Reject any electrode that looks questionable. For galvanized steel, look for white rust (zinc oxide) and clean it off before welding.

Use a checklist. Write down what you inspect: joint preparation, fit-up, preheat (if required), weld passes, final bead. Take photos with your phone as a record. A good visual inspector catches 80% of defects before any expensive testing. Remember: if it looks bad, it probably is bad. Trust your eyes and your experience.

2. Dimensional Inspection

Dimensional inspection ensures your fabricated part matches the drawing. Start with a steel tape measure (5 m) and a try square. For critical dimensions, use a vernier caliper (0-150 mm) or a micrometer (0-25 mm). Check overall length, width, height, and diagonal measurements. For a rectangular frame, the diagonals must be equal within 2 mm. If not, the frame is out of squareadjust before welding.

Use a spirit level to check for twist and straightness. Place the level on the member; if the bubble is off, measure the gap with feeler gauges. Twist can be corrected with clamps and heat. For holes, use a hole gauge or caliper to check diameter and a square to check perpendicularity. Bolt holes must alignif they don't, ream them to the next size, but never elongate with a torch.

In Africa, temperature changes can affect measurements. Steel expands about 0.012 mm per meter per C. So a 10 m beam can grow 6 mm from morning to afternoon. Always measure at the same temperature, preferably early morning. Use a plumb bob and string to check verticality of columns. A 3 m column should be within 3 mm of plumb.

Record all measurements on a dimensional report. Compare with the drawing tolerances. Typical tolerances: 2 mm for lengths under 2 m, 3 mm for longer. If out of tolerance, mark the part with a red paint pen and rework. Don't guessmeasure twice, cut once. A good fabricator keeps a notebook with all dimensions and corrections.

3. Non-Destructive Testing (NDT)

Non-destructive testing (NDT) finds defects without damaging the weld. The most common methods in African workshops are dye penetrant testing (PT), magnetic particle testing (MT), and ultrasonic testing (UT). Radiographic testing (RT) is expensive and usually done in a lab, but you can send samples.

Dye penetrant testing is cheap and easy. Clean the weld with a wire brush and solvent. Spray red penetrant, wait 10 minutes, then wipe off. Spray white developer, wait 10 minutes. Red lines show cracks, porosity, or lack of fusion. PT works on any material, but only for surface defects. A PT kit costs about $50 and does 20 tests.

Magnetic particle testing is for ferromagnetic steel (not stainless). Use a yoke to magnetize the area, then sprinkle iron powder. Black powder shows defects under white light; fluorescent powder needs UV light. MT finds surface and slightly subsurface defects. A yoke costs $200-500. Always demagnetize after testing, or the part will attract metal chips.

Ultrasonic testing uses sound waves to find internal defects. A portable UT machine costs $1000-3000. You need a trained operator. Couplant (gel or oil) is applied, and a probe is moved over the weld. Echoes show defects. UT is good for thick welds (over 10 mm). In Africa, UT is often used for pressure vessels and structural steel. Always calibrate the machine with a reference block before testing. Record defect location, size, and depth. If a defect is found, mark it and repair by grinding and rewelding. After repair, re-test.

4. Welding Procedure Specifications (WPS)

A Welding Procedure Specification (WPS) is a written recipe for making a weld. It tells you: base metal type, thickness, welding process (SMAW, MIG, TIG), electrode type and size, current (amps), voltage, travel speed, preheat, interpass temperature, and post-weld heat treatment. Without a WPS, you are guessing. With a WPS, you get consistent, code-compliant welds.

A WPS is created from a Procedure Qualification Record (PQR). You weld a test piece following a proposed procedure, then cut and bend it to check for defects. If it passes, the procedure is qualified and becomes a WPS. In Africa, many small shops don't have WPSs, but big projects (bridges, buildings, pipelines) require them. You can write a simple WPS for common jobs. For example, for 10 mm mild steel with E6013 electrode: root pass 80-90 A, fill 100-110 A, cap 100-110 A. Travel speed 150 mm/min. Preheat not required for thickness under 25 mm.

Always follow the WPS. If you change an essential variable (electrode type, current range, preheat), you need a new WPS. Keep a copy at the welding station. The welder must sign off that they followed it. In Africa, where power can fluctuate, use a clamp meter to check actual current. If current is too low, you get lack of fusion; too high, you get burn-through and undercut.

For critical jobs, a welding inspector checks that the WPS is being followed. They may ask to see the electrode certificate and the welder's qualification. A welder qualified to a WPS can weld any joint covered by that WPS. Without qualification, the weld is suspect. So insist on a WPS for any structural work.

5. Codes and Standards (AWS, ASTM)

Codes and standards are rulebooks that ensure safety and quality. The most common for steel fabrication are AWS D1.1 (structural welding), ASME Section IX (pressure vessels), and ASTM specifications for materials. AWS D1.1 tells you acceptable weld sizes, inspection methods, and acceptance criteria. For example, undercut shall not exceed 0.8 mm for material under 25 mm thick. Porosity limits: maximum 1/8 inch (3 mm) for visual, and specific limits for RT.

ASTM standards cover materials: A36 (mild steel), A572 (high-strength low-alloy), A992 (wide-flange beams). When you buy steel, ask for the mill certificate. It shows chemical composition and mechanical properties (yield strength, tensile strength). For A36, yield strength is 250 MPa minimum. If you weld A572 to A36, you need a WPS that covers both.

In Africa, many projects follow British Standards (BS) or European Norms (EN), but AWS is widely accepted. For a small shop, keep a copy of AWS D1.1 and ASTM A6 (general requirements for rolled steel). Learn the acceptance criteria for visual inspection: cracks are never allowed. Undercut, overlap, and porosity have limits. For example, AWS D1.1 allows porosity up to 3 mm for statically loaded structures, but zero for cyclically loaded.

You don't need to memorize everything. Make a cheat sheet with the most common limits. Use a welding gauge to measure weld size: leg length for fillet welds, reinforcement for butt welds. If a weld fails, the code tells you how to repair it. Always document that you followed the code. In Africa, where third-party inspection may be rare, self-inspection to code is your best guarantee.

6. Documentation and Traceability

Documentation is proof that you did the job right. For every weld, keep a record: date, welder ID, WPS number, electrode batch number, base metal heat number, and inspection results. This is called traceability. If a weld fails later, you can trace back to the exact materials and welder. In Africa, where record-keeping is often weak, good documentation sets you apart.

Start with a welding log. A simple notebook works. Write: project name, drawing number, joint ID, welder name, date, and any defects found. Take photos of the weld before and after. Store photos on a phone or computer, backed up. For critical jobs, use a weld map: a drawing with each weld numbered. Then fill in a table with the details for each weld number.

Material traceability: when you receive steel, record the mill certificate number and heat number. Mark the steel with a paint pen or tag. When you cut a piece, transfer the heat number to the cut piece. This way, if a defect appears, you know which heat it came from. Electrodes: keep the batch number and date opened. Reject electrodes that are damp or damaged.

Inspection reports: after visual inspection, fill out a report. Include the acceptance criteria (e.g., AWS D1.1) and the results. Sign and date. If NDT was done, attach the report. For repairs, document the defect, the repair method, and re-inspection. Keep all documents for at least 5 years. In Africa, many clients require a final dossier with all records. A well-documented job wins repeat business. Remember: if it isn't written down, it didn't happen.

7. Practical Reference

Practical Reference: Quality Control & Inspection for Steel Fabrication in Africa

In African steel fabrication, quality control is not a paperwork exercise it is the difference between a structure that survives Harmattan dust, coastal salt, and monsoon loading, and one that fails an inspection and stalls payment. Whether you are fabricating roof trusses in Lagos, transmission towers in Nairobi, or mining chutes in Kitwe, the reference values below are the ones you will actually reach for on the shop floor. This module consolidates the visual, dimensional, NDT, WPS, standards, and traceability content into a single working reference.

Business-first logic: every inspection step costs time and money. The goal is not maximum testing it is right-sized testing that satisfies the client's specification, protects your liability, and keeps the project profitable. Over-inspection erodes margin; under-inspection destroys reputation and invites rework or rejection.

1. Dimensional Inspection Tolerances You Will Actually Measure

Dimensional inspection is the cheapest form of quality control and the one most often skipped under schedule pressure. A tape measure, spirit level, plumb bob, and a good square will catch 80% of fabrication errors before they become weld defects or assembly failures. The table below gives practical tolerances for common African fabrication work roof trusses, transmission towers, and structural frames.

Feature Typical Tolerance Measurement Tool Business Impact if Exceeded
Member length (truss chord, column) 3 mm up to 6 m; 6 mm over 6 m Steel tape, laser distance meter Site fit-up failure; crane time wasted
Squareness (diagonal difference) 3 mm per 1 m of diagonal Steel tape, framing square Cladding and roofing panels will not close
Straightness of member L/1000 max, not exceeding 10 mm String line, straight edge Visible distortion; client rejection
Hole position (bolted connections) 2 mm from drawing Vernier caliper, template Reaming, oversized holes, or re-fabrication
Weld size (fillet leg length) 0 to +3 mm over specified Weld gauge (fillet gauge) Under-size = reject; over-size = wasted consumables
Bow / camber in built-up beams 5 mm over full length String line, level Misalignment in service; fatigue risk
Base plate flatness 1.5 mm over 300 mm Feeler gauge, straight edge Grouting failure; column plumb error

Sources: AWS D1.1/D1.1M:2020 Table 6.1 (fabrication tolerances); BS EN 1090-2:2018 Table B.1 (execution tolerances for steel structures); ISO 13920:1996 (welding general tolerances for welded constructions).

2. Non-Destructive Testing Method Selection Guide

NDT is where cost escalates quickly. In many African fabrication shops, RT (radiography) requires bringing in a certified subcontractor with a gamma source expensive and logistically difficult, especially in remote mining or energy sites. Method selection should be driven by defect type, joint geometry, code requirement, and budget. The table below is the practical decision matrix.

Method Detects Best For Relative Cost Africa Field Note
VT Visual Surface defects, undercut, porosity, spatter, dimensional 100% of welds, all stages Low Requires good lighting; use torch in evening shifts
PT Dye Penetrant Surface-breaking cracks, porosity Stainless steel, aluminium, non-magnetic materials LowMedium Consumables available in most capital cities; heat-sensitive
MT Magnetic Particle Surface and near-surface cracks Ferritic steel welds, fillet welds Medium Portable yokes work on 12 V; ideal for site work
UT Ultrasonic Internal flaws: lack of fusion, cracks, inclusions Full-penetration welds, thick sections MediumHigh Needs Level 2 technician; equipment imported
RT Radiographic Internal porosity, slag, lack of penetration Critical full-pen welds, pressure vessels High Gamma source logistics & licensing; often subcontracted

Sources: ISO 17635:2016 (NDT of welds general rules); ASNT Recommended Practice SNT-TC-1A:2020 (personnel qualification); AWS D1.1/D1.1M:2020 Clause 6.7 (NDT requirements).

SAFETY WARNING RADIOGRAPHIC TESTING (RT)

Industrial radiography uses gamma sources (Ir-192, Se-75) that can cause severe burns, radiation sickness, and death with short exposure. In African site conditions open fabrication yards, shared compounds, and limited perimeter control the risk of unauthorised entry is high.

  • Only certified radiographers with a valid source licence may operate RT equipment.
  • Establish a minimum 30 m exclusion zone (more for higher activity sources) and physically barricade it.
  • Use a survey meter at all times; never assume the source is shielded.
  • Notify all site personnel including cleaners and security before any exposure.
  • Keep a source log; lost sources are a national emergency and a criminal offence.

Reference: IAEA Safety Standards Series No. SSG-11 (Radiation Safety in Industrial Radiography), 2011.

3. WPS, Codes, and Documentation The Paper Trail That Gets You Paid

In African projects funded by DFIs, mining houses, or government infrastructure programmes, the WPS (Welding Procedure Specification) and its supporting PQR (Procedure Qualification Record) are contract documents. No WPS = no welding = no payment. Every welder must have a valid qualification (WPQ) traceable to the WPS they are using.

The governing codes you will encounter:

  • AWS D1.1/D1.1M Structural Welding Code Steel. Dominant in Anglophone Africa and US-funded projects.
  • BS EN 1090-2 Execution of steel structures. Common in EU-funded and Commonwealth projects.
  • ISO 3834 Quality requirements for fusion welding. Increasingly required by mining and energy clients.
  • ASTM A6/A6M General requirements for rolled structural steel. Governs material acceptance.
  • ASME BPVC Section IX Welding qualifications for pressure equipment (boilers, tanks, piping).

Documentation and traceability is the step that separates professional fabricators from backyard welders. For every project you must be able to trace:

  • Material: Mill certificates (EN 10204 3.1) linked to heat numbers, linked to specific members.
  • Consumables: Electrode batch numbers, storage conditions (especially in humid coastal climates electrodes must be baked and kept in holding ovens).
  • Welder: ID, qualification certificate, WPS used, date, and weld location.
  • Inspection: VT reports, NDT reports, dimensional check sheets, and non-conformance reports (NCRs).
  • Final: As-built drawings, ITP (Inspection and Test Plan) sign-offs, and the Manufacturing Data Book (MDB).

In practice, a simple Excel or paper-based weld map a drawing with each weld numbered and cross-referenced to a log is enough for most African fabrication contracts. The key is that the system is consistently used, not sophisticated.

Quick Reference Checklist Shop Floor

  1. Before welding: Verify WPS is available, welder is qualified, and materials match mill certificates.
  2. Fit-up check: Root gap, bevel angle, and alignment within drawing tolerance before tacking.
  3. Consumables: Confirm electrode type matches WPS; check holding oven temperature and batch number.
  4. Visual inspection: Check every weld after each pass undercut, porosity, spatter, crater cracks.
  5. Dimensional check: Measure member length, diagonal, squareness, and weld size against the tolerance table.
  6. NDT: Confirm method and sampling percentage meet the code or client specification; verify technician certification.
  7. Traceability: Record weld number, welder ID, date, and WPS on the weld map and inspection sheet.
  8. Close-out: Compile VT/NDT reports, NCRs, and as-built data into the Manufacturing Data Book before dispatch.
